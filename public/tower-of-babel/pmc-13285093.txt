High-throughput human microfluidic organoid-on-a-chip platform for modeling liver diseases and screening nanotherapeutics
Authors: Defu Kong, Shihang Yu, Janette Heegsma, Tjasso Blokzijl, Tian Qin, Yixiao Pan, Hans Blokzijl, Vincent E de Meijer, Yourong Duan, Klaas Nico Faber, Qiang Xia, Kang He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHuman liver tissue–derived organoids recapitulate key hepatic phenotypes but are commonly maintained under static conditions, whereas microfluidic organ-on-chip systems provide controllable perfusion and mass transport. Scalable integration of human liver tissue–derived organoids into a perfused, human-relevant Liver-on-Chip remains limited.ResultsWe combined healthy human liver tissue–derived organoids with a high-throughput three-lane OrganoPlate microfluidic format to establish a perfused organoid Liver-on-Chip (HepLoC) featuring 3D luminal tubules under continuous flow. After hepatocyte-directed differentiation under perfusion, bioengineered HepLoC formed mature hepatocyte-like architectures with increased mature hepatocyte marker proteins, enrichment of hepatic transcriptomic signatures, and functional bile canaliculi. As a proof-of-concept for drug-induced liver injury, troglitazone induced dose-dependent hepatocyte injury accompanied by tight-junction disruption, MRP2 mislocalization, and impaired bile acid export, recapitulating key features of cholestatic liver injury. To model metabolic liver disease, free fatty acids triggered lipid droplet accumulation, increased triglycerides and reactive oxygen species, and upregulated lipogenic and inflammatory genes while largely preserving viability, consistent with early-stage metabolic dysfunction–associated fatty liver disease. The high-throughput HepLoC format further enabled parallel testing of reference hepatotoxic drugs and curcumin liposomes by reduced lipid accumulation in fatty-acid–treated HepLoC with minimal hepatotoxicity.ConclusionsOur perfused, organoid-based microfluidic Liver-on-Chip recapitulates essential human liver structure and function and enables integrated, parallel evaluation of hepatotoxicity and optimization of nanotherapeutic strategies, which deciphers the mechanisms of liver diseases, bridging the gap between preclinical research and clinical translation.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12951-026-04477-0.Keywords: Microfluidics; Organoid-on-chip; Liver-on-Chip; High-throughput screening; Lipid nanoparticle; Bile canaliculi; Cholestasis; Drug-induced liver injury; MAFLD, nanomedicine

Introduction

The liver regulates various complex metabolic processes in humans. Liver diseases, such as metabolic associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD), affect hundreds of millions worldwide and remain inadequately managed due to an incomplete understanding of disease progression, limited effective therapies, and the scarcity of donor livers for transplantation [1, 2]. A major bottleneck in liver research and drug development is the lack of in vitro models that both recapitulate key aspects of human liver physiology and reliably reproduce complex disease phenotypes. Conventional in vivo liver models based on genetic, dietary, or drug-induced manipulations in rodents often fail to capture human-specific disease mechanisms and drug responses [3, 4]. This limitation is particularly critical for the emerging field of nanomedicine, where species-specific differences in liver clearance and barrier function often confound the translation of rodent data to humans [5]. In vitro systems using hepatoma-derived cell lines provide convenient and scalable platforms, but they exhibit only partial features of mature hepatocytes and lack appropriate tissue architecture [6, 7]. Primary human hepatocytes and precision-cut liver slices more closely resemble the in vivo liver; however, their use is restricted by rapid functional decline in culture and dependence on rare donor tissue [8].

Organoids, defined as three-dimensional (3D) multicellular structures that self-organize from stem/progenitor cells, embryonic stem cells (ESCs), or induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs), have emerged as powerful tools to model human organ development, homeostasis, and disease [9, 10]. Compared with 2D monolayer culture, 3D organoids more faithfully reproduce the cellular composition, spatial organization, and functional properties of native tissues [11, 12]. Among different organoid types, tissue-resident progenitor cell–derived organoids are particularly attractive for personalized medicine because they preserve patient-specific genetic and epigenetic backgrounds and can be expanded long-term [13].

Liver organoids derived from leucine-rich repeat–containing G protein–coupled receptor 5 (LGR5)–positive liver progenitor cells (LPC) can be stably maintained for months and can differentiate into functionally competent hepatocytes and cholangiocytes [14–16]. These LPC-derived liver organoids are increasingly used to model monogenic liver diseases as well as complex acquired disorders [17, 18]. Nevertheless, their culture in static 3D matrices such as Matrigel does not fully recapitulate key structural and functional features of the native liver, including a well-organized bile canalicular network and physiologically relevant fluid flow. This limitation may constrain their ability to model bile acid transport, cholestatic injury, and other processes that depend on tissue polarity and microenvironmental cues.

Microfluidic organ-on-chip (OoC) technologies have been developed to bridge the gap between conventional cell culture and in vivo organ physiology by recreating tissue-specific microenvironments, including controlled perfusion, oxygen and nutrient gradients, and mechanical cues at the submillimeter scale [19]. Liver-on-chip (LoC) platforms, in particular, offer advantages in maintaining hepatocyte function, promoting cell–cell and cell–matrix interactions, and enabling dynamic exposure to drugs and metabolites in a manner more akin to the intact liver [20, 21]. To date, most LoC systems have relied on primary hepatocytes, hepatoma-derived cell lines, or iPSC-derived hepatocyte-like cells as their cellular source, each of which carries limitations in terms of availability, stability, or maturity. The systematic integration of human tissue-derived liver organoids into microfluidic LoC platforms remains underexplored, especially with regard to reconstructing functional bile canaliculi, modeling complex disease states, and evaluating advanced therapeutic modalities such as nanomedicines.

In this study, we integrated human liver tissue–derived organoids with a high-throughput microfluidic OrganoPlate to establish a liver-on-chip system (HepLoC). Organoid-derived progenitor cells formed 3D epithelial tubules in three-lane chips and were induced toward hepatocyte differentiation, resulting in a hepatocyte-like phenotype with functional bile canaliculi and enhanced liver-specific functions compared with conventional 3D organoids. We further show that HepLoC can model drug-induced cholestasis using troglitazone and early-stage MAFLD via free fatty acid stimulation. Crucially, we demonstrate that HepLoC and its steatotic derivative support not only parallel hepatotoxicity testing of small molecules but also the nanotoxicological profiling of lipid nanoparticle (LNP) components and the evaluation of nanotherapeutic efficacy. Thus, this organoid-based LoC platform provides a human-relevant tool for mechanistic studies and preclinical screening in cholestatic and metabolic liver diseases, bridging the gap between material science and biological validation.

Materials and methods

Establishment and culture of human liver organoids

Human liver organoids were generated following the protocol described by Broutier et al. with modifications based on our previous research [22, 23]. First, human healthy liver tissues were obtained from Renji Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University and University Medical Center Groningen following surgical resection or liver transplantation. Then, liver tissues were dissected into small fragments, which were then enzymatically digested to obtain a single-cell suspension. The isolated single cells were mixed with either 60% Matrigel (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA) or 60% Ultimatrix (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA). After the Matrigel/cell mixture solidified, Expansion Medium (EM) was added to the culture system. The cultures were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. EM was prepared using Advanced Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium/F12 (AdDMEM/F12; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) as the basal medium, supplemented with the following components: 50% homemade Wnt-3a conditioned medium, 1× B27 supplement (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA), 1 × N2 supplement (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 1.25 mM N-acetylcysteine (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), 100 ng/mL fibroblast growth factor 10 (FGF10; PeproTech, Rocky Hill, CT, USA), 10 mM nicotinamide (Sigma-Aldrich), 50 ng/mL hepatocyte growth factor (HGF; PeproTech), 0.1 µg/mL R-spondin 1 (Rspo-1; Stemcell Technologies, Cologne, Germany), 50 ng/mL epidermal growth factor (EGF; PeproTech), 100 ng/mL Noggin (R&D Systems), 10 µM Y27632 (Sigma-Aldrich), 0.5 µM A83-02 (Tocris Bioscience, Bristol, UK), and 10 µM forskolin (Sigma-Aldrich). Organoids were passaged via either mechanical disruption or enzymatic digestion (Triple E; Thermo Fisher Scientific), then reseeded into fresh Matrigel at a split ratio of 1:2 to 1:4. The EM was replaced every 48–72 h.

Liver organoid cell culture in microfluidic 3D chips

Organoids were cultured on a chip in the 3D Organoplastyjk 3-lane 40 (Mimetas BV, the Netherlands) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The cells were grown in the upper channel after the middle channel was loaded with collagen I gel. In brief, 2.5 µL of gel composed of 4 mg/mL collagen-I (AMSbio Cultrex 3D collagen-I Rat Tail, 5 mg/mL), 100 mM HEPES (Life Technologies), and 3.7 mg/mL NaHCO3 (Sigma) was dispensed in the gel inlet and polymerized for 15 min at 37 °C. The upper channel was then coated by adding 20 µL PBS containing 50 µg/mL collagen-I or Matrigel (1:100) to the top medium outlet for 1 h at 37 °C. For the seeding of organoids in the upper tubule, single cells are required. Therefore, the organoids were digested with TrypLE™ Express Enzyme (Thermo Fisher) containing 10 µM Y-27,632 for 10 min at 37 °C, and single cells were collected with the use of a 70 μm cell strainer (Corning). After removing the coating solution, 2 µL of the single-cell suspension was loaded into the upper channels with a seeding density of 10,000 live cells/µL, and EM without A83-21(to support the cell attachment channel) was added to the inlet and outlet of the upper channel in the chip (Mimetas BV, the Netherlands). The plate was composed of 40 microfluidic units positioned underneath a 360-microtiter plate. Each unit had a 3 × 3 array of wells in a microfluidic device. The next day, the medium was changed to EM, and OrganoPlatekjk was placed on OrganoFlowkjk (Mimetas BV, the Netherlands) with an inclination of 7° and an interval of 8 min. Cells were grown at 37 °C in ambient air with 5% CO2. The medium was changed every alternate day.

Hepatocyte differentiation of human liver organoids

Hepatocyte differentiation of human liver organoids was carried out following the protocol established in our prior work, with minor adjustments [24]. Briefly, Organoids were first cultured in EM supplemented with 25 ng/mL bone morphogenetic protein 7 (BMP7; PeproTech, Rocky Hill, CT, USA) for a duration of one week. After the preliminary induction, the culture medium was switched to hepatocyte differentiation medium (DM) for an additional week to facilitate the maturation of organoid cells into functional hepatocytes. The DM was formulated using Advanced Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium/F12 (AdDMEM/F12) as the basal medium, with the following supplements added: 1 × B27 supplement, 1 × N2 supplement, 1.25 mM N-acetylcysteine, 10 nM gastrin (Tocris Bioscience, Bristol, UK), 50 ng/mL epidermal growth factor (EGF; PeproTech), 25 ng/mL hepatocyte growth factor (HGF; PeproTech), 0.5 µM A83-01 (Tocris Bioscience), 10 µM N-[N-(3,5-difluorophenacetyl)-L-alanyl]-S-phenylglycine t-butyl ester (DAPT; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), 3 µM dexamethasone (sourced from the University Medical Center Groningen, UMCG), 25 ng/mL BMP7 (PeproTech), and 100 ng/mL fibroblast growth factor 19 (FGF19; PeproTech).

Albumin and Alpha-1-Antitrypsin production

To determine the secretion of human Albumin and α-1-antitrypsin, supernatants of chips and 3D organoids were collected after 48 h of culture. Freshly isolated primary hepatocytes were seeded on 12-well plates and maintained in medium for 48 h until collection of supernatants. These supernatants were examined using a Human Alpha-1-Antitrypsin and Albumin ELISA kit (both from Bethyl Laboratory, UK).

Urea production and ammonia elimination

For the evaluation of cellular functions associated with urea production and ammonia removal, chips and 3D orgnoids were incubated in a medium fortified with 3 mM NH₄Cl, and the corresponding supernatants were collected 48 h after the initiation of the incubation. Meanwhile, freshly prepared primary hepatocytes were plated onto 24-well plates and cultured in 3 mM NH₄Cl-containing medium for 48 h, after which the supernatants were gathered. Following supernatant collection, all cell samples were dissociated into single cells through digestion, and cell counting was performed subsequently. The Urea Assay Kit (Abcam, USA) was employed to detect urea content, whereas an enzymatic colorimetric assay (Megazyme International) was used for NH₄⁺ concentration measurement.

Immunofluorescence

For immunofluorescence staining, the cells in the OrganoPlate® were fixed with 3.7% formaldehyde in PBS for 15 min and permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X100 (Merck) for 10 min. After washing 2 times with PBS, the cells were blocked with 2% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Thermo Fisher), 2% bovine serum albumin (BSA, Merck Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany), 0.1% Tween 20 (Sigma) in PBS, and incubated with the following primary antibodies: LGR5 (dilution 1:200), albumin (dilution 1:200), Hepatocyte nuclear factor 4 alpha (HNF4a, dilution 1:100), KRT19 (dilution 1:200), BSEP (homemade, 1:50, also called SPGP, named K12 in reference [25]), albumin (1:200), and MRP2 (1:20) at RT for 1 h. Finally, the chips were incubated with suitable secondary antibodies and 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, 1:10000, Vector Laboratories, CA, USA) for 30 min. The chips were then washed with PBS. The antibodies used in our study is provided in Supplementary Table 1. Due to the complex 3D architecture of the HepLoC system and the optical density of the collagen-filled central channel, high-magnification imaging was subject to inherent physical limitations, including light scattering and restricted objective working distances. Zeiss 410 inverted laser scanning microscope (Leica Microsystems, Wild Heerbrugg, Germany) with 10X magnification objectives. was employed to provide the most accurate representation within these technical constraints.

Barrier integrity assay

To evaluate the barrier integrity of the lateral vessels within the microfluidic chips, 50 µL of culture medium supplemented with 0.5 mg/mL fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-labeled dextran (molecular weight: 150 kDa; Sigma-Aldrich, cat. no. 46946) was added to both the inlet and outlet ports of the upper channels. Time-series imaging was then performed using a Zeiss 410 inverted laser scanning microscope maintained at 37 °C, with images captured at 1-minute intervals over an 8-minute period.

Bile canaliculi staining

To visualize functionally active bile canaliculi in the chips, staining was performed using 5-(and-6)-carboxy-2′,7′-dichlorofluorescein diacetate (CDFDA; Sigma-Aldrich, cat. no. 21884) following the procedure outlined below. Briefly, the culture medium was aspirated from all channels of the chip. Subsequently, 50 µL of either EM or DM. Each containing 5 µM CDFDA and Hoechst 33,342 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. no. H33342; diluted 1:2000 in PBS)—was added to the inlet and outlet of the top channel. The chips were placed on an OrganoFlow® S platform and incubated for 30 min in a controlled environment (37 °C, 5% CO₂). During incubation, the platform was set to an inclination angle of 14° and a medium circulation interval of 8 min to mimic physiological flow. After incubation, the chips were rinsed six times with PBS to remove unincorporated stain. Static imaging was conducted using a Micro XLS-C High Content Imaging System (Molecular Devices, Sunnyvale, CA, USA). For time-lapse imaging experiments, the cells were continuously maintained in DM containing CDFDA. For experiments evaluating drug-induced effects on bile canaliculi, cells were pre-incubated with troglitazone (Tocris Bioscience, Bristol, UK) at the specified concentrations for 6 h at 37 °C prior to CDFDA staining.

Nanoparticle preparation and integrated high-throughput screening

Curcumin-loaded liposomes (Curculosomes) were synthesized using the thin-film hydration method followed by extrusion, where a lipid mixture comprising DSPC, Cholesterol, and DSPE-PEG 2000 (60:35:5 molar ratio) was co-dissolved with curcumin (1:10 w/w drug-to-lipid ratio), dried into a thin film, hydrated with PBS at 60 °C, and extruded through 100 nm polycarbonate membranes to generate uniform nanoparticles.

For nanotoxicological and hepatotoxicity profiling, HepLoC were incubated with either lipid nanoparticle (LNP) components (DSPE-PEG 2000, D-Lin-MC3-DMA, DOTAP chloride; 0–512 µM) or reference hepatotoxins (Acetaminophen 0–10 mM; Tetracycline 0–100 µM) for 48 h. Toxicity endpoints were assessed via PrestoBlue cell viability assays according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, the PrestoBlue™ HS Cell Viability assay (Thermo Fisher) was performed culture media were aspirated in all channels, and 50 µL of Presto Blue reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, diluted 1:10) was added to the inlet and outlet of the top channel. The cells were incubated for 10 min in an incubator (37 °C, 5% CO2) on the OrganoFlow® S with an inclination of 14° and an interval of 8 min. After incubation, the medium was harvested and transferred to 96 well plate, and the absorbance was measured using a microplate reader at 450 nm.

MAFLD model was established by perfusing HepLoC with a free fatty acid mixture (oleic acid/palmitic acid, 2:1, FFA-HepLoC) for 48 h; during this induction, chips were co-treated with either clinical-stage small molecule candidates or the nano-formulated Curculosomes versus free curcumin, followed by BODIPY staining and Triglyceride-Glo assay according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

RNA isolation, cDNA synthesis, and Real-time quantitative PCR

Real-time quantitative reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) was conducted following the protocol described in our earlier work, with minor adaptations [26]. Briefly, Total RNA was isolated from cultured organoids and fresh liver tissues using TRIzol® reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, The Netherlands), strictly adhering to the manufacturer’s recommended protocol. The purity and concentration of the isolated RNA were determined using a Nanodrop 2000c UV-visible spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), with absorbance ratios (A260/A280 and A260/A230) used to verify RNA integrity. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized via reverse transcription, using M-MLV reverse transcriptase and random hexamers (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) as primers. TaqMan® primers and fluorescent probes for target genes were designed using Primer Express 3.0.1 software, with detailed sequences provided in Supplementary Table 2. Quantitative amplification of target genes was performed using a qPCR Core Kit Master Mix (Eurogentec, The Netherlands) on a QuantStudio™ 3 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific), following the manufacturer’s cycling parameters.

Reactive oxygen species staining

Intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels were detected using the DCFDA/H2DCFDA-Cellular ROS Assay Kit (Abcam, cat. no. ab113851). In brief, First, the culture medium was completely aspirated from all channels of the microfluidic chip. The cells were then rinsed twice with the washing buffer provided in the assay kit. And The cells were incubated with the kit’s diluted DCFDA solution for 45 min at 37 °C in the dark. After incubation, the cells were re-rinsed twice with washing buffer to eliminate unbound DCFDA probe. Live-cell fluorescence imaging was performed using a microscope equipped with a FITC-compatible filter set. At least five pictures were captured for each condition.

Statistics

All experimental data are presented as the mean ± Standard Deviation (SD). Statistical analysis of differences between groups was performed using either one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) (for comparisons among three or more groups) or unpaired two-tailed t-tests (for comparisons between two groups). All calculations were conducted using GraphPad Prism 8 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). A two-tailed p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Schematic representation of liver organoid chips using high-throughput OrganoPlate

First, we successfully established organoids derived from leucine-rich repeat-containing G-protein-coupled receptor 5-positive (LGR5⁺) liver progenitor cells using tissues from healthy human livers (Fig. 1A). Liver organoids were first established and pre-expanded in expansion medium (EM) within Matrigel droplets, a condition that supports the maintenance of progenitor cell properties the organoids display stable proliferation characteristics and morphology across different generations (Fig. 1B). In addition to exhibiting robust proliferative capacity (Fig. 1C, KI67, a marker of actively proliferating cells), the organoids also demonstrate the dual differentiation potential into cholangiocytes (EPCAM, SOX9 and KRT7) and hepatocytes (HNF4A). Subsequently, we utilized a 40-chip OrganoPlate to develop the Liver-on-Chip (LoC) system, with each chip featuring a three-channel design (Fig. 1D). Among the three channels of each chip, the central channel was loaded with a user-selected extracellular matrix (ECM); this ECM could optionally contain cells embedded within it, depending on experimental needs (Fig. 1E-S1). In the present study, the central channel was filled with cell-free type I collagen to serve as a structural scaffold. The two adjacent channels (designated as “perfusion channels”) were designed for medium sampling and refreshing throughout the culture period—critical for maintaining long-term cell viability. Prior to cell seeding, the inner surfaces of the perfusion channels were coated with ECM to promote cell adhesion. Comparing the channel coating with Matrigel or rat tail collagen 1, the latter appeared superior in supporting cell attachment and growth (data not shown) and was used for all subsequent experiments. After digestion, single cells of liver organoids were seeded into the perfusion channel (Fig. 1E-S2). To enhance cell attachment to the ECM surface, the chips were placed in a sideways orientation during the initial culture stage (Fig. 1E-S3). Following cell adhesion, the plate was transferred to Rock platform for dynamic perfusion culture. The platform was set to a 7° inclination angle and an 8-minute medium circulation interval—parameters optimized to mimic physiological shear stress and ensure uniform nutrient distribution. After approximately one week of culture in EM under the aforementioned dynamic conditions, the liver organoid-derived cells in the chips self-organized to form luminal structures, completing the construction of the LoC’s core structural unit (Fig. 1E-S4).

Establishing 3D lumen structure with human liver organoids-derived cells in LoC

As described in the preceding section, human liver organoid-derived single cells were seeded into perfusion channels of the 40-chip OrganoPlate, followed by dynamic culture on the Rock platform (7° inclination, 8-minute circulation interval) in EM. After seven days of this culture regimen, we observed the formation of a cell layer at the top and bottom surfaces of the perfusion channels, this layer appeared confluent overall but exhibited heterogeneous thickness across different regions (Fig. 2A). Further characterization via 3D reconstruction of organoid cell nuclei confirmed that this cell layer had self-assembled into a continuous cellular tubule, a key structural feature of Liver-on-Chip (LoC) system (Fig. 2B and C). Positive KI67 signals were readily detected in the cell layer, with a focal distribution pattern, indicating that a subset of cells in chips retained proliferative activity even after 7 days of culture (Fig. 2D). Next, epithelial barrier integrity of the cellular tubules, a critical functional attribute for mimicking in vivo hepatic epithelial properties, was assessed by fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-labeled dextran and the leakage of FITC-dextran from the perfusion channel to the central channel was monitored. Compared to tubules that culture for 3 days with incomplete cell confluence (where gaps remained between cells), the perfusion channels that culture for 6 days with fully confluent cell layers showed an obviously delayed rate of FITC-dextran leakage to the central channel (Fig. 2E). This result demonstrated that the confluent cellular tubules had formed a functional epithelial barrier, validating the structural and functional maturity of the 3D lumen in LoC.

Differentiating organoid-derived cells to hepatocytes in LoC

Next, we aimed to drive the differentiation of organoid towards mature hepatocytes in the LoC, which is critical for enabling the LoC to mimic in vivo hepatocyte physiology and respond to drug treatments—key functions for studying liver disease mechanisms and drug efficacy. First, we validated the established hepatocyte differentiation protocols [24] using 3D liver organoids cultured in 3D Matrigel Fig. S1). Organoids (Org) cultured in the EM were transparent monolayer spheres (Fig. S1A, upper panel). In contrast, after sequential culture in BMP7-supplemented EM (preliminary induction phase) and differentiation medium (DM, maturation phase), the organoids (HepOrg) transitioned into dense cellular aggregates—morphological changes consistent with hepatocyte differentiation (Fig. S1A, bottom panel). Next, we applied identical culture conditions to induce LoC to hepatocyte differentiation (HepLoC). Primary human hepatocytes (PH) were included as a reference to evaluate the maturation status of HepLoC. As shown in Fig. 3A, compared with LoC and HepOrg, HepLoC exhibited significant upregulation of mature hepatocyte markers (ALB, HNF4A), with expression patterns resembling those of primary hepatocytes. Meanwhile, stem cell markers LGR5 (some samples showed undetected values) and the cholangiocyte marker KRT19 showed no significant difference following hepatocyte differentiation. Additionally, HepLoC demonstrated significantly increased secretion levels of albumin (ALB) and α-antitrypsin (AAT), enhanced urea synthesis and ammonia elimination, compared with HepOrg and LoC, reaching levels close to those of PH (Fig. 3B).

Immunofluorescence staining revealed that nuclear HNF4A and cytoplasmic CK19 were detected in both LoC and HepLoC, with significantly higher HNF4A expression in HepLoC. In addition, the expression of the stem cell marker LGR5 in LoC, confirming its progenitor potential, whereas LGR5 protein was undetectable in HepLoC (Fig. 3D). However, DAPI staining indicated that cell density was higher in HepLoC than in LoC, suggesting that the cells retained proliferative capacity in HepLoC. Consistent with the gene expression patterns of hepatocyte markers, Albumin protein was readily detectable in HepLoC but virtually absent in LoC (Fig. 3E). Notably, significant membrane staining for both Multidrug Resistance-Associated Protein 2 (MRP2) and Bile Salt Export Pump (BSEP) was observed in HepLoC but not in LoC, indicating acquisition of bile salt transport capacity. Collectively, these findings indicate that HepLoC promotes a higher level of differentiation of organoids compared to 3D Matrigel culture.

Promotion of hepatocyte maturation in HepLoC compared with 3D liver organoids

To further verify the advantages of LoC over 3D Matrigel in terms of hepatocyte differentiation, we performed transcriptome sequencing on both HepLoC and HepOrg that from three different donors. The results indicated an exhibited enhanced hepatocyte feature on HepLoC. PCA analysis and heatmap showed the obvious differences between HepOrg and HepLoC (Fig. 4A and S2A, and volcano plot indicated that the expression of some liver-specific genes (SAA2, SAA, GC, CYP3A5, ALB, ADH6) was upregulated in HepLoC (Fig. 4B). Additionally, tissue- and cell-type-specific gene-set enrichment analysis (Fig. 4C) revealed that HepLoC were predominantly enriched for liver-specific gene signatures, with minimal expression of non-hepatic markers, underscoring the superior hepatocyte maturation potential of HepLoC. GO enrichment analysis showed that the top changed DEGs in HepLoC were mainly related to identical protein binding, signal transduction and cell division and other GO terms (Fig. 4D). KEGG enrichment analysis of the regulated genes in HepLoC showed that they were significantly enriched in vitamin digestion, drug metabolism, ether lipid metabolism, alcoholic liver disease and Wnt pathway (Fig. 4E). Heat maps exhibited that the genes related to CYP450 metabolism, lipid transport, bile acid metabolism, alcoholic metabolism, fat acid metabolism, and xenobiotic metabolism were upregulated in HepLoC (Fig. 4F and Fig. S2B). In summary, these results demonstrate that HepLoC promotes a more mature hepatocyte phenotype, as evidenced by the enrichment of liver-specific metabolic pathways and the distinct gene expression profile compared to 3D liver organoids.

Visualization of the bile canaliculi network using CDFDA in LoC

The primary role of the liver lies in the synthesis and release of bile acids. Bile acids are synthesized in hepatocytes and secreted into the interconnected network of bile canaliculi through specific cell membrane transporters such as BSEP and MRP2, followed by subsequent translocation into the bile ducts. The bile canaliculi network is formed by tight junctions between the mature hepatocytes. First, we performed co-immunostaining of ZO-1 and beta-catenin to distinguish the apical and basolateral domains to substantiate the establishment of functional polarity within HepLoC. The staining in Fig. S3A revealed a distinct spatial segregation between the two markers: ZO-1 formed a continuous network delineating the apical (canalicular) membranes, whereas beta-catenin was localized to the basolateral interfaces, showing a mutually exclusive distribution with ZO-1. This clear spatial separation of tight and adherens junction markers provides robust evidence that the HepLoC attained physiological membrane asymmetry and structural maturation within the microfluidic environment. Next, to assess the bile canaliculi network functionality in HepLoC, we utilized 5(6)-carboxy-2,7-dichlorofluorescein (CDF), a fluorescent substrate for MRP2, to visualize bile acid transport. After cellular uptake of non-fluorescent CDFDA, intracellular esterases convert it into fluorescent CDF, which is then actively transported into the bile canaliculi network by MRP2. (Fig. 5A) [27]. In both LoC and HepLoC, CDF was rapidly detectable and accumulated inside the cells (Fig. 5B). While fluorescent staining remained largely inside the LoC over a timeframe of 30 min (Fig. 5B, left panels). In contrast, a distinct accumulation of fluorescent staining between cell junctions was observed in the HepLoC and became more prominent with time (Fig. 5B, right panels), suggesting efficient transport of CDF into the bile canaliculi network. To further confirm the structural integrity of the bile canaliculi, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images revealed that HepLoC cultures had significantly larger tight junctions and bile canaliculi compared to LoC. Additionally, cilia were observed on the cell surface of HepLoC, further supporting the more mature hepatocyte-like features in HepLoC (Fig. 5C). Together, these results demonstrate that HepLoC are capable of forming a bile canaliculi network and may serve as an in vitro disease model for cholangitis.

Modeling of drug-induced cholestasis in HepLoC

To investigate the applicability of HepLoC for modeling drug-induced cholestasis, we exposed HepLoC cultures to Troglitazone (TGZ), a known cholestatic liver injury agent that inhibits MRP2 and BSEP activity [28, 29]. (Fig. 6A). As TGZ concentration increased, cell viability in the TGZ-treated HepLoC significantly decreased (Fig. 6B), accompanied by a marked elevation in lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) levels in the supernatant, indicating substantial hepatocyte damage. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that TGZ treatment caused a notable redistribution of MRP2 and tight junction protein ZO-1 (Fig. 6C). In untreated HepLoC, MRP2 was primarily localized at the plasma membrane, consistent with its role in bile acid transport. However, in TGZ-treated HepLoC, MRP2 predominantly localized to the cytoplasm, suggesting impaired transport function. Similarly, ZO-1, which normally localizes to the membrane at tight junctions in HepLoC, was observed to shift to the cytoplasm and show localized enrichment at the plasma membrane, particularly in regions lacking cell-cell contact. This disruption of protein localization was further confirmed by live CDFDA imaging (Fig. 6D), which demonstrated a significant impairment in bile acid transport to the intra-hepatocyte network in TGZ-treated HepLoC. Further TEM revealed that, compared to HepLoC, TGZ led to significant disruption of the tight junctions between hepatocytes (Fig. 6E). Overall, these findings support the use of HepLoC as a model for studying drug-induced cholestasis by recapitulating key features of cholestatic injury, including hepatocyte damage, disrupted bile acid transport, and altered tight junction integrity.

Modeling of early-stage MAFLD in HepLoC

The major functions of the liver are detoxification and metabolic homeostasis. We exposed HepLoC to a mixture of free fatty acids (FFA; oleic acid 500 µM and palmitic acid 250 µM) to evaluate the applicability of HepLoC for modelling MAFLD, a disease associated with excess lipid accumulation in the liver [30, 31]. After 3 days, phase-contrast microscopy revealed prominent dark cytoplasmic inclusions in FFA-treated HepLoC (FFA-HepLoC), consistent with lipid droplet accumulation (Fig. 7A), and intracellular triglyceride measurements confirmed a significant increase in triglyceride content compared with untreated HepLoC (Fig. 7B). In line with this, FFA stimulation upregulated lipogenic genes such as ACACA and FASN and downregulated the fatty acid oxidation–related transcription factor PPARα (Fig. 7D), indicating a shift toward lipid accumulation, which was further corroborated by BODIPY staining showing robust lipid storage in FFA-HepLoC (Fig. 7F). GSEA revealed significant enrichment of the HALLMARK_OXIDATIVE_PHOSPHORYLATION gene set in FFA-HepLoC relative to HepLoC (NES ≈ 1.74, FDR < 0.01), suggesting a compensatory activation of mitochondrial respiratory pathways, consistent with the increased intracellular ROS levels observed in FFA-treated cultures (Fig. 7G). Despite these pronounced metabolic alterations, PrestoBlue assays showed no significant difference in cell viability between FFA-HepLoC and control HepLoC (Fig. 7C), indicating that lipid droplet induction at this time point did not cause overt cytotoxicity. However, inflammation-related genes TNFA and IL6 were markedly upregulated in FFA-HepLoC (Fig. 7D), which was supported by significant GSEA enrichment of the HALLMARK_TNFA_SIGNALING_VIA_NFKB gene set in FFA-HepLoC compared with HepLoC (Fig. 7E), indicative of early inflammatory activation. Together, these findings demonstrate that FFA-treated HepLoC recapitulates key features of early-stage MAFLD, including intracellular lipid accumulation, mitochondrial activation with increased ROS, and initiation of inflammatory signaling, supporting the utility of HepLoC for modelling MAFLD progression in vitro.

Integrated nanotoxicological profiling and efficacy screening of therapeutic candidates via FFA-HepLoC

Current preclinical evaluation of MAFLD therapeutics relies heavily on rodent models, which often fail to predict human-specific hepatotoxicity and efficacy. Moreover, the rapid expansion of nanomedicine necessitates robust, human-relevant systems to assess the safety of novel delivery vectors and the therapeutic advantage of nano-formulations. We hypothesized that the FFA-HepLoC platform could serve as a comprehensive high-throughput screening tool to simultaneously profile the nanotoxicology of lipid nanoparticle (LNP) components and evaluate the efficacy of both small-molecule and nanotherapeutic candidates. To validate the platform’s utility in toxicology, we first exposed HepLoC to a diverse panel of compounds. This included reference hepatotoxins (Acetaminophen and Tetracycline) for validation, alongside critical LNP components (DSPE-PEG 2000, D-Lin-MC3-DMA, and DOTAP chloride, Table S3) widely used in RNA delivery. As shown in Fig. 8A, HepLoC successfully discriminated the toxicity profiles based on cell viability. While reference hepatotoxins triggered expected dose-dependent cell death, the LNP lipids exhibited distinct nanotoxicological signatures. DSPE-PEG 2000 showed excellent biocompatibility, whereas the cationic lipid DOTAP chloride induced significant dose-dependent hepatotoxicity, consistent with its known membrane-destabilizing properties. The ionizable lipid D-Lin-MC3-DMA displayed a favorable safety profile at therapeutically relevant concentrations but exhibited toxicity at higher doses, underscoring the sensitivity of HepLoC in defining the therapeutic window for nanocarriers.

Building on these safety profiles, we utilized the FFA-HepLoC model to screen for anti-steatotic efficacy. We first evaluated the hepatotoxicity of three preclinical MAFLD therapeutic candidates (Fenofibrate, Obeticholic acid, and FGF19, Table S3) in HepLoC, and subsequently assessed their anti-steatotic efficacy in the FFA-HepLoC model. Treatment with these agents resulted in a substantial reduction of intracellular lipid accumulation (Bodipy staining) and triglyceride content compared to controls (Fig. 8B and C), without inducing cytotoxicity (Fig. 8A), confirming the model’s physiological responsiveness. To further demonstrate the platform’s specific utility in nanomedicine, we evaluated the efficacy of curculosomes (curcumin-loaded liposomes) versus free curcumin. While free curcumin showed moderate effects limited by aqueous solubility, curculosomes demonstrated superior efficacy, significantly potentiating lipid clearance without nanotoxicity (Fig. 8B and C). This enhanced potency is likely attributed to the improved cellular uptake and stability of the nano-formulation within the microfluidic channels.

Collectively, these data demonstrate that FFA-HepLoC is a versatile, human-relevant platform capable of profiling the nanotoxicology of delivery vehicles and validating the superior efficacy of nanotherapeutics over conventional formulations, bridging the gap between material science and preclinical drug development.

Discussion

In this study, we established a human liver organoid–derived liver-on-a-chip (HepLoC) platform that recapitulates key structural and functional features of the hepatic epithelium and its bile canalicular network and enables modeling of drug-induced injury and early stage MAFLD. Organoid-derived progenitor cells seeded into a high-throughput microfluidic OrganoPlate self-organized into 3D luminal tubules forming a confluent epithelial barrier. Upon hepatocyte-directed differentiation, these tubules acquired a mature hepatocyte-like phenotype compared with conventional 3D organoid cultures. Functional bile canaliculi were evidenced by vectorial transport and accumulation of fluorescent bile acid analogues and by ultrastructural visualization of tight junctions and canalicular structures. Using this system, the cholestatic antidiabetic drug troglitazone induced hepatocyte damage, tight junction disruption, mislocalization of MRP2 and ZO-1, and impaired bile acid export, thereby recapitulating key features of drug-induced cholestasis. Furthermore, exposure to free fatty acids triggered steatosis, oxidative stress, and early inflammatory activation in the absence of overt cytotoxicity, modeling early-stage MAFLD. Finally, HepLoC permitted parallel assessment of hepatotoxicity and anti-steatotic efficacy of candidate MAFLD therapeutics in a miniaturizable, human-relevant format. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that HepLoC, by integrating human organoid biology with microfluidic culture, provides a versatile platform for mechanistic studies and preclinical drug evaluation in cholestatic and metabolic liver disease.

OoC systems have become increasingly used in academia and the pharmaceutical industry to model human organ function and pathophysiology as well as for drug testing [8, 19]. While using human cells, OoC may show better translatability to human physiology, pathophysiology, and drug responses when compared to animal models. Moreover, and not least important, it will reduce the use of laboratory animals. Early development of these OoC platforms typically made use of “organ-specific” cell lines, like HepG2 cells to model hepatocytes [32] or Caco-2 cells for enterocytes [33]. Following these successes, OoC is being further developed using primary cells such as hepatocytes, stellate cells, and Kupffer cells from the human liver [34, 35]. Alternatively, tissue-derived stem cells or induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSC) are increasingly being used [24, 30, 36]. Human liver tissue-derived organoids can be easily expanded in vitro, are bipotent, and differentiate into hepatocytes and/or cholangiocytes. In addition, these organoids show high genomic stability [24, 30] therefore, they have specific advantages over the use of primary or iPSC-derived liver cells.

Various liver-on-chip technologies have been developed to recapitulate liver pathophysiology, ranging from simple 2D monolayers under flow to complex 3D co-culture systems using specialized microfluidic housings, each of which may have advantages or limitations in answering specific research questions [35, 37–39]. Our HepLoC platform offers distinct advantages over existing models by bridging the gap between high-through-put screening and clinical fidelity. Specifically, our pump-free perfusion system is integrated into a 36-unit plate, significantly surpassing the throughput limitations of conventional microfluidic chips that rely on complex external tubing. Furthermore, the utilization of human liver tissue-derived organoids ensures the preservation of donor-specific genetic and functional traits, enabling reliable individualized drug testing. This unique combination of high-throughput architecture and organized 3D luminal structures provides a superior biomimetic environment for studying complex biological processes, such as nanoparticle transport.

Our results show that cells from human adult liver-derived organoids develop cellular tubules within one week in chips and can be enriched for specific hepatocyte functions, such as albumin production, urea synthesis and ammonia clearance. Efficient formation of cellular tubules was only observed when the culture channel was precoated with collagen type I rather than Matrigel. This was unexpected, as human liver organoids are typically cultured as 3D spheres in Matrigel. On the other hand, primary hepatocytes are routinely grown on collagen I-coated culture plates. It remains to be determined whether collagen-I also stimulates the differentiation of LPC-derived cellular tubules towards hepatocytes in the chip. In addition, Although HepLoC exhibits strong albumin synthesis and metabolic enzyme activity after differentiation, it still fails to reach these levels of primary hepatocytes, and further differentiation protocols need to be developed [40]. Significant amounts of MRP2 and BSEP were detected in the cell membrane, allowing the export of fluorescent bile acids. Troglitazone treatment in chips induced mislocalization of MRP2 and BSEP, impaired the accumulation of fluorescent bile acids in the intercellular network, and reduced cell viability. These findings are in line with earlier reports using either primary hepatocytes in 2D or sandwich cultures [41] and validate the applicability of human liver tissue-derived organoids in chips to study the molecular mechanisms associated with drug-induced cholestasis. Notably, in drug toxic test, obeticholic acid treatment slightly enhanced cell viability relative to the control group (Fig. 8). This potential cytoprotective effect likely arises from the potent activation of the Farnesoid X Receptor (FXR). Beyond regulating lipid metabolism, FXR agonists are known to alleviate endoplasmic reticulum stress and suppress oxidative damage by upregulating antioxidant and anti-apoptotic pathways [42, 43]. In the context of our HepLoC, obeticholic acid may optimize cellular homeostasis by mitigating basal metabolic stressors, thereby manifesting as a modest increase in overall metabolic activity. Similar pro-survival effects of FXR activation have been documented in various hepatic injury models [44]. Extending this utility to nanotoxicology, the platform also effectively discriminated the cytotoxicity profiles of LNP components, identifying the cationic lipid DOTAP as a potent hepatotoxin while confirming the biocompatibility of DSPE-PEG 2000. Moreover, in the context of MAFLD therapy, FFA-HepLoC highlighted the superior efficacy of curculosomes over free drug, likely due to enhanced cellular uptake in the microfluidic environment. This demonstrates the system’s unique value in screening LNP formulations and optimizing nanotherapeutics.

Although HepLoC successfully recapitulates the 3D luminal architecture and core metabolic functions of the human liver, we acknowledge that the current model, based primarily on parenchymal cells, represents a simplified version of the complex liver microenvironment. To further enhance its physiological mimicry, future iterations of HepLoC will focus on the integration of non-parenchymal cells (NPCs), such as hepatic stellate cells and Kupffer cells. Such multicellular co-cultures will be essential for modeling the intricate inflammatory and fibrotic cascades characteristic of advanced metabolic liver diseases. Furthermore, the multi-channel design of the chip system offers an inherent advantage for scaling the complexity of the model. We are currently exploring the development of multi-organ-on-a-chip networks by populating the 3rd channel with diverse organoid types such as gut, which allow to investigate inter-organ crosstalk like gut-liver axis, and is crucial for evaluating the systemic absorption, metabolism, and safety of nanotherapeutics. By evolving from a single-organ unit to an integrated multi-organ system, the HepLoC platform holds the potential to provide more holistic and predictive insights into human systemic pathophysiology. Evidently, this work is only the beginning of possible applications of human liver tissue-derived organoids cultured in the LoC to model liver health and disease, as well as a potential screening platform for novel nanomedicines and drug-based therapies for liver disease.

Conclusion

In summary, we established a high-throughput human liver organoid–derived liver-on-a-chip (HepLoC) that surpasses conventional models by recapitulating polarized hepatic tubules with functional bile canaliculi and mature metabolic programs. This physiological relevance enabled the accurate modeling of drug-induced cholestasis and early-stage MAFLD, capturing key pathological features ranging from bile transport defects to inflammatory activation. Crucially, beyond standard disease modeling, the platform demonstrated unique utility in nanomedicine by facilitating the nanotoxicological profiling of LNP components and validating the superior potency of liposomal formulations. Our study thus positions HepLoC as a versatile, human-relevant tool for mechanistic research and the accelerated screening of both small-molecule and nanotherapeutic candidates, bridging the critical gap between material science innovations and biological validation.
