Knapsack on Graphs with Relaxed Neighborhood Constraints
Palash Dey, Ashlesha Hota, Sudeshna Kolay

Abstract
In the knapsack problems with neighborhood constraints that were studied before, the input is a graph 𝒢 \mathcal{G} on a set 𝒱 \mathcal{V} of items, each item v ∈ 𝒱 v\in\mathcal{V} has a weight w v w_{v} and profit p v p_{v} , the size s s of the knapsack, and the demand d d . The goal is to compute if there exists a feasible solution whose total weight is at most s s and total profit is at most d d . Here, feasible solutions are all subsets 𝒮 \mathcal{S} of the items such that, for every item in 𝒮 \mathcal{S} , at least one of its neighbors in 𝒢 \mathcal{G} is also in 𝒮 \mathcal{S} for 1-Neighborhood Knapsack , and all its neighbors in 𝒢 \mathcal{G} are also in 𝒮 \mathcal{S} for All-Neighborhood Knapsack [ 2 ] . In this paper, we study a ”relaxation” of the above problems. Specifically, we allow all possible subsets of items to be feasible solutions. However, only those items for which we pick at least one or all of its neighbor (out-neighbor for directed graph) contribute to profit whereas every item picked contribute to the weight; we call the corresponding problems Relaxed 1-Neighborhood Knapsack and Relaxed All-Neighborhood Knapsack . We fill some of the important research gaps that were present for 1-Neighborhood Knapsack and All-Neighborhood Knapsack and perform an extensive study of the classical and parameterized complexity of Relaxed 1-Neighborhood Knapsack and Relaxed All-Neighborhood Knapsack for both directed and undirected graphs. In particular, we show that both Relaxed 1-Neighborhood Knapsack and Relaxed All-Neighborhood Knapsack are strongly 𝖭𝖯 \mathsf{NP} -complete even on undirected graphs. Regarding parameterized complexity, we show both Relaxed 1-Neighborhood Knapsack and 1-Neighborhood Knapsack are 𝖶 ⁡ [ 𝟤 ] \mathsf{W[2]} -hard parameterized by the size s s of the knapsack size. Interestingly, both Relaxed All-Neighborhood Knapsack and All-Neighborhood Knapsack are 𝖶 ⁡ [ 𝟣 ] \mathsf{W[1]} -hard parameterized by (i) knapsack size, s s plus profit demand, d d and also (ii) parameterized by solution size, b b . For Relaxed 1-Neighborhood Knapsack and 1-Neighborhood Knapsack , we present a randomized color-coding-based pseudo- 𝖥𝖯𝖳 \mathsf{FPT} algorithm with running time 𝒪 ⋆ ​ ( e b ⋅ b b ⋅ 2 b 2 ⋅ 𝗆𝗂𝗇 ⁡ { s 2 , d 2 } ) \mathcal{O}^{\star}\left(e^{b}\cdot b^{b}\cdot 2^{b^{2}}\cdot{\sf min}\{s^{2},d^{2}\}\right) , parameterized by the solution size b b , and consequently by the demand d d . This indicates that 1-neighborhood constraints may be easier to handle algorithmically than all-neighborhood constraints in knapsack. We then consider the treewidth of the input graph (for directed graphs, treewidth of the undirected graph obtained by ignoring the directions) as our parameter and design an 𝒪 ⋆ ​ ( 2 tw ⋅ 𝗆𝗂𝗇 ⁡ { s 2 , d 2 } ) \mathcal{O}^{\star}\left(2^{\text{tw}}\cdot{\sf min}\{s^{2},d^{2}\}\right) time pseudo fixed-parameter tractable ( 𝖥𝖯𝖳 \mathsf{FPT} ) algorithm parameterized by treewidth, tw for 1-Neighborhood Knapsack and All-Neighborhood Knapsack , and an 𝒪 ⋆ ​ ( 4 tw ⋅ 𝗆𝗂𝗇 ⁡ { s 2 , d 2 } ) \mathcal{O}^{\star}\left(4^{\text{tw}}\cdot{\sf min}\{s^{2},d^{2}\}\right) time algorithm for both the relaxed variants. Note that, assuming 𝖯 ≠ 𝖭𝖯 \mathsf{P}\neq\mathsf{NP} , we cannot have an 𝖥𝖯𝖳 \mathsf{FPT} algorithm parameterized by the treewidth of the input graph for any of the four problems studied here since all of them are 𝖭𝖯 \mathsf{NP} -complete even for trees. Finally, we present an additive 1 1 approximation for Relaxed 1-Neighborhood Knapsack when both the weight and profit of every vertex is 1 1 .

Introduction

We study two new variants of the classical
Knapsack
problem. In our problems, we have a graph structure on the set of items of the
Knapsack
problem. Formally, the input to our problems is a directed graph
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
on the set
\mathcal{V}
of vertices (these are the items in the classical
Knapsack
problem) where every vertex
v\in\mathcal{V}
has a non-negative weight
w_{v}
and a non-negative profit
p_{v}
. We are also given the size
s
of the knapsack and a target profit or demand
d
, both of which are non-negative integers. The computational task is to find a subset
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
whose profit is at least
d
and weight at most
s
. The weight of
\mathcal{S}
is the sum of the weights of the vertices in
\mathcal{S}
. It is the profit of
\mathcal{S}
where our two problems differ. In
Relaxed 1-Neighborhood Knapsack
, the profit of a subset
\mathcal{S}
is the sum of the profits of the vertices
v
in
\mathcal{S}
for which at least one of its out-neighbors (if any exist in
\mathcal{G}
) is included in
\mathcal{S}
. Whereas, in
Relaxed All-Neighborhood Knapsack
, the profit of
\mathcal{S}
is the sum of the profits of the vertices
v
in
\mathcal{S}
for which all its neighbors (if any exist in
\mathcal{G}
) are included in
\mathcal{S}
.

To imagine a concrete potential application of our problems, let us think of a city planner who needs to choose a set of public projects to fund from an extensive list of proposals. Each project has a fixed cost. The natural interdependency among the project proposals makes a project valuable only if one/some/all of the other proposals on which it depends materialize. For example, a public gym is useful only if a residential complex is nearby. A natural objective of the planner is to maximize the total value of the projects funded, subject to some budget constraint.

Motivated by these applications, a line of research work, initiated by Borradaile
[
2
]
, studies the
Knapsack
problem with neighborhood constraints and develops many interesting algorithms and other results. They call these problems
1-Neighborhood Knapsack
and
All-Neighborhood Knapsack
. In this setting, unlike ours, not all subsets of vertices constitute a valid solution. In
1-Neighborhood Knapsack
and
All-Neighborhood Knapsack
, the valid solutions are only those subsets of vertices which include, for every vertex in the subset,
respectively at least one out-neighbor and all out-neighbors of that vertex. The goal in these problems is to check if there exists a solution which meets the target demand and weight limit.

The stringent requirement to pick at least one or all neighbors of any vertex which is already picked can sometime limit the number of possible solutions in a given instance. For example, there are only two valid solutions in any path graph for both
1-Neighborhood Knapsack
and
All-Neighborhood Knapsack
. However, in many real-life situations, we may want to choose some vertex so that another vertex is useful. Indeed, this could be the case in our earlier example of selecting a subset of public projects to fund. We remedy this drawback of the existing model by allowing all possible subsets of vertices to be valid solutions, and use the neighborhood constraint to decide which of the selected vertices contribute to the profit. Notice that this relaxation makes all possible subsets of the vertices valid solutions for our problems, thereby increasing, often exponentially, the number of valid solutions from the existing problems. Consequently, we often need different techniques to find the computational complexity of our problems.

,
Relaxed 1-Neighborhood Knapsack
and
Relaxed All-Neighborhood Knapsack
problems parameterized by the treewidth of the input graph (ignoring the directions of the edges for directed graphs), using dynamic programming over a nice tree decomposition of the input graph.

We next consider the size of a minimum solution as our parameter and develop a color-coding based algorithm. We color the edges randomly and guess a partition of the color set into subsets, each representing a connected component of the solution with at least two vertices in the underlying graph, ignoring the direction of edges. For each color subset, we enumerate all possible tree structures over those colors and perform dynamic programming to compute all undominated feasible (weight-profit) pairs for colorful trees matching that structure. Each DP state maintains a list of such pairs rooted at a specific vertex. We then merge the solutions from all components to obtain the global set of undominated weight-profit pairs. Finally, we output the best pair with weight at most the given budget. This algorithm can be derandomized using standard techniques involving splitters. The randomized version runs in time
\mathcal{O}^{\star}\left(e^{b}\cdot b^{b}\cdot 2^{b^{2}}\cdot{\sf min}\{s^{2},d^{2}\}\right)
, and the deterministic version adds only a polynomial overhead. Importantly, note that the size of any minimum solution is at most twice the demand, as each vertex of the solution that contributes to the profit may require at most one additional neighbor to become profitable. Hence, this algorithm also yields a pseudo-
\mathsf{FPT}
algorithm when parameterized by the demand. We observe that undirected
Relaxed 1-Neighborhood Knapsack
with unit weights and profits on vertices is solvable in linear time using the algorithm proposed by Borradaile et al.
[
2
]
.

1.2
Related Work

The classical
Knapsack
problem on graphs has been studied widely. Knapsack with neighborhood constraints have a rich literature. Borradaile et al. studied the hard versions of the problem in
[
2
]
under the name 1-neighbour knapsack problem and all-neighbour knapsack problem. They introduce the concept of
viable sets
, which are structured subsets of vertices that maintain feasibility while adhering to a predefined
viable family
(e.g., stars, arborescences). For the
1-neighbour knapsack
problem, they develop a polynomial-time approximation scheme (PTAS) for uniform, directed graphs and show that the general, directed case is hard to approximate within a
\frac{1}{\Omega(\log^{1-\varepsilon}n)}
factor. In the undirected case, they prove that partitioning into stars enables an approximation ratio of
\frac{(1-\varepsilon)}{2}\cdot(1-\frac{1}{e^{(1-\varepsilon)}})
. For the
all-neighbours knapsack
problem, they establish that the uniform, undirected case reduces to the classical
Knapsack
, while the uniform, directed case is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard
but admits a PTAS. Their results provide a structured approach to solving constrained knapsack problems via viable sets.

Knapsack with neighborhood constraints were further explored for special kinds of graphs in
[
6
,
7
]
. The authors analyze the computational complexity of the hard version problems across different graph classes- undirected, directed, trees, complement reducible graphs, and minimum series parallel digraphs. For undirected graphs,
All-Neighborhood Knapsack
admits a polynomial time approximation scheme (PTAS), whereas
1-Neighborhood Knapsack
is APX-hard, implying that no PTAS exists unless
\mathsf{P}
=
\mathsf{NP}
. The authors design efficient dynamic programming algorithms for solving special graph families such as directed trees, directed co-graphs, and minimal series-parallel digraphs, achieving pseudo-polynomial runtime.

Carraway et al.
[
3
]
studied the Stochastic Linear Knapsack Problem. In this version, costs are fixed and certain, but the returns are uncertain and follow independent normal distributions. The primary goal of the problem is to maximize the probability that the total return from the selected items meets or exceeds a specified target value. The authors propose a hybrid algorithm that guarantees optimality. This new method combines Dynamic Programming with branch-and-bound techniques, which improves performance compared to traditional Pareto optimization approaches. Mougouei et al.
[
11
]
defined the Binary Knapsack Problem with Dependent Item Values (BKP-DIV) problem, in which the value of an item may depend on the presence or absence of other items in the knapsack. These dependencies can be positive or negative, meaning the value of an item may increase or decrease depending on the selection of other items. This dependency is modeled using fuzzy graphs, which help capture the imprecision in these relationships.

The authors in
[
9
]
studied the Knapsack Problem with Setups expands on the classical Knapsack Problem (KP) by introducing a more complex variant where items are partitioned into distinct groups, each of which incurs a fixed setup cost if at least one item from that group is selected. The authors extend the dynamic programming solution for the classical knapsack problem to handle the added complexity of group setup costs.

In the undirected, unweighted setting of the
Relaxed 1-Neighborhood Knapsack
problem where all vertex weights and profits are
1
and the knapsack size and demand are both equal to

, the structure of any feasible solution exhibits a simple yet crucial property. A subset of vertices forms a feasible solution if it induces a forest in
\mathcal{G}
, where every connected component has size at least

. This structural restriction arises directly from the
Relaxed 1-Neighborhood Knapsack
semantics: a vertex contributes to the profit only if at least one of its neighbors is also selected. A pendant vertex of a component selected alone cannot be profitable, as it has no selected neighbor. Thus, the optimal solution consists only of non-trivial components—trees of size at least
2
—where every vertex has at least one neighbor in the solution and hence contributes to the profit. Notice that this is the exact structure an optimal
1-Neighborhood Knapsack
solution admits.

Observation 25

In a uniform, undirected graph

.

The

Unlike the undirected version, uniform, directed

is strongly
\mathsf{NP}
-complete
. We design an additive 1 approximation algorithm for uniform, directed

Theorem 27
.

The
Relaxed 1-Neighborhood Knapsack
problem on directed graphs with unit weights and unit profits, and a given knapsack of size
k
, admits a linear-time algorithm that returns a solution with profit at least
\mathsf{OPT}-1
, where
\mathsf{OPT}
is the profit of an optimal solution.

Proof.

We are given a directed graph
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
with unit weights and unit profits, and a knapsack size
k
. The goal is to find a subset
\mathcal{S}\subseteq V
of size at most
k
that satisfies the
Relaxed 1-Neighborhood Knapsack
profit conditions and achieves a total profit of at least
\mathsf{OPT}-1
.

We first compute the strongly connected components (SCCs) of
\mathcal{G}
, which can be done in linear time using Kosaraju’s or Tarjan’s algorithm. We sort the components in non-increasing order of their sizes. Let the components be
C_{1},C_{2},\cdots,C_{l}
. We initialize an empty solution set
\mathcal{S}=\emptyset
and a counter

.

Next, we iteratively add vertices from entire components to
\mathcal{S}
as long as the total size remains within the knapsack budget
k
and update

accordingly. Let
C_{j}
be the first component that cannot be fully included due to size constraints. Let

denote the remaining budget.

We now analyze the remaining possibilities to fill the remaining budget

. Construct the subgraph
\mathcal{G}[C_{j}]
and perform a DFS from any vertex
v\in C_{j}
to build a DFS tree. Add the first
t
vertices visited in DFS order to the solution set
\mathcal{S}
.

For each of the first
t-1
vertices in the DFS order, either the DFS tree edge or a back edge guarantees that the vertex has an out-neighbor among the previously selected vertices. Hence, these
t-1
vertices satisfy the
Relaxed 1-Neighborhood Knapsack
profit condition. Only the last selected vertex (i.e., the
t
-th in the DFS order) may not have any out-neighbor among the selected set if all its outgoing edges point to vertices outside the selected subtree. In such a case, this single vertex may not contribute to the profit. Nevertheless, at least
t-1
vertices do contribute, which guarantees a total profit of at least
\mathsf{OPT}-1
.
∎

Appendix

5.1
Conclusion

Neighborhood knapsack problems have been extensively studied in the literature, particularly the hard variants, under the lens of classical complexity theory and approximation. However, their parameterized complexity has received limited attention. To address this gap, we design fixed-parameter tractable (
\mathsf{FPT}
) algorithms for the hard variants of the neighborhood knapsack problem parameterized by treewidth, and establish W-hardness results with respect to natural parameters such as weight, profit, and solution size.
In addition, we introduce and study two novel variants—
Relaxed 1-Neighborhood Knapsack
and
Relaxed All-Neighborhood Knapsack
—that relax the neighborhood constraints. For these soft variants, we explore both classical and parameterized complexity. We develop
\mathsf{FPT}
algorithms using dynamic programming over nice tree decompositions, maintaining tables of undominated (weight, profit) pairs. In soft variants, the dynamic program explicitly distinguishes between selected vertices and those contributing to profit. We further present a color-coding-based
\mathsf{FPT}
algorithm for
Relaxed 1-Neighborhood Knapsack
, parameterized by solution size
b
, where we enumerate colorful tree structures and compute feasible (weight, profit) pairs via dynamic programming. Using splitters, we de-randomize the algorithm. Since the solution size
b
is at most twice the target profit
d
, the algorithm is also
\mathsf{FPT}
when parameterized by
d
. Our results show that even these relaxed variants remain computationally hard on several restricted graph classes. We prove strong
\mathsf{NP}
-completeness and W-hardness results, offering a comprehensive complexity landscape for both hard and soft neighborhood knapsack problems.

D[t^{\prime},\mathcal{S}^{\prime}=\mathcal{S}\setminus\{u\},\mathcal{P}^{\prime}=\mathcal{P}\setminus\{u\}]
with
w+w_{u}\leqslant s
, we add
(w+w_{u},p+p_{u})
and copy to
D[t,\mathcal{S},\mathcal{P}]
.

ii.

if
u
has a out-neighbor
x
in
X_{t}\setminus\mathcal{S}
, then
u\not\in\mathcal{P}
, and
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
, then for every pair
(w,p)
in
D[t^{\prime},\mathcal{S}^{\prime}=\mathcal{S}\setminus\{u\},\mathcal{P}^{\prime}=\mathcal{P}\setminus\{u\}]
with
w+w_{u}\leqslant s
, we add
(w+w_{u},p+0)
and copy to
D[t,\mathcal{S},\mathcal{P}]
.

(c)

Otherwise, we store
D[t,\mathcal{S},\mathcal{P}]=\emptyset

3.

Forget node.
Suppose
t
is a forget vertex node. Then it has only one child
t^{\prime}
, and there is a vertex
u\neq v
such that
X_{t}\cup\{u\}=X_{t^{\prime}}
. Then for all
\mathcal{S}\subseteq X_{t}
and all
\mathcal{P}\subseteq X_{t}
, if
\mathcal{P}
is not a subset of
\mathcal{S}
, we store
D[t,\mathcal{S},\mathcal{P}]=\emptyset
; hence assume otherwise. Then for all
\mathcal{S}\subseteq X_{t}
:

(a)

we copy all feasible undominated pairs stored in
D[t^{\prime},\mathcal{S}\cup\{u\},\mathcal{P}\cup\{u\}]
to
D[t,\mathcal{S},\mathcal{P}]

u\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
and
u\in\mathcal{P}^{\prime}

(b)

or we copy all feasible undominated pairs stored in
D[t^{\prime},\mathcal{S}\cup\{u\},\mathcal{P}]
to
D[t,\mathcal{S},\mathcal{P}]
if
u\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
and
u\not\in\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
,

(c)

or we copy all feasible undominated pairs stored in

to
D[t,\mathcal{S},\mathcal{P}]
otherwise.

4.

Join node.
Suppose
t
is a join node. Then it has two children
t_{1},t_{2}
such that
X_{t}=X_{t_{1}}=X_{t_{2}}
. Then for all
\mathcal{S}\subseteq X_{t}
, let
(w(\mathcal{S}),p(\mathcal{S}))
be the total weight and value of the vertices in
\mathcal{S}
Consider a pair
(w_{1},p_{1})
in
D[t_{1},\mathcal{S}_{1},\mathcal{P}_{1}]
and a pair
(w_{2},p_{2})
in
D[t_{2},\mathcal{S}_{2},\mathcal{P}_{2}]
where
\mathcal{S}_{1}\cup\mathcal{S}_{2}=\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{P}_{1}\cap\mathcal{P}_{2}=\mathcal{P}
. Suppose
w_{1}+w_{2}-w(\mathcal{S}_{1}\cup\mathcal{S}_{2})\leqslant s
, then we add
\left(w_{1}+w_{2}-w(\mathcal{S}_{1}\cup\mathcal{S}_{2}),p_{1}+p_{2}-p(\mathcal{P}_{1}\cap\mathcal{P}_{2})\right)
to
D[t,\mathcal{S},\mathcal{P}]
.

Finally, in the last step of updating
D[t,\mathcal{S},\mathcal{P}]
, we go through the list saved in
D[t,\mathcal{S},\mathcal{P}]
and only keep undominated pairs.

The output of the algorithm is a pair
(w,p)
that is maximum of those stored in
D[r,\{v\},\{v\}]
and
D[r,\{v\},\emptyset]
such that
w\leqslant s
and
p
is the maximum value over all pairs in
D[r,\{v\},\{v\}]
and
D[r,\{v\},\emptyset]
.

Proof of correctness
:
Recall that we are looking for a solution that is a set of vertices
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
that contains the fixed vertex
v
that belongs to all bags of the nice tree decomposition. In each state we maintain a list of feasible and undominated (weight, profit) pairs that correspond to the solution
\hat{\mathcal{S}}
of
\mathcal{G}_{t}
and satisfy
\hat{\mathcal{S}}\cap X_{t}=\mathcal{S}
and the set of profit contributing vertices is
\mathcal{P}
.

We now show that the update rules holds for each
X_{t}
. To prove this formally, we need to consider the cases of what
t
can be:

1.

Leaf node.
Recall that in our modified nice tree decomposition we have added a vertex
v
to all the bags. Suppose a leaf node
t
contains a single vertex
v
,
D[t,\phi,\phi]
stores the pair
\{(0,0)\}
,
D[t,\{v\},\{v\}]
=

if
w_{v}\leqslant s
,
D[t,\{v\},\phi]
stores the pair
\{(w_{v},0)\}
if
w_{v}\leqslant s
, otherwise
\emptyset
. This is true in particular when

, the base case. From now we can assume that for a node

solution in
\mathcal{G}_{t}
. when

.

2.

Introduce node.
When

is an introduce node, there is a child

. We are introducing a vertex
u

\mathcal{G}_{t}

\mathcal{P}

\mathcal{S}

because it violates the definition of
\mathcal{P}
; hence assume otherwise. Let us prove for each case.

(a)

When
u
is not included in
\mathcal{S}
, it is also not in
\mathcal{P}
and the families of sets
\hat{\mathcal{S}}
considered in the definition of
D[t,\mathcal{S},\mathcal{P}]
and of
D[t^{\prime},\mathcal{S},\mathcal{P}]
are equal. Notice that there may be a vertex
w\in X_{t}
that belongs to
\mathcal{P}
such that
N^{+}(w)\cap V_{t^{\prime}}=\emptyset
, i.e.
w
did not have any out-neighbor in the graph
\mathcal{G}_{t^{\prime}}
. However, with the introduction of the vertex

and the edges associated to it, if

i.e.

is the only out-neighbor of

, since

does not belong to
\mathcal{S}

Relaxed All-Neighborhood Knapsack

stores
\emptyset
as it is no longer feasible. Otherwise we copy all pairs of
D[t^{\prime},\mathcal{S}^{\prime}=\mathcal{S},\mathcal{P}^{\prime}=\mathcal{P}]
to
D[t,\mathcal{S},\mathcal{P}]
.

(b)

Now consider the case when
u
is part of
\mathcal{S}
. Let
\hat{\mathcal{S}}
be a feasible solution for
Relaxed All-Neighborhood Knapsack
attained in the definition of
D[t,\mathcal{S},\mathcal{P}]
. Then it follows that
\hat{\mathcal{S}}\setminus\{u\}
is one of the sets considered in the definition of
D[t^{\prime},\mathcal{S}\setminus\{v\},\mathcal{P}\setminus\{v\}]
. Now we must compute entries depending on whether
u
contributes to profit or not. It suffices to just check if
u
has a neighbor in
X_{t}
or not because the nice tree decomposition ensures that the newly introduced vertex
u
can have neighbors only in
X_{t}
. There are two subcases:

i.

Subcase 1:
u\in\mathcal{P}
. When
u
has all out-neighbors
x
selected in
\mathcal{S}
or
u
does not have any out-neighbor in
\mathcal{G}_{t}
, then
u\in\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\cup\{u\}
, and then for every pair
(w,p)
in
D[t^{\prime},\mathcal{S}^{\prime}=\mathcal{S}\setminus\{u\},\mathcal{P}^{\prime}=\mathcal{P}\setminus\{u\}]
with
w+w_{u}\leqslant s
, we add
(w+w_{u},p+p_{u})
and copy to
D[t,\mathcal{S},\mathcal{P}]
.

ii.

Subcase 2:
u\notin\mathcal{P}
. When
u
has at least one out-neighbor in
\mathcal{G}_{t}
that is not selected are selected in
\mathcal{S}
, then
u
does not become profitable and thus does not belong to
\mathcal{P}
. For then for every pair
(w,p)
in

with
w+w_{u}\leqslant s
, we add
(w+w_{u},p+0)

.

Since
\ell(t^{\prime})>\ell(t)
, by induction hypothesis all entries in
D[t^{\prime},\mathcal{S}^{\prime}=\mathcal{S},\mathcal{P}^{\prime}=\mathcal{P}]
,
D[t^{\prime},\mathcal{S}^{\prime}=\mathcal{S},\mathcal{P}^{\prime}=\mathcal{P}\setminus\{u\}]
and
D[t^{\prime},\mathcal{S}^{\prime}=\mathcal{S}\setminus\{u\},\mathcal{P}^{\prime}=\mathcal{P}\setminus\{u\}]

\forall

\mathcal{S}^{\prime},\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\subseteq X_{t^{\prime}}
are already computed. We update pairs in

3.

Forget Node.
When

is a forget node, there is a child

. Let

Running time
: There are
n
choices for the fixed vertex
v
. Upon fixing
v
and adding it to each bag of
(\mathbb{T},\mathcal{X})
we consider the total possible number of states. For every node
t
, we have
2^{|X_{t}|}
choices of
{\mathcal{S}}
and
2^{|X_{t}|}
choices of
{\mathcal{P}}
for each choice of
\mathcal{S}
. For each state, for each
w
, there can be at most one pair with
w
as the first coordinate; similarly, for each
p
, there can be at most one pair with
p
as the second coordinate. Thus, the number of undominated pairs in each
D[t,\mathcal{S},\mathcal{P}]
is at most
{\sf min}\{s,d\}
time. Since the treewidth of the input graph
\mathcal{G}
is at most
tw
, it is possible to construct a data structure in time
\text{tw}^{\mathcal{O}(1)}\cdot n
that allows performing adjacency queries in time
\mathcal{O}(\text{tw})
. For each node
t
, it takes time
\mathcal{O}\left(4^{\text{tw}}\cdot\text{tw}^{\mathcal{O}(1)}\cdot{\sf min}\{s^{2},d^{2}\}\right)
to compute all the values
D[t,\mathcal{S},\mathcal{P}]
and remove all undominated pairs. Since we can assume w.l.o.g that the number of nodes of the given tree decompositions is
\mathcal{O}(\text{tw}\cdot n)
, and there are
n
choices for the vertex
v
, the running time of the algorithm is
\mathcal{O}\left(4^{\text{tw}}\cdot n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}\cdot{\sf min}\{s^{2},d^{2}\}\right)
.
