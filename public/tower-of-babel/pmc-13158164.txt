Deciphering Bax Dynamics: Regulation, Retrotranslocation, and Mitochondrial Interplay in the Commitment to Apoptosis: A Narrative Review
Authors: Mehrin Haque Tanisha, Md. Ayman Siddique, Zubaier Ahmed, Nashrah Mustafa, Fariha Tasneem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe apoptotic pathway mediated by mitochondria depends on the activation of pro‐apoptotic Bcl‐2 proteins Bax and Bak. When they permeabilize the outer mitochondrial membrane (OMM), mitochondrial dysfunction occurs, leading to caspase activation. A recent study proposed that Bax accumulation on mitochondria increases apoptotic susceptibility, with adhesion‐initiated signals regulating Bax. When adhesion signaling is inhibited, Bax translocates to the OMM, undergoes conformational change, and forms complexes that create pores. Although MOMP is well studied, mechanisms regulating Bax shuttling, non‐canonical partners, and mitochondrial dynamics remain poorly understood.AimsThis review aims to provide insights by discussing the mechanisms through which Bax regulates its mitochondrial targeting, the mitochondrial dynamics associated with Bax translocation, and their influence to apoptosis. Understanding these processes could reveal new insights into the decision‐making checkpoints that determine cell death.MethodsA comprehensive literature search was conducted across scientific databases for peer‐reviewed publications up to 2025. Search terms included Bax retrotranslocation, mitochondrial dynamics, Bcl‐2 family interactome, and apoptotic priming.ResultsRecent evidence shows that Bax continuously retrotranslocates from mitochondria to the cytosol under survival conditions, a process driven by anti‐apoptotic Bcl‐2 proteins. When adhesion signaling or other survival cues are inhibited, this cycle is disrupted, leading to Bax accumulation on mitochondria, its oligomerization, and the onset of MOMP. Several mitochondrial proteins, including Drp1, MAVS, OCIAD1, PTPN1, and AKAP1, have also been linked to a mitochondrial interaction network that influences Bax localization and retrotranslocation. Proximity‐labeling approaches such as BioID are further identifying new proteins that may regulate Bax's mitochondrial targeting and apoptotic priming.ConclusionThe mechanisms driving Bax accumulation on mitochondria and its retrotranslocation remain unclear, especially the role of mitochondrial proteins. Evidence suggests mitochondrial interaction networks influence Bax and Bak regulation. Further research is needed to define how these networks integrate with the Bcl‐2 interactome to shape apoptotic decisions.

Introduction

The B‐cell lymphoma 2 (Bcl‐2) protein is identified as a crucial regulator in mitochondrial apoptosis, a process centered around mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization (MOMP). While mitochondria primarily function is to provide cellular energy, they also become the cornerstone of apoptotic cell death under physiological stress. MOMP facilitates the release of cell death factors, including cytochrome c (cyt c), triggering activation of caspases and consequent degradation of cell components [1]. According to the established paradigm of apoptosis, apoptotic stimuli activate pro‐apoptotic proteins while concurrently inhibiting anti‐apoptotic counterparts. Activated pro‐apoptotic proteins, including Bax and Bak, initiate forming pores in the outer mitochondrial membrane (OMM) via oligomerization [2]. This process establishes a delicate balance between both groups of Bcl‐2, influencing the cell fate, organ homeostasis, and overall health of the organism. Disruption of this balance is associated with many diseases, like autoimmune conditions and cancer, underscoring the critical importance of the Bcl‐2 family in maintaining cellular health [3].

A significant portion of our knowledge about Bcl‐2 protein interactions focuses on MOMP, where pro‐apoptotic proteins form pores in the OMM. However, there is also a phenomenon known as incomplete or minority MOMP (iMOMP), where only a fraction of mitochondrial membrane permeabilizes, releases small amounts of cyt c without triggering cell death. Bcl‐2 proteins can be engaged on mitochondria without full caspase activation and commitment to cell death, highlighting complexity in their regulation beyond their typical interactions [4]. Furthermore, investigating the retrotranslocation of Bax and its consequential influence on MOMP represents a crucial area for comprehending these dynamic interactions. Various theories have been posited regarding the Bax translocation process and its role in determining cellular fate [5, 6]. Nevertheless, the mechanisms governing Bax accumulation on mitochondria or the regulation of retrotranslocation by proteins interacting with mitochondria remain unresolved. In addition to established interactions within the Bcl‐2 family, several non‐typical partners have been reported to have an impact on Bax retrotranslocation. These include interactions with elements of the mitochondrial fusion and fission machinery. However, a comprehensive understanding of the entire network of interactions involving Bax from Bcl‐2 family within cells is currently incomplete. To delve deeper into the interactions between Bax and non‐typical partners, it's crucial to comprehensively grasp how proteins of Bcl‐2 family control over apoptosis. Consequently, this review aims to offer a detailed comprehension of the mechanisms by which programmed cell death is regulated via Bcl‐2 family and how these interactions, particularly those relating to mitochondrial dynamics and pro‐apoptotic protein translocation, impact this intricate process.

Apoptotic Landscape for Cell Death

Cell death is not a randomized process; it follows a programmed series of molecular steps where the cell methodically breaks down internally and is subsequently consumed by other cells, leaving no trace. Programmed cell death can occur through various mechanisms like apoptosis and necroptosis, each governed by specific signaling pathways [7]. In many instances, necrosis is likely a consequence of energy depletion, resulting in metabolic impairments and the disruption of ionic gradients across the cell membrane. In contrast, apoptosis represents a genetically controlled mode of cell death, essential for the programmed elimination of cell to uphold normal development and eukaryotic homeostasis. Apoptosis occurs not only in response to cellular damage or external stress but also as a regular part of cell development and morphogenesis [8, 9]. This process also encompasses the formation of membrane blebs, leading to the exposure of phosphatidylserine and the activation of cysteine‐aspartic protease (caspase) proteins. Similar to numerous complex regulatory pathways, apoptosis plays a dual role in the progression of cancer. For instance, while apoptosis triggered by anti‐cancer therapies has the potential to eradicate cancer cells, the sublethal activation of caspase‐3 may contribute to the development of cancer. Consequently, achieving a delicate equilibrium between effective apoptosis and inefficient activation of the apoptotic pathway poses a challenge, as the latter may lead to the progression of cancer [1, 10].

Caspases in Apoptosis

Caspases play a pivotal role in apoptotic machinery, serving as essential components that execute and regulate key steps in the programmed cell death process. These proteases possess a cysteine residue at their active site and exhibit cleavage activity at specific aspartic acid residues, leading to their classification as caspases (c for cysteine and asp for aspartic acid). They are integral to the process of apoptosis and are divided into two groups, including initiator caspases and effector caspases [11]. An initiator caspase possesses a lengthy N‐terminal prodomain comprising 90 amino acids), which holds significance for its functionality. Conversely, an effector caspase contains a prodomain sequence spanning 20–30 residues. Caspases are initially synthesized as inactive precursors within cells and necessitate proteolytic activation during apoptosis. Initiator caspase activates effector caspase by cleaving specific internal aspartic acid residues that divide the large and small subunits [12, 13].

The initiation of apoptosis involves activation of initiator caspases, which exist as inactive monomers in the cytosol. Upon apoptotic signaling, they are recruited to multiprotein platforms where dimerization induces their activation. Within these complexes, caspases undergo reciprocal cleavage, stabilizing their active form. Activated initiator caspases then cleave and activate executioner caspases, triggering a proteolytic cascade (Figure 1). This amplification results in widespread substrate cleavage by executioner caspases, ultimately leading to cell death [11].

Regulatory Pathways of Apoptosis

The mechanism of apoptosis mainly consists of two core pathways (Figure 2) involved in inducing apoptosis: death receptor (DR) mediated pathway or extrinsic pathway and mitochondrial‐mediated pathway or intrinsic pathway [14]. DR‐Regulated Pathway

To initiate this process, apoptotic signal becomes activated when Fas ligand (Fas‐L), tumor necrosis factor (TNF) and TNF related apoptosis inducing ligand (TRAIL) bind to the extracellular domain of DR. Engagement of DRs by specific death ligands (DLs) leads to the formation of a death‐inducing signaling complex (DISC). Subsequently, caspase‐8 undergoes activation wherein the prodomain of caspase‐8 remains associated within the DISC, while the activated form of caspase‐8 separates from the DISC. This activated form initiates a cascade of caspase activation, marking the onset of the execution phase of apoptosis [15].

In the extrinsic pathway, apoptotic signaling is initiated when Fas‐L, TRAIL, or TNF bind to death receptors, triggering receptor clustering and recruitment of adapter proteins to form the death‐inducing signaling complex (DISC). This facilitates activation of caspase‐8, which initiates downstream caspase cascades and cleaves BID to link extrinsic signaling to mitochondrial pathways. In the intrinsic pathway, stress signals promote the activation and mitochondrial translocation of pro‐apoptotic proteins such as BAX and BID, resulting in cytochrome c release. Cytochrome c associates with Apaf‐1 to form the apoptosome, leading to caspase‐9 activation and subsequent activation of executioner caspases. This figure highlights the convergence of extrinsic and intrinsic pathways at MOMP, emphasizing the integrative role of mitochondrial signaling in apoptotic commitment.

Mitochondrial‐Mediated Pathway

The intrinsic pathway of apoptosis primarily involves the mitochondrial‐mediated apoptotic pathway, activated due to different internal and external stresses like exposure to cytotoxic drugs and irradiation [16, 17]. In Figure 2 both intrinsic and extrinsic pathways are depicted, wherein Bax/Bak proteins insert into the mitochondrial membrane, leading to the release of cyt c the cytosol from mitochondrial membrane. Upon release, cyt c eventually associates with procaspase‐9 and apoptotic protease‐activating factor 1 (Apaf‐1) to form apoptosome (known as a complex multi‐protein structure characterized by seven‐spoked ring‐shaped configuration). Caspase‐9 becomes activated due to formation of this complex, initiating a signaling cascade that ultimately triggers the activation of caspase‐3. This step eventually leads to a series of events resulting in cellular death and culminating in apoptosis [18, 19].

Execution phase commences with the execution caspases, subsequently triggering the activation of cytoplasmic endonucleases. Execution caspases are crucial in orchestrating the restructuring of the cytoskeleton and the creation of cytoplasmic protrusions known as blebs, along with the formation of apoptotic bodies. These endonucleases are responsible for breaking down proteases, leading to the degradation of proteins in nucleus and cytoskeleton.

Bcl‐2 Protein Family: Landscape for Apoptosis

The Bcl‐2 group of proteins serves as crucial regulator in mitochondrial apoptosis, functioning either as promoters or suppressors of cell death. The dynamic interaction and equilibrium between the opposing members of this family govern the OMM permeabilization which then leads to the release of activators of the caspase cascade into the cytosol, ultimately resulting in cellular death. Initially discovered in association with the t [14, 17] translocation in chromosome among B‐cell lymphoma patients, Bcl‐2's positioning near the immunoglobulin locus was found to alter the regulation of transcription [20]. However, this alteration did not induce proliferation. The recognition that overexpression of this protein contributes to cancer prompted studies aimed at the identification of regulators involved in apoptosis. In vertebrates, almost 20 members of this protein group have presently been recognized [21]. The intricate interactions among these proteins play a central role in how both normal and cancer cells respond to cytotoxic damage. The complex interplay of these groups of proteins is pivotal in determining the response of both cancerous and normal cells to cytotoxicity. The balance between Bcl‐2 protein members decides cellular fate, homeostasis of organs, and overall organism health [22].

Classification and Structural Features of Bcl‐2 Proteins

Over the past 20 years, progressions have been made in identifying and categorizing Bcl‐2 proteins, based on their domains and functional attributes. Classification within the Bcl‐2 family relies on the presence of conserved regions within their sequence, referred to as Bcl‐2 homology (BH) domains. The grouping of these proteins (illustrated in Figure 3) is determined by their role in the involvement of apoptosis and depending on the presence status of BH‐domains [7, 23].

Structural and functional classification of Bcl‐2 family proteins (image independently created by authors on BioRender).

The Bcl‐2 family of proteins is divided into three groups based on their functional roles in apoptosis and the number of BH domains they possess. Pro‐apoptotic proteins include multidomain proteins (BAX, BAK, and BOK) and BH3‐only proteins (BID, BIM, BAD, BIK, BMF, HRK, NOXA, and PUMA), while anti‐apoptotic proteins include BCL‐2, BCL‐XL, BCL‐W, MCL1, and A1.

Belonging to the anti‐apoptotic group, Bcl‐2, Bcl‐XL, and Bcl‐W consist of four specific BH‐domains (BH1–4) and MCL‐1, A1 consist of three specific domains (BH1–3). They particularly function by inhibiting the function of pro‐apoptotic proteins through binding [21].

Multiple BH‐domains are possessed by effector proteins, including Bax, Bak, and Bok. Bak and Bax, well‐understood proteins, facilitate apoptosis by promoting MOMP, resulting in the release of cyt c. Bax and Bak are directed to the membrane, mainly the mitochondria, through a C‐terminal tail anchor (TA). The role of Bok is less clear, and despite sharing domains with Bax and Bak, this protein appears to be regulated at the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) by the degradation of proteasome [24].

The final category of Bcl‐2 proteins, known as BH3‐only proteins, displays considerable diversity by sharing only a single homologous region with other family members. Members of this group include Bid, Bad, Bim, Bmf, Hrk, Noxa, PUMA, and Bik. Distinct binding specificities arise from variations in BH3‐domain sequences among different BH3‐only proteins [25]. Bcl‐2 Proteins: Key Controllers of Mitochondrial Mediated Apoptotic Pathway

Bcl‐2 plays a vital role as potential regulator of mitochondrial apoptosis. The control of intrinsic apoptosis through the mitochondrial pathways depends on intricate balances interactions among various members of the protein family. Activated form of Bax or Bak on the mitochondrial surface causes an allosteric shift, leading them to form large pores through oligomerization, initiating MOMP. MOMP then prompts the apoptotic proteins from the intermembrane space, facilitating their entry into the cytoplasm [35]. Caspase activation is initiated by released mitochondrial proteins, either through direct interactions like cyt c binding with APAF1 to form the apoptosome, or indirectly via proteins such as SMAC and serine protease OMI (HTR2A) countering the caspase‐inhibitory effects of XIAP [36]. Apoptotic stimuli initiate signaling cascades that upregulate BH3‐only proteins or activate BID, which interact with pro‐survival Bcl‐2 family members. In the absence of sufficient anti‐apoptotic buffering, BAX and/or BAK are activated, leading to oligomerization and MOMP. This results in the release of apoptogenic factors, such as cytochrome c, SMAC, and OMI, which promote apoptosome formation and caspase activation.

Inhibiting downstream caspase activity in cancers undergoing mitochondrial apoptosis because of therapy could boost cGAS–STING signaling. This, in turn, may stimulate the generation and release of type‐I interferons, known as DAMPs, fostering an immune response against cancer [37, 38]. Moreover, recent advancements in apoptosis research involve development of BH3 mimetics, such as venetoclax—a class of small molecules designed to mimic particular pro‐apoptotic proteins. BH3 mimetics are a pivotal class of targeted anticancer drugs that replicate BH3 domains of pro‑apoptotic proteins to inhibit anti‑apoptotic BCL‑2 family members, thereby restoring mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization (MOMP) and apoptosis. The first‑in‑class compound venetoclax (ABT‑199), a selective BCL‑2 inhibitor, has achieved remarkable efficacy in hematologic malignancies such as chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) and acute myeloid leukemia (AML) [39]. However, therapeutic success has been more variable in solid tumors, where mitochondrial priming, metabolic state, and microenvironmental cues alter dependence on BCL‑2, BCL‑xL, and MCL‑1, modulating drug sensitivity.

A key limitation lies in dose‑dependent toxicity. BCL‑xL antagonism induces thrombocytopenia owing to its essential role in platelet survival, while dual BCL‑2/BCL‑xL inhibitors such as navitoclax show restricted tolerability. To mitigate on‑target toxicity, newer compounds exploit the structural heterogeneity of BH3‑binding grooves to achieve more selective, context‑specific activity [40].

Resistance also constrains clinical outcomes. Cancer cells frequently upregulate MCL‑1 or BFL‑1/A1, restoring apoptotic blockade [41]. Additional mechanisms include mutations in the BH3‑binding pocket, altered OMM lipid composition, and metabolic rewiring. Moreover, BAX retrotranslocation and BAK activation thresholds may determine sensitivity to BH3 mimetics, emphasizing that mitochondrial state and apoptotic priming influence drug efficacy [42, 43]. Emerging strategies combine BH3 mimetics with MCL‑1 inhibitors, kinase inhibitors, or metabolic modulators to enhance apoptotic responsiveness while minimizing toxicity. Collectively, these insights highlight both the promise and the complexity of BH3 mimetics, underscoring that optimal therapeutic outcomes depend on cell‑type‑specific apoptotic wiring and mitochondrial context.

Regulation of Bax Retrotranslocation and Impact on Apoptotic Priming

Within the Bcl‐2 family, Bax serves as a principal effector of MOMP and is therefore central to intrinsic apoptotic signaling. Bax activity is tightly regulated through multiple interconnected steps, including its cytosolic‐mitochondrial redistribution, membrane insertion, conformational activation, and retrotranslocation [6, 22]. These processes define a key regulatory checkpoint that determines the extent of apoptotic priming and commitment to cell death.

In non‐apoptotic conditions, Bax predominantly resides in the cytosol and continuously undergoes retrotranslocation from mitochondria, maintaining a dynamic equilibrium between cytosolic and mitochondrial pools [22]. Upon apoptotic stimulation, this equilibrium shifts toward mitochondrial accumulation, where Bax undergoes conformational activation, membrane insertion, and oligomerization to promote MOMP [6]. Despite significant progress, the precise mechanisms governing Bax retrotranslocation and its integration with activation and membrane engagement remain incompletely resolved.

Different Proposed Models and Studies on Bax Translocation

In non‐apoptotic cells, Bax is predominantly cytosolic, whereas apoptotic signaling increases its accumulation at the mitochondrial outer membrane, where activation and oligomerization contribute to MOMP [44, 45, 46, 47, 48].

The regulation of mitochondrial apoptosis by the Bcl‐2 protein family remains a central question in cell biology. Central to this process is the activation of the pro‐apoptotic proteins Bax and Bak, which mediate mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization (MOMP). Despite decades of study, conflicting models persist regarding how Bax is activated, how it is retrotranslocated by anti‐apoptotic factors, and the precise function of BH3‐only proteins in this process.

Historically, two major models have been proposed to explain Bax activation: the direct activation model and the indirect activation model.

Direct Activation Model: This model proposes that specific BH3‐only proteins—most notably Bid and Bim directly bind to and activate Bax and Bak. Experimental evidence supports this, as in vitro reconstitution systems have shown that BH3‐only peptides can induce Bax conformational change and oligomerization within membranes. These activators engage the hydrophobic groove of Bax, resulting in structural rearrangements that expose its transmembrane domain and enable pore formation [49, 50].

Indirect Activation Model: Alternatively, the indirect model argues that BH3‐only proteins primarily neutralize anti‐apoptotic Bcl‐2 members (e.g., Bcl‐xL and Mcl‐1), thereby releasing Bax and Bak to self‐activate [51]. Genetic deletion studies of BH3‐only proteins show that Bax and Bak can still trigger apoptosis once anti‐apoptotic relatives are removed, suggesting a degree of autonomy [52].

Emergence of a Third “Lipid‐Mediated” Model: Recent CRISPR‐based studies propose a membrane‐mediated spontaneous model, wherein Bax activation can be facilitated directly by mitochondrial lipids in the absence of BH3‐only triggers. This model posits that the mitochondrial membrane environment itself induces Bax conformational changes once inhibitory Bcl‐2 proteins are neutralized [53, 54].

While direct activation by BH3‐only proteins remains well supported experimentally, genetic and biochemical evidence indicate that Bax can also auto‐activate under certain conditions. The lipid‐mediated model reconciles these perspectives by proposing that the mitochondrial membrane acts as both scaffold and catalyst for Bax activation, reducing reliance on direct BH3‐mediated stimulation. Thus, direct and spontaneous models may not be mutually exclusive but context‐dependent, varying with cellular stress intensity and mitochondrial membrane composition.

Involvement of BH3‐Only Proteins on Bax Translocation

The BH3‐only paradigm is evolving from a simplified activator/sensitizer binary toward a continuum of functions modulated by binding affinity, localization, and membrane context. Structurally, BH3 motifs display flexibility and variable specificity depending on the interacting Bcl‐2 groove, explaining contradictions across model systems [50].

The initial “rheostat model” proposed that Bax activity is controlled by heterodimerization with anti‐apoptotic proteins [55]. This model was challenged when Bax was found as a soluble cytoplasmic monomer in healthy cells, unlike the mitochondrial localization of Bcl‐2 [56, 57].

Both Bak and Bax use a C‐terminal tail anchor (TA) to localize to the outer mitochondrial membrane [21]. TA proteins associate with the endoplasmic reticulum or mitochondria and insert via a single membrane span after posttranslational targeting [58]. Unlike Bak, Bax is largely cytosolic under normal conditions, with its TA folded into a hydrophobic groove [57, 59]. Bax regulation involves interactions with prosurvival Bcl‐2 proteins [60], though how these exert control without direct cytoplasmic contact remains unclear. BH3‐only proteins may connect cytosolic Bax to mitochondrial prosurvival proteins, facilitating activation by Bim, Puma, or tBid [61, 62, 63, 64].

In non‐apoptotic cells, “activator” BH3‐only proteins are sequestered by prosurvival Bcl‐2 members; upon apoptosis induction, they are released—potentially through competitive binding—and activate Bax [62]. Bax contains two binding sites: the rear activation site (α1–α6 helices) identified by NMR of stapled Bim‐BH3 and Bax [65, 66] and the hydrophobic groove seen in Bid‐BH3–Bax crystal structures [67]. BH3‐only binding at the rear site displaces the α1–α2 loop, allowing Bax transmembrane domain movement and membrane insertion. Engagement of the hydrophobic groove triggers N‐terminal rearrangement and BH3 exposure, promoting dimerization. Hence, activator BH3‐only proteins enable stepwise Bax activation: membrane insertion followed by BH3 exposure [67, 68].

BH3‐only proteins can also activate Bak by influencing helix 6 [69], while antibodies identified the α1–α2 helix as another activation site in Bak and mitochondrial Bax [70]. Since BH3‐only proteins do not bind directly to this loop, other activators may exploit it to induce MOMP. Activation of Bax/Bak requires α9 helix membrane insertion and BH3‐domain exposure for oligomerization and pore formation.

Bim and tBid synergize in Bax‐mediated permeabilization [48, 71]. Knockout studies show some apoptotic pathways remain Bim/tBid‐independent [72], while triple knockout of Bid, Bim, and Puma reduces apoptosis, highlighting their cooperative control over MOMP. Prevailing models suggest BH3 binding to Bax triggers conformational changes, driving mitochondrial targeting and MOMP. Yet, Bax targeting is more complex—its interaction with BH3‐only proteins likely occurs once both are membrane‐associated [73], and direct binding in cells is often undetectable (Walensky et al., 2006). Hence, Bax activation through BH3‐dependent mechanisms may require mitochondrial localization first. Moreover, Bax retrotranslocation to the cytosol involves Bcl‐XL [74] and can reverse upon cell reattachment to the extracellular matrix [75]. These findings indicate that mitochondrial targeting of Bax is not the final activation step mediated by BH3‐only proteins.

Dynamic Equilibrium of Bax Between Mitochondria and Cytosol

Schellenberg et al. (2013) [6] proposed that Bax localization is regulated by a dynamic equilibrium between mitochondrial and cytosolic pools. This model interprets structural and biochemical data as reflecting conformational states within a continuous exchange rather than isolated alternatives. When survival signaling is inhibited, the rate of Bax dissociation from mitochondria decreases, causing rapid accumulation on the outer mitochondrial membrane (OMM) and heightened apoptotic priming (illustrated in Figure 5). Conversely, faster dissociation lowers priming. Their findings indicate that Bax association and retrotranslocation occur without direct activation by BH3‐only proteins.

Using PUMA⁻/⁻ Bim⁻/⁻ double knockout (DKO) and Bak⁻/⁻ Bax⁻/⁻ DKO MEFs expressing tBid, the authors found no difference in Bax dynamics compared with wild‐type cells, implicating that BH3‐only proteins are nonessential for Bax membrane association or retrotranslocation [6]. Likewise, treatment with ABT‑737, a BH3 mimetic, did not alter Bax dissociation, reinforcing the idea that Bax cycling between cytosol and mitochondria is primarily autonomous from BH3‐only control. These observations align with the “embedded together” model, which suggests that OMM association precedes Bax activation by BH3‐only proteins [39].

Cells can exist in different states of apoptotic priming depending on the balance between pro‐ and anti‐apoptotic Bcl‐2 family proteins. Normal cells can adjust priming in response to stress signals without necessarily undergoing MOMP, whereas cancer cells often exist in a primed state that renders them more sensitive to apoptotic stimuli and BH3 mimetics. (2013) [6] also demonstrated that attenuated kinase signaling slows Bax retrotranslocation, promoting mitochondrial accumulation and enhanced apoptotic priming, whereas signaling restoration reverses this effect. Mutation BaxS184V, substituting serine with hydrophobic valine, increased Bax retention on mitochondria, implying that tail‐anchor (TA) hydrophobicity stabilizes Bax in the OMM. This contrasts with Bak, whose more hydrophobic TA prevents retrotranslocation. The continuous shuttling of Bax appears influenced by mitochondrial proteins that modulate this equilibrium [76, 77].

Critically, the concept reframes Bax activation not as a binary event but as part of a reversible, signal‑responsive cycle linking membrane insertion, activation, and retrotranslocation. While this model integrates biochemical and structural evidence effectively, the molecular determinants governing TA stability and the exact regulatory factors controlling retrotranslocation remain unresolved, underscoring the need for mechanistic clarification through structural and kinetic studies.

Involvement of Mitochondrial Dynamics in the Regulation of Apoptosis

Mitochondrial dynamics—comprising fission, fusion, and membrane potential maintenance play a central role in determining a cell's susceptibility to apoptosis. Dysregulated mitochondria not only contribute to cancer progression but also modulate apoptotic sensitivity by influencing the balance between mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization (MOMP) and the incomplete or “minority MOMP” (iMOMP) phenomenon [78]. iMOMP, driven by sublethal stress, allows partial caspase activation, promoting DNA damage and inflammatory signaling without full cell death, thereby facilitating oncogenic transformation [79, 80].

Mechanistic Link Between Mitochondrial Dynamics and Bax Translocation

Mitochondrial dynamics comprising fission, fusion, and regulation of inner membrane potential critically influence how cells integrate metabolic stress and apoptotic signaling. While broad mitochondrial dysfunction (such as depolarization or oxidative imbalance) contributes to cellular pathology, apoptosis proceeds through a tightly controlled intrinsic pathway orchestrated by Bcl‑2 family proteins. These processes converge at the level of mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization (MOMP), the “point of no return” for intrinsic apoptosis [81]. Under moderate stress, cells may instead undergo incomplete MOMP (iMOMP), triggering limited caspase activation and sublethal DNA damage that fosters genomic instability and tumorigenic adaptation [82, 83].

Mechanistically, mitochondrial morphology modulates Bax behavior without necessarily initiating apoptosis. In healthy cells, Bax continuously shuttles between mitochondria and the cytosol through a retrotranslocation cycle mediated by prosurvival proteins such as Bcl‑xL [5, 6]. When mitochondrial membrane potential declines or fragmentation increases, retrotranslocation slows, leading to Bax accumulation on the outer mitochondrial membrane (OMM) and enhanced apoptotic priming [4]. Rehm et al. (2003) [42] further demonstrated that Bax retrotranslocation strengthens Bcl‑xL's anti‑apoptotic efficacy, enabling rapid switch‑like transitions between MOMP‑competent and MOMP‑resistant states. Even modest ( ~ 15%) decreases in membrane potential can significantly increase Bax mitochondrial residency, although these thresholds remain cell‑type specific [4].

Additionally, mitochondrial membrane curvature influences Bcl‑2 protein interactions. Santuli (2025) [84] revealed that curvature dictates how BCL‑w dimers reorganize Bax–Bcl‑2 networks, rewiring apoptotic decision‑making independently of global depolarization. Wolf et al. (2022) [85] demonstrated via cryo‑electron microscopy that Bax and Bak can assemble into variable‑sized lipidic pores on the OMM, suggesting that dynamic protein–lipid architectures govern MOMP efficiency and the diversity of apoptotic outcomes.

Pharmacologically, a study identified a small‑molecule allosteric inhibitor that stabilizes Bax in an inactive conformation, thereby protecting against doxorubicin‑induced cardiomyopathy [43]. This study notably distinguishes specific inhibition of Bax‑driven apoptosis from nonspecific mitochondrial dysfunction, showing how selective control of apoptotic signaling can prevent organellar damage without impairing respiration.

Advances in quantitative live‑cell imaging now confirm that apoptotic priming reflects the equilibrium dynamics of Bcl‑2 proteins between cytosol and mitochondria, rather than fixed localization [4, 6]. Integrating these findings clarifies that mitochondrial dynamics shape apoptotic sensitivity mainly by fine‑tuning Bax/Bcl‑2 equilibrium and membrane topology, not by instigating general mitochondrial collapse.

In summary, mitochondria function not only as victims of damage during apoptosis but as dynamic regulatory platforms. The combined effects of membrane curvature, retrotranslocation kinetics, and potential maintenance establish a gradient of apoptotic thresholds that determine whether a cell withstands stress, maintains readiness, or irreversibly commits to death.

Roles of Different Mitochondrial Proteins in Apoptosis

Recent studies indicate the active involvement of proteins engaged in mitochondrial fission and fusion in apoptosis induction. However, these observations do not uniformly demonstrate direct regulation of Bax. In several cases, the available data support association with apoptotic susceptibility or mitochondrial remodeling rather than a defined mechanistic interaction with Bax activation, insertion, oligomerization, or retrotranslocation. Mitochondria are dynamic organelles that undergo continuous fusion and fission, and these processes involve proteins such as Dynamin‐related protein 1 (Drp1), Mitofusins 1 and 2 (Mfn1/2), and Optic Atrophy 1 (Opa1). Accordingly, throughout this section we distinguish between proteins for which a direct role in Bax‐associated events has been proposed and proteins whose effects may be indirect or context‐dependent. The interplay of mitochondrial dynamics with cell death has been evidenced, with multiple studies revealing an association between Bcl‐2 family proteins (including Bax, Bak, Bcl‐2, and Bcl‐XL) and proteins (Mfn1, Mfn2, and Drp1) participating in mitochondrial morphogenesis [86, 87, 88].

The process of mitochondrial fission involves Drp1, a key component of the fission machinery [89]. Expression of the phosphomimetic mutant Drp1 S656D results in mitochondrial elongation and increased resistance to apoptosis induced by staurosporine and etoposide, whereas the S656A mutant promotes fragmentation and increases sensitivity to apoptotic stimuli [90]. Drp1 sumoylation also increases during apoptosis and coincides with increased mitochondrial fission [91]. A recent study further reported co‐localization of Bax and Drp1 at mitochondria during apoptosis [92]. These findings support a close association between Drp1‐dependent remodeling and Bax‐positive apoptotic mitochondria; however, they do not yet establish that Drp1 directly activates Bax or independently controls its retrotranslocation.

Knocking down either Mfn1 or Mfn2 leads to mitochondrial fragmentation and increased susceptibility to apoptotic triggers [93]. In addition, overexpression of mitofusins delays Bax activation, cytochrome c release, and apoptotic cell death [94]. Within viable cells, Bax and Bak promote Mfn2 activity and oligomerization, whereas during apoptosis Bak dissociates from Mfn2 and associates with Mfn1 [88, 95]. Phosphorylation of Mfn1 has also been reported to promote Bak oligomerization in response to apoptotic cues [96]. These findings indicate functional crosstalk between mitochondrial fusion machinery and Bcl‐2 family proteins, but for Bax in particular the evidence is stronger for contextual association than for a fully resolved mechanistic pathway.

Loss of Opa1 triggers spontaneous apoptosis, establishing a critical connection between mitochondrial morphogenesis and apoptosis [97]. Study indicated overexpression of Bcl‐2 inhibits cell death induced by Opa1 RNAi, positioning mitochondrial fission's role in cell death upstream of MOMP. Opa1 overexpression also hinders mitochondrial fission, protecting cells from apoptotic death via intrinsic pathway [98]. However, OPA1 mutants lacking GTPase and C‐terminal GTPase effector domains (GED) fail to protect against cell death, critical for OPA1's mitochondrial fusion activity [99], suggesting a potential linkage between apoptosis protection and mitochondrial fusion.

Voltage‐dependent anion channel 2 (VDAC2) provides one of the stronger examples of a mitochondrial protein with a more direct role in Bax/Bak regulation [100, 101, 102]. VDAC2 has been reported to act as a platform for Bax at the OMM and to impede its retrotranslocation to the cytosol [74, 103]. Genetic ablation of VDAC2 markedly impairs mitochondrial insertion of Bak and Bax [104]. Thus, compared with many mitochondrial dynamics proteins, VDAC2 is supported by stronger mechanistic evidence linking it to Bax/Bak membrane engagement. This contrasts with many mitochondrial dynamics proteins, for which direct mechanistic links to Bax remain less clearly defined.

Mitochondrial carrier homolog 2 (Mtch2), additionally facilitates the binding tBid to the OMM by inducing a conformational change in tBid. This alteration results in a more profound membrane insertion, crucial for enhancing its Bax‐activating capability [105, 106]. Hexokinases (HKs) localized within the mitochondria are also recognized for their interference with Bcl‐2 family members, thereby regulating Bax‐mediated mitochondrial membrane damage. Recent investigations reveal that elevated glucose levels may instigate apoptosis by downregulating Hexokinase 2 (HK2), causing reduced interactions between VDACs and HK2. This downregulation leads to an increased association between VDACs and Bax, subsequently augmenting the release of cyt c. These findings imply a potential shared binding site on VDACs at the MOM for both Bax and HKs [107, 108].

Many studies investigating the relationship between mitochondrial dynamics and apoptosis face a central interpretive challenge: whether fission and fusion machinery directly modulate Bax/Bak function, or whether they alter mitochondrial physiology in ways that secondarily influence apoptotic susceptibility. Mitochondrial fragmentation, depolarization, and metabolic alterations can independently affect the cellular threshold for MOMP without requiring direct regulation of Bax. This distinction is particularly important because changes in membrane potential, ATP production, and reactive oxygen species can all influence apoptotic priming [4]. Accordingly, correlations between mitochondrial dynamics and Bax localization or activation should not be interpreted as evidence of direct mechanistic control without further validation. Some reported interactions may therefore reflect direct roles in apoptosis, whereas others may arise from broader non‐apoptotic functions of mitochondrial remodeling and cellular metabolism. A more complete understanding will require experimental approaches that distinguish direct protein‐level regulation of Bax from secondary effects driven by changes in mitochondrial bioenergetic state.

The current literature provides a spectrum of evidence, ranging from correlation with apoptotic sensitivity and mitochondrial morphology to more direct mechanistic involvement in Bax/Bak membrane regulation [104]. Distinguishing these levels of evidence is essential to avoid overstating the current state of the field.

Looking to the Future Prospect

Growing evidence indicates that the mitochondrial context fundamentally shapes apoptotic regulation, influencing the localization and activation of Bax and Bak. Beyond canonical Bcl‑2 family interactions, multiple mitochondrial proteins—including Drp1, Mfn1/2, VDACs, hexokinases, and Mtch2 have been implicated in modulating apoptotic sensitivity and mitochondrial outer membrane remodeling. While these findings are intriguing, they remain largely associative and do not yet establish a unified mitochondrial network governing Bax/Bak regulation.

Recent studies using systems‑level and proximity labeling approaches have proposed a mitochondrial interactome linking pro‑ and anti‑apoptotic Bcl‑2 proteins with additional mitochondrial factors [109]. For example, Li et al. (2025) [110] reported that reduced Bax retrotranslocation correlates with enrichment of mitochondrial proteins, such as Drp1, MAVS, OCIAD1, and YKT6. These datasets offer valuable hypotheses but require mechanistic validation to discriminate direct Bax regulators from proteins indirectly affected by mitochondrial reorganization or metabolic stress.

Methodologies such as BioID‑based proximity labeling hold particular promise for mapping dynamic Bax interactions in living cells. Because Bax continually redistributes between cytosol and mitochondria during apoptotic priming, it represents an informative molecular “bait.” The Bax S184V substitution, which enhances mitochondrial retention by reducing retrotranslocation, provides a useful experimental model for identifying proteins that accumulate under conditions of elevated mitochondrial Bax. Candidate interactors, including MAVS, Drp1, OCIAD1, YKT6, PTPN1, and AKAP1 have emerged from such studies [110]. Nonetheless, proximity enrichment alone cannot confirm functional relevance; future work must integrate proximity mapping with biochemical validation, structural characterization, and live‑cell functional assays to define the mechanistic basis of Bax regulation and its relationship to mitochondrial organization.

Conclusion

The Bcl‑2 family remains central to mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization (MOMP), with Bax serving as a dynamic effector whose cytosolic–mitochondrial cycling determines apoptotic priming. Bax localization is maintained through continuous retrotranslocation, modulated by survival signaling pathways that adjust its mitochondrial accumulation and readiness for activation [6, 74].

This review has synthesized current insights into how mitochondrial dynamics intersect with Bax behavior. Evidence supports functional coupling between mitochondrial state and Bax translocation, although mechanistic clarity is still emerging. Among known mitochondrial factors, VDAC2 shows the strongest evidence for direct participation in Bax/Bak membrane engagement, whereas the roles of fission and fusion proteins such as Drp1 and Mfn1/2 appear context dependent and remain to be mechanistically confirmed.

Systems‑level analyses, including those by Li et al. (2025) [110], have identified mitochondrial protein networks correlated with curtailed Bax retrotranslocation, yet these findings should be regarded as hypothesis‑generating rather than proof of direct regulation. Distinguishing causal regulators from secondary responders to mitochondrial remodeling or bioenergetic stress is now a key challenge.

Looking forward, integrative studies combining structural, biochemical, and live‑cell imaging approaches are essential to reconcile competing models of Bax activation and retrotranslocation. Such efforts will clarify how mitochondrial architecture and metabolic state together determine whether a cell remains resilient, poised, or committed to apoptosis.
