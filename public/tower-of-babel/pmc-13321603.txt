Reduction of primary tumor growth and metastasis in models of triple-negative breast cancer following tilorone administration via type I interferon signaling
Authors: Alastair A E Saunders, Kellie A Mouchemore, Laura Vojtech, Lauren S James, Thomas B Chadwick, Lap Hing Chi, Chris Karagiannis, Caroline Bell, Rachel E Thomson, Belinda S Parker, Robin L Anderson, Paul Gregorevic
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPeople with triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) typically have poorer survival than those with other breast cancer subtypes, with fewer treatment options available. Type I interferon (IFN) signaling has been reported as an important regulator of metastasis and therapeutic response in TNBC, but the off-target toxicity of IFN therapies has limited progression to clinical use. Augmenting Bone Morphogenetic Protein (BMP) signaling has also been associated with anti-metastatic effects, but BMP therapies are not clinically available presently. Tilorone is an anti-viral drug that induces IFN signaling, which has recently gained added attention as an enhancer of BMP signaling. As an agent capable of enhancing two mechanisms of interest, we therefore aimed to test the therapeutic effects of tilorone in preclinical models of metastatic breast cancer.MethodsThe effect of tilorone on 4T1.2, EMT6.5, TBCP-1 and MDA-MB-231-HM cells was studied using multiple in vitro assays. We also compared metastatic burden by qPCR in mice bearing orthotopic 4T1.2 mammary tumors – a syngeneic and metastatic model of TNBC – with and without tilorone by unpaired t-tests. The effect of tilorone in combination with 4 mg/kg doxorubicin was tested by a 2-way ANOVA.ResultsIn 4T1.2 cells, tilorone increased expression of IFN stimulated genes (ISGs). Tilorone also reduced lung metastatic burden. Improvements in survival associated with tilorone administration were blunted in mice that lacked the IFNα/β receptor (IFNAR1). The anti-metastatic impact of tilorone was further examined in combination with doxorubicin. In mice bearing 4T1.2 tumors, and receiving doxorubicin, co-treatment with tilorone increased natural killer (NK) cell maturation in the primary tumor, increased CD4+ & CD8+ T cell activation and NK cell maturation in the pre-metastatic lung, and enhanced metastasis-free survival. Analysis of patient data identified high ISG gene expression in tumors correlated with increased overall survival in TNBC but not in patients with other breast cancer subtypes. Additionally, most ISGs were more highly expressed in the tumors of TNBC patients who responded to chemotherapy, compared to non-responders.ConclusionsCollectively, the data support the utility of tilorone and other inducers of IFN signaling to enhance the effects of chemotherapeutic drugs for treating metastatic breast cancers.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13058-026-02296-7.Keywords: Interferon, IFNAR, Metastasis, Tilorone, Doxorubicin, Chemotherapy, Breast cancer, Triple negative breast cancer, 4T1.2, Immune microenvironment

Background

Breast cancer is the most common cancer diagnosed in females, with approximately 1 in 7 females (and 1 in 500 males) diagnosed in their lifetime [1]. Whilst long-term survival for breast cancer patients has increased greatly due to increased screening [2] and improved neoadjuvant therapies such as immune checkpoint inhibitors [3] and antibody-drug conjugates [4], many patients still develop metastatic disease, for which there is no effective curative therapy. Tumors that do not express heightened levels of estrogen receptors (ER) or progesterone receptors (PR), or the human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) are known as triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), characterized by an enhanced rapid metastasis rate compared with other subtypes [5–7]. Without ER, PR and HER2 present, endocrine and anti-Her2 therapies are ineffective, highlighting the need for novel therapies for this cancer subtype.

Interferons (IFN) were identified in the 1950s as proteins that are activated during viral infections. Canonical IFN signaling occurs through the JAK-STAT pathway, which stimulates genes involved in antigen presentation (e.g. TAP1 and PSMB9), antiviral activity (e.g. OAS1, MX1), members of the signaling pathway such as interferon regulator factors (IRFs), and signal transducer and activator of transcriptions (STATs) [8], as well as negative regulators of the pathway such as suppressors of cytokine signaling 1 (SOCS1) [9]. IFNs have been identified as a potential target for breast cancer therapy, being implicated in both suppression of tumor-intrinsic cell proliferation pathways [10, 11] and in facilitating an anti-tumor immune response [12]. For instance, increased activation of type I IFNs, such as IFNα and IFNβ, leads to the upregulation of processing and presentation of tumor-associated antigens on the surface of cancer cells, increasing immunogenicity in ovarian and breast cancers [13]. Type I IFN signaling also stimulates cancer cells to secrete CXCL10, resulting in the recruitment of T cells to the tumor microenvironment [14]. In the setting of metastasis, the importance of IFN signaling in breast cancer was demonstrated in mice that lacked the interferon-α/β receptor (IFNAR), which led to an increase in bone metastases compared to wildtype mice, with a key mechanism being an impairment in natural killer (NK) cell cytotoxicity [15]. However, a barrier to successful IFN therapies is their dose-limiting side effects [11]. Agents that can enhance IFN signaling could offer an attractive alternative strategy to IFN-based agents, and the means to combine with other interventions targeting other aspects of cancer metastasis biology.

Bone Morphogenetic Proteins (BMPs) have also been reported to influence metastatic cancer progression [16]. Canonical BMP signaling occurs through engagement of dimeric BMP ligands with heteromeric BMP receptors to activate Smad1/5/8 signal transduction proteins that influence the transcription of genes associated with a diverse range of processes including proliferation, differentiation, proteostasis, metabolic regulation, immune cell function, and fibrosis. In preclinical models of breast cancer metastasis, BMP4 reduced metastasis through suppression of myeloid-derived suppressor cells [17], via promotion of cancer cell anoikis [18], and by inhibition of cholesterol biosynthesis [19]. BMP7 has also been shown to reduce metastasis through suppression of transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) signaling [20]. The short half-life of exogenously administered recombinant BMPs presently hinders their use as viable therapeutics for patients [21]. However, agents than can enhance BMP signaling may provide an attractive alternative strategy through which to exert anti-metastatic effects.

Tilorone is a broad-spectrum antiviral, which has been demonstrated to activate IFN signaling [22, 23]. Recently, tilorone has been studied in the context of Ebola and COVID-19, where it has reduced viral replication of COVID-19 in vitro [24] and improved survival following Ebola exposure in vivo [25]. In humans, tilorone has been reported to increase IFN expression [26–28], although one report showed no IFN induction following oral administration, or via eye drops [29], raising questions about mechanism of action [30]. In a preclinical compound screen, tilorone was recently identified as an inducer of BMP signaling and subsequently observed to potentiate expression of BMP2 and BMP7 (but not BMP4) ligands in lung epithelial cells [31]. Tilorone has since been reported to increase downstream BMP signaling in muscles in a murine model of cancer cachexia [32], in C2C12 immortalized mouse myoblasts [33] and in liver steatosis [34]. These results suggest that tilorone could be used as an agent to induce BMP ligand signaling in the context of metastatic breast cancer, including in tissues that are frequently affected by aggressive metastases, such as the lung and liver, with consequences for survival. As tilorone has been reported as well tolerated in children and adults, can be produced at a very low cost, and is water soluble [30], its attributes present an attractive prospect by which to potentially enhance both IFN and BMP signaling, to counter processes that promote breast cancer metastasis.

Tilorone has been studied in preclinical models of breast cancer, where it reduced proliferation of MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 cells in vitro, and reduced primary tumor growth rate in vivo with a concomitant increase in expression of the tumor suppressor p53 [35]. The gene encoding for p53 has been characterized as an ISG that induces apoptosis in cancer cells [36]. Despite these promising pre-clinical results, a study evaluating tilorone in patients with advanced breast cancer showed no improvement of survival [37]. There are some notable shortcomings of this trial, for instance, all patients had histologically confirmed metastatic breast cancer that no longer responded to conventional therapy and many patients withdrew from the trial due to toxicity issues. Additionally, patients were not stratified by breast cancer subtype. Given that 88% of patients in the tilorone cohort had previously received hormone therapy, it is likely that this patient population had mainly luminal breast cancers. Type I IFN signaling has differing outcomes in different subtypes, with type I IFN related genes correlating with improved survival in TNBC and HER2 enriched breast cancer, but with worsened survival in ER positive cancers [38]. Furthermore, TNBC is typically more inflammatory than other subtypes, as indicated by high tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) [39]. In light of the need for effective strategies to treat TNBC, and the potential attractiveness of tilorone as an agent that could exert desirable anti-metastatic effects upon both IFN and BMP signaling, we examined whether tilorone would be effective at reducing spontaneous metastasis in a preclinical model of TNBC, when administered alone, and with a frontline chemotherapy.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and in vitro assays

The highly metastatic 4T1.2 mouse mammary cancer cells were derived from the 4T1 Balb/c mouse mammary cancer line [40], which was originally recovered by Dr. F. Miller from a spontaneous mammary tumor in a Balb/c mouse [41]. 4T1.2 cells were transfected with lentiviral constructs expressing mCherry as described previously [42]. Cells from 4T1.2 primary tumor (4T1.2-PT) and bone metastases (4T1.2-BM) cell lines were recovered from lesions isolated from Balb/c mice. 4T1.2 cells were cultured in α-minimum essential medium Eagle (α-MEM; Thermo Fisher Scientific, #12561056), and supplemented with 5% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Thermo Fisher Scientific, #SH30084.03) and penicillin (100 µg/ml)/ streptomycin (100 µg/ml) (P/S; Thermo Fisher Scientific, #15140-122). The EMT6.5 cell line is a derivative of the EMT6 murine mammary carcinoma line [43], and was maintained in Dulbecco’s modified eagle medium (DMEM; Thermo Fisher Scientific, #11965-092) containing HEPES (20 mM) supplemented with 10% FBS and P/S. MDA-MB-231-HM cells are a highly metastatic derivative of the MDA-MB-231 cell line [44], and were maintained in DMEM with 10% FBS + P/S. TBCP-1 cells are characterized as HER2-positive and were derived from a spontaneous Balb/c mammary tumor [45], and were maintained in DMEM with 10% FBS + P/S. All cells were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Cells were passaged at least twice prior to experimentation.

For assessment of gene expression, cells were seeded in 6-well plates in a total volume of 2 ml and treatment commenced when they reached ~ 80% confluence. Culture medium was supplemented with either 10 ng/ml to 1 µg/ml of tilorone (Sigma, #220957) or an equal volume of phosphate buffered saline (PBS; Thermo Fisher Scientific, #20012-050) for 4 h before cells were harvested for qRT-PCR analysis.

Live cell proliferation was measured through continuous measurements using the xCELLigence Real Time Cell Analyzer Dual Purpose (RTCA DP; ACEA Biosciences, California, USA) operated following the manufacturer’s instruction. Bioimpedance of cells was measured by the xCELLigence RTCA DP every 20 min as a measure of cell proliferation. Cells were seeded (1000 for 4T1.2 & EMT6.5 and 5000 for TBCP-1 & 231-HM), and media was replaced ~ 6 h later with media containing tilorone and or PBS and replenished daily.

For assessment of migration, cells were seeded at 75,000 cells per well in a 24-well plate. One day prior to experimentation, the medium was replaced with fresh medium containing 1 µg/ml of tilorone or an equal volume of PBS. At 90–100% confluence, the medium was replaced with non-supplemented DMEM containing 10 µg/ml Mitomycin C (Sigma, #10107409001) for 2 h to arrest cell proliferation. Cells were washed with PBS, and a scratch was made with a P200 pipette tip. Cells were washed again with PBS, and medium supplemented with fresh medium containing either tilorone or PBS which was re-introduced to the wells. Cells were then imaged with an EVOS cell imaging system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and migration was measured using ImageJ.

For colony formation assays, one day prior to experimentation, the medium was replaced with fresh medium supplemented with either 1 µg/ml of tilorone and/or 10 nM doxorubicin (Sigma, #D1515) or respective vehicles (PBS and distilled water). Cells (150 per well) were seeded in a 6-well plate with compounds added at the time of seeding. After 7 days for 4T1.2, EMT6.5 and TBCP-1, and 15 days for 231-HM, cells were washed with PBS, and fixed in 0.1% crystal violet (Hurst Scientific, #CV1) and 50% methanol, followed by 3x washes in PBS. The number of colonies (> 50 cells) was scored for each well. For assessment of anoikis, 3000 cells per ml were mildly agitated in 15 ml conical centrifuge tubes (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #339650) in a water bath for 6 h, before being subjected to colony formation assay conditions as described above.

Animal experiments

All experiments were conducted in accordance with the relevant code of practice for the care and use of animals for scientific purposes (National Health and Medical Research Council of Australia) and were approved by the University of Melbourne Animal Ethics Committee (ethics #24412) or by the Animal Ethics Committee at the Peter MacCallum Cancer Centre (ethics #2023–29). Female Balb/c mice were obtained from the Walter and Eliza Hall Institute, Victoria, Australia, or Australian Bio Resources (ABR), New South Wales, Australia. Balb/c-IFNAR−/− mice were bred at the Peter MacCallum Cancer Centre. Animals were housed in a temperature-regulated facility with food and water available ad libitum. Mice were inoculated with 100,000 4T1.2 mouse mammary cancer cells in 15 µL PBS, to establish orthotopic tumors in the inguinal mammary gland, as described previously [46]. Tumor volume was monitored by caliper measurements and calculated as ½(length × width2), and resected at 300–400mm3, with a maximal size of 500mm3 permitted by the ethics committee, which was not exceeded during experiments conducted in this study. Tilorone was administered at 50 mg/kg every 3 days via intraperitoneal injection, starting at either tumor palpation or tumor resection as indicated in the figure legends. For doxorubicin experiments, mice were administered 4 mg/kg doxorubicin for a total of 3 doses via intravenous (IV) injection every 5 days. Mice were humanely euthanized at set timepoints for metastatic burden analysis (as described in figure legends), or earlier if they developed signs of ill-health due to metastatic disease. For survival analysis, mice were euthanized upon signs of metastatic burden/distress, including alterations in respiration, limb mobility, loss of body condition, or > 15% body weight loss.

Assessment of metastatic burden

Lungs were homogenized mechanically in lysis buffer (0.1 mg/mL proteinase K, 100 mM NaCl, 25 mM EDTA, 5% SDS and 10 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8) and incubated at 55 °C overnight. Excess protein was precipitated by incubation with 0.7 volumes of saturated NaCl solution (> 5 M) on ice for 30 min. The genomic DNA (gDNA) was then extracted with phenol/chloroform/isoamyl alcohol (25:24:1, Thermo Fisher Scientific, #15593049) supplemented with 0.1% 8-hydroxyquinoline. gDNA was precipitated from the separated aqueous phase with 100% ice-cold ethanol and washed with 80% ethanol before suspension in DNAse free water (Life Technologies Australia, #AM9916). Metastatic burden was assessed by qPCR quantitation of tumor cell-specific mCherry DNA relative to Vimentin DNA expression. Primers and probes used in this assay are specified in Supplemental Table 1. Metastatic burden is calculated as 2 ^ -(CTmCherry – CTVimentin) × 10,000. Imaging of mCherry+ metastatic lesions was achieved with fluorescence imaging using the CRi Maestro 2 Multispectral Imaging System, and with hematoxylin and eosin staining as described previously [18].

Quantitative reverse transcription PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was isolated from cells using TRIzol (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #15596026) as described previously [46]. RNA (1 µg) was reverse transcribed using the High-Capacity RNA-to-cDNA kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #4368813). Gene expression was assessed using SYBR primers (Supplemental Table 1) and analyzed by qRT-PCR. Data were analyzed using the ΔΔCt method of analysis and are presented as a fold change compared to the control samples (expressed as 1) [47]. Target gene expression was normalized to pan-Actin.

Immune profiling by flow cytometry

To characterize tumor-infiltrating and lung immune cells, tissues were minced manually into fine pieces and further dissociated in collagenase type IV (1 mg/mL in DMEM) at 37 °C for 45 min with constant agitation (~ 120 rpm). Cells were passed through a 70 μm strainer and resuspended in red blood cell lysis buffer (155 mM NH4Cl, 10 mM KHCO3 and 0.1 mM EDTA) before incubation at room temperature for five minutes. Fc receptors were blocked with anti-mouse CD16/CD32 antibodies (mouse BD Fc Block™, BD Biosciences, #553141) for thirty minutes before incubation with fluorescently labeled primary antibodies (see Supplemental Table 2) for thirty minutes. Cell suspensions were fixed using the FoxP3 transcription factor staining buffer set (BD Biosciences, #00-5523-00) and analyzed by FACS on a Cytek Aurora (Cytek Biosciences). Data were expressed as count of cells multiplied by total volume of sample (µl)/ volume of sample run (µl)/ tissue mass (g), or as a percentage of cells of interest.

Analysis of online gene expression datasets

Correlation of target gene mRNA expression in tumor biopsies and survival in people with breast cancer was conducted with Kaplan-Meier (KM) plotter [48] (with multiple datasets curated by KM plotter, including from GEO, EGA, TCGA, Metabric, Impact, and PubMed repositories). Patients were split by the median value and the hazard ratio with 95% confidence intervals and logrank p values were calculated with KM plotter. Assessment of gene expression in non-responders vs. responders to chemotherapy was assessed using ROCplot [49]. Response to chemotherapy was assessed using 5-year survival rates. p values were calculated by ROCplot.

Statistical analysis

A paired t-test was used to determine differences across 2 groups in vitro, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), with Holm-Sidak post hoc test was used to determine the statistical significance in dose response experiments in vitro, and a two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test was used to determine differences across cell lines. An unpaired t test was completed to determine the statistical significance in experiments with two groups in vivo. Data of two treatments over time was compared with a two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test. Survival analysis was analyzed with a log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test. Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) unless otherwise stated.

Results

Tilorone reduces metastatic capacity of triple-negative breast cancer cell lines

To gain insight into whether tilorone alters the metastatic capacity of triple-negative breast cancer cells, several surrogate in vitro assays were used initially. Daily tilorone treatment (1 µg/ml) reduced cell proliferation of human 231-HM (p < 0.01), and mouse 4T1.2 (p < 0.01) and EMT6.5 (p < 0.01) cell lines, (Fig. 1A, B). In contrast, in a HER2-enriched cell line (TBCP-1) proliferation was not altered (p = 0.12, Fig. 1A, B). To assess the migratory capacity of these cell lines, a wound-healing assay was used, which entailed analysis of cell migration following an experimental scratch of the cultured cell monolayers. Tilorone reduced migration in 231-HM (p < 0.01) and 4T1.2 (p = 0.01) but not EMT6.5 cells (p = 0.79; Fig. 1C, D). In TBCP-1 cells, tilorone administration increased migration (p = 0.04, Fig. 1C, D). Colony formation capacity was reduced by Tilorone in 231-HM cells (p = 0.04), EMT6.5 (p = 0.01) and TBCP-1 cells (p = 0.04), but not in 4T1.2 cells (p = 0.71 Fig. 1E, F). However, tilorone reduced colony formation of 4T1.2 cells sensitized to anoikis (Supplemental Fig. 1A, B).

Tilorone increases expression of interferon responsive genes but not BMP signaling genes.

To assess the acute effect of tilorone upon IFN and BMP signaling, tilorone was added to cultured and isolated cells and gene expression was assessed by qRT-PCR (Fig. 2A). As tilorone is considered an activator of IFN signaling [50], we tested whether tilorone treatment increased transcription of ISGs in 4T1.2 mammary tumor cells. Increased gene expression peaked at 1 µg/ml (Supplemental Fig. 1C–I), with considerable cell death occurring at 10 µg/ml (Supplemental Fig. 1J). Expression of Irf7 (Fig. 2B), Irf9 (Fig. 2C) and Stat1 (Fig. 2D) increased following exposure to tilorone. However, expression of other ISGs such as Socs1 (Fig. 2E), Oas1, Psmb1 and Psmb9 was not altered in 4T1.2 cells by tilorone (Supplemental Fig. 2A–C). We next assessed cells isolated from primary tumors (4T1.2-PT) or bone metastases (4T1.2-BM) of mice bearing 4T1.2 tumors. In 4T1.2-PT cells, expression of Irf7 (Fig. 2F) and Irf9 (Fig. 2G) was significantly increased following tilorone treatment; however, the same induction was not observed in 4T1.2-BM cells. Other ISGs were unaltered in these cells (Fig. 2H, I, Supplemental Fig. 2I–K). Previous reports have shown that tilorone can induce expression of BMP ligands [31, 33]. We therefore tested whether tilorone increased BMP ligand expression in 4T1.2 breast cancer cells. In contrast with previous reports in other non-cancerous lines [31, 33], expression of Bmp2, Bmp4, Bmp7, (Supplemental Fig. 2D–F, L–N) and downstream targets, Id1 and Id3 was not altered (Supplemental Fig. 2G–H, O–P).

Tilorone decreases tumor growth and metastasis in a mouse model of triple-negative breast cancer

To assess the effects of tilorone in a preclinical model of spontaneous breast cancer metastasis. 4T1.2 cells were orthotopically inoculated into Balb/c mice. Commencement of treatment with tilorone at primary tumor palpation on day 7 (Fig. 2I) resulted in reduced primary tumor growth by day 14 (p < 0.01, Fig. 2J). Tumors were resected at approximately ~ 300 mm3, and tumor mass was not different at tumor resection (Supplemental Fig. 3A, C–E). Lung metastatic burden as quantified by qPCR was reduced by 72.4% with tilorone treatment (p < 0.05, Fig. 2K). Assessment of lung metastasis by fluorescence imaging (Fig. 2L) and by H&E staining (Supplemental Fig. 3B), confirmed a reduction in lung metastasis burden. Based on our observation that tilorone induced expression of ISGs in vitro, we compared responses in wild-type Balb/c and Balb/c-IFNAR−/− mice, where stromal and other host cells could not respond to IFN cytokine [15]. With tilorone treatment beginning at primary tumor palpation (on day 7), improved survival was achieved only in wild-type mice (p < 0.01, Fig. 2M), indicating that the activity of tilorone in reducing metastasis in this model is dependent on IFNAR expression in host cells. To test whether the reduction in lung metastasis was dependent on changes in the primary tumor, we initiated a separate experiment where tilorone treatment commenced at the time of tumor resection, around day 16 (Fig. 2N–O). Even with this later onset of treatment after removal of the primary tumor, lung mean metastatic burden was reduced 2-fold by tilorone administration (p < 0.05, Fig. 2O).

Tilorone increases the overall survival of mice bearing 4T1.2 tumors when administered with doxorubicin

IFN signaling has been reported to correlate with an improved response to chemotherapy [14, 51]. Furthermore, Irf7 overexpression in 4T1.2 tumor cells has been shown to improve the response to chemotherapy in mice [52]. Therefore, we tested the efficacy of tilorone in combination with doxorubicin in 4T1.2 cells and tumors. Cell accumulation was measured through an xCelligence proliferation assay (Fig. 3A–C). The growth of doxorubicin-treated 4T1.2 cells was significantly lower relative to the control group as indicated by the slope of the growth curve (p < 0.01, Fig. 3B). Furthermore, the combination of tilorone and doxorubicin was more effective than administration of either agent alone (p < 0.01, Fig. 3B). When the data were analyzed by estimation of doubling time, only the combination group displayed slower growth rate than cells administered the vehicle (p < 0.01, Fig. 3C). In 4T1.2-PT tumor cells, colony formation was reduced significantly by both tilorone and doxorubicin monotherapy, with a further reduction in the combination group relative to cells administered doxorubicin alone (p = 0.01, Fig. 3D, E). In 4T1.2-BM cells, a similar trend was seen, with a significant additive effect of the combination treatment relative to tilorone (p < 0.01) and doxorubicin (p < 0.01, Fig. 3F, G).

To assess the effects of tilorone and doxorubicin combination treatment in vivo, mice bearing 4T1.2 tumors commenced treatment at tumor palpation on day 7 (Fig. 3H). Primary tumor growth was reduced in mice administered the combination treatment relative to mice receiving the vehicle on day 15 post tumor inoculation (p = 0.04, Fig. 3I). Furthermore, survival after tumor resection was increased only with the combination treatment relative to the vehicle treatment (p = 0.04, Fig. 3J).

Tilorone activates the immune system in the premetastatic lung

To assess the impact of tilorone on immune cell infiltration into lungs at an early stage of metastatic dissemination, mice were examined 14 days after tumor cell inoculation and seven days after commencement of treatment with tilorone and/or doxorubicin (gating strategy can be seen in Supplemental Fig. 4). Assessment of lungs revealed that there was no significant difference in CD4+ T cell number with tilorone or doxorubicin treatment (Fig. 4A). The combination treatment significantly increased the proportion of activated CD4+ T cells (as measured by CD69 staining) present in the lung at a pre-metastatic timepoint, but only compared to the vehicle control cohort (p = 0.04, Fig. 4B, C). CD8+ T cell number was reduced in the tilorone and combination treatment groups relative to doxorubicin alone, but not to the vehicle control cohort (Fig. 4D). The proportion of CD69+/CD8+ T cells in lung tissue was greater with tilorone (p < 0.01) and combination (p < 0.01, Fig. 4E, F) relative to the vehicle group. The total NK cell number in lungs was not altered by any treatment (Fig. 4G), but tilorone (p < 0.01) and combination treatment (p < 0.01) increased the proportion of activated NK cells positive for CD69 (Fig. 4H, I). NK cells can be grouped into CD27−/CD11b−, CD27+/CD11b−, CD27+/CD11b+, and CD27−/CD11b+ populations based on their maturation status [53, 54]. CD27−/CD11b− NK cells display an immature phenotype and a greater potential for differentiation. CD27+/CD11b− and CD27+/CD11b+ NK cells are more mature and are more capable of secreting cytokines. Finally, CD27−/CD11b+ NK cells exhibit the greatest cytolytic activity of the subtypes. Immature CD27−/CD11b− NK cells were less abundant in the combination group (p = 0.04, Fig. 4K). CD27+/CD11b− NK cells were more abundant in the lung of the doxorubicin treatment mice relative to the tilorone treated mice (p < 0.01), and combination treated mice (p = 0.03, Fig. 4L). CD27+/CD11b+ NK cells were more abundant in the lungs of combination treated mice compared to vehicle treated mice (p = 0.03, Fig. 4M). CD27−/CD11b+ NK cells trended to be more abundant in the combination group relative to lungs of the vehicle treatment (p = 0.06, Fig. 4N).

In the primary tumors recovered from mice on day 14 after inoculation, we found that tilorone and doxorubicin treatment did not significantly alter total CD4+ T cells or the proportion of cells expressing CD69 (Supplemental Fig. 5A–C). CD8+ T cells showed a trend towards increased numbers in the primary tumor with tilorone treatment relative to the vehicle cohort (p = 0.0734, Supplemental Fig. 5D), as did the number of CD69+/CD8+ T cells compared to the doxorubicin group (p = 0.0201) and the combination treatment group (p = 0.08, Supplemental Fig. 5E, F). Consistent with T cell populations, NK cell number was not altered in the primary tumor with any treatment (Supplemental Fig. 5G), however tilorone treatment increased the proportion of CD69+ NK cells compared to the vehicle (p = 0.02, Supplemental Fig. 5H, I).

CD27−/CD11b− NK cells were more abundant in the tumors of mice administered vehicle (vs. tilorone: p = 0.02, vs. combination: p < 0.01, Supplemental Fig. 5J, K), which was consistent with proportions of CD27+/CD11b− NK cells (vs. tilorone: p = 0.0027, vs. combination: p < 0.01, Supplemental Fig. 5J, L). Conversely, CD27+/CD11b+ NK cells were more abundant in the tumors of mice administered tilorone, and combination treatment (vehicle vs. tilorone: p = 0.0015, vehicle vs. combination: p = 0.01, Supplemental Fig. 5J, M). Only in the combination group was the proportion of the mature and cytotoxic subset of CD27−/CD11b+ NK cells increased (p = 0.03, Supplemental Fig. 5J, N). Furthermore, neither tilorone nor doxorubicin altered the total number of CD45+ cells, neutrophils or monocytes/macrophages in the primary tumor (Supplemental Fig. 6B–F) or in pre-metastatic lungs (Supplemental Fig. 6H–K).

ISG expression correlates with improved survival and response to chemotherapy in patients with triple-negative breast cancer

ISG expression has been correlated with improved survival and therapy response to TNBC [52]. We therefore assessed whether genes induced by tilorone were prognostic for survival or response to chemotherapy in breast cancer patients. In all subtypes of breast cancer, IRF7 (p = 0.34, Fig. 5A) was not prognostic for relapse-free survival. High STAT1 was associated with poorer relapse-free survival (p = 0.02) whilst IRF9 (p = 0.04) and SOCS1 was positively associated (p = 0.04) with relapse-free survival (Fig. 5A). However, the combined mean expression of these genes was not predictive. The same four genes, either alone or combined, were not predictive for response to chemotherapy (combined gene signature: p = 0.13, Fig. 5B). In contrast, in basal-like breast cancer (which overlaps with TNBC), expression of IRF7 (P = 0.03), IRF9 (P < 0.01) and STAT1 (P < 0.01) but not SOCS1, were prognostic for relapse-free survival (Fig. 5C), that persisted when the combined expression of all four genes was considered (p < 0.01) (Fig. 5C). In support of our observation that tilorone increased the response to doxorubicin, IRF9 (p = 0.01), STAT1 (p < 0.01) and SOCS1 (p = 0.01) transcript levels were high in responders to chemotherapy (Fig. 5D). The combined signature of these genes at an equal ratio was also higher in responders to chemotherapy (p < 0.01) (Fig. 5D). These observations are consistent with the concept that tilorone could act both directly and through the immune system to reduce tumor cell growth and suppress metastatic outgrowth (Fig. 5E).

Discussion

Treatment of metastatic breast cancers could benefit greatly from the development of more effective therapeutics. As pre-clinical studies have demonstrated that IFN- and BMP-based agents can exert desirable effects upon the growth and metastasis of breast cancers, we reasoned that an agent capable of enhancing the activity of both pathways, such as tilorone, as could offer an attractive addition to treatment plans for aggressive metastatic breast cancers, such as TNBC.

Tilorone has been reported to reduce primary tumor growth [35]; however, whether it could be an effective anti-metastatic agent is unclear. A clinical trial testing the efficacy of tilorone as a monotherapy in patients with histologically confirmed metastatic breast cancer who had failed conventional chemotherapy, was reported in 1981 [37]. No partial or complete responses were obtained for the 28 patients in the trial, however, all patients had advanced disease and there was no indication of cancer subtype. We hypothesized that tilorone is more likely to have utility in triple-negative breast cancer since low type I IFN response predicts poor prognosis in triple-negative and HER2 enriched cancers, but not in the much more common luminal cancers [38]. Furthermore, this type I IFN response is also dampened in tumor cells with higher metastatic capacity [38]. Consistent with these findings, we observed reduced proliferation and migration capacity of multiple triple-negative and HER2-enriched cell lines. Interestingly, there was heterogeneity in the migration response to tilorone across the cell lines, which presents an opportunity for future studies to examine the underlying mechanisms. In mice we demonstrated that tilorone can reduce lung metastatic burden, and that this reduction in metastatic burden was dependent on the presence of the IFNAR1 in host cells. Additionally, tilorone increased expression of ISGs such as Irf7, Irf9, Stat1 and Socs1 in vitro. In combination with the chemotherapy agent doxorubicin, tilorone increased survival, potentially through increased activation of NK cells. This survival effect indicates that tilorone and similarly acting agents that can enhance interferon signaling may be effective adjuvants with standard-of-care chemotherapy.

The IFN pathway is dysregulated in breast cancer metastasis [55, 56] and therefore stimulation of the pathway has emerged as a potential therapy for metastatic disease. IFN signaling is dysregulated at metastatic sites relative to the primary tumor [15, 56]. Furthermore, low type I IFN expression predicts poor prognosis in triple-negative and HER2 enriched breast cancer patients [38, 56]. In a preclinical model of TNBC, restoration of IFN signaling through Irf7 reduced bone metastasis [56]. Here, Irf7 expression was increased following tilorone stimulation, further supporting a role for this gene in suppressing breast cancer metastasis. Chemotherapy induces DNA damage, and this is also a potent stimulator of type I IFN signaling, detected through pathways such as cGAS/STING [57, 58]. Enforced Irf7 expression in 4T1.2 tumors has been shown to improve survival when combined with doxorubicin [52] and IFN signaling is predictive of the response to chemotherapy [52]. In the course of preparing this manuscript, Chhipa and colleagues reported that tilorone and doxorubicin demonstrated a synergistic role in reducing the viability of MDA-MB-231 and MDA-MB-468 cells in vitro, and in reducing primary tumor growth in a DMBA chemically-induced breast cancer model [59]. Our data presented herein compliment their findings and further show that tilorone and doxorubicin have synergistic action in vitro in a metastatic cell line, and in improving survival in a model of metastatic TNBC. Furthermore, we demonstrated that this combination treatment may lead to increased NK cell maturation in both primary tumor and in pre-metastatic lungs. Other research supporting this combination treatment was demonstrated by a study in mice, that showed that tumors lacking the IFNAR fail to respond to chemotherapy unless the downstream interferon responsive gene CXCL10 was experimentally enhanced [14]. This observation indicates that IFN signaling may be essential for some patients to respond effectively to chemotherapy.

In models of breast cancer, BMPs can exert strong anti-metastatic properties and improve survival [17, 18, 42, 60]. Given reports that tilorone increases BMP ligand expression and BMP signaling in a range of tissue types [31–33], here, we sought to investigate the effectiveness of tilorone to increase BMP ligand expression in preclinical models of metastatic breast cancer. We hypothesized that tilorone would reduce metastatic breast cancer through increased BMP ligand expression and signaling. However, we did not observe an increase in BMP ligand expression in 4T1.2 cells when treated with tilorone. There may be cell type specific responses to tilorone to explain this discrepancy. For instance, 4T1.2 cells have low expression of endogenous BMP4, and can express endogenous inhibitors of BMP ligands [18]. Future studies could investigate whether tilorone can induce BMP ligand expression in other breast cancer lines.

Tilorone is well characterized as an IFN activator that also activates NK cell activity [61]. Our data are consistent with a previous report demonstrating that tilorone can activate NK cells and CD8+ and CD4+ T cells in vitro [62]. Tilorone has been shown to improve survival in mouse models of sarcoma, lymphoma and melanoma following intravenous injection [63–65]. Tilorone treatment also reduced prostate cancer cell proliferation and colony formation in vitro [66]. In breast cancer there are conflicting reports regarding the efficacy of tilorone. For instance, tilorone was not efficacious in a phase II trial in breast cancer patients [37] or in a rat mammary adenocarcinoma model [67]. More recently however, tilorone was shown to reduce the proliferation of MCF-7 and MDA‐MB‐231 human breast cancer cells [35]. Tilorone treatment also resulted in reduced tumor volume and increased survival with a concomitant increased IFN‐β and p53 expression in the primary tumor [35]. In a rat model of breast carcinoma, tilorone was also effective in reducing lung metastasis, but was ineffective in models of hepatoma, further demonstrating that effects can vary by cancer type [68]. Our data in a model of metastatic TNBC showed that tilorone effectively reduced lung metastasis, which was associated with activation of NK cells, in both the primary tumor and pre-metastatic lungs. We also reported that key ISGs associated with the type I IFN signaling pathway and antigen presentation were upregulated by tilorone in 4T1.2 tumor cells. These same ISGs were correlated with increased survival and chemotherapy response in patients with TNBC but not in all breast cancers. We propose that tilorone could be effective in TNBC through its ability to stimulate a type I IFN response and anti-tumor immunity.

Conclusions

Herein, we have presented that tilorone effectively induces IFN signaling in a preclinical model of TNBC metastasis. Tilorone is a broad-spectrum anti-viral compound that we have demonstrated as being effective at reducing lung metastasis in models of breast cancer, including in an adjuvant setting, with treatment commencing after tumor resection. The anti-tumor immune response mediated by IFNs has emerged as a key factor in determining metastatic spread and outgrowth in multiple organs [69, 70]. Certain aggressive breast cancer subtypes with typically poor prognosis, such as TNBC and HER2-overexpressed breast cancer are typically more immunogenic than other subtypes [71], indicating that therapies that activate the host immune response may be particularly effective in these subtypes. Indeed, TNBC likely responds better to immunotherapy, given the presence of more tumor infiltrating lymphocytes, higher PD-L1 expression and T cell inhibition, and higher tumor mutation burden relative to other breast cancer subtypes [72]. Therefore, despite tilorone being ineffective in early trials for breast cancer patients [37], it may be selectively effective in TNBC. This conclusion is supported by our analysis of publicly available datasets showing that ISGs correlate with improved survival and response to chemotherapy in TNBC. Analogs with better antitumor activity than tilorone have now been developed [73], so future studies should explore their efficacy in breast cancer. Increasing IFN signaling through the toll-like receptor 3 (TLR3) agonist poly(I: C) has also been shown to further reduce metastatic burden in the presence of anti-PD-1 therapy [51]. Therefore, future studies could investigate the effectiveness of tilorone in combination with anti-PD-1 therapies. Despite the conflicting reports regarding the effectiveness of tilorone in treating breast cancer, our data indicate that agents capable of enhancing IFN signaling, such as tilorone or alternatives that employ a similar mechanism, could be effective in TNBC when used as an adjuvant to traditional chemotherapies.
