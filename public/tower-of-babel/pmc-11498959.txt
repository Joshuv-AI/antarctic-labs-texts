Does competitive school climate really harm to students’ anti-bullying attitude: empirical evidence from China
Authors: Hu Penghui, Xia Mengfan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSchool bullying remains a social problem and changing students’ attitudes towards bullying is crucial for effective prevention in schools. Based on social-ecological system theory, this study examines how school climate influences anti-bullying attitudes among students. Specifically, it studies how a competitive school environment influences these attitudes, and how students’ competitive attitudes mediate these attitudes.MethodsThe data for this study comes from The Programmed for International Student Assessment (PISA), which is a global student assessment initiative led by the Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD). The sample consists of 12,058 Chinese students from the 2018 PISA survey. Data analysis was conducted using STATA (version 14.0) software.ResultsSchool competitive climate has a positive and substantial impact on their anti-bullying attitudes (r = 0.1551, p < 0.001). Students’ competitive attitudes are also positively and significantly correlated with their anti-bullying attitudes (r = 0.2249, p < 0.001). Competitive attitudes partially mediate the relationship between the school bullying climate and students’ anti-bullying attitudes.ConclusionIn the context of Chinese education, a competitive school climate clearly has a positive effect on students anti-bullying attitudes. Students’ competitive attitudes also act as partial mediator in this relationship. These findings suggest that a healthy school ecosystem that reflects sound value guidance and fair rules is imperative for fostering anti-bullying attitudes in students.Keywords: Competitive climate, Anti-bullying attitude, Competitive attitude, School climate

Introduction

School bullying is increasingly recognized as a pervasive global social issue [1, 2]. Internationally, approximately one-third of students are subjected to school bullying [3]. In China, regional small-scale studies indicate victimization rates from conventional bullying—verbal, physical and relational—range between 2 and 66%, along with perpetration rates between 2 and 34% [4]. Considering the prevalence of school bullying and the long run effects on youth development, such issues are of key concern for educators and policymakers throughout numerous countries.

Bandura’s social cognitive theory holds that individual behavior is shaped by cognitive frameworks rather than by emotional impulses. Cognitive processes such as beliefs, attitudes and expectations are central to the selection and implementation of behaviors [5]. Thus, changing someone’s behavior requires a change in their core cognitive values. Studies have shown that students’ attitudes toward bullying are related to their participation in bullying [6, 7]. Specifically, pro-victim attitudes are associated with lower odds of carrying out bullying activity [8, 9], along with a weaker anti-bullying position is associated with increased odds of involvement [10].

In this context, students’ attitudes toward bullying are critical to the success of anti-bullying programmes and interventions [11–13]. Olweus, who pioneered bullying research, says reducing bullying involves changing attitudes, behaviors and routines in the school environment. Thus, changing students’ attitudes is an important component of any strategy to prevent bullying [12]. Empirical evidence from interventions against bullying further supports those changing students ‘attitudes may result in a reduction in bullying [14, 15]. Consequently, the subject of academic research on bullying has become increasingly about students’ attitudes and beliefs regarding bullying. In recent years, the application of social-ecological systems theory to school bullying research has gained significant momentum [16], with many studies have examined how environmental factors such as school climate influence bullying behaviors [17–19]. A key component of school climate is competitive spirit [20, 21]. Research has examined the complex relationship of a competitive school climate to bullying, with some studies indicating a positive correlation [22, 23]. In highly competitive environments, students may be more pro-bullying in their attitudes toward competition [24].

However, a lot of this research has occurred in Western cultural settings, which tend to be Machiavellian [25]. There is still relatively little research on students’ attitudes towards bullying in Confucian cultures such as China [26, 27]. Several studies have shown that countries and regions of the Confucian culture tend to be “score-centric,” and that many educational administrators place academic performance above competitive management practices [28, 29]. Competition is an essential part of education in China in particular. Cultural proverbs like “knowledge makes a man” and “be a good official” are ingrained in our collective consciousness, reflecting the dominant competitive spirit in schools which generally operate on a model of competition [30, 31]. Although traditional Chinese culture is one of harmony and cooperation [21], the educational landscape of China combines competition with cooperation [32].

This research investigates if middle school students’ attitudes toward anti-bullying are impacted by a competitive school climate within this cultural context. In addition, because students are active agents, the research explores the possible mediating effect of attitudes toward competition. This study asks two questions based on data from PISA 2018 for Chinese students: (1) Does a competitive school climate affect student anti-bullying attitude? (2) What mediates competitive attitudes in the relationship of school competition to anti-bullying attitudes?

Our study has two important contributions. First, knowledge of victimization remains limited in mainland China, particularly in underdeveloped regions [33]. Few large-scale representative studies of mechanisms and effects of bullying are available in China, which limits the design of effective policies as well as targeted interventions [34]. Potential benefits for a protective school environment to prevent campus violence and support victims have not been explored adequately. This paper analyses school bullying in China using national sample, and our findings offer empirical evidence that a competitive school setting doesn’t necessarily worsen anti-bullying behaviors among students. Rather, it can significantly enhance them. We also show that competitive attitudes in students can influence anti-bullying attitudes as well. These insights can help policymakers and schools design more effective, evidenced based anti-bullying intervention strategies.

Second, school bullying has received relatively little empirical investigation in China as compared to Western contexts. More attention should be paid to the broader social ecology influence, notably the role of school climate. Previous research has shown that competition and cooperation are important features of school climate in East Asian countries with collectivist societies and Confucian cultural roots [11]. Yin et al. also showed that school bullying prevalence and strategies employed to deal with it are culturally dependent [35]. Consequently, our research aims to fill this particular research gap by providing details from the perspective of a developing nation in a Confucian society context. Academic competitiveness is stressed in China—and in the Confucian cultural field in East Asia—while harmony is a core cultural value. Nevertheless, very little research continues to be conducted on the way the competitive climate in this cultural context impacts students anti-bullying attitudes which differ from those in Western countries. This study fills that gap by examining how competitive school climates affect anti-bullying attitudes among Confucian cultural students and the mechanisms at play.

Literature review and hypothesis

Competitive climate and anti-bullying attitude

Social-ecological systems theory was first proposed by Bronfenbrenner in 1979 [36]. This theory holds that a person develops in response to their environment and that research on individual behavior must be contextualized within an ecological framework. This framework demonstrates that behavior is shaped not only by the individual but by the environment as well [37, 38]. On the basis of this theory researchers agree that the social-ecological framework is necessary and relevant for bullying research [39].

Furthermore, several studies have investigated how environmental factors such as school climate influence bullying in schools [16, 17, 19, 40]. School climate refers to feelings and attitudes caused by school environment [41], including academic, community, security and institutional environment [42]. There is no international consensus on the essential dimensions of school climate, but it is recognized as a complex concept encompassing almost all aspects of the school experience [43].

School climate is one such meso-system component, and one of the most important is the degree to which the environment promotes competition versus cooperation [20]. Volks et al. state that kids and adolescents spend the vast majority of the time in school creating social hierarchies with peers [24]. In a competitive school setting, performance is often more important than developing good social relationships, and students vie for social standing and academic success [44]. They learn about their own abilities and ranking in this competitive structure through interactions. This suggests that bullying behaviors may increase in a competitive climate whereas positive peer interactions tend to occur in a cooperative climate [24].

That is due to competitive schools tend to reward and recognize only those students who outperform others [45]. Students may think that only the best will receive resources, success and social recognition. If all achievements are viewed as shame or failure, students may engage in more competitive behavior such as bullying to achieve greater social status within the school [46]. Therefore, the relation between competition, cooperation and bullying attitudes needs to be examined within the ecological context of school climate.

Research has identified micro-mechanisms that explain the positive association of a competitive school climate with students’ bullying behavior. Olweus argued bullying is more likely in environments where students are expected to compete for grades in a competitive school setting [47]. Similarly, US educator Aronson said intense competition for grades and teacher approval in classrooms can create animosity among students and lead to exclusion, ridicule and bullying [48].

Cristina Salmivalli, that designed and applied the Kiva antibullying program such as Sonia Sharp and Peter Smith. The new project KiVa includes general and indicative actions to reduce bullying. This study reports the influence of KiVa on the samples of 5,651 students from 78 schools (39 interventions and 39 controls). identified six possible roles that children might play during bullying—victim, bully, bully assistant, reinforcer, outsider, or victim defender. Bullying rates among students in control schools were 1.32—1.94 times greater than in intervention schools after nine months of intervention. This suggests that well designed, research-based interventions can reduce multiple types of bullying without planning for each type of bullying. [49]

Essentially, within frameworks that view bullying as a struggle for social dominance or scarce resources, there is a consensus in western academia regarding the “competition hypothesis.” This hypothesis posits that an environment of competition in educational settings correlates with heightened levels of bullying.

Competitive attitude mediates the relationship between competitive climate and anti-bullying attitude

The competitive climate of a school influences student bullying behavior, which is an important area of current research. Common assumptions hold that such environments predispose students to competitive behaviors that may include bullying [24, 48]. this view fails to account for the subjectivity and agency experienced by students themselves. As Hedstrom points out, understanding the mechanisms that cause social change requires acknowledging individual actor agency, as is necessary for a full account of social phenomena. He advocates for the systematic development of sociological theory that is both explanatory and precise, offering insights that bridge theoretical and empirical research, while establishing strong connections between micro and macro levels of analysis [50]. The relevance of Hedstrom’s theory to this study is clear: The macro, meso and micro factors have to be connected to understand the dynamics at play. Previous work has shown that the school environment as meso-level system influences bullying behaviors whereas individual competitive attitudes are a micro-level system. Thus, examining the association of a competitive school environment with students’ anti-bullying attitude requires determining how this climate affects students—the agents—and what mechanisms may trigger their anti-bullying attitudes. Zhao argues that students ‘competitive attitudes are shaped more by their educational environment and experience than by their inherent characteristics or family socioeconomic status [51]. Thus, the school environment influences students’ perceptions of competitiveness. Research suggests that in environments where competition is valued and only the best performers are given resources, success and recognition students are more likely to engage in competitive behaviors to increase their chances of achieving goals [46, 52].

In China, the educational ethos has long been shaped by the adages “all things are inferior, only study is high” and “excel in studies to become an official” [53]. This cultural context has led schools to impose a competitive system based on enrollment rates as means of survival and development [54, 55], competing for very limited educational opportunities [56]. This creates a culture of competition in schools and the whole system is based on competitive expressionism [31]. Thus the competitive school climate influences students’ competitive attitudes. Hu found a positive association between psychological stress students feel in learning environments and bullying behavior [57]. This finding is consistent with research in the UK. Sutton and Keogh’s study involving 198 children from two Glasgow primary schools revealed that a competitive attitude among students was inversely related to their support for bullying victims, suggesting a diminished anti-bullying stance [25].

Existing research tends to regard collaboration as mutually exclusive and competition as inevitably negative. But some scholars dispute this view and argue that competition and cooperation need not necessarily be opposed and can exist independently. In fact, competition sometimes has positive effects in addition to negative effects. Competitiveness is divided into excessive competition and healthy competition. In extreme cases of too much competition, people feel compelled to succeed to maintain or boost their sense of self-worth. Such individuals may be aggressive and seek to attack, manipulate or undermine others in a variety of contexts [58]. On the other hand, healthy competition encourages people to focus on the enjoyment of the activity and to achieve success by improving oneself and oneself alone rather than by causing harm to others [59].

Based on the social reconnection hypothesis, those who are marginalized or persecuted are far more likely to cooperate because they require social reconnection. Such individuals may adopt proactive coping strategies to build social networks in response to experiences of marginalization and / or abuse [60]. Although Chinese schools emphasize competition, China’s harmonious cultural value system—which places great emphasis on cooperation, mutual benefit and support even in competitive situations—also has a great impact [61]. These cultural elements restrain the urge to attack or play games with others in competitive situations, and maintain a balance between competitiveness and psychological well-being. In this cultural context, a competitive environment promotes competitive spirit without compromising values rather than promoting psychological imbalances and bullying tendencies.

That is to say, teens with competitive attitudes may also have more positive values. The contribution of this study is to investigate how competitive school climate influences anti-bullying attitudes of students in the Confucian cultural field. It also examines the mediating effect of competitive attitudes to better understand the interaction of anti-bullying attitudes with competition in different cultural settings.

Research gap and hypotheses

Theoretical and empirical literature show that a competitive school climate predicts more bullying than other school climates. But the connection between this climate and anti-bullying attitudes of pupils is understudied. Attitudes affect behavior, and a virulent anti-bullying position is associated with lower levels of bullying [9].

In the Chinese context wherein competition and cooperation are normative characteristics of the educational system, there is a lack of empirical work looking at the consequences associated with a competitive school environment on anti-bullying attitudes of pupils in addition to associated mechanisms. To fill this research gap, this study formulates three hypotheses which are tested empirically with data from PISA 2018 for Chinese students. H1: A competitive school climate is inversely related to students’ anti-bullying attitudes.

H2: Students’ competitive attitudes mediate the relationship between the school’s competitive climate and their anti-bullying attitudes.

H3: Students’ competitive attitudes correlate positively with a competitive school climate and negatively with anti-bullying attitudes.

Method

Sample

The Programmed for International Student Assessment (PISA) is a global student assessment initiative led by the Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD). Its objective is to assess whether 15-year-old students, at the culmination of compulsory education, possess the knowledge and skills essential for societal participation. PISA 2018 employed a two-stage stratified sampling method. Initially, schools were selected using a probability proportional to size (PPS) sampling technique. Subsequently, a specific number of 15-year-old students, primarily at the OESC 1 to OESC 2 education level, were randomly chosen from these schools. The primary data sources were student questionnaires, which comprised standardized self-reported tests with multiple-choice questions assessing aspects such as educational development, mental health, campus performance, family background, and more. PISA 2018 gathered data from 294,527 students across 37 OECD countries. This dataset is publicly accessible on the OECD’s official website. For this study, data on students from four regions in China – Beijing, Shanghai, Jiangsu, and Guangdong – were extracted from the PISA 2018 student questionnaire, encompassing a total of 12,058 students from 361 schools [62]. Since this study is a secondary data analysis of publicly available data (PISA 2018), the secondary data analysis did not require ethical approval.

Measurements

Dependent variable: students’ anti-bullying attitude

Teenagers’ attitude towards school bullying mainly includes their clear belief in bullying and anti-bullying behavior, that is, moral judgment, and a series of emotional reactions inspired by it [63]. Anti-bullying attitudes were measured using a modified version of the Pro-Victim Scale [64]. This scale consists of five questions. PISA2018 treats this abstract definition as five specific moral judgments and emotional reactions and assess anti-bulling attitude by five items: (1) It irritates me when nobody defends bullied students. (2) It is a good thing to help students who can’t defend themselves. (3) It is a wrong thing to join in bullying. (4) I feel bad seeing other students bullied. (5) I like it when someone stands up for other students who are being bullied. The items were evaluated on a 4-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree;4 = strongly agree). In this paper, the exploratory factor analysis of the five items shows that they have strong internal consistency reliability and are suitable for factor analysis (Cronbach’s α = 0.8565, KMO = 0.856, Bartlett p < 0.001) [65], and the psychometric properties revealed an excellent model fit (RMSEA = 0.087, CFI = 0.982, TLI = 0.965). Then, the common factor values obtained in the factor analysis are indexed to make them continuous variables with values ranging from 0 to 100 points. The higher the score, the stronger the students’ anti-bullying attitude is.

Independent variable: school competitive climate

In PISA 2018, competitive climate was measured according to following questions: (1) Students seem to value competition. (2) It seems that students are competing with each other. (3) Students seem to share the feeling that competing with each other is important. (4) Students feel that they are being compared with others [14]. Participants provided responses on a 4-point Likert rating scale (1 = ‘strongly disagree’, to 4 = ‘strongly agree’). In this paper, the exploratory factor analysis of the four items shows that they have strong internal consistency reliability and are suitable for factor analysis (Cronbach’s α = 0.8198, KMO = 0.790, Bartlett p < 0.001) [65], and psychometric properties revealed a good model fit (CTI = 0.997, TLI = 0.992, SMSEA = 0.044). Then, the common factor values obtained in the factor analysis are indexed to make them continuous variables with values ranging from 0 to 100 points. The higher the score, the stronger the students’ competitive climate is.

Mediating variable: students’ competitive attitude

Three items are used in PISA 2018 to measure students’ competitive attitude: (1) I enjoy working in situations involving competition with others. (2) It is important for me to perform better than other people on a task. (3) I try harder when I’m in competition with other people. The scores ranged from 1 = strongly disagree to 4 = strongly agree. In this paper, the exploratory factor analysis of the three items shows that they have strong internal consistency reliability and are suitable for factor analysis (Cronbach’s α = 0.7224, KMO = 0.660, Bartlett p < 0.001) [65]. As this model only has three questions, the constructed model is saturated and df is 0, and the model fit results will not be evaluated [66]

Then, the common factor values obtained in the factor analysis are indexed to make them continuous variables with values ranging from 0 to 100 points. The higher the score, the stronger the students’ competitive attitude is.

Control variables

Referring to relative studies [67, 68], the study controlled for student and school background characteristic variables. Student-related controlled variables include students’ gender (0 = female, 1 = male), family socio-economic status, and types of schools attended by students (1 = junior high school, 2 = senior high school, 3 = vocational high school). School-related control variables include the location of students’ schools (1 = rural schools, 2 = county and town schools, 3 = urban schools), school nature (0 = private schools, 1 = public schools). Results

Correlations between perceived competitive climate, competitive attitude and anti-bullying attitude

The bivariate correlation analysis (Table 2) reveals significant interrelations among school competitive climate, students’ competitive attitudes, and their anti-bullying attitudes. The school competitive climate has a positive and substantial impact on both students’ competitive attitudes (r = 0.1772, p < 0.001) and their anti-bullying attitudes (r = 0.1551, p < 0.001). Additionally, students’ competitive attitudes are also positively and significantly correlated with their anti-bullying attitudes (r = 0.2249, p < 0.001). Notably, the competitive climate, competitive attitudes, and anti-bullying attitudes vary significantly across different genders, types of schools, and school locations. However, the perceived competitive climate, competitive attitudes, and anti-bullying attitudes do not show significant differences between students in public and private schools.

The mediating role of students’ competitive attitude

To examine the mediating role of competitive attitude in the relationship between perceived school competitive climate and students’ anti-bullying attitude, we incorporated the mediator variable of competitive attitude into Model 2 (Table 3). The findings indicate that, while the perceived school competitive climate remains significantly and positively related to students’ anti-bullying attitude, the coefficient decreases from 0.142 in Model 1 to 0.115 after including competitive attitude. This suggests that competitive attitude is a crucial partial mediator between perceived school competitive climate and students’ anti-bullying attitude. Additionally, competitive attitude shows a significant and positive correlation with students’ anti-bullying attitude (p < 0.001), with each unit increase in competitive attitude leading to a 0.195 increase in anti-bullying attitude. These results confirm Hypothesis 2 but refutes Hypothesis 3. Furthermore, after incorporating students’ competitive attitude, factors other than the nature of the school and student gender, such as family socio-economic status and the type and location of schools, continue to significantly influence students’ anti-bullying attitude.

This partially validates Hypothesis 3. Additionally, upon controlling for other variables, the analysis reveals a significant effect of school location on anti-bullying attitudes, with urban students exhibiting stronger competitive attitudes than their rural and township counterparts.

Bootstrap test of the mediation effect of competitive attitude

To ascertain the robustness of the mediating role of competitive attitude in the relationship between perceived school competitive climate and students’ anti-bullying attitudes, we employed bootstrapping estimates. Bootstrapping is preferred due to its higher statistical power compared to other methods in testing mediation effects [70]. This analysis followed the bootstrap methodology advocated by Preacher and Hayes [71], with a sample size of 5000. The bootstrap results (Table 5) confirmed the significance of the mediation effect, as the 95% confidence interval (CI) did not include zero (β = 0.0254, 95% CI ranging from 0.0210 to 0.0297), indicating a mediation effect of 0.0254. Thus, a competitive attitude indeed serves as a mediator in this context. Consequently, Hypothesis 2 is further substantiated. In essence, the school competitive climate directly influences students’ anti-bullying attitudes and also indirectly through the mediating impact of their competitive attitude.

Conclusion and discussion

Conclusion

China is grappling with the significant issue of school bullying. Utilizing a subset of data from China within the PISA 2018 dataset, this study investigates the correlation between school competitive climate and students’ anti-bullying attitudes, alongside the mediating influence of students’ competitive attitudes. We have deduced three key conclusions: (1) Within the Chinese educational milieu, there exists a positive and notable link between the competitive climate in schools and students’ anti-bullying attitudes, indicating that a more pronounced competitive climate correlates with stronger anti-bullying attitudes among students. (2) The competitive attitude of students serves as a mediator between the school’s competitive climate and their anti-bullying attitudes. (3) There is a discernible positive relationship between students’ competitive attitudes and their anti-bullying attitudes, implying that an intensified competitive attitude is associated with a more robust anti-bullying stance.

Theoretical and practical implications

These findings have theoretical and practical implications. First, we challenge the established literature [24, 72] by showing that a competitive school environment positively and significantly influences anti-bullying attitudes among students. Competition and cooperation have traditionally been considered oppositional forces in school environments [24, 60], with cooperative climates reducing bullying and competitive climates increasing it. However, our analysis suggests an even more nuanced interaction: competition and cooperation may be independent but coexisting elements [73, 74]. The OECD (2019) reports that the indices of student competition and cooperation in China are both approximately 0.2, indicating a higher-than-average perception of both cooperation and competition among Chinese students compared to their OECD counterparts. Moreover, these indices appear to be relatively balanced [62]. Additionally, PISA 2018 data reveals a correlation coefficient of 0.6188 (p < 0.001) between perceived school competition and cooperation atmospheres among Chinese students. This suggests that in Chinese schools, cooperation and competition are not mutually exclusive or in a trade-off relationship, but rather coexist. Thus, the competitive climate perceived by students does not necessarily result in increased bullying or diminished anti-bullying attitudes.

To further verify our viewpoint, this research tried to incorporate the cooperative climate felt by students into the total regression model (see model 4 in Table 3). The results reveal that: (1) even after the school cooperative climate is included, school competitive climate still positively and significantly affects students’ anti-bullying attitude (β = 0.075, p < 0.001); (2) the explanatory power of this model to middle school students’ anti-bullying attitude increased from 0.09 to 0.15, which indicated that the cooperative climate in school was also an important factor affecting students’ anti-bullying attitude. When other variables are controlled, students’ anti-bullying attitude will increase by 0.188 (p < 0.001) for every unit of school cooperative climate. From this, we can see that the climate of school competition and cooperation independently and actively affect students’ anti-bullying attitude. It also proves that school competition does not necessarily result into negative consequences but do positive effects. The “competition hypothesis” points out that in a competitive environment, students may bully others because of competing for scarce resources [38, 75]. However, in the environment of scarce resources, a competitive goal structure can form a stronger impetus [76], and competition can also make people feel excited and enjoy [77]. Especially in the climate of cooperation and competition are both emphasized, the performance and enjoyment of participants will be significantly higher than that of pure cooperation and competition [78]. In other words, the competitive climate can bring stronger motivation and clear goals, and it does not necessarily lead individuals to take inappropriate actions to achieve their goals. With proper value guidance and fair rules, individuals can pursue their goals according to the track that conforms to the norms.

Secondly, students’ competitive attitude mediates the link between perceived competitive climate and anti-bullying attitude. And students’ competitive attitude is also positively and significantly affecting their anti-bullying attitude, which is inconsistent with existing research [25]. In Chinese schools, teachers and parents emphasize academic performance competitions [30, 79], nevertheless, the traditional Chinese culture of harmony, which promotes student unity and cooperation, is also held in high regard. As mentioned earlier, the correlation index of Chinese students perceived the index of school competition and cooperation is as high as 0.6188. In Chinese harmony culture, when competing to be excellent students, students should not only focus on individual performance but also maintain good interpersonal relationships. Collaboration and support are crucial aspects in fostering a sense of class culture in primary and secondary schools in China. Therefore, they are unlikely to adopt bullying strategies to cope with intense competition. Conversely, students with stronger competitive attitudes are more likely to hold anti-bullying attitudes.

Thirdly, this study holds important practical implications. On one hand, competition should not be invariably viewed as harmful, and a nuanced understanding of its dynamics is crucial. Contrary to the commonly held “competition hypothesis” in Western contexts, which posits that competition escalates students’ psychological stress, thereby increasing bullying behaviors [57], our findings indicate that students’ competitive attitudes can actually exert a positive and significant influence on their anti-bullying stance. Wang and Xu’s study, demonstrating the positive effects of competition on students’ academic performance, purpose, and self-efficacy, further corroborates our perspective [32]. Therefore, it is vital for families, educators, and society to recognize that competition, when understood and managed properly, can be beneficial. Acknowledging and mitigating the potential negative impacts of competition is essential. Given that most competitions are zero-sum games, the perception of failure due to personal inadequacy can adversely affect students’ self-esteem. It is critical to provide students with appropriate guidance on competition, tailored to their individual characteristics. Moreover, schools must enforce clear, fair competition rules, ensuring impartiality and transparency. Additionally, competition and collaboration are not mutually exclusive; they can coexist and complement each other. Schools should cultivate an environment that integrates both competitive and collaborative elements, promoting a healthy balance between the two. By fostering a school climate that values both competition and cooperation, and guiding students’ behaviors and values, students are equipped with clear goals, strong motivation, and positive psychological support, paving the way for their holistic development. Such an educational approach fosters a more conducive ecosystem for students’ overall growth.

Limitations and future directions

This study is subject to certain limitations, highlighting areas for future research. Primarily, all variables were assessed using self-reported data. While self-report questionnaires are a standard tool in research, they are prone to social desirability bias, where respondents may answer in a manner, they consider socially acceptable [80]. Consequently, future studies should consider employing diverse measurement techniques, incorporating perspectives from teachers, classmates, and parents for a more objective analysis. Additionally, our results are based on data from Chinese students, who are influenced by a culture that highly values harmony. This cultural context may affect the applicability of our findings to other regions. Therefore, it is essential to extend this research to diverse cultural settings to evaluate the universality of these conclusions.
