Skin autofluorescence may be a biomarker associated suboptimal glycemic control in pregnant women with gestational diabetes mellitus
Authors: Manel Salsabil Alakrab, Dhekra Toumi, Ameni Selmi, Ahmed Hajji, Olfa Zoukar, Salwa Abid, Raja Faleh, Mohsen Kerkeni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdvanced glycation end products (AGEs) can be measured non-invasively through skin autofluorescence (SAF). Studies on SAF in gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) remain limited. This study aims to evaluate SAF in Tunisian pregnant women with GDM and its association with the requirement for insulin therapy.MethodsA cross sectional study was conducted of 242 pregnant women who visited at the Monastir-Tunisia Center for Maternity. Screening for GDM was performed using the OGTT. 200 pregnant women were included in the study with and without GDM. We measured SAF using the AGE reader Mu device and the age-adjusted SAF-z score was calculated.ResultsHigher SAF levels and SAF-z scores were observed in pregnant women with GDM compared to healthy controls (2.1 ± 0.5 AU vs 1.9 ± 0.4 AU p < 0.001, 1.02 ± 0.9 vs 0.64 ± 0.7 p = 0.002, respectively). The ROC analysis revealed that an SAF >2.3 AU was the optimal cut-off point for predicting insulin therapy requirement with a sensitivity of 90.9% and a specificity of 69.7% (p < 0.001).ConclusionSAF was increased in GDM, and can be a biomarker associated suboptimal glycemic control in pregnant women with GDM. Our findings highlight the relevance of SAF measurement and suggest SAF monitoring during pregnancy.Keywords: skin autofluorescence, advanced glycation and products, gestational diabetes mellitus, predictive biomarker

Introduction

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is an increasingly prevalent condition worldwide, affecting approximately 14% of pregnancies. 1 This rising prevalence is driven by various risk factors, including age, obesity, a family history of diabetes, and sedentary lifestyles.2,3 GDM is characterized by hyperglycemia first detected during pregnancy. 4 This condition is associated with a heightened risk of obstetric and neonatal complications, as well as an increased prevalence of metabolic diseases in both mother and child, in the short and long term. 5 These consequences underscore the importance of early screening and rigorous follow-up to minimize complications. Understanding the risk factors for GDM is crucial for early identification and intervention, particularly in high-risk populations. Investigating new biomarkers as predictive factors warrants further attention.

Advanced glycation end products (AGEs) are well-established as a predictive factor for diabetes complications. They formed through a non-enzymatic reaction between reducing sugars and proteins, lipids or nucleic acids 6 and play a pivotal role in the pathophysiology of metabolic diseases.7–9 Their accumulation accelerates in the presence of hyperglycemia, leading to oxidative stress and inflammation which are key mechanisms underlying metabolic complications.10–12 Skin autofluorescence (SAF), a non-invasive measure reflects tissue accumulation of AGEs levels and correlates with glycation process observed in skin biopsies. 13 SAF shows potential for detecting glycemic abnormalities and monitoring at-risk subjects. 13 However, while studies have explored the utility of SAF in chronic diabetes, its application to GDM remains underexplored, particularly in the Tunisian context. Furthermore, to our knowledge, no study to date has investigated the relationship between SAF and the glycemic control that refers to the fluctuations in blood glucose levels over time in GDM. This study aims to evaluate the tissue accumulation profile of AGEs in Tunisian pregnant women with and without GDM and to investigate its association with the requirement for insulin therapy.

Methods

Study design and population

This cross sectional study was conducted at the Monastir-Tunisia Center for Maternity and Neonatology between January 1, 2024, and September 30, 2024. The study included pregnant women aged 18 years or older with singleton pregnancies. All participants underwent screening for gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) at or after the 24th week of gestation using the 75 g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT). According to the recommendations of the International Association of Diabetes and Pregnancy Study Group (IADPSG), 14 the reference values for the OGTT are a fasting glucose level below 5.1 mmol/L, a 1-h glucose level below 10.0 mmol/L, and a 2-h glucose level below 8.5 mmol/L, with a diagnosis of GDM being established if any of these thresholds were met. Participants were divided into two groups: the gestational diabetes group, consisting of women diagnosed with GDM, and the control group, comprising pregnant women without GDM. The group was screened for GDM, were followed using blood glucose measurements to see glycemic control. Women whose glucose values were not adequately controlled with diet alone were started on insulin therapy. Women with pre-existing medical conditions identified during the screening process or those who declined to participate were excluded from the study. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Higher Institute of Biotechnology in Monastir, and all participants provided written informed consent.

Skin autofluorescence measurement

For all participants, skin autofluorescence (SAF) was measured at or after the 24th week of gestation using the AGE Reader Mu version device (Diagnoptics, Groningen, The Netherlands), a non-invasive tool designed to detect tissue accumulation of advanced glycation end-products (AGEs) in the skin.15,16 The device operates on the principle that certain AGEs emit autofluorescence when exposed to ultraviolet (UV) light. The excitation light used has a wavelength range of 300–420 nm, with a peak intensity around 370 nm. This light is projected onto a 4 cm2 area on the inner forearm, and the emitted light (420–600 nm) is measured using an integrated spectrometer. The SAF score is expressed in arbitrary units (AU) and is calculated by dividing the average intensity of the emitted light by the intensity of the excitation light, then multiplying the result by 100.15,16 Age-adjusted SAF scores (z-scores) were calculated for all participants, as described in previous studies. 17

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software version 22.0 (IBM, USA). Data are presented as means ± standard deviations (SD) for continuous variables and as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. Group comparisons were performed using the student’s t-test for quantitative variables and the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for qualitative variables, as appropriate. Linear regression analysis was utilized to identify predictors of the SAF. Correlation coefficients were calculated using Pearson’s or Spearman’s correlation analysis, depending on the distribution and nature of the variables. Variables with a p-value less than 0.2 in the univariate analysis were included in the multivariate regression analysis. A p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Among the 242 pregnant women recruited, 108 exhibited abnormal results on the 75 g OGTT, while 134 had normal results. In the group with abnormal tests, 8 women declined to participate, leading to the inclusion of 100 women diagnosed with gestational diabetes. In the group with normal test results, 18 women declined to participate, and 16 had other pathologies, resulting in the selection of 100 women with uncomplicated pregnancies to form the control group (Figure 1).

Factors associated with skin autofluorescence levels

In a linear model that included age, BMI, category (GDM or control), and OGTT75 g values at the time of diagnosis, the univariate analysis revealed a significant association between SAF, age, category (GDM or control), and the 1-h OGTT75 g value. The multivariate analysis confirmed that age and category (GDM or control) were independent predictors of SAF (β = 0.165, p = 0.023; β = 0.215, p = 0.012, respectively) (Table 2).

Receiver operating characteristic curve

To predict the need for insulin therapy in the GDM group based on SAF, a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was constructed, as shown in Figure 2. The area under the curve (AUC) was 0.849 (95% CI: 0.727–0.971), p < 0.001. The maximum Youden index indicated that a SAF >2.3 AU was the optimal cut-off point for predicting insulin therapy requirement with a sensitivity of 90.9% and a specificity of 69.7% (p < 0.001). According to our results, the positive predictive value (PPV) was 27.0% and the negative predictive value (NPV) was 98.4%.

Discussion

This is the first study conducted on an Arab population residing in Tunisia, North Africa, evaluating the profile of tissue accumulation of AGEs in pregnant women with gestational diabetes mellitus compared to those without diabetes. The study highlighted several significant differences between pregnant women with and without gestational diabetes mellitus, particularly regarding age, body mass index, family history of diabetes and cardiovascular diseases, and personal history of GDM. These results are consistent with the existing literature, which identifies advanced age, obesity, family history of diabetes and cardiovascular diseases, as well as personal history of gestational diabetes mellitus, as risk factors for gestational diabetes mellitus.2,3

Regarding skin autofluorescence, the study identified significantly higher values in women with gestational diabetes mellitus, indicating an increased tissue accumulation of advanced glycation end products. This finding aligns with previous studies demonstrating that diabetes is associated with elevated SAF levels, driven by hyperglycemia and impaired glucose metabolism.15,17,18 Similar increases in serum AGEs levels have also been observed in women with GDM compared to healthy pregnant women.19,20 In a study by Guosheng et al., 19 the relationship between maternal serum AGE levels and adverse fetal outcomes was explored. They reported significantly higher AGE levels in women with GDM during mid-to-late pregnancy compared to a control group. 19 Furthermore, Maury et al. demonstrated that among women with gestational diabetes, 71 had a history of hyperglycemia (i.e., previous gestational diabetes, macrosomia in a previous pregnancy, or diabetes diagnosed before the 24th week of gestation in the current pregnancy). 21 These women exhibited higher skin autofluorescence levels (1.83 ± 0.35 AU) compared to women with gestational diabetes without a history of hyperglycemia (1.73 ± 0.30 AU; p = 0.04, non-significant, adjusted for age). Additionally, the authors found that skin autofluorescence increased with the number of criteria for previous hyperglycemia (trend p = 0.008) and was significantly associated with the presence of two or three hyperglycemia criteria after adjusting for age (p = 0.02). 21

Conversely, Ranitez et al. found no significant difference in SAF levels among women with GDM, suggesting that hyperglycemia in these patients might be too mild to induce rapid AGEs accumulation. 22 However, AGE accumulation has been reported even in cases of moderate hyperglycemia, including in well-controlled patients with type 2 diabetes or impaired glucose tolerance.23,24 Ranitez et al. proposed that the relatively short duration of glycemic exposure—gestational diabetes typically arises during the second trimester—might also limit AGEs buildup. While AGEs accumulation is generally a gradual process, evidence of short-term accumulation has also been reported. 22 The differences observed in our study may reflect ethnic variations compared to the population in Ranitez’s research. The authors have already highlighted significant difference in SAF levels between white Europeans and other ethnic groups, with white Europeans appearing less prone to risks associated with skin AGEs accumulation. 22

Our study showed also a significant relationship was observed in the univariate analysis between 1-h OGTT values and SAF levels. This finding aligns with another study, who demonstrated that as glucose dysregulation worsens, the proportion of women with elevated SAF levels increases. 25 For instance, 41.7% of women without gestational diabetes had SAF levels above the mean +1 standard deviation for their age, while this proportion reached 54.8% in women with GDM. Moreover, the percentage of women with SAF levels exceeding the mean +2 standard deviations increased from 8.3% in the non-GDM group to 25.8% in the GDM group. These results suggest a clear association between the severity of glucose dysregulation and skin AGEs accumulation. Specifically, the greater the disruption in glucose regulation, as seen in gestational diabetes, the higher the SAF levels, reflecting prolonged exposure to elevated glucose concentrations. 25 This accumulation may indicate an underlying mechanism of tissue damage linked to chronic hyperglycemia. In this context, skin autofluorescence could serve as an effective biomarker to assess the impact of glucose exposure prior to pregnancy and to identify women at higher risk for complications due to impaired glucose regulation. 25

According to our findings, the optimal threshold of the SAF value (2.3) for predicting insulin therapy requirement to achieve glycemic control in women with GDM suggests that skin autofluorescence could be used as a potential marker to detect abnormalities in glucose metabolism, with high sensitivity (90.9%) and moderate specificity (69.7%). Although the SAF cut-off demonstrated high sensitivity and negative predictive value, the positive predictive value was low (27%), limiting its ability to predict which women would require insulin therapy. These findings suggest that SAF measurement may be more useful for ruling out the need for insulin, rather than positively identifying women who will require it. The association between SAF and elevated glucose levels may explain this part of the results, emphasizing the importance of monitoring SAF as an indicator of altered glucose metabolism in pregnant women with gestational diabetes.

The present study has some limitations. Skin pigmentation may affect measurement, which limits the generalisability of our findings to populations with different skin types. Also, we did not have any information on fructosamine or HbA1c levels from the participants, so these measurements are very limited in clinical practice at the Center for Maternity. Additionally, we were unable to follow up with our participants to measure SAF after childbirth.

Conclusion

This study conducted on Tunisian pregnant women demonstrated an increase in SAF levels among subjects with gestational diabetes compared to healthy subjects. The SAF value for insulin therapy requirement revealed that an optimal threshold above 2.3 AU could serve as a prognostic marker for identifying women with GDM who are unlikely to require insulin therapy. Further studies on a larger sample and a more diverse population are necessary to confirm these findings. Additionally, longitudinal studies are crucial to determine whether SAF levels change during pregnancy and if there are differences in these changes between normal pregnancies and those complicated by gestational diabetes.
