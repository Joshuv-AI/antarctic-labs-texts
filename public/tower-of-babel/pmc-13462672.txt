Associations Between Caffeinated Beverage Consumption and Blood Pressure Parameters in Nurses Based on 24‐Hour Ambulatory Blood Pressure Monitoring: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Suzhi Zhang, Jie Liu, Huiling Liu, Wenjia Liu, Ni Chen, Yufang Zhu, Yaping Wang, Xiaolin Zhang, Bin Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsNurses frequently consume caffeinated beverages to manage fatigue, but their associations with ambulatory blood pressure are unclear. This cross‐sectional study examined associations between caffeinated coffee, tea, and energy drinks and blood pressure parameters in nurses.MethodsA total of 335 nurses from a tertiary hospital completed questionnaires on beverage consumption (past month and during 24‐h monitoring) and underwent 24‐h ambulatory blood pressure monitoring. Blood pressure levels (24‐h, awake, asleep) and short‐term blood pressure variability were measured. Generalized linear regression was employed to analyze associations between beverage consumption and blood pressure, with Benjamini‐Hochberg FDR correction (q < 0.10) for exploratory analyses.ResultsBefore correction, several coffee/tea patterns and energy drinks were associated with blood pressure levels or blood pressure variability (p < 0.05). After FDR correction, three associations remained statistically significant: medium‐frequency high‐volume coffee (β = 4.951, 95% CI: 1.714–8.188, p = 0.003) and high‐frequency high‐volume coffee (β = 3.599, 95% CI: 1.057–6.142, p = 0.005) were positively associated with short‐term systolic blood pressure variability; high‐frequency low‐volume coffee was positively associated with asleep mean systolic blood pressure (β = 10.680, 95% CI: 3.920–17.439, p = 0.002). Observed differences ranged from approximately 3–5 mmHg, with a 10 mmHg effect for the asleep BP association. These observed differences (3–5 mmHg) are considered clinically meaningful in the context of population‐level cardiovascular risk. All other associations (e.g., tea, energy drinks) were no longer significant after correction (q ≥ 0.10).ConclusionSpecific high‐volume coffee patterns (medium‐ and high‐frequency) were associated with increased short‐term systolic BPV, while high‐frequency low‐volume coffee was associated with higher asleep systolic blood pressure. Other exploratory findings did not survive FDR correction. These results are exploratory and require prospective validation.Keywords: ambulatory blood pressure monitoring, blood pressure level, caffeine, nurses, short‐term blood pressure variability

Introduction

Hypertension is a leading global cause of premature mortality [1]. Blood pressure (BP) is a dynamic cardiovascular indicator influenced by behavioral and environmental factors [2]. 24‐h ambulatory blood pressure monitoring (ABPM) records BP levels and fluctuations at fixed intervals over a full day using an automated device, and is currently recommended as the gold standard for BP assessment by multiple guidelines [2, 3, 4]. BP levels and short‐term blood pressure variability (BPV) are two core indicators derived from ABPM, both of which independently predict cardiovascular events [5, 6].

Nurses face multiple occupational stressors, including rotating shifts, circadian disruption, chronic stress, sleep deprivation, and irregular lifestyles [7, 8]. These factors collectively disturb autonomic balance—manifesting as increased sympathetic activity, blunted nocturnal BP dipping, elevated BPV, and impaired baroreflex sensitivity [9, 10]. Such autonomic dysregulation may sensitize the cardiovascular system to exogenous pressor agents, amplifying their potential impact on BP.

Notably, caffeine is one of the most commonly consumed exogenous pressor substances among nurses. To cope with high occupational demands, many nurses rely on caffeinated beverages such as coffee, tea, or energy drinks to maintain alertness [11]. However, beyond caffeine, each beverage contains distinct bioactive compounds: tea is rich in catechins, which inhibit angiotensin‐converting enzyme and promote vasodilation; coffee provides chlorogenic acids with antioxidant properties [12]; energy drinks contain taurine and high levels of sugar, with taurine exerting complex BP effects and excess sugar linked to insulin resistance and hypertension [13, 14]. The interplay between caffeine and these other ingredients determines the net BP effect of each beverage type. Therefore, investigating the associations between caffeinated beverage consumption and BP in nurses holds unique scientific value.

In addition, BP regulation and short‐term BPV are influenced by multiple behavioral and lifestyle factors, including sleep quality, meal timing, dietary habits, occupational stress, physical activity, and socioeconomic determinants [15, 16]. These factors may affect BP independently or interactively, and may also confound the association between caffeinated beverage consumption and BP. For instance, caffeine intake may disrupt sleep, and insufficient sleep itself is associated with sympathetic activation and elevated BP; thus, caffeine may affect BP indirectly through its influence on sleep. Therefore, without accounting for these factors, observed caffeine‐BP associations may partly reflect lifestyle clustering rather than the independent effect of caffeine.

This study aims to evaluate BP levels and short‑term BPV in nurses using 24‑h ABPM and to explore associations with coffee, tea, and energy drinks, providing evidence for beverage recommendations in high‑stress occupations.

Methods

Study Design

This study aimed to determine the association between caffeinated beverages and 24‐h ambulatory BP in nurses. The study design and reporting followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guideline [17].

Participants

This cross‐sectional study employed convenience sampling to recruit 427 nurses from a tertiary hospital in Hebei Province between June 2024 and April 2025. Inclusion criteria were: (1) registered nurses in clinical practice; (2) aged ≥ 18 years; (3) no diagnosed mental or physical illnesses; and (4) provided informed consent. Exclusion criteria were pregnancy, medical leave, or participation in advanced degree programs during the study period.

Data Collection

This study used a self‐developed questionnaire and ABPM to collect data. Although the questionnaire was developed through multiple rounds of expert discussions and its response categories were based on authoritative literature, it did not undergo formal validity testing, which is a limitation of this study.

General Data Collection Methods

Consumption of Caffeinated Beverages in the Past Month

The questionnaire assessed consumption of caffeinated coffee, tea, and energy drinks over the past month, including frequency (never, 1–3/month, 1–2/week, 3–4/week, 5–6/week, 1/day, 2/day, ≥ 3/day) and amount per serving (< 50 mL, 51–100 mL, 101–150 mL, 151–200 mL, 201–250 mL, > 250 mL). Based on the 200 mL threshold—informed by the Chinese Dietary Guidelines (standard cup ≈ 150 mL), previous caffeine research (≤ 200 mL/day as low intake), and the approximate median intake in our sample—and frequency categories derived from the previous studies [18, 19, 20], the original frequency options were consolidated into three groups (< 1/week, 1–2/week, ≥ 3/week) according to response distribution and prior literature.

Caffeinated Coffee and tea consumption patterns were categorized as follows [21, 22]:

a.“No”: Never drink;

“No”: Never drink;

b.“Low frequency and low volume intake group” (LL): < 1 time/week, ≤ 200 mL per occasion;

“Low frequency and low volume intake group” (LL): < 1 time/week, ≤ 200 mL per occasion;

c.“Low frequency and high volume intake group” (LH): < 1 time/week, > 200 mL per occasion;

“Low frequency and high volume intake group” (LH): < 1 time/week, > 200 mL per occasion;

d.“Medium frequency and low volume intake group” (ML): 1–2 times/week, ≤ 200 mL per occasion;

“Medium frequency and low volume intake group” (ML): 1–2 times/week, ≤ 200 mL per occasion;

e.“Medium frequency and high volume intake group” (MH): 1–2 times/week, > 200 mL per occasion;

“Medium frequency and high volume intake group” (MH): 1–2 times/week, > 200 mL per occasion;

f.“High frequency and low volume intake group” (HL): ≥ 3 times/week, ≤ 200 mL per occasion;

“High frequency and low volume intake group” (HL): ≥ 3 times/week, ≤ 200 mL per occasion;

g.“High frequency and high volume intake group” (HH): ≥ 3 times/week, > 200 mL per occasion.

“High frequency and high volume intake group” (HH): ≥ 3 times/week, > 200 mL per occasion.

Energy Drinks Consumption: Due to the low number of energy drink consumers (n = 15), participants were simply classified as either consumers or nonconsumers.

Consumption of Caffeinated Beverages During 24‐h Monitoring Period

Consumption of caffeinated coffee, tea, and energy drinks during the 24‐h monitoring period was recorded using an ABPM diary card. Based on these records, intake of each beverage type was classified as either consumption or nonconsumption.

Other Relevant Factors

Demographic characteristics and potential factors influencing BP were collected via an online questionnaire. These included sex, age, height, weight, marital status, department, use of antihypertensive medications, personal and family history of hypertension, sleep patterns over the past month (the duration of nighttime sleep, frequency of difficulty in falling asleep, and nocturnal awakening or early awakening), dietary habits (salt intake, time interval between the first and last meal each day, midnight snacking, and consumption of carbonated beverages), and exercise situation. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in meters.

ABPM diary cards documented work shift, work position, sleep duration, meal interval, midnight snacking, and exercise during monitoring.

24‐h ABPM

A validated oscillometric ambulatory sphygmomanometer was used. The cuff was positioned 2–3 cm above the cubital fossa, allowing one to two fingers to fit beneath. The arm with higher BP was used if interarm difference exceeded 10 mmHg; otherwise, the nondominant arm was used. Measurements were programmed at 30‐min intervals during daytime (6:00 a.m.–10:00 p.m.) and hourly at night (10:00 p.m.–6:00 a.m.). After 24 h, the device was retrieved, and two manual measurements verified performance. Data completeness required ≥ 70% valid readings over 24 h, including ≥ 20 awake and ≥ 7 asleep measurements.

Calculation Methods for Outcome Indicators

BP levels

Mean BP was calculated for 24‐h, awake, and sleep periods based on valid measurements. Awake and sleep periods were defined according to diary card records.

Short‐Term BPV

Short‐term BPV, which encompasses both short‐term systolic BPV (SBPV) and short‐term diastolic BPV (DBPV), was evaluated using the 24‐h average real variability (24hARV). This metric was determined by calculating the average of the absolute differences in BP values recorded over 24‐h monitoring period.

Quality Control

All researchers received standardized training. A uniform protocol was followed. Questionnaires and diary cards were distributed systematically; researchers supervised questionnaire completion on‐site and collected diary cards after monitoring. Devices were regularly inspected and calibrated. Two researchers independently entered data daily; missing or anomalous entries were followed up immediately. Weekly checks and a final comprehensive review ensured data integrity.

Statistical Analysis

We have specified that data were entered and cleaned using Microsoft Excel 2021, main statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 27.0 (IBM Corp.), and the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction was implemented using R version 4.6.0 (R Core Team) with the p. adjust function. All tests were two‐sided, and a p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

This study addressed the following primary research questions, each with a corresponding analytical approach. All statistical analyses and reporting were performed in accordance with the Guidelines for Reporting of Statistics for Clinical Research in Urology and the Statistical Analyses [23] and Statistical Analyses and Methods in the Published Literature (SAMPL) guidelines [24]. The analyses were classified as prespecified or exploratory as follows:

Prespecified analysis: The association between overall caffeinated beverage consumption (yes vs. no) and each BP outcome was defined a priori as the primary research question.

Exploratory analyses: All other analyses, including the associations between specific beverage consumption patterns (coffee, tea, energy drinks, with detailed frequency and volume categories) and blood pressure outcomes, were exploratory. These analyses were not preregistered, and no prior hypotheses were made regarding the direction or magnitude of the associations. To account for multiple testing, the Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction was applied to p‐values from the exploratory analyses, with a FDR level set at q < 0.10. After correction, associations with q < 0.10 were considered statistically significant; those with original p < 0.05 but q ≥ 0.10 were interpreted as suggestive findings requiring independent validation.

The following subsections describe each analytical approach in detail.

Comparison of Baseline Characteristics Between Caffeinated and Noncaffeinated Beverage Consumers

Participants were categorized into two groups: noncaffeinated beverage consumers and caffeinated beverage consumers. Continuous variables were tested for normality. Normally distributed variables were presented as mean and standard deviation (SD) and compared using the independent samples t‐test. Nonnormally distributed continuous variables were summarized as median and interquartile range M(IQR) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U‐test. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies (percentages) and analyzed using the chi‐square test.

Association Between Overall Caffeinated Beverage Consumption and BP Outcomes

The primary outcomes were BP level, and short‐term BPV. Generalized linear regression (GLM) models with an identity link function were fitted for two exposure factors: (i) consumption over the past month, and (ii) consumption during the 24‐h monitoring period. Covariates were prespecified based on biological plausibility and previous literature. The following covariates were prespecified based on biological plausibility and previous literature and included in all GLMs: demographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, clinical history, and ABPM‐related variables. Association Between Specific Beverage Types (Coffee, Tea, Energy Drinks) With Different Consumption Patterns and BP Outcomes

For coffee and tea, consumption patterns were categorized into seven groups based on frequency and volume (No, LL, LH, ML, MH, HL, HH), as defined in the Data collection section. For energy drinks, due to the small number of consumers (n = 15), participants were simply classified as consumers or nonconsumers. Each beverage type and pattern was entered as an independent variable in GLMs, with the same adjustment covariates as described in Section 2.6.2. Models were run separately for past month consumption and for consumption during the 24‐h monitoring period.

Ethics Approval

The research was reviewed and approved by the ethics committee at the Second Hospital of Hebei Medical University (2024‐R517) and conformed to the ethical standards for medical research involving human subjects, as outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. All participants provided their informed consent.

Result

Statistical Description and Univariate Analysis

Of 427 enrolled nurses, 92 were excluded due to incomplete BP data, leaving 335 participants. Of these, 93.1% (312/335) were female, with median age 36 years (IQR: 33.0–40.0) and median BMI 23.0 kg/m2 (IQR: 21.0–25.0). There were 168 noncaffeinated and 167 caffeinated consumers. Significant differences between groups were observed in age, midnight snacking, family history of hypertension, and work position during monitoring (p < 0.05). Detailed results are in Table 1.

Associations of Overall Caffeinated Beverage Consumption With BP levels and Short‐term BPV

We did not find evidence of a statistically significant association between overall caffeinated beverage consumption and any BP level (p > 0.05). However, consumption during the 24‐h monitoring period was associated with higher short‐term SBPV. Detailed results are presented in Table 2.

Discussion

The Mean BP Levels of the Nurses

Based on 24‐h ABPM criteria (24‐h mean ≥ 130/80, awake ≥ 135/85, asleep ≥ 120/70 mmHg [2], all BP measurements in our cohort remained below these thresholds: 24‐h mean 112.04 ± 12.31/73.07 ± 9.19 mmHg, awake 115.71 ± 9.69/75.30 ± 8.36 mmHg, asleep 105.03 ± 11.84/66.16 ± 9.09 mmHg. SBPV averaged 12.89 ± 4.80 mmHg and DBPV 12.11 ± 4.87 mmHg. These findings align with a large‐scale cross‐sectional survey conducted by our research group, which reported a hypertension prevalence of 6.86% among nurses [25], indicating a relatively low overall rate. The consistently lower BP levels observed in this cohort may be attributed to the relatively young age (all under 60 years) and predominantly female composition of the sample.

Associations of Overall Caffeinated Beverage Consumption With BP and Short‐term BPV

We did not find a statistically significant association between overall caffeinated beverage consumption and BP levels, but we observed that caffeinated beverage intake during the 24‐h monitoring period was associated with higher short‐term SBPV. A systematic review reported that an increase in BP occurs within the first 60 min after ingestion of 200–300 mg of caffeine, and this effect persists for the subsequent 180 min [22], which is broadly consistent with our findings. It is known that caffeine can increase BP by blocking adenosine receptors and stimulating the release of dopamine and norepinephrine [26]. In this study, for the BP outcome—mean BP level—the acute pressor effect of caffeine may last only a few hours, which might not be sufficient to significantly alter the full‐day average BP. This interpretation also suggests that although caffeinated beverages may be associated with transient acute increases in BP, their impact on overall mean BP may be limited.

Associations of Specific Beverage Types With BP Levels and Short‐term BPV

After FDR correction, only three associations remained: MH and HH coffee with higher short‐term SBPV, and HL coffee with higher asleep mean SBP. All other nominal associations (LL coffee, tea, energy drinks) were no longer significant and should be interpreted as suggestive.

Coffee Consumption and BP

Before correction, LL coffee was associated with higher awake and 24‐h BP (≈ 5 mmHg), consistent with infrequent drinkers lacking caffeine tolerance [27, 28]. Mechanistically, repeated caffeine exposure upregulates adenosine receptors and enhances sympathetic tone adaptation; lack of regular intake leaves the acute pressor response unopposed, leading to transient BP elevations [29].

HL coffee was associated with higher asleep BP (≈10 mmHg), remaining significant after FDR correction. This larger effect during sleep may reflect circadian autonomic dysregulation, with habitual caffeine shifting sympathetic–parasympathetic balance toward sympathetic predominance during sleep [30] or disrupted sleep quality contributing to higher nocturnal BP.

MH and HH coffee were associated with higher short‐term SBPV—but not mean BP—suggesting coffee may affect BP stability rather than absolute values. This novel finding may reflect increased beat‐to‐beat BPV through sympathetic overactivity and impaired baroreflex sensitivity [31].

Tea Consumption and BP

Before correction, LH tea was associated with lower 24‐h (≈3 mmHg) and asleep (≈4 mmHg) BP, while ML tea showed a nonsignificant trend toward higher BP. One possible mechanistic hypothesis, based on previous experimental and observational studies, is that tea polyphenols (e.g., catechins) may inhibit angiotensin‐converting enzyme and promote vasodilation, thereby counteracting the pressor effects of caffeine [32, 33, 34]. However, achieving a sufficient concentration of these bioactive compounds may require a larger single serving, because tea polyphenols have unstable absorption in the digestive tract and low intestinal permeability [35]. This could explain why lower‐volume intake did not show a clear benefit and even exhibited a tendency toward higher BP. However, our study did not measure tea polyphenol metabolites, ACE activity, or any other biological markers. Therefore, this interpretation remains speculative and should be viewed as hypothesis‑generating rather than confirmatory.

These opposite associations cannot be attributed solely to tea polyphenols. Alternative explanations include residual confounding from unmeasured lifestyle factors (e.g., diet, physical activity, socioeconomic status) that may cluster with specific tea consumption patterns; reverse causation, whereby nurses with higher BP may have reduced their tea intake; and tea preparation variations (type, brewing time, additives) that affect polyphenol bioavailability—details not captured by our questionnaire. Thus, these pattern‑dependent associations should be interpreted with caution, and prospective studies with detailed dietary assessment are needed to clarify causality.

After FDR correction, none of the tea‐associated BP differences remained significant. Thus, these findings are hypothesis‐generating and require prospective validation. Future studies with larger samples and standardized protocols are needed to determine whether higher‐volume tea consumption confers consistent BP benefits or may produce opposite effects under different conditions.

Energy Drinks and BP

Before correction, energy drink consumption was associated with higher 24‐h DBP and, during the monitoring period, with higher short‐term SBPV. These associations are consistent with prior systematic reviews [36] and may be attributable to the combination of caffeine, taurine, and high sugar content [37]. However, after FDR correction, none of these associations remained significant, likely due to the small number of energy drink consumers (n = 15) and the limited statistical power. Thus, our results regarding energy drinks are only suggestive and require replication in larger samples.

Potential Influences of Sodium Intake, Hydration Status, and Ultra‐processed Beverages

This study focused on the associations between consumption patterns of coffee, tea, and energy drinks and BP. However, when interpreting our findings, other relevant nutritional factors should be considered. First, coffee, tea, and energy drinks contain sodium, dose‐response meta‐analyses have demonstrated a near‐linear relationship between reduced dietary sodium intake and decreased BP [38]. However, given the relatively low sodium content of the beverages examined in this study [39], the observed BP differences are unlikely to be directly attributable to sodium intake from these beverages. Second, the diuretic effect of caffeine may influence hydration status [40], thereby modulating BP. Hypohydration has been shown to impair endothelial function, increase sympathetic nervous system activity, and impair orthostatic tolerance, and has been associated with an increased risk of adverse cardiovascular events [41]. Theoretically, caffeine‐induced mild dehydration could lead to reduced blood volume; however, its acute pressor effect may be more pronounced. The complex interplay between caffeine intake, hydration status, and BP warrants further investigation. Finally, according to the NOVA classification system, energy drinks fall into the category of ultra‐processed foods [42]. Beyond caffeine, they often contain high levels of sugar and other additives. Systematic reviews and meta‐analyses have shown that high consumption of ultra‐processed foods is associated with an increased risk of hypertension [43]. Therefore, the pressor effects of energy drinks may not be attributable solely to caffeine; their ultra‐processed characteristics, such as high sugar content, also warrant attention. Future studies should collect detailed information on sugar and various additive intakes to more accurately delineate the independent and interactive effects of different components in energy drinks on BP.

Clinical Significance of the Observed BP Differences

The observed BP differences in this study ranged from approximately 3–5 mmHg, with high‐frequency low‐volume coffee associated with an approximately 10 mmHg higher asleep SBP (FDR‐corrected). Although modest, large‐scale evidence shows that a 5 mmHg systolic reduction lowers major cardiovascular events by ~10% [44], and a 2 mmHg diastolic reduction would decrease hypertension prevalence by 17% and coronary heart disease risk by 6% [45]. A continuous log‐linear relationship between BP and cardiovascular risk exists from 115/75 mmHg [46, 47], and in the Chinese population, high‐normal BP is associated with a 28% increase in cardiovascular disease mortality [48]. The 10 mmHg effect observed for high‐frequency low‐volume coffee exceeds the threshold for meaningful BP change. However, because our study is cross‐sectional, these findings are hypothesis‐generating, and causality cannot be inferred. Prospective studies are needed to confirm the clinical relevance of the observed associations.

All mechanistic interpretations discussed herein are based on previous literature and are presented as hypotheses to guide future research. Because our study did not directly measure any biological or biochemical parameters, these interpretations should not be considered evidence of causality or mechanism.

Strengths and Limitations

Strengths

This study used scientifically standardized 24‐h ABPM, which provides objective and reliable BP data. We examined both long‐term (past month) and acute (24‐h monitoring) consumption windows, allowing differentiation of chronic tolerance from acute effects. The focus on nurses—an occupational group with medical knowledge—enhances the internal validity of self‐reported data.

Limitations

(1) Cross‐sectional design: Causal inference is precluded; reverse causation and residual confounding cannot be excluded. All findings are exploratory and hypothesis‐generating. (2) Multiple comparisons: Extensive testing increases type I error risk. However, FDR correction (q < 0.10) was applied to exploratory analyses, and nonsignificant results are reported as suggestive. Although the ≥ 3/week group may mask moderate/daily differences, the dose‐response pattern (HH: β = 4.951; HL: β = 10.680) suggests our main findings are robust. (3) Unmeasured confounders: Data on smoking, alcohol, hormonal status, psychological stress, dietary potassium, hydration status, and reproductive factors were not collected. However, a previous survey conducted by our research group at the same hospital (n = 2572) showed a very low prevalence of these factors (all < 2%), limiting their confounding impact. (4) Questionnaire validity: The beverage questionnaire was self‐developed and not formally validated; recall bias and measurement error are possible. Although it was based on expert input and literature, this remains a limitation. (5) Caffeine quantification: Caffeine exposure was assessed only by volume and frequency, without conversion to daily milligram intake. The questionnaire was designed to capture real‐world beverage consumption patterns rather than precise compositional analysis; therefore, details such as preparation methods and additives were not collected, which may have led to exposure misclassification. (6) Single‐center and limited generalizability: The study was conducted at a single tertiary hospital in Hebei Province with a predominantly female sample (93.1%), limiting generalizability to male nurses or other settings. (7) Small sample for energy drinks: Only 15 participants consumed energy drinks, limiting statistical power; findings should be interpreted with caution. (8) Missing data: Of 427 enrolled nurses, 92 (21.5%) were excluded due to intolerance to the ABPM device; this is unlikely to be directly related to BP or beverage intake, but selection bias cannot be entirely ruled out. No imputation was performed.

Conclusion

Based on 24‐h ABPM data, after FDR correction, we identified three robust associations: medium‐frequency high‐volume and high‐frequency high‐volume coffee were associated with higher short‐term SBPV, and high‐frequency low‐volume coffee was associated with higher asleep mean SBP. Overall caffeinated beverage intake during the 24‐h monitoring period was also associated with higher short‐term SBPV. All other associations, including those with tea and energy drinks, did not survive multiple‐testing correction and should be interpreted as hypothesis‐generating. Owing to the cross‐sectional design, causality cannot be inferred. The differential associations between various caffeinated beverages and blood pressure parameters highlight the need for prospective studies to confirm these findings before any clinical recommendations can be made.
