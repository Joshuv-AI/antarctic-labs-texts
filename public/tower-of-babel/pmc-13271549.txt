Cavitary retinoblastoma: Clinical features and treatment outcomes
Authors: Nakul Verma, Rohan Kapoor, Vijitha S Vempuluru, P Vijay Anand Reddy, Vishal R Raval, Swathi Kaliki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Purpose:To describe clinical characteristics and treatment outcomes of children diagnosed with cavitary retinoblastoma (RB).Methods:Retrospective analysis of children treated for cavitary RB.Results:Of the 40 patients (43 eyes) with cavitary RB, 14 (35%) were female and 26 (65%) were male. At diagnosis, the average age was 25 months (median, 24 months; range 3–84 months). In 3 cases (8%), bilateral cavitary RB was seen. The most prevalent presenting symptoms were leukocoria (n = 21, 53%) and strabismus (n = 6, 15%) with a mean duration of symptoms of 23 weeks (median, 16 weeks; range 0–104 weeks). As per the ICRB classification, 15 eyes (35%) were in group D, 12 eyes (28%) in group C, 10 eyes (23%) in group B, and 6 eyes (14%) in group E. Primary cavities, within the tumor, were seen in 14 eyes (33%), whereas secondary cavities were seen in 29 eyes (67%). Most of the eyes (n = 42, 98%) were treated with intravenous chemotherapy (IVC), and one eye (2%) received intra-arterial chemotherapy. In ten eyes (23%), the tumor recurred following IVC, and two cases exhibited a partial response to IVC. At the last follow-up (mean, 31 months), all eyes (100%) had local tumor control with no evidence of systemic metastasis or death. Regression of the cavities within the tumor was noted in 27 eyes (63%), while it remained stable in 16 eyes (37%).Conclusion:Cavitary RB (both primary and secondary) had a good initial response to standard chemotherapy with an overall excellent prognosis for globe salvage.

The most prevalent malignant intraocular tumor in children, the retinoblastoma (RB), typically manifests before the age of five.[1] It occurs due to mutations in the RB gene, RB1 located on (13q14.2) chromosome.[2] About 1 in 16,000 live births suffer from this condition, and between 8000 and 10,000 children are diagnosed each year.[3] Apart from Japan, half of the new cases identified each year come from Asia, with India accounting for up to 1500–2000 of these cases.[4] In developed countries, the goal of treatment has shifted from globe salvage to vision preservation. In contrast, in underdeveloped countries, which account for more than 90% of RB children, the intraocular tumor often goes undiagnosed and presents with advanced disease threatening the globe.[35]

Cavitary retinoblastoma, a rare variant of RB, is characterized by the existence of translucent cavities within the tumor mass that are visible ophthalmoscopically.[67] It has been found that between 2% and 4% of RB tumors develop cavities. These cavities show up on ultrasound as acoustically hollow or hypoechoic cavities and are hypofluorescent on fluorescein angiography.[678] Cavitary RB tumors grow slowly and have a highly distinct histology.[9] The term “cavitary variant” was coined since histopathological reports from earlier research showed that there were no endothelium or epithelial cells lining the lesions.[9] In-depth information is lacking in the present literature, especially about the cavity’s origin, its benign or premalignant character, etiology, any genetic alterations, and the varied response to chemotherapy.[8] A recent study by Xu et al.[10] has reported diminished MYCN expression inducing formation of a less aggressive, well-differentiated cavitary RB with a more favorable prognosis.

In this study, we aim to analyze the demographics, clinical presentation, and treatment outcomes of patients with cavitary RB presenting to a referral center in India.

Methods

A retrospective study was conducted at a referral eye care center in India. Institutional review board approval was obtained for the study. A computerized database search was performed for all medical records of patients treated for cavitary RB from March 2017 to June 2024. The study included patients with sufficient clinical data and tumor grouping information at the initial visit. The participants with insufficient clinical data were excluded.

Age, sex, and tumor laterality were reviewed among the documented demographic information. Presenting complaints and family history of RB were noted. The Ret Cam II widefield imaging equipment (Clarity Medical Systems Inc.) was used for photographic documentation in every instance, and fundus drawings were used to record every tumor. To ensure precise tumor features, both fundus drawings and wide field photos were reviewed.

Cavitary RB was defined as the presence of a solid tumor mass with translucent cavities, which were visible on fundus examination. The International Classification of Intraocular Retinoblastoma (ICRB) was used to categorize all eyes with an intraocular tumor.[11] Information about the presence of cavitary RB at presentation (primary type) or during treatment (secondary type) was recorded. The ‘primary cavities’ were defined as those presented in treatment naïve tumors, while ‘secondary cavities’ were defined as the ones that became clinically apparent during tumor management. The central cavities were defined as those situated within the central two-thirds of the tumor surface. In contrast, “peripheral cavities” were defined as those situated within the peripheral one-third of the tumor surface.[6] Additional characteristics were recorded, including the number of cavities, tumor location, changes in appearance of the cavities during treatment, and whether the tumor regressed or remained stable. Intravenous chemotherapy (IVC), intra-arterial chemotherapy (IAC), transpupillary thermotherapy (TTT), cryotherapy, external beam radiation, and enucleation were among the key treatment aspects that were documented. In accordance with the institute’s protocol, the IVC regimen, including six cycles of vincristine, etoposide, and carboplatin given every 3 weeks, was used to treat all bilateral patients.[12] Every unilateral instance was given the choice between IVC and IAC, and the parents’ decision determined the course of therapy. Very advanced tumors were treated with primary enucleation. At the ocular oncologist’s discretion, focal TTT was administered to the tumor’s nonleading edge in the macular region, sparing the fovea, following one to two cycles of chemotherapy, or as an adjuvant treatment following the completion of six cycles of IVC. A diode laser (810 nm) (Iridex Corp., Mountain View, CA) was used to administer TTT. The indirect ophthalmoscope and a 20 D lens were used to aim the 1.2 mm spot size lasers through a pharmacologically dilated pupil. Initially, the laser’s strength was set at 300 mW, and it was then adjusted as needed until a gray-white burn was achieved.

Every tumor recurrence and its treatment were documented. Atrophic changes in the macular area and tumor regression patterns were documented after treatment as: Type 0 (no discernible remnants), type I (completely calcified tumor), type II (fleshy tissue without calcification), type III (mixed calcific and fleshy tumor), or type IV (atrophic chorioretinal scar).[13] A residual patch of active tumor within the inactive area was considered partial regression of the tumor. Local tumor control, changes in the cavity’s appearance at the last follow-up, visual potential (if applicable), and globe salvage were the primary outcome measures. The fovea’s anatomic integrity served as the basis for evaluating visual potential.

SPSS ver. 17.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) was used for all statistical analyses. Statistical significance was assigned at P ≤ 0.05. The Fisher exact test or χ2 test was used to compare the primary and secondary cavities, and all proportions were displayed as numbers and percentages. The χ2 test was performed to look for any association between primary and secondary variants with any clinical and treatment-related factors.

Results

Forty-three eyes of 40 children with cavitary RB were studied retrospectively [Table 1]. The mean age was 25 months (median, 24 months; range, 3–84 months). Twenty-six children were male (65%), and 14 were female (35%). Fourteen children (35%) had unilateral RB, and 26 children (65%) had bilateral RB. Unilateral cavitary RB was seen in 37 cases (93%), and bilateral were seen in 3 cases (7%). Hereditary RB was seen in 3 patients (7%). Most of the cases presented with symptoms of leukocoria (n = 21, 53%), strabismus (n = 6, 15%), leukocoria with strabismus (n = 4, 10%), poor vision (n = 2, 5%) and redness (n = 2, 5%). The mean duration of symptoms was 23 weeks (median, 16 weeks; range, 0–104 weeks). The location less than or equal to 3 mm from the optic disc was seen in 37 eyes (86%) and >3 mm from the optic disc in 6 eyes (14%). The tumors were located ≤3 mm from the fovea in 29 eyes (67%) and >3 mm from the fovea in 14 eyes (33%). The tumor covered the entire optic disc in 11 eyes (26%) and partly in 3 eyes (7%). The tumor covered the entire fovea in 19 eyes (44%) and partly in 2 eyes (5%). Localized subretinal fluid (SRF) was seen in 18 eyes (42%). Subretinal fluid was seen in all four quadrants in 9 eyes (21%), two quadrants in 5 eyes (12%), and one quadrant in 4 eyes (7%). Similarly, vitreous seeding was seen in all four quadrants in 5 eyes (12%), two quadrants in 5 eyes (12%), and one quadrant in 4 eyes (9%). Subretinal seeding was seen in all four quadrants in 7 eyes (16%), two quadrants in 2 eyes (5%), and one quadrant in 6 eyes (14%). None of the cases had vitreous hemorrhage. As per the ICRB classification, 15 eyes (35%) were in group D, 12 eyes (28%) in group C, 10 eyes (23%) in group B, and 6 eyes (14%) in group E. Based on clinical appearance, the tumor was endophytic in 29 eyes (67%), exophytic in 9 eyes (21%), and mixed in 5 eyes (12%). Of the 43 eyes, 29 eyes (67%) had secondary cavities, and 14 eyes (33%) had primary cavities [Table 2]. Within the tumor, central cavities were observed in 23 eyes (54%), peripheral in 11 eyes (26%), and both central and peripheral in 9 eyes (21%).

Primary treatment was initiated with systemic standard dose IVC in 32 eyes (74%), high dose IVC in 10 eyes (23%), and IAC in one eye (3%) [Table 3]. Post-treatment tumor recurrences were seen in 10 eyes, and a partial response was seen in 2 eyes. The mean period of tumor recurrence after chemotherapy was 15 months (median, 13 months; range, 3–43 months). Eyes with recurrence and partial response were further treated with IVC (n = 4), IAC (n = 2), and focal adjuvant treatments, TTT (n = 7), and cryotherapy (n = 3). Twenty-four eyes (56%) regressed with partially calcified remnant (type 3 pattern), 9 eyes (21%) regressed with a completely noncalcified remnant (type 2 pattern), 7 eyes (16%) regressed with completely calcified remnant pattern (type 1 pattern), and 3 eyes (7%) regressed with an atrophic chorio-retinal scar (type 4 pattern). The regression pattern among cases with primary cavities was observed as 4 eyes (type 1), 4 eyes (type 2), 3 eyes (type 3), and 3 eyes (type 4), whereas in eyes with secondary cavities, the regression pattern was 21 eyes (type 3), 5 eyes (type 2), and 3 eyes (type 1). The type 3 regression pattern was most associated with tumor recurrence. Regarding the overall treatment outcome, 39 eyes (92%) regressed completely, 2 eyes (4%) regressed partially, and 2 eyes (4%) had an inadequate response. In 27 eyes (63%), the cavities regressed completely, whereas in 16 eyes (37%), the cavities remained stable at the last follow-up. Globe salvage was achieved in 100% cases, and there was no event of enucleation, systemic metastasis, or death during the follow-up period (mean, 31 months; range, 3–83 months). Discussion

In this study, we report our experience of treating 43 eyes of 40 children diagnosed with the cavitary variant of RB. These cases were treated with primary systemic/local chemotherapy with or without focal laser treatment. There are very few published studies describing treatment of cavitary RB. The cavities of the tumor were described as primary or secondary. In this study, we aimed to study the details related to the presence of primary and secondary cavities and other features such as the number of cavities, location within the tumor, change in appearance over the course of treatment, and outcome in terms of tumor or cavity regression in children diagnosed to have cavitary RB in the Indian population.

The mean age of diagnosis was 25 months (median, 24 months; range, 3–84 months). This was similar to previous reported literature, where the median age of presentation of RB in India was 29–33 months (unilateral, 36 months; bilateral, 18–24 months).[121415] A significant association was observed between the age group (age ≤36 months and >36 months) and the type of cavities. Younger children (age ≤36 months) were more likely to have secondary cavities, whereas children age >36 months were more likely to have primary cavities (P = 0.03) [Table 4]. In our study, 26 children were males (65%) and 14 were females (35%). This was similar to previous studies reported wherein male subjects outnumbered females.[6910] In our study, 29 eyes (20 males, 9 females) were having secondary cavities, and 14 eyes (8 males, 6 females) were having primary cavities in the tumors. No significant relationship was observed between gender and the type of cavities (P = 0.5). Unilateral cavitary RB was seen in 37 cases (93%), and bilateral cavitary RB was seen in 3 cases (7%). Our study showed similar results as the previous study done by Rishi etalon 13 tumors in ten eyes, where they reported unilateral cavitary RB in 100% eyes.[6] In our study, most of the cases presented with symptoms of leukocoria (53%) and strabismus (15%). The presence of strabismus indicates foveal involvement of the tumor, thereby compromising final visual acuity.[15] In most cases, tumor was located within 3 mm of the optic disc (86%) and 26% of these were covering the optic disc completely. Rishi et al.[6] in their case series of 6 cases of cavitary RB reported that all these tumors were located near the optic disc and 50% of these were obscuring the view of optic disc. Significant association was observed between the secondary cavities and the tumor obscuring or covering the optic disc (P = 0.01), which shows that secondary cavities were more common in the tumors covering the optic disc. The location of tumor in relation to fovea was within 3 mm in 29 eyes (67%), and 44% of these were covering the fovea completely. Chandra et al.[16] in their study of 47 eyes with macular RB also reported that the tumor was overlying the optic disc in 30 (64%) eyes and covering the fovea in 12 (26%) eyes. The presence of localized subretinal fluid was seen in 42% of eyes, which was strongly associated with the presence of secondary cavities and subretinal fluid (P = 0.002).

Rishi et al.,[6] in their study of 13 tumors in ten eyes observed that most of the tumors regressed with type 2 and type 3 regression patterns. In our study also, most of the tumors regressed with type 3 regression pattern. Excellent tumor control was achieved using systemic IVC and adjuvant TTT/cryotherapy, and none of the eyes had been enucleated by the last follow-up. This is in accordance with the belief that cavitary RB has an excellent treatment response with IVC and IAC.[91718]

Cavitary RB usually remains dimensionally stable and shows minimal regression after chemotherapy, reflecting their high differentiation and limited chemosensitivity.[19] In our study, the cavities regressed completely in 63% of eyes (n = 27), and the cavities were stable in 37% of eyes (n = 16). A study by Mashayekhi et al.[9] in 16 eyes, cavities completely disappeared in 5 eyes (31%), were stable in 7 eyes (44%), and were more visible in one eye (6%). Similarly, Rishi et al.[6] found that while the cavities were generally stable in their retrospective analysis of 13 eyes of 10 patients, a 2-year-old boy with group C cavitary RB showed vitreous and subretinal seeds. Following the second systemic IVC cycle, the cavity spontaneously burst, releasing “seeds” into the vitreous. To the best of our knowledge, there have never been any prior reports of cavity rupture resulting in vitreous seeds in the literature. Contrary to previously published studies, none of the tumors in our series displayed cavity collapse at the last follow-up.

In a study by Mashayekhi A et al.,[9] histological analysis of three cavitary RB eyes revealed regions of photoreceptor differentiation in two of the tumors. The third has many Flexner–Wintersteiner rosettes but few fleurettes. Tumor cells were seen lining the cavities, and there was no indication of endothelium or the epithelial lining of these spaces. Cavity formation within the tumor was hypothesized due to secondary ischemia and liquefactive necrosis. A recent report by Xu et al.[10] revealed that a reduced MYCN expression is linked to translucent cavity formation, marking a well-differentiated cavitary subtype with a favorable prognosis. A comprehensive literature search was conducted by Raval et al.[8] A study on cavitary RB from 1952 to 2021 concluded that cavitary RB is linked to a favorable prognosis, with high globe salvage rates and minimal likelihood of tumor recurrence, which is comparable to the findings of our study.

This study is constrained by a modest sample size, which, despite being relatively large for a rare cavitary variant, limits the broader applicability of the findings. Visual acuity outcomes could not be systematically assessed due to the young age of patients. Furthermore, genetic testing was not uniformly performed, preventing genotype–phenotype correlations. Last, short follow-up (3 months) in some cases may have underestimated late recurrences and long-term visual outcomes.

Conclusion

Cavitary RB, though uncommon, represents a distinct clinical variant. Both primary and secondary cavitary RB demonstrated favorable responses to standard IVC with adjunct focal therapies, resulting in excellent globe salvage rates. Secondary cavities were significantly associated with younger age, tumor obscuring the optic disc, and the presence of subretinal fluid. Most cases demonstrated regression, particularly with type 3 (partially calcified) patterns, challenging earlier concerns of chemoresistance. Identification of cavitary morphology plays an important role in both diagnosis and disease monitoring. With appropriately tailored therapy, successful outcomes can be achieved even in these atypical cases.
