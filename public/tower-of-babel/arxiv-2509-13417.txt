Coherently enhanced decoherence and cloud substructure of atom interferometers Preprint: CALT-TH/2025-028 CERN-TH-2025-174
Clara Murgui, Ryan Plestid

Abstract
We study how coherent scattering of a background gas off an atom (or other matter) interferometer can lead to enhanced signals from phase shifts and contrast loss. We focus on the inclusion of realistic features of atom interferometers such as finite temperature, cloud substructure, and time-dependent cloud radii. The inclusion of these effects, extending beyond the previously considered point-like cloud approximation, naturally allow us to study the smooth transition between the coherent and incoherent scattering regimes. We discuss how the formalism presented herein can be tested in the laboratory (with near-infrared photons or an eV-scale electron gun), and discuss an application for the detection of dark matter interacting via long-range forces.



I
Introduction

The intricate control of quantum systems has emerged as a key tool for investigating fundamental quantum physics. Beyond issues in quantum foundations, there has recently been a groundswell of interest in quantum sensing for problems related to issues in gravitational and particle physics.

Atom interferometers were conceived as accelerometers and sensors for measuring some of the fundamental constants of nature
Kasevich and Chu 1992
;
Zhou et al. 2015
;
Rosi et al. 2017
;
Yu et al. 2019
;
Morel et al. 2020
. They have emerged as a promising platform to search for ultra-light dark matter
Arvanitaki et al. 2018
;
Geraci and Derevianko 2016
;
Blas et al. 2025
;
Badurina et al. 2025
, gravitational waves
Dimopoulos et al. 2009
;
Graham et al. 2013
;
Graham et al. 2016a
, fifth forces
Wacker 2010
;
Graham et al. 2016b
;
Abe et al. 2025
, among other phenomena beyond the Standard Model. Of particular note is the proposal to search for phase shifts and/or a loss of contrast induced by a background gas of virialized dark matter particles
Riedel 2013
;
Riedel and Yavin 2017
;
Du et al. 2022
. This phenomenon lies beyond the often-considered classical-field limit for ultralight dark matter, and is known as collisional decoherence
Joos and Zeh 1985
;
Gallis and Fleming 1990a
;
Hornberger and Sipe 2003a
;
Tegmark 1993
;
Giulini et al. 1996
, and offers a threshold-free detection mechanism, which lies in stark contrast to conventional particle physics detectors which are blind to depositions below their energy thresholds (see, for example,
Zurek 2024
).

In recent work
Badurina et al. 2024
, we have developed an extension of the single-atom framework for collisional decoherence
Hornberger and Sipe 2003a
that accounts for macroscopic coherent enhancements from an ensemble of atoms. Coherent effects can increase certain observables by orders of magnitude, concretely by the square of the number of atoms that interact coherently with the background gas. Since atom interferometers involve clouds of millions of atoms, it is crucial to have reliable theory of these coherent effects in order to accurately interpret interferometer data, particularly when searching for low momentum transfer scattering of elusive particles (e.g., dark matter) with atoms.

While Ref.
Badurina et al. 2024
put forth a formalism for calculating decoherence rates, phenomenological applications were restricted to the point-like limit (where the cloud radius tends to zero). This approach ignored experimentally realistic issues such as the finite temperature of the cloud, its density profile, and by extension its time-dependent radius. The cloud’s density profile is a key input in the theory of coherent scattering since its Fourier transform yields the elastic form factor
Riedel and Yavin 2017
;
Bednyakov and Naumov 2018
.

In this paper we extend the results of Ref.
Badurina et al. 2024
to account for the above-mentioned physical effects. A schematic of the setup we consider is shown in
Fig.

1
. We organize our discussion around two distinct approximation schemes: one which neglects cloud-spreading but treats decoherence exactly, and one which includes cloud-spreading but treats decoherence perturbatively. We apply our results to a thermal atom cloud prepared in an harmonic trap, provide explicit computations of the induced phase shift for an infrared photon lamp and
1~{\rm eV}
electron gun, and comment on the application of our formalism to searches for dark matter.

II
Collisional decoherence
and atom interferometers

We are interested in atom interferometers, and specialize to a two-arm setup for concreteness. We focus only on “center of mass” interferometry in which atoms inside a cloud are placed into a coherent superposition of states that trace macroscopically separated paths.

Atom interferometers can be influenced by scattering with a rarefied background gas.
This phenomenon is known as “collisional decoherence”
Gallis and Fleming 1990b
;
Hornberger and Sipe 2003b
, and can be dramatically enhanced when the background gas scatters coherently off the entirety of the atom cloud (containing
N\gg 1
atoms).

Tracing over the density matrix of the background gas and neglecting all atomic recoil in the collisions, the rate of change due to collisional decoherence is given by
Gallis and Fleming 1990b
;
Hornberger and Sipe 2003a
;
Badurina et al. 2024

\quantity(\pdv{\rho}{t})_{\rm coll.}=-\quantity(\lambda_U + \lambda_D)\rho\,,

(1)

where we have written our result in the position representation, and in the basis of the internal states of the atoms,
\{s\}
, i.e.

\rho=\rho(\{\mathbf{x}\},\{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\};\{s\},\{s^{\prime}\};t)\,.

(2)

Without loss of generality, we take these to be the ground and an excited state of the atom.​
1
1

1

The excited state has to be sufficiently long-lived to last over the experiment. Typically, it corresponds to some hyperfine splitting of the atomic ground state
Cronin et al. 2009a
.
These internal states are used to generate interferometer paths; however, during the interferometer’s pulse sequence, the mapping between the internal states
\{e,g\}
and the two paths
\{L,R\}
changes. Nevertheless, the mapping remains bijective at the read-out time
\mathcal{T}
.

Including the rate of change due to the interferometer’s own isolated dynamics, assumed to be generated by a Hamiltonian
H_{\rm sys.}
, the time evolution of the density matrix is given by

\partialderivative{\rho}{t}=-\mathrm{i}\quantity[ H_{\rm sys.}, \rho]-\quantity(\lambda_U + \lambda_D)\rho~,

(3)

where we work in the position representation. In what follows, we take
H_{\rm sys.}
to the be the free Hamiltonian

H_{\rm sys.}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}-\frac{{\bf\it\nabla}_{i}^{2}}{2M}~,

(4)

where
M
is the mass of the atom.
Equation

3
can be written in Lindblad form where
\lambda_{D}
is generated by jump-operators
Badurina et al. 2024
.

The right-hand side of
Eq.

1
contains a “unitary” (
\lambda_{U}
) and “decohering” (
\lambda_{D}
) piece, induced by interactions with probe particles from a dilute, background gas.
2
2

2

We assume the background to be homogeneous and Markovian.

These are as integrals over kernels
Badurina et al. 2024

\displaystyle\lambda_{U}

\displaystyle=\int_{L}\omega_{U}(\mathbf{L})\,K_{U}(\mathbf{L},\{\mathbf{x}\},\{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\})~,

(5)

\displaystyle\lambda_{D}

\displaystyle=\int_{q}\omega_{D}(\mathbf{q})\,K_{D}(\mathbf{q},\{\mathbf{x}\},\{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\})~,

(6)

where
\int_{k}\equiv\int\differential^{3}k/(2\pi)^{3}
, while the leading Born-series (perturbative) expressions for
\omega_{U,D}
and
K_{U,D}
are given explicitly below.

Consider an interaction potential
3
3

3

In relativistic applications
\tilde{V}(\mathbf{q})
is replaced by a relativistic matrix element
Badurina et al. 2024
.
in momentum space,
\hat{V}(\mathbf{q})\sim O(g)
with
g\ll 1
a perturbative coupling quantifying the interaction between the probe and the atom. Then, to second order in the Born series, the real-valued functions
\omega_{U,D}
for non-relativistic scattering are given by

\displaystyle\omega_{U}(\mathbf{L})

\displaystyle=\int_{p}\!\!\rho_{\pi}(\mathbf{p})|\tilde{V}(\mathbf{L})|^{2}2\text{Re}[G_{\pi}(\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{L})]~,

(7)

\displaystyle\omega_{D}(\mathbf{q})

\displaystyle=\int_{p}\rho_{\pi}(\mathbf{p})(2\pi)\delta(\Sigma E)|\tilde{V}(\mathbf{q})|^{2}~,

(8)

where
G_{\pi}
and
\rho_{\pi}
are the probe propagator and density matrix, respectively, and
\delta(\Sigma E)
enforces energy conservation. The kernels
K_{U,D}
are given by

\displaystyle\!\!K_{U}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\sum_{ij}^{N}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{L}\cdot(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x}_{j}^{\prime})}-e^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{L}\cdot(\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{j})}~,

(9)

\displaystyle\!\!K_{D}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ij}^{N}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{q}\cdot(\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{j})}+\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{q}\cdot(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x}_{j}^{\prime})}-2\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{q}\cdot(\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{j}^{\prime})}~.

(10)

The above expressions keep only the leading contributions to decoherence, proportional to
g^{2}
.

Since we specialize to two-arm interferometers, it will often be convenient to define a set of “comoving” coordinates
\mathbf{y}_{i}
which account for the displacements of the atoms with respect to the cloud’s center of mass,
\mathbf{Y}_{\mathcal{P}_{i}}(t)
,

\mathbf{x}_{i}=\mathbf{Y}_{\mathcal{P}_{i}}(t)+\mathbf{y}_{i}\,,

(11)

where
\mathcal{P}_{i}
is the path taken by the
i^{\rm th}
atom, as dictated by its internal label
s_{i}
. Note that, for a superposition, this means that the two branches of the atom’s wavefunction are described by two different sets of comoving coordinates. See
Fig.

1
for a schematic illustration of their meaning.

The trajectory
\mathbf{Y}_{\mathcal{P}_{i}}(t)
solves the classical equations of motion subject to initial conditions, with initial momentum
\mathbf{p}_{0}=\mathbf{0}
for the ground state, and
\mathbf{p}_{0}=\mathbf{k}_{\gamma}
for the excited state, where
\mathbf{k}_{\gamma}
is the momentum imparted by the pulse sequence (which can be much larger than the level splitting
Rudolph et al. 2020
;
Baynham et al. 2025
). At a later time, a
\pi
-pulse is applied and the internal states are exchanged. The trajectories then are “kicked”, leading to convergence of the clouds at the time of measurement,
t=\mathcal{T}
, such that the trajectories coincide,
Y_{L}(\mathcal{T})=Y_{R}(\mathcal{T})
. Finally, a
\pi/2
-pulse is applied to decouple the trajectories from the internal degrees of freedom, which are then used for readout.
4
4

4

We have in mind a Mach-Zender
\pi/2
-
\pi
-
\pi/2
pulse sequence. While we are aware that atom interferometer architectures differ in their technical implementation, our discussion applies equally well to any system with entanglement between the center of mass and internal degrees of freedom.

When defining our initial conditions, we consider a (pure or mixed) quantum state,
\varrho_{0}
. We define
\varrho_{0}
such that its center of mass does not evolve in time, and its dynamics lead to trivial interferometry (i.e., it just “sits there”). Next, some state preparation is performed,

\varrho_{0}\rightarrow\begin{gathered}\boxed{\underset{\text{e.g. }\pi/2\text{ pulse}}{\text{\scriptsize state prep.}}}\end{gathered}\rightarrow\rho_{0},

(12)

such that
\rho_{0}
now has a
non-trivial
evolution in the interferometer.

As a concrete example, let us consider a cloud of atoms in a trap. The atoms will, in general, have some distribution
\varrho_{0}
which is localized in some region of space, for example a thermal state in a harmonic trap. However, we will assume that all atoms are initially in their own internal ground states,
\ket{g}
,

\varrho_{0}=\varrho_{y}(\{\mathbf{y}\},\{\mathbf{y}^{\prime}\})\otimes\varrho_{s}(\{s\},\{s^{\prime}\})~,

(13)

where
\varrho_{s}(\{s\},\{s^{\prime}\})=\bigotimes_{i}\ket{g_i}\bra{g_i}
. At
t=0
, coordinates
\mathbf{y}
and
\mathbf{x}
coincide and we have
\mathbf{y}_{i}=\mathbf{x}_{i}
. Next, a
\pi/2
-pulse is applied to all of the atoms, placing them in a superposition of ground and excited states. For an atom-cloud we then have, at
t=0
,

\rho_{0}=\varrho_{y}(\{\mathbf{y}\},\{\mathbf{y}^{\prime}\}){\,\otimes\,\textstyle\bigotimes_{i}}\big(\ket{\psi_i}\bra{\psi_i}\big)~,

(14)

where
\ket{\psi_i}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{g_i}+\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{k}_{\gamma}\cdot\mathbf{y}_{i}}\ket{e_i})
. Notice that the state
\ket{\psi_i}
has a non-trivial dependence on
\mathbf{y}_{i}
, and the coordinate-space and internal degrees of freedom have become entangled.

The clock starts. The imparted momentum
\mathbf{k}_{\gamma}
now generates a separate trajectory for the
\ket{e}
state vs. the
\ket{g}
state. The state of the system then bifurcates into many branches upon evolution under the unitary dynamics, induced by
H_{\rm sys.}
. The state
\rho_{0}
in
Eq.

14
then serves as an initial condition for
Eq.

3
, and leads to a time-dependent density matrix
\rho(t)
. During time evolution, the environment induces non-unitary decoherence. This phenomenon is described in detail for single atoms in Refs.
Gallis and Fleming 1990b
;
Hornberger and Sipe 2003b
, and for point-like clouds with
N\geq 2
atoms in Ref.
Badurina et al. 2024
.

As discussed above, measurements are performed at some time
t=\mathcal{T}
, defined such that, after a certain combination of laser pulses, (a) the two trajectories coincide, i.e.
\mathbf{Y}_{L}(\mathcal{T})=\mathbf{Y}_{R}(\mathcal{T})
, and (b) the coordinate-space and internal degrees of freedom disentangle. These guarantee that the measurement will not projectively decohere the interferometer, as it would if performed at intermediate times when internal and center of mass degrees of freedom are still entangled and the two interferometer paths are spatially separated.

Next, one must define the observable of interest. Often it only requires some partial information about the matter-wave’s density matrix, and one can trace over all other degrees of freedom such as the positions of the atoms. Typically, to detect the atom number of the interferometer outputs, only the internal state of the atom is read-out and the position degrees of freedom can be traced over. This read-out is done after a pulse sequence and physically the
\ket{g}
and
\ket{e}
states of the atom are directly interrogated (e.g. via fluorescence
Cronin et al. 2009b
;
Rocco et al. 2014
). However, from a theoretical perspective, it is convenient to work in the basis of states prior to the final pulse applied, such that the experimentalist effectively interrogates two states which we will take to be
\ket{\pm}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\quantity(\ket{g} \pm\ket{e})
.

If one counts the number of atoms in the “
+
” port, then the relevant operator may be written for the
i^{\rm th}
atom as

\begin{split}\frac{1}{\mathcal{V}}\int\differential^{3}x_{i}\big(\ket{\vb{x}_i}\bra{\vb{x}_i}\big)\otimes\mathcal{O}_{i}~,\end{split}

(15)

with
\mathcal{O}_{i}=\big(\ket{+}\bra{+}\big)_{i}
, where we have normalized by the volume of space,
\mathcal{V}
, over which we integrate. A counting experiment corresponds to measuring

\mathcal{O}_{+}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{O}_{i}~.

(16)

Other observables can be constructed by raising
\mathcal{O}_{+}
to higher powers. These are related to higher statistical moments; e.g.,
\langle\mathcal{O}_{+}^{2}\rangle
is related to the variance.

The form of
Eq.

15
makes it clear that one can trace over all of the atomic coordinates to compute the expectation value of the observable for a system of
N
atoms,

\begin{split}\langle\mathcal{O}_{+}\rangle_{t=\mathcal{T}}&={\rm Tr}\quantity[\mathcal{O}_+ \rho(\mathcal{T}) ]~\\
&={\rm Tr}_{\{s\}}\quantity[\mathcal{O}_+ {\rm Tr}_{\{\vb{x}\}}\qty[\rho(\mathcal{T})] ]~.\end{split}

(17)

It is therefore convenient to introduce the
2^{N}\times 2^{N}
reduced density matrix,
\rho_{r}(\{s\},\{s^{\prime}\})={\rm Tr}_{\{\mathbf{x}\}}\quantity[ \rho(\mathcal{T})]
, written only in the basis of internal degrees of freedom
\{s\}
and
\{s^{\prime}\}
.

Conventional atom interferometers measure a fringe built from the number of counts,
N_{\pm}
, in the
(\pm)
-ports defined above. The relative population
N_{+}/(N_{+}+N_{-})
is plotted against different choices of a tuneable phase-shift,
\Phi
, between the two paths of the interferometer. The tuneable phase-shift
\Phi
depends on experimental parameters and the energy splitting between
\ket{g}
and
\ket{e}
.

Atom interferometer data is composed of a series of data points,
\{n_{+}^{(1)},n_{+}^{(2)},\cdots,n_{+}^{(\aleph)}\}
, obtained after projecting the internal state of the atoms. The counting index
\aleph
corresponds to the number of times the experiment is repeated for a given
\Phi
. The number
n_{\pm}^{(i)}
is how many atoms were counted to be in the
\ket{\pm}
state in the
i^{\rm th}
iteration of the experiment.

Denoting experimental quantities with a subscript “exp”, we define

\left\langle\frac{N_{+}}{N_{+}+N_{-}}\right\rangle_{\rm exp}\equiv\frac{1}{\aleph}\sum_{i=1}^{\aleph}\frac{n_{+}^{(i)}}{n_{+}^{(i)}+n_{-}^{(i)}}~.

(18)

The measured visibility or contrast,
V
, and phase-shift,
\varphi
, are given by fitting a sinusoidal curve to the observable,

\left\langle\frac{N_{+}}{N_{+}+N_{-}}\right\rangle_{\rm exp}~~\underset{\text{\tiny best fit}}{\longrightarrow}~~\frac{1}{2}\bigg(1+V\cos(\Phi+\varphi)\bigg)~,

(19)

for different values of
\Phi
(which we assume to be known). We note that both the left- and right-hand side depend on the tuneable phase
\Phi
.

The experimentally determined quantities will converge (in a frequentist sense) to the theory prediction

\left\langle\frac{N_{+}}{N_{+}+N_{-}}\right\rangle_{\rm th}\equiv\frac{1}{N}\langle\mathcal{O}_{+}\rangle=\text{Tr}\big[~\rho_{1}\ket{+}\bra{+}~\big]~,

(20)

where
\rho_{1}
is the one-body density matrix,
\rho_{1}\equiv\text{Tr}_{N-1}\quantity[\rho_r]
.

In the absence of interactions of the environment,
Eq.

20
gives
V=1
and
\varphi=0
. Deviations from these expectations can arise from unitary effects, which induce a phase shift,
\varphi\neq 0
. A loss of contrast can arise from multiple sources: (1) genuine decoherence in which off-diagonal entries of
\rho_{r}
decrease in magnitude, (2) dephasing in which an average over unitary phases results in destructive interference, or (3) classical stochastic processes such as a time-varying phase shift which is “averaged out” in the data. In what follows, we will only discuss scenarios (1) and (2).

Having defined the state preparation protocol, the equations of motion which govern time evolution, and the observable(s) of interest, we now turn to explicit solutions for
\rho(t)
.

Most atom interferometers satisfy the hierarchy

t_{\rm kick}\ll t_{\rm spread}\ll t_{\rm decoh.}~.

(21)

Here,
t_{\rm kick}
is defined as fothe time taken from the clouds to be separated by more than one cloud radius,
t_{\rm spread}
is the time scale over which the cloud’s radius changes by an
O(1)
amount, and
t_{\rm decoh.}
is the time scale over which the interferometer decoheres by one
e
-fold.

In what follows, we will first examine a hierarchy different than
Eq.

21
, in which the cloud’s structure changes very slowly in time. In this limit, one can employ a “static approximation”, in which the spreading of the cloud is neglected, while collisional decoherence persists. This is a convenient analytical limit that provides clear insight into the dynamics of an atom interferometer. After completing this analysis, we return to the hierarchy in
Eq.

21
and discuss the relevant dynamics in
Section

IV
.

III
Static approximation

It is convenient to consider the limit in which cloud spreading is negligible, but decoherence is not. In this limit, the cloud is “static” in the sense that it does not spread, but its center of mass moves along the classical trajectories
\mathbf{Y}_{L,R}(t)
. This limit is tractable, and physically realized whenever

t_{\rm kick}\sim t_{\rm decoh.}\ll t_{\rm spread}~.

(22)

It is therefore well suited to the limit of slowly spreading, or rapidly decohering atom interferometer. This maps cleanly to the point-like limit considered in Ref.
Badurina et al. 2024
(where cloud structure was neglected entirely), and can be applied when decoherence rates are large. Furthermore, the static limit is applicable to matter interferometry (where inter-atomic positions are fixed), which we briefly discuss in
Section

V
.

The static limit described above corresponds to a semi-classical approximation where gradients of the density matrix are neglected, but one retains
\mathbf{k}_{\gamma}
from the laser-kick. Mathematically, this corresponds to

\begin{split}-\mathrm{i}\frac{1}{2M}{{\bf\it\nabla}}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{k}_{\gamma}\cdot\mathbf{x}}&=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{k}_{\gamma}\cdot\mathbf{x}}\quantity(\frac{1}{M}\vb{k}_\gamma\cdot\vb*{\nabla} + \iu\frac{1}{2M} {\vb*{\nabla}}^2)\\
&\simeq\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{k}_{\gamma}\cdot\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla~,\end{split}

(23)

where
\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{k}_{\gamma}/M
.

The solution of the equations of motion in
Eq.

3
can then be obtained using the method of characteristics. It is worth noting that a background for the atoms, such as the Earth’s gravitational field, can be straightforwardly incorporated within the semi-classical approximation by altering the classical trajectories to solve the equations of motion in the presence of a potential, but for simplicity we do not consider this here.

The result is that the solution for an atom interferometer, evolving under the free Hamiltonian in
Eq.

4
, can be written as

\rho(\{\mathbf{x}\},\{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\};\{s\},\{s^{\prime}\};\mathcal{T})=\varrho_{y}(\{\mathbf{y}\},\{\mathbf{y}^{\prime}\})\varrho_{s}(\{s\},\{s^{\prime}\})\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\tilde{\Phi}(\{s\},\{s^{\prime}\})}\exp\quantity[ - \int_0^\mathcal{T} \dd t \, \lambda(\{\vb{x}_s(t)\},\{\vb{x}'_{s'}(t)\}) ]~,

(24)

where
\lambda=\lambda_{D}+\lambda_{U}
, and
\tilde{\Phi}(\{s\},\{s^{\prime}\})
depends on the internal states of the matrix element.
Equation

24
resums effects of order
g^{2}\mathcal{T}
in the limit of negligible cloud-spreading.

The coordinates in the exponential are defined in
Eq.

11
, and encode the “which path” information via the labels
s
and
s^{\prime}
. Notice that the dependence on the initial coordinate-space density matrix,
\varrho_{y}(\{\mathbf{y}\},\{\mathbf{y}^{\prime}\})
,
factorizes
from the rest of the expression. This non-trivial property follows from the fact that collisional decoherence is diagonal in the coordinate representation,
and
from the pulse sequence of lasers which leads to all branches of the wavefunction having zero center-of-mass momentum at the time of measurement; this final property is realized by definition at
t=\mathcal{T}
.

The phase
\tilde{\Phi}(\{s\},\{s^{\prime}\})
can be expressed in terms of the single-atom phase
\Phi
, cf.
Eq.

19
,

\tilde{\Phi}(\{s\},\{s^{\prime}\})=n~\Phi~,

(25)

with
\Phi
being the tuneable relative single atom phase-shift between the two atom interferometer arms. The variable
n
is defined via

n\equiv N_{L}-N_{L}^{\prime}~,

(26)

where
N_{L}
is the number of atoms in the left arm of the interferometer in
\ket{\{\vb{x}\}}
, and
N_{L}^{\prime}
is the number of atoms in the left-arm of the interferometer in
\bra{\{\vb{x}'\}}
.

Having constructed an explicit solution for
\rho(\mathcal{T})
, we can now perform the trace over position to obtain

\rho_{r}(\{s\},\{s^{\prime}\})\equiv{\rm Tr}_{\{\mathbf{x}\}}\quantity[~ \rho(\{\vb{x}\},\{\vb{x}'\};\{s\},\{s'\};\mathcal{T})~]~.

(27)

Defining
\int_{y}\equiv\frac{1}{\mathcal{V}}\int\differential^{3}y
, with
\mathcal{V}
the volume of integration, we have

\rho_{r}\propto\int_{y}~\varrho_{y}(\{\mathbf{y}\},\{\mathbf{y}\})\mathrm{e}^{-\int_{0}^{\mathcal{T}}\differential t\lambda(\{\mathbf{x}_{s}(t)\},\{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{s^{\prime}}(t)\})}~.

(28)

We see that
\rho_{r}
is indexed by the labels
\{s\}
and
\{s^{\prime}\}
and has a dimensionality of
2^{N}\times 2^{N}
.

Within
\rho_{r}
, the decoherence terms
\lambda_{U}
and
\lambda_{D}
simplify to

\displaystyle\lambda_{U}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}\int_{L}\omega_{U}(\mathbf{L})\sum_{ij}^{N}\mathcal{Y}_{U}^{ij}(\mathbf{L},t)\,\mathrm{e}^{i\mathbf{L}\cdot(\mathbf{y}_{i}-\mathbf{y}_{j})}~,

(29)

\displaystyle\lambda_{D}

\displaystyle=\int_{q}\omega_{D}(\mathbf{q})\sum_{ij}^{N}\mathcal{Y}_{D}^{ij}(\mathbf{q},t)\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{q}\cdot(\mathbf{y}_{i}-\mathbf{y}_{j})}~~~.

(30)

All time dependence is contained in

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}_{U}^{ij}(\mathbf{L},t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left[\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{L}\cdot(\mathbf{Y}_{\mathcal{P}_{i}^{\prime}}-\mathbf{Y}_{\mathcal{P}_{j}^{\prime}})}-\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{L}\cdot(\mathbf{Y}_{\mathcal{P}_{i}}-\mathbf{Y}_{\mathcal{P}_{j}})}\right],

(31)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{Y}_{D}^{ij}(\mathbf{q},t)&=\frac{1}{2}\left[\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{q}\cdot(\mathbf{Y}_{\mathcal{P}_{i}}-\mathbf{Y}_{\mathcal{P}_{j}})}+\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{q}\cdot(\mathbf{Y}_{\mathcal{P}_{i}^{\prime}}-\mathbf{Y}_{\mathcal{P}_{j}^{\prime}})}\right.\\
&\left.\hskip 117.29874pt-2\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{q}\cdot(\mathbf{Y}_{\mathcal{P}_{i}}-\mathbf{Y}_{\mathcal{P}_{j}^{\prime}})}\right]~,\end{split}

(32)

where the classical trajectory variable can only realize two possible outcomes at a given time, either
\mathbf{Y}_{L}(t)
or
\mathbf{Y}_{R}(t)
. Using
Eqs.

30
and
29
, we can factor out
\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{q}\cdot(\mathbf{y}_{i}-\mathbf{y}_{j})}
(or
\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{L}\cdot(\mathbf{y}_{i}-\mathbf{y}_{j})}
) from the integral over
\differential t
in the exponential of
Eq.

28
. The effects of
\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{q}\cdot(\mathbf{y}_{i}-\mathbf{y}_{j})}
(or
\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{L}\cdot(\mathbf{y}_{i}-\mathbf{y}_{j})}
) are then realized after performing the partial trace over coordinates
\int_{y}
.

It is informative to expand the exponential and consider each term in the Taylor series,

\mathrm{e}^{-\int_{t}\lambda(t)}=1-\int_{t}\lambda(t)+\frac{1}{2}\int_{t}\int_{t^{\prime}}\lambda(t)\lambda(t^{\prime})+\ldots~,

(33)

with
\int_{t}\equiv\int_{0}^{\mathcal{T}}\differential t
, which defines a related expansion,

\rho_{r}=\rho_{r}^{(0)}+\rho_{r}^{(1)}+\rho_{r}^{(2)}+\ldots~.

(34)

At first non-trivial order (i.e., for
\rho_{r}^{(1)}
) we encounter the correlation function
Bednyakov and Naumov 2018
(or the Debye-Waller factor
Lipkin 2004
)

G_{ij}(\mathbf{q})\equiv\int_{y}\varrho_{y}(\{\mathbf{y}\},\{\mathbf{y}\})\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{q}\cdot(\mathbf{y}_{i}-\mathbf{y}_{j})}~,

(35)

which is given by

G_{ij}(\mathbf{q})=\begin{cases}1&i=j~,\\
G(\mathbf{q})&i\neq j~.\end{cases}

(36)

We then obtain

\begin{split}\!\!\!\!\rho_{r}^{(1)}(\mathcal{T})=&\ \mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}n\Phi}\varrho_{s}(\{s\},\{s^{\prime}\})\quantity[\int_{q,t} \!\!\! \omega_D \bigg[ \big(1-G(\vb{q}) \big) \sum_i \Y_D^{ii}(t,\vb{q})
\!+\! G(\vb{q}) \sum_{ij} \Y_D^{ij}(t,\vb{q})\bigg]
\!\!+ \iu\! \int_{\qu,t}\!\!\! \omega_U \ G(\vb{\qu}) \sum_{ij}\Y_U^{ij}(t,\vb{\qu}) ]~.\!\end{split}

(37)

This expression captures the incoherent (scales like
\sim N
) and coherent (scales like
\sim N^{2}
) contributions to
\lambda_{U,D}
. Using
Eq.

26
, the sums (running from
i,j=1
to
N
) over
\mathcal{Y}_{D,U}^{ij}
are given by
Badurina et al. 2024

\displaystyle\!\sum\mathcal{Y}_{U}^{ij}\!

\displaystyle=\!n(N+n-2N_{L})(1-\cos(\vb{\qu} \cdot\vb{\Delta x}))~,

(38)

\displaystyle\hskip-5.69046pt\!\sum\mathcal{Y}_{D}^{ij}\!

\displaystyle=\!n^{2}(1-\cos(\vb{q} \cdot\vb{\Delta x}))-\!\mathrm{i}nN\!\sin(\vb{q} \cdot\vb{\Delta x})~,\quad

(39)

\displaystyle\!\sum\mathcal{Y}_{D}^{ii}\!

\displaystyle=\!|n|(1-\cos(\vb{q} \cdot\vb{\Delta x}))-\mathrm{i}n\sin(\vb{q} \cdot\vb{\Delta x})~.

(40)

Next, let us consider
\rho^{(2)}_{r}
. We encounter new objects beyond
G_{ij}(\mathbf{q})
because correlation functions of the form,

G_{ijk\ell}(\mathbf{q}_{1},\mathbf{q}_{2})=\left\langle\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{q}_{1}\cdot(\mathbf{y}_{i}-\mathbf{y}_{j})}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{q}_{2}\cdot(\mathbf{y}_{k}-\mathbf{y}_{\ell})}\right\rangle_{y}~

(41)

appear, where the angle brackets denote averaging with respect to
\varrho_{y}(\{\mathbf{y}\},\{\mathbf{y}\})
.

For uncorrelated systems, such as the gas of non-interacting atoms we consider, one has that

G_{ijk\ell}(\mathbf{q}_{1},\mathbf{q}_{2})=G(\mathbf{q}_{1})G(\mathbf{q}_{2})\quad\mbox{for}\quad i\neq j\neq k\neq\ell~.

(42)

When one or more indices are degenerate, however, this factorization is spoiled (even for uncorrelated states), which in turn spoils the exponentiation of
G(\mathbf{q})
starting at
\rho_{r}^{(2)}
. By adding and subtracting the
missing
terms, required for exponentiation, we can write

\langle\mathrm{e}^{-\int_{t}\lambda}\rangle_{y}=\mathrm{e}^{-\int_{t}\langle\lambda\rangle_{y}}(1+\delta^{(2)}+\delta^{(3)}+\cdots)~,

(43)

where we have omitted
\delta^{(1)}
since it vanishes by definition. The multiplicative corrections,
\delta^{(m)}
, are suppressed by
1/N^{m-1}
with respect to the terms participating in the exponentiation due to combinatorics.

In
Appendix

A
we explicitly compute the leading correction,
\delta^{(2)}
, and find that it is non-vanishing in general. However, for
|n|=1
measurements,
\delta^{(2)}=0
vanishes identically; we conjecture that this holds to all orders for
|n|=1
, but we do not attempt a proof. Furthermore, in the limit of a point-like cloud,
\varrho_{y}(\{\mathbf{y}\},\{\mathbf{y}\})\rightarrow\prod_{i}\delta(\mathbf{y})
, the exponentiation is exact (as can be easily proven) and we obtain the point-like limit derived in Ref.
Badurina et al. 2024
.

Let us now neglect
\delta^{(m)}
, such that we approximate
Eq.

43
by its exponentiation form. Then the expectation value of the observable count-rate,
\langle O_{+}\rangle_{t=\mathcal{T}}
, maps to
Eq.

19
with

V=\mathrm{e}^{-(s+s_{0})}\cos^{N-1}(\tau),\quad\text{ and }\quad\varphi=\gamma_{0}+N\gamma~,

(44)

with,

\displaystyle s_{0}

\displaystyle=\int_{t,q}\omega_{D}(\mathbf{q})(1-\cos(\vb{q} \cdot\vb{\Delta x}))(1-G(\mathbf{q}))~,

(45)

\displaystyle\gamma_{0}

\displaystyle=\int_{t,q}\omega_{D}(\mathbf{q})\sin(\vb{q} \cdot\vb{\Delta x})(1-G(\mathbf{q}))~,

(46)

\displaystyle s

\displaystyle=\int_{t,q}\omega_{D}(\mathbf{q})(1-\cos(\vb{q} \cdot\vb{\Delta x}))G(\mathbf{q})~,

(47)

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle=\int_{t,q}\omega_{D}(\mathbf{q})\sin(\vb{q} \cdot\vb{\Delta x})G(\mathbf{q})~,

(48)

\displaystyle\tau

\displaystyle=\int_{t,L}\omega_{U}(\mathbf{L})(1-\cos(\vb{\qu}\cdot\vb{\Delta x}))G(\mathbf{L})~.

(49)

Above, we have explicitly separated the incoherent (
s_{0}
and
\gamma_{0}
) and coherent (
s
,
\gamma
and
\tau
) contributions to the observables.
Notice that
s+s_{0}
contains no coherent enhancements and reduces to the naive single-particle calculation
Gallis and Fleming 1990b
;
Hornberger and Sipe 2003a
. For
n\geq 2
one must track
s
and
s_{0}
separately. If our conjecture that
\delta^{(m)}(|n|=1)=0
for all
m
, then the above equations are exact for
\langle O_{+}\rangle_{t=\mathcal{T}}
. Higher moments, such as the variance, will involve
|n|>1
-body density matrices where corrections in
Eq.

43
will first appear at
{\cal O}(g^{4}\mathcal{T}^{2})
, i.e.,
\delta^{(2)}\neq 0
.

Let us briefly summarize the main results of this section. When the spreading of the cloud can be neglected the master equation,
Eq.

3
, can be solved by the method of characteristics giving
Eq.

24
. When measurements are performed on the internal labels of the atom, we trace over
\{\mathbf{y}\}
and obtain a reduced density matrix
\rho_{r}(\{s\},\{s^{\prime}\})
. The trace over
\{\mathbf{y}\}
introduces form factors, and higher order correlation functions. The resulting expression approximately exponentiates as discussed around
Eq.

43
.

We note that
Eq.

37
maps to existing results in the literature in the appropriate kinematical regime. For example, in the point-like limit where the background does not resolve the interferometer’s cloud, i.e.
q\ll 1/r_{c}
, the Debye-Waller factor in
Eq.

35
becomes the identity, and
Eq.

38
and
Eq.

39
are the only contributions to the reduced density matrix, which map to the unitary and decoherence kernels obtained in
Badurina et al. 2024
. In this limit, only the coherent
s
,
\gamma
and
\tau
affect the visibility and phase-shift. Allowing the background to resolve the cloud, we have shown that, for 1-body measurements, the Debye-Waller factor exponentiates up to
{\cal O}(g^{6}\mathcal{T}^{3})
. This reproduces the formalism used in
Du et al. 2022
;
Du et al. 2023
where, for
N\gg 1
,
G(\bf{q})
appears as a form factor convoluting the differential interaction rate.

IV
Perturbative decoherence

Having solved the case of static cloud structure, we now consider approximations that are appropriate when including the spreading of the cloud as a function of time.
For very feeble couplings between the atoms and the decohering background gas, we may study the hierarchy,

t_{\rm kick}\sim t_{\rm spread}\ll t_{\rm decoh.}~.

(50)

In this limit, decoherence is weak and can be treated perturbatively on top of standard unitary evolution of the interferometer.

Let us solve

\partialderivative{\rho}{t}=-\mathrm{i}\quantity[ H_{\rm sys.}, \rho]-\quantity(\lambda_U + \lambda_D)\rho~,

(51)

perturbatively in
g
, counting
\lambda_{U}\sim\lambda_{D}\sim g^{2}
. We expand
\rho
order-by-order in
g^{2}

\rho(t)=\rho^{(0)}(t)+\rho^{(1)}(t)+\ldots~.

(52)

The zeroth order solution
\rho^{(0)}
satisfies

\partial_{t}\rho^{(0)}+\mathrm{i}[H_{\rm sys},\rho^{(0)}]=0~,

(53)

and is given explicitly by,

\displaystyle\rho^{(0)}(\{\mathbf{x}\},\{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\},t)

(54)

\displaystyle=\!\!\int_{t,y,y^{\prime}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!G_{0}(\{\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\},t)\varrho_{y}(\{\mathbf{y}\},\{\mathbf{y}^{\prime}\})G_{0}^{*}(\{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\mathbf{y}^{\prime}\},t)~,

where
G_{0}
is the free particle propagator,

G_{0}(\{\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\},t)=\left(\frac{m}{2\pi\mathrm{i}~t}\right)^{\frac{3N}{2}}\prod_{i}^{N}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\frac{m(\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{y}_{i})^{2}}{2t}}~.

(55)

Next,
\rho^{(1)}
is obtained by solving,

\partial_{t}\rho^{(1)}+\mathrm{i}[H_{\rm sys},\rho^{(1)}]=-\lambda\rho^{(0)}~.

(56)

The homogeneous solution vanishes because of boundary conditions of
\rho^{(1)}(t=0)=0
, and so we are left only with the particular solution,

\begin{split}\rho^{(1)}&(\{\mathbf{x}\},\{\mathbf{x^{\prime}}\},t)=-\int_{0}^{t}\differential t^{\prime}\int_{y,y^{\prime}}\!\!\!G_{0}(\{\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\},t^{\prime})\lambda(\{\mathbf{y}\},\{\mathbf{y}^{\prime}\},t^{\prime})\rho^{(0)}(\{\mathbf{y}\},\{\mathbf{y}^{\prime}\},t^{\prime})G_{0}^{*}(\{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\mathbf{y}^{\prime}\},t^{\prime})~.\end{split}

(57)

Tracing over position,
\int_{x}
, at
t=\mathcal{T}
gives,

\begin{split}\rho_{r}(\{s\},\{s^{\prime}\})&=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}n\varphi_{0}}\varrho_{s}\quantity(\{s\},\{s'\})\quantity[ 1 - \int_0^\mathcal{T} \dd t' \int_y~\lambda(\{\vb{x}_s(t')\},\{\vb{x}_{s'}(t')\}) \times\rho^{(0)}(\{\vb{y}\},\{\vb{y}\},t')]+O(g^{4}\mathcal{T}^{2})~,\end{split}

(58)

where
\mathbf{x}_{s}(t)=\mathbf{Y}_{s}(t)+\mathbf{y}
as given in
Eq.

11
, and
we have used the identity

\int_{x}G_{0}(\{\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\})G_{0}^{*}(\{\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y^{\prime}}\})=\prod_{i}^{N}\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf{y}_{i}-\mathbf{y}_{i}^{\prime})~.

(59)

Equation

58
can be re-written in the same form as
Eq.

37
, but now with a time-dependent form factor,

\begin{split}G(\mathbf{q},t)&=\int_{y}\rho^{(0)}(\{\mathbf{y}\},\{\mathbf{y}\},t)~\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{q}\cdot(\mathbf{y}_{i}-\mathbf{y}_{j})}~,~i\neq j~.\end{split}

(60)

Including higher orders in the
g^{2}\mathcal{T}
expansion, the calculation becomes more involved. One can generically obtain the solution up to
\alpha
-th order by performing an integral of nested propagators and
\lambda
factors that connect the initial condition
\varrho_{y}
to
\rho_{r}
as follows:

\begin{split}&\rho_{r}\simeq 1-\int_{t}\int_{y_{1}}\!\!\!\!\lambda(\{\mathbf{y}_{1}\},\{\mathbf{y}_{1}\})\int_{y_{2},y_{2}^{\prime}}\!\!\!\!\!\!G_{0}(\{\mathbf{y}_{1}-\mathbf{y}_{2})\\
&\times\bigg[\varrho_{y}(\{\mathbf{y}_{2}\},\{\mathbf{y}_{2}^{\prime}\})-\lambda(\{\mathbf{y}_{2}\},\{\mathbf{y}_{2}^{\prime}\})\int_{y_{3},y_{3}^{\prime}}\!\!\!\!\!\!G_{0}(\{\mathbf{y}_{2}-\mathbf{y}_{3})\\
&\times\bigg[\varrho_{y}(\{\mathbf{y}_{3}\},\{\mathbf{y}_{3}^{\prime}\})-\lambda(\{\mathbf{y}_{3}\},\{\mathbf{y}_{3}^{\prime}\})\times\cdots\\
&\times\bigg[G_{0}(\{\mathbf{y}_{\beta+1}-\mathbf{y}_{\beta}\})\varrho_{y}(\{\mathbf{y}_{\beta},\mathbf{y}^{\prime}_{\beta})G^{*}_{0}(\{\mathbf{y}^{\prime}_{\beta+1},\mathbf{y}^{\prime}_{\beta}\})\bigg]\\
&\hskip 34.5021pt\times\cdots\bigg]G_{0}^{*}(\{\mathbf{y}_{2}^{\prime}-\mathbf{y}_{3}^{\prime}\})\bigg]G_{0}^{*}(\{\mathbf{y}^{\prime}_{1}-\mathbf{y}^{\prime}_{2}\})~,\end{split}

(61)

where
\beta=2\alpha-1
, and we have dropped the time dependence of the functions for simplicity. At higher orders, effects cannot be captured with a time-dependent cloud form factor.
We do not pursue this more general solution here, and content ourselves with the leading
O(g^{2}\mathcal{T})
contribution in
Eq.

58
when considering decoherence rates that satisfy the hierarchy in
Eq.

50
.

Again let us summarize our intermediate results. For weak couplings
g\ll 1
, decoherence can be approximated by
Eq.

58
which incorporates the spreading of the atom cloud as a function of time. In their common domain of validity (
g\ll 1
, with negligible cloud spreading)
Eqs.

58
and
24
agree with one another. The results of
Section

III
are best suited to scenarios in which decoherence is strong such that the full series of
g^{2}\mathcal{T}
effects must be resummed; the cost is that time-dependent cloud substructure has been neglected. The formulation in
Eq.

58
is best suited when decoherence rates are small, or time dependent cloud substructure must be included; the cost is that one must work order-by-order in
g^{2}\mathcal{T}
. To our knowledge, this is the first time that cloud spreading effects have been properly incorporated in the open-system formalism of collisional decoherence, and generalizes the results obtained in previous literature, e.g.
Riedel and Yavin 2017
;
Du et al. 2022
. The mapping to the point-like limit studied in Ref.
Badurina et al. 2024
is straightforward.

V
Applications

Having derived formulae for the static approximation and using standard fixed-order perturbation theory, we now apply our results to some simple examples. First we consider a thermal cloud of atoms in a harmonic trap studying the time-dependent form factor in the context of time-dependent perturbation theory. Second, we consider a realistic pulse sequence (as used in current tabletop atom interferometers) and compute the coherently enhanced phase shift induced by a photon beam via Rayleigh scattering. We then perform a similar calculation for the phase shift induced by the scattering of electrons from neutral atoms (including both the coherent and incoherent parts). Next, we sketch how to apply our results to matter interferometers such as the proposed MAQRO experiment
Kaltenbaek et al. 2016
;
Kaltenbaek et al. 2023
or the microsphere proposal of Ref.
Pino et al. 2018
. Finally, we outline how the results presented herein bear on the detection of dark matter interacting with atoms via long range forces.

V.1
Thermal cloud

Consider a cloud of atoms initially prepared in a thermal state with temperature
\beta^{-1}
, using a harmonic oscillator trap
V(\mathbf{x})=\tfrac{1}{2}M\omega^{2}\mathbf{x}^{2}
with an oscillator frequency
\omega
. The system of atoms is then described, at
t=0
, by a thermal density matrix,

\displaystyle\varrho_{y}(\{\mathbf{y}\},\{\mathbf{y}^{\prime}\})=\prod_{i}^{N}\left[\frac{M\omega}{\pi}\tanh\left(\frac{\omega\beta}{2}\right)\right]^{\frac{3}{2}}

(62)

\displaystyle~\times\text{exp}\bigg[\!\!-\!\!\frac{M\omega/2}{\sinh(\omega\beta)}\bigg(\!\!(\mathbf{y}_{i}^{2}+\mathbf{y}_{i}^{\prime 2})\cosh(\omega\beta)-2\mathbf{y}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{y}_{i}^{\prime}\!\bigg)\!\bigg]~.

This then gives for the initial density distribution of the gas,

\varrho_{y}(\{\mathbf{y}\},\{\mathbf{y}\})=\prod_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{y}_{i}^{2}/r_{c}^{2}}}{(\sqrt{2\pi}~r_{c})^{3}}~,

(63)

where the thermal cloud radius at
t=0
is given by,

r_{c}(t=0)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2M\,\omega\tanh(\beta\omega/2)}}~.

(64)

Using
Eq.

62
and
Eq.

55
we can obtain the time-dependence of the cloud form factor

G(\mathbf{q},t)=\mathrm{e}^{-\tfrac{1}{2}q^{2}r_{c}^{2}(t)}~,

(65)

where
q=|\mathbf{q}|
. We follow this convention hereafter. The cloud radius evolves now as a function of time,

\begin{split}r_{c}(t)&=r_{c}(0)\times\sqrt{1+\omega^{2}t^{2}}~.\end{split}

(66)

Notice that the cloud’s expansion rate is set by the oscillator frequency and not by the temperature
Naraschewski et al. 1996
. The static approximation is valid when
\omega\mathcal{T}\ll 1
and we may use
G(\mathbf{q},t=0)
.

All time-dependence in physical observables is contained in factors of
\sin(\vb{q}\cdot\vb*{\Delta}\vb{x})
,
\cos(\vb{q}\cdot\vb*{\Delta}\vb{x})
, and the time-dependent form factor
G(\mathbf{q},t)
,

G(\mathbf{q},t)=\exp[-\tfrac{1}{2}q^{2}r_{0}^{2}(1+\omega^{2}t^{2})]~.

(67)

Whenever
{\bf\it\Delta}\mathbf{x}
depends linearly on time (which is generic for atom interferometers), we can perform the time integral first and obtain an analytic result,

\begin{split}\mathcal{I}(\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{v})&=\int\differential t\,\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{q^{2}\omega t^{2}}{4M\tanh(\beta\omega/2)}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{v}t}\\
&=\mathrm{i}\sqrt{\tfrac{\pi M\tanh(\beta\omega/2)}{q^{2}\omega}}e^{-\frac{M(\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{v})^{2}\tanh(\beta\omega/2)}{q^{2}\omega}}\\
&\quad\times\bigg(\text{erfi}\left[\sqrt{\tfrac{M(\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{v})^{2}\tan(\beta\omega/2)}{q^{2}\omega}}\right]\\
&\qquad-\text{erfi}\left[\tfrac{\mathrm{i}q^{2}\omega\mathcal{T}+2M\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{v}\tanh(\beta\omega/2)}{2\sqrt{Mq^{2}\tanh(\beta\omega/2)\omega}}\right]\bigg)~.\end{split}

(68)

This result holds for arbitrary interactions (since we have performed the time integral first), and allows for the inclusion of cloud spreading in computations of both unitary and decohering effects. This may be relevant for e.g., the Stanford atom fountain, where the cloud expands by a factor of 30
Asenbaum et al. 2020
;
Overstreet et al. 2022
.

In practice, it is often easier to perform other integrations (e.g., over the momentum transfer) first. These integrals depend on the interaction between the background gas and the atoms. In what follows we discuss concrete physical examples for sake of illustration.

V.2
Phase shift from an IR lamp

Let us illustrate the impact of a decohering probe on the interferometer observables defined above with an example. Consider a beam of photons traveling in the
\hat{\mathbf{z}}
direction. We assume a spectrum
\differential\Phi/\differential E
which when integrated gives a total flux.

At long wavelengths, photons scatter from atoms via Rayleigh scattering, which may be classically understood as the scattering of light from an induced electric dipole. The Rayleigh
-
scattering rate is given by:

\frac{\differential\sigma}{\differential q^{2}}=\pi\alpha_{\rm pol}^{2}E^{2}\left(1+\quantity[1 - \frac{q^2}{2E^2}]^{2}\right)~.

(69)

Here
E=k=|\mathbf{k}|
is the energy of the incoming photon, and
\alpha_{\rm pol}
is the polarizability of the atom.

Since a phase shift is the cleanest observable to access experimentally, let us consider the coherently enhanced phase shift due to the scattering of a photon beam with the atoms,

\gamma=\!\!\!\int\!\!\differential t\!\int\!\!\differential E\,\frac{\differential\Phi}{\differential E}\!\int\!\differential q^{2}\frac{\differential\sigma}{\differential q^{2}}G(\mathbf{q},t)\!\!\int\!\frac{\differential\phi}{2\pi}\sin(\vb{q} \! \cdot\vb{\Delta x}).

(70)

In general, the pulse sequence is given by

{\bf\it\Delta}\mathbf{x}(t)=\mathbf{v}t~\Theta(\mathcal{T}/2-t)+\mathbf{v}(\mathcal{T}-t)~\Theta(t-\mathcal{T}/2)~.

(71)

This describes an atom cloud which is kicked apart, then kicked again at
\mathcal{T}_{1/2}=\mathcal{T}/2
, and then recombined at the time of measurement
\mathcal{T}
.
We note that the integral is symmetric in the two halves of the path when the spreading of the cloud is neglected. For the purpose of deriving analytical expressions for
\gamma
, in what follows we ignore the cloud spreading, i.e.,

G(\mathbf{q},t)\rightarrow G(\mathbf{q})=\mathrm{e}^{-\tfrac{1}{2}q^{2}r_{c}^{2}}~,

(72)

with
r_{c}
given in
Eq.

64

The integral over
\phi
can be readily performed, using
\gamma=2\times\gamma_{1/2}
(as discussed above) and gives
5
5

5

We use
k

E

\begin{split}\gamma=2\times&\int_{0}^{\mathcal{T}_{1/2}}\!\!\!\differential t\int\differential E\,\frac{\differential\Phi}{\differential E}\\
&\!\!\!\times\int\differential q^{2}\frac{\differential\sigma}{\differential q^{2}}\,G(\mathbf{q})\,J_{0}(q_{\perp}v_{\perp}t)\,\sin\left(\frac{q^{2}v_{z}t}{2k}\right)~.\end{split}

(73)

Above,

\begin{split}q_{\perp}&=q\sqrt{1-\frac{q^{2}}{4k^{2}}}~,\end{split}

(74)

For coherent scattering, with

V.5
Long-range dark matter scattering

Atom interferometers have the potential to supply world-leading sensitivity to certain models of dark matter. In particular, if dark matter interacts with ordinary matter via a light mediator with feeble couplings (i.e., a long-range but very weak force), then its effective cross section in a conventional direct detection experiment, with an energy threshold

VI
Discussion and conclusions

When a background gas interacts with an atom interferometer, the soft scatterings between the gas and heavy atoms lead to phase shifts and contrast loss. When a large number of atoms are used in a single run of the experiment, these observables can receive enhancements due to coherent interference of the relevant scattering amplitudes. We have extended the existing formalism for point-like clouds
Badurina et al. 2024
to include realistic features such as finite temperature effects, cloud spreading, and a finite cloud form factor. The impact of these realistic features on phenomenology has also been investigated.

All finite temperature and structure dependent effects arise from a partial trace over the coordinate degrees of freedom of the cloud at the time of measurement. When cloud spreading is negligible,
Eq.

24
provides a closed-form solution for the density matrix at the time of read-out. When cloud spreading is important, but the interactions between the atoms and the gas can be treated perturbatively,
Eq.

58
gives the leading-order result including time-dependent corrections arising from the spreading of the atomic cloud. These two equations are our major results. While
Eq.

24
generalizes previous results in the literature (e.g. point-like limit in
Badurina et al. 2024
, phase-shift effects in
Du et al. 2022
;
Du et al. 2023
, decoherence in matter interferometers in
Riedel 2013
;
Riedel and Yavin 2017
),
Eq.

58
incorporates, for the first time, the influence of cloud spreading into the evolution formalism of an atom interferometer in a dilute, homogeneous, Markovian background gas. As we show explicitly for a thermal cloud, to leading order in perturbation theory, these effects are properly captured by a time-dependent cloud radius in agreement with basic physical intuition.

We have applied this formalism to an atom interferometer whose initial state is prepared at temperature
T
in a harmonic trap. Potential experimental architectures that could test the results of this paper have been proposed (including infrared photon- and
1~{\rm eV}
electron-scattering), that we believe to be realistic for current and near term experiments. Explicit expressions for the coherent phase shift have been derived (with all integrals computed analytically) as well as the incoherent phase shift in the case of electron scattering. We have sketched how the static approximation of
Eq.

24
may be immediately applied to matter interferometers. A discussion of dark matter phenomenology, including new closed-form solutions for the phase shift from a Yukawa potential, has been provided.

Our work opens the door to a reliable inclusion of coherent enhancements in searches for dark matter and other beyond the Standard Model physics with atom interferometers. This includes both the enhancement of the signal rate discussed above, but also allows one to quantify the effects from Standard Model backgrounds as first discussed in
Du et al. 2023
. A detailed understanding of both signal and background is crucial to any future search with atom interferometers including: measurements of gravitational waves, precision measurements of fundamental constants, and searches for physics beyond the Standard Model.

The enhancements to a particle-like dark matter signal scale with the number of atoms in the cloud and can easily exceed six orders of magnitude with present architecture, and even exceed ten orders of magnitude for futuristic proposals; they must be properly understood to interpret future experimental data. In the phenomenological applications presented above we have only studied the coherent and incoherent contributions to the phase shift. Other observables, including those related to contrast loss and statistical fluctuations, will be studied in future work.

B.1
Asymptotic analysis for
Eq.

88

We have used an asymptotic expansion of the following integral with
a
,
b
, and
\mathcal{T}
assumed real,

I(a,b,\mathcal{T})=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{\differential\phi}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\mathcal{T}}\!\!\differential t\sin(a t + b t \cos\phi)~.

(136)

This formula is a little subtle and so we give details here for completeness. First we do the integral over time,

I(a,b,\mathcal{T})=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{\differential\phi}{2\pi}\,\frac{1-\cos\quantity(~\TT f(\phi)~)}{f(\phi)}~,

(137)

with
f(\phi)=a+b\cos\phi
. Let us first consider the case
|a|>|b|
, for which the large-
\mathcal{T}
analysis of this integral is straightforward: one applies the Riemann-Lebesgue lemma (the cosine term is
1/\mathcal{T}
suppressed), and the integral gives

I(a,b,\mathcal{T})=\frac{{\rm sgn}(a)}{\sqrt{a^{2}-b^{2}}}+O(1/\mathcal{T})~~~\text{for}~~~|a|>|b|~,

(138)

with
{\rm sgn}(a)=a/|a|
. For completeness, we include here the solution of
I(a,b,\mathcal{T})
valid for any
\mathcal{T}
,

\begin{split}I(a,b,\mathcal{T})&=\frac{\text{sgn}(a)}{\sqrt{a^{2}-b^{2}}}\bigg(1-J_{0}(b\mathcal{T})\cos(a \TT)\\
&-2\cos(a \TT)\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-)^{k}J_{2k}(b\mathcal{T})\kappa^{2k}\\
&-2\sin(a \TT)\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(-)^{k}J_{2k+1}(b\mathcal{T})\kappa^{2k+1}\bigg)~,\end{split}

(139)

with

\kappa=\left(\frac{a-\sqrt{a^{2}-b^{2}}}{b}\right).

(140)

Equation

139
maps to
Eq.

138
in the limit
\mathcal{T}\to\infty

Next consider
|a|<|b|
. Now the function
f(\phi)
has roots at two values of
\phi
in the integration domain. Since
\cos\phi
is even in
\phi

I(a,b,\mathcal{T})=\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{\differential\phi}{\pi}\,\frac{1-\cos\quantity(~\TT f(\phi)~)}{f(\phi)}~.

(141)

In this case, we cannot directly apply the Riemann-Lebesgue lemma, since

becomes very large near the root of

at
\phi=\arccos(-a/b)

Let us proceed by changing variables to

I(a,b,\mathcal{T})=\!\!\int_{(a-b)\mathcal{T}}^{(a+b)\mathcal{T}}\!\!\differential u\frac{1}{\sqrt{b^{2}-(a-\tfrac{u}{\mathcal{T}})^{2}}}\frac{1-\cos u}{u}~,

having been mapped to

\int_{(a-b)\mathcal{T}}^{(b+a)\mathcal{T}}\!\!\differential u=\int_{(a-b)\mathcal{T}}^{-\Delta}\!\!\differential u~+~\int_{-\Delta}^{\Delta}\!\!\differential u~+~\int_{\Delta}^{(b+a)\mathcal{T}}\!\!\differential u~,

\begin{split}I(a,b,\mathcal{T})&=\!\!\int_{\Delta}^{(b+a)\mathcal{T}}\!\!\differential u\frac{1}{\sqrt{b^{2}-(a-\tfrac{u}{\mathcal{T}})^{2}}}\frac{1-\cos u}{u}~\\

(144)

(145)

A change of variables

and
\mathcal{T}\rightarrow-\mathcal{T}

(146)
