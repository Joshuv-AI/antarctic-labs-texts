Joint Epidemiological Investigation Results of the Canine Brucellosis Outbreak at a Breeding Facility Associated with a Human Brucellosis Case, 2025
Authors: Jin A Oh, Min Hye Lee, Woo Ri Shim, Hyoseon Jeong, Sang Gu Yeo, Jong-Hee Kim, 진아 오, 민혜 이, 우리 심, 효선 정, 상구 여, 종희 김
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesCanine brucellosis, caused by Brucella canis, is a zoonosis transmitted through contact with infected dogs or infectious reproductive materials. Following a report of human B. canis infection in Paju-si, Gyeonggi-do, in April 2025, canine brucellosis was identified at a dog breeding facility operated by the patient’s spouse. A joint investigation was conducted to assess potential transmission and prevent further spread.MethodsInterviews assessed symptoms, facility visits, and dog contact; blood culture results were reviewed. Of 99 dogs at the facility, 85 aged ≥6 months underwent serologic and culture testing. Fifty-eight puppies distributed during the previous 6 months were traced, and 34 final owners were monitored for 24 weeks after their last exposure.ResultsThe patient intermittently visited the facility and had contact with dogs. Her asymptomatic spouse had a negative blood culture on April 29, 2025. Of 85 dogs tested, 11 (12.9%) were positive (n=10) or suspected positive (n=1), representing at least 11.1% of all 99 dogs; B. canis was isolated from four dogs. Among 34 distributed dogs with identified final owners, all 14 tested were negative; the remaining 20 dogs, aged <6 months, had no clinical signs and untested. No owner reported symptoms suggestive of brucellosis during follow-up.ConclusionsTransmission to the patient was likely from infected dogs or infectious materials. Detection of infected dogs in all breeding units highlights the limitations in early recognition. Testing newly introduced and breeding dogs, prompt diagnosis after reproductive abnormalities, and stronger information sharing between human and animal health sectors are needed.Keywords: Canine brucellosis, Brucella canis, B. canis, Zoonoses, Joint epidemiological investigation

Key messages

① What is known previously?

Brucella canis can be transmitted to humans through contact with infected dogs or infectious reproductive materials.

② What new information is presented?

A human B. canis infection led to the detection of a canine brucellosis outbreak at a dog breeding facility operated by the patient’s spouse, prompting puppy tracing and owner monitoring.

③ What are implications?

Rapid information sharing, puppy tracing, and coordinated management of exposed persons are required when canine brucellosis occurs at breeding facilities.

Introduction

Canine brucellosis is a zoonosis caused by Brucella canis. In dogs, it can cause reproductive disorders, such as abortion, stillbirth, infertility, and orchitis, although asymptomatic infection may also occur [1,2]. In humans, infection generally causes nonspecific symptoms, such as fever, fatigue, myalgia, and arthralgia, and can occur through direct contact with infected dogs or infectious materials, including blood, placentas, aborted materials, and genital secretions [2-4]. Individuals with repeated exposure to infected dogs or infectious materials, such as workers at dog breeding facilities, veterinarians, and animal shelter personnel, as well as laboratory personnel handling Brucella culture specimens, are considered to be at increased risk of exposure [3,4].

In the Republic of Korea (ROK), human brucellosis is managed as a Class 3 notifiable infectious disease. One human B. canis infection was reported in 2021. It was confirmed as a B. canis infection through pathogen analysis by the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency (KDCA). However, human B. canis infection may be underdiagnosed because diagnostic experience is limited and standardized serologic testing methods are not readily available [5]. Because a large number of dogs are bred and housed at dog breeding facilities and are moved to multiple regions through distribution, an outbreak of canine brucellosis requires not only testing of co-housed dogs but also tracing the movement of distributed dogs and providing guidance to their final owners. Recent reports from Europe and the United Kingdom have also raised concerns about the introduction and spread of B. canis associated with the movement of imported dogs, rescue dogs, and distributed dogs, emphasizing the importance of information sharing between the animal and public health sectors, tracing the movement of infected dogs, and assessing exposed persons [6,7].

Coordination, communication, and collaboration among the human, animal, and environmental health sectors are recognized as core principles of zoonotic disease response [8]. In the ROK, a joint epidemiological investigation manual has been established to facilitate rapid information sharing and coordinated response between the human and animal health sectors when brucellosis occurs. When epidemiologically linked human and animal cases are identified, investigations are conducted to determine the transmission pathway and to coordinate the management of distributed dogs and exposed persons [9]. Following notification of a human B. canis infection in Paju-si, Gyeonggi-do, in April 2025, a joint epidemiological investigation involving the human and animal health sectors was conducted among the dogs housed at the dog breeding facility visited by the patient, distributed puppies, and related exposed persons. This joint epidemiological investigation was conducted to identify the transmission pathway and source of infection and to prevent further spread.

Methods

1. Case Definitions and Study Subjects

A human case was defined as a person with symptoms suggestive of brucellosis or an epidemiologic link in whom B. canis was isolated and identified by blood culture. An animal case was defined as a dog housed at the epidemiologically linked dog breeding facility or distributed from the facility within the previous 6 months that was classified as positive or presumptive positive for canine brucellosis by the 2-mercaptoethanol rapid slide agglutination test (2ME-RSAT) and immunochromatographic test (ICT), or in which B. canis was confirmed by bacterial isolation. An exposed person was defined as a person who had direct contact with an infected dog or an epidemiologically linked dog during the breeding or distribution process at the facility, or who was exposed to infectious materials such as aborted materials, blood, or genital secretions. The human subjects comprised 36 persons: the patient, the patient’s spouse (husband), and 34 final owners of epidemiologically linked distributed dogs. The animal subjects comprised 157 dogs, including 99 dogs housed at the dog breeding facility and 58 puppies distributed within the previous 6 months.

2. Epidemiological Investigation and Information Collection

In the human health sector, KDCA and the local public health center interviewed the patient and her spouse using standardized epidemiological investigation forms. Information was collected on demographic characteristics, clinical symptoms, date of symptom onset, visits to and activities performed at the dog breeding facility, contact with dogs, exposure to aborted materials, blood, and genital secretions, and use of personal protective equipment [10]. In addition, final owners of distributed puppies were assessed for symptoms suggestive of brucellosis and were advised to visit their local public health center or a healthcare facility if symptoms developed.

In the animal health sector, the Animal and Plant Quarantine Agency (APQA), Gyeonggi-do, Paju-si, and local animal disease control agencies visited the dog breeding facility and conducted an investigation using standardized epidemiological investigation forms. They assessed the number and housing status of dogs, breeding and distribution history, placement of individual dogs by breeding unit, and animal movement, and collected blood samples from dogs aged 6 months or older. Dogs aged under 6 months were assessed for symptoms and classified as subjects for follow-up testing and management by local authorities [10]. In accordance with the Gyeonggi-do guidelines for the control and management of canine brucellosis, the movement histories of puppies distributed within the previous 6 months were traced sequentially through the breeding facility, auction house, animal sales establishment, and final owner. Positive dogs underwent humane euthanasia with owner consent, and when consent was not provided, owners were instructed to isolate the dogs, discontinue breeding, and provide treatment. All co-housed dogs were tested twice at intervals of 30–60 days in accordance with the “Guidelines for the Prevention and Control of Tuberculosis and Brucellosis,” and movement restrictions were lifted if the final retest conducted 6 months after the last detection was negative [11]. KDCA, APQA, and local authorities shared information on human exposures, animal test results, and movement histories of distributed dogs for use in exposed-person management and animal disease control measures.

3. Laboratory Testing

Human specimens were tested by blood culture for the isolation and identification of B. canis. Dogs aged 6 months or older housed at the dog breeding facility underwent 2ME-RSAT and ICT, and whole-blood specimens from dogs with positive or presumptive positive results underwent bacterial isolation testing [12].

4. Management and Monitoring of Final Owners of Distributed Dogs

Among the 34 distributed dogs for which final owners were identified, the 34 owners were assessed for contact with their dogs; exposure to aborted materials, blood, and secretions; and symptoms suggestive of brucellosis. This assessment took into account that B. canis can be detected in fetuses and newborn puppies as a result of in utero or perinatal vertical transmission, and thus, infection cannot be excluded solely on the basis of being younger than 6 months [13]. Given the limited availability of standardized serologic monitoring methods applicable to humans after B. canis exposure, the local public health centers monitored the owners for fever, chills, myalgia, arthralgia, fatigue, and other suspected symptoms for 24 weeks after their last exposure and advised them to seek medical care immediately if symptoms developed. The monitoring period was set at 24 weeks after the last exposure, based on the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention principles for managing persons exposed to Brucella, which recommend symptom monitoring for up to 24 weeks after exposure, as well as investigations of persons exposed to B. canis in the US [14,15].

5. Data Analysis

The numbers of human and animal subjects, numbers tested and test results, tracing results for distributed dogs, and owner monitoring results were analyzed descriptively and presented as frequencies and percentages. Data were organized using Microsoft Excel 2021 (Microsoft Corporation).

Generative AI (Gemini Web, Google LLC) was used during the visualization of workflow diagrams and infographics to refine the layout and improve the structural clarity and readability of the data.

Results

1. Investigation Results of the Human Infection Case

The patient developed symptoms, including fever and abdominal pain, on March 28, 2025, and visited a healthcare facility. The patient was reported as a case of brucellosis on April 18. During the epidemiological investigation conducted on April 22, it was confirmed that her spouse operated a dog breeding facility and that the case occasionally visited the facility for activities such as feeding dogs and had contact with dogs. B. canis was ultimately confirmed in the patient’s blood culture on April 28. Her spouse participated in dog husbandry and whelping but had no clinical symptoms, such as fever, chills, or myalgia, at the time of the investigation, and his blood culture, performed on April 29, was negative (Figure 1).

2. Testing Results for Dogs at the Dog Breeding Facility

On April 29, KDCA requested an animal epidemiological investigation from the APQA. On May 7, the Gyeonggi-do Animal Health Testing Laboratory visited the dog breeding facility, assessed the housing status, and collected specimens. A total of 99 dogs were housed in three breeding units at the facility, and 85 dogs aged 6 months or older were tested. Serologic testing on May 8 identified 10 dogs (11.8%) as positive, 1 (1.2%) as presumptive positive, and 74 (87.1%) as negative. Positive or presumptive positive dogs represented 11.1% (11/99) of all dogs at the facility and 12.9% (11/85) of the dogs tested and were identified in all three breeding units. On June 12, B. canis was isolated from four of the 11 positive or presumptive positive dogs whose whole-blood specimens were subjected to bacterial isolation testing (Table 1).

3. Tracing and Testing of Distributed Dogs

Following identification of positive or presumptive positive dogs, the movement histories of distributed dogs were traced in parallel with disease control measures, including management of positive dogs and disinfection. The movement histories of 58 puppies distributed from the dog breeding facility between November 2024 and April 2025 were traced. After excluding 12 puppies that had died and 12 that were lost to follow-up, 34 dogs with identified final owners were included in diagnostic evaluation, and their owners were included in exposed-person management. Serologic testing was performed for 14 dogs aged 6 months or older that were eligible for testing, and all were negative. The 20 dogs aged under 6 months had no specific symptoms at the time of the investigation, and the relevant local authorities developed plans for subsequent testing.

4. Monitoring of Final Owners of Distributed Dogs and Additional Human Infections

The 34 final owners of distributed puppies for whom final ownership was identified were monitored for symptoms for 24 weeks after their last exposure date. During the monitoring period, none of them developed symptoms suggestive of brucellosis, such as fever, chills, myalgia, or fatigue, and no additional human cases were identified.

Conclusion

The patient had occasionally visited the dog breeding facility operated by her spouse and had a history of caring for and having contact with dogs, and no other clear source of exposure was identified. In addition, positive or presumptive positive results for canine brucellosis were identified in 11.1% of all dogs housed at the facility. Although molecular epidemiologic linkage could not be established because direct comparative analysis of the human and animal isolates was not performed, the combined epidemiological evidence suggested that transmission to the patient through infected dogs or infectious materials was likely.

The identification of positive or presumptive positive dogs in all three breeding units suggests that infection may have persisted at the facility for some time without being recognized. Canine brucellosis can be asymptomatic, and reproductive abnormalities such as abortion and infertility may be regarded as problems affecting individual dogs, making early detection based on clinical symptoms alone difficult [1,4]. In addition, repeated exposure to infectious genital secretions may occur at breeding facilities through mating, whelping, movement of dogs, and the sharing of personnel and equipment across breeding units shared use of personnel. Although the initial source of introduction could not be identified, transmission within the facility may have expanded after newly introduced dogs or dogs brought in for breeding entered the facility without testing, or after infected dogs participated in breeding.

To facilitate early detection of canine brucellosis at dog breeding facilities, newly introduced dogs and dogs brought in from outside for breeding should be separated from existing dogs and tested. Canine brucellosis testing should also be initiated promptly when reproductive abnormalities such as abortion, stillbirth, infertility, or orchitis occur. Moreover, workers should be educated to use gloves, protective clothing, and eye protection when handling aborted materials and genital secretions, and movement and breeding of dogs suspected of infection should be restricted. When animal exposure is identified in a human infection case or canine brucellosis occurs at a dog breeding facility, the human and animal health sectors should immediately share information and jointly investigate dogs housed at the facility, distributed dogs, and exposed persons.

Tracing the movement histories of 58 distributed puppies identified 34 final owners, who were monitored for symptoms for 24 weeks. None reported symptoms suggestive of brucellosis, and no additional human cases were identified. These findings demonstrate the usefulness of linking information on animal movement from breeding facilities through auction houses and animal sales establishments to final owners with the management of exposed persons to prevent further spread. However, some distributed dogs had died or could not be traced, and some puppies aged under 6 months were not tested at the time of the investigation [11]. Therefore, further movement tracing using animal registration and transaction records and review of age-appropriate follow-up test results are needed.

There are several limitations to this investigation. First, whole-genome sequencing data for the animal isolates were not available, preventing comparative analysis of the human and animal isolates. Second, the initial route of introduction of B. canis into the facility and the detailed transmission process within the facility could not be determined. Third, some distributed dogs had died or could not be traced, and some dogs aged under 6 months were not tested; and therefore, the overall extent of infection among the animals could not be fully assessed. Fourth, the management of final owners focused primarily on symptom monitoring, making it difficult to completely exclude the possibility of asymptomatic or mild human infections.

This joint epidemiological investigation identified an outbreak of canine brucellosis following the detection of a human B. canis infection and implemented measures to prevent further spread, including tracing distributed dogs and managing exposed persons. Future responses to canine brucellosis should include early information sharing between the human and animal health sectors, clarification of the timing for initiating joint epidemiological investigations, strengthened epidemiological investigations involving companion animals, preventive education for high-risk groups, and improvements to management systems for dog breeding facilities. Furthermore, when human infection and an animal outbreak are both suspected, comparative analysis of human and animal isolates should also be performed to strengthen the evidence base for assessing transmission pathways.

서 론

개브루셀라병은 Brucella canis에 의해 발생하는 인수공통감염병으로 개에서는 유산, 사산, 불임, 고환염 등 번식 관련 질환을 유발할 수 있고, 무증상 감염도 가능하다[1,2]. 사람에서는 발열, 피로감, 근육통, 관절통 등 비특이적인 증상이 주로 나타나며 감염견의 혈액, 태반, 유산물, 생식기 분비물 등 감염성 물질과의 직접 접촉을 통해 감염될 수 있다[2-4]. 동물생산업소 종사자, 수의사 및 동물보호시설 관계자와 같이 감염견 또는 감염성 물질에 반복적으로 노출되는 사람과 Brucella 배양검체를 취급하는 실험실 종사자는 상대적으로 노출 가능성이 높은 집단으로 알려져 있다[3,4].

국내 사람 브루셀라증은 제3급감염병으로 관리되고 있으며, 이 중 B. canis 감염은 2021년 1건이었다(질병관리청 원인병원체 분석 결과 B. canis 최종 확인). 그러나 사람의 B. canis 감염은 진단 경험과 표준화된 혈청검사법이 제한되어 과소 진단될 가능성이 있다[5]. 동물생산업소에서는 다수의 개가 번식 및 사육되고 분양을 통해 여러 지역으로 이동하므로, 개브루셀라병 발생 시 동거견 검사뿐 아니라 분양견 이동경로 추적과 최종 보호자 안내가 필요하다. 최근 유럽과 영국에서도 수입견, 구조견 및 분양견의 이동과 관련한 B. canis 유입과 확산 가능성이 제기되면서 동물 부문과 공중보건 부문 간 정보 공유, 감염견 이동경로 추적 및 노출자 평가의 중요성이 강조되고 있다[6,7].

인수공통감염병 대응에서는 사람, 동물 및 환경 부문 간 조정, 소통, 협력을 핵심 원칙으로 제시한다[8]. 국내에서도 브루셀라증 발생 시 사람 및 동물 부문 간 신속한 정보 공유와 공동 대응을 위해 공동 역학조사 매뉴얼을 마련하고, 역학적으로 연관된 사람과 동물의 발생이 확인되면 전파경로를 파악하고 분양견 및 노출자 관리를 연계하고 있다[9]. 2025년 4월 경기도 파주시에서 사람 B. canis 감염 사례가 신고됨에 따라 사례가 출입한 동물생산업소의 사육견과 분양견 및 관련 노출자를 대상으로 사람 및 동물 부문 간 공동 역학조사를 수행하였다. 이 공동 역학조사는 유행의 전파경로와 감염원을 파악하고 전파 확산을 차단하기 위해 수행하였다.

방 법

1. 사례 정의 및 조사대상

사람 사례는 브루셀라증 의심 증상 또는 역학적 관련성이 있고 혈액배양검사에서 B. canis가 분리, 동정된 사람으로 정의하였다. 동물 사례는 역학적으로 관련된 동물생산업소에서 사육 중이거나 최근 6개월 이내 분양된 개 중 2-메르캅토에탄올 신속평판응집검사(2-mercaptoethanol rapid slide agglutination test, 2ME-RSAT)와 면역크로마토그래피검사(immunochromatographic test, ICT)에서 개브루셀라병 양성 또는 의양성으로 판정되거나 균분리검사에서 B. canis가 확인된 개로 정의하였다. 노출자는 해당 동물생산업소의 사육과 분양 과정에서 감염견 또는 역학적 관련성이 있는 개와 직접 접촉하거나, 유산물, 혈액, 생식기 분비물 등 감염성 물질에 노출된 사람으로 정의하였다. 조사대상은 사람 총 36명으로 사례, 사례의 배우자(남편) 및 역학적 관련이 있는 분양견의 보호자 34명이 포함되었다. 조사대상 동물은 관련 동물생산업소 사육견 99두와 최근 6개월 이내 분양된 자견 58두 등 총 157두였다.

2. 역학조사 및 정보 수집

사람 부문에서는 질병관리청과 관할 보건소가 역학조사 서식에 따라 사례와 배우자를 면담하여 인적사항, 임상증상, 증상 발생일, 동물생산업소 출입 및 업무 내용, 개 접촉력, 유산물, 혈액 및 생식기 분비물 노출 여부와 개인보호구 착용 여부 등을 조사하였다[10]. 또한 분양 자견의 최종 보호자를 대상으로 브루셀라증 의심 증상 발생 여부를 확인하고, 증상 발생 시 관할 보건소 또는 의료기관을 방문하도록 안내하였다.

동물 부문에서는 농림축산검역본부, 경기도, 파주시 및 지자체 가축방역기관이 역학조사 서식에 따라 해당 동물생산업소를 방문하여 사육 현황, 번식 및 분양 이력, 사육동별 개체 배치와 동물 이동을 조사하고 6개월령 이상 사육견을 채혈하였다. 6개월령 미만 개체는 증상 여부를 확인하고 지자체의 후속 검사 및 관리 대상으로 분류하였다[10]. 경기도의 개 브루셀라병 방역관리 지침에 따라 최근 6개월 이내 분양된 자견은 생산업소, 경매장, 동물판매업소와 최종 보호자의 순서로 이동경로를 추적하였다. 양성견은 보호자 동의 시 인도적 안락사를 실시하고 미동의 시 격리사육, 교배 중단 및 치료를 시행하도록 안내하였다. 동거견은 「결핵병 및 브루셀라병 방역실시요령」을 참고하여 전수 2회 검사(30–60일 간격)를 실시하고, 마지막 발생일로부터 6개월 후 최종 재검사 결과 음성 시 이동제한을 해제하였다[11]. 질병관리청, 농림축산검역본부 및 지자체는 사람 노출 정보, 동물 검사 결과와 분양견 이동 이력 등을 공유하여 노출자 관리와 방역조치에 활용하였다.

3. 실험실 검사

사람 검체는 혈액배양검사로 B. canis를 분리 및 동정하였다. 동물생산업소의 6개월령 이상 사육견에는 2ME-RSAT 및 ICT를 실시하고, 양성 또는 의양성 개체의 전혈 검체에 대해 균분리검사를 실시하였다[12].

4. 분양견 보호자 관리 및 모니터링

최종 보호자가 확인된 분양견 34두의 보호자 34명을 대상으로 개와의 접촉, 유산물, 혈액 및 분비물 노출 그리고 브루셀라증 의심 증상을 조사하였다. 이는 B. canis는 태아와 신생 자견에서도 자궁 내 주산기 수직감염으로 검출될 수 있으므로, 6개월령 미만이라는 이유만으로 감염 가능성을 배제하기 어렵다는 점을 고려하였다[13]. B. canis 노출 후 사람에게 적용할 수 있는 표준화된 혈청 모니터링 수단이 제한적인 점을 고려하여 관할 보건소가 최종 노출일로부터 24주 동안 발열, 오한, 근육통, 관절통 및 피로 등 의심 증상 발생 여부를 확인하고 증상 발생 시 즉시 의료기관을 방문하도록 안내하였다. 모니터링 기간은 브루셀라 노출 후 최대 24주까지 증상감시를 권고하는 미국 질병통제예방센터의 노출자 관리 원칙과 실제 미국의 B. canis 노출자 조사 사례를 참고하여 최종 노출일로부터 24주로 설정하였다[14,15].

3. 분양견 이동경로 추적 및 검사 결과

양성 또는 의양성 개체가 확인됨에 따라 양성견 관리와 소독 등 방역조치와 함께 분양견 이동경로 추적을 수행하였다. 해당 동물생산업소에서 2024년 11월부터 2025년 4월까지 분양된 자견 58두의 이동경로를 추적하였다. 이 중 폐사 12두와 추적 불가 12두를 제외하고, 최종 보호자가 확인된 34두를 노출자 관리와 병성감정 대상으로 선정하였다. 최종 보호자가 확인된 자견 중 검사가 가능한 6개월령 이상 14두에 대해 혈청검사를 실시한 결과 모두 음성이었으며, 6개월령 미만 20두는 조사 당시 특이증상이 없어 향후 각 지자체별로 검사 계획을 수립하였다.

4. 분양견 보호자 모니터링 및 추가 인체감염 여부

최종 보호자가 확인된 자견의 보호자 34명을 대상으로 최종 노출일로부터 24주간 증상 모니터링을 실시하였다. 감시기간 동안 발열, 오한, 근육통, 피로감 등 브루셀라증 의심 증상을 보인 대상자는 없었으며, 추가 인체감염 사례도 확인되지 않았다.

결 론

사례는 배우자가 운영하는 동물생산업소에 가끔 출입하며 개를 돌본 이력이 있었고, 다른 명확한 노출원은 확인하지 못하였다. 또한 시설 전체 사육견의 11.1%에서 개브루셀라병 양성 또는 의양성 결과가 확인되었다. 사람과 동물 분리주의 직접 비교 분석을 수행하지 못해 분자역학적 연관성을 확인하지 못하였지만, 이러한 역학적 정황을 종합하면 감염견 또는 감염성 물질을 통해 사례에게 전파되었을 가능성이 높은 것으로 추정된다.

양성 또는 의양성 개체가 3개 사육동 모두에서 확인된 점은 감염이 시설 내에서 일정 기간 인지되지 않은 채 유지되었을 가능성을 시사한다. 개브루셀라병은 무증상일 수 있고 유산, 불임과 같은 번식 이상이 개별 개체 문제로 인식될 수 있어 임상증상만으로 조기 발견하기 어렵다[1,4]. 또한 번식시설에서는 교배, 분만, 개체 이동과 사람 및 기구의 공동 사용을 통해 감염성 생식기 분비물에 대한 노출이 반복될 수 있다. 최초 유입원은 확인하지 못했지만, 외부 반입견이나 교배견에 대한 검사 없이 시설에 도입되었거나 감염 개체가 번식 과정에 참여하면서 시설 내 전파가 확대되었을 가능성이 있다.

동물생산업소에서 개브루셀라병을 조기에 발견하려면 신규 반입견과 외부 교배견을 기존 사육견과 분리하여 검사하고, 유산, 사산, 불임 및 고환염 등 번식 이상이 발생하면 즉시 개브루셀라병 검사를 연계해야 한다. 또한 종사자에게 유산물과 생식기 분비물 취급 시 장갑, 보호복과 눈 보호구를 사용하도록 교육하고, 감염 의심견의 이동과 교배를 제한할 필요가 있다. 사람 감염 사례에서 동물 노출이 확인되거나 동물생산업소에서 개브루셀라병이 발생하면 사람과 동물 부문이 즉시 정보를 공유해 사육견, 분양견 및 노출자를 함께 조사해야 한다.

분양견 58두의 이동경로를 추적하여 최종 보호자 34명을 확인하고 24주간 증상 모니터링을 실시한 결과 브루셀라증 의심 증상을 보고한 보호자는 없었으며, 추가 사람 사례는 확인되지 않았다. 이는 생산업소, 경매장, 판매업소 및 보호자로 이어지는 동물 이동정보를 사람 노출자 관리와 연결하는 것이 확산 방지에 유용함을 보여준다. 다만 폐사하거나 추적하지 못한 분양견과 조사 당시 검사하지 않은 6개월령 미만 자견이 있었으므로[11], 향후 동물 등록 및 거래자료를 활용한 추가 이동경로 확인과 연령에 따른 후속 검사 결과를 확인할 필요가 있다.

이번 조사에는 다음과 같은 제한점이 있다. 첫째, 동물 분리주의 전장유전체 분석자료를 확보하지 못하여 사람과 동물 분리주의 비교 분석을 수행하지 못하였다. 둘째, B. canis의 시설 내 최초 유입경로와 세부 전파 과정을 확인하지 못하였다. 셋째, 분양견 일부가 폐사하거나 추적되지 않았고 6개월령 미만 일부 개체를 검사하지 않아 전체 동물 감염 규모를 완전히 평가하지 못하였다. 넷째, 보호자 관리는 증상 모니터링을 중심으로 이루어져 무증상 또는 경미한 사람 감염 가능성을 완전히 배제하기 어렵다.

이번 공동 역학조사에서는 사람 B. canis 감염 사례를 계기로 동물 개브루셀라병 유행을 확인하고, 분양견 추적과 노출자 관리 등 추가 확산 방지 조치를 시행하였다. 향후 개브루셀라병 발생 시 사람과 동물 부문 간 조기 정보 공유, 공동 역학조사 개시 시점의 명확화, 반려동물 관련 역학조사 강화, 고위험군 예방 교육 및 동물생산업소 관리체계 보완이 필요하다. 또한 사람 감염과 동물 유행이 함께 의심되는 경우에는 사람 및 동물 분리주에 대한 비교 분석을 통해 전파경로 평가의 근거를 강화할 필요가 있다.
