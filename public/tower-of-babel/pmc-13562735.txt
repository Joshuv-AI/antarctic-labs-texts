Targeted reflex RNA sequencing for enhanced variant classification on exome and genome sequencing improves patient outcomes
Authors: Xiaonan Zhao, Robert Rigobello, Morgan Driver, Sydney Lau, Mei Ling Chong, Jason Chibuk, Hongzheng Dai, Wilson C. W. Wu, Christina Peroutka, Laura Famularo, Alexander Y. Kim, Bailey Hummel, Sarah Dugan, Tinsley Douglas, Linyan Meng, Fan Xia, Christine M. Eng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) has been utilized to provide functional evidence regarding the impact of splicing variants. This study explores the utility of targeted reflex RNA-seq to inform classification of predicted splicing variants identified through clinical exome sequencing (ES) and genome sequencing (GS). A retrospective analysis was conducted on consecutive ES/GS cases completed at a single center in which targeted reflex RNA-seq was performed following identification of eligible variants. There were 131 cases (4.1%) that had at least one RNA-seq eligible variant reported, with eight of these cases having two unique eligible variants. Of the 139 eligible variants, 125 were classified as variants of uncertain significance (VUS). Sixty-four cases had targeted reflex RNA-seq completed with 27 cases having at least one variant reclassified (42.2%). After reclassification, 23 cases had positive results, and two cases had a likely diagnosis of an autosomal recessive condition. Clinical outcomes data regarding positive RNA-seq cases showed that 71% (10/14) had clinical management changes and 43% (6/14) had treatment changes. Incorporation of targeted reflex RNA-seq analysis into the diagnostic pipeline of rare diseases enhances variant classification and resolves uncertainty regarding predicted splice variants, leading to an estimated 1.6% increase in diagnostic yield of clinical ES/GS.

Introduction

The application of exome sequencing (ES) and genome sequencing (GS) in clinical practice has revolutionized genetic testing, leading to a substantial increase in the identification of genetic variants in individuals undergoing investigation for genetic disorders. However, this rapid expansion of genetic data can introduce significant uncertainty in clinical settings, which may have profound implications for both individuals and their families. A clear genetic diagnosis is crucial, as it allows for evidence-based management decisions and more informed genetic counseling1,2.

Among the genetic variants identified, those causing abnormal splicing of pre-messenger RNA (pre-mRNA) are thought to account for up to 50% of all disease-causing variations3. For example, deep intronic variants, which are difficult to detect using standard ES methods, contribute to approximately 25% of genetically diagnosed GS cases4. Similarly, synonymous variants, which may affect splicing but are typically filtered out early in the analysis process, are often overlooked5. Despite their potential significance, most splicing variants, particularly those outside the canonical GT-AG splice sites, are classified as variants of uncertain significance (VUS) according to the current guidelines set by the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics and the Association of Molecular Pathology (ACMG-AMP)6. These non-coding and synonymous variants are often not considered clinically impactful due to a lack of sufficient evidence.

To address these challenges, several computational tools have been developed to predict the potential splicing effects of variants detected during clinical genetic testing7. However, these tools often lack high positive predictive value, particularly when applied to variants outside of essential splice sites (ESS)8. Consequently, their predictions are typically not considered reliable for variant interpretation unless there is consensus across multiple tools6. Currently, the only reliable method to confirm the impact of a variant on splicing is through functional testing of the spliced mRNA. This confirmation allows for accurate interpretation of the variant’s consequences on the encoded protein and facilitates reclassification according to ACMG-AMP guidelines, ultimately leading to a definitive molecular diagnosis9–12.

Reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) and RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) have been effectively utilized in clinical hereditary cancer testing to reclassify VUS, increasing diagnostic yield and reducing uncertainty in cancer risk assessment13,14. Targeted RNA-seq has shown to have similar implications in rare disease, with up to 75% of potentially significant splicing variants reclassified following RNA-seq15–17. While these small studies demonstrate the potential utility of targeted RNA-seq in rare disease, these methods have not yet been widely adopted in routine clinical genetic testing for ES or GS. The following study is a retrospective analysis of ES and GS cases with clinically validated targeted reflex RNA-seq from a clinical diagnostic laboratory. This study explores the feasibility and utility of targeted reflex RNA-seq as a tool for variant interpretation, particularly in confirming or refuting the splicing effects of computationally predicted splicing variants identified through ES and GS. Additionally, this study examines the clinical impact of reclassifying these splicing variants to further support the incorporation of targeted RNA-seq into routine clinical ES and GS.

Results

Retrospective analysis revealed clinically significant splicing variants across diverse genetic diseases

Of the 3206 consecutive ES/GS cases with provider opt-in for targeted reflex RNA-seq that were reviewed, 131 (4.1%) had at least one RNA-seq eligible variant reported. The majority were captured by GS (113/131), with 67 being identified through trio GS. In total, there were 139 unique variants across these cases (Supplementary Information), with eight cases having two unique variants that were RNA-seq eligible. Seven of these cases had two unique variants reported in the same gene, and one case had eligible variants reported in two different genes. Ten cases were homozygous for an eligible variant in a gene associated with an autosomal recessive condition. Overall, 61 variants were associated with autosomal dominant conditions, 44 were associated with autosomal recessive conditions, 18 were associated with X-linked conditions, and 16 were associated with autosomal dominant and recessive conditions. Four unique variants were seen in TTN, three in COL4A3, and two across nine other genes, including CHD5, CSMD1, DMD, DNAH1, GLDC, KDM5C, RTTN, SCN1A, and UBA2. Eligible variants were identified in genes associated with a broad spectrum of diseases, including metabolic disorders, neurodevelopmental disorders, connective tissue disorders, and muscular dystrophies. Of the 25 cases with diagnoses or likely diagnoses following reclassification, 16 cases had a clinical indication that included intellectual disability/developmental delay (ID/DD) (Table 2). All 16 cases had a genetic diagnosis that explained their ID/DD phenotype, with almost all of the conditions being associated with other features such as congenital anomalies or dysmorphic features. The remaining nine cases did not present with ID/DD. Clinical indications for these cases included immunodeficiency, congenital ichthyosis, sensorineural hearing impairment, nephrotic syndrome, hypocalcemia and small for gestational age, and hematologic abnormalities. One individual had a hemizygous VUS reclassified as LP in OFD1, an X-linked condition associated with variable phenotypes including genitourinary anomalies, dysmorphic features, developmental delays, and retinitis pigmentosa. To our knowledge, this individual did not have ID/DD but had vision loss, nystagmus, and rod-cone dystrophy.

Targeted reflex RNA-seq identified diverse splicing events across various diseases

Thirteen variant upgraded reclassifications were due to full or partial intron retentions, and 14 were due to full or partial exon skippings/deletions (Table 2). Most events were out-of-frame and were predicted to cause a premature stop gain and truncated protein. One splicing event was identified as an in-frame intron retention, leading to reclassification of the associated variant to LP due to the location of the intron retention within a helicase domain that contains multiple LP and P missense variants for the same condition. For the one variant that was downgraded from VUS to LB, RNA-seq analysis showed that no significant alternative splicing was observed, and nonsense-mediated decay (NMD) was ruled out.

Sixteen of 26 intronic variants located within 20 bp of the exon–intron boundary, six of 26 deep intronic variants, five of eight exonic synonymous variants, and one of nine exonic missense variants were reclassified through targeted reflex RNA-seq (Table 1). All six of the deep intronic variants that were reclassified from VUS to LP had been identified through GS.

Among genes with multiple eligible variants and completed targeted RNA-seq analysis, one out of two KDM5C variants, two out of three COL4A3 variants, zero out of two TTN variants, two out of two RTTN variants, zero out of two DNAH1 variants, and one out of two CHD5 variants were reclassified.

Clinical outcome data

Clinical outcome data were collected from ordering providers for 14 positive RNA-seq cases. Of these cases, 71% (10/14) had clinical management changes, and 43% (6/14) had treatment changes. Three cases became eligible for research trials, and six cases had the opportunity to use variant reclassification information for family planning reasons. Several ordering providers mentioned other specific benefits of variant reclassification, such as confirmation of a diagnosis without additional testing needed and greater specificity for referrals to specialty providers.

Case 1: CHD3 c.3547+5G>C

Case 1 was a 5-year-old male with macrocephaly, global DD, language impairment, cognitive impairment, attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, and speech delay. He did not have previous genetic testing. His sister had hearing loss, global DD, language impairment, pulmonic stenosis, macrocephaly, and obesity. The clinical team working with the family suspected that the patient and sister might be affected by the same condition. Her genetic testing at an outside laboratory identified several VUS, including an intronic CHD3 c.3547+5G>C VUS. Pathogenic variants in CHD3 are associated with autosomal dominant Snijders Blok-Campeau syndrome (SNIBCPS, MIM #618205), characterized by global DD, impaired intellectual development, hypotonia, delayed speech acquisition, and dysmorphic features18.

Proband GS revealed that they are heterozygous for this same CHD3 VUS. As the variant was predicted to impact splicing (SpliceAI: 0.94), it qualified for targeted reflex RNA-seq. RNA-seq showed that this variant results in a partial in-frame intron retention (Fig. 2), adding eight amino acids after N1182 (r.3547_3548ins24, p.(N1182_A1183insGEGDTLVP)). An average of 22–28% of junction reads were abnormal (Table 3). This eight-amino acid insertion may disrupt a critical region of the protein by altering the conformation of the helicase C-terminal domain. Case 2: FOXP4 c.1357+2T>G

Case 2 was a 5-year-old male who presented with delayed speech and language development, motor delay, autism spectrum disorder, hypotonia, abnormal electroencephalogram (EEG), multiple food allergies, irritable bowel symptoms, chronic constipation, inflammation of the large intestine, and carnitine deficiency. At two years of age, the patient had negative or uninformative genetic testing, including chromosomal microarray, a gene panel for neurodevelopmental disorders, and targeted testing for Fragile X syndrome. The patient’s mother reported a degree of developmental and learning delay in childhood, which resolved.

Trio GS was ordered, identifying a maternally inherited heterozygous VUS in FOXP4 c.1357+2T>G. FOXP4 has a regulatory role in the development of the central nervous system. Pathogenic variants in this gene have been reported in individuals with developmental disorders, language delays, and congenital abnormalities; incomplete penetrance and variable expressivity have been observed19. Based on prediction models (SpliceAI: 0.990), the variant was predicted to disrupt the splicing donor site and qualified for targeted reflex RNA-seq. RNA-seq showed that this variant results in abnormal splicing due to out-of-frame exon skipping (Fig. 3), and 30–45% of junction reads were abnormal (Table 3), resulting in reclassification to LP. This resolved the patient’s multi-year diagnostic odyssey and enabled exploration of clinical trial opportunities for antisense oligonucleotide therapy, although such therapy is not currently applicable to splicing variants. Case 3: CRELD1 c.637+3A>T

Case 3 was a 2-year-old male who was admitted to the PICU for status epilepticus. He also presented with DD, hypotonia, and a prolonged QTc interval. His family history was unremarkable for seizures or suspected genetic conditions. Previous negative genetic testing included chromosomal microarray, seizure panel, congenital hypotonia panel, and Prader-Willi syndrome testing.

Trio GS revealed a compound heterozygous pathogenic variant (c.575G>A, p.C192Y) and an intronic VUS (c.637+3A>T) in CRELD1 associated with Jeffries-Lakhani neurodevelopmental syndrome (JELANS, MIM #620771). JELANS is a recently described autosomal recessive condition with less than 20 individuals published in the literature. Most individuals with JELANS have early-onset seizures (median age 5 months), hypotonia, and global DD; many have dysmorphic features and cardiac arrhythmias20. c.575G>A has been previously reported in individuals with JELANS, while c.637+3A>T has not been described in diagnosed individuals or in ClinVar. As c.637+3A>T was predicted to impact splicing by prediction models (SpliceAI: 0.72), it qualified for targeted reflex RNA-seq, which demonstrated that the variant results in abnormal splicing (Fig. 4) and introduces an out-of-frame deletion with premature stop codon. An average of 46–56% of junction reads were abnormal (Table 3). This functional evidence allowed the variant to be upgraded to LP. Discussion

Findings from this study show that almost half of RNA-seq eligible variants were reclassified following targeted reflex RNA-seq analysis. This is similar to a recent study, which showed that six of 10 cases with a predicted spicing VUS were solved following RNA-seq analysis16. A majority of the reclassifications in our study were VUS upgraded to LP variants, leading to a genetic diagnosis. Therefore, results suggest that the overall estimated impact of targeted reflex RNA-seq could increase diagnostic yield of ES/GS by 1.6%.

Genetic diagnoses informed by targeted reflex RNA-seq had an impact on treatment and medical management, eligibility in research trials, and family planning, which was emphasized by the ordering providers who had a patient’s genetic test results impacted by RNA-seq. The use of functional evidence from targeted reflex RNA-seq in identifying and clarifying the impact of novel variants in genes with limited known information benefits individuals, families, and the broader community. Reclassification of variants contributes to the broader understanding of these splice variants and the associated genetic conditions, which may impact future individuals identified with these variants or conditions. For example, phenotypic information regarding the individual diagnosed with JELANS, an ultra-rare genetic disorder, contributes to our knowledge regarding the phenotypic spectrum of this condition.

Targeted reflex RNA-seq resolved variant uncertainty in cases with a variety of clinical indications. ACMG currently recommends ES/GS as a first- or second-tier test for children with global DD/ID or congenital anomalies21 and the American Academy of Pediatrics now also recommends ES/GS as a first-tier test for children with global DD/ID22. While two-thirds of resolved cases had a clinical indication that included DD/ID, there was a significant proportion of cases with clinical indications that did not include DD/ID. These findings both demonstrate the benefit of targeted reflex RNA-seq analysis following ES/GS with eligible variants for those with DD/ID indications and underscore the utility of targeted reflex RNA-seq for a broad spectrum of clinical indications.

Targeted reflex RNA-seq allows for the reclassification of splicing variants to occur in a shorter amount of time, providing answers in a more impactful manner. Most splicing variants located outside of canonical splice sites are classified as VUS, and the uncertainty associated with VUS poses challenges to healthcare providers, as well as individuals and their families23,24. Typically, as more data regarding VUS are collected, there is the possibility of VUS to be reclassified; however, the timing of reclassification cannot be predicted and can take years25. This study demonstrates the benefit of the functional evidence provided by targeted reflex RNA-seq in resolving uncertainty in about half of cases with eligible splicing VUS, with results being updated several weeks after a blood sample was received. Therefore, the clinical benefits of the reclassifications occurred in a timelier manner through the use of targeted reflex RNA-seq.

While RNA-seq eligible variants were detected through ES/GS across diverse genomic locations, a larger proportion of deep intronic eligible variants were identified through GS as compared to ES. This was expected due to the broader coverage of GS to include noncoding regions of the genome. About one quarter of upgraded variants were deep intronic variants identified through GS, which could have been missed through ES. This not only highlights the utility of GS in identifying noncoding variants relevant to rare disease but the ability of targeted RNA-seq to resolve uncertainty regarding deep intronic variants that may have limited information.

Additionally, about two-thirds of exonic synonymous eligible variants were reclassified from VUS to LP. As synonymous variants do not alter the amino acid sequence, they typically have much lower priority or are ignored during the prioritization of disease-causing variants identified through ES/GS. While these variants are often overlooked, targeted RNA-seq proves to play a critical role in clarifying the impact of synonymous variants on RNA and predicted protein consequences.

An advantage of targeted RNA-seq analysis is that transcripts can be detected at much lower transcripts per million (TPM) values compared to whole transcriptome analysis. Targeted RNA-seq analysis for genes with low expression level in blood (TPM < 0.1) is more likely to have inconclusive or uninformative results, but there is not a defined criteria for the minimum TPM needed for analysis. Genes in this cohort with TPM values greater than 0.5 TPM were able to be reliably studied. As more data is generated from RNA-seq analysis, the data can be used to provide insight into minimum TPM values for diagnostically informative RNA-seq and how the cutoffs may vary across sample types.

About half of the cases with RNA-seq eligible variants were not complete at the time of this study, with a majority of these pending cases awaiting a new blood sample from the individual for analysis. However, the data presented provides early insight regarding the impact of targeted reflex RNA-seq analysis on variant classification and patient outcomes in rare disease testing. While not within the scope of this study, obtaining long-term clinical outcomes for patients with reclassified variants has potential to provide valuable insight into the lasting benefits of establishing genetic diagnoses through targeted RNA-seq. Additionally, a 20% threshold for aberrant junction usage was utilized for this study. While this threshold was determined based on internal data and thresholds utilized in other studies26, variant reclassification relied on additional data such as phenotype correlation, segregation, and disease mechanisms. Finally, the current targeted reflex RNA-seq assay is validated exclusively for EDTA-blood specimens, which in many cases allows RNA-seq to be completed on the original blood sample without need for PAX-blood specimen collection. The assay does not provide insights into gene expression levels or the impact of NMD due to test limitations, which could further refine the functional interpretation of splicing variants. Functional validation studies were also not performed for the reclassified variants. Future efforts will focus on expanding specimen types and incorporating additional tools to assess these critical aspects.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates the utility of targeted RNA-seq analysis following the identification of variants through ES/GS that are predicted to impact splicing. The findings highlight that the incorporation of targeted reflex RNA-seq analysis into the diagnostic pipeline of rare diseases enhances variant classification, leading to an appreciable increase in diagnostic yield of clinical ES/GS. Targeted reflex RNA-seq also provides substantial clinical utility by providing management and treatment changes along with other benefits to individuals, families, and providers.

Methods

Cases

A retrospective analysis was conducted on consecutive ES/GS cases completed at Baylor Genetics between May 2024, when targeted reflex RNA-seq became clinically available, and May 2025. Targeted RNA-seq is offered through Baylor Genetics as a reflex to ES/GS with provider opt-in. Inclusion criteria were defined as (1) provider opt-in to targeted reflex RNA-seq, (2) eligible variant(s) for RNA-seq reported as defined below, and (3) original ES/GS results reported by May 15, 2025. Clinical information, gene and variant(s) reported, the status of targeted reflex RNA-seq, and other information were reviewed for each case with at least one RNA-seq eligible variant. Baylor Genetics is accredited by the College of American Pathologists (CAP) and certified by the US Department of Health and Human Services Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments (CLIA). This study was conducted in accordance with protocols (H-41191) approved by the Institutional Review Board at Baylor College of Medicine, as well as in accordance with all relevant ethical regulations, including the Declaration of Helsinki. Consent for genetic testing was obtained by the ordering provider. Additional informed consent was obtained by legal guardians/parents of the three patient cases presented in the results. Due to the use of de-identified and aggregate data in the remainder of the study, consent was waived for the remaining patients per Institutional Review Board protocol.

Exome sequencing and genome sequencing

DNA was extracted from blood or buccal swab samples using standard protocols. ES libraries were prepared using Illumina-compatible capture kits targeting approximately 100X mean coverage, while GS libraries were PCR-free and sequenced at 40X coverage with 2 × 150 bp reads on an Illumina NovaSeq platform. FASTQ files were processed locally using the Illumina DRAGEN pipeline (v4.2.4), aligning to the GRCh37 (hg19) reference genome for ES and to GRCh38 (hg38) for GS. DRAGEN pipelines were used to call single nucleotide variants, copy number variants, regions of homozygosity, and uniparental disomy. Variants were annotated and prioritized based on population frequency, predicted functional impact, and curated clinical significance using resources such as ClinVar, OMIM, and ClinGen. Variant interpretation was supported by Emedgene, an AI-driven clinical decision support platform that prioritizes candidate variants from ES and GS data27.

Targeted reflex RNA-seq

Targeted reflex RNA-seq was performed to evaluate the splicing impact of VUS or to support the classification of a variant’s pathogenicity. Variants were selected for targeted reflex RNA-seq analysis based on the following criteria: (1) in a gene with phenotype concordant to the clinical presentation, (2) classification as VUS or LP in ES or GS analysis without previous RNA studies, and (3) in silico prediction of a splicing effect. Splicing predictions were assessed using the deep learning–based SpliceAI algorithm with a delta score threshold ≥0.228 and reviewed with splicing prediction tools provided in Alamut Visual Plus version 1.7.1.

Total RNA was extracted from peripheral blood in an EDTA tube using standard protocols. For cases initially tested using buccal swab or other samples, peripheral blood was requested for targeted reflex RNA-seq. Targeted reflex RNA-seq was performed using an amplicon-based next-generation sequencing (NGS) approach, following validated standard operating procedures. Primers were designed to cover all junction sites of transcripts in the gene of interest, either using one full-length amplicon or several partial amplicons. cDNA was synthesized using Oligo(dT) and random primers. The primer location and amplicon size were determined by the length of the transcript, with each amplicon size < 6.5 kb. The amplicon products were used for library preparation using KAPA HyperPrep kit, and then subject to sequencing using NovaSeq X. NGS data were converted from BCL to FASTQ and processed using DRAGEN software. Manual review was initiated when the percentage of Q30+ bases was < 80%. The required number of junction spanning reads was > 50X coverage. Nine de-identified healthy capture ES RNA-seq controls were used as reference for genes with coverage ≥ 50X, and amplicon RNA-seq controls were used when the coverage of capture controls was < 50X. Mapping and alignment were performed using paired-end reads mapped with DRAGEN RNA pipeline. TPM for the targeted gene of interest was reviewed for each case with targeted reflex RNA-seq. A minimum transcript expression level was not required; however, a targeted RNA-seq analysis failed when there was no amplification product.

Variant assessment and reclassification

Variant classification was performed using an in-house refined protocol, which integrated targeted reflex RNA-seq data with other lines of evidence based on recommendations from ACMG6 and ClinGen SVI Splicing Subgroup29. Specifically, PS3_Splice Altering_Strong was assigned to variants with positive findings of an aberrant transcript. For each variant analyzed, the ratio of junction reads corresponding to normal versus abnormal splicing was calculated and manually reviewed using Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV)30. Abnormal splicing was assessed in the context of the reference transcript by comparing the relative proportion of aberrant junction reads associated with the variant of interest to normal reads from the surrounding, constitutively spliced regions of the same gene within the case sample, as well as separately within control samples. The minimum net change in proportion of abnormal junction reads between the case and control was 20% in order to be considered a significant splicing event. Alternative splicing events, such as intron retention or exon skipping, were categorized as previously described31.

Aberrant transcripts upgraded the classification of VUS when a variant demonstrated abnormal splicing resulting in: (1) out-of-frame intron retention or exon skipping/deletion predicted to introduce a premature stop codon and potential NMD; or (2) in-frame insertions/deletions that altered the amino acid sequence, particularly when the resulting substitutions were previously reported as LP or P, or occurred within a known functional domain; and (3) absence of any conflicting functional evidence in the literature. Conversely, VUS were downgraded if (1) no significant splicing change was observed (defined as ≤ 10%) and (2) available heterozygous informative SNPs supported the exclusion of NMD. VUS remained unchanged in classification if criteria for either an upgrade or downgrade were not met.
