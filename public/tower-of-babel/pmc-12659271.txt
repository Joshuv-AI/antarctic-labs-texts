Impact of an integrated stewardship intervention at a secondary care hospital in Ras Al Khaimah, United Arab Emirates: findings from September 2018-March 2019
Authors: Paria Sadat Mousavi, Areeg Anwer Ali, Duaa Salem Jawhar, Bhoomendra A. Bhongade, Srinivasa Swamy Bandaru, Nawal AlFaki, Magdy Imam, Khawla Abdullah Drweesh, Dina Ataig
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHealthcare systems have been increasingly utilizing antimicrobial stewardship programs (ASPs) as a focused effort to optimize antimicrobial prescribing practices. Prospective audit and feedback intervention (PAFI) are important core ASP strategy. Notwithstanding global data endorsing the efficacy of ASPs, a considerable gap persists in their implementation and evaluation within the United Arab Emirates (UAE), where most hospitals are still in the early stages of ASP adoption, and published local data on the impact of ASP initiatives, notably PAFI, is limited. Therefore, the present study aims to address this gap by comparing the impact of PAFI on antimicrobial prescribing between the pre-ASP and post-ASP periods in a UAE Government hospital. This evaluation will furnish evidence to inform future policy, enhance stewardship practices, and aid the national initiative to combat antimicrobial resistance.MethodsThe current study was a quasi-experimental pre-post intervention study with retrospective and prospective data collection, and included data from the electronic medical records of adult inpatients admitted to the secondary care government hospital in Ras Al Khaimah, United Arab Emirates who underwent clean or clean-contaminated major surgeries. We evaluated the impact of PAFI on the prescribing practices of surgical prophylactic antibiotics. In the pre-intervention phase (PIP), the data of 409 patients was collected for major operations done between September 2018 to October 2018. The post intervention evaluation phase (PIEP) contained data collected for surgical procedures conducted on 336 patients between February 2019 to March 2019.ResultsThe surgical site infections rate decreased from 0.14% to 0.0% and the selection of antibiotics improved in PIEP, increasing from 67.5% to 76.5% (p = 0.006). However, the total adherence rate decreased from 36.4% to 31.9% due to the prolonged postoperative duration of antibiotics (p = 0.154) and the improper administration of the first prophylactic dose within 1 h of incision (296; 88.1%) in PIEP (p = 0.002). The average antimicrobial cost per procedure was higher in PIEP (19.85 AED) as compared to PIP (p < 0.000) due to the concurrent reduction in ceftriaxone usage and increase in cefuroxime administration, in addition to the increased duration of surgical prophylaxis.ConclusionThis study demonstrates that the incorporation of prospective audit and feedback intervention into the newly implemented antimicrobial stewardship program in the hospital enhanced the selection of surgical prophylactic antibiotics and supported the reduction in surgical site infection rates. However, challenges persist, particularly in maintaining adherence to the recommended timing and duration of prophylaxis, which limited overall compliance and led to higher antimicrobial costs. These findings emphasize the importance of continuous cycles of antibiotic stewardship audit-feedback interventions to standardize prescribing practices and enhance surgical prophylaxis outcomes in the UAE healthcare setting.

Background

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a global issue that poses a significant global health threat mainly due to the misuse and overuse of antimicrobial drugs [1, 2]. Nosocomial infections in healthcare settings are a major concern, often caused by multidrug-resistant (MDR) microorganisms. Several healthcare-associated infections (HAIs) include respiratory tract, urinary tract, bloodstream, skin and soft tissue and surgical site infections (SSIs) are often associated with improper selection, timing, or duration of surgical antibiotic prophylaxis, which can subsequently contribute to AMR [3, 4]. Invasive surgeries, urine catheterization, prolonged hospital stays, and underlying health issues all increase the risk of HAIs [5, 6].

Antibiotic prophylaxis is critical in lowering the incidence of SSIs that occur due to microbial contamination from endogenous (patient’s flora) or exogenous (environmental) sources [7]. Surgical site infections typically manifest 3–7 days post-surgery and exhibit inflammation signs, with purulent discharge indicating infection [8]. They lead to various consequences, including prolonged hospitalization for patients undergoing surgery, antibiotic resistance, proliferation of MDR microorganisms, and increased mortality rate [9]. Antibiotic prophylaxis must be chosen and administered at the right time and for the appropriate period [10]. However, there is a considerable issue of antibiotic abuse and overuse in surgical procedures [11]. The World Health Organization (WHO) has emphasized the value of monitoring systems for tracking antibiotic resistance patterns and trends, specifically for Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) [12, 13].

Antibiotic Stewardship Programs (ASPs) seek to promote appropriate hospital antimicrobial prescribing practices to address antimicrobial resistance and lowers hospital costs leading to better patient care. These programs include activities to educate healthcare professionals and the general public on proper antibiotic use. To ensure that antibiotics remain effective for future generations, stewardship initiatives must be implemented throughout all healthcare sectors [14].

In UAE, there are few reports regarding the proper implementation of surgical prophylaxis guidelines [15–17]. In addition, the surgical prophylaxis stewardship program was newly implemented in Saqr hospital in April 2018 [18]. Monitoring resistance patterns, particularly for bacteria such as MRSA, is crucial for informing empirical therapy and stewardship guidelines, which constituted a core function of our hospital’s ASP committee. Moreover, prospective audit and feedback intervention (PAFI) was selected as it is a core, recommended ASP strategy that enables direct, educational engagement with prescribers. In our settings, which had recently established ASP, PAFI was a feasible and tailored tool to encourage adherence to the newly implemented local surgical prophylaxis guidelines. Therefore, in the current study, we evaluated the impact of a PAFI on adherence to local guidelines for surgical antibiotic prophylaxis regarding selection, timing, duration, and cost, and its subsequent effect on SSI rates.

Methods

Study design, setting and population

This quasi-experimental pre-post intervention study with retrospective and prospective data collection was conducted in the inpatient wards of Saqr Hospital in Ras Al Khaimah, UAE. The hospital was established in 1981 and had about 225 beds during the study period. It offered medical services in various specialties that include critical care with intensive care unit (ICU), urology; orthopedic, obstetrics; gynecology; dentistry; ear-nose-throat; and ophthalmology. The hospital serves as a teaching facility for undergraduate medical, pharmacy, and nursing students in Ras Al Khaimah and neighboring Emirates.

The study examined surgical prophylactic antibiotics prescribed for clean and clean-contaminant surgical operations two months before and after antibiotic stewardship face-to-face department level prospective audit and feedback intervention. The study was planned and implemented over a period of nine months from October 2018 to June 2019 after obtaining permission from the Research and Ethics Committee of Ras Al Khaimah Medical and Health Sciences University (RAKMHSU REC) and Ras Al Khaimah Research and Ethics Committee (RAKREC) (approval number: MOHAP/REC/2019/19–2019-PG-P). Since this study was considered a surveillance activity, RAKREC and RAKMHSU REC waived patient’s informed consent. Consecutive sampling was used. All patients meeting the inclusion/exclusion criteria who underwent surgery during the specified pre- and post-intervention periods were enrolled in the study. While entering the patient profile in data collection forms, the patient’s name was disguised and coded as initials. The data collection forms and materials were handled by investigators in the study to protect data privacy. No patient-identifiable data is published or provided. Inclusion criteria were patients with clean or clean-contaminated wound type who underwent major surgeries, considering both male and female genders. Patients with documented infections, those who did not undergo surgery in the main operating theatre, those with dirty/contaminated wounds, immunocompromised patients including organ transplants, AIDS, and cancer were excluded from the study. After applying the inclusion–exclusion criteria, 87 patients were eliminated, and 745 were enrolled.

Intervention

The researchers compared the prescribing of surgical prophylactic antibiotics two months before and after PAFI. A two-month period was selected for both phases to ensure an adequate sample size was gathered while limiting the data collecting timeframe to minimize the influence of confounding temporal trends. The intervention consisted of daily, face-to-face, department-level PAFI sessions conducted by the multidisciplinary Antibiotic Stewardship Committee which consists of physicians, microbiologists, nurses, infection control practitioners, and clinical pharmacists. Physicians received direct, verbal feedback on their adherence to the local surgical prophylaxis guidelines (specifically on antibiotic selection, timing of the first dose, and duration). The feedback was immediate, relating to recent cases, and was documented. Follow-up on previous feedback was provided in subsequent sessions. All physicians involved in surgical care were targeted.

Data collection and outcome measures

The electronic medical records system (Wareed) and standardized data collection forms were used to record patients’ data. The data was collected by trained investigators using a standardized data collection form to ensure consistency. Pre-intervention phase (PIP) data was collected retrospectively for surgeries between September–October 2018. The PAFI was conducted in November 2018-January 2019. Post-intervention evaluation phase (PIEP) data was then collected prospectively for surgeries between February–March 2019. The antimicrobial stewardship strategy prospective audit and feedback intervention was assessed for primary and secondary outcomes. The primary outcomes focused on patient-related factors, including hospital length of stay, surgical site infection rates, and adverse drug reactions (if any). Secondary outcomes encompassed administered antibiotics cost and various aspects of antibiotic prescription, such as appropriate selection, timing, and duration a per the hospital’s local guidelines. The surgical site infection data was identified and reported by the Hospital Infection Control and Prevention Committee based on CDC criteria. The electronic systems Wareed and AX-Dynamics were utilized to assess antibiotic surgical prophylaxis costs, providing valuable cost-related information. Wareed is the hospital’s electronic medical record system, while AX-Dynamics is the integrated pharmacy inventory and costing system implemented by UAE’s Ministry of Health and Prevention (MOHAP) to manage patient health data and provide integrated healthcare services.

While this study focused primarily on quantitative outcomes to evaluate the effectiveness of the intervention, future phases are intended to adopt a mixed-methods approach. This will entail qualitative interviews with surgeons, nurses, and operating theatre staff to better understand contextual enablers, barriers, and behavioral drivers affecting prescribing practices. This integration will facilitate the co-design of more sustainable and contextually pertinent antimicrobial stewardship interventions.

Data analysis

The collected data were analyzed by Microsoft Excel and Statistical Package for the Social Sciences software, version 24.0 for Windows (IBM, Corp. Released 2016, IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows version 24.0, Armonk, NY: USA) and was summarized using the mean ± standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables and percentages (%) for categorical variables. The prior and post intervention data were compared using an unpaired t-test/Mann Whitney U test for continuous variables and a Chi-Square test/Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. All p-values less than 0.05 were classified as statistically significant. Parametric tests were selected for continuous variables because their assumptions were met, the sample size was large, and they provide greater statistical power compared to non-parametric tests.

Results

Demographics and clinical characteristics of patients

The data of 745 patients who met the inclusion and exclusion criteria were assessed, and a sample size of 409 patients was utilized for data collection in pre-intervention phase (PIP), and the data of 336 patients were used to determine the effect in post intervention evaluation phase (PIEP). The age of 745 patients ranged from1 to 102 years with the mean age 36.2 ± 20.7. Both phases showed almost similar results in terms of gender, age, nationality and body weight distribution, with an overall male predominance. The largest age group was 21–40 years (p < 0.001), the most common nationality was UAE national, and the predominant body weight group was 61–80 kg. Among the 745 patients, 54.8% and 65% patients enrolled in in PIP and PIEP respectively, had one or two co-morbid conditions such as diabetes mellitus, hypertension, asthma, hypothyroidism, hyperthyroidism, osteoporosis, ischemic heart diseases, chronic kidney diseases and other medical conditions (p = 0.004). Medication history reflected with these trends, with patients taking anti-diabetic, anti-hypertensive drugs, and non-steroidal anti -inflammatory drugs (p = 0.002) as main medications. A few (1.5%) patients were allergic to ibuprofen or penicillin. Surgical prophylaxis medications, duration of treatment and hospital stay

Depending on the type of surgery and surgical prophylaxis administration, the length of hospital stays varied from 0 to over 51 days, with the most extended stays occurring between 0 and 10 days for preoperative (95%) and postoperative (96.6%) conditions, respectively. Before the intervention, patients had an average length of hospital stay of 81.8 ± 170.7 days, whereas patients admitted after the intervention period had an average length of hospital stay of 83.8 ± 160.2 days. During the PIP, the most prescribed antibiotics were cefuroxime (28.8%) and ceftriaxone (27.7%), with 65% of patients receiving IV prophylactic antibiotics and 15.6% receiving eye drops. Antibiotics were commonly given for 3 days (33.3%), followed by 1 day (20%), 5 days (15.6%), and 7 days (2.4%). In the PIEP, cefuroxime use increased (41.1%) and topical tobramycin eye drops became more frequent (24.3%). A similar proportion received IV antibiotics (65.2%), and 15.6% received eye drops. Antibiotics were again most often prescribed for 3 days (30.7%), followed by 1 day (16.1%), 5 days (15.6%), and 7 days (7.7%). Notably, there was a significant shift in prescribing, with reduced use of piperacillin/tazobactam, co-amoxiclav, and metronidazole, and increased use of cefuroxime, ceftriaxone, tobramycin, and cefprozil (p < 0.000) (Table 3). In summary, the PAFI yielded a mixed outcome. A significant improvement was observed in antibiotic selection and reduction in surgical site infections. However, many patients received antibiotics for longer than recommended durations and a high proportion did not receive the first prophylactic dose within one hour before incision. Therefore, the positive clinical outcomes were coupled with a decline in overall adherence, primarily driven by persistent challenges in timing and duration, leading to an increased cost per procedure. Moreover, our findings underscore the necessity for routine laboratory testing.

Discussion

The present quasi-experimental study provides a critical evaluation of a prospective audit and feedback intervention on surgical antibiotic prophylaxis practices at a secondary care hospital in the UAE. Comparing both the phases before and after the intervention, the intervention yielded mixed yet informative results, revealing both encouraging improvements and persistent challenges.

The most significant achievement of the PAFI was the demonstrable improvement in antibiotic selection coupled with complete elimination of surgical site infections (0.0%) in the post-intervention period, down from 0.14% pre-intervention, confirming the potential of audit-feedback intervention in improving clinical outcomes. This is consistent with findings from other UAE hospitals and international research studies indicating that structured stewardship interventions mitigate the incidence of SSIs while ensuring patient safety [19–21].

The antibiotics were selected based on the established antimicrobial guidelines at the hospital. Cefuroxime (29%), a second-generation broad-spectrum cephalosporin, was the most frequently prescribed antibiotic before the intervention period, followed by ceftriaxone (27.81%), a third-generation broad-spectrum cephalosporin. During the post-intervention period, physicians utilized ceftriaxone less frequently (14.4%) and increased cefuroxime administration (41.1%). This was recommended by the local guidelines in the hospital emphasizing on the progression from the third to the second generation of antibiotics. In contrast, Hedef Ozgun et al. reported that cefazolin sodium, a first-generation cephalosporin, was the most frequently prescribed antibiotic for surgical prophylaxis in Turkish hospitals before and after interventions [22]. In our study, the enhancement in antibiotic selection, favouring cefuroxime in line with the local guidelines, indicates that direct educational feedback can narrow the gap between knowledge and practice. This is consistent with prior findings where targeted, real-time feedback enhanced prescriber adherence to institutional protocols [14, 22, 23].

Despite these clinical benefits, the study revealed persistent non-adherence in two key domains: timing and duration of prophylaxis. Guidelines suggest that antibiotic surgical prophylaxis should be administered within one hour before the incision and discontinued within twenty-four hours after the procedure [24]. There was a greater prevalence of physician non-adherence regarding the timing of administering the initial dose of surgical prophylaxis. This contrasts the findings by Mohammed El Hassan et al., who documented 173 cases (69.2%) of improper surgical prophylaxis administration in a tertiary hospital in Abu Dhabi [17]. The decline in adherence to timing (88.1% non-adherence) is likely multifactorial, reflecting logistical constraints within the operating theatre and the noncompliance to standardized nurse-led protocols to ensure timely administration. Similarly, the prolonged duration of prophylaxis (76.5% non-adherence) may be attributed to entrenched prescribing habits that favor lengthy courses under the perception of improved safety, coupled with concerns about postoperative infections, especially in comorbid patients, and limited awareness or reinforcement of guideline-based recommendations.

According to the medical histories of the 745 patients, majority suffered of hypertension and diabetes mellitus in accordance to previous research studies [25–28]. The existence of theses comorbidities in the current study, may partly explain the tendency toward prolonged prophylaxis and the preference of physicians to administer broader-spectrum antibiotics. Moreover, diabetes in patients undergoing surgery is linked with poor wound healing and an increased risk of postoperative infections, prompting physicians to extend prophylaxis beyond prescribed durations as a precaution. Similarly, hypertension and accompanying cardiovascular risks may impact perioperative decision-making, with physicians choosing broader-spectrum medicines in the expectation of providing more protection against potential consequences. This reinforces the need for tailored guidelines that consider local patient demographics and comorbidity profiles, rather than relying solely on generic protocols.

The misuse of antibiotics results in medication expenses and contributes to the development of resistant bacterial infections. Additionally, the patient may be subjected to increased antibiotic toxicity and adverse effects, which could lead to extended hospitalization and increased healthcare expenditures [29]. In our study, despite the improvement in antimicrobial prophylaxis, the length of hospital stay did not decrease post-intervention. This could be due to the inclusion of patients from different surgical specialties, including ophthalmology and orthopedics, where rehabilitation or delayed discharges are common, resulted in higher length of hospital stay. Our results were opposite to the findings of a similar study in the UAE [19] and comparable to another study that was carried out in Egypt, wherein the average duration of hospitalization for patients before intervention was shorter at 4.3 ± 5.0 days and for those who underwent intervention, it was longer at 5.1 ± 5.6 days [20]. Therefore, we emphasize the need for future research that stratify length of hospital stay by surgical type and adjust for comorbidity burden.

The observed increase in antimicrobial costs, in spite of improvements in selection, presents a critical learning point for stewardship practice [30, 31]. The shift in prescribing trends directly influenced the cost outcomes. Following intervention, there was a significant rise in the usage of cefuroxime (from 29.0% to 41.1% of prescriptions) and a decrease in the use of co-amoxiclav (17.1% to 10.5%). While this was consistent with guideline recommendations to use narrower-spectrum agents, the administration of the higher-cost antibiotic cefprozil (1.6% of prescriptions, accounting for 18.6% of total post-intervention cost) and a continued high rate of prolonged durations (e.g., 7.7% of patients received 7 days of prophylaxis post-intervention vs. 2.4% pre-intervention) contributed to the overall increase in expenditure. Consequently, despite a more guideline-concordant antibiotic selection, the economic outcome was an increase in the average antimicrobial cost per procedure from 14.76 AED to 19.85 AED. Physicians should reduce the duration of surgical prophylaxis administration to facilitate cost savings on surgical prophylactic medications and hospital stays. However, the importance of cost-effectiveness in government hospitals in the UAE has not been fully recognized. This is because, following government regulations, all Emirati nationals are eligible for complementary medical treatment at public facilities. Ras al-Khaimah, like the other emirates, provides a high standard of healthcare through the national medical insurance program [25].

Strict adherence to local hospital guidelines on the timing, selection, and duration of surgical antimicrobial prophylaxis remains critical for optimizing outcomes. Integrating a clinical pharmacist into the antibiotic stewardship team at Saqr Hospital and implementing a prospective audit and feedback intervention improved the adherence to antimicrobial prophylaxis and achieved a complete elimination of surgical site infections (0.0%), compared with 0.14% before the intervention. These findings are consistent with the results of a hospital-wide ASP in Dubai, where a Lean Six Sigma-based audit-feedback method led to a 52.4% reduction in antimicrobial use and enhanced protocol adherence while maintaining clinical outcomes [21]. Similar results were observed in Ajman where the implementation of pharmacist-led prospective audits and feedback intervention led to improvement in antibiotic usage in the hospital without any increase in duration of stay, readmissions, or mortality [19].

Study limitations

This study has several limitations that should be noted. First, it was conducted at a single secondary care hospital, limiting the findings’ applicability to other healthcare settings in the UAE. Second, the brief period of the post-intervention evaluation may not accurately reflect long-term prescription behaviours or the intervention’s sustained impact. The quasi-experimental method, which lacked randomization, increased the likelihood of bias and restricted the ability to ascribe observed changes solely to the intervention. Furthermore, clinical outcomes were only assessed in terms of surgical site infections, without considering broader antimicrobial stewardship indicators such as resistance patterns or rates of antimicrobial consumption. The cost study was equally constrained, as it disregarded indirect expenditures such as nursing time and prolonged hospitalization, resulting in an underestimation of the actual economic impact. Finally, the decrease in adherence rates post-intervention emphasizes the importance of continuous monitoring and reinforcement, which was not incorporated into the study design due to the short duration of the study period.

Future research studies ought to extend beyond quantitative prescribing and result data to encompass qualitative investigations with surgical teams, examining their perspectives, practical obstacles, and impediments to adherence. Incorporating a mixed-methods framework would facilitate a more profound comprehension of the human and systemic factors affecting antimicrobial stewardship and could guide the collaborative co-design of more sustainable, surgeon-centered interventions.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that the prospective audit and feedback intervention successfully improved appropriate antibiotic selection to 76.5% while reducing surgical site infections to 0%, highlighting its potential benefit in improving surgical prophylaxis practices. These improvements occurred despite a rise in overall antimicrobial costs, owing mostly to shifts in prescribing practices and extended prophylactic durations. To address these issues, including the non-adherence to prophylactic antibiotic timing, targeted strategies such as standardized nurse-driven protocols for timing, integration of electronic reminders into prescribing systems, and physician education on the risks of prolonged prophylaxis are recommended. Additionally, implementing regionally tailored guidelines for high-risk patients with comorbidities such as diabetes and hypertension may further reduce inappropriate prescribing. A mixed-methods approach incorporating qualitative interviews with surgeons, nurses, and operating theatre staff can further provide valuable insights into contextual enablers, perceived barriers, and behavioral drivers influencing prescribing practices. Continuous cycles of face-to-face department level audit and feedback intervention, complemented by consultative approaches are encouraged to minimize variations in prescribing practices and enhance healthcare quality and outcomes.
