Risk factors for hidden blood loss in unilateral biportal endoscopic lumbar interbody fusion : a single-center retrospective study
Authors: Jian-fang Jin, Hao-ran Chen, Yu-jian Peng, Jun Dai, Qian-liang Wang, Jun Yan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHidden blood loss (HBL) is often ignored unilateral biportal endoscopic interbody fusion surgery (ULIF). We investigated the amount and influencing factors of HBL in ULIF surgery in this study.MethodsFrom October 2020 to November 2023, 100 patients’ clinical and radiological data were retrospectively analyzed. Pearson or Spearman correlation and multivariate linear regression were used to identify factors linked to HBL.ResultsThe mean hidden blood loss (HBL) was 255.84 ± 290.89 ml, making up 62.48% of total blood loss. Correlation analysis showed HBL positively related to ASA classification (P = 0.009), operation time (P = 0.004), number of operation levels (P = 0.046), and paraspinal muscle thickness (P = 0.043), but negatively related to tranexamic acid use (P = 0.001). A multivariate linear regression analysis showed that HBL was positively associated with ASA classification (P = 0.038) and operation time (P = 0.046), but negatively associated with tranexamic acid use (P = 0.001).ConclusionPatients undergoing ULIF surgery incurred a great deal of HBL. More importantly, ASA classification, operation time and tranexamic acid use were independent risk factors for HBL.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-024-08104-7.

Introduction

The concept of hidden blood loss (HBL) was introduced by Sehat et al. in 2000, where blood loss resulting from diffusion into tissue, residual dead space, or haemolysis was described as HBL [1]. Due to its invisibility, HBL is rarely recognized during general assessments. Despite this, HBL can have negative effects on postoperative recovery, such as an increased risk of blood transfusion, prolonged hospitalization, delayed wound healing, and increased infection rate [1, 2]. As a result, an in-depth investigation into the association between HBL and spine surgery, ascertaining relevant risk factors, and implementing interventions to eliminate potential complications are needed.

Unilateral biportal endoscopic transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion (ULIF) has emerged as a favoured alternative for managing lumbar degenerative diseases due to its numerous advantages, including reduced surgical trauma, expedited postoperative recovery, diminished postoperative complications, and decreased incidence of intraoperative haemorrhage [3–5]. Nevertheless, a significant proportion of patients continue to experience anaemia or related disorders after ULIF surgery. In the previous literature, HBL in ULIF patients ranges from 158.48 to 748.1 ml [6]. More importantly, compared with transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion (TLIF) patients, ULIF patients have lost a greater proportion of HBL to total blood loss (TBL) [7]. HBL may serve as a significant contributing factor to postoperative anaemia in patients receiving treatment with ULIF.

In our knowledge, there are few studies on ULIF surgery and HBL and related risk factors. Hence, a retrospective analysis of the medical records of patients who underwent ULIF in our department was conducted, aiming to assess intraoperative HBL and identify the factors that may have an impact on HBL.

Patients and methods

The study included 100 patients diagnosed with lumbar spinal stenosis and lumbar disc herniation who underwent ULIF between October 2020 and November 2023. The exclusion criteria included the following: (1) severe trauma, infection, or cancer; (2) anticoagulant or antiplatelet medications; (3) dysfunction of an organ, such as liver or kidney disease, bleeding abnormalities, or abnormal coagulation; (4) spinal deformities such as scoliosis and ankylosing spondylitis; and (5) missing medical records. Anemia was defined according to the WHO criteria: Hb < 13 g/dL in men and < 12 g/dL in women. The operations were performed by two experienced surgeon. Blood pressure was monitored in every patient during surgery.

Data collection

Detailed clinical data were collected, including weight, height, body mass index (BMI), age, sex, hypertension, diabetes status, American Society of Anaesthesiologists (ASA) classification, operation time, number of operation levels, and tranexamic acid therapy. Additionally, the following laboratory indices were measured: haemoglobin (Hb), haematocrit (Hct), platelet (PLT), triglyceride (TG), total serum cholesterol (TC), prothrombin time (PT), activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), international normalized ratio (INR), fibrinogen and D-dimer (D-d) levels. Measurements of HCT, Hb, and drainage levels were performed again two to three days after surgery A lumbar magnetic resonance image (MRI) was used to measure the thickness of the soft tissue, subcutaneous layer, and paraspinal muscle at the target level by two experienced radiologists independently (Fig. 1). As demonstrated by Cronbach’s alpha values ranging from 0.82 to 0.91, the MRI measurements had a high level of internal consistency. All patients underwent MRI examinations within one week prior to surgery. All imaging was performed on a 3.0TMR scanner. Perioperative fluid management strategy

A total of 1000 mL of fluid was infused into the patients in the perioperative period. Multiple drugs were prescribed, including antibiotics, nonteroidal analgesics, diuretics, and proton pump inhibitors.

Statistical analysis

Data for categorical variables were converted to numerical representations, while data for continuous variables were expressed as a mean and standard deviation. HBL was examined using Pearson’s correlation, Spearman’s correlation, and multiple linear regression incorporating continuous and categorical variables .P < 0.05 was established as the statistical significance threshold. The data analysis was conducted using SPSS v25.0 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, United States).

The present study included a cohort of 100 patients who underwent ULIF surgery; Table 1 provides a comprehensive summary of their demographic and baseline characteristics. The mean BMI was 24.03 ± 3.80 kg/m2. The surgery duration was 183.59 ± 40.05 min. The preoperative Hct and Hb levels were 39.96 ± 4.11 and 133.81 ± 16.07 g/L, respectively. The postoperative Hct and Hb levels were 36.28 ± 4.30 and 119.13 ± 15.92 g/L, respectively.

Discussion

Spinal fusion accompanied by massive blood loss can produce a series of complications and increase the risk of postoperative death; this process has been recognized by most spine surgeons [10, 11]. In recent years, ULIF surgery has attracted increasing attention for the treatment of lumbar degenerative diseases due to the use of endoscopy, which causes less damage to bone and muscle tissue during surgery and has a faster postoperative recovery, fewer complications, and shorter hospitalization times [3]. As reported in the literature, HBL in ULIF patients ranges from 158.48 to 748.1 ml [6] and therefore cannot be so easily ignored. The influencing factors related to HBL in ULIF surgery have not been confirmed. We investigated and determined the risk factors for HBL following surgery using multiple linear regression. This study aimed to provide valuable insights for spine surgeons in identifying patient cohorts with a heightened susceptibility to bleeding. During minimally invasive surgery, haemostasis and perioperative blood loss management are highly important. Ultimately, this research is expected to contribute to a reduction in perioperative complications and a greater level of patient safety.

The ASA classification was found to be an independent risk factor for HBL in our study .This value was identical to a previously reported result [12, 13]. The higher the ASA class is, the poorer the patient’s health status. The ASA classification describes patients with ASA II to IV as having mild or severe systemic diseases. The quality of muscle and tissue was found to be inferior when compared to that of normal individuals. As a result, they may be more susceptible to anaemia problems due to impaired haemodynamic function.

HBL risk was related with operation time and number of operation level according to our study. According to previous studies, these results are consistent [14, 15]. There are several reasons for these findings. First, as the operation time and number of operation level increase, the blood flow from cancellous bone surfaces, and the venous plexus increases. Although spine surgeons use bipolar electrocoagulation and gelatine sponges to stop bleeding intraoperatively, the lumbar spine has a rich blood supply, and bleeding from the spinal venous plexus and cancellous bone surfaces is particularly difficult to stop [16, 17]. According to multiple linear regression analysis, tranexamic acid was identified as a protective factor and demonstrated a negative correlation with HBL. Tranexamic acid, a synthetic derivative of lysine, can displace fibrinogen from fibrin surfaces, blocking the interaction between plasmin and fibrin clot lysis and retarding fibrinolysis [19, 20]. Tranexamic acid has been used extensively in orthopaedic surgery to reduce blood loss without increasing the risk of thrombosis or reducing the chance of postoperative blood transfusion [21–23]. The potential mechanism by which tranexamic acid application reduces HBL may involve direct targeting of the bleeding site after surgical haemostasis and before wound closure. In addition, increasing the volume and strength of the clot on the original surgical surface by inhibiting local fibrinolytic activity prevents fibrin clot dissolution, thereby enhancing microvascular haemostasis [22, 24, 25]. However, there is no published evidence suggesting that tranexamic acid decreases HBL in ULIF surgery according to our study. There is a need for additional randomized controlled studies.

Muscle thickness in the ULIF was found to be a significant predictor of HBL. A thicker paraspinal muscle at the target level results in a greater amount of HBL. As a result of surgical manoeuvres, not only the bone surface and spinal canal but also muscle and subcutaneous fat are damaged, resulting in bleeding. Interestingly, in our study, HBL was not associated with subcutaneous fat thickness but with muscle thickness. The following reasons may account for this difference. First, muscle tissue may have a more abundant blood supply than fat tissue. A thicker paraspinal muscle at the target level suggested that the ULIF procedure requires the creation of a longer working channel, increasing the extent of the wound and increasing the incidence of intraoperative haemorrhage. Second, compared with fat tissue, blood infiltrates into surrounding muscle tissue more easily. More importantly, the manipulation focuses on the paravertebral muscle instead of fat tissue. These findings are consistent with previous studies of HBL in patients with cervical or lumbar fusions [12, 26].

We believe that preoperative and postoperative blood tests are necessary to clarify whether a patient is anaemic or predisposed to anaemia during the perioperative period after ULIF surgery. The surgical team should also pay attention to the ASA classification, operation time and number of operation levels to evaluate whether the patient is at increased risk of postoperative bleeding. Intraoperative intravenous tranexamic acid is recommended for reducing HBL in ULIF surgery.

There are several limitations to our study due to its retrospective nature. First, our sample size was small, leading to a high potential for bias. Second, additional parameters need to be included to fully assess the risk factors. Third, when we reviewed relevant studies and references, there was no consensus on when to remove the drains. In daily clinical practice, drains are removed after 50 ml of drainage fluid has passed through the drain within 24 h. Further investigations are warranted to determine how the timing of drain removal or evaluation criteria can impact the outcome of HBL. Consequently, additional validation of the study findings is necessary, particularly through the inclusion of related research conducted in diverse regions and countries.

Conclusion

A significant amount of hidden blood loss (HBL) was observed in patients undergoing unilateral lumbar interbody fusion (ULIF) surgery. Independent risk factors for HBL included the ASA classification and operation time. Tranexamic acid administration was negatively correlated with HBL.
