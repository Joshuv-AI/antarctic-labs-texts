A hybrid fuzzy–ensemble method for time series forecasting
Authors: Ali Zafer Dalar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study proposes a rule-free hybrid forecasting framework that integrates type-1 fuzzy function modeling with ensemble regression via random forests. The proposed hybrid fuzzy–ensemble model first constructs lagged representations of a univariate time series and applies fuzzy c-means clustering to obtain soft partitions of the input space. For each cluster, a dedicated random forest regressor is trained, and final forecasts are produced through membership-weighted aggregation of cluster-level predictions. This architecture eliminates the need for expert-defined fuzzy rules while enabling the model to capture both local (regime-dependent) and global temporal dynamics. Key hyperparameters, including lag order, number of clusters, membership thresholds, and random forest settings, are jointly optimized using random search on the training data. The framework is evaluated on thirteen real-world time series from financial, industrial, and environmental domains. Experimental results demonstrate competitive and, in several cases, improved forecasting accuracy compared to classical statistical methods, fuzzy regression models, and plain random forest baselines. The findings suggest that combining soft fuzzy partitioning with cluster-specific ensemble learning provides a flexible and data-driven mechanism for modeling nonlinear structures and gradual regime-like behaviors within univariate time series.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-51585-w.

Introduction

Forecasting time series data continues to be a foundational task in numerous scientific and applied disciplines, ranging from finance and meteorology to energy systems and healthcare analytics. However, the inherent uncertainty, non-linearity, and potential non-stationarity of real-world time series often challenge the predictive power of classical statistical models. In recent years, the integration of machine learning and fuzzy inference systems has emerged as a promising avenue to overcome these limitations.

Fuzzy systems have long been employed for time series forecasting due to their ability to handle uncertainty and linguistic ambiguity in data. However, conventional fuzzy inference systems (FISs) often rely on rule-based formulations, where defining and tuning fuzzy rules can be highly subjective, labor-intensive, and domain-dependent. Moreover, these systems may struggle when faced with complex, nonlinear, and high-dimensional datasets where interpretability and scalability must be balanced.

Recent studies have attempted to alleviate these challenges by incorporating machine learning models such as neural networks or support vector machines (SVMs) into fuzzy frameworks. Nevertheless, such hybrid approaches still often suffer from issues related to overfitting, lack of interpretability, and the need for extensive hyperparameter tuning. In particular, defining the structure of fuzzy rule bases remains a persistent bottleneck in expanding fuzzy systems to real-world large-scale datasets.

Fuzzy systems, particularly Type-1 Fuzzy Functions (T1FFs), offer a powerful framework for handling the imprecision and ambiguity often found in observational time series data. Unlike traditional rule-based fuzzy systems that rely on expert-defined logic rules1,2, T1FFs eliminate the need for a rule base by constructing fuzzy functions directly from data through regression-based learning3,4. This characteristic makes them especially suitable for large-scale or automated forecasting contexts.

In parallel with the evolution of fuzzy systems, ensemble learning models have become increasingly prominent in handling high-dimensional and nonlinear time series. Random Forests (RFs), first introduced by Breiman5, remain one of the most influential ensemble learning methods, particularly known for their ability to model complex nonlinear relationships while reducing variance through bootstrap aggregation. Their robustness against noise, automatic feature selection, and reduced susceptibility to over-fitting make them highly desirable for forecasting applications where interpretability and performance must coexist. In its classical formulation, RFs construct an ensemble of decision trees over bootstrap samples and use feature randomness to enhance generalization. Owing to these properties, RFs have been widely adopted in fields ranging from financial forecasting to environmental modeling and bioinformatics6.

Building on the complementary strengths of rule-free fuzzy functions and ensemble learning, this study introduces a hybrid model that eliminates rule dependency by integrating T1FFs with Random Forests—an ensemble method known for its robustness and minimal tuning requirements—hereafter referred to as the proposed hybrid fuzzy–ensemble model. Our method employs fuzzy C-means (FCMs) clustering to obtain soft partitions of the lagged input space that may correspond to regime-like behaviours, and then trains separate RFs models per cluster. Forecasts are aggregated through a fuzzy membership-weighted mechanism. In addition, a randomized hyper-parameter search framework is employed to tune both the fuzzy and ensemble learning components jointly. An analytical discussion of this mechanism is provided in the Analytical properties of the proposed model subsection.

Real-world time series often exhibit a mixture of linear and nonlinear dynamics arising from structural breaks, regime changes, volatility clusters and interaction effects that cannot be captured by a single global linear model. Although classical T1FFs already eliminate the need for rule bases and provide a flexible soft-partitioning mechanism, each fuzzy region still relies on a linear regression surface. This assumption becomes restrictive for financial and industrial series, where local patterns are highly nonlinear and may vary substantially across regimes. Prior studies have attempted to address this limitation by replacing the linear consequents with neural networks or other nonlinear learners, but such models typically suffer from increased tuning complexity and reduced interpretability. Motivated by these gaps, the present study replaces the linear regressors of T1FFs with cluster-specific Random Forests, a nonparametric ensemble method capable of capturing complex nonlinear relations with minimal parameter tuning. Importantly, while Random Forests are not inherently interpretable at the individual tree level, the proposed architecture retains a form of structural interpretability through its fuzzy soft partitions. The fuzzy C-means memberships provide an explicit and transparent mechanism that governs how local models contribute to the final prediction, thereby preserving the rule-free organizational structure of T1FFs without relying on expert-defined if–then rules. Membership-weighted aggregation ensures smooth transitions between regimes, yielding a data-driven forecasting model that is both durable and capable of adapting to heterogeneous temporal structures.

This study addresses three practical challenges in fuzzy time-series forecasting: (i) reliance on expert-defined rule bases that hampers scalability and portability across datasets, (ii) instability of single global models under regime shifts and local nonlinearities, and (iii) the difficulty of tuning interacting hyperparameters at scale. Our approach tackles (i) by adopting rule-free fuzzy functions, (ii) by learning cluster-specific regressors combined through membership-weighted aggregation, and (iii) by selecting all model parameters—including the number of clusters—via random search on the training data only.

Conventional type-1 fuzzy rule–based forecasting systems rely on explicitly defined “if–then’’ rules whose antecedents describe regions of the input space and whose consequents are typically linear or affine functions. Such models require rule extraction, rule pruning, and rule-weight tuning, which introduce subjectivity and impose structural constraints on the final model. In contrast, the proposed framework is rule-free: fuzzy C-means is used solely to obtain soft partitions of the lagged input space, and each cluster is modeled by a data-driven random forest regressor rather than an explicit rule consequent. This eliminates the need for expert-defined rules, avoids manual rule design and optimization, and shifts interpretability to the memberships that govern how local models contribute to the final forecast.

While the architecture is generic, the evidence presented in this paper is limited to offline univariate forecasting problems with moderate series length and real-valued responses. Therefore, claims of generalizability and scalability are restricted to time series with characteristics similar to the thirteen financial, industrial, and environmental datasets studied here.

Literature review

Initial studies on fuzzy time series (FTS) models, such as those pioneered by7–9, primarily utilized type-1 fuzzy logic with predefined rules. Over the years, several extensions have been proposed, including high-order FTS, weighted FTS, and hybrid models that combine fuzzy logic with neural networks, ARIMA, or support vector machines.

More recent advances include intuitionistic fuzzy time series models (e.g.10,11, type-2 fuzzy systems for uncertainty modeling12, and deep learning-fuzzy hybrids such as those incorporating LSTM13 architectures. While these approaches enhance forecasting flexibility, they often suffer from interpretability issues or require intensive parameter tuning.

The fusion of fuzzy inference systems and machine learning models has catalyzed significant advancements in time series forecasting over the past two decades. This integration is particularly visible in the evolution of fuzzy regression functions (FRFs), which bypass traditional fuzzy rule bases by using data-driven regressors built over fuzzy clusters. Classical fuzzy rule-based systems, while intuitive, often rely on subjective rule extraction and lack scalability for large datasets. This led researchers to explore rule-free paradigms such as fuzzy functions, which bypass rule elicitation by directly modeling the input-output relationships through parametric or non-parametric functions. This historical evolution from rule-based systems to fuzzy functions is thoroughly reviewed by Turksen14, outlining the paradigm shift in fuzzy inference architectures.

The foundational concept of fuzzy functions was pioneered by Turksen4 and further developed in subsequent works15. These methods construct fuzzy functions for each cluster, using membership values as input features, and have shown effectiveness across various forecasting tasks. Additional contributions expanding the application of fuzzy functions for time series forecasting include the works by Aladag et al.16 and Dalar et al.17, who demonstrated the effectiveness of T1FFs using covariate-based structures and benchmark datasets such as Australian beer production and electricity consumption. Extensions such as the Meta Fuzzy Functions (MFFs) and Meta Possibilistic Fuzzy Functions (MPFFs) proposed by Tak18,19 improved upon classical T1FFs by incorporating ensemble and possibilistic frameworks.

Recent advancements in fuzzy clustering have extended the classical FCM algorithm used in T1FFs. For instance, Bas and Egrioglu20 proposed the use of the Gustafson–Kessel (GK) clustering algorithm to enhance the adaptability of fuzzy regression functions, especially in detecting ellipsoidal clusters. Similarly, Tak21 developed an autoregressive moving average type possibilistic fuzzy forecasting model (ARMA-PFFs), combining autoregressive structure with possibilistic clustering and metaheuristic optimization via the Grey Wolf Optimizer, offering robustness against noisy data and improved forecasting flexibility. Recent extensions have also incorporated metaheuristic optimization strategies. For example, Dalar22 employed particle swarm optimization (PSO) to fine-tune T1FF parameters for improved performance across multiple benchmark time series, including stock and index data.

The robustness of FRFs in the presence of outliers has been a recurring concern. Several researchers have proposed robust versions of fuzzy regression functions by introducing noise clusters or applying robust loss functions. For instance, Chakravarty et al.23 introduced Modified Fuzzy Regression Functions with Noise Clusters (MFRFN), demonstrating enhanced accuracy in wind speed estimation tasks. Earlier work by the same authors24 benchmarked this approach against classical machine learning models and highlighted the performance gains under adversarial data conditions. Similarly, Bas25 and Egrioglu and Bas26 explored robust FRFs using robust estimation techniques such as Huber and Cauchy loss functions to mitigate the influence of outliers in both dependent and independent variables.

From a modelling architecture standpoint, hybrid frameworks that integrate fuzzy systems with neural networks or support vector machines have also garnered attention. Aktoprak and Cagcag Yolcu27 proposed Fuzzy Regression Network Functions (FRNFs), where non-linear dependencies are modelled via multiple feedforward neural networks, each associated with a fuzzy cluster. Their architecture provides a flexible and interpretable alternative to black-box deep learning models. The FRF-SVM model proposed by Baser and Demirhan28 provided improvements in solar radiation estimation. Beyond neural and SVM-based hybrids, recent studies have explored recurrent and bootstrapped versions of fuzzy functions. Egrioglu et al.29 introduced recurrent fuzzy time series functions and demonstrated their success across energy and economic datasets. Tak30 incorporated recurrent intuitionistic fuzzy structures and used grey wolf optimization to improve performance under uncertainty. Recent advances have emphasized recurrent and elastic-net-based fuzzy systems (E-FRFs, Tak and Inan31, ridge-regularized variants to mitigate multicollinearity issues32, and hybrid architectures combining elastic-net fuzzy regression with deep models like long short-term memory (LSTM)33. Although the Tak and Inan31‘s E-FRFs model addresses overfitting via regularization and provides interpretability, it still operates on a global modeling structure, which may overlook localized data dynamics. In contrast, the proposed method leverages fuzzy clustering to decompose the series and learns localized models through random forests, thus capturing heterogeneous temporal behaviors more effectively. Gourdazi et al.34 introduced recurrent fuzzy functions structures within nonlinear autoregressive models with exogenous inputs (NARX) for modeling chaotic time series, demonstrating improved performance on systems with hidden attractors. In particular, Dalar and Egrioglu35,36 proposed hybrid frameworks incorporating subsampling bootstrap mechanisms for generating confidence intervals within fuzzy regression functions. This enhancement not only improves forecast reliability but also enables probabilistic scenario modeling—especially valuable in financial and high-volatility domains.

Several researchers have focused on rule-based fuzzy systems, such as Takagi and Sugeno2 and Mamdani and Assilian37 models, for time series forecasting. While these systems provide interpretability, their reliance on expert-defined rules often limits scalability and generalizability across heterogeneous datasets. To mitigate this, fuzzy functions approaches—such as those proposed by Aladag et al.16 and Dalar et al.17—have emerged, offering rule-free architectures. However, these models may struggle to adapt to local fluctuations in high-variance series due to their single-model nature. In parallel, ensemble learning methods, particularly RFs, have gained popularity for capturing nonlinear dynamics and handling noise, but their integration with fuzzy reasoning remains underexplored. Previous hybrid methods (e.g., fuzzy clustering with neural networks or SVMs) have shown improvements but often require complex tuning or lack interpretability. These gaps underscore the need for a flexible yet interpretable hybrid approach that leverages the advantages of both fuzzy reasoning and ensemble learning.

In the context of time series forecasting, RFs have gained significant traction due to their non-parametric, data-driven nature. Despite not being explicitly designed for fuzzy systems, their capability to handle non-linearities and interactions complements fuzzy methods well. Recent studies (e.g., Wei et al.38 have shown that combining RFs with fuzzy inference structures can yield highly competitive forecasting results, especially under uncertainty and heterogeneity.

Beyond hybrid fuzzy-neural architectures, recent studies have increasingly adopted RFs as core regressors for time series prediction tasks. Owing to their inherent ensemble nature, RFs are particularly well-suited for handling nonlinearity, noise, and variable interactions in high-dimensional temporal data. For instance, Tihi and Popov39 conducted a comparative study of auto regressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) and RF models for urban drought prediction, concluding that although classical models performed well under specific assumptions, RFs offered competitive accuracy and greater adaptability to changing climate conditions. Similarly, Altincop and Oktay40 applied RF models for air quality forecasting in Turkish metropolitan regions, demonstrating that RFs outperformed artificial neural networks in predicting PM10 and CO concentrations across fluctuating meteorological conditions. These findings underscore the versatility of RFs in real-world forecasting tasks, especially when data exhibit structural heterogeneity and require robust nonparametric learning mechanisms. As such, the integration of RFs into fuzzy inference frameworks—as pursued in our study—aligns with this broader trend in the literature, combining the explainability of fuzzy partitioning with the predictive strength of tree-based ensembles.

In addition to their general strengths in forecasting, recent studies have increasingly recognized RFs as effective components in fuzzy system architectures. In line with this trend, ensemble models—particularly RFs—have attracted attention as robust, nonparametric alternatives to conventional regressors in fuzzy systems. While most fuzzy forecasting systems rely on traditional statistical or neural components, integrating RFs introduces additional flexibility and interpretability. Additionally, algorithmic variants such as Extremely Randomized Trees41 and efficient implementations like ranger42 point to further opportunities for scaling fuzzy-ensemble hybrids in real-world settings.

Our work situates itself at the intersection of these research streams. While previous studies have explored fuzzy–neural hybrids or fuzzy–SVM integrations, our approach distinguishes itself by embedding RF regression into the structure of Type-1 Fuzzy Functions, enabling a transparent, interpretable, and high-performing model architecture. Moreover, the use of a randomized hyper-parameter optimization strategy allows the model to dynamically adapt to the characteristics of each dataset, enhancing generalizability across domains.

To summarize, while several fuzzy computing models have been proposed for time series forecasting, many suffer from either rule dependency, lack of flexibility, or limited model interpretability. This study aims to bridge these gaps by integrating fuzzy partitioning with ensemble regression to deliver a more adaptive and rule-free framework for time series forecasting.

The proposed methodology

This section introduces the mathematical and algorithmic formulation of the proposed hybrid fuzzy–ensemble model, designed for univariate time series forecasting. The methodology integrates soft partitioning through FCM clustering and regression modeling through RFs, yielding a hybrid architecture capable of capturing both local and global nonlinearities without requiring expert-defined fuzzy rules.

The forecasting process proceeds through three primary stages: (i) fuzzy partitioning of the lagged time series using FCM, (ii) feature transformation and cluster-specific RF model training, and (iii) aggregation of predictions based on fuzzy memberships weights.

While the RF training itself follows the standard regression formulation, its role in the proposed framework is not to introduce a new tree-based learner, but to act as a variance-reducing nonlinear approximator within each fuzzy region. This architectural placement is critical, as it decouples regime identification (handled by FCM) from local nonlinear approximation (handled by RF), which is not present in classical type-1 fuzzy functions.

Figure 1 illustrates the internal aggregation mechanism of a RF within a single fuzzy region, whereas Fig. 2 summarizes how these cluster-specific ensembles are embedded into the overall fuzzy–ensemble forecasting pipeline.

Random forests were chosen for the cluster-level regressors because they require substantially less hyperparameter tuning than boosting algorithms and are known to be more durable to noisy or short time series due to their parallel, non-iterative structure5,45. In contrast, gradient boosting methods iteratively fit residual errors, which often increases the risk of overfitting within small cluster-specific subsets. RFs also provide smoother response surfaces and more stable behaviour in repeated training across different random seeds, making them suitable for repeated training inside each fuzzy region.

Although the experiments in this study focus on univariate time series, the structure of the proposed framework naturally accommodates higher-dimensional inputs. Random forests mitigate the effects of high dimensionality through the random subspace mechanism, in which each tree is trained on a randomly selected subset of features46. This procedure reduces the effective dimensionality seen by individual trees and is known to improve learning stability in high-dimensional settings5. Unlike boosting methods, which may propagate noise across iterative residual fits in large feature spaces, RFs maintain decorrelation between trees and avoid aggressive feature–residual interactions45. Consequently, if multivariate extensions are explored in future work, the cluster-specific RFs would remain computationally feasible and would not require explicit feature-selection heuristics at each fuzzy region.

As shown in Fig. 2, the process integrates both data-driven partitioning and ensemble learning. Combined with fuzzy soft partitions, this design preserves a level of structural interpretability inherited from T1FFs at the partition level—where fuzzy memberships indicate regime affiliation—while allowing each cluster to learn its own nonlinear local dynamics through data-driven ensemble models. The subsequent section outlines how cluster-level predictions are aggregated into a final forecast.

In the proposed model, each fuzzy cluster is equipped with a RF regressor. RFs construct multiple decision trees trained on randomly selected subsets of features, following the random subspace principle introduced by Ho46. This mechanism produces structural diversity among trees even when bootstrap sampling is disabled, as is the case in all optimal configurations identified by the random search procedure (see Table 6). The final prediction is obtained by averaging the outputs of the trees5, which is known to reduce the instability associated with individual decision trees and yields smoother aggregated responses. In this study, RF therefore contributes a flexible nonlinear approximation component at the cluster level without relying on sample-level resampling.

In contrast to grid search—which evaluates all parameter combinations and scales poorly with dimensionality—random search explores the hyperparameter space more efficiently by sampling from predefined ranges. As shown by Bergstra and Bengio47, random search allocates trials to the most influential parameters and often reaches near-optimal configurations with typically fewer evaluations. Within the proposed framework, this leads to improved scalability because both fuzzy and RF components contain multiple interacting hyperparameters. Moreover, since each sampled configuration induces a different fuzzy partition and a different set of local forests, variability across trials implicitly assesses the stability of the model structure. This mechanism reduces the risk of over-fitting to a particular cluster configuration and enables the procedure to adapt to heterogeneous datasets without exhaustive exploration. The empirical results in the Results and discussion section illustrate that random search consistently identified moderate, stable cluster numbers and tree settings, supporting its suitability for the proposed hybrid forecaster.

Analytical properties of the proposed model

The proposed hybrid forecaster can be viewed as a three–stage mapping in which lagged vectors are first constructed, then softly partitioned by fuzzy c-means, and finally passed through a set of cluster-specific Random Forest regressors whose outputs are combined by membership-weighted averaging. This section summarizes several analytical properties of this architecture, clarifying why it is well suited to heterogeneous, nonlinear time series without relying on explicit fuzzy rules.

First, the FCM stage induces an overlapping partition of the lagged input space that minimizes the within-cluster dispersion in the Euclidean sense43,44. Because each row of the lag matrix encodes a short history window of the series, samples with similar recent trajectories tend to receive similar membership profiles. The resulting membership matrix therefore acts as a data-driven representation of local temporal regimes: different clusters emphasize different regions of the lag space, while the fuzziness parameter and the threshold \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\alpha\:}_{cut}$$\end{document} jointly control the degree of overlap between these regions. Unlike hard clustering, this soft assignment allows each time point to influence multiple local models, which is important when regime boundaries are gradual rather than abrupt.

Second, within each cluster the mapping from lag vectors to one-step-ahead responses is learned nonparametrically by a Random Forest regressor. RFs build an ensemble of decision trees on randomly selected subsets of features and average their predictions5,46. This random subspace mechanism creates diversity among trees even when bootstrap resampling is disabled, as in the best-performing configurations found by the random search (Table 6). Training a separate forest on each cluster-specific subset al.lows the local models to adapt to distinct nonlinear patterns that may appear in different regions of the lag space, while still preserving the relatively low tuning effort and piecewise-constant approximation behaviour characteristic of tree ensembles.

Third, the final forecast at time \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:t$$\end{document} is obtained by aggregating the cluster-level predictions through the fuzzy memberships, as in (6). Provided that the memberships are non-negative and do not all vanish simultaneously, this yields a convex combination of the local forecasts. Consequently, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\widehat{y}}_{t}$$\end{document} remains within the range spanned by the cluster-level outputs and changes smoothly as the membership vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mu\:\left({\mathbf{d}}_{t}\right)$$\end{document} varies. This property distinguishes the proposed scheme from winner-takes-all or crisp switching models, where small perturbations in the input can trigger discontinuous jumps between regimes.

These analytical observations do not constitute formal consistency proofs or generalization guarantees; rather, they explain how the individual components—soft clustering, cluster-wise tree ensembles, and membership-weighted aggregation—interact to produce a flexible yet structured forecasting mechanism. The empirical results in the Results and discussion section illustrate that, under the experimental conditions considered in this study, this architecture can effectively accommodate regime changes and local nonlinearities without relying on hand-crafted fuzzy rules.

At the same time, the framework has clear limitations. The quality of the forecasts critically depends on the lagged representation and the FCM partition: if relevant lags are omitted or the series exhibits strongly non-spherical regimes, the induced clusters may not align with meaningful dynamics. Moreover, the method is essentially non-extrapolative: Random Forests, being tree-based, cannot reliably predict outside the range of training responses. Finally, the approach is designed for offline univariate forecasting; extending it to high-dimensional multivariate streams would require additional mechanisms for feature selection and concept drift adaptation, which are left for future work.

Across the financial, industrial, and environmental datasets considered in this study, no domain-specific failure modes were observed. Instead, performance variations were closely aligned with structural characteristics of the individual series. Highly volatile and regime-shifting financial indices (e.g., TAIEX 2000–2002, BIST 2011) reduced the stability of fuzzy partitions and made local RF regressors more sensitive to abrupt transitions. In contrast, smoother and more regularly evolving series—such as the seasonal Australian beer production data and the low-amplitude Giresun temperature records—yielded more coherent cluster structures and more stable local models. These results indicate that the proposed framework is broadly domain-agnostic; its limitations arise primarily from volatility, irregular regime changes, and weak local stationarity rather than from the application domain itself.

Results and discussion

In this section, the empirical performance of the proposed model is evaluated through experiments on multiple real-world time series datasets drawn from finance, manufacturing, and climate domains. The evaluation focuses on forecasting accuracy, model adaptability, and parameter behaviour across structurally diverse temporal datasets.

Experimental configuration and datasets

To validate the proposed methodology, thirteen time series datasets were utilized. The financial datasets include daily observations from the Borsa Istanbul Stock Exchange (BIST100) across five different years between 2009 and 201348, where the first five months of each year were used for training and the final 15 observations were held out for testing. Similarly, six datasets from the Taiwan Stock Exchange (TAIEX) were selected, covering daily measurements from 1999 to 200449. For each TAIEX series, the last two months of data were used as the test segment.

Beyond financial series, the model was also applied to an industrial dataset: the Australian beer production data (ABP50, , measured quarterly between March 1956 and June 1994. This series represents a well-known benchmark in forecasting literature due to its clear seasonality and gradual trend structure. The final 16 quarterly observations were excluded from training and used for forecasting evaluation.

Lastly, the methodology was tested on a meteorological dataset consisting of daily average temperatures observed in Giresun Province (Türkiye) between January and April 2017, a period marked by abrupt short-term temperature variations.

All experiments were conducted under a one-step-ahead forecasting horizon (h = 1). Multi-step forecasts were generated iteratively by recursively feeding each predicted value as an input for the subsequent time step. No direct multi-horizon models were trained in this study. This setup ensures consistency across datasets and facilitates fair comparison with existing univariate forecasting benchmarks.

To visually illustrate the temporal structures and variability across the selected datasets, Fig. 3 presents a sample of representative time series plots.

For each dataset, the model was trained using varying lag orders (from 1 to 15), number of fuzzy clusters (between 2 and 7), and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\alpha\:}_{\mathrm{c}\mathrm{u}\mathrm{t}}$$\end{document} membership thresholds (ranging from 0.0 to 0.2). Within each fuzzy region, RF regressors were constructed using randomized values for hyperparameters such as the number of estimators (between 10 and 200), tree depth (2 to 20), minimum sample split and leaf sizes, and bootstrap usage. A randomized hyperparameter search strategy was executed with 100 iterations to identify the best model configuration per dataset.

Computationally, one FCM fit on n samples with input dimension p and c clusters costs \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:O(n\:c\:p\cdot\:I)$$\end{document}, while a Random Forest with T trees costs \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:O(T\:n\:\mathrm{log}n\cdot\:m)$$\end{document}. At inference, computing memberships and traversing \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:c$$\end{document} forests costs \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:O\left(c\hspace{0.17em}p\:+\:c\hspace{0.17em}T\hspace{0.17em}d\right)$$\end{document} per time point. Because membership thresholding (αcut) reduces effective nk in practice, the sum \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\sum\:}_{k}{n}_{k}\mathrm{log}{n}_{k}$$\end{document} often grows sublinearly with c. The cluster-wise structure is also amenable to parallelization across k=1, …, c but no wall-clock times are reported here since our focus is asymptotic complexity and predictive accuracy.

We adopt randomized hyperparameter search since, in high-dimensional and non–axis-aligned spaces like (c, p , αcut and RF params), uniform random sampling explores more distinct configurations than grid search at the same budget and is supported by strong empirical/theoretical evidence51.

Performance was assessed on test segments using standard forecasting metrics: root mean square error (RMSE) and mean absolute percentage error (MAPE). Each forecasting experiment was independently seeded to preserve stochastic reproducibility.

Detailed descriptions of each dataset, including sampling frequency, forecast horizon, and original data characteristics, are available in the referenced studies from which the datasets were obtained. In this work, all series are used in their original univariate form without additional domain-specific preprocessing, ensuring consistency and fair comparability with prior benchmark evaluations.

All three ablation baselines (Plain RF, Standard Type-1 Fuzzy Functions, and Global RF with membership features) were fully re-implemented by the author(s) and evaluated under identical preprocessing, lag construction, hyperparameter search protocol, and chronological train–test splits as the proposed model.

Other comparison results cited from the literature are explicitly indicated in the corresponding tables.

All preprocessing parameters were estimated exclusively on the training data to prevent information leakage into the test set. All implementations were conducted using Python 3.10.6. The random forest models were constructed via the Random Forest Regressor class from the scikit-learn library52, a widely adopted machine learning toolkit. Apart from this external module, all algorithmic components—including fuzzy partitioning, membership-based transformations, and model orchestration—were custom-developed by the author to enable full control over the proposed methodology and its evaluation pipeline.

Hyperparameter search results revealed a tendency toward compact fuzzy structures: most optimal models selected 2 to 4 clusters and low to zero \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\alpha\:}_{\mathrm{c}\mathrm{u}\mathrm{t}}$$\end{document} thresholds, supporting the hypothesis that soft overlaps among partitions enhance generalization. Decision trees typically converged to moderate depths (5 to 10), suggesting that deep trees were not necessary to capture localized behaviors due to the already segmented input space. Bootstrap sampling was consistently disabled in all optimal configurations, with no dataset—regardless of its domain or temporal structure—favoring bootstrapped resampling. We prefer random search over exhaustive grid search because the joint space (c, lagn, αcut and RF params) is high-dimensional and interactions are non-separable; sampling-based search identifies good regions with far fewer evaluations, a well-established result in the hyperparameter optimization literature51.

Because the experiments follow an iterative one-step-ahead protocol, error accumulation may occur in longer forecasting sequences. Investigating direct multi-horizon extensions constitutes a direction for future research.

Overall, these results emphasize that the success of the proposed model lies not only in the integration of fuzzy logic and ensemble learning but also in its ability to finely tune model settings according to the underlying structure of the input data. The experimental results suggest that the proposed hybrid fuzzy–ensemble model offers a competitive and, in several cases, superior alternative to established baselines within the class of univariate time series examined in this study. We do not claim universal superiority across all forecasting domains. In particular, the current analysis does not cover multivariate settings, high-frequency tick data, or streaming scenarios with real-time constraints. Extending the method to such regimes, and assessing its robustness under more extreme distributional shifts, are important directions for future work.

Although the proposed model does not incorporate explicit concept-drift detection or online adaptation mechanisms, its soft partitioning structure allows partial accommodation of regime-like behaviors through cluster-specific local models. However, in the presence of abrupt structural breaks or rapidly evolving drift patterns, performance may degrade, and online extensions would be required.

While the proposed methodology demonstrates strong forecasting performance, it inherently carries additional computational cost due to its dual-phase structure: fuzzy partitioning and per-cluster ensemble training. The application of random search over a multidimensional hyperparameter space further increases runtime. However, these costs are mitigated by the parallelizable nature of both the clustering and random forest training steps. In practice, this allows the model to scale well with modern multi-core computing architectures. Future work may focus on integrating reinforcement learning for adaptive hyperparameter tuning or applying the proposed method to multivariate and streaming time series scenarios.

Notably, these parameter trends were consistent across datasets with markedly different temporal structures, suggesting that the proposed framework adapts its complexity primarily through data-driven partitioning rather than manual tuning.

Conclusion

This study introduced a hybrid forecasting methodology that integrates type-1 fuzzy function modeling with ensemble regression via random forests. By combining soft clustering of lagged inputs with localized ensemble learning, the proposed framework removes the need for manually defined fuzzy rules and offers a structured way to capture both global and local nonlinearities in univariate, offline time series. This architecture provides a data-driven alternative to conventional fuzzy inference systems while maintaining interpretability through membership-weighted aggregation.

The inclusion of RF–based baselines in the empirical evaluation further clarifies the contribution of the proposed architecture. While plain and global RF models benefit from nonlinear approximation and ensemble averaging, their performance was consistently inferior to the proposed approach across datasets with pronounced regime changes. This observation suggests that the performance gains are not solely attributable to the use of tree ensembles, but rather to their integration with fuzzy soft partitioning, which enables localized learning and smooth regime transitions that global models fail to capture.

Empirical evaluations across thirteen real-world datasets—including financial indices, industrial production data, and meteorological measurements—showed that the method can adapt to heterogeneous temporal structures. The model achieved competitive performance on series with varying degrees of volatility, smoothness, and seasonal regularity, illustrating the complementary strengths of fuzzy partitioning and tree-based ensemble regression. The randomized hyperparameter search further enabled automatic adjustment of lag order, cluster number, and RF configurations without domain-specific tuning, supporting the model’s applicability across diverse datasets.

The main contribution of this work lies in establishing a flexible and reproducible learning mechanism that unifies soft computing principles with ensemble modeling under a rule-free structure. While the model leverages only standard components—FCM clustering, nonlinear membership transformations, and Random Forest regressors—their integration into a coherent forecasting pipeline enables a level of adaptability and interpretability not typically present in purely statistical or purely machine-learning approaches. Future extensions may investigate multivariate forecasting settings, interval-valued or type-2 fuzzy formulations to better represent uncertainty, and more efficient ensemble variants, such as Extremely Randomized Trees41, to further enhance model diversity and reduce computational cost.

Despite its promising results, the framework exhibits several limitations. Forecast quality may degrade on very short or highly unstable series, where fuzzy partitions become poorly defined and cluster-specific models lack sufficient support. Membership distributions that are excessively diffuse or imbalanced can also reduce the reliability of local regressors. In addition, the current empirical evaluation does not include intermittent or zero-inflated time series. In such settings, where long stretches of zero values dominate the signal, both fuzzy partitioning and tree-based regression may require structural modifications (e.g., demand-occurrence modeling or alternative loss functions). Although random search provides a scalable optimization strategy, it may still miss subtle parameter interactions in high-dimensional search spaces. Furthermore, as with most clustering-based techniques, extreme outliers can distort membership patterns; while the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\alpha\:}_{cut}$$\end{document} threshold mitigates such effects, robust or possibilistic clustering (e.g., GK or PFF) variants may yield improved stability under heavy-tailed noise. Addressing these challenges, particularly through adaptive clustering mechanisms and hybrid optimization strategies, constitutes a promising direction for future research.
