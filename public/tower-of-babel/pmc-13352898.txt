Construction of a predictive model for the risk of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease in patients with sleep apnea syndrome based on multiple machine learning algorithms: a multicenter study
Authors: Shu Yang, Ming Huo, Guoqing Li, Jinfang Zeng, Yusong Zhang, Xiao Zhang, Xiao Liang, Minmin Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSleep apnea syndrome (SAS) is closely related to an increased risk of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), but current clinical tools lack the integration of multidimensional data for accurate risk prediction. This study employs various machine learning algorithms to develop and validate a risk prediction model for the occurrence of NAFLD in SAS patients.MethodsThis retrospective multi-center study used data from 595 SAS patients diagnosed in 2024 at Wuxi Fifth People’s Hospital (training cohort) and 372 patients from Wuxi Second People’s Hospital (external validation cohort). Demographic, anthropometric, biochemical, and comorbidity data were collected. Multivariate logistic regression was used to explore the risk factors for NAFLD in SAS patients, and restricted cubic spline (RCS) non-linear trend analysis was performed on the risk factors. After preprocessing and variable selection using Boruta and LASSO regression, nine machine learning models were trained. The best-performing model was selected based on the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) and underwent external validation.ResultsMultifactorial logistic regression analysis found that sex, obesity, hyperlipidemia, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), AST to ALT ratio, uric acid, γ-glutamyltransferase (GGT), albumin, and PLT were significantly associated with the occurrence of NAFLD in SAS patients. The aspartate aminotransferase to alanine aminotransferase ratio (AST to ALT ratio), GGT, albumin, and platelet count (PLT) exhibited a non-linear relationship with the occurrence of NAFLD in SAS patients. Among the 9 machine learning models, logistic regression was the best model (AUC = 0.752, 95% CI: 0.654–0.850). Key predictive features included LDL-C, hyperlipidemia, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), AST/ALT, and GGT. SHAP analysis showed that elevated LDL-C, hyperlipidemia, and GGT increased the risk of NAFLD, while higher HDL-C levels had a protective effect. The AST/ALT ratio was negatively correlated with risk.ConclusionThis study found that sex, obesity, hyperlipidemia, LDL-C, AST to ALT ratio, uric acid, GGT, albumin, and PLT were significantly associated with the occurrence of NAFLD in SAS patients. The logistic regression model established in this study effectively predicts the risk of NAFLD in SAS patients, emphasizing the core role of lipid metabolism abnormalities and markers of oxidative stress. This tool provides a practical and easily interpretable solution for early risk stratification and targeted intervention in clinical practice.Clinical trial number Not applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12911-026-03547-5.

Introduction

Sleep Apnea Syndrome (SAS) is a heterogeneous group of disorders characterized by recurrent episodes of apnea during sleep, mainly including Obstructive Sleep Apnea (OSA), Central Sleep Apnea (CSA), and Mixed Type, with OSA being the most common subtype, associated with an increased risk of various metabolic diseases [1, 2]. Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), as an increasingly prevalent chronic liver disease, has been confirmed to be closely associated with obstructive sleep apnea (OSA): studies have shown that sleep fragmentation indicators in OSA patients, such as awakening index (AI) and rapid eye movement apnea-hypopnea index (REM-AHI), are independent predictors of NAFLD [3, 4], suggesting that intermittent hypoxia and micro-awakening may promote hepatic steatosis through oxidative stress and inflammatory pathways [5, 6]. At the same time, the clinical phenotype of OSA shows high diversity. For example, cluster analysis indicates that middle-aged men in the severe OSA group often exhibit more severe metabolic disorders and a higher prevalence of NAFLD [7]. This highlights the heterogeneity of NAFLD risk among patients with SAS (especially the OSA subtype). However, current clinical practice for assessing NAFLD in SAS patients largely relies on isolated polysomnography parameters (such as the apnea-hypopnea index) or biochemical markers, lacking a comprehensive tool that can integrate multidimensional features (including population statistics, anthropometric data, detailed sleep structure parameters, blood markers, and comorbidities).

Traditional statistical models also have limitations in capturing these complex nonlinear relationships. Machine learning algorithms, due to their advantages in handling high-dimensional data, identifying potential patterns, and optimizing predictive accuracy, provide new avenues for developing individualized risk stratification models [8–10]. This study aims to construct and validate a NAFLD risk prediction model for SAS patients using various machine learning algorithms, with the goal of providing early warning and precise intervention strategies for a broader SAS population, filling the gap in current risk assessment tools.

Materials and methods

Study design and subjects

This study is a retrospective study. Patients diagnosed with sleep apnea syndrome in 2022–2024 were obtained from Wuxi Fifth People’s Hospital, and all their clinical information was collected to construct a forecasting model. Information from patients diagnosed with sleep apnea syndrome in 2022–2024 was obtained from Wuxi Second People’s Hospital for external validation. The requirement for informed consent was waived because the data used was anonymized. All methods were carried out in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations, including the Declaration of Helsinki. For this study, data from all patients was collected when they were first diagnosed with sleep apnea. All patients followed the inclusion criteria as follows: (1) Age greater than 18 years; (2) First diagnosed SAS, apnea-hypopnea index (AHI) ≥ 5 times/h, confirmed by polysomnography (PSG) or type III sleep monitoring; (3) Has not received treatment such as CPAP, surgery, or oral appliances; (4) Newly developed NAFLD after the initial diagnosis of sleep apnea syndrome; (5) Never diagnosed with liver-related metabolic diseases; (6) Glucocorticoids, amiodarone, methotrexate, estrogens, and high-dose statins with clear hepatotoxicity/lipid-altering drugs were not used; (7) Non-pregnant or non-lactating period. Primary endpoint

New-onset NAFLD, defined as: the first detection of hepatic steatosis via transabdominal ultrasound during follow-up (meeting ≥ 2 of the following criteria: (i) increased liver echogenicity; (ii) enhanced liver-kidney echogenicity contrast; (iii) blurred intrahepatic vascular structures), with serum Alanine Aminotransferase (ALT) > 40 U/L (male) or > 35 U/L (female), while excluding excessive alcohol consumption (male < 30 g ethanol/day, female < 20 g ethanol/day), viral hepatitis (HBsAg, anti-hepatitis C virus negative), autoimmune liver disease, and other known causes of chronic liver disease. The time of NAFLD onset is defined as the follow-up time point when the diagnostic criteria are first met. The patients with outcome events included in this study all met the above requirements.

Data collection

In this study, personal information was processed using the “de-identification and safe harbor standards” method: 18 direct identifiers, such as name, national ID number, address, and phone number, were removed; birthdates were truncated to just the year, and medical visit dates were rounded to the nearest quarter; continuous variables (such as age and test values) were grouped according to clinical cut-off values, and extreme values were capped; free-text fields were manually checked to remove any terms that might reveal personal identity, and then we generated an irreversible unique code using a 256-bit secure hash algorithm (SHA-256) to replace the original medical record number. The processed dataset has been stripped of ‘identifiable information’ as defined by China’s Personal Information Protection Law (PIPL). A third-party privacy risk assessment confirmed that the likelihood of re-identification was less than 0.05, in compliance with the safe harbor provisions of the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA).

We collected biochemical blood data from the hospital system, including patients’ age, gender, ALT, aspartate aminotransferase (AST), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), white blood cell count (WBC), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and other related biochemical markers. All data were from patients who were first diagnosed with obstructive sleep apnea, and we deleted any variables that had more than 40% missing data.

Statistical analysis

Data that were continuously distributed and followed a normal distribution are depicted as the Mean ± Standard Deviation, with the t-test for two independent samples used to assess differences between the two groups. For continuously distributed data that were not normally distributed, the Median (Q₁, Q₃) is used for representation, and the Mann-Whitney U test is applied for comparing two independent samples. Categorical data are presented as frequencies (n (%)), with the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test used for comparisons. A significance level of P < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.

We used multivariate logistic regression analysis to explore the significant factors associated with the occurrence of NAFLD in those with sleep apnea syndrome, and we used restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis to examine the nonlinear relationship between significant factors and NAFLD. We used Lasso regression to select variables from the training set, and we trained nine machine learning models, including XGBoost, LightGBM, Random Forest, Adaboost, Decision Tree, MLP, SVM, KNN, and Logistic Regression, using AUC as the primary metric. After we selected the best model, we used external data for validation and conducted an interpretation of the best model. The interpretability of the model was assessed using feature importance analysis, SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) analysis, decision curve analysis (DCA) and calibration curves. Multiplicative interaction tests were performed to determine whether there were synergistic or antagonistic effects when the core characteristics worked together. Interactions were analyzed using the “epiR” package in R 4.5.1. We performed all analyses in this study using SPSS 26.0 and R 4.5.1.

Results

Patients’ baseline information

A total of 595 people were included in Wuxi fifth People’s Hospital (Table 1), among which 426 were non-NAFLD, accounting for 71.60%, and 169 were NAFLD, accounting for 28.40%. The results showed that there were significant differences between the two groups in variables such as age, hemoglobin, uric acid, total protein, atrial fibrillation, heart failure, cerebral infarction, obesity, and hyperlipidemia (p < 0.05). Patients in the NAFLD group were younger (51.0 vs. 55.4 years old), had higher proportions of obesity (20.7% vs. 5.4%) and hyperlipidemia (46.7% vs. 18.1%), while the prevalence of atrial fibrillation, heart failure, and cerebral infarction was lower. Other laboratory indicators such as triglycerides, ALT, gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), total cholesterol (TC), LDL-C, lymphocyte count, eosinophil count, and basophil count also showed intergroup differences, but baseline characteristics such as gender, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, coronary artery disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and chronic kidney disease (CKD) were evenly distributed.

Wuxi Second People’s Hospital included a total of 372 people (Supplementary Table 1), among which 244 were non-NAFLD, accounting for 65.59%, and 128 were NAFLD, accounting for 34.41%. Patients in the NAFLD group were significantly younger, but had significantly higher rates of obesity, hyperlipidemia, triglycerides, LDL, liver enzymes (ALT/AST/GGT), and uric acid levels. In contrast, the non-NAFLD group was older and showed a higher trend of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular comorbidities (coronary artery disease, cerebral infarction, atrial fibrillation).

Factors related to non-alcoholic fatty liver in sleep apnea syndrome

All samples included in this study underwent multifactorial logistic regression to explore the associated factors for the occurrence of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease in patients with sleep apnea syndromes. The results (Table 2) showed significant associations between sex, obesity, hyperlipidemia, LDL-C, AST to ALT ratio, uric acid, GGT, albumin, PLT and the occurrence of NAFLD. Among them, female sex (OR = 0.35), AST to ALT ratio (OR = 0.16), and PLT (OR = 0.99) were protective factors, while the remaining factors were risk factors. In multiple regression analysis, we found that the odds ratio (OR) 95% confidence interval (CI) for variables such as LDL-C and obesity was too wide, indicating that we had insufficient sample size or insufficient bias for the variable. Future studies will need to include larger populations, and our current study is only a preliminary exploration.

We further conducted RCS nonlinear trend analysis on variables significantly associated with the occurrence of NAFLD, and the results (Fig. 2) showed that AST to ALT ratio, GGT, albumin, and PLT all have a nonlinear relationship with NAFLD.

To verify the effectiveness of our model in different subgroups, we conducted subgroup analysis on the most important variables (LDL-C) of the model output and performed interaction tests on the relevant variables. To determine whether there is a synergistic or antagonistic effect when two or more exposure factors act together, we performed multiplicative interaction tests on the main characteristic factors. Discussion

This multicenter study developed and validated a machine learning-based risk prediction model for predicting the risk of NAFLD in patients with SAS. Our findings indicate that abnormal lipid metabolism, oxidative stress markers, and demographic factors collectively contribute to the pathogenesis of NAFLD in this population, with LDL-C, hyperlipidemia, HDL-C, AST/ALT ratio, and GGT emerging as the most influential predictors. These results provide new insights into the mechanistic link between SAS and hepatic steatosis, while offering a practical clinical tool for early risk stratification.

The strong association between SAS and NAFLD observed in our study is consistent with emerging evidence highlighting sleep-disordered breathing as a significant risk factor for hepatic metabolic dysfunction [1–3]. Although previous studies have primarily focused on OSA, our research encompasses a broader spectrum of SAS, including central and mixed types. This distinction is clinically significant because intermittent hypoxia, as a hallmark pathophysiological feature of SAS, triggers a series of molecular events that ultimately affect hepatic lipid metabolism, regardless of the underlying respiratory pattern [5, 6].

Intermittent hypoxia activates hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α), a master transcriptional regulator that orchestrates adaptive responses to oxygen deprivation [11, 12]. In the liver, HIF-1α activation upregulates sterol regulatory element-binding protein-1c (SREBP-1c), a key transcription factor driving de novo lipogenesis [11–13]. This molecular pathway explains why our model identified LDL-C and hyperlipidemia as the primary risk factors for the development of NAFLD in patients with SAS. Elevated LDL-C levels reflect increased hepatic very low-density lipoprotein (VLDL) secretion and impaired clearance function, while dyslipidemia is both a cause and a consequence of hepatic steatosis [14, 15]. Notably, our SHAP analysis revealed that elevated LDL-C consistently increases the risk of NAFLD across the entire prediction range, highlighting its central role in the pathogenesis of the disease.

The protective effect of HDL-C observed in our study deserves special attention. In addition to its established role in reverse cholesterol transport (RCT), HDL-C also exhibits potent antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, which may counteract the oxidative stress induced by intermittent hypoxia [16, 17]. Our interaction analysis shows that elevated levels of LDL-C attenuate the protective effect of HDL-C, suggesting that the balance between lipoproteins—rather than their absolute levels—determines metabolic risk. This finding has important clinical implications: therapeutic strategies targeting both the reduction of LDL-C and the elevation of HDL-C may be more effective than single-mode interventions.

GGT emerged as another critical predictor in our model, reflecting its role as a sensitive marker of oxidative stress and hepatic injury [18, 19]. The nonlinear relationship between GGT and NAFLD risk observed in our RCS analysis suggests a threshold effect, where moderate GGT elevation may indicate early adaptive responses, while persistently high levels suggest progressive hepatocyte damage. This interpretation is supported by longitudinal studies, which have shown that dynamic changes in GGT can independently predict the development of NAFLD, independent of other metabolic parameters [18]. Elevated GGT in patients with SAS may stem from multiple factors: direct hepatocyte damage induced by intermittent hypoxia -mediated oxidative stress [5, 6], mitochondrial dysfunction [20], and systemic inflammation [21].

The negative correlation between the AST/ALT ratio and the risk of NAFLD observed in our study may seem counterintuitive, but it is consistent with recent evidence suggesting a complex relationship between dynamic changes in transaminases and disease severity [22–24]. In the early stages of NAFLD, due to hepatocyte damage, elevated ALT typically predominates, resulting in a lower AST/ALT ratio. As the disease progresses to hepatic fibrosis, mitochondrial damage releases AST, causing this ratio to gradually increase [22, 23]. Our findings indicate that a lower AST/ALT ratio predicts the occurrence of NAFLD, thus likely reflecting early-stage disease detected in our cohort, where the release of ALT from damaged hepatocytes exceeds that of AST [24].

The gender differences in susceptibility to NAFLD observed in our study—with females having approximately a 65% lower risk—are consistent with established epidemiological patterns [25–27]. The protective effects mediated by estrogen are achieved through multiple mechanisms: inhibiting the expression of hepatic SREBP-1c, enhancing fatty acid oxidation via the activation of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor α (PPARα), and modulating the adipokine profile to favor insulin sensitivity [25, 26]. However, this protection diminishes after menopause, explaining the narrowing gender gap in NAFLD prevalence among older adults [27]. Our subgroup analysis confirmed that the association between LDL-C and NAFLD remained consistent across different gender stratifications, indicating that lipid metabolism represents a common pathway regardless of hormonal status.

The association between uric acid and NAFLD in SAS patients reflects the intersection of purine metabolism, oxidative stress, and insulin resistance [28, 29]. During the conversion of hypoxanthine to xanthine, the production of uric acid occurs simultaneously with the generation of reactive oxygen species, creating a pro-oxidative environment that exacerbates hepatic steatosis [28]. Furthermore, uric acid suppresses endothelial nitric oxide bioavailability, impairing hepatic microcirculation and promoting ischemia-reperfusion injury [29]. Our findings suggest that hyperuricemia may serve as an early biomarker for metabolic dysfunction in patients with SAS, warranting screening and intervention.

Platelet count showed a moderate but significant negative correlation with NAFLD in our multifactorial model. Thrombocytopenia in chronic liver diseases traditionally suggests advanced hepatic fibrosis and portal hypertension; however, our cohort excluded patients with cirrhosis, indicating the presence of other mechanisms [30, 31]. Decreased platelet count may reflect subclinical portal hypertension, bone marrow suppression caused by chronic inflammation, or alterations in thrombopoietin metabolism in hepatic steatosis [30–32]. The nonlinear relationship observed in the restricted cubic spline analysis—where the risk plateaus at lower platelet counts—supports the hypothesis that thrombocytopenia represents a continuum of liver dysfunction rather than a binary threshold phenomenon.

Albumin was positively correlated with NAFLD in our study, which initially appeared contradictory given its traditional role as a marker of liver synthetic function. However, this finding likely reflects the complex interplay between nutritional status, inflammation, and metabolic regulation [33, 34]. Albumin serves as the primary circulating antioxidant, binding free fatty acids and bilirubin while scavenging reactive oxygen species [33]. In the context of SAS-induced oxidative stress, compensatory upregulation of albumin may represent an adaptive response to maintain redox homeostasis. Alternatively, higher albumin levels may be associated with better overall nutritional status, which paradoxically increases caloric availability for hepatic lipid accumulation in sedentary, overweight individuals [34].

The superiority of logistic regression over complex machine learning algorithms in our study highlights an important principle in clinical predictive modeling: when dealing with medium-sized data sets and interpretable clinical variables, simplicity often outperforms complexity [8–10]. Although ensemble methods such as XGBoost and Random Forest excel at capturing nonlinear interactions and high-dimensional patterns, our Feature selection process has identified a set of core, biologically consistent predictors that can be effectively modeled by Logistic regression models. The comparable performance of a simplified model using only the top five features further supports the robustness of these key predictors and enhances clinical feasibility.

Our subgroup and interaction analyses revealed significant effect modifications, which can aid in personalized risk assessment. The synergistic interaction between hyperlipidemia and obesity—where their coexistence increases the risk by nearly 20-fold—demonstrates the multiplicative nature of metabolic risk factors [35, 36]. This finding aligns with the “multiple-hit” hypothesis of NAFLD pathogenesis, where genetic susceptibility, environmental exposure, and metabolic damage collectively act, exceeding the critical threshold for disease progression [20, 35, 36]. The additive interaction index showing a 63% attributable proportion indicates that the basis of this relationship is biological synergy, not merely statistical correlation, involving common pathophysiological pathways such as insulin resistance, chronic inflammation, and lipotoxicity [20, 36].

However, this study also has some limitations. First, our retrospective design precludes causal inference, and the observed associations may reflect reverse causality or unmeasured confounding factors. Second, the diagnosis of NAFLD relies on ultrasound examination, which has limited sensitivity for mild steatosis (hepatic fat content < 30%) and cannot reliably distinguish simple steatosis from steatohepatitis [37–39]. Although ultrasound is still recommended as the preferred screening method in clinical practice due to its accessibility and safety, future studies should include controlled attenuation parameter (CAP) or magnetic resonance imaging-proton density fat fraction (MRI-PDFF) to achieve more accurate quantification [37–39]. Third, our Cohort mainly consists of Chinese patients, which may limit the general applicability to other Ethnic groups with different genetic backgrounds and Metabolomics. In addition, our study could not include all variables related to patient status, such as all past medical history, surgical history, etc., which may potentially influence the progression of the disease.

Despite these limitations, our study has several strengths. The use of a multicenter design with external validation enhances generalizability and reduces institutional bias. The inclusion of a comprehensive feature set encompassing demographic, anthropometric, biochemical, and comorbidity data captures the multidimensional nature of metabolic risk. The application of SHAP analysis provides individualized explanations for model predictions, facilitating clinical interpretation and patient communication. Finally, the identification of modifiable risk factors—particularly dyslipidemia and obesity—suggests actionable targets for intervention.

Future research should prospectively validate our model in different populations and evaluate its impact on clinical outcomes when integrated into routine care pathways. Mechanistic studies exploring the molecular crosstalk between intermittent hypoxia signaling and hepatic lipid metabolism may identify novel therapeutic targets. Furthermore, randomized trials assessing whether continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) therapy or other SAS interventions alter the course of NAFLD will elucidate the causal nature of this association and inform treatment guidelines [1, 40, 41].

In summary, this study established a robust and interpretable predictive model for NAFLD in patients with SAS, highlighting the central role of abnormal lipid metabolism and oxidative stress markers. The identified risk factors align with the current understanding of SAS-related liver pathophysiology while providing a practical tool for early identification of high-risk individuals. Integrating this model into clinical workflows may facilitate targeted screening, lifestyle interventions, and pharmacological treatments to mitigate the growing burden of NAFLD in the expanding SAS population.

Conclusion

This study found that gender, obesity, dyslipidemias, LDL-C, the ratio of AST to ALT, uric acid, GGT, albumin, and PLT are significantly associated with the occurrence of NAFLD in SAS patients. The Logistic regression model established in this study effectively predicts the risk of NAFLD in SAS patients, emphasizing the core role of lipid metabolism abnormalities and markers of oxidative stress. This tool provides a practical and easily interpretable solution for early risk stratification and targeted intervention in clinical practice.
