Tetanus after trauma despite post-exposure prophylaxis with human tetanus immunoglobulin: A case report
Authors: Xuan Chen, Xiu Liu, Haijun Cao, Jianxia Zhang, Si Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Tetanus is an acute, life-threatening disease caused by Clostridium tetani infection through breaches in the skin or mucous membranes, typically following traumatic injury. While human tetanus immunoglobulin (HTIG) is the preferred passive immunizing agent, documented cases of prophylaxis failure are rare despite theoretical possibilities. We present a case of generalized tetanus that developed 1 month after HTIG administration following trauma. This report highlights the potential failure of HTIG prophylaxis and analyzes its contributing factors to inform clinical practice.

Introduction

Tetanus is an acute, potentially fatal neurotoxin-mediated disease caused by Clostridium tetani. Despite effective vaccination programs, tetanus remains clinically relevant due to ubiquitous environmental exposure and incomplete immunization coverage.

Diagnostic and therapeutic approaches for tetanus are well established. We report a case of recurrent generalized tetanus in a patient who developed the disease 1 month after receiving human tetanus immunoglobulin (HTIG) following a new injury, despite a prior history of successfully treated tetanus. The case provides valuable insights for clinical doctors to prevent tetanus, which is of great significance for realizing "Healthy China 2030".

Case report

A 75-year-old male presented to emergency department of our hospital on April 4, 2024, with a 1-day history of trismus. The patient denied photophobia, phonophobia, hydrophobia, nuchal rigidity, or dyspnea. On March 3, 2024, he presented to a hospital in Hebei Province with "head trauma for 1 h", underwent wound debridement and suturing. The patient's head wound was caused by a falling flowerpot full of soil. He received an intramuscular injection of 250 IU of HTIG and refused tetanus vaccination. The sutures were removed 1 week later.

His medical history included a 9-year history of hypertension and type 2 diabetes mellitus, both of which were well-controlled with regular medication. In 2022, the patient experienced multiple minor hand injuries with mild bleeding and without signs of local infection or systemic symptoms. The patient was treated at medical institutions using intramuscular HTIG. On November 14, 2022, a brick lacerated the patient's right thumb. He managed the wound without seeking medical attention (the most recent HTIG dose had been administered 2 months prior). On November 23, 2022, he developed trismus and dysphagia, and was diagnosed with tetanus at another hospital on November 25. He received sedation and 3000 IU of HTIG. His symptoms improved within a week, and he was discharged on December 10.

Upon admission to our hospital this time, vital signs were as follows: temperature, 36.5°C; heart rate, 69 beats per minute; respiratory rate, 18 breaths per minute; blood pressure, 132/80 mmHg; and oxygen saturation, 100% on room air. The patient was alert, with normal neck mobility and limited mouth opening (interincisal distance, around 1.0 cm) without temporomandibular joint crepitus. No abdominal rigidity was present, and the limb muscle tone was normal. The tongue depressor test result was positive (Supplementary Video 1). Two cm scar was observed at the vertex of the head from the trauma, with the scab detached and no local redness or tenderness. Laboratory tests revealed: white blood cell: 5.65 × 109/L, hemoglobin: 109 g/L, neutrophil ratio: 43.9%, creatinine: 137 μmol/L, blood urea nitrogen: 9.26 mmol/L, normal electrolytes and coagulation parameters. Cranial CT revealed bilateral frontoparietal lacunar infarcts, leukoaraiosis, and left maxillary sinusitis. Consultation with a stomatologist ruled out a temporomandibular joint disorder as the cause of trismus. Generally, the symptoms of this episode were relatively mild compared with the first episode.

The patient was admitted to an isolation room in the emergency resuscitation area. Light and sound stimulation were minimized, and unnecessary procedures were avoided. Oxygen was delivered via a nasal cannula at a rate of 3 L/min, under electrocardiogram monitoring. HTIG 3000 IU was administered via multi-point intramuscular injection to the gluteal region. A dose of adsorbed tetanus vaccine was administered intramuscularly to the deltoid muscle on the same day. Venous blood samples were collected before the HTIG injection and at 6, 12, and 24 h post-injection. The serum anti-tetanus toxin antibody titers measured by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) were 0.051, 0.086, 0.110, and 0.140 IU/mL, respectively. Antimicrobial therapy included intravenous metronidazole sodium chloride injection (0.5 g every 8 h). The patient received parenteral nutrition combined with nasogastric enteral nutrition. Under local anesthesia, extensive debridement was performed at the site of the vertex scar. A cruciate incision was made. Irrigated wound with saline. No foreign bodies or purulent discharge were observed. A drainage strip was placed in the wound. Wound dressings were changed regularly.

On day 9, the patient's trismus partially resolved, and he was able to eat normally. The tongue depressor test result remained positive, but less severe (Supplementary Video 2). The wound healed well with no increase in muscle tone. He was discharged.

On day 30, the patient returned for the second dose of the tetanus vaccine. Examination showed a significant improvement in trismus, and the tongue depressor test was negative (Supplementary Video 3).

Discussion

Clostridium tetani exists widely in the natural environment (usually in soil) and in the intestines and feces of humans and warm-blooded animals. When Clostridium tetani proliferates under anaerobic conditions in vivo, it produces its primary virulence factor: tetanus neurotoxin (tetanospasmin). The toxin is an extremely toxic neurotoxin that can cause increased muscle tone, painful spasms, and widespread autonomic nerve instability, leading to generalized tonic contractions and paroxysmal spasms of the skeletal muscles throughout the body. The patient enjoyed growing flowers and fruits at home. He often gets injured for this reason, and his wounds often touched the soil. Clostridium tetani in the soil infected him twice. For the patient, the purpose of repeated debridement is to eliminate Clostridium tetani and the release of toxins by the bacteria. Even in healed wounds, there may still be Clostridium tetani present.

The diagnosis of tetanus is primarily based on characteristic clinical manifestations. The presence of either of the following symptoms can lead to the diagnosis of non-neonatal tetanus: (1) trismus or risus sardonicus; (2) painful muscle spasms.1 Auxiliary tests, such as wound bacterial cultures, are supportive but not definitive. The spatula test, performed by stimulating the posterior pharynx with a tongue depressor, results in reflexive masseter spasm (rather than a gag reflex), demonstrated a high diagnostic accuracy with 94% sensitivity and 100% specificity.2

In this case, the patient had a history of trauma, presented with trismus, tested positive on the spatula test, and responded to tetanus-specific treatment. Temporomandibular joint disorder was excluded. The ELISA-measured anti-tetanus antibody levels were <0.1 IU/mL. Based on these findings, the diagnosis of tetanus was confirmed.

The tetanus antitoxin levels detected at disease onset were likely residual from the HTIG administered 1 month earlier,3 which may explain the patient's relatively mild symptoms. The first episode (November 23, 2022) happened 2 months after the injection of HTIG. The protective effect of HTIG weakened even more. As a consequence, the symptoms of the first episode were relatively severe compared with the second episode.

However, the ELISA-measured titer of 0.051 IU/mL was insufficient to resist tetanus toxin.4 The protective threshold for tetanus varies according to the assay. While a neutralization assay in vivo defines ≥0.01 IU/mL as protective, ELISA-based assessments in vitro—due to their tendency to overestimate antitoxin levels and lower specificity—require higher thresholds (typically 0.1 – 0.2 IU/mL) to ensure protection.

Tetanus prophylaxis after a contaminated wound involves thorough debridement, passive immunization, and active immunization with vaccines.5 In China, equine tetanus antitoxin (TAT) and HTIG are the primary passive immunizing agents. The half-life of TAT in humans is short, providing passive immunity for only approximately 2 weeks, and TAT-only prophylaxis failures, including fatal cases, have been reported.6 In contrast, HTIG provides approximately 28 days of coverage, encompassing the typical tetanus incubation period.7 Although HTIG provides immediate passive protection by neutralizing circulating tetanus toxin that has not yet bound to nerve endings, it cannot neutralize toxin that has already entered neural tissue, eradicate Clostridium tetani from the wound, or induce long-term immunological memory. Failure of treating tetanus with HTIG, without concomitant vaccination, is rare but has been reported. Theoretically, HTIG 250 IU may fail to provide sufficient protection in certain scenarios, such as high-toxin-load wounds requiring greater antibody neutralization, the small wound but neglecting debridement, tetanus toxin entering into neural tissue before HTIG administration, or cases with prolonged incubation periods.8,9 The patient in the present case developed tetanus 1 month after the administration of HTIG 250 IU, which may also be attributed to the aforementioned reasons. Theoretically, cases where infection occurs before the protective effect of HTIG takes effect should exist, as there is already literature proving that the antibody level cannot reach the protective threshold right now after intramuscular injection of HTIG.10 However, due to the rarity of such cases, it could not be retrieved. Notably, a newly approved monoclonal antibody against tetanus toxin (siltartoxatug) has demonstrated longer-lasting protection and higher antibody titers than HTIG in clinical trials, potentially offering superior efficacy in such situations.11

In unvaccinated individuals, passive immunization provides immediate but temporary protection, bridging the gap until vaccines induce a long-lasting protective immunity. A single vaccine dose confers limited protection; the second dose (at 28 days) extends immunity to around 3 years,12 while a third dose further prolongs it to 5 – 10 years.13,14 This case represents a rare failure of HTIG prophylaxis, underscoring the necessity of combining passive immunization with vaccination in unvaccinated trauma patients. In China, tetanus-containing vaccines (e.g., diphtheria–tetanus–pertussis vaccine) were included in the national immunization schedule in 1978; individuals born earlier often lacked vaccination records and required standard post-injury prophylaxis. Furthermore, the administration of tetanus vaccines should also be enhanced based on specific circumstances.15 Tetanus toxin is a poor immunogen with an extremely low human lethal dose (<1μg), so previous infection does not guarantee immunity.16,17 In this case, the patient suffered from tetanus twice in 2022 and 2024. Although cases of recurrent tetanus are rare, such cases still exist globally.18 All patients who have contracted tetanus should be fully immunized after recovery with a primary series of 3 toxoid doses and boosters every 10 years thereafter.17
