Design and validation of a questionnaire for perception of academic bullying in the clinical medical education environment
Authors: Nastaran Maghbouli, Fatemeh Azizi, Shadab Behkam, Saeed Pourhassan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcademic bullying (AB) in clinical medical education settings often involves excessive workloads, threats to professional standing, and verbal or sexual harassment. These behaviors can severely impact trainees’ psychological well-being and career development. Although general tools exist for assessing workplace bullying, no validated instrument is currently designed specifically to capture the unique dynamics of bullying within clinical medical education. This study aimed to develop and validate a comprehensive questionnaire tailored to this context.MethodsThis mixed-method study was conducted across several teaching hospitals affiliated with Tehran University of Medical Sciences. Questionnaire items were initially developed through literature review and focus group discussions. Content and face validity were established via expert panel evaluations and cognitive interviews. Construct validity was assessed using exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses. Internal consistency was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha and composite reliability. Convergent and divergent validity were assessed via average variance extracted (AVE), composite reliability (CR), and the Fornell-Larcker criterion. The final version of the questionnaire was administered to 403 participants, including clinical medical students, residents, and fellows.ResultsThe finalized questionnaire included 41 items grouped into eight distinct factors, capturing a wide range of bullying behaviors such as professional sabotage, verbal and sexual harassment, gender and racist discrimination, educational deprivation, and abuse of authority. Exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses supported the factor structure, with satisfactory model fit indices. The questionnaire demonstrated strong internal consistency across all subcategories (Cronbach’s alpha > 0.7) and acceptable composite reliability (CR > 0.76). Convergent and discriminant validity were supported overall.ConclusionThis study developed and validated a reliable questionnaire to measure academic bullying in clinical medical education. The tool captures a wide range of bullying behaviors and can help guide future research, support institutional evaluations, and inform efforts to create safer and more respectful learning environments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08161-z.Keywords: Workplace bullying, Academic medical centers, Medical education, Questionnaire, Validation study, Occupational stress, Emotional abuse

Background

Academic bullying (AB) can be defined as the abuse of power by a perpetrator who bullies the victim in an academic or work environment through repetitive, punitive behaviors, including overwork, destabilization, professional status threats, and isolation, preventing the victim from studying or advancing in their career [1, 2]. A systematic review found that common workplace bullying behaviors included unfair work pressure (38.2%), being asked to work below competency (36.1%), and being deprived of information (30.7%). Other behaviors noted were excessive monitoring, criticism, and social or professional exclusion [3]. They found that the perpetrators were primarily consultants, residents, and nurses, with 67.2% being men. The most bullying rotations for medical students were surgery, obstetrics and gynecology, and internal medicine [3].

A 2021 global survey of 2006 participants, mainly graduate students and postdocs, found that 84% experienced bullying, 59% witnessed it, and 49% had encountered both [4]. Fear of reprisal deterred 64% of the targets from reporting these behaviors. In Estonia and Finland, a study found that 27% and 18% of university faculty personnel experienced bullying [5]. In Iran, a survey of ICU nurses reported a 10.11% incidence of workplace bullying [6]. Another study noted a 29% rate among employees of the University of Medical Sciences in Zahedan, Iran [7]. A study in Kerman, Iran, highlighted a 93% prevalence of mistreatment against medical students and residents, including academic abuse (73.2%), gender discrimination (56.5%), and physical abuse (19.2%). Residents primarily experienced verbal and emotional mistreatment, whereas interns encountered more physical mistreatment [8].

A crucial aspect of an educational environment is that the attitudes and actions of professors or supervisors have a major influence on learners’ career outcomes. This susceptibility creates an opportunity for bullying behavior within educational settings. Experiencing or observing bullying at university can have extensive consequences, such as emotional exhaustion, anxiety, depression, job burnout, and behavioral problems. These issues may lead to workplace deviance, family conflicts, mistakes in medical judgments, and even cause students to withdraw from education [3, 9–12]. Bullying might also be a precursor of suicidal thoughts in the victims [13]. The lack of legislation and reliable reporting guidelines makes it harder for the victim to find support [9].

Researchers have used various tools to evaluate the prevalence of academic bullying. The Negative Acts Questionnaire (NAQ) and its revised version (NAQ-R) are the most widely used [14]. It has 22 items evaluating person-oriented, work-related, and physically intimidating bullying. It has been revised to various versions, shortened [15] or specified by the country’s culture being studied [16]. Although this questionnaire is widely used to investigate nurses’ bullying [17, 18], it does not explicitly address bullying in academic or educational medical environments. In 1999, Lyn Quine developed a workplace bullying questionnaire for all healthcare system staff, comprising 25 items based entirely on earlier studies’ findings [19].

Despite its significant importance, there is currently a lack of specific and valid tools to identify academic bullying in clinical medical education environments. Therefore, this study aims to design and psychometrically assess a reliable and valid questionnaire to investigate perceptions and instances of academic bullying in clinical and educational settings.

Materials and methods

Our mixed-method study followed the guidelines outlined in AMEE guide no.87 (entitled “Developing questionnaires for educational research”) [20] and the COSMIN checklist [21]. We conducted this study from fall 2023 to spring 2024 in multiple affiliated hospitals of Tehran University of Medical Sciences (TUMS). The interviews, focus groups, and questionnaire are designed for this study and have not been published previously.

Data collection and item pool formation

The following steps were taken (Fig. 1):

The eight steps we have followed to create and validate the questionnaire

Literature review

Two reviewers extracted the questionnaire items from the existing articles through a literature review. An expert initially translated the items into Persian and then gave them to 5–6 individuals to re-translate into English and assess the similarity of the sentences. Then, the same individuals examined Persian items in terms of concept and transparency.

Focus group meetings

Each focus group consisted of 6–8 participants, with a total of six groups comprising 38 participants. The participants included medical students of the clinic stage (trainees and interns), specialized assistants, and sub-specialized assistants. An experienced individual in qualitative evaluation led the sessions. These meetings were held in a virtual environment (using Skype) for one hour each, with participants and a facilitator present. Academic bullying was defined to the participants, who were then asked to share their perceptions with an open question. The conversations were recorded with the participants’ informed consent. The recorded data were transcribed, and themes were extracted, categorized, and analysed using the inductive thematic analysis (ITA) method [22]. To remain anonymous, participants did not review the themes again. Focus group meetings were held until data saturation was achieved. Then, the authors planned for item generation using the themes they found.

Expert panel Delphi rounds

Items developed from literature reviews and focus groups were sent for Delphi rounds. We convened 20 experts (four psychiatrists, five medical educators, four epidemiologists, and seven medical clinical learners and assistants with personal experience of academic bullying) (Table 1). The questionnaire was distributed to experts via email, and a week later, a reminder was sent through SMS. A summary of responses was shared with the panel, and according to their anonymous feedback, adjustments were made. In studies that implemented the Delphi method, a consensus of around 70% (ranging from 50% to 97% in various studies) among the experts served as the criterion for agreement, which was also applied in this study [23, 24].

Primary items of the questionnaire

Roundtable & cognitive interviews for face validity

To assess the preliminary items’ face validity, we held a roundtable with six experts (drawn from the Delphi panel). They were asked to evaluate the items regarding clarity, language, and apparent relevance to the construct of interest, rating on a 4-point Likert scale (1 = unclear to 4 = completely clear). Items with a mean clarity of < 3.0 were reworded and retested until all experts rated them ≥ 3.0. Based on their feedback, minor modifications were made to improve item phrasing and comprehensibility. No items were removed at this stage.

Content validity

The questionnaire and content validity form were emailed to the 20 expert panel participants, and a week later, a reminder was sent via SMS. Experts rated each draft item as “essential,” “useful but not essential,” or “not necessary.” We applied Lawshe’s formula to calculate each item’s Content Validity Ratio (CVR), and set a cutoff value of 0.4 according to the number of our experts [25, 26].

Item relevance, clarity, and simplicity

Items were rated on a 4-point scale for relevance, clarity, and simplicity (1 = irrelevant/unclear to 4 = highly relevant/clear). We computed the Item-Content Validity Index (I-CVI) for each item as the proportion of experts giving a rating of 3 or 4. Items with an I-CVI < 0.7 were eliminated [26, 27]. We also calculated scale-level indices—S-CVI/Ave (average of all I-CVIs) and S-CVR (average of all CVRs)— based on the final set of retained items after excluding those not meeting the minimum thresholds to confirm overall content validity.

To finalize the questionnaire, we held a roundtable meeting with six experts, including professionals from psychiatry, medical education, student support, and specialized and sub-specialized assistants. Their consensus resulted in minor modifications and the approval of the questionnaire, without omitting any other questionnaire items.

Participants and sample size justification

After finalizing the questionnaire’s first draft, 421 participants were included in this study. This sample size aligns with established recommendations for questionnaire validation studies, which often suggest a subject-to-item ratio ranging from 5:1 to 10:1, or a minimum of 300 participants to ensure robust factor analysis and generalizability [28].

Data collection

Data were collected by distributing questionnaires across various teaching hospitals affiliated with Tehran University of Medical Sciences (TUMS). The target group included medical trainees, interns, and assistants in multiple specialties and subspecialties. They rated the items using five options (always, most of the time, sometimes, never, and I have no opinion). This method guaranteed a varied and representative sample of the desired demographic. The questionnaire data, including demographics and item answers, were entered into Excel sheets (Table 2).

Construct validity

Missing data and outliers

Before initiating the next step, we identified missing data. The CVI and CVR analysis data had no missing data due to the nature of Google Forms, which won’t allow the responder to submit the form until all questions are answered. In the data for questionnaire responses, three participants had significant missing values in their answers and were excluded (more than 20% of their responses). The missing information for each of the questionnaire’s items was below 2%, so the mean imputation method was used in SPSS version 27 to address the missing data. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Shapiro-Wilk tests were calculated for univariate normality along with Z-scores. Multivariate outliers were assessed using Mahalanobis distance (p < 0.001). Fifteen participants were identified as outliers and removed from subsequent analyses, resulting in a final sample of 403 participants.

Factorability

The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy and Bartlett’s test of sphericity were used to evaluate the data’s suitability for factor analysis in SPSS version 27.

Determining the factor structure

To examine the questionnaire’s factor structure, we conducted Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) using SPSS version 27, with a total sample size of 403 participants. The principal axis factoring (PAF) method with Promax rotation was used based on the hypothesis that the factors may be correlated [28, 29]. The number of factors was determined based on the “total variance explained” table with Eigenvalues >1, and the Scree plot’s elbow line findings. The results from the pattern matrix were used to determine the factor structure and loading values [29].

Validating the factor structure

Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) was conducted to test the factor structure of the questionnaire using the responses of all 403 participants. The analysis was performed in R (version 4.4.1) using the lavaan package. The robust maximum likelihood estimator (MLR) was applied to account for potential deviations from multivariate normality. Model fit was evaluated using several indices, including the Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI), Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR), Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) [28].

Convergent and discriminant validity

The Average Variance Extracted (AVE) and Composite Reliability (CR) were calculated for each latent construct to assess convergent validity. AVE values exceeding 0.50 indicate that a factor explains more than half of the variance of its indicators, while CR values above 0.70 signify satisfactory internal consistency [30, 31]. For discriminant validity, the Fornell-Larcker criterion was applied. This method compares the square root of each construct’s AVE with its correlations with other constructs (using R version 4.4.1). Cronbach’s Alpha was also computed to evaluate the reliability of each factor [32].

Ethical considerations

According to the Declaration of Helsinki, ethical principles were followed in all research stages. The TUMS ethical committee approved the study with the given ethical code IR.TUMS.MEDICINE.REC.1401.454. Participation was voluntary, accompanied by a clear explanation of the study’s objectives and a guarantee that it would not impact grades or professional standing. In focus groups, participants were informed of the study’s purpose, and their voices were recorded with informed consent. No identifiers were used from the recordings, and all data were stored securely. To minimize the risk of retaliation, responses were anonymized at the point of collection, and results were reported in aggregate form only, ensuring that no individual participant or institution could be identified.

Results

Content validity

The initial draft of the questionnaire included 54 items with Likert scoring (completely agree, agree, I have no opinion, disagree, completely disagree). After the content validity process, item 22 was removed because of a CVR lower than 0.4, and items 23, 24, and 28 for a CVI lower than 0.7. The remaining 50 items were included in the next round with 421 participants (Table 1).

Missing data and outliers

The response data missing from three participants was significantly high (more than 20%), leading to their removal to avoid introducing bias. The responses to the items were also checked for missing values, and the rate was lower than 2%; thus, we used the mean implementation method to address the missing information. Univariate normality analysis revealed a non-normal pattern (Z-scores >|2|, and Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Shapiro-Wilk test p-value < 0.05); however, this will not affect the results of the EFA analysis, as it has been previously proven that parametric statistics are not problematic with non-normal Likert data [33]. The Mahalanobis distance was calculated to assess multivariate normality, resulting in a Critical Chi-square value of 86.66 for 50 items (p < 0.001). This analysis revealed 15 outliers, and after excluding them, the final sample size was reduced to 403, which is still robust and large enough for factor analysis.

Exploratory factor analysis

The factorability of the questionnaire was confirmed by KMO (0.922) and Bartlett’s test results (χ² = 12347.928, df = 1225, p < 0.001). The PAF method with Promax rotation addressed the non-normality and correlations between factors. The first analysis yielded ten factors without restricting the analysis to a fixed number of factors, and it was based on Eigenvalues greater than one. The Scree plot elbow occurred between the 8th and 9th factors (Fig. 2)—the tenth factor contained only one item, which was insufficient to establish an independent factor. Therefore, to ensure a more dependable selection of factors, the analysis was conducted three times for a fixed number of factors: 7, 8, and 9. The 8-factor model yielded better theoretical factor structures and higher CFA analysis results, explaining 57.17% of the total variance (as reported in the “Extraction Sums of Squared Loadings” section of the output, using PAF analysis with Promax rotation). According to Factor Matrix results of the 8-factor model, the following items were removed due to loadings below 0.4: 11, 12, 13, 15, 16, 17, 21, 25, 37, leaving 41 items for CFA analysis. Confirmatory factor analysis

To determine the best-fitting model for the questionnaire, three competing models were tested using confirmatory factor analysis (CFA): a 7-factor, 8-factor, and 9-factor solution. The 8-factor model demonstrated superior fit across almost all indices. Specifically, it yielded the lowest chi-square value (χ² = 1921.912, df = 751), as well as the lowest AIC (41924.152) and BIC (42364.035), indicating the most favorable balance between fit and model complexity. Additionally, the 8-factor model showed improved comparative fit (CFI = 0.866) and Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI = 0.854), surpassing conventional thresholds for acceptable fit. Although the 9-factor model showed a marginally lower RMSEA (0.064), the 8-factor model achieved a very similar RMSEA (0.065) alongside the lowest SRMR (0.059), reinforcing its superior performance. These findings support the selection of the 8-factor model as the final structure for the questionnaire (Table 4).

Convergent and discriminant validity

As shown in Table 3, six of the eight factors met the AVE threshold (AVE > 0.50), with values ranging from 0.535 to 0.725. Two factors (F1: AVE = 0.470; F3: AVE = 0.488) fell slightly below the threshold, although their CR values remained high (CR > 0.88), suggesting adequate internal consistency despite marginal AVE values. Discriminant validity is supported when the diagonal elements (square roots of AVE) are greater than the corresponding inter-construct correlations. As shown in Table 5, most constructs satisfied this criterion. However, F1 and F4 showed high inter-factor correlations (r = 0.830), and their AVE values (F1 = 0.470; F4 = 0.546) were not substantially higher than the squared correlation between them (find the finalized version of the questionnaire in Additional file 1).

Discussion

We aimed to create and validate a questionnaire to assess the perception of academic bullying in clinical medical educational environments. This was intended to represent various overt and covert aspects of bullying to address the issue and improve the mental health of medical educators. The finalized version included 41 items, categorized into eight factors, exhibiting strong internal consistency and construct validity (Additional file 1).

Convergent and discriminant validity were generally supported, with all factors showing strong reliability (CR >0.768) and six meeting the AVE >0.50 threshold. Although F1 and F3 had slightly low AVEs, they were retained due to strong loadings and theoretical backing. While one discriminant validity concern emerged between F1 (Professional Sabotage & Undermining) and F4 (Abuse of Authority & Unethical Demands), with a high inter-factor correlation (r = 0.830), we argue for their conceptual distinctiveness based on both empirical and theoretical grounds. First, despite the correlation, the factors loaded distinctly in both EFA and CFA, and each demonstrated excellent internal consistency (CR >0.87). Second, a close review of item content suggests different underlying mechanisms: F1 reflects covert, often passive-aggressive behaviors aimed at undermining a trainee’s progress, credibility, or evaluations without direct confrontation. In contrast, F4 centers on overt exploitation of hierarchical power, such as forcing trainees into unethical actions or abusing them in clinical situations. The use of “force” is clearly present in most items of F4, which creates a noticeable difference from passive-aggressive behaviors in F1 items. Also, the literature supports that discriminant validity is a matter of degree and context, not an absolute property, and that theoretical differentiation can justify separate factors even when correlations approach or exceed 0.8 [32, 34]. Merging two highly correlated constructs can obscure important distinctions. Literature on multicollinearity and factor analysis cautions that combining factors can lead to a loss of unique qualitative information, particularly when the underlying mechanisms are not identical [35]. Overall, the factor structure remains valid and reliable for capturing the complex dimensions of academic bullying.

Iran has a well-defined hierarchy in its medical education system: trainees primarily observe, final-year interns assume responsibilities alongside residents and professors, while residents operate within a senior-junior framework. This multilayered chain of command creates conditions that allow power imbalances to go unchecked [36]. In our study, bullying was most frequently reported in clinical work settings, suggesting that hierarchical pressure plays a significant role. This is consistent with international findings, including a global survey of 2006 respondents, where most perpetrators were in positions of authority over their targets [4].

The factor structure highlights a broad spectrum of bullying behaviors, from professional sabotage and unethical supervisory practices to discrimination based on race, gender, and sexual orientation. Notably, two distinct factors emerged about sexual misconduct: one about harassment and inappropriate behavior (e.g., physical contact, sexual jokes), and another focused on discriminatory practices tied to gender and sexuality in task distribution and evaluations. This distinction emphasizes the multifaceted nature of sexualized power abuse in academic medicine.

A survey on gender-based discrimination (GBD) perceptions among Boston hospital trainees found that 61% experienced GBD, with women reporting higher rates. Additionally, 35% of female and 5% of male respondents experienced sexual harassment. They presented their findings at departmental conferences and committees before formal publication, generating attention and emphasizing the impact of perceptual surveys on addressing this issue [37].

The concept of “professional sabotage and undermining” had the highest number of items identified in our factor structures. These items reflect indirect, strategic, or systemic interference with someone’s reputation, rights, work progress, or evaluations. It involves creating conditions that harm someone professionally without direct confrontation, and passively. Two items (53 (excessive supervision) and 48 (venting anger)) of this factor may feel slightly more emotional or erratic than “sabotage,” but they still fit as methods of undermining trust or stability. In previous similar studies, humiliation, undermining the trainers’ work, belittling, and verbal abuse are the most common bullying events reported [38]. The “Educational Resource & Opportunity Deprivation” factor captures a form of academic bullying where individuals are denied access to essential learning opportunities. While it shares some similarities with sabotage, it emphasizes withholding resources, rather than direct interference, creating an adequate distinction to be considered a separate category.

The “direct verbal harassment” factor concerns confrontational, emotionally charged, or overtly aggressive interactions, especially those involving speech, tone, or hostile tasking. Even unfair workload and time pressure (items 3 and 14) can manifest verbally through orders, shouting, or passive-aggressive remarks.

Environments that breed bullying are characterized by being competitive with autocratic leadership [39]. The “abuse of authority and unethical demands” items consistently reflect the use of power, coercion, and inappropriate pressure by individuals in positions of authority. A recurring theme is forcing others to perform tasks below or beyond their training level, operate unsupervised, or act unethically or conceal mistakes. Notably, overwork is the most frequently described pattern of bullying among the 28 studies included in a systematic review of academic bullying in medical settings [3]. This alignment on coercive authority misuse reinforces the factor structure, emphasizing a behavioral dimension where academic bullying serves as a tool for manipulation, exploitation, or violating professional norms.

The “Ethno-racial Discrimination” factor reveals both subtle and overt bias, including racial labeling and unequal treatment. In previous studies, minority resident physicians have described experiencing microaggressions and being counted as “others.” These findings reinforce the hierarchical power and deprivation, highlighting the need for culturally responsive support from the medical educators’ supervisors [40].

Despite being less frequently reported than other forms of bullying, “Physical Confrontation” behaviors are particularly concerning due to the immediate danger they pose to one’s safety and well-being. Of 892 residents participating in a study in Iran, 19.7% experienced physical abuse. About 43% of these victims believed they had no support from the hospital’s managers at all [41]. Since these behaviors are against professional conduct and can have long-lasting psychological effects on victims, they must be accurately recognized and addressed [42].

Limitations and strengths

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, although the items were developed through a robust, culturally grounded process, some may reflect context-specific experiences. Therefore, cross-cultural adaptation and validation would be necessary before applying this tool in different countries or healthcare systems. Such efforts would require standard methodological steps, including forward–backward translation, cognitive interviewing with local stakeholders to ensure conceptual equivalence, and confirmatory factor analysis to test structural invariance across contexts. Second, our sample included only medical trainees, which may limit perspectives from faculty members, attending physicians, or nurses. Third, due to the cross-sectional design, we could not assess test-retest reliability or estimate measurement error over time; future longitudinal studies are recommended to evaluate the tool’s stability. Finally, the absence of comparable validated tools in this context in medical clinical-educational settings limited our ability to establish criterion validity through external comparisons.

Despite these limitations, the study has several notable strengths and delivers a high level of methodological rigor. It was conducted as a multicenter study across several teaching hospitals affiliated with Tehran University of Medical Sciences, enhancing the diversity of responses and reducing potential single-site bias. The sample size was adequate and statistically confirmed through KMO and Bartlett’s tests, supporting the robustness of our factor analyses. Our methodology incorporated a comprehensive, multi-phase design, including literature review, focus groups, expert panel assessments, both exploratory and confirmatory factor analysis, and convergent and divergent validity. The final questionnaire demonstrated strong psychometric properties, with high internal consistency and clearly defined factors. To our knowledge, this is the first and most comprehensive instrument specifically designed and validated to assess academic bullying in medical clinical-educational environments.

Conclusion

This study led to the development and validation of a comprehensive, culturally grounded questionnaire to assess academic bullying in clinical-educational medical settings. The tool demonstrated strong psychometric properties and captured a wide range of bullying behaviors relevant to hierarchical medical environments. It can serve as a foundation for future research, institutional assessments, and targeted interventions aimed at fostering safer and more respectful learning environments in medical education.
