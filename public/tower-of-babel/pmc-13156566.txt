Superselective intra-arterial infusion of cisplatin and concomitant radiation therapy (RADPLAT) using gadolinium-based contrast agent in a patient with severe iodine allergy: A case report
Authors: Yume Sanji, Mizuki Ozawa, Seiya Mochizuki, Yuko Kubo, Madoka Sakuramachi, Hideaki Takahashi, Akane Yoshiba, Go Omura, Miyuki Sone
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMaxillary sinus squamous cell carcinoma (MS-SCC) is a rare malignancy that is often diagnosed at an advanced stage. We report a case of locally advanced MS-SCC that was successfully treated with super-selective intra-arterial cisplatin infusion concurrent with radiotherapy (RADPLAT) using gadolinium-based contrast agents due to a severe allergy to iodinated contrast media. Despite limitations in image quality, gadolinium-based contrast agents enable sufficient visualization with digital subtraction angiography and computed tomography angiography. Seven treatment sessions were completed without adverse events, using a maximum gadolinium dose of 28 mL per session. Post-treatment imaging revealed no residual tumors. This case illustrates the feasibility of using gadolinium-based contrast agents as an alternative to iodinated contrast media for angiographic guidance during RADPLAT in patients with contraindications to iodinated contrast. However, its long-term safety warrants further investigation.Keywords: Maxillary sinus squamous cell carcinoma, Super-selective intra-arterial cisplatin infusion concurrent with radiotherapy, RADPLAT, Gadolinium-based contrast agent

Introduction

Maxillary sinus squamous cell carcinoma (MS-SCC) is a rare but aggressive malignancy of the paranasal sinus. It is often associated with a poor prognosis and is diagnosed at an advanced stage owing to the absence of subjective symptoms [1]. For individuals with locally advanced MS-SCC who are unsuitable candidates for surgery, super-selective intra-arterial infusion of high-dose cisplatin combined with concurrent radiation therapy, known as RADPLAT, has been developed as a less invasive alternative treatment option [2]. RADPLAT has demonstrated favorable outcomes while preserving organ function in locally advanced MS-SCC [2,3,5].

In general, frequent imaging with iodinated contrast media, including digital subtraction angiography (DSA) and computed tomography angiography (CTA), is required during intra-arterial cisplatin infusion to assess vascular anatomy accurately. Therefore, in cases where iodinated contrast media are contraindicated or unavailable, identifying the target vessels may be challenging and could compromise the treatment accuracy. Previous studies have reported the use of gadolinium-based contrast agents as alternatives to iodinated contrast media in patients with contraindications to endovascular treatment. Although gadolinium-based contrast agents generally provide lower image quality than iodinated contrast media, their use has been reported to be diagnostically adequate in endovascular procedures [4,6,7]. However, the application of gadolinium-based contrast agents to RADPLAT has rarely been reported.

Here, we present a case in which RADPLAT was successfully performed using gadolinium-based contrast agents in a patient with locally advanced MS-SCC and a history of severe allergy to iodinated contrast medium.

Case report

A 40-year-old male with no prior history of cancer presented with pain in the left upper jaw and was referred to our hospital for further evaluation and treatment. Initial assessment, including contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) using iodinated contrast media, revealed a mass in the left maxillary sinus. During the examination, the patient developed a severe allergic reaction, accompanied by hypotension and impaired consciousness. Subsequent contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrated a soft tissue mass in the left maxillary sinus with bone destruction and invasion into the infratemporal fossa, pterygoid process, and orbital floor without orbital apex involvement or intracranial extension. No radiological evidence of lymph node or distant metastases was observed (Fig. 1A). Notably, no allergic reactions occurred during the MRI examination. Based on the clinical and imaging findings, the tumor was staged as cT4aN0M0. Histopathological examination confirmed a non-keratinizing squamous cell carcinoma on biopsy. Although surgery is the standard treatment, a multidisciplinary team concluded that, given the patient’s young age and the importance of preserving function, RADPLAT was a more suitable option. Owing to the patient's severe allergy to iodinated contrast media, gadolinium-based contrast agents were used as an alternative during the interventional procedure.

The off-label use of a gadolinium-based contrast agent received institutional approval, and written informed consent was obtained from the patient. The contrast agent used was generic meglumine gadoterate (GE Healthcare Pharma, Japan). RADPLAT was conducted according to the JCOG1212 (Japan Clinical Oncology Group) protocol, consisting of weekly intra-arterial cisplatin infusion at 100 mg/m² combined with concurrent radiotherapy of 70 Gy in 35 fractions over 7 weeks under local anesthesia with mild intravenous analgesics [5]. To ensure safety, the maximum dose of gadolinium-based contrast agent per session was limited to 28 mL based on previously reported safety thresholds for gadolinium administration. Baseline renal function was evaluated before treatment because gadolinium-based contrast agents were repeatedly administered during the RADPLAT course. Serum creatinine was 1.03 mg/dL with an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) of 65 mL/min/1.73 m². During the RADPLAT course, renal function was monitored twice weekly, and renal function remained stable throughout treatment. Intravenous sodium thiosulfate was concurrently administered with intra-arterial cisplatin. To prevent angiitis, 100 mg prednisolone sodium succinate was infused into each feeding artery immediately after each session. A 5-F vascular sheath (Supersheath; Medikit, Tokyo, Japan) was placed in the right femoral artery, and the left external carotid artery was selected using a 5-F guiding sheath (ENVOY; Johnson & Johnson, FL) and a 0.032-inch hydrophilic guidewire (Radifocus Guidewire M; Terumo, Tokyo, Japan). DSA via the external carotid artery was performed at each session using 5 mL of a 1:1 gadolinium–normal saline mixture to confirm catheter position. For subsequent selective catheterization of the peripheral branches, a 1.7-F microcatheter (Progreat Lambda; Terumo) and a 0.014-inch micro-guidewire (ASAHI CHIKAI black; ASAHI INTECC, Aichi, Japan) were used. As the vascular anatomy had not been fully evaluated and the internal maxillary artery (IMA) was considered the primary feeding vessel based on tumor location, the third segment of the IMA was selected for full-dose cisplatin during the initial 3 sessions. After the third treatment, a contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was performed. The images revealed a residual tumor in the anterior portion, suggesting a possible contribution from the transverse facial artery (TFA) (Fig. 1B). Therefore, additional TFA treatment was required from the fourth session; however, the exact origin of the TFA was not clearly identified. To accurately assess the vascular anatomy, a CTA with a gadolinium-based contrast agent, injected via the main trunk of the external carotid artery, was planned. Intra-arterial CTA was performed using an angiography system equipped with a 320-row multidetector CT scanner (Aquilion ONE/PRISM Edition, Canon Medical Systems, Tochigi, Japan). A pre-contrast scan and an extra-carotid CTA were performed to acquire arterial and tumor-phase images. Gadolinium-based contrast agent (meglumine gadoterate; GE Healthcare Pharma Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) was administered using a continuous 2-phase injection protocol. The first phase involved a 1:1 contrast–saline injection (1.5 mL/s for 6.5 s (4.9 mL contrast agent). The second phase consisted of a 4:6 injection ratio at 0.8 mL/s for 7.5 s (2.4 mL). The total volume of the undiluted meglumine gadoterate was 7.3 mL, corresponding to 0.05 mmol/kg. Arterial and tumor phase images were acquired 2 s and 9.5 s after the contrast injection, respectively. Imaging parameters were as follows: tube voltage, 80 kV; tube current, modulated automatically (Volume EC, Canon Medical Systems) between 30 and 450 mA, targeting a noise level of 19 Hounsfield units; slice thickness, 3.0 mm; rotation time, 0.5 s; pitch, 0.64; detector configuration, 80 × 0.5 mm. Image reconstruction was performed using a deep-learning-based algorithm (Advanced Intelligent Clear-IQ Engine [AiCE], Canon Medical Systems). CTA clearly demonstrated the origin of TFA, enabling selective catheterization (Fig. 2). The fusion of volume-rendered (VR) images with fluoroscopy further reduced contrast use (Fig. 3). In the subsequent 4 sessions, 70% of the cisplatin dose was administered via the IMA and 30% via the TFA. The target vessels, total gadolinium contrast volume, and procedure times are summarized in Table 1. The patient was discharged 4 days after the final treatment. Both the one-month follow-up MRI (Fig. 1C) and the 3-month 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) PET/MRI demonstrated no residual tumor; however, outpatient follow-up is ongoing to monitor for recurrence. In accordance with ethical standards, written informed consent was obtained from the patient for the publication of this case report and accompanying images.

Discussion

In this case report, the entire course of RADPLAT was completed using a gadolinium-based contrast agent, without any severe adverse events. Moreover, CTA and VR images obtained with this agent enabled accurate assessment of vascular anatomy and reduced contrast volume per session.

Several case reports have described endovascular procedures using gadolinium-based contrast agents in patients with severe allergies to iodinated contrast media. For example, Male et al. reported the successful use of a gadolinium-based contrast agent for cerebral DSA and mechanical thrombectomy in a patient with acute ischemic stroke and a known allergy to iodinated contrast medium [4,[6], [7], [8]]. In cardiology, similar outcomes have been reported, including successful coronary angiography with gadolinium-based contrast agents, with sufficient image quality and no adverse effects, in patients with contrast allergy [7]. Although various reports have described their use in endovascular interventions, the application of RADPLAT remains rare.

The safety of intra-arterial administration of gadolinium-based contrast agents remains uncertain, and a clearly defined maximum safe dose has not been established. In this case, the maximum dose per session was limited to 28 mL, guided by a previous systematic review that indicated an increased risk of renal dysfunction at doses above 0.4 mmol/kg in patients with impaired renal function [8]. This threshold was based on the patient’s body weight (70 kg) and the agent’s concentration (0.1 mmol/mL), yielding a dose of 0.4 mmol/kg. All the treatment sessions were completed without exceeding the dose. However, the long-term safety of repeated intra-arterial gadolinium-based contrast use in the same patient, as was performed here, remains unclear. The potential risk of gadolinium deposition after repeated administration of gadolinium-based contrast agents has been reported in recent years, starting with the initial report by Kanda et al. [9]. Although no adverse effects on the human body have been demonstrated to date, the effects of repeated short-interval intra-arterial administration, as in the present case, remain uncertain. Moreover, this patient will likely require further gadolinium-enhanced MRI examinations during future follow-ups. Therefore, careful, long-term monitoring is required. Given the potential risk of cumulative gadolinium exposure and the limited data regarding long-term safety after repeated intra-arterial administration, careful long-term monitoring is essential. In this case, the patient is scheduled for outpatient follow-up every 3 months during the first year, including clinical examination and imaging evaluation using CT or MRI to assess both tumor control and potential delayed adverse effects associated with repeated gadolinium exposure.

Premedication with corticosteroids is commonly used in clinical practice to reduce the risk of allergic reactions to iodinated contrast media; however, its efficacy remains uncertain [10,11]. The effectiveness of such prophylaxis is even more questionable in patients with a history of severe reactions such as hypotension or loss of consciousness, as observed in the present case. Although gadolinium-based contrast agents are limited by the volume that can be safely administered and provide inferior vascular visualization in DSA compared to iodinated agents, they may still represent a feasible alternative in selected cases, depending on the vascular anatomy and lesion location. This report is limited by its single-patient design and short follow-up duration, and further studies are needed to evaluate the safety and effectiveness of gadolinium-based contrast agents in intra-arterial chemoradiotherapy. Despite these limitations, the current case demonstrates the potential role of gadolinium-based contrast agents in expanding the therapeutic options for patients with severe allergy to iodinated contrast media undergoing complex endovascular therapies.

In conclusion, gadolinium-based contrast agents may serve as viable alternatives for endovascular treatment in patients with a history of severe allergy to iodinated contrast agents, particularly when imaging and treatment planning can be tailored to vascular territory and contrast-dosing constraints.
