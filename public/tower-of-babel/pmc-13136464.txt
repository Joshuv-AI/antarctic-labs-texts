Antipathogenic carbon dots synthesized from fluorescent amino acids and their photodynamic activity
Authors: Selin S Suner, Mehtap Sahiner, Jorge H Torres, Nurettin Sahiner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNitrogen-doped carbon dots (CDs) were prepared from essential fluorescent amino acids, such as lysine (Lys), phenylalanine (Phe), tyrosine (Tyr), and tryptophan (Trp) by two routes, microwave and teflon-lined autoclave. The fluorescent properties of these amino acids were significantly improved in their corresponding CD forms, especially for those prepared via the hydrothermal process. A significantly high fluorescent intensity was measured in the emission range of 420–470 nm at the excitation wavelengths of 300–350 nm. While a negatively zeta potential value of − 9.8 ± 2.6 mV was obtained for Lys CDs suspension, the CDs of Phe, Tyr, and Trp afforded strong positive zeta potentials, + 24.0 ± 2.4, + 12.5 ± 3.1, and + 31.9 ± 3.8 mV, respectively. The Lys CDs were found not to be antimicrobial even at a 10 mg/mL concentration, which is likely due to their negative surface charge that weakens electrostatic interactions with negatively charged microbial membranes. Whereas the Phe, Tyr, and Trp CDs had a 2.5 mg/mL minimum inhibition concentration (MIC) against a Gram-negative bacterium, Klebsiella pneumoniae. The Phe CDs commenced the highest antibacterial effect against Bacillus subtilis (ATCC 6633) Gram-positive bacteria, but the lowest MIC value of 1.25 mg/mL was determined for Tyr CDs against Candida albicans (ATCC 10231) fungus. Furthermore, a UV light exposure, 30 min treatment of UV-A light with a 6.88 mW/cm2 irradiance value on amino acids CD exhibited improved photodynamic activity. The natural amino acid-derived CDs show great biocompatibility on L929 fibroblast cells with > 86% cell viability, for all formulations except Tyr CDs, retaining 78 ± 3% viability even at 1000 μg/mL concentration, and blood compatibility at 500 μg/mL concentration. Therefore, these CDs derived from fluorescent amino acids are photoactivated and are of excellent nanosized materials in a variety of biomedical in vitro and in vivo uses, including diagnostic, sensor, and therapeutic applications.Keywords: Fluorescent amino acids, Carbon dots, Biocompatible, Photodynamic antimicrobial activity, ROS generation

Introduction

The rapidly emerging antibiotic-resistant strains only add to the seriousness of bacterial infections, which remain a major health problem worldwide. Despite this, traditional antibiotics are being overused and misused [1]. The result is a dramatic increase in multidrug-resistant bacteria, making it almost impossible to use existing antibacterial therapies effectively. Thus, there is an urgent need to develop new antibacterial materials with high efficacy, biocompatibility, and a low likelihood of inducing resistance. Carbon dots (CDs) have attracted growing interest in biomedical applications due to their unique physicochemical properties, including excellent water solubility, reversible surface functionality, fluorescence, low toxicity, and excellent photostability [2–4]. In recent years, CDs have been widely explored for bioimaging, nano drug delivery, sensing, and antimicrobial applications [5–7]. Notably, CDs derived from natural or biomolecule-based precursors have shown enhanced biocompatibility and promising biological activities, making them particularly attractive for antibacterial applications [8–10].

Amino acids are basic biological building blocks with different functional groups and intrinsic biological activity. The use of amino acids as carbon sources for CDs synthesis is a green, simple, and sustainable strategy to produce functional nanomaterials [11]. In addition, CDs derived from amino acids may inherit specific chemical properties from their precursors, such as surface charge, hydrophobicity, and functional moieties, which may play a key role in their interaction with bacterial membranes [12, 13].

Lysine (Lys), 2,6-diaminohexanoic acid, is an aliphatic amino acid with basic properties [14]. Lys is a proteinogenic, ketogenic, and essential amino acid [15]. Phenylalanine (Phe), tyrosine (Tyr), and tryptophan (Trp) are the three primary aromatic amino acids that serve as essential building blocks for both plant and human life, often synthesized in plants via the shikimate pathway [16, 17]. In human physiology, Trp is a critical essential amino acid obtained through diet, acting as the primary precursor for bioactive compounds such as the neurotransmitter serotonin, the sleep-regulating hormone melatonin, and various kynurenine metabolites involved in immune homeostasis [18, 19]. Both Trp and Tyr have been shown to exert anabolic effects on bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells by promoting proliferation and upregulating osteogenic markers such as Runx2; however, their beneficial functions can be disrupted by oxidative stress, leading to the formation of inhibitory oxides [20]. Additionally, amino acids such as Lys and Phe are commonly used in biomedical engineering to develop biocompatible, biodegradable polypeptide carriers, like poly(L-lysine), for targeted cancer drug delivery [21, 22]. Interestingly, under conditions of extreme nutrient stress, such as Trp depletion in tumors, cancer cells may even substitute Phe for Trp during protein synthesis to support their rapid proliferation [23].

Phe, Tyr, and Trp contain aromatic rings that may facilitate hydrophobic interactions with bacterial cell membranes [16, 17, 21]. Carbon dots derived from these amino acids are therefore expected to display varied antibacterial behaviors arising from their distinct surface chemistry [24].

In this study, we report the synthesis of carbon dots derived from tryptophan, lysine, phenylalanine, and tyrosine using a simple carbonization strategy, and systematically investigate their antibacterial activities. By comparing the antibacterial performance of these amino acid–based CDs, this work aims to elucidate the relationship between precursor molecular structure and antibacterial efficacy. The results provide new insights into the rational design of amino acid–derived carbon dots as effective and biocompatible antibacterial agents.

Materials and methods

Materials

Lysine (Lys), phenylalanine (Phe), tyrosine (Tyr), tryptophan (Trp), citric acid monohydrate (CA, > 99%, Carlo Erba, France), and dialysis membrane, molecular weight cut off ≥ 12,000 Da, were used in the synthesis of amino acid CDs. Klebsiella pneumoniae (K. pneumoniae ATTC 700603), Bacillus subtilis (B. subtilis ATCC 6633), and Candida albicans (C. albicans, ATCC 10231) strains were taken from KWIK-STIK, Microbiologics, France. Nutrient agar (NA, Condolab, Madrid, Spain), nutrient broth (NB, Merck, Darmstadt, Germany), and potato dextrose agar (for microbiology, Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) were used as growth medium for bacteria/fungus culture. In cytotoxicity analysis, fibroblast cells (L929, mouse C3/An connective tissue) were taken from the SAP Institute, Ankara, Turkey. As a growth medium of the fibroblast cells, Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM, containing 4.5 g/L glucose, 3.7 g/L sodium pyruvate, and 0.5 g/mL L-Glutamine), fetal bovine serum (FBS), and antibiotic solution (100 IU/mL penicillin/100 μg/mL streptomycin) were purchased from PanBiontech (GmbH, Germany). Trypsin/EDTA (0.25%Trypsin/0.02% EDTA, PanBiontech, GmbH, Germany), trypan blue solution (0.5%, Biological Industries, Haifa, Israel), and 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT agent, neoFroxx, GmbH, Germany) were obtained as received. Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, 99.9%, Carlo-Erba, Val-de-Reuil, France), acetone (99%, BRK, Turkey), ethanol (99%, Carlo-Erba GmbH, Emmendingen, Germany), and DI water (Millipore-Direct Q UV3 at 18.2 M.Ω.cm) were used as solvents.

Synthesis of amino acid CDs

Synthesis of Lys, Phe, Tyr, and Trp CDs was applied with two different methods, microwave (MW) and hydrothermal (HT) techniques [25, 26]. In the MW technique, 0.1 g of amino acid and 0.3 g of citric acid were solubilized in 3 mL of deionized water by mixing at 200 rpm for 15 min and then subjected to microwave carbonization at 1000 W for 3 min. It is important to note that the weight ratio of amino acid to citric acid in both microwave (MW) and hydrothermal (HT) synthesis methods were maintained at 1:3 to ensure comparability between the two synthetic approaches despite the difference in the total reaction scale. The synthesized amino acid CDs were immediately suspended in DI water and enclosed within a dialysis membrane (12 kD, Fisher Scientific). After dialysis for 10 h in DI water, the suspension of the amino acid CDs was filtered through a 0.22 μm pore filter. Then, the suspension was precipitated in an excess amount of acetone by using a centrifuge at 10,000 rpm for 10 min. The obtained amino acid CDs were dried in an oven at 50 °C and kept in sealed containers. In the HT process by using a Teflon-lined autoclave, 0.3 g of amino acid and 0.9 g of citric acid were solubilized in 20 mL of deionized water by mixing at 200 rpm for 15 min. The solution was put into a Teflon-lined autoclave and carbonized in a muffle furnace at 250 °C for 4 h. The synthesized amino acid CDs were enclosed within a dialysis membrane (12 kD, Fisher Scientific). After dialysis for 10 h in DI water, the suspension of the amino acid CDs was filtered through a 0.22 μm pore filter. Then, the suspension was precipitated in an excess amount of acetone by using a centrifuge at 10,000 rpm for 10 min. The obtained amino acid CDs were dried in an oven at 50 °C and kept in sealed containers.

Characterization of amino acid CDs

The zeta potential measurements of amino acid CDs were performed using the Zeta Potential Analyzer (Zeta-Pals, BIC) in 1 mM KNO3 solutions suspended in DI water at a concentration of 1.0 mg/mL. In addition, the isoelectric points were calculated by measuring the surface charges of amino acid CDs at different pH values. The size distribution of the amin acid CDs was measured using dynamic light scattering (DLS, Brookhaven Instrument Nanobrook Omni, Brookhaven, NY, USA) at a 10 mM KNO3 solution.

The optical characteristics of the amino acid CDs were examined using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (T80 + UV/Vis spectrometer, PG Instrument) and a fluorescence spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, Lumina). Briefly, 1 mg/mL of the amino acid CDs was prepared in DI water, and UV–Vis absorbance in the range of 200–600 nm wavelength and fluorescence emission spectrum at different excitation wavelengths from 300 to 450 nm were conducted. Blood compatibility of amino acid CDs

The blood compatibility of the amino acid CDs was assessed using hemolysis and blood coagulation analysis using the approach outlined in our previously published method [25]. Blood was supplied by volunteers in compliance with the 2011-KAEK-27/2022 Human Research Ethics Committee protocol of Canakkale Onsekiz Mart University.

Cytotoxicity of amino acid CDs

MTT analysis was used to examine the toxicity of the amino acid CDs on L929 fibroblasts. We utilized DMEM medium with 10% and 1% antibiotics as a growth medium for the cells. They were kept at 37 °C in a 5% CO2/95% air environment. The cells were cultured in a 5% CO2 environment at 37 °C in DMEM medium that included 10% FBS and 1%antibiotics. In a 96-well plate, approximately 100 µL of a 1 × 104 cell suspension in the medium was put into each well and was cultured for a period of 24 h. Following cell adhesion, 100 µL of the sterile CDs suspension in the medium at a concentration range of 1000 µg/mL was applied on the cells. For a further 24 h, the plate was incubated under the same conditions. Subsequently, the cells were slowly rinsed with phosphate-buffered solution (PBS) and treated with 100 μL of 0.5 mg/mL MTT reagent in the dark for 4 h. After dissolving the formazan crystals in each well with 200 μL of DMSO, the plate's absorbance at 570 nm wavelength was measured using a microplate reader (Heales, MB-530, Shenzhen Huisong Technology Development Co., Ltd, Shenzhen China). The analysis was conducted thrice, and the findings were presented with standard deviations. Statistical analysis was performed using Microsoft Office Excel software (Analysis ToolPak Add-ins) due to a one-way ANOVA test. Significant differences were observed with *p < 0.05 compared to the control group.

Antimicrobial properties of amino acid CDs

The antimicrobial efficacy of the amino acid CDs was assessed using a microtiter broth dilution assay against Gram-negative K. pneumoniae ATCC 700603, Gram-positive B. subtilis ATCC 6633, and C. albicans ATCC 10231 as a fungus. Before analysis, the CDs were sterilized by irradiation to UV light (420 nm) for two minutes. Subsequently, a 20 mg/mL concentration of the CDs suspension was prepared in 0.9% NaCl aqueous solution, and 100 μL of this suspensions were diluted in 100 μL liquid growth medium as nutrient broth, and well in a 96-well plate. Then, the CD suspension was diluted by a factor of half correspondingly into the wells in 100 μL of growth medium. After that, 5 μL of bacteria/fungus stock culture, with a concentration of about 1.5 × 108 colony forming unit (CFU)/mL, which was adjusted using the MacFarland 0.5 standard, was added to each well. Following incubation at 35 °C for 18–24 h in bacterial culture and at 25 °C for 48 h in fungal culture, the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) was identified as the lowest concentration at which no visible turbidity was seen in the wells. Subsequently, 50 µL was removed from the wells and seeded on nutrient agar in bacteria culture or potato dextrose agar in fungus culture, which was kept at 35 °C for 24 h more for bacteria growing and at 25 °C for 48 h to provide fungus growing. The MBC/MFC value was defined as the lowest concentration at which there was no growth at a rate of 99.99% at the conclusion of incubation. Following the guidelines laid forth by ISO 20776-1, the antimicrobial analysis was repeated three times, with the most prevalent MIC or MBC value being recorded throughout all three runs.

Photosensitive antimicrobial activity of amino acid CDs

Photodynamic bacteria/fungus inhibition efficacy of the amino acid CDs against K. pneumoniae, B. subtilis, and C. albicans were assessed by comparing the microbial growing differences via UV-A light treatment vs no UV light exposure. Stock bacteria/fungus suspension was prepared by collecting bacteria/fungus colonies on solid growth medium and placed into liquid growth medium. The stock suspension was cultivated for 6 h to adjust the mid-logarithmic growth phase. After that, the concentration of the bacteria/fungus colony was adjusted using the MacFarland 0.5 standard at about 1.5 × 108 CFU/mL dispersed in 0.9% NaCl solution as a stock microorganism solution. In test tubes, 100 μL of 1 mg/mL CDs suspension in a 0.9% NaCl aqueous solution was placed in 1 mL of the stock microorganism solution and irradiated with UV-A light source (λ = 315–400 nm, Ultra Vitalux, Osram GmbH, Munich, Germany) or waited without UV-A light for 30 min. As a control, only 1 mL of the stock microorganism solution without any material was treated under UV-A light or incubated without light for 30 min. The distance between the tube and light source was adjusted to 20 cm with 6.88 mW/cm2 irradiance and 12.38 J/cm2 fluence values. Microbial viability was evaluated prior to and following treatments using a microplate reader (Heales, MB-530, Shenzhen Huisong Technology Development Co., Ltd, Shenzhen, China) at 590 nm. In addition, the usual CFU counting technique was also used to evaluate the survival of bacteria/fungal colonies on a solig agar plate. This study was carried out three times, and the findings were presented with errors.

Results and discussion

Amino acids are generally used in the manufacture of green synthesis carbon dots (CDs) due to their outstanding biocompatibility and as sources of nitrogen. In the literature, amino acid-based CDs were synthesized and compared depending on their side chains [24, 27]; however, no study has examined antipathogenic activities of carbon dots derived from inherently fluorescent amino acids. It is well-known that phenylalanine (Phe), tyrosine (Tyr), and tryptophan (Trp), essential amino acids, possess intrinsic fluorescence ability owing to their aromatic chromophores [28]. Besides these amino acids, the lysine (Lys) solution exhibited great fluorescence intensity attributed to aggregation in aqueous solution through intermolecular interactions, in addition to hydrogen bonding [29]. This study examined the photodynamic antimicrobial activity of CDs generated from intrinsically fluorescent amino acids. Here, lysine (Lys), phenylalanine (Phe), tyrosine (Tyr), and tryptophan (Trp) CDs were individually synthesized through two prevalent methods: microwave (MW) and hydrothermal (HT) treatment. In the MW technique, N-doped amino acid-derived CDs were synthesized through a carbonization process lasting only 3 min, whereas the same CDs were produced for 4 h at 250 °C in a Teflon-lined autoclave using the HT technique. Figure 1 illustrates significant fluorescence emissions in CDs produced from Lys and Trp, using both procedures, while CDs derived from Phe and Tyr exhibited minimal light emission under UV light exposure at a wavelength of 365 nm (λex). Indeed, fluorescence emission is absent in Phe CDs synthesized via the MW technique, whereas it is present in Phe CDs produced by the HT method.

Preparation of Nitrogen-doped carbon dots (CDs) from fluorescence essential amino acids: lysine (Lys), phenylalanine (Phe), tyrosine (Tyr), and tryptophan (Trp), and photograph of their suspensions in DI water at 1 mg/mL concentration under UV light at 365 nm excitation

The absorbance value of Lys, Phe, Tyr, and Trp amino acids and their CD forms prepared by MW and HT processes were investigated by UV–Vis spectroscopy as shown in Fig. 2. The amino acids observed the strong absorbance peak at approximately 210–220 nm coming from the π–π* transitions of the peptide backbone (–COO⁻/–NH₃⁺) and n–π* transitions of the carboxyl groups of the amino acids. A strong peak was also seen in the CD forms for all types of amino acid-based CDs. In addition, the peak at 280 nm was generally attributed to π–π* transitions of aromatic side chains of Phe, Tyr, and Trp. Also, the weak absorption observed at 280 nm was not intrinsic to Lys, which belongs to n–π* transitions of aggregation or complexation of Lys. In CD forms, the slight absorbance was presented at 310–390 nm, which is due to newly formed conjugated carbon domains and surface states of C = O and C = N groups.

Figure 3a illustrates that Lys had significant fluorescence intensity, peaking at an emission wavelength of 475 nm when excited at 390 nm. According to the literature, the lysine amino acid is often non-emissive due to the absence of an aromatic network and an extensive π-conjugated structure, which are essential for chromophore characteristics. Consequently, crystalline lysine exhibited negligible fluorescence emission, whereas the aqueous form demonstrated blue fluorescence [30] attributable to molecule aggregation via hydrogen bonding and intermolecular interactions [29]. Lys CDs synthesized using MW and HT procedures demonstrated nearly identical peak photoluminescence emission in the blue-green spectrum at approximately 475 nm when excited at around 390 nm, attributed to nitrogen-related surface states post-carbonization. Figure 3b and c illustrate a deficiency in fluorescence intensity within the Phe and Tyr spectra, attributable to the absence of π-conjugation; their CD forms exhibited emissive surface/defect states and conjugated networks at around 450 nm when excited at around 370 nm [6]. Furthermore, excellent fluorescence emission intensity at 440 nm was obtained for Trp CDs@MW and Trp CDs@HT at λex 360 nm and 390 nm, respectively. As seen in Table 1, the quantum yield of the Lys CDs@MW and Lys CDs@HT were found as 11.7 ± 1.3% and 28.4 ± 2.1%, respectively. In Phe and Tyr CDs for both methods showed low QY values as ~ 6%, but the highest QY was observed in Trp CDs with 42.8 ± 0.9% for Trp CDs@MW and 88.3 ± 2.7% for Trp CDs@HT. The quantum yield of HT-prepared Lys and Trp CDs was much superior to that of MW-assisted Lys and Trp CDs, mainly attributable to the distinct carbonization dynamics involving prolonged thermal exposure [5] or surface passivation or functionalities [11].

Table 1 shows the hydrodynamic dimensions of the synthesized amino acid CDs. The dimensions of the amino acid CDs vary between 100 and 200 nm.

Zeta potential measurements of the CD suspensions at different pH values were used to determine their isoelectric points (IEPs), as presented in Fig. 4 and Table 1. Figure 4 shows the zeta potential values of Lys CDs synthesized in HT and MW environments, respectively, depending on the pH. Lys@HT and Lys CDs@MW had an IEP of 4.6 and 4.0, respectively. The CDs synthesized in different environments are shown in Fig. 4 b, depending on pH. They have IEPs of 3.8 and 4.8, respectively. The pH-dependent zeta potentials of Tyr CDs@MW and Tyr CDs@HT are given in Fig. 4c. The IEP of Tyr CDs and Tyr@HT CDs were determined as 2.8 and 4.6. Since all the synthesized amino acid CDs are lower than IEP 7, they are all acidic in character. Also, around the isoelectric point, the zeta potential values of Lys CDs approach zero, indicating minimal electrostatic stabilization and a high probability of particle aggregation.

In Fig. 4d, the zeta potentials of Trp CDs synthesized in HT and MW environments are given, respectively, depending on pH. The IEPs of Trp@HT and Trp@MW CDs were found to be 4.1 and 6, respectively.

Hemocompatibility and cytotoxicity of the amino acid CDs at 1000 µg/mL concentration were determined by hemolysis, blood clotting tests, in addition to the effect on L929 fibroblast cells, as indicated in Fig. 5.

Blood compatibility of amino acid CDs prepared from hydrothermal process for a hemolysis and b blood clotting assays at 1000 µg/mL concentration and c their cytotoxicity on L929 human fibroblast cells for 24 h incubation time at 1000 µg/mL concentration

Hemolysis ratio, which shows the destroyed erythrocyte cells, could be a maximum 5% for intravenous applications. As depicted in Fig. 5a, the hemolysis % of Lys, Phe, Tyr, and Trp CDs were found as 2.4 ± 0.9, 3.1 ± 0.8, 3.6 ± 1.2, 1.8 ± 0.4%, respectively. Lys, Phe, and Tyr CDs were slightly hemolytic with a 2–5% hemolysis ratio, whereas Trp CDs were non-hemolytic as below to 2% hemolysis ratio. In the other blood compatibility test, no important blood clotting was measured with more than 82% blood clotting index when interacting with 1000 µg/mL concentration of the CDs synthesized from fluorescence amino acids as demonstrated in Fig. 5b. It could be said that all types of amino acid-derived CDs could be safely used in intravenous applications up to 1000 µg/mL concentration with lower hemolysis effect, and no significant blood clotting activity. Moreover, cell viability of L929 fibroblast cells treated with 1000 µg/mL concentration of Lys, Phe, Tyr, and Trp CDs were found as 86 ± 5, 89 ± 2, 79 ± 3, and 89 ± 2%, respectively, as shown in Fig. 5c. Similar to blood compatibility results, amino acid CDs exhibited great biocompatibility on fibroblast cells even at a high concentration of 1000 μg/mL. These results indicate that the Lys, Phe, Tyr, and Trp CDs have great potential as biomaterials for in vivo applications.

Antimicrobial effect of the amino acid CDs was determined against K. pneumoniae (ATCC 700603) as Gram-negative bacteria, B. subtilis (ATCC 6633) as Gram-positive bacteria, and C. albicans (ATCC 10231) as a fungus through microtiter broth dilution.

As listed in Table 2, the Lys CDs did not show antimicrobial activity up to 10 mg/mL concentration. This behavior can be attributed to the surface charge characteristics of the synthesized CDs. Zeta potential analysis revealed that Lys CDs possess a negative surface charge, which weakens electrostatic interactions with negatively charged microbial cell membranes. In contrast, Phe, Tyr, and Trp CDs that exhibit positive surface charges enable strong electrostatic attraction toward the negatively charged outer membranes of bacteria and fungi. Such interactions facilitate membrane disruption and enhance antimicrobial efficacy, which explains the higher antimicrobial potency observed for these positively charged CDs. Furthermore, the Phe CDs showed the highest antibacterial effect on B. subtilis with a 1.25 mg/mL MIC value, whereas Tyr CDs were more susceptible against C. albicans fungus with 1.25 mg/mL MIC and MFC values. In addition, Trp CDs demonstrated the same antimicrobial activity with a 2.5 mg/mL MIC value on both strains. The obtained antimicrobial variations in MIC values among the CDs could be dependent on the surface charge, aromatic structure, carbonization degree, and surface defect states. Gram-negative K. pneumoniae is a more resistant species in comparison with Gram-positive B. subtilis, and the synthesized CDs show the lowest antimicrobial activity on K. pneumoniae. Furthermore, aromatic moieties of Phe and Tyr CDs provided stronger hydrophobic interaction with the bacterial/fungal cells and facilitated the disruption of membrane integrity.

Photoinduced antimicrobial activity of the amino acid CDs@HT at 100 μg/mL concentration was investigated against K. pneumoniae, B. subtilis, and C. albicans as depicted in Fig. 6.

Photoinduced antimicrobial effects of 100 μg/mL concentration of amino acid CDs@HT against K. pneumoniae, B. subtilis, and C. albicans under no UV light and UV light exposure with 315–400 nm, UVa, 300 W UV lamp for 30 min

The bacteria/fungus suspension was exposed to UV-A light with 6.88 mW/cm2 irradiance and 12.38 J/cm2 fluence values or incubated without UV-A for 30 min in the presence of Lys, Phe, Tyr, and Trp CDs@HT. Viability % of K. pneumoniae was inhibited to 84 ± 1 and 76 ± 2% in Lys CDs@HT and Trp CDs@HT containing medium through 30 min UV-A exposure, as demonstrated in Fig. 6a. Similar bacterial killing was found in B. subtilis with 79 ± 4 and 69 ± 2% bacterial viability for Lys CDs@HT and Trp CDs@HT, respectively. No significant photodynamic activity was seen in Phe CDs@HT and Tyr CDs@HT treatment for bacterial species, but all forms of amino acid CDs presented a slightly photoinduced antipathogenic effect on C. albicans fungus. The wide-spectrum antimicrobial properties of the amino acid carbon dots (CDs) were substantially improved by exposure to UV irradiation for 30 min, especially for Lys CDs@HT and Trp CDs@HT. This photosensitive antimicrobial activity of the amino acid CDs could be caused by reactive oxygen species (ROS) production by induced electron–hole separation in the carbon core under UV light. These ROS, including superoxide radicals, singlet oxygen, and hydroxyl radicals, created oxidative stress in the bacterial/fungal cells [31].

Conclusions

Nitrogen-doped carbon dots (CDs) were prepared from fluorescent essential amino acids: lysine (Lys), phenylalanine (Phe), tyrosine (Tyr), and tryptophan (Trp) by employing two reactor systems: a) microwave (MW) and Teflon-lined autoclave (HT). Nanometer size range with a graphitic carbon network was obtained by a one-pot hydrothermal treatment of amino acids and citric acid at a 1:3 weight ratio. The fluorescence property of these amino acids was significantly improved by the synthesis of CD forms, and great fluorescence intensity was measured in the range of 420–470 nm emission wavelength at 300–350 nm excitation wavelengths. The prepared amino acid CDs show great biosafety on L929 fibroblast cells and blood cells at a 1000 μg/mL concentration. Great antibacterial activity was determined for Phe, Tyr, and Trp CDs against pathogenic microorganisms such as Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacterial and fungal species. Furthermore, UV exposure in the presence of amino acids CD shows slightly photodynamic antimicrobial activity for just 30 min treatment of UV-A light. Especially, Trp CDs@HT exhibited the most effective photosensitive antipathogenic materials with its highest quantum yield as approximately 88%.
