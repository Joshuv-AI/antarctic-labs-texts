Global Incidence of Appendectomy: A Population-Based Study of the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development
Authors: Elizabeth M. Buie, Stephanie Coward, Michael J. Buie, Lindsay Hracs, James A. King, Lucy M. Wilson, Joseph W. Windsor, May Lynn Quan, Tamara I. Gimon, Gilaad G. Kaplan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Study Design:Retrospective, observational, multi-region population-based cohort study.Objective:To determine temporal changes in appendectomy incidence over time and compare appendectomy rates before and during the COVID-19 pandemic.Background:Appendicitis is a common cause of abdominal pain presenting to emergency departments. However, temporal trends of appendicitis rates vary globally.Methods:We conducted a population-based observational cohort study using data from regions in the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development across 2000 to 2023. Poisson regression, or negative binomial if overdispersion was present, was used to calculate the average annual percentage change (AAPC) in appendectomy rates per 100,000 person-years with associated 95% confidence intervals (CI). Davies’ test was used to identify inflection points. AAPCs were pooled across regions using meta-analysis. Incidence rate ratios were calculated using Poisson, or negative binomial regression, comparing rates before (2018–2019) and during (2020–2021) the pandemic and pooled across regions using meta-analysis.Results:The pooled AAPC was −0.75% (95% CI: −0.86 to −0.53%). Appendectomy rates were significantly decreasing in 24 regions, significantly increasing in seven, and remaining stable in nine. Inflection points were found in 30 regions, and 17 regions showed a reversal in trend direction after the inflection. The pooled incidence rate ratios estimate before and during the pandemic was 0.94 (95% CI: 0.91–0.97).Conclusions:Appendectomy rates across Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development regions declined by 0.75% annually, with an additional 6% drop during the COVID-19 pandemic. Geographic variability likely reflects differences in healthcare access, diagnostic practices, and non-surgical management. Ongoing surveillance is essential to understand evolving trends and guide surgical planning and global health policy.

INTRODUCTION

Appendicitis is one of the most common causes of abdominal pain presenting to emergency departments.1,2 If left untreated, appendicitis can lead to severe complications including sepsis, bowel obstruction, intraabdominal abscess, peritonitis, perforation, and, in extreme cases, death.3 Treatment for appendicitis has evolved from surgery as the primary approach to include nonoperative management with antibiotics.2,4

A prior systematic review of population-based studies evaluating the global incidence of appendicitis found that in early industrialized regions of North America, Europe, and Oceania, the incidence of appendicitis had plateaued or declined, potentially due to advances to imaging leading to more accurate diagnosis and increased use of nonoperative management with antibiotics.5 In contrast, more limited data from newly industrialized and emerging regions in Africa, Asia, and Latin America showed a dramatic increase in the incidence of appendicitis.

A systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrated an increased rate of complicated appendicitis during the COVID-19 pandemic.6 The increase was attributed to multiple factors, most notably delayed treatment out of fear of contracting severe acute respiratory syndrome, coronavirus 2, the virus that leads to COVID-19, and longer wait times between presentation at the emergency department and receiving surgical treatment due to COVID-19 screening protocols.6

We aim to evaluate changes in appendectomy incidence over time across member nations of the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) and compare appendectomy rates in the 2 years before and during the COVID-19 pandemic, providing insight into the potential factors that may influence the incidence and management of appendicitis.

METHODS

Study Design

This study is a retrospective, observational, multi-region population-based cohort study involving 33 of the 38 member regions of the OECD and 7 nonmember regions that provide longitudinal data on appendectomies to the OECD database; excluded regions lack the necessary data for this analysis.

Data Sources

The OECD is an international organization that collects data to inform global social, economic, and environmental policies.7 The 38 member nations contribute to data collection, analyses, share experiences, dissemination of best-practices, and provide guidance on public policies to create international standards. The OECD collects a wide range of health-related data including, mortality trends, health expenditures and financing, health outcomes, social determinants of health, and healthcare resources.7 The OECD database has previously been used to evaluate global trends in the epidemiology of digestive diseases, including peptic ulcer disease and inflammatory bowel disease.8,9

We investigated the global incidence and temporal trends of hospitalization rates for appendectomy using the public dataset from the 33 member nations of the OECD with available data: Australia (2000–2021), Austria (2000–2022), Belgium (2006–2022), Canada (2006–2022), Costa Rica (2000–2023), Czechia (2005–2022), Denmark (2000–2022), Estonia (2003–2022), Finland (2000–2022), France (2000–2022), Germany (2005–2022), Greece (2000–2017), Hungary (2004–2022), Iceland (2011–2022), Ireland (2000–2022), Israel (2000–2022), Italy (2001–2022), South Korea (2010–2022), Lithuania (2005–2022), Luxembourg (2000–2022), Mexico (2000–2022), Netherlands (2005–2021), New Zealand (2000–2020), Norway (2013–2023), Poland (2005–2022), Portugal (2000–2023), Slovak Republic (2000–2008, 2018–2022), Slovenia (2005–2022), Spain (2004–2022), Sweden (2005–2022), Switzerland (2002–2008, 2014–2022), Türkiye (2018–2023), and the United Kingdom (2000–2022). Four OECD member regions (Chile, Columbia, Japan, and the United States) were excluded due to absence of appendectomy data, and 1 OECD member region (Latvia) was excluded due to insufficient longitudinal data.7,10

Additionally, appendectomy data from 7 non-OECD members regions were included: Bulgaria (2000–2022), Croatia (2012–2022), Cyprus (2005–2010, 2013–2022), Malta (2012–2021), North Macedonia (2010–2021), Romania (2009–2022), and Serbia (2014–2022).7,10 Reporting for most regions began in 2000 or later, except for few regions starting in the early to mid 1990s. To ensure consistency across nations, analyses were limited to 2000 to 2023. We also investigated appendectomy incidence before and during the COVID-19 pandemic, comparing 2 years prepandemic (2018–2019) to 2 years during the pandemic (2020–2021).

Study Population

Each region provides health outcome data based on their national health surveillance datasets. For example, Canada’s data are derived from the Canadian Institute for Health Information using the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Canada (ICD-10-CA). Australia’s data is derived from the Australian Institute of Health and Welfare, which uses the Australian Modification of the Tenth Revision (ICD-10-AM). Some member regions of the OECD have transitioned from ICD-9 to ICD-10 codes to align with international standards; however, not all regions in the OECD use ICD coding as a universal standard.7,11 The specific data sources and coding classifications for an appendectomy procedure for each region are summarized in Supplemental Tables 1 and 2, https://links.lww.com/AOSO/A619.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes are appendectomy incidence and temporal trends in annual appendectomy incidence, measured per 100,000 person-years. The secondary outcome is a comparison of appendectomy incidence in the 2 years prepandemic (2018–2019) and during (2020–2021) the COVID-19 pandemic.

Statistical Methods and Data Analysis

The database includes, for each region and year: procedure type, number of annual procedures, and incidence of appendectomy per 100,000 person-years. For each region, the appendectomy incidence and the estimated number of appendectomies performed for the most recent year of data are reported directly from the OECD dataset. The average annual percentage change (AAPC) in appendectomy incidence rates with associated 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated using Poisson regression, or negative binomial if overdispersion was present, based on the total appendectomies and the calculated population denominators. The denominators were calculated through back calculation using the rate per 100,000 and number of appendectomies, which was obtained from the OECD dataset. The AAPCs are analyzed from 2000 to 2023 for regions with at least 3 datapoints spanning 5 or more years. AAPCs were estimated across available years for each region, including those that did not report data every year; missing years are reported to provide transparency.

Temporal inflection points were assessed for in regions with at least 10 data points. AAPCs before and after inflection points were calculated using a Poisson regression with a segmented regression analysis. Significant differences in AAPCs before and after inflection points were determined using Davies’ test, which compares the differences in the slope parameters from the segmented model.

AAPCs were pooled across regions using a random effects meta-analysis model with corresponding 95% CIs to account for expected heterogeneity in reporting appendectomy across regions. Additionally, regions were stratified into 9 geographic strata (Oceania, Western Europe, Eastern Europe, Northern Europe, Southern Europe, North America, Latin America and the Caribbean, Western Asia, and Eastern Asia) and pooled AAPC values were calculated. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic.

For the secondary analysis to compare the 2 years before the pandemic (2018–2019) with the 2 years during the pandemic (2020–2021), incidence rate ratios (IRRs) were calculated for each period using Poisson regression, or negative binomial regression if overdispersion was present. The IRRs were first calculated separately for each region with a minimum of 2 data points and then pooled across all regions using a random effects meta-analysis with corresponding 95% CIs. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic.

All analyses were conducted using RStudio version 2024.09.1 + 364 with packages dplyr version 1.1.4., DT version 0.33, ggplot version 3.4.1., MASS version 7.3-58.2, readxl version 1.4.3., and segmented version 2.1-3.

RESULTS

Geographic variation in appendectomy incidence rates was observed, with rates ranging from 56.8 per 100,000 in Portugal (2023) to 173.07 per 100,000 in Romania (2022) (Supplemental Table 3, https://links.lww.com/AOSO/A619, Fig. 1A). Appendectomy rates significantly decreased in 24 regions, significantly increased in 7 regions, and remained stable in nine regions (Figs. 1B and 2). AAPCs ranged from −9.04% (95% CI: −10.62 to −7.43) in Romania to 1.58% (95% CI: 1.00–2.18) in Mexico (Supplemental Table 3, https://links.lww.com/AOSO/A619). The pooled AAPC estimate across all regions was −0.75% (95% CI: −0.86 to −0.53) (Fig. 3).

There are 30 regions that demonstrated an inflection point, 17 of which showed a reversal in their trend after the inflection point (Supplemental Table 3, https://links.lww.com/AOSO/A619). For example, the trend changed from decreasing to increasing in Finland from −3.52% (95% CI: −4.13 to −2.90) to 1.34% (95% CI: 0.97–1.72) after 2007 and in Denmark from −4.45% (95% CI: −4.89 to −4.00) to 1.53% (95% CI: 1.14–1.92) after 2006. The trend changed from increasing to decreasing in Canada from 0.72% (95% CI: 0.55–0.89) to −1.90% (95% CI: −3.59 to −0.18) after 2019; in Australia from 2.10% (95% CI: 1.56–2.63) to −1.28% (95% CI: −1.71 to −0.83) after 2013; and in Costa Rica from 1.94% (95% CI: 1.54–2.34) to −1.40% (95% CI: −1.68 to −1.13) after 2007. The trend changed from decreasing to stable in Belgium from −1.02% (95% CI: −1.44 to −0.61) to 0.19% (95% CI: −0.11 to 0.48) after 2014 and increasing to stable in Spain from 1.24% (95% CI: 0.86–1.63) to 0.10% (95% CI: −0.32 to 0.52) after 2009.

Eastern Europe’s pooled AAPC is −0.95 (95%CI: −0.99 to −0.75) (Supplemental Fig. 1, Supplemental Table 4, https://links.lww.com/AOSO/A619); Western Europe’s pooled AAPC is −0.83 (95% CI: −0.94 to −0.54) (Supplemental Fig. 2, Supplemental Table 5, https://links.lww.com/AOSO/A619); and Southern Europe’s pooled AAPC is −0.81 (95% CI: −0.96 to −0.22) (Supplemental Fig. 3, Supplemental Table 6, https://links.lww.com/AOSO/A619), each showing a significant decrease in appendectomy rates whereas Northern Europe was stable, with a pooled AAPC of −0.06 (95%CI: −0.48 to 0.71) (Supplemental Fig. 4, Supplemental Table 7, https://links.lww.com/AOSO/A619). Supplemental Figs. 5 to 9 and Supplemental Tables 8 to 12, https://links.lww.com/AOSO/A619 present findings for the remaining supra-regions (Latin America and the Caribbean, Western Asia, Eastern Asia, North America, and Oceania), which had insufficient data (≤2 regions) to permit pooled meta-analysis.

Figure 4 demonstrates the IRR of appendectomy between the 2 years prepandemic (2018–2019) and during the COVID-19 pandemic (2020–2021), with 18 regions showing a significant decrease in appendectomy rates during the pandemic, and 4 regions with a significant increase in appendectomy rates during the pandemic. No significant difference was found in the remaining 17 regions. Romania had the lowest significant IRR at 0.62 (95% CI: 0.59–0.66) whereas Norway had the largest significant IRR at 1.08 (95% CI: 1.05–1.10) (Supplemental Table 13, https://links.lww.com/AOSO/A619). The pooled IRR estimate across all regions was 0.94 (95% CI: 0.91–0.97).

DISCUSSION

Our study reveals that appendectomy rates have undergone substantial changes across OECD nations in the 21st century, with a general trend toward decline. These shifts likely reflect evolving clinical practices, including improved diagnostic accuracy, increased adoption of nonoperative management strategies, and health system adaptations during global events such as the COVID-19 pandemic.2,12 Although most regions are experiencing declining rates, the direction and magnitude of change varies, underscoring the influence of local healthcare policies, resource availability, and clinical guidelines. These findings highlight the need for ongoing surveillance of surgical trends and suggest that appendectomy rates may serve as a marker for broader transformations in emergency and surgical care delivery.

Incidence estimates from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 study were generated through statistical modeling informed by administrative health data, including insurance claims and hospital discharge records. As a result, GBD produces modeled incidence rates that are methodologically distinct from the population-based surveillance data captured by the OECD and tend to yield higher incidence estimates. For example, GBD estimated the age-standardized global incidence rate of appendicitis in 2021 to be 214 per 100,000 person-years across 204 regions, substantially higher than the average rates reported in our analysis.13 For regions that overlapped with OECD regions, the modeled GBD incidence rates were considerably higher; for example, Switzerland GBD estimate 2021: 363 per 100,000 person years; Switzerland OECD estimate 2021: 167.6 per 100,000 person years.10,13 Some differences may be explained by higher rates of appendicitis in regions outside the OECD, where appendicitis may occur more frequently due to differences in environmental exposures, healthcare access, or population demographics. For example, the North African incidence rate of appendicitis reported by the GBD is 192 per 100,000 person-years and the Chinese incidence rate of appendicitis reported by GBD is 312 per 100,000 person-years, both considerably higher than the incidence rates for the OECD regions.13 However, population-based studies within the last decade in regions outside of the OECD such as mainland China reported incidence of appendicitis to be 90 per 100,000 person-years,14 which is considerably lower than the GBD estimates for incidence of appendicitis at 312 per 100,000 in China.13 Although limited, the population-based studies from regions like China suggest potential overestimation in GBD modeled incidence estimates and help explain the observed differences between the OECD and GBD reported incidence rates.

Regional heterogeneity in appendectomy rates and temporal trends is evident across the OECD dataset. A key contributor to this variability is that our analysis focused on appendectomy procedures rather than the underlying diagnosis. Although appendicitis is the most common indication, appendectomies may also be performed for other conditions, such as appendiceal neoplasms. Furthermore, increasing adoption of nonsurgical management, particularly antibiotic therapy, introduces additional variability, as medically managed cases of appendicitis are not captured in surgical datasets.

Clinical decision-making regarding first-line treatment varies across regions, influenced by factors such as practice guidelines, healthcare infrastructure, and physician or patient preferences.2 A 2025 individual-level meta-analysis demonstrated the use of antibiotics as a first line therapy is a safe and effective alternative to appendectomy for acute appendicitis, with two-thirds of patients avoiding surgery in the first year after treatment.15 New research promoting the use of antibiotics as a first line of treatment for acute appendicitis may contribute to the declining appendectomy rates observed in our study. Additionally, the methodological differences in data collection, data sources, and reporting across regions may lead to overestimation or underestimation of appendectomy rates. Understanding regional and temporal trends in annual appendectomy rates can support health system planning, resource allocation, and the development of evidence-based strategies to reduce the burden of disease.

Previous studies have shown a global decline in the incidence of appendicitis, although the cause is not fully understood.16 Proposed explanations include changes in diet, increased use of antibiotics, improvements in socioeconomic status, and changes in patterns of immunity and infectious disease exposures.1,3,16 Conversely, some studies have reported rising incidence in lower income regions, potentially explained by healthcare access and advanced diagnostics.13 Global epidemiologic trends have been previously postulated to transition through stages whereby a region initially experiences a rise in appendicitis incidence followed by a decline and eventual plateau.5 This framework may help contextualize the trends observed in our analysis as most OECD regions seem to be in the declining phase. Given that OECD membership generally reflects a level of economic development and democratic governance, it is possible that most included regions have already undergone these epidemiologic transitions.5

The COVID-19 pandemic seems to have influenced appendectomy trends across many regions. An overall decline of approximately 6% was observed through 2020 to 2021 across nations reporting to the OECD, suggesting a pandemic-associated reduction in appendectomy rates. Multiple studies have reported similar trends, attributing them to increased use of nonoperative management strategies, and efforts to alleviate pressure on healthcare systems.12 Delayed care-seeking due to fear of COVID-19 exposure may have also contributed, alongside reports of rising complicated appendicitis. Differences in healthcare infrastructure, pandemic restrictions, and clinical practices across regions likely influenced these patterns. Although causality cannot be established, the pandemic’s impact underscores the importance of considering external disruptions when interpreting surgical epidemiology.

The OECD monitors and updates annual trends in appendectomy rates, which can help prepare regions for time and resource allocation around diagnosis and management in emergency and operating rooms. Understanding the potential impact of COVID-19 is especially important, as the pandemic may have altered the trajectory of epidemiologic trends. Significant differences between periods suggest that external factors could compromise the accuracy of long-term analyses that do not account for these factors. Ongoing surveillance of appendectomy rates in OECD regions is essential to assess whether trends shift as regions transition beyond the pandemic.

The OECD offers several strengths, including over 2 decades of geographic appendectomy data from 33 of 38 member nations and 7 nonmember regions. The depth of the data provides a greater insight into long-term temporal trends around global appendectomy rates. Additionally, the OECD includes only population-based data, which limits selection bias and enhances the generalizability of the findings.

Our study has the following limitations. First, we did not have access to the crude individual-level data from the national databases that make up the OECD, which limits the granularity of our analyses. The OECD does not differentiate between indications for appendectomy, among acute, recurrent, complicated, or tumor. Additionally, the OECD does not capture nonoperative management such as antibiotic treatment. The dataset lacks age- or sex-specific appendectomy rates, preventing standardized, stratified, or adjusted analyses. Countries contributed only years with available appendectomy data in the OECD database. As some began reporting partway through the study period, time series differed by country. AAPCs were estimated using each country’s observed data, with no imputation of missing values.

Our study aimed to evaluate global trends; however, the OECD database only includes 33 analyzable member regions and seven nonmember regions.7 OECD membership requires democratic governments with free market economies; therefore, the data only includes select populations, which introduces sampling bias on global trends, limiting generalizability to low- and middle-income regions. Differences in how data are collected and reported between national administrative health systems may result in underreporting and misclassification errors, leading to measurement and selection bias. For instance, incidental appendectomies may inflate procedure counts, leading to overestimation of trends. Further, the annual data prevents more granular temporal analyses such as monthly appendectomy rates. As a result, our pandemic period for 2020 includes January to March, months preceding the declaration of the COVID-19 pandemic, potentially diluting observed effects.

The OECD dataset demonstrates an overall decreasing trend in appendectomy rates over the past 2 decades, potentially driven by factors such as diet changes, rising socioeconomic status, increased use of antibiotics to manage appendicitis, and changes in infectious disease patterns.1,16 The decreased incidence rates during the COVID-19 pandemic may reflect region-specific factors, including barriers to accessing care, government-imposed restrictions, and delayed diagnoses. Future studies should evaluate the underlying reasons of these trends, including the impact of industrialization, economic development, clinical decision-making, healthcare accessibility, and the broader influence of the pandemic. Expanding this research to include low- and middle-income regions will offer a more comprehensive and globally representative understanding of appendectomy trends.
