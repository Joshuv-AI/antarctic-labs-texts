Symplectic cuts and open/closed strings II
Luca Cassia

Abstract
In [ CLZ25 ] we established a connection between symplectic cuts of Calabi–Yau threefolds and open topological strings, and used this to introduce an equivariant deformation of the disk potential of toric branes. In this paper we establish a connection to higher-dimensional Calabi–Yau geometries by showing that the equivariant disk potential arises as an equivariant period of certain Calabi–Yau fourfolds and fivefolds, which encode moduli spaces of one and two symplectic cuts (the maximal case) by a construction of Braverman [ Bra99 ] . Extended Picard–Fuchs equations for toric branes, capturing dependence on both open and closed string moduli, are derived from a suitable limit of the equivariant quantum cohomology rings of the higher Calabi–Yau geometries.

Introduction

The computation of disk potentials of toric branes by means of

[

Recently, a deformation of the disk potential by a collection of equivariant parameters

The definition of

The cut of a Calabi–Yau threefold

X_{3}\quad\rightsquigarrow\quad X_{3}^{<}\cup_{X_{2}}X_{3}^{>}\,.

The standard non-equivariant disk potential is recovered from the small

In this paper, we build on the relation between toric branes and symplectic cuts to uncover new connections between equivariant disk potentials and higher dimensional Calabi–Yau geometries.

\begin{array}[]{c}\text{CY3 with toric brane}\\

\begin{array}[]{c}\text{disk potential}\\

Quantum cuts and mirror curves

We provide a derivation of the connection between symplectic cuts and disk potentials of toric branes observed in our previous work
[
CLZ25
]
. More precisely, we show that the quantum Lebesgue measure
\mathcal{H}^{D}
admits a closed form expression in terms of Lauricella’s
D

\mathcal{H}^{D}(t,c,\epsilon)=\Ga\Big(\sum_{i}\epsilon_{i}\Big)\,\mathrm{e}^{-t\cdot M\cdot\epsilon}\,x^{A_{1}\cdot\epsilon}\mathrm{B}\Big(A_{2}\cdot\epsilon,k\sum_{i}\epsilon_{i}-A_{2}\cdot\epsilon\Big)\left(\prod_{j=1}^{k}(-y_{j})^{-\sum_{i}\epsilon_{i}}\right)\\
\times F^{(k)}_{D}\Big(A_{2}\cdot\epsilon,\sum_{i}\epsilon_{i},\dots,\sum_{i}\epsilon_{i},k\sum_{i}\epsilon_{i};1+y_{1}^{-1},\dots,1+y_{k}^{-1}\Big)

(1.8)

whose arguments are given by the sheets
y_{j}\equiv y_{j}(x)
of the mirror curve of the toric brane, in the sense of
[
Aga+14
]
, where
x=\mathrm{e}^{-c}
.
The

are directly related to the usual non-equivariant disk potential of the brane by the Abel–Jacobi map
[
AV00
]
, namely
W_{\mathrm{disk}}=\int\log y(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\log x
.
Using this direct correspondence between
\mathcal{H}^{D}
and

we obtain an exact relation between the monodromy of
\mathcal{H}^{D}
and the disk potential, which holds for all toric Calabi–Yau threefolds.

Connections to Calabi–Yau fourfolds

It was observed in
[
Bra99
]
that there exists a complex manifold
X_{4}

which contains the symplectic cut (
1.1
) of
X_{3}
as a complex codimension-one subspace.
The manifold
X_{4}
is defined by the symplectic quotient

X_{4}=(X_{3}\times\mathbb{C}_{+}\times\mathbb{C}_{-})\sslash U(1)\,,

(1.9)

with respect to a canonical extension of the moment map from
X_{3}
to
X_{3}\times\mathbb{C}_{+}\times\mathbb{C}_{-}
. The resulting space
X_{4}
can be regarded as a fibration over
\mathbb{C}
, such that the generic fiber is complex isomorphic to the original space
X_{3}
, while the fiber over the origin is complex isomorphic to the singular (reducible) space (
1.1
).
If we require that the moment map used to define the symplectic cut satisfies the Calabi–Yau condition, then it follows that
X_{4}
is itself a CY manifold.

We study a quantization of the equivariant volume of the fourfold, defined by the hemisphere partition function of the
\epsilon
-GLSM with target
X_{4}
, and find that the equivariant disk potential (
1.2
) admits a natural uplift as an equivariant period associated to
X_{4}
, which recovers the former in the limit where two additional equivariant parameters vanish (
i.e.
those associated to the homogeneous coordinates on
\mathbb{C}_{\pm}
).
An important property of the four-dimensional uplift is that it arises as the equivariant period associated with an intersection of toric divisors of
X_{4}
, implying that it obeys the fourfold’s equivariant Picard–Fuchs (PF) equations.
In the limit
\epsilon_{\pm}\to 0
, these reduce to
extended
Picard–Fuchs equations for
W(t,c,\epsilon)
and for
\mathcal{F}_{\lessgtr}(t,c,\epsilon)
in the original CY3, with dependence on both open and closed string moduli.
In the fully non-equivariant limit
\epsilon_{i}\to 0
, in which
W(t,c,\epsilon)
reduces to the standard disk potential
W_{\mathrm{disk}}
, we show that the extended Picard–Fuchs equations become inhomogeneous.

The relation between symplectic cuts and CY4 makes contact with earlier results in the literature on several fronts. In particular, the fact that disk potentials obey inhomogeneous Picard–Fuchs equations was already observed in the case of the real quintic
[
Wal07
,
Wal08
,
MW09
]
. Moreover, a relation between open strings and periods of Calabi–Yau fourfolds was observed in
[
May02
,
LM01
]
. Our results provide a derivation of this correspondence, and generalize it to the equivariant setting.

Double cuts and Calabi–Yau fivefolds

Finally, we consider the possibility of performing multiple symplectic cuts on
X_{3}
. We assume that for each cut, the intersection of the two half-spaces,
X_{3}^{<}
and
X_{3}^{>}
, forms a Calabi–Yau manifold. This corresponds to requiring that the hyperplane associated with the cut contains an affine line parallel to the vector
(1,1,\dots,1)
in the base of the toric fibration. Under this condition, Braverman’s construction ensures that each resulting fourfold remains Calabi–Yau.
We refer to such symplectic cuts as “
Calabi–Yau type cuts
”, or simply “
Calabi–Yau cuts
”.

If, furthermore, the hyperplanes are in generic position

2

This excludes parallel, overlapping hyperplanes, which would lead to quotients by trivially acting

groups and thus to Artin stacks that are not of Deligne–Mumford type.
with nontrivial intersection, we deduce that at most two CY symplectic cuts can be performed simultaneously. Moreover, the intersection of all resulting half-spaces defines a complex one-dimensional submanifold in
X_{3}
.

For a general CY

The four divisors are further glued along a common divisor, which is a Calabi–Yau onefold

.
The quantum volume of

Here

We find that the quantum volume

\displaystyle=\Ga\Big(\sum_{i}\epsilon_{i}\Big)\,\mathrm{e}^{-t\cdot M\cdot\epsilon}\frac{x_{1}^{A_{1}\cdot\epsilon}x_{2}^{A_{2}\cdot\epsilon}}{H(x_{1},x_{2},z)^{\sum_{i}\epsilon_{i}}}\,,

(1.10)

where

X_{5}

\mathbb{C}^{2}

Organization of the paper

In Section
2
, we review the definition of quantum symplectic cuts and their relation to disk potentials for toric branes. We then give a general formula for the monodromy of the quantum Lebesgue measure, and show that under suitable assumptions its regular part reproduces the prediction of open string mirror symmetry for the disk potential.
The Calabi–Yau fourfold description of symplectic cuts is discussed in Section
3
. Here we also discuss the
\epsilon
-GLSM quantization of the fourfold and how it leads to extended Picard–Fuchs equations for quantum cuts of the CY3.
In Section
4
, we discuss double cuts, providing a description for these and for their quantization in terms of a Calabi–Yau fivefold geometry.
Section
5
contains a summary of the main results and concluding remarks.

2.1
Toric branes, symplectic cuts and equivariance

In

As argued in

Let

X_{3}=\mathbb{C}^{r+3}\sslash U(1)^{r}=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{r+3}Q^{a}_{i}|z_{i}|^{2}=t^{a}\right\}\Big/U(1)^{r}

(2.1)

The

The classical limit of the partition function, defined by taking

At finite values of

The disk function

\left(\prod_{\{i|\sum_{a}\gamma_{a}Q^{a}_{i}>0\}}\left(\cD_{i}\right)_{\sum_{a}\gamma_{a}Q^{a}_{i}}-\mathrm{e}^{-\sum_{a}\gamma_{a}t^{a}}\prod_{\{i|\sum_{a}\gamma_{a}Q^{a}_{i}\leq 0\}}\left(\cD_{i}\right)_{-\sum_{a}\gamma_{a}Q^{a}_{i}}\right)\mathcal{F}^{D}(t,\epsilon)=0

(z)_{n}

2.2

The computation of

The symplectic cut of

13

3.3
Quantum cuts of CY3 from equivariant periods of CY4

Next we discuss how the
\epsilon
-GLSM for

X_{3}

The main issue is that equivariant (quantum) volumes and periods in the fourfold depend on additional equivariant parameters
\epsilon_{\pm}
. To recover results for
X_{3}
we therefore need to study the limit
\epsilon_{\pm}\to 0
.

Observe that for small
\epsilon_{\pm}
, Euler’s beta function has leading order behavior
16
16

16

This is a well-known property, that can be derived using the integral representation

\mathrm{B}(a,b)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{a-1}}{(1+t)^{a+b}}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t
as follows

\mathrm{B}(\mathrm{i}x,-\mathrm{i}x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{\mathrm{i}x-1}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}xy}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y=2\pi\,\delta(x)\,.

(3.26)

\mathrm{B}(\epsilon_{+}-\phi,\epsilon_{-}+\phi)=\mathrm{B}(-\phi,\phi)\left(1+O(\epsilon_{\pm})\right)=2\pi\mathrm{i}\,\delta(\phi)\left(1+O(\epsilon_{\pm})\right)

(3.27)

It follows then that the leading behavior of
\rho
defined in (

) is

\rho(c,\epsilon_{\pm})=\Ga(\epsilon_{+}+\epsilon_{-})\left(1+O(\epsilon_{\pm})\right)

(3.28)

and therefore the quantum volume of

reduces to that of
X_{3}
times a constant factor that depends on
\epsilon_{\pm}

\mathcal{F}^{D}_{X_{4}}(t,c,\epsilon,\epsilon_{\pm})=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}c^{\prime}\,\mathcal{H}^{D}(t,c^{\prime},\epsilon)\cdot\rho(c-c^{\prime},\epsilon_{\pm})=\mathcal{F}^{D}(t,\epsilon)\,\Ga(\epsilon_{+}+\epsilon_{-})\left(1+O(\epsilon_{\pm})\right)

(3.29)

where, in the second equality we used relation (
2.18
) between
\mathcal{H}^{D}
and
\mathcal{F}^{D}
.

Similarly, we can recover equivariant half-space functions
\mathcal{F}^{D}_{\lessgtr}
by taking a limit on the equivariant periods of divisors in
X_{4}
.
For this purpose, observe that

\frac{(1-\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}\cD_{\pm}})}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\,\rho(c,\epsilon_{\pm})=-\mathrm{e}^{\pi\mathrm{i}\epsilon_{\pm}}\int_{\mathrm{i}\mathbb{R}}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\varphi}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\,\mathrm{e}^{\varphi(c\mp\pi\mathrm{i})}\,\frac{\Ga(\epsilon_{\mp}\pm\varphi)}{\Ga(1-\epsilon_{\pm}\pm\varphi)}=-\frac{\Theta_{H}(\pm(c\mp\pi\mathrm{i}))\,\mathrm{e}^{\mp\epsilon_{\mp}c+\pi\mathrm{i}(\epsilon_{+}+\epsilon_{-})}}{(1+\mathrm{e}^{\mp c})^{\epsilon_{+}+\epsilon_{-}}\Ga(1-\epsilon_{+}-\epsilon_{-})}

(3.30)

where
\Theta_{H}
is a Heaviside theta function and the integral has been evaluated using the Jeffrey--Kirwan residue prescription.
17
17

17

The JK prescription in this case is equivalent to closing the imaginary contour with a semicircle at infinity either to the left or to the right according to the value of the coefficient of
\varphi
in the exponential.

Then setting
\epsilon_{\pm}
to zero gives

\lim_{\epsilon_{\pm}\to 0}\frac{(1-\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}\cD_{\pm}})}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\,\rho(c,\epsilon_{\pm})=-\Theta_{H}(\pm c-\pi\mathrm{i})

(3.31)

This shows that

\lim_{\epsilon_{\pm}\to 0}\Pi(D_{+})=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}c^{\prime}\,\mathcal{H}^{D}(t,c^{\prime},\epsilon)\cdot\Theta_{H}(c-c^{\prime}-\pi\mathrm{i})=-\mathcal{F}^{D}_{<}(t,c-\pi\mathrm{i},\epsilon)

(3.32)

and

\lim_{\epsilon_{\pm}\to 0}\Pi(D_{-})=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}c^{\prime}\,\mathcal{H}^{D}(t,c^{\prime},\epsilon)\cdot\Theta_{H}(c^{\prime}-c-\pi\mathrm{i})=-\mathcal{F}^{D}_{>}(t,c+\pi\mathrm{i},\epsilon)\,.

Observe that, while the argument here is rather formal, in specific examples one can take the limit rigorously by computing the monodromy of the analytic function
\mathcal{F}^{D}_{X_{4}}
as explained in Remark
3.1
.

A similar statement holds for the intersection of divisors, which classically corresponds to
X_{2}
. Indeed, observe that the behavior of
\sigma
as
\epsilon_{\pm}
approach zero is

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon_{\pm}\to 0}\sigma(c,\epsilon_{\pm})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathrm{i}\mathbb{R}}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\varphi}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\,\mathrm{e}^{\varphi c}\,\frac{1}{\Ga(1+\varphi)\Ga(1-\varphi)}

(3.34)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathrm{i}\mathbb{R}}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\varphi}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\,\mathrm{e}^{\varphi c}\,\frac{\sin(\pi\varphi)}{\pi\varphi}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{c-\pi\mathrm{i}}^{c+\pi\mathrm{i}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}c^{\prime}\,\int_{\mathrm{i}\mathbb{R}}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\varphi}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\,\mathrm{e}^{\varphi c^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{c-\pi\mathrm{i}}^{c+\pi\mathrm{i}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}c^{\prime}\,\delta(c^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\Big(\Theta_{H}(c+\pi\mathrm{i})-\Theta_{H}(c-\pi\mathrm{i})\Big)\,,

therefore we obtain

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon_{\pm}\to 0}\Pi(D_{+}\cap D_{-})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}c^{\prime}\,\mathcal{H}^{D}(t,c^{\prime},\epsilon)\cdot\Big(\Theta_{H}(c-c^{\prime}+\pi\mathrm{i})-\Theta_{H}(c-c^{\prime}-\pi\mathrm{i})\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\Big(\mathcal{F}_{<}^{D}(t,c+\pi\mathrm{i},\epsilon)-\mathcal{F}_{<}^{D}(t,c-\pi\mathrm{i},\epsilon)\Big)\equiv W(t,c,\epsilon)\,.

\epsilon_{\pm}\to 0
, the period associated to

computed as intersection of divisors

coincides with the equivariant disk potential computed from the quantum cut of

Moreover, the derivation of the limit in (

W(t,c,\epsilon)=\oint\prod_{a}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\phi_{a}}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\varphi}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\,\frac{\sin(\pi\varphi)}{\pi\varphi}\,\mathrm{e}^{\sum_{a}\phi_{a}t^{a}+\varphi c}\prod_{i}\Ga(\epsilon_{i}+\sum_{a}\phi_{a}Q_{i}^{a}+\varphi q_{i})

(3.36)

which is exactly of the form (
2.17
), the only difference being the insertion of the function
\frac{\sin(\pi\varphi)}{\pi\varphi}

18
18

18

Notice that this insertion does not introduce any new poles in the integrand. Formally, we can write
W(t,c,\epsilon)=\frac{\sin(\pi\partial_{c})}{\pi\partial_{c}}\mathcal{H}^{D}(t,c,\epsilon)
where the operator
\frac{\sin(\pi\partial_{c})}{\pi\partial_{c}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\pi\mathrm{i}\partial_{c})^{2n}}{(2n+1)!}
is regarded as a power series in derivatives.

It is important here to distinguish between the period associated to

regarded as a subvariety of
X_{4}
and the quantum volume of
X_{2}
as a CY twofold.
The equivariant period associated to

even in the limit

X_{4}
, while the function
\mathcal{H}^{D}
is the partition function of an
\epsilon
-GLSM with target
X_{2}
without reference to any embedding into a larger space, and as such, it satisfies instead the PF equations of
X_{2}
.
For this reason, the functions
\Pi(D_{+}\cap D_{-})
and

are not the same, and instead they are related according to the formulae above.

3.4
Extended Picard–Fuchs equations for quantum cuts

While
X_{2}
,
X_{3}
and
X_{4}
are all Calabi–Yau manifolds by construction,
the half-spaces
X_{3}^{\lessgtr}
defined by the symplectic cut are generally not.
Additionally, the functions
\mathcal{F}^{D}_{\lessgtr}
are not obtained as disk partition functions
of
\epsilon
-GLSM with targets
X_{3}^{\lessgtr}
, but rather they are defined as integrals of
\mathcal{H}^{D}
. For this reasons we would not necessarily expect
\mathcal{F}^{D}_{\lessgtr}
to satisfy Picard–Fuchs equations for the spaces
X_{3}^{\lessgtr}
, and in fact this is not the case.
However, as we will argue shortly, the functions
\mathcal{F}^{D}_{\lessgtr}
do obey a certain generalization of the Picard–Fuchs equations of the Calabi–Yau threefold
X_{3}
.

This is a direct consequence of the observation that both
\mathcal{F}^{D}_{\lessgtr}
, as well as the equivariant disk potential
W
, arise from the limit
\epsilon_{\pm}\to 0
of equivariant periods of divisors (and their intersection) in
X_{4}
.

According to the general theory of
\epsilon
-GLSM developed in
[
CPZ23
]
, the quantum volume of
X_{4}
obeys a system of equations of the form

\PF_{\gamma}\cdot\mathcal{F}^{D}_{X_{4}}=0

(3.37)

where
\gamma\in\mathbb{Z}^{r+1}
is a vector of integers, and

\PF_{\gamma}:=\prod_{\{i|\sum_{a}\gamma_{a}\widetilde{Q}^{a}_{i}>0\}}\left(\cD_{i}\right)_{\sum_{a}\gamma_{a}\widetilde{Q}^{a}_{i}}-\mathrm{e}^{-\sum_{a}\gamma_{a}t^{a}}\prod_{\{i|\sum_{a}\gamma_{a}\widetilde{Q}^{a}_{i}\leq 0\}}\left(\cD_{i}\right)_{-\sum_{a}\gamma_{a}\widetilde{Q}^{a}_{i}}

(3.38)

where
\widetilde{Q}
is the charge matrix
Q
augmented by
q
and by the extension to
\mathbb{C}_{+}\times\mathbb{C}_{-}
as in (
3.2
) and we use the convention that the index
i
ranges from
1
to
r+5
where the last two values are identified with the labels
\pm
.
In particular, we have
\epsilon_{r+4}=\epsilon_{+}
,
\epsilon_{r+5}=\epsilon_{-}
and similarly
\cD_{r+4}=\cD_{+}
,

\cD_{r+5}=\cD_{-}
for the corresponding divisor operators.
We adopt an analogous convention for the Kähler moduli and we identify
t^{r+1}\equiv c
.

Restricting to vectors of the form
\gamma=(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\dots,\gamma_{r},0)

gives operators
\PF_{\gamma}
that formally have the same structure as the Picard–Fuchs operators of
X_{3}
, with the difference that
\cD_{i}
now also include derivative terms
q_{i}\partial_{c}
for the modulus of the symplectic cut.

The same operators annihilate also solutions associated to toric divisors, and intersections thereof, because of the commutation relations

\left[\PF_{\gamma},\tfrac{(1-\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}\cD_{i}})}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\right]=0

(3.39)

with the finite difference/monodromy operators.
This implies that

\PF_{\gamma}\cdot\,\Pi(D_{\pm})=0\,,\hskip 30.0pt\PF_{\gamma}\cdot\,\Pi(D_{+}\cap D_{-})=0\,.

(3.40)

Taking the limit
\epsilon_{\pm}\to 0
of these equations leads to Picard–Fuchs equations involving open string moduli for the equivariant disk potential and for the half-volumes defined by the quantum cut of
X_{3}
, namely

\left.\PF_{\gamma}\right|_{\epsilon_{\pm}=0}\cdot\,\mathcal{F}^{D}_{\lessgtr}(t,c,\epsilon)=0\,,\hskip 30.0pt\left.\PF_{\gamma}\right|_{\epsilon_{\pm}=0}\cdot\,W(t,c,\epsilon)=0\,.

(3.41)

We thus find that half-volumes and the equivariant disk potential obey an
extension
of the Picard–Fuchs equations, where
\cD_{i}
are modified by the addition of
q_{i}\partial_{c}
.
Of course, these equations also admit solutions that are independent of
c
, which correspond to
\mathcal{F}^{D}
and equivariant periods of intersections of divisors of
X_{3}
.
However, when considering more general solutions that do depend on
c
,
we find among these
\mathcal{F}^{D}_{\lessgtr}
and
W
.

Remark 3.2

(Non-equivariant limit)
.

As will be shown in examples below, the equations (
3.41
) become inhomogeneous in the non-equivariant limit.
The same property was encountered in the study of extended Picard–Fuchs equations for open Gromov–Witten invariants on the real quintic
[
Wal07
,
Wal08
,
MW09
]
.
This shows that, at least in the context of toric threefolds (the examples that we consider), the inhomogeneous PF equations for disk potentials become homogeneous after turning on equivariance.

3.4.1
Relation to work of Mayr and Lerche

Mayr and Lerche
[
May02
,
LM01
]
observed that the disk potentials of open topological strings in toric Calabi–Yau threefolds with a toric brane can be derived from the periods of certain CY fourfolds. Liu and Yu
[
LY22
,
LY22a
]
later formalized this observation for more general CY manifolds and orbifolds.
In all explicit cases we examined, the CY fourfold constructed via Braverman’s fibration matches that of Mayr and Lerche.

Our construction of the equivariant disk potential, along with our observation that it corresponds to the
\epsilon_{\pm}\to 0
limit of the equivariant period associated with an intersection of divisors in
X_{4}
, provides an alternative formalization of the Mayr–Lerche observation for general CY3s and embedded toric branes.
In particular, our construction of the fourfold
X_{4}
is well-suited for studying equivariant Picard–Fuchs equations and quantum cohomology rings using the language of
\epsilon
-GLSMs.

Although certain aspects of Liu and Yu’s fourfold construction resemble elements of Braverman’s approach
19
19

19

For instance, the threefold
Y
in
[
LY22
]
, constructed as a partial compactification of
X_{3}
, appears closely related to one of the two half-spaces
X_{3}^{\lessgtr}
. Similarly, the divisor
D
in
Y
seems related to the divisor
X_{2}
in
X_{3}^{\lessgtr}
.
,
it remains unclear whether both methods necessarily lead to the same CY fourfold
X_{4}
.

4
Two branes, double symplectic cuts and CY5

In this section, we consider pairs of toric branes in Calabi–Yau threefolds, and model them by double symplectic cuts. We give a description of double symplectic cuts in terms of a Calabi–Yau fivefold, extending Braverman’s original construction.

4.1
Double cuts from Calabi–Yau fivefolds

Given a toric Calabi–Yau threefold
X_{3}
, one may consider two symplectic cuts defined by charge vectors
q^{1}
and
q^{2}
. As in the case of a single cut, each of
q^{\alpha}
’s defines a family of framed toric branes. The parameter space of the CY3 with branes is the Kähler moduli space with local coordinates
(t^{a},c_{\alpha})
, and consists of several chambers corresponding to the phases of the two branes and of the threefold.
20
20

20

To obtain the toric Lagrangian one needs to complement each
q^{\alpha}
with another charge vector. This is uniquely fixed by a choice of chamber, by taking the unique hyperplane orthogonal to
(1,1,1)
that also contains the corresponding edge of the toric diagram.

Braverman’s construction relating symplectic cuts of toric threefolds to toric fourfolds has a natural generalization to the case of double cuts.
We consider the Calabi–Yau fivefold defined by the symplectic quotient

X_{5}:=\mathbb{C}^{r+3}\times(\mathbb{C}_{+}\times\mathbb{C}_{-})\times(\mathbb{C}^{+}\times\mathbb{C}^{-})\ \sslash\ U(1)^{r+2}

(4.1)

where
U(1)^{r+2}
acts according to the following charge matrix

\left[\begin{array}[]{ccccc|c}z_{i}&z_{+}&z_{-}&z^{+}&z^{-}&\\
\hline\cr Q^{a}_{i}&0&0&0&0&t^{a}\\
q^{1}_{i}&-1&1&0&0&c_{1}\\
q^{2}_{i}&0&0&-1&1&c_{2}\end{array}\right]

(4.2)

Similar to the fourfold case,
X_{5}
admits a fibration over
\mathbb{C}^{2}

\pi:\ X_{5}\to\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}

(4.3)

defined by

\pi:\ (x,z_{+},z_{-},z^{+},z^{-})\mapsto(z_{+}z_{-},z^{+}z^{-})\,\qquad x\in X_{3},\ z_{\pm}\in\mathbb{C}_{\pm},\ z^{\pm}\in\mathbb{C}^{\pm}\,.

(4.4)

The
U(1)^{2}
action defined by
q^{\alpha}_{i}
on the ambient space descends to a
U(1)^{2}
action on
X_{3}\times(\mathbb{C}_{+}\times\mathbb{C}_{-})\times(\mathbb{C}^{+}\times\mathbb{C}^{-})
.
It follows that

X_{5}\simeq X_{3}\times\mathbb{C}_{+}\times\mathbb{C}_{-}\times\mathbb{C}^{+}\times\mathbb{C}^{-}\sslash U(1)^{2}\,.

(4.5)

Next we consider fibers of (
4.3
). If
z_{+}z_{-}=w_{1}
and
z^{+}z^{-}=w_{2}
are both nonzero, the fiber is complex isomorphic to
X_{3}

\pi^{-1}(w_{1},w_{2})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\mu_{q^{1}}(x)=c_{1}+|z_{+}|^{2}-|z_{-}|^{2},\\
\mu_{q^{2}}(x)=c_{2}+|z^{+}|^{2}-|z^{-}|^{2},\\
z_{+}z_{-}=w_{1},\;z^{+}z^{-}=w_{2}\end{array}\right\}\Bigg/U(1)^{2}\simeq X_{3}\,.

(4.6)

Here
\mu_{q^{\alpha}}
denote the moment maps of the
U(1)^{2}
action on
X_{3}
, as it descends from the action defined by
q^{\alpha}
on the ambient manifold
\mathbb{C}^{r+3}\times(\mathbb{C}_{+}\times\mathbb{C}_{-})\times(\mathbb{C}^{+}\times\mathbb{C}^{-})
, cf. (
3.1
).

To see this, we proceed in a way that is analogous to the fourfold case.
First, we observe that the map
\pi
is invariant under the
U(1)^{2}
-action, so that it gives rise to a well-defined map on the quotient
X_{5}
as in (
4.3
).
Moreover, the conditions
z_{+}z_{-}=w_{1}
,
z^{+}z^{-}=w_{2}
and

\mu_{q^{1}}=c_{1}+|z_{+}|^{2}-|w_{1}|^{2}/|z_{+}|^{2}
,
\mu_{q^{2}}=c_{2}+|z^{+}|^{2}-|w_{2}|^{2}/|z^{+}|^{2}
define,
for each
x\in X_{3}
, a torus
T^{2}
with fixed values of, say
|z_{+}|^{2}
and
|z^{+}|^{2}
.
The fact that we get a torus,
i.e.
that
|z_{+}|,|z^{+}|>0
is crucial, and it is ensured by the assumption
w_{1},w_{2}\neq 0
.
The
U(1)^{2}
quotient then reduces this torus to a point, showing that to each point in the base
(w_{1},w_{2})
corresponds a copy of
X_{3}
.

The fiber degenerates when either of
w_{\alpha}
vanishes. Over the locus
w_{1}=0,w_{2}\neq 0
the fiber is complex isomorphic to the symplectic cut defined by
q^{1}
, while over the locus
w_{1}\neq 0,w_{2}=0
is complex isomorphic to the symplectic cut defined by
q^{2}
.
At the origin
w_{1}=w_{2}=0
the fiber is complex isomorphic to the double cut of
X_{3}
.

We focus on the most degenerate case,
i.e.
the fiber over the origin.
The fiber has now four components corresponding to independent choices of signs in

z_{\pm}=0\ \text{with}\ z_{\mp}\in\mathbb{C}_{\mp}\,,\text{ and }z^{\pm}=0\ \text{with}\ z_{\mp}\in\mathbb{C}^{\mp}\,.

(4.7)

Each leads to a reduction of (
4.1
) to a threefold that corresponds to a ‘quarter space’ defined by the double symplectic cut. For example, the reduction of the locus where

z_{-}=z^{-}=0
corresponds to the intersection of two toric divisors, which we denote as
D_{-}
and
D^{-}
. The intersection can be described as

D_{-}\cap D^{-}=\left\{(x,z_{+},z^{+})\in X_{3}\times\mathbb{C}_{+}\times\mathbb{C}^{+}\;\middle|\;\begin{array}[]{l}\mu_{q^{1}}(x)=c_{1}+|z_{+}|^{2}\,,\\
\mu_{q^{2}}(x)=c_{2}+|z^{+}|^{2}\end{array}\right\}\Big/U(1)^{2}\,,

(4.8)

which we will denote as
X_{3}^{>,>}
to indicate that it is obtained as a reduction of the locus where
\mu_{q^{1}}(x)>c_{1}
and
\mu_{q^{2}}(x)>c_{2}
.
The remaining three spaces
X_{3}^{>,<},X_{3}^{<,>},X_{3}^{<,<}
are defined in a similar way by the requirement that
\mu_{q^{1}}(x)\lessgtr c_{1}
and
\mu_{q^{2}}(x)\lessgtr c_{2}
in
X_{3}
(with signs chosen according to the labels).
This presentation makes it clear that all of the
X_{3}^{\lessgtr,\lessgtr}
are obtained as intersections of toric divisors
D_{\pm}
and
D^{\pm}
inside of
X_{5}
.

Of special interest will be the intersection locus
z_{\pm}=z^{\pm}=0
of all four divisors, which defines a complex variety of dimension one

D_{+}\cap D_{-}\cap D^{+}\cap D^{-}=\left\{x\in X_{3}\;\middle|\;\mu_{q^{1}}(x)=c_{1}\,,\;\mu_{q^{2}}(x)=c_{2}\right\}/U(1)^{2}\,.

(4.9)

Note that, by a standard result for symplectic quotients known as “
reduction in stages
”
[
Aud04
]
, this variety can be viewed either as a quotient of the threefold
X_{3}\sslash U(1)^{2}
or equivalently
as the quotient
\mathbb{C}^{r+3}\sslash U(1)^{r+2}
.
We will then define the onefold associated to the double cut as follows

X_{1}:=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{r+3}Q^{a}_{i}|z_{i}|^{2}=t^{a},\ \sum_{i=1}^{r+3}q^{1}_{i}|z_{i}|^{2}=c_{1},\ \sum_{i=1}^{r+3}q^{2}_{i}|z_{i}|^{2}=c_{2}\right\}\Big/U(1)^{r+2}\,.

(4.10)

Here both
q^{\alpha}
have entries that add up to zero because of our assumptions on the type of cuts, which implies that the canonical bundle of
X_{1}
is trivial, so that it is a Calabi–Yau onefold.

4.2
Quantization of the double cut of a CY3

We now turn to the quantization of the double cut of a toric Calabi–Yau threefold.
First, we define the quantum volume of the Calabi–Yau onefold defined in (
4.10
) as the equivariant disk partition function of an
\epsilon
-GLSM

\mathcal{K}^{D}(t,c_{1},c_{2},\epsilon)=\int\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\varphi_{1}}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\varphi_{2}}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int\prod_{a=1}^{r}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\phi_{a}}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\mathrm{e}^{\varphi_{1}c_{1}+\varphi_{2}c_{2}+\sum_{a}\phi_{a}t^{a}}\prod_{i=1}^{r+3}\Ga(\epsilon_{i}+\sum_{a}\phi_{a}Q^{a}_{i}+\varphi_{1}q^{1}_{i}+\varphi_{2}q^{2}_{i})\,.

(4.11)

This integral can be evaluated explicitly, and the result is

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}^{D}(t,c_{1},c_{2},\epsilon)

\displaystyle=\Ga\Big({\sum_{i}}\epsilon_{i}\Big)\,\mathrm{e}^{-t\cdot M\cdot\epsilon}\frac{x_{1}^{A_{1}\cdot\epsilon}x_{2}^{A_{2}\cdot\epsilon}}{H(x_{1},x_{2},z)^{\sum_{i}\epsilon_{i}}}\,,

(4.12)

where
x_{\alpha}=\mathrm{e}^{-c_{\alpha}}
and
z_{a}=\mathrm{e}^{-t^{a}}
while
A_{\alpha}\cdot\epsilon
and
t\cdot M\cdot\epsilon
are certain linear functions of
\epsilon_{i}
(and
t^{a}
).
The function in the denominator is a complex power of
H(x_{1},x_{2},z)
, which is a Laurent polynomial in
x_{\alpha}
and
z_{a}
. It is related to the Hori–Vafa mirror curve of the Calabi–Yau threefold
X_{3}
by a simple change of coordinates.
More details and a derivation of this formula are given in Appendix
B.3
.

Via the function
\mathcal{K}^{D}
, we can further define the four ‘quarter space’ functions associated to
X_{3}^{\lessgtr,\lessgtr}
as defined by the double cut procedure.
They are as follows

\begin{aligned} \mathcal{F}^{D}_{<,<}&=\int_{-\infty}^{c_{1}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}c^{\prime}_{1}\,\int_{-\infty}^{c_{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}c^{\prime}_{2}\,\mathcal{K}^{D}(t,c^{\prime}_{1},c^{\prime}_{2},\epsilon)\,,\\
\mathcal{F}^{D}_{>,<}&=\int_{c_{1}}^{\infty}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}c^{\prime}_{1}\,\int_{-\infty}^{c_{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}c^{\prime}_{2}\,\mathcal{K}^{D}(t,c^{\prime}_{1},c^{\prime}_{2},\epsilon)\,,\end{aligned}\qquad\begin{aligned} \mathcal{F}^{D}_{<,>}&=\int_{-\infty}^{c_{1}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}c^{\prime}_{1}\,\int_{c_{2}}^{\infty}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}c^{\prime}_{2}\,\mathcal{K}^{D}(t,c^{\prime}_{1},c^{\prime}_{2}\epsilon)\,,\\
\mathcal{F}^{D}_{>,>}&=\int_{c_{1}}^{\infty}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}c^{\prime}_{1}\,\int_{c_{2}}^{\infty}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}c^{\prime}_{2}\,\mathcal{K}^{D}(t,c^{\prime}_{1},c^{\prime}_{2},\epsilon)\,.\end{aligned}

(4.13)

For later convenience, we also introduce the double Fourier transform of
\mathcal{K}^{D}

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}^{D}(t,\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2},\epsilon)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}c_{1}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\varphi_{1}c_{1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}c_{2}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\varphi_{2}c_{2}}\,\mathcal{K}^{D}(t,c_{1},c_{2},\epsilon)\qquad(\varphi_{\alpha}\in\mathrm{i}\mathbb{R})

(4.14)

\displaystyle=\int\prod_{a=1}^{r}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\phi_{a}}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\mathrm{e}^{\sum_{a}\phi_{a}t^{a}}\prod_{i=1}^{r+3}\Ga(\epsilon_{i}+\sum_{a}\phi_{a}Q^{a}_{i}+\varphi_{1}q^{1}_{i}+\varphi_{2}q^{2}_{i})

This can be viewed as a deformation of quantum volumes encountered previously, in the following sense

\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}^{D}(t,\varphi_{1},0,\epsilon)=\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{1}^{D}(t,\varphi_{1},\epsilon)\,,\hskip 30.0pt\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}^{D}(t,0,\varphi_{2},\epsilon)=\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{2}^{D}(t,\varphi_{2},\epsilon)\,,

(4.15)

\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}^{D}(t,0,0,\epsilon)=\mathcal{F}^{D}(t,\epsilon)\,.

(4.16)

The latter expression can be viewed as a statement that
\mathcal{K}^{D}
is also a quantum Lebesgue measure for
X_{3}

\mathcal{F}^{D}(t,\epsilon)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}c_{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}c_{2}\,\mathcal{K}^{D}(t,c_{1},c_{2},\epsilon)\,.

(4.17)

In fact, also
\mathcal{H}^{D}
can be expressed in terms of
\mathcal{K}^{D}

\begin{split}\mathcal{H}_{1}^{D}(t,c_{1},\epsilon)&=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}c_{2}\,\mathcal{K}^{D}(t,c_{1},c_{2},\epsilon)\,,\\
\mathcal{H}_{2}^{D}(t,c_{2},\epsilon)&=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}c_{1}\,\mathcal{K}^{D}(t,c_{1},c_{2},\epsilon)\,.\end{split}

(4.18)

In this sense,
\mathcal{K}^{D}
is the most fundamental in the hierarchy of quantum volumes.

Finally, we remark also that the regular term in the non-equivariant expansion of
\mathcal{K}^{D}
can be computed straightforwardly as

\left[\mathcal{K}^{D}(t,c_{1},c_{2},\epsilon)\right]_{0}=P_{\mathcal{K}}(t,c)-\log H(x_{1},x_{2},z)

(4.19)

where

is a polynomial in
t^{a}
and
c_{\alpha}

4.4
Extended Picard–Fuchs equations for double cuts

The equivariant periods associated to divisors of the CY5, and their intersections (
4.22

\epsilon
-GLSM describing
X_{5}
.
Taking the 3d limit gives a two-parameter extension of the Picard–Fuchs equations of the CY3, whose solutions include the ‘quarter volumes’
\mathcal{F}^{D}_{\lessgtr,\lessgtr}
defined in (
4.13

These doubly-extended Picard–Fuchs equations represent a generalization of the extended Picard–Fuchs equations obtained in the previous section for a single symplectic cut, to the case of two cuts.

The logic is similar to the one developed in Section
3.4
.
The quantum volume of
X_{5}

5
Summary and conclusions

In this work, we developed the relation between symplectic cuts and disk potentials first observed in
[
CLZ25
]
in several directions.

Our first main result is a general expression for the quantum Lebesgue measure
\mathcal{H}^{D}
in terms of Lauricella’s hypergeometric function of type
D
, given in (
2.30
).
We then showed that, under certain technical assumptions, the monodromy of its regular part is related to the derivative of the toric brane’s disk potential by formula (
2.32
). This makes contact with expectations from open string mirror symmetry
[
AV00
,
AKV02
]
and shows that the correspondence observed in
[
CLZ25
]
holds in larger generality, including in the context of toric geometries not considered there.
We expect the correspondence to hold for all (CY) symplectic cuts of both smooth and orbifold toric Calabi–Yau threefolds, and that this may be proven either by studying monodromies of Lauricella’s function or by a careful analysis of the
B
-model-like integral representation for
\mathcal{H}^{D}
given in (
2.28
).

Another central point of this work hinges on the description of symplectic cuts in terms of higher-dimensional geometries.
Based on Braverman’s observation that the symplectic cut of a Calabi–Yau threefold
can be described via an associated Calabi–Yau fourfold, we have showed that equivariant periods of the CY4’s divisors and their intersections descend, in a partially non-equivariant limit, to the functions
\mathcal{F}^{D}_{\lessgtr}
associated to the half-spaces of the symplectic cut, and to the equivariant disk potential
W(t,c,\epsilon)
. An interesting consequence of this correspondence is that the equivariant disk potential must obey extended Picard–Fuchs equations. We have verified this in concrete examples, and found that in the non-equivariant limit the extended PF equations become inhomogeneous.

Finally, we have further generalized our construction by considering double symplectic cuts, which correspond to the maximal allowed number of simultaneous CY cuts of a Calabi–Yau threefold. We have shown that their geometry is encoded by a Calabi–Yau fivefold, whose equivariant periods correspond to deformations of functions
\mathcal{F}^{D}_{\lessgtr,\lessgtr}
associated with the four subspaces defined by the double cut.

Open problems

To complete our description of the quantum symplectic cut, it would be desirable to develop an appropriate notion of its quantum cohomology ring.
The Calabi–Yau fourfold description of the cut provides a natural approach to this problem, since the CY4 comes with its own Picard–Fuchs equations, and since the various strata of the cut arise as toric divisors as in the case of
X_{3}^{\lessgtr}
, or as intersections of divisors as in the case of
X_{2}
.
In particular, we have showed that in the limit
\epsilon_{\pm}\to 0
the CY4 Picard–Fuchs operators descend to extended Picard–Fuchs operators for the CY3 which annihilate
\mathcal{F}^{D}_{\gtrless}
and the equivariant disk potential
W(t,c,\epsilon)
.
One may speculate that these may provide a description of the equivariant quantum cohomology ring for
X_{3}^{<}\cup_{X_{2}}X_{3}^{>}
.
It would be interesting to make this expectation more precise. In particular one may ask whether the extended PF equations completely characterize the involved equivariant periods, and what would constitute appropriate initial conditions.
Similarly, one may ask whether the extended Picard–Fuchs equations arising from the CY5 can be regarded as a description of the quantum cohomology ring for the double cut of
X_{3}
.

A related question is how to interpret the symplectic cut procedure from the perspective of local mirror symmetry. In particular, it would be interesting to clarify the role of the CY fourfold
X_{4}
on the mirror-dual side.
We expect that manipulations of equivariant
B
-model integrals, such as those discussed in Appendix
B
, may provide insight into this direction.

Another question raised in this work is whether double cuts encode more than just the disk potentials of two toric branes—specifically, whether they also capture the annulus potential. A natural candidate for this additional information is the CY1 quantum volume
\mathcal{K}^{D}(t,c_{1},c_{2},\epsilon)
, which depends on the open string moduli of both Lagrangians. It would be interesting to investigate whether its analytic structure encodes annuli stretching between branes.
A hint that this might be the case comes from its relation to the mirror curve, as given in (
1.10
).
A direct approach might be to study the GLSM on a circle with suitable boundary conditions at the two spatial boundaries, suggesting that candidates for the annulus potential might be the Witten index, or certain generalizations thereof
[
Cec+92
]
.
21
21

21

We thank the anonymous referee for suggesting a possible role of the Witten index in relation to annulus potentials.

It would be very interesting to explore this question further, but we leave it to future work.

\mathcal{K}^{D}(\widetilde{t},\epsilon)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\,\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u_{j}}{u_{j}^{1-\epsilon_{j}}}\left(\prod_{a=1}^{r+2}(\widetilde{z}_{a}^{-1}u_{j}^{\widetilde{Q}^{a}_{j}})^{-\sum_{i\neq j}\left[\widetilde{Q}(j)^{-1}\right]^{i}_{a}\epsilon_{i}}\right)\mathrm{e}^{-u_{j}-\sum_{i\neq j}\prod_{a=1}^{r+2}(\widetilde{z}_{a}^{-1}u_{j}^{\widetilde{Q}^{a}_{j}})^{-\left[\widetilde{Q}(j)^{-1}\right]^{i}_{a}}}\\
=\mathrm{e}^{-\sum_{a=1}^{r+2}\sum_{i\neq j}\widetilde{t}^{a}\left[\widetilde{Q}(j)^{-1}\right]^{i}_{a}\epsilon_{i}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u_{j}}{u_{j}}\,u_{j}^{\epsilon_{j}-\sum_{a=1}^{r+2}\sum_{i\neq j}\widetilde{Q}^{a}_{j}\left[\widetilde{Q}(j)^{-1}\right]^{i}_{a}\epsilon_{i}}\mathrm{e}^{-u_{j}-\sum_{i\neq j}\prod_{a=1}^{r+2}(\widetilde{z}_{a}^{-1}u_{j}^{\widetilde{Q}^{a}_{j}})^{-\left[\widetilde{Q}(j)^{-1}\right]^{i}_{a}}}\,.

(B.14)

To proceed we note two properties of the matrix
R^{i}_{k}:=\sum_{a=1}^{r+2}\left[\widetilde{Q}(j)^{-1}\right]^{i}_{a}\widetilde{Q}_{k}^{a}
:

•

[R(j)]^{i}_{k}=\delta^{i}_{k}
, where
R(j)
is the square matrix obtained from
R
by removing the
j
-th column. This trivially follows by noting that
R(j)=\widetilde{Q}(j)^{-1}\widetilde{Q}(j)
.

•

R^{i}_{j}=-1
for every
i=1,\dots,r+2
. This follows from the CY condition on the matrix of charges
\widetilde{Q}
, namely we have
\widetilde{Q}_{j}^{a}=-\sum_{k\neq j}\widetilde{Q}_{k}^{a}
and by the previous point, we deduce

R^{i}_{j}=\sum_{a=1}^{r+2}\left[\widetilde{Q}(j)^{-1}\right]^{i}_{a}\widetilde{Q}_{j}^{a}=-\sum_{k\neq j}\sum_{a=1}^{r+2}\left[\widetilde{Q}(j)^{-1}\right]^{i}_{a}\widetilde{Q}_{k}^{a}=-\sum_{k\neq j}\delta^{i}_{k}=-1

(B.15)

Using these facts, the above expression simplifies to

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}^{D}(\widetilde{t},\epsilon)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{-\sum_{a=1}^{r+2}\sum_{i\neq j}\widetilde{t}^{a}\left[\widetilde{Q}(j)^{-1}\right]^{i}_{a}\epsilon_{i}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u_{j}}{u_{j}}\,u_{j}^{\sum_{i=1}^{r+3}\epsilon_{i}}\,\exp\left(-u_{j}\left(1+\sum_{i\neq j}\prod_{a=1}^{r+2}\widetilde{z}_{a}^{\left[\widetilde{Q}(j)^{-1}\right]^{i}_{a}}\right)\right)

(B.16)

Let us define

H(x_{1},x_{2},z):=1+\sum_{i\neq j}\prod_{a=1}^{r+2}\widetilde{z}_{a}^{\left[\widetilde{Q}(j)^{-1}\right]^{i}_{a}}

(B.17)

and

A_{\alpha}^{i}:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\left[\widetilde{Q}(j)^{-1}\right]^{i}_{r+\alpha}&\text{if $i\neq j$}\\
0&\text{if $i=j$}\end{array}\right.\,,\hskip 30.0ptM_{a}^{i}:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\left[\widetilde{Q}(j)^{-1}\right]^{i}_{a}&\text{if $i\neq j$, $a=1,\dots,r$}\\
0&\text{if $i=j$}\end{array}\right.

(B.18)

where
x_{\alpha}=\mathrm{e}^{-c_{\alpha}}=\widetilde{z}_{r+\alpha}
for
\alpha=1,2
, and
z^{a}=\mathrm{e}^{-t^{a}}=\widetilde{z}^{a}
for
a=1,\dots,r
.
Note that these definitions depend on the choice of
j
made at the beginning.
This leads us to the final expression for
\mathcal{K}^{D}

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}^{D}(t,c_{1},c_{2},\epsilon)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{-t\cdot M\cdot\epsilon}x_{1}^{A_{1}\cdot\epsilon}x_{2}^{A_{2}\cdot\epsilon}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u_{j}}{u_{j}}\,u_{j}^{\sum_{i=1}^{r+3}\epsilon_{i}}\mathrm{e}^{-u_{j}H(x_{1},x_{2},z)}

(B.19)

\displaystyle=\Ga\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{r+3}\epsilon_{i}\Big)\mathrm{e}^{-t\cdot M\cdot\epsilon}\frac{x_{1}^{A_{1}\cdot\epsilon}x_{2}^{A_{2}\cdot\epsilon}}{H(x_{1},x_{2},z)^{\sum_{i=1}^{r+3}\epsilon_{i}}}

where we used the shorthands
A_{\alpha}\cdot\epsilon=\sum_{i=1}^{r+3}A_{\alpha}^{i}\epsilon_{i}
and

t\cdot M\cdot\epsilon=\sum_{i=1}^{r+3}\sum_{a=1}^{r+2}t^{a}M_{a}^{i}\epsilon_{i}
.

B.4
H(x_{1},x_{2})
vs the Hori–Vafa mirror curve

We recall the standard definition of the Hori–Vafa mirror curve of a toric CY threefold. Let
X_{3}
be a toric quotient as in (
2.1
), then the mirror geometry can be described in terms of
r+3
complex coordinates
Y^{i}\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
subject to the constraints

\sum_{i=1}^{r+3}Q_{i}^{a}Y^{i}=-t^{a}\,,\hskip 30.0pta=1,\dots,r\,,

(B.27)

whose solutions form a three-dimensional family (see
[
KKV97
,
HV00
]
). Because of the CY condition on
Q_{i}^{a}
, the space of solution is invariant under a simultaneous translation of all the coordinates as

Y^{i}\mapsto Y^{i}+s

(B.28)

therefore we can quotient by this action to reduce the space of solutions to a two-dimensional family parametrized by two coordinates
x,y\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
. Solving explicitly the constraints w.r.t. the
Y^{i}\cong Y^{i}(x,y)
as functions of
x
and
y
, we can define the mirror CY manifold as the complex hypersurface

UV=\sum_{i=1}^{r+3}\mathrm{e}^{Y^{i}(x,y)}=:F(x,y)\,,

(B.29)

where
U,V\in\mathbb{C}
are auxiliary complex variables.
Because
F(x,y)
depends on an explicit choice of coordinates for the two-dimensional family of solutions, we have an ambiguity in defining this hypersurface, related to the underlying freedom in the way we parametrize the variables
Y^{i}
as functions of
x
and
y
.
This ambiguity is of the same nature as the one observed in solving the Dirac-delta constraints in (
B.11
). In fact, we can formally identify the variables
Y^{i}=\log u_{i}
, where
u_{i}
are the integration variables in a
B
-model integral representation of the quantum volume of
X_{3}
,

\mathcal{F}^{D}(t,\epsilon)=\prod_{i=1}^{r+3}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u_{i}}{u_{i}}\,u_{i}^{\epsilon_{i}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-u_{i}}\,\prod_{a=1}^{r}\delta\Big(t^{a}+\sum_{i}Q^{a}_{i}\log u_{i}\Big)\,,

(B.30)

so that the constraints in (
B.27
) match with the moment map equations defining
X_{3}
.

Similarly, a Lagrangian toric brane
L
in
X_{3}
is mapped under mirror symmetry
[
Hor00
,
AKV02
]
to a mirror brane given by the equation

U=0=F(x,y)\,,

(B.31)

which defines a holomorphic submanifold of the hypersurface (
B.29
). Its moduli space is complex one-dimensional and it corresponds to the curve

F(x,y)=0

(B.32)

which is known as the
mirror curve
.
In general, the open string modulus
c
of the brane can be identified with a function of the two coordinates
x,y
, so that both
x
and
y
are completely fixed by these two equations. In practice, the mirror curve is defined by solving the moment map constraints in such a way that
x
can be directly identified with
\mathrm{e}^{-c}
, while
y=y(x)
becomes implicitly defined as a solution to
F(x,y)=0
. The specific data associated to the location and orientation of the toric brane
L
are then encoded by the explicit form of the function
F(x,y)
.

The polynomial
H(x_{1},x_{2})
appearing in the formula for
\mathcal{K}^{D}
is related to, but does not quite coincide with, the Hori–Vafa mirror curve
F(x,y)
.
More precisely, since
\mathcal{K}^{D}
is defined by a double cut, which corresponds a pair of toric Lagrangians, there are
two
mirror curves that describe the moduli spaces of branes
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
associated to the cut.

First of all, the branes may come with independent framings and different choices of coordinates. Therefore their moduli spaces would be given by different expressions
F_{1}(x_{1},y_{1})=0
and
F_{2}(x_{2},y_{2})=0
, with
x_{\alpha}=\mathrm{e}^{-c_{\alpha}}
,
\alpha=1,2
. Neither of these functions is equal to
H(x_{1},x_{2})
on general grounds.
However, all three polynomials are related, since they all come from resolving the moment map constraints, on the universal curve
\sum_{i=1}^{r+3}\mathrm{e}^{Y^{i}}=0
. We illustrate this point with an explicit computation.

An example.

For illustration, consider the double cut of
\mathbb{C}^{3}
in Section
4.5.1
.
The symplectic cuts are specified by the charge matrix (
4.31
), whose restriction to the three coordinates of
\mathbb{C}^{3}
is

\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc|c}z_{1}&z_{2}&z_{3}&\\
\hline\cr 0&1&-1&c_{1}\\
1&-1&0&c_{2}\\
\end{array}\right]

(B.33)

Let us work in the phase
c_{1},c_{2}>0
. This means that the first Lagrangian ends on the first leg of the toric diagram, at
(p_{1},p_{2},p_{3})=(c_{1},0,0)
where
p_{i}:=|z_{i}|^{2}
, and the second Lagrangian ends on the second leg at
(p_{1},p_{2},p_{3})=(0,c_{2},0)
.
Following
[
AV00
]
, we introduce a complexification of the
p_{i}
coordinates

u_{i}=\exp(-p_{i}+\mathrm{i}\theta_{i})\,,\hskip 30.0pti=1,2,3

(B.34)

and use the moment map equations

p_{2}-p_{3}=c_{1}\,,\hskip 30.0ptp_{1}-p_{2}=c_{2}\,,

(B.35)

to identify the open string moduli as follows

x_{1}:=\mathrm{e}^{-c_{1}}=\frac{u_{2}}{u_{3}}\,,\hskip 30.0ptx_{2}:=\mathrm{e}^{-c_{2}}=\frac{u_{1}}{u_{2}}\,.

(B.36)

The phase determines the homogeneous hyperplanes for both toric Lagrangians: we have respectively
p_{1}-p_{3}=0
for brane 1 and
p_{3}-p_{2}=0
for brane 2.
Therefore we identify dual variables
y_{\alpha}
with

y_{1}=\frac{u_{1}}{u_{3}}\,,\hskip 30.0pty_{2}=\frac{u_{3}}{u_{2}}\,.

(B.37)

The mirror curves for each brane are obtained by Hori–Vafa as follows

\begin{split}u_{1}+u_{2}+u_{3}&=u_{3}(1+y_{1}+x_{1})=:u_{3}F_{1}(x_{1},y_{1})\\
&=u_{2}(1+y_{2}+x_{2})=:u_{2}F_{2}(x_{2},y_{2})\\
\end{split}

(B.38)

In this case, both branes are taken in the same framing, and for this reason the polynomials
F_{1}(x,y)
and
F_{2}(x,y)
coincide as functions of two variables
(x,y)
, however more generally this is not the case.

Notice that the relation to ambient coordinates
u_{i}
implies the relation

x_{2}=y_{1}x_{1}^{-1}\,,\hskip 30.0pty_{2}=x_{1}^{-1}

(B.39)

which corresponds to the action of
TS\in SL(2,\mathbb{Z})
on
\mathbb{C}^{\times}\times\mathbb{C}^{\times}
. This is the cubic root of the identity which permutes toric Lagrangians on the three toric legs of
\mathbb{C}^{3}
, and therefore naturally relates the two branes (when they are in the same framing, as in this case.)

The curves should be contrasted with the formula for
\mathcal{K}^{D}
given in (
4.33
).
We rederive that result here by hand: if we single out column 3 in the matrix
\widetilde{Q}^{a}_{i}
, it means we express
u_{1},u_{2}
in terms of
u_{3}
by using the moment map constraints

\begin{split}&p_{2}-p_{3}=-\log u_{2}+\log u_{3}=c_{1}\quad\Rightarrow\quad u_{2}=u_{3}\,\mathrm{e}^{-c_{1}}=u_{3}x_{2}\\
&p_{1}-p_{2}=-\log u_{1}+\log u_{2}=c_{2}\quad\Rightarrow\quad u_{1}=u_{2}\,\mathrm{e}^{-c_{2}}=u_{3}x_{1}x_{2}\,.\end{split}

(B.40)

The Hori–Vafa universal curve can be written as

u_{1}+u_{2}+u_{3}=u_{3}(x_{1}x_{2}+x_{2}+1)=:u_{3}H(x_{1},x_{2})

(B.41)

where
H(x_{1},x_{2})
coincides with the polynomial in the denominator of
\mathcal{K}^{D}
as in (
4.33
).

Since
H(x_{1},x_{2})
comes from the universal curve
u_{1}+u_{2}+u_{3}
, it should coincide with
F_{1},F_{2}
.
This is indeed the case, in fact, using the relations between
y_{\alpha},x_{\alpha}
given above by the ambient variables
u_{i}
, we find that

H(x_{1},x_{2})=F_{1}(x_{1},y_{1})=y_{2}^{-1}F_{2}(x_{2},y_{2})\,.

(B.42)

Therefore, we learn that we might have written
\mathcal{K}^{D}
in terms of either
F_{1}
or
F_{2}
.
It is clear that this kind of simple relation holds more generally for all toric geometries,
because all three polynomials are just rewritings of the universal curve
\sum_{i=1}^{r+3}u_{i}=0
.

-model integrals for

From (

Appendix C
Monodromy of
F_{D}^{(n)}

In this appendix we compute the regular (
i.e.
order
O(\epsilon^{0})
) term in the monodromy of
\mathcal{H}^{D}
. Schematically, if

\mathcal{H}^{D}(t,c,\xi\epsilon)=[\mathcal{H}^{D}]_{-2}\,\xi^{-2}+[\mathcal{H}^{D}]_{-1}\,\xi^{-1}+[\mathcal{H}^{D}]_{0}\,\xi^{0}+O(\xi)

(C.1)

represent different terms in the
\epsilon
-expansion of
\mathcal{H}^{D}
, the monodromy can be computed term by term

\Delta_{c}\,\mathcal{H}^{D}(t,x,\xi\epsilon)=\sum_{d=-2}^{\infty}\Delta_{c}\,[\mathcal{H}^{D}(t,c,\epsilon)]_{d}\,\xi^{d}\,.

(C.2)

Here
\Delta_{c}
is the monodromy operator defined as in (
2.21
).
In particular, we will be interested in the regular terms
[\mathcal{H}^{D}]_{0}
and
\Delta_{c}\,[\mathcal{H}^{D}]_{0}
.

C.1
Non-equivariant expansion of the quantum Lebesgue measure

It follows from (
2.30
) that
\mathcal{H}^{D}
is the product of three distinct pieces,
each with a different leading order behavior

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{D}(t,c,\epsilon)=

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-t\cdot M\cdot\epsilon}\,x^{A_{1}\cdot\epsilon}}{H(x,0,z)^{\sum_{i}\epsilon_{i}}}\times\frac{\Ga(\sum_{i}\epsilon_{i})\Ga(A_{2}\cdot\epsilon)\Ga(k\sum_{i}\epsilon_{i}-A_{2}\cdot\epsilon)}{\Ga(k\sum_{i}\epsilon_{i})}

(C.3)

\displaystyle\times F^{(k)}_{D}\Big(A_{2}\cdot\epsilon,\sum_{i}\epsilon_{i},\dots,\sum_{i}\epsilon_{i},k\sum_{i}\epsilon_{i};1+y_{1}^{-1},\dots,1+y_{k}^{-1}\Big)\,.

The first factor has the following expansion

\left[\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-t\cdot M\cdot\epsilon}\,x^{A_{1}\cdot\epsilon}}{H(x,0,z)^{\sum_{i}\epsilon_{i}}}\right]_{n}=\frac{(-1)^{n}}{n!}\Big(t\cdot M\cdot\epsilon+c\,A_{1}\cdot\epsilon+\log H(x,0,z)\sum_{i}\epsilon_{i}\Big)^{n}

(C.4)

where we recall that
H(x,0,z)=\prod_{j=1}^{k}(-y_{j}(x,z))
.
Similarly, the ratio of
\Ga
functions can be expanded as

\frac{\Ga(\sum_{i}\epsilon_{i})\Ga(A_{2}\cdot\epsilon)\Ga(k\sum_{i}\epsilon_{i}-A_{2}\cdot\epsilon)}{\Ga(k\sum_{i}\epsilon_{i})}=\underbrace{\frac{k}{A_{2}\cdot\epsilon\left(k\sum_{i}\epsilon_{i}-A_{2}\cdot\epsilon\right)}}_{O(\epsilon^{-2})}\;\underbrace{-\frac{\gamma k\sum_{i}\epsilon_{i}}{A_{2}\cdot\epsilon\left(k\sum_{i}\epsilon_{i}-A_{2}\cdot\epsilon\right)}}_{O(\epsilon^{-1})}\\
+\underbrace{\frac{k}{12}\left(\frac{(6\gamma^{2}+\pi^{2})\left(\sum_{i}\epsilon_{i}\right)^{2}}{A_{2}\cdot\epsilon\left(k\sum_{i}\epsilon_{i}-A_{2}\cdot\epsilon\right)}-2\pi^{2}\right)}_{O(\epsilon^{0})}+O(\epsilon)\,.

(C.5)

The non-equivariant expansion of
F_{D}^{(k)}
is more involved and relies on a choice of power series representation.
In the following, we will use the power series representation from (
A.1
) and expand to second order in the parameters
a,b_{i},c
.
It is important to observe that use of this series expansion implies that the following results hold only when all the arguments
x_{i}
are strictly contained within the unit disk.

First, observe that the Pochhammer symbols appearing in the coefficients can be expanded as

(x)_{n}=x(x+1)\cdots(x+i-1)=x\Ga(n)\left(1+xH_{n-1}+O(x^{2})\right)\,,\hskip 30.0ptn\geq 1

(C.6)

where

H_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{i}

(C.7)

is the
n
-th harmonic number.
Clearly, we have

\left[F^{(k)}_{D}\right]_{0}=1\,.

(C.8)

At order one in the parameters, the only contribution comes from the terms of the sum (
A.1
) for which only one of the indices
(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})
is not zero, namely

\left[F^{(k)}_{D}\right]_{1}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sum_{i_{j}=1}^{\infty}\left[\frac{(a)_{i_{j}}(b_{j})_{i_{j}}}{(c)_{i_{j}}\,i_{j}!}\right]_{1}x_{j}^{i_{j}}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{ab_{j}}{c}\sum_{i_{j}=1}^{\infty}\frac{x_{j}^{i_{j}}}{i_{j}}=-\frac{a}{c}\sum_{j=1}^{k}b_{j}\log(1-x_{j})\,.

(C.9)

At order two, there are two contributions: one coming from terms where one of the indices
(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})
is not zero and one coming from terms where two indices are not zero, namely

\displaystyle\left[F^{(k)}_{D}\right]_{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sum_{i_{j}=1}^{\infty}\left[\frac{(a)_{i_{j}}(b_{j})_{i_{j}}}{(c)_{i_{j}}\,{i_{j}}!}\right]_{2}x_{j}^{i_{j}}+\sum_{1\leq j_{1}<j_{2}\leq k}\sum_{i_{j_{1}},i_{j_{2}}=1}^{\infty}\left[\frac{(a)_{i_{j_{1}}+i_{j_{2}}}(b_{j_{1}})_{i_{j_{1}}}(b_{j_{2}})_{i_{j_{2}}}}{(c)_{i_{j_{1}}+i_{j_{2}}}\,i_{j_{1}}!i_{j_{2}}!}\right]_{2}x_{j_{1}}^{i_{j_{1}}}x_{j_{2}}^{i_{j_{2}}}

(C.10)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{ab_{j}(a+b_{j}-c)}{c}\sum_{i_{j}=1}^{\infty}\frac{x_{j}^{i_{j}}}{i_{j}}H_{{i_{j}}-1}+\sum_{1\leq j_{1}<j_{2}\leq k}\frac{ab_{j_{1}}b_{j_{2}}}{c}\sum_{i_{j_{1}}=1}^{\infty}\frac{x_{j_{1}}^{i_{j_{1}}}}{i_{j_{1}}}\sum_{i_{j_{2}}=1}^{\infty}\frac{x_{j_{2}}^{i_{j_{2}}}}{i_{j_{2}}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{ab_{j}(a+b_{j}-c)}{c}\frac{1}{2}\log^{2}(1-x_{j})+\sum_{1\leq j_{1}<j_{2}\leq k}\frac{ab_{j_{1}}b_{j_{2}}}{c}\log(1-x_{j_{1}})\log(1-x_{j_{2}})

\displaystyle=\frac{a}{2c}\left((a-c)\sum_{j=1}^{k}b_{j}\log^{2}(1-x_{j})+\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{k}b_{j}\log(1-x_{j})\Big)^{2}\right)\,.

Putting all of this together and specializing the parameters and arguments of the Lauricella as

a=A_{2}\cdot\epsilon\,,\hskip 30.0ptb_{j}=\sum_{i}\epsilon_{i}\,,\hskip 30.0ptc=k\sum_{i}\epsilon_{i}\,,\hskip 30.0ptx_{j}=1+y_{j}^{-1}\,,

(C.11)

we finally obtain the non-equivariant expansion of
\mathcal{H}^{D}
up to order zero in
\epsilon
as desired.

C.2
Monodromy of the quantum Lebesgue measure

We now compute the monodromy of the regular part of
\mathcal{H}^{D}
. In order to do so, we make use of the following observation: if we assume that the roots
y_{j}(x)
behave as

y_{j}(x)\sim x^{\alpha_{j}}

(C.12)

for
x\to 0
, then we can deduce that the monodromy of each
y_{j}(x)
as a multivalued function of
x
is the same as that of
x^{\alpha_{j}}
. Therefore we obtain

y_{j}(\mathrm{e}^{-(c\pm\pi\mathrm{i})})=\mathrm{e}^{\mp\pi\mathrm{i}\alpha_{j}}y_{j}(-\mathrm{e}^{-c})\,.

(C.13)

Applying this result systematically to every term in the expansion of
\mathcal{H}^{D}
,
we find that the only regular instantonic contributions to the monodromy come from terms of the form

\Delta_{c}\left(\left[\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-t\cdot M\cdot\epsilon}\,x^{A_{1}\cdot\epsilon}}{H(x,0,z)^{\sum_{i}\epsilon_{i}}}\right]_{n_{1}}\times\left[\frac{\Ga(\sum_{i}\epsilon_{i})\Ga(A_{2}\cdot\epsilon)\Ga(k\sum_{i}\epsilon_{i}-A_{2}\cdot\epsilon)}{\Ga(k\sum_{i}\epsilon_{i})}\right]_{-2}\times\left[F^{(k)}_{D}\right]_{n_{2}}\right)

(C.14)

where
n_{1},n_{2}
are positive integers such that
n_{1}+n_{2}=2
, and the middle term in the product is just a constant on which
\Delta_{c}
acts trivially.

From an explicit computation of the monodromy of these three terms, we find

\Delta_{c}\left(\left[\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-t\cdot M\cdot\epsilon}\,x^{A_{1}\cdot\epsilon}}{H(x,0,z)^{\sum_{i}\epsilon_{i}}}\right]_{2}\times\left[F^{(k)}_{D}\right]_{0}\right)=\sum_{i}\epsilon_{i}\Big(A_{1}\cdot\epsilon-\sum_{i}\epsilon_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\alpha_{j}\Big)\sum_{j=1}^{k}\log(-y_{j}(-x))+\dots

(C.15)

\Delta_{c}\left(\left[\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-t\cdot M\cdot\epsilon}\,x^{A_{1}\cdot\epsilon}}{H(x,0,z)^{\sum_{i}\epsilon_{i}}}\right]_{1}\times\left[F^{(k)}_{D}\right]_{1}\right)=-\frac{A_{2}\cdot\epsilon}{k}\Big(A_{1}\cdot\epsilon-2\sum_{i}\epsilon_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\alpha_{j}\Big)\sum_{j=1}^{k}\log(-y_{j}(-x))+\dots

(C.16)

\Delta_{c}\left(\left[\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-t\cdot M\cdot\epsilon}\,x^{A_{1}\cdot\epsilon}}{H(x,0,z)^{\sum_{i}\epsilon_{i}}}\right]_{0}\times\left[F^{(k)}_{D}\right]_{2}\right)=\frac{A_{2}\cdot\epsilon}{k}\Big(k\sum_{i}\epsilon_{i}-A_{2}\cdot\epsilon\Big)\sum_{j=1}^{k}\alpha_{j}\log(-y_{j}(-x))\\
-\frac{A_{2}\cdot\epsilon}{k}\sum_{i}\epsilon_{i}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\alpha_{j}\Big)\sum_{j=1}^{k}\log(-y_{j}(-x))+\dots{}

(C.17)

Summing them all together and multiplying by the constant, we finally conclude that

\Delta_{c}\left[\mathcal{H}^{D}\right]_{0}=\frac{1}{A_{2}\cdot\epsilon}\left(A_{1}\cdot\epsilon-\sum_{i}\epsilon_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\alpha_{j}\right)\sum_{j=1}^{k}\log(-y_{j}(-x))+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\alpha_{j}\log(-y_{j}(-x))+\dots

(C.18)

where, as usual, we omit dependence on
z
in the roots and we neglect all terms which are polynomial in
t^{a}
and
c
.

We should remark here that derivation of the formula for the monodromy of
\mathcal{H}^{D}
makes use of the explicit series expansion (
A.1
) for the Lauricella function
F_{D}^{(n)}
in (
2.30
), whose domain of convergence is the product of unit disks

|1+y_{j}^{-1}|<1\qquad j=1,\dots,k\,.

(C.19)

In particular, this condition must hold for the asymptotic values of the roots
y_{j}(x)
within some region of the large volume regime, as in (
2.31
).

Whenever condition (
C.19
) is not satisfied, we find ourselves in a region of parameters space where the series expansion (
A.1
) is no longer valid. In this case, we need to compute the series expansion of
F_{D}^{(n)}
in the new region by means of analytic continuation formulas, see
e.g.

[
Bez18
]
and references therein.
Once an appropriate series expansion has been found, we can then repeat the steps in this Appendix to compute the correct formula for the monodromy compatible with the values of the parameters.
In
[
CLZ25
]
, we address one example when this issue arises in the case of local
\mathbb{P}^{2}
, where the equivariant disk potential is computed in a phase in which (
C.19
) is violated. In that instance, the Lauricella function specializes to a simpler hypergeometric series, whose analytic continuations are well-known.
