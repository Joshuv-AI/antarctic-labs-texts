Strong and weak solutions to a structural acoustic model with a C 1 C^{1} source term on the plate
Andrew R. Becklin, Yanqiu Guo

Abstract
. In this manuscript, we consider a structural acoustic model consisting of a wave equation defined in a bounded domain Ω ⊂ ℝ 3 \Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3} , strongly coupled with a Berger plate equation acting on the flat portion of the boundary of Ω \Omega . The system is influenced by an arbitrary C 1 C^{1} nonlinear source term in the plate equation. Using nonlinear semigroup theory and monotone operator theory, we establish the well-posedness of both local strong and weak solutions, along with conditions for global existence. With additional assumptions on the source term, we examine the Nehari manifold and establish the global existence of potential well solutions. Our primary objective is to characterize regimes in which the system remains globally well-posed despite arbitrary growth of the source term and the absence of damping mechanisms to stabilize the dynamics.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
The Model

In this paper, we study a structural acoustic model influenced by a nonlinear source term
h(w)
on the plate. Precisely, we study the coupled system of PDEs:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}u_{tt}-\Delta u=0&\text{ in }\Omega\times(0,T),\\[2.84526pt]
w_{tt}+\Delta^{2}w+u_{t}|_{\Gamma}=h(w)&\text{ in }\Gamma\times(0,T),\\[2.84526pt]
u=0&\text{ on }\Gamma_{0}\times(0,T),\\[2.84526pt]
\partial_{\nu}u=w_{t}&\text{ on }\Gamma\times(0,T),\\[2.84526pt]
w=\partial_{\nu_{\Gamma}}w=0&\text{ on }\partial\Gamma\times(0,T),\\[2.84526pt]
(u(0),u_{t}(0))=(u_{0},u_{1}),\hskip 14.22636pt(w(0),w_{t}(0))=(w_{0},w_{1}),\end{cases}

(1.1)

where the initial data reside in the finite energy space, i.e.,

(u_{0},u_{1})\in H^{1}_{\Gamma_{0}}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)\,\text{ and }(w_{0},w_{1})\in H^{2}_{0}(\Gamma)\times L^{2}(\Gamma).

In this model,
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
is a bounded, open, connected domain with smooth boundary
\partial\Omega=\overline{\Gamma_{0}\cup\Gamma}
, where
\Gamma_{0}
and
\Gamma
are two disjoint, open, connected sets of positive Lebesgue measure. The set
\Gamma
is the
flat
portion of the boundary of
\Omega
and is referred to as the elastic wall, while
\Gamma_{0}
represents the rigid wall. The nonlinearity
h(w)
represents a source term acting on the plate equation; it is allowed to have a “bad” sign and may therefore cause instability. The vectors
\nu
and
\nu_{\Gamma}
denote the outer normals to
\Gamma
and
\partial\Gamma
, respectively.

Models such as (
1.1
) arise in the context of modeling gas pressure in an
acoustic chamber
\Omega
which is surrounded by a combination of rigid and
flexible walls. The pressure in the chamber is described by the solution
to a wave equation, while vibrations of the flexible wall are described
by the solution to a Berger plate equation. We refer the reader to
[
13
]
and the references quoted therein for more details on the Berger model. The coupling of the wave and plate equations occurs through the term
u_{t}|_{\Gamma}
. This acoustic pressure term can often create unique challenges outside of those generally faced in the study of nonlinear hyperbolic equations. We refer the reader to work by Beale
[
8
]
for a derivation of this term.

The necessity to examine models containing nonlinearities lies in their ability to allow for refinements on weaker responses from equilibrium. More generally, they enable one to capture coupling of the model with other types of dynamics. Oftentimes the implementation of nonlinear source terms comes with some amount of damping terms in order to control the behavior of solutions, although none are present in (
1.1
). In light of the significance of studying nonlinear source terms, we are primarily interested in treating
h
in great generality, requiring only that it be a
C^{1}
function in this paper.

1.2.
Literature Overview

Structural acoustic interaction models have a rich history. These models are well known in both the physical and mathematical literature and date to the canonical models considered in
[
8
,
19
]
. In the context of stabilization and controllability of structural acoustic models, there is a large body of literature, and we refer the reader to the monograph by Lasiecka
[
22
]
which provides a comprehensive overview and quotes many works on these topics. Other related contributions worthy of mention include
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
11
,
16
,
17
,
21
]
.

Previous work by Becklin and Rammaha
[
9
]
studied a model similar to (
1.1
), where nonlinear source and damping terms appeared in both the wave and plate equations, and they established well-posedness criteria. Feng, Guo, and Rammaha
[
14
,
15
]
built on Becklin and Rammaha
[
9
]
to establish potential well solutions, energy decay rates, and blow-up of solutions under various scenarios.

We utilize monotone operator theory and nonlinear semigroups to establish local well-posedness of strong and weak solutions to system (
1.1
), in particular implementing Kato’s Theorem as in
[
7
,
31
]
. The novelty of this manuscript lies in the limited assumptions imposed on the term
h
, as well as the absence of any damping term on the plate.
We establish the local well-posedness of strong and weak solutions for (
1.1
) under the sole assumption that
h
is a
C^{1}
function.
Moreover, we show that if
|h(s)|\leq c(|s|+1)
, then both strong and weak solutions exist globally.

This manuscript also considers potential well solutions of system (
1.1
).
The study of such solutions for nonlinear hyperbolic equations has a long history.
For example, in their classical work
[
27
]
, Payne and Sattinger studied potential well solutions of a nonlinear hyperbolic equation in the canonical form

\displaystyle u_{tt}=\Delta u+f(u),\;\;\text{with}\;\;u(0)=u_{0},\;\;u_{t}(0)=u_{1},

(1.2)

subject to the boundary condition
u=0
on the boundary of a bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
In the literature, power-type nonlinear terms are usually considered when applying potential well theory to hyperbolic PDEs.
Previous work involving non-power source terms was carried out by Alves and Cavalcanti
[
1
]
,
who studied an exponential source in a two-dimensional wave equation and established potential well solutions,
building on the work of Ambrosetti and Rabinowitz
[
2
]
. See also
[
25
,
26
]
for studies of nonlinear hyperbolic equations with exponential nonlinearities.
In this manuscript, by taking advantage of the additional regularity of solutions provided by the plate,
we consider a more general
C^{1}
source term
h(w)
satisfying Assumption
2.7
,
and we establish the global existence of potential well solutions in both the weak and strong sense.

Preliminaries and Main Results

2.1.
Notation

Throughout the paper, the following notational conventions for
L^{p}
space norms and standard inner products will be used:

\displaystyle||u||_{p}=||u||_{L^{p}(\Omega)},

\displaystyle(u,v)_{\Omega}=(u,v)_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

\displaystyle|u|_{p}=||u||_{L^{p}(\Gamma)},

\displaystyle(u,v)_{\Gamma}=(u,v)_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}.

We use both
\gamma u
and
u_{|_{\Gamma}}
to denote the
trace
of
u
on
\Gamma
. We write
\frac{d}{dt}(\gamma u(t))
as
\gamma u_{t}
. As is customary,
C
shall always denote a positive constant which may change from line to line.

We put

H^{1}_{\Gamma_{0}}(\Omega):=\{u\in H^{1}(\Omega):u|_{\Gamma_{0}}=0\}.

Furthermore, we adopt the standard convention of using Poincaré’s Inequality to identify the norm
\|u\|_{H^{1}_{\Gamma_{0}}}
with
\|\nabla u\|_{2}
. In this we put:

\left\|u\right\|_{H^{1}_{\Gamma_{0}}(\Omega)}=\left\|\nabla u\right\|_{2},\,\,(u,v)_{H^{1}_{\Gamma_{0}}(\Omega)}=(\nabla u,\nabla v)_{\Omega}.

Similarly, we put:

\left\|w\right\|_{H_{0}^{2}(\Gamma)}=\left|\Delta w\right|_{2},\,\,(w,z)_{H^{2}_{0}(\Gamma)}=(\Delta w,\Delta z)_{\Gamma}.

For convenience and brevity, we shall frequently use the notation:

\left\|u\right\|_{1,\Omega}=\left\|\nabla u\right\|_{2},\,\,\,\left\|w\right\|_{2,\Gamma}=\left|\Delta w\right|_{2}.

With
Y
a Banach space, we denote the duality pairing between the dual space
Y^{\prime}
and
Y
by

\langle\psi,y\rangle_{Y^{\prime},Y}
, or simply by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
when the context is clear. That is,

\displaystyle\langle\psi,y\rangle=\psi(y)\text{ for }y\in Y,\,\psi\in Y^{\prime}.

2.2.
Assumptions

Throughout the paper, we study (
1.1
) under the following assumption.

Assumption 2.1
.

h:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a function in
C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
.

Under Assumption
2.1
, we obtain the following result.

Proposition 2.2
.

Suppose
h\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
. Let
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a bounded open domain with a
C^{1}
boundary.
Then the Nemytskii operator
h
is locally Lipschitz from
H^{1+\epsilon}_{0}(\Gamma)
into
L^{2}(\Gamma)
for any
\epsilon>0
.

Proof.

Assume
\|w_{1}\|_{H^{1+\epsilon}_{0}(\Gamma)},\|w_{2}\|_{H^{1+\epsilon}_{0}(\Gamma)}\leq R
.
Due to the imbedding
H^{1+\epsilon}_{0}(\Gamma)\hookrightarrow L^{\infty}(\Gamma)
in two dimensions,
we have
|w_{1}|_{\infty},|w_{2}|_{\infty}\leq CR
. By the mean value theorem, we have

\displaystyle|h(w_{1})-h(w_{2})|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Gamma}|h(w_{1})-h(w_{2})|^{2}d\Gamma

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Gamma}\Big[\max_{|s|\leq CR}|h^{\prime}(s)|\Big]^{2}|w_{1}-w_{2}|^{2}d\Gamma

\displaystyle\leq C(R)|w_{1}-w_{2}|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C(R)\|w_{1}-w_{2}\|_{H^{1+\epsilon}_{0}(\Gamma)}^{2}.

∎

2.3.
Well-posedness of Strong Solutions

To state our results regarding the local and global well-posedness of strong solutions, we need to introduce the necessary framework to present (
1.1
) in operator-theoretic form. Such a setup is essential for applying the nonlinear semigroup technique.

First, we introduce the Dirichlét-Neumann Laplacian, given by:
A=-\Delta:\mathcal{D}(A)\subset L^{2}(\Omega)\longrightarrow L^{2}(\Omega)
, with its domain
\mathcal{D}(A)=\{u\in H^{2}(\Omega):u|_{\Gamma_{0}}=0,\,\,\partial_{\nu}u|_{\Gamma}=0\}
. The operator
A
can be extended to a continuous map
A:H^{1}_{\Gamma_{0}}(\Omega)\longrightarrow(H^{1}_{\Gamma_{0}}(\Omega))^{\prime}
, where:

\displaystyle\langle Au,\phi\rangle=\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\cdot\nabla\phi\,dx=(\nabla u,\nabla\phi)_{\Omega},

(2.1)

for all
u,\phi\in H^{1}_{\Gamma_{0}}(\Omega)
.

We next define the Dirichlét-Neumann map:
R:H^{s}(\Gamma)\longrightarrow H^{s+\frac{3}{2}}(\Omega)\cap H^{1}_{\Gamma_{0}}(\Omega)
where
s\geq 0
by:

\displaystyle q=Rp\iff q\text{ is the weak solution of the problem }\begin{cases}\Delta q=0\text{ in }\Omega,\\
q=0\text{ on }\Gamma_{0},\\
\partial_{\nu}q=p\text{ on }\Gamma.\end{cases}

(2.2)

It is well known (see, for instance Lasiecka and Triggiani
[
23
,
24
]
) that
R
is continuous from
H^{s}(\Gamma)
to
H^{s+\frac{3}{2}}(\Omega)\cap H^{1}_{\Gamma_{0}}(\Omega)
, for
s\geq 0
.
Let us note here that (
2.2
) and standard computation yield the following useful identity:

\displaystyle\langle ARp,\phi\rangle=(\nabla Rp,\nabla\phi)_{\Omega}=(p,\gamma\phi)_{\Gamma},

(2.3)

for all
p\in L^{2}(\Gamma)
and
\phi\in H^{1}_{\Gamma_{0}}(\Omega)
.

Additionally, the biharmonic operator
\Delta^{2}:\mathcal{D}(\Delta^{2})\subset L^{2}(\Gamma)\longrightarrow L^{2}(\Gamma)
with its domain

\mathcal{D}(\Delta^{2})=H^{4}(\Gamma)\cap H^{2}_{0}(\Gamma)
can be extended as a continuous mapping from
H^{2}_{0}(\Gamma)
to
H^{-2}(\Gamma)
, where

\displaystyle\langle\Delta^{2}w,\phi\rangle=(\Delta w,\Delta\phi)_{\Gamma},

(2.4)

for all
w,\phi\in H^{2}_{0}(\Gamma)
.

By using the operators defined above, system (
1.1
) can be formally cast as:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}u_{tt}+A(u-Rw_{t})=0&\text{ in }\Omega\times(0,T),\\[5.69054pt]
w_{tt}+\Delta^{2}w+\gamma u_{t}=h(w)&\text{ in }\Gamma\times(0,T),\\[5.69054pt]
(u(0),u_{t}(0))=(u_{0},u_{1})\in H^{1}_{\Gamma_{0}}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega),\\[5.69054pt]
(w(0),w_{t}(0))=(w_{0},w_{1})\in H^{2}_{0}(\Gamma)\times L^{2}(\Gamma).\end{cases}

(2.5)

Note that the equation
\partial_{\nu}u=w_{t}
on
\Gamma
in system (
1.1
) has been incorporated into the term
A(u-Rw_{t})
.

Next, we introduce the space
H=H^{1}_{\Gamma_{0}}(\Omega)\times H^{2}_{0}(\Gamma)\times L^{2}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Gamma)
with the natural norm

\|U\|_{H}^{2}=\left\|\nabla u\right\|_{2}^{2}+\left|\Delta w\right|_{2}^{2}+\left\|y\right\|_{2}^{2}+\left|z\right|_{2}^{2},\text{ for all }U=(u,w,y,z)\in H,

and define the
nonlinear
operator

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}:\mathcal{D}(\mathscr{A})\subset H\longrightarrow H

by

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}\begin{bmatrix}u\\
w\\
y\\
z\end{bmatrix}^{tr}=\begin{bmatrix}-y\\
-z\\
A(u-Rz)\\
\Delta^{2}w+\gamma y-h(w)\end{bmatrix}^{tr}

(2.6)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\mathscr{A})=\Big\{(u,w,y,z)\in

\displaystyle\left(H^{1}_{\Gamma_{0}}(\Omega)\times H^{2}_{0}(\Gamma)\right)^{2}:A(u-Rz)\in L^{2}(\Omega),\;\Delta^{2}w\in L^{2}(\Gamma)\Big\}.

Observe that the nonlinear term
h(w)\in L^{2}(\Gamma)
because
w\in H^{2}_{0}(\Gamma)\hookrightarrow L^{\infty}(\Gamma)
and
h\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
.

With
U=(u,w,u_{t},w_{t})
, system (
2.5
) can be written in the operator-theoretic form:

\displaystyle U_{t}+\mathscr{A}U=0,\quad U(0)=U_{0}=(u_{0},w_{0},u_{1},w_{1})\in H.

(2.7)

Theorem 2.3
.

(Local and global well-posedness of strong solutions)
Assume
h\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
and
U_{0}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathscr{A})
. Then equation (
2.7
) has a unique local strong solution
U\in W^{1,\infty}(0,T_{0};H)
for some
T_{0}>0
, satisfying
U(t)\in\mathcal{D}(\mathscr{A})
for all
t\in[0,T_{0}]
, where
T_{0}>0
depends on the initial quadratic energy
E(0)
, where the quadratic energy is defined as

\displaystyle E(t)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}\left(\|u_{t}(t)\|_{2}^{2}+\|\nabla u(t)\|_{2}^{2}+|w_{t}(t)|_{2}^{2}+|\Delta w(t)|_{2}^{2}\right).

Furthermore, the following energy identity holds for all
t\in[0,T_{0}]
:

\displaystyle E(t)=E(0)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Gamma}h(w)w_{t}d\Gamma d\tau.

(2.8)

If in addition, we assume
|h(s)|\leq c(|s|+1)
for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
where
c>0
is a constant, then the local strong solution can be extended to a global strong solution and
T_{0}
can be taken arbitrarily large.

Remark 2.4
.

Theorem
2.3
establishes the well-posedness of strong solutions
U
to equation (
2.7
).
Since system (
1.1
) can be reformulated in the abstract form (
2.7
),
U
is also a strong solution to system (
1.1
).
Therefore, Theorem
2.3
establishes the well-posedness of strong solutions to system (
1.1
) as well.

2.4.
Well-posedness of Weak Solutions

To present our results on local and global well-posedness of weak solutions, we first introduce the definition of a suitable weak solution for (
1.1
).

Definition 2.5
.

A pair of functions
(u,w)
is said to be a
weak solution
of (
1.1
) on the interval
[0,T]
provided:

(1)

u\in C([0,T];H^{1}_{\Gamma_{0}}(\Omega))
,
u_{t}\in C([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega))
,

(2)

w\in C([0,T];H^{2}_{0}(\Gamma))
,
w_{t}\in C([0,T];L^{2}(\Gamma))
,

(3)

(u(0),u_{t}(0))=(u_{0},u_{1})\in H^{1}_{\Gamma_{0}}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)
,

(4)

(w(0),w_{t}(0))=(w_{0},w_{1})\in H^{2}_{0}(\Gamma)\times L^{2}(\Gamma)
,

(5)

The functions
u
and
w
satisfy the following variational identities
for all
t\in[0,T]
:

\displaystyle(u_{t}(t),\phi(t))_{\Omega}

\displaystyle-(u_{1},\phi(0))_{\Omega}-\int_{0}^{t}(u_{t}(\tau),\phi_{t}(\tau))_{\Omega}d\tau+\int_{0}^{t}(\nabla u(\tau),\nabla\phi(\tau))_{\Omega}d\tau

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{t}(w_{t}(\tau),\gamma\phi(\tau))_{\Gamma}d\tau=0,

(2.9)

and

\displaystyle(w_{t}(t)

\displaystyle+\gamma u(t),\psi(t))_{\Gamma}-(w_{1}+\gamma u(0),\psi(0))_{\Gamma}-\int_{0}^{t}(w_{t}(\tau),\psi_{t}(\tau))_{\Gamma}d\tau

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{t}(\gamma u(\tau),\psi_{t}(\tau))_{\Gamma}d\tau+\int_{0}^{t}(\Delta w(\tau),\Delta\psi(\tau))_{\Gamma}d\tau

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Gamma}h(w(\tau))\psi(\tau)d\Gamma d\tau,

(2.10)

for all test functions
\phi
and
\psi
satisfying:

\phi\in C([0,T];H^{1}_{\Gamma_{0}}(\Omega))
,

\psi\in C\left([0,T];H^{2}_{0}(\Gamma)\right)
with

\phi_{t}\in L^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
, and
\psi_{t}\in L^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(\Gamma))
.

The next theorem states the existence and uniqueness of local weak solutions to (
1.1
), as well as their continuous dependence on the initial data. Moreover, under an additional assumption on the source term, the local weak solution can be extended to a global weak solution for all time.

Theorem 2.6
.

(Local and global well-posedness of weak solutions)

Assume
h\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
. Let
U_{0}=(u_{0},w_{0},u_{1},w_{1})\in H=H^{1}_{\Gamma_{0}}(\Omega)\times H^{2}_{0}(\Gamma)\times L^{2}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Gamma)
.
Then problem (
1.1
) possesses a unique local weak solution
(u,w)
in the sense of Definition
2.5
on
[0,T_{0}]
, where
T_{0}>0
depends on the initial quadratic energy
E(0)
.
In addition, the energy identity (
2.8
) holds for all
t\in[0,T_{0}]
. Furthermore, the weak solutions depend continuously on initial data, namely, if
U^{n}_{0}=(u^{n}_{0},w^{n}_{0},u^{n}_{1},w^{n}_{1})\in H
is a sequence of initial data such that
U^{n}_{0}\longrightarrow U_{0}
in
H
as
n\longrightarrow\infty
, then the corresponding weak solutions
(u^{n},w^{n})
and
(u,w)
of (
1.1
) satisfy:

\displaystyle(u^{n},w^{n},u^{n}_{t},w^{n}_{t})\longrightarrow(u,w,u_{t},w_{t})\text{ in }C([0,T_{0}];H),\text{ as }n\longrightarrow\infty,

where
T_{0}>0
can be chosen independent of
n\in\mathbb{N}
. If in addition, we assume
|h(s)|\leq c(|s|+1)
for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
where
c>0
is a constant, then the local weak solution is indeed a global weak solution and
T_{0}
can be taken arbitrarily large.

2.5.
Potential Well Solutions

Before our final results can be presented, some preliminary work pertaining to the potential well must be done. First, we place additional assumptions on
h
.

Assumption 2.7
.

Let
h\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
satisfy:

(1)

there exists
H(s)
and
\theta>2
such that
H^{\prime}(s)=h(s)
and
0<\theta H(s)<h(s)s
for all
s\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
,

(2)

|h(s)|\leq h(|s|)
,

(3)

h(s)
is non-decreasing on
(0,\infty)
,

(4)

\frac{h(s)}{s}\rightarrow 0^{+}
as
s\rightarrow 0^{+}
.

Remark 2.8
.

Condition (1) is the well-known Ambrosetti-Rabinowitz condition often used in elliptic problems.
Note that letting
s\rightarrow 0
in the inequality
0<\theta H(s)<h(s)s
implies

H(0)=0
. Also, by condition (4), we see that
h(0)=0
. Thus, both
H
and
h
pass through the origin. An example of a function
h(s)
satisfying Assumption
2.7
is
e^{|s|^{q}}|s|^{p}s
, where
p,q>0
. Another example is
e^{e^{|s|}}|s|^{p}s
where
p>0
.

Define
V=H^{1}_{\Gamma_{0}}(\Omega)\times H^{2}_{0}(\Gamma)
and endow it with the natural norm given by

\displaystyle\|(u,w)\|^{2}_{V}=\|\nabla u\|_{2}^{2}+|\Delta w|_{2}^{2}.

(2.11)

Next, let
H(s)
be as in Assumption
2.7
, and define the nonlinear functional
\mathscr{J}:V\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by

\displaystyle\mathscr{J}(u,w)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\|\nabla u\|_{2}^{2}+|\Delta w|_{2}^{2}\right)-\int_{\Gamma}H(w)d\Gamma.

(2.12)

Note that the potential energy of (
1.1
) at time
t
is given by
\mathscr{J}(u(t),w(t))
. The Frechet derivative of
\mathscr{J}
at
(u,v)\in V
is given by

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathscr{J}^{\prime}(u,w),(\phi,\psi)\right\rangle=\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\cdot\nabla\phi dx+\int_{\Gamma}\Delta w\Delta\psi d\Gamma-\int_{\Gamma}h(w)\psi d\Gamma,

(2.13)

for all
(\phi,\psi)\in V
.
The Nehari manifold associated with the functional
\mathscr{J}
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathscr{N}:=\left\{(u,w)\in V\setminus\{(0,0)\}:\left\langle\mathscr{J}^{\prime}(u,w),(u,w)\right\rangle=0\right\}.

(2.14)

Combining (
2.13
) and (
2.14
) allows us to write

\displaystyle\mathscr{N}=\left\{(u,w)\in V\setminus\{(0,0)\}:\|\nabla u\|_{2}^{2}+|\Delta w|_{2}^{2}=\int_{\Gamma}h(w)wd\Gamma\right\}.

(2.15)

Next, define the potential well associated with
\mathscr{J}(u,w)
by

\displaystyle\mathscr{W}:=\left\{(u,w)\in V:\mathscr{J}(u,w)<d\right\},

(2.16)

where
d
is the depth of the potential well
\mathscr{W}
and taken to be

\displaystyle d:=\inf_{(u,w)\in\mathscr{N}}\mathscr{J}(u,w).

(2.17)

We must first verify that
d
is strictly positive in order to ensure
\mathscr{W}
is non-empty.

Proposition 2.9
.

Let
h
satisfy Assumption
2.7
. Then
d>0
.

Proof.

Let
(u,w)\in\mathscr{N}
. We claim
w\not=0
. Indeed, if
w=0
, then since
\|\nabla u\|_{2}^{2}+|\Delta w|_{2}^{2}=\int_{\Gamma}h(w)wd\Gamma
, we obtain
\|\nabla u\|_{2}=0
. By the Poincaré inequality, it follows that
\|u\|_{2}=0
. Hence
(u,w)=(0,0)
, contradicting
(u,w)\in\mathscr{N}
.

Then we have from (
2.12
), (
2.15
), and condition (1) of Assumption
2.7
that

\displaystyle\mathscr{J}(u,w)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\|\nabla u\|_{2}^{2}+|\Delta w|_{2}^{2}\right)-\int_{\Gamma}H(w)d\Gamma

\displaystyle>\frac{1}{2}\left(\|\nabla u\|_{2}^{2}+|\Delta w|_{2}^{2}\right)-\frac{1}{\theta}\int_{\Gamma}h(w)wd\Gamma

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{\theta}\right)\left(\|\nabla u\|_{2}^{2}+|\Delta w|_{2}^{2}\right),

(2.18)

where
\theta>2
. Note that as
(u,w)\in\mathscr{N}
, and using conditions (2)-(3) of Assumption
2.7
, we have

\displaystyle\|\nabla u\|_{2}^{2}+|\Delta w|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Gamma}h(w)wd\Gamma

\displaystyle\leq|\Gamma|\sup_{x\in\Gamma}|h(w)w|

\displaystyle\leq|\Gamma|\sup_{x\in\Gamma}\left(h(|w|)|w|\right)

\displaystyle\leq|\Gamma|h\left(|w|_{\infty}\right)|w|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq|\Gamma|h\left(C|\Delta w|_{2}\right)C|\Delta w|_{2},

(2.19)

since
H^{2}_{0}(\Gamma)\hookrightarrow L^{\infty}(\Gamma)
.

Let
y(t):=\left(\|\nabla u\|_{2}^{2}+|\Delta w|_{2}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}
and note
y(t)\geq|\Delta w(t)|_{2}
. Then from (
2.5
) one has

\displaystyle y^{2}(t)\leq|\Gamma|h(Cy(t))Cy(t),

(2.20)

and in turn

\displaystyle\frac{1}{C^{2}|\Gamma|}\leq\frac{h(Cy(t))}{Cy(t)},

(2.21)

since
y(t)>0
. By condition (4) of Assumption
2.7
, we can conclude that there is some
c_{0}>0
such that
y(t)\geq c_{0}
. Combining this with (
2.5
) yields

\displaystyle\mathscr{J}(u,w)

\displaystyle>\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{\theta}\right)\left(\|\nabla u\|_{2}^{2}+|\Delta w|_{2}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{\theta}\right)y^{2}(t)

\displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{\theta}\right)c_{0}^{2}>0.

(2.22)

As
c_{0}
is independent of the choice of
(u,w)\in\mathscr{N}
, we can conclude that
d\geq\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{\theta}\right)c_{0}^{2}>0
.
∎

Note as expected that the potential well and Nehari manifold are disjoint by (
2.16
) and (
2.17
), that is

\displaystyle\mathscr{W}\cap\mathscr{N}=\varnothing.

(2.23)

This enables the decomposition of the potential well into two parts, the ‘stable’ part denoted
\mathscr{W}_{1}
and ‘unstable’ part denoted
\mathscr{W}_{2}
. Formally we define them as follows:

\displaystyle\mathscr{W}_{1}:=\left\{(u,w)\in\mathscr{W}:\|\nabla u\|_{2}^{2}+|\Delta w|_{2}^{2}>\int_{\Gamma}h(w)wd\Gamma\right\}\cup\{(0,0)\},

\displaystyle\mathscr{W}_{2}:=\left\{(u,w)\in\mathscr{W}:\|\nabla u\|_{2}^{2}+|\Delta w|_{2}^{2}<\int_{\Gamma}h(w)wd\Gamma\right\}.

Clearly we have
\mathscr{W}_{1}\cap\mathscr{W}_{2}=\varnothing
and
\mathscr{W}_{1}\cup\mathscr{W}_{2}=\mathscr{W}
.

Lastly we define the total energy
\mathscr{E}(t)
of the system (
1.1
) as follows:

\displaystyle\mathscr{E}(t):

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\|u_{t}(t)\|_{2}^{2}+|w_{t}(t)|_{2}^{2}\right)+\mathscr{J}(u(t),w(t))=E(t)-\int_{\Gamma}H(w)d\Gamma,

(2.24)

where the quadratic energy
E(t)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\|u_{t}(t)\|_{2}^{2}+\|\nabla u(t)\|_{2}^{2}+|w_{t}(t)|_{2}^{2}+|\Delta w(t)|_{2}^{2}\right)
. In this manner, one can rewrite the energy identity (
2.8
) as

\displaystyle\mathscr{E}(t)=\mathscr{E}(0),

(2.25)

for all
t\in[0,T_{0}]
, and we can conclude that
\mathscr{E}(t)
is constant on
[0,T_{0}]
. We can now state our final results of the paper:

Theorem 2.10
.

(Global existence of potential well solutions-Part 1)

Let
h
satisfy Assumption
2.7
. Assume that
(u_{0},w_{0})\in\mathscr{W}_{1}
and
\mathscr{E}(0)<d
. Then system (
1.1
) admits a global weak solution
(u(t),w(t))\in\mathscr{W}_{1}
for all
t\geq 0
. Moreover, for all
t\geq 0
, the following estimates hold for the potential energy
\mathscr{J}(u(t),w(t))
, the total energy
\mathscr{E}(t)
, and the quadratic energy
E(t)
:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\mathscr{J}(u(t),w(t))\leq\mathscr{E}(t)=\mathscr{E}(0)<d,\\
E(t)<\dfrac{\theta d}{\theta-2},\end{cases}

(2.26)

where
\theta>2
is the constant given in Assumption
2.7
.

By assuming additional regularity of the initial data, we obtain the global existence of strong solutions in the potential well.

Theorem 2.11
.

(Global existence of potential well solutions-Part 2)

Let
h
satisfy Assumption
2.7
. Assume that
(u_{0},w_{0})\in\mathscr{W}_{1}
and
\mathscr{E}(0)<d
.
Suppose further that
U_{0}=(u_{0},w_{0},u_{1},w_{1})\in\mathcal{D}(\mathscr{A})
. Then equation (
2.7
) admits a unique global strong solution
U=(u,w,u_{t},w_{t})\in W^{1,\infty}(0,T;H)
, satisfying
U(t)\in\mathcal{D}(\mathscr{A})
and
(u(t),w(t))\in\mathscr{W}_{1}
for all
t\in[0,T]
, where
T>0
is arbitrary.
Moreover, all inequalities in (
2.26
) hold.

3.
Well-posedness of Strong Solutions

This section is devoted to proving Theorem
2.3
, which establishes the local and global well-posedness of strong solutions.
We employ the theory of nonlinear semigroups and monotone operators. The first step was to reformulate the PDE system (
1.1
) in the operator-theoretic form (
2.7
), as demonstrated in the previous section. Due to the presence of the nonlinear source term
h(w)
acting on the plate, our strategy involves first addressing the case where
h
is globally Lipschitz from
H^{2}_{0}(\Gamma)
to
L^{2}(\Gamma)
, and subsequently extending the analysis to the locally Lipschitz scenario.

3.1.
Globally Lipschitz Sources

This step addresses the case where the source term
h:H^{2}_{0}(\Gamma)\rightarrow L^{2}(\Gamma)
is globally Lipschitz. Under this assumption, we establish the following lemma.

Lemma 3.1
.

Assume that
h:H^{2}_{0}(\Gamma)\to L^{2}(\Gamma)
is globally Lipschitz. For any initial data
U_{0}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathscr{A})
, equation (
2.7
) admits a unique global strong solution
U\in W^{1,\infty}(0,T;H)
for arbitrary
T>0
, with
U(t)\in\mathcal{D}(\mathscr{A})
for all
t\in[0,T]
.

Proof.

Recall that by Kato’s Theorem (see, for example,
[
31
]
), if
\mathscr{A}+\omega I
is
m
-accretive for some positive
\omega
, then for each
U_{0}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathscr{A})
there is a unique strong solution
U
of (
2.7
), i.e.,
U\in W^{1,\infty}(0,T;H)
such that
U(0)=U_{0}
,
U(t)\in\mathcal{D}(\mathscr{A})
for all
t\in[0,T]
, and equation (
2.7
) is satisfied a.e. on
[0,T]
, where
T>0
is arbitrary. In this light, it suffices to show that the operator
\mathscr{A}+\omega I
is
m
-accretive for some positive
\omega
. Recall that an operator
\mathscr{A}:\mathcal{D}(\mathscr{A})\subset H\longrightarrow H
is accretive if
(\mathscr{A}x_{1}-\mathscr{A}x_{2},x_{1}-x_{2})_{H}\geq 0
, for all
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathscr{A})
, and it is
m
-accretive if, in addition,
\mathscr{A}+I
maps
\mathcal{D}(\mathscr{A})
onto
H
.

Step 1: Accretivity of
\mathscr{A}+\omega I
for some positive
\omega
.

Let
U=(u,w,y,z)
,
\hat{U}=(\hat{u},\hat{w},\hat{y},\hat{z})\in\mathcal{D}(\mathscr{A})
. We aim to find
\omega>0
such that

\displaystyle((\mathscr{A}+\omega I)U-(\mathscr{A}+\omega I)\hat{U},U-\hat{U})_{H}\geq 0.

By straightforward calculations, we obtain:

\displaystyle((\mathscr{A}

\displaystyle+\omega I)U-(\mathscr{A}+\omega I)\hat{U},U-\hat{U})_{H}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\mathscr{A}(U)-\mathscr{A}(\hat{U}),U-\hat{U})_{H}+\omega|U-\hat{U}|_{H}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-(\nabla(y-\hat{y}),\nabla(u-\hat{u}))_{\Omega}-(\Delta(z-\hat{z}),\Delta(w-\hat{w}))_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle+\langle A(u-\hat{u}),y-\hat{y}\rangle-\langle AR(z-\hat{z}),y-\hat{y}\rangle+\langle\Delta^{2}(w-\hat{w}),z-\hat{z}\rangle

\displaystyle+(\gamma(y-\hat{y}),z-\hat{z})_{\Gamma}-(h(w)-h(\hat{w}),z-\hat{z})_{\Gamma}+\omega|U-\hat{U}|_{H}^{2}.

(3.1)

Using (
2.1
), (
2.3
), and (
2.4
), we have

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\langle A(u-\hat{u}),y-\hat{y}\rangle=(\nabla(u-\hat{u}),\nabla(y-\hat{y}))_{\Omega},\vskip 7.22743pt\\
\langle AR(z-\hat{z}),y-\hat{y}\rangle=(z-\hat{z},\gamma(y-\hat{y}))_{\Gamma},\vskip 7.22743pt\\
\langle\Delta^{2}(w-\hat{w}),z-\hat{z}\rangle=(\Delta(w-\hat{w}),\Delta(z-\hat{z}))_{\Gamma}.\end{cases}

(3.2)

Since
h:H^{2}_{0}(\Gamma)\longrightarrow L^{2}(\Gamma)
is globally Lipschitz, we let
L
be the Lipschitz constant of
h
. Then, by Young’s inequality,

\displaystyle-(h(w)-h(\hat{w}),z-\hat{z})_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle\geq-L|\Delta(w-\hat{w})|_{2}|z-\hat{z}|_{2}

\displaystyle\geq-\frac{L}{2}\left(|\Delta(w-\hat{w})|_{2}^{2}+|z-\hat{z}|_{2}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\geq-\frac{L}{2}|U-\hat{U}|_{H}^{2}.

(3.3)

Combining (
3.1
)-(
3.1
), we find

\displaystyle((\mathscr{A}

\displaystyle+\omega I)U-(\mathscr{A}+\omega I)\hat{U},U-\hat{U})_{H}\geq\left(\omega-\frac{L}{2}\right)|U-\hat{U}|_{H}^{2}.

Thus, by selecting
\omega>\frac{L}{2}
, we establish the accretivity of
\mathscr{A}+\omega I
.

Step 2:
m
-accretivity of
\mathscr{A}+\lambda I
for some
\lambda>0
.

To invoke Kato’s Theorem and complete the proof of Lemma
3.1
, one must establish that
\mathscr{A}+\lambda I:\mathcal{D}(\mathscr{A})\longrightarrow H
is onto for some
\lambda>0
.

Let
\lambda>0
(to be determined later) and
(a,b,c,d)\in H
. We must show that there exists
(u,w,y,z)\in\mathcal{D}(\mathscr{A})
such that
(\mathscr{A}+\lambda I)(u,w,y,z)=(a,b,c,d)
, that is,

\displaystyle\begin{cases}-y+\lambda u=a\\
-z+\lambda w=b\\
A(u-Rz)+\lambda y=c\\
\Delta^{2}w+\gamma y-h(w)+\lambda z=d.\end{cases}

(3.4)

Note, (
3.4
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\lambda}Ay-ARz+\lambda y=c-\frac{1}{\lambda}Aa\vskip 7.22743pt\\
\frac{1}{\lambda}\Delta^{2}z+\gamma y-h(\frac{b+z}{\lambda})+\lambda z=d-\frac{1}{\lambda}\Delta^{2}b.\end{cases}

(3.5)

Let
V=H^{1}_{\Gamma_{0}}(\Omega)\times H^{2}_{0}(\Gamma)
and notice that the right hand side of (
3.5
) belongs to
V^{\prime}
. We now define the operator
\mathscr{B}:V\longrightarrow V^{\prime}
by:

\displaystyle\mathscr{B}\begin{bmatrix}y\vskip 7.22743pt\\
z\end{bmatrix}^{tr}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{\lambda}Ay-ARz+\lambda y\vskip 7.22743pt\\
\frac{1}{\lambda}\Delta^{2}z+\gamma y-h(\frac{b+z}{\lambda})+\lambda z\end{bmatrix}^{tr}.

At this point we wish to establish that
\mathscr{B}
is surjective. By Corollary 1.2 (p.45) in Barbu
[
7
]
, it is enough to show that
\mathscr{B}
is maximal monotone and coercive. To accomplish this we will first show that
\mathscr{B}
is strongly monotone. To this end, let
Y,\,\hat{Y}\in V
, where
Y=(y,z)
and
\hat{Y}=(\hat{y},\hat{z})
. By straightforward calculations, we obtain

\displaystyle\langle\mathscr{B}

\displaystyle(Y)-\mathscr{B}(\hat{Y}),Y-\hat{Y}\rangle_{V^{\prime},V}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda}\langle A(y-\hat{y}),y-\hat{y}\rangle-\langle AR(z-\hat{z}),y-\hat{y}\rangle+\lambda\|y-\hat{y}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\lambda}\langle\Delta^{2}(z-\hat{z}),z-\hat{z}\rangle+(\gamma(y-\hat{y}),z-\hat{z})_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle-\left(h\left(\frac{b+z}{\lambda}\right)-h\left(\frac{b+\hat{z}}{\lambda}\right),z-\hat{z}\right)_{\Gamma}+\lambda|z-\hat{z}|_{2}^{2}.

(3.6)

Thanks to (
2.1
), (
2.3
), and (
2.4
), we have

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\langle A(y-\hat{y}),y-\hat{y}\rangle=\|\nabla(y-\hat{y})\|_{2}^{2},\vskip 7.22743pt\\
\langle AR(z-\hat{z}),y-\hat{y}\rangle=(z-\hat{z},\gamma(y-\hat{y}))_{\Gamma},\vskip 7.22743pt\\
\langle\Delta^{2}(z-\hat{z}),z-\hat{z}\rangle=|\Delta(z-\hat{z})|_{2}^{2}.\end{cases}

(3.7)

Note from Hölder’s inequality that

\displaystyle\left(h\left(\frac{b+z}{\lambda}\right)-h\left(\frac{b+\hat{z}}{\lambda}\right),z-\hat{z}\right)_{\Gamma}\leq\left|h\left(\frac{b+z}{\lambda}\right)-h\left(\frac{b+\hat{z}}{\lambda}\right)\right|_{2}\left|z-\hat{z}\right|_{2},

and, by way of
h
being globally Lipschitz with Lipschitz constant
L
, we further establish that

\displaystyle\left(h\left(\frac{b+z}{\lambda}\right)-h\left(\frac{b+\hat{z}}{\lambda}\right),z-\hat{z}\right)_{\Gamma}\leq\frac{L}{\lambda}|\Delta(z-\hat{z})|_{2}\left|z-\hat{z}\right|_{2}.

(3.8)

It follows now from (
3.1
), (
3.7
), (
3.8
), and Young’s inequality (with an
\eta>0
) that:

\displaystyle\langle\mathscr{B}

\displaystyle(Y)-\mathscr{B}(\hat{Y}),Y-\hat{Y}\rangle_{V^{\prime},V}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\lambda}\|\nabla(y-\hat{y})\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\|y-\hat{y}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{\lambda}|\Delta(z-\hat{z})|_{2}^{2}-\frac{L}{\lambda}|\Delta(z-\hat{z})|_{2}|z-\hat{z}|_{2}+\lambda|z-\hat{z}|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\lambda}\|\nabla(y-\hat{y})\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\|y-\hat{y}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{\lambda}|\Delta(z-\hat{z})|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt-\frac{L^{2}}{4\eta\lambda}|\Delta(z-\hat{z})|_{2}^{2}-\frac{\eta}{\lambda}|z-\hat{z}|_{2}^{2}+\lambda|z-\hat{z}|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\lambda}\|\nabla(y-\hat{y})\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\|y-\hat{y}\|_{2}^{2}+\left(\frac{1}{\lambda}-\frac{L^{2}}{4\eta\lambda}\right)|\Delta(z-\hat{z})|_{2}^{2}+\left(\lambda-\frac{\eta}{\lambda}\right)|z-\hat{z}|_{2}^{2}.

(3.9)

By selecting
\eta=\frac{L^{2}}{2}
and then selecting
\lambda=\frac{L}{\sqrt{2}}
, it follows from (
3.1
) that

\displaystyle\langle\mathscr{B}(Y)-\mathscr{B}(\hat{Y}),Y-\hat{Y}\rangle_{V^{\prime},V}\geq\frac{1}{2\lambda}\Big(\|\nabla(y-\hat{y})\|_{2}^{2}+|\Delta(z-\hat{z})|_{2}^{2}\Big)=\frac{1}{2\lambda}\|Y-\hat{Y}\|^{2}_{V},

proving
\mathscr{B}
is strongly monotone. It is easy to see that strong monotonicity implies coercivity of
\mathscr{B}
.

Next, we show that
\mathscr{B}
is continuous. It is clear that the mappings
A:H^{1}_{\Gamma_{0}}(\Omega)\longrightarrow(H^{1}_{\Gamma_{0}}(\Omega))^{\prime}
,
\Delta^{2}:H^{2}_{0}(\Gamma)\longrightarrow H^{-2}(\Gamma)
, and
\gamma:H^{1}_{\Gamma_{0}}(\Omega)\longrightarrow H^{-2}(\Gamma)
are continuous. Moreover,
h:H^{2}_{0}(\Gamma)\longrightarrow L^{2}(\Gamma)
is globally Lipschitz continuous, thus the mapping
z\mapsto h(\frac{b+z}{\lambda})
is also continuous from
H^{2}_{0}(\Gamma)
to
H^{-2}(\Gamma)
. In addition, by the properties of the Dirichlét-Neumann map
R
, we deduce that
AR:H^{2}_{0}(\Gamma)\longrightarrow(H^{1}_{\Gamma_{0}}(\Omega))^{\prime}
is continuous. It follows that
\mathscr{B}
is continuous. Recall again from
[
7
]
that along with the monotonicity established earlier, continuity allows us to conclude that
\mathscr{B}
is maximal monotone. With coercivity we finally have that
\mathscr{B}
is surjective.

As
\mathscr{B}
is surjective, we have established the existence of
(y,z)\in V=H^{1}_{\Gamma_{0}}(\Omega)\times H^{2}_{0}(\Gamma)
such that
(y,z)
satisfies (
3.5
). In addition,
(u,w)=\left(\frac{a+y}{\lambda},\frac{b+z}{\lambda}\right)\in H^{1}_{\Gamma_{0}}(\Omega)\times H^{2}_{0}(\Gamma)
.
Moreover, by (
3.4
), we have
A(u-Rz)\in L^{2}(\Omega)
and
\Delta^{2}w\in L^{2}(\Gamma)
.
Therefore,
(u,w,y,z)\in\mathcal{D}(\mathscr{A})
with the property that
(\mathscr{A}+\lambda I)(u,w,y,z)=(a,b,c,d)
for
\lambda=\frac{L}{\sqrt{2}}
. Thus, the proof of maximal accretivity is completed and so is the proof of Lemma
3.1
.
∎

3.2.
Locally Lipschitz Sources

In this subsection, we employ a standard truncation procedure (see, e.g.,
[
9
,
10
,
12
,
18
]
) combined with Lemma
3.1
to establish a result for the case where
h
is locally Lipschitz continuous from
H^{2}_{0}(\Gamma)
to
L^{2}(\Gamma)
.

In Theorem
2.3
, concerning the well-posedness of strong solutions, we assume
h\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
. This implies the local Lipschitz continuity of the Nemytskii operator
h:H^{2}_{0}(\Gamma)\to L^{2}(\Gamma)
, by Proposition
2.2
. Consequently, the local well-posedness of strong solutions stated in Theorem
2.3
follows directly from Lemma
3.2
below.

Lemma 3.2
.

Assume that
h:H^{2}_{0}(\Gamma)\to L^{2}(\Gamma)
is locally Lipschitz continuous.
For any initial data
U_{0}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathscr{A})
, equation (
2.7
) admits a unique local strong solution
U\in W^{1,\infty}(0,T_{0};H)
for some
T_{0}>0
, with
U(t)\in\mathcal{D}(\mathscr{A})
for all
t\in[0,T_{0}]
, where
T_{0}>0
depends on the initial quadratic energy
E(0)
.

Proof.

Recall from the previous subsection
V=H^{1}_{\Gamma_{0}}(\Omega)\times H^{2}_{0}(\Gamma)
and define

\displaystyle h^{K}(w)=\begin{cases}h(w)&\text{ if }|\Delta w|_{2}\leq K,\\
h\left(\frac{Kw}{|\Delta w|_{2}}\right)&\text{ if }|\Delta w|_{2}>K,\end{cases}

where
K
is a positive constant such that
K^{2}\geq 4E(0)+1
, where the energy
E(t)
is given by

\displaystyle E(t)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\|u_{t}(t)\|_{2}^{2}+|w_{t}(t)|_{2}^{2}+\|\nabla u(t)\|_{2}^{2}+|\Delta w(t)|_{2}^{2}\right).

With the truncated source, we consider the following (
K
) problem:

\displaystyle(K)\begin{cases}u_{tt}+A(u-Rw_{t})=0&\text{ in }\Omega\times(0,T),\\[5.69054pt]
w_{tt}+\Delta^{2}w+\gamma u_{t}=h^{K}(w)&\text{ in }\Gamma\times(0,T),\\[5.69054pt]
(u(0),u_{t}(0))=(u_{0},u_{1})\in H^{1}_{\Gamma_{0}}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega),\\[5.69054pt]
(w(0),w_{t}(0))=(w_{0},w_{1})\in H^{2}_{0}(\Gamma)\times L^{2}(\Gamma).\end{cases}

We note here that for each such
K
, the operator
h^{K}:H^{2}_{0}(\Gamma)\longrightarrow L^{2}(\Gamma)
is globally Lipschitz continuous (see, e.g.,
[
12
]
), and therefore by Lemma
3.1
, the
(K)
problem has a unique global strong solution
U_{K}\in W^{1,\infty}(0,T;H)
for any
T>0
provided the initial datum
U_{0}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathscr{A})
.

The analysis that follows considers one (
K
) problem and thus for cleanliness we express
(u_{K}(t),w_{K}(t))
as
(u(t),w(t))
.
Since
u_{t}\in H^{1}_{\Gamma_{0}}(\Omega)
and
w_{t}\in H^{2}_{0}(\Gamma)
, we may use the multiplier
u_{t}
and
w_{t}
on the
(K)
problem and obtain the following energy identity:

\displaystyle E(t)=E(0)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Gamma}h^{K}(w)w_{t}d\Gamma d\tau.

(3.10)

Note that we have local Lipschitz constant
L_{K}
for the function
h
on the ball
\{(u,w)\in V:\|(u,w)\|_{V}\leq K\}
. Note also that
\left|\Delta\left(\frac{Kw}{|\Delta w|_{2}}\right)\right|\leq K
for all
w\in H^{2}_{0}(\Gamma)
. Thus
L_{K}
further suffices as a global Lipschitz constant for
h^{K}
.

By Hölder’s and Young’s inequalities, we now have:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle h^{K}(w)w_{t}dxd\tau\leq\int_{0}^{t}\left|h^{K}(w)\right|_{2}|w_{t}|_{2}d\tau

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}|w_{t}|_{2}^{2}d\tau+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\left|h^{K}(w)\right|_{2}^{2}d\tau

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}|w_{t}|_{2}^{2}d\tau+\int_{0}^{t}\left(\left|h^{K}(w)-h^{K}(0)\right|_{2}^{2}+\left|h^{K}(0)\right|_{2}^{2}\right)d\tau

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}|w_{t}|_{2}^{2}d\tau+L_{K}^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\|w\|_{2,\Gamma}^{2}d\tau+t|h(0)|^{2}|\Gamma|.

(3.11)

Let
C_{0}=|h(0)|^{2}|\Gamma|
,
C_{1}=\max\left\{2L_{K}^{2},1\right\}
, and in turn select

\displaystyle T_{0}=\min\left\{\frac{1}{4C_{0}},\frac{1}{C_{1}}\log 2\right\}.

(3.12)

By combining (
3.10
)-(
3.12
), one has

\displaystyle E(t)

\displaystyle\leq E(0)+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}|w_{t}|_{2}^{2}d\tau+L_{K}^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\|w\|_{2,\Gamma}^{2}d\tau+t|h(0)|^{2}|\Gamma|

\displaystyle\leq E(0)+C_{0}T_{0}+C_{1}\int_{0}^{t}E(\tau)d\tau,

(3.13)

for all
t\in[0,T_{0}]
. Thus by Gronwall’s inequality, one has

\displaystyle E(t)\leq(E(0)+C_{0}T_{0})e^{C_{1}t}\text{ for all }t\in[0,T_{0}].

(3.14)

Note our selection
K
such that
K^{2}\geq 4E(0)+1
. Thus it follows from (
3.14
) and (
3.12
) that

\displaystyle E(t)\leq 2\left(E(0)+\frac{1}{4}\right)\leq\frac{K^{2}}{2},

(3.15)

for all
t\in[0,T_{0}]
. This implies that
\|(u(t),w(t))\|_{V}\leq K
, for all
t\in[0,T_{0}]
, and therefore
h^{K}(w)=h(w)
on the time interval
[0,T_{0}]
.
By the uniqueness of solutions to the (
K
) problem, this solution coincides with the solution of system (
2.5
) for
t\in[0,T_{0}]
.
From (
3.12
), we see that
T_{0}
depends on
K
, which is chosen to satisfy
K^{2}\geq 4E(0)+1
. Hence,
T_{0}
depends on
E(0)
. This completes the proof of Lemma
3.2
. Thus, the local well-posedness of strong solutions stated in Theorem
2.3
follows.
∎

3.3.
Global Existence of Strong Solutions

In this subsection, we show that if
h
is bounded by a linear function, i.e.,
|h(s)|\leq c(|s|+1)
for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
,
then the local strong solution can be extended to a global strong solution for all
t\geq 0
.

Proposition 3.3
.

Assume
h\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
such that
|h(s)|\leq c(|s|+1)
for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
where
c>0
is a constant. Let
U=(u,w,u_{t},w_{t})
be a strong solution of (
2.7
) on
[0,T]
. Then the quadratic energy
E(t)
satisfies

\displaystyle E(t)\leq\left(E(0)+Ct\right)e^{Ct},\text{ for all}\;\;t\in[0,T].

(3.16)

Proof.

From (
2.8
), we have the energy identity:

\displaystyle E(t)=E(0)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Gamma}h(w)w_{t}d\Gamma d\tau,\text{ for}\;t\in[0,T].

(3.17)

To estimate the source term on the right-hand side of (
3.17
), we employ Hölder’s and Young’s inequalities as follows:

\displaystyle\left|\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Gamma}h(w)w_{t}d\Gamma d\tau\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}|h(w)|_{2}^{2}d\tau+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}|w_{t}|_{2}^{2}d\tau

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{0}^{t}|w|_{2}^{2}d\tau+Ct+\int_{0}^{t}E(\tau)d\tau

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{0}^{t}E(\tau)d\tau+Ct,

(3.18)

where we have used the Poincaré inequality
|w|_{2}\leq C|\Delta w|_{2}
.

It follows from (
3.17
) and (
3.3
) that, for all
t\in[0,T]
,

\displaystyle E(t)\leq E(0)+Ct+C\int_{0}^{t}E(\tau)d\tau.

(3.19)

By Gronwall’s inequality, we conclude that

\displaystyle E(t)\leq\left(E(0)+Ct\right)e^{Ct},\text{ for all}\;t\in[0,T].

(3.20)

∎

We now prove that the local strong solution can be extended to a global strong solution under the assumption that
h\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
such that
|h(s)|\leq c(|s|+1)
, using Proposition
3.3
and Lemma
3.2
.

Fix an arbitrary
T>0
. Define

\displaystyle M:=\left(E(0)+CT\right)e^{CT}.

(3.21)

Choose

\displaystyle K:=(4M+1)^{1/2},

(3.22)

so that
K^{2}=4M+1\geq 4E(0)+1
. By Lemma
3.2
, there exists a unique local strong solution to equation (
2.7
) on
[0,T_{0}]
, where, due to (
3.12
),

T_{0}=\min\left\{\frac{1}{4C_{0}},\frac{1}{C_{1}}\log 2\right\}
, with
C_{0}=|h(0)|^{2}|\Gamma|
and
C_{1}=\max\left\{2L_{K}^{2},1\right\}
. Note that
T_{0}
depends on
K
.

From Proposition
3.3
, we obtain

\displaystyle E(t)\leq\left(E(0)+CT_{0}\right)e^{CT_{0}}\leq M,\quad\text{for all }t\in[0,T_{0}],

(3.23)

provided
T_{0}\leq T
, by using (
3.21
).

Next, we extend the strong solution from
T_{0}
to
2T_{0}
. Using the same
K
as in (
3.22
), we have
K^{2}=4M+1\geq 4E(T_{0})+1
by (
3.23
). Applying Lemma
3.2
again, we extend the solution to
[T_{0},2T_{0}]
. Repeating this process iteratively, we extend the solution up to time
T
. The key observation is that, at each extension step, the same
K
is used, and the energy remains bounded by
M
before time
T
. Since
T
is arbitrary, the strong solution is global in time. This completes the proof of the global existence of a strong solution under the assumption that
h\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
such that
|h(s)|\leq c(|s|+1)
.

In conclusion, we have proved Theorem
2.3
, that is, the local and global well-posedness of strong solutions
to equation (
2.7
) and to system (
1.1
) as well.

\displaystyle(w_{t}(t)+\gamma u(t),D_{h}w(t))_{\Gamma}-(w_{1}+\gamma u(0),D_{h}w(0))_{\Gamma}-\int_{0}^{t}(w_{t}(\tau),(D_{h}w)_{t}(\tau)_{\Gamma}d\tau

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt-\int_{0}^{t}(\gamma u(\tau),(D_{h}w)_{t}(\tau))_{\Gamma}d\tau+\int_{0}^{t}(w(\tau),D_{h}w(\tau))_{2,\Gamma}d\tau

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Gamma}h(w(\tau))D_{h}w(\tau)d\Gamma d\tau.

(4.31)

Next we must justify passing to the limit as
h\rightarrow 0
in (
4.2.2
) and (
4.2.2
). Since
u,\,u_{t}\in C([0,t];L^{2}(\Omega))
and
w,\,w_{t}\in C([0,t];L^{2}(\Gamma))
, then as
h\rightarrow 0
, it follows from (

) that

\displaystyle D_{h}u(0)

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\frac{1}{2}u_{t}(0)\text{\,\,\,and\,\,\,}D_{h}u(t)\longrightarrow\frac{1}{2}u_{t}(t)\text{\,\,\,weakly in\,\,\,}L^{2}(\Omega),

\displaystyle D_{h}w(0)

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\frac{1}{2}w_{t}(0)\text{\,\,\,and\,\,\,}D_{h}w(t)\longrightarrow\frac{1}{2}w_{t}(t)\text{\,\,\,weakly in\,\,\,}L^{2}(\Gamma).

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\lim_{h\rightarrow 0}\Big((u_{t}(t),D_{h}u(t))_{\Omega}-(u_{1},D_{h}u(0))_{\Omega}\Big)=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\|u_{t}(t)\|_{2}^{2}-\|u_{t}(0)\|_{2}^{2}\Big),\vskip 7.22743pt\\
\lim_{h\rightarrow 0}(w_{t}(t)+\gamma u(t),D_{h}w(t))_{\Gamma}=\frac{1}{2}|w_{t}(t)|_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}(\gamma u(t),w_{t}(t))_{\Gamma},\vskip 7.22743pt\\
\lim_{h\rightarrow 0}(w_{1}+\gamma u(0),D_{h}w(0))_{\Gamma}=\frac{1}{2}|w_{t}(0)|_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}(\gamma u(0),w_{t}(0))_{\Gamma}.\\
\end{cases}

(4.32)

Also, by (
4.24
),

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}(u_{t},(D_{h}u)_{t})_{\Omega}d\tau=\int_{0}^{t}(w_{t},(D_{h}w)_{t})_{\Gamma}d\tau=0.

(4.33)

In addition, since

, then (

) yields

\displaystyle\lim_{h\rightarrow 0}\int_{0}^{t}(u,D_{h}u)_{1,\Omega}d\tau=\frac{1}{2}\left(\|u(t)\|_{1,\Omega}^{2}-\|u(0)\|_{1,\Omega}^{2}\right).

(4.34)

Similarly, we obtain

\displaystyle\lim_{h\rightarrow 0}\int_{0}^{t}(w,D_{h}w)_{2,\Gamma}d\tau=\frac{1}{2}\left(\|w(t)\|_{2,\Gamma}^{2}-\|w(0)\|_{2,\Gamma}^{2}\right).

(4.35)

In order to address the plate source, we note that
w\in C([0,t];H^{2}_{0}(\Gamma))
and thus
h(w)\in L^{2}(\Omega\times(0,t))
. It follows then from (
4.27
) that

\displaystyle\lim_{h\rightarrow 0}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Gamma}h(w)D_{h}wd\Gamma d\tau=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Gamma}h(w)w_{t}d\Gamma d\tau.

(4.36)

The trouble terms,
\int_{0}^{t}(w_{t}(\tau),\gamma D_{h}u(\tau))_{\Gamma}d\tau
and
\int_{0}^{t}(\gamma u(\tau),(D_{h}w)_{t}(\tau))_{\Gamma}d\Gamma d\tau
, are handled as follows. For all sufficiently small
h>0
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}(\gamma u(\tau),(D_{h}w)_{t}(\tau))_{\Gamma}d\tau

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt=\frac{1}{2h}\left(\int_{0}^{t}(\gamma u(\tau),w_{t}(\tau+h))_{\Gamma}d\tau-\int_{0}^{t}(\gamma u(\tau),w_{t}(\tau-h))_{\Gamma}d\tau\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt=\frac{1}{2h}\left(\int_{h}^{t}(\gamma u(\tau-h),w_{t}(\tau))_{\Gamma}d\tau-\int_{0}^{t-h}(\gamma u(\tau+h),w_{t}(\tau))_{\Gamma}d\tau\right),

(4.37)

where we have used a change of variables in (
4.2.2
) and the fact that

outside of the interval

. By rearranging the terms in (

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}(\gamma u(\tau),(D_{h}w)_{t}(\tau))_{\Gamma}d\tau=-\int_{0}^{t}(\gamma D_{h}u(\tau),w_{t}(\tau))_{\Gamma}d\tau

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt-\frac{1}{2h}\left(\int_{0}^{h}(\gamma u(\tau-h),w_{t}(\tau))_{\Gamma}d\tau-\int_{t-h}^{t}(\gamma u(\tau+h),w_{t}(\tau))_{\Gamma}d\tau\right).

(4.38)

We now utilize the continuity of
w_{t}
in the last two terms of (
4.2.2
) as follows.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2h}\int_{0}^{h}(\gamma u(\tau-h),w_{t}(\tau))_{\Gamma}d\tau=\frac{1}{2h}\int_{0}^{h}(\gamma u(0),w_{t}(\tau))_{\Gamma}d\tau

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt=\frac{1}{2h}\int_{0}^{h}(\gamma u(0),w_{t}(\tau)-w_{t}(0))_{\Gamma}d\tau+\frac{1}{2h}\int_{0}^{h}(\gamma u(0),w_{t}(0))_{\Gamma}d\tau

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt\longrightarrow\frac{1}{2}(\gamma u(0),w_{t}(0))_{\Gamma},

(4.39)

as
h\rightarrow 0
. Similarly, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2h}\int_{t-h}^{t}(\gamma u(\tau+h),w_{t}(\tau))_{\Gamma}d\tau=\frac{1}{2h}\int_{t-h}^{t}(\gamma u(t),w_{t}(\tau))_{\Gamma}d\tau

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt=\frac{1}{2h}\int_{t-h}^{t}(\gamma u(t),w_{t}(\tau)-w_{t}(t))_{\Gamma}d\tau+\frac{1}{2h}\int_{t-h}^{t}(\gamma u(t),w_{t}(t))_{\Gamma}d\tau

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt\longrightarrow\frac{1}{2}(\gamma u(t),w_{t}(t))_{\Gamma},

(4.40)

as
h\rightarrow 0
. Finally, by adding (
4.2.2
) and (
4.2.2
) and combining (
4.32
)-(
4.2.2
),
we can pass to the limit as

to obtain the energy identity (

) for weak solutions.

4.3.
Continuous Dependence on Initial Data and Uniqueness of Weak Solutions

In this subsection, we prove that weak solutions continuously depend on the initial data. As a corollary, we also obtain the uniqueness of weak solutions.

Let
U_{0}=(u_{0},w_{0},u_{1},w_{1})\in H
, where
H=H^{1}_{\Gamma_{0}}(\Omega)\times H^{2}_{0}(\Gamma)\times L^{2}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Gamma)
.

Assume that
\{U^{n}_{0}=(u^{n}_{0},w^{n}_{0},u^{n}_{1},w^{n}_{1})\}
is a sequence of initial data in
H
that satisfies

\displaystyle U^{n}_{0}\longrightarrow U_{0}\text{ in }H,\text{ as }n\rightarrow\infty.

(4.41)

Let
\{(u^{n},w^{n})\}
and
(u,w)
be the weak solutions to (
1.1
) in the sense of Definition
2.5
, corresponding to the initial data
\{U^{n}_{0}\}
and
\{U_{0}\}
, and define the quadratic energy
E^{n}(t)
for a weak solution
(u^{n},w^{n})
by

\displaystyle E^{n}(t):=\frac{1}{2}\left(\|u^{n}(t)\|_{1,\Omega}^{2}+\|w^{n}(t)\|_{2,\Gamma}^{2}+\|u^{n}_{t}(t)\|_{2}^{2}+|w^{n}_{t}(t)|_{2}^{2}\right).

(4.42)

Similarly to the work in subsection
4.1
on approximate solutions,
we can choose a
K
large enough so that
K^{2}\geq 4E(0)+1
and
K^{2}\geq 4E^{n}(0)+1
for all
n
, and further establish a uniform interval of existence

for
(u,w)
,
(u^{n},w^{n})
independent of

, such that

for all
t\in[0,T_{0}]
.

Next define
y^{n}(t):=u(t)-u^{n}(t)
,
z^{n}(t):=w(t)-w^{n}(t)
, and

\displaystyle\tilde{E}_{n}(t):=\frac{1}{2}\left(\|y^{n}(t)\|_{1,\Omega}^{2}+\|z^{n}(t)\|_{2,\Gamma}^{2}+\|y^{n}_{t}(t)\|_{2}^{2}+|z^{n}_{t}(t)|_{2}^{2}\right),

(4.43)

for
t\in[0,T_{0}]
. As
(u_{n},w_{n})
is a weak solution satisfying the variational identities (
5
) and (
5
), we have by construction that
y^{n}
and

satisfy:

\displaystyle(y^{n}_{t}(t),\phi(t))_{\Omega}-(y^{n}_{t}(0),\phi(0))_{\Omega}-\int_{0}^{t}(y^{n}_{t}(\tau),\phi_{t}(\tau))_{\Omega}d\tau+\int_{0}^{t}(y^{n}(\tau),\phi(\tau))_{1,\Omega}d\tau

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt-\int_{0}^{t}(z^{n}_{t}(\tau),\gamma\phi(\tau))_{\Gamma}d\tau=0,

(4.44)

and

\displaystyle(z^{n}_{t}(t),\psi(t))_{\Gamma}-(z^{n}_{t}(0),\psi(0))_{\Gamma}-\int_{0}^{t}(z^{n}_{t}(\tau),\psi_{t}(\tau))_{\Gamma}d\tau+(\gamma y^{n}(t),\psi(t))_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt-(\gamma y^{n}(0),\psi(0))_{\Gamma}-\int_{0}^{t}(\gamma y^{n}(\tau),\psi_{t}(\tau)_{\Gamma}d\tau+\int_{0}^{t}(z^{n}(\tau),\psi(\tau))_{2,\Gamma}d\tau

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Gamma}(h(w(\tau))-h(w^{n}(\tau)))\psi(\tau)d\Gamma d\tau,

(4.45)

for all
t\in[0,T_{0}]
and for all test functions
\phi
and
\psi
as described in Definition
2.5
. Let
\phi(\tau)=D_{h}y^{n}(\tau)
in (
4.3
) and
\psi(\tau)=D_{h}z^{n}(\tau)
in (
4.3
) for
\tau\in[0,t]
where the difference quotients
D_{h}y^{n}
and
D_{h}z^{n}
are defined in (
4.21
). Using a similar argument as in obtaining the energy identity for weak solutions in subsection
4.2
, we can pass to the limit as
h\longrightarrow 0
and deduce

\displaystyle\tilde{E}_{n}(t)=\tilde{E}_{n}(0)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Gamma}(h(w(\tau))-h(w^{n}(\tau)))z^{n}_{t}(\tau)d\Gamma d\tau,

for all

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Gamma}(h(w(\tau))-h(w^{n}(\tau)))z^{n}_{t}(\tau)d\Gamma d\tau

\displaystyle\tilde{E}_{n}(t)\leq\tilde{E}_{n}(0)+C(K)\int_{0}^{t}\tilde{E}_{n}(\tau)d\tau,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\tilde{E}_{n}(t)\leq\tilde{E}_{n}(0)e^{C(K)T_{0}},\end{split}

4.4.
Global Weak Solutions

In this subsection, we address the proof of global weak solutions when the source term is bounded by a linear function. We will use the strategy seen in
[
9
,
18
,
28
]
and other works. It is the case here that either a given weak solution

must exist globally in time or else one may find a value of

, so that

\displaystyle\limsup_{t\to T_{0}^{-}}E(t)=+\infty,

(4.49)

where

is the quadratic energy. We aim to show that (

|h(s)|\leq c(|s|+1)
. This assertion is contained in the following proposition, whose proof is identical to the proof of Proposition
3.3
for strong solutions, as the energy identity remains valid for weak solutions.

Proposition 4.3
.

Assume
h\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
such that
|h(s)|\leq c(|s|+1)
for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
, where

is a constant. Let
(u,w)
be a weak solution of (
1.1
) on

. Then the quadratic energy

satisfies

\displaystyle E(t)\leq\left(E(0)+Ct\right)e^{Ct},\text{ for all}\;\;t\in[0,T].

5.
Global Existence of Potential Well Solutions

5.1.
Local Behavior of Potential Well Solutions

To begin our proof of Theorem
2.10
, we must first establish the local behavior of potential well solutions with initial data in
\mathscr{W}_{1}
. Indeed, we have the following proposition.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
h
satisfy Assumption
2.7
. Assume
(u_{0},w_{0})\in\mathscr{W}_{1}
with
\mathscr{E}(0)<d
. Let
(u(t),w(t))
be the local weak solution to (
1.1
) on
[0,T_{0}]
. Then
(u(t),w(t))\in\mathscr{W}_{1}
for all
t\in[0,T_{0}]
.

Proof.

Let
(u(t),w(t))
be local weak solutions pertaining to initial data
(u_{0},w_{0})\in\mathscr{W}_{1}
with
\mathscr{E}(0)<d
.
Note from (
2.24
) and (
2.25
), we have that

\displaystyle\mathscr{J}(u(t),w(t))\leq\mathscr{E}(t)=\mathscr{E}(0)<d,

(5.1)

and thus
(u(t),w(t))\in\mathscr{W}
for all
t\in[0,T_{0}]
, by (
2.16
).

Assume by way of contradiction that there exists a time
t_{1}\in[0,T_{0}]
such that
(u(t_{1}),w(t_{1}))\notin\mathscr{W}_{1}
. But as
(u(t_{1}),w(t_{1}))\in\mathscr{W}
and
\mathscr{W}=\mathscr{W}_{1}\cup\mathscr{W}_{2}
, we can conclude that
(u(t_{1}),w(t_{1}))\in\mathscr{W}_{2}
.

We next wish to show the continuity on
[0,T_{0}]
of the map

\displaystyle t\mapsto\int_{\Gamma}h(w(t))w(t)d\Gamma.

(5.2)

To start, note that

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle\Big|h(w(t))w(t)-h(w(t_{0}))w(t_{0})\Big|d\Gamma

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Gamma}|h(w(t))-h(w(t_{0}))||w(t)|d\Gamma+\int_{\Gamma}|h(w(t_{0}))||w(t)-w(t_{0})|d\Gamma.

(5.3)

Since
w\in C([0,T_{0}];H^{2}_{0}(\Gamma))
, there exists a constant
M>0
such that

\displaystyle|w(t)|_{\infty}\leq C|\Delta w(t)|_{2}\leq M,\;\;\text{for all}\;\;t\in[0,T_{0}].

(5.4)

Utilizing that
h\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
and the mean value theorem, as well as (
5.4
), we can write

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle|h(w(t))-h(w(t_{0}))||w(t)|d\Gamma

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Gamma}\max_{|s|\leq M}|h^{\prime}(s)||w(t)-w(t_{0})||w(t)|d\Gamma

\displaystyle\leq C|w(t)-w(t_{0})|_{2}|w(t)|_{2}.

(5.5)

Also with Hölder’s inequality, we can write

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma}|h(w(t_{0}))||w(t)-w(t_{0})|d\Gamma\leq|h(w(t_{0}))|_{2}|w(t)-w(t_{0})|_{2}.

(5.6)

Combining (
5.1
) and (
5.1
)-(
5.6
), we conclude

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle\Big|h(w(t))w(t)-h(w(t_{0}))w(t_{0})\Big|d\Gamma\leq C\left(|w(t)|_{2}+|h(w(t_{0}))|_{2}\right)|w(t)-w(t_{0})|_{2}.

(5.7)

As
w\in C([0,T_{0}];H^{2}_{0}(\Gamma))
, then
w\in C([0,T_{0}];L^{2}(\Gamma))
. Thus, we can let
t
approach
t_{0}
in (
5.7
) and conclude that

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma}h(w(t))w(t)d\Gamma\longrightarrow\int_{\Gamma}h(w(t_{0}))w(t_{0})d\Gamma

(5.8)

as
t\rightarrow t_{0}
. This yields the desired continuity of (
5.2
). Therefore, the map

\displaystyle t\mapsto\|\nabla u(t)\|_{2}^{2}+|\Delta w(t)|_{2}^{2}-\int_{\Gamma}h(w(t))w(t)d\Gamma

is continuous on
[0,T_{0}]
.

Considering that
(u(0),w(0))\in\mathscr{W}_{1}
and
(u(t_{1}),w(t_{1}))\in\mathscr{W}_{2}
, we can conclude by the continuity of (

) that there exists a time

such that

\displaystyle\|\nabla u(s)\|_{2}^{2}+|\Delta w(s)|_{2}^{2}=\int_{\Gamma}h(w(s))w(s)d\Gamma.

(5.10)

Let
t^{*}
be the supremum of all

Consider briefly the possibility of

Note now that
(u(t),w(t))\in\mathscr{W}_{2}
for all

allows us to conclude from the definition of

that

\displaystyle\|\nabla u(t)\|_{2}^{2}+|\Delta w(t)|_{2}^{2}<\int_{\Gamma}h(w(t))w(t)d\Gamma

(5.11)

for all

)-(

5.2.
Extension in Time of Potential Well Solutions

In this final subsection we look to prove that under the hypotheses of Proposition

To this end, note from (

Substituting (

Moreover, by assuming additional regularity of the initial data, i.e.,
