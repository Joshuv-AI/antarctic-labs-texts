Dilemma to conform or to come out: an in-depth exploration with sexual minority women
Authors: Dhammasagar Ujagare, Seema Sahay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSexual Minority Women (SMW) in India remain underrepresented in research and policy. Globally, coming out is considered a significant milestone among gender and sexual minority individuals, highlighting the importance of the social and psychological significance. However, there is a paucity of data on how SMW negotiate this disclosure within India. Therefore, a qualitative study was conducted to understand the coming out process of SMW in India.MethodsIn Maharashtra (India), a total of twenty-nine (29) interviews (19 in-depth and 10 key informant interviews) were conducted, between 23 May 2022 and 27 February 2024, which were either face-to-face or telephonic. Participants were recruited purposively through LGBTQ NGOs in which the snowball sampling method was applied. On the other hand, data were audio-recorded, transcribed, translated ad verbatim into English, and analysed using NVivo14 software. Principles of the grounded theory were employed to identify themes within the narratives.Results‘Hiding feelings as SMW’ captured participants’ struggles with guilt, shame, and fear of rejection, often resulting in secrecy and concealment of identity. Further, the deeply rooted cultural and patriarchal expectations constrained identity disclosure among participants. However, social support from close peers and the LGBTQ community emerged as an identity-affirming and validating factor for participants and for selective disclosure. Presence of corrective rape and treatment from a psychiatrist and family was voiced by participants when identity was disclosed or discovered. The limited impact of decriminalization of Section 377, economic dependency, and the role of social media as both affirming and risky were highlighted.Conclusion‘Coming out’ for SMW in India emerged as a continuous negotiation rather than a singular event. Hidden feelings of being an SMW, cultural pressures, and structural limitations hinder identity disclosure; support from peers, social media, and financial independence were the enabling factors. Recognizing these contexts is critical for developing queer-affirmative health, policy, and community interventions that address the invisibility of SMW in India. The impact of Section 377 decriminalization in the study shows that legal reforms and social reforms to break the patriarchal structures are essential to help SMW become visible. Furthermore, legal reform to protect SMW from family to community level is recommended.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04483-9.

Introduction

“What I see is when a girl is attracted to other girl, then where/ to whom she should share? From here the questions start.” [KII-01, LGBTQ representative].

“What I see is when a girl is attracted to other girl, then where/ to whom she should share? From here the questions start.” [KII-01, LGBTQ representative].

The Lesbian, Gay, Bisexual, Transgender, and Queer (LGBTQ) individuals are more likely to experience adverse mental, physical, sexual, and reproductive health outcomes due to marginalization, stigma, and discrimination based on gender and sexual identity [1]. Compared to heterosexual or cisgender counterparts, LGBTQ individuals report high rates of emotional distress, depression, anxiety, suicidal ideation/ attempts, self-harm, substance use, and barriers in accessing health care services [2–5].

Globally, several studies have been conducted in the LGBTQ domain; however, experiences of sexual and gender minorities other than gay men are mostly overlooked by dominant narratives centered on gay men [6–8]. Under the LGBTQ spectrum, sexual minority women (SMW), viz., lesbian, bisexual, and queer women, face unique challenges due to the intersection of gender and sexual identity, leading to mental and behavioral health risks [9, 10]. The SMW face compounded structural and psychological challenges that are rooted in patriarchal norms that marginalize women’s voices. The minority stress model of Meyers [11] explains the process of stigma, discrimination, and prejudice contributing to chronic stress, leading to internalized stigma, fear of rejection, and concealment of identity in a heteronormative context.

In India, homosexuality was criminalized under Section 377 of the Indian Penal Code (IPC), and this promoted a restricted view of sexuality in the country, even though in historical texts/ art, non-heteronormative relationships have been depicted [12, 13]. This decriminalization of homosexuality in September 2018 was a significant milestone, social stigma and discrimination continue to hinder open discussions on sexuality and gender identity in India [14]. Consequently, in India, SMW are more subjected to family and community control and surveillance. Therefore, SMW experience a double burden of marginalization, due to the intersection of gender inequalities faced by women and stigma associated with their non-heterosexual orientation [15].

The identity formations among marginalized communities are shaped by social, cultural, legal, and socioeconomic contexts [16, 17]. Once an SMW realizes her orientation and becomes aware of her identity which is a slow and multi-stage process, the next step is that of ‘coming out’; often regarded as a crucial event for identity development in the Western world [18]. Studies from west have confirmed that coming out is a continuous process of identity negotiation and selective disclosure rather than a single event [17, 19]. Initial coming out is reportedly difficult and multi-dimensional which often culminates into many changes in the life of LGBTQ individuals due to overarching socio-cultural and individual issues [20–24]. It has been reported that rejection from family makes the community more closeted and connected with each other, and this strengthens resilience among them as they stay united [24, 25]; however, these are the reports from western world where networking with their own sub-cultures seems possible. A study from India indicates parental validation and approachability in whom one wants to disclose to be critical for coming out [26].

India, being a country of strong traditions with its own patriarchal and cultural norms, LGBTQ individuals especially SMW, face unique and strong challenges. Even in western world, SMW’s needs, concerns, and identities remain limited in the context of social and scientific research, with their specific needs more often either ignored or assumed to be the same as women in the general population or common across the LGBTQ population [27]. For an SMW in India, these negotiations could be even more complex, as ‘coming out’ is not only a personal but also a deeply fraught decision accompanied by cultural monitoring and sanctions along with the risk of social stigma, familial rejection, and personal distress [22, 26]. As a result, many SMW in India remain hidden, not out of choice, but out of necessity. This invisibility, however, comes at a high psychological cost.

Although some emerging research has begun to explore the lived experiences of SMW in India [28–31], most studies of coming out in India have approached LGBTQ populations as a single group [22, 26], thereby overlooking the distinct trajectories, barriers, and emotional landscapes of SMW. In India, Maharashtra is a socio-economically diverse western state with big metropolitan cities such as Mumbai and Pune, along with semi-urban and rural regions. Despite the presence of LGBTQ community organizations in the state, social acceptance is uneven, particularly for SMW who face strong family control and expectations around marriage [28]. There is a paucity of focused research on the realities of the lived experiences of SMW in India, who remain hidden and not understood. We conducted a study to explore the coming out process and the needs of SMW in India.

Methodology

A total of 29 interviews, 19 in-depth interviews (IDIs) and 10 key informant interviews (KIIs) were conducted between May 2022 and February 2024. Detailed demographic information of IDI and KII participants is reported elsewhere [32]. Respondents for the IDIs were lesbian, bisexual, pansexual, and queer women, while KIIs were conducted among LGBTQ community representatives, LGBTQ NGO leaders, a college teacher, and LGBTQ health care providers. Convenient, purposive, and snowball sampling were used to recruit KII and IDI participants. This sampling method was adopted to access this hidden, vulnerable, and stigmatized population ethically while ensuring the safety of the participants. IDI participants were recruited through a combination of digital outreach, trusted non-government organizations (NGOs) working for the LGBTQ community, and through key informants, as this population is often “hidden” due to intense social stigma and safety concerns. The SMW in rural settings was not approached owing to critical sensitivity and safety reasons.

The topic guides for IDI and KII were developed specifically for this study (supplementary files). The major domain focused on SMW’s social issues, health needs, network and relationships, and their expectations. Similarly, the KII guide focused on the KIs’ experience with the SMW community, their social issues, health needs, networking, and expectations, and solutions. Socio-demographic data were captured for the IDI and KII participants, which included age, gender, sexual identity, education, occupation, and income.

Recruitment

SMW is a hidden and sensitive population in India. Recruitment was done by engaging community gatekeepers, including LGBTQ NGO representatives and staff members, who trained the researchers in community literacy. Social media (Instagram) and dating apps (Tinder and Bumble) were also used to approach SMW. However, these strategies were not successful. The PhD scholar (DU), who identifies as a male researcher and does not belong to the LGBTQ community, has carried out the research. Before the study initiation, DU underwent extensive training in working with LGBTQ communities, including prior engagement in research with men who have sex with men (MSM) and transgender women in the institute. However, as directed by the institutional ethics committee, IDIs were conducted by master level women interviewers (who did not identify as LGBTQ) with more than 4 years of qualitative research experience working with key populations, and were trained and supervised by DU and the PhD guide (SS) to work with the sensitive population. The interviewer was accompanied by a note taker with similar training and qualifications. Interviews were conducted in English and the local language as per the choice of the participant. In addition to KIIs conducted by DU, for a few IDIs, DU served as a note taker after obtaining permission from the participants.

Before the initiation of the interview, participants were informed by DU that the interviewers were trained to work with sensitive and vulnerable populations, had master’s level qualifications, and were GCP trained. IDIs and KIIs were conducted in-person (face-to-face) or virtually, i.e., telephonically or through Zoom meetings, as per the participants’ request, in a confidential setting, convenient to the participants. No pictures were taken during the interview. The IDIs and KIIs lasted for about 40–170 and 30–90 mins, respectively. Data were continuously reviewed by DU for missing links, gaps, and any change in participants’ narratives over the period of time. Three repeat interviews were conducted with two IDI participants and a key informant participant for the missing information. During the interviews, grounded theory of data collection was applied, and subsequently, paraphrasing and summarizing were done. In addition, data collection was informed continuously by the information collected from each participant. Memos and field notes were used to examine the assumptions and power dynamics at play in the research process. DU, who is fluent in all three languages, transcribed the audio-recorded interviews in Marathi or English verbatim using GoldWave software and then translated verbatim into English. Efforts were made to preserve phrases and metaphors as closely as possible. The translated transcripts were thoroughly reviewed by DU and SS to ensure accuracy and clarity. All the audio-recorded files and identifying forms were stored separately, with access only to DU and SS. Data were analysed using grounded theory principles [33]. Initially, open coding was employed to break the data into smaller units, allowing the emergence of new concepts and categories directly from the data. Intercoder reliability checks were conducted to reduce the influence of researchers’ assumptions on the findings. The data were managed and analyzed using NVivo 14 software. The emerging themes were organized using a life-course perspective and a socio-ecological framework to bring conceptual clarity.

The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of ICMR-National AIDS Research Institute (ICMR-NARI), now renamed as ICMR-National Institute of Translational Virology & AIDS Research (ICMR-NITVAR). Written informed consent was obtained from all the participants before conducting and audio recording the interview.

Definitions/ terms used

In this study, the term ‘Sexual Minority Women’ (SMW) refers to women who are assigned female at birth and self-identify themselves as lesbian, bisexual, queer, or any identity or label other than cis-woman, straight, or heterosexual women.

Results

Of the 17 key informants approached, 10 consented and participated. Of the 23 SMW participants approached, 19 participants consented and completed interviews. Two of the SMW were rejected because of the conflict in their names, owing to which the correct identity could not be established. Brief description of the study participants is presented in Table 1. The IDI respondents’ ages ranged from 23 to 72 years; all were literate, and more than three-fourths of the participants reported completing a bachelor’s degree. Most of the IDI (15/19) participants were unmarried; 7/19 reported living with romantic partner and 5/10 of the KIIs were married (data not shown in the table). There were seven students.

The analytical framework highlights that coming out happens at multiple stages of life and is shaped by multiple layers of social context. The life-course approach illustrates the awareness of same-sex attraction at adolescence and at early adulthood and is followed by concealment of identity, internalized stigma, and mental health issues. Additionally, forced marriages and no safe place for expression lead SMW to live in negotiated identities. However, achieving financial independence, employment, and support from peers emerged as a critical turning point for disclosure among SMW. Throughout the life-course, the participants reported having mental health issues and a need for support.

The socio-ecological approach complements the framework by illustrating how experiences at different levels interact with life course stages. At the individual level, concealment of identity, internalized stigma, lack of awareness, and mental health concerns influence self-acceptance. At the interpersonal level, non-acceptance of identity, expectation, and pressure for marriage, and support from peers influence the disclosure among SMW. At the community level, LGBTQ peer networks, support groups, digital spaces, and representation of SMW in media offer validation to identity, promoting selective disclosure. At the institutional level, such as work place, educational, and healthcare settings, non-affirming behaviors and judgmental attitudes hinder coming out. At the structural and societal level, heteronormativity, patriarchal norms, and the limited impact of Section 377 continue to constrain visibility of SMW and forcing few of them to live in negotiated identities.

The emerging themes captured the multi-layered dimensions of the coming out process, which intersect and overlap with each other: (i) Hiding feelings as SMW, (ii) Mental Health issues and needs, (iii) Questioning heterosexual norm in the context of marriage, (iv) Loneliness: finding space and addressing with support groups, (v) Social/ electronic media: usage by SMW, (vi) Financial independence provides a safety net, (vii) Section 377: Everyday challenges despite legal reform.

Hiding feelings as SMW

(Individual level; early stage, in adolescence)

The theme elaborates the coming out journey among SMW, which begins long before the disclosure of identity, mostly during early adolescence.

“So, from the beginning, yeah, so at the age, when I was in sixth standard, when I realized, I might be into women [/attracted to women/].” [IDI-23, lesbian woman, IT sector, 30yrs].

“So, from the beginning, yeah, so at the age, when I was in sixth standard, when I realized, I might be into women [/attracted to women/].” [IDI-23, lesbian woman, IT sector, 30yrs].

This early awareness of identity was often described as confusing and emotionally challenging, with deep personal, internal struggles to understand the sexual needs and identity within a heteronormative environment. The implicit and probably explicit shame led to feelings of regret, resulting in guilt regarding their sexual identity. The self-blame culminated into internalizing negative labels, seeing themselves as ‘pervert’ or ‘abnormal’ or ‘deviant’. The lack of an accurate source of information and support further added to the void that SMW struggles with, and forcing them to keep their feelings hidden and keep themselves invisible over time.

“So, when I first realized, I had a crush on a woman… I started to see myself as a pervert.” [IDI-01, pansexual woman, therapist, 25yrs].

“So, when I first realized, I had a crush on a woman… I started to see myself as a pervert.” [IDI-01, pansexual woman, therapist, 25yrs].

The need to talk with someone who could affirm or at least guide, seemed to be universal across all strata of society. An SMW from low socio-economic strata echoed similar feelings and needs as her counterparts in higher socio-economic strata spoke of:

“So that time slowly I started to feel that something was different, but who should I be telling this? I was not getting it, and the place where I am staying means the place where I was brought up, is a slum area. There, I cannot share anything like this with anybody, nor could anyone give me guidance, who would tell me properly that all these things are there. [IDI-02, lesbian woman, student, 22yrs]

The inner struggles to understand one’s feelings kept emerging throughout the study. Keeping a secret of feeling of being ‘not normal’ can be very isolating.

“I just thought it was a deviant nature or something like a deviant in the sense, not normal… Everyone talks about it, but nobody knows what [/it/] really is. I didn’t know what it really was.” [IDI-27, lesbian woman, student, 22yrs].

The feeling of being different, especially ‘sexually different’, is concealed to avoid rejection and embarrassment. A key informant shared about blaming herself. These feelings became a major barrier to coming out.

“There’s always been the stigma and prejudices [respondent coughing], which have contributed to the guilt and the self-recrimination and probably avoidance of actually expressing oneself.” [KII-07, Queer affirmative psychiatrist].

The constant pressure about same-sex relationships, that too among women, is a challenge perhaps because there is no space for female sexuality in cultural and social spaces. Burdens of being a woman and SMW are described. She brings out the gendered nature of ‘being a woman’ who has the additional burden of ‘her orientation’ and her utter loneliness about her inability to share her situation with even a person as close as her mother. She feels claustrophobic all the time, leading to mental health issues.

“If a girl is attracted to other girl… the ‘Ghusmat’ [/suffocation/] she has inside she keeps it deep within, and she keeps on living her life; already whatever problems girls have, they don’t want them to be gone outside the four walls of the house. Because the father-daughter relation is distant, or even if it is the mother, the mother doesn’t know this scientific knowledge, so firstly, where she can open up/share about this suffocation that she has inside, she doesn’t understand that.” [KII-01, LGBTQ community representative].

The conflict of an SMW in a heteronormative environment shuts doors to ‘coming out’. Locus of control for a woman’s sexuality is not with the woman herself. SMW, as any other woman, is expected to be subservient, subjugate, and therefore, lacks autonomy. The lessons in patriarchy and relinquishing her own autonomy day by day start at an early stage of a girl’s life, leaving no space for any alternative, as shared by a psychologist below:

“So like the pressure starts from even before the person has come out and say that ‘hey! I am not a heterosexual person; I am a lesbian or a gay person’. Because, it’s like a small certain thing which are been told in the families, let’s say that okay stuff like you are somebody else’s property [/a woman is a man’s property/], like you have to go to your husband’s place eventually. So, it is ingrained that you must marry a man in the end. Right? So, stuff like these, imagine for a child being brought up in a family! She is still debating what her identity is, and then, the family gives subtle indications. I am terrified of coming out to my family now, because I am seeing that you are not even accepting that there is an alternate way of being. It’s not just one way, one set way, so my chances of coming out stops over there.” [KII-04, Psychologist].

This internalized feeling of pressure against one’s own orientation, dilemma about feelings, guilt, being wrong, and societal stigma acted as a barrier to self-acceptance, culminating into poor mental health.

Mental health issues and needs

(Individual and institutional levels; adolescence to ongoing stage)

Participants highlighted that hiding their identity for a long period had a significant impact on their mental health and well-being.“So then, so of course, then I decided not to come out to anyone for the longest time and to seem normal in other’s eyes. I also started participating in such a homophobic banter and all of that.” [IDI-21, lesbian woman, IT sector, LLB graduate, 34yrs].“I was in really bad depression, where I didn’t see, like, I felt hopeless about everything.” [IDI-09, Pansexual women, pub hostess, 24yrs].

The intense need to confirm one’s feelings about orientation was shared by a senior 72 year old, late bloomer married SMW. The stigmatizing terminologies were deterrent to ‘coming out’. She hid her orientation for a long period, but from within, she really wanted to share it and wanted her orientation validated by others. The inner dilemma and need to talk about it has been shared as follows:“When I was studying in my post-graduation, there was a couple, and everyone used to call her [/them/], ‘oh, these are the cooing pigeons,’ and we knew what that meant. So, when you hear such comments, it’s even harder for someone who identifies with them to really say, well, even I am one of them. You just recede into silence and then, you feel, you are on the wrong side. It’s hard for me to say that it was the first time. Because I think the realization that one has [/realization that I am SMW/] also needs to be supported by others. So that shows [/gives/] the validity of who you are.” [IDI-10, lesbian woman, social worker, 72yrs].

To overcome these issues, accessing healthcare is the solution. However, participants reported that accessing healthcare becomes a challenge for them due to healthcare providers’ limited knowledge, awareness, and lack of sensitivity towards SMW and their health needs. Participants pointed out that even after Section 377 was decriminalized, non-acceptance of LGBTQ identities among the Health Care Providers (HCPs) persists. The values of the cis community take a very dominant role when an SMW opens to an HCP. An SMW participant narrated her experience of being stereotyped by the HCP without understanding the issues being faced or felt by his/her SMW patient.“Then he [/doctor/] asked me who I was? [/Who are you? A man or a woman?/] Then I told them this is the identity [/I shared my identity/]. So, they [/doctor/] replied this is not allowed in our India [/in our country], your family will not support you, or this all may be for the short term, like this doctor suggested to me.” [IDI-02, lesbian woman, student, 22yrs].

Further, SMW who came out to their families, willingly or through accidental disclosure, reported the denial in acceptance of identity because of the heteronormativity embedded in the society. Participants reported families trying to ‘fix’ or ‘cure’ them through conversion therapies or through quacks or HCPs. Lack of affirmative understanding, combined with heteronormative assumptions in clinical practice and patriarchal norm emerged. An SMW narrated her parents engaging with medical professionals, for ‘conversion therapy’.“So, that, basically, according to him [/uncle/], whatever I am, it’s not normal. And, that he doesn’t condone it. So, the psychiatrist that my uncle took me to, those sessions continued for many years. At least one and a half years. So, every time there was a session, I used to come to Pune. My mom also used to come. So it got very confusing. Throughout, the psychiatrist [/tried to cure/], also she [/mother/] once took me to some Tantrik [/occultist/], you know, to cure me or whatever she thought.” [IDI-21, lesbian woman, IT sector, LLB graduate, 34yrs].

Discussion

Situation in India is long way to go with the existence of violence like corrective rape, and shock therapies. This study highlights the experiences of sexual minority women and the challenges faced while navigating through a society structured around heterosexual norms in India. This study addresses a critical gap in the literature, as there are very few studies in India focusing on the experiences of SMW. Findings reveal sexuality-based discrimination, particularly at the social and family level, which significantly affects SMW’s mental health and wellbeing.

Invisibility, marginalization, and inequity

The inequities observed in this study are consistent with the global literature showing that sexual minorities experience marginalization across different social and cultural settings [34–37]. The study highlights the social invisibility of SMW, which is the result of SMW being scared, non-accepted, and therefore, they tend to hide their sexual orientation. This leads to multiple psychological issues and life course events. SMW cannot get any community support as such communities are either not visible or inaccessible due to lack of mobility among SMW owing to social restrictions. The lack of support enhances with the lack of economic independence, and they face strict family restrictions. Compared to MSM, SMW face both health and social invisibility in India [29]. MSM are more visible because of strong collectivization and activism within their communities. In addition, socially, SMW do not have agency within the strong patriarchal norms, and they are regulated closely by their families. Finally, forced into marriage, SMW are rendered invisible while sometimes they live in negotiated identities if they can get into ‘lavender marriage’. The lavender marriage between individuals with heterosexual and homosexual orientations is carried out for non-romantic purposes, such as maintaining social image, meeting family demands, or avoiding social stigma [38]. Other reasons for invisibility identified were protective strategies against forced rapes or conversion therapies perpetrated or forced by their own families. In a study by Fernandez and Gomathy, similar reports on violence and corrective practices faced by lesbian women within the family and social setting, reinforcing their marginalization in heteronormative contexts were reported [39]. Our findings highlight the need for urgent actions by the legal, community and women's organizations to investigate corrective rapes among SMW which should be dealt under IPC 376. The girls and the women may be educated and made aware of the legal provisions that can protect them from any kind of rape, even if the perpetrator is from the family. Anti-discrimination laws extending to the deeper levels of the private sector and comprehensive social transformation to dismantle the patriarchal structures that govern SMW’s state in the country are recommended.

Coming out: dilemma and negotiations

The dilemma in coming out is ambiguous in nature. Many SMW continue to live in negotiated identity; for them it might not be a universal outcome, it is a careful and context- dependent decision shaped by safety, family acceptance, and their life situations. ‘Lavender marriage’ is an example of remaining in negotiated identity to conform to the social expectation.

Participants reported that same-sex attraction among women remains unacknowledged and prohibitive. This invisibility prolongs the psychological isolation. The process of identity negotiation narrated by participants echoes Cass’s ‘Homosexual Identity Development Model,’ which conceptualizes identity development as not a single event, but a continuous and selective process, depending on social safety and perceived risk [23]. This aligns with cross-cultural research showing that in collectivist societies, disclosure highly depends in the socio-cultural context of family and community acceptance [24, 40].

The need for visibility and inclusivity was voiced by all the SMW in the study. Those who could fight invisibility, ‘coming out’ was the step that was not a one-time event, but rather a continuous process shaped by intersecting factors of stigma and patriarchy at personal, cultural, and community levels. This ‘coming out’ process emerged as an evolving process through the life course of an SMW, with early identity awareness, concealment of identity, mental health issues, the issue of marriage, and disclosure at a later stage.

Non- disclosure of identity was a protective strategy to avoid family conflicts, identity rejection and forced marriages. The process of coming out was strongly influenced by socio-cultural factors such as lack of autonomy, family control, expectations of heterosexual marriage, patriarchal norms, and economic dependence, which acted as major barriers to disclosure. Participants reported using different strategies such as hiding/ concealment of identity, selective disclosure, and not to disclose until having financial independence. When SMW reported being dependent on family for financial support, silence was described as a survival strategy against fear of rejection, abandonment, forced marriage, or lack of independence in decision-making. This aligns with the findings that non-disclosure acts as a protective strategy for LGBTQ individuals until they achieve financial stability [22, 26, 41].

The socio-ecological framework further highlighted that individual experiences were shaped not only by personal factors but also by interpersonal relationships, community support, institutional, and structural factors. If they could reach the members of their subcultures, support from close friends and LGBTQ+ peers played an important role in validating and affirming their identity, leading to selective disclosure. Consistent with other studies, peer support acts as a buffer during adverse mental health, and it enhances resilience among LGBTQ communities [42]. However, in contrast to the western situations [24, 25], in India collectivization of SMW is a challenge. These highlight the need for strengthening peer networks and support systems among SMW.

Further, the participants described digital platforms as both empowering and risky. Online spaces facilitated SMW in identity exploration, affirmation, and networking, often providing the first site of belonging and seeing oneself as ‘visible being’. However, the risks related to bullying, monitoring by family members and friends, and unintended disclosure emerged as prominent concerns. These uncertainties are evident in global research [43–45]. For SMW in India, digital spaces appear particularly significant given the lack of offline visibility and cultural/ heteronormative norms. This underscores the need for robust protections and the development of safe, moderated online communities tailored towards SMW.

The lack of representation of SMW in mainstream media and culture, intensifies the erasure of SMW, unlike MSM or transgender women, who have gained visibility through public health disclosures around HIV prevention and activism from NGOs and CBOs. The narratives of SMW participants in this study show that invisibility of SMW fosters identity confusion and reinforces the belief of one being wrong and in dilemma, leading to mental stress. Evidence of stress and suicide attempts were narrated several times.

Health care needs: mental health

Heteronormativity is widely assumed to be normal and universal [46]. This forces SMW to live in hiding, echoing findings from qualitative studies conducted among SMW in India [15, 26, 29, 30]. The internalized stigma has a significant impact on mental health outcomes among LGBTQ individuals, which has been reported globally too [5, 47, 48]. In this study, participants were aligned to Meyers’ Minority Stress Model [11]. They reported their feelings of guilt and self-blame, which were shaped by deeply rooted patriarchal and heteronormative norms within the Indian socio-cultural context.

Mental health issues such as depression, anxiety, and suicidal ideation among participants were due to concealment of identity, societal pressure, and family expectations. These findings align with global research showing that sexual minorities experience higher mental health risk compared to the heterosexual population [2, 5, 49, 50]. Indian studies similarly report elevated psychological distress among LGBTQ individuals due to stigma, discrimination, and concealment of identity [28, 30, 51, 52].

Barriers to accessing health services emerged as an important institutional-level concern. Participants reported difficulties in accessing mental health services due to a lack of knowledge, embedded heteronormativity, fear of corrective treatment, and judgmental behavior of HCPs. SMW affirmative and non-judgmental care was voiced as a major critical gap, which was also reported in a study conducted in Latin America and the Caribbean [53]. Similarly, Bowling et al. reported barriers in accessing health care related to acceptability, quality of care, and sensitivity among HCPs [29]. While disclosure was perceived as important for accessing appropriate mental health care, non-affirming behaviors of HCPs posed barriers to such disclosure. Therefore, to understand concerns of SMW, LGBTQ-affirmative and non-judgmental healthcare capacities need to be strengthened among the healthcare providers of India.

Marriage: concerns in context of patriarchal norms

The Indian culture is shaped by patriarchal norms and heteronormative expectations, which puts immense pressure on the coming out process among SMW. Participants described pressure to marry, family continuation, and conform to prescribed gender roles, mirroring findings from gender studies, which highlight the patriarchal systems that constrain women’s autonomy [26, 54]. In this patriarchal environment, deviation from heterosexual norms is not merely seen as an act of non-conformity, but it is perceived as a threat to family and social obligations/ expectations, often compelling SMW to conceal their identities and leaving SMW with little to no space to assert their desires and relationships. Participants reported forced marriages and conforming to socially expected gender roles, compelling them to live in negotiated identities, very similar to MSM [55]. The emerging solution to secure rights for same-sex couples, including SMW, has been to focus on the legal recognition of relationships for which advocacy is required. Examples of alternative legal models, such as ‘maitri karar’ (friendship contract) or gender-neutral marriage act or special marriage act 1954, should be carefully studied and legally refined to couples who face familial opposition [56, 57].

Legal challenges: collectivist culture of families

Even though Section 377 was struck down in 2018 in India, participants highlighted its limited impact on their everyday lives. SMW emphasized that it failed to address the structural issues of stigma and discrimination, lack of recognition, and exclusion. The reason could be India’s own collectivist culture where the family is the predominant, independent, and controlling micro-state for all family members [58]. All the legal rights of the family members are subordinate to social conformity. Therefore, the Section 377 was used by the administrative state-led system for harassment, but with decriminalization, no impact is observed because in the realm of strong patriarchy and collectivist culture, the SMW are still primarily monitored and negatively controlled by their families. The conversion therapies and violence shared by the SMW is result of the sovereign power of the family in India. Legal recognition of SMW relationship was an expectation to deal with these psychosocial and legal problems.

Future implications

Inclusivity of SMW in the program is essential which could be facilitated with the affirmation in the program through epidemiological and health data; for example, in the census or national surveys for health. The study findings highlights the need to adopt more inclusive approaches while studying SMW in India, particularly focusing on rural populations, individuals from diverse socio-economic backgrounds, and intersections such as caste and religion. There is a need to develop culturally sensitive and LGBTQ-inclusive mental health and healthcare interventions that address the specific needs of SMW. Health system need to acknowledge the SMW population and train health care providers in providing equitable affirmative heath care. Recognition of mental health needs of SMW is critical. Structural interventions addressing patriarchy, diversity and advocacy for the same is essential to garner support and acceptance for SMW.

Strengths and limitations

The study contributes to the SMW literature in India, brings forth the invisibility of SMW, and need for social reforms. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study in India that exclusively disseminates the facilitators and barriers regarding the ‘coming out’ process among SMW. The findings of the study are based on self-reported narratives/ experiences of SMW. Participants were recruited from Maharashtra, with most of them residing in the urban areas at the time of data collection. During the data collection period, despite ongoing legal hearings related to LGBTQ rights, we found no substantial differences observed between interviews conducted earlier and later in the study period. The sampling method was convenient and purposive to gain thick descriptions of the experiences and needs of SMW in India, who were willing to talk and share without fear. We submit our limitation that the SMW from socially isolated setting or lacking access to LGBTQ community networks or those who had belonged to rural settings, could not be recruited owing to intense stigma and safety concerns.

Conclusion

The coming out experiences of Sexual Minority Women in India are shaped by a complex interplay of psychosocial, cultural, and daily challenges. While social support, digital spaces, and financial independence offer pathways to empowerment among SMW and would help in to address issues of patriarchal norms, and heteronormativity, which continue to constrain disclosure and acceptance. The impact of Section 377 decriminalization in the study shows that legal change is only a step. True visibility for SMW requires anti-discrimination laws with the capacity to bring comprehensive social transformation to break the patriarchal structures and provide freedom to SMW. Legal reform to protect SMW from family to community level is recommended. HCPs especially psychiatrist and informal HCPs need education and affirmative care trainings.
