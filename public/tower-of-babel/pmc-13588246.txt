Analysis of Sleep Quality and Its Associated Factors in Adolescents with Migraine
Authors: Zhenyang Zheng, Yue Xiang, Shenggen Chen, Hanbin Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study was designed to investigate sleep quality and its associated factors in adolescents with migraine.MethodsSubjects were enrolled and assessed using the Pediatric Migraine Disability Assessment (PedMIDAS) questionnaire, the Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale (HAMA), the 24-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD-24), and the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) to evaluate migraine-related disability, anxiety, depression, and sleep quality.ResultsA total of 168 adolescents were enrolled, including 50 healthy controls and 118 migraine patients, of whom 25 had good sleep quality (PSQI ≤ 5) and 93 had poor sleep quality (PSQI > 5). Adolescents with migraine exhibited significantly higher HAMA, HAMD-24, and PSQI global scores than healthy controls (all p < 0.05). Within the migraine cohort, those with poor sleep quality reported significantly more monthly headache days, higher numerical rating scale (NRS) pain intensity, and elevated PedMIDAS, HAMA, and HAMD-24 scores compared to those with good sleep quality (all p < 0.05). Moreover, the PSQI global score showed statistically significant positive correlations with disease duration (r = 0.266, p = 0.004), monthly headache days (r = 0.428, p < 0.001), NRS pain intensity (r = 0.298, p = 0.001), PedMIDAS score (r = 0.580, p < 0.001), HAMA score (r = 0.513, p < 0.001), and HAMD-24 score (r = 0.462, p < 0.001). After multivariate adjustment, NRS pain intensity (OR = 2.61; 95% CI, 1.56–4.37; p < 0.001), and PedMIDAS score (OR = 1.59; 95% CI, 1.31–1.94; p < 0.001) emerged as independently associated factors of poor sleep quality in adolescents with migraine. Both HAMA and HAMD-24 scores partially mediated the association between NRS pain intensity and PSQI global score, whereas the HAMA score alone partially mediated the association between PedMIDAS score and PSQI global score.ConclusionAdolescents with migraine exhibit an elevated prevalence of comorbid anxiety disorders, depressive disorders, and sleep disturbances. Within this population, sleep quality is negatively correlated with disease duration, monthly headache frequency, pain intensity, migraine-related disability, anxiety severity, and depression severity. Furthermore, pain intensity, and migraine-related disability are independently associated with poor sleep quality. These findings underscore the clinical imperative to routinely assess and manage sleep quality in adolescents with migraine, especially those experiencing high headache frequency and severe pain intensity. Nevertheless, the relatively small sample size and class imbalance may limit the statistical power of subgroup analyses. Therefore, future longitudinal multicenter studies employing larger and more balanced samples are warranted.

Introduction

The Global Burden of Disease Study 2021 estimates that migraine affects approximately 1.16 billion individuals, or 14.7% of the global population, rendering it the leading cause of disability among neurological disorders in children and adolescents (aged 5 to 19 years).1 Although migraine in adolescence has received increasing clinical and research attention, evidence-based pharmacological and non-pharmacological interventions, including acute and preventive treatments, remain scarce, largely due to age-related physiological and developmental constraints, ethical limitations on trial enrollment, and a persistent paucity of adequately powered, adolescent-specific randomized controlled trials.2 Migraine and sleep disturbances exhibit a well-documented, bidirectional comorbidity: sleep deprivation or sleep deficit can act as a trigger for migraine attacks; restorative sleep may alleviate acute symptoms or shorten attack duration; and recurrent migraine, particularly with nocturnal onset or high attack frequency, can itself disrupt sleep architecture and circadian regulation.3,4 Individuals with migraine consistently report impaired subjective sleep quality, elevated insomnia prevalence, and a significantly higher incidence of comorbid sleep disorders.5,6 This reciprocal interaction forms a self-perpetuating cycle that substantially exacerbates functional impairment, school absenteeism, emotional distress, and overall health-related quality of life in adolescents.7 Despite its clinical relevance, the neurobiological, behavioral, and environmental determinants underlying this comorbidity in adolescents remain incompletely elucidated. Moreover, the factors associated with sleep quality in this population, particularly sociodemographic and neuropsychological correlates, have been insufficiently investigated. To address this knowledge gap, the present study employs a cross-sectional design to identify and characterize these associated factors among adolescents with migraine.

Methods

Participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted at the Headache Center of Fujian Medical University Union Hospital from January 2023 to October 2025. Participants were enrolled if they met all of the following criteria: (a) a confirmed diagnosis of migraine according to the International Classification of Headache Disorders, third edition (ICHD-3);8 (b) a documented headache history of at least one year; (c) age 14–18 years; and (d) absence of severe psychiatric disorders, active malignancy, systemic infectious diseases, rheumatic diseases, or connective tissue disorders. Healthy controls were recruited from individuals aged 14 to 18 years without a personal or first-degree family history of primary headache and absence of severe psychiatric disorders, active malignancy, systemic infectious diseases, rheumatic diseases, or connective tissue disorders, who presented for routine health examinations during the same period. All procedures involving human participants adhered strictly to the ethical principles outlined in the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments and were approved by the Ethics Committee of Fujian Medical University Union Hospital (Title of the Research Project: A Systematic Investigation of Sleep Quality Among Patients with Migraine, Ethics Approval Reference Number: 2022KY184). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants; for minors aged 14–17 years, written assent was obtained in addition to parental or legal guardian consent.

Anticipating a 20% non-response or incomplete-response rate, the final target sample size was 104 participants.

Baseline Information Collection

Demographic and clinical characteristics, including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), school grade, self-reported academic performance, regular physical activity status, analgesic utilization, family history of primary headache, aura status, migraine disease duration, monthly headache days, and pain intensity, were collected. Academic performance was categorized as “good,” “moderate,” or “poor” based on participants’ self-report relative to their peer group. Regular physical activity was defined as engaging in at least 60 minutes per day of moderate-to-vigorous intensity aerobic physical activity across the week, in accordance with the World Health Organization 2020 guidelines for adolescents.10 Analgesic utilization was defined as acute medication use during more than 50% of migraine attacks over the preceding three months. Family history of primary headache was recorded if at least one first-degree relative (parent or sibling) or second-degree relative (grandparent or aunt/uncle) had a clinician-confirmed diagnosis of migraine, tension-type headache, or another primary headache disorder. Pain intensity was assessed using the 11-point numerical rating scale (NRS), where 0 indicates “no pain” and 10 indicates “worst possible pain”.11

Neuropsychology Assessment

Migraine-related disability, anxiety, depression, and sleep quality were assessed using the Pediatric Migraine Disability Assessment (PedMIDAS) questionnaire,12 the Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale (HAMA),13 the 24-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD-24),14 and the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI),15 respectively. All assessments were administered independently by two trained physicians (Shenggen Chen and Hanbin Lin) who were blinded to participants’ clinical diagnoses, migraine characteristics, and other outcome measures. Cut-off scores indicating absence of clinically significant impairment were defined as follows: PedMIDAS score ≤ 10,12 HAMA score < 7 (maximum = 56), HAMD-24 score < 8 (maximum = 76),16 and PSQI global score ≤ 5 (maximum = 21).17 Based on the PSQI global score, adolescent participants with migraine were dichotomized into two mutually exclusive groups: those with poor sleep quality (PSQI > 5) and those with good sleep quality (PSQI ≤ 5).

Statistical Analysis

Normality of continuous variables, including age, BMI, disease duration, monthly headache days, NRS pain intensity score, PedMIDAS score, HAMA score, HAMD-24 score, and PSQI global score, was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared across groups using the independent samples t-test (for two-group comparisons) or one-way analysis of variance (for multi-group comparisons), followed by pairwise post hoc tests with the least significant difference (LSD) test where assumption of homogeneity of variance was satisfied. Non-normally distributed variables were presented as median (interquartile range, IQR) and compared across groups using the Mann–Whitney U-test (for two-group comparisons) or the Kruskal–Wallis H-test (for multi-group comparisons), followed by pairwise post hoc tests with Bonferroni correction. Categorical variables, including sex, school grade, academic performance, regular physical activity status, analgesic utilization, family history of primary headache, and aura status, were compared using the chi-square test (or Fisher’s exact test where expected cell counts were < 5). Spearman’s rank-order correlation coefficients were calculated to evaluate monotonic associations between the PSQI global score and all continuous clinical and demographic variables. Multicollinearity among candidate associated factors in regression models were evaluated using variance inflation factor (VIF) and tolerance; variables with VIF ≥ 10 or tolerance ≤ 0.1 were excluded from final models. Binary logistic regression analysis was performed to identify independent correlates of poor sleep quality; results are presented as adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Model fit and calibration were assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test (p > 0.05 indicating adequate fit), and discriminative performance was evaluated via the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Mediation analyses were conducted using PROCESS macro Model 4 (Hayes, 2017) with 5000 bootstrap resamples and 95% bias-corrected confidence intervals. A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics Version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Baseline Information

A total of 50 healthy adolescents (30 females, 20 males) served as controls, and 118 adolescents with migraine (76 females, 42 males) who met the inclusion criteria were enrolled. The analgesics used by adolescents with migraine in our study included acetaminophen, ibuprofen, diclofenac, and loxoprofen, either as monotherapies or in combination, as well as the zolmitriptan (administered as a nasal spray or orally disintegrating tablet) and rizatriptan. Notably, none of the participants met criteria of medication overuse headache (MOH). Based on the PSQI global score, the migraine group was stratified into two subgroups: 25 participants with good sleep quality and 93 with poor sleep quality (Figure 1). The overall prevalence of poor sleep quality in adolescents with migraine was 78.81%. No statistically significant differences were observed between healthy controls and adolescents with migraine in age, sex, BMI, school grade, academic performance, or regular physical activity status (all p > 0.05; Table 1). Similarly, no significant between-group differences emerged between the good- and poor-sleep-quality subgroups of adolescents with migraine regarding age, sex, BMI, school grade, academic performance, regular physical activity status, analgesic utilization, family history of primary headache, aura status, or disease duration (all p > 0.05; Table 2). Associated Factors of Poor Sleep Quality in Adolescents with Migraine

Univariate logistic regression analysis identified monthly headache days, NRS pain intensity, PedMIDAS score, HAMA score, and HAMD-24 score as statistically significant associated factors of poor sleep quality in adolescents with migraine (all p < 0.001; Table 6), whereas age, sex, BMI, school grade, academic performance, regular physical activity status, analgesic utilization, family history of primary headache, aura status, and disease duration were not significantly associated (all p > 0.05; Table 6). The variance inflation factors (VIFs) and tolerances for the five significant variables, including monthly headache days, NRS pain intensity, PedMIDAS score, HAMA score, and HAMD-24 score, were 1.154, 1.141, 2.500, 2.285, 2.370, and 0.867, 0.876, 0.400, 0.438, 0.422, respectively, confirming the absence of substantial multicollinearity among these variables. The PedMIDAS score, partially derived from monthly headache days, was included in the multivariate logistic regression model alongside three other non-redundant and significant variables: NRS pain intensity, HAMA score, and HAMD-24 score. To mitigate multicollinearity and align with clinical reasoning, these four variables were selected a priori and entered simultaneously into the model to assess their independent associations with poor sleep quality. After mutual adjustment for confounding, both NRS pain intensity (OR = 2.61; 95% CI, 1.56–4.37; p < 0.001) and PedMIDAS score (OR = 1.59; 95% CI, 1.31–1.94; p < 0.001) remained statistically significant correlates of poor sleep quality in this population (Table 7). Discussion

This study demonstrates that adolescents with migraine exhibit significantly higher HAMA, HAMD-24, and PSQI global scores compared with healthy controls. Within the migraine cohort, those with poor sleep quality reported significantly greater monthly headache days, higher NRS pain intensity, and elevated PedMIDAS, HAMA, and HAMD-24 scores relative to those with good sleep quality. Moreover, the PSQI global score showed statistically significant positive correlations with disease duration, monthly headache days, NRS pain intensity, PedMIDAS score, HAMA score, and HAMD-24 score. After multivariate adjustment, NRS pain intensity, and PedMIDAS score emerged as independently associated factors of poor sleep quality in adolescents with migraine. Both HAMA and HAMD-24 scores partially mediated the association between NRS pain intensity and PSQI global score, whereas the HAMA score alone partially mediated the association between PedMIDAS score and PSQI global score.

Our findings revealed that adolescents with migraine exhibited significantly higher PSQI global scores, indicating a high prevalence of poor sleep quality (78.81%). Individuals with migraine commonly experience co-occurring headaches and sleep disturbances, reflecting a well-documented, bidirectional pathophysiological relationship. Clinically significant sleep impairments are highly prevalent among youth with continuous headache.18 Recent epidemiological data indicate that 27% of adolescents with migraine experience insomnia;19 this comorbidity is independently associated with adverse clinical outcomes, including greater headache-related disability, increased attack frequency, higher pain intensity, and elevated symptoms of depression and anxiety.20,21 Sleep disorders are highly prevalent in children and adolescents with migraine and consistently correlate with greater headache severity and reduced responsiveness to acute pharmacotherapy.22 Even in non-clinical pediatric and adolescent populations, headache is associated with sleep disturbances, and individuals with migraine exhibit significantly poorer sleep quality, greater daytime sleepiness, and a stronger evening chronotype.23 Interictally, individuals with migraine exhibit persistent deficits in sleep quality and high prevalence of insomnia symptoms, independent of acute attacks. Critically, interventions targeting sleep improvement demonstrate parallel reductions in migraine burden, underscoring the therapeutic reciprocity between these domains. Cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia yields significant reductions in headache frequency, intensity, and disability among adults and adolescents with chronic migraine and comorbid insomnia.24 Collectively, these findings indicate that insomnia and poor sleep quality in migraine are not merely epiphenomena of acute attacks but represent stable, interictal features associated with disease progression and worsening prognosis. Sleep disruption may serve as both a precipitant and amplifier of migraine: insufficient or fragmented sleep acts as a validated trigger for attacks, particularly in susceptible individuals, and contributes to headache chronification and severity escalation.3 Conversely, migraine attacks, especially nocturnal episodes, directly fragment sleep continuity and impair restorative function.4

Moreover, adolescents with migraine exhibited significantly higher HAMA and HAMD-24 scores in this study. The comorbidity of migraine with psychological disorders, particularly major depressive disorder and generalized anxiety disorder, has been extensively documented in previous studies. A systematic review and meta-analysis reported a significantly stronger association between migraine and depression in adolescents.25 Children and adolescents with migraine exhibit a higher prevalence of anxiety and depressive symptoms compared with healthy peers.26 Population-based studies further indicate that individuals with migraine are more likely to receive a comorbid diagnosis of generalized anxiety disorder (GAD) relative to headache-free controls.27 Psychiatric assessments consistently reveal elevated depression scale scores in adolescents with migraine versus matched controls.28 Notably, migraine with aura confers a threefold increased odds of clinically significant anxiety levels in youth.29 Migraine, particularly with aura, is robustly associated with both depression and anxiety across developmental stages; moreover, adolescent migraine is independently linked to anxiety disorders, mood dysregulation, and behavioral problems.30 At the diagnostic level, migraine is associated with significantly elevated prevalence rates of major depressive disorder (MDD) and GAD, suggesting shared neurobiological and psychosocial vulnerabilities.31

We found that monthly headache frequency, pain intensity, and migraine-related disability were associated with poor sleep quality in adolescents with migraine. Consistent with our findings, increased headache frequency is associated with short sleep duration in individuals with migraine.32 Pain intensity and monthly headache days predict higher risk of insomnia, depression, and anxiety.33 Greater monthly migraine frequency correlates with poorer global sleep quality, particularly reduced sleep efficiency, and this association persists across both episodic and chronic migraine subtypes.34 Notably, multidimensional sleep health, including sleep regularity, satisfaction, alertness, timing, and duration, is inversely associated with monthly headache days among patients with episodic migraine.35 The strength of the headache-insomnia association intensifies with increasing headache frequency, severity, or psychiatric comorbidity, indicating a dose-response pattern.36 While insomnia severity consistently tracks with monthly headache days, its association with acute pain intensity appears less robust and may be mediated by affective factors.37 Paradoxically, higher NRS pain intensity predicts greater self-reported hours slept in some cohorts, potentially reflecting fatigue-driven compensatory behavior rather than restorative sleep, though these findings lack consistency across studies.38 Prospective data further show that both headache intensity and nocturnal onset timing predict incident sleep disturbances in youth with migraine over 90-day follow-up.39 Among individuals meeting criteria for probable migraine, those with poor sleep quality report significantly higher headache frequency and greater NRS pain intensity compared with those with good sleep quality.40 Poor sleep quality is prevalent across migraine subtypes but exhibits distinct clinical correlates: in episodic migraine, it is significantly associated with headache frequency and MIDAS-measured disability among undergraduate students;41 in chronic migraine, it is robustly linked to anxious symptoms, depressive symptoms, pain catastrophizing, and functional impairment, rather than headache frequency per se.42,43 Critically, poor sleep quality is independently associated with total pain burden, including pain intensity, frequency, and functional impact, as well as anxiety, depression, and reduced quality of life.44 A strong positive correlation exists between PSQI global score and MIDAS score, reinforcing the bidirectional interplay between sleep disruption and migraine-related disability.45 Finally, migraine-related disability is associated not only with PSQI global score but also with cumulative sleep disturbances. This association is partially mediated by central sensitization, the key neurobiological mechanism underlying multiple unexplained pain conditions.46

This study found that both HAMA and HAMD-24 scores partially mediated the association between NRS pain intensity and PSQI global score, whereas the HAMA score alone partially mediated the association between PedMIDAS score and PSQI global score. Consistent with prior evidence, migraine frequency exhibited a dose-response relationship with symptom severity of anxiety and depression.47 Anxiety, depression, and insomnia frequently co-occur in adolescents with migraine, not as epiphenomena, but as clinically meaningful comorbidities independently associated with greater migraine intensity and higher attack frequency.48 Furthermore, recurrent headaches, particularly migraine, were robustly linked to anxiety and depressive symptoms, with headache frequency showing a strong positive correlation with the severity of these symptoms.49 Finally, migraine frequency demonstrated a graded association with insomnia severity, and this relationship persisted after accounting for headache burden, indicating independent contributions of depression and anxiety.50

In this study, analgesic use, not medication overuse, was highly prevalent among adolescents with migraine, affecting 69.49% of participants. However, neither analgesic use, family history of primary headache, aura status, nor disease duration was significantly associated with poor sleep quality in this population. Notably, MOH, a chronic migraine complication, was positively associated with familial migraine history, higher headache frequency and intensity, sleep disturbances, and emotional stress.51 Adolescents with migraine and a positive family history were at increased risk for migraine onset,52 and in our cohort, 28.81% reported such a family history, supporting the role of genetic predisposition in migraine susceptibility. Furthermore, consistent with prior evidence,50 aura status showed no significant association with insomnia severity, suggesting that aura may represent an epiphenomenon rather than a causal driver of migraine attacks. Finally, although disease duration has been linked to cortical thickness alterations in migraine,53 no association between disease duration and sleep quality was observed in our sample.

Several limitations warrant careful consideration in interpreting the findings. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. While robust associations were observed between poor sleep quality and clinical variables, including migraine frequency, pain intensity, and psychiatric symptom severity, temporal precedence and directionality cannot be established. Prospective longitudinal studies are therefore essential to determine whether sleep disturbances precede, follow, or reciprocally exacerbate migraine burden and comorbid psychopathology. Second, the stringent inclusion and exclusion criteria, designed to minimize confounding from medication use, neurological comorbidities, and psychiatric disorders requiring active treatment, resulted in a relatively modest sample size and class imbalance, potentially limiting statistical power for subgroup analyses and detection of smaller effect sizes. Third, the control group consisted of healthy adolescents recruited from a single center during routine health examinations, a setting that inherently introduced a risk of selection bias. Although stringent inclusion criteria were applied to minimize this risk, residual selection bias remained an acknowledged limitation. Fourth, several methodological limitations warrant cautious interpretation of the findings: the single-center design restricted external validity; and certain custom metrics lacked established objective standards. Future multicenter studies with more balanced samples and larger adolescent cohorts are needed to enhance generalizability and validate these associations across heterogeneous migraine phenotypes.

Conclusion

Adolescents with migraine exhibit a significantly elevated prevalence of comorbid anxiety disorders, depressive disorders, and sleep disturbances. Within this population, PSQI global score demonstrated significant positive correlations with disease duration, monthly headache frequency, NRS pain intensity, PedMIDAS-measured disability, HAMA-assessed anxiety severity, and HAMD-24-assessed depression severity. Furthermore, NRS pain intensity and PedMIDAS score are independently associated with poor sleep quality. Both HAMA and HAMD-24 scores partially mediated the association between NRS pain intensity and PSQI global score, whereas the HAMA score alone partially mediated the association between PedMIDAS score and PSQI global score. These findings underscore the clinical imperative to routinely assess and manage sleep quality in adolescents with migraine, especially those experiencing high headache frequency and severe pain intensity. Future prospective studies should rigorously evaluate the efficacy of sleep-focused interventions in reducing migraine burden in this population.
