Thiol-disulfide parameters in recurrent aphthous stomatitis: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Tuba Betül Karadeniz, Aynure Öztekin, Coşkun Öztekin, Engin Şenel, Ünsal Savcı, Salim Neşelioğlu, Özcan Erel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRecurrent aphthous stomatitis (RAS) is an idiopathic inflammatory condition of the oral mucosa, affecting approximately one-fifth of the population. Oxidative stress (OS) has recently been suggested to contribute to the etiopathogenesis of RAS. Thiols are potent antioxidants that play vital roles against oxidation. This study aimed to determine how OS contributes to the pathophysiology of RAS by analyzing the thiol/disulfide parameters.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted with a total of 67 participants (31 patients with clinically diagnosed RAS and 36 healthy, age- and gender- matched controls) at a university hospital from January 2022 to June 2023. Blood samples were taken from patients during the active ulcer phase. Native thiol, total thiol, and disulfide levels were measured in the serum of patients and controls. The ratios of disulfide/native thiol, disulfide/total thiol, and native thiol/total thiol were calculated. The parameters were compared between the groups.ResultsNo significant differences were found between the patient and control groups regarding the calculated thiol/disulfide homeostasis parameters. A significant negative correlation was observed between age and total thiol (P < .001, r = − .572).ConclusionsAlthough OS is widely believed to play a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of RAS, conflicting findings in the literature suggest that the underlying mechanisms may be more complex and multifaceted. Therefore, more comprehensive studies are needed to clarify the oxidant and antioxidant mechanisms in RAS.Keywords: Minor; Stomatitis, aphthous; Oxidative stress; Antioxidants; Thiols; Disulfide

Background

Recurrent aphthous stomatitis (RAS) is an immune-mediated inflammatory condition of the oral mucosa characterized by aphthous ulcers that recur more than three times a year, affecting approximately one-fifth of the population. The ulcers are usually minor, meaning they are smaller than 1 cm. Other less frequent clinical types of aphthae are major and herpetiform [1, 2]. These painful ulcers, which may appear single or multiple, negatively affect the quality of life due to their interference with oral intake and frequent recurrence, persisting for years. Although the diagnosis is clinically straightforward, the management of RAS poses several challenges for physicians. The presence of many risk factors, the fact that some conditions such as Behçet’s disease may present with aphthous lesions, and the reality that topical therapies, which are first-line treatments, often fail to prevent disease recurrence, the lack of an approved systemic treatment contribute to these difficulties [3, 4]. Given the high prevalence of RAS, the search for new treatment approaches has led researchers to focus on better understanding the underlying processes involved in RAS pathogenesis [5]. Such research could aid in both the prevention and treatment of RAS.

The etiology of RAS has been associated with multiple factors, including genetic predisposition, endocrine disorders, micronutrient deficiencies, and psychological stress [2]. T-cell-mediated immune activation and increases in cytokines such as TNF-α are core features of RAS pathogenesis [5, 6]. In recent years, oxidative stress (OS) has also been claimed to play a role in this process. Two main hypotheses have been proposed: the first hypothesis suggests the trigger of oxidative reactions due to proinflammatory cytokines and disease exacerbation, and the second hypothesis proposes the imbalance between oxidants and antioxidants which might be primarily responsible for RAS development [5].

OS is defined as a disruption in the balance between oxidants and antioxidants in favor of oxidants and has been linked to many chronic diseases over the past two decades [7]. Therefore, ongoing research is needed to identify OS markers that could be diagnostically, prognostically, or therapeutically valuable for chronic diseases. Among the many markers used to evaluate OS levels, thiols hold a prominent position. They are the first antioxidants consumed in the neutralization of free radicals and constitute a major part of the total antioxidant capacity. Thiols bind free oxygen radicals in the environment by donating a hydrogen ion, thereby forming disulfide bonds. This process is reversible, meaning that disulfides can be reduced back to thiols. This dynamic conversion is referred to as thiol–disulfide homeostasis. The balance between thiols and disulfides plays a crucial role in maintaining cellular structure and function. Decreased thiol and increased disulfide levels are indicators of OS. Disturbances in the thiol/disulfide balance have been associated with various systemic and dermatological diseases [8]. On the basis of this knowledge, we aimed to evaluate the thiol/disulfide parameters in RAS patients and to compare them with healthy controls.

Methods

Approval of the committee of ethics

This cross-sectional, case-control study was performed at the dermatology department out-patient clinic of a university hospital after local ethics committee approval (19/01/2021, 2022-2). All the participants were informed about the study and their signed consent was obtained. Every procedure was conducted in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study design and inclusion criteria

The study was conducted in the Dermatology Outpatient Clinic of Hitit University Faculty of Medicine, Çorum Erol Olçok Training and Research Hospital between January 2022 and June 2023. The power analysis was performed using G*Power 3.1 program to estimate the sample size required to detect potential effects on thiol-disulfide parameter levels. Based on previous literature [9], an effect size of d = 0.8 was assumed. This analysis showed that at least 26 participants were required to achieve 80% statistical power at a 95% confidence level.

Thirty-one patients (22 females, 9 males) presenting with mouth ulcers and diagnosed with recurrent minor aphthous stomatitis and 36 healthy age- and gender- matched volunteers (25 females, 11 males) participated in the study. All participants were diagnosed by the dermatologists who took part in the study. Diagnosis was based on established clinical criteria, defined as the occurrence of painful, shallow, round, or oval oral ulcers lasting 7–14 days each, at least three times per year, and the absence of a systemic disease that could cause oral ulcers [10]. Only patients meeting these diagnostic criteria were included in the study. Lesions were not categorized by stage (fresh or healing). Only patients with active ulcers were included in the study.

Exclusion criteria

The exclusion criteria included individuals with systemic or dermatologic diseases other than RAS, individuals under 18 years of age, pregnant or breastfeeding women, smokers or alcohol users, those with dental disease (such as dental abscess, tooth decay, periodontal diseases, diagnosed by a dentist) or recent dental treatment, those receiving systemic ulcer treatment in the past three months, or those using any systemic medication, vitamins, or dietary supplements. Additionally, patients with systemic conditions that could cause oral ulcers (Behçet’s disease, Crohn’s disease, periodic fever, aphthous stomatitis, pharyngitis, and cervical adenitis (PFAPA) syndrome, etc.) were excluded from the study. Exclusion was carried out meticulously, taking into account the medical history, clinical examination, and relevant laboratory tests.

Blood sampling

All samples from RAS patients were collected during the active ulcerative phase. Peripheral venous blood samples from both patient and control groups were collected into collection tubes containing ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) after at least eight hours of fasting. Blood samples were centrifuged immediately at 4000 rpm for five minutes, then serum samples were quickly placed in Eppendorf tubes and stored at −80 °C until analyzed.

Serum native and total thiol concentrations, which indicate thiol/disulfide homeostasis, were calculated using a novel automated colorimetric method developed by Erel and Neselioğlu [11]. To measure total thiol levels, dynamic disulfide bonds were first reduced to functional thiol groups by adding a reducing agent, sodium borohydride (NaBH4). Unused NaBH4 in the medium was consumed by adding formaldehyde. All thiol groups, including reducing and native thiols, were determined after reaction with 5,5’-dithiobis-(2-nitrobenzoic acid). The amount of dynamic disulfide was determined as half the difference between total thiols and native thiols (i.e., total thiol-native thiol/2). All assessments were performed by a single assessor. After determining the levels of native thiols, total thiols, and disulfides, the percentage ratios of disulfide/total thiol, disulfide/native thiol, and native thiol/total thiol were calculated.

Statistical analysis

The Statistical Package for Social Science (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) version 21.0 program was used to conduct the statistical analysis. While categorical variables were presented as n (%), continuous variables were presented as the mean ± standard deviation or median (25–75% interquartile range [IQR]) according to the normality of data distribution. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to determine the normality of continuous variables. Normally distributed variables were compared using the Student’s t-test, while non-normally distributed variables were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test between two groups. The χ 2 or Fisher’s Exact test was used to compare categorical variables. Pearson correlation analysis was used to evaluate the association between age and total thiol. Statistical significance was defined as P <.05.

Results

The study involved 67 participants in total. The mean age of control group and RAS patients were 37.6 ± 14.0 (18–65) years and 36.0 ± 13.8 (18–64) years, respectively. There was no significant difference between the groups for parameters of age and gender (Table 1). A significant negative correlation was observed between age and total thiol (P <.001, r = −.572).

Discussion

In this study, thiol/disulfide ratios were measured in RAS patients, and no significant difference was found between them and the control group. Furthermore, total thiol, native thiol, and disulfide levels were also measured in RAS patients, and similarly, no difference was found between them and the control group.

OS occurs when oxidants cannot be neutralized by antioxidants and has been associated with various systemic, immunologic, malignant, and inflammatory diseases. Free oxygen radicals resulting from cellular metabolic processes are the main source of endogenous oxidants. Exogenous sources of free radicals are mostly related to the oral cavity, such as periodontitis, dental procedures, dental materials, oral care products, bacteria in the oral flora, smoking, and coffee consumption [12, 13]. These unstable radicals are beneficial at low doses because they support detoxification, immunity, and intracellular signalling. However, at high doses, they bind to lipids, proteins, and DNA and increase pro-inflammatory cytokines, leading to cell damage or death [7]. The association between OS and DNA damage has been demonstrated in patients with RAS [14].

Antioxidants, which are found in all body fluids including saliva, neutralize free radicals and protect against damage. Examples of antioxidants (endogenous and exogenous) are enzymes (catalase, paraoxonase 1, superoxide dismutase (SOD)), detox systems (e.g., NADPH), mucus and metal-binding compounds (such as lactoferrin and transferrin), transcription factors (such as NRF2), and molecules such as vitamin E, melatonin, and uric acid [5]. An increase in oxidants and/or a deficiency in antioxidants can damage cellular components and DNA, initiating chronic inflammatory processes. Increased inflammatory cytokines exacerbate cellular injury via free oxygen radicals [6]. The relation between inflammation and OS has been shown in many chronic inflammatory diseases [7].

Suspected factors in the aetiology of RAS include genetic predispositions, allergens, microbiota disorders, stress, trauma, and vitamin deficiencies. However, the exact cause remains uncertain [2]. Recently, OS has been the most frequently emphasized factor in studies on RAS. The mutual trigger relationship between OS and inflammation may contribute to the pathogenesis of RAS [15]. OS may be a causative, predisposing, or exacerbating factor in RAS. However, its exact role in the pathogenesis of the disease remains unclear.

To elucidate the role of OS in RAS, numerous studies have investigated various oxidant and antioxidant markers; however, the results remain inconsistent. Variations can be observed among the results of studies evaluating the same biomarker. Moreover, discrepancies exist between blood and salivary levels of these biomarkers [5]. This suggests that OS may exert different effects at local and systemic levels.

Total antioxidant capacity (TAS) and the oxidative stress index are the most commonly investigated OS markers. These markers include both oxidants and antioxidants [6, 16]. Nitric oxide and malondialdehyde (MDA) are oxidant markers that have been found to be elevated in both saliva and blood of patients with RAS [17–20]. In contrast, studies on another oxidant marker, myeloperoxidase, have demonstrated no significant differences between RAS patients and healthy controls [6, 16]. The findings of studies investigating antioxidant markers are likewise inconsistent. While some studies assessing TAS in saliva and blood have reported decreased levels in RAS patients, others have found no significant differences [6, 21, 22]. SOD is an important antioxidant enzyme, and studies have reported that its levels in patients with RAS are either lower or comparable to those of healthy controls [23, 24]. In one study, SOD levels were found to be decreased in the blood but elevated in the saliva of RAS patients [24]. Paraoxonase 1, another antioxidant enzyme, was reported to be reduced in RAS patients, whereas no significant difference was observed in an another study [22, 25]. Most studies investigating antioxidant vitamins have demonstrated deficiencies in patients with RAS [26, 27]. Vitamin deficiencies may contribute to increased susceptibility to RAS or exacerbate its severity, while vitamin supplementation may exert a protective effect [28]. The discrepancies among study findings may be explained by the multifactorial nature of RAS aetiology and OS generation, as well as variations in sample sizes, genetic backgrounds of different populations, and study-specific methodologies. Nevertheless, the exact role of OS in the pathogenesis of RAS remains to be fully elucidated.

Understanding the role of OS in the development of chronic diseases has also led to the emergence of new therapeutic options aimed at reducing or preventing OS. Studies using antioxidant enzymes, such as ebselen for Ménière’s disease, GC4419 for squamous cell carcinoma, and sulforaphane for chronic obstructive pulmonary disease exemplify these developments [29]. In the treatment of RAS, topical and systemic steroids are the most commonly used agents, particularly in the minor aphthous ulcer group. In severe cases, although costly, biological agents appear to be beneficial [2]. While there is no direct evidence supporting the efficacy of antioxidants in RAS treatment, studies suggest that deficiencies in antioxidants, such as vitamin deficiencies, may cause predisposition to the disease. Some studies evaluating antioxidant markers indicate that in RAS, antioxidant levels in saliva or serum may increase in response to inflammation or decrease due to consumption in redox reactions. Other studies, including ours, have found no significant difference in antioxidant concentrations between RAS patients and healthy controls [5]. In our study, no differences were observed in thiol-disulfide values; however, the study was limited by a small sample size, inclusion of only patients with minor aphthous ulcers, and the lack of assessment of ulcer duration. Given that RAS affects a large portion of the population, research on new therapeutic approaches remains of considerable interest. Low efficacy and side effects limited the use of many treatment modalities like topical nicotine, minocycline, or systemic thalidomide, dapsone, colchicine, pentoxifylline, etc. Research is ongoing for promising new therapies, such as laser treatments [2]. Therefore, the potential effect of antioxidant therapy—which has shown promise for chronic diseases—on RAS, a condition in need of novel treatment options, warrants further investigation.

Overall, antioxidants are highly diverse (including vitamins, enzymes, and redox systems) and play important roles in numerous cellular functions. Their functions are not limited to scavenging free radicals but extend to more complex roles [29]. Considering the complexity of antioxidant functions, the diversity of antioxidant sources, and the rapid dynamics of OS and antioxidant responses, we believe that evaluating multiple parameters simultaneously would be beneficial—or even necessary—when assessing antioxidant levels to better understand the role of OS in RAS. Such parameters may include disease duration, diet, and oral hygiene status. We believe that the limitations in our study may have influenced the results, and that more comprehensive studies will allow a more accurate understanding of the role of antioxidants in RAS.

Thiols are sulfur-containing compounds. These molecules are the first antioxidants depleted in response to OS. In healthy individuals, these compounds account for approximately 53% of the antioxidant capacity of serum. They eliminate free radicals via enzymatic and non-enzymatic mechanisms, and play roles in enzyme activity, the regulation of transcription factors, signal transduction, and apoptosis. Thiols are reliable markers of OS, particularly protein damage. Since the development of a fully automated method to measure the thiol/disulfide balance by Erel and Neşelioğlu in 2014, many studies have been conducted in this area [11]. There is a decrease in the thiol/disulfide parameters in diseases such as coronary artery disease, Alzheimer’s, diabetes mellitus, migraine, urticaria and atopic dermatitis [8]. Similarly, in aging, the increase in oxidative stress and the decline in antioxidant capacity are associated with a reduction in thiol levels [30]. In our study, a negative correlation was also observed between age and thiol levels in both the patient and control groups.

To our knowledge, the only study examining thiol/disulfide ratios in RAS patients was by Yavuz et al. In this study, serum thiol/disulfide levels of 43 RAS patients were compared with the same number of healthy volunteers. While no difference in thiol levels was found between groups, disulfide levels were significantly higher in RAS patients unlike our findings. The authors noted that this result aligns with Erel and Neşelioğlu’s thesis: thiol levels increase and disulfide levels decrease in proliferative diseases, whereas disulfide levels increase in degenerative diseases. The small sample size and inability to evaluate subgroups of minor, major, or herpetiform ulcers were stated as limitations of that study [9]. The discrepancy between our study and Yavuz et al.‘s findings may have several factors. One of them is our small sample size. Another difference is the gender distribution. Yavuz et al. included 26 women and 17 men, whereas our study included 22 women and only 9 men indicating a higher female-to-male ratio. RAS is more common in women aged 20–50 years, which is consistent with the predominance of female patients in both studies [4]. Only a limited number of studies have investigated the underlying reasons for the female predominance in RAS. It has been reported that the incidence of RAS increases during the luteal phase of menstruation and menopause, whereas remission is observed during pregnancy and in women using oral contraceptives. These findings suggest a potential association with progesterone, which warrants further investigation [31]. Another possible contributing factor may be the effect of estrogen. Estrogen is a sex steroid with antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects. Estrogen has been associated with reduced oxidative stress and increased lifespan [32]. Unfortunately the menopausal status of our study population were not elucidated. The predominance of female gender in our study may have influenced the antioxidant levels and contributed to overall redox balance.

In the study by Yavuz et al. [9] 80% of patients had minor aphthae, whereas all patients in our study presented with minor aphthae. The studies evaluating differences between minor and major aphthae, researchers measured glutathione and MDA levels and, although the difference was not statistically significant, found that patients with major aphthae had lower glutathione levels compared to those with minor aphthae [19]. Jesija et al. İnvestigated antioxidant enzyme levels in patients with RAS, and they had compared major and minor aphthae groups and reported higher SOD levels in patients with major aphthae and higher uric acid levels in patients with minor aphthae. The type of ulcer in the disease may influence the levels of OS markers. Jesija et al. also found lower serum SOD levels but higher salivary SOD levels in RAS patients compared to controls, suggesting that this may be related to the migration of antioxidants from the serum to the saliva. In the same study, no significant differences were observed between OS parameters and ulcer duration in days, presence of single or multiple ulcers, or disease duration [23].

In another study, Rezaei et al. performed daily measurements of TAS in the saliva of RAS patients during the lesion period. While TAS values in the acute phase were similar to those of healthy controls, they increased significantly on a daily basis from the active phase to the healing phase. Rezaei suggested that this might be due to activation of the antioxidant system in response to increased inflammation and OS at the ulcer site, or due to dietary changes by patients because of pain; previous studies have shown that diet can have an enhancing effect on antioxidants [21]. Ekinci et al. found no difference in prolidase levels between RAS patients and healthy controls, suggesting that the rapid healing process in RAS may prevent the detection of certain markers [25].

As indicated in these studies, OS markers in saliva and blood may undergo dynamic changes during the ulcer healing process. Therefore, the disease stage at which the ulcer is present may influence parameter levels, and consequently, study outcomes. We believe that further research is needed to better understand the effect of ulcer duration on oxidative parameters. Additionally, factors such as dietary supplements, sleep patterns, and psychological stress can affect an individual’s antioxidant capacity [20, 28, 33]. Similarly, many endogenous and exogenous factors contribute to free radical formation [12, 13]. This finding supports the multifactorial natüre of RAS, which arises from interactions among environmental, microbial, and host factors. When investigating the role of OS in RAS, these influences on the oxidant-antioxidant balance should be considered.

Limitations

The single-centre and cross-sectional design, small study population, inclusion of only patients with minor aphthae, failure to categorize lesions are fresh or healing and failure to assess disease-specific metrics like ulcer duration are among the limitations of this study. Other limitations include the lack of any assessment of factors that may influence antioxidant capacity, such as psychological stress, sleep patterns, seasonal variation, dietary habits, and oral hygiene habits between the patient and control groups.

Conclusions

Although oxidative stress is widely believed to play a role in the pathogenesis of RAS, as it does in many other diseases, no significant differences in thiol-disulfide parameters were observed between the RAS patients and healthy controls in this study. This finding suggests the complexity of the etiopathogenesis of the disease. Understanding the underlying mechanisms of disease development could aid in disease prevention and, more importantly, in the development of novel therapeutic approaches. Considering that RAS is a common condition in the population and that many aspects of its etiopathogenesis remain unclear, our study, despite its limitations, may contribute to future research in this field. We believe that larger-scale studies including a greater number of patients with subtype classification and simultaneous assessment of both disease-related and oxidative stress-related parameters are warranted in order to fully elucidate the oxidative and antioxidant mechanisms involved in RAS.
