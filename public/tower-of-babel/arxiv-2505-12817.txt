The log-concavity of eigenfunction to complex Monge-Ampère operator in ℂ 2 \mathbb{C}^{2}
Wei Zhang, Qi Zhou

Abstract
. Following the authors’ recent work [ 39 ] , we further explore the convexity properties of solutions to the Dirichlet problem for the complex Monge-Ampère operator. In this paper, we establish the log \log -concavity of solutions to the Dirichlet eigenvalue problem for the complex Monge-Ampère operator on bounded, smooth, strictly convex domain in ℂ 2 \mathbb{C}^{2} . The key ingredients consist of the constant rank theorem and the deformation method.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification. Primary 35B50; Secondary 32W20.

Keywords and phrases. Log-concavity, complex Monge-Ampère operator, eigenvalue problem, constant rank theorem.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Main result

The classical Dirichlet eigenvalue problem

(1.1)

\begin{cases}\Delta u=-\lambda u&\mathrm{in}\ \Omega,\\
\hskip 8.53581ptu=0&\mathrm{on}\ \partial\Omega\end{cases}

has been widely studied, where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a bounded convex domain. Among these studies, the investigation of the
\log
-concavity of its solutions, also known as the eigenfunctions of the Laplacian operator, is of particular importance. This property has significant applications in geometry, physics, and other fields. Initially, in 1976, Brascamp and Lieb
[
8
]
employed parabolic methods to prove the
\log
-concavity of the first Dirichlet eigenfuction of the Laplacian operator of a bounded convex domain, leading to the Brunn-Minkowski inequality for the first eigenvalue as following:

\lambda\left((1-t)\Omega_{0}+t\Omega_{1}\right)^{-1/2}\geq(1-t)\lambda(\Omega_{0})^{-1/2}+t\lambda(\Omega_{1})^{-1/2},

where
t\in[0,1]
,
\Omega_{0},\Omega_{1}
are nonempty convex bodies in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. In 1985, Singer, Wong, Yau and Yau
[
36
]
utilized the maximum principle combined with the continuity method to obtain the same concavity result and provided estimates for the gap between the first and second non-zero eigenvalues. The optimal lower bound, known as the famous fundamental gap conjecture, was completely resolved by Andrews and Clutterbuck
[
3
]
.

The eigenvalue problem for fully nonlinear elliptic operators has also been extensively studied by many authors. Lions
[
26
]
first investigated the eigenvalue problem for the Monge-Ampère operator on strictly convex domain
\Omega
with smooth boundary in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. He proved the existence of a unique positive constant
\lambda(\Omega)
and a unique non-zero convex solution
u\in C^{\infty}(\Omega)\cap C^{1,1}(\bar{\Omega})
solving

\begin{cases}\det(u_{ij})=\lambda(\Omega)(-u)^{n}&\mathrm{in}\ \Omega,\\
\hskip 31.29802ptu=0&\mathrm{on}\ \partial\Omega.\end{cases}

Later, Wang
[
37
]
generalized this result to the
k
-Hessian operator. More details on the eigenvalue problem for the Monge-Ampère operator can be found in Chapter 11 of Le’s recent book
[
25
]
.

In the complex setting, Kutev and Ramadanov
[
22
,
23
]
were the first to initiate such studies. In 2023, Badiane and Zeriahi
[
4
]
studied the existence and uniqueness of the first eigenvalue and an associated eigenfunction with Dirichlet conditions for the complex Monge-Ampère operator on a bounded strictly pseudoconvex domain
\Omega
in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Soon after, Chu, Liu and McCleerey
[
16
]
extended this result to the complex
k
-Hessian operator on manifolds, proving the eigenfunction
u\in C^{\infty}(\Omega)\cap C^{1,1}(\bar{\Omega})
. In more detail, assume that
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
is a bounded strictly pseudoconvex domain with smooth boundary, and that
0<\psi\in C^{\infty}(\bar{\Omega})
. Then there exists a real number
\lambda(\Omega,\psi)
and a plurisubharmonic (PSH) function
u\in C^{\infty}(\Omega)\cap C^{1,1}(\bar{\Omega})
such that the pair
(\lambda,u)
is a unique solution to the eigenvalue problem

(1.2)

\begin{cases}\det(u_{i\bar{j}})=\lambda(\Omega)\psi(-u)^{n}&\mathrm{in}\ \Omega,\\
\hskip 31.29802ptu=0&\mathrm{on}\ \partial\Omega.\end{cases}

The aim of this paper is to study the
\log
-concavity of eigenfunction for the complex Monge-Ampère operator, specifically to prove that the solution to the Dirichlet value probelm (
1.2
) is
\log
-concave when
\psi=1
and
n=2
. We state the theorem as follows.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded, smooth and strictly convex domain in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
. Suppose that
u\in\mathrm{PSH}(\Omega)\cap C^{\infty}(\Omega)\cap C^{1,1}(\bar{\Omega})
is the unique solution of Dirichlet problem

(1.3)

\begin{cases}\det(u_{i\bar{j}})=\lambda(\Omega)(-u)^{2}&\mathrm{in}\ \Omega,\\
\hskip 31.29802ptu<0&\mathrm{in}\ \Omega,\\
\hskip 31.29802ptu=0&\mathrm{on}\ \partial\Omega.\end{cases}

Then the function
v=-\log(-u/4)
is strictly convex in
\Omega
.

1.2.
Previous works

Convexity has been a central topic in partial differential equations (PDEs) for a long time. In this subsection, we briefly review the historical progress on the convexity of solutions to elliptic PDEs, emphasizing the
\log
-concavity of eigenfunctions for elliptic operators. For more detailed discussions, we refer the reader to the authors’ recent article
[
39
]
.

In 1971, Makar-Limanov
[
30
]
studied the Dirichlet problem for the Laplace equation

\begin{cases}\Delta u=-1&\mathrm{in}\ \Omega,\\
\hskip 8.53581ptu=0&\mathrm{on}\ \partial\Omega,\end{cases}

where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a bounded, strictly convex domain with smooth boundary, and established the strict concavity of the function
\sqrt{u}
on
\Omega
. His main idea was to introduce the auxiliary function

P=v^{4}\det(\nabla^{2}v),

where
v=\sqrt{u}
and
u
is a solution of the Dirichlet problem, and then demonstrate that
P
is superharmonic in
\Omega
. This approach is known as the
P
-function method. Later, Acker, Payne and Philippin
[
1
]
applied this method to provide an alternative proof of the result by Brascamp and Lieb, namely, to establish the
\log
-concavity of the first Dirichlet eigenfunction for the Laplacian operator on planar convex domains. More recently, Ma, Shi and Ye
[
28
]
, Jia, Ma and Shi
[
15
]
, advanced this technique to derive similar results in higher dimensions.

A breakthrough in the study of convexity properties of solutions to linear and quasilinear elliptic equations was achieved by Korevaar
[
19
,
20
]
in the early 1980s. For a bounded convex domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a solution
u
, he introduced the convexity test function

\mathcal{C}(x,y,\mu)=u\left((1-\mu)x+\mu y\right)-(1-\mu)u(x)-\mu u(y)

for
(x,y,\mu)\in\bar{\Omega}\times\bar{\Omega}\times[0,1]
, where the nonnegativity of
\mathcal{C}(x,y,\mu)
implies the concavity of
u
. This method, now widely known as Korevaar’s concavity maximum principle, has become a fundamental tool in the field; see
[
18
,
17
,
33
,
24
,
7
,
32
]
. Among its applications, Korevaar
[
20
]
used this method to rederive the
\log
-concavity of solution to the Dirichlet problem (
1.1
), as also discussed in Caffarelli and Spruck
[
10
]
.

In 1997, Alvarez, Lasry and Lions
[
2
]
developed a new approach, the convex envelope method, to establish the convexity of viscosity solution of general fully nonlinear degenerate elliptic equation

F(\nabla^{2}v,\nabla v,v,x)=0

in a bounded convex domain
\Omega
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. In particular, they provided a new proof of the concavity result originally established by Brascamp and Lieb
[
8
]
. Naturally, this also serves as a powerful tool for studying convexity problems, as discussed in
[
6
,
12
,
14
]
.

Another significant method for studying the convexity of solutions to PDEs is the constant rank theorem. This approach was initially introduced by Caffarelli and Friedman
[
9
]
for semilinear elliptic equations in two dimensions. By combining it with the deformation process, they were able to reprove Makar-Limanov’s result as well as the
\log
-concavity of the first Dirichlet eigenfunction for the Laplacian operator in the two dimensional case. In higher dimensions, Korevaar and Lewis
[
21
]
utilized the same approach to derive corresponding proofs.

As we mentioned earlier, in 1994, Wang
[
37
]
studied the existence and uniqueness of the eigenvalue problem for the
k
-Hessian operator, specifically considering the Dirichlet problem

(1.4)

\begin{cases}\sigma_{k}(\nabla^{2}u)=\lambda(\Omega)(-u)^{k}&\mathrm{in}\ \Omega,\\
\hskip 34.1433ptu=0&\mathrm{on}\ \partial\Omega,\end{cases}

where
1<k<n
and
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a bounded domain satisfying certain convexity conditions. Later, Liu, Ma and Xu
[
27
]
applied the constant rank theorem to prove the
\log
-concavity of solution to the Dirichlet problem (
1.4
) for the case
k=2
and
n=3
, that is, the
\log
-concavity of eigenfunction to the
2
-Hessian operator. They also established the Brunn-Minkowski inequality for
2
-Hessian eigenvalues. Subsequently, Salani
[
34
]
provided a new proof of this result. In more recent developments, several lower dimensional convexity results of solutions to fully nonlinear elliptic boundary value problems appeared via constant rank theorems, for example
[
13
,
38
,
11
]
.

Recently, we have discovered that the constant rank theorem is also effective for studying convexity problems in the complex setting. In
[
39
]
, we considered the Dirichlet problem for the complex Monge-Ampère equation

\begin{cases}\det(u_{i\bar{j}})=1&\mathrm{in}\ \Omega,\\
\hskip 31.2982ptu=0&\mathrm{on}\ \partial\Omega,\end{cases}

where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}^{2}
is a bounded, smooth, and strictly convex domain, and proved that
-\sqrt{-u/2}
is strictly convex.

1.3.
Remarks on the proof

In this subsection, we briefly describe the proof of Theorem
1.1
, which relies on the ideas of Caffarelli and Friedman
[
9
]
and Korevaar and Lewis
[
21
]
. The proof involves two key steps: deriving convexity estimates near the boundary and establishing a constant rank theorem for the transformed equation involving
v=-\log(-u/4)
, where
u
solves the complex Monge-Ampère Dirichlet problem (
1.3
).

Due to the strict convexity of the domain
\Omega
, we can easily obtain the convexity estimates for
v
near the boundary
\partial\Omega
. For details, see Lemma
4.2
.

From (
1.3
), by letting
v=-\log(-u/4)
, we find that
v
satisfies the Dirichlet problem

\begin{cases}F(\nabla^{2}v,\nabla v)=0&\mathrm{in}\ \Omega,\\
\hskip 51.21504ptv\rightarrow+\infty&\mathrm{on}\ \partial\Omega,\end{cases}

where

\displaystyle F(\nabla^{2}v,\nabla v)=

\displaystyle~(v_{11}+v_{33})(v_{22}+v_{44})-(v_{12}+v_{34})(v_{21}+v_{43})-(v_{14}-v_{32})(v_{41}-v_{23})

\displaystyle-(v_{1}^{2}+v_{3}^{2})(v_{22}+v_{44})-(v_{2}^{2}+v_{4}^{2})(v_{11}+v_{33})

\displaystyle+(v_{1}v_{2}+v_{3}v_{4})(v_{12}+v_{21}+v_{34}+v_{43})

\displaystyle+(v_{1}v_{4}-v_{2}v_{3})(v_{14}-v_{32}+v_{41}-v_{23})-\lambda.

For more details on the derivation of this equation, we refer to Section
2.2
. The remaining task is to eatablish a constant rank theorem for the equation

(1.5)

F(\nabla^{2}v,\nabla v)=0.

Let us briefly review the result of Bian and Guan
[
5
]
. They considered the general fully nonlinear elliptic equation

(1.6)

F(\nabla^{2}v,\nabla v,v,x)=0\quad\mathrm{in}\ \Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n},

where the elliptic operator
F
satisfies the structural condition that

(1.7)

F(A^{-1},p,v,x)\ \mbox{is locally convex in $(A,v,x)$ for each $p$}.

Consequently, they established a constant rank theorem for convex solutions of equation (
1.6
). The key to their proof was introducing the quotient type auxiliary function

(1.8)

\phi(x)=\sigma_{l+1}(\nabla^{2}v(x))+q(\nabla^{2}v(x)),

where

(1.9)

q(\nabla^{2}v(x))=\begin{cases}\frac{\sigma_{l+2}(\nabla^{2}v(x))}{\sigma_{l+1}(\nabla^{2}v(x))}&\mathrm{if}\ \sigma_{l+1}(\nabla^{2}v(x))>0,\\
0&\mathrm{if}\ \sigma_{l+1}(\nabla^{2}v(x))=0.\end{cases}

They then derived a differential inequality for this function and applied the maximum principle. The reason why Bian and Guan’s result cannot be directly applied to our equation (
1.5
) is that it is unclear whether the elliptic operator
F
satisfies the structural condtion (
1.7
). Therefore, we need to carry out extensive computations to directly establish the constant rank theorem, which is one of the challenging aspects of our work. Nevertheless, it is worth noting that the function (
1.8
) introduced by Bian and Guan
[
5
]
provides significant inspiration for our proof.

Define

l=\min_{x\in\Omega}\mathrm{rank}(\nabla^{2}v(x)).

By equation (
1.5
), it is clear that
l\geq 2
. Hence to prove the constant rank theorem (Corollary
3.3
), we only need to consider two cases, namely,

\bullet

l=2
(Theorem
3.1
);

\bullet

l=3
(Theorem
3.2
).

For the case
l=2
, to simplify our calculations, we require the function (
1.8
) introduced by Bian and Guan
[
5
]
. As pointed out by Bian and Guan
[
5
]
, proving the maximum principle for the usual auxiliary function
\sigma_{3}(\nabla^{2}v(x))
(
l=2
) is difficult in the context of general fully nonlinear elliptic PDEs. For our
relatively specific equation (
1.5
), we find that choosing
\sigma_{3}(\nabla^{2}v(x))
as the auxiliary function also leads to computational difficulties.

The case
l=3
is significantly challenging. To prove the differential inequality for the auxiliary function
\sigma_{4}(\nabla^{2}v(x))
in this case, we must verify the positivity of a five-variable quadratic form with most of its coefficients being non-zero. Equivalently, we need to ensure that the coefficient matrix of this quadratic form is positive definite. This involves computing its leading principal minors and verifying their positivity modulo
\phi
. We observe that, through elementary column and row operations, the
5\times 5
coefficient matrix can be transformed into a block diagonal form, consisting of a
2\times 2
block and a
3\times 3
block. For the second order matrix, computing its leading principal minors is straightforward. However, for the third order matrix, calculating its leading principal minors and their semi-positivity requires more intricate techniques.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. In Section

2
, we review some preliminary results, including calculations on Bian and Guan’s quotient type auxiliary function, etc. In Section
3
, we analyze two cases and establish differential inequalities in each, leading to the required constant rank theorem. This constitutes the main part of the paper. Section
4
is devoted to the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

2.
Preliminaries

As mentioned in Section
1.3
, to establish the constant rank theorem in the case when the minimal rank of
\nabla^{2}v
is
2
, namely, to prove Theorem
3.1
, we must employ the quotient type auxiliary function introduced by Bian and Guan
[
5
]
. In this section, we first recall several formulas associate with this auxiliary function. The proofs are omitted, and for detailed derivations, we refer the reader to their original work
[
5
]
. Since this paper focuses on convexity, a concept rooted in real-variable analysis, we will derive the real form of the two-dimensional complex Monge-Ampère operator. Finally, we carry out some preliminary computations that will be used in subsequent sections.

2.1.
Bian and Guan’s quotient type auxiliary function

We recall the definition of
k
-th elementary symmetric functions. For
1\leq k\leq n
and
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\cdots,\lambda_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we define

\sigma_{k}(\lambda)=\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{k}\leq n}\lambda_{i_{1}}\lambda_{i_{2}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{k}}.

We also make the convention that
\sigma_{0}(\lambda)=1
and
\sigma_{-1}(\lambda)=\sigma_{n+1}(\lambda)=0
. The definition of
\sigma_{k}
for vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
can be extended to
n\times n
symmetric matrices in a natural way by letting
\sigma_{k}(A)=\sigma_{k}(\lambda(A))
, where
\lambda(A)=(\lambda_{1}(A),\lambda_{2}(A),\cdots,\lambda_{n}(A))
are the eigenvalues of the symmetric matrix
A
. We denote by
\sigma_{k-1}(A|i)
the
(k-1)
-th elementary symmetric function with
\lambda_{i}=0
and
\sigma_{k-2}(A|ij)
(
i\neq j
) the
(k-2)
-th elementary symmetric function with
\lambda_{i}=\lambda_{j}=0
.

The first and second derivatives of the elementary symmetric functions are listed in the following lemma.

Lemma 2.1
.

Suppose that
A=(A_{ij})_{n\times n}
is a diagonal matrix. For
k=1,2,\cdots,n
, then we have

\frac{\partial\sigma_{k}(A)}{\partial A_{ij}}=\begin{cases}\sigma_{k-1}(A|i)&\mathrm{if}\ i=j,\\
0&\mathrm{if}\ i\neq j,\end{cases}

and

\frac{\partial^{2}\sigma_{k}(A)}{\partial A_{ij}\partial A_{pq}}=\begin{cases}\sigma_{k-2}(A|ip)&\mathrm{if}\ i=j,p=q,i\neq p,\\
-\sigma_{k-2}(A|ip)&\mathrm{if}\ i=q,j=p,i\neq p,\\
0&\mathrm{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Below we recall some notations and lemmas from Bian and Guan
[
5
]
that will be used in Section
3
. Suppose that
\nabla^{2}v(x)
attains its minimal rank
l
(
l=0,1,\cdots,n-1
) at
x_{0}\in\Omega
. We pick an open neighborhood
\mathcal{O}
of
x_{0}
. For any
x\in\mathcal{O}
, let
\lambda_{1}(x)\geq\lambda_{2}(x)\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{n}(x)\geq 0
be the eigenvalues of
\nabla^{2}v(x)
. There exists a positive constant
c
independent of
x
, such that
\lambda_{1}(x)\geq\lambda_{2}(x)\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{l}(x)\geq c
. Let
G=\{1,2,\cdots,l\}
and
B=\{l+1,l+2,\cdots,n\}
be the “good” and “bad” sets of indices, respectively. If there is no confusion, we also denote the “good” and “bad” eigenvalues of
\nabla^{2}v(x)
by
G
and
B
, respectively. Note that for any
\delta>0
, we may choose
\mathcal{O}
small enough such that
\lambda_{i}<\delta
for all
i\in B
and
x\in\mathcal{O}
.

For two functions
h(x)
and
k(x)
defined on
\mathcal{O}
, by
h(x)=O(k(x))
we mean that there exists a positive constant
C
such that
|h(x)|\leq C|k(x)|
. Clearly,
\lambda_{i}(x)=O(\phi)
for each
i\in B
, where
\phi
is the quotient type auxiliary function defined in (
1.8
).

The rest of this subsection reviews some crucial lemmas obtained by Bian and Guan in
[
5
]
.

(Lemma 2.4 in
[
5
]
)
.

For
q(\nabla^{2}v)
be defined as in (
1.9
). Assume that
\nabla^{2}v

\frac{\partial q}{\partial v_{ij}}=\begin{cases}\frac{\sigma_{1}^{2}(B|i)-\sigma_{2}(B|i)}{\sigma_{1}^{2}(B)}+O(\phi)&\mathrm{if}\ i=j\in B,\\

Lemma 2.3

(Proposition 2.1 in

]
)
.

Let
q(\nabla^{2}v)
be defined as in (

). Assume that
\nabla^{2}v(x)
is diagonal for

. Then, for any
\alpha,\beta\in\{1,2,\cdots,n\}
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq i,j,k,m\leq n}\frac{\partial^{2}q}{\partial v_{ij}\partial v_{km}}v_{ij\alpha}v_{km\beta}=

where

Lemma 2.4

(Lemma 3.3 in
[

]
)
.

Let
M
be a positive constant such that
0<\lambda_{i}\leq M
and
\frac{1}{M}\leq\gamma_{i}\leq M
for

2.2.
Real form of the complex Monge-Ampère operator

In this subsection, we need to express the complex Hessian matrix
(u_{i\bar{j}})_{2\times 2}
as a
4\times 4
real Hessian matrix
\nabla^{2}u
, allowing us to transform the equation

(2.1)

\det(u_{i\bar{j}})=\lambda(\Omega)(-u)^{2}

from
\mathbb{C}^{2}
to
\mathbb{R}^{4}
. Then, by applying a logarithmic transformation to the equivalent equation on
\mathbb{R}^{4}
to get a new equation with respect to
v
.

Let
z_{1},z_{2}
be complex coordinates in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
, where
z_{i}=x_{i}+\sqrt{-1}y_{i}
. Recall that for a smooth real-valued function
u
defined on a domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}^{2}
,

u_{i}=\frac{\partial u}{\partial z_{i}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}}-\sqrt{-1}\frac{\partial u}{\partial y_{i}}\right)=\frac{1}{2}(u_{x_{i}}-\sqrt{-1}u_{y_{i}})

and

u_{\bar{i}}=\frac{\partial u}{\partial\bar{z}_{i}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}}+\sqrt{-1}\frac{\partial u}{\partial y_{i}}\right)=\frac{1}{2}(u_{x_{i}}+\sqrt{-1}u_{y_{i}}).

Furthermore, we have

(2.2)

u_{i\bar{j}}=\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial z_{i}\partial\bar{z}_{j}}=\frac{1}{4}(u_{x_{i}x_{j}}+u_{y_{i}y_{j}})+\frac{1}{4}\sqrt{-1}(u_{x_{i}y_{j}}-u_{y_{i}x_{j}}).

For the sake of convenience, we denote the partial derivatives of
u
with respect to the real variables by

\displaystyle u_{x_{1}}=u_{1},\ u_{x_{2}}=u_{2},\ u_{y_{1}}=u_{3},\ u_{y_{2}}=u_{4},

\displaystyle u_{x_{1}x_{1}}=u_{11},\ u_{y_{1}y_{1}}=u_{33},\ u_{x_{1}y_{1}}=u_{13},\ \cdots.

According to (
2.2
), we have

\displaystyle\det(u_{i\bar{j}})=

\displaystyle~u_{1\bar{1}}u_{2\bar{2}}-u_{1\bar{2}}u_{2\bar{1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\frac{1}{16}(u_{11}+u_{33})(u_{22}+u_{44})-\frac{1}{16}(u_{12}+u_{34})(u_{21}+u_{43})-\frac{1}{16}(u_{14}-u_{32})(u_{41}-u_{23}).

Consequently, equation (
2.1
) can be written as

(2.3)

16\lambda u^{2}=(u_{11}+u_{33})(u_{22}+u_{44})-(u_{12}+u_{34})(u_{21}+u_{43})-(u_{14}-u_{32})(u_{41}-u_{23}).

If we let
v=-\log(-u/4)
, then we have

u=-4e^{-v},\ u_{i}=4e^{-v}v_{i},\ \mathrm{and}\ u_{ij}=4e^{-v}(v_{ij}-v_{i}v_{j})

for
i,j=1,2,3,4
. Thus, equation (
2.3
) can be expressed as

(2.4)

\displaystyle\lambda=

\displaystyle(v_{11}+v_{33})(v_{22}+v_{44})-(v_{12}+v_{34})(v_{21}+v_{43})-(v_{14}-v_{32})(v_{41}-v_{23})

Theorem 3.2

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{4}
be a domain and let

and a positive constant

(independent of

\sum_{i,j}F^{ij}\phi_{ij}\leq C(\phi+|\nabla\phi|)\quad\mathrm{in}\ \mathcal{O}.

Proof.

Following the notations in
[

,
21
]
, for two functions
h(x)
and

defined in
\mathcal{O}
, we say that
h(x)\lesssim k(x)
if there exists a positive constant
C_{1}
such that

h(x)-k(x)\leq C_{1}(\phi(x)+|\nabla\phi|(x)).

We also write
h(x)\sim k(x)
if
h(x)\lesssim k(x)
and
k(x)\lesssim h(x)
. Next, we write
h\lesssim k
if the above inequality holds for any
x\in\mathcal{O}
, with constant
C_{1}
independent of
x

h\sim k
means
h\lesssim k
and
k\lesssim h
. In a similar way, we will also use the notation
h\gtrsim k
which means
-h\lesssim-k

-h+k\leq C_{2}(\phi+|\nabla\phi|)\quad\mathrm{in}\ \mathcal{O},

or equivalently,

h-k\geq-C_{2}(\phi+|\nabla\phi|)\quad\mathrm{in}\ \mathcal{O}.

Thus,
h\sim k
can also be interpreted as
h\lesssim k
and
h\gtrsim k
, meaning that there exist positive constants
C_{3}
and
C_{4}
such that

-C_{3}(\phi+|\nabla\phi|)\leq h-k\leq C_{4}(\phi+|\nabla\phi|)\quad\mathrm{in}\ \mathcal{O}.

We can also write this as

Lemma 4.1
.

Let

be the ball in

centered at the origin with radius

By uniqueness, the solution

where

for

A straightforward computation yields

Then the equation (

Since

is plurisubharmonic, we have

, which is equivalent to

(4.2)

\displaystyle u^{\prime\prime}+3r^{-1}u^{\prime}>0

for

. In particular, taking the limit as

and applying L’Hôpital’s rule yields

. Moreover, by evaluating both sides of equation (

) as

, we further obtain

. Since

, we have

\displaystyle v^{\prime}(0)=-\frac{u^{\prime}(0)}{u(0)}=0,

\displaystyle v^{\prime\prime}(0)=-\frac{u^{\prime\prime}(0)}{u(0)}+\left(\frac{u^{\prime}(0)}{u(0)}\right)^{2}=-\frac{u^{\prime\prime}(0)}{u(0)}>0.

We now aim to show that

for all

. Suppose that there exists some

. On the other hand,

Differentiating both sides of equation (

\displaystyle v^{\prime\prime\prime}(r_{0})=\frac{16\lambda}{v^{\prime}(r_{0})}+\frac{(v^{\prime}(r_{0}))^{2}}{r_{0}}.
