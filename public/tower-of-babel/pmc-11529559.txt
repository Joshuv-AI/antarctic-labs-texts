Burden and causes of anemia in Vietnam: insights from the global burden of disease data
Authors: Deepak Kumar Behera, Dil B Rahut, Snehasish Tripathy, Sapna Negi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAnemia is a significant global health issue, impacting 1.92 billion people and ranking as a leading cause of disability. It is marked by low hemoglobin levels, which hinder oxygen delivery to vital organs and result from causes such as nutritional deficiencies and chronic diseases. Despite some progress, anemia remains a major concern in Southeast Asia, including Vietnam. Although the country has reduced its anemia rates, the progress is insufficient to achieve the WHO’s 2030 targets for anemia reduction among women of reproductive age. This study explores anemia trends and socio-economic determinants in Vietnam, with a focus on age and gender differences, to inform targeted interventions and policy development.MethodsThis study used the Global Burden of Diseases, Injuries, and Risk Factors (GBD) dataset to explore the burden of anemia and its causes in Vietnam from 1990 to 2021. The GBD study provides data on anemia prevalence and years lived with disability (YLD) by age, sex, and 37 causes across 204 countries and territories. Further, this study employed a regression model to examine how the anemia burden (i.e., prevalence rate and YLD rate) relates to socio-economic factors such as the Human Development Index (HDI), gender disparities in human development achievement, and gender-based inequalities in reproductive health, empowerment, and economic status in Vietnam over this period.ResultsIn 1990, there were 16.30 million anemia cases in Vietnam, decreasing slightly to 16.05 million by 2021. Male prevalence dropped from 34.90 to 26.58%, while female prevalence increased from 65.10 to 73.42%. Iron deficiency was the main cause, affecting 68.35% of cases in 1990 and 68.84% in 2021, with 24.96% in males and 75.04% in females in 2021. While anemia has decreased among infants, young children, and adolescents due to improved healthcare, it has increased among middle-aged and older adults, especially females. Regression analysis shows that higher human development and gender equality reduce anemia prevalence and disability, whereas gender inequality worsens the issue.ConclusionsThe study underscores that anemia remains a major health concern in Vietnam, marked by significant gender and age disparities. Despite a decrease in overall anemia rates and the effectiveness of specific interventions like iron-folic acid supplementation, females, particularly those of reproductive age and older adults, continue to face higher rates. Socio-economic improvements, including higher income and better human development, are linked to lower anemia rates, though the impact differs by gender. The persistent gender disparity and the shift in burden to older adults highlight the need for more comprehensive, gender-sensitive interventions. Future efforts should integrate nutritional support with broader socio-economic enhancements to effectively address anemia and improve public health in Vietnam.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-20497-7.Keywords: Anemia, Vietnam, Global burden of disease, Prevalence, Years lived with disability

Introduction

Anemia remains a substantial global health challenge, affecting 1.92 billion people and ranking as the third-highest cause of years lived with disability (YLD) according to the Global Burden of Disease Study (GBD) 2021 [1]. This condition, characterized by a reduced blood cell count, compromises the supply of oxygen to vital organs. Anemia is typically diagnosed in the general population using WHO-defined hemoglobin (Hb) cutoffs, which consider factors such as age, gender, and pregnancy status [2]. Various causes of anemia include nutritional deficiencies (iron, folate, vitamin B12), viral and inflammatory diseases (Helminthic infection, HIV, tuberculosis), and hereditary hemoglobin disorders (thalassemia, sickle cell disease) [3]. Iron deficiency anemia alone accounted for 66.2% of total anemia cases in 2021, with young children and women of reproductive age being the most vulnerable [1, 4]. The severe health consequences of iron deficiency anemia include cognitive impairment, lethargy with reduced productivity, increased susceptibility to infections, and adverse outcomes for both mothers and children [5–7].

Despite significant progress, the Southeast Asian region remains the second-largest contributor to global anemia, with a prevalence of approximately 43% in 2021 [1]. In recent decades, Vietnam has made considerable progress in socio-economic and health outcomes. The GBD 2021 revealed a decline in all-cause anemia in Vietnam from 23.9% in 1990 to 16.2% in 2021 [1]. Similarly, the global nutrition report reflects a 6.7% decline (from 27.3% in 2000 to 20.6% in 2019) in anemia among women of reproductive age in Vietnam [8]. However, these improvements fall short of the World Health Organization’s (WHO’s) Global Nutrition Target, which aims for a 50% reduction in anemia prevalence among women of reproductive age (15–49 years) by 2030 to achieve the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) indicator 2.2 (end all forms of malnutrition) [9]. Notably, rural areas, northern mountainous regions, and ethnic minorities experience slow or worsening anemia rates, underscoring geographic disparities [10–12].

To address these gaps, Vietnam’s National Nutrition Strategy (NNS), issued in 2022, aims to minimize inequities or barriers to access to care [13]. However, delays in NNS approval and implementation, coupled with data and resource constraints, have hindered early intervention integration. Stagnant trends in anemia reduction, combined with these constraints, suggest that the SDG targets for reducing anemia will remain unmet by 2030. Therefore, understanding anemia’s prevalence, trends, and causes is critical for developing effective, context-specific measures to combat anemia and improve health outcomes. This study aims to conduct an in-depth examination of anemia’s prevalence, trends, and causes in Vietnam, highlighting age and gender differences to inform targeted interventions and policy decisions to achieve multiple health-related SDG targets by 2030.

This study stands out in several ways compared to previous research, making significant contributions to understanding the burden of anemia in Vietnam. First, while previous studies have explored the socio-economic determinants of anemia in various contexts, such as among young women in Ethiopia [14] and the Iranian population [15], our study uniquely focuses on Vietnam, a country with distinct socio-economic and demographic characteristics. By examining the impact of human development, gender development, and gender inequality on the anemia burden in the long run, this study offers a comprehensive and context-specific analysis that has not been previously explored in Vietnam. Second, this study utilizes the latest Global Burden of Disease (GBD) data over a much longer period—spanning nearly 32 years from 1990 to 2021—compared to other studies that often focus on shorter time frames [16, 17].

This robust and methodologically sound approach delivers valuable insights for policy makers, researchers, and healthcare practitioners, facilitating the development of effective interventions and policies to combat anemia. Third, in addition to the descriptive analysis of GBD data, this study goes a step further by employing an empirical regression model to quantitatively assess the effects of Vietnam’s socio-economic dimensions on the anemia burden [18]. Our study uses a macro-level regression model to explore national trends and socio-economic determinants of anemia in Vietnam, differing from the micro-level multi-level analysis conducted in East Africa [19]. While both methodologies offer valuable insights, our approach provides a broader understanding of national trends specific to Vietnam. Furthermore, unlike previous studies that focused primarily on childhood malnutrition in Vietnam [20], this study uniquely addresses anemia across all age groups and incorporates a broader range of socio-economic factors, thereby offering a more comprehensive view.

Method

This study has used the Global Burden of Diseases, Injuries, and Risk Factors Study (GBD) 2021 dataset to explore the burden of anemia and its causes in Vietnam. This GBD 2021 data is published by the Institute of Health Metric Evaluation under the umbrella of the Global Buren of Disease Collaborative Networks [1]. This dataset provides estimates of anemia prevalence and years lived with disability by 37 underlying causes, three severity levels (i.e., Mild anemia, Moderate anemia, and Severe anemia), age, and sex for 204 countries and territories, and selected subnational geographies in 5-year increments from 1990 to 2021. In this study, we have extracted data related to burden anemia (i.e., Prevalence cases and YLD rate) in Vietnam from 1990 to 2021 for male and female sexes and 25 age groups (0–6 days, 7–27 days, 1–5 months, 6–11 months, 12–23 months, 2–4 years, 5–94 years in 5-year age bins, and ≥ 95 years). Further, we have divided the age groups into different categories, such as Infants (0–23 months), Early Childhood (2–9 years), Adolescents (10–19 years), Young Adults (20–39 years), Middle-Aged Adults (40–64 years), and Older Adults (65 + years).

In GBD 2021, anemia has been defined as a decrease in hemoglobin (Hb) concentration in the blood, regardless of the underlying cause, red blood cell morphology, or red blood cell function [11]. The thresholds used to determine if an individual is anemic and the severity of the anemia are based on the WHO’s hemoglobin thresholds in g/L [21]. The GBD 2021 anemia collaborators’ (2023) [22] study outlines the hemoglobin concentration thresholds (in g/L) used for the classification of anemia severity by age, sex, and pregnancy status based on WHO guidelines. Mild Anemia: Hemoglobin levels fall within the lower end of the normal range. For example, in males aged ≥ 15 years, mild anemia would typically be classified with hemoglobin levels between 110 and 129 g/L; and for females aged ≥ 15 years, non-pregnant, the threshold for would be between 110 and 119 g/L. Moderate anemia: Hemoglobin levels fall significantly below the normal range, indicating a moderate deficiency in red blood cells. For instance, in males aged ≥ 15 years, moderate anemia would be classified with hemoglobin levels between 80 and 109 g/L; for females aged ≥ 15 years, non-pregnant, the threshold would be between 80 and 109 g/L. Severe anemia: Hemoglobin levels are critically low, indicating a severe deficiency in red blood cells. For example, in males aged ≥ 15 years, severe anemia would typically be classified with hemoglobin levels below 80 g/L; and for females aged ≥ 15 years, non-pregnant, it would be classified with hemoglobin levels below 80 g/L.

Apart from collecting data from the GBD study for descriptive analysis, this study uniquely estimates the determinants of anemia burden in Vietnam from 1990 to 2021. This study empirically examines the effects of socio-economic factors such as human development, gender development, and gender inequality on the burden of anemia (i.e., measured as prevalence rate and YLDs rate) in Vietnam from 1990 to 2021. While previous research has highlighted the intricate links between anemia and socio-economic factors such as household wealth, poverty, maternal education, and healthcare, significantly affecting vulnerable populations [16, 20, 22]. The choice of the Human Development Index (HDI), Gender Development Index (GDI), and Gender Inequality Index (GII) as independent variables in our model is grounded in comprehensive literature that highlights their relevance in understanding the burden of anemia. The HDI is a composite measure of three key dimensions of human development: health (life expectancy at birth), education (mean years of schooling and expected years of schooling), and standard of living (gross national income per capita). Studies have shown a strong correlation between higher HDI scores and improved health outcomes, including lower prevalence of anemia [23, 24].

Studies have found a significant relationship between HDI and the prevalence of iron deficiency anemia in the Asian female population, indicating that higher HDI is associated with lower anemia rates [23]. Previous studies demonstrated that the proportion of anemia associated with iron deficiency varies significantly across countries with different HDI levels, underscoring the importance of socio-economic development in reducing the anemia burden [24]. The GDI measures gender disparities in human development achievements across the same three dimensions as the HDI. A higher GDI score indicates low gender disparity. Gender equality in access to resources and opportunities is crucial for improving health outcomes, including reducing the burden of anemia [25]. This study highlighted gender disparities in the functional significance of anemia, showing that gender-based differences impact health outcomes. Higher GDI scores, indicating greater gender equality, are associated with better health outcomes [25]. The GII reflects gender-based inequalities in reproductive health, empowerment, and economic status. Higher GII scores indicate greater gender inequality, which can limit women’s access to healthcare and nutrition, increasing the risk of anemia. A study found that social inequalities, including gender inequality, play a significant role in the prevalence of anemia among women in India. Addressing gender inequality is essential for reducing anemia rates [26].

The summary statistics and correlation analysis provide valuable insights into the socio-economic determinants of anemia in Vietnam over the period 1990–2021. The data indicates that higher human development and gender equality are strongly associated with lower anemia prevalence and disability. Conversely, higher gender inequality correlates with a greater anemia burden. These findings underscore the importance of improving socio-economic conditions and promoting gender equality to address anemia effectively.

Results

Prevalence of total anemia cases and severity level

Online Supplementary Table 2 presents total anemia cases across all ages in Vietnam between 1990 and 2021, and reveals both progress and persistent challenges. In 1990, the total number of anemia cases was 16.30 million, which decreased slightly to 16.05 million in 2021. This represents a modest reduction of 0.25 million cases, or approximately 1.5%. When breaking down the data by gender, a clear divergence in trends emerges. In 1990, there were 5.69 million anemia cases among males and 10.61 million among females. By 2021, anemia prevalence among males had dropped to 4.27 million, a significant 25% reduction. In contrast, anemia cases among females increased by 11%, from 10.61 million in 1990 to 11.78 million in 2021. The slight overall reduction in anemia prevalence masks notable shifts in the age distribution of cases. While anemia has decreased significantly among infants, young children, and adolescents, reflecting improvements in maternal and child healthcare, the burden has shifted toward middle-aged and older adults. Anemia cases among middle-aged adults and older people have surged, particularly among females. This rising trend among older populations may be driven by factors such as aging-related health conditions, chronic diseases, and inadequate attention to nutrition and healthcare for these groups.

Figure 1 illustrates the prevalence of anemia cases categorized by severity (mild, moderate, and severe) across all age groups and both sexes in Vietnam from 1990 to 2021. The data indicates a notable shift in the distribution of anemia severity over the period. In 2021, mild anemia accounted for 58% of total anemia cases, marking an increase from 53% in 1990. Conversely, moderate anemia constituted 38% of cases in 2021, down from 42% in 1990. Severe anemia remained relatively low throughout the period, comprising only 4% of cases.

Hookworm disease, a historically significant cause of anemia, saw a dramatic reduction, dropping from 0.32 million cases in 1990 to just 12,792 cases in 2021, falling from the 6th to the 20th rank. This significant decline highlights the success of deworming programs and improved sanitation in reducing parasitic infections that contribute to anemia [30, 31]. Vitamin A deficiency as a cause of anemia decreased from 0.27 million (1.65%) in 1990 to 66,637 cases (0.42%) in 2021, reflecting improved nutritional interventions and fortification programs that have likely helped reduce the prevalence of vitamin deficiencies. PfPr malaria, a previously significant contributor to anemia, was effectively eliminated as a cause by 2021, demonstrating the success of malaria eradication efforts in Vietnam. Chronic kidney disease (CKD), particularly at stage 3, increased as a cause of anemia, rising from 192,113 cases (1.18%) in 1990 to 327,856 cases (2.04%) in 2021. This reflects a growing burden of chronic diseases as the population ages and highlights the need for targeted healthcare interventions to manage anemia in patients with CKD. HIV/AIDS emerged as a notable cause of anemia, with cases rising from 2,084 in 1990 to 18,062 in 2021. This increase underscores the need for continued focus on HIV/AIDS management, particularly its impact on anemia. Anemia related to gastritis and duodenitis, as well as higher-stage CKD, also saw increases in 2021, reflecting ongoing challenges associated with chronic gastrointestinal and renal conditions. Some causes, such as schistosomiasis and hemoglobin SC disease, showed negligible or zero prevalence by 2021, indicating successful healthcare interventions and eradication efforts. Additionally, clinical malaria saw a near elimination by 2021, another testament to successful disease control programs in the country.

Among adolescents (10–19 years), the prevalence of anemia shifted, with iron deficiency accounting for 10.28% and beta-thalassemia trait making up 10.39%, highlighting the growing influence of genetic hemoglobinopathies alongside nutritional factors. As the population enters young adulthood (20–39 years), the leading causes become iron deficiency at 28.45% and beta-thalassemia trait at 25.60%, indicating a shift towards genetic factors as the primary drivers of anemia. Notably, in middle-aged adults (40–64 years), iron deficiency (28.44%) and beta-thalassemia trait (29.46%) remained the predominant causes, showing continuity from young adulthood. In older adults (65 + years), CKD Stage 4 (66.81%) emerged as the leading cause, reflecting the increasing role of chronic diseases, particularly kidney disease, in anemia prevalence among the elderly population.

A comparison of the 2021 data with 1990 reveals several shifts in the top causes of anemia in Vietnam. In 1990, iron deficiency was the leading cause across most age groups, with higher prevalence rates compared to 2021. For instance, iron deficiency contributed to 11.57% of anemia cases in infants in 1990, while in 2021, it reduced to 7.58%. This decline suggests improvements in nutrition and public health interventions targeting iron supplementation in younger children. Similarly, iron deficiency was more prevalent in early childhood in 1990 (21.52%) compared to 2021 (12.97%), further indicating progress in addressing micronutrient deficiencies.

The role of Vitamin A deficiency, however, remained prominent, particularly in early childhood. In 1990, it accounted for 61.41% of anemia cases in early childhood, and though slightly reduced, it still constituted 59.97% in 2021. This suggests that while there have been advancements in addressing iron deficiency, Vitamin A deficiency continues to be a persistent issue. In contrast to 1990, the prevalence of hemoglobinopathies such as beta-thalassemia trait and other hemoglobinopathies became more pronounced in 2021. In 1990, beta-thalassemia trait accounted for 7.87% of anemia cases among infants, whereas by 2021, it had risen to 10.39% in adolescents and 25.60% in young adults. This increase reflects greater recognition and possibly improved diagnostic capabilities for genetic causes of anemia over time. In the older adult population (65 + years), a notable shift occurred from iron deficiency being the leading cause in 1990 (6.66%) to CKD Stage 4 (66.81%) becoming the predominant cause in 2021. This change illustrates the growing impact of chronic diseases, particularly kidney disease, on anemia among older adults. The rise in anemia linked to chronic kidney disease (CKD) in older adults highlights the transition from nutritional and infectious causes of anemia in younger populations to more chronic and degenerative conditions in aging individuals.

Causes of anemia YLDs rate

Table 2 presents the ranking of YLDs rates (per 100,000 population) and the percent distribution of anemia-related YLDs by different causes in Vietnam for the years 1990 and 2021. In both 1990 and 2021, iron deficiency remained the leading cause of anemia, responsible for 67.44% of anemia YLDs in 1990 and increasing slightly to 68.79% in 2021. Although the YLDs rate for iron deficiency anemia decreased from 482.1 to 294.65 per 100,000 population over time, it still accounts for the largest share of anemia YLDs in Vietnam. The overall YLDs rate for all causes of anemia decreased from 714.8 per 100,000 population in 1990 to 428.33 in 2021. This reduction reflects progress in managing and reducing the anemia burden, though significant challenges remain. Shifts in other causes of anemia YLDs rates, such as other hemoglobinopathies, other NTDs (Neglected Tropical Diseases), and beta-thalassemia trait, were observed in 2021 compared to 1990. Decreases in Vitamin A deficiency and hookworm disease YLDs rates were noted in Vietnam in 2021 compared to 1990. Emerging contributions of chronic conditions, such as Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD), particularly stages 3 and 4, show increasing relevance as causes of anemia. The contribution of malaria (both PvPr and PfPr types) to anemia YLDs has drastically reduced over time, with rates nearly reaching zero in 2021. Anemia related to menstrual disorders and maternal hemorrhage continues to contribute to the overall burden, but remains relatively minor compared to other causes.

Causes of anemia YLDs rate by sex and by age in Vietnam

Online Supplementary Figs. 4–5 illustrate the causes of anemia-related disability across age groups for males and females in 2021. In early childhood (0–9 years), both sexes, iron deficiency is the leading cause, contributing to the majority of YLDs in infants. This reflects the critical need for improved nutrition in early childhood for both boys and girls to prevent anemia, with iron deficiency being a common factor. During adolescence (10–19 years), the anemia burden drops significantly for both males and females. However, females continue to experience a higher anemia rate (136.61 YLDs) compared to males (80.04 YLDs). As individuals transition into young adulthood (20–39 years), the anemia burden continues to decline for both sexes, with females exhibiting 89.01 YLDs and males 68.72 YLDs. For females, iron deficiency remains a significant concern, particularly in reproductive years due to menstruation and pregnancy. In middle adulthood (40–64 years), the anemia burden remains relatively low for both sexes, though females have a slightly higher rate (91.71 YLDs) compared to males (76.44 YLDs).

In older adults (65 + years), there is a notable increase in the anemia burden for both sexes, with females experiencing a significantly higher rate (1003.29 YLDs) compared to males (762.08 YLDs). This sharp rise in anemia prevalence among older women may be associated with age-related factors such as malnutrition, chronic health conditions, and decreased nutrient absorption. Iron deficiency remains the leading cause of anemia in both sexes, though other factors like hemoglobinopathies and non-communicable diseases also begin to contribute more prominently in older adults. Overall, the YLDs data reveals that anemia disproportionately affects females across most age groups, particularly in adolescence, reproductive years, and older adulthood, with iron deficiency being the leading cause. While males also face a significant burden of anemia, the differences in prevalence between the sexes are most pronounced in adolescence and old age.

The dependent variables are the prevalence rate of anemia (columns 1–3) and the Years Lived with Disability (YLDs) rate (columns 4–6). Starting with the prevalence rate of anemia, the results show that HDI has a statistically significant negative relationship with anemia prevalence. Specifically, a 1 unit increase in HDI is associated with a 2.143-unit reduction in the anemia prevalence rate, suggesting that higher levels of human development substantially reduce the prevalence of anemia. Similarly, GDI shows a negative and significant coefficient of -1.824, indicating that improvements in gender development lead to a 1.824 unit decrease in the prevalence of anemia. These results suggest that both overall human development and gender equality play crucial roles in reducing the anemia burden. On the other hand, the Gender Inequality Index (GII) has a positive and significant association with anemia prevalence, with a coefficient of 1.523. This means that a 1 unit increase in GII, indicating greater gender inequality, results in a 1.523 unit rise in anemia prevalence. The positive relationship underscores the detrimental impact of gender inequality on public health, particularly on anemia. Thus, policies that reduce gender inequality may contribute significantly to alleviating anemia in Vietnam.

In the case of YLDs rate, the findings are consistent with the prevalence results. A 1 unit increase in HDI leads to a 1.592-unit reduction in the YLDs rate, further supporting the idea that improved human development reduces the disability burden associated with anemia. Similarly, GDI is associated with a 1.146 unit decrease in the YLDs rate, suggesting that improvements in gender development lessen the disability impact of anemia. In conclusion, the regression results highlight the critical role of socio-economic factors in shaping the anemia burden in Vietnam. Higher human development and gender equality significantly reduce both anemia prevalence and the disability burden associated with it, while gender inequality exacerbates the problem. The findings underscore the importance of continuing efforts to improve socio-economic conditions and reduce gender inequality to combat anemia effectively.

Discussion

The analysis of anemia prevalence in Vietnam from 1990 to 2021 reveals both progress and persistent challenges. Despite a slight overall reduction in total anemia cases, the gender disparity in anemia prevalence has widened significantly. Male anemia cases decreased by 25%, whereas female cases increased by 11% over the same period. This divergence suggests that while public health interventions have been effective for males, females continue to face significant barriers. Factors such as reproductive health challenges, societal inequalities, and differential access to healthcare and nutrition likely contribute to this persistent gender gap [2, 3].

The shift in the age distribution of anemia cases is another critical finding. Anemia has decreased among infants, young children, and adolescents, reflecting improvements in maternal and child healthcare. However, the burden has shifted toward middle-aged and older adults, particularly females. This rising trend among older populations may be driven by aging-related health conditions, chronic diseases, and inadequate attention to nutrition and healthcare for these groups [4, 32]. The success of deworming programs and improved sanitation is evident in the significant reduction of anemia due to hookworm disease, highlighting effective public health interventions [30].

Iron deficiency remains the dominant cause of anemia, accounting for approximately 68% of cases in both 1990 and 2021. However, other causes, such as beta-thalassemia trait [33, 34] and chronic kidney disease (CKD), have become more prominent over time. The increase in anemia cases related to CKD, particularly in older adults, underscores the growing burden of chronic diseases in Vietnam’s aging population [17, 35]. The near elimination of malaria as a cause of anemia by 2021 is a significant public health achievement, reflecting successful eradication efforts [1]. However, the persistently high prevalence of anemia due to iron deficiency among females, particularly those of reproductive age, indicates that nutritional interventions need to be more targeted and effective [36].

The YLD rates for anemia have declined significantly from 1990 to 2021, indicating an overall reduction in anemia-related disability. However, the gender disparity in YLD rates has widened, with females bearing a greater share of the anemia burden. This is particularly pronounced among adolescents and young adults, where reproductive health factors and socio-economic inequalities likely play a significant role [20, 37, 38].

The regression analysis highlights the critical role of socio-economic factors in shaping the anemia burden in Vietnam. Higher levels of human development (HDI) and gender development (GDI) are associated with significant reductions in both anemia prevalence and YLD rates. Conversely, greater gender inequality (GII) exacerbates the anemia burden, underscoring the detrimental impact of socio-economic disparities on public health [23, 24].

Addressing the root causes of anemia, particularly among females, requires comprehensive strategies that encompass nutritional interventions, improved access to healthcare, and efforts to reduce gender inequalities. Enhancing public health education, focusing on reproductive health, and ensuring adequate nutrition for all age groups are essential steps. Additionally, targeted interventions for chronic diseases, particularly CKD, are crucial as the population ages [17, 32].

The GBD data offers significant advantages for understanding and addressing anemia in Vietnam. By integrating a wide array of data sources and employing advanced disease modeling techniques, the GBD study provides accurate and comprehensive estimates of anemia’s prevalence, incidence, mortality, and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs). The incorporation of critical covariates such as socio-economic status, dietary factors, and healthcare access ensures a nuanced understanding of anemia’s determinants. Additionally, rigorous uncertainty analysis enhances the reliability of the estimates, while the longitudinal data spanning from 1990 to 2021 allows for tracking trends over time. One of the key issues is data availability, especially in low- and middle-income countries like Vietnam at the province level. In such regions, national health statistics are often incomplete, leading to a reliance on estimates and models that introduce uncertainty [38, 39]. Moreover, the frequent methodological changes in GBD studies can cause inconsistencies across different iterations, making longitudinal comparisons more challenging [38]. These challenges highlight the need for better data collection mechanisms and consistent methodologies to improve the accuracy of health burden estimates. Additionally, frequent methodological changes can lead to inconsistencies across different GBD iterations, complicating longitudinal comparisons [38, 40]. Apart from GBD data, the study can also analyze Demographic Health Survey (DHS) data, which is rare in the context of Vietnam. This could be one of our limitations in not exploring the DHS data. However, our study has attempted to address the burden of anemia factors using available socio-economic indicators at the aggregate level. The findings from Vietnam may not be directly applicable to other countries due to differing socio-economic and health care factors. Despite these limitations, the study provides comprehensive estimates of anemia prevalence, etiology, and YLD by age and gender, aiding public health policy makers in creating effective strategies to reduce anemia in Vietnam.

Conclusions

This study highlights both progress and ongoing challenges in addressing anemia in Vietnam from 1990 to 2021. While overall anemia rates have declined and specific interventions like iron-folic acid supplementation and deworming have been effective, gender disparities persist and have worsened, particularly among females and older adults. Current strategies, though impactful, are insufficient for tackling the complex nature of iron deficiency anemia (IDA). A more comprehensive approach is needed, addressing additional factors such as vitamin deficiencies, chronic diseases, environmental stressors, and socio-economic inequalities. The shift in anemia burden to older adults and the continued gender disparity emphasizes the need for targeted interventions. Improvements in socio-economic factors, such as human development and education, are crucial for reducing anemia rates. Future research should focus on enhancing data quality and methodological consistency. A holistic approach integrating nutritional interventions with socio-economic improvements will be essential for significantly reducing anemia prevalence and improving public health in Vietnam.
