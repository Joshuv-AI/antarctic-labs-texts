Integrative risk mapping of musculoskeletal disorders among office workers: a hybrid Delphi–Fuzzy AHP approach
Authors: Marzieh Abbasinia, Elham Entezarizarch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMusculoskeletal disorders (MSDs) remain a major occupational health concern among office workers due to the combined effects of ergonomic, biomechanical, psychosocial, organizational, environmental, and individual factors.This study aimed to investigate and prioritize key risk factors contributing to musculoskeletal disorders (MSDs) among office workers using a structured Delphi–FAHP approach. In the first stage, a structured non-systematic literature review was conducted to identify an initial pool of risk factors. These factors were then refined and validated through a two-round Delphi process involving experts in occupational health and ergonomics. In the final stage, FAHP was applied to determine the relative weights of the main domains and sub-factors. A total of 42 risk factors were identified and categorized into six main domains.The results showed that ergonomic/workstation factors had the highest relative weight (0.223), followed by physical/biomechanical (0.195), psychosocial (0.195), organizational (0.190), environmental (0.145), and individual factors (0.050). At the sub-factor level, awkward postures (0.42), job stress (0.39), working hours and scheduling (0.38), and monitor-related factors (0.37) were identified as the most influential contributors to MSD risk.These findings emphasize the dominant role of workstation design and postural factors, while also focusing on the significant contribution of psychosocial and organizational dimensions. These findings provide a prioritized understanding of MSD risk factors in office environments, which can support more targeted ergonomic interventions and informed decision-making.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-026-09993-6.Keywords: Musculoskeletal disorders , Office workers, Delphi method, Fuzzy AHP, Risk mapping, Ergonomics

Introduction

Musculoskeletal disorders (MSDs) constitute one of the most widespread as well as most expensive occupational health problems. The burden of these disorders affects individuals, organizations, and society. Educators and practitioners need to recognize that these disorders, although preventable, continue to be a major problem across different work settings. MSDs are associated with loss of vitality at work, increased absenteeism, reduced productivity, and increased costs of production and loss of service [1]. MSDs are defined as adverse effects on the muscles, tendons, ligaments, and peripheral nerves of various body regions, such as the lower back, neck and shoulders, lower leg and forearms, and hands [1].

In 2015 U.S. reports, MSDs represented 32% of all occupational illnesses [2]. In addition, according to the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, MSDs account for 29% of all lost work time, indicating a considerable economic burden. MSDs are also estimated to represent approximately one-third of occupational diseases [3]. In the UK, in 2011, out of 1.073 million work-related illnesses, 439,000 cases were attributed to MSDs [4]. It has been projected that by 2035, MSDs will affect approximately 2.161 billion people, increasing disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) [5]. MSDs are also among the most prevalent work-related diseases in Iran, accounting for 7% of total illnesses and 14% of medical visits [6]. Overall, reduced productivity, absenteeism, and healthcare costs associated with MSDs impose a substantial burden on organizations [7].

The economic burden of MSDs can be categorized into direct, indirect, and intangible costs, including medical expenses, productivity loss, and reduced quality of life [8–10]. These costs highlight the substantial impact of MSDs on both employees and organizations.

Musculoskeletal disorders are common among office workers, mainly because their work involves prolonged sitting and repetitive computer-based activities. Many studies have reported that the prevalence of MSDs among office workers can reach approximately 60% [11].

In this study, office workers are defined as individuals primarily engaged in administrative and computer-based tasks in office environments. Workstation-related factors such as the arrangement, tilt, and alignment of desks, chairs, monitors, keyboards, and mice play an important role in the development of these disorders [12]. In addition, previous studies have applied multi-criteria decision-making (MCDM) approaches to optimize ergonomic parameters in physically demanding tasks, demonstrating their effectiveness in reducing musculoskeletal risk and improving worker comfort and performance [13, 14].

These conditions are often associated with persistent pain, reduced work performance, and long-term health consequences. MSDs are complex and multifactorial in nature. In addition to personal factors such as age, gender, general health, and medical history, physical risk factors including awkward postures, repetitive motions, and inappropriate equipment contribute to their development. In addition, psychosocial risk factors (such as work-related stress, low job satisfaction, and limited social support) can increase mental fatigue and psychological strain, ultimately increasing the risk of MSDs [15].

Furthermore, several less obvious factors contribute to the development of MSDs but are difficult to identify and quantify. High cognitive load, lack of sleep, and mental exhaustion have been linked to poor concentration, impaired posture control, and increased muscle tension [16]. Sleep disturbances can also increase fatigue levels [17] and indirectly contribute to musculoskeletal pain [18]. Moreover, factors such as leadership style, management practices, and burnout may influence MSD risk but are often difficult to measure objectively. Furthermore, many ergonomic assessment methods were originally developed for physically demanding occupations and may not adequately capture static loads, psychosocial stressors, and cognitive workload in office environments.

Work-related musculoskeletal disorders (WMSDs), which represent a subset of MSDs specifically associated with occupational exposures, negatively affect employee health and well-being.

Numerous studies have reported their association with reduced job performance, increased absenteeism, and higher medication use [19]. Despite growing attention to risk assessment, the identification and prioritization of WMSD risk factors in office settings remain insufficiently addressed.

To address this gap, it is essential to systematically identify, classify, and prioritize these factors using structured and integrated approaches. Therefore, this study employs a combination of the Delphi technique and the Fuzzy Analytic Hierarchy Process (FAHP). The Delphi method enables expert consensus, while FAHP allows prioritization under uncertainty through pairwise comparisons [18]. These methods have been widely applied in occupational health and decision-making studies to address complex, multi-criteria problems, making them suitable for analyzing and prioritizing MSD risk factors in office environments [20].

Methods

This study utilized a descriptive-analytical design with a mixed quantitative–qualitative approach to identify, classify, and prioritize the risk factors associated with musculoskeletal disorders (MSDs) among office workers engaged in administrative and computer-based tasks. Due to the multifactorial nature of these disorders, two established methodologies, the Delphi technique and the Fuzzy Analytic Hierarchy Process (FAHP) were integrated to collect expert knowledge, reduce uncertainty, and prioritize the identified risk factors.

Phase 1: Literature review and identification of risk factors

The initial phase involved a structured non-systematic literature review conducted using scientific databases including Web of Science, PubMed, and Scopus. This review was designed to identify a broad range of potential risk factors rather than to perform a fully systematic synthesis.

Unlike a systematic review, which follows strict protocols such as PRISMA and predefined quality appraisal, this structured review focused on comprehensively identifying relevant factors for subsequent expert evaluation. Therefore, flexibility in study selection was maintained to avoid excluding potentially relevant risk factors.

The screening process included removal of duplicates, title/abstract screening, and full-text review. Two researchers independently reviewed the selected studies, and disagreements were resolved through discussion.

From the selected studies, an initial list of risk factors was extracted based on their reported relevance and frequency across literature. These factors were then grouped and refined based on conceptual similarity and expert judgment, forming the basis for the Delphi phase. The inclusion of risk factors in the Delphi–FAHP analysis was guided by both evidence from the literature and expert consensus to ensure relevance and completeness.

Validation and consistency

The consistency of pairwise comparisons at both factor and domain levels was assessed using the fuzzy consistency index to ensure the scientific validity of the results. Additionally, the Delphi rounds were repeated until achieving sufficient consensus, defined as a minimum of 75% agreement on the importance of the factors.

Data analysis

After collecting the completed checklists from the experts, along with the pairwise comparison matrices and the corresponding preference values assigned by each specialist, the data were extracted and initially processed using Microsoft Excel. Subsequently, the aggregated expert judgments (based on arithmetic means) and the coefficients of each pairwise comparison matrix for the 15 experts participating in the study were calculated using MATLAB software.

Discussion

During this research, using a combined Delphi–FAHP method, a detailed list of 42 risk factors related to office workers’ WMSDs was identified and categorized into six conceptual clusters. The findings clearly indicate that ergonomic and workstation-related factors are the dominant contributors to WMSDs in office settings, with the highest weight (0.223). In contrast, individual factors had the lowest weight (0.05), suggesting that workplace conditions may play a more significant role than personal characteristics. This indicates that in office environments, the design of the workspace and how employees interact with office equipment are key determinants of musculoskeletal disorders. The relative weights of psychosocial and organizational factors also reveal that non-physical components can indirectly influence the severity and occurrence of WMSD symptoms. Overall, these findings highlight the need for an integrated approach (including physical, psychosocial, and organizational interventions) in preventing musculoskeletal disorders.

A key interpretation of these results is the central role of workstation design in shaping musculoskeletal risk. Furniture such as chairs, monitors, and desks appears to have the most influential effect on the development of MSDs in office environments. This is supported by the findings of Khademi et al. (2025), which reported that the interaction between office furniture and anthropometric characteristics, particularly chair design and monitor height, is strongly associated with neck and back discomfort [21]. Several studies have also indicated that specific workstation design elements, including chair dimensions, armrests, monitor height, telephone position, and mouse placement, significantly contribute to MSDs among office workers [22]. Rodrigues et al. similarly reported that poor ergonomic design of office chairs and workstation setups is associated with greater musculoskeletal pain complaints [23]. Moreover, Zeverdegani et al. (2022) emphasized the importance of ergonomic chair design in reducing MSD risk [24]. The highly affected body areas (such as the lower back, neck, and knees) can be alleviated through the use of adjustable and supportive seating systems [25]. From a biomechanical perspective, mismatches between workstation dimensions and user anthropometry, combined with prolonged use, result in sustained static loading of muscles, leading to fatigue, inflammation, and chronic musculoskeletal conditions.

Another important finding is the comparable contribution of ergonomic, biomechanical, and psychosocial factors (approximately 0.195 each), indicating that MSDs are not solely driven by physical conditions. Workplace stress, awkward posture, and workload were identified as the most influential sub-factors. This highlights the multidimensional nature of MSD risk in office settings. Previous studies have shown that job stress significantly contributes to MSD development [26] and can increase symptom severity by up to 34% [27]. Putsa et al. also reported a strong relationship between MSDs and moderate to severe psychological stress among workers [28]. In addition, prolonged stress can lead to increased muscle tension and reduced attention to posture, thereby increasing the likelihood of musculoskeletal symptoms [28]. Furthermore, repetitive movements and high workload can result in cumulative fatigue and chronic pain.

The results of this study also emphasize the interaction between multiple risk factors. Even when workstation design is appropriate, adverse psychosocial or organizational conditions (such as high workload, poor scheduling, or inadequate organizational support) can negatively influence posture, movement patterns, and physical strain. Studies have shown that working posture, work behavior, and organizational factors such as workload and scheduling, as well as environmental conditions like lighting, noise, and temperature, play a significant role in the development and aggravation of MSDs [29, 30]. Environmental factors, including glare, inappropriate temperature, ventilation issues, and noise, further contribute to MSD risk [25]. Therefore, addressing MSDs requires a holistic approach that considers both physical and non-physical work conditions.

From a mechanical and physiological perspective, improper workstation configurations (such as mismatched desk, chair, and monitor dimensions) lead to sustained muscle loading over time. This results in muscle fatigue, ischemic changes, and tendon inflammation, which may eventually become chronic. Additionally, psychosocial factors such as job stress and workload can amplify these physical effects by increasing muscle tension and reducing attention to proper posture.

This study contributes both theoretically and practically to existing literature. From a theoretical perspective, the findings support the biopsychosocial model of MSDs by demonstrating the combined influence of physical, psychological, and organizational factors. From a practical perspective, the study provides a structured framework for prioritizing risk factors using Delphi–FAHP, which can assist decision-makers in designing targeted interventions. Unlike many previous studies that are primarily descriptive, this research offers a weighted prioritization of risk factors, enabling more effective resource allocation.

Based on the FAHP results, resource allocation should be prioritized according to the relative importance of the identified factors. Interventions should initially focus on improving workstation design, including adjustable chairs, monitor positioning, and desk configuration. Secondary interventions should target biomechanical, organizational, and psychosocial factors. For example, structured rest breaks, workload management, improved organizational support, and stress reduction strategies can help reduce MSD risk. In situations with limited resources, focusing on the highest-weight factors (such as chairs and monitors) may yield the greatest reduction in MSD burden.

There are some limitations to this study. The findings are based on expert judgment and literature review, which may introduce subjectivity and potential bias. The expert panel may not fully represent all relevant disciplines, and the results may not be generalizable to other sectors or countries. Additionally, Delphi–FAHP relies on expert opinion, which may not always align with objective field data such as EMG measurements or clinical prevalence. Future studies should incorporate objective measurements (e.g., wearable posture sensors, EMG) and longitudinal designs to validate these findings. There is also a need for well-designed randomized controlled trials to evaluate the effectiveness of prioritized interventions.

Conclusion

This study identified, classified, and prioritized the risk factors associated with musculoskeletal disorders (MSDs) among office workers using a hybrid Delphi–Fuzzy Analytic Hierarchy Process (FAHP) approach. The results indicate that ergonomic and workstation-related factors are the most influential contributors, while psychosocial and organizational factors also play a meaningful role in shaping MSD risk.

These findings show that MSDs in office environments are not driven by a single factor, but rather by the interaction of physical, organizational, and psychological conditions. Therefore, effective prevention strategies should combine improvements in workstation design with interventions targeting workload management, job stress, and organizational support.

The proposed prioritization framework can assist decision-makers in allocating resources more effectively and designing targeted interventions to reduce the burden of MSDs in office settings.

However, the findings are based on expert judgment and literature review, and future studies are recommended to incorporate objective measurements and longitudinal designs to further validate the results.
