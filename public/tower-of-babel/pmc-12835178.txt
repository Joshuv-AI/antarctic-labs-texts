Optimized deep learning for Indian Classical Dance Classification a novel application based on a refined version of chameleon swarm algorithm
Authors: Di Zhu, Jinliang Sun, Yanwen Lu, Changiz Bastani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIndian Classical Dance (ICD) is a cultural art form much older than history, where the body movements convey emotions, spiritual meanings, or narrative meanings. Automated classification of ICD styles from visual data presents great challenges because of subtle inter-class variations, intra-class variations, and the complexity of human pose representation. Deep learning models have been applied to this kind of work, but the performance is usually limited due to suboptimal parameter tuning. Therefore, this work introduces a new approach to optimize Deep Belief Network (DBN) for accurate classification of ICD styles. The method proposes to use the newly improved Refined Chameleon Swarm Algorithm (RCSA). By incorporating the non-linear adaptive weight mechanism and the Bernoulli chaotic map, RCSA effectively adjusts its search strategy by balancing its tendency to explore with that of exploit, thereby overcoming CSA’s tendency for rapid convergence and improving its global search capability in complex optimization landscapes. The RCSA fine-tunes the architecture and parameters of the DBN, thereby yielding a hybrid model of DBN/RCSA. The model is extensively validated on Indian Dance form Classification (ICD) and Bharatnatyam Dance Poses (BDP) datasets using 5-fold cross-validation against existing state-of-the-art techniques such as DCNN, PointNet, CNN-HPSGW, Transfer Learning, and MobileNet. Empirical results reveal that the DBN/RCSA model gave superior performance in this research with 95% accuracy, 94% in precision, sensitivity, and specificity, and 94% in the F1 score. Further ablation studies have established that RCSA is decisive in enhancing DBN’s performance, corroborated by the results of confusion matrix analysis, which shows reasonably distinguishing ability across classes. This establishes DBN/RCSA as a new model for ICD classification with such accuracy and reliability that it may be widely challenged for application domains like cultural preservation, dance pedagogy, and digital heritage studies.Keywords: Deep learning, Indian classical dance, Deep belief network, Refined chameleon swarm algorithm, Image classification, OptimizationSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering

Introduction

Looking at the history of mankind, we can see different and specific styles in the vast area of ​​human musical dance life, which have always been evolving1. In West Asia, the Arabic-speaking region of North Africa, Iran, Pakistan, India, and Bangladesh, such a melodic system has been achieved that is the ultimate in beauty and harmony, and in which the human voice is used as the most beautiful and expressive musical element2.

Even these vast geographical areas have their divisions, and each division in turn requires numerous other subgroups and groupings, each of which is exclusive in its way and often has a local characteristic3.

To identify different cultures, ecology plays an important role4. Some of these musical dance cultures reached their peak period hundreds of years ago and had significant effects on the thinking and musical creativity of the countries and territories around them, the territories that have experienced numerous wars and victories, floods, and they have seen droughts, but they have still kept their traditions5.

One of the best of these traditions, which never lost its durability but enriched itself both through internal forces and through external thoughts and imaginations, is the tradition of Indian dance6.

Indian dance has a special place in its ancient tradition. Like the multitude of ancient goddesses, there are different types of dances in India, among which we can mention the new Indian dance and the traditional Indian dance7.

Indian classical dance is an art that has always been considered a powerful tool to communicate with God, and although it has a high diversity, only six of its models are known at the national level8.

Indian classical dance was performed by the Russians in 1947 during the last period of the life of the Mughals and Nawabs9. In the same year, India gained its freedom, and the artistic community felt that it was possible to perform the tradition and the magnificent art of dance as in the past10. During these events, special attention was paid to the cultural and traditional differences of each region and tribe, and various Indian dances were performed in the tribes11.

Every Indian dance style is formed based on criteria such as nationality, religion, social needs, and even lifestyle; In such a way that it can be said that the native rituals of many neighboring countries of India are influenced by the traditions of Indian dancers12.

These types of classical dances are performed in different situations, when planting or harvesting crops, celebrations, and local gatherings13.

The advancement of ICD requires the formation of automated systems capable of precisely classifying and identifying various dance styles14. Conventional techniques for dance recognition have depended on manual labeling and rule-based methodologies, which are labor-intensive, subjective, and insufficient in addressing the intricacies of ICD movements.

However, significant strides have been made, a significant gap remains to be filled in terms of research. The only deep learning model available today for the classification of ICDs is effective but applied in the basic form, which fails to capture the subtle, sophisticated features of classical dance poses15. Therefore, these fine distinctions are accompanied by great intra-class variations and fine inter-class differences (like minute differences in hand gestures or foot positions)16.

This, therefore, necessitates a finer tuning of deep network parameters, something that is not always done efficiently with conventional optimization strategies17. Thus far, not much has been carried out concerning the use of newer, refined metaheuristic algorithms to optimize deep generative models such as DBNs for this particular task of cultural recognition18.

This study aims to eliminate that gap with the following purposes: (1) Proposing a new Refined Chameleon Swarm Algorithm (RCSA) with an improved exploration-exploitation balance for better optimization; (2) Designing a fresh DBN/RCSA hybrid model for the precise classification of the Indian Classical Dance forms; and (3) Rigorously comparing performance of the proposed model with state-of-the-art techniques on standard ICD datasets to show superiority in terms of accuracy and robustness.

This study introduces an innovative method for the classification of ICD through an optimized Deep Belief Network (DBN) architecture, which has been fine-tuned based on a refined Chameleon Swarm Algorithm. The method has shown exceptional accuracy and has surpassed leading models on a dataset comprising ICD videos.

The results of this research hold considerable significance for the advancement of automated ICD classification systems, with potential applications in dance education, cultural preservation, and digital media analysis. The key contributions of this research can be outlined as follows:

Related works

The emergence of deep learning methodologies has transformed the domain of computer vision, facilitating the establishment of reliable and precise models for analyzing images and videos. Nevertheless, the utilization of these techniques for ICD classification has been constrained by the absence of extensive datasets and the intricate nature of ICD movements.

For instance, Jain et al.19 established a network to categorize Indian dances into 8 diverse categories. A DCNN (Deep Convolutional Neural Network) is recommended to categorize the data while utilizing ResNet50, and it performed better than the other models. The input data of the recommended network is preprocessed while sampling and thresholding the image. Next, the recommended network was applied to the preprocessed samples. Ultimately, the recommended network achieved an accuracy of 91%.

Naik and Supriya20 utilized geometric deep learning in 3-dimensional images. The PointNet framework functioned efficiently, and the data of the point cloud was used simultaneously. The framework has been employed to categorize Indian classical dance into 5 diverse types, such as Odissi, Kathak, Yakshagana, Bharatanatyam, and Kathakali. The accuracy values of 73% and 96.64% were, in turn, achieved via the recommended network while validating and training stages during 250 epochs.

Challapalli and Devarakonda21 suggested CNN-HPSGW (Hybrid Particle Swarm Grey Wolf). This algorithm is employed to discover the CNN’s optimal parameters, namely the number of hidden layers, size of batch, filter size, and number of epochs. 3 various datasets are involved within the present study, called CIFAR, Indian Classical Dance (ICD), and MNIST, which assess the recommended network. The recommended network achieved the accuracy values of 91.1%, 99.4%, 97.3 for CIFAR, MNIST, and ICD.

After that, a method was established by Reshma et al.22 that categorized diverse kinds of Indian classical dance. There exist numerous utilities suggested via the dance, including entertainment, online learning, remedial treatment, and cultural conservation. Because the data is not that large, deep neural networks and transfer learning are, in turn, utilized for feature classification and extraction. The recommended method demonstrated a noteworthy level of accuracy, which was 97.05%.

Parthasarathy and Palanichamy23 employed CNNs in their study, which was considered a critical technology to recognize the accurate mudra. It was represented by the results that the obtained accuracy ranged from 85% to 95% while utilizing MobileNet with a processing duration of 0.172. In addition, it was revealed that MobileNet performed superior than Inception v3, which was the other type of CNN.

The above advances notwithstanding, a significant gap in the research remains. One, while metaheuristic optimization has been applied to CNNs, it has not been explored for generative deep models like Deep Belief Networks (DBNs) for ICD classification. DBNs possess the power of unsupervised feature learning, which is very relevant to pose classification. The second, typical optimization methods utilize standard algorithms that may suffer from premature convergence or poor exploration-exploitation balance.

There is no metaheuristic algorithm of novelty and refinement aimed specifically at optimizing deep networks for fine-grained cultural recognition tasks. Third, many studies do not focus on accuracy and cannot provide an exhaustive ablation study to quantify the optimization component’s contribution to the overall performance gain. Thus, the actual value of the optimization strategy proposed is difficult to assess. This work attempts to fill these gaps with the introduction of a novel Refined Chameleon Swarm Algorithm (RCSA) to optimize a DBN architecture in the challenging ICD classification case.

Image preprocessing pipeline

Both the ICD and BDP datasets were established during a uniform image preprocessing pipeline which is done before training and evaluation to confirm reproducibility and ensure optimal performance of the model26. Initially, all pixel JPEG images were greyscaled, just so that all pertinent structure and pose information essential for dance classification could be preserved while eliminating computational redundancy.

Then each intensity value was normalized to [0, 1] range by dividing each by 255 to enable gradient descent stabilization during training and speeding convergence. In order to avoid overfitting and improve the model’s generalization performance, given the small dataset sizes, the data augmentation on-the-fly was done while training by MATLAB.

It random flipped images horizontally (50% probability) to accommodate symmetrical dance poses, random rotation (± 15 degrees) to simulate minor viewpoint variations, and width/height shifts of up to 10% of the image dimensions to introduce spatial robustness. Contrast modifications were done since gray scaled images lose any color information.

These augmentation methods were only applied to the training set; validation and test set samples were strictly gray scaled and normalized, ensuring an unbiased evaluation of model performance. This preprocessing regimen ensured that the input fed into the DBN/RCSA model was consistent, noise-resilient, and optimized for feature learning.

Deep belief network (DBN)

DBN is a type of unsupervised learning approach employed in AI that uses probability distributions and generative algorithms27. These models originate from the Boltzmann machine, which was established by Geoffrey Hinton. The main concept of DBN is extracting abstract attributes by the utility of hidden units’ layers. These models have been broadly employed in several tasks, like pattern recognition in contexts such as computer vision, speech recognition, and natural language processing.

A considerable advantage of DBN is its skill in extracting information from unorganized data, decreasing its dependence on organized data28. This becomes particularly useful when there is a shortage of organized data, for example, when processing text or classifying images. Furthermore, DBN is recognized for its capacity to apply knowledge to different tasks and datasets29. This network consists of layers with interconnected neurons that each of which is configured to process input data in a specific way.

The first layer of the DBN is referred to as the layer of input layer. The hierarchical layers are composed of hidden neurons that extract characteristics from the input data. The ultimate layer, called the output layer, has the responsibility of arranging or forecasting the input data. Following the training process, each layer is utilized to make predictions and detect patterns in the input data.

It is important to find a setup for the DBN that improves the chances of a given dataset. This needs to choose an optimal estimation approach or the best option. DBN is implemented using diverse methods, like bio-inspired and backpropagation strategies. Also, using regularization techniques, such as weight decay and dropout, can help avoid overfitting and improve the model’s skill in generalization.

The framework of the DBN is improved by the utilization of an enhanced kind of DBN approach. The number of nodes in the input is ascertained by the data’s attributes, and the number of nodes in the output is ascertained by the classifications.

This makes the approach essential for obtaining the best outcomes. It is crucial to comprehend the framework of the base layer, including the neuron count in each hidden layer and the range of particles. In the end, several initial systems are ready in accordance with an improved metaheuristic algorithm. Refined chameleon swarm algorithm

The Chameleon Swarm Algorithm (CSA) is a bio-inspired metaheuristic approach developed to tackle complicated optimization problems. This algorithm draws inspiration from the exceptional defense mechanism of chameleon swarm, which inflates their bodies to deter predators. The CSA has been specifically crafted to overcome the shortcomings of current optimization methods, addressing challenges related to accuracy, computational costs, and convergence rates.

The Refined Chameleon Swarm Algorithm (RCSA) introduces substantial improvements, including an innovative search strategy, adaptive parameter management, and a balanced approach to exploration and exploitation. These enhancements significantly improve the algorithm’s capacity to deliver superior optimization results and increased accuracy.

A key advancement has been the incorporation of a non-linear adaptive weight component, which broadens the search space by evaluating the quality of potential solutions. This adaptive feature is crucial as it mitigates the risk of premature convergence within the population, fosters diversity, and ultimately enhances overall effectiveness. Algorithmic components and complexity analysis of RCSA

The Refined Chameleon Swarm Algorithm (RCSA), which is an extension of the existing Chameleon Swarm Algorithm (CSA) has 2 notable innovations to it: (1) a non-linear adaptive scheme of weights and (2) the addition of a Bernoulli chaotic map-both of which have some major influences on both convergence behavior and solution quality.

The adaptive weight, represented in Eq. (33) is a dynamically adjusting scaling of the contribution of each candidate solution according to its relative fitness with low-fitness agents being weighted more heavily to encourage a wider exploration of the search space whereas the high-fitness agents are weighted less heavily to encourage local exploration.

This self-regulating equilibrium stops early convergence as is often the case in CSA since the balance keeps a variety of population in the initial sequences and slowly increases focus towards the potential good areas as optimization advances. In addition to this, the Bernoulli chaotic map (Eq. (31)) substitutes the deterministic coefficient in the velocity update (Eq. (30)) by a chaotic sequence, which is non-repetitive and ergodic. It adds randomness to the search path allowing RCSA to better avoid local optima than the linear or random coefficients used by CSA.

The combination of these elements produces less jagged convergence curves (Fig. 14) and greater stability of the solutions (Table 2). In terms of computational complexity, CSA and RCSA both have a population size N and a maximum number of iterations T which results in the optimal time complexity of O(N.T.D) where D is the dimensionality of the search space (i.e. number of hyperparameters in the DBN). RCSA offers small overhead costs associated to adaptive weighting fitness ranking (O(NlogN) per iteration), and chaotic sequence generation (O(1)), and thus the overall complexity is O(N.T.(D + logN)).

Though full-scale video-based modeling (e.g., with 3D CNNs or Transformers) is a direction, this experiment confirms that, even with relatively small increments in dataset size and context diversity (especially through real world performance footage), the generalizability is significantly improved, without the need of architectural modifications.

The identified results highlight the importance of data curation in cultural AI usage and justify our demand of multimodal, community-driven, and ethically sourced dance data collections that could accommodate the diversity of the Indian classical performance traditions.

Temporal dynamics and sequence modeling considerations

The use of motionless frames of the image necessarily allows one to overlook the temporal, rhythmic, and transition qualities inherent to the Indian Classical Dance (ICD). There are many features such as like the flowing nature of lasya in Mohiniyattam, the incisive footwork (tatkar) in Kathak, or the dynamic movement between karanas, which is coded in motion but not pose only.

Though the phenomenon of static classification has been effectively discriminating pose, it does not reflect the semantics of time that often define the stylistic identity and intent of expression (abhinaya). To test this shortcoming, we conducted an initial experiment using short video clips generated using 5-frame sequences sampled at 2fps of twelve publicly available ICD performance videos, which were trained on a lightweight Convolutional Recurrent Neural Network (CRNN) baseline which is a 2D CNN with a bidirectional LSTM.

Categorized on these time series, the CRNN showed an average of 3.2 per cent higher F1 -score than our best static-frame model (DBN/RCSA) in motion-sensitive classes, including Mohiniyattam and Kathak, and thus supports the notion that time modelling does enhance discriminative ability. However, the progression to the full video-based architectures, e.g., 3D CNNs, or Vision Transformers, is out of the scope of the current study, the main goal of which is to optimize the problem of static-pose recognition with limited data availability.

The DBN/RCSA architecture, however, has been designed to be extensible; the high-quality DBN encoder may be used to act as a high-quality spatial feature extractor in a complete spatiotemporal pipeline. Future studies will be concerned with accumulating coordinated video-audio collections and the creation of multimodal frameworks that all concurrently project pose sequences, rhythmic structure (through tala annotations), and music cues, to introduce the performative spirit of ICD in a wholesome manner.

Computational overhead and mitigation strategies

Although the DBN/RCSA model has reaches state-of-the-art accuracy (95) and speed in inference (12 ms/image), the training stage has high computational overhead 8.2 h on the ICD dataset and 14.5 h on BDP - namely, the ability to search hyperparameters via an iterative process called the Refined Chameleon Swarm Algorithm (RCSA).

This price is a result of testing hundreds of candidate architectures on 5-fold cross-validation, with one candidate architecture needing to be fully pre-trained on the DBN on the fine-tuning phase. This burden is tolerable when dealing with offline cultural heritage applications where model reliability is important more than training speed, but does restrict real time adaptability and scalability in resource constrained systems.

To solve this, we considered three potential mitigation options: (1) knowledge distillation to reduce the size of DBN/RCSA to a lightweight student network, (2) mixed-precision training on modern GPUs and (3) early stopping in RCSA using convergence heuristics. We used them on both data sets and compared their effect on training time, model size and performance retention.

The Table 6 results show that the three strategies greatly cut training time with negligible performance loss. Knowledge distillation has the shortest training (less than 3 h on BDP) and allows real-time inference on edge devices (8 ms) although with an accuracy reduction of about 2%--both of which remain competitive to the previous art (e.g., DCNN at 90%).

Mixed-precision training uses Tensor Cores on NVIDIA GPUs to reduce training time by approximately 38 with insignificant loss in accuracy (less than 0.3%), and therefore is suitable in deployment to clouds. The complete DBN/RCSA is preferable to when precision is needed (e.g. in digitalizing archives), the distilled or FP16 variants may become available in real-time or edge cases (e.g. in mobile dance tutoring apps). Neural architecture search (NAS) will be used in the future to optimize accuracy and efficiency jointly with RCSA.

Human-centered design: augmenting cultural expertise

It is necessary to make clear that the DBN/RCSA system is not supposed to substitute the erudite judgment, embodied knowledge, or interpretive wisdom of human gurus, performers, or cultural scholars, but is in fact a facilitating tool that helps increase accessibility, uniformity, and scalability in cultural education.

In dance teaching, as an example, the model can ensure real-time objective feedback to student dancers on the accuracy of poses in case of self-practice: pointing out deviations in the hand mudras or the position of the stance, and leaving the instructor to work on the higher-order artistic components of the practice, such as emotional expression (bhava), rhythmic timing (tala), and narrative narration (abhinaya).

On the same note, within an archival and museum setting, the system has the potential to help curators catalogue and locate visual inventory of particular dance forms or poses of large digital repositories quickly to hasten research without replacing scholarly exegesis. Having the educators to sort through large volumes of repetitive, low-level visual classification work will be demanded less by our method and maintain human agency in the qualitative, context-dependent aspects of ICD that cannot be simulated by algorithms.

This augmentation philosophy as opposed to automation makes sure that the technology is used as a bridge and not a wall between tradition and innovation to empower both the learners and custodians of the cultural heritage as well as respect the invaluable role of human mentorship in passing on the intangible forms of art.

DBN/RCSA model has an accuracy of 95% with F1-score of 94% with a Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC) of 0.92, and has always been shown to perform better than random or opportunistic data splits. The architectural design and optimization strategy of it give a holistic solution to the Indian Classical Dance classification (ICD).

Transfer Learning models have generic ImageNet priors which are not adapted to grayscale, pose-consistent ICD imagery resulting in poor feature transfer and an increase in false-positives. The 3D point cloud predictor, PointNet, is ineffective on 2D RGB data, due to its permutation-invariant design which removes the spatial organization which is vital in mudra recognition.

Despite the fact that CNN-HPSGW manages to better than the conventional CNNs through tuning using hybrid PSO-GWO, it still optimizes only a small number of superficial hyperparameters (e.g., filter size, batch size), at the fixed cost of the network depth and capacity, hence restricting its ability to adapt to data-scarce regimes. Conversely, DBN/RCSA uses unsupervised pre-training to learn useful hierarchical representations directly off unlabeled pose data, thus avoiding the need to use large annotated datasets.

More importantly, RCSA conducts an architecture search, which dynamically finds the best number of hidden layers, neurons and learning rates, controlled by a nonlinear adaptive weight and a Bernoulli chaotic map which averts early convergence and increases global exploration. Ablation experiments confirm that RCSA adds a + 3 -percentage point F1 -score to baseline DBN and + 2 -percentage point to DBN/CSA, thus confirming that refined optimization is decisive. Moreover, Visualization Grad-Cam images depict that DBN/RCSA always foci on culturally salient areas: hands, face, and torso alignment, whereas CNNs tend to be focused on the background or clothing artifact. \Accordingly, the strength of DBN/RCSA is in its co-design: a generative deep architecture specifically designed to operate on sparse, structured pose data and combined with a culturally aware optimizer able to search through challenging search spaces to extract as much discriminative power as possible out of small samples, i.e. it is specially designed to solve fine-grained cultural classification problems where data efficiency and semantic fidelity are the key concerns.

Qualitative evaluation expert feedback from dance practitioners

A qualitative study involving five certified Indian Classical Dance practitioners and educators (three Bharatanatyam gurus, one Mohiniyattam performer, and one Odissi teacher) who had a teaching experience of 10–25 years was used to evaluate the practical value of the DBN/RCSA system outside of the effects shown on the numbers.

Experts were introduced to a subset of images (40) in the ICD and BDP datasets that were correctly and incorrectly classified by the model and how the outputs from the system could be used in two application domains: (1) pedagogical feedback to novice students and (2) digital archiving of pose libraries. In the pedagogy aspect, all five professionals concurred that the system with right classifications (e.g., correct recognition of Swastika or Nataraj with 100% certainty) would be of great advantage to the self-practice, especially in remote or resource-deprived environments where there is no information on live instructors.

According to one Bharatanatyam guru: This tool can immediately indicate inappropriate deviations in position or arm placement in basic poses like Ardhamandalam or Samapadam, which allows the guru to concentrate on abhinaya and rhythm in the classroom. Concerning archiving, the experts acclaimed the consistency of the model in labeling large groups of images, and one Odissi scholar said that it would speed up the digitisation of photo archives of past performances in the 1950–1980 s.

Yet, practitioners also pointed to major shortcomings: all of them were worried that the misclassifications of the system were based on its failure to read bhava (emotional expression) or tala (rhythmic context), both of which are key elements of stylistic identity. A Mohiniyattam dancer has commented: The hand can be the same, but the gentleness in the eyes and the movement of the torso is the tale how this model perceives no more than geometry.

In spite of these cautions, the system received the support offered by four out of five experts as a worthy assistant, though not a substitute, to human teaching, when it is later supplemented with video or audio clues. This qualitative feedback confirms that DBN/RCSA has a concrete potential in cultural education and heritage conservation, as long as it is implemented as a facilitating tool in a human-focused system, that will not ignore the embodied, expressive nature of Indian Classical Dance.

Limitations

The DBN-RCSA model suggested here is a very good classifier for the various forms of Indian Classical Dance (ICD). Several limitations, however, need to be addressed. First, the model has been evaluated on the two curated datasets only, i.e. ICD and BDP datasets from Kaggle, with somewhat controlled settings regarding lighting, background, and camera angles.

It is worth questioning how well the model will generalize to in-the-wild, real-world scenarios, which concern the lighting conditions, occlusions, dynamic background, and different dancer dresses. Second, the analysis was based purely on static image frames; temporal dynamics of the movements and continuity in the dance flow are neglected.

A sequence-based analysis using video data would have captured transitions and the rhythmic patterns that are vital for the distinction between similar poses. Third, it is unimodal; whereas the rich components like music, rhythm, and vocal aspects associated with ICD are equally important, this emphasizes the use of visual data, ignoring the integral audio components.

Fourth, the Deep Belief Network optimized with the Refined Chameleon Swarm Algorithm (RCSA) has a heavy computational requirement, a training time of about 14.5 h on the larger dataset, which restricts its use in real-time or large-scale applications.

Finally, like most deep learning models, the DBN-RCSA system functions as “black box”, and the work at hand does not give an in-depth description of the contributions of specific features or RCSA’s parameter choice in classifying otherwise. These are all limitations that highlight key areas of potential future research and refinement.

Conclusions

Indian Classical Dance (ICD) represents a profound and diverse aspect of India’s cultural legacy, encompassing different officially recognized classical dance forms, each distinguished by its own style and expressive qualities. As digital media continues to gain traction, there is an increasing demand for automated systems capable of accurately classifying and recognizing the various forms of ICD. However, the complicated movements characteristic of ICD present significant challenges for computer vision and machine learning algorithms. Although there have been advancements in dance recognition technologies, the classification of ICD remains a largely underexplored domain. This paper introduced an innovative methodology for ICD classification based on an optimized Deep Belief Network (DBN) architecture. The DBN was refined to improve its accuracy by optimal selection of its parameters based on a refined version of the Chameleon Swarm Algorithm (RCSA). The proposed methodology harnesses the advantages of deep learning and swarm intelligence to extract robust features from dance videos and categorize them into distinct ICD forms. The performance of the approach was assessed on a ICD dataset and benchmark it against leading models, including Deep Convolutional Neural Network (DCNN), PointNet, Hybrid Particle Swarm Grey Wolf (CNN-HPSGW), Transfer learning (TL), and MobileNet, showing its capability to achieve high accuracy and surpass existing techniques. This research played a vital role in the advancement of automated ICD classification systems, which hold potential applications in dance education, cultural preservation, and digital media analysis. In achieving high accuracy with visual data alone, the proposed DBN/RCSA model must understand that Indian Classical Dance is a multimodal art, where audio elements-rhythmic percussion (tabla, mridangam), vocal cues (nattuvangam), and melodic music-are very much at the center of the structure, timing, and expressions of movements. The current analysis focuses on the spatial pose recognition from static frames, and the incorporation of audio signals that may offer complementary information in distinguishing between dance forms with similar visual postures yet differing rhythmic patterns has been ignored. Consequently, the context-dependent performance nuances that are usually audible would be missed in this model. The DBN/RCSA model proposed for use with visual data alone shows considerable promise for accuracy; however, it must be understood within the context of Indian Classical Dance as a multimodal art, wherein aural elements such as rhythmic percussion coming from instruments (e.g. tabla, mridangam), vocal cues (nattuvangam), and melodic music have importance to structure, timing and expression in the dance sequences. This study has been restricted to recognizing poses spatially from static frames without the inclusion of any audio signals, which may have information complementary to the visual contrasts found in similar-looking postures for dance forms but with different rhythmic patterns. Thus, a possibly limiting condition arises where the nuance of performance that comes from its context is lost to the model, only visible through integration with audio-visual conditions. On a practical note, the authors state that the DBN/RCSA model attains the status of a state-of-the-art algorithm by producing results with 95% accuracy, 94% precision, and 94% F1 score on the two standard ICD datasets, which surpass the performance of existing models such as DCNN, PointNet, and MobileNet-in doing so, it is a reliable tool for applications in dance education, cultural preservation, and digital heritage analysis. The results of the proposed DBN/RCSA model are highly accurate by using static image frames, it is important to realize that Indian Classical Dance is primarily a temporal art form where timing of expressive gestures, transitions between poses, and rhythmic movements are the key for accurate classification and understanding. The present study was based on spatial feature extraction from each frame, without incorporating modeling of sequences or temporal dynamics like 2D/3D pose tracking through the video sequence. As a result, some subtle motion patterns and transitional movements, which are important in distinguishing similar-looking poses across different dance forms, may not be picked up. Future extensions would include spatiotemporal models like Convolutional Recurrent Neural Networks (CRNNs) or Transformer-based architectures, trained on synchronized video data, for modeling pose evolution and dynamic motion cues, thereby enhancing the system’s chance of disentangling the whole range of expressive possibilities of ICD performances. Also, in the future, the collection of larger datasets, the exploration of other deep learning architectures, and the development of real-time systems for ICD classification will be considered. Moreover, this research can be extended to other applications, such as dance recommendation systems, dance analysis, and dance generation. By the way, this research has made a significant contribution to the development of automated ICD classification systems, which can have a lasting impact on the preservation and promotion of India’s cultural heritage.

Ethical implications

The DBN/RCSA provides powerful resultw for ICD forms, where the humane practice of classification for these culturally are rich and spiritually significant art forms raises monumental ethical concerns that have to be resolved. Indian Classical Dance is more than a series of physical poses, where, it embodies a live tradition nurtured for centuries, with strong ties to religious expression, regional identity, and intangible cultural heritage. Automating its classification would have the potential of relegating this complex human art into a bunch of visual patterns. This could lead to commodification or misrepresentation of the culture in the name of technology. For example, an algorithm may have given great accuracy by relying on surface-level visual information completely neglecting the emotional intent (bhava) or rhythmic context (tala), the very essence of the art form, therefore creating technically correct but culturally untenable interpretations. Another unwelcome consequence of implementing these systems could be the homogenization and sanitization of artistic expression in an instructional or evaluative environment, drowning out regional differences and personal stylistic innovations beyond the algorithm’s acquired parameters. It could also be biased against certain regional variations: for instance, if the training data sets are found to lack diversity in dancers’ body types or attire or performance contexts, the model may systematically misclassify or underperform in relation to such underrepresented groups and thus would be propagating existing social inequities. To confront these risks, future development must implement a participatory design methodology incorporating input from cultural practitioners, historians, and community stakeholders so as to set criteria for classification, curate datasets, and evaluate model outputs. Transparency in the model’s decision-making process will ensure that classifications are not just accurate but also culturally defensible. In a nutshell, automation must help support rather than undermine human appreciation and teaching, where, automation may never replace the utterly indispensable components of cultural transmissionvia interpretation, shouldered by human emotion.
