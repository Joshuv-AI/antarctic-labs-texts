Association between medial posterior tibial slope and medial proximal tibial angle with medial meniscus posterior root tears
Authors: Tao Huang, Changling Du, Tao Meng, Heng Cao, Shoutao Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Although tibial plateau morphology is recognized as a critical determinant of meniscal pathology, the specific associations of the medial posterior tibial slope (MPTS) and medial proximal tibial angle (MPTA) with medial meniscus posterior root tears (MMRTs) remain underexplored in contemporary literature. This study aimed to evaluate the effects of MPTS and MPTA on MMRTs. Clinical data were retrospectively collected from patients undergoing knee arthroscopy for medial meniscus injuries between June 2023 and June 2024. A total of 78 patients were divided into the MMRTs group (MMRTs, n = 39) and the control group (tears in other parts of the medial meniscus, n = 39). The observed indices included gender, age, laterality, duration of illness, body mass index (BMI), Outerbridge classification of the medial compartment articular cartilage, MPTS, and MPTA. Independent-samples t tests, chi-square tests, and multivariable logistic regression analyses were used to compare variables between the 2 groups. Receiver operating characteristic curve analysis was employed to determine the critical values of risk factors. P < .05 was considered statistically significant. There were no statistically significant differences in age, laterality, gender, duration of illness, and BMI between the 2 groups (P > .05). The MPTS in the MMRTs group was significantly greater than in the control group (8.0° ± 0.8° vs 6.6° ± 1.0°, P < .05), the MPTA was significantly lower than in the control group (85.1° ± 1.4° vs 87.1° ± 1.4°, P < .05), and the intraoperative Outerbridge classification of the medial compartment articular cartilage was significantly higher in the MMRTs group compared with the control group (P < .05). Multivariable logistic regression analysis confirmed that MPTS > 7.35° (odds ratio = 9.663, 95% confidence interval: 2.863–32.621, P = .0003) and MPTA < 85.85° (odds ratio = 1.233, 95% confidence interval: 1.634–7.194, P = .0011) were independent anatomical risk factors for MMRTs after adjusting for age, gender, and BMI. Receiver operating characteristic curve analysis indicated that the optimal cutoff value for MPTS is 7.35° (sensitivity 79.5%, specificity 82.1%, area under the curve = 0.876), and the optimal cutoff value for MPTA is 85.85° (sensitivity 84.6%, specificity 71.8%, and area under the curve = 0.841). MPTS >7.35° and MPTA <85.85° are anatomical risk factors for MMRTs. Moreover, MMRTs can exacerbate the degeneration of articular cartilage, providing a critical reference for preoperative risk stratification and individualized treatment planning of knee joint diseases.

1. Introduction

The meniscus is a critical knee biomechanical component, responsible for 40% to 80% of load transmission and essential for shock absorption, joint stabilization, lubrication, and proprioception.[1–4] Medial meniscus posterior root tears (MMRTs) were defined as avulsions within 1 cm of the medial meniscus’ posterior tibial attachment and are the most functionally devastating meniscal tear subtype, with biomechanical consequences similar to total medial meniscectomy.[5] Epidemiologically, MMRTs account for ~10% of all meniscal tears and 22% to 28% of medial meniscal injuries.[6,7]

Disruption of the posterior root via MMRTs impairs hoop stress maintenance, leading to meniscal extrusion, increased medial compartment cartilage contact pressure (25% elevation per cadaveric studies),[5] degenerative joint changes,[8] and exacerbated knee instability.[5] Proximal tibial morphology influences knee soft tissue integrity[9]: greater posterior tibial slope increases anterior cruciate ligament strain,[10,11] while reduced medial proximal tibial angle (MPTA) elevates medial meniscal compressive stress.[12] However, the relationship among medial posterior tibial slope (MPTS), MPTA, and MMRTs remains poorly characterized, and further preliminary research is needed to clarify their potential associations. Existing studies have primarily focused on the general correlation between proximal tibial morphology and medial meniscal injuries but lack targeted exploration of whether MPTS and MPTA specifically contribute to the occurrence of MMRTs, and the underlying biomechanical mechanisms linking these tibial anatomical parameters to MMRT development remain unclear.[13–15] Limited preliminary observations suggest that abnormal MPTS and MPTA may alter force distribution at the medial meniscus posterior root. However, consistent evidence and systematic analysis remain lacking, underscoring the need for further investigation.

This study aims to compare MPTS and MPTA between patients with MMRTs and those with non-root medial meniscal tears; identify the optimal diagnostic cutoff values of MPTS and MPTA for MMRTs via receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis; verify whether MPTS and MPTA are independent anatomical risk factors for MMRTs after adjusting for potential confounding factors; and explore the correlation between MMRTs and medial compartment articular cartilage degeneration. Our findings may inform clinical risk stratification, preoperative screening, and therapeutic decision-making for patients at high risk of MMRTs.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Patient selection and study design

This retrospective study analyzed patients undergoing arthroscopic management for medial meniscus injuries at our hospital (June 2023–June 2024). This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Binzhou Medical University Hospital, and all patients provided informed consent. A sample size calculation was performed a priori based on the primary endpoint of intergroup differences in MPTS and MPTA. With an expected effect size of 1.5, α = 0.05, and power = 80%, a total sample size of 70 to 80 subjects was estimated to be sufficient to detect the hypothesized associations between tibial morphological parameters and MMRTs. From an initial cohort of 85 patients, 7 were excluded for the following reasons: prior ipsilateral knee surgery (meniscectomy = 2), preexisting inflammatory arthritis (n = 3: rheumatoid arthritis = 2 and gouty arthritis = 1), prior septic arthritis of the knee (n = 1), and significant trauma history (ipsilateral limb fracture = 1). The remaining 78 patients were stratified into 2 groups: the MMRTs group (n = 39, MMRTs) and the control group (n = 39, preserved root integrity with medial meniscus tears in other parts: anterior horn, n = 0; mid-body, n = 15; posterior horn, n = 24) (Fig. 1). The patients’ gender, age, body mass index (BMI), duration of disease, laterality, MPTS, MPTA, and Outerbridge classification of medial compartment articular cartilage under arthroscopy was obtained.

Flowchart of patient selection. MMRTs = medial meniscus posterior root tears.

Inclusion criteria: primary knee arthroscopy without prior surgical intervention; preoperative magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrating either MMRTs or non-root medial meniscal tears (mid-body or posterior horn); availability of complete preoperative MRI datasets encompassing full-length lower extremity alignment and detailed knee joint anatomy; and absence of prior traumatic injury to the ipsilateral knee joint.

Exclusion criteria: previous surgery on the index knee (meniscectomy = 2); diagnosed inflammatory arthropathies (n = 3: rheumatoid arthritis = 2, gouty arthritis = 1); prior septic arthritis of the knee (n=1); history of fracture in the affected limb (n=1).

2.2. Measurement of MPTS and MPTA

MPTS was quantified using the technique established by Hudek.[16] All measurements were performed on a 3.0T MRI scanner (Siemens) with sagittal fat-suppressed proton density-weighted imaging sequences (slice thickness = 3 mm, interslice gap = 0.3 mm, field of view = 16 × 16 cm). In the PACS system, sagittal fat-suppressed proton density-weighted imaging sequences were used to visualize the posterior cruciate ligament’s inferior insertion and the anterior and posterior tibial cortical margins. A circle was drawn intersecting the tibial plateau and both cortical margins. A 2nd circle was then created along the distal edge of the 1st circle, intersecting the anterior and posterior tibial cortices. The longitudinal tibial axis (line A) was defined by connecting the centers of these 2 circles. After identifying the midpoint of the medial tibial plateau on axial images, a line connecting the anterior and posterior plateau margins (line B) was plotted on the corresponding sagittal image. The MPTS (angle α) was calculated as the angle between line B and the perpendicular to line A (line C) (Fig. 2).

Methodological illustration of MPTS and MPTA measurements. (A) Determination of the longitudinal tibial axis (line A); (B) MPTS quantification (angle α); and (C) MPTA assessment (angle β). MPTA = medial proximal tibial angle, MPTS = medial posterior tibial slope.

For MPTA measurement, preoperative full-leg radiographs were obtained with the patient in a standing position, lower limbs in neutral rotation, and knees extended (tube focus-film distance = 100 cm). The knee joint center (femoral intercondylar notch midpoint) and ankle joint center (talar dome midpoint) were identified on preoperative full-leg radiographs. Line D was drawn connecting the medial and lateral edges of the tibial plateau. The tibial mechanical axis (line E) was defined as the line connecting the centers of the knee and ankle. The MPTA (angle β) was measured as the internal angle between lines D and E (Fig. 2).

Before collecting data, the 3 independent assessors underwent standardized training and calibration for measuring MPTS and MPTA. The training covered theoretical lessons on anatomical landmarks, standardized PACS system operation, and joint measurement of 10 pilot cases to ensure consistent understanding and application of the measurement protocol. To guarantee measurement accuracy and reproducibility, 3 experienced physicians independently performed MPTS and MPTA measurements: 1 musculoskeletal radiologist with 10 years of experience and 2 orthopedic surgeons specializing in joint surgery with 9 and 12 years of experience, respectively. Final values were calculated by averaging the measurements, and interobserver reliability was evaluated using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) (MPTS group: ICC = 0.87; MPTA group: ICC = 0.89).

2.3. The Outerbridge classification

Articular cartilage damage in the medial compartment was graded arthroscopically using the Outerbridge classification.[17] This included: grade 1: cartilage softening, edema, or blister formation (1 point); grade 2: superficial fissures, fibrillation, or partial-thickness defects <1 cm in diameter (2 points); grade 3: deep ulceration >1 cm without subchondral bone exposure (3 points); and grade 4: full-thickness cartilage loss with exposed subchondral bone (4 points). Stratified analysis of the Outerbridge classification was further performed by duration of illness (acute: ≤10 months and chronic: >10 months) to explore the correlation between MMRTs and cartilage degeneration across disease stages.

2.4. Statistical analysis

Descriptive analysis was 1st conducted in this study. A normality test (Shapiro–Wilk test) was performed on both continuous variable sets, confirming normal distribution. Continuous variables were expressed as mean and standard deviation, whereas categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Comparative analyses between the 2 groups were conducted using independent-samples t tests for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to identify independent risk factors for MMRTs, with age, gender, and BMI as confounders. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for each variable. ROC curve analysis was used to determine optimal cutoff values for risk-factor quantification, and a combined ROC curve analysis of MPTS and MPTA was conducted to evaluate their synergistic diagnostic value. All statistical analyses were performed with SPSS 23.0 (IBM Corp.), with MedCalc 20.1 (MedCalc Software Ltd) specifically employed for ROC curve analysis. Statistical significance was set at P < .05.

3.2. Comparative analysis of imaging parameters and intraoperative cartilage status

The imaging parameters of this study include MPTS and MPTA. The MMRTs group demonstrated significantly greater MPTS values (8.0° ± 0.8° vs 6.6° ± 1.0°; P < .05). Concurrently, the MPTA was markedly reduced in the MMRTs group (85.1° ± 1.4° vs 87.1° ± 1.4°; P < .05). Arthroscopic evaluation demonstrated significantly greater severity of articular cartilage degeneration in MMRTs group, with 62% (24/39) exhibiting Outerbridge grade 3 to 4 lesions compared to 26% (10/39) in controls (P < .05). Stratified analysis by disease duration revealed no significant difference in Outerbridge grades between the 2 groups in the acute subgroup (≤10 months) (57.1% vs 44.4%, P > .05). In contrast, the Outerbridge grades were significantly higher in the MMRTs group than in the control group in the chronic subgroup (>10 months) (64.0% vs 9.5%, P < .05). These findings indicate that MMRTs exacerbate articular cartilage degeneration with the prolongation of disease duration (Table 2).

3.3. Multivariable logistic regression analysis

Multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed with MMRTs as the dependent variable and age, gender, BMI, MPTS (>7.35°/≤7.35°), and MPTA (<85.85°/≥85.85°) as independent variables. 3.4. ROC analysis

The predictive value of the MPTS and the MPTA for MMRTs was assessed using ROC curves. The optimal diagnostic thresholds were established at 7.35° for MPTS (sensitivity 79.5%, specificity 82.1%, and area under the curve (AUC) = 0.876) and 85.85° for MPTA (sensitivity 84.6%, specificity 71.8%, and AUC = 0.841). Combined ROC curve analysis of MPTS and MPTA showed higher diagnostic accuracy for MMRTs (sensitivity 89.7%, specificity 89.7%, and AUC = 0.934) than either single parameter, indicating a synergistic effect of the 2 tibial morphological parameters in predicting MMRTs (Fig. 5).

4. Discussions

Among meniscal pathologies, MMRTs are clinically significant because they compromise the medial meniscus’s essential hoop-stress mechanism, producing biomechanical consequences comparable to those of total meniscectomy.[18] Notably, MMRTs often occur in patients without a history of acute trauma or concomitant intra-articular pathology, suggesting that inherent anatomical variations beyond activity-related mechanical stressors may predispose individuals to this specific injury. Despite this implication, research on anatomical risk factors specific to MMRTs remains sparse.[7,19] This knowledge gap is critical, as a comprehensive understanding of these anatomical determinants could enable orthopedic surgeons to implement targeted prevention strategies, optimize early diagnosis, and develop personalized treatment protocols for high-risk patients, ultimately reducing MMRT incidence and associated degenerative sequelae.

The MPTS and MPTA are critical anatomical parameters for evaluating proximal tibial morphology. Biomechanical evidence suggests that an increased posterior tibial slope amplifies horizontal shear forces under axial loading, thereby facilitating anterior tibial translation and increasing anterior cruciate ligament strain, which represents a well‑established risk factor for anterior cruciate ligament injury.[10,11] Meanwhile, a decreased MPTA increases compressive stress on the medial meniscus and articular cartilage, thereby increasing susceptibility to medial meniscal tears.[12] During cohort assembly, rigorous exclusion criteria were applied to remove traumatic etiology, thereby minimizing selection bias while preserving the hypothesized causal relationship between tibial plateau morphology and meniscal pathology. In this study, we used Hudek measurement protocol[16] to assess MPTS with knee MRI and MPTA with full-length lower-limb radiographs, both of which have established methodological reliability for measurement accuracy and reproducibility. Our analysis revealed that the MMRTs group had significantly higher MPTS values. ROC curve analysis identified MPTS > 7.35° as a significant threshold for increased MMRTs risk. These findings corroborate previous reports associating elevated MPTS with MMRTs pathogenesis,[7,20,21] and the underlying biomechanical mechanism is that an increased MPTS increases posterior translation of the femoral condyle relative to the tibia during knee flexion and extension, thereby significantly amplifying tensile stress on the posterior root attachment of the medial meniscus. This persistent, excessive tensile stress leads to microtears and progressive damage to the meniscal root tissue, eventually resulting in complete tears.[21] Therefore, increased MPTS is a risk factor for MMRTs. Concurrently, the MMRTs group demonstrated significantly reduced MPTA values, with ROC analysis indicating MPTA < 85.85° as a critical risk threshold. While the ideal MPTA is approximately 90° in mechanical alignment, normal physiological values typically range from 87° ± 3°.[22] Reduced MPTA induces medial compartment overload through 2 mechanisms: increased medial tibial plateau stress concentration directly elevates meniscal loading; and altered force vectors generate excessive traction at the posterior root insertion. Specifically, a decreased MPTA leads to varus alignment of the knee joint, which shifts the majority of the body’s load to the medial compartment, increasing compressive stress on the medial meniscus and shear stress on the posterior root. In the long term, this abnormal stress distribution causes structural damage to the meniscal root.[23] This pathological cascade creates reciprocal reinforcement between meniscal insufficiency and progressive varus deformity. MMRTs compromise load distribution capacity, accelerating medial compartment degeneration, while progressive varus alignment exacerbates meniscal stress.[24]

The circumferential collagen fibers of the meniscus enable effective conversion of axial compressive forces into circumferential hoop stresses, thereby distributing joint loads evenly across articular surfaces.[25] MMRTs critically compromise this load-transfer mechanism, resulting in abnormal stress concentrations on medial compartment cartilage that accelerate degenerative changes.[26] Our intraoperative analysis showed significantly higher Outerbridge classification scores in the MMRTs group than in the control group, independent of disease duration, BMI, or gender distribution. Stratified analysis by disease duration revealed no significant difference in cartilage degeneration between the 2 groups with short pain duration. In contrast, prolonged disease duration exacerbated articular cartilage degeneration, suggesting that MMRTs may directly mediate cartilage degeneration. Specifically, this finding implies that complete disruption of the meniscal hoop-stress mechanism in MMRTs permits medial meniscal extrusion under compressive forces, thereby exposing articular cartilage to biomechanically abnormal loading conditions. These observations align with Allaire biomechanical model, which demonstrates that medial meniscal posterior root avulsion results in cartilage degradation equivalent to that of total meniscectomy.[5] In contrast, non-root tear patterns (predominantly radial/horizontal tears) typically preserve partial circumferential fiber integrity, resulting in less severe chondral damage. The critical biomechanical consequence of MMRTs lies in their dual pathological effects: immediate loss of load-sharing capacity leading to focal cartilage overload; and progressive meniscal extrusion exacerbating joint space narrowing.[27] This self-perpetuating cycle establishes a pathological feedback loop between meniscal insufficiency and cartilage degeneration, ultimately accelerating medial compartment degeneration and elevating osteoarthritis risk. A growing number of clinical and biomechanical studies have confirmed that MMRTs are associated with a 2- to 3-fold increase in the risk of knee osteoarthritis, and that the severity of cartilage degeneration is significantly higher in patients with MMRTs than in those with non-root meniscal tears.[28,29]

The identified cutoff values of MPTS > 7.35° and MPTA < 85.85° have important clinical applicability in different populations and can guide preoperative screening and risk stratification of MMRTs. For young athletes participating in high-impact sports, excessive MPTS and decreased MPTA increase the risk of MMRTs due to repeated knee flexion and extension and high load-bearing. Routine measurement of MPTS and MPTA can identify high-risk athletes, and targeted interventions, such as muscle strength training and movement correction, can be implemented to reduce injury risk. For elderly patients with early knee osteoarthritis, abnormal tibial morphological parameters are important predictors of MMRTs, and early detection can avoid the progression of meniscal injury and cartilage degeneration. In clinical practice, MPTS and MPTA can be included in the routine imaging evaluation of patients with medial knee pain, and patients with abnormal parameters can be closely followed up or given early conservative treatment. Historically, MMRTs were treated with partial meniscectomy. Previous studies have demonstrated that partial meniscectomy does not delay osteoarthritis progression due to loss of meniscal integrity, ultimately leading to higher rates of subsequent arthroplasty.[30,31] Consequently, partial meniscectomy is reserved exclusively for symptomatic MMRTs manifesting mechanical symptoms (locking) when root repair is contraindicated due to advanced degenerative changes or compromised meniscal tissue integrity.[32,33] Contemporary evidence supports meniscal root repair as the standard treatment, as this approach restores native meniscal function by reestablishing circumferential hoop tension and normalizing joint biomechanics, thereby effectively retarding osteoarthritis progression and reducing arthroplasty requirements.[34–36] Surgical indications include patients with minimal varus malalignment (<5°) and early-stage cartilage degeneration (Outerbridge grade 1–2). The procedure can be performed arthroscopically using either transtibial pull-out techniques or suture anchor fixation.[27] For patients with MPTS > 7.35° and/or MPTA < 85.85°, simple meniscal root repair may not eliminate the anatomical predisposition to MMRTs, and concurrent high tibial osteotomy should be considered to correct the abnormal tibial morphology, reduce stress on the medial meniscus root, and improve long-term therapeutic effect. Relevant studies[37–39] have indicated that 1- to 3-year follow-up of patients who underwent combined surgery showed significant improvement in knee joint function scores (Lysholm scores), and that the recurrence rate of MMRTs and the incidence of osteoarthritis were both significantly lower than in the simple meniscal repair group. However, the long-term efficacy of combining meniscal root repair with high tibial osteotomy for patients with abnormal tibial morphological parameters remains unproven in large-sample prospective clinical studies, and further research is needed to confirm its clinical value.

This study has several limitations. First, it is a single-center retrospective study, which may introduce selection bias and limit the generalizability of the results. Future research should adopt a multicenter, large-sample prospective design, enroll patients from different regions and across various hospital levels, increase sample size and population coverage, and further validate the findings of this study. Additionally, future multicenter studies should standardize measurement methods for MPTS and MPTA, unify inclusion and exclusion criteria, reduce measurement and selection bias, and enhance the credibility and generalizability of the research outcomes. Second, the average age of the patients (54.4 ± 5.7 vs. 53.0 ± 7.3 years) was relatively high, necessitating further age stratification to improve the reliability and applicability of the results. Third, both groups were restricted to patients with meniscal injuries. Future studies should include control groups comprising individuals without meniscal pathology to strengthen the validity of comparative analyses. Fourth, the study did not explore the prognostic implications of MPTS and MPTA abnormalities in patients with MMRTs, including their influence on the postoperative meniscal re-tear rate, progression of cartilage degeneration, and clinical outcome scores. Prospective follow-up studies are needed to evaluate the long-term prognostic value of these anatomical parameters in patients with MMRTs after surgical treatment. Fifth, the study did not account for other potential confounding factors, such as patient activity levels and comorbidities, which may have affected the results.

5. Conclusion

The medial meniscus posterior root plays a critical biomechanical role in load distribution within the medial compartment of the knee joint. After adjusting for age, gender, and BMI, MPTS > 7.35° and MPTA < 85.85° were identified as independent anatomical risk factors for MMRTs. The combined use of MPTS and MPTA yields higher diagnostic accuracy for MMRTs than single-parameter assessment. Furthermore, these tears accelerate articular cartilage degeneration through altered joint loading. The identified cutoff values of MPTS and MPTA have important clinical implications for preoperative screening and risk stratification of MMRTs across different populations. Future multicenter prospective studies are needed to validate these results and to explore the prognostic value of MPTS and MPTA in patients with MMRTs.
