Human behavioral basis for recommending changes to the physical activity guidelines
Authors: Fábio Hech Dominski, Hassan Mohamed Elsangedy, Diogo S. Teixeira, Alexandro Andrade
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A growing body of evidence supports the central role of human behavior and affect in the promotion of physical activity (PA), yet a persistent gap remains between PA guidelines and actual population levels of PA. Over the past three decades, public health initiatives have focused on disseminating information about the health benefits and recommended doses of PA through top-down guidelines. However, these efforts have not consistently translated into meaningful increases in PA participation. Accumulating evidence indicates that human behavior is not governed solely by rational cognitive evaluations. In this commentary, we argue that PA guidelines should extend beyond epidemiological recommendations to explicitly incorporate behavioral and psychological dimensions. Our rationale draws on theories of behavior change, motivation, basic psychological needs, and affective responses to PA. The immediate subjective experience of movement plays a critical role in determining the likelihood of future engagement, as affective responses during PA are intrinsically linked to adherence. Therefore, in addition to cognitive-based messaging, PA communication strategies should emphasize affective components and highlight short-term benefits, particularly those related to social connection and mental well-being.

We read with great interest the commentary by Skinner and Durstine (2025)1 regarding the physiological basis for recommending changes to the physical activity (PA) guidelines. The authors provided significant recommendations, such as ensuring that no more than two days elapse between exercise sessions, as well as encouraging adults to do vigorous aerobic and strength exercises. Building upon their work, we would like to highlight fundamental behavioral aspects that can help people become less sedentary while becoming more active, considering the pandemic of physical inactivity. This commentary aims to synthesize evidence on the psychological and behavioral processes underlying physical activity promotion, informing how physical activity guidelines may be further refined.

Brief history of physical activity guidelines

Over the past three decades, public health has emphasized informing populations about the health benefits and recommended doses of PA. PA guidelines are a cornerstone of Exercise Science.2 The first formal recommendation, issued in 1995 by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM), advised adults to accumulate at least 30 minutes (min) of moderate-intensity PA on most days.3

In 1996, the U.S. Surgeon General's Report on Physical Activity stated: “All people over the age of 2 years should accumulate at least 30 minutes of endurance-type PA, or at least moderate-intensity, on most – preferably all – days of the week”. The recommendation includes a dose-response relationship: more PA is better.4

In 2007, ACSM and the American Heart Association (AHA) updated the guidelines to recommend either 30 min of moderate-intensity PA five days per week or 20 min of vigorous-intensity PA three days per week, performed in bouts of at least 10 min.5

The U.S. Department of Health and Human Services released the Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans in 2008,6 introducing the central message - which is still valid today, recommending at least 150 min of PA per week or 75 min of vigorous-intensity PA (in episodes of at least 10 min), as well as muscle-strengthening activities on 2 or more days a week.

World Health Organization (WHO) released its first global guidelines in 2010, maintaining the target of 150/75 min of moderate/vigorous-intensity PA.7 The 10-min bout requirement was removed in the 2018 Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans,8 recognizing that physical activity accumulated in bouts of any duration contributes to the overall health benefits associated with total volume, which also incorporates cognitive and mental health benefits.8 The 2018 U S. PA Guidelines also highlighted several immediate benefits of physical activity, including reductions in blood pressure and anxiety, as well as improvements in cognitive function and sleep.8

The 2020 WHO update, based on US recommendations, retained the 150-min weekly threshold and expanded recommendations across the lifespan and for special populations.9 Many countries have adopted aligned national guidelines, such as Australia, Brazil, Canada, Ireland, and the United Kingdom.10, 11, 12, 13, 14 Despite improved design and accessibility, a gap persists between guidelines and actual PA levels.

The (dis)connection between guidelines and physical (in)activity levels

Despite the evolution of PA guidelines over the past 30 years,15 there has been no corresponding increase in global PA levels. A recent collection of studies in Nature highlights persistently low levels of physical activity despite two decades of policy progress.16 Diverse population data indicate that physical inactivity is at an all-time high,17, 18, 19, 20 characterizing an ever-growing pandemic of physical inactivity and an interesting paradox.

Drawing from 507 surveys across 163 countries and territories, Strain et al. (2024)17 demonstrated a rising prevalence of physical inactivity across most nations and world regions. Given these data, the WHO warns that the world is off track to meet the target of a 15% reduction in physical inactivity by 2030, emphasizing the urgent need for robust national policies and effective interventions.

Current PA recommendations are primarily informed by epidemiological and medical findings, which could be complemented by insights from other perspectives. Essentially, these documents follow a standard structure: first, providing the rationale for engaging in PA, followed by the recommended dosage for a given population.9,11 For decades, the strategy has relied on informing the public of the health benefits and the required weekly volume of PA, under the assumption that individuals will modify their behavior accordingly. However, as noted by Skinner and Durstine (2025),1 these top-down efforts to promote PA in the general population have not consistently yielded success.

Maltagliati et al. (2024)21 attributed the disconnect between guidelines and population PA levels to an exclusive focus on health benefits, an issue that echoes earlier seminal work by Ekkekakis, who proposed a tripartite rationale for exercise behavior emphasizing not only cognitive but also affective responses to exercise.22

While long-term benefits are necessary, they are insufficient on their own, as they weaken the subjective value assigned to physically active behavior. Drawing from behavioral economics, individuals often undervalue PA because it entails immediate costs, such as time, physical effort, and sometimes financial expenses, while offering delayed and often abstract long-term rewards, such as improved health or reduced all-cause mortality risk. Consequently, Maltagliati et al. (2024)21 criticized PA promotion campaigns, including the 2020 WHO guidelines, which mention 'health' over 200 times but fail to mention 'affect' or 'pleasure,' citing 'enjoyable' only three times. A similar pattern is observed in the Brazilian National Guidelines,11 where 'health' appears more than 150 times, whereas 'pleasure' is cited only twice and 'fun' once. Moving toward a paradigm shift, the National Physical Activity and Sedentary Behavior Guidelines for Ireland repeatedly incorporate the encouraging phrase: 'Move your body and be active in a way that you enjoy'.13

According to Milton et al. (2020),23 guidelines in isolation are unlikely to increase population-level PA. Considering the individual level of PA, a growing body of evidence supports the fundamental role of human behavior and affect in the promotion of PA; therefore, we aim to discuss these psychological dimensions as critical components for the future of PA guidelines.

We are predictably irrational, with flashes of rationality

The theoretical basis for recommending changes to PA guidelines draws on consolidated behavioral theories, including both traditional cognitive-based approaches and more recent affective frameworks. PA is a complex behavior in which motivation quality is central. Self-Determination Theory (SDT), a prominent evidence-based framework,24 is presented here as one of several possible theoretical approaches to understanding motivation as a multidimensional construct, highlighting autonomy, competence, and relatedness as basic psychological needs essential for sustained engagement.

Individuals are often able to adopt positive health behaviors in the short term, but sustaining these changes remains difficult due to a combination of individual and environmental factors. PA initiation is largely driven by extrinsic motives (e.g., health, fitness, aesthetics), which are insufficient for long-term adherence. Sustained behavior requires a shift toward more autonomous or intrinsic motivation.2

Nevertheless, traditional cognitivist theories used to explain, predict, and modify PA and exercise behavior assume that individuals are rational decision-makers who gather information, weigh costs and benefits, and anticipate future outcomes.22 However, if behavior were driven primarily by such deliberative processes, global PA levels would have improved. Increasing evidence shows that human behavior is not consistently governed by rational cognitive evaluations alone.

In their commentary, Skinner and Durstine1 argue: "Unfortunately, without a good explanation as to why PA provides better health benefits, inactive people are less likely to become active because they want specific details regarding what to do, how much to do, and, more importantly, why they should follow PA guidelines." This argument reflects a widespread misconception that inactive individuals remain sedentary simply because they lack a 'good explanation' or specific details regarding the health benefits of PA. This rationalist perspective, often termed the 'information deficit model',25 mistakenly assumes that human behavior is primarily driven by reflective deliberation (Type-2 processing). However, current theoretical frameworks, such as the Affective-Reflective Theory of physical inactivity and exercise (ART)26 and the Theory of Effort Minimization in Physical Activity (TEMPA),27 posit that momentary behavior is frequently governed by automatic, Type-1 processes that prioritize immediate affective valuations over delayed rewards.

For an inactive and sedentary person, the prospect of exercise often triggers an automatic negative valuation (e.g., anticipation of discomfort or displeasure), which creates a restraining action impulse that can easily override the rational desire to be healthy.26

However, this individual-level perspective should be interpreted within a broader socioecological context. Over the past decades, multiple societal changes, including increased reliance on labor-saving devices, greater access to electronic entertainment, reduced opportunities for physical education, and dependence on personal automobiles, have collectively promoted sedentary lifestyles and reduced opportunities for PA.28,29 These changes reflect a broader “physical activity transition” driven by economic development, urbanization, and technological advancement, through which PA has been progressively engineered out of daily life. These environmental and structural factors may constrain behavior to such an extent that they overwhelm the potential impact of individual intentions or even public health guidelines.

In this context, providing more 'specific details' on what to do or why to do it is often futile if the immediate affective response remains negative, as the rational input fails to penetrate the barrier of visceral resistance.

This limitation is empirically underscored by the substantial 'intention-behavior gap,' where approximately 47% of individuals who successfully form the rational intention to exercise still fail to act on it.30 Thus, knowledge of health benefits alone - the 'why' - is insufficient to start or sustain behavior when both evolved tendencies to conserve energy and pervasive environmental constraints act in the same direction.

By focusing excessively on rational justifications and prescriptive dosages, health professionals may inadvertently disregard behavioral, psychological, and motivational considerations now evident to help initiate and support PA, and communicate a PA practice that is not aligned with current recommendations (less is better than nothing).

What we feel matters

By moving beyond an excessive focus on rational justifications and prescriptive dosages, the immediate subjective experience of movement determines the likelihood of repetition. What we feel during PA itself is intrinsically linked to adherence.31 If the experience generates pleasure, the motivation becomes intrinsic, reinforcing the behavior. Conversely, if the experience is unpleasant (e.g., excessive exertion, pain, or embarrassment), it generates an avoidance impulse, contributing to physical inactivity and sedentary behavior.32

Affective responses during PA may fluctuate, and these fluctuations are motivationally relevant. The systematic review by Rhodes and Kates (2015)31 supports the basic premise of hedonic theory: positive affective responses during exercise are consistently related to future PA behavior, whereas post-exercise affect (how one feels after stopping) has a null or weaker relationship. This distinction is crucial. It suggests that the promise of "feeling good afterwards" is not enough; we need to communicate and program for the importance of positive affect during the activity. Prioritizing an intensity and modality that the individual finds pleasurable - or at least tolerably comfortable - is not a luxury, but a behavioral need for habit formation.

Suggestions and future perspectives

Although there is an association between knowledge of PA guidelines and the adoption of more active and less sedentary behaviors,33,34 this alone is insufficient for adherence. Therefore, additional strategies are needed to improve PA guidelines.

PA guidelines should address not only epidemiological issues, but also physiological aspects (as suggested by Skinner and Durstine1) and behavioral and psychological aspects. In a manifesto, researchers argue that future guidelines should differ, aligning with what people want from PA in the short term, and prioritizing pleasure, fun, and enjoyment over just health.2 In fact, this should extend to exercise prescriptions, where current guidelines seem disconnected to behavioral recommendations within the same entity (e.g., ACSM chapters 5 [General principles of exercise prescription] and 12 [Behavioral Theories and strategies for promoting exercise]).35

In addition to cognitive/informational-based messages, greater emphasis should be placed on affective components when communicating PA, promoting it as a behavior that can generate pleasure, enjoyment, and positive experiences.

While low-to moderate-intensity activity may facilitate more pleasant initial experiences, affective responses are shaped by multiple factors beyond intensity. These include autonomy (e.g., allowing individuals to self-select intensity and modality), perceived competence, social context, environmental characteristics (e.g., outdoor vs. indoor settings), as well as the overall meaning and motivation to PA.36 Strategies such as self-paced PA, choice-based exercise programs, as well as extrinsic strategies such as music, and supportive social environments have been shown to enhance enjoyment and positive affective responses. Therefore, rather than focusing exclusively on intensity, a more comprehensive approach should emphasize affective optimization through flexible, person-centered, and context-sensitive strategies.

Williamson et al. (2020)37 suggested that PA messaging should emphasize short-term outcomes - specifically those related to social and mental health - as these represent immediate benefits derived from body movement. We, as human beings, may be more interested in what we obtain in the short term when we perform a behavior. Therefore, the positive effects of acute exercise, such as cognitive boosts, may serve as a catalyst for promoting regular participation.38,39

Considering motivation quality, most PA guidelines rely on controlling language (e.g., “adults should do PA”), which may reinforce extrinsic motivation and emphasize distal outcomes. To promote adherence, PA messaging should instead adopt autonomy-supportive language that emphasizes choice, flexibility, and self-initiation, rather than simply replacing “should” with terms such as “want” or “can.” For example, statements such as “adults can choose to engage in physical activity in ways that fit their preferences and daily routines”, or “any movement during leisure time counts”, and “individuals can start with amounts that feel manageable”, may better support personal agency and highlight immediate, meaningful benefits, as proposed by Smith et al. (2022).2

The WHO slogans “every move counts” and “some PA is better than none” could be clarified in the sense that even low PA intensities during leisure time bring benefits, including positive affective experiences, but recognizing that not all expressions of PA may be good/adjusted for all. This can be understood by considering at least two complementary aspects. First, while low to moderate-intensity and self-selected activities are more likely to elicit positive affect, higher-intensity or imposed activities may result in discomfort or negative affect, especially among inactive individuals.40 Furthermore, there is a growing body of evidence demonstrating the paradox of physical activity.41,42

The paradox of PA suggests that not all PA is inherently beneficial to health, particularly when performed by workers under conditions of low autonomy, high physical strain, and insufficient recovery.43 Evidence indicates that occupational PA does not always lead to improved health outcomes and may, in some cases, be detrimental to health.41,42

In this sense, Varela and Hallal (2024)44 cited examples of work and household PA, noting that many forms of child labour, such as agricultural work, street vending, and domestic service, as well as underpaid, unsafe, and unhealthy occupations, including construction work, waste picking, and manual material handling in informal sectors, and even situations of modern-day slavery, are characterized by high levels of energy expenditure. Even in the context of transport-related PA, particularly in low-to middle-income countries, it is often driven more by necessity than by choice.44 These activities often involve prolonged periods of walking, standing, or physically demanding tasks such as lifting or pulling heavy loads.

Considering inactive individuals with high sedentary behavior, who are often in the early stages of behavior change (i.e., precontemplation and contemplation stages according to The Transtheoretical Model45), the staircase approach could help people to be less sedentary while becoming more active.46 First, reducing total sitting time and interruptions in sedentary behavior with standing and moving, then including light-intensity PA, next moderate-to-vigorous intensity PA, especially during leisure time, to finally gain health benefits. The sequence consists of sit less – move more – keep fit.47

From a behavioral perspective, reducing sedentary behavior requires strategies that go beyond prescribing activity levels and instead focus on facilitating small, achievable changes in daily routines while enhancing self-efficacy and perceived competence. This may include breaking up prolonged sitting, incorporating brief bouts of movement throughout the day (e.g., “exercise snacks”), and progressively increasing activity levels over time.

Regarding exercise snacks, these are a relatively new approach involving multiple short bouts (≤ 10 min) of accumulated exercise,48 which may represent a flexible and autonomy-supportive strategy. In particular, exercise snacks allow individuals to self-select key aspects of their activity, including modality, intensity, location, duration, and frequency, which may enhance adherence and engagement while still providing meaningful health benefits.49

Beyond refinements in messaging, more substantive changes to PA guidelines may be warranted. First, future guidelines should provide guidance not only on how much physical activity to perform, but also on how to experience it positively to support long-term adherence. Second, guidelines could include specific recommendations to enhance positive affect and enjoyment, such as encouraging self-selected intensities of PA to improve pleasure, as well as social engagement and supportive environments. Third, in light of the paradox of PA, guidelines should more clearly differentiate between domains of PA (e.g., leisure-time vs. occupational), acknowledging that not all forms of PA confer similar health benefits.

Conclusion

There is an urgent need to include behavioral and psychological aspects in the next PA guidelines. Therefore, we hypothesize that positive experiences with PA can alter and strengthen beliefs about its benefits. PA imposes intrinsic costs that are stable and positive affective experiences can tip the balance in favor of PA.

To promote long-term adherence to PA, keeping people active, it is essential to consider how this behavior is experienced in the moment. Positive affect and enjoyment can be enhanced by engaging in activities that individuals find pleasurable, participating in social contexts, and performing PA in supportive and enjoyable environments. In this sense, emphasizing the immediate benefits of physical activity, such as improved mood, pleasure, reduced anxiety, and enhanced well-being, may be a key strategy to foster more sustainable engagement in PA guidelines.
