Clinical findings, prognostic factors, and outcome of protein‐losing nephropathy in cats: A retrospective study
Authors: Noam Sugar, Hilla Chen, Gilad Segev
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrimary glomerular disease resulting in protein‐losing nephropathy (PLN) is an uncommon cause of chronic kidney disease in cats, yet is important to recognize because it warrants specific treatment that impacts outcome.Hypothesis/ObjectiveCharacterize clinicopathologic findings, prognostic indicators, and short‐ (≤30 days) and long‐term survival of cats with PLN.AnimalsThirty‐seven cats with naturally occurring PLN.MethodsMedical records of cats with PLN admitted to a veterinary teaching hospital were retrospectively reviewed.ResultsMedian age was 3 years (range, 1.5‐11.5 years) and 17/37 (46%) were males. INTRODUCTION

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is common in cats and a leading cause of mortality in this species. 1 There are multiple causes of CKD, but in many cases, the etiology is unknown. 2 Protein‐losing nephropathy (PLN) caused by primary glomerular disease is an uncommon cause of CKD in cats, but it is important to recognize this condition because specific treatment is indicated and PLN in cats has been associated with mortality rates as high as 46%. 3 Primary glomerular diseases can be broadly categorized as immune‐complex glomerulonephritis (ICGN) and non‐immune‐complex glomerulonephritis (non‐ICGN). 4 Membranous glomerulonephropathy is the most commonly recognized form of ICGN in cats, followed by membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis, whereas other forms of ICGN are reported less frequently. 5 , 6 The causes of ICGN in cats are mostly presumed, and include primary (eg, idiopathic) or immune‐complex deposition secondary to chronic antigenic stimulation such as inflammation, neoplasia, or infectious causes (eg, retroviruses). 7 Non‐ICGN is less common and includes causes such as amyloidosis and focal segmental glomerulosclerosis. 5 The clinical presentation of PLN varies and it may manifest itself as nonspecific clinical signs associated with azotemia (eg, lethargy decreased appetite, weight loss) or as effusions or edema associated with hypoalbuminemia. 5 , 8 , 9 , 10

Early diagnosis of primary glomerular disease and prompt appropriate treatment are crucial, potentially increasing the chance of remission. Definitive diagnosis of primary glomerulopathy can only be made based on renal biopsy. Yet, renal biopsies are not performed often because the procedure is invasive, done under general anesthesia, and associated with potential complications in approximately 20% of cats (eg, severe hemorrhage, macroscopic hematuria, hydronephrosis, death), 11 especially in cats with azotemia, severe hypoalbuminemia, hypertension, or a combination of these. 12 In addition, few reference laboratories can provide appropriate interpretation for renal biopsy samples, and sample transport can be technically and financially limiting. Therefore, tentative diagnosis often is made based on clinical presentation and laboratory findings.

Currently, information regarding appropriate treatment, prognostic indicators, and survival of cats with primary glomerular disease is limited. Therefore, identification of prognostic indicators and evaluation of the effects of therapeutic interventions on the outcome of cats with PLN are needed to guide treatment and to establish prognosis. Treatment for primary glomerular disease in cats largely is extrapolated from treatment in humans and dogs and is based on anecdotal evidence. 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 The most commonly administered treatments for cats with primary glomerular disease are dietary modification, antiproteinuric drugs, and immunosuppression. Recently, it has been recognized that >70% of cats with primary glomerular disease, based on renal biopsy, have ICGN, further supporting the rationale to consider judicious immunosuppression as part of the treatment regimen, even if renal biopsy results are unavailable. 5

The prognosis of PLN caused by primary glomerular disease in cats is considered poor, and approximately half of affected cats either die or are euthanized shortly after diagnosis. Yet, previous data are based on a small number of cats with highly variable survival. 3 , 5 , 17 Thus, the disease needs to be further characterized, including outcome and risk factors for mortality.

Our aim was to characterize the clinicopathologic findings and prognostic indicators for cats with primary glomerular disease. The second objective was to evaluate the associations between clinical variables and therapeutic interventions with short (30 days) and long‐term survival and assess whether response to treatment is associated with survival.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Cats and definitions

The medical records of cats diagnosed with PLN presented to the Koret School of Veterinary Medicine, the Hebrew University of Jerusalem between 2009 and 2023 were reviewed. The primary glomerular disease was defined as urine protein‐to‐creatinine ratio (UPC) persistently >2.0 after exclusion of extrarenal causes for proteinuria. Medical records of all cats were reviewed by an internal medicine specialist with advanced training in nephrology (GS, HC) to assure that only cats with unequivocal clinical evidence for primary glomerular disease were included in the study.

Data extracted from the electronic medical records included signalment, clinical signs (eg, polyuria, polydipsia, weight loss, edema), clinicopathologic findings, coagulation test results, urinalysis, UPC, urine culture, ultrasonographic findings, fine needle aspirate cytology findings when available, and diagnostic testing for infectious diseases (eg, retroviruses, Mycoplasma haemofelis, Leishmania). Data regarding treatment included the use of IV fluids (eg, lactated Ringer's solution, 0.9% saline, hetastarch), immunosuppressants (eg, corticosteroids, mycophenolate), antiproteinuric treatments (eg, angiotensin converting enzyme inhibitors [enalapril, benazepril] or angiotensin receptor blockers [telmisartan]), antibiotics, and antithrombotic drugs (eg, clopidogrel, acetylsalicylic acid, low‐molecular weight heparin, rivaroxaban, or combination of these).

Acute kidney injury (AKI) and chronic kidney injury were defined based on the International Renal Interest Society guidelines. 18 Hypoalbuminemia was defined as serum albumin concentration <2.2 g/dL and azotemia as serum creatinine concentration >1.6 mg/dL. Complete remission or response (CR) was defined as a UPC ≤0.4. Partial remission or response (PR) was defined as a decrease in UPC by ≥50% compared with UPC at diagnosis. Survival data were collected from the medical records and via telephone interviews with owners or referring veterinarians. Short‐term survival (ie, cats discharged and alive at 30 days) and long‐term survival (time from initial evaluation to death or euthanasia) also were documented.

Statistical analysis

For all continuous variables, the normality of data distribution was evaluated using the Shapiro‐Wilk's test. Normally and nonnormally distributed continuous variables are reported as mean and SD or as median and range, respectively, and were compared between survivors and nonsurvivors using the Student's t‐test or the Mann‐Whitney U‐test, respectively. Chi‐squared or Fisher's exact tests were used to compare categorical variables between outcome groups. Long‐term survival was assessed using Kaplan‐Meier curves, and comparisons between groups were performed using the log‐rank test. The association between continuous variables and long‐term outcome was assessed using the Cox regression model. For all tests applied, P < .05 was considered significant. All analyses were performed using statistical software (SPSS 29.0 for Windows, IBM, IL).

RESULTS

Cats

Forty‐three cats with a diagnosis of PLN initially were considered, of which 3 were excluded because of a UPC <2; 2 additional cats were excluded because of inability to confidently rule out proteinuria of extrarenal cause, and 1 cat was excluded because of a lack of UPC measurement. Thirty‐seven cats eventually were included in the study. Seventeen (46%) were males (16 neutered), and 20 (54%) were females (all spayed). Median age was 3 years (range, 1.5‐11.5 years). Twenty‐eight (76%) cats were <5 years of age, 7 cats (19%) were between 5 and 10 years, and 2 cats (5%) were >10 years old. Breeds included 33 (89%) domestic short hair cats, 3 (8%) domestic long hair, and 1 (3%) British shorthair. Age, breed, and reproductive status were not associated with short‐term survival (Table 1).

Survival

Short‐term survival data were available for all 37 cats. Twenty‐five cats survived to discharge (68%), of which 4 (16%) died within 30 days, resulting in 57% short‐term survival. The estimated median survival time of all cats was 424 days (range, 1‐5090 days, 95% confidence interval [CI], 0‐1098 days; Figure 1). Long‐term (>30 days) follow‐up data were available for 20/21 cats (1 cat survived >30 days, but was lost to follow‐up), of which 5 (25%) died within 1 to 3 months, 5 (25%) died between 3 and 12 months, and 10 (50%) survived >12 months.

Clinical signs

The most common clinical signs and physical examination findings included weakness or lethargy (21/37, 57%), edema (17/37, 46%), weight loss (13/37, 35%), hyporexia or anorexia (12/37, 32%), polyuria and polydipsia (15/37, 41%), vomiting (10/37, 27%), fever (8/37, 22%), and diarrhea or skin lesions (3/37, 1% each). Abdominal distention, tachypnea or dyspnea, polyphagia, adipsia, dysphagia, cough, dysuria, hematuria, pica, halitosis, and oral bleeding were documented in ≤2 cats each (Table 1). Edema was significantly more common in short‐term survivors compared with nonsurvivors (OR, 0.21; 95% CI, 0.05‐0.86; P = .04; Table 1).

Clinicopathologic finding

The most common abnormalities on CBC and serum biochemistry included serum total hypocalcemia (19/20, 95%), increased serum urea concentration (26/29, 89%), hypoproteinemia (26/29, 89%), increased creatine kinase (CK) activity (14/18, 77%), hypoalbuminemia (27/35, 77%), increased serum creatinine concentration (27/37, 73%), and anemia (24/37, 65%; Tables 2 and 3). Activities of CK (P = .001) and, aspartate aminotransferase (AST; P = .01), and serum concentrations of urea (P = .003), creatinine (P = .05), and phosphorus (P = .003) were higher in nonsurvivors, whereas concentrations of glucose (P = .01) and total calcium (P = .01) were lower. Concentrations of albumin and cholesterol and UPC did not differ significantly between the short‐term outcome groups (Table 3). Hypoalbuminemia was more common in cats presented with edema compared with cats without edema (16/16 [100%] vs 11/19 [58%], respectively; P = .003), but no significant difference in UPC was found between the groups (P = .54). No difference in antithrombin 3 concentration was identified between short‐term survivors and nonsurvivors (P = .54).

Serum creatinine concentration at presentation was associated with long‐term survival (hazard ratio [HR], 1.3; 95% CI, 1.03‐1.52; P = .01), whereas the presence of azotemia and hypoalbuminemia were not (P = .78 and .79, respectively).

Screening for infectious diseases and concurrent diseases

Thirty‐three cats were tested for infectious disease as follows: 33 cats were screened for retroviral infections, of which 4 cats (12%) were positive for FIV and 1 was positive for FeLV; 5 cats were screened for leishmaniasis, of which 2 were positive (1 by serology and 1 by PCR using a splenic aspirate). Additional screening for infectious agents, all of which were negative, included PCR for Mycoplasma hemofelis (n = 11), serology for toxoplasmosis (n = 4), serology for feline enteric coronavirus (n = 3), PCR for Bartonella henselae (n = 2), PCR for ehrlichiosis (n = 1), and serology for neosporosis (n = 1). No association was found between the presence of infectious disease and survival (short‐ or long‐term). Three of the 4 FIV‐positive cats died within 30 days and 1 survived 190 days; the FeLV‐positive cat survived 92 days.

Concurrent diseases diagnosed at presentation included pancreatitis or pancreatic edema (17/37, 46%), heart disease (4/37, 10% based on echocardiography [2 cats] or presence of gallop rhythm [2 cats]), immune‐mediated hemolytic anemia (IMHA; 1/37, 0.9%), stomatitis (1/37), severe dermatitis (1/37), cholecystitis (1/37), and suspected hindlimb thrombosis (1/37).

Ultrasonographic findings

Ultrasonography was performed in 33 cats, of which 21/33 cats (63%) had abdominal effusion, 17/33 cats (51%) had hyperechoic renal cortex, 13/33 (39%) had renomegaly, 13/33 cats (39%) had thickened renal cortex, 9/33 cats (27%) had decreased corticomedullary differentiation (mostly mild), 1 cat had cortical cysts, and none of the cats had decreased kidney size.

Treatment

Treatment of cats that were hospitalized included IV fluids (27/28, 96%), hetastarch (21/28, 75%), fresh frozen plasma (5/28, 21%), packed red blood cells (5/28, 18%), and human albumin (1/28, 0.3%). Cats also were treated with immunosuppression (27/37 cats, 73%), antiproteinuric treatment (25/37 cats, 68%), and antithrombotic treatment (23/37 cats, 62%). The most common immunosuppressive medications administered to cats were corticosteroids (median dose, 2.5 mg/kg/day; range, 0.6‐4.2 mg/kg/day) and mycophenolate (median dose, 19 mg/kg/day; range, 10‐22 mg/kg/day). Fourteen cats received a single immunosuppressive agent (corticosteroids [8 cats, of which 4 achieved CR or PR], mycophenolate mofetil [6 cats, of which 1 achieved PR]), whereas 13 cats received a combination of 2 immunosuppressive agents (corticosteroids and mycophenolate mofetil [8 cats, of which 4 achieved CR or PR], corticosteroids and chlorambucil [5 cats, of which 1 achieved PR]). None of the cats was treated with >2 immunosuppressive agents. Twenty‐five cats received antiproteinuric medications, of which 17 cats (68%) initially received angiotensin converting enzymes inhibitors, including benazepril (14 cats, median dosage, 0.4 mg/kg/day; range, 0.2‐1.2 mg/kg/day) and enalapril (6 cats; median dosage 0.6 mg/kg/day; range, 0.4‐0.9 mg/kg/day). Five cats (20%) were treated with the angiotensin receptor blocker telmisartan as the initial antiproteinuric (median dosage, 0.5 mg/kg/day; range, 0.4‐1.0 mg/kg/day) whereas 2 cats that were initially treated with enalapril (both received 0.9 mg/kg/day) were switched telmisartan. Ten of the 25 cats that received antiproteinuric medication achieved CR or PR. Only 1 cat achieving PR did not receive immunosuppression. This cat had a positive serology result for leishmaniasis and was treated with benazepril and allopurinol. Twenty cats received antithrombotic treatments, including clopidogrel (median dosage, 4 mg/kg/day; range, 2.6‐6.7 mg/kg/day), aspirin (median dosage, 0.49 mg/kg/day; range, 0.47‐0.5 mg/kg/day), enoxaparin (3 mg/kg/day), and rivaroxaban (0.83 and 0.56 mg/kg/day). Eighteen cats received a single antithrombotic (clopidogrel [17 cats, of which 6 achieved CR or PR] and aspirin [3 cats, of which all achieved CR or PR]). Two cats received dual antithrombotic therapy, of which 1 cat achieved PR.

Administration of immunosuppression was more common among short‐term survivors compared with cats that did not survive short‐term (18/20, [90%] vs 9/16 [56%]; OR, 7.0; 95% CI, 1.2‐40.8; P = .05).

Administration of antiproteinuric treatment also was more common among short‐term survivors compared with nonsurvivors (17/20 [85%] vs 7/16 [44%]; OR, 7.3; 95% CI, 1.5‐35.2; P = .01). No association was found between administration of antithrombotic treatment and short‐term outcome (P = .18). Of the long‐term survivors, 17/20 (85%) were treated with immunosuppressants, 17/19 (89%) with antiproteinuric drugs, and 15/20 (75%) with antithrombotic medication. Data regarding antiproteinuric treatment were missing in 1 cat.

Response to treatment

Partial or complete remission was determined based on the evaluation of UPC during the follow‐up period. Partial remission/response or CR was documented in 11/31 (36%) cats, of which PR was documented in 8/31 cats (26%) and CR was documented in 3/31 cats (10%). Immunosuppression was administered to 10/11 (91%) of the responders. The median time to CR or PR was 94 days (range, 19‐280 days). Of the 11 responders, 8 cats (72%) presented with edema, 10 cats (90%) with hypoalbuminemia (median, 1.4 g/dL; range, 0.9‐2.0 g/dL), and 5 cats (45%) with increased serum creatinine concentration (median, 1.9 mg/dL; range, 1.7‐3.2 mg/dL). Median UPC at the time of PR was 2 (range, 0.6‐3.8), and values of 0.1, 0.3, and 0.4 were documented in the 3 cats with CR. Of the responders, edema resolved in 5/8 cats, and did not resolve in 1 (data were missing for 2 cats). Serum albumin concentration increased in all cats with CR or PR (in 1 cat data were unavailable) and azotemia resolved in 3/5 with CR or PR. Comorbidities of these cats included pancreatitis, stomatitis, FeLV infection, leishmaniasis, and severe dermatitis (1 cat each).

Partial remission or CR was associated with short‐term survival (11/17 [65%] vs 0/14 [0%]; OR, 3.3; 95% CI, 1.7‐6.5; P < .001). Partial remission or CR also was associated with long‐term survival. The estimated mean survival time of cats with PR or CR was 3999 ± 839 days compared with 220 ± 65 days for cats without remission or response (P = .003).

DISCUSSION

Our results suggest that cats with PLN present with edema or nonspecific clinical signs associated with azotemia. These 2 distinct clinical presentations seem to carry different outcomes. The overall prognosis should be considered favorable in cats without azotemia and excellent in those that achieve remission.

Cats in our study were young (median age, 3 years) and most commonly DSH breed, consistent with previous reports of PLN in cats secondary to primary glomerular disease. 5 , 17 In contrast to previous studies suggesting a predilection in males, 5 , 17 equal numbers of males and females were affected in our study. Possible explanations could be genetic variations among geographical areas, differences in underlying causes, and possibly the lower prevalence of retroviral infections that may trigger glomerular disease, which is more common in male cats. 19

Edema was a common clinical sign in this cohort (46% of cats), and was associated with lower risk for mortality. Conversely, markers of decreased kidney function, including serum creatinine, urea, and phosphorus concentrations were associated with increased risk for mortality. These findings suggest that PLN can be manifested in 2 distinct clinical presentations. One is associated predominately with severe hypoalbuminemia resulting in edema, and the other predominately with decreased kidney function. The former is likely the consequence of severe proteinuria with normal glomerular filtration rate, thus edema is the clinical sign promoting owners to seek medical care. Affected cats with decreased kidney function are presented for nonspecific signs of decreased glomerular filtration rate (eg, anorexia, vomiting). Therefore, cats presented with edema might be diagnosed at an earlier, and at a potentially reversible, stage of glomerular disease, and consequently may have a more favorable outcome. Conversely, cats presented with clinical signs related to azotemia could have had less severe glomerular damage initially and proteinuria was not severe enough to result in severe hypoalbuminemia. Yet, persistent proteinuria might have resulted in a gradual decrease in kidney function because of nephron damage and loss. Thus, these cats potentially are more likely diagnosed at a later stage of the disease, after irreversible damage has already occurred. This hypothesis should be evaluated in additional studies. Our results should prompt clinicians to initiate earlier diagnostic evaluation of PLN before irreversible renal damage occurs. In accordance with our results, it has been shown that dogs with amyloidosis presenting with a high UPC and lower degree of azotemia have a better prognosis compared with dogs presenting with higher degree of azotemia and lower UPC. 20 Our findings however are not consistent with a recent study in which neither presence of edema nor azotemia was associated with outcome in cats with PLN. 5 However, most of the cats in that study had edema or effusion and azotemia, and the authors concluded the study was underpowered to determine the effects of these variables on survival.

As expected, the presence of edema was associated with hypoalbuminemia, but although 77% of cats were hypoalbuminemic, edema was only present in 46% of cats. The pathophysiology of edema is complex and is influenced by multiple factors in addition to the decreased colloid osmotic pressure, such as increased vascular permeability, as well as sodium and water retention. 13 , 21

Creatine kinase activity was significantly higher in nonsurvivors. Increased CK activity indicates muscle injury and has been associated with poor outcome in several clinical settings in ill cats. 22 Nonsurvivors may have sustained more severe or prolonged hypovolemia leading to muscle hypoperfusion, and resulting in increased activities of muscle enzymes. Alternatively, venipuncture, restraint, and intramuscular injections might have resulted in muscle damage and contributed to the increase in CK activity in some cats. As can be expected, serum creatinine concentration was associated with both short‐ and long‐term outcome. The presence of azotemia also may affect the outcome because owners may be more likely to euthanize animals because of the guarded short‐term and long‐term prognosis.

Overall, infectious causes for PLN were relatively uncommon in this cohort, with only 7 cats testing positive for retroviral infection or leishmaniasis. This finding is consistent with studies of dogs diagnosed with glomerular disease, in which the underlying cause could not be identified in most cases. 23 Moreover, positive PCR or serology results for infectious diseases are not conclusive evidence for a cause‐and‐effect relationship. Interestingly, 1 of 5 cats screened for leishmaniasis was positive. In endemic areas, leishmaniasis is a leading cause of glomerular diseases in dogs, 24 and the most common histologic finding associated with this diagnosis is glomerulonephritis. 25 , 26 , 27 Cats are considered relatively resistant to leishmaniasis, but with emerging cases in endemic areas, reports suggesting active infection are emerging. 28 , 29 The primary affected organ systems in cats with leishmaniasis are the skin and mucous membranes, whereas visceral involvement is less common, 28 , 29 and in contrast to dogs, renal lesions in cats with leishmaniasis are less frequent. In a report of 16 cats with clinical leishmaniasis, 4 were proteinuric and 3 were azotemic. 30 In another histopathological study of 15 cats with leishmaniasis, renal lesions were documented in only 1 cat. 31 Therefore, the association between leishmaniasis and PLN in cats needs further characterization. Yet, it is reasonable to test cats diagnosed with PLN for leishmania, along with other infectious diseases mostly in endemic areas, because identification and elimination of the underlying disease is considered a major principle of treatment. 32 Thus, successful management of leishmaniasis might improve outcome.

The ultrasonographic findings were variable and included renomegaly, hyperechoic renal cortex with increased thickness, decreased corticomedullary differentiation, and, in some cases, no abnormal findings. Therefore, glomerulopathy should be considered as a differential diagnosis in such cases.

In our study, approximately 50% of the cats died or were euthanized during hospitalization or shortly after discharge, consistent with findings of previous studies, 5 , 17 as well as with the reported mortality of cats with AKI. 33 Short‐term survival was highly influenced by death during hospitalization and euthanasia, which potentially was influenced also by financial resources or perceived poor prognosis, before allowing sufficient time to assess response to treatment. Complete or partial remission was associated with outcome. Two thirds of the cats that survived the initial 30 days went into remission, with 17% achieving complete remission. In this subset of cats, the long‐term prognosis was very good, especially when considering the fact that the reported long‐term MST is likely an underestimation (because some of the long‐term survivors were still alive at the last follow‐up). The proportion of cats achieving remission is likely an underestimation, because many of the cats were euthanized before receiving appropriate treatment, and follow‐up data were not available for all cats. Thus, it is possible that remission was not documented in some cats. Moreover, partial remission potentially could reflect initial recovery from glomerular disease that could subsequently become complete remission. Also, some cats survived with excellent quality of life for years, although they did not achieve complete remission (ie, UPC >0.4).

One of our goals was to evaluate the effect of different treatments on the outcome of cats with PLN caused by primary glomerular disease. However, as noted above, assessing any treatment effects on short‐term survival might result in inherent bias, because the response to treatment is not expected to occur immediately, and some cats that potentially could have responded to treatment did not survive long enough for this response to occur. Short‐term survival is more likely associated with the presence of azotemia and hemodynamic stability. Cats receiving immunosuppressive and antiproteinuric medications were more likely to survive short‐term, but these associations must be carefully evaluated in prospective studies. These medications may have been administered more commonly to cats that were hemodynamically more stable, and therefore favorable outcome cannot necessarily be attributed to the treatment itself. Thus, these associations need to be considered carefully and do not prove a cause‐and‐effect relationship. Evaluation of the efficacy of most treatments on long‐term survival was not possible either, because nearly all surviving cats were treated with a standardized treatment protocol including immunosuppressants and antiproteinuric drugs. Treatment with antithrombotic drugs was not associated with short‐term survival. Thrombotic events are not commonly reported in cats with PLN, and only 1 cat in our study was suspected to have hindlimb thrombosis. However, thromboembolism is a well‐established complication in dogs with PLN, and antithrombotic drugs are part of the treatment recommendations for dogs with glomerular disease. 14 , 34 , 35 , 36 Such treatment probably should be considered in cats with PLN despite the apparent low frequency of thromboembolism in our study, because such events might go undiagnosed.

Our study had several limitations. Renal biopsy, the only definitive method of diagnosis for glomerular disease, was only performed in 1 cat in our study. Efforts were made to exclude prerenal or postrenal causes of proteinuria before inclusion, and cats were only included after a thorough review of the medical records by an internal medicine specialist with advanced training in nephrology. Moreover, high‐magnitude proteinuria (ie, UPC >2) is very unlikely without glomerular involvement. In our cohort, the lowest UPC was 3.4 in 1 cat, followed by a cat with a UPC of 4.6, and the remainder had a UPC >5, making hyperfiltration caused by other factors (eg, interstitial nephritis) unlikely. Despite the above, misclassification cannot be entirely ruled out. Lack of histopathologic diagnosis also precluded the diagnosis of a specific glomerulopathy and whether it was ICGN or non‐ICGN. Different types of glomerular disease could result in different responses to treatment (especially immunosuppression) and have different outcomes. Thus, if a certain glomerular disease was overrepresented, it could skew the results. Because of the retrospective nature of our study, diagnostic evaluation (particularly screening for infectious causes) and treatment protocols, including timing and combinations of medications prescribed, were at the clinicians' discretion. Some cats were diagnosed with concurrent inflammatory conditions such as gingivitis or cholecystitis, which might contribute to glomerular injury and proteinuria. Unfortunately, because of the retrospective nature of the study, we cannot isolate the effect of these inflammatory conditions on changes in UPC or response to treatment.

The small number of cats limited additional analyses that could have identified other prognostic indicators for remission and survival. Finally, one cannot differentiate between response to treatment vs spontaneous remission.

In conclusion, cats with PLN have a guarded prognosis, but cats achieving remission or response have a good long‐term prognosis. Cats presenting with acute disease associated with severe hypoalbuminemia and edema, rather than with azotemia, are more likely to respond to treatment.
