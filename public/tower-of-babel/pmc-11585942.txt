Sleep disorders as independent predictors of taste dysfunction risk
Authors: Rongcai Huang, Quanyi Zheng, Juan Dai, Jinfeng Long, Yansong Wang, Junxin Ni
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThis study aimed to investigate the association between sleep disorders and the prevalence of taste dysfunction and the mediation effect of oral microbe in adults over 40 years.Materials and methodsCross-sectional data were utilized from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (2011–2014). Regression models were employed, adjusting for demographic variables and covariates. Subgroup analyses were conducted based on age, sex, ethnicity, and education level. Multiplicative interactions were assessed through likelihood ratio tests. Additionally, the impact of sleep disturbance on the alpha diversity of the oral microbiome was examined using the rank-sum test (significance threshold: p < 0.05). Mediation analysis based on oral microbiota was conducted.ResultsThe analysis included 4869 participants. After adjusting for adjusting for demographic variables and covariates, individuals with sleep disorders exhibited a 36% increased risk of taste dysfunctions compared to those without sleep disorders (OR: 1.36, 95% CI: 1.00-1.84, p = 0.05). Interaction analyses indicated no significant differences between sleep disorders and taste dysfunctions concerning sex, educational level, and age across various models (Crude Model, Model 1, Model 2, and Model 3; p for interaction > 0.05). Furthermore, compared with the non-sleep disorder group, patients with sleep disorders demonstrated decreased numbers of OTUs, Shannon-Wiener indices, and Faith’s phylogenetic diversity indices in the oral microbiota (p < 0.05). However, the mediation analysis failed to reveal an indirect effect of oral microbiome on taste dysfunction (p > 0.05.)ConclusionSleep disorders independently correlate with a higher risk of taste dysfunctions, potentially associated with alterations in oral flora.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-024-05190-w.

Introduction

Taste is postulated to serve as a guiding mechanism for organisms, facilitating the accurate identification and consumption of essential nutrients. In the human sensory system, five fundamental taste modalities have been conclusively identified: sour, salty, sweet, bitter, and umami. Additionally, recent research has recognized emerging taste categories, including metallic, fatty, and calcium sensations [1]. Taste dysfunction can manifest as alterations in the intensity, quality, or recognition of these basic taste sensations, which can result from various etiological factors, including infections, adverse effects of medications, nerve damage, nutritional deficiencies, or underlying health conditions, such as COVID-19 [2].Taste dysfunction may lead to weight gain by changing dietary habits, potentially favoring high-sugar, high-salt, or high-fat foods and neglecting nutritionally essential alternatives. In addition, diminished ability to perceive food flavors may increase the risk of consuming spoiled or harmful foods, as individuals may struggle to accurately assess freshness or safety, and affecting people’s health [3–6]. Recent study has found that sleep quality plays crucial role in taste sensitivity [7]. Poor sleep quality could decreased the taste sensitivity and has an imply on feeding behavior [8]. In view of the regulatory role of oral flora in sleep structure [9]and taste preference [10], we hypothesized that oral microbiota might moderate both sleep and taste. Although the earliest research exploring the relationship between sleep and taste dates back to the 1950s, and several studies have been published on the subject, the association between sleep disorder and taste dysfunction remains controversial [6]. In the present study, we aimed to investigate the association between sleep disorders and taste dysfunction, and explored the potential mediator effect of oral microbes in such associations in the U.S. population.

Materials and methods

Data source

Data was collected from The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 2011–2014. The NHANES is cross-section studies strategically developed to evaluate the health and nutritional status of both adults and children in the United States. The NHANES protocol received approval from the institutional review board at the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) (Protocol #2011-17; Continuation of Protocol #2011-17), and comprehensive written informed consent was secured from all participants. The study design adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines for reporting cross-sectional studies, and all procedures were conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the 1975 Helsinki Declaration.

Definition of sleep disorders

The diagnosis of sleep habits and disorders were derived from the NHANES questionnaire survey, specifically focusing on the responses to the following questions: SLQ050 - Ever told doctor had trouble sleeping? SLQ060 - Ever told by doctor have sleep disorder? SLD010H - How much sleep do you get (hours)? Trained interviewers asked the above-mentioned questions in the home using the Computer-Assisted Personal Interviewing system. By analyzing the collective responses to these questions, we identified individuals within the surveyed population who potentially experience sleep disorders. The sleep hours were classified as < = 7 h and > 7 h [11].

Definition of taste dysfunction

Taste dysfunctions were diagnosed using the Taste & Smell Questionnaire Section of NHANES [12], which gathered interview data on taste and smell abilities along with associated health conditions. Eligible participants were individuals aged 40 years and above, of both genders. Taste dysfunctions were identified in individuals who reported taste-related issues, changes in the ability to taste food flavors, persistent taste alterations within the past 12 months, including salt, sour, sweet and bitter.

Covariates

Demographic information for participants was derived from questionnaire responses, encompassing age, sex, ethnicity, and educational attainment. Covariates considered in the analysis comprised body mass index (BMI), smoking status, alcohol consumption, taking medication, health eating index (HEI) scores, sleep duration, and a history of systemic diseases (including diabetes mellitus (DM), hypertension, periodontitis, Parkinson’s disease, and cardiovascular disease). Ethnicity/race was categorized as white, black, Mexican, and others. Smoking status was dichotomized into no smoking and smoking. Alcohol consumption was classified as either no drinking or drinking. The level of education was grouped into < 9th grade, 9th -12th grade and > 12th grade. Prescription medications information was collected form NHANES 2011–2014, which provides personal interview data on use of prescription medications during a one-month period prior to the survey date. Alcohol consumption was delineated as non-drinking and drinking. The HEI is a measure designed to assess the overall quality of an individual’s dietary patterns. Developed by the United States Department of Agriculture, the HEI-2015 scores evaluate adherence to key dietary guidelines and reflects the extent to which a person’s food choices align with recommended nutritional practices [13]. Hypertension was diagnosed with systolic blood pressure exceeding 140 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure surpassing 90 mmHg. Diagnostic parameters for DM included clinical diagnosis by a medical practitioner, glycohemoglobin concentrations exceeding 6.5%, fasting plasma glucose concentrations equal to or exceeding 7.0 mmol/L, random or two-hour oral glucose tolerance testplasma glucose levels reaching or exceeding 11.1 mmol/L, or documented use of antidiabetic medication/insulin therapy [14]. Cardiovascular disease (CVD) was determined based on self-reported diagnoses provided by respondents, indicating confirmation from a medical professional regarding conditions such as congestive heart failure, coronary heart disease, angina, heart attack, or stroke [15]. The definition of periodontitis conformed to the 2012 classification criteria established by the Centers for Disease Control and the American Academy of Periodontology [16]. Parkinson’s disease was diagnosed through the administration of antiparkinsonian agents. Depression was assessed using the nine-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) [17]. The total PHQ-9 score ranges from 0 to 27, with a score of > = 10 indicating depression.

Oral microbiome sample collection

The oral microbiome dataset from the NHANES database originates from oral rinse samples collected during consecutive survey cycles in 2011–2012. Subsequent to sample collection, oral microbiome sequencing was conducted, yielding microbial sequences classified as Operational Taxonomic Units (OTUs). Additionally, the dataset encompasses alpha diversity metrics and relative abundance. This comprehensive dataset facilitates in-depth exploration and analysis of oral microbial composition. Researchers can leverage the alpha diversity measures to assess diversity within individual samples.

Statistical analysis

The respondents were weighted to accurately represent the non-institutionalized civilian population of the United States. Continuous variables with normally distributed data were presented as mean and standard deviation (SD), while continuous variables without normal distribution data were presented as median and interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables were expressed as counts (percentages). The comparison of continuous variables between individuals with taste dysfunctions and those without taste dysfunctions was performed using the Kruskal-Wallis Test, encompassing age, BMI, the number of days taking medicine, and HEI scores. Categorical variables between taste dysfunctions and non-taste dysfunctions were compared using the Chi-squared Test, including sex, ethnicity, education level, smoking, alcohol consumption, and the occurrence of systemic diseases (such as DM, CVD, Parkinson’s disease and hypertension). To control for Type I error in multiple comparisons, the p value was adjusted by Bonferroni correction. Covariates with missing data will be replaced by regression imputation by using R package (missForest). MissForest handles missing data by using random forest models to predict and fill in these missing values based on other variables.

A survey-weighted univariate and multivariate logistic regression analysis were employed to evaluate the independent correlation between sleep disorder (independent variable) and taste dysfunction (outcome variable) with varying degrees of severity using odd ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Model 1 was adjusted for body mass index (BMI), age, sex, ethnicity and education. Model 2 was adjusted for BMI, age, sex, ethnicity, education, smoking, alcohol consumption and taking medicines. Model 3 was adjusted for BMI, age, sex, ethnicity, education, smoking, alcohol consumption, taking medicines, hypertension, cardiovascular disease, periodontitis, diabetes mellitus, Parkinson’s disease, sleep hours and severe depression. To evaluate whether the effect of sleep disorders on taste dysfunction is dependent on demographic factors, analyses stratified according to sex (male and female), age ( < = 50 and > 50 years), educational level were conducted to examine the strata effect. To investigate whether the oral microbiome mediates the relationship between sleep disorder and taste dysfunction, a mediation analysis was performed using the mediation package in R. The Bootstrap method was employed to estimate the standard error of the mediation effect, enhancing the robustness of the analysis. The mediation analysis focused on two key effects: average causal mediation effect (ACME) and average direct effect (ADE). The ACME represents the indirect effect of sleep disorder on taste dysfunction through the oral microbiome, while the ADE captures the direct effect of sleep disorder on taste dysfunction, independent of the oral microbiome.

The above statistical analysis was conducted on R software (version 4.1). The p value less than 0.05 was considered significant. Utilizing the rank-sum test, we examined the impact of sleep disturbance on alpha diversity of the oral microbiome, considering a significance threshold of p < 0.05. Statistical significance was determined based on this criterion.

Result

Basic characteristics of study population from NHANES 2011–2014

Among 4,869 participants, a total of 263 (5.40%) missing values were identified in the covariates. Specifically, 4.37% of participants had missing values for PHQ-9, 4.21% for alcohol consumption, 0.71% for BMI, 0.10% for DM, 0.08% for smoking, 0.06% for education, and 0.02% for CVD. Missing values were addressed using regression imputation. Table 1 presents the overall characteristics of the study participants. A total of 4,869 participants met the inclusion criteria, including 715 with taste dysfunction and 4,154 without. These participants represent 105,754,131 noninstitutionalized residents of the United States. The cohort comprised 2,371 males (48.39%) and 2,498 females (51.61%) with a mean age of 56 years (interquartile range: 47–64). Significant differences in educational attainment, medication history, sleep disorders, and the prevalence of systemic diseases were observed between individuals with taste dysfunction and those without (Table 1). Compared to those without taste dysfunction, individuals with taste dysfunction had a higher prevalence of sleep disorders, took more medications, and had an increased likelihood of CVD and depression. Compared to those without sleep disorder, individuals with sleep disorder tend to be female, have a high risk of took more medications, periodontitis, DM, CVD, Parkinson, and depression (p < 0.05) (Supplementary Table S1).

Univariate regression analysis for the association between sleep disorders and taste dysfunctions

The results of the univariate logistic regression analysis showed significant associations between sleep disorders and taste dysfunction (p < 0.05). Additionally, individuals with CVD, depression, hypertension, higher education levels, and higher BMI exhibited a postive association with taste dysfunction (p < 0.05). However, the analysis did not reveal significant associations between taste dysfunction and factors such as smoking, alcohol consumption, history of Parkinson’s disease, HEI, or sleep duration (p > 0.05) (Table 2).

Multiple logistic regression analysis for the association between sleep disorders and taste dysfunctions

Table 3 presents the relationships between sleep disorders and taste dysfunction based on the logistic regression analysis. In the crude model, sleep disorders were positively associated with taste dysfunction (OR: 1.59, 95% CI: 1.27–2.01, p < 0.001). After adjusting for BMI, age, sex, ethnicity, and education in Model 1, sleep disorders remained positively associated with taste dysfunction, showing a 1.61-fold higher odds compared to individuals without sleep disorders (OR: 1.61, 95% CI: 1.26–2.06, p < 0.001). In Model 2, after further adjustment for smoking, alcohol consumption, and medication use, the odds of taste dysfunction decreased slightly but remained significant (OR: 1.48, 95% CI: 1.12–1.96, p = 0.01). Finally, in the fully adjusted model (Model 3), individuals with sleep disorders had a 36% increased risk of taste dysfunction compared to those without sleep disorders (OR: 1.36, 95% CI: 1.00-1.84, p = 0.05).

Discussion

This study confirms a positive correlation between sleep disorders and taste dysfunctions after adjusted confounding factors. Consistent with our study, recent large-scale, population-based study from Chinese has investigated the relationship between sleep quality and taste perception. Conducted with 11,030 adults, the study found that poor overall sleep quality, including factors like insomnia, daytime sleepiness, and short sleep duration, was associated with a higher risk of altered taste perception [18]. Another nationwide cohort study involving 15,730 individuals revealed that patients with obstructive sleep apnea syndrome (OSAS) had a significantly increased risk of incident taste disorders over a 15-year follow-up period (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR] = 1.91, 95% CI = 1.08–3.38). Additionally, individuals with hyperlipidemia, particularly females with OSAS, exhibited higher risks of taste disorders [5]. Interestingly, no relationship was found between sleep time and taste dysfunction. In contrast to the results of this study, a study has demonstrated that the sleep time presented a positive correlation with sweet taste [19]. Taste function play an important role in diet selection and metabolism. Many drugs and numerous diseases can induce taste dysfunction. Numerous pharmaceuticals have been documented to induce gustatory disorders by modifying taste perception, attenuating or intensifying taste sensitivity, or precipitating a complete loss of taste. However, the underlying mechanisms for these disruptions frequently remain elusive [20]. After modified by taking medication, in this study, we have determined that there is an association between increased risk of taste disorders and sleep disturbances by adjusting the covariate “usage of prescription drugs” in a multiple logistic regression model.

Sleep architecture could moderate chemosensory function. A cross-section study from Brazil has found that poor sleep quality was associated with decreased taste sensitivity, including sweet and salty taste [7]. Another study has found that OSAS decreased the thresholds of taste, including sweet, sour, salty and bitter taste [21]. The reduction in arterial oxygen saturation associated with OSAS might lead to decreased expression of molecules related to sweet taste, including T1R2, T1R3, α-gustducin and PLCβ2 [22]. Furthermore, the vibrations caused by snoring in OSAS could contribute to peripheral nerve degeneration affecting pharyngeal chemosensitivity [23]. Not only sleep disorder has an imply on taste sensitivity but also taste hedonic perception [6]. Recent systematic review has demonstrated that short sleep duration increased the preferred sweetness concentration, rather than salt taste [6]. Another randomized crossover trial study has suggested that sleep curtailment increases sweet taste preference [24].

Systematic conditions closely link to sleep disorder and taste dysfunction. Short sleep duration and poor sleep quality are associated with an increased risk of systemic diseases [25]. Some studies propose that sleep disorder reduced salt taste sensitivity might result in increased salt consumption and elevated blood pressure [26]. Since systemic diseases impact both sleep [25, 27] and taste [28], we accounted for these as confounding factors in the regression model. This adjustment confirmed a correlation between sleep disorders and taste dysfunction. A longitudinal study included 5190 Chinese individuals has found that individuals with altered taste and smell perception experienced larger increases in systolic blood pressure (5.1 mmHg) and mean arterial pressure (3.8 mmHg) after 2 years, suggesting an association between chemosensory function and blood pressure [28]. Another research also indicated that patients with CVD were less sensitive to sweet or salt tastes [29]. In addition, antihypertensive medications may modify taste perception [30]. Recent study has found that Type 2 DM patients exhibited a diminished taste response, particularly in the perception of sweetness followed by sour and salt tastes, which might promote increased intake of sweets [31]. The pathophysiology of taste alteration in DM could be linked to a reduced receptor turnover rate [32]. Additionally, increased candidal colony count was detected in DM patients with disorder in the taste perception [33], which suggest a role of oral flora in taste disorders.

Oral microbiota also plays an important role in the association between sleep disorder and taste dysfunction. In this study, we have found that individuals with sleep disorders exhibited a decrease in both the number of OTUs and overall diversity, as indicated by lower Shannon-Wiener and Faith’s phylogenetic diversity indices. This suggests a reduction in both species richness and phylogenetic diversity in the oral microbiota of sleep disorder patients. However, no significant difference was observed in the Inverse Simpson index, indicating comparable evenness of species abundance between the two groups, and the mediation analysis failed to reveal an indirect effect of oral microbiome on taste dysfunction (p > 0.05). These findings imply that sleep disorders may impact the composition and structure of the oral microbiome, potentially influencing oral health. Similarly, several studies have showed that sleep disorder especially obstructive sleep apnea hypopnea syndrome (OSAHS), could alter the oral bacterial flora [34–36]. Previous research has demonstrated that sleep disorders are associated with elevated levels of Kingella genus in the oral microbiota [37]. Another study has also found a significant alteration in the oral microbiome composition of pediatric OSAHS compared to normal controls. These alterations were particularly evident in the relative abundances of Firmicutes, Proteobacteria, Bacteroidetes, Fusobacteria, and Actinobacteria [38]. The above studies suggest that alteration of oxygen concentrations due to sleep disorder may reshape the oral bacterial community [38]. In addition, the decreased airflow during sleep apnea can lead to a dry mouth, creating an environment that favors the growth of certain bacteria.

Our study elucidated the significant impact of sleep disorders as a risk factor influencing oral microbiota composition. Interestingly, oral microbiota can also influence taste sensitivity and contribute to food preferences [39, 40]. The elderly peoples in hospital who with poor oral hygiene could decrease taste sensitivity. Another research has found an association between alteration of oral microbiota and lower taste sensitivity in obesity child [41]. Recent research has found that increased proportions of Actinobacteria and Firmicutes in saliva was linked to reduced taste sensitivity, while an elevated proportion of Bacillales on the tongue dorsum was associated with increased taste sensitivity [42]. Oral microbiota creates a physical barrier through biofilm formation, thereby restricting the access of tastants to their receptors [39]. Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) from oral microbiota could also alter the splicing and function of sweet taste receptor T1R2 [43]. Additionally, the metabolic activity of the oral microbiota community modulates taste sensitivity. After sucrose consumption, sustained citric acid cycle activity was more common in high-sensitivity taste perceivers, while pyruvate was more efficiently converted to lactate in low-sensitivity perceivers [44]. Even if no significant mediation effect of oral microbiota in this cross-section study was found, this does not entirely rule out potential mediation effects that could be observed in future studies. Thus, our study suggested that oral microbiota played an important role in sleep disorder and taste dysfunctions. However, whether or how the oral microbiota community is involved in this interaction needs to be elucidated. It is noteworthy that this study lacks an exploration of beta diversity. The absence of an analysis on beta diversity limits our understanding of the dissimilarity or heterogeneity in oral microbial communities between individuals with and without sleep disorders. Future research incorporating beta diversity metrics would provide a more comprehensive perspective on the overall structural differences in the oral microbiota associated with sleep disorders.

We recognize the inherent challenges in our methodology. A key limitation of this study stems from the utilization of a cross-sectional research design, preventing the establishment of causal relationships. Both the diagnosis of sleep disorders and tastes dysfunction were based on meticulous 24-hour dietary recall data instead of objective measurements (such as polysomnography for sleep disorder). The recalling bias and reporting bias limit the accurate estimation of the association between sleep disorders and taste dysfunction. The types of sleep disorders were also not available to analyzed. Another limitation of this cross-sectional study is that certain variables, such as dry mouth were not included. Reduced salivary flow rate and dry mouth might impair the taste [45]. In addition, there are over 200 medications can induce taste dysfunction [46]. The specific types of medications were not classified in this study. As a result, potential confounding factors might not have been accounted for, which could bias the observed associations and limit the ability to draw definitive conclusions about causality. Future study need to consider include the above risk factors. Additionally, it’s crucial to emphasize that our analysis is limited to the geographic scope of the United States.

Conclusions

This study suggests a positive association between sleep disorders and taste dysfunction. Furthermore, the observed reduction in both species richness and phylogenetic diversity within the oral microbiota of patients with sleep disorders may contribute to this relationship. Future prospective studies should prioritize investigating the causal relationship between sleep disorders and taste dysfunction.
