Potential causal role of mitochondrial biological effects in COVID-19: Evidence from Mendelian randomization study
Authors: Jiaxin Li, Hongbo Ding, Sumin Zuo, Yi Wang, Ying Guo, Senlin Niu, Zhe-an Shen, Yongan Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Potential mitochondrial biomarkers play an important role in probing COVID-19 physiopathology. We aimed to investigate the causal effects of 82 mitochondrial biomarkers on COVID-19 in a population-based public database. Based on the IEU Open genome-wide association studies database and the genome-wide association studies Catalog database, the significant single-nucleotide polymorphisms of mitochondrial and COVID-19 were extracted as instrumental variables. The inverse variance weighting (IVW) method and the Bayesian weighting method in the two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) method were used for the main causal analysis. Sensitivity tests were performed using the MR-Egger regression test, the MR-PRESSO test for multiple residuals and outliers, the Cochran Q statistic, and the leave-one-out method, and directionality tests were performed using the MR-Steiger method. The protective factors of COVID-19 were determined by IVW method and Bayesian weighting method: Apoptosis-inducing factor 1, mitochondrial (PIVW = 9.6 × 10−5; PBWMR = 4.2 × 10−2) and risk factors: 39S ribosomal protein L33, mitochondrial (PIVW = 2.8 × 10−3; PBWMR = 1.9 × 10−3) and 39S ribosomal protein L52, mitochondrial (PIVW = 2.0 × 10−2; PBWMR = 7.3 × 10−3) and Mitochondrial ubiquitin ligase activator of NF-κB-1 (PIVW = 2.7 × 10−2; PBWMR = 4.8 × 10−2). In the sensitivity test, we did not find heterogeneity, pleiotropic and reverse causality. In this study, we identified 4 potential biomarkers of mitochondrial dysfunction associated with COVID-19, providing new insights into the realization of COVID-19 precision medicine and potential mechanisms.

1. Introduction

Severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus type 2 (SARS-CoV-2) is an enveloped positive-stranded RNA virus of the family Coronaviridae. Its genome consists of 30 kb of single-stranded RNA.[1] COVID-19, caused by SARS-CoV-2, has been prevalent worldwide.[2] As of September 2024, the World Health Organization has reported a cumulative total of 776,281,230 confirmed cases of COVID-19, including 7065,880 deaths.[3] The ongoing epidemic of COVID-19 has had a significant impact on people physical and mental health and quality of life, and has attracted attention as a major public health threat. The complex mechanism of its interaction with host cells is beginning to be understood. A recent study showed that SARS-CoV-2 may enhance its replication capacity and transmission efficiency by affecting mitochondrial function.[4]

Mitochondria are not only the energy factory of cells, but also the key factor in regulating the stress and immune response of cells, which is essential for maintaining cell and system homeostasis.[5] The potential mechanism of acute COVID-19 and its long-term sequelae may involve mitochondrial dysfunction.[6] Studies have shown that COVID-19 infection can cause persistent mitochondrial dysfunction through a variety of mechanisms. For example, viral proteins may interact with mitochondrial antiviral signaling proteins (MAVS) to interfere with their normal role in viral defence responses, leading to chronic mitochondrial dysfunction.[7] COVID-19 infection prevents completion of mitophagy, leading to impaired mitochondrial accumulation, increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, chronic inflammation, and organ failure.[8] In addition, SARS-CoV-2 promotes mitochondrial fusion and elongation and increases ATP synthesis by inducing an increase in mitochondrial transmembrane potential, which provides energy for virus replication in host cells and exacerbates disease severity.[9] Therefore, research into potential biomarkers of mitochondrial dysfunction may be a key target for early detection and long-term monitoring of COVID-19. However, due to the complex clinical symptoms of COVID-19, there is no clear biomarker of mitochondrial dysfunction as one of the reference indicators for diagnosis and assessment of disease progression and severity in COVID-19. The lack of uniform diagnostic criteria for mitochondrial dysfunction makes it particularly difficult to provide patients with accurate medical intervention.

In traditional epidemiological studies, many confounding factors and possible reverse causal effects limit the ability to explore the causal relationship between exposure and disease outcomes. However, genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have revealed genetic determinants associated with a wide range of diseases, providing a new perspective for a deeper understanding of disease mechanisms.[10] The problem of confounding factors is naturally solved by the principle of random distribution of alleles during conception.[11] Therefore, the Mendelian randomization (MR) method uses single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) identified in GWAS as instrumental variables (IVs) to explore the causal relationship between exposure and disease, which helps to reveal the true relationship between genetic factors and diseases. This study aims to explore the causal relationship between mitochondrial biological function and COVID-19 using two-sample MR analysis. The main findings of our research include the protective factors of COVID-19: Apoptosis-inducing factor 1, mitochondrial, and the risk factors of COVID-19: 39S ribosomal protein L33, mitochondrial, and 39S ribosomal protein L52, mitochondrial, and Mitochondrial ubiquitin ligase activator of NFKB 1.

2. Method

The data used in this MR study are all from publicly available GWAS data, so there is no need to apply for permission to access the GWAS database. Researchers who provide data sources have obtained ethical approval from relevant institutions and informed consent from participants. Detailed content can be queried in the original publications and alliances of relevant data.

2.1. Data sources

This MR study was conducted in strict accordance with the guidelines of the STROBE-MR statement (Supplementary Table 1).[12]We screened 82 genetic variations associated with mitochondrial biological functions, which were derived from the IEU Open GWAS database (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/) as the main exposure factor for this study.[13] To evaluate the outcome of COVID-19, we selected 5 cohorts of genetic variables associated with COVID-19 from the GWAS Catalog database (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/gwas) as the outcome indicators of the study. The sample of this study includes both male and female individuals of European ancestry to ensure the reliability of the results and to reduce potential data bias due to racial differences.

2.2. Selecting instrumental variables

We strictly abide by the 3 assumptions of Mendel randomization when selecting instrumental variables: instrumental variables only have a direct correlation with the mitochondria studied (Objective criteria: P < 5 × 10−8); the instrumental variables are independent of the potential confounding factors between mitochondria and COVID-19; the instrumental variable only affects COVID-19 through the mitochondrial pathway.[14] In addition, because only a small number of SNPs with mitochondrial biological functions were significantly associated with COVID-19, to solve the problem of the limited number of mitochondrial-related SNPs, we adjusted the SNP screening threshold to P < 5 × 10−6 to ensure that there were enough instrumental variables to meet the basic needs of MR analysis. However, the relaxation threshold may include weak instrumental variables with insufficient evidence associated with mitochondrial biomarkers and increase the potential risk of false positives. Therefore, in order to ensure the accuracy of the results, we use the F statistic to strictly eliminate the weak instrumental variable and combine the heterogeneity analysis to verify the stability of the instrumental variable effect, thereby reducing the bias that the weak instrumental variable may cause. In addition, the results of the Bayesian weighting method and the inverse variance weighting (IVW) method were cross-validated, and a Bonferroni correction was used to control the risk of multiple tests to avoid the interference of false positives on the results, so as to ensure the reliability of the analysis results.

To exclude working variables that may have linkage disequilibrium, we group SNPs according to the parameters window size = 10,000 kb and R2 < 0.001 to ensure the independence of SNPs. To ensure that the selected instrumental variables are strong instrumental variables, we retain SNPs with F > 10 as strong instrumental variables and exclude weak instrumental variables with F < 10.[15] Finally, in order to make the results more reliable, we performed a meta-analysis using METAL to combine the association results of all SNPs from the 5 cohorts of COVID-19 to expand the sample size[16](Table 1). In addition, we performed cross-cohort heterogeneity analysis on the 5 COVID-19 cohorts before METAL merging. After eliminating missing and abnormal values, 7,188,440 high-quality SNPs were retained. The analysis results showed that the overall heterogeneity was much lower than the 50% critical value, Mean _ I2 = 14.896172% and the heterogeneity test was not statistically significant, Mean_Q_pval = .6614243, and the SNPs with high heterogeneity (SNP_with_high_I2 = 281764) only accounted for 3.92% (281,764/7,188,440). The results show that all SNP effect sizes are highly consistent among the 5 cohorts, and the difference between the cohorts is only due to random errors. The cross-cohort data are homogeneous, which ensures the stability of the instrumental variable effect size in MR analysis and makes the results more reliable and extrapolated.

2.3. Two-sample Mendelian randomization

We used IVW as the primary assessment method and weighted median (WME), simple mode (SM), and weighted mode (WM) as complementary methods to IVW for assessing global pleiotropy by MR-Egger regression.[17,18] Since IVW is more accurate than the other 4 methods in assessing causality, we use IVW as the main method for assessing causality in MR analysis.[19] IVW P value was <.05 as the basis for judging whether the causal relationship was significant. If the P value was <.05, we considered it to be statistically significant. The effect size was then assessed using the odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) (95% CI). Exposure with OR < 1.00 was defined as a protective factor for the outcome; it was a risk factor.[20] However, for the IVW method to be reliable, there must be no horizontal pleiotropic effect.[21] Therefore, in order to test whether there is horizontal pleiotropy in the results, we conducted a sensitivity analysis to ensure the accuracy of the IVW results. Cochran Q statistic and its corresponding P-value are used to test whether there is heterogeneity in the instrumental variables.[22] To ensure the stability and directionality of the results, we use MR-Egger to test whether the instrumental variables violate the second hypothesis of MR and to assess the global directional pleiotropy. If the MR-Egger intercept is not zero, it indicates that the IVW results show pleiotropy.[23] MR-PRESSO was used to test the results for multiple residuals and outliers. Applying the Bonferroni-corrected P-value as a threshold for establishing revised statistical significance in sensitivity analyses, the Bonferroni-corrected P-value for mitochondria was 6.09 × 10−4 (0.05/82), and for those cases where the P-value was below .05 but did not reach the Bonferroni-corrected threshold, we could consider them as a preliminary indication of a causal relationship Hints. In addition, we also used the ‘leave-one-out’ method to observe its effect on the stability of the results by removing 1 SNP at a time. In order to test whether there is a reverse causal relationship between exposure factors and outcome factors, we conducted the MR-Steiger direction test.[24] Finally, we use the Bayesian weighting method to strengthen the results of the IVW method. If the P-values in the IVW and Bayesian weighting methods are both <.05, we consider the results to be significant.[25]

2.4. Statistical analysis

Based on R (version 4.3.1) environment. Through the ‘TwoSample MR’ package, we complete inverse-variance weighted, MR-Egger regression, weighted median estimator, SM, WM, Cochran Q statistic, ‘leave-one-out’ method and MR-Steiger. Visualization results, such as leave 1 out plots and scatter plots, are generated. Complete the MR-PRESSO test in the ‘MRPRESSO’ package. Bayesian weighting method and posterior mean weighting visualization as well as weighted data plots and visualization of their regression results, are done by the ‘BWMR’ software package (Fig. 1).

3.3. Bayesian weighting method analysis

Contrary to the conclusion obtained by the IVW method, we revealed a significant causal relationship between the 4 mitochondrial biological functions and COVID-19 through the Bayesian weighting method. It is worth noting that the P values of rRNA methyltransferase 3, mitochondrial, and NADH dehydrogenase (ubiquinone) iron-sulfur protein 4 were no longer statistically significant in the Bayesian weighting method. The inconsistency between IVW and BWMR results may be due to the different characteristics of the 2 methods. IVW integrated all instrumental variables according to the reciprocal of variance of genetic instrumental variable effect value, and did not adjust the heterogeneity instrumental variables existing in NADH dehydrogenase (ubiquinone) iron-sulfur protein 4 (Cochran Q P = .02) and rRNA methyltransferase 3, mitochondrial (Cochran Q P = .0005), and further amplified the suggestive association due to sensitivity to outliers and heterogeneity. The Bayesian weighting method solves the uncertainty caused by the weak instrumental variables in the traditional method. By calculating the posterior probability weight through the Bayesian framework, the weight of the instrumental variables with effect deviation and heterogeneity in the above 2 indicators can be automatically reduced, and the outliers and heterogeneous interference can be effectively filtered, indicating that the suggestive correlation of IVW may be driven by interference rather than real robust correlation.

In order to ensure the reliability and accuracy of the results, we finally retained 39S ribosomal protein L33, mitochondrial (PBWMR = 1.9 × 10−3), and 39S ribosomal protein L52. Mitochondrial (PBWMR = 7.3 × 10−3), Mitochondrial ubiquitin ligase activator of NFKB 1 (PBWMR = 4.8 × 10−2), and Apoptosis-inducing factor 1, mitochondrial (PBWMR = 4.2 × 10−2) as evidence of a causal relationship between mitochondrial biological function and COVID-19 (Table 3). We also test and visualize the quality of the posterior mean of each variable. We find that the posterior distribution of each variable is relatively concentrated, indicating that we have a more accurate estimate of the true value of the parameter (Fig. 5).

4. Discussion

Mitochondrial dysfunction plays an important physiological role in long-term COVID-19 and COVID-19 infection and severe COVID-19 populations. Among them, oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) disorder, increased ROS, oxidative damage, increased mitochondrial genome mutation rate, and failure of energy metabolism and antioxidant protection mechanisms affect the severity of COVID-19 and lead to increased mortality.[26,27] A prospective observational study evaluated mitochondrial oxygen consumption in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCS) from participants with no history of COVID-19, participants with a history of COVID-19 and full recovery, and participants with long-term COVID-19 symptoms. It was found that the mitochondrial oxygen consumption of PBMCS from participants with a history of COVID-19 and full recovery and participants with long-term COVID-19 symptoms was significantly different from that of those without a history of COVID-19. Among them, the abnormal changes in mitochondrial respiratory function and energy metabolism of PBMCs in patients with long-term COVID-19 were the most significant. This abnormality leads to impaired mitochondrial capacity, which in turn leads to persistent fatigue and muscle weakness in patients.[28] Dai S et al found mitochondrial metabolic disorders and extensive immune inflammation in the analysis of heart failure myocardial tissue in patients with long-term COVID-19, suggesting that mitochondrial dysfunction and immune inflammation may serve as key pathways linking COVID-19 and cardiovascular disease.[29] In addition, increased cardiac damage was observed in patients with COVID-19 infection, which may be related to viral infection that activates mitochondrial oxidative stress to inhibit myocardial cytoskeletal protein and energy metabolism, leading to cardiac dysfunction and heart failure.[30] Ajaz S et al.found that the levels of fibroblast growth factor 21 (FGF-21) and interleukin 6 (IL-6) increased in the plasma of patients with severe COVID-19, and mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and inflammation can lead to an increase in the level of FGF-21.[31] Inflammation and thrombosis may cause severe and fatal consequences in patients with COVID-19.[32] During a COVID-19 infection, the production of mitochondrial ROS is triggered, which leads to the transformation of cell energy metabolism from OXPHOS to glycolysis, which may aggravate the inflammatory response and promote the proliferation of the virus.[33] In addition, elevated inflammatory or oxidative status caused by COVID-19 infection may lead to mitochondrial dysfunction, leading to platelet damage and apoptosis,[34] this dysfunctional interaction of platelets with the coagulation cascade exacerbates coagulation events and thrombosis, which seriously affects the progression and severity of the disease. Viral proteins may interact with mitochondrial complexes, triggering an overreaction of immune cells, thereby directly destroying mitochondrial function. However, traditional observational studies often face reverse causality and confounding by confounding factors. Our MR analyses use information on genetic variation established by the random allocation of gametes during maternal conception to minimize confounding and provide reliable assessment results. MR is therefore superior to traditional observational studies.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first MR study to investigate the causal relationship between mitochondria and COVID-19, and our study suggests that Apoptosis-inducing factor 1, mitochondrial, may exist as a protective factor for COVID-19. In contrast, 39S ribosomal protein L33, mitochondrial, and 39S ribosomal protein L52, mitochondrial as well as mitochondrial ubiquitin ligase activator of NF-κB-1 may be risk factors for COVID-19.

To date, epidemiological reports on the relationship between mitochondrial biomarkers and COVID-19 have been limited, despite the fact that the prevalence of COVID-19 is closely linked to immune-mediated biological functions of mitochondria.[35] In our study, relevant mitochondrial potential biomarkers of COVID-19 were explored by large-scale genome-wide association as well as two-sample MR. It has been found in previous studies that Apoptosis-inducing factor 1 is an important redox-active mitochondrial enzyme under normal physiological conditions. However, in pathological conditions, Apoptosis-inducing factor 1 is transferred from mitochondria to the nucleus of injured neurons, promoting apoptosis to help clear infected or damaged cells, thereby maintaining the balance of the immune system and preventing autoimmune responses.[36] In addition, the tight binding of Apoptosis-inducing factor 1 to the mitochondrial membrane helps maintain oxidative phosphorylation and cellular respiration, supporting the energy needs of immune cells by maintaining mitochondrial function.[37] Artus C et al.[38]proposed that Apoptosis-inducing factor 1 may play an anti-inflammatory role by inhibiting the activation of inflammasomes to prevent excessive inflammatory response and related tissue damage. To investigate the potential interaction mechanism between COVID-19 and apoptosis-inducing factor, Yang et al found that COVID-19 M protein activated mitochondrial apoptosis of B-cell lymphoma 2 (BCL-2)-related ovarian killers, thereby exacerbating COVID-19-induced lung injury in vivo.[39] Archer SL et al found that COVID-19 induced mitochondria-induced apoptosis of pulmonary airway epithelial cells by damaging mitochondria in alveolar epithelial cells and pulmonary artery smooth muscle cells, resulting in COVID-19 pneumonia hypoxia.[40] In addition, apoptosis-inducing factors have been shown to be associated with the apoptosis of porcine enteric coronavirus PEDV.[41] Previous studies have shown that Apoptosis-inducing factor 1 has a complex two-way role in different pathological scenarios, which can eliminate virus-infected cells by promoting apoptosis of damaged cells, or may aggravate lung epithelial cell injury when overactivated. Although our MR results showed a causal association between Apoptosis-inducing factor 1 and COVID-19 risk reduction (PIVW = 9.6 × 10−5; PBWMR = 4.2 × 10−2), this only reflects its overall genetic association and cannot completely rule out the harmful effects of local tissues. Therefore, interventions targeting Apoptosis-inducing factor or its upstream regulators may help to develop new treatments to reduce virus-induced cell damage.

Our MR results show that the increased genetic level of Mitochondrial ubiquitin ligase activator of NFKB1 has a certain enlightening association with the increased risk of COVID-19, which can be mutually confirmed with the existing mechanism research conclusions. Mitochondrial ubiquitin ligase activator of NFKB1 is a key transcription factor in the nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) signaling pathway and plays an important role in anti-inflammatory and immune responses.[42] In SARS-CoV-2 infection, an abnormal NF-κB signaling pathway may be associated with excessive inflammatory response in severe COVID-19.[43] In a systematic review, it is mentioned that SARS-CoV-2 infection can activate NF-κB through various pathways, thereby promoting the proliferation of the SARS-CoV-2 virus and the expression of inflammatory factors.[44] In addition, some drugs used to treat COVID-19 may play a role by inhibiting NF-κB activity, such as glucocorticoids, monupivir, and paxilovir.[45,46] Therefore, the mitochondrial ubiquitin ligase activator of NFKB1 may be a key factor in exacerbating the severity of COVID-19, but this conclusion still needs functional experiments to further verify its direct regulatory role.

39S ribosomal protein L33, mitochondrial, and 39S ribosomal protein L52, mitochondrial are part of the large subunit of mitochondrial 39S, which are involved in the formation of mitochondrial 55S ribosomes and play an important role in the synthesis of 13 hydrophobic peptides that play a key role in oxidative phosphorylation.[47] COVID-19 infection is highly correlated with inhibition of mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation and an increase in mitochondrial ROS by activation of hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α), shifting metabolism to glycolysis to drive viral proliferation.[48] Liu L et al found that deletion of the mitochondrial long isomer of 39S ribosomal protein L33, which contains an alternative exon 3, leads to impaired cancer cell proliferation and increased apoptosis, and also causes mitochondrial dysfunction, including increased ROS accumulation, ATP production, and 16S rRNA levels.[49] In breast cancer cells, Li X et al found that the expression of 39 S ribosomal protein L52, mitochondrial is regulated by hypoxia-inducible factor-1 (HIF-1) and is related to the hypoxic environment, and plays a role in inhibiting apoptosis and promoting cell migration and invasion.[50] Although the specific regulatory mechanism of 39S ribosomal protein L33, mitochondrial and 39S ribosomal protein L52, mitochondrial on COVID-19 in mitochondrial dysfunction has not been directly reported, according to the existing mechanism clues, they are the core components of the mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation system. Abnormal expression will affect oxidative phosphorylation activity, and COVID-19 can further inhibit oxidative phosphorylation by activating HIF-1α. Therefore, the expression and activity of 39S ribosomal protein L33, mitochondrial and 39S ribosomal protein L52, mitochondrial can reflect the dynamic changes of mitochondrial function to a certain extent, and become potential indicators for monitoring the progress of COVID-19, but it needs to be verified by a larger sample clinical cohort.

It is worth noting that although rRNA methyltransferase 3, mitochondrial and NADH dehydrogenase (ubiquinone) iron-sulfur protein 4 may not pass the BWMR test due to limited sample size or potential confounding factors. However, the mitochondrial function of NADH dehydrogenase (ubiquinone) iron-sulfur protein 4 and rRNA methyltransferase 3, mitochondrial, and their mechanism in immune response still have important research value in SARS-CoV-2 infection. For example, Li N et al proposed that depletion of rRNA methyltransferase 3, mitochondrial, may regulate m6A levels in SARS-CoV-2 viral RNA and the innate immune response of host cells,[51] NADH dehydrogenase (ubiquinone) iron-sulfur protein 4, as a component of mitochondrial respiratory chain complex I, is essential in the electron transport chain and maintaining mitochondrial morphology and function.[52]

Our approach has several advantages. First, we eliminate the interference of reverse causality and confounding factors in MR sensitivity analysis and remove outliers. Second, the instrumental variables we include are significantly associated with exposure and are representative. In addition, we eliminate the presence of weak instrumental variables through F-statistics, and all instrumental variables have F-values >10. In addition, the population samples we included were all European, thus avoiding bias in the results due to racial differences. Our research also has some limitations. First, the lack of data on COVID-19 and mitochondrial non-European populations in the GWAS database, as well as the lack of detailed statistical data on the participating population, makes it impossible for us to perform further subgroup analysis and hierarchical discussion. Secondly, although the inclusion of a single European ancestry reduces the stratification bias caused by genetic heterogeneity between races, recessive stratification, such as regional genetic differences within the same ancestry, may still exist. In addition, although we have reduced the confounding effect to the greatest extent through strict instrumental variable screening thresholds and heterogeneity analysis in the two-sample MR analysis, it is still unable to completely avoid unmeasured residual confusion due to data availability. Future research can further correct population stratification and residual confusion by genome control, principal component analysis, and other methods to improve the robustness of the results. The results of this study are currently only applicable to people of European descent, which limits the extrapolation to other races. In the future, repeated studies should be carried out in different ethnic groups to verify the cross-population applicability of the results, so that the conclusions are generally applicable to other ethnic groups.

5. Conclusion

The purpose of this study is to explore the causal relationship between mitochondrial biological function and COVID-19 through the 2 public databases of IEU OpenGWAS and GWAS catalog. After MR analysis, we found 4 mitochondrial biological functions, among which apoptosis-inducing factor 1, mitochondrial, was the protective factor of COVID-19, while 39S ribosomal protein L33, mitochondrial, 39S ribosomal protein L52, mitochondrial, and Mitochondrial ubiquitin ligase activator of NFKB 1 are risk factors for COVID-19. In conclusion, our findings may provide a direction for exploring potential biomarkers of mitochondrial dysfunction and new ideas for early detection and monitoring of long-term, precise treatment strategies for COVID-19. Future research should increase the sample size and combine different omics platforms to explore potential biomarkers of mitochondrial dysfunction and the pathogenesis of COVID-19 in more detail from different perspectives and levels.
