Introduction

The

-number

While its motivation is geometric, the

This fusion of geometry and combinatorics places the

Let

,

By

The sequence

The

For instance, when

Theorem 1.1

Let

The first problem concerns the

Since the definition of the

For all

The second problem deals with the

For complex complete toric varieties, it is well known that (even-degree) Betti numbers form a unimodal sequence due to the Hard Lefschetz theorem.

However, this does not generally hold for the real loci of toric varieties.
Interestingly, for many graphs

This phenomenon was posed as an open question in the authors’ previous work

Corollary 1.3

The

It is also worth noting that these

While topological spaces with log-concave Betti number sequences have received considerable attention (see, e.g.,

The paper is organized as follows.
In Section

Section

Lemma 2.4

Let

For each vertex

(G^{\ast}_{I})^{\ast}_{\{v\}}=G^{\ast}_{I\cup\{v\}}.

Proof.

Both graphs have the same vertex set, namely

Assume that

Theorem 2.5
.

The following graph classes are closed under reconnected complements, and their minimal graphs are as follows.

(1)

The class of connected graphs, whose minimal graphs are trees.

(2)

The class of Hamiltonian graphs, together with the null, single-vertex, and single-edge graphs. The minimal graphs in this class are the null graph, the single-vertex graph, the single-edge graph, and cycle graphs.

(3)

The class of graphs with a universal vertex, whose minimal graphs are star graphs.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let

be a finite simple graph with vertex set

.

(1)

If

is odd, then

\left|b(G)\right|=(-1)^{\frac{\left|V(G)\right|-1}{2}}b(G).

(2)

If
G
has a connected component of even order, then

b(G)=0.

(3)

Assume that
\left|V(G)\right|=2i
is even, and
G
has no connected component of even order.
If the number of connected components of
G
is
2j
, then

\left|b(G)\right|=(-1)^{i-j}\cdot b(G).

Proof.

The first two cases were proved in
[
8
]
.
We prove the third case.
Let
G_{1},\ldots,G_{2j}
be the connected components of
G
, where
\left|V(G_{i})\right|=2k_{i}+1
for some non-negative integers
k_{i}
.
Since
G
has
2i
vertices, we have

k_{1}+\cdots+k_{2j}+j=i.

By (
1
), for each
1\leq i\leq 2j
, we know that

\left|b(G_{i})\right|=(-1)^{k_{i}}\cdot b(G_{i}).

Therefore, we obtain

\displaystyle\left|b(G)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|b(G_{1})\cdot b(G_{2})\cdots b(G_{2j})\right|

\displaystyle=\left|b(G_{1})\right|\cdot\left|b(G_{2})\right|\cdots\left|b(G_{2j})\right|

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k_{1}+\cdots+k_{2j}}\cdot b(G)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{i-j}\cdot b(G),

as desired.
∎

4.
A decomposition formula

Let
G
be a finite simple graph.
For each pair
\mathbf{e}
of vertices of
G
, let
G+\mathbf{e}
denote the graph obtained from
G
by adding
\mathbf{e}
as an edge.
Note that
G=G+\mathbf{e}
if
\mathbf{e}
is already an edge of
G
.

Recall the definition of
\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}(G)
from (
3.1
), and we now describe some of its properties.

Proposition 4.1
.

For a finite simple graph
G
, let
J\in\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}(G)
, and
\mathbf{e}
a pair of vertices of
G
.
The following statements hold.

(1)

For
I\subseteq J
,
I\in\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}(G)
if and only if
I\in\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}(G|_{J})
.

(2)

For
J\subseteq I
,
I\in\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}(G)
if and only if
I\setminus J\in\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}(G^{\ast}_{J})
.

(3)

\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}(G)\subseteq\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}(G+\mathbf{e})
.
Moreover,
I\in\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}(G+\mathbf{e})\setminus\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}(G)
implies that
\mathbf{e}\subseteq I
.

Proof.

The statements (
1
) and (
3
) are straightforward to verify.
We now consider case (
2
).
By Lemma
2.4
, we may assume without loss of generality that
|J|=2
, in which case
J
is regarded as an edge of
G
.

Assume
I\in\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}(G)
and
J\subseteq I
.
Then there exists a subset
I_{0}\subseteq I
such that
G|_{I_{0}}
is a connected component of
G|_{I}
and contains
J
.
By (
1
) in Theorem
2.5
, the reconnected complement
(G|_{I_{0}})^{\ast}_{J}
is connected.
Moreover, since
(G|_{I_{0}})^{\ast}_{J}
is also a connected component of
G^{\ast}_{J}
with even order, it follows that
I\setminus J\in\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}(G^{\ast}_{J})
.

Conversely, assume that
I\setminus J\in\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}(G^{\ast}_{J})
.
If
G|_{J}
is an isolated edge of
G
, then we observe that
G^{\ast}_{J}=G|_{V(G)\setminus J}
, where
V(G)
is the set of vertices of
G
.
Then it is immediate that
I\in\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}(G)
.
If
G|_{J}
is not isolated in
G
, then there exists a connected component
G^{\ast}_{J}|_{I_{0}}
of
G^{\ast}_{J}
such that
G|_{I_{0}\cup J}
forms connected component of
G
.
Since
|I_{0}\cup J|
is even,
I\in\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}(G)
.
This completes the proof.
∎

Assume that
G
is a simple graph with
2i
vertices.

Lemma 4.2
.

Given
J\in\overline{\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}}(G)
,

\left|C_{k}(J;G)\right|=\left|C_{k}(\emptyset;G^{\ast}_{J})\right|

for each
0\leq k\leq i-\frac{\left|J\right|}{2}
.

Proof.

Since the statement is immediate when
k=0
, we assume
k\geq 1
.
Let
\mathcal{C}\in C_{k}(J;G)
be a chain of the form

J=I_{0}\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq I_{k}=V(G).

Define
\varphi_{J}(\mathcal{C})
to be a chain

\emptyset=(I_{0}\setminus J)\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq(I_{k}\setminus J)=V(G^{\ast}_{J}).

For each
1\leq\ell\leq k
, we have
I_{\ell}\in\overline{\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}}(G)
, and by (
2
) in Proposition
4.1
, it follows that
I_{\ell}\setminus J\in\overline{\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}}(G^{\ast}_{J})
.
We obtain that
\varphi_{J}(\mathcal{C})\in C_{k}(\emptyset;G^{\ast}_{J})
, and, hence,
\varphi_{J}
is regarded as a map from
C_{k}(J;G)
to
C_{k}(\emptyset;G^{\ast}_{J})
.

Conversely, let
\mathcal{C}\in C_{k}(\emptyset;G^{\ast}_{J})
be a chain of the form

\emptyset=I^{\ast}_{0}\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq I^{\ast}_{k}=V(G^{\ast}_{J}).

Define
\psi_{J}(\mathcal{C})
to be

J=J\cup I^{\ast}_{0}\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq J\cup I^{\ast}_{k}=V(G).

Since each
J\cup I^{\ast}_{\ell}
belongs to
\overline{\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}}(G)
by Proposition
4.1
(
2
), it follows that
\psi_{J}(\mathcal{C})\in C_{k}(J;G)
, and thus
\psi_{J}
defines a map from
C_{k}(\emptyset;G^{\ast}_{J})
to
C_{k}(J;G)
.

It is straightforward to check that
\varphi_{J}
and
\psi_{J}
are inverses of each other, establishing a one-to-one correspondence between
C_{k}(J;G)
and
C_{k}(\emptyset;G^{\ast}_{J})
.
∎

Let a pair
\mathbf{e}
of vertices of
G
be given.
Consider a chain
\mathcal{C}\in C_{k}(\emptyset;G+\mathbf{e})
given by

\emptyset=I_{0}\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq I_{k}=V(G+\mathbf{e}).

Assume that there is an index
0\leq\ell\leq k
such that
I_{\ell}\notin\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}(G)
.
Since
\emptyset\in\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}(G)
, any such index
\ell
should be at least
1
.
We choose the smallest such index
\ell
, and we call
\mathcal{C}

\mathbf{e}
-singular
at

.
If no such index exists, then
\mathcal{C}
is said to be
\mathbf{e}
-regular
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
G
be a simple graph with
2i
vertices, where
i\geq 1
, and
\mathbf{e}
a pair of vertices of
G
.
If
J\in\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}(G+\mathbf{e})\setminus\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}(G)
, then the number of
\mathbf{e}
-singular chains
\mathcal{C}\in C_{k}(\emptyset;G+\mathbf{e})
at
J
is given by

\sum_{p+q=k-1}\Bigl(\bigl|C_{p}(J;G+\mathbf{e})\bigl|\cdot\sum_{J^{\prime}\in\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}(G|_{J})}\left|C_{q}(\emptyset;G|_{J^{\prime}})\right|\Bigl),

where
1\leq k\leq i
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{C}\in C_{k}(\emptyset;G+\mathbf{e})
be an
\mathbf{e}
-singular chain at
J
, where
k\geq 1
, given by:

\emptyset=I_{0}\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq I_{k}=V(G+\mathbf{e}).

Then there exists an index
1\leq\ell\leq k
such that
I_{\ell}=J
.
We denote
I_{\ell-1}
by
J^{\prime}
.
By Proposition
4.1
(
1
),
J^{\prime}\in\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}(G)
and
J^{\prime}\subseteq J
, and hence
J^{\prime}\in\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}(G|_{J})
as well.
Thus,
\mathcal{C}
splits into two chains:

\emptyset=I_{0}\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq I_{\ell-1}=J^{\prime}\quad\text{and}\quad J=I_{\ell}\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq I_{k}=V(G+\mathbf{e}).

The former is an element of
C_{\ell-1}(\emptyset;G|_{J^{\prime}})
and the latter is an element of
C_{k-\ell}(J;G+\mathbf{e})
.

Now, choose
J^{\prime}\subsetneq J
with
J^{\prime}\in\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}(G|_{J})
.
Let chains in
C_{p}(J;G+e)
and in
C_{q}(\emptyset;G|_{J^{\prime}})
be defined as

J=J_{0}\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq J_{p}=V(G+\mathbf{e})\quad\text{and}\quad\emptyset=J^{\prime}_{0}\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq J^{\prime}_{q}=V(G|_{J^{\prime}}),

respectively, where
p,q\geq 0
and
p+q=k-1
.
We define
\mathcal{C}
as the chain obtained by combining the two chains above as

\emptyset=J^{\prime}_{0}\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq J^{\prime}_{q}=J^{\prime}\subsetneq J=J_{0}\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq J_{p}=V(G+\mathbf{e}).

By (
1
) and (
3
) of Proposition
4.1
, it follows that, for each
0\leq\ell\leq q
,

J^{\prime}_{\ell}\in\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}(G|_{J^{\prime}})\subseteq\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}(G)\subseteq\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}(G+\mathbf{e}).

Moreover, since
J\in\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}(G+\mathbf{e})\setminus\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}(G)
, we conclude that
\mathcal{C}
is
\mathbf{e}
-singular at
J
.

Finally, the splitting and combining described above yield a one-to-one correspondence between the set of
\mathbf{e}
-singular chains in
C_{k}(\emptyset;G+\mathbf{e})
at
J
and the set

\bigsqcup_{p+q=k-1}\Bigl(C_{p}(J;(G+\mathbf{e}))\times\bigsqcup_{J^{\prime}\in\overline{\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}}(G|_{J})}C_{q}(\emptyset;G|_{J^{\prime}})\Bigl).

This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
G
be a simple graph on
2i
vertices.
For a pair
\mathbf{e}
of vertices of
G
,

a(G+\mathbf{e})=a(G)+\sum_{J\in\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}(G+\mathbf{e})\setminus\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}(G)}\left|b(G|_{J})\right|\cdot a((G+\mathbf{e})^{\ast}_{J}).

Proof.

For each
0\leq k\leq i
, let
RC_{k}
denote the set of
\mathbf{e}
-regular chains in
C_{k}(\emptyset;G+\mathbf{e})
, and
SC^{J}_{k}
the set of
\mathbf{e}
-singular chains at
J\in\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}(G+\mathbf{e})\setminus\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}(G)
in
C_{k}(\emptyset;G+\mathbf{e})
.
It is immediate that

C_{k}(\emptyset;G+\mathbf{e})=RC_{k}\sqcup\Bigl(\bigsqcup_{J\in\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}(G+\mathbf{e})\setminus\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}(G)}SC_{k}^{J}\Bigl).

Let
1\leq k\leq i
be given.
It can be readily verified that
RC_{k}=C_{k}(\emptyset;G)
.
Now let us consider
J\in\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}(G+\mathbf{e})\setminus\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}(G)
with
k\leq j:=\frac{\left|J\right|}{2}\leq i
.
Since it is clear that
G|_{J}
has exactly two components of odd order, by Proposition
3.3
, we have

(4.1)

\left|b(G|_{J})\right|=(-1)^{j-1}\cdot b(G|_{J}).

Furthermore, it follows that

\displaystyle\left|SC^{J}_{k}\right|

\displaystyle=\sum_{p+q=k-1}\Bigl(\bigl|C_{p}(J;G+\mathbf{e})\bigl|\cdot\sum_{J^{\prime}\in\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}(G|_{J})}\left|C_{q}(\emptyset;G|_{J^{\prime}})\right|\Bigl)\text{ (by Lemma~\ref{lemma:split})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{p+q=k-1}\Bigl(\bigl|C_{p}(\emptyset;(G+\mathbf{e})^{\ast}_{J})\bigl|\cdot\sum_{J^{\prime}\in\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}(G|_{J})}\left|C_{q}(\emptyset;G|_{J^{\prime}})\right|\Bigl)\text{ (by Lemma~\ref{lemma:collap})}.

Using the convention stated in (
3.2
), we derive the following equality:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{i}(-1)^{k}\left|SC^{J}_{k}\right|

\displaystyle=-\sum_{p=0}^{i}\sum_{q=0}^{i}\Bigl((-1)^{p+q}\bigl|C_{p}(\emptyset;(G+\mathbf{e})^{\ast}_{J})\bigl|\cdot\sum_{J^{\prime}\in\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}(G|_{J})}\left|C_{q}(\emptyset;G|_{J^{\prime}})\right|\Bigl)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{p=0}^{i}\Bigl((-1)^{p}\bigl|C_{p}(\emptyset;(G+\mathbf{e})^{\ast}_{J})\bigl|\cdot\sum_{J^{\prime}\in\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}(G|_{J})}\sum_{q=0}^{i}(-1)^{q}\left|C_{q}(\emptyset;G|_{J^{\prime}})\right|\Bigl)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{p=0}^{i}\Bigl((-1)^{p}\bigl|C_{p}(\emptyset;(G+\mathbf{e})^{\ast}_{J})\bigl|\cdot\sum_{J^{\prime}\in\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}(G|_{J})}sa(G|_{J^{\prime}})\Bigl)\quad\text{(by Proposition~\ref{prop:anum})}

\displaystyle=-b(G|_{J})\sum_{p=0}^{i}(-1)^{p}\bigl|C_{p}(\emptyset;(G+\mathbf{e})^{\ast}_{J})\bigl|\ \quad\bigl(\because b(G|_{J})=\sum_{J^{\prime}\in\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}(G|_{J})}sa(G|_{J^{\prime}})\bigl)

\displaystyle=-b(G|_{J})\cdot sa((G+\mathbf{e})^{\ast}_{J})\quad\text{(by Proposition~\ref{prop:anum})}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{j-1}\cdot b(G|_{J})\cdot(-1)^{j}\cdot sa((G+\mathbf{e})^{\ast}_{J})

\displaystyle=\left|b(G|_{J})\right|\cdot a((G+\mathbf{e})^{\ast}_{J})\quad\text{(by \eqref{eq:bGJ})}.

We conclude that

\displaystyle a(G+\mathbf{e})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{i}(-1)^{i+k}\left|C_{k}(\emptyset;G+\mathbf{e})\right|

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{i}(-1)^{i+k}\Bigl(\left|RC_{k}\right|+\sum_{J\in\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}(G+\mathbf{e})\setminus\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}(G)}\left|SC^{J}_{k}\right|\Bigl)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{i}(-1)^{i+k}\left|C_{k}(\emptyset;G)\right|+\sum_{J\in\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}(G+\mathbf{e})\setminus\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}(G)}\sum_{k=0}^{i}(-1)^{i+k}\left|SC^{J}_{k}\right|

\displaystyle=a(G)+\sum_{J\in\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}(G+\mathbf{e})\setminus\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}(G)}\left|b(G|_{J})\right|\cdot a((G+\mathbf{e})^{\ast}_{J}).

∎

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
G
be a finite simple graphs with the vertex set
V(G)
, and
\mathbf{e}
a pair of vertices of
G
.
For each
i\geq 0
,

a_{i}(G+\mathbf{e})-a_{i}(G)=\sum_{J\in\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}(G+\mathbf{e})\setminus\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}(G)}\left|b(G|_{J})\right|\cdot a_{i-\frac{|J|}{2}}((G+\mathbf{e})^{\ast}_{J}).

Proof.

To begin, since
V(G)=V(G+\mathbf{e})
, we have

a_{i}(G+\mathbf{e})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subseteq V(G)\\
|I|=2i\end{subarray}}a((G+\mathbf{e})|_{I}).

We now divide the sum according to whether
I
contains
\mathbf{e}
or not; that is,

a_{i}(G+\mathbf{e})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{e}\not\subset I\subseteq V(G)\\
|I|=2i\end{subarray}}a((G+\mathbf{e})|_{I})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{e}\subseteq I\subseteq V(G)\\
|I|=2i\end{subarray}}a((G+\mathbf{e})|_{I}).

For each
I\in\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}(G+\mathbf{e})
, note that

(G+\mathbf{e})|_{I}=\begin{cases}G|_{I}+\mathbf{e},&\mbox{if }\mathbf{e}\in I\\
G|_{I},&\mbox{otherwise}.\end{cases}

It follows that

a_{i}(G+\mathbf{e})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{e}\not\subset I\subseteq V(G)\\
|I|=2i\end{subarray}}a(G|_{I})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{e}\subseteq I\subseteq V(G)\\
|I|=2i\end{subarray}}a(G|_{I}+\mathbf{e}).

We then apply Lemma
4.4
to
G|_{I}+\mathbf{e}
, which confirms that

\displaystyle a_{i}(G+\mathbf{e})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{e}\not\subset I\subseteq V(G)\\
|I|=2i\end{subarray}}a(G|_{I})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{e}\subseteq I\subseteq V(G)\\
|I|=2i\end{subarray}}\Big(a(G|_{I})+\sum_{J\in\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}((G+\mathbf{e})|_{I})\setminus\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}(G|_{I})}\left|b(G|_{J})\right|a\big(((G+\mathbf{e})|_{I})^{\ast}_{J}\big)\Big)

\displaystyle=a_{i}(G)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{e}\subseteq I\subseteq V(G)\\
|I|=2i\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}J\in\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}(G+\mathbf{e})\setminus\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}(G)\\
J\subseteq I\end{subarray}}\left|b(G|_{J})\right|a\big(((G+\mathbf{e})|_{I})^{\ast}_{J}\big)\quad\text{ (by Proposition~\ref{prop:conn}~\eqref{prop:conn1})}.

From Proposition
4.1
(
3
), for each
J\in\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}(G+\mathbf{e})\setminus\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}(G)
, we have
\mathbf{e}\subseteq J
.
It follows that interchanging the order of summation in the second term on the right-hand side yields

a_{i}(G+\mathbf{e})=a_{i}(G)+\sum_{J\in\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}(G+\mathbf{e})\setminus\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}(G)}\Big(\left|b(G|_{J})\right|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}J\subseteq I\\
|I|=2i\end{subarray}}a\big(((G+\mathbf{e})|_{I})^{\ast}_{J}\big)\Big).

By replacing
I^{\prime}=I\setminus J
with (
2.1
), we obtain that

\displaystyle a_{i}(G+\mathbf{e})

\displaystyle=a_{i}(G)+\sum_{J\in\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}(G+\mathbf{e})\setminus\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}(G)}\Big(\left|b(G|_{J})\right|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I^{\prime}\in V((G+\mathbf{e})^{\ast}_{J})\\
|I^{\prime}|=2i-\left|J\right|\end{subarray}}a\big(((G+\mathbf{e})^{\ast}_{J})_{I^{\prime}})\big)\Big)

\displaystyle=a_{i}(G)+\sum_{J\in\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}(G+\mathbf{e})\setminus\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}(G)}\left|b(G|_{J})\right|\cdot a_{i-\frac{|J|}{2}}((G+\mathbf{e})^{\ast}_{J}),

as desired.
∎

By Theorem
4.5
, the monotonicity of the
a_{i}
-numbers and the
a
-numbers are established, as shown in Corollary
4.6
.

Corollary 4.6
.

Let
G
be a finite simple graph and
H
a subgraph of
G
.
Then, for all
i\geq 0
,

a_{i}(H)\leq a_{i}(G).

In particular, if
H
is a spanning subgraph of
G
, then

a(H)\leq a(G).

Proof.

If
H
is a spanning subgraph of
G
, the corollary is clear.
Otherwise, we add isolated vertices to
H
so that it has the same number of vertices as
G
.
Note that this modification does not change
a_{i}(H)
for all
i\geq 0
.
Therefore, the corollary follows immediately from Theorem
4.5
.
∎

5.
Unimodality of
a
-sequences

In this section, for each simple graph
G
, we discuss the unimodality of the
a
-sequence

(a_{0}(G),a_{1}(G),a_{2}(G),\ldots)

in
i
of
G
.
Note that this sequence is eventually zero.

We say that a sequence satisfies the
tail-peaked condition
if it satisfies the following two conditions:

(1)

it is eventually zero, and

(2)

it increases up to its second-to-last nonzero term.

In this case, the sequence is trivially unimodal.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a graph class closed under reconnected complement.
If the
a
-sequence of each minimal graph in
\mathcal{G}
satisfies the tail-peaked condition, then the same holds for every graph in
\mathcal{G}
.

Proof.

Let
G\in\mathcal{G}
be a simple graph on
m
vertices.
It is immediate that the
a
-sequence of
G
satisfies the tail-peaked condition when
m\leq 2
.
We proceed by induction on
m
.
Assume that for some integer
N\geq 2
, the
a
-sequence of every graph in
\mathcal{G}
with at most
N
vertices satisfies the tail-peaked condition.

Now let
G\in\mathcal{G}
be a graph on
N+1
vertices.
If
G
is minimal in
\mathcal{G}
, then by assumption, its
a
-sequence satisfies the tail-peaked condition.
Otherwise, there exists a graph
G^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}
with
N+1
vertices and an edge
\mathbf{e}
such that
G=G^{\prime}+\mathbf{e}
, where
\mathbf{e}
is not an edge of
G^{\prime}
.
Suppose that the
a
-sequence of
G^{\prime}
satisfies the tail-peaked condition: that is,

a_{0}(G^{\prime})\leq a_{1}(G^{\prime})\leq\cdots\leq a_{\lfloor\frac{N-1}{2}\rfloor}(G^{\prime}).

For each
J\in\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}(G)
with
\frac{|J|}{2}=j
, consider the induced graph
G^{\ast}_{J}=(G^{\prime}+\mathbf{e})^{\ast}_{J}
.
Since
G^{*}_{J}
has fewer than
N+1
vertices, the inductive hypothesis applies, and thus

a_{0}\big(G^{\ast}_{J}\big)\leq\cdots\leq a_{\lfloor\frac{N-1}{2}\rfloor-j}\big(G^{\ast}_{J}\big).

Then, by Theorem
4.5
, the
a
-sequence of
G
also satisfies the tail-peaked condition, completing the inductive step.
Therefore, by induction, the
a
-sequence of every graph
G\in\mathcal{G}
satisfies the tail-peaked condition.
∎

Corollary 5.2
.

If
G
is a Hamiltonian graph, then the
a
-sequence satisfies the tail-peaked condition, and hence is unimodal.

Proof.

Consider the graph class consisting of Hamiltonian graphs together with the single-edge graph.
Observe that the cycle graph of order
n\geq 3
is the unique minimal graph with
n
vertices in the class.
It is known
[
8
]
that the
a
-sequence of each cycle graph coincides with the half Pascal triangle, and in particular, it satisfies the tail-peaked condition.
Therefore, by Theorem
2.5
(
2
) and Theorem
5.1
, we conclude this corollary.
∎

Now, we consider the star graph
K_{1,n-1}
.
For each
0\leq i\leq\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor
, the
a_{i}
-number of a star graph is computed by

(5.1)

a_{i}(K_{1,n-1})=\binom{n-1}{2i-2}A_{2i-1},

where
A_{k}
denotes the Euler zig-zag number (
[
21
]
A000111).
In particular,
A_{2n-1}
is known as the tangent number (
[
21
]
A000182).
For more details, see
[
8
]
.

Recall that

(5.2)

A_{2i-1}=\frac{2^{2i}(2^{2i}-1)|B_{2i}|}{2i},

where
B_{2i}
denotes the
2i
th Bernoulli number.
See
[
21
]
A000367 and A002445.

Proposition 5.3
.

[
1
,
4
]

For any positive integer

,

C_{i}:=\frac{2(2i)!}{(2\pi)^{2i}}\cdot\frac{1}{1-2^{-2i}}<|B_{2i}|<D_{i}:=\frac{2(2i)!}{(2\pi)^{2i}}\cdot\frac{1}{1-2^{1-2i}}

Combining (
5.2
) with Proposition
5.3
, we obtain the inequality

(5.3)

\bar{C}_{i}:=\frac{2^{2i}(2^{2i}-1)}{2i}\cdot C_{i}<A_{2i-1}<\bar{D}_{i}:=\frac{2^{2i}(2^{2i}-1)}{2i}\cdot D_{i}.

Lemma 5.4
.

For each
k\geq 3
,

a_{k-1}(K_{1,2k})<a_{k}(K_{1,2k}).

Proof.

Substituting
n=2k+1
and
i=k-1,k
into (
5.1
), we obtain

a_{k-1}(K_{1,2k})=\binom{2k}{2k-3}\cdot A_{2k-3},\qquad a_{k}(K_{1,2k})=\binom{2k}{2k-1}\cdot A_{2k-1}.

Applying (
5.3
), we further have

a_{k-1}(K_{1,2k})<\binom{2k}{2k-3}\cdot\bar{D}_{k-1},\qquad\binom{2k}{2k-1}\cdot\bar{C}_{k}<a_{k}(K_{1,2k}).

It is enough to verify the inequality

(5.4)

\binom{2k}{2k-3}\cdot\bar{D}_{k-1}<\binom{2k}{2k-1}\cdot\bar{C}_{k},

for all
k\geq 3
.
A straightforward computation shows that (
5.4
) is equivalent to

(5.5)

(2\pi)^{2}\cdot k\cdot(2^{2k-2}-1)<48(k-1)(2^{2k-1}-4).

For
k=3,4
, and
5
, this inequality can be verified directly.
Moreover, the following two inequalities

\begin{cases}(2\pi)^{2}\cdot k<48(k-1),\\[2.0pt]
2^{2k-2}-1<2^{2k-1}-4,\end{cases}

hold for all
k\geq 6
.
This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 5.5
.

The
a
-sequence of a star graph
G=K_{1,n-1}
satisfies the tail-peaked condition.
In particular,

(5.6)

a_{0}(G)<\cdots<a_{\lfloor\frac{n-1}{2}\rfloor}(G).

Proof.

The base cases
n\leq 6
can be verified directly.
We proceed by induction on
n
.
Assume that, for some integer
N\geq 7
, the statement holds for
n\leq N-1
.

Let
G
be a star graph on
N
vertices with the universal vertex
v
, and fix any non-universal vertex
u
.
Let
G^{\prime}
be a spanning subgraph of
G
obtained by removing the edge
\mathbf{e}=\{u,v\}
of
G
, so that
G=G^{\prime}+\mathbf{e}
.
Then
G^{\prime}
is isomorphic to the star graph
K_{1,N-2}
, and by the induction hypothesis,

a_{0}(G^{\prime})<\cdots<a_{\lfloor\frac{N-2}{2}\rfloor}(G^{\prime}).

For each
J\in\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}(G)\setminus\mathcal{E}\mathcal{C}(G^{\prime})
, we have
v\in J
, so the reconnected complement
G^{\ast}_{J}
is a complete graph by Theorem
2.5
.
Hence, by Corollary
5.2
, the
a
-sequence of each
G^{\ast}_{J}
satisfies the tail-peaked condition:

a_{0}(G^{\ast}_{J})\leq\cdots\leq a_{\lfloor\frac{N}{2}\rfloor-j}(G^{\ast}_{J}).

Applying Theorem
4.5
, we conclude that

a_{0}(G)<\cdots<a_{\lfloor\frac{N-2}{2}\rfloor}(G).

Finally, Lemma
5.4
ensures that

a_{\lfloor\frac{N-2}{2}\rfloor}(G)<a_{\lfloor\frac{N}{2}\rfloor}(G),

so that (
5.6
) holds for
G
.

This completes the induction, and hence the proof.
∎

Applying Theorem
5.1
, together with Theorem

(

) and Lemma
5.5
, we deduce the following corollary.

Corollary 5.6
.

If
G
has a universal vertex, then the
a
-sequence of
G
satisfies the tail-peaked condition, and therefore is unimodal.

We turn our attention to the path graph
P_{n}
on
n
vertices.
It is known
[
8
]
that the
a
-sequence of
P_{n}
corresponds to the rows of the
Catalan triangle
(
[
21
]
A008315).
In particular, the sequence satisfies the recurrence

(5.7)

a_{i}(P_{n+1})=a_{i-1}(P_{n})+a_{i}(P_{n}),

where we set
a_{-1}(P_{n}):=0
.
The initial cases can be directly checked: the
a
-sequence of
P_{n}
is unimodal for small
n
.
We claim that the recurrence (
5.7
) preserves unimodality.
Indeed, if the sequence
(a_{0}(P_{n}),a_{1}(P_{n}),\ldots)
is unimodal, then the entrywise sum with its left-shifted version
(0,a_{0}(P_{n}),a_{1}(P_{n}),\ldots)
also yields a unimodal sequence.
Thus, by induction on
n
, the
a
-sequence of
P_{n}
is unimodal for all

.
However, the tail-peaked condition does not hold when
n\geq 16
.

We further observe that the

-sequence appears to be unimodal for all simple graphs that we have checked.
In fact, it was already conjectured in
[

]
that the
a
-sequence is unimodal for every finite simple graph.
It remains an open problem whether this unimodality holds for all finite simple graphs.

Remark 5.7
.

Given this apparent unimodality, a natural question is whether the
a
-sequence is also log-concave.
However, this does not hold in general.
For example, consider the star graph
K_{1,6}
.
It is known that the
a
-sequence of

is

(a_{0}(K_{1,6}),a_{1}(K_{1,6}),\ldots)=(1,6,40,96,\ldots).

We compute that
(a_{1}(K_{1,6}))^{2}-a_{0}(K_{1,6})a_{2}(K_{1,6})=36-40<0
, which shows that the

-sequence of

is not log-concave.

6.
Toric blow-ups and real loci

A normal algebraic variety
X
is called a
toric variety
if it contains an algebraic torus
T_{X}
as a dense open subset, and the action of
T_{X}
on itself extends to an action on
X
.
Toric varieties are determined, up to isomorphism, by their associated fans.
We briefly review some basic concepts, following
[
11
]
.

Let
N
be a free abelian group of rank
n
. Then the real vector space
N_{\mathbb{R}}:=N\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{R}
has dimension
n
. A
cone

\sigma\subseteq N_{\mathbb{R}}
generated by finitely many elements
v_{1},\dots,v_{k}\in N
is defined as

\sigma:=\cone(v_{1},\ldots,v_{k})=\left\{r_{1}v_{1}+\cdots+r_{k}v_{k}\colon r_{1},\dots,r_{k}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\right\}.

If
\sigma\cap(-\sigma)=\{0\}
, then
\sigma
is called a
strongly convex rational polyhedral cone
. When the generating set
\{v_{1},\dots,v_{k}\}
is linearly independent over
\mathbb{Q}
, the cone is said to be
simplicial
. Moreover, if these generators form part of a basis for
N
, then
\sigma
is referred to as
smooth
.
Each cone
\sigma
has naturally defined faces. A
fan

\Sigma
in
N
is a finite collection of strongly convex rational polyhedral cones in
N_{\mathbb{R}}
satisfying the following conditions:

(1)

Every face of a cone in
\Sigma
is also contained in
\Sigma
.

(2)

The intersection of any two cones in
\Sigma
is a common face of both.

The dimension of a fan
\Sigma
is the maximum dimension of its cones, which equals
n
in this setting.
If every cone in
\Sigma
is smooth (respectively, simplicial), then
\Sigma
is called a
smooth
(respectively,
simplicial
) fan. Furthermore, if the union of all cones in
\Sigma
covers the entire space
N_{\mathbb{R}}
, then
\Sigma
is said to be
complete
.

By the
fundamental theorem of toric geometry
, there is a one-to-one correspondence between the class of
n
-dimensional toric varieties
X_{\Sigma}
and the class of fans
\Sigma
in
N_{\mathbb{R}}
.
In particular,
X_{\Sigma}
is smooth (respectively, compact) if and only if
\Sigma
is smooth (respectively, complete).
There is also a one-to-one correspondence between the set of cones in
\Sigma
and the set of torus orbits in
X_{\Sigma}
, such that the sum of their dimensions equals
\dim X_{\Sigma}
.
This is known as the
orbit-cone correspondence
.

Let
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
be toric varieties associated with
\Sigma_{1}
and
\Sigma_{2}
, respectively.
A morphism
\varphi\colon X_{1}\to X_{2}
is
toric
if
\varphi(T_{X_{1}})\subseteq T_{X_{2}}
and
\varphi|_{T_{X_{1}}}
is a group homomorphism.
We assume that
\Sigma
is smooth.
Let
\tau\in\Sigma
be a cone of dimension
k
generated by the primitive vectors
v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}
, and define

u_{\tau}:=v_{1}+\cdots+v_{k}.

Consider a cone
\sigma
such that
\tau\in\sigma
,
where
\sigma
is generated by the primitive vectors
u_{1},\ldots,u_{\ell}
.
Define

\Sigma_{\sigma}(\tau)=\{\cone(u_{\tau},\{u_{j}\mid j\in A\})\colon A\subsetneq\{1,\ldots,\ell\}\}.

We define the fan
\Sigma^{\ast}(\tau)
, called the
star subdivision
relative to
\tau
, as follows:

\Sigma^{\ast}(\tau)=\{\sigma\in\Sigma\colon\tau\not\subseteq\sigma\}\cup\bigcup_{\tau\subseteq\sigma}\Sigma_{\sigma}(\tau).

One can check that
\Sigma^{\ast}(\tau)
is smooth.
The star subdivision relative to
\tau
induces a toric morphism
X_{\Sigma^{\ast}(\tau)}\to X_{\Sigma}
, which is the blow-up of
X_{\Sigma}
along the orbit closure corresponding to
\tau
.
The toric variety
X_{\Sigma^{\ast}(\tau)}
is called the
toric blow-up
of
X_{\Sigma}
along
\tau
.

Let
X
be a smooth and compact toric variety, and let
\widetilde{X}
be a toric blow-up of
X
.
It is known
[
24
]
that the
i
th Betti numbers satisfy

\beta_{i}(X;\mathbb{Q})\leq\beta_{i}(\widetilde{X};\mathbb{Q})

for all
i\geq 0
.

A toric variety
X
admits a canonical involution induced from a complex conjugation.
The fixed point set
X^{\mathbb{R}}
is called a
real toric variety
associated with
X
.
This naturally leads to the following question.

Question 6.1
.

Characterize all pairs
(X,\widetilde{X})
of smooth and compact toric varieties
X
and its toric blow-up
\widetilde{X}
such that

(6.1)

\beta_{i}(X^{\mathbb{R}};\mathbb{Q})\leq\beta_{i}(\widetilde{X}^{\mathbb{R}};\mathbb{Q})\quad\text{for all }i\geq 0.

In general, the inequality (
6.1
) does not hold.
See Example
6.2
.

Example 6.2
.

For odd integer
n
, consider the
n
-dimensional permutohedral variety
X_{A_{n}}
.
We denote by
\mathbb{C}P^{n}
the
n
-dimensional complex projective space.
The fan of
X_{A_{n}}
is obtained from the fan of
\mathbb{C}P^{n}
by applying successive star subdivisions along all cones of the fan of
\mathbb{C}P^{n}
, starting from those of dimension
n-1
down to dimension
1
.
In particular, this gives rise to a sequence of toric blow-ups

X_{A_{n}}\to\cdots\to\mathbb{C}P^{n}.

The real locus of
\mathbb{C}P^{n}
is
\mathbb{R}P^{n}
, and the real locus
X^{\mathbb{R}}_{A_{n}}
of
X_{A_{n}}
is known
[
16
,
10
]
as the real permutohedral variety.
Since
n
is an odd number, we have

\beta_{n}(\mathbb{R}P^{n};\mathbb{Q})=1\quad\text{while}\quad\beta_{n}(X^{\mathbb{R}}_{A_{n}};\mathbb{Q})=0,

which provides a counterexample to (
6.1
)

In the rest of this section, we focus on toric varieties
X_{\mathcal{B}}
associated with building set
\mathcal{B}
.
See Section
2
for relevant definitions.
Let
\mathcal{B}
be a connected building set on
[n+1]=\{1,\ldots,n+1\}
.
A subset
N\subseteq\mathcal{B}\setminus\{[n+1]\}
is called a
nested set
if it satisfies the following conditions:

(1)

For each
I,J\in N
one has either
I\subseteq J
,
J\subseteq I
, or
I\cap J=\emptyset
.

(2)

For any collection of
k\geq 2
disjoint subsets
I_{1},\ldots,I_{k}\in N
, the union
I_{1}\cup\cdots\cup I_{k}\notin\mathcal{B}
.

Let
e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}
be the standard basis vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
e_{n+1}:=-(e_{1}+\cdots+e_{n})
.
For any proper subset
I\subsetneq[n+1]
, define the vector

v_{I}=\sum_{i\in I}e_{i}.

Let
\Sigma_{\mathcal{B}}
be the fan whose cone
\sigma_{N}
is generated by the vectors
\{v_{I}\colon I\in N\}
for each nested set
N\subseteq\mathcal{B}\setminus\{[n+1]\}
.
The fan
\Sigma_{\mathcal{B}}
is called the
nested fan
of dimension
n
associated with
\mathcal{B}
, and it is known
[
25
]
to be the fan of the toric variety
X_{\mathcal{B}}
.

Now, let
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
be connected building sets on a finite set
S
.
If
\mathcal{B}\subset\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
, then there is a sequence of toric blow-ups as above:

X_{\mathcal{B}^{\prime}}\to\cdots\to X_{\mathcal{B}}.

As a refinement of Question
6.1
, we state the following.

Question 6.3
.

Characterize pairs
(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{B}^{\prime})
of connected building sets
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{B}
on a finite set
S
such that

(6.2)

\beta_{i}(X^{\mathbb{R}}_{\mathcal{B}^{\prime}})\leq\beta_{i}(X^{\mathbb{R}}_{\mathcal{B}})\quad\text{for all }i\geq 0.

Note that the permutohedral variety
X_{A_{n}}
is the toric variety associated with
the maximal connected building set
2^{[n+1]}\setminus\{\emptyset\}
on
[n+1]
,
while the projective space
\mathbb{C}P^{n}
corresponds to the minimal connected building set

\{\{1\},\{2\},\ldots,\{n+1\},[n+1]\}

.
Thus, together with Example

does not always hold.

Let
G
be a finite simple graph, and let

be an unordered pair of elements of

Consider the graphical building sets

Explicitly,

\mathcal{B}_{G+\mathbf{e}}=\mathcal{B}_{G}\cup\{I\subseteq V(G)\colon\mathbf{e}\subseteq I\text{ and }G|_{I}\text{ is connected.}\}.

Corollary 6.4
.

Let

If

\beta_{i}(X^{\mathbb{R}}_{H})\leq\beta_{i}(X^{\mathbb{R}}_{G}).
