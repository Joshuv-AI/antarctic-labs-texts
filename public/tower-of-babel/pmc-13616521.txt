Beach safety as a family affair: exploring the role of parents on young adolescents’ knowledge, bathing risk perceptions, and behaviours
Authors: Jeoffrey Dehez, Robert W Brander, Amy E Peden, Sandrine Lyser, Bruno Castelle
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBeaches are popular destinations among young adolescents despite the safety risks they may pose. This study aimed to explore how young adolescents are influenced by parents at the beach and how parental presence impacts both their risk perception and their self-reported risk-taking behaviour. We conducted a cross-sectional survey among pupils aged 11–15 years (n = 416) in a public high school in south-western France and assessed their sociodemographic characteristics, water-based recreational use, beach safety knowledge, risk perceptions, and risky behaviours. Pupils were also asked about their likelihood of entering the water outside of lifeguard-supervised areas, as well as their perception of risk, both with and without parental supervision. To analyse pupils’ responses, we estimate two cumulative link mixed models with random intercepts for individuals. Two-thirds of pupils reported bathing outside supervised areas within the last 12 months. Just over half of the pupils were able to correctly define rip currents or supervised bathing areas. Our findings highlight the crucial but ambivalent influence of parents’ behaviours as parental presence decreased the odds of declaring higher perceived risks and increased the odds of declaring higher willingness to enter the water. Older pupils declared lower perceived risks compared to younger ones, and males were more likely to report entering the water outside of supervised areas than females. Parents constitute the primary information source on water safety for young adolescents. Therefore, beach safety prevention messages should attempt to reach the entire family unit.

Introduction

Beaches are among the world’s most popular destinations for tourism and leisure (Ghermandi and Nunes 2013), offering a wide range of benefits for visitors (Britton et al. 2020). However, beaches come with inherent physical hazards associated with dynamic environmental conditions including large waves, strong currents, and variable water depths, and it is not uncommon for drowning and injury to occur (Castelle et al. 2018, Koon et al. 2021, 2023b). Among beachgoers, adolescents aged 10–19 years are one of the most at risk groups. Indeed, drowning is a leading cause of adolescent death worldwide (Peden et al. 2023). In high-income countries, drownings often occur during leisure activities in open waters, with beaches accounting for a significant proportion (Willcox-Pidgeon et al. 2017, Sakamoto et al. 2020). Within this context, there are various risk profiles depending on gender and age-specific factors. Males, in particular, consistently account for a disproportionate share of incidents and fatalities at the beach (Quan et al. 2011), a phenomenon generally attributed to factors such as lower aversion to risk and greater self-confidence in dealing with hazardous situations (Moran 2011, Willcox-Pidgeon et al. 2018). Age is another important risk factor as adolescence is a period during which adolescents’ perceptions of risk and one’s own vulnerability begin to change (Millstein and Halpern-Felsher 2001). It is therefore very common to observe an increase in drowning deaths among older adolescents (Sakamoto et al. 2020, Abihasira et al. 2023). In an effort to reduce the number of aquatic injuries and drowning deaths, supervision is often regarded as the primary preventive action (Leavy et al. 2016, WHO 2024). Such supervision may be provided by professional lifeguards or trained volunteers (Surf Life Saving Australia 2025).

Parents’ influence on preventing water-related risks among adolescents is largely overlooked, however. Typically, research on parental control has focused on the supervisory conditions for young children (Morrongiello et al. 2013, Cenderadewi et al. 2024). When it comes to adolescents, the emphasis generally shifts to studying individual knowledge, attitudes, and behaviours, especially among those aged 15 years and older (Moran 2009, Willcox-Pidgeon et al. 2018, Peden et al. 2024). In particular, no studies have examined the relationship between parental supervision and adolescent risk-taking behaviour in the context of ocean surf beaches. Parental supervision can reduce the risk among adolescents by encouraging them to voluntarily refrain from risky behaviour in a variety of situations (Li et al. 2000, Dittus et al. 2023, Ghanbari et al. 2025), but this relationship can become complicated when adolescents reach an age when they seek greater independence (Moran 2009). Moreover, very little is known about the beach safety knowledge and behaviour of young adolescents aged 11–15 years (Koon et al. 2023a). We believe that this period is important to study because it marks the beginning of an increase in the desire for autonomy and in risky behaviour (Millstein and Halpern-Felsher 2001, Daddis 2011).

In this study, we aim to address this important gap for drowning prevention by analysing parent’s influence on young adolescents’ beach safety knowledge, risk perception, and their self-reported risk-taking behaviour. We also examine the impact of gender and age on parental influence.

Method

Study area

Our analysis is based on data collected through a cross-sectional survey conducted in a public high school in south-western France. The study took place at a public high school in the municipality of Merignac located in the Bordeaux metropolitan area in south-western France (Fig. 1). The high school is located about 50 km (≈1 hour drive) from the nearest ocean beaches, which are among the most hazardous in France, if not Europe, particularly due to the presence of strong offshore-flowing rip currents (Castelle et al. 2016, 2018). According to Castelle et al. (2018), ∼40% of injuries in the surf zone within this area involve individuals aged between 10 and 20 years.

Location of the high school where the survey was conducted in regard to Western Europe (a) and the French coast (b).

The nearby ocean beaches are patrolled by lifeguards, mainly during the summer. Since 2022, lifeguard-supervised bathing areas are marked with yellow and red rectangular flags on the beach, in line with international guidelines that denote a safer and supervised bathing zone. Additionally, another coloured flag is hoisted at the lifeguard post to indicate whether bathing is dangerous or permitted. This flag comes in three colours: (i) green flag means that bathing is supervised with no particular danger; (ii) yellow-orange flag means that bathing is dangerous, but supervised; and (iii) red flag means that bathing is forbidden.

Questionnaire

The questionnaire included 25–29 questions, depending on pupils’ responses and took less than 10 min to complete. The questionnaire collected data on pupil demographics, self-reported swimming skills, beach recreational activities, beach safety knowledge, hazard identification, and risk perception. The full questionnaire is available in the Supplementary Table S1. The questionnaire was administrated in French and translated in English for publication. Data were numerical, ordinal, or categorical.

Pupils who reported visiting ocean beaches during the past 12 months were asked whether they had entered the water or not and whether they did so outside of lifeguard-supervised areas. Questions about beach safety knowledge, hazard identification, information sources, and hypothetical scenarios were presented to all pupils. Beach safety knowledge focused on three main topics: identifying supervised bathing areas, significance of the coloured flag on the flagpole at the lifeguard post, and the definition of a rip current. For all questions, pupils were asked to select the correct response from a range of options. Identification of main local hazards and information sources about local hazards were assessed using multiple-choice questions based on predesigned lists.

In order to analyse pupils’ risk perception and self-reported risk-taking behaviour in a variety of situations, two scenarios were presented in the questionnaire, each consisting of a set of two questions. The first asked, ‘Suppose you are at the beach by the ocean on a fine day and the waves are chest high. You are accompanied by friends as old as you who want to go for a swim. There is no one around and no lifeguards on duty. Your parents are not there either. What are the chances that you will go bathing?’. Five options were offered along a 5-point rating scale: ‘extremely unlikely’, ‘somewhat unlikely’,’ likely’, ‘somewhat likely’, and ‘extremely likely’. The second question asked the pupils to assess their perception of the risk of bathing in the given scenario. Again, answers were presented on a 5-point rating scale: ‘not at all risky’, ‘somewhat risky’, ‘moderately risky’, ‘very risky’, and ‘extremely risky’. The second scenario was similar with the only difference being that the pupil’s parents were nearby. The assessment of perceived bathing risk using a 5-point scale has previously been validated by Dehez et al. (2024) in an adult population and by Willcox-Pidgeon et al. (2018) in adolescents.

One teacher and a group of pupils were involved in designing the questionnaire and testing it. Involving pupils ensured that the questions were correctly interpreted by adolescents aged 11–15 years (Koon et al. 2023a). A first draft was presented to the pupil group who were invited to provide feedback. The final version was approved by the pupil group, who were then excluded from the survey to avoid biased responses. At every stage of the research, the teacher acted as a facilitator between the pupils and the researcher. The teacher was also responsible for organizing on-site administration of the survey.

Data collection

The survey collected responses between 5 May and 12 May 2025 in a dedicated classroom during school time. Pupils from every grade level from Year 6 (i.e. aged 11–12 years old) to Year 9 (i.e. aged 14–15 years old) were surveyed. Groups of about 15 pupils were formed to avoid pupils talking to each other. As it was not possible to survey all the pupils at the high school due to scheduling constraints, we used a convenience sampling method, aiming to interview as many pupils as possible. We sought to survey approximately the same number of pupils from each school year however. The surveys were administered using ODK Collect, a free and open-source field data collection software (Hartung et al. 2010). Pupils filled out questionnaires on laptops, which synchronized responses to a central database. All data were anonymous and securely stored on a password-protected network. Prior to undertaking the questionnaire, the teachers and some of the pupils involved in the survey design delivered a brief statement describing participant information including the rights of the participant.

The survey was not introduced from the perspective of safety or bathing risks so as not to create an overly anxiety-inducing atmosphere which might skew responses. Instead, the survey was introduced from the perspective of leisure activities and visiting beaches. Thereafter, completion of the questionnaire was considered as implied consent. No pupils had previously received any beach safety education within the school curriculum. No questions pertaining to personal information were included in the questionnaire to ensure it was General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) compliant regarding the collection, processing, and storage of E.U. residents’ personal data. The project received ethical approval from the INRAE Research Ethics Committee (IRB00013805/IORG0011649-CEPR39a), and the high school staff validated that these conditions were in conformity with French regulations on adolescents’ consent.

Data analysis

To enable comparisons with other international studies, we used age rather than school level. We found two missing values for gender. As the number of cases was insufficient to consider it as a separate category, we excluded these observations whenever necessary in statistical tests. The pupils’ responses regarding the number of laps they declared they would be able to swim in the pool were converted into distance groupings. Responses regarding the three beach safety knowledge questions were recoded into a dichotomous variable (incorrect answer/correct answer). Responses regarding hazard identification and hazard information sources were also recoded in a way that each modality became a dichotomous variable (no/yes). Preliminary statistical analyses consisted of frequency calculations. χ² tests and Fisher’s exact tests were used to examine the relationship between two categorical variables, Mann–Whitney U tests were used to examine the relationships between an ordinal variable (e.g. distance swum in a pool), and categorical variables (e.g. gender) and univariate regression were used to examine the relationship between a categorical variable and a continuous variable (i.e. age).

In order to investigate the relationship between adolescents’ perceived risk, self-reported risk-taking behaviour, and parental supervision, we estimated two cumulative link mixed models (logit link) with random intercepts for individuals, considering perceived risk and self-reported risk-taking behaviour (i.e. propensity to enter the water) as dependent variables, respectively. The two responses regarding risk perception (i.e. without and with parental supervision) were treated as two observations for a repeated single question. We proceeded in the same way with the two responses regarding the likelihood of entering the water. To distinguish between the two scenarios presented to the pupil, we created a specific dichotomous variable (without/with parental supervision). Gender, age, parental supervision, and the interaction effects between parental supervision and both age and gender were considered as potential predictors in both of the models. All predictors have been introduced simultaneously in the models. Age was introduced as a normalized numerical variable. All statistical analyses were conducted in the R statistical programming environment (R Core Team 2025). The two cumulative link mixed models with random intercepts for individuals were estimated with the ordinal package (Christensen 2023). We only consider statistical relationships that are statistically significant at the 0.05 level or higher.

Perceived risk and risk-taking behaviour

Finally, the parameters of the model designed to analyse pupils’ responses regarding their perceived risk and their self-reported risk-taking behaviour are summarized in Table 4. In the perceived risk models, the fixed effect of parental supervision was large and clearly negative (AOR = 0.19, 95% CI [0.13–0.29]). Consequently, parental presence decreased the odds of young adolescents declaring higher perceived risks by a factor of five. Similar conclusions hold for age as the effect was negative and moderate (AOR = 0.75, 95% CI [0.58–0.97]). The older the pupils were, the lower they perceived the risks. There were no meaningful differences in risk perception between males and females. Interaction terms between scenario (i.e. without and with parental supervision) and age had a positive effect (AOR = 1.37, 95% CI [1.05–1.79]) suggesting that the influence of parental supervision becomes weaker for older pupils. In other words, the decrease in risk perception associated with parental supervision is lower for older pupils. In contrast, younger pupils were more sensitive to parental supervision at the beach. Notably, the variance of the individual random intercept is large (σ2 = 2.95) indicating substantial unobserved heterogeneity across individuals.

Regarding the components of the model for pupil self-reported risk-taking behaviour, fixed effect associated with parental supervision was also large, but clearly positive (AOR = 8.43, 95% CI [5.47–12.99]) suggesting that pupils’ odds of declaring higher willingness to bathe increased by a factor greater than eight if pupils were informed that their parents would be at the beach with them. Both parameters associated with gender (AOR = 2.06, 95% CI [1.17–3.60]) and age (AOR = 1.82, 95% CI [1.37–2.42]) were clearly positive indicating that males and older pupils reported higher propensity to enter the water. Similar to what we observed in the previous model, only the interaction term parameter between parental supervision and age had a significantly statistical influence (AOR = 0.63, 95% CI [0.48–0.83]) suggesting that older pupils were less sensitive to the presence of their parents when it comes to entering the water as compared with younger ones. Once again, we did not identify any significant statistical influence of interaction term between parental supervision and gender. As in the previous model, the variance of the individual random intercept is large (σ2 = 4.30) indicating substantial unobserved heterogeneity across individuals.

Discussion

Parents have a vital role to play in reducing young adolescents’ vulnerability to injury. This is certainly the case in the area of beach safety, where such supervision has proven to be an effective strategy in most situations (Leavy et al. 2016). However, no research had previously been conducted into how young adolescents (i.e. those aged 11–15 years) are influenced by parents and how parental presence impacts both their risk perception and their self-reported risk-taking behaviour. To address this gap, we conducted a survey among pupils in a public school in south-western France located about an hour’s drive from high energy ocean beaches characterized by hazards such as rip currents. We found that these young adolescents were highly exposed to ocean hazards with 92.3% having visited ocean beaches at least once in the last 12 months. Of these, 97.6% reported having bathed and 68.1% reported having bathed outside lifeguard-supervised areas. We believe our findings have several important implications in beach safety, but also more generally in the areas of drowning prevention.

First, we confirm that young adolescents have only a fragmented understanding of beach safety. In our sample, pupils felt that rip currents are something to pay attention to at the beach, as they ranked them first among all potential hazards, but only 58.5% were able to correctly define them. It is worth noting that public awareness of the rip current hazard is widespread among the local adult population, largely due to the long-standing public information campaigns (Dehez and Lyser 2024) and most reported feeling ‘anxious’ or ‘very anxious’ about the hazard (Dehez et al. 2024). Given that parents and family remain the primary source of information about beach hazards for the pupils in our study, it is likely that parents have shared their concerns about rip currents to their children. A similar scenario likely applies to waves, which pupils cited as the second-most common hazard, and which adults surveyed in the same region also often identified as a major hazard (Dehez et al. 2024, 2025). As Koon et al. (2023a) pointed out in a survey carried out in New South Wales, Australia, there is a good chance that parents decide which beach and location on the beach relative to the supervised areas to go to once they arrive. By doing this, parents do not give their children the chance to question the meaning of the flags for themselves. This might help explain the incorrect identification of supervised bathing areas by a large proportion of young adolescents in our study.

Second, we observed that parental supervision had an influence on both young adolescents’ perception of risk and their intention to enter the water outside lifeguard-supervised areas. Not only does parental supervision lower the level of perceived risk among young adolescents, it also tends to increase their likelihood of entering the water in an unsupervised area. Unlike what has been observed with other adolescent behaviours (Li et al. 2000, Ghanbari et al. 2025), parental supervision might not decrease the propensity of engaging in risky behaviours at the beach. We believe that this relationship highlights a distinctive challenge for beach safety—that water activities, regardless of lifeguard presence, are perceived to be inherently enjoyable rather than risky, by both adolescents and parents (Gstaettner et al. 2017, Oliver et al. 2023). This sense of complacency may be reinforced if adolescents have previously observed their parents entering the water outside supervised areas. Indeed, such behaviour is very widespread among adults in south-western France (Dehez and Lyser 2024). By doing so, parents may unknowingly transmit risky behaviour to their children.

Third, we noted that the above relationships are influenced by the respondents’ gender and age. Overall, our findings regarding gender-related effects are consistent with those in the literature, which identify that young males often declare engaging in risky behaviour at the beach more than young females due in part to an excess of self-confidence or sensation seeking (Moran 2011, Sakamoto et al. 2020, Lawes et al. 2021). Our results also emphasized the critical influence of age on pupils’ safety at beaches as older pupils perceiving risks to be lower and being more likely to report entering the water at unsupervised beaches than younger ones. These findings are consistent with documented observations that adolescents’ risk perceptions gradually decrease and converge with those of adults as they get older (Millstein and Halpern-Felsher 2001, Blankenstein et al. 2024). The results of our regression models allow us to identify a two-fold influence of age. On the one hand, age directly affects risk perception and self-reported risk-taking behaviour, and on the other hand, age lowers the influence of parental supervision on both of these outcomes. In the models, the estimated value for the interaction terms between parental supervision and age is consistent with the literature-documented phenomenon of adolescents’ ‘desire for autonomy’ (Daddis 2011). In the present case, this induced additional effect of age may reduce the risk among older pupils who, being less sensitive to parents’ influence, would be less likely to underestimate the risk and less likely to enter the water. However, based on odds ratio values, such an impact appears to be of limited magnitude in the final decision-making process compared with other variables (e.g. fixed effect of parental presence or age). Further research is needed on this finding. Statistical differences regarding information sources were slightly pronounced with the older adolescents tending to receive beach safety information more often on TV and radio, internet and social networks, but also at school.

We are aware of the limitations of this study and how they may be addressed in future research. We acknowledge that the results can only be extended beyond this particular cohort of pupils with caution as they are not necessarily representative of the entire age group population in France or elsewhere. For this reason, it would be useful to replicate the study in other geographical areas including those further away from the ocean and in less affluent locations, given known impacts of social determinants of health and cultural norms on drowning risk (Leavy et al. 2024, Willcox-Pidgeon et al. 2025). Increasing the sample size might make it possible to identify effects that are smaller in magnitude that our current sample was too small to detect. We are also aware of traditional limitations of declarative responses that can be influenced by ‘social desirability bias’ among pupils who may have provided answers they perceived to be socially acceptable rather than fully reflecting their actual attitudes or behaviours (Zaal et al. 2026). A further limitation stems from the use of hypothetical scenarios, which allow for more refined control of the situations to which participants are exposed, but can skip the potential affective component of risk assessment that could be grasped in ‘real-world’ situations (Wolff et al. 2019). Nevertheless, involving a group of pupils in the design of the questionnaire helped to make the material more relevant to young adolescents (Koon et al. 2023a). We are also limited by the use of cross-sectional data, particularly when studying the impact of age. Longitudinal data would help to address this issue.

By highlighting the various ways in which parents influence the knowledge and behaviour of young adolescents regarding ocean-bathing hazards, we believe that our findings could have important implications for beach safety and health promotion in general. Parents’ influence comes first through their own behaviour. We have shown that the parents’ presence can act as an unintentional incentive for young adolescents to bathe outside supervised areas. This tendency can be further accentuated if parents themselves bathe at unsupervised beaches. Parents must be aware that their own supervision can hardly replace the supervision provided by professional lifeguards. Indeed, bystander rescuers, often parents, can drown whilst trying to rescue a child in the ocean (Lawes et al. 2020). Parental supervision may also be incomplete. In a study based on a sample of surfing and non-surfing beaches in New Zealand, Moran (2010) found that 29% of people observed did not simultaneously meet the supervisory criteria of attention, proximity, and continuity (Saluja et al. 2004). Among those who had failed to simultaneously meet the criteria, one-third lay on the beach sunbathing, one-quarter talked to others, and one-quarter used cell phones. Consequently, parents should encourage their young adolescents to bathe in supervised areas and act as role models by doing so themselves. Lack of vigilance may be fostered by the belief among parents that the need to supervise children decreases as they learn to swim (Morrongiello et al. 2013, 2014). In our study, it appears that parents prioritized having their children learn basic swimming skills as two-thirds of the pupils reported having taken swimming course outside of school. This misconception undermines the important difference between swimming skills and water safety knowledge (Petrass and Blitvich 2014). Therefore, young adolescents should be provided with specific swimming and water safety skills relevant to natural water environments. We also have shown that parents serve as a key source of information for young adolescents regarding ocean-bathing hazards. In this context, there is a clear need to treat adolescent beach safety as a family affair, involving both parents and adolescents equally. Parents must have access to relevant information about ocean water safety so that they can pass it to their children, particularly young males.

Conclusion

Surf beaches are popular destinations for young adolescents despite the safety risks they may pose. It is therefore crucial to ensure young adolescents can enjoy them under the safest possible conditions. Whilst parental supervision remains a critical component of drowning prevention, we show that the influence of parents must be viewed in a broader perspective. Indeed, we show that parents may unwittingly influence young adolescents to take risks through their own behaviour and that they represent an essential source of information on ocean hazards and beaches safety for them. Young adolescents’ knowledge and specific ocean-related skills still need to be improved. Measures should not only target young adolescents but should be designed to involve the whole family.
