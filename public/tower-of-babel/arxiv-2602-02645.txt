Complexity and the Hilbert space dimension of 3D gravity
Vijay Balasubramanian, Rathindra Nath Das, Johanna Erdmenger, Jonathan Karl, Herman Verlinde

Abstract
A central problem in formulating a theory of quantum gravity is to determine the size and structure of the Hilbert space of black holes. Here we use a quantum dynamical Krylov complexity approach to calculate the Hilbert space dimension of a black hole in 2+1-dimensional Anti-de Sitter space. We achieve this by obtaining the spread of an initial thermofield double state over the Krylov basis. The associated Lanczos coefficients match those for chaotic motion on the S ​ L ​ ( 2 , ℝ ) SL(2,\mathbb{R}) group. By including non-perturbative effects in the path integral, which computes coarse-grained ensemble averages, we find that the complexity saturates at late times. The saturation value is given by the exponential of the Bekenstein-Hawking entropy. Our results introduce a new way to compute the Hilbert space dimension of complex interacting systems from the saturating value of spread complexity.

Introduction—

An outstanding problem in quantum gravity is to understand the size and structure of Hilbert spaces describing different gravitating objects. One way to determine the Hilbert space dimension is to work out the number of vectors in a complete, orthonormal basis. This is the procedure used in
49
to explain the entropy of certain extremal, supersymmetric black holes in string theory. A second approach is to consider any sufficiently large collection of distinct vectors. The rank of the Gram matrix of overlaps of these vectors equals the Hilbert space dimension. This is the procedure used in
7
;
23
;
34
;
13
;
6
;
12
;
11
to account for the entropy of general black holes. The latter approach employed the Euclidean gravity path integral and its saddlepoint approximation, assumed to be well defined by some high energy completion, perhaps by a string theory. There is a third approach which applies to chaotic, or at least sufficiently ergodic, systems: to compute the dimension of the span of all states explored by a generic initial vector as it evolves over time.

We apply this third approach to asymptotically AdS
3
gravity by computing spread complexity
4
of evolving thermofield double (TFD) states describing near-extremal eternal black holes. Spread complexity is computed from the support of quantum states on the Krylov basis, constructed to span the time evolution. Our Krylov basis will be related to the generalized TFD states in
34
;
13
.
In chaotic theories, generic initial states are eventually completely delocalized in the Krylov basis
4
;
10
;
27
, so that measuring the spread reveals the dimension of the accessible part of the Hilbert space. We work with thermal states of 3d gravity with support on the entire infinite dimensional energy eigenspace, but the exponential suppression of high energies effectively restricts dynamics to a microcanonical band as in statistical mechanics.

We start with the return amplitude, i.e., the probability for a state to remain unchanged, processed into hopping amplitudes, known as Lanczos coefficients, between a chain of Krylov basis states
4

;

;
5

e^{-V(E)}=|\phi_{0}(E)|^{2}\rho(E)
specified by the reference state
\phi_{0}(E)
and microscopic spectral density
\rho(E)
.

We apply this procedure to 3d gravity in a near-extremal limit, where the spectral density
\rho(E)

The idea behind spread complexity is to quantify the spread of a wavefunction in the Hilbert space as it evolves over time

(3)

Complexity saturation from quantum gravity—

We expect spread complexity to saturate at late times at a value set by the dimension of the underlying Hilbert space. For black holes we are studying this implies that complexity should saturate at
e^{S_{0}}
. The reason why (
19
) does not saturate is that we obtained it from a continuous energy spectrum (
14
) with infinitely many states in any energy band. By contrast the full quantum gravity theory should have a discrete spectrum if the finite black hole entropy has a sensible microscopic interpretation. These considerations suggest that the computations above are only valid for time scales shorter than the inverse of the typical energy spacing
t\ll 1/\delta E\sim e^{S_{0}}
, and that to go to later times we have to find a way to resolve the discreteness of the spectrum.

The continuum spectral density in (
14
) was obtained by using the gravitational path integral (GPI), an effective field theory description of the underlying unltraviolet complete theory. By construction, the GPI only contains coarse grained information about the fundamental degrees of freedom, and is believed to compute quantities that act like averages in some underlying ensemble (see, e.g.,
46
;
36
;
24
;
14
;
47
;
7
;
26
). This suggests that the density of states (
14
) should be interpreted as an ensemble average that coarse grains a fundamentally discrete spectrum.

From this perspective, we cannot simply substitute (
14
) into the integral expression for spread complexity (
9
). Rather, if we use the GPI to compute the integrand, the
\rho(E^{\prime})\rho(E)
factor should act like a density-density correlator in the effective ensemble. Thus, if we use the GPI to directly calculate the integrand in the complexity integral, we are effectively computing

\begin{split}\overline{C_{S}(t)}&=\int dE\,dE^{\prime}\,e^{-i(E-E^{\prime})t}\,\overline{\rho(E^{\prime})\rho(E)}\\
&\times\left|\phi_{0}(E)\right|^{2}\left|\phi_{0}(E^{\prime})\right|^{2}A(E,E^{\prime})\,,\end{split}

(20)

where the overbar indicates that the quantity is computed by the GPI, and so acts effectively as an ensemble average. We also introduced the complexity kernel

A(E,E^{\prime})=\sum_{n}n\,h_{n}(E^{\prime})h_{n}^{*}(E)\,.

(21)

The fine grained structure of the spectrum is encoded in the density-density correlator
\overline{\rho(E^{\prime})\rho(E)}
.

This spectral correlator can be computed as an inverse Laplace transform of the two-boundary partition function
\overline{Z(\beta^{\prime},\beta)}
, where
\beta,\beta^{\prime}
are the periodicities of the two thermal circles. The leading path integral contribution to this partition function is disconnected
13
, resulting in the factorized form

, leading to spread complexity of the form (

24
. In the near-extremal limit discussed above (
13
) the wormhole contribution has a universal random matrix form
19
, consistent with the fact that the effective dimensionally reduced description has a known random matrix dual in the Gaussian Unitary Ensemble (GUE) universality class
37
.

Thus, the spectral correlator in (
20
) shows random matrix universality for
E\approx E^{\prime}
, so that

\begin{split}\overline{\rho(E^{\prime})\rho(E)}&\approx\rho(E^{\prime})\rho(E)-\frac{\sin^{2}(\pi\rho(\overline{E})(E^{\prime}-E))}{\pi^{2}(E^{\prime}-E)^{2}}+\dots\,.\end{split}

(22)

Here
\overline{E}=\frac{E+E^{\prime}}{2}
and “

\delta(E-E^{\prime})
, which lead to a constant offset in the spread complexity (

Summary and outlook—

We used spread complexity to study the structure of the black hole Hilbert space in 3d gravity. For generic initial states of chaotic systems with discrete, bounded spectra, the saturating value of spread complexity is related to the Hilbert space dimension. In the context of gravity, the challenge is that without a complete microscopic theory, we can only compute complexity from the density of states in an effective theory described by the gravitational path integral. As this approach results in a continuous and unbounded spectrum, we find that naively complexity grows for all times, suggesting an infinite dimensional Hilbert space. We overcome this obstacle by extracting information about the discreteness of the spectrum in the microscopic theory from the density-density correlator appearing in the spectral representation of complexity. This correlator can be computed from the effective path integral, which averages over the microscopic details of the fundamental theory. The outcome is saturation of spread complexity at a value exponential in the black hole entropy.

There is a conjecture that in holographic theories of gravity, the complexity of evolving TFD states is geometrized, possibly as the volume of distinguished spacelike slices passing through the Einstein-Rosen bridge in the gravitational description
50
;
48
. Alternative proposals suggest that complexity might be related to other diffeomeorphism invariant quantities
20
;
15
;
28
;
25
. In most of these proposals, the complexity in question was conceived of in terms of a dual field theory. But since, in holography, the field theory state and the quantum gravity state must be dual to each other, we might as well compute complexity directly in the bulk gravity if we are able to do it. The question is what notion of complexity to use. Here we looked at a measure of complexity associated to the spreading quantum state
4
that has found numerous applications. In fact, recently the authors of
33
;
44
showed that this notion of spread complexity applied to TFD states of the Double-Scaled SYK model
18
;
17
precisely reproduces the growing classical length of wormholes in the dual 2d Jackiw-Teitelboim gravity. Then by invoking RMT correlations in the Lanczos coefficients, the authors of
9
;
43
;
8
showed that the spread complexity saturates at late times, implying large quantum corrections to the classical notion of length.

In a complementary approach, the authors of
31
defined a non-perturbative notion of wormhole length directly from the path integral of a class of 2d dilaton gravity theories, including JT gravity with defects, which is a dimensionally reduced description of the near-extremal sector of 3d gravity
37
(also see
39
;
38
). The resulting expression for 2d wormhole length is very similar to our result for spread complexity (
24
) and only differs in the functional form of the
\mathcal{C}(\overline{E})
(compare (
A.31
) and (
24
) to Eqs. 3.10 and 4.6 in
31
). An explanation for this discrepancy is that the non-perturbative wormhole length in
31
is defined by summing over geodesics in all Euclidean 2d geometries with a single circular boundary, the simplest example being the disc that analytically continues to the Lorentzian black hole. If we define the non-perturbative wormhole length in a similar way for 3d gravity, we have to sum over geometries with a single torus boundary
S_{\beta}^{1}\times S^{1}
. Some of these lead to the aforementioned 2d geometries after dimensional reduction. However, there are infinitely many 3d geometries which do not have this 2d interpretation. Therefore, the 2d result of
31
will receive corrections, which should lead to our 3d formula (
24
). We leave the non-perturbative study of wormhole length in 3d gravity to future work.

\begin{split}E\,c_{n}(E)=a_{n}\,c_{n}(E)+b_{n}\,c_{n-1}(E)+b_{n+1}c_{n+1}(E)\,,\end{split}

(A.1)

with initial conditions
c_{-1}(E)=0
and
c_{0}(E)=\phi_{0}(E)
. We now prove inductively that the solution to this recursion relation takes the form (
7
), with a polynomial of degree
n

h_{n}(E)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\alpha_{nk}E^{k}\,.

(A.2)

proof:
We first show that
c_{1}(E)
takes the form (
7
), with a polynomial
h_{1}(E)
of degree one. Indeed, we find from (
A.1
)

\begin{split}c_{1}(E)&=b_{1}^{-1}(E\,c_{0}(E)-a_{n}c_{0}(E)-b_{0}c_{-1}(E))=\frac{\phi_{0}(E)}{b_{1}}(E-a_{0})\,,\end{split}

(A.3)

and consequently
h_{1}(E)=(E-a_{0})/b_{1}
. By induction, we now prove that if all
c_{n}(E)
take the form (
7
) with
h_{n}(E)
given by (
A.2
) up to a fixed finite
n\in\mathbb{N}
, then we have
c_{n+1}(E)=\phi_{0}(E)h_{n+1}(E)
with a polynomial
h_{n+1}(E)
given by (
A.2
). Indeed, we find from (
A.1
)

\begin{split}c_{n+1}(E)&=b^{-1}_{n+1}(E\,c_{n}(E)-a_{n}c_{n}(E)-b_{n}c_{n-1}(E))=\frac{\phi_{0}(E)}{b_{n+1}}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n}\alpha_{nk}E^{k+1}-a_{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\alpha_{nk}E^{k}-b_{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\alpha_{n-1,k}E^{k}\right)\\
&=\phi_{0}(E)\sum_{k=0}^{n+1}\frac{\alpha_{n,k-1}-(a_{n}\alpha_{nk}+b_{n}\alpha_{n-1,k})}{b_{n+1}}E^{k}=\phi_{0}(E)\sum_{k=0}^{n+1}\alpha_{n+1,k}E^{k}=\phi_{0}(E)h_{n+1}(E)\,,\end{split}

(A.4)

where we impose
\alpha_{nk}=0
for
k<0
and
k>n
. Thus,
c_{n+1}(E)
takes the form (
7
) and
h_{n+1}(E)
is a polynomial of degree
n+1
, with coefficients satisfying a recursion relation

\alpha_{n+1,k}=\frac{\alpha_{n,k-1}-(a_{n}\alpha_{nk}+b_{n}\alpha_{n-1,k})}{b_{n+1}}\,,

(A.5)

with initial conditions
\alpha_{00}=1
, which concludes the proof.

\square

Lanczos coefficients of the MWK density—

Here we give more details about the Lanczos coefficients which we obtain from the MWK density of states (
13
). First, we determine the MWK partition function from (
11

\;Z_{\text{MWK}}(\beta)=e^{-\beta\left|J\right|}\left[\frac{A\,e^{S_{0}}\Gamma\bigr(\frac{3}{2}\bigl)}{\beta^{3/2}}+\frac{B\,(-1)^{J}e^{S_{0}/2}\Gamma(\frac{1}{2}\bigl)}{\beta^{1/2}}\right]\,.\;

(A.14)

Thus, we obtain the return amplitude from (

S_{\text{MWK}}(t)=\frac{e^{-i\left|J\right|t}}{1+\delta}\left[\Big(\dfrac{\beta}{\beta+it}\Big)^{3/2}+\delta\cdot\Big(\dfrac{\beta}{\beta+it}\Big)^{1/2}\right]\,,

(A.15)

where we set
\delta=2\frac{B}{A}(-1)^{J}e^{-S_{0}/2}\beta

\begin{gathered}a_{0}=-\left|J\right|-\frac{1}{2\beta}\left(1+\frac{2}{1+\delta}\right)\,,\\
a_{n}=-\left|J\right|-\frac{1}{2\beta}\Biggl[(4n+1)-(2n-1)(2n)\frac{Q_{n-2}(\delta)}{Q_{n-1}(\delta)}+(2n+1)(2n+2)\frac{Q_{n-1}(\delta)}{Q_{n}(\delta)}\Biggr]\,,\quad n\geq 1\,,\end{gathered}

(A.16)

and

b_{0}=1\,,\qquad b_{n}=\frac{1}{\beta}\,\sqrt{\frac{n(2n-1)}{2}\;\frac{Q_{n-2}(\delta)\,Q_{n}(\delta)}{Q_{n-1}(\delta)^{2}}}\,,\quad n\geq 1\,,

(A.17)

where we introduce the functions

\begin{split}Q_{n}(\delta):&=2^{n+1}(n+1)!\,L_{n+1}^{(-1/2)}\left(-\frac{\delta}{2}\right)\,,\end{split}

(A.18)

for
n\geq-1
. We now show that these Lanczos coefficients approach (
17

. Therefore, we consider the asymptotic behavior of the ratio

R_{n}:=\frac{Q_{n-1}(\delta)}{Q_{n}(\delta)}=\frac{1}{2n}\left(1-\frac{\sqrt{\delta/2}}{\sqrt{n}}+\frac{\delta-1}{2n}+O(n^{-3/2})\right)\,,

(A.19)

where we use the standard asymptotics of the Laguerre polynomials. We thus find the large
n

a_{n}=-\frac{4n+3}{2\beta}-\left|J\right|\,,\quad b_{n}=\frac{n}{\beta}\,.

(A.20)

This agrees with the form of the Lanczos coefficients for a particle on the

a_{n}=\gamma(h+n)+\xi,\qquad b_{n}=\sigma\sqrt{n(2h+n-1)}\;,

(A.21)

Calculation of the spread complexity kernel—

In this appendix we give a detailed derivation of the complexity kernel (
21
) and show that it has a double pole in
\omega=E-E^{\prime}
. Therefore, we introduce the generating function

F(s;E,E^{\prime}):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}e^{-sn}h_{n}(E)h_{n}(E^{\prime})\,,

(A.22)

which is related to (
21
) by

A(E,E^{\prime})=-\partial_{s}F(s;E,E^{\prime})\Big|_{s=0^{+}}\,.

(A.23)

Note that the orthogonal polynomials in (
A.22
) are given by generalized Laguerre polynomials (
18
). Thus, we use the Hille-Hardy kernel to evaluate the generating function (
A.22
) explicitly as

\begin{split}&F(s;x,y)=\Gamma\!\big(\tfrac{3}{2}\big)\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{n!\cdot e^{-sn}}{\Gamma\!\big(n+\tfrac{3}{2}\big)}L_{n}^{(1/2)}(-x)L_{n}^{(1/2)}(-y)=\Gamma\!\big(\tfrac{3}{2}\big)\frac{\exp\left(\frac{x+y}{e^{s}-1}\right)}{\big(1-e^{-s}\big)\big(xy\,e^{-s}\big)^{1/4}}\,I_{1/2}\!\!\left(\frac{2\sqrt{xy\,e^{-s}}}{1-e^{-s}}\right)\,,\end{split}

(A.24)

where we again set
x:=\beta(E+E_{0}(J))
, and
y:=\beta(E^{\prime}+E_{0}(J))
. We set
t=e^{-s}
and evaluate the right hand side of (
A.23
) through

-\partial_{s}\log(F)=t\partial_{t}\log F=\frac{t}{1-t}-\frac{t(x+y)}{(1-t)^{2}}-\frac{1}{4}+\,\sqrt{xy}\,\frac{t^{2}+t}{(t-1)^{2}\sqrt{t}}\,\frac{I^{\prime}_{1/2}\left(\frac{2\sqrt{xy\,t}}{1-t}\right)}{I_{1/2}\left(\frac{2\sqrt{xy\,t}}{1-t}\right)}\,,

(A.25)

where we set
I^{\prime}_{1/2}(u)=\frac{d}{du}I_{1/2}(u)
. We don’t actually need the full closed form of those algebraic pieces; what we need is the singular behavior as
x\to y
. Therefore, we set
x=z+\frac{\beta\omega}{2}
, and
y=z-\frac{\beta\omega}{2}
with
\omega:=E-E^{\prime}
and
z=\frac{x+y}{2}
. Then, as
t\to 1^{-}
, i.e.,
s\to 0^{+}
,

\begin{split}\frac{2\sqrt{xyt}}{1-t}=\frac{2z}{1-t}\sqrt{1-\frac{\beta^{2}\omega^{2}}{4z^{2}}+O(\omega^{3})}\sim\ \frac{2z}{1-t}-\frac{\beta^{2}\omega^{2}}{4(1-t)z}+O(\omega^{3}).\end{split}

(A.26)

Using the closed form of the Bessel function
I_{1/2}(u)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi u}}\sinh u
and
\sinh u\sim\tfrac{1}{2}e^{u}
we find the characteristic edge exponent

\begin{split}e^{\frac{t(x+y)}{1-t}}\sinh\left(\frac{2\sqrt{xy\,t}}{1-t}\right)&\sim\,\tfrac{1}{2}\exp\!\left[\frac{\beta^{2}\omega^{2}}{4z(1-t)}\right].\end{split}

(A.27)

Hence, up to an overall smooth prefactor
G(z)
that stays finite and nonzero as
t\to 1
and
\omega\to 0
, we have

F(t;z,\omega)\ \sim\ \frac{G(z)}{\sqrt{1-t}}\exp\!\left[-\frac{\beta^{2}\omega^{2}}{4z(1-t)}\right],\quad\text{with}\quad(1-t)=1-e^{-s}\sim s.

(A.28)

From (
A.23
) we find the leading singular term of the complexity kernel (
21
), as

A(z,\omega)\ \sim\ \frac{G(z)}{(1-t)^{3/2}}\left[1+\frac{\beta^{2}\omega^{2}}{2z(1-t)}+\cdots\right]\exp\!\left[\frac{\beta^{2}\omega^{2}}{4z(1-t)}\right].

(A.29)

In the strict
s\to 0^{+}
limit, with fixed
\omega
we find that the kernel has a double pole in
\omega

A(\overline{E},\omega)\ =\ \frac{\mathcal{C}(\overline{E})}{\omega^{2}}\ +\ \text{(terms regular in }\omega)\,,

(A.30)

with a non-zero, smooth coefficient

\mathcal{C}(\overline{E})=-\frac{\pi}{8}\ \frac{\Gamma(\tfrac{3}{2})}{\beta^{2}z}\ =-\frac{\pi}{8}\ \frac{\sqrt{\pi}/2}{\beta^{3}(\overline{E}+E_{0}(J))}\ ,

(A.31)

where
z=\beta(\overline{E}+E_{0}(J))
.

Late-time behavior of spread complexity—

Here we analyze the late-time behavior of (
20

10

\overline{C_{S}(t)}=\frac{1}{Z(\beta)^{2}}\int dE\,dE^{\prime}\,e^{-i(E-E^{\prime})t}\,e^{-\beta(E+E^{\prime})}\,\overline{\rho(E^{\prime})\rho(E)}\,\frac{\mathcal{C}(\overline{E})}{(E-E^{\prime})^{2}}\,,

where

A.31

22

\hat{\omega}=\omega t

\overline{C_{S}(t)}=C_{0}+\frac{t}{Z(\beta)^{2}}\int d\overline{E}\;e^{-2\beta\bar{E}}\;\rho(\bar{E})^{2}\;\mathcal{C}(\bar{E})\int d\hat{\omega}\,e^{i\hat{\omega}}\frac{1}{\hat{\omega}^{2}}\left[1-\left(\frac{t}{\pi\rho(\bar{E})\hat{\omega}}\right)^{2}\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi\rho(\bar{E})\hat{\omega}}{t}\right)\right]\,,

where the constant term arises from contact terms in the spctral correlator and we fix it below such that

\hat{\omega}
