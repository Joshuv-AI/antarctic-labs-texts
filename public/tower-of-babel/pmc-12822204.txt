A retrospective study on the efficacy of ex vivo liver resection combined with autologous liver transplantation in treating advanced alveolar echinococcosis at high altitude
Authors: Xiao-Song Wang, Hai-Bo Zheng, Hai-Hong Zhu, Jian-Guo Feng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the Feasibility and efficacy of total ex vivo liver resection combined with autologous liver transplantation for the treatment of advanced alveolar echinococcosis in high-altitude regions.MethodsA retrospective (consecutive) analysis was conducted on 19 patients who underwent ex vivo liver resection with autologous liver transplantation at Qinghai Provincial People’s Hospital between May 2013 and December 2024. Patient data, including age, gender, ethnicity, intraoperative blood loss, operative time, and follow-up duration, were collected and subjected to descriptive statistics and comparative analysis.ResultsAll 19 patients with advanced hepatic alveolar echinococcosis (HAE) successfully underwent ex vivo liver resection with autotransplantation (ELRA) with no intraoperative mortality. Postoperative complications occurred in 84.2% (16/19) of cases. Perioperative mortality was 15.8% (3/19), including deaths due to massive intra-abdominal hemorrhage (1 case), intracranial hemorrhage (1 case), and septic shock (1 case). The median operative time was 840 min (range, 500–1277), with a median intraoperative blood loss of 2000 mL (range, 600–12000 mL). Inferior vena cava reconstruction primarily utilized synthetic graft interposition (63.2%, 12/19), while portal vein reconstruction mostly employed suture repair (89.5%, 17/19). Among 16 surviving patients, the median follow-up duration was 595 days, with no recurrence of HAE observed. Three additional deaths occurred during follow-up. ELRA demonstrated technical feasibility in high-altitude regions but requires vigilant monitoring for unique complications such as postoperative intracranial hemorrhage.ConclusionDespite the elevated surgical risks imposed by the unique high-altitude environment, total ex vivo liver resection combined with autologous liver transplantation demonstrates feasibility and potential clinical value as a treatment option for advanced hepatic alveolar echinococcosis (HAE) in plateau regions. This technique significantly improves patients’ quality of life and long-term prognosis, demonstrating strong feasibility and clinical efficacy. It thus represents a valuable new treatment option for patients with end-stage HAE.Keywords: Plateau area, Total ex vivo hepatectomy, Autologous liver transplantation, Hepatic alveolar echinococcosis, Retrospective analysis

Introduction

Hepatic alveolar echinococcosis (HAE) is a zoonotic parasitic disease caused by the larval stage of Echinococcus multilocularis; 95% of HAE lesions localize to the liver. Due to its tumor-like invasive growth, alveolar echinococcosis is termed “parasitic cancer“ [1–3] —a label coined by Piarroux et al. (2011) to describe its aggressive features (local invasion, metastasis, poor prognosis), despite not being a true malignancy. Untreated or inadequately treated, more than 90% of HAE patients have been reported to die within 10 years after the onset of clinical symptoms, and almost 100% of patients will die within 15 years [4]. Surgery is the preferred treatment for HAE. Radical resection combined with albendazole has been the historical cornerstone of HAE treatment. Early diagnosis is crucial for reducing the incidence of unresectable lesions [5–7]. However, due to its strong concealment, it often does not cause clinical symptoms in the early and middle stages (5–10 years), and the opportunity for surgical resection is frequently lost when it is discovered [8, 9]. Western China is the most prevalent area of HAE [10, 11], particularly in Qinghai Province, located in the northeastern part of the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau, which is a high-incidence area of HAE. Due to the limitations of education level, religious belief, economic condition, and medical level, most HAE patients we receive are in the terminal stage when they visit, and they have lost the chance of radical surgical resection [12, 13]. Liver transplantation used to be the only cure for end-stage HAE, especially when hydatid lesions invaded the hepatic vein and inferior vena cava. However, due to the high cost of surgical treatment, donor shortage, and lifelong immunosuppressive medication, the use of this surgical method is discouraged [14, 15]. Ex vivo liver resection and autotransplantation (ELRA) has filled this gap in recent years. Professor Wen Hao and his team from Xinjiang Medical University successfully performed the first case of ELRA for intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma in China in 2006. They used autologous liver transplantation to treat advanced HAE in 2010 [16, 17]. Autologous liver transplantation can not only effectively remove the diseased tissue but also retain part of the healthy liver tissue to provide strong support for the recovery of liver function. This technique requires neither organ donors nor postoperative immunosuppressants and has low early postoperative mortality and acceptable morbidity. However, due to the high requirements of this technique, the selection of patients and the evaluation of remnant liver volume (RLV) are critical [18]. The World Health Organization estimated that 91% of the annual new AE cases worldwide originated from the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau in China [19], and AE in China accounted for 90% of the global disease burden [20]. The prevalence of human echinococcosis in Qinghai Province was 1.02%, and the detection rate of patients with alveolar echinococcosis accounted for 42.99% [21]. The high-altitude environment of the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau presents unique challenges, including chronic hypoxia, limited healthcare resources, and delayed diagnosis of HAE. These factors contribute to a higher proportion of advanced-stage cases, making ELRA a crucial option for patients unsuitable for conventional resection or allogeneic liver transplantation. Located within this region, Qinghai Provincial People’s Hospital therefore finds ELRA to be a promising strategy for managing such patients in its clinical practice.

Materials and methods

Clinical data

The clinical data of 19 patients with advanced hepatic echinococcosis who underwent ex vivo liver resection combined with autologous liver transplantation at Qinghai Provincial People’s Hospital from March 2013 to December 2024 were collected, including age, gender, ethnicity, preoperative examination results, and operation time. All patients had a history of living in pastoral areas or close contact with cattle and sheep. All patients were positive for hepatic hydatid IgG antibodies (enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay) before the operation, and the routine pathological examination after the operation revealed HAE. Inclusion Criteria: Patients were enrolled if they were diagnosed with advanced HAE based on clinical symptoms, serological testing (positive for hepatic hydatid IgG antibody), and postoperative pathology, and had no other severe comorbidities such as significant cardiopulmonary dysfunction (left ventricular ejection fraction < 50%, FEV1 < 60% of predicted value) or liver failure (Child-Pugh class C). All patients underwent total ex vivo liver resection combined with autologous liver transplantation. Exclusion Criteria: Patients were excluded if they had extensive extrahepatic metastasis (e.g., to the lungs, brain, or bones), concurrent other malignancies (e.g., hepatocellular carcinoma, colorectal cancer), had undergone major surgery or radiotherapy/chemotherapy within the last 3 months, or had an estimated future liver remnant volume of less than 40%. Strict inclusion and exclusion criteria were used to ensure the homogeneity of the study subjects, thereby improving the reliability and validity of the study results.

Between March 2013 and December 2024, a total of 87 patients with advanced hepatic alveolar echinococcosis (HAE) were admitted. Among them, 19 patients met the inclusion criteria for ex vivo liver resection and autotransplantation (ELRA), while 42 received palliative therapy (albendazole alone). A total of 26 patients were excluded, including 18 due to an insufficient future liver remnant (FLR) volume of < 40% and eight due to irreversible cardiopulmonary disease.

Indications and contraindications of ELRA surgery

Indications: Extensive infiltration of hepatic alveolar echinococcosis (HAE) involving the hepatic-vena cava confluence area and at least two central veins. Portal vein/hepatic artery reconstruction should be performed if the third-level branches of the portal vein or hepatic artery are involved. A functional liver graft volume of ≥ 40% of the estimated standard liver volume (ESLV) was required, with the ESLV calculated using the Urata formula: ESLV (mL) = 706.2 × BSA (m²) + 2.4 [14, 22]; Percutaneous transhepatic biliary drainage (PTCD) was performed in patients with obstructive jaundice [23] (Six patients presented with obstructive jaundice and underwent percutaneous transhepatic cholangial drainage (PTCD) 2 to 4 weeks before surgery). Contraindications: liver decompensation, Child-Pugh grade C, Irreversible cardiopulmonary diseases, or unreconstructable major intrahepatic vascular invasion, and complicated with uncontrolled systemic infection or multiple organ failure. These factors may result in a significant increase in surgical risk and, therefore, require a thorough evaluation before surgery. This included assessing the degree of vascular invasion via contrast-enhanced CT or MRI, particularly involvement of the retrohepatic inferior vena cava, hepatic veins, and portal vein branches. ELRA was considered when the lesion’s proximity to major vascular structures rendered conventional in-situ resection unsafe.

All patients underwent preoperative liver biopsy to exclude cirrhosis (F4 fibrosis). Liver function of the future remnant was assessed by the indocyanine green retention rate at 15min (ICG-R15), with all patients demonstrating a rate below 15%. Our institution employs a dual-modality protocol for assessing future liver remnant (FLR) volume, combining preoperative imaging with intraoperative verification. Preoperatively, the FLR volume and estimated standard liver volume (ESLV) are calculated using enhanced CT with three-dimensional reconstruction to ensure an FLR/ESLV ratio of ≥ 40%. Intraoperatively, following lesion resection, the actual weight of the remnant liver is measured by the water displacement method, which is then converted to volume based on the patient’s body surface area to confirm that the FLR/ESLV ratio meets the required threshold.

Surgical methods

After anesthesia was adequate, the patient was placed in the supine position, the skin of the abdominal operation field was disinfected, and a sterile surgical towel was laid. A “Mercedes-Benz” incision was employed. Specifically, a bilateral subcostal arciform incision was made in the upper abdomen (extending to the right midaxillary line and the left anterior axillary line), connected by a vertical midline incision inferior to the xiphoid process to achieve the characteristic configuration. The abdominal cavity was entered through a standard layered laparotomy. Hemostasis was meticulously achieved with electrocautery. Upon entry, adhesions were sharply and bluntly dissected using an electrocautery knife and an ultrasonic scalpel. Finally, a sterile wound protector was placed. The abdominal cavity and hydatid lesions were thoroughly explored, the falciform ligament, coronary ligament, and left and right deltoid ligament were severed, and the liver was fully dissociated. The space between the dorsal side of the inferior vena cava and the retroperitoneum was dissociated, and the liver was fully dissociated. The surgical procedure proceeded with the systematic dissection of the three hepatic hilum. First, the first hepatic porta (the hilum containing the portal vein, hepatic artery, and bile duct) was dissected to mobilize the left and right hepatic arteries, the central portal vein, and the common bile duct. Subsequently, the second hepatic porta (the confluence of the hepatic veins and the inferior vena cava, IVC) was approached, where the suprahepatic IVC was sufficiently mobilized and pre-looped with a blocking tape. Finally, the third hepatic porta (the short hepatic veins draining directly into the retrohepatic IVC) was addressed; the caval ligament and short hepatic veins were carefully divided and ligated, achieving full mobilization of the retrohepatic IVC. To isolate the adhesion of liver lesions to other tissues, the superior hepatic vein, inferior vena cava, retrohepatic inferior vena cava, portal vein, hepatic artery, and common bile duct were thoroughly dissected. An 8-mm polytetrafluoroethylene(PTFE)graft was used to establish a passive venovenous bypass between the retrohepatic inferior vena cava and the portal vein, utilizing a passive shunt technique. The isolated liver was placed in a liver repair basin covered with sterile plastic film to isolate ice. A catheter was inserted through the portal vein, and about 3000 ml of University of Wisconsin solution (UW) at 4 °C was continuously perfused until the hepatic vein outflow was clear. During the continuous lavage, another group of surgeons sharp resection of the lesion along the boundary of normal liver tissue, assess the degree of invasion of inferior vena cava and hepatic vein, retain the undisturbed liver parenchyma and main pipeline, mild invasion can be repaired with autologous round ligament patch, if severe invasion can be reconstructed with allogeneic hepatic vein transplantation or artificial blood vessel outflow tract. After liver repair, the repaired remnant liver was placed into the abdominal cavity in situ, and the liver was cooled with sterile ice scraps around the liver (the liver temperature was maintained at 4–8 °C) and protected with gauze. Before anastomosis, intravenous heparin sodium (1 mg/kg) was administered to maintain the activated clotting time (ACT) between 250 and 300 s. The ends of the vessels to be anastomosed were irrigated with a pulsed jet of heparinized saline. Continuous end-to-end anastomosis of the suprahepatic inferior vena cava, infrahepatic inferior vena cava, and retrohepatic inferior vena cava was then performed using 5 − 0 Prolene suture. The branches of the portal vein and the main trunk of the portal vein were anastomosed end-to-end with 6 − 0 Prolene thread (anterior wall semi-purse-string suture to prevent stenosis), and the left and right hepatic artery and proper hepatic artery were anastomosed end-to-end with 8 − 0 Prolene thread by microsurgery. At the end of the anastomosis, a large amount of warm saline was used for rewarming, and methylprednisolone (500 mg) was given intravenously. The suprahepatic inferior vena cava and portal vein were opened successively. After the circulation was opened, the liver sections were immediately examined for active hemostasis using two-stage electrocoagulation, and the bile leakage was checked. Whether choledochojejunostomy should be performed depends on the degree of invasion and repair of the common bile duct. The liver section was covered with hemostatic gauze and gelatin sponge, and hemostatic powder was sprayed. After checking the gauze and instruments, one anti-adhesion rinse solution was placed into the abdominal cavity to prevent postoperative intestinal adhesion, and the abdomen was finally closed layer by layer (Fig. 1).

Postoperative management

All patients were transferred to the ICU for observation after the operation, and given liver protection, anti-infection, rehydration, maintenance of blood volume and water, electrolyte, internal environment stability, and other treatment measures. Liver and kidney function, blood routine, coagulation, and other indicators of patients were regularly reviewed. When the patient’s condition was stable, he was transferred to the intensive care unit, and other complications, such as fever, postoperative pain, and postoperative bleeding, were treated in time. For patients undergoing artificial vascular replacement, low molecular weight heparin sodium (e.g., enoxaparin 4000 IU, once every 12 h) was injected subcutaneously once active abdominal bleeding was excluded after the operation, and the dose was adjusted according to the INR value of the patient. The patients were switched to oral warfarin on postoperative day 3 to 5, which lasted for at least 3 months, and the INR was maintained at 2.0–3.0 [24]. All patients received regular oral albendazole (15 mg/kg/day) for 2 years [25] (The 2-year treatment course was selected based on our center’s experience with HAE patients undergoing radical resection, aiming to balance efficacy and safety, prevent recurrence from microscopic residual disease, and avoid hepatotoxicity associated with long-term drug use). All discharged patients were followed up every 3 to 6 months, and the size and quality of residual liver, vascular status, recurrence, and metastasis were evaluated by liver function tests, serological tests, ultrasound, CT, etc.

Indicators of observation

After total ex vivo liver resection combined with autologous liver transplantation in the plateau area, the detection indicators of postoperative recovery are significant. These indicators usually include liver function indicators (such as ALT, AST, bilirubin, etc.), coagulation function, infection indicators, and the physiological state of the patient (such as blood pressure, heart rate, etc.). In the early postoperative period, the liver function of patients may be affected to some extent, especially in the plateau environment, and the insufficient oxygen supply may increase the metabolic burden of the liver. Therefore, timely monitoring of these indicators is helpful to evaluate the postoperative recovery and provide a basis for further treatment. Ethics approval and consent to participate

This retrospective study was reviewed and approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Qinghai Provincial People’s Hospital, Approval No. (2023)−110. Informed consent was obtained from all participants, and the study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

General information

A total of 19 patients were enrolled, including 10 males (53%) and nine females (47%). The age ranged from 19 to 71 years, with a median age of 38 years (mean 39.26 ± 14.27 years). The ethnic distribution was Tibetan 14 cases (73.7%) and Han 5 cases (26.3%). The American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification of all patients was grade 1, and only one patient (5.3%) had mild liver disease. The median body mass index (BMI) was 21 kg/m2 (16.8–25 kg/m2). The total liver volume was 969.75–1296.86.75.86 CC, with a median of 1121.73 CC. Details of the characteristics of the patients at baseline are provided in Table 2. Although all patients underwent Child-Pugh scoring to assess hepatic functional reserve, this did not necessarily indicate the presence of cirrhosis. Furthermore, there was no clinical or radiological evidence of portal hypertension in any patient. Intraoperative exploration and postoperative histopathology confirmed that the hepatic parenchyma was non-cirrhotic in all cases. The etiology of liver dysfunction was attributable to extensive HAE infiltration, not chronic liver disease.

Surgical situations

ELRA was successfully performed in all 19 patients with advanced HAE without intraoperative death. The median operative time was 840 min (range, 500–1277 min). The liver cold ischemia time ranged from 82 to 550 min, with a median of 325 min. The median intraoperative blood loss was 2000 ml (range, 600 − 12 000 ml). Intraoperative blood transfusion: red blood cell suspension 2–60 U, median 5 U; The median fresh frozen plasma was 1280 ml (range 0–4300 ml). Methods of vascular reconstruction:. The methods for inferior vena cava (IVC) reconstruction were as follows: direct suture repair in 1 case (5.3%), autologous ligamentum teres hepatis patch in 4 cases (21.1%), preservation of the native retrohepatic IVC in 2 cases (10.5%), and prosthetic graft interposition in 12 cases (63.2%). The choice of reconstructive technique was dictated by the extent of IVC involvement: prosthetic grafts were used for segmental defects, autologous ligamentum teres was utilized for partial defects, and direct repair was performed for minor injuries. Among the methods of portal vein reconstruction, 17 cases (89.5%) were repaired with suture, 1 case (5.3%) was repaired with a patch, and 1 case (5.3%) was repaired with synthetic material. The hepatic veins of 19 patients were all repaired (replanted) in vitro. Detailed patient surgical data are presented in Table 4.

Prognosis and complications of patients

Length of stay

The total length of hospital stay of the 19 patients was 23–112 days, with a median of 46 days. The length of ICU stay was 0.4–3.4 days, with a median of 1 day.

Perioperative outcomes

Among the 19 patients, 3 patients (15.8%) died during the perioperative period, and the remaining 16 patients (84.2%) were successfully transferred to the general ward. Among the 3 patients who died during the perioperative period, 1 patient suffered from abdominal hemorrhage 2 h after the operation, and was transferred to the ICU for emergency treatment. Finally, the patient died of multiple organ failure due to hemorrhagic shock, secondary disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), and died 38 h after the operation; One patient’s condition worsened 1 day after operation, and the reexamination of CT showed right parietal cerebral hemorrhage, subarachnoid hemorrhage, and sinusitis, and died 2 days after operation because of right parietal cerebral hemorrhage and subarachnoid hemorrhage; Another patient developed bile leakage 48 h after operation, followed by bilateral lung infection and abdominal abscess formation. Despite multidisciplinary treatment, the patient still developed septic shock induced by progressive sepsis, complicated with hepatic encephalopathy and multiple organ dysfunction syndrome, and finally died on the 29th day after the operation. The risk of perioperative mortality may be closely associated with Child-Pugh class B, intraoperative blood loss exceeding 3000 mL, operative time longer than 900 min, and major postoperative complications.

Outcomes after discharge and postoperative complications

Postoperative complications occurred in 16 patients (84.2%). There were 9 cases of pleural effusion (47.4%), 5 cases of peritoneal effusion (26.3%), 6 cases of bile leakage (31.6%), 2 cases of biliary stricture (10.5%), 2 cases of postoperative bleeding (10.5%), 1 case of gastrointestinal bleeding (5.3%), 1 case of cerebral hemorrhage (5.3%), and 1 case of abdominal wall degestion (5.3%). Follow-up

Follow-up using outpatient examination, telephone interview, and other methods was conducted until December 31, 2024. All 16 surviving patients were followed up for 1 to 4232 days. All patients were given long-term oral albendazole tablets for anti-hydatid treatment after discharge. Liver and kidney function, upper abdominal enhanced CT, and magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) were regularly detected. No recurrence of hepatic echinococcosis was found, and 3 new deaths occurred during the follow-up period; three patients succumbed to liver failure.

Discussion

Advantages of ELRA in the treatment of advanced HAE

As an emerging surgical technique, ELRA has shown significant advantages in the treatment of advanced HAE. HAE is a chronic and progressive parasitic disease caused by Echinococcus multilocularis. The lesions of HAE are extensive and often involve the main blood vessels and bile ducts of the liver, which makes it difficult to perform traditional radical resection [14]. In patients with advanced HAE, liver lesions are usually extensively infiltrated and involve essential structures such as the hepatic vein, portal vein, and inferior vena cava, making surgical resection extremely difficult [26]. ELRA technology provides an effective treatment option for such patients by ex vivo resection of diseased tissue and reconstruction of blood vessels and bile ducts [27]. The main advantage of ELRA is that it can achieve radical resection of the lesion tissue, while avoiding the problems of donor shortage, the use of immunosuppressants, and postoperative infection recurrence in liver transplantation [28]. In this study, all 19 patients with advanced HAE were successfully treated with ELRA. The 1-year survival rate was as high as 89.5%, and the 3-year survival rate showed a good prognostic trend, although the statistics were not complete. These findings indicate that ELRA represents a highly viable and effective treatment option for advanced HAE. Compared to palliative treatment (which has a 0–5 year recurrence rate of 37% [29]) and allogeneic transplantation (which carries a high recurrence risk and requires lifelong immunosuppression [30, 31]), no recurrence was observed during follow-up in this series of patients undergoing ELRA, highlighting its curative potential. However, the study is limited by the lack of a control group, and prospective trials are necessary to confirm further the superiority of ELRA in reducing recurrence rates. In addition, ELRA surgery provides support for the recovery of liver function and reduces the risk of postoperative liver failure by preserving part of the healthy liver tissue [16]. ELRA surgery offers a new treatment option for patients, especially in the face of limitations of existing drugs and traditional surgical methods. These findings provide a new perspective on our understanding of the clinical management of HAE and also highlight the importance of adopting innovative surgical techniques in the treatment of liver diseases. In terms of vascular reconstruction, ELRA technology allows surgeons to perform fine manipulation of the liver without blood perfusion, to achieve precise repair or reconstruction of the involved blood vessels. In this study, synthetic materials were mainly used for inferior vena cava reconstruction (63.2%), and suture repair was mainly used for portal vein reconstruction (89.5%), which showed that this technique had high flexibility and success rate in vascular reconstruction [32, 33]. By extracorporeal repair, the occurrence of postoperative vascular complications can be minimized, and the long-term survival rate of patients can be further improved. ELRA is indicated for patients with end-stage HAE where the lesion involves the hepatic hilum and retrohepatic inferior vena cava, thereby rendering in-situ resection unsafe. Although HAE is a benign disease, its invasive growth pattern mimics that of a malignancy, and the prognosis is exceedingly poor without radical resection. ELRA offers a potentially curative option without the need for lifelong immunosuppression, which represents a significant advantage in resource-limited high-altitude regions.

Comparison with existing studies and the particularity of high altitude areas

Although ELRA has shown significant advantages in the treatment of advanced HAE, its application at high altitude still faces special challenges. The high-altitude environment, characterized by chronic hypoxia, presents significant anesthetic challenges, including difficulty in maintaining intraoperative oxygen saturation (requiring pure oxygen inhalation to sustain SpO₂ >95%) and an increased risk of cerebral edema. In this study, the fatal cerebral hemorrhage observed in one patient was likely attributable to high-altitude hypoxia, which can increase cerebral blood flow and vascular permeability, potentially leading to vessel rupture. Furthermore, the physiological status and postoperative recovery of these patients differ from those in low-altitude regions. The intraoperative fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO₂) was maintained at 80–100% to counteract chronic hypoxia. Arterial oxygen saturation (SpO₂) was continuously monitored and kept above 95%. In this study, the incidence of postoperative complications was 84.2%, mainly including pleural effusion, peritoneal effusion, bile leakage, etc. These complications may be related to surgical trauma, postoperative infection, and the underlying diseases of the patients. In particular, the occurrence of bile leakage may lead to delayed recovery of patients and increase the risk of re-hospitalization [34]. Therefore, postoperative monitoring and management of patients should be strengthened, and complications should be found and treated in time to improve the quality of postoperative recovery of patients. Under high altitude hypoxia, the regenerative capacity of the liver is significantly affected. A hypoxic environment may lead to hypoxic injury of hepatocytes and aggravate the metabolic burden of the liver, thereby affecting the liver regeneration ability, delaying the recovery of liver function after surgery, and increasing the risk of postoperative complications [35–37]. Hypoxia can suppress immune function and increase the risk of postoperative infections. In this study, the biliary leakage rate reached 31.6%, and hypoxia-induced immunosuppression may have accelerated the progression of disease. Additionally, hypoxia may lead to platelet dysfunction, increasing the risk of postoperative hemorrhage. One case of major postoperative intra-abdominal bleeding in this study may be associated with this mechanism. Compared with studies in plain areas, HAE patients at high altitude tend to present with more severe conditions, mainly due to limited local medical resources, poor patient awareness of the disease, and inconvenient transportation. In this study, all patients had a history of living in pastoral areas or close contact with cattle and sheep, and most patients were at an advanced stage at the time of diagnosis. They had lost the opportunity for radical resection. Furthermore, the unique high-altitude environment imposes heightened demands on intra- and postoperative management. The two cases of postoperative hemorrhage in this study may be linked to intraoperative ACT levels exceeding 300 s. This suggests that more precise monitoring of anticoagulation therapy is required in high-altitude settings. Postoperative patients need closer monitoring, especially in terms of liver function, coagulation function, and infection indicators [38]. In the present study, AST and ALT levels were significantly increased in patients within 1 day after surgery, suggesting that liver function was affected to some extent. This fluctuation of liver function may be more evident at high altitude, and timely treatment measures such as liver protection and anti-infection should be taken to promote the recovery of patients.

Limitations of the study

Although this study has achieved some results in assessing the Feasibility and efficacy of ELRA in the treatment of advanced HAE, there are still some limitations. First, the small sample size of only 19 patients may limit the statistical power and generalizability of the findings. Second, the relatively short follow-up period prevents a comprehensive assessment of long-term outcomes for some patients. Additionally, as a single-center retrospective study involving patients exclusively from high-altitude pastoral areas in Qinghai Province, it is susceptible to potential selection and information biases, which could compromise the validity and reliability of the results. Furthermore, the absence of a control group necessitates validation through future multicenter prospective studies.

In terms of the statistics of complications, this study only recorded complications during the postoperative hospital stay, and the data on complications and long-term survival status after discharge were not complete. This may lead to an underestimation of the incidence and severity of postoperative complications. Future studies are needed to further improve the follow-up mechanism and collect more comprehensive postoperative data to more accurately evaluate the long-term efficacy and safety of ELRA.

Future research directions

Future studies should focus on addressing the limitations present in this study and further exploring the potential of ELRA technology in the treatment of advanced HAE. Firstly, multicenter, prospective studies are recommended to expand the sample size and improve the reliability of the study results. Multi-center cooperation can better evaluate the application effect of ELRA in different regions and different medical levels, and provide a stronger basis for clinical practice. The establishment of a multi-center database of plateau AE will provide us with a valuable resource to facilitate the collection and analysis of larger clinical data and a better understanding of the effects of different treatment strategies on liver function recovery. By collecting data from other centers, we can identify the best regimen and guide clinical practice.

Second, future studies should focus on the postoperative management and rehabilitation of HAE patients at high altitude. The effects of a hypoxic environment on postoperative recovery of patients need to be further studied, including oxygen therapy, nutritional support, and rehabilitation training. In addition, the establishment of a long-term follow-up mechanism is essential to evaluate the long-term efficacy of ELRA. Through regular follow-up, postoperative recurrence, chronic complications, and other problems can be found in time, and corresponding intervention measures can be taken.

Finally, as medical technology continues to advance, ELRA technology itself continues to evolve. Future studies could explore new vascular reconstruction materials, cryopreservation techniques, and minimally invasive surgical methods further to improve the Feasibility and effectiveness of the procedure. At the same time, combined with molecular biology and imaging techniques, in-depth research on early diagnosis and individualized treatment of HAE is expected to provide more accurate treatment for patients.

In conclusion, ELRA is an effective treatment that can be used in patients with advanced HAE at high altitude. However, the successful implementation of the procedure relies on rigorous case screening and optimized surgical techniques. Postoperative monitoring and multidisciplinary management are essential to improve the long-term prognosis of patients. Future studies should further optimize surgical techniques, improve postoperative management, explore individualized treatment options for AE patients in plateau areas, and establish a multi-center database to accumulate more clinical data to promote progress in this field and improve the survival rate and quality of life of patients.
