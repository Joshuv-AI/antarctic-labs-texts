M-CSF priming enhances the efficiency and reproducibility of hiPSC-derived osteoclast differentiation and pharmacological responsiveness to anti-resorptive agents
Authors: Jujin Jeong, Yun Ju Choi, Yujin Sa, Narae Kang, Don-Kyu Kim, Tae-Hoon Lee, Sang-Wook Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Human induced pluripotent stem cell (hiPSC)-derived osteoclasts offer a potentially scalable platform for studying osteoclast biology and anti-resorptive pharmacology. However, differentiation efficiency and inter-line reproducibility remain limited. To address this limitation, we investigated whether cytokine priming during the monocyte-to-macrophage transition is an important determinant of efficient and reproducible osteoclast differentiation from hiPSCs across diverse genetic backgrounds. Monocytes derived from three independent hiPSC lines were primed briefly with M-CSF, GM-CSF, or no cytokine, followed by osteoclast differentiation with M-CSF plus RANKL. Among these conditions, M-CSF priming reproducibly increased osteoclast differentiation efficiency compared with GM-CSF priming or no priming, yielding > 70% CD51/CD61⁺ cells across hiPSC lines derived from distinct tissue sources. Osteoclast maturation and function were then assessed by multinucleation, TRAP staining, and bone resorption assays, which further showed that M-CSF priming enhanced osteoclast maturation and resorptive function across the tested hiPSC lines. We also performed extracellular flux-based measurements of OCR/ECAR and ATP production rates to examine metabolic changes associated with osteoclast maturation. This analysis showed that an early rise in OXPHOS-derived ATP production preceded peak induction of mature osteoclast genes, suggesting that early oxidative metabolism may support subsequent fusion and functional maturation. Finally, we assessed the pharmacological responsiveness of hiPSC-derived osteoclasts using two clinically used bisphosphonates, alendronate and zoledronate, both of which elicited dose-dependent anti-osteoclastic effects. Collectively, these results indicate that brief M-CSF priming enhances the efficiency and inter-line reproducibility of hiPSC-derived osteoclast differentiation, while early oxidative metabolism temporally precedes osteoclast gene induction. This improved reproducibility and functional maturation may allow hiPSC-derived osteoclasts to support mechanistic studies of osteoclastogenesis and the evaluation of pharmacological responses to osteoclast-targeted agents.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-52457-z.

Introduction

Osteoclasts are specialized multinucleated giant cells derived from monocyte/macrophage precursors1. They resorb bone by secreting protons (H⁺), chloride ions, cathepsin K, and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) at the bone surface, thereby degrading the mineralized matrix and organic components2. This bone-resorbing activity is essential for skeletal homeostasis and is tightly coupled to osteoblast-mediated bone formation. Disruption of this balance can contribute to a range of skeletal disorders, including osteoporosis, metastatic bone disease, and chronic inflammatory conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis3. Consistent with the central role of osteoclasts in these disorders, major anti-resorptive therapies, including bisphosphonates and denosumab, act primarily by suppressing osteoclast activity and reducing bone resorption4.

Experimental studies of osteoclast biology and anti-resorptive agents have relied largely on primary human cell models, including human bone marrow cells and human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (hPBMCs) containing CD14⁺ monocyte/macrophage precursors5–8. Although these systems recapitulate many features of osteoclast differentiation and function, they are constrained by donor-to-donor variability, limited expandability, and practical and ethical limitations associated with primary human samples9,10. To overcome these limitations, human induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSCs) offer a renewable, genetically modifiable platform that is amenable to disease modeling. Previous studies have shown that hiPSCs can be differentiated into osteoclasts through stepwise cytokine-based protocols designed to mimic developmental and hematopoietic cues in vitro11–13. In addition, patient-specific and gene-edited hiPSC lines provide opportunities to model osteoclast-related genetic disorders and to perform isogenic comparisons for mechanistic studies and therapeutic testing14–16.

Despite these advances, several issues continue to limit the broader use of hiPSC-derived osteoclast models. In particular, the efficiency and inter-line reproducibility of osteoclast differentiation across genetically distinct hiPSC lines have not been systematically evaluated. Another variable that may influence differentiation is the functional state of monocyte/macrophage-lineage precursor cells at the time of osteoclastogenic induction. M-CSF and GM-CSF are well-known regulators of monocyte/macrophage differentiation and have been shown to promote distinct macrophage programs through their respective receptors and downstream signaling pathways17–19. Across experimental monocyte/macrophage systems, M-CSF generally supports macrophage survival and polarization toward homeostatic or tissue-repair-associated states, whereas GM-CSF more commonly promotes inflammatory activation programs with distinct cytokine profiles17,18,20. This distinction may be relevant to osteoclastogenesis because osteoclasts arise from monocyte/macrophage-lineage precursors, and their commitment, survival, and maturation are regulated by coordinated M-CSF- and RANKL-mediated signals1,21,22. In line with this concept, previous studies in murine macrophage models and primary cell systems have suggested that the activation state of macrophage-like precursors may influence subsequent osteoclast formation and resorptive function23–25. However, whether similar precursor state-dependent effects are preserved in hiPSC-derived monocyte systems remains unclear. In addition, recent studies suggest that metabolic rewiring contributes importantly to osteoclast development and function26,27, yet the metabolic features of hiPSC-derived osteoclast differentiation remain incompletely understood.

In this study, we investigated whether intermediate cues during the monocyte-to-macrophage transition influence downstream osteoclastogenic capacity and thereby affect differentiation efficiency across multiple hiPSC lines. We also performed metabolic profiling to define bioenergetic changes during hiPSC-derived osteoclast differentiation. In addition, we examined the response of the optimized hiPSC-derived osteoclast system to clinically used anti-resorptive agents.

Materials and methods

Maintenance of human induced pluripotent stem cell lines

Three human induced pluripotent stem cell lines (hFSiPS3-1, hUSiPS2 and CMC003) were obtained from the National Stem Cell Bank of Korea28–30. hiPSCs were maintained in mTeSR™1 medium (STEMCELL Technologies, Cat#85850) in Matrigel-coated (Corning, Cat#354277) 6-well plates (Corning, Cat#3516) within a normoxic incubator (37 °C, 5% CO2). When the cultures reached 80–90% confluency, cells were detached using ReLeSR™ (STEMCELL Technologies, Cat#100–0483) and passaged at a 1:20 ratio in fresh mTeSR™1 medium.

Monocyte differentiation

hiPSCs were differentiated into monocytes using a previously reported method16. When undifferentiated hiPSCs reached approximately 80–90% confluency, they were detached using Accutase (Invitrogen™, Cat#00-4555-56) and centrifuged at 126×g for 3 min. The resulting cell pellet was resuspended in mTeSR™1 medium (STEMCELL Technologies, Cat#85850) for cell counting. A total of 25,000 cells were seeded per well in 96-well round-bottom ultra-low attachment plates (Corning, Cat#7007) in mTeSR™1 medium supplemented with 50 ng/mL human bone morphogenetic protein 4 (BMP4; PeproTech, Cat#120-05ET)), 50 ng/mL human vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF; PeproTech, Cat#100−20), 20 ng/mL human stem cell factor (SCF; PeproTech, Cat#300-07), and 20 µM Y-27632 (STEMCELL Technologies, Cat#72308). The plates were centrifuged at 126×g for 10 min and incubated in a hypoxic incubator (5% O₂, 5% CO₂, 37 °C). Half of the culture medium was replaced on days 1 and 2. On day 4, eight embryoid bodies (EBs) were transferred per well to Matrigel-coated 12-well plates (Corning, Cat#3512), each receiving 1 mL of differentiation medium composed of X-VIVO™ 15 (Lonza, Cat#04-418Q), 2 mM GlutaMAX™ (Gibco, Cat#35050061), 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P/S; GenDEPOT, Cat#CA005-010), 25 ng/mL human interleukin-3 (IL-3; PeproTech, Cat#200-03), and 100 ng/mL human macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF;PeproTech, Cat#300−25). Every 2 days, suspended hiPSC-derived monocytes were harvested by centrifugation (126 ×g for 3 min) and returned to the original plates in 1 mL of fresh differentiation medium to sustain continuous production.

Osteoclast differentiation

Between 2-4 weeks after attachment of hiPSC-derived EBs, floating hiPSC-derived monocytes were collected and filtered through a 40 μm cell strainer (SPL, Cat#93040) and centrifuged at 126×g for 3 min to pellet. The cells were then resuspended in X-VIVO™ 15 medium for cell counting. A total of 50,000 cells/cm² were seeded into cell culture plates under priming conditions consisting of X-VIVO™ 15 medium supplemented with 2 mM GlutaMAX™ and 1% P/S, and treated with either no cytokine (untreated), 100 ng/mL M-CSF, or 100 ng/mL human granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF; PeproTech, Cat# 300-03). After 3 days, the cells were switched to osteoclast differentiation medium, which comprised αMEM (Gibco, Cat#12561-056), 10% FBS (Gibco, Cat#16000044), 1% P/S, 50 ng/mL M-CSF, and 100 ng/mL human soluble receptor activator of nuclear factor-κB ligand (RANKL; PeproTech, Cat#310–01C). The differentiation was maintained for 6–10 days, with the specific duration optimized for each hiPSC line: 10 days for hFSiPS3-1, and 6 days for both CMC003 and hUSiPS2. Half of the culture medium was replaced with fresh differentiation medium every 2 days.

Flow cytometry

A single-cell suspension of hiPSC-derived monocytes or osteoclasts was filtered through a 40 μm cell strainer and subsequently collected. The collected cells were washed twice with FACS buffer (PBS; Biosesang, Cat#PR2007-100-00 with 1% FBS) and stained at 4 °C for 30 min using specific surface markers. After staining, the cells were washed twice with FACS buffer and analyzed. The antibodies used for flow cytometry are listed in Supplemental Table 1. Fluorescence was measured using a flow cytometer (Navios EX, Beckman Coulter or BD FACSLyric, BD Biosciences) and analyzed via FlowJo software Version 10.8.1.

Phagocytosis assay

hiPSC-derived monocytes were primed with no cytokine, M-CSF, or GM-CSF for 3 days prior to the phagocytosis assay. Following priming, cells were incubated with Alexa Fluor™ 488-conjugated Zymosan A particles (Invitrogen, Cat#Z23373) at a final concentration of 0.5 mg/mL for 1 h at 37 °C. After incubation, cells were washed three times with 1× PBS to remove non-internalized particles. Samples were subsequently imaged using a fluorescence microscope. The phagocytic activity was quantified by measuring the total fluorescence area (µm2) of internalized particles using ImageJ software (version 1.54P).

Quantitative RT-PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted using TRI Reagent® (MRC, Cat#TR118). Subsequently, cDNA synthesis was performed using the PrimeScript™ RT Reagent kit (TaKaRa, Cat#RR037A) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The primers used for qRT-PCR are listed in Supplementary Table 2. In this study, Quantitative RT-PCR (qRT-PCR) was conducted using the QuantStudio 3 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) with TOPreal SYBR Green qPCR PreMIX (Enzynomics, Cat#RT500M). Gene expression levels were normalized to the reference genes HPRT1. All analyses were performed in triplicate using a minimum of three biological replicates. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−∆∆Ct method.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

To induce macrophage polarization, hiPSC-derived monocytes were treated for 3 days with no cytokine (Mock), 100ng/mL GM-CSF, or 100ng/mL M-CSF to generate unpolarized, M1-like, or M2-like macrophages, respectively. Following the 3-day induction, the culture supernatants were collected and analyzed. The concentrations of secreted cytokines were quantified using ELISA kits (human IL-6; BioLegend, Cat#430504, human IL-10; BioLegend, Cat#430604) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader, and the results were calculated based on a standard curve generated for each assay.

Tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) staining

hiPSC-derived osteoclasts were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA; Bio-Solution, Cat#BP031) at room temperature for 15 min. After washing with 1×PBS, Tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) staining was performed using the TRAP Staining kit (Cosmo Bio USA, Cat#PMC-AK04F) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. Stained cells were visualized and imaged using a bright-field microscope. TRAP-positive multinucleated cells (containing three or more nuclei) were identified as mature osteoclasts.

Immunostaining

For F-actin ring visualization, hiPSC-derived osteoclasts were fixed with 4% PFA at room temperature for 15 min. Unless otherwise specified, all subsequent procedures were performed at room temperature, and the cells were washed five times for 5 min each with PBS-T (0.1% Tween20; Sigma-Aldrich, Cat#P2287-500ML in PBS) between each step. Permeabilization was performed using permeabilization buffer (0.1% Triton X-100; in PBS) at room temperature for 15 min. Blocking was performed using a blocking buffer (5% normal donkey serum; Abcam, Cat# ab7475 in PBS-T) at room temperature for 1 h. Subsequently, samples were stained with Alexa Fluor™ 488 Phalloidin (Invitrogen, Cat#A12379) at 4 °C overnight. Nuclei were stained with DAPI at room temperature for 15 min and observed under a fluorescence microscope.

Bone resorption assay

Bone resorption activity was evaluated using a Bone Resorption Assay kit (Cosmo Bio, Cat# CSR-BRA-48KIT). Briefly, 0.25 mL of the fluorescein-labeled calcium phosphate coating solution was added to each bone resorption assay 48-well plate well and incubated in the dark at 37 °C for 1 h. After incubation, the plate was washed twice with 0.5 mL of 1×PBS. Subsequently, 25,000 cells/cm² were seeded in 0.5 mL of induction medium supplemented with no cytokine, 100 ng/mL of GM-CSF, or M-CSF per well. After 3 days, the medium was replaced with an osteoclast differentiation medium supplementing 50ng/ml of M-CSF and 100ng/ml of RANKL, and the cells were cultured for an additional 6–10 days (optimized for each cell line). On day 10, to visualize the resorbed areas (pits), the cells were removed by treating the plate with a 5% sodium hypochlorite solution (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat#239305) for 3 min. This was followed by washing with distilled water (DW) and drying. Finally, the resorbed pit areas were imaged using a bright-field microscope, and the pit area was quantified using ImageJ software (version 1.54P).

Seahorse real-time cell metabolic analysis

Oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) were measured using a Seahorse XFe96 Extracellular Flux Analyzer (Agilent) to obtain real-time bioenergetic profiles for each cell type. Cells were seeded in Seahorse XFe96/XF Pro Cell Culture Microplates (Agilent, Cat#103792-100) at a density of 20,000 cells per well and primed with 100ng/mL hM-CSF for 3 days. Subsequently, the cells were differentiated for up to 10 days (hFSiPS3-1) or 6 days (CMC003 and hUSiPS2) with 50 ng/mL M-CSF and 100 ng/mL RANKL in osteoclast differentiation medium Extracellular flux data were normalized by equal initial seeding density (20,000 cells/well) across all conditions. On the measurement day, the cells were washed three times with Seahorse XF DMEM Base Medium (Agilent, Cat#103575-100), and the medium was replaced with fresh XF DMEM Base Medium. Mitochondrial stress assays were performed by sequentially adding 2 µM oligomycin (ATP synthesis inhibitor; Sigma-Aldrich, Cat#495455), 1 µM carbonyl cyanide 4-(trifluoromethoxy) phenylhydrazone (FCCP, mitochondrial uncoupler; Sigma-Aldrich, Cat#C2920), and 0.5 µM rotenone (complex I inhibitor; Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# 557368) and antimycin A (complex III inhibitor; Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# A8674). The analytical results were validated using Wave XFe Analyzer software, Wave Desktop 2.6, and dependent variables such as basal respiration and ATP production were calculated according to the white paper provided by Agilent.

Measurement of cell viability

hiPSC-derived monocytes were seeded in 96-well plates (Costar, Cat# 3596) at a density of 20,000 cells per well. Cells were primed with 100 ng/mL M-CSF for 3 days prior to treatment with bisphosphonates (alendronate or zoledronate). After priming, cells were treated with alendronate at final concentrations of 0, 1, 10, 100, 1000, and 10,000 nM, or zoledronate at final concentrations of 0, 37.5, 75, 150, 300, and 600 nM for up to 10 days (hFSiPS3-1) or 6 days (CMC003 and hUSiPS2). Cell viability was measured using the EZ-Cytox (DoGenBio, Cat# EZ-3000) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, after drug treatment, the culture medium was replaced with fresh medium supplemented with EZ-Cytox solution, and the plates were incubated at 37 °C in 5% CO₂ for 30 min. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. All measurements were performed in triplicate using independent biological samples.

Statistics

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). The choice of statistical tests was based on the number of groups compared and the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance. Group comparisons were analyzed using one-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for all pairwise comparisons or Dunnett’s post hoc test for comparisons against a single control group. Two-tailed Student’s t-tests (paired or unpaired, as appropriate) were used for two-group comparisons. Error bars represent the mean ± standard deviation (SD), and n indicates the number of independent biological replicates. For image-based quantifications in Fig. 3B and C, and 3E, n = 5 indicates five randomly selected microscopic fields analyzed per condition. The number of biological replicates per group is indicated in the figure legends. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05 (*p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001; ****p < 0.0001; ns, non-significant).

Results

Robust generation of monocytes from hiPSC lines of diverse tissue origins

As an initial step for osteoclast differentiation, hiPSCs were differentiated into monocytes using a previously established protocol, given that osteoclasts are derived from the monocyte/macrophage lineage (Fig. 1A)16. To assess the applicability of this protocol across different hiPSC lines, we applied it to three independently generated hiPSC lines reprogrammed by Sendai virus-mediated delivery of reprogramming factors from dermal fibroblasts (hFSiPS3-1), urine-derived cells (hUSiPS2), and bone marrow cells (CMC003)28–30. Approximately two weeks after initiating differentiation, monocytes emerged from the attached EBs and were observed floating in the culture medium (Fig. 1B). Over four weeks following the attachment of hiPSC-derived EBs to culture dishes, the number of floating monocytes increased to more than 10 million cells, with a cell viability exceeding 80% (Fig. 1C and D). To examine whether the floating cells exhibited monocyte-like phenotypes, the expression of monocyte surface markers was analyzed by flow cytometry. Across all three tested hiPSC lines (hFSiPS3-1, hUSiPS2, and CMC003), more than 90% of the cells were found to express CD45 (a pan-hematopoietic marker), as well as monocyte markers CD14, CD16, and CD11b (Fig. 1E). These results demonstrate that monocytes can be robustly and reproducibly produced from hiPSC lines irrespective of tissue of origin.

Brief M-CSF priming enhances functional maturation and resorptive activity of hiPSC-derived osteoclasts

After deriving monocytes from multiple hiPSC lines, we first evaluated whether short-term cytokine priming exhibits the expected macrophage-like functions prior to downstream osteoclast differentiation. Following a three-day priming period, hiPSC-derived monocytes transitioned into distinct macrophage-like populations with varying functional capacities. Compared to the baseline phagocytic activity observed in the no cytokine primed group (Mock), the M-CSF-primed group exhibited the most prominent phagocytic activity among all experimental groups (Supplementary Fig. 1A, B). To further characterize the phenotypic identity of the primed cells, cytokine secretion profiles were assessed by ELISA. Consistent with the established effects of these cytokines on macrophage polarization, M-CSF-primed cells secreted relatively higher levels of IL-10, whereas GM-CSF-primed cells produced higher levels of IL-6 (Supplementary Fig. 1C). Together, these data indicate that the priming conditions elicited the expected macrophage-like functional and phenotypic states prior to osteoclast induction. These observations align with well-established macrophage polarization paradigms reported in previous studies17,31, providing a verified cellular foundation for analyzing their downstream osteoclastogenic potential.

Next, to evaluate how these priming states affect downstream osteoclastogenesis, we compared osteoclast differentiation efficiency of hiPSC-derived monocytes across the no cytokine, M-CSF, and GM-CSF primed groups. In multiple hiPSC lines, osteoclasts differentiated from M-CSF-primed monocytes exhibited higher expression levels of osteoclast-specific genes, such as CTSK (cathepsin K; matrix degradation), MMP9 (matrix metalloproteinase 9; extracellular matrix remodeling), CA2 (carbonic anhydrase II; proton generation for resorption lacuna acidification), and NFATC1 (nuclear factor of activated T cells 1; a master transcription factor for osteoclastogenesis) (Fig. 2A). In addition, because integrin αvβ3 (CD51/CD61) is markedly upregulated during osteoclast differentiation and concentrates at the sealing zone, where it mediates tight adhesion to the bone surface and supports actin ring formation required for bone resorption32, we quantified CD51/CD61 surface expression by flow cytometry after M-CSF or GM-CSF treatment to assess osteoclast differentiation efficiency. Using this approach, we found that across three independent hiPSC lines, CD51/CD61 expression was consistently higher in cultures derived from M-CSF-primed monocytes (~ 70%) than in those derived from GM-CSF-primed (~ 50%) or unprimed monocytes (~ 40%) (Fig. 2B). We next assessed functional differences between osteoclasts generated after GM-CSF versus M-CSF priming. F-actin ring staining analysis revealed that GM-CSF–primed cultures contained more multinucleated osteoclasts than M-CSF–primed cultures (Fig. 3A upper panel, B). In contrast, M-CSF-primed cultures displayed a larger osteoclast area and a higher number of nuclei per osteoclast (Fig. 3A upper panel, C, D). Notably, while the nuclei count in GM-CSF-primed osteoclasts was relatively constrained (mostly below five nuclei per cell), the nuclei count in M-CSF-primed osteoclasts varied widely, with some cells containing up to 40 or more nuclei. These results indicate that M-CSF priming enhances hiPSC-derived osteoclast fusion and functional maturation. Consistent with the F-actin ring staining data, TRAP activity assays showed that M-CSF-primed osteoclasts exhibited a significantly larger TRAP-positive area than GM-CSF-primed osteoclasts (Fig. 3A, middle panel, E). Moreover, bone resorption assays demonstrated that M-CSF-primed osteoclasts generated a significantly larger resorptive area compared to those derived from GM-CSF priming (Fig. 3A lower panel, F). These pro-osteoclastogenic effects of M-CSF priming were reproduced across distinct hiPSC lines, CMC003 and hUSiPS2 (Supplementary Fig. 2). Taken together, these data indicate that M-CSF priming is more effective than GM-CSF priming in promoting the functional maturation and bone-resorptive activity of hiPSC-derived osteoclasts.

Early increases in OXPHOS-derived ATP production precede osteoclast gene induction during osteoclast differentiation

Mature osteoclasts are highly metabolically active cells, secreting substantial protons and proteolytic enzymes to degrade the bone matrix. Several studies have highlighted significant metabolic changes during osteoclast differentiation and maturation, with glucose metabolism serving as a key pathway in supporting both differentiation and functionality26,33,34. However, the temporal relationship between OXPHOS-derived ATP production and osteoclast gene induction during hiPSC-derived osteoclastogenesis remains insufficiently defined. Having confirmed that M-CSF priming significantly enhances the functional maturation of hiPSC-derived osteoclasts, we reasoned that this group would be well suited for investigating the metabolic requirements associated with osteoclast maturation. To address this, we performed time-course analysis in the hFSiPS3-1 cell line, combining qRT-PCR analysis of osteoclast-related genes and extracellular flux assays during osteoclast differentiation (Fig. 4). Time-course qRT-PCR showed that osteoclast-associated genes critical for maturation and resorptive function were robustly upregulated from day 6 (Fig. 4A). In contrast, extracellular flux analysis revealed that both the oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) increased as early as day 2, peaked at day 4, and then modestly declined by day 10 (Fig. 4B). This temporal pattern was reproducible in additional hiPSC lines (CMC003 and hUSiPS2), in which OCR and ECAR similarly peaked on day 4, preceding the peak expression of most osteoclast genes at day 6 (Supplementary Fig. 3A, 3B, 4A, 4B). Importantly, ATP rate analysis revealed that glycolytic ATP production showed only modest changes from day 2, whereas OXPHOS-derived ATP production increased rapidly during the early phase of differentiation (day 2–4) (Fig. 4C; Supplementary Figs. 3C, 4C). Consistent with this increase in mitochondrial ATP production, OCR-linked parameters, including basal and maximal respiration, proton leak, spare respiratory capacity, and non-mitochondrial oxygen consumption, were elevated at these early time points (Supplementary Fig. 5). Together, these data show that OXPHOS-derived ATP production increases early during differentiation, preceding substantial osteoclast gene upregulation, and suggest that early enhancement of mitochondrial bioenergetic capacity may support subsequent osteoclast maturation and resorptive function.

Pharmacological validation of hiPSC-derived osteoclasts with clinically used bisphosphonates

To confirm the pharmacological relevance of the hiPSC-derived osteoclast (hiPSC-OC) differentiation system, we evaluated the anti-osteoclastic efficacy of two clinically used bisphosphonates, alendronate and zoledronate. When hFSiPS3-1 hiPSC-derived macrophages were treated with both compounds during osteoclast differentiation, TRAP activity decreased in a dose-dependent manner. Specifically, treatment with alendronate led to a marked decrease in TRAP activity starting from low nanomolar concentrations. For instance, at 1 nM, TRAP activity was reduced by approximately 50% compared to the control and was almost completely abolished at 10,000 nM. Similarly, zoledronate showed a progressive reduction in osteoclast activity across the tested range (37.5–600 nM), with significant inhibition observed from 150 nM (Fig. 5). To distinguish the specific anti-osteoclastogenic effects of these drugs from potential non-specific cytotoxicity, we normalized the TRAP activity against the corresponding cell viability index (Fig. 5C, F). The reduction in TRAP activity was independent of cytotoxicity, as significant inhibition was observed even at concentrations where cell viability remained unaffected. These findings suggest that the anti-osteoclastic effects are not solely attributable to reduced cell survival but are also consistent with inhibition of the osteoclast differentiation program. Notably, similar drug responses were reproduced in hiPSC-OCs derived from distinct genetic backgrounds (Supplementary Fig. 6). Collectively, these results indicate that hiPSC-OCs display dose-dependent pharmacological responsiveness to two clinically used bisphosphonates, with inhibitory effects that are independent of cytotoxicity.

Discussion

Studies of bone cell function and preclinical assessment of bone-resorptive pathways have relied heavily on rodent model systems. However, interspecies differences in bone remodeling dynamics may limit direct translation to human bone biology and disease mechanisms35,36. Furthermore, FDA Modernization Act 2.0 and recent FDA roadmaps promoting validated new approach methodologies underscore the need for human-relevant in vitro platforms for bone disease research, including osteoclast-targeted therapies37. In this context, hiPSCs offer a scalable and genetically diverse system for modeling human osteoclast biology across distinct genetic backgrounds. Recent studies have shown that functional osteoclasts can be generated not only from healthy hiPSC lines but also from patient-derived hiPSCs carrying rare genetic bone disorders, in which disease-associated osteoclast phenotypes are reproduced13,15,16,38. Nevertheless, inter-line variability and suboptimal differentiation efficiency remain important limitations of hiPSC-derived osteoclast models.

Here, we found that short-term M-CSF priming, compared with GM-CSF priming or no priming, was consistently associated with enhanced osteoclast maturation and resorptive activity across distinct hiPSC backgrounds. This finding is consistent with the established roles of M-CSF and GM-CSF in promoting distinct macrophage-like precursor states with different implications for osteoclastogenesis. Previous studies in macrophage and primary precursor systems have shown that precursor activation state can influence downstream osteoclast formation and function25,39–42. Our data extend these observations to hiPSC-derived monocyte systems and suggest that M-CSF priming provides a more favorable precursor state for downstream osteoclast fusion and functional maturation.

Parallel to this functional maturation, our metabolic profiling revealed bioenergetic changes during hiPSC-derived osteoclast differentiation that align with reports from primary human osteoclast models (Fig. 4, Supplementary Figs. 3, 4)33,34,43,44. Since osteoclasts are metabolically active cells that degrade the extracellular bone matrix by secreting large amounts of protons and proteolytic enzymes, understanding their bioenergetics may provide therapeutic opportunities for anti-resorptive treatments45. Indeed, studies using primary osteoclasts and extracellular flux analyses have shown that osteoclast maturation involves a coordinated regulation of mitochondrial respiration and glycolytic activity, with dynamic changes in OCR/ECAR and ATP production34. Moreover, osteoclast multinucleation has been linked to enhanced oxidative metabolism, supporting the idea that increased mitochondrial respiratory capacity can be associated with functional maturation44. Mechanistically, RANKL signaling has been reported to promote mitochondrial biogenesis and upregulate genes involved in the TCA cycle and oxidative phosphorylation, thereby reinforcing the central role of mitochondrial bioenergetics in supporting the osteoclastogenic program26,46. In this context, our observed early rise in OXPHOS-derived ATP production suggests the early establishment of mitochondrial bioenergetic capacity, which likely serves as a metabolic foundation to support subsequent osteoclast maturation and resorptive function.

Finally, we assessed the pharmacological responsiveness of hiPSC-derived osteoclasts using two representative bisphosphonates, alendronate and zoledronate. Treatment of osteoclast-differentiated cells derived from multiple independent hiPSC lines resulted in dose-dependent reductions in TRAP activity, while higher concentrations also decreased cell viability (Fig. 5, Supplementary Fig. 6). These responses are consistent with the established mechanism of bisphosphonates, which act through inhibition of farnesyl pyrophosphate synthase in the mevalonate pathway47–49. Although direct benchmarking against PBMC-derived osteoclasts or other established human osteoclast models was not performed, the bisphosphonate response observed in our hiPSC-derived osteoclasts was broadly concordant with prior findings from primary human osteoclast models, including PBMC-derived systems, where bisphosphonates suppress osteoclast formation and bone resorptive activity6,50,51. Collectively, these results support the pharmacological responsiveness and functional relevance of the hiPSC-derived osteoclast model, while direct comparison with PBMC-derived osteoclasts will be needed in future studies.

Conclusions

In summary, short-term M-CSF priming improved the reproducibility and functional maturation of hiPSC-derived osteoclasts across genetically diverse lines. Our findings also suggest that an early shift toward oxidative phosphorylation precedes osteoclast gene induction. By enhancing reproducibility and functional maturation, this hiPSC-derived osteoclast platform may provide a scalable human in vitro system for studies of osteoclast biology and osteoclast-focused pharmacological evaluation.
