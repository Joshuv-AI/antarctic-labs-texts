Assessment of Cerebral Perfusion Asymmetry in Chronic Migraine Patients Using Intravoxel Incoherent Motion (IVIM)–Derived Pseudodiffusion Coefficient and Perfusion Fraction
Authors: Yunus Emre Senturk, Ahmet Peker, Sabahattin Yuzkan, Huseyin Ekin Ergin, Eser Bulus, Mohammed Salman Shazeeb
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTChronic migraine (CM) is associated with neurobiological alterations beyond episodic attacks, yet cerebral microvascular perfusion changes during the interictal phase of CM remain underexplored. Intravoxel incoherent motion (IVIM) magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) provides diffusion‐derived pseudoperfusion parameters that reflect regional microvascular alterations in the brain parenchyma. Accordingly, this study aimed to investigate right‐to‐left hemispheric asymmetry of IVIM‐derived perfusion parameters in migraine‐implicated cortical and subcortical regions during the interictal phase of CM. Thirty patients with CM imaged during the interictal phase, along with 30 age‐ and sex‐matched healthy controls, underwent IVIM imaging. IVIM parameters, including the pseudodiffusion coefficient (D*), perfusion fraction (f), and their composite metric (fD*), were estimated using a Bayesian probability‐based fitting algorithm. Region‐of‐interest (ROI) analysis was performed bilaterally in migraine‐implicated cortical and subcortical regions. Hemispheric asymmetry indices (HAIs) of IVIM parameters were calculated and compared between groups using Benjamini–Hochberg (FDR) false discovery rate correction. No absolute IVIM parameter showed significant interhemispheric differences in either group after FDR correction. In addition, no significant group differences in HAI of IVIM parameters were identified across the six cortical regions or pontine tegmentum. In contrast, posterior thalamic fD* asymmetry was significantly greater in patients with CM than in controls (median HAI: 27.91 vs. 10.43; p = 0.002, q = 0.017). Exploratory analysis confirmed greater separation between bilateral posterior thalamic fD* measurements in CM versus control subjects (0.429 vs. 0.151 × 10−3 mm2/s; p = 0.002), whereas leave‐one‐out analysis showed that this outcome was not driven by any single participant. Interobserver agreement for HAI measurements was good for D* (ICC = 0.81) and moderate for f and fD* (ICC = 0.70 and 0.69, respectively). These findings indicate that CM is associated with increased hemispheric asymmetry of IVIM‐derived microvascular perfusion in the posterior thalamus during interictal phase, suggesting lateralized thalamic microvascular alterations in CM.Keywords: headache chronic migraine, hemispheric asymmetry index (HAI), intravoxel incoherent motion (IVIM), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)

Introduction

Chronic migraine (CM) is a disabling condition that affects 1%–2% of people globally, characterized by the presence of headache for more than 15 days per month or meeting the International Classification of Headache Disorders, 3rd edition (ICHD‐3) criteria for migraine for more than 8 days per month [1, 2]. CM is more frequently associated with comorbid conditions than episodic migraine, including sleep disturbances, anxiety, and allodynia [3]. Unlike episodic migraine, CM may be characterized by persistent neurobiological changes. Resting‐state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs‐fMRI) connectivity studies have demonstrated altered brain networks in migraine patients compared with healthy controls, highlighting the role of ongoing symptoms and central sensitization during the interictal phase, especially in CM [4].

Migraine is clinically characterized by recurrent unilateral or side‐predominant headache, sometimes accompanied by lateralized sensory symptoms. Repeated activation of the trigeminovascular system in conjunction with cortical spreading depression is postulated to initially propagate within a single hemisphere before spreading bilaterally. Regional engagement of these mechanisms during the early phase of migraine attacks is associated with vasodilation during the ictal period, followed by subsequent vasoconstriction [5]. These pathophysiological processes have prompted neuroimaging studies to investigate regional perfusion differences during the peri‐ictal state [6, 7]. Although several studies reported asymmetric cortical network activation during the pain‐free interictal phase, it remains unclear whether a persistent hemispheric difference of cerebral regional perfusion exists during the interictal phase of CM [8, 9].

Noncontrast perfusion imaging techniques, such as arterial spin labeling (ASL), have attracted increasing attention in migraine research. In this context, intravoxel incoherent motion diffusion‐weighted imaging (IVIM‐DWI) has emerged as a promising noncontrast MRI technique capable of distinguishing true tissue diffusion of water molecules from microvascular perfusion, which was originally proposed by Le Bihan et al. [10]. The IVIM concept is hypothesized to estimate perfusion in tissue via incoherent motion of water molecules within random capillaries, formerly reported as a pseudodiffusion effect at low diffusion‐sensitizing gradients (b‐values typically between 0 and 200 s/mm2), which is not concurrently observed in the higher b‐values in addition to normal diffusion signal decay [11]. IVIM‐derived parameters, including the pseudodiffusion coefficient (D*), perfusion fraction (f), and their composite metric (fD*), may provide an opportunity to assess tissue‐level quantification of microvascular perfusion while simultaneously characterizing true molecular diffusion of water.

Recent rs‐fMRI studies have also demonstrated differences between both cerebral hemispheres in terms of functional abnormalities during the symptom‐free interictal period, indicating that migraine lateralization extends beyond transient ictal activation. In particular, QuYang et al. reported altered interhemispheric connectivity involving the salience, visual, default mode, sensorimotor, and limbic networks, together with distinct biological pathways related to oxidative stress and energy metabolism in left‐sided migraine and synaptic plasticity in right‐sided migraine [12]. These findings suggest that migraine lateralization may reflect an underlying neurobiological organization rather than merely the clinical side of headache. However, whether these functional hemispheric abnormalities are accompanied by hemispheric alterations in cerebral microvascular perfusion remains unclear.

Although CM is diagnosed clinically according to the ICHD‐3 criteria, brain MRI is frequently performed in patients with recurrent migraine‐like headaches to exclude intracranial structural abnormalities that may mimic migraine. In this setting, IVIM‐DWI can be readily incorporated into a brain MRI examination, which may provide complementary information on cerebral microvascular perfusion. Rather than serving as a diagnostic tool for migraine, IVIM may provide additional perfusion information that complements functional abnormalities previously demonstrated by rs‐fMRI [9, 12]. Accordingly, the present study aimed to investigate hemispheric differences in IVIM‐derived microvascular perfusion within migraine‐related cortical and subcortical regions during the interictal phase of CM.

Material and Method

Ethics Information

This single‐center retrospective study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Institutional review board approval was obtained from the local ethics committee (IRB no: 2025.623.IRB2.281). The requirement for written informed consent was waived by the ethics committee.

Study Population

This single‐center retrospective study was conducted between January 2023 and November 2025 and included patients presenting to the outpatient clinic with chronic headache. Among these patients, the diagnosis of CM was established in accordance with the ICHD‐3. CM was diagnosed according to the ICHD‐3 criteria as migraine‐like or tension‐type‐like headache occurring on ≥ 15 days per month for more than 3 months, with migraine features on ≥ 8 days per month. Patients were required to have an established history of migraine with or without aura, and the headache could not be better accounted for by another ICHD‐3 diagnosis [13].

Brain MRI with IVIM‐DWI was performed to rule out secondary intracranial pathology or any emergent causes of chronic headache for each case. The inclusion criteria comprised patients with CM who underwent brain MRI with IVIM‐DWI during the interictal phase, defined as the absence of headache, aura, or other migraine‐related symptoms at the time of imaging, and with at least 48 h having elapsed since the most recent migraine attack. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (i) brain MRI with IVIM‐DWI performed during an active migraine attack, defined by the presence of headache, aura, or other migraine‐related symptoms at the time of imaging or within 48 h after such symptoms; (ii) nonmigraine type primary chronic headache disorders, such as trigeminal autonomic cephalalgias, or cluster headache; (iii) secondary headache disorders, including those attributed to head trauma, vascular disorders, substance or medication withdrawal, intracranial tumors, intracranial hypotension or hypertension, or pathology of the paranasal sinuses or aerodigestive tract; (iv) age younger than 18 years; (v) presence of incidental intracranial imaging abnormalities, regardless of clinical relevance to headache, including demyelinating lesions, acute or chronic cerebral infarction, vascular malformations, tumors, or equivalent structural abnormalities on brain MRI; (vi) presence of major concurrent systemic disorders, such as diabetes mellitus, hypertension, vasculitis, or renal failure; and (vii) inadequate image quality due to significant motion artifacts, geometric distortion, poor signal‐to‐noise ratio, or incomplete IVIM‐DWI or fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) sequences.

During the study period, 177 patients with chronic headaches underwent brain MRI with IVIM‐DWI to evaluate for potential secondary causes. Among these, 25 patients had experienced an active headache within the preceding 48 h, as documented on the MRI request forms. Of the remaining 152 patients who underwent brain MRI with IVIM‐DWI during a headache‐free period, only 30 fulfilled the diagnostic criteria for CM in the interictal phase. Thirty age‐ and sex‐matched healthy controls were retrospectively selected from individuals who had undergone IVIM‐DWI as part of a health check‐up examination. Control subjects had no history of migraine or other primary or secondary headache disorders, no headache within the preceding 48 h, and no aura‐suggesting symptoms within the past 12 months. Individuals with a history of neurologic or systemic disorders, illicit drug use, long‐term medication use, or current use of analgesic or vasoactive medications were excluded. All control subjects had no clinically significant abnormalities on brain MRI.

MRI Protocol

IVIM‐DWI was acquired on a 1.5‐T MRI system (MAGNETOM Aera; Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) using a standard 16‐channel head coil and a single‐shot spin‐echo echo‐planar imaging sequence. Imaging parameters were as follows: repetition time (TR), 5100 ms; echo time (TE), 115 ms; field of view (FOV), 230 × 230 mm2; acquisition matrix, 192 × 192; acquired voxel size, 1.2 × 1.2 × 3 mm3, reconstructed voxel size, 0.6 × 0.6 × 3.0 mm3; slice thickness, 3 mm with a 30% interslice gap; and flip angle, 90°. Diffusion encoding was performed using a three‐scan trace acquisition with three orthogonal diffusion directions and a bipolar diffusion gradient scheme. Eleven diffusion weightings (b = 0, 50, 100, 150, 200, 250, 300, 350, 400, 600, and 800 s/mm2) were acquired with one signal average for each b‐value. The sequence incorporated strong spectral fat saturation, GRAPPA parallel imaging (acceleration factor = 2), and 6/8 partial Fourier acquisition in the phase‐encoding direction to reduce echo‐planar imaging–related geometric distortion and acquisition time. Axial T2‐weighted FLAIR images were acquired using the following parameters: TR, 7500 ms; TE, 86 ms; inversion time (TI), 2300 ms; field of view (FOV), 230 × 230 mm2; acquisition matrix, 320 × 240; acquired voxel size, 0.7 × 0.9 × 3 mm3; reconstructed voxel size, 0.6 × 0.6 × 3.0 mm3; and slice thickness, 3 mm. Vendor‐implemented inline preprocessing consisted of two‐dimensional distortion correction and prescan intensity normalization. No dedicated retrospective motion correction, image denoising, or additional preprocessing algorithms were applied before IVIM parameter estimation. IVIM parametric maps were generated using Olea Sphere v3.0 (Olea Medical, La Ciotat, France), which implemented a Bayesian probability‐based fitting algorithm for estimation of the biexponential IVIM model. The Bayesian algorithm estimates all IVIM parameters simultaneously using the complete diffusion dataset, thereby improving the robustness of parameter estimation, particularly for perfusion‐related parameters [14]. Mean IVIM parameter values were subsequently extracted from manually defined regions of interest (ROIs) for statistical analysis. The present implementation is consistent with the Bayesian fitting strategy previously reported for Olea Sphere v3.0 by Lecler et al. [15].

The D* and f parametric maps were rigidly co‐registered to the FLAIR series to improve anatomical localization and facilitate accurate ROI placement within the cerebral cortex and deep gray nuclei. This registration was intended to optimize spatial correspondence between the IVIM parameter maps and anatomical images rather than to correct the inherent geometric distortions associated with echo‐planar DWI. Minor residual geometric mismatches, predominantly involving the amygdala and dorsomedial prefrontal cortex due to susceptibility‐related echo‐planar distortion near the skull base, were visually accounted for during ROI placement. In these regions, the co‐registered FLAIR images and IVIM parametric maps were reviewed simultaneously using optimized image windowing, allowing anatomical landmarks to guide accurate ROI positioning despite mild residual distortion.

ROI‐Based Analysis of Brain Parenchyma

Based on functional imaging–derived evidence from the contemporary literature, eight key anatomical locations were selected for ROI placement to measure IVIM parameters. Since performing ROI analysis across the entire gyral system is not feasible, ROI placement was restricted to six cortical regions implicated in migraine, along with the posterior thalamus and the dorsal pontine region, resulting in a total of eight regions. The hypothesized associations between ROI‐placed regions and CM pathophysiology are summarized in Table 1, together with relevant supportive literature [16, 17, 18, 19, 20]. After co‐registration of the D* and f maps with the FLAIR images, circular ROIs with an area of 30–60 mm2 were placed, with the exact size adjusted according to the cortical thickness of six cortical regions considered relevant in migraine pathophysiology. ROI placement was performed on the FLAIR series to ensure optimal conspicuity and to allow precise delineation within the cerebral cortex, avoiding inclusion of underlying subcortical white matter. The initial ROI positions were subsequently verified on the rigidly registered D* and f maps to exclude spatial misregistration. Similar ROI placement was achieved by precisely mirroring each ROI to the anatomically corresponding contralateral cortical location. Measurements of the posterior thalamus and pontine tegmentum were likewise obtained using exact contralateral mirrored ROIs. Specifically, the posterior thalamic ROI encompassed the pulvinar and ventroposteromedial (VPM) nuclei, consistent with the probabilistic 3D subsegmentation model proposed by Chen et al. [21]. The ROI placement method is illustrated in Figure 3. All measurements were performed by two raters: a central rater with 8 years of experience in neuroimaging (Y.E.S.) and a co‐rater with 7 years of experience (A.P.), who independently performed ROI placement and measurements for the entire cohort.

For each ROI‐based measurement, the hemispheric asymmetry index (HAI) was calculated from the derived IVIM perfusion parameters (f, D*, and fD*). An absolute HAI value greater than 10% was considered indicative of asymmetric perfusion between the right and left relevant cortical ROI‐based measurements [6, 19].

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS (version 28.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Normality was evaluated with the Shapiro–Wilk test. Depending on the type of group comparisons, normally distributed variables were analyzed using paired‐samples or independent‐samples Student's t‐tests, whereas nonparametric variables were analyzed using the Wilcoxon signed‐rank or Mann–Whitney U test, respectively. ROI‐based HAIs from eight brain regions were compared between CM patients and healthy controls. Multiple comparisons across the absolute IVIM parameters and their HAIs were corrected using the Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) procedure, with the false discovery rate (FDR) set at 5% (q = 0.05). Adjusted q‐values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Inter‐rater agreement of ROI placement on D*, f maps, and HAI of these parameters was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), calculated with a two‐way random‐effects model for absolute agreement between both raters. To facilitate interpretation of HAI after FDR correction, an additional exploratory analysis was performed. For regions demonstrating significant group differences in the HAI of IVIM‐DWI parameters, bilateral measurements were ranked into the lower value and higher value hemispheres irrespective of anatomical laterality. The absolute interhemispheric separation (higher value minus lower value hemisphere) was subsequently compared between the CM and healthy control groups using the Mann–Whitney U test. For this exploratory analysis, a two‐sided p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. This exploratory analysis was intended only to facilitate interpretation of the HAI and was not considered a predefined primary outcome measure. Furthermore, to evaluate the robustness of statistically significant findings, a leave‐one‐out sensitivity analysis was performed by sequentially excluding each participant and repeating the corresponding Mann–Whitney U comparison.

Results

The mean age was 36.2 ± 12.3 years in the CM group (18 females/12 males) and 36.8 ± 13.1 years in the healthy control group (18 females/12 males), with no significant difference between groups (p = 0.68). Among the eight brain regions evaluated in the CM and control group, the initial comparative analyses revealed significant differences between right and left hemispheres only for amygdala fD* (p = 0.02) and postcentral gyrus facial homunculus f (p = 0.04) in the control group. However, neither difference remained significant after FDR correction (q = 0.16 and q = 0.28, respectively). Based on ROI‐based analysis of other brain locations, no other absolute IVIM parameters, including D*, f, and fD*, showed a significant difference between right and left hemispheres either in the CM or control group, before and after FDR correction (Table 2). Inter‐rater agreement of ROI placement between the two raters was excellent for D* (ICC = 0.94) and good for f (ICC = 0.79) and fD* (ICC = 0.75) measurements.

Analysis of HAI for IVIM‐derived parameters (D*, f, and fD*) demonstrated a mild degree of right–left asymmetry in both the CM group and healthy controls. After BH‐FDR correction for multiple comparisons, no significant increase in the HAI of IVIM parameters was noted in the CM group compared with the control group, across six cortical regions or in the pontine tegmentum (Table 3). In contrast, the median fD* HAI of the posterior thalamus was 27.91 (IQR: 16.87) in the CM group and 10.43 (IQR: 19.58) in the control group, indicating a significantly greater absolute right–left asymmetry of combined perfusion fraction and pseudodiffusion in CM patients compared with controls (p = 0.002, q = 0.017 after FDR correction). Inter‐rater agreement for the calculated HAIs was additionally assessed using independently derived HAI measurements from both readers. The HAI of the pseudodiffusion coefficient D* demonstrated good interobserver agreement (ICC = 0.81), whereas the HAI of the perfusion fraction f and the composite perfusion parameter fD* demonstrated moderate agreement (ICC = 0.70 and 0.69, respectively). These findings indicate acceptable reliability of the asymmetry measurements used for the primary outcome analysis. The interobserver agreement for the HAI of the IVIM‐derived parameters was lower than that observed for the corresponding absolute IVIM‐derived perfusion parameters. Figure 4 illustrates the distribution of interhemispheric asymmetry of IVIM‐DWI–derived perfusion parameters in the posterior thalami. Although the fD* HAI in the anterior cingulate cortex was higher in CM patients than in healthy controls, this effect did not remain significant after BH‐FDR correction (p = 0.024, q = 0.098; Table 3). In contrast, fD* asymmetry in the bilateral posterior thalamic nuclei remained statistically significant after correction for multiple comparisons (p = 0.002, q = 0.017).

To further evaluate the robustness of the posterior thalamic fD* HAI, a leave‐one‐out sensitivity analysis was performed by sequentially excluding each participant and repeating the group comparison. The posterior thalamic fD* HAI remained significantly greater in patients with CM than in control subjects across all 60 iterations (p‐range: 0.0007–0.0035). Furthermore, the median group difference in the posterior thalamic fD* HAI remained stable throughout the analysis (range, 16.08–18.88), indicating that the observed posterior thalamic fD* asymmetry was robust and was not driven by any single participant or isolated measurement variability.

Discussion

The present study was designed to investigate whether the interhemispheric functional asymmetry reported by previous rs‐fMRI studies is accompanied by corresponding hemispheric differences in cerebral microvascular perfusion during the interictal phase of CM. Using IVIM‐derived perfusion imaging, we demonstrated significantly increased interhemispheric asymmetry of the combined perfusion parameter (fD*) within the posterior thalamus, whereas migraine‐implicated cortical regions did not show comparable perfusion asymmetry between cerebral hemispheres. These findings suggest that previously reported functional hemispheric lateralization in migraine may have a corresponding regional microvascular correlate rather than representing solely alterations in functional connectivity.

Importantly, our findings should not be interpreted as supporting an imaging‐based diagnosis of CM. Instead, they provide complementary evidence linking previously reported functional hemispheric lateralization with regional microvascular alterations during the interictal phase, thereby facilitating our understanding of CM pathophysiology. Previous ASL‐based studies claimed that cerebral perfusion abnormalities may continue during the interictal phase of migraine. However, they did not objectively note significant hemispheric asymmetry using ASL‐based cerebral blood flow (CBF) measurements [6, 22]. Moreover, the reported regional CBF alterations were heterogeneous, and the consistency and biological significance of these regional findings were uncertain. Therefore, whether functional hemispheric lateralization in migraine is accompanied by corresponding regional microvascular perfusion asymmetry remains an open question, which is particularly relevant given the clinically lateralized nature of migraine headache. In this context, our preliminary findings extend the existing literature by suggesting that IVIM‐derived microvascular perfusion imaging may reveal regional hemispheric perfusion asymmetry within the posterior thalamus, which is a key region involved in nociceptive transmission and pain modulation. These findings may provide complementary evidence supporting a regional microvascular correlate of functional hemispheric lateralization in CM.

The posterior thalamus represents one of the principal relay centers of the trigeminothalamic nociceptive pathway and has consistently been implicated in migraine pathophysiology [23, 24]. The VPM nucleus serves as the primary relay for trigeminovascular nociceptive transmission to the cerebral cortex, whereas the pulvinar participates in multisensory integration, pain modulation, and visual processing [24, 25]. Experimental studies have further demonstrated that posterior thalamic neurons showed increased responsiveness to dural nociceptive stimulation and contribute to central sensitization and photophobia [23, 26]. Likewise, resting‐state fMRI studies have demonstrated altered functional connectivity between the posterior thalamus and frontal, visual, and somatosensory cortices during the interictal phase of migraine [9, 18]. Collectively, these observations provide a biological rationale for the posterior thalamus as one of the key regions in migraine pathophysiology. Our findings complement these previous studies by suggesting that previously remarked functional abnormalities of the dorsal thalami may also be accompanied by regional microvascular perfusion asymmetry during the interictal phase of CM. Although the underlying mechanisms remain uncertain, such hemispheric microvascular perfusion asymmetry may reflect subtle neurovascular alterations within the dorsal thalamus and may contribute to understanding the biological basis of the clinically lateralized nature of migraine. It may also contribute to lateralized trigeminothalamic nociceptive processing by lowering the threshold for nociceptive transmission within the affected hemisphere.

Despite the functional hemispheric lateralization consistently reported by rs‐fMRI studies, we did not observe corresponding hemispheric asymmetry of IVIM‐derived perfusion parameters within migraine‐related cortical regions during the interictal phase [9, 12, 18]. This observation should not necessarily be interpreted as conflicting with previous functional neuroimaging studies because rs‐fMRI evaluates neuronal functional connectivity, whereas IVIM‐derived perfusion imaging reflects tissue microvascular perfusion [27]. In terms of cortical perfusion in migraine, our findings are also consistent with previous ASL studies. Bai et al. demonstrated bilateral thalamic and regional cortical perfusion abnormalities in 18 patients with CM without evidence of hemispheric lateralization, whereas Shimoda et al. reported persistent interictal perfusion abnormalities but did not identify significant hemispheric asymmetry using conventional CBF measurements [6, 28]. Collectively, these findings suggest that persistent microvascular perfusion asymmetry in CM may preferentially involve subcortical deep nuclei rather than representing a diffuse cortical phenomenon. Future studies integrating IVIM‐derived perfusion imaging combined with functional neuroimaging may further clarify the relationship between regional microvascular alterations and hemispheric functional lateralization in CM.

Another important finding of the present study was that significant hemispheric asymmetry was observed only for the combined perfusion parameter fD*, whereas D* and f alone did not demonstrate comparable regional differences. According to the IVIM model, D* primarily reflects flow‐related pseudodiffusion associated with microvascular blood velocity, whereas f represents the fractional microvascular blood volume [27]. The greater asymmetry observed for f than for D* may suggest that the hemispheric fD* imbalance in our study may primarily reflect differences in capillary recruitment or microvascular density within the posterior thalamus rather than alterations in blood flow velocity. This interpretation is supported by Wu et al., who demonstrated that the perfusion fraction provides a more stable and reproducible surrogate of cerebral perfusion than D* in normal brain tissue [29]. In our study, HAI of IVIM parameters demonstrated lower interobserver agreement than the corresponding absolute IVIM parameters, because HAI parameters are derived from bilateral hemispheric IVIM measurements and therefore vulnerable to parameter variability from both hemispheres. Nevertheless, the observed reproducibility remained acceptable, indicating that the detected hemispheric perfusion asymmetry is unlikely to be explained only by measurement variability.

Our study presents several limitations. First, the retrospective design and relatively low sample size may limit the reproducibility of our preliminary results. Second, IVIM‐derived parameters, particularly the pseudodiffusion coefficient D*, are known to be sensitive to noise and organ‐specific local factors [30]. Third, ROI‐based analysis was restricted to predefined cortical and subcortical regions implicated in migraine pathophysiology, which may overlook additional cortical regions associated with perfusion asymmetry during the interictal phase of CM. Fourth, imaging was performed exclusively during the interictal phase, and without correlation with the most recent and most frequent attack laterality, disease duration, or treatment status. Furthermore, due to the cross‐sectional design of the present study, longitudinal changes in IVIM‐derived perfusion parameters, including their hemispheric asymmetry, as well as the effects of preventive treatments on D, f, and fD*, were not assessed. Finally, we employed a pre‐defined composite ROI‐based quantitative approach where the voxels of each ROI were combined to calculate each of the IVIM parameters for subject‐level analysis. Although this strategy enabled robust comparison of hemispheric asymmetry between groups, future studies incorporating voxel‐wise or parametric mapping approaches may provide additional spatial characterization of hemispheric perfusion asymmetry and complementary validation of the regional findings reported herein. Overall, these limitations highlight the necessity for larger, prospective studies with longitudinal imaging to establish the robustness, temporal dynamics, and clinical relevance of IVIM‐derived perfusion parameters in migraineurs.

Conclusions

IVIM‐DWI‐based clinical study offers insights into the parenchymal microvascular correlates of CM during the interictal phase. The observed hemispheric asymmetry of IVIM‐derived microvascular perfusion parameters in the posterior thalamus supports the concept that side‐predominant activation of the trigeminothalamic nociceptive network may drive regional perfusion alterations, thereby contributing to a better understanding of migraine pathophysiology and asymmetric initiation of the headache. Future studies with larger cohorts are warranted to validate the potential clinical role of IVIM‐DWI during the interictal phases of migraine.
