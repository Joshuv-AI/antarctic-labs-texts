Graph theoretic properties of Speyer’s matroid polynomial g M ​ ( t ) g_{M}(t)
Erik Panzer

Abstract
We prove relations between the number of k k -connected components of a graph, Crapo’s invariant β ⁡ ( M ) \beta(M) of a matroid, and Speyer’s polynomial g M ​ ( t ) g_{M}(t) . These yield a simple interpretation of g M ′ ​ ( − 1 ) g_{M}^{\prime}(-1) when M M is graphic or cographic. Furthermore, we improve Ferroni’s algorithm to compute g M ​ ( t ) g_{M}(t) and provide an implementation and an extensive data set. These calculations reveal a large number of graph theoretic constraints on the second derivative g M ′′ ​ ( − 1 ) g_{M}^{\prime\prime}(-1) , which we thus advertise as an intriguing new invariant of graphs. We also propose a relation between the flow polynomial and g M ′′ ​ ( 0 ) g_{M}^{\prime\prime}(0) for cubic graphs.

Matroids: Speyer and Ferroni

Our second set of results links these identities to Speyer’s polynomial
g_{M}(t)\in t\mathbbm{Z}[t]
. This invariant was constructed from
K
-theory in
[
47
]
for all realizable matroids, and extended to all matroids in
[
23
]
. It has non-negative coefficients
[
1

and further intriguing properties, but combinatorial interpretations of its evaluations are rare:

•

For every matroid
M
without loops or coloops,
[
47
,
23
]
show that

g_{M}(-1)=(-1)^{c(M)}\qquad\text{and}\qquad g^{\prime}_{M}(0)=\beta(M).

(1.4)

•

The coefficient of the highest power
t^{\rk(M)}
of
g_{M}(t)
is studied in
[
22
]
, especially for matroids whose rank is very small or close to half the number of edges.

•

If
M
is a Schubert matroid, then the coefficients
\mathsf{N}_{i}(M)\in\mathbbm{Z}
in the expansion

g_{M}(t)=t\sum_{i=0}^{\rk(M)-1}\mathsf{N}_{i}(M)\cdot(1+t)^{i}

(1.5)

are non-negative and count certain lattice paths with
i
diagonal steps
[
19
]
.

In terms of the expansion (
1.5
), the first identity of (
1.4
) amounts to
\mathsf{N}_{0}(M)=(-1)^{c(M)-1}
for any matroid without loops or coloops.

We add to these a useful formula for the next coefficient
\mathsf{N}_{1}(M)
, which we state equivalently in terms of the first derivative
g^{\prime}_{M}(-1)=(-1)^{c(M)-1}-\mathsf{N}_{1}(M)
:

Proposition 1.5
.

For every matroid
M
without loops or coloops,

g^{\prime}_{M}(-1)=(-1)^{c(M)-1}\sum_{A\subseteq M}(-1)^{\ell(A)}\beta(A)\rk(A).

Remark 1.6
.

Expanding

according to (

as the alternating double sum of

over nested pairs

can be obtained as an evaluation of a derivative from other matroid polynomials; e.g. the

[

or the

[

Example 1.7
.

Take a uniform matroid
M=\mathsf{U}^{n}_{r}
without loops or coloops (

). Then every
A\subseteq M
is itself uniform:
A\cong\mathsf{U}^{i}_{r}
when

, unless

\beta(\mathsf{U}^{i}_{r})=\binom{i-2}{r-1}

g^{\prime}_{\mathsf{U}^{n}_{r}}(-1)=\sum_{e\in\mathsf{U}^{n}_{r}}1+\sum_{i=r+1}^{n}\binom{n}{i}(-1)^{i-r}\binom{i-2}{r-1}r=n-r(n-r).

Specializing to (co)graphic matroids, combining

Theorem 1.8

For every graphic or cographic matroid

g_{M}^{\prime}(-1)=(-1)^{c(M)-1}c(M).

Cycle matroids of biconnected graphs thus satisfy

We assume throughout that graphs

A subset of vertices

Definition 2.1

A

Lemma 2.2

Let

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}N\in\mathscr{N}(G)\\
v\in N\end{subarray}}(-1)^{\left|E(N)\right|}=0.

Since
\rk(G)=\left|V(G)\right|-\left|\pi_{0}(G)\right|
, this concludes the proof whenever
G
has exactly one connected component that contains edges, for then
\left|\pi_{0}(G)\right|=1+i
. If
G
has multiple connected components that contain edges, then the cycle matroid of
G
is disconnected and
\beta(G)=0
by
[
11
]
, so both sides of (
2.4
) vanish.
∎

Theorem 2.6
.

For any graphic or cographic matroid, the number of connected components is

c(M)=\left|M\right|-\sum_{A\subseteq B\subseteq M}(-1)^{\left|B\right|-\left|A\right|}\ell(A)\rk(B).

(2.5)

Proof.

Rewriting the corank
\ell(A)=\left|A\right|-\rk(A)
and recognizing (
1.1
), the sum over
A
evaluates to

\sum_{A\subseteq B}(-1)^{\left|A\right|}\ell(A)=\sum_{A\subseteq B}(-1)^{\left|A\right|}\left|A\right|-(-1)^{\rk(B)}\beta(B)=-\delta_{\left|B\right|,1}-(-1)^{\rk(B)}\beta(B)

where
\delta_{\left|B\right|,1}=1
if
\left|B\right|=1
and
\delta_{\left|B\right|,1}=0
otherwise. For a single edge
B=\{e\}
with
\rk(B)=1=\beta(B)
(i.e. not a self-loop), this combination is zero (since
\ell(A)=0
). For a self-loop, the sum over
A
gives
-1
; but this does not contribute to (
2.5
) because
\rk(B)=0
in this case. Therefore,

-\sum_{A\subseteq B\subseteq M}(-1)^{\left|B\right|-\left|A\right|}\ell(A)\rk(B)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}B\subseteq M\\
\left|B\right|\neq 1\end{subarray}}(-1)^{\left|B\right|}(-1)^{\rk(B)}\beta(B)\rk(B).

(2.6)

Now assume that
M
is graphic. Then so is every submatroid
B\subseteq M
and we can invoke
theorem

2.5
for all
\left|B\right|\neq 1
(the theorem also applies to the trivial case
B=\emptyset
).
For any single edge
\left|B\right|=1
, self-loop or not, we have
\sum_{A\subseteq B}(-1)^{\left|A\right|}c(A)=-1
. The sum over
\left|B\right|\neq 1
thus combines with the number of edges
\left|M\right|
on the right-hand side of (
2.5
) to

\sum_{B\subseteq M}(-1)^{\left|B\right|}\left(\sum_{A\subseteq B}(-1)^{\left|A\right|}c(A)\right)=\sum_{A\subseteq M}c(A)\sum_{B\subseteq M,B\supseteq A}(-1)^{\left|B\setminus A\right|}=c(M)

by inclusion-exclusion. This proves (
2.5
) for graphic matroids. Now suppose
M
is cographic. Then the identity holds for its (graphic) dual matroid
M^{\star}
, which has the same
c(M)=c(M^{\star})
and
\left|M\right|=\left|M^{\star}\right|
. The ranks
r^{\star}
and coranks
\ell^{\star}
of subsets
A
of the dual
M^{\star}
relate to the coranks and ranks of the complementary sets
A=M\setminus A
in
M
as

\ell^{\star}(A)=\rk(M)-\rk(A),\quad\rk^{\star}(B)=\ell(M)-\ell(B).

Hence re-indexing the summation variables
(A,B)\mapsto(B,A)
, we have the identity

\displaystyle\left|M\right|-c(M)=\left|M^{\star}\right|-c(M^{\star})

\displaystyle=\sum_{A\subseteq B\subseteq M^{\star}}(-1)^{\left|B\right|-\left|A\right|}\ell^{\star}(A)\rk^{\star}(B)

\displaystyle=\sum_{A\subseteq B\subseteq M}(-1)^{\left|B\right|-\left|A\right|}(\rk(M)-\rk(B))(\ell(M)-\ell(A)).

Expanding the summand, the parts with
\rk(M)
or
\ell(M)
sum to zero:

\sum_{A\subseteq M}\rk(M)(\ell(M)-\ell(A))\sum_{B\subseteq M,B\supseteq A}(-1)^{\left|B\setminus A\right|}=\rk(M)(\ell(M)-\ell(A))\Big|_{A=M}=0

by inclusion-exclusion, and similarly the sum over
A
in
\sum_{A\subseteq B\subseteq M}(-1)^{\left|B\right|-\left|A\right|}\rk(B)\ell(M)
localizes to
B=\emptyset
where
\rk(B)=0
. Therefore, only the sum of
(-1)^{\left|B\right|-\left|A\right|}\ell(A)\rk(B)
remains, which concludes the proof that (
2.5
) also holds for cographic matroids.
∎

This completes the proof of
theorem

1.1
as stated in the introduction:

Corollary 2.7
.

The identity (
2.4
) from
theorem

2.5
also holds for cographic matroids with at least two edges.

Proof.

The first part of the proof of
theorem

2.6
shows that the identity of
theorem

2.5
is equivalent, under inclusion-exclusion, to
theorem

2.6
.
∎

We now generalize the above relations to higher connectivity (
theorem

1.2
from the introduction). For a graph
G
without self-loops, all edges have
\beta(e)=1=\rk(e)
. Hence, including the summands with
\left|B\right|=1
in (
2.6
) adds precisely
\left|E(G)\right|
, so that (
2.5
) can be written as

c(G)=\sum_{A\subseteq E(G)}(-1)^{\ell(A)}\beta(A)\rk(A).

If
G
is biconnected, then this sum is equal to
1
; otherwise
c_{1}(G)=1+\sum_{v}(\left|\pi_{0}(G\setminus v)\right|-1)
counts the 1-vertex cuts of
G
, weighted by the number of extra components their deletion creates. We generalize
c(G)=c_{1}(G)
as follows:

Definition 2.8
.

For any graph
G
and any positive integer
i
, define the sum

c_{i}(G)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=(-1)^{i-1}\sum_{A\subseteq E(G)}(-1)^{\ell(A)}\beta(A)\binom{\rk A}{i}

where the binomial coefficient is understood to vanish when
i>\rk(A)
.

The integers
c_{i}(G)
measure the connectivity of
G
. We call a graph
k
-connected if it has more than
k
vertices, and deleting any set of less than
k
vertices leaves a connected graph.

Theorem 2.9
.

For any
k\geq 1
and any
k
-connected graph
G
without self-loops, we have the identities
c_{1}(G)=\ldots=c_{k-1}(G)=1
and

c_{k}(G)=1+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subset V(G)\\
\left|S\right|=k\end{subarray}}\Big(\pi_{0}(G\setminus S)-1\Big).

(2.7)

In other words, for a
k
-connected graph, the sum
c_{k}(G)
from
definition

2.8
counts
k
-vertex cuts, weighted by the number of extra components that their deletion creates.

Proof.

Let
V(A)\subseteq V(G)
denote the vertices incident to at least one edge of
A
. Since
\beta(A)=0
whenever
c(A)>1
, the summand in
definition

2.8
is zero unless
A
defines a biconnected subgraph on
V(A)
. In this case,
\rk(A)=V(A)-1
, so that we can interpret
\binom{\rk(A)+1}{i}=\binom{\rk(A)}{i}+\binom{\rk(A)}{i-1}
as choosing
i
vertices out of
V(A)
:

c_{i}(G)-c_{i-1}(G)=(-1)^{i-1}\sum_{A\subseteq E(G)}(-1)^{\ell(A)}\beta(A)\binom{\left|V(A)\right|}{i}.

Expanding the binomial into a sum over all subsets
S
of
i
vertices, swapping the sums over
S
and
A
, and expanding
\beta(A)
according to (
1.1
), yields

c_{i}(G)-c_{i-1}(G)=(-1)^{i-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subset V(G)\\
\left|S\right|=i\end{subarray}}\sum_{B\subseteq E(G)}\rk(B)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\supseteq B\\
V(A)\supseteq S\end{subarray}}(-1)^{\left|A\setminus B\right|}.

For fixed
S
and
B
, consider the partition
S=X\sqcup Y
with
X=S\cap V(B)
. Then
A\supseteq B
reduces the condition
V(A)\supseteq S
down to
V(A)\supseteq Y
. Any edge
e\in E(G)\setminus B
with neither endpoint in
Y
may be added or removed from
A
without affecting these conditions; however this switch swaps the sign
(-1)^{\left|B\setminus A\right|}
and forces the sum over
A
to vanish. Therefore, the sum over
B
receives contributions only from induced subgraphs, where
B=E(G\setminus Y)
consists of
all
edges with no endpoint in
Y
. Denoting by
E_{Y}\subseteq E(G)
those edges with at least one endpoint in
Y
, and relabeling
A\setminus B
as
A
, we have

c_{i}(G)-c_{i-1}(G)=(-1)^{i-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subset V(G)\\
\left|S\right|=i\end{subarray}}\sum_{Y\subseteq S}\rk(G\setminus Y)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\subseteq E_{Y}\\
V(A)\supseteq Y\end{subarray}}(-1)^{\left|A\right|}.

In fact, in order to have
V(B)=X=S\setminus Y
, the sum should only be over such partitions of
S
such that each vertex
v\in X
has at least one neighbour in
V(G)\setminus Y
. However, this is automatically the case for all
i\leq k
, because the existence of a vertex
x\in X\neq\emptyset
with all neighbours in
Y
would imply that
Y
is a vertex cut of size
\left|Y\right|=i-\left|X\right|<i\leq k
, contradicting the assumption that
G
is
k
-connected.

Similarly, if any vertex
v\in Y
had all its neighbours contained in
Y
, then
Y\setminus\{v\}
would be a vertex cut of size less than
i\leq k
. Thus, we may assume that each
v\in Y
has at least one edge to a vertex in
V(G)\setminus Y
. Pick one such edge
e_{v}
(by construction,
e\in E_{Y}
), then the summation domain
\mathcal{A}=\{A\colon A\subseteq E_{Y},V(A)\supseteq Y\}=\mathcal{A}_{1}\sqcup\mathcal{A}_{2}\sqcup\mathcal{A}_{3}
splits into
\mathcal{A}_{1}=\{e_{v}\notin A\}
,
\mathcal{A}_{2}=\{e_{v}\in A,A\setminus\{e_{v}\}\in\mathcal{A}\}
, and
\mathcal{A}_{3}=\{e_{v}\in A,A\setminus\{e_{v}\}\notin\mathcal{A}\}
. The bijection
A\mapsto A\sqcup\{e_{v}\}
between
\mathcal{A}_{1}
and
\mathcal{A}_{2}
shows that the corresponding contributions to the sum of
(-1)^{\left|A\right|}
cancel. Since
v
is the only endpoint of
e_{v}
in
Y
, in the remaining sum over
\mathcal{A}_{3}
we have
V(A\setminus\{e_{v}\})=V(A)\setminus\{v\}\supseteq Y\setminus\{v\}
. Thus, by induction,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\subseteq E_{Y}\\
V(A)\supseteq Y\end{subarray}}(-1)^{\left|A\right|}=-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\subseteq E_{Y\setminus v}\\
V(A)\supseteq Y\setminus\{v\}\end{subarray}}(-1)^{\left|A\right|}=\ldots=(-1)^{\left|Y\right|}.

Combining this result for the sum over
A
with the identity
\rk(B)=\rk(G\setminus Y)=\left|V(G\setminus Y)\right|-\left|\pi_{0}(G\setminus Y)\right|
, we have shown

c_{i}(G)-c_{i-1}(G)=(-1)^{i-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subset V(G)\\
\left|S\right|=i\end{subarray}}\sum_{Y\subseteq S}(\left|V(G)\right|-\left|Y\right|-\left|\pi_{0}(G\setminus Y)\right|)(-1)^{\left|Y\right|}.

Because
G
has no vertex cuts of size less than
k\geq i
, we know that
\left|\pi_{0}(G\setminus Y)\right|=1
unless perhaps for the summand with
Y=S
. We can therefore write the right-hand side as

(-1)^{i-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subset V(G)\\
\left|S\right|=i\end{subarray}}\sum_{Y\subseteq S}(\left|V(G)\right|-\left|Y\right|-1)(-1)^{\left|Y\right|}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subset V(G)\\
\left|S\right|=i\end{subarray}}(\left|\pi_{0}(G\setminus S)\right|-1).

The elementary sum over
Y
depends not on the set
S
, but only its size
i
:

\sum_{Y\subseteq S}(\left|V(G)\right|-\left|Y\right|-1)(-1)^{\left|Y\right|}=\sum_{r=0}^{i}\binom{i}{r}(\rk(G)-r)(-1)^{r}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $i=1$,}\\
0&\text{if $i\geq 2$.}\\
\end{cases}

In conclusion, for all
1\leq i\leq k
we have established the identity

c_{i}(G)=c_{i-1}(G)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subset V(G)\\
\left|S\right|=i\end{subarray}}(\left|\pi_{0}(G\setminus S)\right|-1)+\begin{cases}\left|V(G)\right|&\text{if $i=1$,}\\
0&\text{if $i\geq 2$.}\\
\end{cases}

(2.8)

It follows immediately that
c_{k-1}(G)=c_{k-2}(G)=\ldots=c_{2}(G)=c_{1}(G)
, which reduces
theorem

2.9
for the cases
k\geq 2
to merely the claim that
c_{1}(G)=1
, which in turn follows from (
2.7
) in the case
k=1
. So we are only left having to prove (
2.7
) for
k=1
, which is precisely the relation between the number
c_{1}(G)=c(G)
of blocks and degrees of vertices in the block-cut tree
[
30
]
that we already used to prove
theorem

2.5
.

In fact, we can use (
2.8
) to give an independent proof even of this base case
k=1
, without referring to
[
30
]
: Setting
i=1
in (
2.8
) yields

c_{1}(G)=c_{0}(G)+\left|V(G)\right|+\sum_{v\in V(G)}(\left|\pi_{0}(G\setminus\{v\})\right|-1)

so that it only remains to note that for a (1-)connected graph,

c_{0}(G)=-\sum_{A\subseteq E(G)}(-1)^{\ell(A)}\beta(A)=-\sum_{B\subseteq A\subseteq E(G)}\rk(B)(-1)^{\left|A\setminus B\right|}=-\rk(G)=1-\left|V(G)\right|

where the second sum over
A
(for all
A\supseteq B
, with fixed
B
) gives zero unless
B=E(G)
.
∎

3
Speyer’s invariant from Ferroni’s algorithm

We use a combinatorial characterization of the polynomial
g_{M}(t)
, which was proposed in
[
47
]
and confirmed in
[
21
, Theorem 9.15]
and
[
19
, Definition 1.2]
. Namely, Speyer’s polynomial is the unique
covaluative
matroid invariant
M\mapsto g_{M}(t)\in\mathbbm{Z}[t]
such that:

1.

If
M
has a loop or coloop, then
g_{M}(t)=0
.

2.

If
M
is series-parallel, then
g_{M}(t)=t
.

3.

If
M=M_{1}\oplus M_{2}
is a direct sum, then
g_{M}(t)=g_{M_{1}}(t)\cdot g_{M_{2}}(t)
.

A
loop
of
M
is an edge (element of the ground set) of rank zero; in a graphic matroid this is an edge with both endpoints at the same vertex (“self-loop”). A
coloop
is an edge
e
with
\rk(M\setminus e)=\rk(M)-1
, i.e. an edge contained in
every
basis—also called a
bridge
in a graphic matroid. We call a matroid
series-parallel
if it can be obtained from the 2-cycle
M\cong\mathsf{U}^{2}_{1}
by a sequence of series extensions
M\mapsto M\oplus_{2}\mathsf{U}^{3}_{2}
(subdivide an edge) and parallel extensions
M\mapsto M\oplus_{2}\mathsf{U}^{3}_{1}
(add an edge parallel to an existing edge).

The covaluative property states that
g_{M}
can be computed from any decomposition of the matroid polytope of
M
into other matroid polytopes; see
[
19
, §2.3]
. The algorithm for computing
g_{M}
elaborated in
[
19
]
exploits this in two steps:

1.

Decompose
M
into
Schubert matroids
, following
[
29
]
using the lattice of cyclic flats.

2.

Decompose Schubert matroids into direct sums of series-parallel matroids, using a description in terms of lattice paths.

In the sequel, we discuss both of these steps, in order to develop formulas that are explicit and efficient to evaluate. We apply these to prove
Proposition

1.5
from the introduction, and in
Section

3.4
we present our algorithm for efficient computation of
g_{M}(t)
.

3.1
Cyclic flats, chains, and the Möbius function

A subset
A\subseteq M
of a matroid is called a
flat
if
\rk(A\cup\{e\})=\rk(A)+1
for every edge
e\notin A
. Dually,
A
is called
cyclic
if
\rk(A\setminus\{e\})=\rk(A)
for every
e\in A
. Any given
A
contains a unique largest cyclic subset
\cyc(A)
, and any given
A
is contained in a unique smallest flat
\cl(A)
.
In the case of a graphic matroid, a subset
A\subseteq E(G)
is cyclic if each
e\in A
is contained in a cycle that is contained in
A
, and
A
is a flat if all edges
e\notin A
have their endpoints in two different connected components of
A
(that is, each connected component of
A
is an
induced
subgraph of
G
). The operation
A\mapsto\cyc(A)
removes all bridges from
A
, and the closure
A\mapsto\cl(A)
adds all edges
e\in A
whose endpoints are in the same connected component of
A
.

The lattice
\mathcal{Z}
of cyclic flats as studied in
[
5
]
consists of all
A\subseteq M
that are simultaneously cyclic and flat. Cyclic flats form a poset under the inclusion relation
A\leq B\Leftrightarrow A\subseteq B
, and in fact they form a
lattice
: every pair
A,B\in\mathcal{Z}
has a
join
(least upper bound)
A\vee B\in\mathcal{Z}
and a
meet
(greatest lower bound)
A\wedge B\in\mathcal{Z}
. Concretely, these are

A\vee B=\cl(A\cup B)\quad\text{and}\quad A\wedge B=\cyc(A\cap B).

The minimum
0_{\mathcal{Z}}=\cl(\emptyset)
of
\mathcal{Z}
is the set of all loops and the maximum
1_{\mathcal{Z}}=\cyc(M)
is the complement of the coloops. Since
g_{M}(t)
is only non-zero for matroids without loops or coloops, in fact we will have
0_{\mathcal{Z}}=\emptyset
and
1_{\mathcal{Z}}=M
.

Definition 3.1
.

A chain (of length

\mathcal{L}
is a sequence
C_{0}<C_{1}<\ldots<C_{r}
(denoted

) of elements
C_{i}\in\mathcal{L}

. A chain

refinement
of a chain

if each
C_{i}
appears somewhere in

\mathcal{L}

and the maximum

This collection of subsets
\{C_{1},\ldots,C_{k}\}
of
\mathcal{L}^{\circ}

of
\mathcal{L}
. In the notation of

[
48

, the order complex
\Delta(\mathcal{L}^{\circ})\cong\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{L})\setminus\{1_{\mathscr{C}}\}
also contains the empty simplex of dimension

corresponding to the chain
0_{\mathscr{C}}
.

The algorithm
[
19
, §5]
computes the Speyer polynomial
g_{M}(t)
of a matroid by decomposing the matroid polytope of
M
into matroid polytopes of
Schubert matroids
. A Schubert matroid is a matroid whose lattice of cyclic flats is a chain, and given a chain
C_{\bullet}\in\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{Z}_{M})
of cyclic flats of
M
, we denote by
\mathsf{SM}\left(C_{\bullet}\right)
the Schubert matroid whose lattice of cyclic flats is precisely this chain, so that
\mathcal{Z}_{\mathsf{SM}\left(C_{\bullet}\right)}=\{C_{0},\ldots,C_{r}\}
.

Theorem 3.5

(
[
19
, Corollary 5.3]
)
.

Given a chain
C_{\bullet}\in\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{Z}_{M})
of cyclic flats, set
\lambda_{C_{\bullet}}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=-\mu_{\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{Z}_{M})}(C_{\bullet},1_{\mathscr{C}})
.
Then for every matroid
M
without loops or coloops,

g_{M}(t)=(-1)^{c(M)-1}\sum_{C_{\bullet}\in\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{Z}_{M})\setminus\{1_{\mathscr{C}}\}}\lambda_{C_{\bullet}}g_{\mathsf{SM}\left(C_{\bullet}\right)}(t).

(3.1)

This can be thought of as a sum over all simplices in the order complex
\Delta(\mathcal{Z}_{M}^{\circ})\cong\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{Z}_{M})\setminus\{1_{\mathscr{C}}\}
of the poset of the proper, non-empty cyclic flats
\mathcal{Z}_{M}^{\circ}=\mathcal{Z}_{M}\setminus\{\emptyset,M\}
.

To evaluate (
3.1
), one needs to find the coefficients
\lambda_{C_{\bullet}}\in\mathbbm{Z}
. They can be computed as
\lambda_{C_{\bullet}}=1-\sum_{D_{\bullet}>C_{\bullet}}\lambda_{D_{\bullet}}
, which follows from the defining recursion of the Möbius function in the lattice of chains
\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{Z}_{M})
.
Our first contribution is a simple formula for
\lambda_{C_{\bullet}}
directly in terms of the Möbius function of the lattice
\mathcal{Z}_{M}
. This identity holds in any lattice and is not specific to cyclic flats; a version for the lattice of all flats is implicit in
[
22
, §8]
.

Since a lattice is typically much smaller than the lattice of all its chains, this formula provides a much more efficient route to compute
\lambda_{C_{\bullet}}
than the defining recursion.

Proposition 3.6
.

For any chain
C_{\bullet}\in\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{L})
of length
r
in a finite lattice
\mathcal{L}
, we have

-\mu_{\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{L})}(C_{\bullet},1_{\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{L})})=\prod_{i=1}^{r}\left(-\mu_{\mathcal{L}}(C_{i-1},C_{i})\right).

(3.2)

Proof.

By the recursion
\mu(x,z)=-\sum_{x\leq y<z}\mu(x,y)
, the left-hand side of (
3.2
) is equal to a sum over all chains
D_{\bullet}\in\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{L})
,
D_{\bullet}\neq 1_{\mathscr{C}}
, which refine
C_{\bullet}
:

-\mu_{\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{L})}(C_{\bullet},1_{\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{L})})=\sum_{C_{\bullet}\leq D_{\bullet}}\mu_{\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{L})}(C_{\bullet},D_{\bullet}).

(3.3)

Refinement means
C_{i}=D_{k_{i}}
for some sequence
k_{1}<\ldots<k_{r}
. In particular, the subsequences
D^{(i)}_{\bullet}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\{D_{k_{i}}<D_{k_{i}+1}<\ldots<D_{k_{i+1}}\}
are refinements of the length one chains
C^{(i)}_{\bullet}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\{C_{i}<C_{i+1}\}
. In other words, the interval between
C_{\bullet}
and
D_{\bullet}
is a product

[C_{\bullet},D_{\bullet}]_{\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{L})}\cong\prod_{i=0}^{r-1}[C^{(i)}_{\bullet},D^{(i)}_{\bullet}]_{\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{L})},

where we denote intervals as
[C_{\bullet},D_{\bullet}]_{\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{L})}=\{X_{\bullet}\in\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{L})\colon C_{\bullet}\leq X_{\bullet}\leq D_{\bullet}\}
. By
[
48
, Proposition 3.8.2]
, the Möbius function factorizes, and we conclude

-\mu_{\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{L})}(C_{\bullet},1_{\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{L})})=\prod_{i=0}^{r-1}\sum_{C_{\bullet}^{(i)}\leq D_{\bullet}^{(i)}}\mu_{\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{L})}(C_{\bullet}^{(i)},D_{\bullet}^{(i)}).

A chain
D_{\bullet}^{(i)}
that refines
C_{\bullet}^{(i)}
is nothing but a chain of elements that belong to the subset
J_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=[C_{i},C_{i+1}]_{\mathcal{L}}=\{X\in\mathcal{L}\colon C_{i}\leq X\leq C_{i+1}\}
of
\mathcal{L}
formed by the interval between
C_{i}
and
C_{i+1}
. Hence we can apply (
3.3
) in reverse to each factor, so that

-\mu_{\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{L})}(C_{\bullet},1_{\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{L})})=\prod_{i=0}^{r-1}\left(-\mu_{\mathscr{C}(J_{i})}(0_{\mathscr{C}(J_{i})},1_{\mathscr{C}(J_{i})})\right),

where
0_{\mathscr{C}(J_{i})}=C_{\bullet}^{(i)}
is the length one chain between the minimal and maximal elements of the lattice
J_{i}
. We conclude by
[
48
, Exercise §3.141]
, which states that

\mu_{\mathcal{L}}(0_{\mathcal{L}},1_{\mathcal{L}})=\mu_{\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{L})}(0_{\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{L})},1_{\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{L})}).

Namely, these two Möbius functions compute the Euler characteristics of the order complexes of the poset
\mathcal{L}^{\circ}
and its chain poset
\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{L})^{\circ}
(
Proposition

3.2
). The chains of
\mathcal{L}^{\circ}
label the simplices in the barycentric subdivision of
\Delta(\mathcal{L}^{\circ})
. Therefore,
\Delta(\mathcal{L}^{\circ})
and
\Delta(\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{L})^{\circ})
are homotopic and in particular share the same Euler characteristic.
∎

3.2
Lattice paths

To compute the Speyer polynomial of a Schubert matroid,
[
19
]
exploits a representation of Schubert matroids in terms of lattice paths. We review this description below, and then suggest an efficient method (
Algorithm

1
) to compute Speyer’s polynomial for any Schubert matroid.

A word
P\in\{\mathsf{N},\mathsf{E}\}^{\times}
in two letters
\mathsf{N}
(north) and
\mathsf{E}
(east) encodes a lattice path starting at the origin
(0,0)
with steps
\mathsf{N}=(0,1)
and
\mathsf{E}=(1,0)
. If
P
has
r
letters
\mathsf{N}
and
n
letters in total, then this path ends at
(n-r,r)
. All such paths are determined by the positions
I=\{i_{1}<\ldots<i_{r}\}
of the letters
\mathsf{N}
, such that
P=P(I)
where

P(I)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mathsf{E}^{i_{1}-1}\mathsf{N}\mathsf{E}^{i_{2}-i_{1}-1}\mathsf{N}\ldots\mathsf{E}^{i_{r}-i_{r-1}-1}\mathsf{N}\mathsf{E}^{n-i_{r}}.

This gives a bijection
I\mapsto P(I)
from subsets
I
of
\{1,\ldots,n\}
of size
r
, to lattice paths of length
n
with
r
letters
\mathsf{N}
.
Given another such subset
J=\{j_{1}<\ldots<j_{r}\}
, we write
P(J)\leq P(I)
if and only if
j_{k}\geq i_{k}
for all
1\leq k\leq r
. Visually, this condition dictates that the path
P(J)
stays on or below the path
P(I)
, but never protrudes above it; see
fig.

8
.

Figure 8:
The red lattice path
\mathsf{N}\mathsf{N}\mathsf{E}\mathsf{E}\mathsf{N}\mathsf{N}\mathsf{E}\mathsf{E}\mathsf{N}\mathsf{E}=P(I)
with
I=\{1,2,5,6,9\}
, passes on or above the blue lattice path
\mathsf{N}\mathsf{E}\mathsf{E}\mathsf{N}\mathsf{N}\mathsf{E}\mathsf{E}\mathsf{E}\mathsf{N}\mathsf{N}=P(J)
with
J=\{1,4,5,9,10\}
. Thus
P(J)\leq P(I)
, so
J
is one basis (of many) of the matroid
\mathsf{LM}\left(P(I)\right)
.

Definition 3.8
.

The
lattice path matroid

\mathsf{LM}\left(P\right)
for
P=P(I)\in\{\mathsf{N},\mathsf{E}\}^{n}
with
r
letters
\mathsf{N}
is the matroid of rank
r
on the ground set
\{1,\ldots,n\}
such that
J=\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{r}\}
is a basis of
\mathsf{LM}\left(P\right)
if and only if
P(J)\leq P(I)
.

Example 3.9
.

The path
P=\mathsf{N}^{r}\mathsf{E}^{n-r}=P(I)
for thus subset
I=\{1,\ldots,r\}
bounds (from above) the rectangle
[0,n-r]\times[0,r]
. Therefore,
P(J)\leq P(I)
is true for
every
subset
J
of
\{1,\ldots,n\}
with size
r
. Therefore,
\mathsf{LM}\left(\mathsf{N}^{r}\mathsf{E}^{n-r}\right)\cong\mathsf{U}^{n}_{r}
is the uniform matroid.

These lattice path matroids are indeed Schubert matroids: By collecting repeated consecutive letters, any lattice path
P
can be written uniquely in the form

P=\mathsf{E}^{a_{0}}\mathsf{N}^{b_{1}}\mathsf{E}^{a_{1}}\mathsf{N}^{b_{2}}\mathsf{E}^{a_{2}}\ldots\mathsf{N}^{b_{k}}\mathsf{E}^{a_{k}}\mathsf{N}^{b_{k+1}}

with integers
a_{0},b_{k+1}\geq 0
and
a_{1},b_{1},\ldots,a_{k},b_{k}\geq 1
. Then by
[
4
, Lemma 4.3]
, the matroid
\mathsf{LM}\left(P\right)
has precisely
k+1
cyclic flats
C_{0}<\ldots<C_{k}
, which form a chain. Concretely,
C_{i}=\{1,\ldots,m_{i}\}
corresponds to the initial segment of
P
of length
m_{i}
, from the origin to the point where the
i
th sequence
\mathsf{N}^{b_{i}}
of north steps begins; that is
m_{i}=a_{0}+\sum_{j\leq i}(a_{j}+b_{j})
. In particular, the loops (coloops) of
\mathsf{LM}\left(P\right)
are the first
a_{0}
(last
b_{k+1}
) edges.

Example 3.10
.

For the path
P=\mathsf{N}\mathsf{N}\mathsf{E}\mathsf{E}\mathsf{N}\mathsf{N}\mathsf{E}\mathsf{E}\mathsf{N}\mathsf{E}=P(\{1,2,5,6,9\})
from

and

. Hence the lattice path matroid

has four cyclic flats
\mathcal{Z}=\{C_{0},C_{1},C_{2},C_{3}\}
where

,

,

, and

.

Conversely, consider any Schubert matroid
\mathsf{SM}\left(C_{\bullet}\right)
with cyclic flats
C_{0}<\ldots<C_{k}
on the ground set
\{1,\ldots,n\}
. For the computation of Speyer’s polynomial, we may assume that
\mathsf{SM}\left(C_{\bullet}\right)
has neither loops nor coloops; that is,
C_{0}=\emptyset
and
C_{k}=\{1,\ldots,n\}
. Such a Schubert matroid is isomorphic to the lattice path matroid
\mathsf{LM}\left(P\right)\cong\mathsf{SM}\left(C_{\bullet}\right)
with

P=\mathsf{N}^{\rk(C_{1})}\mathsf{E}^{\ell(C_{1})}\mathsf{N}^{\rk(C_{2})-\rk(C_{1})}\mathsf{E}^{\ell(C_{2})-\ell(C_{1})}\ldots\mathsf{N}^{\rk(C_{k})-\rk(C_{k-1})}\mathsf{E}^{\ell(C_{k})-\ell(C_{k-1})}

(3.5)

where
\rk(C_{i})
and
\ell(C_{i})=\left|C_{i}\right|-\rk(C_{i})
denote the rank and corank (nullity), respectively, of a cyclic flat. Combined with
Theorem

3.5
, we can thus decompose the Speyer polynomial of any matroid (without loops or coloops) into Speyer polynomials of lattice path matroids.

The algorithm proposed in

Lemma 3.12

Proof.

The shorter paths

Proposition 3.21
.

For any matroid
M

(3.14)

Proof.

More generally, we show that the expression

\Phi(\mathcal{L})\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A,B\in\mathcal{L}\\
A\leq B\end{subarray}}\mu_{\mathcal{L}}(A,B)\ell(A)\rk(B)

takes the same value for
\mathcal{L}
any of the following lattices: cyclic flats
\mathcal{Z}_{M}
, all flats
\mathcal{F}_{M}
, and all subsets
\mathcal{P}_{M}
. These lattices are related by closure operators. The matroid closure

\cl\colon\mathcal{P}_{M}\longrightarrow\mathcal{F}_{M},\quad A\mapsto\cl(A)=\{e\in M\colon\rk(A\cup\{e\})=\rk(A)\}

projects the Boolean lattice
\mathcal{P}_{M}
onto the lattice
\mathcal{F}_{M}
of flats. Since
\rk(B)=\rk(\cl(B))
, we can collect the double lattice sum over
A\leq B
by
B^{\prime}=\cl(B)
to find

\Phi(\mathcal{P}_{M})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\in\mathcal{P}_{M},B^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}_{M}\\
A\leq B^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\ell(A)\rk(B^{\prime})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}B\in\mathcal{P}_{M}\\
A\leq B,\cl(B)=B^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\mu_{\mathcal{P}_{M}}(A,B)=\Phi(\mathcal{F}_{M}),

since by
Theorem

3.20
the sum over
B
produces
\mu_{\mathcal{F}_{M}}(A,B^{\prime})
and restricts the sum over
A
to flats. Similarly, we have a projection from flats to cyclic flats, given by the operator

\cyc\colon\mathcal{F}_{M}\longrightarrow\mathcal{Z}_{M},\quad A\mapsto\cyc(A)=\{e\in A\colon\rk(A\setminus\{e\})=\rk(A)\}.

This is a
downward
closure operator, fulfilling all the axioms of a closure operator but with
\cyc(A)\leq A
instead of
A\leq\cl(A)
. The corresponding version of (
3.13
) is that for all
A\in\mathcal{F}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\{A\in\mathcal{L}\colon\cyc(A)=A\}
and
B\in\mathcal{L}
with
A\leq B
,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}X\in\mathcal{L}\\
\cyc(X)=A,X\leq B\end{subarray}}\mu_{\mathcal{L}}(X,B)=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if $B\notin\mathcal{F}$,}\\
\mu_{\mathcal{F}}(A,B),&\text{if $B\in\mathcal{F}$.}\\
\end{cases}

Since
\ell(A)=\ell(A^{\prime})
for
A^{\prime}=\cyc(A)
, we can collect the sum over
A
in
\Phi
by
A^{\prime}
to conclude

\Phi(\mathcal{F}_{M})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}_{M},B\in\mathcal{F}_{M}\\
A^{\prime}\leq B\end{subarray}}\ell(A^{\prime})\rk(B)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\in\mathcal{F}_{M}\\
\cyc(A)=A^{\prime},A\leq B\end{subarray}}\mu_{\mathcal{F}_{M}}(A,B)=\Phi(\mathcal{Z}_{M}),

since the sum over
A
produces
\mu_{\mathcal{Z}_{M}}(A^{\prime},B)
and restricts the sum over
B
to cyclic flats

. This finishes the proof that

We exploit the recursive structure of

Our data set of small graphs (

along the sequence is itself a 3-connected, planar, simple graph. Since

is invariant under the series and parallel simplifications,

and the star-triangle identities

would have

connected components, then contracting each of these components to a single vertex produces a complete bipartite graph

is a minor of

---which would contradict planarity.

Each

must have edges to each of the 3 vertices

would not be 3-connected.

(1)

4.8
Connection to Feynman integrals

In perturbative quantum field theory, scattering amplitudes of particles are computed as a sum over graphs (Feynman diagrams). The contribution of each graph is determined by a (Feynman) integral. In the simplest cases, these integrals evaluate to real numbers
12
12

12

In general, the integrals are functions that depend on masses and momenta of the particles.
, called
Feynman periods

[
3
,
42
]
.

These Feynman periods are hard to compute and they are only defined for sufficiently connected graphs (otherwise the integral diverges). For example, let
G
be a cyclically 6-connected 4-regular graph, that is, a 4-regular graph which has no 4-edge cuts other than those that isolate one of the vertices. Then for any choice of vertex
v
, the Feynman period of the graph
G\setminus\{v\}
is well-defined. Various formulas for these Feynman periods, which we denote
\mathcal{P}(G\setminus\{v\})\in\mathbbm{R}
, can be found in
[
42
]
. For example,

\mathcal{P}(K_{5}\setminus\{v\})=\mathcal{P}\left(\vbox{\hbox{\includegraphics[scale]{graphs/w3A}}}\right)=6\zeta(3),\quad\mathcal{P}(C^{6}_{1,2}\setminus\{v\})=\mathcal{P}\left(\vbox{\hbox{\includegraphics[scale]{graphs/ws4A}}}\right)=20\zeta(5).

where
\zeta(s)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}1/n^{s}
is the Riemann zeta function.

Crucially for the link to
g_{G}(t)
, the Feynman period integrals are independent of the choice of the vertex
v
. Therefore, we may view these integrals as defining a function

G\mapsto\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}(G)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mathcal{P}(G\setminus\{v\})

on the set of cyclically 6-connected 4-regular graphs. This construction generalizes to cyclically 4-connected 3-regular graphs.
13
13

13

Feynman periods are defined for more general graphs, but we limit our attention to the cubic and quartic cases, which are the most interesting from the point of view of physics.

The Feynman period satisfies identities for twists and
3
-sums:

\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}(G)=\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}(G^{\prime})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}(G_{1}\oplus_{3}G_{2})=\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}(G_{1})\cdot\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}(G_{2})

in the notation of
1.15
and
1.18
; indeed the twist was discovered in this context
[
42
]
.
The invariant
\mathsf{N}_{2}(G)=-g_{G}^{\prime\prime}(-1)/2
obtained from Speyer’s polynomial
g_{G}(t)
thus behaves similar to the Feynman period. In fact, referring to the tables of periods for 4-regular graphs
[
40
]
and 3-regular graphs
[
6
]
, we confirmed the following conjecture for all graphs whose periods are known.

Conjecture 4.13
.

If two cyclically 6-connected 4-regular graphs (or cyclically 4-connected 3-regular graphs)
G_{1},G_{2}
have equal period
\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}(G_{1})=\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}(G_{2})
, then also
\mathsf{N}_{2}(G_{1})=\mathsf{N}_{2}(G_{2})
.

This claim goes beyond
1.15
and
1.18

G\setminus\{v\}
is planar, then its Feynman period
\mathcal{P}(G\setminus\{v\})=\mathcal{P}((G\setminus\{v\})^{\star})
equals that of the planar dual graph
(G\setminus\{v\})^{\star}
. Hence
4.13
implies in particular:

Conjecture 4.14
.

If two cyclically 6-connected 4-regular graphs
G_{1},G_{2}
have vertices
v_{i}\in V(G_{i})
so that
G_{i}\setminus\{v_{i}\}
are planar and dual to each other, then
\mathsf{N}_{2}(G_{1})=\mathsf{N}_{2}(G_{2})
.

We stress that this identity is unrelated to
1.13

need not be planar. An example of such a duality “after deleting a vertex” is shown in
[
41

.

Only a few other invariants of graphs are currently known that satisfy the identities of Feynman periods like

in
4.13

-invariant
[
43
]

[
14

13
]

[
38
]

[
41
]
. It would be very interesting to understand how these invariants are related to
\mathsf{N}_{2}(G)
. In particular, we expect:

Conjecture 4.15
.

For a cyclically 6-connected 4-regular (or cyclically 4-connected 3-regular) graph

\operatorname{\mathsf{M}}(G^{\bullet})
determines the value of
\mathsf{N}_{2}(G)

The Martin sequence is an integer sequence whose first entry, called

Our implementation is tailored towards graphs, but the code can be extended to other matroids by replacing/extending the function

in memory for all cyclic flats

, the primary driver of the runtime of

2

(I)

computing the lattice
\mathcal{Z}
, i.e. its elements and covering relations,

(II)

computing the Möbius function
\mu_{\mathcal{Z}}(A,B)
for all pairs
A<B
,

(III)

computing for each
B
and
k
the sum over
A<B
and
k^{\prime}
.

A direct implementation of
Algorithm

2
would use
\mathcal{O}(\left|\{(A,B)\in\mathcal{Z}_{M}^{2}\colon A\leq B\}\right|\cdot\rk(M)^{2})
polynomial additions and multiplications for (III), but the sum over
k^{\prime}
does not depend on the rank of
A
. Hence we first sum
\mu_{\mathcal{Z}}(A,B)\cdot\text{{{gRecursive(}}{\emph{$A,k^{\prime}$}}{{)}}}
over all
A
, grouped by the pairs
(k^{\prime},\ell(A))
. Then for each
B
, there are at most
\rk(B)
values of
k^{\prime}
and
\ell(B)
values of
\ell(A)
to consider. Due to this implementation, we reduced the complexity for (III) to
\mathcal{O}(\left|\{(A,B)\in\mathcal{Z}_{M}^{2}\colon A\leq B\}\right|\cdot\rk(M)+\left|\mathcal{Z}_{M}\right|\cdot\rk(M)\cdot\ell(M))
.

Asymptotically, the most time consuming parts of our implementation are therefore steps (I) and (II). We implemented (II) by computing once, for each pair
A<B
, the defininig recursion
\mu(A,B)=-\sum_{A\leq C<B}\mu(A,C)
. Hence we can estimate the time for step (II) by the number

\left|\{(A,C,B)\in\mathcal{Z}_{M}^{3}\colon A<C<B\}\right|

of 2-simplices in the order complex of
\mathcal{Z}
. Asymptotic improvements would thus require a faster method for Möbius inversion.
However, the integer additions for
\mu
are much quicker than the calculations with polynomials
gRecursive(
A
,
k^{\prime}
)
in step (III), such that in practice we often find that the time spent on steps (II) and (III) is comparable.

For very large graphs, also step (I) takes significant time, because our implementation effectively enumerates the lattice of all flats (and then filters out the cyclic ones), which can be exponentially larger than the lattice of only cyclic flats. In comparison, however, for the largest graphs we calculated, the runtime is dominated by step (II).
