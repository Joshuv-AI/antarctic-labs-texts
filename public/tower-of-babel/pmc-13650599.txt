The molecular landscape of nodular fasciitis: novel USP6 fusions, genomic stability, and an exploratory clinicopathological model – a 20‐year retrospective study
Authors: Kristijan Skok, Maximilian T Buchwald, Maria Anna Smolle, Theresa Godschachner, Andreas Leithner, Christian Viertler, Bernadette Liegl‐Atzwanger
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNodular fasciitis (NF) is a benign myofibroblastic neoplasm characterized by rapid growth and recurrent USP6 rearrangements. Despite its benign nature, NF may exhibit worrisome histologic or clinical features that mimic sarcoma, posing diagnostic challenges. The relationship between molecular diversity and morphologic heterogeneity remains incompletely understood. We retrospectively analyzed 283 NF cases diagnosed between 2000 and 2024 at a tertiary referral center. Molecular testing for USP6 rearrangements was performed in 44 cases using targeted RNA sequencing. Histologic features, clinical parameters, and fusion partner distribution were evaluated, with emphasis on lesions showing infiltrative or worrisome morphology. Selected cases underwent additional genomic profiling, including hybrid‐capture DNA sequencing and copy‐number analysis. Among 44 molecularly confirmed cases, 18 distinct USP6 fusion partners were identified. The most frequent fusion was MYH9::USP6 (40.9%), followed by COL1A1::USP6, MIR22HG::USP6, and RRBP1::USP6. Rare and previously unreported fusions, including CLK1::USP6 and FOSL2::USP6, further expanded the molecular spectrum. Two cases with infiltrative, aggressive‐appearing morphology showed no additional pathogenic alterations and a genomically stable profile. Based on these findings, we propose an exploratory, hypothesis‐generating framework to summarize the observed clinicopathological spectrum of NF. NF demonstrates substantial molecular heterogeneity but retains a genetically simple and largely benign biological behavior. Even lesions with alarming morphology appear genomically stable and clinically indolent. The proposed framework may facilitate diagnostic interpretation and provide a conceptual framework for future studies but requires validation before any biological or clinical implications can be inferred.

Introduction

Nodular fasciitis (NF) represents a benign mesenchymal neoplasm that typically arises within the superficial soft tissues of young adults [1]. NF was at first described as a pseudosarcomatous lesion in the 1960s [2]. Due to its rapid growth, NF may clinically resemble a malignant neoplasm; however, it is a benign lesion that may regress spontaneously [3, 4]. The upper extremities, the head and neck region, and the trunk constitute the most common localizations, although virtually any anatomical site can be affected [3, 5, 6]. Moreover, histologically it can imitate a malignant process due to high cellularity, frequent mitotic figures, and a focal infiltrative pattern. Diagnostic challenges occur mainly in the evaluation of infiltrative lesions or in small tissue biopsies [7]. Molecular investigations over the past decade have revealed that the great majority of NF lesions, estimated at more than 90%, harbor a rearrangement of the USP6 (ubiquitin‐specific protease 6) gene, resulting in overexpression of the encoded deubiquitinating enzyme [3, 8]. USP6 rearrangements have also been identified in several other benign mesenchymal lesions, collectively referred to as USP6‐associated neoplasms, including aneurysmal bone cyst (ABC) [9], myositis ossificans (MO), fibro‐osseous pseudotumor (FOP) of the digits, cranial fasciitis, and fibroma of the tendon sheath [10, 11, 12, 13]. They typically harbor characteristic gene fusions in which the USP6 coding region is placed under the control of various partner gene promoters, resulting in transcriptional upregulation of USP6 [12]. Identification of USP6 rearrangement has become an important diagnostic adjunct, especially in small tissue samples where morphology alone may be inconclusive, thereby helping to prevent unnecessary aggressive treatment [7]. Several USP6 fusion partners have been reported, with MYH9 being the most frequent. Additional partners include RRBP1, MIR22HG, COL1A1, COL1A2, COL3A1, COL6A2, PAFAH1B1, CALD1, CALU, EIF5A, EMP1, NR1D1, SPARC, SERPINH1, THBS1, THBS2, SREBF1, PPP6R3, STAG1, among others [14]. To date, more than 25 fusion partners have been identified, all resulting in transcriptional upregulation of USP6 through promoter swapping [7].

This retrospective single‐center study was undertaken to characterize the clinicopathological and molecular spectrum of NF, with particular emphasis on lesions demonstrating pronounced infiltrative growth. The study aimed to investigate whether these uncommon infiltrative presentations were associated with additional molecular alterations. Considering prior reports of malignant behavior in morphologically classic lesions, we also systematically reviewed our archival material to identify comparable cases. Based on our clinicopathological and molecular observations, we propose a conceptual, hypothesis‐generating framework summarizing the observed clinicopathological spectrum.

Materials and methods

Case selection

A comprehensive search of our institutional database was performed to identify both in‐house and consultation cases with a confirmed diagnosis of NF from 2000 to 2024. In total, 283 NF cases were retrieved. In 44 cases molecular analysis was performed and demonstrated a USP6 gene rearrangement. Molecular testing was not performed consecutively in all NF cases but was requested as part of routine diagnostic work‐up in cases where the diagnosis remained uncertain after conventional histopathological and immunohistochemical evaluation. Indications for RNA sequencing included unusual histomorphology, infiltrative growth, atypical anatomical location, limited biopsy material, discordance between morphology and clinical or radiological findings, or a differential diagnosis including malignant spindle‐cell neoplasms. To provide molecular context for the spectrum of USP6‐associated neoplasms, we additionally reviewed 12 non‐NF USP6‐rearranged lesions, comprising ABC (n = 8), MO (n = 2), fasciitis ossificans (FO; n = 1), and one lesion morphologically classified proliferative myositis‐like (PM; n = 1) identified in our institutional archives. These cases were analyzed descriptively and were not included in the primary analyses of NF. As the pathology institute functions as the national reference center for sarcoma pathology in Austria, nearly half of the cases (48.4%; 137/283) were consultation specimens referred from external institutions, highlighting the considerable diagnostic challenges associated with these tumors, particularly when evaluated on biopsy material (EK 1042/2025 and 32‐222 ex 19/20) (supplementary material, Table S1). Of the 44 molecularly investigated cases, 31 (70.5%) were consultation cases from external institutions, 6 (13.6%) originated from regional cooperation partners, and 7 (15.9%) represented cases from the Medical University of Graz (University Hospital).

Not all infiltrative lesions underwent molecular testing, particularly among older archival cases diagnosed before the routine implementation of RNA sequencing. However, as none of these cases showed recurrence, further retrospective molecular testing was not considered informative. The infiltrative cases included in the present study therefore reflect those for which molecular analysis was clinically indicated.

Clinical and pathological data collection

Clinical, histopathological, and surgical data were retrieved from institutional archives and consultation reports. Extracted variables included age, sex, anatomical site, lesion size, surgical approach, and recurrence status. Histopathological parameters comprised matrix composition, growth pattern, cellular morphology, mitotic activity, and the presence of extravasated erythrocytes. Cases without molecular testing contributed to the description of the overall retrospective NF cohort. All tissue samples were formalin‐fixed paraffin‐embedded (FFPE). Diagnoses were established on hematoxylin–eosin (H&E) stained sections and supported by immunohistochemical profiling [strong expression of smooth muscle actin (SMA)] and molecular analysis.

Histological review

Histologic material was obtained either through surgical excision or biopsy. All available slides were independently reviewed by at least three participating pathologists (BL‐A, CV, TG, and KS). Discrepant interpretations were resolved by joint review and consensus. The review included assessment of surgical margins, growth pattern, infiltrative growth pattern, cellular morphology, matrix quality, presence of extravasated erythrocytes, lesion size, and mitotic figures. Molecular analysis

USP6 rearrangements were investigated by targeted RNA sequencing. RNA was extracted using the Maxwell RSC RNA FFPE Kit on a Maxwell RSC 48 Instrument (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) and quantified using a NanoDrop ND‐1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Anchored multiplex PCR‐based libraries were prepared initially using the in‐house‐modified Archer FusionPlex MUG Sarcoma Panel v4 (Diagnostic and Research Institute of Pathology, Medical University of Graz, Graz, Austria) and subsequently using the FusionPlex Sarcoma v2 or Pan Solid Tumor v2 panels for Ion Torrent (Integrated DNA Technologies, Coralville, IA, USA). Following quantification with the Ion Library TaqMan Quantitation Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific), template preparation and chip loading were performed on the Ion Chef System, followed by sequencing on an Ion S5 XL or Ion GeneStudio S5 Prime instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Selected infiltrative cases underwent additional DNA profiling following extraction using established protocols [15]. Libraries were enriched by hybrid capture using the Agilent_MUG_Solid_DNAV6 panel, covering approximately 800 genes (~3.5 Mb; Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA), and sequenced on a DNBSEQ‐T7 sequencer (Wuhan MGI Tech Co., Ltd., Wuhan, Hubei, China). Variants were identified using the institutional bioinformatics pipeline. Microarray‐based copy‐number analysis (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA; Sentrix identifiers: 209503260096_R08C01 and 209503260096_R07C01) was additionally performed, and the resulting data were evaluated using the EpiDiP Methylome & CNV Viewer (J. Hench, IfP Basel, Basel, Switzerland, version 8.0c; https://epidip.org/sh/apps/allMethylomesCNV08c/; accessed February 2026).

Clinical data analysis

Clinical records from involved specialties were reviewed to obtain data on localization, surgical approach, date of diagnosis, recurrence, last follow‐up, age, and sex.

Statistical analyses

Descriptive statistics were computed using IBM SPSS Statistics, Version 25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables are reported as means, medians, and ranges, while categorical variables are presented as absolute counts and percentages. Given the descriptive and exploratory nature of the study and the limited sample sizes in subgroups, no formal assessment of data distribution or inferential statistical analyses were performed.

Results

Clinical and molecular characteristics

The cohort comprised 283 cases of NF. Patients' age ranged from 1 to 64 years, with a median age of 31.5 years at diagnosis. There was a near‐equal sex distribution, with 143 females and 140 males. A second search of the database, specifically looking at the USP6‐rearranged mesenchymal neoplasms, showed 56 cases. Nodular fasciitis

NF patients ranged in age from 1 to 64 years (median: 31.5 years), with an almost equal sex distribution (22 males and 22 females) (Table 1). Lesions most involved the trunk region (12; 27.3%), upper extremity (12; 27.3%), followed by the head and neck region (10; 22.7%) and lower extremity (9; 20.5%). The median lesion size was 1.8 cm (range: 0.8–5 cm). NF demonstrated the greatest diversity of USP6 fusion partners, with 18 distinct partner genes identified among 44 cases. MYH9::USP6 was the most frequent fusion (18/44; 40.9%), followed by COL1A1::USP6 (5/44; 11.4%), MIR22HG::USP6 (3/44; 6.8%), and RRBP1::USP6 (3/44; 6.8%). ASPN::USP6 was identified in two cases (2/44; 4.5%), whereas the remaining 13 fusion partners were each detected in single cases (Figures 1, 2; supplementary material, Figure S1).

Case 2: FOSL2::USP6 fusion

A 31‐year‐old female presented with a soft‐tissue lesion of the upper extremity (arm). Histologic examination (Figure 4) revealed fibrous and adipose tissue with an infiltrative spindle cell proliferation, arranged in fascicles and embedded in a predominantly myxoid stroma. The spindle cells showed round to elongated oval nuclei with bipolar basophilic cytoplasmic processes. Cellular density was variable, ranging from moderately to highly cellular areas. A markedly increased mitotic activity was observed, with up to 15 mitoses per 10 high‐power fields, while atypical mitotic figures were not identified. The lesion was traversed by a delicate capillary vascular network, and the spindle cells showed infiltration between adjacent adipocytes. Immunohistochemically, the lesional cells demonstrated diffuse SMA positivity, while the rest of the performed IHC stains remained negative, and H3K27me3 retained. Molecular analysis identified a FOSL2::USP6 fusion.

Molecular diagnostics from both cases

Detection of USP6 rearrangements was successfully achieved in both cases using the Archer® FusionPlex Sarcoma v4 (Diagnostic and Research Institute of Pathology) (in‐house kit). Case 1 (ASPN::USP6 fusion) demonstrated a high fusion abundance, with 95.1% of USP6‐aligned reads supporting the fusion event (969 reads; 138 unique start sites). In contrast, Case 2 showed an RRBP1::USP6 fusion with 18.7% fusion‐positive USP6 reads (72 reads; 40 start sites), correlating with its substantially better DNA integrity. Subsequent hybrid‐capture DNA sequencing (≈3.5 Mb panel, 800 genes) identified no pathogenic somatic mutations in either case. Likewise, copy‐number variation (CNV) analysis revealed no chromosomal gains or losses indicative of structural genomic instability. Overall, both lesions exhibited a genetically stable diploid profile.

DNA methylation‐based clustering consistent with NF

Both cases underwent DNA methylation microarray analysis, from which copy‐number profiles and unsupervised methylation‐based embeddings were derived [16]. In both profiles, no significant copy‐number gains or losses were detected, apart from minor stochastic variations considered within normal analytical noise. Unsupervised clustering positioned both cases firmly within the NF molecular reference region, closely aligning with multiple canonical USP6‐rearranged NF cases in the database.

Non‐NF USP6‐associated entities

In contrast to NF, the non‐NF USP6‐associated lesions demonstrated a more restricted fusion spectrum. ABC (n = 8) occurred exclusively in osseous sites and harbored FAT1::USP6 (n = 2), OMD::USP6 (n = 2), and single cases with ACTG1::USP6, CDH11::USP6, SAR1A::USP6, and TNC::USP6. MO (n = 2) showed COL1A1::USP6 in both cases. FO (n = 1) and PM‐like lesion (n = 1) also revealed COL1A1::USP6 fusions. No aggressive recurrences or metastatic behavior were observed in any of these lesions during the available follow‐up.

Discussion

The present study expands the clinicopathological and molecular understanding of NF while providing descriptive context regarding other USP6‐associated lesions encountered during the database review. A key aspect of the study is the detailed morphologic and genomic characterization of selected NF cases with distinctly infiltrative, ‘aggressive‐appearing’ morphology.

Our findings support three principal observations. First, the two NF cases with distinctly infiltrative and otherwise alarming morphology remained genomically simple and clinically indolent following comprehensive molecular characterization. Second, the molecularly investigated cohort demonstrated substantial diversity of USP6 fusion partners, including rare and previously unreported fusions. Third, the broader retrospective cohort confirmed the predominantly benign clinical behavior of NF.

The following four‐class framework should be regarded as an exploratory conceptual model for the morphologic categorization of USP6‐rearranged NF. It is intended to organize the clinicopathologic spectrum observed in the present cohort and reported in the literature rather than to define biologically distinct entities. The proposed subclasses integrate recurring morphologic patterns with currently reported genetic alterations that may provide additional biological context and inform future refinement of this framework following validation in larger, independent cohorts (Table 3).

Class 1 comprises morphologically conventional NF, which represents the predominant clinicopathological phenotype and is characterized by classic histomorphology and typically benign, self‐limited biological behavior. In the vast majority of cases, the combination of characteristic morphology and an appropriate clinical context is sufficient for diagnosis. A small subset of otherwise conventional lesions may, however, present with clinically alarming features, including rapid enlargement, pain, neurological symptoms, site‐related functional impairment, or, rarely, early local recurrence [17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22]. The diagnostic challenges described in the literature include lesions arising at uncommon or anatomically sensitive sites, such as the periorbital soft tissues, a peripheral nerve, the joint synovium, the buccal mucosa, and the palm, where NF may clinically or radiologically mimic a malignant neoplasm [17, 18, 19, 20, 21]. In addition, rapidly enlarging lesions may produce substantial local morbidity despite entirely conventional histomorphology, as illustrated by the palmar NF associated with nerve‐compression symptoms reported by Sawamura et al [21]. Rare recurrence after apparently complete excision has also been documented in oral NF [22]; incomplete removal and persistence of an actively proliferating lesion may plausibly contribute to recurrence. Overall, these presentations are more likely to reflect the dynamic growth characteristics, anatomical setting, and local effects of NF than true biological progression. In clinicopathologically discordant or otherwise challenging cases, demonstration of a USP6 rearrangement in combination with classic morphology may therefore increase diagnostic confidence, facilitate appropriate classification, distinguish conventional NF from malignant or site‐specific mimics, and reduce the risk of unnecessary aggressive treatment [17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22].

Class 2 comprises NF with entirely conventional histomorphology but potential for aggressive biological behavior. This concept is based on a very limited number of reported cases in which morphologically typical NF exhibited an unexpected clinical course characterized by infiltrative growth, progressive erosion of the adjacent bone, repeated local recurrence, or, exceptionally, distant metastasis [14, 23, 24]. Two independent reports identified tumors harboring a PPP6R3::USP6 fusion [23, 24], while Saoud et al additionally described aggressive NF with a COL1A1::USP6 fusion and one further case with a PPP6R3::USP6 fusion [14]. In the index case reported by Guo et al, the unusual clinical behavior was accompanied by amplification of both PPP6R3 and USP6, resulting in increased USP6 expression, suggesting that secondary genomic alterations, rather than the fusion partner alone, may contribute to the altered biological phenotype [23]. However, comprehensive genomic profiling was unavailable in most reported cases [14, 24], and the current evidence is insufficient to establish a causal or prognostic association between specific USP6 fusion partners and aggressive behavior. Nevertheless, morphologically conventional NF presenting with an unusually deep‐seated location, infiltrative growth, progressive bone erosion, repeated recurrence, or metastatic disease should prompt comprehensive molecular characterization extending beyond fusion detection and warrant close clinical follow‐up to further define this rare clinicopathological subset.

Class 3 comprises NF with an unusual infiltrative growth pattern. These lesions are characterized by distinctly infiltrative margins partly in combination with multinodular architecture, prominent vascular proliferation, and variable mitotic activity while lacking overt cytologic atypia or atypical mitotic figures. The two cases in our series exemplify this pattern and both followed an indolent clinical course. We showed that despite their worrisome morphology, molecular profiling including mutation analysis, copy‐number assessment, and DNA methylation profiling, demonstrated a genomically stable profile beyond the USP6 rearrangement. Furthermore, methylation profiling clustered both tumors with conventional NF, whereas low‐grade myofibroblastic sarcoma (LGMS) occupied distinct regions of the methylation embedding (https://epidip.org/; Methylome & CNV Viewer, J. Hench, IfP Basel, version 8.0c; 02/2026), supporting classification of these lesions within the spectrum of USP6‐rearranged NF.

Likewise, Malik et al [25] reported three deep‐seated pediatric myofibroblastic tumors harboring COL1A1::USP6 or COL3A1::USP6 fusions that displayed infiltrative growth and clinically alarming features but lacked overt malignant cytology and behaved benignly following conservative excision [25]. Wang et al [26] further described a large intramuscular NF with infiltrative borders, increased mitotic activity (not more than 10 mitoses/HPF), and pseudosarcomatous morphology that raised suspicion for fibrosarcoma; however, demonstration of a USP6 rearrangement confirmed the diagnosis, and no recurrence or metastasis developed during 56 months of follow‐up [26]. Collectively, these observations indicate that infiltrative growth, increased mitotic activity, deep location, or locally aggressive clinical and radiological features do not necessarily predict aggressive biological behavior in USP6‐rearranged NF. In such diagnostically challenging cases expanding the molecular profile of these rare but morphologically distinctive tumors will improve classification and distinction between class 2 and 3.

Class 4 includes USP6‐rearranged lesions with frankly atypical or sarcoma‐like cytologic features (e.g. pleomorphism, marked nuclear atypia, atypical mitoses) but without proven malignant biological behavior [27, 28, 29, 30, 31, 32] or any specific genetic change. A small subset of these USP6‐rearranged lesions may in addition show infiltrative growth. Pediatric cases of NF with strikingly atypical or overtly malignant‐appearing morphology have been reported, including lesions with marked pleomorphism, increased cellularity, atypical mitotic figures, ulceration, and other features that may closely mimic sarcoma. Despite these concerning histologic findings, recurrent USP6 rearrangements (e.g. CALD1::USP6, PAFAH1B1::USP6, COL6A2::USP6, and EIF5A::USP6) support their interpretation as part of the spectrum of USP6‐associated lesions and underscore the potential risk of overtreatment.

With respect to the morphomolecular distribution of USP6 fusion partners and their apparent anatomical tropism, our findings partially corroborate those of Cordier et al [12]. Both cohorts demonstrate considerable molecular heterogeneity, with multiple fusion partners occurring across different anatomical sites. MYH9::USP6 was the most consistently shared fusion, occurring in nearly all anatomical regions in both studies. Additional overlapping fusion partners included COL1A1 in the hand, COL6A1 and CTNNB1 in the head and neck region, ASPN in the thigh, and PAFAH1B1 in the arm. The trunk and head and neck region exhibited the greatest diversity of fusion partners in our cohort, whereas the extremities showed less overlap and a higher proportion of unique fusions. The trunk shared only MYH9::USP6 between both cohorts, while the head and neck region additionally shared CTNNB1::USP6 and COL6A2::USP6. Furthermore, MYH9::USP6 was identified in upper‐leg lesions in our cohort but not in the series reported by Cordier et al [12] (Figure 2). The reasons for these regional differences remain unclear. They may reflect biological variation related to the greater anatomical and mesenchymal diversity of the trunk and head and neck region, but sampling effects cannot be excluded. Beyond NF, our cohort also included other USP6‐associated neoplasms, including MO, FOP, ABC, and PM‐like lesion. Among these entities, COL1A1::USP6 was identified in FOP, MO, PM‐, and NF, making it the only fusion partner shared across multiple tumor types as well as between both cohorts. Although PM has historically been considered closely related to NF, recent molecular findings suggest a more heterogeneous pathogenesis (FOS rearrangement). In this context, the COL1A1::USP6‐positive PM‐like lesion in our cohort may further illustrate the morphological overlap within the expanding spectrum of USP6‐rearranged lesions.

Based on our findings and the available literature, additional molecular characterization beyond USP6 fusion detection should be considered for NF‐like lesions with infiltrative growth mimicking low‐grade myofibroblastic sarcoma (class 3), lesions with worrisome sarcoma‐like cytologic features (class 4), recurrent tumors, cases with discordant clinicoradiologic and histopathologic findings irrespective of morphologic class, and lesions harboring rare USP6 fusion partners that have been reported in association with aggressive clinical behavior (class 2). Such analyses may help identify additional genomic alterations that contribute to unusual infiltrative growth, marked cytologic atypia, or exceptional clinical behavior. Although the available evidence remains limited, the rare, reported cases with aggressive biological behavior have also demonstrated additional genomic alterations beyond the primary USP6 rearrangement, suggesting that secondary molecular events may contribute to biological progression in a subset of USP6‐associated neoplasms. Additional well‐characterized cases with comprehensive genomic profiling and long‐term clinical follow‐up will be essential to refine risk stratification, better define biological boundaries within the spectrum of USP6‐associated neoplasms, and inform future iterations of tumor classification systems. Based on the currently available clinicopathological and molecular evidence, we propose that these lesions should remain within the spectrum of USP6‐associated neoplasms despite their morphologic overlap with low‐grade myofibroblastic sarcoma (Table 4).

This study has several limitations. Molecular testing was performed according to routine diagnostic indications rather than systematically across the entire cohort. Consequently, the molecular subset was enriched for consultation cases and diagnostically challenging lesions and should not be regarded as representative of the full clinicopathological spectrum of NF. The proposed framework is therefore exploratory and requires validation in independently assembled cohorts. We believe that rather than a binary benign–malignant framework, these lesions warrant a workup in which integrated morphologic, clinical, and molecular assessment is critical for accurate diagnosis. Future studies will determine whether specific molecular alterations carry prognostic significance. Taken together, the proposed four‐class framework should be regarded as an exploratory conceptual model summarizing the clinicopathological spectrum of NF. Future multicenter studies with systematic molecular characterization are required to determine whether any of the proposed subclasses carry biological or clinical significance.
