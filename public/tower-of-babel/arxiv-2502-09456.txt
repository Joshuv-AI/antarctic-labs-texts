A Cut-free Sequent Calculus for Basic Intuitionistic Dynamic Topological Logic
Amirhossein Akbar Tabatabai, Majid Alizadeh, Alireza Mahmoudian

Abstract
As part of a broader family of logics, [ 1 , 3 ] introduced two key logical systems: 𝗂𝖪 𝖽 \mathsf{iK_{d}} , which encapsulates the basic logical structure of dynamic topological systems, and 𝗂𝖪 𝖽 ∗ \mathsf{iK_{d*}} , which provides a well-behaved yet sufficiently general framework for an abstract notion of implication. These logics have been thoroughly examined through their algebraic, Kripke-style, and topological semantics. To complement these investigations with their missing proof-theoretic analysis, this paper introduces a cut-free G3-style sequent calculus for 𝗂𝖪 𝖽 \mathsf{iK_{d}} and 𝗂𝖪 𝖽 ∗ \mathsf{iK_{d*}} . Using these systems, we demonstrate that they satisfy the disjunction property and, more broadly, admit a generalization of Visser’s rules. Additionally, we establish that 𝗂𝖪 𝖽 \mathsf{iK_{d}} enjoys the Craig interpolation property and that its sequent system possesses the deductive interpolation property. Keywords: sequent calculus, cut elimination, disjunction property, Visser’s rules, Craig interpolation, deductive interpolation, dynamic topological systems

Abstract Implications

The logical realm encompasses a wide variety of implications, ranging from Boolean and Heyting implications to sub-intuitionistic implications

, and various types of conditionals

, to name a few.
Efforts have been made to identify an overarching notion of

that unifies some or all of these variants
[

,

]
. Here, we adopt the abstract notion of implication proposed in
[
1
,
3
]
, interpreting implications as the logical mechanism to
internalize
the meta-linguistic provability order
A\vdash B
between propositions into the proposition
A\to B
within the formal language itself.

Definition 1
.

Let
\mathsf{A}=(A,\wedge,1)
be a bounded meet semi-lattice and
\leq
be its meet semi-lattice order. A binary order-preserving operation
\to
from
(A,\leq)^{op}\times(A,\leq)
to
(A,\leq)
is called an
implication
, if we have

a\to a=1
and

(a\to b)\wedge(b\to c)\leq a\to c
,
for any
a,b,c\in A
.

This notion of implication is highly general, and its broad scope proves useful in establishing
negative
results regarding the non-existence of certain types of implication—such as the non-triviality of geometric implications
[
2
]
. However, compared to Heyting implication, these abstract implications tend to be mathematically ill-behaved
[
1
,
3
]
. In
[
1
,
3
]
, it is suggested that the ill-behaved nature of abstract implication stems from the absence of an adjunction—a fundamental feature of Heyting implication. For a comprehensive argument, we refer the reader to
[
3
]
. Here, it suffices to emphasize that the adjunction of which Heyting implication is a part is nothing but a complete pair of introduction and elimination rules for the intuitionistic implication. Philosophically, having such a pair means having a complete determination of the proof-theoretic
meaning
of intuitionistic implication, while mathematically, it leads to the elegant proof theory of intuitionistic logic.

To restore this missing adjunction,
[
1
,
3
]
introduced a new algebraic concept, called a
\nabla
-algebra:

Definition 2
.

A
\nabla
-algebra
is a structure
(\mathsf{A},\nabla,\to)
, where
\mathsf{A}
is a bounded lattice and
\rightarrow
and
\nabla
are binary and unary operations on
\mathsf{A}
, respectively, such that:

\nabla c\wedge a\leq b\quad\text{iff}\quad c\leq a\rightarrow b

for any
a,b,c\in\mathsf{A}
.
It follows from this definition that
\nabla
is monotone and join-preserving. A
\nabla
-algebra is called
normal
if
\nabla
preserves all
finite
meets. It is called
Heyting
, if
\mathsf{A}
is a Heyting algebra.

\nabla
-algebras provide a unifying framework that generalizes both bounded lattices (
\nabla a=0
and
a\to b=1
) and Heyting algebras (
\nabla a=a
and
a\to b=a\supset b
, where
\supset
denotes the Heyting implication). Additionally, they encompass
temporal Heyting algebras
. To illustrate, in any Heyting
\nabla
-algebra, if we denote the Heyting implication by
\supset
and define
\Box a
as
\top\to a
, we obtain the identity
a\to b=\Box(a\supset b)
. Thus, the binary operation
\to
reduces to the unary operator
\Box
, making a Heyting
\nabla
-algebra effectively a Heyting algebra enriched with the adjunction
\nabla\dashv\Box
.

This structure naturally lends itself to a temporal interpretation:
\nabla a
can be understood as “
a
was true at some point in the past” while
\Box a
expresses that “
a
will be true at all points in the future.” The adjunction
\nabla\dashv\Box
then corresponds to the well-known relationship between the
existential past
modality and the
universal future
modality. This temporal perspective can be rigorously formulated within the framework of intuitionistic Kripke frames. For the connection with Heyting
\nabla
-algebras, see
[
3
]
.

Returning to implications, in
[
1
,
3
]
, it is proven that in any
\nabla
-algebra, the operator
\to
is an implication in the sense of Definition
1
, and any abstract implication can be represented as an implication of a normal
\nabla
-algebra, up to a correcting term. Therefore, one can argue that the presence of the adjunction in
\nabla
-algebras ensures a well-behaved family of implications, while the representation theorem guaranties the generality of this well-behaved family. Consequently,
studying normal
\nabla
-algebras offers a mathematical strategy for analyzing all possible implications.

Dynamic Topological Systems

A
dynamic topological system
is a topological space interpreted as the
state space
of a system, augmented with a continuous function that represents the system’s
dynamism

[
5
]
. To make this non-elementary notion more accessible to logical analysis, several modal approaches have been proposed
[
7
,
13
,
9
]
, using either
\mathsf{S4}
or
\mathsf{IPC}
to represent the state space, along with some modalities to capture the dynamism. Here, we adopt a basic yet conceptual approach, as outlined below.

First, note that it is beneficial to reformulate the definition of dynamic topological systems in a point-free manner—focusing on open subsets rather than points, since propositions correspond to open sets in a direct manner.
The point-free counterpart of a topological space is a
locale

\mathscr{X}
, which is a complete lattice (i.e., a lattice that has suprema and infima for all its subsets and is necessarily bounded) where the binary meet operation distributes over arbitrary joins. We can think of this lattice as the lattice of the open subsets of our point-free space. Similarly, the point-free analogue of a continuous function is a
localic map
, which is a function
f^{*}:\mathscr{Y}\to\mathscr{X}
between locales that preserves finite meets and arbitrary joins. The localic map
f^{*}:\mathscr{Y}\to\mathscr{X}
can be interpreted as the inverse image of the point-free continuous map
f
from the point-free space
\mathscr{X}
to the point-free space
\mathscr{Y}
. Combining these ideas, a
dynamic locale
is a locale
\mathscr{X}
equipped with a localic map
f^{*}:\mathscr{X}\to\mathscr{X}
.

This definition is clearly non-elementary, as it refers to arbitrary joins. To make it elementary and hence more logic-friendly, one should replace join-preservation with adjoint operators. Therefore, the fact that binary meet preserves arbitrary joins is transformed into the existence of a right adjoint for the binary meet—a connective known as the Heyting implication. Similarly, the condition that
f^{*}
preserves arbitrary joins must be replaced with the right adjoint to
f^{*}
, denoted by
f_{*}
. This suggests the following structures as the algebraic and elementary version of a dynamic topological system:
(\mathsf{H},f^{*},f_{*})
, where
\mathsf{H}
is a Heyting algebra,
f^{*},f_{*}:\mathsf{H}\to\mathsf{H}
, with
f^{*}\dashv f_{*}
and
f^{*}
preserves all finite meets. This structure is nothing but a normal Heyting
\nabla
-algebra, where
f^{*}=\nabla
and
f_{*}=\Box
. Therefore,
the study of normal Heyting
\nabla
-algebras corresponds to the algebraic study of dynamic topological systems.

Logics
\mathsf{iK_{d}}
and
\mathsf{iK_{d*}}

Motivated by the role of
\nabla
-algebras in representing implications, generalizing (temporal) Heyting algebras, and formalizing dynamic topological systems in an elementary way,
[
1
,
3
,
4
]
have studied various algebraic, topological, and logical aspects of
\nabla
-algebras in a series of papers.
In particular,
[
1
,
4
]
introduced two specific logics, namely the logic
\mathsf{iK_{d}}
for normal Heyting
\nabla
-algebras and its Heyting implication-free fragment,
\mathsf{iK_{d*}}
, for normal
\nabla
-algebras.
2
2

2

These logics are referred to as
\mathsf{STL(N,H)}
and
\mathsf{STL(N)}
, respectively in
[
3
]
.
The former can be interpreted as the
basic intuitionistic dynamic topological logic
, while the latter serves as a logic for sufficiently general yet well-behaved implications. In
[
1
,
4
]
, these systems were extensively investigated from algebraic and topological perspectives; however, a proof-theoretic approach remained absent due to the lack of a cut-free sequent calculus for these logics.

In this paper, we aim to fill this gap by introducing the cut-free sequent calculi
\mathbf{iK_{d}}
and
\mathbf{iK_{d*}}
for the logics
\mathsf{iK_{d}}
and
\mathsf{iK_{d*}}
, respectively. We then use these systems to demonstrate that both
\mathsf{iK_{d}}
and
\mathsf{iK_{d*}}
enjoy the disjunction property and, more generally, admit a generalization of Visser’s rules. Additionally, we establish that
\mathsf{iK_{d}}
satisfies the Craig interpolation property and that its sequent system possesses the deductive interpolation property.

The structure of the paper is as follows. In Section
2
, we recall some preliminary notions and the definition of a sequent system for the logics
\mathsf{iK_{d}}
and
\mathsf{iK_{d*}}
, as presented in
[
1
]
.
In Section
3
, we introduce our cut-free sequent systems and investigate their basic properties, including inversion lemmas, the admissibility of contraction, and the deduction theorem. We also prove the equivalence between these systems and those introduced in
[
1
]
, assuming the admissibility of cut in our systems.
In Section
4
, we establish the admissibility of cut as promised. Then, in Section
5

Finally, in Section
6

\mathsf{iK_{d}}
satisfies the Craig interpolation property and that its sequent system enjoys the deductive interpolation property.

Consider the language
\mathcal{L}=\{\top,\bot,\wedge,\vee,\supset,\to,\nabla\}
, where

. Furthermore, unless explicitly stated otherwise, the term “formula” always refers to an

-formula.
We denote by

.

Capital Greek letters such as

.

The next property to consider is
\nabla
-analyticity
.
The systems
\mathbf{iK_{d}}
and
\mathbf{iK_{d*}}
are not analytic. For example, in the premise of the rule

is a subformula of a formula in the conclusion. In other words, every formula in any premise is a subformula of a formula in the conclusion, up to additional
\nabla

\nabla
-analyticity captures nearly all of the power associated with analyticity. For instance, a direct implication of the
\nabla
-analyticity of
\mathbf{iK_{d}}
is the

of the calculus
\mathbf{iK_{d}}
over the calculus
\mathbf{iK_{d*}}

Lemma 2

\mathbf{iK_{d*}}\vdash_{h}\Gamma\Rightarrow\Delta
iff
\mathbf{iK_{d}}\vdash_{h}\Gamma\Rightarrow\Delta
, for any sequent
\Gamma\Rightarrow\Delta
over
\mathcal{L}_{*}
.

Proof.

The left-to-right direction is straightforward, as
\mathbf{iK_{d}}
includes
\mathbf{iK_{d*}}
. For the converse, we leverage the
\nabla
-analyticity of
\mathbf{iK_{d}}
. Since the sequent
\Gamma\Rightarrow\Delta
contains no occurrence of the connective

(L\!\supset^{n})
or
(R\!\supset)
. As a result, the derivation remains entirely within the system

, where these rules are not present.

3.3
Deduction Theorem

Let
\mathbf{LJ}^{+}
be the calculus
\mathbf{LJ}
extended with the cut rule, and recall the
deduction theorem
stating the equivalence between
\Rightarrow A\vdash_{\mathbf{LJ}^{+}}\,\Rightarrow B
and
\vdash_{\mathbf{LJ}}A\Rightarrow B
. This form of the deduction theorem does not hold for
\mathbf{iK_{d}}
and
\mathbf{iK_{d*}}
. For example, using the rule
(N)
, it is easy to see that
\Rightarrow p\vdash_{\mathbf{iK_{d}}}\,\Rightarrow\nabla p
. However, by examining the form of the rules of
\mathbf{iK_{d}}
, it becomes clear that the sequent
p\Rightarrow\nabla p
cannot be provable in
\mathbf{iK_{d}}
. In this section, we present a modified version of the deduction theorem that holds for
\mathbf{iK_{d}}
and
\mathbf{iK_{d*}}
. First, we need the following definition:

Definition 3
.

The set of
variants
of a formula
A
, is defined inductively as follows:

(i)

A
is a variant of
A
, and

(ii)

if
B
is a variant of
A
, then
\nabla B
and
\Box B
are also variants of
A
.

To put it simply, the variants of a formula
A
are all possible combinations of
\nabla
and
\Box
applied to
A
.

Let
\mathbf{iK_{d}^{+}}
(resp.
\mathbf{iK_{d*}^{+}}
) be the calculus
\mathbf{iK_{d}}
(resp.
\mathbf{iK_{d*}}
) extended by the cut rule. Note that the cut admissibility in
\mathbf{iK_{d}}
(resp.
\mathbf{iK_{d*}}
) only shows that the provable sequents in
\mathbf{iK_{d}^{+}}
(resp.
\mathbf{iK_{d*}^{+}}
) and
\mathbf{iK_{d}}
(resp.
\mathbf{iK_{d*}}
) are the same if the proofs have no assumptions. As usual, in the presence of assumptions, these systems are different, and the task of any deduction theorem is to connect the provability of these two systems in such a general situation:

Theorem 10
.

(Deduction Theorem)
For any multisets
\Gamma
and
\Delta
of formulas and any formula
A
, the following are equivalent:

(i)

\Rightarrow A\vdash_{\mathbf{iK_{d}^{+}}}\Gamma\Rightarrow\Delta
.

(ii)

\mathbf{iK_{d}}\vdash\Gamma,\Sigma_{A}\Rightarrow\Delta
, for a set
\Sigma_{A}
of the variants of
A
.

A similar claim also holds, replacing
\mathbf{iK_{d}}
with
\mathbf{iK_{d*}}
and
\mathbf{iK_{d}^{+}}
with
\mathbf{iK_{d*}^{+}}
.

Proof.

We only prove the case for
\mathbf{iK_{d}}
. The other is identical. Note that in
(ii)
, we can also use the cut rule in the proofs in
\mathbf{iK_{d}}
as it is admissible and we have no assumptions. To prove
(ii)
from
(i)
, we use an induction on a proof-tree
\mathcal{D}
of
\,\Rightarrow A\vdash_{\mathbf{iK_{d}^{+}}}\Gamma\Rightarrow\Delta
.
If
\Gamma\Rightarrow\Delta
is an instance of the axioms of
\mathbf{iK_{d}}
, the claim is trivial by setting
\Sigma_{A}=\varnothing
. If
\Gamma\Rightarrow\Delta
is the sequent
\Rightarrow A
, itself, then set
\Sigma_{A}=\{A\}
. The claim is proved by Lemma
1
, part
(i)
.
If
\mathcal{D}
is not an axiom, it ends with one of the rules in
\mathbf{iK_{d}}
or the rule
(cut)
. There are two cases to consider: either the last rule is in
\{(N),(R\!\to)\}
or it is not:

\bullet

(N)

\mathcal{D}
ends with

and

\mathcal{D}

\mathcal{D}^{\prime}

\Gamma^{\prime}\Rightarrow\Delta^{\prime}

\Sigma^{\prime}_{A}

\bullet

(R\!\rightarrow

4
Admissibility of
cut
in
\mathbf{iK_{d}}

In this section, we establish the admissibility of the cut rule in
\mathbf{iK_{d}}
by proving Theorem
6
. Using Lemma
2
, this also implies the cut admissibility in
\mathbf{iK_{d*}}
. For technical reasons, we prove the admissibility of the following more general rule:

\Pi\Rightarrow A

\Phi,\nabla^{n}A\Rightarrow\Lambda

\Phi,\nabla^{n}\Pi\Rightarrow\Lambda

where
n\geq 0
is an arbitrary natural number. Clearly, in the special case
n=0
, the rule reduces to the standard cut rule. To establish the admissibility of this generalized cut rule, we first need to define the rank of a formula.

Definition 4
.

The rank of a formula
A
, denoted by
\rho(A)
, is defined recursively as follows:

\rho(A)=\begin{cases}1&\quad A\in V\cup\{\bot,\top\}\\
\rho(B)&\quad A=\nabla B\\
max(\rho(B),\rho(C))+1&\quad A=B\circ C\quad(\circ\in\{\land,\lor,\rightarrow,\supset\})\end{cases}

Notice that
\nabla
does not increase the rank of a formula.

Theorem 11
.

(Generalized cut admissibility)
If
\mathbf{iK_{d}}\vdash\Pi\Rightarrow A
and
\mathbf{iK_{d}}\vdash\Phi,\nabla^{n}A\Rightarrow\Lambda
, then
\mathbf{iK_{d}}\vdash\Phi,\nabla^{n}\Pi\Rightarrow\Lambda
.

Proof.

Consider the lexicographic well-ordering on
\mathbb{N}^{2}
. By induction on this order, we show that for any pair
(r,h)
of natural numbers, any formula
A
of rank
r
and any proof-trees
\mathcal{D}_{1}
and
\mathcal{D}_{2}
for

\Pi\Rightarrow A
and
\Phi,\nabla^{n}A\Rightarrow\Lambda
, respectively, if
h(\mathcal{D}_{1})+h(\mathcal{D}_{2})\leq h
, then there is a proof in
\mathbf{iK_{d}}
for
\Phi,\nabla^{n}\Pi\Rightarrow\Lambda
.

Let us prove the claim for
(r,h)
and let
\mathcal{D}_{1}
and
\mathcal{D}_{2}
be proof-trees for

\Pi\Rightarrow A
and
\Phi,\nabla^{n}A\Rightarrow\Lambda
, respectively. For simplicity, we use the following notation. Let
\mathcal{D}_{1}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{D}_{2}^{\prime}
be proof-trees for
\Pi^{\prime}\Rightarrow A^{\prime}
and
\Phi^{\prime},\nabla^{n^{\prime}}A^{\prime}\Rightarrow\Lambda^{\prime}
such that either
\rho(A^{\prime})<\rho(A)
or
\rho(A^{\prime})=\rho(A)
but
h(\mathcal{D}_{1}^{\prime})+h(\mathcal{D}_{2}^{\prime})<h(\mathcal{D}_{1})+h(\mathcal{D}_{2})
. Then, as the induction hypothesis is applicable on
\mathcal{D}_{1}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{D}_{2}^{\prime}
, we can have a proof for
\Phi^{\prime},\nabla^{n^{\prime}}\Pi^{\prime}\Rightarrow\Lambda^{\prime}
. We denote this proof by:

\mathcal{D}_{1}^{\prime}

\Pi^{\prime}\Rightarrow A^{\prime}

\mathcal{D}_{2}^{\prime}

\Phi^{\prime},\nabla^{n^{\prime}}A^{\prime}\Rightarrow\Lambda^{\prime}

\mathrm{IH}_{A^{\prime}}

\Phi^{\prime},\nabla^{n^{\prime}}\Pi^{\prime}\Rightarrow\Lambda^{\prime}

To prove the claim for
(r,h)
, we investigate all possible last rules for
\mathcal{D}_{1}
and
\mathcal{D}_{2}
, and we partition all these cases into three parts:

1.

The cases where
A
is not principal in the last rule of
\mathcal{D}_{1}

2.

The cases where
A
is principal in the last rule of
\mathcal{D}_{1}

\nabla^{n}A
is not principal in the last rule of
\mathcal{D}_{2}

3.

The cases where
A
is principal in the last rule of
\mathcal{D}_{1}
and
\nabla^{n}A
is principal in the last rule of
\mathcal{D}_{2}
.

In the first two parts, the general strategy is to apply the induction hypothesis to the immediate subtrees of
\mathcal{D}_{1}
and
\mathcal{D}_{2}
, using the cut formula with the same rank as
A
. In the final part, however, the induction hypothesis is also applied to formulas with ranks smaller than that of

. Below, we provide a detailed explanation of the key cases in each part.

\mathcal{D}_{1}

\mathcal{D}_{2}

(R\top)
, because the left-hand side of this axiom is empty, whereas the antecedent of
\Phi,\nabla^{n}A\Rightarrow\Lambda
cannot be empty. Therefore, we conclude that
\mathcal{D}_{2}
is not an axiom.

For the sake of brevity, we will only elaborate on the cases where
\mathcal{D}_{2}
ends with the rules
(L\wedge^{r})
,
(R\vee_{1})
,
(R\!\rightarrow)
, or
(N)
. The remaining cases are either straightforward or analogous.

\bullet

(L\wedge^{r})
: As
\mathcal{D}_{2}
ends with
(L\wedge^{r})
and the principal formula is different from
\nabla^{n}A
, we have
\Phi=\Sigma,\nabla^{r}(B\wedge C)
, for some multiset
\Sigma
and some formulas
B
and
C
. Moreover,
\mathcal{D}_{2}
is in the form:

\mathcal{D}_{2}^{\prime}

\Sigma,\nabla^{r}B,\nabla^{r}C,\nabla^{n}A\Rightarrow\Lambda

L\wedge^{r}

\Sigma,\nabla^{r}(B\wedge C),\nabla^{n}A\Rightarrow\Lambda

By the induction hypothesis on
\mathcal{D}_{1}
and
\mathcal{D}_{2}^{\prime}
, we have
\Sigma,\nabla^{r}B,\nabla^{r}C,\nabla^{n}\Pi\Rightarrow\Lambda
. By the rule
(L\wedge^{r})
, we have
\Sigma,\nabla^{r}(B\wedge C),\nabla^{n}\Pi\Rightarrow\Lambda
which is what we wanted.

\bullet

(R\vee_{1}
): As
\mathcal{D}_{2}
ends with
(R\vee_{1})
, we have
\Lambda=\{B\vee C\}
, for some formulas
B
and
C
, and
\mathcal{D}_{2}
is in the form:

\mathcal{D}_{2}^{\prime}

\Phi,\nabla^{n}A\Rightarrow B

R\vee_{1}

\Phi,\nabla^{n}A\Rightarrow B\vee C

Again, use the induction hypothesis for
\mathcal{D}_{1}
and
\mathcal{D}_{2}^{\prime}
to get
\Phi,\nabla^{n}\Pi\Rightarrow B
. Then apply the rule
(R\vee_{1})
to reach the desired sequent.

\bullet

(R\!\rightarrow
): As
\mathcal{D}_{2}
ends with
(R\!\rightarrow)
, we have
\Lambda=\{B\rightarrow C\}
, for some formulas
B
and
C
, and
\mathcal{D}_{2}
has the following form:

\mathcal{D}_{2}^{\prime}

\nabla\Phi,\nabla^{n+1}A,B\Rightarrow C

R\!\rightarrow

\Phi,\nabla^{n}A\Rightarrow B\rightarrow C

By the induction hypothesis for
\mathcal{D}_{1}
and
\mathcal{D}_{2}^{\prime}
, we have
\nabla\Phi,\nabla^{n+1}\Pi,B\Rightarrow C
. Then, we can apply the rule
(R\!\rightarrow)
to get the desired sequent
\Phi,\nabla^{n}\Pi\Rightarrow B\rightarrow C
.

\bullet

(N)
: As
\mathcal{D}_{2}
ends with
(N)
, we have
\Phi,\nabla^{n}A=\nabla\Gamma
and
\Lambda=\nabla\Delta
, for some multisets
\Gamma
and
\Delta
and
\mathcal{D}_{2}
has the following form:

\mathcal{D}_{2}^{\prime}

\Gamma\Rightarrow\Delta

N

\nabla\Gamma\Rightarrow\nabla\Delta

Note that
\nabla^{n}A\in\nabla\Gamma
. We claim that
n\geq 1
, because if
n=0
, then
A\in\nabla\Gamma
, which implies that
A=\nabla B
for some formula
B
. However, we have already established that this is impossible. Therefore, since
n\geq 1
, it follows that
\nabla^{n-1}A\in\Gamma
. Thus, we can express
\Gamma
as
\Sigma,\nabla^{n-1}A
, where
\Sigma
is a multiset. Therefore,
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{2}
proves
\Sigma,\nabla^{n-1}A\Rightarrow\Delta
. By the induction hypothesis for
\mathcal{D}_{1}
and
\mathcal{D}_{2}^{\prime}
, we have
\Sigma,\nabla^{n-1}\Pi\Rightarrow\Delta
. Applying
(N)
, we obtain
\nabla\Sigma,\nabla^{n}\Pi\Rightarrow\nabla\Delta
, which is the desired sequent.

Part
(3)
. In this part, we assume that
A
is principal in the last rule of
\mathcal{D}_{1}
and
\nabla^{n}A
is principal in the last rule of
\mathcal{D}_{2}
. Clearly, the last rules in both
\mathcal{D}_{1}
and
\mathcal{D}_{2}
must match in order for
A
and
\nabla^{n}A
to be their principal formulas. Thus, if
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
are the last rules in
\mathcal{D}_{1}
and
\mathcal{D}_{2}
, the only possible pairs for
(R_{1},R_{2})
are
(R\wedge,L\wedge^{n})
,
(R\vee_{1},L\vee^{n})
,
(R\vee_{2},L\vee^{n})
,
(R\!\supset,L\!\supset^{n})
, or, if
n\geq 1
,
(R\!\rightarrow,L\!\rightarrow^{n-1})
. Note that the number
n
in these rules corresponds to the same number
n
in
\nabla^{n}A
. We now investigate each case separately in the following:

\bullet

(R\wedge,L\wedge^{n})
: As
\mathcal{D}_{1}
ends with
(R\wedge)
and
\mathcal{D}_{2}
ends with
(L\wedge^{n})
, we have
A=B\wedge C
, for some formulas
B
and
C
and
\mathcal{D}_{1}
and
\mathcal{D}_{2}
are in the form:

\mathcal{D}_{1}^{\prime}

\Pi\Rightarrow B

\mathcal{D}_{1}^{\prime\prime}

\Pi\Rightarrow C

R\wedge

\Pi\Rightarrow B\wedge C

\mathcal{D}_{2}^{\prime}

\Phi,\nabla^{n}B,\nabla^{n}C\Rightarrow\Lambda

L\wedge^{n}

\Phi,\nabla^{n}(B\wedge C)\Rightarrow\Lambda

Now, consider the following proof-tree:

\mathcal{D}_{1}^{\prime\prime}

\Pi\Rightarrow C

\mathcal{D}_{1}^{\prime}

\Pi\Rightarrow B

\mathcal{D}_{2}^{\prime}

\Phi,\nabla^{n}B,\nabla^{n}C\Rightarrow\Lambda

\mathrm{IH}_{B}

\Phi,\nabla^{n}\Pi,\nabla^{n}C\Rightarrow\Lambda

\mathrm{IH}_{C}

\Phi,(\nabla^{n}\Pi)^{2}\Rightarrow\Lambda

Lc^{(*)}

\Phi,\nabla^{n}\Pi\Rightarrow\Lambda

where
Lc^{(*)}
means a repeated application of the rule
(Lc)
, which is admissible in
\mathbf{iK_{d}}
by Theorem
5
. Notice that both
B
and
C
have a lower rank than
A
. Therefore, regardless of the height of the proof-trees, the use of the induction hypothesis is permitted.

\bullet

(R\vee_{1},L\vee^{n})
: As
\mathcal{D}_{1}
ends with
(R\vee_{1})
and
\mathcal{D}_{2}
ends with
(L\vee^{n})
, we have
A=B\vee C
, for some formulas
B
and
C
and
\mathcal{D}_{1}
and
\mathcal{D}_{2}
are in the form:

\mathcal{D}_{1}^{\prime}

\Pi\Rightarrow B

R\vee_{1}

\Pi\Rightarrow B\vee C

\mathcal{D}_{2}^{\prime}

\Phi,\nabla^{n}B\Rightarrow\Lambda

\mathcal{D}_{2}^{\prime\prime}

\Phi,\nabla^{n}C\Rightarrow\Lambda

L\vee^{n}

\Phi,\nabla^{n}(B\vee C)\Rightarrow\Lambda

Then, using the induction hypothesis for
B
, we have

\mathcal{D}_{1}^{\prime}

\Pi\Rightarrow B

\mathcal{D}_{2}^{\prime}

\Phi,\nabla^{n}B\Rightarrow\Lambda

\mathrm{IH}_{B}

\Phi,\nabla^{n}\Pi\Rightarrow\Lambda

which is what we wanted. The proof of the case
(R\vee_{2},L\vee^{n})
is similar.

\bullet

(R\!\rightarrow,L\!\rightarrow^{n-1})
: As
\mathcal{D}_{1}
and
\mathcal{D}_{2}
end with
(R\!\rightarrow)
and
(L\!\rightarrow^{n-1})
, respectively, we have
A=B\rightarrow C
, for some formulas
B
and
C
, and
\mathcal{D}_{1}
and
\mathcal{D}_{2}
are in the form:

\mathcal{D}_{1}^{\prime}

\nabla\Pi,B\Rightarrow C

R\!\rightarrow

\Pi\Rightarrow B\rightarrow C

\mathcal{D}_{2}^{\prime}

\Phi,\nabla^{n}(B\rightarrow C)\Rightarrow\nabla^{n-1}B

\mathcal{D}_{2}^{\prime\prime}

\Phi,\nabla^{n}(B\rightarrow C),\nabla^{n-1}C\Rightarrow\Lambda

L\!\rightarrow^{n-1}

\Phi,\nabla^{n}(B\rightarrow C)\Rightarrow\Lambda

Consider the following proof-tree and call it
\mathcal{D}
:

\mathcal{D}_{1}^{\prime}

\nabla\Pi,B\Rightarrow C

\mathcal{D}_{1}

\Pi\Rightarrow B\rightarrow C

\mathcal{D}_{2}^{\prime\prime}

\Phi,\nabla^{n}(B\rightarrow C),\nabla^{n-1}C\Rightarrow\Lambda

\mathrm{IH}_{B\rightarrow C}

\Phi,\nabla^{n}\Pi,\nabla^{n-1}C\Rightarrow\Lambda

\mathrm{IH}_{C}

\Phi,(\nabla^{n}\Pi)^{2},\nabla^{n-1}B\Rightarrow\Lambda

Notice that the first use of the induction hypothesis is for
\mathcal{D}_{1}
and
\mathcal{D}_{2}^{\prime\prime}
, which is permitted because
\mathcal{D}_{2}^{\prime\prime}
has a smaller height. The second use is also allowed since
C
has a lower rank than
A
. Now, consider the following proof-tree:

\mathcal{D}_{1}

\Pi\Rightarrow B\rightarrow C

\mathcal{D}_{2}^{\prime}

\Phi,\nabla^{n}(B\rightarrow C)\Rightarrow\nabla^{n-1}B

\mathrm{IH}_{B\rightarrow C}

\Phi,\nabla^{n}\Pi\Rightarrow\nabla^{n-1}B

\mathcal{D}

\mathrm{IH}_{\nabla^{n-1}B}

\Phi^{2},(\nabla^{n}\Pi)^{3}\Rightarrow\Lambda

Lc^{(*)}

\Phi,\nabla^{n}\Pi\Rightarrow\Lambda

Here,

refers to a repeated application of the rule
(Lc)
. As before, the first application of the induction hypothesis is allowed because the height of
\mathcal{D}_{2}^{\prime}
is smaller than the height of
\mathcal{D}_{2}
. For the second application, observe that the rank of
\nabla^{n-1}B
is equal to the rank of
B
, which is smaller than the rank of

.

\bullet

(R\!\supset,L\!\supset^{n})
: As
\mathcal{D}_{1}
and
\mathcal{D}_{2}
end with
(R\!\supset)
and
(L\!\supset^{n})
, respectively, we have
A=B\supset C
, for some formulas
B
and
C
, and
\mathcal{D}_{1}
and
\mathcal{D}_{2}
are in the form:

\mathcal{D}_{1}^{\prime}

\Pi,B\Rightarrow C

R\!\supset

\Pi\Rightarrow B\supset C

\mathcal{D}_{2}^{\prime}

\Phi,\nabla^{n}(B\supset C)\Rightarrow\nabla^{n}B

\mathcal{D}_{2}^{\prime\prime}

\Phi,\nabla^{n}C\Rightarrow\Lambda

L\!\supset^{n}

\Phi,\nabla^{n}(B\supset C)\Rightarrow\Lambda

Let
\mathcal{D}
be the following proof-tree:

\mathcal{D}_{1}^{\prime}

\Pi,B\Rightarrow C

\mathcal{D}_{2}^{\prime\prime}

\Phi,\nabla^{n}C\Rightarrow\Lambda

\mathrm{IH}_{C}

\Phi,\nabla^{n}\Pi,\nabla^{n}B\Rightarrow\Lambda

Now, consider the following proof-tree:

\mathcal{D}_{1}

\Pi\Rightarrow B\supset C

\mathcal{D}_{2}^{\prime}

\Phi,\nabla^{n}(B\supset C)\Rightarrow\nabla^{n}B

\mathrm{IH}_{B\supset C}

\Phi,\nabla^{n}\Pi\Rightarrow\nabla^{n}B

\mathcal{D}

\mathrm{IH}_{\nabla^{n}B}

\Phi^{2},(\nabla^{n}\Pi)^{2}\Rightarrow\Lambda

Lc^{(*)}

\Phi,\nabla^{n}\Pi\Rightarrow\Lambda

where
Lc^{(*)}
denotes a repeated application of the rule
(Lc)
. This completes the proof for all cases. Thus, we have constructed a proof-tree for the desired sequent for every possible pair of end rules for
\mathcal{D}_{1}
and
\mathcal{D}_{2}
. This concludes the proof of the theorem.
∎

, designed for the logics

and

, respectively, in order to establish certain properties of these logics.

5
Visser’s Rules and Disjunction Property

Recall that intuitionistic propositional logic has the property that for any finite set
I
, if

\mathsf{IPC}\vdash[\bigwedge_{i\in I}(A_{i}\supset B_{i})]\supset(C\vee D),

then either
\mathsf{IPC}\vdash[\bigwedge_{i\in I}(A_{i}\supset B_{i})]\supset C
or
\mathsf{IPC}\vdash[\bigwedge_{i\in I}(A_{i}\supset B_{i})]\supset D
or
\mathsf{IPC}\vdash[\bigwedge_{i\in I}(A_{i}\supset B_{i})]\supset A_{i}
, for some
i\in I
. These admissible rules are called
Visser’s rules
and play a crucial role, as they provide a basis for all admissible rules of
\mathsf{IPC}

[
10
]
. Moreover, note that for the empty
I
, as
\bigwedge\varnothing=\top
, we obtain the well-known
disjunction property
of
\mathsf{IPC}
: if
\mathsf{IPC}\vdash C\vee D
, then either
\mathsf{IPC}\vdash C
or
\mathsf{IPC}\vdash D
.

In this section, we introduce generalizations of Visser’s rules and prove their admissibility in the logics
\mathsf{iK_{d}}
and
\mathsf{iK_{d*}}
. Since the languages
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{L}_{*}
are more expressive than the usual propositional language, we consider three types of Visser’s rules: disjunctive, implicative, and Heyting implicative.

Theorem 12
.

(Disjunctive Visser’s rules for
\mathsf{iK_{d}}
)
Let
I
and
J
be finite sets (possibly empty),
\{m_{i}\}_{i\in I}
and
\{n_{j}\}_{j\in J}
be sequences of natural numbers and

X=\bigwedge_{i\in I}\nabla^{m_{i}}(A_{i}\supset B_{i})\wedge\bigwedge_{j\in J}\nabla^{n_{j}}(C_{j}\rightarrow D_{j}).

If

\mathsf{iK_{d}}\vdash X\rightarrow E\vee F,

then one of the following holds:

\bullet

\mathsf{iK_{d}}\vdash X\rightarrow\nabla^{m_{i}}A_{i}
, for some
i\in I
,

\bullet

\mathsf{iK_{d}}\vdash X\rightarrow\nabla^{n_{j}-1}C_{j}
, for some
j\in J
, where
n_{j}\geq 1
,

\bullet

\mathsf{iK_{d}}\vdash X\rightarrow E
,

\bullet

\mathsf{iK_{d}}\vdash X\rightarrow F
.

Proof.

First, note that for any multiset
\Gamma
, we have
\mathbf{iK_{d}}\vdash\Gamma\Rightarrow\bigwedge\Gamma
. Therefore, using Corollary
9
, Lemma
3
, Part
(i)
, and the admissibility of
(cut)
in
\mathbf{iK_{d}}
, it is enough to prove that for any number
h\in\mathbb{N}
and any sequent
\Gamma\Rightarrow E\vee F
, where
\Gamma
has the form
\{\nabla^{m_{i}}(A_{i}\supset B_{i})\}_{i\in I}\cup\{\nabla^{n_{i}}(C_{j}\rightarrow D_{j})\}_{j\in J}
, if
\mathbf{iK_{d}}\vdash_{h}\Gamma\Rightarrow E\vee F
, then one of the following holds:

\bullet

\mathbf{iK_{d}}\vdash\Gamma\Rightarrow\nabla^{m_{i}}A_{i}
, for some
i\in I
,

\bullet

\mathbf{iK_{d}}\vdash\Gamma\Rightarrow\nabla^{n_{j}-1}C_{j}
, for some
j\in J
, where
n_{j}\geq 1
,

\bullet

\mathbf{iK_{d}}\vdash\Gamma\Rightarrow E
,

\bullet

\mathbf{iK_{d}}\vdash\Gamma\Rightarrow F
.

We prove the claim by induction on
h
. First, note that
h\neq 0
, as
\Gamma\Rightarrow E\vee F
cannot be an axiom, given its form. Hence, the base case of the induction trivially holds. For the inductive step, let
\mathcal{D}
be a proof of
\Gamma\Rightarrow E\vee F
in
\mathbf{iK_{d}}
with the height at most
h>0
. Based on the form of formulas in the sequent
\Gamma\Rightarrow E\vee F
, the last rule in
\mathcal{D}
must be one of the following:
(LW)
,
(Rw)
,
(R\vee_{1})
,
(R\vee_{2})
,
(L\!\supset^{m_{i}})
, for some
i\in I
, or
(L\!\rightarrow^{n_{j}-1})
, for some
j\in J
, where
n_{j}\geq 1
.

If the last rule in
\mathcal{D}
is
(LW)
, then there is a proof
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
of height
h^{\prime}<h
for the sequent
\Sigma\Rightarrow E\vee F
, where
\Sigma\subseteq\Gamma
. The claim then follows easily by the induction hypothesis for
h^{\prime}
.

For
(Rw)
, we have a proof-tree for
(\Gamma\Rightarrow\,)
. Applying
(Rw)
, we can easily prove
\Gamma\Rightarrow E
.

The cases
(R\vee_{1})
,
(R\vee_{2})
,
(L\!\supset^{m_{i}})
, and
(L\!\rightarrow^{n_{j}-1})
prove the claim directly. We only explain the case
(L\!\rightarrow^{n_{j}-1})
, as the rest follow similarly.
Suppose
\mathcal{D}
ends with
(L\!\rightarrow^{n_{j}-1})
, for some
j\in J
, where
n_{j}\neq 0
. Then,
\mathcal{D}
has the following form:

\Gamma\Rightarrow\nabla^{n_{j}-1}C_{j}

\Gamma,\nabla^{n_{j}-1}D_{j}\Rightarrow E\vee F

\Gamma\Rightarrow E\vee F

Hence,
\Gamma\Rightarrow\nabla^{n_{j}-1}C_{j}
is provable in
\mathbf{iK_{d}}
.
∎

, for some
i\in I
, where
m_{i}\geq 1
,

.
∎

Theorem 15
.

(Implicative Visser’s rules for
\mathsf{iK_{d}}
)
Let
I
and
J
be finite sets (possibly empty) and
\{m_{i}\}_{i\in I}
and
\{n_{j}\}_{j\in J}
be sequences of natural numbers. If

\mathsf{iK_{d}}\vdash[\bigwedge_{i\in I}\nabla^{m_{i}}(A_{i}\supset B_{i})\wedge\bigwedge_{j\in J}\nabla^{n_{j}}(C_{j}\rightarrow D_{j})]\rightarrow\nabla^{k}(E\to F),

then one of the following holds:

\bullet

\mathsf{iK_{d}}\vdash[\bigwedge_{i\in I}\nabla^{m_{i}}(A_{i}\supset B_{i})\wedge\bigwedge_{j\in J}\nabla^{n_{j}}(C_{j}\rightarrow D_{j})]\rightarrow\nabla^{m_{i}}A_{i}
, for some
i\in I
,

\bullet

\mathsf{iK_{d}}\vdash[\bigwedge_{i\in I}\nabla^{m_{i}}(A_{i}\supset B_{i})\wedge\bigwedge_{j\in J}\nabla^{n_{j}}(C_{j}\rightarrow D_{j})]\rightarrow\nabla^{n_{j}-1}C_{j}
, for some
j\in J
, where
n_{j}\geq 1
,

\bullet

\mathsf{iK_{d}}\vdash[\bigwedge_{i\in I^{\prime}}\nabla^{m_{i}-k+1}(A_{i}\supset B_{i})\wedge\bigwedge_{j\in J^{\prime}}\nabla^{n_{j}-k+1}(C_{j}\rightarrow D_{j})\wedge E]\rightarrow F
, for some
I^{\prime}\subseteq I
and
J^{\prime}\subseteq J
such that
m_{i},n_{j}\geq k
, for any
i\in I^{\prime}
and
j\in J^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Similar to the proof of Theorem
12
, we use induction on the height
h
of a proof-tree
\mathcal{D}
for any sequent of the form

\{\nabla^{m_{i}}(A_{i}\supset B_{i})\}_{i\in I},\{\nabla^{n_{j}}(C_{j}\to D_{j})\}_{j\in J}\Rightarrow\nabla^{k}(E\to F).

First, note that
\mathcal{D}
cannot be an axiom. Hence,
h\neq 0
, which implies that the base case of the induction trivially holds.
For the induction step, based on the form of the formulas in the end sequent, the last rule in
\mathcal{D}
must be either
(LW)
,
(Rw)
,
(N)
,
(L\!\supset^{m_{i}})
, for some
i\in I
,
(L\!\to^{n_{j}-1})
, for some
j\in J
such that
n_{j}\geq 1
, or
(R\!\to)
if
k=0
. The cases
(LW)
,
(Rw)
,
(L\!\supset^{m_{i}})
, and
(L\!\to^{n_{j}-1})
are similar to the corresponding ones in the proof of Theorem
12
.

For
(N)
, if the last rule in
\mathcal{D}
is
(N)
, then
m_{i},n_{j},k\geq 1
, for any
i\in I
and
j\in J
. There is a proof-tree
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
of height
h^{\prime}<h
for

\{\nabla^{m_{i}-1}(A_{i}\supset B_{i})\}_{i\in I},\{\nabla^{n_{j}-1}(C_{j}\rightarrow D_{j})\}_{j\in J}\Rightarrow\nabla^{k-1}(E\to F)

in
\mathbf{iK_{d}}
. Then, by the induction hypothesis, one of the following holds:

\bullet

\mathbf{iK_{d}}\vdash\{\nabla^{m_{i}-1}(A_{i}\supset B_{i})\}_{i\in I},\{\nabla^{n_{j}-1}(C_{j}\rightarrow D_{j})\}_{j\in J}\Rightarrow\nabla^{m_{i}-1}A_{i}
, for some
i\in I
,

\bullet

\mathbf{iK_{d}}\vdash\{\nabla^{m_{i}-1}(A_{i}\supset B_{i})\}_{i\in I},\{\nabla^{n_{j}-1}(C_{j}\rightarrow D_{j})\}_{j\in J}\Rightarrow\nabla^{n_{j}-2}C_{j}
, for some
j\in J
, where
n_{j}-1\geq 1
,

\bullet

\mathbf{iK_{d}}\vdash\{\nabla^{m_{i}-k+1}(A_{i}\supset B_{i})\}_{i\in I^{\prime}},\{\nabla^{n_{j}-k+1}(C_{j}\rightarrow D_{j})\}_{j\in J^{\prime}},E\Rightarrow F
, for some
I^{\prime}\subseteq I
and
J^{\prime}\subseteq J
such that
m_{i}-1,n_{j}-1\geq k-1
, for any
i\in I^{\prime}
and
j\in J^{\prime}
.

In the first and second cases, it is enough to apply
(N)
. In the last case, there is nothing to prove. Finally, if
k=0
and the last rule is
(R\!\to)
, the claim is already proved.
∎

.

)

, for some

,

, for any

.

over

, as established in Corollary

.
∎

Theorem 17
.

(Heyting Implicative Visser’s rules for
\mathsf{iK_{d}}
)
Let
I
and
J
be finite sets (possibly empty) and

6
Interpolation Theorems

In this section, we establish the
Craig interpolation theorem
for
\mathsf{iK_{d}}
using a method similar to that of Maehara
[
15
]
. We then apply the deduction theorem for
\mathbf{iK_{d}}
(Theorem
10
) to show that the calculus
\mathbf{iK_{d}^{+}}
, which extends
\mathbf{iK_{d}}
with the
(cut)
rule, satisfies deductive interpolation. Our approach relies heavily on the Heyting implication, even in the language
\mathcal{L}_{*}
. Consequently, this method does not extend to proving Craig interpolation for
\mathsf{iK_{d*}}
or deductive interpolation for
\mathbf{iK_{d*}^{+}}
.

Definition 5
.

A logic
L
has the
Craig Interpolation Property
if for any formulas
A
and
B
if
(A\to B)\in L
, then there exists a formula
C
such that
(A\to C)\in L
,
(C\to B)\in L
and
V(C)\subseteq V(A)\cap V(B)
.

To demonstrate that
\mathsf{iK_{d}}
satisfies the Craig interpolation property, we establish the following theorem.

Theorem 18
.

For any multisets
\Gamma_{1}
,
\Gamma_{2}
and
\Delta
of formulas, if
\mathbf{iK_{d}}\vdash\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2}\Rightarrow\Delta
, then there is a formula
C\in\mathcal{L}
such that:

(i)

\mathbf{iK_{d}}\vdash\Gamma_{1}\Rightarrow C
,

(ii)

\mathbf{iK_{d}}\vdash\Gamma_{2},C\Rightarrow\Delta
, and

(iii)

V(C)\subseteq V(\Gamma_{1})\cap V(\Gamma_{2}\cup\Delta)
.

Consequently, the logic
\mathsf{iK_{d}}
enjoys Craig interpolation property.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be a proof-tree for
\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2}\Rightarrow\Delta
in
\mathbf{iK_{d}}
. By induction on the height of
\mathcal{D}
, we construct a formula
C
satisfying the properties
(i)
,
(ii)
, and
(iii)
. For the base case, suppose
\mathcal{D}
is an axiom. If the axiom is
(Id^{p})
, there are two cases to consider: either
\Gamma_{1}=\{p\}
and
\Gamma_{2}=\varnothing
, or
\Gamma_{1}=\varnothing
and
\Gamma_{2}=\{p\}
. In the first case, set
C=p
, and there is nothing to prove. In the second case, set
C=\top
. Then,
\Gamma_{1}\Rightarrow\top
by
(R\top)
, and
p,\top\Rightarrow p
by
(Id^{p})
and
(LW)
. Note that
V(\top)=\varnothing
, and hence the condition
(iii)
is automatic.
If the axiom is
(L\bot)

\Gamma_{1}=\varnothing
and
\Gamma_{2}=\{\bot\}
, or
\Gamma_{1}=\{\bot\}
and
\Gamma_{2}=\varnothing

\Gamma_{1}=\varnothing
, then take
C=\top
. The conditions
(i)
and
(iii)
are trivial, and as
\mathbf{iK_{d}}\vdash\bot,\top\Rightarrow
, we also have
(ii)
. If
\Gamma_{1}={\bot}
, then take
C=\bot
. This time,
(ii)
and
(iii)
are trivial, and as
\mathbf{iK_{d}}\vdash\bot\Rightarrow\bot
, we also have
(i)
.
If the axiom is
(R\top)
, then take
C=\top
. Then,
(i)
and
(iii)
are trivial. For
(ii)
, we have
\mathbf{iK_{d}}\vdash\top\Rightarrow\top

\mathcal{D}

\mathcal{D}

(LW)

(R\wedge)

(L\vee^{n})

(L\!\to^{n})

(R\!\to)

\bullet

\mathcal{D}
is
(LW)

\Gamma_{1}=\Gamma_{1}^{\prime},\Sigma_{1}

\Gamma_{2}=\Gamma_{2}^{\prime},\Sigma_{2}

\Gamma_{1}^{\prime}

\Gamma_{2}^{\prime}

\Gamma_{1}^{\prime},\Gamma_{2}^{\prime},\Sigma_{1},\Sigma_{2}\Rightarrow\Delta

D

\Gamma_{1}^{\prime}\Rightarrow D

C=D

(LW)

\bullet

(R\wedge)

Remark 2
.

Notice that the proof for Theorem
18
does not work for
\mathbf{iK_{d*}}
, since we are using
\supset
in the interpolant formula to handle the case
(L\!\to^{n})
.

We now use the deduction theorem, Theorem
10
, and Theorem
18
to prove that
\mathbf{iK_{d}^{+}}
enjoys the deductive interpolation.

Theorem 19

(Deductive Interpolation for
\mathbf{iK_{d}}
)
.

For any formulas
A
and
B
, if
\Rightarrow A\vdash_{\mathbf{iK_{d}^{+}}}\ \Rightarrow B
then there exists a formula
C
such that:

\bullet

\Rightarrow A\vdash_{\mathbf{iK_{d}^{+}}}\,\Rightarrow C

\bullet

\Rightarrow C\vdash_{\mathbf{iK_{d}^{+}}}\,\Rightarrow B
and

\bullet

V(C)\subseteq V(A)\cap V(B)
.

Proof.

Let
\Rightarrow A\vdash_{\mathbf{iK_{d}^{+}}}\,\Rightarrow B
. By deduction theorem, Theorem
10
, there is a set
\Sigma_{A}
of the variants of
A
such that
\mathbf{iK_{d}}\vdash\Sigma_{A}\Rightarrow B
. By Theorem
18
for
\Gamma_{1}=\Sigma_{A}
,
\Gamma_{2}=\varnothing
and
\Delta=\{B\}
, we get a formula
C
such that
\mathbf{iK_{d}}\vdash\Sigma_{A}\Rightarrow C
,
\mathbf{iK_{d}}\vdash C\Rightarrow B
and
V(C)\subseteq V(\Sigma_{A})\cap V(B)
. Using Theorem
10
again,
it is easy to see that
\Rightarrow A\vdash_{\mathbf{iK_{d}^{+}}}\,\Rightarrow C
and

\Rightarrow C\vdash_{\mathbf{iK_{d}^{+}}}\,\Rightarrow B
. Moreover, as

V(C)\subseteq V(\Sigma_{A})\cap V(B)
and
V(\Sigma_{A})\subseteq V(A)
, we reach
V(C)\subseteq V(A)\cap V(B)
.
∎
