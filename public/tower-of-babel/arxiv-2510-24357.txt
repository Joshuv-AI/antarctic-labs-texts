Spatial-Kinematic Absorption Models of the Circumgalactic Medium I. Structures, Orientations, and Kinematics email: cwc@nmsu.edu
Christopher W. Churchill

Abstract
In this two-paper series, we present a straightforward mathematical model for synthesizing quasar absorption line profiles from sight lines through idealized, spatial-kinematic models of the circumgalactic medium (CGM) and their host galaxies. Here, in Paper I, we develop the spatial geometries of multiple galaxy/CGM structures and populate these structures with 3D velocity fields. For arbitrary viewing angles and galaxy-quasar impact parameters, we derive observer coordinate-based expressions for the perceived azimuthal angle and galaxy inclination and a generalized scalar expression for the line-of-sight velocity as a function of position along the line of sight. We motivate and develop four idealized galaxy/CGM spatial-kinematic structures based on empirical data and theoretical predictions: (1) a rotating galactic disk/extra-planar gas, (2) a static or dynamic spherical halo, (3) an outflowing bi-polar galactic wind, and (4) an inward spiraling flared planar accretion. Using a small set of free parameters, the spatial geometries and velocity fields can be adjusted and explored, including velocity gradients, wind stalling, and accretion trajectories. These spatial-kinematic models are designed to be flexible and easily modified and can be tailored for studying individual galaxy-absorber pairs or galaxy group environments; they can be applied to real-world observations or hydrodynamic simulations of the baryon cycle as studied through quasar absorption line systems. These models also serve as tools for developing physical intuition. In \citetalias churchill25-skamII, we will present the formalism for populating the galaxy/CGM structures with multiphase photoionized and collisionally ionized gas and for generating absorption profiles for ions of interest.

Introduction

The circumgalactic medium (CGM), a region of dynamically active multiphase gas surrounding individual galaxies, is a massive reservoir of baryons that plays a prominent role in the evolution of galaxies
(see
Tumlinson et al. 2017
;
Faucher-Giguère & Oh 2023
, and references therein)
. Dynamically, the CGM serves as a conduit through which infalling gas from the intergalactic medium (IGM) accretes into galaxies to form stars and through which stellar-driven winds are expelled from or recycled back into galaxies
(
Oppenheimer & Davé 2008
;
Davé et al. 2011
;
van de Voort et al. 2011
, e.g.,)
. As such, this important medium channels and regulates the flow of baryons in and out of galaxies. Through this “baryon cycle,” metals and energy are transported between the galaxy’s star forming interstellar medium (ISM), its CGM, and the IGM in which it is embedded. The energetics include an array of dynamical, radiative, and collisional processes that induce photo-heating, shock heating, radiative cooling, turbulent mixing, advection and/or diffusion, and ionization balancing, all of which induce multiphase gas structure
(
Birnboim & Dekel 2003
;
Maller & Bullock 2004
;
Dekel & Birnboim 2006
;
Ceverino & Klypin 2009
;
Faerman et al. 2017
;
Faerman et al. 2020
;
Oppenheimer et al. 2018
;
Fielding et al. 2020
;
Esmerian et al. 2021
;
Pandya et al. 2023
, e.g.,)
.

Dark matter plus hydrodynamic simulations of galaxies in the cosmic setting show that these processes give rise to a variety of spatially and kinematically correlated regions surround galaxies on scales extending to hundreds of kiloparsecs, and that each is functionally engaged in the galactic baryon cycle in a fairly specific manner
(
Ford et al. 2014
;
Stewart et al. 2017
;
Nelson et al. 2019
;
Péroux et al. 2020
;
Hafen et al. 2022
;
Trapp et al. 2022
;
Faucher-Giguère & Oh 2023
;
Kocjan et al. 2024
;
Stern et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
. Observations of the CGM using the technique of quasar absorption lines, which record the spatial distribution and the chemical, kinematic, and ionization conditions of CGM gas, have generally supported the growing body of theoretical predictions
(
Bordoloi et al. 2011
;
Bouché et al. 2012
;
Kacprzak et al. 2012
;
Kacprzak et al. 2015
;
Rubin et al. 2012
;
Rubin et al. 2014
;
Nielsen et al. 2015
;
Ho et al. 2017
;
Martin et al. 2019
;
Roberts-Borsani & Saintonge 2019
;
Ho & Martin 2020
;
Péroux et al. 2020
;
Zabl et al. 2020
;
Beckett et al. 2023
, e.g.,)
. As such, an idealized canonical composite picture of a spatially and kinematically coherent CGM has developed over the last decade.

In this picture, much of the gas that accretes onto galaxies originates from the IGM and cosmic web and is concentrated within infalling filaments
(
Ceverino & Klypin 2009
;
van de Voort et al. 2011
;
Danovich et al. 2015
;
Nelson et al. 2018
, e.g.,)
. This infalling gas meets resistance from stellar-driven outflows
(
Habe & Ikeuchi 1982
, e.g.,)
, which are propelled outward perpendicular to the galaxy plane and concentrated mostly near the rotation axis
(
Nelson et al. 2019
;
Péroux et al. 2020
;
Nguyen & Thompson 2022
;
Trapp et al. 2022
;
Faucher-Giguère & Oh 2023
, e.g.,)
. The outflows tend to fan out as they rise above the disk, forming a bi-polar conical geometry
(
Rupke 2018
;
Fielding & Bryan 2022
;
Nguyen & Thompson 2022
, see)
. This overall dynamic, in tandem with angular momentum conservation, compels the infalling gas to spiral inward and to concentrate in an extended co-planar geometry
(
Kereš et al. 2005
;
Stewart et al. 2011
;
Stewart et al. 2017
;
Stewart 2017
;
Hafen et al. 2022
;
Trapp et al. 2022
;
Kocjan et al. 2024
;
Stern et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
. The relative flow rates and interactions between the metal-enriched galactic outflows and presumably lower-metallicity accreting gas are influential in creating the observed universe of galaxies, including the stellar-mass to halo-mass function, the global galaxy mass-metallicity relationship, and the mean star formation rate density of the universe
(
Dalcanton 2007
;
Davé et al. 2011
;
Behroozi et al. 2013
;
Madau & Dickinson 2014
;
Chisholm et al. 2018
;
Wechsler & Tinker 2018
, e.g.,)
.

This simplified distillation of the CGM is schematically illustrated in Figure
1
. There are four main spatial-kinematic components: (1) A spherical halo (shaded yellow) encompasses the overall CGM. This region can be kinematically diverse, with outflowing, inflowing, polar streaming, recycling, and/or semi-static material. (2) Stellar-driven outflowing winds can be represented by a bi-polar structure (shaded red) confined within a conical geometry. (3) The ISM is confined within the galaxy disk (shaded green), above which extra-planar gas can form a “lower halo.” (4) The bulk of the accretion is confined to a “flared” extended planar structure (shaded blue) in which material spirals inward toward the disk.

To deepen and broaden our understanding, it is important that we continue to study the salient characteristics of the CGM with the goal of organizing our thoughts on its complex roles in the baryon cycle and galaxy evolution. One approach to testing and refining this canonical picture is to apply the observational technique of quasar absorption lines to hydrodynamical cosmological simulations. By running “mock” quasar lines of sight through simulated galaxies over a range of impact parameters and synthesizing absorption line spectra, the simulated CGM can be studied in detail

(
Kacprzak et al. 2010
;
Kacprzak et al. 2019

A powerful benefit of using simulations is that the absorbing gas spatial location relative to both the galaxy and the line of sight (LOS) can be directly examined in relation to the LOS velocities measured from the spectra. However, with simulations it is challenging to establish clear spatial-kinematic relationship between the absorption component velocities and the absorbing gas; individual simulated halos do not necessarily reflect the distilled canonical picture as illustrated in Figure

Analytic models provide a useful bridge between simulations and observations. Simple models provide intuitive insight into the characteristic observables from the real-world universe. They can be used to unravel the tangled complexity of simulations. Simple intuitive spatial-kinematic absorption models of the CGM can enhance our interpretation of LOS kinematics observed in high resolution quasar absorption line profiles.

Such models are a rich part of the history of CGM studies.
Weisheit 1978
developed a constant rotating spherical halo model with a declining power-law density profile and generated some of the first mock absorption profiles based on a model of the CGM.
Lanzetta & Bowen 1992
compared observed high-resolution absorption profiles to rotating disks and spherical infall spatial-kinematic models. Since those early days, a array of idealized models of accreting disks, spherical halos, and bi-conical outflows have been developed and compared to observed absorption lines
(
Charlton & Churchill 1998
;
Prochaska & Wolfe 1998
;
Steidel et al. 2002
;
Kacprzak et al. 2010
;
Kacprzak et al. 2011
;
Bouché et al. 2012
;
Gauthier & Chen 2012
;
Chen et al. 2014
;
Kacprzak et al. 2019
;
Bordoloi et al. 2014
;
Diamond-Stanic et al. 2016
;
Ho et al. 2017
;
Lan & Mo 2018
;
Martin et al. 2019
;
Schroetter et al. 2019
;
French & Wakker 2020
;
Ho & Martin 2020
;
Zabl et al. 2020
;
Nateghi et al. 2021
;
Beckett et al. 2022
;
Beckett et al. 2023
;
Carr & Scarlata 2022
;
Casavecchia et al. 2023
, e.g.)
. With these idealized models, there is no ambiguity about which CGM component, what location relative to the central galaxy, or what underlying kinematics has given rise to absorption lines at a given LOS velocity. Various spatial geometries and kinematics can be quickly adjusted and flexibly explored and tested without requiring time-intensive, expensive computations on competitive, high-powered computing facilities.

The majority of previous studies that used idealized models to help explain observed quasar absorption lines have focused on only a single spatial-kinematic CGM component (such as simple disk rotation, or spherical infall/outflow, or bi-conical outflows with constant radial velocities) or compared and contrasted two components
(
Beckett et al. 2022
;
Beckett et al. 2023
, however, see)
. The growing maturity of our canonical picture of the CGM motivates a broader approach in which multiple idealized spatial-kinematic components are simultaneously included using spatial, kinematic, and gas phase properties that are able to reflect our continually evolving collective insights.

Considering the potential benefits such a model would have for studying the CGM using quasar absorption lines, we have endeavored to develop a multi-component spatial-kinematic absorption model (SKAM). Ideally, SKAMs should be simple in their construction and straightforward to program. The hope is that SKAMs can serve as evolvable tools for guiding interpretations of observed absorption line profiles through a wide range of astrophysically representative galaxy/CGM environments. We will present the development of a SKAM in two phases, each presented in a separate paper.

The topic of this Paper I is three fold.
In Section
2

V_{\hbox{\tiny LOS}}

The topic of

2
Definitions

Here, we define the characteristics of the galaxy dark-matter halo of mass
M_{\rm vir}
and radius
R_{\rm vir}
, which are specified by the circular velocity of the galaxy. We then construct two coordinate systems with coincident origins, one in the frame of the galaxy and one in the observer frame. From these two coordinate systems, transformations are derived that yield the galaxy inclination and the LOS impact parameter and azimuthal angle.

2.1
The Galaxy Dark Matter Halo

It is appropriate to characterize the galaxy by its virial mass,

M_{\rm vir}=\frac{4\pi}{3}R_{\rm vir}^{3}(\Delta_{c}\rho_{c})\,,

(1)

where
R_{\rm vir}
is the virial radius. The virial radius is defined such that the average mass density within this radius is
\Delta_{c}\rho_{c}
, where
\rho_{c}
is the critical density of the universe. Typically, an overdensity factor of
\Delta_{c}\sim 200
is adopted. If we employ an “NFW” dark matter halo
(
Navarro et al. 1996
)
, we can also write

M_{\rm vir}=4\pi\rho_{0}R_{s}^{3}A_{\cal C}\,,

(2)

where
\rho_{0}
is related to the mean density of the dark matter halo,
R_{\rm vir}=\mathcal{C}R_{s}
where
R_{s}
is the scale radius, and where

A_{\cal C}=\ln(1+\mathcal{C})-\mathcal{C}/(1+\mathcal{C})
, where
\mathcal{C}
is the “concentration parameter.”
Simulations predict that the median value of
\mathcal{C}
follows the relation

\mathcal{C}\simeq 10.5M_{12}^{-0.11}
, where
M_{12}=M_{\rm vir}/10^{12}
in solar masses
(
Macciò et al. 2007
)
, where the uncertainty is
\sigma_{\mathcal{C}}=\pm 2
independent of
M_{\rm vir}
. This dispersion reflects the fact that, at a fixed mass, the halo concentration correlates to a galaxy’s assembly time, and therefore redshift of formation and environment
(
Bullock et al. 2001
;
Wechsler et al. 2002
;
Zhao et al. 2003
, see)
.

Given that we are developing spatial-kinematic models, it is desirable that we define the galaxy mass and virial radius in terms of its rotation kinematics. For an NFW halo, the radius at which the circular velocity,
V_{c}
, is a maximum is given by
R_{c}=\xi R_{s}
, where
\xi=2.16258
is a constant.

The relation between circular velocity and the mass enclosed within the radius

V^{2}_{c}(R_{c})=\frac{GM(R_{c})}{R_{c}}\,.

(3)

Applying the relation,

M(r)=M_{\rm vir}\frac{\ln(1\!+\!x_{s})-x_{s}/(1\!+\!x_{s})}{A_{\cal C}}\,,

Our aim is to develop a simple yet flexible formalism for allowing an observer to view a galaxy with an arbitrary spatial orientation. Furthermore, the LOS and sky position of the background quasar has to be expressed in terms of the relationship between the observer and the galaxy. We first set up a fixed coordinate system (denoted
G

2

\hat{\hbox{\bf e}}_{x}
,
\hat{\hbox{\bf e}}_{y}
, and
\hat{\hbox{\bf e}}_{z}
. The corresponding spherical coordinates are

{\hat{\hbox{\bf e}}_{r}}
,
{\hat{\hbox{\bf e}}_{\uptheta}}
, and
{\hat{\hbox{\bf e}}_{\upphi}}

z

As illustrated in Figure
3

O

Consider an observer placed some distance from the origin on the

axes prior to coordinate rotation. A single rotation of

\beta

These two rotations, carried out in this order, are illustrated in Figure

As shown in Figure
4
, we place the observer at

In

Thus, in

(7)

Since the equation of line in a 3D space requires its direction, in this case

2.3
The Line of Sight (LOS)

We define the location, or position, along the LOS using the parametrized coordinate

\sigma_{x}=-({\hat{\hbox{\bf e}}_{x_{o}}}\!\!\cdot{\hat{\hbox{\bf e}}_{x}})

\sigma_{y}=-({\hat{\hbox{\bf e}}_{x_{o}}}\!\!\cdot{\hat{\hbox{\bf e}}_{y}})

In Appendix

\begin{array}[]{rcl}x(t)&=&\!R_{\perp}(\cos\gamma\cos\beta\sin\alpha-\sin\gamma\sin\beta)\\

2.4
The Observed Galaxy Orientation

The two observable quantities that define the orientation of the galaxy with respect to the LOS are the inclination and the sky-projected azimuthal angle. It would be desirable that these two observed quantities could be defined to determine the coordinate rotations,
\alpha
and
\beta
. However, degeneracies in the projection of the galaxy on the sky plane render this impossible to geometrically map with a unique one-to-one relation. Instead, we express the observed inclination in terms of the coordinate rotation angles, i.e.,
i=i(\alpha,\beta)
, and express the azimuthal angle in terms of both the rotation angles and the quasar position angle, i.e.,
\Phi=\Phi(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
.

The inclination angle is defined as the angle between the
z
-axis of the galaxy and the LOS. When this angle is a right angle, the galaxy is viewed edge on. When the two vectors are parallel, the galaxy is viewed face on. As such, the inclination is given by the dot product between these two vectors,
\cos i={\hat{\hbox{\bf s}}}\cdot{\hat{\hbox{\bf e}}_{z}}
. In Appendix
B
, we show that the observed inclination can be written,

\cos i=\sin|\beta|\cos\alpha\,.

(12)

The observed sky-projected azimuthal angle,
\Phi
, is defined as the angle between the projected major axis of the galaxy and a line connecting the galaxy center to the LOS position on the sky plane.
In Appendix
C
, we show that the sky-projected azimuthal angle can be written

\Phi=\gamma+\cot^{-1}\left(\frac{\cos\beta}{\sin\beta\sin\alpha}\right)\,.

(13)

We define
\Phi\in[0^{\circ},90^{\circ}]
such that it is always the primary angle. In the special case where
\alpha=0
, we have
{\cos i=\sin\beta}
, or
i=90^{\circ}-\beta
, and
\Phi=\gamma
. Also note that when
\beta=0^{\circ}
the galaxy is viewed edge-on so that any rotation by
\alpha
will always yield
i=90^{\circ}
, and
\Phi=\gamma
. When
\beta\rightarrow\pm 90^{\circ}
, we have the less intuitive result that
i\rightarrow\alpha
and
\Phi\rightarrow\gamma-90^{\circ}
. However, for
\beta=\pm 90^{\circ}
,
\alpha
is a degenerate quantity from the point of view of the observer, giving
i=0
, and
\Phi
cannot be measured.

3
Idealized CGM Structures

As described in the Section
1
, observations and theory suggest that the CGM can be segregated into a few somewhat unique spatial-kinematic components each playing a dominant role in the baryon cycle. These components were schematically illustrated in Figure
1
.

In summary, the CGM itself can be loosely visualized as a spherical region surrounding the central galaxy. Distributed throughout the CGM are galactic winds, accreting filaments, and recycling gas flows. Tidal streams from past mergers and/or from orbiting satellite galaxies may also be present. The inner halo (within
\sim\!10
kpc above and below the galaxy disk) is populated by extra-planar structures such as galactic fountains and high velocity clouds.

As a LOS passes through the CGM, it likely probes or partially probes multiple CGM components. Therefore, for modeling purposes, it is important we determine the locations on the LOS that intersect and probe each geometric structure, as this allows us to determine LOS velocities. For any geometric structure, the point(s) of LOS intersection are derived from the solution

G

3.1
The Spherical Halo

The historically motivated and simplest “halo” model for interpreting quasar absorption line data is an extended spherical geometry
(
Bahcall & Spitzer 1969
;
Weisheit 1978
;
Lanzetta & Bowen 1990
;
Bergeron & Boissé 1991
;
Steidel 1995
;
Charlton & Churchill 1996
, e.g.,)
. Efforts to develop increasingly sophisticated analytic models of extended gaseous halos has remained an active field of study
(
Li & Ikeuchi 1992
;
Mo & Miralda-Escude 1996
;
Maller & Bullock 2004
;
Fang et al. 2013
;
Sharma et al. 2014
;
Stern et al. 2016
;
Stern et al. 2018
;
Stern et al. 2019
;
Stern et al. 2024
;
Faerman et al. 2017
;
Faerman et al. 2020
;
Mathews & Prochaska 2017
;

;

;
Sormani et al. 2018
;

. These models are computationally inexpensive, and based on a few simple parameters, often provide density,

, column density,

, temperature,
T({\bf r})
, and/or rotational velocity,
V_{\upphi}({\bf r})
, distribution functions.

All points on the surface of a sphere of radius

are given by

X_{h}^{2}+Y_{h}^{2}+Z_{h}^{2}=R^{2}_{h}\,.

(15)

where the subscript “
h

M_{\rm vir}
, which would relate to the virial radius,
R_{\rm vir}
.
We would then define
R_{\hbox{\tiny CGM}}\!=\!R_{h}
, and substitute
R_{\hbox{\tiny CGM}}\!=\!\eta_{\hbox{\tiny CGM}}R_{\rm vir}
, into Eq.
15
where
\eta_{\hbox{\tiny CGM}}\in(0,\infty]
is a free parameter. The choice of
\eta_{\hbox{\tiny CGM}}
should be physically motivated based on theory or observations. Following
Shull 2014
, we point out the vagaries in the nomenclature “extended gas in galactic halos,” “CGM,” “filaments,” and “IGM,” as adopted by observational astronomers; if one aims to enclose all gravitationally bound gas within their adopted CGM boundary, then the CGM can extend beyond the virial radius (
\eta_{\hbox{\tiny CGM}}>1
), but then one would likely enclose some gas at the CGM/IGM interface that is unbound.

To obtain the spatial location where the LOS probes the sphere, we equate Eq.
15
and Eq.
9
with
R_{h}=R_{\hbox{\tiny CGM}}
, and write,

(X_{0}\!+\!\sigma_{x}t)^{2}+(Y_{0}\!+\!\sigma_{y}t)^{2}+(Z_{0}\!+\!\sigma_{z}t)^{2}=R^{2}_{\hbox{\tiny CGM}}\,,

(16)

which yields a quadratic equation of the form

f(t)=At^{2}+Bt+C=0\,,

(17)

with solution

t=\frac{-B\pm\sqrt{\Delta}}{2A}\,,

(18)

where
\Delta=B^{2}-4AC
is the determinant, and where the geometric coefficients are

\begin{array}[]{rcl}A&=&\!1\,,\\[3.0pt]
B&=&\!2(\sigma_{x}X_{0}+\sigma_{y}Y_{0}+\sigma_{z}Z_{0})\,,\\[3.0pt]
C&=&\!R^{2}_{\perp}-R^{2}_{\hbox{\tiny CGM}}\,,\end{array}

(19)

employing
\sigma_{x}^{2}+\sigma_{y}^{2}+\sigma_{z}^{2}=1
and
X_{0}^{2}+Y_{0}^{2}+Z_{0}^{2}=R^{2}_{\perp}
.

For
R_{\perp}<R_{\hbox{\tiny CGM}}
, we have
\Delta>0
and there are two points of intersection, one on each side the sky plane. If
R_{\perp}\!=\!R_{\hbox{\tiny CGM}}
, there is one intersection point tangent to the surface at
t_{0}=0
(on the sky plane). For
R_{\perp}>R_{\hbox{\tiny CGM}}
, we have
\Delta<0
and there is no intersection. When there are two points of intersection, we adopt the convention that
t_{1}<t_{2}
. The gas structure is being probed by the LOS for all
t\in(t_{1},t_{2})
. This domain of
t
always satisfies the trivial condition

r(t)\leq R_{\hbox{\tiny CGM}}\,.

(20)

where
r(t)
is given by Eq.
11
. Note that Eq.
20
is equivalent to the condition
f(t)\geq 0
.

The Bi-Polar Wind

Observations indicate clear trends between outflow and inflow velocities and galaxy inclination, star formation rate, and stellar mass that are consistent with bi-polar outflow and planar accretion spatial kinematic distributions

(
Bordoloi et al. 2011
;
Bouché et al. 2012
;
Kacprzak et al. 2012
;
Rubin et al. 2012
;
Rubin et al. 2014
;
Chisholm et al. 2015
;
Nielsen et al. 2015
;
Rupke 2018
;
Zhang 2018
;
Roberts-Borsani & Saintonge 2019
;
Veilleux et al. 2020
;
Bizyaev et al. 2022a
, e.g.,)
. Simulations are generally consistent with bi-polar outflows, as they predict that outflowing gas driven by stellar winds and/or supernovae is preferentially directed perpendicular to the disk
(
Nelson et al. 2019
;
Péroux et al. 2020
;
Trapp et al. 2022
;
Faucher-Giguère & Oh 2023
, e.g.,)
.

. Multiple other scenarios can occur, such as the highly special case when the LOS intersects tangent to the structure surface at a single point,

.

A cone or hyperboloid of one sheet serves as an appropriate geometry for modeling bi-polar outflowing winds
(
Bustard et al. 2016
;
Rupke 2018
;
Zhang 2018
;
Fielding & Bryan 2022
;
Nguyen & Thompson 2022
, e.g.,)
. As concluded by
Rupke 2018
in their review of stellar driven galactic winds, “The simple model of a modest-velocity, biconical flow of multiphase gas and dust perpendicular to galaxy disks continues to be a robust descriptor of these flows.” Indeed, an idealized cone model of the spatial distribution of bi-polar winds for interpreting quasar absorption line data has been explored and supported by several observational studies
(
Bouché et al. 2012
;
Chen et al. 2014
;
Schroetter et al. 2019
;
Zabl et al. 2020
;
Beckett et al. 2023
, e.g.,)
.

For a conical surface, assuming the
z
axis is
the axis of symmetry, we have

\frac{X_{w}^{2}}{a^{2}}+\frac{Y_{w}^{2}}{b^{2}}-\frac{Z_{w}^{2}}{c^{2}}=d\,,

(21)

where
d=0
maps the upper and lower nappes of a cone and
d=1
maps the continuous surface of a hyperboloid of one sheet. For conic sections with circular cross sections,
b=a
. The inverse tangent of the ratio
a/c
defines the opening angle, which we will hereafter call the wind opening angle,
\Theta_{w}
, where
\tan\Theta_{w}=a/c
. We have adopted the subscript “
w
” to denote “wind.”

A shortcoming of a cone geometry is that there is a vertex at the origin; the wind would thus originate from a single point at the galaxy center. Studies of winds indicate that they are launched and entrain material over extended region of the galaxy disk
(
Nguyen & Thompson 2022
, see)
. An extended base can be modeled by adopting a hyperboloid of one sheet. The key difference between the hyperboloid (
d=1
) and the cone (
d=0
) is that the geometric length
a
now defines the hyperboloid “skirt radius,” which is the radius of the circular cross section in the
z=0
plane. We will adopt the notation
\rho_{w,0}
for the skirt radius and hereafter denote it as the base radius of the wind.

The parametric equations for the surface of a hyperboloid of one sheet are written

\begin{array}[]{rcl}X_{w}&=&\!\left(\rho_{w,0}^{2}+z^{2}\tan^{2}\Theta_{w}\right)^{1/2}\cos\phi\,,\\[3.0pt]
Y_{w}&=&\!\left(\rho_{w,0}^{2}+z^{2}\tan^{2}\Theta_{w}\right)^{1/2}\sin\phi\,,\\[4.0pt]
Z_{w}&=&\!z\,.\end{array}

(22)

In Eq.
22
, a cone is trivially recovered from the hyperboloid when the base radius of the wind is nulled, i.e.,
\rho_{w,0}=0
. As written, Eq.
22
, represents a hyperboloid of infinite height above and below the
z=0
(galactic) plane. If we wish to give the “wind” a finite extent we can apply a “cap,” or maximum wind radius,
R_{w}=\eta_{w}R_{\rm vir}
, where
\eta_{w}\in(0,1)
would confine the wind structure to extend no further than the boundary of the CGM. However, values of
\eta_{w}>1
can be explored.

As with the spherical structure, the point(s) of intersection of the LOS with the surfaces of the cone or hyperboloid wind model are determined by equating the LOS position at
t
with the surface points. For the following, we first assume a structure of infinite extent and later apply constraints for a structure of finite extent. Equating Eq.
22
and Eq.
9
yields,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\left(\rho_{w,0}^{2}+z^{2}\tan^{2}\Theta_{w}\right)^{1/2}\cos\phi&=&\!X_{0}+\sigma_{x}t\,,\\[3.0pt]
\left(\rho_{w,0}^{2}+z^{2}\tan^{2}\Theta_{w}\right)^{1/2}\sin\phi&=&\!Y_{0}+\sigma_{y}t\,,\\[5.0pt]
z&=&\!Z_{0}+\sigma_{z}t\,.\end{array}

(23)

We substitute
z
to obtain

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\left[\rho_{w,0}^{2}+T^{2}_{\Theta}(Z_{0}\!+\!\sigma_{z}t)^{2}\right]^{1/2}\cos\phi&=&\!X_{0}+\sigma_{x}t\,,\\[4.0pt]
\left[\rho_{w,0}^{2}+T^{2}_{\Theta}(Z_{0}\!+\!\sigma_{z}t)^{2}\right]^{1/2}\sin\phi&=&\!Y_{0}+\sigma_{y}t\,,\end{array}

(24)

where we have introduced the notation
T_{\Theta}=\tan\Theta_{w}
. We square both equations and add them. After rearranging and collecting terms of
t^{2}
and
t
, we obtain the quadratic equation given by Eq.
17
with geometric coefficients

\begin{array}[]{rcl}A&=&\!\sigma_{z}^{2}T^{2}_{\Theta}-\sigma_{x}^{2}-\sigma_{y}^{2}\,,\\[3.0pt]
B&=&\!2(\sigma_{z}Z_{0}T^{2}_{\Theta}-\sigma_{x}X_{0}-\sigma_{y}Y_{0})\,,\\[3.0pt]
C&=&\!\rho^{2}_{w,0}+Z^{2}_{0}T^{2}_{\Theta}-(X^{2}_{0}\!+\!Y^{2}_{0})\,,\end{array}

(25)

and solution given by Eq.
18
. In Eq.
18
, if
\Delta<0
there is no intersection. If
\Delta=0
there is a single intersection point at
t_{1}=-B/2A
. If
\Delta>0
, then there are two points of intersection,
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
. As with the spherical model, we adopt
t_{1}<t_{2}
. In Figure
6
, we present multiple examples of possible LOS intersections with the wind structure.

Alternatively, the LOS probes the wind structure wherever it resides within the wind surface and this can be established by testing for the condition that the axial distance of the LOS position,
\rho(t)=[x^{2}(t)+y^{2}(t)]^{1/2}
, is smaller than the axial distance of the wind surface,
\rho_{w}(t)
. Applying this condition yields

\rho^{2}(t)\leq\rho^{2}_{w,0}+T^{2}_{\Theta}z^{2}(t)\,.

(26)

If we substitute Eq.
9
into Eq.

, we obtain a quadratic inequality of the form
f(t)\geq 0
, where
f(t)
is given by Eq.
17
invoking Eq.

(27)

are simultaneously satisfied.

3.3
The Galaxy Disk

For the galaxy disk, we adopt a cylindrical structure with axial radius
\rho_{d}
and height
h_{d}
. The parametric equations for the surface of this cylinder are

X_{d}=\rho_{d}\cos\phi\,,\quad Y_{d}=\rho_{d}\sin\phi\,,\quad Z_{d}=z\,,

(28)

where we have adopted the subscript “
d
” to denote “disk,” and where
z\in[-h_{d},+h_{d}]
. However, this would be a model component more appropriate for the ISM, rather than the CGM.

To obtain the LOS locations probing the disk, we equate the
x
and

components of Eq.
28
and Eq.
9
, and square and sum them to obtain

(X_{0}+\sigma_{x}t)^{2}+(Y_{0}+\sigma_{y}t)^{2}=\rho_{d}^{2}\,,

(29)

which yields the quadratic equation

given by Eq.

, with geometric coefficients

\begin{array}[]{rcl}A&=&\!\sigma_{x}^{2}+\sigma_{y}^{2}\,,\\[3.0pt]

C&=&\!X^{2}_{0}+Y^{2}_{0}-\rho_{d}^{2}\,,\end{array}

(30)

and solution given by Eq.
18
. Since the disk is given a finite height,

, we find that the LOS is probing the disk when the conditions

f(t)\leq 0\quad\hbox{and}\quad|z(t)|\leq h_{d}\,,

Disk warping can be approximated using a simple flare model, such as the one adopted by

h_{d}(\rho)=h_{d}(0)\exp\{(\rho\!-\!\rho_{f,0})/\rho_{f}\}\,,

(32)

valid for

, where
\rho_{f}
is the exponential flare scale length, and
\rho_{f,0}
is the axial radius at which the flare begins. For the Milky Way,

adopted

kpc,
\rho_{f}=9.8
kpc, and
\rho_{f,0}=5
kpc. For this augmentation to the disk model, the region where the LOS probes the disk is still given by Eq

h_{d}(t)
replacing the constant

. In this work, we will not treat the flaring of the disk using Eq.

3.4
Extended Planar Accretion

Theoretically, planar accretion is predicted to be a significant component to the building of galaxy disks
(
Kereš et al. 2005
;
Stewart et al. 2011
;
Stewart et al. 2013
;
Stewart et al. 2017
;
Stewart 2017
;
van de Voort et al. 2011
;
Hafen et al. 2022
;
Trapp et al. 2022
;
Gurvich et al. 2023
;
Kocjan et al. 2024
;
Stern et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
. In simulations,
Stewart et al. 2011
;
Stewart et al. 2013
reported the formation of a transient “cold flow disk” structure around their galaxies. These massive extended planar structures were often warped, not rotationally supported, and comprise inflowing cold halo gas. Most interestingly, these structures are kinematically aligned with the angular momentum of the central galaxy rather than with the angular momentum of the inflowing cold filamentary gas.

Subsequent theoretical work using hydrodynamic cosmological simulations has confirmed that the infalling material spirals inward, and as doing so, tends to align with the plane of the galactic disk (see Fig. 2 of
Hafen et al. 2022
and Fig. 1 of
Stern et al. 2024
).
Trapp et al. 2022
shows that this accretion fans inward over a flare angle ranging from
15^{\circ}
–
30^{\circ}
. According to
Hafen et al. 2022
,
Trapp et al. 2022
, and
Stern et al. 2024
rotational support occurs within a narrow annulus at the galaxy disk edge.

As such, in terms of an idealized spatial structure, an extended planar accretion CGM component can be modeled as a flared extension to the galactic disk. Geometrically, this simple spatial structure should exhibit azimuthal symmetry and have a vertical height that increases with axial distance from the galaxy center. A modified hyperboloid of one sheet is well suited. In addition to having an azimuthal symmetry about the galaxy rotation axis, the hyperboloid skirt radius can be interpreted as the axial distance at which the accretion interfaces to the galactic disk.

To model the expected spatial location of the extended planar accreting gas, we modify Eq.
22
by replacing the wind opening angle
\Theta_{w}
with the complementary angle
\Theta_{a}
, which we call the accretion opening angle, or “flare” angle.
As illustrated schematically in Figure
5

(36)

are simultaneously satisfied.

4
Velocity Fields and LOS Velocities

The motions of the gas associated with given dynamical structures, such as galactic winds, extended galactic disks, intergalactic infall and accretion, recycling gas, and galactic fountains, etc., can be modeled by simple velocity fields in which the velocity vector of the material is defined over a range of spatial regions. We define a velocity field as the vector field
{\hbox{\bf V}}({\hbox{\bf r}})
at spatial location
r
in coordinate system
G
. At LOS location
t
, the LOS velocity is the dot product of the velocity vector at
{\hbox{\bf r}}={\hbox{\bf r}}(t)
and the LOS unit vector,

V_{\hbox{\tiny LOS}}(t)={\hbox{\bf V}}(t)\cdot{\hat{\hbox{\bf s}}}\,,

(38)

where the LOS unit vector is
{\hat{\hbox{\bf s}}}=\sigma_{x}{\hat{\hbox{\bf e}}_{x}}+\sigma_{y}{\hat{\hbox{\bf e}}_{y}}+\sigma_{z}{\hat{\hbox{\bf e}}_{z}}
(see Eq.
92
) and
{\hbox{\bf r}}(t)=x(t){\hat{\hbox{\bf e}}_{x}}+y(t){\hat{\hbox{\bf e}}_{y}}+z(t){\hat{\hbox{\bf e}}_{z}}
, where
x(t)
,
y(t)
, and
z(t)
are given by Eq.
10
. Note that the LOS velocity is negative when it is directed toward the observer and is positive when directed away from the observer.

Any velocity field can be defined in terms of its principle velocity components. The principle components are those parallel to the principle unit vectors in the coordinate system. Once defined, the principle components can be flexibly combined via vector addition to describe general velocity fields applicable to the kinematics of the CGM. Here, we derive the LOS velocities for the principle components of an arbitrary velocity field. We consider radially directed velocities,
{\hbox{\bf V}}_{r}({\hbox{\bf r}})=V_{r}({\hbox{\bf r}})\,{\hat{\hbox{\bf e}}_{r}}({\hbox{\bf r}})
, azimuthal velocities,
{\hbox{\bf V}}_{\upphi}({\hbox{\bf r}})=V_{\upphi}({\hbox{\bf r}})\,{\hat{\hbox{\bf e}}_{\upphi}}({\hbox{\bf r}})
, polar orbital velocities,
{\hbox{\bf V}}_{\uptheta}({\hbox{\bf r}})=V_{\uptheta}({\hbox{\bf r}})\,{\hat{\hbox{\bf e}}_{\uptheta}}({\hbox{\bf r}})
, vertical velocities,
{\hbox{\bf V}}_{z}({\hbox{\bf r}})=V_{z}({\hbox{\bf r}})\,{\hat{\hbox{\bf e}}_{z}}
, and axial flow,
{\hbox{\bf V}}_{\uprho}({\hbox{\bf r}})=V_{\uprho}({\hbox{\bf r}})\,{\hat{\hbox{\bf e}}_{\uprho}}({\hbox{\bf r}})
. We present convenient multiplicative “LOS projection functions” for each principle component.

4.1
Radial Velocities

If we assume a purely radial directed velocity,
V_{r}({\hbox{\bf r}})
, we have
{\hbox{\bf V}}_{r}(t)=V_{r}(t){\,\hat{\hbox{\bf e}}_{r}}(t)
, where the unit radial vector at LOS position
t
is

{\hat{\hbox{\bf e}}_{r}}(t)=\frac{{\bf r}(t)}{r(t)}=\frac{x(t)}{r(t)}\,{\hat{\hbox{\bf e}}_{x}}+\frac{y(t)}{r(t)}\,{\hat{\hbox{\bf e}}_{y}}+\frac{z(t)}{r(t)}\,{\hat{\hbox{\bf e}}_{z}}\,,

(39)

where
r(t)=[x^{2}(t)+y^{2}(t)+z^{2}(t)]^{1/2}
. We define the LOS projection function as
\mathcal{P}_{r}(t)={\hat{\hbox{\bf e}}_{r}}(t)\cdot{\hat{\hbox{\bf s}}}
. Recalling that
{\hat{\hbox{\bf s}}}=\sigma_{x}{\hat{\hbox{\bf e}}_{x}}+\sigma_{y}{\hat{\hbox{\bf e}}_{y}}+\sigma_{z}{\hat{\hbox{\bf e}}_{z}}
, we have

\mathcal{P}_{r}(t)=\frac{x(t)}{r(t)}\sigma_{x}+\frac{y(t)}{r(t)}\sigma_{y}+\frac{z(t)}{r(t)}\sigma_{z}\,.

(40)

The observed LOS velocity of radially directed velocity at LOS position
t
is then

V^{(r)}_{\hbox{\tiny LOS}}(t)=V_{r}(t)\mathcal{P}_{r}(t)\,.

(41)

Eq.
41
can be employed to model constant velocity spherical outflow or infall. If we wish to model outflow, we adopt
V_{r}({\hbox{\bf r}})>0
. If we wish to model inflow, we adopt
V_{r}({\hbox{\bf r}})<0
.

4.2
Azimuthal/Circular Velocities

For an azimuthal/circular velocity,
V_{\upphi}({\hbox{\bf r}})
, we have
{\hbox{\bf V}}_{\upphi}(t)=V_{\upphi}(t)\,{\hat{\hbox{\bf e}}_{\upphi}}(t)
, where the unit azimuthal rotation vector at LOS position
t
is

{\hat{\hbox{\bf e}}_{\upphi}}(t)=\frac{x(t)}{\rho(t)}\,{\hat{\hbox{\bf e}}_{y}}-\frac{y(t)}{\rho(t)}\,{\hat{\hbox{\bf e}}_{x}}\,,

(42)

and where the axial radius is
\rho(t)=[x^{2}(t)+y^{2}(t)]^{1/2}
. The LOS projection function of the unit azimuthal rotation vector is
\mathcal{P}_{\upphi}(t)={\hat{\hbox{\bf e}}_{\upphi}}(t)\cdot{\hat{\hbox{\bf s}}}
, which is written

\mathcal{P}_{\upphi}(t)=\frac{x(t)}{\rho(t)}\sigma_{y}-\frac{y(t)}{\rho(t)}\sigma_{x}\,.

(43)

The observed LOS velocity of a circular velocity at LOS position
t
is then

V^{(\upphi)}_{\hbox{\tiny LOS}}(t)=V_{\upphi}(t)\mathcal{P}_{\upphi}(t)\,.

(44)

A simple example would be a flat rotation curve, which can be obtained for “solid body” rotation for which we would adopt a constant
V_{\upphi}({\hbox{\bf r}})=V_{\upphi}
.

Vertical and Axial Flow Velocities

For a purely vertical velocities,
V_{z}({\hbox{\bf r}})

{\hbox{\bf V}}_{z}(t)=V_{z}(t)\,{\hat{\hbox{\bf e}}_{z}}
. The LOS projection function is

\mathcal{P}_{x}(t)={\hat{\hbox{\bf e}}_{z}}\!\cdot{\hat{\hbox{\bf s}}}
, which yields

\mathcal{P}_{z}=\sigma_{z}\,,\quad V^{(z)}_{\hbox{\tiny LOS}}(t)=V_{z}(t)\mathcal{P}_{z}\,.

(45)

Note that the vector projection function is independent of LOS position. We also trivially find that

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathcal{P}_{x}&=&\sigma_{x}\,,\quad V^{(x)}_{\hbox{\tiny LOS}}(t)=V_{x}(t)\mathcal{P}_{x}\,,\\[4.0pt]
\mathcal{P}_{y}&=&\sigma_{y}\,,\quad V^{(y)}_{\hbox{\tiny LOS}}(t)=V_{y}(t)\mathcal{P}_{y}\,.\end{array}

(46)

For a purely axial velocity flow,
V_{\uprho}({\hbox{\bf r}})
, we have
{\hbox{\bf V}}_{\uprho}(t)=V_{\uprho}(t){\,\hat{\hbox{\bf e}}_{\uprho}}(t)
, where
the unit axial radius vector at LOS position
t
is

{\hat{\hbox{\bf e}}_{\uprho}}(t)=\frac{x(t)}{\rho(t)}\,{\hat{\hbox{\bf e}}_{x}}+\frac{y(t)}{\rho(t)}\,{\hat{\hbox{\bf e}}_{y}}\,.

(47)

This velocity is pointing directly outward from the polar axis (is divergent) if
V_{\uprho}({\hbox{\bf r}})>0
and directly toward the polar axis (is convergent) if
V_{\uprho}({\hbox{\bf r}})<0
. The LOS projection function is

\mathcal{P}_{\uprho}(t)={\hat{\hbox{\bf e}}_{\uprho}}(t)\cdot{\hat{\hbox{\bf s}}}
, is written

\mathcal{P}_{\uprho}(t)=\frac{x(t)}{\rho(t)}\sigma_{x}+\frac{y(t)}{\rho(t)}\sigma_{y}\,,

(48)

from which we obtain

V^{(\uprho)}_{\hbox{\tiny LOS}}(t)=V_{\uprho}(t)\mathcal{P}_{\uprho}(t)\,.

(49)

4.4
Polar Orbital Velocities

If we assume a purely polar velocity,
V_{\uptheta}({\hbox{\bf r}})
, we have
{\hbox{\bf V}}_{\uptheta}(t)=V_{\uptheta}(t)\,{\hat{\hbox{\bf e}}_{\uptheta}}(t)
, where the unit polar orbit vector at LOS position
t
is

{\hat{\hbox{\bf e}}_{\uptheta}}(t)=\frac{z(t)}{r(t)}\,{\hat{\hbox{\bf e}}_{\uprho}}(t)-\frac{\rho(t)}{r(t)}\,\,{\hat{\hbox{\bf e}}_{z}}

(50)

The LOS projection function is
\mathcal{P}_{\uptheta}(t)={\hat{\hbox{\bf e}}_{\uptheta}}(t)\cdot{\hat{\hbox{\bf s}}}
, which is written

\mathcal{P}_{\uptheta}(t)=\frac{z(t)}{r(t)}\mathcal{P}_{\uprho}(t)-\sigma_{z}\frac{\rho(t)}{r(t)}\,.

(51)

The observed LOS velocity of a polar orbital velocity at LOS position
t
is then

V^{(\uptheta)}_{\hbox{\tiny LOS}}(t)=V_{\uptheta}(t)\mathcal{P}_{\uptheta}(t)\,.

(52)

4.5
The Full Velocity Field

Having expressed the principle velocity vectors and their LOS projection functions, we can construct an arbitrary velocity vector field and determine its LOS velocity projection at any location along the LOS. Consider a velocity field at location
r
with principle velocity components
V_{k}({\hbox{\bf r}}){\hat{\hbox{\bf e}}_{k}}({\hbox{\bf r}})
, where
k
represents a principle component and
{\hat{\hbox{\bf e}}_{k}}({\hbox{\bf r}})
represents its unit vector direction at
r
.
The velocity vector field is written

{\bf V}({\hbox{\bf r}})=\sum_{k}V_{k}({\hbox{\bf r}}){\hat{\hbox{\bf e}}_{k}}({\hbox{\bf r}})\,.

(53)

At LOS location
{\hbox{\bf r}}={\hbox{\bf r}}(t)
, the LOS projected velocity is

V_{\hbox{\tiny LOS}}(t)={\bf V}(t)\!\cdot{\hat{\hbox{\bf s}}}
, which is

\begin{array}[]{rcl}V_{\hbox{\tiny LOS}}(t)=\displaystyle\sum_{k}V_{k}(t)[{\hat{\hbox{\bf e}}_{k}}(t)\!\cdot{\hat{\hbox{\bf s}}}]=\sum_{k}V_{k}(t)\mathcal{P}_{k}(t)\,,\end{array}

(54)

where
\mathcal{P}_{k}(t)
is the LOS projection function for principle component
k
. In Table
2
, we list the principle component projection functions. As we will describe below, unique velocity fields can be spatially “confined” to the various CGM spatial structures using the constraints listed in Table
1
.

5
Idealized CGM Kinematics

In this section, we describe idealized kinematics for the four spatial galaxy/CGM components.

5.1
The Spherical Halo

Though the halo is modeled as a sphere, it can be argued there is no single velocity field that applies throughout the spherical space. By design, portions of this volume are occupied by the wind and the accretion structures and these two spatial regions are expected to be dominated by outflow and inflow, respectively. As such, we could adopt a working definition that halo kinematics, per se, applies within the volume
not
occupied by the disk, wind, and accretion structures. In other words, the LOS probing conditions in Table
1
are simultaneously false for the disk, wind, and accretion while the condition for the halo is true (most simply, where
r(t)\leq R_{\hbox{\tiny CGM}}
).

This halo region can be kinematically tangled due to added complexities such as high-velocity clouds
(
Richter et al. 2015
;
Marasco et al. 2022
)
, tidal streams, such as the Galactic Magellanic Stream
(
D’Onghia & Fox 2016
, e.g.,)
, and polar rings
(
Macciò et al. 2006
;
Brook et al. 2008
;
Spavone et al. 2010
;
Tepper-García et al. 2015
, e.g.,)
, all of which are likely to have a prominent polar component to their kinematics.
Other models characterize the halos as “cloudy with a chance of rain” and invoke accretion braking of cold clouds that results in terminal infall velocities
(
Benjamin & Danly 1997
;
Tan et al. 2023
, e.g.,)
.

A spherically symmetric spatial-kinematic model would have the form
{\bf V}_{h}({\hbox{\bf r}})=V_{r}({\hbox{\bf r}})\,{\hat{\hbox{\bf e}}_{r}}({\hbox{\bf r}})
, with
V_{r}({\hbox{\bf r}})
being positive everywhere for outflow or negative everywhere for infall
(
Lanzetta & Bowen 1992
, e.g.,)
. For pure radial kinematics, Eq.
54
simplifies to Eq.
41
and we obtain
V_{\hbox{\tiny LOS}}^{(h)}(t)=V_{r}(t)\mathcal{P}_{r}(t)
for the LOS velocity. Alternately, the radial velocity
V_{r}({\hbox{\bf r}})
could have a functional form such as
V_{r}(r)=V_{0}(r/r_{0})^{\epsilon}
for a simple radial dependency, where
\epsilon
is a free parameter.

Varieties of radial dependent infall and outflow velocities can be explored. For inflows,
Stern et al. 2024
, for example, developed an analytical model (see their Eq. 11) to estimate the density, temperature, pressure, sound speed, velocity, and Mach number for a radiative cooling adiabatic ideal gas in a gravitational potential. For outflows,
Chisholm et al. 2015
, for example, observationally determined that outflow velocities scale as
V_{r}=V_{c}^{\epsilon}
with
\epsilon=0.44
–
0.87
. We also note that outflow or infall may decelerate. To approximate deceleration, one could invoke a “stall” or “breaking function,”
\mathcal{S}(r)
, which we introduce in Section
5.1.1
.

Another possibility is that the spherical CGM has a rotational component
(
Weisheit 1978
, e.g.,)
, in which case the velocity field is
{\bf V}_{h}({\hbox{\bf r}})={\bf V}_{r}({\hbox{\bf r}})+{\bf V}_{\upphi}({\hbox{\bf r}})
, which yields LOS velocity
V_{\hbox{\tiny LOS}}^{(h)}(t)=V_{r}(t)\mathcal{P}_{r}(t)+V_{\upphi}(t)\mathcal{P}_{\upphi}(t)
, where we note that the rotational velocity vector,
{\bf V}_{\upphi}({\hbox{\bf r}})
, can also be a function of radius or height above the disk, etc.

Polar orbits would be modeled as,
{\bf V}_{h}({\hbox{\bf r}})={\bf V}_{\uptheta}({\hbox{\bf r}})
. For a predominantly polar orbit with a rotational component, we would write
{\bf V}_{h}({\hbox{\bf r}})={\bf V}_{\uptheta}({\hbox{\bf r}})+{\bf V}_{\upphi}({\hbox{\bf r}})
with
|V_{\uptheta}|>|V_{\upphi}|
, which would represent a precessing polar orbit. One could invoke a predominantly polar component with a radial component,
{\bf V}_{h}({\hbox{\bf r}})={\bf V}_{\uptheta}({\hbox{\bf r}})+{\bf V}_{r}({\hbox{\bf r}})
with
|V_{\uptheta}|>|V_{r}|
,, which could represent slowly infalling polar orbit. Finally, we can write a general case for which
{\bf V}_{h}({\hbox{\bf r}})={\bf V}_{\uptheta}({\hbox{\bf r}})+{\bf V}_{\upphi}({\hbox{\bf r}})+{\bf V}_{r}({\hbox{\bf r}})
.

Tidal streams are typically not distributed spherically about a galaxy. A modification of the full spherical spatial structure to form a segment of a spherical shell to model such streams would be straight forward using the formalism of Section
3
, which is designed for flexibility. For example, the surface of a torus is easily computed and has well-behaved closed form solution for the LOS intersection points
(
Skala 2023
, see)
.

5.1.1
Wind Stalling and Accretion Breaking

Whether we are modeling spherical radial outflow or bi-polar outflows confined within cones or hyperboloids, we might expect the gas to decelerate. The wind models of
Fielding & Bryan 2022
, for example, predict that hot galactic winds quickly become supersonic and then, under certain conditions, can stall. Their models predict that in galaxies with
V_{c}\simeq 150

km s
-1
and mass loading factors of
M_{w}/M_{\ast}\simeq 1
–3, the wind velocity is on the order of a 200–300
km s
-1
, and when subject to cooling and gravity, the winds can stall out. The stall heights can vary and the stall can occur over distance scales as small as few percent of the height of the wind material.

A physics-based expression characterizing decelerating wind gas is beyond the scope of this work. What we desire is a flexible function that can model a range of deceleration, either a smooth continuous deceleration as the wind height increases or a stall (rapid deceleration) at arbitrary height above the galactic plane. A normalized modified Fermi-Dirac function provides such flexibility. We introduce the stall function,

{\cal S}(r;\eta_{s},\Delta\eta_{s})=\frac{1+\exp\left\{-\eta_{s}R/\Delta\eta_{s}\right\}}{1+\exp\left\{(r-\eta_{s}R)/\Delta\eta_{s}\right\}}\,,

(55)

where
R
is the maximum extent of the spatial structure and
\eta_{s}
defines the “stall height,”
\eta_{s}R
, at which the outflow velocity has decreased to
\sim\!50
% of its launch velocity. The parameter
\Delta\eta_{s}
defines the “stall ratio,”
\Delta\eta_{s}/\eta_{s}
. The smaller the stall ratio, the more rapid the “stall.” As long as the ratio
\Delta\eta_{s}/\eta_{s}\leq 0.2
, then at height
(\eta_{s}\!-\!\Delta\eta_{s})R
the wind velocity has decreased to
\sim\!75
% of its launch value, and for
(\eta_{s}\!+\!\Delta\eta_{s})R
it has decreased to
\sim\!25
%.

Radially infalling cool clouds in a hot halos will experience “accretion braking.”

5.2
Bi-Polar Outflows/Winds

To ensure that the velocity field associated with the outflows is confined within the geometry of the hyperboloid wind structure, we apply the conditions
f(t)>0
and
r(t)\leq R_{w}
as given by Eq.
27
to identify the LOS locations that probe the wind. Outside the wind structure, we assume there is no wind material and therefore disregard the wind velocity field for all LOS locations external to the wind structure. Wind models can be explored for a range of opening angles,
\Theta_{w}
, wind base radii,
\rho_{w,0}
, and wind heights,
R_{w}
.

A constant radial velocity field,
{\bf V}_{w}({\hbox{\bf r}})=V_{w}\,{\hat{\hbox{\bf e}}_{r}}({\hbox{\bf r}})
, where
V_{w}
is a free parameter, provides the simplest kinematic model for the winds. This radial velocity field works appropriately enough for a wind confined to a cone for which the radial wind trajectories diverge from a single point at the cone vertex. However, for a hyperboloid wind structure with a finite base radius, a radial wind velocity in the plane of the galaxy disk, or at small heights (
|z|\sim h_{d}
) above the disk plane, would have velocity primarily directed horizontally to the galactic plane, when in fact material in this region should be directed vertically and perpendicular to the plane. Thus, we adopt a velocity field of the form

{\bf V}_{w}({\hbox{\bf r}})=V_{\uprho}({\hbox{\bf r}})\,{\hat{\hbox{\bf e}}_{\uprho}}({\hbox{\bf r}})+V_{z}({\hbox{\bf r}}){\,\hat{\hbox{\bf e}}_{z}}\,,

(60)

and determine
V_{\uprho}({\hbox{\bf r}})
and
V_{z}({\hbox{\bf r}})
such that
{\bf V}_{w}({\hbox{\bf r}})
is perpendicular to the plane for small
|z|
, i.e., we achieve
|V_{\uprho}({\hbox{\bf r}})|\ll|V_{z}({\hbox{\bf r}})|
and asymptotically approaches radial for large
|z|
, i.e.,
|V_{\uprho}({\hbox{\bf r}})|\simeq|V_{z}({\hbox{\bf r}})|
.

For opening angle
\tan\Theta_{w}=T_{\Theta}=\rho_{w,0}/c
, the streamline trajectories bound within a hyperboloid would follow paths constrained by
(\rho/\tilde{a})^{2}+(z/c)^{2}=1
, where
c=\rho_{w,0}/T_{\Theta}
. Rearranging and substituting
c
, we write

\rho^{2}=\tilde{a}^{2}+z^{2}\left(\frac{\tilde{a}}{c}\right)^{2}=\tilde{a}^{2}\left[1+T^{2}_{\Theta}\left(\frac{z}{\rho_{w,0}}\right)^{2}\right]\,,

(61)

where
\tilde{a}
is the azimuthal distance in the disk plane at which the trajectory is launched. Adopting
V_{\uprho}=d\rho/dt
and
V_{z}=dz/dt
and differentiating both sides yields,

V_{\uprho}({\hbox{\bf r}})=\frac{z}{\rho}\left(\frac{\tilde{a}}{\rho_{w,0}}\right)^{2}\!\!T^{2}_{\Theta}\,V_{z}({\hbox{\bf r}})\,.

(62)

Solving for
\tilde{a}
in Eq.
61
and substituting into Eq.
62
, we obtain
V_{\uprho}({\hbox{\bf r}})=\Upsilon({\hbox{\bf r}})|V_{z}({\hbox{\bf r}})|
, where

\Upsilon({\hbox{\bf r}})=\Upsilon(\rho,z)=\frac{|z|\rho T^{2}_{\Theta}}{\rho^{2}_{w,0}+z^{2}T^{2}_{\Theta}}\,.

(63)

From
V_{w}^{2}({\hbox{\bf r}})=V^{2}_{\uprho}({\hbox{\bf r}})+V_{z}^{2}({\hbox{\bf r}})
, we derive

V_{\uprho}({\hbox{\bf r}})=\displaystyle\frac{\Upsilon({\hbox{\bf r}})V_{w}}{\sqrt{1+\Upsilon^{2}({\hbox{\bf r}})}}\,,\quad V_{z}({\hbox{\bf r}})=\displaystyle\frac{{\rm sgn}(z)V_{w}}{\sqrt{1+\Upsilon^{2}({\hbox{\bf r}})}}\,,

(64)

where
V_{w}
is a free parameter. The signum function ensures that the vertical component of the wind velocity,
V_{z}({\hbox{\bf r}})
, is always outflowing. Note that
\Upsilon({\hbox{\bf r}})=0
for
z=0
(in the galactic plane) and/or for
\rho=0
(along the wind axis), yielding
V_{\uprho}(\rho,0)=V_{\uprho}(0,z)=0
and also
V_{z}(\rho,{z\!\rightarrow\!0})=V_{w}
. A schematic of this wind kinematic model is illustrated in Figure
11
.

The LOS wind velocity is then

V_{\hbox{\tiny LOS}}^{(w)}(t)=\frac{V_{w}\left[\Upsilon(t)P_{\uprho}(t)+{\rm sgn}(z(t))P_{z}\right]}{\sqrt{1+\Upsilon^{2}(t)}}\,.

(65)

For galaxies that do not classify as starburst or ultra-luminous galaxies, the maximum outflow velocities are
V_{w}\sim 200

km s
-1
and have a mild dependence on the central star formation surface density
(
Chisholm et al. 2015
;
Bizyaev et al. 2022a
)
.
Chisholm et al. 2015
reports outflow velocities relationships with star formation rate,
V_{w}\propto SFR^{\,0.08-0.2}
, stellar mass,
V_{w}\propto M_{\ast}^{0.12-0.20}
, and circular velocity,
V_{w}=V_{c}^{0.44-0.87}
. Further, guidance on the magnitude of

can be gleaned from theoretical works
(

;
Fielding & Bryan 2022
;
Nguyen & Thompson 2022
)
and observational scaling relations between galactic outflows and their host galaxies
(
Rupke et al. 2005
;

, e.g.,)
. In addition, observationally and/or theoretically inspired variations can easily be introduced to the wind velocity field, for example deceleration or stalling of outflows and/or axial variations in the velocity flows.

5.2.2
Enhanced Wind Core Velocity

We might expect that velocities of the outflowing wind material launched nearest the galactic polar axis are higher than those launched nearest the “walls” of the wind structure (along the circumference of the wind base radius,
\rho_{w,0}
). As a first-order approximation, this expectation may serve to account for a higher kinetic energy density injected by supernovae in the galactic nucleus that drives the core of the wind, while kinetic energy is lowered due ISM entrained along the wall of the wind structure
(
Bustard et al. 2016
;
Rupke 2018
;
Zhang 2018
;
Gronke & Oh 2020
;
Fielding & Bryan 2022
, e.g.,)
.

One approach to modeling this enhanced outflow velocity in the wind core is to employ a functional form that exhibits axial symmetry with peak amplitude on the wind axis. We write the “enhanced wind” velocity as

V^{\prime}_{w}({\hbox{\bf r}})=V_{w}(r)+V_{\hbox{\tiny E}}(\rho,z)\,,

(68)

where
V_{w}(r)
can be the characteristic wind speed, i.e.,
V_{w}(r)=V_{w}
. We define
V_{\hbox{\tiny E}}(\rho,z)=\tilde{V}_{\hbox{\tiny E}}f_{\hbox{\tiny E}}(\rho,z)
as the “enhancement velocity,” where
\tilde{V}_{\hbox{\tiny E}}
is amplitude in velocity units, which can be positive or negative in magnitude, and where
f_{\hbox{\tiny E}}(\rho,z)
is an arbitrary axial-symmetric “enhancement function.” The enhancement function is required to have the properties that, at every height
z
,
f_{\hbox{\tiny E}}(0,z)=1
(on the wind axis) and
f_{\hbox{\tiny E}}(\rho_{w}(z),z)
vanishes at
\rho_{w}(z)=[\rho_{w,0}^{2}+z^{2}T^{2}_{\Theta}]^{1/2}
(on the surface of the wind. Thus, we see that the enhancement is evaluated over the axial range
\rho(z)\in[0,\rho_{w}(z)]
for the domain
z\in[0,\pm R_{w}]
. With this formalism, the velocity along the wind surface will be
V^{\prime}_{w}(\rho_{w}(z),z)=V_{w}
and in the wind core will be
V^{\prime}_{w}(0,z)=V_{w}+\tilde{V}_{\hbox{\tiny E}}
.

In Appendix
D
, we further discuss the properties of enhancement functions and provide selected examples. One simple enhancement function is a truncated cosine function,
f_{\hbox{\tiny E}}(\rho,z)=\cos\{(\pi/2)(\rho/\rho_{w}(z))\}
, which is illustrated in Figure
19
(c). Wind models using this truncated cosine enhancement function are illustrated in Figure

(b,c). In panel
12
(b), we show enhancement in the core with
\tilde{V}_{\hbox{\tiny E}}=5V_{w}
with no wind stalling. For example, if
V_{w}=50
km s
-1
, then the speed of the wind along the surface of the structure is
50
km s
-1
and the speed along the central axis of the wind is
250
km s
-1
. We can include stalling of enhanced wind models by employing the stalling function. This latter scenario is illustrated in Figure
12
(c) with
\tilde{V}_{\hbox{\tiny E}}=10V_{w}
and stalling function parameters
\eta_{s}=0.8
and
\Delta\eta_{s}=0.1
, which are the same as those employed for the illustration in panel

(a).

Since arbitrary enhancement functions can be applied, there is a great deal of versatility for approximating theoretical expectations. As we will explore in
30
, similar “enhancement” functions can serve to model the density and temperature profiles to approximate spatially varying changes in the gas phases.

, we obtain the LOS velocity

(69)

where we have incorporated enhanced velocity field
V^{\prime}_{w}({\hbox{\bf r}})
and wind stalling.

5.3
Galactic Disks

The morphology and kinematics of gaseous galactic disks have exhibit turbulence, asymmetry, and warps
(
Sancisi et al. 2008
;
Marasco & Fraternali 2011
, e.g.,)
. Nevertheless, suitable idealistic models can provide insight into the absorption signatures of the galaxy disk itself. A simple approach would be to confine the disk material to a cylinder with axial radius
\rho_{d}
and height
h_{d}
, as given by Eq.
28
. This might be appropriate for an ISM component to the model. However, we will adopt a more general approach in which the velocity field and (as will be discussed in
30
) the density field will define the “the disk” extent. Here, we discuss the kinematics.

5.3.1
Disk and Extra-planar Gas

For ISM gas, the simplest kinematic model is solid-disk rotation,
{\bf V}({\hbox{\bf r}})=V_{c}\,{\hat{\hbox{\bf e}}_{\upphi}}({\hbox{\bf r}})
confined within the disk spatial structure. Here,
V_{c}
could be the maximum circular velocity employed to defined the galaxy virial mass and radius (see Eq.
3
). However, the galactic rotation of extra-planar gas (EPG) is seen to slow with increasing height above the disk
(
Sancisi et al. 2008
;
Zschaechner et al. 2015
;
Zschaechner & Rand 2015
;
Bizyaev et al. 2017
;
Bizyaev et al. 2022b
;
Levy et al. 2019
, e.g.,)
.

The origin of this rotational lag gradient
is not fully understood. Accretion from galactic fountains
(
Bregman 1980
)
is expected to occur because of cooling/condensation of the hot CGM (corona) triggered by the fountain predicts a disc growth rate compatible with the observations
(
Fall & Efstathiou 1980
, e.g.,)
. Fountain-driven corona condensation is also a likely mechanism to sustain star formation, as well as the disc inside-out growth in local disc galaxies
(
Marasco & Fraternali 2011
;
Tan et al. 2023
)
. Based on absorption lines from both the Galaxy and from external galaxies, the observed gas kinematics in the “lower halo” is inferred to trace galactic fountain flows that persist to
R_{\perp}>5
kpc
(
Marasco & Fraternali 2011
;
Rubin et al. 2022
)
. Simulations indicate that galactic fountains have complex mixing histories in the lower halo in that their angular momentum is a result of interactions with lower angular momentum wind-recycled gas and with higher angular momentum accreting IGM gas such that prograde accretion enhances their angular momentum whereas retrograde accretion reduce their angular momentum
(
Grand et al. 2019
, e.g.,)
.

Bizyaev et al. 2017
;
Bizyaev et al. 2022b
reported that the lag correlates with galactic stellar mass, rotation curve amplitude, and central stellar velocity dispersion; they suggested larger lags are preferentially observed to be associated with higher-mass early-type disk galaxies as a consequence of higher proportions of infalling IGM gas relative to the local extra-planar gas in these galaxies as compared to lower-mass (slower rotating) galaxies
(
Fraternali et al. 2007
, also see)
. An alternative hypothesis is that more massive galaxies host hotter more massive gaseous halos that interact with the EPG and induce lagging velocities
(
Marinacci et al. 2011
;
Pezzulli et al. 2017
;
Sormani et al. 2018
, e.g.,)
. Theoretical predictions from galactic fountains are that larger extra-planar velocity lags are expected in the central regions of galaxies as compared to their peripheries due to momentum conservation

(
Bregman 1980
;
Fraternali et al. 2007
;
Marasco & Fraternali 2011
;
Grand et al. 2019
;
Li et al. 2023
, e.g.,)
.

In view of EPG rotational lags, an alternative approach to modeling galaxy disk kinematics is to eliminate a physical geometric disk boundary and specify a velocity field reflecting EPG halo lag. This approach was adopted by
Steidel et al. 2002
, who allowed for an inclined disk of infinite radius and height, but introduced an exponential scale height,
H_{v}
, to the circular velocity,

{\bf V}({\hbox{\bf r}})=V_{\upphi}(\rho,0)\exp\left\{-|z|/H_{v}\right\}{\hat{\hbox{\bf e}}_{\upphi}}({\hbox{\bf r}})\,,

(70)

where
V_{\upphi}(\rho,0)
is the rotation velocity in the galaxy plane (
z=0)
at axial radius
\rho
on the galaxy disk.

Steidel et al. 2002
adopted
V_{\upphi}(\rho,0)=V_{c}
. Many studies have investigated rotational kinematics in quasar absorption line systems using this “Steidel model”
(
Kacprzak et al. 2010
;
Kacprzak et al. 2011
;
Kacprzak et al. 2019
;
Ho et al. 2017
;
Ho & Martin 2020
;
Martin et al. 2019
;
French & Wakker 2020
;
Beckett et al. 2023
, e.g.,)
. Alternatively,
Bizyaev et al. 2017
characterized rotation lags using the velocity model

{\bf V}({\hbox{\bf r}})=\left[V_{\upphi}(\rho,0)+|z|\frac{dV_{\upphi}}{dz}\right]{\hat{\hbox{\bf e}}_{\upphi}}({\hbox{\bf r}})\,,

(71)

where

V_{\upphi}(\rho,0)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcl}\displaystyle V_{c}(\rho/\rho_{d,0})&&(\rho\leq\rho_{d,0})\\[4.0pt]
\displaystyle V_{c}+(\rho-\rho_{d,0})\frac{dV_{\upphi}}{d\rho}&&(\rho>\rho_{d,0})\,,\end{array}\right.

where
\rho_{d,0}
is the axial radius on the disk out to which the rotation curve increases linearly. Typically
{\rho_{d,0}\simeq 0.1\rho_{d}}
. In Eq.
71
, the rotation cut-off height is
|z|=V_{c}/|dV_{\upphi}/dz|

-1

5.3.2
Axial and Vertical Flows

Models of the Milky Way gaseous disk have required not only lag gradients above the plane, but also a vertical infall component and axial “migration” of gas in the plane. For example, the best fit model by
Marasco & Fraternali 2011
included a lag component of
dV_{\upphi}/dz\simeq-15

km s
-1
kpc

(

Bizyaev et al. 2022b

vertical infall onto the galactic plane with
V_{z}\simeq-20

km s
-1

V_{\uprho}\simeq-30

km s

Qu et al. 2020

km s

km s
-1

V_{\uprho}\simeq-40

These observationally constrained velocities are, for the most part, consistent with theoretical expectations; for example, the vertical infall velocity component may be related to the condensation of baryonic gas above the galactic plane that then “rains” on to the disk

;
Tan et al. 2023

The inclusion of a vertical infall and/or axial inward migration into the kinematic model for the disk would be straight forward. We could assume a constant vertical velocity of the form

and/or axial velocity component,
{\bf V}({\hbox{\bf r}})=V_{\uprho}{\hat{\hbox{\bf e}}_{\uprho}}({\hbox{\bf r}})

V_{z}>0

for axial inward migration

5.4
Extended Planar Accretion

The spatial scale heights of the ISM and EPG from galactic fountains and accretion are on the order of 1–10 kpc
(
Sancisi et al. 2008
;
Gaensler et al. 2008
;
Li et al. 2023
, e.g.)
. As such, spatial-kinematic models of the ISM and EPG are most relevant for LOS with impact parameters on the order of
R_{\perp}\leq\rho_{d}

(
Bregman et al. 2013
;
Rubin et al. 2022
, e.g.,)
.

As we mentioned in Section
3.4
, extended planar accretion in the form of inward spiraling infall kinematics is suggested by theory
(
Kereš et al. 2005
;
Stewart et al. 2011
;
Stewart et al. 2013
;
Stewart et al. 2017
;
Danovich et al. 2015
;
DeFelippis et al. 2021
;
Hafen et al. 2022
;
Trapp et al. 2022
;
Gurvich et al. 2023
;
Stern et al. 2024
;
Kocjan et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
.
Absorption line observations of low-ionization gas also corroborate a coupling between the angular momentum of the rotating galaxy disk and extended planar gas
(
Steidel et al. 2002
;
Kacprzak et al. 2010
;
Kacprzak et al. 2025
;
Ho et al. 2017
;
Martin et al. 2019
;
Zabl et al. 2019
, e.g.,)
.
Such signatures of extended planar accretion are expected if the formation of galactic disks is predominantly outside-in
(
Fall & Efstathiou 1980
, see)
.

In simulations of
z\simeq 0
Milky-Way like galaxies,
Trapp et al. 2022
found that planar infalling gas approaches the galaxy disk such that it nearly matches the angular momentum at the outer edge of the gaseous disk. On approach, the inward radial velocities are suppressed as the gas piles-up on the disk edge and enters a regime of rotational support. From there the accreting gas slowly moves axially inward parallel to the disk plane and, joining the galaxy in the disk outskirts. Similar findings were reported by
Hafen et al. 2022
for the formation of thin, star-forming disk galaxies (but not for warped and amorphous disks). The simulations of
Stern et al. 2024
and
Kocjan et al. 2024
corroborated these findings.
Stern et al. 2024
also derived an azimuthally symmetric analytical model for the rotating inflowing gas in the slow rotating limit that provides analytical expressions for
V_{r}(r,\theta)
,
V_{\upphi}(r,\theta)
, and
V_{\uptheta}(r,\theta)
.

We will show that a simple Keplerian model of the infall gas trajectories can be configured to capture many of the essential properties just described. In particular, the infall trajectories described by these models are inspired by the trajectories shown in Fig. 11 of
Kocjan et al. 2024
.

5.4.1
Keplerian Velocity Fields

A viable kinematic model for infalling spiral paths of the accreting material is a Keplerian trajectory using conic sections with the center of the galaxy at a focus point.
2
2

2

We also examined the kinematics along logarithmic spiral trajectories
\rho=\rho_{0}e^{k\phi}

(
Dürer 1525

ewige Linie

The material falls inward along a conic section orbital path from a galactocentric axial distance
\rho_{2}
to its periapsis at
\rho_{1}

\rho_{1}

The general parametric form of a trajectory on a conic section is

\rho=\frac{\ell}{1+e\cos\nu}

(76)

where
\rho
is the distance from the focus (galactic center) as a function of the true anomaly,
\nu
, and where
\ell=h^{2}/\mu
is the semi-latus rectum with gravitational parameter
\mu=GM(r)
and specific angular momentum,
h
. If we enforce velocity
v_{1}
at the periapsis
\rho_{1}
, we have
h=\rho_{1}v_{1}
and
\mu=\rho_{1}v_{1}^{2}/(e+1)
. We then have
\ell=\rho_{1}(e+1)
. The eccentricity is
0<e<1
for an ellipse,
e=1
for a parabola, and
e>1
for a hyperbola.

For an ellipse the trajectories are bound and the specific energy is negative,
\epsilon=v^{2}_{\rm e}/\rho-\mu/r=-\mu/2a
, from which we have the
vis-viva
equation giving the instantaneous orbital speed
v_{\rm e}(\rho)
,

v_{\rm e}(\rho)=\sqrt{\mu\left(\frac{2}{\rho}-\frac{1}{a}\right)}=v_{1}\sqrt{\frac{2}{(e\!+\!1)}\frac{\rho_{1}}{\rho}-\frac{1-e}{1+e}}\,,

(77)

where
\rho\in[\rho_{1},\rho_{2}]
, and where
a=(\rho_{1}+\rho_{2})/2
is the semi-major axis for an ellipse with periapsis
\rho_{1}
and apoapsis
\rho_{2}
. For such an ellipse, the eccentricity is
e=(\rho_{2}-\rho_{1})/2a
.
The
vis-viva
equation yielding the velocity field of elliptical trajectories is thus fully specified by the choice of
\rho_{1}
,
\rho_{2}
, and
v_{1}
at
\rho_{1}
.

For parabolic trajectories, the specific energy is zero and we have
\epsilon=v^{2}_{\rm p}/\rho-\mu/\rho
= 0, from which the
vis-viva
orbital speed equation is

v_{\rm p}(\rho)=\sqrt{\frac{2\mu}{\rho}}=v_{1}\sqrt{\frac{\rho_{1}}{\rho}}\,,

(78)

where
\rho\in[\rho_{1},\infty)
. For a parabola,
e=1
, so
\ell=2\rho_{1}
, where the periapsis is
\rho_{1}=a
by definition and there is no apoapsis. The
vis-viva
velocity field of parabolic trajectories is thus fully specified by the choice of
\rho_{1}
and
v_{1}
at
\rho_{1}
.

For hyperbolic trajectories, the specific energy is positive,
\epsilon=v^{2}_{\rm h}/\rho-\mu/\rho=\mu/(2|a|)
, with

vis-viva
equation,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}v_{\rm h}(\rho)&=&\!\displaystyle\sqrt{\mu\left(\frac{2}{\rho}+\frac{1}{|a|}\right)}\,,\\[15.0pt]
&=&\!\displaystyle v_{1}\sqrt{\frac{2}{(e\!+\!1)}\frac{\rho_{1}}{\rho}+\frac{|1-e|}{1+e}}\,,\end{array}

(79)

where
e>1
and
\rho\in[\rho_{1},\infty)
. There is no apoapsis. Unlike the ellipse, where both
e
and
a
are defined in terms of the periapsis and the apoapsis, or the parabola for which
e=1
and
a
is the periapsis, there is no geometric constraint on the choice of
e
for a hyperbola. For a periapsis
\rho_{1}
, we have
a=\rho_{1}/(e-1)
, where
e
is a free parameter. The
vis-viva
velocity field of hyperbolic trajectories is thus fully specified by the choice of
e
,
\rho_{1}
, and
v_{1}
at
\rho_{1}
.

Along a any conic section, the axial radial component of the velocity trajectory is
V_{\uprho}=(\mu/h)e\sin\nu
. We can write this expression as

\begin{array}[]{lcl}V_{\uprho}(\rho)&=&\displaystyle\frac{\mu}{h}\sqrt{e^{2}-\left(1-\frac{\ell}{\rho}\right)^{2}}\,,\\[12.0pt]
&=&\displaystyle\frac{v_{1}}{1\!+\!e}\left[e^{2}-\left(1-(e\!+\!1)\frac{\rho_{1}}{\rho}\right)^{2}\right]^{1/2}\,,\end{array}

(80)

which follows from the identity
\sin^{2}\nu+\cos^{2}\nu=1
and the rearrangement of Eq.
76
, yielding
e\cos\nu=1-(\ell/\rho)
, where
\ell=\rho_{1}(e+1)
. It can be shown that the quantity
\mu/h=v_{1}/(1+e)
. Note that
V_{\uprho}(\rho_{1})=0
at the periapsis
\rho_{1}
, as this velocity is purely azimuthal in the orbital plane. For the ellipse, we also find
V_{\uprho}(\rho_{2})=0
at the apoapsis, which follows from
\rho_{1}/\rho_{2}=(1-e)/(e+1)
.

The azimuthal velocity component can readily be obtain from the
vis-viva
orbital speed and the radial component, which gives

V^{2}_{\upphi}(\rho)=v^{2}_{\rm x}(\rho)-V^{2}_{\uprho}(\rho)\,,

(81)

where
\rho\in[\rho_{1},\rho_{2}]
for an ellipse and
\rho\in[\rho_{1},\infty)
for a parabola or hyperbola, and where

v_{\rm x}(\rho)
is given by Eq.
77
for an ellipse, by Eq.
78
for a parabola, or by Eq.
79
for a hyperbola.
Over the spatial domain and range of eccentricity of each of the three Keplerian trajectories, it can be shown that Eq.
81
simplifies to

V_{\upphi}(\rho)=\left(\frac{\rho_{1}}{\rho}\right)v_{1}=\frac{h}{\rho}\,,

(82)

which is a statement of the conservation of angular momentum. We have

{\bf V}_{a}(\rho)=\displaystyle V_{\uprho}(\rho)\,{\hat{\hbox{\bf e}}_{\uprho}}(\rho)+V_{\upphi}(\rho)\,{\hat{\hbox{\bf e}}_{\upphi}}(\rho)\,,

(83)

where
V_{\uprho}(\rho)
is given by Eq.
80
and
V_{\upphi}(\rho)
is given by Eq.
82
.
Applying Eq.
54
, the LOS velocity is

V^{(a)}_{\hbox{\tiny LOS}}(t)=\displaystyle V_{\uprho}(t){\cal P}_{\uprho}(t)+V_{\upphi}(t){\cal P}_{\upphi}(t)\,.

(84)

Since, for all conic sections,
\mu=\rho_{1}v_{1}^{2}/(e+1)
and
h=\rho_{1}v_{1}
, we see that we define the specific energy and angular momentum by specifying the orbital eccentricity,
e
, and the velocity at periapsis,
v_{1}
. To match the disk rotation, we would set
v_{1}=V_{c}
for the
Steidel et al. 2002
disk kinematic model or
v_{1}=V_{\upphi}(\rho_{1},0)
for the
Bizyaev et al. 2017
disk kinematic model. For consistency with the accretion radius of the planar wind structure, one would adopt
\rho_{1}=\rho_{a,0}
.

By employing conic-section infalling trajectories, we are effectively assuming that the accreting material begins its “observable” journey at
R_{a}
, and, conserving energy and angular momentum, ends its journey accreting at the periapsis. In Figure

5.4.2
Planar Accretion Kinematics

The Keplerian model captures many of the most salient attributes of the accretion kinematics as predicted by hydrodynamic cosmological simulations
(
Hafen et al. 2022
;
Trapp et al. 2022
;
Stern et al. 2024
;
Kocjan et al. 2024
, in particular, see)
. As the gas spirals radially inward toward the disk, it largely conserves angular momentum until it achieves rotational support, typically
just after crossing the gaseous disk edge
where it tends to “pile up.” That is, most gas joins the disk slightly interior to the disk edge, which is well beyond the stellar disk. As the accreting gas amalgamates with the disk while transitioning through this narrow “accretion zone”, its radial velocity component diminishes rapidly. The gas enters the accretion zone at angles ranging from
0^{\circ}
–
30^{\circ}
relative to the vertical of the disk plane; gas accreting from larger angles has larger vertical velocities during the process. Entering the accretion zone, the vertical velocities tend to increase, and once in the disk, the average vertical velocity settles down to small fraction of the circular velocity. Similarly, the axial average radial velocity settles down to a non-zero finite fraction of the circular velocity.

Concluding Remarks

We have derived the basic mathematical framework for building idealized spatial-kinematic absorption models (SKAMs) comprising multiple geometric galaxy/CGM components each with their own 3D velocity fields. In this Paper I of a two paper series, we focused on geometric and kinematic relationships. We then presented a few selected models using
V_{\hbox{\tiny LOS}}
–LOS plots. In
30
, we will develop formalism for generating absorption lines from these SKAMs. This Paper I can be summarized as follows:

1.

We defined the central galaxy in terms of its NFW dark matter halo properties, which are characterized by three free parameters, the maximum circular velocity, the overdensity contained within the virial radius, and the concentration parameter. We derived the mathematical formalism describing the galaxy/CGM as a set of geometric structures and their spatial relationships, including the sky-view perspective of an arbitrarily positioned distant observer and the position along the LOS of the background quasar.

2.

We adopted four galaxy/CGM structures to represent the components of a fiducial galaxy/CGM model based on observations and theoretical considerations. The galaxy disk is represented by a cylinder and the “halo” is represented by a sphere. Hyperboloids of one sheet represents both a bi-polar wind and a flared extended planar accretion zone. The spatial domains of these geometric structures can be easily adjusted using a small set of free parameters.

3.

We derived a set of simple quadratic inequalities for each galaxy/CGM structure that identify the positions along the LOS that reside within the spatial domains of the respective geometric structure. The roots of these quadratic equations yield the position(s) on the LOS that intersect the geometric surfaces. The interpretation is that LOS positions within the spatial domain of a given geometric structure are probing the galaxy/CGM component represented by the structure.

4.

We developed the formalism for obtaining the LOS velocity as a function of position along a LOS that probes an arbitrary 3D velocity field from any orientation and impact parameter through the SKAM. A set of simple LOS projection functions have been tabulated for vertical, radial, azimuthal, axial, and polar velocity components. As a function of position along the LOS, these effectively provide the scalar dot product of the LOS vector and the respective velocity vector component.

5.

We derived expressions for 3D velocity fields for each galaxy/CGM structure that are motivated from observations and theoretical considerations. The velocity field corresponding to a given galaxy/CGM structure is simply nulled outside its spatial domain (or more simply, when the position along the LOS is outside a structure spatial domain). These velocity fields are specified by a small set of adjustable kinematic parameters. Additional physics-based realism can be incorporated in the form of rotational lag and adjustable “stalling,” “breaking,” and “enhancement” functions.

Though SKAMs lack the higher degree of realism represented in computational methods such as hydrodynamical simulations, they provide a valuable tool with which we can directly confront the distilled essence of our ideas about the nature of the CGM. In addition, analytic models provide a useful gateway between observations and simulations. SKAMs can be immediately understood on an intuitive level. Their intuitive-based predictions are founded on simple physical constructs that help us untangle the complexity of astrophysics and computational constraints bedeviling the predictions of simulations.

Examination of
V_{\hbox{\tiny LOS}}
–LOS plots shows that there can be a degeneracy in
V_{\hbox{\tiny LOS}}
along the LOS. That is, for a given LOS/galaxy orientation, spatial-kinematic models clearly reveal that similar/overlapping LOS velocities can arise from multiple unique and spatially separated CGM structures (different LOS positions probing different CGM structures can yield a similar/overlapping LOS velocity). In Figure
16
, the color bars on the right hand sides of the individual panels show the degree of these overlaps for the few simple illustrations in this work. For example, the LOS may sample the wind paraboloid over a finite LOS velocity, but it might also sample the extended accretion structure over this same LOS velocity range. This indicates that absorption lines that may arise from the different spatial-kinematic components of the CGM would be superposed in the observed spectrum
(
Marra et al. 2024
, also see)
.

How is it that we can determine from which structure (galaxy/CGM component) absorption arises? Or, how can we estimate the relative proportions of the absorption from each galaxy/CGM structure? Forward modeling of absorption systems observed to have multiple ions spanning many ionization states is one method
(
Sameer et al. 2021
;
Sameer et al. 2022
;
Sameer et al. 2024
, see)
. For example, as shown in Fig. C10 of
Sameer et al. 2024
, what appeared as a single component in
C
iii
at
50

km s
-1
actually comprises two distinct gas phases. Such detailed decomposition is enabled by using profile shapes from all the observed lines. For such cases, inferring which CGM structures are giving rise to the distinct gas phases from the modeling would require spatial information. Even if the LOS/galaxy orientation is known, there remains ambiguity as to where along the LOS the gas resides, and this is information needed for inferring outflow or infall because it breaks degeneracies. Incorporating SKAMs that produce realistic absorption profiles into the analysis can provide the requisite spatial-kinematic information.

If there are differences in the average gas phase properties (density, temperature, chemical abundances, and ionization conditions) for the different idealized structures, then the absorption from different galaxy/CGM structures would reveal those signatures.
An advantage of generating synthetic absorption profiles with SKAMs is that the optical depth from each individual galaxy/CGM structure can be synthesized as a function of LOS velocity. The magnitudes of the optical depths can be compared and the contribution to the absorption from each structure can be quantified.

This last consideration motivates us to investigate the predicted observable effects of the gas physical conditions for a given CGM spatial-kinematic structure and what those conditions would need to be in order to yield absorption lines generally consistent with observations. In
30
, we will extend our formalism beyond models that simply predict LOS velocities as a function LOS positions for idealized spatial-kinematic components of the CGM. To synthesize absorption profiles for atomic transitions of various ions, we need to include formalism for determining the optical depths of various ion transitions as a function of LOS position and velocity. Since optical depth is proportional to the product of the number density of the absorbing ion and the total absorption cross section, we must specify the densities of all ions and the line-broadening physics of the gas at all locations within each unique CGM structure. The first step will be to populate the CGM structures with gas density-temperature-ionization fields (in a manner similar to how the velocity fields were defined). The second step will be to develop the radiative transfer formalism accounting for degenerate LOS velocities.

A rudimentary SKAM GUI (with the capability to generate absorption lines) is available as a Fortran 95 code at
github.com/CGM-World
.

B
is the amplitude of the enhancement to
A
, and
f_{\hbox{\tiny E}}(\rho,z)
is the enhancement function.

The enhancement functions are axisymmetric and, because they operate only within the wind structure, at a given height
z
we have the axial domain
\rho\in[0,\rho_{w}(z)]
for
z\in[-R_{w},R_{w}]
, where

\rho_{w}(z)=(\rho_{w,0}^{2}+z^{2}T^{2}_{\Theta})^{1/2}\,,

(96)

is the axial distance to the surface of the wind at height
z
. The quantity
\rho_{w,0}
is the base radius of the wind and
T_{\Theta}=\tan\Theta_{w}
, where
\Theta_{w}
is the opening angle of the wind structure, and
R_{w}
is the “cap” radius of the wind. Enhancement functions must be designed to have peak value
f_{\hbox{\tiny E}}(0,z)=1
when evaluated on the wind axis (the wind core), yielding
Q(0,z)=A+B
, and vanish when evaluated at the wind surface, i.e.,
f_{\hbox{\tiny E}}(\rho_{w}(z),z)=0
, yielding
Q(\rho_{w}(z),z)=A
.

One potential enhancement function is a Gaussian function, which can be invoked to obtain a highly peaked core enhancement,

f_{\hbox{\tiny E}}(\rho,z)=\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{\rho/\rho_{w}(z)}{\sigma_{c}}\right]^{2}\right\}\,,

(97)

where
\sigma_{c}
is the core “width” in units of the normalized axial distance,
\rho/\rho_{w}(z)
. As illustrated in Figure
19
(a), for “narrow” cores (
\sigma_{c}\leq 0.33
), Eq.
97
will effectively vanish for
\rho/\rho_{w}(z)\geq 3\sigma_{c}
. For broader wind cores (
\sigma_{c}>0.33
), the Gaussian function does not vanish at the wind surface (which is to say that the wind surface will also be enhanced, which is a valid condition, if desired).

Whereas the Gaussian function can provide a highly peaked core enhancement, a high-power polynomial,

f_{\hbox{\tiny E}}(\rho,z)=1-[\rho/\rho_{w}(z)]^{p}\,,

(98)

where
p
is an even integer, can yield a high rapidly changing enhancement along the wind surface. If
p=2
, then
f_{\hbox{\tiny E}}(\rho,z)
is a parabola with apex of unity at
\rho=0
(the wind core), and minimum of zero at
\rho=\rho_{w}(z)
(the wind surface). If
p=4
, then
f_{\hbox{\tiny E}}(\rho,z)
is quartic function, etc. As
p
is increased the functional form of Eq.
98
behaves as shown in Figure
19
(b). A rapid change near the wind surface may be desired, for example, for an extended enhanced core with a diminished “thin” circumferential wind “wall.”

An example of a more gradually changing enhancement profile would be a truncated cosine function,

f_{\hbox{\tiny E}}(\rho,z)=\cos^{p}\!\left\{\frac{\pi}{2}\left(\frac{\rho}{\rho_{w}(z)}\right)\right\}\,,

(99)

where
p\geq 1
is an integer power.

p=1

5.2.2
as applied to the wind velocity field illustrated in Figure

(b). As illustrated in Figure

(c), for
p=1
, Eq.
98
peaks at the wind core and vanishes at the wind surface and, as
p
is increased, the function begins to take on the shape of a Gaussian.
Indeed,
a Gaussian with
\sigma_{c}=0.2
and a truncated cosine with
p=10
are highly similar. Similarly, the polynomial of degree
p=2
(parabola, Eq.
98
) is highly similar to the truncated cosine with
p=1
. Such “quasi-degeneracy” suggests that functions of different families can yield similar spatial distributions of outflow velocity within the wind structure. One should adopt functions that are computationally expedient and intuitive in their parameterization.

For galactic winds, a significant mass and momentum of cold gas can be entrained along the wall of the wind structure
(

. An additional refinement to enhancement might be to model “thick walls,” or a circumferential wind surface with a finite thickness. By applying a “horizontal dilation,” we design an enhancement function that vanishes at an axial distance
\rho<\rho_{w}(z)
for all
z
. Such an enhancement function would yield
Q(\rho,z)=A
within a finite distance from the wind surface (representing a thickness to the wind surface).

Defining
w
as the fractional thickness of the wind “walls,” we modify a given enhancement function to be null (no enhancement) over the wind circumference with thickness
\Delta\rho(z)=w\rho_{w}(z)
, where
w\in(0,1]
. Note that, with this formalism, the thickness of the wind wall would increase with height above the galactic plane since it is a fixed fraction of
\rho_{w}(z)
, which increases with
z
per Eq.
96
. Alternatively, one could develop a formalism for a constant thickness or for a thickness that decreases with height.

E. Alternative Accretion Kinematics

Figure 22:

. The orbital trajectories and velocity field for (a)
k=-0.1
, which follows for
\phi\simeq-6^{\circ}
, (b)
k=-0.3
for
\phi\simeq-17^{\circ}
, and (c)
k=-0.6
for
\phi\simeq-31^{\circ}
. As these kinematic models enforce the velocity to be strictly azimuthal with magnitude
v_{1}=V_{c}
at
\rho=\rho_{1}
, the velocity fields for different orbital eccentricities become less distinguishable as
\rho
approaches
\rho_{1}
. The main differences are in the outer regions.

Here we describe an alternative kinematic model for the accretion structure known as the logarithmic spiral,
\rho=\rho_{0}e^{k\phi}

(see
Wysocki 2015
, for details)
. This curve was first presented as the
ewige Linie

(
Dürer 1525
)
and later studied as the
spira mirabilis
by J. Bernoulli (circa 1695). It has the dual benefit of providing both a velocity field expressible in closed form that conserves angular momentum and a spiral structure compatible with density wave theory
(
Bertin & Lin 1996
;
Pringle & Dobbs 2019
, e.g.,)
. A central force scaling as
r^{-3}
results in a logarithmic spiral orbit, which is asymptotically consistent with an NFW potential for
r\gg R_{s}

(
Bassetto et al. 2022
, see)
.

The constant parameter
k=\tan(\psi)
is the tangent of the “pitch angle,” measured as the angle between the tangent to the curve and the normal to the axial radial vector at
\rho
. For galactic spiral arms, observations indicate
\psi\sim 15^{\circ}
–
20^{\circ}
, or
k=0.27
–0.36 for a range of galaxy mass and spiral morphology
(
Savchenko & Reshetnikov 2013
;
Lingard et al. 2021
, e.g.,)
.

Defining
V_{\uprho}=(d\rho/d\phi)(d\phi/dt)
and
V_{\upphi}=\rho(d\phi/dt)
, we have

V_{\uprho}=k\rho_{0}e^{k\phi}\frac{d\phi}{dt}\,,\quad V_{\upphi}=\rho_{0}e^{k\phi}\frac{d\phi}{dt}\,.

(103)

From the solution of the equation of motion including acceleration due to gravity
(
Bassetto et al. 2022
)
,

\frac{d\phi}{dt}=\left[\frac{2\mu}{\rho^{3}}\frac{[1-e^{(k^{2}+2)\phi/k}]}{k^{2}+2}\right]^{1/2}\,,

(104)

where
\mu=GM(r)
is the gravitational parameter for a total mass contained within radius
r
.
Substituting
\rho=\rho_{0}e^{k\phi}
into Eq.
104
and inserting into Eq.
103
, we write

V_{\uprho}=k\chi(\phi)\,,\quad V_{\upphi}=\chi(\phi)\,,

(105)

where

\chi(\phi)=\displaystyle e^{-(k/2)\phi}\left[\frac{2\mu}{\rho_{0}}\frac{[1-e^{(k^{2}+2)\phi/k}]}{k^{2}+2}\right]^{1/2}\,.

(106)

To convert the velocity of the trajectory into a velocity field that depends only on the axial distance from the
z
axis, we write

\phi=\ln(\rho/\rho_{0})/k
and substitute into Eq.
106
. Adopting
V_{c}^{2}=\mu/\rho_{1}
, where
\rho_{1}
is the periapsis, we have
\mu/\rho_{0}=V_{c}^{2}\rho_{1}/\rho_{0}
. We obtain,

\chi(\rho)=\displaystyle V_{c}\left[\frac{2\rho_{1}}{\rho}\frac{[1-(\rho/\rho_{0})^{(k^{2}+2)/k^{2}}]}{k^{2}+2}\right]^{1/2}\,.

(107)

For an inward spiral, we define
k<0
, in which case
\rho_{0}
is the maximum axial distance,
\rho_{1}
is the minimum axial distance, and
\rho\in(\rho_{1},\rho_{0})
. The resulting velocity field is

{\bf V}_{a}(\rho)=\displaystyle k\chi(\rho)\,{\hat{\hbox{\bf e}}_{\uprho}}(\rho)+\chi(\rho)\,{\hat{\hbox{\bf e}}_{\upphi}}(\rho)\,.

(108)

Applying Eq.
54
, the LOS velocity is

V^{(a)}_{\hbox{\tiny LOS}}(t)=\displaystyle\left[k\,{\cal P}_{\uprho}(t)+{\cal P}_{\upphi}(t)\right]\chi(t)\,.

(109)

In Figure
22
, we show three logarithmic spiral velocity fields:
k=-0.1,-0.3
, and
-0.6
. Whereas
k=-0.3
is somewhat representative of the velocity field of density wave theory for spiral arms
(
Bertin & Lin 1996
;
Savchenko & Reshetnikov 2013
;
Pringle & Dobbs 2019
;
Lingard et al. 2021
)
, the value
k=-0.1
might more closely model the manner in which accreting streams blend into “extended rings” similar to those predicted for the highest angular momentum infalling streams
(
Danovich et al. 2015
)
. For
k=-0.3
, the velocity field and gas orbital trajectories are similar to those of
(
Wang & Lilly 2023
, see their Fig. 2)
for their “MAD” model, in which they assume a velocity field of the form
V_{\upphi}(r)=V_{c}\tan^{-1}(r/R_{t})
, where
R_{t}
is a multiple of the scale length of the star formation surface density of the disk. For
k\leq-0.6
, the logarithmic spiral trajectories resemble the trajectories seen in the simulations of
Hafen et al. 2022
and
Kocjan et al. 2024
and begin to approach the ballistic infall kinematics that are more akin to the Keplerian trajectories described in Section
5.4.1
.

To fully describe a logarithmic spiral model, one needs to specify
k
,
\rho_{1}
, and
\rho_{2}
(assuming
V_{c}
for the velocity at
\rho_{1}
). Values of
k
in the range
k=-0.1
to
k=-0.6
provide suitable velocity fields that crudely emulate a viable range of accretion trajectories that resemble those seen in multiple simulations
(
Kereš et al. 2005
;
Stewart et al. 2011
;
Stewart et al. 2013
;
Hafen et al. 2022
;
Gurvich et al. 2023
;
Kocjan et al. 2024
;
Stern et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
. The natural choice for the maximum axial distance is
\rho_{0}=R_{a}
, the extent of the accretion hyperboloid structure. The minimum axial distance,
\rho_{1}
, could be the accretion radius,
\rho_{a,0}
, the axial radius of the disk
\rho_{d}
, or the radius of maximum circular velocity,
R_{c}
.
