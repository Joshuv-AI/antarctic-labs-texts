Microbiome-host communication via vaccenic acid modulates LRH-1/NR5A2 activity and ameliorates metabolic liver disease
Authors: Anna Pia Plazzo, Verena Filz, Michaela Prothiwa, Rebekka Lambrecht, M Eugenia Delgado, Jennifer R Fleming, Jerome Duschek, Elia Pilgram, Juliane Friedrich, Raphael Eisenring, Lea Kurtz, Kerstin Stemmer, Lotta K Stenman, Fabian Amaya Garcia, Miriam Unterlass, Olga Mayans, Thomas Böttcher, Thomas Brunner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Interactions between microbiota and the host can profoundly affect human health. In particular, microbiota-derived metabolites influence liver physiology and contribute to the development of metabolic diseases, yet the molecular mechanisms underlying microbiome-host communication remain incompletely understood. Here, we identify bacterial lipids as modulators of the nuclear receptor Liver Receptor Homolog-1 (LRH-1/NR5A2), a regulator of hepatic metabolism. Lipid extracts from multiple bacterial species, including the probiotic Bifidobacterium animalis subsp. lactis B420TM, activated LRH-1, leading to the identification of the long-chain fatty acid vaccenic acid (VA) as a previously unrecognized endogenous LRH-1 ligand. VA directly bound the LRH-1 ligand-binding domain and stimulated receptor-dependent transcriptional activity. In high-fat diet-induced obese and hyperglycemic mice, VA-mediated LRH-1 activation improved glucose homeostasis and attenuated metabolic liver disease. Transcriptomic analyses revealed suppression of hepatic de novo lipogenesis as a principle downstream response to LRH-1 activation. Together, these findings establish a microbiota-LRH-1 signaling axis that links bacterial lipid metabolism to host metabolic regulation, identify VA as a functional LRH-1 agonist, and provide a mechanistic rationale for targeting LRH-1 in metabolic liver disease.

Problem

Our body hosts trillions of microbes, especially in the gut, collectively known as the microbiota. These microorganisms can produce small molecules that can travel through the body and influence the health of different organs, including the liver. Communication with the host occurs in part via binding to certain proteins, called nuclear receptors. As disturbances in the equilibrium of such populations of microorganisms has been linked to several diseases, such as obesity, type 2 diabetes and fatty liver disease, understanding the mechanisms how bacteria-derived molecules can activate nuclear receptors is fundamental to develop new therapeutic opportunities.

Results

In this study, we investigated how gut bacteria communicate with the liver through the nuclear receptor LRH-1, which is a protein that plays a central role in controlling blood sugar levels, fat metabolism, and liver health, among others. We discovered that lipids isolated from certain commensal and probiotic bacteria can regulate LRH-1 activity. Most importantly, we identified vaccenic acid (VA), a natural lipid molecule mostly found in meat and dairy products, as a new activator of LRH-1.

To test the medical relevance of this finding, we studied obese mice with high blood sugar caused by a high-fat diet. Activating LRH-1 with VA in these mice significantly improved glucose control and reduced liver disease. At the molecular level, VA decreased production of new fats by the liver, a process that contributes to fatty liver disease and insulin resistance.

Impact

These findings reveal a new microbiota-liver communication pathway, where molecules produced by health-promoting bacteria help to regulate host metabolism through LRH-1 activation. Clinically, this opens the possibility of treating metabolic disorders, linked to impaired LRH-1 function, using targeted diets, probiotics, prebiotics, or VA-based therapies.

Introduction

Host-microbiota interactions are being increasingly recognized to critically regulate various physiological and pathological processes in the host. Dysbiosis, the disturbance of the equilibrium of the microbiome, has been linked to several pathological conditions, such as gastrointestinal and hepatic inflammatory disorders, metabolic and cardiovascular diseases, autoimmune reactions and allergies (Hou et al, 2022; Fan and Pedersen, 2021; Gomaa, 2020). Bacteria populating the gastrointestinal tract represent the vast majority of the human microbiome. These gut bacteria contribute significantly to intestinal health by maintaining epithelial homeostasis and supporting the integrity of the intestinal barrier, as described for the probiotic Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, Streptococcus and some Escherichia coli strains (Eckburg et al, 2005; King et al, 2019). In addition, microbial metabolites and secretory factors can enter the portal circulation and shape the health status and lipid storage profile of the liver.

Bacterial metabolites interact with host receptors, including nuclear receptors (NRs), to regulate transcriptional programs and enzymatic functions in target tissues. Disruption of these microbiota-host interactions is the underlying cause of several pathological conditions, such as inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), hepatic steatosis and type-2 diabetes (Ranhotra, 2017; Wang et al, 2021).

NRs belong to a superfamily of ligand-activated transcription factors with major roles in the regulation of a wide variety of physiological processes. NR ligands are, in general, small lipophilic natural or synthetic compounds. One of the first discovered examples of bacteria-host NR communication is represented by the Farnesoid-X-Receptor (FXR, NR1H4) and its cognate ligands bile acids (BA). Primary BA are synthesized from cholesterol by hepatocytes in a series of enzymatic steps that include the two key enzymes cholesterol 7α-hydroxylase (CYP7A1) and sterol 12α-hydroxylase (CYP8B1). These BA act as FXR ligands, which regulate their own synthesis and transport via a well-established feedback mechanism (Goodwin et al, 2000; Lu et al, 2000). Hepatic, ligand-stimulated FXR induces the expression of Small Heterodimer Partner (SHP, NR0B2), which in turn inhibits Liver Receptor Homolog-1 (LRH-1, NR5A2)-mediated expression of CYP7A1 and CYP8B1 (Lee and Moore, 2002). In addition, FXR-regulated expression of Fibroblast Growth Factor FGF15/19 further represses BA synthesis in the liver by inhibiting CYP7A1 (Inagaki et al, 2005). Primary BA are biotransformed in the intestinal tract by the resident bacteria into secondary BA with different affinities for FXR. Consequently, gut bacteria populations can differentially regulate BA synthesis and efficacy of intestinal lipid absorption (Sayin et al, 2013; Li et al, 2013). Thus, nutrients affecting microbiota composition can also indirectly regulate NR-regulated host processes. For example, theabrownin, a pigment in Pu-erh tea, modulates the gut microbiome and thereby regulates BA homeostasis via FXR/FGF15 signaling (Huang et al, 2019).

LRH-1 is a NR primarily expressed in tissues of endodermal origin, including the liver, intestine, pancreas and ovaries (Bookout et al, 2006), where it regulates embryonic development, intestinal glucocorticoid (GC) synthesis and inflammation, cell proliferation and metabolic processes (Michalek and Brunner, 2021). Dysfunction and/or overexpression of LRH-1 is associated with pathological conditions, including type-2 diabetes, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD, also referred to as metabolic-associated fatty liver disease, MAFLD), IBD and cancer (Antwi et al, 2024; Michalek and Brunner, 2021; Sun et al, 2021; Wu et al, 2024). For this reason, pharmacological targeting of this receptor with agonistic and antagonistic ligands represents a promising therapeutic strategy (Lang et al, 2021). LRH-1 is still considered an orphan nuclear receptor, as thus far no bona fide endogenous ligand has been identified. Yet, current research efforts identified novel natural and synthetic LRH-1 ligands and led to the development of synthetic LRH-1 modulators (recently reviewed in (Lang et al, 2021; Wu et al, 2024). Various of these ligands have already been tested in preclinical mouse models for their activities to modulate LRH-1-regulated physiological and pathophysiological processes. For example, administration of the LRH-1 agonist 1,2-dilauroyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (DLPC) improved serum glucose and insulin sensitivity, and mitigated hepatic lipid accumulation in mouse models of insulin resistance (Lee et al, 2011). Additionally, “phospholipid mimics”, synthetic molecules that combine the phospholipid interactions with the mouth of LRH-1 ligand-binding pocket (LBP) with the deep pocket interactions of the synthetic ligand RJW100, have been shown to reduce lipogenic gene expression in the liver of mice overexpressing human LRH-1 (Cato et al, 2022). We have previously demonstrated that in the intestine LRH-1 regulates steroidogenic enzyme expression, thereby controlling extra-adrenal GC synthesis (Mueller et al, 2006; Coste et al, 2007; Ahmed et al, 2023; Noti et al, 2010). Accordingly, synthetic LRH-1 ligands have been found to promote intestinal steroidogenesis and lower hepatic lipogenic genes such as Fasn, Scd1 and Acacb (Mays et al, 2022).

Given that the intestinal epithelium and the liver are directly exposed to bacteria and their products, we hypothesized that specific microbiota-derived metabolites can modulate LRH-1 activity, thereby influencing intestinal and hepatic physiology. To explore a potential cross-talk between intestinal bacteria and LRH-1, and evaluate the ability of bacterial metabolites to serve as ligands for LRH-1, we screened crude lipid extracts from various bacterial strains for their ability to modulate LRH-1 transcriptional activity. From Bifidobacterium animalis subsp. lactis B420TM extracts, which transactivated LRH-1, we isolated and identified the fatty acid vaccenic acid (VA, C18:1) as a bacterial LRH-1 ligand. Both the cis- and trans-isomers of VA directly bound to the LRH-1 ligand-binding domain (LBD) and stimulated LRH-1 transcriptional activity. Accordingly, VA was able to regulate LRH-1 target genes in the intestinal epithelium and the liver. The significance of VA-induced LRH-1 regulation was further validated in a mouse model of diet-induced metabolic syndrome, where VA administration improved glucose homeostasis and reduced hepatic lipogenesis. Our results establish a novel biochemical link between microbiota-derived metabolites, LRH-1 activation and regulation of metabolic processes, suggesting an interesting chemical crosstalk between the intestinal microbiome and LRH-1. They further highlight the therapeutic potential of microbiome-derived compounds in the LRH-1-regulated management of metabolic diseases.

To confirm the specificity of LRH-1 in the B420-induced transcriptional activation, we used two well-characterized LRH-1 pharmacological inhibitors, 3d2 and SR1848. 3d2 binds to the LRH-1 LBD, induces a conformational change that impairs its interaction with co-regulatory proteins (Benod et al, 2013) and reduces LRH-1 basal transcriptional activity in cell lines (Michalek et al, 2022). SR1848 promotes rapid translocation of LRH-1 from the nucleus to the cytoplasm (Corzo et al, 2015). Both molecules effectively reduced the B420-stimulated LRH-1 activity (Fig. 1B), suggesting that the stimulatory effect of B420 is dependent on LRH-1. Neither B420 extracts nor the LRH-1 inhibitors affected cell viability, as monitored by measuring cellular respiration by MTT assay (Fig. EV1F,G). To evaluate whether endogenous LRH-1 could also be activated by the bacterial extracts, we employed human Caco-2 cells, which express higher levels of endogenous LRH-1 (Sidler et al, 2011). Again, we could confirm that B420 extracts dose-dependently stimulated LRH-1 transcriptional activity, which was inhibited by 3d2 (Fig. EV1H).

In order to investigate whether the effect of B420 extracts on LRH-1 transcriptional activity was mediated via binding of potential ligands to the LBP, we made use of a mutant LRH-1, in which the mutations F342W and I416W prevent ligand interaction with the LBP (Ortlund et al, 2005; De Jesus Cortez et al, 2016; Lee et al, 2011). The Flag-tagged mutant LRH-1 F342W/I416W protein was induced in LS174T cells, resulting in comparable expression levels to those of wild-type LRH-1 after 4 h and overnight induction (Fig. 1C). Expression of mutant LRH-1 reduced basal activity and markedly impaired responsiveness to B420 extracts (Figs. 1D and EV1I), indicating that B420 extracts contain ligands that directly activate LRH-1.

Interestingly, the LRH-1-activating capacity was not restricted to B420 nor limited to probiotic strains, as extracts from various Gram-negative and Gram-positive commensal and pathogenic bacteria also promoted LRH-1 transactivation. Among the strains tested (Lactobacillus rhamnosus, Proteus mirabilis, Enterobacter cloacae, Vibrio cholerae, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Enterococcus faecalis, Pseudomonas aeruginosa), all extracts modulated LRH-1 activity (Fig. 1E). LRH-1 transcriptional activity was stimulated by all bacterial extracts, with the exception of extracts from P. aeruginosa. Pre-treatment with the LRH-1 inhibitor 3d2 reduced this response to varying degrees. Additionally, lipid extracts from different Escherichia coli strains, including commonly used laboratory strains, also transactivated LRH-1 (Fig. EV1J). Interestingly, extracts from P. aeruginosa inhibited LRH-1 transactivation, which may be partly attributable to a decrease in reporter cell viability (Fig. EV1K). No major effects on cell viability were observed for the other bacterial extracts.

In summary, our findings demonstrate that lipid ligands capable of activating LRH-1 transcriptional activity are present in different human gut bacteria, including the probiotic strain B420.

Identification of vaccenic acid as a novel LRH-1-activating ligand

While an increasing number of compounds have been described to bind to and/or activate LRH-1, at present a definite physiological ligand remains elusive. Notably, the synthetic lipid DLPC was shown to bind to the LRH-1 LBD and to modulate its activity both in cell lines and mice (Lee et al, 2011; Musille et al, 2012). A small sample from every second vial was analyzed by thin-layer chromatography (TLC) and vials with similar retention factor (Rf) were combined resulting in ten pools. To confirm the presence of cis- and trans-VA in active B420 extracts, we analyzed these fatty acids as FAMEs (fatty acid methyl esters) by GC-MS/MS. Comparison of retention times and fragmentation patterns with a reference mixture of cis- and trans-VA methyl esters confirmed the presence of both isomers in B420 extracts (Fig. 2D). We further investigated the presence of cis- and trans-VA in the other bacterial extracts that showed LRH-1 transactivation, namely P. mirabilis, E. cloacae, V. cholerae, E. faecalis, and P. aeruginosa. Cis-VA was found in all bacteria extracts tested, whereas the trans isomer was only detected in B420 (Fig. 2E).

As the active fraction 1.5.3.47 contained both cis- and trans-VA, the individual isomers were purchased and tested separately for LRH-1 transactivation and potential cytotoxicity (Fig. EV3A). The murine LRH-1 protein is considered constitutively active and less dependent on agonist binding compared to human LRH-1 (Sablin et al, 2003; Krylova et al, 2005). Nevertheless, many studies have demonstrated that LRH-1 modulation by agonistic molecules is also effective in mice (Cobo-Vuilleumier et al, 2018b; Mays et al, 2022; Lee et al, 2011). For this reason, we were interested in understanding whether murine LRH-1 activity could also be modulated by bacterial extracts and VA. In LS174T cells with inducible mLRH-1 overexpression (Fig. EV3F), transactivation of murine LRH-1 was induced by B420 extracts, and inhibited by 3d2 and SR1848 (Fig. EV3G). Both cis- and trans-VA stimulated murine LRH-1 activity, although to a lesser extent than human LRH-1 (Fig. EV3H).

In conclusion, our results identify the fatty acids cis- and trans-VA present in bacterial extracts as novel ligands for LRH-1, and demonstrate their ability to modulate LRH-1 activity in both human and murine models.

We then sought to determine whether unsaturated C18 fatty acids might exhibit similar LRH-1-stimulating activity to VA. OA contains one double bond between C9 and C10 in cis configuration, while elaidic acid (EA) is the trans-isomer of OA at the same position. To further investigate this hypothesis, we expressed and purified the LBD of human LRH-1 as a His6-tagged protein (Fig. EV4B,C) and quantified the binding affinity of the different long-chain fatty acids with microscale thermophoresis (MST). The analysis confirmed binding of cis- and trans-VA to LRH-1 LBD at dissociation constants (Kd) of 14.2 μM and 144.4 μM, respectively, whereas the other fatty acids tested did not show binding at relevant Kd (Figs. 3D–H and EV4D).

Saturated and unsaturated fatty acids are well-known regulators/activators of the peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR) transcription factors of the NR superfamily, namely PPARα (NR1C1), PPARβ/δ (NR1C2), and PPARγ (NR1C3). These NRs form heterodimers with their obligate partner RXR (retinoid X receptor), and bind to specific DNA sequences, known as PPAR response element (PPRE), thereby regulating lipid metabolism and inflammation (reviewed in (Varga et al, 2011)). In our experiments, VA failed to transactivate PPARγ/RXR heterodimers, but was able to stimulate transcriptional activity of PPARα, which was notably enhanced by cotransfection with RXR. No effect was observed on the transcriptional activity of the glucocorticoid receptor (GR, NR3C1) (Fig. EV4E–G). These results highlight the specificity of VA in modulating LRH-1 activity.

Activation of LRH-1 in the liver has been associated with improvement of glucose and insulin homeostasis, likely through transcriptional regulation of de novo lipogenesis (Lee et al, 2011; Mays et al, 2022). Specifically, several key lipogenic genes, including sterol regulatory element binding transcription factor 1 (Srebf1, also called Srebp1-c), acetyl-CoA carboxylase 2 (Acacb), fatty acid synthase (Fasn), and stearoyl-CoA desaturase-1 (Scd1), are downregulated following treatment with the LRH-1 agonists DLPC and 10CA, an optimized derivative of the synthetic LRH-1 agonist RJW100 (Flynn et al, 2018). Similarly, treatment of HepG2 cells with the LRH-1 agonist 6N reduced the expression of SCD1, the rate-limiting enzyme in de novo lipogenesis (Mays et al, 2019; Cato et al, 2022).

To assess endogenous LRH-1 transcriptional activity in vivo in response to VA, we measured the hepatic expression of Scd1, as well as of the established hepatic LRH-1 targets Cyp7a1 and Cyp8b1 in C57Bl/6 mice following a single i.p. injection of VA. Consistent with prior studies, VA administration decreased expression of Scd1, as well as Cyp7a1 and Cyp8b1 (Fig. 4C). Notably, a single VA injection also significantly lowered serum glucose, although serum insulin levels remained unchanged under these conditions (Fig. 4D). These results indicate that VA can activate endogenous LRH-1 and regulate metabolic pathways in the liver. Interestingly, hepatic Shp and intestinal Fgf15 were upregulated after VA treatment (Fig. EV5B,C), suggesting involvement of FXR signaling and the existence of a complex cross-talk between LRH-1 and the FXR-SHP-FGF15 axis.

LRH-1 plays a central role in liver lipid homeostasis by coordinating several metabolic pathways. Accordingly, disturbances in these LRH-1-regulated pathways result in the development of metabolic disorders, such as NAFLD and type 2 diabetes (Sun et al, 2021). Given that activation of LRH-1 by DLPC protected mice from hepatic steatosis (Lee et al, 2011), we examined the impact of VA administration on liver lipid accumulation in HFD-fed mice. Histological analysis of H&E- and Oil Red O (ORO)-stained liver sections revealed a mild reduction in lipid deposition following VA treatment (Fig. 5F), although no significant change in hepatic TG levels was detected (Fig. EV7F). We performed a more detailed analysis by quantifying macro- and microvesicular steatosis and hepatocyte hypertrophy separately (Fig. EV7G) and calculated the NAFLD activity score (NAS) (Liang et al, 2014). VA administration significantly improved the NAS value (Fig. 5G), predominantly by reducing the contribution of microvesicular steatosis and hepatocyte hypertrophy in comparison to vehicle-treated mice (Fig. EV7H). No effects were observed on collagen deposition and fibrosis, assessed by Sirius Red staining (Fig. 5H), or on serum cholesterol and TG levels (Fig. EV7I,J).

VA decreases the expression of lipogenic genes in the liver

To explore the molecular mechanisms underlying the improvements in glucose and lipid homeostasis induced by VA, we performed RNA-sequencing analyses on the liver samples from VA- or vehicle-treated HFD mice. Gene ontology analysis of significantly differentially expressed genes revealed that VA treatment modulates pathways related to lipid biosynthesis and fatty acid metabolism (Fig. 6A), alongside altered expression of LRH-1 target genes (Fig EV8 and Table EV1). Notably, VA treatment suppressed expression of key de novo lipogenesis genes, including Acacb, Fasn, and Scd1 (Fig. 6B,C). These findings align with previous studies showing downregulation of lipogenic genes following LRH-1 activation with DLPC or with the synthetic agonists 10CA and 6N (Lee et al, 2011; Mays et al, 2022; Cato et al, 2022). Additionally, the expression of Mid1ip1, a gene involved in the positive regulation of fatty acid biosynthesis via the nuclear receptor LXR (Izumi et al, 2021; Inoue et al, 2011), and Cd36, which encodes a fatty acid transporter, was also downregulated in VA-treated mice. Interestingly, VA treatment also decreased expression of the LRH-1 targets Cyp7a1 and Cyp8b1. RT-qPCR analysis of Cyp8b1 and Scd1 confirmed their downregulation upon VA treatment (Fig. 6D). Notably, while the downregulation of Scd1 was restricted to the context of the HFD, a reduction in Cyp8b1 expression was also detected in SD-fed mice. Discussion

It is increasingly appreciated that the gut microbiota plays a central role in the modulation of host physiological processes, with different bacteria having health-promoting effects. At least in part these effects may be mediated by soluble factors activating NR-regulated processes in the host. One example is provided by Bifidobacteria, Gram-positive bacteria commonly found in the human oral cavity, gastrointestinal tract and dairy products (Hidalgo-Cantabrana et al, 2017). Among these, the strain B420 of Bifidobacterium animalis subsp. lactis has been shown to provide various health benefits (reviewed in (Uusitupa et al, 2020)), including enhancement of mucosal integrity (Putaala et al, 2008; Mokkala et al, 2016; Zhang et al, 2020), improvement of glycemic control (Stenman et al, 2014), and increased host resistance to pathogens (Amar et al, 2011) in different preclinical studies. Furthermore, B420 administration in mice mitigated liver damage in the context of autoimmune hepatitis (Zhang et al, 2020). A clinical trial showed that B420 supplementation reduced body fat mass, waist circumference, energy intake and body weight compared to placebo in overweight or obese individuals (Stenman et al, 2016). These effects were later associated with changes in the microbiota composition, fecal metabolites and plasma bile acid levels (Hibberd et al, 2019). Interestingly, B420 did not colonize the host gut microbiome after ten consecutive days of administration (Lopez-Pier et al, 2021), which may suggest that the beneficial effect could rely on transiently produced metabolites. Indeed, many bacteria-derived metabolites, including short-chain fatty acids, BA and vitamins, are known to promote host health, in part by directly modulating NR activity.

In our study, we provide evidence that the NR LRH-1 can also be regulated by bacterial metabolites, and we describe VA for the first time as an LRH-1 ligand. VA was able to stimulate LRH-1 activity, and its effect in vivo on the regulation of metabolism correlated well with the beneficial effects previously described for known LRH-1 ligands (Lee et al, 2011; Cato et al, 2022). Thus, this study identifies VA as an LRH-1 ligand and health-promoting component in B420 probiotic bacteria.

Targeting LRH-1 with agonistic and antagonistic ligands is an attractive therapeutic approach. Indeed, the small molecule LRH-1 agonist BL001 could prevent autoimmune diabetes development in mouse models of type 1 diabetes, and promote human beta cell survival and function (Cobo-Vuilleumier et al, 2018a, 2018b). Also, the LRH-1 agonist DLPC improved glucose homeostasis, and reduced hepatic lipid accumulation in mouse models of insulin resistance (Lee et al, 2011). The discovery that DLPC interacts with amino acid residues located at the mouth of the LRH-1 LBP (Musille et al, 2012) encouraged the development of PL-based ligands that combine these interactions at the mouth of the LBP with those deep within the pocket, characteristic of RJW100-based ligands. Indeed, the hybrid molecules 6 N, with a sulfamide group at position R1 (Mays et al, 2019), and 10CA, where a carboxylic acid tail was added at position R4 (Flynn et al, 2018), act as potent LRH-1 ligands and induce expression of LRH-1-regulated anti-inflammatory genes, thereby promoting amelioration of colitis (Flynn et al, 2018; Mays et al, 2022).

Ligands that inhibit LRH-1 activity also have promising therapeutic applications, for example, in cancer, where LRH-1 acts as an oncogene. We successfully employed the LRH-1 inhibitor 3d2 to inhibit proliferation and sensitize T-ALL to GC treatment (Michalek et al, 2022). Interestingly, intestinal GC synthesis, known to be regulated by LRH-1 (Mueller et al, 2006; Coste et al, 2007), as well as blood glucose and insulin levels have already been associated with alterations of the intestinal microbiome in a different context (Mukherji et al, 2013).

LRH-1 has been shown to interact with and/or to be modulated by a variety of structurally different compounds. Examples include the herbicide atrazine (Suzawa and Ingraham, 2008), unconjugated bilirubin (Chapagain et al, 2024) or ivermectin-related compounds, like abamectin and doramectin (Malabanan et al, 2023). Thus, a scenario with no strict binding rules for LRH-1 ligands emerges, where likely multiple interaction possibilities exist within and in proximity of the LBP of LRH-1, that are able to facilitate binding of co-regulators. With VA we identified a novel bacteria-derived molecule that possibly engages key interactions within the LRH-1 LBP, with its carboxylic acid group and/or the acyl chain, similar as described for PL ligands. Interestingly, the position of the double bond appears to be an important factor determining binding to and transactivation of LRH-1. In our study, fatty acids of equal length (C18), but different positions of the double bond than VA, had lower binding activities and failed to activate LRH-1. However, the possibility that these compounds are metabolized or unstable in vitro and in vivo cannot be excluded. Also, whether VA effects in vivo are a consequence solely of direct LRH-1 activation or could involve induced biosynthesis of alternative LRH-1 ligands remains to be investigated. Interestingly, although a tenfold higher Kd was estimated for trans-VA compared to cis-VA, both displayed similar LRH-1-activating capacity in cell-based functional studies. In the cell, multiple determinants of the final functional output, including signal amplification via interaction with cofactors or partners, metabolic conversion to effectors shared with other NRs, potential nonlinear or saturated functional response or improved bioavailability may account for these differences.

VA is also detected in human tissue and plasma, and seems to accumulate primarily due to the consumption of meat and dairy products. Notably, physiological concentrations of trans-VA in human blood are reported to be in the µM range, as documented in the Human Metabolome Database (https://hmdb.ca/). Bacteria are also a source of VA, as we have found VA in different bacterial species. Thus, likely cows incorporate bacteria-derived VA in their milk and meat, and upon consumption VA ends up in human tissues.

Interestingly, VA-induced health benefits correlate with those already shown for the LRH-1 ligands DLPC and 10CA (Lee et al, 2011; Mays et al, 2022). Likewise, dietary supplementation of VA in animal models was shown to improve hepatic steatosis, and decrease serum glucose and insulin (Jacome-Sosa et al, 2014). The authors suggested that VA can improve whole-body insulin resistance, although another study reported that long-term feeding of beef fat enriched in trans-VA did not improve glucose homeostasis (Xu et al, 2024).

Remarkably, a human study investigating the association of fatty acids in the de novo lipogenesis pathway with metabolic risk factors and development of type 2 diabetes revealed that VA was associated with lower diabetic risk (Wenjie et al, 2015). Indeed, effects of VA on expression of Scd1, coding for the rate-limiting enzyme in this pathway, have already been reported (Minville-Walz et al, 2012). We also found downregulation of Scd1, together with Acacb and Fasn, as one of the major gene expression changes induced by VA treatment, that correlate well with the metabolic benefits observed in our mouse model of metabolic disorders. These data are also in agreement with the downregulation of lipogenic genes following LRH-1 activation with DLPC or with 10CA and 6 N (Mays et al, 2022; Cato et al, 2022; Lee et al, 2011). In addition, VA-induced alterations of expression of the LRH-1 targets Fads2 and Elovl5 correlate with altered expression of these genes in LRH-1-deficient hepatocytes (Miranda et al, 2018).

In conclusion, we show here that LRH-1 activity can be modulated by the bacteria-derived, exogenous metabolite VA. This not only establishes VA as a novel LRH-1 ligand, but also opens the perspective that LRH-1, similarly to other NRs (Keely, 2017), mediates communication between the gut bacteria and the host, allowing microbiota-derived dynamic regulation of host physiological and metabolic processes, in homeostasis and disease. In this context, modulation of the host microbiota composition may represent a new therapeutic approach in the treatment of LRH-1-regulated disorders.

Animal studies

All animal experiments were conducted according to the animal experimentation regulations of Germany and were approved by the Ethics Review Committee of the Regional Council Freiburg (reference number: 35-9185.81/G-17/91). Mice were bred and housed under specific pathogen-free conditions in individually ventilated cages at the animal facility of the University of Konstanz and were acclimated to a 12 h light/dark cycle with free access to food and water. Male C57BL/6 mice (originally obtained from Jackson Laboratory) aged 8–12 weeks were randomly assigned to groups fed either a standard diet (SD, SAFE A03, standard diet) or a high-fat diet with 45% calories from fat (HFD, SAFE 245HF, D12451) for 15 weeks. A mixture of cis- and trans-VA (1:1) dissolved in 40% PEG-400, 2% Tween-80 in PBS or the vehicle was administered daily to mice at 50 mg/kg by intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection (volume = 150 μl/mouse) for 2 weeks, while continuing the previously assigned diet. Blood glucose values were assessed right before the first i.p. injection (day 1) and after 7 and 13 days of treatment with a glucometer (GlucoCheck) by puncturing the tail vein. Mice were sacrificed 4 h after the last treatment (day 14) by CO2 inhalation. Blood and tissues (liver, small intestine) were immediately harvested. Part of the tissues were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for RNA isolation, cholesterol and triglyceride (TG) analysis or formalin-fixed for paraffin sections. In parallel, tissue was embedded in Tissue-Tek O.C.T. Compound (Sakura) to prepare cryosections. Mice exhibiting a body weight gain of less than 45%, or greater than 75%, relative to their initial value were excluded from subsequent analyses.

Intestinal epithelial cell-specific LRH-1 knockout mice (LRH-1 VilCre) and the isolation of intestinal crypts have been described previously (Noti et al, 2010; Coste et al, 2007; Ahmed et al, 2023).

Serum analysis

After collection, blood was allowed to clot at RT for 30 min and was then centrifuged for 15 min at 13,000×g. Serum was collected and glucose, cholesterol, TG and ALT/GTP values were immediately analyzed with a Reflotron instrument (Roche). An aliquot was stored at −20 °C for determination of insulin content with the Ultra Sensitive Mouse Insulin ELISA Kit (CrystalChem) following the manufacturer´s instructions.

Histological analysis of liver sections

Tissue biopsies were fixed in formalin and embedded in paraffin. Paraffin sections of 4 µm were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and evaluated blindly by light microscopy. Macrovesicular and microvesicular steatosis as well as hepatocellular hypertrophy were scored separately based on the percentage of the total area affected (0: area affected <5%, 1: 5-33%, 2: 34-66%, 3: >66%). Three images/mouse were inspected and the NAFLD Activity Score (NAS) was determined as the sum of the averaged scores, ranging from 0 to 9 (Liang et al, 2014).

Analysis of liver fibrosis

Connective tissue and collagen deposition in the hepatic tissue was visualized by Picro Sirius Red staining. De-waxed and rehydrated paraffin liver tissue sections were stained using 0.1% (w/v) Direct Red 80 (Sigma-Aldrich) dissolved in an aqueous solution of saturated picric acid for 1 h and then washed with 0.5% acetic acid and water. Stained slides were dehydrated in three changes of 100% ethanol (3 min for each step), mounted and examined by light microscopy with a Zeiss Observer Z.1 microscope. Quantification of fibrosis was determined by measuring the percentage of red-stained area in three non-overlapping images per mouse based on thresholds using ImageJ software (Schindelin et al, 2012).

Oil Red O staining

Lipid deposition in hepatic tissue was determined by Oil Red O staining (Mehlem et al, 2013). Frozen liver samples were cut with a Hydrax C500 cryostat (Zeiss) at −14 °C with a thickness of 10 μm, fixed in 10% formalin for 15 min and washed twice with water and once with 60% isopropanol for 5 min. Sections were stained with filtered Oil Red O solution (15 mL of 0.3% Oil Red O (Sigma) in pure isopropanol mixed with 10 mL H2O) for 5 min, and washed with water until clear. Hematoxylin counterstaining was performed for 1 min, then slides were washed, mounted and sealed. Images were taken with a Zeiss Observer Z.1 microscope.

Liver triglyceride measurement

Lipid extraction from snap-frozen livers was adapted from Storlien et al and Frayn and Maycock (Frayn and Maycock, 1980; Storlien et al, 1991). Samples (50 mg) were mixed with 1 mL of chloroform/methanol (2:1, vol/vol) and homogenized using a TissueLyser (Qiagen) for 1 min. The mixture was then incubated on a rotator for 6–8 h. After incubation, 0.3 mL of double-distilled water was added, followed by vortexing and centrifugation at RT for 20 min at 2000 rpm. The lower organic phase was transferred to a new 2 mL tube and chloroform was evaporated overnight. A second extraction was performed by adding 0.5 mL of chloroform/methanol (2:1, vol/vol) to the remaining phases, followed by rotation and centrifugation. The samples from both the first and second extractions were combined and left overnight for complete evaporation. Samples were then vortexed in 200 μL of chloroform. Three microliters of the sample were pipetted in duplicate into a 96-well plate. Triglycerides (TG) were quantified using the Triglyceride Quantification Kit (MAK266, Sigma-Aldrich), following the manufacturer’s protocols.

RNA sequencing

RNA-Seq experiments were conducted at QIAGEN Genomic Services. The library preparation was done using TruSeq® Stranded mRNA Sample preparation kit (Illumina Inc). The starting material (500 ng) of total RNA was mRNA enriched using the oligodT bead system. The isolated mRNA was subsequently fragmented using enzymatic fragmentation. Then first strand synthesis and second strand synthesis were performed, and the double stranded cDNA was purified (AMPure XP, Beckman Coulter). The cDNA was end-repaired, 3’-adenylated, Illumina sequencing adaptors were ligated to the fragments ends and the library was purified (AMPure XP). The mRNA stranded libraries were pre-amplified by PCR and purified (AMPure XP). The libraries size distribution was validated and quality inspected on a Bioanalyzer 2100 or BioAnalyzer 4200 tapeStation (Agilent Technologies). High quality libraries were pooled in equimolar concentrations based on the Bioanalyzer Smear Analysis tool (Agilent Technologies). The library pool(s) were quantified using qPCR and the optimal concentration of the library pool were used to generate the clusters on the surface of a flowcell before sequencing on a NextSeq500 instrument (75 cycles) according to the manufacturer's instructions (Illumina Inc.). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were calculated with an adjusted P value < 0.05 using the R package DESeq2 (version 1.40.2), and GO analysis was performed with clusterProfiler (version 4.8.3). Visualization was performed using the R package “EnhancedVolcano” (version 1.18.0) and pheatmap (version 1.0.12).

Lipid extraction from bacteria, chromatographic separation and identification of lipids

Lyophilized Bifidobacterium animalis subsp. lactis 420 (B420TM) was provided by Danisco Deutschland GmbH (Niebull, Germany). The other bacterial strains were grown overnight in 30 mL Luria-Bertani (LB) medium, except for L. rhamnosus, which was grown in De Man, Rogosa and Sharpe (MRS) medium and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C and 180 rpm. Bacteria were then pelleted for 5 min at 3000 rpm and the pellet was washed twice with PBS before proceeding to lipid extraction.

For lipid extractions, 10–100 g of fresh or lyophilized bacteria were rehydrated with 3 volumes of H2O. After adding 3.75 volumes of chloroform/methanol (1:2) the mixture was stirred and incubated overnight. First 3 volumes of chloroform and then 3 volumes of 40 mM acetic acid were added, each step followed by 1 min vigorous shaking. The solution was filtered and transferred to a separatory funnel to allow separation of the organic and inorganic phases. The organic phase was collected and evaporated and lipid extracts were stored in glass flasks at −20 °C.

Phospholipid determination

Phospholipid (PL) estimation was performed with a colorimetric method based on formation of complexes between PLs and ammonium ferrothiocyanate (Charles et al, 1980). Bacterial extracts and PL standards were dissolved in 2 mL chloroform; 1 mL ammonium ferrothiocyanate (prepared by dissolving 27 g of ferric chloride (FeCl3 · 6 H2O) and 30 g of ammonium thiocyanate (NH4SCN) in 1000 mL H2O) was added and the mixture vortexed for 1 min. To separate the two phases, samples were centrifuged at 700×g for 10 min at RT. The lower phase was transferred to a quartz cuvette and absorbance at 488 nm was measured.

Lipid extraction from bacteria, chromatographic separation and identification of lipids

Assay-guided chromatographic separation of crude lipid extracts from B. lactis B420TM was achieved in four steps. First, the crude extract was purified by silica flash column chromatography (Teledyne Rf combiflash, 40 g silica column) in chloroform by using a 0– 100% gradient of chloroform/methanol (7:3) in 50 min and a flow rate of 40 mL/min. Individual vials of 20 mL were collected and screened by thin layer chromatography (TLC) using phosphomolybdic acid stain for detection. Vials with similar content (determined by Rf) were pooled together, evaporated and the residue was solubilized in ethanol and tested for LRH-1 activity by luciferase-based reporter assays. Pool 1 was further fractionated by silica flash column chromatography (Teledyne Rf combiflash, 12 g silica column) using a 0– 100% hexane and ethyl acetate gradient in 50 min and a flow rate of 30 mL/min. Individual vials of 20 mL were collected and analyzed by TLC. Vials with similar content were pooled, evaporated, and tested again for LRH-1 activity. Pool 1.5, was then further purified by HPLC (Shimadzu LC20-AT) with a preparative Reprosil 100 silica column (5 μM, 250 × 10 mm) using 0–100% ethyl acetate in hexane over 90 min and a flow rate of 4 mL/min. Individual vials were collected and analyzed by TLC. Again, vials with similar content (determined by Rf) were combined, evaporated and tested for LRH-1 activity. Pool 1.5.3 was further purified by HPLC (Shimadzu LC20-AT) with a preparative Reprosil 100 silica column (5 μM, 250 × 10 mm) and dichloromethane (A)/methanol (B) as mobile phase (0–2.5% B in 45 min) in a flow rate of 4 mL/min. Fraction 1.5.3.47, eluting at 1% B after 32.1 min, was analyzed by 1D NMR (1H, 13 C), 2D NMR (COSY, HMBC, HSQC) and GC-MS (Appendix Fig S1–S7).

To detect DLPC in bacteria extracts, a MicrOTOF II ESI mass spectrometer was used with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source operating in positive ion mode. Parameters were set as follows: capillary voltage: 4500 V; nebulizer pressure: 12 bar; drying temperature: 180 °C; drying gas flow: 10 L/min; end plate offset: 80 V.

Lipid analysis by GC-MS/MS

Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) was performed to assess the lipid profile of B. lactis B420. For this aim, lipids were extracted according to a modified Bligh and Dyer procedure (Jensen, 2008). Six hundred mg of lyophilized or fresh bacteria were dissolved in 1 mL 1 M HCl and heated to 80 °C for 1 h. Then, 1.5 mL methanol and 1 mL chloroform were added and the mixture was vortexed for 1 min. Thereafter, 1 mL water and 2 mL chloroform were added, and the mixture was vortexed again for 1 min. The mixture was centrifuged for 10 min at 4500 rpm and the chloroform phase was transferred into a glass vial and evaporated. For fatty acid methylation, 1 mg of extract was dissolved in 50 μL of hexane and 25 μL of trimethylsulfonium hydroxide (TMSH) solution (0.25 M in methanol) were added, and the solution was heated to 100 °C for 10 min. The resulting residue was dissolved in 100 μL hexane and subjected to GC-MS/MS measurement. Gas chromatography was performed on a Trace GC ultra (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) using an OPTIMA 5 MS, 0.25 μm, 30 m, 25 mm ID column (Macherey-Nagel). Nitrogen was used as carrier gas. Throughout the measurements a constant gas linear velocity of 20 mL/min was maintained. The injection volume was 0.1 μL with a split ratio of 12:1. The initial oven temperature was set to 50 °C, immediately heated up to 80 °C at 20 °C/min, then ramped up to 280 °C with 6 °C/min and held for 2 min. MS/MS analysis was performed with a ISQ Single Quadruple GC-MS System (Thermo Fisher Scientific) mass spectrometer. The emission current was set to 50 μA and an ionization energy of 70 eV was applied. The ion source temperature was set to 200 °C. For ion fragmentation, methane was used as the collision-induced (CI) gas. Data was acquired in a mass range of m/z 41–600, and MS/MS acquisition of all compounds was performed in positive mode, fixed collision energy of 30 eV and dwell time of 0.2 s. Qualitative analysis was performed using Thermo Xcalibur 3.1.66.10 software and the NIST Database.

Protein purification

The ligand binding domain (LBD, residues 299-541) of human LRH-1 (Uniprot O00482) was cloned into the IPTG-inducible pETM-11 vector (EMBL collection) that incorporates a His6-tag followed by a tobacco etch virus (TEV) protease cleavage site N-terminally to the inserted gene. This expression plasmid was transformed into E. coli RosettaTM 2 (DE3) (Novagen) and bacterial cultures were grown at 37 °C in LB medium to an OD600 of 0.6. Protein expression was induced using 0.5 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) and cultures were grown further at 18 °C for 16 h. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 5000×g at 4 °C for 20 min and the pellets were resuspended in 30 mL of lysis buffer (300 mM NaCl, 20 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.4, 1 mM CHAPS, 10% Glycerol, 5 mM DTT) freshly supplemented with EDTA-free complete protease inhibitor (Roche). Bacteria were lysed via sonication on ice. Cell lysates were centrifuged (40,000×g for 90 min at 4 °C) and the supernatant was applied to a HisTrap HP column (Cytiva) pre-equilibrated in buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.4, 200 mM NaCl, 1 mM CHAPS, 10% Glycerol, 5 mM DTT). The protein was eluted with an imidazole gradient.

Fractions containing LRH-1 LBD were pooled, treated with TEV-protease to cleave off the His6-tag and dialyzed overnight at 4 °C against 500 mL of lysis buffer. The sample was then subjected to subtractive HisTrap chromatography. Fractions containing LRH-1 LBD were concentrated to 1.5 mL by centrifugal ultrafiltration using a 10 kDA Amicon Ultra tube (Millipore). The protein was further purified using a HiLoad 16/60 Superdex® 200 PG column (GE Healthcare) pre-equilibrated in 20 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM CHAPS, 10% Glycerol, 10 mM DTT. The protein was flash frozen and stored at −80 °C until use.

Microscale thermophoresis

To determine binding affinities, microscale thermophoresis (MST) was performed using the Monolith NT.115 instrument (NanoTemper Technologies). The purified LRH-1 LBD was labeled using the Monolith NT Protein Labeling Kit GREEN-NHS following the manufacturer´s procedure. Before labeling, protein buffer was exchanged to 20 mM HEPES, pH 7.4 by gel filtration chromatography with a PD SpinTrap G-25 column (Cytiva). Unbound dye was removed by overnight dialysis with a 3.5 KDa Slide-A-Lyzer MINI Dialysis Device (ThermoFisher Scientific). The labeled LRH-1 LBD concentration was adjusted to 60 nM in assay buffer (150 mM NaCl, 10 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.4, 5% glycerol, 0.1% N-octyl β-D-glucopyranoside, 0.05% Tween-20). Lipid stocks in ethanol were dissolved in assay buffer, vortexed and sonicated. A series of sixteen 1:1 dilutions of 20 μL was prepared for each lipid ligand. Each dilution was mixed with 20 μL of labeled LRH-1 LBD to a final concentration of protein of 30 nM and final lipid concentration ranging from 1 mM to 30.5 nM. Samples were incubated for 30 min at RT in the dark and loaded into Monolith NT.115 capillaries (NanoTemper Technologies). MST was measured using a Monolith NT.115 instrument at 22 °C. Instrument parameters were set to 60% LED power and 40% MST power. MST traces were analyzed with the MO. Affinity Analysis software version 2.2.4 (NanoTemper Technologies) selecting the Kd binding model. The cold region was defined as the 1 s region before the T-Jump while the hot region was defined as 0.5–1.5 s after the T-Jump to minimize possible temperature-dependent artifacts (López-Méndez et al, 2021).

Western blotting

For Western Blot analysis, cells seeded in 6-well plates were lysed in 100 μL RIPA Buffer (50 mM Tris pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% SDS, 1% NP-40, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, freshly added Complete™ Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Roche)) on ice for 15 min. Lysates were centrifuged for 20 min at 14,000×g and 4 °C, and total protein concentration of supernatants was determined with the Pierce™ BCA Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher). Samples were boiled in Laemmli buffer for 5 min at 95 °C and equal protein amounts (30–50 µg/sample) were separated on a 12% SDS-polyacrylamide gel. Then, proteins were blotted onto a PVDF membrane (Roche) in Blotting Buffer (25 mM Tris, 192 mM glycine, 0.1% SDS, 21.5% methanol). Non-specific binding was blocked in 5% non-fat dry milk/TBS-T (137 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl, 15 mM Tris, 0.1% Tween-20) for 1 h and afterwards primary antibodies diluted in 5% BSA/TBS-T were incubated overnight at 4 °C. After washing three times for 5 min with TBS-T, incubation with peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies in 5% BSA/TBS-T was performed for 2 h at RT followed by three additional washing steps. An ECL solution (250 mM Luminol, 90 mM p-coumaric acid, 10 mM Tris) with 1% hydrogen peroxide was applied on the membranes and proteins were detected with an Image Quant LAS4000 (GE Healthcare).

Cell culture and treatments

The human cell lines Caco-2 (ATCC HTB-37) and LS174T (ATCC CL-188) were obtained from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and cultured in Iscove’s Modified Dulbecco’s Medium supplemented with 10% FCS, 4 mM L-glutamine and 10 μg/mL gentamicin (all from Sigma-Aldrich, Steinheim, Germany). HEK293T were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 5% FBS, 4 mM L-glutamine and 10 μg/mL gentamicin. Cell lines were regularly tested for mycoplasma contamination using the Venor®GeM Classic kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Minerva Biolabs, Berlin, Germany).

Site-directed mutagenesis

The LRH-1 double mutant F342W/I416W, which displays reduced ligand binding activity, was generated with the QuikChange II XL Site-Directed Mutagenesis Kit (Agilent Technologies) in the pF-5xUAS-MCS-SV40 expression plasmid containing the human Flag-tagged LRH-1 in two steps, following the manufacturer´s instructions. Primers used are indicated in Table 1. Introduced mutations were confirmed by sequencing.

Generation of 4-hydroxytamoxifen-inducible cell lines

GEV16-mediated inducible overexpression of LRH-1 was achieved as described previously (Vince et al, 2007; Lambrecht et al, 2023). LS174T cells were transduced with HEK293T-derived lentiviruses containing the GEV16 plasmid (pHCA/GAL4(1-93).ERα.VP16) and a stable cell line was generated by selection with 1 mg/mL hygromycin B. The second step included transduction of GEV16-expressing LS174T cells with lentiviruses containing the pF-5xUAS-MCS-SV40-Puro-based expression plasmid with either human Flag-tagged LRH-1, the ligand binding mutant human Flag-tagged LRH-1 F342W/I416W or murine LRH-1. GEV16-LS174T cells transduced with EGFP-expressing lentiviruses were used as control. Stable cell lines were generated by selection with 8 µg/mL puromycin. Overexpression of LRH-1 or EGFP was achieved by treatment of cells with 200 nM 4-hydroxytamoxifen (4-HT) for 4 h, unless stated differently.

MTT cell viability assay

Cell viability after indicated treatments was estimated with the 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyl-tetrazolium bromide (MTT, Sigma) viability assay. After removing the cell culture medium, cells in 96-well plates were incubated in 10% MTT/medium solution for 1 h. Generated violet formazan crystals in living cells were dissolved in DMSO and absorbance was measured at 562 nm with the Infinite® 200 PRO plate reader (TECAN). Values were normalized to those of vehicle-treated cells.

Statistical analysis
