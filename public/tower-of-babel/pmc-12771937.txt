Knowledge of fertile period and associated factors among adolescent girls and young women in Tanzania: a nationwide survey
Authors: Elihuruma Eliufoo Stephano, Victoria Godfrey Majengo, Mtoro J Mtoro
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundKnowledge of fertile period among adolescent girls and young women (AGYW; 15–24 years) is crucial for understanding and managing reproductive health, yet it remains under explored. Many AGYW possess limited knowledge about their reproductive systems and the menstrual cycle, which can lead to misconceptions regarding fertile period and contraceptive use. In regions like Tanzania, where early and unintended pregnancies are prevalent, enhancing fertile period awareness through tailored educational initiatives becomes increasingly vital. This study aimed to assess the knowledge of the fertile period and associated factors among AGYW in Tanzania for evidence-based interventions.MethodsAn analytical cross–sectional study was conducted using secondary data from the 2022 Tanzania demographic and health survey. Given the survey’s complex design, weighted binary logistic regression model was used to determine factors associated with knowledge of the fertile period. Adjusted odds ratio (AOR) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI) were used to estimate the strength of the association. A p–value of < 0.05 was declared statistically significant.ResultsThe overall prevalence of knowledge about the fertile period was 19.4% (95% CI: 17.9–20.9). Several factors were associated with a higher likelihood of having knowledge about the fertile period; being aged 20–24 years (AOR = 1.43, 95% CI: 1.16–1.76), having secondary education (AOR = 3.15, 95% CI: 2.23–4.45), exposure to media (AOR = 1.28, 95% CI: 1.02–1.59), visiting a health facility in the past 12 months (AOR = 1.40, 95% CI: 1.29–1.66), and residing in the central (AOR = 2.55, 95% CI: 1.67–3.89) or southern zones of mainland Tanzania (AOR = 1.67, 95% CI: 1.11–2.48).ConclusionThis study revealed the factors impacting knowledge of fertile period among AGYW, highlighting the roles of educational attainment, media exposure, healthcare access, and geographical location. Increased education and health service access correlate with better knowledge. To address these challenges, tailored interventions that account for above would be essential. ​Ultimately, enhancing fertile period awareness necessitates engaging educational programs across various platforms, empowering AGYW to make informed reproductive health choices. Further research needs to be conducted to monitor the knowledge on fertile periods.Keywords: Adolescent girls, Young women, Knowledge of fertile period, Fertile period, Tanzania

Background

Understanding fertile period among adolescent girls and young women (AGYW; 15–24 years) is considered natural family planning, crucial in reproductive health [1, 2]. Knowledge of fertile period refers to the understanding of the specific days in woman’s menstrual cycle when she is most likely to conceive if she has unprotected sex [1, 2]. This knowledge empowers AGYW over their bodies and health while preventing them from unforeseen reproductive health challenges [2]. The global context of AGYW fertility is characterized by increased rates of early pregnancies, which remain a significant public health concern [3]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), an estimated 21 million girls aged 15–19 in developing regions become pregnant each year, with around 12 million giving birth [4]. This issue is exacerbated by the fact that many AGYW pregnancies are unintended, approximately 13% of AGYW give birth before age 18, often resulting from a lack of knowledge about reproductive health and access to effective contraception [3]. Understanding the knowledge of fertile period among AGYW is crucial for reproductive health and effective family planning.​ However, most of AGYW often lack good knowledge in understanding their fertile period during the menstruation period, which predisposes many into unplanned pregnancies [2].

Studies across various countries indicate a significant lack of awareness regarding the ovulatory and fertile period among AGYW. In the United States, approximately 51% lack knowledge of the fertile period [5, 6], with similar findings among black women where nearly 91% could not correctly identify their fertile time [6, 7]. In Turkey, only 40% of young people were well-informed, while 46% of a specific adult population in Pakistan recognized the fertile period of the female cycle [2, 8].

In sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), the situation is even more pronounced, as the region bears the highest rate of adolescent pregnancies worldwide. Reports indicate that the adolescent birth rate (ABR) is approximately 100 births per 1,000 women in the 15–19 age group across sub-Saharan Africa, which is nearly double the global average [4]. The pooled prevalence of knowledge in the fertile period was 8.3% which indicate low knowledge with highest birth rate [9]. Kenya reported that 8% of women are aware of their fertile period [10], while in Ghana, it was reported that there were 42.3% among females [11]. Another study conducted in Kenya showed most of AGYW are not aware of their fertile period and the whole menstruation circle. The study showed only 26% of AGYW understand their fertile window [1, 2].

Contributing factors to this trend include cultural practices that favor early marriages, high rates of gender inequality, inadequate access to reproductive health services, and a lack of comprehensive sexuality education [12]. Notably, the region faces structural barriers that prevent AGYW from obtaining essential information about their reproductive health, further complicating efforts to improve knowledge of fertile period and related health services [1, 9, 12]. Another study conducted in SSA, which analyzed data from 29 countries, showed factors associated with low knowledge of the fertile period were age, sex, residence, economic status, educational status, living in rural areas, and a low level of education [9].

​In Tanzania, the adolescent fertility rate stands out as one of the highest in the region, with recent estimates suggesting that 114 out of every 1,000 girls aged 15 to 19 give birth​ [13]. The adolescent pregnancy rate in the country has shown fluctuating trends, first decreasing from 26% in 2004 to 22.8% in 2010, then rising again to 27% in 2016 [14, 15]. Maternal mortality is still high at 238 women die per 100,000 live births due to several pregnant-related causes including unsafe abortion [13]. With the increasing rate of adolescent pregnancies and reported high maternal mortality, there is a need for understanding the knowledge of fertile period. Most of AGYW are unaware of their fertile window, which leads to unplanned pregnancies, whereby most of them end in unsafe abortion [2, 16]. Factors contributing to this high rate of adolescent pregnancies include low levels of reproductive health knowledge, inadequate education, and prevailing socio-cultural norms that discourage open discussions around sexuality and family planning.

Further, in Tanzania, many AGYW lack access to youth-friendly reproductive health services, which are crucial in ensuring that AGYW receive accurate information about fertility and family planning [17]. Approximately 23% of teenagers in Tanzania have initiated childbearing, with access to contraceptives being a significant barrier, as evidenced by previous study showing that only 15.2% of adolescents use modern contraceptives [18]. Knowledge on the fertile window helps AGYW avoid unintended pregnancies even in low access to modern contraceptives [3]. Furthermore, socio-cultural factors, such as initiation ceremonies and early marriage practices, compound the challenges in accessing sexual and reproductive health services, presenting significant barriers to adequate health communication and education and hence low knowledge of fertile period [3, 19].

In Tanzania, where AGYW fertility rates are notably high [20], targeted reproductive health education becomes vital for empowering young women and addressing issues related to unwanted pregnancies [14, 21]. The nation has developed various national strategies and policies for AGYW reproductive health include Health Policy (2007), Fifth revision of the Health Sector strategic plan (HSSPV-2021), International Conference on Population and Development (ICPD25) Commitments, Family planning (FP2030) Commitments, National Plan for Reproductive, Maternal, Newborn, Child and Adolescent Health & Nutrition, (One plan III-2021), the National Accelerated Action and Investment Agenda for Adolescent Health and Wellbeing (NAIA-AHW, 2021-25) and the National Adolescent Health and Development (ADHD-2018-2022) [22]. All these policies and programs aimed to address these issues, but there remains a critical gap in reaching adolescents with adequate information about their reproductive health. Despite this, awareness regarding fertile period among adolescents in Tanzania remains underexplored. Previous studies have reported several factors which influence knowledge on fertile period, including socio-cultural barriers, educational factors, and limited access to health services geared towards AGYW [3, 19, 23]. Therefore, a comprehensive analysis of the factors influencing the knowledge of fertile period among AGYW in Tanzania is necessary to inform health policies and educational programs. This study aimed to assess the knowledge of fertile period which is the knowledge of ovulatory circle and their associated factors among AGYW in Tanzania.

Methods

Data source and design

This study was an analytical cross–sectional survey that utilized secondary data from the 2022 Tanzania Demographic and Health Survey (TDHS), a population–based household surveys, which is conducted typically every five years, by the Tanzania National Bureau of Statistics, surveyed with the Ministries of Tanzania Mainland and Zanzibar [24]. The data were extracted in the individual file record (IR).

Population and sampling

This study used data from recent DHS thet was conducted between February 24 and July 21, 2022, across all regions of Tanzania. The target population for the 2022 TDHS included women of reproductive age (15–49 years), men, children and household from the 32 administrative regions of Tanzania. However, this study explicitly focused on AGYW (15–24 years). A sampling frame was developed at the national level, with the country being stratified by geographic region and urban/rural areas within each region to reduce sampling errors. A two–stage sampling process was then used to select households for the survey. The first stage involved selecting primary sampling units (PSUs), typically based on census enumeration areas (EAs). A probability proportional to size method was used to select PSUs within each stratum. For each selected PSU, a complete household listing was created, followed by the selection of a fixed number of households using equal probability systematic sampling. All AGYW who had spent the previous night in the selected households were eligible to participate in the survey. In total, 5,810 weighted AGYW were included in this study.

Study variables

Dependent variable

The outcome variable for this study was women’s correct knowledge of the fertile period. In the TDHS, this question answered by AGYW was “Knowledge of ovulatory cycle” Response options were: “during her period”, “after the period ended”, “middle of the cycle”, “before the period begins”, “at any time”, and “don’t know”. The outcomes variable was recoded and all respondents who indicated “middle of the cycle” were considered as correct knowledge of the fertile period and coded as “1”, and the other responses, incorrect knowledge of the fertile period, were coded as “0”.

Independent variables

The current study examined variables based on the available data and literature [1, 8, 10, 23]. The following variables were included; age in years (15–19 or 20–24), education level (no formal education, primary, secondary, or higher), marital status (never married or married), wealth index (poorest, poorer, middle, richer or richest), media exposures (yes as listening to the radio, reading the newspaper or watching Television less than once a week or at least once a week or no if otherwise), parity (none, 1–2, ≥ 3), working status (working or not working), current contraceptive use (yes or no), visited a health facility in the past 12 months (yes or no), sexual experience (never had sex or ever had sex), exposure for family planning messages (yes or no), sex of household head (male or female), residence (urban or rural), geographical zones (western, northern, central, lake, southern, eastern or Zanzibar).

Data management and analysis

Considering the complexity of the survey design, we applied individual sampling weights, primary sampling units (clusters), and strata to adjust for over or under sampling. Data management and analysis was performed using STATA version 18.5 (STATA Corp, College Station, TX). Descriptive statistics were summarized using means, standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables, frequency and proportion for categorical variables. The prevalence of knowledge of fertile period was computed as a number of AGYWs who were knowledgeable about fertile period divided by the total number of AGYWs in the study. The Pearson chi–square test was used to compare the differences in the prevalence of the knowledge of fertile period across participants’ characteristics.

Crude and adjusted Odds ratio (OR) with their respective 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported to estimate the strength and magnitude of the association. Univariate analyses were performed by fitting each explanatory variable against the response variable. The backward stepwise regression at a p–value of < 0.1 was used to identify the covariates to be included in the multivariable analyses. We compared the modified Poisson regression model and the logistic regression model using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), as logistic regression may overestimate the association when the prevalence of outcome is not rare (> 10%). Despite this, the logistic regression model was chosen because it had the lowest AIC, suggesting a better fit. Therefore, a weighted binary logistic regression model was fitted to determine factors associated with knowledge of fertile period. Sexual experience and parity were excluded from the final model due to strong multicollinearity with age and marital status. Multicollinearity was formally assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), with a mean VIF < 10 indicating no significant collinearity among the retained independent variables. All tests were two-sided, and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Six in ten (64.1%) had ever had sex and 73.5% were exposed to family planning messages. Nearly two–thirds (64.3%) were from rural setting and only 27.6% of household had female head. (Table 1).

Prevalence of knowledge about fertile period

The current study revealed an overall weighted prevalence of knowledge about fertile period among AGYW was 19.4% (95%CI: 17.9–20.9). Bivariate analysis revealed a significant association between knowledge about fertile period with age, education level, wealth index, media exposure, parity, current contraceptive use, sexual experience, exposure to family planning messages, visiting health facility in the past 12 months and geographical zones (p < 0.05).

There were significant differences in knowledge about fertile period across education level (p < 0.001), AGYW with no formal education had a significantly lower prevalence of knowledge about fertile period (11.6%) compared to AGYW with secondary/higher education (25.7%). Similarly, AGYW from poor household reported lower prevalence of knowledge about fertile period (0.6%) compared to AGYW in rich quintile (22.4%).

Reproductive health-related characteristics revealed a significant association with knowledge about fertile period. AGYW who were currently using contraceptive reported a higher prevalence of knowledge about fertile period (24.2%) compared to their counterparts (18.2%). Surprisingly, AGYW with more than two children (16.9%) had lower prevalence of knowledge about fertile period compared to nulliparous AGYW (18.2%). (Table 1).

Factors associated with knowledge of the fertile period

In the adjusted analysis; AGYW aged 20–24 years (AOR = 1.3, 95%CI: 1.16–1.76) had higher likelihood of knowledge on fertile period compared to their counterparts. AGYW with secondary or higher education had increased odds of knowledge on fertile period compared to those with no formal education (AOR = 3.15, 95%CI: 2.23–4.45). Additionally, AGYW who were exposed to media (AOR = 1.28, 95%CI: 1.02–1.59) had higher odds of being knowledgeable about fertile period compared to their counterparts. Those who visited a health facility in the past 12 months had higher odds of being knowledgeable about fertile period compared to their counterparts (AOR = 1.40, 95%CI: 1.29–1.66). On the geographical zones, residing in central zones (AOR = 2.55, 95%CI: 1.67–3.89) and southern zones (AOR = 1.67, 95%CI: 1.11–2.48) were associated with higher likelihood of knowledge on fertile period (Table 2).

Discussion

This study aimed to assess the prevalence of knowledge of fertile period and their associated factors among AGYW in Tanzania by secondary analysis of the 2022 TDHS. The current study’s findings showed an overall weighted prevalence of 19.4%, which aligns with several previous studies that highlight the generally low levels of fertile period awareness in similar demographic groups [23, 25].​ A study conducted among SSA countries has consistently reported inadequate fertile period knowledge [12]. Conversely, other contrasting studies have indicated a slightly higher level of awareness among urban populations [11]. This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in educational opportunities, access to reproductive health information, and sociocultural factors affecting awareness in various regions [12]. Such contrasting findings show the need for further research to address the significant gaps in fertile period knowledge highlighted by the current study and others conducted in comparable areas. Therefore, policy makers should prioritize integrating comprehensive, age-appropriate reproductive health education into national curricula, particularly in rural and underserved areas, to specifically target and improve fertile period knowledge among adolescent girls and young women.

The finding of this study showed that young women aged 20 to 24 years have a higher likelihood of knowledge about fertile period. This finding is consistent with existing literature suggesting that young women often possess a more comprehensive understanding of reproductive health concerns [3, 12]. ​Studies have shown that increased age often correlates with greater exposure to educational programs and resources about family planning, which is crucial for understanding fertile period [26]. Another study in South Africa reported that young women were significantly more aware of their fertile period than adolescent girls, likely due to increased personal and social responsibility regarding reproductive health as they transition into adulthood [27]. However, some research showed that despite being older, many women still demonstrate significant gaps in fertile period knowledge [28]. These contrasting results highlight the complexity of addressing fertile period awareness and the need for special educational strategies that consider both age and contextual factors influencing knowledge among female youth [27, 28]. Findings of this study indicate a need for interventions that ensure all age groups are adequately informed and empowered to make reproductive health decisions after understanding their fertile period; thus, policy and practice should prioritize early and continuous reproductive health education, tailored to developmental stages starting in early adolescence, to establish foundational knowledge before the age of 20 and reinforce it thereafter.

The finding that AGYW with secondary or higher education have increased odds of knowledge about fertile period strongly supports the hypothesis that educational attainment significantly influences reproductive health knowledge. Research conducted in Nigeria demonstrated that adolescent girls with secondary education were more likely to possess accurate knowledge of reproductive health issues, including fertile period [29]. ​This effect is often attributed to the improved critical thinking and access to information that formal education provides, enabling individuals to understand complex health-related topics better [29, 30].​ Contrasting studies have shown that educational attainment does not always uniformly translate to increased fertile period knowledge due to cultural influences, socioeconomic factors, and disparities in the quality of education received [31]. In some contexts, even highly educated women may exhibit significant gaps in understanding fertile period, particularly regarding culturally sensitive topics [32]. Therefore, while higher education plays a crucial role in enhancing fertile period knowledge, it is vital to recognize the different factors that can either act as barriers or facilitators to the effective reproductive health intervention among AGYW. To address these nuances, policy and practice should develop comprehensive reproductive health curricula that integrate culturally sensitive information and address socioeconomic disparities, ensuring that education effectively translates into improved fertile period knowledge for all AGYW.

​The research indicated the significance of media as an essential vehicle for health education.​ Studies have consistently demonstrated that exposure to media, particularly when channeled through focused health campaigns and educational initiatives, plays a vital role in enhancing knowledge and shaping attitudes towards reproductive health [33–35]. Study conducted in Uganda found that adolescents who consumed media content focused on sexual health were more informed about reproductive issues and contraception use [36]. Nevertheless, it is crucial to consider varying perspectives on this factor. Not all instances of media exposure yield advantageous health knowledge. Research has pointed out that the quality and relevance of the content are crucial determinants in this relationship [37]. AGYW exposed to sensationalized or explicit media may struggle to acquire accurate information about fertile period and reproductive health, which complicates the dynamics between media consumption and knowledge acquisition [38]. Consequently, the effectiveness of media interventions is contingent upon the nature of the content and the contextual factors influencing how AGYW access and interpret this information. To optimize media’s role in health education, policy and practice should prioritize the development and dissemination of high-quality, evidence-based, and culturally appropriate reproductive health content through diverse media channels, while simultaneously promoting media literacy among adolescent girls and young women to critically evaluate information.

The finding that individuals who visited health facilities in the past 12 months were more likely to have knowledge about their fertile period supported by several studies, which have indicated that regular interactions with healthcare providers facilitate the dissemination of important reproductive health information [23, 39].​ Research conducted in various low-income countries demonstrated that patients who engaged in health services were more informed about family planning options and fertile period awareness than those who did not, highlighting a direct link between healthcare access and knowledge acquisition [3]. This highlights the need for health systems to prioritize adolescent-friendly services to ensure that knowledge pertaining to fertile period is not only provided but is also relevant and actionable. The government should prioritize the policy that encourage consumer-friendly health services provision.

The observation that residing in central zones and southern zones correlates with a higher likelihood of having knowledge about fertile period highlights significant geographic disparities in access to reproductive health information. ​The reason behind this finding can be due to several interventions being implemented in the Central zone, like “Afya Yangu-Shule Yangu,” [40] and that of the southern zone, the Sexual and Reproductive Health (SRH) Project [41]. Therefore, the findings suggest the success of interventions delivered in these regions and call for the implementation review of ongoing interventions in other zones. Based on these findings, policy and practice should focus on replicating the successful “Afya Yangu-Shule Yangu” and SRH Project intervention models in other zones, while simultaneously conducting a comprehensive review of existing programs in underperforming regions to identify and address implementation gaps and resource disparities.

Strength and limitations

This study has several strengths. First, it utilizes a large sample size derived from nationally representative, weighted data, which ensures sufficient power to accurately detect the effects of explanatory variables. The sampling weights further contribute to producing reliable estimates and standard errors. However, there are some limitations to consider. Since the DHS survey relies on women’s self–reports, there may be potential biases due to recall inaccuracies or social desirability. Additionally, the cross–sectional nature of the survey prevents the establishment of causal relationships between knowledge of fertile period and the independent variables. Lastly, because the data used were secondary, we were unable to include variables such as women’s knowledge and attitudes toward knowledge on fertile period. Further studies building from our findings my include qualitative methods to uncover personal and community perspectives. Based on these limitations, the findings can be interpreted cautiously.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the discussions surrounding the various factors influencing knowledge about the fertile period among AGYW reveal an important understanding of reproductive health education. The findings indicate that educational attainment, media exposure, healthcare facility visits, and geographical location significantly contribute to young women’s awareness of fertile period. While higher levels of education and access to health services correlate with increased knowledge, disparities persist based on cultural beliefs and the quality of information provided. Thus, addressing these challenges requires tailored interventions that not only enhance the availability of reproductive health education but also consider the sociocultural contexts in which these AGYW reside. ​Ultimately, to improve fertile period awareness comprehensively, it is essential to implement effective educational programs that engage AGYW through multiple channels, thereby empowering them to make informed decisions about their reproductive health.

Implications of the findings

Understanding the fertile period plays a crucial role in fertility awareness and informed decision making regarding reproductive health. This knowledge is particularly vital in the context of Tanzania, where there is a low prevalence of contraceptive use, a significant unmet need for modern contraception, and a considerable rate of early and/or unintended pregnancies [4, 18]. By promoting accurate awareness of the fertile period through educational initiatives and media campaigns, there is a potential to improve AGYW’s ability to make more informed fertility decisions. Such efforts can contribute to increased contraceptive use, including modern methods, as well as fertility–based approaches like natural family planning. These methods rely on precise knowledge of fertile period patterns, and thus, raising awareness about the fertile period can empower AGYW to manage their reproductive health better. This study recommends developing a national reproductive health education curriculum that integrates accurate fertile periods awareness, delivered through both formal schooling and community-based outreach programs. Additionally, investing in media campaigns that utilize local dialects and trusted community leaders can significantly enhance the reach and acceptance of fertile period education, thereby increasing the uptake of modern contraception and fertile period-based family planning methods among AGYW.
