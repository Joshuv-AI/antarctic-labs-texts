Challenges of healthcare quality in Kenya’s tertiary hospitals: assessing the contribution and constraints of asset leasing financing mechanism
Authors: Ezekiel Karino, James Ndegwa, Vincent Were
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundKenya’s public tertiary healthcare is facing persistent quality of healthcare challenges characterized by acute shortage of healthcare workers, frequent industrial unrest, broken-down healthcare facilities, and erratic supply of essential commodities. To address these systemic challenges the government introduced the asset lease financing (ALF) mechanism aimed at strengthen tertiary hospitals through modern medical equipment and technologies. However, the effect of ALF on quality remains highly debated and controversial. This study examined the effect and constraints of ALF in improving quality of healthcare within Kenya’s tertiary hospitals.MethodsA convergent parallel mixed-methods design was employed with quantitative data collected from 145 hospital managers, staff and patients. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participants characteristics and indicators of study variables. Ordinary least square regression was then used to estimate the effect of ALF on quality of tertiary healthcare, controlling for existing traditional funding. Complementary qualitative insights were gathered from 26 policymakers, hospital managers, and health financing experts through semi-structured interviews and analyzed using thematic analysis to identify patterns in strengths and constraints. Integration of findings happened through triangulation to enhance interpretation and understanding.ResultsAnalysis showed that asset lease financing had a significant positive effect on quality of tertiary healthcare (β = 0.587, p < 0.01), explaining 26% of the variance. When traditional funding was controlled, ALF remained significant (β = 0.495, p < 0.01), with the model explaining 33% of the variance. Respondents attributed this to improved access to advanced diagnostic and therapeutic equipment, as well as expanded service capacity. However, descriptive summaries and qualitative perspectives revealed several constraints limiting ALF optimal effect in improving tertiary healthcare quality in Kenya. Stakeholders noted high recurrent costs, under-utilized assets, weak contract negotiation, and top-down procurement processes that limited hospital autonomy and contribution. Operational gaps, including inadequate training and delayed maintenance, further constrained ALF effect on quality.ConclusionsALF has the potential to enhance quality of healthcare and technological capacity in Kenya’s tertiary hospitals, but its effects are contingent on robust governance, effective contract design, and alignment with institutional capacity which seem lacking in the Kenyan context. Without these safeguards, current leasing arrangements risk becoming fiscally unsustainable with little quality enhancement. Policymakers should strengthen transparency, decentralize decision-making, and incorporate performance-based provisions into leasing contracts to maximize ALF effect in enhancing quality of care.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13666-w.Keywords: Asset lease financing, Innovative financing mechanism, Tertiary healthcare, Quality of healthcare

Background

The World Health Organization (WHO) emphasizes the importance of quality of healthcare in achieving Sustainable Development Goals (SDG 2030) and Universal Health Coverage [1]. Yet, quality of care remains complex, defined as the degree to which services increase the likelihood of desired outcomes while aligning with professional knowledge [2, 3]. WHO [4], and Titi-Ofei et al. [5] highlight multiple pillars of health systems that underpin quality, including financing, workforce, governance, information systems, and infrastructure. Despite progress, nearly 5.7 million people die annually in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) due to poor quality care, with Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) disproportionately affected [6, 7].

In Africa, weak health systems, limited personnel, inadequate infrastructure, and shortages of essential supplies undermine quality of care [8]. Studies from Nigeria, Ethiopia, and Uganda link persistent gaps to fragile leadership, poor accountability, and systemic underfunding [9, 10]. In Kenya, although access to quality healthcare is constitutionally guaranteed [11], studies highlight serious deficiencies in tertiary hospitals, including poor clinical outcomes, unsafe services, long waiting times, understaffing, and medication shortages [12, 13]. Government reports highlighted infrastructure failures and low-quality scores, with other studies reporting increased facility mortality linked to poor quality [14–17].

Persistent underfunding, overreliance on donor aid, and fragmented tax-based revenue have been identified as major drivers of quality challenges [18–23]. To address this, the government launched reforms under the Kenya Health Public-Private Collaboration Strategy [24], including adoption of Asset Lease Financing (ALF) for medical equipment [25, 26].

Studies from high-income countries reported a promising picture where ALF has enhanced care access through the availability of high-cost equipment with minimal pressure on hospitals to raise upfront capital investment [27–29]. High-income countries such as the United Kingdom (UK), the United States of America (US), and Russia have long used leasing to upgrade technologies and improve resource allocation [30, 31]. In SSA, where fiscal constraints limit capital investments, leasing is gaining traction as a strategy to bridge infrastructure and technology gaps [32, 33]. Countries such as South Africa, Nigeria, and Rwanda have piloted leasing schemes with varying outcomes depending on governance and system capacity [34].

In 2015, Kenya adopted ALF under Managed Equipment Services (MES) program, a public–private partnership (PPP) framework for the supply, installation, and maintenance of specialized equipment in tertiary hospitals [25, 26, 35]. While the project improved availability of modern equipment, it has equally generated intense national debate on whether the ALF innovative financing achieved its intended objective of improving facilities modernization, access to care, and patients’ experiences.

Though ALF is gaining traction as an innovative financing mechanism to mobilize sustainable private financing for public healthcare by enabling hospitals to access advanced medical technologies, infrastructure, and equipment without incurring prohibitive upfront costs, empirical evidence also points to important limitations, especially in LMICs. Gamova et al. [27] emphasized efficiency benefits of ALF, while Simeoni and Kinoti [26] and Senate of the Republic of Kenya [35] highlighted cost escalation and inequities. Other reviews relied heavily on secondary data [30, 36], pointing to methodological gaps. Most evidence has been derived from high-income countries such as the UK, the US, and Russia with stronger financing and governance systems.

In the Kenyan context, empirical evidence on the actual effect of ALF on healthcare quality—especially in coexisting traditional financing methods (TFM)—remains scarce. This limits applicability to Kenya’s resource-constrained environment. Practical gaps also persist in governance, PPP contract management, and transparency, as studies highlighted under the MES program [26, 35].

Theoretical perspectives

Several theoretical frameworks which informed this study’s constructs have advanced the nexus between financing and health services performance. The WHO health systems framework by WHO [4], emphasizes the interdependence between healthcare financing and service delivery, drawing association between financing mechanisms such as ALF and TFM to the quality of healthcare. Further, the resource mobilization theory by McCarthy & Zald [37] creates the link between successful resource mobilization and organization’s ability to achieve their goals. Under this lens, the study places hospitals as socio-economic institutions whose ability to deliver quality care depends on ALF mechanism mobilizing adequate financing.

Further, to link ALF mechanism and Quality of healthcare, the principal–agent theory by Jensen and Meckling [38] provides a lens where principals such as private investors and agents such as Ministry of Health, National Treasury, and hospital managers interact to finance healthcare through ALF so that citizens can enjoy the expected healthcare quality, informing the study’s construct of governance and accountability in ALF financing. Finally, the Donabedian model of quality healthcare by Donabedian [39] informs the relationship linking financing (structure), processes of care, and outcomes, informing ALF and TFM financing to be conceptualized as structural enablers and confounders respectively, whose effect is reflected in improved processes and outcomes of tertiary healthcare.

Overall goal and novelty of the study

The study examined the challenges of healthcare quality in Kenya’s tertiary hospitals and assessed the contribution and constraints of the ALF mechanism by employing a convergent parallel mixed-methods design. Quantitative assessment drew the perceptions of both managers, healthcare professionals, and patients, while qualitative assessment explored the perspectives of key policymakers and hospital managers responsible for oversight and management of tertiary healthcare financing and quality standards. The theoretical grounding, methodological rigor, and robust analysis underscore the novelty of this study in bridging existing knowledge and practice gaps on ALF deployment, as well as provide new evidence to inform current policy debate on innovative financing and healthcare quality within LMICs’ health systems.

Methods

Research design

The study This study adopted a convergent parallel mixed-method design. This was guided by the pragmatism paradigm, which allowed a combination of inductive and deductive approaches [40]. Both qualitative and quantitative data were collected concurrently, analyzed separately, and then integrated during interpretation [41].

Study setting and population

Under Kenya’s devolved system of government, the public healthcare system is largely decentralized, with the national government and the 47 county governments taking specific roles and functions [42]. The national government sets health policies, provides stewardship, and manages tertiary healthcare facilities (level 6). The county governments manage secondary healthcare facilities (levels 4–5) and primary healthcare facilities (levels 1–3). The Kenya Essential Package for Health (KEPH) guidelines provide for the specific care provided at each respective facility level, with tertiary hospitals providing the most advanced and complex care, leading in research, and setting quality standards [15, 43, 44].

Kenya’s tertiary hospitals (level 6) are Semi-Autonomous Government Agencies (SAGAs) under the Ministry of Health (MoH). SAGAs are corporate entities that can engage in contractual arrangements such as PPPs to raise their own resources [45]. Kenya’s tertiary hospitals receive 70–80% of their budget from the National Treasury, but this funding largely covers recurrent expenditure, leaving limited resources for capital investment in advanced medical equipment [17].

To address persistent underfunding and infrastructure gaps, the government introduced the Managed Equipment Services (MES) project in 2015. The MES program involved seven-year renewable contracts valued at approximately USD 432 million with international equipment suppliers and was structured as a PPP. Contractors supplied, installed, and maintained specialized equipment, as well as trained healthcare workers. The program focused on strengthening five equipment-intensive service areas: operating theaters, central sterile units, renal (dialysis) units, intensive care units, and radiology units across 98 facilities (all 5 level 6 hospitals at the national level and another 93 level 5 and 4 hospitals at the county level) [25, 26, 35]. Nevertheless, while the MES program improved equipment availability, it also generated considerable debate around costs, transparency, contract design, and governance.

Empirical studies indicated that Kenya’s ALF through the MES program faced governance and implementation challenges. There were prolonged procurement processes, insufficient stakeholder goodwill, delays in implementation, and PPP contracts that lack transparency and tailoring to the tertiary hospitals and counties [35, 46, 47]. Itenyi and Njeru [48] and Njagi et al. [49] reported that the MES contract management included performance monitoring with penalties for non-compliance, but weak oversight impacted efficiency. Senate of the Republic of Kenya [35] also observed challenges stemming from inadequate infrastructure, lack of specialized personnel to operate the equipment, high operational costs, and unsustainability.

This study focusing on tertiary healthcare was therefore conducted in 3 of the 5 tertiary hospitals—Moi Teaching and Referral Hospital (MTRH), Mathari National Teaching and Referral Hospital (MNTRH), and the National Spinal Injury Referral Hospital (NSIRH), which granted requisite permission. It targeted senior hospital managers, clinical staff, and patients. All three institutions fall under the MoH and benefited from equipment lease financing through the MES program. In addition, policymakers from the National Government, the MoH, and the National Treasury Headquarters, and financing experts from leading social health investors within the Development Partners in Health–Kenya (DPH-K) network were included. Together, this population comprised actors from policymakers, service providers, service beneficiaries, and social investors, thus the comprehensive ecosystem enablers involved in the financing, implementation, and governance of asset lease financing in Kenya’s health sector. Further, the selection of these populations was based on the multi-level stakeholder framework on innovative healthcare financing ecosystems [34]. These participants were assigned to the qualitative and quantitative strands as guided by the study design.

The qualitative component targeted policymakers and financing stakeholders. The targeted MoH policymakers were heads of departments responsible for stewardship, financing, and policy design [50]. The targeted National Treasury policymakers were heads of departments overseeing health sector PPPs, budget, planning, and resource mobilization [45, 51]. Health financing experts included lead experts from the social health investors under the DPH-K network [24].

The quantitative component focused on the 3 national referral hospitals—MTRH, NRSIH, and MNTRH [52–54]. Respondents were drawn from three categories. The hospital managers included heads of departments—administration, finance, resource mobilization, medical, nursing, HR, and quality assurance. The clinical staff included licensed health professionals across recognized cadres such as physicians, nurses, clinical officers, pharmacists, laboratory technologists, radiographers, etc., while the patients comprised all the direct service beneficiaries across inpatient, outpatient, emergency, and specialized clinics.

Population sampling

Both census and purposive sampling techniques were applied. A census sampling was used where the population size was small but accessible, allowing all eligible respondents to be included. A census eliminates sampling error. Purposive sampling was used where specific expertise, experience, or exposure was required to provide information-rich cases relevant to the study objectives [41].

Purposive sampling was used to select National Treasury policymakers and health financing experts from DPH-K actively engaged in innovative healthcare financing PPPs. The choice of census and purposive sampling for both populations was consistent with the pragmatic mixed-methods design, where the goal was not statistical representativeness but rather the capture of maximum variation and depth of perspectives from information-rich respondents who were directly engaged in or affected by asset lease financing in tertiary healthcare. This approach prioritized information-rich respondents to maximize the depth and relevance of insights. The study emphasized judgment-based sampling aligned with the pragmatic mixed-methods design.

Data collection

Ethical approvals and research license (as detailed in the ethics approval and consent), was obtained before qualitative and quantitative data were collected concurrently between January 2025 and March 2025, within the three facilities and respective offices of the interviewees. Beforehand, the researcher held entry meetings with institutional leadership and obtained organizational clearance before accessing the participants. This process helped build confidence among respondents and improved participation rates. The study also minimized fatigue and enhanced the validity of the information obtained by ensuring each category of respondent engaged only with questions relevant to their expertise. The quantitative data was collected using structured questionnaires with 5-point Likert scale items (ranging from 1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree). Patients and staff mainly responded to items on the quality of tertiary healthcare (QoTH), while managers completed items on asset lease financing (ALF), traditional funding methods (TFM) and QoTH. The qualitative data was collected using semi-structured interviews conducted with the interviewees, allowing the participants to express their experiences and perspectives on asset leasing and quality of care [57]. The interviews were recorded as well as notes taken during the sessions.

Reliability and validity of study instruments

The study instruments demonstrated strong reliability and validity across all respondent categories. Cronbach’s alpha values for main constructs ranged from 0.78 to 0.91, exceeding the recommended threshold of 0.70, indicating good reliability. Construct validity was confirmed through expert review and exploratory factor analysis, showing satisfactory item loadings ( > 0.50) and clear differentiation among constructs. Convergent validity was supported by average variance extracted (AVE) values above 0.50, while discriminant validity was established as the square root of AVE for each construct exceeded inter-construct correlations. These results confirm that the questionnaires reliably and accurately captured perceptions of innovative financing mechanisms, operational challenges, and service outcomes. Trustworthiness of qualitative data was achieved based on Lincoln and Guba [58] four criteria: credibility (triangulation, member checking), transferability (thick description, variation in respondents), dependability (audit trail, peer debriefing), and confirmability (reflexivity, software-based coding).

Data analysis

Qualitative data were analyzed thematically using six-step framework, Braun and Clarke [59], supported by NVivo software for coding and theme development, ensuring systematic identification of patterns across respondents while maintaining transparency and replicability [60]. Quantitative data were analyzed in SPSS Version 26 using descriptive statistics to summarize respondent characteristics and study variables. Inferential analysis employed stepwise regression to assess the effect of ALF on the QoTH, while controlling for TFM. The analytical model applied was:

Hypothesis testing was guided by the following formulation:

H01: Asset lease financing (ALF) has no significant effect on the quality of tertiary healthcare (QoTH) in Kenya’s tertiary hospitals when controlling for traditional funding methods (TFM).

H01: Asset lease financing (ALF) has no significant effect on the quality of tertiary healthcare (QoTH) in Kenya’s tertiary hospitals when controlling for traditional funding methods (TFM).

The findings were triangulated, strengthening validity and providing nuanced insights into the relationships between ALF and QoTH.

Theoretical and conceptual framework of the study

The study was guided by an integrated conceptual framework linking asset lease financing (ALF) to quality of tertiary healthcare (QoTH) (see Fig. 1). Firstly, Donabedian Quality of Care Model by Donabedian [39] provided the foundation for conceptualizing healthcare quality in terms of structures, processes, and outcomes. The study conceptualized ALF to strengthen QoTH structures (governance, equipment, and infrastructure) and processes (efficiency and care protocols) and outcomes (patient satisfaction and results of interventions). Second, the Resource Mobilization Theory by McCarthy and Zald [37] offered a lens for understanding resource generation and allocation within the health systems, and ALF was viewed as a mechanism that enables hospitals to mobilize financial and technological resources beyond the traditional funding methods (TFM). Third, the WHO Health Systems Framework by WHO [4], situated ALF within the broader complex health system, emphasizing how financing interacts with other system building blocks—such as service delivery, governance, and human resources—to influence overall health system performance and quality of care.

Conceptual framework

Based on these perspectives, the study conceptual framework positioned ALF as the independent variable, hypothesized to improve QoTH in Kenya’s hospitals, while TFM was modeled as a contextual factor (control variable) also shaping QoTH. The framework informed the development of study instruments, data analysis, and interpretation, ensuring consistency between the study’s objectives and its methodological orientation.

Results

Participants characteristics and response rate

The characteristics of the study sample enabled capturing of diverse perspectives of policy formulation, operational management, and patient experience, affording a comprehensive assessment of the ALF mechanism and its effect on QoTH. Qualitative participants—MoH policymakers (n = 9), National Treasury officials (n = 7), and social health investors (n = 10)—with overall response rates (81.25%). They were mostly aged 36–55, with balanced gender representation and high educational attainment, majorly master’s degrees. All held senior management roles, with professional experience ranging from 1 to over 15 years.

Quantitative respondents were hospital managers (n = 29), clinical staff (n = 58), and patients (n = 58), with an overall response rate of 96.7%. Hospital managers were primarily 36–55 years old and gender-balanced, with most holding master’s degrees and having varied professional experience. Clinical staff were mid-career professionals with undergraduate qualifications and extensive tenure, while patients were largely 18–35 years old, with diverse education levels and hospital stays ranging from short-term to recurrent admissions.

Comprehensive tables detailing the characteristics of both quantitative and qualitative participants have been included in the supplementary materials to this manuscript.

Descriptive results

The study analyzed perceptions of traditional funding, asset leasing, and quality of tertiary healthcare across tertiary hospital managers, clinical staff, and patients using a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly agree; 5 = strongly disagree).

Traditional funding methods

Treasury Transfers (TTs): There was some agreement that TTs offer more revenue than innovative mechanisms (M = 2.39, SD = 1.49). The high SD point to considerable variation in perceptions, indicating that while some respondents strongly agreed, others were less convinced of TTs revenue strength. Effectiveness in meeting budgetary needs was largely neutral (M = 3.27, SD = 1.33), with equally dispersed views highlighting inconsistent satisfaction with budget adequacy. Sufficiency to cover funding requirements was neutral to disagreement (M = 3.50, SD = 0.99), with lower here SD suggesting stronger consensus that TTs are generally insufficient, highlighting reliance on alternative financing mechanisms.

Social Health Insurance (SHI): Reliability in enhancing QoTH was moderately agreed on (M = 2.83, SD = 1.27), with the moderate SD indicating mixed experiences among respondents. Sufficiency for long-term improved outcomes (M = 2.14, SD = 1.17), and the relatively narrowed clustering suggests shared concerns about sustainability. Concern about hindering innovative partnerships was moderately agreed (M = 2.76, SD = 0.96), with the low SD pointing to stronger agreement that SHI does not fully incentivize innovation.

Out-of-Pocket Payments (OOPs): Patients rely on OOP for healthcare showed agreed on (M = 1.93, SD = 1.01), with low mean and moderate SD suggest widespread recognition of reliance, but with differing intensities across respondents. Stability and reliability for QoTH improvement were rated moderately neutral (M = 3.03, SD = 1.18), with variation in views indicating uneven experiences. The impact on adoption of innovative financing indicate neutrality to disagreement (M = 3.45, SD = 1.08), with moderate variation, reflecting that while OOPs are recognized as a critical revenue source, they remain unstable and may limit quality financing.

Asset lease financing mechanisms

Lease Premiums: Reasonableness relative to QoTH gains was moderately agreed on (M = 2.76, SD = 1.21), with the SD suggesting varied opinions about value for money. Budget accommodation by government was somewhat neutrally rated (M = 3.21, SD = 1.03), with largely consistent views across respondents. Adverse effects on hospital operations were moderately agreed on (M = 2.99, SD = 1.09), and the relatively low SD suggests greater agreement on operational challenges. Overall cost-effectiveness satisfaction was also moderately neutral (M = 3.27, SD = 1.52), with the high SD suggesting sharp variations in perception between supportive and dissatisfied respondents.

Equipment/Asset Utilization: Efficiency in utilization to improve QoTH was agreed on (M = 2.83, SD = 1.51) and maximum use in hospitals showed neutrality (M = 3.00, SD = 1.56) both had high SDs, highlight variability in how facilities experienced utilization. Consistent use as intended was nearly disagreed upon (M = 3.52, SD = 1.19), with lower variability pointing to largely shared concerns about underutilization. Effect on overall quality was moderately neutral (M = 3.10, SD = 1.11), with moderate variation suggesting a mixed but recognizable effect.

Equipment/Asset Maintenance Costs: Preventive maintenance coverage showed generally neutral views (M = 3.28, SD = 1.22) and pressure on hospital budgets equally generally neutral views (M = 3.11, SD = 1.25) and moderate SDs, suggesting differing but not extreme opinions on this. Machine downtime affecting service quality (M = 3.17, SD = 1.18) reflected a similar pattern of moderate variability on slightly neutral opinion. The cost comparison of leasing versus purchasing (M = 2.41, SD = 1.22) was relatively low with tighter clustering, indicating broader agreement that maintenance remains a significant financial challenge for tertiary hospitals.

Quality of tertiary healthcare

Hospital managers’ perceptions

Care Structure: Adequate staffing (M = 2.07, SD = 1.23) showed general agreement mean but moderate variability, suggesting widespread concern though with differing severity. Governance structures (M = 2.93, SD = 1.36) and quality improvement teams (M = 2.83, SD = 1.34) displayed moderate SDs, reflecting mixed but consistent general agreement. Infrastructure adequacy (M = 3.72, SD = 1.26) and equipment adequacy (M = 3.83, SD = 1.18) showed general neutrality to disagreement opinion but notable variation, highlighting varied experiences across facilities. Budget sufficiency (M = 3.97, SD = 1.30) and operational planning sufficiency (M = 4.17, SD = 1.21) reflected general disagreement but also wide divergence, suggesting strong concerns among some but more optimism among other respondents.

Care Process: Infection prevention (M = 3.62, SD = 1.39) and community involvement (M = 4.10, SD = 1.18) showed general disagreement views with moderate variability, suggesting some facilities perform well while others lag. Patient identification (M = 4.24, SD = 1.14) reflected generally poor performance with less variation. Other processes such as feedback mechanisms (M = 3.90, SD = 1.30) were neutrally perceived with wider variability, indicating inconsistent implementation across hospitals.

Care Outcomes: Patient satisfaction (M = 2.27, SD = 1.08), resuscitation success (M = 2.17, SD = 1.33), complications (M = 2.17, SD = 1.25), and mortality (M = 2.34, SD = 1.31) showed generally agreed views and moderate-to-high SDs, pointed to widespread concerns but with differing severity across institutions. Stock-outs of essential medicines showed general neutrality (M = 3.41, SD = 1.40) with higher variability, indicating highly uneven availability.

Clinical staff perceptions

Care Structure: Governance structures (M = 4.00, SD = 1.12) and strategic plans (M = 4.24, SD = 0.66) scored was generally disagreed to be satisfactory with low SDs, reflecting strong consensus. In contrast, staffing adequacy (M = 2.96, SD = 1.28) and budget sufficiency (M = 2.39, SD = 0.94) was generally agreed on, with moderate variability, reflecting widely shared perceptions.

Care Process: Outpatient service delivery (M = 4.34, SD = 0.76) and infection prevention (M = 4.31, SD = 0.62) was generally disagreed to be good with very low SDs, showing strong consensus that processes are not functioning well. Patient rights protection (M = 3.79, SD = 1.00) and feedback mechanisms (M = 3.80, SD = 0.99) showed more neutrality to disagreement with variability, supporting to uneven implementation views.

Care Outcomes: Complications (M = 2.07, SD = 0.87) and stock-outs (M = 2.41, SD = 1.10) was generally agreed upon with relatively low SDs, reflecting widespread agreement. In contrast, mortality (M = 2.48, SD = 1.04) and readmissions (M = 2.48, SD = 0.98) was agreed to be high with moderate variation, suggesting differing hospital-level performance on the critical indicators.

Patients’ perceptions

Care Structure: Staffing adequacy (M = 3.76, SD = 1.25) and equipment availability (M = 3.59, SD = 1.32) was largely disagreed by this group and high variability, reflecting divergent experiences of service quality. Availability of medicines and supplies (M = 2.44, SD = 1.24) was agreed to be an issue with less variation, suggesting a shared concern about shortages.

Care Process: Infection prevention (M = 4.03, SD = 0.89) was largely disagreed to be not 90satisfactory with low variability, reflecting widespread dissatisfaction. Similarly, feedback mechanisms (M = 3.11, SD = 1.58) was neutral to disagreement but the highest SD, suggests highly uneven implementation and divergent patient experiences.

Care Outcomes: Overall satisfaction (M = 4.07, SD = 0.95) and complication prevention (M = 3.76, SD = 1.10) showed disagreement by this group with low-to-moderate variability, indicating consensus on dissatisfactory service. Consequently, readmissions (M = 2.72, SD = 1.25) and critical outcomes (M = 2.83, SD = 1.40) were agreed to be high with high SDs, showing both concern but uneven performance across hospitals.

Regression analysis

Asset lease financing mechanism and the quality of tertiary healthcare

The hypothesis of the study was stated in the null form as follows:

H₀₁: ALF mechanism has no significant effect on the QoTH in Kenya’s tertiary hospitals when controlling for the TFM.

H₀₁: ALF mechanism has no significant effect on the QoTH in Kenya’s tertiary hospitals when controlling for the TFM.

To test this hypothesis, a stepwise multiple linear regression analysis was conducted with the QoTH as the dependent variable and ALF mechanism and TFM as independent variables. The analysis aimed to determine whether ALF significantly affects QoTH while accounting for the influence of TFM. Table 4 shows the regression results.

The regression equations derived from the analysis were as follows:

Model 2: QoTH = 0.578 + 0.495(ALF) + 0.389(TFM)

As illustrated in Table 5, the regression findings in Model 1 show that ALF had a positive and statistically significant effect on the QoTH in Kenya (β = 0.587, t = 3.041, p < 0.01), explaining 26% of the variance. In Model 2, when TFM was included as a control variable, ALF remained significant (β = 0.495, t = 3.414, p < 0.01), while TFM also showed a positive and significant effect (β = 0.389, t = 2.543, p < 0.05). As noted, the explanatory power of the model increased from R2 = 0.262 to R2 = 0.334, indicating an improvement of about 7% points on addition of TFM.

Qualitative results

Thematic analysis

Analysis of the qualitative interviews yielded 7 dominant themes (see complementary materials) regarding the perceived effects of ALF on QoTH in Kenya. These themes were inductively derived from the data using NVivo-assisted coding and reflect both areas of convergence and divergence across respondent groups. Together, they illustrate how ALF has simultaneously expanded access to modern technology while exposing structural, governance, and institutional weaknesses that constrain its effects.

Theme 1: Cost-effectiveness and value-for-money concerns

A dominant theme was concern over the fiscal implications of ALF. Many respondents perceived ALF as financially burdensome, pointing to escalating operational costs, idle equipment, and inflexible lease obligations that persisted even when machines were underutilized.

“… … … . [leasing] triples cost of operation of machines. Illogical … we continue paying even when equipment is idle … … .” (MoH Policymaker 2).

Similarly, health financing experts highlighted risks of budget diversion, with one noting that

“… … . high lease costs may strain hospital budgets, potentially diverting resources from other critical areas” (Health financing expert 5).

Contrasting perspectives emerged, however, particularly around short-term affordability versus long-term sustainability. Some respondents recognized ALF as a pragmatic approach to overcome capital barriers, enabling hospitals to access modern equipment without prohibitive upfront costs. For instance, one participant observed,

“… … . leasing medical equipment has significantly improved availability, though at a higher long-term cost. For example, a facility would have spent approximately KES 200 million (app. USD 1.56 million [61]; if purchased upfront, compared to KES 700 million (app. USD 5.47 million; [61]) for leasing.” (Health financing expert 3).

Overall, while ALF was viewed as instrumental in reducing initial capital barriers, the sustainability and cost-effectiveness of the model remained contested, reflecting tension between immediate accessibility and long-term fiscal prudence.

Theme 2: Operational and maintenance challenges

Respondents consistently pointed to operational and maintenance gaps as critical constraints undermining the effect of ALF in improving quality of care. Hospital managers reported frequent downtime due to delayed servicing and weak maintenance arrangements. One manager highlighted,

“… … Lessees take time before undertaking repairs … the equipment is now out of order” (Hospital Manager 3).

Beyond service delays, a pervasive concern was the mismatch between advanced leased technologies and the technical capacity available to operate them. Both policymakers and managers described inadequate staff training and shortages of specialized personnel, leaving equipment underutilized or idle. As one national treasury policymaker noted,

“… … . lack of specialists to run the facilities … staff do not know how to use the equipment” (National Treasury Policymaker).

Together, these accounts suggest that leasing without parallel investment in human resource development and technical support creates a fragile arrangement: equipment may be physically available but not functionally integrated into service delivery.

Theme 3: Centralized procurement and governance deficiencies

A strong and recurrent critique across respondents was that ALF in Kenya has been overly centralized, with procurement decisions driven primarily by the National Treasury and the MoH rather than by facility-level needs. This disconnects, participants argued, undermined the relevance and utility of leased equipment in tertiary hospitals.

“… . there is a disconnect between the people managing the process at the central level and the users … some counties received equipment they didn’t need or couldn’t use” (Health financing expert 3).

Concerns also extended to the governance of leasing contracts, where respondents perceived limited technical capacity and weak negotiation on the government side.

“… … those appointed to negotiate don’t understand health systems … it’s a conspiracy” (Health financing expert 4).

These perspectives reflect a broader governance challenge: leasing arrangements were often characterized by opaque processes, inadequate stakeholder consultation, and a lack of technical oversight.

Taken together, the evidence suggests that centralized procurement and weak governance structures contributed to asset misallocation, inefficiency, and reduced ownership at the hospital level, limiting ALF’s potential to improve QoTH.

Theme 4: Improved access to technology

Despite governance and cost concerns, many respondents acknowledged that ALF has expanded access to advanced medical technologies that hospitals would otherwise have struggled to afford. Leasing was described as a practical mechanism for equipping tertiary hospitals with modern diagnostic and therapeutic tools, thereby reducing start-up capital requirements and enabling service upgrades. One hospital manager explained,

“… . asset lease has enabled the hospital to access equipment that would otherwise be very costly … start-up capital is reduced” (Hospital Manager 7).

From this perspective, ALF has played an equalizing role in the health system by bridging gaps between resource-constrained facilities and better-funded urban hospitals. The improved availability of equipment such as imaging tools and ambulances was viewed as a tangible benefit, enhancing timeliness and QoTH.

However, some respondents emphasized that these benefits were conditional on contract execution and operational readiness. Where equipment was functional and supported by adequate training, ALF was seen as a transformative enabler; yet in contexts of poor maintenance and limited technical capacity, the promised gains were less visible. This conditionality underscores that while ALF improves access to technology, its impact on QoTH ultimately depends on complementary system supports.

Theme 5: Contractual transparency and oversight gaps

A recurring concern across respondents was the lack of transparency and accountability in the design and oversight of ALF contracts. Several participants noted that leasing arrangements were often negotiated without sufficient user input, resulting in agreements that prioritized political or vendor interests over clinical effectiveness. As one treasury policymaker observed,

“… . contracts [were] informed by self-interest … performance-based leasing is lacking … .” (Treasury Policymakers 1).

This theme highlights weaknesses in the contractual framework that govern ALF implementation. The absence of clear performance benchmarks and accountability mechanisms left hospitals vulnerable to delayed service delivery, inflated costs, and underperforming equipment. In some cases, respondents suggested that contractual opacity contributed to mistrust between frontline facilities and central agencies, particularly where oversight responsibilities were poorly defined.

In contrast, a minority of respondents proposed that ALF could be structured more effectively through outcome-linked contracts and robust monitoring. These suggestions align with global best practices in PPPs, where risk-sharing and performance-based incentives enhance efficiency. Nevertheless, in the Kenyan context, the prevailing perception was that weak contractual design undermined ALF’s potential to consistently improve QoTH.

Theme 6: Quality of care trade-offs

While ALF was acknowledged as a pathway for improving technological access, many respondents questioned whether it advanced overall QoTH. Concerns centered on the diversion of scarce hospital resources toward lease payments, potentially at the expense of other critical inputs such as staff, drugs, or essential supplies.

“… … high lease costs may strain hospital budgets, potentially diverting resources from other critical areas … leasing doesn’t represent value for money” (Health financing expert 5).

Hospital managers echoed this sentiment, noting that the prioritization of equipment acquisition sometimes overshadowed patient-centered priorities. For instance, one manager remarked,

“… … the impact on quality of care depends on how well this hospital balances lease cost with patient care priority” (Hospital Manager 5).

This perspective reflects concerns that leasing arrangements risk privileging technology acquisition over fundamental care processes.

In contrast, a smaller group of respondents argued that ALF could still enhance QoTH if carefully integrated into broader hospital planning. They suggested that the challenge lay not in the financing mechanism itself but in weak alignment with health system priorities. Overall, the dominant view was that ALF, unless accompanied by deliberate safeguards, may inadvertently compromise clinical priorities by redirecting financial and managerial focus toward lease obligations rather than patient outcomes.

Theme 7: Limited awareness or utilization of ALF equipment

A final theme that emerged was the limited awareness, engagement, or utilization of ALF mechanisms among hospital managers. In several cases, respondents expressed uncertainty or minimal involvement in leasing arrangements, highlighting gaps in communication and institutional integration. One manager candidly noted,

“… . Actually, I don’t have sufficient information on this matter/financing mechanism in the hospital” (Hospital Manager 4). Another added, “… we don’t have many leased assets/equipment with lease financing” (Hospital Manager 9).

This lack of awareness was compounded by reports of delayed or absent rollout of leasing arrangements at the facility level. As one manager put it,

“… late funding. So far, no equipment is leased at the moment” (Hospital Manager 8).

Such accounts illustrate that while ALF has been positioned as a national financing innovation, it has not been consistently institutionalized at the facility level.

The findings suggest that weak dissemination and poor stakeholder engagement reduce the perceived legitimacy and utility of ALF among hospital managers, who are the frontline implementers. Without greater institutional integration, leasing risks being viewed as an externally imposed policy rather than an embedded financing tool that facilities can own, adapt, and sustain.

Integrative interpretation (triangulation of results)

The mixed-methods findings demonstrate both convergence and divergence between the quantitative and qualitative strands. Quantitatively, ALF was positively correlated with perceived QoTH, suggesting its role in improving service access and infrastructure, even after accounting for TFM. Qualitatively, respondents confirmed this enabling effect, especially in expanding access to advanced diagnostic and therapeutic technologies. However, the interviews nuanced this picture by revealing persistent governance, fiscal, and operational constraints that have offset ALF’s intended effect. Whereas the quantitative results point to a net positive association and significance, the qualitative themes underscore that this effect is conditional on tackling the weakness on contract design, decentralization, and capacity alignment. Taken together, the results indicate that while ALF holds potential as a financing innovation, its contribution to QoTH is moderated by institutional and governance factors, and its added value over TFM is contingent on context-specific implementation.

Discussion

In the quantitative analysis, the results demonstrated that both ALF and TFM exert statistically significant and positive effects on healthcare quality, with results revealing that ALF had the stronger predictive effect on quality of tertiary healthcare when TFM was controlled.

The Resource Mobilization Theory, which emphasizes the capacity of institutions to creatively mobilize and deploy resources to achieve organizational goals, can explain the effect of ALF goals. can explain. ALF enables tertiary hospitals to spread payments over time, overcome liquidity constraints, simultaneously expand service capacity, and access modern diagnostic and treatment technologies without incurring the prohibitive upfront costs of direct procurement. This reflects a strategic mobilization of external financial resources, aligning with global shifts toward leveraging private capital for public health outcomes.

Through the WHO Health Systems Framework lens, ALF contributes directly to strengthening the medical products, technologies, and infrastructure building block. By modernizing hospital equipment through leasing, tertiary hospitals enhance their capacity to deliver advanced services that would otherwise be unattainable under resource-limited traditional financing systems. In this regard, ALF goes beyond basic operational stability and creates opportunities for transformative improvements in service delivery and patient care.

Interpreted through the Donabedian Quality of Care Model, ALF predominantly enhances the structural dimension of quality—introducing advanced imaging machines, surgical tools, and laboratory technologies. Improved structure facilitates more accurate diagnostics and more effective clinical processes, which ultimately lead to better patient outcomes. This is consistent with the MES program outcomes, which, despite challenges, demonstrated that leased technologies could expand service availability across counties [48].

While TFM remained a significant predictor of healthcare quality, its role was comparatively weaker. This aligns with critiques by Tsofa et al. [62], who noted that input-based budgeting systems under TFM often constrain innovation, lock resources into recurrent expenditures such as salaries and utilities, and provide limited flexibility for investing in new technologies. Nevertheless, TFM’s contribution to stability cannot be ignored—it ensures continuity of basic services and underpins routine hospital operations, thereby complementing ALF’s transformative effects.

The interviewed respondents also contextualized opportunities and constraints of ALF, including underutilization of equipment, inadequate training of personnel, and weak governance frameworks. These challenges mirror critique of the MES program and underscore the argument that PPP financing mechanisms require strong governance, regulatory oversight, and institutional readiness to maximize value for money [25, 26, 35]. However, the insights also pointed to opportunities that ALF implementors can leverage to optimize effect.

Theme 1: Cost-effectiveness and value-for-money concerns

The findings revealed a persistent conflict between the intended cost-saving benefits of ALF and the fiscal realities of long-term contractual commitments. While the quantitative analysis showed a positive correlation between ALF and perceived QoTH, qualitative insights suggested that leasing often increased recurrent expenditures and, in some cases, strained hospital budgets. This paradox illustrates that ALF may deliver technological gains in the short term but risks undermining allocative efficiency when contractual obligations outpace absorptive capacity.

From the perspective of Donabedian Quality of Care Model, this dynamic reflects a trade-off between the structural dimension of care (equipment availability) and the financing processes required to sustain it. The results also resonate with Resource Mobilization Theory, which emphasizes that resource inflows alone do not guarantee effective service delivery unless aligned with institutional capacity. Similar concerns have been reported in other LMIC contexts, where deferred capital obligations under PPP framework or lease contracts have raised questions about long-term financial sustainability [63, 64].

Taken together, the evidence suggests that the value-for-money proposition of ALF in tertiary healthcare depends not only on access to equipment but also on careful contract design, cost-sharing mechanisms, and integration into broader fiscal sustainability frameworks. Without these safeguards, ALF risks becoming a “lease trap,” locking facilities into inflexible financial obligations that compromise other critical inputs such as medicines or workforce investment.

Theme 2: Operational and maintenance challenges

A recurrent theme in the study was the operational fragility of leased equipment, particularly due to delayed maintenance, inadequate training, and misalignment between available technology and staff capacity. These weaknesses limited the sustained utilization of assets, undermining the efficiency gains that ALF was expected to deliver. The findings underscore the argument that financing mechanisms cannot be evaluated in isolation from the systems that enable their use.

Within Donabedian Quality of Care Model, this reflects a gap between structural improvements (equipment acquisition) and the process dimension of care (competence in operating and maintaining the equipment). Similarly, the WHO Health Systems Framework highlights the interdependence of service delivery, workforce, and medical technologies; without adequate investment in health workforce training and technical support, even well-financed assets risk becoming idle. Previous studies have similarly observed that infrastructure investments in LMICs often fail to translate into improved outcomes when not paired with human resource development and biomedical engineering capacity [65].

These findings also raise concerns about long-term sustainability. A reliance on external service contracts for maintenance creates vulnerability to delays, and central procurement arrangements exacerbate the disconnect between local needs and available capacity. Ultimately, the results suggest that the effect of ALF as a financing mechanism is contingent not only on financial design but also on parallel investments in institutional capacity, technical training, and service-level agreements that ensure timely and responsive maintenance.

Theme 3: Centralized procurement and governance deficiencies

The study revealed that the top-down nature of ALF implementation in Kenya often resulted in a mismatch between procured equipment and facility-level needs. Centralized decision-making by the Treasury or national MoH constrained the autonomy of tertiary hospitals, producing a principal–agent misalignment where the procuring entity was distanced from the end users. This governance structure contributed to underutilization of equipment and reduced efficiency of the leasing scheme.

From a theoretical standpoint, this aligns with principal-agent theory value for money theory by Jensen and Meckling [38], which emphasizes the risks of divergent interests and information asymmetry between policymakers and service providers. It also reflects the WHO Health Systems Framework principle of strategic purchasing, which advocates for aligning financial decisions with provider-level discretion to ensure that investments are relevant and responsive to local needs. International experiences—such as in Turkey, Rwanda, and Thailand—illustrate that decentralization and participatory procurement can strengthen the link between financing innovations and quality of care [66, 67].

In Kenya’s case, centralized ALF implementation also exposed weaknesses in contract negotiation and oversight. Limited technical expertise in procurement left the system vulnerable to political capture and commercial interests, as suggested in prior critiques of Kenya’s health sector procurement [25, 68]. This resonates with public choice theory by Tullock [69], which cautions that procurement processes can be distorted by rent-seeking behaviors when governance safeguards are weak.

Overall, the findings highlight that ALF cannot be considered a purely technical financing tool; its success depends on governance structures that ensure transparency, facility-level participation, and alignment of incentives. Without such safeguards, ALF risks reproducing systemic inefficiencies rather than serving as a driver of QoTH improvement.

Theme 5: Contractual transparency and oversight gaps

A recurring concern in this study was the lack of transparency and accountability in ALF contracts. Respondents highlighted that many agreements were negotiated without sufficient user involvement, resulting in weak oversight mechanisms and limited responsiveness to facility-level needs. From a governance perspective, this reflects elements of public choice theory, where procurement processes may be shaped by political or self-interested actors rather than by technical efficiency or patient-centered priorities [25, 68].

The weakness of contractual arrangements theory undermines the potential of ALF to deliver value for money. Without clear service-level agreements, performance-based clauses, or mechanisms for monitoring uptime and utilization, facilities risk paying high premiums without corresponding improvements in QoTH. Comparative experiences from other LMICs have shown that leasing contracts that lack robust accountability provisions are vulnerable to rent-seeking, inefficiencies, and poor enforcement [70].

These findings reinforce the need to shift ALF from transactional procurement models toward value-based partnerships. Incorporating performance-linked payments, independent monitoring, and transparent reporting could strengthen alignment between financiers, providers, and users. This would also resonate with the WHO Health Systems Framework, which emphasizes governance and accountability as foundational building blocks of health system performance. In Kenya, embedding such safeguards is critical if ALF is to evolve from a contested financing tool into a credible pathway for enhancing QoTH.

Theme 6: Quality of care trade-offs

While ALF was perceived to expand access to advanced technologies, several stakeholders cautioned that the high recurrent lease costs may divert resources from other critical inputs necessary for QoTH, such as medicines, consumables, and human resources. This reflects a broader trade-off within constrained fiscal environments, where financing innovations can create new opportunities but also intensify budgetary pressures.

From the lens of Donabedian Quality of Care Model, quality is determined not only by the availability of equipment (structure) but also by the adequacy of processes and outcomes. Excessive allocation toward lease payments risks undermining other dimensions of quality, particularly where staffing and drug supply are equally essential to effective care. This aligns with evidence from other LMICs, where heavy emphasis on capital-intensive infrastructure has sometimes strained operational budgets and created tradeoffs in service delivery [63, 65].

The fiscal space theory by Tandon and Cashin [64] also provides insight: while leasing can create the appearance of expanded fiscal capacity by deferring upfront costs, it does not expand long-term budgetary room if recurrent obligations crowd out priority expenditures. This creates what some stakeholders described as a “lease trap,” where hospitals are bound to costly contracts even when equipment becomes underutilized or obsolete.

These findings underscore that ALF should not be appraised solely in terms of technological acquisition but value for money. acquisition but also through its opportunity costs in relation to comprehensive service delivery. A more sustainable pathway may lie in blended financing models that balance capital leasing with parallel investments in workforce, supplies, and maintenance systems.

Theme 7: Limited awareness or utilization of ALF equipment

The study also revealed gaps in awareness and utilization of leased equipment across tertiary hospitals, with some facilities reporting minimal or no uptake. This reflects weaknesses in communication, rollout, and institutional preparedness that may limit the intended benefits of ALF. From a systems perspective, these gaps indicate that procurement alone does not guarantee service improvement; adoption depends on user awareness, technical capacity, and integration into clinical workflows.

The WHO Health Systems Framework highlights that effective service delivery requires coordinated alignment across financing, health workforce, and governance building blocks. In the Kenyan context, the absence of adequate sensitization, training, and local-level engagement has undermined the potential impact of leasing. Comparable findings from other LMICs show that technological innovations often falter when frontline providers are not adequately informed or empowered to adopt them [70].

From a resource mobilization theory perspective, underutilized equipment represents both a financial inefficiency and a missed opportunity to maximize scarce health sector resources. This inefficiency is exacerbated in resource-constrained settings, where the costs of idle or poorly integrated equipment could otherwise support essential medicines or staffing.

Taken together, these findings emphasize the need for stronger institutional communication strategies, participatory procurement planning, and structured training programs to ensure that ALF investments translate into meaningful improvements in service delivery and patient outcomes.

Study limitations

This study has some limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the cross-sectional design restricts causal inference; observed associations between ALF and QoTH cannot be interpreted as definitive cause-effect acquisitions but cause-effect relationships. Second, the study relied on self-reported perceptions from hospital managers, policymakers, and health financing experts, which may be subject to recall bias or social desirability bias. Third, the quantitative analysis used facility-level perceptions of QoTH rather than documented patient outcome measures, which limits generalizability to clinical effectiveness. Fourth, the study was conducted in tertiary (level 6) hospitals and may not capture experiences from lower-level facilities that adopted the ALF over the same period. Fifthly, while triangulation of qualitative and quantitative data enhanced robustness, some thematic nuances may have been influenced by the composition and representativeness of key informants. Additionally, we also report a scope limitation of the cause-effect study, as the study focused only on Kenya’s context. This country focus scope was guided by the limited support/funding available for the study. Despite these limitations, the integration of mixed methods and rich key informants’ opinions and perspectives provide a strong basis for drawing policy-relevant insights on the role of ALF in Kenya’s healthcare system.

Areas for future research

We suggest that there are areas for future research to build on this study in several directions. First, as the outcomes of ALF may change over time, there’s a need for longitudinal studies to assess its effects over time and its long-term impact on QoTH, to account for evolving policy dynamics or external pressures such as economics and politics. Second, comparative studies between asset lease financing and TFM across different levels of care, such as primary facilities, would clarify relative efficiency and value for money. Third, economic evaluations—such as cost-effectiveness or cost-benefit analyses—could provide more precise estimates of fiscal trade-offs. Fourth, research into governance and contract design, including performance-based leasing models, would help identify mechanisms that minimize inefficiencies and political capture. Finally, greater attention should be given to understanding how ALF influences equity, access, and quality of care from the empirical data standpoint.

Conclusion and recommendations

In ccontrast to the conventional assumptions that TFM is the most effective model for health financing in LMICs, the findings revealed that ALF had the stronger predictive effect on quality of tertiary healthcare. The findings showed that ALF can offer better quality enhancement than TFM. ALF has addressed structural inequities by redistributing high-end medical infrastructure across tertiary hospital geographies and contributed to improved timeliness and reach of care. However, qualitative evidence points to the fact that it seems to have not achieved its anticipated transformative quality of care in Kenya’s tertiary hospitals. This is attributed to constraints converging around governance weaknesses, implementation inefficiencies, and institutional misalignments. As tertiary healthcare hospitals navigate tightening budgets and rising demands, there’s a need for policy-backed, accountable, and facility-decentralized leasing models.

Recommendation

The decentralization efforts could be operationalized through Kenya’s ongoing health system financing reforms. The targeted reforms should include a review of the National Health Financing Strategy 2020–2030, a review of UHC policies under the new Social Health Authority (SHA), a review of the Kenya Health Public Private Collaboration Strategy 2020, and legislative amendments like the PPP ACT of 2021 and the Quality of Healthcare bill (currently before Parliament). Under these reform landscapes, this study is timely to provide evidence-based and provoke debate for incorporating the ALF mechanism into health policy and the legislative framework. Across the LMICs, the study provides perspective for health policies to move from viewing ALF as a stand-alone financial fix for healthcare to adopting it systematically within the complex and adaptive health ecosystem. This allows integration with planning, workforce development, and accountability reforms. However, cognizant of the system thinking on healthcare improvement, these recommendations may face barriers such as political interests and technological and economic constraints. Therefore, such factors ought to be systemically managed along with the recommended reforms to realize the statistical effect of leasing as a tool for healthcare quality improvement.
