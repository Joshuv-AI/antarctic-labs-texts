Association between lactate-to-albumin ratio and 28-day all-cause mortality in critically ill patients with chronic kidney disease: A retrospective cohort study from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV database
Authors: Lei Chen, Chenwei Lv, Haibin Ni, Xingxing Hu, Xiaomei Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe lactate-to-albumin ratio has been associated with adverse outcomes in various diseases. However, the impact of the lactate-to-albumin ratio on the prognosis of critically ill patients with chronic kidney disease remains unclear. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the predictive ability of the lactate-to-albumin ratio for 28-day all-cause mortality in critically ill patients with chronic kidney disease.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included 4271 critically ill patients with chronic kidney disease from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV database. The exposure factor was the lactate-to-albumin ratio, and the outcome was the 28-day mortality rate. Hazard ratios were computed along with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals using multivariate Cox regression.ResultsMultivariable Cox regression revealed that, compared with the Tertile 1 group, the hazard ratio for 28-day mortality was 1.55 (95% confidence interval: 1.26–1.92, p < 0.001) in the Tertile 2 group and 1.91 (95% confidence interval: 1.54–2.36, p < 0.001) in the Tertile 3 group. Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated that the higher lactate-to-albumin ratio group had higher 28-day, 90-day, and 1-year mortality rates (p < 0.001).ConclusionsIn our study population, the lactate-to-albumin ratio was independently associated with 28-day all-cause mortality, and this ratio may provide incremental prognostic value when combined with established severity of illness scores.Keywords: Lactate, albumin, chronic kidney disease, critically ill patient, Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV

Background

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a major global health challenge, with an estimated prevalence of 10%–16% worldwide, and substantially affects the quality of life of the affected individuals.1,2 Patients with CKD frequently experience compromised immune function, which may increase their susceptibility to sepsis. 3 Studies have shown that patients with CKD and lower estimated glomerular filtration rates (eGFRs) have a higher risk of infection than those with higher eGFR levels. 4 Consequently, these patients may be more likely to require intensive care unit (ICU) admission, and the coexistence of sepsis and persistent renal dysfunction in patients has been associated with an increased risk of mortality. 5 Furthermore, the coexistence of CKD with diabetic complications, such as diabetic foot disease, may confer an additive mortality risk, highlighting the need for accessible prognostic tools in this population. 6 Therefore, identifying indicators that can predict adverse outcomes in critically ill patients with CKD is clinically important to enable accurate diagnosis and effective treatment in these patients.

Blood lactate levels can significantly increase due to cellular ischemia and hypoxia caused by inadequate tissue perfusion.7,8 It is a reliable indicator for diagnosing circulatory shock and predicting organ dysfunction and mortality in critically ill patients.9–11 The 2021 Surviving Sepsis Campaign (SCC) guidelines also recommend dynamic monitoring of blood lactate levels to guide hemodynamic resuscitation. 12 Serum albumin is another commonly used biomarker for assessing disease severity. Hypoalbuminemia is associated with poor prognosis in various diseases; patients with CKD are also prone to hypoalbuminemia, which is correlated with increased mortality.13,14 However, prognostic assessment based on a single indicator, either lactate or albumin level, is associated with certain limitations. In recent years, scholars have proposed and studied the evaluation value of the lactate-to-albumin ratio (LAR), which may be associated with an increased risk of clinical prognosis deterioration in various diseases, including acute myocardial infarction, heart failure, septic shock, and ischemic stroke.15–18

Currently, studies investigating the prognostic value of LAR in critically ill patients with CKD are limited, and the optimal cutoff value for LAR remains debatable. Therefore, our study utilized the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) database to calculate the optimal cutoff value for LAR and investigated the predictive value of LAR in the prognosis of critically ill patients with CKD. The study objective was to provide evidence that would aid healthcare professionals in prompt patient assessment and offer a foundation for subsequent prognostic assessment.

Methods

Study participants

This retrospective cohort study used data from the MIMIC-IV database, which serves as a freely accessible critical care medical dataset. MIMIC-IV is a collaborative initiative involving the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT) Laboratory for Computational Physiology, Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (BIDMC), and Philips Healthcare. The dataset encompasses clinical data from hospitalized patients at the BIDMC between 2013 and 2019. The author of this article has been granted authorization (Record ID: 52921014) to access and use the database for this study. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki of 1975, as revised in 2024. All patient data in the MIMIC-IV database have been deidentified in accordance with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) Safe Harbor provision, and no individual patient can be identified from the data used in this study. In total, 4271 patients were included in the study. The study sample size was determined based on all eligible patients whose data were available in the MIMIC-IV database during the study period. Eligible participants met the following criteria: (a) age ≥18 years and (b) first-time admission to the ICU with a diagnosis of CKD. CKD was defined based on the presence of at least one International Classification of Diseases (ICD) code for CKD documented in the patient's medical history or hospital admission problem list at or before the index ICU admission (ICD-9-CM: 585.1–585.5, 585.9, 403.00, 403.10, 403.90, 404.00, 404.02, 404.10, 404.12, 404.90, and 404.92; ICD-10-CM: N18.1–N18.5, N18.9, I12.9, I13.0, and I13.10). Patients who lacked lactate or serum albumin data on the first day of ICU admission (n = 16,534), those who were discharged or died within 24 h (n = 126), and those with a documented history of chronic dialysis dependence prior to the ICU admission (n = 1974) were excluded from the analyses (Figure 1).

Data extraction

We utilized PostgreSQL (version 15.0) and Navicat Premium (version 16.0) software to extract variables from the MIMIC-IV database. Variables with ≥40% values missing were excluded from the analysis, whereas the remaining variables underwent k-nearest neighbor imputation to address the missing data. The extracted dataset encompasses the following categories: (a) demographic information on age, sex, weight, and ICU type; (b) vital signs, including heart rate, respiration rate (RR), systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), and blood oxygen saturation (SpO2); (c) laboratory indicators, including white blood cell (WBC) count, platelet count, international normalized ratio (INR), activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), prothrombin time (PT), pH, partial pressure of arterial oxygen (PaO2), partial pressure of arterial carbon dioxide (PaCO2), anion gap, and base excess (BE) as well as hemoglobin, albumin, hematocrit, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine, bicarbonate, blood glucose, lactate, sodium, potassium, calcium, and chlorine levels; (d) comorbidities, including respiratory failure, cirrhosis, heart failure, atrial fibrillation, coronary, malignancy, and sepsis; (e) severity of illness score, including Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score, Acute Physiology Score III (APS III), Oxford Acute Severity of Illness Score (OASIS), Simplified Acute Physiology Score II (SAPS II), Systemic Inflammatory Response Syndrome (SIRS) score, Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), and Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score; and (f) norepinephrine, vasopressin, and dopamine use as well as ventilation and continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT).19–22 The primary outcome was 28-day mortality. The LAR was calculated using the formula ((admission lactate (mmol/L))/serum albumin(g/dL)).

Statistical analyses

According to the tertiles of LAR, the study population was divided into three groups: (a) Tertile 1 group (LAR < 0.44); (b) Tertile 2 group (0.44 ≤ LAR < 0.79); and Tertile 3 group (LAR ≥ 0.79). Continuous variables with a normal distribution were represented as mean ± SD values, whereas non-normally distributed continuous variables were presented as median (interquartile range) values. Between-group comparisons were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or the Kruskal–Wallis test. Categorical variables were expressed as percentages and compared between groups using the χ2 test.

We established univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazard models to examine the correlation between LAR as a continuous variable and the LAR tertiles with clinical outcomes. The analysis included three models. Model 1 was adjusted for age, sex, and ICU type. Model 2, built upon Model 1, was further adjusted for heart rate, SBP, DBP, RR, temperature, SpO2, WBC count, platelet count, INR, APTT, PT, pH, PaO2, PaCO2, anion gap, BE, and eGFR as well as hemoglobin, creatinine, BUN, sodium, and bicarbonate levels. Model 3 was further adjusted for GCS score, SOFA score, OASIS, SAPS II, APS III, CCI, SIRS score, norepinephrine, vasopressin, dopamine, dobutamine, ventilation, CRRT, heart failure, respiratory failure, atrial fibrillation, coronary, malignancy, sepsis, and cirrhosis. We generated receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and calculated the area under the curve (AUC) to evaluate the predictive ability of lactate levels, albumin levels, LAR, and SOFA score for 28-day mortality. Simultaneously, the accuracy of predicting 28-day all-cause mortality was evaluated by adding the LAR to severity of illness scores (SOFA score, OASIS, APS III, SAPS II, SIRS score, and CCI). Kaplan–Meier (KM) curves were used to compare the survival probabilities among the three patient groups. Additionally, restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis was performed to determine the nonlinear relationship between LAR and 28-day mortality rate. Finally, subgroup analyses were performed to validate the impact of LAR on the 28-day mortality rate within specific participant subsets, categorized by age (≤65 and >65 years), SOFA score (≤8 and >8), eGFR (≤30 and >30 mL/min/1.73 m²), ventilation, norepinephrine, cirrhosis, heart failure, and sepsis. All statistical analyses were executed using R software (version 4.2.2), with the significance level set at p <0.05. The reporting of this study conforms to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines. 23

Results

Baseline characteristics

The median age of the 4271 patients included in the study was 70.0 (59.0, 80.0) years. Among these, 2597 (60.81%) were male, and 889 (20.8%) died within the first 28 days. Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of patients, stratified into three groups based on the LAR tertiles. The group with higher LARs exhibited significantly more severe clinical conditions. The Tertile 3 group exhibited higher heart rates, RRs, blood glucose levels, WBC counts, INRs, APTTs, PTs, anion gaps, and lactate levels than the Tertile 1 and Tertile 2 groups. In contrast, the Tertile 3 group had lower SBP and DBP values, temperature, platelet counts, pH, PaO2, PaCO2, and BE as well as albumin, sodium, calcium, and bicarbonate levels. Moreover, the Tertile 3 group had higher disease-severity scores and a higher likelihood of requiring mechanical ventilation, vasopressors, and CRRT. Patients with higher LARs exhibited significantly higher 28-day, 90-day, and 1-year mortality rates than those with lower LARs (all p < 0.001).

ROC curve analysis

The AUC for predicting the 28-day mortality rate in critically ill patients with CKD using the LAR was 0.679 (95% confidence interval (CI): 0.659–0.698), which was not inferior to that for the SOFA score (AUC: 0.691, 95% CI: 0.672–0.710) (Figure S1). Furthermore, compared with lactate levels (AUC: 0.654, 95% CI: 0.633–0.674) and albumin levels (AUC: 0.612, 95% CI: 0.591–0.633), LAR demonstrated higher accuracy in predicting the 28-day mortality rate (Figure 2).

ROC curves of LAR correlate for predicting 28-day mortality.

Additionally, we observed that the accuracy for predicting the 28-day mortality rate was higher when LAR was combined with various disease-severity scores for different diseases compared with when these scores were used individually (Table 2).

Results of univariate and multifactorial analyses of LAR and 28-day mortality

When considered as a continuous variable, LAR was an independent risk factor for adverse outcomes in patients with severe CKD. In addition, in Model 1, compared with the Tertile 1 group, the hazard ratios (HRs) for 28-day mortality were 2.09 (95% CI: 1.70–2.55, p < 0.001) in the Tertile 2 group and 3.80 (95% CI: 3.14–4.59, p < 0.001) in the Tertile 3 group. When confounding factors were adjusted in Model 2, the 28-day all-cause mortality rates remained elevated in the Tertile 2 and Tertile 3 groups compared with those in the Tertile 1 group (Tertile 2, HR: 1.93, 95% CI: 1.57–2.38, p < 0.001; Tertile 3, HR: 2.99, 95% CI: 2.45–3.67, p < 0.001). Model 3, which accounted for all confounding factors, revealed that the 28-day mortality risk was 1.55 times higher in the Tertile 2 group than in the Tertile 1 group (HR: 1.55, 95% CI: 1.26–1.92, p < 0.001) and 1.91 times higher in the Tertile 3 group (HR: 1.91, 95% CI: 1.54–2.36, p < 0.001). Similar findings were observed for 90-day and 1-year mortality rates (Table 3).

KM curve

We constructed a KM survival curve based on the LAR categories to illustrate the 28-day survival rate among patients. The high LAR group exhibited a significantly higher incidence of all-cause mortality at 28 days than the low LAR group (log-rank test: p < 0.001, Figure 3). Similar results were revealed by the KM analysis for 90-day and 1-year all-cause mortality rates.

Kaplan–Meier survival analysis curve for 28-day mortality.

Analyses of the nonlinear relationship

RCS analysis, which accounted for all factors in Model 3, revealed a nonlinear association between LAR and survival rate (p < 0.001). The inflection point of the RCS curve was identified at LAR of 0.6, signifying the critical point in the LAR–survival rate relationship (Figure 4). Using this inflection point, the data were split into two groups: LAR <0.6 and LAR ≥0.6. Subsequent segmented regression analyses were conducted for each group, revealing a significant positive correlation between the LAR and 28-day mortality rates in both groups (Table 4).

Discussion

This study included 4271 critically ill patients with CKD admitted to the ICU and found a significant correlation between LAR and 28-day mortality. The optimal cutoff value for LAR was determined to be 0.67. Even after adjusting for various variables, LAR remained an independent predictor of adverse outcomes. Our results suggest that LAR is a reliable predictor of 28-day mortality in patients with CKD and can enhance the predictive value of different disease scores.

Patients with severe CKD are commonly admitted to the ICU; however, there remains a lack of specific evaluation systems for early prediction and risk assessment for this population. Currently, commonly used ICU scoring systems such as the SOFA score reflect the severity of the patient's condition and organ dysfunction.24,25 However, these scoring systems involve many indicators, involve complex calculations, and can be cumbersome to apply, potentially leading to delayed assessment of patients’ conditions and increased mortality risk. 26 Therefore, there is a need for a simple and highly sensitive indicator to predict disease severity in this patient population. In the present study, the diagnostic efficacy of the LAR for prognosis assessment in critically ill patients with CKD was comparable with that of the SOFA score. Moreover, the predictive performance of LAR, when combined with disease-severity scores, was superior to that achieved using either lactate or albumin levels individually.

The metabolism of lactate primarily involves oxidation to pyruvate or conversion to acetyl-coenzyme A (acetyl-CoA), subsequently undergoing oxidative metabolism through the citric acid cycle. Approximately 30% of these processes occur in the kidneys, which rank second only to the liver in their contribution.27,28 Importantly, unlike the liver, the kidney's ability to clear lactate increases in the presence of acidosis. 29 Therefore, renal dysfunction may lead to impaired lactate clearance. The local accumulation of lactate in renal tissues can exacerbate kidney damage.30,31 Albumin plays a renal protective role by effectively maintaining colloid osmotic pressure, increasing circulating blood volume, renal blood flow, and ensuring renal perfusion. 32 Additionally, albumin helps maintain the integrity of the structure and function of proximal renal tubular cells through various signal transduction pathways.33,34 Patients with CKD often experience hypoalbuminemia due to excessive loss of albumin in urine, increased degradation of albumin in renal tubules, and inflammation-induced albumin leakage. 35 This also explains the adverse prognosis associated with decreased albumin levels. Previous studies have demonstrated that reduced serum albumin levels are a risk factor for all-cause mortality in patients with CKD. 13 However, lactate and albumin levels may be influenced by underlying diseases, nutritional status, and medications.36,37 Considering the specificity of the studied population, we believe that the use of single indicators is associated with certain limitations. Therefore, in this study, we used LAR for prognostic assessment. Ultimately, our research findings demonstrated that the efficacy of LAR for predicting clinical outcomes is significantly superior to that using lactate or albumin levels individually, aligning with previous studies.38,39

To the best of our knowledge, this is the one of the first studies that has focused on the relationship between LAR and the prognosis of critically ill patients with CKD. The critical value determined in this study is 0.67, similar to that reported by previous studies on patients with infectious diseases.40,41 However, the critical value for LAR is yet to be standardized, and results may vary across different study populations.39,42

Furthermore, we observed that LAR exhibited stronger predictive performance for outcomes in patients aged ≤65 years. This may be attributed to the reduced metabolic rate, organ function decline, changes in the immune system, and increased complications and medication use in older patients, ultimately resulting in less accurate measurements of lactate and albumin levels than in younger patients.43,44

This study has certain limitations. First, this was a retrospective investigation based on a database, inherently susceptible to drawbacks such as missing data and outliers, rendering it challenging to mitigate the inherent limitations of observational studies. Second, the lengthy duration of data collection from the MIMIC-IV database may have introduced inaccuracies in our results due to changes in the relevant guidelines. Third, the study cohort comprised a critically ill population at a singular medical center, lacking geographical diversity, which may impact the generalizability of the findings due to the unique characteristics of a single-center hospital. Fourth, we did not assess changes in LAR during the treatment process, which could have impacted our results. Fifth, patients with missing lactate or albumin level measurements on ICU day 1 were excluded. As the LAR could not be computed in these patients, a formal comparison was infeasible; however, the resulting enrichment for greater illness severity may limit the generalizability of absolute mortality estimates. Finally, although a multilevel strategy was applied to distinguish CKD from acute kidney injury (AKI), including ICD code-based exclusion, diagnosis-timing requirements, and pre-admission creatinine criteria, some misclassification cannot be excluded given the reliance on administrative coding. To minimize potential bias, we adjusted for multiple demographic variables, comorbidities, laboratory indicators, severity scores, and treatment variables in multivariable Cox regression models. Additionally, subgroup and RCS analyses were performed to assess the robustness of the findings.

Conclusions

LAR may be independently associated with 28-day all-cause mortality, and thereby provide incremental prognostic value when combined with established severity of illness scores.
