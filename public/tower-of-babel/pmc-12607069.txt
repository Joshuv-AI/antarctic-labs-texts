Awareness of gestational diabetes mellitus among pregnant women in the Mopani district of Limpopo Province in South Africa: a quantitative study
Authors: Elelwani Thelma Ntshauba, Eric Maimela, Thembelihle Sam Ntuli, Pamela Mamogobo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDespite the increasing burden of non-communicable diseases in developing countries, knowledge about gestational diabetes mellitus among pregnant women receiving antenatal care is insufficient. The aim of this study was to investigate the level of awareness and knowledge of Gestational Diabetes Mellitus (GDM) among pregnant women receiving antenatal care at local healthcare facilities in the mopani district of the Limpopo Province in South Africa.MethodsSimple random sampling was used to identify and select pregnant women receiving antenatal care during clinic visits. Health facilities in the district were selected using a multistage sampling technique: the facilities were initially clustered before being randomly sampled. A semi-structured questionnaire and data entry form were used to collect data. Collected data was subsequently analysed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 29.0.0.0.ResultsThe findings of this study show that most pregnant women (87.4%) were uninformed regarding GDM. Healthcare workers were found to be the most common source of health-related information for patients. Most pregnant women had inadequate knowledge about the predisposing factors associated with GDM, testing methods, treatment, and future health outcomes for both the mother and child. Health education on GDM was found to be limited to pregnant women who were diagnosed with the condition.ConclusionThe study revealed that awareness and knowledge about GDM among pregnant women receiving antenatal care at healthcare facilities insufficient.

Background

Non-communicable diseases in developing countries is increasing [1]. Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is defined as diabetes which is first detected during pregnancy [2]. GDM is usually diagnosed between 24 and 28 weeks of gestation [3], and is characterised by an increase in the maternal secretion of progesterone, leading to an increase in insulin secretion and hyperinsulinemia, resulting in the foetus requiring more glucose [4]. There are various factors which seem to influence the occurrence of gestational diabetes and these include obesity, a westernised diet, ethnicity, advanced maternal age, the intrauterine environment (low or high birth weight), a family history of diabetes, excessive gestational weight gain, polycystic ovarian syndrome (PCOS) and genetic polymorphisms [5].

With the GDM risk factors described above, this health condition can pose a significant health adverse outcomes which includes greater risk of developing Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (Type 2 DM) and cardiovascular diseases.⁶ Also, neonates born to mothers with GDM are susceptible to hypoglycaemia, birth trauma such as shoulder dystocia, brachial plexus injury, fractures of the clavicle and humerus, obesity, metabolic syndrome and Type 2 DM [5–7]. Studies focusing on health system barriers have shown that inadequate knowledge about GDM among pregnant women leads to sub-optimal understandings of medical information which, in turn, contributes to a deficiency in health literacy [8]. Health literacy refers to “the achievement of a level of knowledge, personal skills, and confidence to take action to improve personal and community health by changing personal lifestyles and living conditions [9].

In reference to previous studies, a study conducted in Saudi Arabia found that pregnant women had no GDM-related knowledge despite a high prevalence of GDM in the area [10]. Likewise, another study emphasised that age (25–29 years), higher educational level, employment status and a family history of diabetes were significant predictors of GDM knowledge among pregnant women receiving antenatal care in clinics [11]. Treatment non-adherence, and adverse pregnancy outcomes are associated with inadequate health education regarding GDM [12].

This research was part of a larger study aimed at developing an evidence-based management model for the mitigation of risk factors and improvement of maternal and infant health outcomes associated with GDM in the Mopani district of Limpopo Province. The first phase of the exploratory sequential mixed method study explored the perceptions of pregnant women regarding the management of GDM by healthcare providers working in the maternity unit. Nurses and doctors working in the maternity unit revealed that most pregnant women receiving antenatal care were uninformed regarding GDM.

In order to address the problem identified above, the rationale for conducting this study was the paucity of literature relating to awareness and knowledge of GDM among pregnant women in the Limpopo Province. Hence, the study thus aimed to determine the level of awareness and knowledge of GDM amongst pregnant women receiving antenatal care in healthcare facilities located in the Mopani district of the Limpopo province.

Methods

A cross-sectional design was used in this study as it allowed for multiple variables to be assessed simultaneously. The study took place in distinct levels of care in the Mopani district of the Limpopo Province in South Africa. The levels of care are inclusive of a regional hospital, a district hospital, a community health centre (CHC) and four primary healthcare clinics (PHCs). Firstly, a regional hospitals offer services at general specialist level, receive referrals from district hospital and serve as platforms for education training and research whilst a district hospital provides 24-h services including emergency care as well as inpatient and outpatient care.

To add unto the services rendered at district hospital; rehabilitation services, trauma counselling services and referrals to regional hospitals or tertiary hospitals which offer specialist and sub-specialist care for further management are offered. Secondly, CHCs provides 24-h comprehensive maternity care and emergency services, chronic disease management, care for minor ailments, as well as health promotion and prevention services. Also, patients in need of advanced care are referred from CHCs to district hospitals. Lastly, PHCs operate on weekdays during normal working hours and the services rendered include antenatal care, family planning, child health, chronic disease management and referrals to district hospitals for further management.

In this study, health facilities were selected using a multistage sampling technique consisting of three stages. In terms of stage one, the health facilities were clustered into four groups – a regional hospital, district hospitals, CHCs and PHCs which reduced costs and saved time. During stage two of sampling, district hospitals, CHCs and PHCs were further divided into smaller clusters by selecting CHCs and PHCs. It is important to note that there was no clustering of regional hospitals as there is only one regional hospital in the district. The third stage of sampling entailed using simple random sampling to select one district hospital, one CHC and four PHCs using a lottery method.

A total of 424 pregnant women receiving antenatal care at their healthcare facilities in selected study settings were randomly selected to participate in the study. A lottery/envelope method was used to select participants: patients were assigned numbers and those with the same number selected by the researcher from a bowl were approached to participate in the study. Of the 424 pregnant women, 386 agreed to participate in the study, which is a 91% response rate. The sample size for each healthcare facility was calculated by multiplying the total number of women in all facilities by the sample weight.

To calculate sample weight, Cochran’s formula for dichotomous variables, with Greater Giyani Area, Mopani District GDM prevalence 1.96% was used [13]. After obtaining the sample weight, sample size for each health facility was distributed proportionally to the number of pregnant women seen on a monthly basis. Only pregnant women receiving antenatal care at a selected healthcare facility and consented to participate were included in the study while women who were not pregnant and those with pre-existing diabetes were excluded.

After recruitment of participants, data collection took place between February and May 2023. Prior to data collection, the questionnaire was piloted to identify misleading and irrelevant questions. Due to the pilot study participants’ poor response rate, the questions had to be framed as closed-ended questions (Yes or No). Every Yes was scored 1 and every No was scored 0. Data was collected during antenatal clinic visits on Tuesdays at the regional hospital, Tuesdays and Wednesdays at the district hospital and Monday to Friday at the CHC and PHCs.

The researcher used a data entry form to capture information from participants’ maternity case record books as they were completing the questionnaire. Prior to data collection, prospective participants were provided with information about the study and given time to decide whether they wanted to participate in the study or not. Once a participant agrees to take part in the study, an informed consent form had to be completed.

At the end of the data collection process, questionnaires were filed in arched files for record-keeping and information was captured on Microsoft Excel spreadsheets. To understand data obtained from the participants, SPSS version 29.0.0.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA) was used as a data analysis software tool whereby descriptive analysis was performed to understand participants’ socio-demographic information and their level of awareness and knowledge regarding GDM. Thereafter, the results were presented as percentages and frequencies in tables and graph.

Results

Participants socio-demographics

Of the 386 pregnant women who agreed to participate in the study, most (79%) of the participants attended their 1st ANC visit after 8 weeks of gestation. The gestational age of approximately 44.3% of the participants was 24–34 weeks. Most of the participants (24.9%) were in the age group 30–34 years. More than half (53.4%) of the women were unmarried, while 73.8% had a secondary school education and 80.6% were unemployed. Less than half (41.7%) of the pregnant women were obese (> 30 kg/m2), and nearly a third of the women have been pregnant at least twice (26.2%), while 30.8% of the women have given birth to more than one child. A high proportion (81.6%) of the participants had never had a miscarriage before, and 82.6% had no family history of diabetes. In our study, two thirds of the participants (66%) did not consider family history as a risk factor for GDM, while 34% reported that diabetes is a familial health condition. Most participants (72.3%) reported that obesity was not a risk factor associated with GDM, with only 27.7% reporting obesity as a risk factor. Three quarters of the participants (75.4%) reported that previous history of GDM was not a risk factor for GDM, compared to a quarter of participants (24.6%) who agreed that GDM could reoccur in subsequent pregnancies. When participants were asked if rapid weight gain (RWG) during pregnancy to be a risk factor associated with GDM, 78.2% of the participants disagreed, while 21.8% agreed. When participants were asked if obesity before pregnancy was a risk factor associated with GDM, the majority (72.3%) disagreed, while only 27.7% agreed that it was.

Discussion

A quantitative study approach was employed to investigate pregnant women’s awareness and knowledge about GDM. Our study found that most pregnant women receiving ANC were not aware of GDM and its associated predisposing factors. Interestingly, GDM was rarely focused on during antenatal care visits as it was described as a rare health condition with few cases being identified by nurses, doctors, and child and maternal health district managers. In similar studies such as the study conducted at Kawempe National Referral Hospital in Uganda, it was found that a third (31%) of pregnant women were informed about GDM, its risk factors and adverse health outcomes [14]. In another study, it was found that a small proportion of pregnant women were aware of GDM.

In our study, the knowledge of GDM among pregnant women was inadequate as small proportion of pregnant women knew about this health condition [15]. Concerning the knowledge of GDM, a study conducted in Western Türkiye found that the knowledge of GDM amongst pregnant women was extremely poor [16].

However, the abovementioned study findings did not resonate with other studies. In Idia, a cross-sectional study conducted at SRM Medical College Hospital and Research Centre found that 51.5% of pregnant women had good knowledge regarding GDM, followed by 34% with fair knowledge and only 14.5% pregnant women had poor knowledge [17]. Similarly, a descriptive cross-sectional study conducted in Dharan, Nepal, 40% of pregnant women diagnosed with GDM had knowledge and awareness about this condition [18].

In our study, most of the participants who were aware of GDM had heard about it from healthcare workers. The participants had received basic information about what the meaning of GDM, the causes and self-care management. Emphasis was placed on living a healthy lifestyle and adhering to medication. Contrary to our study finding, a study conducted in Nigeria found that pregnant women who knew about GDM did not receive information from health workers, but from friends and family [19, 20].

The lack of awareness about GDM amongst pregnant women may be due to the study setting (urban/semi-urban, rural), the educational level and occupation of participants, and the inaccessibility of ANC educational programs [17, 18]. Our study has noted health system barriers which influenced pregnant women’s knowledge and awareness of GDM. That is, health education on GDM was only provided to pregnant women who were diagnosed with GDM. In this study, we found that factors which contributed to low levels of GDM health education during antenatal care were time constraints, many patients to be seen, understaffing, and limited physical space. Moreover, a qualitative study which concurs with our study revealed that long waiting times, understaffing and short consultation times prevented in-depth health education [21]. Likewise, a qualitative study conducted in public hospitals of South India found that delayed visits to hospitals, long waiting periods, unavailability of resources, lack of standardised protocols, poor follow-up visits, inadequate supportive oversight and shortage of staff were some of the barriers to screening and management of GDM [22]. Crowded clinics and lack of privacy also influenced the effectiveness of some of the services provided [23].

It is not surprising that most pregnant women receiving antenatal care in healthcare facilities are not aware of GDM. If GDM was included in ANC health educational programs for all pregnant women and not exclusively for those diagnosed with GDM, then the level of awareness and knowledge would likely increase. To reduce the risk of GDM. every pregnant woman should be informed about GDM and should receive educational materials on GDM highlighting signs and symptoms, consultations with a doctor, treatment including self-care management strategies and preventative strategies. To reinforce such efforts, posters – or similar educational materials – could be placed in areas which are visible to all patients and visitors in all healthcare facilities. Media platforms such as radio can also assist in educating the public. Moreover, these strategies will assist women to be aware of GDM and be cognisant of their lifestyle as the prevalence of GDM in developing countries is increasing [1].

This study had limitations. Data was collected during ANC visits in healthcare facilities while patients were waiting to be seen by the healthcare professionals. In addition to this; noisy environment, prolonged waiting times and elevated temperature might have caused participants to complete the questionnaire in a hurry to avoid missing their appointment.

Conclusions

The aim of this study was to determine the level of awareness and knowledge regarding GDM amongst pregnant women receiving antenatal care in healthcare facilities situated in mopani district of Limpopo Province. Our study revealed that awareness and knowledge pertaining to GDM was insufficient due to highlighted health systems barriers identified in this study. In addition, it is important to note that only pregnant women diagnosed with GDM were provided with health education. GDM causes long-term morbidities for both mother and child which are likely to affect long-term health and well-being – so it is thus logical to consider a broader preventative approach. As a first step towards increasing preventative measures, information regarding GDM needs to be made accessible to all women in communities, including pregnant women receiving antenatal care during clinic visits. Moreover, this can raise awareness regarding healthy nutrition during pregnancy and enhance the women’s knowledge on health conditions which can occur during pregnancy. In addition, this can assist in addressing the issue of undiagnosed or late diagnosis cases of GDM as the women will now be able to identify clinical symptoms of GDM they might be experiencing during pregnancy, which can assist the nurses/doctors to thoroughly investigate, then diagnose and manage promptly.

The findings of our study suggest a need for collaboration by different stakeholders to improve the preventative measures to curb GDM because the continued increase in non-communicable diseases will likely have a devastating impact on South Africa’s health and economic status. Healthcare workers need to receive further training on GDM to enhance their knowledge and skills, as they play a vital role in raising awareness about GDM to pregnant women receiving ANC. Future studies should examine the factors contributing to limited awareness and knowledge regarding GDM among pregnant women. Furthermore, correlation between knowledge of GDM and contributory factors should be focused on to determine the association.
