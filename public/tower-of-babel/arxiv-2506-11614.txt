Abstract
. Delta debugging assumes search space monotonicity: if a program causes a failure, any supersets of that program will also induce the same failure, permitting the exclusion of subsets of non-failure-inducing programs. However, this assumption does not always hold in practice. This paper introduces Probabilistic Monotonicity Assessment ( PMA ), enhancing the efficiency of ddmin -style algorithms without sacrificing effectiveness. PMA dynamically models and assesses the search space’s monotonicity based on prior tests tried during the debugging process and uses a confidence function to quantify monotonicity, thereby enabling the probabilistic exclusion of subsets of non-failure-inducing programs. Our approach significantly reduces redundant tests that would otherwise be performed, without compromising the quality of the reduction. We evaluated PMA against two leading ddmin -style tools, CHISEL and ProbDD . Our findings indicate that PMA cuts processing time by 59.2% compared to CHISEL , accelerates the reduction process (i.e., the number of tokens deleted per second) by 3.32 × 3.32\times , and decreases the sizes of the final reduced programs by 6.7%. Against ProbDD , PMA reduces processing time by 22.0%, achieves a 1.34 × 1.34\times speedup in the reduction process, and further decreases the sizes of the final reduced programs by 3.0%. These findings affirm PMA ’s role in significantly improving delta debugging’s efficiency while maintaining or enhancing its effectiveness.



1.
Introduction

The scale and complexity of software have rapidly increased, raising the risk of severe bugs. For example, an update from CrowdStrike on July 19, 2024 caused 8.5 million Windows devices to crash, resulting in over one billion dollars in losses
(
Weston 2024
)
. This underscores the critical need for efficient bug identification. Manual bug identification in large codebases is time-consuming
(
Yang et al. 2011
)
, with software maintenance consuming nearly 70% of development resources, primarily for bug localization and repair
(
Ogheneovo et al. 2014
)
.

Delta debugging

(
Zeller 1999
)
was introduced to minimize failure-inducing programs while maintaining the failure. This approach has been widely applied across various software engineering domains, including compiler testing
(
Yang et al. 2011
;
Le et al. 2014
;
Sun et al. 2016
;
Chen et al. 2020
;
Donaldson et al. 2021
)
, SMT solver testing
(
Winterer et al. 2020
;
Yao et al. 2021
)
, fault localization
(
Cleve and Zeller 2005
;
Zeller 2002
)
, and software debloating
(
Zhang et al. 2022
;
Heo et al. 2018
)
.

Formally, delta debugging is defined as follows: Let
S
be a program, represented as a set of elements or tokens. Let
\mathbb{S}=2^{S}
denote the search space, which includes all subsets of
S
, referred to as test cases
S^{\prime}
. The property test function
\psi:\mathbb{S}\to\{\textbf{true},\textbf{false}\}
evaluates each test case
S^{\prime}
,
returning
true
if
S^{\prime}
maintains the failure-inducing property and
false
otherwise. Given
\psi(S)=\textbf{true}
, delta debugging aims to efficiently find the smallest subset
S^{*}\in\mathbb{S}
that still maintains this property (
\psi(S^{*})=\textbf{true}
), thus minimizing the object’s size while preserving the failure-inducing property.

In compiler testing, failure-inducing test cases are often large. Both GCC and LLVM recommend test case reduction in bug reports
(
GCC Wiki 2024
;
LLVM 2024
)
. Delta debugging generates minimal test programs that reproduce the bug, where
\mathbb{S}
represents program variants,
S
is a bug-triggering variant, and
\psi
verifies the bug’s reproducibility.

Delta debugging
(
Zeller 1999
)
assumes search space monotonicity: if a program induces failure
\psi
, any superset satisfies
\psi
; if not, no subset satisfies
\psi
. However, this assumption may not hold in practice.

Problem Statement.

We improve delta debugging—specifically
ddmin
-style algorithms
(
Zeller and Hildebrandt 2002
)
—by dynamically evaluating search space monotonicity to eliminate non-failure-inducing test cases.

Prior Work.

In delta debugging, the foundational
ddmin
algorithm
(
Zeller and Hildebrandt 2002
)
employs a divide-and-conquer approach to minimize failure-inducing programs. Refinements of
ddmin
focus on reducing redundancy by minimizing non-failure-inducing tests, enhancing efficiency. Early efforts leveraged outcome caching and strict monotonicity assumptions to reduce redundancy
(
Zeller 2009
)
. Later methods like HDD
(
Misherghi and Su 2006
)
and Perses
(
Sun et al. 2018
)
aimed to generate only syntactically valid tests but often produced semantically invalid, redundant cases. Recent strategies leverage historical test data to improve effectiveness. For instance,
CHISEL

(
Heo et al. 2018
)
uses reinforcement learning to prioritize test cases likely to pass the property test
\psi
, while ProbDD
(
Wang et al. 2021
)
probabilistically generates test cases based on element success likelihood. However, these approaches may not fully exploit the broader search space, potentially overlooking further opportunities to refine delta debugging.

This Work.

A key challenge in delta debugging is efficiently leveraging historical test data to avoid non-failure-inducing test cases. Our insight is that search space monotonicity enables eliminating such subsets. We propose
Probabilistic Monotonicity Assessment
(
PMA
), which integrates with
ddmin
-style algorithms to boost efficiency without sacrificing effectiveness. Unlike traditional methods (
CHISEL

(
Heo et al. 2018
)
,
ProbDD

(
Wang et al. 2021
)
),
PMA
dynamically models and assesses search space monotonicity, using a confidence function to probabilistically exclude non-failure-inducing cases and uncover patterns missed by prior work.

Evaluation.

We evaluated
PMA
’s effectiveness through integration with
CHISEL

(
Heo et al. 2018
)
, comparing against both
CHISEL
and
ProbDD

(
Wang et al. 2021
)
(the latter implemented within
CHISEL
). Our comprehensive benchmarks demonstrate that
PMA
achieves a 59.2% reduction in processing time, a
3.32\times
improvement in token deletion rate, and yields 6.7% smaller final programs on average compared to
CHISEL
. When compared to
ProbDD
,
PMA
reduces processing time by 22.0%, improves reduction speed by
1.34\times
, and further decreases program size by 3.0%. These results establish
PMA
’s superior performance in enhancing delta debugging efficiency while maintaining reduction quality. The findings validate the benefits of probabilistic search space analysis and suggest promising applications in program reduction and bug localization.

Contributions.

We make the following major contributions:

•

We introduce
Probabilistic Monotonicity Assessment
(
PMA
), a novel approach that enhances delta debugging efficiency through probabilistic modeling of search space monotonicity;

•

We establish that probabilistic analysis of search space properties (particularly monotonicity) advances delta debugging methodology, opening new research directions in probabilistic program reduction and bug localization; and

•

We empirically demonstrate
PMA
’s superiority over state-of-the-art techniques through comprehensive evaluation.

2.1.
Delta Debugging

Delta debugging relies on three core assumptions about the programs being reduced: monotonicity, unambiguity and consistency
(
Zeller 1999
;
Zeller and Hildebrandt 2002
)
. We utilize the property test function
\psi:\mathbb{S}\to\{\textbf{true},\textbf{false}\}
, where
\mathbb{S}=2^{S}
represents the search space for a program
S
—composed of elements or tokens—that satisfies
\psi(S)=\textbf{true}
(
Section

1
).

Assumption 1 (Monotonicity).

A search space
\mathbb{S}
is
monotone
if, for all
S,S^{\prime}\in\mathbb{S}
such that
S\supseteq S^{\prime}
, then
\psi(S)=\textbf{false}\implies\psi(S^{\prime})=\textbf{false}
, or equivalently,
\psi(S^{\prime})=\textbf{true}\implies\psi(S)=\textbf{true}
.

According to the monotonicity of the search space, any subset of a non-failure-inducing test case will not induce failure, ensuring consistent propagation of the failure property from the test case to all its subsets within the search space
\mathbb{S}
.
Conversely, any superset of a failure-inducing test case will also induce the same failure.

Assumption 2 (Unambiguity).

A search space
\mathbb{S}
is
unambiguous
if, for any
S_{1},S_{2}\in\mathbb{S}
where both
\psi(S_{1})=\textbf{true}
and
\psi(S_{2})=\textbf{true}
, it follows that
\psi(S_{1}\cap S_{2})=\textbf{true}
.

Thus, a failure is caused by a specific subset rather than independently by two disjoint subsets.

Assumption 3 (Consistency).

A search space
\mathbb{S}
is
consistent
if, for any
S\in\mathbb{S}
,
\psi(S)\in\{\textbf{true},\textbf{false}\}
.

Despite recent advances in delta debugging aimed at testing only consistent sequences from a given input program
(
Misherghi and Su 2006
;
Sun et al. 2018
;
Regehr et al. 2012
)
, some test cases may still yield inconsistent results due to factors such as syntactic or semantic errors. We examine these effects on
PMA
and discuss potential improvements in
Section

4
.

In an ideal debugging environment where monotonicity, unambiguity and consistency prevail, if a failure-inducing program
S
satisfies
\psi(S)=\text{true}
, we can always pinpoint the smallest program
S^{*}
in
\mathbb{S}
that maintains the failure-inducing property by solving the following optimization problem:

(1)

S^{*}=\underset{S\in\mathbb{S}}{\text{argmin}}\left|S\right|\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\psi(S)=\textbf{true}

In practice, however, these two assumptions may not hold, making the problem of finding a global optimal solution NP-complete
(
Zeller 2002
)
. Consequently, the focus of delta debugging shifts toward identifying a locally optimal solution, typically characterized by
1-minimality

(
Zeller 2002
)
. A test case
S^{*}
is considered
1-minimal
if removing any single element results in a variant
S^{\prime}
that fails the property test, ensuring that
S^{*}
is the smallest subset that maintains the desired property.

2.2.
The
ddmin
Algorithm

The
ddmin
algorithm
(
Zeller and Hildebrandt 2002
)
isolates the minimal failure-inducing subset of an input program
S
through a systematic process that iteratively partitions and tests its subsets. This approach, grounded in Assumptions
1
and
2
, comprises the following three main steps:

:

Step 1: Initial Partitioning.

Initially set
n=2
. Divide
S
into
n
partitions:
U=\{u_{1},u_{2},\dots,u_{n}\}
.
Test each partition: for each
i
in
[1,n]
, check if
\psi(u_{i})=\textbf{true}
.
If any partition
u_{i}
satisfies
\psi(u_{i})=\textbf{true}
, update
S\leftarrow u_{i}
and restart at
Step 1
.

:

Step 2: Complement Testing.

If no partition passes the test (
\forall\ i:\psi(u_{i})=\textbf{false}
), evaluate the complements: for each
i
in
[1,n]
, test
\psi(\overline{u_{i}})=\psi(S\setminus u_{i})
.
If a complement
\overline{u_{i}}
passes (
\psi(\overline{u_{i}})=\textbf{true}
), update
S\leftarrow\overline{u_{i}}
, reduce granularity
n\leftarrow\max(n-1,2)
, and restart at
Step 1
.

:

Step 3: Granularity Adjustment.

If all tests in
Steps 1
and
2
fail and
n<|S|
, increase granularity:
n\leftarrow\min(2n,|S|)
and return to
Step 1
.
If
n\geq|S|
, terminate the process and return
S
as the minimal failure-inducing subset.

3.1.
The Probabilistic Model

PMA
is designed to enhance a
ddmin
-style algorithm by eliminating redundant tests using a simple yet effective probabilistic model, as shown in
Figure

3
. While the
ddmin
-style algorithm operates normally, it integrates with
PMA
, which determines whether to skip or execute each newly generated test case.
PMA
intervenes both after a test case
T
has been executed, to update the model, and just before its execution, to apply the model, as detailed below:

•

Model Update (
Section

3.1.1
).
After each test
T
is executed,
PMA
updates the search space’s monotonicity by first assessing
\textsc{monoAssr}(T)
(line A1). It then updates
\mathcal{M}
using
\mathcal{M}\leftarrow\textsc{MonoUpdate}(T)
, which quantifies the net difference between observed monotonicity compliances and violations from previous tests (line A2). This updated measure refines the confidence function,
\mbox{$\mathfrak{C}$}(\mathcal{M})
, evaluating current monotonicity (line A3).

•

Model Application (
Section

3.1.2
).
Before executing each test
T
,
PMA
evaluates the search space’s monotonicity through
\textsc{SkipEnabled}(T)
outcomes (line B1) to determine if
T
can be classified as redundant.
Additionally,
\textsc{SkipAllowed}(\mathcal{M})
is assessed using the confidence function
\mbox{$\mathfrak{C}$}(\mathcal{M})
to probabilistically decide if
T
should be skipped (line B2).
PMA
will only skip
T
if both
\textsc{SkipEnabled}(T)
and
\textsc{SkipAllowed}(\mathcal{M})
concur on this decision (lines B3-B6).

Note that the input program
S
, to be reduced, is initially treated as a test case with
\psi(S)=\textbf{true}
.

3.1.1.
Model Update

The essence of our probabilistic model lies in capturing the search space’s monotonicity in a
ddmin
-style algorithm. Traditionally viewed as a binary property in formal logic
(
Nielson et al. 2015
)
—where a search space is either monotone or not—our approach introduces a probabilistic interpretation to handle real-world software system complexities more effectively.

In practice, software systems often display non-deterministic behavior due to factors like concurrency, environmental variables, or hidden dependencies. Furthermore, test results may be affected by noise or intermittent failures, obscuring the true nature of the search space. Consequently, a strictly binary view of monotonicity can result in premature or overly conservative conclusions.

To address these challenges, we define monotonicity
M
in probabilistic terms:

(2)

P(M\mid\mathcal{E})=\mbox{$\mathfrak{C}$}(\mathcal{M})

where
P(M\mid\mathcal{E})
represents the probability of monotonicity given the current evidence
\mathcal{E}
(captured by historical test data). This probability is estimated using our confidence function
\mbox{$\mathfrak{C}$}(\mathcal{M})
, with
\mathcal{M}
quantifying the net difference between monotonicity compliances and violations observed.

When assessing monotonicity, we avoid setting
P(M\mid\mathcal{E})
to binary extremes like one or zero. Instead, by adjusting
\mathcal{M}
and
\mbox{$\mathfrak{C}$}(\mathcal{M})
, we gradually modulate the probability. This continuous approach integrates new evidence smoothly and aligns with the formal definition of monotonicity (Assumption
1
), offering a practical way to handle uncertainties in software debugging.

Below, we detail how
PMA
updates its probabilistic model after executing each test case
T
generated by the
ddmin
-style algorithm. This includes assessing
\textsc{monoAssr}(T)
,
updating “
\mathcal{M}\leftarrow\textsc{MonoUpdate}(T)
” and
recalibrating

\mbox{$\mathfrak{C}$}(\mathcal{M})
, as illustrated in
Figure

3
(lines A1-A3).

Assessing
\textsc{monoAssr}(T)
.

We focus on assessing the monotonicity of the search space by concentrating on failure-inducing test cases, which are fewer than non-failure-inducing ones, thus minimizing computational overhead. We define
\textsc{SupSet}(S)
as the set of test cases that are supersets of
T
and were executed before
T
. The evaluation of
\textsc{monoAssr}(T)
proceeds as follows:

(3)

\hskip-7.5pt\textsc{monoAssr}(T)=\begin{cases}\begin{cases}\textbf{false}&\exists\ T^{\prime}\in\textsc{SupSet}(T):\\
&\quad\psi(T^{\prime})=\textbf{false}\\
\textbf{true}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}&\psi(T)=\textbf{true}\\
\top&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

Under Assumption
1
, a new failure-inducing test case
T
, where
\psi(T)=\textbf{true}
, preserves the search space’s monotonicity if

\textsc{monoAssr}(T)=\textbf{true}
and violates it if
\textsc{monoAssr}(T)=\textbf{false}
.

Computing
\mathcal{M}\leftarrow\textsc{MonoUpdate}(T)
.

\mathcal{M}
, which quantifies the net difference between monotonicity compliances and violations, is initialized to zero when the
ddmin
-style algorithm begins. After each failure-inducing test case, we use
\textsc{monoAssr}(S)
to determine if the test outcome conforms to the expected monotonicity, and we update
\mathcal{M}
accordingly:

(4)

\mathcal{M}\leftarrow\textsc{MonoUpdate}(T)=\begin{cases}\mathcal{M}+1&\textsc{monoAssr}(T)=\textbf{true}\\
\mathcal{M}-1&\textsc{monoAssr}(T)=\textbf{false}\\
\mathcal{M}&\textsc{monoAssr}(T)=\top\end{cases}

By the definition of
\textsc{monoAssr}(T)
,

\mathcal{M}
remains unchanged if
T
is a non-failure-inducing test case.

Under Assumption
1
, if a test case does not induce a failure, its non-failure-inducing property should consistently propagate to all its subsets. When
T
emerges as a new failure-inducing test case, we evaluate monotonicity compliance: if
\textsc{monoAssr}(T)=\textbf{true}
, signaling compliance, we increase
\mathcal{M}
by one. If
\textsc{monoAssr}(T)=\textbf{false}
, indicating a violation, we decrease
\mathcal{M}
by one.

Recalibrating
\mbox{$\mathfrak{C}$}(\mathcal{M})
.

We use this confidence function to gauge the search space’s monotonicity:

(5)

\mbox{$\mathfrak{C}$}(\mathcal{M})=\frac{1}{1+e^{-\mathcal{M}}}

When
PMA
starts,
\mathcal{M}=0
, setting
\mbox{$\mathfrak{C}$}(\mathcal{M})=0.5
, indicating neutral confidence in the search space’s monotonicity.
As each new test case is executed,
\mbox{$\mathfrak{C}$}(\mathcal{M})
is recalibrated based on the updated
\mathcal{M}
, adapting the confidence level to observed monotonicity.

Our confidence function,
\mbox{$\mathfrak{C}$}(\mathcal{M})
, is grounded in the Logistic Growth Model
(
Tsoularis and Wallace 2002
)
, which we have adapted to quantify our belief in the search space’s monotonicity during the debugging process. This function offers several desirable properties:

(1)

Bounded Output:

\mbox{$\mathfrak{C}$}(\mathcal{M})\in(0,1)
.

(2)

Monotonicity:

\frac{d\mbox{$\mathfrak{C}$}}{d\mathcal{M}}>0
.

(3)

Symmetry:

\mbox{$\mathfrak{C}$}(x)=1-\mbox{$\mathfrak{C}$}(-x)
.

(4)

Convergence:

\lim_{\mathcal{M}\to\infty}\mbox{$\mathfrak{C}$}(\mathcal{M})=1
and
\lim_{\mathcal{M}\to-\infty}\mbox{$\mathfrak{C}$}(\mathcal{M})\newline
=0
, illustrating the asymptotic behavior of

\mbox{$\mathfrak{C}$}(\mathcal{M})
.

3.1.2.
Model Application

The confidence function
\mbox{$\mathfrak{C}$}(\mathcal{M})
is central to our debugging approach, quantifying the search space’s monotonicity and enabling
PMA
efficiently to skip redundant tests.

Below, we detail how
PMA
applies its probabilistic model just before executing each test case
T
generated by the
ddmin
-style algorithm. This involves evaluating
\textsc{SkipEnabled}(T)
and
\textsc{SkipAllowed}(\mathcal{M})
to decide if the test should be skipped, as depicted in
Figure

3
(lines B1-B6).

Evaluating
\textsc{SkipEnabled}(T)
.

To decide if test case
T
can be skipped, we assess whether its execution could violate monotonicity. We continue to write
\textsc{SupSet}(S)
to represent the set of test cases that are supersets of
T
and were previously executed. The criterion for
\textsc{SkipEnabled}(T)
is:

(6)

\hskip-9.44pt\textsc{SkipEnabled}(T)=\begin{cases}\textbf{true}&\exists\ T^{\prime}\in\textsc{SupSet}(T):\psi(T^{\prime})=\textbf{false}\\
\textbf{false}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

If
\textsc{SkipEnabled}(T)
returns true, it indicates that a previously executed superset of
T
was non-failure-inducing. By Assumption
1
,
T
can then be eliminated as redundant. Otherwise,
T
can proceed without violating monotonicity.

Note that some regenerated non-failure-inducing test cases, marked by
\texttt{F}^{*}
in
Figure

2
, can lead to unnecessary overhead if avoided entirely. However, since each test case inherently includes itself, such redundancies are probabilistically eliminated.
PMA
’s effectiveness in reducing redundant tests
including these as a special case is demonstrated in our evaluation (
Section

4.3
).

Evaluating
\textsc{SkipAllowed}(\mathcal{M})
.

If
\textsc{SkipEnabled}(T)=\textbf{true}
, there is a potential to skip
T
as a redundant, non-failure-inducing test. The final decision depends on evaluating
\textsc{SkipAllowed}(\mathcal{M})
, as outlined in
Figure

3
(line B2) and reproduced below, guided by our confidence function
\mbox{$\mathfrak{C}$}(\mathcal{M})
defined as follows:

(7)

\textsc{SkipAllowed}(\mathcal{M})=\begin{cases}\textbf{true}&\mbox{$\mathfrak{C}$}(\mathcal{M})>\mathcal{U}(0,1)\\
\textbf{false}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

The test case
T
will be skipped if
\textsc{SkipAllowed}(\mathcal{M})
agrees (positively) with
\textsc{SkipEnabled}(T)
as outlined in lines B3-B6 of
Figure

3
; otherwise, it will be executed. Here,
\mathcal{U}(0,1)
represents a random draw from a uniform distribution over the interval (0, 1), ensuring impartial decisions.
The model uses
\mbox{$\mathfrak{C}$}(\mathcal{M})
as a dynamic threshold integrating historical test data. A high
\mbox{$\mathfrak{C}$}(\mathcal{M})
value enables strategic omission of certain subsets of a non-failure-inducing test case, while a low value necessitates comprehensive testing.

3.1.3.
Discussion

Leveraging feedback from the confidence function
\mbox{$\mathfrak{C}$}(\mathcal{M})
about search space monotonicity,
PMA
optimizes a
ddmin
-style algorithm with an adaptive testing strategy:

(1)

Iterative Updates:
Each failure-inducing test updates
\mathcal{M}
, continuously adjusting
\mbox{$\mathfrak{C}$}(\mathcal{M})
to refine the testing strategy based on evolving insights into the search space.

(2)

Selective Testing:
Tests are chosen based on their potential impact on the efficiency and effectiveness of
the
ddmin
-style algorithm and the prevailing confidence level, prioritizing those likely to provide significant insights or addressing the most significant uncertainties.

By leveraging
\mbox{$\mathfrak{C}$}(\mathcal{M})
,our approach boosts the debugging process’s efficiency and focuses efforts where they are most needed, guided by a probabilistic understanding of the search space’s monotonicity.
This strategy minimizes unnecessary tests while ensuring effective identification of minimal failure-inducing test cases.

3.2.
Theoretical Justification

We provide a brief theoretical justification for our
PMA
approach, which can be seen as a special case of two established theoretical models discussed below.

3.2.1.
Reliability of Confidence Estimation

The
Law of Large Numbers (LLN)

(
Révész 2014
)
ensures sample averages converge to the expected value, providing a theoretical foundation for analyzing random variables.

Theorem 3.1 (LLN
(
Révész 2014
)
).

Let
X_{1},X_{2},...,X_{n}
be independent and identically distributed Bernoulli random variables with an expected value
\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]=\mu
. Then for any
\epsilon>0
, we have:

\lim_{n\to\infty}P\left(\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}-\mu\right|<\epsilon\right)=1

Applying
\textsc{MonoUpdate}(T)
updates
\mathcal{M}
when executing the
i
-th failure-inducing test case (
\psi(T)=\textbf{true}
), where the event
X_{i}
is 1 for compliance and 0 for violation. Here,
\mu
denotes the probability of monotonicity in the search space. As
n
increases, the empirical average
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
converges to
\mu
, ensuring that
PMA
’s estimates increasingly reflect the true probability of monotonicity. As
n\to\infty
, with
\mathcal{M}\approx n\mu
, the confidence function
\mbox{$\mathfrak{C}$}(\mathcal{M})
translates accumulated monotonicity evidence into a probability measure.

3.2.2.
Effectiveness of Redundancy Elimination

PMA
’s effectiveness in skipping redundant tests at high
\mbox{$\mathfrak{C}$}(\mathcal{M})
values is underpinned by information gain, leveraging Shannon entropy—a metric that measures uncertainty in information systems
(
Shannon 1948
)
.

Theorem 3.2 (Information Gain
(
Shannon 1948
)
).

Information gain
IG(X;Y)
from observing a random variable
Y
about another variable
X
is defined as follows:

IG(X;Y)=H(X)-H(X|Y)

where
H
represents Shannon entropy, which quantifies the level of uncertainty or expected information content in
X
both when considered independently and in the context of knowing the value of
Y
.

Here,
IG(X;Y)
measure quantifies how observing
Y
reduces uncertainty or boosts knowledge about
X
, thus enhancing our understanding relative to not knowing
Y
.

In our
PMA
approach,
\mathcal{M}
represents the net difference between monotonicity compliance and violation. Here,
X
denotes the inherent monotonicity of the search space, and
Y
represents the outcome of an executed test case, classified as failure-inducing or not by
\psi
.
As
|\mathcal{M}|
increases, reflecting more executed tests and enhanced confidence through
\mbox{$\mathfrak{C}$}(\mathcal{M})
,
H(X)
decreases due to reduced uncertainty about monotonicity. However,
H(X|Y)
—the entropy of
X
given a new test outcome
Y
—remains close to
H(X)
, suggesting that additional tests contribute little new information. Consequently, when
|\mathcal{M}|
is large, the following relationship holds:

IG(X;Y)=H(X)-H(X|Y)\approx 0

This minimal information gain justifies skipping redundant tests at high
\mbox{$\mathfrak{C}$}(\mathcal{M})
levels, as further testing offers minimal additional insight in identifying the minimal failure-inducing test.

(

)

(

)

4.1.
Methodology

We demonstrate the superiority of
PMA
over two leading baselines:
CHISEL
and
ProbDD
.
CHISEL

(
Heo et al. 2018
)
, a well-established program reduction tool for C programs, employs
ddmin
to generate test cases and uses reinforcement learning to prioritize those likely to pass the property test
\psi
(i.e., reproduce the failure considered).
ProbDD

(
Wang et al. 2021
)
, a recent advancement, enhances
CHISEL
by substituting
ddmin
with a probabilistic
algorithm that increases each element’s likelihood of successfully passing the property test
\psi
.

We use the original open-source implementations of both
CHISEL
and
ProbDD
, with
ProbDD
adapted and embedded into
CHISEL
’s framework. For
PMA
, we have integrated it with
CHISEL
to skip redundant test cases and avoid unnecessary executions. Additionally, we will discuss why directly integrating
PMA
with
ProbDD
does not result in significant benefits (
Section

4.4
).

This tripartite comparison allows us to comprehensively evaluate
PMA
’s advantages against leading representatives of both traditional and recent improvements in program reduction.

To ensure a fair and consistent evaluation of these three
tools, we disabled features like Dead Code Elimination (DCE) and Dependency Analysis (DA). This decision aligns with findings from previous studies
(
Wang et al. 2021
)
, which showed that these features could skew program reduction outcomes. Notably, DCE, while effective in debloating, can misfire in scenarios with compiler bugs triggered by unreachable code. Similarly, DA has been observed to produce erroneous results, especially when functions are passed as parameters.

4.2.
Experiment Setup

4.2.1.
Subjects

To ensure robust evaluation and minimize selection bias, our study employs established benchmarks recognized in prior research, comprising two datasets:

•

CHISEL Benchmark Suite:
This dataset, employed in
CHISEL

(
Heo et al. 2018
)
and
ProbDD

(
Wang et al. 2021
)
evaluations, comprises 10 C programs from GNU packages. These are typically used in embedded systems after debloating, feature complex property test functions
\psi
involving numerous properties.

•

Perses Benchmark Suite:
Adopted from the Perses benchmark suite
(
Sun et al. 2018
)
, extensively used in delta debugging research including ProbDD
(
Wang et al. 2021
)
, T-PDD
(
Wang et al. 2023
)
, and Vulcan
(
Xu et al. 2023
)
, this dataset features 20 C programs that trigger crashes or miscompilation issues
in various GCC (4.8.2, 4.9.0, 4.8.0) and LLVM (3.6.0, 3.7.0, 3.8.0) versions.

4.2.2.
Metrics

Our evaluation utilizes the following metrics:

•

Reduced Program Size (
S(#)
):
The number of tokens in the reduced program.

•

Processing Time (
P(s)
):
The total time taken for the reduction process, in seconds.

•

Test Case Count (
T(#)
):
The number of test cases actually executed.

•

Reduction Speed (
R(#/s)
):
The token deletion rate per second.

•

Performance Improvement (
\uparrow\!\!M(\%)
):
The percentage improvement of a metric
M\in\{S,P,T\}
with
PMA
over a baseline:

\uparrow M(\%)=\left(\frac{M_{\text{baseline}}-M_{\textsc{PMA}}}{M_{\text{baseline}}}\right)\times 100\%

•

Speedup Factor (
\times R
):
The ratio of reduction speeds of
PMA
to a baseline:

\times R=\frac{R_{\textsc{PMA}}}{R_{\text{baseline}}}

For each
tool, we documented the original size of each subject, the size of the smallest failure-inducing program found, the number of test cases executed, and the processing time. We also measured efficiency by calculating the token deletion rate per second, dividing the total tokens reduced by the total processing time.

To ensure fairness and handle potential timeouts, we applied a 3-hour timeout for each reduction process, following
(
Wang et al. 2021
;
Zhang et al. 2024
)
.
In timeout cases, we recorded the smallest failure-inducing test case and marked the processing time as ’-’ to indicate the time limit was reached. The token deletion rate was still calculated, but timeout cases were excluded from percentage reduction in processing time calculations
\uparrow P(\%)
across the 30 subjects.

4.2.3.
Computing Platform

All experiments were conducted on a Linux server featuring an 8-core, 16-thread Intel(R) Xeon(R) CPU E5-1660 v4 @ 3.20 GHz, 256 GB of RAM, and running Ubuntu 16.04.

RQ1:
CHISEL
vs.
PMA

As detailed in Table
1
,
PMA
significantly enhances
CHISEL
’s efficiency without compromising its effectiveness. Across 30 test subjects, on average,
PMA
cuts processing time by 59.2%, increases the token deletion rate by
3.32\times
, and reduces the size of the final reduced programs by 6.7%.

This improved performance is due to
PMA
avoiding numerous redundant tests that
CHISEL
executes, with percentage reductions (
\uparrow\!\text{T(\%)}
) from 38.1% (
mkdir-5.2.1
) to 90.4% (
gcc-66375
), averaging 80.5%. Consequently,
PMA
consistently achieves faster token deletion rates, ranging from
1.05\times
(
gzip-1.2.4
) to
22.20\times
(
tar-1.15
), and reduces processing time in all non-timeout cases (
\uparrow\text{P(\%)}>0
), from
14.5\%
(
mkdir-5.2.1
) to
78.2\%
(
gcc-65383
).

PMA
achieves efficiency gains over
CHISEL
while maintaining or improving effectiveness. For three out of 30 subjects—
mkdir-5.2.1
,
rm-8.4
, and
clang-27137
—
PMA
slightly increased the sizes of the reduced programs compared to
CHISEL
(
\uparrow\!\text{S(\%)}=-0.9\%
,
\uparrow\!\text{S(\%)}=-2.7\%
, and
\uparrow\!\text{S(\%)}=-1.3\%
), but reduced processing times by 14.5%, 54.8%, and 63.5%, respectively. For
uniq-8.16
, the best among the non-timeout cases,
PMA
reduced both program size by 1.1% and processing time by 25.4%.
Both
CHISEL
and
PMA
encountered timeouts on six programs, including
grep-2.19
,
bzip2-1.0.5
,
sort-8.16
,
gzip-1.2.4
,
date-8.21
, and
tar-1.14
, due to complex property test functions
\psi
(used in program debloating).
By eliminating redundant tests,
PMA
sped up token deletion and produced smaller programs than
CHISEL
, demonstrating its efficiency gains even when occasionally resulting in slightly larger reduced programs.

However, it should be noted that
PMA
’s use of a probabilistic model introduces subtle variations in specific cases. This approach, which allows for selective skipping of test cases, can result in minor divergences in results. For instance, we observed such variations in program size reduction rates (
\uparrow S(\%)
) for the 20 Perses benchmarks in
Table

1
, as reported by
PMA
compared to
CHISEL
.

PMA
and
CHISEL
each produced 19 identically sized reduced programs (
\uparrow\!S(\%)=0
), with one from the
CHISEL
benchmark suite
(
Heo et al. 2018
)
and 18 from the Perses Benchmark Suite
(
Sun et al. 2018
)
. Excluding
chown-8.2
, both tools generated the same final reduced program for each of the remaining 18 programs, highlighting
PMA
’s enhancements in processing time and token deletion rates, alongside maintaining comparable effectiveness to
CHISEL
.

To further explore the findings presented in
Table

1
,
Figure

illustrates
PMA
’s effectiveness in selectively skipping tests that
CHISEL
executes, while also offering insights into potential improvements by highlighting its limitations.

Figure

(a) illustrates the number of failure-inducing test cases
PMA
eliminates for each program, complementing the totals in
Table

1
. While
PMA
effectively reduces program size by skipping non-failure-inducing tests, it sometimes inadvertently skips failure-inducing tests due to violations of monotonicity (Assumption
1
) and inconsistency (Assumption
3
), posing two distinct challenges to delta debugging.
While our approach generally improves efficiency by reducing test executions, it risks missing further reduction opportunities when tests are incorrectly omitted.

Among the 30 subjects in
Table

1
,
PMA
produced larger reduced programs for three subjects:
mkdir-5.2.1
,
rm-8.4
, and
clang-27137
. Focusing on
clang-27137
, improper optimization in
clang-3.8
under the “
-O3
" setting triggers a bug due to an always-false loop, resulting in erroneous output. As shown in
Figure

5
(a),
PMA
inadvertently skipped four failure-inducing tests for
clang-27137
, yielding a reduced program 1.3% larger than
CHISEL
’s. We denote the
N
-th test sequence in
CHISEL
for
clang-27137
as
T_{N}
(starting from 1). The skipping of these four tests—
T_{3095}
,
T_{4012}
,
T_{4120}
, and
T_{7922}
—highlights two key challenges in
PMA
’s reduction strategy.

The first skipped test,
T_{3095}
, illustrates the challenge of handling inconsistency in program reduction.
PMA
inadvertently skipped it because
\textsc{SkipEnabled}(T_{3095})=\textbf{true}
based on the superset
T_{3091}
, which failed with a "
label used but not defined
" error, making
\psi(T_{3091})=\textbf{false}
. Removing the label in
T_{3091}
produced
T_{3095}
, which fixed the error and reproduced the bug, rendering
\psi(T_{3095})=\textbf{true}
. Though this appears to violate monotonicity (Assumption
1
), it actually reflects a violation of consistency (Assumption
3
), as
\psi(T_{3091})\notin\{\textbf{true},\textbf{false}\}
. This case highlights the challenge of accurate skip decisions when consistency is violated.

Recent advances in delta debugging have focused on consistent sequences from a given input program
(
Misherghi and Su 2006
;
Regehr et al. 2012
;
Sun et al. 2018
)
, yet cases like
T_{3091}
may still yield inconsistent results. A potential solution is to refine the categorization of non-failure-inducing tests by expanding the ‘
false
’ category to include
\{\textbf{false},\texttt{unknown}\}

(
Zeller 1999
)
(Assumption
3
), or even further, to
\{\textbf{false},\texttt{unknown}_{1},\texttt{unknown}_{2},...,\texttt{unknown}_{n}\}
, which could strengthen Assumption
1
for more accurate test elimination.

Studying such
unknown
cases and their monotonicity patterns is crucial.
Failures from a missing main function often persist in subsets, while errors like undefined variables may resolve when problematic usage is removed. By categorizing and analyzing these cases, we can develop heuristics to better predict test outcomes and refine delta debugging strategies. We hope this work offers valuable insights for improving our approach in this direction.

The remaining three failure-inducing test cases—
T_{4012}
,
T_{4120}
, and
T_{7922}
in
clang-27137
—pose a distinct challenge.
PMA
skipped these because
\psi
evaluated their corresponding supersets—
T_{4003}
,
T_{4116}
, and
T_{7911}
—as
false
, even though these supersets executed correctly while their subsets triggered the bug. This discrepancy occurred when removing if statements from the supersets allowed for more aggressive optimizations, thus exposing the latent bug. These cases reveal violations of the monotonicity assumption critical to
PMA
’s skip mechanism, highlighting missed reduction opportunities. They also emphasize the need for further research into monotonicity violations, which may differ across various programs and delta debugging contexts.

Among the four failure-inducing test cases skipped by
PMA
in
clang-27137
, skipping
T_{7922}
had the most significant impact on reduction efficacy. This caused
PMA
to miss the opportunity to remove 174 additional tokens, resulting in
\uparrow S(\%)=1.3\%
(

RQ2:
ProbDD
vs.
PMA

As shown in Table
1
, comparing
PMA
with
ProbDD
across 30 test subjects, on average,
PMA
reduces processing time by 22.0%, boosts the token deletion rate by
1.34\times
, and shrinks final program sizes by 3.0%.
PMA
outperforms
ProbDD
in processing time for all non-timeout subjects except
gcc-70586
and exceeds
ProbDD
in token deletion rate for all but
gzip-1.2.4
and
gcc-70586
. Notable gains include
chown-8.2
with a 38.8% time reduction and
1.63\times
increase in token deletion, and
gcc-61383
with a 54.6% time reduction and
2.20\times
increase in token deletion.

The performance differences between
PMA
and
ProbDD
are rooted in their distinct algorithmic approaches.
ProbDD
replaces
CHISEL
’s
ddmin
component, using probabilistic analysis to better detect failure-inducing elements. Conversely,
PMA
enhances
ddmin
within
CHISEL
, improving efficiency by employing a simple yet effective model to skip redundant tests.
Due to
ProbDD
’s focus on individual elements, it is more computationally intensive than
PMA
’s strategy, which manages entire test sequences. As illustrated in
Figure

6
,
PMA
achieves an average reduction in processing time per test case of 26.4% across 30 subjects. Excluding
gzip-1.2.4
and
date-8.21
, which show decreases of
-6.1\%
and
-5.1\%
, respectively, the reductions for the remaining 28 subjects range from 10.6% (
gcc-70127
) to 45.1% (
gcc-61383
), averaging 28.7%.

Looking at program reduction effectiveness,
PMA
generally outperforms
ProbDD
, achieving an average decrease of 3.0%. This improvement is particularly pronounced in cases like
clang-27137
, which sees a 6.6% reduction. However, in instances such as
clang-22704
, while
PMA
increases the program size by 25.4%, it also cuts processing time by 42.7%, highlighting the trade-off between efficiency and effectiveness. Judiciously navigating such trade-offs remains a challenging problem.

Comparing the token deletion rates,
PMA
outperforms
ProbDD
with an average speedup of
1.34\times
. Notable improvements are seen in
gcc-71626
, achieving a speedup of
2.38\times
. Conversely,
PMA
slightly lags in
gcc-70586
, with a speedup of
0.99\times
and a minimal increase in processing time of 1.2%, albeit with a slight improvement in program size reduction by 0.6%. In all six programs that reached the 3-hour timeout limit, except for
gzip-1.2.4
,
PMA
consistently produced smaller reduced programs at faster token deletion rates.

.

ProbDD
and
PMA
in processing time

Integrating our
PMA
approach directly into
ProbDD
yielded negligible efficiency gains, highlighting both the challenges and opportunities of merging these strategies. The limited improvement stems from
ProbDD
’s architecture within
CHISEL
, where it replaces
ddmin
and prioritizes smaller test cases to probabilistically detect failure-inducing elements. This strategy often generates non-failure-inducing cases, progressing from smaller to larger ones, diminishing the effectiveness of
PMA
’s skip mechanism.
PMA
is crafted to eliminate redundant tests through structured, sequence-based reductions using a divide-and-conquer approach, similar to those seen in
ddmin
-style algorithms.

Future research could explore combining
PMA
’s sequence-based reduction with
ProbDD
’s granular approach—using
PMA
for rapid space reduction followed by
ProbDD
’s precise refinement. Alternatively, optimizing
ProbDD
to prioritize efficiency, even if it introduces some redundancies, would give
PMA
greater scope to eliminate excess tests, thereby enhancing overall efficiency.

In summary, our
PMA
approach, combined with
CHISEL
, significantly outperforms
ProbDD
(also based on
CHISEL
), further demonstrating its advancements in delta debugging.

5.
Related Work

ddmin
-based Delta Debugging.

The
ddmin
algorithm
(
Zeller and Hildebrandt 2002
)
establishes a systematic foundation for test case reduction. It uses an iterative approach to minimize failure-inducing inputs, testing subsets to identify and isolate the minimal failure-inducing elements.

To overcome
ddmin
’s limitations with large, structural inputs, Hierarchical Delta Debugging (HDD)
(
Misherghi and Su 2006
)
extends
ddmin
for hierarchical structures (e.g., parse trees), applying reduction root-to-leaf to handle large inputs efficiently. Later variants—Coarse HDD
(
Hodován et al. 2017
)
, modernized HDD
(
Hodován and Kiss 2016
)
, and HDDr
(
Kiss et al. 2018
)
—further optimize this approach.

Perses
(
Sun et al. 2018
)
advanced delta debugging by leveraging formal syntax to guide the reduction process, utilizing
ddmin
to prune parse tree nodes while maintaining syntactic validity.

CHISEL

(
Heo et al. 2018
)
, a baseline for
PMA
evaluation, employs reinforcement learning with a Markov decision model to prioritize
ddmin
test cases more likely to reproduce failures.

ProbDD

(
Wang et al. 2021
)
, another baseline for
PMA
evaluation, improves
ddmin
through Bayesian optimization, generating test cases based on the likelihood of each element’s success. T-PDD
(
Wang et al. 2023
)
further refines this by leveraging syntactic relationships to estimate the probability of retaining each element in a test case.

Among existing probabilistic approaches, ProbDD and T-PDD use historical test data to update element probabilities and select high-probability test sequences. In contrast,
PMA
focuses on search space monotonicity rather than individual element probabilities.
While
CHISEL
employs a statistical model to prioritize
ddmin
sequences likely to pass the property test
\psi
,
PMA
’s monotonicity-based approach enables more flexible and efficient pruning of the search space. This distinctive strategy allows
PMA
to potentially surpass these existing methods in both efficiency and effectiveness.
Our evaluation demonstrates that
PMA
significantly improves performance compared to
CHISEL
and
ProbDD
, highlighting its potential to advance delta debugging techniques.

Combining
ddmin
and Program Transformations.

C-Reduce
(
Regehr et al. 2012
)
focuses on deletion operations enhanced by language-specific transformations such as function inlining and constant propagation. Utilizing C/C++ semantics, it effectively reduces programs, often surpassing general-purpose reducers in creating minimal test cases that still expose bugs within the programs.

Similarly, ddSMT
(
Niemetz and Biere 2013
)
is tailored for the SMT-LIBv2 format, using language-specific transformations like command-level scope and term substitution to effectively reduce SMT formulas. Its successor, ddSMT2.0
(
Kremer et al. 2021
)
, introduces a hybrid strategy combining SMT-specific transformations with
ddmin
and hierarchical approaches while supporting all SMT-LIBv2 dialects.

Vulcan
(
Xu et al. 2023
)
is a general framework that enhances
ddmin
-based methods by integrating transformations. It starts with a
ddmin
-based AGR (Agnostic Program Reduction), such as Perses
(
Sun et al. 2018
)
, then applies transformations like Identifier Replacement and Tree-Based Local Exhaustive Enumeration to create new reduction opportunities. By iteratively combining transformation and reduction, Vulcan surpasses traditional
ddmin
’s 1-minimality limitation.

Transformation-based approaches target smaller reductions but often face efficiency challenges. This paper enhances the efficiency of existing
ddmin
-based methods, setting aside the refinement of transformation-based approaches for future research.

6.
Conclusion

In this paper, we introduce Probabilistic Monotonicity Assessment (
PMA
), a novel approach that enhances delta debugging by dynamically modeling search space monotonicity with probabilistic techniques.
Unlike approaches that focus on individual element probabilities,
PMA
assesses the entire search space, enabling more efficient pruning of test sequences. Through a dynamically updated confidence function,
PMA
adapts to changes in monotonicity, significantly boosting efficiency by reducing the number of required tests while maintaining or enhancing effectiveness. This focus on search space monotonicity offers a fresh perspective on delta debugging strategies and suggests potential for future research to explore additional search space characteristics to further refine program reduction and bug localization in software engineering.

While
PMA
enhances the efficiency and effectiveness of
ddmin
-style algorithms such as
CHISEL

(
Heo et al. 2018
)
, opportunities for further improvement remain. First, although it reduces the number of tests executed by
CHISEL
by 80.5% on average (
Table

1
),
PMA
achieves only a
3.32\times
average speedup in token deletion rate, suggesting room to reduce analysis overhead for greater performance gains. Second,
PMA
may inadvertently filter out failure-inducing tests, as illustrated in
Figure

5
(a), due to inconsistencies with Assumptions
1
and
3
in practice. Some subsets of a failing test case may still pass
\psi
. Expanding the categorization of non-failure-inducing tests—for example, broadening the
false
category (in Assumption
3
) to
\{\textbf{false},\texttt{unknown}\}

(
Zeller 1999
)
—could refine Assumption
1
and enable more nuanced elimination rules. Third, although
PMA
significantly reduces the number of tests executed, many tests failing
SkipEnabled
are still retained (
Figure

5
(b)), suggesting the potential for further reduction techniques. Finally, while
PMA
combined with
CHISEL
outperforms
ProbDD
, integrating
PMA
directly with
ProbDD
yields negligible gains, as detailed in
Section

4.4
.
ProbDD
reduces redundant tests from

but increases per-test processing time compared to
PMA
-
CHISEL
(

). Speeding up

PMA

ProbDD
combination by giving
PMA
more opportunities to eliminate excess tests. Alternatively, a hybrid approach could apply
PMA
first to narrow the search space, followed by
ProbDD
for finer-grained reduction.
