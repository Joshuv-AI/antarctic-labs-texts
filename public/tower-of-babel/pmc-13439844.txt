Exploring the benefits and safety of combining nebivolol and valsartan for managing hypertension: a systematic review
Authors: Mohamed Khalid, Wojoud M Majzoub, Yasmeen M Ibrahim, Hatoon Makki, Esraa Ibrahim, Manar M Amin, Kannan O Ahmed, Bashir A Yousef
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHypertension is considered the world’s leading risk factor for mortality. Many individuals with hypertension need to take multiple blood pressure (BP)-lowering medications. Combining Nebivolol, a third-generation beta-blocker, and Valsartan, an angiotensin receptor blocker (ARB), presents a promising therapeutic approach for BP management. Although several clinical studies have evaluated this combination, evidence regarding its overall efficacy and safety remains fragmented. This study aims to evaluate the efficacy and safety of this combination therapy.MethodThe systematic review followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. A systematic literature search was conducted across PubMed, Scopus, and the Cochrane Library from inception until February 2025. The studies that met the requirements to assess the safety and effectiveness of a combination therapy of nebivolol and valsartan for the treatment of hypertension included open-label studies, crossover trials, and randomized controlled trials (RCTs). Databases searched included PubMed, Scopus, and Cochrane Library. The main topics of data extraction were the changes in systolic and diastolic blood pressure (SBP/DBP), cardiovascular events, treatment adherence, and quality-of-life measures. The risk of bias was assessed using ROB 2.0 and ROBINS-I frameworks.ResultsOut of 99 initially screened studies, six met the inclusion criteria, including two randomized controlled trials (one of which generated two prespecified sub-analyses), one open-label single-arm study, and one crossover trial. The results showed that combining Nebivolol and valsartan decreased SBP and DBP. The highest dose (20/320 mg) achieved a mean SBP reduction of -15.4 mmHg and a DBP reduction of -10.0 mmHg over 8 weeks. Long-term Studies of a 52-week duration showed sustained blood pressure control, with an average drop in SBP of -25.5 mmHg and a drop in DBP of -19.0 mmHg. Additionally, the combination improved heart rate variability and reduced renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system activity. Clinical trials have reported that Nebivolol has a favorable safety profile, with mild and transient symptoms, including fatigue, headache, dyspnea, insomnia, dizziness, and paresthesia, making it a patient-preferred option.ConclusionThis systematic review provides evidence for the safety and efficacy of the nebivolol-valsartan combination as an antihypertensive regimen. The combination is also beneficial in terms of hemodynamic parameters and overall cardiac load. Nevertheless, large-scale, long-term trials are needed to confirm these results and to define who can benefit most from this therapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-026-06043-w.Keywords: Hypertension, Nebivolol, Valsartan, Combination therapy, Systematic review

Introduction

The World Health Organization (WHO) defines hypertension as a condition in which the blood pressure in the arteries consistently exceeds 140/90 mmHg [1]. This chronic elevation is a major risk factor for cardiovascular diseases and, if not properly managed, can lead to severe complications such as angina pectoris, myocardial infarction (heart attack), heart failure, arrhythmias, stroke, kidney damage, and sudden death [2, 3]. According to the WHO, an estimated 1.28 billion adults aged 30 to 79 worldwide have hypertension, with the majority residing in low- and middle-income countries [1].

Despite the availability of a wide range of antihypertensive medications, achieving optimal blood pressure control remains challenging for many patients. Monotherapy often fails to reach target blood pressure levels, particularly in those with moderate to severe hypertension, necessitating the use of combination therapies [4, 5]. Combination therapy allows antihypertensive agents to act synergistically through different mechanisms, providing more effective BP control while minimizing dose-related side effects [6]. Combination therapies approved by the Food and Drug Administration, such as the Nebivolol and Valsartan combination (Byvalson®), have emerged as promising options targeting the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system for more effective hypertension management [7]. Nebivolol is a long-acting third-generation, highly selective β1-blocker with unique vasodilatory properties, along with anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects [8]. Unlike other β-blockers, it does not adversely affect insulin sensitivity or lipid metabolism, making it suitable for patients with metabolic syndrome [9].

Valsartan is an angiotensin receptor blocker (ARB) that selectively inhibits angiotensin II from binding to the angiotensin II type 1 receptor (AT1), thereby preventing vasoconstriction mediated by angiotensin II, which results in lowered blood pressure [10]. Clinical evidence supports that this combination not only leads to more substantial systolic and diastolic BP reduction compared with monotherapies and improves vascular and metabolic profiles but also improves patient adherence with minimal adverse effects, ultimately contributing to optimal blood pressure control and improved quality of life for the patient [11–14]. Clinical trials confirm that the single-pill combination of nebivolol and valsartan is safe, well-tolerated, and associated with long-term safety, while also lowering treatment costs, representing a practical advantage over free combinations [15].

Despite these promising findings, a systematic review evaluating the overall efficacy and safety of nebivolol–valsartan across diverse populations and clinical settings has not yet been conducted. Such a review is essential to consolidate available trial data, assess variations in outcomes, and clarify potential safety concerns. By synthesizing the evidence, this work aimed to provide clinicians with an evidence-based guide to optimize treatment decisions and ensure that patients receive effective and safe therapy. The objective of this review was therefore to systematically evaluate the efficacy and safety of nebivolol–valsartan in patients with hypertension, while also investigating its role in the obese and non-obese population.

Methodology

Study design

This systematic review adhered to quality criteria and pre-established inclusion and exclusion factors and was conducted in accordance with the guidelines established by the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews (PRISMA) to ensure transparency and reproducibility [16]. Before commencing the study, the protocol for this systematic review was registered with PROSPERO (Registration ID: CRD420250630922) to promote methodological rigor and reduce the risk of bias. The review focused on assessing the efficacy and safety of the nebivolol and valsartan combination for the management of hypertension. Studies were included if they met the predefined inclusion criteria, encompassing randomized controlled trials (RCTs), analysis papers, crossover trials, and open-label trial studies involving adult patients treated with the nebivolol and valsartan combination. Studies were excluded if they involved nonhuman subjects, were reviews or editorials, or did not provide quantitative data.

Literature search strategy

Search databases

The search strategy was developed to gather as many studies as possible from the main scientific databases; thus, three databases were selected: PubMed, Scopus, and Cochrane Library.

Search terms

The search strategy included Medical Subject Headings terms and keywords related to “hypertension,” “blood pressure,” “Nebivolol,” and “Valsartan.” The details of the search strategy are listed and described in the supplementary table.

Study selection

Using the Rayyan website [17], documents were filtered, screened, and excluded. Two review authors first screened the titles of replicates, animal studies, review papers, conference papers, and meta-analyses. Abstracts were then read to identify included and excluded studies, after which the remaining literature was read in full by two review authors for final determination of inclusion. In this process, the two researchers independently screened the literature and compared their findings. If the results matched, the studies were included; any disagreements were discussed and resolved by a third investigator.

Eligibility criteria

Eligibility criteria were established to determine the appropriateness of studies for inclusion in the review. For this study, literature was screened according to the following PICOS criteria: patients were adults with primary hypertension (mild or moderate); the intervention was nebivolol and valsartan combination; the comparator was placebo, other combinations, or no comparator; outcomes included antihypertensive efficacy and safety or adverse drug events; and study design included randomized clinical trials (RCTs), crossover trials, open-label trials, and analysis papers.

Articles were included in the systematic review if they involved human studies with patients aged 18 years or older diagnosed with primary hypertension. Eligible designs included randomized controlled trials (RCTs), open-label trials, and analysis papers. At least one study group must have received a combination of nebivolol and valsartan, and the treatment duration was required to be at least six weeks to provide sufficient time to observe meaningful clinical outcomes.

Exclusion criteria included patients with secondary hypertension, studies not focusing on the combination of nebivolol and valsartan for hypertension management, animal or in vitro studies, review articles, meta-analyses, case-control studies, cohort studies, and editorials. Studies lacking sufficient data on safety, efficacy, or clinical outcomes were excluded, as were articles published in languages other than English and those with incomplete data or an inability to obtain the full text.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Data extraction

Three researchers independently screened the title and abstract to identify potentially relevant studies. The extracted data included study characteristics, patient characteristics, interventions, comparators, outcomes, and other relevant results. References in identified articles were also searched for possible inclusion. Another three investigators verified the extracted data. In cases where numerical data were not provided in the text or tables, data were extracted from graphs using WebPlotDigitizer (version 5) [18]. Discrepancies were handled using a consensus procedure. It is important to clarify that this extraction was performed solely to retrieve accurate numerical data for the narrative description and synthesis of results; it was not intended for statistical pooling or quantitative meta-analysis, given the qualitative nature of this systematic review.

Quality assessment

Quality assessment was conducted independently by two researchers using the ROB 2.0 tool for randomized controlled trials and crossover studies [19], and the ROBINS tool for open-label studies [20]. The following seven domains were considered: randomized sequence generation, treatment allocation concealment, blinding of participants, blinding of personnel, incomplete outcome data, selective reporting, and other sources of bias. Trials were categorized into three levels of risk of bias (ROB) based on the number of components with potential high ROB: high risk, moderate risk, or some concern, and low risk. Risk of bias visualization was performed using the Robvis website [21].

Ethics statement

Ethical approval was not required because this research is based on previously published studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any authors.

Results

Study selection

During the search process, an initial number of 99 studies was identified and evaluated for screening based on their titles and abstracts. Fifty-four articles were removed and classified as duplicates, of which 42 were initially identified by automation, and 12 were manually removed. Moreover, 36 studies that did not meet the inclusion criteria were excluded, leaving nine studies, of which one paper could not be retrieved. A total of eight studies proceeded to full-text evaluation. Out of these, one was found to be irrelevant and excluded, while another was considered a duplicate, resulting in six eligible papers. These included two randomized controlled trials (one of which generated two prespecified sub-analyses), one open-label single-arm study, and one crossover trial. Lastly, a paper with a crossover study design was utilized in the process of quantitative data synthesis, as shown in the PRISMA Flow Chart (Fig. 1).

Characteristics of included studies

A total of four clinical trials conducted by Joel M. Neutel, MD, Joseph L. Izzo Jr., Selvia M. Farag, and Thomas D. Giles met our eligibility criteria [22–25]. Two of these studies were double-blinded, randomized clinical trials, while one was a double-blinded, randomized, crossover-controlled trial. The fourth study was open-label. Two additional sub-analysis studies from the Giles trial were included, one of which was conducted by Christain W Mende (26.27). The primary outcomes measured were trough-seated systolic and diastolic blood pressures, 24-hour ambulatory cardiac oxygen consumption (ACRPP), and peripheral and central blood pressures. These trials involved 5,134 participants aged 18 or older, with a mean age of 53.55 (SD ± 2.84) years.

Three studies compared the combination of valsartan and nebivolol with a control group, while one study compared valsartan/nebivolol with amlodipine/valsartan. Two analysis sub-studies derived from the clinical trial conducted by Thomas D. Giles that measured systolic and diastolic blood pressure and heart rate in one study, and 24-hour systolic and diastolic blood pressure via ambulatory blood pressure monitoring (ABPM) in the other, which included a total of 1,610 participants (858 males and 757 females) with a mean age of 50.68 ± 1.176 years [26, 27].

The studies were conducted in the United States and Egypt. Controlled trials published from 2014 to 2018 were included. Most studies were conducted in multicenter outpatient hypertension clinics across North America, specifically in the United States of America and Egypt. Most patients had untreated primary hypertension with baseline systolic and diastolic blood pressure levels generally within the stage 1–2 hypertension range, making the populations broadly representative of typical patients requiring combination therapy. Dosing regimens commonly included nebivolol combined with valsartan in the following concentrations: (5/80), (5/160), (10/160), (10/320), (20/320), (40/320) mg/day. These choices align with guideline-supported initiation doses for beta-blocker/ARB combinations. Several studies also evaluated titration strategies, allowing dose escalation after 2–4 weeks based on BP response. Treatment duration ranged from 8 to 52 weeks, sufficient to capture steady-state antihypertensive effects. Risk of bias

The first RCT by Thomas D. Giles [25] and the two analysis papers [26, 27] derived from this study demonstrated a low overall risk of bias because they had a well-defined randomization process, allocation concealment, and blinding of participants, investigators, and outcome assessors. This minimized the potential for performance and detection biases, and selective reporting was avoided as all pre-specified outcomes were reported with minimal and balanced missing outcome data across study arms. In contrast, the study by Selvia M. Farag et al. exhibited a high risk of bias due to deviations from the intended intervention, as participants, caregivers, and those delivering the intervention were not blinded [24]. Deviations from the intended intervention were not addressed using appropriate analysis approaches, and the study also showed a high risk of missing outcome data, with no evidence that the results were not biased by these missing data. The study by Joel M. Neutel et al. raised some concerns, mainly attributed to the lack of randomization and the absence of a control group, which led to selection bias [22]. Additionally, the study by Joseph L. Izzo Jr. et al. demonstrated a high risk in terms of period and carryover effects due to the absence of a washout period, and further showed a high risk in the measurement of outcomes, as the employed methods were considered inappropriate [23]. Outcomes

Clinical outcomes

Clinical evidence consistently demonstrates that the fixed-dose combination of nebivolol and valsartan produces substantial and sustained reductions in both systolic and diastolic blood pressure across diverse hypertensive populations. Large randomized studies have shown that nebivolol–valsartan significantly lowers systolic blood pressure by approximately 15–35 mmHg, with comparable or superior efficacy to monotherapy and results similar to those achieved with amlodipine–valsartan combinations. These effects were observed across short- and long-term treatment durations, including up to 52 weeks of follow-up (Table 6).

Comparative studies also indicated meaningful reductions in both peripheral and central blood pressure, with central systolic blood pressure reductions becoming comparable to amlodipine–valsartan by 12 weeks. Adverse events (safety outcomes)

The safety and tolerability of the nebivolol-valsartan combination were evaluated through two Phase III multicentre trials: a short-term, 8-week randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled study (Giles et al.) and a 52-week open-label [25], single-arm study (Neutel et al.) [22]. In both trials, safety was assessed by the systematic recording of treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs), monitoring of vital signs at every visit, and periodic measurement of clinical laboratory parameters from blood and urine samples. The 8-week study further included 12-lead electrocardiograph (ECG) measurements at screening, baseline, and the final treatment visit to monitor for potential cardiac effects, such as QT interval changes. While the 8-week trial focused on comparing various fixed-dose combination (FDC) strengths to monotherapy and placebo, the long-term study assessed safety during a 52-week treatment phase that allowed for dose titration and the supplemental addition of hydrochlorothiazide to achieve blood pressure goals. In both investigations, safety analyses were based on the safety population, defined as all randomized participants who received at least one dose of the study drug, providing a comprehensive view of the combination’s long-term and comparative tolerability profile (Tables 10 and 11).

Both the short-term and long-term investigations concluded that the nebivolol-valsartan combination is safe and well-tolerated for the treatment of hypertension. In the 8-week placebo-controlled trial, the overall incidence of TEAEs was comparable across all treatment arms, with 30–36% of participants in each group experiencing at least one event. Specifically, the TEAE rates for the fixed-dose combination (FDC) groups (33–35%) were similar to or slightly lower than the placebo group (36%). Over 52 weeks of therapy, 59.2% of patients experienced at least one AE, though the majority were judged to be mild or moderate in severity. Headache was the most frequent adverse event reported in both studies, with an incidence of 5.7% in the long-term trial and ranging from 3.6% to 4.3% in the FDC groups of the short-term trial. Other common events included nasopharyngitis and upper respiratory tract infections. Notably, side effects typically associated with beta-blockers such as bradycardia, fatigue, and dizziness were reported at low rates and were primarily associated with high-dose monotherapy (40 mg/day nebivolol) rather than clinically common doses (5–20 mg/day) used in the FDCs. Serious adverse events occurred in less than 1% of FDC-treated patients in the 8-week trial, and none were considered related to the study treatment. Finally, no deaths in either study were deemed related to the study medication.

Discussion

This systematic review pooled evidence from several RCTs, open-label, crossover, and analysis studies that evaluated the antihypertensive efficacy and safety of Nebivolol-Valsartan combination therapy in different patient populations, as well as dosing regimens and treatment durations. The primary objective of this review was to evaluate the Nebivolol-Valsartan combination therapy for reducing blood pressure through the assessment of systolic and diastolic blood pressure, 24-hour ambulatory blood pressure, modulation of the renin-angiotensin system, and overall cardiovascular benefits. In addition, the safety and tolerability of the Nebivolol/Valsartan combination were examined to provide a comprehensive understanding of its clinical utility [28–31].

The aggregated data from the included studies demonstrate that the combination of Nebivolol and Valsartan consistently reduces both systolic and diastolic blood pressure compared to placebo and monotherapy. The observed dose-dependent response allows clinicians to titrate the combination dose according to patient response and tolerability, thereby supporting individualized treatment while minimizing unnecessary exposure to high doses of a single agent. Although nebivolol possesses nitric oxide-mediated vasodilatory properties, a direct comparison, such as in the Nebivolol, Bisoprolol Multicenter Study (NEBIS trial), indicates that its antihypertensive effect is similar to that of non-vasodilating beta-blockers, such as bisoprolol [32].

The maintenance of blood pressure control over 52 weeks in the single-arm open-label follow-up supports the potential utility of this regimen for long-term hypertension management. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and considered complementary to randomized data, rather than as conclusive evidence of long-term outcome benefit [22]. Furthermore, the combination achieved effective control of 24-hour ambulatory blood pressure, a recognized predictor of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality [22, 25]. However, interpretation of these results is constrained by the small sample size of the ambulatory blood pressure monitoring (ABPM) sub-study, which may have been inadequate to detect significant differences between groups treated with fixed-dose combinations of 20 and 320 mg/day and Nebivolol 40 mg/day.

The observed reduction in plasma renin activity and aldosterone levels highlights the dual-pathway blockade of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS), which may confer organ-protective effects beyond blood pressure reduction [26]. Dual RAAS blockade may be particularly beneficial through aldosterone inhibition in patients whose hypertension is partially mediated by the direct pressor effects of aldosterone. this mechanism of action may be of importance in the treatment of patients with so-called treatment-resistant hypertension, in whom excess aldosterone may be an important factor in sustaining high BP levels [33].

A comparative study by Farag et al. reported that the combination of Nebivolol and Valsartan provided marked reductions in both central and peripheral blood pressure, specifically over a longer duration. Despite the efficacy of both treatment regimens, the Amlodipine/Valsartan combination was more effective. This highlights the need for a direct head-to-head comparison to evaluate their long-term effectiveness in blood pressure control [24].

Izzo et al. demonstrated that Nebivolol Valsartan combination reduced ambulatory central rate-pressure product compared with valsartan alone, despite comparable brachial and central blood pressure levels. This effect was primarily driven by sustained heart-rate reduction, which was not attenuated by concomitant valsartan therapy [23]. Notably, the combination was not superior to Nebivolol alone in reducing myocardial oxygen consumption, suggesting that the cardioprotective benefit is largely attributable to nebivolol’s β-blockade rather than additive renin–angiotensin system inhibition. These findings highlight that the rationale for nebivolol–valsartan combination therapy may lie in complementary hemodynamic and cardiac effects rather than enhanced blood pressure lowering alone, with consistent results observed in African American patients. However, these results should be interpreted cautiously given the small sample size and reliance on surrogate measures of myocardial workload rather than clinical cardiovascular outcomes.

The pooled evidence indicates that the Nebivolol-Valsartan combination has a favorable tolerability profile. The adverse event profile is consistent with the established safety profiles of the individual monotherapies, with most events classified as mild to moderate. The incidence of common adverse effects, such as headache and fatigue, was comparable to placebo, suggesting that the addition of a beta-blocker to an angiotensin receptor blocker (ARB) does not substantially increase somatic side effects. Although a dose-dependent increase in bradycardia was observed, overall discontinuation rates remained low. These findings support the FDA-approved lower-dose formulation (5 mg/80 mg) as an optimal starting point to minimize adverse events while maintaining blood pressure control. Furthermore, long-term open-label data confirm the persistence of this safety profile over 52 weeks, addressing concerns regarding delayed-onset adverse reactions.

Limitations

This study is the first systematic review conducted to assess the safety and efficacy of the Nebivolol-Valsartan combination. We utilized different studies, including RCTs, crossover trials, and open-label trials, to provide clinicians with evidence-based practice. Despite our efforts to systematically retrieve all relevant literature, this review is subject to several important limitations that temper the generalizability and strength of our conclusions. We identified only six studies (derived from four principal trials) that met our eligibility criteria. This small sample size and the significant heterogeneity in study design (mixing double-blind RCTs, open-label trials, and crossover studies) comparator structure, outcome reporting, and follow-up duration precluded a formal quantitative meta-analysis precluded a formal quantitative meta-analysis.

Several included studies exhibited a high or moderate risk of bias. Specifically, the open-label study by Joel M. Neutel et al. lacked randomization and a control group, introducing significant selection bias, and a key crossover trial by Joseph L. Izzo Jr. et al. had a high risk of carryover effects due to the absence of a washout period. Additionally, the study by Selvia M. Farag et al. showed a high risk of bias due to a lack of blinding and significant missing outcome data, which may have biased the results.

The weight of the evidence is heavily reliant on the findings and subsequent sub-analyses of a single principal investigator (Thomas D. Giles). This concentration may introduce authorship bias and limit the external validity of the findings, underscoring the need for independent replication. Most trials had relatively short follow-up durations. Consequently, the current evidence is insufficient to draw definitive conclusions about the long-term impact of nebivolol–valsartan on critical hard cardiovascular outcomes, such as all-cause mortality or major adverse cardiac events. In addition, due to the limited sample size and study heterogeneity, we were unable to conduct robust subgroup analyses for populations with comorbidities (such as diabetes or chronic kidney disease) or sensitivity analyses to fully test the robustness of the primary efficacy signals. While necessary for complete reporting, reliance on WebPlotDigitizer to extract numerical data from graphical representations may introduce minimal imprecision compared to the primary reported data.

Conclusions

The available evidence from the studies included in this systematic review suggests that the nebivolol–valsartan combination may provide effective blood pressure reduction with an acceptable safety profile, along with favorable effects on heart rate variability, RAAS modulation, and 24-hour ambulatory blood pressure. These findings suggest potential physiological and hemodynamic benefits beyond blood pressure control. However, the current evidence base is limited by the small number of studies, heterogeneity in study design, comparator regimens, outcome reporting, and follow-up duration, as well as reliance on a limited number of principal trials. Consequently, the available data remains insufficient to support definitive clinical conclusions or guideline-level recommendations. Further large-scale, long-term randomized controlled trials are required to confirm these findings and to clarify the role of this combination therapy in diverse patient populations, including those with chronic kidney disease, diabetes, or heart failure, before definitive clinical positioning can be established.

Recommendations

The primary goal of pharmacological interventions in hypertension is to reduce cardiovascular outcomes, as emphasized by the European Society of Cardiology. The nebivolol–valsartan combination may be considered in selected patients when both drug classes are clinically indicated, particularly within a personalized treatment approach [34–36]. However, the current evidence base remains limited and heterogeneous and is insufficient to support guideline-level recommendations. Further long-term, large-scale randomized controlled trials are needed to evaluate the impact of this combination on hard cardiovascular outcomes, including all-cause mortality, myocardial infarction, and stroke. Future studies should also be specifically designed to assess efficacy and safety in patients with comorbid conditions such as diabetes mellitus and chronic kidney disease.
