Successful treatment with olanzapine and aripiprazole of a schizophrenic patient who developed priapism after switching from risperidone to paliperidone
Authors: Saki Kawahara, Kazuyuki Watanabe, Kazuhiko Inazumi, Makoto Kimura, Yuki Hirose, Hiraki Koishikawa
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIschemic priapism is a rare pathological condition, and delayed intervention can result in irreversible sequelae. Most cases are attributed to the use of antipsychotics. The blockade of α1‐adrenergic receptors is thought to be associated with the disease onset, although data supporting this hypothesis are lacking. No consensus regarding the optimal choice of medication is available.Case PresentationA 59‐year‐old man with schizophrenia, who had been receiving long‐acting injections of risperidone, developed ischemic priapism after receiving paliperidone treatment. Following improvement in ischemic priapism, we administered a combination of aripiprazole and olanzapine, which improved his psychiatric symptoms. We did not observe any recurrence of ischemic priapism.ConclusionsSwitching the antipsychotic drug causing ischemic priapism to patients having a relatively low affinity for α1‐adrenergic receptors may enable the treatment of schizophrenia without recurrence of ischemic priapism. In addition to the affinity for α1‐adrenergic receptor, differences in metabolic enzyme types and antipsychotic doses may be involved in the occurrence of ischemic priapism. Accumulating evidence is necessary to establish guidelines for selecting medication of patients with ischemic priapism.

INTRODUCTION

Priapism is a rare condition of prolonged and painful penile erection without sexual stimulation. 1 The most common presentation of priapism is the ischemic type that can result in a significant risk of persistent erectile dysfunction 2 ; in severe cases, it requires penile amputation. 3 Pharmacologically induced priapism is the most common cause of priapism, with approximately 50% drug‐related priapism being attributed to the use of antipsychotics. 4 The mechanism of priapism associated with antipsychotics is thought to be related to the blocking of α1‐adrenergic receptors; however, clinical data supporting this hypothesis are lacking. 2 Some reports recommend a switch to other medications with relatively low potential of blocking α1‐adrenergic receptor. 5 However, owing to limited number of case reports and studies, no clear guidelines are available for the selection of appropriate medications to switch for in cases of ischemic priapism. Studies have recommended the use of amisulpride that does not have an affinity for α1‐adrenergic receptors; 5 , 6 however, its use has not been approved in Japan.

Here, we present a case report of a patient who had long been treated with long‐acting injections of risperidone and developed ischemic priapism after administering paliperidone. The patient successfully transitioned to combination therapy with aripiprazole and olanzapine for continuing the treatment of schizophrenia without recurrence of ischemic priapism.

CASE

The patient was a 59‐year‐old Japanese man who was diagnosed with schizophrenia in his 20s. His medical history included asthma, while he had no history of inappropriate alcohol or illicit drug use. He had a history of multiple hospitalizations in psychiatric wards owing to nonadherence with medication. He was treated with 3.75 mg/day olanzapine and a long‐acting injection of risperidone (50 mg/3 weeks), and his condition remained stable for 7 years. However, owing to a decrease in income, he stopped regularly attending the doctor and began self‐adjusting his medications. Approximately 4 months later, his paranoia worsened, and he was admitted to a psychiatric ward.

We decided to switch to paliperidone palmitate that has a dosing interval longer than the long‐acting injection of risperidone. We initiated treatment with paliperidone (3 mg/day) on the day after hospitalization and increased the dose to 6 mg/day after 5 days. He reported severe pain in the penis and persistent erection 19 days after starting paliperidone and 14 days after the dose was increased (on the 20th day of hospitalization). We immediately discontinued paliperidone and requested a consultation with a urologist. The urologist diagnosed the patient with ischemic priapism and performed emergency blood aspiration from his penile corpora cavernosa.

After a medication‐free period of 3 days, we switched to aripiprazole because of its relatively low affinity for α1‐adrenergic receptors and potential for future use as a long‐acting injection. Aripiprazole treatment was initiated at a dose of 15 mg/day on the 23rd day of hospitalization, which was gradually increased to 24 mg/day on the 95th day. However, the patient remained partially symptomatic of paranoia and delusions of poisoning. We considered that his symptoms were not adequately controlled with aripiprazole monotherapy; therefore, we decided to initiate treatment with a combination of aripiprazole and olanzapine, which also has low affinity for α1‐adrenergic receptors. We initiated 5 mg/day olanzapine treatment on the 104th day of hospitalization and gradually increased it to 20 mg/day on the 123rd day. Consequently, paranoia and delusions of poisoning disappeared. According to some case reports, the onset of ischemic priapism occurs within several days of starting aripiprazole treatment. 7 Therefore, we considered that the patient is unlikely to develop ischemic priapism owing to aripiprazole. We administered a long‐acting injection of aripiprazole on the 131st day of hospitalization. The patient's condition stabilized with concomitant treatment using long‐acting injections of aripiprazole 400 mg/4 weeks and olanzapine (20 mg/day), with no recurrence of ischemic priapism. DISCUSSION

Affinity for α1‐adrenergic receptor varies among atypical antipsychotics. While clozapine, risperidone, and paliperidone show the highest α1‐adrenergic antagonism, olanzapine, quetiapine, and aripiprazole show moderate effects, and amisulpride shows not antagonism (Table 1). 8 Evidence from case reports in Japan suggests that patients administering risperidone and quetiapine have a higher frequency of ischemic priapism than those administering olanzapine, aripiprazole, and clozapine. 9

Based on these reports, we selected olanzapine and aripiprazole and successfully continued the treatment of schizophrenia without the recurrence of ischemic priapism. However, some questions still remained unanswered.

Paliperidone is an active metabolite of risperidone and has a high affinity for α1‐adrenergic receptors similar to that of risperidone. 8 , 10 Therefore, we considered that both risperidone and paliperidone pose a similar risk for developing ischemic priapism. However, in reality, this patient developed ischemic priapism while administering paliperidone but did not experience it during treatment with risperidone.

Risperidone is metabolized to 9‐hydroxyrisperidone (paliperidone) by cytochrome P450 (CYP) 2D6. 11 CYP2D6 is highly polymorphic, which leads to a wide range of CYP2D6 activity from complete lack of function in poor metabolizers to excessive function in ultrarapid metabolizers. 12 Analysis of clinical studies suggests that poor and normal metabolizers do not have different rates of side effects. 13 In contrast, some studies suggest that the ratio of risperidone to 9‐hydroxyrisperidone, which is determined by CYP2D6 genotype, may be associated with the difference in types of side effects. 11 In the case of poor CYP2D6 metabolizers, standard doses of risperidone lead to increased plasma levels of risperidone and decreased levels of 9‐hydroxyrisperidone. 11 Additionally, paliperidone has a slightly higher affinity for α1‐adrenergic receptors (Ki value = 2.5 nM) than risperidone (Ki value = 5 nM). 14 Therefore, we speculate that this patient did not experience priapism with risperidone treatment, while it occurred during paliperidone treatment, potentially owing to the patient being a poor CYP2D6 metabolizer, which led to decreased levels of 9‐hydroxyrisperidone. However, determining a patient's CYP2D6 phenotype is challenging owing to the complexity of CYP2D6. 15 We did not conduct genotype analysis of CYP2D6 and measurements of 9‐hydroxyrisperidone concentration in this case.

Additionally, the patient received long‐acting injections of risperidone every 3 weeks, although the package insert recommends administration every 2 weeks. Therefore, the blood levels of risperidone may have been lower than normal. The dosage of antipsychotics may also have influenced the difference in the occurrence of ischemic priapism.

In this case, monotherapy was insufficient for controlling the patient's psychiatric symptoms; therefore, we used a combination of two antipsychotics. We chose two antipsychotics with low affinities for α1‐adrenergic receptors. However, whether this combination therapy increased the risk of recurrent ischemic priapism was uncertain.

Previous case reports have shown that recurrent ischemic priapism occurs until the effects of long‐acting injections diminish. 16 Therefore, confirming the absence of ischemic priapism during a certain period of treatment with oral medication is necessary before introducing long‐acting injections. In this case, we decided to switch from oral medication to long‐acting injections based on the reported onset timing of ischemic priapism. Owing to the limited number of reported cases of ischemic priapism, the precise period of onset from the initiation of oral medication has not been clearly determined. Consequently, we encountered difficulties in making definitive judgments when considering switching to medication.

Overall, our findings indicate that we are far from fully understanding the association between medications and ischemic priapism. Gathering a large number of reports on the occurrence of ischemic priapism may help clarify the differences in the risk of developing ischemic priapism by different antipsychotics, the relationship between antipsychotic dosage and the occurrence of ischemic priapism, and preferred timing of the onset of ischemic priapism. This would enable the issuance of guidelines for selecting antipsychotics in case of ischemic priapism.

CONCLUSION

The occurrence of ischemic priapism owing to antipsychotics is rare; however, it is a urological emergency, which requires immediate attention. Therefore, we must keep in mind the occurrence of serious adverse effects. Switching from an antipsychotic that causes ischemic priapism to one with a relatively low affinity for α1‐adrenergic receptors may enable the treatment of schizophrenia without the recurrence of ischemic priapism. Additionally, we suggest that the occurrence of ischemic priapism may be related to the affinity of drugs for α1‐adrenergic receptor, differences in metabolic enzyme types, and dosage of antipsychotics.

However, this is a single case, and it does not provide general guidelines for the association between medications and ischemic priapism. Further studies are necessary for proposing alternative treatments for ischemic priapism.
