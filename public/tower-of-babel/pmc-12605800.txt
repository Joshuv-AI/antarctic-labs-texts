Clinical Implications of Time to Culture Conversion in Mycobacterium Avium Complex Pulmonary Disease
Authors: Nakwon Kwak, Youngchan Cho, Jiin Bae, Joong-Yub Kim, Jae-Joon Yim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe optimal timing for assessing treatment response in Mycobacterium avium complex pulmonary disease (MAC-PD) remains unclear. This study evaluated the association between time to culture conversion and microbiological cure.MethodsWe retrospectively included MAC-PD patients treated at Seoul National University Hospital between January 2011 and April 2024 who achieved culture conversion within 12 months of treatment initiation. Patients were categorized by time to culture conversion: 0–30, 31–60, 61–90, 91–180, and 181–360 days. Multivariable logistic regression was used to assess the impact of conversion timing on microbiological cure.ResultsAmong 440 patients, 294 (66.8%) achieved microbiological cure. Compared with those who converted within 30 days, patients who converted at 91–180 days (adjusted odds ratio [aOR], 0.45; 95% CI, 0.24–.87; P = .017) and 181–360 days (aOR, 0.29; 95% CI, .14–.61; P = .001) were significantly less likely to achieve cure. No significant differences were observed for patients converting at 31–60 days (aOR, 1.02; P = .951) or 61–90 days (aOR, 1.19; P = .664), compared with the reference group.ConclusionsFailure to achieve culture conversion within 90 days was associated with a significantly lower likelihood of microbiological cure. These findings support the need for timely reassessment and potential modification of treatment strategies in patients who remain culture-positive beyond 90 days.Keywords: culture conversion, cure, mycobacterium avium complex

Mycobacterium avium complex (MAC) comprises 12 species, including Mycobacterium avium, Mycobacterium intracellulare, and Mycobacterium chimaera, and accounts for approximately 90% of the nontuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) isolated from humans [1]. MAC can cause chronic infectious diseases in humans, and MAC infections most frequently manifest as pulmonary disease (PD). The treatment of MAC-PD generally involves prolonged administration of a multidrug regimen that includes a macrolide and ethambutol [2, 3]. However, overall treatment outcomes remain suboptimal, with reported success rates of around 60% [4]. In addition, long-term treatment with multiple drugs is associated with a range of adverse effects, which often negatively impact treatment adherence [5, 6].

Nonetheless, treating MAC-PD with antibiotics has clinical benefits. For example, treatment has been shown to improve health-related quality of life [7] and, more importantly, prolong survival, particularly in patients with advanced disease [8]. This survival advantage is most notable among individuals who achieve microbiological cure, with studies showing that microbiological cure at the end of treatment is significantly associated with reduced mortality [9, 10]. Therefore, achieving microbiological cure is a key objective in the treatment of MAC-PD.

Microbiological cure is generally preceded by culture conversion, which is defined as at least three consecutive negative cultures [11]. Culture conversion typically occurs within 2–3 months of the initiation of treatment [12–15]. The absence of culture conversion 6 months after the initiation of treatment was previously determined to be indicative of treatment refractoriness or failure [16–18], and in 2018, the Nontuberculous Mycobacteria-Network European Trials Group (NTM-NET) extended this threshold to 12 months via a consensus statement [11]. However, deferring the determination of treatment failure could delay the strengthening of the treatment regimen or surgical resection in patients who might otherwise benefit from earlier intervention [11].

In this study, we aimed to investigate the association between time to culture conversion and treatment outcomes in patients with MAC-PD. We also aimed to determine the optimal time at which the treatment response should be assessed to ensure timely intervention.

METHODS

Study Population

Patients diagnosed with and treated for MAC-PD between 1 January 2011, and 30 April 2024, at Seoul National University Hospital was identified by screening the electronic health record system of our institution. All patients met the diagnostic criteria for NTM-PD [2, 3, 19], including clinical (pulmonary or systemic symptoms), radiologic (nodular or cavitary opacities on chest radiographs, or a high-resolution computed tomography [CT] scan showing bronchiectasis with multiple small nodules), and microbiological (positive cultures from at least two separate sputum, at least one bronchial wash or lavage, or lung biopsy with histologic features of mycobacterial infection and positive culture for NTM) criteria after exclusion of other disease. The causative organisms were confirmed as MAC via 16S rRNA and hsp65 sequencing [20, 21].

Patients who achieved culture conversion within 12 months of treatment initiation were included in the analysis. Patients who did not achieve culture conversion within 12 months (“treatment failure”) [11], whose treatment regimens did not align with current guidelines [2, 3], and who were infected with macrolide-resistant isolates were excluded from the analysis.

The study protocol adhered to the principles of the amended Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Seoul National University Hospital (No. 2505-014-1636). Some of the patients had participated in our previous research [6, 9, 14].

Variables

Data on demographic variables, including age, body mass index (BMI), and smoking status, were collected. Radiographic findings (presence of cavities) were interpreted by two chest radiologists, using chest CT scans obtained within the three months before treatment initiation. The presence of a cavity was defined as a gas-filled space that appeared as a lucent or low-attenuation area within pulmonary consolidation [22]. Data on microbiological variables were also collected (eg, species identity, acid-fast bacilli [AFB] smear status, and serial culture results during treatment). The antimycobacterial drugs included in the initial treatment regimen were also collected. All data were independently reviewed by three authors (YC, JB, and JYK). Any discrepancies were resolved and the final dataset was compiled by two additional authors (NK and JJY).

Definitions

Culture conversion was defined as at least three consecutive negative mycobacterial cultures obtained at intervals of at least 4 weeks [11]. Time to culture conversion was calculated as the interval between the date of treatment initiation and the date when the first sputum sample of the series that showed culture conversion was submitted. Microbiological cure was defined as the presence of multiple consecutive negative cultures with no intervening positive cultures for the causative organism following culture conversion and continuing until the end of antimycobacterial treatment [11]. Recurrence was defined as the presence of at least two positive cultures of the same species from respiratory specimens obtained after the completion of treatment [11]. Patients who were followed for at least 6 months and had at least two serial mycobacterial cultures after treatment completion were included in the recurrence analysis.

Statistical Analysis

Patients were classified into five groups according to time to culture conversion: 0–30, 31–60, 61–90, 91–180, and 181–360 days. Continuous and categorical variables are presented as median values with interquartile ranges (IQRs) and proportions, respectively. Trends across groups were analyzed using the Cochran–Armitage trend test for categorical variables and the Jonckheere–Terpstra test for continuous variables. To evaluate the association between time to culture conversion and microbiological cure, we performed multivariable logistic regression. The association between time to culture conversion and recurrence was measured using Cox proportional hazards regression. Adjusted odds ratios (aORs) or adjusted hazard ratios (aHRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were obtained after adjusting for clinically significant variables (age, sex, BMI, and presence of cavitary lesions) and statistically significant variables in a univariable analysis [23]. P values <.05 were considered significant, and all statistical analyses were conducted using Stata software (version 18, College Station, Texas, USA).

RESULTS

Patient Characteristics

Among the 549 patients who were treated for MAC-PD during the study period, 445 achieved culture conversion within 12 months of treatment initiation. After excluding five patients (three whose treatment did not include macrolides and two who were infected with macrolide-resistant strains), 440 patients were included in the final analysis. The median age of the cohort was 64 years (IQR, 57–71), and there were more female patients (N = 324, 73.6%) than male patients in the cohort. The median time to culture conversion was 40 days (IQR, 14–97), with the largest subgroup achieving culture conversion within 30 days (N = 184, 41.8%). A longer time to culture conversion was associated with AFB smear positivity (P for trend: < .001) and the presence of cavitary lesions (P for trend: .003) at the initiation of treatment. Treatment Modalities and Outcomes

Macrolides were administered to all patients. Ethambutol, rifampicin, clofazimine, inhaled amikacin, and parenterally delivered amikacin were administered to 425 (96.6%), 256 (58.2%), 87 (19.8%), 33 (7.5%), and 24 (5.5%) patients, respectively. Amikacin liposome inhalation suspension was not administered to any patients because it is not covered by insurance in South Korea [24]. The median total treatment duration was 518 days (IQR, 399–623), with longer durations observed in patients with delayed culture conversion (P for trend: < .001). After culture conversion, antibiotic therapy was continued for a median of 435 additional days (IQR, 352–548). Among the 440 patients with culture conversion, 294 (66.8%) achieved microbiological cure. Compared with the patients who achieved culture conversion within 90 days of treatment (134/184, 72.8%), the rate of microbiological cure was significantly lower in those who achieved culture conversion in 91–180 days (37/67, 55.2%) and 181–360 days (19/47, 40.4%) (P for trend: < .001; Table 2).

Impact of Culture Conversion

After adjusting for age, sex, BMI, presence of cavitary lesions, and AFB smear positivity, culture conversion after 91 days was significantly associated with a reduced likelihood of microbiological cure. Compared with the group of patients who achieved culture conversion within 30 days, the groups that contained patients who took longer to reach culture conversion were found to have the following aOR values: 31–60 days = 1.02 (95% CI, .55–1.89; P = .951), 61–90 days = 1.19 (95% CI, .55–2.59; P = .664), 91–180 days = 0.45 (95% CI, .24–.87; P = .017), and 181–360 days = 0.29 (95% CI, .14–.61; P = .001) (Table 3). Of the 294 patients who achieved microbiological cure, 177 were eligible for recurrence analysis (69 who converted in 0–30 days, 44 in 31–60 days, 20 in 61–90 days, 27 in 91–180 days, and 17 in 181–360 days). During the median follow-up period of 818 days (IQR, 284–1532), 40 patients (22.6%) experienced recurrence. In contrast to its impact on microbiological cure, the time taken to reach culture conversion did not significantly influence the risk of recurrence. DISCUSSION

In this study of 440 patients with MAC-PD, culture conversion after 90 days was associated with a significantly reduced likelihood of achieving microbiological cure. However, among those who ultimately achieved microbiological cure, the time it took to reach culture conversion did not affect the risk of recurrence. These findings suggest that earlier intervention may be necessary for patients with MAC-PD who do not achieve culture conversion within 90 days to improve the likelihood of microbiological cure.

While the treatment goals for MAC-PD have traditionally focused on the eradication of the causative bacteria, there is increasing interest in improving patient-reported outcomes [25, 26]. Nonetheless, achieving microbiological cure remains a critical goal, as it has been associated with improved survival [9, 10], and only 60% of patients reportedly achieve microbiological cure [4]. In addition, when culture negativity is maintained through the end of treatment, patient survival tends to improve significantly [9, 10]. Despite these findings, the clinical significance of time to culture conversion has not been thoroughly explored.

In cases of pulmonary tuberculosis (TB), another mycobacterial disease, time to culture conversion is a well-established predictor of long-term outcomes. Patients who achieve culture conversion within the first 2 months of therapy are more likely to experience treatment success at the end of treatment [27–29]. Earlier culture conversion has also been associated with a lower risk of relapse after treatment completion [30]. These findings have made earlier culture conversion a widely accepted surrogate marker of favorable outcomes in patients with TB [30]. In contrast, such prognostic benchmarks have not been clearly defined for NTM-PD, including MAC-PD.

In terms of evaluating the efficacy of MAC-PD treatment, a lack of culture conversion within six months of initiating treatment is commonly used as an indicator of treatment refractoriness [18, 31]. In a large cohort study conducted in the USA, 83% of the participants achieved culture conversion within 6 months of treatment initiation [32]. In the first randomized controlled trial that focused on MAC-PD, treatment failure was defined as the absence of culture conversion within 6 months of starting treatment [17]. In contrast, the NTM-NET defines treatment failure as the absence of culture conversion by 12 months [11]. However, our data suggest that culture conversion data available at earlier and more defined timepoints may better indicate treatment efficacy. More specifically, patients who achieved culture conversion within 30 days had significantly higher cure rates than those who reached culture conversion in 91–180 days or 181–360 days, whereas conversions within 31–60 days or 61–90 days were associated with similar probabilities of cure. These findings suggest that, for patients who do not achieve culture conversion in three months, modifying the therapeutic approach (eg, by including new drugs or performing surgical resection) may be warranted to improve the likelihood of microbiological cure [31].

Interestingly, among the patients in whom microbiological cure was achieved, the time taken to reach culture conversion did not affect the risk of recurrence. In contrast, delayed culture conversion (beyond 2 months) in patients with pulmonary TB is associated with a higher risk of recurrence [30]. Our results may be attributed to the fact that antibiotic therapy was continued for a median time of 435 days after culture conversion in our cohort. These findings support the strategy of continuing antibiotic treatment for 12 months following culture conversion to prevent recurrence [15, 33].

To fully interpret the results of this study, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design limited our ability to fully capture treatment decisions influenced by physician or patient preferences, even though all patients received macrolide-based therapy. Second, we were unable to assess the impact of culture conversion timing on patients’ health-related quality of life (HRQOL). As HRQOL may improve even in patients who do not achieve culture conversion [7], future studies should explore the association between the timing of culture conversion and HRQOL. Third, although we adjusted for factors known to affect recurrence, such as radiographic patterns and infecting species [34, 35], unmeasured factors including lifestyle and environmental exposures could not be accounted for. Nevertheless, the relatively large sample size of 440 patients supports the robustness and generalizability of our findings.

In conclusion, a lack of culture conversion within 90 days of treatment initiation was associated with a significantly lower likelihood of microbiological cure. Our findings provide new, clinically meaningful insights into the prognostic significance of time to culture conversion in MAC-PD. These findings also underscore the importance of timely reassessment and indicate that more proactive interventions should be considered for patients who do not achieve culture conversion within 90 days.
