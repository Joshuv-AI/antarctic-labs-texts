Hippocampal recording with a soft microelectrode array in a cranial window imaging scheme: a validation study
Authors: G. Juhász, M. Madarász, B. Szmola, F. Z. Fedor, Z. Balogh-Lantos, Á. Szabó, B. Rózsa, Z. Fekete
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The hippocampus has a crucial role in the formation, consolidation and recall of memories as well as in navigation related processes. These functions are in the focus of neuroscience and different disciplines have contributed to this research field for decades. Two-photon imaging in awake animals is a valuable new aspect for these observations, especially when it is supported by electrophysiology. In this study, we applied high speed two-photon hippocampal imaging through a chronically implanted, soft, transparent microelectrode (STM) device incorporated into a cranial window chamber in awake mice. We monitored the impedance of the recording sites over the course of the experiments to observe long-term changes in recording quality. The large-scale ipsilateral local field potential (LFP) recordings from the dorsal hippocampus provided reliable sharp wave-ripples (SPW-Rs), multi-unit activity (MUA) and single-unit activity (SUA) for up to two months. Calcium imaging of GCaMP6f. labeled cells from the CA1 pyramidal layer under the transparent device was possible even after six months in thy1-GCaMP6f. transgenic mice. We investigated the immune response with GFAP staining after the end of the long-term experiments. Based on our results, this dedicated transparent electrode device proved to be suitable for simultaneous two-photon imaging and large-scale electrophysiological measurements in chronic experiments in mice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-75170-1.

Introduction

The hippocampus has been extensively studied and assigned to several functions covering information encoding, memory consolidation and retrieval, as well as spatial memory. Being an embedded structure even in the murine brain, most of the in vivo studies were restricted to electrophysiological recordings (for references see1,2), then advancements in two-photon imaging technology allowed access to direct imaging of the dorsal hippocampus under physiological conditions3. Combining the two research fields would yield insight into complementing and detailed understanding of hippocampal function on the microcircuit level with cell-wise resolution of two-photon imaging and associated local field potential recordings. However, a handful of work has successfully utilized calcium imaging with contralateral LFP recordings4,5, while combined simultaneous ipsilateral recordings appeared only in recent years2,6.

In this study, we coupled high-speed two-photon hippocampal imaging with large-scale electrophysiological recordings by a transparent, shape memory polymer microelectrode device applied to the hippocampal surface in awake, head-fixed mice. The major advantage of the thiol-ene/acrylate based electrode grid is that its Young’s modulus is gradually reduced from the GPa range down to the 100 MPa range at glass transition temperature, which is engineered to be close to tissue temperature7. This softening property has been leveraged to create intracortical neural probes8,9 and has been proved to positively influence the immune response during long-term recordings10,11. This unique responsive substrate has been incorporated into ECoG devices12,13, and has been successfully tested to perform intracranial recordings in the awake mice. In the present experimental scheme, the capability of the device technology is challenged, and it is utilized to record hippocampal activity, while providing access to calcium imaging through the transparent substrate.

The electrochemical properties of the device were monitored weekly over the course of the experiments and physiological signals such as SPW-Rs and MUA/SUA were identified from the LFP recordings of the CA1 region. We found discrete topographical appearance of SPW-Rs and electrophysiological activity to be stable for at least up to two months. Calcium imaging of GCaMP6f. labeled cells in the CA1 region was possible even after 6 months while immunohistological analysis revealed only modest alterations. These decent properties of the shape memory polymer thiol-ene/acrylate device prove its potential and applicability in large-scale ipsilateral electrophysiological recordings in combination with two-photon imaging for long term experiments, which is particularly desired in the recent multimodal neuroimaging experiments14.

Methods

The thiol-ene/acrylate based microelectrode device

The material of the soft microelectrode array was selected such that it can withstand the folded state through multiple steps in the craniotomy necessary to provide electrical connection from the external connector to the recording array. Our previous studies indicated that thiol/ene-acrylate based substrates may be resilient against this harsh use12,13, and they may be combined with the large working distance arrangement required by the in vivo hippocampal imaging scheme in mice. The device consisted of a sputtered iridium oxide conductive film embedded in a 9 μm thick top and bottom insulating layer. The insulation is composed of a seven μm thick thiol/ene-acrylate layer and a two μm thick Parylene C layer, where the latter one provides the leak-free barrier that helps to maintain stable electrical properties in liquid media. The diameter of the single recording site of the 31-channel array is 115 μm, and the distance between adjacent sites is 400 μm. The microfabrication of each device was performed at Qualia Labs, Inc. in agreement with a previously described recipe12, while the encapsulation of the microelectrode array with a proper through-hole electrical connector is also published in our prior work9. Electrochemical characterization

The stability of the thiol-ene/acrylate based STM devices prior to the in vivo experiment was tested in detail with electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) and presented in a previous paper12. From one week after surgery, electrode impedance was measured between 1 and 5000 Hz by connecting the implanted device to a 32-channel RHD recording headstage and RHD2000 Evaluation system (INTAN Technologies, Los Angeles, CA, USA). The number of working channels was determined based on the regular impedance measurements up to ten weeks. The chosen acceptance criteria were a minimum of 1 kΩ and a maximum of 1 MΩ impedance during the whole measurement period.

Surgery

Transgenic mice

All experiments were performed in accordance with the guidelines and approval of the Animal Care and Experimentation Committee of the Institute of Experimental Medicine (approval reference numbers PEI/001/194-4/2014 and PEI/001/1771-2/2015). All procedures complied with Hungarian and European regulations for animal research, as well as with the guidelines of Society for Neuroscience. Transgenic adult mice mice (16–54 weeks old) of both sexes (n = 4 males, n = 3 females) were used (FVB.129p2—Pde6b + Tyrc—ch:Tg (Thy1—Gcamp6f.)/EMGTU) expressing GCaMP6f. under the Thy1 promoter, which predominantly labels pyramidal cells15. All animals were sourced from the animal facility of HUN-REN Institute of Experimental Medicine. Animals were housed in a temperature-controlled environment (24 ± 1 °C) on a 12 h reverse light cycle (dark period between 08:00 and 20:00) and humidity between 40 and 70% and were kept in small groups (2–4 mice/homecage) in enriched environment with cardboard rolls, rotary discs, and extra nesting material (sizzle pet). The mice had ad-libitum access to food and water.

Surgical procedures

The surgical process was similar to that described previously6 with some modifications. Briefly, mice were anaesthetised deeply with a fentanyl/midazolam cocktail and placed on a heating pad (Supertech TMP-5b-SFAC-5, 37 °C). Dexamethasone (Baxter Healthcare Corp., 0.2 mg/kg) and Baytril (Bayer, 200 µl/10 g bodyweight) were administered subcutaneously to reduce brain edema and local tissue inflammation. Ropivacaine (Astra-Zeneca, 0.2%, 0.05 ml) was applied subcutaneously over the skull prior to the surgery. An ophthalmic ointment was applied to the eyes to prevent drying during surgery. A custom-made stainless-steel head-plate was attached to the skull using dental cement (C&B Superbond) for head-fixation. Subsequently, a 3 mm craniotomy was made above the hippocampus using a dental drill with some extension towards the lambda to make place for the electrode grid. The overlying cortex was carefully removed by aspiration (Aspiret, National Industrial Corporation) and the vessels were sealed with a bipolar coagulator (Diatrom 80, Fazzini).

The thiol-ene/acrylate microelectrode device was then gently placed onto the hippocampal surface kept down by a custom-made deep brain adapter. The adapter was a cured silicone elastomer mixture (Sylgard 184 silicone elastomer base and curing agent kit, Lot: 0008444444, Dow Corning Gmbh, USA), cylindrical in shape with a diameter of 3 mm and a height of 1.2–1.4 mm. Details of the preparation of the adapter can be found in6. Briefly, the two liquid components of Sylgard 184 were mixed manually with a spatula. Undesirable air cavities introduced by stirring were removed with needles or by waiting. Then the mixture was poured into a Petri dish, filling it to an approximate height of 1.2–1.4 mm, and kept at 125 °C until it solidified (~ 25 min). After curing, cylinders were cut out with a 3 mm biopsy punch and measured with a caliper. Sizes between 1.2 and 1.4 mm were kept for implantation. During surgery, cylinders were hand selected to best fit the particular animal, accounting for individual variations in cortical and cranial thickness. On one end of the cylinders, a 4 mm diameter round coverslip (Glaswarenfabrik Karl Hecht GmbH & Co KG) was glued eccentrically with UV curable adhesive (Norland Products). This eccentric positioning left space for guiding the flexible part of the electrode on the side of the craniotomy out to the skull surface. The Omnetics connector of the electrode grid was then positioned to the contralateral side of the head and held in place with dots of cyano/acrilate glue (Superbond, Loctite) and luting cement (3 M ESPE RelyX, 3 M) while a small craniotomy was made above the cerebellum using a small drill-bit (0.3 mm diameter) for a simple pin electrode to be implanted as reference. The Omnetics connector was then fixed to the skull tilted at an angle of ~ 45° to leave the required space for the two-photon imaging objective (Nikon 16 × CFI LWD Plan Fluorite Objective with 0.8 NA and 3 mm WD, N16XLWD-PF). At the end of the surgery, the animals were given a wake-up cocktail (Revertor: CP-Pharma GmbH, 2.5 mg/10 g bodyweight; Flumazenil: Fresenius Kabi GmbH, 2.5 mg/10 g bodyweight; Nexodal, G.L. Pharma GmbH, 1.2 mg/10 g bodyweight) to aid recovery and were treated with Ringer’s lactate solution and Melosolute for the next 3 days (CP‑Pharma GmbH, 1 mg/10 g bodyweight) to minimize postoperative discomfort. Baytril (Bayer, 2.5%, 200 μl/10 g bodyweight) was administered to reduce tissue inflammation.

Recording sessions

Mice were habituated to the recording environment and to head fixation before the measurements. Impedance measurements for the electrochemical characterisation of the implanted STM devices were taken in a closed, sound insulated box, with no lighting. Two-photon imaging with simultaneous LFP recordings were performed with an acousto-optical microscope (FEMTO3D Atlas Plug & Play, Femtonics) and mice were placed on a custom-designed linear treadmill16, Figure S10). Trainings and experiments were conducted during the dark cycle of animals.

Electrophysiology recording and analysis

Local field potentials were recorded using the RHD2132 amplifier board (Intan Technologies) with an amplifier bandwidth from 0.1 Hz to 7.5 kHz and sampled at 20 kHz. For detection of sharp wave-ripples, we set the two cut-off frequencies to 150 Hz and 250 Hz and used the difference between the two Gaussian filters. Only channels with the highest ripple band LFP power and events exceeding 25 ms and 4 SD over the baseline were analyzed in a MATLAB-based program (Data Analysis Suite, https://github.com/BenceSzmola/DataAnalysisSuite). Signals were discarded when they were also present on the reference channels. The automatic detections were manually curated to exclude artifacts.

For the spike detection, Kilosort3 spike sorting algorithm was used, followed by manual curation of the data using Phy software. The spike clusters were then categorized into multi-unit activity (MUA) and single-unit activity (SUA) based on waveform characteristics, amplitude distribution, and auto-correlogram analysis. In order to provide a comprehensive evaluation of the spike sorting performance, a supplementary figure (Fig. S1), has been included which displays several quality metrics for all detected units, calculated with Spikeinterface.

To calculate the auto-correlograms and assess the signal-to-noise ratio, we utilized Spikeinterface along with custom Python and MATLAB scripts. The signal was determined as the maximum amplitude of the spike waveforms, while the noise was defined as the standard deviation of the noise present on the same channel. For visualizing the waveforms, Spikes (Cortex-lab) and MATLAB scripts were employed, whereas custom MATLAB scripts were utilized to compute parameters such as firing rate, amplitude distribution, number of spikes, and real amplitudes.

In order to distinguish between the various cell types, putative pyramidal cells, narrow-waveform interneurons, and wide-waveform interneurons were classified based on two criteria: the trough-to-peak time of the waveform and the burstiness index (BI) which was calculated based on the autocorrelograms of the recorded single units. The threshold for determining burstiness was set according to the literature17.

Immunostaining and analysis

Tissue preparation

Immunohistochemical staining was performed approximately six months after the surgical procedure. The analysis consisted of five mice implanted with the soft transparent microelectrode array combined with a silicone cylinder whilst one mouse was only implanted with a transcortical silicone cylinder without the array and served as secondary control during the analysis. We excluded one animal from the immunohistochemical analysis because, unfortunately, it died before the measurements were completed. After approximately six months after the implantation, mouse was deeply anaesthetized with isoflurane (Isoflurane, 0.5–1% Forane, Abbott), then transcardially perfused first with Ringer’s solution (Braun), then with 4% (w/v) paraformaldehyde for 20 min. Following the removal of the cylinder and the detachment of the microelectrode array, brains were explanted, then postfixed (4% paraformaldehyde). Brains were cryoprotected in a solution of 20% sucrose in 0.1 M PB overnight before coronal Sects. (40 μm, cryo) were cut on a sliding microtome (Leica SM2010 R) from the surgical area. Coronal sections were kept in 24-well plates in 1% PBS containing 0.3% Sodium Azide until immunohistochemical staining.

Antibody characterization

Free-floating sections were washed 3 times for 10 min with 1X PBS, then blocked for an hour at room temperature in a blocking buffer (5% Bovine Serum Albumin and Goat Serum (Thermo Scientific) containing 0.5% Triton-X (Sigma) for membrane permeabilization). After blocking and following 3 subsequent 10-min washing steps, first with 0.3% Triton-X then with 1% PBS, GFAP primary antibody (1:2500, chicken anti-GFAP, #PA1-10004, Invitrogen) was applied directly. Slices were kept at 4 °C overnight before additional 3 subsequent 10-min washing steps and the application of the species-specific secondary antibody (1:200, goat anti-chicken Alexa Fluor 647, #A-21449, Invitrogen). Unbound secondary antibodies were then washed off with 1X PBS (3 × 10 min). Sections were mounted using Fluoromount-G mounting medium containing DAPI (#00–4,959-52, Invitrogen) to co-label total cell nuclei. The slides were stored at 4 °C in darkness until immunofluorescent imaging was performed the next day.

Image analysis

Immunohistological analysis consisted of five animals implanted with the device and cylinder and one control animal implanted with only the cylinder. Fluorescent images were acquired using a Slide Scanner (Pannoramic MIDI II, 3DHISTECH) equipped with a 20X objective under blue (DAPI filter), green (FITC filter) and far red (Cy5 filter) illumination. The digital images were processed in CaseViewer (3DHISTECH) and were exported at 300 dpi in TIFF file format. Histological slices that were determined to originate from the implantation area by comparison of surgical coordinates and the slices’ position from bregma, according to The Mouse Brain atlas, were included in the analysis (Franklin and Paxinos 200118). Twelve Regions of Interest (ROI) were chosen from each animal from either the implanted and non-implanted hemisphere. We were interested in the differences between the animals’ implanted and non-implanted hemispheres as well as the cellular differences caused by the implantation of the device, therefore we performed the surgery without electrode implantation that served as secondary control. As the main focus of the histological analysis was to shed light to the cellular changes caused by the implantation of the device, ROIs (rectangular, 40 μm × 30 μm) were placed just below the surgical area arranged so that the CA1 pyramidal region of the hippocampus could be located in the middle of the ROI. Three main questions were to be answered via histological analysis: Were the cell population affected by the implantation? As the CA1 pyramidal layer plays a pivotal role in the processing of hippocampus-dependent memory and was almost directly below the implantation site, did it suffer any damage related to the implantation? Was there a prominent gliotic activity after six months’ of implantation? We aimed to answer these questions via observing the overall cell number, the cell number in the CA1 pyramidal region of the hippocampus and the astrocyte cell number using the QuantCenter plugin of 3DHISTECH’s SlideViewer program. Significance calculations were performed using Student’s t-tests (two-tailed, paired), comparing to the control hemisphere, and a two-tailed two-sample heteroscedastic Student’s t-test was used for comparison with the control animal implanted without an electrode. In our statistical analysis, we used the standard methodology and considered results with p-values less than 0.05 to be significant.

Two-photon imaging and analysis

Two-photon calcium imaging was performed with an acousto-optical microscope (FEMTO3D Atlas Plug & Play, Femtonics) equipped with an Alcor 920-4 laser (Spark Lasers), an H10770B-40 PMT (Hamamatsu) and a CFI75LWD 16 × objective (Nikon). Fluorescence emission was detected in the 490–550 nm range using a ET520/60m-2p dichroic mirror (Chroma). Calcium activity was recorded from the hippocampus under the implanted STM device with chessboard scanning16. ROIs were placed in regions between electrode traces and sites and recorded with chessboard scanning (63–111 Hz). Measurements were recorded and analyzed with MES software (Femtonics) running on MATLAB 2020b (Mathworks). Imaging data was analysed with MES (Femtonics). Active cells were manually labeled as ROIs. Traces were filtered with a gauss kernel of 4 and dF/F0 was calculated with F0 being the gauss average of the 500 neighbouring frames for each frame.

Hippocampal LFP recordings with the STM device

The key hallmark of the hippocampal LFP during immobility, drinking, eating and grooming is the appearance of SPW-Rs, while theta oscillations dominate LFP activity of the walking animal1. Besides weekly recordings for monitoring electrochemical properties of the device, LFP recordings were taken and used for SPW-R and MUA/SUA detection. In order to prove the capability of the device in chronic electrophysiological recordings, SPW-Rs detected on identical channels were compared longitudinally (Fig. 3A–C). SPW-Rs can arise from any site of the hippocampal CA1 region and propagate along the septotemporal axis depending on ripple magnitude (Patel et al., 201319). Our recordings with the STM device from the surface of the dorsal hippocampus further support these statements (Figs. 4 and S2). Recording of single-unit activity

For seven weeks, the data collected from a single animal consistently showed spikes. The recordings included both multi-unit and single-unit activity. To distinguish between the various cell types, putative pyramidal cells, narrow-waveform interneurons, and wide-waveform interneurons were classified based on the trough-to-peak time of the waveform and the burstiness index. The clustering resulted in an average of 24.01% ± 7.8% of the cells being classified as narrow-waveform interneurons, 52.15% ± 14.63% as wide-waveform interneurons and 23.84% ± 14.24% as pyramidal cells. The distribution of detected cell types over the seven weeks is summarized in Table S1. The mean burstiness index for the different neuron types was 1.267 ± 0.208 for narrow-waveform interneurons, 1.033 ± 0.142 for wide-waveform interneurons, and 1.426 ± 0.273 for pyramidal cells. These values indicate that pyramidal cells tend to have a higher burstiness index than interneurons, reflecting their well-known burst firing patterns. Figure 5A displays the waveforms of 82 spikes, along with their average, extracted from a cluster of a putative pyramidal cell that exhibited single-unit activity. Figure 5B shows the auto-correlogram of this cluster, while Fig. 5C presents the firing rate and spontaneous firing of the single-unit activity (SUA) cluster. The investigation involved the analysis of spike clusters recorded over seven weeks using all available channels. The number of detected clusters varied based on recording quality and cell firing rates. The signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) was assessed over the seven weeks of recordings (see Fig. 5D). The signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) fell below 1 in week five, although it was also within the same range as in weeks one and three. Figures 4E,F show the mean amplitude and mean number of spikes observed in long-term recordings, respectively. The amplitude decreased over time, while the number of spikes increased. Additionally, Fig. 5G illustrates the average count of spike clusters detected in weekly recordings, which varied over the seven weeks. In addition to the overall analysis, a detailed examination was carried out on the spike clusters identified on two specific channels during long-term recordings. This enabled a detailed examination of the characteristics of neuronal activity over an extended period. Figure 5H illustrates the spike amplitude for the two identified spike clusters. Figure 5I presents the corresponding firing rates for each cluster. It is noteworthy that the spike clusters under examination are in close alignment with the average amplitude and spike count data presented in Fig. 5E,F. We leveraged the flexible 3D scanning capability of the AO microscope and used chessboard scanning in multiple planes within the selected FOV. Immunohistological evaluation

In order to map the long-term effects of electrode implantation, we examined cellular changes using immunohistochemical markers. The analysis was based on labeling of cell nuclei (DAPI) and astrocytes (GFAP) in six mice. We identified 12 regions of interest (ROIs) beneath the implantation site and in the corresponding area of the opposite hemisphere, which served as our primary control (Fig. 7/B-G) for each of the 5 implanted animals included. Additionally, one animal was implanted with a cylinder alone, without an electrode, to determine whether the implantation of the electrode caused any additional damage (Fig. 7/A). One implanted animal was excluded from immunohistological evaluation due to early termination without perfusion. Fundamentally, we sought answers to three questions during the analysis: Did the implantation influence the total cell count? Did the CA1 pyramidal layer of the hippocampus, which lay nearly directly beneath the electrode, sustain damage? Initially, we compared data from the implanted hemisphere of the five animals with data from the intact hemisphere. In all four animals implanted with electrodes, there was a significant increase in the number of astrocytes (p = 7.71E-14), correlating with an increase in total cell count (p = 3.52E-09), and significant cell death was observed in the CA1 pyramidal cells (p = 2.88E-14). Similar changes were observed in the control animal implanted only with a cylinder, without an electrode (p = 0.01, 0.04, 0.04, respectively).

Subsequently, we compared data from the implanted hemisphere of the experimental animals with data from the operated hemisphere of the control animal without an electrode to observe whether electrode implantation enhanced cellular changes. In this case, contrary to comparison with its own control hemisphere, astrocyte activity significantly decreased (n°1: p = 9.64E-10, n°2: p = 0.01, n°3: p = 0.00022, n°4: 0.0078, n°5: 1.02E-07) and this trend was followed by a significant decrease in total cell count in three out of four animals, though not significantly in two (n°1: p = 2.56E-6, n°2: p = 0.00026, n°3: p = 0.0066, n°4: 0.45, n°5: 0.074). The number of cells counted in the CA1 pyramidal cell layer decreased in all cases except one (animal n°5), of which only one was significant (n°1: p = 0.0011, n°2: p = 0.1, n°3: p = 0.4, n°4: 0.25, n°5: 0.19) (Fig. S5).

Discussion

Long-term recordings with an intracranially implanted shape memory polymer based soft electrode grid has been presented by our group12. In that experiment, the ECoG device exhibited a stable electrical performance even ten weeks after the implantation. In our current study the soft nature of the device is leveraged to a greater extent, since the substrate is folded at 90 degrees at two locations by the silicone cylinder (see Fig. 1C inset) and at three further points during the fixation with dental cement. As being attached to the silicone cylinder, it is preserved in this position. Based on our in vivo impedance tests carried out on four animals, the majority of the channels remained usable (Fig. 2B) until at least seven weeks, while the absolute value of their impedance measured at 1 kHz frequency was also found sufficient to continue the recordings (Fig. 2C). The average electrode yield was 59% compared to the 75% of the previously publicated cortical experiment12. The implantation to the cortex with minimal folding showed no additional electrode loss. During this current experiment, we found after the 10 weeks of impedance tracking a less than 6% additional decrease in the electrode yield. Similarly to the recent work of Liu et al. based on a 16-channel graphene/PET electrode2, our dataset provided firm evidence on the feasibility to detect sharp wave ripples in the hippocampus in this experimental scheme. However, the device was not designed to record single unit activity, we also proved that recording of action potentials is possible at sufficient quality. To optimise the performance of SUA recordings using our technology, recording site area, and inter-site distance should be reduced, while channel numbers should be increased (Khodagholy et al., 2014). A larger density of recording sites will also improve localization of the signal sources within the hippocampus20, however, high-density arrays also limit the field of view for optical imaging. This trade-off could be partially addressed by the application of transparent wiring based on e.g., indium-tin-oxide21 or PEDOT:PSS22. The STM device, even with the current density of channels and size of the recording sites, would allow detection of subtle spatiotemporal changes both in electrophysiological and calcium signals in the early stages of progressive disorders.

One of the specific aims to integrate the STM device into the high-working-distance cranial chamber was to maintain optical access to the exposed hippocampal regions, while recording brain potentials. Our observation on the quality of calcium signals detected through the transparent grid substrate in the str. pyramidale of the hippocampus confirmed that our measurement scheme relying on the parallel use of the STM device facilitated the fluorescent signal acquisition for six months after the implantation. This time period is in agreement with the published data in our previous report on the use of such implant for multimodal imaging on the intact cortical surface13.

Immunohistochemical examination revealed that the implantation of the electrode caused significant cellular changes in the brains of the examined mice following approximately six months of chronic implantation. In terms of the effect of electrode implantation surgery, it was associated with increased gliotic activity and a decrease in cell population in the hippocampal CA1 region. When comparing surgery with and without implantation, no significant difference was observed in the total number of cells and the changes in the CA1 region. However, it can be stated that during surgery without implantation, astrocyte activity was significantly lower. Although gliotic activity increased, gliotic scar tissue did not develop during the observed period, which would have isolated the electrode from the brain tissue, thus the quality of the measurements could have been maintained. Furthermore, the fact that gliotic activity increased when compared in all of the cases when comparing with the contralateral hemisphere, leads us to the conclusion supported by our previous work12,23 that the surgical procedure greatly influences cellular organisation, and the changes observed are not solely caused by the implantation of the STM device.

Our study highlighted the first successful chronic application of a soft microelectrode grid incorporated in a hippocampal cranial chamber to record sharp wave-ripples, action potentials, while facilitating optical access to measure calcium signals through the transparent substrate layer. The chronically implanted shape memory polymer thiol-ene/acrylate device provided stable electrophysiological signals from the surface of the dorsal hippocampus, including SPW-Rs and MUAs/SUAs, for several weeks with preserved transparency for two-photon imaging up to six months in GCaMP6f. expressing transgenic mice. These valuable features make the STM device a strong candidate for future studies when long term electrophysiological monitoring and simultaneous calcium imaging might support discoveries, such as epilepsy research or drug development in neurodegenerative diseases.
