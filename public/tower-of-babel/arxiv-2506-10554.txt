Abstract
The acquisition of Downlink (DL) channel state information at the transmitter (CSIT) is known to be a challenging task in multiuser massive MIMO systems when uplink/downlink channel reciprocity does not hold (e.g., in frequency division duplexing systems). From a coding viewpoint, the DL channel state acquired at the users via DL training can be seen as an information source that must be conveyed to the base station via the UL communication channels. The transmission of a source through a channel can be accomplished either by separate or joint source-channel coding (SSCC or JSCC). In this work, using classical remote distortion-rate (DR) theory, we first provide a theoretical lower bound on the channel estimation mean-square-error (MSE) of both JSCC and SSCC-based feedback schemes, which however requires encoding of large blocks of successive channel states and thus cannot be used in practice since it would incur in an extremely large feedback delay. We then focus on the relevant case of minimal (one slot) feedback delay and propose a practical JSCC-based feedback scheme that fully exploits the channel second-order statistics to optimize the dimension projection in the eigenspace. We analyze the large SNR behavior of the proposed JSCC-based scheme in terms of the quality scaling exponent (QSE). Given the second-order statistics of channel estimation of any feedback scheme, we further derive the closed-form of the lower bound to the ergodic sum-rate for DL data transmission under maximum ratio transmission and zero-forcing precoding. Via extensive numerical results, we show that our proposed JSCC-based scheme outperforms known JSCC, SSCC baseline and deep learning-based schemes and is able to approach the performance of the optimal DR scheme in the range of practical SNR.



I
Introduction

Accurate and timely channel state information at the transmitter (CSIT) is crucial for massive MIMO systems to achieve the benefits of the large dimensional antenna array, such as high beamforming gain and large spectral efficiency
[
1
,
2
]
. The uplink (UL) CSI can be estimated at the base station (BS) by receiving the pilot signals from the users in UL. However, the CSI acquisition in downlink (DL) is generally more difficult to obtain, especially in systems where
UL/DL channel reciprocity does not hold. Examples of such systems are frequency division duplexing (FDD) systems, systems with non-reciprocal/calibrated radios, and systems such as IEEE 802.11ax, where the UL and DL scheduler make independent and possibly random/access decisions, such that it is not guaranteed that each DL frame is preceded by a UL frame carrying timely CSI. For such systems, the DL CSI at the BS is obtained by explicit DL probing (via pilots) and closed-loop feedback.

I-A

Related Work

In the literature, most works consider codebook-based quantized CSI feedback where the CSI is encoded into bits by the users and transmitted back and decoded at the BS
[
2
,
3
]
. We refer to this “classical codebook-based feedback” separated source-channel coding feedback (SSCC-feedback) scheme, which is also used in the current 3rd Generation Partnership Project (3GPP) standard and industry release, see e.g.,
[
4
,
5
]
. Nevertheless, these works avoid discussing the difficulty of SSCC-feedback with respect to channel coding, which is essential since the codebook-based feedback scheme requires sending bits through the UL, which must be received with high reliability and low delay before the channel changes significantly. As an alternative to SSCC-feedback, the joint source-channel coding feedback (JSCC-feedback) scheme (also known as the “analog” feedback that was first introduced in
[
6
]
and recently studied in
[
7
,
8
]
) is based on the direct mapping of the received training signal onto a UL modulated signal and direct mapping of the noisy UL signal into the channel estimate at the BS, which can be implemented directly in the “low physical layer” without the need of piggybacking the CSI bits into a UL codeword
[
9
,
10
]
, resulting in much lower complexity and delay for the JSCC feedback.

A particular challenge of CSI feedback in modern wideband massive MIMO systems is the prohibitively large channel dimension due to the large antenna array and large number of subcarriers. In order to guarantee the UL data transmission rate, the DL CSI feedback signal should not cost too much in terms of the UL channel resources. This typically requires some form of compression (aka, lossy source coding). Designing the feedback strategies under limited feedback resources has received significant attention in recent years, where the proposed solutions typically depend on the deep learning technology (e.g.,
[
11
]
) to overcome the high computational complexity of traditional CSI feedback methods, such as codebook-based (e.g.,
[
2
]
) and compression sensing-based (e.g.,
[
12
]
) methods, see more references in
[
13
]
.
A deep neural network (DNN)-based JSCC feedback scheme was first proposed in
[
14
]
using a convolutional neural network (CNN).
More recently, a state-of-the-art deep learning-based approach has incorporated attention mechanisms in both the encoder and decoder functions
[
15
]
.
A more general deep JSCC (DJSCC) framework with signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) adaption for CSI feedback is proposed in
[
16
]
. In
[
17
]
, JSCC combined with DNN is also considered to solve the channel aging problem due to feedback delay.
Nevertheless, the DNN-based approaches generally have severe disadvantages and limitations. First, the DNN-based works often assume perfect CSI at the user for compression and feedback, which is an unrealistic assumption in massive MIMO systems with large channel dimensions. Second, DNN models are typically trained on limited environmental scenarios and they are highly dependent on training data sets, resulting in weak generalization ability.
In addition, DNN-based works do not explicitly incorporate second-order statistics.
To the best of our knowledge, the only DNN-based work that exploits the second-order statistics of the channel is our recent work
[
18
]
, where it has been shown that even the Linear JSCC scheme outperforms the seminal deep-learning-based work in
[
19

IV
Practical JSCC and SSCC Feedback Schemes

Although the remote distortion-rate function
D_{k}^{\rm r}(R)
is a lower bound for any feedback schemes in (
9
) and (
12
), it requires encoding blocks of
n
DL pilot observations together via high-dimensional vector quantizers.
Beyond its high complexity, the main problem that makes such an approach inapplicable for the CSI feedback is that a block-feedback message can be transmitted only after a very large number
n
of DL training signals is received. At this point, even though the joint compression and transmission of the block results in a low channel estimation MSE at the BS, these estimates are completely useless since they are stale. As a matter of fact, the CSI estimation and feedback loop must have a low overall delay, such that the BS receives an updated CSI estimate at each coherence interval of
T
OFDM symbols.
We refer to such schemes as block-by-block (minimum delay) estimation and feedback schemes.
Focusing on such schemes, in this section, we study the practical JSCC and SSCC schemes aiming at encoding only short block-length symbols. We first introduce the classical SSCC and JSCC schemes, and then propose a novel JSCC scheme.

IV-A

IV-C

Truncated Karkune-Loève (TKL) JSCC Scheme

Although the LJSCC scheme requires less channel second-order statistics knowledge and simple signal processing, its performance could be worse especially under limited feedback dimension since it treats all dimensions equally. Therefore, in this paper, we propose a novel JSCC feedback that fully exploits the channel second-order statistics.

In the proposed TKL scheme, we adopt the non-linear encoder function, where the received measurements are first mapped into its eigenspace and then truncated based on the eigenvalues, and finally the power allocation of the truncated signal is optimized to minimize the estimation error. Concretely, we start by finding a proper format of the eigenspace of the covariance matrix of
{{\bf y}_{k}^{\rm tr}}
. First, we write the singular value decomposition (SVD) of
{\bf X}{\bf C}_{{\bf h}_{k}}^{\frac{1}{2}}
as

\displaystyle{\bf X}{\bf C}_{{\bf h}_{k}}^{\frac{1}{2}}={\bf U}_{k}\hbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{k}{\bf Q}_{k}^{{\sf H}},

(38)

where the main diagonal of
\hbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{k}\in\mathbb{C}^{\beta_{\rm tr}\times MN}
contains the singular values in the descending order, and where
{\bf U}_{k}\in\mathbb{C}^{\beta_{\rm tr}\times\beta_{\rm tr}}
and
{\bf Q}_{k}\in\mathbb{C}^{MN\times MN}
are the corresponding left-singular vectors and right-singular vectors, respectively. Then, with (
38
) the eigenvalue decomposition of the covariance matrix of
{{\bf y}_{k}^{\rm tr}}
is derived as

\displaystyle{\bf C}_{{\bf y}_{k}^{\rm tr}}

\displaystyle={\bf U}_{k}\hbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{k}\hbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{k}^{{\sf H}}{\bf U}_{k}^{{\sf H}}+{\bf I}_{\beta_{\rm tr}}

\displaystyle={\bf U}_{k}(\widebar{\hbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}}_{k}+{\bf I}_{\beta_{\rm tr}}){\bf U}_{k}^{{\sf H}},

(39)

where
\widebar{\hbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}}_{k}\triangleq\hbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{k}\hbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{k}^{{\sf H}}
.
Given (
39
), the TKL scheme transforms the received pilot signal into the eigenspaces and truncates at the first
\beta_{\rm fb}
dominant subspace. Specifically, the feedback symbols can be obtained by

\displaystyle\widehat{{\bf z}}_{k}

\displaystyle={\text{diag}}(\sqrt{\hbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}_{k}}){\bf S}_{k}{\bf U}_{k}^{{\sf H}}{\bf y}^{\rm tr}_{k},

(40)

where
{\bf S}_{k}\in\{0,1\}^{\beta_{\rm fb}\times\beta_{\rm tr}}
is the selection matrix with
{\bf S}_{i,i}=1,\forall i\in[\beta_{\rm fb}]
and zeros otherwise,

8

8

Note that the TKL scheme focus on the case with limited feedback dimension, i.e.,
\beta_{\rm fb}\leq\beta_{\rm tr}
and thus

is a fat matrix with
{\bf S}_{k}{\bf S}_{k}^{{\sf H}}={\bf I}_{\beta_{\rm fb}}
.

\hbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}_{k}\geq 0
is the scaling vector to satisfy the power constraint (

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\|\widehat{{\bf z}}_{k}\|_{2}^{2}]

\displaystyle=\mathsf{tr}({\text{diag}}(\sqrt{\hbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}_{k}}){\bf S}_{k}(\widebar{\hbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}}_{k}+{\bf I}_{\beta_{\rm fb}}){\bf S}_{k}^{{\sf H}})

(41)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\beta_{\rm fb}}\alpha_{k,i}(\widebar{\lambda}_{k,i}+1)\leq P_{\rm ul}.

(42)

\displaystyle\widehat{{\bf y}}_{k}^{\rm fb}

\displaystyle=\widehat{{\bf z}}_{k}+{\bf n}_{k}^{\rm ul}

\displaystyle{\bf C}_{{\bf h}_{k}\widehat{{\bf y}}^{\rm fb}_{k}}

\displaystyle={\text{diag}}({\hbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}_{k}}){\bf S}_{k}(\widebar{\hbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}}_{k}+{\bf I}_{\beta_{\rm tr}}){\bf S}_{k}^{{\sf H}}+{\bf I}_{\beta_{\rm fb}}.

\displaystyle D_{k}^{\rm tkl}(\beta_{\rm fb},\mathsf{snr}_{\rm ul})=\mathsf{tr}({\bf C}_{{\bf h}_{k}}-{\bf C}_{\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k}^{\rm tkl}}),

\displaystyle{\bf C}_{\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k}^{\rm tkl}}={\bf C}_{{\bf h}_{k}\widehat{{\bf y}}^{\rm fb}_{k}}{\bf C}_{\widehat{{\bf y}}^{\rm fb}_{k}}^{-1}{\bf C}_{{\bf h}_{k}\widehat{{\bf y}}^{\rm fb}_{k}}^{{{\sf H}}}.

Remark 3

(Comparable complexity of TKL and LJSCC)

Since the compressed feedback dimension typically satisfies
MN\gg\beta_{\rm fb}^{2}
, the SVD operation in (
38
) dominates the overall computational complexity. Hence, the total complexity of the TKL scheme is
O(\beta_{\rm tr}(MN)^{2})
. However, the SVD computation depends solely on the channel’s second-order statistics, which only need to be updated when these statistics undergo significant changes. The second-order channel statistics remain constant over a much longer period, known as the channel covariance/geometry coherence time, which is typically
100
to
1000
times longer than the channel coherence time
[
30
, Section I]
.
This means that the high computational SVD of TKL scheme need to be much less frequently performed compared to CSI estimation, so that their computational cost becomes negligible in long-term periods.
At each time of CSI estimation, only a linear encoder function in (
40
) needs to be applied that is with the same complexity order to the encoder function of LJSCC in (
28
).
As a result, TKL has comparable low complexity to LJSCC on average.

VII
Conclusion

In this paper, we have studied the DL channel estimation problem in the FDD multiuser massive MIMO system. We first provide a lower bound on the CSIT estimation error of any studied JSCC or SSCC feedback schemes based on the remote distortion rate theory. Focusing on the practically demanded case with compressed feedback and minimum delay, we proposed a JSCC feedback scheme (TKL feedback) that exploits the second-order statistics of the channel and analyzed its channel estimation MSE decay behavior in high SNR. For easier evaluation of DL spectral efficiency based on the different feedback schemes, we derived closed-form rate expression under MRT precoding. Our extensive numerical results show that the proposed JSCC scheme outperforms a simple JSCC scheme, and a standard SSCC scheme in terms of both the MSE of the channel estimation and the resulting spectral efficiency under the range of practical SNR. In addition, it outperforms a deep learning-based scheme in an idealized scenario.
We notice that, in general, the JSCC paradigm breaks the paradigm of layer separation and thus has not found wide applications beyond the theoretical research for general source transmission problems (e.g., video, audio, etc). We believe that CSI feedback is an ideal application for JSCC and advocate that future standards will adopt such type of feedback schemes.

is the tall matrix with eigenvectors and
\hbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}_{{\bf h}_{k}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{r_{k}\times r_{k}}
is a diagonal matrix with
r_{k}
non-zero eigenvalues
\lambda^{{\bf h}_{k}}_{1}\geq\lambda^{{\bf h}_{k}}_{2}\geq\dots\geq\lambda^{{\bf h}_{k}}_{r_{k}}
in descending order on its diagonal. By further denoting
\hbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}\triangleq{\bf U}_{k}{\bf S}_{k}^{{\sf H}}{\rm diag}(\sqrt{\hbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}_{k}})
, we find that the resulting terms of
{\bf C}_{{\bf h}_{k}\widehat{{\bf y}}^{\rm fb}_{k}}
and
{\bf C}_{\widehat{{\bf y}}^{\rm fb}_{k}}
have the same formulations as the corresponding terms of the “analog feedback” scheme in
[
8
]
. Therefore, following the similar steps in
[
8
, Appendix B]
, the MSE of channel estimation can be derived as

\displaystyle D_{k}^{\rm tkl}(R)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\|{\bf h}_{k}-\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k}^{\rm tkl}\|^{2}]

\displaystyle=\mathsf{tr}\left(\hbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}_{{\bf h}_{k}}\left({\bf I}-{\bf G}_{k}+{\bf G}_{k}({\bf I}+{\bf G}_{k})^{-1}{\bf G}_{k}\right)\right),

(77)

where
{\bf G}_{k}
is defined as

\displaystyle{\bf G}_{k}

\displaystyle\triangleq\hbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}_{{\bf h}_{k}}^{\frac{1}{2}}{\bf U}_{{\bf h}_{k}}^{{\sf H}}{\bf X}^{{\sf H}}\hbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}(\hbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}^{{\sf H}}\hbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}+{\bf I})^{-1}\hbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}^{{\sf H}}{\bf X}{\bf U}_{{\bf h}_{k}}\hbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}_{{\bf h}_{k}}^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(78)

Using the same trace inequality on (
B
) as in
[
8
, (29) in Appendix A]
, one can show that

\displaystyle\lambda^{{\bf h}_{k}}_{r_{k}}g({\mathsf{snr}}_{\rm dl})\leq D_{k}^{\rm tkl}(R)\leq\lambda^{{\bf h}_{k}}_{1}g({\mathsf{snr}}_{\rm dl}),

(79)

where
g({\mathsf{snr}}_{\rm dl})\triangleq\mathsf{tr}\left({\bf I}-{\bf G}_{k}+{\bf G}_{k}({\bf I}+{\bf G}_{k})^{-1}{\bf G}_{k}\right)
.
Then, we show the trend of
g(\cdot)
in large
{\sf snr}_{\rm dl}
.
For a given pilot matrix
{\bf X}
, denote the eigenvalues of
{\bf G}_{k}
by
u_{k}^{i},i=[r_{k}]
,
g({\mathsf{snr}}_{\rm dl})
is given by

\displaystyle g({\mathsf{snr}}_{\rm dl})

\displaystyle=r_{k}-\sum_{i=1}^{r_{k}}u^{i}_{k}+\sum_{i=1}^{r_{k}}\frac{{u^{i}_{k}}^{2}}{u^{i}_{k}+1}

(80)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{r_{k}}\frac{1}{u^{i}_{k}+1}.

(81)

We now derive an approximation of
{\bf G}_{k}
in large
{\sf snr}_{\rm dl}
for further analysis, given as

\displaystyle{\bf G}_{k}

\displaystyle=\hbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}_{{\bf h}_{k}}^{\frac{1}{2}}{\bf U}_{{\bf h}_{k}}^{{\sf H}}{\bf X}^{{\sf H}}{\bf U}_{k}{\bf S}_{k}^{{\sf H}}{\rm diag}(\sqrt{\hbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}_{k}})({\rm diag}({\hbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}_{k}})+{\bf I})^{-1}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad{\rm diag}(\sqrt{\hbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}_{k}}){\bf S}_{k}{\bf U}_{k}^{{\sf H}}{\bf X}{\bf U}_{{\bf h}_{k}}\hbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}_{{\bf h}_{k}}^{\frac{1}{2}}

(82)

\displaystyle\approx\hbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}_{{\bf h}_{k}}^{\frac{1}{2}}{\bf U}_{{\bf h}_{k}}^{{\sf H}}{\bf X}^{{\sf H}}{\bf U}_{k}{\bf S}_{k}^{{\sf H}}{\bf S}_{k}{\bf U}_{k}^{{\sf H}}{\bf X}{\bf U}_{{\bf h}_{k}}\hbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}_{{\bf h}_{k}}^{\frac{1}{2}},

(83)

where the approximation in (
83
) holds when
{\mathsf{snr}}_{\rm dl}\rightarrow\infty
, which results in
\hbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}_{k}\rightarrow\infty
based on (
76
), and
({\rm diag}({\hbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}_{k}})+{\bf I})^{-1}\approx{\rm diag}({\hbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}_{k}})^{-1}
.
Checking the items in (
83
), we find out: (a)
\hbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}_{{\bf h}_{k}}
and
{\bf U}_{{\bf h}_{k}}
are independent of
{\mathsf{snr}}_{\rm dl}
; (b) the training matrix can be written as
{\bf X}=\sqrt{{\mathsf{snr}}_{\rm dl}}{\bf X}^{o}
, where
{\bf X}^{o}
is randomly generated and independently from
{\mathsf{snr}}_{\rm dl}
; and (c)
{\bf S}_{k}^{{\sf H}}{\bf S}_{k}
is a diagonal matrix with first
\beta_{\rm fb}
diagonal elements as ones and all other elements as zeros. Therefore, the behavior of the non-zero eigenvalues of
{\bf G}_{k}
in high
{\sf snr}_{\rm dl}
is

u_{k}^{i}=\Theta({\mathsf{snr}}_{\rm dl}),\quad\forall u_{k}^{i}\neq 0.

(84)

Using (
81
) and (
84
), we deduce that if
{\bf G}_{k}
is full rank, i.e.,
u_{k}^{i}\neq 0,\forall i\in[r_{k}]
, we have
g({\sf snr}_{\rm dl})=\Theta({\sf snr}_{\rm dl}^{-1})
and then
D_{k}^{\rm tkl}(R)=\Theta({\sf snr}_{\rm dl}^{-1})
using (
79
). Conversely, if
{\bf G}_{k}
has at least one zero eigenvalue, i.e.,
\exists u_{k}^{i}=0
, we have
g({\sf snr}_{\rm dl})=\Theta(1)
from (
81
) and then
D_{k}^{\rm tkl}(R)=\Theta(1)
using (
79
). In short:

D_{k}^{\rm tkl}(R)=\begin{cases}\Theta({\sf snr}_{\rm dl}^{-1}),&u_{k}^{i}\neq 0,\forall i\in[r_{k}],\\
\Theta(1),&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(85)

Finally, we check the rank of
{\bf G}_{k}
. It is clear from (
83
) that when
\beta_{\rm fb}\geq r_{k}
,
{\bf G}_{k}
is full rank with probability one, and thus
D_{k}^{\rm tkl}(R)=\Theta({\sf snr}_{\rm dl}^{-1})
. Conversely, if
\beta_{\rm fb}<r_{k}
,
{\bf G}_{k}
has a rank at most equal to
\beta_{\rm fb}
for any realization of the training matrix
{\bf X}
, leading to
u_{k}^{i}=0
for some
i
and thus
D_{k}^{\rm tkl}(R)=\Theta(1)
. This completes the proof.

Appendix C
The proof of Lemma
2

Given
{\bf v}_{k}=\sqrt{\eta}\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k},~\forall k\in[K]
in MRT precoding, in order to satisfy the power constraint in (
51
), it is evident that the power scaling factor
\eta
can be obtained from (
58
).
Next, we derive the three terms
\mathbb{E}[{\bf h}_{k}^{{\sf H}}{\bf v}_{k}]
,
{\rm Var}({\bf h}_{k}^{{\sf H}}{\bf v}_{k})
and
\mathbb{E}[|{\bf h}_{k}^{{\sf H}}{\bf v}_{j}|^{2}]
for
j\neq k
in (
56
) as follows. First, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{\bf h}_{k}^{{\sf H}}{\bf v}_{k}]

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\eta}\mathbb{E}[{{\bf h}_{k}^{{\sf H}}\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k}}]

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\eta}\mathbb{E}[{(\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k}+{\bf e}_{k})^{{\sf H}}\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k}}]

(86)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\eta}\mathbb{E}[\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k}^{{\sf H}}\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k}]

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\eta}{\hbox{tr}}({\bf C}_{\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k}}),

(87)

where (
86
) holds due to (
57
). Then, we have

\displaystyle{\rm Var}({\bf h}_{k}^{{\sf H}}{\bf v}_{k})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[({\bf h}_{k}^{{\sf H}}{\bf v}_{k}-\mathbb{E}[{\bf h}_{k}^{{\sf H}}{\bf v}_{k}])({\bf h}_{k}^{{\sf H}}{\bf v}_{k}-\mathbb{E}[{\bf h}_{k}^{{\sf H}}{\bf v}_{k}])^{{\sf H}}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[{{\bf h}_{k}^{{\sf H}}{\bf v}_{k}{\bf v}_{k}^{{\sf H}}{\bf h}_{k}}]-\eta{\hbox{tr}}^{2}({\bf C}_{\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k}})

\displaystyle=\eta\left(\mathbb{E}[{(\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k}+{\bf e}_{k})^{{\sf H}}\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k}\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k}^{{\sf H}}(\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k}+{\bf e}_{k})}]-{\hbox{tr}}^{2}({\bf C}_{\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k}})\right)

\displaystyle=\eta\left(\mathbb{E}[{\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k}^{{\sf H}}\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k}\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k}^{{\sf H}}\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k}}]+\mathbb{E}[{{\bf e}_{k}^{{\sf H}}\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k}\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k}^{{\sf H}}{\bf e}_{k}}]-{\hbox{tr}}^{2}({\bf C}_{\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k}})\right)

(88)

\displaystyle=\eta\left({\hbox{tr}}({\bf C}_{\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k}}^{2})+{\hbox{tr}}^{2}({\bf C}_{\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k}})+{\hbox{tr}}({\bf C}_{\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k}}{\bf C}_{{\bf e}_{k}})-{\hbox{tr}}^{2}({\bf C}_{\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k}})\right)

(89)

\displaystyle=\eta\left({\hbox{tr}}({\bf C}^{2}_{\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k}})+{\hbox{tr}}({\bf C}_{\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k}}{\bf C}_{{\bf e}_{k}})\right)

\displaystyle=\eta{\hbox{tr}}({\bf C}_{\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k}}{\bf C}_{{{\bf h}}_{k}}),

(90)

where (
88
) holds since
{\bf e}_{k}
and
\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k}
are uncorrelated, and where the term
\mathbb{E}[{\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k}^{{\sf H}}\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k}\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k}^{{\sf H}}\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k}}]
from (
88
) to (
89
) is derived as follows. Let
{\bf C}_{\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k}}={\bf U}_{\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k}}\hbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}_{\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k}}{\bf U}_{\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k}}^{{{\sf H}}}
be the eigenvalue decomposition and denote
\widetilde{{\bf h}}_{k}\triangleq{\bf U}_{\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k}}^{{{\sf H}}}\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k}
, we have
\widetilde{{\bf h}}_{k}\sim{\cal C}{\cal N}(\mathbf{0},\hbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}_{\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k}})
.
Further denoting
\widetilde{h}_{k,i}
as the
i
-th element of
\widetilde{{\bf h}}_{k}
and
\lambda_{\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k},i}
as the
i
-th eigenvalue of
{\bf C}_{\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k}}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k}^{{\sf H}}\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k}\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k}^{{\sf H}}\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k}}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\|\widetilde{{\bf h}}_{k}\|^{2}\|\widetilde{{\bf h}}_{k}\|^{2}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{i=1}^{MN}|\widetilde{h}_{k,i}|^{2}\right)^{2}\right]

(91)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{MN}\mathbb{E}[|\widetilde{h}_{k,i}|^{4}]+\sum_{i=1}^{MN}\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{MN}\mathbb{E}[|\widetilde{h}_{k,i}|^{2}|\widetilde{h}_{k,j}|^{2}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{MN}2\lambda_{\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k},i}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{MN}\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{MN}\lambda_{\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k},i}\lambda_{\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k},j}

(92)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{MN}2\lambda_{\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k},i}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{MN}\lambda_{\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k},i}\left(\left(\sum_{j=1}^{MN}\lambda_{\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k},j}\right)-\lambda_{\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k},i}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{MN}2\lambda_{\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k},i}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{MN}\lambda_{\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k},i}\sum_{j=1}^{MN}\lambda_{\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k},j}-\sum_{i=1}^{MN}\lambda_{\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k},i}^{2}

\displaystyle={\hbox{tr}}({\bf C}_{\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k}}^{2})+{\hbox{tr}}^{2}({\bf C}_{\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k}}),

(93)

where (
92
) holds based on [Appendix A.2.4]
[
44
]
, which states that given a random variable
z\sim{\cal C}{\cal N}(0,\lambda)
, the fourth-order moment is given as
\mathbb{E}[|z|^{4}]=2\lambda^{2}
.
Finally, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|{\bf h}_{k}^{{\sf H}}{\bf v}_{j}|^{2}]

\displaystyle=\eta\mathbb{E}[|(\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k}+{\bf e}_{k})^{{\sf H}}\widehat{{\bf h}}_{j}|^{2}]

\displaystyle=\eta\mathbb{E}[\widehat{{\bf h}}_{j}^{{\sf H}}(\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k}+{\bf e}_{k})(\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k}+{\bf e}_{k})^{{\sf H}}\widehat{{\bf h}}_{j}]

\displaystyle=\eta\left(\mathbb{E}[\widehat{{\bf h}}_{j}^{{\sf H}}\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k}\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k}^{{\sf H}}\widehat{{\bf h}}_{j}]+\mathbb{E}[\widehat{{\bf h}}_{j}^{{\sf H}}{\bf e}_{k}{\bf e}_{k}^{{\sf H}}\widehat{{\bf h}}_{j}]\right)

\displaystyle=\eta{\hbox{tr}}({\bf C}_{\widehat{{\bf h}}_{j}}{\bf C}_{\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k}}+{\bf C}_{\widehat{{\bf h}}_{j}}{\bf C}_{{\bf e}_{k}})

\displaystyle=\eta{\hbox{tr}}({\bf C}_{\widehat{{\bf h}}_{j}}{\bf C}_{{\bf h}_{k}}),

(94)

where (
94
) holds due to the fact that
{\bf C}_{{\bf h}_{k}}={\bf C}_{\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k}}+{\bf C}_{{\bf e}_{k}}
.
Combining (
87
), (
90
) and (
94
), the closed-form expression of the UatF rate under MRT precoding is given as (
60
).

Appendix D
the proof of Lemma
3

Given
{\bf V}=\sqrt{\widetilde{\eta}}\widehat{{\bf H}}(\widehat{{\bf H}}^{{{\sf H}}}\widehat{{\bf H}})^{-1}
in ZF precoding, according to the power constraint in (
51
), it is evident to obtain the result in (
61
).
Next, we derive three terms
\mathbb{E}[{\bf h}_{k}^{{\sf H}}{\bf v}_{k}]
,
{\rm Var}({\bf h}_{k}^{{\sf H}}{\bf v}_{k})
and
\mathbb{E}[|{\bf h}_{k}^{{{\sf H}}}{\bf v}_{j}|^{2}]
for

in (

Based on the properties of ZF precoding, the inner product of
\widehat{{\bf h}}_{i}
and
{\bf v}_{j}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{\bf h}_{k}^{{\sf H}}{\bf v}_{k}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[(\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k}+{\bf e}_{k})^{{{\sf H}}}{\bf v}_{k}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k}^{{{\sf H}}}{\bf v}_{k}]+\mathbb{E}[{\bf e}_{k}^{{{\sf H}}}{\bf v}_{k}]

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\widetilde{\eta}},

(96)

where (
96
) holds according to (
95
). Then, we have

\displaystyle{\rm Var}({\bf h}_{k}^{{\sf H}}{\bf v}_{k})=\mathbb{E}[({\bf h}_{k}^{{\sf H}}{\bf v}_{k}-\mathbb{E}[{\bf h}_{k}^{{\sf H}}{\bf v}_{k}])({\bf h}_{k}^{{\sf H}}{\bf v}_{k}-\mathbb{E}[{\bf h}_{k}^{{\sf H}}{\bf v}_{k}])^{{\sf H}}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[{{\bf e}_{k}^{{\sf H}}{\bf v}_{k}{\bf v}_{k}^{{\sf H}}{\bf e}_{k}}]

\displaystyle=\mathsf{tr}({\bf C}_{{\bf v}_{k}}{\bf C}_{{\bf e}_{k}}),

(98)

where

and (
97

{\bf e}_{k}
and
{\bf v}_{k}
are uncorrelated. Finally, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|{\bf h}_{k}^{{{\sf H}}}{\bf v}_{j}|^{2}]

\displaystyle={\hbox{tr}}\left(\mathbb{E}[(\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k}+{\bf e}_{k})^{{{\sf H}}}{\bf v}_{j}{\bf v}_{j}^{{{\sf H}}}(\widehat{{\bf h}}_{k}+{\bf e}_{k})]\right)

\displaystyle={\hbox{tr}}\left(\mathbb{E}[{\bf e}_{k}^{{{\sf H}}}{\bf v}_{j}{\bf v}_{j}^{{{\sf H}}}{\bf e}_{k}]\right)

\displaystyle={\hbox{tr}}({\bf C}_{{\bf v}_{j}}{\bf C}_{{\bf e}_{k}}),

(100)

where (
99
) holds based on (

98

\displaystyle R_{k}^{{\rm ZF}}=\log\left(1+\frac{\widetilde{\eta}}{\mathsf{tr}({\bf C}_{{\bf v}_{k}}{\bf C}_{{\bf e}_{k}})+\sum_{j\neq k}{\hbox{tr}}({\bf C}_{{\bf v}_{j}}{\bf C}_{{\bf e}_{k}})+1}\right),

(101)

where the denominator in (

\displaystyle\mathsf{tr}({\bf C}_{{\bf v}_{k}}{\bf C}_{{\bf e}_{k}})+\sum_{j\neq k}{\hbox{tr}}({\bf C}_{{\bf v}_{j}}{\bf C}_{{\bf e}_{k}})={\hbox{tr}}\left(\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}{\bf C}_{{\bf v}_{k}}\right){\bf C}_{{\bf e}_{k}}\right)

\displaystyle={\hbox{tr}}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[{\bf V}{\bf V}^{{{\sf H}}}\right]{\bf C}_{{\bf e}_{k}}\right)

(102)

Putting (
102
