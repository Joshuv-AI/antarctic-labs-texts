Stress granule dynamics orchestrate colitis-to-cancer transition via TOM1-mediated disassembly and driven oncogenesis in response to inflammatory oxidative stress
Authors: Ziyu Wan, Meirui Qian, Jiawen Yu, Taiyu Shu, Dan Wang, Xue Liang, Ruo Chen, Bo Shi, Hongyong Cui, Zhinan Chen, Jianli Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractColitis-associated colorectal cancer (CAC) is a serious complication of inflammatory bowel disease. As stress-responsive membraneless organelles, stress granules (SGs) are involved in modulating inflammatory suppression and promoting tumorigenesis, but their role in the pathogenesis of CAC remains unclear. This study reveals that SGs exhibit a stage-specific function during CAC progression, being protective in acute colitis but transitioning to a tumor-promoting role in the dysplasia-carcinoma sequence. We first demonstrate that, during acute colitis, fluctuating oxidative stress drives dynamic SGs assembly. However, despite persistently elevated oxidative stress from chronic colitis to dysplasia, SGs levels paradoxically decline. This decline is attributed to the upregulation of TOM1, a novel negative regulator that binds the NTF2L domain of the core SGs scaffold protein G3BP1 to promote SGs disassembly, thereby counteracting the oxidative stress-driven assembly. The consequent disassembly facilitates the nuclear translocation of the oncogenic transcription factor FUBP1, which in turn promotes c-Myc expression and thereby contributes to tumorigenesis. Our findings establish the regulation of SGs dynamics, particularly via TOM1 and G3BP1, as a promising therapeutic strategy for CAC.

Introduction

Colitis-associated colorectal cancer (CAC), a serious complication of long-standing inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), develops through a multistep process involving persistent mucosal injury, dysregulated immunity, and eventual malignant transformation [1]. Epidemiologically, IBD patients exhibit a 2-8-fold increased risk of colorectal cancer (CRC) compared to the general population, with chronic inflammation driving key oncogenic events such as epithelial-mesenchymal transition, genomic instability, and remodeling of the tumor microenvironment [2]. Although the pathogenesis of IBD has been increasingly elucidated, the molecular cascades linking chronic colitis to carcinogenesis remain incompletely understood, underscoring the need to identify critical regulators of this "inflammation-cancer axis."

A critical hallmark of CAC is the dysregulation of stress-responsive cellular machinery, among which stress granules (SGs) - membraneless ribonucleoprotein condensates-have emerged as key players in stress adaptation and disease progression [3]. SGs form dynamically in response to environmental or cellular stress (e.g., oxidative stress, endoplasmic reticulum stress, nutrient deprivation) by sequestering translationally stalled mRNAs, RNA-binding proteins, and signaling molecules, thereby preserving proteostasis and metabolic balance [4]. Notably, inflammatory tissues exhibit significant oxidative stress fluctuations [5,6], yet whether and how these fluctuations drive the spatiotemporal dynamics of SGs assembly in CAC progression remains a key unanswered question. In chronic stress or pre-neoplastic cells, SGs can hijack cellular pathways to support malignant phenotypes. Recent studies have linked SGs accumulation to enhanced cancer cell proliferation, chemoresistance, and immune evasion in pancreatic cancer, breast cancer, and melanoma [7]. In CRC, SGs markers such as G3BP1 and TIA-1 are upregulated in tumor tissues and correlate with poor prognosis, suggesting a potential role for SGs in CRC progression [8]; yet, the functions and regulatory mechanisms of SGs in CAC pathogenesis remain poorly defined. Although SGs are known to selectively recruit molecules such as RACK1 and TRAF2 to modulate signaling pathways [9,10], and aberrant accumulation of mutant RNA-binding proteins within SGs contributes to neurodegeneration [11,12], it remains unknown whether similar sequestration mechanisms operate in CAC.

The assembly and disassembly of SGs are tightly controlled by core RBPs, with Ras-GTPase-activating protein SH3-domain-binding protein 1 (G3BP1) serving as a master regulator of SGs nucleation [13]. G3BP1 drives SGs formation through RNA-dependent liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS), a process mediated by its nuclear transport factor 2-like (NTF2L) dimerization domain and intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) [14,15], while its activity and SGs disassembly are fine-tuned by post-translational modifications: specifically, the E3 ligase TRIM21 catalyzes K63-linked polyubiquitination of G3BP1's NTF2L domain to suppress its oligomerization and LLPS capacity, which is followed by FAF2-recruited p97/VCP to extract G3BP1 from SGs cores and initiate disassembly, and TRIM21 can also target G3BP1 for autophagic degradation under prolonged stress, whereas phosphorylation at key residues such as Y40 (within NTF2L) and Ser149 (mediated by CK2) impairs G3BP1's dimerization and RNA-binding ability, further weakening SGs integrity and promoting disassembly to restore cellular homeostasis after stress relief [16,17].

As critical regulators of inflammatory signaling, SGs have been shown to suppress excessive innate immune activation by sequestering DDX3X to inhibit NLRP3 inflammasome assembly, thereby inhibiting the secretion of IL-1β and IL-18 as well as pyroptosis [18,19]. Pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IFN-γ and TNF-α drive SGs formation via eIF2α phosphorylation [20]. Moreover, SGs act as signaling hubs that sequester RLR pathway components to prevent dsRNA-induced hyper-inflammation and immune-mediated cell death, further linking SGs biology to the control of inflammatory stress responses [21]. However, whether and how SGs dynamics are regulated and contribute to the colitis-to-cancer transition in CAC remains largely unexplored.

This study elucidates the stage-specific functions and regulatory mechanisms of SGs in CAC. We first demonstrate that SGs exhibit a distinct dynamic pattern during CAC progression: SGs are scarce under physiological conditions, markedly accumulate in acute colitis, gradually decline in chronic colitis and dysplasia, and re-accumulate in carcinoma tissues. We further show that SGs exert a protective anti-apoptotic role in acute colitis while promoting malignant transformation in the dysplasia-carcinoma sequence. Using hydrophobic interaction chromatography, we identify TOM1 as a novel SGs-associated protein that is progressively upregulated during colitis-to-cancer transition. Mechanistically, TOM1 directly binds to the NTF2L domain of G3BP1, suppresses G3BP1 homodimerization, and inhibits G3BP1-mediated LLPS, thereby accelerating SGs disassembly. Disassembly of SGs promotes the nuclear translocation of the oncogenic transcription factor FUBP1, which subsequently activates downstream oncogenic signaling pathways. Collectively, our findings establish a dynamic model of SGs in CAC, identify TOM1 as a critical regulator of SGs disassembly by targeting G3BP1 dimerization and LLPS, and link TOM1-mediated SGs remodeling to FUBP1 nuclear translocation and oncogenic pathway activation, providing novel mechanistic insights into inflammation-driven colorectal tumorigenesis.

Results

1.A stage-specific dynamic of SGs formation from Colitis to Colorectal Carcinogenesis

A stage-specific dynamic of SGs formation from Colitis to Colorectal Carcinogenesis

To explore the molecular mechanisms of colitis-cancer transformation, a mouse CAC model was established via AOM/DSS induction (Fig. 1A, S1A-S1B), which mimics human inflammation-driven CRC by recapitulating the "acute colitis-chronic colitis-dysplasia-carcinogenesis" progression [2,22]. In acute colitis, colon length was significantly reduced (P = 0.0022; Fig. 1B–D) with mucosal hemorrhage and ulceration (Fig. 1E); DAI showed cyclical fluctuations corresponding to DSS administration (Fig. S1G), while histological scores increased progressively (Fig. S1H). H&E staining confirmed stage-specific pathological changes: acute colitis showed mucosal edema, epithelial necrosis, goblet cell loss, crypt abscesses, and submucosal inflammation (Fig. S1C); subsequent stages exhibited crypt distortion, nuclear disarray, papillary/serrated projections, and nuclear pleomorphism (Fig. S1D–S1E); established CAC tumors displayed severe glandular disruption, cytological atypia, and desmoplastic stroma (Fig. S1F). Based on DAI, histological scores, and morphology, the disease was stratified into three phases: acute colitis (DSS-1-5d to DSS-1-12d), chronic colitis-dysplasia (DSS-2-5d to DSS-3-12d), and dysplasia-carcinoma (Adjacent-to-CAC) (Fig. 1A), consistent with previous reports [23].

LC-MS/MS and KEGG analysis of differentially expressed proteins (fold change >1.5 or < 0.67) revealed enrichment of ribosome-related proteins (Fig. 1F and G). Given that ribosomes and SGs share core translational components, stress-induced eIF2α phosphorylation halts translation, causing ribosomes to dissociate from mRNA. The free mRNA then nucleates SGs assembly [9,10]. Given the link between ribosomes, SGs, and translation, SGs dynamics were investigated: SGs abundance was low in healthy colon, increase in acute colitis (P < 0.0001; Fig. 1H and I, S1I), declined in chronic colitis-dysplasia (P < 0.0001; Fig. 1J and K, S1J), and increased markedly from dysplasia to carcinoma (P < 0.0001; Fig. 1L and M, S1K). This dynamic may reflect cytoprotection in acute colitis, tissue adaptation in the chronic phase, and tumor cell survival support in carcinoma; immunofluorescence further confirmed significantly more SGs in human CRC tissues than adjacent tissues (P = 0.0035; Fig. 1N–P, S1L), verifying clinical relevance.

To identify upstream signals, oxidative stress (a key SGs trigger [24,25]) was assessed. Flow cytometry showed that SGs assembly/disassembly was coupled with ROS upregulation/downregulation (Fig. S2A and S2B). Meanwhile, IHC revealed that oxidative stress was markedly increased in acute colitis and remained continuously elevated from acute colitis through dysplasia (Fig. S2C–S2F). Consistent with the dynamic pattern of SGs, phosphorylation of eIF2α Ser51 (the canonical SGs initiation pathway) exhibited a parallel trend: it was upregulated in acute colitis (Fig. S2G and S2H), declined in chronic colitis - dysplasia, and elevated again in tumors (Fig. S2I–S2L), indicating that it mediates stress-dependent SGs biogenesis. Notably, despite sustained oxidative stress, both p-eIF2α and SGs levels decreased from acute colitis to dysplasia. This uncoupling between oxidative stress levels and SGs dynamics indicates that, during the colitis-to-tumor transformation, additional molecular mechanisms interfere with SGs assembly and disassembly, thereby decoupling their formation from the canonical oxidative stress signaling.

During malignant transformation, however, cancer cells reactivate SGs assembly to boost stress resistance and survival, causing a marked rebound in p-eIF2α and SGs.

2.The stage-specific roles of SGs from Colitis to Colorectal Carcinogenesis

The stage-specific roles of SGs from Colitis to Colorectal Carcinogenesis

To elucidate SGs’ function in CAC progression, ISRIB, a small-molecule inhibitor suppressing SGs assembly by reversing eIF2α phosphorylation-mediated translational inhibition and modulating stress response, was used [26]. IF confirmed ISRIB completely blocked AS-induced SGs formation (P = 0.0033; Fig. 2B, 2C, S3A), accompanied by reduced p-eIF2α expression in colon tissues (Fig. S3B and S3C). In mice with acute colitis (first 2.5% DSS induction), ISRIB gavage (Fig. 2A) accelerated body weight loss (P < 0.001; Fig. 2D) and increased mortality vs. DSS alone (Fig. 2E), with H&E showing aggravated colonic damage (enlarged ulcers, extensive epithelial necrosis, substantial goblet cell loss, complete crypt destruction, worsened mucosal edema; Fig. 2F), indicating SGs exert a protective effect in acute colitis.

To test if SGs’ protective function persists into chronic colitis, ISRIB was administered from the second DSS cycle (Fig. 2G). ISRIB alleviated DSS-induced colonic hemorrhage (P < 0.0001) but had no significant effect on weight or mortality (Fig. 2H–J), indicating a stage-specific functional shift, SGs inhibition does not exacerbate chronic intestinal damage, suggesting their function transitions from protective to potentially maladaptive or neutral with persistent inflammation.

To explore SGs’ role in dysplasia-to-cancer progression, AS (inducing G3BP1/TIA1-labeled SGs, Fig. S3D and E) was orally administered every 3 days for 28 days, starting 2 weeks after the final DSS dose (Fig. 2K). AOM/DSS-induced weight loss was further aggravated by AS (P < 0.0001; P = 0.0013; Fig. 2L), with more severe tumor lesions and higher tumor burden in AS-treated mice (Fig. 2M, S3F); colon length showed no significant difference (Fig. S3G and S3H). IF confirmed increased SGs assembly in AS-treated mice (P < 0.0001; Fig. S3I and S3J), indicating SGs induction promotes tumorigenesis in this phase.

Collectively, our results demonstrate the stage-specific roles for SGs function in CAC. SGs inhibition during acute colitis exacerbates tissue damage, whereas their protective effect diminishes during chronic colitis. Conversely, SGs induction in the tumorigenic phase promotes neoplasia and increases tumor burden.

3.Conditional Knockout of G3BP1 Delays Inflammation-Driven Tumorigenesis

Conditional Knockout of G3BP1 Delays Inflammation-Driven Tumorigenesis

Our previous results confirmed that SGs exert stage-specific functions in CAC progression: protective in acute colitis but pro-tumorigenic during dysplasia-to-carcinoma transition. As a core scaffold essential for SGs assembly, G3BP1 was further investigated for its role in inflammation-driven tumorigenesis in vivo.

Pan-cancer analysis showed that G3BP1 was significantly upregulated in multiple cancers, especially in CRC (Fig. S4A). TCGA survival analysis revealed no significant association between G3BP1 expression and overall survival in CRC patients (Fig. S4B). RNA-seq and IHC further verified elevated G3BP1 expression in mouse CAC tissues relative to normal controls (Fig. S4C and S4DMultimedia component 1), indicating that G3BP1 participates in colorectal tumorigenesis rather than regulating prognosis. In vitro assays using HCT116 cells with G3BP1 knockdown or overexpression showed that G3BP1 inhibited cell migration and proliferation, while G3BP1 knockdown promoted proliferation (Fig. S4E–S4J). This paradox suggests that G3BP1 may intrinsically suppress malignant behaviors in epithelial cells, and its pro-tumorigenic function in CAC is likely mediated through non-cell-autonomous mechanisms involving SGs dynamics and the inflammatory microenvironment.

To directly validate the role of G3BP1-dependent SGs in CRC, we generated intestine-specific G3BP1 knockout mice (G3BP1(flox/flox); Vil-Cre) and control littermates, then subjected them to AOM/DSS-induced CAC (Fig. 3A). During acute colitis, G3BP1-deficient mice exhibited comparable body weight loss and mortality to controls, but significantly more severe intestinal hemorrhage (P = 0.0283; Fig. 3B–E). Throughout CAC progression, G3BP1 knockout mice displayed attenuated body weight loss (P = 0.0151) but no statistically significant change in mortality (Fig. 3F and G), with alleviated ulceration, hemorrhage and mucosal erosion (Fig. 3H and I). At the endpoint, tumor burden was significantly decreased in G3BP1-deficient mice (Fig. 3J and K).

These findings indicate that G3BP1 depletion impairs SGs assembly, mitigates chronic inflammatory injury and suppresses inflammation-driven tumorigenesis. G3BP1-mediated SGs formation thus displays stage-dependent functions: protective in acute colitis but pathogenic during chronic carcinogenesis.

4.The SGs protein TOM1 is involved in colitis-dysplasia transformation

The SGs protein TOM1 is involved in colitis-dysplasia transformation

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms by which SGs contribute to CAC, we first treated cells with AS to induce SGs assembly and examined their dynamics. Live-cell imaging revealed that arsenite stress induced the rapid nucleation of numerous small SGs aggregates. These structures underwent progressive growth through the continuous recruitment of components and fusion upon contact, ultimately forming larger, mature SGs (Video S1). Upon stress removal, SGs disassembly occurred on a considerably slower timescale than their assembly, characterized by gradual condensate shrinkage until complete dissolution (Video S2).

To identify key functional proteins within SGs that mediate colitis-dysplasia transformation, we established a hydrophobic interaction chromatography (HIC) method for SGs isolation based on the hydrophobicity of SGs, instead of traditional centrifugation (Fig. 4A) [10,27]. Validation experiments showed that no SGs markers were enriched in unstressed cells, ruling out false positives (Fig. 4I–K). In three stressed tumor cell lines, HIC specifically extracted SGs components with marked enrichment of core markers (Fig. 4F–H, 4L-4Q), confirming the specificity and reliability of this method.

Compared with traditional centrifugation, HIC exhibited high consistency in capturing core SGs components, despite weak correlations between mRNA and protein levels, which may result from dynamic SGs assembly and disassembly (Fig. 4B–S5A). Using this novel method, we identified known SGs core proteins (G3BP1/2, Caprin1, USP10) and a new candidate TOM1. Notably, SGs assembly-promoting proteins (ATXN2, Caprin1, PABPN1) were highly enriched by HIC, whereas disassembly-related proteins (USP10) were preferentially recovered by centrifugation, suggesting that the traditional method may bias toward static SGs fractions and miss dynamic components [3,9,11,28].

In the mouse CAC model, TOM1 expression was significantly decreased in acute colitis (P = 0.0205; Fig. S5B–S5D), gradually elevated during chronic colitis-to-dysplasia transition (P = 0.0167; Fig. 4C and D, S5E), and further upregulated in dysplasia-to-carcinoma progression (Fig. 4E–S5F). The inverse correlation between TOM1 and SGs abundance in the chronic phase suggests that TOM1 may act as a negative regulator of SGs stability during this critical transition. Pan-cancer analysis showed that TOM1 was markedly upregulated in CRC and associated with worse prognosis (Fig. S5G–S5J), supporting a close link between TOM1 and colitis-dysplasia transformation.

Selective autophagy is a major pathway for SGs degradation, during which autophagy receptors target SGs components for lysosomal degradation. As an adaptor protein in selective autophagy, TOM1 may mediate SGs disassembly via autophagy. During AS washout-induced SGs disassembly, autophagic flux was activated, accompanied by increased LC3-II and decreased SQSTM1 (p62) levels (Fig. S5K), indicating that autophagy participates in SGs component degradation.

In the AOM/DSS model, autophagic activity was significantly reduced in carcinoma tissues (Fig. S5L). Such stage-dependent autophagy alteration dynamically regulates TOM1-mediated SGs disassembly: In colitis-to-dysplasia, intact autophagy cooperates with TOM1 to promote efficient SGs disassembly and degradation, limiting excessive SGs accumulation. In carcinoma, impaired autophagy uncouples TOM1-dependent SGs disassembly from degradation, allowing SGs components to persist and recondense. Thus, defective autophagy in tumors attenuates TOM1-mediated clearance of SGs components, contributing to the pathological SGs accumulation during colitis-associated colorectal carcinogenesis.

5.TOM1 Promotes SGs Disassembly via Interacting with G3BP1 NTF2L Domain

TOM1 Promotes SGs Disassembly via Interacting with G3BP1 NTF2L Domain

Given that TOM1 was co-enriched with the SGs disassembly regulator USP10 by traditional centrifugation, we hypothesized that TOM1 participates in SGs disassembly. In HEK293T cells stably overexpressing GFP-TOM1, immunofluorescence showed that TOM1 overexpression did not affect SGs assembly at 30 and 60 min after AS stimulation (Fig. S6A–S6F). However, it significantly accelerated SGs disassembly at 30, 60, and 90 min after stress removal (P < 0.0001; P < 0.0001; P < 0.0001; Fig. 5A–E), indicating that TOM1 mainly regulates SGs disassembly.

In vitro LLPS reconstitution assays showed that G3BP1 formed liquid condensates in the presence of total RNA (Fig. S6G–S6J). Adding purified TOM1 dose-dependently inhibited G3BP1/RNA-induced condensate formation (Fig. 5F and G). FRAP analysis revealed that TOM1 knockdown increased the mobile fraction of G3BP1 in SGs (P < 0.0001; Fig. 5H and I), suggesting that TOM1 restricts G3BP1 dynamics and promotes SGs disassembly by regulating G3BP1-mediated LLPS.

Immunofluorescence confirmed the colocalization of TOM1 and G3BP1 (Fig. S7A–S7C). Structural prediction and biochemical assays showed that TOM1 binds with high affinity to the NTF2L domain of G3BP1 (Fig. S7D–S7H), which is critical for G3BP1 dimerization and SGs assembly. Co-IP assays demonstrated that TOM1 knockdown significantly enhanced G3BP1 dimerization (Fig. S7I). These results reveal that TOM1 binds the G3BP1 NTF2L domain to inhibit dimerization and LLPS, thereby accelerating SGs disassembly.

Domain-mapping analysis showed that TOM1 contains VHS, GAT, and MYO6-binding domains (Fig. S8A). SPR and in vitro LLPS assays revealed that the VHS domain directly binds G3BP1 and strongly suppresses G3BP1-mediated phase separation (Figs. S8B, S8E, S8F). The GAT domain interacted with G3BP1 but did not inhibit condensation, while the MYO6-binding domain exerted mild inhibition (Figs. S8C, S8D, S8G-S8J). Collectively, the VHS domain is the key effector of TOM1. TOM1 targets the G3BP1 NTF2L domain via its VHS region to disrupt SGs stability and promote SGs disassembly.

6.SGs Dynamics Promote CAC through Persistent MAPK Activation and Altered Mutational Profiles

SGs Dynamics Promote CAC through Persistent MAPK Activation and Altered Mutational Profiles

To clarify how SGs regulate CAC progression, we first examined their effect on colonic epithelial cell apoptosis. Immunofluorescence showed reduced Cleaved Caspase-3 (CC3) intensity upon SGs assembly (P < 0.0001; Fig. 6A and B), indicating an anti-apoptotic effect. Annexin V/PI flow cytometry revealed SGs assembly increased early apoptotic cells (Annexin V+/PI-) but significantly suppressed late apoptosis (Annexin V+/PI+); ISRIB-mediated SGs inhibition abrogated this suppression, increasing terminal cell death (Fig. 6E–I). Thus, SGs do not prevent apoptosis initiation but impede the early-to-late apoptosis transition, consistent with their protective role in acute colitis by preserving mucosal integrity.

We further explored the underlying signaling pathways: SGs assembly was associated with increased phosphorylation of p38 MAPK and NF-κB p65. ISRIB-induced SGs inhibition significantly reduced p38 and p65 phosphorylation (Fig. 6C, D, 6J-6O), demonstrating SGs actively sustain these pro-survival and inflammatory pathways. After SGs disassembly, p65 phosphorylation returned to baseline, while p38 MAPK phosphorylation persisted (Fig. 6J–N), indicating SGs induce transient NF-κB/p38 synergy with a lasting p38 legacy effect.

To investigate SGs’ impact on genomic stability, trinucleotide mutation frequency analysis in AOM/DSS mice at the chronic inflammation-dysplasia transition (DSS-2-12d) showed ISRIB treatment significantly increased T > C transitions vs. controls (P = 0.0002; Fig. S9A–S9H). This indicates SGs inhibition alters the mutational profile (not overall mutation burden), suggesting SGs dynamics regulate DNA repair pathways or inflammation-induced DNA damage types, thereby influencing driver mutation spectra during CAC development.

7.SGs Disassembly Promotes FUBP1 Nuclear Localization Drives Tumorigenesis

To explore additional mechanisms by which SGs regulate CAC progression, we performed intersection analysis of the SGs proteome and differentially expressed proteins during inflammation-tumor transformation, identifying 321 candidate proteins and 218 upregulated in tumors (Fig. 7A and B). Screening these for known oncogenes (Fig. 7C) and immunofluorescence confirmed FUBP1 co-localizes with SGs in colonic epithelial cells (Rearson r = 0.62, Fig. 7D and E), suggesting SGs sequester FUBP1 to prevent its nuclear translocation and pro-proliferative activity during inflammation.

Consistent with FUBP1's association with CRC progression and poor prognosis [29,30], we investigated its SGs-dependent regulation: compared to normal NCM460 cells, HCT116 CRC cells showed reduced FUBP1 recruitment to SGs but increased nuclear accumulation after SGs induction (P = 0.0290; P = 0.0008; Fig. 7F–H), indicating cancer cells adapt to favor FUBP1 release from SGs and nuclear import for tumor promotion. IHC of stage-specific CAC mouse tissues showed gradual increases in FUBP1 nuclear localization during chronic colitis-to-dysplasia transition (P = 0.0129; Fig. 7I and J), implicating this translocation in carcinogenesis.

FUBP1 nuclear translocation is critical for its tumor-promoting function, as it activates oncogenes like c-Myc to drive tumor proliferation and invasion [29,31], FUBP1 knockdown significantly reduced c-c-Myc protein expression (Fig. 7N), confirming FUBP1 as a positive upstream regulator of c-Myc. Immunofluorescence showed progressive nuclear FUBP1 enrichment during neoplastic transformation (P < 0.0001) and strong co-localization with c-Myc (Pearson r = 0.74; Fig. 7K–M), indicating SGs-released FUBP1 is transcriptionally active.

We speculate that during colitis, SGs sequester FUBP1; during neoplastic transformation, enhanced SGs disassembly (potentially via upregulated TOM1) releases FUBP1, promoting its nuclear import. Nuclear FUBP1 then drives tumorigenesis by activating c-Myc expression and fueling epithelial proliferation.

Discussion

SGs are emerging as a novel class of biomarkers and key molecular hubs that link chronic colitis and tumor development by modulating inflammatory signaling, metabolic stress, and oncogenic pathways [4,32]. During chronic colitis, SGs promote cell survival under stress conditions such as hypoxia and nutrient deprivation, thereby facilitating tissue repair. As lesions progress to neoplasia, SGs support malignant progression by enhancing stress adaptation, invasion, metastasis, and immune evasion [7,33,34]. However, their functional significance in CAC pathogenesis remains unclear. In this study, we reveal a stage-specific functional shift of SGs from protective in acute colitis to tumor-promoting during carcinogenesis. Notably, the SGs burden progressively increases in intestinal epithelial cells during the transition from dysplasia to carcinoma, suggesting that SGs accumulation may represent a critical rate-limiting step in tumor development.

As a key regulator of inflammation, SGs have been shown to suppress inflammatory responses by sequestering DDX3X to inhibit the NLRP3 inflammasome pathway and by negatively regulating the NF-κB signaling pathway [18,19,35,36]. Emerging evidence indicates that, within the integrated stress response framework, ISRIB enhances the cytotoxic function of CD8+ T cells [37]. This model of multi-layered governance is consistent with our findings in a CAC mouse model. Our research indicates that SGs play a protective role in acute colitis. In our study, administration of ISRIB led to markedly aggravated intestinal colitis and significantly impaired barrier function. Furthermore, we discovered for the first time that SGs gradually disassemble during the progression from chronic colitis to dysplasia, releasing tumor-associated transcription factors. This provides experimental evidence for understanding the role of SGs in the early stages of cancer development.

We identified TOM1, an autophagy adaptor progressively upregulated in colitis, dysplasia, and carcinoma, as a key driver of SGs disassembly. TOM1 acts by directly binding the G3BP1 NTF2L domain and inhibiting phase separation. A mechanistic paradox emerges if TOM1 continuously promotes SGs disassembly and is increasingly expressed, yet SGs accumulate significantly in carcinoma. Our data resolve this paradox through stage-dependent autophagy activity that dictates the functional outcome of TOM1-mediated SGs regulation. In the colitis-to-dysplasia stage, autophagy remains functionally competent, and TOM1 not only accelerates SGs disassembly but also targets disassembled SGs components for autophagic degradation, resulting in a net reduction of SGs. In the dysplasia-to-carcinoma stage, autophagy is strongly suppressed, and TOM1 still drives SGs disassembly, but disassembled SGs components cannot be efficiently degraded and cleared, leading to recondensation into stable and oncogenic SGs and thus pathological accumulation.

Notably, stress adaptation is recognized as a key driver of tumorigenesis [38]. In the study, we observed a reduction in SGs numbers during the shift from acute to chronic colitis, indicating adaptive tissue reprogramming due to persistent inflammation. This suggests a change in cellular stress response, from protective to maladaptive, favoring cell survival and growth under ongoing adverse conditions. Furthermore, during the transition from dysplasia to carcinoma, we observed an accumulation of SGs. The assembly of SGs downregulated the expression of CC3, enabling the survival of colonic epithelial cells that would otherwise undergo apoptosis during chronic colitis, thereby providing a prerequisite for tumor progression.

As a core scaffolding protein of SGs, G3BP1 initiates LLPS by recruiting and binding RNA, which induces a conformational change that exposes the NTF2L domain interface and enables antiparallel homodimerization, thereby triggering SGs assembly [28,[39], [40], [41], [42]]. We first established that the fluctuating oxidative stress within the inflammatory microenvironment might act as a primary upstream signal governing the stage-specific dynamics of SGs assembly. To elucidate how this inflammatory signal is translated into SGs disassembly during the critical transition to dysplasia, we investigated potential regulatory partners of G3BP1. We identified TOM1 binds directly to the NTF2L domain of G3BP1, interferes with its homodimerization and thereby promotes SGs disassembly. This conclusion was supported by FRAP assays, which revealed significantly increased mobility of SGs components following TOM1 knockdown. Given that dynamic exchange between SGs components and the cytosol is critical for SGs homeostasis. We propose that TOM1 upregulation during the progression from chronic colitis to dysplasia destabilizes SGs, accelerates their disassembly, and facilitates the release of SGs-sequestered pro-tumorigenic factors such as FUBP1, thereby promoting tumorigenesis.

Moreover, we identified FUBP1, a recently characterized oncogenic transcription factor, as a component recruited to SGs. Following SGs disassembly, FUBP1 translocates to the nucleus to exert regulatory functions. Previous evidence indicates that FUBP1 may modulate MYC expression during CRC development and progression [29,43]. Consistent with this, in an colitis-driven model of colorectal carcinogenesis, we observed progressive accumulation of FUBP1 in the nucleus, where it showed significant colocalization with c-Myc. Notably, FUBP1 release and nuclear translocation coincide precisely with TOM1-mediated SGs disassembly in the dysplasia stage, directly linking dynamic SGs remodeling to oncogenic transcriptional programs. These results suggest that FUBP1 acts as a critical mediator in SGs-driven tumorigenesis, though the underlying molecular mechanisms require further validation.

Several limitations of this study should be noted. First, the disassembly of SGs involves a complex regulatory network, including translation resumption-driven dissolution, chaperone- and disaggregase-mediated component degradation or remodeling, post-translational modifications, and autophagic clearance [27,[44], [45], [46], [47]]. In subsequent studies, we will further investigate these alternative disassembly mechanisms in the context of CAC development. As reported, SGs selectively sequester mRNAs involved in cell survival and proliferation [3,48], but the specific role of SGs-resident mRNAs in CAC progression was not addressed in this study. Future studies should identify these mRNAs and their functional role in CAC.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that SGs play a stage-specific functional role in the progression of CAC, exerting a cytoprotective function during acute colitis while facilitating malignant transformation and tumor progression during the transition from dysplasia to carcinoma. TOM1 is progressively upregulated during colitis-to-cancer progression and promotes SGs disassembly by interacting with the NTF2L domain of G3BP1. Notably, the functional consequence of TOM1-mediated SGs disassembly is determined by autophagic activity in a stage-dependent manner: TOM1 contributes to physiological SGs disassembly when autophagy is active, whereas suppressed autophagy in carcinoma leads to uncoupling of disassembly from degradation and subsequent pathological SGs accumulation (Fig. 8). These findings deepen our understanding of SGs dynamics and functional switching during colitis-driven carcinogenesis, and establish TOM1-mediated regulation of SGs remodeling as a promising therapeutic target for intervention and prevention of colitis-associated colorectal cancer.

Methods

Cell culture

HEK-293T (Human Embryonic Kidney 293 cells transformed with SV40 large T antigen), NCM460 (Normal Colon Mucosal Epithelial Cell Line) and HCT116 (Homo sapiens colorectal carcinoma cell line) cells were cultured in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum at 37 °C under 5% CO2. For SGs assembly experiments, cells were subjected to the following treatment conditions prior to subsequent analysis: +AS, cells were treated with 0.5 mM sodium arsenite (AS) for 1 h to induce SGs formation. +AS-AS (Recovery or Re), cells were first treated with 0.5 mM AS for 1 h, the AS-containing medium was then removed, and cells were gently rinsed with complete medium, fresh complete medium was added, followed by a 2-h incubation to allow SGs disassembly during recovery. +AS + ISRIB (Inhibition, ISRIB), to inhibit SGs assembly, cells were co-treated with 0.5 mM AS and 4 μM or 8 μM ISRIB (integrated stress response inhibitor) simultaneously for 1 h.

Plasmids

Construct plasmids pcDNA3.1-eGFP-TOM1, shTOM1 and G3BP1-mCherry. Perform transfection according to the instructions of Lipofectamine 3000, and culture the cells for 48 h before conducting subsequent experiments.

Animal

Wild-type C57BL/6 mice were obtained from SiPeiFu (Beijing, China). The Vil-Cre and G3BP1-flox strains were purchased from Cyagen (Suzhou, China) and backcrossed onto a C57BL/6 background. All mice, including wild-type and genetically modified strains, were housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions in individually ventilated cages (IVC) with a maximum of 5 mice per cage. They were maintained on a 12-h light/dark cycle at a controlled temperature (22 ± 2 °C) and humidity (50% ± 10%), with free access to standard laboratory rodent diet and autoclaved water. Environmental enrichment was provided. For experiments, 6-8-week-old male mice were used, encompassing wild-type C57BL/6, Vil-Cre; G3BP1(flox/flox), and their control littermates (G3BP1(flox/flox); Vil-Cre(−)). Animals were randomly assigned to experimental groups using a computer-generated sequence.

CAC model

Acute Colitis Model: For the acute colitis model, 6- to 8-week-old male C57BL/6 mice were administered 2.5% (w/v) dextran sodium sulfate (DSS) in drinking water ad libitum for 5 days, followed by a 7-day recovery period with regular water. Body weight and fecal occult blood were monitored daily to assess disease activity.

CAC Model: The colitis-associated cancer (CAC) model was established by an intraperitoneal injection of 10 mg/kg azoxymethane (AOM). Mice subsequently underwent three cycles of 2.5% DSS treatment (each cycle: 5 days of DSS administration followed by 2 weeks of recovery with regular water). The experiment was terminated 4 months after the AOM injection. Body weight and fecal occult blood were monitored regularly throughout the study. Animals exhibiting >20% body weight loss were euthanized as a humane endpoint. All remaining mice were euthanized at the experimental endpoint for tissue collection.

Pharmacological Interventions: To investigate the effects of specific compounds, separate interventions were administered. The stress granule assembly inhibitor ISRIB (Sigma-Aldrich, SML0843) was formulated in corn oil (5 mg/kg) and administered daily via oral gavage starting from the initiation of DSS treatment to assess its role in acute inflammation. For the AS treatment, mice received 4 mg/kg AS via oral gavage every 3 days, commencing after the third DSS cycle and continuing until the end of the model establishment, with body weight monitored every 3 days.

The disease activity index (DAI) was evaluated every three days according to three parameters: body weight loss, stool consistency, and rectal bleeding. Each indicator was scored from 0 to 4 as described previously.

Colonoscopy

Anesthetize mice with 1.5% isoflurane, and perform colonoscopy on the mice using the ENDOQ-E1 Colonoscopy system (ENDOQ, Guangzhou, China).

Super-resolution image acquisition

Live-cell imaging was performed using an Elyra 7 microscope with Lattice SIM2 (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany) and an oil-immersion objective lens (Plan-Apochromat 63 × /1.46 Oil DIC M27, Zeiss). Time-lapse imaging for SGs assembly was conducted at 5-s intervals, while SGs disassembly was recorded at 1-min intervals. Acquired images were processed using the SIM2 module in ZEISS ZEN 3.4 software. Image reconstruction was carried out with the 3D-SIM (Zen 3.0 SR software, Zeiss) wizard in ZEN 3.0 SR software (Zeiss).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

After the tissue samples were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, they were paraffin-embedded and sectioned into 4-μm-thick slices. Following the completion of dewaxing and hydration steps, antigen retrieval was performed via high-pressure heating in a citrate retrieval solution. Subsequent steps were performed in accordance with the instructions of the universal two-step detection kit. Primary antibodies were incubated overnight at 4 °C in a humidified chamber. 3,3′-Diaminobenzidine (DAB) was used as the chromogen, and cell nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin. Finally, the sections were mounted with neutral balsam. After scanning the slides using a digital slide scanner (3DHISTECH, Budapest, Hungary), the positive cell rate was analyzed with HELO software.

Detection of Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS)

Cells were seeded in 6-well plates one day in advance. The DCFH-DA probe was diluted 1:1000 in serum-free medium to a final concentration of 10 μmol/L. After removing the cell culture medium, the diluted DCFH-DA solution was added, and the cells were incubated at 37 °C for 20 min. Cells were washed three times with serum-free medium, harvested by trypsinization, and centrifuged. The supernatant was discarded, and the cell pellet was washed once with PBS before resuspension in PBS for flow cytometry analysis.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)

Mouse colon tissues were collected, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, followed by dehydration, embedding, and sectioning, and then H&E staining was performed for 3 min.

Immunofluorescence (IF)

Seed the cells in dishes and culture them overnight. Add 0.5 mM AS; at the specified time points, wash the cells once with PBS, then fix them with 1 mL of 4% paraformaldehyde. Permeabilize the cells with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 5 min, followed by three washes with PBS. Block non-specific binding with goat serum for 30 min, then incubate the cells with primary antibodies overnight. After three additional washes with PBS, incubate the cells with secondary antibodies for 1 h and with DAPI for 15 min. Image the cells using a laser scanning confocal microscope (Leica STELLARIS 5, Wetzlar, Germany). All primary antibodies used are listed in the table. Quantitate and qualitatively analyze the fluorescent images using ImageJ software.

For paraffin sections, deparaffinize them with xylene and gradient ethanol, followed by heat-induced antigen retrieval. Block non-specific binding with goat serum, then incubate the sections with primary antibodies against G3BP1 and TOM1 at 4 °C overnight. Subsequently, incubate with secondary antibodies and mounting medium with DAPI. Perform whole-slide scanning using a multicolor fluorescent slide scanner (AKOYA Biosciences, Massachusetts, USA), and finally quantitate SGs using ImageJ software.

LC-MS

Digest the proteins overnight at 37 °C, then separate the peptide mixture using a C18 column. Subsequently, analyze the peptide fractions using a Thermo Scientific™ Orbitrap Eclipse™ Tribrid™ mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher, San Jose, USA) equipped with an EASY-nLC-1200™ liquid chromatography system (Thermo Fisher, San Jose, USA). Identify the proteins against the UniProt protein database.

Western Blot

Tissue samples were quickly frozen in liquid nitrogen, ground with a mortar, and then lysed on ice by adding RIPA lysis buffer. For cell samples, after digestion and centrifugation, the supernatant was discarded, and lysis buffer was added to the pellet. The supernatant was collected by centrifugation, mixed with 5 × loading buffer, and boiled for denaturation. Proteins were separated by electrophoresis using 10%–12.5% SDS-PAGE gels, then transferred to a PVDF via the wet transfer method. After blocking with 5% non-fat milk, the membrane was incubated with primary antibodies against primary antibody. Following incubation with the secondary antibody, the target protein bands were detected using a chemiluminescence detector (BIO-RAD, United States).

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

Perform the experiment using the Pierce™ Co-Immunoprecipitation Kit according to the manufacturer's instructions: couple 10 μg of antibody to the resin. Subsequently, rotate and incubate the target protein suspension with the antibody-coupled resin on a mixer at 4 °C overnight. After six washes, elute and collect the coupled proteins using elution buffer for subsequent mass spectrometry detection or Western blotting.

Surface Plasmon Resonance (SPR)

Immobilize the purified His-G3BP1 and GST-TOM1 proteins on a CM5 chip (Biacore, GE Healthcare), pass the synthesized peptides or antibodies as the mobile phase over the chip, perform SPR analysis using a Biacore T200 instrument, and finally conduct multi-cycle kinetic fitting using Biacore evaluation software.

Extraction of stress granules

To induce stress granules (SGs) assembly, cells were treated with 0.5 mM sodium arsenite in culture medium for 1 h. Following treatment, the cells were rapidly rinsed twice with ice-cold PBS and lysed on ice for 20 min using a lysis buffer (50 mM Tris pH 7.6, 50 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 0.1% NP-40, 1 mM β-mercaptoethanol, 1% EDTA-free protease inhibitor cocktail, and 0.4 U/μL RNase inhibitor). The lysate was then centrifuged at 13,000×g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the resulting supernatant was collected for subsequent fractionation.

Protein fractionation was performed using an ÄKTA™ pure chromatography system (Cytiva, Uppsala, Sweden) with a 5 mL HiTrap™ Phenyl Sepharose HP hydrophobic interaction column. The supernatant was diluted 10-fold with the starting buffer (50 mM sodium phosphate, 1.5 M ammonium sulfate, pH 7.0) to promote protein binding. The column was pre-equilibrated with the starting buffer, and the sample was loaded at a flow rate of 2 mL/min. Under these high-salt conditions, proteins bind to the hydrophobic resin. Elution was achieved using a low-salt elution buffer (50 mM sodium phosphate, pH 7.0), which resulted in five distinct protein peaks.

The fractions corresponding to each peak were analyzed by western blotting for canonical SGs markers. The peak fraction enriched with SGs proteins was concentrated and used for subsequent analyzes, including nanoparticle tracking analysis for size determination, mass spectrometry for proteomic profiling, and RNA sequencing.

Nanoparticle Tracking Analysis (NTA)

The size distribution and concentration of SGs were characterized by Nanoparticle Tracking Analysis (NTA). Measurements were performed using a ZetaView® PMX instrument (Particle Metrix, Meerbusch, Germany). The instrument was calibrated according to the manufacturer's instructions using standard particles of known size prior to analysis.

The concentrated protein fraction enriched with SGs, obtained from hydrophobic interaction chromatography, was appropriately diluted in PBS to achieve a particle concentration within the optimal range for NTA detection. The diluted sample was loaded into a sterile syringe and injected into the instrument's sample cell under controlled temperature (e.g., 25 °C). For each measurement, 11 particle positions within the cell were automatically tracked and analyzed. At each position, two videos of 30 s were recorded by the built-in camera. The Brownian motion of individual particles was tracked and analyzed by the integrated ZetaView® software (version 8.x). The hydrodynamic diameter of the particles was calculated using the Stokes-Einstein equation. All measurements were performed in at least three technical replicates.

Protein Purification

All plasmids (His-G3BP1, GST-TOM1, NTF2L, ΔNTF2L, VHS, GAT and MYO6 BD) were purchased from Tsingke (Beijing, China). For transformation, 1 μg of plasmid was added to 100 μL of BL21 competent cells, followed by incubation on ice for 20 min, heat shock at 42 °C for 90 s, and immediate placement on ice for 3 min. Subsequently, 1 mL of antibiotic-free LB medium was added, and the mixture was incubated at 37 °C with shaking for 30 min. After centrifugation, the supernatant was removed, and the pellet was resuspended and spread onto a kanamycin-containing (or Ampicillin) solid LB plate for overnight incubation at 37 °C.

A single colony was picked and inoculated into 10 mL of kanamycin-supplemented (or Ampicillin) LB medium and cultured overnight. The culture was then scaled up to 1 L of LB medium and grown for 8 h. Protein expression was induced by the addition of IPTG, followed by incubation at 16 °C with shaking at 180 rpm for 16 h. Cells were harvested by centrifugation, and the pellet was resuspended in an appropriate volume of loading buffer. The resuspended cells were lysed by sonication until the lysate became clear, followed by centrifugation to remove cell debris. The supernatant was filtered through a 0.45 μm filter. The clarified lysate was subjected to purification using an ÄKTA Basic protein purification system (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA). The process included an initial crude purification step followed by size-exclusion chromatography. The target protein fractions were pooled, concentrated using an ultrafiltration device, and analyzed by Coomassie Brilliant Blue staining to confirm purity, resulting in the final purified protein product.

Liquid-Liquid Phase Separation (LLPS)

In vitro LLPS assays were conducted at room temperature, wherein purified protein (G3BP1, TOM1) and total RNA (at a defined concentration) were mixed in a solution containing 150 mM NaCl in a 200 μL microcentrifuge tube. The mixture was transferred to a chamber assembled from a coverslip and a glass slide with double-sided spacers. Except for the micrographs taken to capture LLPS dynamics over time, all micrographs were acquired immediately upon sample preparation, with image capture for each sample completed within 5 min, using Differential Interference Contrast (DIC) mode on a Leica fluorescence microscope (Leica STELLARIS 5, Wetzlar, Germany). The RNA used in these assays was total RNA extracted from HCT116 cells with an RNA extraction kit, and its concentration was quantified using a Nanodrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

Fluorescence Recovery After Photobleaching (FRAP)

Treat cells stably expressing mCherry-G3BP1 with sodium arsenite for 1 h, perform FRAP experiments using a Leica confocal microscope, and acquire confocal microscope images at different time points after photobleaching. Calculate the mobile fraction Mf (F represents fluorescence intensity).

Data Analysis

All experiments were independently repeated at least three times. GraphPad Prism 10, Image Lab, ImageJ, and Adobe Illustrator CS6 were used for graphing and statistical analysis. Differences were analyzed using a two-tailed Student's t-test and a two-way ANOVA (or mixed model), with statistical significance set at (P < 0.05). The final statistical comparison between groups was conducted after the blinding code was broken.
