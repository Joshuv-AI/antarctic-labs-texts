Enhanced Partial Fourier MRI With Zero-Shot Deep Untrained Priors
Authors: SO HYUN KANG, JIHOO KIM, JAEJIN CHO, CLARISSA ZIMMERMAN COOLEY, AMIR HEYDARI, BERKIN BILGIC, TAE HYUNG KIM
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
We present a novel method for partial Fourier reconstruction using a zero-shot unsupervised deep learning approach. Specifically, this method enhances partial Fourier reconstruction by integrating the traditional phase constraint with recent advancements in zero-shot deep learning techniques. Recently, a zero-shot deep learning method based on an untrained generative prior has been proposed, demonstrating effectiveness in multi-echo/contrast reconstruction. This approach is based on the assumption that MR images can be nonlinearly represented through an untrained artificial neural network, allowing for simultaneous image reconstruction and prior learning without requiring any training data. In this study, we integrate this framework with the virtual conjugate coil (VCC) phase constraint to enable robust partial Fourier reconstruction. We evaluated the proposed method on the fastMRI dataset, the QALAS multi-contrast dataset, and a low-field dataset. Our experiment results confirm that the proposed method yields improved reconstruction quality compared to existing methods, with approximately 1.15x to 2.5x reductions in NRMSE. The proposed technique enables robust partial Fourier reconstruction, proving valuable in numerous applications. Moreover, it adopts a zero-shot approach, eliminating the need for training data, which enhances its utility in scenarios where data collection is challenging.

INTRODUCTION

Partial Fourier methods [1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6] have been widely studied and utilized for accelerating magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). In partial Fourier MRI, certain regions of the k-space are not sampled, and reconstruction is conducted using phase constraints or conjugate symmetry in k-space. Conventional techniques, such as homodyne reconstruction, conjugate synthesis, and projection on convex sets (POCS) [1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6], have incorporated explicit phase constraints to fill in missing partial Fourier k-space samples. Subsequently, model-based methods like POCSENSE, Phase-constrained SENSE, VCC-SENSE, VCC-GRAPPA, and other related techniques [7], [8], [9], [10], [11], [12], [13] were developed by integrating phase constraints with parallel imaging approaches, such as SENSE [14], [15] and GRAPPA [16], enabling further acceleration with enhanced fidelity. Later advancements in model-based reconstruction [17], [18], [19], [20] and deep learning [21], [22] have further improved their performance and applicability.

Meanwhile, accelerated MRI through image reconstruction have been extensively studied [23], [24], [25]. In particular, constrained model-based reconstruction [23], [24], [25] and the latest deep learning techniques [26], [27], [28] are being widely used in various applications. Model-based reconstruction techniques employ optimization-based approaches grounded in statistical models [25], [29], where data consistency is derived from the likelihood distribution, while regularization is induced by a user-defined prior model. Model-based reconstruction does not require additional training data and relies solely on individual, scan-specific data, making it flexibly applicable to diverse MR acquisition scenarios. However, it potentially suffers from lengthy computation times and limited reconstruction performance in highly ill-posed cases.

Recent deep learning-based methods utilize artificial neural networks (ANNs) trained with substantial amounts of training data [26], [27], [28], [30], [31]. Initially, deep learning techniques adopted a physics-agnostic approach, allowing the model to learn the entire reconstruction process solely from data, without incorporating a forward model [26], [27], [28]. However, these models have the significant drawback of requiring vast amounts of training data. Recent approaches embed MR physics into deep learning models, enabling them to learn only the priors from the dataset [30], [31], [32]. This approach significantly reduces the training burden and the amount of required training data, while still maintaining strong reconstruction performance. Despite the advantages of fast inference and high performance, deep learning-based methods face significant challenges in obtaining large training datasets and struggle with issues of generalization and hallucination, particularly when there is a significant discrepancy between the training and the test data [33].

To address these issues, methods such as scan-specific deep learning and zero-shot deep learning [34], [35], [36], [37], [38], which do not depend on external training data, have been proposed. These approaches train and utilize ANNs without requiring external datasets [39], [40], enabling flexible application across a variety of acquisition settings and demonstrating reliable reconstruction performance. Notably, our preliminary version of this work, the Zero-Shot Deep Untrained Generative Prior (ZS-DUGP) [41] has successfully addressed multi-echo/contrast imaging by exploiting the premise that spatio-temporal MRI data can be nonlinearly compressed using ANNs. This approach uses images generated from untrained neural networks as priors for constrained image reconstruction. The neural network is trained solely on intermediate images generated during the reconstruction process, allowing for simultaneous image reconstruction and network training without any external training data. However, this preliminary version of ZS-DUGP did not effectively address the partial Fourier reconstruction problem.

In this study, we introduce a novel partial Fourier MRI reconstruction technique that integrates a phase constraint with the Zero-Shot Deep Untrained Generative Prior [41]. Specifically, by incorporating the virtual conjugate coil (VCC) [11], [13] phase constraint into the ZS-DUGP [41] framework, we enhance the robustness of partial Fourier reconstruction. The proposed technique has been validated with a fastMRI dataset, a QALAS dataset, and a low-field MR dataset, demonstrating its effectiveness across various MRI experiment scenarios compared to existing methods. The following sections will provide a detailed description of the proposed method and the experimental results.

Notably, the proposed Eq. (7) presents a large-scale non-convex objective function, and finding its optimal solution is both a non-trivial and computationally intensive problem. To address this challenge, our alternating minimization approach, optimizing m and θ alternately, can be highly effective in avoiding undesired local minima and accelerating the optimization process [47], [48].

The proposed process assumes that the image phase P is either already known or can be estimated in advance. In conventional phase-constrained reconstruction methods, the phase is often estimated from a low-resolution prescan or using a fully-sampled autocalibration signal (ACS) [9]. This approach can be effective because MRI phase typically exhibits smooth characteristics [17], and the central k-space region, containing low-frequency information, includes most of the phase information. However, this approach requires an additional burden for prescan or ACS acquisition.

Instead, in this study, we estimated the phase without relying on a prescan or a fully-sampled ACS [49]. We initially performed SENSE reconstruction on the undersampled data. We then selected only the central k-space region of the reconstructed image and utilized it as a synthetic ACS. This approach is potentially effective because the central k-space region generally exhibits high SNR, allowing for relatively accurate reconstruction. To further enhance the phase estimation and reduce sidelobes, we applied a Hamming window of the same size to this synthetic ACS. We then converted the synthetic ACS into the image domain, from which we extracted the low-resolution magnitude and the estimated phase. This approach facilitates the estimation of the image phase P, as illustrated in Fig. 2. Our empirical findings indicate that the size of the synthetic ACS did not significantly affect the final reconstruction results; we used a size of 64 × 64 in our study. Fig. 3 compares three phase images: the ideal phase (as a reference), the estimated phase from fully-sampled ACS (using the central 64 × 64 k-space region), and the phase estimated by our proposed method without fully-sampled ACS (using the undersampled ACS shown in Fig. 4). This comparison demonstrates that our method can estimate a phase comparable to that obtained from fully-sampled ACS, despite using only undersampled data. Notably, the phase estimation presented here depends on the quality of the synthetic ACS, potentially compromising its accuracy. To address this, we treated the image m to be reconstructed as complex-valued in Eq. (7), allowing us to compensate for the imperfections in the phase estimation process and enabling the restoration of residual phase.

METHODS

NETWORK ARCHITECTURE

The neural network fθ used for this method employs a custom convolutional decoder [36], [40] structure. The target image for training this network, as given in Eq. (9), uses m(i+1), splitting this complex-valued tensor into real and imaginary components that are concatenated along the channel dimension. The input tensor z is randomly generated with a size of 8 × 8 and 24 channels. This network is made up of 6 upsampling blocks. Within each upsampling block, the data passes through one (1st block) or two (2nd to 5th blocks) 2D convolution layers, followed by a 2D transposed convolution, with each layer interconnected by a ReLU activation function. The final (6th) block consists of three 2D convolutions without a transposed convolution. As it passes through each block, the number of channels changes from (input channels = 24) to 48, 96, 96, 96, 96, and then to (output channels = 2c), respectively. In each transposed convolution layer, the feature size doubles in both the horizontal and vertical dimensions, resulting in a final output size of 512 × 512. This output is then cropped to align with the dimensions of the target MR image m(i+1). If the target MR image m(i+1) exceeds 512 × 512 in size, the network can be adjusted by either appending additional upsampling blocks or increasing the size of the input tensor to expand the output size. Because the network consists solely of convolutional layers, there are no restrictions on the input size, and the number of upsampling blocks can also be easily customized to match the output image size. The loss function for training this network employs MSE loss, as presented in Eq. (9), where the network is iteratively trained and fine-tuned with an updated target m(i+1). DATASETS

For the evaluation of the proposed method, three different datasets were prepared. The first dataset is the fastMRI brain dataset [33], [50], from which a fully-sampled axial FLAIR k-space data was selected from the validation set. From the original (readout×PE)=(640×320) sized data, the oversampled and zero-padded regions were removed, resulting in a 2D dataset with a matrix size of 320 × 224. For convenience, the original 18-channel data was coil-compressed into 12 channels. For the experiment, the data was retrospectively undersampled using a 1D 4x-uniform mask combined with 5/8 partial Fourier, resulting in the effective acceleration factor of 6.4x. The data and sampling mask are shown in Fig. 4. We compared the proposed method with SENSE [14], [15], VCC-SENSE [11], [13], LORAKS [17], [20] (SENSE-LORAKS version with S-matrix), MoDL [31] (trained with 710 slices from the fastMRI validation dataset), and ZS-SSL [38] techniques.

The second dataset is the 3D-QALAS [51], [52], [53] dataset acquired from a Siemens scanner with a 32-channel head coil, with the Approval of the Institutional Review Board at Massachusetts General Hospital. The 3D-QALAS sequence consists of five readouts, with the initial readout occurring after a T2 preparation module, and the subsequent four readouts taking place after an inversion pulse, which is used to capture T1 dynamics. These five multi-contrast images enable simultaneous measurement of T1 and T2 maps. Acquiring such a large-scale 3D multi-contrast dataset takes a considerable amount of scan time, and accelerating this process could be advantageous. The obtained data have a matrix size 208 × 208 × 176, with a resolution of 1.15mm × 1.15mm × 1.15mm. Elliptical regions were sampled in the ky−kz domain, involving corner cutting in the k-space plane. For the experiment, a single 2D slice with size 208 × 176 from the readout dimension was extracted. The data was retrospectively undersampled using a 2D variable density mask combined with partial Fourier, with an effective acceleration factor of 16.3x. The data and sampling masks are illustrated in Figs. 5, 6, 7, and 8. The proposed method was compared with SENSE [14], [15], VCC-SENSE [11], [13], the subspace method with locally low-rank constraint (Subspace+LLR) [54], [55], J-LORAKS [49], MoDL [31] (trained with 1,040 slices of the QALAS dataset), ZS-SSL [38], and ZS-SSL with joint multi-contrast reconstruction constraint (J-ZS-SSL) [56].

The third dataset is a low-field dataset [57], [58] obtained from a 73mT, 50 kg portable “Halbach dome” head scanner, with the Approval of the Institutional Review Board at Massachusetts General Hospital. The matrix size of the data is 256 × 101, acquired with a single coil, and a prospective partial Fourier sampling (1.3x) was applied. The proposed method was applied to this dataset to demonstrate the feasibility of partial Fourier reconstruction and the potential for image quality improvement. The data and sampling mask are shown in Fig. 10.

DISCUSSION

Previous sections described the advantages of the proposed method across various MR applications. This section will discuss several considerations for employing the proposed method.

HYPERPARAMETERS

The proposed method requires the adjustment of two hyperparameters: λ, as utilized in Eq. (8), and the early stopping iteration number for network training, as outlined in Eq. (9). Focusing on the latter, Eq. (9) is analogous to the Deep Image Prior (DIP) [39], and employing an overparameterized neural network can result in overfitting to the incomplete image m(i+1), leading to suboptimal outcomes. To mitigate this, DIP introduced early stopping as a tunable hyperparameter. In this study, we have also implemented early stopping (denoted as t) and tuned both λ and t to minimize the NRMSE. Specifically for t, we assessed the reconstruction performance by increasing t from 100 to 1000 in increments of 100, and from 1000 to 10000 in increments of 1000. For each case, we measured the NRMSE of the final reconstruction result. During this experiment, λ was fixed at 0.1. Similarly for λ, we checked the performance by increasing it from 10−4 to 103 in factors of 10. For each case, we measured the NRMSE of the final reconstruction result. During this experiment, t was fixed at 1000. The results are shown in Fig. 11. The selected hyperparameters were t=1000 and λ=0.1, which we used consistently across all datasets. As shown in Fig. 11, our empirical findings with the fastMRI experiment indicate that the results remain stable across a broad spectrum of hyperparameter choices. However, if tuning these hyperparameters is practically challenging, automated hyperparameter tuning methods are available (e.g., SURE-based methods [59], [60], training-based methods [38]), or hyperparameters used on a similar dataset can be reused.

Additionally, an analysis was conducted on the number of upsampling blocks of the suggested untrained network presented in Sec. III-A. The NRMSE values were assessed by increasing the number of the upsampling blocks from the original 6 to 9, with other hyperparameters held constant. The results reveal that performance is relatively insensitive to the number of upsampling blocks, as shown in Fig. 11.

COMPUTATION

Another consideration is the time and memory usage required for reconstruction. For instance, reconstructing the 2D fastMRI dataset requires approximately 5 minutes of computation time and 0.5GB of GPU memory on an Nvidia Tesla T4 GPU. Similarly, reconstructing a 2D slice of QALAS data (32 channels, 5 echoes) requires 20 minutes and 1.1GB of GPU memory using the same hardware. Reconstructing 3D data in its entirety is expected to demand significantly more memory and time. However, this experiment was a proof-of-concept implementation, with no specific optimization for reconstruction time and memory. Enhancements in hardware, algorithms, and parallelization are expected to lead to substantial improvements in both reconstruction time and memory usage. Another promising approach is to utilize transfer learning [56], [61]. By introducing a pre-trained network, we can perform the network training in Eq. (9) solely through fine-tuning, significantly reducing the overall computation time. Fig. 12 compares the reconstruction results and computation time between the original training method using an untrained network, and transfer learning using a network pre-trained on a similar 2D slice and fine-tuned on the target dataset. While both methods yield nearly identical reconstruction quality, the original training technique requires approximately 5 minutes, whereas transfer learning completes the reconstruction in only about 45 seconds. These potential improvements are worth exploring in future work.

CURRENT LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS

A potential limitation of the proposed technique is its reliance on accurate phase estimation. While our synthetic ACS approach shows promise, it may encounter challenges in scenarios with extreme undersampling, complex phase characteristics, or rapid phase variations, which could result in inaccurate phase estimation. Future work could focus on developing more robust phase estimation techniques that incorporate advanced reconstruction methods, as well as exploring the joint optimization of phase and magnitude during the iterative reconstruction process. Extending our method to higher-dimensional data (e.g., 3D MRI) also presents challenges, including increased computational complexity, memory requirements, longer acquisition times, and difficulties in maintaining SNR. Future research could address these challenges by developing GPU-efficient high-dimensional implementations, exploring data partitioning and parallel processing techniques, and investigating transfer learning approaches using pre-trained networks. Despite these challenges, the method’s potential for practical implementation includes addressing various challenging MRI scenarios by accelerating scan speeds and improving SNR without the need for a training dataset. These characteristics make the method particularly useful in diverse low-field and portable MRI settings, as well as in motionprone imaging scenarios such as pediatric, musculoskeletal, and cardiovascular imaging. Furthermore, it has the potential to accelerate EPI acquisition for functional MRI and enhance data acquisition in various quantitative MRI applications, thereby improving overall imaging efficiency and quality.

CONCLUSION

In this study, we introduce a novel zero-shot partial Fourier reconstruction method using deep untrained generative prior. The proposed method employs VCC phase-constraint for data consistency and integrates regularization based on the concept that MR images can be nonlinearly compressed and represented using ANNs. This strategy facilitates simultaneous prior learning and image reconstruction, eliminating the need for an extra dataset for the network training. The effectiveness of the proposed technique was demonstrated through experiments on the fastMRI, QALAS, and low-field datasets, with results showing improved performance compared to existing methods. The proposed method can be readily adapted to various acquisition conditions, including different sampling trajectories (including non-uniform ones), pulse sequences, and coil geometries, without the requirement for additional data collection for network training. We believe this method is expected to be useful in various MR applications utilizing partial Fourier acquisition, such as echo planar imaging (EPI), quantitative imaging, and low-field MR.
