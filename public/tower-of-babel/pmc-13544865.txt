Acute Turkey Berry Poisoning Mimicking a Myasthenic Variant: A Rare Presentation
Authors: Bhargav Mundlapudi, Amirtha Varsheni, H Santosh, Santosh Kumar
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

Many berries are safe to eat, but some species contain powerful toxins that may lead to severe symptoms or even death. Toxicity varies by species and the amount ingested. Common poisonous berries—including American bittersweet, juniper, pokeweed, and holly—usually produce gastrointestinal symptoms such as nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, and dizziness. Others, like mistletoe, yew, cotoneaster, and Turkey berries, may affect the central nervous system, leading to altered mental status, delirium, seizures, stupor, or visual disturbances.[1] Consumption of small quantities may not always require medical care. Outbreaks of Turkey berry poisoning have been reported since 1867, with documented clusters in Jamaica and more recent cases in Toronto, Florida, and New York (2003, 2004, 2006).[23]

A 67-year-old woman with pre-existing conditions of diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and hypothyroidism presented to the emergency department with sudden-onset slurred speech and lethargy. Her vital signs and general physical examination were unremarkable, but neurological assessment revealed ptosis, dysarthria, and diminished motor strength (graded 4/5). Concurrently, her 71-year-old husband, who had no reported comorbidities, was admitted with a similar clinical picture of dysarthria and lethargy. His physical examination revealed bilateral ptosis, dysarthria, notable right wrist weakness, and preserved motor strength (5/5) in all extremities. Upon further questioning, the family revealed a history of consuming Turkey berries around 3 PM the previous day [Figure 1]. The female patient, who had been asymptomatic before ingestion, had consumed a larger quantity of the berries than her husband. Diagnostic imaging, including cerebral magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and angiography, performed to exclude an acute stroke, yielded normal results. Spinal MRI and urine toxicology were also unremarkable. Serum pseudocholinesterase and creatinine phosphokinase levels were normal.

A positive response was observed following a trial of 0.5 mg intramuscular neostigmine, with marked improvement in ptosis and dysarthria and restoration of full motor power (5/5). Intramuscular neostigmine was subsequently switched to oral pyridostigmine 60 mg administered every eight hours. Over the subsequent 48-hour period, symptoms gradually abated, allowing discontinuation of the pyridostigmine regimen. Electrophysiological studies, including repetitive nerve stimulation (RNS), showed no evidence of a decremental response, and nerve conduction studies of the bilateral facial nerves did not reveal any significant abnormality. Both patients were subsequently discharged on the fourth day of hospitalization. No similar clinical events or long-term sequelae were observed at the 6-month follow-up.

Turkey berries, scientifically known as Solanum torvum, belong to the Solanaceae family. They are commonly referred to by various names—Turkey berry, Jamaican susumber, devil’s fig, prickly nightshade, rock nightshade, and macaw bush. These berries share lineage with nightshade plants such as potatoes, tomatoes, peppers, and eggplants.[4] They contain steroidal glycoalkaloids, chiefly solanine, which decrease as the fruit ripens, making fully ripened berries generally safer. Variations in cultivation, storage, and temperature can increase their solanine content, potentially converting ordinarily edible berries into toxic ones.[5]

Solanine, a key glycoalkaloid, acts as a natural pesticide and is variably distributed across plant parts.[4] Symptoms typically appear 2–24 hours after exposure and depend on the ingested concentration.[5] High doses may cause gastrointestinal and neurological symptoms, including nausea, abdominal pain, diarrhea, dizziness, confusion, hallucinations, paralysis, fever, jaundice, dilated pupils, or hypothermia.[4]

The exact mechanism of action of solanine glycoalkaloids is not fully understood.[4] Solanine toxicity likely results from the following mechanisms:

Cholinergic system disruption: Solanine inhibits acetylcholinesterase and activates cholinergic receptors, causing excessive muscarinic and nicotinic stimulation and widespread neurological manifestations.

Further research is needed to clarify the molecular events responsible for solanine-induced mitochondrial dysfunction. High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) is the preferred method for quantifying glycoalkaloids, although enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits provide a rapid alternative for screening.[4]

Initial management focuses on limiting toxin absorption through gastric lavage with dilute potassium permanganate, followed by activated charcoal. Most mild cases resolve within 24 hours.[46]

A systematic review of Jamaican susumber poisoning presenting as stroke mimics noted high frequencies of dysarthria (94%), gait instability, blurry vision (59%), nystagmus or ocular deviations (47%), gastrointestinal symptoms (41%), and autonomic features (29%). Intensive care unit (ICU) care was required in 35% of cases, with 18% needing intubation. Most patients (88%) recovered within one week, though some required hospitalization for up to one month.[3]

Compared with typical solanine poisoning profiles, our patients mainly demonstrated bulbar weakness—ptosis, dysphagia, and dysarthria—closely resembling myasthenia gravis, which is an uncommon presentation. Neuromuscular junction–blocking toxins such as botulinum toxin or neuroparalytic venoms can mimic myasthenic paralysis.[7] Similar cases of susumber toxicity mimicking severe neurological disease have been described. Smith et al.[5] documented a cluster in Toronto where six family members developed susumber berry toxicity. Two individuals exhibited rapidly progressive neurological deterioration resembling botulism; however, empiric administration of botulinum antitoxin led to further decline in their motor function. A notable finding in our cases was the dramatic response to neostigmine, suggesting that solanine may transiently impair neuromuscular transmission through cholinergic receptor blockade. This observation highlights the need for further research on the neurotoxicity of solanine glycoalkaloids.

This atypical presentation emphasizes the broad and sometimes unexpected neurotoxic effects of solanine glycoalkaloids. When multiple individuals present with bulbar palsy or stroke-like symptoms after a shared meal, susumber/Turkey berry poisoning should be strongly considered.

Turkey berry (S. torvum) poisoning remains an underrecognized toxicological entity that can closely mimic serious neurological conditions, including stroke and myasthenic syndromes. Our two cases highlight an unusual predominance of bulbar symptoms with striking responsiveness to neostigmine, suggesting transient cholinergic dysfunction at the neuromuscular junction induced by solanine glycoalkaloids. The clinical overlap with acute neurological emergencies poses a significant diagnostic challenge, underscoring the importance of obtaining a detailed dietary history, particularly when multiple individuals present simultaneously with similar deficits after a shared meal. Early recognition of this toxin-mediated presentation can prevent unnecessary investigations, reduce morbidity, and guide appropriate management. Greater awareness and further research into the neurotoxic mechanisms of solanine are essential to improve diagnostic accuracy and clinical outcomes in future cases.
