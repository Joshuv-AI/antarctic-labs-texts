Screen-based simulation assessment for emergency medicine learners: a rapid review
Authors: Patrick Zeniecki, Rebecca Kamens, Jared Kilpatrick, Dimitrios Papanagnou, Xiao Chi Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSimulation has played a vital role in training medical professionals. The COVID-19 Pandemic has highlighted the need for physically distant educational assessment methods. Screen-based simulation (SBS) demonstrates one alternative to traditional in-person simulation methods to assess learners clinical reasoning and communication skills. This study aims to compare in-person simulation to SBS as assessment methods in Emergency Medicine (EM).MethodsA rapid review literature search methodology of an electronic database (PubMed) was performed in February 2022 with search terms such as “Computer Simulation,” “Patient Simulation,” “Simulation Training,” “Education, Distance,” “Virtual OSCE,” “Academic Performance,” or “Emergency Medicine.” Reference lists of relevant articles were manually analyzed for additional studies. Studies were manually reviewed by multiple authors following strict inclusion/exclusion criteria.Results751 articles were identified based on title and abstract. Sixty articles were selected for retrieval, of which seven pilot and small population studies were included. Study participants varied based on experience level. Three key findings were derived from these studies. First, SBS is comparable to in-person simulation as a clinical competence and communication skill assessment method when evaluated by independent raters and mock EM oral board examination scores. Second, SBS is capable of discerning EM learners by educational level given SBS/serious game mean clinical score clustering. Lastly, various SBS software demonstrated strong participant interest across the EM learner spectrum through subjective exit questionaries.ConclusionCurrent research regarding SBS as an assessment method for EM learners is severely limited as demonstrated by low number of included studies and small populations. Even so, SBS shows promise as a possible alternative and/or supplement to traditional in-person simulation to assess clinical reasoning/communication skills and discern learners by educational level. Further large-scale studies are required to establish the validity of SBS as an assessment method.Keywords: Simulation, Virtual Simulation, Screen-Based Simulation, Computer-Based Simulation, Medical Education, Emergency Medicine

Introduction

Virtual simulation (VS), as defined as by the Healthcare Simulation Dictionary is “a type of simulation that injects humans in a central role by exercising motor control skills, decision, skills, or communication skills” [1]. This study specifically sought to analyze the medical educational impact of Screen-Based Simulation (SBS); a subset of VS defined as “a form of simulation in which a clinical scenario with one or more patients is presented through a digital screen surface, using graphical images and text, similar to popular gaming format, where the operator interacts with the interface using keyboard, mouse, joystick, or other input device. The programs can provide feedback to, and track actions of learners for assessment, eliminating the need for an instructor” [1].

SBS is differentiated from Virtual Reality (VR) and Augmented Reality (AR) based on SBS’s lack of a 3D environment and synthetic stimuli to support the 3D environment, respectively. VS has been proposed as the future of simulation modalities due to its’ advantages over in-person simulation such as: standardization, automated feedback, flexible availability, and a relatively decreased instruction time with relation to costs [2]. Additionally, SBS adds these advantages without taking away from simulation’s foundation of reproducibility, repeatability, and exposure to infrequent scenarios without exposing patients to avoidable risk. This review explores the application of SBS’s advantages over in-person simulation regarding clinical assessment. More recently, SBS shows significant promise due to the COVID-19 Pandemic’s educational impact [3]. A need for physically distant but effective educational tools has seen SBS’s incorporation into educational curricula. SBS provides an ideal opportunity to repeatedly apply knowledge, develop professional attitudes, and most importantly minimize patient risk, all within a standardized, replicable environment [4]. A review by Coyne et al. further empowers SBS’s plausibility as an assessment tool of healthcare students’ clinical competency, specifically noting that various “virtual simulation programs enhanced students’ knowledge, confidence, and virtual clinical performance relevant to the clinical skills taught” [5].

However, little is known about SBS’s application as an assessment tool when compared to in-person assessment methods (e.g. Objective Structured Clinical Examinations). This review aims to compare in-person simulations and SBS as clinical assessment tools within the context of Emergency Medicine (EM) due to EM’s early adoption and curricular integration of various educational simulation modalities, including SBS [6].

Methods

Search strategy/sources

We performed a rapid review methodology as described by Khangura et al. [7]. Khangura describes eight steps in their approach to rapid reviews; while not all of the steps apply to this study, we conducted a Needs Assessment (step 1), Question Development and Refinement (step 2), Systematic literature search (step 3), Screening and selection of studies (step 5), and Narrative synthesis of included studies (step 6), all of which are described in the following figures and tables below. Rapid reviews, in contrast to systematic reviews, can be used for narrow scopes of focus, using limited database searches, and require fewer reviewers for screening and data extraction. This type of review was completed secondary to time constraints. Three authors (RK, XZ, and PZ) performed a literature search of a single electronic database (PubMed) in February 2022 on such terms such as “Computer Simulation,” “Patient Simulation,” “Simulation Training,” “Education, Distance,” “Virtual OSCE,” “Academic Performance,” or “Emergency Medicine” with no limiting date range. A detailed search terms list is available in Appendix 1. Reference lists of relevant articles were manually analyzed for additional studies possibly missed during the electronic search.

We sought studies comparing SBS and traditional means of in-person assessment for EM learners (e.g. nurses, students, residents, and attendings). Learner assessment focused on clinical reasoning, clinical decision-making skills, and communication skills. The review resulted in three main takeaways:

Comparable Performance Between SBS and Traditional SimulationTwo studies reported no statistically significant difference in assessment scores when comparing SBS with traditional simulation modalities. Youngblood et al. found that participants trained via screen-based simulation performed similarly to those trained with instrumented mannequin-based simulation in terms of mean summary scores as assessed by independent raters [8]. McGrath et al. demonstrated that EM residents who underwent a virtual oral board exam in the SBS platform Second Life had “no difference in pass rates, no difference in examination scores for the American Board of Emergency Medicine’s (ABEM) individual competency categories, and results consistent with mock oral examinations administered in prior years” [9].

SBS Differentiates Learners by Experience LevelPrevious work by Hawkins et al. in 2004 found that SBS lacked the ability to differentiate learners based on their experience or specialty [10]. However, more recent evidence supports the ability of SBS platforms to distinguish between learners of varying clinical experience. Roy et al. demonstrated that screen-based simulation could differentiate medical students from practicing physicians based on performance, although the study was limited by a small sample size (N = 12) and single-case design [11]. Gerard et al. extended this finding with a larger sample spanning from medical students to attending physicians, showing clear clustering of scores by experience level and a negative correlation between experience and task completion time [12]. Chatterjee et al. used the Human Diagnosis Project (Human Dx) platform to show that diagnostic accuracy and efficiency varied with clinical experience across a large-scale participant pool, further supporting SBS as a discriminative tool [13].

Positive Learner Perceptions of SBSLearners expressed high engagement, perceived realism, and ease of use with SBS platforms. Youngblood et al. noted favorable participant feedback on both SBS and traditional simulations, with SBS rated highly for immersion and perceived utility [8]. In McGrath et al., residents were introduced to a new SBS software, Second Life, and found the interface intuitive and preferred the screen-based format. Specifically, EM residents commented on the improved realism of SBS for their oral boards due to ease of accessing labs, images, and visual patient clues consistent with prior findings by Schwaab et al. [9, 14]. Gerard et al. found similarly strong endorsements for PediatricSim, a serious game platform, with learners across levels describing it as overwhelmingly “engaging, realistic, easy to use, and had educational value” [12].

Discussion

SBS has historically been used to assess emergency responders’ preparedness for major casualty events due to its ability to generate fictitious environments [8, 15–19]. Today, SBS has evolved as a supplemental teaching tool for the standard medical curricula to enhance learning outcomes [9]. This review demonstrates that screen-based simulation offers a comparative utility as an assessment tool, compared to in-person simulation when used in medical education, particularly within emergency medicine.

One of the central advantages of SBS is its scalability and standardization. In-person simulation often requires significant direct observation by trained faculty that can be limited due to personnel, timing, and physical locale challenges. Furthermore, multi-learner scenarios may require additional expert faculty as nuances may be missed between each learner during complex scenarios [20]. Two studies (Youngblood et al., and McGrath et al.) report no significant differences in assessment outcomes when comparing SBS to traditional simulation formats, including mannequin-based scenarios and oral board examinations [8, 9]. These findings suggest that SBS may serve as a cost-effective and viable alternative (especially when considering multi-center efforts) for assessing clinical competence, particularly when in-person simulation resources are limited [21]. However, the reliance of SBS may result in a divestment of available education funding in content creation, computer programming, performance tracking, or outsourcing this effort to third-party companies with expensive subscription models to maintain the application and data analytics.

More notably, the literature on SBS remains constrained by significant limitations. Many existing studies are pilot in nature, rely on small sample sizes, and often lack rigorous psychometric evaluation and validity testing. Even so, questions remain about the generalizability of these results across learner populations, specialties, and case types. SBS modalities also vary widely in design, content, and interactivity, making it difficult to draw overarching conclusions or compare studies directly. Finally, these studies predominately focused on individual learning experiences, whereas in-person simulation can include multi-learner scenarios with tailored debriefing that focus on interprofessional interactions, task delegations, and procedural skills required to succeed in a real-life emergency.

One last final note is the difficulty with standardization that impacted this study. Evaluating and analyzing healthcare simulation remains challenging due to the persistent lack of standardization in terminology and conceptual frameworks. Despite efforts by organizations such as the Society for Simulation in Healthcare (SSH) to establish common language through its Healthcare Simulation Dictionary, variability endures [1]. The dictionary itself reflects this complexity—many key terms (e.g., fidelity, competence, debriefing) include multiple definitions to accommodate differing contexts, disciplines, and theoretical perspectives. While this inclusivity enhances accessibility across diverse simulation practices, it simultaneously complicates comparative evaluation, data synthesis, and the development of unified metrics for simulation effectiveness and quality.

Limitations

Most importantly, the various studies within this review included small sample sizes, particularly Roy et al. (12 study participants), limiting the power of conclusions able to be drawn [11]. Though care was taken during the initial literature search to cast a wide net, the possibility of alternative simulation terms to describe the specific type of simulation in question remains a real possibility. This limitation is further expounded upon due to the use of a single electronic database and rapid review methodology likely restricted the number of studies included. As the field of simulation continues to develop at an astonishing rate, future literature reviews will become crucial to establish SBS’s definitive educational role, if any.

Additionally, the heterogeneity of the included studies again limits the power of conclusions to be drawn. Only 3 of the 7 included studies directly compared SBS to in-person simulation while the remaining studies compared SBS to other forms of assessment (written exams, critical action checklists, etc.).

Conclusion

Current research regarding SBS as an assessment method for EM learners is severely limited, as demonstrated by the low number of included studies and small populations. SBS benefits from its scalability and standardization and serves as a cost-effective and viable alternative for assessing clinical competence for individual learners. As more educators become comfortable with this new technology, we believe SBS can be used as a means of assessing EM students’ knowledge and clinical competency. Implementation logistics, simulation optimization, and the need for continued technological advances remain as barriers to SBS’s broader adoption in healthcare. However, further studies are required to establish strict standardization criteria for further implementation.
