Genome‐wide network analysis identifies the lncRNA‐92467/miR‐205‐5p/PTPRM/CAMs axis in a rat model of hypoxic pulmonary hypertension
Authors: Yan‐Ying Shen, Yi‐Bing Chen, Xin Li, Xiang Xu, Ge Guo, Han‐Lu Li, Yong‐Ming Yao, Chun‐Lei Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHypoxic pulmonary hypertension (HPH) is a progressive chronic disorder characterized by elevated mean pulmonary arterial pressure. It severely impairs right ventricular function and ultimately causes right ventricular failure. Clarifying the complex pathogenesis of HPH remains a critical challenge.MethodsRat HPH models were established by exposure to a hypobaric hypoxia chamber for 4 consecutive weeks. Lung tissues were harvested for RNA sequencing. The Coding‐Non‐Coding Index (CNCI) and Coding Potential Calculator (CPC) were used to construct a competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) network. Pulmonary artery endothelial cells were transfected with modified lncRNA‐92467 or miR‐205‐5p to assess their regulatory functions. Finally, mice were injected with AAV‐Control or AAV‐PTPRM shRNA (2.5 × 1011 v.g./mL per mouse). Hemodynamic and echocardiographic analyses were performed to evaluate the in vivo role of PTPRM.ResultsWe identified that lncRNA‐92467 functions as a competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) by competitively binding to miR‐205‐5p, thereby upregulating its target gene PTPRM. Knockdown of lncRNA‐92467 significantly promoted pulmonary artery endothelial cell proliferation and migration. These effects were abolished by a miR‐205‐5p inhibitor, which also restored PTPRM expression. Functional assays confirmed that miR‐205‐5p enhanced cell proliferation and migration while inhibiting apoptosis; these effects were reversed by PTPRM overexpression. In vivo, exogenous PTPRM overexpression attenuated HPH progression in mice. Mechanistically, PTPRM directly targeted cell adhesion molecules (CAMs), which increased catenin production, thereby inhibiting abnormal cell proliferation and migration and promoting apoptosis.ConclusionsOur findings reveal a novel lncRNA‐92467/miR‐205‐5p/PTPRM/CAMs regulatory axis in HPH pathogenesis. These results suggest that PTPRM may serve as a promising therapeutic target for HPH.Keywords: co‐expression networks, competing endogenous RNA, high‐throughput sequencing, hypoxic pulmonary hypertension

INTRODUCTION

Hypoxic pulmonary hypertension (HPH) is a chronic progressive disease, primarily characterized by a mean pulmonary artery pressure > 20 mmHg. 1 HPH is pathologically characterized by progressive vascular remodeling of the distal pulmonary arteries, which results in elevated pulmonary arterial pressure, subsequent right ventricular (RV) overload, and ultimately RV failure and mortality. 2 Chronic hypoxia serves as a primary etiological factor in the development of HPH. Vascular remodeling includes all pathological changes caused by endothelial cell apoptosis, smooth muscle cell proliferation, and fibroblast hyperplasia. 3 , 4 Despite considerable efforts over recent decades to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying HPH, a comprehensive understanding remains elusive. Accordingly, the identification of novel diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets for HPH is urgently needed.

Regulatory networks composed of non‐coding RNAs and protein‐coding genes play critical roles in the pathogenesis of cardiovascular diseases. 5 Among them, long non‐coding RNAs (lncRNAs), acting as molecular sponges, competitively bind to microRNAs (miRNAs) to relieve their inhibition on downstream target genes, forming ceRNA (competing endogenous RNA) axes that are widely involved in cell fate determination and disease progression. 6 Previous studies have confirmed that specific lncRNA/miRNA/mRNA axes participate in endothelial‐mesenchymal transition and phenotypic transformation of vascular smooth muscle cells by regulating TGF‐β, HIF‐1α and other signaling pathways, providing new insights into the mechanism of HPH. 7 , 8

Cell adhesion molecules (CAMs), key mediators of cell–cell and cell‐extracellular matrix interactions, are essential drivers of pulmonary vascular remodeling. CAMs not only mediate immune cell recruitment and infiltration but also regulate cytoskeleton rearrangement and focal adhesion formation, thereby affecting proliferation, migration and barrier function of vascular wall cells, playing a central role in inflammatory amplification and structural damage in HPH. 9 Protein tyrosine phosphatase (PTPRM), a transmembrane phosphatase, maintains vascular homeostasis by modulating cell adhesion and signal transduction. Its dysregulation may disrupt intercellular junction stability and promote pathological pulmonary vascular remodeling. 10

Although previous studies have revealed altered expression of several lncRNAs, miRNAs and functional genes in HPH, systematic genome‐wide dissection of the integrated regulatory network involving lncRNA‐miRNA‐mRNA and CAMs remains largely unexplored. In particular, whether lncRNA‐92467, as a potential core regulator, modulates the expression of PTPRM and CAM family members by sponging miR‐205‐z and thereby affects adhesion, migration and inflammation of pulmonary vascular cells under hypoxia has not been reported.

METHODS

Animals

Adult male Sprague–Dawley rats (200 ± 20 g) were randomly divided into normoxic control and chronic hypobaric hypoxia groups (n = 12 per group). Hypoxia rats were exposed to a hypobaric hypoxia chamber for 4 consecutive weeks. The chamber was set at a simulated altitude of 5500 m (≈50% normal oxygen partial pressure), with temperature and humidity maintained at 28 ± 2°C and 60% ± 5%, respectively. This environment simulates low‐pressure hypoxia. After 4 weeks, rats were anesthetized with isoflurane for transthoracic echocardiography, then tracheotomized and mechanically ventilated. A Millar SPR‐838 pressure‐volume catheter was inserted into the right ventricle and advanced into the pulmonary artery for hemodynamic measurements. Hearts and right lungs were snap‐frozen in liquid nitrogen; fresh hearts and upper left lungs were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for H&E, Masson and immunofluorescence staining. All rats were provided by the Animal Experiment Center of Chinese PLA General Hospital. All procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Chinese PLA General Hospital (approval No. 2020‐X16‐01).

RNA sequencing

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) following the manufacturer's protocol. RNA quality was assessed using an Agilent Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, CA, USA). Purified mRNAs and lncRNAs were fragmented, reverse‐transcribed into cDNA with random primers, and second‐strand cDNA was synthesized using DNA polymerase I, RNase H and dUTP. cDNA fragments were then purified with a QiaQuick PCR extraction kit (Qiagen, Venlo, The Netherlands), end‐repaired, A‐tailed, and ligated to Illumina sequencing adapters. Second‐strand cDNA was digested with uracil‐N‐glycosylase (UNG). Library fragments were size‐selected by agarose gel electrophoresis, PCR‐amplified, and sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq™ 4000 platform.

Competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) network construction

Coding‐Non‐Coding Index (CNCI) distinguishes protein‐coding and non‐coding sequences effectively by profiling adjoining nucleotide triplets, independently of known annotations, and it shows good performance in classifying incomplete transcripts and sense‐antisense pairs. The CNCI software can be accessed at http://www.bioinfo.org/software/cnci. Both CNCI 11 and Coding Potential Calculator (CPC, version 0.9‐r2; http://cpc.cbi.pku.edu.cn) 12 were employed to assess the protein‐coding potential of novel transcripts under default parameters. Transcripts predicted to be non‐coding by both tools were defined as lncRNAs. Pearson correlation coefficients (PCC) were calculated between differentially expressed lncRNAs (DElncRNAs) and mRNAs (DEmRNAs). Pairs with |r| > 0.8 and p < 0.05 were regarded as significantly correlated and used to construct a co‐expression network, which was visualized by Cytoscape 3.6.0. Spearman rank correlation coefficients (SCC) 13 , 14 , 15 were applied to evaluate correlations between miRNAs and mRNAs/lncRNAs. For ceRNA network construction, negatively correlated pairs (SCC < −0.7) were retained. Only triplets in which the lncRNA and mRNA shared a common miRNA were targeted by this miRNA, negatively correlated with the miRNA, and positively correlated with each other (PCC > 0.8) were included. The ceRNA network was visualized using Cytoscape 3.6.0 (https://www.cytoscape.org/). Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were performed for DEmRNAs and predicted target genes of DEmiRNAs and DElncRNAs. Terms and pathways with adjusted p < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched.

RNA extraction and reverse transcription‐quantitative real‐time PCR (RT‐qPCR) analysis

Total RNA was extracted using a TRIzol reagent kit (Invitrogen, USA) following the manufacturer's protocol. RNA was precipitated with chloroform and dissolved in DEPC‐treated water. Then, 5 μg of RNA was reverse‐transcribed with the iScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio‐Rad, USA). Primer sequences for RT‐qPCR are listed in Table 1. qPCR was performed using PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, China) under the following conditions: 95°C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 5 s, 60°C for 20 s, and 65°C for 15 s. The PCR was performed in accordance with the MIQE standards.

HPAEC culture and transfection with miRNA mimic/inhibitor and assay of luciferase reporter gene expression

HPAECs were purchased from ScienCell (San Diego, USA, product number #3000). The culture conditions were ECM medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin and 1% ECG, and the cells were cultured in a 37°C, 5% CO2 incubator. miR‐205‐z mimics and inhibitors were synthesized by KeyGEN BioTECH (Nanjing, China). HPAEC cells were transfected with mimics or inhibitors using Lipofectamine® RNAiMAX Reagent. For dual‐luciferase reporter assays, cells were co‐transfected with pGL3‐Basic reporter plasmids using Lipofectamine 3000 and the indicated miRNA mimics using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX. Transfected cells were available for cell modeling after 48 h of cell transfection. Cells were subjected to hypoxia for 24 h to induce cell injury. Then, luciferase activity was detected using the Dual‐Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega, USA). Firefly luciferase activity was normalized to Renilla luciferase activity.

Western blot

Proteins were separated by 10% SDS‐PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA). Membranes were blocked with Rapid Blocking Buffer (1×, YGYR‐BIOTECH, China) and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP‐conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at 37°C. Protein bands were visualized using ECL substrate (Millipore, USA). Primary antibodies used included: rabbit anti‐PCNA (1:1000), anti‐caspase‐3 (1:1000), anti‐caspase‐9 Core (1:1000), anti‐PTPRM (1:1000), anti‐p‐Smad3 (1:1000), anti‐p‐EGFR (1:1000), anti‐TGF‐βR II (1:1000) all from Abcam or CST; mouse anti‐β‐actin (1:1000); rabbit anti‐Nectin3 (1:1000), anti‐JAM‐A/CD321 (1:1000), anti‐CADM1 (1:1000), anti‐Selp/P‐Selectin/CD62P (1:1000), anti‐p‐Akt (1:1000) all from Abcam or Proteintech. Secondary antibodies were HRP‐conjugated goat anti‐rabbit IgG (1:5000) and goat anti‐mouse IgG (1:5000) from Proteintech.

Flow cytometry

HPAECs were subjected to hypoxia or negative control conditions for 24 h. 16 , 17 We then used an Annexin V‐FITC/PI apoptosis detection kit (Sigma‐Aldrich) to determine cell apoptosis. Cells were seeded in 6‐well plates (5 × 105/well). After the different treatments, the cells were collected and resuspended in 195 μL of binding buffer, 5 μL of Annexin V‐FITC and 10 μL propidium iodide (PI) working solution and then incubated at room temperature for 20 min in darkness. Cell apoptosis were detected by flow cytometry (BD FACS Calibur).

Animal experiments with AAV intervention

Eight‐week‐old male C57BL/6 mice were randomly divided into normoxic control and chronic hypobaric hypoxia groups (n = 12 per group). Hypoxic mice were housed in a hypobaric chamber (Fenglei, China) at a simulated altitude of 5500 m for 4 weeks, reducing oxygen partial pressure by half. For AAV intervention, 8‐week‐old male C57BL/6 mice were injected via the tail vein with 2.5 × 1011 v.g./mL of AAV‐Control (CGCTGAGTACTTCGAAATGTC) or AAV‐PTPRM shRNA (GCCGCTTGATCTGCTGCTTTC) once at the start of the experiment. Gene editing was successful in only about half of the mice and these mice were used for the different experimental groups (n = 5–8 per group). Chamber temperature and humidity were maintained at 28 ± 2°C and 60% ± 5%, respectively. After 4 weeks, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane for transthoracic echocardiography, then tracheotomized and mechanically ventilated. A Millar SPR‐839 pressure‐volume catheter (AD Instruments, CO, USA) was inserted into the right ventricle for hemodynamic measurements. Hearts and right lungs were snap‐frozen in liquid nitrogen; fresh heart and upper left lung tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for H&E, Masson, and immunofluorescence staining. All mice were purchased from the Animal Experiment Center of the Chinese PLA General Hospital (Beijing, China). Experimental procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the Chinese PLA General Hospital (approval no. 2020‐X16‐39).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 8.4.3 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Quantitative data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Comparisons between two groups were conducted using a two‐tailed Student's t‐test. A p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, NS represents not significant).

RESULTS

Foundation of the hypoxic pulmonary hypertension model

To systematically identify and characterize lncRNAs and mRNAs, and to construct co‐expression and ceRNA networks involved in HPH progression, male rats were exposed to a hypobaric hypoxia chamber simulating 5500 m (≈380 mmHg) for 28 days. Elevated hemoglobin concentration was an early physiological response to hypoxia. Red blood cell count, hemoglobin level, and hematocrit increased in a time‐dependent manner in hypoxic rats (Figure 1A–C). Pulmonary arterial pressure, right ventricular systolic pressure (RVSP), and the RV/(LV + S) ratio were all significantly higher in the hypoxia group than in controls (Figure 1D–F).

The body weight of rats under the modeling are shown in Figure 1G. Hemodynamic assessment demonstrated that mPAP, RVSP were all significantly elevated in hypoxic rats (Figure 1H). Ultrasonic assessment demonstrated that RVFW, dp/dt max, and dp/dt min were all significantly elevated in hypoxic rats (Figure 1I). Immunohistochemical staining revealed severe pulmonary vascular remodeling, with a significantly increased vessel wall‐to‐diameter ratio in hypoxic rats (Figure 1J). H&E staining showed right ventricular hypertrophy, with markedly increased right ventricular wall thickness (Figure 1K), confirming successful establishment of the chronic HPH rat model.

Functional and pathway analysis of DEmRNAs and lncRNAs in the pulmonary artery of rats

Rats were randomly selected from the hypoxic and normoxic control groups for transcriptomic analysis. mRNA, lncRNA, and miRNA expression profiles were annotated using the Ensembl database (release 100). A workflow is shown in Figure S1A. In total, 343 DEmRNAs were identified (190 upregulated, 153 downregulated) (Figure S1B), with distribution displayed in a volcano plot (Figure S1C). KEGG analysis showed that DEmRNAs were mainly enriched in hypoxia‐related cardiac pathways, including MAPK, calcium, cGMP‐PKG, and PPAR signaling (Figure S1D). Using the STRING database, a PPI network was constructed for key pathway genes, including 11 upregulated and 13 downregulated mRNAs (Figure S1E). These were associated with WNT (Rac3, Rspo1, Sfrp2), AMPK (Foxo1, Scd, Ppp2r5d, Acaca), and PI3K‐Akt (Chad, Ibsp, Thbs4, Egfr) pathways. Hub genes including Egfr, Sgk1, Nr4a1, Foxo1, Rac3, and PTPRM were validated by qRT‐PCR. Consistent with RNA‐seq, Egfr, Foxo1, and Rac3 were upregulated, while Nr4a1, Sgk1, and PTPRM were downregulated in hypoxic rats (Figure S1F).

The coding potential of novel transcripts was evaluated using CNCI and CPC software. A total of 7209 candidate lncRNAs were identified, including 4756 intergenic (66.0%), 431 antisense (6.0%), 190 intronic (2.6%), 1056 sense (14.6%), 219 bidirectional (3.0%), and other lncRNAs (7.7%) (Figure S2A). With FDR < 0.05 and |log2FC| > 1, 172 DElncRNAs were identified (70 upregulated, 102 downregulated) (Figure S2B). Their distribution is shown in a volcano plot (Figure S2C), and expression patterns are displayed in a heatmap (Figure S2D).

Construction of lncRNA‐miRNA‐mRNA network

Functional roles of DElncRNAs were predicted by functional annotation of their co‐expressed mRNAs. A co‐expression network was constructed using five significantly upregulated and five downregulated mRNAs. The network showed that upregulated lncRNA 76272 was positively correlated with Foxo1 and Ccnd2 (cell proliferation‐related). Upregulated lncRNAs 3494 and MSTRG.59803.4 were positively correlated with Rac3 and Ibsp (cell proliferation and migration). Downregulated lncRNAs 92467 and MSTRG.19351.1 were positively correlated with Rspo1 and Scd (cell proliferation and differentiation) (Figure 2A). Four DElncRNAs and four DEmRNAs were randomly validated by qRT‐PCR. Expression trends were consistent with RNA‐seq (Figure 2B,C).

A ceRNA network was further constructed by integrating expression profiles and predicted interactions among lncRNAs, miRNAs, and mRNAs, showing complex one‐to‐many and many‐to‐one regulatory relationships. Hub lncRNAs 92424 and 88836 were identified as ceRNAs of miR‐6849‐y, which targets Foxred2, Usp54, Lilrb2, Megf8, and Fam168a (Figure 2D). Hub lncRNA 92467 was predicted as a ceRNA of miR‐205‐z, rno‐miR‐205, and miR‐317‐z, which target PTPRM, Akip1, and Prom1 (Figure 2E).

Three axes were selected for validation: lnc92467/miR‐205‐z/PTPRM, lnc92424/miR‐6849‐y/Usp54, and lnc88836/miR‐6849‐y/Foxred2. In HPH tissues, expression of lnc92467, PTPRM, and miR‐6849‐y was decreased, while lnc92424, lnc88836, Foxred2, Usp54, and miR‐205‐z were increased (Figure 2F), consistent with sequencing data. PTPRM was positively correlated with lnc92467 and enriched in CAM signaling and adherens junction pathways. These results indicated that lncRNA‐92467 may be critical in HPH pathogenesis. Thus, the lncRNA‐92467/miR‐205‐5p/PTPRM axis was selected for further functional study.

LncRNA‐92467 knockdown prevents reduced proliferation and increased apoptosis induced by hypoxia

We next examined lncRNA‐92467 expression in HPAECs. RT‐qPCR confirmed that lncRNA‐92467 was significantly downregulated under hypoxia versus normoxia (Figure 3A). As lncRNA function depends on subcellular localization, nuclear and cytoplasmic fractions were isolated, revealing that lncRNA‐92467 was predominantly cytoplasmic in HPAECs (Figure 3B). We then knocked down lncRNA‐92467 using three siRNAs (siRNA1, siRNA2, siRNA3) and negative control (NC) siRNA. siRNA‐3 achieved efficient, dose‐dependent knockdown and was used for subsequent experiments (Figure S3A). Knockdown of lncRNA‐92467 under hypoxia further upregulated miR‐205‐z (Figure 3C) and downregulated PTPRM (Figure 3D). Hypoxia promoted HPAEC proliferation, and lncRNA‐92467 silencing amplified this effect (Figure 3E,F). Hypoxia reduced caspase‐3 and caspase‐9 expression, indicating suppressed apoptosis; lncRNA‐92467 knockdown further inhibited apoptosis under hypoxia (Figure 3G–I). Moreover, siRNA‐92467 enhanced hypoxia‐induced cell migration (Figure 3J,K).

LncRNA‐92467 functions through its association with miR‐205‐z

To investigate the regulatory interplay between lncRNA‐92467 and miR‐205‐5p in hypoxia‐induced HPAEC proliferation, apoptosis, and migration, we first performed bioinformatic prediction. RNAhybrid analysis identified a putative binding site between lncRNA‐92467 (chromosome 1) and miR‐205‐5p, with a predicted minimum free energy (MFE) of −18.37 kcal/mol, suggesting stable interaction (Figure 4A).

Wild‐type (WT) and mutant (MUT) sequences of the predicted miR‐205‐z binding site were cloned into the pGL3 luciferase reporter vector. Luciferase assays showed that miR‐205‐z mimics suppressed, while inhibitors enhanced, luciferase activity in WT‐transfected cells; MUT abrogated this response (Figure 4B). Efficient overexpression or knockdown of miR‐205‐z following mimic or inhibitor transfection was confirmed by RT‐qPCR (Figure S3B,C). Notably, silencing lncRNA‐92467 increased endogenous miR‐205‐z levels, reducing PTPRM expression and promoting cell proliferation (elevated PCNA) while suppressing apoptosis (reduced caspase‐3/9). Conversely, miR‐205‐z inhibitor reversed these effects of lncRNA‐92467 knockdown, whereas mimics had no additional effect (Figure 4C,D). Scratch wound healing and flow cytometry revealed that hypoxia enhanced HPAEC migration and suppressed apoptosis. Upregulating miR‐205‐5p (via lncRNA‐92467 knockdown or mimics) potentiated these hypoxia‐induced changes, whereas miR‐205‐5p inhibition attenuated migration and restored apoptosis under hypoxic conditions (Figure 4E,F).

MiR‐205‐z regulates HPAEC proliferation/migration through its association with PTPRM

To investigate the regulatory interplay among lncRNA‐92467, miR‐205‐5p and PTPRM in hypoxia‐induced HPAEC proliferation, apoptosis and migration, we examined PTPRM expression. Knockdown of lncRNA‐92467 significantly reduced PTPRM levels (Figure 5A). Co‐transfection of si‐lncRNA‐92467 with miR‐205‐5p inhibitor restored PTPRM expression, whereas miR‐205‐5p mimics did not further enhance suppression. MiR‐205‐5p mimics alone also downregulated PTPRM (Figure 5B). These results indicate that lncRNA‐92467 positively regulates PTPRM by sponging miR‐205‐5p. Overexpression and knockdown of PTPRM were confirmed by Western blot (Figure S3D,E). miR‐205‐5p mimics increased PCNA and decreased caspase‐3/9 expression, whereas PTPRM overexpression exerted the opposite effects. Rescue assays showed that PTPRM overexpression reversed miR‐205‐5p mimic‐induced proliferation and apoptosis inhibition, while PTPRM knockdown showed no additional effects (Figure 5C). Scratch assays revealed that hypoxia‐induced migration was further enhanced by miR‐205‐5p mimics (Figure 5D). PTPRM overexpression reversed this effect and reduced migration alone. Flow cytometry showed that miR‐205‐5p mimics further suppressed hypoxia‐reduced apoptosis, whereas PTPRM overexpression restored apoptosis (Figure 5E). Western blot demonstrated that miR‐205‐5p mimics increased Selp, JAM‐A, ItgaM, Nectin3, and CADM1, and phosphorylated Akt, SMAD3, TGF‐β and EGFR, while PTPRM overexpression decreased these proteins (Figure 5F). PTPRM overexpression reversed the effects of miR‐205‐5p mimics, whereas si‐PTPRM showed no additional effects. Notably, distinct expression patterns of lncRNA‐92467 and PTPRM were observed in hypoxic human pulmonary artery smooth muscle cells (H‐PASMCs), which were opposite to those identified in HPAECs (Figure S4), suggesting cell‐type‐specific regulatory characteristics of this molecular axis in pulmonary vascular cells during hypoxic pulmonary hypertension. Together, these results indicate that lncRNA‐92467 acts as a ceRNA for miR‐205‐5p to upregulate PTPRM, thereby regulating HPAEC proliferation, apoptosis and migration via the p‐CAM/p‐catenin/p‐Akt pathway during HPH progression.

PTPRM improves right heart function damaged by HPH via the p‐CAM/ p‐catenin / p‐Akt pathway in vivo

To further confirm the cardioprotective role of PTPRM in HPH, cardiac function was evaluated by echocardiography and hemodynamic analysis. After 4 weeks, HPH induced significant right ventricular (RV) dysfunction, characterized by reduced pulmonary artery acceleration time (PAT) and elevated RV systolic pressure (RVSP), RV free wall (RVFW) thickness, and left ventricular free wall (LVFW) thickness. PTPRM overexpression (OE) restored PAT and reduced RVSP, RVFW and LVFW (Figure 6A,B). HE and cTNT staining showed that the PTPRM‐OE group exhibited milder pulmonary vascular remodeling, less fibrosis and thinner RV walls compared with hypoxic wild‐type mice (Figure 6C). Hypoxia upregulated Selp, JAM‐A, ItgaM, Nectin3, CADM1, p‐Akt, p‐SMAD3, p‐TGF‐β and p‐EGFR, whereas PTPRM‐OE significantly downregulated these proteins (Figure 6D). In addition, PTPRM overexpression decreased PCNA expression and increased caspase‐3/9 levels in vivo (Figure 6E). These results indicate that PTPRM represents a promising therapeutic target for HPH by regulating CAMs and p‐Akt signaling, thereby inhibiting abnormal HPAEC proliferation and promoting apoptosis.

DISCUSSION

Chronic exposure to low barometric pressure hypoxia at high altitudes triggers pulmonary vascular remodeling, leading to the development of high‐altitude pulmonary hypertension. This condition is associated with high rates of disability and mortality, largely due to challenges in early diagnosis and limited treatment options. Therefore, a deeper understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying HPH is urgently needed to improve diagnostic and therapeutic strategies. In the present study, we observed that lncRNA‐92467 and PTPRM were significantly downregulated, while miR‐205‐5p was upregulated, in hypoxic pulmonary arteries. Functional experiments demonstrated that knockdown of lncRNA‐92467 promoted HPAEC proliferation and migration, effects that were abrogated by inhibition of miR‐205‐5p. Moreover, miR‐205‐5p enhanced cell proliferation and migration while suppressing apoptosis, all of which were reversed upon overexpression of PTPRM. In vivo, exogenous PTPRM overexpression attenuated HPH progression in mice by targeting CAMs, thereby inhibiting aberrant cell proliferation and migration and promoting apoptosis. Collectively, these findings identify a critical role for the lncRNA‐92467/miR‐205‐5p/PTPRM/CAM regulatory axis in HPH pathogenesis and suggest PTPRM as a potential therapeutic target for this disease.

Increasing evidence suggests that lncRNAs have shown potential as diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers because of the relationship between lncRNA expression and their functions. Several lncRNA‐focused signatures have been reported to be involved in pulmonary hypertension. lncRNA VELRP was reported to modulate pulmonary arterial smooth muscle cell proliferation and promotes vascular remodeling in pulmonary hypertension. 18 Bonnet et al. 19 commented on early evidence for the role of lncRNA TUG1 in vascular remodeling in pulmonary hypertension. Yang et al. 20 reported that lncRNA TUG1 is involved in the pulmonary vascular remodeling in mice with hypoxic pulmonary hypertension via the microRNA‐374c‐mediated Foxc1. Jiang et al. 21 recognized the clinical value of lncRNA SOX2‐OT in pulmonary arterial hypertension and its role in pulmonary artery smooth muscle cell proliferation, migration, and apoptosis. In this study, we identified lncRNA‐92467 as a novel protective lncRNA in HPH. Knockdown of lncRNA‐92467 promoted HPAEC proliferation and migration. Mechanistically, lncRNA‐92467 directly bound to miR‐205‐5p. Although miR‐205‐5p is known to regulate proliferation, migration and apoptosis in various cancers, 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 its role in HPH has rarely been reported. Our results showed that miR‐205‐5p promoted endothelial cell proliferation and migration while inhibiting apoptosis, indicating that miR‐205‐5p may contribute to HPH progression. Rescue experiments further verified that the effects of lncRNA‐92467 knockdown were reversed by miR‐205‐5p inhibitor, confirming the ceRNA regulatory network of lncRNA‐92467/miR‐205‐5p in HPH.

Subsequently, we identified PTPRM as a direct target gene of miR‐205‐5p. PTPRM is a member of the protein tyrosine phosphatase receptor family and is involved in cell adhesion, migration, and signal transduction. 27 Previous studies have shown that PTPRM is downregulated in various cancers and inhibits tumor cell proliferation and migration. 28 , 29 The knockdown of PTPRM in PRR11‐deficient GC‐1 cells led to a reversal of GC‐1 cell proliferation and migration. 30 Further, PTPRM downregulation has been shown to promote keratinocyte proliferation through excessive ERK1/2 signaling, and PTPRM knockdown increased proliferation, adhesion, invasion, and migration of breast cancer cells. 31 However, the role of PTPRM in HPH has not been reported. In this study, we found that overexpression of PTPRM reversed the effects of miR‐205‐5p on endothelial cell proliferation, migration, and apoptosis. In vivo experiments further demonstrated that overexpression of PTPRM significantly improved hemodynamic parameters and attenuated HPH progression in mice. These results indicate that PTPRM is a protective gene in HPH and is negatively regulated by miR‐205‐5p.

To further explore the downstream mechanism of PTPRM, we performed target gene prediction and verification and found that PTPRM directly targeted CAMs. CAMs are a class of transmembrane proteins that mediate cell–cell and cell–matrix adhesion and play an important role in maintaining vascular homeostasis. 32 Abnormal expression of CAMs is closely related to vascular remodeling in HPH. 33 Our results showed that PTPRM upregulated the expression of CAMs and increased catenin production. Catenin is a key molecule that connects CAMs to the cytoskeleton and regulates cell proliferation, migration, and apoptosis. 34 Increased catenin production inhibited abnormal endothelial cell proliferation and migration and promoted apoptosis, thereby alleviating vascular remodeling in HPH. These results suggest that the PTPRM/CAMs/catenin pathway is a critical downstream mechanism for the protective effect of lncRNA‐92467 in HPH.

This study has several limitations. First, all in vitro mechanistic explorations were conducted only in endothelial cells, and complementary validation in smooth muscle cells was not included. Given the critical contribution of smooth muscle cell proliferation and phenotypic transition to pulmonary vascular remodeling, additional cellular experiments covering multiple vascular cell types will help complement and consolidate the conclusions of this work in future research. Second, the clinical significance of lncRNA‐92467/miR‐205‐5p/PTPRM axis was not verified in clinical samples of HPH patients. Third, the specific regulatory mechanism between PTPRM and CAMs needs to be further explored at the protein level.

In summary, our findings demonstrate that lncRNA‐92467 regulates miR‐205‐5p‐mediated cell migration and apoptosis by targeting PTPRM. Specifically, miR‐205‐5p promotes cell migration through downregulation of PTPRM and subsequent suppression of CAM‐associated cell adhesion complexes. The miR‐205‐5p/PTPRM/CAM regulatory axis may therefore represent a promising therapeutic target for the treatment of HPH, offering a novel strategy for intervention in this disease.
