The fine structure of the mean magnetic field in M31 2025 The fine structure of the mean magnetic field in M31 – A.3
Indrajit Paul, R. Vasanth Kashyap, Tuhin Ghosh, Rainer Beck, Luke Chamandy, Srijita Sinha, Anvar Shukurov

Abstract
To explore the spatial variations of the regular (mean) magnetic field of the Andromeda galaxy (M31), we use Fourier analysis in azimuthal angle along four rings in the galaxy’s plane. Earlier analyses indicated that the axisymmetric magnetic field (azimuthal Fourier mode m = 0 m=0 ) is sufficient to fit the observed polarization angles in a wide range of galactocentric distances. We apply a Bayesian inference approach to new, more sensitive radio continuum data at λ ​ λ ​ 3.59 \lambda\lambda 3.59 , 6.18 6.18 , and 11.33 ​ cm 11.33\thinspace\text{cm} and the earlier data at λ ​ 20.46 ​ cm \lambda 20.46\thinspace\text{cm} to reveal sub-dominant contributions from the modes m = 1 m=1 , 2, and 3 along with a dominant axisymmetric mode. Magnetic lines of the axisymmetric mode are close to trailing logarithmic spirals which are significantly more open than the spiral arms detectable in the interstellar dust and neutral hydrogen. The form of the m = 0 m=0 mode is consistent with galactic dynamo theory. Both the amplitudes and the pitch angles of the higher azimuthal modes ( m > 1 m>1 ) vary irregularly with r r reflecting local variations in the magnetic field structure. The maximum strength of the mean magnetic field of 1.8 ​ – ​ 2.7 ​ μ ​ G 1.8\text{--}2.7\thinspace\upmu\text{G} (for the axisymmetric part of the field) occurs at 10 ​ – ​ 14 ​ kpc 10\text{--}14\thinspace\text{kpc} but we find that its strength varies strongly along the azimuth; this variation gives rise to the m = 1 m=1 mode. We suggest a procedure of Bayesian inference which is independent of the specific nature of the depolarization and applies when the magneto-ionic layer observable in polarized emission is not symmetric along the line of sight because emission from its far side is completely depolarized.

Introduction

Magnetic fields play a crucial and multifaceted role in astrophysics, impacting various processes and phenomena in galaxies. In particular, they affect the propagation of cosmic rays
(
Zweibel 2013
)
, the formation and evolution of galaxies
(
Boulanger et al. 2018
;
Tabatabaei 2018
)
, the star formation rate, and the initial mass function
(
Krumholz & Federrath 2019
)
. A reliable, detailed knowledge of the structure of a large-scale galactic magnetic field is essential for the mean-field dynamo theory (for reviews, see
Beck et al. 2019
and
Shukurov & Subramanian 2021
).

Magnetic fields can exist on small turbulent scales or as large-scale coherent structures, depending on the physical processes involved. Polarized synchrotron emission enables the study of the ordered component of the magnetic field projected onto the sky plane using radio observations. Galactic magnetic fields appear more ordered in radio observations than in the far-infrared
(
Borlaff et al. 2023
)
, suggesting that different parts of the interstellar medium and different scales are probed at different wavelength ranges.

Magnetic fields ordered on a kiloparsec scale in spiral galaxies have two contributions: regular (mean) fields, which vary little in either direction or strength, and anisotropic random fields, which exhibit frequent field reversals but a similar anisotropic orientation
(
Beck 2015
)
. Both types of the magnetic field contribute to the polarized radio emission. The Faraday rotation of the polarization angle, combined with the density of thermal electrons, provides a method to determine both the strength and orientation of the line-of-sight component of the regular magnetic field and distinguish between the regular and anisotropic random interstellar magnetic fields.

Although random fields do not contribute to the mean Faraday rotation, they contribute to its dispersion. Randomly oriented small-scale turbulent fields decrease the degree of polarization, especially at longer wavelengths, via a range of mechanisms collectively known as Faraday depolarization
(
Burn 1966
;
Sokoloff et al. 1998
)
.
Various configurations of synchrotron-emitting regions (e.g., electron density and magnetic field strength distributions) can also affect the depolarization. By observing polarized synchrotron emission over a sufficiently wide range of wavelengths, it is possible to investigate both the depolarization effects and the Faraday rotation, providing insights into the magnetic field structure and the intervening medium. If the anisotropic random field is negligible compared to the regular field, it becomes possible to infer the three-dimensional (3D) global magnetic field structure of the galaxy by combining polarized synchrotron and Faraday rotation data using multi-frequency radio observations.

Depolarization mechanisms in the radio range can be categorized as either wavelength-independent or wavelength-dependent
(
Burn 1966
;
Sokoloff et al. 1998
)
. Wavelength-independent depolarization is typically caused by the tangling of the magnetic field within the telescope beam. In contrast, wavelength-dependent depolarization arises from factors such as rotation measure (RM) gradients, differential Faraday rotation, and both internal and external Faraday dispersion. The wavelength-independent change in the polarization between the wavelengths
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
(
\text{DP}_{{\lambda_{1}}/{\lambda_{2}}}
) is quantified in terms of the ratio of polarization intensities at those wavelengths. When magnetic field fluctuations occur within a region with both synchrotron emission and Faraday rotation, the dominant cause of depolarization is usually the internal Faraday dispersion
(
Williams et al. 2024
)
.

The large-scale magnetic field structures in nearby galaxies have been extensively studied using radio polarimetric observations in the wavelength range
3
to
20\thinspace\text{cm}
(see table 4 of
Beck et al. 2019
). The intrinsic polarization angle (
\psi_{0}
) is measured in the sky plane with respect to the local outward radial direction in the galactic mid-plane. A model of the regular magnetic field can capture the azimuthal variation of
\psi_{0}
with a set of Fourier modes (

(

) modes across four rings at galactocentric distances 2–7 kpc
(

. In the case of M33,

modelled the regular field in two radial ranges (1–5 kpc) and found a statistically good fit for multiple Fourier mode combinations mostly dominated by the

mode in the disc
(

)
. For the barred galaxy NGC 1097, the measured polarization angles in the inner two rings (1.25–3.75 kpc) were divided into two halves on either side of the bar axis and fitted separately to obtain the best-fit combinations of Fourier modes. The polarization angles in the first ring (1.25–2.5 kpc) and half of the second ring (2.5–3.75 kpc)
are fitted well with

Key features of the magnetic field structure in M31 include a well-ordered large-scale magnetic field which is roughly aligned with the spiral arms of the galaxy
(

)

(
Gräve et al. 1981

5\thinspace\upmu\text{G}

(

;

(
Gießübel & Beck 2014

.

In this work, we apply the mode analysis technique of
Fletcher et al. 2004

Beck et al. 2020

20.46

We sample the joint posterior distribution of the Fourier mode parameters to study the correlation between the parameters of interest and reliably estimate the uncertainties. The total number of parameters in the models used here is
3\thinspace n_{m}+2
where
n_{m}
is the number of fitted azimuthal Fourier modes of the regular magnetic field, and the other two parameters allow for the wavelength-dependent reduction of the effective path length in polarized emission associated with Faraday depolarization at longer wavelengths.

2.1
Data averaging in rings and sectors

The turbulent magnetic field strongly dominates at small scales
(
Beck et al. 2019
)
. However, the isotropic turbulent field is not observable in the polarized emission. Since we are interested in the large-scale structure of the magnetic field, we average the data over a sufficiently large area to eliminate the contribution of the random magnetic fields to the Faraday rotation. To do so, we split the galaxy into rings (annuli) of a fixed radial width of
2\thinspace\text{kpc}
in the galaxy plane and smooth the data over 18 sectors of equal angular size along the azimuthal coordinate
\theta
, after transforming to the cylindrical coordinate system
(r,\theta,z)
, where
z
is the distance from the galactic mid-plane.
Sectors of
20^{\circ}
in width are wide enough to derive the standard deviation of polarization angle in each sector (with at least five data points per sector). There are typically five data points per beam diameter while each sector contains 2.4–4.5 beam areas at
8\text{--}14\thinspace\text{kpc}
for the
3′
beam width (1.1–2.1 for the
4.4′
beam). Thus, individual angle measurements used is averaging over a sector are not completely statistically independent but this does not appear to be a significant problem. Nevertheless, we avoid using narrower sectors (see, however, Appendix
A.1
). Using wider sectors would reduce the number of usable polarization angle measurements and cause stronger contamination by systematic gradients.
The signal-to-noise ratio of polarized emission in the inner 1–
6\thinspace\text{kpc}
is low, hence this region is excluded from our analysis. The resulting cylindrical grid is shown in Fig.
1
.

From the polarimetric observations, we have the maps of the Stokes parameters
I
,
Q
, and
U
at each wavelength. To compare them with the model, we convert them into the polarization angle
\psi
and polarized intensity
PI
, using

\psi=\frac{1}{2}\arctan\frac{\langle U\rangle}{\langle Q\rangle}\thinspace,

(1)

\text{PI}=\sqrt{\langle Q\rangle^{2}+\langle U\rangle^{2}-(1.2\sigma_{Q,U})^{2}}\thinspace,

(2)

where angular brackets denote the average value over all the pixels within the sector and
\sigma_{Q,U}
is the rms noise in the measurements of
Q
or
U
, respectively. The term
(1.2\sigma_{Q,U})^{2}
represents the correction of positive bias due to noise
(
Wardle & Kronberg 1974
)
. The error in the polarization angle is computed as the standard deviation of the polarization angles within one sector, including only pixels whose signal-to-noise ratio of
Q
and
U
is greater than 3, whereas the error in the polarized intensity is estimated as the standard deviation between all pixels in the sector. The average polarization angle
\psi
within a sector is measured in the sky plane from the local outward radial direction. An appropriate coordinate transformation is performed to translate between the sky-plane and galactic-plane coordinates using the galaxy inclination angle of
i=75^{\circ}
and the position angle of the major axis
37^{\circ}
east of north
(
Chemin et al. 2009
)
.

For the depolarization analysis in
Section

5.1
, we use the derived non-thermal (synchrotron) intensity maps at three wavelengths from
Beck et al. 2020
. The original map at
\lambda 3.59\thinspace\text{cm}
was corrected for the contribution of anomalous microwave emission by multiplying the total non-thermal intensity by a factor of 0.831
(see
Beck & Berkhuijsen 2025
, for details)
.

3
Model of the magnetic structure

The polarization angle of the synchrotron emission as a function of the azimuthal angle
\theta
and wavelength
\lambda
in a ring sufficiently narrow to neglect the radial variation of the parameters can be written as

\psi(\theta)=\psi_{0}(\theta)+\lambda^{2}\text{RM}(\theta)+\lambda^{2}\text{RM}_{\rm fg}\ ,

(3)

where
\psi_{0}
is the intrinsic polarization angle,
RM
is the intrinsic Faraday rotation measure in the galaxy, and
\text{RM}_{\rm fg}
is the foreground Faraday rotation measure produced in the medium between the galaxy and the observer.

\theta
is measured counter-clockwise in the galaxy plane from the northern major axis of the galaxy.
The quantities
\psi_{0}
and
RM
depend on the regular magnetic field
\boldsymbol{B}
, inclination angle
i
of the galaxy, the number density of the thermal electrons
n_{\text{e}}
, and the line-of-sight path length
L
through the thermal disc in a given ring.
\text{RM}_{\rm fg}
is assumed to be independent of
\theta
in a given ring.

The anisotropy of the random magnetic field, which also contributes to the polarized synchrotron emission, might affect
\psi_{0}
but there are reasonable arguments for why we can neglect this.
Beck & Berkhuijsen 2025
find that the anisotropic random magnetic field in M31 is stronger than the mean (regular) magnetic field.
However, the pitch angle of the total magnetic field (which includes both the mean and random components),
-14^{\circ}\pm 2^{\circ}

(
Beck et al. 2020
)
, does not differ much from the pitch angle
-(15^{\circ}\text{--}16^{\circ})
of the predominant axisymmetric mean magnetic field at
6<r<12\thinspace\text{kpc}
(
Table

4
below). Therefore, the change in
\psi_{0}
due to the contribution of the random magnetic field cannot be very strong in this radial range. The magnetic pitch angle at
12<r<14\thinspace\text{kpc}
is slightly larger in magnitude but the anisotropy of the random field produced by the differential rotation
(
Shukurov & Subramanian 2021
, section 4.4.1 of)
is expected to be weaker in the outer galaxy and cannot explain the difference. Since the orientation of the principal axes of the anisotropic field is not known with confidence from either theory or observations, the effect on
\psi_{0}
cannot be estimated with a high degree of confidence.

The formalism used here is introduced and discussed in detail by
Sokoloff et al. 1992
and
Berkhuijsen et al. 1997
, who present it for a magnetic field containing all three cylindrical components. Here, we introduce it briefly, assuming that the vertical magnetic field component
B_{z}
is negligible
(
Fletcher et al. 2004
)
. The remaining cylindrical components of
\boldsymbol{B}=(B_{r},B_{\theta})
in the disc plane are expanded into the Fourier modes in
\theta
,

\begin{split}B_{r}&=\sum\limits_{m=0}^{N}B_{m}\thinspace\sin{p_{m}}\thinspace\cos[m(\theta-\beta_{m})]\thinspace,\\
B_{\theta}&=\sum\limits_{m=0}^{N}B_{m}\thinspace\cos{p_{m}}\thinspace\cos[m(\theta-\beta_{m})]\thinspace,\end{split}

(4)

where
B_{m}
is the amplitude of the Fourier mode of the wave number
m
,
p_{m}
is its pitch angle (the angle between the field direction and the tangent to the circumference),
\beta_{m}
is the azimuth where a non-axisymmetric mode is maximum, and
N
is the maximum number of modes required to fit the given data following the Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem. For a horizontal magnetic field,
\psi_{0}
is related to the model parameters as
(
Berkhuijsen et al. 1997
, equation A3 of)

\psi_{0}=\arctan\left[\frac{\sin{(2\theta-p)}\sin^{2}{i}-(1+\cos^{2}{i})\sin{p}}{2\cos{p}\cos{i}}\right],

(5)

where
p
is the pitch angle of the total horizontal component of the regular magnetic field defined as
p=\arctan(B_{r}/B_{\theta})
with
-90^{\circ}<p\leq 90^{\circ}
and obtained using equation (
4
).
A trailing spiral has a negative pitch angle.

In the analysis of Faraday rotation, the amplitudes of the Fourier modes
B_{m}
are convenient to characterise in terms of the related quantities

R_{m}=0.81\left(\frac{B_{m}}{1\thinspace\upmu\text{G}}\right)\left(\frac{\langle n_{\mathrm{e}}\rangle}{1\thinspace\text{cm}^{-3}}\right)\left(\frac{L}{1\thinspace\text{pc}}\right)\frac{\text{rad}}{\text{m}^{2}}\thinspace.

(6)

The parameters
B_{m}
and
h
can be understood as the equivalent amplitude of the magnetic mode and half-thickness of the magneto-ionic layer or as the maximum amplitude and scale height of an exponential layer.
L
is the path length through the magneto-ionic layer,
L=2h/\cos i
.
Multiplying equation (
4
) by
\langle n_{\mathrm{e}}\thinspace\rangle L
, we obtain the corresponding quantities
R_{r}
and
R_{\theta}
which are the used to derive, via the projection on the line of sight, the intrinsic Faraday rotation measure
(
Berkhuijsen et al. 1997
, equation A2 with
B_{z}=0
of)

R_{\parallel}=-(R_{r}\sin\theta+R_{\theta}\cos\theta)\sin i\thinspace.

(7)

Because of Faraday depolarization by magneto-ionic fluctuations, polarized radio emission at different wavelengths originates at different depths within the synchrotron emitting layer. For shorter wavelengths, Faraday depolarization is weak, making it transparent to polarized emission
(
Fletcher et al. 2004
;
Beck et al. 2020
)
. M31 does not have a detectable radio halo
(
Gräve et al. 1981
)
, so
RM
depends on the magnetic field in the disc. To allow for the fact that only a fraction of the synchrotron disc thickness is visible in polarized emission at a wavelength
\lambda
, we introduce the factor
\xi_{\lambda}

(
Berkhuijsen et al. 1997
)
via

\text{RM}=\frac{1}{2}\thinspace\xi_{\lambda}\thinspace R_{\parallel}\thinspace,

(8)

where
R_{\parallel}
is the Faraday rotation measure accumulated through the whole disc thickness and the factor 1/2 gives the observable
RM
in a uniform slab or a magneto-ionic layer symmetric along the line of sight
(
Burn 1966
;
Sokoloff et al. 1998
)
.
This factor is different when the profile of magnetic field strength, thermal electron density, or density of cosmic-ray electrons in the magneto-ionic layer is not symmetric with respect to the galactic mid-plane
(
Sokoloff et al. 1998
, Sections 3.1–3.3 of)
. As we argue in Section
5.1
, the galaxy is not transparent to the polarized emission at
\lambda\lambda 11.33
and
20.46\thinspace\text{cm}
, so the symmetry is broken at these wavelengths. As a result, the relation between
RM
and
R_{\parallel}
becomes significantly more complicated and wavelength-dependent. Therefore, the wavelength-dependent factor
\xi_{\lambda}
allows not only for the reduced effective path length of the polarized emission but also for the effective asymmetry of the magneto-ionic layer. We assume that
\xi_{3}=\xi_{6}=1
at
\lambda\lambda 3.59
and
6.18\thinspace\text{cm}
and
\xi_{\lambda}<1
at the longer wavelengths (see Section
5.1
for justification).

We fit the values of
\text{RM}_{\text{fg}}
,
R_{m}
,
p_{m}
, and
\beta_{m}
,
0\leq m\leq N
, together with
\xi_{11}
and
\xi_{20}
to the observed polarization angles at all available wavelengths simultaneously. We adopt the value
L
from
Fletcher et al. 2004
and
\langle n_{\mathrm{e}}\rangle
from
Beck & Berkhuijsen 2025
, and use equation (
6
) to obtain the corresponding magnetic field amplitudes
B_{m}
. For an axisymmetric spiral (ASS) magnetic field (
N=0
), the model has three free parameters,
R_{0}
,
p_{0}
, and
\text{RM}_{\rm fg}
in addition to
\xi_{11}
and
\xi_{20}
. For a combination of axisymmetric and bisymmetric field (BSS) fields (
N=1
), we have three additional parameters,
R_{1}
,
p_{1}
, and
\beta_{1}
. Each non-axisymmetric mode has three parameters, so the total number of fitted parameters in a model containing
n_{m}
Fourier modes is
n=3n_{m}+2
, including
\xi_{11}
and
\xi_{20}
.

The number of azimuthal modes which can be included into the model depends on the width of the sectors within which the data are averaged. In order to maintain a sufficiently high signal-to-noise ratio with the data available, the optimal size of the sector is
20^{\circ}
, providing 18 data points in each radial range. To detect a periodic signal, the sampling rate must be twice the frequency of the signal according to the Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem
(
Nyquist 1928
;
Shannon 1949
)
. Therefore, we can detect no more that
n_{m}\leq 18/2=9
Fourier modes. Since we omit some data points (see
Section

5.2
), there are effectively less than 18 points where the magnetic field is sampled. Therefore, we limit our analysis to
n_{m}\leq 8
. The availability of the data at several wavelengths improves the reliability and accuracy of resulting magnetic field parameter estimates but this does not help to reveal higher azimuthal modes as long as the sector width is not reduced.

The model for the polarization angles is highly non-linear and its parameter space is multi-dimensional. Therefore, the technique of residual minimisation used, e.g., by
Sokoloff et al. 1992
,
Berkhuijsen et al. 1997
, and
Fletcher et al. 2004
is inefficient, depends on the initial parameter values used to initialize the minimisation, and does not allow for correlations between the parameters. We use the Bayesian inference technique for the fitting.

4
Bayesian inference

We introduce the notation
\alpha=(R_{m},p_{m},\beta_{m},\text{RM}_{\rm fg},\xi_{11},\xi_{20})
,
0\leq m\leq N
, for the model parameters introduced in
Section

3
and denote

\mathcal{M}
the model itself and
\mathcal{D}
the set of the polarization angle measurements to which it is fitted. The Bayes theorem expresses the posterior probability distribution
P(\alpha|\mathcal{D},\mathcal{M})
of the parameters given the data as

P(\alpha|\mathcal{D},\mathcal{M})=\frac{\mathcal{L}(D|\alpha,\mathcal{M})\thinspace\pi(\alpha|\mathcal{M})}{\mathcal{Z}(\mathcal{D}|\mathcal{M})}\ ,

(9)

where
\pi(\alpha|\mathcal{M})
is the prior probability distribution of the parameters,
\mathcal{L}(D|\alpha,\mathcal{M})
is the likelihood, and
\mathcal{Z}(\mathcal{D}|\mathcal{M})
is the evidence. The evidence is defined as

\mathcal{Z}(\mathcal{D}|\mathcal{M})=\int\mathcal{L}(D|\alpha,\mathcal{M})\thinspace\pi(\alpha|\mathcal{M})\thinspace\mathrm{d}\alpha\thinspace.

(10)

We assume a uniform prior for all the parameters within the allowed ranges:
R_{m}\geq 0
,
|p_{m}|\leq 90^{\circ}
, and
|\beta_{m}|\leq 180^{\circ}/m
.

We adopt a Gaussian likelihood and assume that the data are sampled from a multivariate Gaussian distribution with a covariance matrix
\mathbf{\Sigma}(\alpha)
. The data uncertainties are known from the observations.
When the path length through a turbulent magneto-ionic medium is much larger the the turbulent scale, the polarization angle of radio emission affected by the Faraday rotation depends on
RM
, the integral of the thermal electron density and magnetic field. Then the probability distributions of both the polarization angle and
RM
tend toward a Gaussian distribution (the central limit theorem)
(
Kronberg 1994
)
.
The sampled polarization angle data can be written as
\boldsymbol{\psi}^{\text{d}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}(\alpha),\mathbf{\Sigma}(\alpha))
where
\mathcal{N}
is the Gaussian distribution. For the likelihood of the model polarization angles
\boldsymbol{\psi}^{\text{m}}
given by equation (
3
) with
n=3n_{m}+2
parameters, we adopt

\mathcal{L}_{\rm G}(\mathcal{D}|\alpha,\mathcal{M})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}\sqrt{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}\thinspace\text{exp}\left(-\frac{\chi^{2}}{2}\right),

(11)

where

\chi^{2}=(\boldsymbol{\psi^{\text{d}}}-\boldsymbol{\psi^{\text{m}}})^{T}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\psi^{\text{d}}}-\boldsymbol{\psi^{\text{m}}})\thinspace.

(12)

This choice is motivated by the fact that the observed polarization angles
\psi^{\text{d}}
and, hence, the model residuals,
\boldsymbol{\psi^{\text{d}}}-\boldsymbol{\psi^{\text{m}}}
, have a Gaussian probability distributions. We also reasonably assume that the observed polarization angle uncertainties
\sigma^{\text{d}}_{\lambda}(\theta_{j})
in different sectors
\theta_{j}
are uncorrelated, so that the covariance matrix is a diagonal one. Then equation (
12
) reduces to

\chi^{2}={\sum_{\lambda}}{\sum_{j}}\left[\frac{\psi^{\text{d}}_{\lambda}(\theta_{j})-\psi^{\text{m}}_{\lambda}(\theta_{j})}{\sigma^{\text{d}}_{\lambda}(\theta_{j})}\right]^{2},

(13)

where the summations extend over the wavelengths and sectors within a given ring.

We sample the posterior distribution given by equation (
9
) to obtain the joint probability distribution of the model parameters. We use the publicly available
cobaya
package written in
python

(
Torrado & Lewis 2019
)
to sample the likelihood and derive the posterior distribution of the model parameters.
cobaya
uses the Metropolis–Hastings algorithm of the Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) method as described in
Lewis 2013
. The first 30 per cent of the chains are discarded (‘burn-in’). The algorithm stops when the Gelman–Rubin convergence parameter
\mathcal{R}
becomes sufficiently close to unity or the total number of MCMC samples specified (
1\times 10^{6}
) is attained, and we use
\mathcal{R}-1<0.01
. As discussed below, the M31 data are fitted with
n_{m}\leq 4
azimuthal modes; for them, the MCMC converges before reaching the maximum number of samples. In order to validate the fitting procedure in
Section

4.1
we consider models with larger
n_{m}
, and for some of them the chain stops because of reaching the maximum number of samples.

We use the Bayesian complexity parameter
\mathcal{C}

(
Spiegelhalter et al. 2002
)
to
determine the maximum number of the Fourier modes that the observed data can constrain
(
Kunz et al. 2006
, see equation (6) of)
. In the case of a Gaussian likelihood, the complexity parameter is given by

\mathcal{C}=\left<\chi^{2}(\alpha)\right>-\chi^{2}(\hat{\alpha})\ ,

(14)

where the angular brackets denote the mean of
\chi^{2}
taken over the posterior probability distribution and
\hat{\alpha}
is the vector of the best-fitting parameters for a given model
\mathcal{M}
which provide a maximum of the likelihood
\mathcal{L}_{G}
. We use the complexity parameter to decide whether or not a more complicated model is justified even if it provides a higher likelihood. Thus defined, the complexity parameter gives the effective number of degrees of freedom in the strongly non-linear models considered here.

When comparing two models,
\mathcal{M}_{A}
and
\mathcal{M}_{B}
, we use both the complexity parameter and the model likelihood. Let
n_{A}
and
n_{B}
be the numbers of free parameters of the models
\mathcal{M}_{A}
and
\mathcal{M}_{B}
, respectively, and suppose that
n_{B}<n_{A}
. The criteria for choosing the best model given the data are the following
(
Kunz et al. 2006
;
Trotta 2008
)
:
(i)
If
\log{\mathcal{L}_{\rm G}}(\mathcal{M}_{A})\gg\log{\mathcal{L}_{\rm G}}(\mathcal{M}_{B})
, model
\mathcal{M}_{A}
is favoured over model
\mathcal{M}_{B}
.
(ii)
If
\log{\mathcal{L}_{\rm G}}(\mathcal{M}_{A})\approx\log{\mathcal{L}_{\rm G}}(\mathcal{M}_{B})
and
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{M}_{A})>\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{M}_{B})
, the additional parameters of the model
\mathcal{M}_{A}
do not sufficiently improve the fit to the data, and we prefer model
\mathcal{M}_{B}
as it has fewer parameters.
(iii)
If
\log{\mathcal{L}_{\rm G}}(\mathcal{M}_{A})\approx\log{\mathcal{L}_{\rm G}}(\mathcal{M}_{B})
and
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{M}_{A})\approx\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{M}_{B})
, both models are equally valid, and we need additional information to discriminate between them.

5
Implementation

Faraday depolarization of the radio emission of M31 is significant at the longer wavelengths used in our analysis, especially at
\lambda 20.46\thinspace\text{cm}
. As a result, the polarization signal at this wavelength is accumulated at a fraction
\xi_{\lambda}
of the total path length through the disc of M31, significantly shorter than the path lengths at
\lambda 3.59,\lambda 6.18
, and
\lambda 11.33\thinspace\text{cm}
. Therefore, we first analyse the polarized intensity to verify that the depolarization at the shorter wavelengths is sufficiently weak to be negligible. In our analysis, the depolarization factors
\xi_{11}
and
\xi_{20}
are fitted together with the other model parameters of the mean magnetic field using the Bayesian inference approach described in
Section

4
.

5.1
Depolarization

Fletcher et al. 2004
attributed the depolarization pattern obtained from the observations available to them to a rotation measure gradient in a Faraday screen, as suggested by a periodic variation of the depolarization with
\theta
(see their figures 8 and 9). The more sensitive observations at
\lambda\lambda 3.59,6.18,
and
11.33\thinspace\text{cm}
used here do not support that interpretation. In particular, there are only weak (if any) signs of the periodicity, which makes Faraday depolarization by random fields (which do not vary with
\theta
as strongly as
RM
) more plausible. The depolarization is defined as the ratio of the degrees of polarization
p(\lambda)
at two wavelengths,

\text{DP}_{\lambda_{1}/\lambda_{2}}=\frac{p(\lambda_{1})}{p(\lambda_{2})}\thinspace.

(15)

When the depolarization is caused by the Faraday rotation of either mean or random magnetic field,
\text{DP}_{\lambda_{1}/\lambda_{2}}<1
when
\lambda_{1}>\lambda_{2}
, and
\text{DP}_{\lambda_{1}/\lambda_{2}}
is smaller when the ratio
\lambda_{1}/\lambda_{2}
is larger. The stronger is depolarization, the smaller is
DP
when
\lambda_{1}>\lambda_{2}
.

Fig.
4
shows the depolarization between
\lambda\lambda 20.46
and
3.59\thinspace\text{cm}
at 3
′
HPBW, between
\lambda\lambda 20.46
and
6.18\thinspace\text{cm}
at 3
′
HPBW and between
\lambda\lambda 11.33
and
6.18\thinspace\text{cm}
at 5
′
HPBW. If the depolarization was caused by the gradient of
RM
due to the mean magnetic field,
DP
would be larger near the major axis,
\theta=0^{\circ},180^{\circ}
, where
RM
changes rapidly with
\theta
because of the large inclination of M31 to the line of sight
(
Fletcher et al. 2004
)
. Some values of
DP
near
\theta=180^{\circ}
in Fig.
4
are relatively large but this does not happen near
\theta=0^{\circ}
.
Data points which deviate strongly from their typical values have larger uncertainties.

The median depolarization values (
M
) in the rings 6–8, 8–10, 10–12, and 12–
14\thinspace\text{kpc}
are
0.08
,
0.13
,
0.12
, and
0.15
for
\text{DP}_{20/3}
,
0.13
,
0.13
,
0.11
, and
0.18
for
\text{DP}_{20/6}
, and
0.27
,
0.40
,
0.42
, and
0.29
for
\text{DP}_{11/6}
, respectively. The difference between
\text{DP}_{20/6}
and
\text{DP}_{20/3}
is not large and given that the Faraday depolarization at
\lambda 3.59\thinspace\text{cm}
is negligible, we neglect it at
\lambda 6.18\thinspace\text{cm}
as well, thus adopting
\xi_{3}=\xi_{6}=1
. Indeed, the degrees of polarization at
\lambda\lambda 3.59
and
6.18\thinspace\text{cm}
are very similar
(
Beck & Berkhuijsen 2025
, section 2.5 and fig. 4 of)
and depolarization at these wavelengths is due to the wavelength-independent effects (the scatter in
\psi_{0}
due to the random magnetic field within the telescope beam) rather than Faraday rotation. Meanwhile,
Beck & Berkhuijsen 2025
show that the degrees of polarization at
\lambda\lambda 11.33
and
20.46\thinspace\text{cm}
are significantly lower, apparently because of the Faraday depolarization.

Since the depolarization does not vary much with
\theta
, we conclude that the magneto-ionic fluctuations (Faraday dispersion in the synchrotron-emitting region and screen) are the main sources of depolarization. The origin of weaker depolarization near the major axis visible in Fig.
4
remains to be understood.

The depolarization mechanism at
\lambda\lambda 11.33
and
20.46\thinspace\text{cm}
can also involve significant depolarization by the regular (mean) magnetic field and thus be not only stronger but also more complicated than at the shorter wavelengths. Therefore, we treat
\xi_{11}
and
\xi_{20}
as independent parameters to be fitted.

5.2
Data screening

Although the magnetic field model includes high-order modes and, thus, can tolerate rapid changes of
\psi
with
\theta
, a few polarization angle measurements (15 out of 288, specified in
Table

3
) cannot be fitted. These data points only occur at the three shorter wavelengths and are mostly located near the major axis,
\theta=0^{\circ},180^{\circ},360^{\circ}
, where the polarization angle changes rapidly with
\theta
. Because of the high inclination of M31 to the line of sight, the partial overlap of telescope beams affects the observational results more strongly near the major axis, leading to an underestimated uncertainty of
\psi^{\text{d}}
. This does not happen at
\lambda 20.46\thinspace\text{cm}
where the resolution is significantly higher than at the shorter wavelengths (see
Table

1
). In the ring 6–8
kpc
, the data at
\theta=280^{\circ}\text{--}40^{\circ}
at
\lambda 3.59\thinspace\text{cm}
are unreliable due to technical issues in the subtraction of the foreground radio emission. An exception is the sector at
\theta=100^{\circ}
,
r=6\text{--}8\thinspace\text{kpc}
located near the minor axis. This sector exhibits a significantly higher polarization than its neighbours (Fig.
1
). This suggests that this data point is affected by a strong local fluctuation.

The data from the sectors shown in
Table

3
are treated as outliers and excluded from any further modelling of the polarization angles.

6.1
Magnetic field structure and strength

The axisymmetric magnetic field (
m=0
) dominates at all galactocentric distances, its amplitude
R_{0}
is more than twice as large as that of the higher-order modes. Fig.
9
shows the variation of the mode amplitudes
|R_{m}|
and their pitch angles
p_{m}
with the galactocentric radius.
Also shown is the azimuthally averaged strength of the total regular magnetic field
(B_{r}^{2}+B_{\theta}^{2})^{1/2}
(in terms of
R_{m}
rather than
B_{m}
) and its pitch angle: both are close to the corresponding parameters of the
m=0
mode.
Together with the total regular magnetic field, its axisymmetric part has a maximum at
r=10\text{--}12\thinspace\text{kpc}
in the bright radio ring, but the amplitudes of the higher-order modes are significantly smaller and vary little with
r
. The pitch angles of both the total regular field and its axisymmetric part remain practically constant as
r
varies, so the magnetic lines are trailing logarithmic spirals. The pitch angles of the higher modes vary in a rather disordered manner and can be both positive and negative. This suggests that the axisymmetric part of the magnetic field is produced by a systematic physical process (presumably, the mean-field dynamo action) but the higher-order modes emerge from a distortion of the axisymmetric magnetic field by local perturbations in the magneto-ionic medium such as the hole at
\theta\simeq 149^{\circ}
,
r\simeq 10\thinspace\text{kpc}
resulting from the passage of M32 through the disc of M31
(
Gordon et al. 2006
)
. The bisymmetric mode
m=1
introduces some asymmetry in the magnetic structure, with the magnetic field slightly stronger around
\theta=\beta_{1}=20\text{--}50^{\circ}
in all the rings. The mode
m=2
might be associated with a two-armed spiral pattern but the lack of any systematic variation of its pitch angle
p_{2}
and phase
\beta_{2}
do not support such an interpretation.

The amplitudes of the magnetic field strength in each azimuthal mode can be obtained from the values of
R_{m}
(
m=0,1,2,\ldots
) shown in
Table

using equation (

). For the thermal electron number densities

, we use the estimates of
Beck & Berkhuijsen 2025

.
The scale height

of the thermal disk of

(
Fletcher et al. 2004
)
yields the path length

, and the relation between

and the strength of the large-scale regular field

The strength of the regular magnetic field of mode

The magnetic field structure and its relation to the
24\thinspace\upmu
m infrared emission (a tracer of interstellar dust) is illustrated in the left-hand panel of Fig.

.
Gordon et al. 2006

which trace most of the dust structures at

shown in the figure in grey. The spiral structure in H

The axisymmetric part of the magnetic field also represents a logarithmic spiral but but it is more open, with the pitch angle
p_{0}=-15^{\circ}\pm 2^{\circ}
. As in other galaxies, the pitch angles of the magnetic field and spiral arms are not the same since they depend on different galactic parameters
(Section 13.10 of
Shukurov & Subramanian 2021
, and references therein)
. The regular magnetic field of M31 at the scales captured by our analysis shows little correlation with the spiral patterns visible in the dust and neutral gas distributions. As shown in the left-hand panel of Fig.
10
magnetic field can be stronger (vectors shown in yellow) both in and between the spiral arms delineated by either the interstellar dust or neutral hydrogen.

The large-scale magnetic field is not uniform along the bright radio ring at
r\approx 11\thinspace\text{kpc}
being stronger in the northern part of the galaxy. This asymmetry extends across the whole range of
r
and is visible in the original maps shown in Fig.

. It causes the presence of the
m=1
(bisymmetric) magnetic mode which has maximum magnitude at
\theta=\beta_{1}=20\text{--}40^{\circ}
and
\theta=\beta_{1}+180^{\circ}=200\text{--}220^{\circ}
at
r=8\text{--}14\thinspace\text{kpc}
.

Although the dust and gas appear to be well mixed in M31
(
Gordon et al. 2006
)
, the strength of the regular magnetic field is clearly not well correlated with the dust emission often being high where the
24\thinspace\upmu
m emission is quite low. This is not surprising since, apart from the gas density, the strength of the regular magnetic field depends on the galactic angular velocity, large-scale velocity shear due to differential rotation, and turbulent velocity among other parameters
(
Chamandy et al. 2014b
)
. Although the infrared
(
Gordon et al. 2006
;
Fritz et al. 2012
)
, ultraviolet
(
Thilker et al. 2005
)
and CO
(
Nieten et al. 2006
)
emission are strongly concentrated in spiral arms suggesting a significant arm–interarm contrast in the gas density, the regular magnetic field remains predominantly axisymmetric. Of course, spiral arms add structure to the magnetic field and the magnetic modes with
m>0
are caused by these deviations from the axial symmetry.

7
Discussion and conclusions

With the recently observed, more sensitive radio data at the wavelengths
3.59
,
6.18
, and
11.33\thinspace\text{cm}
, along with previously observed
\lambda 20.46\thinspace\text{cm}
polarization angle data, we revise and extend the Fourier mode analysis for the regular magnetic field between the galactocentric distances
r=6
and
14\thinspace\text{kpc}
using the Bayesian inference framework, an approach more robust and general that in earlier similar analyses.

We confirm earlier findings that the large-scale magnetic field in M31 is predominantly axisymmetric but reveal significant deviations from axial symmetry at all galactocentric distances explored. The axisymmetric part of the magnetic field is consistent with predictions of the mean-field dynamo theory
(
Nazareth et al. 2025
, and references therein)
. The non-axisymmetric magnetic modes (
m>0
for the azimuthal wave number) reflect local magnetic features at a kiloparsec scale resulting from the overall asymmetry of the galaxy, its spiral pattern and other perturbations, such as the hole at
r=8\text{--}12\thinspace\text{kpc}
,
\theta=130^{\circ}\text{--}150^{\circ}
produced by the passage of M32
(
Gordon et al. 2006
)
. Since the magnitudes and pitch angles of the non-axisymmetric modes vary rather irregularly with
r
(
Table

4
), they do not appear to be directly supported by the dynamo action. The general picture is fully consistent with mean-field galactic dynamo models which include complicated spiral patterns and bars
(
Mestel & Subramanian 1991
;
Subramanian & Mestel 1993
;
Moss et al. 2007
;
Chamandy et al. 2013a
;
Chamandy et al. 2013b
;
Chamandy et al. 2014a
;
Moss et al. 2015
;
Chamandy et al. 2015
, e.g.)
.

Synchrotron emission at
\lambda 11.33\thinspace\text{cm}
and especially
\lambda 20.46\thinspace\text{cm}
is depolarized by Faraday rotation in both the regular and random magnetic fields. Since such parameters of the magneto-ionic medium as the correlation scale of
RM
, the number density of thermal electrons and correlation between the magnetic and electron density fluctuations are highly uncertain, it is difficult to estimate the amount of depolarization
a priori
. Instead, we suggest to parametrize the depolarization using the parameters
\xi_{11}
and
\xi_{20}
to allow for the effective reduction of the Faraday rotation measure at
\lambda\lambda 11.33
and
20.46\thinspace\text{cm}
. These parameters are fitted together with the magnetic field parameters. The results are quite satisfactory (Appendix
A.2
), demonstrating a modest amount of depolarization at
\lambda 11.33\thinspace\text{cm}
(
\xi_{11}\approx 1
) and significant depolarization at
\lambda 20.46\thinspace\text{cm}
(
\xi_{20}
is significantly smaller than unity – see
Table

4
).

Our conclusions are as follows:

1.

We confirm that the large-scale (regular) magnetic field in M31 is dominated by the axisymmetric spiral (azimuthal wave number
m=0
) but detect higher-order modes
m\leq 4
. The
m=0
mode amplitude varies smoothly with the galactocentric radius with a maximum at
r=10\text{--}12\thinspace\text{kpc}
, where the field strength is about
2.7\thinspace\upmu\text{G}
. It has a nearly constant pitch angle
p=-(15^{\circ}\text{--}16^{\circ})
(a trailing logarithmic spiral) at
r=6\text{--}12\thinspace\text{kpc}
with a more open spiral
p=-22^{\circ}
at
r=12\text{--}14\thinspace\text{kpc}
. This dominant part of the magnetic field is consistent with the expectations of the galactic mean-field dynamo theory. The magnetic field extends to
r<6\thinspace\text{kpc}
and
r>14\thinspace\text{kpc}
.

2.

The higher modes (
m>0
) have much smaller amplitudes than the
m=0
mode which vary irregularly with
r
, have positive and negative pitch angles which also vary strongly with
r
. This suggests that they are not related to any large-scale dynamo action but rather arise from various deviations form axial symmetry in the galactic velocity field and gas density distribution. The
m=1
(bisymmetric) mode is slightly stronger than those with
m>1
and its presence reflects the fact that the magnetic field is stronger in the north-eastern part of the galaxy.

3.

The amount of depolarization of the radio emission at
\lambda\lambda 11.33
and
20.46\thinspace\text{cm}
varies little with the azimuthal angle in the galactic plane. With earlier observational data, the depolarization was attributed to the rotation measure
RM
gradients in a Faraday screen. The more sensitive data do not support this explanation. The lack of a strong, systematic azimuthal variation of the depolarization suggests that Faraday depolarization by random magnetic fields dominates although some contribution from the large-scale field cannot be excluded. Both the depolarization and deviations of the galactic magneto-ionic layer from symmetry along the line of sight (especially significant at the longer wavelength where the galaxy is not transparent to the polarized radio emission) are parametrized with the factors
\xi_{11}
and
\xi_{20}
which are considered free parameters fitted together with the other parameters of the magnetic field model. Thus, our analysis is independent of the nature of the depolarization.

4.

The foreground Faraday rotation measure
\text{RM}_{\text{fg}}
attributable to the Milky Way is not consistent across all the radial rings. At
r=8\text{--}14\thinspace\text{kpc}
,
\text{RM}_{\text{fg}}\approx-100\thinspace\text{rad}\thinspace\text{m}^{-2}
but stronger foreground Faraday rotation with
\text{RM}_{\text{fg}}\approx-134\thinspace\text{rad}\thinspace\text{m}^{-2}
apparently occurs at
r=6\text{--}8\thinspace\text{kpc}
.

We have neglected the contribution of the anisotropic random magnetic fields to the intrinsic polarization angle. We confirm that this is justified in M31 where the pitch angle of the large-scale magnetic field happens to be close to that of the total magnetic field which includes both the large-scale and anisotropic random parts. However, this may not be true in other galaxies.
