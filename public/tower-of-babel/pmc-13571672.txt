Factors Associated with Active Tuberculosis Disease in Patients with Lung Cancer Following Immune Checkpoint Blockade Therapy: A Retrospective Cohort Study and Case Reports
Authors: Xiao Wang, Yu-Xiao Wu, Wei-Ping Hu, Yue Ren, Jing Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundImmune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have revolutionized lung cancer treatment by improving survival in patients with advanced disease and expanding treatment options for patients with early-stage disease. However, ICIs may increase the risk of active tuberculosis (TB) disease. Our study investigated potential warning markers for active TB disease in patients with lung cancer receiving ICIs, aiming to inform management strategies.MethodsA retrospective cohort study at Zhongshan Hospital of Fudan University reviewed 4157 solid tumor patients treated with ICIs from January 2021 to February 2023. A total of 118 patients with lung cancer were included, either diagnosed with latent TB infection or non-active TB, or had a confirmed TB history before the start of ICI therapy. The primary outcome was the occurrence of active TB disease, with follow-up extending to February 1, 2024. Propensity score matching (PSM) was applied to account for differences in baseline characteristics.ResultsAmong the 118 patients, 5 (4.24%) developed active TB. Post-PSM Fisher’s exact test suggested that confirmed TB history (RR: 4.00, p = 0.029), high tumor necrosis factor-alpha (RR: 8.64, p = 0.030), high interleukin-2 receptor (RR: 6.00, p = 0.031), low white blood cell count (RR: 8.50, p = 0.009), and low lymphocyte count (RR: 9.33, p = 0.014) were significantly associated with active TB disease. Among patients who developed active TB disease, two had a decrease in T-SPOT.TB spot count after receiving ICI therapy.ConclusionThe identification of significant factors associated with active TB underscores the importance of vigilant monitoring and early intervention. A decrease in the T-SPOT.TB spot count may not necessarily reflect an improvement in TB infection, but may instead indicate potential TB activation.

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB), caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb), remains a major global public health concern and the leading cause of death from a single infectious agent. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), an estimated 10.7 million people developed TB worldwide in 2024, with China accounting for approximately 6.5% of global incident cases and remaining one of the 30 high TB burden countries.1 A comprehensive meta-analysis has indicated an elevated risk of TB in cancer patients when compared to the general population.2 Particularly in the realm of solid tumors, lung cancer stands out with a sixfold increased risk of TB in patients.2

Meanwhile, the advent of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) has revolutionized the therapeutic landscape for various malignancies, particularly lung cancer. ICIs have become a cornerstone of lung cancer treatment, with established roles in advanced disease and expanding applications in neoadjuvant, adjuvant, and perioperative treatment for resectable early-stage disease.3,4 In selected patients with advanced non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), first-line pembrolizumab resulted in a 5-year overall survival rate of 31.9%, compared with 16.3% with chemotherapy.5 However, this immune modulation may carry the risk of reactivating latent or chronic infections, including TB.6 Recent case reports, registry studies, animal modeling and in vitro mechanistic studies all support an increase in TB incidence.7 Among them, most of the reported TB cases occurred in patients receiving programmed death-1/programmed death-ligand 1 checkpoint inhibitors.8–10

The diagnosis of active TB in patients receiving ICIs can be challenging because its clinical and radiological manifestations may overlap with those of tumor progression, ICI-related pneumonitis, and sarcoid-like reactions.6,11,12 Such diagnostic overlap may complicate the timely recognition of active TB and subsequent clinical management. Therefore, careful integration of clinical, radiological, microbiological, and pathological findings is essential to distinguish active TB from these conditions. Despite the increasing number of reported TB cases, the specific impact of ICIs on TB reactivation and the development of active TB disease remains poorly understood. Moreover, established approaches for identifying patients who may be more likely to develop active TB during ICI therapy are currently lacking.

Taken these together, we investigated the medical history and clinical data of 118 lung cancer patients with latent TB infection (LTBI), non-active TB (non-ATB), or confirmed TB history, enrolled at Zhongshan Hospital of Fudan University from January 2021 to February 2023. We aim to investigate the potential warning markers of active TB disease following ICI therapy in patients with lung cancer, thus providing guidance for clinical prevention, developing better screening and management strategies and eventually reducing the risk of active TB disease in this specific population undergoing tumor ICI therapy.

Patients and Methods

Study Design and Participant Selection

This study was a retrospective clinical cohort study. We reviewed the medical records of 4157 patients with solid tumors receiving ICI therapy at Zhongshan Hospital of Fudan University from January 2021 to February 2023 and identified a total of 953 patients with “lung cancer” documented in the discharge diagnosis. Data for the present study were retrospectively extracted from the electronic medical records in June 2024.

The subjects meeting the inclusion and exclusion criteria were included for further investigation. The inclusion criteria were as follows: 1) the patient met the diagnostic criteria of lung cancer in accordance with Chinese Medical Association guideline for clinical diagnosis and treatment of lung cancer;13 2) the patient’s laboratory and imaging examination can be traced; 3) the patient had no evidence of active TB disease at the time of initial ICIs therapy according to Computed Tomography (CT) images, clinical symptoms, laboratory tests (including pathogenic evidence, inflammatory indicators, and T-SPOT.TB test results) and medical history documented in the medical records; 4) meeting with the diagnosis of LTBI or non-ATB, or having a confirmed TB history before the start of ICI therapy.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: 1) the patient had secondary lung cancer; 2) the patient’s laboratory and imaging examination were missing; 3) the patient received ICI therapy for other tumors.

After applying the eligibility criteria, 826 of the 953 screened patients were excluded: 161 had secondary lung cancer or received ICI therapy for other malignancies, 1 had active TB disease at the initiation of ICI therapy, and 664 had no confirmed TB history and no evidence of LTBI or non-active TB. A total of 127 patients met the eligibility criteria, of whom 9 were lost to follow-up. The participant selection process is shown in Figure 1. None of the patients included in the final cohort were receiving long-term systemic corticosteroid therapy (defined as continuous oral or intravenous corticosteroid use for more than 4 weeks) or other immunosuppressive agents at baseline. The outcome of this study was the occurrence of active TB disease. The follow-up period ended on February 1, 2024, and the follow-up time for cases without the occurrence of active TB disease or patient death exceeded one year.

Propensity score matching (PSM) was performed to reduce potential imbalance in selected clinical characteristics between patients who developed active TB disease and those who did not. Propensity scores were estimated based on age, sex, performance status (PS), body mass index (BMI), T-SPOT.TB status before ICI therapy, smoking history, diabetes mellitus (DM), and short-term systemic corticosteroid use during ICI therapy. Patients were matched at a ratio of 1:3 using nearest-neighbor matching based on the estimated propensity scores. Covariate balance before and after matching was evaluated and visualized using a Love plot.

Diagnostic Criteria and Definitions

The patients with lung cancer in this cohort were diagnosed in accordance with Chinese Medical Association guideline for clinical diagnosis and treatment of lung cancer13 and were confirmed or classified by pathology. The definitions related to TB are referenced from the Classification of TB (WS 196-2017) and Diagnostic Criteria for TB (WS288-2017) issued by the Chinese Centers for Disease Control and Prevention in 2017.14,15 LTBI is defined as an infection with Mtb, confirmed by the T-SPOT. TB test, but without clinical, bacteriological, or radiological evidence of active TB disease. Active TB is defined as having clinical symptoms and signs related to TB, with evidence of active TB disease from pathogenic, pathological, or radiological examinations. Non-ATB is defined as the absence of clinical symptoms and signs related to active TB disease, negative bacteriological tests, and radiological findings (excluding pulmonary changes caused by other reasons) consistent with one or more of the following manifestations: 1) calcified lesions (solitary or multiple); 2) cord-like lesions (with well-defined margins); 3) indurated lesions; 4) clean cavity; 5) pleural thickening, adhesions, or calcification. A confirmed TB history is defined as having been diagnosed with TB and having received treatment for it.

Receipt of short-term systemic glucocorticoids referred to taking an average daily dose of at least 10 mg of prednisone or dose-equivalent glucocorticoids16 for a minimum of continuous 10 days, starting after the beginning of ICI therapy. For the definition of ICI therapy, all the patients included had received more than one cycle of programmed death-1/programmed death-ligand 1 checkpoint inhibitors. Immunosuppressive agents included tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) inhibitors, azathioprine, cyclosporine, methotrexate, Tripterygium glycosides, tacrolimus, rapamycin, mycophenolate mofetil, and cyclophosphamide. Smoking history was defined as smoking index over 400 (number of cigarettes smoked per day × number of years smoked), regardless of current smoking status.

Data Collection and Analysis

The patients’ clinical data were collected from the electronic medical record system of Zhongshan Hospital of Fudan University. An independent investigator checked the collected data. If inconsistent data were found, the examiner would correct it after checking the original case history. The information to be collected was as follows: baseline information (including age, sex, smoking history, Body Mass Index (BMI), Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status (PS), cancer types, cancer stages, Tumor Proportion Score (TPS), gene mutation, ICI agents, previous therapy, systemic corticosteroids use during ICI therapy, chronic comorbidities, immune-related adverse events (irAEs), laboratory examinations (including blood routine examination, lymphocyte subsets examination, cytokine examination, albumin, globulin, pathogenic evidence, inflammatory indicators, T-SPOT.TB test results), CT and positron emission tomography-computed tomography (PET-CT) images, pathological evidence, and completed medical history.

Patients were divided into the active TB group and the non-active TB group according to whether the active TB disease occurred during the follow-up period. The laboratory parameters (including results of lymphocyte subsets and cytokines) of the most recent follow-up within a month before the outcome occurred or the follow-up period ended were adopted in statistical analyses.

This was a retrospective cohort study. We reviewed the medical records of 4157 patients with solid tumors who received ICI therapy at Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University, between January 2021 and February 2023, and identified 953 patients with a discharge diagnosis of lung cancer. Data for the present study were retrospectively extracted from the electronic medical records in June 2024.

The relevant reference values of the laboratory indexes of Zhongshan Hospital are presented in Supplementary Table 1.

Descriptive statistics were produced for demographic, clinical, and laboratory characteristics of patients. Statistical analysis was performed by R 4.3.1 or SPSS Statistics 26. Categorical variables were presented as absolute numbers and rates or percentages. Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was used for comparison of categorical data. The Shapiro–Wilk test would evaluate the normality of continuous variables. The continuous normally distributed variables were described by mean ± standard deviation (X±SD), and the two independent samples conforming to normal distribution were analyzed by t-tests. The non-normally distributed continuous variables were presented as medians and quartiles, and the difference between the two groups was compared using Mann–Whitney U-test. In all cases, p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All tests were two-tailed.

Patient Consent Statement and Ethical Approval

This study achieved formal approval from the Clinical Research Unit (KY2023572) and the Ethic Committee of Zhongshan Hospital (B2024-009). Extra patient consent statement was waived because all patients admitted to Zhongshan Hospital previously signed one to allow their medical records to be used in clinical observational studies.

Description of Confirmed Active TB Cases

Patient 1

Patient Background

Patient 1 sought medical care in September 2021 for a sternal protrusion, and a mass in the right lung’s lower lobe was detected. Subsequent imaging from October 2021 to February 2022 indicated an enlarging mass (3.2 × 2.0 cm to 4.1×3.1 cm) with potential hilar and mediastinal lymph node metastasis. Bronchoscopy confirmed squamous cell carcinoma, and grade II differentiation. Immunohistochemistry showed TPS ≥ 50, suggesting potential responsiveness to ICI therapy.

Course of Treatment and TB Diagnosis

The patient received Pembrolizumab (200 mg q3w) starting in February 2022. The patient was treated with corticosteroids due to liver dysfunction after two cycles and ICI therapy was discontinued. After liver function improved upon re-evaluation, treatment with monotherapy of Pembrolizumab was resumed. However, a CT scan after one cycle of treatment revealed potential pneumonia, and a lung biopsy showed granulomatous lesions. Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) culture tested positive for Mtb. Anti-TB treatment began with isoniazid, rifapentine, levofloxacin, and ethambutol for two months. The liver dysfunction recurred, leading to the cessation of anticancer treatment.

Patient 2

Patient Background

In August 2022, Patient 2 presented with a cough and sputum. Chest CT scan showed right lung consolidation, micronodules, and pericardial thickening. Bronchoscopy revealed a 0.5 cm neoplasm in the right main bronchus. Pathology confirmed squamous cell carcinoma, grade II, with 1 ≤ TPS < 5 and no mutations in key genes (EGFR, ALK, etc).

Course of Treatment and TB Diagnosis

The patient initiated first-line therapy with the TC regimen (paclitaxel and carboplatin) combined with Tislelizumab in September 2022. Due to grade III bone marrow suppression, paclitaxel was reduced in subsequent cycles. In February 2023, treatment was paused due to a diagnosis of tuberculous pleurisy through pleural fluid culture and CT evidence (Supplementary Figure 1). The patient started anti-TB treatment (isoniazid, rifapentine, ethambutol, pyrazinamide, and moxifloxacin) and, following disease progression, switched to oral anlotinib and triple anti-TB therapy (isoniazid, moxifloxacin, and ethambutol) in April 2023.

Patient 3

Patient Background

In October 2021, Patient 3 presented with shortness of breath and mild cough. A chest CT scan showed a mass near the right lung hilum, and bronchoscopy revealed neoplasm-induced bronchial stenosis. Cellular analysis of BALF indicated poorly differentiated malignant tumor cells. No genetic testing was performed. The patient had a history of TB and a strong positive T-SPOT.TB result, prompting the initiation of isoniazid for preventive treatment on November 3, 2021.

Course of Treatment and TB Diagnosis

The patient started the PP regimen (pemetrexed and cisplatin) combined with Pembrolizumab in November 2021, later switching to the PC regimen (pemetrexed and carboplatin) with Pembrolizumab. Maintenance Pembrolizumab continued until June 2022, before new lung lesions appeared. Cefixime was added for potential anti-infection treatment, along with continued isoniazid for TB prevention. In October 2022, a CT-guided lung puncture revealed granulomatous lesions, and tissue Xpert MTB/RIF result was positive, leading to the cessation of anti-tumor therapy and initiation of quadruple anti-TB treatment (isoniazid, rifampicin, ethambutol, and pyrazinamide).

Patient 4

Patient Background

In October 2021, Patient 4 sought care for low back pain. Bronchoscopy revealed small cell carcinoma in the left lung, and a chest CT raised suspicion of both TB and cancer. An MRI scan of the lumbar spine indicated metastatic tumor. The patient was diagnosed with small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma upon pathological consultation.

Course of Treatment and TB Diagnosis

The patient began chemotherapy with etoposide and lobaplatin in October 2021. A PET-CT scan showed left lung malignancy with extensive metastases, and EP chemotherapy with Durvalumab was initiated in November 2021. During this period, the patient had been treated with corticosteroids due to liver dysfunction. The patient was admitted on December 14, planning to receive chemotherapy and ICI therapy, but a CT scan revealed potential pneumonia. Xpert MTB/RIF result of BALF was positive, and next-generation sequencing detected a small number of Mtb complex group sequences. In response, quadruple anti-TB therapy was initiated, and chemotherapy and ICI therapy were halted.

Patient 5

Patient Background

In December 2021, Patient 5 presented with a cough. A lung CT scan revealed a left hilar mass and enlarged lymph nodes. Further evaluation with contrast-enhanced CT scan indicated a high likelihood of a malignant tumor in the left upper lung, with enlarged lymph nodes in the mediastinum, left hilum, and both clavicular regions, along with right lung nodules. Bronchoscopy in January 2022 confirmed adenocarcinoma, with TPS ≤ 1. Genetic testing did not reveal any mutations.

Course of Treatment and TB Diagnosis

The patient began a PC regimen with Tislelizumab in February 2022. The patient received six cycles of therapy through August 2022, before a new lesion in the left upper lung was identified. A CT-guided lung biopsy suggested granulomatous lesions with coagulative necrosis (Supplementary Figures 2 and 3). BALF culture confirmed the presence of Mtb. The patient started a quadruple anti-TB regimen, but experienced severe adverse reactions, leading to discontinuation. In December 2022, new concerns for metastasis arose by a follow-up chest CT scan, but a biopsy and BALF culture confirmed TB again. The patient started rifampicin and levofloxacin for anti-TB treatment, and the anti-tumor therapy was halted.

Discussion

In this study, we sought to explore the potential warning markers of active TB disease occurred in patients with lung cancer following ICI therapy. Our retrospective cohort included a lung cancer population at high risk for TB infection, including individuals meeting with the diagnosis of LTBI or non-ATB, or having a confirmed TB history before the initiation of ICI therapy. We innovatively found that patients with a confirmed TB history, higher levels of certain inflammatory markers (eg, TNF-α, IL-2R), and lower WBC and lymphocyte counts were associated with active TB disease.

In most Mtb-exposed individuals, there is a stable symbiotic relationship between the host and the pathogen, resulting in 90% of those exposed exerting lifelong control of the pathogen.17 TB can arise as a disease of both deficient18 and pronounced immune responses.19,20 In our cohort, 5 patients (4.24%) with lung cancer developed active TB disease after receiving ICI therapy, a proportion higher than studies from developed countries, including Japan, South Korea, and Singapore.21–23 Notably, a total of 953 patients diagnosed with “lung cancer” were initially studied, with 5 cases of active TB disease occurring during the follow-up period, and all the active TB cases were observed within the enrolled study population. These findings can provide a reference for future studies and suggest that clinical practice should pay more attention to patients at high risk of active TB disease, including individuals meeting with the diagnosis of LTBI or non-ATB, or having a confirmed TB history before the initiation of ICI therapy. Among the 5 infected patients, 4 were male and 1 female, with all cases over 60 years of age. This gender disparity and age distribution have also been reflected in other studies.22,24 All the cases reported in our study were pulmonary TB, including a case of tuberculous pleurisy. Nevertheless, Fujita et al7 reported that a quarter of adverse events were extrapulmonary TB, and there has even been a report of intestinal TB during lung cancer ICI therapy, indicating that researchers should also pay attention to extrapulmonary TB.25

Tissue diagnosis of active TB in patients receiving ICIs requires careful interpretation. Granulomatous inflammation alone is not specific for active TB and may also occur in ICI-associated sarcoid-like reactions or other treatment-related inflammatory changes.26,27 Therefore, histopathological findings, including the presence of caseous or coagulative necrosis and acid-fast staining results, should be interpreted in conjunction with clinical and radiological features and, whenever available, microbiological or molecular evidence of Mtb, including molecular tests such as TB PCR or Xpert MTB/RIF, as well as mycobacterial culture of tissue specimens. Among the three patients who underwent histopathological examination, Patients 1 and 5 showed granulomatous inflammation with coagulative necrosis; acid-fast staining was suspiciously positive in Patient 1 and negative in Patient 5, while BALF cultures were positive for Mtb in both patients. Patient 3 showed granulomatous inflammation and had a positive tissue Xpert MTB/RIF result. These findings highlight the importance of integrating histopathological findings with microbiological or molecular evidence when evaluating suspected active TB during ICI therapy.

For the first time, we have reported the association between the cytokine level before the onset of active TB disease and the development of active TB disease in patients with lung cancer following ICI therapy. Post-PSM Fisher’s exact test suggested that confirmed TB history, high TNF-α, high IL-2R, low WBC count, and low lymphocyte count should be of concern.

Some studies have reported the dual role of TNF-α in the host immune response. On one hand, PD-1 inhibition accelerates Mtb growth through excessive TNF-α secretion, and excessive TNF-α is associated with poor prognosis of TB.17,28 On the other hand, TNF-α is crucial for an effective host immune response as it plays an important role in controlling Mtb growth in zebrafish macrophages29 and human alveolar macrophages,30 and TB infections often occur after using anti-TNF-α antibodies.31 A classic study detected higher levels of soluble IL-2R in the serum of pulmonary TB patients, which decreased with improvement after anti-TB treatment, suggesting that sIL-2R-a could be a marker for monitoring disease activity in pulmonary TB patients.32 Our study indicates that high TNF-α and high IL-2R might serve as potential warning markers for active TB disease in patients with lung cancer following ICI therapy. These cytokines are related to tissue damage and cavity formation, further promoting the occurrence and development of TB.

Both TNF-α and IL-2R can be produced by T cells and can participate in T cell activation, proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis.33,34 Our study suggests that patients with low lymphocyte counts differ in TB infection rates compared to those with normal or high lymphocyte counts. Although T cell exhaustion primarily concerns functional changes, quantitative changes might also occur.35 In individuals with decreased T cells, the prevalence of infectious diseases dependent on T-cell-mediated immunity increases.36 After receiving ICI therapy, a significant reduction in antigen-specific effector T cells exposes patients to dual risks of infections and tumors.37 The T-SPOT.TB spot count in patients following ICI therapy is determined by the combination of T cell functions and the in vivo load of Mtb.37 A study has shown that when T-SPOT.TB test result shifts from positive to negative or when there is a decrease in spot count following ICI treatment, patients may be experiencing antigen-specific activation and depletion of T lymphocytes.37 In our cohort, among patients who developed active TB disease, two had a decrease in T-SPOT.TB spot count after receiving ICI therapy, emphasizing that T-SPOT.TB spot count reduction does not necessarily indicate an improvement in TB infection but might be a warning signal for TB activation.

Patient 1 received 3 cycles of ICI therapy and 2 months of anti-TB drugs before experiencing liver damage. Although Susan et al38 reported acceptable safety of simultaneous anti-TB therapy and ICI therapy, patient 1’s liver damage indicates the need to be vigilant about potential adverse reactions when using both treatments concurrently.

Only 2 patients in our cohort received preventive anti-TB treatment after starting ICI therapy. Patient 3, who had both a strong positive T-SPOT.TB and a history of TB, and CT showed old TB lesions in the right upper lung, developed active TB disease despite using isoniazid for preventive treatment. The other patient used isoniazid due to an old lesion in the right middle lobe and elevated T-SPOT.TB test result before ICI therapy and did not develop active TB disease before the end of follow-up. These observations highlight the uncertainty regarding preventive anti-TB treatment in this population. Prospective studies are needed to identify patients who may benefit from preventive treatment and to evaluate the efficacy and safety of different preventive regimens.

This study has some limitations. First, given the relatively low incidence of TB infections among patients undergoing ICI therapy, the sample size for those who do develop TB can be quite small. This limitation affects the power of statistical tests and the robustness of the findings. The application of Fisher’s exact test, while appropriate for identifying associations and differences, can be limited by the small sample size and the potential for overfitting. The limited sample size may also account for not identifying traditional TB risk factors, such as corticosteroid, DM, and others. Second, as a single-center study, our data may have inevitable biases due to the concentration of severe cases and regional reasons. Third, serological indicators may lack specificity. Moreover, as the laboratory variables analyzed in this study were obtained from the most recent follow-up within one month before the occurrence of active TB or the end of follow-up, abnormalities in these parameters may partly reflect early or subclinical TB disease. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted as potential warning markers rather than baseline predictors, and more specific predictive indicators should be explored and validated in future prospective studies.

Conclusion

In this retrospective cohort of patients with lung cancer at increased risk of TB, a confirmed history of TB and alterations in several inflammatory and immune parameters, including TNF-α, IL-2R, WBC, and lymphocyte counts, were associated with active TB disease following ICI therapy. These exploratory findings suggest that T-SPOT.TB and other immune parameters may warrant further investigation as potential warning markers. Larger prospective studies are needed to validate these findings and optimize TB screening and monitoring during ICI therapy.
