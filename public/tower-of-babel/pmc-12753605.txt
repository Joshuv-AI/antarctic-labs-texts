Enhanced attenuation correction in FDG-PET/CT with respiratory mismatch correction
Authors: Yoshiyuki Kitamura, Takuro Isoda, Motohiro Sando, Masaoki Kusunoki, Kazufumi Kikuchi, Shingo Baba, Kousei Ishigami
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT), CT is typically used for attenuation correction during the creation and reconstruction of PET images. Respiratory motion can cause artifacts due to misalignment of PET images, known as respiratory misalignment artifacts (RMA), which occur in up to 30% of lesion-based evaluations, often necessitating rescans of both PET and CT. A new method for reconstructing PET/CT artifacts caused by respiratory motion, termed ‘Enhanced AC’ (EAC) (GE HealthCare, USA), was developed to improve RMA. To evaluate the effectiveness of EAC, radiologists’ visual assessment using a misalignment score is appropriate regardless of the presence or absence of target lesions. This study aimed to assess the usefulness of EAC based on radiologist evaluations.MethodsConsecutive F-18 fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) PET/CT cases imaged at our hospital between July and August 2024 were included. Two radiologists independently assessed the deviation of FDG-PET/CT images from all cases imaged conventionally. Cases identified by either radiologist as misaligned were designated as the misalignment case group and used for evaluating EAC. A 5-point scoring system was established for the misalignment score. To evaluate the effect of EAC, PET coronal section images from the misalignment case group before EAC adaptation (pre-images) and the EAC-reconstructed images (EAC images) were displayed side by side. Five radiologists independently evaluated both images using a misalignment score.ResultsFifty-four patients (male/female = 36/18, average age 64.1 ± 15.0 years, median 66.5 years) were identified as the misalignment case group. The average misalignment scores from the five radiologists for the pre-images were 3.5, 3.4, 3.3, 3.1, and 3.1, respectively, whereas the scores for the EAC images were significantly lower (p < 0.01) across all radiologists: 2.1, 2.4, 2.5, 2.2, and 1.9, respectively.ConclusionsEAC is an effective and practical method for reducing RMA in selected cases. This method can be easily tailored to individual patients and does not require a large, deep-learning dataset, making it suitable for routine diagnostics.

Background

In positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT), CT is typically used for attenuation correction in the creation and reconstruction of PET images. Reconstructed PET images often contain various artifacts (Boellaard 2009; Todd et al. 2011), which can lead to incorrect evaluations and diagnoses in PET/CT examinations. One cause of these artifacts is respiratory motion, resulting in the misalignment of PET images. In a previous study, respiratory misalignment artifacts (RMA) were observed in up to 30% of lesion-based evaluations (Erdi et al. 2004), often necessitating repeat PET and CT scans.

Several methods to reduce RMA have been proposed, such as breath-holding (Kawano et al. 2011) and respiratory gating during free breathing (Nehmeh et al. 2002; Chang et al. 2010; Kang et al. 2021), both of which have shown positive effects. However, these reports focused primarily on lesion-based evaluations such as standard uptake value (SUV) and signal-to-noise ratio (SNR). Because they relied on quantitative values, they did not consider the overall impression and visibility from a radiologist’s perspective. In parallel with the rapid development of artificial intelligence technology, several methods using deep learning to reduce RMA have been proposed (Li et al. 2020; Lu et al. 2024). Although these methods may eventually yield PET/CT images with minimal RMA, challenges remain, including the need for dataset preparation specific to each PET/CT device, long calculation times, and the opaque nature of the algorithms.

A new method for reconstructing PET/CT artifacts caused by respiratory motion, known as ‘Enhanced AC’ (EAC) (GE HealthCare, USA) (Carmi et al. 2023), was recently developed. In this method, PET image artifacts are directly evaluated from an initial standard PET image volume using the CT images first. Key parameters are derived to control the reshaping of CT image data based on a predictable anatomical respiratory motion pattern. The reshaped CT volume is used to reconstruct corrected PET images (Figs. 1 and 2). This method uses only the images already captured from patients and does not require rescans of PET and CT or extensive datasets for deep learning. It can be used for all patients with free breath control, can be applied after the examination, and does not necessitate repeat CT or PET scans.Fig. 1Flowchart of the EAC process, adapted from Carmi et al. In evaluating the effectiveness of EAC, we believe that relying on tumor features is not appropriate, as we cannot precisely determine effectiveness if the target tumor does not appear on PET/CT images. Therefore, we opted to use visual scores of misalignment instead of SUV or SNR. The misalignment score is based on the radiologist’s visual assessment, enabling evaluation of artifact improvement in diagnosis regardless of lesion presence or absence.

This study aimed to assess the usefulness of EAC based on radiologist evaluations.

Materials and methods

Patients

A total of 179 consecutive F-18 fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) PET/CT cases imaged at our hospital with an Omni Legend 32 (GE HealthCare) between July and August 2024 were included. The following exclusion criteria were applied: 1) patients younger than 12 years, 2) patients with obvious body movements during imaging, and 3) patients who underwent imaging in a position other than prone (Fig. 3).Fig. 3Inclusion and exclusion criteria for this study

Inclusion and exclusion criteria for this study

Ultimately, 145 FDG-PET/CT examinations were included in the study (male/female = 79/66, average age 65.1 ± 15.6 years, median 69 years).

FDG-PET/CT examination

All PET/CT examinations in this study were performed using the Omni Legend 32 (GE HealthCare, Waukesha, WI, USA). The PET images were acquired at 2 min per bed position, with a field of view of 500 mm, a matrix size of 384 × 384, and a slice thickness of 2.07 mm using data-driven respiratory gating (Advanced MotionFree®; GE HealthCare) (Walker et al. 2020). The CT images were acquired with a tube voltage of 120 kV, a tube current of 80–220 mA, a slice thickness of 3.75 mm, and a spacing of 2.07 mm between slices. The reconstruction method employed was block sequential regularized expectation maximization (beta value 300) with a deep-learning system (Precision DL ®; GE HealthCare) (Mehranian et al. 2022; Dadgar et al. 2024), both with and without EAC. Patient breathing was not controlled by physicians. FDG was injected at a dose of 4 MBq/kg, 60 min before the examination. Patients fasted for 4 hours before the injection.

Visual evaluation with the misalignment score

The degree of misalignment was subjectively scored from 1 to 5 by each evaluator based on the examples shown in Fig. 4.Fig. 4Sample misalignment scoring system used by the radiologists. A score of 1 indicates no significant misalignment, while scores of 2‒5 represent increasing degrees of misalignment

Sample misalignment scoring system used by the radiologists. A score of 1 indicates no significant misalignment, while scores of 2‒5 represent increasing degrees of misalignment

To select the exams for visual evaluation, two radiologists specializing in nuclear medicine (YK and MS) independently assessed the deviation of FDG-PET/CT images from all conventionally imaged cases. Cases in which either radiologist identified misalignment were designated as the misalignment case group and used for the evaluation of EAC. Finally, the presentation order of case images was randomized by another radiologist specializing in nuclear medicine (TI) and set for using image evaluation.

To evaluate the effect of EAC, PET coronal section images from the misalignment case group before EAC adaptation (pre-images) and the EAC-reconstructed images (EAC images) were displayed side by side in a diagnostic monitor using for daily PET/CT diagnosis in our institution. Both images were evaluated using a misalignment score by five radiologists with over three years of experience in diagnosing nuclear medicine examinations (YK, MS, SB, KK, and MK), who performed the evaluation independently. The order of image presentation used for evaluation was the same for all evaluators and was conducted only once.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using JMP Ver. 17 (JMP Statistical Discovery LLC, Cary, NC, USA).

The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was employed for comparisons between the two groups, while the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC(2,1)) was used to compare ratings between the radiologists. ICC(2,1) ratings were classified as follows: below 0.5 as poor, 0.5 or above but below 0.75 as moderate, 0.75 or above but below 0.9 as good, and 0.9 or above as excellent. A p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Fifty-four patients (male/female = 36/18, average age 64.1 ± 15.0 years, median 66.5 years) were identified as the misalignment case group (Fig. 3).

In evaluating the pre-images of the misalignment case group, the average misalignment scores from the five radiologists were 3.5, 3.4, 3.3, 3.1, and 3.1, respectively. The scores for EAC images were significantly lower across all radiologists (p < 0.01): 2.1, 2.4, 2.5, 2.2, and 1.9, respectively. Discussion

EAC has specific features, such as using only the patient’s images, improving the alignment of the lungs and liver near the diaphragm, eliminating the need for breath holding, being less affected by individual differences in respiratory status and guidance provided during imaging, allowing adaptation even after the examination, and not requiring additional imaging. Radiologists in this study reported lower misalignment scores for PET images with EAC applied compared to conventional images. The responses from radiological technologists responsible for PET/CT imaging were not strictly standardized; however, improvement was still observed with the use of EAC in this study. This indicates that EAC is effective regardless of imaging conditions.

In the radiologists’ evaluation, some cases of RMA worsened after EAC was applied (Fig. 7). Additionally, 91 cases with no RMA, which we did not use for radiologists’ evaluation, 35 cases (38.4 %) showed worsening of RMA. We speculated that another structure resembling the diaphragm was recognized by EAC in some non-RMA cases. On the other hand, in some cases with large RMA, the misaligned diaphragm was not recognized by EAC. It is interesting that what is a condition that did not improve the RMA with the EAC; however, we were unable to find the condition. Since EAC does not require rescanning and can be applied to previously obtained PET and CT images, it does not extend examination time and reduces unnecessary exposure from CT rescans. This method is also advantageous because if the initial imaging reveals even slight diaphragmatic misalignment, EAC reconstruction can be performed during or after the examination. From the patient’s perspective, even if RMA existed in the PET/CT exam, EAC can avoid extending the examination time and increasing the patient’s radiation exposure caused by added CT or PET examination.

In this study, visual evaluation using a misalignment score was employed as the assessment method. Although the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) for misalignment scores before and after EAC application was low, the improvement in evaluation values across all evaluators supports the usefulness of this method, with a medium ICC observed for cases classified as improved. This is because the difference of the misalignment score of evaluators is due to difference in the individual perception of the degree of RMA than difference in reading experience. This individual perception can’t be improved through training to approach the correct answer. The results of this study indicate that while applying EAC can likely improve RMA with high probability for some readers, some readers may not recognize RMA improvement in same EAC applied case images. However, to evaluate EAC performance more convincingly, quantitative evaluation methods such as standardizing misalignment scores, which may improve the inter-rater ICC, are needed.

Previous reports typically assessed methods for improving image deviation caused by respiratory motion using the SUV of the lesion or the SUV ratio of the lesion to surrounding tissue (SNR) (Nehmeh et al. 2002; Erdi et al. 2004; Kawano et al. 2011; Todd et al. 2011). We believe this quantitative method is challenging to apply with EAC because improvements in SUV and SNR do not consistently align with the evaluator’s subjective visual assessment. Moreover, it is difficult to gather cases with lesions suitable for evaluation using this method due to the introduction of selection bias. While methods exist to assess homogeneity in organs, such as standard deviation (SD) or coefficient of variation (CV), evaluating relatively homogeneous tissues, like those in the lung and liver, becomes problematic when these tissues are affected by the correction process in EAC-applied cases. Therefore, we believe that the misalignment score is useful for evaluating the efficiency of EAC in vivo, even if these quantitative methods may apply in phantom models. However, in order to claim that EAC can be applied universally, a quantitative evaluation system is needed because the misalignment score system is qualitative evaluation. Several methods are considered in our future, such as standardization of the misalignment score system or to measure difference of lengths or area between before and after EAC application which caused by RMA. On the other hand, it is necessary to evaluate the SUV of lesions and verify changes in the organ homogeneity of accumulation, such as the liver and lungs.

The cases with misaligned PET images near the diaphragm accounted for 54 of the 145 exams (37.2%). This rate is nearly the same as previously noted (Erdi et al. 2004). In addition, 56 of 161 exams (34.8%) were from another silicon photomultiplier PET/CT used at our hospital, and 34 of 93 exams (36.6%) were from a previously used conventional photomultiplier tube-type PET/CT, based on our simplified and imperfect study. These results indicate that RMA does not depend on the type of PET/CT machine used.

The number of exams with misaligned PET images was greater in males. This may be attributed to body size or differences in response to breath-holding instructions, although the specifics remain unclear.

This study has several limitations. First, it is difficult to determine whether this test improves diagnostic performance due to the small number of cases with lesions. Second, this study was retrospective at a single center. We think that a prospective study is needed to clarify whether the EAC can be applied as a universal method. Additionally, multi-center validation is necessary, and for this purpose, the misalignment score system must be standardized. Third, we have not made direct comparisons with other methods for reducing diaphragmatic misalignment, potentially due to a lack of widely accepted evaluation methods. However, EAC is not intended to replace other misalignment-reducing methods, as it utilizes available PET/CT images and can be combined with other approaches.

Conclusion

EAC is an effective and practical method for reducing RMA in selected cases. This method can be easily tailored to individual patients and does not require a large, deep-learning dataset, making it suitable for routine diagnostics. Still, further refinement is needed for universal application.
