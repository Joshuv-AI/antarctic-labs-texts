Tourist arrival forecasting with game-theoretic social influence weighting and deep learning
Ali Nikseresht

Abstract
In the era of Industry 5.0, data-driven decision-making has become indispensable for planning in service systems such as tourism. This paper proposes a non-linear hybrid approach for forecasting tourist arrivals to Hong Kong from five major source markets before COVID-19 and to Sanya, China, through the COVID-19 shock. The method integrates multiple sources of Internet big data and employs a cooperative game-theoretic algorithm to identify coalitions of opinion leaders on social media, whose content then receives larger weights. The Empirical Wavelet Transform (EWT), applied causally, manages nonstationary attributes, and a Stacked Bi-directional Long Short-Term Memory (Stacked BiLSTM) network tuned by Bayesian optimization generates the forecasts. We prove that the Shapley values of the underlying trust-weighted coverage game have a closed form and that the coalition of maximal synergy can be found exactly in polynomial time. Relative to the same network fed only with past arrivals, Internet big data reduce the one-step-ahead root mean squared error by 4.1–41.7% across the Hong Kong markets and opinion-leader weighting by a further 10.1–49.4%, against 5.3% and 8.7% for Sanya. The approach compares favorably with published benchmarks and remains robust under volatile conditions, which suggests uses in logistics, supply chains and production planning.

Big data analytics, Artificial Intelligence (AI) and game-theoretical modeling are revolutionizing forecasting and resource management by enabling organizations to anticipate demand patterns, mitigate uncertainties and optimize operations. Furthermore, the exchanges between users can be valuable in determining the collective viewpoint of a cohort, and promotional activities that build on such exchanges have occasionally proved advantageous for the industry. The interplay between individuals has an impact on the conduct and cognitive processes of others
(
Jain et al., 2020

(

By the extent of their influence, global opinion leaders influence large and densely connected communities, whereas local leaders influence a limited set of communities that may be homogeneous or diverse. By domain, monomorphic leaders are influential in a single field, while polymorphic leaders are influential in several fields at once and therefore tend to combine broad knowledge with wide reach. By the character of their influence, leaders can be beneficial or detrimental, since some influential users try to damage the standing of a product by spreading unfavorable information as part of a marketing tactic. Finally, by the duration of their influence, leaders can be short-term or long-term. The influence of opinion leaders has a significant impact on the decision-making process of their followers, as the preferences of the latter are often shaped by the trust and belief dynamics established with their respective opinion leaders
(
Bamakan et al., 2019
)
. Opinion leaders are active in a broad range of fields such as political science, healthcare, financial business, marketing, dissemination of information, education and economics, and the methods proposed to detect them are correspondingly diverse
(
Bamakan et al., 2019
)
. In contrast to the present investigation, previous research in the field of TM has not explored identifying opinion leaders through game theory. This approach involves the identification of a group of users who exhibit the highest level of synergy, which is then designated as the coalition of opinion leaders
(
Jain et al., 2020
)
. Subsequently, these opinion leaders and their associated network (coalition) are assigned a greater positive or negative weight, reflecting their heightened impact and significance in relation to the content they generate and endorse. As a result, our
second contribution
posits that game theory plays a crucial role in identifying opinion leaders within social networks, and it adapts to tourism the coalition approach of
Jain et al. (2020)
, which combines trust, centrality and cooperative and non-cooperative games. In a social network, the actions undertaken by a user have an impact on the outcomes of the choices and decisions made by another user, and although individuals may lack knowledge regarding the impact of their actions on the outcomes of other users, they are aware that other users observe and react to what they do. Studies of social networks have used the Shapley value to identify influential individuals who can effectively promote and drive the growth of products in the practical business sector
(
Narayanam and Narahari, 2011
)
. The application of game theory can yield valuable insights into the identification of various forms of power centrality and the dynamics of relationships among users within a social network
(
Michalak et al., 2013
)
. Game theory approaches have also been found to address various concerns about network privacy and security
(
Manshaei et al., 2013
)
. In line with our second contribution, we refrain from indiscriminately extracting and examining all cases using social media data. Instead, we first detect the most impactful communities and opinion leaders through a game-theoretic approach and assign significance weights to them. In this way, the social media analytics that feed the forecasting stage become goal-oriented, and the procedure is consistent with the Pareto principle (80–20 rule), under which a small share of users accounts for a large share of the influence.

In addition, the time series pertaining to tourism demand encompasses four latent sub-components, namely the seasonal element, cycles, trends and an irregular term. Seasonality is the dominant factor in tourism demand time series. Recurring seasonal patterns can be observed in tourism demand time series, such as the number of international tourist arrivals, even upon a cursory examination. Tourism demand cycles, which can endure for multiple years and can be impacted by non-seasonal factors, including economic cycles, exchange rates for currencies, money supply, promotional efforts and terrorism, are concealed behind the prominent seasonal fluctuations
(
Botha and Saayman, 2022
)
. The long-term trend, being one of the four components of the tourism demand time series, can be readily identified, as the rise of global tourism demand over the past four decades shows. Despite the relevance of dynamic behaviors in the tourism demand forecasting literature, there is a dearth of prior research in TM that has taken into account these dynamic statistical behaviors to predict the tourism demand of a destination. Accordingly, as the
third contribution
, the present study utilizes a capable wavelet-based time-frequency analysis, the Empirical Wavelet Transform (EWT)
(
Gilles, 2013
)
, to capture these dynamic statistical data features. It applies the transform causally, so that the decomposition never uses observations beyond the forecast origin
(
Zhang et al., 2015
)
, and it forecasts the tourism demand with a memory-aware hybrid method based on Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM), namely a robust Stacked BiLSTM model whose hyperparameters are tuned by Bayesian optimization (BO). Decomposition–ensemble designs are now common in technology-enabled forecasting and handle nonstationary and noisy series in other domains as well
(
Mohammadi et al., 2023
;
Wang et al., 2023
)
, and the EWT suits tourism data because its filter bank adapts to the spectrum of each series.

Beyond the empirical application, the paper makes the game-theoretic component analytically tractable, which is our
fourth contribution
. We formalize trust-weighted influence as a coverage game and prove that its Shapley values have a closed form that can be computed in time linear in the size of the network (Theorem
1
). We show that the coalition of maximal synergy, which enumeration-based procedures search for over all subsets of users, can be found exactly in polynomial time and that the eigenvector-centrality tie-break used in practice is resolved by a unique largest optimal coalition (Theorem
2
). We also characterize the equilibria of the pairwise persuasion games that decide whether two users cooperate (Proposition
3
), show that the associated opinion dynamics converge geometrically to a consensus (Proposition
B.3
) and bound how errors in the estimated influence probabilities and synergies can change the selected coalition (Proposition
B.4
). Finally, we prove that a full-sample EWT leaks future information and state the conditions under which the forecasting pipeline is free of look-ahead bias (Lemma
1
and Proposition
5
).

In summary, the present study’s primary goal is to predict tourists visiting (i) Hong Kong from five major source markets before COVID-19 and (ii) Sanya, located in the Hainan province of China, over a period that includes the COVID-19 shock. This will be achieved by incorporating tourist-related online big data into the destination-predicting system. The present study addresses previously identified research gaps by introducing a new and resilient hybrid methodology that utilizes online data to predict a destination’s tourism demand, and its integrated algorithmic components, to the best of our knowledge, have not previously been deployed together in tourism demand prediction. Ablation experiments on the same series isolate the contributions of Internet data, opinion leader detection and the EWT, and the framework is compared with benchmark models reported for the same destinations. Empirical findings indicate that the proposed technique achieves the lowest errors among the configurations and benchmarks considered.

2
Literature review

In this section, a background on tourism demand forecasting, social media analytics and the detection of opinion leaders in social media using game theory is provided.

2.1
Tourism demand forecasting

Various techniques for forecasting have been utilized in the analysis of tourism demand, encompassing both quantitative and qualitative methodologies
(
Law et al., 2019
)
. Qualitative techniques such as Delphi and consensus methodologies are frequently utilized to predict tourism demand. These techniques are based on the subjective knowledge of specialists in particular tourism sectors. The limitations that are inherent in qualitative methods, such as their restricted generalizability, have resulted in a growing interest among researchers in utilizing quantitative approaches to estimate relationships among various factors in tourism data. As per the findings of
Law et al. (2019)
, two primary approaches are commonly employed to improve the efficacy of quantitative methods. These include adding supplementary variables that may be pertinent and creating more intricate models to enhance the generalizability of outcomes, and the present study follows both. The primary quantitative methodologies employed in the analysis of tourism demand comprise time series analysis, econometric analysis and AI techniques
(
Song et al., 2019
)
. Time series models are commonly employed, particularly those encompassing Autoregressive Moving Average (ARMA) and its derivatives, such as Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA) and seasonal ARIMA. The models above are utilized to predict forthcoming tourism demand based on past trends and patterns and have been extensively embraced as standard models in associated research
(
Song et al., 2019
)
. The conventional models encounter challenges in handling intricate and non-linear tourism demand data due to their dependence on the stability of historical patterns and economic structure in real-world scenarios
(
Zhang et al., 2021
)
. The utilization of AI-based models has garnered significant attention in the pursuit of enhanced non-linear modeling, exhibiting notable predictive precision
(
Zhang et al., 2021
;
Nikseresht et al., 2026
)
. AI models exhibit strong adaptability and powerful processing capacity for non-linear time series, without requiring assumptions of data stationarity and distribution. Based on their layer depth, AI models can be categorized into shallow and DL models
(
Law et al., 2019
)
. Radial basis function networks
(
Claveria et al., 2015
)
, multi-layer perceptron and support vector regression are commonly utilized shallow learning models. Compared to other models, DL models have demonstrated the ability to overcome such limitations and garnered significant attention in recent research endeavors. LSTM-based methodologies are prevalent among these models.
Law et al. (2019)
utilized a DL framework to predict Macau tourism arrivals from a large set of search intensity indices, and their model outperformed several benchmark models, among them support vector regression and an artificial neural network.
Zhang et al. (2020)
and
Bi et al. (2020)
integrated multiple explanatory variables, such as search engine and weather data, into their LSTM models of daily tourist flows, and
Bi et al. (2020)
showed that these variables improved forecasting accuracy. In addition to the utilization of a singular LSTM model, several studies have sought to expand upon the LSTM model in order to mitigate its inherent limitations, including but not limited to model overfitting
(
Gal and Ghahramani, 2016
)
, a high number of parameters
(
Greff et al., 2017
)
and a sluggish rate of convergence, for example by replacing the LSTM cell with a lighter gated recurrent unit inside a decomposition-based hybrid
(
Wang et al., 2021
)
.
Kulshrestha et al. (2020)
utilized a Bayesian Bidirectional LSTM model to predict the tourism demand in Singapore originating from various prominent source countries, and
Tan and Li (2026)
combined two recurrent networks in an ensemble to forecast outbound tourism before and during the pandemic. Despite the significant enhancement in forecasting accuracy brought about by DL models, such as LSTM, the pursuit of further improvement in forecasting remains ongoing.

Furthermore, prior research has presented various approaches aimed at enhancing the accuracy of forecasting outcomes, such as the development of combination models to mitigate the risk of forecasting failure
(
Gao et al., 2025
)
, the construction of decomposition-ensemble models that divide complex forecasting tasks into simpler subtasks to generate more precise forecasts
(
Nikseresht et al., 2025a
;
Wang et al., 2023
;
Zhang et al., 2021
)
, and the inclusion of additional influencing factors (e.g., data from social media platforms) to better explain and model the non-linear stochastic statistical behaviors in tourism demand time series
(
Hu et al., 2022
;
Li et al., 2021
;
Park et al., 2021
)
. Outside tourism, news-sentiment indices fed to interpretable machine learning models have improved forecasts of economic cycles under uncertainty shocks
(
Sun et al., 2025
)
, and such cycles are among the drivers of tourism demand cycles. Decomposition-based hybrids must, however, be applied with care, because decomposing a whole series before splitting it into training and test samples lets future values leak into the inputs and makes reported accuracy optimistic
(
Zhang et al., 2015
)
, and in real-time use the decomposed sub-series change whenever new observations arrive, so that models trained on earlier sub-series may no longer fit them
(
Wang and Wu, 2016
)
. Search data have been combined with mixed-frequency regressions
(
Bangwayo-Skeete and Skeete, 2015
)
, denoising them before modeling improves tourism forecasts
(
Cao et al., 2025
)
, wavelet analysis of a Google search index has dated the phases of the COVID-19 crisis
(
Szczygielski et al., 2024
)
, and
Liu et al. (2018)
showed with a vector autoregression that web search volumes and visitor arrivals at a cultural destination Granger-cause each other. Turning to multisource designs,
Li et al. (2020)
utilized Internet big data from various sources, such as search engines and online reviews, to predict the number of tourists that would visit a national park, and found that utilizing multiple sources of big data can lead to improved performance in predicting tourist arrivals compared to relying on a single source of big data, such as online reviews or search engines. More recently,
Gao et al. (2025)
let the weights of a forecast combination vary over time and across the features of economic and search engine data for Hong Kong and Sanya, the two destinations studied here, and
Qin et al. (2025)
built platform-specific indices of how online content converts into later visits.

2.2
Roles of social media analytics

There is increasing attention towards social media usage in various applications, such as the forecasting of sales and of warranty claims
(
Cui et al., 2018
;
Nikseresht et al., 2024a
)
. The impacts of social media information on consumer co-creation
(
Zhang et al., 2023
)
and purchasing behavior
(
Cui et al., 2018
)
have been explored in prior research. The literature distinguishes two distinct effects of such information, the attention effect and the endorsement effect. The effects have been confirmed by pertinent literature, for example in the finding that social media word of mouth raises product awareness while displayed sales act as a signal of quality
(
Li and Wu, 2018
)
, and social media signals have also improved operational forecasts such as those of consumer returns
(
Nikseresht et al., 2025b
)
. Scholars have argued that the awareness of a product is necessary for consumers to engage in purchase behavior. Consumers’ purchasing decisions are ultimately influenced by the purchases and endorsements of their peers. The attention effect pertains to the level of consumer product awareness. Scholars have posited that the allocation of attention can exert an impact on the purchasing behavior of individuals, as well as serve as a tool for predicting economic outcomes. As demonstrated by prior studies
(
Cui et al., 2018
;
Duan et al., 2008
)
, the attention garnered by social media platforms has the potential to increase the sales of products. The impact of attention is typically quantified through metrics such as count or popularity data.
Dellarocas et al. (2007)
posited that the utilization of volume data is rooted in the notion that heightened customer discourse regarding a product is positively correlated with increased visibility and awareness of the said product among potential consumers, and
Duan et al. (2008)
showed that the volume of reviews indicates the strength of electronic word-of-mouth (eWOM), a form of awareness effect that significantly influences product sales. The phenomenon of the endorsement effect is indicative of the product’s perceived quality. The distinction between the endorsement and attention effect lies in the former’s ability to convey a consumer’s inclination or sentiment orientation, which is indicative of their level of satisfaction and attitude towards a given product.
Dellarocas et al. (2007)
and
Duan et al. (2008)
employed valence data to examine the impact of product endorsement, whereas alternative studies utilized sentiment analysis for the same purpose. The evidence on valence is less clear-cut than that on volume. Once the endogeneity of ratings was taken into account,
Duan et al. (2008)
found no significant effect of the average rating on box office revenues, whereas the volume of online posts remained a significant driver. The sentiment categorization can be divided into three distinct classifications, namely negative, neutral or positive
(
Geetha et al., 2017
)
. Sentiment has been characterized as displaying diverse levels of intensity or potency
(
Thelwall et al., 2010
)
. Individuals have the ability to express their opinions on various social media platforms. Research has demonstrated that sentiment analysis can offer supplementary insights and advantages when compared to data based solely on counts or volumes
(
Lau et al., 2018
)
.
Dellarocas et al. (2007)
expounded upon the rationale for utilizing valence data, positing that unfavorable reviews can dissuade consumers from making a purchase, whereas favorable reviews may stimulate consumer interest and promote sales. Furthermore, a number of scholars have confirmed the impact of the endorsement effect on consumers’ buying patterns. The impact of a customer’s sentiment on an item’s page has been observed to influence the perceptions of others regarding the quality of the product, subsequently affecting the sales of the product
(
See-To and Ngai, 2018
)
. Big data on millions of hotel reviews further show that the ratings themselves depend on the platform and on the device used to submit them
(
Mariani et al., 2023
)
, and user-generated content media differ in how much semantic, sentiment and trend information they carry
(
Park and Lee, 2025
)
. Both findings are reasons to combine several platforms rather than rely on one.

2.3
Identification of opinion leaders within social networks

In contemporary times, there has been a significant shift in human decision-making and attitudes, which has been influenced by the vast amount of data available on the Internet, mainly through social media. This has led to a transformation in individuals’ perspectives toward firmly held values. The crucial role of opinion leaders in the dissemination of information and promotional activities is paramount within online social networks. Numerous methodologies have been suggested to ascertain the opinion leader within the realm of online social networks, owing to the progression of technological advancements
(
Bamakan et al., 2019
)
. Trust is a crucial factor in the establishment of relationships within social networks
(
Kwan and Ramachandran, 2009
;
Mayer et al., 1995
)
, and it also shapes how node centrality and link strength are assessed
(
Ruan and Durresi, 2016
)
. The degree of connection in a network is influenced by a range of factors, including but not limited to indegree–outdegree covariance, clustering coefficient, network size, density and transitivity
(
Jain et al., 2020
)
.

Our methodology makes the identification of opinion leaders a constituent element of tourism demand forecasting and performs it with an innovative game-theoretic technique. The approach combines trust statistics, network measures and cooperative and non-cooperative solution concepts, and it addresses three weaknesses of earlier methods, namely their reliance on a single centrality measure, their neglect of how users jointly create value, and the exponential cost of searching over all coalitions.

2.4
Research gaps

Three gaps emerge from this review. First, most Internet-based tourism forecasting studies use one or two kinds of online data, for example search queries
(
Liu et al., 2018
;
Hu et al., 2023
)
, news
(
Park et al., 2021
)
or reviews
(
Hu et al., 2022
)
. Studies that combine several platforms are rare
(
Li et al., 2020
;
Gao et al., 2025
)
, and none, to our knowledge, combines search engines, two social networks and a review platform while separating volume from valence. Second, the content entering these models is seldom linked to the influence of the users who produce it.
Fronzetti Colladon et al. (2019)
showed that the centralization of forum communication networks, which reflects the presence of eminent contributors, helps to forecast arrivals, and
Qin et al. (2025)
weighted platforms by how their content converts into visits, but no study weighs individual items of online content by the game-theoretic influence of the users who produce or endorse them. Third, decomposition and deep learning have each improved tourism forecasts
(
Zhang et al., 2021
;
Kulshrestha et al., 2020
)
, yet their combination with multisource Internet data is scarce, and the causal application of the decomposition, which is necessary for out-of-sample evaluation
(
Zhang et al., 2015
)
, is seldom discussed. The framework developed in Section
3
addresses all three gaps.

3
Methodology

Developing reliable forecasts for business-related time series is instrumental for practical applications in areas such as cost control, profit maximization and resource allocation. In this context, enhancing accuracy directly translates into more effective decision-making and improved planning outcomes. The present study contributes to the business literature, particularly in the domain of tourism demand forecasting, by proposing an advanced forecasting algorithm that bridges the domains of engineering and computer science and offers a robust and innovative prediction framework. The subsequent sections detail the data utilized in this study, outline the data preprocessing steps and describe the core components of our hybrid algorithm. We also explain the evaluation metrics used to assess forecasting performance and establish the theoretical properties of the game-theoretic and decomposition components on which the framework relies.

3.1
Data and evaluation metrics

3.1.1
Brief data description

In this study, we utilize two tourism demand cases concerning the arrivals into two different tourist destinations. We chose two different cases for two reasons. The first is to test the robustness of the proposed hybrid algorithm in versatile settings, and the second is that COVID-19 has had devastating impacts on the tourism industry, so we wanted to see how the method proposed in this paper performs during the pandemic. The first case is Hong Kong. Hong Kong has earned the “Asia’s World City” moniker due to its distinct cultural amalgamation of Western lifestyle and Chinese traditions
(
Park et al., 2021
)
. For this case, Internet big data sourced from Baidu, Google, Twitter, Facebook and TripAdvisor are combined with monthly tourist arrival data from five selected source markets. These markets include the most significant short-haul market,
Mainland China
, which accounted for 78.29% of all tourist arrivals in 2019, and the most substantial long-haul market,
the U.S.
, which constituted 28.09% of the long-haul market in the same year. The study also considers Canada, the U.K. and Australia as significant source markets. The data on monthly tourist arrivals from the aforementioned countries spanning from August 2012 to May 2019 (82 months per market) were collected from the Hong Kong Tourism Board

3.1.3
Evaluation metrics

Root Mean Square Error (RMSE), Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE) and Root Mean Squared Relative Error (RMSRE) are utilized to identify forecasting errors. A lesser numerical value of said indices demonstrates superior performance. For a forecast horizon
h
, let
\mathcal{T}_{h}
be the set of test months whose
h
-step-ahead forecasts are evaluated,
y_{t}>0
the observed arrivals and
\hat{y}_{t}
the forecast made
h
months earlier. The three measures are computed as follows,

\displaystyle\mathrm{MAPE}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}_{h}|}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\left|\frac{y_{t}-\hat{y}_{t}}{y_{t}}\right|,

(3)

\displaystyle\mathrm{RMSE}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}_{h}|}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\left(y_{t}-\hat{y}_{t}\right)^{2}},

(4)

\displaystyle\mathrm{RMSRE}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}_{h}|}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\left(\frac{y_{t}-\hat{y}_{t}}{y_{t}}\right)^{2}},

(5)

where MAPE and RMSRE are reported as proportions, so that a MAPE of 0.0026 corresponds to 0.26%
(
Hu et al., 2022
;
Park et al., 2021
)
. The RMSE is scale-dependent, whereas MAPE and RMSRE are scale free
(
Hyndman and Koehler, 2006
)
, which is why the three measures are reported together.

3.2
Proposed hybrid approach

This section develops a multi-staged algorithmic framework as our hybrid solution for tourism demand forecasting comprising game-theoretical detection of opinion leaders in social networks, the EWT and the Stacked BiLSTM model as its backbone. As for the implementation steps, since social media data are mainly unstructured and vast, to make the social media analytics organized and goal-oriented, at the first stage we implement a game-theoretical approach to detect the opinion leaders in tourism and assign them and their vicinity network (coalition) a higher positive or negative weight (showing and considering their more positive or negative impact and significance) with regard to the content that they create and promote. This weighting is the highest for the leaders themselves, and it decreases with the network distance from the leaders. The holistic view regarding the whole process of the utilized Internet big data is shown in Fig.
2
. After detecting the leaders and assigning the weights, we go to the second stage and compensate for the cyclical, seasonal and trend features, among others, utilizing the EWT and the stacked version of the BiLSTM. We execute the EWT on all our data, i.e., the Internet big data and tourism demand time series, within the walk-forward protocol of Section
3.2.5
. Finally, we do the ultimate forecasting by implementing the stacked version of the BiLSTM that not only considers the memory features of our data but also better deals with the nonstationary features of the data.

Concerning both cases of the current study, the proposed hybrid approach and its ablation variants were estimated on training data, and the main comparisons were reported with regard to the out-of-sample data (test data) forecasts, whereas the results of benchmark models from earlier studies are reported as published. The forecasting outcomes were inspected on the basis of the RMSRE, MAPE and RMSE. In the rest of this section, every component of the proposed hybrid prediction algorithm is detailed.
B
details the opinion-leader module,
C
the stacked architecture and its dropout layers, and
D
proves all formal results.

3.2.1
Game-theoretical detection of opinion leaders in social networks

Detection of opinion leaders in social networking platforms is a complex undertaking. Nonetheless, we aim to identify the users who hold the most influential perspectives within the given social network, and
B
begins with a review of the criteria that earlier studies used to select them. The approach combines non-cooperative games between pairs of users, represented in normal or extensive form, with a cooperative game in which every coalition of users has a value
(
Osborne and Rubinstein, 1994
)
. Trust relations are represented by an extensive-form structure, namely a trustor–trustee tree generated on the basis of trust. Trust and centrality metrics serve as the primary means of characterizing a user’s attributes within a given network
(
Jain et al., 2020
)
. Our hypothesis posits that users can be likened to players within a network, wherein trust and other centrality measures serve as attributes that aid in determining the marginal contribution of each user in the game. The degree of trust was initially assessed by analyzing the user’s frequent interaction patterns. The nature of trust can vary based on the actions of the user, resulting in either unidirectional or bidirectional trust, which is why the trust of
i
towards
j
and that of
j
towards
i
are distinguished below. Having access to prior information or recommendations regarding an individual’s past experiences and performances can heighten the level of trust placed in said individual
(
Jain et al., 2020
)
. Trust is categorized into three modes, namely direct, indirect and recommendation trust, as illustrated in Fig.
4
. Direct trust (DT) refers to a situation where a user trusts another individual without any intervening factors or external influences. In contrast, indirect trust (IDT) refers to a scenario where a user places trust in another individual through the effects they experience as a result of the actions of a third party. Recommendation trust (RT) is a trust mechanism established through the endorsement of other individuals based on their personal experiences.

Influence probabilities.
The trustor–trustee tree is completed with conditional probabilities of influence. Let
\mathcal{A}_{i}
denote the event that user

p_{ij}=\Pr(\mathcal{A}_{j}\mid\mathcal{A}_{i})=\frac{\Pr(\mathcal{A}_{i}\mid\mathcal{A}_{j})\,\Pr(\mathcal{A}_{j})}{\Pr(\mathcal{A}_{i})},

Proposition 2

(Accuracy of the influence probabilities)

Suppose that, conditionally on

\Pr\big(|\hat{p}_{ij}-p_{ij}|\geq\varepsilon\mid m_{i}\big)\leq 2\exp(-2m_{i}\varepsilon^{2})

Coalitional game and Shapley value.
Upon computation of the trust metric, the subsequent objective is determining the Shapley value
(
Shapley, 1953
)
within a given coalition. Within a network, individuals form a coalition predicated upon their mutual interests, as well as factors such as dyadic and triadic closure, strong and weak ties, and other network-specific considerations
(
Granovetter, 1973
)
. The present study posits that every player either belongs to the coalition or is in close proximity to at least
\kappa
neighbors who belong to it. The primary basis for this assertion is that, in actuality, individuals tend to engage with others who exhibit similar conduct and are predisposed to form a collective with them. Accordingly, a user is reached by a coalition if she belongs to it or if at least
\kappa
of her trusted neighbors do, and the value of a coalition is the trust mass that it reaches. With unit trust scores this is the coverage game of
Michalak et al. (2013)
.

Definition 1

(Trust-weighted
\kappa
-coverage game)
.

For an integer threshold
\kappa\geq 1
, the characteristic function on coalitions
C\subseteq V_{T}
is

v(C)=\sum_{u\in V_{T}}\mathrm{TS}_{u}\,\mathds{1}\big\{u\in C\ \text{or}\ |N_{T}(u)\cap C|\geq\kappa\big\},\qquad v(\emptyset)=0,

(12)

and
\varphi_{i}(v)
denotes the Shapley value of user
i
in this game
(
Shapley, 1953
)
, i.e., the marginal contribution
v(C\cup\{i\})-v(C)
of
i
averaged over all
n_{T}!
orders in which the trusted users can join the coalition.

The procedure of
Jain et al. (2020)
evaluates this average by enumerating permutations, which is impossible for networks with thousands of users. The next theorem, which extends the unweighted coverage result of
Michalak et al. (2013)
to trust weights, removes the difficulty.

Theorem 1

(Closed-form Shapley value)
.

For every trusted user
i
,

\varphi_{i}(v)=\mathrm{TS}_{i}\min\Big\{1,\frac{\kappa}{1+d_{i}}\Big\}+\sum_{u\in N_{T}(i)}\mathrm{TS}_{u}\,\frac{\big(d_{u}-\kappa+1\big)^{+}}{d_{u}\,(1+d_{u})},

(13)

where
(x)^{+}=\max\{x,0\}
. All Shapley values can therefore be computed in
O(n_{T}+|E_{T}|)
time.

Corollary 1
.

(i)

Efficiency holds,
\sum_{i\in V_{T}}\varphi_{i}(v)=\sum_{u\in V_{T}}\mathrm{TS}_{u}
.

(ii)

For
\kappa=1
,
\varphi_{i}(v)=\sum_{u\in\{i\}\cup N_{T}(i)}\mathrm{TS}_{u}/(1+d_{u})
.

(iii)

If
\kappa
exceeds the maximum degree of
G_{T}
, then
v(C)=\sum_{u\in C}\mathrm{TS}_{u}
is additive,
\varphi_{i}(v)=\mathrm{TS}_{i}
for every
i
, and no coalition creates synergy. A meaningful game therefore requires users whose degree is at least
\kappa
.

Part (iii) of Corollary
1
shows that node degrees must reach the coverage threshold for the calculation to discriminate between users, because a coalition whose members have fewer than
\kappa
trusted neighbors each reaches no one outside itself. When the combined marginal contributions of all users within a coalition surpass the sum of marginal contributions of users in any other group, the users within that coalition are deemed to be the opinion leaders within the network. When several coalitions share the same synergy, the coalition whose members possess greater eigenvector centrality is preferred. Theorem
2
below turns this rule into a well-posed optimization problem.

Pairwise persuasion games.
The employed methodology is distinctive and can produce a wide range of outcomes depending on the parameters of each platform. In practical scenarios, it is not uncommon for certain participants in a game to lack sufficient expertise, resulting in an inaccurate reading of the game. In such instances, it is common for individuals to experience betrayal from their associates and fellow professionals. Novice players may base their decisions on the shrewd maneuvers executed by their counterparts. Under certain circumstances, it is possible that one player’s strategy may have an impact on the other player’s decisions to a certain degree, leading them to adjust their actions accordingly. Therefore, taking into account the aforementioned facts, four options are evaluated and elaborated upon in

i<j\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{\Omega_{ij}}{|C|}\right)^{c},\qquad\delta\in(0,1],\ c\in(0,1].

(16)

The exponent
c
controls how strongly size is penalized. The following result shows which members raise the synergy of a coalition, and why the definition favors cohesive groups over large but loosely connected ones.

Proposition 4

(Admission rule)
.

For a coalition
C\subseteq V_{T}
and a user
k\notin C
, let
S(C)=\sum_{i<j\in C}\Omega_{ij}^{c}
and
S(k,C)=\sum_{j\in C}\Omega_{kj}^{c}
, so that
\Phi(C)=\delta S(C)/|C|^{c}
, and let
s=|C|
. Then
\Phi(C\cup\{k\})>\Phi(C)
if and only if
S(k,C)>\big[(1+1/s)^{c}-1\big]S(C)
. In particular,
S(k,C)>cS(C)/s
is sufficient for admission and
S(k,C)\leq cS(C)/(s+1)
is sufficient for rejection.

A newcomer is therefore admitted only if the synergy it creates with the current members exceeds roughly
c
times the average synergy per member. A user who cooperates with every member of a clique and has with each of them the synergy that the members have with one another is therefore always admitted, whereas a user attached to a single member is admitted only if that one link is strong. The coalition of opinion leaders is defined as a maximizer of
\Phi
, and the next theorem shows that this maximizer can be computed exactly without enumerating the
2^{n_{T}}-1
coalitions.

Theorem 2

(Exact coalition selection)
.

Let
S(C)
be as in Proposition
4
, and let
q^{\ast}
be the maximum of
S(C)/|C|^{c}
over the nonempty coalitions
C\subseteq V_{T}
, so that the maximal synergy is
\delta q^{\ast}
. The following statements hold.

(i)

For every
q\geq 0
, the function
F_{q}(C)=S(C)-q|C|^{c}
is supermodular on the subsets of
V_{T}
.

(ii)

The Dinkelbach iteration, which starts from
C^{(0)}=V_{T}
and sets
C^{(\ell+1)}\in\arg\max_{C}F_{q^{(\ell)}}(C)
and
q^{(\ell+1)}=S(C^{(\ell+1)})/|C^{(\ell+1)}|^{c}
, strictly decreases
|C^{(\ell)}|
for
\ell\geq 1
until
\max_{C}F_{q^{(\ell)}}(C)=0
, at which point
C^{(\ell)}
is optimal. It therefore needs at most
n_{T}+1
maximizations, each of which is a submodular minimization, so the whole procedure runs in polynomial time, and for
c=1
each maximization reduces to a minimum
s
–
t
cut
(
Goldberg, 1984
)
.

(iii)

The optimal coalitions, together with the empty set, are closed under union and intersection. Hence there is a unique inclusion-maximal optimal coalition
C^{\ast}
, and because
\mathrm{EC}_{i}\geq 0
it has the largest total eigenvector centrality among the optimal coalitions, strictly so if all
\mathrm{EC}_{i}>0
, as in a connected
G_{T}
.

Part (iii) reconciles the tie-breaking rule of Fig.
5
with the optimization view, because the union of tied coalitions is itself optimal and has at least their total eigenvector centrality. Algorithm
B.2
in
B
implements part (ii), which applies the parametric method of
Dinkelbach (1967)
to the ratio
S(C)/|C|^{c}
. In the empirical application the coalitions are ranked by
\Phi
, and
C^{\ast}
yields the roster of opinion leaders. The normalization by
|C|^{c}
is what makes exact selection tractable. If the number of leaders were fixed in advance at
s
, maximizing
S(C)
over coalitions of
s
users would include, for synergies equal to
0
or
1
, the problem of deciding whether the agreement graph contains a clique of
s
users, which is NP-complete, so no polynomial algorithm can be expected for that variant unless
\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}
. Proposition
B.4
in
B
adds that the selected coalition does not change under small estimation errors in the influence probabilities and the synergies when no linked pair lies close to the agreement threshold of Eq. (
15
) and the optimality margin of the coalition is large enough.

Opinion-leader weighting.
Once
C^{\ast}
is known, every user
u
receives the weight

w_{u}=1+\omega_{\mathrm{OL}}\,\xi^{\,\mathrm{dist}_{G}(u,C^{\ast})},\qquad\omega_{\mathrm{OL}}>0,\ \xi\in(0,1),

(17)

where
\mathrm{dist}_{G}(u,C^{\ast})
is the shortest-path distance from
u
to the coalition, equal to
0
for leaders and to
\infty
(so that
w_{u}=1
) for users not connected to it. For each platform and period
t
, let
\mathcal{E}_{t}
be the set of posts, comments, likes, shares and reviews,
u(e)
the user behind item
e
and
\chi_{e}\in[-1,1]
its sentiment polarity. The weighted volume and valence features of the platform are

V^{w}_{t}=\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{t}}w_{u(e)},\qquad\bar{\chi}^{w}_{t}=\frac{\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{t}}w_{u(e)}\,\chi_{e}}{\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{t}}w_{u(e)}},

(18)

and the other features of Table

are weighted in the same way. Because
1\leq w_{u}\leq 1+\omega_{\mathrm{OL}}
, the weighted valence remains a convex combination of item polarities in
[-1,1]
. Leaders thus tilt the indicators toward their own polarity, positive or negative, without changing their scale, which is the “higher positive or negative weight” of the framework, and the weight decays geometrically with the distance from the leaders.

3.2.2
Causal empirical wavelet transform

The EWT of
Gilles (2013)
decomposes a series
s(t)
into
M
modes with an adaptive filter bank. The Fourier spectrum on
[0,\pi]
is segmented at boundaries
0=\Delta_{0}<\Delta_{1}<\dots<\Delta_{M-1}<\Delta_{M}=\pi
placed midway between the
M
largest local maxima of the spectrum magnitude, and a low-pass filter
\hat{\psi}_{1}
and band-pass filters
\hat{\psi}_{2},\dots,\hat{\psi}_{M}
with smooth transitions of relative width
\gamma
are built on these bands, as recalled in the proof of Theorem
3
(
D
). The modes are
s_{m}=s\ast\psi_{m}\ast\psi_{m}
, that is,
\hat{s}_{m}=\hat{s}\,|\hat{\psi}_{m}|^{2}
. The example in Fig.
3
(a) has six modes. Two properties matter for forecasting.

Theorem 3

(Tight frame and exact additivity)
.

If
\gamma<\gamma^{\ast}=\min_{1\leq m\leq M-1}(\Delta_{m+1}-\Delta_{m})/(\Delta_{m+1}+\Delta_{m})
, then
(i)

\sum_{m=1}^{M}|\hat{\psi}_{m}(\omega)|^{2}=1
for all
\omega\in[-\pi,\pi]
,
(ii)

\|s\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\|s\ast\psi_{m}\|_{2}^{2}
for every finite-energy series
s
, and
(iii)

\sum_{m=1}^{M}s_{m}=s
. The same identities hold for the discrete Fourier transform of a finite window.

Part (iii) guarantees that the modes passed to the network lose none of the information in the series. Without the bound on
\gamma
, three filters can overlap and the identities fail, and filtering the spectrum only once,
\hat{s}_{m}=\hat{s}\,\hat{\psi}_{m}
, would not suffice either, because the modes would then fail to add up to the series in the transition bands. The second property is a warning rather than a guarantee, and Section
3.2.5
shows how the framework accounts for it.

3.2.3
Stacked Bi-directional Long Short-Term Memory model

The proposed model employs a stacked version of BiLSTM and optimizes it using the Bayesian approach to fine-tune hyperparameters. The model comprises stacked LSTM layers, drop-out layer architecture, BiLSTM architecture and hyperparameter tuning through BO. A detailed exposition of the stacked LSTM and drop-out architecture is provided in
C
(Fig.
1
), together with the number of trainable weights of each layer and the comparison with LSTM layers without dropout. The architecture of the Stacked BiLSTM and its hyperparameter tuning via BO are delineated as follows.

Figure 6:
Architecture of the proposed Stacked BiLSTM. Each block holds a forward and a backward LSTM cell that read the same input, both directions run inside the input window, which ends at the forecast origin
t
, and the readout joins the last forward state with the last backward state.

The LSTM model encompasses multiple parameters that impact distinct facets of the model’s architecture. Processing the data in both temporal directions captures supplementary features
(
Schuster and Paliwal, 1997
;
Siami-Namini et al., 2019
)
. Fig.
6
illustrates the optimized Stacked BiLSTM architecture built on this bidirectional processing. At the forecast origin
t
, the network reads the window
\bm{x}_{t-L+1},\dots,\bm{x}_{t}
, where each vector
\bm{x}_{\tau}
stacks the scaled arrivals, their EWT modes and the weighted Internet indicators with their modes. An LSTM cell
(
Gers et al., 2000
;
Hochreiter and Schmidhuber, 1997
)
updates its hidden state
\bm{h}_{\tau}
and memory cell
\bm{c}_{\tau}
through input, forget and output gates, which we write compactly as
(\bm{h}_{\tau},\bm{c}_{\tau})=\mathcal{U}(\bm{x}_{\tau},\bm{h}_{\tau-1},\bm{c}_{\tau-1})
. The combination of bidirectional Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) and LSTM yields the BiLSTM model, which enables access to the training data in both forward and backward directions
(
Schuster and Paliwal, 1997
;
Graves et al., 2013
)
. A BiLSTM layer runs one LSTM forward and another, with its own weights, backward over the window,

\begin{gathered}\overrightarrow{\bm{h}}_{\tau}=\overrightarrow{\mathcal{U}}\big(\bm{x}_{\tau},\overrightarrow{\bm{h}}_{\tau-1},\overrightarrow{\bm{c}}_{\tau-1}\big),\qquad\overleftarrow{\bm{h}}_{\tau}=\overleftarrow{\mathcal{U}}\big(\bm{x}_{\tau},\overleftarrow{\bm{h}}_{\tau+1},\overleftarrow{\bm{c}}_{\tau+1}\big),\\
\bm{y}_{\tau}=\big[\overrightarrow{\bm{h}}_{\tau};\overleftarrow{\bm{h}}_{\tau}\big],\end{gathered}

(19)

for
\tau=t-L+1,\dots,t
, with zero initial states at both ends of the window. The forward pass therefore uses
\bm{h}_{\tau-1}
and the backward pass uses
\bm{h}_{\tau+1}
, and the output
\bm{y}_{\tau}
of the layer at step
\tau
concatenates the two hidden states. In addition, deep architectures were employed through the stacking of BiLSTM layers, where the output sequence of one layer was utilized as the input sequence for the subsequent layer
(
Graves et al., 2013
)
. The computation of hidden sequences in a stack with
N_{l}
layers is performed in a sequential manner, starting from
\ell=1
to
N_{l}
and
\tau=t-L+1
to
t
,

\overrightarrow{\bm{h}}^{(\ell)}_{\tau}=\overrightarrow{\mathcal{U}}^{(\ell)}\big(\bm{y}^{(\ell-1)}_{\tau},\overrightarrow{\bm{h}}^{(\ell)}_{\tau-1},\overrightarrow{\bm{c}}^{(\ell)}_{\tau-1}\big),\qquad\overleftarrow{\bm{h}}^{(\ell)}_{\tau}=\overleftarrow{\mathcal{U}}^{(\ell)}\big(\bm{y}^{(\ell-1)}_{\tau},\overleftarrow{\bm{h}}^{(\ell)}_{\tau+1},\overleftarrow{\bm{c}}^{(\ell)}_{\tau+1}\big),

(20)

with
\bm{y}^{(0)}_{\tau}=\bm{x}_{\tau}
and
\bm{y}^{(\ell)}_{\tau}=[\overrightarrow{\bm{h}}^{(\ell)}_{\tau};\overleftarrow{\bm{h}}^{(\ell)}_{\tau}]
, so that each hidden layer receives input from both the forward and backward layers at the lower level. Dropout with rate
N_{d}
is applied to the output of every layer (
C
), and the network outputs are provided as follows,

\hat{y}_{t+h\mid t}=\bm{w}_{y}^{\top}\bar{\bm{h}}_{t}+b_{y},\qquad\bar{\bm{h}}_{t}=\big[\overrightarrow{\bm{h}}^{(N_{l})}_{t};\overleftarrow{\bm{h}}^{(N_{l})}_{t-L+1}\big],

(21)

where
\bar{\bm{h}}_{t}
joins the last state of the forward pass and the last state of the backward pass of the top layer, and the fully connected layer and the regression layer of Fig.
6
are written as one affine map for brevity. The backward state at position
t
has seen only
\bm{x}_{t}
, which is why the readout takes the backward state at the start of the window, the only one that summarizes the whole window. Both directions operate inside the window that ends at the origin
t
, so bidirectional processing never reaches beyond the information available when the forecast is made.

3.2.4
Stacked BiLSTM hyperparameter tuning via BO

Predicted outcomes in DL models are profoundly influenced by the choice of model hyperparameters, a determination that can be both labor-intensive and time-intensive when done by hand. Exhaustive grid search is susceptible to the curse of dimensionality, becoming markedly less efficient as the range and number of hyperparameters grow. Random search was introduced to alleviate some of grid search’s limitations, but it can still be inadequate for more intricate model architectures. As a result, many view hyperparameter tuning as a computational optimization problem, prompting the adoption of BO
(
Snoek et al., 2012
)
to streamline this process. BO constructs a prior over the objective or fitness function, here the validation regression loss, and incrementally refines that prior by incorporating insights from previously sampled hyperparameter sets. The underlying mechanism relies on a Gaussian process to model the fitness function, while an acquisition function directs the selection of the next hyperparameter point. In this study, the “expected-improvement-plus” criterion serves as the acquisition function
a_{\mathrm{EI}}(\bm{z})
, guiding the identification of promising hyperparameter configurations
\bm{z}
. Table
1
outlines the hyperparameters investigated, and Fig.
7
illustrates the flow of the BO procedure. This methodological choice reduces the protracted search times seen in random search, ensuring a more targeted exploration of hyperparameter space and leading to superior forecasting performance
(
Snoek et al., 2012
;
Jones et al., 1998
)
.

In this setting, the hyperparameter search space is denoted as

\mathcal{L}\colon\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}_{+},\qquad\bm{z}=(N_{u},N_{l},N_{lr},N_{L2},N_{d},N_{lm})\mapsto\mathcal{L}(\bm{z}),

(22)

that is, the root mean squared error on a validation block formed by the last observations of the training sample
(
Bergmeir and Benítez, 2012

, of the network trained with hyperparameters
\bm{z}
. To determine the optimal configuration of model hyperparameters, BO seeks

\bm{z}^{\ast}\in\arg\min_{\bm{z}\in\mathcal{H}}\mathcal{L}(\bm{z}).

(23)

In BO, two key elements underpin the minimization process, namely the Gaussian process model
\mathcal{L}(\bm{z})\sim\mathcal{GP}\big(\bar{\mathcal{L}},k(\bm{z},\bm{z}^{\prime})\big)
, with constant prior mean
\bar{\mathcal{L}}
and kernel
k
, and a Bayesian update procedure that incrementally refines this model after each new evaluation of
\mathcal{L}
. With posterior mean
\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{z})
, posterior standard deviation
\varsigma(\bm{z})
and best observed loss
\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{best}}
, the acquisition function is the expected improvement
a_{\mathrm{EI}}(\bm{z})=\mathbb{E}\big[\max\{\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{best}}-\mathcal{L}(\bm{z}),0\}\big]
, which has a closed form in
\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{z})
and
\varsigma(\bm{z})

(
Jones et al., 1998
)
. The “plus” variant modifies the kernel whenever the chosen point is over-exploited, i.e., when
\varsigma(\bm{z})
falls below a fixed fraction of the noise standard deviation, which keeps the search from stalling.

3.2.5
Leakage-free evaluation protocol and implementation

Decomposition-based forecasts are easy to evaluate incorrectly. The next lemma shows why a full-sample EWT cannot be used to produce test forecasts.

Lemma 1

(EWT modes are two-sided)
.

Let
M\geq 2
and
\gamma<\gamma^{\ast}
as in Theorem
3
, and let
g_{m}
be the impulse response with
\hat{g}_{m}=|\hat{\psi}_{m}|^{2}
, so that
s_{m}(t)=\sum_{\tau\in\mathbb{Z}}g_{m}(\tau)s(t-\tau)
. Then
g_{m}
is real and even, and
g_{m}(\tau)\neq 0
for some
\tau>0
. Hence
s_{m}(t)
depends on future values
s(t+\tau)
with weight
g_{m}(-\tau)=g_{m}(\tau)\neq 0
.

If the EWT were applied once to the whole series, the modes at a test month would contain information from later months, and the reported accuracy would be optimistic, a pitfall documented for other decomposition hybrids
(
Zhang et al., 2015
)
. We therefore use a walk-forward (rolling-origin) protocol
(
Tashman, 2000
)
, illustrated in Fig.
3
(b). At each origin
t
in the test window, (a) the scaling uses the training statistics of Eq. (
1
), (b) interpolation onto the higher-frequency grid uses observations up to
t
, (c) the EWT is applied to the segment ending at
t
, with boundaries detected once on the training sample so that the meaning of each mode stays fixed even though re-decomposition changes the modes as new observations arrive
(
Wang and Wu, 2016
)
, (d) the network reads the last
L
values of every mode and indicator, and (e) the network weights, the hyperparameters
\bm{z}^{\ast}
and the coalition
C^{\ast}
are estimated on the training sample only. Let
\mathcal{I}_{t}
be the information generated by arrivals and Internet data up to month
t
.

Proposition 5

(No look-ahead)
.

Under steps
(a)–(e)
, every forecast
\hat{y}_{t+h\mid t}
in the test window is a measurable function of
\mathcal{I}_{t}
. In particular, the test target
y_{t+h}
does not enter the inputs, the scaling, the decomposition or the model selection that produce
\hat{y}_{t+h\mid t}
.

Proposition
5
is the formal counterpart of the statement that only out-of-sample data are used for reporting. Forecasts produced on the higher-frequency grid are de-normalized with Eq. (
2
) and aggregated to months before the error measures of Eqs. (
3
)–(
5
) are computed (Fig.
8
). All the execution steps of the hybrid algorithm are coded in MATLAB R2021b, and the text preprocessing uses the Python NLTK library.

5
Discussion and implications

This study utilized various tourism-related Internet big data sources to develop a hybrid destination tourism demand forecasting approach. The approach was applied to two cases to predict the number of tourist arrivals. The first case concerns Hong Kong from five key source countries before COVID-19, and the second is Sanya, over a period that includes the pandemic. The present study utilizes internet big data sourced from prominent platforms such as Baidu, Google, Twitter, Facebook and TripAdvisor. The TripAdvisor platform incorporated evaluations of prominent tourist destinations, diverse lodging categories and significant commercial markets for tourism in Hong Kong. In the context of Facebook, Twitter and TripAdvisor, the analysis of data encompasses both quantitative measures of volume and qualitative assessments of valence. Furthermore, before integrating the Internet’s vast data into our hybrid forecasting methodology, we employ a game-theory-based algorithm for detecting opinion leaders from diverse social media data. This algorithm identifies a cohort of users with the highest degree of synergy, which is referred to as the coalition of opinion leaders. We offer a robust Stacked BiLSTM model to perform the final forecasting, which captures long and short memory in the data. The results suggest that (a) tourism demand forecasting based upon multiple Internet big data sources can improve forecasting accuracy and precision by a large margin, (b) the utilized combination makes it possible for the developed method to be sufficiently dynamic to treat the VUCA of the data, and (c) the proposed hybrid approach outperformed the benchmark models considered when multiple-frequency data from various Internet big data sources, along with a game-theoretical opinion leader detection method and the hybrid memory-based algorithm, were incorporated into the forecasting exercise. In what follows, significant theoretical and managerial insights and implications are given.

5.1
Theoretical contributions

\bullet

Our research extends multisource forecasting
(
Li et al., 2020
)
by combining diverse Internet big data sources, including electronic word-of-mouth, to forecast tourism demand. Previous studies primarily focused on singular or dual data sources for destination-level demand prediction. Relying exclusively on attention-based or Internet traffic data, such as search queries and web traffic, is inadequate as it may fail to account for unfavorable conditions despite increasing tourist attention. Augmenting imperfect data with relevant information is essential for improving forecasting precision, and Table
3
shows that valence features are the more informative of the two social media families.

\bullet

This paper captures the seasonal, cyclical, trend and other dynamics of tourism demand through the memory-based algorithm (robust Stacked BiLSTM model) and the EWT time-frequency algorithm.

\bullet

This research extends mixed-frequency modeling in tourism demand forecasting
(
Bangwayo-Skeete and Skeete, 2015
;
Hu et al., 2022
)
. The utilization of the mixed-frequency approach is a proficient approach to circumvent the loss of information that may occur when higher-frequency data are aggregated
(
Bangwayo-Skeete and Skeete, 2015
)
. The findings of this research demonstrate that combining interpolation-based mixed-frequency inputs with the EWT and a stacked BiLSTM produced more precise predictions than the published results of alternative techniques such as MIDAS
(
Hu et al., 2022
)
, or models that incorporate aggregated online review data as explanatory factors and benchmark forecasting algorithms. Hence, the present study makes a valuable contribution to the advancement of model development in the domain of tourism demand forecasting.

\bullet

Building on the strong predictive capacity of our Stacked BiLSTM model, there is significant scope for incorporating additional predictor variables and features into the suggested algorithm to further refine tourism demand forecasts. The model’s architecture affords considerable flexibility, enabling analysts to expand its input dimensions, with the BO stage adjusting the network to the new inputs.

\bullet

Furthermore, our framework supports short-term prediction strategies, one to three months ahead, while retaining the potential for mid- and long-term forecasting improvements, for example through network architectures designed for long-range dependencies, such as the dilated causal convolutions of
van den Oord et al. (2016)
.

\bullet

According to the results, our suggested algorithm is robust across markets with very different levels and dynamics and through the COVID-19 shock.

\bullet

The BiLSTM architecture exhibits a high degree of flexibility and robustness when applied to various data sets, and BO is capable of estimating the necessary parameter set for each of them. The superior performance of the proposed design can be attributed to a search space that includes the depth of the stack and the dropout rate, so that BO can select deep, regularized architectures when the data support them. All of the proposed architectures feature base LSTM layers that retain prior information and account for temporal dependencies.

\bullet

The study used game theory to identify opinion leaders from Internet big data, which brings social influence into the forecasting model in a principled way. A trustor–trustee tree was created to represent trust dynamics among users. Four alternative methods for acquiring payoffs, based on different parameters and conditions, were proposed in
B
, and their equilibria were characterized (Propositions
3
,
B.1
and
B.2
). In extensive games with imperfect information, individual users may not know other players’ strategies. Similarly, in social networks, users may lack awareness of each other and rely on shared experiences and recommendations to build trust, and the persuasion dynamics of Proposition
B.3
show how two such users converge to a common opinion that lies between their initial ones.

\bullet

The concept of Shapley value (Definition
1
) was employed in our study to detect opinion leaders in Internet big data. This approach allowed us to determine the marginal contribution of a user within a coalition. The Shapley value is utilized to ascertain the requisite expectations for establishing synergy. Theorems
1
and
2
show that both the Shapley values and the coalition of maximal synergy can be computed exactly at scale, which removes the main computational objection to game-theoretic opinion leader detection on large platforms, and Proposition
B.4
shows that the selected coalition is robust to small estimation errors when its optimality margin is large enough.

\bullet

The opinion leader detection approach has the advantage of improving its reliability as the volume of observed interactions increases. Proposition
2
shows that the estimation error of the influence probabilities shrinks at the rate
1/\sqrt{m}
in the number
m
of observed endorsements, so busier networks yield more accurate trust and influence estimates and, in turn, a more reliable coalition.

5.2
Managerial insights

\bullet

From a managerial perspective, accurate tourism demand forecasting is critical for businesses in the travel and tourism industry. It enables businesses to optimize their operations, make informed decisions and improve their profitability. By using Internet big data, time-frequency analysis and a hybrid memory-aware prediction approach, businesses can develop more accurate and reliable forecasting models, which can help them stay ahead of the competition and adapt to changing market conditions.

\bullet

Additionally, since we combined various Internet big data via the data mining procedure shown in Fig.
2
, we can have better forecasting results and demonstrate Internet big data’s true power and value in the tourism industry.

\bullet

By incorporating opinion leader detection into the analysis of Internet big data, this study avoids a blanket examination of all social media posts. Rather, we employ a game-theoretic approach to identify highly influential communities and opinion leaders, assigning them proportional weights to reflect their importance. This method not only yields more targeted and effective social media insights for the forecasting process, but also aligns with the Pareto principle (80–20 rule), wherein the majority of influential outcomes can be traced back to a relatively small subset of individuals and discussions. Destination marketers can monitor the roster of leaders directly, because a shift in their sentiment moves the weighted valence indicators before it shows up in arrivals.

\bullet

Accurate and timely forecasting results should be utilized by policymakers in destinations like Hong Kong and Sanya for the purpose of planning, for example to schedule staff, transport capacity and crowd management one to three months ahead.

\bullet

Our new method allows policymakers to identify factors influencing variations in projected tourist arrivals. By employing an algorithm to detect opinion leaders among tourists on social media platforms, decision-makers can pinpoint the sectors with the most significant impact on destination demand fluctuations, for example by comparing the weighted review indicators of shopping, attractions and hotels. The algorithm identifies a coalition of opinion leaders, representing users with the highest level of synergy. The use of multisource Internet big data further enhances the advantages of this approach.

6
Conclusions and future work

This study demonstrated that harnessing multisource Internet big data can substantially enhance the precision of tourism demand forecasts. By incorporating data from multiple online channels, we achieved high accuracy in predicting monthly tourist arrivals for Hong Kong (from five key source countries) and Sanya, with lower errors than those reported for several advanced forecasting methods in the literature. Central to our approach is a game-theory-based algorithm, which identifies highly influential user groups, referred to as the coalition of opinion leaders, within social media platforms. By detecting these opinion leaders, we gain deeper insight into real-time tourist sentiments and preferences, thereby refining the predictive power of our model. Relative to the same network fed only with past arrivals, the full framework reduces the one-step-ahead RMSE by 18.4–67.2% across the Hong Kong markets and by 13.5% for Sanya, and opinion leader detection alone accounts for reductions of 10.1–49.4% and 8.7%, respectively.

Our framework also features a robust Stacked BiLSTM model, which is particularly effective in handling non-stationary time-series data with complex seasonal, cyclical and trend components. The adoption of a memory-based algorithm, in tandem with the EWT time-frequency method, helps the proposed forecasting system cope with high levels of VUCA. Experiments show that this hybrid algorithm remains robust in various scenarios, including pre-COVID-19 conditions and the pandemic shock, and can efficiently manage diverse data sets laden with noise and sudden shifts in tourist behavior. On the theoretical side, we showed that the Shapley values of the trust-weighted coverage game have a closed form, that the coalition of maximal synergy is computable exactly in polynomial time and, given a sufficient margin, robust to small estimation errors, that the pairwise persuasion games generically have unique equilibria that decide which users cooperate, and that a causal application of the EWT is necessary for out-of-sample evaluation. As far as current knowledge permits, the algorithmic constituents in this study have not been employed together in prior tourism demand research. This work on multisource Internet big data analytics allows destination authorities, management businesses and stakeholders to obtain timely and accurate monthly forecasts, while also facilitating crowd management and long-term strategic planning. Moreover, the insights gleaned from social media enable the continuous tracking of tourists’ interests, thereby further refining policymaking and enhancing the competitiveness of destinations.

Looking to the future, several directions can push this line of research forward. First, researchers may explore alternative optimization approaches for tuning hyperparameters to potentially improve performance and reduce computational overhead. Additionally, expanding data collection to multilingual social media platforms could broaden the model’s applicability and augment the generalizability of findings across diverse tourism markets. Another promising avenue involves segmenting online communities by employing techniques such as dynamic topic modeling, testing the resulting network associations with the quadratic assignment procedure (QAP), followed by targeted application of the proposed framework to each identified subgroup. Re-estimating the benchmark models on identical test windows, adding formal tests of predictive accuracy and analyzing the sensitivity of the results to the parameters of the opinion-leader module would further strengthen the evidence. Finally, adopting influential factor analysis methods, such as SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP)
(
Lundberg and Lee, 2017
)
and partial dependence plots (PDP), may offer deeper interpretations of the forecasting outputs by clarifying the contributions of various predictors, as Shapley-based explanations have recently done for deep demand forecasts
(
Shajalal et al., 2024
)
and for news-based forecasts of economic cycles
(
Sun et al., 2025
)
. Such explanations would also connect naturally with the Shapley values that already drive the selection of opinion leaders. Because none of its components is specific to tourism, the framework can also be tested on demand planning in logistics, supply chains and production.

It is anticipated that these advancements will further underscore the utility of Internet big data within TM and beyond. As innovative uses of large-scale online data continue to emerge, this work can inspire researchers to integrate both structured and unstructured data from diverse Internet sources, ultimately refining models of demand forecasting and offering actionable, data-driven insights for better tourism planning and policy formulation.

Criteria used to select opinion leaders

The identification of an opinion leader can be achieved through both qualitative and quantitative methods, which take into account the individual’s area of expertise and the specific demand for their opinions. The criteria for selecting opinion leaders may differ across various approaches. Numerous scholars have conducted analyses and investigations on diverse methodologies utilizing a range of parameters to categorize opinion leaders. Their study presents a compilation of significant papers pertaining to the subject matter, highlighting their respective advantages and drawbacks. Diverse scholars employed a range of parameters and centrality measures to select the opinion leaders’ roster. In the realm of opinion leadership, the process of selecting individuals to serve as influential figures is typically categorized into two distinct groups. These groups are differentiated by the inherent characteristics of the network in question and the behavior exhibited by users within said network. The initial classification is contingent upon the network’s configuration, size, compactness and nodal degree. Conversely, the second approach is predicated upon the actions exhibited by the user, including but not limited to retweets, response time, trustworthiness and text mining. In addition, it is worth noting that various scholars hold distinct perspectives and employ diverse approaches in identifying opinion leaders within a network. According to
Walter and Brüggemann (2020)
, diverse circumstances and distinct opportunities in human existence contribute to the emergence of opinion leaders. The proposition posits that if a user consistently obtains primary information from reputable sources, there is no necessity for them to depend on alternative social media platforms. The information that has been collected is adequate to establish them as opinion leaders.
Wang et al. (2020)
established the functions and investigated the impacts of opinion leaders on the dissemination of popular mobile games. The researchers conducted an experiment to determine the impact of opinion leaders on the diffusion rate of game-related topics. The results indicated that when an opinion leader promotes such topics, the diffusion rate is significantly higher than when promoted through user-generated content, which supports giving the content of opinion leaders a larger weight. In summary, each theory has presented a distinct approach to identifying opinion leaders, and each captures a different facet of opinion leadership.

Solution 1.
In this scenario, the two players engage in persuasive communication with the aim of convincing the other to adopt their proposed plan or adjust their strategy in alignment with the other player’s plan. The acceptance of a player’s strategy by another player is not a straightforward matter. As a result, a fixed payoff is linked to each choice. Let
g(\mathsf{o})=\pi_{\mathsf{o}}
and
g(\mathsf{a})=-\pi_{\mathsf{a}}
, so that a player gains
\pi_{\mathsf{o}}
when it maintains its original plan and loses
\pi_{\mathsf{a}}
when it alters it, and the choice of the opponent affects the player in the opposite way. The payoffs are
M_{A}(s_{A},s_{B})=g(s_{A})-g(s_{B})
and
M_{B}=-M_{A}
, so that

M_{A}=\begin{bmatrix}0&\pi_{\mathsf{o}}+\pi_{\mathsf{a}}\\
-\pi_{\mathsf{a}}-\pi_{\mathsf{o}}&0\end{bmatrix},\qquad M_{B}=\begin{bmatrix}0&-\pi_{\mathsf{o}}-\pi_{\mathsf{a}}\\
\pi_{\mathsf{a}}+\pi_{\mathsf{o}}&0\end{bmatrix}.

(25)

The aforementioned scenario demonstrates that in the event that player A maintains its strategy, player B is presented with two alternatives, namely to modify its plan or adhere to it. In both scenarios, the resulting payoff would be
-\pi_{\mathsf{o}}-\pi_{\mathsf{a}}
and 0, correspondingly. Opting for 0 is the optimal choice as opposed to selecting
-\pi_{\mathsf{o}}-\pi_{\mathsf{a}}
, so B does not alter its plan either. Based on the preceding discourse, it can be inferred that neither player would receive a payoff, resulting in a failure to reach a mutually beneficial agreement. Consequently, an alternative resolution has been presented whereby both parties reach a mutually acceptable compromise.

Solution 2.
The proposed solution involves the introduction of an intermediate strategy known as “agreement”, wherein both players agree to a compromise without completely altering their existing strategies. Moving to the agreement costs a player the concession
\pi_{\mathsf{g}}
, i.e.,
g(\mathsf{g})=-\pi_{\mathsf{g}}

M_{A}(s_{A},s_{B})=g(s_{A})-g(s_{B})
and
M_{B}=-M_{A}

M_{A}=\begin{bmatrix}0&\pi_{\mathsf{o}}+\pi_{\mathsf{a}}&\pi_{\mathsf{o}}+\pi_{\mathsf{g}}\\

(26)

In this instance, it was observed that in the event that a player maintains its initial plan, the opposing player has three options available to them. These options include altering their own plan, maintaining their original strategy or reaching a mutually agreed upon compromise. Across all three alternatives, the outcome for the opposing player would be
-\pi_{\mathsf{o}}-\pi_{\mathsf{a}}
, 0 and

(Deadlock in Solutions 1 and 2)

(\mathsf{o},\mathsf{o})

It was demonstrated that the optimal course of action for both players is to maintain their current strategy, resulting in no payoff. Therefore, it can be inferred that neither player in this scenario is able to reach a satisfactory outcome, indicating a need for an improved strategy to achieve the maximum possible reward. The present approach incorporates a novel parameter denoted as “distance”, represented by
d
, that denotes the average centrality among the users. Centrality is a significant factor in determining the significance of a node within a social network. The degree of centrality exhibited by a user is contingent upon the underlying dynamics and structure of the network. The temporal evolution of network dynamics results in fluctuations in the centrality of individual users. The degree to which a user is central within a network significantly impacts their accessibility and potential to influence the behavior and knowledge of other users.

The present study employs the Closeness Centrality (CC), Betweenness Centrality (BC), Degree Centrality (DC)
(

and the local clustering coefficient

as metrics to establish the distance

(27)

where

is a small constant that keeps

and

. The coefficient

weights the larger and

and the outcome of a game does not depend on which user is labeled

. It was also observed that a gradual increase in the clustering coefficient of a user results in a corresponding increase in the distance between users. Each player that moves to the agreement now adds the closeness bonus

to the payoffs of both players, where

for the two users

Synergy calculation

The concept of synergy in a network refers to the collaborative capacity of specific nodes, which, when combined, generate more significant value than the sum of their individual contributions. The coalition game involves players who possess individual resources that hold significant value. Upon forming an alliance, the players may generate a collective synergy that surpasses or falls short of the total value of their resources at a given time. Consider a hypothetical scenario in a game where the individual payoffs of two players, denoted as

Stacked LSTM and dropout architecture

Stacked LSTM and dropout architecture are explained in this appendix, which also counts the trainable weights of the network.

Stacked LSTM

Customized neural networks with greater depth are frequently employed to address complex prediction tasks and to enhance accuracy. Employing a stacked LSTM architecture makes it possible to depict increasingly intricate input patterns at each layer
(
Graves et al., 2013
)
. The proposed approach adheres to a fundamental stacked LSTM architecture, as depicted in Fig.

The present investigation involves the utilization of a stacked LSTM model, wherein multiple LSTM layers are employed in conjunction with dropout layers and an output regression layer at the last output. The output sequences generated by the lower LSTM layers are utilized as inputs for the subsequent LSTM layer. The stacked LSTM model can enhance the non-linear transformation of raw data and expedite convergence because its parameters are distributed over several layers. Consequently, the present investigation incorporates a stacked LSTM architecture within our proposed model (stacked BiLSTM).

Dropout architecture

The regularization technique known as dropout reduces overfitting in feedforward and recurrent networks alike

;

In these experiments, dropout LSTMs performed better than conventional LSTMs, and the dropout architecture has therefore been integrated into this study.

|\tilde{\Phi}(C)-\Phi(C)|\leq\delta|C|^{-c}\,e(C)\,\varepsilon_{\Omega}^{c}
, where
e(C)
is the number of edges of the agreement graph with both ends in
C
. Every user has at most
\Delta^{\mathrm{ag}}
such edges, so
2e(C)\leq\Delta^{\mathrm{ag}}|C|
, and the bound follows. (iii) Since
|C|\leq n_{T}
and
1-c\geq 0
, part (ii) gives
|\tilde{\Phi}(C)-\Phi(C)|\leq\varepsilon_{\Phi}
for every nonempty
C
. For a maximizer
\tilde{C}
of
\tilde{\Phi}
,
\Phi(\tilde{C})\geq\tilde{\Phi}(\tilde{C})-\varepsilon_{\Phi}\geq\tilde{\Phi}(C^{\ast})-\varepsilon_{\Phi}\geq\Phi(C^{\ast})-2\varepsilon_{\Phi}
. Under the margin condition,
\tilde{\Phi}(C)\leq\Phi(C)+\varepsilon_{\Phi}<\Phi(C^{\ast})-\varepsilon_{\Phi}\leq\tilde{\Phi}(C^{\ast})
for every nonempty
C\neq C^{\ast}
, so
C^{\ast}
is the unique maximizer of
\tilde{\Phi}
.
∎
