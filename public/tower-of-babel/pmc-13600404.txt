CMV IgG and EBV Cell-Associated DNA Independently Associate With Veterans Aging Cohort Study Index 2.0 Scores in People With HIV on ART
Authors: Patricia K Riggs, Gordon Honerkamp-Smith, Milenka Meneses, Gemma Caballero, Antoine Chaillon, Donald Franklin, Ronald J Ellis, Scott L Letendre, Sara Gianella
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPeople with HIV (PWH) are at increased risk for non-AIDS comorbidities despite suppressive ART. Chronic co-infections with CMV and EBV may contribute to systemic immune activation and comorbidities, but the relative contribution of viral activity vs host immune response is unclear.MethodsWe evaluated associations between viral measures and the Veterans Aging Cohort Study (VACS) Index 2.0, a validated 5-year mortality risk score for PWH. Plasma CMV and EBV IgG were quantified by ELISA, and CMV, EBV, and HIV cell-associated DNA (CA-DNA) were measured in peripheral blood mononuclear cells by droplet digital PCR. Total globulin levels were abstracted from clinical panels as a marker of generalized immune activation. Regression models were adjusted for sex, race, ethnicity, HIV duration, and history of AIDS.ResultsAmong 485 ART-suppressed PWH (mean age 54 years; 17% women; 59% White), 96.5% were CMV seropositive, 100% EBV seropositive, CMV CA-DNA was detected in 45.9%, EBV in 95.4%, and HIV in 99.0%. Higher VACS scores were associated with higher CMV IgG, EBV IgG, EBV CA-DNA, HIV CA-DNA, and total globulin in adjusted models. In multivariable models, CMV IgG, EBV CA-DNA, and total globulins remained independently associated with VACS Index 2.0. HIV CA-DNA and sex were retained in the best-fit model with trend-level significance.ConclusionsThese findings suggest distinct mechanisms by which CMV, EBV, and HIV may contribute to morbidity in PWH on ART: CMV through immune activation, and EBV and HIV through viral persistence. Longitudinal studies are needed to clarify causal pathways and guide interventions.Keywords: HIV, Cytomegalovirus (CMV), Epstein-Barr virus (EBV), latent herpesviruses, Veterans Aging Cohort Study (VACS) Mortality Risk Index

(See the Editorial Commentary by Lichtner and Vita on pages 323–5.)

Despite effective ART, people with HIV (PWH) remain at increased risk of non-AIDS morbidity and mortality compared to people without HIV [1–3]. Persistent immune activation and systemic inflammation are key contributors to this excess risk [3]. Chronic co-infections with herpesviruses, particularly CMV and EBV, are nearly universal in PWH and represent potentially modifiable drivers of immune dysregulation [4, 5].

CMV establishes lifelong infection and undergoes frequent subclinical reactivations, resulting in persistent antigenic stimulation and broad immune system engagement [6]. CMV encodes viral cytokines and cytokine receptor homologs that modulate host signaling pathways, producing mixed pro- and anti-inflammatory effects [7–15]. CMV also promotes expansion of highly differentiated, senescent CD8+ T cells (“memory inflation”), reducing immunologic diversity and contributing to immune aging [16]. Additionally, CMV has been linked to endothelial dysfunction and increased cardiovascular disease risk, even in immunocompetent individuals [17–19]. In PWH on suppressive ART, CMV-specific immune responses and subclinical viral activity, independent of overt viremia or end-organ disease, have been associated with inflammation-related comorbidities and non-AIDS mortality [20–24].

EBV, another ubiquitous herpesvirus, establishes latency in memory B cells and persists through intermittent reactivation, B-cell clonal expansion, and immortalization [25–27]. EBV-driven B-cell activation and dysregulation can sustain systemic immune perturbation. In PWH, EBV co-infection has been associated with increased HIV reservoir size and diversity, likely via chronic immune activation and impaired immune surveillance [13, 24]. Compared with CMV, fewer studies have evaluated EBV's contribution to non-AIDS comorbidities.

A critical challenge is disentangling whether adverse health outcomes are driven by direct viral activity, host immune responses (general or virus specific), or their interactions. While higher IgG levels are often assumed to reflect more frequent reactivation, several studies show no associations between CMV IgG and DNA levels or reactivation events [4, 28–31]. A prior analysis of men with HIV even found that higher CMV IgG levels were associated with less frequent detection of CMV in seminal fluid and blood [13]. Other studies have found stronger associations between CMV-specific immune responses (CD8+ T cells and total IgG) and adverse outcomes than with measures of viral replication, suggesting that immune activation may be the key mediator [30, 32, 33]. This relationship is even less clear for EBV. Clarifying whether viral burden or host responses are more clinically relevant is critical for informing clinical trial design and therapeutic strategies.

Most prior studies have focused on a single virus or immunologic parameter, limiting insights into the combined and virus-specific effects of chronic co-infections. Few studies have incorporated general markers of immune activation to contextualize virus-specific findings. Total globulin levels (or “gamma gap”) represent a nonspecific but readily available marker of polyclonal immune activation and have been associated with increased morbidity and mortality risk across multiple chronic disease settings [34–38]. We included total globulin levels to help distinguish general immune activation from virus-specific effects.

To assess the clinical significance of chronic viral burden and immunologic markers, we leveraged the Veterans Aging Cohort Study (VACS) Mortality Index 2.0, a validated composite risk score incorporating HIV-specific parameters alongside general indicators of physiologic dysfunction [39]. The VACS Index 2.0 provides a robust surrogate for global morbidity and mortality risk in PWH, making it a valuable tool for evaluating links between chronic viral burden, immune dysregulation, and long-term health outcomes [40–42].

In this study, we examined the relationships between VACS Index 2.0 scores and quantitative measures of CMV, EBV, and HIV-1, including CA-DNA and IgG titers, in a large cohort of ART-suppressed PWH. We hypothesized that stronger immune responses (IgG) and higher levels of viral DNA would be independently associated with increased VACS scores, reflecting greater risk of non-AIDS comorbidity and mortality.

METHODS

Study Design and Participants

This was a retrospective cross-sectional analysis incorporating additional viral and serological testing on stored biospecimens from 2 large observational cohort studies of PWH in the United States: CHARTER (CNS HIV ART Effects Research) and HNRP (HIV Neurobehavioral Research Program) [43, 44]. A total of 485 participants were included based on the following criteria: (1) availability of stored plasma specimens collected during a comprehensive clinical research assessment between 2003 and 2019; (2) documented use of ART at the time of specimen collection, and (3) plasma HIV-1 RNA <200 copies/mL.

HNRP assessments [2] were completed at the University of California San Diego, and CHARTER visits were completed at 1 of 6 US sites (Baltimore, Galveston, New York City, San Diego, Seattle, and St Louis) [45]. Original cohort enrollment excluded participants with active opportunistic infections, severe psychiatric disorders (eg, untreated schizophrenia), substance use that would interfere with participation, or inability to complete assessments [43]. All projects were approved by the Human Research Protections Program at the University of California San Diego, and all participants provided written informed consent for the study procedures, including future use of their data and stored biospecimens for retrospective analyses.

Laboratory Assessments

All participants had a confirmed HIV-1 diagnosis by ELISA. HIV-1 RNA levels in plasma were quantified by a commercial PCR assay; clinical viral suppression was defined as <200 copies/mL as per United States Department of Health and Human Services guidelines. Peripheral blood T-cell subsets were measured by flow cytometry. Routine clinical laboratory tests (eg, metabolic panels and T-cell subsets) were performed by a Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments–certified laboratory. Total globulin levels are calculated from routine metabolic panels as total protein − albumin (reported in g/dL).

Viral Cell–Associated DNA and IgG Antibody Measurements

Cell-associated (ca-) DNA for CMV, EBV, and HIV-1 was extracted from 5 million peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC)s using the AllPrep DNA/RNA minikit (Qiagen, Carlsbad, CA). Viral DNA was quantified by droplet digital PCR (ddPCR) as previously described [13, 29, 46]. Copy numbers were normalized to cell input using the RPP30 reference gene.

Quantitative CMV and EBV IgG levels were measured in plasma by commercially available enzyme immunoassay kits: Epstein–Barr Virus VCA IgG ELISA Kit # KA1444 (Abnova, Taoyuan City, Taiwan) and CMV IgG ELISA Kit #GWB-892399 (Genway Biotech, San Diego, CA). Antibody concentrations were calculated by interpolation from a standard curve, as per manufacturer protocol.

The VACS Index 2.0

The VACS Index, originally developed to predict all-cause mortality in PWH, has since been updated (VACS Index 2.0) to improve prognostic accuracy, particularly in older individuals with suppressed HIV viral loads [39, 42, 47, 48]. VACS Index 2.0 incorporates both HIV-specific variables and markers of systemic health, including age, BMI, CD4+ T-cell counts, HIV RNA viral load, hemoglobin, platelets, white blood cell count, albumin, aspartate and alanine transaminases (AST, ALT), creatinine, and hepatitis C virus serostatus. Composite indices within VACS Index 2.0 include the FIB-4 index calculated as [FIB 4: (age AST)/(platelet count ALT1/2)] to estimate liver fibrosis risk, and eGFR, calculated using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI 2009) equation.

Statistical Analyses

Continuous variables were summarized using medians and interquartile ranges, and categorical variables as counts and percentages. CA-DNA and EBV IgG values were log10 transformed to reduce skewness and improve model assumptions. Relationships between pairs of continuous viral, immunologic and clinical variables were assessed using Spearman correlation matrices.

Associations between individual viral or immunologic markers and VACS Index 2.0 scores were first evaluated separately using unadjusted and adjusted linear regression models; adjusted models forced inclusion of prespecified covariates (sex assigned at birth, race, ethnicity, estimated duration of HIV infection [EDI], and prior AIDS diagnosis [49]). Then, to assess combined and relative contributions, additional multivariable linear regression was conducted in 2 stages: Model 1: the 6 virologic and serologic predictors of interest simultaneously and Model 2: Backward selection based on Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) was used to derive a parsimonious best-fit model with the 6 predictors of interest and the 5 prespecified covariates (sex, race, ethnicity, EDI, and prior AIDS diagnosis) as candidates. Variance inflation factors (VIF) were examined to assess multicollinearity. To evaluate potential confounding of the CMV IgG association by age (excluded from primary models as age is a component of the VACS Index 2.0) sensitivity analysis of the VACS Index 2.0 association with CMV IgG was performed by adding age alone and age plus total globulins. False discovery rate correction was applied to adjust for multiple comparisons. A 2-sided significance level of α = .05 was used. Post hoc power calculations based on the final sample size (n = 485) confirmed that the study was well powered (≥87%) to detect small-to-moderate effect sizes in the primary univariable regression analyses. Analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.5.1) and JMP Pro (version 17.2.0).

RESULTS

Participant Characteristics

This cross-sectional analysis included 485 virally suppressed individuals with HIV-1. Participants were predominantly White (59%) men (83%) with a median age of 54 years and had HIV for a median of 20.3 years (Table 1). The median nadir CD4+ T-cell count was 159 cells/µL, and the median CD4+ T-cell count at the time of the assessment was 616 cells/µL. Thirty percent were HCV seropositive. All participants were on virally suppressive ART with HIV-1 RNA <200 copies/mL.

Relationships Among Viral Measures, Immune Markers, and Clinical Characteristics

To characterize relationships among continuous viral, immunologic, and clinical variables, we generated a Spearman correlation matrix (Figure 1). Most correlations were statistically significant but modest (ρ < 0.3). EBV IgG showed the strongest correlations, including positive correlations with EBV CA-DNA (ρ = 0.6, P < .0001), total globulins (ρ = 0.3, P < .001) and CMV IgG (ρ = 0.24, P < .001), and an inverse correlation with the CD4:CD8 ratio (ρ = −0.3, P < .001).

Spearman correlation matrix between total globulins, IgG (EBV and CMV) titers, cell-associated viral DNA (EBV, CMV, and HIV) levels, and clinical characteristics. P value: *<.05; **<.01; ***<.001.

CMV IgG was also inversely correlated with the CD4:CD8 ratio (ρ = −0.3, P < .001); however, in contrast to EBV, CMV IgG was not correlated with CMV CA-DNA in the full cohort (P = .90) or when restricting to participants with detectable CMV CA-DNA (P = .60). HIV CA-DNA was inversely correlated with nadir CD4+ T-cell count (ρ = −0.2, P < .0001). CMV CA DNA showed weak inverse correlations with EDI (ρ = −0.2, P < .0001).

We also evaluated whether viral and immunologic measures differed by sex, race, or ethnicity using ANOVA with Tukey post hoc testing. Total globulin levels were higher in women than men (P = .036). EBV IgG levels differed by race (overall P = .01), with the highest levels among participants identifying as Black and the lowest among those identifying as Asian/Pacific Islander. Total globulins also varied by race (overall P < .0001), driven by higher levels in Black compared with White participants. No significant differences by ethnicity were observed for CMV IgG, EBV IgG, CMV, EBV, or HIV CA-DNA, or for total globulins.

Associations Between VACS Index 2.0 and Viral and Immunologic Measures

To test the hypothesis that chronic viral burden and immune activation contribute to increased mortality and morbidity risk in PWH, we performed unadjusted and adjusted linear regression analyses using the VACS Index 2.0 score as the outcome and each viral or immunologic marker as a predictor (Figure 2). All adjusted models included prespecified covariates: sex assigned at birth, race, ethnicity, EDI, and prior AIDS diagnosis.

Associations between VACS Index 2.0 and viral and immunologic measures—unadjusted and adjusted models. Forest plot of standardized beta coefficients and P values. Horizontal bars indicate 95% CIs. Each model contains a single viral or immunologic measure as predictor and VACS Index 2.0 as outcome. Adjusted models also include sex, race, ethnicity, estimated duration of infection (EDI), and prior AIDS diagnosis.

In both unadjusted and adjusted models, higher VACS Index 2.0 scores were associated with higher CMV IgG (standardized β = .18, P < .001), EBV IgG (std β = .15, P < .001), EBV CA-DNA (std β = .18, P = .001), HIV CA-DNA (std β = .11, P = .025), and total globulins (std β = .16, P < .001). CMV CA-DNA was inversely associated with VACS Index 2.0 in unadjusted analyses (std β = −.11, P = .023) but not after adjustment (P = .76), suggesting confounding by demographic or HIV-related factors.

CMV IgG showed a weak but statistically significant correlation with age (ρ = 0.16, P = .001); because age is a component of the VACS Index 2.0 and was therefore excluded from primary adjusted models, we performed sensitivity analyses to confirm the CMV IgG association was not confounded by age. CMV IgG remained independently associated with VACS Index 2.0 when age was added alone (std β = .18, P < .001; R2 = 0.257) and when age and total globulins were added simultaneously (std β = .13, P = .002; R2 = 0.339), with no meaningful multicollinearity (VIF < 1.06 for all variables).

Relative and Combined Contributions of CMV, EBV, and HIV Measures in Multivariable Models

To assess the combined and independent contributions of viral and serologic markers, we constructed multivariable linear regression models including the 6 viral and serologic predictors of interest (CMV IgG, EBV IgG, total globulins, CMV, EBV, and HIV CA-DNA; Table 2). These analyses were conducted in 2 stages. Model 1 evaluated the 6 predictors simultaneously, and Model 2 incorporated these predictors together with prespecified covariates (sex assigned at birth, race, ethnicity, EDI, and prior AIDS diagnosis) as candidate variables for backward selection to identify the best-fitting model.

Model 1: Viral and Serologic Predictors Only

Model 1 included the 6 primary predictors of interest (CMV IgG, EBV IgG, total globulins, CMV DNA, EBV DNA, and HIV DNA) and explained 17.9% of the variance in VACS Index 2.0 scores (R2 = 0.179, P = 1.3 × 10−15). Higher VACS Index 2.0 scores were independently associated with CMV IgG (β = .183, P < .001), EBV CA-DNA (β = .176, P = .001), total globulins (β = .164, P < .001), and HIV CA-DNA (β = .115, P = .012). CMV CA-DNA showed a nonsignificant trend toward a negative association (β = −.0870, P = .054). EBV IgG was no longer independently associated (P = .50) with VACS Index 2.0 in multivariable regression. VIF were <2 for all variables, indicating minimal multicollinearity.

Model 2: Best-Fit Model

Using backward selection based on AIC, a model including the 6 viral/serologic predictors and 5 prespecified covariates (sex, race, ethnicity, EDI, and prior AIDS diagnosis) as candidate covariates was evaluated. The best-fit model retained CMV IgG, EBV CA-DNA, total globulins, HIV CA-DNA, EDI, prior AIDS diagnosis, and sex. Race and ethnicity were not retained. This model explained 27.1% of the variance in VACS Index 2.0 scores (R2 = 0.271, P = 7.4 × 10−24). CMV IgG (β = .1407, P = .002), EBV CA-DNA (β = .1321, P = .003), and total globulins (β = .1884, P < .001) remained significantly associated with higher VACS Index 2.0 scores. HIV CA-DNA (β = .0648, P = .142) and sex (β = .2022, P = .070) did not reach statistical significance but improved overall model fit and were retained. Both EDI (β = .2172, P < .001) and prior AIDS diagnosis (β = .3743, P < .001) were strongly associated with higher VACS Index 2.0 scores.

Integrated Summary of Multivariable Models

Across all models, CMV IgG, EBV CA-DNA, and total globulin levels emerged as the most consistent and robust predictors of higher VACS Index 2.0 scores, supporting their independent contributions to morbidity risk in ART-suppressed PWH. Although HIV CA-DNA was associated with VACS scores in unadjusted analyses, this association was attenuated after adjustment. CMV CA-DNA and EBV IgG were not independently associated with VACS scores in any model. The best-fit model highlights the additive influence of virus-specific immune responses, viral persistence, and generalized immune activation on morbidity risk.

DISCUSSION

The mechanisms and relative contributions of CMV and EBV co-infections to excess morbidity and mortality risk in PWH on suppressive ART remain incompletely understood. In this study, we examined associations between the VACS Index 2.0—a validated composite mortality risk score—and quantitative CMV, EBV, and HIV markers (CA-DNA and IgG) in a well-characterized cohort of 485 ART-suppressed PWH. By adjusting multivariable models for total globulin levels, we aimed to distinguish virus-specific effects from generalized immune activation. We found that higher CMV IgG and EBV CA-DNA were independently associated with higher VACS Index 2.0 scores, whereas CMV CA-DNA and EBV IgG were not. These findings suggest that, in people with well controlled HIV on ART, CMV-associated risk is primarily associated with host immune responses, whereas EBV-associated risk reflects more direct effects of cellular viral persistence.

CMV and EBV: Distinct Mechanisms of Risk

The association between CMV IgG—but not CMV CA-DNA in PBMCs—and mortality risk suggests that host humoral responses may better capture clinical vulnerability than circulating PBMC CA-DNA, at least in PWH on stable ART. This association remained robust across adjusted models and sensitivity analyses accounting for age and total globulins, supporting a virus-related contribution beyond generalized immune activation. The low CMV CA-DNA copy numbers and the lack of association with either VACS scores or CMV IgG are consistent with PBMC-associated CMV DNA reflecting latent genomes rather than active replication in this population. This aligns with prior evidence that PBMC-based CMV DNA associations with inflammation are lost after ART suppression [31]. This is consistent with CMV reactivation occurring predominantly in tissues such as salivary glands, the genital tract, and vascular endothelium, rather than in circulating leukocytes. CMV IgG likely reflects both CMV-specific humoral immunity and some nonspecific immune dysregulation warranting cautious interpretation. As a stable, blood-based marker integrating cumulative host–virus immune engagement, it offers practical advantages over more technically demanding, invasive, or transient measures such as CMV-specific T- and NK-cell responses or mucosal viral shedding [50]. As with all cross-sectional findings, directionality cannot be established—greater comorbidity burden could drive higher CMV IgG rather than the reverse—and longitudinal studies are needed to clarify causal pathways and identify which CMV-specific immune correlates best predict clinical risk in PWH on ART.

In contrast, EBV CA-DNA in PBMCs remained significantly associated with VACS Index 2.0 scores across all models, while EBV IgG did not, suggesting that EBV-associated risk is more closely linked to cellular viral persistence than to humoral immune responses. That EBV CA-DNA was the more specific predictor—despite its strong correlation with EBV IgG—likely reflects direct effects of EBV persistence and activity in circulating B cells on immune activation and increased HIV transcription, reservoir size, and diversity [24, 29]. EBV remains less studied than CMV, and our findings support further investigation.

HIV CA-DNA

Although HIV CA-DNA was detectable in PBMCs in nearly all participants, its association with VACS Index 2.0 was modest and attenuated after adjustment. HIV CA-DNA was retained in the best-fit model with trend-level significance, likely reflecting limitations of total HIV CA-DNA as a surrogate for replication-competent reservoir [24]. Nevertheless, residual HIV DNA may still mark prior immune injury or ongoing low-level inflammation, partially explaining the weaker associations observed.

Total Globulins and Nonspecific Immune Activation

Total globulin levels were significantly associated with VACS Index 2.0 scores across all models, reinforcing their value as a marker of generalized immune activation and polyclonal B-cell stimulation [35, 38]. Elevated globulin levels have been linked to increased morbidity and mortality risk in multiple chronic disease contexts [34–38]. The retention of CMV IgG, EBV CA-DNA, and total globulins as independent predictors in multivariable models suggests that these measures capture overlapping yet distinct aspects of immune dysregulation, likely reflecting bidirectional relationships between chronic viral infections and host immune activation.

Notably, total globulin levels were higher in female and Black participants in this cohort; whether this reflects differences in immune activation, higher sex hormone binding globulin, or other factors warrants further investigation in cohorts with more balanced demographic representation.

Limitations and Future Directions

This study has several limitations. Its cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. The use of IgG titers targeting immunodominant antigens as markers of antiviral immunity underrepresents the complexity of host–virus interactions, as these measures do not capture cellular immunity, broader antibody repertoires, or functional T-, B-, or NK-cell responses. Viral quantification by ddPCR does not distinguish latent genomes from active replication for CMV and EBV, nor replication competent from defective HIV proviruses. While the VACS Index 2.0 is a validated surrogate for morbidity and mortality risk, it remains an indirect, composite measure and may not fully reflect organ-specific disease processes. Generalizability is limited, as participants were predominantly White men with long-standing HIV infection and substantial historical immune compromise; whether similar associations exist among PWH who initiated ART during early infection or groups underrepresented in our sample, including women and some racial/ethnic minorities, remains uncertain. Additionally, future studies should incorporate other herpesviruses, particularly given emerging evidence linking VZV and HSV to aging and dementia risk.

Future studies should prioritize longitudinal designs linking serial viral and immunologic measures to incident non-AIDS outcomes to clarify causal pathways. More detailed immune profiling will be necessary to distinguish host–viral interactions and to identify determinants of interindividual variability in risk. Mucosal or tissue-based assessments of CMV and EBV activity may better capture biologically relevant viral effects than PBMC-based measures alone. Ultimately, interventional studies will be needed to determine whether targeting herpesvirus-associated immune activation—through antiviral, immunomodulatory, or vaccine-based strategies—can meaningfully reduce comorbidity and mortality risk, and to identify which patients are most likely to benefit from personalized approaches.

CONCLUSIONS

CMV IgG, EBV CA-DNA, and total globulins are independently associated with mortality risk in ART-suppressed PWH, suggesting CMV and EBV contribute through distinct mechanisms: CMV primarily through host immune responses, EBV through direct viral effects. These distinct host- and virus-specific patterns underscore the complexity of herpesvirus-associated morbidity in PWH and highlight the need for longitudinal and mechanistic studies to determine whether targeting herpesvirus-driven immune dysregulation can meaningfully reduce comorbidity risk.
