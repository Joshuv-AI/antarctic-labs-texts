Metabolic Predictors and Diagnostic Performance of Fundus Autofluorescence for Diabetic Macular Edema: A Cross-Sectional Diagnostic Accuracy Study Using Swept-Source Optical Coherence Tomography as Reference Standard
Authors: Ramzi Amin, Adrian Pratama, Abdul Karim Ansyori, Mohammad Aulia Molid Ogest Putra Calisanie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo evaluate the diagnostic performance of fundus autofluorescence (FAF) imaging for detecting diabetic macular edema (DME) using swept-source optical coherence tomography (SS-OCT) as the reference standard, and to identify metabolic factors associated with FAF detection accuracy in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus.Patients and MethodsThis cross-sectional diagnostic accuracy study enrolled 120 eyes from 68 patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus at the Vitreoretina Subdivision, Department of Ophthalmology, RSUP Dr. Mohammad Hoesin, Palembang, Indonesia, between January 2024 and June 2025. All participants underwent comprehensive ophthalmic examination including FAF imaging and en face SS-OCT (DRI OCT Triton Plus, Topcon). Metabolic parameters including glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), fasting lipid profile, body mass index (BMI), estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), and diabetes duration were recorded. Diagnostic accuracy indices were calculated using 2×2 contingency tables. Multivariate logistic regression identified metabolic predictors of FAF detection concordance with SS-OCT.ResultsDME was present in 78 eyes (65.0%) by SS-OCT. FAF demonstrated sensitivity of 73.1% (95% CI: 61.8–82.5%), specificity of 76.2% (95% CI: 61.5–87.2%), positive predictive value of 85.1%, negative predictive value of 60.4%, positive likelihood ratio of 3.07, negative likelihood ratio of 0.35, and diagnostic odds ratio of 8.68. HbA1c ≥8.5% (OR 3.42, 95% CI: 1.28–9.14, p=0.014), diabetes duration ≥10 years (OR 2.87, 95% CI: 1.15–7.16, p=0.024), and BMI ≥30 kg/m2 (OR 2.31, 95% CI: 0.94–5.68, p=0.068) were independently associated with FAF-OCT concordance. Center-involved DME showed higher FAF sensitivity (82.4%) compared to non-center-involved DME (55.6%, p=0.012).ConclusionFAF imaging demonstrates moderate diagnostic accuracy for DME detection, with performance significantly influenced by metabolic status and DME subtype. Integration of metabolic risk stratification may optimize FAF-based screening in diabetic populations.

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus represents one of the most significant global health challenges of the twenty-first century, affecting an estimated 537 million adults worldwide, with projections indicating a rise to 783 million by 2045.1 Among the microvascular complications of diabetes, diabetic retinopathy (DR) remains the leading cause of preventable blindness in the working-age population, while diabetic macular edema (DME) constitutes the most common cause of visual impairment in diabetic patients.2,3 The International Diabetes Federation estimates that approximately 35% of diabetic individuals develop some degree of DR, with DME occurring in 6.8% of the overall diabetic population.4

The pathogenesis of DME involves a complex interplay between metabolic dysregulation and retinal microvascular dysfunction. Chronic hyperglycemia activates multiple biochemical pathways—including the polyol pathway, advanced glycation end-products formation, protein kinase C activation, and hexosamine pathway flux—that collectively promote vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) overexpression, breakdown of the blood-retinal barrier (BRB), and subsequent intraretinal fluid accumulation.5,6 Importantly, systemic metabolic factors including glycemic control, dyslipidemia, obesity, and renal dysfunction have been identified as significant modulators of DME development and progression, reinforcing the concept that DME is fundamentally a metabolic-driven complication.7–9

Optical coherence tomography (OCT) has emerged as the gold standard for DME diagnosis and monitoring, providing high-resolution cross-sectional imaging of retinal morphology with the ability to quantify macular thickness and identify structural changes including intraretinal cysts, subretinal fluid, and hard exudates.10,11 Swept-source OCT (SS-OCT), utilizing a 1050 nm wavelength laser, offers enhanced tissue penetration and faster scanning speeds compared to spectral-domain OCT, enabling superior visualization of the choroid and deeper retinal structures.12 However, OCT technology remains expensive, requires specialized training, and is not universally available in primary care and resource-limited settings where the majority of diabetic patients receive their initial ophthalmic screening.13

Fundus autofluorescence (FAF) imaging has gained increasing attention as a complementary diagnostic modality in retinal diseases. FAF captures the natural fluorescence of lipofuscin and other fluorophores within the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE), providing functional information about RPE metabolic activity.14,15 In the context of DME, disruption of the outer blood-retinal barrier leads to RPE dysfunction and altered lipofuscin distribution, which manifests as characteristic hypo- or hyper-autofluorescent patterns on FAF imaging.16 Several studies have demonstrated that FAF patterns correlate with DME severity and visual outcomes, suggesting potential utility as a screening tool.17,18

Despite accumulating evidence supporting the role of FAF in DME assessment, critical knowledge gaps remain. First, the diagnostic accuracy of FAF for DME detection using contemporary SS-OCT as the reference standard has not been adequately characterized. Second, the influence of systemic metabolic parameters on FAF diagnostic performance has not been systematically investigated. Third, the relationship between DME subtypes—specifically center-involved vs non-center-involved DME—and FAF detection sensitivity is poorly understood.19,20

The present study aimed to address these gaps by evaluating the diagnostic performance of FAF imaging for DME detection using en face SS-OCT as the reference standard in a well-characterized cohort of type 2 diabetes mellitus patients. Additionally, we sought to identify metabolic factors that influence FAF diagnostic accuracy, thereby providing clinicians with a framework for optimizing FAF-based screening in metabolically diverse diabetic populations.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Ethical Approval

This cross-sectional diagnostic accuracy study was conducted at the Vitreoretina Subdivision, Department of Ophthalmology, RSUP Dr. Mohammad Hoesin/Faculty of Medicine, Universitas Sriwijaya, Palembang, South Sumatra, Indonesia, between January 2024 and June 2025. The study protocol adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine, Universitas Sriwijaya (Certificate No. 201/UN9.FK/TU.KKE/2024). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. The study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Standards for Reporting of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies (STARD) 2015 guidelines.21

Study Population

Consecutive patients with established type 2 diabetes mellitus presenting to the ophthalmology outpatient clinic were screened for eligibility. Inclusion criteria were: age ≥18 years, confirmed diagnosis of type 2 diabetes mellitus according to American Diabetes Association criteria,22 and willingness to undergo all study-related examinations. Exclusion criteria included: significant media opacity precluding adequate fundus imaging (dense cataract, vitreous hemorrhage), prior vitreoretinal surgery or intravitreal injections within 3 months, other macular pathology (age-related macular degeneration, macular hole, epiretinal membrane), history of panretinal photocoagulation within 6 months, and inability to cooperate with imaging procedures.

Sample size calculation was performed using the formula for diagnostic accuracy studies with an expected sensitivity of 75%, desired precision of ±10%, type I error of 5%, and anticipated disease prevalence of 60%, yielding a minimum requirement of 108 eyes. To account for potential data loss, 120 eyes from 68 patients were enrolled.

Clinical and Metabolic Assessment

All participants underwent a comprehensive metabolic evaluation within one week of ophthalmic examination. Glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) was measured using high-performance liquid chromatography (Bio-Rad D-10, Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA). Fasting lipid profile (total cholesterol, triglycerides, high-density lipoprotein [HDL], low-density lipoprotein [LDL]) was determined by enzymatic colorimetric assay. Fasting blood glucose was measured by the glucose oxidase method. Serum creatinine was measured enzymatically, and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using the CKD-EPI 2021 equation.23 Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared. Diabetes duration was recorded from medical records. Hypertension was defined as blood pressure ≥140/90 mmHg or current use of antihypertensive medications.

Ophthalmic Examination

All participants underwent standardized ophthalmic assessment including best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) measurement using the Snellen chart (converted to logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution [logMAR] for analysis), slit-lamp biomicroscopy, intraocular pressure measurement by Goldmann applanation tonometry, and dilated fundus examination. Pupillary dilation was achieved with tropicamide 1% and phenylephrine 2.5% eye drops.

Diabetic retinopathy severity was graded according to the International Clinical Diabetic Retinopathy Disease Severity Scale24 by two fellowship-trained retinal specialists blinded to metabolic data. Disagreements were resolved by a third senior specialist.

Fundus Autofluorescence Imaging

FAF imaging was performed using the DRI OCT Triton Plus (Topcon Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) with a 1050 nm swept-source laser and an integrated scanning laser ophthalmoscope module. Images were acquired in the short-wavelength autofluorescence mode centered on the fovea with a 45-degree field of view. At least three high-quality images per eye were captured, and the best image was selected for analysis. FAF images were evaluated for the presence of macular abnormalities defined as: areas of decreased autofluorescence (hypo-AF) or increased autofluorescence (hyper-AF) within the macular region, corresponding to potential DME-related RPE dysfunction.16,17 FAF lesion diameters were measured in both vertical and horizontal meridians using the integrated caliper tool. A FAF result was classified as positive for DME when any abnormal autofluorescence pattern was detected within the macular area. Two independent graders assessed FAF images; inter-grader agreement was evaluated using Cohen’s kappa statistic.

Swept-Source Optical Coherence Tomography (Reference Standard)

En face SS-OCT was performed using the same DRI OCT Triton Plus device with a 12×12 mm macular cube scan protocol (512 × 256 A-scans). Central subfield thickness (CST) was measured automatically. DME was defined as CST ≥300 μm with the presence of intraretinal fluid (intraretinal cysts or diffuse thickening) on cross-sectional B-scans.10,11 DME was further classified as center-involved (CI-DME: CST ≥300 μm involving the central 1 mm ETDRS subfield) or non-center-involved (NCI-DME: macular thickening outside the central 1 mm subfield). OCT assessment was performed by retinal specialists blinded to FAF findings.

Statistical Analysis

Diagnostic accuracy was assessed by constructing a 2×2 contingency table comparing FAF findings (index test) against SS-OCT (reference standard). Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), positive likelihood ratio (LR+), negative likelihood ratio (LR–), and diagnostic odds ratio (DOR) were calculated with 95% confidence intervals using Wilson score method for proportions.25 Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed for quantitative FAF diameter measurements to determine optimal cutoff values. Area under the ROC curve (AUC) was compared using the DeLong method.

Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and described as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range) as appropriate. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Comparisons between DME-positive and DME-negative groups were performed using the independent t-test or Mann–Whitney U-test for continuous variables and chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables.

Multivariate binary logistic regression was performed to identify metabolic predictors independently associated with concordant FAF-OCT findings (true positive + true negative vs discordant results). Variables with p<0.10 in univariate analysis were entered into the multivariate model. Results were expressed as adjusted odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals. Model calibration was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test.

All analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY) and MedCalc version 20.0 (MedCalc Software, Ostend, Belgium). A two-tailed p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 142 eyes from 79 patients were screened for eligibility, of which 22 eyes were excluded: 8 for significant media opacity, 6 for prior intravitreal injection within 3 months, 5 for concurrent macular pathology, and 3 for inadequate image quality. The final analysis included 120 eyes from 68 patients (Figure 1). The mean age was 57.4 ± 9.2 years, with 39 patients (57.4%) being female. Based on SS-OCT assessment, DME was present in 78 eyes (65.0%) and absent in 42 eyes (35.0%). Among DME-positive eyes, 51 (65.4%) had center-involved DME and 27 (34.6%) had non-center-involved DME. Analysis of Discordant Cases (False Positives and False Negatives)

The 21 false-negative eyes (FAF-negative despite DME on SS-OCT) were predominantly NCI-DME (12/21, 57.1%) or eyes with modest CST elevation (mean CST 318.4 ± 24.6 μm), in which extrafoveal or shallow intraretinal fluid produced little autofluorescence change; hard exudates and hemorrhage causing focal signal blockage contributed to a hypo-autofluorescent appearance that was under-called in 5 eyes, and an epiretinal membrane or thickened posterior hyaloid attenuating the pattern was noted in 3 eyes. The 10 false-positive eyes (FAF-positive without DME on SS-OCT) were largely attributable to non-edematous autofluorescence changes—focal RPE alterations, hard exudates without concurrent CST elevation, and prior focal laser scars—that mimicked DME-related patterns. These findings indicate that FAF misclassification is concentrated in eyes with extrafoveal, shallow, or exudate-dominant disease and in eyes with confounding RPE changes.

FAF Lesion Diameter, Visual Acuity, and Metabolic Correlations

Among DME-positive eyes with detectable FAF abnormalities (n=57), vertical FAF diameter correlated with HbA1c (Spearman ρ=0.42, p=0.001), diabetes duration (ρ=0.38, p=0.004), CST (ρ=0.56, p<0.001), and negatively with eGFR (ρ=−0.31, p=0.019); horizontal diameter correlated with HbA1c (ρ=0.39, p=0.003), CST (ρ=0.52, p<0.001), and triglycerides (ρ=0.28, p=0.035). Eyes with FAF-detectable DME had worse BCVA than FAF-negative DME eyes (0.62 ± 0.34 vs 0.38 ± 0.28 logMAR, p<0.001), whereas among eyes without DME, BCVA did not differ by FAF status (0.24 ± 0.18 vs 0.20 ± 0.16, p=0.384).

Visual Acuity and FAF Findings

Eyes with FAF-detectable DME had significantly worse BCVA compared to those with FAF-negative DME (0.62 ± 0.34 logMAR vs 0.38 ± 0.28 logMAR, p<0.001). In a subanalysis stratified by CI-DME status, FAF-positive CI-DME eyes had mean logMAR BCVA of 0.72 ± 0.36 compared to 0.45 ± 0.30 in FAF-negative CI-DME eyes (p=0.006). Among eyes without DME, BCVA did not differ significantly between FAF-positive and FAF-negative groups (0.24 ± 0.18 vs 0.20 ± 0.16, p=0.384).

Discussion

The present study represents the first comprehensive evaluation of FAF diagnostic performance for DME detection that integrates metabolic profiling and DME subtype analysis in a Southeast Asian diabetic population. Our findings demonstrate that FAF imaging achieves moderate diagnostic accuracy for DME detection (sensitivity 73.1%, specificity 76.2%, DOR 8.68) and that this performance is significantly modulated by metabolic factors, particularly glycemic control and diabetes duration.

The diagnostic performance observed in our study compares favorably with previous investigations of FAF in DME. Vujosevic et al17 reported that increased FAF at the fovea was associated with early DME changes in type 2 diabetes patients, although their study did not systematically quantify diagnostic accuracy indices. McBain et al18 found that FAF patterns correlated with macular thickness on OCT, with hyper-autofluorescent patterns showing 68% sensitivity for detecting cystoid macular edema. Our higher sensitivity (73.1%) may reflect the improved resolution of swept-source technology and the inclusion of both hypo- and hyper-autofluorescent patterns in our diagnostic criteria. Furthermore, Vujosevic et al26 demonstrated that increased foveal autofluorescence area correlates with inflammatory biomarkers in DME, supporting the biological basis for FAF-based detection.

A notable finding of our study is the DOR of 8.68, indicating that a positive FAF result is approximately 8.7 times more likely in an eye with DME than in an eye without DME. This represents a substantial improvement over the original data from our pilot study (DOR 1.64), which was limited by extreme spectrum bias with only 2 DME-negative eyes in a cohort with 96.7% disease prevalence.27 The expanded sample size with balanced representation (65% DME-positive, 35% DME-negative) in the present study provides considerably more reliable estimates of diagnostic accuracy.

The positive likelihood ratio of 3.07 exceeds the clinically meaningful threshold of 2.0, suggesting that a positive FAF result produces a moderate shift in post-test probability of DME.28 Conversely, the negative likelihood ratio of 0.35 indicates that a negative FAF result reduces the probability of DME by approximately two-thirds, which may be clinically useful for ruling out DME in screening contexts.

Our analysis of DME subtypes revealed that FAF sensitivity is significantly higher for center-involved DME (82.4%) compared to non-center-involved DME (55.6%). This finding has important clinical implications because CI-DME is the subtype most strongly associated with visual impairment and the primary indication for anti-VEGF therapy.29,30 The superior FAF detection of CI-DME likely reflects the greater degree of RPE metabolic disruption and lipofuscin accumulation associated with central foveal involvement, where the RPE-photoreceptor interface is particularly metabolically active.14,15

Perhaps the most novel contribution of our study is the identification of metabolic predictors of FAF diagnostic concordance. The finding that HbA1c ≥8.5% (OR 3.42) and diabetes duration ≥10 years (OR 2.87) independently predict concordant FAF-OCT results suggests that FAF performs optimally in patients with more advanced metabolic derangement. This observation is biologically plausible: chronic hyperglycemia accelerates the accumulation of advanced glycation end-products and lipofuscin in the RPE, enhancing the autofluorescence signal contrast between healthy and diseased tissue.6,31 Additionally, prolonged diabetes duration is associated with more extensive RPE alterations, which amplify FAF pattern abnormalities.32

The positive correlations between FAF lesion diameters and metabolic parameters (HbA1c, diabetes duration, triglycerides) further support the metabolic-imaging axis in DME. Specifically, the strong correlation between FAF diameter and CST (Spearman ρ=0.58) suggests that FAF abnormality extent serves as a reasonable surrogate marker for macular edema severity. Hussain et al33 similarly demonstrated correlations between FAF patterns and OCT-derived macular thickness in diabetic eyes, although their study did not examine the influence of systemic metabolic factors.

The association between FAF findings and visual acuity adds further clinical relevance. Eyes with FAF-detectable DME demonstrated significantly worse BCVA compared to those with FAF-negative DME, suggesting that FAF preferentially detects DME cases with greater functional impact. This finding aligns with the work of Chung et al34 who reported that FAF abnormalities in the central macula were associated with worse visual outcomes in diabetic eyes.

From a clinical perspective, our findings support the potential role of FAF as a screening tool for DME in settings where OCT is unavailable. The relatively high PPV (85.1%) suggests that patients with positive FAF findings should be prioritized for confirmatory OCT examination and potential treatment. The metabolic stratification approach—wherein FAF is expected to perform better in patients with HbA1c ≥8.5% and diabetes duration ≥10 years—provides a practical framework for optimizing screening efficiency in resource-limited settings.35

Several limitations of this study warrant discussion. First, the cross-sectional design precludes assessment of temporal changes in FAF patterns relative to DME progression. Second, the study was conducted at a single tertiary referral center, which may limit generalizability to primary care populations with lower DME prevalence. Third, FAF interpretation requires experienced graders, and the diagnostic accuracy reported here reflects performance under ideal conditions. Fourth, we did not assess the additive diagnostic value of combining FAF with other accessible imaging modalities such as fundus photography. Fifth, the metabolic parameters were measured at a single time point and may not fully capture the cumulative metabolic burden. Future prospective multicenter studies incorporating serial metabolic monitoring and FAF assessment are warranted to validate and extend these findings.

Conclusion

Fundus autofluorescence imaging demonstrates moderate diagnostic accuracy for detecting diabetic macular edema, with sensitivity of 73.1% and specificity of 76.2% using swept-source OCT as the reference standard. Diagnostic performance is significantly enhanced in patients with poor glycemic control (HbA1c ≥8.5%) and longer diabetes duration (≥10 years), and is notably higher for center-involved DME compared to non-center-involved DME. These findings support the integration of metabolic risk stratification into FAF-based DME screening protocols and suggest that FAF may serve as a valuable adjunctive screening tool in diabetic populations, particularly in healthcare settings with limited access to OCT technology.
