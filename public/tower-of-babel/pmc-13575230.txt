High-entropy engineering of spinel catalysts for nonradical catalytic ozonation with improved resistance to humidity and sulfur poisoning
Authors: Shulin Zuo, Muke Lin, Guizhi Xu, Jiahao Huang, Rumeng Zhang, Ji Mei, Jingyun Fang, Dehua Xia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Conventional catalytic ozonation of electron-rich sulfur-containing pollutants shows limited efficiency due to nonselective oxidation of hydroxyl radicals (•OH) and sulfate-induced deactivation of active metal sites. Here, we synthesized a high-entropy spinel, (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4, featuring sublattice-resolved electronic reconstruction that enables selective ozone activation and suppresses sulfur poisoning. The catalyst achieves 99% removal of methyl mercaptan (CH3SH), significantly outperforming 51% of CoFe2O4. Spectroscopic analyses and density functional theory calculations reveal that high-entropy doping induces lattice distortion and local coordination disorder, which strengthen A–O–B covalency and inter-site electronic communication. This structural perturbation further drives a sublattice-resolved electronic reconstruction, enriching Co/Mn sites while depleting Fe/Cu/Ni sites. This electronic differentiation promotes cooperative dual-end activation of O3 on Fe–Co/Mn motifs and preferential CH3SH adsorption on Cu/Ni sites. Orbital modulation of Co t2g and Fe eg states near the Fermi level balances interfacial charge transfer, favoring formation of a surface-bound *O3 species over conventional O–O bond cleavage. The resulting spatially separated activation and oxidation sites enable CH3SH mineralization via an electron transfer process while suppressing •OH generation and minimizing sulfate accumulation. This spatially differentiated reaction environment ensures durable performance under humid conditions. These findings establish sublattice-engineered electronic asymmetry as a strategy for selective catalytic ozonation.

Introduction

Catalytic ozonation is widely employed for removing sulfur-containing pollutants, owing to its potent oxidative capacity and relatively low energy consumption1,2. Typically, ozone (O3) adsorbs onto electron-rich transition-metal (TM) sites and dissociates into oxygen (O2) or singlet oxygen (1O2), and surface atomic oxygen (*O)3,4. Although *O is highly reactive toward sulfur-containing compounds5–8, it readily reacts with H2O to yield hydroxyl radicals (•OH)4. The non-selective and highly oxidative nature of •OH often promotes the complete oxidation of sulfur to sulfate (SO42−), which can deposit on TM sites, particularly those responsible for ozone activation, and cause catalyst deactivation, a phenomenon known as sulfur poisoning8,9. In contrast, forming a reactive surface-bound O3 species (*O3) at TM sites shifts O3 activation from radical-mediated pathways toward a dominant electron-transfer process (ETP). This pathway suppresses •OH generation and mitigates sulfate deposition, thereby prolonging catalyst lifetime. Besides, the catalyst-oxidant complex preferentially degrades electron-rich contaminants, enabling selective oxidation.10–13

The formation of reactive *O3 is governed by electron transfer from TM centers into the O3 antibonding π orbital. Effective activation requires a specific electronic interaction: sufficient charge transfer to activate O3, yet controlled to avoid O–O bond cleavage. Conventional O3 activation typically proceeds via a single-end adsorption configuration2,14, which localizes electron density on one O–O bond and facilitates dissociation. In contrast, a dual-end adsorption configuration enables more delocalized charge distribution, stabilizing the O3 as a reactive surface-bound *O3 species (Fig. 1a). For example, Liu et al. engineered a confined TM single-atom catalyst with cross-linked hierarchical graphitic nanochannels that redirected a single-end to a dual-end configuration, thereby lowering the barrier of O3 activation and generating *O3 formation15. To precisely tailor the electronic structure of TM sites for *O3 formation, a material platform that enables rational modulation of active sites is required. Spinel-type TM oxides (AB2O4), such as MnFe2O4 and CoFe2O4, are ideal candidates due to their structural flexibility and chemical stability16–18. Their inherent inter-site (A–B) distances are geometrically compatible with dual-end O3 adsorption. However, in a typical spinel, octahedral B-site cations generally exhibit stronger O3 affinity than tetrahedral A-site cations, leading to localized electron transfer and preferential O–O bond cleavage rather than *O3 formation2,19. Moreover, the lack of functional differentiation among TM sites often results in competitive adsorption of sulfur species at the same centers responsible for O3 activation, thereby accelerating sulfate-induced deactivation. Consequently, fine regulation of the electronic coupling between A and B sites is essential to suppress this reaction pathway. Recent studies have shown that strategic metal incorporation can enhance A–O–B covalency and orbital overlap20–22, thereby promoting inter-site electron communication and enabling precise modulation of the local electronic environments at both A and B centers22–24. Building on this concept, high-entropy oxides (HEOs), multi-metal oxides containing five or more cation species, provide a promising platform for extensive electronic tunability25–28. The compositional complexity of HEOs induces lattice distortion and amplifies electronic heterogeneity within the spinel framework, generating coexisting electron-deficient and electron-rich TM centers. Lattice distortion is regarded as the structural origin that perturbs the local metal-oxygen coordination environment, whereas the sublattice effect refers to the resulting site-specific electronic differentiation between tetrahedral A sites and octahedral B sites. Such heterogeneity may enable functional differentiation among TM sites, allowing O3 activation and sulfur-containing pollutant adsorption to occur at electronically distinct centers rather than competing for identical sites. Previous reports indicate that Fe-, Co-, and Mn-containing oxides are often more effective in O3 activation owing to their redox flexibility and favorable metal-oxygen orbital interactions1,2,6,7. In contrast, Cu and Ni species are expected to exhibit enhanced interaction with sulfur-containing pollutants and intermediates due to their relatively stronger affinity toward soft sulfur ligands. By spatially separating O3 activation motifs from sulfur-binding sites, this differentiated site environment could, in principle, reduce competitive sulfate accumulation at the primary O3-activation centers, thereby mitigating sulfur-induced deactivation. Such a configuration would create favorable conditions for *O3 formation and sustained catalytic performance.

Our previous work reported that high-entropy doping effectively modulated the high-spin Co active sites to improve O3 activation2. However, that study mainly focused on regulating Co-centered O3 activation and did not identify the distinct catalytic roles of the incorporated metal species. In the present work, we further show that high-entropy incorporation induces sublattice-resolved electronic reconstruction, enabling site-specific functional differentiation among Fe, Co, Mn, Cu, and Ni centers. This engineered electronic structure promotes *O3 formation over conventional O–O bond cleavage pathways. Methyl mercaptan (CH3SH), a representative electron-rich sulfur-containing pollutant29–32, was selected to evaluate the catalytic performance of (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4. Mechanistic investigations reveal that high-entropy doping enhances A–O–B covalency and facilitates inter-site electron redistribution, with Co and Mn accepting electrons but Fe, Cu, and Ni losing electrons. This electronically differentiated configuration enables *O3 formation on the Fe-Co/Mn pairs, while Cu/Ni sites preferentially adsorb CH3SH. Consequently, CH3SH undergoes efficient mineralization via the ETP. We demonstrate the use of high-entropy-induced sublattice-resolved electronic reconstruction to achieve functional differentiation of transition-metal sites in a spinel catalyst. This design spatially separates O3 activation from CH3SH adsorption, enabling non-dissociative *O3 formation on Fe–Co/Mn motifs and preferential CH3SH interaction with Cu/Ni-related sites. Such a site-differentiated mechanism provides a strategy for selective nonradical catalytic ozonation with enhanced resistance to sulfate poisoning.

Results

Characterization of catalysts

Bimetallic spinel CoFe2O4, high-entropy (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4, and three other compared oxides (CuFe2O4, NiFe2O4, MnFe2O4) were synthesized using a method detailed in Supplementary Fig. S1. Thermogravimetry-differential scanning calorimetry (TG-DSC) analysis and X-ray diffractometer (XRD) and N2 adsorption-desorption characterization of samples calcined at different temperatures were added in the Supplementary Information to clarify the selection of 400 °C as the optimized calcination temperature (Supplementary Text S1 and Figs. S2–S4). The XRD patterns (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. S5) confirm that (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4 exhibits diffraction peaks at positions identical to those of the four bimetallic oxides, 18.6° (111), 30.4° (220), 35.8° (311), 37.3° (222), 43.8° (400), 53.5° (422), 57.9° (511), and 62.5° (440). All peaks align well with the standard spinel reference of CoFe2O4 (PDF#22-1086), confirming the successful formation of a typical cubic spinel structure. Notably, in (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4, all diffraction peaks not only shifted slightly to lower angles but also broadened compared to those of CoFe2O4, which is a consequence of lattice distortion induced by multi-metal doping at the A-site2. The Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectra (Fig. 1c) reveal that (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4 exhibits prominent peaks at 3430 and 1620 cm−1, corresponding to the asymmetric stretching and bending vibrations of surface hydroxyl groups (–OH) and adsorbed water molecules, respectively33. No significant spectral differences are detected between (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4 and the bimetallic oxides (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. S6), indicating that the introduction of multiple metal elements did not alter the surface functional groups. As shown in the Raman spectra (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Fig. S7), (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4 and the bimetal oxides exhibit characteristic peaks at 345 cm−1 (Eg), 487 cm−1 (T2g), 564 cm−1 (T2g), 634 cm−1 (A1g), and 692 cm−1 (A1g), consistent with reported vibrational modes of spinel structures33. The A1g modes (634 and 692 cm−1) are primarily associated with symmetric stretching vibrations of metal-oxygen bonds in tetrahedral sites34. The preservation of these characteristic vibrational modes without additional impurity peaks confirms the structural integrity of the cubic spinel phase. Slight peak broadening for (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4 reflects local structural disorder arising from the high-entropy effect, rather than phase separation. These results collectively confirm the successful synthesis of a single-phase high-entropy spinel.

The morphology of synthesized catalysts was systematically characterized using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM). As shown in Fig. 1e, f and Supplementary Fig. S8, all samples exhibit a sponge-like porous morphology, endowing the catalysts with abundant porosity and high specific surface areas (SSA). High-resolution TEM (HRTEM) images and selected-area electron diffraction (SAED) patterns further confirm the crystalline nature of (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4 (Fig. 1g, h)35. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping (Supplementary Fig. S9) demonstrates a homogeneous distribution of Mn, Co, Ni, Cu, and Fe throughout (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4. Notably, the element contents of Cu, Ni, Mn, and Co approach near-equimolar proportions (6.18, 8.33, 6.67, and 8.05 wt%), facilitating the formation and stabilization of the high-entropy spinel phase. Nitrogen adsorption-desorption measurements were conducted to evaluate the pore characteristics. As depicted in Supplementary Fig. S10, all catalysts exhibit type IV isotherms with H3 hysteresis loops, indicating the presence of mesoporous structures36. (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4 possesses a SSA of 232 m2 g−1, significantly exceeding those of MnFe2O4 (145 m2 g−1), CuFe2O4 (115 m2 g−1), CoFe2O4 (96 m2 g−1), and NiFe2O4 (71 m2 g−1). This trend is consistent with previous reports on high-entropy oxides37. The high SSA of (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4 is mainly attributed to preserved meso-porosity during optimized low-temperature calcination. The homogeneous nitrate/citrate precursor promotes multi-cation mixing and gas release upon decomposition, while multiple cations may suppress severe crystallite coarsening. This leads to a higher SSA and more developed meso-porosity than the bimetallic ferrites, favoring catalyst-reactant interfacial contact during catalytic ozonation.

The chemical composition and elemental states of (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4 were investigated using extended X-ray absorption fine-structure (XAFS) spectroscopy and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). The X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) spectra (Fig. 1i) show that the Fe K-edge position of (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4 shifts slightly toward higher energy relative to CoFe2O4, approaching that of Fe2O3. This suggests that (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4 and CoFe2O4 exhibit an average oxidation state of Fe with a near +3 valence, and high-entropy doping leads to electron loss at the Fe site38. The local atomic environment was further probed through the Fourier transform (FT) analysis of the extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) at the Fe K-edge (Fig. 1j). The Fe K-edge FT-EXAFS spectrum of (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4 displays a broadened Fe–O peak relative to CoFe2O4, indicating increased local structural disorder around Fe sites. The appearance of a weak additional feature at higher radial distance further suggests perturbation of the Fe coordination environment induced by high-entropy incorporation39. These results were corroborated by wavelet transform (WT) analysis of the EXAFS data (Supplementary Fig. S11). Cu, Ni, Mn, and Co K-edge XAFS analyses further support the incorporation of these metals into the spinel framework (Supplementary Figs. S12–S15 and Table S1). XANES confirms their oxidized states, while FT-EXAFS/WT-EXAFS reveal TM–O and TM–TM scattering features without obvious metallic TM–TM or segregated oxide-domain signatures. First-shell EXAFS fitting using a tetrahedral TM–O model gives reasonable TM–O bond lengths and fitting residuals, supporting predominant A-site-related local coordination environments for Co, Cu, Ni, and Mn in the high-entropy spinel. These results, together with XRD, HRTEM/SAED, and homogeneous EDS mapping, support the formation of a high-entropy spinel framework rather than a simple mixture of bimetal oxides.

The XPS survey spectrum of (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4 (Supplementary Fig. S16) confirms the successful incorporation of Mn, Ni, and Cu into the high-entropy spinel structure. The Co 2p3/2 spectrum is fitted with two peaks at 780.2 eV (Co3+) and 782.1 eV (Co2+), and (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4 shows a notable increase in the relative content of Co2+ compared to CoFe2O4 (Supplementary Figs. S17 and S18)40. Analysis of the Fe 2p3/2 spectra (Supplementary Figs. S17 and S18) shows characteristic peaks at 710.2 eV (Fe2+) and 712.1 eV (Fe3+)41. Quantitative analysis demonstrates that the Fe2+/Fe3+ ratio in the high-entropy spinel (0.79) is lower than that in CoFe2O4 (0.86), consistent with the EXAFS analysis. Deconvolution of the Mn 2p3/2 peak reveals two components at 643.2 eV (Mn3+) and 641.1 eV (Mn2+)40. Similarly, (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4 demonstrates a higher Mn2+/Mn3+ ratio compared to MnFe2O4 (Supplementary Figs. S18 and S19), indicating a lower average oxidation state of Mn. For the Ni 2p3/2 region, peaks at 854.9 eV (Ni2+) and 856.7 eV (Ni3+) suggest an enhanced average oxidation state of Ni in (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4 relative to NiFe2O4 (Supplementary Figs. S18 and S20)42. The Cu 2p3/2 peak shifts slightly to a higher binding energy in the high-entropy spinel (from 933.8 to 934.5 eV), suggesting a decreased local electron density around the Cu ions (Supplementary Figs. S18 and S21). In summary, XPS analysis reveals that the average valence states of Co and Mn are reduced in (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4, while those of Fe, Ni, and Cu are increased compared to the corresponding bimetallic oxides. This opposite valence evolution suggests that the high-entropy environment does not merely perturb the local coordination structure, but also redistributes charge through the electronically coupled A–O–B network, laying the foundation for the distinct orbital responses and functional differentiation of Co/Mn and Ni/Cu sites.

Electronic structure and covalency calculations

Crystal models of the bimetallic oxides (CoFe2O4, MnFe2O4, NiFe2O4, and CuFe2O4) and the high-entropy (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4 were constructed (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. S22). The spinel structure adopts a cubic crystal system consisting of tetrahedrally coordinated A-sites and octahedrally coordinated B-sites. The optimized configurations (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. S23) reveal significant lattice distortion in (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4, which aligns well with the characterization results. Since the A–O–B covalency reflects the electron transfer between A and B sites12, the electronic structure was investigated by computing the projected density of states (PDOS) for metallic elements and oxygen in both high-entropy and bimetallic oxides. From PDOS, the d-band centers (Ed) of TMs and the p-band centers (Ep) of oxygen were determined. The covalency of the TM–O bonds was quantified by the energy difference (ΔE = |Ed − Ep | ), where a smaller value of ΔE indicates stronger covalency. Within the spinel framework, A–O and B–O bonds exhibit distinct covalent strengths, and the maximum ΔE value among these bonds was used as an indicator of the overall A–O–B electronic coupling strength20,21. Analysis of the maximum ΔE values of these bonds reveals the following trend (Fig. 2b, c, and Supplementary Fig. S24): (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4 (1.15 eV) < CoFe2O4 (1.40 eV) < NiFe2O4 (1.61 eV) < MnFe2O4 (3.26 eV) < CuFe2O4 (3.91 eV). (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4 exhibits the smallest ΔE, demonstrating the highest A–O–B covalency and strongest electronic communication among different TM sites. This conclusion is further supported by electron localization function (ELF) analysis (Fig. 2d). The ELF plots reveal higher maximum values for all A–O bonds and enhanced electron density between TM and O in (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4, which facilitates electron transfer between TM centers19. The crystal orbital Hamilton population (COHP) analysis was employed to assess bond strength. The larger integrated −COHP (−ICOHP) values for TM–O bonds in the high-entropy spinel (Fig. 2e) further indicate strengthened orbital hybridization and enhanced covalent interaction. Furthermore, electron counting for the A-site TMs (Fig. 2f) shows a relative increase in electron density at Co and Mn centers, accompanied by electron depletion at Fe, Ni, and Cu sites in the high-entropy structure compared to the corresponding bimetal oxides. Collectively, these results substantiate electron exchange and redistribution among the different TM cations in (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4, consistent with the EXAFS and XPS analysis. Consequently, high-entropy doping establishes a sublattice-resolved electronically differentiated TM configuration, featuring electron-enriched Co/Mn centers and electron-depleted Fe/Cu/Ni centers, as depicted in Fig. 2g. This asymmetric charge redistribution can be attributed to the high-entropy-enhanced A–O–B covalency and the distinct orbital responses of the incorporated cations within the electronically coupled spinel lattice. Strengthened TM–O hybridization facilitates intersite electron exchange through oxygen bridges, whereas differences in orbital-energy alignment determine the direction of charge redistribution. As a result, Co and Mn retain relatively higher 3d electron density, as supported by their reduced average valence states and increased electron counting values, while Ni and Cu become relatively electron-deficient, as reflected by their increased oxidation states and decreased electron density. Catalytic performance

The catalytic performance of CoFe2O4 and (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4 was evaluated for the removal of sulfur-containing compound (CH3SH) under ambient conditions (RH = 50%) in a continuous flow fixed-bed microreactor (diameter of 10 mm). As shown in Fig. 3a, (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4 achieved 71% of CH3SH removal under a N2 atmosphere and about 99% removal in an O3 atmosphere, significantly outperforming CoFe2O4 (11% and 51%, respectively). It should be noted that the comparison between N2 and O3 atmospheres was designed to distinguish CH3SH adsorption/limited surface transformation from O3-assisted catalytic oxidation. Under N2 atmosphere, no external oxidant was introduced; therefore, the decrease in gaseous CH3SH concentration mainly reflects adsorption and limited surface-induced transformation rather than deep oxidation. The corresponding mass activities are presented in Fig. 3b. Since (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4 possesses a much larger Brunauer-Emmett-Teller specific surface area (BET SSA) than CoFe2O4, the activity was further normalized by SSA for a surface-area-normalized comparison. After normalization, the activities of (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4 and CoFe2O4 are 12.28 and 4.38 ppm m−2 under N2 atmosphere, respectively, whereas under O3 atmosphere they are 16.98 and 21.04 ppm m−2, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S25). These results indicate that the catalytic performance of (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4 under ozonation cannot be simply ascribed to a universally higher area-normalized activity. Under an O3 atmosphere, (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4 already achieves nearly complete CH3SH removal under the present conditions, and the corresponding normalized activity is therefore limited by the conversion upper bound of the reaction system. As a result, this metric does not fully reflect the performance advantage of the high-entropy catalyst under an O3 atmosphere. The catalytic performance of single-A-site ferrites and lower-complexity mixed-A-site ferrites was systematically evaluated to determine whether the performance of (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4 originates simply from mixed-metal substitution. For the single-A-site ferrites, MnFe2O4, NiFe2O4, and CuFe2O4 showed CH3SH removal efficiencies of 11%, 21%, and 23% under N2 atmosphere, and 42%, 32%, and 39% under O3 atmosphere, respectively (Supplementary Figs. S26 and S27). In addition, a series of dual-A-site mixed ferrites, including (CuCo)Fe2O4, (NiMn)Fe2O4, (CuNi)Fe2O4, (CuMn)Fe2O4, (CoNi)Fe2O4, and (CoMn)Fe2O4, were further examined (Supplementary Fig. S28). These dual-A-site ferrites showed moderate CH3SH removal under N2 atmosphere and improved activity under O3 atmosphere, indicating that lower-complexity mixed-metal substitution can partially enhance CH3SH adsorption and catalytic ozonation. However, none of these single- or dual-A-site ferrites reached the performance of high-entropy (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4. These results demonstrate that simple mixed-metal substitution can partially improve CH3SH removal, whereas the high-entropy configuration is required to achieve the optimal catalytic performance within the investigated catalyst set. Although (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4 exhibits the highest removal capacity of CH3SH both with and without O3, its CH3SH mineralization efficiency under O3 atmosphere (68.5%) is significantly higher than that under N2 (4.2%), underscoring the essential role of O3 in achieving deep oxidation (Supplementary Fig. S29)43. This marked difference between removal and mineralization under N2 indicates that adsorption alone cannot account for efficient sulfur elimination, and that O3-mediated oxidation dominates the mineralization process. These results suggest that high-entropy doping significantly enhances both the catalytic activity for O3 activation and the affinity for CH3SH. The effect of RH on the catalytic performance of (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4 was also investigated. As depicted in Fig. 3c, CH3SH removal remains near 100% as RH rises from 5% to 90%, indicating that H2O molecules have a negligible influence on its activity. Water-contact-angle measurements (Supplementary Fig. S31) show that (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4 has a slightly larger contact angle than CoFe2O4 (137.1° vs 132.9°), suggesting relatively weaker water affinity and reduced competitive water adsorption under humid conditions. This result demonstrates that moisture is not a strong competitive interferent for the present catalyst, supporting its robust tolerance toward humidity under realistic operating conditions. Furthermore, (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4 was loaded onto an Al2O3 carrier (named (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4@Al2O3) to evaluate its practical application potential. Catalyst stability was evaluated under demanding conditions. (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4@Al2O3 maintained a CH3SH removal of 96.8% after 48 h of continuous operation (Fig. 3d). To further evaluate the performance level of (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4, its catalytic ozonation performance was benchmarked against representative CH3SH catalytic ozonation systems reported in the literature (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Table S2). Overall, (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4/O3 exhibits competitive CH3SH removal efficiency, high mineralization efficiency, broad humidity tolerance, and long-term stability, highlighting its potential for practical catalytic ozonation of sulfur-containing VOCs. Figure 3f shows the recyclability of (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4@Al2O3, and Fig. 3g illustrates the experimental setup. Specifically, 50 ppm of O3 and 40 ppm of CH3SH were fully mixed in the gas mixer, and the gaseous mixture was introduced into the cubic reactor (volume: 1.3 m3). When the CH3SH concentration inside the reactor reached 40 ppm, the gas supply was halted, and the system was sealed. Figure 3f shows that the CH3SH concentration decreased gradually and was eliminated after approximately 30 min. In contrast, without O3 introduction, the CH3SH concentration only declined to about 25 ppm over the same duration. After seven recycles, the CH3SH removal rate still exceeds 96%, demonstrating the potential for practical application of the (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4/O3 system. Besides gas-phase CH3SH removal, the applicability of (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4 was further evaluated using representative aqueous organic pollutants, including phenol and bisphenol A (BPA) as phenolic/endocrine-disrupting compounds, and tetracycline (TC) as typical tetracycline antibiotics. (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4 enhanced the ozonation degradation of all tested pollutants compared with O3 alone, suggesting its potential for aqueous organic-pollutant treatment (Supplementary Fig. S32).

Cyclic voltammetry (CV) and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) were performed to assess the intrinsic electron transfer capability of the catalysts. As shown in Fig. 4a, (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4 exhibits a significantly higher current response than CoFe2O4, and its EIS Nyquist plot (Fig. 4b) reveals a lower charge-transfer resistance44. As illustrated in Supplementary Fig. S33, the electrochemical properties of other bimetallic oxides are also inferior to (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4. These results suggest a slightly improved interfacial charge-transfer response of (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4 compared with the binary ferrites. Chronoamperometric (i-t) measurements were performed to track the electron transfer during catalytic ozonation. As shown in Fig. 4c and Supplementary Fig. S34, the injection of saturated aqueous O3 solution at 100 s and sodium methanethiolate (CH3SNa) at 200 s elicited distinct current fluctuations. The current variation detected at 100 s corresponds to electron transfer from the catalyst to O3, and the fluctuation at 200 s confirms the electron transfer from CH3SNa to the catalyst2. The normalized i-t response in Fig. 4c shows distinct interactions with O3 and CH3SNa. The moderate O3-induced current variation and slightly improved interfacial charge-transfer response are consistent with controlled electron transfer for stabilizing surface-bound *O3 rather than intensive O–O bond cleavage. In contrast, the stronger CH3SNa-induced response indicates efficient electron exchange with sulfur-containing species45. Crucially, (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4 exhibits a significantly stronger response than all bimetallic oxides (Supplementary Fig. S35). The signal intensity induced by O3 follows the sequence of (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4 > CoFe2O4 > MnFe2O4 > NiFe2O4 > CuFe2O4, while the CH3SNa-induced signal shows a different order of (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4 > CuFe2O4 > NiFe2O4 > MnFe2O4 > CoFe2O4, the latter trend matching the CH3SH removal under N2. Specifically, Cu and Ni exhibit stronger interaction with CH3SH, whereas Co and Mn show higher propensity for O3 activation. Owing to the electron redistribution facilitated by high-entropy doping, (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4 exhibits strong simultaneous affinity for O3 (preferring electron-rich Co/Mn sites) and CH3SH (preferring electron-deficient Cu/Ni sites)2. Consequently, although each A-site TM in (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4 constitutes only a quarter of that in the corresponding bimetallic oxides, it demonstrates the highest electronic response toward O3 and CH3SH. In conventional catalytic ozonation, bond cleavage of O3 generates the primary products of *O and 1O2, and *O can further be converted to other reactive oxygen species (ROS) such as •OH and O2•−, collectively contributing to pollutant degradation1,29. Therefore, quenching experiments were conducted to identify the reason for the CH3SH degradation over (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4 and CoFe2O4 under O3. Specifically, dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), 4-hydroxy-2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidin-1-oxyl (TEMPOL), furfuryl alcohol (FFA), and tert-butyl alcohol (TBA) were utilized as quenchers for *O, O2•−, 1O2, and •OH, respectively3,46,47. Quenching experiments and concentration-dependent evaluation of quencher experiments are provided in Supplementary Text S2 and Fig. S36. In the CoFe2O4/O3 system (Fig. 4d), DMSO caused the most severe inhibition (~20% removal), approaching the removal rate of O3 alone, which implicates that *O is a key ROS, while other quenchers had moderate effects. A markedly different trend was obtained in the (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4/O3 system (Fig. 4e), where all quenchers showed negligible impact. Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectra further confirmed minimal generation of 1O2, O2•−, and •OH in the (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4/O3 system compared to the CoFe2O4/O3 system (Supplementary Fig. S37). In situ Raman spectroscopy (Fig. 4f) shows that the *O3 peak appeared at 641 cm−1 and no signals of *O (864 cm−1) or *O2 (984 cm−1) were detected over (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O42. In contrast, both peaks of *O and *O2 appeared over CoFe2O4, but no *O3 signal was found, confirming O3 dissociation. Collectively, these results strongly indicate that O3 is predominantly converted as a surface-activated *O3 complex on (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4, rather than undergoing dissociation15. Furthermore, open-circuit potential (OCP) measurements (Supplementary Fig. S38) tracked the interfacial electron state. The (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4/O3 system showed a significant potential promotion upon adding O3, demonstrating the formation of (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4/*O3 complexes. The subsequent sharp potential drop upon the CH3SNa addition marks reductive decomposition of the complex via ETP from the pollutant11. In contrast, the CoFe2O4/O3 system exhibited a lower potential change after the addition of O3, which could be attributed to the limited formation of *O and *O2, indicating the stronger electron interaction between (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4 and O3.

To elucidate the CH3SH degradation pathway, in situ diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform spectroscopy (in situ DRIFTS) was employed. Under pure N2 (Supplementary Fig. S39), peaks at 2922 and 2839 cm−1 were assigned to asymmetric (νₛ(C–H)) and symmetric (νₐₛ(C–H)) stretching vibrations of adsorbed methyl groups (–CH3)2,48, and an adsorption peak at 956 cm⁻1 indicated the presence of CH3SSCH31. Under continuous co-feeding of O3 and CH3SH (Fig. 4g), new vibrational peaks emerged at 1429 and 1568 cm⁻1, assigned to the symmetric (νₛ(COO−)) and asymmetric (νₐₛ(COO−)) stretching vibrations of carboxylate species, respectively49. Peaks at 1677 and 1773 cm−1 were attributed to C=O stretching vibrations32. The broad and faint peak around 2600–3100 cm−1 and the strong peak at 1342 cm−1 corresponded to the stretching vibration (ν(OH)) and in-plane bending vibration (δ(OH)), respectively. The peaks at 1201 and 1052 cm−1 were assigned to asymmetric (νₐₛ) and symmetric (νₛ) stretching vibrations of the S = O bond, and an additional peak at 795 cm−1 was attributed to an S–O stretching vibration8. Besides, the XPS survey (Supplementary Fig. S40) and S 2p spectrum (Fig. 4h) of (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4 after catalytic ozonation show an inconspicuous sulfur peak at 166.4 eV, indicating that sulfur exists in +4 valence and does not deposit on active TM sites. By comparison, the valence of +6 sulfur was detected in CoFe2O4 after reaction (Supplementary Fig. S41). These results confirmed the degradation of CH3SH, wherein carbon was progressively converted into carboxylates (COO−) and ultimately mineralized into carbon dioxide (CO2). Besides, sulfur was oxidized to sulfur dioxide (SO2), which subsequently reacts with water molecules to form bisulfite (HSO3−). It is noteworthy that almost no signal of sulfate (SO42−, 168–169 eV) was detected on the catalyst, indicating that HSO3− readily desorbs from (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4. This effectively prevents the poisoning of metal active sites by SO42−. As for the CoFe2O4/O3 system (Fig. 4i), two new peaks at 1305 and 862 cm⁻1 are attributed to *O2 and *O50, further demonstrating O3 dissociation. Additionally, no HSO3− and C=O signals were detected, indicating limited mineralization. The composition of organic compounds in the effluent gas after 1 h of reaction was analyzed using proton transfer reaction time-of-flight mass spectrometry (PTR-TOF-MS). In the (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4/N2 system, the primary intermediates were (H2S)+ (m/z = 34), (CH3SH)H+ (m/z = 49), (C2H6S)H+ (m/z = 63), and (CH3SSCH3)H+ (m/z = 95), at concentrations of 3.2, 14.6, 2.5, and 2.8 ppm, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S42)2,51. DFT calculations for mechanism elucidation

Electrostatic potential (ESP) analysis (Fig. 5a) shows that ozone activation can adopt a single-end or dual-end O3 adsorption14,31, and CH3SH tends to adsorb onto TM sites via its S atom12. Considering the inherent inter-site (A–B) distance in the spinel structure matches the molecular dimensions of O3, both modes are geometrically feasible. DFT calculations reveal that the adsorption energies (Eads) of O3 on Co and Fe sites in a single-end configuration are −0.31 and −1.32 eV, respectively, while the Eads of dual-end adsorption on the Co–Fe sites is −1.54 eV (Fig. 5b). This indicates a clear thermodynamic preference for the dual-end adsorption mode on the spinel. The calculated Eads of CH3SH were −0.99 eV on Co and −0.58 eV on Fe (Supplementary Fig. S43), suggesting that O3 and CH3SH undergo competitive adsorption on CoFe2O4, which diminished degradation efficiency. To elucidate the effect of high-entropy doping, we further computed the adsorption behaviors on (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4. As shown in Fig. 5c, the Eads of O3 on different dual sites of Fe–Co, Fe–Mn, Fe–Ni, and Fe–Cu sites are −1.65, −1.38, −0.86, and −0.89 eV, respectively. In contrast, the Eads of CH3SH on Co, Mn, Fe, Ni, and Cu sites are −0.21, −0.18, −0.48, −0.93, and −0.97 eV, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S44). Based on the DFT analysis, Co/Mn-containing motifs are predicted to preferentially stabilize O3 adsorption via cooperative A–O–B interactions, whereas Cu/Ni sites exhibit a stronger affinity toward CH3SH. Gas-phase tests using CH3SH and O3 as probe molecules were further performed to validate the site-differentiated adsorption/activation behavior (Supplementary Fig. S45). Under an O3-free N2 atmosphere, (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4 exhibited the highest CH3SH removal and cumulative uptake, followed by CuFe2O4 and NiFe2O4, supporting the preferential interaction of CH3SH with Cu/Ni-related sites. Considering the CH3SNa-induced current response and CH3SSCH3 intermediates detected under N2, this uptake reflects adsorption/chemisorption coupled with slight surface conversion. In the O3-only test, CoFe2O4 and MnFe2O4 showed higher O3 conversion, indicating stronger dissociative O3 consumption. In contrast, (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4 showed moderate O3 conversion, while in situ Raman confirmed surface-bound *O3 formation rather than *O/*O2 generation. These results demonstrate that high-entropy-induced electronic communication enhances CH3SH adsorption on Cu/Ni-related sites and regulates O3 activation on Fe–Co/Mn motifs toward a non-dissociative *O3 pathway. The catalytic comparisons with the lower-complexity mixed-metal ferrites discussed above further support that simple mixed-metal substitution can only partially reproduce the activity enhancement, whereas the high-entropy configuration enables more effective cooperative site functions (Supplementary Fig. S28). These results demonstrate that among the A-site metals, Co and Mn exhibit a stronger affinity for O3 adsorption, whereas Ni and Cu favor CH3SH adsorption, consistent with the electrochemical characterizations and experimental results. The high A–O–B covalency on (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4 facilitates an ETP between CH3SH and O3, which is strongly supported by the electron transfer from CH3SH to the catalyst and the catalyst to O3 (Fig. 5d). When O3 and CH3SH co-adsorb on their respective favorable sites, the combined Eads increases to −2.29 eV. Differential charge analysis reveals significantly enhanced electron interaction among the catalyst, O3, and CH3SH compared to individual adsorption (Fig. 5d). Such cooperative co-adsorption provides a thermodynamically favorable configuration for interfacial electron transfer. This configuration facilitates the oxidation of CH3SH to CO2, SO2, and H2O at Cu/Ni sites via the ETP. The produced SO2 hydrolyzes to form HSO3⁻, which, due to its relatively low Eads52, readily desorbs from Cu and Ni sites rather than being converted to SO42– (Supplementary Figs. S46 and S47). To gain deeper insight into the distinct O3 activation mechanisms on CoFe2O4 and (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4, we analyzed their projected density of states (PDOS). The spin-up orbitals show that compared to CoFe2O4, the Co 3d orbital of (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4 shifts toward the Fermi level, and the electron density of the Fe 3d orbital near the Fermi level decreases significantly (Fig.5e). The calculated d-band centers shift from −2.56 eV (Co) and −2.09 eV (Fe) in CoFe2O4 to −1.03 eV (Co) and −2.12 eV (Fe) in (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4 (Supplementary Fig. S48). Orbital-resolved analysis shows that high-entropy doping elevates the energy levels of the Co t2g orbitals (dxy, dyz, dxz), increasing electron density near the Fermi level, while depleting it in the Fe eg orbitals (dx2 − y2, dz2) (Fig. 5f, g). These electronic modifications imply that in CoFe2O4, Fe dominates the interaction with O3, whereas in (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4, the role of Co is enhanced while that of Fe is slightly attenuated. Differential charge and Bader charge analyses (Fig. 5h) confirm significant electron transfer from both catalysts to O3, with values of 0.44 e⁻ and 0.51 e⁻ for CoFe2O4 and (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4, respectively, indicating a more effective O3 activation. Bond length analysis further reveals the adsorption geometry: upon O3 adsorption on CoFe2O4, the Co–O and Fe–O distances are 2.34 Å and 1.88 Å, respectively, while on (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4, they become 2.28 and 1.92 Å. The two O–O bonds in adsorbed O3 are 1.31 and 1.43 Å on CoFe2O4, and 1.34 and 1.40 Å on (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4. This reduced asymmetry in O–O bond length on (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4 reflects a more balanced dual-end pulling effect, which activates the O3 molecule but not cleavage. Orbital interaction analysis indicates that high-entropy doping raises the energy levels of the spin-up orbitals of Co, which leads to elevated molecular orbital (MO) and anti-bonding (MO*) levels upon hybridization with O3 and reduced electron filling in the MO*. In contrast, the Fe hybrid orbitals slightly shift downward, increasing the electron population in the MO* (Fig. 6a–c and Supplementary Fig. S49). This electronic redistribution disfavors O–O bond rupture. Consistently, the crystal orbital Hamiltonian population (COHP) analysis (Fig. 6d, e) shows the integrated −COHP values (−ICOHP) for Fe–O bonds are 4.88 and 5.06 eV, and for Co–O bonds are 2.59 and 1.86 eV, in (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4 and CoFe2O4, respectively. These electronic changes collectively drive the shift in O3 activation from dissociation to *O3. Additionally, the PDOS in Fig. 5e shows that Mn also exhibits considerable electron density near the Fermi level, second only to Co and Fe, indicating its non-negligible role in O3 activation. In contrast, Ni and Cu exhibit peaks at deeper energy levels, suggesting lower catalytic activity and a tendency to adsorb electron-rich CH3SH11,13. Additional magnetic, ESR, O2 temperature-programmed desorption (O2-TPD), and NH3-TPD measurements were further conducted to examine possible changes in surface chemistry and magnetic/electronic characteristics. The altered magnetization behavior (Supplementary Fig. S50) indicates that high-entropy incorporation modifies the magnetic coupling and local spin/electronic environment of the transition-metal centers, consistent with the PDOS results showing redistributed metal 3d states near the Fermi level. The ESR signal further suggests a modified defect-related electronic environment (Supplementary Fig. S51). Meanwhile, O2-TPD and NH3-TPD profiles (Supplementary Fig. S52) reveal changes in oxygen adsorption/desorption behavior and acid-site distribution. These results confirm that the high-entropy spinel undergoes multiple surface-chemistry reconstructions beyond A–O–B covalency modulation. However, these variations do not change the proposed dominant mechanism. Although oxygen vacancies, lattice oxygen activity, and Lewis acid sites may participate in O3 adsorption and activation, previous studies on catalytic O3 decomposition and catalytic ozonation have shown that such defect- or acid-site-related pathways are often accompanied by intermediate oxygen species, peroxide/superoxide species, or ROS generation5,44,53,54. In contrast, the oxygen-vacancy EPR, quenching experiments, ROS-trapping EPR spectra, and in situ Raman results in this work collectively indicate that (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4 preferentially stabilizes surface-bound *O3 rather than promoting dissociative O3 activation. DFT calculations elucidate that in (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4, the asymmetric electronic structure creates specialized sites: Fe-Co/Mn pairs activate O3 into a stabilized *O3 complex, while Cu/Ni sites adsorb CH3SH. This site differentiation enables spatially separated, non-competitive adsorption, facilitating an efficient ETP pathway for CH3SH oxidation and effectively circumventing sulfate poisoning.

Discussion

This work demonstrates that high-entropy doping provides an effective strategy for precisely modulating the electronic structure of spinel oxides to steer the ozone activation pathway toward the formation of a reactive *O3 complex. DFT calculations and characterizations reveal that the high-entropy doping facilitates electron communication between TM sites, leading to the results that Fe–Co and Fe–Mn pairs act as active centers for *O3 formation, while Ni and Cu sites preferentially adsorb CH3SH. Such a spatial separation of functions effectively prevents competitive adsorption between O3 and CH3SH, achieving mineralization efficiency via an ETP. This work not only presents a high-performance catalyst for removing reduced VSCs but also offers a general design strategy, high-entropy doping, that can be extended to other catalytic ozonation targeting electron-rich pollutants.

Chemicals

All chemicals used in this study were analytical grade as received. Iron (III) nitrate nonahydrate (Fe(NO3)3·9H2O, 99.9%), manganese (II) acetate tetrahydrate (Mn(CH3COO)2·4H2O, 99.0%), copper (II) nitrate monohydrate (Cu(NO3)2·H2O, 99.9%), cobalt (II) nitrate hexahydrate (Co(NO3)2·6H2O, 99.9%), nickel (II) nitrate hexahydrate (Ni(NO3)2·6H2O, 99.9%), and citric acid (C6H8O7, 99.5%), 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline-N-oxide (DMPO, 97.0%), 2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidine (TEMP, 98.0%) 4-hydroxy-2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidine-1-oxyl (TEMPOL, 10 mM in DMSO), tert-butyl alcohol (TBA, 99.5%), and methanol (CH3OH, 99.5%) were purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co. Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, 99.8%) was provided by Adamas-beta, and Furfuryl alcohol (FFA, 98.0%) was obtained from Energy Chemical. Ultrapure water with a resistivity of 18.2 MΩ cm at 25 °C was used throughout the experiments.

Catalyst synthesis

The synthesis procedure of high-entropy spinel (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4 is shown in Fig. S1. Precursors, including iron (III) nitrate nonahydrate (Fe(NO3)3·9H2O, 4 mmol), manganese (II) acetate tetrahydrate (Mn(CH3COO)2·4H2O, 0.5 mmol), copper (II) nitrate monohydrate (Cu(NO3)2·H2O, 0.5 mmol), cobalt (II) nitrate hexahydrate (Co(NO3)2·6H2O, 0.5 mmol), nickel (II) nitrate hexahydrate (Ni(NO3)2·6H2O, 0.5 mmol), and citric acid (C6H8O7, 3.15 g), were thoroughly mixed by mechanical grinding until a homogeneous dark brown viscous gel formed. The resulting mixture was then transferred into a 200 mL crucible and subjected to calcination in a muffle furnace. The calcination protocol consisted of heating to 400 °C at a ramp rate of 2 °C/min, followed by an isothermal hold at this temperature for 2 h. Subsequently, the furnace was allowed to cool naturally to room temperature. Finally, the calcined black product was ground to obtain the high-entropy spinel (CuNiMnCo)Fe2O4 powder. The bimetallic oxides, CoFe2O4, MnFe2O4, NiFe2O4, and CuFe2O4 were synthesized using an analogous procedure, and the sole modification was the substitution of the metal salt precursors. Specifically, 2 mmol of the respective single transition metal salt was used in place of the metal salt mixture, while maintaining the iron nitrate (Fe(NO3)3·9H2O, 4 mmol) and citric acid (C6H8O7, 3.15 g) quantities.

Characterization of catalysts

The crystal structure and phase composition of the samples were analyzed using an X-ray diffractometer (XRD, Ultima IV, Japan) equipped with a Cu Kα radiation source. The diffraction patterns were collected at room temperature over a 2θ range of 5–80° at a scanning rate of 10° min⁻1. Samples calcined at different temperatures were cooled to room temperature before conventional ex situ XRD measurements. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR, EQUINOX 55, Germany) was used to analyze the surface functional groups of the catalysts. Raman spectra of the catalysts were collected by a laser confocal Raman spectrometer (Raman, inVia Qontor, Renishaw Plc, England) under the excitation of a 532 nm laser. The morphology and elemental composition of the catalysts were analyzed using field emission scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Gemini 500, Germany) with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS). Further microstructural analysis was carried out by transmission electron microscopy (TEM), selected-area electron diffraction (SAED), and scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) on a transmission electron microscope (TEM, Tecnai G2 F30 S-Twin, American). N2 adsorption-desorption isotherms were measured at 77 K using a Micromeritics ASAP 2460 analyzer (American). Before measurement, the samples were degassed under vacuum at 200 °C for 8 h. The specific surface areas were calculated using the Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) method. Samples calcined at different temperatures were analyzed under identical measurement conditions. Composition and content of each element were analyzed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo Scientific K-Alpha, America), and the obtained data were analyzed by Avantage (version 5.9). X-ray absorption fine structure (XAFS) spectra, including X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) and extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS), were collected on an easyXAFS 300+ spectrometer (easyXAFS, LLC, USA), and the obtained data were analyzed by Athena (version 0.9.26) and Artemis (version 0.9.26). Electrochemical tests were performed using a three-electrode quartz cell system: a saturated calomel electrode (SCE) as the reference electrode, a platinum plate as the counter electrode, indium tin oxide (ITO) glass coated on the sample as the working electrode, and a 0.5 mol L−1 Na2SO4 solution as the electrolyte. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) were detected by the electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR, A300, Germany). DMPO was used as a spin trap for •OH (in aqueous solution) and •O2⁻ (in methanol), while TEMP was employed to detect 1O2 in aqueous medium. Gas-phase in situ diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform spectroscopy (DRIFTS) was performed on a Bruker TENSOR II spectrometer. The catalyst was first exposed to 40 ppm CH3SH/N2 to record CH3SH adsorption, and then the gas stream was switched to a mixed flow containing 40 ppm CH3SH and 50 ppm O3 at the same relative humidity to monitor the catalytic ozonation process. In situ Raman spectra were acquired using a Renishaw in Via spectrometer (UK) with a 532 nm laser under various atmospheres at a gas flow rate of 60 mL min⁻1. The exhausted gas after reaction was analyzed using a PTR-TOF-MS instrument (PTR-TOF, 1000Ionicon Analytik GmbH, Austria), and the obtained data were analyzed by PTRMS-viewer (version 3.2). Thermogravimetric and differential scanning calorimetry (TG-DSC) analyses were performed using a HITACHI STA200l (Japan) under an air atmosphere at a gas flow rate of 50 mL min⁻1. Approximately 5.94 mg of precursor was heated from 30 to 800 °C at a heating rate of 5 °C min⁻1, and an alumina crucible was used during the measurement.

Quenching experiments

Quenching experiments were performed to comparatively evaluate the possible contributions of reactive oxygen species during CH3SH catalytic ozonation. Catalyst powder was immersed in 2 mM aqueous solutions of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), furfuryl alcohol (FFA), tert-butanol (TBA), or 4-hydroxy-2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidine-1-oxyl (TEMPOL) for 2 h and subsequently dried at 60 °C for 4 h. DMSO, FFA, TBA, and TEMPOL were used as operational probes for surface-bound atomic oxygen (*O), singlet oxygen (1O2), hydroxyl radicals (·OH), and superoxide radicals (O2·⁻), respectively. A water-treated control catalyst was prepared using the same immersion and drying procedure without the addition of a quencher. The quencher-treated and water-treated catalysts were evaluated under the same gas-phase catalytic ozonation conditions as the untreated catalyst: CH3SH concentration = 40 ppm, O3 concentration = 50 ppm, total gas flow rate = 100 mL min⁻1, relative humidity = 50%, room temperature, and catalyst dosage = 10 mg. To optimize the quencher concentration, preliminary experiments were conducted using DMSO and FFA solutions at concentrations of 1, 2, and 5 mM, following the same immersion and drying procedure described above. In addition, O3-only control experiments were performed in the absence of CH3SH using the untreated catalyst, and catalysts pretreated with 2 mM DMSO or FFA. For these measurements, 50 ppm O3 was passed through the catalyst bed at a total gas flow rate of 100 mL min−1 and a relative humidity of 50%, and the outlet O3 concentration was continuously monitored.

Catalytic performance test

The selection of CH3SH as the target pollutant was driven by its electron-rich structure, coupled with its substantial environmental relevance due to a very low odor threshold, prevalence in various industries, and associated health risks. The catalytic ozonation performance of catalysts for CH3SH is carried out at room temperature in a continuous flow fixed-bed quartz microreactor (diameter of 10 mm). In a typical experiment, a certain amount of catalyst was added to the center of the reactor and fixed with Cotton. The intake humidity is regulated by a humidity generator. Ozone generator (YDG, YE-TG-02PII) was used to generate 500 ppm of O3, and the O3 flow rate is controlled at 10 mL min−1 through a mass flow controller. To maintain a CH3SH inlet concentration of 40 ppm, CH3SH was diluted with air at an initial concentration of 1000 ppm. The total intake flow rate was controlled at 100 mL min−1 through a mass flow controller. To prevent atmospheric release, the exhaust gas was subjected to treatment with spent catalyst/an alkaline solution and activated carbon after analysis. Continuous monitoring of CH3SH concentration at the inlet (C0) and outlet (C) using a CH3SH sensor (Detcon, DM-400IS). Density functional theory (DFT) calculations

All spin-polarized density functional theory calculations were performed using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP, version 5.4). The generalized gradient approximation with the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof exchange-correlation functional was used. The van der Waals interactions were described using the Grimme D2 correction method. The projector augmented-wave method was employed to describe the interactions between ionic cores and valence electrons. The valence electron density was expanded using a plane-wave basis set with a kinetic energy cutoff of 450 eV. A Gaussian smearing width of 0.2 eV was adopted, and a 3 × 3 × 1 k-point mesh was used for sampling the Brillouin zone. A vacuum layer of 20.4 × 11.8 × 23.9 Å was applied along the surface-normal direction to avoid interactions between periodic images. During structural optimization, the bottom atomic layers of the catalyst were fixed, while the remaining atoms were fully relaxed. During geometry optimization, the convergence criteria were set to 0.01 eV Å−1 for atomic forces and 10−5 eV for energy. The same computational settings were used for the bare catalyst, isolated adsorbates, and adsorption complexes unless otherwise specified. The atomic coordinates of the optimized computational models are provided in the Supplementary Data 1. ELF analyses were performed using VESTA (version 3.3). COHP analysis was performed using LOBSTER (version 5.1), based on the wavefunction files obtained from VASP calculations.
