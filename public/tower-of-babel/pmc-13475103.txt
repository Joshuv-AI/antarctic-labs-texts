What is the benefit of visiting a doctor, if we cannot afford a diagnostic test or buy medicine? An exploratory study of equity in antimicrobial resistance among the urban informal settlements of Dhaka
Authors: Syeda Tahmina Ahmed, Farha Musharrat Noor, Sneha Paul, Bachera Aktar, Nahitun Naher, Rosie Steege, Katy Davis, Meenakshi Monga, Sally Theobald, Khaleda Islam, Rifat Afroze, Easrat Jahan Esha, S M Shahriar Rizvi, Nure Alam Siddiky, Syed Masud Ahmed, Sabina Faiz Rashid
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAntimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a critical health problem worldwide. In low and middle-income countries, over-the-counter sales of antibiotics occur at the patient’s request or on the recommendation of a community service provider. This qualitative study explored how social, structural and behavioural determinants among the population and informal providers contribute to AMR risk in urban informal settlements in Bangladesh.MethodsWe conducted focus group discussions and in-depth interviews (25 IDIs and 6 FGDs; 31 in total) with community people and informal healthcare providers from two settlements in Dhaka North and South City Corporations, following a thematic analysis of coded transcripts according to predetermined themes of ‘healthcare access’ and ‘challenges’, ‘autonomy’, ‘gendered roles and norms’ in urban low-income communities.ResultsIn both settlements, year-round respiratory and water-borne diseases were common, such as diarrheal episodes, jaundice and influenza. Children frequently suffered respiratory infections, while the elderly grappled with chronic diseases. Readymade garment factory workers, mostly women, regularly developed urinary tract infections and physical injuries from long-seated shifts and minimal health breaks (water intake, bathroom breaks and movement), leading to repeated antibiotic courses. Women, financially dependent and resource-constrained, often engaged in non-recommended use of antibiotics (using leftover antibiotics and self-regulating doses and courses), except when in their caregiving role. Men generally controlled household spending but postponed seeking formal care and consulted unqualified providers for affordability and convenience. Both discontinued antibiotics when they felt slightly better. For working men and women, rigid facility hours that coincided with work schedules and a lack of gender-sensitive services were cited as barriers. Widespread non-recommended antibiotic use was reported by both community members and informal providers.ConclusionsOur study highlights the importance of gender norms and behaviours, gendered livelihoods and the role of informal service providers in managing AMR in Bangladesh.Keywords: Disease Transmission, Infectious; Environmental Exposure; Community Health; Health Services Accessibility; Public Health

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is one of the top 10 global public health threats affecting populations disproportionately.1 Southeast Asia bears an excessive burden as antimicrobials are widely available here in the form of over-the-counter (OTC) drugs at local medicine shops and community pharmacies.2–4 Disaggregated surveillance data are a critical step to identify who will be the most affected; however, facility-based surveillance data may not be representative of the burden of AMR among the community, nor allow for an exploration of social drivers.5,6

Intersectional gender equity is increasingly considered for improving the health of populations through disease prevention and control.7 This approach has been utilised in studying complex topics like gender-indigeneity, intimate partner violence in Bangladesh,8,9 but has had limited use in AMR research, policies and interventions. This represents a critical missed opportunity to tailor AMR interventions for improving programmatic effectiveness and sustainability and avoid further entrenchment.10,11 Social factors like gender or poverty are often overlooked in the discussion of AMR. Socially constructed roles, relations and norms assigned to men, women and non-binary individuals, together with the population’s location, livelihood and structural neglect, create a complex dynamic in AMR discourse and are rarely studied with an intersectional lens.12 Studies in Bangladesh have found more AMR organisms in all of drinking, surface and wastewater from urban areas than from rural sources, with the prevalence highest from the wastewater from food markets, indicating vulnerabilities for males in this gendered occupation.13,14 In low-income settings, such as those in Bangladesh, women’s caregiving and household responsibilities can expose them to infections while caring for ill family members, domestic animals, gardens and conducting other household tasks.15 In these contexts, women often experience restricted mobility and limited financial autonomy within households, which prevents them from timely healthcare seeking and adherence to treatments. Due to combined sex and gender factors, they are also more exposed and susceptible to hospital-acquired infections during pregnancy, childbirth, abortion and the postabortion period.16 Conversely, pharmacies, informal drugstores and salespersons at the drugstores are the first point of contact for the majority of the population in both urban and rural areas and migrant communities.17,18 Unnecessary dispensing of antibiotics at community people’s requests due to their limited autonomy, healthcare access or resources increases people’s vulnerability to AMR.19

Urban informality was once considered as poor settlements, now more commonly seen as a process of metropolitan urbanisation.20 Overcrowding, insufficient housing facilities, illegal arrangements of basic amenities, poor water, sanitation and hygiene facilities, informal jobs and poor socioeconomic conditions—they all play an important role in aggravating the community’s chance at healthy living and environments. The residents of the informal settlements are often daily wage earners—males predominantly in the transport and construction industry, while women in informal domestic care work, RMG industry, etc.21,22 These populations have to cope constantly just to get by, even more so in the face of a crisis or emergency situation, such as a pandemic or tackling AMR. For example, not much is known about the ready-made garment (RMG) workers’ vulnerability to AMR risks, though the sector has very high stakes from occupational hazards. The RMG sector contributes to two-fifths of the economy in Bangladesh, and a majority of the workforce is dominated by female workers.23 It is worth exploring as women make up a majority of the global workforce in the apparel supply chain too,24 yet a gendered analysis of the occupational hazards in this industry remains obscure, equally devoid of health issues specific to women. One study shows that approximately 6% of RMG workers in the readymade, woven and knitwear industry have frequent occurrences of urinary tract infections (UTIs) in self-reported responses.25 Characterised by unhealthy and poor working conditions, some systematic review studies have established prolonged sitting, resulting in risks of musculoskeletal injury, more generally, injuries in women working in factories, where design considerations largely overlook anthropometric differences of women.26–28

While there may be other health issues stemming from prolonged sitting, one systematic review links UTI to uncomfortable sitting/working conditions and limited access to toilets, although data are not segregated for RMG or factory workers.29 All of this evidence makes the issue equally important to be addressed for both men and women, as men face longer working hours and overtime than women in these jobs.28 Some of the literature from Bangladesh on RMG workers’ occupational hazards and working conditions in the RMG industry reveal uncomfortable sitting positions to come first as causes for suffering from their ailments30 and lack of pure drinking water at the workplace31; both with potential to recurrent infections, factors inducing lower urinary tract symptoms outcomes, and conceivably AMR. At the same time, in urban informal settlements, prescription practices that do not follow recommended international guidance and sales of antibiotics by informal providers, poor drug regulatory administrations, a lack of diagnostic facilities and the use of antibiotics in animal production are all important contributing factors to the rising burden of AMR.32

In this context, a recent report by WHO shows that 125/145 AMR national action plans (NAPs) do not mention gender.33 The Bangladesh National Action Plan for Antimicrobial Resistance Containment provides major policy directions for the country34 but does not undertake a gender emphasis (Paul and Noor, unpublished). The Bangladesh counterpart of the GEAR up consortium recently contributed to possible integrations of gender focus into the National AMR Surveillance Strategy 2025–2030 on various levels. However, there is a gap in the literature on in-depth exploration of drivers of AMR, from the perspective of sociobehavioural and gender lenses.

The aim of this study was to explore the intersectional factors that contribute to both disease transmission and shape antibiotic use among urban informal populations. More specifically

To identify social determinants and intersectional vulnerabilities relating to domestic roles, and environmental and occupational factors that shape exposure to AMR in urban informal settlements in Bangladesh.

Methods

This qualitative study followed an exploratory design and the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) framework (online supplemental appendix 1) for reporting items and outlining the workflow.35

Study design and study site

The study included in-depth interviews (IDIs) with community members and informal service providers and focus group discussions (FGDs) with male and female groups to understand their individual and community perspectives, respectively.

We conducted the study in two informal urban settlements of the Dhaka metropolis: Settlement A from Dhaka North and Settlement B from Dhaka South City Corporation.

Study populations and sampling

We reached 70 participants (23 adult men and 47 women) in total between 6 and 17 January 2025. We recruited participants from each settlement, following a mix of purposive and convenience sampling, under the following categories: adult males (mostly daily wagers, factory and construction workers, rickshaw pullers, etc.) and females (mostly engaged in domestic care work at home and outside), mothers, elderly (65+years), persons with disability or chronic health issues, climate-affected migrants, etc, to understand whether their conditions or exposure placed them at a higher risk of AMR. We also included 2 FGDs of RMG workers, as it is a heavily feminised sector in Bangladesh, and workers typically live/migrate in these informal settlements. The exact numbers are shown below (table 1). Respondents were recruited until data saturation was reached and available resources in the project budget. The Sex and Gender Equity in Research guidelines checklist (online supplemental appendix 2) was included to report how we have incorporated sex and gender equitably in this study and manuscript.

Data collection

Tools were pretested, and five researchers (four females and one male) conducted the individual interviews and group discussions in pairs (of one interviewer and one note-taker). Written informed consent was obtained from each study participant. All interviews were recorded. All participants (IDIs and FGDs) were provided refreshments as a token of compensation for their time in this research. After finishing each day’s data collection, the researchers undertook a debrief session to discuss field notes and key findings of the day (online supplemental appendixs 3–7).

Data analysis

The audio recordings were transcribed directly into English by trained transcribers (a group of 4). Four researchers read the transcripts until they were familiar with the content. We followed a thematic framework analysis (online supplemental appendix 8) approach, and coding was done both inductively and deductively. A priori codes and subcodes were developed based on the research questions, and emerging themes/subthemes were included while reading transcripts. Next, data were extracted into a predesigned matrix in Microsoft Excel V.2013. To ensure intercoder reliability, two researchers read and extracted each transcript. Additionally, the researchers resolved any confusion or disagreement through discussions. Once the extraction was complete, the data were analysed and summarised. Relevant quotes were preserved and terms were presented (online supplemental appendix 9) for the synthesis of findings.

Patient and public involvement statement

Two community researchers from these sites, who were trained under a previous urban health research project, ARISE,36 were consulted to identify and locate potential participants from the community, assess the length of the tools and time, and finally, schedule appointments for the interviews.

Results

Overview and characteristics of the participants

We conducted 31 IDIs and FGDs (25 IDIs and 6 FGDs) in the communities of settlement A and settlement B. Table 1 shows the types of participants and numbers sampled from each settlement.

We spoke to a total of 70 participants from poor socioeconomic backgrounds, living in these low-income informal settlements. One interview of the elderly (considered respondents’ age to be 65 years or older) included (with consent) a married couple who both had disabilities and chronic health conditions. FGDs of workers included only female groups, as these were conducted on the weekend, making it difficult to gather men’s groups even on those days. The focus groups were formed with RMG workers in settlement A, and both RMG and factory workers in tin-container production factories in settlement B. Except for three unemployed individuals, all male participants were workers, such as rickshaw pullers and workers in the construction industry, head masons, paint workers, etc. In the female FGD groups, the majority of participants were homemakers.

Participants’ ages varied between 18 and 80, the majority were married and had little formal education. There were newly arrived migrants as well as people who had lived in the settlements for more than 40 years.

Prioritisation of healthcare and household members: social norms as drivers of AMR risks

In both communities, participants reported a shortage of services within their communities and highlighted related challenges, There isn’t any nearby medical center where we can quickly take a patient during an emergency (FGD, male, settlement B). This geographic distance from formal care normalises reliance on informal drug sellers, whose antibiotic dispensing is unregulated, directly amplifying AMR risks.

For the females, accessing the facilities was a struggle, especially for people with disabilities. An elderly woman from settlement A shared, Sometimes I wait for my son to take me, but he’s busy earning money. Dependence on family members’ schedules for healthcare access introduces treatment delays, encouraging self-medication with leftover antibiotics from pharmacies as a quick solution. A female respondent who was a caregiver to her elderly mother said,

My mother cannot walk much. She is not able to go by (public) bus. She has to be taken in a rickshaw. Also, she cannot wait in long queues or sit for long periods. We might be able to stand for two hours, but elderly people cannot. (FGD, Female, Settlement A)

Physical and logistical barriers to formal facilities disproportionately push elderly and people with disabilities towards informal providers, where incomplete courses of antibiotics are common due to cost and convenience, fueling resistance. Thus, in both informal settlements, pharmacies were the most reliable source of care for community people.

Normal illnesses, like a cold or a fever… we go to the pharmacy (medicine shop) for that. They (sellers/owners) give us medicine, and we trust them. If they can’t give us medicine, they write down a note and ask us to go to a certain medical center. Then we take that note to the medical centre (Male, FGD, Settlement B).

This trust in medicine shop sellers, who often dispense antibiotics without prescriptions, transforms minor illnesses into opportunities for inappropriate antibiotic use, a key driver of community-level AMR.

These providers were also the first responders during emergencies or in cases of acute conditions in urban informal settings:

If someone suddenly has a stroke and we cannot find any nearby doctor to help, we rush to these doctors (drug sellers). If they can’t handle the case, they tell us … That’s when we go to bigger hospitals. But initially, we would go to them for quick treatment. (Female, age 38 years, FGD participant, Settlement A)

In these times, their service to the community goes beyond medical advice, rather in supporting the community’s needs, transporting the patient or referring to an affordable facility.

First, I go to the small pharmacies near us. If that doesn’t cure me, then I go to SH Hospital” (IDI (female), Person with Disabilities, Settlement B)

The stepwise pathway from medicine shop to hospital reflects a default to informal care, where antibiotics are often the first-line response, creating selective pressure for resistance before any formal diagnosis occurs.

Government hospitals with subsidies were among the popular choices due to the low cost of health services, such as clinic A and public medical university B near these settlements.

…So we stand in line at Clinic A and get treatment there. No consultation fee is required here (Male, age 26 years, FGD participant, Settlement B)

While subsidised public facilities reduce financial barriers, their overburdened queues incentivise patients to return to pharmacies for quicker antibiotic access, undermining antimicrobial stewardship.

Again, the long wait in government hospitals drove some to the private facilities. At SH (public hospital), the queue is so long that it takes the entire day just to see a doctor (IDI, Climate migrant, Settlement B). Time required at public hospitals thus indirectly drives AMR by pushing patients toward informal providers who offer rapid, prescription-free antibiotic courses, often incomplete and at low doses.

Parents of children sought care from a formal facility, public or private, attended by a qualified doctor, as children took priority.

The pharmacy doesn’t examine the child. A real doctor will know what’s going on. I always tell people, don’t take children to pharmacies. If you go to a proper doctor, they will examine and give the right medicine. (IDI, Mother, Settlement A)

This protective attitude towards children, however, coexists with self-medication for adults, illustrating how household prioritisation creates a two-tier system where adult infections are routinely treated with informal antibiotics, fostering resistance that ultimately affects all family members.

Elderly people with chronic health problems or comorbidities (with heart/kidney diseases/diabetes/physical disability/cancer patients) or critical health conditions, such as a postoperative or trauma patient experiencing disability, tended to go to a formal facility-government subsidised hospitals/clinics, specialised hospitals or private hospitals/clinics, and were likely to follow the doctor’s advice diligently. An elderly couple, both of whom had disabilities and serious health issues of kidney and high blood pressure, consulted doctors, rather than take suggestions from an informal medicine shop. Husband and wife’s level of education (class 9 and 5, respectively) might have led to an increased level of awareness about their own health conditions and available services.

Workers in RMG and tin-container production factories had first aid supplies, a doctor available for consultation anywhere between once every week to once every month and were treated for normal health problems. In case of a sudden health need or serious injuries, primary treatment or referral would be provided at the factories, then granted leave to take the individual to a nearby facility, or home. Still, most female workers faced difficulties getting treatment during working hours. On typical weekdays, they worked from 8:00 to 20:00, even later, while men often worked overtime. In need of visiting a doctor, they could not attend work the whole day, leading them to unqualified doctors or local medicine shopkeepers after work. Men earning daily wages displayed similar choices of healthcare. That’s why we run to the pharmacy. Even if it’s 3:00, we call the pharmacist (drug seller). (FGD, male, Settlement A). Round-the-clock availability of drug sellers normalises antibiotics as emergency commodities, erasing the distinction between prescription drugs and OTC remedies and entrenching AMR-conducive behaviours in daily survival routines. Thus, formal facilities routinely lose patients to informal providers. We followed the narrative of one male participant who had migrated to Settlement B 7 years ago; his case is presented in box 1.

Access to formal healthcare: a health system problem or a financial problem?

Md. Shafiqul (Pseudonym), a 53-year-old resident living in settlement B for 7 years with his wife, two daughters and a son, after his house in the countryside, Sunamganj, Sylhet, was completely destroyed by floods over the years. He was suffering from a sharp pain in the stomach for the last 4 months. Additionally, he had a condition of abnormal bone growth of his spine, which put him off any physically demanding work on the doctor’s advice, leaving his ready-made garment factories—working daughters as the only earning members in the family. Shafiq recalls, When my stomach pain started, I endured it for a month, and when I couldn’t bear it anymore, I went to the doctor (Medicine shop).

Shafiq took medicine for 7 days based on a diagnosis of gastritis from a medicine shopkeeper. He completed two courses of syrup based on the shopkeepers’ advice before turning to another drugstore when his symptoms persisted. The next drugstore keeper recommended Shafiq to get some tests done. That was the first time he was referred to a private facility to get a diagnostic test.

I went there, but there was a money issue. When I went, they told me I would need a CT scan. They told me it would cost 4000 taka. After I took 4000 taka with me, they said, No, 4000 won’t be enough; it will cost 8000 taka. I left and came back.

Unable to afford the diagnostics, he consulted a doctor at the largest, renowned and public Medical College Hospital. There, he again gave up in despair, exhausted from visiting the hospital 20 times in 4 months.

(The) DMC doctor prescribed me a month’s medicine… Later, when I went back, he said, ‘You need an endoscopy’. Then I did an endoscopy, ultrasound, blood tests and an ElectroCardioGram. After these tests, he told me, …if you don’t treat it properly, it will turn into an ulcer. Since they were delaying, I quickly got private treatment to relieve my conditions….

…A doctor from our village, from Sunamganj, comes here. He’s a good doctor, although he isn’t an MBBS (Bachelor of Medicine and Bachelor of Surgery), but his qualifications are equivalent to those. After that, I went to him.

Shafiqul’s story exposes a grave concern, where community people routinely end up choosing informal healthcare and service providers and remain well outside the statistics of formal facility-based AMR surveillance.

Conversely, healthcare-seeking behaviour in the community is significantly influenced by cultural beliefs and social norms, along with gender roles. Even if we skip a meal, we make sure to get the medicine for the children, shared a parent, highlighting the priority of her children’s health (IDI, female with chronic disease, age 40, settlement B). This prioritisation of children’s medicines over adult prescriptions normalises interrupted antibiotic courses for caregivers, directly promoting subtherapeutic dosing and resistance selection in adult populations. Similarly, We didn’t buy my medicines, only the medicines for my children, said another respondent (IDI, breastfeeding mother, settlement B). Maternal self-neglect in adhering to medications creates a reservoir of untreated or partially treated infections among women, who then become chronic carriers of resistant organisms transmissible within households.

An informal healthcare provider shared her experience that prevailing social norms weigh in on such behaviour,

Some pregnant women come, and their mothers-in-law say, ‘Why do you need checkups? In our time, we didn't have checkups. We never took medicine. (IDI, Female informal health care provider, Age 42 years, Settlement B).

Intergenerational resistance to antenatal care perpetuates a culture where preventive measures are dismissed, while reliance on informal remedies for pregnancy-related ailments often involves untargeted antibiotics, endangering both mother and fetus.

For a woman with physical disability, lack of support from her husband, in-laws or paternal families meant layers of vulnerability. One of the respondents (settlement B) fared well as her husband was attentive to her needs, while another (settlement A) had to manage her treatment costs with her disability allowance and save household expenses.

Actually, no one cares much about me, including my mother or siblings. Everyone is busy with their own interests. I have to look after myself whenever I feel sick. What else can I do? My husband doesn’t regard me as important. So, I have to go to the doctor by myself. (IDI, Person with disability (female), Settlement A)

Social isolation among women living with disabilities removes the oversight of their families that might ensure prescription completion, leaving them to self-manage with partial antibiotic courses from pharmacies, amplifying their vulnerability to resistant infections.

Disrespect and abuse were commonly faced by poor women and men when visiting formal facilities, while those with money and connections were given priority services, influencing the choice of healthcare.

Even in hospitals, when they hear we’re from the slum, they don’t treat us properly. They (the hospital administration) say that these are slum people. (FGD, Female, Settlement A)

Stigmatisation at formal facilities erodes trust in prescribed regimens, prompting patients to abandon diagnostic workups and instead seek rapid, antibiotic-heavy solutions from drug sellers.

Social norms also discouraged women from openly discussing health issues, as the majority of local drug sellers were males. A conservative culture and gender expectations of modesty are huge deterrents.

For gynecological problems, women feel shy. Men do not understand or pay attention to these issues in women. Young girls feel shy and don’t share them even with family or doctors. (FGD, Female group, Settlement A).

Cultural modesty around gynaecological issues blocks women from seeking appropriate diagnostic cultures or susceptibility testing, forcing them into self-treatment with OTC medicines/antibiotics, often at incorrect doses.

All of the drug sellers are male; is it possible to talk with them about all kinds of things? (FGD, Female (factory/RMG workers), Settlement B)

The gender mismatch between female patients and male drug-sellers silences reproductive health concerns, leading to empiric antibiotic use for symptoms without proper examination or follow-up, fueling resistance in urogenital infections.

On the other hand, adult males tended to avoid seeking care as they were seen as providers, conforming to the social norms.

People of my age, if they catch a cold or get sick, they don’t go to the doctor. They don’t take their sickness seriously…They think, “If I spend money on myself, how will I manage my family’s expenses?” They neglect their health, they neglect themselves. (FGD, Male age 27, Settlement B)

Masculine norms delay care in low-cost communities until infections are severe, at which point empirical broad-spectrum antibiotics are more likely to be prescribed, and financial responsibilities pressure men to shorten courses, accelerating AMR acquisition.

I believe I should first take my wife and child to the doctor. What is there to do for myself? When fulfilling responsibilities, our problems just fade away. There are thousands of people like us… (FGD, Male, Settlement B).

The male breadwinner identity systematically deprioritises men’s infectious disease treatment to family needs, creating a cycle where untreated infections in men recur and require repeated, often incomplete, antibiotic exposures.

In a conservative society, men felt uncomfortable when visiting facilities predominantly occupied by women. One male participant shared,

Going to the local (government) clinic isn’t really possible for us….That place is crowded with women…If a woman stands in front of me, I feel shy to stand behind her…there may be physical contact. Some women may misunderstand it. That’s why men don’t want to stand in line (with them). We feel more comfortable going to non-government hospitals for this reason (FGD, Male, Settlement B).

An absence of gender-responsive service, delivering health professionals and segregated spaces, pushes men towards private informal sellers who are more responsive to their needs, dispense antibiotics without diagnostics, bypassing stewardship protocols and increasing selective resistance in the male population.

Overall, for men, it was uncomfortable to wait with other women, and seeking health was seen as a sign of weakness.

Socioeconomic status and AMR

Disease vulnerabilities in the communities

We discuss vulnerabilities in terms of exposure to infections, whether community people contracted an infectious disease from their immediate environments, contaminated water, food or a zoonotic crossover from household livestock to humans, and susceptibility to infections, such as any particular subpopulation that is more likely to catch an infection due to their weak immunity, and so on. Settlements A and B can be characterised by housing with inadequate water, sanitation and hygiene facilities and other utilities for healthy living, inhabited by people from a poor socioeconomic background, generally making their livelihoods on various low-paid, informal jobs. With very few options of services within the communities, poverty and overcrowding intensified the dynamic of life manyfold here, often experienced differently by men, women and other genders.

The majority of the participants from settlements A and B reported suffering from recurrent fever, common cold, cough, asthma, gastric problems and skin diseases. These were particularly apparent for the elderly and children. Water-borne and vector-borne diseases like diarrhoea, cholera, typhoid, jaundice, dengue and chikungunya frequently affected them due to poor living conditions, polluted air, weak immunity systems and seasonal weather changes. In addition, community people, especially the elderly, suffered from non-communicable diseases like hypertension and diabetes. UTIs and cut injuries were the most commonly mentioned issues by factory and garment workers.

Financial struggles in the development of AMR

Lack of financial resources in day-to-day living directed harmful care-seeking behaviours conducive to building AMR in populations. Out-of-pocket payments prohibited community people from seeking timely and quality healthcare, neglect own health and overriding the instructions of antibiotic prescriptions. ‘I stopped taking my antibiotics early because we had to buy school supplies for my children’ (IDI, female with chronic disease, age 40, settlement B); it was a common scenario in the community. Diversion of household funds from adult antibiotic purchases to children’s education directly fragments prescribed courses, leaving residual bacterial populations to develop resistance before retreatment occurs.

One female climate migrant from settlement B shared, I have problems with back pain and pressure. I do not see any doctor. If I go, I need money. Where could I find that much money? Poverty projected a harsh reality on elderly people, too. An elderly couple whose old age allowance had stopped post-COVID-19 pandemic, with only one son who had to provide for his own family before their parents, and having exhausted all social support, had to resort to begging for daily living. The wife, who had a chronic kidney problem and physical disability, said,

…My condition has worsened, leading to a 20000 Taka loan. I have now stopped taking medicines because I need to repay the loan before it increases any further. (IDI, Elderly couple with chronic diseases and disabilities, Female, Settlement B)

Debt-induced medication cessation among the elderly with chronic infections creates perfect conditions for bacterial persistence and regrowth, transforming treatable infections into multidrug-resistant threats within this population.

Similar experiences were shared in both male and female group discussions,

Admission in government hospitals is free, but the tests keep piling up. Whatever money we have is spent entirely on tests and medicines. The tests alone are overwhelming… They keep saying they need blood tests, this and that, and it costs so much money. (FGD, Male, age: 45, Settlement A)

The cumulative cost of diagnostic tests effectively prohibits pathogen identification and susceptibility testing, ensuring that any antibiotics bought are taken blindly, often unnecessarily, or at ineffective doses.

The perceived futility of formal visits without funds for tests reinforces a binary choice: no care or informal antibiotic access, eliminating the possibility of guided therapy and embedding resistance in community-wide practice.

What is the benefit of visiting a doctor, if we cannot afford a diagnostic test or buy medicine? That’s why we usually don't go to the doctors. (FGD, Female, Settlement B)

The narratives above indicate how active surveillance may appear unsuccessful in representing the marginally poor, more so when intersected with other factors such as migration, gender, disability or old age. For men, seeking treatment was a trade between ‘extra expense’ and a day’s wage. One respondent said,

(If I go to a doctor) my total expense becomes 1000 taka, including the visit (doctor’s visit fee), transportation cost, and duty (read: daily wages) will be skipped for a day. …this math stops us from going to the doctor’s. (FGD, Male, Settlement A)

The dual loss of wages and consultation fees makes the opportunity cost of formal care prohibitive, forcing male workers to rely on immediate, cheap antibiotic fixes from pharmacies that ignore pharmacokinetic principles.

Not only managing health-related costs out of their own pockets, but community people also relied on close family members and relatives for borrowing money as a way of coping.

Sometimes we don’t have any money on hand, but the child is sick, so leaving it untreated isn’t an option. In such cases, we borrow some money, 500–1000 taka, from someone and pay it back later. That’s how we manage. (IDI, Female with chronic disease, Settlement B)

Informal borrowing is often the only answer for many households, which may cover only partial treatment, thus leaving them with no other choice than to purchase antibiotic courses partially, creating pathways to non-adherence.

For medicines, financial constraints barring people from purchasing prescribed doses had bigger implications and were applicable for antibiotics as well. The non-completion of medicine/antibiotic doses was reported in a number of ways, such as buying only a portion of the prescribed dose, only consuming doses until the patients started feeling better, consuming leftover medicines from an old prescription or taking medicines on and off following the inflow of cash into the household, as illustrated below,

When they prescribe any medicines, we initially buy a small portion. If it is finished, we buy more. We can't buy everything at once, (IDI, Female with disability, Age 30, Settlement A).

Incremental purchasing of prescribed antibiotics disrupts the peak concentrations needed for bacterial eradication, turning a curative regimen into a subtherapeutic selection event for AMR.

Sometimes it takes 1200–1300 taka for one week’s medicine. Where will we get the money for that? So, we buy medicine little by little, and when we feel better, we stop buying (FGD, Male, Settlement B).

Symptom-driven cessation rather than course completion trains communities to associate antibiotic improvement with cure, ignoring the surviving resistant subpopulations that later recolonise and spread.

Suppose they told me to take 7 tablets and I bought 3-4 tablets, and I recovered. Then there is no (more) need. (If) I take 4 and feel better, then I don’t complete the course anymore…

…If I take one tablet and recover, then I do not take the next one…I keep leftover medicines and take them if next time I have a fever or fall sick. (IDI, Pregnant woman, age 22, Settlement B).

Hoarding leftover antibiotics for future fevers institutionalises non-recommended prescription reuse, where incorrect dosing for new syndromes fosters cross-resistance.

A middle-aged woman who was a cancer patient relying on the household income and borrowed money for treatment shared that she has to skip taking medicines for a few days.

We need to manage everything. If I can manage the money, if my son can earn money, and my husband can earn money by driving, then I [can] take medicine. But if I cannot manage, then for 2–4 days, I stop taking medicine. (IDI, Chronic disease patient, Settlement A).

Intermittent adherence tied to household cash flow creates oscillating drug levels in the body, exerting maximum selective pressure for resistance mutations while failing to clear the primary infection.

These experiences highlight how structural inequities affected the lack of follow-up in antimicrobial use and consumption in communities. Conversely, a woman from settlement B shared, ‘Once I had a fever and took antibiotics for 21 days from the pharmacy (local medicine shop)’. We discuss over-consumption of antibiotics in the AMR stewardship section of this paper.

Livelihoods and AMR

Women’s care work in domestic environments was found to be a gendered role. Women in a household—rural or urban, are typically primary caregivers to children, husband, in-laws and the elderly. In urban settlements A and B, we found women taking care of livestock beside their families, within close proximity as space is scarce in urban areas. Mixed with women’s childbearing roles in their lifecycles, women’s clinical characteristics and gendered role made them susceptible and placed them at a greater risk of repeated infections.

On the other hand, paid, informal domestic cleaners/care workers also did not have the luxury to prioritise their health. Being the only earning member of family was the sole motivation for staying healthy.

We also interviewed two groups of RMG and metal container production factory workers, which revealed insights into whether being a working woman from a poor socioeconomic background and living in urban informal settlements contributed to increasing AMR in the community.

Female garment and factory workers reportedly suffered from colds and breathing difficulties, which they linked to the garment dust. They also reported frequent cuts and injuries from getting pricked by needles and manoeuvring heavy machinery. Workers described repeated occupational injuries that normalised infection risk. Garment workers from settlement A shared,

…And hands get cut. It happened to me. The needle went in through one side and came out through the other. Due to dust in the garments, workers often get colds and coughs. (FGD, RMG workers, Female age 22, Settlement A)

This incident bypasses wound culture and sensitivity testing, normalising prophylactic broad-spectrum antibiotic use that drives resistance among skin and respiratory organisms.

Female garments and factory workers also suffered from UTIs frequently due to less water intake. One worker from settlement B shared,

For example, if we drink less water while working, there’s a burning sensation during urination, pain in the lower abdomen, and chest pain. (FGD, workers, Female age 28, Settlement B)

Dehydration-induced UTIs in factory workers are self-medicated with leftover or purchased antibiotics, which makes suboptimal urinary antibiotic concentrations select for resistant uropathogens.

This was a common scenario as there were strict instructions on completing the daily quota of tasks accomplished, and workers were discouraged from getting off their workstations by managers. This normalisation of injury created conditions where antibiotics were treated as routine recovery tools rather than medical interventions. A working woman’s health choices—work pressure for money, or leave for a health checkup?

Shirin (pseudonym) sits patiently in the Community Resource Center with a group of her neighbours and colleagues who worked in the same readymade garments factory. When the focus group discussion began, she voiced the struggles of women at workplaces like hers. She talked about how they had to sit at their workstations through the day, deal with cuts, operate heavy machinery, unable to take breaks for eating, drinking water, or even going to the toilet. ‘The salary has increased a little, that’s why the workload is even more’, she says. ‘It (Burning in the urinary tract while urinating) happens due to drinking less water. Also, many people can’t go to the washroom on time (when they need to) because of work pressure. This is a common issue for garment workers’. Indeed, the experience was common within the group. Some sought medical advice, while some never consulted a doctor for this problem.

Talking about the health benefits of an employee, visible frustration swept across the group. One of them mentioned the struggle with leave requests, which was not received well by the supervisors. ‘There was a time, Apa, when I was so sick—fever, stomach pain, headaches—but the line supervisor didn’t want to grant leave. This is how it works in the garments—not just for me, but for everyone’.

The health centre at the factory provided first aid and medicines, but not regular visiting hours for a doctor’s consultation, driving workers outside, unable to avail the health service at work. Shirin explained,

Madam, let me tell you. If we go to a government hospital, we have to leave very early in the morning, even before dawn. Because you can’t get a serial number there. At S (a private hospital), the treatment is good. It’s expensive, but the treatment is good. But in government hospitals, we don’t get a ticket. If we stand in line today, the test will be done tomorrow, the report will be given the day after, and then we’ll see the doctor. This way, 4–5 days are wasted. We are garment workers. Losing one day means a big loss. That’s why many go to private hospitals. In government hospitals, you waste time and have to rush a lot.

On many occasions, Shirin would be considered an empowered woman. A working woman with risks of infections from her workplace, from a poor socioeconomic background, living in an urban informal settlement, stricken by poverty and few choices of healthcare; her risks to AMR seemed no less complex than the women who were engaged in care work at home.

Sources of information for healthcare, antimicrobial drugs and awareness of AMR

Women relied on their personal experience and social networks for general health-related information, services, medicines and antibiotics. Among them, pregnant women and mothers with young children had more links to the health system and information about quality health services and facilities. This could be due to frequent visits by community health workers (CHWs) at home or to a facility for routine check-ups of their own or the child. A mother who had received education, job experience working in a social safety net programme in her community, and regular consultation from her CHW and educated peers mentioned the recommended use of antibiotics and a general idea of AMR. Additionally, when asked for the recommended mode of AMR awareness campaigns, the communities were accustomed to discussion sessions arranged in their neighbourhoods, homes and open places, and suggested this way of sourcing information which was a common form of health promotion messaging by CHWs in rural households, and sometimes urban, covering—maternal and child health services, family health and nutrition, social safety net programmes, etc.

A respondent relied on professional experts for her information,

Once I had a fever and took antibiotics for 21 days from the pharmacy (local Medicine shop). But my fever did not recover, and I visited the government hospital, P, and the doctor was very angry. He said that I should not take antibiotics for 21 days without any test. I realized it after that incident. (FGD, Female (general), Settlement B)

This incident reveals that awareness of AMR often arises only after failed empirical therapy, yet reliance on prolonged courses persists because formal feedback loops remain weak, allowing dangerous overuse to continue unchecked until severe outcomes occur.

Antibiotics are typically viewed as a potent medicine in the community that should only be used in cases where OTC medicines are ineffective. In other cases, people relied on sensational news on social media.

AMR stewardship

Respondents of this study reported consuming antibiotics based on the drug sellers’ advice. This process could be iterative between the seller and the customers, Then the [drug seller] advised me to give him (child) an antibiotic… said a pregnant mother with young children. This iterative advice-seeking transforms drug sellers into de facto prescribers, embedding antibiotic use into everyday parenting decisions without any diagnostic basis. On a separate occasion of her child’s high fever, she recalls, At that time I took antibiotics for my child. I went to the pharmacy and asked for antibiotics… (IDI, Pregnant mother, Settlement B)

Mothers actively demanding antibiotics for febrile children reverses the stewardship hierarchy, placing clinical decisions in the hands of caregivers who may equate fever with bacterial infection, guaranteeing unnecessary and often repeated antibiotic exposures in early childhood.

It was also reported that communities tend not to take advice from the local medicine shop owners when inconvenient. If I don't sell it, they'll just go to another shop’, acknowledged another medicine shop owner (IDI, Informal health service provider, Male, Settlement B).

This admission exposes the competitive market logic that overrides stewardship, as refusal to dispense antibiotics without a prescription is economically unviable, guaranteeing that community demand drives supply regardless of clinical indication.

The majority of service providers reported that they had some form of training ranging from 2 weeks to several months; however, in all cases, without any refresher training. This ranged from taking formal certifications to informal hands-on training in running a medicine shop business from a close acquaintance. Most of the informal providers denied selling antibiotics without prescriptions, though the female owners/sellers assured that referral or counselling on the importance of buying and consuming the full course of prescribed antibiotics could be offered as an alternative.

Before giving it, I tell them, ‘If you can complete the dosage, then take it; otherwise, don’t. If you can take it for five or seven days, then take the antibiotic; otherwise, don’t. (IDI, Informal service provider, Female, Settlement B).

This conditional counselling, while well-intentioned, paradoxically depends on the patient’s own judgement of what constitutes a complete antibiotic course.

If it doesn’t get better, then I tell them that if the illness persists for a long time, they should visit a doctor. After visiting the doctor, if they bring a ‘slip’ (prescription) for antibiotics, I give it to them if I have it. If not, I tell them that since the doctor prescribed antibiotics, they should buy them from another pharmacy as I don’t have them. (IDI, Informal provider, Drug seller/Medicine shop, Female, Settlement A)

We present a case study (box 3) on similar excessive non-recommended use of antibiotics by community people.

Non-recommended use of antibiotics in the community

Monir and Shahana (Pseudonyms) are a married couple from settlement A who jointly live with their married son’s family in the community. Monir (65 years), with multiple comorbidities and uncontrolled diabetes as the major issue for him, was our primary respondent. Shahana (55–60 years) was Monir’s primary caregiver, and having worked briefly as a community health worker to help pregnant women get to a facility before their deliveries caught our attention. She joined the conversation frequently and told us a striking story. In the context of suffering from a skin disease, boils or ‘bishfora’ in local terms, she reported having consumed Flucloxacillin (a narrow-spectrum penicillin antibiotic used to treat bacterial infections) for nearly a month.

While Monir regularly consulted a qualified physician at a public hospital, Shahana never went to the hospital for her ailments due to the long queue. She went to a local medicine shop, where 7 days of FLUCLOX 500 was advised, written on a slip, and if not cured, was recommended to go to the hospital. In her words, They gave me the antibiotic for 'Bishfora,’ (skin disease, boils) and I felt terrible after taking it four times a day. My chest hurt, and I felt dizzy, so I reduced the dose to twice a day…When I couldn’t handle it, I started taking it once a day. I took them for a month, and my wound healed. She further clarified, I took it four times a day for 10 days, then twice a day for 15 days, and so on. For one month, I took that medicine regularly. When asked where she learned to taper the antibiotic dosage, she affirmed that she made the decision herself, used the same prescription to buy more tablets and confidently attributed the knowledge to her previous work experience. We sat in awe, unable to decide which was more dangerous—deciding to take antibiotics on her own, her in the role of a caregiver or the fact that the action came from the exposure of being a community health worker previously—Shahana’s story is a stark indication at the importance of raising general awareness and professional trainings for community people and informal providers in AMR awareness and prevention campaigns.

Medicine shopkeepers often suggested antibiotics based on their sense of patients’ affordability and financial backgrounds. This appeared accommodating for the customers as well. This poses an opportunity for antibiotic stewardship in the community; by nurturing the trusted relationship between informal health providers and community people, providing and strengthening the providers’ capacities for antibiotic dispensing following international standards, and codeveloping local leadership of AMR awareness campaigns for community populations.

Discussion

The study findings reveal that community members in the urban informal settlements can be excessively vulnerable to infectious diseases due to poverty, unhygienic and challenging living and working conditions, and societal gendered norms, which affect healthcare-seeking behaviour. There is limited awareness and poor stewardship around AMR. Marginalised groups such as children and the elderly with weak immunity and comorbidities and RMG/similar-type factory workers are more vulnerable to repeated infections (UTI and injuries), thus bearing AMR risks in urban informal communities.

Facing financial barriers, including barriers at family and workplace to seek timely healthcare, community members and local medicine shop owners/sellers (unlicensed, un/semi-skilled, commonly termed as ‘pharmacists’) undergo a mutual arrangement where informal health providers may provide antibiotics without a doctor’s consultation/prescription, based on symptoms of illnesses. We found evidence of individuals consuming antibiotics on and off, from leftovers from the old courses, and not completing the full course. Such decisions and behaviours are shaped by one’s financial capacity, which we found to be an intersectional factor influencing AMR risks deeply. This study also covered the voices of a few marginal groups, such as the elderly, persons with disabilities, and we found that people in need of immediate and routine care for critical diseases/emergencies were more likely to choose and adhere to a physician’s prescription.

One of the crosscutting socioeconomic factors contributing to AMR was the community’s impoverished background, as it led to loss-to-follow-up with treatment and antimicrobial, following different pathways for men and women. In addition, environmental factors such as general hygiene practices and availability of resources in the community and safe working conditions with healthy practices were found to be critical to address the AMR issue.

High prevalence of infectious diseases in the community and occupational risks of contracting infections at urban factories in the RMG and similar sectors

We found infectious diseases to be prevalent in both the studied communities in Dhaka, Bangladesh, which agrees with global estimates for Sub-Saharan Africa and Southeast Asia regions.37 For this reason, the importance of community-based delivery of interventions targeted at infection control has been considered to address the looming effect of AMR.38 According to the Bloomfield report, food hygiene and respiratory hygiene in the community are key to preventing the spread of new infectious diseases.39 Our observation of overcrowded, semiconstructed houses with poor water and sanitation facilities, including poor hygiene practices, therefore, helps explain the self-reported occurrence of respiratory, gastrointestinal and other infections in these communities. The added burden of environmental contamination in such settings has been linked to increased antimicrobial use in low-income urban populations.40,41

Our study found UTIs to be a potential risk factor for AMR among RMG workers. Women have been found more vulnerable to factory situations due to, for example, sitting for long hours, resulting in musculoskeletal injuries, or not having enough time to visit the toilet or drink water as necessary.26,27 In a study from Bangladesh, a staggering number of occupational hazards are highlighted for RMG workers, but not specific outcomes. In other settings, such as using the unhygienic public toilets, female street hawkers in Bangladesh were found to avoid these facilities.42 FGDs conducted exclusively with RMG factory workers in this study reveal the possibility of the risk of AMR arising from repeated exposure to UTIs from workplace conditions. Similar observation regarding the relation between insufficient restroom access and repeated UTI infections was made in the USA.43 Quantitative studies are needed to substantiate specific risk factors and explore the causal link between factory/working conditions, recurrent UTIs and AMR. Meanwhile, factory owners and targeted interventions can focus on improving working conditions in the factories: proper seating arrangement and recess, pure drinking water stations and dedicated medical centres staffed by quality health professionals to minimise the risk of exposure to repeated infections.44

Delay in seeking care, choice of healthcare and missed opportunity for AMR surveillance

We learnt that both men and women, as well as workers’ pathways to healthcare, are significantly delayed by several issues. Men’s delay to healthcare has been explained in literature due to a disinclination to express emotions or concerns about health and negligence of symptoms until complications, which aligns with our study findings of men disregarding their own health due to societal gender norms and common negligence.45,46 For women, there is a perceived lack of gender-sensitivity of caregivers, as also seen in our study.47,48 In some cases, travel distance and costs are mentioned,49,50 similar to our study. Among the workers in a factory, loss of a day’s wages, coinciding with visiting hours, were found as a primary reason for delay, but did not find much evidence in literature.51

On the other hand, local medicine shops are commonly deemed as a responsive service provider across India, Kenya as well as in Bangladesh.52 Easy access to antibiotics, payment options on loan or instalments, and other factors make it favourable for community members to turn to the medicine shops in Bangladesh and Nigeria.53,54 Whether community people chose these drug shops as their only means of treatment or as a long route to formal facilities until expensive diagnosis and lost-to-follow-up on a treatment, the low-income urban populations are systematically kept outside the reach of conventional facility-based AMR surveillance. There needs to be a strategic approach to include these populations in the surveillance—whether by passive surveillance or safety net programmes—as our study found evidence of their exposure to multiple AMR risk exposures from their lifestyles, environments and health behaviour.55

Poverty, non-recommended use of antibiotics and AMR risks

Poverty and lack of financial resources were the main cross-cutting factors in our study that prevented or delayed seeking care,52,56,57 caused lost-to-follow-up from a treatment course58 and developed inappropriate antibiotic use and consumption behaviour,53,56,59,60 supporting previous literature in this area.48,61,62 This also corroborated a multicountry economic analysis that out-of-pocket expenditure was the sole factor correlated with AMR in low-income and middle-income countries.63

On the community end, our study findings support that females engage in non-recommended prescriptions by international standards more,19,64,65 perhaps more so within a domestic environment. However, we also found that men seek health advice from the medicine shops more frequently, both for themselves and other family members. This calls for mass awareness campaigns on AMR and proper use of antibiotics, perhaps in the modality community people-recommended, through open discussion sessions and multimedia content. We argue that the informal providers at medicine shops are a critical missed opportunity in AMR stewardship.66 Training and campaigns for un/semiskilled medicine shop owners/sellers should be designed to raise awareness on the responsible dispensing of antibiotics. Supported by the community members’ recommendation to establish more low-cost and government subsidised health programmes and gender-responsive facilities within the proximity of the communities, it is possible to examine more of this issue, how the gap in healthcare seeking can be reduced, without compromising quality healthcare. Such integrated primary-care models, when combined with antibiotic stewardship, have shown promising reductions in unnecessary antibiotic use in low-income settings.67

Limitations of the study

The study was conducted in only two informal urban settlements in Dhaka, Bangladesh, which restricts the findings’ applicability to other large cities and rural ones where sociocultural dynamics are different. This study focused on the human health domain of AMR, while animal and environmental health domains are crucial to understand the complexities of AMR. Additionally, data were collected only for sociodemographic factors, general health-seeking practice, antibiotic use and AMR knowledge; there was little provision to make observations of the environmental factors at home and workplaces, and to collect and analyse clinical data.

In this study, we relied on participants’ memories for data on medicines and antibiotics use, which may have caused recall bias and inaccurate reporting of particular health events or medication use. To overcome this bias, the interviewers asked for the prescriptions and observed where these were available. It was challenging to interpret certain local terms and culturally specific practices related to healthcare and the use of medicine, which could result in an insufficient understanding of their significance. To overcome this situation, we consulted community researchers and community members to better understand the concepts in depth.

Conclusions

The study concludes that AMR risks in Dhaka’s urban informal settlements are structurally embedded in poverty, environmental deprivation and gendered sociocultural norms. Financial precarity emerges as the principal driver, compelling residents to fragment antibiotic courses, self-medicate with leftovers and rely on informal medicine shops, bypassing formal diagnostics and stewardship. Marginalised groups, especially the RMG workers facing recurrent UTIs and workplace occupational injuries, alongside the elderly and disabled, experience compounded vulnerabilities of AMR. The mutual arrangement between community members and informal drug sellers poses a parallel health system that is responsive but unregulated, underscoring the need for a multisectoral approach: occupational health reforms in factories, subsidised gender-responsive primary care near settlements and stewardship training for informal providers. Community-informed awareness campaigns through open discussions must be paired with structural interventions to reduce out-of-pocket expenditures. Without addressing these socioeconomic determinants, hospital-based AMR stewardship will remain disconnected from the daily realities of the urban poor, rendering global resistance targets unattainable.
