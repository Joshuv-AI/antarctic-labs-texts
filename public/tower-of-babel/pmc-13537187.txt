Introducing a CoFe2O4@CS-BAPT/CuII nanocomposite as an efficient catalyst for the synthesis of 4H-pyran derivatives
Authors: Mohammad Ali Bodaghifard, Najmieh Ahadi, Mojdeh Ordouzadeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study introduces the development of an innovative and environmentally friendly bio-nanocatalyst (CoFe2O4@CS-BAPT/CuII nanocomposite), designed and employed for three-component synthesis of 2-amino-3-cyano-4H-pyrans. Chitosan (CS) as a biodegradable polymer was functionalized with bis(2-aminopyridine)triazine (BAPT) moieties. Subsequently, CoFe2O4 magnetic nanoparticles (MNPs) were incorporated and copper ions were immobilized on the composite to form the final magnetic nanocomposite (CoFe2O4@CS-BAPT/CuII). Characterization techniques such as Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR), X-ray diffraction (XRD), thermogravimetric analysis (TGA), field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM), energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) analysis, vibrating sample magnetometry (VSM), inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES), dynamic light scattering (DLS), and zeta potential were used to analyze the nanocomposite structure. XRD analysis showed a near-amorphous structure after functionalizing CoFe2O4 with chitosan. FE-SEM images revealed uniform particles without aggregation, with an average size of 12–14 nm. TGA indicated thermal stability up to 300 °C. CoFe2O4@CS-BAPT/CuII was successfully used in the green synthesis of 2-amino-3-cyano-4H-pyrans with potential biological properties. The desired products were synthesized with high yields (83–95%) in short reaction times (20–45 minutes). Furthermore, the catalyst exhibited easy magnetic separation and good reusability, highlighting its potential as an efficient and sustainable heterogeneous catalyst. Key advantages of this approach include the use of an easily accessible bio-polymer, environmentally friendly catalytic protocols, a simplified and cost-effective synthesis process, clean reactions that generate no additional waste, and the reusable nature of the catalyst.

Introduction

Heterocyclic compounds have garnered significant attention because of their diverse biological and pharmacological properties.1–5 Their heterocyclic core is crucial in imparting medicinal benefits. Among these, tetrahydrobenzopyran derivatives, known as fused pyran scaffolds, stand out as a prominent class of heterocycles with drug-like structural motifs, offering a wide range of applications in medicinal chemistry.1,6 These compounds exhibit a broad spectrum of biological and pharmaceutical activities, including anti-inflammatory,7 antibacterial,8 anticancer,9 spasmolytic,10 calcium antagonistic,11 antianaphylactic,12 diuretic,13 photoactive,14 and cytotoxic effects.15 Such noteworthy pharmaceutical and biological properties make them excellent candidates for medicinal and synthetic applications.

The green synthesis of tetrahydrobenzopyrans under mild conditions has captured considerable interest due to their extraordinary characteristics.16 Multicomponent reactions (MCRs) are frequently employed to minimize purification steps and reduce the use of toxic solvents.17–21 The adoption of MCRs aligns with the principles of green chemistry, promoting a cleaner environment.22 Green chemistry has emerged as an important approach for developing sustainable chemical processes by minimizing waste generation, reducing energy consumption, employing safer reagents, and improving the overall environmental compatibility of chemical transformations.23–25 In this context, the design of recyclable heterogeneous catalysts together with multicomponent reactions has attracted considerable attention, since these strategies combine high efficiency with simplified procedures and reduced environmental impact.26–28 Therefore, the development of environmentally benign catalytic systems remains an active area of research in modern synthetic chemistry.24,29,30 Numerous studies detail the three-component synthesis of tetrahydrobenzopyrans using dimedone, activated CH-acidic compounds, and aldehydes in the presence of catalysts.2,31–33 Catalysts play a vital role in achieving efficient synthesis, ensuring shorter reaction times and higher yields. Heterogeneous catalyst supports play a crucial role in determining catalytic activity, stability, and recyclability. Various supports, including boehmite,34 metal–organic frameworks (MOFs),35,36 silicates,37 natural asphalt,38 and magnetic nanoparticles,39 have been widely employed in organic synthesis. MOFs possess high surface areas and tunable pore structures, providing abundant accessible active sites; however, their synthesis is often relatively complex and some frameworks exhibit limited stability under harsh reaction conditions. In comparison, magnetic nanoparticles offer the unique advantage of rapid magnetic separation, excellent reusability, and facile surface functionalization, making them particularly attractive supports for heterogeneous catalysis.40–42

Recently, nanocatalysts have emerged as key tools in the synthesis of tetrahydrobenzopyrans because nanocatalysts combine the advantages of homogeneous and heterogeneous catalysts (more contact surface and easy separation).43,44 Among these, magnetic nanocatalysts stand out due to their distinguishing magnetic properties, allowing easy separation using magnets.45,46 However, they exhibit a tendency to agglomerate, increasing particle size. To overcome this limitation, surface modification techniques involving various polymers, biopolymers, and organic compounds have been employed.47–49 Chitosan as a biopolymer has proven to be an ideal choice for this purpose due to its functional OH and NH groups. Moreover, chitosan is affordable, readily available, has biological activity, and is eco-friendly.50–52 Recent studies highlight the use of chitosan as a coating material for different types of nanoparticles, further demonstrating its versatility and effectiveness in facilitating nanoparticle-based applications.53–57

Building on our prior research into the application of chitosan within nanocomposite structures58,59 and the synthesis of 4H-pyran derivatives,60,61 we aim to develop a bio-nanocatalyst for facilitating the synthesis of 4H-pyran derivatives.

Experimental

Materials and methods

All chemicals and solvents were purchased from Merck, Fluka, and Aldrich chemical companies and used without further purification. The melting point of 4H-pyrans was determined using a digital electrothermal system. The progress of the chemical reaction of 4H-pyrans was monitored by thin-layer chromatography on UV-active aluminum-backed plates of silica gel 60 F254. Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR spectra) was performed using a Bruker Alpha series instrument in the 400–4000 cm−1 range with KBr pellets. 1H NMR spectra were recorded on a Bruker Avance spectrometer at 300 MHz using DMSO-d6 as a solvent. 13C NMR spectra were also recorded on the Bruker Avance spectrometer (400 MHz) at 100 MHz using DMSO-d6 as a solvent. Inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectroscopy was performed using an analytikjena 9100 model ICP spectrometer to determine the amount of Cu immobilized on the MNPs. A vibrating sample magnetometer (model 730 VSM, Lake Shore Cryotronics, Westerville, OH, USA) was used at room temperature in an applied field of ±8000 Oe to investigate the magnetic properties of CoFe2O4 and the CoFe2O4@CS-BAPT/CuII nanocomposite. Additionally, the morphology and size of the CoFe2O4@CS-BAPT/CuII nanocomposite were measured using a Tescan Mira П field emission-scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM). The histogram chart was extracted from FE-SEM images using Digimizer software (version 5.7.2). Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) coupled with SEM was conducted to identify the chemical elements of the CoFe2O4@CS-BAPT/CuII nanocomposite. CHN analysis for CS@CC and CS-BAPT was performed on the LECO-TruSpec system to measure elemental percentages in the structures. The zeta potential of the CoFe2O4@CS-BAPT/CuII nanocomposite was determined using a Zeta Potential Analyzer (CADA) at pH = 5.5. The crystal structure of CoFe2O4 and the CoFe2O4@CS-BAPT/CuII nanocomposite was investigated using a Philips X'Pert MPD PRO PW3040/6Panalytical and X'Pert Pro powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) diffractometer (Cu Kα radiation, λ = 0.15406 nm) in the Bragg angle range of 2θ = 10–80°. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) of CoFe2O4@CS-BAPT/CuII was carried out using a TA4000 (Q600TA) system under an N2 atmosphere in the range of 25–700 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C per minute.

Preparation of CoFe2O4 MNPs

CoFe2O4 MNPs were prepared using the previously reported method.62 Firstly, CoCl2.6H2O (5 mmol, 1.19 g) and FeCl3.6H2O (10 mmol, 2.70 g) were dissolved in deionized water (200 mL). The solution was heated under a N2 atmosphere at 95 °C for 90 minutes. Afterwards, NH4OH (25%) was slowly added to the mixture until the pH reached 12 and heated for 90 minutes under the same conditions. Finally, the dark brown mixture was cooled to room temperature. The obtained magnetic sediment was separated using a magnet and washed with boiled deionized water (250 mL) until the pH was adjusted to 7. Then, the product was dried in a vacuum oven at 80 °C for 12 hours and calcined at 500 °C for 5 hours.

Preparation of modified chitosan with cyanuric chloride (CS@CC)

For the modification of chitosan with cyanuric chloride, 3.8 mmol (0.7 g) of cyanuric chloride was slowly added to 0.5 g of chitosan in 10 mL of toluene over 30 minutes. The mixture was then dispersed under ultrasound waves for 20 minutes. This mixture was refluxed for 24 hours. The product was separated from the solvent by filtration, washed several times with toluene, and dried in a vacuum oven at 80 °C.63 FT-IR and CHN analyses for CS@CC are presented in Fig. 1 and Table 1. Preparation of bis(2-aminopyridine)triazine functionalized chitosan (CS-BAPT)

A mixture of CS@CC (0.1 g), 2-aminopyridine (1 mmol, 0.094 g), and K2CO3 (2 mmol, 0.27 g) was poured to a round bottom flask, and DMF (20 mL) was added to the mixture. The mixture was sonicated for 20 minutes and refluxed for 24 hours. Afterward, the product was filtered using a centrifuge, washed with deionized water and EtOH, and dried in a vacuum oven at 80 °C. FT-IR and CHN analyses for CS-BAPT are presented in Fig. 1 and Table 1. IR (KBr) ν (cm−1): 3421, 2959, 2878, 1659, 1594, 1466, 1420, 1379, 1261, 1154, 1091, 1030, 895 (Fig. S1).

Preparation of the CoFe2O4 nanocomposite based on functionalized chitosan (CoFe2O4@CS-BAPT)

CS-BAPT (0.09 g) was slowly dissolved in 10 mL of 2% acetic acid, and the pH was adjusted to 5 using 0.1 M NaOH. Then, CoFe2O4 MNPs (0.3 g) were added to the mixture and sonicated for 20 minutes. The mixture was stirred at room temperature for 24 hours under a N2 atmosphere. The obtained product was isolated using a magnet, washed several times with EtOH, and dried in a vacuum oven at 80 °C.64

Immobilization of Cu ions on CoFe2O4@CS-BAPT (CoFe2O4@CS-BAPT/CuII nanocomposite)

Firstly, CoFe2O4@CS-BAPT (0.28 g) was dispersed in 10 mL of EtOH (99%) for 20 minutes. Then, Cu(OAc)2 (2 mmol, 0.398 g) was added to the mixture and stirred at room temperature for 24 hours. The brown sediment was isolated using an external magnetic field, washed with deionized water and EtOH, and dried in a vacuum oven at 80 °C.65

General procedure for the synthesis of 2-amino-3-cyano-4H-pyran derivatives

A mixture of dimedone (1 mmol, 0.14 g), malononitrile (1 mmol, 0.066 g), and an aryl aldehyde (1 mmol) was added to 10 mg of CoFe2O4@CS-BAPT/CuII in EtOH : H2O (1 : 1) (3 mL) as the catalyst and solvent, respectively. The mixture was stirred at room temperature for a certain period. The progress of the reactions was followed by TLC. After completion of the reaction, hot ethanol was added to dissolve the crude product, while the magnetic catalyst remained insoluble and was separated using an external magnet. The resulting ethanolic solution was heated to obtain a saturated solution, followed by the gradual addition of water to precipitate the desired product (EtOH : H2O = 1 : 5, v/v). The product was collected by filtration, washed with water, and dried. The isolated products were obtained as powders. 2-Amino-3-cyano-4H-pyran derivatives were characterized by FT-IR, 1H, and 13C-NMR spectroscopy.

Recycling study

After the separation of the CoFe2O4@CS-BAPT/CuII magnetic nanocomposite using a magnet from the reaction mixture, the catalyst was washed with ethanol to remove residual organic compounds, dried in an oven at 80 °C, and then utilized in the next reaction.

Results and discussion

The modification of chitosan by cyanuric chloride is carried out through the formation of a covalent bond between CS and CC. Then, 2-aminopyridine was reacted with the Cl groups to produce CS-BAPT. Subsequently, CoFe2O4 MNPs were synthesized using the co-precipitation method and loaded onto the modified polymer framework. Finally, Cu was immobilized on the nanocomposite to form a CoFe2O4@CS-BAPT/CuII magnetic nanocomposite (Scheme 1). After the preparation of the CoFe2O4@CS-BAPT/CuII magnetic nanocomposite, it was characterized using Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR), X-ray Diffraction (XRD), Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA), Field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM), Energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) analysis, Vibrating Sample Magnetometry (VSM), Inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectroscopy (ICP-AES), dynamic light scattering (DLS), and zeta potential analyses.

NH and OH stretching vibrations, OH twisting, and some features of amide groups (amide I and amide II bands) are observed in the ranges of 3000–3500 cm−1, 1620 cm−1, and 1450–1650 cm−1, respectively (Fig. 1b–d) with little change in intensity. The elemental analysis results for carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen and chlorine in chitosan and for all modified polymers, as well as the C/N ratio, are summarized in Table 1. The findings revealed a decrease in the percentage of nitrogen, hydrogen, and carbon in chitosan following its modification to CS@CC. This decrease in the percentages suggests that a chemical reaction occurred, leading to the formation of bonds between cyanuric chloride and certain nitrogen atoms in chitosan. The interaction between cyanuric chloride and the nitrogen of CS, alongside the removal of hydrogen atoms, contributed to a lower nitrogen-to-total-mass ratio. Essentially, the modification process results in a new composition that still contains nitrogen but at a reduced concentration per unit compared to the original chitosan. Upon further modification using 2-aminopyridine, it was observed that there was an increase in the nitrogen content in CS-BAPT compared to CS@CC (4.15 to 5.35 mmol g−1). These values are consistent with the incorporation of cyanuric chloride and 2-aminopyridine molecules as pendant chains onto the chitosan polymeric backbone. Also, the decrease in chlorine content from 0.32 mmol g−1 to 0.26 mmolg−1 may confirm the grafting of 2-aminopyridine onto the CS@CC structure.

Characterization of CoFe2O4@CS-BAPT/CuII

FT-IR spectra

FT-IR spectra of CS-BAPT (a), CoFe2O4 (b), CoFe2O4@CS-BAPT (c), and the CoFe2O4@CS-BAPT/CuII magnetic nanocomposite are shown in Fig. 2. The broad bands in the range of 3000–3500 cm−1 belong to OH stretching, which is overlapped with NH stretching in the nanostructure (Fig. 2a–d). The absorption band in the range of 2800–3000 confirms the CH aliphatic groups (Fig. 2a–d). OH twisting is observed at 1625 cm−1 in CoFe2O4 (Fig. 2b), which shifts to 1619 cm−1 and 1617 cm−1 in the nanocomposites (Fig. 2c and d). The Fe–O band in Fig. 2b is observed at 572 cm−1 and shifts to 563 cm−1 in CoFe2O4@CS-BAPT (Fig. 2c) and CoFe2O4@CS-BAPT/CuII structure (Fig. 2d). The absorption bands in the range of 1200–1600 cm−1 are related to the presence of CN, CC, C–N, and C–O and confirm the modification of CoFe2O4 with CS-BAPT (Fig. 2c and d). In Fig. 2d, a new band is observed at 802 cm−1, confirming the presence of the Cu–O band in the nanostructure.66

FT-IR spectra of CS-BAPT (a), CoFe2O4 (b), CoFe2O4@CS-BAPT (c), and CoFe2O4@CS-BAPT/CuII magnetic nanocomposite (d) in the range of 400–4000 cm−1.

XRD patterns

The XRD patterns of CoFe2O4 (a) and CoFe2O4@CS-BAPT/CuII (b) are shown in Fig. 3. The XRD patterns in Fig. 3a show peaks at 2θ = 30.1° (220), 35.4° (311), 43.1° (400), 53.4° (422), 57.0° (511), and 62.6° (440) that are consistent with JCPDS File No. 00-001-1121.67 These patterns are also observed in the XRD pattern in Fig. 3b. All diffraction peaks of CoFe2O4 (a) and CoFe2O4@CS-BAPT/CuII (b) were assigned to the cubic spinel structure. Additionally, it can be seen that weak peaks appear at 2θ = 32.5° and 53.3° (Fig. 3b), which could be related to the formation of a Cu complex with the nitrogen groups. The Debye–Scherrer equation (d = kλ/β cos θ) was used to determine the crystallite size of CoFe2O4 (a) and CoFe2O4@CS-BAPT/CuII (b). This equation establishes a relationship between peak broadening in XRD and particle size. According to the Debye–Scherrer equation, the crystalline sizes of CoFe2O4 (a) and CoFe2O4@CS-BAPT/CuII (b) are estimated to be 12.12 nm and 7.54 nm, respectively. It appears that the modification of CoFe2O4 with CS causes the formation of a nearly amorphous structure (Fig. 3b). There is also a decrease in the intensity of peaks after the modification of CoFe2O4, confirming that the formation of CoFe2O4@CS-BAPT/CuII was successful.

XRD patterns of CoFe2O4 (a) and CoFe2O4@CS-BAPT/CuII (b) at 2θ = 10–80° at room temperature.

FE-SEM images

FE-SEM analysis was carried out to investigate the morphology and size of the CoFe2O4@CS-BAPT/CuII nanocomposite (Fig. 4). The FE-SEM images of CoFe2O4@CS-BAPT/CuII display a spherical shape and uniform distribution for CoFe2O4@CS-BAPT/CuII without any aggregation. The histogram column (Fig. 4b) extracted from FE-SEM shows an average diameter of about 12–14 nm for the CoFe2O4@CS-BAPT/CuII nanocomposite, which is consistent with the results obtained from XRD patterns.

FE-SEM images of CoFe2O4@CS-BAPT/CuII (a) and the histogram column extracted from FE-SEM using Digimizer software (version 5.7.2) (b).

EDX and MAP analyses

The results of EDX and MAP analyses of CoFe2O4@CS-BAPT/CuII are presented in Fig. 5. These analyses were used to characterize the presence of the elements and their dispersion in the nanocomposite. As can be seen, the findings confirm the presence of C, O, N, Fe, Co, and Cu elements in the structure. The weight and atomic percentages of the detected elements are also summarized in Fig. 5 (weight percentage for C: 5.34%, N: 0.87%, O: 23.14%, Fe: 45.35%, Co: 22.96%, Cu: 2.33%). The table mentioned in the right-hand corner of Fig. 5 shows a weight percentage ratio of Fe/Co of about 1.97. This ratio is consistent with the CoFe2O4 core, while the presence of C, N, and Cu confirms the successful functionalization of the magnetic nanoparticles with chitosan, BAPT, and copper species. Furthermore, the higher atomic percentages of the lighter elements (C, N, and O) compared to their weight percentages are attributed to their lower atomic masses. Additionally, ICP-OES analysis showed a loading of Cu on CoFe2O4@CS-BAPT of about 0.175 mmol g−1. The obtained results confirm that CoFe2O4@CS-BAPT/CuII has been well constructed.

The EDX and MAP analyses of CoFe2O4@CS-BAPT/CuII.

VSM analysis

VSM analysis was used in the field of ±10 000 at room temperature to investigate the effect of modification on the magnetic properties of CoFe2O4 (Fig. 6). As can be seen, the saturation magnetization (Ms) for CoFe2O4 (a) and CoFe2O4@CS-BAPT/CuII (b) was estimated to be about 55 Oe and 27 Oe, respectively. Also, the coercivity (Hc) and remanent magnetization (Mr) obtained for CoFe2O4 were 370 Oe and 21.5 emu/g, respectively, while those for CoFe2O4@CS-BAPT/CuII were −1100 Oe, and 12.3 emu/g, respectively. A comparison of the magnetic properties shows a decrease in the magnetic properties of CoFe2O4@CS-BAPT/CuII after the modification of CoFe2O4 with the organic layers of chitosan, cyanuric chloride and 2-aminopyridine.

TGA

The thermal stability of CS (a), CS-BAPT (b), and CoFe2O4@CS-BAPT/CuII (c) was investigated by TGA in the range of 25–800 °C (Fig. 7). CS (Fig. 7a) exhibited an initial weight loss of approximately 7% below 120 °C due to the removal of physically adsorbed water, followed by a major degradation step between 200 and 350 °C, which was attributed to the decomposition of the polysaccharide backbone through depolymerization and cleavage of glycosidic linkages. CS-BAPT (Fig. 7b) exhibited three weight-loss steps. The first weight loss (15%) occurred below 150 °C due to the removal of physically adsorbed water, whereas the second (175 °C < T < 425 °C) and third (425 °C < T < 800 °C) degradation stages resulted in a combined weight loss of approximately 50%, which was attributed to the thermal decomposition of the grafted organic functionalities and chitosan structure. The modification of CS with cyanuric chloride (CC) and 2-aminopyridine (2-AP) enhanced its thermal stability and resulted in a higher residual mass. The CoFe2O4@CS-BAPT/CuII nanocomposite exhibited the highest thermal stability, with a total weight loss of only 17% (2% below 125 °C and 15% between 125 and 625 °C). This behavior is mainly attributed to the thermally stable CoFe2O4 core and immobilized Cu species, as well as the strong interfacial interactions between the inorganic nanoparticles and the functionalized polymer matrix. The gradual improvement in thermal stability from CS to CS-BAPT and finally to CoFe2O4@CS-BAPT/CuII confirms the successful surface modification and the formation of a stable hybrid nanocomposite.

Zeta potential and DLS analyses

Zeta potential (a) and Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS) analyses were conducted to analyze the structure of the CoFe2O4@CS-BAPT/CuII bio-nanocomposite (Fig. 8). The zeta potential at pH 5.6 for the CoFe2O4@CS-BAPT/CuII bio-nanocomposite was estimated to be approximately +1.16 mV. This positive and low value confirms a positive surface charge for CoFe2O4@CS-BAPT/CuII with inter-particle van der Waals interactions. Consequently, there is no force to prevent the particles from coming together, leading to nanoparticle aggregation.68–70 DLS analysis (Fig. 8b) shows a uniform and Gaussian distribution with a Z-average of 11.10 nm, confirming the homogenization process and narrow particle size distribution.58

Catalytic activity of CoFe2O4@CS-BAPT/CuII in the synthesis of 2-amino-3-cyano-4H-pyran derivatives

The CoFe2O4@CS-BAPT/CuII bio-nanocomposite was synthesized and characterized, followed by an investigation of its catalytic activity in the synthesis of 2-amino-3-cyano-4H-pyrans. To achieve this, the condensation reaction of dimedone, 4-chlorobenzaldehyde, and malononitrile was selected as a model system. Subsequently, key reaction parameters such as solvent types, catalyst amounts, and temperature were optimized (Table 2). In alignment with green chemistry principles, the reaction was carried out using environmentally friendly solvents including water, ethanol, ethanol–water mixtures (1 : 1), and solvent-free conditions. Among these, ethanol–water (1 : 1) proved to be the most effective solvent (Table 2, entries 1–4). Optimization of catalyst loading revealed that increasing the amount of catalyst to 10 mg enhanced reaction efficiency and reduced reaction time. However, raising the catalyst amount beyond this threshold (up to 20 mg) produced minimal additional benefits (entries 1, 5, 6, and 9). Temperature studies showed no significant impact on the reaction progress when compared across room temperature, 50 °C, and 70 °C (entries 1, 7, and 8). Thus, the optimal conditions were determined to involve the use of 10 mg of CoFe2O4@CS-BAPT/CuII as a bio-catalyst in a mixture of ethanol–water (1 : 1) at room temperature. This approach offers advantages such as eco-friendliness, simple workup procedures, and high yields. A comparative evaluation of the catalytic performance of CoFe2O4@CS-BAPT/CuII and its individual components (CoFe2O4, CS, CS-BAPT, and Cu(OAc)2) was also conducted under similar conditions (entries 10–13). The results indicated enhanced catalytic activity for CoFe2O4@CS-BAPT/CuII, which is attributed to synergistic interactions among CoFe2O4, CS, and Cu(OAc)2.

Reusability, stability and recyclability of the bio-nanocomposite

The reusability of the CoFe2O4@CS-BAPT/CuII bio-nanocomposite was evaluated in the synthesis of compound 4b. After completing the reaction, the catalyst was removed using an external magnetic field, washed with hot EtOH several times, and reused for the synthesis of 4b. The results indicate that, after five runs, the effectiveness of the CoFe2O4@CS-BAPT/CuII bio-nanocomposite has slightly decreased. Additionally, the leaching rate of Cu ions from the CoFe2O4@CS-BAPT/CuII nanocomposite was estimated via ICP analysis after each cycle. It was observed that only 4.3% of Cu leached after the fifth cycle. Results indicate minimal loss of Cu into the organic solution and verify the heterogeneous nature of the system, thus aiding the stability of the framework under the reaction conditions (Fig. 9a).

The proposed mechanism for synthesis of the 2-amino-3-cyano-4H-pyrans in the presence of CoFe2O4@CS-BAPT/CuII

The suggested mechanism for the synthesis of 2-amino-3-cyano-4H-pyran derivatives is presented in Scheme 3. Initially, the active sites of the bio-catalyst (Lewis acid sites) activate the carbonyl groups to start a Knoevenagel condensation between malononitrile and carbonyl groups, producing intermediate I with the removal of H2O molecule. CS and CoFe2O4 also have a synergistic effect on the progress of the reaction. Subsequently, the enol form of dimedone undergoes a Michael addition to intermediate I, leading to the formation of intermediate II. Thereafter, the enolic carbon of intermediate II intramolecularly attacks the nitrile group, resulting the cyclization and formation of intermediate III. Finally, the imine–enamine tautomerization leads to the desired product.78,80

Conclusion

The present work reported the development of a novel and eco-friendly bio-catalyst, CoFe2O4@CS-BAPT/CuII nanocomposite, for three-component reactions in the synthesis of 2-amino-3-cyano-4H-pyrans. The modification of the chitosan bio-polymer by cyanuric chloride was carried out through the formation of a covalent bond between CS and CC. Then, 2-aminopyridine was reacted with the Cl groups to produce CS-BAPT. Subsequently, CoFe2O4 MNPs were loaded onto the modified polymer framework. Finally, Cu(OAc)2 was immobilized on the composite to form the CoFe2O4@CS-BAPT/CuII magnetic nanocomposite. The characterization of the bio-nanocomposites was done using Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR), X-ray Diffraction (XRD), Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA), Field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM), Energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) analysis, Vibrating Sample Magnetometry (VSM), and dynamic light scattering (DLS) analyses. The XRD patterns and FE-SEM images confirmed a nearly amorphous structure with a spherical shape and uniform distribution without any aggregation with an average size of 12–14 nm for the CoFe2O4@CS-BAPT/CuII bio-nanocomposite. TGA showed thermal stability up to 300 °C. It was utilized in the green synthesis of 2-amino-3-cyano-4H-pyrans with high conversion and selectivity. The use of an easily accessible bio-polymer, the implementation of environmentally friendly catalyst protocols, the simplification and cost-effectiveness of the synthesis process, clean reaction without additional waste generation, and the easy recycling of the catalyst are some advantages of this work. Although the present study demonstrates the efficiency of the catalyst toward a representative series of aromatic aldehydes, evaluation of a wider range of heterocyclic and aliphatic substrates will be the subject of future investigations.
