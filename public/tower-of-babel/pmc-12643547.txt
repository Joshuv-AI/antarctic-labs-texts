Artificial intelligence-generated draft replies to patient messages in pediatrics
Authors: April S Liang, Shivam Vedak, Alex Dussaq, Dong-Han Yao, Joshua A Villarreal, Sijo Thomas, Nicholas Chen, Tanya Townsend, Natalie M Pageler, Keith Morse
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study describes the utilization and experiences of artificial intelligence (AI)-generated draft responses to patient messages in pediatric ambulatory clinicians and contextualizes their experiences in relation to those of adult specialty clinicians.Materials and MethodsA prospective pilot was conducted from September 2023 to August 2024 in 2 pediatric clinics (General Pediatric and Adolescent Medicine) and 2 obstetric clinics (Reproductive Endocrinology and Infertility and General Obstetrics) within an academic health system in Northern California. Participants included physician, nurse, and medical assistant volunteers. The intervention involved a feature utilizing large language models embedded in the electronic health record to generate draft responses. Proportion of AI-generated draft used was collected, as were prepilot and follow-up surveys.ResultsA total of 61 clinicians (26 pediatric, 35 obstetric) enrolled, with 46 (75%) completing both surveys. Pediatric clinicians utilized 13.3% (95% CI, 12.3%-14.4%) of AI-generated drafts, and usage rates when responding to patients vs their proxies was similar (15% vs 12.9%, P = .24). Despite using AI-generated drafts significantly less than obstetric clinicians (18.3% [17.2%-19.5%], P < .0001), pediatric clinicians reported a significant reduction in perceived task load (NASA Task Load Index: 59.9–50.9, P = .04) and were more likely to recommend the tool (LTR: 7.0 vs 5.2, P = .04).Discussion and ConclusionPediatric clinicians used AI-generated drafts at a rate within previously reported ranges in adult specialties and experienced utility. These findings suggest this tool has potential for enhancing efficiency and reducing task load in pediatric care.

Introduction

Clinicians dedicate substantial time responding to patient messages in the electronic health record (EHR),1–5 an activity identified as a contributor to clinician burnout.2,6–9 Large language models, or LLMs, have demonstrated potential in responding to patient queries with favorable quality and empathy.10–13 Several health systems have piloted an EHR-integrated LLM to automatically draft responses to patient messages, demonstrating modest utilization, reduced perceived task load, and no significant change in time expenditure.14–16 However, all were conducted in adult primary and subspecialty care, and the performance of this tool in pediatric care settings remains unknown. This gap is particularly concerning given evidence that LLMs may perform poorly when applied to pediatric use cases in the absence of additional fine-tuning,17–19 or have differential performance across the pediatric age ranges.20 Furthermore, pediatric patient messages have the added complexity of proxy authorship, where messages are written by parents or guardians on behalf of the patient. Large language models may not reliably identify these instances, potentially resulting in inappropriate tone, incorrect information, or loss of confidentiality.21,22 Understanding the utilization and impact of artificial intelligence (AI)-generated draft replies in pediatrics is therefore essential to ensuring the equity and safety of this novel technology.23 We report utilization and user experience of this technology in 2 pediatric ambulatory specialties at a pediatric/obstetric academic medical center. Leveraging our institution’s unique organizational structure, we also report outcomes from 2 obstetric ambulatory specialties in the same health system. Results from these obstetric practices serve as a proxy reference for the adult specialty clinician experience, controlling for institution-specific implementation considerations (eg, prompt configuration, workflows) and ensuring comparability with prior studies conducted in adult care settings.

Methods

Technology implementation

Stanford Medicine Children’s Health activated the integrated AI-generated draft response feature to patient messages provided by our EHR vendor (Epic Systems Corporation, Verona, WI, United States). This feature displays an LLM-composed draft response beneath eligible patient messages, and clinicians who have been provisioned access may choose to use the AI-generated draft reply as a starting point or to start with a blank response after review. In accordance with local regulations and institutional guidance, a disclaimer on the use of AI-generated content was not appended to drafted responses. Further workflow details have previously been described in Garcia et al.14 A single organizational prompt including structured EHR data elements was developed to handle all 4 vendor-determined patient message categories (general, results, medications, and paperwork) (eMethods, Supplementary Material). The underlying LLM model was gpt-4-0613 with temperature set to 0. Vendor requirements for prompt validation in a test environment were fulfilled prior to implementation. The feature did not support non-English messages, messages with attachments, or nonpatient advice request message types (eg, staff message, results message). Draft responses to messages sent by a proxy for patients <12 years old or ≥18 years old were enabled on January 22, 2024.

Project design, setting, and participant enrollment

This prospective pilot of AI-generated drafts was conducted at a single academic pediatric/obstetric health system in Northern California (Stanford Medicine Children’s Health) between September 27, 2023, and August 26, 2024. In addition to 2 ambulatory pediatric specialty practices (Adolescent Medicine and General Pediatrics), we included 2 ambulatory obstetric specialty practices (Reproductive Endocrinology and Infertility [REI] and General Obstetrics) to control for institution-specific implementation factors and contextualize results in relation to prior studies conducted exclusively in adult specialties. Pilot enrollment occurred in chronological waves by specialty. Acknowledging that differences in clinician responsibilities and workflows existed in handling patient messages between the various clinics and specialties, all physicians (including trainees), clinical nurses, and medical assistants within each practice, were eligible to participate, maximizing role inclusivity. Interested participants reviewed educational materials and completed a mandatory online presurvey before gaining access to the automated draft response feature.

Data collection and user experience measures

Draft-level data including message recipient, whether the AI-generated draft was viewed by the pilot user, and whether the AI-generated draft was used as a starting point were generated using EHR-provided reporting tools on a monthly basis. Draft-level usage data were collected over 11 months between September 27, 2023, and August 26, 2024.

The mandatory presurvey collected participant specialty, role, and select demographic information. After at least 8 weeks of tool access, pilot users were emailed an invitation to complete a follow-up survey. Both surveys assessed the perceived workload of responding to patient messages using the nonweighted, 6-item NASA Task Load Index (NASA-TLX).24 Task load scores range from 0 to 100, with lower scores indicating less subjective demand. Burnout was evaluated using the 10-item Mini Z 2.0 Worklife and Burnout Reduction Instrument (Mini Z).25 Mini Z scores range from 10 to 50, with higher scores indicating better satisfaction, less burnout, and a more supportive workplace. The follow-up survey also asked users to estimate the impact of AI-generated draft responses on message response time, ranging from –5 (saves 5 min per message) to 5 (adds 5 min per message). Satisfaction was measured using likelihood to recommend (LTR), ranging from 0 (not at all likely) to 10 (extremely likely),26 and the related net promoter score (NPS). Net promoter score is calculated by subtracting the percentage of detractors (LTR 0-6) from the percentage of promoters (LTR 9-10) and ranges from −100 to 100, with higher scores indicating greater satisfaction.26 The content of both surveys is available in eMethods (Supplementary Material).

Statistical analysis

Draft utilization was defined as the percentage of clinician replies that used the AI-generated draft as a starting point among all instances where an AI-generated draft response was displayed to a clinician during the pilot period. Bootstrap resampling (10 000 iterations with replacement, sample size matching the smaller group in the comparison analysis) was performed for 2 comparisons: (1) utilization rates between pediatric and obstetric clinician groups and (2) within the pediatric group, utilization for replies to messages from proxy accounts vs those from patients directly. Two-tailed bootstrap hypothesis tests were used to compare utilization between groups. The 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) for draft utilization were calculated using the percentile method. Statistical analysis of draft utilization was performed using R version 4.4.2 (R Core Team).

Clinician demographics were described with counts and proportions. User experience survey results were paired using respondents’ unique institutional identifiers. Summary statistics were calculated as means and SDs. For measures assessed in both pre- and follow-up surveys (NASA-TLX, Mini Z), paired t-tests were used to detect significant changes. Measures evaluated in only the follow-up survey (LTR, perceived time impact) were compared between the obstetric and pediatric clinician groups using 2-tailed t-tests. Net promoter score was calculated for both clinician groups as previously described and compared using a 2-tailed t-test. P < .05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analysis of survey data was performed using Python version 3.10.9 (Python Software Foundation).

Ethical approval

This project was given a nonhuman subjects research determination following review by the Stanford University Institutional Review Board on December 27, 2024 (Protocol 78727). STrengthening the Reporting of OBservational studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines were followed.

Results

Out of 131 total eligible, 61 clinicians (47%) enrolled in the pilot, including 26 pediatric clinicians (39% of 67 invited) and 35 obstetric clinicians (55% of 64 invited) (Table 1). Pediatric users (43% of pilot group) comprised attending physicians, nurses, and medical assistants, while obstetric users (57% of pilot group) included attending physicians, nurses, and trainees (fellow physicians). The proportion of message replies sent by each clinical role (physicians, nurses, and medical assistants) for the 2 pilot groups over the pilot duration is available as eFigure S1. Most pilot users in both groups were female, with those aged 35-44 years and those more than 15 years beyond training representing the largest proportions. Forty-six clinicians (75%) completed both prepilot and 8-week follow-up surveys and were included in the analysis of self-reported metrics (Table 1). Survey respondents were demographically representative of the pilot population (Table 1).

During the pilot period, AI-generated draft responses were displayed for 4325 patient messages to pediatric clinicians and 15 431 messages to obstetric clinicians. Draft utilization was significantly higher among obstetric clinicians (18.3% [95% CI, 17.2%-19.5%]; 2829 drafts used) than pediatric clinicians (13.3% [95% CI, 12.3%-14.4%]; 577 drafts used) (P < .0001) (Table 2). Among pediatric patient messages where an AI-drafted response was displayed, 3493 originated from proxy accounts and 832 from patient accounts. No significant difference in draft utilization by pediatric clinicians was observed when responding to messages from proxy accounts (12.9% [95% CI, 10.7%-15.3%]) vs patient accounts (15.0% [95% CI, 12.6%-17.5%]) (P = .24).

At 8-week follow-up, pediatric clinicians reported a mean (SD) perceived time savings of 1.2 (1.7) min per patient message response using AI-generated drafts compared with 0.74 (2.3) min for obstetric clinicians (P = .4) (Table 3). Pediatric clinicians experienced significant reduction in mean task load when responding to patient messages, from 59.9 (20.7) at baseline to 50.9 (19.3) at 8 weeks (P = .04) (Table 4). No significant reduction in mean task load was observed for obstetric clinicians (64.2 [13.5]-63.2 [19.3], P = .7), and burnout scores did not significantly change over the follow-up period for either group (Table 4). While pediatric users reported significantly higher likelihood to recommend the tool (7.0 [2.1]) compared with obstetric clinicians (5.2 [3.2], P = .04), NPSs were not significantly different between groups (−10.5 pediatric vs −40.7 obstetric, P = .2) (Table 3).

To investigate the correlation between clinicians’ likelihood to recommend AI-generated drafts and their draft utilization, the Pearson correlation coefficient was calculated and found to be 0.31 with R2 0.095 (eFigure S2), suggesting poor linear correlation between the 2 measures.

Discussion

In one of the first pilots of AI-generated draft replies to patient messages in ambulatory pediatric settings, we found that pediatric clinicians used approximately 13% of the generated drafts. Obstetric clinicians within the same pilot had a utilization rate of approximately 18%, which closely matches the 12%-20% range previously reported for similar tools in adult ambulatory settings.14,16 This concordance suggests that our implementation and adoption experience were comparable to those of prior adult studies, allowing us to interpret our pediatric clinicians’ usage within that same context. The 13% utilization rate observed in our pediatric clinicians therefore closely aligns with those prior adult provider ranges. Furthermore, pediatric clinicians used AI-generated drafts at similar rates when responding directly to patients (15%) and to patient proxies (13%). These findings are reassuring given the high prevalence of proxy-authored communication in pediatric care and support the feasibility of deploying AI-generated draft replies in this setting.

Although overall pediatric utilization rates were comparable to adult groups based on prior studies, we observed a statistically significant difference between the pediatric and obstetric cohorts in our pilot. This difference is less clinically meaningful in the context of the previously reported utilization range that encompasses both groups but likely reflects factors beyond patient age alone, such as variation in practice patterns and clinician role composition. Notably, 93% of message replies within the obstetrics group were sent by nurses (eFigure S1), and previous studies have noted that nurses may demonstrate higher draft utilization and perceived utility.14,16,27 These findings highlight the importance of role composition and underscore the need for dedicated research on how clinician role influences draft utilization and adoption behavior.

Beyond utilization, user experiences with the tool also differed across the groups. Pediatric clinicians reported a significant reduction in perceived task load when responding to patient messages at 8-week follow-up, accompanied by a perceived time savings of approximately 1.2 min per message and an overall positive likelihood of recommending the tool. These findings suggest that deployment of AI-generated drafts resulted in meaningful well-being and perceived efficiency benefits for pediatric users, reducing cognitive burden and facilitating quicker responses even in the absence of specialty-specific customization. In comparison, obstetric clinicians did not report a significant reduction in perceived task load over the same interval, showed a statistically identical perceived time savings, and expressed a significantly lower likelihood to recommend the tool. These between-group differences are likely—similar to those in utilization rate—multifactorial and attributable to variation in practice patterns, message content, and clinician role composition between specialties.

Neither group demonstrated significant changes in burnout scores over the 8-week follow-up period, suggesting that the perceived efficiency gains (and, in the case of pediatrics, reduced cognitive burden) were insufficient to meaningfully affect this multidimensional, slow-to-change construct within a short time horizon. Finally, the overall negative NPSs for both groups and lack of statistical difference in this metric between groups likely reflect the mechanics of the NPS scale itself, which heavily penalizes likelihood-to-recommend scores below 9 and is sensitive to even a small proportion of detractors.

The disconnect between pediatric clinicians’ higher likelihood to recommend this technology but lower draft utilization rate warrants further investigation. The weakness of the linear correlation between these 2 measures suggests that clinicians base their recommendation on factors distinct from those determining whether to use individual AI-generated drafts. It is possible that clinicians derive value from simply viewing the generated draft, which can offer a useful starting point or phrasing cues that ease the cognitive effort required to compose a response, even if they choose not to use the draft itself. While draft utilization rate remains an important metric when evaluating novel AI technologies, our findings emphasize the need for a multifaceted evaluation approach that incorporates qualitative user feedback.

Strengths of this evaluation include the large message volumes and extended follow-up period of 11 months compared with previously published studies, all of which had limited duration. Utilization rates from this study may therefore be more representative of a long-term equilibrium once initial “hype” surrounding this novel feature has dissipated. Limitations of this pilot evaluation include potential volunteer bias and small sample size, wherein motivated participants who volunteered for the pilot may have different usage patterns than the general underlying clinician population. While the survey response rate was high and the respondents were representative of the pilot user population, selection bias nonetheless limits the interpretation of user-reported outcomes as not all pilot users completed the survey. As the emphasis was on users’ lived experience and perception of the tool, time savings relied on participant self-report rather than objective EHR time data; therefore, the true time impact of AI-generated draft replies on responding to patient messages was not examined in this study. Additionally, the pilot included only a limited number of pediatric and obstetric subspecialties, which may not reflect potential experiences across other subspecialties. Finally, the single-site design and availability of drafted responses only for messages in English may limit the generalizability of the study to other health systems or patient messages written in other languages.

Conclusion

Pediatric clinicians used AI-generated draft replies at rates similar to those previously reported in adult settings, reported reductions in task load, and had comparable or higher likelihood to recommend the tool. Our findings provide reassurance regarding the utility and generalizability of this technology in pediatric settings. Health systems should not delay implementation of this promising tool in pediatric care based on concerns about poor applicability.
