Assessment and management of torsional malalignment in paediatric patients with patellofemoral joint instability
Authors: Franck Accadbled, Tim Cheok, Vincent Martinel, Jaideep Rawat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTorsional malalignment is an important but frequently underrecognized contributor to patellofemoral instability (PFI) in children and adolescents. This review aimed to provide a clinically focused overview of the development, assessment, imaging evaluation, and contemporary management of femoral and tibial torsional abnormalities associated with PFI.MethodsA literature search of PubMed and Embase was performed from database inception, using the terms ((patella) OR (patellofemoral)) AND ((rotation) OR (torsion)). Titles and abstracts were screened by two authors, followed by full-text review and synthesis of relevant literature. Evidence concerning developmental changes, clinical and dynamic assessment, imaging modalities, associations with PFI, and surgical management was evaluated.ResultsFemoral anteversion decreases and tibial external torsion increases during childhood, although substantial physiological variability limits the use of universal normative thresholds. Clinical assessment, including hip rotation, thigh–foot angle, gait, and patellar tracking, is essential but demonstrates inter- and intraobserver variability. CT remains a reference standard for torsional measurement, whereas MRI and EOS imaging provide radiation-sparing alternatives. Increased femoral anteversion, tibial torsion, and tibiofemoral rotation are associated with PFI and may contribute to failure of isolated soft-tissue procedures. In selected symptomatic patients, derotational osteotomy, frequently combined with MPFL reconstruction or other procedures addressing concomitant risk factors, can provide favorable clinical outcomes.ConclusionsEvaluation of pediatric PFI should incorporate torsional. Treatment should be individualized according to symptoms, skeletal maturity, clinical findings, dynamic function, and anatomical contributors rather than imaging values alone. Recognition and correction of clinically significant torsional malalignment may improve stability and reduce treatment failure.

Introduction

While coronal and sagittal plane alignment have traditionally dominated pediatric orthopedic evaluation, axial plane alignment is equally important. Femoral and tibial torsion change predictably throughout growth and directly influence limb rotation, gait patterns, and joint loading. Persistent torsional malalignment has been increasingly implicated in patellofemoral instability (PFI), anterior knee pain, in-toeing and functional gait disturbances. A recent survey assessing the experience of pediatric orthopedic surgeon’s practice in the management of PFI in their patient population have found that 21% of respondents offered angular deformity correction as a staged procedure. 1

For the surgeon dealing with PFI, understanding torsional development, mastering clinical assessment, and recognizing the limitations of static imaging are essential. The purpose of this review is to provide a concise, clinically focused overview of torsional malalignment in children and adolescents, emphasizing practical assessment strategies and contemporary treatment principles.

Methods

A literature search of PubMed and Embase was performed using the following search terms and Boolean operators ((patella) OR (patellofemoral)) AND ((rotation) OR (torsion)) from the date of inception of each database to the 22nd of May 2026. The titles and abstracts were screened by two authors for relevance in regard to the broad aims of this review, before carefully curating and summarizing the full text.

Development of Patellofemoral Parameters and Lower Limb Torsion

Patellofemoral parameters are postulated to be influenced by the child’s intrauterine position. Röttinger et al. have demonstrated an association between breech position and a decrease in patellar height. This in turn leads to a tendency to develop a flatter trochlear morphology. 2 Additionally, Kong et al. have also shown that distal femoral torsion alters stress distribution across the epiphyseal plate in mice models. Excessive distal femoral torsion resulted in greater degrees of trochlear dysplasia. 3

Femoral anteversion is greatest at birth (approximately 40°) and gradually decreases during childhood, whereas tibial torsion typically increases with growth (2 – 4° at birth). Longitudinal studies have demonstrated wide physiologic variability, with the tibia externally rotating 15 – 20°, and the femur externally rotating by 25° (thereby reducing femoral anteversion) by 8 – 10 years. 4 Of note, there is a substantial overlap between age groups, and as a result, there is no universally accepted normal range for femoral or tibial torsion.5,6 Despite this variability, femoral torsion values below 5° or above 30°, and tibial torsion below 20° or above 50°, are commonly regarded as abnormal. Spontaneous modification of torsion after approximately 11 years of age in girls and 13 years in boys is minimal, making persistent abnormalities beyond these ages unlikely to remodel. There is a large variation in thresholds for derotational osteotomy, however a recent survey of the International Patellofemoral Study Group has found that >30°of femoral anteversion and >35° of external tibial torsion to be generally accepted thresholds. 7 The clinical evidence for these thresholds would be explored further in this review.

Normal and Pathological Patellar Tracking

The trochlear groove does not have a consistent geometry across its length. This variation in steepness and depth of the trochlear grove thus affects the imbalance in forces required to induce PFI. 8 Under normal circumstances, medial to lateral translation of the patella is predominantly influenced by trochlear topography, whereas patellar rotation on the anterior to posterior axis (spin) and proximal to distal axis (tilt) is controlled by retro patellar topography. 9 As the knee goes from extension to flexion, the patella flexes by 0.7 times the tibiofemoral flexion angle and translates medially by 2 – 5 mm to engage the trochlear groove at 20 – 30° of knee flexion but then translates 7 mm laterally as the knee goes into 90° of knee flexion. At 90° of knee flexion, the patella tilts laterally by 4 – 7°.10–14 Interestingly, the amount of medial/lateral patella translation is less pronounced when weightbearing as internal femoral rotation is less pronounced when compared to the non-weightbearing condition. 15 Additionally, due to the “screw-home” mechanism, the tibia internally rotates, which improves patellar stability by decreasing the tibial tubercle-trochlear groove (TT-TG) distance.11,13,16–18 A recent 4D-computed tomography (CT) study showed that in patients with recurrent patella dislocations, the tibia began to externally rotate in the early phases of knee extension. 19 Hamada et al. described an increase in external femoral rotation after 50° of knee flexion in healthy volunteers. 20 In patients with rotational misalignment, the anterior aspect of the knee may be facing medially and/or the tibia externally, with resultant PFI. 21

A widely recognized aspect of bony alignment is the Q-angle. 22 This is the angle between the lines of action of the patellar tendon and the resultant force of the quadriceps in full knee extension. The normal Q-angle is 10 – 15° in males and 15 – 20° in females. 23 In terminal extension, the Q-angle is largest, as the tibia rotates externally due to the “screw-home” mechanism. Nevertheless, it should be noted that the Q-angle is difficult to measure in clinical practice due to the mobility of the patella, with some authors advocating that this is measured with the patella relocated manually in the trochlear groove and the knee fully extended.8,24 Anatomic variations such as increased external tibial torsion, increased femoral anteversion, valgus knee alignment and increased TT-TG distance can increase the Q-angle, and thus predisposing patients to PFI.25–27

Clinical Assessment

Clinical History

The classic mechanism for patella dislocation involves a non-contact valgus directed moment to a flexed knee with the tibia externally rotated. 28 In the assessment of patients with PFI, salient information from history taking include 1 mechanism of index dislocation (traumatic versus atraumatic), 2 mechanism of current injury, 3 presence of mechanical symptoms which may indicate an intra-articular loose body, 4 frequency as well as its impact on activities of daily living or sporting endeavors, and 5 dislocation type (acute, congenital, habitual, recurrent, or fixed). 23 Certain genetic disorders, such as nail-patella syndrome, may result in patella hypoplasia, which is a risk factor for dislocation. 29 Acquired neuromuscular disorders, such as spastic tetraparesis, can result in significant muscular imbalances that results in PFI. 30 A family history of conditions that may predispose the patient to torsional malalignment, such as achondroplasia, rickets, and metaphyseal dysplasia should also be taken. 4 Patients with rotational malalignment, trochlear dysplasia and increased patella height are at risk of recurrent patella dislocations.31,32 Huntington et al. showed that the presence of three or more risk factors (younger age, open physes, trochlear dysplasia, increased TT-TG distance and patella alta) in a patient who had a patella dislocation was associated with a 78.5% risk of recurrence. 33 The WARPS/STAID classification may be used to separate patients into two distinct subgroups, which is useful to guide clinicians on further management. 34

Physical Examination

Initial observation should focus on overall limb posture, patellar orientation, and foot position in stance. 35 Pes planovalgus should be identified, as it may accentuate apparent rotational abnormalities. Normally, the patellae face forward with symmetric intercondylar and intermalleolar distances. Genu valgum is a risk factor for PFI due to increased Q-angle. A “squinting patella” (patellae pointing inward toward each other when standing or walking) suggests excessive femorotibial torsion (Figure 1).36,37 Generalized ligamentous laxity can be quantified using the Beighton hypermobility score. This is crucial, as excessive motion through the hip and knee can exaggerate axial malalignment without corresponding bony deformity. 23 Further, evaluation of the patient’s gait should be performed. Specific for torsional assessment, an abnormal foot progression angle is indicative of torsional malalignment. With the patient in a supine position, the bulk, tone and strength of lower limb muscles should be assessed. Patella tracking during active and passive range of movement should also be observed. The presence of a J sign when assessing patella tracking is suggestive of excessive tibiofemoral rotation, increased TT-TG distance, and increased patella height. Additionally, the presence of a “jump” is associated with underlying trochlear anomalies and can predispose the patient to failure of medial patellofemoral ligament (MPFL) reconstruction.38–45 The passive patella glide test should also be performed with the knee flexed at 20°. If the patella glides less than a quadrant, this indicates lateral tightness, whereas gliding more than three quadrants may indicate hypermobility.23,46,47 The patella apprehension test may also be conducted by applying a laterally directed force on the patella as the knee is moved from extension to flexion. A positive test, indicated by apprehension or quadriceps muscle activation is highly sensitive and specific for PFI.23,46–48 It is important to acknowledge that all of these findings have low to moderate inter-observer reliability. 49

Femoral torsion is assessed using the trochanteric prominence angle test (Figure 2) 50 and by measuring hip internal and external rotation with the patient prone. Normal range is 30° to 70° both for internal and external rotation. Excessive femoral anteversion is suggested by increased internal rotation with limited external rotation. A practical approximation considers femoral torsion to be roughly one-third of the measured hip internal rotation, although this relationship is imperfect. Tibial torsion is evaluated using the thigh–foot angle with the knee flexed to 90° and the subtalar joint held in neutral (Figure 3). Another technique to measure tibial torsion is the measurement of transmalleolar axis, measured with the knee flexed at 90° (Figure 4). Even in experienced hands, inter- and intraobserver variability of 5–10° should be expected and acknowledged during decision-making.Figure 2.Trochanteric prominence test for assessing femoral torsion. Gait Analysis and Functional Correlation

Instrumented gait analysis has highlighted the complexity of PFI. Scranz et al. have described three discrete compensatory gait strategies in adolescents to cope with recurrent PFI – 1 the patella overloading group had a high knee flexion angle during the stance phase and pronounced internal rotation of the foot, 2 the patella unloading group had a decreased knee flexion during loading response with increased hip abduction as well as hip abductor moment in stance, and 3 the patella norm-loading group with near normal knee movement but pronounced internal rotation of foot. 51

In adolescents with PFI, static femoral torsion measured on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) does not consistently correlate with dynamic hip rotation during stance. Patients with excessive femoral torsion may demonstrate relatively normal dynamic hip rotation because compensatory mechanisms occur at the hip, knee, and foot, potentially masking the underlying torsional deformity. These findings underscore that torsional measurements alone are insufficient. Functional alignment reflects the interaction between bone morphology, soft-tissue constraints, and neuromuscular control, all of which must be considered during evaluation.

Imaging Evaluation

When clinical findings and symptoms warrant further investigation, imaging should be used to define the anatomical risk factors leading to PFI. If torsion is suspected, imaging is helpful to quantify the degree of torsional deformity and to identify the anatomic level of the deformity. Briefly, imaging options include plain radiographs, EOS low dose biplanar radiography, and three-dimensional imaging (e.g., CT and/or MRI). CT has historically served as the reference standard but carries concerns regarding radiation exposure. MRI provides reliable torsional measurements without ionizing radiation and is increasingly preferred in pediatric and adolescent patients. More recently, EOS radiographs enable three-dimensional assessment with minimal radiation and is particularly useful for comprehensive lower-limb alignment evaluation. 52 Several studies have demonstrated that software-based measurements of torsion may be more accurate in less experienced users.53,54

Plain Radiographs

Plain radiographs are crucial in the initial assessments of patients. Important views include anteroposterior (AP) weightbearing views, long-leg alignment view, true lateral, and Merchant/Laurin views of the patella. Assessment of patella height, trochlear dysplasia, and significant torsional deformities can be made using these views. 23 Dong et al. described the medial condylar angle as a predictor of femoral anteversion on weightbearing long-leg alignment views. 55 Similarly, González-Herranz and Penelas-Abelleira have described a technique to measure tibial torsion using long-leg alignment views. 56 These techniques are potentially useful for follow-up imaging in patients who have undergone corrective osteotomies.

Three-Dimensional Imaging

TT-TG Distance: This is measured by drawing a line along the coronal plane between the deepest point in the trochlear groove and the center of the most anterior part of the tibial tubercle, parallel to the posterior femoral condylar line. As previously mentioned, an increased TT-TG distance implies a lateralized tibial tubercle, which increases the Q-angle, resulting in a propensity of PFI. The TT-TG distance can also be influenced by both tibiofemoral rotation and trochlear dysplasia,57–66 and is consistently measured lower on MRI as compared to CT.67–69 Normative mean values for TT-TG distance are 13.6 ± 8.8 mm when measured on CT and 10.0 ± 1.0 mm.70,71 In children, TT-TG distance increases by 0.39 mm annually until 15 years of age. 72

Tibial Tubercle – Posterior Cruciate Ligament (TT-PCL) Distance: This is measured on axial MRI between the midpoint of the patella insertion and the medial border of the posterior cruciate ligament at its tibial insertion, parallel to the posterior tibia condylar line. 73 Like the TT-TG distance, this is a measure of lateralization of the tibial tubercle, however it is not influenced by tibiofemoral rotation and trochlear dysplasia and is thus considered to be more accurate.58,59,64,73,74 The normative mean value for TT-PCL distance have been reported as 16.5 ± 3.5 mm. 75 In children, TT-PCL distance increases by 1.14 mm annually until 11 years of age. 72

Femoral Anteversion Angle: This is the angle between the proximal femoral neck axis (line connecting the center of femoral head to the center of the base of the femoral neck) and the posterior condylar axis of the femur (Figure 5). This can be measured using both CT and MRI with excellent agreement. 76 Chen et al. have shown that increased femoral anteversion measured on CT using the transepicondylar axis as reference (>18.2°) was more predictive of PFI as compared to TT-TG distance. 77 Other authors have also used the posterior femoral condylar axis as a refence point, which can be flawed in cases where there is concomitant trochlear dysplasia, as this may lead to hypoplasia of the posterior femoral condyle.78–81 Normative mean values of femoral anteversion decrease with age, from 31.1 ± 8.9° at the age of one to 15.4 ± 7.6° at the age of sixteen. 82 Figure 5.Overlay technique for measurement of Femoral Version (FV).

Overlay technique for measurement of Femoral Version (FV).

Distal Femoral Torsion Angle: This is the angle between tangent line of the posterior distal femoral shaft at the level of the popliteal surface, just proximal to the gastrocnemius muscle, and the posterior femoral condylar axis. 78 Chen et al. have found that this was a more reliable measure to predict PFI. A recent study by Fan et al. have found that distal femoral torsion was present in patients with normal femoral anteversion and presents as a significant risk factor for PFI. 83 A further study has found a positive correlation between distal femoral torsion with high grade trochlear dysplasia and shortened anterior condyles. 84 The normative adult mean values of this measurement were 8.6 ± 3.5° in males and 8.0 ± 3.0° in females. 79 It is unclear whether normative values vary based on age and should be interpreted with caution in the pediatric population.

Quadriceps Torsion Angle: This is the angle measured on MRI between a line running from the anterior aspect of the sartorius medially, across the leg to the junction of the anterior and posterior compartments at the lateral intermuscular septum, and a horizontal line. Maine et al., who initially described this measure, have found that a quadriceps torsion angle of >53° was predictive of PFI in 88% of patients. 85 It is important to note that this measure has not been externally validated and should therefore also be applied with caution.

Tibial Tubercle Torsion Angle: This is the angle between the tibial tubercle transverse axis and the posterior femoral condylar axis. 86 Normative values for tibial tubercle torsion varied substantially between studies. Chassaing et al. reported mean values of 24.7 ± 5.2°, 87 whereas Jud et al. reported that of 12.0 ± 4.4°. 88 Furthermore, Jud et al. found that tubercle torsion value of ≥17.7 had an increased risk of PFI, outperforming other commonly assessed parameters, such as TT-TG distance and TT-PCL distance. 88 Nevertheless, the substantial variation in normative values makes this measure overall less reliable.

Total Tibial Torsion: This is the angle between a line tangential to the posterior aspect of the tibial plateau and a line through the centers of the medial and lateral malleoli (Figure 6). This has been associated with trochlear dysplasia, varus malalignment and increased femoral anteversion in patients with PFI.89,90 Increased total tibial torsion of > 35° indicates an infra-tuberosity deformity. 91 The normative mean value of this measurement is 21.6 ± 7.6° in adults. 92 It is also unclear whether normative values vary based on age and should be interpreted with caution in the pediatric population.Figure 6.Overlay technique for measurement of Tibial Torsion (TT).

Overlay technique for measurement of Tibial Torsion (TT).

Tibiofemoral Rotation: This is the angle between the posterior femoral condylar axis and the proximal tibial posterior condylar axis. It has also been termed knee rotation angle, through-knee angle and knee version. Although this has been shown to be a predictor of PFI, 93 it is important to appreciate that MRI-based measurements consistently yield lower results as compared to CT and thresholds should not be applied uniformly.94–98 The mean tibiofemoral rotation was 1.3 ± 3.9° on MRI 99 and 5.4 ± 5.7° on CT 100 in healthy adult populations, with a paucity of information on whether there is a significant effect of patient age on this value. Patients with fixed or obligatory PFI have shown to have greater tibiofemoral rotation as compared to those with traumatic PFI (mean = 12.3° vs 5.4°).

EOS Biplanar Radiography

Low dose EOS biplanar radiographs with 3D reconstruction have been shown to be an excellent alternative to both CT for assessment of femoral anteversion and total tibial torsion.101,102 This modality is also useful in the assessment of coronal alignment. 103 Additionally, Zhang et al. have also reported reproducible TT-TG distance measurements using EOS radiographs and were comparable with CT-based measurements. 104 Of interest, Chen et al. showed that in patients with recurrent PFI, femoral anteversion was significantly smaller in the upright position (measured on EOS radiographs) than in the supine position (measured on CT scan), suggesting that the distal femur is more internally rotated in this patient cohort. 105

Torsional Malalignment and Patellofemoral Instability

PFI is a result of a complex interplay between multiple anatomical factors. It is important to remember that torsional malalignment does not generally exist in isolation and is usually associated with other risk factors, such as patella alta, lateralization of the tibial tubercle and/or trochlear dysplasia.30,106,107 Excessive femoral anteversion increases lateralizing forces on the patella, particularly in early knee flexion, and may overwhelm soft-tissue stabilizers. Tibial torsion appears to influence gait patterns more than static patellar position but can exacerbate instability when combined with femoral abnormalities or tibial tubercle malposition.

Advanced imaging studies have demonstrated that torsional abnormalities often arise from multiple anatomic segments.79,100,108–111 In PFI, excessive torsion of the femoral shaft has been associated with increased lateral PFJ forces due to the quadriceps attachment. 112 Another CT scan-based study showed that torsional deformity occurs along the length of the tibia but mostly in the shaft and distal segments of the femur. It suggested that, rather than the distal femur rotating internally on its own, the distal femur and the proximal tibia simultaneously rotate internally, generating an internal rotation deformity of the knee. 113

Biomechanical investigations using three-dimensional motion analysis have shown that increasing internal femoral rotation significantly elevates retro patellar pressure and patellar tilt. These effects are amplified in the setting of medial patellofemoral ligament insufficiency, providing a biomechanical rationale for addressing torsion in selected patients with recurrent instability. 32 Patients with an increased femoral anteversion (> 30°) have inferior postoperative patient-reported outcomes, greater patellar laxity, higher rate of residual J-sign after MPFL reconstruction and combined tibial tubercle osteotomy for the treatment of PFI.114,115

There is also a subset of patients with normal values of femoral and tibial torsion but with increased tibiofemoral rotation through the knee joint and PJI.65,93 The mechanism behind this is not well understood and requires further investigation.

Surgical Management

Conservative management of PFI is still the mainstay of initial treatment, with a focus on quadriceps (vastus medialis oblique) strengthening, patellar mobilization, core strengthening and hip abductor strengthening. 23 This is usually indicated following single episode traumatic dislocation with no mechanical symptoms which would indicate an intra-articular loose body. Where conservative management is inadequate, surgical management of PFI commonly adheres to the “menu à la carte” approach. This approach first described by the Lyon School of Knee surgery in 1987, with periodic updates, aims to address the anatomical risk factors predisposing the patient to PFI. 29 Importantly, torsional malalignment is not considered in this approach but is a risk factor for failure.116–120

MPFL reconstruction and tibial tubercle osteotomies are two of the most common surgical procedures performed for PFI. Interestingly, these procedures may correct a small amount of torsional malalignment.121,122 An important surgical consideration in the pediatric population is the presence of open physes, which presents both a challenge and an opportunity. As tibial tubercle osteotomy (TTO) is not an option, lateral transposition of the extensor mechanism (e.g., Grammont, Roux-Goldthwait) may be considered. Similarly, in cases of coronal malalignment, hemi-epiphysiodesis could also be considered. 30

Whilst the goal may be to correct the femoral anteversion angle to approximately 10°, providers should be cognizant that this may result in significant out-toeing. Indications for distal femoral osteotomy varied substantially in literature, with femoral anteversion angles of 20 – 30° often being cited. As evidenced by the summary of literature in Table 1, re-dislocation rates following distal femoral osteotomy is very low in appropriately selected patients, with a mean postoperative improvement in Kujala and Lysholm scores of between 20 – 30 points to be expected. There is also generally a small improvement in Tegner activity score postoperatively. Zhang et al. compared distal femoral osteotomy with concurrent MPFL reconstruction to MPFL reconstruction only in patients with tibiofemoral rotation angle of >12°. 136 The latter group had an almost 20% re-dislocation rate, underscoring the importance of identifying and correcting severe rotational malalignment.

Although several authors have highlighted the theoretical importance of segmental femoral torsion (rather than femoral anteversion) when determining the level of osteotomy,79,110 the clinical implication is less apparent. Xu et al. have shown that the femoral anteversion angle had better diagnostic performance over segmental values to predict PFI. 139 Following distal femoral osteotomy, Fan et al. found no difference in clinical outcomes between proximal and distal torsion groups, whereas Jia et al. reported better correction and greater improvement in Tegner activity scores as well as quality-of-life indices (EQ-5D-5L and EQ-VAS) in the proximal torsion group. Nonetheless, there was no difference in re-dislocation rates and the improvement in Kujala and Lysholm scores.126,129

Technological Advancements in Surgical Planning and Technique

As alluded to in earlier sections, de-rotation osteotomies often result in correction in multiple planes. For example, a cadaveric study by Kaiser et al. showed that for every 5° of femur de-rotation, the TT-TG distance decreases by approximately 0.6 mm and the patella tilt decreases by 1.3°. 144 Similarly, a simulation study by Watrinet et al. found that for every 1° of varus correction in the coronal plane, the TT-TG distance decreases by 1.05mm. 145 These relationships are complex, and the use of virtual surgical planning may help clinicians determine the degree of correction required in each procedure, to achieve the best overall outcome for the patient.108,146,147 Moreover, the use of 3D-printed patient specific instrumentation has been shown to improve surgical accuracy.148–151 Future studies should investigate the role of computer navigation and robotic-arm assisted correction may further enhance surgical precision, as already seen in the field of arthroplasty.152–154 Moreover, there is an emerging concept of rotational-guided growth, although the clinical studies are limited at present, and thus insufficient to incorporate this into current practice.155–157 Lastly, Frings et al. also described the use of postoperative dynamic MRI to document and quantify the degree of correction achieved. 158

Proposed Surgical Indications and Preferred Technique

The majority of torsional variations do not require intervention. Asymptomatic patients should not be treated based solely on imaging values. Indications for treatment include persistent pain, recurrent PFI, functional limitation, gait cosmesis related to in-toeing or failure of structured nonoperative management. As correctly highlighted by Stevens et al., recognition of significant torsional malalignment is key in reducing the risk of treatment failure. 159 Given the limited potential for spontaneous correction after early adolescence, symptomatic torsional malalignment may warrant consideration of surgical correction. Based on the available literature, we would advocate thresholds of >25° of femoral anteversion (measured on axial imaging utilizing the posterior femoral condyle axis as reference), or external tibial torsion of >40° (measured on physical examination using thigh-foot angle or transmalleolar angle as described). Other parameters, such as hip range of motion, foot progression angle, and gait analysis, may also be considered in the decision-making process.

The key surgical consideration would be the degree of skeletal maturity. In most children or adolescents, distal femoral osteotomy, rather than proximal tibial de-rotation osteotomy, is more appropriate. Our preferred technique would be a distal femoral osteotomy using an interlocking retrograde intramedullary (IM) nail for fixation. This not only allows de-rotation of the distal femur but also allows for coronal plane correction, if required (Figure 7). The amount of rotational correction is determined preoperatively, based on clinical examination and radiographic torsion measurements. Schanz screws or Steinman pins are placed above and below the level of the osteotomy, well away from the planned path of the nail, and the angle difference between the pins is measured using a sterile dial inclinometer or a smartphone with an angle measuring application.160,161 The drilling for the osteotomy is performed before the IM guide wire is advanced and sequential reaming is performed using flexible reamers, as per the manufacturer’s recommendation. Following implantation of the IM nail, the desired amount of rotational correction is applied by manipulating the distal part of the limb and validated by checking against the angle between the pins. Modern nailing systems include angular stable screws– reassuring that the intraoperative derotation is maintained during the consolidation period. The use of IM nailing, as compared to plate fixation is supported by a recent randomized controlled trial. 162 Although it was concluded that there was no difference between the accuracy of the two devices, the less invasive nature and allowance for immediate weightbearing made IM nails our preferred option. Although intramedullary nailing is also a valid option for correcting tibial rotation, we prefer performing the osteotomy at the distal diaphyseal–metaphyseal junction, followed by fixation with a 4-hole locking plate. Concomitant procedures, such as MPFL reconstruction or TTO, may also be performed. This should be tailored to the specific anatomic contributors.

Conclusion

Torsional malalignment represents a complex but clinically important component of lower limb alignment in pediatric and adolescent patients with PFI. Accurate assessment requires integration of developmental considerations, meticulous clinical examination, dynamic analysis, and judicious imaging. For surgeons managing patellofemoral pathology, recognition of clinically relevant torsional abnormalities is essential. When appropriately indicated, derotational osteotomy can effectively address the biomechanical drivers of PFI.
