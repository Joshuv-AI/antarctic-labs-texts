Bidirectional metabolic-vascular feedback loops in heart failure: From myocardial energetics to multisystem cardiometabolic crosstalk
Authors: Haoxuan Wang, Yinghui Li, Chengyi Qin, Xiaodong Chen, Kun Wang, Meihua Zhang, Wei Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryHeart failure (HF) is a multisystem syndrome in which myocardial dysfunction is shaped by metabolic, vascular, and extracardiac abnormalities. Across HFrEF and HFpEF, impaired substrate utilization, mitochondrial function, endothelial signaling, coronary microvascular function, and mechano-energetic coupling disrupt the matching of ATP supply to cardiac demand, while obesity, insulin resistance, inflammation, and vascular stiffness further accentuate cardiometabolic HFpEF. In this Review, we integrate these processes into five self-reinforcing metabolic-vascular circuits: substrate-perfusion mismatch; mitochondrial reactive oxygen species-endothelial dysfunction; endothelial metabolism-nitric oxide-myocardial stiffness; immunometabolic-microvascular inflammation-fibrosis; and afterload-mechano-energetic uncoupling. We also examine how adipose tissue, skeletal muscle, liver, and kidney amplify these circuits. Together, this framework links myocardial energetic dysfunction with systemic vascular-metabolic stress and highlights circuit-directed opportunities for phenotype-informed HF therapy.

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) remains the final common pathway of a broad spectrum of cardiac and extracardiac disorders that converge on adverse cardiac remodeling and progressive pump dysfunction.1 In addition to classical etiologies such as ischemic heart disease, hypertension, valvular disease, and cardiomyopathy, systemic disorders can also drive cardiac remodeling and eventually culminate in HF. Chronic kidney disease (CKD),2 for example, contributes to HF through sustained neurohumoral activation, oxidative stress, inflammation, fibrosis, and hemodynamic overload.3 However, the traditional framework centered on ventricular dysfunction and neurohumoral activation does not fully explain the marked biological heterogeneity of HF, particularly heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF). In HFpEF, the coexistence of obesity, insulin resistance, diabetes, hypertension, vascular stiffness, coronary microvascular dysfunction, and chronic low-grade inflammation suggests that HF is often embedded within a broader systemic cardiometabolic syndrome rather than being a purely cardiac disorder.4

Consistent with this systemic perspective, the conceptual focus of HF research has expanded substantially. Rather than viewing myocardial metabolism merely through the lens of substrate preference, contemporary work increasingly emphasizes the signaling roles of metabolic intermediates, mitochondrial oxidative efficiency, endothelial and microvascular metabolism, immunometabolism, and inter-organ crosstalk.5 This transition is particularly pertinent to HF symptoms linked with metabolic disease, in which fuel overload, reduced metabolic flexibility, endothelial dysfunction, and inflammatory activation evolve and reinforce one another.6,7 These advances point to close coupling between metabolic remodeling and vascular dysfunction, but their reciprocal interactions have not yet been integrated into a coherent mechanistic framework.

Although metabolic remodeling and vascular dysfunction have each been extensively reviewed, they are often discussed as parallel contributors to HF rather than as mutually reinforcing processes. This separation may obscure how metabolic stress is converted into vascular injury and how vascular dysfunction, in turn, worsens myocardial energetic failure. In this review, we propose a loop-based framework in which metabolic and vascular abnormalities form bidirectional feedback circuits that sustain and amplify HF progression. We define several interconnected loops—including substrate-perfusion mismatch, mitochondrial ROS-endothelial dysfunction, endothelial metabolism-NO-myocardial stiffness, immunometabolic-microvascular inflammation-fibrosis, and afterload-mechano-energetic uncoupling—and discuss how their relative dominance differs between HFrEF and HFpEF, with special emphasis on the cardiometabolic HFpEF phenotype. We first outline physiological myocardial energetics and vascular-myocardial coupling, then review metabolic reprogramming and vascular dysfunction as partially distinct pathological domains, before integrating them into phenotype-specific feedback loops and discussing inter-organ crosstalk, assessment strategies, and therapeutic opportunities.

Physiological myocardial energetics and vascular-myocardial coupling

Metabolic flexibility of the myocardium

Myocardial energy metabolism is the foundation of cardiomyocyte function. Although it has long been believed that the adult heart primarily depends on fatty acid oxidation, this theory is now regarded as inadequate. The myocardium functions as a metabolic “omnivore” in a healthy condition, employing fatty acids, glucose, lactate, ketone bodies, and, to a lesser extent, amino acids to produce ATP based on substrate availability, oxygen supply, workload, and hormonal milieu.3 This flexibility is essential because the heart has an exceptionally high and continuous energy demand. Recent syntheses estimate that, under physiological conditions, the majority of ATP is produced by mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, with fatty acids typically accounting for the greatest share of reducing equivalents, while carbohydrates, ketones, and amino acids contribute dynamically depending on nutritional and hemodynamic context.

Importantly, metabolic flexibility should not be reduced to substrate switching alone. A healthy myocardium is characterized by its ability to sustain oxidative capacity, mitochondrial efficiency, redox balance, and metabolic adaptability under shifting physiological demands rather than just by whether it oxidizes more fatty acids or glucose at a given time. In other words, substrate choice is only one layer of myocardial energetics; the deeper determinant of cardiac performance is whether the metabolic network can efficiently match ATP supply to mechanical demand. This broader view is particularly important for understanding how the heart transitions from physiological adaptation to pathological remodeling.

Mitochondria and mechano-energetic coupling

Under physiological conditions, mitochondrial function is tightly integrated with excitation-contraction coupling. Cardiac contraction, relaxation, calcium cycling, and ATP generation are not independent events; rather, they form a coordinated system in which mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation continuously supports ion transport, sarcomeric cycling, and diastolic recovery.8 Proper calcium handling links workload to energy production, enabling the heart to preserve mechano-energetic coupling across a wide range of functional states. Thus, normal cardiac performance is dependent on the precise alignment of mechanical load, calcium flux, mitochondrial tricarboxylic acid cycle activity, and ATP generation.

This concept also clarifies why mitochondrial dysfunction is so central to HF pathophysiology. According to recent research, obesity, hypertension, and age-related vascular stiffness increase preload and afterload in HFpEF, increasing mechanical work that is not adequately matched by mitochondrial ATP supply, whereas defects in excitation-contraction coupling are major drivers of mechano-energetic uncoupling in HFrEF.9,10,11 In both settings, oxidative stress emerges when antioxidant capacity is exhausted, promoting maladaptive remodeling and contractile dysfunction. Consequently, HF is a progressive loss of this coordinated interaction, while the normal state should be considered as one of efficient mechano-energetic coupling.

Role of the vascular system in myocardial metabolism

The vascular system is indispensable for myocardial metabolism because oxygen delivery and substrate transport are prerequisites for oxidative ATP production.12 In addition to controlling bulk blood flow, the microcirculation and coronary arteries also control the temporal and geographical matching of nutrients and oxygen to myocardial demand. Alterations in coronary flow reserve, vasodilatory responsiveness, or microvascular integrity can therefore influence cardiac metabolism even before overt myocardial dysfunction develops.13 While progressive ventricular dysfunction can further impair coronary flow reserve, experimental and clinical research suggests that coronary endothelial dysfunction and perfusion abnormalities may precede overt heart failure, resulting in a reciprocal relationship between myocardial energetics and vascular function.14,15

Beyond simple conduit and resistance functions, endothelial cells actively regulate vascular tone, angiogenesis, barrier integrity, immunomodulation, coagulation, and nutrient handling. Endothelium-dependent vasodilation, largely mediated by nitric oxide and other vasoactive signals, is essential for coupling myocardial oxygen consumption to coronary flow. Recent work further shows that endothelial cells are metabolically specialized and heterogeneous, with disease-specific metabolic programs that shape their phenotype and function.16,17 In the cardiovascular system, endothelial metabolic perturbations can precede overt clinical disease and accelerate the accumulation of dysfunctional endothelial cells under cardiometabolic stressors such as obesity, diabetes, hypertension, and CKD.17,18,19

Cardiac capillary endothelial cells are therefore not passive bystanders. They engage in metabolic crosstalk with cardiomyocytes and contribute to the regulation of local substrate availability, inflammatory signaling, and tissue perfusion. This realization has significant conceptual ramifications: endothelial metabolic dysregulation can be an initiating lesion within the pathophysiological cascade that ultimately results in heart failure, rather than only a subsequent consequence of myocardial infarction. Metabolic reprogramming in heart failure

Cardiac metabolism is indispensable for myocardial function. Because cardiac contraction requires a continuous and massive supply of ATP, the role of metabolism in the heart was traditionally interpreted mainly through the lens of energy production.20 Therefore, metabolic reprogramming should be considered both a result of HF and a potentially harmful factor in the remodeling and advancement of the disease.21,22,23 Moreover, metabolic changes are not confined to cardiomyocytes; they also occur in endothelial cells, fibroblasts, and immune cells, thereby contributing to adverse remodeling at the tissue level.23,24

An additional layer of complexity comes from cardiac development and maturation. The postnatal maturation of mammalian cardiomyocytes is accompanied by a transition from glycolysis to fatty acid oxidation (FAO), together with chromatin reconfiguration, cell-cycle exit, and loss of regenerative competence.25,26 Conversely, reactivation of a more glycolytic metabolic state has been associated with restored proliferative and reparative potential.26,27 Recent work has highlighted α-ketoglutarate (αKG), a key tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle intermediate, as a molecular bridge linking metabolic state to chromatin and transcriptional remodeling in cardiomyocytes.25 These findings support the notion that intermediate metabolism can control cardiomyocyte identity and stress adaptability in addition to being downstream of cell destiny.

Metabolic reprogramming in HFrEF

Metabolic remodeling in heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) is relatively well characterized. HFrEF is commonly initiated by myocardial injury or chronic loading stress and is further propagated by neuroendocrine activation and mechanical strain, both of which increase the energetic cost of contraction. In many forms of HFrEF, particularly advanced disease, myocardial fatty acid oxidation is reduced, although the magnitude and temporal pattern of this change vary with disease stage and etiology.27,28,29 At the same time, myocardial glucose uptake and glycolytic flux increase; however, this increase is not matched by a proportional rise in mitochondrial glucose oxidation.30 As a result, the failing heart develops a characteristic mismatch in which glycolysis is maintained or enhanced, whereas pyruvate oxidation, TCA cycle flux and oxidative phosphorylation are impaired.31 This pattern reflects not successful compensation, but loss of metabolic flexibility and declining oxidative efficiency.

Mitochondrial dysfunction is central to this process. Human failing myocardium exhibits impaired activity of selected respiratory-chain and tricarboxylic-acid-cycle enzymes, including reduced complex I activity.32,33 Experimental models further indicate that disrupted mitochondrial quality control and mitophagy contribute to progressive mitochondrial dysfunction. Defective electron transport, particularly at complex I, may also enhance mitochondrial ROS production.34,35 Although debate remains as to whether the dominant defect lies in intrinsic mitochondrial function, reduced mitochondrial mass, or both, the net effect is an energetic deficit characterized by lower phosphocreatine-to-ATP ratios and impaired creatine kinase flux.36 These abnormalities compromise both systolic performance and contractile reserve. Therefore, the main metabolic pathway in HFrEF can be summed up as follows: decreased FAO to increased glucose uptake but incomplete glucose oxidation to impaired TCA cycle and oxidative phosphorylation to ATP deficit and ROS accumulation to increasing contractile and relaxation dysfunction.

Importantly, nonglycolytic glucose pathways also become more relevant in HFrEF. When glucose utilization becomes uncoupled from mitochondrial oxidative capacity, glucose-derived carbon may be redirected toward the pentose phosphate pathway. Increased activity of this pathway can enhance NADPH availability for superoxide-generating enzymes, thereby contributing to myocardial redox imbalance.37 In parallel, ketone body utilization increases as an alternative fuel adaptation in the failing heart, but this occurs in the setting of persistent disruption of lipid oxidation and mitochondrial energy metabolism. Consequently, the metabolic phenotype of HFrEF represents a progressive loss of coordination between substrate availability, substrate oxidation, and mitochondrial ATP production rather than a benign “switch from fatty acids to glucose”.38

Metabolic features of HFpEF, particularly cardiometabolic HFpEF

Compared with HFrEF, metabolic reprogramming in heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) is more heterogeneous and more closely intertwined with systemic comorbidities.1 HFpEF commonly develops in the setting of aging, arterial hypertension, obesity, insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and chronic kidney disease. Among these, obesity and insulin resistance are especially important in the cardiometabolic HFpEF phenotype because they lead to chronic nutrient oversupply, myocardial lipid overload, and impaired mitochondrial substrate handling.39 In this regard, it is becoming more widely acknowledged that HFpEF represents a systemic metabolic-microvascular syndrome rather than just a condition of diastolic dysfunction.

In obesity-related or cardiometabolic HFpEF, myocardial oxidative capacity may become insufficient relative to excessive substrate availability. Elevated circulating glucose and lipids overwhelm normal metabolic pathways, leading to the accumulation of lipotoxic and glucotoxic intermediates that impair mitochondrial and cellular function. A recent study suggests that, unlike in HFrEF where FAO generally declines, HFpEF—especially in obesity-related forms—may show preserved or even increased dependence on FAO at the organ level, yet this does not translate into effective energy production.40 Instead, incomplete processing of fatty acids favors the accumulation of toxic intermediates such as diacylglycerols and ceramides, which promote mitochondrial dysfunction, ROS production and diastolic dysfunction.41 Electron microscopy studies have also shown more abundant lipid droplets and swollen mitochondria with disrupted cristae in HFpEF with severe obesity.42

Glucose metabolism in HFpEF is likewise complex. Available data indicate that glycolytic flux and glucose oxidation are frequently uncoupled, but the direction of change depends on the specific HFpEF phenotype and the model studied. In several cardiometabolic models, glucose catabolism is reduced while FAO predominates; in others, glycolysis increases without a corresponding increase in pyruvate oxidation. This heterogeneity helps explain why HFpEF is difficult to treat using traditional hemodynamic paradigms alone. It also supports the emerging clinical focus on upstream metabolic interventions such as weight reduction, insulin sensitization, and incretin-based therapies.43 In other words, cardiometabolic HFpEF is best conceptualized as a state of metabolic overload, toxic intermediate accumulation, and microvascular-energetic dysfunction, rather than a simple mirror image of HFrEF.

Signaling roles of metabolic intermediates

The realization that metabolic intermediates include signaling molecules that control cell destiny and stress responses in addition to being substrates or byproducts of ATP synthesis is one of the most significant conceptual developments in recent years. Any contemporary consideration of metabolic reprogramming in HF must take this change into account. In failing hearts, nutrient oversupply and impaired mitochondrial oxidation lead to intracellular accumulation of intermediary metabolites that can modify proteins and chromatin through acetylation, acylation, O-GlcNAcylation, ADP-ribosylation, methylation, succinylation, malonylation, and related modifications.44 These changes affect ion handling, sarcomeric function, mitochondrial enzymes, and transcriptional programs, thereby linking metabolic state to hypertrophy, fibrosis, and inflammation.3

Several concrete examples illustrate this principle. AMP and the ATP/AMP ratio regulate AMPK, a master sensor that promotes ATP-generating pathways while restraining anabolic processes. Glucose-6-phosphate can influence mTOR signaling, thereby contributing to cardiac hypertrophy.36 Elevated branched-chain amino acids facilitate mTOR activation and are implicated in maladaptive growth.45 In addition, several metabolites, including β-hydroxybutyrate, lactate and TCA cycle intermediates, signal through G protein-coupled receptors or other receptor systems.46 Although the precise contribution of each metabolite to HF progression remains incompletely defined, the overall message is clear: intermediary metabolites are active regulators of cardiac biology, not passive markers of metabolic dysfunction.

The αKG story provides a particular example of this metabolic-signaling interface. During postnatal maturation, the metabolic transition toward FAO is coupled to changes in chromatin state and loss of cardiomyocyte proliferative capacity. In adult mouse cardiomyocytes, genetic inhibition of CPT1B-dependent fatty acid oxidation caused α-KG accumulation, activated KDM5-dependent histone demethylation, and suppressed maturation-associated transcriptional programs, promoting a less differentiated and more proliferative state.25 Although this work was framed in the context of regeneration rather than HF per se, it strongly supports the broader concept that changes in intermediary metabolism can reshape gene expression programs through direct epigenetic mechanisms.25 Such findings are highly relevant to HF, where chronic metabolic stress likely drives persistent chromatin and transcriptional remodeling. Therefore, in addition to changing ATP generation, metabolic remodeling in HF may potentially affect transcriptional and epigenetic processes, protein function, and cellular signaling.

Metabolic reprogramming and mechano-energetic uncoupling

Metabolic reprogramming in HF cannot be fully understood without considering mechano-energetic uncoupling. Under physiological conditions, myocardial contraction, relaxation, calcium cycling, and ATP production are tightly coordinated, allowing mitochondrial energy supply to increase rapidly when cardiac workload rises. In HF, however, the energetic cost of contraction and relaxation increases, whereas mitochondrial oxidative capacity, phosphotransfer reserve, and the ability to regenerate ATP progressively decline.47 This mismatch is particularly important when increased wall stress, vascular stiffness, neurohumoral activation, or intrinsic sarcomeric abnormalities impose greater mechanical work on a metabolically compromised myocardium.8,48 The end effect is not only a “energy-starved heart”, but a growing gap between mechanical demand and bioenergetic supply. Understanding how this divergence generates oxidative stress requires consideration of the physiological signals that normally couple ATP consumption to mitochondrial respiration.

Under physiological conditions, mitochondrial ATP production is adjusted to workload through coordinated feedback and feedforward mechanisms. Increased ATP hydrolysis transiently increases the local availability of ADP and inorganic phosphate, which contribute to feedback activation of oxidative phosphorylation.49 Their transport into mitochondria stimulates ATP synthase and accelerates electron transport, thereby providing feedback control that links oxidative phosphorylation to ATP consumption. In parallel, the increased cytosolic Ca2+ transients accompanying increased contractile activity promote mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake. Matrix Ca2+ activates pyruvate dehydrogenase phosphatase and Ca2+-sensitive tricarboxylic-acid-cycle enzymes, including isocitrate dehydrogenase and α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase, thereby increasing NADH production and the supply of reducing equivalents to the respiratory chain. Therefore, mitochondrial Ca2+ offers a feedforward signal that aids metabolic reducing-equivalent synthesis to anticipate increased workload50, while ADP signals the amount of ATP consumption.50

In HF, defects in cytosolic and mitochondrial Ca2+ handling, substrate oxidation, and respiratory-chain activity disrupt this coordinated response. ADP-driven acceleration of respiration may no longer be matched by adequate Ca2+-dependent regeneration of NADH, while impaired electron transfer increases electron leakage and mitochondrial ROS formation.51 Oxidative stress is further amplified when mitochondrial NADPH regeneration becomes insufficient, because NADPH provides the reducing power required by the glutathione and thioredoxin peroxide-detoxifying systems. Nicotinamide nucleotide transhydrogenase (NNT) normally uses the mitochondrial proton-motive force to transfer reducing equivalents from NADH to NADP+, thereby maintaining the NADPH pool for antioxidant defense. Under pathological pressure overload, however, the NNT reaction can reverse, consuming NADPH to regenerate NADH and support ATP production. This response may transiently sustain oxidative phosphorylation but does so at the expense of ROS-detoxifying capacity.52 Mechano-energetic uncoupling therefore promotes oxidative stress through a mismatch among ADP-driven respiratory demand, Ca2+-dependent NADH regeneration, electron-transfer capacity, and NADPH-dependent ROS removal.

The initiating conditions for this uncoupling differ across HF phenotypes, although the downstream energetic and redox consequences overlap. In HFrEF, myocardial injury, contractile inefficiency, abnormal calcium cycling, and reduced oxidative capacity increase the ATP cost of maintaining cardiac output while limiting mitochondrial adaptation to workload.9 In HFpEF, arterial stiffness, concentric remodeling, elevated filling pressures, impaired microvascular reserve, and cardiometabolic stress increase the energetic cost of contraction and relaxation, particularly during exercise despite preserved ejection fraction.10,11 In both settings, failure to coordinate ATP demand with ADP- and Ca2+-dependent mitochondrial responses compromises both energy production and antioxidant defense. The resulting ATP deficiency and ROS accumulation further impair calcium handling, mitochondrial function, and sarcomeric performance and activate redox-sensitive hypertrophic and fibrotic pathways. Therefore, metabolic reprogramming in HF should be viewed as a failure of the entire system that links myocardial workload, mitochondrial energy generation, and redox homeostasis rather than only as a change in substrate choice.

Vascular dysfunction in heart failure

Vascular dysfunction is increasingly recognized as a central component of heart failure rather than a mere epiphenomenon of impaired pump function. Contemporary evidence indicates that both coronary and systemic vascular abnormalities contribute to the development and progression of HF, while HF itself further compromises vascular structure and function, thereby creating a self-perpetuating vicious circle. This reciprocal interaction has consequences far beyond perfusion alone: by altering coronary blood flow, endothelial signaling, afterload, and tissue oxygen delivery, vascular dysfunction directly influences myocardial metabolism, contractile reserve, and cardiac remodeling across the spectrum of ejection fraction.

Bidirectional interplay between coronary and systemic vascular dysfunction and HF

The relationship between vascular dysfunction and HF is fundamentally bidirectional. On the one hand, even in the absence of obstructive epicardial coronary disease, systemic vascular dysfunction and coronary microvascular dysfunction can impair coronary reserve and reduce myocardial oxygen supply, a mechanism that is especially relevant to HFpEF.53 On the other hand, coronary macrovascular disease can cause myocardial ischemia and infarction, contributing to HFrEF.54 However, as heart failure progresses, the vasculature is gradually harmed by neurohumoral activation, oxidative stress, chronic inflammation, venous congestion, and altered hemodynamics, which worsens endothelial function, vascular tone regulation, and arterial compliance. Thus, vascular pathology is both a driver and a downstream amplifier of HF.12

This reciprocal framework is especially useful because it integrates coronary and systemic circulations into the same disease model. Coronary dysfunction limits oxygen and nutrient delivery to the myocardium, whereas systemic vascular dysfunction increases afterload, impairs peripheral perfusion, and reduces exercise capacity.55 These vascular disturbances, in turn, raise myocardial energetic demand and interact with the metabolic abnormalities already present in HF. As a result, the failing heart should be understood as part of a larger heart-vessel unit, where vascular and myocardial failure develop together rather than separately.

Endothelial dysfunction

Endothelial cells have a metabolic program distinct from that of cardiomyocytes. In the healthy vasculature, endothelial cells generate most of their ATP through glycolysis, whereas mitochondrial metabolism and fatty acid oxidation primarily support biosynthesis, redox homeostasis, and intracellular signaling. PFKFB3-dependent glycolysis provides rapidly accessible ATP for endothelial migration and angiogenic responses.16 Fatty acid oxidation has complementary state-dependent functions, supporting nucleotide synthesis in proliferating endothelial cells and NADPH-dependent antioxidant defense in quiescent endothelial cells.17,18 Physiological laminar shear stress restrains excessive glycolysis through KLF2-mediated repression of PFKFB3,19 thereby helping to maintain endothelial quiescence and vascular homeostasis. Under cardiometabolic stress, including hyperglycemia, lipid excess, inflammation, uremic toxin accumulation, hypoxia, and disturbed flow, this metabolic balance is disrupted. Dysregulated glycolysis, impaired fatty-acid-oxidation-dependent redox homeostasis, and increased oxidative stress shift endothelial cells toward inflammatory, poorly reparative, and vasodilator-deficient phenotypes.56 An important point of convergence is reduced eNOS-derived NO bioavailability, which links endothelial metabolic reprogramming to both vascular and myocardial dysfunction.

The mechanisms underlying this reduction in NO bioavailability involve both accelerated NO consumption and impaired eNOS function. Under physiological conditions, eNOS-derived NO acts in both autocrine and paracrine manners. In the vascular wall, NO activates soluble guanylyl cyclase in adjacent vascular smooth muscle cells, increasing cGMP-protein kinase G signaling and promoting vasodilation.57 NO also helps maintain an anti-inflammatory, anti-adhesive, and antithrombotic endothelial phenotype.58 Under cardiometabolic stress, excessive ROS directly consumes NO and oxidizes the eNOS cofactor tetrahydrobiopterin, promoting eNOS uncoupling.59 Uncoupled eNOS generates superoxide rather than NO, thereby further amplifying oxidative stress and endothelial dysfunction. The resulting loss of NO increases microvascular tone, impairs endothelium-dependent vasodilation, reduces coronary flow reserve, and favors inflammatory activation and microvascular injury.

Beyond its effects on coronary perfusion, reduced endothelial NO signaling directly affects cardiomyocytes through impaired paracrine activation of the sGC-cGMP-protein kinase G pathway. Impaired coronary vasodilation limits the ability of oxygen and substrate delivery to match myocardial demand, thereby aggravating cardiomyocyte energetic stress. In parallel, reduced NO availability lowers soluble guanylyl cyclase-cGMP-protein kinase G signaling within cardiomyocytes. Decreased protein kinase G activity reduces the phosphorylation of titin and other myofilament proteins, increases cardiomyocyte passive tension, and impairs diastolic relaxation.60 Consistent with this mechanism, human HFpEF myocardium exhibits reduced cGMP content and protein kinase G activity in association with increased cardiomyocyte stiffness.61 Therefore, reduced cardiac vasodilatory reserve, myocardial energetic stress, increased cardiomyocyte stiffness, and diastolic dysfunction are linked to endothelial metabolic and redox abnormalities through inadequate NO signaling.

Coronary microvascular dysfunction and HFpEF

Coronary microvascular dysfunction (CMD) deserves separate discussion because it has emerged as one of the most important vascular endotypes in HFpEF. In patients with HFpEF without significant obstructive epicardial coronary disease, CMD is highly prevalent and appears to be mechanistically linked to abnormal myocardial relaxation, reduced coronary flow reserve, impaired exercise hemodynamics, and adverse outcomes. Recent studies report that CMD is present in up to 75% of HFpEF cases, while a recent systematic review and meta-analysis estimated a pooled prevalence of approximately 71%. CMD has also been associated with higher rates of rehospitalization, mortality, and worse quality of life.62,63

In HFpEF, cardiometabolic comorbidities such as obesity, diabetes, hypertension, aging, and CKD generate systemic inflammatory and oxidative stress that activates the coronary -microvascular endothelium.64 Myocardial biopsies from patients with HFpEF show increased endothelial expression of adhesion molecules, enhanced endothelial and macrophage NADPH oxidase activity, and eNOS uncoupling, consistent with an inflammatory and oxidatively stressed microvascular phenotype.64 Reduced NO bioavailability impairs endothelium-dependent arteriolar dilation, whereas leukocyte adhesion and increased endothelial permeability further promote local inflammation and tissue injury.64,65 Recurrent endothelial injury, impaired angiogenic repair, endothelial-cell loss, and the disproportionate growth of cardiomyocytes relative to capillaries can progressively reduce myocardial capillary density.66 Human pathological studies accordingly demonstrate coronary microvascular rarefaction together with myocardial hypertrophy and fibrosis in HFpEF.67

Functionally, capillary rarefaction and arteriolar endothelial dysfunction reduce maximal coronary conductance and vasodilatory reserve, increase spatial heterogeneity of microvascular flow, and lengthen the diffusion distance between capillaries and cardiomyocytes.53 Because myocardial oxygen extraction is already high at rest, increases in cardiac workload normally depend largely on augmented coronary flow, with a more limited contribution from increased extraction. In HFpEF, failure to adequately increase coronary flow and myocardial oxygen extraction during exercise can limit oxygen availability and aggravate cardiomyocyte energetic stress and impaired relaxation.68,69 Microvascular inflammatory signaling can activate profibrotic immune-fibroblast pathways, while recurrent oxygen-supply-demand mismatch may provide an additional tissue-injury stimulus.70 Interstitial fibrosis then increases myocardial stiffness, increases oxygen-diffusion distance, and may mechanically constrain intramyocardial microvessels, thereby further impairing perfusion reserve. These processes should nevertheless be regarded as interacting and partially overlapping mechanisms rather than an obligatory linear sequence, because microvascular dysfunction and fibrosis do not correlate closely in all HFpEF cohorts.71

CMD is also clinically attractive because it may help refine phenotype-based classification of HFpEF. Not all patients with HFpEF share the same dominant mechanism: in some, obesity and metabolic overload may predominate; in others, arterial stiffening, atrial dysfunction, or renal-microvascular injury may be more important.72 Therefore, identifying CMD can advance the science toward more accurate vascular phenotyping and may provide insight into why some treatments are more effective in particular HFpEF subgroups.

Arterial stiffness and ventricular-arterial coupling

Systemic vascular dysfunction in HF should not be reduced to coronary pathology alone. Large-artery stiffening is another major vascular abnormality, especially in HFpEF. Progressive arterial stiffness increases pulsatile load and effective arterial elastance, thereby raising left ventricular afterload and worsening ventricular-arterial coupling. As a result, the ventricle must generate more work to maintain stroke volume, which increases myocardial oxygen demand and contributes to the broader mechano-energetic mismatch characteristic of HF. This is particularly relevant in aging-related and hypertension-related HFpEF.11

In HFpEF, arterial stiffening has important functional consequences. Increased vascular stiffness promotes higher systolic load, impaired diastolic filling, reduced reserve during exercise and greater sensitivity to volume shifts.73 These effects are magnified in older adults and in patients with obesity, hypertension and CKD, all of which accelerate vascular aging.74 Thus, ventricular-arterial uncoupling is not simply a hemodynamic descriptor; it is a mechanistic framework that links systemic vascular changes to exercise intolerance and disease progression in HFpEF.

Sex differences are especially relevant here. HFpEF is more common in women, particularly after menopause, and growing evidence suggests that women may exhibit greater arterial stiffness, abnormal vascular reserve, and more pronounced microvascular dysfunction in certain HFpEF phenotypes.75,76 Menopause itself has been associated with increased arterial stiffness independent of traditional risk factors, and recent studies on HFpEF in women highlight vascular dysfunction and arterial load as plausible contributors to sex-specific disease expression and treatment responsiveness.77,78 These observations support the hypothesis that sex-specific vascular mechanisms may influence disease expression and therapeutic responsiveness, although this requires further validation.

Taken together, vascular dysfunction in HF operates across multiple levels, from large-artery stiffening to coronary microvascular dysfunction and endothelial metabolic derangement. These abnormalities not only reduce oxygen and substrate delivery but also alter NO-cGMP signaling, increase afterload, impair exercise reserve, and interact closely with metabolic reprogramming in the failing heart.79 Therefore, rather than being a subsequent consequence, vascular dysfunction should be viewed as a fundamental pillar of HF pathogenesis, especially in HFpEF.

Bidirectional metabolic-vascular feedback loops in heart failure

Metabolic reprogramming and vascular dysfunction in HF should not be interpreted as parallel abnormalities that simply coexist. A more mechanistically useful framework is that they form self-reinforcing feedback loops. In this model, altered substrate handling, mitochondrial dysfunction, ROS generation, endothelial metabolic reprogramming, CMD, arterial stiffness, inflammation, fibrosis, and mechano-energetic uncoupling progressively amplify one another.64,80 Together, these interrelated mechanisms create a broad metabolic-vascular vicious cycle that connects cellular metabolic stress to endothelial damage, reduced perfusion, inflammation, fibrosis, and progressive cardiac remodeling. Importantly, the dominant feedback loop may differ across HF phenotypes. In HFrEF, myocardial injury, impaired oxidative metabolism, and increased energetic cost of contraction often initiate a cycle of mitochondrial dysfunction, ROS generation, endothelial injury, and reduced perfusion reserve. In HFpEF, particularly cardiometabolic HFpEF, systemic nutrient excess, obesity, insulin resistance, endothelial dysfunction, CMD, and arterial stiffness frequently converge to create a different but equally self-sustaining metabolic-vascular circuit.72 Thus, HFrEF and HFpEF are not separated by the presence or absence of metabolic-vascular coupling, but by which component of the loop is most dominant.

According to this framework, the endothelial NO-cardiomyocyte cGMP-PKG-titin axis precisely defines the myocardial-stiffness loop, while ROS is the defining mediator of the mitochondrial ROS-endothelial dysfunction loop and a secondary amplifier of the substrate-perfusion mismatch and endothelial metabolism-NO-myocardial stiffness loops.

The substrate-perfusion mismatch loop

The defining feature of this loop is a mismatch between myocardial metabolic requirements and coronary delivery capacity. In the healthy heart, coronary blood flow and myocardial microvascular blood volume are dynamically adjusted to changes in myocardial oxygen consumption, helping match oxygen and substrate delivery to workload.81 In HF, this coordination can become impaired when reduced myocardial mechanical efficiency or abnormal calcium-dependent energetic adaptation coincides with diminished coronary flow reserve.82,83 In contrast to the mitochondrial ROS-endothelial dysfunction loop discussed later in discussion, this circuit’s primary problem is the inability of vascular delivery to meet cardiac metabolic demand rather than oxidative stress.

The origin of the mismatch differs among HF phenotypes. In HFrEF, contractile inefficiency, abnormal calcium cycling, and the increased energetic cost of maintaining cardiac output raise the amount of oxygen and substrate required for a given level of mechanical work, while ischemic injury or endothelial dysfunction may simultaneously limit coronary delivery.14 In cardiometabolic HFpEF, systemic nutrient excess is accompanied by complex myocardial substrate remodeling and reduced flexibility in the utilization of available fuels.29,84 Coronary microvascular dysfunction and impaired vasodilatory reserve are also common and may limit myocardial oxygen availability during exercise.53,65,68 Thus, the mismatch may arise either from excessive or inefficient myocardial metabolic demand, from impaired vascular delivery, or from both.

The feedback circuit is closed when inadequate oxygen and nutrient delivery further compromises mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation and metabolic flexibility, thereby reducing ATP-generating efficiency and increasing the energetic cost of maintaining myocardial work.83,85 The greater metabolic requirement then becomes even more difficult to meet within the limited perfusion reserve. ROS and reduced NO bioavailability may aggravate vascular dysfunction within this circuit, but they are treated here as cross-loop amplifiers rather than as its defining mechanism. The defining axis is therefore increased or inefficient myocardial metabolic demand, inadequate coronary oxygen, and nutrient delivery, worsening energetic inefficiency, further widening of the demand-delivery mismatch.

The mitochondrial ROS-endothelial dysfunction loop

The defining feature of this loop is the bidirectional propagation of redox stress between mitochondrial dysfunction and the vascular endothelium. Impaired electron transport, incomplete substrate oxidation, defective mitochondrial calcium adaptation, or insufficient NADPH-dependent antioxidant capacity can increase mitochondrial ROS generation in cardiomyocytes and endothelial cells.51,52 In contrast to the metabolic demand-perfusion mismatch loop, ROS is not merely an accompanying consequence in this circuit but the principal intermediate that transmits mitochondrial dysfunction to the vasculature.

Excess ROS and ROS-derived oxidants reduce NO bioavailability and promote oxidation or depletion of bioactive tetrahydrobiopterin, thereby promoting eNOS uncoupling.86,87 Uncoupled eNOS produces superoxide rather than NO, further amplifying endothelial oxidative stress.86 This redox shift reduces endothelium-dependent vasodilation, activates redox-sensitive inflammatory and adhesive pathways, can compromise endothelial barrier and microvascular integrity, and impairs coronary microvascular reserve.56,64 Loss of NO can also reduce myocardial cGMP signaling; however, cardiomyocyte stiffness is not the defining endpoint of the present loop.61,64 Here, the principal consequence of NO depletion is redox-dependent endothelial and microvascular dysfunction.

The loop is closed when endothelial dysfunction and impaired microvascular reserve may increase regional heterogeneity of myocardial perfusion and oxygenation and expose cardiomyocytes to relative hypoxia or oxygen-supply-demand imbalance.69,71 These conditions impair electron transport and substrate oxidation and promote further mitochondrial electron leakage and ROS generation.22 The defining axis is therefore mitochondrial redox imbalance, ROS accumulation and eNOS uncoupling, endothelial and microvascular dysfunction, impaired myocardial oxygenation, and further mitochondrial ROS generation.

The endothelial metabolism-NO-myocardial stiffness loop

Unlike the mitochondrial ROS-endothelial dysfunction loop, which is initiated by mitochondrial redox imbalance, this loop begins with cell-autonomous metabolic reprogramming within the vascular endothelium. In cardiometabolic HFpEF, hyperglycemia, lipid excess, disturbed flow, inflammatory mediators, and uremic stress alter endothelial glycolysis, fatty acid oxidation, and redox metabolism.18,56,88,89,90 These metabolic alterations shift endothelial cells from a quiescent and vasculoprotective state toward inflammatory, poorly reparative, and vasodilator-deficient phenotypes.19,56,88 Although ROS may contribute to this transition, the defining upstream abnormality is endothelial metabolic dysregulation rather than primary myocardial mitochondrial ROS generation.

A principal functional consequence of endothelial metabolic reprogramming is impaired eNOS-derived NO signaling.56,64,91 Recently, it has been reported that reduced endothelial NO bioavailability impairs vascular smooth-muscle relaxation and lowers NO-soluble guanylyl cyclase-cGMP-PKG signaling in cardiomyocytes.56,61,64 Reduced cardiomyocyte PKG activity decreases the phosphorylation of titin and other myofilament proteins, increases passive cardiomyocyte tension, and impairs diastolic relaxation.60,61 The defining forward axis of this loop is therefore endothelial metabolic reprogramming, impaired NO signaling, reduced cardiomyocyte cGMP-PKG activity, increased myocardial stiffness, and diastolic dysfunction.

The feedback circuit is closed when increased myocardial stiffness elevates left-ventricular filling pressure and wall stress.60,92 Elevated filling pressure reduces the effective diastolic coronary perfusion gradient and mechanically compresses intramyocardial microvessels, particularly in the subendocardium. In parallel, abnormal cyclic wall strain activates endothelial mechanosensitive and cytoskeletal signaling, promotes NADPH-oxidase-derived ROS generation and NF-κB-dependent inflammatory activation, and further reduces eNOS-derived NO bioavailability.93,94,95 Thus, increased myocardial stiffness and intracardiac mechanical stress feedback to aggravate endothelial redox and signaling dysfunction. The complete defining axis is endothelial metabolic reprogramming, NO-cGMP-PKG impairment, cardiomyocyte stiffness, increased filling pressure and wall stress, and further endothelial dysfunction.

The immunometabolic-microvascular inflammation-fibrosis loop

The defining feature of this loop is the coupling of immune-cell metabolic activation to microvascular inflammation and fibroblast-driven extracellular matrix deposition. Unlike the preceding ROS- and NO-centered loops, its principal cellular amplifier is the metabolically activated immune cell, and its defining downstream endpoint is persistent fibrotic remodeling. In HF, ischemia, mechanical stress, metabolic overload, and comorbidity-driven inflammation recruit and activate innate and adaptive immune cells within the myocardium and vasculature. Activated immune cells undergo metabolic polarization, often shifting toward increased glycolysis to support cytokine production, ROS generation, and inflammatory effector functions.64,96

This immunometabolic activation worsens vascular dysfunction. Pro-inflammatory cytokines, oxidative stress, and immune-endothelial interactions impair endothelial barrier function, reduce vasodilatory capacity, and promote microvascular rarefaction. In turn, microvascular dysfunction generates local hypoxia, abnormal shear stress, and tissue injury, all of which further sustain immune-cell recruitment and inflammatory activation. Fibroblasts are then activated within this inflammatory microenvironment, producing extracellular matrix and increasing myocardial stiffness.97,98

Fibroblast activation also has a metabolic dimension. Activated myofibroblasts exhibit increased glycolysis, glutaminolysis, mitochondrial respiration, and mitochondrial ROS production,99,100 which provide both biosynthetic support and signaling input for extracellular matrix production and profibrotic gene expression.100,101 Fibrosis then worsens myocardial stiffness, impairs oxygen diffusion, disrupts microvascular architecture, and increases mechanical stress, thereby feeding back into both vascular dysfunction and metabolic inefficiency.67,71,92

When combined, these findings provide credence to a theory that links inflammatory cell states to microvascular and stromal remodeling in heart failure through immunometabolic activation. Sustained glycolytic and oxidative programs in activated immune cells reinforce endothelial injury and microvascular rarefaction, whereas the resulting hypoxic and inflammatory microenvironment promotes fibroblast activation and extracellular matrix deposition.69,70,102,103 Once established, fibrosis further compromises oxygen diffusion, microvascular integrity, and myocardial compliance, thereby creating conditions that perpetuate inflammatory activation and metabolic stress.69,71,92 This interconnected process may be particularly relevant to HF phenotypes characterized by systemic inflammation, coronary microvascular dysfunction, and progressive fibrosis.67,71,104 However, in longitudinal human research, the relative contribution, temporal sequence, and causal hierarchy of fibroblast activation, microvascular damage, and immune-cell metabolic reprogramming are likely to be different between HFrEF and HFpEF.

The afterload-mechano-energetic uncoupling loop

The defining feature of this loop is a hemodynamic increase in myocardial workload caused by systemic vascular stiffness and abnormal ventricular-arterial coupling. Unlike the preceding loops, its initiating lesion is not primarily mitochondrial ROS, endothelial metabolism, or immune-cell activation, but excessive mechanical load imposed on a myocardium with limited energetic reserve.

A fourth feedback circuit links systemic vascular dysfunction to myocardial energetic failure. Large-artery stiffness, impaired vasodilatory reserve, and abnormal ventricular-arterial coupling increase the mechanical load imposed on the ventricle.10,11 As afterload rises, the myocardium must consume more energy to maintain stroke volume and filling dynamics. In a metabolically healthy heart, increased workload can be matched by increased ATP production. In HF, however, mitochondrial oxidative capacity, substrate flexibility, calcium handling, and energetic reserve are already impaired.3,8

This imbalance is reinforced by the reciprocal effects of energetic failure on ventricular mechanics. Inadequate ATP availability impairs actomyosin cross-bridge cycling, sarcoplasmic-reticulum Ca2+ reuptake, and sarcolemmal ion homeostasis, thereby reducing contractile efficiency and delaying myocardial relaxation.105,106 The resulting increase in systolic and diastolic wall stress activates hypertrophic and profibrotic signaling, promotes chamber remodeling, and further compromises ventricular-arterial coupling.9,107 The feedback circuit is therefore closed when vascular loading increases myocardial energy demand, whereas energetically inefficient contraction, relaxation, and remodeling increase the mechanical work required to maintain cardiac output under the same loading conditions.55,106,108

The relative expression of this loop differs across HF phenotypes. In HFrEF, impaired contractility, abnormal Ca2+ cycling, ventricular dilation, and adverse remodeling reduce mechanical efficiency and increase the energetic cost of preserving forward output.9,47,106 In HFpEF, arterial stiffening and abnormal ventricular-arterial coupling interact with concentric remodeling and impaired lusitropic reserve, increasing the energetic cost of pressure generation and diastolic relaxation despite a preserved ejection fraction.55,108 Microvascular dysfunction and cardiometabolic stress may further reduce the capacity of the myocardium to meet this workload.53,65,108 Afterload-mechano-energetic uncoupling should therefore be viewed as a shared organizing mechanism rather than a uniform causal pathway: its initiating factors, relative importance, and interaction with other metabolic-vascular loops are likely to vary according to HF phenotype, disease stage, and comorbidity burden.

Phenotype-specific dominance of metabolic-vascular loops

These feedback loops do not operate in isolation, and their relative contribution and interaction are likely to vary among HF phenotypes, disease stages, and individual patients. The afterload-mechano-energetic uncoupling loop may provide a shared hemodynamic background across the HF spectrum, but it is embedded within different metabolic, vascular, and inflammatory contexts. In ischemic or dilated HFrEF, myocardial injury, impaired contractility, mitochondrial dysfunction, and adverse remodeling may increase the relative contribution of the mitochondrial ROS-endothelial dysfunction and afterload-mechano-energetic uncoupling loops.109,110 In cardiometabolic HFpEF, nutrient excess, insulin resistance,111 coronary microvascular dysfunction,112 endothelial metabolic dysregulation, and systemic inflammation may favor stronger interactions among the metabolic demand-perfusion mismatch, endothelial metabolism-NO-myocardial stiffness,113 and immunometabolic-microvascular inflammation-fibrosis loops. These patterns should not be regarded as mutually exclusive or fixed, because several loops may coexist and their relative importance may change during disease progression.

When seen in this manner, phenotypic specificity does not represent the assignment of each patient to a particular pathway, but rather the arrangement and relative weighting of interconnected feedback loops. This viewpoint may contribute to the understanding of why people with the same clinical HF classification can have various combinations of aberrant ventricular-arterial coupling, inflammation, fibrosis, microvascular dysfunction, metabolic impairment, and therapeutic responses. It also suggests that therapies may influence several interconnected nodes simultaneously. For example, sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors, weight-loss and incretin-based interventions, afterload-reducing therapies, and anti-inflammatory or antifibrotic strategies may modify different components of the network depending on the underlying phenotype.114,115 These therapeutic connections should be viewed as mechanistically informed hypotheses rather than proven loop-specific effects because, in the majority of situations, clinical benefit has not been clearly demonstrated to stem from disruption of a precisely defined metabolic-vascular loop.

Therefore, the main benefit of the loop-based approach is that it offers a testable framework for integrating metabolic, vascular, inflammatory, and mechanical systems rather than imposing a new permanent classification of heart failure. Prospective validation will require longitudinal studies that combine myocardial energetics, coronary and systemic vascular function, circulating and tissue biomarkers, and clinical phenotyping to determine whether reproducible patterns of loop activity can be identified in individual patients. Such studies will also be needed to establish the temporal sequence of loop activation, distinguish causal drivers from secondary amplifiers, and determine whether changes in specific loop-related measures predict treatment response. Phenotype-specific loop configurations should not be seen as a clinically validated categorization system, but rather as a conceptual and hypothesis-generating framework until these issues are resolved. From the heart to the whole body: Inter-organ cardiometabolic crosstalk in heart failure

The myocardium is not the only organ affected by heart failure. Because peripheral organs can function as upstream drivers, systemic amplifiers, or downstream targets of particular loops through altered substrate flux, inflammatory mediators, extracellular vesicles, hepatokines, myokines, uremic toxins, congestion, and hemodynamic stress, the inter-organ perspective expands the metabolic-vascular feedback-loop framework beyond the heart. Its clinical trajectory and biological heterogeneity are shaped by sustained interactions with peripheral organs, especially adipose tissue, skeletal muscle, liver, and kidney.96,98

Adipose tissue-derived inflammatory and metabolic stress

Adipose tissue can act as an upstream modifier of HF rather than a passive comorbidity.126 This is most evident for visceral, epicardial and pericardial fat depots.127 Epicardial adipose tissue is anatomically contiguous with the myocardium, is not separated from it by a fascial barrier, and shares the coronary microcirculation, making local paracrine and vasocrine signaling biologically plausible.128 In health, this depot may buffer fatty acids and provide local metabolic support, but in obesity and cardiometabolic disease it undergoes inflammatory and secretory remodeling. A recent study of HFpEF has therefore emphasized epicardial adipose tissue as a source of adipokines, inflammatory mediators, and lipotoxic stress capable of promoting endothelial dysfunction, myocardial stiffness, and fibrotic remodeling.129

A representative mechanistic example comes from recent experimental work demonstrating that cardiac-adjacent adipose tissue can actively contribute to HF progression. In a mouse model of pressure overload, surgical excision of pericardial adipose tissue did not substantially affect early or chronic concentric remodeling but prevented lung congestion, progressive left ventricular dilation, and systolic dysfunction, thereby attenuating the transition toward HFrEF.129 In a separate study, visceral adipose tissue (VAT)–derived small extracellular vesicles were shown to exacerbate obesity-related HFpEF, with the authors explicitly identifying VAT-heart crosstalk as a pathogenic mechanism.130 Together, these findings support a model in which adipose tissue contributes to HF through local inflammatory signaling, altered lipid handling, and extracellular-vesicle-mediated communication. Within the metabolic-vascular loop framework, adipose tissue is best viewed as a systemic activator of both the substrate-perfusion mismatch loop and the endothelial metabolism-NO-myocardial stiffness loop. Excess adiposity increases circulating fatty acids, adipokines, inflammatory mediators, and extracellular vesicles, thereby promoting myocardial lipid overload, endothelial activation, coronary microvascular dysfunction, and impaired vasodilatory reserve. Epicardial and pericardial fat may further amplify these processes locally because of their anatomical proximity to the myocardium and coronary microcirculation. As endothelial dysfunction and coronary microvascular dysfunction limit oxygen and substrate delivery, myocardial metabolic stress increases further, creating a self-reinforcing adipose-metabolic-vascular circuit. This mechanism may help explain why obesity-related HFpEF often shows strong overlap among metabolic overload, microvascular dysfunction, inflammation, and myocardial stiffness.

Skeletal muscle dysfunction as a determinant of systemic inefficiency

Skeletal muscle abnormalities in HF are not simply downstream consequences of reduced cardiac output. Patients with HF commonly exhibit impaired skeletal muscle oxidative capacity, mitochondrial dysfunction, reduced insulin sensitivity, and progressive wasting, all of which contribute to reduced peripheral oxygen extraction and exercise intolerance.131,132 This is particularly relevant in HFpEF, where symptoms often exceed what would be predicted from resting central hemodynamics alone. Recent studies therefore frame skeletal muscle dysfunction as part of the systemic pathophysiology of HF rather than a secondary bystander lesion.

A useful mechanistic example is the myokine Musclin. In a study, pressure overload was associated with reduced skeletal muscle expression of Ostn, the gene encoding Musclin. Skeletal muscle-specific loss of Musclin worsened cardiac dysfunction and fibrosis, whereas muscle overexpression of Musclin attenuated both. Mechanistically, Musclin enhanced C-type natriuretic peptide availability, improved cardiomyocyte contractility through protein kinase A signaling, and restrained fibroblast activation through protein kinase G signaling.133 This study is valuable because it shows that skeletal muscle can influence cardiac remodeling through a defined endocrine pathway, not just through generalized deconditioning.

In the context of metabolic-vascular feedback loops, skeletal muscle dysfunction can be interpreted as a peripheral amplifier of systemic energetic inefficiency. Impaired skeletal muscle mitochondrial function and reduced oxygen extraction increase the circulatory requirement for a given level of physical activity, thereby raising cardiac output demand and exposing limited cardiac and vascular reserve during exercise.134 This is especially relevant in HFpEF, where exertional symptoms may reflect a mismatch among peripheral oxygen extraction, vascular reserve, and myocardial energetic capacity rather than resting cardiac dysfunction alone. Reduced skeletal muscle insulin sensitivity may also worsen systemic substrate handling and contribute indirectly to myocardial metabolic stress.

An exercise reserve-peripheral oxygen extraction loop may therefore be reinforced by skeletal muscle dysfunction: decreased skeletal muscle oxidative metabolism lowers peripheral oxygen utilization; decreased exercise efficiency raises cardiovascular workload; increased workload reveals myocardial energetic and vascular limitations; and progressive deconditioning worsens skeletal muscle metabolic capacity. Thus, skeletal muscle contributes systemically to the substrate-perfusion mismatch loop and the afterload-mechano-energetic uncoupling in addition to acting as a downstream effect of heart failure.

Liver-derived mediators in cardiometabolic heart failure

The liver is another organ that can shape HF biology through metabolic and inflammatory signaling. This interaction is especially relevant in the setting of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) and cardiometabolic HFpEF, which share upstream drivers including obesity, insulin resistance, lipid overload, endothelial dysfunction, and low-grade inflammation.135 Recent studies argue that the coexistence of MASLD and HFpEF is not incidental; rather, both disorders appear to arise from overlapping systemic metabolic derangements that can mutually amplify vascular dysfunction, myocardial energetic stress, and fibrotic remodeling.136

One representative mechanistic example is FGF21. In a recent study, FGF21 protected against HFpEF in high-fat diet plus L-NAME mice by improving cardiac mitochondrial bioenergetics.137 The protective effect involved increased adiponectin production and suppression of cardiomyocyte PDK4 through PI3K/AKT signaling; notably, adiponectin deletion blunted the benefit of FGF21, whereas genetic PDK4 inactivation mitigated HFpEF. In the reverse direction, an earlier clinical study found that lower circulating fetuin-A levels were associated with hepatic hypoperfusion and predicted adverse cardiac outcomes in patients with HF. These findings indicate that liver-heart communication is facilitated not only by shared risk factors, but also by unique hepatokines and metabolic signals.138

The liver may therefore serve as a metabolic regulator of the substrate-perfusion mismatch loop. In metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, hepatic insulin resistance and lipid dysregulation increase systemic lipid flux, inflammatory signaling, and endothelial stress, thereby favoring myocardial metabolic overload and vascular dysfunction. Conversely, impaired cardiac output, venous congestion, and hepatic hypoperfusion may worsen hepatic metabolic signaling, creating a bidirectional liver-heart circuit. Hepatokines such as fibroblast growth factor 21 and fetuin-A illustrate how liver-derived signals may either buffer or amplify this circuit, depending on the stage and metabolic context of disease.

Kidney dysfunction as a driver of cardiorenal remodeling

CKD is common across the HF spectrum and can substantially shape cardiac phenotype and progression. Renal-cardiac crosstalk is a bidirectional process mediated by sodium retention, congestion, neurohumoral activation, endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, uremic toxins, and chronic inflammation. These processes promote myocardial hypertrophy, fibrosis, vascular dysfunction, and impaired reserve, while HF in turn worsens renal perfusion and venous congestion, thereby accelerating kidney injury.2,139,140

A representative mechanistic example is the uremic toxin indoxyl sulfate. In a 2023 study, indoxyl sulfate increased cardiac FGF23 protein expression, activated FGFR4 signaling in cardiomyocytes, and promoted left ventricular hypertrophy in CKD mice141; FGFR4 inhibition reduced heart weight and ventricular wall thickness in the indoxyl sulfate-treated groups. In chronic kidney disease, the uremic toxin indoxyl sulfate increases endothelial oxidative stress, suppresses eNOS-derived NO production, and impairs endothelium-dependent relaxation, whereas end-stage renal failure is associated with reduced coronary microvascular reserve.142 Persistent metabolite retention may thereby limit myocardial perfusion via NO depletion, vasomotor dysfunction, vascular inflammation, and structural remodeling, rather than simply physical accumulation.

Within the metabolic-vascular feedback-loop framework, kidney dysfunction is a particularly powerful amplifier because it simultaneously affects metabolic, vascular, inflammatory, and hemodynamic domains. Uremic toxins, oxidative stress, and chronic inflammation promote endothelial dysfunction and vascular injury,91 thereby feeding into the mitochondrial ROS-endothelial dysfunction loop. Sodium retention, volume expansion, venous congestion, and neurohumoral activation increase preload, afterload, and ventricular wall stress, thereby reinforcing the afterload-mechano-energetic uncoupling loop.

At the same time, HF worsens renal perfusion and venous congestion, which further aggravates renal dysfunction and sustains cardiorenal injury.143 This results in a two-way cardiorenal-metabolic-vascular circuit: endothelial damage, oxidative stress, inflammation, and hemodynamic overload are all encouraged by kidney dysfunction; these abnormalities exacerbate myocardial energetic stress and remodeling; and progressive heart failure feeds back to accelerate renal injury.144 This loop-based interpretation helps explain why chronic kidney disease strongly modifies HF phenotype, progression, and treatment response across the ejection fraction spectrum.

Together, adipose tissue, skeletal muscle, liver, and kidney contribute to the initiation, maintenance, and amplification of particular metabolic-vascular feedback loops rather than only reflecting the burden of heart failure. The liver regulates metabolic overload, hepatokine signaling, and inflammatory tone145; the kidney increases oxidative stress, endothelial damage, congestion, and hemodynamic load104; adipose tissue mainly reinforces substrate overload, endothelial dysfunction, and microvascular inflammation146; skeletal muscle dysfunction contributes to impaired systemic oxygen extraction and exercise reserve.147 What links these organs is not a single pathway, but a recurring set of processes: metabolic overload or insufficiency, inflammatory signaling, endothelial dysfunction, altered substrate flux, extracellular-vesicle communication,148 neurohumoral activation, and hemodynamic stress.

A multi-organ perspective therefore extends the loop-based model of HF beyond the myocardium. In cardiometabolic HF phenotypes, peripheral organs can determine which feedback loop becomes dominant, how rapidly the loop stabilizes into chronic disease, and which therapeutic strategy is most likely to interrupt it. Instead of concentrating solely on cardiac load or congestion, this perspective offers a mechanistic justification for therapies that address obesity, insulin resistance, cardiorenal stress, skeletal muscle failure, hepatic metabolic illness, and systemic inflammation.

Assessment strategies and biomarkers

Finding the primary biological processes underlying each patient’s heart failure, such as reduced myocardial energetics, coronary microvascular dysfunction, increased vascular stiffness, inflammatory activation, or more general cardiometabolic stress, is crucial in both clinical practice and research. In order to enhance phenotyping and provide more mechanism-based evaluation, contemporary assessment procedures increasingly use sophisticated imaging, circulating biomarkers, and multi-omics techniques.154

Assessment of vascular dysfunction

Vascular dysfunction in heart failure involves the coronary microcirculation, the endothelium, and the large arterial system, and its assessment therefore requires a combination of methods. Coronary microvascular dysfunction has become an important focus, especially in HFpEF, where it is associated with symptoms, exercise intolerance, and adverse outcomes.1,63 PET, stress cardiac magnetic resonance imaging, and transthoracic Doppler-derived coronary flow reserve, but in certain situations, invasive coronary function testing—which includes coronary flow reserve and markers like the index of microcirculatory resistance—can offer more functional information. However, as peripheral endothelial dysfunction, reduced vasodilatory reserve, and more general vascular anomalies may also contribute to the severity of the disease, especially in HFpEF, vascular evaluation should not be restricted to the coronary circulation. All things considered, the primary goal of these methods is to ascertain whether the patient’s heart failure phenotype is significantly influenced by vascular anomalies.

Biomarkers

Circulating biomarkers remain important in the evaluation of heart failure, and their role is gradually extending from diagnostic support to biological stratification. Increasing attention has been given to biomarkers related to inflammation, fibrosis, renal dysfunction, endothelial activation, adipose tissue signaling, and altered intermediary metabolism. In particular, metabolomic studies suggest that certain metabolite profiles may reflect substrate overload, redox imbalance and broader cardiometabolic stress.154 These markers may therefore help identify dominant pathophysiological processes in different heart failure phenotypes, especially in HFpEF, where metabolic, inflammatory, renal, and vascular abnormalities often overlap. Therapeutic implications

From a metabolic-vascular feedback-loop perspective, therapeutic strategies for heart failure can be viewed as attempts to interrupt dominant self-reinforcing circuits rather than simply to relieve congestion or reduce cardiac workload. This is especially important for HFpEF and obesity-related phenotypes, where even when resting hemodynamics seem to be relatively preserved, substrate overload, endothelial dysfunction, coronary microvascular dysfunction, arterial stiffness, systemic inflammation, and multi-organ stress may sustain disease progression.

Established therapies

Among currently available therapies, SGLT2 inhibitors are the clearest example of a drug class whose efficacy has been demonstrated across a broad spectrum of heart failure phenotypes, including HFpEF. Proposed mechanisms include improvement in renal-cardiac interaction, reduction in congestion, attenuation of inflammatory stress, and a more favorable myocardial energetic state. They act at the intersection of metabolism, vascular function, and multi-organ regulation.168 However, since medications like renin-angiotensin system inhibitors and mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists may also affect vascular function, tissue remodeling, and systemic metabolic stress, even though their effects are typically interpreted primarily through hemodynamic and neurohumoral mechanisms, conventional guideline-directed therapies should still be taken into consideration within this broader framework.

Obesity-related heart failure phenotypes

An important recent advance has been the emergence of anti-obesity and incretin-based therapies in obesity-related HFpEF. In this setting, obesity is no longer viewed simply as a background comorbidity, but as a disease-modifying component of the HF phenotype itself. Clinical studies with semaglutide have shown improvements in symptoms, physical limitations, body weight, and exercise capacity in patients with obesity-related HFpEF, including those with type 2 diabetes.169,170 Similar results have been shown with tirzepatide, which has been linked to improved health and fewer deteriorating heart failure events in addition to weight loss. These findings are significant because they imply that upstream cardiometabolic dysfunction is a key treatment target in at least a subgroup of HFpEF. These results validate the importance of treating obesity and associated cardiometabolic problems in a subset of patients with HFpEF.171

Emerging metabolic targets

Beyond currently established therapies, several metabolic strategies remain at an earlier stage of development. One example is ketone-based therapy, since ketone bodies may act as signaling molecules that affect cardiac and vascular function in addition to being alternative energy substrates.172 Early clinical studies in HFpEF suggest that ketone ester administration can modify hemodynamics and improve some measures of cardiac performance, although the available data are still limited and largely mechanistic. Another area of interest is NAD+-targeted therapy.118,173 Preclinical studies, particularly in obesity-related HFpEF, suggest that restoring NAD+ homeostasis may improve mitochondrial function and myocardial energetics, directly addressing one of the central abnormalities discussed in earlier sections of this review. However, both ketone-based and NAD+-targeted approaches remain investigational, and it is still uncertain whether these mechanistic findings will translate into clear clinical benefit.

Phenotype-guided treatment

Taken together, these developments suggest that heart failure treatment is moving toward a more phenotype-guided approach. Not all patients share the same dominant mechanisms, and therapies are unlikely to be equally effective across all biological subtypes of HF. SGLT2 inhibitors appear to have broad applicability, whereas incretin-based therapies seem particularly relevant in obesity-related HFpEF.168 Other strategies, such as ketone supplementation or NAD+-directed therapy, may eventually become useful in more selected metabolic phenotypes if future studies confirm their benefit. More generally, the discipline is increasingly moving away from a restricted focus on neurohumoral activation and cardiac strain and toward a more integrated perspective that takes into account metabolic, vascular, inflammatory, and inter-organ pathways. This change may grow more significant throughout the broader HF spectrum, although it is particularly noticeable in cardiometabolic HFpEF.

From a loop-based perspective, different therapies may act by interrupting distinct components of the metabolic-vascular circuit. Sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors may influence cardiorenal stress, congestion, inflammation, and myocardial energetics, thereby acting across several loops rather than through a single pathway. Anti-obesity and incretin-based therapies may be particularly relevant to obesity-related HFpEF because they can reduce substrate overload, adipose inflammation, circulatory load, and endothelial stress. Ketone-based and NAD+-targeted approaches may act closer to the mitochondrial energetic arm of the circuit, whereas endothelial or microvascular-targeted strategies may be most relevant when CMD or impaired NO-cGMP signaling is dominant. Therefore, future medication development should consider not only whether a medicine is effective in treating HF as a general illness, but also which metabolic-vascular loop it disrupts and which phenotype this loop is most active in. Conclusions and future perspectives

Heart failure should no longer be viewed simply as a disorder of impaired pump function or as the sum of separate metabolic, vascular, and systemic abnormalities. A central message of this review is that metabolic reprogramming and vascular dysfunction form self-reinforcing feedback loops that progressively stabilize disease biology. Bidirectional metabolicvascular circuits amplify the effects of impaired substrate handling, mitochondrial dysfunction, endothelial metabolic reprogramming, coronary microvascular dysfunction, arterial stiffness, inflammation, fibrosis, and mechano-energetic uncoupling. The relative dominance of these loops differs across HFrEF, HFpEF, and cardiometabolic HFpEF, providing a conceptual basis for mechanism-based phenotyping and loop-targeted therapy.

Future studies should therefore move beyond conventional clinical classification and place greater emphasis on identifying the dominant feedback loop in individual patients. Integrated approaches combining metabolic imaging, vascular assessment, circulating biomarkers, and selected multi-omics tools may help distinguish patients in whom energetic failure, endothelial dysfunction, CMD, inflammation-fibrosis, arterial stiffness, or multi-organ cardiometabolic stress is the major driver of progression. Such loop-informed phenotyping may also help explain heterogeneity in treatment response and support the development of therapies that target specific metabolic-vascular circuits rather than heart failure as a single uniform syndrome. All things considered, the loop-based methodology put forth here might assist in moving the field away from syndrome-based care and toward a more mechanism-oriented and phenotype-guided approach.
