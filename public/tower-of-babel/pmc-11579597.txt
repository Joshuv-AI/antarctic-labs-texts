Youth perspectives on the promotional and public relations strategies of health harming industries
Authors: Simone McCarthy, Hannah Pitt, Grace Arnot, Emma Jane Pristov, Samantha L Thomas
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPublic health researchers have investigated how youth are exposed to and influenced by traditional forms of commercial marketing from health-harming industries. Limited research has investigated youth opinions about the broader marketing and public relations strategies of these industries. This study aimed to understand youth perceptions of the impact and influence of a range of marketing and public relations strategies used by health harming industries. An online qualitative survey was conducted with n = 518, 16- to 24-year-olds living in Victoria, Australia. Participants were asked to consider the broad influence of marketing from health harming industries on youth, including the impacts of social media promotions, corporate social responsibility strategies including sponsorship and political donations, as well as their views on marketing restrictions. Reflexive thematic analysis was used to construct themes from the data. Participants stated that excessive marketing strategies had a significant influence on youth consumption of health harming products. Most felt that social media marketing had the most appeal and influence on youth. Most were critical of these strategies and were supportive of strong restrictions on marketing for health-harming products. However, many participants held more positive views about corporate social responsibility strategies, which they perceived could offset the negative impacts of these industries on health. Increased focus is needed from the public health and health promotion communities to ensure that youth are protected from the influence of the range of marketing strategies that are used by health-harming industries to promote their products and resist regulatory reform.

INTRODUCTION

Commercial marketing as part of the corporate playbook

When considering the range of health harming industries that have had the most burden on population health, evidence shows that the alcohol, tobacco, ultra-processed food and fossil fuel industries have the most substantial impact, accounting for at least a third of global preventable deaths each year (Gilmore et al., 2023). However, other industries and corporations can also have a significant negative impact on health and equity. These range from the rapidly transforming and high-tech gambling industry (Thomas et al., 2023a), companies that develop and sell vaping products (Besaratinia and Tommasi, 2019), Big Pharma (Parker et al., 2021), social media companies (Purba et al., 2024) and even supermarkets (Helander et al., 2024). However, as with the social determinants of health, the commercial determinants of health (CDoH) may exacerbate poor health and social outcomes for some population subgroups (McCarthy et al., 2023), including children and young people (hereafter referred to as youth) (Pitt et al., 2024a).

Health harming industries use a range of strategies to promote the consumption of their products, resist health-promoting and harm-preventing regulatory reform and shape public perceptions to protect their profits (Maani et al., 2020). These strategies (sometimes referred to as the ‘corporate playbook’) are designed to create favourable market conditions for commercial entities—largely at the expense of public health (Maani et al., 2020; Lee and Freudenberg, 2022). Commercial marketing—through a range of traditional and social media channels—is central to these strategies and forms part of the discursive power of health-harming industries (Thomas et al., 2023c; Arnot et al., 2024). Jungherr et al. (2019, p. 404) describe that discursive power in contemporary media systems is the ability of ‘contributors in communication spaces to introduce, amplify, and maintain topics, frames, and speakers, thus shaping public discourses…’, including the ability to control and influence normative discourses about an issue. Commercial marketing has received extensive attention in the academic literature (Watts et al., 2021; McCarthy et al., 2022; Giesbrecht et al., 2024). For example, researchers have documented how a range of advertising strategies help to normalize and create social acceptance for products that may be harmful to health (Thomas et al., 2018; Brown et al., 2020), including stimulating consumer demand (Lacy-Nichols et al., 2022; World Health Organization, 2024), building brand loyalty and recognition from a young age (Charlton, 1986; Nyemcsok et al., 2018) and embedding these products in cultural rituals and practices (Room et al., 2022; Thomas et al., 2022; Wright et al., 2022).

However, marketing strategies are much broader than commercial advertising tactics. They encompass a range of promotional and public relations strategies, which may be disseminated through a range of media and community channels (Mialon and McCambridge, 2018). Researchers investigating the tactics of the gambling industry have defined marketing as:

The full range of direct and indirect advertising, promotion, sponsorship, incentives, public relations, lobbying, and donations that are strategically used by the gambling industry to promote its products, gain publicity, attract new customers, shape social and cultural attitudes, and build corporate and product image and support. (Thomas et al., 2023c, p. 3)

Health harming industries engage in an increasingly diverse range of public relations strategies, which may be perceived as having reputational benefits for industries (Droppert and Bennett, 2015). For example, investigations into the promotional tactics of the fossil fuel industry have termed public relations strategies (such as green-washing) as ‘discourses of delay’ by appearing to provide ‘public support and endorsement of climate action but in practice doing little and strategically delaying real change’ (Si et al., 2023, p. 1). While these strategies are clearly influential in shaping public opinions, there is much less critical research focus on the impact and influence of these strategies, particularly in relation to youth.

The impact of commercial marketing on youth

Pervasive and novel commercial marketing tactics from health-harming industries have a clear impact on youth (Murphy et al., 2020; Rozendaal and Buijzen, 2023; Soraghan et al., 2023). Commercial marketing has played a central role in shaping youth’s positive attitudes towards and initiation of smoking (DiFranza et al., 2006); normalizing gambling as a socially accepted activity (Pitt et al., 2016; Thomas et al., 2023b); shaping alcohol consumption practices (Sargent and Babor, 2020); prompting the consumption of ultra-processed food and sugary drinks (Sato et al., 2022; Mulrenan et al., 2023) and reducing perceptions of harm in e-cigarette use (Hung et al., 2022). A range of appeal strategies are particularly impactful for youth, including those that appeal to sensory experiences and social benefits (Struik et al., 2020), utilize social media (Zhang and Esser, 2024), contain celebrity and social media influencer (SMI) endorsements (Pitt et al., 2024c) or include gimmicks (including novel products) (Soraghan et al., 2023, p. 3). There is also growing evidence that price promotions have a particular influence on youth consumer behaviour (Huang et al., 2024), with these arguably having increased impacts during times when there are increased cost of living pressures.

What is less clear is the impact that a broader range of public relations strategies may have on youth. For example, researchers demonstrate that health-harming industries are increasingly using youth-washing as a key public relations strategy (Sloan Morgan et al., 2024). Examples of these strategies include funding for youth education programs by the gambling and alcohol industries (Landman et al., 2002; Betting and Gaming Council, 2020), which have been criticized for drawing attention away from the negative impacts of harmful products and obstructing effective regulation that would protect youth (van Schalkwyk et al., 2022; Pitt et al., 2024a). Fossil fuel companies also engage in public relations strategies directly related to youth, including the development of STEM education materials and programs (Wynne and Trilling, 2021; Shell US, n.d.), sponsoring youth events (Sloan Morgan et al., 2024) and providing work experience and mentoring opportunities (BP UK, 2024). Researchers also argue that alcohol industry-sponsored ‘responsibility’ messages inherently promote drinking for youth (Smith et al., 2006; Dumbili et al., 2022). However, to date, very limited research to our knowledge has investigated youth opinions about these types of initiatives.

Engaging youth in discussions about the practices of health harming industries

In order to address the tactics of health harming industries, practitioners and policy-makers must not only listen to youth views but also engage them in the decision-making process (Garde, 2020; Soraghan et al., 2023; Pitt et al., 2024a, 2024b). Engaging youth in discussions about the role of corporate practices and policy responses is important in developing their ability to civically and politically respond to these issues (Arnot et al., 2023b). While youth are impacted by the practices of corporations and are often asked about traditional forms of marketing, such as television advertising, there are few studies that seek to engage their opinions about broader marketing tactics. This is important in ensuring that youth views are represented in comprehensive public health responses to prevent the impact of harmful commercial marketing on youth (Pitt et al., 2024b).

The following qualitative study, which involved 16- to 24-year-olds in Victoria, Australia, sought their views about a range of marketing and public relations strategies from health-harming industries. The data presented in this article were guided by four research questions:

How do youth conceptualize the impact and influence of marketing tactics from health harming industries?

METHODS

Context and approach

The data presented in this article were part of a broader online qualitative survey that sought to explore how youth (aged 16–24 years) in Victoria, Australia conceptualized and prioritized the CDoH. Victoria is Australia’s second-largest state, with 1.2 million young people aged between 12 and 25 years old making up about 18% of the population, and of these, just over one in five live in rural or regional areas (Office for Youth, 2022). The state of Victoria is ethnically diverse, with 48% of young Victorians born overseas or having at least one parent born overseas. More than 17,000 Aboriginal young people live in the state. The Victorian Youth Strategy 2022–2027 (Office for Youth., 2022), developed in consultation with youth, has two key priority areas relevant to this study: priority 1 is that young people are healthy and well; and priority 4 is that young people are respected and involved in decisions in their communities (p. 23).

The study sought to explore youth ‘subjective experiences and sense-making’ (Braun and Clarke, 2021a, p. 39) of a range of commercial and corporate tactics. Prioritizing these factors is consistent with a ‘Big Q’ approach, which is grounded in ‘distinctly qualitative’ research paradigms and values (Braun and Clarke, 2024, p. 609), and which considers subjectiveness to be ‘a resource for research’ (Braun and Clarke, 2024, p. 610). Online qualitative surveys are increasingly used in public health research and offer a variety of benefits (Thomas et al., 2024), including the ability to recruit a diverse and larger sample of participants in qualitative studies, as well as providing a convenient way for individuals to share qualitative opinions about a range of issues (Clarke and Braun, 2013; Terry and Braun, 2017; Braun et al., 2021). Online qualitative surveys are also useful for gaining rapid and broad insights into a specific phenomenon and are a valuable tool for urgently responding to public health issues affecting a range of populations (Thomas et al., 2024). Among youth in particular, these surveys can facilitate opportunities for qualitative research participation due to their accessibility through familiar online platforms—as well as at a time and via a process that is practical for them (Thomas et al., 2024). There are also benefits in relation to minimizing the power dynamics that can be present in face-to-face interviews between researchers and youth, with the anonymous nature of participation lessening social desirability or power imbalances and allowing for more open responses (Thomas et al., 2024). Ethical approval was received from the Deakin University Human Ethics Advisory Group for Health (HEAG_H 108-2023).

It is important to acknowledge the position, values and experiences that guided the research (Thomas et al., 2024). The team members took an interpretive and experiential public health approach, which recognizes the wider range of determinants that influence population health and well-being (van Schalkwyk et al., 2021; Thomas et al., 2023d). For this study, the team used a CDoH (and political determinants) lens, which centres on the role of corporations and harmful industries as a key vector of population health and (in)equity (Gilmore et al., 2023). It also acknowledges the policy decisions that are important in creating boundaries around how these industries are able to operate.

Criteria for recruitment

The sample size for this study was based on the type of data that can be collected in qualitative surveys and the ability to answer the study aims and research questions. It was guided by the sample sizes of similar online qualitative studies about the CDoH with youth (Arnot et al., 2023b, 2023c; Pitt et al., 2023). The inclusion criteria required participants to be aged between 16 and 24 years (to be able to independently provide informed consent), live in the Australian state of Victoria, have technological access to the online survey and possess sufficient English comprehension skills to enable their confident participation in this predominantly qualitative survey.

The survey was hosted through Qualtrics, a survey platform, which also facilitated recruitment for the study. The research team provided Qualtrics with the URL link to the survey, which was then sent to prospective participants (who met the inclusion criteria) through partner panel providers. Soft quotas were set for gender, geographical location (metro, regional, rural) and age groups. For participants aged 16 and 17 years, some panel companies recruit via their parents’ accounts (whereby the parent has indicated they have children who are also interested in research opportunities), asking them to share the survey link with their child. When they opened the survey link, participants were provided with a description of the study and a downloadable Plain Language Statement. Participants read the terms and then were able to click ‘no’ or exit their browser to leave or click ‘yes’ to confirm their consent before continuing to the survey questions. Participants were informed that they could leave the survey at any time and that the final dataset would only include complete sets of responses submitted at the end of the survey. Participants (or account holders) complete surveys for points that can typically be redeemed for vouchers. This was managed through each panel provider and not the research team.

Data collection

The first section of the survey included screening questions relating to age and location, as well as socio-demographic questions about gender, geographic location, country of birth, student status and education level. The data analysed in this article are related to predominantly open-text questions regarding marketing and public relations strategies. These included questions about the influence of marketing on brand appeal and product consumption among youth; the impact of digital marketing as compared to traditional marketing strategies; the impact of CSR strategies on corporate reputation and offsetting harms; the appropriateness of CSR strategies that specifically target youth; views about sports sponsorship as a specific public relations strategy and support for tobacco-style marketing restrictions. Given that in online surveys the researchers are not able to provide any additional clarification or prompts about meaning, participants were provided with descriptions about different types of marketing strategies, and some visual images to help contextualize questions (which are commonly used in this type of qualitative data collection method (Thomas et al., 2024). These included examples of CSR activities such as industry funding of youth initiatives and charities.

Data collection occurred between July and September 2023. The survey was initially piloted with n = 35 youth to ensure that all technical aspects of the survey were working as intended and to identify any questions that appeared difficult to comprehend. Some minor modifications to language were made to enhance clarity before launching the full survey. A total of 524 responses were collected over a 4-week period. The research team prepared the dataset for analysis with an initial focus on identifying nonsensical responses and participants who appeared to not have genuinely attempted to respond to the survey, including by using AI tools to generate responses. This process included significant deliberation and multiple meetings about participants to include and exclude, with the research team required to make a subjective judgement about whether or not a participant should be included in the final dataset (Thomas et al., 2024). Following this initial process, 89 participants were removed because of poor-quality responses. A further n = 100 participants were recruited via Qualtrics. Of these, n = 17 were removed in the subsequent cleaning period. This resulted in a final sample of n = 518.

Data interpretation

Open-text data responses were analysed using Braun and Clarke’s six-phase reflexive approach to thematic analysis, with the term ‘phase’ used to reflect a non-linear analysis process of analysis as opposed to rigid analytical ‘steps’ (Braun and Clarke, 2021b). The reflexive practices of the research team included meetings to discuss the interpretation of data and the construction of meaning from the data. We engaged in discussions as a team about any differences in our interpretation of the data. We deliberated in meetings and by email, ensuring that the choices that we made in constructing meaning and themes from the data fit with the overall aims and research questions and were based on our shared understandings of the data.

The research team first engaged in a process of familiarization with the data. Discussions were held with the broad team to focus the analysis based on the reading and re-reading of the data. This included reading through the dataset as a whole and then each question individually, during which preliminary codes and ideas were developed by S.M., G.A. and S.T., and meetings were held with the broader team where concepts were reflected upon and discussed. The second phase was to code the data. Codes were assigned based both on identified patterns and key concepts and being mapped to the research questions. This process began with S.T. assigning broad semantic codes for common or reoccurring ideas in the data. Coding became more latent as the research team sought deeper and hidden meanings beyond surface-level ideas (including underlying ideologies or assumptions) (Byrne, 2022). During the third phase, codes were used to construct initial themes and subthemes, with codes analysed for commonalities and prominent ideas and then grouped together under various categories. Phase four involved the revision of themes. Unused codes were removed or collapsed under other codes. Reflective practices were at the forefront of these phases as all authors discussed via meetings and by email the interpretation of the data and construction of themes, making sure that they were distinct from each other and accurately represented the data. The fifth phase involved refining themes, as well as defining and labelling them, during which data were regularly reviewed to ensure that constructed themes accurately reflected the dataset. The sixth and final phases involved the reporting of the results, involving a descriptive write-up of the constructed themes (with all authors providing input and revisions to the content of the article), the development of figures to illustrate concepts, and incorporating participant quotes to illustrate the key concepts and ideas. Peer reviewer comments led to some further consideration of the data and some more nuanced descriptions of young people’s comments, particularly relating to responsibility framing.

Youth perspectives on the promotional and public relations strategies of health-harming industries

Theme one: the role of marketing in prompting the consumption of harmful products

When asked about the influence of marketing for health harming products, most participants commented that marketing for these products targeted youth and that youth experienced excessive and frequent exposure to marketing for these products in multiple media and community environments. Participants perceived that the amount of advertising and promotions that youth saw for these products in their everyday environments influenced their attitudes and behaviours. They commented that youth exposure to these products was pervasive, unavoidable and ‘everywhere’, and that this had a direct impact on youth interest in purchasing these products:

It’s what people see every day, when we are walking, on our phones, even on the TV. – 16-year-old, female

Participants also stated that marketing contributed to increased product awareness and brand recognition, and prompted curiosity to consume novel products. Participants discussed key persuasive strategies, including the use of bright colours, appealing packaging designs, collaborations with celebrities/influencers, humour and price promotions (such as meal deals), as well as promoting products as ‘fun, cool and delicious’. These strategies were seen as particularly influential in shaping youth attitudes about products, who described how marketing ‘changes your idea on products’, made the products seem ‘tempting’ and created a ‘sense of urgency’ to purchase the product:

A lot of marketing is aimed at young people. If it has bright colours, sounds cool or is trendy young people are going to make bad decisions and pick something unhealthy because things that are healthy don’t get advertised. – 17-year-old, female

Others commented that marketing for health harming products was often aligned with youth values, including peer interactions and social status, with products pitched as helping youth to ‘fit in and keep up with the trends’. Some commented that marketing also highlighted that these products were ‘cheap, easy and accessible’ and were ‘influential at masking the negative impacts of something on health’. Participants frequently mentioned the influence of ‘deals’—particularly for junk food and gambling products—and that these types of promotions could prompt impulse purchases. For example, some participants stated that deal-based promotions for gambling were ‘attractive to young people’, while others stated that products that were financially accessible for youth were more desirable:

Making prices cheaper to get more at once is more appealing to young people because we don’t have as much money and try to save it as much as we can even if it means getting something unhealthy or even dangerous (vapes). And of course because of this, the companies put the prices down even just a little bit to make sure that they don’t stop buying their products. If buying a vape without a prescription was $150 then practically everyone would stop almost immediately. – 20-year-old, female

Finally, participants stated that the marketing of health-harming products contained deliberate strategies that sought to deflect the harms associated with their products. Participants gave examples of fruit-flavoured vapes, and specific health claims on packaging like ‘how many foods say fat free, however they contain large quantities of sugar’. If the marketing did contain any mention of negative aspects or harm, participants thought that it was not emphasized and that it was in ‘very small writing’.

Theme two: marketing on social media platforms is more engaging and believable for youth

Participants not only wrote about the influence of different types of appeal strategies but also the influence of different channels used to promote health harming products. For example, when asked if digital marketing was more likely to appeal to young people than traditional forms of marketing such as television ads, many participants perceived that marketing via social media channels—particularly TikTok, YouTube and Instagram—was especially influential for youth—‘If it’s on social media then very influential’. They said this was partly because social media was such a big part of youth lives, which led to their regular exposure to marketing:

Yeah ‘cause in this generation every person has a phone and they can see the ads through their phone. – 19-year-old, female

However, some went further and commented that youth were particularly targeted on social media platforms and that the diverse forms of marketing on these channels were difficult to escape. Social media platforms such as TikTok, Snapchat, YouTube and Instagram were an integral part of youth culture and were able to reach large numbers of youth who spend a considerable part of their day on these apps:

Mediums like TikTok and Instagram are already very popular in young people’s communities as young people are more likely to be on the internet for a majority of their time in day, so using digitals forms of marketing are more likely to reach the younger audience. – 17-year-old, male

They also reported that social media marketing was more dynamic, engaging, believable and convincing for youth and was ‘instrumental’ in influencing their consumption and purchasing habits. Some participants specifically mentioned the range of strategies used on TikTok, including the use of influencers, and the mechanisms that engaged users in reposting, commenting on and sharing videos with their networks. When asked if marketing on digital platforms was more appealing to young people, participants commented that marketers on these platforms were particularly skilled at understanding youth culture and current online trends:

Yes they do because we are on these platforms more and these marketing teams are learning to appeal to our generation by using our humour and culture that we have grown up with. – 16-year-old, female

Participants also described the sense of community that social media platforms could provide youth, including making them ‘feel part of something’. This sense of belonging and shared culture reinforced the influence of social media marketing, as youth felt more connected and engaged with the content and advertisements they encountered:

This is the environment that we’ve grown up in and normalised ourselves in, being and seeing familiar things just make things seem for appealing. – 24-year-old, male

Theme three: CSR strategies help improve the public image of health-harming industries

When asked a yes/no question, over half (n = 308, 59.50%) agreed that CSR and public relations strategies (such as sponsorship) could improve the public image of health-harming industries. However, there were a range of different perspectives about the benefits and risks for youth associated with CSR and public relations activities.

When asked if and how CSR initiatives could improve the public image of health-harming industries, some participants stated that CSR activities indicated that health-harming industries were being more responsible about their products and marketing, and softened the perceptions of the harms associated with consuming products from these companies:

Yes it forces them to be more responsible with their marketing and be true about their products. – 23-year-old, male

Others thought that industry-based CSR activities were important in encouraging individuals to be more responsible with the consumption of their products. Some participants appeared to be echoing industry frames around ‘responsible’ behaviour and informed choices. For example, a few participants thought that industry-based education activities were positive because they highlighted the risks associated with their products:

Yes, encourage(s) responsible behaviours and help(s) to make informed decisions. – 20-year-old, female

Participants perceived that those CSR activities that contributed to youth futures, such as health and education, provided tangible benefits for youth as well as the broader community. These types of activities also enhance a company’s image. However, participants stressed that the underlying intent of these initiatives was important to consider. For example, one participant described that CSR activities were appropriate as long as they were ‘genuine’ and were not a marketing tactic to serve the company’s own interests. Participants emphasized that while they believed it was acceptable for companies to support CSR activities, they should not use them to overtly advertise their brand:

In my view, it can be appropriate for companies to support Corporate Social Responsibility activities targeted at young people, provided these efforts genuinely prioritize the well-being and development of youth rather than just serving as a strategic marketing move. When companies engage in activities that genuinely contribute to education, skill development, health, or positive social change among young people, they can have a positive impact on society. – 16-year-old, male

I don’t think there’s anything inherently bad with doing this as they’re not quite promoting themselves so much as supporting their sponsors. – 21-year-old, male

Some felt that while CSR initiatives were important, and that companies should be contributing to these initiatives, it did not reduce the harm that the companies were causing. However, some took a pragmatic view of these initiatives, including that any contribution back to communities was ‘better than nothing’. Unlike commercial advertising strategies, which many participants in this study viewed critically, participants expressed a more conflicted perspective on the overall risks and benefits of these marketing activities:

They are targeting young people (often their largest market, especially in alcohol, vapes and fast food) so the least they can do is have some form of social responsibility, yet that still doesn’t eliminate the harm they are doing. – 16-year-old, female

Others were confused about these initiatives. A few participants were uncertain about whether CSR activities offset the harms caused by corporations. These participants found it confusing that companies could promote harmful products or practices while simultaneously supporting positive causes. They questioned the true intent behind these activities, feeling that despite the positive aspects of CSR, companies might still be targeting youth to engage with their products:

I’m not sure. I think it’s quite weird they promote something damaging and negative yet support something positive. – 24-year-old, male

However, other participants were concerned CSR activities were simply another strategy used by health-harming industries to encourage the use of their products. They recognized that these activities were simply designed to ‘encourage more people to support these corporations’. For example, one participant mentioned that charitable actions by some companies did not negate the negative impacts of their core business:

[A gambling company] supporting the children’s cancer council doesn’t make their organisation any better. They still ruin lives and families. Gambling is ranked one of the top addictions that people struggle the most to overcome / don’t overcome. The leading cause of suicide is financial issues. – 24-year-old, female

Some participants were particularly concerned about the impact that CSR strategies could have on other youth. They described these types of tactics as ‘manipulative’, stating that youth lacked the critical skills to understand the intention of these types of activities. Some were concerned that these types of tactics would create positive impressions of companies that were harmful to the health and well-being of youth, stating that these strategies created a false narrative about the risks of these products and companies:

No because young minds are susceptible, might have them thinking big corporations are good and righteous for supporting worthy causes. – 20-year-old, female

Some participants described CSR activities as potentially misleading, suggesting that the companies’ ultimate motive was always to drive people towards consuming their products, which could ‘encourage more people to support these corporations’. Some participants stated that CSR tactics were used by companies to ‘entice’ youth to use their products and that youth should be able to ‘go through life’ without this level of corporate influence:

No because that’s when people are most vulnerable. These companies use it to their advantage to entice young people to use their platform. – 20-year-old, female

Theme four: restrictions are needed to counter all forms of marketing

Many participants in this study were supportive of broad restrictions and ‘tobacco style bans’ that were presented to young people in this study on the marketing strategies of health-harming industries. There were a range of reasons given for supporting these restrictions. These included the negative impact on the health and well-being of the community and the overall economic and social costs of these industries:

Yes, they decrease health of the public and cost the government money. – 18-year-old, female

Some participants also saw this as a moral issue—that products that were harmful to the health and well-being of children and should not be promoted in family-friendly environments. Many participants specifically stated that youth were vulnerable to the tactics of these industries and should be protected from promotions. Vaping, gambling and alcohol were particularly mentioned by participants as needing strong marketing restrictions:

I don’t really see any reason why alcohol should be advertised in sport. Families watch these games. Promoting alcohol or gambling around children isn’t right. They are unhealthy habits that ruin people’s lives. – 24-year-old, female

Yes, definitely around gambling and drinking, this will continue to ruin people’s lives and health. – 22-year-old, female

It is important to acknowledge that not all participants were supportive of tobacco-style bans. A few argued that there needed to be individual choice and freedom to make decisions about consuming these products and were concerned that bans could make products seem more appealing to youth or that marketing bans would impact the economy. They also stated that companies may be pushed to find ‘loopholes’ to avoid these restrictions:

No, firstly it’s not effective secondly it would be too strict and encourage companies to find loopholes. – 21-year-old, male

While participants did not always understand the mechanisms of influence associated with broader promotional strategies on policies, they were also largely supportive of restrictions on political donations from health-harming industries. For example, when asked what impact, if any, do you think political donations might have on the decisions that politicians make, some commented that political donations from these industries delayed policy reform and could have an unfair impact on the policy decisions that are made:

…It goes without saying donations are the reason nothing gets done. – 24-year-old, female

It causes bias and unfair decision making as they are receiving large amounts of money. – 23-year-old, male

DISCUSSION

This study aimed to understand youth perceptions of the impact and influence of a range of marketing and public relations strategies used by health harming industries. The findings from this article raise four points for discussion. Figure 1 summarizes the key recommendations from the study.

Youth perspectives on the promotional and public relations strategies of health-harming industries.

First, youth were highly aware of the nature and intent of commercial marketing strategies for health harming products. They had important insights into the range of appeal strategies and marketing channels that were used to promote health harming products and perceived that many of these strategies were designed to target and appeal to youth. Participants discussed how marketing for these products often aligned with youth values and cultures, used novel promotional strategies including influencer and celebrity marketing and was prominent in spaces that were frequented by youth. This ultimately prompted curiosity among youth to try these products. This aligns with previous research which has found that health harming industries have portrayed product consumption as a social and fun activity (Ling and Glantz, 2002; Pitt et al., 2018), used attractive packaging to appeal to youth (Rossheim et al., 2022; Sato et al., 2022) and have aligned their products with youth values and interests (Coombs et al., 2011; Kozinets et al., 2019). Researchers have expressed concern that some marketing tactics may lead youth to be curious and interested in trying harmful products (Pitt et al., 2017; Moran et al., 2021). Importantly, participants in the current study highlighted the constant exposure to health-harming products in their daily lives. While previous studies have investigated youth exposure to these products—such as gambling (Thomas et al., 2023b), unhealthy food (Sutinen et al., 2024) and tobacco and vaping (Venrick et al., 2023)—this study shows that excessive marketing for multiple different health harming products makes it almost impossible for youth to avoid the exposure to such promotions in their everyday lives. While participants in this study showed greater concern about promotions for vaping, tobacco and alcohol, this study shows that it is crucial for the public health community to work together to take a comprehensive approach that looks at overall youth exposure to the marketing for multiple products. As noted by Thomas and colleagues, ‘it is clearly not enough to focus only on strategies that may disrupt young people’s direct engagement… it is important that prevention initiatives focus on reducing young people’s everyday exposure’ (Thomas et al., 2023b, p. 7).

Second, participants viewed social media marketing for health harming products as a particularly influential form of marketing. The global proliferation of the internet and social media has exacerbated the reach and impact of this marketing, with health harming industries collaborating with major tech companies to utilize data-driven techniques to personalize messages and exploit individual vulnerabilities for profit (Pollack et al., 2020; Zenone et al., 2023; World Health Organization, 2024). Participants perceived that the pervasiveness of social media marketing was particularly influential due to the ability of various social media platforms to develop connections and a sense of community through a range of peer engagement strategies. This is important, particularly given the research that also shows that Big Tech companies are potentially problematic CDoH actors in their own right (Hartwell et al., 2024). The use of celebrities and SMIs was particularly influential, supporting research that has found that the use of SMIs transfers symbolic meanings to products and brands, which enhances the trust and appeal of these products (Pitt et al., 2024c). Participants reported that, despite being conscious of the tactics used, they often still found themselves influenced by these strategies. This susceptibility, even when aware of tactics, highlighted the sophisticated nature of these promotions, which were effective at capturing attention by infiltrating the social media spaces that youth frequented. The immense power of social media giants amplifies this issue, making it urgent to implement stricter regulations and enhance transparency in social media marketing of health-harming products (Freeman et al., 2022; Soraghan et al., 2023). This may include honest information about the risks of these products in their advertising, restrictions on the promotion of health-harming products by SMIs, and limits on the use of targeted advertising techniques that exploit personal data.

Third, this study demonstrated that while youth have a good critical understanding of commercial marketing strategies, the impact and influence of some forms of public relations strategies (such as CSR activities and sponsorship) may be less clear. This study found that youth were often sympathetic to and supportive of industry public relations strategies that they thought may benefit youth in some way. While most participants did not think that these strategies could fully mitigate the harm, there was evidence that they thought that industry-based education, employment and youth initiatives could be of benefit to both corporations and youth. This is concerning given literature that suggests that health harming industries are increasingly using youth-washing as a key public relations strategy (Sloan Morgan et al., 2024), including in responsibility-based education initiatives (Landman et al., 2002; Betting and Gaming Council, 2020). Positive perceptions of industry-based education, employment opportunities and youth-focused programs suggest that these specific public relations strategies may have a particular impact on the attitudes of youth towards these companies. The findings of this study highlight the urgent need for public health researchers and organizations to map and monitor the impact of a range of marketing and public relations strategies, with a particular focus on how these may impact youth. The study highlights that comprehensive approaches will be needed to develop strategies to counter and respond to the influence of CSR tactics on youth perceptions and behaviours.

Finally, this study demonstrates the importance of understanding how youth view different forms of marketing, including the range of strategies that could be used to respond. UNICEF highlights that researchers and decision-makers must listen and give weight to youth ideas and opinions on matters that directly affect them (UNICEF, 1995, 2020). This research shows that youth are increasingly critical of the tactics and practices of health-harming industries and are supportive of comprehensive restrictions on a range of marketing and public relations strategies. However, while the support for marketing restrictions extended across various industries, there was a distinct differentiation in the level of concern. Vaping and gambling were consistently identified as areas where stricter regulations were most needed, contrasting with less emphasis on junk food and alcohol marketing. This may be due to the more immediate and visible harms associated with vaping and gambling, as well as successful public health campaigns highlighting the dangers associated with these products (VicHealth, 2023; Butler, 2024). This aligns with previous findings that highlight that youth are critical of corporate political activities and want transparency and fairness in policy-making processes (Arnot et al., 2023a, 2023b; Pitt et al., 2024b). Pitt and colleagues (2022) found that youth were critical about current gambling harm reduction strategies and suggested comprehensive changes. These included changing gambling infrastructure, shifting social perceptions through risk messaging, and disentangling the relationship between gambling and sport. Various youth advocacy and activism movements have sought to platform youth voices in responding to public health issues largely driven by the harmful practices of industries, such as tobacco (Truth Initiative, 2024), food systems (Bite Back, 2024) and fossil fuels (Hilder and Collin, 2022). Such movements demonstrate that youth, including those impacted by socioeconomic inequities, are actively creating and engaging in opportunities to influence agenda-setting and focus political attention on the harmful activities of commercial interests (Zorbas et al., 2023).

LIMITATIONS

There are some limitations associated with online qualitative surveys. This includes a lack of ability to prompt participants for extra meaning and not being able to iteratively reflect upon and adjust questions as done in conversation (Thomas et al., 2024). The data collected for this study occurred before Australia’s new vaping restrictions, which prevent the sale and marketing of vape products in Australian communities. As such, the findings offer a snapshot of youth perspectives prior to these regulations taking effect.

CONCLUSION

Youth are aware of the intent behind most forms of commercial marketing by health harming industries, as well as how they appeal to and influence youth consumption. However, the intent and influence of some marketing strategies—such as CSR—may still not be as clear for youth. Key to public health efforts aimed at protecting the health and well-being of youth will be a consideration of the broader suite of marketing and public relations tactics used by health harming industries. Crucially, public health organizations and researchers should provide a range of opportunities for youth to be consulted in the development of such responses, with their voices respected and involved in discussions that inform responses to commercial marketing by health-harming industries.
