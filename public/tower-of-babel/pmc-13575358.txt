Combined Periacetabular Osteotomy and Hip Arthroscopy Results in High Return to Sport Rates in Contact and Noncontact Athletes With Hip Dysplasia
Authors: Zachariah Whiting, Bhargavi Maheshwer, Marsalis Brown, Michael Salata, Robert Wetzel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Prior studies of patients with hip dysplasia have documented 70% to 90% return to sport (RTS) with arthroscopy alone, 75% to 92% RTS with periacetabular osteotomy (PAO) alone, and approximately 82% to 86% RTS with combined PAO and hip arthroscopy.Purpose:To investigate the RTS rate in patients with hip dysplasia after combined PAO and hip arthroscopy.Study Design:Case series; Level of evidence, 4.Methods:This was a retrospective single-institution review of all patients who underwent a combined PAO and hip arthroscopy between 2016 and 2023. Inclusion criteria were hip dysplasia with a lateral center-edge angle (LCEA) <25° and documented participation in a sport or athletic activity undergoing a combined PAO and hip arthroscopy. Patients were excluded if they had follow-up for <6 months. Demographic data, modified Harris Hip Score (mHHS), RTS, and time to RTS were documented. Preoperative and postoperative radiographs were reviewed with documentation LCEA, extrusion index, acetabular index, and alpha angle.Results:A total of 103 athletic hips in 84 patients were included with a mean age and body mass index of 20.3 ± 6.9 years and 23.9 ± 4.5, respectively. Mean LCEA and AI were 20.8°± 5.4° and 11.9°± 4.8°, respectively. Mean preoperative and postoperative alpha angles were 61.5° and 40.1°, respectively. Mean postoperative follow-up was 514.9 ± 326.5 days. From this cohort, 89.3% of hips returned to sport at a mean of 297.5 days. There was no significant association between RTS rates and any demographics, diagnoses, or preoperative and postoperative measurements. There was no significant association between RTS and mHHS. There was no significant association between degree of dysplasia and RTS. Contact athletes had an 82.5% RTS and noncontact athletes had a 93.7% RTS.Conclusion:This study showed that a combination hip arthroscopy and PAO for the treatment of hip dysplasia exhibited high levels of RTS at 89.3%. There were high rates of RTS in patients with severe dysplasia and those participating in contact sports, specifically. Combined procedures appear to be a very successful option for patients with hip dysplasia hoping to RTS at a high level.

Hip pain is a common complaint of young adult athletes, and hip dysplasia is a well-recognized cause of hip pain in this population.12,16 Hip dysplasia and femoroacetabular impingement constitute a large spectrum of hip pathology that disrupts the normal development and morphology of the growing proximal femur and acetabulum.21,24,29,32 As a result, there is excessive wear on the articular cartilage and degeneration of the acetabular labrum.5,30 Hip dysplasia can lead to severe pain, disability, and early degenerative changes without treatment.28,34 Isolated hip arthroscopy in the setting of hip dysplasia can lead to improved outcomes, but with an unacceptable rate of reoperation and conversion to arthroplasty. Fortunately, treatment with periacetabular osteotomy (PAO) has been shown to improve long-term outcomes.4,19,25,35 Patients with hip dysplasia undergoing PAO procedures have good outcomes with return to sport (RTS) rates of up to 92.5% at around 6 months. 22 Other studies have shown slightly lower RTS rates of around 80% overall and 75% in competitive athletes.6,13 Time to RTS with a PAO alone ranges from 8 to 12 months.6,13 Both a hip arthroscopy and a PAO require months of recovery to return to activity. Therefore, a combined procedure could allow for accelerated recovery without compromising outcomes.

There has been a growth in investigation of combined hip arthroscopy and PAO as the combined procedures are becoming more commonplace to perform. Maldonado et al 23 performed a 5-year follow-up study of concomitant hip arthroscopy followed by PAO, finding that the combined procedures were safe and effective with favorable midterm outcomes. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis by Hecht and colleagues 11 evaluated outcomes, survivorship, and complication rates among patients who underwent concomitant PAO and hip arthroscopy compared with isolated PAO. They found that patients undergoing both procedures experienced excellent outcomes, but there was limited evidence to indicate that performing both procedures resulted in different outcomes compared with isolated PAO. With regard to RTS data, Wyatt et al 33 performed a systematic review investigating RTS in combined hip arthroscopy and PAO versus isolated PAO and only had 1 included study for the combined procedure group.

Despite the increased awareness and publication in concomitant hip arthroscopy and PAO studies, there remains a lack of literature investigating RTS specifically. As such, the purpose of this study was to investigate the RTS rate in patients with hip dysplasia after combined PAO and hip arthroscopy. We hypothesize that RTS rates will be high for patients undergoing combined hip arthroscopy and PAO surgery.

Methods

This study was approved by the institutional review board of Case Western Reserve University. A retrospective review was conducted to identify all patients who underwent a combined nonstaged PAO and hip arthroscopy between January of 2016 and December of 2023. Patients were identified by query of the billing database by Current Procedural Terminology codes 27299, 29860, 29861, 29862, 29863, 29914, 29914, 29916, and S2115. One surgeon (R.W.) performed all PAO procedures and 1 surgeon (M.S.) performed all hip arthroscopies. Indications for PAO included hip dysplasia with Tönnis grade 0 or 1, lateral center-edge angle (LCEA) <25°, acetabular index (AI) >12°, retroversion, or instability. Indications for adding a hip arthroscopy to the PAO included a labral tear on preoperative magnetic resonance imaging, CAM lesion, evaluation of chondral damage, and if a patient's hip with acetabular retroversion was predicted to impinge anteriorly after PAO. Hip dysplasia was defined as LCEA <25°. Patients ≥14 years old with documented participation in a sport or athletic activity undergoing a combined PAO and hip arthroscopy were included in the study. Patients were excluded for not participating in a sport or activity or having follow-up <6 months.

Data Collection

Demographic data were compiled from electronic medical record review to include age, body mass index (BMI), sex, sports or activities, and history of prior ipsilateral hip surgery. Sports were grouped into contact and noncontact based on surgeon experience (Table 1).

Preoperative data were collected including hip-related diagnoses as well as radiographic measurements. Hypermobility was assessed preoperatively using a Beighton score. Specific arthroscopic procedure was noted. Instability was assessed intraoperatively during the hip arthroscopy. Postoperative data were collected including modified Harris Hip Score (mHHS), radiographic measurements, and RTS and time to RTS. Radiographs were reviewed by a single attending surgeon (R.W.) with documentation of LCEA, extrusion index (EI), AI, and alpha angle.

A Ganz PAO was performed with 4 cuts: pubic ramus and the ischial, iliac, and posterior columns. A 3-portal hip arthroscopy was performed. An interportal capsulotomy was the standard with femoral and acetabular osteoplasty, as needed. If a labral repair was indicated, a 2-anchor repair was performed. Capsular closure was always performed. Patients were 13.6 kg flat-foot weightbearing postoperatively and progressed through a standard rehabilitation protocol.

Postoperative notes in the electronic medical record were reviewed for confirmation that a patient had returned to his or her sport or activity. The date of the first note mentioning RTS was considered the RTS date if otherwise not specified. For any patients where RTS was not mentioned directly in the electronic medical record, remote follow-up was performed. Patients were asked whether they returned to the sport or activity that they participated in prior to surgery and were asked to estimate how many months it took them to return. If a patient was not reachable, that patient was excluded from the present study.

Statistical Analysis

Categorical variables were compared between groups utilizing Fisher exact and Pearson chi-square testing while continuous variables were compared with independent t tests. P values <.05 were considered significant. All data were securely stored electronically and analyzed using Social Science Statistics (https://www.socscistatistics.com/).

Results

Review of the billing database identified 215 hips undergoing a combined PAO and hip arthroscopy during the study period. Among these, 86 were excluded for not participating in a sport or activity, 74 hips had a documented RTS in the electronic medical record, 18 patients reported RTS after remote follow-up, 11 patients reported no RTS upon remote follow-up, and 26 patients were unreachable. A total of 103 athletic hips in 84 patients were included with a mean age and BMI of 20.3 ± 6.9 years and 23.9 ± 4.5, respectively. Mean LCEA and AI were 20.8°± 5.4° and 11.9°± 4.8°, respectively. Mean postoperative follow-up was 514.9 ± 326.5 days (Table 2).

The following complications and reoperations occurred in this cohort. A total of 59 hips underwent elective removal of painful hardware. Five patients developed heterotopic ossification, 12 patients developed lateral femoral cutaneous nerve neuropraxia. Two patients developed postoperative infections, and there was 1 case of pubic ramus nonunion.

Discussion

We were able to demonstrate that combination hip arthroscopy and PAO for the treatment of hip dysplasia exhibited high levels of RTS, even in patients with severe dysplasia and those participating in contact sports. Combination PAO and hip arthroscopy is a more recent advancement in the world of hip preservation. Patients may have attempted an isolated hip arthroscopy first, given that a PAO is a larger undertaking. However, failure rates for isolated hip arthroscopy in the setting of borderline dysplasia are between 0% and 53.3% and reoperation rates range from 0 to 44%. 17 Nho et al 29 documented an RTS rate of 75.6% in patients with borderline dysplasia undergoing arthroscopy alone. The current study's RTS rate of 89.3% with a combination procedure suggests better outcomes. Nho et al also reported that it took athletes 8.3 months, or approximately 250 days, to RTS. 1 Athletes in the current study took an additional 2 months to RTS, but they were also recovering from a PAO. Results of RTS after isolated PAO have been well-documented. RTS rates range from 70% to 92% in several studies.6,13,22 Athletes in the current study returned to sport at 89% after combination procedures. Therefore, athletes are at least returning at almost equivalent rates with combination procedures as compared with isolated PAO, but at best are returning at far higher rates.

Demographic data including contact sports, sex, BMI, prior hip surgery, and labral repair were not statistically significantly correlated with RTS (Table 4). While female sex has been identified as a risk factor for failure of hip arthroscopy for dysplasia, our data did not suggest any significant correlation between sex and RTS. 17 Our findings differ from prior literature that shows female patients being less likely to have a successful RTS. 7 Also in contrast to prior literature, our data did not show any significant correlation between BMI and RTS. Leopold et al 22 demonstrated that there was a significant negative correlation between higher BMI and postoperative activity levels. There was no significant difference between contact and noncontact sport RTS rates, although noncontact sports were overall more likely to RTS. Contact sports are oftentimes more physically demanding and incur a higher risk of injury. Prior literature has shown greater number of athletes returning to noncontact sports, but we were unable to show any statistically significant difference.18,22 It is possible that the types of individual sports in our series differed from prior studies. It is not uncommon for patients to alter their activities postoperatively. While the current study demonstrated a high RTS in contact sports, many patients may choose to engage in other lower impact sports and activities. 22 Neither prior hip surgery nor labral repair was significantly associated with RTS. Prior data suggest that revision hip arthroscopy can improve outcome scores in patients who continue to have pain and dysfunction after their index arthroscopy. 10 However, the magnitude of improvement is often less than primary arthroscopy. 2 Patients undergoing revision hip arthroscopy are also less likely to attempt returning to sport. 27 Our data differ from this literature, as prior hip surgery was not associated with RTS in the current study.

Diagnoses including cam deformity, labral tear, FAI, instability, acetabular retroversion, and dysplasia were not significantly associated with RTS (Table 5). Neither the presence of FAI, dysplasia in general, nor severity of dysplasia was significantly associated with RTS. This remained the case when dysplasia was subcategorized into instability and acetabular retroversion. The presence of a labral tear, irrespective of repair, was not associated with RTS. It would be reasonable to think that an athlete without a labral tear would be able to RTS faster and more consistently than an athlete with a labral tear who healed that tear with or without surgery. However, our data do not support that theory. Cam deformity was also not associated with RTS. While larger cam deformities are more common in male patients and are more likely to be treated with hip arthroscopy, our data do not suggest an association between cam deformities and RTS in patients with dysplastic hips.8,15,31

Preoperative radiographic parameters including LCEA, AI, EI, and alpha angle were also not significantly associated with RTS (Table 6). Our overall high RTS rate was not driven by patients with less severe dysplasia doing better since there was no significant difference in RTS rates between severities of dysplasia. In fact, patients with severe and borderline dysplasia returned at a higher rate than patients with normal LCEA. Patients with severe dysplasia and borderline dysplasia returned to sport at 93.5% and 91.9%, respectively (Table 4). This study's data demonstrate that even patients with severe dysplasia can RTS at a high rate and level. The 20 patients with normal LCEA were subcategorized (Table 8). Even though these patients had normal LCEA, their retroversion or instability made them candidates for a PAO. While they returned at a lower rate than their more dysplastic counterparts, three-quarters of these athletes did RTS.

Postoperative radiographic parameters including LCEA, AI, EI, and alpha angle were not associated with RTS (Table 7). This is somewhat surprising, as correcting patients’ lack of coverage and creating a more stable hip would in theory make them more likely to be able to tolerate sports and other strenuous activities. Similar to this study's findings, postoperative alpha angle does not correlate with outcomes after hip arthroscopy. 1 Postoperative alpha angle has had mixed data regarding outcomes and RTS. Multiple studies have shown that a postoperative alpha angle below 46° to 48° is significantly associated with better outcomes and higher RTS.9,26 However, other studies have shown that postoperative alpha angle is not associated with outcomes following hip arthroscopy for FAI. 1

This study did not find an association between postoperative mHHS and RTS (Table 7). It would be reasonable to expect that patients with higher outcome scores would be more likely to return to participating in athletic activities. When looking at hip arthroscopy alone in patients with FAI, outcomes are quite good and mHHS consistently improves after surgery.3,9 Meta-analysis has indicated that patients undergoing combined PAO and hip arthroscopy have significant improvement in outcome scores. 20 Jimenez et al 14 demonstrated that 81.8% of athletes undergoing combined PAO and hip arthroscopy returned to sport and showed significant improvement in mHHS. While we were able to show high RTS rates, we were unable to demonstrate an association between those high RTS rates and mHHS. However, mHHS may not be the best outcome measure for RTS in this population. mHHS is more focused on basic functionality in the less active adult, rather than RTS in the dysplastic, active athlete.

Limitations

This study had several limitations. First, this was a retrospective study that relied on complete electronic medical record documentation. Unfortunately, not all documentation provided RTS information and thus we relied on remote follow-up whose RTS information was missing. Patients are likely quite accurate in recalling whether they returned to sport, but not necessarily as accurate when estimating how many months it took them to RTS from surgery. As a result, a recall bias may have been introduced. Second, radiographic assessment was performed by a single fellowship-trained surgeon (R.W.). This may have introduced an interobserver bias because only 1 surgeon evaluated the radiographs. Additionally, there was a relatively short follow-up period and only 1 outcome score. Our sample size was low, although the largest for a study of this sort, with low numbers for some variables, which could have introduced a type 2 error.

Despite these limitations, all PAO procedures were performed by 1 fellowship-trained trauma and hip reconstruction surgeon (R.W.) and all arthroscopies were performed by 1 sports medicine fellowship-trained surgeon (M.S.). Therefore, outcomes did not differ based on who performed the surgery. Additionally, this was a large series with 103 athletic hips in 84 patients undergoing combination PAO and arthroscopy at a single institution. Future investigations include subcategorizing into level of competition and evaluating maintenance of performance years out from the index procedure.

Conclusion

This study showed that combination hip arthroscopy and PAO for the treatment of hip dysplasia exhibited high levels of RTS at 89.3%. Additionally, there were high rates of RTS in patients with severe dysplasia and those participating in contact sports, specifically. Combined procedures appear to be a very successful option for patients with hip dysplasia hoping to RTS.
