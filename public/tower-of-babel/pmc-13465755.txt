Constant ovulation during oral contraceptive treatment: Follicle-stimulating hormone-secreting pituitary adenoma case report and literature review
Authors: Yang Yang, Lin Qiao, Jing Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH)-producing pituitary neuroendocrine tumor is very rare and usually present with menstrual disorders, ovarian cysts, polycystic ovary morphology (PCOM), and so on. It is often confused with polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) and is difficult to diagnose. Missed or misdiagnosis may lead to unnecessary surgical injury or severe neurologic damage. We describe the case of a 37-year-old woman with persistent menstrual disturbances and ovarian cysts. Because of unceasing ovulation after usage of combined oral contraceptive (COC) she was suspected of having endogenous uncontrolled FSH and eventually a pituitary adenoma was found. In addition, we review the features of onset, diagnostic history, treatment, and prognosis of previous similar reports. We find the symptoms of FSH-secreting pituitary adenomas puzzling. Mild cases are easily overlooked, and severe cases are easily misdiagnosed as ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome and ovarian tumors and receive unnecessary pharmacological and surgical interventions. Attention to abnormal sex hormones is key to diagnosis, and prompt surgical tumor removal is important.

Background

Pituitary neuroendocrine tumors (PitNETs), also known as pituitary adenomas, are classified as functional or nonfunctional PitNETs based on hormone production. Meanwhile, pituitary adenomas are the second most common intracranial tumor in adults.[1] Approximately 64% of nonfunctional PitNETs are confirmed to be gonadotropin tumors by immunohistochemical assay. It is only detected when the tumor is large enough to show signs of compression.[2] Gonadotropin tumors originate from follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) and luteinizing hormone (LH) cells, which account for 35% of all PitNETs and 10% of benign pituitary tumors.[13] In conclusion, functional gonadotropin tumors are very rare PitNETs. A review of the literature reveals fewer than 50 case reports of FSH-only secreting pituitary adenomas in women. LH-only pituitary adenomas are much rarer, and cases of mixed adenomas with combined FSH and LH secretion and combined FSH and thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH) secretion have been reported. Women suffer from menstrual disorders, infertility and enlarged ovaries. Prolonged menstrual cycles and secondary amenorrhea are problems that plague them. Enlarged ovaries are treated as unexplained “pelvic giants” and further investigations reveal possible ovarian tumors. In a small number of cases, polycystic ovarian changes are reported at the time of initial diagnosis.

Women with polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) most often present with spontaneous polycystic ovary morphology (PCOM), and factors such as hyperandrogenemia lead to anovulation. Menstrual disturbances, secondary amenorrhea, and infertility also co-occur. Medically induced interventions such as in vitro fertilization-embryo transfer also lead to PCOM that obviously, occurs as ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome. However, spontaneous ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome (sOHSS) is a rare event that may be caused by FSH-producing adenoma (FSHoma) as well as other causes.[4]

We report a case of pituitary FSHoma in premenopausal woman with menstrual disturbances and PCOM. The symptoms and signs of sOHSS was ignored. Initially, she was misdiagnosed as ovarian cyst with normal serum levels of tumor markers and combined oral contraceptive (COC) was given.

Case Presentation

A 37-year-old woman came to hospital in January 2022 with prolonged periods. In addition, she had no other discomfort. Transvaginal ultrasound revealed a 6.6 × 4.1 × 5 cm septated cyst in her right adnexa; and a 6.1 × 3.9 × 5.8 cm septated cyst in her left adnexa. Serum tumor markers were normal. The levels of sex hormones seemed in the luteal phase, but with very low level of LH and high level of FSH [Table 1]. This woman had previously had normal and regular menstrual periods with occasional mild dysmenorrhea. There was no history of specific illnesses and already had one healthy child. Oral contraceptive was prescribed and then helped her recover normal cycle in the first 3 months. However, irregular menstrual cycle was recurred during COC treatment and even ovulation occurred with high levels of progesterone, estradiol, FSH and low level of LH [Table 1].

Lactation was found in this young woman. Transvaginal ultrasound showed enlarged bilateral ovaries with multiple cysts [Figure 1c]. Other causes of PCOM such as hyperandrogenemia were excluded with low level of testosterone. The patient was suspected of suffering from spontaneous FSH secretion and spontaneous ovulation, and was scheduled for a pituitary MRI. There had been no headaches, vision loss, visual field defects, and so on. Two months after MRI test, the patient underwent transsphenoidal pituitary tumor resection [Figure 1f]. Immunohistochemistry of the tumor tissue suggested: Syn (+), CK8 and 18 (partially +), PIT-1 (−), SF-1 (+), T-PIT (±), ACTH (−), ER (partially +), FSH (+), GH (−), LH (−), prolactin (PRL) (−), TSH (−), and Ki-67 (MIB-1) (+, about 3%). Special staining results: PAS (−), reticular fibers (tumor cell nests +). This result proved that the tumor is a pituitary neuroendocrine tumor of SF-1 spectrum, which is pituitary gonadotropin tumor. The FSH levels returned to normal when examined on day 5 after surgery [Table 1]. On the 25th day, both ovaries were still large, but there was no increase in volume. No signs of hypopituitarism were found. She was asked to monitor pituitary hormone levels and undergo pituitary MRI at regular intervals.

Discussion

After searching PubMed for “gonadotropin pituitary adenoma” and reading the title and abstract, as of November 20, 2024, there were 61 publications describing the disease in women. Of these, 39 were case reports, and we included 43 patients for analysis [Figure 2].[5]

Temporal distribution of reported female FSH-secreting pituitary adenoma cases, 1989–2025

These case reports were published intermittently between 1989 and 2024, with a mean age of illness of 31.07 years. The youngest was 6 years old and was sexually precocious.[5] The oldest was 50 years old with severe headaches and visual disturbances.[6]

The most common symptom was menstrual disorders in about 51% of cases [Figure 3]. This included prolonged periods in 65.4%, amenorrhea in 26.9%, and precocious puberty in 7.7%. Ovarian volume changes were found in 47.1% of reports. This included enlarged ovaries such as polycystic changes in 54.2%; 37.5% reported ovarian cysts or tumors; unknown pelvic masses were reported in two cases. 19.6% presented with abdominal pain, all of which occurred for a short time. 15.7% presented with headache, 15.7% presented with loss of vision and 11.8% presented with lactation. Six cases of primary infertility were reported.

Clinical manifestations in female patients with FSH-secreting pituitary adenomas (n=47)

Sex hormone disturbance is regular. Most patients undergo a sex hormone test, even if it is not timely and complete. We found that 26 (84.1%) patients had increased estrogen, 32 (59.4%) had increased FSH, 26 (76.9%) had decreased LH, and 16 (81.3%) had increased PRL. Previous literature has suggested that increased estrogen and very low LH are more indicative than increased FSH. Co-occurrence of high estrogen and low LH with or without increased FSH was seen in 61.5% of cases.

The size of pathologically confirmed FSH adenoma was reported in 35 cases with a mean maximum diameter of 21.25 mm. The maximum of 41 mm was reported by Omkar Mayur in a case of a woman with history of bilateral ovarian cysts, who underwent hysterectomy of the uterus and both adnexa at the age of 39, and pituitary adenomas were detected in her at the age of 50 due to a sudden loss of vision. Pituitary microadenomas were found in six cases (17.1%).[7]

Thirty-nine patients underwent transsphenoidal surgery after detection of a pituitary tumor. However, we were shocked to find that 20 patients suffered at least one abdominal operation before diagnosis, mainly due to concerns about tumors in the pelvis. Enlarged ovarian cysts were removed or cyst fluid aspirated during surgery. In one patient, anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) was checked after surgery and found to be very low.[789] Therefore, agnogenic cyst existed in ovary might be the indication of surgery, which was also found in present case. Even more, these cysts might be also misdiagnosed as the sign of PCOS.

At first, when faced with disorganized hormones and disturbed menstruation, a small number of patients are treated with COC, gonadotropin-releasing hormone antagonist (GnRH-a), or carbamazepine. Bromocriptine is used for high levels of PRL. COC treats menstrual disorders but is very likely to mask symptoms. GnRH-a does not decrease hormone levels and relieve enlarged ovaries, and may even make things worse.[10] COC and bromocriptine have been found to be effective in reducing ovarian size in some cases.

We found that 55% of postoperative pathologic examinations revealed only FSH (+). Mixed adenomas of FSH (+) and LH (+) (37.9%). FSH (+) mixed with TSH (+) or PRL (+) were reported occasionally.

After pituitary surgery, hormone levels return to normal as soon as the first day after surgery and menstruation recovers as soon as one month. Ovarian volume returns to normal as soon as 3 months after surgery. However, one patient had persistent amenorrhea, possibly related to her undergoing two unnecessary ovarian surgeries.[9] A total of 12 patients had successful pregnancies, two of which were assisted by in vitro fertilization and embryo transfer (IVF-ET).

Recurrence was mentioned in six cases.[1112] The recurrence was detected by MRI at the earliest three months postoperatively, and this patient was asymptomatic. So, she refused radiotherapy and opted for follow-up observation.[12] Two patients received radiotherapy.[13]

Conclusion

The main symptoms and hormonal characteristics of this case are like those previously reported, but are worth to be explored.

In fact, irregular menstruation and enlarged changes in the size of both ovaries are atypical. Suddenly enlarged ovaries are first considered neoplastic and then subjected to unnecessary ovarian surgery. Ovarian cysts in PCOS are usually less than ten mm in size, whereas those in OHSS are usually greater than 15 mm.[14]

In addition to exclusion of ovarian neoplasms, PCOS, and OHSS due to medically induced ovarian stimulation by the relevant screenings, consider an increase in endogenous FSH.

Wang L had found that the diagnosis of functional pituitary adenomas is usually delayed by about 3 years, and about half of the patients had undergone ovarian surgery prior to diagnosis.[15]

If one is fortunate enough to avoid surgery, it can also be misdiagnosed as other reproductive endocrine disorders such as PCOS, where recurrent breakthrough bleeding and atypical hyperplasia of the endometrium often occurs due to chronic hyperestrogenic endometrial hyperstimulation. Oral contraceptives may be able to suppress serum estradiol levels, even when elevated due to FSH-secreting pituitary adenomas.[16]

To adjust hormone levels, oral contraceptives may be effective in the beginning, but can make diagnosis more difficult. In present study, spontaneous ovulation was found during the treatment of COC. So, this clinical clue indicated the spontaneous FSH secreting and prompt the examination of MRI. Using dopamine agonists (bromocriptine, cabergoline) may reduce FSH levels but usually does not decrease tumor size.[17] Treatment with GnRH analogues does not decrease tumor size also.[18]

The situation is further complicated by the fact that some infertile patients may go through IVF-ET and the possibility of using GnRHa exists. In patients who have not been diagnosed with gonadotropin pituitary adenomas, blind use of GnRH-a can exacerbate the condition. Successful pregnancies can also lead to OHSS. De Leener suggests that the pathogenesis of OHSS can be subdivided into three types.[19]

OHSS during pregnancy is type 1, in which elevated human chorionic gonadotropincross-reacts (hCG) with mutated FSH receptors. Types 2 and 3 are associated with high levels of hCG and TSH, respectively. Gonadotrophic pituitary adenomas have spontaneous ovulation and may result in natural conception, but pregnancy may exacerbate the condition.

Hormone measurements are necessary in women of reproductive age with menstrual disorders and are not limited to the gonads. The patients in this case had no abnormalities on pituitary examination 1 year earlier, and a similar case was reported by Osamu Wada-Hiraike.[19]

Hormones are prior to abnormal pituitary imaging, so correctly interpreting the hormone report is critical at the initial diagnosis. Doctors should be aware of abnormal level of hormones. In patients with FSH-producing pituitary tumors, elevated estradiol can inhibit the hypothalamic-anterior pituitary axis through a negative feedback mechanism, reducing excess FSH production to normal levels. Therefore, normal or slightly lower levels of FSH may occur. However, LH levels can be reduced below the lower limit of the normal range by negative feedback mechanisms or by tumor compression of the normal pituitary gland. Therefore, the imbalance ratio of FSH and LH should also be considered as the clinical clue of pituitary adenoma and further tests should be scheduled.

Davis suggested that FSH-secreting pituitary adenomas may be due to their slow rate of cell proliferation, which needs time to gain sufficient mass to induce a clinical syndrome.[20]

We have also found that pituitary macroadenomas are more common than pituitary microadenomas. Andino-Ríos et al.[21] reported a case of a patient with a progressively more hormonally abnormal functional pituitary adenoma who was initially thought to have a nonfunctional pituitary adenoma.

Transsphenoidal pituitary tumor resection is the modality of choice for the treatment of pituitary tumors. Adenoma recurrence correlates with adenoma size and needs to be diagnosed as soon as possible. According to Mehrazin M tumor size as well as the young age of the patient seem to be indicators of a higher risk of pituitary tumor recurrence.[22]

Radiotherapy (Gamma Knife) has a limited therapeutic effect on pituitary tumors, which have a very high recurrence rate, and therefore should only be used as an addition to clinical treatment.[9] Only one patient reported postoperative hypopituitarism and urolithiasis, in which the surgery affected both the neuropituitary and the adenopituitary. Her pituitary tumor also recurred. Therefore, regular postoperative follow-up of pituitary function and pituitary structure is important. Patients require long-term follow-up in gynecology, endocrinology, and neurosurgery.
