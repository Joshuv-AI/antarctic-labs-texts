Health and social services utilisation of adults with a cochlear implant: evidence from a national person-level integrated data asset in Australia
Authors: Tolesa Okuba, Reidar P Lystad, Isabelle Boisvert, Anne McMaugh, Robyn Cantle Moore, Peter Wolnizer, Cassidy Chow, Ramya Walsan, Rebecca J Mitchell
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe frequency and type of health and social services use following cochlear implantation are unclear. This study aimed to examine health and social services utilisation among adult cochlear implant users.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted among adults aged ≥ 18 years who received a cochlear implant between 2011 and 2021 in Australia. Linked data from the Person-level Integrated Data Asset (PLIDA) were utilised. A negative binomial regression was used to examine factors associated with health and social service use in younger (18–64 years) and older adults (≥ 65 years).ResultsA total of 6,305 adults received a cochlear implant: 2,715 (43·1%) younger and 3,590 (56·9%) older adults. Older adults had a higher mean number of general practitioner (GP) visits (48.0) compared with younger adults (34.2), but younger adults visited specialists more often (7.1 vs. 4.7). Audiologists visit numbers were similar for both groups, with mean of 7.1 for younger and 7.5 for older adults. Factors associated with primary care (i.e. general practitioner visits) for both younger and older adults included being female, a culturally and linguistically diverse (CALD) background, comorbidities, needing assistance for activities of daily living (ADL), living in areas of socioeconomic disadvantage and having had ≥ 2 implants. Factors associated with specialist visits for younger adults included being female, a CALD background, being employed, or having a mental health condition, while for older adults these factors included needing assistance with ADLs, and long-term health or mental health conditions. Younger adults from CALD backgrounds, living in socioeconomically disadvantaged areas, with low income and experiencing mental health condition were more likely to receive government benefits.ConclusionSociodemographic factors and comorbidities influence utilisation to both healthcare and social services for adults with a cochlear implant. This underscores the need for integrated care, considering psychological and health needs of the implant users.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13939-4.Keywords: Adults, Cochlear implant, Health, Social, Services use

Background

Hearing loss is a global public health problem. Approximately 1.5 billion individuals – 20% of the world’s population – are living with hearing loss, of which nearly 430 million have moderate or severe hearing loss requiring rehabilitation [1]. Hearing loss prevalence is expected to rise due to population aging and increased exposure to risk factors such as loud noise [1]. In Australia, about four million people or 15% of the country’s population – experience some degree of hearing loss [2]. Hearing loss not only affects communication but is also associated with social isolation and loneliness [3], depression [4, 5], dementia and cognitive decline [3, 6, 7], highlighting the critical need for effective interventions. For example, although hearing aids do not directly treat depression, they can help mitigate the social isolation and communication difficulties that often contribute to its onset [8].

For some adults with moderate to severe hearing loss who obtain limited benefits from hearing aids, cochlear implants can enhance hearing capability [9], resulting in improved health-related quality of life [10–12] and enhanced social experience [13, 14]. Additionally, cochlear implants may improve mental health by reducing anxiety, stress and enhancing quality of life, though their impact on depression remains uncertain [15, 16]. Previous studies on the health and social service use for people who have received a cochlear implant have primarily focused on short-term follow-up [17, 18] and include a small number of participants [19–21]. Research recommendations called for more extensive research, involving large databases, to explore the long-term impact on health and social service use among adults who have received a cochlear implant [10, 15]. Beyond healthcare, cochlear implants can have a profound benefit on an individual’s social outcomes, such as their ability to participate in educational opportunities [22], participate in the workforce [22–25], and can impact their personal income [14, 26].

Despite potential benefits, there remains a paucity of studies on the longitudinal impact on healthcare and social service utilisation among adults who had received a cochlear implant in Australia. For instance, there were very few studies that found a reduction in medication use and audiologist visits among cochlear implant users [17, 19]. Understanding differences in healthcare and social service utilisation among younger and older adults is crucial for healthcare planning and resource allocation for affected individuals. This study aimed to examine health, and social services use among younger and older adult cochlear implant users in Australia.

Methods

Study design and linkage

This was a population-based retrospective cohort study using 2011–2021 data from the Person Level Integrated Data Asset [27]. The Australian Bureau of Statistics (ABS) performed the linkage between datasets using both deterministic and probabilistic techniques, utilising information such as names, date of birth, address and sex.

Data sources

The datasets include the: Medicare Benefits Schedule (MBS); Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme (PBS); Census of Population and Housing; Data Over Multiple Individual Occurrences (DOMINO) Centrelink, and information from Australian income and taxation records. Information on the number of visits to general practitioner (GP), specialists and audiologists were identified using MBS item numbers. Additionally, costs such as fee charged, and benefit paid for GPs and specialist services were identified from the MBS. The PBS includes information on prescribed medicines, date of prescription and supply, and type of drug supplied. The Australian Census of Population and Housing occurs every five years and includes information such as demographic characteristics, cultural backgrounds, needing assistance for ADLs, education and training, health conditions, household and family, housing, income and work, and employment [28]. The DOMINO includes information regarding Centrelink administrated government payments or services. Income and taxation records were obtained from the Australian Taxation Office [29], included total income and reported losses on individual tax returns by financial year. The data used in this study was obtained in a fully de-identified format.

Case identification

All adults aged ≥ 18 years who received a cochlear implant in Australia between 1 January 2011 and 31 December 2021 and had MBS item number indicating a cochlear implant (i.e. item number: 41617) were identified using MBS dataset. Post-cochlear implant use of health and social services was determined based on the date of the initial (index) cochlear implantation. Services accessed from the date of initial implantation onward until census at the end of the study (i.e. 31 December 2021) or death were classified as post-implantation services.

Geographic location of residence and socioeconomic status

Rural and urban classifications were determined using the Australian Statistical Geographical Standard [30], based on residents’ Statistical Area Level (SA2) locations. SA2 data were obtained via PLIDA, which integrates geographic information [27] and Socio-Economic Indexes for Areas (SEIFA) indicators [31]. Geographic details were sourced from the 2021 Census, DOMINO Centrelink, Australian Medicare Consumer Directory, and taxation records. Additionally, socio-economic status was assessed by integrating geographic residence with SEIFA indicators. Each participant was assigned a status based on the Index of Relative Socioeconomic Disadvantage (IRSD), derived from national census data [28]. The IRSD encompasses variables such as income, education, employment, and occupation. Scores were grouped into quintiles, from most disadvantaged (1) to least disadvantaged (5).

Identification of comorbidities and long-term health conditions

The RxRisk comorbidity index [32] was used to identify up to 46 individual’s comorbidity status from the PBS dispensing history. Additionally, the 2021 Census [28] was used to identify up to nine of the most common long-term health conditions such as arthritis, asthma, cancer, diabetes, heart disease, kidney disease, lung condition, a mental health condition, and stroke.

Health and social outcomes

The following health and social outcomes were examined:

Number of GP, specialist and audiologist services accessed post-cochlear implantation, representing healthcare use.

Number of GP, specialist and audiologist services accessed post-cochlear implantation, representing healthcare use.

Fee charged and benefit paid, reflecting both individuals financial burden and broader service costs.

Fee charged and benefit paid, reflecting both individuals financial burden and broader service costs.

Personal income level, indicating socioeconomic status.

Personal income level, indicating socioeconomic status.

Number of government benefits received, and types of concession cards accessed, representing financial support needs.

Statistical analysis

Data management and analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc) within the ABS’s DataLab. Descriptive statistics were used to characterise the cohort, health and social service use. Chi-square tests of independence were used for categorical variables to compare characteristics of younger and older adults. The mean and median GP, specialist and audiologist visits as well as costs for GP and specialist visits were calculated. T-tests or Mann Whitney U tests of independence, as appropriate, were used to compare medians or means between younger and older cohorts. To account for general price changes for all goods and services between 2011 and 2021, all dollar values were adjusted for inflation using the ABS’s Consumer Price Index to the 2021 Australian dollars [33].

Factors associated with health and social service use, informed by literature [14, 17, 19, 22, 26] and available in the data were examined. Variables included in the model were sex, CALD backgrounds (CALD/non-CALD), urban/rural residence, socioeconomic status (most to least disadvantaged), employment status (employed/unemployed), annual personal income bracket (negative or nil, $1–20,999, $20,800 − 51,999 and ≥$52,000), assistance with ADLs (yes/no), number of RxRisk comorbidities (0–2, 3–5, 6–8, 9–11 and ≥ 12 comorbidities), number of long-term health conditions (0, 1 and ≥ 2 conditions), mental health condition (yes/no), and number of cochlear implants (1 or ≥ 2). The personal income categories were collapsed into four groups to address the issue of sparse data in certain brackets. This enhances statistical power and ensure sufficient sample sizes within each category – thereby improving the performance of the model.

Model fit was evaluated using the Scaled Pearson chi-square statistics (good fit if < 1.25). Due to overdispersion, negative binomial regression was used to quantify the association between characteristics and health and social service use. Univariable regression was used to identify candidate variables at p < 0.25. A backward stepwise multivariable regression was used to remove factors that did not significantly contribute to study outcomes, applying a significance level of p < 0.05 for variables screened in the univariable analysis. Rate ratios (RR) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated.

Results

There were 6,305 adults who had received a cochlear implant between 2011 and 2021, including 2,715 (43.1%) younger adults aged 18–64 years and 3,590 (56.9%) older adults aged ≥ 65 years (Table 1). The mean age at the time of the cochlear implantation for younger adults was 48.7 years (standard deviation (SD) 12.2) and for older adults was 75.3 years (SD 6.6). The mean duration cochlear implant use was 4.9 years (SD 3.0) for younger and 4.1 years (SD 2.9) for older adults. Females accounted for 57.3% of the younger adults and for 44.9% of the older adults. The majority of younger adults (77.3%) and older adults (72.4%) lived in urban areas. A higher proportion (15.5%) of younger adults had received at least two cochlear implants during the study period, compared to 9.8% of older adults. In certain situations, adults might have received multiple cochlear implants. These include both ears implanted, encountering complications requiring device removal and re-implantation, or undergoing procedures such as a magnetic resonance imaging scan where the implant is temporarily removed and then replaced.

Health service used after cochlear implantation

The mean number of audiologist visits during the study period was 7.1 (SD 10.5) for younger adults and 7.5 (SD 10.1) for older adults.

Medicare schedule fee and benefit paid for health service use

The median Medicare benefit paid for GP visits per person was AUD$1,157 (interquartile range (IQR) 1,726) for younger adults and AUD$1,801 (IQR 2,825) for older adults (see Table S1). The median benefit paid for a specialist visit was AUD$202 (IQR 475) for younger adults and AUD$230 (IQR 489) for older adults.

Among older adults, the median Medicare benefit paid for GP visits rose and peaked at the sixth year post-implant (Fig. 2b). The median benefit paid for specialist visits was higher for younger adults compared to older adults up to the end of the fourth year post-implant (Fig. 2d).

Characteristics associated with GP and specialist visits

For younger adults, being female (RR 1.22; 95%CI 1.14–1.30), having a CALD background (RR 1.24; 95%CI 1.14–1.35), living in areas of socioeconomic disadvantage (RR 1.23; 95%CI 1.11–1.38), needing assistance for ADLs (RR 1.09; 95%CI 1.01–1.17) and having ≥ 2 implants (RR 1.44; 95%CI 1.31–1.57) were all associated with a higher number of GP visits compared to reference groups (Fig. 3). Additionally, the highest rate ratio observed among young adults with a ≥ 12 RxRisk comorbidity index (RR 3.37; 95%CI 2.83–4.01) and young adults with ≥ 2 long-term health conditions (RR 1.26; 95%CI 1.14–1.40).

Factors associated with general practitioner (GP) and specialist visits among adult cochlear implant users during 2011–2021. CALD: Culturally and linguistically diverse; LHC: Long-term health conditions; Assistance daily livings included self-care, communication or mobility

For older adults, being female (RR 1.12; 95%CI 1.05–1.20), having a CALD background (RR 1.12; 95%CI 1.02–1.24), needing assistance with ADLs (RR 1.32; 95%CI 1.23–1.43), having ≥ 2 implants (RR 1.35; 95%CI 1.21–1.51) and having ≥ 2 long-term health conditions (RR 1.16; 95%CI 1.06–1.27) were associated with an increased number of GP visits. The rate of GP visits increased as the number of comorbidities rose; adults with a ≥ 12 RxRisk comorbidity index had the highest rate ratio (RR 3.06; 95%CI 2.50–3.75).

Younger adults who were female (RR 1.27; 95%CI 1.04–1.54), from a CALD background (RR 1.73; 95%CI 1.32–2.26), were in employment (RR 1.32; 95%CI 1.08–1.60) or had a mental health condition (RR 2.98; 95%CI 2.34–3.78) were more likely to see a medical specialist compared with reference groups. Additionally, the rate of specialist visits rose as the number of comorbidities increased; adults with a ≥ 12 RxRisk comorbidity index had the highest rate ratio (RR 2.95; 95%CI 1.83–4.75).

For older adults, those needing assistance for ADLs (RR 1.33; 95%CI 1.16–1.51), who had a long-term health condition (RR 1.31; 95%CI 1.10–1.56) or who had a mental health condition (RR 2.10; 95%CI 1.72–2.56) were more likely to see a medical specialist than reference groups. Older adults with a ≥ 12 RxRisk comorbidity index had the highest rate of specialist visits compared to older adults without any comorbidity (RR 3.02; 95%CI 1.49–6.11).

Social service use: government benefits

The mean number of government benefits received by younger adults were higher than older adults up to nine years post-implant (see Fig. S1). For older adults, the mean number of benefits slightly increased until fourth year post-implant and gradually decreased for the remaining years of the study time frame.

Characteristics associated with government benefit

Younger adults who were female (RR 1.26; 95%CI 1.07–1.49), had a CALD background (RR 1.50; 95%CI 1.25–1.80), lived in areas of socioeconomic disadvantage (RR 1.29; 95%CI 1.01–1.65), had a low annual income of $1–20,799 (RR 1.61; 95%CI 1.27–2.04) or $20,800 − 51,999 (RR 1.60; 95%CI 1.32–1.96) or had a mental health condition (RR 1.45; 95%CI 1.19–1.76) were more likely to receive government benefits compared to reference groups (Table 3).

Personal taxable income

There were 2,434 (89.7%) younger and 2,054 (57.2%) older adults with information available on their personal income. Around one-third (32.8%) of younger adults had an annual personal income of ≥$78,000 and around one-fifth (21.6%) of older adults had an annual personal income of between $7,800 − 20,799 (see Table S2). The median personal income was consistently higher for younger adults compared with older adults (see Fig. S2).

Discussion

The findings of this study highlight the use of health and social services among adult Australians with a cochlear implant. Both younger and older adults who were female, from a CALD background, requiring assistance with ADLs, had comorbidities, and those with multiple cochlear implants had a higher mean number of GP visits. Younger adults living in socioeconomically disadvantaged areas also showed increased GP visits, while employed adults in both age groups were less likely to seek GP care. Mental health conditions and comorbidities were associated with a higher frequency of specialist visits for both younger and older adults. Younger females, from a CALD background and employed adults had increased specialist visits. Older adults needing assistance with ADLs and those with long-term health conditions also showed high rates of specialist visits.

Younger adults with a CALD background, living in areas of socioeconomic disadvantage, with a low annual personal income, or a mental health condition were more likely to receive government benefits. Younger and older females were more likely to receive government benefits than males. There was a gradual increase in the median personal income for younger adults following cochlear implantation.

A higher use of health services was associated with comorbid conditions, including mental health conditions, among younger and older adults. For older adults, comorbid health is likely due to the cumulative effects of aging, where multiple health issues lead to more frequent healthcare interactions. Previous research indicates comorbidities including mental health conditions are associated with increased health service use [34]. Multimorbidity, particularly when it involves mental health, is increasingly recognised for its complex impact on healthcare demands [35]. Adults with both physical and mental health issues often experience exacerbated health challenges, leading to more frequent interactions with GPs and specialists [36]. Addressing mental health conditions alongside hearing rehabilitation appears crucial for improving individual health outcomes [37].

Younger adults living in socioeconomically disadvantaged areas showed increased GP visits. This is likely influenced by high levels of psychological distress prevalent in young adults [38]. Concerns about financial security and overall well-being [39] may lead to more frequent health consultations. In contrast, employed adults were less likely to seek GP care; however, younger adults were more likely to visit specialists. This is likely due to older adults having multiple conditions that require ongoing management, making GP visits more frequent and necessary. A previous study found that older age [40], and low socioeconomic status [41] were associated with less specialist visits.

Both younger and older adults from CALD backgrounds had a higher likelihood of GP visits compared to non-CALD adults, but only younger adults had a higher likelihood of specialist visits than non-CALD adults. Research shows that individuals from CALD backgrounds often experience language barriers, cultural differences, and difficulties in navigating the healthcare system, which likely lead to delayed care, prompting a higher number of visits for guidance and support [42, 43]. Additionally, current clinical practices face limitations due to cultural barriers, stigma, limited resources, and insufficient assessment tools for CALD cochlear implant users [44, 45]. Individuals experiencing multiple social disadvantages may require more frequent healthcare service to address both physical and mental health needs [46]. On the other hand, evidence shows that many older CALD adults often prefer to rely on family members for care due to these factors [44, 47], which may lead to reluctance in using formal specialist service.

The current study has found that adults needing assistance with ADLs have high rates of health service use. This is likely due to these individuals having complex health needs that require regular medical attention. Research indicates that older adults experiencing challenges in ADLs are more likely to have unmet healthcare needs, which can lead to further health complications and increased reliance on GPs [48]. The intersection of hearing loss and the need for assistance with ADLs may require further investigation.

Younger adults from CALD backgrounds, living in areas of socioeconomic disadvantage, who have a low annual personal income were more likely to receive government benefits. Having a cochlear implantation can enhance communication skills which can lead to increased participation in the workforce [25]. However, individuals from CALD backgrounds often experience financial stress, low socioeconomic status, and unemployment [44]. This is likely due to language barriers, limited local work experience, and challenges in accessing tertiary education [44], which contribute to economic disadvantage that can lead to reliance on government benefits. On the other hand, the increasing trend in median personal income for younger adults following cochlear implantation is encouraging and likely indicates the potential positive economic impact of receiving a cochlear implant on workforce participation. Previous studies have shown a higher personal income level post-cochlear implant among adults [14, 26, 49].

Younger adults with a mental health condition were also more likely to receive government benefits. Previous studies have shown that younger adults with mental health conditions are increasingly receiving government benefits due to the rising prevalence of mental disorders in this age group [50, 51]. Younger adults with mental health conditions may experience higher rates of unemployment [52], leading them to rely more on government benefits for financial support.

A strength of this study is the use of multiple administrative data from the entire country to analyse health and social service use following cochlear implantation. Because the PLIDA Spine and MBS data are directly linked, the authors anticipate a thorough capture of all individuals within the study population who received a cochlear implant in Australia. However, there are limitations associated with the research. First, long-term health conditions were self-reported by adults in the Australian Census, which may be subject to recall bias; also, only common health conditions were reported in the Census. Second, behavioural factors such as smoking status and alcohol dependence were not collected in the Census, which may affect estimates of health and social service use. Third, the removal of cochlear implant due to device complications may lead to higher health service use, but this was not captured by MBS data. Fourth, cochlear implantation services are provided through public and private facilities, with differences in waiting times, post-implantation rehabilitation frequency, and access to multidisciplinary support potentially impacting patient outcomes.

Conclusions

This study examined health and social service utilisation among adults living with hearing loss who received cochlear implants, revealing diverse patterns shaped by age, social context, and healthcare needs. The findings underscore the importance of integrated care models that can accommodate the multifaceted support requirements of this population, particularly for individuals experiencing socioeconomic disadvantage or managing multiple health conditions. These insights contribute to healthcare planning and policy development by identifying factors that influence service engagement and by highlighting opportunities to optimise care for adults using cochlear implants.
