0\leq j\leq k-1\end{subarray}}\phi_{i,j}(y)(s-1)^{i}(\log{\frac{1}{s-1}})^{j}+\varepsilon_{2}(s,y)

was obtained from (4.5), all the
(\log{\frac{1}{s-1}})^{j}
terms come using
\log\zeta(s)=\log\frac{1}{s-1}+\Delta(s)
. On the other hand, all the
\log\log x
terms come from
\log\frac{1}{s-1}
after
s\to 1+\frac{w}{\log x}
. Now notice that in (4.5) the power of
\log\zeta
is
k-i-1
and

(D_{s})^{n}\mathcal{G}_{i}(1,y,-1)=(D_{s})^{n}(D_{z})^{i}g(s,y,z)\mid_{z=-1,s=1}<<_{n,i}(\log y)^{n+1}(\log\log(y+1))^{i}

by (3.2). From here it follows that in (1.2)
J-j+j^{\prime}\leq k-1
. This is consistent with the warning in the beginning of the manuscript about many of the coefficients and the last step in the proof of Theorem 1.1.

A general upper bound

This section provides upper bounds in cases where sifting is faster. I plan to give this topic a much more thorough treatment in a subsequent paper. The familiar reader will know that in such instances, Buchstab iteration provides great insight. Indeed, this has been demonstrated in
[
3
]
for the case
k=1
. In fact, K. Alladi and I are currently detailing the case
k=2
for the full range in
[
1
]
. However, I will give preliminary estimates for the values of
Y_{0}T^{p}\leq Y(x)\leq x^{k}
. These estimates are summarized by the following theorem.

Theorem 5.1
.

Suppose that
y=Y(x)=Y_{0}T^{p}=Y_{0}\exp{{p}\frac{\log x}{\log\log(x+1)}}
where
{p}\in\mathbb{R}
is some fixed power. In this case we get the following relation

m_{k,\omega}(x,Y(x))<<\frac{x(\log\log(x+1))^{k-1}}{\log x}.

More generally, there is a constant
{B}_{k}
that may depend on
k
but no other variables or perimeters, such that for all
1.9\leq y\leq x^{\frac{1}{k}}
we have

{M_{k,\omega}(x,y)}\leq{B}_{k}x\log y(\log\log(x+1))^{k-1}.

Remark
.

To begin with, note that, if we were to substitute
y=Y(x)=Y_{0}\exp{{p}\frac{\log x}{\log\log(x+1)}}
in the quantitative expression of Theorem 1.1, we would get a bound that has at least one less factor of
\log\log(x+1)
.

Because the contributions from (i) and (ii) in the adjusted contours are already controlled by Lemma 4.1 which explicitly includes
y=Y(x)=Y_{0}T^{p}=Y_{0}\exp{{p}\frac{\log x}{\log\log(x+1)}}
, (5.1) will follow from estimating the contribution from (iii), namely, the important contour
\mathcal{C}(x)
.

Proof of (5.1).

As before, subatituting
\log\zeta(s)=\log\frac{1}{s-1}+\Delta(s)
in (5.1) gives

\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k-1!}(D_{z})^{k-1}f(s,y,z)\mid_{z=-1}=\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k-1!N!s\zeta(s)}\sum_{i,r\geq 0}{k-1\choose i,r,k-i-r-1}(\Delta(s))^{k-i-r-1}(\log\frac{1}{s-1})^{r}\mathcal{G}_{i}(s,y,-1)

I will again show that the bounds (5.1) and (5.2) are satisfied by looking at the above finite sum term by term. For a given term we start with

|\frac{1}{N!i!r!k-i-r-1!}\int_{\mathcal{C}(x)}(\Delta(s))^{k-i-r-1}(\log\frac{1}{s-1})^{r}\mathcal{G}_{i}(s,y,-1)\frac{x^{s}}{s\zeta(s)}|.

Utilizing
|\frac{1}{s\zeta(s)}|\leq M_{\zeta}(1-s)
everywhere in the important contour, the above is

\leq x(\frac{2c}{\log T}+\frac{\pi}{2\log x})M_{\zeta}(M_{\Delta})^{k-i-r-1}(\log\log(x+1)+2\pi)^{r}\max_{s\in\mathcal{C}(x)}\{|\mathcal{G}_{i}(s,y,-1)|\}.

If

the bound from Theorem 3.1 gives a constant

that depends only on i and

such that the above is

\leq x(\frac{2c}{\log T}+\frac{\pi}{2\log x})m_{i,N}M_{\zeta}(M_{\Delta})^{k-i-r-1}(\log\log(x+1)+2\pi)^{r}\log{Y(x)}(\log\log(Y(x)+1))^{i}

<<\frac{x(\log\log(x+1))^{k-1}}{\log x}

This establishes (5.1).

∎

All that remains (5.2). What is interesting is that even when if Theorem 3.1 is generalized to a larger range of
y

g(s,y,-1)=-\prod_{p\leq y}\frac{1}{1-\frac{1}{p^{s}}}

Proof of (5.2).

First, note that, just like before, the Leibniz rule gives

\frac{1}{k-1!}(D_{z})^{k-1}f(a,y,z)\mid_{z=1}=\frac{\zeta(a)}{k-1!}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}{k-1\choose i}(\log\zeta(a))^{k-i-1}(D_{z})^{i}g(a,y,z)\mid_{z=1}

as noted in the proof of Lemma 2.1, for real
\sigma>0
, we have

\frac{1}{\sigma-1}\leq\zeta(\sigma)\leq\frac{\sigma}{\sigma-1}

and so, because

, for

and

\log\log x\leq\log\zeta(a)\leq\log\log(x+1)

as
\log
here is the natural logarithm.

Substituting in equation (2.4) of Lemma 2.1 and combining this with (5.4) we get

|\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{a-iT}^{a+iT}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k-1!}(D_{z})^{k-1}f(s,y,z)\mid_{z=-1}\frac{x^{s}}{s}ds-{M_{k,\omega}(x,y)}|\leq\frac{m_{1,k-1}x\log x\log y(2\log\log(x+1))^{k-1}}{T}.

In the above, I will take the non-standard choice

and combine it with the usual
a=1+\frac{1}{\log x}
. This way, the right-hand side of the above already abides by the bounds in the theorem. On the other hand,

|\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{a-iT}^{a+iT}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k-1!}(D_{z})^{k-1}f(s,y,z)\mid_{z=-1}\frac{x^{s}}{s}ds|\leq\frac{x}{\pi}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n=1\\
p_{1}(n)>y\end{subarray}}^{\infty}|\frac{\mu(n){\omega(n)-1\choose k-1}}{n^{a}}|=\frac{x}{\pi}\frac{1}{k-1!}(D_{z})^{k-1}f(a,y,z)\mid_{z=1}

∎

6.
A general discussion on the subradical and radical strict partial orders

Recall that function
f
in the variable
x
has subradical growth if for any
\epsilon>0

f(x)=o(x^{\epsilon})
and more generally, if
f
and
g
are functions of
x
then
f
is subradically dominated by
g
(written as
f<_{\sqrt[\forall]{\,\,}}g
) if for any
\epsilon>0

f(x)=o(g(x)^{\epsilon})
.

Lemma 6.1
.

f
has subradical growth iff
f<<\exp{g(\log{x})}
with
g(x)=o(x)

Proof.

If
f<<\exp{g(\log{x})}
with
g(x)=o(x)
, then for any
\epsilon>0

\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{f(x)}{x^{\epsilon}}=\lim_{x\to\infty}\exp{(-\epsilon\log{x}(1-\frac{g(\log x)}{\epsilon\log x}))=0}

where the last equality used
g(x)=o(x)
.
Conversely, suppose that
f
has subradical growth. WOLOG, we may suppose
that
f
is positive (by replacing
f
with
|f|+1
, for example).

I will show that
f
has an acceptable growth rate directly from the the
(\epsilon-N)
definition. Say we are given
\epsilon>0
. Since
f
is subratical, there exists
N>1
such that
x\geq N
implies

f(x)<x^{\frac{\epsilon}{2}}

but then

0\leq\frac{\log{f(x)}}{\log x}<\frac{\epsilon}{2}

whenever
x\geq N
. Since
e^{x}>x
,
x\geq N\implies e^{x}\geq N
so

0\leq\frac{\log{f(e^{x})}}{x}<\frac{\epsilon}{2}

whenever
x\geq N
. This shows that
g(x)=o(x)
with
g(x)=\log{f(e^{x})}
. The conclusion follows
∎

From the explicit form given for a general subradical function, we directly get the following.

Corollary 6.2
.

Suppose
f
and
g
have subradical growth rates. Then so does
f+g
,
fg
,
f^{p}
and
g^{p}
for any
p>0
. More generally, for any function
f
, the set
\{s:s<_{\sqrt[\forall]{\,\,}}f\}
forms a ring closed under taking positive powers. That is to say,
\forall p>0

g\in\{s:s<_{\sqrt[\forall]{\,\,}}f\}\Leftrightarrow g^{p}\in\{s:s<_{\sqrt[\forall]{\,\,}}f\}.

Proof.

This follows directly from lemma 6.1.
∎

Now, what is interesting about the above ordering is that
\frac{x}{\log x}\not<_{\sqrt[\forall]{\,\,}}x
and, furthermore, these two functions generate the same subradical strict lower set (i.e.
\{f:f<_{\sqrt[\forall]{\,\,}}x\}=\{f:f<_{\sqrt[\forall]{\,\,}}\frac{x}{\log x}\}
). Loosely speaking, the strict partial order
<_{\sqrt[\forall]{\,\,}}
can’t distinguish between
x
and
\frac{x}{\log x}
. However, it is also true that
<_{\sqrt[\forall]{\,\,}}
assigns the same strict lower set to
x
and
x^{10^{10}}
, as
\{f:f<_{\sqrt[\forall]{\,\,}}x\}=\{f:f<_{\sqrt[\forall]{\,\,}}x^{10^{10}}\}
, which certainly feels odd. It was for this reason that I considered the strict partial order
<_{\sqrt[\exists]{\,\,}}
described in the introduction. Recall that for the strict partial order
<_{\sqrt[\exists]{\,\,}}
we say that
f
is radically dominated by
g
, and write
f<_{\sqrt[\exists]{\,\,}}g
, if there exists some
0<\epsilon<1
such that
f=o(g^{1-\epsilon})
. This time, it is clear that
x
and
x^{10^{10}}
do not generate the same strict lower set as
x<_{\sqrt[\exists]{\,\,}}x^{10^{10}}
. On the other hand, we still have
\{f:f<_{\sqrt[\exists]{\,\,}}x\}=\{f:f<_{\sqrt[\exists]{\,\,}}\frac{x}{\log x}\}
. That is to say,
x
and
\frac{x}{\log x}
still generate the same strict lower sets. The following result gives a fascinating link between the two strict partial orders and elaborates on the instance just described.

Theorem 6.3
.

Two nonnegative functions
f
and
g
have the same
<_{\sqrt[\exists]{\,\,}}
-strict lower set iff both
\frac{f}{g}<_{\sqrt[\forall]{\,\,}}f
and
\frac{g}{f}<_{\sqrt[\forall]{\,\,}}g
.

Proof.

Suppose
{\{s:s<_{\sqrt[\exists]{\,\,}}f\}=\{s:s<_{\sqrt[\exists]{\,\,}}g\}}
but
\frac{f}{g}\not<_{\sqrt[\forall]{\,\,}}f
. Then there is
\epsilon>0
such that
f^{\epsilon}<<\frac{f}{g}
, and it follows that

g<<f^{1-\epsilon}\implies g=o(f^{1-\frac{\epsilon}{2}})\implies f^{1-\frac{\epsilon}{2}}\not\in\{s:s<_{\sqrt[\exists]{\,\,}}f\}.

On the other hand, clearly
f^{1-\frac{\epsilon}{2}}<_{\sqrt[\exists]{\,\,}}f\implies f^{1-\frac{\epsilon}{2}}\in\{s:s<_{\sqrt[\exists]{\,\,}}f\}
and thus
{\{s:s<_{\sqrt[\exists]{\,\,}}f\}\not=\{s:s<_{\sqrt[\exists]{\,\,}}g\}}
contradicting our supposition. In exactly the same way, supposing that
{\{s:s<_{\sqrt[\exists]{\,\,}}f\}=\{s:s<_{\sqrt[\exists]{\,\,}}g\}}
and
\frac{g}{f}\not<_{\sqrt[\forall]{\,\,}}g
leads to a contradiction. The conclusion follows.
∎

This last result ties back into the remarks made in the introduction about the significance of the sifting rate
y\leq Y_{0}\exp({p}\frac{\log x}{(\log\log(x+1))^{1+\epsilon}})
.

Theorem 6.4
.

The logarithm of the upper bound function
Y(x)=Y_{0}\exp({p}\frac{\log x}{(\log\log(x+1))^{1+\epsilon}})
of the sifting is a maximal element in the set of logarithms of subradical functions. In other words, for any subradical function
s=s(x)
, either
\log s<_{\sqrt[\exists]{\,\,}}\log Y
or
\log s
and
\log Y
are incomparable.

Proof.

Suppose otherwise, then
\log Y<_{\sqrt[\exists]{\,\,}}\log s\implies\frac{\log x}{(\log\log(x+1))^{1+\epsilon}}<_{\sqrt[\exists]{\,\,}}\log s
. I claim that
\log x<_{\sqrt[\exists]{\,\,}}\log s
. Indeed, there is
0<\epsilon^{\prime}<1
such that

\frac{\log x}{(\log\log(x+1))^{1+\epsilon}}=o((\log s)^{1-\epsilon^{\prime}})\implies(\frac{\log x}{(\log\log(x+1))^{1+\epsilon}})^{{}^{1+\frac{\epsilon^{\prime}}{2}}}=o((\log s)^{1-\frac{\epsilon^{\prime}}{2}}).

On the other hand,
\log x=o((\frac{\log x}{(\log\log(x+1))^{1+\epsilon}})^{{}^{1+\frac{\epsilon^{\prime}}{2}}})
and
\log x<_{\sqrt[\exists]{\,\,}}\log s
follows. This implies that
x<<s
, contradicting the fact that
s
is a subradical function of
x
.
∎
