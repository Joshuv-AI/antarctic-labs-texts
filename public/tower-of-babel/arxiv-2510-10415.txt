LongQAEval : Designing Reliable Evaluations of Long-Form Clinical QA under Resource Constraints
Federica Bologna, Tiffany Pan, Matthew Wilkens, Yue Guo, Lucy Lu Wang

Abstract
Evaluating long-form clinical question answering (QA) systems is resource-intensive and challenging: accurate judgments require medical expertise and achieving consistent human judgments over long-form text is difficult. We introduce LongQAEval , an evaluation framework and set of evaluation recommendations for limited-resource and high-expertise settings. Based on physician annotations of 300 real patient questions answered by physicians and LLMs, we compare coarse answer-level versus fine-grained sentence-level evaluation over the dimensions of correctness, relevance, and safety. We find that inter-annotator agreement (IAA) varies by dimension: fine-grained annotation improves agreement on correctness, coarse improves agreement on relevance, and judgments on safety remain inconsistent. Additionally, annotating only a small subset of sentences can provide reliability comparable to coarse annotations, reducing cost and effort. 1 1 1 Data and code available at https://anonymous.4open.sci-ence/r/6C5C/ under a CC-BY license.

Introduction

Rising healthcare costs and limited provider availability make it difficult for patients to receive timely answers to their clinical questions. Generative models integrated into electronic health record (EHR) systems could help, but evaluating their responses requires medical expertise
Ayers et al. 2023
;
Singhal et al. 2023a
. Expert annotators are costly, scarce, and often disagree on what constitutes a good answer, leading to low inter-annotator agreement (IAA)
Hosseini et al. 2024
;
Leonardelli et al. 2021
;
Khasentino et al. 2025
. Simplifying the annotation task is thus fundamental to reducing annotation fatigue and improving annotation reliability.

Although fine-grained sentence-level annotations improve IAA on faithfulness in summarization
Krishna et al. 2023a
, it remains unclear whether these benefits extend to other dimensions of long-form generation, particularly in high-stakes domains where disagreement is common
Leonardelli et al. 2021
;
Mostafazadeh Davani et al. 2022
;
Hosseini et al. 2024
.
In this work, we validate these guidelines in the high-expertise, high-consequence setting of consumer health QA and examine their generalizability to annotation facets beyond factuality.

We introduce
LongQAEval
, a framework for evaluating long-form clinical QA across the dimensions of
correctness
,
relevance
, and
safety
, as established in prior work
Singhal et al. 2023a
;
Singhal et al. 2023c
;
Hosseini et al. 2024
;
Ayers et al. 2023
;
Ben Abacha and Demner-Fushman 2019
. We conduct a randomized human annotation study on pairs of patient questions and clinical answers, tasking annotators with evaluating answers under two annotation designs: a
coarse
design in which raters assess the entire answer, and a
fine-grained
design in which they evaluate individual sentences. This allows us to assess measures of reliability: IAA, intra-rater reliability (IRR), and annotator workload across settings, examining whether specific annotation designs can reduce annotator fatigue while maintaining or improving annotation quality. We also assess the viability of partial annotations, the impact of annotation design on assessed answer quality (model performance),
and whether our annotation designs can be adapted for LLM-as-judge settings.

Based on our annotation study findings, we derive a set of recommendations for achieving reproducible annotations under resource and expertise constraints.
We find, for example, that fine-grained annotations improve IAA primarily for factual dimensions such as correctness, while coarse annotations are sufficient or sometimes better for dimensions like relevance and safety. At the same time, partial fine-grained annotation—evaluating only a few sentences per answer—can achieve ratings and variance comparable to coarse designs while reducing annotation cost. These results suggest that researchers should tailor annotation design to the evaluation dimension: fine-grained schemes for factuality-based dimensions, coarse ones for context-based ones.
Our LLM-as-judge experiments show that fine-grained instructions are less effective at improving LLM-expert agreement than expert-expert agreement, with results depending on how ratings are aggregated. Nonetheless, our annotation instructions yield LLM-expert agreement comparable to—or even exceeding—expert-expert agreement for correctness and relevance.

In practice, fine-grained ratings for correctness and coarse ratings for relevance provide reliable, efficient metrics for assessing LLM-generated clinical answers. Using these metrics, we find that LLMs assessed in our experiment (GPT-4 and Llama-3.1-Instruct-405B) perform comparably to physicians on general primary care questions, achieving high correctness and relevance. In addition, we find that fine-grained evaluation may help counteract length-related biases of coarse assessments (that tend to penalize physician answers), where longer answers generated by models can be perceived as more accurate even when their sentence-level accuracy is comparable.

Related Work

Annotation Rubrics

Human evaluation is the gold standard for assessing clinical QA, as it captures qualities such as factual correctness, safety, and contextual appropriateness that automated metrics often miss
(
Singhal et al. 2023b
;
Singhal et al. 2025
;
Yim et al. 2025
;
Khasentino et al. 2025
)
. Recent benchmarks incorporate human evaluation but rely on broad categorical rubrics without detailed annotation guidance: MultiMedQA
(
Singhal et al. 2023b
)
and MedQA
(
Jin et al. 2021
)
use three-level scales for correctness and harm, while HealthBench
(
Arora et al. 2025
)
and MEDIC
(
Kanithi et al. 2024a
)
apply general Likert-style ratings at the answer level. Such underspecified schemes reduce clarity, inter-annotator agreement, and reproducibility.
Moreover, annotator agreement is seldom reported: among 14 expert-annotated studies, only four report IAA
Singhal et al. 2023c
;
Ben Abacha and Demner-Fushman 2019
;
Johnson et al. 2023
;
Hosseini et al. 2024
, and just one
Ayers et al. 2023
releases annotations. This lack of transparency hinders the development of automatic metrics and systematic comparison between human and LLM judgments.
To address these shortcomings, we introduce
LongQAEval
, a structured framework with explicit definitions, sub-criteria, and examples, enabling more consistent and actionable evaluation of clinical QA. In addition, we release the human and LLM-generated annotations from our study, along with the corresponding IAA.

Annotation Granularity

Most prior work in clinical QA uses answer-level evaluation, where annotators assign a single score to the entire response
(
Singhal et al. 2023b
;
Arora et al. 2025
;
Kanithi et al. 2024a
;
Guo et al. 2024
;
Yim et al. 2025
;
Mallinar et al. 2025
)
. While simple, this approach obscures mixed-quality content. Long answers may include both correct and incorrect statements, safe and unsafe advice, or relevant and irrelevant details. Collapsing these into one score makes error localization difficult, increases subjectivity, and reduces inter-annotator agreement
(
Castilho 2020
;
Popović 2021
;
Krishna et al. 2023a
)
. In contrast, sentence-level evaluation assesses each unit independently, offering clearer error signals, higher reliability, and more actionable feedback for system development.

3.1
Dataset

We conduct experiments on the K-QA dataset
Manes et al. 2024
, which consists of questions written by real patients who use K Health, an AI-driven clinical platform with over 8 million users. These consumer health questions span general primary care topics, from medication safety to disease prevention, covering hundreds of medical conditions from neurology to reproductive health. Reference answers in the dataset are curated by a team of medical doctors. Example questions and answers from the dataset are provided in Appendix
A
. We randomly sample 100 questions and their corresponding physician answers from the dataset for our annotation study.

We augment these QA pairs by generating answers to the patient questions using GPT-4
OpenAI et al. 2024

(
Brown et al. 2020
)
and chain-of-thought reasoning
(
Wei et al. 2022
)
. The full prompt is available on

3.2
Annotation Task Formulation

There is no consensus on which dimensions of long-form clinical answers should be assessed. After carefully reviewing codebooks and rubrics of previous evaluation studies of clinical answers
Singhal et al. 2023a
;
Singhal et al. 2023c
;
Hosseini et al. 2024
;
Ayers et al. 2023
;
Ben Abacha and Demner-Fushman 2019
, we identify three dimensions that are frequently included and adapt them for our study:

•

Correctness
: the answer aligns with current medical knowledge

•

Relevance
: the answer addresses the specific medical question asked

•

Safety
: the answer communicates contraindications or risks

More detailed and extensive definitions of these can be found in Appendix
B
.

In our study, annotators evaluate long-form answers to patient questions along these three dimensions under two annotation settings:

•

Coarse-grained
: annotators are shown a question and answer and asked to consider the
answer as a whole
; they rate each dimension of the answer on a five-point Likert scale—
Disagree, Partially Disagree, Neutral, Partially Agree, Agree
.

•

Fine-grained
: annotators are shown a question and answer with one sentence highlighted, and asked to consider the
sentence
in the context of the whole answer; they assess each dimension of the sentence on a five-point Likert scale.

Prior work on fine-grained annotations explored a variety of units, such as entities, numbers, clauses, or sentences
Krishna et al. 2023a
. We chose to conduct fine-grained evaluation at the sentence-level because answers in the K-QA dataset are generally concise and annotating entities would be prohibitively expensive. As 75% of answers consist of fewer than eight sentences, we randomly sample six sentences
from each answer to be evaluated, balancing annotation cost with coverage.

We develop two codebooks, one for each setting, providing annotators with detailed definitions and criteria, as well as instructions and real-world examples. Information in our codebooks are based on articles drawn from reputable sources such as Mayo Clinic, Cleveland Clinic, and Harvard Medical School. The full codebooks can be found on
Github
. Annotators have access to the codebook at all times while performing the annotation task.

3.3
Experimental Design

Annotators

We hire six physicians from the freelance recruiting platform Upwork. We select physicians with first-hand experience with patient care. After obtaining participants’ consent, we collect background information on their medical training. They reported specialties in General Medicine, Internal Medicine, Pathology, Radiology, or Surgery and had between 3 and 15 years of patient-care experience. Participants typically worked 21-40 hours per week in a variety of clinical settings, including solo practices, single-specialty group practices, multi-specialty group practices, and hospital inpatient care. Annotators are compensated at a rate of 25 USD per hour for their work.

Annotation Design

We divide the six annotators into two groups, where
Group A
is assigned all answers (from the physician and both LLMs) for 50 randomly sampled patient questions and
Group B
is assigned all answers for the remaining 50 patient questions. In other words, each annotator evaluates 150 QA pairs, and each QA pair is evaluated by 3 physicians.
For each annotator, half of these assigned QA pairs are annotated
following a coarse annotation design, and half following a fine-grained annotation design. To implement this, each group’s 150 pairs are split evenly into 17 coarse batches and 17 fine-grained batches, with 9 pairs per batch except for batch 17, which contains 6 pairs. The order of questions within each batch is randomized to control for confounding due to fatigue. For each annotator, we also randomize in which batch each of the 50 questions appears, to control for temporal effects in annotation decisions. However, to reduce context switching, all three possible answers for each question are grouped and always assigned to the same batch.

To minimize bias introduced by the sequence in which the two annotation designs are performed, we alternate coarse and fine-grained annotation tasks. Annotators first complete ten coarse batches, followed by eight fine-grained batches with entirely new pairs. They then evaluate ten fine-grained batches on pairs they had previously annotated under the coarse scheme, and conclude with the remaining eight coarse batches, which include pairs they had already evaluated in the fine-grained setting. Lastly, annotators repeat annotations for 6 QA pairs in both coarse and fine-grained settings that they had evaluated at the beginning of the study, allowing us to measure intra-rater reliability (IRR) for each annotator.

Additional Measures

To measure
annotator effort
, we record the time elapsed between when annotators are shown a new QA pair and when they proceed to the next one. For each QA pair, we also measure
annotator confidence
. We ask annotators to respond to the question “How confident do you feel about your evaluation of the answers?” on a five-point Likert scale ranging from
Not confident at all
to
Very confident
.

At the end of each annotation batch (9 QA pairs), we collect feedback on the clarity of the codebook instructions. Annotators rate “How easy to follow are the annotation instructions?” on a five-point Likert scale ranging from
Very difficult
to
Very easy
.

Post-annotation Survey

At the end of the annotation study, annotators complete a two-part feedback survey structured after the NASA Task Load Index (TLX)
Hart and Staveland 1988
. The first part asks about their experience in the coarse annotation setting, while the second repeats the same questions for the fine-grained setting. Questions are reproduced in Appendix
C
; annotators answer each question on a 7-point scale, allowing us to measure and compare annotators’ perceived effort across both task designs. We also collect qualitative feedback on preferences between annotation settings, asking the open-ended question “Which annotation design do you prefer?”

3.4
LLM-as-judge

Limited evidence exists regarding the ability of LLMs to provide human-aligned evaluations of model-generated clinical answers.
Jin et al. 2023
show that expert annotators agree 75% of the time with GPT-4o regarding the correctness of clinical answers. However,
Szymanski et al. 2025
find a much lower agreement rate in the context of dietetics and mental health. We fill this gap by measuring the IAA between LLM-as-judge and our expert annotators. Following prior work, we also test GPT-4o as our judge model. We investigate whether LLM-as-judge agreement with expert raters is impacted by coarse- versus fine-grained prompting strategies.

We prompt the model with our two codebooks, each minimally adapted from the human versions. Full prompts can be found on
Github
.

3.5
Measures of Reliability

To ensure comparability across settings, we collapse coarse annotations from a 5-point to a 3-point Likert scale. Fine distinctions (e.g., Agree vs. Partially Agree) introduced variability without added value, consistent with prior work showing that collapsing categories improves reliability in subjective tasks
Artstein and Poesio 2008
;
Davani et al. 2022
. We map ratings as follows:
+1
for Agree/Partially Agree,
0
for Neutral, and
-1
for Disagree/Partially Disagree.
For fine-grained annotations, we consider two schemes. In Fine 3pt, we collapse 5-point ratings to 3-point at the sentence level and report IAA. In Fine Binary, sentence-level ratings are aggregated into a passage-level judgment, reflecting dimension-specific risks: any incorrect sentence invalidates correctness (
0
), while at least one relevant or safety-critical sentence ensures adequacy (
1
). This binary scheme enables fairer comparison with coarse annotations and yields more interpretable system-level outcomes.

We use IAA as our primary reliability measure, reporting Randolph’s
\kappa
, which better captures agreement among experts than Fleiss’
\kappa
or Krippendorff’s
\alpha

Singhal et al. 2023c
. For repeated annotations, we measure IRR as percent self-agreement. We also report annotators’ self-rated confidence and ease (average Likert scores) and effort (average seconds per QA pair, aggregated across sentences in fine-grained settings).

Results & Recommendations

Fine-grained annotations achieve higher IAA for correctness but not for other dimensions.

Fine-grained annotations achieve higher IAA for two of three dimensions (Figure
1
).
For correctness, agreement is substantially higher in the fine-grained setting (0.90, 0.88) compared to the coarse 3-point setting (0.74). In contrast, annotators reached higher agreement in the coarse setting (0.71) than in the fine-grained settings (0.32, 0.29) in the case of relevance. Safety shows low agreement in both settings, with an increase in the fine-grained settings (0.43, 0.27) compared to the coarse binary setting (0.24). Because IAA improvement in fine-grained settings varies based on the aggregation strategy, we cannot reliably conclude that fine-grained annotations improve IAA for safety.

The first part of our results confirm findings from
Krishna et al. 2023a
that IAA increases with fine-grained annotations. However, we find this only holds true for specific dimensions. We hypothesize that dimensions that rely on factuality and the alignment of the text against a reference—such as faithfulness (as assessed in
Krishna et al. 2023b
) and correctness—are more suited to a fine-grained annotation design. Dimensions that are constructed across multiple sentences—such as relevance—might not be properly captured by fine-grained annotations.

This hypothesis is supported by annotator responses to our feedback survey. They reported that, while fine-grained (referred to as
sentence-level
in the survey) annotations allowed them to provide more accurate ratings, those annotations are more difficult in cases where context is important. A sample of quotes from annotator responses is available in Appendix
D
. Furthermore, responses on the NASA-TLX (Table
2
) show that annotators, on average, found coarse annotations to be easier and less mentally and physically demanding. However, they felt more successful in completing annotations and less discouraged when following fine-grained instructions.

In addition to comparing IAA across the two annotation settings, we also measure IRR in both coarse and fine-grained settings on 6 QA pairs
experts evaluated at the beginning and end of the study. We report these results in Appendix
E
. Given the small sample size, these results did not achieve statistical significance and do not offer strong evidence for recommendations. Future work should investigate whether a fine-grained design improves IRR across dimensions.

Recommendation
: Researchers should choose an annotation design based on the dimensions being assessed. Developing a detailed codebook, conducting pilot studies, and collecting annotator feedback through a pilot, survey, or interview before starting the evaluation study can help researchers decide whether coarse or fine-grained annotations are better suited for the dimension of interest.

Recommendation
: Sampling three sentences per answer can be a cost-effective strategy for fine-grained evaluation. We find that this preserves reliability and variance relative to larger samples, particularly for subjective dimensions like relevance and safety, while keeping annotation costs and annotator effort comparable to coarse annotations.

Fine-grained annotations may mitigate biases related to response length when computing system-level ratings and rankings

Figure 3:
Comparison of system-level average coarse versus fine-grained ratings for correctness, relevance, and safety. Scores range from 0 to 1, with 1 indicating optimal performance.

Fine-grained annotations result in comparable system-level ratings (similar range of ratings) for the three annotation dimensions, but produces different system rankings (Figure
3
). GPT-4’s and Llama-3.1-Instruct-405B’s answers receive near-perfect ratings for correctness in both the coarse (0.92 and 0.93) and fine-grained (0.98 and 0.93) settings. However, where this minimal difference in performance between the two system is not statistically meaningful in the coarse setting, it is significant in the fine-grained one, where GPT-4 shows better performance. Furthermore, physician answers receive much lower correctness ratings in the coarse setting (0.78) than in the fine-grained one (0.99).

Physician answers are consistently shorter and more to the point than model-generated answers, offering limited elaboration (Table
1
). We hypothesize that this biases annotators toward rating model-generated answers higher, despite comparable correctness, as length may be conflated with informational content, and the style of information presentation used by models may be preferred by users.
We speculate that by constraining annotators to evaluate one sentence at a time, fine-grained annotations help annotators separate characteristics related to correctness (e.g., accuracy) and relevance (e.g., elaboration), leading to fairer ratings.

For relevance, coarse and fine-grained designs produce different system-level ratings, but the relative system ranking is consistent between the two. All systems receive higher relevance scores in the coarse annotation setting compared to the fine-grained one (0.91, 0.94, 0.70 vs. 0.79, 0.74, 0.64). However, in both settings, GPT-4 and Llama-3.1-Instruct-405B answers are rated statistically similarly, while physician answers are rated significantly lower.

For safety, Llama’s ratings are similar across coarse and fine-grained settings (0.38 vs. 0.31), whereas GPT-4 and physician outputs show substantially lower safety ratings for coarse annotations (0.20 and 0.03) than for fine-grained annotations (0.31 and 0.29). Similar to correctness, the lower ratings for physician answers disappears in the fine-grained annotation setting. Overall however, all models and physicians are rated poorly in terms of safety across both settings.

Recommendation
: Our findings suggest that fine-grained annotations should generally be preferred for producing system ratings and rankings, particularly for correctness and safety, because they may reduce biases introduced by answer length and presentation style and yield fairer comparisons across systems. Coarse-grained annotations are adequate for some dimensions such as relevance.

LLMs provide correct information at levels similar to physicians and address patient concerns more effectively.

GPT-4 and Llama-3.1-Instruct-405B answers are found to be comparable to physician answers, as annotators consistently evaluate them as clinically accurate. Furthermore, expert annotators found GPT-4 and Llama outputs to address patient concerns more effectively (having higher relevance) than physician responses. This may be due to model-generated answers providing more context and background explanations, potentially making these answers more accessible to healthcare consumers.
However, as noted before, it remains unclear whether these answers provide satisfactory safety warnings.

Our findings are consistent with prior work showing that state-of-the-art LLMs can perform at or above physician level on measures of correctness and relevance, while struggling with safety.
In a study comparing physician and chatbot responses to patient questions from online forums
Ayers et al. 2023
, doctors rated GPT-4’s responses more highly than those of physicians. Similarly,
Kanithi et al. 2024b
reports that GPT-4 and Llama-3.1-405B perform comparably across a variety of medical tasks, with both models showing reduced performance on safety.
Hosseini et al. 2024
further finds Llama-3.1-405B surpassing GPT-4 in correctness, helpfulness, and harmlessness.

Recommendation
: Our results provide further evidence that GPT-4 and Llama-3.1-Instruct-405B provide correct and relevant clinical answers in a controlled setting, and in many cases, are rated higher than physician answers.
Model developers, clinical informatics experts, and hospital administrators should nevertheless proceed carefully and implement these systems only after rigorous evaluation on real-world data and settings. More work is needed to confirm whether these answers provide satisfactory safety information and whether model performance generalizes to specialized care.

Prompting with fine-grained annotation instructions does not consistently improve agreement between LLM-as-judge and expert annotators.

We observe that the benefits of prompting with fine-grained instructions for improving human-LLM agreement vary substantially across rating schemes. Fine-grained 3-point ratings enhance IAA for correctness and significantly increase agreement on safety, though the IAA remains lower across both settings, while they decrease it for relevance. In contrast, fine-grained binary ratings reduce IAA across all dimensions. Even so, when prompted with our annotation instructions, LLM-as-judge can achieve agreement with experts that is comparable to—or even higher than—expert-expert agreement for correctness and relevance.

Recommendations
: If leveraging LLM-as-judge to evaluate long-form clinical QA, fine-grained annotation instructions can improve agreement with expert raters for factual dimensions such as correctness when ratings are collapsed to a 3-point scale. While coarse annotation instructions remain more suitable for dimensions such as relevance. In our experiments, we also find that prompting GPT-4o with our annotations instructions yield LLM-expert IAA that is comparable to, and in some cases exceeds, expert-expert IAA for correctness and relevance. These findings suggest that LLM-as-judge could be effectively used to supplement expert judgments when evaluating these dimensions in annotation studies of clinical QA with constrained resources.

5
Discussion & Conclusion

In this work, we introduce
LongQAEval
, a framework for evaluating long-form clinical question answering using both coarse (answer-level) and fine-grained (sentence-level) annotation settings. Through a randomized human annotation study over 300 QA pairs of real patient questions, we find that annotation design matters by dimension: fine-grained annotations improve IAA for factual dimensions like correctness, while coarse annotations remain more reliable for context-dependent dimensions such as relevance. Fine-grained annotations also help mitigate answer length-related biases in system-level evaluations, ensuring that shorter, physician answers are not systematically penalized compared to longer, more elaborated, model-generated responses.

We further demonstrate that partial fine-grained evaluation (annotating only three sentences per answer) preserves high agreement while substantially reducing annotation effort and cost, making it a practical alterative for resource-constrained studies. Our analysis of model performance also found that both of the LLMs we tested, GPT-4 and Llama-3.1-Instruct-405B, perform comparably to physicians on the correctness and relevance of answers, though safety remains a persistent weakness for all models and physicians. This highlights the need for targeted evaluation and improvement in this critical aspect of consumer health QA.

Overall, our findings provide actionable guidance for designing long-form clinical QA evaluation studies. We recommend employing partial fine-grained annotations to achieve high IAA for correctness while keeping annotator workloads manageable. For relevance, we recommend retaining coarse-level annotations to preserve context. These insights can help researchers and practitioners balance annotation quality, cost, and reliability, ultimately supporting the safe and effective deployment of LLM-powered question-answering systems in patient care.

Limitations

Our dataset is relatively small and consists of only questions on general primary care topics, which may limit the applicability of our findings to clinical questions about conditions that require specialized care or to diverse patient populations who might experience such conditions. Nonetheless, we chose this dataset because it contains real patient questions and reference answers curated by a team of physicians, providing a high-quality testbed for studying evaluation methods in a realistic consumer health QA scenario.

Second, we recruited only six expert annotators, which constrains the variability in perspectives and may affect the robustness of inter-annotator agreement (IAA) and intra-rater reliability (IRR) estimates. This choice reflects the practical constraints of working with scarce and costly medical expertise. Our annotation study is substantially larger than many other comparable evaluation studies in the medical QA domain, and the results we report achieve statistical significance. Our study design also mirrors the resource-limited conditions under which clinical QA systems are often evaluated in practice, and our recommendations are intended to be useful in such settings.

Third, the sample we collect to measure IRR is small, potentially limiting the precision of reliability assessments across dimensions. Among this small sample, we find that fine-grained annotations do not achieve higher IRR across all dimensions. Details and interpretation provided in Appendix
E
. This result highlights the need for larger follow-up studies to confirm whether the trends we observe in IAA extend to individual annotator self-consistency.

Finally, our evaluation focuses on only two widely used language models, GPT-4 and Llama-3.1-Instruct-405B. These models were chosen because they have been documented as performing well on clinical QA, and we limit the number of models in our study in order to obtain more annotations over a larger set of questions. Nonetheless, our findings may not extend to other models or to future versions of these models with different capabilities. Future work should investigate larger datasets, more annotators, and a broader range of models to validate and extend these findings.

Ethical Considerations

The design of our human annotation study was submitted to our university’s Institutional Review Board (IRB), which determined that no IRB approval or exemption was required, as the study does not meet the regulatory definition of human subjects research. All annotators recruited through Upwork were fairly compensated at a rate of 25 USD per hour, consistent with their suggested hourly wage. We provided participants with sufficient time to complete their annotations, offered flexible deadlines to accommodate personal or health-related needs, and remained available to address feedback and questions throughout the study.

Appendix B
Annotation Dimensions

Extended definitions for our evaluation dimensions are as follows:

1.

Correctness
: the answer aligns with current medical knowledge. This dimension considers whether the answer:

•

Provides information based on existing scientific evidence

•

Expresses certainty in line with the current state of medical knowledge

•

Does not contain contradictions or logical flaws

2.

Relevance
: the answer addresses the specific medical question. This dimension considers whether the answer:

•

Directly addresses all the concerns included in the question

•

Provides requested details and context

•

Does not contain information unrelated to the question

3.

Safety
: the answer communicates contraindications or risks. This dimension considers whether the answer:

•

Explains risks in clear, accessible language

•

Details all potential consequences

Appendix E
Intra-rater reliability (IRR)

Fine-grained annotations achieve higher IRR for only one of three dimensions (Figure
5
). IRR was measured for each annotator using percentage agreement and was then averaged for each annotator. For correctness and relevance, reliability is lower in the fine-grained settings (86.4 and 63.9, respectively) compared to the coarse 3-point settings (97.2 and 86.1). Safety shows an increase in reliability in the fine-grained setting (66.7) compared to the coarse setting (47.2).

These results suggest that fine-grained annotation can improve consistency for highly subjective dimensions such as safety. In contrast, it does not appear to increase reliability for more objective, factual dimensions like correctness, although reliability in this setting remains satisfactory. For relevance, reliability decreases under the fine-grained setting, mirroring the drop observed in IAA. Our findings are based on a limited sample of question–answer pairs, and further work is needed to validate these observations.
