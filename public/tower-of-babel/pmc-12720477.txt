Exploring the core competencies of operating room nurses in the maritime disaster emergency medical teams based on the Iceberg Model: a qualitative study
Authors: Yuanyuan Peng, Lulu Yan, Chenjiao Guo, Entong Ren, Hao Chen, Weihua Li, Jiang Han
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAs the development and utilization of the ocean have progressed through a new historical period, human-ocean practice activities have become more intense, sea-related public affairs have become increasingly complex, maritime conflicts and natural disasters have occurred frequently, and maritime rescue has become increasingly important. The level of core competence of operating room(OR) nurses in maritime disaster emergency medical teams (MDEMTs) directly affects the efficiency of rescue, but there is a lack of research on the core competence of OR nurses in MDEMTs. This study aims to explore the competencies of OR nurses in MDEMTs based on the Iceberg Model.MethodsBetween November 2024 and January 2025, purposive and snowball sampling techniques were combined to conduct interviews with 12 nurses, 4 surgeons, 1 anesthesiologist, and 3 care managers involved in related roles. All participants were members of MDEMTs who had previously taken part in a maritime exercise or rescue operation. Thematic analysis followed Colaizzi’s seven-step method, based on data collected through semistructured interviews.ResultsEleven generic core competencies for operating room nurses and eleven competencies specific to the MDEMTs were identified through thematic analysis, collectively forming the core competency framework for OR nurses in MDEMTs. Based on the Iceberg Model, these competencies were categorized into four domains: professional knowledge, practical skills, comprehensive competence, and professional and personal traits.ConclusionsOR nurses in MDEMTs are required to demonstrate a wide range of core competencies, and competency-based training should be prioritized to meet the evolving demands of maritime rescue operations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-025-03969-y.Keywords: Maritime rescue, Nurse, Core competency, Emergency medical teams, Qualitative research, Nursing management

Background

In recent years, the development and utilization of marine resources have entered a new phase, with human activities in marine environments becoming increasingly intensive [1, 2]. At the same time, conflicts and natural disasters at sea have occurred more frequently. Therefore, improving maritime rescue capacity is essential to ensuring the safe and sustainable use of the ocean. Marine disasters and accidents not only result in substantial economic losses but also pose serious threats to human life and safety [3, 4]. The uncertainty of the sea area, limited supplies far from the shore, and poor communication equipment all increase the likelihood of rescue delay [5, 6]. The establishment of a surgical force that can rescue people at sea so that the injured can receive a variety of emergency life-saving surgeries the first time can reduce mortality and disability rates and provide opportunities for further evacuation for land treatment. For this reason, many countries have established maritime medical teams tailored to their specific national contexts. These include the U.S. Navy Fleet Surgical Teams (FSTs) [7–9], China Emergency Medical Team (CEMT) [10], and France’s small Surgical Life-saving Module (SLM) [11]; the Singapore Navy also supports the maritime surgical team in conducting damage control surgeries onboard ships [12]. Maritime Disaster Emergency Medical Teams (MDEMTs) are small, rapidly deployable medical units composed of anesthesiologists, surgeons, and surgical nurses. They are capable of performing selected definitive procedures, damage-control surgeries, and controlled resuscitations in complex maritime environments. Currently, most states temporarily assemble such teams from medical institutions based on the specific needs of maritime missions.

The most common injuries at sea are craniocerebral and limb trauma, while drowning, frostbite, and immersion injuries are also observed [13, 14]. Hypertonic seawater exacerbates inflammation and can intensify systemic inflammatory responses [15], thereby complicating the clinical condition. The maritime environment further complicates the execution of surgical procedures. Compared with land rescue, first, the surgical environment is characterized by continuous motion and confined spaces, both of which impair the stability and accuracy of surgical procedures [16].Second, because most members were temporarily transferred to form the team, there was a lack of teamwork and tacit understanding, which hindered effective collaboration. Third, nursing staff are often required to possess multiple competencies and take on multiple roles [9]. Finally, nursing staff must endure a dual challenge: coping with the physical burden of seasickness [17, 18] and the psychological stress of responding to a disaster [19]. These circumstances contribute to the highly specialized and distinctive nature of nursing. Therefore, operating room (OR) nurses in MDEMTs need to possess distinct knowledge, skills, and competencies that differ from those required of conventional land-based operating room nurses.

In 1973, McClelland introduced the concept of competencies, defining them as a combination of knowledge, skills, abilities, motivations, and personal traits that directly influence job performance or important life outcomes. This approach contrasted with the traditional emphasis on academic qualifications and intelligence [20]. On this basis, Spencer proposed the Iceberg Model of Competency in 1993, which divides individual competence into explicit and implicit layers [21]. Above the surface are visible knowledge and skills, superficial characteristics that are relatively easy to train and assess. Below the surface lie self-concept, social roles, motivation, and personality traits, which represent the deeper, intrinsic qualities that are more stable and less observable [21]. The model provides a theoretical framework for understanding core competencies and has been widely applied in competency assessment, talent selection, and performance evaluation [22–25].

In 2003, the International Council of Nurses (ICN) first proposed the core competency framework for general nurses and defined Nursing Core Competency (NCC) as the ability to synthesize and apply knowledge, skills, and judgment in routine nursing practice [26]. This approach has facilitated the dissemination of core competency theory in nursing, and the Iceberg Model has been gradually introduced to stratify nurses’ core competencies across various clinical settings [27, 28]. The core competencies of OR nurses operating in specialized maritime environments consist of both surface-level and deep-level components.

The core competencies of hospital OR nurses have been examined in the existing literature [29, 30], and some studies have also explored the competencies required of nurses serving in mobile surgical teams [31]. However, to date, few studies have specifically addressed the core competencies of OR nurses in MDEMTs. The Iceberg Model not only offers a structured framework for delineating the core competency elements of OR nurses in MDEMTs, but also serves as a theoretical foundation for analyzing qualitative research themes. As an exploratory methodology, qualitative research systematically unpacks the cultural codes and value logics underlying social behaviors by explaining how subjects construct meaning from their experiences [32]. This approach is consistent with the Iceberg Model, which posits that individual behavioral performance is influenced by deeper underlying traits.

Therefore, grounded in the Iceberg Model, this study uses qualitative methods to investigate the core competencies of OR nurses in China’s MDEMTs. Through analyzing the various roles and group dynamics within the team, provide a reference for the selection and training of OR nurses for future MDEMTs.

Methods

Design

This study adopted a qualitative descriptive design and conducted semi-structured interviews between November 2024 and January 2025. This study adhered to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) [33] (Supplemental File 1).

Participants

A combination of purposive and snowball sampling was employed to recruit nurses, physicians, and relevant nursing managers from one hospital in Guangdong Province and two hospitals in Hainan Province, China. As the nursing managers were responsible for selecting and assigning nurses to our MDEMTs, we accordingly included them as interview participants. The inclusion criteria for nurses and physicians were as follows: (1) selected as a member of the MDEMTs; (2) participated in at least one sea rescue mission in the past three years and performed or rehearsed at least one surgery during the mission. Inclusion criteria for relevant nursing managers: (1) Experience with at least one maritime defense and security mission or involvement in the extraction of OR nurses within the past 5 years; (2) Currently holding the position of Director of Nursing; (3) Possessing a professional technical title of Associate Chief Nurse or Chief Nurse. No explicit exclusion criteria were set for this study, mainly due to the limited size of the study population and the fact that all those who met the inclusion criteria were experienced and representative of the task, and were able to effectively support the study objectives.

In China’s healthcare system, professional technical titles differ from academic degrees such as bachelor’s or master’s qualifications. These titles reflect a hierarchical evaluation system for clinical personnel based on experience and competency. The system consists of three levels: junior (e.g., Registered Nurse, Senior Nurse), intermediate (Nurse-in-Charge), and senior (Associate Chief Nurse, Chief Nurse). Participants in this study who held senior titles were considered highly experienced and professionally competent.

Data collection

Based on the research objectives and a review of the relevant literature, and guided by the theoretical framework of the Iceberg Model regarding the structure of core competencies, the team discussed and developed interview outline 1. Interview outline 2, based on outline 1, was revised following in-person validation by experts in qualitative research methodology. Prior to the formal interviews, one nurse and one doctor who met the inclusion criteria were selected for pre-interviews, from which the final interview outlines were developed, as shown in Table 1. Since the interview outlines for the OR nurses in MDEMTs were not revised, their pre-interview data were included in the subsequent analyses. However, after the outlines for the doctors in MDEMTs were revised, their pre-interview data were excluded from the analyses. Before the interviews, the researcher (PYY, a female medical student) first contacted the participants by phone or via WeChat (a popular Chinese social media app). She introduced the purpose and methodology of the study in detail, provided informed consent, promised to anonymize the data and strictly observe confidentiality, and gained the participants’ understanding and support regarding both the researcher and the recordings. This process helped eliminate doubts and establish trust. As per the participants’ preference for a single time, two researchers (PYY and GCJ, both female medical students) conducted the interviews in Mandarin in a quiet, private office of the Department of Nursing at the hospital. During the formal interviews, the process followed the interview outline, with the order of questions adjusted based on the participants’ responses. The participants’ body language and mood changes were also noted. Meanwhile, the researcher took a patient approach, encouraging the participants to fully articulate their thoughts. Follow-up inquiries and clarifications helped address any ambiguity in expression. In total, 10 participants were interviewed via video format (N5-N12, NM1, and NM2), and 10 participants were interviewed in-depth, face-to-face. The duration of each interview ranged from 30 to 60 min, with an average length of 46 min. Each interview was ended when no new themes or relevant information emerged. Regarding the overall sample size, no new themes were identified after analyzing the 19th participant, suggesting that data saturation had been reached. To ensure the completeness of the findings and avoid missing any potentially relevant information, one more participant was recruited. As the 20th participant also yielded no new themes, the recruitment of new participants was stopped [34].

Data analysis

In this study, data collection and analysis were conducted simultaneously. Within 24 h after each interview, two researchers (GCJ and YLL) reviewed the recordings word by word and documented the participants’s nonverbal actions in the interview content. To make sure the coding was consistent, the first author created an initial set of codes using part of the interview data. Other team members then reviewed excerpts from the coded interviews. Any different views were discussed as a group, and the coding system was adjusted based on agreement. This helped the team build a shared understanding and improve the reliability of the analysis. Although Colaizzi’s seven-step method was originally developed for descriptive phenomenology [35], we applied its structured analytical process to systematically code and synthesize themes in this descriptive qualitative study. The steps were as follows: (1) reading the transcripts several times to become familiar with the content; (2) picking out statements that were related to the research question, and coding common ideas; (3) grouping the coded ideas into early themes; (4) describing and explaining each theme clearly; (5) writing a full description of the topic based on these themes; (6) combining similar ideas into final themes; and (7) asking participants to check the final theme structure. The text was stored using Nvivo 20.0 software and analyzed through node coding and the use of memos.

Ethical approval and consent to participate

All research procedures were strictly conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. A total of 20 participants were included in this study, 10 of whom were interviewed face-to-face and signed a written informed consent form prior to the interview. The remaining 10 were interviewed by video, where obtaining signed consent beforehand was difficult due to time and logistical constraints. In these cases, the researcher explained the study’s purpose, recording arrangements, and participants’ rights prior to the interview to ensure full understanding. The interview began only after the respondent explicitly stated, “I agree,” confirming their oral consent. Written informed consent was subsequently obtained from these participants for documentation purposes. The informed consent process was approved by the institutional Ethics Committee. The researcher explained to all participants prior to the interview their right to withdraw at any time, and no one in the study chose to withdraw. The study was approved by the General Hospital of Southern Theatre Command of the PLA’s Scientific Research Ethics Committee (Ethics No. NZLLKZ2024113), and ethical approval was granted on September 29, 2024. Data were collected from November 11, 2024, to January 31, 2025.

Rigor

The rigor of the study was ensured by applying Guba and Lincoln’s criteria: credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transferability [36]. First, all team members proactively engaged in reading literature on qualitative research and methodology. In addition, three researchers (PYY, GCJ, and CH) received systematic instruction through formal qualitative research training courses. The first author, who served both as a medical student rotating in the operating room and as the study interviewer, benefited from professional insight during data collection but also faced a potential risk of bias due to dual-role identity. To minimize potential role bias stemming from the researcher’s dual identity, two researchers (PYY and GCJ) received training in qualitative interviewing techniques prior to data collection. Neutrality and consistency were further enhanced through team discussions and ongoing reflective journaling throughout the process. The corresponding author, who is the director of the hospital’s nursing department and has extensive clinical and managerial experience, contributed to the development of the interview guide, coding alignment, and team discussions. She also provided professional oversight throughout the study, which strengthened the rigor and consistency of the analysis and enhanced the study’s overall credibility.

Finally, to improve the transferability of the findings, we provided detailed descriptions of MDEMT members and associated care managers to help readers assess the findings and consider their applicability to other settings.

Results

Sample size

A total of 20 participants, all of whom were members of MDEMTs, were included in this study: 12 nurses, 4 surgeons, 1 anesthesiologist, and 3 care managers. Of these, 15 were female and 5 were male. The mean age of the participants was 41.85 years (range: 29–55), and the average length of clinical experience was 20.68 years (range: 3–35). Fourteen participants held a bachelor’s degree, while six held a master’s or doctoral degree. The demographic characteristics of the participants are presented in Table 2.

Elements of core competencies for OR nurses in MDEMTs

A total of four themes and 22 subthemes were derived from the Iceberg Model to construct a core competency model for OR nurses in MDEMTs. See Fig. 1 for details.

Iceberg Core Competency Model for OR Nurses in Maritime Disaster Emergency Medical Teams

Theme 1: Specialized knowledge

The surface level of the Iceberg Model is characterized by observable knowledge and skills. Specialized knowledge is fundamental to the execution of OR nurses’ tasks in MDEMTs at sea, and comprehensive knowledge mastery is essential for the success of rescue operations. Most participants indicated that the OR nurses in MDEMTs should possess core operating room competencies, such as knowledge of aseptic techniques and the use of surgical instruments and equipment, as well as an understanding of the maritime environment and disease patterns specific to offshore settings. This approach may also enhance their confidence in performing tasks in offshore settings.

Subtheme 1: Basic knowledge of the OR

N4: As surgical nurses, we need to know all about asepsis because we do not have laminar flow or reliable conditions to work with.

D5: How do you set up the operating room, clean contaminated areas, and manage and care for these areas (knowledge)?

Subtheme 2: Knowledge of the maritime environment

N5: If we had received some training in this area before departure, like about seawater, I wouldn’t have felt so nervous.

N8: Hmmm… (thinking), maritime rescue isn’t just about complex and unpredictable environments…,The maritime climate is typically hot and humid all year round…

Subtheme 3: Knowledge of Sea-related diseases

N2: During sea mission, we also carry special medications for things such as eczema, skin condition, the digestive issues, and so on.

N8: Sometimes the patient’s injuries aren’t limited to one area—there are multiple traumas happening at once. And with hypothermia and seawater immersion on top of that, it gets even more complicated.

Theme 2: Practical skills

Skills are also a component of the surface level in the Iceberg Model. Eighteen participants emphasized that, beyond general operating room proficiency, OR nurses in MDEMTs must possess a diverse set of practical skills adapted to the maritime context.

Subtheme 4: Fundamental OR operational skills

N2: What matters most is that surgical team nurses are highly competent from basic hand hygiene and gowning to setting up the instrument table and handling a wide range of surgical instruments. These skills ensure the surgery at sea gets done efficiently.

D1: Each department has its own specialized instruments, for example, in neurosurgery we have cranial drills, in thoracic surgery there are rib spreaders, and in orthopedics, there are fixation plates. The surgical team nurse needs to know how to use all of them.

Subtheme 5: Emergency nursing skills

N4: Learn how to do sea fluid resuscitation, how to recognize shock, and how to manage it.

N7: You’ve got to be skilled in emergency procedures—like CPR, using a defibrillator, a ventilator, and the usual ones like bone marrow cavity puncture, especially when you’re trying to resuscitate someone who’s hemorrhaging. We’ve only done it a few times on the ship, but its effectiveness is obvious. (The participant gave a thumbs up while speaking)

Subtheme 6: Perioperative psychological nursing skills

N5: Some patients are truly anxious, so you need to be able to talk to them and calm them down.

Subtheme 7: Shipboard nursing operational skills

N11: If I had been on land, I would have done the procedure standing, kneeling, or whatever was needed. But since I was at sea, I had to kneel on one leg and lower my center of gravity to give her the injection. (The participant demonstrated the procedure while speaking)

N12: The wind and waves can really affect how we work—like when doing skin tests, giving IV fluids, or even passing instruments during surgery.

Subtheme 8: Maritime Survival, Self-rescue, mutual aid skills

D1: It’s important to understand how to perform self- and mutual-rescue after falling into the water.

N10: You have to be able to survive at sea. If you say you are in a state of vertigo yourself, how are you going to resuscitate the patient? The first thing is to keep yoursel1f physically stable.

NM1: That includes sea survival. I think swimming is pretty essential at sea, if you fall into the water, you can still save yourself.

Subtheme 9: Damage control skills

N2: We perform surgeries for fractures and hemorrhages, and when the patient’s vital signs are stable, we immediately transfer them.

D1: The surgeries we perform on board are primarily emergency and damage control procedures, including orthopedic amputations. These require short time frames, quick results, and effective bleeding control to save lives.

Subtheme 10: Sterile supply skills

N2: It also includes a supply of sterilized instruments, and we keep our own autoclave on board.

D5: In fact, there needs to be a decontamination nurse who’s specifically in charge of managing and sterilizing the instruments.

Theme 3: Comprehensive competencies

In the iceberg theory, the intermediate layer below sea level includes self-concepts and social roles, which we identified through analysis as comprehensive competencies. These competencies reflect the inner qualities and characteristics of OR nurses in MDEMTs, serving as the cornerstone for delivering quality care.

Subtheme 11: Intra-team communication and coordination competencies

N1: When we go out on a mission, not everyone is from our department or unit. We haven’t worked together or even met before. This is the time to learn to communicate: we should take the initiative to understand how they work at the operating table and how they pass instruments.

N1: It’s still mostly communication within the team during the rescue. Good communication helps us do our job better.

Subtheme 12: Teamwork competencies

N5: Teams have to be collaborative, work closely together, and complement each other.

D2: No one was there to change the IV fluids when they were done dripping, and that kind of tacit understanding between them had to be trained.

Subtheme 13: Emergency handling competencies

N1: And it’s emergency handling, what to do when my partner pulls away.

D4: If the patient suddenly starts bleeding heavily and at the same time I need a sterile pack opened, the nurse has to decide what to handle first and how to help.

Subtheme 14: Critical thinking competencies

D1: I think it’s important to point it out when there are gaps in the procedure. In an emergency, you might forget a step, that’s when the surgical team nurse needs to remind you that something hasn’t been rinsed yet, or what needs to be done.

NM3: It’s about the ability to analyze the pros and cons of things, to think independently, and to make sound judgments.

Subtheme 15: Professional development competencies: Teaching, self-study, and research

N2: And you have to go teach others… so we developed a lot of standardized procedures for portable ventilators, ultrasound machines, and so on — so that next time, someone unfamiliar with them can just follow the process.

N4: We look at day-to-day work with a research mindset. When the signal is poor at sea, we write reflective diary entries and bring them back to shore to give feedback and work on the problems we’ve identified.

Subtheme 16: Interdepartmental communication and coordination competencies (Movement control, ship management)

N4: It also emphasizes the ability to communicate and coordinate with the ship’s political commissar, medical officer, hygienist, and others.

N10: You have to know how to talk to the leaders of the casualty’s unit and get the unit head’s consent. At the same time, doing surgery means coordinating with a lot of departments, like the ship’s captain, sea and air transfer, transport, so the rescue can actually happen.

Subtheme 17: Professional development competencies: Frontier technology assistance

N8: We have to master new equipment and information technology tools on our own, like portable ultrasound and devices for telemedicine consultations.

N9: We need to focus on collecting and organizing data while at sea.

Theme 4: Professional and personal traits

The Iceberg Model theorizes that motivation and personal traits, those below the surface, are what drive individuals to perform tasks. Professionalism and personal characteristics are integral to the core competencies of OR nurses in MDEMTs.

Subtheme 18: Shendu1 (Ethical self-regulation)

N5: It’s about having a spirit of Shendu, no matter what the setting is, you need to strictly follow the procedures and take responsibility for the quality control in nursing care. It’s different from being in the hospital, where there’s always someone watching or checking on you.

Subtheme 19: Responsibility

N1: No matter what your reasons are, you were given the order, so you should go on this mission.

N3: This role has a high level of responsibility.

Subtheme 20: Discipline

NM1: Like, since most of us are Party members, we just have to follow the orders. No saying no.

Subtheme 21: Composure

N3: To be strong enough inside and resilient to stress……

N6: Always deal with any injury calmly, no matter what it is.

Subtheme 22: Sense of mission

N12: Having accepted this mission, we have to make sure that we complete it successfully and live up to the trust placed in us by the Party and the people.

NM2: When we select someone for such a mission, we usually know their personality and work habits, and we also talk to them beforehand to understand what they think on a deeper level. For example, one of our nurses was very eager to go on a mission and did a lot of preparation for it.

Discussion

This study used semistructured interviews to explore the levels and content of core competencies required of OR nurses in MDEMTs, based on the Iceberg Model. The findings indicate that, in addition to the general competencies of operating room nurses in fixed hospitals, OR nurses must also possess specialized competencies suited to the maritime environment. This is largely because the competencies of OR nurses in MDEMTs reflect the multifaceted nature of their role. First, as OR nurses, they must possess the core competencies common to operating room nurses in land-based medical facilities. Second, because their tasks are carried out in a complex and dynamic maritime environment, they also require additional competencies to address its specific challenges, ensuring both surgical proficiency and environmental adaptability.

Specialized knowledge

In the Iceberg Model, specialized knowledge, considered part of explicit competence, forms the foundation of an individual’s overall competence. In this study, specialized knowledge included basic knowledge of OR, knowledge of the maritime environment, and knowledge of sea-related diseases. The majority of participants indicated that basic operating room knowledge is the most fundamental and essential requirement for OR nurses in MDEMTs, forming the foundation of their core competency structure. This finding is not only consistent with the core curriculum of the European Operating Room Nurses Association (EORNA) for perioperative nurses [37], but also demonstrates that the offshore environment does not alter the fundamental requirements for MDEMTs nurses in terms of operative practices and core knowledge. At the same time, several participants further emphasized that knowledge of the maritime environment and sea-related diseases is essential for OR nurses in MDEMTs to adapt to the specific conditions of rescue missions. Because maritime climate conditions, such as temperature and humidity, may affect the progression of patients’ injuries [38], OR nurses in MDEMTs are required to understand the associated pathophysiological changes in order to improve their responsiveness and clinical decision-making in such environments. The knowledge-related competencies identified in this study are observable, trainable, and critical to performance in specific operational contexts. This unique knowledge enriches the core competencies of OR nurses in MDEMTs. The governing body should consider embedding this knowledge into the competency standards for OR nurses in MDEMTs and support the development of practical training content, for instance, addressing acute illness patterns in hot and humid conditions, and the clinical impact of seawater on injury treatment. These elements should also be reflected in selection and entry criteria to ensure that incoming personnel are well-prepared for the demands of maritime environments and can contribute effectively to emergency medical systems at sea.

Practical skills

In the Iceberg Model, specialized knowledge and practical skills together constitute the surface-level explicit competencies. In this study, we found that OR nurses in MDEMTs needed to acquire competencies in shipboard nursing operations, maritime survival, self-rescue, and mutual aid, damage control, and sterilization supply management, in addition to the general core competencies expected of operating room nurses. In particular, environmental instability caused by ship swaying and low-frequency vibration significantly affects operational precision [16], and nurses need to maintain physical stability in dynamic environments [39] to ensure aseptic technique and critical operations. This highlights the need for OR nurses in MDEMTs to acquire competencies in shipboard nursing operations. Maritime survival, self-rescue, and mutual aid skills, including swimming and resistance to seasickness, are essential to ensuring the personal safety and sustained working capacity of OR nurses in MDEMTs. Damage control skills constitute an essential component of the core competencies required of OR nurses in MDEMTs. Predeployment training for European military surgical teams includes damage control resuscitation and surgery in specialized operational environments in austere environments [40]. Maritime disasters and accidents are also significant causes of limb trauma [13, 14]. OR nurses serving in MDEMTs often face harsh environments and limited medical supplies. Therefore, they must be proficient in damage control techniques to stop bleeding, stabilize vital signs, and create the conditions necessary for evacuation and transport [41, 42]. In addition, in the context of personnel streamlining and multi-role responsibilities, OR nurses in MDEMTs are expected to demonstrate multitasking capabilities. They must be able to shift flexibly between roles such as scrub nurse, circulating nurse, and sterile supply staff, enabling them to perform their duties with a high level of adaptability. However, most current nurse selection mechanisms do not include assessments of the aforementioned skills and competencies, and there is a lack of corresponding training for environmental adaptation and related competency development. Building on this foundation, the governing body is also encouraged to integrate the four categories of key skills into a core practical competency framework for OR nurses in MDEMTs, and to establish clear training standards and evaluation criteria. Particular attention should be given during selection to abilities such as nursing operations in dynamic environments and resistance to motion sickness, which may be used as prerequisites for entry. This approach ensures that recruitment is aligned with the actual demands of maritime operations and reflects both task-specific and scenario-based assessment principles.

Comprehensive competencies

In the Iceberg Model, comprehensive competencies correspond to mid-level traits such as social roles and self-concept. These lie beneath the surface of the iceberg but do not extend to the deepest layers of individual motivation and traits. Such competencies are not directly observable, but they manifest indirectly through specific behaviors and reflect an individual’s perception of their professional identity, role, and relationship with the organization. A lack of continuity and stability in team familiarity can lead to communication barriers that directly affect surgical patient safety and diminish both individual and team performance [43, 44]. Moreover, poor coordination in disaster response can hinder the implementation of effective rescue efforts [45]. In this study, participants repeatedly emphasized the importance of communication, coordination, and teamwork skills. This is largely because, during maritime rescue missions, OR nurses in MDEMTs must function within multidisciplinary and cross-unit teams. As coordinators, they play a critical role in ensuring patient safety and mission success by establishing common goals and facilitating effective communication across departments [46]. Moreover, critical thinking, which reflects initiative in professional practice, was mentioned by only four participants. This finding is consistent with previous studies in Turkey, which reported that critical thinking skills ranked lowest among nurses’ core competencies related to disaster preparedness [47]. The traditional nursing role has been largely confined to carrying out physicians’ orders [48]. In the hierarchical environment of the operating room, power is concentrated in the hands of surgeons, which limits the autonomy and initiative of perioperative nurses [49]. However, in modern healthcare settings, nurses are no longer passive implementers of medical orders but active professionals who exercise judgment, think independently, and participate in decision-making. These responsibilities significantly enhance nurses’ engagement and self-efficacy [50]. Therefore, it is recommended that critical thinking development be strengthened in training programs to encourage more active participation by nurses in healthcare decision-making and to enhance their professional role identity [51]. Finally, the interview data revealed that OR nurses in MDEMTs also exhibited a self-concept of continuous professional development. Despite limited access to regular learning resources due to oceanic geography and satellite communication constraints [52], the study found that nurses maintained their professional competence through intensive pre-departure training and active peer learning during missions. This intrinsic drive for professional growth reflects the maturation of their self-concept level and underpins the development of deeper-level competencies in the Iceberg Model.

Communication, coordination, teamwork, critical thinking, and professional development, among other competencies, form a comprehensive hierarchy of capabilities for OR nurses in MDEMTs. These competencies are not as easily assessed as technical knowledge and skills, yet they have a profound impact on stability, adaptability, and career continuity in high-pressure environments and must be reinforced through training and selection mechanisms. On one hand, the training system should place greater emphasis on communication, coordination, and critical thinking modules, enhancing nurses’ situational judgment and responsiveness through case-based rehearsals and cross-departmental simulation exercises. On the other hand, behavioral observation tools, covering communication skills, teamwork adaptability, and problem-solving ability, should be developed and systematically applied in both nurse selection and routine evaluation as a key supplement to competency assessment. Beyond individual-level skills, cross-sector joint training should be introduced to support coordination across medical, command, and transport systems, enabling regular collaborative drills and improving system-wide alignment.

Professional and personal traits

In the Iceberg Model, motivation and traits constitute the deeper layers of the competency structure. They serve as the fundamental drivers of individual behavioral performance. Research has shown that a strong sense of professional identity is positively correlated with individuals’ engagement with their work, and that sound professional values can effectively guide career development [53]. This study found that the deeper competencies required by OR nurses in MDEMTs consist, on the one hand, of motivational elements that reflect an internal drive for career development, including a prudent and independent mindset, a strong sense of responsibility, and self-discipline. On the other hand, these competencies include personality traits driven by internal motivation, such as composure and a strong sense of mission. The concept of Shendu (慎独) embodies nurses’ awareness of upholding professional standards even in the absence of external supervision. It serves as a fundamental basis for ensuring both ethical conduct and patient safety1. Although Shendu is culturally rooted in Chinese Confucian philosophy, it closely aligns with global nursing values that emphasize ethical conduct and professional responsibility, particularly in high-pressure or emergency care settings in which ethical nursing care must be provided [54, 55]. Responsibility is reflected in nurses’ active commitment to both patient care and team obligations. It serves as an internal driving force that motivates them to rise to the occasion and perform their duties with consistency and professionalism [56]. Additionally, previous studies have indicated that discipline plays a vital role in maintaining team safety and achieving mission objectives [31]. This finding is also reflected in the present study, where some nurses expressed concerns about risk after being selected by the nursing department, while those with a strong sense of commitment and discipline accepted the assignment without objection and ultimately became the most reliable staffing resource for high-risk medical tasks. The operating room is inherently a complex, dynamic, and high-pressure environment [57, 58]. During emergency sea rescues in particular, OR nurses in MDEMTs are exposed to heightened physical and psychological stress, as well as interpersonal conflict. Only those with positive, optimistic, and composed personal traits, along with strong physical and mental adaptability, are able to perform effectively in such demanding conditions [59]. The sense of mission originates from OR nurses’ deep recognition of their professional value and social responsibility in MDEMTs. This is reflected in their active engagement in rescue operations, even under adverse sea conditions. Therefore, nursing administrators should systematically explore the deeper competencies of OR nurses in MDEMTs from both motivational and trait dimensions when selecting and developing them. On the one hand, standardized job competency models and simulation-based training scenarios can be used to observe individuals’ behavioral responses and normative awareness under pressure. On the other hand, value-oriented guidance and long-term career development support help internalize Shendu, responsibility, and discipline as integral components of professional identity, thereby fostering a competency structure for OR nurses in MDEMTs that aligns closely with the demands of the role. As the deepest core components of the Iceberg Model, professional and personality traits play a critical role in sustaining OR nurses’ performance in MDEMTs under extreme conditions and over extended durations. Although vital, these traits are not easily assessed through objective indicators and should therefore be prioritized in both selection criteria and training frameworks. Therefore, managers should conduct ongoing observation, and prioritize identifying, cultivating, and appropriately applying these traits in clinical practice and team development.

Limitations

This study is subject to several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, there may be deviations in applying the theory of the Iceberg Model, which we attempted to minimize through generalization and refinement. Second, as a qualitative study, the design inherently limits the generalizability of the results, but the findings still offer transferable value for similar offshore medical teams. Third, the interviewer’s status as a medical postgraduate may have created a power imbalance with senior participants, potentially affecting interview quality and data depth. Fourth, all participants were from Guangdong and Hainan provinces in southern China, which may limit the regional applicability and cultural transferability of the findings. In addition, this study combined data from both real sea rescue missions and simulated rehearsal scenarios. Variations in environmental stress, resource availability, and psychological states across these settings may have influenced participants’ understanding of core competencies. Finally, all interviews and analyses were conducted in Chinese, and there is a potential risk of translation inaccuracies during the process of reporting in English. Future studies may consider expanding the geographical scope of the sample, incorporating quantitative methods for validation, and further examining the applicability of the Iceberg Model across diverse cultural and operational contexts, in order to enhance the robustness and generalizability of the findings.

Conclusion

Using the competency iceberg model as a theoretical framework and taking into account the practical demands of China’s far-sea operations, this study conducted an initial qualitative exploration of the core competency characteristics of OR nurses in MDEMTs. The adoption of the Iceberg Model strengthens the theoretical foundation of this study, while the study’s findings expand the practical application of the model and contribute to the development of core competencies among OR nurses in MDEMTs. OR nurses in MDEMTs, as frontline personnel in maritime rescue, are tailored to their unique core competencies to develop targeted training and are preparing for rescue at sea.
