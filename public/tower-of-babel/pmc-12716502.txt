Measuring and monitoring safety of older adults living in retirement homes: a scoping review protocol
Authors: Chantal Backman, Franciely Daiana Engel, Aurelie Tonjock Kolle, Rosie Papp, Ashley Lam, Laura Freeman, Wenshan Li, Evan Sterling, Stephen G Fung, Samir K Sinha, Derek R Manis, Tzu-Fei Wang, Krystal Kehoe MacLeod, Catherine Hecimovich, Colleen Webber, Courtney Maskerine, Ania Syrowatka, Carol Fancott, Daniel Kobewka

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe global population of older adults has grown at an unprecedented rate, and projections indicate that the number of older adults will continue to increase considerably in the coming decades. The clinical complexity of older adults living in retirement homes, also known as assisted living settings, is also increasing, and the regulations to ensure quality and safety standards in retirement homes are highly variable. The purpose of this scoping review is to map and summarise the methods used to monitor and measure the safety of older adults living in retirement homes, providing an overview of existing approaches and areas requiring further investigation.MethodsThis scoping review will follow the five stages of the Arksey and O’Malley scoping review process. We will report this review using the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews. A comprehensive search of four electronic databases (MEDLINE (Ovid), EMBASE (Ovid), CINAHL (EBSCO) and Web of Science Core Collection) will be performed, and reference lists of included articles will be searched. We will conduct a two-step screening process, data extraction and analysis of the included studies. We will include all primary study designs that report on safety measurement and monitoring of any type of harms. Relevant grey literature will also be identified. We will exclude studies that only report results from facilities providing 24-hour skilled nursing care or specialised care services, and those not published in English or French. This protocol is registered on the Open Science Framework (osf.io/8rheq).Ethics and disseminationNo ethical approval is needed for the review, and we plan to present the results at research conferences and in a peer-reviewed article. Our findings may inform future research studies that seek to support and improve safety practices in retirement homes.

Background

With the global older adult population projected to reach 2.1 billion by 2050,1 and the number of Canadians aged 65+ expected to reach 14 million by 2070 under most growth scenarios,2 enabling ageing in place is a priority for most governments and healthcare systems.3 Ageing in place requires maintaining social connections, mental health, independence and management of long-term conditions.35 When these needs can no longer be met at home, the concept of ageing in the right place means working with older adults to select the most appropriate living arrangement for them considering their culture, identity, preferences and care needs.6 Retirement homes are congregate settings defined as residences that provide services to older adults who are predominantly independent and require limited support with activities of daily living (ADLs).7 Other commonly used terms include assisted living, residential care setting, independent senior living, supportive housing, continuing care retirement community, home care villages, among others. These terms are often used interchangeably and vary by region. For this review, we refer to all these settings collectively as retirement homes. These retirement homes are an option that promotes ageing in place, promotes social interaction and autonomy, reduces isolation and depressive symptoms8 and contributes to improvements in mental health and functional independence.9 The flexible nature of retirement homes allows residents to maximise the autonomy over their care, unlike skilled nursing facilities, which are heavily regulated and prescriptive. Although retirement homes can support the care needs and quality of life for older adults, some residents may experience safety risks.8

The WHO10 defines patient safety as ‘the absence of preventable harm to a patient and reduction of risk of unnecessary harm associated with healthcare to an acceptable minimum’, focusing mainly on the absence of physical harms. According to Healthcare Excellence Canada’s (HEC)11 focus on ‘Rethinking Patient Safety’, they recognise an expanded view of harm including physical (eg, falls, infections, physical violence, pressure ulcers, malnutrition, dehydration, incontinence, unplanned weight change, catheterisation, decline in ADLs, pain, mortality); social (eg, loss of social relationships, such as friends); emotional and psychological (eg, loss of autonomy, independence and privacy, sense of not belonging, loss of an individual’s sense of self, loss of connectedness, lack of comfort and security, emotional distress, lack of privacy, respect, autonomy and dignity) and financial (eg, scams, fraud, financial abuse) harms.8 10 11 At the same time, HEC recognises that the absence of harm does not mean that care is safe and encourages a more proactive stance in the creation and promotion of safety.11

Although long-term care (LTC) and retirement homes are often addressed under a common umbrella of congregate residential care settings, there are key differences in operational expectations (eg, staffing ratios) and resident characteristics (eg, personal care requirements) within and between different jurisdictions and types of homes. Within retirement homes, there is wide variability in services provided and how they are inspected and regulated.12 These aspects can influence the healthcare utilisation patterns of retirement home residents, as studies by Manis et al have found that despite similar levels of frailty and comorbidity, retirement home residents have a 2.8 times higher rate of acute care transfers when compared with LTC homes13 (although this may be due to higher rates of advance care planning among LTC residents14), and 3.3 times higher rate of emergency department visits, 6.2 times higher rate of hospital admissions and 25.7 times higher rate of alternative levels of care days relative to community-dwelling older adults.14 These findings suggest that retirement home residents may have higher acute care needs that are not being fully met by the scope of services available in those settings13 and that primary and post-acute on-site medical care in retirement homes is likely needed to reduce the increasing healthcare utilisation rates among this population.14

There are currently no federal reporting standards or standardised assessments for retirement homes and their residents in Canada, as each province and/or territory licences and/or regulates these facilities differently,12 15 which creates barriers to identifying best practices in this context.16 This could be related to the lack of standardised reporting and assessment tools, which limits the understanding of older adults’ needs, outcomes and the implementation of effective regulation.13 Therefore, measuring and monitoring safety in retirement homes is essential for understanding risks, preventing adverse events and improving clinical processes, especially given that the clinical complexity of retirement home residents has progressively increased.17 18 The purpose of this scoping review is to map and summarise the methods used to monitor and measure the safety of older adults living in retirement homes, providing an overview of existing approaches and areas requiring further investigation.

Methods

Study design and guiding framework

This review will follow the five stages of the Arksey and O’Malley19 20 process and the Joanna Briggs Institute19 20 methodology for scoping reviews: (1) define the research question, (2) develop the search strategy, (3) proceed with the screening, (4) conduct data extraction and (5) conduct the data synthesis. We will report this review using the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses for Scoping Reviews.21 This protocol is registered on the Open Science Framework (osf.io/8rheq).

We will use the Measurement and Monitoring of Safety Framework22 23 as our guiding approach for the analysis. This framework has been used in previous reviews of patient safety to categorise the study findings.2426 It provides a structured assessment of safety that focuses on more than just the absence of harm, combining both retrospective and prospective indicators to identify risks and opportunities for improvement.23 The Measurement and Monitoring of Safety Framework consists of five dimensions: (1) past harm, which includes both psychological and physical measures; (2) reliability, that is, measures of behaviour and systems; (3) sensitivity to operations, that is, the information and capacity to monitor safety on an hourly or daily basis; (4) anticipation and preparedness, that is, the ability to anticipate, and be prepared for, problems and (5) integration and learning, that is, the ability to respond to, and improve from, safety information.22 23 Each domain includes prompting questions that guide users of the framework to comprehensively address safety issues.

Research question

The research question guiding this scoping review is: ‘How is the safety of older adults living in retirement homes measured and monitored?’. Specific objectives are to: (1) identify and categorise safety measurement and monitoring methods being used in retirement homes and (2) identify gaps in safety monitoring and highlight areas for the development of evidence-based solutions and future research.

Search strategy

A comprehensive search of the academic literature will be completed. Four databases, MEDLINE (Ovid), EMBASE (Ovid), CINAHL (EBSCO) and Web of Science Core Collection, will be searched from inception to 14 July 2025, by the librarian specialist (ES), using search terms that focus on the following three major concepts: retirement homes, safety and metrics/indicators. The search strategy will be informed by published articles and previously conducted systematic searches811 2730 and peer-reviewed by another librarian in accordance with the Peer Review of Electronic Search Strategies method.31 See online supplemental appendix 1 for the MEDLINE (Ovid) search details. Search results will be exported to Covidence (Melbourne, Australia), and duplicate records will be removed.

In addition to the comprehensive search of the published literature, we will also conduct a grey literature search using Google. We will screen the first 100 websites from a pre-defined search strategy of keywords that will include the key concepts related to the context (eg, ‘retirement home’, ‘assisted living’, ‘senior living’), safety (eg, ‘safety’, ‘harm’, ‘adverse event’, ‘near miss’, ‘mortality’) and methods (eg, ‘metric’, ‘tool’, ‘instrument’, ‘checklist’). These terms may be refined based on the terminology encountered during the academic literature review process.

Eligibility criteria and screening

The records found will then be assessed for eligibility based on the population, concept, context format.

Population: We will focus on the older adult population living in retirement homes.

Concept: We will include any methods to measure and monitor safety, such as consistency of care delivery (eg, audits, risk assessments), real-time monitoring (eg, briefings and debriefings, routine reviews), proactive tools (eg, safety registers, safety culture assessments, checklists) and responsive actions based on past experiences (eg, population level analysis, incident analysis).23

Context: We will focus on retirement home settings that primarily provide housing, meals and assistance with ADLs. Other services that may be offered include housekeeping, recreational activities and optional on-site healthcare. Retirement homes may also address some safety concerns, such as providing an environment adapted for people with reduced mobility and 24-hour surveillance through video monitoring.

We will include all primary study designs (eg, randomised controlled trials, non-randomised experimental studies, cohort studies, cross-sectional studies, qualitative studies). For the grey literature, we will include toolkits, practice guidelines, government reports, conference proceedings, dissertations, white papers or technical reports.

We will exclude studies focusing exclusively on settings providing 24-hour skilled nursing care or specialised care services, such as nursing homes, long-term care homes or group homes, as well as studies that did not address methods to measurement or monitoring safety. We will also exclude articles not published in English or French.

Title/abstract screening and, subsequently, full-text review will be carried out independently by two reviewers in Covidence using the inclusion and exclusion criteria described above. Full texts will be uploaded to Covidence for full-text screening. Any conflicts at either screening stage will be resolved by a third independent reviewer. Any articles that cannot be obtained will be excluded. We will also examine the reference lists of included studies identified during the screening process to identify additional studies that meet the inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Data extraction

Two reviewers will independently perform data extraction for the eligible studies. A structured spreadsheet will be created and piloted. We will capture information such as author names, publication year, country, research purpose, theoretical framework (if applicable), study design, population under study, study limitations, type of tool, description of the tool or method to measure or monitor safety, study results or outcomes. The extraction form will be revised as needed based on the results of the pilot testing.

Data synthesis

The data extracted from the included studies will be synthesised descriptively and categorised according to the Measurement and Monitoring of Safety Framework dimensions.22 23 Each included tool or method will be assigned to the dimension that best reflects its purpose and application, and a method can be classified in more than one dimension. Furthermore, we will adapt the framework to reflect the retirement home context. This adaptation will be based on the range of tools, indicators and processes identified in the included studies. The goal of the adapted framework is to suggest the types of instruments that could be used to measure and monitor different aspects of safety in retirement homes, reflecting both the existing evidence and the specific characteristics of this setting.

Ethics and dissemination

No ethical approval is needed for this review, and we plan to present the results of this research at a conference and in a peer-reviewed journal. This study aims to provide new directions for future research to validate and improve safety protocols in retirement homes.

Conclusion

The findings of this review will provide a comprehensive overview on how the safety of residents in retirement homes is measured and monitored. By adopting an expanded concept of safety, the study will reinforce the importance of a broad approach to promoting safety. Also, by identifying and categorising methods to monitor and measure safety, this study will incorporate the context of retirement homes into the Measurement and Monitoring of Safety Framework, contributing to the advancement of knowledge in this area. The findings will inform future studies on safety monitoring approaches as well as assist policymakers in evaluating the existing safety policies and designing evidence-based regulatory or quality improvement initiatives for retirement homes.
