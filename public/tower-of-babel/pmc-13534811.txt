Effect of Tertiary Lymphoid Structures on Immune Cell Infiltration in the Tumor Microenvironment and Prognosis in Lung Adenocarcinoma
Authors: Sihong Le, Weidan Fang, Yitao Le, Ling Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTertiary lymphoid structures (TLSs) modulate immune responses in various solid tumors, but their comprehensive role in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) remains unclear. In this study, we analyzed RNA‐seq data from 539 LUAD patients in The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and microarray data from 223 samples from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, GSE13213, and GSE37745). TLS signatures were evaluated via unsupervised consensus clustering based on 12 chemokine transcriptome signatures. The relationships between TLS and clinical characteristics, tumor microenvironment (TME) cell infiltration, and prognosis were assessed using ESTIMATE and CIBERSORT. A prognostic model was established using LASSO regression and validated with external datasets. Additionally, H&E and IHC analyses were performed to explore associations between intratumoral TLS density, immune‐related molecular expression, and patient prognosis in LUAD. Consensus clustering of the TCGA cohort revealed two distinct LUAD patient clusters according to TLS abundance. Cluster 1 exhibited greater immune cell infiltration, more favorable prognosis, and increased expression of immune checkpoint molecules. We developed a prognostic model comprising eight survival‐associated genes that act as independent prognostic factors for patient survival. H&E/IHC analyses revealed that TLS density—regardless of pathological stage—was associated with better prognosis; higher intratumoral TLS density/proportion was also related to more favorable outcomes. IHC confirmed that survival‐associated genes (CD5, HLA‐DMB, and P2RY13) are independent prognostic indicators in LUAD. Our study demonstrated the close relationship between TLS signatures and an active immune microenvironment, highlighting their potential as independent prognostic indicators in LUAD.Keywords: immune infiltration, lung adenocarcinoma, prognosis, tertiary lymphoid structures, tumor microenvironment

Introduction

Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) is the most common subtype of non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and is a leading cause of cancer‐related death worldwide, accounting for approximately 40% of lung cancer cases, with the incidence continuing to rise [1]. LUAD arises from small airway epithelial cells and type II alveolar cells, which are essential for maintaining pulmonary homeostasis by secreting mucus and surfactants [2, 3]. Although targeted therapies for oncogenic drivers such as EGFR and ALK are available, only a minority of patients achieve durable responses, limiting improvements in overall survival [4]. The advent of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) has transformed treatment strategies and improved survival for some LUAD patients, but both primary and acquired resistance are common, partly due to tumor‐driven immune tolerance and an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment [5, 6]. Thus, identifying reliable biomarkers and elucidating molecular predictors of immunotherapy efficacy are urgently needed [7].

Tertiary lymphoid structures (TLSs) are ectopic lymphoid aggregates formed at chronic inflammation sites, including tumors [8], and their presence is associated with antitumor immune activity and favorable prognosis [9, 10]. Structurally, TLSs resemble secondary lymphoid organs (SLOs) and are composed mainly of T cells, B cells, dendritic cells (DCs), macrophages, and neutrophils [8], as well as high endothelial venules and lymphatics that guide immune cell migration into the tumor microenvironment (TME) [11]. Previous studies revealed that B cells and TLSs are closely linked to immunotherapy responses [12, 13, 14], and TLSs are positively associated with better ICI response in melanoma [15], sarcoma [16], and bladder cancer [17]. Consequently, TLS‐focused interventions are considered promising strategies for cancer therapy [9, 18].

Pathological section was the simplest strategy to detect TLSs based on morphology [8], which may introduce observer variability and sampling bias [19]. Presently, 12 chemokine patterns (CCL2, CCL3, CCL4, CCL5, CCL8, CCL18, CCL19, CCL21, CXCL9, CXCL10, CXCL11, and CXCL13) have been shown to accurately evaluate the presence of TLSs in human tissues [20, 21]. Accumulating studies have confirmed the high correlation of 12 chemokines with the formation of TLS [22, 23, 24]. Nevertheless, it should be noted that RNA sequencing also faces sampling bias, as it does not examine the entire tumor tissue. Recent studies using TCGA‐LUAD data have shown that high TLS scores predict favorable prognoses in LUAD [25, 26]. Evidence further suggests that TLSs are protective prognostic factors in various solid tumors, including breast cancer [27], colorectal cancer [28], malignant melanoma [15], ovarian cancer [29], gastric cancer [30], and pancreatic cancer [31], and facilitate memory T and B cell generation required for durable antitumor immunity [32]. However, the mechanisms underlying TLS–LUAD interactions and immune cell crosstalk within TLSs remain unclear.

In this study, we integrated multiple independent LUAD datasets to evaluate TLSs in patients with LUAD on the basis of the 12‐chemokine transcriptomic signature. We performed survival and TME infiltration analyses between clusters and identified cluster‐related differentially expressed genes (DEGs) to define genomic subgroups. We assessed the relationships among TLS patterns, gene clusters, immune infiltration, and drug sensitivity. Finally, we validated our bioinformatics findings at the histopathological level to elucidate the functional and prognostic significance of TLSs in LUAD.

Materials and Methods

Data Acquisition

RNA‐seq data from 539 LUAD tumor samples and 59 normal lung samples were downloaded from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA, https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/repository). The expression values were normalized as fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM) and further transformed to log2 (FPKM + 1) values for subsequent analysis. Corresponding clinical information, including age, sex, pathological stage, and survival outcomes (such as follow‐up time and status), was collected for each sample. Of these, 497 cases with complete prognostic data were used as the training set for model construction.

Microarray‐based transcriptional profiles and clinical data were also obtained for two independent external validation cohorts from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/), specifically GSE13213 and GSE37745. In total, GSE13213 included 117 LUAD samples, and GSE37745 included 106 LUAD samples. Before analysis, probe sets not annotated to official gene symbols were filtered out, and the data were collapsed to gene‐level expression values.

Estimation of TLS Patterns and Identification of DEGs in LUAD

A metagene consisting of 12 chemokines (CCL2, CCL3, CCL4, CCL5, CCL8, CCL18, CCL19, CCL21, CXCL9, CXCL10, CXCL11, and CXCL13) was used as the transcriptomic TLS signature [20]. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) associated with TLS patterns were identified via the “edgeR” package (version 3.38.4, http://www.bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/edgeR.html), which uses linear regression and empirical Bayes methods [33]. Multiple testing correction was conducted via the Benjamini and Hochberg method, with an FDR < 0.05 and |log2 fold change (FC)| > 1 set as the thresholds for significant DEGs.

Molecular Subgroup Prediction via the TLS Signature and TLS Score Development

Unsupervised consensus clustering was conducted via the R package “ConsensusClusterPlus” (version 1.60.0, http://www.bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/ConsensusClusterPlus.html) on the basis of the expression levels of the 12‐chemokine signature genes in LUAD samples [34]. Clustering employed a Pearson correlation distance with 80% sample resampling performed over 10 iterations. The consensus matrix, cumulative distribution function (CDF) plot, item tracking plot, and scree plot were generated to evaluate cluster stability and determine the optimal number of clusters (k). Principal component analysis (PCA) was used to identify the appropriate k value. On this basis, samples in both the TCGA and GEO cohorts were subdivided into TLS molecular subgroups.

Analysis of the Correlation Between the TME and TLS Score in LUAD

To assess the prognostic relevance of the TLS signature, Kaplan–Meier survival curves were constructed via the R package “survival” [35]. Immune cell infiltration and immune scores for each sample were evaluated via the ESTIMATE [36] and CIBERSORT [37] algorithms, respectively. The relationship between the TLS signature score and immune infiltration was further examined via linear regression analysis.

Acquisition of Checkpoint, Antigen Presentation (HLA), and Signature Genes

The expression matrices of immune checkpoint genes and HLA family genes were extracted for each TLS subgroup. The Wilcoxon test was applied to compare gene expression differences between TLS subtypes. Genes specifically associated with different TLS patterns were identified via the “edgeR” package, and significance was adjusted via the Benjamini and Hochberg method. Volcano plots were generated to visualize specific gene differences. The prognostic value of the signature genes was subsequently evaluated via survival analyses for all patients.

Construction of Risk Score and Analysis of Its Correlation With TLS Signature

DEGs between the two TLS subtypes were identified via previously defined thresholds (FDR < 0.05, |log2 FC| > 1). Prognosis‐related DEGs were further screened, and a LASSO Cox regression model was employed to construct a multigene risk signature. Risk scores were calculated for each patient as the sum of the product of gene expression (Exp_gene) and regression coefficients (Coef_gene): Risk score = ∑ (Coef_gene × Exp_gene). Patients were stratified into high‐ and low‐risk groups according to the optimal cutoff on the basis of receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was then performed to compare survival outcomes between the groups.

Patient Cohort

Between 2020 and 2022, a prospective cohort of 98 patients with stage I–IV LUAD who underwent standard surgical resection at the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University was enrolled. There were 56 males and 42 females. Surgical tissue slides were reviewed by experienced pathologists to confirm the primary tumor site and associated lymph nodes (LNs). The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) solitary primary NSCLC; (2) anatomical resection with systematic LN sampling or dissection; (3) availability of both resected tumor and LN tissue for histopathological assessment; and (4) sufficient tissue for IHC and multiplex immunofluorescence (MIF) analyses. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) multiple lesions; (2) small cell or noninvasive lung cancer; (3) diagnostic biopsy prior to surgery; and (4) neoadjuvant therapy before surgery. Patients were followed every 3 months for 2 years postoperatively and at least annually thereafter. The study protocol was approved by the institutional review board, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Experimental Methods for H&E and IHC Staining

H&E staining: Hematoxylin stains nuclei blue–purple, while eosin stains cytoplasmic proteins pink–red, facilitating morphological assessment. Formalin‐fixed paraffin‐embedded (FFPE) sections (4–5 μm) were dewaxed with xylene, rehydrated in graded alcohols, subjected to hematoxylin staining (3–8 min), differentiated in 1% acid–alcohol, blued (e.g., 0.2% ammonia water), subjected to eosin counterstaining (1–3 min), dehydrated, and mounted. The quality control criteria included sharp nuclear–cytoplasmic contrast and section thickness ≤ 5 μm. IHC staining: For antigen–antibody reactions, target proteins, including CD3 (Proteintech, 1:1000), CD8a (Proteintech, 1:10,000), CD20 (Zenbio, 1:50), HLA‐DMB (Proteintech, 1:300), CD5 (Zenbio, 1:100), and P2RY13 (Proteintech, 1:50), were detected. The stained slides were scanned via an HS6 slide scanner (Ningbo Sunny).

TLS Identification and Evaluation in Lung Cancer Tissues

The slides were independently reviewed by two pathologists blinded to the clinical data. TLSs were identified on HE‐stained sections via ImageScope ×64 according to published criteria. Tumors with at least one intratumoral TLS were classified as TLS‐positive, and those without were classified as TLS‐negative. TLS density was calculated in all areas of lymphoid aggregation; a median value of 0.089594057 TLS/mm2 distinguished low versus high TLS tumors. IHC for CD3, CD8, CD20, CD5, HLA‐DMB, and P2RY13 was performed to further explore marker expression within TLSs. Positive staining regions were annotated via both H&E and IHC results with ImageScope ×64. The marker density and proportion were quantified by ImageJ software: density was calculated as the number of positive cells per TLS area, and the proportion was calculated as the number of positive cells per total TLS cell count. Median values were used to dichotomize density and proportion into high and low groups, respectively.

Statistical Analysis

All data processing, analysis, and visualization were performed via R version 4.2.1. Differences between groups were assessed with the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Identification of Key TLS‐Related Genes

As depicted in the study workflow (Figure 1), a total of 598 samples (539 tumor and 59 normal) from the TCGA LUAD dataset were included in the analysis. We first compared the expression of the 12 chemokines comprising the TLS signature between tumor and normal samples. Five of these chemokines were differentially expressed: CCL19, CXCL9, and CXCL13 were upregulated, whereas CCL2 and CCL18 were downregulated in tumor tissues compared with normal tissues (Figure 2a). Unsupervised consensus clustering of the TLS signature genes was performed. The delta area plot indicated optimal separation at k = 2 (Figure 2b). Using this classification, 531 LUAD tumor samples in TCGA were assigned to two clusters: TLS Cluster 1 (n = 322) and Cluster 2 (n = 209) (Figure 2c). K–M analysis revealed significantly longer survival for patients in Cluster 1 than those in Cluster 2 (p < 0.05; Figure 2d).

An eight‐gene prognostic model was developed and implemented to stratify patients by risk, then validated in two independent GEO cohorts (GSE13213 and GSE37745).

Risk Score Calculation and Validation

To further characterize the two TLS‐based subtypes, DEGs were identified between clusters in the TCGA cohort, yielding a total of 2309 genes (Figure 3a). Among these genes, 1419 genes were downregulated and 890 genes were upregulated in Cluster 2 compared with Cluster 1. Univariate Cox regression identified 47 DEGs significantly associated with prognosis (Figure 3b), which were subjected to LASSO Cox analysis. This modeling approach selected eight optimal genes (CD5, P2RY13, HLA‐DMB, LTF, SCGB1A1, S100P, SMOC1, and SCGB3A1) to construct a multigene risk score (Figure 3c). Risk scores for each patient were calculated via the TCGA‐derived coefficient formula and subsequently applied to the external GEO validation cohort. In all datasets, patients with lower risk scores exhibited significantly longer overall survival than those with higher risk scores did (Figure 3d). Multivariate Cox regression demonstrated that the risk score was an independent prognostic indicator in LUAD (Figure 3e). Notably, a greater proportion of Cluster 1 patients than Cluster 2 patients had low‐risk scores (63.1% vs. 58.2%), which correlated with prolonged survival and elevated immune cell infiltration (Figure 3f).

(d) Kaplan–Meier survival curves and ROC analyses demonstrating the prognostic value of the gene signature in the TCGA cohort (left) and two external GEO validation cohorts (middle: GSE13213, right: GSE37745), with corresponding AUC values. Nomogram for Disease‐Free Survival Prediction

The median survival in the TCGA LUAD cohort was 2.11 years. We constructed a nomogram incorporating the risk score to predict patient survival probability via multivariate Cox analysis (Figure 4a). The calibration plots for the 1‐ and 3‐year disease‐free survival probabilities showed strong agreement between the predicted and observed outcomes, whereas the 5‐year calibration showed somewhat diminished fidelity (Figure 4b–d).

Nomogram for predicting disease‐free survival (DFS) probability and calibration plots. (a) Nomogram integrating the risk score for individualized DFS prediction. (b–d) Calibration curves for the predicted 1‐year (b), 3‐year (c), and 5‐year (d) DFS probabilities in the TCGA cohort, demonstrating agreement between the predicted and observed outcomes.

TLS‐Associated Tumor Microenvironment Characteristics in Two Distinct Molecular Clusters

Next, to compare the components of immune cell infiltration between the two TLS patterns, we used ESTIMATE and CIBERSORT to explore the relationship between TLS subtypes and TME cell infiltration. Cluster 1 exhibited high infiltration levels of memory B cells, CD8+ T cells, CD4+ T cells, Tfh cells, and M1 macrophages. In contrast, Cluster 2 showed high frequencies of M0 macrophages, M2 macrophages, and activated dendritic cells (Figure 5a). Additionally, the ESTIMATE algorithm was used to generate immune scores for the TCGA dataset (Figure S1); the results showed no significant difference between the two clusters (p = 0.926). To further investigate the influence of the TLS signature on immune characteristics, we compared the expression of immune checkpoint genes (Figure 5b) and HLA family genes (Figure 5c) between the two subtypes. The results indicated that positive immune checkpoint genes (ICOS, HAVCR2, PDCD1, CD274, CD47, CTLA4, SIRPA, TNFRSF4, and TNFRSF9) and antigen‐presenting genes (B2M and HLA‐related genes) were significantly enriched in Cluster 1, further proving that patients in Cluster 1 have higher immune activation and better prognosis.

The p values for intercluster differences are labeled above each cell type. Associations of TLS Density With Clinicopathological Features and Patient Survival

We validated the transcriptomic findings at the tissue level via H&E and IHC staining of 98 surgical LUAD samples. Among the 98 patients evaluated, TLSs were detected in the postresection specimens of 49 patients (Figure 6). Detailed morphological analysis of TLSs revealed a range of sizes and maturation stages: aggregates (AGGs, immature flat/elongated), primary follicle‐like (FL‐I, immature round/oval), and secondary follicle‐like (FL‐II, with germinal center; Figure 6a).

Morphological features and prognostic impact of TLSs in LUAD patient samples. (a) Representative H&E images showing three maturation stages of TLSs: aggregates (AGGs, immature), primary follicle‐like (FL‐I, immature round/oval), and secondary follicle‐like (FL‐II, mature with germinal center) stages. (b) Immunohistochemical staining for CD3+, CD8+, and CD20+ cells within TLSs from high‐ and low‐density LUAD samples, illustrating the spatial distribution of immune subsets. (c) Representative H&E staining of low‐ and high‐density TLS samples. (d) Quantitative analysis of TLS, CD3, CD8, and CD20 densities across TNM stages, showing isolated subgroup differences but no progressive linear trend by stage. (e) Kaplan–Meier analysis of overall survival by TLS, CD3+, CD8+, and CD20+ cell density, indicating improved prognosis for patients with high intratumoral TLS/immune cell density.

IHC staining of TLSs for CD3, CD8, and CD20 demonstrated distinct regional compartmentalization of T and B‐cell subsets (Figure 6b). Quantitative scoring of the intratumoral TLS density (TLSs/mm2) via both H&E and immune marker staining stratified patients into low‐ and high‐density groups (Figure 6c). K–M analysis revealed that high TLS density was associated with significantly improved overall survival (log‐rank p = 0.0433; Figure 6e). Similar associations were observed for high CD3+ cell density. TLS density was not significantly correlated with stage, age, sex, or T and N classification (Figure 6d, Figure S1).

Prognostic Immune Genes and Their Clinicopathological and Survival Associations

To further validate the prognostic value of key immune genes, we performed IHC staining to examine the in situ protein expression levels of these key immune genes in both tumor and TLS regions. Representative images of P2RY13, CD5, and HLA‐DMB staining are shown in Figure 7a. These three genes are the top three most representative genes among the eight key prognostic genes. Patients were stratified into high‐ and low‐expression groups on the basis of the density of marker‐positive cells within TLS regions. High expression of these markers was strongly associated with longer overall survival, which is in line with bioinformatics predictions (P2RY13 log‐rank p = 0.0369; Figure 7c).

Analysis revealed no significant correlations between the expression levels of these genes and major clinicopathological parameters, including age, sex, tumor stage, or T/N classification (Figure 7b, Figure S2).

Discussion

The TME, which is composed of diverse immune cell subsets and molecular mediators, plays a central role in regulating antitumor efficacy and clinical outcomes in cancer patients [38, 39, 40]. Among the key components of the TME, TLSs have increasingly attracted attention as unique ectopic lymphoid aggregates that support antitumor immune responses [8, 12, 41]. Multiple studies have demonstrated that the presence of TLSs is strongly correlated with improved survival and more favorable clinical outcomes for numerous solid tumors, including melanoma, sarcoma, and bladder cancer. Recent evidence also suggests that the spatial architecture and density of TLSs are critical for predicting immunotherapy responses, underscoring the clinical relevance of TLS assessment [41].

In this study, we classified LUAD into two subtypes according to TLS signature gene expression via unsupervised clustering. We systematically explored the associations of TLS subtypes with survival outcomes, immune infiltration patterns, tumor microenvironment features, and predicted sensitivity to chemotherapy, providing new insights into the molecular heterogeneity of LUAD in relation to TLS biology. Our results expand the current understanding of TLS diversity in LUAD and highlight the potential of TLSs as both prognostic and therapeutic biomarkers.

Cumulative evidence indicates that coordinated and effective immune responses against tumors are initiated and sustained within TLSs, which structurally and functionally resemble secondary lymphoid organs [8, 42]. Transcriptomic profiles—such as the 12‐chemokine signature, T follicular helper (Tfh) cell signature, and gene signatures for Th1, B, and plasma cells—have been validated as reliable markers for TLS detection in cancer tissues [15]. In our cohort, five key TLS‐associated chemokines (CCL19, CXCL13, and CXCL9—upregulated; CCL2 and CCL18—downregulated) were differentially expressed in LUAD compared with normal lung tissue. Of these, CXCL13 and CCL19 are especially noteworthy, as they mediate the recruitment and organization of B and T cells within TLSs—both of which are critical for effective antitumor immunity [43, 44, 45].

By employing consensus clustering of TLS signature genes, we divided LUAD patients into two subgroups (Cluster 1 and Cluster 2) displaying distinct features of immune infiltration and prognosis. Cluster 1 was characterized by increased infiltration of CD8+ T cells, CD4+ T cells, Tfh cells, and M1 macrophages—all of which are indicative of an immune‐activated environment—and was associated with the most favorable survival outcomes [46, 47]. Interestingly, Cluster 1 also exhibited increased levels of immunosuppressive subsets, such as regulatory T cells (Tregs), reflecting the complex nature of the TLS compartment [15, 48, 49]. Although no significant difference in the overall immune score was observed between the subtypes, Cluster 1 was enriched in positive immune checkpoints (e.g., ICOS, HAVCR2, PDCD1, SIRPA, TNFRSF4, and TNFRSF9) and antigen‐presenting genes (B2M and various HLA genes), which may contribute to enhanced immune activation and survival advantage in this group. These findings suggest that the balance and interplay of activating and suppressive immune signals within TLSs may critically shape the antitumor microenvironment and treatment outcomes.

We further identified differentially expressed genes between clusters and established an eight‐gene risk model that stratified patients according to survival outcomes. This risk score was found to be an independent prognostic factor and was significantly associated with TLS cluster assignment. Notably, patients classified as low risk were more likely to be in Cluster 1 and exhibited increased sensitivity to multiple chemotherapeutic agents, suggesting a link between a favorable immune microenvironment, TLS status, and therapy response. These findings suggest that the integration of TLS‐based immune signatures may help guide both prognosis and personalized treatment decisions for LUAD patients.

Nevertheless, this retrospective study has several limitations. First, most analyses were based on bulk transcriptomic data, which can obscure the contributions of rare or phenotypically distinct immune cell subpopulations—such as regulatory B cells—that are difficult to distinguish computationally. Second, although several core prognostic genes have been validated at the protein level in clinical samples, their mechanistic roles in regulating drug sensitivity require further functional studies. Third, the complex cellular interactions within TLSs and their dynamic crosstalk with the TME remain to be fully elucidated. Therefore, additional prospective studies and experimental models will be necessary to fully validate the translational and mechanistic implications of our findings.

In summary, our work demonstrated that LUAD can be subdivided according to TLS molecular signatures, resulting in subtypes that differ in prognosis, immune microenvironment features, and predicted chemotherapy sensitivity. These results provide a foundation for future research into TLS‐directed biomarkers and therapeutic strategies, with the goal of advancing novel, immune‐based precision medicine approaches in LUAD.

In conclusion, this study revealed that TLS‐associated gene signatures serve as robust molecular classifiers for stratifying lung adenocarcinoma into distinct immune subtypes with meaningful differences in clinical outcomes and therapeutic responses. Lower TLS‐based risk scores are associated with increased antitumor immune activity, longer survival, and improved chemosensitivity. Our findings underscore the prognostic and therapeutic potential of incorporating TLS profiling into routine clinical evaluation for LUAD, providing a basis for future studies aimed at individualized immunotherapy and improved patient outcomes.
