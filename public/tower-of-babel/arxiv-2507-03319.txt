Lieb-Robinson bounds, automorphic equivalence and LPPL for long-range interacting fermions
Stefan Teufel, Tom Wessel

Abstract
We prove a Lieb-Robinson bound for lattice fermion models with polynomially decaying interactions, which can be used to show the locality of the quasi-local inverse Liouvillian. This allows us to prove automorphic equivalence and the local perturbations perturb locally (LPPL) principle for these systems. The proof of the Lieb-Robinson bound is based on the work of [ 5 ] , and our results also apply to spin systems. We explain why some newer Lieb-Robinson bounds for long-range spin systems cannot be used to prove the locality of the quasi-local inverse Liouvillian, and in some cases may not even hold for fermionic systems.

Lieb-Robinson bounds measure locality in lattice systems, where the Hamiltonian is given as a sum of local terms

where each

Here,

Denoting with

where the observables

and

For

Accordingly, this region is often called the

1

The main purpose of this paper is to prove locality for the quasi-local inverse Liouvillian and automorphic equivalence of gapped ground states in the same phase, which we explain later, for

2
Mathematical framework

This section introduces the mathematical framework that we use to describe long-range interacting fermions on a lattice.

2.1
Lattice and operator algebras

As lattices, we consider finite graphs
(\Lambda,E)
with graph distance
d\lparen\mathop{\cdot},\mathop{\cdot}\rparen
such that there exist constants
D\in{\mathbb{N}}_{+}
and
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{A}}\geq 1
such that

\big\lvert S_{y}(R)\big\rvert:=\big\lvert\{\nonscript\,x\in\Lambda\nonscript\,|\allowbreak\nonscript\,\mathopen{}d\lparen y,x\rparen=R\nonscript\,\}\big\rvert\leq\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{A}}\,R^{D-1}\quad\text{for all $y\in\Lambda$ and $R\geq 1$}.

(2)

These graphs are called
surface-regular
, and the set of all
D
-dimensional surface-regular graphs with growth constant
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{A}}
is denoted
\mathcal{G}(D,\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{A}})
.
As the graphs are assumed to be finite, the existence of these constants is trivial.
The crucial point is that all bounds we derive will be uniform for all graphs in the class
\mathcal{G}(D,\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{A}})
, i.e. they only depend on the dimension
D
and the growth constant
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{A}}
, but not the specific
\Lambda
.
In particular, they are independent of
\lvert\Lambda\rvert
.
Hence, one can always take an increasing sequence
\Lambda_{k}\subset\Lambda_{k+1}
and obtain bounds independent of
k
whenever
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{A}}
and
D
are uniformly bounded in
k
.

All the following definitions implicitly depend on the specific lattice
\Lambda\in\mathcal{G}(D,\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{A}})
which will be clear from the context later on.
If
\Lambda
is not explicitly specified, the statements and definitions hold for all
D\in{\mathbb{N}}_{+}
,
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{A}}>0
and
\Lambda\in\mathcal{G}(D,\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{A}})
.

A simple integration shows that every surface-regular graph is also a regular graph in the sense that

\big\lvert B_{y}(R)\big\rvert:=\big\lvert\{\nonscript\,x\in\Lambda\nonscript\,|\allowbreak\nonscript\,\mathopen{}d\lparen y,x\rparen\leq R\nonscript\,\}\big\rvert\leq\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{V}}\,(R+1)^{D}\quad\text{for all $y\in\Lambda$ and $R\geq 0$},

(3)

with
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{V}}\leq\max\{1,\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{A}}/D\}
.
One could use the more general class of regular graphs, but all the examples we have in mind are surface-regular and one obtains stronger results under this restriction, compare
[
14
]
.

The above conditions in particular include the square lattices

\Lambda:=\{-k,\dotsc,k\}^{D}\subset{\mathbb{Z}}^{D}\quad\text{for some $k\in{\mathbb{N}}$}

with
\ell^{1}
-metric and additionally allow for torus or cylinder geometries.
In these geometries, points on opposite ends of the lattice
\Lambda
, would have distance
1
.

We use
d\lparen\mathop{\cdot},\mathop{\cdot}\rparen
also to denote the distance
d\lparen x,Y\rparen:=\inf_{y\in Y}d\lparen x,y\rparen
between a point
x\in\Lambda
and a set
Y\subset\Lambda
and
d\lparen X,Y\rparen:=\inf_{x\in X,y\in Y}d\lparen x,y\rparen
between two sets
X
,
Y\subset\Lambda
.
We denote with

\operatorname{diam}\lparen X\rparen:=\sup\,\big\{\nonscript\,d\lparen x,y\rparen\nonscript\,|\allowbreak\nonscript\,\mathopen{}x,y\in X\nonscript\,\big\}

the diameter and with
\lvert X\rvert
the cardinality of any set
X\subset\Lambda
.
Furthermore, for
X\subset\Lambda
and
m\geq 0
, denote by
X_{m}
the
fattening

X_{m}:=\big\{\nonscript\,y\in\Lambda{}\nonscript\,\big|\allowbreak\nonscript\,\mathopen{}d\lparen y,X\rparen\leq m\nonscript\,\big\}.

(4)

2.2
Operator Algebra for fermions

For a fermion with spin
{\mathcal{s}}\in{\mathbb{N}}
on the lattice
\Lambda
, the one particle Hilbert space is
{{\mathcal{H}}:=\ell^{2}(\Lambda,{\mathbb{C}}^{\mathcal{s}})}
.
For
N\in{\mathbb{N}_{+}}
, the
N
-particle Hilbert space is the antisymmetric tensor product
{{\mathcal{H}}_{N}:=\bigwedge_{k=1}^{N}{\mathcal{H}}}
, and the fermionic Fock space is
\mathcal{F}:=\bigoplus_{N=0}^{{\mathcal{s}}\,\lvert\Lambda\rvert}{\mathcal{H}}_{N}
, where
{\mathcal{H}}_{0}:={\mathbb{C}}
.
These Hilbert spaces are all finite-dimensional and hence all linear operators on them are bounded.
The C*-algebra
{\mathcal{A}}_{\Lambda}={\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{F})
of bounded operators on
\mathcal{F}
is generated by the fermionic annihilation and creation operators
a_{z,i}
and
a^{*}_{z,i}
.
They satisfy the canonical anti-commutation relations (CAR)

\big\{a_{x,i},a_{y,j}\big\}=0=\big\{a^{*}_{x,i},a^{*}_{y,j}\big\}\quad\text{and}\quad\big\{a_{x,i},a^{*}_{y,j}\big\}=\delta_{i,j}\,\delta_{x,y}\,\mathbf{1}

for all
i,j\in\{1,\dotsc,{\mathcal{s}}\}
and
x,y\in\Lambda
, where
\{A,B\}:=A\,B+B\,A
denotes the anti-commutator.
For each subset
Z\subset\Lambda
one defines the subalgebra
{\mathcal{A}}_{Z}
as the one generated by annihilation and creation operators supported in
Z
.
This yields a natural inclusion
{\mathcal{A}}_{Z^{\prime}}\subset{\mathcal{A}}_{Z}
for
Z^{\prime}\subset Z\subset\Lambda
.

By
{\mathcal{A}}^{+}_{Z}\subset{\mathcal{A}}_{Z}
we denote the subalgebra generated by products of an even number of creation and annihilation operators located in
Z
.
For disjoint subsets
X,Y\subset\Lambda
and operators
A\in{\mathcal{A}}^{+}_{X}
,
B\in{\mathcal{A}}_{Y}
, it then holds that
[A,B]=0
by the CAR.
Moreover, if
A\in{\mathcal{A}}_{X}
,
B\in{\mathcal{A}}_{Y}
and
[A,B]=0
, then
A\in{\mathcal{A}}^{+}_{X}
or
B\in{\mathcal{A}}^{+}_{Y}
must hold true
[
19
, Proposition 2.1]
.

The restriction to even operators is natural for most applications, because in standard physical models no single fermionic particles are created or annihilated.
For our purpose, we are actually often interested in operators conserving the particle number.
And indeed, the subset
{\mathcal{A}}^{{\mathcal{N}}}_{Z}\subset{\mathcal{A}}_{Z}
of elements commuting with the number operator

{\mathcal{N}}_{Z}=\sum_{z\in Z}\sum_{i=1}^{\mathcal{s}}a^{*}_{z,i}\,a_{z,i}

is a subalgebra
{\mathcal{A}}^{{\mathcal{N}}}_{Z}\subset{\mathcal{A}}^{+}_{Z}
of the even algebra.

2.3
Interactions and operator-families

An
interaction
is a function

\Phi\colon\{\nonscript\,Z\subset\Lambda\nonscript\,\}\rightarrow{\mathcal{A}}^{+}_{\Lambda},\quad Z\mapsto\Phi(Z)\in{\mathcal{A}}^{+}_{Z}\quad\text{where}\quad\Phi(Z)=\Phi(Z)^{*}\text{ for all $Z\subset\Lambda$}.

We denote the space of interactions defined on
\Lambda
as
\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda}
.
Associated to each interaction is a
sum-of-local-terms
(SLT) operator

S:=\sum_{Z\subset\Lambda}\Phi(Z).

Note that each interaction term is assumed to be even and self-adjoint.
Hence, also the corresponding SLT operator is even and self-adjoint.

To control the locality of an interaction, we use the
interaction norm

\lVert\Phi\rVert_{\alpha,n}:=\sup_{z\in\Lambda}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Z\subset\Lambda\mathpunct{\ordinarycolon}\\
z\in Z\end{subarray}}\lvert Z\rvert^{n}\,\frac{\lVert\Phi(Z)\rVert}{F_{\alpha}\l\lparen\operatorname{diam}\lparen Z\rparen\bigr\rparen},

(5)

where
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
\alpha\geq 0
and
F_{\alpha}(r):=(r+1)^{-\alpha}
, and we write
\lVert\mathop{\cdot}\rVert_{\alpha}:=\lVert\mathop{\cdot}\rVert_{\alpha,0}
for short.

For an interval
I\subset{\mathbb{R}}
,
time-dependent interactions
are functions
\Phi(\cdot,\cdot)
where
\Phi(\cdot,t)
is an interaction for each
t\in I
and
\Phi(Z,\cdot)
is norm continuous for all
Z\subset\Lambda
.
We denote the space of time-dependent interactions defined on
\Lambda
for an interval
I
as
\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda}(I)
.
For
\Phi\in\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda}(I)
we write
\mathord{\lVert\Phi(t)\rVert_{\alpha,n}}:=\lVert\Phi(\cdot,t)\rVert_{\alpha,n}
and define

\lVert\Phi\rVert_{\alpha,n}:=\sup_{t\in I}\mathord{\lVert\Phi(t)\rVert_{\alpha,n}}.

(6)

Furthermore, for any function
F\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, denote

\lVert F\rVert_{\Lambda}:=\sup_{x\in\Lambda}\sum_{z\in\Lambda}F\l\lparen d\lparen x,z\rparen\bigr\rparen.

(7)

Then, note that

\lVert F_{\alpha}\rVert_{\Lambda}=\sup_{x\in\Lambda}\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\in\Lambda\mathpunct{\ordinarycolon}\\
r=d\lparen x,z\rparen\end{subarray}}F_{\alpha}\l\lparen d\lparen x,z\rparen\bigr\rparen\leq\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{A}}\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}(r+1)^{D-1-\alpha}<\infty

(8)

for all
\Lambda\in\mathcal{G}(D,\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{A}})
and
\alpha>D
uniformly in
\lvert\Lambda\rvert
.

It is important to note that all the constants will only depend on
D
and
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{A}}
and the interaction norm, even though our setting is only dealing with finite lattices
\Lambda\in\mathcal{G}(D,\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{A}})
.
In particular, given an interaction
\Phi
on an infinite surface regular lattice
\Gamma
, e.g.
\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}^{D}
, and defining an interaction norm on
\Gamma
as in (
5
) with the sum over finite
Z\Subset\Gamma
, the restrictions
\Phi\rvert_{\Lambda}
onto finite subsets
\Lambda\subset\Gamma
satisfy our statements uniformly in
\Lambda
, because
\lVert\Phi\rvert_{\Lambda}\rVert_{\alpha,n}\leq\lVert\Phi\rVert_{\alpha,n}
.

Improved Lieb-Robinson bounds for time-dependent long-range interactions

In this section we prove Lieb-Robinson bounds for long-range interacting fermions, improving and extending the previous results from
[
5
,
14
]

We begin by setting up some more notation, which will be used throughout the rest of the paper.

We say that
H_{0}\colon I\rightarrow{\mathcal{A}}^{+}_{\Lambda}
is an on-site Hamiltonian if
H_{0}(t)=\sum_{z\in\Lambda}h_{z}(t)
where each
h_{z}\colon I\rightarrow{\mathcal{A}}^{+}_{\{z\}}
is norm continuous and pointwise self-adjoint and even.

In this section, whenever there is the time-dependent interaction
\Phi\in\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda}(I)
and an on-site Hamiltonian
H_{0}
, we associate with them the operators

H(t)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Z\subset\Lambda\end{subarray}}\Phi(Z,t)+H_{0}(t)\quad\text{and}\quad H_{<R}(t)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Z\subset\Lambda\mathpunct{\ordinarycolon}\\
\operatorname{diam}\lparen Z\rparen<R\end{subarray}}\Phi(Z,t)+H_{0}(t).

(9)

From continuity, it follows that there exists a unique solution
U(t,s)
of

\mathrm{i}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}U(t,s)=H(t)\,U(t,s)\quad\text{with}\quad U(s,s)=\mathbf{1}\quad\text{for all}\quad t,s\in I,

which is a two-parameter evolution family of unitary operators, i.e.

U(t,r)\,U(r,s)=U(t,s)\quad\text{and}\quad U(t,s)^{-1}=U(t,s)^{*}=U(s,t)

for all
t,r,s\in I
.
We call
\tau_{t,s}\colon{\mathcal{A}}_{\Lambda}\rightarrow{\mathcal{A}}_{\Lambda}
defined by

\tau_{t,s}\lparen A\rparen=U(t,s)^{*}\,A\,U(t,s)\quad\text{for all $A\in{\mathcal{A}}_{\Lambda}$}

the
dynamics
of
H
.
Similarly,
\tau_{t,s,<R}
denotes the dynamics of

.

Extending the finite-range Lieb-Robinson bound from
[

]
to time-dependent Hamiltonians, we obtain the following
Proposition
.
Within the statement, we make explicit the trivial bound
\big\lVert[\tau_{t,s,<R}\lparen A\rparen,B]\big\rVert\leq 2\,\lVert A\rVert\,\lVert B\rVert

A
and

.
For the convenience of the reader, the proof, a slightly stronger bound and some comments are given in

Proposition 1
.

Let
D\in{\mathbb{N}}_{+}
,
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{A}}>0
,

and
\Lambda\in\mathcal{G}(D,\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{A}})
.
Moreover, let
I\subset{\mathbb{R}}
be an interval,
\Phi\in\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda}(I)

be an on-site Hamiltonian.
For all
X

Let

The rough idea is to use the interaction picture and consider the long-range part as a perturbation of the short-range Hamiltonian.

6
Comments on spin systems and the conditional expectation

In this section, we want to briefly outline, how slightly better bounds can be obtained directly from the previous bounds
[
14
,
5
]
for spin systems.
To do so, one uses a simple trick, which is relatively common in the quantum information community.
We formulate it here using the conditional expectation and argue, why we believe that the same argument does not work for lattice fermions.
This also poses the question, how linear light cones as in
[
22
,
23
]
could be obtained for lattice fermions, as the proofs rely on the same trick.

6.1
Mathematical framework

In this section we consider the same lattice geometries as in the rest of the work.
But instead of lattice fermions, we consider lattice spins, which requires us to change the involved Hilbert spaces and operator algebras.
On each lattice site
z\in\Lambda
, we attach
{\mathcal{s}}\in\mathbb{N}
spin degrees of freedom such that the local Hilbert space is
{\mathcal{H}}^{\mathrm{spin}}_{z}=\mathbb{C}^{\mathcal{s}}
.
For any
Z\subset\Lambda
we then define the Hilbert space and algebra of bounded linear operators

{\mathcal{H}}^{\mathrm{spin}}_{Z}=\bigotimes_{x\in Z}{\mathcal{H}}^{\mathrm{spin}}_{x}\quad\quad\text{and}\quad\quad{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathrm{spin}}_{Z}={\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}}^{\mathrm{spin}}_{Z}).

For any
Z^{\prime}\subset Z\subset\Lambda
, we have
{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathrm{spin}}_{Z^{\prime}}\subset{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathrm{spin}}_{Z}
due to the trivial identification of
A\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathrm{spin}}_{Z^{\prime}}
with
A\otimes\mathbf{1}_{Z\mathbin{\mathchoice{\displaystyle\setminus}{\textstyle\setminus}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\setminus$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\setminus$}}}Z^{\prime}}\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathrm{spin}}_{Z}
.
In comparison to the fermionic systems, for
X
,
Y\subset\Lambda
disjoint,
[A,B]=0
for all
A\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathrm{spin}}_{X}
and
B\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathrm{spin}}_{Y}
.
Hence, for spin systems there is no need to define an algebra of even operators and all the statements of the rest of the work hold after replacing
{\mathcal{A}}\to{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathrm{spin}}
and
{\mathcal{A}}^{+}\to{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathrm{spin}}
.

6.2
Conditional expectation to improve Lieb-Robinson bounds in spin systems

In most cases, there is no difference between the analysis of lattice spin systems or fermions on a lattice, as long as one is concerned with even operators in the latter.
In our analysis of the lattice fermions, we do restrict to even interactions and even observables to make the analysis possible and this is justified by physical reasons.

We now explain a useful trick using the conditional expectation to improve Lieb-Robinson bounds for spin systems.
In
Section

6.3
we then explain, why this does not work well enough for fermions, due to the distinction between even and odd observables.
For spin systems, the conditional expectation is given by the partial trace, and can be generalized to infinite systems
[
20
]
.
It satisfies the following
Lemma
, which is exactly what we also have for fermionic systems, but without the restriction to even operators in some of the statements, see
Lemma

Lemma 11

(
[
20

)
.

Let
X\subset\Lambda
.
Then there exists a unit-preserving, completely positive linear map
\mathbb{E}_{X}\colon{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathrm{spin}}_{\Lambda}\rightarrow{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathrm{spin}}_{\Lambda}

(a)

\mathbb{E}_{X}\lparen{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathrm{spin}}_{\Lambda}\rparen\subset{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathrm{spin}}_{X}

(b)

\mathbb{E}_{X}\lparen ABC\rparen=A\,\mathbb{E}_{X}\lparen B\rparen\,C

and

This in particular implies
\mathbb{E}_{X}\lparen A\rparen=A

(c)

\lVert\mathbb{E}_{X}\rVert=1

\mathbb{E}_{X}\circ\mathbb{E}_{Y}=\mathbb{E}_{X\cap Y}

(e)

(20)

The conditional expectation is especially useful to approximate the support of a time evolved observable, therefore let

.
Then, it is standard to approximate

This indeed also works for lattice fermions and is used in the proofs.

Moreover, the conditional expectation allows for a trick which is often used for spin systems

6.3
Difference in lattice fermions

We now explain, why the above argument does not apply to fermionic systems.

If the goal is to obtain a good bound on the local approximation of
