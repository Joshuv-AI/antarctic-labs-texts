Association between healthcare providers type and maternal-child healthcare services utilization in Pakistan: evidence from 2018 DHS using generalized structural equation modelling
Authors: Rashed Nawaz, Shaoqing Gong, Razia Anjum, Neelum Khalid, Zhongliang Zhou, Farooq Ahmed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHealthcare provider's distributional shortages, especially in developing countries, result in a slowdown in progress towards better healthcare outcomes and achieving the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs-3). The objective of current research is to examine the influence of professional and non-professional healthcare provider distribution on maternal and child healthcare services among married women who may face structural and social barriers that limit their autonomy in seeking skilled maternal care in Pakistan.MethodsBivariate and multivariate regression models were used for the 2018 Pakistan Demographic and Health Survey (PDHS) (N = 8287 women aged 15–49). The association between the distribution of healthcare providers, antenatal care visits (ANC), place of delivery, and postnatal care visits (PNC) for mothers and children was measured by three types of healthcare provider’s distribution, including no care, non-professional, and professional healthcare providers. All the investigations were performed using STATA V.16.1.ResultsAround 50.7% of mothers have less than three ANC visits, and 49.3% of women have four or more ANC visits. 67% of women have medical facility delivery, whereas the remaining 33% have non-medical facility delivery. Around 69.9% of women have no PNC visit, 5.8% visited non-professional, and the remaining 24.3% visited professional healthcare providers for PNC visits individually. The regression outcomes revealed that professional healthcare providers were positively and significantly associated with the increased number of ANC visits, medical facility delivery, and postnatal checks for mothers and children. Additionally, non-professional healthcare providers were also positively and significantly connected with the number of ANC visits and postnatal checks for mothers by non-professionals.ConclusionOur findings demonstrate that professional healthcare provider’s distribution indicates a positive and significant association with maternal and child healthcare outcomes. This suggests the need for tailored interventions for the distribution of healthcare providers and promoting maternal education, empowering women's decision-making especially in remote areas, to improve maternal and child healthcare utilization services (MCH) and healthcare outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-025-08488-x.

Introduction

Human resources for health (HRH) are worldwide recognized as a critical factor in achieving better maternal and child healthcare outcomes [1]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), HRH includes all individuals aimed at improving health. This contains a different range of professionals, from clinical staff to managers, technicians, and researchers [2–4]. A highly trained, skilled, and motivated healthcare provider's accessibility is important for ensuring the quality of maternal and child healthcare services. Therefore, enhancing healthcare provider's distribution and accessibility is vital for accomplishing Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) for maternal and child healthcare outcomes [5]. Lower and middle-income countries (LMICs), especially sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) and South Asia, are still facing determined shortages of healthcare providers, with many professionals not distributed according to the health services needs and performing below the required standards [6–8]. A study in America demonstrated that 10% of the global disease burden is borne by the world’s 37% of healthcare providers. On the other sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) has 3% of global healthcare providers for 24% of the global disease burden [9]. The SSA has the lowest number of healthcare providers with just 2.3% doctors and 13% nurses per 10,000 inhabitants [2, 9]. Asia has half of the world’s population can access 30% of the health care providers [2]. A recent study determined that to achieve SDGs, it is vital to conduct evidence-based research on effective HRH interventions to improve maternal and newborn healthcare outcomes [10]. A systematic review conducted in 2016 concluded that improving the management, capacity, and motivation of existing HRH is essential for improving the quality of maternal healthcare [6].

Studies demonstrated that Pakistan is also declared as one of the 57th countries with unequal healthcare provider distributions [11]. Research reported the shortage of dentists, midwives, and healthcare professionals in Pakistan due to insufficient planning [12]. A study highlighted that Sindh has a higher number of doctors and suffers from a lower number of nurses, whereas Khyber Pakhtunkhwa (KPK) has a higher number of nurses. The province of Punjab also has shortages of nurses obstructing to achievement of SDGs [13]. Another study highlighted that unequal healthcare resource distribution threatens the public health system, with hospitals, clinics, and healthcare provider’s distribution concentrated in urban regions, leaving behind rural regions [14]. This leads to significant disparities in healthcare access and health outcomes among urban and rural populations [14]. Another study reported that in Pakistan, approximately 32,879 physicians graduate every year, with 40% migrating abroad for better opportunities because of low income, long duties, and unequal distribution [15]. These factors collectively affect maternal and child healthcare services (MCH) in Pakistan.

Maternal and child healthcare remains a key universal health challenge that has shown minimal progress, especially in 2015 [16]. Nearly 303,000 females die each year from avoidable pregnancy and childbirth-related issues, with over 99% of these deaths occurring in LMICs, including Pakistan [17]. While pregnancy-related deaths have reduced by 38% over the past two decades, the annual reduction of 3% is too slow to meet the SGD targets by 2030 [18]. These avoidable deaths are predominantly caused due to poor quality of care provided in health facilities [19]. LCIMs countries rapidly require improved quality MCH services to meet the 2030 SDG goals of reducing maternal and neonatal mortality to under 70/100,000 and 12/1000 live births, respectively. This requires improving the distribution, skills, and availability of professional healthcare providers [6, 20].

Improving MCH services requires strengthening evidence-based interventions in ANC, place of delivery, and PNC visits. In developed nations, around 97% of women received at least one ANC visit, 99% delivered in a medical facility, and 90% received at least one PNC visit [21]. On the contrary, in developing nations, 69% of the females in sub-Saharan Africa and 54% in Asia receive at least one ANC visit [22]. DHS data from 23 African countries revealed that two-thirds of the females in sub-Saharan Africa delivered in non-medical facilities, and only 13% of the females received PNC visits two days after their delivery [23].

ANC, Place of delivery, and PNC visits have the potential to minimize maternal and child morbidity and mortality [24, 25]. WHO has intensely focused on promoting MCH services through its Safe Motherhood Initiative (SMI) [26]. Quality ANC during pregnancy includes nutritional counselling, eight ANC visits, health counselling regarding pregnancy, and early birth danger signs [26, 27]. Regular ANC visits recognize high-risk pregnancies and improve access to skilled delivery care. Females with more ANC visits are four times more likely to deliver in a medical facility [28]. Quality PNC visits include examining cords, measuring temperature, counselling dangers, counselling breastfeeding, and observing breastfeeding for both maternal and newborns, and three PNC visits [26, 29].

However, the quality of MCH services was criticized for its limited ability to prevent, diagnose, or treat complications [24]. Studies have questioned the effectiveness of MCH services in minimizing maternal and child mortality [30, 31]. Skilled healthcare provider’s distribution and quality of MCH services are essential for achieving better results. Several structural and social factors that limit women's autonomy in seeking skilled maternal and child healthcare are the quality of MCH services are geographic location, population density, healthcare infrastructure, healthcare provider distribution, wealth, maternal education, and culture [28, 32, 33]. As per an estimation, seven out of ten females who deliver their child in a non-medical facility do not receive PNC [34]. Policy and programs, especially in developing nations like Pakistan, have mainly neglected this critical phase that obstructs the efforts to achieve the Millennium Development Goals (MDGs) for maternal and child survival.

Pakistan did not meet most of the MDGs, including 4 and 5, and still has high rates of maternal and child health challenges [35]. The maternal mortality ratio (MMR) in Pakistan decreased from 521 per 100,000 live births in 1990 to 178 per 100,000 in 2019. Major reasons for MMR in LCIMs are postpartum hemorrhage, eclampsia, obstructed labor, and sepsis, contributing to 20% of the 8,000 yearly deaths in Pakistani women aged 15–49. MMR also varies among regions, with rural regions having a higher MMR (319/100,000) compared to urban areas (175/100,000). Baluchistan has the highest MMR (298/100,000) compared to Punjab (157/100,000) [36–38]. Pakistan contributes 49% of all childhood deaths globally and recently ranked first among the countries contributing to newborn mortality [35]. Child mortality also varies among the regions and households. Child mortality is 2.5 times greater in a poor household (119/1000 live births) compared to wealthier households (48/1000 live births). Likewise, Baluchistan has a higher child mortality rate (111/1000 live births) as compared to Khyber Pakhtunkhwa (70/1000 live births) [36, 37]. A key factor in deprived health outcomes is partly due to the government’s low prioritization of healthcare; geographical inequalities further worsen the situation, as 80% of spending goes to tertiary care, leaving 20% for primary healthcare and women facing social and structural barriers that limit their autonomy in seeking professional and skilled maternal healthcare [39]. Existing literature focused on socio-economic and demographic factors influencing MCH outcomes [39, 40].

However, the association between healthcare provider’s distribution and women facing structural and social barriers in seeking skilled MCH services is not well investigated in Pakistan. First, limited studies have examined the relationship between HRH and maternal health in Pakistan. Second, previous researchers did not investigate the relationship between the types of healthcare providers and MCH, and it is of great importance to recognize and distinguish how the different types of healthcare providers are linked with MCH services. Lastly, as far as we know, current research is the first to explore the association between the type of healthcare provider distribution and maternal and child healthcare services in Pakistan. Whereas, child healthcare services are specific to examining cords, measuring temperature, counselling dangers, counselling breastfeeding, and observing breastfeeding within the first 48 h of the birth.

Hence, the current study aims to assess the impact of healthcare provider’s distribution and women facing structural and social barriers in seeking skilled MCH services in Pakistan. Using the 2018 PDHS and generalized structural equation model (GSEM), we seek to (1) assess the impact of professional, non-professional healthcare provider distribution on maternal and child healthcare services and (2) how the professional, non-professional healthcare provider distribution affects child healthcare specific to five services. The outcomes of the current study will contribute to the development of policies and programs aimed at the geographical distribution of healthcare providers, enabling women’s education, enhancing access and decision-making power to access MCH services, especially in remote regions.

Methods

Study setting and statistics

Data for the current study were drawn from the Pakistan Demographic and Health Survey 2018. DHS is a nationally representative multistage sampling survey conducted every five years in numerous countries. The latest, the fifth wave, collected between November 2017 to 30th April 2018, was utilized in this study. The 2018 PDHS has been launched by the National Institute of Population Studies (NIPS) in collaboration with the United States Agency for International Development (USAID) funding [41]. The PDHS survey samples were stratified into two stages among all four provinces and the other two regions. Islamabad and FATA were stratified by dividing them into eight areas: urban and rural. Females aged 15 to 49 were all eligible for interviews. Additional details about the survey setting and data collection can be final report [42]. The data was gathered through interviews with females aged between 15 to 49 years. The PDHS includes three main questionnaires: household, women, and men. Our research gathered data on MCH services-related covariates from the women's questionnaire. To minimize recall bias, this study utilized Pakistan DHS data, which follow standardized data collection protocols, including pre-tested, structured questionnaires. The recall period for key variables, such as healthcare providers, ANC visits, and place of delivery, postnatal check for mothers, and postnatal check for children, is limited to five years or less. Additionally, the PDHS dataset undergoes rigorous data cleaning and validation procedures to identify and correct inconsistencies in self-reported information. These measures help to ensure the reliability and accuracy of the data. The analysis focused on 8255 ANC visits and 8287 last-born children from the 2018 survey.

Sample selection

The analysis sample focused on children born five years before the Pakistan DHS who had comprehensive evidence of their survival status. The total number of births in the past five years was 12,708 recorded in the survey. The source of the population also consisted of all the women who utilized MCH services and gave birth within the five years prior to the 2018 PDHS survey in Pakistan. Considering the importance of ANC and PNC information to assess MCH was recorded for last-born children only. Therefore, the sample was further restricted to the last-born children. The total number of last-born children was 8287. The data regarding ANC was collected based on two criteria. The first criterion is the count of the number of ANC visits as a continuous measure, and the second criterion is the number of ANC visits as fewer than three visits and more than three visits, as a binary measure. The total number of ANC visits was 8255. Furthermore, the place of delivery in the sample selection also includes where the last-born child was delivered. The total number of women who delivered their last child either in a medical facility or a non-medical facility was 8273. Furthermore, the sample selection for PNC checks for the mother included the PNC visits within two months from professional healthcare providers and non-professional healthcare providers, and no PNC visits. Moreover, the postnatal checks for the child included the data for children within two days after the delivery. The postnatal check for the child has been specific to five services, which are 1) examine the cord, 2) measure temperature, 3) Counsel: dangers, 4) Counsel: breastfeeding, and 5) observe: Breastfeeding. We have created a composite index score (0–5) for all the services included in the postnatal check for the child. Finally, the sample selection for professional healthcare providers includes doctors, nurses, and midwives. Whereas, the chemists, pharmacists, and other traditional health personnel were categorized as non-professional healthcare providers. However, our examination varied to some extent across the models due to the proportion of missing values between the control variables. Since the missing data was random, we used list-wise deletion, which resulted in a missing value rate of up to 1.5%.

Variables and measurement

Dependent variable

This study used the number of antenatal care visits (ANC), place of delivery, postnatal checks for mothers (PNC), and postnatal checks for children as dependent variables. The number of ANC visits was collected on two criteria; the first criterion is to count the number of ANC visits as a continuous measure, whereas the second criterion was the number of ANC visits as a binary variable, that is, the number of ANC visits as less than three visits and more than three visits. The second dependent variable is the place of delivery. The place of delivery indicates where the last-born child has been delivered. We have categorized the place of delivery as 0 = non-medical and 1 = medical facility and treated it as a binary measure. Postnatal check for the mother was utilized as a categorical variable; the information regarding postnatal checks for the mother was collected within two months after delivery. Furthermore, the postnatal check for the child was also used as a continuous dependent variable, and the information was collected within two days after the delivery. The postnatal check for the child was specific to five services: examining the cord, measuring temperature, counseling dangers, counseling breastfeeding, and Observing Breastfeeding.

Independent variable

This study used healthcare providers as an independent variable. The healthcare providers were divided into three categories: professional healthcare providers (doctors, nurses, and midwives) and non-professional healthcare providers (pharmacists, chemists, compounders, and traditional health personnel), and no care. The respondents who have seen no one for ANC services were categorized as having no care received = 1. The respondents who received care from chemists, pharmacists, and other traditional persons for their ANC service were classified as Non-professional = 2. The respondents who have received care from doctors, nurses, and midwives for their ANC service have been categorized as Health professionals = 3 [11, 43, 44]. For a robust comparison, no care received by mothers was taken as a reference group. Control variables

Numerous socio-demographic features at the children, maternal, domestic, and community levels, designated as probable determinant features, were recognized to determine the women and child status strongly and were included as control variables [47–49]. Child-related factors include gender, age, and place of delivery (medical institutions vs. non-medical institutions). Maternal level factors consist of sequential age, alive children, and profession. Household-related factors contain distance to a medical facility (a problem vs. not a problem) and household wealth. The household wealth index was calculated using principal component analysis, which took into account the number and variety of household assists, including items like a television, bicycle, or car. The index was accessible Pakistan DHS dataset. Community-level variables contain the residency in a specific geopolitical zone and whether the area is rural or urban. Analytical strategy

We have used descriptive statistics to explain the characteristics of the analysis samples. These statistics encompassed the weighted percentage, which was adjusted for over and under-sampling of population groups and the mean and standard deviation. To conduct the bivariate analysis, percentages and bar charts were used individually to describe the independent variable relationship to each dependent variable, such as ANC visits, place of delivery, postnatal (mother), and postnatal check (child). The variance Inflation factor (VIF) was 1.54. Furthermore, Multivariate analysis has been employed to recognize the factors influencing the independent variables. Only variables that showed a t-statistic value outside the band of between + 2 and ˗2 and (or) a significant probability value less than 0.1 were included in the generalized structural equation model (GSEM). As standard practice, predictor variables with a t-statistic value of + 2 or higher and −2 or lower are assumed to influence the dependent variable significantly [50]. A multicollinearity test has also been conducted to recognize any collinearity issue among the predictor variables.

We chose GSEM for its ability to analyze and quantify relationships and model a wide range of dependent variable types (continuous, binomial, and multinomial) while adjusting for the control variables. The GSEM has the flexibility to incorporate latent variables. It can also model complex associations and interdependencies between dependent variables. The GSEM can handle non-normal data distributions, making it an ideal choice for the current study analysis. It also has superior analytical power and could help prevent Type I errors. By utilizing GSEM, we confirm a complete and effective approach to recognize the association between the categorical independent variable and multiple outcome variables.

We utilized different regression models concerning the nature of the outcome variables. Model 1 contains a regression of independent variables, healthcare providers (No care received by mothers, non-professional, professional) on the outcome variable (the number of ANC visits) at p < 0.001. Model 2 predicts the independent variable healthcare providers (no care received by mothers, non-professional, professional) on (place of delivery) at p < 0.001. Model 3 regressed the independent variable, healthcare providers (no care received by mothers, non-professional, professional), on the dependent variable (Postnatal checks for mothers) at p < 0.001. Finally, model 4 builds on the regression of independent variable healthcare providers (no care received by mothers, non-professional, professional) on the outcome variable (Postnatal checks for children) at p < 0.001.

Based on the nature of the outcome variables, firstly, for the number of ANC visits, we specified the Gaussian (identity) function to indicate a continuous measure. Since the estimated effects on this outcome variable were a continuous measure, we reported the coefficient values. Similarly, for place of delivery, we identified the Binomial (identity) function to indicate a binary outcome we reported odd ratios, because odd ratios provide a clear and interpretable measure of the strength and direction of the association among the independent and binary outcome, making it easier to understand the effect of predictors on the likelihood of the event occurring. Moreover, we utilized a multinomial (identity) function for the postnatal checks for mothers to indicate that the outcome variable has a categorical measure, we also reported the odd ratios because they provide a straightforward way to interpret the relative likelihood of each outcome category with the reference category, allowing for easier comparison of the effects of predictors across multiple categories. Finally, for Postnatal checks for children, we first created a composite index of these five specific services, which include 1) examination of the cord, 2) measurement of temperature, 3) counselling: dangers, 4) counselling: breastfeeding, and 5) observation of breastfeeding. The index ranges from 0 to 5. We further specified the Gaussian (identity) function to indicate a continuous measure. The composite index helps quantify the association between independent variables and the index score. Since the composite index is treated as a continuous measure, the coefficient obtained through the GSEM indicates the strength and direction of the effect that each predictor has on the composite index, which represents all levels of child postnatal care services. This allows for a more nuanced understanding of how various factors affect the likelihood of receiving multiple postnatal child healthcare services and provides an understanding of which factors significantly impact the quality and quantity of the care provided after the two days of the delivery.

Methodological assumptions

The current study has several important methodological assumptions that strengthen the models used to analyze the data. Firstly, we assumed a linear association between the independent and the dependent variables. We also assumed that errors across the equations are independent, with no correlation among residuals. The variance of the error term is also assumed to be constant across all the observations. For the categorical independent variables, we also ensured that the issue of multicollinearity would be avoided, and the related variables must be added to prevent omitted variable bias. Furthermore, the assumption of the appropriate model specification is also very critical, to ensure that the chosen link functions (Gaussian, binomial, and multinomial) exactly represent the nature of the outcome variables.

Ethical approval

This study involves human participants and procedures and questionnaires for standard DHS surveys have been reviewed and approved by the ICF Institutional Review Board (IRB). Additionally, country-specific DHS survey protocols are reviewed by the ICF, IRB, and typically by an IRB in the host country. ICF and IRB ensure that the survey complies with the US Department of Health and Human Services regulations for protecting human subjects (45 CFR 46), while the host country IRB ensures that the survey complies with the laws and norms of the nation. Participants gave informed consent to participate in the study before taking part. Patients and/or the public were not involved in this research's design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination plans. The DHS also employs culturally sensitive methodologies to ensure that survey instruments are appropriately adapted to local contexts. This includes translating questionnaires into local languages, training interviewers to be culturally aware, and using locally trusted community members to conduct interviews. These measures help overcome cultural barriers and ensure that participants feel comfortable and willing to provide accurate information.

BIC is identical to AIC but applies a stronger penalty for model complexity. Model 2 has the lowest BIC (8076.849), suggesting its superiority. Model 3 (10734.17) ranks second, while Model 1 (37090.93) and Model 4 (30783.67) have the highest BIC values, indicating they may not be suitable due to overfitting or inefficiency.

Overall, Model 2 is the best model among all four models based on AIC, BIC, and LL, followed by Model 3. On the other hand, Model 1 and Model 4 perform the poorest, suggesting that they may not be choices. Hence, we conclude that Model 2 is the most suitable model for this data set, as it best trades off between the model complexity and fit. Model 3 is also a reasonable choice, whereas models 1 and 4 show poor performance and should be reconsidered for further improvements or alternative modelling approaches.

Discussion

The current study has utilized the data from PDHS 2018 to investigate the association between the healthcare provider's distribution (Professional, non-professional, and no care) and the number of ANC visits and place of delivery, as well as a postnatal check (mother). This research also examined the trends in the relationship between healthcare and postnatal check (child) specific to five services: examine cord, measure temperature, counsel: dangers, counsel: breastfeeding, and observe: breastfeeding in Pakistan. Our findings illustrated that mothers have a positive attitude towards ANC and PNC visits. However, despite the recognized advantages, the place of delivery and PNC visits limit women's autonomy in seeking professional maternal healthcare.

GSEM was applied for the multivariate analysis to recognize the factors influencing the independent variables. The relationship between the healthcare providers (professional, non-professional, and no ANC received by mothers) and the total number of ANC visits illustrated that 100% of mothers who received no prenatal care had less than three ANC visits. Furthermore, out of 100% of the population of women who received their prenatal care from non-professional healthcare providers, around 68.06% of those mothers had less than three ANC visits, and the remaining 31.94% of the mothers had four or more ANC visits. Consistently, out of the 100% population of the women who received their prenatal care from professional healthcare providers group around 40.63% of those mothers had less than three ANC visits, and the remaining 59.37% of those mothers had four or more ANC visits. The outcomes of our study are similar to [51–54].

Furthermore, the outcomes of our research also provide the effect of healthcare providers (professional, non-professional, and no ANC received by mothers) distribution on the place of delivery. Out of 100% of the mothers who receive no ANC care, around 74.43% of them delivered in non-medical facilities, and the remaining 25.57% delivered their children in medical facilities. Likewise, of the females who received their ANC from non-professional healthcare providers, 68.04% of them delivered their babies in non-medical facilities, and the remaining 31.96% delivered in medical facilities. Furthermore, out of a total of 100% population of mothers who received prenatal care from professional healthcare providers, around 75.53% have delivered in medical facilities, and the rest, 24.47%, were delivered in non-medical facilities [55, 56].

Moreover, this current research also examined the effect of healthcare provider's (professional, non-professional, and no ANC received by mothers) distribution of postnatal checks (mothers). The outcomes drawn that out of the total 100% population of mothers who have no prenatal care, around 85.02% received no postnatal care, 11.01% of the mothers have received their postnatal care from non-professional healthcare providers, whereas 3.9% received their postnatal care from professional healthcare providers. Additionally, out of the total 100% population of mothers who received their ANC from non-professionals, about 71.31% received no postnatal care, 25.29% received postnatal care from non-professionals, and 3.4% received postnatal care from professional healthcare providers. Finally, out of a total of 100% of the mothers who received their prenatal care from professional healthcare providers, 60.81% received no postnatal care, 6.2% received postnatal care from non-professional healthcare providers, and the remaining 32.99% visited professional healthcare providers for their postnatal care. These results of our study align with the studies of [57, 58].

Likewise, our study also investigated the effect of healthcare providers (professional, non-professional, and no ANC received by childs) distribution with all five postnatal checks for child services. Out of the total 100% population of the children whose cords were examined, 68.79% have received postnatal checks from professional healthcare providers. Non-professional healthcare providers examined 40.97%. No one examined the cords of 38.55% of the children within the two days of their birth. Similarly, out of a total 100% population of the children whose temperature was checked, 52.64% of the children's temperatures were checked by professional healthcare providers, non-professionals checked 22.9% of temperatures, and the remaining 17.59% of temperatures were not checked. Out of the total 100% of children's mothers who were counseled about dangers, 30.77% were counseled by professional healthcare providers, non-professionals counseled 9.93%, and 10.38% remained uncounseled. Additionally, out of the total 100% population of mothers counseled about breastfeeding, professional healthcare providers counseled 49.32%, non-professionals counseled 31.47%, and no one counseled the remaining 21.75%. Eventually, out of the total 100% population of mothers who were observed breastfeeding their child, 29.69% were observed breastfeeding by professional healthcare providers, non-professionals observed 23.47%, and no one observed the remaining 10.94%. The outcomes of this research are similar to those of [33, 57].

Finally, our results recognized that the women who received their prenatal care from the professional healthcare providers group were associated with increased ANC visits, place of delivery, postnatal checks (mothers), and child's postnatal check-up composite index (0–5) (examine cord, measure temperature, counsel dangers, counsel breastfeeding, and observed breastfeeding compared to non-professional healthcare providers and no care received group who face structural and social barriers that limit their access to seeking skilled maternal care. Moreover, the mothers who received prenatal care from non-professional healthcare provider groups were linked with a higher number of ANC visits and an improved number of postnatal checks for mothers from non-professional healthcare provider groups relative to women facing structural and social barriers that limit their access to seeking skilled maternal care. These findings complement the findings from LMICs and Brazil that showed a significant positive association between healthcare professional density and ANC coverage with nurses having a greater impact than doctors [59]. The findings of Burkina Faso discovered a significant correlation between the number of healthcare professionals in healthcare centers and the percentage of medical facility deliveries. Similarly, research across six WHO regions demonstrated a strong association between health workforce density and maternal and child health outcomes [60, 61].

Compared to the global benchmarks, Pakistan’s healthcare provider density remains critically low [62]. The Pakistani healthcare system comprises public, para-statal, private and philanthropic sectors. The doctor-to-population ratio (1:1127) falls short of the WHO standard (1:1100), and the doctor-to-nurse ratio (2.7:1) is far below the ideal (1:4) [13]. Pakistan's health system strength lies in its extensive primary care outreach through over 100,000 lady health workers [63]. On the contrary, in India, the current doctor-to-population ratio is around 1:836, beyond the WHO recommended ratio and doctor-to-nurse ratio (2.02:1) at the national level, with variation across the states [64]. Bangladesh, on the other hand, has a significantly lower doctor-to-population ratio with only 0.67 doctors per 1000 population and a doctor-to-nurse ratio of 2.5:1 [65]. These inequalities in distribution reflect underlying systematic challenges and emphasize the urgent need for policy interventions to enhance the distribution of healthcare providers and ensure equitable access to healthcare services.

The outcomes of the current study on regional and socio-economic disparities in the distribution of healthcare providers demonstrate critical gaps. These findings suggest that there is a need for more equitable distribution of healthcare providers planning. Policies and programs that prioritize the distribution of community health workers in remote and underserved regions, attached with targeted incentives for healthcare providers to work in rural, underdeveloped and low-income communities or regions, could be beneficial to bridge access gaps. Additionally, local recruitment and training programs could support a sustainable workforce strategy to improve maternal and child healthcare outcomes.

The results of current research highlight that key policy implications are that increased healthcare provider distribution is associated with improved maternal and child healthcare outcomes. The findings proposed that professional healthcare provider group distribution is linked with an increased number of ANC visits, higher medical facility deliveries, and increased mother and children postnatal checks in Pakistan. This highlights the need for policies and programs that strengthen the distribution, availability, and quality of professional healthcare services, especially in underdeveloped and remote regions of Pakistan, where access to professional healthcare providers is limited. Extending and ensuring the continuity of free MCH in these areas is important to improve accessibility. Policies should also aim to enhance both access and decision-making power for women in the maternal health context. At the same time, empowering and educating non-professional healthcare provider groups through community-based training can improve the quality of healthcare in regions with limited access to professional healthcare providers. A combined approach to improving healthcare facilities with professionals, offering free MCH services, and training non-professionals can greatly improve maternal and healthcare outcomes and will help to make progress towards the achievement of SDGs.

There are several limitations associated with this study, though the chosen model specifications are theoretically appropriate, potential limitations include model misspecification, where non-linear associations might better be suited to the data. There is also the possibility of omitted variable bias, where unmeasured factors could affect both independent and dependent variables, leading to biased estimation. Another limitation is that the issue of multicollinearity among the independent variables may misrepresent coefficient estimates, making it difficult to assess the true effect of each independent variable. The generalizability of outcomes may be limited if the sample is not representative of other populations, impacting the wider applicability of the results. Moreover, the cross-sectional data utilization limits the ability to identify the causal association. In some cases, there may be under-reporting of mortality due to cultural barriers against discussion of the deceased or the emotional distress of recalling dead children. Additionally, the father's education level was reported by the mother, which introduces potential errors, particularly since we concentrated on the years of education completed. The DHS dataset includes information from different provinces and regions across Pakistan, which allows the identification of regional inequalities in professional and non-professional healthcare provider access and health outcomes. While the present investigation is more focused on national trends, visualizing these regional differences could offer deeper insights into inequalities and inform target policy responses. Future studies should further explore these geographical patterns.

Notwithstanding the limitations, our research strength lies in its use of extensive nationally representative data, enabling the consideration of household-level correlations. Our findings suggested the need for targeted health policies that focus on healthcare provider distribution to enhance MCH services and healthcare outcomes in Pakistan. It is vital to have trained healthcare providers and medical facilities across different regions. Improving healthcare distribution and outcomes requires education, food security, hygiene, and sanitation. Policymakers should prioritize resource allocation to areas where healthcare utilization services are limited to promote better maternal and child healthcare outcomes.

Conclusions

The outcome of the current study demonstrates the important role of healthcare provider groups (professional and non-professional) in improving maternal and child healthcare outcomes, especially in increasing ANC visits, increasing medical facility deliveries, and postnatal care for both mother and children. The outcomes suggest that an equitable distribution of professional healthcare providers, including doctors, nurses, and midwives, is of vital importance for the expansion of MCH services, particularly in underdeveloped and remote areas of Pakistan, where women may be facing structural and social barriers that limit their autonomy in seeking skilled maternal care.

To strengthen the MCH services, policymakers need to prioritize the equitable distribution of healthcare providers and invest in both professional training and community health initiatives. Policies and programs that incorporate non-professional healthcare providers into the healthcare system, such as community-based education, maternal education regarding ANC visits, place of delivery, and postnatal care with professional care, should be supported to bridge the gap in healthcare access. Policy interventions should focus on providing incentives for healthcare providers to serve in remote regions. Collaboration between government and non-government organizations through incentives, counseling, empowering women in decision-making in acquiring maternal healthcare, and community awareness campaigns can help MCH services across different regions of Pakistan. Future research should investigate the long-term effect of improved healthcare provider distribution groups on health outcomes, especially in rural areas, by utilizing the Poisson or negative binomial regression method, the multivariate Probit model (MVP), and Latent Class Analysis. These models can add flexibility for modeling relationships between the outcomes in different ways.

In conclusion, ensuring well-distributed and supported healthcare provider groups, promoting maternal education, empowering women's decision-making in acquiring maternal care, and expanding access to free MCH services, especially in areas where it has been not offered yet, are the essentials to enhancing health outcomes. These efforts are vital for developing healthcare equity and achieving improved MCH outcomes in Pakistan.
