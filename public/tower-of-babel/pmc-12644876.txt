Neurocomputational mechanisms of social presence effects on instrumental learning
Authors: Chuangbing Huang, Yaner Su, Binjie Yang, Zituo Liu, Chencen Liu, Luansu Zhang, Li Li, Ran Zhou, Yue-Jia Luo, Chunliang Feng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSocial presence affects behavioral performance and cognition, producing both facilitation and inhibition. However, how it modulates distinct cognitive components to shape goal-directed behavior remains unclear. This study investigated the neurocomputational mechanisms underlying social presence effects on instrumental learning by integrating behavioral, computational, and electroencephalographic (EEG) analyses. Participants performed a probabilistic learning task alone or under observation. Results indicated that social presence improved learning performance, especially with high feedback validity. This social facilitation effect was associated with more flexible behavioral adaptations in response to performance monitoring, evidenced by changes in win-stay/lose-switch strategies and post-error slowing. EEG analyses revealed that social presence promoted reliance on internal performance monitoring while diminishing weighting of external task feedback, reflected by increased correct-related negativity amplitudes following correct actions and reduced theta power to feedback. Furthermore, computational modeling revealed that social presence enhanced choice perseverance and working memory. Together, these findings provide insights into neurocomputational mechanisms through which social presence shapes goal-directed behavior, highlighting interactions between socially induced motivation and task-specific cognitive processes including performance monitoring, choice perseverance, and working memory. This work advances the mechanistic understanding of social facilitation and informs interventions leveraging social contexts to optimize learning.Subject terms: Computational neuroscience, Working memory, Social neuroscience, Social behaviour, Motivation

Introduction

Human performance are sensitive to social presence of others, such as the mere presence or evaluative observation of others1–3. Social presence can either facilitate or hinder performance depending on task structure, a phenomenon documented as social facilitation or inhibition4,5. Although prior research has elucidated how social presence modulates isolated cognitive processes such as attention6,7, it remains poorly understood how social presence influences complex, goal-directed behaviors during instrumental learning that necessitate the integration of multiple cognitive systems8,9. Elucidating this relationship is essential for advancing theoretical models of social facilitation and inhibition, as the diverse processes engaged by instrumental learning may be differentially modulated by social presence.

Several theories explain social facilitation/inhibition. The drive-based account suggests social presence increases arousal and strengthens dominant responses, enhancing or impairing performance according to task difficulty10, but overlooks the cognitive processes involved. The attention-distraction account proposes that social presence directs attention to central cues but may impair processing of peripheral information1,6,11. However, the diversity of social facilitation/inhibition effects suggests more than one process is involved5,12,13. The motivation-based account thus argues that social presence raises evaluation concerns and motivation, shaping behavior by interacting with task-specific cognitive subcomponents3,14. This account suggests the influence of social presence varies across different cognitive processes. Instrumental learning paradigms, by engaging multiple processes such as performance monitoring, heuristics, and working memory8, offer a promising framework for examining these mechanisms.

In instrumental learning, participants needed to identify and implement responses that maximize positive feedback, which requires continuous performance monitoring15,16. This ongoing monitoring is typically evidenced by behavioral adaptations, most notably the use of win–stay and lose–switch strategies, wherein choices are repeated or altered contingent upon prior outcomes17,18. Additional proxies of performance monitoring include post-error slowing, reflected in increased reaction times subsequent to erroneous responses, which is thought to indicate the deployment of additional cognitive control following mistakes19,20. These compensatory behavioral adjustments provide quantifiable measures of the extent to which ongoing performance monitoring is incorporated to adapt subsequent responses21.

Moreover, electroencephalographic (EEG) correlates of performance monitoring—particularly error-related negativity (ERN) and correct-response negativity (CRN) after responses, as well as feedback-related negativity (FRN) after feedback—primarily index neural prediction error signals that arise when actual outcomes deviate from prior expectations, rather than reflecting the immediate desirability of outcomes22–24. Accordingly, the amplitudes of ERN/CRN and FRN are modulated by the predictive value of responses, defined as the extent to which a response reliably predicts task outcomes prior to the presentation of external feedback—that is, explicit task-related information indicating whether a response is correct or incorrect22,23. As response–outcome contingencies are acquired through learning, increases in predictive value are typically accompanied by augmented response-locked ERN and CRN amplitudes, indicative of enhanced internal performance monitoring. This enhancement is generally paralleled by attenuation of feedback-locked FRN amplitudes, reflecting a decreased reliance on external feedback. The reciprocal relationship between ERN/CRN and FRN amplitudes aligns with the reinforcement learning framework, where the prediction error signals propagate back in time from the external feedback to the response25. Importantly, the development of internal performance monitoring, as reflected by changes in ERN and FRN amplitudes, is contingent upon the validity of feedback15,26. Our study integrated EEG signals with behavioral adaptation metrics to elucidate how social presence modulates distinct aspects of performance monitoring.

Beyond performance monitoring, instrumental learning relies on cognitive heuristics and executive functions. People often use heuristics and are subject to biases, such as placing more weight on positive than negative feedback—showing a positivity bias in value updating27–29. Choice perseverance, or persisting with previous choices regardless of outcome, is also common30–32. While these biases may impede optimal decision-making, they can facilitate learning in stable environments by filtering out stochastic negative feedback associated with otherwise optimal choices27,33. Instrumental learning also engages working memory, which supports temporary maintenance and manipulation of limited information. In this context, working memory is necessary for retaining stimulus–response–outcome associations34, and learning is constrained by its capacity, as seen in delay and set size effects34,35. Taken together, these findings suggest that human instrumental learning relies not only on performance monitoring, but also engages a broader repertoire of cognitive heuristics and higher-order executive functions8. However, it remains unclear how social presence modulates these distinct component processes.

This study investigated the neurocomputational mechanisms of social facilitation and inhibition in instrumental learning, integrating one of the most enduring topics in social psychology with contemporary perspectives from cognitive psychology and neuroscience. We combined computational modeling, EEG, and behavioral analyses to probe how social presence alters different cognitive components of learning. Participants completed a probabilistic learning task under either social observation (with a confederate present) or in isolation. The task required acquisition of stimulus–response associations across three feedback validity conditions. In the high-validity condition (100%), perfect response–feedback contingencies enabled straightforward learning; the medium-validity condition (80%) introduced uncertainty and greater challenge; and the low-validity condition (52%) rendered reliable learning unattainable. These manipulations, consistent with prior studies15,22,36, allowed for a precise dissociation of the cognitive mechanisms underlying social presence effects.

At the behavioral level, major theories—drive-based, attention-distraction, and motivation-based—predict social facilitation in the high-validity condition, either due to heightened attentional focus on task-relevant cues or increased likelihood of correct dominant responses in simple tasks. For medium validity, the drive-based account predicts social inhibition due to enhanced task difficulty, while other accounts are less specific, as multiple cognitive processes might interact and exert opposing effects. In low validity, all accounts predict minimal influence of social presence, as feedback is unreliable for learning.

The manipulation of feedback validity also allowed for the dissociation between the predictive value of responses and immediate outcome processing. If social presence mainly heightened sensitivity to immediate feedback, we would expect to observe increased win–stay and lose–switch rates across all conditions, without altering ERN/CRN or FRN amplitudes. Alternatively, if social presence primarily facilitated learning and utilization of response predictive value, its behavioral effects would depend on the degree to which immediate feedback reflected actual response correctness. In high-validity, immediate feedback consistently reflected response correctness; thus, social presence would increase both win–stay and lose–switch rates as learning progressed. In medium validity, while positive feedback usually corresponded to correct responses, negative feedback was less indicative of errors; accordingly, social presence would enhance win–stay rates but attenuate lose–switch rates over time. Moreover, social presence was hypothesized to augment ERN while attenuating FRN in high- and medium-validity conditions, indicative of increased reliance on internal performance monitoring. In the low-validity condition, where the predictive value of responses could not be established through learning, social presence was not expected to modulate win–stay or lose–switch rates, nor EEG correlates of performance monitoring. Additionally, we examined the impact of social presence on theta-band oscillatory activity, as neural oscillations within the theta frequency range (4–7 Hz) have been robustly associated with the FRN component and are posited to reflect the neural processes underlying performance monitoring and feedback evaluation37,38.

Manipulating feedback validity may also help separate cognitive subprocesses using computational models. For example, working memory might be more involved in high- and medium-validity conditions, where participants need to retain learned stimulus–response mappings, but becomes negligible in low-validity conditions, where no reliable mappings can be learned34. The motivation-based account predicts that social presence would enhance the use of working memory and heuristics3, while the attention-distraction account predicts impaired working memory in the presence of others1.

In summary, by systematically manipulating feedback validity and social context, this study aimed to clarify how social presence modulates instrumental learning across behavioral and neurocomputational levels, advancing integration between social, cognitive, and computational neuroscience (Fig. 1).

Results

Model-free analysis

Accuracy

The generalized linear mixed models (GLMM) on accuracy showed a significant three-way interaction among feedback validity, learning stage, and social context (χ2(2) = 8.84, p = 0.012). Post-hoc comparisons revealed that audience presence enhanced accuracy compared to the alone condition, but only in the high-validity condition during the late learning stage (odds ratio ± s.e. = 2.18 ± 0.54, z = 3.15, p = 0.0099, Fig. 2a). No significant differences emerged between audience and alone contexts in other conditions (all p > 0.05, Fig. 2b, c). Stay/switch

The GLMM on stay/switch revealed a four-way interaction among feedback validity, learning stage, feedback of previous trial of the same stimulus, and social context (χ2(2) = 10.52, p = 0.005). In the high-validity condition, social presence (vs. alone) increased both win-stay behavior (odds ratio ± s.e. = 2.04 ± 0.44, z = 3.32, p = 0.011, Fig. 2d) and lose-switch behavior (odds ratio ± s.e. = 1.60 ± 0.26, z = 2.95, p = 0.036, Fig. 2g) in the late stage but not the early stage (all p > 0.05). In the medium-validity condition, social presence tended to reduce lose-switch behavior at the late learning stage (odds ratio ± s.e. = 0.70 ± 0.09, z = −2.73, p < 0.05 uncorrected, Fig. 2h). No significant differences emerged between audience and alone contexts in other conditions (all p > 0.05).

For the alone context, win-stay and lose-switch rates remained stable across learning stages regardless of validity (all p > 0.05). In contrast, in the social context, both win-stay (odds ratio ± s.e. = 1.68 ± 0.17, z = 5.08, p < 0.001, Fig. 2d) and lose-switch (odds ratio ± s.e. = 1.43 ± 0.19, z = 2.64, p < 0.05 uncorrected, Fig. 2g) increased with learning stage for high validity. In the medium-validity condition, win-stay behavior increased with learning stage (odds ratio ± s.e. = 1.36 ± 0.14, z = 3.02, p = 0.025, Fig. 2e), whereas lose-switch behavior decreased (odds ratio ± s.e. = 0.72 ± 0.075, z = −3.12, p = 0.020, Fig. 2h). A complete list of statistical results on stay/switch is presented in Supplementary Table 3.

RT

For the raw reaction time (RT) model, linear mixed models (LMM) analyses revealed an interaction between feedback validity and learning stage (χ2(2) = 11.03, p = 0.004, Fig. S4a), indicating that participants’ RTs decreased with learning stage in the low-validity condition (mean difference ± s.e. = −11.02 ± 4.31, z = −2.63, p = 0.026) but not in high- or medium-validity conditions (all p > 0.05). A complete list of statistical results on raw RT is presented in Supplementary Table 3.

For the immediate RT model, analyses revealed an interaction between social context and feedback of the immediate previous trial (χ2(1) = 9.40, p = 0.002, and Supplementary Fig. 4b), showing that negative feedback compared to positive feedback accelerated participants’ responses in the immediate next trial for the alone context (mean difference ± s.e. = −12.50 ± 3.37, z = −3.72, p < 0.001) but not for the audience context (mean difference ± s.e. = 2.10 ± 3.14, z = 0.67, p = 0.50). A complete list of statistical results on immediate RT is presented in Supplementary Table 3.

For the delay RT model, analyses with feedback of previous trial of the same stimulus did not reveal any significant effects (all p > 0.05). However, analyses with response correctness of previous trial revealed an interaction between social context and response correctness (χ2(1) = 6.36, p = 0.012, and Supplementary Fig. 4c), such that incorrect choices compared to correct choices slowed participants’ responses in the next trial of the same stimulus for the audience context more than for the alone context. A complete list of statistical results on delay RT is presented in Supplementary Table 3.

Model-based analysis

Model comparisons identified the hybrid reinforcement learning (RL) model (M7g, Supplementary Table 1) as the optimal model, incorporating asymmetric learning rates for positive and negative feedback (α0_pos, α0_neg), a forgetting process (f), a tendency to repeat previous choices (αCK, τCK), and feedback validity-dependent modulation of learning rate decay (θhig, θmed, θlow). The optimal model was further validated with absolute model fit, revealing that the action probabilities predicted by the model closely matched actual behaviors across contexts and feedback validity (Supplementary Fig. 5). These results confirmed that the winning model accurately captured participants’ decision-making patterns.

Statistical analyses of estimated parameters were performed based on the winning model. The learning rate following positive feedback exceeded that following negative feedback in both contexts (audience context: 95% highest density intervals (HDI): [0.08, 0.43], Fig. 3a; alone context: 95% HDI: [0.06, 0.34], Fig. 3b), demonstrating positivity bias in both conditions. However, the magnitude of this bias did not differ between audience and alone contexts (95% HDI: [−0.15, 0.31]). Regarding forgetfulness, the parameter f was higher in the alone context than in the audience context (95% HDI: [−0.41, −0.01], Fig. 3c), indicating greater forgetting of reward values when participants were alone. For choice perseverance, the parameter τCK was higher in the audience context than in the alone context (95% HDI: [0.29, 1.33], Fig. 3d), suggesting that participants in the audience context relied more heavily on Choice Kernel (CK) values when making decisions.

EEG results

CRN

For the CRN amplitudes (Fig. 4a), there was a significant main effect of learning stage [F (1, 36) = 5.60, p = 0.023, ηp2 = 0.14]. Pairwise comparisons showed that CRN amplitudes were significantly larger in the late stage (M = 3.59, SE = 0.67) compared to the early stage (M = 2.94, SE = 0.61, Fig. 5a). A significant main effect of feedback validity was also observed, [F (1.74, 62.59) = 10.77, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.23]. Pairwise comparisons showed that the high-validity condition (M = 3.82, SE = 0.70; p = 0.001) and the medium-validity condition (M = 3.45, SE = 0.63; p = 0.007) had significantly larger CRN amplitudes than the low-validity condition (M = 2.52, SE = 0.61, Fig. 5b), while the high- and medium-validity conditions did not significantly differ (p > 0.05). These findings indicate that CRN amplitudes increase as learning consolidates and are further enhanced by more valid feedback, highlighting the sensitivity of performance monitoring processes to both the learning stage and feedback reliability.

Moreover, a significant interaction between social context and learning stage was observed [F (1, 36) = 5.61, p = 0.023, ηp2 = 0.14], with larger CRN amplitudes in the late versus early stage in the audience context (late stage: M = 3.96, SE = 0.94; early stage: M = 2.67, SE = 0.86, p = 0.002, Fig. 5c), but no significant difference between stages in the alone context (late stage: M = 3.21, SE = 0.94; early stage: M = 3.21, SE = 0.86, p > 0.05). Exploratory post-hoc analyses revealed that the interaction between task stage and social context reached significance only in the high-validity condition [F (1, 36) = 9.29, p = 0.004, ηp2 = 0.21]. In the audience context, CRN amplitudes were significantly larger in the late stage (M = 4.99, SE = 1.01) than in the early stage (M = 3.03, SE = 1.01; p < 0.001), while no significant difference across stages was observed in the alone context (late stage: M = 3.66, SE = 1.01; early stage: M = 3.60, SE = 1.01; p > 0.05). No significant interactions were observed in the medium [F (1, 36) = 3.48, p = 0.070, ηp2 = 0.088] or low-validity conditions [F (1, 36) = 0.341, p = 0.563, ηp2 = 0.009].

No other main effects or interaction effects reached statistical significance (Supplementary Table 5), including the main effect of social context [F (1, 36) = 0.01, p = 0.934, ηp2 = 0.000], the feedback validity × learning stage interaction [F (1.60, 57.61) = 2.44, p = 0.107, ηp2 = 0.063], the social context × feedback validity interaction [F (1.74, 62.59) = 0.75, p = 0.458, ηp2 = 0.020], and the social context × feedback validity × learning stage interaction [F (1.60, 57.61) = 0.67, p = 0.483, ηp2 = 0.018].

ERN

For the ERN amplitudes (Fig. 4b), a significant main effect of learning stage was observed [F (1, 36) = 19.73, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.35], with more negative ERN amplitudes in the late stage (M = −0.09, SE = 0.54) than the early stage (M = 1.46, SE = 0.55, Fig. 5d). The main effect of feedback validity was also significant [F (1.94, 69.78) = 15.97, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.31]. Pairwise comparisons showed that the ERN amplitudes were more negative in the high-validity condition (M = −0.24, SE = 0.49, p < 0.001) and the medium-validity condition (M = −0.04, SE = 0.61, p < 0.001) compared to the low-validity condition (M = 2.33, SE = 0.67, Fig. 5e), while no significant difference was found between the high- and medium-validity conditions (p > 0.05).

Moreover, a significant interaction between feedback validity and learning stage was observed [F (1.97, 71.05) = 4.18, p = 0.02, ηp2 = 0.10], revealing that ERN amplitudes in the high-validity (early: M = 0.62, SE = 0.53; late: M = −1.09, SE = 0.64, p = 0.013, Fig. 5f) and medium-validity conditions (early: M = 1.27, SE = 0.63; late: M = -1.35, SE = 0. 70, p < 0.001, Fig. 5f) were more negative in the late stage compared to the early stage, whereas no significant difference was found in the low-validity condition between stages (p > 0.05).

No other main effects or interaction effects reached statistical significance (Supplementary Table 5), including the main effect of social context [F (1, 36) = 0.43, p = 0.517, ηp² = 0.012], the social context × feedback validity interaction [F (1.94, 69.78) = 0.24, p = 0.784, ηp² = 0.006], the social context × learning stage interaction [F (1, 36) = 0.11, p = 0.745, ηp² = 0.003], and the social context × feedback validity × learning stage interaction [F (1.97, 71.05) = 1.11, p = 0.334, ηp² = 0.030].

FRN

For the FRN amplitudes (Fig. 4c), a significant main effect of feedback validity was found [F (2, 84) = 5.51, p = 0.006, ηp2 = 0.12]. Post-hoc comparisons showed that the FRN amplitudes in the low-validity condition (M = −2.82, SE = 0.45) were more negative than in the high-validity condition (M = −1.36, SE = 0.45, p = 0.006). The medium-validity condition (M = −2.10, SE = 0.38) did not differ significantly from the other conditions (both p > 0.05).

No other main effects or interaction effects reached statistical significance (Supplementary Table 5), including the main effect of social context [F (1, 42) = 0.06, p = 0.816, ηp² = 0.001], the main effect of learning stage [F (1, 42) = 1.22, p = 0.276, ηp² = 0.028], the social context × feedback validity interaction F (2, 84) = 0.46, p = 0.632, ηp² = 0.011], the social context × learning stage interaction [F (1, 42) = 1.00, p = 0.323, ηp² = 0.023], the feedback validity × learning stage interaction [F (2, 84) = 0.89, p = 0.413, ηp² = 0.021], and the social context × feedback validity × learning stage interaction [F (2, 84) = 1.38, p = 0.258, ηp² = 0.032].

Correlational results

The correlations between behavioral performance (i.e., accuracy) and neurocognitive processes measured across multiple levels were examined.

For behavioral adaptations, the win-stay rates were positively correlated with accuracy in the high- and medium-validity conditions, but not in the low-validity condition (high: r = 0.959, q(FDR) < 0.001; medium: r = 0.897, q(FDR) < 0.001; low: r = 0.078, q(FDR) = 0.791, Fig. 7a). The lose-switch rates were positively associated with accuracy in the high-validity condition, negatively associated in the medium-validity condition, and showed no significant association in the low-validity condition (high: r = 0.781, q(FDR) < 0.001; medium: r = −0.410, q(FDR) = 0.021; low: r = −0.089, q(FDR) = 0.788, Fig. 7b). For the measures of post-error slowing, the prolonged RT following an error (vs. correct) response of the previous presence of the same stimulus were positively correlated with accuracy in the high- and medium-validity conditions, but not in the low-validity condition (high: r = 0.679, q(FDR) < 0.001; medium: r = 0.597, q(FDR) < 0.001; low: r = 0.036, q(FDR) = 0.953, Fig. 7c). In contrast, the prolonged RT following a negative (vs. positive) feedback of the immediate previous trial did not significantly correlate with accuracy across validity conditions (high: r = 0.356 q(FDR) = 0.095; medium: r = 0.247, q(FDR) = 0.331; low: r = -0.218, q(FDR) = 0.370, Supplementary Fig. 6a).

Discussion

Our results indicate that social presence enhances instrumental learning, particularly under the condition of high feedback validity, consistent with a social facilitation effect. This facilitation was accompanied by more flexible behavioral adaptations in response to performance monitoring, as evidenced by changes in win-stay/lose-switch strategies and post-error slowing. At the neural level, social presence was associated with progressive increases in response-locked CRN amplitudes, most prominently under the high-validity condition, as well as attenuated feedback-locked theta power under the high-validity condition. These neural modulations suggest that social presence promotes reliance on internal performance monitoring mechanisms while diminishing the weighting of external feedback. Beyond effects on performance monitoring, computational modeling further revealed that social presence enhanced both choice perseverance and working memory capacity. Taken together, these findings provide a mechanistic account of how social presence shapes goal-directed behavior by modulating multiple cognitive subprocesses, including performance monitoring, choice perseverance, and working memory.

Our findings demonstrate that social presence effects on instrumental learning are most pronounced in the high-validity condition, consistent with prominent theories of social facilitation and inhibition. Both drive-based and motivation-based accounts posit that social presence amplifies dominant responses, which are most likely to be correct in simple, highly valid contexts. Moreover, the motivation-based account further posits that social presence enhances the motivational salience of performance3. In the context of instrumental learning, heightened sensitivity to performance under social observation not only augments incentive motivation but also facilitates utilization of performance-relevant information39,40, thereby improving learning through more effective performance monitoring. The attention-distraction account offers a complementary explanation, suggesting that the presence of others can exogenously direct attentional resources towards salient task cues, such as feedback1. This attentional enhancement is particularly adaptive in the high-validity condition, where feedback serves as the most reliable cue of performance, in contrast to medium- or low-validity conditions, where feedback is less predictive. Crucially, beyond overt behaviors, it is only the motivation-based account that explicitly predicts interactions between socially induced motivational states and multiple task-specific cognitive subprocesses3,14. Our integrated analyses—spanning behavioral adaptation, neural dynamics, and computational modeling—provide empirical support for this mechanistic perspective.

First, our behavioral and EEG findings converge to demonstrate that social presence facilitates reliance on internal performance monitoring mechanisms, rather than merely enhancing sensitivity to immediate external feedback. In both high- and medium-validity conditions, the predictive value of responses increased, whereas the informational content of feedback diminished with learning22. Participants thus increasingly adapted their behavior based on internal monitoring signals rather than external cues. As such, changes in win–stay and lose–switch rates depended on the extent to which immediate feedback corresponded with actual response accuracy. Under the high-validity condition, feedback provides unequivocal information about performance accuracy, such that enhanced behavioral adjustments following feedback—manifested as increased win–stay and lose–switch rates—reflect effective monitoring and adaptive behavioral adjustment. Social presence might amplify vigilance and motivation to optimize task performance3, thereby further promoting this adaptive process. In contrast, under the medium-validity condition, positive feedback reliably reinforced adaptive behavior, but negative feedback was less diagnostic due to outcome stochasticity. Consequently, increased win–stay rates paired with reduced lose–switch rates in the presence of an observer support the hypothesis that social presence strengthens the reliance on internal performance monitoring rather than on immediate external feedback. Such refined monitoring reflects an enhanced capacity to buffer against stochastic outcome noise, sustaining stable and goal-directed behavior27,33. This pattern is consistent with our correlational analyses, which showed that in the high-validity condition, both win–stay and lose–switch rates positively predicted performance, whereas in the medium-validity condition, only win–stay rates were positively associated with performance and lose–switch rates were negatively related.

This behavioral profile was mirrored in our EEG findings. Consistent with the reinforcement learning framework22,23, we observed an inverse relationship between response- and feedback-locked components: response-locked CRN/ERN amplitudes were elevated in the high-validity condition, whereas feedback-locked FRN and theta power were more pronounced under low-validity. These EEG measures thus reflect the predictive value of responses, rather than the immediate processing of outcomes. Specifically, increased CRN/ERN amplitudes accompanied by diminished FRN/theta responses indicate a greater reliance on internal performance monitoring as opposed to processing of external feedback. As such, the enhancement of the CRN—a neural marker sensitive to the predictive value of internal monitoring—suggests that social observation augments the motivational salience of correct action–outcome contingencies, thereby consolidating accurate internal performance evaluation19,39,41. Consistent with this interpretation, our correlational analyses demonstrated that greater CRN amplitudes were associated with better behavioral performance. Similarly, attenuated feedback-locked theta power may reflect a reduced dependence on external feedback as internal representations become more reliable37,42. Together, the reciprocal modulation of the CRN and theta power by social presence is consistent with reinforcement learning framework, wherein the locus of predictive value—and thus monitoring resources—shifts from external feedback toward internal response evaluation22,23.

However, contrary to the predictions of the reinforcement learning framework, the expected interaction between feedback validity and learning stage emerged only for ERN amplitudes, not for other EEG measures. This discrepancy may be attributable to limitations inherent in the stage-wise analyses, which may not capture the nuances of participant-specific learning trajectories23. It is conceivable that EEG correlates of learning may have evolved at variable, individual-dependent stages during the task15, thereby obscuring putative stage-related effects.

Our results further revealed that social presence exerts a significant impact on post-error slowing. While participants responded more quickly following immediate negative feedback regardless of stimulus identity when alone, the presence of an observer was associated with prolonged response latencies specifically after committing an erroneous response to the same stimulus. Post-error slowing is widely regarded as an index of adaptive cognitive control, reflecting engagement of additional monitoring and adjustment processes in the aftermath of performance errors. This compensatory adjustment is hypothesized to reduce the likelihood of future errors by enhancing information processing and encouraging more cautious responding19,20,43. Our correlational findings support this interpretation by demonstrating that only prolonged responses following an erroneous response, rather than following negative feedback, predicted enhanced behavioral performance. This result underscores the instrumental role of internal performance monitoring mechanisms in behavioral adaptation. The observed amplification of post-error slowing under social observation therefore suggests that social presence heightens the internal detection of response errors and/or enhances the recruitment of cognitive control resources after errors, ultimately facilitating more effective behavioral regulation.

Second, our computational modeling results revealed that social presence increased reliance on choice perseverance—the propensity to repeat prior choices irrespective of their outcomes. Also termed decision inertia or status quo bias, choice perseverance constitutes a well-documented cognitive heuristic in human decision-making and learning30,44. It is generally regarded as an intuitive, automatic process, often shaped by arousal and affective states31,45. Accordingly, our findings support the drive- and motivation-based accounts arguing that social presence amplifies arousal or performance motivation, thereby reinforcing dominant response tendencies. Notably, while heightened choice perseverance may enhance performance in stable environments with consistent stimulus–action contingencies—as in the present paradigm—it could be maladaptive in volatile contexts where flexibility is required27,33. These results underscore the context-dependent utility of choice perseverance and highlight the need for future research to determine whether individuals can strategically override this heuristic in dynamically changing environments, as predicted by a motivation-based account of social facilitation14.

Third, our computational modeling results further revealed that social presence facilitated working memory processes, as evidenced by improved retention of previously acquired action values. While the contribution of working memory has historically been underappreciated in traditional reinforcement learning frameworks, a growing body of research now highlights its essential role in supporting goal-directed behavior34,35,46. Notably, this enhancement in working memory performance is uniquely consistent with the motivation-based account, which posits that social presence amplifies cognitive control engagement through increased motivations14. In contrast, the attention-distraction account would predict reduced availability of cognitive control resources under social observation. Consistent with our findings, prior studies have shown that social presence enhances other high-level executive functions, such as proactive cognitive control following errors19 and response inhibition during conflict resolution47,48. Taken together, our results suggest that social presence not only heightens the motivational significance of performance and cognitive heuristics but also high-level executive processes underlying instrumental learning, thus offering strong support for the motivation-based framework.

Several limitations of the present study warrant consideration. First, although each participant completed a substantial number of trials (n = 750), the sample size was relatively small for a between-subjects design. Replication in larger samples is therefore necessary to assess the robustness and generalizability of the current findings. Additionally, due to a programming issue, a subset of trials for one stimulus deviated from the intended feedback contingencies during the late learning stage. As a result, the data from these trials could not be meaningfully classified under any of the predefined experimental conditions and were thus excluded from the primary analyses. To ensure transparency, we report the results for this stimulus in the supplementary information, which indicate that participants’ choices remained consistent with reinforcement learning predictions. This experience highlights the importance of thorough quality control in experimental programming and should be taken into consideration when interpreting our findings.

Second, to our knowledge, this is the first study to examine how social presence modulates multiple cognitive subcomponents of instrumental learning by employing computational modeling. However, the current modeling approach focused solely on choice behavior, as is common in reinforcement learning research. Future work should employ more integrative modeling frameworks that jointly incorporate choice, reaction time, and EEG data to more fully characterize the underlying cognitive processes and their neural correlates49,50.

Third, further research is warranted to elucidate the contextual and individual factors that modulate the effects of social presence. The present design examined the effect of evaluative presence rather than mere physical presence, consistent with prominent social facilitation theories that identify evaluative presence as a critical mediating factor3,51. However, recent research suggests that evaluative and physical presence may exert qualitatively distinct influences on behavior and neurocognitive processes52, underscoring the need for future studies to directly compare these social contexts in human instrumental learning paradigms. In addition, this study did not assess or control for individual differences in social anxiety. Such differences may result in varied levels of stress or concern about negative evaluation when participants are observed, potentially affecting both behavioral and neural outcomes. Incorporating standardized assessments of social anxiety in future research would help clarify whether, and to what extent, these individual differences modulate the impact of social presence on learning. In sum, both contextual and individual variability may elicit distinct motivational states, such as perceived challenge or threat, in response to social presence. This heterogeneity may contribute to the divergent findings in the literature, wherein social presence has been shown to yield both facilitative and detrimental effects1,14,19.

Moreover, task structure critically determines the informational value of performance feedback. In cognitive control tasks, negative outcomes might be more informative, signaling the need to upregulate control19, whereas in instrumental learning paradigms, positive outcomes may be more valuable for reinforcing adaptive behavior by confirming action-outcome contingencies33. These differences in informational value may underlie the selective effects of social presence on error-related neural responses (e.g., ERN) in cognitive control tasks39,53 but on correct-related neural responses (e.g., CRN) in reward-related tasks40,54,55, as observed in the present study.

Despite these limitations, our study advances a novel neurocomputational framework for understanding social presence effects on instrumental learning. The current findings enhance our understanding of how social presence shapes goal-directed behavior by elucidating the interplay between socially induced motivation and task-specific cognitive subcomponents, including performance monitoring, choice perseverance, and working memory. These findings contribute to both the theoretical advancement of social presence research and the development of effective intervention strategies.

Methods

Participants

A total of forty-seven participants (mean age = 22.91 years, SD = 2.01, 23 males) were recruited in the experiment. Among them, twenty-three participants (mean age = 22.17 years, SD = 2.14, 11 males) were randomly assigned to the alone context, and twenty-four participants (mean age = 23.63 years, SD = 1.61, 12 males) were assigned to the audience context. The power sensitivity analysis was conducted with the “pwr” R package. Using a 2 × 2 × 3 mixed-measures analysis of variance, effect sizes in the moderate-to-large range (η2p ≥ 0.097, within-subject factors: 2 × 3, between-subject factors: 2, α = 0.05, 1 − β = 0.80) could be reliably detected given the current sample size56. All participants were right-handed, had a normal or corrected-to-normal vision and did not suffer from neurological or psychiatric medical problems. This study has been approved by the Institutional Review Board of Beijing Normal University, Beijing, China, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. All ethical regulations relevant to human research participants were followed.

Stimuli

The stimulus set was collected from a standardized picture database57 and presented against a dark gray background. A total of 36 pictures were selected from six categories (six per category), including clothes, vehicles, furniture, electric appliances, fruits and musical instruments.

Task procedure

Participants were randomly assigned to one of two contexts for a probabilistic learning task. In the audience context (Fig. 1a), they were informed that another participant (a confederate) had arrived early and needed to familiarize themselves with the procedure. With the participant’s permission, the confederate sat behind them to observe their performance. This design thus examined the effect of evaluative presence rather than mere physical presence2,3,58. Importantly, no verbal or nonverbal interaction between the participant and the confederate was allowed. In the alone context (Fig. 1b), participants completed the task alone. In both contexts, the experimenter left the room after the task began, stating he needed to prepare for the next participant in another room and would return when the experiment was about to end. Before participants started, the experimenter deliberately closed the door. Thus, aside from the confederate’s presence, all other conditions were identical across contexts.

In the learning task, participants completed a two-choice probabilistic learning task in which they responded to different pictures by pressing either the left or right key as quickly and accurately as possible. They learned the association between each picture and its corresponding keypress through trial and error, with these associations remaining consistent throughout the experiment. The task included three feedback validity conditions—high, medium, and low—with two pictures assigned to each condition and counterbalanced for the correct response. In the high-validity condition, also referred to as the deterministic condition, feedback was always valid, meaning that for one stimulus (A), pressing the left key consistently resulted in positive feedback, while the other stimulus (B) always required the right key for positive feedback. In the medium-validity condition, or probabilistic condition, feedback was valid in 80% of trials and invalid in 20%. Specifically, pressing the left key for stimulus C (or D) resulted in positive feedback 80% (or 20%) of the time and negative feedback in the remaining trials, while pressing the right key reversed these probabilities. In the low-validity condition, which approximated a chance level, positive feedback occurred in 52% of trials when pressing the left key for stimulus E and the right key for stimulus F.

The learning task comprised five blocks (Fig. 1c), each featuring a distinct set of six images corresponding to stimuli A through F, as described above. Each image was presented 25 times per block, yielding a total of 150 trials per block. To assess learning dynamics, each block was further subdivided into early and late learning stages, with 25 trials per feedback validity condition in each stage. This subdivision enabled a more fine-grained analysis of learning trajectories under varying feedback contingencies22. Trials across validity were presented in a pseudorandomized order within each stage. The randomization was constrained to ensure that no more than three consecutive trials involved the same stimulus. Notably, in the late learning stage, feedback validity for stimulus E was inadvertently set to 0% for the left response due to a coding error, such that participants consistently received negative (or positive) feedback for left (or right) responses. Data from this condition were therefore excluded from subsequent analyses. The data for this condition was presented in the Supplementary Results (see also Supplementary Fig 1). Prior to the main task, participants completed a 75-trial training block to familiarize themselves with the procedure. The images used in this training phase were distinct from those employed in the experimental blocks.

Each trial (Fig. 1d) began with a fixation cross displayed for 400 to 600 ms, followed by the presentation of a stimulus at the center of the screen for 500 ms. The response window was adaptively adjusted15, starting at 800 ms and varying in 100-ms increments between 300 and 900 ms, depending on the proportion of time-out trials relative to executed trials. This adaptive mechanism ensured that all participants experienced a comparable proportion of time-out trials, thereby providing an equivalent learning environment for acquiring stimulus-response associations from feedback. During the response window, participants were instructed to respond by pressing either the left or right key. Following their response, feedback was displayed for 700 ms to indicate performance on the current trial. Following each response, feedback was provided in the form of a check mark to denote positive outcomes and a cross to signify negative outcomes. If no response was registered within the allotted time, a “Too slow” message would be displayed. The average proportion of time-out trials was below 7% in each condition and did not differ between the alone and audience contexts (see also Supplementary Results & Supplementary Fig. 3). Stimulus presentation and data collection were conducted using Psychtoolbox.

Model-free analysis

Model-free analyses were conducted using LMMs or GLMMs in R (version 4.1.0), employing the “lme4” package59. To determine the optimal random-effects structure, a combined maximal-model and backward-selection approach was applied, following previous recommendations60–62. Initially, a full model with the maximal random-effects structure was specified, and a more parsimonious model was identified using the rePCA function from the “RePsychLing” package and likelihood ratio tests, as outlined by Bates et al. 60. P values were obtained through likelihood ratio tests comparing the full model, which included the effect of interest, against a reduced model without it63,64. Post-hoc comparisons were conducted using the “emmeans” package65, with p values adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Holm–Bonferroni method66 unless stated otherwise. Marginal effects were extracted using the “ggeffects” package67, and visualized with the “ggplot2” package68.

Accuracy

Participants’ accuracy was modeled using feedback validity (high, medium, low) and learning stage (early, late) as within-subjects variables, and social context (alone vs. audience) as a between-subjects variable.

Stay/switch

To determine whether participants learned the stimulus-response associations via performance evaluation and how learning processes were influenced by feedback validity and social context, we examined the extent to which feedback from the previous trial influenced participants’ choices in the next round18. We coded a choice on a given trial as “stay” (1) if it matched the choice made for the same stimulus in the previous trial, and as “switch” (0) if it differed. The resulting stay/switch variable was then analyzed using a GLMM, with feedback validity, learning stage, feedback from the previous trial (0 = lose, 1 = win), and social context as fixed variables. This analysis employed a regression framework to determine whether feedback reinforced choices, specifically whether positive feedback (versus negative feedback) led participants to repeat their choice for a given stimulus, depending on feedback validity, learning stage, and social context.

RT

Three LMMs were constructed to analyze RTs. The first LMM (hereafter referred to as the “raw RT model”) represents a traditional analysis that does not account for the influence of previous feedback on the current response. In this model, participants’ RTs were modeled with feedback validity, learning stage, and social context as fixed variables. RT data from seven participants (2 males and 2 females in the alone context, and 2 males and 1 female in the audience context) were excluded from the analyses due to technical issues in behavioral data collection.

The second LMM (hereafter referred to as the “immediate RT model”) examined the effect of feedback from the previous trial on the RT of the immediately subsequent trial, regardless of the stimulus identity. In this model, learning stage, feedback from the immediate previous trial, and social context were included as fixed variables.

The third model (hereafter referred to as the “delay RT model”) investigated the effect of feedback from the previous trial for the same stimulus on the RT in the next trial. In this model, learning stage, feedback from the previous trial of the same stimulus, and social context were included as fixed variables. Additionally, another delay RT model was constructed by replacing feedback with the correctness of the previous response (0 = incorrect, 1 = correct), aiming to examine the effect of response correctness in the previous trial on RT in the subsequent trial for the same stimulus.

Similar to the analyses of stay/switch, the immediate and delay RT models were designed in accordance with prior empirical evidence indicating that participants tend to slow down their responses following negative feedback/action and speed up their responses after positive feedback/action42. In other words, adjustments in both choices (i.e., stay/switch) and response latencies reflect learning-related adaptations to prior feedback and response correctness.

Model-based analysis

Model construction

Computational modeling was employed to dissociate the distinct processes underlying instrumental learning and capture latent variables that are not immediately observable69. The current study extended conventional reinforcement learning models by incorporating additional cognitive components, including asymmetric learning rates, choice perseverance, and working memory. Notably, our complementary model-free analyses implicated that all of these factors contributed to participants’ choice behaviors in the instrumental learning task (see also “Supplementary Methods and Results”, Supplementary Figs. 2 and 3).

We adopted a stage-wise model construction approach to examine how participants learned the correct response for each picture stimulus based on choices and outcomes, and how task performance was modulated by feedback validity in both the audience and alone contexts70. In each stage, we added or modified a specific component of the model and compared the enriched model to the best model from the previous stage using the LOOIC, which estimates point-wise out-of-sample prediction accuracy based on the log-likelihood evaluated at the posterior simulations of the parameter values for the specific fitted Bayesian models71. LOOIC is on the deviance scale, where lower values indicate better model fits. As with other information criteria, differences in LOOIC values greater than 3 are typically interpreted as positive evidence, with differences between 4 and 7 indicating strong evidence, and those exceeding 10 considered very strong evidence72.

In the first stage, we compared the Rescorla–Wagner (RW) model (M1) to several variants of the RW model that respectively incorporate components of asymmetric learning rates (M2), working memory (M3), and choice perseverance (M4).

Parameter estimation

Parameter estimation was conducted using hierarchical Bayesian analysis (HBA), which leverages Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling to simulate posterior predictive distributions78. Compared to traditional methods like Maximum Likelihood Estimation, HBA has gained increasing adoption due to its ability to account for both group and individual differences simultaneously79. By drawing individual parameters from group-level distributions, this approach enhances estimation power while maintaining a constrained relationship between individual- and group-level parameters80,81. HBA was implemented using the “RStan” package in R, which provides an interface to Stan79,82. Sampling was performed with three MCMC chains, each consisting of 4000 iterations (2000 warm-up and 2000 for sampling). The trace plot and R-hat values (Gelman–Rubin R-hat statistics) was applied to be the criterion of the convergence of sampling which was standard-compliant in the current study with the R-hat values of all parameters are close to 1.0 and smaller than 1.0183.

Parameters were estimated separately for the audience and alone contexts. To compare them, we computed the differences in group-level parameters and assessed their 95% HDIs. If the 95% HDI did not include zero, the difference was considered meaningful84.

Model validation

The one-step-ahead prediction approach was implemented to assess the absolute model fit of the optimal model85. For each participant and trial, a combination of parameter values was drawn from the individual’s joint posterior distribution. These values, along with the observed choice and outcome in the current trial, were used to update the expected values of both options. The probability of selecting each option in the next trial was then computed based on these updated values. This prediction process was repeated 6000 times for each trial of each participant, and the resulting action probabilities were averaged.

EEG acquisition

Electroencephalogram (EEG) data were recorded by the NeuroScan system, with 64 Ag/AgCl active electrodes sampled at 500 Hz. The M1 electrode was used as the online-recording reference. The impedance of all electrodes was kept below 10 kΩ. Eye movements (electro-oculogram, EOG) were monitored by four electrodes which were placed above and below the right eye and on the outer canthi of the two eyes. The amplified band-pass of EEG and EOG data was 0.05–100 Hz.

EEG analysis

Preprocessing

EEG data were analyzed using EEGLAB86 in the Matlab environment (R2016a, MathWorks). Continuous EEG data were band-pass filtered between 0.1 and 30 Hz. Bad channels were interpolated using the Spherical algorithm in EEGLAB. Next, the data were segmented separately to collect the response-locked and feedback-locked ERPs. The filtered data were segmented between −500 and 1000 ms from the onset of feedback for the analysis of the FRN. The average activity from −200 ms to the onset served as baseline. Moreover, epochs were extracted from −800 to 300 ms relative to the response onset and baseline-corrected with the first 100 ms of each epoch. The independent component analysis (ICA)86 with the square mixing matrix method was applied in each data. The components corresponding to eye movement and muscle artifacts were removed. Epochs above 100µV were rejected for they contained artifacts. Finally, the data were re-referenced to the average of the left mastoid and right mastoid.

Time-domain analysis

For ERP analysis, the CRN/ERN locked to responses15,87, and FRN locked to feedback were the components of interest55,88,89. The preprocessed data were then averaged separately for each condition and each participant. The resulting average amplitudes of each component were analyzed with a mixed ANOVA using SPSS 21.0 (IBM, Somers, USA) with a threshold of p < 0.05 (two-tailed), with the social context (alone vs. audience) as a between-subjects factor and learning stage (early vs. late) and feedback validity (high, medium, low) as within-subjects factors.

The CRN/ERN was measured as the mean amplitude during −50 ms to 50 ms at FCz for correct and incorrect responses, respectively90,91. Data from eleven participants were excluded from the analysis of CRN/ERN. Seven (2 males and 2 females in the alone context and 2 males and 1 female in the audience context) of them were due to mistakes in collecting reaction times, and four (2 males and 2 females in the audience context) of them were rejected for less than half the number of trials left after pre-processing.

For FRN, the difference waveforms between positive and negative feedback were computed. Specifically, the FRN was measured using a peak-to-peak method87,92,93, that is, the difference of the most negative value within 250–350 ms and the most positive value within 150–250 ms at FCz. If the peak-to-peak value of the FRN was non-negative, the amplitude of the FRN was considered to be 0 µV92. The difference waves were then calculated by subtracting the value of the positive feedback from the value of the negative feedback. Data from four participants (1 female in the alone context and 2 males and 1 female in the audience context) were excluded from the analysis of the FRN for less than half the number of trials left after pre-processing.

Time-frequency domain analysis

For oscillation analysis, the theta band (4–7 Hz) was examined given its established role in performance monitoring and its modulation by social presence19,38,40. A short-time Fourier transform with a fixed 200 ms Hanning window was performed for each preprocessed EEG epoch and a time-frequency distribution was obtained, which ranged from −500 to 1000 ms (in 2 ms interval) in the time domain and from 1 to 30 Hz (in 1 Hz interval) in the frequency domain. The resulting spectrogram represented the signal power at each time-frequency point as a joint function of time and frequency. These data were baseline-corrected by subtracting the average over −300 to −200 ms relative to the onset of feedback94. The activity of the theta band was captured in the 300–400 ms from the feedback onset. Both were obtained from electrode FCz because the topographical maps showed that the largest activation occurred at the fronto-central region and peaked at FCz. Similar to the FRN component, differences between negative and positive feedback were calculated. These difference frequency activities for the theta band were analyzed with a mixed ANOVA with the social context (alone, audience) as a between-subjects factor and learning stage (early, late) and feedback validity (high, medium, low) as within-subjects factors.

Exploratory correlational analyses

To explore associations between behavioral performance (i.e., accuracy) in the instrumental learning task and cognitive subprocesses at multiple analytical levels (i.e., behavioral adaptations, computational parameters, and EEG metrics), partial correlations (Person’s r) were computed. Social context was included as a covariate in all analyses to control for potential confounding effects arising from group differences. Multiple comparisons were addressed using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure, which provides a balanced approach to controlling false positives while preserving statistical power95.

Statistics and reproducibility

Power sensitivity analysis conducted with the “pwr” package56. Statistical analyses were performed using multiple computing environments. In R environment, we conducted model-free analyses with (generalized) LMMs (lme4 package) and model-based analyses with hierarchical Bayesian inference (RStan package)59,79,82. EEG data were preprocessed and analyzed in MATLAB using EEGLAB 86. In SPSS 21.0, the mixed ANOVAs were applied to behavioral and EEG data. Additionally, exploratory partial correlations were conducted with FDR correction95. The alpha level was set at 0.05 for all statistical tests and all were two-tailed unless otherwise specified. Graphs display data distributions with individual data points, means, and error bars representing 95% CI.
