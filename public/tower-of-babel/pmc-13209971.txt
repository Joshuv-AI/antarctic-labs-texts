Comparison of the effect of standardized patient-based training and video-based training on the skill of measuring the level of consciousness among nursing students: A randomized controlled trial
Authors: Sajad Zare Garizi, Faezeh Soltanian, Nazanin Sabagh Nezhad Yazd, Tannaz Faregh, Mostafa Javadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Ensuring accurate assessment of patients’ health is paramount in nursing interventions. The Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) plays a crucial role in evaluating patients’ conditions and is deemed a fundamental clinical skill for nursing teams. However, errors in GCS assessment can significantly impact patient care, particularly as nursing students often lack sufficient training in assessing consciousness levels.OBJECTIVE:This study aimed to compare the effectiveness of standardized patient-based training and video-based training (VBT) on nursing students’ ability to assess the level of consciousness.METHOD AND MATERIALS:This randomized controlled trial study included nursing students from the fourth semester in 2023. Seventy students were selected through convenience sampling and randomly allocated to two groups (n = 35 per group). Group 1 received VBT, while Group 2 received training with standardized patients. The skill of assessing consciousness levels was evaluated using the Objective Structured Clinical Examination method. Data analysis was performed using SPSS V22 statistical software (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences, SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA).RESULTS:The mean score of the verbal response assessment in the standardized patient-assisted education group was 3.42 ± 0.84 and that in the video-based training (VBT) group was 2.85 ± 1.09, which was statistically significant (P = 0.025). Additionally, the total test score was significantly higher in the standardized patient-assisted training group (13.21 ± 1.49) compared to the VBT group (12.15 ± 2.22) (P = 0.032).CONCLUSION:Training with standardized patients significantly improves nursing students’ ability to assess consciousness levels compared to VBT. This approach aligns with health policy benchmarks by promoting safe, experiential learning methods, ultimately enhancing psychomotor skills while minimizing risks to real patients.

Introduction

Traumatic brain injury contributes to worldwide death and disability more than any other traumatic insult. Most morbidity and mortality happen because of traumatic brain injury according to epidemiological data.[12] The level of consciousness is the sensitive and reliable indicator of the patient’s neurological status to be evaluated in clinical assessment.[3] Although there are numerous tools to determine the level of consciousness, the first and most common standardized neurological instrument is the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS).[3] The GCS is a neurological scale that strives to provide a reliable, objective method of recording a person’s conscious status for both initial and follow-up patient assessment via observation of the best eye-opening, verbal, and motor responses.[34] Even beyond trauma, the GCS is used routinely to monitor the clinical care of patients with virtually any condition as long as it affects the nervous system directly or indirectly.[5] Regarding the broad usability of the tool across different clinical situations, it is imperative for all nurses and clinicians alike to be familiar and conversant with it since they may need to perform a GCS on a patient at any given time.[5]

Notwithstanding the simplicity and objectivity of the GCS, the validity and usefulness of its scores require accurate knowledge of the tool and ability to apply it in a clinical situation. Inadequate knowledge would consistently impact negatively on the care of patients in emergency circumstances.[5] Therefore, it is very important that every nurse working in areas needing critical care has enough knowledge to assess and intervene appropriately to be able to communicate any change in a patient’s condition for multidisciplinary intervention,[2] but unfortunately, actual research shows that when nurses conduct the GCS in a mentoring system, they are inconsistent and erroneous.[4] The results of many studies reveal the poor knowledge of nurses and other clinicians about this tool.[5] On the other hand, some researches demonstrate the good basic theoretical knowledge of the tool among nurses and physicians, though they were not able to integrate their knowledge with clinical practice.[25]

One of the major problems of nursing education is the lack of coordination between theory and practice. Nursing students encounter difficulties in the practical implementation of theoretical knowledge, and this is a problem seen on an international scale.[6] Traditional teaching methods are often cited as a primary reason for this shortcoming. To address this, nursing students must be provided with opportunities to bridge theory and practice, rehearse skills, and enhance their competencies. As a result, innovative teaching strategies, such as simulation and virtual technologies, are increasingly being adopted to improve both basic and advanced nursing skills.[7]

Simulation is an experiential learning process that provides a safe environment for learning while preventing patient harm.[8] Standardized patients (SPs) allow learners to simulate real-life clinical scenarios in a standardized manner.[9] Although SPs have been widely used in teaching programs for medical students since 1960s, their use in nursing education is not as widespread.[7] Generally, an SP is an individual who is adequately trained to imitate a patient’s set of symptoms and provide verbal and written feedback to learners, providing students concrete opportunities to develop the skills required for practicing clinicians such as deeper diagnostic reasoning, physical examination, and communication skills.[79] It is a strategy for adult learning, emphasizing the importance of experiential learning and building on the principle that adult learners bring knowledge to learning situations and are goal- and self-directed.[10] The use of SPs has been shown to enhance cognitive, affective, and psychomotor skills, as well as communication abilities, learning motivation, satisfaction, and clinical competence, with the most significant impact observed in the psychomotor domain.[7] Thus, simulation methods, particularly SPs, are key to preparing nursing students for the dynamic and evolving clinical environment.[7]

In light of the COVID-19 pandemic, the demand for e-learning methods has surged. However, the shortage of trained instructors and limited teaching time pose challenges to effective education.[111] Video-based learning has emerged as a potential solution, offering an engaging and flexible resource that learners can access repeatedly at their convenience. While some studies suggest the benefits of video-based learning,[1] further research is needed to establish its effectiveness conclusively. Given the importance of the abovementioned issues and the lack of research comparing the effectiveness of standardized patient-based training (SPT) and video-based training (VBT) in teaching nursing students to assess consciousness levels, this study was conducted to address this gap.

Method and Material

Study design and setting

This randomized controlled trial study was conducted in Nursing and Midwifery School in Yazd, Iran, from September to December 2023.

Study participants and sampling

All fourth-term nursing students of the Nursing and Midwifery School in Yazd, Iran, formed the statistical population of the study. Sample size determination was based on the study by Jang and colleagues.[12] Using the formula below and considering a confidence level of 95%, a test power of 80%, and a subject attrition rate of 10%, 35 individuals were included in each group using convenience sampling.

The inclusion criteria for the study included completing 80% of the assigned units according to the school’s scheduling program and willingness to participate in the study. Nonparticipation in a similar training course during the past year was considered as an exclusion criterion. Also, absenteeism from theoretical and practical training sessions more than once, nonparticipation in the Objective Structured Clinical Examination (OSCE) exam to participate in the study, and the occurrence of any unexpected event affecting life during the research period were considered as dropout criteria.

Nursing students were approached for consent to participate in the study, and necessary explanations were provided regarding the approximate duration of the research, how the research would be conducted, its objectives and conditions, and the possibility of being assigned to one of the test or control groups.

Following the acquisition of written and informed consent from eligible participants, a comprehensive list was compiled. Utilizing the RANDBETWEEN function in Microsoft Excel (version 14.00 for Windows), participants were randomly allocated into two distinct groups.

For all participants, theoretical basics were provided using PowerPoint, which lasted 20 min. Then, in Group 1, VBT was provided, and in Group 2, training with a standardized patient-based was separately conducted for each group. Further details of the training methods are provided below.

Training method in the video-based group

Development of the educational video

To create the educational video, the research team conducted a comprehensive review of the GCS assessment process and designed a detailed implementation scenario. A nursing instructor collaborated with a trained individual to enact the scenario, and the preliminary video was recorded as follows: The nursing instructor provided a step-by-step explanation of the GCS assessment process, while the trained individual simulated motor, verbal, and eye responses.

The GCS assessment focused on evaluating patient responses to stimuli in three categories: motor, verbal, and eye responses. Each possible response state was demonstrated systematically, accompanied by detailed explanations. For instance, in the motor response category, the trained individual simulated various states, including obeying commands, localizing pain, withdrawing from pain, abnormal flexion, extension to pain, and unresponsiveness. This approach allowed students to gain familiarity with diverse response patterns. A similar methodology was employed for verbal and eye responses.

Additionally, three practice scenarios featuring unique combinations of motor, verbal, and eye responses were created and recorded. The video content underwent editing and finalization to ensure clarity and consistency before being utilized in the study.

Implementation of the educational video

Participants were divided into three groups, each consisting of 11–12 students, to maintain an optimal instructor-to-student ratio. The finalized video demonstrating GCS assessment was presented to each group. Students were encouraged to ask questions, which were addressed by the instructor to enhance understanding.

Afterward, the practice scenarios were shown, and students were instructed to record their GCS assessments on note cards. A volunteer from each group was invited to share their answers with their peers, fostering group discussion. Students were encouraged to identify and correct any inaccuracies in the assessments collaboratively. Finally, they were informed that the educational video would be made available to them upon completion of the study.

Training method with the assistance of the standardized patient

Selection and training of the standardized patient

The process of training and standardizing the standardized patient involved five stages. Four individuals served as active SPs, selected from fourth-term nursing students, male, with no medical background.

Their training included explaining the study objectives, evaluating the performance of the standardized patient, initial information about GCS, scenario simulation design, scenario execution by the standardized patient, evaluation and corrective feedback, scenario execution by the standardized patient, and practicing the scoring method by the examiner for mastering the skill of mimicking the patient, verbal feedback to students, and more. Training with the assistance of the standardized patient

In the standardized patient training approach, participants were divided into three groups of 11–12 students to ensure an optimal instructor-to-student ratio. Practical training sessions were held in the Clinical Skills Center of the School of Nursing and Midwifery. At the beginning of each session, students were briefed on the rules for interacting with SPs and the session’s objectives. They were explicitly instructed to avoid engaging in unnecessary conversations with the standardized patient.

The GCS assessment training commenced with the guidance of a standardized patient. The three components of the GCS—motor, verbal, and eye responses—were systematically reviewed. Each response category was demonstrated step-by-step by the standardized patient, with detailed explanations provided by the instructor to ensure clarity for the students.

For hands-on practice, four stations were prepared, each featuring one standardized patient. Upon entering a station, the instructor would invite a volunteer from the group to perform a GCS assessment on the standardized patient. The standardized patient acted according to a pre-prepared scenario. Meanwhile, other students observed and independently recorded their GCS scores on note cards. After the assessment, the group engaged in a structured discussion to analyze and compare their evaluations.

Each case assessment and discussion was allocated five min, allowing for a total of 20 min across the four stations. The session concluded with a 10-min wrap-up, during which the instructor addressed any remaining questions and provided a final summary.

Data collection tool and technique

The instrument of this study included a checklist of demographic information for nurses (age, sex, marital status) and a checklist of consciousness level assessment skills based on the GCS criterion.

To develop the checklist, the research team first drafted the content of the scenarios and questions. Relevant materials were gathered from reference books and related articles. The draft was then sent via email to ten experts (four faculty members, two head nurses from the neurosurgical invasive care unit (ICU), four ICU nurses, and two emergency nurses, all holding a master’s degree and with at least 5 years of experience). After reviewing their feedback and making the necessary revisions, the final questionnaire, consisting of three questions and four scenarios, was prepared.

To assess the reliability of the scoring for the four checklist stations, 20 seventh-semester nursing students participated in an OSCE. Each station was evaluated by two trained examiners. The inter-rater reliability was calculated to be 0.96. The total score for each individual ranged from 0 (minimum) to 15 (maximum).

Skill stations of consciousness assessment by OSCE method

Each scenario was evaluated through a structured scoring system. The examiner recorded each student’s scores on a checklist. Each scenario consisted of three components: eye response, motor response, and verbal response. A score of 1 was assigned for each correct response, while incorrect responses received a score of 0.

For the fifth station, which included three specific questions, a score of 1 was awarded for each correct answer and 0 for incorrect answers.

For each individual, the total score was calculated as follows: the score obtained from visual assessment (ranging from 0 to 4), the score obtained from verbal assessment (ranging from 0 to 4), the score obtained from motor assessment (ranging from 0 to 4), and the score obtained from questions (ranging from 0 to 3). Thus, the total score for each individual ranged from 0 to 15.

Ethical considerations

All research stages followed the Helsinki Declaration and ethical statements of the Ethics Committee of Yazd University of Medical Sciences. This study was approved on 5/10/2023 by the Ethics Committee of Yazd University of Medical Sciences (ethics code: IR.SSU.REC.1402.010). Subsequently, it was registered in the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials system with the code IRCT2023081405914, and the update date was 23/8/2023. Written and informed consent was obtained from all participants. Additionally, research samples were free to participate or not participate in the study.

Statistical analysis method

The collected data were entered into the SPSS V 22 statistical software (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences, SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA). The normality of the data distribution was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Data analysis was conducted through descriptive statistics, including frequency distribution tables, mean, and standard deviation, as well as inferential statistics, such as the independent t-test. A significance level of 0.05 was applied for all statistical analyses.

Discussion

The present study aimed to compare the effects of SPT and VBT on the skill of measuring the level of consciousness among nursing students performed in Yazd collage of nursing and midwifery. Its result delineates that the total score and score of evaluating verbal response, as a component of the GCS scale, among students who were taught with SP method were higher than those who received educational content on videos, which is statistically significant, but in other areas of the GCS eye and motor response, no significant statistical difference is observed.

As it is indicated that students enjoy the simulation learning experience and perceive educational benefits of it, many studies were conducted to determine if these outcomes transfer knowledge, skills, and attitudes in the clinical setting.[8] In this regard, Ren and his colleagues conducted a study to evaluate effects of a standardized patient-based simulation in anaphylactic shock management for new graduate nurses, and subsequently, students reported a better understanding of their roles and responsibilities during the rescue of a patient with anaphylactic shock in this method besides the six competencies assessed on the clinical competency evaluation list of the rescue for anaphylactic shock improved in all participants after the first training session and still remained high in the second training 1 month later. Generally, they illustrated simulated scenarios were practical and effective as a method to teach new graduate nurses.[13] Although they did not evaluate the efficacy of VBT method, the findings of using SPs in teaching clinical skills are consistent with the present one. Another study which compared effects of standardized patient-based simulation education and clinical practice education for preoperative nursing care for high-risk pregnant women showed that clinical judgment, nursing performance competency, communication ability, and learning satisfaction, except for problem-solving ability, were significantly higher in the SP method group.[14]

The findings of this study demonstrate that SPT is more effective than VBT in enhancing nursing students’ skills in assessing the level of consciousness. These results are consistent with those reported by Saber et al. (2022),[15] who showed that simulated patient-based approaches significantly improve both practical and ethical competencies among medical students. The interactive nature of SPT, which provides real-time feedback and hands-on practice, likely accounts for its superiority over passive learning modalities such as VBT.

Similarly, Javidi-Sarafan et al.[16] reported that standardized patient-based education yielded greater improvements in midwives’ knowledge and clinical practice compared to traditional lecture-based methods. Their findings align with our results, highlighting the role of active engagement and experiential learning in fostering deeper knowledge acquisition and skill retention. Additionally, Norouzi and co-workers emphasized the effectiveness of SPT over team-based learning in enhancing midwifery students’ knowledge in specialized clinical scenarios. Their study reinforces the argument that SPT bridges the gap between theoretical instruction and practical application, making it particularly effective for developing clinical competencies. Taken together, these findings underscore the pivotal role of SPT in nursing and medical education, suggesting it as a superior approach for cultivating clinical proficiency when compared to passive or traditional training methods.[17] These results are all aligned with the current study findings. Actually, because of replicating real clinical situations, prompting students to solve clinical problems as well as making key decisions, this approach is considered an effective educational method.[13] The setting where the simulation is performed promotes learning, and, through the repetition of a skill, this skill becomes fully understood and clear; besides, through choosing rare or unusual examples or scenarios, a student becomes acquainted with a wide range of interventions and skills; thus, the effectiveness of education is enhanced. Furthermore, through reducing anxiety and fear, this technique minimizes errors, increases the satisfaction of students from the educational process, and enhances their self-confidence, self-esteem, and comfort in skill performance.[6] Regarding these points, it can be defined how the SP-based method as compared to the other techniques could lead to abovementioned results among learners.

On the other hand, there are some investigations which depict that although using the SP method had positive impacts on the performance of the students in post-test as compared to the pretest situation for learning various skills, no significant differences were identified in comparison with other teaching methods. It implies that the skill of the participants improved regardless of the method. These findings are discordant with the present study which can be due to inadequate explanation, limited discussion, and practice time,[715] and different natures of educated content- counseling skills are less tangible than psychomotor skills and need more attempts to be learned properly.[18] Although in the present research we denied to evaluate student feedbacks, some studies reveal that students taught by the SP method had a higher level of satisfaction and self-confidence. They stated this method was more attractive and enjoyable, and they would like to attend more of such trainings.[710]

Advantages of learning through audio-visual aids have been known since Dale’s landmark publication and its widely quoted “cone of experience or learning pyramid”, which include that this method is better than reading or hearing a lecture; the recorded video can be viewed multiple times and whenever desired, also, when the video shows examples over patients (real or dummy), it becomes easy to understand and visual memory helps it to be retained.[1] According to this, Bajaj and his colleagues carried out a study to teach the GCS scale assessment via videos to surgical residents. They reported that there was a significant difference in all three responses of GCS for first-year residents and for the motor response in second- and third-year residents (P < 0.05), which may be because the second- and third-year residents already had a prior basic knowledge during their short neurosurgical rotatory training. As it is presented in several studies that the motor score in the GCS scale is the best predictor of the patient outcome and this score improved after watching videos in all residents, it could be concluded that to attain a certain level of basic training, GCS can be learned through videos and it could make a significant difference in learning GCS for the residents, irrespective of their previous training, even though it did not achieve 100% accuracy for the participants.[1] Another investigation in this field showed an improvement in GCS scoring from 14.7% to 64% for emergency medical service providers through the video method, but the individual components of GCS were not assessed separately.[19] Moreover, Rana and his co-workers in their study with the aim of evaluating the effect of video tutorial training on improving pharmacy students’ knowledge and skills about medication reconciliation demonstrated that this visual teaching method significantly improved the total knowledge scores about medication reconciliation and more students performed better in the post-video assessment quiz and students were able to identify more discrepancies after watching the videos, which indicates that students were able to apply their gained knowledge into practice. The majority of students were satisfied with the overall training process, and they believed that the educational video made them understand the concept of medication reconciliation. Furthermore, the use of video tutorials as a teaching strategy had the advantage of being available at any time for them.[20] These results are in the contrary with our present study that could be rooted in the fact that these studies only represented a one group pre-post study design that has evaluated the incorporation of video education without the comparator group, but our study compared this method with an SP-based learning approach and did not evaluate pre-post situation; also, different video contents and target groups can affect the results as well. In the Bajaj study, scoring motor response of the GCS noticeably improved among all residents after watching videos, even though in our study, assessing verbal response experienced a significant statistical difference, and it was higher in the SP-based learning group not in VBTs; besides, scoring other components of the GCS scale did not show any significant differences.

It is affirmed that to achieve evidence-based practice as the basis of clinical decision making, it is essential for researchers to report their study powers because merely relying on significant or nonsignificant statistical difference between SP methods and other approaches might hide the real clinical significance of such results.[7] The main strength of this study is that we compared the findings of SP-based simulation and VBT methods with each other in comparison with other similar researches in teaching assessment of level of consciousness with the GCS scale; besides, based on our present knowledge, no specific investigation has been conducted to compare these two methods at all.

Limitations and recommendations

This study has several limitations. First, it was not feasible to blind participants and researchers to the intervention groups due to the distinct nature of the training methods (standardized patient-based vs video-based). This limitation may have introduced potential bias in outcome assessments. Second, the VBT content faced challenges related to lower engagement and reduced appeal, which could have influenced participants’ motivation and learning outcomes. To address this, nursing instructors and standardized patient trainers were involved in the video production process to improve its quality and relevance. However, despite these efforts, the intrinsic differences between the two training modalities remained a limitation that was beyond the researchers’ control.

Conclusion

Given the critical importance of nurses’ knowledge and skills in using the GCS scale to assess consciousness levels and apply critical thinking to interpret findings—thereby safeguarding patients and preventing avoidable errors—adopting innovative educational strategies is imperative. The findings of this study highlight the practical and effective role of SP-based simulation in enhancing clinical and psychomotor skills. This approach aligns with health policy benchmarks by ensuring safe, hands-on learning and bridging the gap between theory and practice. Furthermore, SP-based training can be integrated into nursing curricula at undergraduate and graduate levels, as well as for continuing education and certification programs for registered and advanced practice nurses, supporting lifelong learning and improved clinical outcomes.
