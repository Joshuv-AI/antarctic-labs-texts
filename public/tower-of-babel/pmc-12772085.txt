Associations between mental health and sociodemographic and work-related factors in Canadian SME workers: an observational study
Authors: Simon Coulombe, Marie-France de Lafontaine, Joël Gagnon, Tyler Pacheco, Carol-Anne Gauthier
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSmall and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) employ a considerable proportion of the workforce in many countries. However, little research has specifically explored how SME employees in Canada fare in terms of mental health. This study aimed to (1) draw a detailed portrait of employees’ mental health using positive (i.e., flourishing, work well-being) and negative (i.e., psychological distress, burnout) mental health indicators, (2) examine how these indicators’ scores vary according to sociodemographic (e.g., gender) and work-related (e.g., SME size) factors, (3) examine workers’ perceptions of the COVID-19 pandemic’s impact on their general mental health and work well-being, and (4) identify mental health profiles of SME workers based on these mental health indicators, and determine which sociodemographic and work-related factors predict profile membership.MethodsTwo thousand five hundred SME workers (18+, living in Canada, working at least 14 h/week at a private SME) who were members of a large online panel participated in an online survey, in which they answered mental health measures (e.g., personality disorder, burnout, post-traumatic stress, flourishing).Results54.70% of SME workers reported having at least one mental health issue, 40.38% met the cut-off for psychological distress, and 22.13% met the criteria for burnout. Workers also reported moderately high work well-being and flourishing. Less than half of workers reported a negative impact of COVID-19 on their general (47.07%) and work-related (33.41%) mental health. Proportionally more older workers reported a perceived negative or neutral effect of COVID-19 on their general and work mental health; more women reported a perceived negative impact of the pandemic on their general mental health. Four latent mental health profiles were found. Individual- (gender, age) and organizational-level (SME size, economic sector) factors predicted mental health scores and profile membership.ConclusionsThis study provides a comprehensive overview of Canadian SME workers’ mental health. Management should prioritize primary, secondary, and tertiary occupational health interventions in SMEs to increase work well-being and flourishing, with particular attention given to at-risk workers.

Background

Small- (1–99 workers) and medium-sized (100–499 workers)1 enterprises (SMEs) play a vital role in the Canadian economy [1]. These enterprises employ a large proportion of the labour force in many countries. For example, in the United States, businesses with less than 500 employees account for 46% of private sector employment [2]. Similarly, companies with less than 250 employees employ 68% of workers in Mexico [3] and 64% of workers in the European Union [4]. In Canada, where the current study was conducted, SME workers represent around two-thirds of the Canadian workforce [5] and approximatively 98% of all businesses employing Canadians [6].

Despite SMEs’ apparent role in the Canadian economy, there is very little research specifically devoted to drawing a clear portrait of their workers’ mental health [7] nor of the different interventions aimed at promoting it [8]. Large Canadian population-wide statistical surveys (e.g., see [9]) related to mental health seldom consider employers’ business size. Such lack of research is surprising given that employee mental health is of particular importance from a public health perspective and should be examined for multiple reasons. First, before the pandemic, high rates of mental health problems were reported in many working populations [10–12] and this phenomenon seems to have been exacerbated by the COVID-19 pandemic [13, 14]. This global pandemic generated important changes in employment (e.g., temporary or permanent job loss) and the ways work is organized (e.g., teleworking; [13, 15]). Secondly, mental health concerns have been linked to decreased physical health [16] and increased health care expenditures [17], as well as to significant economic costs for employers (e.g., absenteeism, presenteeism; [18]). Thirdly, it is increasingly recognized that psychosocial risk factors in the workplace, such as inadequate workload, low autonomy, and poor support and recognition, pose risks to employees’ mental health. Prevention of these risks is now included in many countries’ (e.g., Canada, France, Finland, United Kingdom, Dominica) occupational health and safety (OHS) legislation [19, 20]. Finally, workplaces provide a strategic setting for mental health interventions across primary, secondary, and tertiary prevention levels. This is particularly true for small- and medium-sized enterprises, which engage a large portion of the population, and thus offer broad reach and impact.

There is a recognized lack of adequate studies with regards to mental health in SMEs [21, 22], and particularly SME employees, as most of the few studies conducted in the SME context have focused on their owners [23]. Interestingly, there are only a few peer-reviewed articles (e.g., theoretical, reviews, empirical; [22, 24, 25]) that have focused on intervention approaches developed to protect or enhance workers’ mental health in SME settings. However, to our knowledge, there are virtually no post-pandemic studies that provide a clear descriptive portrait of SME employees’ mental health. A descriptive portrait of SME workers’ mental health is essential to better guide the design and implementation of workplace mental health interventions in SMEs as they can orient interventions and mental health support that fit SME workers’ specific issues and needs. Non-peer-reviewed Canadian evidence suggests that mental health support is made available by only a small proportion of workplace organizations [26]. More research on SME workers’ mental health is important to help guide the development and wider scale implementation of mental health promotion and support activities.

It may be difficult to effectively determine the mental health needs and resources in SMEs, due in part to the fact that most of the research on workplace mental health is being directed towards large enterprises [27] and to the misconceived perception that SMEs are only smaller versions of larger businesses [28]. However, mental health needs and resources could be different in SMEs. While studies on this topic are limited in number, there are a few international studies suggesting that exposure to psychosocial risk factors (e.g., high job demands, low job autonomy), leading to poorer mental health, may vary based on business size, although findings are inconsistent across the very few retrieved studies (see review by [21]). In SMEs, the resources available may greatly differ from those present in larger organizations [23]. Due to lower human and financial resources, small businesses could be less likely to include mental health initiatives or their leaders may think that such initiatives do not have their place in their work environment, when compared to larger businesses [8, 29]. A study [30] showed that small businesses, compared to larger ones, were less likely to offer comprehensive health promotion programs. Such differences in the offering of health programs may also contribute to amplifying negative mental health outcomes among employees; however, we know very little about SMEs’ programming with regards specifically to the mental dimensions of health.

Further, we have access to very little research on how mental health challenges in SMEs may vary depending on sociodemographic characteristics, which limits our capacity to tailor interventions to different workers (e.g., based on gender and age groups). According to research conducted with larger non-SME workforces, specific groups’ (e.g., immigrants, women, racialized individuals) mental health tends to be worse [31–33]. With regards to work and employment, there is some evidence that the type of tasks and weekly working hours could influence the prevalence of mental health problems, although this finding varies between employment sectors [34]. However, whether sociodemographic factors and work characteristics are related to contrasting mental health issues in SME workers more specifically is yet to be determined.

To our knowledge, only one study has provided a mental health portrait of Canadian SME workers. In the study, levels of workplace factors known to influence mental health varied between SMEs and larger businesses [7]. Whereas psychological demands were lower in very small and small (vs. larger) enterprises, employees in very small (vs. larger) enterprises tended to have higher supervisors’ support [7]. However, this study was conducted in the province of Quebec ten years ago, meaning there is a need for a more recent, Canada-wide investigation exploring SME workers’ mental health and how it differs according to various sociodemographic (e.g., gender, age) and work-related (e.g., enterprises’ economic sector) factors. Given the repercussions and changes stemming from the COVID-19 pandemic (e.g., telework, social isolation), an in-depth analysis of this population’s mental health is required.

Moreover, in previous research and in organizational interventions, mental health has often been evaluated or targeted by focusing mostly on reducing negative outcomes, such as symptoms related to stress, distress, fatigue, burnout, anxiety and depression [35, 36], which does not offer a complete representation of mental health. To help address this conceptual limitation, it is useful to refer to Keyes’ validated Two Continua Model [37]. The first dimension of this complete mental health model concerns the symptom severity of mental health problems, ranging from an absence of symptoms to the presence of severe symptoms. The second dimension is a continuum of positive mental health, ranging from a state of languishing (stagnation) to flourishing (self-actualization; [37]). Positive mental health encompasses manifestations of positive well-being, including positive emotions (e.g., life satisfaction), positive individual functioning (e.g., autonomy, environmental mastery, personal growth) and positive social functioning (e.g., social integration, social contribution; [37]). The advantage of Keyes’ framework is that it takes into account that people suffering from mental health problems could nevertheless present some level of psychological well-being, and vice versa. In workplace-specific research, flourishing, and more generally, positive mental health, has been shown to protect against work stress’ detrimental effects on psychological distress [38]. Despite calls for considering flourishing (or equivalent concepts) alongside mental health disorders in occupational mental health research and intervention [24, 39–41], it still remains underexplored in workplace settings, including SMEs. While it has been shown with general and clinical samples that flourishing does not always present along with low levels of mental health issues, positive and negative indicators of mental health have, to our knowledge, rarely been considered together with an explicit focus on workers or work (e.g., rare simultaneous consideration of positive and negative work-specific indicators of mental health, such as burnout and work well-being).

A holistic conceptualization of workers’ mental health [42] should focus on indicators of how workers feel in general, such as depressive symptoms and life satisfaction, as well as specifically with regards to their work, such as burnout and job satisfaction [43]. There is evidence that work-related and general mental health are reciprocally and prospectively associated [44], such that personal life and work-related well-being influence each other through a spillover effect; thus, understanding worker’s mental health necessitate a careful consideration of dimensions of how workers feel both in general and in their work lives. Further, from a public and occupational health perspective, adopting a holistic approach is increasingly seen as important for employers and societies, recognizing that health issues that are not necessarily caused by work factors can nevertheless impact workers’ productivity (e.g., presenteeism/absenteeism), with ripple effects for organizations and populations [45]. Also, the work environment constitutes a setting in which people spend a large amount of their time, thus it offers a great opportunity (i.e., easiness to reach people) for mental health promotion broadly conceived, beyond the prevention of disorders specifically caused by work. In the current economic and social context, employers increasingly leverage the offering of health initiatives and the creating of healthy environments that promote overall mental health to help retain and attract employees [46, 47]. This goes beyond simply preventing mental health disorders, to consider workers’ mental health holistically (e.g., workers’ flourishing).

Despite the importance of holistic approaches integrating positive and negative indicators of general and work mental health, we know relatively little about the different patterns (or profiles) that these various indicators may yield among (SME) workers. Such a portrait is necessary to identify organizational- and policy-level actions that would address multiple aspects of mental health.

Objectives

We aimed to (1) provide an overview of the mental health of SME employees in Canada by individually using positive and negative mental health indicators, (2) compare how scores on these mental health indicators differ based on sociodemographic factors (age, gender) and work-related variables (SME size, economic sectors), (3) evaluate workers’ perceptions of the COVID-19 pandemic’s effect on their general mental health and work well-being, and (4) identify holistic profiles of SME workers’ mental health based on the simultaneous consideration of multiple positive and negative mental health indicators, as well as which sociodemographic factors and work-related variables, when considered together, characterize each profile.

Methods

Participants and procedure

Participants were recruited in February 2022 from the LEO web panel administered by Léger Marketing and were invited to complete a confidential online survey on the Qualtrics survey platform. To participate, workers were required to live in Canada, be at least 18 years old, be employed by a private (for- or not-for-profit) SME (≤ 499 employees), and work at least 14 hours per week for that organization. Participants first completed a consent form, then answered screening questions related to the inclusion criteria. If eligible, they could access the remaining of the survey. The participants completed sociodemographic, work-related and mental health measures (described below), presented to them in French or English. All the measurement scales had been previously translated and validated in both languages. Upon survey completion, participants received compensation in the form of redeemable points from LEO. Two thousand five hundred people were recruited with the goal of obtaining a large sample with a diversified representation of genders, Canadian regions and SME sizes. This study was conducted in compliance with the Canadian Tri-Council Policy Statement on the ethical conduct for research involving humans and was approved by Université Laval’s cross-faculty research ethics committee (2021 − 445).

Measures

The following measures were selected for their wide utilization and established psychometric properties [42, 48–50]. Scales were presented to participants in French or English depending on their first language.

General mental health scales

Psychological distress

Anxiety and depression symptoms were assessed using either the original English version or the validated French version of the Patient Health Questionnaire-4 (PHQ-4; [48, 51]), which contains four items. The PHQ-4 has two subscales, each composed of two items assessing either depression (e.g. “feeling down, depressed, or hopeless”) or anxiety (e.g., “feeling nervous, anxious, or on edge”) symptoms that occurred in the last two weeks. Items are rated on a four-point Likert scale, ranging from 0 (Not at all) to 3 (Nearly every day). In the present study, the internal reliability of the overall scale was good (α = 0.88). Based on recommendations from a recent national survey, a PHQ-4 score greater or equal to 6.00 on the overall scale was used to detect psychological distress that may warrant a clinical evaluation [52].

Flourishing

Six items (e.g., “I lead a purposeful and meaningful life”, “I am optimistic about my future”) from the Flourishing Scale (FS; [42]) were used to evaluate the participants’ perceived flourishing in different areas, which are in line with several components of Keyes’ conceptualization of mental health [53]. The French version of these items was administered to French-speaking participants [54]. The statements were rated on a seven-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (Strongly disagree) to 7 (Strongly agree). The FS had excellent reliability (α = 0.93). Each participant’s average score across the items was used in the present study to evaluate flourishing levels.

Mental health at work scales

Work well-being

The Index of Psychological Well-Being at Work – Thriving at Work Subscale (IPW-T) contains five items (e.g., “I find meaning in my work”, “I have a great sense of fulfillment at work”) assessing the “perception of accomplishing a significant and interesting job that allows one to fulfill oneself as an individual” ([49], p. 670). As with the other scales, either the French or the English version of the scale was administered [49]. The items were rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (Not at all in agreement) to 4 (Completely in agreement). The internal reliability for this subscale was excellent (α = 0.94). Each participant’s average score across the IPW-T’s items was used in this study to evaluate work well-being levels.

Burnout

Burnout symptoms were assessed using items from the Shirom-Melamed Burnout Measures (SMBM; [50]). The items (e.g., “I have difficulty concentrating”, “I feel physically drained”) are rated on a seven-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (Never) to 7 (Always). To limit the survey’s length, six items were selected based on their highest loadings, according to findings reported by Sassi’s and Neveu’s (2010) validation of the French version [55]. These six items were then administered to the participants. The scale had excellent internal reliability (α = 0.90). To determine if the participants were meeting the criteria for burnout, participants’ scores across the scale’s items were averaged, and a cut-off score of 4.40 was used [56].

Other items

Two questions were developed by the research team to evaluate participants' perceived impact of COVID-19 on their general mental health (i.e., “How is the COVID-19 pandemic, and everything it has caused, currently affecting your mental health?”) and work well-being (i.e., “How is the COVID-19 pandemic, and everything it has caused, currently affecting your sense of well-being at work?”). Each question was rated on a seven-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (Very negatively) to 7 (Very positively). These two scores were trichotomized, with scores from 1 to 3 indicating a negative impact of the pandemic, scores of 4 indicating a neutral impact, and scores from 5 to 7 representing a positive impact. A questionnaire was developed by the research team to assess sociodemographic (e.g., age, gender), as well as work-related variables (size and economic sector of the workers’ employing SME). We also provided a list of 15 mental health problems and disorders (e.g., depression, anxiety disorders, substance abuse) and asked participants to self-report if they were experiencing any of these issues.

Data analysis

SPSS (v.28; [57]) was used to prepare data, as well as conduct the descriptive statistics and main analyses for this study, in addition to Mplus (v.8.4, [58]) for latent profile analysis. There was no missing data on mental health indicators’ scale scores (psychological distress, flourishing, work well-being and burnout). To better represent Canadian SME workers, data was weighted (based on Government of Canada’s statistics) on several factors: age, gender, main regions (Atlantic Provinces, British Columbia, Ontario, Quebec, and Prairies) of Canada’s working population, as well as proportion of people working in businesses of different sizes [59]. For workers living in the Quebec region, their first language and whether they lived in the metropolitan area of Montreal-Laval was considered in the weighting to account for sociolinguistic and cultural realities specific to this province. This province has a particular situation, given that its only official language is French; however, many people in the larger Montreal-Laval region speak English as a first language. Although participants from all sectors were included in the overall analysis when considering the sector of employment variables, we focused on the following economic sectors for the sake of that specific analysis: construction, finances, health, manufacturing, professional services and retail. These sectors were selected because they were highly frequent in the sample (ns ≥ 165). In analyses regarding this specific variable, workers from each of these sectors were compared to workers from all the other sectors (considered as a whole).

Data were screened to verify normal distribution for the continuous scores (i.e., the IPW-T and FS scores). The skewness and kurtosis coefficients, as well as graphical representation of the data, confirmed that these scores were normally distributed. For the first objective, descriptive statistics were calculated. Based on Keyes’ conceptualization of mental health, a t-test was also conducted post-hoc to assess participants’ mean FS score based on whether they met the cut-off for psychological distress or not [48]. Regarding the second objective, t-tests, ANOVAs, and chi-square tests were conducted to compare scores on the mental health indicators according to the sociodemographic and work-related variables. ANOVAs were followed by post-hoc analyses using the Bonferroni correction. To answer the third objective, descriptive statistics and chi-square tests were conducted. Regarding the last objective, mixed-indicator (continuous and categorical) latent profile analysis (referred to as latent profile analysis or LPA for brevity hereafter) was conducted multiple times, each model with an increasing number of profiles, until fit indices suggested the right number of profiles was reached. Profile indicators used were flourishing (continuous variable), work well-being (continuous variable), psychological distress (binary, based on cut-off mentioned above) and burnout (binary, based on cut-off mentioned above). Once the number of profiles was determined, the R3STEP auxiliary function in Mplus was used to examine which demographics and work-related covariates (considered simultaneously) were associated with profile membership. Adding demographics and work-related covariates through the R3STEP function does not change the profiles’ meaning, but involves a built-in multinomial logistic leading to examining the associations of covariates with the profiles [60]. Using R3STEP for determining profile membership is advantageous to other functions (e.g., BCH, DCAT) as it considers all the predictor variables simultaneously [61]. Pacheco et al.'s [62] reporting is used to communicate the LPA results.

Results

Sample characteristics

As shown in Table 1, the mean age of the sample was 44.42 years old (SD = 14.00). Most participants were anglophone (68.85%), Caucasian (70.53%), and Canadian-born (80.10%). Men were more likely than women to work in the manufacturing (χ2 = 26.83, p <.01) and construction (χ2 = 40.14, p <.01) sectors; women were more likely than men to work in the health (χ2 = 68.83, p <.01) and finance (χ2 = 3.95, p <.05) sectors. No gender differences were observed in the professional services (χ2 = 1.52, p >.05) and retail (χ2 = 1.88, p >.05) sectors (findings not presented).

Mental health portrait

Most participants (54.70%) self-reported living with at least one mental health problem. The reported issues were the following: burnout (25.16%), anxiety disorder (22.71%), depression (19.53%), addiction/substance use (8.25%), eating disorder (6.77%), attention deficit disorder (with or without hyperactivity; 6.25%), post-traumatic stress disorder (5.73%), obsessive-compulsive disorder (3.63%), compulsive gambling (3.04%), personality disorder (1.66%), bipolar disorder (1.64%), psychosis (1.37%), autism spectrum disorder (1.33%), schizophrenia (1.25%), and other mental health problem(s) (2.83%). More than a third of participants (40.38%) met or exceeded the cut-off score on the PHQ-4. The mean FS score was 5.12 out of 7.00, meaning the participants had a moderately high flourishing level. The participants below the PHQ-4 cut-off (n = 1,490) had a significantly higher mean flourishing score (5.51) than those who met or exceeded the PHQ-4 cut-off (n = 1,010; M = 4.56; t = 18.20, p <.01). Of the surveyed workers, 22.13% met the burnout cut-off on the SMBM. The mean IPW-T score was 2.59 out of 4.00, meaning the participants had a moderately high work well-being level.

Comparisons based on sociodemographic and work-related factors

Age

The chi-square tests revealed significant age differences in the rates of psychological distress based on the established PHQ-4 cut-off (χ2 = 109.76, p <.01; see Table 2). A higher proportion of workers aged from 18 to 24 years met or exceeded this threshold when compared to each of the other age categories (p <.05), except for the workers aged 25 to 34 years. Most workers aged 18 to 24 years (56.71%) were classified as having psychological distress. Further, the workers aged from 25 to 34 were more numerous to meet or exceed the PHQ-4 cut-off when compared to those aged 45 to 54 years, 55 to 64 years, and 65 years or older (p <.05). With regards to burnout, no significant difference on the SMBM was observed between women and men (χ2 = 2.68, p =.10). In summary, women (vs. men) reported better scores on positive mental health indicators, but were also more likely to report psychological distress.

Workers’ perceptions of the COVID-19 pandemic’s effects

A high proportion of workers (47.07%) perceived that the COVID-19 pandemic had a negative impact on their general mental health, while 13.64% perceived it had a positive impact. The remaining 39.29% of workers perceived the pandemic had a neutral effect in that matter.

Examining these results based on the sociodemographic and work-related variables, a smaller proportion of workers aged 18 to 24 years (37.06%) reported a negative impact of the pandemic on their mental health than that of workers aged 25 to 34 years (48.98%) and those aged 45 to 54 years (50.25%; p =.02). Proportionally more women (vs. men) reported a negative impact of COVID-19 on their mental health (50.69% vs. 44.33%, respectively), while a higher proportion of men (vs. women) reported a neutral impact of the pandemic (41.57% vs. 36.45%; p =.01). Workers in the finance sector were marginally less numerous than those not working in that sector to perceive a neutral impact of the pandemic on their mental health (31.58% vs. 39.84%, respectively; p =.05). No difference was observed for SME size (p =.46), nor for the professional services (p =.12), manufacturing (p =.49), retail (p =.40), construction (p =.53) or health (p =.80) economic sectors.

Additionally, 33.41% of workers perceived a negative impact of the pandemic on their work well-being, while 47.20% reported a neutral impact and 19.39% a positive impact. Workers aged 18 to 24 years were proportionally more numerous (24.88%) than those aged 55 to 64 years (15.28%) to report a positive impact of the pandemic on their work well-being (p =.04). No difference was observed for gender (p =.15), SME size (p =.34), nor any of the economic sectors (ps ≥ .18).

Mental health profiles

Profile number identification

Latent profile analysis was conducted six times, each model with an increasing number of profiles (Table 6). Model building halted after the model with six profiles was conducted, as the VLMR and ALMR statistics were not (marginally) significant. Following Pacheco et al. [62] procedure based on Ram and Grimm’s [63] protocol, the relative indices of fit (AIC, BIC, SSA-BIC, CAIC) were first investigated. As shown in Fig. 1, these relative indices of fit decreased across the models, indicating improved fit [64, 65]. Models 2, 3, and 4 show a relatively steep decrease in the indicator values. No steep decreases were present after model 4. These results provided support for retaining the two- and four-profile models. Next, supporting their retention, the VLMR and ALMR for models with two, three, and four profiles significantly fit the data better than models with one fewer profile. Lastly, only model 4 exceeded entropy recommendations (≥ .80; [66]), indicating confidence in classifying workers in one profile versus the others. Although model 3 had an almost acceptable entropy, model 4 was chosen as the final model, given its theoretical advantages (i.e., uncovering a profile, not found in the 3-profile model, that represents a unique reality experienced by a proportion of workers).

Profile interpretation

Based on the final model, Fig. 2a contains the standardized means on the flourishing and work well-being indicators for each profile; Fig. 2b contains the proportion of workers in each profile that meet the cut-offs for psychological distress (≥ 6.00) and burnout (≥ 4.40). The y-axis in Fig. 2a represents the relative score of each profile against the overall sample’s score, which is denoted by zero (0). Scores greater than 0 indicate that workers within the focal profile are higher than the overall sample on the dependent variable; scores lower than zero indicate that workers within the focal profile are lower than the overall sample on the dependent variable. In the description below, the terms “better,” “best,” “worse,” and “worst” are used to indicate workers’ mental health relative to the overall sample.

Profile 1 contained most workers (52.20%). Unlike the other profiles, workers within this profile have relatively better flourishing and work well-being scores than the overall sample. Moreover, compared to the other profiles, Profile 1 has the least number of workers who exceed the psychological distress (15.70%) and burnout (7.70%) cutoffs. With this in mind, Profile 1 represents the best profile a worker can be a member of. Profile 2 contains 6.56% of the sample. Workers in this profile have relatively worse flourishing and work well-being scores than the overall sample. These workers’ work well-being scores are the worst across all of the profiles. Most workers in this profile met the cutoffs for psychological distress (88.90%) and burnout (59.10%). Compared to the other profiles, Profile 2 had the highest proportion of workers meeting these cutoffs. Thus, Profile 2 represents the worst profile a worker could be a member of. Profile 3 contained the fewest workers (3.16%). Unlike other profiles, Profile 3 had a mixed (i.e., non-congruent) direction of results. Workers in this profile had relatively worse flourishing scores and relatively higher work well-being scores than the overall sample. This profile’s flourishing scores were the worst across all four profiles; the profile’s work well-being scores were the best across all four profiles. Of those within the profile, 25.90% and 20.00% of workers exceeded the cutoffs for psychological distress and burnout, respectively. Profile 4 contained 38.12% of the sample. This profile is similar to Profile 2 in that workers’ flourishing and work well-being scores were relatively lower than the overall sample, and a considerable number of people also exceeded the psychological distress (65.40%) and burnout (34.50%) cutoffs. However, the flourishing and well-being scores of these workers, as well as the proportion of workers who exceeded cutoffs, were better than those of Profile 2, suggesting it is a “moderate” profile.

Discussion

This study’s first objective was to provide a comprehensive portrait of Canadian SME workers’ positive and negative mental health. Most SME workers reported one or more mental health issues; workers also reported moderately high general and work-related positive well-being. Second, we aimed to compare how mental health in SMEs varied based on sociodemographic and work-related factors. Older workers, as well as those in smaller enterprises and the health sector reported better mental health. Results regarding women were mixed. Third, we evaluated workers’ perceptions regarding the impact of COVID-19 on their general mental health and work well-being. Slightly less than half of the workers perceived a negative impact of COVID-19 on their general mental health and about a third perceived a negative impact on work-related mental health. Workers in older age categories were more likely to report that COVID-19 negatively impacted their general mental health; workers in older age categories were less likely to report that COVID-19 had a positive impact on their work well-being. More women reported a negative impact of COVID-19 on their general well-being. Lastly, we aimed to identify latent profiles of SME workers’ mental health. LPA indicated four profiles (i.e., realities) representative of SME workers’ mental health. Most workers were members of a prospering profile. Women tended to be members of the best mental health profile or a profile characterized by slightly maladaptive mental health and work well-being scores; retail workers and those in medium-sized enterprises were more likely to be in the worst profile. Older workers were likely to be in the best or mixed mental health profile.

Mental health portrait and realities

The reported mental health portrait illustrates that Canadian SME workers experienced moderately high general and work-related positive well-being despite enduring mental health concerns. Interestingly, depression (19.50% vs. 5.40%) and anxiety (22.70% vs. 4.60%) prevalence rates are much higher in our sample than in working-age Canadian adults in another recent study [67]. The current study took place during COVID-19 (towards the end of the most active phases of the pandemic, at the time when social restrictions were progressively removed), and the associated social isolation and changes in work conditions could have produced an increased prevalence of mental health problems. Seemingly counterintuitive, however, is the presence of high general and work-related positive well-being despite such experiences of mental health concerns. As workers’ negative mental health concerns and positive mental health is shown in previous research to be negatively correlated (e.g., see [68]), one would expect workers to report lower positive mental health scores if they were experiencing mental health concerns. However, our findings provide evidence that workers can experience elevated scores in both the pathogenic and salutogenic dimensions, thereby validating the independent, but related, nature of the Two Continua Model’s axes [37]. Such co-occurrence is also found in the reported latent mental health profiles, which are discussed hereafter.

We found four latent mental health profiles representative of SME workers’ mental health. Three of the four profiles (Profiles 1, 2, and 4) align with the common theory that experiencing elevated negative mental health concerns is generally associated with experiencing less positive mental health. Profile 3, on the other hand, supports the previously noted idea that whilst these indicators are related, individuals can experience both poor levels of flourishing in one’s general life and a relatively low prevalence in distress and burnout. These mixed results are similar to findings presented in one of our previous studies that found that Canadian workers, not specifically in SMEs, were suffering in terms of their distress and stress levels, but not in terms of productivity, which was neither adverse nor favourable [62]. In a similar vein, the differing directions of general and work-related well-being scores in Profile 3 presents interesting insights into domain-specific well-being. While that profile was characterized by the worst general flourishing score, it simultaneously presented the highest work well-being scores. As work is an important domain from which meaning in life is derived [69–71], it is likely that some workers may also experience a sense of thriving by working. Logically, if meaning is a potential contributor to elevated thriving at work, it should indirectly elevate general flourishing. For example, Steger and Dik [70] show that meaning in one’s work satisfies global meaning in life, a core aspect of flourishing, across two studies with undergraduate students. However, although LPA assumes that a profile’s indicators are uncorrelated [72], it is fascinating that there are no cascading effects of well-being at work on general flourishing in Profile 3. As such, future research is encouraged to investigate why some workers may have a disconnect between their general and work-related positive well-being. More generally, future research is also encouraged to examine whether our mental health portrait changes post-pandemic. A more recent snapshot will allow researchers and stakeholders to better refine (person-centered) mental health programs and interventions, whilst also providing insight into how pandemics affect SME workers’ mental health. The latter would inform on how to best support these workers during global crises.

The fact that multiple distinct profiles of workers emerged − for example, some with currently high levels of mental health concerns and some with no major issues, but relatively low positive mental health − underlines the potential of integrated approaches in occupational mental health interventions. Such approaches combine, in a coherent whole, diverse activities spanning the prevention (e.g., primary, secondary, tertiary) and promotion spectrum, thereby addressing the multiple needs of diverse profiles of workers [24, 40].

Mental health covariates and predictors

At the individual level, older age generally predicted better mental health scores and membership to the best mental health profile, although also predicting membership to a mixed mental health reality. However, a larger proportion of older (vs. younger) workers reported that COVID-19 had a negative or non-positive impact on their general mental health and work well-being. Gender was inconsistent in predicting workers’ mental health as women were likelier to (1) experience distress despite reporting higher positive mental health, and (2) be members of the best mental health profile or a moderately suffering profile characterized by slightly maladaptive mental health scores. Unlike age, perceptions regarding the pandemic’s impact are more congruent with the direction of well-being findings, as more women (vs. men) reported that COVID-19 had a negative impact on their general mental health.

The findings regarding women’s and younger workers’ worse mental health scores are similar to those of a scoping review containing 187 studies, as well as results from a recent empirical study. Respectively, the articles, not specific to SMEs, showed that (1) women and younger workers typically had worse mental health during such crises [73], and (2) women and younger workers in Canada were likelier to experience worse mental health realities during COVID-19’s onset [62]. With previous and present findings suggesting that women and younger workers have worse mental health, it becomes increasingly evident that some women and younger workers need more mental health support. If feasible, SMEs can support these at-risk workers by making mental health resources more available or providing benefits that cover mental health care. If providing such services is not feasible, workplaces can provide workers with external resources (e.g., workshops at non-profit organizations) to improve their mental health. As SMEs possess limited resources [74], provincial and federal governments are urged to also work towards improving worker mental health by subsidizing mental health care. Researchers can better support women and younger workers by conducting studies to disentangle better why sub-groups of these workers are seemingly experiencing worse mental health scores or realities.

At the organizational level, workers employed in small enterprises had better work well-being. Whereas those in the health sector had higher general and work well-being, those in finance, although not to a significant extent, tended to have worse well-being in both domains. Retail workers seemingly had the worst mental health as they were likelier to meet the clinical cut-off for psychological distress, as well as had worse general and work well-being. Although one study [75] to our knowledge has explored this area, it may be plausible that SME workers’ sense of meaning attached to their work may explain why those employed by smaller enterprises have better work well-being. Working in SMEs may provide employees with a chance for more meaningful family-like connections [76, 77] and opportunities to see how their work fits within the organization [75], which may increase their work well-being. With a more close-knit work community, employers may also be more in-tune with workers’ mental health and intervene faster when concerns arise. To better support workers’ mental health in medium-sized enterprises, employers are encouraged to check in with workers more often, provide opportunities for meaningful connection and provide updates on how their work has created meaningful change within or outside the enterprise. Taking a broader view, it is evident that retail workers are also experiencing poorer mental health compared to other industries. Such experiences may be due to the precariousness of retail work. In Canada, The Law Commission of Ontario [78] indicated that retail workers were the second-highest precarious occupational group in 2011 (also see, [62]). In that sector, certain forms of precariousness, such as unpredictable layoffs, irregular scheduling and shifting work tasks, may have worsened during the COVID-19 pandemic, as suggested by a U.S. study on low-wage service workers [79]. Because such forms of precariousness are present to varying degrees across this entire economic sector, provincial and federal governments are urged to develop or strengthen policies that provide better support to precarious workers and safeguard their mental health.

Limitations

First, our study’s cross-sectional nature did not allow us to establish causality between the sociodemographic and work-related factors and the mental health indicators, nor to see how SME workers’ mental health changes over time. Second, this study was conducted solely with SME workers. Although this focus was part of the study’s objective, this limits our ability to speak to the experiences of workers in large enterprises and to examine the mental health differences of those in small and medium enterprises with those in large ones. Thus, these results should only be regarded as relevant to SME workers’ experiences. Third, although our data was weighted based on some factors (i.e., gender, age, SME size) presented by the Government of Canada, our convenience sampling has resulted in findings that are less representative of workers in several groups (e.g., non-binary and trans workers, racialized and immigrant workers, those in the food economic sector) that were underrepresented in our sample compared to population-level demographics. In a similar vein, the sampling also prevented us from obtaining data from participants that were not working at the time of the survey, including those temporarily or indefinitely laid off from SMEs because of the pandemic. Fourth, participants relied on their understanding of each mental health issue when completing the self-reported mental health issue question in the survey. This reliance on their knowledge of these issues may have resulted in an over- or underrepresentation of the prevalence of self-reported mental health problems. Fifth, we did not conduct multivariate analyses to explore the relationships between each indicator of worker mental health with all the covariates considered simultaneously. Sixth, the scoring range of the four mental health indicators varied, thus limiting direct comparability between scales; however, it does not invalidate the findings reported. Finally, a few variables were selected when conducting our analyses. Although it is rare that many indicators of positive and negative general and work mental health are considered simultaneously, there are several other indicators (e.g., job satisfaction, sense of meaning in life, affect) that were not explored here. Adding other relevant mental health indicators during profile estimation could have also changed the number and shape of the reported profiles. Moreover, only a few factors were tested to see how they are related to profile membership, which limited our ability to report on how other factors (e.g., income, disability and employment status) predict profile membership.

Conclusion

This study is an important step towards actualizing a portrait of Canadian SME workers’ complete mental health. The results underline the importance of measuring both positive and negative mental health indicators to ensure a comprehensive portrait, as well as using several sociodemographic and work-related factors to determine which workers are at risk for poor mental health outcomes. By knowing who is at risk, organizations and occupational public health policymakers can play a more active role in preventing mental health difficulties and promoting positive mental health. Organizations should provide or adapt mental health supports so that all employees’ diverse mental health needs are taken care of. Public institutions in charge of the implementation and dissemination of labour policy as well as OHS regulations should include targeted information and support for SMEs, particularly the medium-sized ones (based on our findings), to aid them in preventing or reducing occupational mental health issues. Concretely, public health officials, policymakers and governments have the potential to preserve, or even enhance, workers’ complete mental health. This includes providing more resources (e.g., funding, expertise support) to SMEs and subsidizing care for work- and non-work-related mental health. These contributions that diverse stakeholders can make highlight the need for an integrated approach in which employers, governmental and public bodies, as well as SME employees themselves act in a synergistic way, sharing responsibility in promoting workers’ complete mental health.
