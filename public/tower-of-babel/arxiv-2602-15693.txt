On the intersections of projected Hamiltonian orbits in cotangent bundles
Lucas Dahinden, Jacobus de Pooter

Abstract
. We study the generic behavior of Hamiltonian trajectories on a regular level set in the cotangent bundle, after projection to the base. We prove that for a generic submersive level set, projected trajectories have discrete (self-)intersections. Additionally, fixing end-point fibers, we prove that all intersections can be perturbed away if the base has dimension ≥ 3 \geq 3 . In particular, this applies to periodic orbits, and both results hold for Reeb flows on fiber-wise star-shaped hypersurfaces, including non-reversible Finsler flows, which answers a question of Rademacher. In the proof we make use of a multi-jet transversality theorem.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Context and Background

On a closed Riemannian manifold, there always exist closed geodesics. In the non-simply connected case this follows by length minimization in a non-trivial homotopy class, and in the simply connected case this is a classic result by Ljusternik and Fet
[
17
]
. Given two points, there exists also a geodesic connecting them (a chord), which is proved by similar techniques. A geodesic is called simple if it admits an injective parametrization. The length-minimal non-contractible geodesic in the non-simply connected case is always simple, as a crossing may be used to split the curve into two shorter ones, at least one of which is non-contractible. In the simply connected case, the existence of a simple closed geodesic is an open problem
[
1
]
. It is a classic consequence of Morse theory that the loop space on a manifold (the space of closed paths or the space of paths from a point to the other) is homotopy equivalent to a CW-complex, which can be constructed from closed geodesics and chords, cf.
[
21
]
, which guarantees a wealth of closed geodesics chords. As is pointed out in
[
24
, Problem C]
, these geodesics may be not geometrically distinct. The hunt for geometrically distinct geodesics is thus a challenge that goes beyond the simple homological construction, see e.g.
[
11
,
8
,
3
]
for compact and non-compact settings in which arbitrarily many geometrically distinct geodesics exist. In contrast, a complementary example can be found in non-reversible Finsler geometry, where the Bangert sphere only has two geometrically distinct geodesics
[
5
]
.

Recent theorems by Rademacher
[
22
,
23
]
treat the problem for generic metrics: He proves that for generic metrics periodic geodesics and chords do not intersect (each other and themselves). We loosely and collectively state the theorems as follows.

Theorem

(Rademacher)
.

Let
M
be a closed manifold of dimension
\geq 3
and let
p,q\in M
be two points, not necessarily distinct. For a
C^{r}
-generic set of Riemannian metrics (
r\geq 2
), geodesic segments connecting
p
to
q
have no self-intersection, and geometrically distinct segments have no interior intersections.

Moreover, for a
C^{r}
-generic set of reversible Finsler metrics (
r\geq 4
), the same holds true.

As a corollary, Rademacher shows
[
23
, Corollary 1]
that generically there are infinitely many geometrically distinct geodesic chords connecting the given end-points.

Rademachers proof of the above theorem relies at some point on a simple observation, which we state here loosely as follows.

If two regular curves intersect at accumulating times,

then they are tangent.

In Lemma
2.8
, we show that we can even conclude tangency to arbitrary order. If these two curves are Riemannian geodesics, then the existence of an injectivity radius lets us conclude that they coincide.

Rademachers proof has essentially three steps:

(1)

The geodesics of interest are non-degenerate for a generic metric. In particular, for a length
L
the set of geodesics of interest of length
\leq L
is finite.

(2)

By the simple observation, tangent geodesics coincide. Thus, all intersections are transverse, thus isolated and there are only finitely many.

(3)

Perturb the metric to resolve the finitely many intersections of the finitely many geodesics. Do repeat for every integer
L
to get a genericity statement.

In Rademachers proof, the difficult step is the perturbation (3), which involves an implicit function theorem.

The theorems still work well if the situation is generalized to reversible Finsler dynamics. However, for non-reversible Finsler dynamics there are tangent geodesics that do not coincide. This led Rademacher to ask:

Question 1.1

(Rademacher)
.

Does this theorem generalize to non-reversible Finsler metrics?

1.2.
Generalization

We aim here to generalize Rademachers theorem beyond the class of Finsler metrics to a broader range of Hamiltonian dynamical systems. Note that non-intersecting geodesics are in particular geometrically distinct. For a Hamiltonian
H:T^{*}Q\to\mathbb{R}
, we focus on the energy level set
\Sigma=\Sigma_{H}=H^{-1}(0)
.

Definition 1.2
.

We denote by
\operatorname{Ham}_{s}
the set of Hamiltonians
H\in C^{\infty}(T^{*}Q,\mathbb{R})
such that the following properties hold.

•

The zero level set is regular, i.e.,
dH|_{\Sigma_{H}}\neq 0
.

•

The projection

\pi_{Q}:\Sigma=H^{-1}(0)\to Q

is a submersion.

These conditions have important consequences for
\Sigma
. Regularity of the level set implies that
\Sigma
is a manifold. The submersivity implies that the projection to the base manifold of the Hamiltonian vector field does not vanish
d\pi_{Q}X_{H}|_{\Sigma}\neq 0
(see Lemma
3.1
).

Remark 1.3
.

It would also be convenient to demand that
Q
is compact and
H
is proper, which is in many applications the case. Then,
\Sigma
would be a compact set, and together with submersivity we could conclude that it is a fiber bundle with pairwise diffeomorphic fibers. The main benefit of compactness of
\Sigma
would be automatic completeness of the Hamiltonian flow. Our main result is local in nature, so that the comfort of completeness can be approximated by an exhaustion by compact sets.

In the following statements, we equip
C^{\infty}(T^{*}Q,\mathbb{R})
with either the weak or strong Whitney
C^{k}
-topologies. The results hold for either. See for example
[
14
, Chapter 2]
for their definitions and properties. In the results below,
C^{k}
residual means that the set of Hamiltonians for which the statement is true, is a countable intersection of
C^{k}
-open and
C^{\infty}
dense set. Since the set of Hamiltonians is a Baire space, this intersection is still
C^{\infty}
dense. We prove the following generalization of Rademachers theorem.

Theorem 1
.

Let
Q
be a manifold of dimension
\geq 3
and fix two points
q_{1},q_{2}
. There is a set of Hamiltonians
H\in\operatorname{Ham}_{s}
that is
C^{2}
-residual in the weak and strong Whitney topology such that the following statements are true:

•

For any two distinct flow lines
\gamma_{i}:I_{i}\to\Sigma
of the Hamiltonian vector field
X_{H}
that connect the fibers
\Sigma_{q_{1}}
and
\Sigma_{q_{2}}
the projections to the base manifold
\pi_{Q}\circ\gamma_{i}
intersect exclusively in the end points
\pi_{Q}\circ\gamma_{1}\cap\pi_{Q}\circ\gamma_{2}=\{q_{1},q_{2}\}
.

•

No flow line
\gamma:I\to\Sigma
of the Hamiltonian vector field
X_{H}
that connects the fibers
\Sigma_{q_{1}}
and
\Sigma_{q_{2}}
admits a self-intersection:
\pi_{Q}\circ\gamma
is injective (except at the end points if they coincide).

•

For any pair of geometrically distinct periodic flow lines
\gamma_{i}:\mathbb{R}/T_{i}\mathbb{Z}\to\Sigma
of the Hamiltonian vector field
X_{H}
the projections to the base manifold
\pi_{Q}\circ\gamma_{i}
do not intersect.

•

For any periodic flow line
\gamma:\mathbb{R}/T\mathbb{Z}\to\Sigma
of the Hamiltonian vector field
X_{H}
that is not a multiple cover of the projection to the base manifold
\pi_{Q}\circ\gamma_{i}
does not self-intersect:
\pi_{Q}\circ\gamma
is injective.

The main difficulty in proving this theorem is step (2): it is easy to construct examples of flow lines with accumulating intersection points. Thus, we aim for something weaker than an injectivity radius, and we expect it to hold only generically.

Definition 1.4
.

We say that
H\in\operatorname{Ham}_{s}
has
discrete intersections
at the 0-energy level
\Sigma_{H}
if for any two Hamiltonian flow lines
\gamma_{1}:I_{1}\to\Sigma_{H},\gamma_{2}:I_{2}\to\Sigma_{H}
that do not coincide up to time shift the set of intersection times

\{(t_{1},t_{2})\in I_{1}\times I_{2}\mid\pi_{Q}(\gamma_{1}(t_{1}))=\pi_{Q}(\gamma_{2}(t_{2}))\}

is discrete.

Note that discreteness of intersections does not propose a lower bound on the distance between intersections, and the time of first intersection is also not continuous. This is illustrated by the following example.

Example 1.5
.

The
s-
family of lines
\gamma_{s}(t)=(t,st-t^{2})
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
intersects the
x
-axis for each
s>0
twice:
\gamma_{s}\cap\{y=0\}=\{(0,0),(s,0)\}
. The distance between the intersections is not bounded away from zero. It is easy to construct a Hamiltonian in
T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that the lines are projected flow lines.

The essential step in the proof of Theorem
1
is to show that generic Hamiltonians have discrete intersections.

Theorem 2
.

Let
Q
be a manifold of dimension at least 3. Then, a subset of
\operatorname{Ham}_{s}
that is
C^{7}
-residual in the weak and strong topology Whitney topology has discrete intersections at
\Sigma
.
If the dimension of
Q
is two, then a subset of
\operatorname{Ham}_{s}
that is
C^{6}
-residual in the weak and strong topology Whitney topology has discrete intersections at
\Sigma
.

We prove Theorem
1
below assuming Theorem
2
and sketch the proof of Theorem
2
.

1.3.
Notable special cases

We note that the subset
\operatorname{Cont}\subseteq\operatorname{Ham}_{s}
of Hamiltonians whose hypersurface
\Sigma
is fiberwise starshaped with respect to 0 is
C^{k}
-open (
k\geq 1
). These flows are (up to reparametrization) precisely the contact flows on the spherization
S^{*}Q
.

A further
C^{k}
-open (
k\geq 2
) subset
{\mathcal{F}}\subseteq\operatorname{Cont}
consists of Hamiltonians such that
\Sigma
is fiberwise analytically convex (with 0 in its interior). These flows are (up to reparametrization) precisely the Finsler geodesic flows: the Finsler norm can be recovered as
F(q,v)=\max\{p(v)\mid p\in\Sigma_{q}\}
.

If a set
A\subseteq\operatorname{Ham}_{s}
is
C^{k}
-residual and
B\subseteq\operatorname{Ham}_{s}
is
C^{k}
-open, then
A\cap B
is
C^{k}
-residual in
B
. Thus, we immediately gain the following corollary.

Corollary 3
.

The conclusions of Theorem
1
and Theorem
2
also hold if one replaces
\operatorname{Ham}_{s}
by
\operatorname{Cont}
or
{\mathcal{F}}
.

1.4.
Many geometrically distinct geodesics

In many cases, one can construct filtered homologies from chords. As pointed out before, in the geodesic case, one can construct a Morse theory from the energy functional, which coincides with the loop space homology filtered by energy
[
21
]
. The growth of this homology induces growth of number of chords since they are the generators of the homology. By a result of Gromov
[
15
]
, see also
[
25
, Theorem 5.10]
, there is a lower bound to this growth coming from the growth of the homology filtered by degree, which is at least linear on closed manifolds.

In the case of generic compact, exactly fillable hypersurfaces, one can define symplectic homology relative Legendrians that are fillable by exact Lagrangians, or the corresponding positive Rabinowitz–Floer homology
[
6
]
(which is the same homology,
[
7
]
). They are generated by chords between the selected Legendrians, which are provided by the fibers of
\Sigma
over the selected points. If the hypersurface is fiberwise starshaped, then this homology coincides with loopspace homology
[
4
]
. If the loop space homology grows exponentially in degree, then automatically the corresponding growth of chords in length is positive by a sandwiching argument, for more details see
[
18
]
.

Corollary 4
.

For a closed manifold
Q
and two fibers of the cotangent bundle, for a
C^{2}
residual set of exactly fillable compact hypersurfaces, the number of geometrically distinct chords connecting them grows in length at least as much as the dimension of symplectic homology filtered by length.

If furthermore the hypersurface is fiberwise starshaped, then the growth is at least linear. If the loop space homology grows exponentially in degree, then also the number of geometrically distinct chords grows exponentially in length.

Proof of Theorem

.  We mimic the proof of Rademacher. Note that since we only consider closed orbits and chords between fibers, the possible non-completeness of the flow is of no concern. We choose a time

(Step 1)

In the Hamiltonian setting it is well known that a

-residual subset of

Consider an intersection point

We try to displace

from the other segments. For this, we need to choose where

should go instead, so we search alternative curves

Remark 1.6
.

We note that Theorem
1
is wrong for lower regularities by referring to the Riemannian case. For
C^{0}
regularity on the metric
g
, the result is easily seen to be wrong as we can produce closed orbits and chords wherever we want by
C^{0}
perturbation by methods explained in
[
2
, Example 43]
.
To see that the statement is wrong for
C^{1}
regularity we inspect the case of chords: If we fix a starting fiber and look at all orbits of length
\leq T
(but that end anywhere), there may be many mutually- and self-intersecting ones whose existence cannot be perturbed away. However, we are only interested in the selected few that land on the target (non-conjugate) fiber. Non-intersection (away from the diagonal) of these selected chords is an open property in
C^{0}
-topology of the selected chords. But the selected chords are not continuous in
C^{1}
-small changes of
g
: we can manipulate the curvature along the chord arbitrarily by adding
C^{1}
-small but
C^{2}
-large bumps. This way we construct in any
C^{1}
neighbourhood of
g
a
g^{\prime}
with the same chord, but for which the geodesic spray is focused at the target fiber, making it a conjugate point. Further
C^{1}
perturbation then makes sure the chord bifurcates into multiple ones (or even an infinite family), the mutual intersection pattern of which we have no control over. Thus, the mutual intersection statement is wrong for
C^{1}
.

Sketch of proof of Theorem
2

We first reformulate the problem for a fixed Hamiltonian
H\in\operatorname{Ham}_{s}
. For each point in
\Sigma
we get a flow line
\varphi^{t}_{H}(p,q)
line going through this point. Restricting the flow line to a small time interval, it is a submanifold with base point
\varphi^{0}_{H}(p,q)
. The 1-dimensional submanifolds with base point form a bundle
\operatorname{Sub}(\Sigma,1)
over
\Sigma
and
H
thus provides a section

\sigma_{H}:\Sigma\longrightarrow\operatorname{Sub}(\Sigma,1).

Actually, there is a subset
\operatorname{Sub}_{\pi_{Q}}(\Sigma,1)
of sub-manifolds for which the base projection
\pi_{Q}
is an immesion. Our section
\sigma_{H}
has image in
\operatorname{Sub}_{\pi_{Q}}(\Sigma,1)
since it avoids curves that are tangent to the fiber due to the assumption that the projection
\pi_{Q}\colon\Sigma\to Q
is a submersion. Additionally,
\pi_{Q}
induces a map

\operatorname{Sub}_{\pi_{Q}}(\Sigma,1)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\pi_{Q}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\operatorname{Sub}(Q,1)

to the 1-dimensional submanifold bundle of
Q
. Further, we consider the bundle of jets of submanifolds
J^{k}_{\pi_{Q}}(\Sigma_{1})
and
J^{k}(Q,1)
with corresponding maps that fit into the following diagram.

2.1.
Jet bundles

Let us first revisit jet bundles, as introduced for example in
[
10
, Chapter 1]
,
[
16
, Chapter 1.1]
and
[
14
, Chapter II]
. Let
E\to M
be a smooth fiber bundle, with
\dim M=m
. We think of

as follows: Consider the space of smooth local sections with a choice of marked point

C^{\infty}(E)_{loc}\times M=\{(\gamma,x)\mid\gamma:\operatorname{Op}(x)\to E\}.

Note that we focus on local sections in an arbitrarily small neighbourhood
\operatorname{Op}(x)
of the marked point, as we are not interested in how (or if at all) the section extends beyond that neighbourhood. For each point
x\in M
there is an equivalence relation on
C^{\infty}(E)\times\{x\}
that identifies sections that are tangent to
k
-th order at
x
. The jet bundle is then
J^{k}(E)=C^{\infty}(E)\times M/\sim
. We topologize
J^{k}(E)
by taking the quotient of the topology induced by the
C^{k}
-norm (which gives the same topology as the weak Whitney
C^{k}
-topology) on
C^{\infty}(E)
. The jet bundle can be seen as a bundle over the base space
J^{k}(E)\to M;[\gamma,x]\mapsto x
or as a bundle over the total space
E
,
J^{k}(E)\to E;[\gamma,x]\mapsto(x,\gamma(x))
.

We make this more precise in local coordinates: tangency to order
k
means that all order
r
derivatives agree at
x
, where
0\leq r\leq k
runs through all options. Given a small enough neighbourhood
U
, we fix a trivialization of
E|_{U}
as a product
U\times E_{x}
and choose a neighbourhood
V\subseteq E_{x}
in the fiber so that we parametrize
U\times V
with a product map
\phi\times\psi:U\times V\to U^{\prime}\times V^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{\dim E_{x}}
that is a diffeomorphism onto its image. A local section
\sigma:U\subseteq M\to U\times V
is represented by a function
f:U^{\prime}\to V^{\prime}
that is defined by
f(x)=\psi\circ\sigma\circ\phi^{-1}(x)
. This gives rise to local coordinates of the jet bundle
\Psi^{k}:J^{k}(E)|_{U\times V}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
by declaring the jet of
f
at
x
to be

\Psi\circ j^{k}\sigma(\phi^{-1}(x))=j^{k}f(x)=\left(x,f(x),f^{(1)}(x),\ldots,f^{(k)}(x)\right)

where
f^{(r)}=(\partial^{\alpha_{1}}_{x_{1}}\cdots\partial^{\alpha_{m}}_{x_{m}}f)
is the collection of partial derivatives with the multi-index
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})
running through all non-negative integers with
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}=r
. Note that we are taking ordered derivatives so that there is no redundancy.

We note that the jet of
f
at
x
depends only on a small neighbourhood of
x
: if
f|_{U^{\prime\prime}}=g|_{U^{\prime\prime}}
for some open set
x\in U^{\prime\prime}\subset U^{\prime}
, then
j^{k}f(x)=j^{k}g(x)
, so restricting the coordinates to a smaller set does not change the coordinates for the jet bundle. This is why we will use the notation
\operatorname{Op}(x)
for an arbitrary small neighbourhood of
x
, that we possibly make smaller if needed.

Note that the distinction of strong and weak Whitney topologies becomes relevant when topologizing the space of sections of the jet bundles, but not when topologizing the jet bundles.

2.2.
Jets of submanifolds

We will make use of
the jet bundle of submanifolds
, denoted by
J^{k}(M,n)
. The main reference we use is
[
19
]
, which also contains a historical overview of the introduction and application of the concept. The definition is originally due to Ehresmann
[
9
]
.

Geometrically, the concept is very similar to jet bundles: We consider the space
\operatorname{Sub}(M,n)
of
n
-dimensional submanifolds
N
with a marked point
*\in N
and intend to take a quotient modulo order
k
tangency. We consider only neighbourhoods of the marked point. As we can restrict a chart, we can think of a marked
n
-dimensional manifold locally as an open neighbourhood
\operatorname{Op}(0)\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
of the marked point
0\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. When needed, we allow ourselves to restrict
\operatorname{Op}(0)
to an even smaller neighbourhood of
0
, which does not change the tangency behaviour at 0.

2.2.1.
Bundle of submanifolds

We denote the space of immersions of
\operatorname{Op}(0)
into
M
by

C^{+}(\operatorname{Op}(0),M)=\{\gamma\in C^{\infty}(\operatorname{Op}(0),M),d\gamma\mbox{ injective}\}.

Every immersion can be made into an embedding by shrinking the neighbourhood
\operatorname{Op}(0)
. The space
C^{+}(\operatorname{Op}(0),M)
is a bundle over
M
by the base point map
\gamma\mapsto\gamma(0)
. Reparametrizations
\operatorname{Diff}_{0}(\operatorname{Op}(0))
of
\operatorname{Op}(0)
that fix
0
act on elements of
C^{+}
.

Definition 2.1
.

The bundle of local marked
n
-dimensional submanifolds is the quotient space

\operatorname{Sub}(M,n)=C^{+}(\operatorname{Op}(0),M)/\operatorname{Diff}_{0}(\operatorname{Op}(0)).

The bundle map
\operatorname{Sub}(M,n)\to M
is the evaluation at zero of some - and thus any - representative
[\gamma]\mapsto\gamma(0)
.

We endow this space with the
C^{k}
topologies as follows:
[\gamma_{i}]
converges to
[\gamma]
in
C^{k}
if for a parametrization
\gamma:\operatorname{Op}(0)\to M
there are parametrizations
\gamma_{i}:\operatorname{Op}(0)\to M
(all defined on a common domain) that converge on a compact neighbourhood in the
C^{k}
-norm to
\gamma
.

We find local coordinates for the bundle of submanifolds by choosing local coordinates of
M
that are especially adapted to the submanifold. Let
\gamma:\operatorname{Op}(0)\to M
be a local parametrization of a marked submanifold
N
as before and let
V
be a neighbourhood of
\gamma(0)
containing the image of
\gamma
.

Definition 2.2
.

Coordinates
(x_{j},\nu_{l}):V\subseteq M\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
of
M
,
1\leq j\leq n
and
1\leq l\leq\dim m-n
, are
divided
with respect to
N
if
N
is a graph manifold over the local coordinates
x_{j}
:

x\circ\gamma=(x_{1}\circ\gamma,\ldots,x_{n}\circ\gamma):\operatorname{Op}(0)\to\mathbb{R}^{n}

is a diffeomorphism onto its image. Equivalently, there are smooth functions
y_{1},\ldots y_{m-n}:\operatorname{Op}(x\circ\gamma(0))\to\mathbb{R}^{m-n}
such that

(x_{j}\circ\gamma,\nu_{l}\circ\gamma)=(x_{j}\circ\gamma,y(x_{j}\circ\gamma)).

Lemma 2.3
.

Any
\gamma:\operatorname{Op}(0)\to M
admits divided coordinates (possibly after shrinking
\operatorname{Op}(0)
).

Proof.

We find the coordinates by constructing a tubular neighbourhood: we use the parametrization
\gamma
as coordinates
x
.
Choose a normal bundle
\mathfrak{n}\subseteq TM|_{N}
that complements
TN
. Choose a trivialization
\nu:\mathfrak{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m-n}
. Then, the desired coordinates are given by the exponential map with respect to some auxiliary Riemannian metric to a neighbourhood of the zero section

\exp(\gamma(x),\nu_{\gamma(x)}):\operatorname{Op}^{n}(0)\times\operatorname{Op}^{m-n}(0)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m-n}\to M.

Note that in these coordinates,
N
itself is represented by the zero function
y(x)\equiv 0
.
∎

It is crucial that if coordinates are divided with respect to some submanifold
N
which is described by
y
, then they are also divided with respect to every
C^{1}
-nearby
N^{\prime}
. Using the diffeomorphism
x\circ\gamma
as a coordinate change allows us to use
(x_{j})
as coordinates for every nearby
N
in a coordinated manner. Nearby
N^{\prime}
for which the coordinates are also divided are equivalent to functions
y^{\prime}
nearby
y
. We have thus proven the following structural statement.

Lemma 2.4
.

The bundle of local
n
-dimensional submanifolds with marked point
\operatorname{Sub}(M,n)
is locally around
\gamma
modeled by (small) sections of the tubular neighbourhood defined by a normal bundle
\mathfrak{n}
, together with the choice of marked point. In internal coordinates of
\gamma
and
\nu
, the model becomes (small) sections together with a choice of marked point of the base
\gamma

C^{\infty}(\operatorname{Op}^{n}(x\circ\gamma(0)),\operatorname{Op}^{m-n}(\nu\circ\gamma(0)))\times\operatorname{Op}^{n}(x\circ\gamma(0))\subseteq C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{m-n})\times\mathbb{R}^{n}.

This is a bundle over the tubular neighbourhood by the map
(y,x)\mapsto(x,y(x))
.
The
C^{k}
-norm topologies on
\operatorname{Sub}(M,n)
coincide in the local model with the
C^{k}
-norm topologies on local sections.

2.2.2.
Jets of submanifolds

Two nearby submanifolds
N,N^{\prime}
whose marked point has coordinates
x\circ\gamma(0)=x\circ\gamma^{\prime}(0)=x^{*}
and who are described in common directed coordinates by the functions
y,y^{\prime}:\operatorname{Op}^{n}(0)\to\operatorname{Op}^{m-n}(0)
have a
k
-th order tangency at their marked point if the functions
y,y^{\prime}
coincide at
x\circ\gamma(0)
to
k
-th order
j^{k}y(0)=j^{k}y^{\prime}(0)
. This defines an equivalence relation
\sim
.

Definition 2.5
.

The jet bundle of submanifolds is

J^{k}(M,1)=\operatorname{Sub}(M,n)/\sim.

It is a bundle over
M
by sending
[\gamma]
to
\gamma(0)
. The total space is a manifold. A (local) tubular neighbourhood defines local coordinates of
J^{k}(M,1)
modeled on (small) sections of the normal bundle

J^{k}(\operatorname{Op}^{n}(0),\operatorname{Op}^{m-n}(0))\subseteq J^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{m-n}).

There is a natural map
j^{k}:\operatorname{Sub}(M,n)\to J^{k}(M,n)
, which assigns to a marked submanifold its jet at the marked point.

We note that the base point map
(y,x)\mapsto(x,y(x))
coincides with the 0-jet of the function.

For jet bundles of sections
f:M\to E
, a bundle morphism
F:E\to E^{\prime}
naturally induces a bundle morphism
F^{k}_{*}:J^{k}(E)\to J^{k}(E^{\prime}),[f]_{x}^{k}\mapsto[F\circ f]^{k}_{x}
. For the jet bundle of embedded submanifolds, this is more delicate: embedded submanifolds might not be mapped to immersions by a smooth map
F:M\to M^{\prime}
since a tangent vector
v\in TN
may be crushed to zero
dF(v)=0
. Generally, we will say that a marked submanifold
N\subset M
is
compatible
with a smooth map
F:M\to M^{\prime}
at its marked point
*\in N
if
T_{x}N\cap\ker T_{x}F=\{0\}
. Then, for a parametrization
\gamma:\operatorname{Op}(0)\to N
the differential
dF\circ d\gamma(0)
is injective, and since injectivity is an open condition,
F\circ\gamma
is an embedding (after shrinking
\operatorname{Op}(0)
if necessary).

We denote by
\operatorname{Sub}_{F}(M,n)
the set of marked
n
-dimensional submanifolds that are compatible with
F
. On this subspace,
F
induces a well defined map

F_{*}:\operatorname{Sub}_{F}(M,n)\mapsto\operatorname{Sub}(M^{\prime},n);\quad[\gamma]\mapsto[F\circ\gamma].

We can transport this notion to jet bundles.

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
N,N^{\prime}
be two marked sub-manifolds such that
j^{1}N=j^{1}N^{\prime}
. Then,
N
is compatible with a smooth map
F
if and only if
N^{\prime}
is.

Proof.

The first jet determines the tangent space.
∎

Corollary 5
.

For
k\geq 1
, the space of
k
-jets
J^{k}_{F}(M,n)
compatible with
F
is well defined. Moreover, it is an open subset of
J^{k}(M,n)
.

2.2.3.
One dimensional submanifolds

We specialize to the setting of one dimensional submanifolds. Then,
\operatorname{Sub}(M,1)
again modeled on the space

C^{\infty}(\operatorname{Op}^{1}(0),\operatorname{Op}^{m-1}(0))\subseteq C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{m-1}).

Since there is only one variable, the corresponding jet space
J^{k}(M,1)
is modeled on

J^{k}(\operatorname{Op}^{1}(0),\operatorname{Op}^{m-1}(0))\subseteq J^{k}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{m-1})=\mathbb{R}\times(\mathbb{R}^{m-1})^{k+1}

and thus has dimension
1+(m-1)(k+1)
.

Two curves intersect at their marked point if
\gamma^{1}(0)=\gamma^{2}(0)=q
. In the local coordinates above with marked point
*
this becomes a statement about the zero yet: the curves are represented by
y^{1},y^{2}\in C^{\infty}_{V}(\operatorname{Op}(*),\mathbb{R}^{n-1})
and they intersect if
j^{0}y^{1}(*)=j^{0}y^{2}(*)
. The crucial observation is that if the two curves intersect
again

\gamma^{1}(t_{1})=\gamma^{2}(t_{2})
, then the coordinates
(x,\nu)\circ\gamma^{1}(t_{1})
and
(x,\nu)\circ\gamma^{2}(t_{2})
must coincide and thus for
*=x\circ\gamma^{1}(t_{1})=x\circ\gamma^{2}(t_{2})
the values
y^{1}(*)=y^{2}(*)
must coincide. Our ultimate objective is to bound such an intersection time
\tau
away from the marked time
*
.

Definition 2.7
.

Two curves
\gamma^{1},\gamma^{2}\in C^{\infty}_{V}(\operatorname{Op}(*),\mathbb{R}^{n-1})
are
transverse
of order
k
if they are not tangent of order
k
, i.e., if the
k
-jets at
*
disagree:

j^{k}y^{1}(*)\neq j^{k}y^{2}(*).

Transversality is essential for us, as it prevents accumulation of intersections.

Lemma 2.8
.

If two curves
\gamma^{1},\gamma^{2}\in C^{\infty}_{V}(\operatorname{Op}(*),\mathbb{R}^{n-1})
are transverse of order
k
, then there is an
\varepsilon>0
such that
y^{1}(\tau)\neq y^{2}(\tau)
for all
\tau\in(-\varepsilon+*,*)\cup(*,*+\varepsilon)
.

Proof.

If the curves do not intersect at their marked time, then the statement follows from continuity. If they do intersect, then the statement follows from the Taylor estimate for
y^{2}-y^{1}
.

It shall be noted that the existence of
\varepsilon
is independent of the directed coordinate system on
V
, but the size of
\varepsilon
may degenerate if
dx(\dot{\gamma})
approaches 0.
∎

Remark 2.9
.

If two regular curves coincide
\gamma^{1}=\gamma^{2}
, then note that the set of intersection times
{\mathcal{I}}=\{(t_{1},t_{2})\mid\gamma^{1}(t_{1})=\gamma^{2}(t_{2})
contains the diagonal as an isolated subset. The lemma above can be applied to
{\mathcal{I}}\setminus\Delta
, i.e., as statement about self-intersections.

2.2.4.
Vector fields define sections

Given a smooth flow of a vector field
X:M\to TM
on a manifold without boundary, not necessarily complete, its integral curves are immersed one-dimensional submanifolds. For a point
x\in M
, there is a neighbourhood
U
of
x
in which the flow of
X
exists for
t\in(-\epsilon,\epsilon)
for some
\epsilon>0
. Let
\gamma_{y}:(-\epsilon,\epsilon)\to M
be the integral curve starting at
y\in U
. The smoothness of
X
implies that the induced smooth map
j^{k}\gamma_{y}:(-\epsilon,\epsilon)\to J^{k}(M,1)
extends to a smooth local section
U\to J^{k}(M,1),x\mapsto j^{k}\gamma_{x}(0)
.

2.3.
Hamiltonians

Here we discuss everything concerning the Hamiltonian vector fields. In Subsubsection
2.3.1
, we will recall how a Hamiltonian vector field is defined. In Subsubsection
2.3.2
we explain how we model the perturbation space globally in the level surface
\Sigma
and how we topologize the perturbations. In Subsubsection
2.3.3
we refine the perturbations to covers, which allows us finally in Subsection
2.4
to reformulate the problem from Hamiltonian to locally contact.

2.3.1.
The Hamiltonian vector field

Let
Q^{n}
be a manifold. Given local coordinates
q=(q_{1},\ldots,q_{n})
on
Q
, we obtain a frame
(dq_{1},\ldots,dq_{n})
of
T^{*}Q
, which defines linear coordinates
(p_{1},\ldots,p_{n})
of the fibers
T_{q}^{*}Q
: any
p
decomposes as
p=\sum p_{i}dq_{i}
. Together, they form coordinates
(p,q)
on
T^{*}Q
.

The form
\omega=\sum dp_{i}\wedge dq_{i}
is symplectic and independent of the coordinates. A Hamiltonian is a function
H:T^{*}Q\to\mathbb{R}
whose purpose is to generate the vector field
X_{H}
by solving the Hamiltonian equation

\iota_{X_{H}}\omega=-dH.

With respect to the coordinates
p,q
the Hamiltonian equation for
X_{H}
takes the form

\displaystyle X_{H}=

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{i}dH(\partial_{p_{i}})\;\partial_{q_{i}}

\displaystyle-\sum_{i}dH(\partial_{q_{i}})\;\partial_{p_{i}}.

Lemma 2.10
.

The vector field
X_{H}
is tangent to
\Sigma
and the conformal class of the vector field
X_{H}|_{\Sigma}
depends only on
\Sigma
. More precisely, if
H,G\in\operatorname{Ham}_{s}
and
\Sigma=H^{-1}(0)=G^{-1}(0)
, then there is a smooth function
f:\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
such that
X_{G}|_{\Sigma}=fX_{H}|_{\Sigma}.

Proof.

Since
dH(X_{H})=-\omega(X_{H},X_{H})=0
, the vector field
X_{H}
preserves
H
and is thus tangent to the level set
X_{H}\in T\Sigma
. On
\Sigma
,
\omega
is a maximally non-degenerate 2-form, which implies that
\chi_{\Sigma}=\ker\omega|_{\Sigma}=\mathbb{R}\cdot X_{H}\subseteq T\Sigma
is a line field (which is called the characteristic line field). For any other vector
V\in T\Sigma
we have
\omega(X_{H},V)=-dH(V)=0
, thus the vector field
X_{H}
is a section of
\chi
. Since
\Sigma
is a regular level set of
H,G
, the vector fields don’t vanish
X_{H}|_{\Sigma},X_{G}|_{\Sigma}\neq 0
and thus we can find the desired function
f
. More precisely, the function
f
satisfies
dG|_{\Sigma}=fdH|_{\Sigma}
.
∎

Given
H
, this defines a flow line
\varphi^{t}_{X_{H}}(p,q)
for each point
(p,q)
that is marked at
t=0
. Thus, a Hamiltonian
H
immediately produces a section

\sigma:\Sigma\mapsto\operatorname{Sub}(\Sigma,1);\quad(p,q)\mapsto\varphi^{t}_{X_{H}}(p,q)

of the bundle
\operatorname{Sub}(\Sigma,1)
and the corresponding jet bundles

\sigma_{k}:\Sigma\mapsto J^{k}(\Sigma,1);\quad(p,q)\mapsto j^{k}y(t),

where for the local expression the functions
y
are extracted from any local coordinates
(p,q)
such that
dq_{1}(X_{H})\neq 0
, which is possible since
\Sigma\to Q
is a submersion. From Lemma
2.10
we immediately obtain the following.

Corollary 6
.

The sections
\sigma
and
\sigma_{k}
only depend on
\Sigma
, not on the choice of
H\in\operatorname{Ham}_{s}
such that
H^{-1}(0)=\Sigma
.

We can define an equivalence relation on
\operatorname{Ham}_{s}
where
H\sim G
if
\Sigma_{H}=\Sigma_{G}
. Note that if
g
is a nonzero function on
T^{*}Q
, then
gH\sim H
. The following Lemma shows that the converse is also true.

Lemma 2.11
.

Let
H\sim G
in
\operatorname{Ham}_{s}
. Then, there is a smooth positive function
g
such that
G=gH
. This function depends smoothly on
H
and
G
.

Proof.

Away from the zero set, we can set
g=G/H
, which smoothly depends on
H
and
G
. This function
g
extends smoothly over the zero set by Hadamards lemma.
∎

Thus, it makes sense to say that the space of hypersurfaces is a quotient space
\operatorname{Ham}_{s}/C^{\infty}(T^{*}Q,\mathbb{R}_{\neq 0})
. We do not make use of this directly, instead we will construct a perturbation space for the set of hypersurfaces and show that this perturbation space times positive functions is
\operatorname{Ham}_{s}
, see Corollary
7
.

2.3.2.
Perturbations of
\Sigma

Hypersurfaces are submanifolds and thus a neighbourhood in the set of hypersurfaces can be described as graphs of a normal bundle.

It would be tempting to believe that
C^{k}
-graphs in these normal bundles are the same
C^{k}
perturbations of
H
. However, since we deal also with non-compact
\Sigma
, this is not true, as the following example shows.

Example 2.12
.

Consider a bump function
b:[-1,1]\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
with support in a small ball
B_{0.1}(0,0)
and such that
b^{-1}(1)
is a transversely cut out sphere. Periodically (in
x
) extend
b
to
\tilde{b}
and let
B(x,y)=(\tilde{b}(x,y)-1)\cdot e^{-x^{2}}
. Then,
B^{-1}(0)
is a countable union of spheres. For
\varepsilon\to 0
,
B+\varepsilon
converges to
B
in
C^{\infty}
. However, for every
\varepsilon>0
the level set
(B-\varepsilon)^{-1}(0)
is only a finite union of spheres. However, we can exhaust
\mathbb{R}^{2}
by compact sets, say balls
B_{N}(0,0)
for
N\in\mathbb{N}
. For each of these compact sets, there is an
\varepsilon
such that
(B|_{B_{N}(0,0)}-\varepsilon)^{-1}(0)
is a graphlike perturbation of
(B|_{B_{N}(0,0)})^{-1}(0)
.

We thus pick a relatively compact subset
K\subseteq T^{*}Q
, and we assume it is open and has smooth boundary. In the following, all objects are restricted to
K
:
\Sigma\cap K
,
H|_{K}
etc., but for the sake of readability we only mention this when it makes a difference. We explain further how we patch neighbourhoods together in Subsubsection
2.3.3
.

Pick any line bundle
TT^{*}Q\supset\mathfrak{n}\to\Sigma
that complements
T\Sigma
. A good candidate comes from an auxiliary Riemannian metric on the base space
Q
, which together with the dual metric
g^{*}
defines a Riemannian metric
g\oplus g^{*}
on
T^{*}Q
. Then,
\mathfrak{n}=T\Sigma^{\perp}
is a natural choice. The line bundle is trivial because
\Sigma
is a regular level set of
H
and thus
dH|_{\mathfrak{n}}
is a choice of volume form. Choose a trivialization
\nu:\mathfrak{n}\to\mathbb{R}
.

A neighbourhood of
K\cap\Sigma
is parametrized by the exponential map

\exp:K\cap\Sigma\times\operatorname{Op}(0)\subseteq\mathfrak{n}\to T^{*}Q;\quad(x,n)\mapsto\exp(x)[\nu^{-1}_{x}(n)].

Remark 2.13
.

K
is important here so that we can uniformly choose
\operatorname{Op}(0)
for
\nu
. If we make
K
larger, then we may to shrink
\operatorname{Op}(0)
to avoid overlaps.

We interpret
\Sigma
as the graph of the zero section. A section
f
of
\mathfrak{n}
produces a Hamiltonian of the form

H_{f}=\nu\circ(\exp^{-1})-f.

On the other hand, a
C^{1}
close
H^{\prime}
will produce a
C^{1}
-close graphlike hypersurface
K\cap\Sigma^{\prime}
by the implicit function theorem.

Remark 2.14
.

K
is important here because the implicit function theorem may fail on non-compact sets, as illustrated by Example
2.12
.

Thus, there are maps between sections of
\mathfrak{n}
and
\operatorname{Ham}_{s}
defined in neighbourhoods of the zero section and
H
, respectively

H_{f}:C^{\infty}(\mathfrak{n})\supset\operatorname{Op}(0)\longrightarrow\operatorname{Ham}_{s},\quad\Xi:\operatorname{Ham}_{s}\supset\operatorname{Op}(H)\longrightarrow C^{\infty}(\mathfrak{n}).

We have that
\Xi
is a left-inverse to
H_{f}
,
\Xi\circ H_{f}=id
which is really just saying that the radial Hamiltonian produced by graph set has that graph as zero set. The map
H_{f}
is not surjective onto a neighbourhood of
H
as there is no reason for elements of
\operatorname{Ham}_{s}
to be homogeneous. However, we can multiply
H_{f}
with any nonzero function
g
to produce the same hypersurface. Let the compact neighbourhood
K
be described by sections of the normal bundle. We define the mapping

{\mathcal{F}}:C^{\infty}(\mathfrak{n})\times C^{\infty}(K,\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})\supset\operatorname{Op}(0,1)\mapsto\operatorname{Ham}_{s};\quad(f,g)\mapsto gH_{f}.

Corollary 7
.

Let
K
be a compact neighbourhood of
K\cap\Sigma
, modeled by a small enough neighbourhood of the zero section of the bundle
\mathfrak{n}
. Let
{\mathcal{U}}\subseteq C^{\infty}(\mathfrak{n})\times C^{\infty}(\mathfrak{n},\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})
be a small enough
C^{k}
neighbourhood of
(0,1)
such that all images are contained in the neighbourhood described by the normal bundle. We denote
{\mathcal{V}}={\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{U}})
. Then
{\mathcal{F}}:{\mathcal{U}}\to{\mathcal{V}}
is a homeomorphism onto its image with respect to the Whitney
C^{k}
topology (weak or strong).

In particular, if
{\mathcal{A}}\subset{\mathcal{W}}=\pi_{C^{\infty}(\mathfrak{n})}{\mathcal{U}}
is a
C^{k}
-residual subset of
{\mathcal{W}}
, then
F({\mathcal{A}}\times C^{\infty}(\mathfrak{n},\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}\cap{\mathcal{U}})
is
C^{k}
-residual in
{\mathcal{V}}
.

Proof.

The first claim is a local version of Lemma
2.11
. The second claim is obvious.
∎

2.3.3.
Refinement by open covers

In the previous section, we restricted our attention to compact subsets
K\subseteq T^{*}Q
in order to employ the implicit function theorem. This is not the only reason to restrict to a cover of
\Sigma
: since we are free to choose
H
, we can as well choose a nice one. We do so by choosing the normal bundle
\mathfrak{n}
fiberwise radial with respect to some center. We explain here how to define bundles and how to switch between local patches. In Subsection
2.4
we explain why this makes
H
particularly useful.

A set
K\subseteq T^{*}Q

2.4.1.
From perturbing the surface to perturbing the flow

Perturbing surfaces is less convenient than perturbing the flow on a fixed surface. We achieve this in the following way: Projecting along our normal bundle

that maps the total bundle of

The characteristic flow on

We can finish by recalling that the Reeb flow of

Proof of the main theorem

We will find iterative conditions for accumulated intersection and break them: A zeroth order condition is that the curves intersect, a first order condition that they are tangent etc.

The jet
j^{k+1}h
will then be our pre-image. Anticipating the submersive step, we make sure that the choices can be made in a uniform way for a neighbourhood of
\Psi_{0}
.

Pick a representative path
\psi
of
\Psi_{0}
. We first choose coordinates that are directed with respect to
\psi|_{\operatorname{Op}(0)}
. I.e., we choose
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})
such that
x_{1}
is monotone along any short enough representative of
\Psi_{0}
, which means
dx_{1}|_{\psi_{0}}\neq 0
. For any
\Psi
near
\Psi_{0}
and any representative
\psi
of
\Psi
, this coordinate system is also directed for
\psi
, because
dx_{1}|_{\psi}\neq 0
is an open condition (possibly we need to shrink
\operatorname{Op}(0)
). Reparametrizing
t
by
x_{1}
and noting that the marked time of
\Psi
is
x_{1}(\Psi)
, we can write

•

x_{1}(\psi(t))=t
,

•

j^{k}\psi(x_{1}(\Psi))=\Psi
.

A valid choice for representatives
\psi
of
\Psi
are polynomials in the coordinates
x_{2},\ldots,x_{m}
of degree
\leq k
. Then, the assignment
\Psi\mapsto\psi
is uniquely determined and smooth in
\Psi
.

To choose a contact Hamiltonian
h
, we use machinery from contact geometry. We observe that a point is an isotropic submanifold of
\Sigma
, and that
\psi
is thus an isotopy
\psi(t)
of isotropic submanifolds. We employ the Isotropic Isotopy Extension Theorem
[
12
, Theorem 2.6.2]
to produce a (time dependent) Hamiltonian
h_{t}
generating a contact isotopy
\varphi(t)
(we choose the start time as
\varphi(x_{1}(\Psi))=id
) whose restriction to the point
\pi_{J^{0}_{*}(U,1)}\Psi
is the isotropic isotopy
\varphi(t)|_{\pi_{J^{0}_{*}(U,1)}\Psi}=\psi(t)
. Geiges’ proof first finds conditions on the 1-jet of
h
along
\psi(t)
and then extends linearly. The 1-jet conditions on
h
are (note that the right hand sides are determined by
\psi
:

\displaystyle h_{t}(\psi(t))

\displaystyle=\alpha(\dot{\psi}(t))

This is forced by the first contact Hamiltonian equation.

\displaystyle dh_{t}(\psi(t))|_{\xi}

\displaystyle=-\iota_{\dot{\psi}(t)}d\alpha|_{\xi},

\displaystyle\mbox{This is forced by the second contact Hamiltonian equation along $\xi$}.

\displaystyle dh_{t}(\psi(t))[\dot{\psi}(t)]

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dt}\left(\alpha(\dot{\psi}(t))\right)

This is an arbitrary choice, in which we deviate from Geiges.

We note that since
\dot{\psi}(t)
and
\xi
build a frame, this completely determines the 1-jet of
h_{t}
at time
t
at the place
\psi(t)
. Geiges’ proof involves showing that any Hamiltonian satisfying these equations indeed generates the desired contact isotopy and we refer the reader to his proof for that verification.

The third condition is actually not a necessary condition to generate the contact isotopy, it could be set to any other number to complete the 1-jet information (Geiges makes a different arbitrary choice
dh_{t}(\psi(t))[R_{\alpha}]=0
). We choose the number specifically as we do because we are (as opposed to Geiges in the Theorem 2.6.2) interested in choosing a contact Hamiltonian that is
autonomous
, i.e., independent of time
h_{t}=h
. For this, it is crucial to recognize that by the Leibniz rule

\frac{d}{dt}h_{t}(\psi(t))=\dot{h}_{t}(\psi(t))+dh_{t}(\dot{\psi}(t)).

In order to have first order time independence, we require
\dot{h}_{t}(\psi(t))=0
, and by the first equation
\frac{d}{dt}h_{t}(\psi(t))=\frac{d}{dt}\left(\alpha(\dot{\psi}(t))\right)
so the Leibniz rule forces the third equation.

We then extend the 1-jet of
h_{t}
along
\psi(t)
to an actual function in the following way: We choose an auxiliary Riemannian metric on
U
and map the transverse bundle
\xi|_{\psi(t)}
to
U
by the exponential map

\exp\colon\xi|_{\psi(t)}\to U;\quad(v,t)\mapsto\exp(\psi(t),v).

Restricted to a small enough neighbourhood of the zero section
V_{\xi}(\psi(t))
, this map is a diffeomorphism onto its image
V\subseteq U
(because
\psi
is transverse to
\xi
). We define
h
fiberwise affine linearly in the
\xi
-bundle in accordance with the first two equations

\tilde{h}\colon V_{\xi}(\psi(t))\to\mathbb{R};\quad\tilde{h}(t,v)=\alpha(\dot{\psi}(t))-\dot{\psi}(t)d\alpha(\dot{\psi}(t),v)

and then we move that function by the exponential map

h\colon V\to\mathbb{R};\quad h(x)=\tilde{h}(\exp^{-1}x).

Then,
h
satisfies the first two equations by construction, and third equation is a consequence of being autonomous.

We thus have constructed our pre-image

{\mathcal{R}}_{k}(j^{k+1}h(\pi_{J^{0}_{\xi}(U,1)}\Psi))=\Psi.

We could extend
h
beyond the neighbourhood
V
by choosing a cutoff function and interpolating to constant 1, but for the purpose of taking the jet this is not necessary.

Submersivity
.
Let
v
be a vector in
TJ^{k}_{*}(U,1)
. We choose a differentiable path
\Psi_{s}
such that
\frac{d}{ds}\Psi_{s}=v
. We are done if we find a differentiable path of contact Hamiltonians
h(s)
such that

{\mathcal{R}}_{k}(j^{k+1}h_{s}(\pi_{J^{0}_{\xi}(U,1)}\Psi(s)))=\Psi(s).

We first note that the associated polynomial representatives
\psi_{s}(t)
of
\Psi(s)
form a differential path of
C^{k}
paths since the coefficients of the polynomials are differentiable, since they are coordinates of
\Psi(s)
.

As a result, the
s
-dependent 1-jet prescription for
h_{s}
is differentiable in
s
and thus also the fiberwise linear extension to actual functions
h_{s}
is differentiable in
s
, and thus so is
j^{k+1}h
. The evaluation at the base point
\pi_{J^{k}_{\xi}(U,1)}\Psi(s)
is also differentiable since
\Psi(s)
is a differentiable path.
∎

We thus study the following composition of bundle maps

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces J^{k+1}(\Sigma,\mathbb{R}_{>0})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces J^{k}_{\xi}(\Sigma,1)\subseteq J_{\pi_{Q}}^{k}(U,1)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces J^{k}(Q,1).}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{R}}_{k}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi^{k}_{Q}}

A choice of contact Hamiltonian induces a holonomic section of these bundles which is compatible with the bundle maps.

3.1.4.
Transversality

We apply the established submersivity to the set of homopodal points.

Theorem 9

(Transversality for homopodal points)
.

Let
Q
be a manifold of dimension
d\geq 2
. Let
k\geq 1
. For a
C^{k+2}
-residual set of Hamiltonians
H\in\operatorname{Ham}_{s}(Q)
, the following is true:

•

The set of antipodal points
{\mathcal{H}}^{-}_{k}(\Sigma)\subseteq\Sigma\times\Sigma
is a submanifold of dimension
(3-k)(n-1)+1
.

•

The set of non-diagonal isopodal points
{\mathcal{H}}^{+}_{k}\setminus\Delta_{\Sigma}\subseteq\Sigma\times\Sigma
is a submanifold of dimension
(3-k)(n-1)+1
.

Proof.

As explained in Lemma
2.18
, it is enough to prove the statement for a neighbourhood
{\mathcal{U}}
of any
H\in{\mathcal{H}}_{s}
, and any radial neighbourhood
K\subseteq T^{*}Q
over a neighbourhood
U\subseteq\Sigma
. Since we look at a pair of jets, for two points
x_{1}\neq x_{2}
in
\Sigma
, we consider two radial neighbourhoods
K_{i}\subseteq T^{*}Q
over neighbourhoods
U_{i}\subseteq\Sigma
.

We thus fix
H\in\operatorname{Ham}_{s}
and consider only nearby Hamiltonians, which are in the radial neighbourhood
K
over
U\subseteq\Sigma
sufficiently modeled by contact Hamitlonians
h\in C^{k+1}(U,\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})
. The Homopodal sets
{\mathcal{H}}_{k}\cap U_{1}\times U_{2}
, which we are continuing to denote by
{\mathcal{H}}_{k}
, are then the preimage of the diagonal

{\mathcal{H}}_{k}=(j^{k+1}h\circ{\mathcal{R}}_{k}\circ\pi^{k}_{Q}\times j^{k+1}h\circ{\mathcal{R}}_{k}\circ\pi^{k}_{Q})^{-1}(\Delta_{J^{k}(Q,1)})

under the map

j^{k+1}h\circ{\mathcal{R}}_{k}\circ\pi^{k}_{Q}\times j^{k+1}h\circ{\mathcal{R}}_{k}\circ\pi^{k}_{Q}:U_{1}\times U_{2}\subseteq\Sigma\times\Sigma\to J^{k}(Q,1)\times J^{k}(Q,1).

By design this map factors by first takin a section

j^{k+1}h\times j^{k+1}h:U_{1}\times U_{2}\to J^{k+1}(U_{1},\mathbb{R}_{>0})\times J^{k+1}(U_{2},\mathbb{R}_{>0})

and then continuing with the mapping

({\mathcal{R}}_{k}\times{\mathcal{R}}_{k})\circ(\pi_{Q}^{k}\times\pi_{Q}^{k}):J^{k+1}(U_{1},\mathbb{R}_{>0})\times J^{k+1}(U_{2},\mathbb{R}_{>0})\to J^{k}(Q,1)\times J^{k}(Q,1).

As established above, the composition
{\mathcal{R}}_{k}\circ\pi_{Q}^{k}
is a submersion, therefore the same is true for the product map. We conclude that the partial preimage of the diagonal

\mathbb{D}:=\left(({\mathcal{R}}_{k}\times{\mathcal{R}}_{k})\circ(\pi_{Q}^{k}\times\pi_{Q}^{k})\right)^{-1}(\Delta_{J^{k}(Q,1)})

is a submanifold (whose dimension we discuss below). Note that
\mathbb{D}
is independent of
h
.

By construction,
{\mathcal{H}}_{k}=(j^{k+1}h\times j^{k+1}h)^{-1}(\mathbb{D})
. But this is the same as intersecting
\mathbb{D}
with the graph of the section
j^{k+1}h\times j^{k+1}h
and projecting back

{\mathcal{H}}_{k}=\pi_{\Sigma\times\Sigma}((j^{k+1}h\times j^{k+1}h)(U_{1}\times U_{2})\cap\mathbb{D}).

If the intersection
(j^{k+1}h\times j^{k+1}h)(\Sigma\times\Sigma)\cap\mathbb{D}
is transverse, then it is a submanifold and then (since
j^{k+1}h\times j^{k+1}h
is a section) the projection back to the base is a submanifold. Thus we have proved:

If for a contact Hamiltonian
h\in C^{k+1}(\Sigma,\mathbb{R}_{>0})
the graph of the jet
j^{k+1}h
is transverse to
\mathbb{D}
, then
{\mathcal{H}}_{k}
is a sub-manifold of
\Sigma\times\Sigma
.

All that is left is the proof that for a residual set of
h
the claimed transversality holds. The following is a modern reformulation of Mather’s result on multijet transversality
[
20
]
.

Theorem 10

(Theorem 10.2.1 of
[
13
]
)
.

Let
E\to M
be a bundle, let
W\subset(J^{r}E)^{s}
be a submanifold in the multijet bundle, and let
K\subset M^{s}\setminus\Delta
be a closed subset, where
\Delta
denotes the large diagonal. Consider the set

T_{W,K}:=\{f\in\Gamma^{\infty}E:(j^{r})^{s}f\pitchfork W\mathrm{\>over\>}K\}.

It holds that

(1)

T_{W,K}
is
C^{r+1}
-residual in both the weak and strong Whitney topologies, and

(2)

if
W
is closed and
K
is compact, then
T_{W,K}
is open in both the weak and strong Whitney topologies.

Remark 3.7
.

The citation
[
13
]
only concludes
C^{\infty}
-residual, but without changing the proof it also proves
C^{r+1}
-residual.

For
M=\Sigma
,
E
the trivial line bundle,
s=2
,
r=k+1
,
K=\overline{U_{1}\times U_{2}}
and
W=\mathbb{D}
, this is precisely what we want. It shows transversality for the
k+1
-jet of
h
, and thus holds for a
C^{k+2}
-residual
h
.

We conclude the proof in the case of transversal intersection by dimension counting:
J^{k}(Q,1)
consists of
k
-jets of
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
-valued functions and thus is a
1+(k+1)(n-1)=kn+n-k
-dimensional space. Thus, the diagonal
\Delta_{J^{k}(Q,1)}\subseteq J^{k}(Q,1)\times J^{k}(Q,1)
has codimension
kn+n-k
. Since
\mathbb{D}
is the preimage by a submersion, also
\mathbb{D}\subseteq J^{k}_{*}(\Sigma,1)\times J^{k}_{*}(\Sigma,1)
has codimension
kn+n-k
. The dimension of
\Sigma
is
2n-1
and thus the graph of
j^{k+1}h\times j^{k+1}h
has dimension
2(2n-1)
. Finally, since the intersection is transversal, we can compute the dimension of the intersection

\displaystyle\dim{\mathcal{H}}_{k}

\displaystyle=\dim(j^{k+1}h\times j^{k+1}h)(\Sigma\times\Sigma)\cap\mathbb{D})

\displaystyle=\dim(j^{k+1}h\times j^{k+1}h)(\Sigma\times\Sigma)-\operatorname{codim}\mathbb{D}=2(2n-1)-(kn+n-k)

\displaystyle=3n-2-kn+k=(3-k)(n-1)+1,

which is the claimed dimension. The splitting into
\pm
flavour works since
k\geq 1
.
∎

Corollary 11
.

If
n=2
and
k\geq 5
, or if
n\geq 3
and
l\geq 4
, then there is a
C^{k+2}
-residual subset of
\operatorname{Ham}_{s}
such that the set of
k
-th order homopodes is empty except for the diagonal
{\mathcal{H}}_{k}\setminus\Delta_{\Sigma}=\emptyset
.

Proof.

We merely need to estimate the dimension
(3-k)(n-1)+1
. If the dimension is negative, then
{\mathcal{H}}_{k}
is empty except for the diagonal. By Theorem
9
transversality can be arranged for a
C^{k+2}-
residual subset of
\operatorname{Ham}_{s}
.

If
n=2
, then

(3-k)(n-1)+1=4-k,

which is negative for
k\geq 5
.

If
n\geq 3
and
k\geq 4
, then

(3-k)(n-1)+1\leq-1,

which is negative.
∎
