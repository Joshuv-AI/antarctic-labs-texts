Precision Sedation for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy in Older Adults: Brain Vulnerability and Recovery
Authors: Haoran Qian, Han Gu, Juan Zhu, Kefan Chen, Peng Jiang, Longbin Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOlder adults undergoing gastrointestinal endoscopy are particularly susceptible to oversedation, hypotension, hypoventilation, hypoxemia, and delayed recovery because physiological and cognitive reserve decline with age. Precision sedation provides a practical approach to these problems by tailoring drug selection, dose titration, monitoring, and supportive measures to the individual patient and the requirements of the procedure. Propofol remains widely used because of its rapid onset and predictable recovery, although cardiorespiratory depression remains dose dependent. Remimazolam and ciprofol may offer greater hemodynamic or respiratory stability in selected patients, while dexmedetomidine and combination regimens can reduce exposure to other sedatives but introduce their own limitations. Monitoring with processed electroencephalography, cerebral oximetry, capnography, and routine hemodynamic assessment may help identify excessive sedation or physiological deterioration when findings are interpreted in clinical context. However, improvements in short-term cardiopulmonary outcomes or recovery should not be assumed to prevent postoperative delirium or longer-term cognitive impairment. In older adults, sedation should therefore aim for the minimum effective depth while avoiding excessive drug exposure and promptly correcting respiratory and circulatory disturbances. Further endoscopy-specific studies are needed to determine whether these strategies improve cognitive and functional recovery.

Introduction

Population aging has increased the use of gastrointestinal endoscopy for screening, diagnosis, treatment, and follow-up in older adults.1 A national survey of 2758 Chinese hospitals reported 9.81 million gastroscopies and 4.35 million colonoscopies in 2016; approximately 6.84 million procedures were performed under sedation.2 As the use of sedation for endoscopy increases, achieving adequate procedural conditions and patient comfort while minimizing risk in older adults has become an important clinical priority.

Adequate sedation improves patient tolerance and facilitates gastrointestinal endoscopy.3 However, older adults vary widely in cognitive and functional reserve, cardiopulmonary compensation, frailty, comorbidity burden, and sensitivity to sedative drugs.4 These factors influence both sedative response and susceptibility to hypotension, hypoventilation, upper-airway obstruction, residual sedation, and delayed functional recovery.3,4 Chronological age should therefore trigger an individualized assessment of cognition, frailty, cardiopulmonary reserve, and medication exposure rather than serve as a substitute for such assessment. Sedation quality should be assessed beyond procedural completion and time to awakening. It should also account for avoidance of unnecessary deep sedation and physiological instability, as well as recovery toward the patient’s preprocedural cognitive and functional baseline.5

The growing emphasis on perioperative brain health supports this approach. Brain health-oriented care focuses on identifying baseline vulnerability and reducing modifiable risks rather than attributing neuroprotection to a particular drug or device.5 Consensus recommendations for postoperative delirium (POD) likewise emphasize risk identification and multicomponent prevention.6 However, routine gastroscopy and colonoscopy are brief procedures associated with substantially less tissue injury and inflammatory stress than major surgery. Evidence on delirium, electroencephalographic suppression, and cerebral oxygenation derived from surgical populations therefore cannot be directly extrapolated to endoscopic sedation.3 Endoscopy-specific studies have mainly evaluated sedation success, cardiopulmonary adverse events, and early recovery, whereas data on standardized cognitive recovery, POD, and longer-term brain health outcomes remain limited.7,8 The central clinical challenge is therefore to balance patient vulnerability, procedural requirements, and physiological stability rather than simply pursue “lighter” or “less” sedation.

Although precision sedation has not been formally defined in endoscopy guidelines, its principles are consistent with individualized sedation based on patient risk, procedural requirements, and physiological responses.3,9 In this review, precision sedation denotes a closed-loop approach that integrates preprocedural risk assessment with the minimum effective sedation depth, individualized drug titration, monitoring, physiological support, and recovery assessment.3,4 It does not imply direct neuroprotection from a specific drug or device; rather, it emphasizes identification and correction of modifiable risk.

This review examines the biological basis of brain vulnerability, individualized pharmacological strategies, multimodal monitoring, periprocedural brain health management, and limitations of the current evidence. We distinguish improvements in short-term cardiopulmonary or surrogate outcomes from demonstrated benefits to brain health. Terminology follows the 2018 recommendations for perioperative cognitive change. POD is treated as a distinct delirium syndrome, whereas the term perioperative neurocognitive disorders (PND) is used as an overarching framework. When earlier studies used the term postoperative cognitive dysfunction (POCD), we retain the terminology used by the original investigators. Short-term changes in the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), Mini-Cog, or psychomotor testing are described as early cognitive or psychomotor recovery and are not equated with PND.10

Literature Search and Evidence Selection

This is a narrative review. Targeted searches of PubMed/MEDLINE were conducted, supplemented by DOI and title searches in Europe PMC and publisher records. The final literature search was conducted on August 9, 2026. Publications available through that date, including older foundational studies, were considered; no language filter was applied. Searches used combinations of terms relating to older adults or older patients; gastrointestinal endoscopy, gastroscopy or colonoscopy; sedation and sedative agents (propofol, remimazolam, ciprofol and dexmedetomidine); frailty, cognitive recovery and delirium; and physiological monitoring, hypotension and hypoxemia, including electroencephalography, cerebral oximetry and capnography. Additional searches addressed age-related pharmacokinetics, pharmacodynamics and brain vulnerability. Representative evidence was selected for relevance to the clinical questions, study design, population, procedural setting and outcome definitions. Priority was given to endoscopy guidelines, systematic reviews, randomized trials and clinically informative observational studies, particularly in older adults. Mechanistic and major-surgery evidence was used to explain biological plausibility or evidence gaps, with its indirectness stated. The existing reference set was re-examined and supplemented with frailty-assessment, endoscopy-specific prediction and capnography evidence. Selection was purposive and non-exhaustive; no systematic-review workflow or PRISMA flow diagram was used.

Biological Basis of Brain Vulnerability in Older Adults

“Brain vulnerability” is not a single disease or a distinct diagnostic entity. It refers to an age- and disease-related reduction in the capacity of the nervous system to maintain homeostasis, compensate for physiological stress, and recover to its previous functional state. This vulnerability reflects several interacting processes, including reduced neural and cognitive reserve, chronic neuroinflammation, mitochondrial and metabolic dysfunction, altered excitation-inhibition balance, and impaired cerebrovascular regulation.11,12 These changes do not imply that brief endoscopic sedation inevitably causes neurological injury in older adults. Instead, they may reduce tolerance to physiological disturbances such as excessive central nervous system depression, hypotension, hypoventilation, hypoxemia, and fluctuations in carbon dioxide.

Brain vulnerability therefore helps explain why similar sedative exposure and physiological disturbances produce different cerebral responses among older patients. This heterogeneity provides the rationale for individualized titration and contextual monitoring of sedation depth, oxygenation, ventilation, and perfusion.

Reduced Neural and Cognitive Reserve

Neural and cognitive reserve contribute to the brain’s ability to maintain function despite age-related degeneration and physiological stress. Normal aging is associated with reduced brain volume and white-matter integrity, loss of synaptic density and plasticity, and declining neural network efficiency. Neurodegenerative disease, cerebral small-vessel disease, and previous stroke may further reduce this reserve.13,14 Cognitive performance may remain relatively preserved at baseline despite these changes. However, the available compensatory reserve may be limited when the brain is exposed to sedative drugs or transient physiological disturbances.

Cognitive reserve is influenced by several factors, including educational attainment, baseline cognitive function, sensory function, frailty, and neurological disease.14,15 Mild cognitive impairment, dementia, previous delirium, prior stroke, and marked frailty may therefore indicate reduced functional reserve. At a given sedative dose, these patients may experience greater central nervous system depression and require more time to return to their preprocedural cognitive and functional state.

Sedation planning should therefore incorporate baseline cognitive and functional status rather than age and body weight alone, with avoidance of unnecessary depth and confirmation of recovery toward the preprocedural baseline.

Increased Susceptibility to Neuroinflammation

Age-related low-grade inflammation, microglial priming, altered astrocytic function, and disruption of blood-brain barrier integrity may increase the sensitivity of the aging brain to peripheral inflammatory and stress signals.11,12 These processes may amplify oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and impaired synaptic plasticity. They contribute to underlying brain vulnerability but are not currently practical therapeutic or monitoring targets during gastrointestinal endoscopy. Delirium in older adults is a multifactorial form of acute brain dysfunction and cannot generally be attributed to a single inflammatory pathway.16,17

Neuroinflammation is therefore best regarded as a mechanism of susceptibility: brief diagnostic endoscopy does not reproduce the inflammatory burden of major surgery, and no sedative has demonstrated clinically meaningful anti-inflammatory neuroprotection in this setting.

Mitochondrial Dysfunction and Reduced Metabolic Reserve

Neurons have a high and continuous requirement for adenosine triphosphate (ATP). Age-related mitochondrial DNA damage, impaired electron transport chain function, and disruption of mitochondrial homeostasis may reduce ATP production and increase oxidative stress.12,13 These changes can diminish the metabolic reserve of the aging brain.

These changes may reduce tolerance to hypoxemia, hypoperfusion, and respiratory depression during sedation, although mitochondrial biomarkers are not established clinical endpoints of brain protection during endoscopy.

Altered Excitation-Inhibition Balance and Reduced Network Stability

Normal cognitive function depends on a dynamic balance between excitatory and inhibitory neurotransmission. Aging is associated with changes in gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA)-mediated interneuron function, glutamatergic transmission, synaptic plasticity, and network synchronization.12 Together, these changes may reduce the stability of excitation-inhibition (E-I) balance.

These age-related changes are relevant to the cerebral effects of sedative drugs. Propofol and benzodiazepines produce hypnosis predominantly through enhancement of GABA-mediated inhibitory transmission, whereas other sedative agents may also affect excitatory pathways such as N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) signaling. In older adults with limited neural reserve, a given drug exposure may produce greater cortical suppression and more pronounced changes in the electroencephalogram (EEG). These may include reduced EEG amplitude, increased slow-wave activity, and, in some patients, burst suppression.

Age-related EEG changes should not automatically be interpreted as evidence of excessive anesthetic or sedative depth. During anesthesia or sedation, older adults may exhibit lower total EEG power and reduced alpha-band activity even at similar clinical levels of sedation, with substantial variability among individuals.18,19 A single bispectral index (BIS) threshold may therefore not accurately reflect the degree of central nervous system suppression in every older patient.

These neurophysiological changes support cautious titration, but low BIS values or burst suppression should be interpreted with recent dosing, raw EEG, blood pressure, and ventilation rather than a fixed index alone.

Impaired Cerebrovascular Regulation and Reduced Perfusion Reserve

Stable brain function requires both neuronal integrity and adequate regulation of cerebral blood flow. Under normal conditions, cerebral autoregulation maintains relatively stable perfusion across a range of arterial pressures. Carbon dioxide reactivity and neurovascular coupling further adjust cerebral blood flow in response to metabolic and neuronal activity.20 Arterial stiffening, endothelial dysfunction, and microvascular changes associated with aging may impair these regulatory mechanisms.

In older adults, chronic hypertension, diabetes, cerebral small-vessel disease, heart failure, and previous stroke may further alter cerebrovascular regulation and the relationship between arterial pressure and cerebral blood flow. Current human evidence does not support a uniform rightward shift of the autoregulatory curve or a universal requirement for higher arterial pressure solely because of aging or chronic hypertension. Consequently, the same mean arterial pressure (MAP) may not correspond to the same cerebral perfusion state in all patients. Rapid or substantial reductions from an individual’s baseline may be particularly relevant during sedation.20,21

Hypoventilation-related changes in arterial carbon dioxide partial pressure (PaCO2) can also substantially alter cerebral blood flow. When hypotension, respiratory depression, and hypoxemia occur concurrently, their effects may interact and compromise the balance between cerebral oxygen supply and demand. For older adults undergoing gastrointestinal endoscopy, the magnitude and duration of blood pressure reduction relative to baseline should therefore be interpreted together with ventilation, oxygenation, and heart rate. A universal “safe MAP” is unlikely to capture this physiological complexity.

Individualized perfusion management should focus on combinations of abnormalities that may impair cerebral oxygen balance, using regional cerebral oxygen saturation (rSO2) and hemodynamic trends to identify and correct reversible causes rather than enforce a single threshold.

Interacting Mechanisms May Reduce Tolerance to Physiological Stress

Brain vulnerability in older adults does not result from any single mechanism in isolation. Reduced neural reserve limits compensatory capacity, mitochondrial dysfunction reduces energy availability, neuroinflammation increases cellular stress, altered E-I balance destabilizes neural networks, and impaired cerebrovascular regulation compromises oxygen and substrate delivery. These processes interact and may collectively lower the threshold for cerebral dysfunction during physiological or pharmacological stress.

During gastrointestinal endoscopy, procedures are often brief, but sedation depth and physiological status can change rapidly. The more clinically relevant question is therefore not whether a particular sedative directly injures or protects the brain. The priority is to identify patients with limited physiological reserve and minimize potentially modifiable exposures, including excessive peak drug effects, unnecessary deep sedation, hypoventilation, hypoxemia, and rapid or sustained hypoperfusion.

From Brain Vulnerability to Preprocedural Risk Stratification

For precision sedation to be clinically useful, brain vulnerability must be translated into identifiable preprocedural factors and actionable clinical decisions. Risk assessment should extend beyond chronological age and consider three interacting domains: patient vulnerability, pharmacological and physiological vulnerability, and procedural demand. Baseline cognitive impairment, previous delirium, marked frailty, and limitations in activities of daily living are important indicators of reduced functional reserve.15,22 Drug sensitivity, hepatic and renal function, polypharmacy, and cardiopulmonary reserve should be interpreted in the context of age-related pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic changes.4,23 Previous stroke or cerebral small-vessel disease, obesity or obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), chronic hypertension, and heart failure may further reduce tolerance to hypoventilation or rapid decreases in blood pressure.3,20 Together, these factors can inform the initial sedative dose, the rate and interval of titration, the intensity of respiratory and hemodynamic monitoring, and the level of postprocedural recovery assessment.

Frailty is particularly relevant because it reflects multidimensional loss of physiological reserve that is not captured by chronological age alone. The Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS) is a nine-point, clinician-rated measure of baseline fitness, function, and dependence. Assessment should focus on the patient’s usual mobility, cognition, activity, and need for assistance with daily tasks, while distinguishing baseline function from acute deterioration.24 The score can help document reduced reserve and support individualized titration and monitoring, but it should not be converted into a fixed sedative-dose reduction or treated as an endoscopy-specific adverse-event probability.3,22

Endoscopy-specific prediction tools are also emerging. Xu et al developed a nomogram in a single-center cohort of 520 adults aged at least 60 years receiving etomidate-propofol-based sedation. The model incorporated age, snoring, Fried-phenotype frailty, hypertension, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, fasting duration, physical activity, and initial etomidate-propofol dose to estimate sedation-related adverse events (SRAEs), defined as hypotension or hypoxemia. Because the initial sedative dose is itself a model variable and validation relied on an internal random split rather than an independent external cohort, the tool is better regarded as a periprocedural risk-assessment aid than as a stand-alone preprocedural screening instrument. It may complement clinical assessment, but requires external validation and clinical-impact evaluation across populations and regimens; CFS scores should not be substituted for its Fried-phenotype variable.25

Risk stratification should also incorporate the characteristics of the planned procedure. Brief diagnostic gastroscopy or colonoscopy differs substantially from advanced procedures such as endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP), endoscopic ultrasonography, and endoscopic submucosal dissection in duration, stimulus intensity, patient positioning, airway access, and analgesic requirements.3,26 Sedation risk therefore reflects the interaction between patient reserve and procedural demand rather than either factor in isolation. Patients with greater vulnerability or those undergoing prolonged or highly stimulating procedures may require lower initial doses, slower titration, and longer intervals between supplemental doses. Earlier ventilation monitoring may also be appropriate, with processed electroencephalography (pEEG) or cerebral oxygenation monitoring considered selectively according to individual risk. Routine use of all advanced monitoring modalities is unlikely to be justified in low-risk patients undergoing brief procedures. Age should therefore prompt an assessment of vulnerability rather than determine dosing by itself. Guideline-recommended dose reductions provide a starting point, with subsequent titration guided by the drug, comorbidities, procedural requirements, and clinical response.3

Precision sedation should therefore move beyond age-based dosing and integrate patient reserve, pharmacological sensitivity, procedural requirements, and monitoring needs into a unified risk-stratified approach. This assessment provides the basis for subsequent drug selection and titration, real-time physiological intervention, and recovery planning. Figure 1 summarizes this framework. Individualized Pharmacological Strategies for Precision Sedation

Goal of Precision Sedation

The goal of precision sedation is not simply to minimize sedation depth or total drug dose. Rather, it is to avoid unnecessarily deep sedation, excessive peak drug concentrations, and prolonged exposure while maintaining adequate procedural conditions.3,4 Insufficient sedation may lead to patient movement, pain, procedural interruption, and repeated supplemental dosing, whereas excessive sedation increases the risk of hypoventilation, hypotension, and marked EEG suppression. Dose titration should therefore incorporate patient vulnerability, procedural stimulation, clinical sedation assessment, and real-time cardiopulmonary responses rather than rely on a predetermined dose or a single numerical index.

Propofol

Propofol produces hypnosis and amnesia primarily through positive allosteric modulation of γ-aminobutyric acid type A (GABAA) receptors. Its rapid onset and short recovery profile make it well suited to endoscopic sedation. However, vasodilatation, myocardial depression, and respiratory depression are closely related to dose and peak effect-site concentration.4,27 Age-related pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic changes increase sensitivity to propofol, making fixed loading doses and rapidly repeated boluses more likely to produce excessive peak effect-site concentrations. Lower initial doses, slower administration, incremental titration, and sufficient time to assess peak effect are therefore preferable in older patients.

Endoscopy-specific evidence suggests that recovery of consciousness and cognitive recovery after propofol sedation may not occur simultaneously. In outpatients undergoing gastrointestinal endoscopy, Allampati et al found that cognitive flexibility returned to baseline approximately 30–45 min after propofol sedation, whereas psychomotor speed and reaction time remained impaired.28 In another age-stratified study of adults aged 18–72 years, the oldest subgroup analyzed (55–64 years) showed slower recovery in several psychomotor measures when conventional discharge criteria were first met. Performance approached baseline after further observation.29 These findings suggest that eye opening, orientation, and conventional discharge scores may not fully capture mild residual central nervous system effects.

Transient cognitive changes should not be equated with PND. Potestio et al prospectively studied 400 patients aged ≥65 years receiving propofol sedation for gastrointestinal endoscopy. Using the terminology of the original study, POCD was reported in approximately 1.3% of patients at 7 days and 2.95% at 90 days. Neither episodes of Patient State Index (PSI) <26 nor total propofol dose predicted cognitive change.7 In a study of 69 patients aged ≥65 years undergoing day-case endoscopy, Chua et al observed lower MoCA scores at discharge in some patients and several cases of hypoactive delirium. However, the small sample and study design did not permit conclusions about causality.30 Persistent cognitive impairment was uncommon in the available follow-up cohort, but its frequency in older, frail, or cognitively impaired patients remains uncertain. Subtle cognitive changes before discharge may not be detected by routine recovery criteria.

Current evidence does not establish that propofol independently causes persistent cognitive impairment after brief gastrointestinal endoscopy. Clinical management should instead focus on limiting excessive peak drug effects and modifiable physiological disturbances, including hypotension and hypoventilation. Particular attention to psychomotor and orientation recovery may be warranted in older, frail, or cognitively impaired patients.7,28

Remimazolam

Remimazolam is an ultra-short-acting benzodiazepine that produces sedation through the benzodiazepine-binding site of the GABAA receptor. Its ester linkage undergoes rapid hydrolysis by tissue esterases, and its sedative effects can be reversed with flumazenil.31 These properties facilitate titration during brief endoscopic procedures. Nevertheless, reversibility and greater hemodynamic stability should not be interpreted as evidence of lower cognitive risk, particularly given the substantial variability in GABAergic drug sensitivity among older adults.

Randomized trials in older adults have shown effective endoscopic sedation with remimazolam and fewer episodes of hypotension or respiratory depression than with propofol in selected settings, although induction and recovery vary with dose and regimen.32–34 Remimazolam has also been evaluated against midazolam in adults aged ≥65 years undergoing upper gastrointestinal endoscopy.35

Available endoscopy data also suggest that early cognitive effects of remimazolam are dose dependent. Tan et al randomized 99 older patients undergoing upper gastrointestinal endoscopy to remimazolam 0.1 mg/kg, remimazolam 0.2 mg/kg, or propofol. At discharge, the 0.2 mg/kg remimazolam group showed significant declines from baseline in immediate and short-delayed recall; these declines were not statistically significant in the 0.1 mg/kg group. The findings suggest dose-related differences in early memory recovery but do not establish cognitive superiority over propofol.36 Dose, administration rate, and supplemental dosing remain relevant to early cognitive performance despite the drug’s short pharmacokinetic profile.

Larger colonoscopy studies further indicate that improved physiological stability does not necessarily translate into better cognitive outcomes. In 228 patients aged ≥65 years, cognitive recovery on postoperative day 3 was similar between remimazolam and propofol, although hypotension and hypoxemia occurred less often with remimazolam.37 In a multicenter trial that enrolled 300 older adults and included 270 in the full analysis set, hypotension was less frequent with remimazolam. The mean decrease in Mini-Cog score was smaller with remimazolam than with propofol (0.14 vs 0.42 points; P = 0.001).38 These findings suggest a difference in early cognitive recovery, but neither a peri-procedural Mini-Cog change nor improved hemodynamic stability establishes prevention of POD or longer-term cognitive impairment.

Patient-centered recovery outcomes also require cautious interpretation. Trials assessing the 15-item Quality of Recovery score, sleep, cognitive recovery, and alternative comparators have not demonstrated a consistent cognitive advantage of remimazolam.39–41 Meta-analyses in older adults most consistently show reductions in hypotension, respiratory depression, and injection pain, whereas cognitive instruments and assessment times remain heterogeneous.8 Remimazolam may therefore be particularly useful in patients with circulatory or respiratory vulnerability, but faster awakening or improved cardiopulmonary stability should not be interpreted as established neuroprotection.

Flumazenil can shorten awakening after remimazolam, but available studies do not show parallel improvements in psychomotor or longer-term cognitive recovery.42,43 Its role in older adults undergoing endoscopy is therefore better confined to selective reversal of clinically important residual sedation rather than routine use for presumed neuroprotection.

Ciprofol

Ciprofol (HSK3486) is a structural analog of propofol that produces sedation and hypnosis at substantially lower milligram doses. Studies in healthy volunteers have demonstrated rapid onset and recovery, and its major circulating metabolite, M4, has no appreciable hypnotic activity.44,45 Dose-ranging studies in gastrointestinal endoscopy have demonstrated dose-dependent clinical effects, supporting incremental titration according to patient response rather than fixed-dose administration.46 Ciprofol nevertheless remains a GABAergic central nervous system depressant. Greater potency and a lower administered milligram dose should not be assumed to confer lower neurocognitive risk.

The strongest current evidence for ciprofol concerns sedation efficacy and short-term tolerability. A multicenter Phase III non-inferiority trial has shown that ciprofol is non-inferior to propofol for successful deep sedation during gastroscopy and colonoscopy, with less injection pain and fewer selected cardiovascular or respiratory adverse events.47 In the 2025 multicenter REST trial, 890 patients aged ≥65 years undergoing gastroscopy were randomized and 871 were included in the full analysis set; ciprofol was associated with fewer respiratory-related adverse events and less injection pain. In contrast, induction, complete awakening, and postanesthesia care unit (PACU) discharge were faster with propofol.48 A 2026 multicenter randomized controlled trial (RCT) in overweight patients also reported lower rates of hypoxemia and severe hypoxemia with ciprofol than with propofol, providing evidence in a population at increased respiratory risk.49 Two meta-analyses published in 2024 similarly reported reductions in hypotension, respiratory depression, or injection pain.50,51 Updated meta-analyses in 2025 reached broadly similar conclusions, although substantial heterogeneity remained in dosing, co-administered drugs, and definitions of adverse events.52,53

Direct evidence on cognitive outcomes with ciprofol remains limited compared with the evidence on cardiopulmonary safety. Existing comparative endoscopy studies, including one conducted specifically in older patients, have mainly used brief assessments of early cognitive recovery. Some were non-randomized, and differences in sample size, age distribution, and concomitant drug regimens limit causal interpretation.54,55

At present, ciprofol can be considered a GABAergic sedative with similar sedation efficacy and potential advantages in cardiopulmonary tolerability and injection pain. Whether its lower administered dose reduces EEG suppression, cumulative hypoperfusion or hypoventilation, or impairment of standardized cognitive recovery remains uncertain and requires dedicated RCTs in older adults undergoing endoscopy.48,54

Another uncertainty concerns drug disposition during repeated dosing in older adults. Two 2026 pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) studies in surgical patients suggest that age-related changes in ciprofol disposition may increase residual drug exposure in older adults. A population model identified age and body weight as significant determinants of ciprofol clearance and reported large peripheral distribution volumes. A direct comparison between younger and older patients showed lower clearance, a longer elimination half-life, and greater overall exposure in the older group.56,57 These findings do not demonstrate clinically important accumulation after brief endoscopy, but they indicate that a lower milligram dose does not necessarily imply negligible residual exposure. It remains unclear whether repeated supplemental boluses during prolonged or complex endoscopy delay psychomotor or cognitive recovery, particularly in older adults with altered body composition or other factors affecting drug disposition. Endoscopy-specific PK/PD studies linking concentration–time profiles to standardized measures of functional recovery are needed.

Dexmedetomidine

Dexmedetomidine reduces central noradrenergic activity through α2-adrenoceptor agonism, producing arousable sedation with analgesic-sparing effects and relatively limited respiratory depression.58 These potential advantages are offset by dose-dependent bradycardia and hypotension, and rapid loading may cause transient hypertension. Dexmedetomidine may therefore be less suitable for older patients with hypovolemia, baseline bradycardia, or cardiac conduction abnormalities.

Meta-analyses of gastrointestinal endoscopy illustrate this trade-off. A systematic review of 40 RCTs involving 2955 patients found that dexmedetomidine reduced hypoxemia during gastrointestinal endoscopy but increased the risk of bradycardia.59 Another meta-analysis comparing dexmedetomidine directly with propofol reported a lower risk of hypoxemia with dexmedetomidine (risk ratio approximately 0.26) but an approximately threefold higher risk of bradycardia. Differences in hypotension were inconsistent.60 Respiratory stability should therefore not be considered in isolation when evaluating overall physiological benefit. In older patients with limited cerebral perfusion reserve, bradycardia and hypotension may offset potential respiratory benefits.

In advanced endoscopy, dexmedetomidine may be particularly useful as a propofol-sparing adjunct in selected older patients. In 49 older patients undergoing ERCP, Chen et al administered dexmedetomidine 0.5 μg/kg with propofol and observed lower cumulative propofol exposure, fewer airway interventions, and less hypotension, but substantially more bradycardia.61 By contrast, in a randomized outpatient colonoscopy trial, low-dose dexmedetomidine reduced propofol consumption but decreased the proportion of patients meeting discharge criteria at 30 min from 88% to 51% and caused a greater reduction in MAP.62 These contrasting findings indicate that the clinical effects of dexmedetomidine depend on dose, procedural stimulation, co-administered drugs, circulatory reserve, and the desired recovery profile.

Evidence for cognitive outcomes after major surgery is also inconsistent. One meta-analysis, using the definitions reported in the original studies, suggested a possible reduction in POCD.63 In contrast, an RCT involving 404 older adults undergoing non-cardiac surgery found no benefit for POD or cognition at 3 or 6 months.64 These surgical data cannot be directly extrapolated to brief gastrointestinal endoscopy. Dexmedetomidine can therefore be considered an adjunct for reducing exposure to GABAergic agents and preserving respiratory drive, provided that its effects on heart rate, blood pressure, and recovery are also considered.

Combination Regimens and Balanced Sedation

Balanced sedation combines agents with complementary pharmacological effects to achieve hypnosis, analgesia, and adequate procedural conditions while reducing the dose of individual drugs. Combination regimens should therefore be evaluated according to overall drug exposure, hypotension, hypoventilation, recovery profile, and cognitive outcomes rather than propofol sparing alone.4,65

Endoscopy studies also indicate that the effects of the adjunctive drug itself must be considered. A randomized double-blind trial comparing ciprofol alone with ciprofol plus fentanyl, together with a colonoscopy RCT evaluating the addition of midazolam and/or fentanyl to propofol, showed that adjunctive agents can affect procedural conditions, total drug exposure, early recovery, and cognitive performance.66,67

A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis of 18 gastroscopy or colonoscopy studies involving 2691 patients examined cognitive outcomes after adjunctive drugs were added to propofol. Combination regimens showed no consistent benefit across cognitive domains, including processing speed, attention, and working memory. Substantial heterogeneity reflected differences in adjunctive agents, cognitive instruments, and assessment time points.68

Studies of ketamine or esketamine combined with propofol further illustrate this distinction. In 200 patients undergoing colonoscopy, Tian et al found that ketamine 0.25 mg/kg combined with propofol reduced hypotension and respiratory depression but was associated with greater impairment in one-card learning and One Back memory on CogState testing.69 Conversely, a randomized trial in 90 adults aged 65–89 years undergoing gastrointestinal endoscopy showed that esketamine 0.25–0.5 mg/kg reduced the median effective concentration of propofol, attenuated the decrease in MAP from baseline, and shortened recovery time.70 Standardized cognitive outcomes were not assessed.

Dexmedetomidine combined with propofol may reduce airway interventions during ERCP in older adults, but it may also delay recovery and cause greater reductions in MAP during outpatient colonoscopy.61,62 A small study of etomidate combined with oxycodone reported improvements in selected cognitive measures, but the evidence is insufficient to support this combination as a preferred regimen.71

Combination sedation is therefore better viewed as a strategy for optimizing drug exposure and procedural conditions rather than as a form of pharmacological neuroprotection. Endoscopy guidelines and pharmacological reviews support defining the intended role of each agent, titrating drugs incrementally, and considering residual or interacting drug effects when hypoventilation, hypotension, bradycardia, or delayed recovery occurs.3,4,65 Representative clinical evidence is summarized in Table 1. Brain and Physiological Monitoring in Precision Sedation

The clinical value of monitoring depends less on the number of devices used than on whether abnormal signals lead to timely and appropriate intervention. Sedation depth, ventilation, oxygenation, and perfusion should be interpreted together so that reversible abnormalities can be identified and corrected promptly.3,74 No individual monitoring modality has been shown to independently reduce POD or PND in older adults undergoing gastrointestinal endoscopy. Monitoring should therefore be tailored to patient vulnerability, anticipated sedation depth, and procedural complexity.

Electroencephalography, Bispectral Index, and Burst Suppression

Processed electroencephalography (pEEG), including the bispectral index (BIS), provides continuous information on changes in cortical suppression and may help identify unnecessarily deep sedation or burst suppression.75,76 Aging is associated with lower total EEG power, reduced alpha activity, and greater interindividual variability. Consequently, the same processed index may not represent an equivalent degree of cerebral suppression in every older patient. Raw EEG, spectral features, suppression ratio, blood pressure, and ventilatory data should be interpreted in context.18,19

Endoscopy-specific evidence indicates that lighter sedation does not necessarily improve cognitive recovery. Allen et al randomized 200 outpatients receiving propofol–fentanyl for colonoscopy to lighter sedation targeting BIS 70–80 or deeper sedation with BIS <60. Lighter sedation reduced sedation-related complications and shortened recovery and time to discharge readiness. However, cognitive impairment at discharge was similar between groups (19% vs 16%), and intra-procedural recall was more frequent with lighter sedation.72 Thus, the aim is not to maximize the BIS value, but to avoid unnecessary depth while maintaining adequate procedural conditions and patient comfort.72

In another RCT involving 115 outpatients undergoing colonoscopy, BIS correlated reasonably well with the Modified Observer’s Assessment of Alertness/Sedation score. However, BIS monitoring did not reduce propofol consumption or complications or improve patient or endoscopist satisfaction.77 Its incremental value therefore appears to depend on the clinical context.

The interpretation of pEEG is particularly relevant in older adults undergoing gastrointestinal endoscopy. Potestio et al prospectively studied 400 patients aged ≥65 years receiving propofol sedation, with continuous Patient State Index (PSI) monitoring using SedLine. Cognitive impairment at 7 and 90 days was uncommon. Neither episodes of PSI <26 nor other markers of deep sedation predicted cognitive change, and total propofol dose was not consistently associated with cognitive outcome.7 This observational study did not demonstrate an association between pEEG-defined deep sedation exposure and subsequent cognitive change after gastrointestinal endoscopy. These findings do not exclude greater sensitivity to EEG suppression in older patients or in those with baseline cognitive impairment or limited physiological reserve.7

Surgical studies of EEG-guided anesthesia have produced inconsistent results for POD despite reductions in anesthetic exposure or EEG suppression.78–83 These findings cannot establish a neuroprotective BIS threshold for brief endoscopic sedation.

pEEG/BIS is therefore best used to identify excessive suppression and guide titration, not as a proven intervention against POD or PND. Low indices or burst suppression should prompt review of recent dosing, raw EEG, hemodynamics, ventilation, and other reversible causes.20,76

Near-Infrared Spectroscopy and Cerebral Oxygenation

Regional cerebral oxygen saturation (rSO2), measured by near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS), reflects mixed arterial–venous oxygenation in superficial frontal tissue. Values are influenced by cerebral blood flow, arterial oxygen content, oxygen consumption, extracranial blood flow, hemoglobin concentration, and device-specific algorithms.20,84 rSO2 is therefore neither a direct measure of global cerebral oxygenation nor a biomarker of neuronal injury.

Endoscopy-specific evidence on cerebral oxygenation remains limited. In 41 patients undergoing propofol-sedated colonoscopy, Curtin et al showed that functional NIRS detected changes in prefrontal hemodynamics after propofol boluses.85 However, functional NIRS differs from clinical rSO2 monitoring, and the study did not assess POD or standardized cognitive recovery.

Surgical studies have yielded inconsistent evidence regarding cognitive or delirium benefits from rSO2-guided management, and the certainty of evidence remains low.84,86,87

Thresholds such as a 20% decrease from baseline were derived mainly from cardiac and major non-cardiac surgery and should not be adopted directly as neuroprotective thresholds for brief gastrointestinal endoscopy. During endoscopy, rSO2 is more appropriately used as an adjunctive trend measure in selected high-risk patients. When a sustained decline is observed, clinicians should assess signal quality, patient positioning, peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2), ventilation and carbon dioxide, MAP, heart rate, and hemoglobin and correct reversible abnormalities.20,87

Ventilation and Oxygenation Monitoring

Oxygenation and ventilation represent distinct physiological processes. Supplemental oxygen may maintain SpO2 despite reduced respiratory rate, upper-airway obstruction, apnea, or carbon dioxide retention. Reliance on pulse oximetry alone may therefore delay recognition of sedation-related hypoventilation.3,88 This limitation is particularly relevant during deep sedation, in older patients, in patients with obesity or obstructive sleep apnea, and during upper gastrointestinal procedures.3,89

RCTs suggest that the clinical benefit of capnography depends on both patient risk and how abnormal findings are acted upon. In the ColoCap study, capnographic monitoring during propofol sedation for colonoscopy reduced oxygen desaturation and hypoxemic events.90 By contrast, displaying capnography alarms during routine moderate sedation in American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status I–II patients undergoing esophagogastroduodenoscopy or colonoscopy did not reduce hypoxemia.91 A meta-analysis of nine RCTs nevertheless found that adding capnography to standard monitoring reduced both hypoxemia and severe hypoxemia overall.88 Benefit has also been reported in patients at increased respiratory risk, including those with mild obesity.92 The main value of capnography is earlier detection of apnea or abnormal ventilation before substantial oxygen desaturation occurs. This provides an opportunity to reduce sedative administration, perform airway maneuvers, or initiate assisted ventilation.

Direct evidence is also available in older adults. A multicenter randomized trial analyzing 1777 patients aged 65–79 years found that additional capnography reduced hypoxia during propofol-sedated gastrointestinal endoscopy from 19% to 12% (P < 0.001), although the difference in severe hypoxia was not statistically significant.93

Direct evidence for enhanced oxygenation strategies is stronger in older adults undergoing gastroscopy. In 125 patients aged ≥65 years undergoing sedated gastroscopy, Yin et al found that high-flow nasal oxygen (HFNO) reduced hypoxemia from 22.6% to 3.2% compared with conventional nasal cannula oxygen and also shortened the duration of hypoxemia.73 Preoxygenation before endoscopic sedation has also been shown to reduce hypoxemic events in older adults.94 Recent network meta-analyses and systematic reviews further support reductions in hypoxemia with HFNO and other enhanced oxygenation or airway-support strategies, although the magnitude of benefit varies between approaches.95,96

The clearest demonstrated benefit of capnography, preoxygenation, and HFNO is a reduction in hypoxemic events. Effects on apnea and airway interventions vary across interventions and studies; benefits for POD or standardized cognitive outcomes remain unestablished.88,94,96 These approaches reduce clinically relevant respiratory adverse events, but whether this translates into improved brain health remains unknown.

Hemodynamics and Cerebral Perfusion

Cerebral perfusion risk in older adults cannot be defined by the lowest MAP alone. Relevant factors include baseline blood pressure, the magnitude and duration of hypotension, heart rate, carbon dioxide, and the individual autoregulatory range.20,21 Long-standing hypertension, cerebral small-vessel disease, heart failure, and autonomic dysfunction may alter cerebral autoregulation. Consequently, the same MAP may correspond to different cerebral perfusion states among patients.20,21

More aggressive blood pressure maintenance should not be assumed to improve cognitive outcomes. In high-risk major surgery, individualized blood pressure management improved a composite organ dysfunction outcome, supporting a physiological rationale for tailoring pressure targets to baseline blood pressure.97 However, a randomized trial evaluating postoperative neurocognitive disorders found no significant cognitive benefit from individualized MAP targets.98 An exploratory analysis of another randomized trial in older surgical patients also found no clear association between intraoperative hypotension and postoperative cognitive dysfunction.99 These findings do not support a simple causal relationship between a single blood pressure threshold and postoperative cognitive outcome.

These surgical findings should be extrapolated cautiously to routine gastrointestinal endoscopy, which involves shorter procedures with less tissue injury and smaller fluid shifts. A more practical objective in older endoscopy patients is to avoid rapid, severe, or sustained hypotension after sedation induction and to assess its likely mechanism in relation to drug timing, heart rate, ventilation, and, when appropriate, pEEG or rSO2 trends.20,98 Management should focus on the predominant mechanism rather than a single MAP value. Excessive peak drug effect, hypovolemia, vasodilatation, hypoventilation, and bradycardia require different corrective strategies.20,21

Multimodal Integration and Closed-Loop Intervention

Monitoring is clinically useful only when abnormal findings lead to timely and appropriate intervention. Basic monitoring should include clinical sedation response, respiratory pattern, SpO2, non-invasive blood pressure, and heart rate/electrocardiography. The British Society of Gastroenterology (BSG) guideline recommends capnography during propofol deep sedation or general anesthesia for gastrointestinal endoscopy. Its use during moderate sedation should reflect patient risk, procedural factors, and applicable guidance. pEEG may be considered when drug sensitivity is pronounced, whereas rSO2 may be reserved for selected patients with substantial cerebrovascular or perfusion risk.3,74

Monitoring signals should be interpreted together rather than in isolation. When a low BIS value or burst suppression occurs, recent sedative exposure and the raw EEG should be reviewed.76 Concurrent decreases in MAP or rSO2 should prompt consideration of systemic hypoperfusion.20 When rSO2 decreases despite a relatively stable MAP, ventilation, carbon dioxide, SpO2, head and neck position, and signal quality should be assessed.20 An abnormal end-tidal carbon dioxide (EtCO2) pattern with preserved SpO2 may identify an opportunity for intervention before hypoxemia develops.92

Monitoring should therefore follow a cycle of detecting abnormalities, identifying likely causes, correcting reversible factors, and reassessing the response rather than targeting an isolated BIS, rSO2, EtCO2, or MAP value. Current endoscopy evidence shows that several monitoring and supportive strategies can reduce excessive sedation, hypoxemia, or other short-term physiological adverse events. Whether these improvements reduce POD or improve longer-term cognitive outcomes after gastrointestinal endoscopy remains unestablished.7,84,88 pEEG, rSO2, capnography, and advanced oxygenation strategies should therefore be regarded as tools for risk detection and physiological management rather than established neuroprotective interventions.

Periprocedural Recovery and Safe Discharge: From Recovery of Consciousness to Functional Recovery

Recovery of Consciousness Is Not Equivalent to Cognitive and Psychomotor Recovery

After brief endoscopy, eye opening, responsiveness, and stable vital signs indicate recovery of consciousness and basic physiological stability. They do not necessarily indicate recovery of attention, executive function, reaction time, or psychomotor performance to the preprocedural baseline. Studies of propofol sedation for endoscopy show that some cognitive domains recover within 30–45 min, whereas reaction time and psychomotor speed may remain impaired. Lower MoCA scores have also been reported when conventional discharge criteria were met in some patients aged ≥65 years undergoing day-case endoscopy.28,30 Such early cognitive changes are better interpreted as measures of short-term functional recovery and should not, on the basis of a single screening assessment, be classified as PND.

This distinction is particularly relevant in older adults. Baseline cognitive impairment, frailty, sensory deficits, and previous delirium may delay recovery of functional safety despite apparent recovery of consciousness. Time to eye opening should therefore be distinguished from recovery of mobility and the ability to resume everyday activities safely.

Risk-Stratified Recovery Assessment and Safe Discharge

Postprocedural recovery assessment should reflect the patient’s preprocedural risk profile. Standardized discharge criteria remain appropriate for low-risk patients undergoing brief diagnostic procedures. Additional assessment may be appropriate in older patients, those with baseline cognitive impairment or marked frailty, those receiving complex drug combinations, and those with recurrent intra-procedural hypoxemia or hypotension or delayed awakening. Before discharge, orientation and attention can be reassessed, with evaluation of standing, gait, or mobility when clinically relevant.3

Studies of advanced gastrointestinal endoscopy indicate that recovery remains incomplete in some patients and that baseline cognitive impairment alone does not fully explain differences in recovery.100 Persistent unexplained disorientation, marked psychomotor slowing, or gait instability should prompt extended observation and assessment for residual drug effects, hypoxemia, hypoperfusion, or metabolic abnormalities before discharge.

Recovery can therefore be considered across three related domains: consciousness, cognitive and psychomotor function, and functional safety. Routine comprehensive neuropsychological testing is unlikely to be practical after outpatient endoscopy. A more feasible approach is to use brief, repeatable functional assessments in high-risk patients and to standardize assessment time points in clinical studies.

Current Controversies, Future Research Directions, and Conclusions

Current Controversies

The current evidence base includes mechanistic studies, observational cohorts, endoscopy RCTs, major perioperative trials, and systematic reviews, with substantial heterogeneity in patient populations and outcome definitions. A key unresolved question is whether abnormal EEG activity, hypotension, or reduced cerebral oxygenation represents a modifiable contributor to neurological risk or simply identifies patients with greater pre-existing brain vulnerability. Preprocedural cognition combined with resting-state alpha power has been associated with POD risk,101 and systematic reviews have reported associations between POD and lower alpha power, lower alpha peak frequency, and longer periods of EEG suppression.102,103 These associations may aid risk stratification but do not establish anesthetic depth as an independent or sole causal mechanism.

A second issue is whether changes in surrogate endpoints translate into clinically meaningful benefit. The AlphaMax, ENGAGES-Canada, and rSO2-guided studies show that EEG characteristics or cerebral oxygenation can be modified by intervention, yet POD has not consistently decreased.87,104,105 Demonstrating neurocognitive benefit therefore requires more than modification of a physiological surrogate. An intervention should alter clinical management, reduce the relevant adverse exposure, and ultimately improve patient-centered outcomes.

Evidence should also be interpreted according to procedural context. Findings from major surgery and advanced endoscopy cannot be directly extrapolated to brief diagnostic procedures, particularly when defining BIS, rSO2, or MAP targets.3,26 Although newer sedatives may reduce selected cardiopulmonary adverse events, evidence for cognitive benefit remains limited.106

Another consideration is overall clinical benefit. An intervention that improves one cardiopulmonary or POD outcome may also increase bradycardia, delay recovery, increase resource use, or introduce other disadvantages.107 For older adults undergoing gastrointestinal endoscopy, the relevant goal is to improve functional recovery and safe discharge without introducing clinically important cardiovascular, respiratory, or recovery-related harms.

Future Directions

Future trials should standardize outcomes and assessment times, incorporate baseline cognition into stratification and treatment-effect analyses, and separate validated delirium assessment from cognitive and functional recovery. Delirium outcomes should include occurrence, severity, and duration, while endoscopy-specific studies should assess mobility, activities of daily living, and patient-reported recovery rather than infer recovery from a single MoCA or Mini-Cog change.108,109 The prognostic effects of POD and preprocedural cognitive impairment support this broader outcome framework.110,111

Monitoring trials should prespecify responses to abnormal signals and record resulting drug, airway, and circulatory interventions. Hypotension, hypoventilation, hypoxemia, and EEG suppression should be quantified by severity, duration, and cumulative exposure, with stratification by procedure, frailty, cognition, OSA, and cerebrovascular or cardiovascular risk.3,15

Implementation studies should address feasibility, resource use, and cost-effectiveness. Multicenter pragmatic trials should test risk-stratified advanced monitoring, because routine use is unlikely to provide equal value in low-risk patients undergoing brief procedures.

Artificial intelligence may facilitate multimodal risk prediction, but current evidence is derived mainly from major surgical or hospitalized populations. Systematic reviews of machine-learning models for POD report potentially useful predictive performance, but external validation, calibration, and evidence of clinical impact remain limited.112,113 Endoscopy-specific models will require external validation in appropriate datasets and, importantly, evidence that their use changes management and improves patient outcomes rather than prediction metrics alone.

Conclusions

Precision sedation aims to reduce modifiable pharmacological and physiological risks and to support functional recovery in older adults with differing brain vulnerability. In gastrointestinal endoscopy, current evidence supports improvements in selected short-term physiological outcomes, but it does not establish that these strategies prevent POD or longer-term cognitive impairment.83,84,88,96,106 pEEG/BIS and rSO2 should therefore be regarded as adjuncts for risk detection and management rather than neuroprotective interventions.

A practical strategy is to assess cognitive, frailty-related, respiratory, and circulatory vulnerability; target the minimum effective sedation depth; and correct reversible ventilatory and hemodynamic disturbances. Before discharge, patients should meet standard discharge criteria, with additional assessment of orientation, attention, and functional safety in those at increased risk or with delayed recovery. Multicenter endoscopy trials are needed to determine whether this risk-stratified approach improves patient-centered outcomes.
