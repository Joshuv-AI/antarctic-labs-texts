Downregulation of the cysE/cysK genes of Clostridioides difficile induced by cysteine revealed the response of bacteria to iron homeostasis
Authors: Wenpeng Gu, Feng Liao, Lulu Bai, Wenzhu Zhang, Senquan Jia, Jianwen Yin, Yongming Zhou, Xiaoqing Fu, Yuan Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCysteine can affect the expression level of Clostridioides difficile toxins. CysE and cysK are the key genes involved in de novo cysteine synthesis in C. difficile. This study used the cysE/cysK gene as the key point to investigate the responsiveness of different genotypes of C. difficile to cysteine addition and the causes of this reactivity.ResultsThe different clinical isolates presented various changes in the copy numbers of cysE/cysK and tcdA/tcdB in response to cysteine addition. The results of the RNA-seq and metabolomics analyses of CD12038 C. difficile revealed that the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the strains with and without cysteine addition were enriched mainly in the carbon fixation pathways of prokaryotes, ribosomes, butanoate metabolism, the phosphotransferase system (PTS) and sulfur metabolism pathways. Moreover, the RT-qPCR results suggested that bacterial iron transport, uptake, and chelation related genes were upregulated in the presence of added cysteine. Compared with that in normal C. difficile cultures, the iron concentration in cysteine-supplemented BHI broth decreased sharply. Caco-2 cell coculture results indicated that the effect of cysteine addition on the virulence or pathogenic abilities of strains of C. difficile was inconsistent across genotypes and clinical isolates or reference strains.ConclusionsDifferent reference strains of C. difficile and clinical isolates had different doses and durations of responsiveness to cysteine addition, and the critical link involved the availability of iron in the environment of the bacteria. This is possibly the response of bacteria to the regulation of gene expression through the maintenance of iron homeostasis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-025-04490-7.Keywords: Clostridioides difficile, CysE, CysK, Cysteine, Iron homeostasis

Background

Clostridioides difficile is a gram-positive spore-forming anaerobic bacterium [1]. Toxigenic C. difficile causes antibiotic-associated diarrhea and colon dysfunction in patients through the secretion of enterotoxin A (TcdA) and cytotoxin B (TcdB) [2, 3]. Inflammation, or severe fatal pseudomembranous colitis, is collectively referred to as C. difficile infection (CDI) [4]. The morbidity, mortality and disease burden of CDI are gradually increasing worldwide, and it has become one of the main public health issues [5, 6]. Although most CDI infections are treated with antibiotics to which CDI is susceptible, the common problem of CDI is recurrence after treatment [7]. In recent years, antibiotic resistance of C. difficile and the emergence of highly pathogenic strains of CDI have been constantly increasing [8, 9].

Cysteine is a sulfur-containing amino acid that plays an important role in the normal physiological activities of cells, including the biosynthesis of methionine and several cofactors (biotin, lipoic acid, thiamine, and coenzyme A) [10]. Cysteine is also the sulfur donor of iron-sulfur clusters and can also participate in protein folding and assembly, catalysis of some enzymes, and maintenance of the intracellular redox environment. The de novo biosynthesis of bacterial cysteine requires two enzymes, serine acetyltransferase and O-acetylserine sulfhydrylase, which are encoded by the cysE and cysK genes, respectively [11]. CysE catalyzes the transfer of acetate from acetyl-CoA to serine, resulting in O-acetylserine (OAS) [12]. CysK uses pyridoxal 5’-phosphate (PLP) as a cofactor to convert OAS and sulfide to cysteine and acetate [13]. Studies have shown that CysE and CysK are critical for the survival of various microorganisms, including Mycobacterium tuberculosis, Klebsiella pneumoniae, methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus and Shigella [14–17].

A previous study reported that cysteine could significantly reduce the toxin production level of the C. difficile 630Δerm strain, VPI10463 strain and other clinical isolates [18]. The addition of cysteine and other substances to C. difficile medium significantly inhibited toxin expression and the growth of C. difficile, and this response was dose dependent. However, the changes in the expression of C. difficile-related genes caused by cysteine are still not fully understood. This study used cysE/cysK in C. difficile to explore the causes of different C. difficile genes in response to different concentrations of cysteine and the reasons for this reactivity.

Methods

Development of absolute quantitative PCR methods for the cysE/cysK and tcdA/tcdB genes

After the recovery of CD12038 (ST11/RT-, GenBank accession: CP033214) [19] C. difficile on BHI plates and anaerobic culture at 37 °C, the total genome of the strain was extracted using the Bacterial Genomic DNA Nucleic Acid Extraction Kit (Tiangen, Beijing, catalogue number: DP302). The cysE and cysK amplification primers were used to amplify the full-length fragments of the two genes. The primers used are shown in Additional file 1. For amplification, 10 µL of Premix Taq (TaKaRa, catalogue number: R004Q), 8 µL of water, 0.5 µL of primers (10 µmol), and 1 µL of template were used. The amplification procedure was as follows: 94 °C for 5 min; 30 cycles of 94 °C for 15 s, 60 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30 s; and 72 °C for 10 min. The amplification products were electrophoresed in 1.5% agarose. The electrophoresis bands were recovered by cutting the gel and used for subsequent plasmid cloning.

The pUC57 cloning vector was used for the construction of plasmids. The amplification products and plasmids were digested with BamHI and EcoRI at 30 °C and 37 °C, respectively, and the digested fragments were purified by using DNA purification kit (Tiangen, Beijing, catalogue number: DP204). The cysE and cysK genes and plasmids were ligated using T4 DNA ligase at 16 °C for 4 h. Then, the plasmids were transformed into Top10 competent cells, and positive clones were selected using ampicillin (Amp) plates. After the LB (Amp) plates were cultured at 37 °C for 24 h, positive clones were picked. The recombinant plasmids were extracted using a plasmid extraction kit and sent for sequencing (Shuoqing, Kunming).

The recombinant plasmid concentrations were determined with a UV spectrophotometer. After the concentration of the recombinant plasmid was calculated, it was serially diluted 10-fold. The gradient of recombinant plasmids for cysE was 2.88 × 102−2.88 × 1010 copies/µl, and the gradient of recombinant plasmids for cysK was 2.62 × 102−2.62 × 1010 copies/µl. Nuclear-free water, E. coli Top10 and pUC57 empty plasmid were used as negative controls for the qPCR assay. The quantitative PCR system contained 10 µL of 2×TSINGKE Master qPCR Mix, 0.8 µL of each primer (10 µmol), 1 µL of template, 7.4 µL of water, and a total volume of 20 µL. The amplification procedure was as follows: 50 °C for 2 min; 95 °C for 2 min; 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 30 s; and melting curve analysis.

The development of quantitative methods for the tcdA/tcdB genes was the same as that for the cysE/cysK genes. The length of the tcdA gene clone was 780 bp between 801 nt and 1580 nt of the full-length gene; the length of the tcdB gene clone was 800 bp between 3201 nt and 4000 nt. The gradient of the 10 recombinant plasmids for tcdA was 2.71 × 102−2.71 × 1010 copies/µl, and the gradient of the 10 recombinant plasmids for tcdB was 2.59 × 102−2.59 × 1010 copies/µl. Triplicate wells were set up for each sample for qPCR. The primer sequences are shown in Additional file 1.

Cysteine addition and gene copy number changes

The C. difficile reference strains R20291 (ST1/RT027, GenBank accession: CP029423), CD630 (ST54/RT012, GenBank accession: CP010905), CD12038 (ST11/RT-, GenBank accession: CP033214), CD21062 (ST11/RT078, ac: CP033216), CD279 (ST54, SRA accession: SRX24283857), and CD413 (ST37, SRA accession: SRX24283864) were used in this experiment [20]. BHI medium was used for the recovery and culture of the strains. The strain was streaked on BHI plates and placed in a plastic anaerobic jar (Mitsubishi, Japan, catalogue number: C-32) at 37 °C.

BHI broth was prepared by adding cysteine at concentrations of 5 mM, 20 mM and 100 mM. A bacterial solution at a concentration of 0.5 McF was prepared in PBS, and 200 µL of the abovementioned bacterial suspension was added to media with and without cysteine. The strains were collected at the 16 h and 24 h time points. The bacteria were subsequently centrifuged at 12,000×g for 5 min, after which the supernatants were removed. Total RNA was extracted from the bacteria using RNAiso Plus (TaKaRa, catalogue number: 9109) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

The expression of the cysE/cysK and tcdA/tcdB genes was analysed using One Step TB Green PrimeScript RT-PCR Kit II (TaKaRa, catalogue number: RR086A). The CT values of the four genes at different time points and under culture conditions of different cysteine concentrations were used to calculate the respective gene copy numbers according to each standard curve. The primers used are listed in Additional file 1.

RNA-seq and result validation

C. difficile CD12038 was used as a representative strain. BHI broth without cysteine addition (C0), BHI broth with 5 mM cysteine (C5) and culture with 20 mM cysteine (C20) were used as the three experimental groups. The CD12038 strain was adjusted to 0.5 McF by using PBS, 200 µL was added to the abovementioned media, and the broth was cultured anaerobically for 24 h. The bacteria were collected by centrifugation, and the supernatant was discarded. Total RNA was extracted from the bacteria using RNAiso Plus (TaKaRa, catalogue number: 9109) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Each strain included three biological replicates.

The methods used for RNA library construction, sequencing and bioinformatics analysis were previously described [21]. The RNA-seq results were verified via RT-qPCR by the 2−ΔΔCT method, and several genes were selected as differentially expressed candidates, whereas 16 S rRNA was used as an internal reference gene. The primer sequences are shown in Additional file 1.

Growth curve of the strain

The CD12038 strain was cultured as described above, and 200 µl of 0.5 McF bacterial suspension was added to 5 ml of BHI or BHI cysteine broth, respectively. The OD values (600 nm) of the strains in the C0, C5, C20 and C100 groups were subsequently determined every two hours until 24 h. The growth curves of the strains were plotted according to the measured OD values of CD12038 at different time points.

Western blot analysis of toxins

The culture process for the CD12038 strain was the same as that described above. After 24 h of culture, the medium was collected by centrifugation, and the supernatants were filtered through a 0.45 μm filter. WB detection of the TcdA and TcdB toxin proteins was performed using the Abby (Biotechne) automatic protein expression analysis system, and the experiments were performed according to the instruction manual of the kits. TcdA and TcdB were detected using anti-TcdA and anti-TcdB antibodies (chicken IgY; 1:1000 dilution; List Biological Laboratories, catalogue number: 753 A and 754 A), and a goat anti-chicken IgY secondary antibody (HRP-conjugated; 1:2000 dilution; Invitrogen, catalogue number: A16054) was used [8]. The bacterial GAPDH was used as an internal reference for the relative quantitative study of the WB results in terms of the relative grayscale values of toxin proteins/internal reference proteins.

ELISA detection of TcdA and TcdB

Determination of toxins A/B in C. difficile strain CD12038 in media supplemented with different concentrations of cysteine was carried out as previously described [8]. The bacteria were cultured for 24 h described above. Two hundred microliters of medium was added to the 96-well plate at 4 °C overnight, and the plate was then blocked with 5% skim milk. The TcdA and TcdB primary antibodies as well as the HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies were diluted as in the WB process. A TMB chromogenic kit (Sangon, China, catalogue number: C520026) was used to determine the absorbance values at 450 nm, and the concentrations of the toxins were calculated on the basis of the standard curve of TcdA/TcdB [8].

Determination of iron in bacterial culture

The cultivation process of the CD12038 strain was as described above. After culturing for 24 h, the supernatants were collected by centrifugation and filtered through a 0.45 μm filter. An iron content detection kit (Biosharp, catalogue number: BL898B) was used to detect the iron in the culture media of the different experimental groups.

Metabolomics of CD12038

The cultivation process of the CD12038 strain was the same as that described above. After culturing for 24 h, the culture supernatants were collected by centrifugation, filtered through a 0.45 μm filter, and then subjected to metabolomics assays. There were three groups of microbial metabolite samples, which were consistent with the grouping in the RNA-seq experiment. Each group included three biological replicates and was subjected to LC-MS-based metabolomics detection [22].

In general, 100 µL samples were taken, mixed with 400 µL of extraction mixture (MeOH: ACN, 1:1), sonicated for 10 min at 4℃, and incubated for 1 h at −40℃ to precipitate proteins. The samples were subsequently centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4℃. The supernatants were transferred to the fresh glass vials for analysis. LC-MS/MS analyses were performed using an UHPLC system (Vanquish, Thermo Fisher Scientific) with a Waters ACQUITY UPLC BEH Amide (2.1 mm × 50 mm, 1.7 μm) coupled to an Orbitrap Exploris 120 mass spectrometer (Orbitrap MS, Thermo). The Orbitrap Exploris 120 mass spectrometer was used for its ability to acquire MS/MS spectra in information-dependent acquisition mode under the control of acquisition software (Xcalibur, Thermo).

The raw data were converted to mzXML format via ProteoWizard software (V3.0) [23], and R package software (V4.3.3) was used for metabolite identification. The database used was BiotreeDB (V3.0), and the R package was used for visual analysis.

Changes in gene expression back to normal culture

The CD12038 strain cultured in BHI and BHI supplemented with 20 mM cysteine for 24 h was used. The culture mixture was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm at 4 °C for 10 min, the supernatant was discarded, the bacteria were resuspended in PBS and mixed well, and this process was repeated twice to thoroughly wash the cysteine added to the medium. Then, the BHI and BHI-cysteine strains were resuspended in PBS, and 200 µL of BHI and BHI-cysteine strains adjusted to a concentration of 1 McF were added to non-supplemented BHI broth and incubated at 3, 6, 12, 24, and 36 time points. After that, the bacteria were harvested, and total RNA was extracted with an RNAiso Plus (TaKaRa, catalogue number: 9109) kit. The gene copy numbers of cysE/cysK and tcdA/tcdB were detected using the One Step TB Green PrimeScript RT-qPCR Kit II (TaKaRa, catalogue number: RR086A). The primer sequences are shown in Additional file 1.

Cytotoxicity and apoptosis assay

C. difficile strains CD12038 and R20291 incubated for 24 h in BHI (C0 group), and BHI supplemented with 20 mM cysteine (C20 group) were used for pathogen-host interaction analysis with Caco-2 cells [24, 25]. The cultured bacteria were washed three times and suspended in PBS to a 0.5 McF concentration. Monolayer Caco-2 cells were prepared by seeding T25 cell culture flasks at 37 °C in 5% CO2 for 24 h. Five hundred microliters of C. difficile strains CD12038 and R20291 suspensions (5 × 105 CFU/ml) were added to Caco-2 cells in flasks respectively and incubated for 3 h anaerobically. The cells were examined by light microscopy (Motic), and three biological replicates were performed in triplicate. The supernatants of the co-cultures were filtered and used to measure cytotoxicity by measuring LDH release (Biosharp, catalogue number: BL1405A) in the cells.

An ApoAlert Annexin V (TaKaRa, catalogue number: 630110) kit was used to detect the apoptosis and necrosis of Caco-2 cells after interaction with C. difficile [26]. The experimental procedure was carried out according to the manufacturer’s instructions. In general, Annexin V-FITC was used to measure the level of apoptosis, resulting in green fluorescence; Propidium iodide (PI) was used to measure the level of cell necrosis, resulting in red fluorescence. Fluorescence microscopy was used to observe the cell staining results. ImageJ software (V1.54) was used to calculate the average fluorescence intensity of the cellular fluorescence images for quantitative analysis of apoptosis and necrosis [27, 28]. Each fluorescence photograph was split into separate green and red fluorescence and converted to grayscale values for calculating the average fluorescence intensity. Three biological replicates were performed in triplicate.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed with GraphPad Prism (V8.0) software. The data were expressed as x ± s. Comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Comparisons between two groups were performed using an independent samples t test. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. SnapGene (V7.2.1) was used to display the structural composition of the sequenced plasmids.

RNA-seq and validation of the results

The CD12038 strain was used for subsequent experiments. Pairwise comparisons of differentially expressed genes among BHI (C0 group), BHI-5 mM cysteine (C5 group), and BHI-20 mM cysteine (C20 group) were performed after RNA-seq of the strains. Several genes were upregulated or downregulated in each group (Fig. 3a), among which the number of genes whose expression was downregulated was higher in the C5 and C20 groups than in the C0 group. In addition, more genes were downregulated in the C20 group than in the C5 group.

The shared and unique gene sets between the different groups are shown in a Venn diagram in Fig. 3b. Pairwise comparisons revealed that there were 99 common gene sets across all the groups, 136 gene sets were unique to the C0 vs. C5 comparison, and 176 gene sets were unique to the C0 vs. C20 comparison. There were 126 consensus genes in the C5 vs. C20 comparison.

PCA revealed that the C0, C5, and C20 groups formed their own clusters (Fig. 3c). GO functional enrichment of the DEGs revealed that in the cysteine addition group, the enriched genes were involved mainly in transport, localization, establishment of localization, carboxylic acid metabolic process, oxoacid metabolic process, organic acid metabolic process, amide metabolic process, and amide biosynthetic process, as shown in Fig. 3d. In terms of molecular function, the main enriched genes included transporter activity, transmembrane transporter activity, oxidoreductase activity, RNA binding, structural molecule activity, structural constituent of ribosome, rRNA binding, monoatomic cation transmembrane transporter activity, monoatomic ion transmembrane transporter activity, inorganic cation transmembrane transporter activity and active monoatomic ion transmembrane transporter activity, as shown in Fig. 3e. The results of KEGG functional enrichment revealed that the enriched genes were involved mainly in carbon fixation pathways in prokaryotes, ribosomes, butanoate metabolism, the phosphotransferase system (PTS), and sulfur metabolism (Fig. 3f) in the cysteine addition group. In addition, six candidate genes with significant differential expression were selected for relative quantitative PCR to validate the RNA-seq results. These differentially expressed genes were ferrous iron transport protein A (RS07920), ferrous iron transport protein B (RS07925), metal ABC transporter substrate-binding protein (RS08035), metal ABC transporter permease (RS08045), iron chelate uptake ABC transporter family permease subunit (RS08770), and siderophore ABC transporter substrate-binding protein (RS08785). All these genes are involved in bacterial iron transport, uptake, and chelation. The relative quantitative results revealed that the expression levels of all six genes in the C20 group were higher than those in the C0 group (Fig. 3g-l), and the differences were statistically significant (P < 0.05), with the RS07925 gene exhibiting the greatest differential expression, followed by RS08785 and RS08770. The results of relative quantification were consistent with those of RNA-seq.

Determination of toxin, iron and metabolomics analysis

The growth status of CD12038 in the presence of different amounts of cysteine is shown in Fig. 4a. In BHI-5 mM cysteine, the bacteria appeared black (ferrous sulfide), and the black color was the most obvious in BHI-20 mM cysteine. The growth of the strain was significantly inhibited at the concentration of 100 mM cysteine. The growth curve of strain CD12038 in media supplemented with different concentrations of cysteine is shown in Fig. 4b. The strain rapidly increased from 6 h to 14 h in the C5 group, with higher OD values than those in the C0, C20 and C100 groups. During this logarithmic growth phase, the difference between the OD values of the four concentration groups was statistically significant according to pairwise comparisons. The C. difficile in the C0 group reached the logarithmic growth stage at the 16 h time point, and gradually approached those in the C5 and C20 groups. In the C100 group, the CD12038 strain did not significantly grow until the 24 h time point.

The WB results for TcdA and TcdB revealed that the expression levels of TcdA and TcdB in CD12038 in BHI-5 mM cysteine were higher than those in cultures without cysteine or cysteine supplemented with other concentrations, which was consistent with the mRNA expression data, as shown in Fig. 4c and d. The relative grayscale value of TcdA normalized to the internal reference GAPDH was the highest in the BHI-5mM group at 0.517 ± 0.008, followed by the BHI-20mM group with a relative value of 0.475 ± 0.003, and the lowest in the BHI-100mM group. The pairwise comparisons between the four groups were statistically significant (Fig. 4e). The relative grayscale values of TcdB toxin were similar to those of TcdA, which were also the highest in the BHI-5mM at 0.606 ± 0.008 and the lowest in the BHI-100mM group. The differences among the four groups were also statistically significant in pairwise comparisons (Fig. 4f). The origin western blot data are shown in Additional file 3.

The ELISA results for toxins A/B revealed that the concentration of TcdA was the highest in the BHI-5mM (0.190 ± 0.012 ng/ml) and 20mM (0.175 ± 0.011 ng/ml) cysteine groups, and the differences were statistically significant between the BHI and BHI-100mM groups. However, the difference between the two groups was not significant, as shown in Fig. 4g. The concentration of TcdB was the highest in the BHI-5mM group (2.033 ± 0.036 ng/ml), followed by the BHI-20mM (1.625 ± 0.025 ng/ml), and the lowest toxin concentration was found in the BHI-100mM. The TcdB concentrations in different groups were statistically significant for pairwise comparisons, as shown in Fig. 4h.

We measured the iron concentration in BHI broth, and BHI C. difficile cultures in the C0, C5, C20 and C100 groups. The results revealed that the iron concentrations in the BHI broth were 26.70 ± 2.05 µmol/L, and 17.19 ± 1.94 µmol/L for the C0 group, and the iron concentrations in C5, C20 and C100 groups were 8.78 ± 0.80, 2.75 ± 0.45, and 24.35 ± 0.66 µmol/L, respectively. The difference between pairwise comparisons was statistically significant except for that between C100 and BHI broth, as shown in Fig. 4i.

A metabolomics study on CD12038 cultures from BHI, BHI-5 mM cysteine and BHI-20 mM cysteine was also performed. Discussion

As important genes in cysteine metabolism and sulfur metabolism, cysE and cysK play key roles in the physiological and biochemical processes of many pathogens [12, 29]. For example, CysK participates in the cysteine synthase complex (CSC) formed by CysE; activates contact-dependent growth inhibition toxin; acts as a transcription enhancer by stabilizing the interaction between the transcription factor CymR and DNA; Vibrio biofilm formation; resistance to ofloxacin and tellurite; and cysteine desulfurization [30–35]. Therefore, the inhibition of cysE/cysK expression not only downregulates the cysteine biosynthetic pathway but also may affect the above biological functions. Notably, CysK also serves as a drug/adjuvant target in the numerous species mentioned above. Modulating CysK expression within pathogens may further influence pathogen growth and virulence expression, facilitating its application in clinical treatment. As demonstrated in this study, cysK can indeed affect toxin expression in C. difficile. Previously, we found that the internal reference genes rpoA and rpoC, which were commonly used in C. difficile, changed greatly under different culture conditions, especially when some amino acids were added to the medium. It was difficult to accurately quantify the gene expression levels of strains via relative quantification methods. Therefore, in this study, we used the pUC57 plasmid to clone the cysE and cysK genes, which were serially diluted and amplified to obtain a standard curve. The linear range, sensitivity, specificity, and repeatability of this method were sufficient, and it could be used for subsequent studies of C. difficile on the basis of the cysE and cysK genes. Moreover, we developed absolute quantification methods for tcdA and tcdB for subsequent studies.

Several studies have reported that the addition of certain amino acids (especially cysteine) to C. difficile medium can reduce toxin expression [18, 36]. This phenomenon varied according to the strain. Karlsson et al. added nine amino acids to peptone yeast extract cultures of the VPI 10,463 C. difficile strain to decrease the production of C. difficile toxin [36]. Among them, seven amino acids were found to have a definite inhibitory effect on toxin production, whereas proline, especially cysteine, had the greatest effect on the reference strains and clinical isolates. These results indicated that there was a coupling relationship between the specific metabolic processes of C. difficile and toxin expression and that certain amino acids could coordinately altered these pathways.

The changes in toxin expression in the commonly used C. difficile reference strains R20291 and CD630 were consistent with those reported previously in this study. In media without cysteine for 16 h, the gene copy numbers of tcdA and tcdB in R20291 and CD630 were significantly higher than those in media supplemented with cysteine. The changes in the copy numbers of the cysE/cysK genes were consistent with those of the toxin genes, whose expressions were downregulated in both reference strains.

However, the different clinical isolates presented various changes in the copy numbers of cysE/cysK and tcdA/tcdB. The ST37 genotype of C. difficile (CD413) was characterized by high expression of the cysE/cysK gene at the 16 h time point when 5 mM cysteine was added. When 5 mM cysteine was added, the copy numbers of both tcdA and tcdB increased for the CD21062, CD12038, CD279 and CD413 strains, which was higher than that without supplementation. In other words, the addition of low concentrations of cysteine to clinical isolates of C. difficile, such as the ST11, ST54 and ST37 genotypes, could increase the expression level of C. difficile toxins. This phenomenon occurred for R20291 and CD630 after 24 h of culture.

Dubois et al. reported that the toxin synthesis level of CD630 C. difficile was affected by environmental signals, such as the phosphotransferase system (PTS), biotin and amino acids, especially cysteine [37]. In the presence of 10 mM cysteine, large amounts of sulfide and pyruvate were produced, indicating that cysteine was actively degraded by cysteine desulfurase. They found that cysteine could regulate the activities of cysteine metabolism, amino acid biosynthesis, fermentation, energy metabolism, iron acquisition and stress. This study revealed that, for C. difficile CD12038, the amount of hydrogen sulfide produced at 20 mM cysteine was the highest; when cysteine reached 100 mM, the copy numbers of the two genes decreased to their lowest observed values in this experiment. Similarly, the RNA-seq results suggested that the DEGs associated with the addition of cysteine were enriched mainly in the carbon fixation pathways of prokaryotes, ribosomes, butanoate metabolism, the phosphorotransferase system (PTS) and the sulfur metabolism pathways, which was similar to the results of previous studies. Further differential expression and relative quantification of genes suggested that iron plays a key role in the cultivation and metabolic processes of C. difficile. In 20 mM cysteine medium, the iron concentration in BHI broth decreased sharply, the sulfide generated from the degradation of cysteine combined with iron to form ferrous sulfide, and the bacteria appeared black. Owing to the sharp decrease in iron content in the environment, the expression of genes involved in multiple pathways, such as iron uptake, transport, and chelation, increased in response to the iron deficiency environment in the medium. Transition metals, especially iron, are very important substances for the metabolism of organisms and participate in many important biochemical reactions and physiological processes‌, especially by serving as the active reaction centers of various enzymes.

In this study, the metabolomic results indicate that cysteine S-sulfate is a product highly correlated with cysteine metabolism in the C20 group. It may represent a storage form of cysteine or an intermediate in sulphur transfer. Within the C5 group, nitrogen-containing compounds presented elevated abundance levels. These nitrogen compounds influence the demand for and degradation strategy of cysteine in C. difficile, whereas the H₂S produced during cysteine metabolism affects the bacterial viability when these nitrogen sources are utilized. Furthermore, H₂S influences bacterial adaptability and pathogenicity within the environment by chelating iron, providing antioxidant effects, and stabilizing core enzyme systems. Across all groups receiving varying doses of cysteine, the relative abundance of O-acetylserine remained low, closely correlating with the inhibition of the cysteine synthetic metabolic pathway of C. difficile.

In fact, relevant studies on the effects of cysteine addition on the expression of C. difficile toxins have indicated that iron transport was one of the main pathways enriched in the differential expression of genes. Gu et al. analysed the effects of the addition of different amino acids to TY media on the pathogenesis of R20291 C. difficile [38]. They indicated that cysteine addition significantly inhibited the expression level of toxin genes in the strain. The response of C. difficile to cysteine was a complex and systematic process that involved many intracellular metabolic processes. On the basis of existing reports, processes such as iron transport, amino acid metabolism, energy metabolism and flagellar synthesis may have played important roles. In the following study, ClosTron was used to inactive several genes, and the toxin production levels of the mutant and wild-type strains in TY and TYC media were compared [39]. They demonstrated that only the cdsB gene abolished the effect of cysteine in TYC medium on the toxin production level of C. difficile. CdsB was a major inducible cysteine desulfurase in C. difficile. The expression level of the cdsB gene was significantly increased, and hydrogen sulfide production was significantly increased. They concluded that the inhibitory effect of cysteine on the toxigenesis of C. difficile may be caused by the accumulation of cysteine degradation products, especially hydrogen sulfide.

We considered that the inactivation study of the cdsB gene also revealed the important role of iron because cdsB was a cysteine desulfurase that led to the production of hydrogen sulfide. The subsequent hydrogen sulfide reacted with iron to generate ferrous sulfide. The deletion of this gene actually affected the free iron concentration in subsequent cultures, which was also a reactive function of bacteria in maintaining iron homeostasis itself. On the basis of the results of this study, combined with those of previous reports, we propose that the addition of cysteine significantly influences multiple metabolic processes and toxin expression levels in C. difficile. Iron availability and bacterial iron homeostasis may play pivotal roles in this process; however, direct evidence linking these two factors is lacking, necessitating further investigation.

The relationship between cysteine addition and C. difficile pathogenicity was initially investigated using the Caco-2 cell interaction model. The CD12038 strain induced by 20 mM cysteine had a higher pathogenic effect on cells than the uninduced strain. The LDH in the cell supernatant indicated that the addition of cysteine increased the virulence or pathogenicity of CD12038. Compared with the C0 group, the CD12038 C20 group exhibited higher levels of cell necrosis, although the C0 group exhibited higher levels apoptosis than did the C20 group. This finding was also consistent with previous findings that when CD12038 C. difficile was induced with cysteine at concentrations of 5 mM and 20 mM for 24 h, the tcdA/tcdB gene copy number of the strain was significantly higher than that of the uninduced strain. In contrast, R20291 C. difficile had completely different pathogenic effects. Regardless of the concentration of cysteine added, the pathogenicity induced by R20291 decreased, and the pathogenicity of the uninduced R20291 strain was always the highest. Both the LDH of the cell supernatants measured and the degree of apoptosis or necrosis induced were consistently highest in the R20291 C0 group. This finding also coincided with the results of the virulence gene copy number studies of BHI and BHI-cysteine addition to R20291. Thus, the effects of cysteine addition on the virulence-producing ability or pathogenicity of C. difficile were inconsistent across genotypes and clinical isolates or reference strains.

Conclusions

Transition metals, especially iron, are very important substances for the metabolism of organisms and participate in many important biochemical reactions and physiological processes‌, especially by serving as the active reaction centers of various enzymes. Different reference strains of C. difficile and clinical isolates had different doses and durations of responsiveness to cysteine addition in this study, and the critical link involved the availability of iron in the environment of the bacteria. We considered that this was a response of bacteria to the regulation of gene expression through the maintenance of iron homeostasis.
