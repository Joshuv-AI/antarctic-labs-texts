Preoperative step count as a predictor of early postoperative mobility and patient-reported outcomes following lumbar spine surgery
Authors: Junho Song, Austen D. Katz, Timothy Hoang, Ryan Hoang, Alex Ngan, Joshua Zhang, John J. Corvi, Nikan K. Namiri, Samuel K. Cho, Andrew C. Hecht, Sheeraz A. Qureshi, Sohrab Virk, David Essig
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectives:Objective activity monitoring may provide scalable, real-world functional data in lumbar spine surgery patients. We evaluated whether preoperative daily step count predicts early postoperative ambulation and patient-reported outcomes (PROs).Materials and Methods:We performed a retrospective cohort study of adults undergoing elective lumbar decompression, discectomy, or fusion with remote monitoring of daily step counts (preoperative days 7-1 and postoperative days [PODs] 1–28) and longitudinal PROs (Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System [PROMIS] – Pain/Physical/Mental and Oswestry Disability Index [ODI] at baseline, 6 weeks, 12 weeks, 6 months, and 1 year). Patients were stratified by mean preoperative step count into low activity (<2500 steps/day) versus normal activity (≥2500 steps/day). Between-group comparisons used the Chi-square tests for categorical variables and independent t-tests for continuous variables (P < 0.05).Results:Forty-seven patients were included (mean age: 51.8 ± 13.7 years; body mass index: 29.85 ± 5.00), including 21 (44.7%) low-activity and 26 (55.3%) normal-activity patients. Preoperative steps were lower in the low-activity group (1354 ± 639 vs. 5148 ± 2399; P < 0.001). Postoperative step counts did not differ during POD 1–7 (717 ± 857 vs. 1342 ± 1468; P = 0.091) but were higher in the normal-activity group during POD 8–14 (1515 ± 2048 vs. 2863 ± 2288; P = 0.044) and cumulatively through POD 28 (POD 1–28: 1360 ± 1584 vs. 2784 ± 1978; P = 0.010). By POD 8–14, 53.8% of normal-activity patients achieved ≥2500 steps/day versus 14.3% of low-activity patients (P = 0.005). Normal-activity patients reported lower PROMIS Pain at 6 weeks (2.9 ± 2.4 vs. 4.8 ± 2.1; P = 0.011) and 12 weeks (2.4 ± 2.2 vs. 4.0 ± 2.5; P = 0.029) and lower ODI at 12 weeks (17.4 ± 14.7 vs. 29.5 ± 17.7; P = 0.018).Conclusions:Preoperative step count stratifies early postoperative mobility and short-term pain/disability trajectories after lumbar spine surgery. Objective activity monitoring may support perioperative risk stratification and targeted prehabilitation.

INTRODUCTION

Lumbar degenerative spine disorders commonly limit walking and daily function, and improving mobility is a central goal of surgical treatment.[1234] Most postoperative assessments rely on episodic patient-reported outcomes (PROs), which are subjective and may miss short-term recovery dynamics.[56] Objective activity monitoring with step counts is feasible in spine surgery and can capture real-world function continuously, with baseline steps correlating with disability measures.[78] This approach may be particularly useful in the early postoperative period, when recovery changes day-to-day and clinic follow-up is infrequent.

Early postoperative mobilization is associated with better short-term outcomes after lumbar surgery, including lower length of stay and fewer early complications in large database studies.[910] Beyond the immediate perioperative window, step-based recovery targets also appear clinically meaningful, with higher steps/day at 6 weeks associated with favorable 1-year outcomes and no opioid use.[11] These findings suggest that step counts can function as more than a passive metric and may serve as an interpretable indicator of recovery progress.

Preoperative activity is often low in lumbar surgical candidates and is inversely associated with disability and fear of movement, suggesting that baseline steps may reflect both functional reserve and psychosocial constraints relevant to recovery.[12] However, the extent to which preoperative step-defined activity status stratifies early postoperative mobility and longer-term PRO trajectories after lumbar surgery remains incompletely characterized.

The objective of this study was to determine whether preoperative daily step count, stratified by a clinically used threshold of 2500 steps per day, is associated with early postoperative ambulation and postoperative PRO trajectories after lumbar spine surgery.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This was a retrospective cohort study of adults (≥18 years) undergoing elective lumbar decompression, lumbar discectomy, or lumbar fusion at a single institution. Patients participated in a remote recovery tracking program that collected daily step counts via smartphone and/or wearable device sensors synced to the platform. Eligible patients were required to have sufficient perioperative step-count data to characterize baseline activity and early postoperative recovery, defined as step data during the preoperative window (days 7-1 before surgery) and through postoperative day (POD) 28. Patients undergoing non-lumbar procedures (cervical or thoracic) were excluded.

The primary exposure was the mean preoperative daily step count calculated from preoperative days 7-1. Patients were stratified into low-activity (<2500 steps/day) and normal-activity (≥2500 steps/day) groups using a clinically used threshold. Objective postoperative mobility was assessed using mean daily step counts during POD 1–7 and POD 8–14 and cumulatively during POD 1–14, POD 1–21, and POD 1–28. In addition, the proportion of patients achieving ≥2500 steps/day was evaluated within each postoperative interval. PROs were collected electronically preoperatively and at 6 weeks, 12 weeks, 6 months, and 1 year postoperatively, including Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) Pain, PROMIS Physical, PROMIS Mental, and the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI). Demographic and clinical variables included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), race, and procedure type (decompression, discectomy, and fusion).

Continuous variables were compared between activity groups using independent samples t-tests and categorical variables using the Chi-square tests (or Fisher’s exact tests when appropriate), with statistical significance defined as P < 0.05. Analyses were performed using SPSS version 28 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). This retrospective study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the senior author’s institution, and informed consent was waived when applicable due to analysis of de-identified data.

RESULTS

A total of 47 patients were included, including 21 (44.7%) classified as low activity and 26 (55.3%) classified as normal activity. The mean age was 51.8 ± 13.7 years, and the mean BMI was 29.85 ± 5.00 kg/m2. Compared with the low-activity group, the normal-activity group was younger (48.2 ± 13.0 vs. 56.3 ± 13.6 years, P = 0.045). There were no statistically significant between-group differences in BMI (28.54 ± 3.87 vs. 31.41 ± 5.79, P = 0.051), sex (female: 34.6% vs. 61.9%, P = 0.062), race (P = 0.377), or surgery type (P = 0.243) [Table 1].

DISCUSSION

In this retrospective cohort of lumbar spine surgery patients with perioperative step-count monitoring, preoperative activity status stratified by a 2500 steps/day threshold was associated with meaningful differences in early postoperative mobility and short-term recovery. Although step counts were not statistically different during POD 1–7, patients in the normal-activity group demonstrated higher ambulation during POD 8–14 and across cumulative intervals through POD 28, and they were more likely to achieve ≥2500 steps/day during POD 8–14. In parallel, normal-activity patients reported lower PROMIS Pain at 6 and 12 weeks and lower ODI at 12 weeks, suggesting that preoperative step counts may reflect functional reserve relevant to both objective functional recovery and early patient-perceived improvement.

The timing of divergence in step counts is clinically informative. Immediate postoperative ambulation is strongly influenced by perioperative factors such as pain, nausea, discharge timing, and inhospital mobilization protocols, which may attenuate between-patient differences in the 1st postoperative week.[131415] Large database studies nevertheless demonstrate that earlier ambulation after lumbar surgery is associated with improved short-term outcomes, including shorter length of stay and fewer readmissions and urinary retention events, supporting the broader principle that mobilization matters in spine recovery.[910] Our finding that baseline activity stratified recovery beginning in the 2nd postoperative week suggests that once the acute perioperative phase passes, underlying functional reserve becomes a stronger determinant of mobility.

Our results also align with prior work supporting step counts as a clinically meaningful recovery metric after lumbar surgery. A prospective pilot study demonstrated the feasibility of continuous activity monitoring after spine surgery and showed that greater baseline steps correlated with lower disability and higher physical health status, supporting step counts as an objective adjunct to conventional PROs.[7] In addition, a secondary analysis reported that steps/day at 6 weeks after lumbar surgery were associated with higher odds of favorable 1-year outcomes and no opioid use, and it evaluated step thresholds, including 2500 and 3500 steps/day, as potential discriminators of longer-term recovery.[11] While our study focused on an earlier period (POD 1–28) and used a preoperative threshold rather than a 6-week target, the overall message is consistent: step counts provide interpretable functional information that relates to clinically important outcomes.

The association between preoperative activity and early postoperative recovery likely reflects a combination of physiologic and behavioral factors. In patients scheduled for lumbar fusion, objectively measured steps/day are often low, and step counts are negatively associated with disability and fear of movement, suggesting that baseline ambulation captures not only physical capacity but also psychosocial barriers that may persist into recovery.[12] In this context, step-defined low activity may identify patients who could benefit from targeted prehabilitation, expectation setting, and early structured walking support.[16] Importantly, our low-activity group was older, and age-related differences in conditioning, comorbidity burden, or postoperative caution may confound observed associations.[1718] Future work using multivariable adjustment and prospective designs will be necessary to better isolate the independent contribution of baseline step counts.

These findings have practical implications for perioperative care pathways. Because step counts can be collected passively through common consumer devices and viewed longitudinally, they may help clinicians identify patients at risk for slower early mobility recovery and guide allocation of supportive resources such as early outpatient physical therapy, remote coaching, or tailored walking goals.[19202122] Step thresholds should be framed as guidance rather than rigid targets, but existing literature suggests that step goals in the early postoperative period may be clinically meaningful and feasible.[112324] Integrating objective activity monitoring into recovery pathways may also complement enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS)-based efforts emphasizing early mobilization, which have been associated with improved short-term outcomes when ambulation occurs within the 1st POD or within hours of surgery.[910]

This study has important limitations that must be considered. First, the cohort was small and derived from a single institution, which limits generalizability and statistical power, particularly for subgroup analyses by procedure type. Second, this observational design is vulnerable to confounding, including the between-group age difference and unmeasured variables such as comorbidity burden, analgesic use, and socioeconomic factors that influence both baseline activity and recovery. Third, step counts depend on device wear and sensor characteristics; measurement error and missingness are possible, and step counts do not capture activity intensity, gait quality, or nonambulatory exercise. Finally, we evaluated objective mobility through POD 28; longer follow-up may better characterize how early step trajectories relate to sustained functional recovery.

CONCLUSION

In summary, preoperative step count stratified early postoperative ambulation through POD 28 and was associated with improved short-term pain and disability outcomes after lumbar spine surgery. Objective activity monitoring may provide a scalable adjunct to PRO-based follow-up and inform patient counseling and targeted perioperative optimization strategies.
