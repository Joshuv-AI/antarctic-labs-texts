INTRODUCTION

Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocols represent a significant advancement in surgical care, designed to standardize and optimize patient recovery following various types of surgeries, including abdominal procedures. Conventional postoperative recovery was a difficult period associated with long period of hospitalization, a high incidence of complications, and frequent re-hospitalization.[1] In the past, postoperative orders included early immobilization, minimal enteral intake, and frequent administration of opioids, which were injurious to the patient’s health and hence slowed postoperative recovery. EREAS strategies were therefore instituted as measures to help minimize the physical and psychological ordeal of surgery, enhance recovery periods, and eventually boost patients’ outcomes.[2] Surgery involves damage to body tissues and, therefore, exposes patients to complications afterward.[3] A sample of patient records from 474 centers across 27 nations revealed that 16% of patients experienced poor results following elective surgeries. The overall postoperative complication rate was 8%, with a 5% incidence of Surgical site infection, 3% of bleeding, 2.7% of arrhythmia, and 1. The consequences of such postoperative complications can be multiple. For example, they may lead to outcomes such as psychological effects, deterioration in the quality of life, longer hospital stays, higher costs, and mortality.[4] It is estimated that around the globe, four million people die within 30 days of surgery annually, which could account for 7%. 7% of global deaths.[5]

Therefore, the main objective of the study is to determine the effectiveness of ERAS protocols in improving postoperative outcomes and reducing hospital readmission rates in patients undergoing abdominal surgery.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This prospective cohort study was conducted at the Department of General Surgery, London North West NHS Trust, United Kingdom, during the period from 2023 to 2024. This study involves a total of 650 patients who were divided into two groups: the intervention group, which received care according to ERAS protocols, and the control group, which received standard postoperative care. Patients aged >18 years and scheduled for elective abdominal surgery were included in the study. Those with ASA III or above, those requiring emergency surgery, or having previous history of opioids dependency were excluded. Patients’ identity was concealed by study numbers, and they were randomly allocated to either the ERAS group or the standard care group. The ERAS group received care according to an implemented ERAS protocol, which comprised preoperative talks, telephone consultations, the use of carbohydrates, short fasting time, and the use of non-opioid analgesics. Normothermia, minimal access surgery, and intraoperative fluid balance were the other preventive strategies employed in the operating theatre. In the postoperative period, patients began receiving regular food intake and were mobilized as soon as possible. The control group was treated like other general surgical patients who undergo preoperative fasting, intraoperative fluid infusion according to traditional teachings, and postoperative management that entailed early feeding restriction, overuse of opioids, and gradual ambulation. Data were collected prospectively from all patients. The main quantitative data collected and analyzed in the study were postoperative complications, duration of hospital stay, and the rate of readmissions within a one-month period from discharge. Descriptive statistics were performed using SPSS v23. 0. A statistical analysis was conducted in which a multivariate logistic regression model was employed to control for possible confounding variables. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant, for the study and the study was approved by the Hospital’s Institutional Review Board in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Participants’ permission was sought before joining the study, and all participants agreed to voluntariness of the study.

RESULTS

The mean age of patients in the ERAS group was 56.4 ± 12.3 years, while in the standard care group it was 57.1 ± 11.8 years. Out of 650 patients, there were 339 male and 311 female patients. Table 1 shows that the ERAS group demonstrated significantly better outcomes compared to the standard care group. Postoperative complications were lower in the ERAS group (13.8% vs. 24%), and patients experienced shorter hospital stays (4.2 vs. 6.7 days). The ERAS group had lower 30-day readmission rates (6.2% vs. 15.4%), quicker mobilization (12.5 vs. 24.8 hours), and faster resumption of oral intake (8.6 vs. 18.2 hours). Pain levels were lower (VAS 3.1 vs. 5.8), and patient satisfaction was higher (89% vs. 74%) in the ERAS group, with all differences being statistically significant.

Table 2 showed that ERAS protocol implementation was significantly associated with improved postoperative outcomes, with an odds ratio (OR) of 0.52 (95% CI: 0.35–0.78, P = 0.001). Other variables, such as age, gender, and type of surgery (colorectal, hepatobiliary, or gastric), did not show statistically significant associations with outcomes, indicating that ERAS was the primary factor influencing the improved results.

DISCUSSION

The findings from this study underscore the effectiveness of ERAS protocols in improving postoperative outcomes and reducing hospital readmission rates among patients undergoing elective abdominal surgery. The findings analyzed in this study also show that the implementation of ERAS protocols leads to better result in parameters such as postoperative complications, the length of the hospital stay, and patient satisfaction indicating the potential of ERAS protocols to improve patient outcome and subsequent delivery of health care.[6] The adverse effects could also have been minimized, as the use of multimodal analgesia, early mobilizations, and early initiation of oral food intake were more pronounced in the ERAS group. This led to less physiological stress in the patients and faster recovery.[7] In addition to attending to the needs of patients who can be discharged early from the hospital and thus avoid associated risks, early discharge impacts multiple dynamics of healthcare systems, include bed turnover rates and general hospital costs.[8] The study also noted a reduction in readmission to the hospital within 30 days after discharge for the ERAS group, which was 6%, and the standard care group, which was 15%. This is especially important for readmissions because it means that the effectiveness of the initial postoperative care using ERAS protocols does not deteriorate once the patient is discharged, and hence the overall morbidity is lower.[9] Fewer readmissions are also a crucial objective, as improved patient handling can help to decrease the likelihood of readmission and contribute to substantial amounts of cost savings for healthcare organizations. These additional secondary outcomes support the use of ERAS protocols even more. The patients in the ERAS group started mobilization and oral intake at an earlier time compared to the patients in the SC group.[10]

CONCLUSION

It is concluded that ERAS protocols significantly improve postoperative outcomes, including reducing complications, shortening hospital stays, and lowering readmission rates for patients undergoing elective abdominal surgery. The findings support the broader adoption of ERAS protocols in clinical practice to enhance patient recovery and optimize healthcare efficiency.
