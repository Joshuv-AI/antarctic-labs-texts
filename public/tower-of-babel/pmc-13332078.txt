Stiripentol induces ER stress and pyroptosis in pancreatic cancer cells under metabolic stress by dysregulating SFAs/UFAs homeostasis
Authors: Jie Ding, Jingbing Pan, Wenduo Xue, Siyuan Ying, Siyu Zuo, Runhe Xia, Yandong Li, Ming Quan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is a highly lethal malignancy characterized by profound chemoresistance, creating an urgent need for novel therapies to overcome treatment failure. Although stiripentol has shown antitumour activity in glioblastoma (GBM), its precise mechanism of action remains unclear. Further investigation is needed to elucidate both its therapeutic efficacy and the underlying molecular mechanisms in PDAC.MethodsThis study validated the proposed mechanisms and therapeutic efficacy through a multi-faceted approach integrating transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses, complemented by both in vivo and in vitro experiments.ResultsCCK8, EdU, and colony formation assays demonstrated that stiripentol inhibited the proliferation of PDAC cells in vitro. PI staining and Western blotting (WB) confirmed that stiripentol induced pyroptosis in PDAC cells cultured under low-serum (1% FBS) condition. Integrated multiomics (RNA-seq and lipid metabolomics), combined with functional validation, revealed that stiripentol triggered endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress and pyroptosis via SREBP1-SCD/FADS2 pathway-mediated disruption of saturated/unsaturated fatty acids (SFAs/UFAs) homeostasis. Finally, we confirmed that stiripentol synergized with gemcitabine (GEM) to suppress PDAC growth both in vitro and in vivo.ConclusionsOur findings demonstrate that stiripentol disrupts SFAs/UFAs homeostasis, thereby inducing ER stress and pyroptosis in PDAC cells under serum deprivation condition. Notably, stiripentol has synergistic antitumour effects with GEM, highlighting its translational potential as a promising combination therapy for PDAC treatment.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s13402-026-01205-6.

Background

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is a highly lethal malignancy characterized by late diagnosis, rapid proliferation, and early metastasis [1]. Currently, gemcitabine (GEM)-based chemotherapy regimens remain the mainstay of PDAC treatment [2]. However, drug resistance frequently develops, leading to a poor prognosis [3]. Therefore, novel therapeutic strategies are urgently needed to improve treatment efficacy.

Reprogramming of energy metabolism is a hallmark of cancer [4]. In addition to glucose metabolism, tumor cells are strongly dependent on de novo fatty acid (FA) synthesis and desaturation due to the limited nutrient supply in the tumor microenvironment [5]. Aberrant FA synthesis has been reported to play critical roles in tumor progression [6]. FA can be classified into saturated FA (SFA), monounsaturated FA (MUFA), and polyunsaturated FA (PUFA) on the basis of the number of carbon‒carbon double bonds (C = C bonds) [7]. Tumor cells rely on the desaturases stearoyl-CoA desaturase (SCD) and fatty acid desaturase 2 (FADS2) to convert SFA into MUFA [8], while FADS2 also catalyzes PUFA synthesis [7]. The expression of SCD and FADS2 is transcriptionally regulated by sterol regulatory element-binding protein 1 (SREBP1), a master regulator of FA metabolism [9]. Previous studies have confirmed that an excess of SFA can trigger programmed cell death in cancer cells, including pyroptosis mediated by the gasdermin (GSDM) family [10], whereas MUFA and PUFA protect cancer cells from this process [11–13]. These findings highlight the therapeutic significance of targeting SREBP1 and its downstream desaturases in cancer. More importantly, studies have shown that the inhibition of SREBP1/SCD can chemosensitize cancer cells to GEM, including those in PDAC [14–18]. Nevertheless, despite their therapeutic potential, no pharmacological inhibitors targeting SREBP1, SCD, or FADS2 have been approved for clinical application in cancer therapy [19–21].

Stiripentol, an FDA-approved medication for Dravet syndrome, is known to potentiate GABAergic transmission, inhibit ion channels, and suppress the activity of several cytochrome P450 (CYP) enzymes. It also inhibits glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) [22, 23]. Given the critical role of LDH in cancer metabolism, the antitumour effects of stiripentol on glioblastoma (GBM) have been studied [24]. Moreover, it can sensitize tumor cells to inducers of cell death through diminished lactylation in gastric cancer, esophageal squamous cell carcinoma, and GBM [25–27]. However, its precise antitumour mechanism remains unclear, particularly in PDAC. Notably, whether stiripentol can modulate FA metabolism in cancer has not yet been investigated.

In this study, we demonstrate that stiripentol induces endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress and pyroptosis in PDAC cells under metabolic stress and exerts synergistic antitumour effects with GEM. Mechanistically, stiripentol disrupts SFAs/UFAs homeostasis downstream of the SREBP1-SCD/FADS2 pathway, thereby triggering ER stress and pyroptosis. Collectively, our findings reveal the therapeutic potential of stiripentol in PDAC.

Methods

Reagents and antibodies

Stiripentol (HY-103392) and GEM (HY-17026) were purchased from MedChemExpress (USA). Oleic acid (KC005X) was obtained from Kunchuang Biotechnology (China).

Anti-GSDMA (ab230768), anti-GSDME (ab215191), anti-FADS2 (ab314317) and anti-γ-H2AX (ab81299) antibodies were purchased from Abcam (UK). Anti-GSDMB (abs117921) was purchased from Absin (China). Anti-GSDMC (TA-315616) was purchased from OriGene (USA). Anti-GSDMD (WH0079792M1) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Germany). Anti-SREBP1 (14088–1-AP), anti-SCD (28678–1-AP), anti-RAD51 (14961–1-AP), anti-cleaved-caspase-3 (25546–1-AP) and anti-β-actin (66009–1-Ig) were purchased from Proteintech (China). Anti-IRE1α (A21021), anti-p-IRE1α-S724 (AP1442), anti-EIF2α (A21221), anti-p-EIF2α-S51 (AP0692), anti-p-EIF2α-S49 (AP0341), anti-AMPKα1 (A1229), anti-FASN(A19050) and anti-p-AMPKα1-S496 (AP1002) were purchased from ABclonal (China). Anti-rabbit IgG, HRP-linked (#7074) and anti-mouse IgG, HRP-linked Antibody (#7076) were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology (USA).

Cell lines

The human PDAC cell lines PANC-1 and BxPC3 were obtained from Wuhan Pricella Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The KPC cell line was kindly provided by Prof. Xue Jing’s laboratory (Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine). PANC-1 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Sigma-Aldrich) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco). BxPC3 and KPC cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 (Corning, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. All the cells were maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO₂.

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK8) assay

Cell viability was assessed via the CCK8 (Belongbio, ZH001) assay. Briefly, PANC-1, BxPC3 or KPC cells were seeded at a density of 10,000 cells per well in 96-well plates and incubated overnight. After being subjected to the indicated treatments for 24 or 48 h, cell viability was measured at 450 nm via a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Colony formation assay

For the colony formation assays, cells were seeded at a density of 1000 cells per well in 6-well plates. After 48 h of stiripentol treatment, the medium was replaced, and the cells were cultured until colonies became visible. Colonies were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Beyotime), stained with 0.5% crystal violet, and counted using ImageJ software.

5-ethynyl-2´-deoxyuridine (EdU) staining assay

PANC-1, BxPC3 or KPC cells at approximately 80% confluency were subjected to the indicated treatments for 18 h under full-serum (10% FBS) condition. Then, the EdU assay was performed using EdU working solution (Beyotime, C0071S) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Propidium iodide (PI) staining

The cells were seeded at a density of 10,000 cells per well in 96-well plates and subjected to the indicated treatments in the presence of PI (Servicebio). Images were acquired via a fluorescence microscope (ZEISS).

Western blotting (WB) assay

After the indicated treatments, dying cells in the culture medium were collected via centrifugation at 1000 × g for 5 min. Both the surviving adherent cells and the collected dying cells were lysed using RIPA buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 89900) supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche, 4693159001). WB assay was performed as previously described [28].

RNA-sequencing analysis

Briefly, PANC-1 cells were treated with 600 μM stiripentol for 18 h under low-serum (1% FBS) condition. Total RNA was isolated from treated and untreated cells using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA sequencing was performed by LC-Bio Technology Co., Ltd. (China). Differential gene expression analysis was conducted using the edgeR package (https://bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/edgeR.html). Genes with a fold change > 2 or <0.5 and p < 0.05 were considered differentially expressed genes (DEGs).

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT‒PCR)

PANC-1, BxPC3, and KPC cells were treated with stiripentol or DMSO for 18 h under low-serum (1% FBS) condition, followed by RNA extraction via TRIzol (Invitrogen, USA). qRT‒PCR was performed as previously described [28]. The primer sequences are listed in Supplementary data1.

Lipid-targeted metabolomics analysis

PANC-1 cells at approximately 80% confluency were treated with 600 μM stiripentol under low-serum (1% FBS) condition for 18 h. The cells were then harvested for lipid-targeted metabolomic profiling via liquid chromatography‒tandem mass spectrometry (LC‒MS/MS; Biotree Biotech Co., China). Principal component analysis (PCA) (Fig. S1A) was used to visualize the sample distribution. Supervised orthogonal projections to latent structures discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) (Fig. S1B–D) was performed to identify significantly altered metabolites. The variable importance in projection (VIP) score of the first principal component in OPLS-DA was also obtained. Significantly altered metabolites were defined as those with p < 0.05 (Student’s t test) and VIP > 1.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

PANC-1, BxPC3, and KPC cells were treated with DMSO or 600 μM stiripentol (12 h, 1% FBS) and then fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde at 4 °C overnight. After standard dehydration and epoxy resin embedding, the sections were double-stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. Images were acquired via a TECNAI G20 TWIN transmission electron microscope (FEI).

Oil red O (ORO) staining

For ORO staining, frozen sections were equilibrated at room temperature and then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Beyotime) for 15 min, followed by washing with PBS. The sections were then stained with ORO working solution (Record Biological Technology, RCF016) for 10 min and washed with 60% isopropanol and PBS.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Beyotime) and embedded in paraffin. The sections were deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated, and subjected to heat-induced epitope retrieval. After being blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin, the sections were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. The antigen‒antibody complexes were visualized via a diaminobenzidine chromogenic substrate, which produced a brown precipitate at antigen localization sites.

In vivo therapeutic evaluation

All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with protocols approved by the Ethical Committee of Shanghai East Hospital, School of Medicine, Tongji University, China (No. 2022 − 127). Male C57BL/6JGpt mice (5 weeks old; Gem Pharmatech, China) were subcutaneously inoculated with 5 × 105 KPC cells in the right flank. Tumor volume (calculated as length × width2/2) and body weight were monitored every other day via digital callipers. When the tumors reached approximately 200 mm3, the mice were randomly divided into the designated treatment groups.

To evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of stiripentol in vivo, the mice were randomly divided into two groups (n = 5 per group) and treated with vehicle or stiripentol (40 mg/kg, i.p., three times weekly) for two weeks. The dosage selection of stiripentol here is based on the results of preliminary experiments to reduce the side effects of weight loss.

To evaluate drug synergy, tumor-bearing mice (n = 5 per group) were randomly assigned to four treatment groups: (1) vehicle, (2) stiripentol monotherapy (40 mg/kg, i.p., three times weekly), (3) GEM monotherapy (25 mg/kg, i.p., twice weekly), and (4) combination therapy (40 mg/kg stiripentol + 25 mg/kg GEM, maintaining respective dosing schedules). All the treatments were administered for two consecutive weeks.

Analysis of hepatic or renal function

Serum levels of alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), UREA, and creatinine (CREA) were measured via an automated biochemistry analyser (Hitachi, Japan).

Drug synergistic effect analysis

The q value of the drug combination was calculated as q = E(A+B)/(EA + EB - EA × EB) [29, 30], where EA and EB represent the inhibition rates of the individual drugs (A and B, respectively), and E(A+B) denotes the combined inhibition rate. q values < 0.85 indicate antagonism, 0.85 ≤ q < 1.15 indicate additive effects, and q values ≥ 1.15 indicate synergistic activity.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis and half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC₅₀) calculations were performed via GraphPad Prism 10.0. Each experiment was performed in triplicate, and all the data are presented as the means ± SEMs. Statistical differences were assessed via Student’s t test or ANOVA. Statistical significance was defined as follows: *, p < 0.05; **, p < 0.01; ***, p < 0.001; ****, p < 0.0001; ns, not significant.

Results

Stiripentol inhibits the proliferation of PDAC cells

To assess the effect of stiripentol on PDAC cells, three PDAC cell lines, PANC-1, BxPC3 and KPC, were subjected to CCK8 assays. The results revealed that stiripentol inhibited the viability of PDAC cells in a dose-dependent manner after 24 h of treatment. The IC50 values of stiripentol for PANC-1, BxPC3 and KPC cells were 632.2, 655.7, and 611.4 μM, respectively (Fig. 1A). Colony formation and EdU assays further confirmed that stiripentol suppressed the proliferation of PDAC cells (Fig. 1B–C).

Stiripentol induces pyroptosis in PDAC cells under low-serum condition

Several interventions have been reported to induce pyroptosis in cancer cells under low-serum condition [31, 32]. On the basis of these findings, we investigated whether stiripentol could similarly trigger pyroptotic cell death in PDAC cells under serum deprivation condition. CCK8 assays were performed on these three PDAC cell lines treated with stiripentol for 24 h under low-serum culture (1% FBS). Notably, compared with full-serum condition, serum deprivation significantly increased the inhibitory efficacy of stiripentol, reducing the IC50 value by approximately 50% (Fig. 1A). PI staining was subsequently performed to assess pyroptosis induction. Consistent with our hypothesis, nearly all PDAC cells exhibited PI staining and characteristic bubble-like pyroptotic morphology after 24 h of stiripentol treatment under low-serum condition [32]. In contrast, minimal cell death was observed under full-serum condition (Fig. 2A).

Pyroptosis is mediated by GSDM family proteins, including GSDMA, GSDMB, GSDMC, GSDMD, and GSDME. Proteolytic cleavage of GSDM proteins results in the release of their N-terminal pore-forming domain (GSDM-N), which results in pyroptotic cell death [10]. To identify the specific GSDM proteins involved in stiripentol-induced pyroptosis in PDAC, we performed WB analysis after 24 h of stiripentol treatment. Among the five GSDM family proteins, only GSDME was cleaved (Fig. 2B). Importantly, consistent with the observed morphological changes, the active GSDME-N fragment was clearly detected under serum-deprived condition but remained undetectable under full-serum condition (Fig. 2C). These findings demonstrated that stiripentol specifically activated GSDME-dependent pyroptosis in PDAC cells under serum deprivation condition.

Stiripentol inhibits the expression of FA synthesis-related genes

Next, RNA sequencing was performed to investigate the mechanism underlying stiripentol-induced pyroptosis in PDAC cells under low-serum condition. A total of 1602 DEGs were identified between the stiripentol-treated and DMSO-treated groups, among which 647 genes were upregulated and 955 genes were downregulated (Fig. 3A). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) suggested that the lipid biosynthetic process might be regulated by stiripentol (Fig. 3B). We further intersected the DEGs with known lipid metabolism-related genes (LMRGs) [33]. A total of 104 differentially expressed LMRGs were identified (Fig. 3C), and we focused on the significantly downregulated LMRGs. Among the top ten downregulated LMRGs, SCD, FADS2, and fatty acid synthase (FASN) were involved in FA synthesis (Fig. 3D). Concurrently, SREBP1, a known upstream regulator of these genes [6], was also downregulated (Supplementary data 2). Previous studies revealed that FA plays important roles in pyroptosis [11, 34]. Therefore, we validated the expression of these genes via qRT‒PCR. Consistent with the RNA sequencing results, stiripentol treatment inhibited the expression of SCD, FADS2, FASN, and SREBP1 in PDAC cells (Fig. 3E–G). WB analysis further demonstrated that stiripentol inhibited the expression of these genes regardless of the serum condition (Fig. 3H–J). Moreover, consistent with previous studies showing that activation of the AMPKα pathway can protect cells against pyroptosis [35] or SCD/FASN inhibition [36], we observed a compensatory increase in p-AMPKα1 levels in PDAC cells, particularly under low-serum condition (Fig. S2A-C).

Collectively, these results indicated that stiripentol might play a regulatory role in FA metabolism by inhibiting the SREBP1-SCD/FADS2 pathway.

Stiripentol disrupts SFAs/UFAs homeostasis in PDAC cells

To further investigate the role of stiripentol in FA metabolism, we performed lipid-targeted metabolomics analysis on PANC-1 cells treated with DMSO or stiripentol under serum-deprived condition. A total of 2495 metabolites were quantified, among which 329 were significantly upregulated, whereas 408 were downregulated in stiripentol-treated PANC-1 cells (Fig. 4A). Consistent with the GSEA results based on RNA sequencing (Fig. 4B), lipid enrichment analysis indicated that the biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids in PDAC cells was affected by stiripentol (Fig. 4C). Further analysis of the 33 quantified FAs (Fig. 4D) revealed that the total level of free FA (FFA) was significantly reduced in PANC-1 cells following stiripentol treatment (Fig. 4E). While the levels of two major SFAs—FA (16:0) and FA (18:0)—remained unchanged, another SFA, FA (24:0), which has been reported to induce necrosis mode of cell death in 158N murine oligodendrocytes [37, 38], was upregulated in stiripentol-treated cells. In contrast, four UFAs—FA (18:1), FA (20:3), FA (20:4) and FA (26:5)—were significantly downregulated upon stiripentol treatment (Fig. 4F). Notably, FA (18:1) is the catalytic product of SCD, whereas FA (20:3) and FA (20:4) are the catalytic products of FADS2 [39]. These results suggested that stiripentol could suppress the expression of FA synthesis-related genes, particularly SCD and FADS2, thereby disrupting SFAs/UFAs homeostasis in PDAC cells.

Oleic acid prevents stiripentol-induced ER stress and pyroptosis in PDAC cells

Previous studies have suggested that an imbalance in SFAs/UFAs metabolism can trigger ER stress and cell death [11–13]. Consistent with these findings, GSEA revealed that stiripentol regulated the unfolded protein response pathway (Fig. 5A). WB assays revealed that after stiripentol treatment, the ER stress markers p-IRE1α and p-EIF2α were upregulated in PDAC cells (Fig. 5B–D). In agreement with these findings, TEM imaging revealed pronounced ER dilation following stiripentol treatment (Fig. 5E–G), further confirming ER dysfunction. To determine whether disruption of SFAs/UFAs homeostasis contributes to stiripentol-induced ER stress and pyroptosis, we supplemented cells with oleic acid—FA (18:1), the predominant cellular MUFA. Notably, exogenous oleic acid administration attenuated stiripentol-induced ER stress and pyroptosis in a dose-dependent manner under low-serum condition (Fig. 5H–J). These findings indicated that stiripentol-induced disruption of SFAs/UFAs homeostasis played a pivotal role in initiating ER stress and pyroptosis in PDAC cells.

Stiripentol inhibits the growth of PDAC cells in vivo

To further evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of stiripentol in vivo, a PDAC mice model was established via the subcutaneous injection of KPC cells. When the tumor volume reached approximately 200 mm3, the mice were randomly assigned to two groups and treated intraperitoneally with either stiripentol (40 mg/kg) or the vehicle control. Compared with that in the vehicle group, tumor growth was significantly suppressed in the stiripentol-treated group (Fig. 6A–B). Histological analysis revealed markedly reduced lipid accumulation (ORO staining) and decreased expression of the proliferation marker Ki-67 in stiripentol-treated tumors, accompanied by downregulation of the FA synthesis regulators SCD and SREBP1 (Fig. 6C). Conversely, tumors from stiripentol-treated mice presented elevated levels of cleaved caspase-3 and the ER stress marker p-EIF2α (Fig. 6C), which was consistent with our in vitro observations. Overall, the above results demonstrated that stiripentol inhibited the growth of PDAC cells in vivo.

Stiripentol synergizes with GEM to inhibit PDAC growth

Previous studies have shown that the inhibition of SCD can sensitize GBM to temozolomide by downregulating RAD51 expression [36, 40]. Given that reduced SREBP1, SCD or RAD51 levels are known to increase the sensitivity of tumor cells to GEM [14–18, 41–43], we investigated whether stiripentol could similarly downregulate RAD51 in PDAC cells and thereby enhance GEM efficacy. First, PDAC cells were treated with stiripentol or DMSO under full- or low-serum condition. WB analysis revealed that stiripentol significantly reduced RAD51 expression regardless of the serum level (Fig. 7A–B). Notably, stiripentol selectively increased the DNA damage marker γ-H2AX under low-serum condition (Fig. 7A–B), suggesting that serum deprivation may potentiate stiripentol-induced DNA damage. Next, we treated PDAC cells with stiripentol, GEM, or their combination. WB analysis demonstrated that both stiripentol alone and its combination with GEM dose-dependently suppressed RAD51 expression (Fig. 7C–D), confirming its regulatory role in RAD51 downregulation. Furthermore, although low-dose stiripentol alone did not significantly alter γ-H2AX levels, the combination treatment markedly enhanced γ-H2AX expression in a dose-dependent manner compared with GEM monotherapy (Fig. 7C–D), indicating that stiripentol could potentiate GEM-induced DNA damage. To further assess the synergistic effect, CCK-8 assays were performed. The results revealed that, compared with either stiripentol or GEM alone, the combination treatment suppressed PDAC cell viability more significantly (Fig. 7E–F). We also calculated the q value of the combined drug (where q ≥ 1.15 indicates synergy) as previously described [29, 30]. The analysis revealed a synergistic interaction between stiripentol and GEM, particularly at doses of 300 μM stiripentol+10 μM GEM in PANC-1 cells (Fig. 7G) and 300 μM stiripentol+5 μM GEM in BxPC3 cells (Fig. 7H).

Finally, we established a subcutaneous KPC tumor model to evaluate in vivo efficacy. The results demonstrated that the combination treatment suppressed tumor growth more effectively than stiripentol or GEM monotherapy (Fig. 7I–K). Consistent with reported appetite suppression associated with stiripentol [44], both the combo and stiripentol groups exhibited mild weight loss (Fig. 7L). However, no significant differences in ALT, AST, CREA, or UREA (Fig. 7M–P) levels were observed between the vehicle and combo groups, indicating that there were no adverse effects on hepatic or renal function. Collectively, these results demonstrated that stiripentol synergized with GEM to suppress PDAC growth without significant adverse effects on liver or kidney function.

Discussion

PDAC is a deadly malignancy, with a 5-year relative survival rate of 12% [45]. Currently, chemotherapy regimens based on GEM remain the mainstay of treatment for PDAC [2]. However, chemotherapy resistance hinders therapeutic efficacy [3], highlighting the importance of identifying novel combined therapies for treating PDAC. In this study, we revealed that stiripentol induces pyroptosis and ER stress in PDAC cells under low-serum condition via disruption of cellular SFAs/UFAs homeostasis (Fig. 8). More importantly, stiripentol synergizes with GEM to suppress PDAC growth.

Stiripentol, an FDA-approved drug for Dravet syndrome, has been reported to be an LDH inhibitor [23]. Given the central role of LDH in cancer metabolism, its antitumour effects have been investigated in GBM [24]. However, the specific antitumour mechanisms of stiripentol remain incompletely understood, and its role in FA metabolism has not been previously described. Here, we show that stiripentol perturbs the cellular SFAs/UFAs balance in PDAC cells, triggering ER stress and pyroptosis. Furthermore, whether stiripentol can enhance the therapeutic efficacy of GEM remains unknown. Here, we revealed that stiripentol enhances GEM efficacy, likely via downregulation of RAD51, which is downstream of the SREBP1/SCD pathway and a DNA repair protein previously linked to GEM sensitivity [41–43]. It should be noted that as an LDH inhibitor, alterations in LDH activity may also contribute to its antitumour effects. Furthermore, its LDH inhibitory effect reduces lactate production, which may increase tumor cell sensitivity to cell death inducers (e.g., cisplatin [25], temozolomide [26], and cuproptosis inducers [27]) through diminished lactylation. Given the complexity of cellular metabolic networks, there also exists the possibility that the FA metabolism modulated by stiripentol may result from the inhibition of G6PD or LDH activity. However, our results demonstrate that its antitumour effects and synergistic efficacy with GEM are mediated, at least in part, through the downregulation of FA synthesis-related genes and, consequently, imbalanced SFAs/UFAs homeostasis. To date, the regulatory effect of G6PD on SREBP1 expression has not been reported. The catalytic product of LDH is lactate, and previous research has found that exogenous lactate intake can upregulate the expression of SREBP1-SCD by inhibiting p-AMPKα, leading to ferroptosis resistance in tumor cells. This result suggests that AMPKα activation is a positive regulator of ferroptosis [46]. However, previous studies have also indicated that AMPKα activation can render cells resistant to ferroptosis [47], necroptosis [48], pyroptosis [35], and SCD inhibitors [36]. Therefore, AMPKα activation does not appear to be the upstream mechanism underlying stiripentol-induced SREBP1 downregulation, which requires further validation. Further studies are warranted to elucidate the precise mechanisms by which stiripentol suppresses the SREBP1-SCD/FADS2 axis.

Malignant cells are highly dependent on de novo FA biosynthesis to sustain rapid proliferation in nutrient-deprived tumor microenvironments [6]. This process requires the activity of lipogenic enzymes, including SCD and FADS2, which are transcriptionally regulated by SREBP1 [9]. Our results revealed that stiripentol can inhibit the expression of these genes, suggesting its role in regulating de novo FA biosynthesis. Consistent with previous reports that tumor cells become more sensitive to FA biosynthesis inhibitors under lipid-deprived condition [49], stiripentol preferentially induces pyroptosis in PDAC cells cultured under low-serum condition. This differential sensitivity likely reflects the dependence of cancer cells on de novo FA biosynthesis when exogenous lipid uptake is limited by serum restriction. This observation also indicates the regulatory role of stiripentol in FA metabolism. Our lipidomic data supports this hypothesis. Consistent with prior reports that UFA synthesis is more sensitive to SREBP1 inhibition than SFA synthesis [50], we observed no significant changes in the levels of two major SFAs—FA (16:0) and FA (18:0)—after stiripentol treatment. Unexpectedly, another type of SFA—FA (24:0)—is upregulated by stiripentol, which might play a role in stiripentol-induced lipotoxicity. We also observed the downregulation of UFAs after stiripentol treatment. Among them, FA (18:1) is the product of SCD, whereas FA (20:3) and FA (20:4) are the products of FADS2. Collectively, these observed phenotypic changes are consistent with stiripentol-mediated suppression of FA synthesis genes.

The balance between SFAs and UFAs is a critical determinant of ER homeostasis and cell death regulation [9]. The inhibition of SREBP1, SCD, or FADS2 has been shown to disrupt this balance, thereby inducing ER stress and promoting apoptosis or ferroptosis [12, 51–54]. However, the role of these lipogenic regulators in pyroptosis in cancer remains unexplored. Notably, palmitic acid—FA (16:0)—has been shown to trigger ER stress and pyroptosis in HepG2 hepatoma cells, an effect that is counteracted by oleic acid—FA (18:1) [11], suggesting that SFAs/UFAs imbalance is a potential pyroptosis inducer in cancer. In our study, among the four UFAs downregulated by stiripentol, in addition to FA (18:1), FA (20:4) has also been reported to protect cancer cells against SCD inhibition-induced ER stress and cell death in tumors [12]. Moreover, we found that exogenous oleic acid—FA (18:1)—supplementation successfully inhibited stiripentol-induced ER stress and pyroptosis. Consistent with a recent study showing that palmitoylation of the C-terminal of GSDME is essential for chemotherapy drug-induced pyroptosis [55], stiripentol did not induce significant changes in palmitic acid—FA (16:0), a substrate for palmitoylation. Therefore, the above changes might provide a foundation for pyroptosis induction. We also observed that the SFA—FA (24:0)—ratio was increased by stiripentol. FA (24:0) has been reported to trigger necrosis mode of cell death in 158N murine oligodendrocytes [37, 38]. Thus, stiripentol may induce pyroptosis in PDAC cells by upregulating this specific SFA, a hypothesis that warrants further validation.

Pyroptosis is a form of programmed cell death mediated by the gasdermin protein family [10]. Previous studies have found that in cells expressing GSDME, caspase-3 can cleave GSDME, thereby converting chemotherapy drug- and TNF-induced apoptosis into pyroptosis [56]. Therefore, the occurrence of pyroptosis induced by alterations in SFAs and UFAs homeostasis may find an explanation in previous apoptosis studies. For instance, disruption of SFAs and UFAs homeostasis can induce apoptosis by triggering endoplasmic reticulum stress [53]. Previous studies have also found that the occurrence of ER stress can induce pyroptosis [57–59]. Although our study has shown that stiripentol can induce ER stress in pancreatic cancer, whether this serves as an upstream mechanism for pyroptosis remains to be clarified. Given that previous studies have found that disruption of saturated and unsaturated fatty acid homeostasis can also induce apoptosis and ferroptosis [51, 53], we need to further clarify whether apoptosis and ferroptosis also play a role in the anti-tumor effects of stiripentol.

Recent study has highlighted the essential role of SREBP-driven lipid metabolism in PDAC progression [60]. However, no clinically approved inhibitors targeting SREBP1, SCD, or FADS2 are currently available [19–21]. Our findings position stiripentol as a promising therapeutic candidate that targets this pathway. However, considering the appetite-suppressive effects of stiripentol in clinical use [44], future therapeutic development should aim to optimize dosing regimens and minimize side effects, such as weight loss.

Conclusions

This study identified stiripentol as a pyroptosis inducer in PDAC under metabolic stress. Mechanistically, stiripentol disrupts SFAs/UFAs homeostasis by inhibiting the SREBP1-SCD/FADS2 pathway, triggering ER stress and pyroptosis. Furthermore, we revealed that stiripentol synergizes with GEM to suppress PDAC growth. Collectively, these findings highlight the therapeutic potential of stiripentol, particularly in combination regimens with GEM, for PDAC treatment.
