Abstract
Federated learning has been considered a promising privacy preserving distributed edge learning framework. Over-the-air computation technique leveraging analog transmission enables the aggregation of local updates directly over-the-air by exploiting the superposition properties of wireless multiple-access channel, thereby drastically reducing the communication bottleneck issues of federated learning compared with digital transmission schemes. This work points out that existing over-the-air computation-federated learning overlooks a key practical constraint, the instantaneous peak-power constraints imposed by the non-linearity of radio-frequency power amplifiers. We present and analyze the effect of the classic method to deal with this issue, amplitude clipping combined with filtering. We investigate the effect of instantaneous peak-power constraints in over-the-air computation-federated learning for both single-carrier and multi-carrier orthogonal frequency-division multiplexing systems. We highlight the specificity of over-the-air computation-federated learning: the samples depend on the gradient value distribution, leading to a higher peak-to-average power ratio than that observed for uniformly distributed signals. Simulation results demonstrate that, in practical settings, the instantaneous transmit power regularly exceeds the power-amplifier limit; however, by applying clipping and filtering, the FL performance can be degraded. The degradation becomes pronounced especially in multi-carrier OFDM systems due to the in-band distortions caused by clipping and filtering.



I
Introduction

An unprecedented volume of data is now generated at the wireless edge. Leveraging these data to train machine learning models at a centralized server is challenged by privacy concerns and limited uplink bandwidth requirements. Federated learning addresses both challenges by keeping raw data on devices and only exchanging model updates
[
1
]
. Federated learning consists of a collaborative learning framework, in which repeatedly, each edge device trains on its own data for certain iterations and then uploads the model or the gradients to a central server for aggregation.
However, the digital transmission of large-scale model updates still creates a communication bottleneck, especially as the number of participants scales
[
2
]
. As more and more end devices are wirelessly connected, considering federated learning within wireless networks is essential
[
3
]
.

Over-the-air computation technique proposed in
[
4
,
5
]
is investigated as a means to counter the communication bottleneck issue of federated learning
[
6
,
7
,
8
]
. By exploiting the waveform superposition of the wireless multiple-access channel, a server can directly aggregate local updates “over the air,” enabling scalability with the number of devices to participate in each communication round. One of the central design challenges in over-the-air computation-federated learning is power control for signal power or amplitude alignment. The ideal aim is to obtain equally average received power from user equipments at the base station so that a summation operation can be performed.
Under average power constraints, channel inversion and threshold-based policies have been shown to minimize aggregation mean-squared error: user equipments with weak channels transmit at their maximum power budget, while user equipments above a threshold invert their channels
[
9
,
10
,
11
]
.

However, the above designs ignore the instantaneous peak-power limitations due to the non-linearity of power amplifiers from a certain power level. Operating in the nonlinear region causes signal distortion and receiver misalignment. This is a common issue for orthogonal frequency-division multiplexing, whose peak-to-average power ratio is known to be high. While recent works have advanced over-the-air computation in orthogonal frequency-division multiplexing systems
[
12
,
13
,
14
]
, they typically optimize mean-squared error under average-power budgets and imperfect channel state information but do not quantify or enforce instantaneous peak-power constraints.

This paper studies over-the-air computation-federated learning under both average and instantaneous peak-power constraints for single-carrier transmission and multi-carrier orthogonal frequency-division multiplexing systems. We characterize the induced aggregation error when peaks are constrained, and evaluate the effect of practical mitigation mechanisms.

The main contributions are summarized as follows:

•

We highlight the practical limitations of peak power constraint in a practical system due to the non-linear region of power amplifiers.

•

We present and evaluate the most common method to handle the peak power constraints: amplitude clipping that repeatedly applies clipping, i.e., clip the time-domain peak power value that is set by the nonlinearity of the power amplifier, and filtering the out-of-band distortion. Implementation details for both multiple-access schemes are provided.

•

Empirical evaluation and design insights. Evaluating under LeNet trained on CIFAR-10, we quantify peak-to-average power ratio, aggregation mean-squared error, and learning accuracy. We observe substantially high peak-to-average power ratio of a over-the-air computation-federated learning signal, either in single-carrier or multi-carrier OFDM. Enforcing peak constraints via clipping yields accuracy degradation and may cause model divergence.

The remaining sections are organized as follows: Section II introduces the FL-OTA system models for single-carrier and multi-carrier OFDM and the associated average power allocation based on MSE minimization. Section III formalizes the instantaneous peak-power constraint and presents the clipping mechanisms. Section IV presents the simulation settings and results.

\displaystyle=\EX[\|\tilde{\bm{{g}}}-\overline{\bm{{g}}}\|_{2}^{2}]

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma^{2}N}{K^{2}}\Big(\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}(h_{k}\sqrt{\alpha p_{k}}-1)^{2}+\alpha\sigma^{2}\Big).

\displaystyle\min_{\alpha>0,\ \{p_{k}\}_{k\in\mathcal{K}}}

Instantaneous peak power constraint

Typical input and output power characteristics curve for a power amplifier.

Practical radio-frequency power amplifiers operate linearly only up to certain input power
[
19
,
20
]
. Given a certain average power as input
P_{\mathrm{avg}}^{(in)}
, the actual operating region would be between zero and the maximum peak instantaneous input power which can be obtained by multiplying
P_{\mathrm{avg}}^{(in)}
with peak-to-average power ratio (addition in dB domain), as illustrated in Fig.

Operating in nonlinear region leads to signal distortions, resulting in strong in-band and out-of-band distortions (details see later), which is to be avoided.
Note that all previously determined powers are actually output powers, but can be easily converted to the input power by a one-to-one relationship. We denote
p_{k}^{(in)}
and
b_{k\ell M+m}^{(in)}
respectively the corresponding input power of

and

.
We model this by an instantaneous amplitude limit
A_{\max}>0
that corresponds equivalently to the input peak-power limit
P_{\mathrm{inst},\max}^{(in)}=A_{\max}^{2}
. The transmitted time-domain waveform
X(t)
generated at a user equipment must satisfy

|X(t)|^{2}\leq P_{\mathrm{inst},\max}^{(in)}=A_{\max}^{2}.\vskip-2.84544pt

(19)

III-A

Peak-to-Average Power Ratio

This limitation directly interacts with the peak-to-average power ratio of the transmitted waveform as illustrated in Fig.
1
. A low PAPR can keep the operating region within the linear region.

Single-carrier transmission

With the gradient normalization in Section II, the average per-communication round transmit power equals the configured average power budget. The peaks for single carrier transmission are directly determined by the gradient values. The
k
-th user equipment transmits the real-valued sample
g_{k}[n]
matching the amplitude of waveform. Its PAPR is

\mathrm{PAPR}_{k}=\frac{\max_{n}|g_{k}[n]|^{2}}{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}|g_{k}[n]|^{2}}.\vskip-2.84544pt

(20)

Multi-carrier OFDM transmission

The time-domain signal for multi-carrier OFDM can be written as

s_{k,\ell}(t)=\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}g_{k}[\ell M+m]b_{k,\ell M+m}e^{j\frac{2\pi mt}{M}}.\vskip-4.26773pt

(21)

The ideal PAPR is denoted as

\mathrm{PAPR}_{k,\ell}^{\mathrm{ideal}}=\frac{\max_{t}|s_{k,\ell}(t)|^{2}}{\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}|s_{k,\ell}(t)|^{2}dt}.

(22)

In practice, only digital discrete samples can be employed to estimate PAPR. It has been shown that samples sampled at Nyquist rate inaccurately captures PAPR.

Oversampling
:
To accurately capture PAPR in the time-domain OFDM signal, oversampling is essential. An oversampling factor
L_{\mathrm{os}}
(typically
L_{\mathrm{os}}=4
is sufficient
[
21
]
) is applied by zero-padding the frequency-domain signal before the IDFT. The oversampled time-domain signal is generated as:

s_{k,\ell}^{(\mathrm{os})}[i]=\!\!\!\sum_{m=0}^{L_{\mathrm{os}}M-1}\!\!G_{k,\ell}[m]e^{j\frac{2\pi mi}{L_{\mathrm{os}}M}},\quad i\!=\!0,\ldots,L_{\mathrm{os}}M\!-\!1,\vskip-2.84544pt

(23)

where the zero-padded frequency-domain vector is defined as:

\hskip-7.11317ptG_{k,\ell}[m]\!=\!\begin{cases}g_{k}[\ell M\!+\!m]b_{k,\ell M+m}^{(in)},&0\!\leq\!m\!<\!M,\\
0,&M\!\leq\!m\!<L_{\mathrm{os}}M.\end{cases}\vskip-2.84544pt

(24)

The per-symbol PAPR can therefore be estimated as

\mathrm{PAPR}_{k,\ell}=\frac{\max_{i}|s_{k,\ell}^{(\mathrm{os})}[i]|^{2}}{\frac{1}{L_{\mathrm{os}}M}\sum_{i=0}^{L_{\mathrm{os}}M-1}|s_{k,\ell}^{(\mathrm{os})}[i]|^{2}}.\vskip-4.26773pt

(25)

III-B

Clipping with Oversampling

The commonly used technique to deal with the high peak-to-average power ratio issue is applying the procedure of iterative clipping-and-filtering. Since clipping results in in-band and out-of-band distortions in multi-carrier OFDM
[
19
,
20
]
.
A low-pass filter is applied afterwards to limit out-of-band distortion,
which may result in the rise of PAPR, necessitating repeated such procedures.

Amplitude Clipping

Let
\mathcal{C}_{A_{\max}}(x)\triangleq\min\{1,A_{\max}/|x|\}\,x
denote the time-sample-wise amplitude clipper.

III-B
1
Single-carrier

Each UE transmits

x_{k}[n]\!=\!\mathcal{C}_{A_{\max}}\!\bigl(\!\sqrt{\!p_{k}^{\!(in)}}\!\!g_{k}[n]\!\bigr)\!=\!\min\Bigl\{1,\!\frac{A_{\max}}{\sqrt{\!p_{k}^{\!(in)}}\,\!\!\!g_{k}[n]}\!\Bigr\}\!\sqrt{p_{k}^{\!(in)}}\,\!\!\!g_{k}[n].

(26)

In single-carrier, this is equivalent to considering the transmission of a clipped version of the gradient values, with nothing else altered by the clipping.

III-B
2
Multi-Carrier OFDM

Amplitude Clipping

For each OFDM symbol
\ell
, the oversampled complex time-domain samples are clipped while preserving phase:

x_{k,\ell}^{(\mathrm{os})}[i]=\mathcal{C}_{A_{\max}}\bigl(s_{k,\ell}^{(\mathrm{os})}[i]\bigr)=\min\Bigl\{1,\frac{A_{\max}}{|s_{k,\ell}^{(\mathrm{os})}[i]|}\Bigr\}s_{k,\ell}^{(\mathrm{os})}[i].

(27)

Since the clipping operation is nonlinear, distortions are inevitable.
The clipping distortion in the time domain can be written as:
d_{k,\ell}^{(\mathrm{os})}[i]=x_{k,\ell}^{(\mathrm{os})}[i]-s_{k,\ell}^{(\mathrm{os})}[i].

Frequency-Domain Distortion

Transforming the clipped signal back to the frequency domain via DFT yields:

X_{k,\ell}[m]=\mathrm{DFT}\bigl\{x_{k,\ell}^{(\mathrm{os})}[i]\bigr\}[m]=G_{k,\ell}[m]+D_{k,\ell}[m],

(28)

where
D_{k,\ell}[m]=\mathrm{DFT}\bigl\{d_{k,\ell}^{(\mathrm{os})}[i]\bigr\}[m]
represents the clipping distortion in the frequency domain.

This distortion consists of two components:

•

In-band distortion
: distortion on the considered subcarriers
m\in\mathcal{M}
, which degrades the aggregated gradients.

•

Out-of-band emissions
: spectral regrowth on the zero-padded subcarriers
M\leq m<L_{\mathrm{os}}M
due to clipping, which causes interference to adjacent channels.

Filtering

To suppress out-of-band emissions, a low-pass filteris applied. Define a rectangular filter:

H_{\mathrm{filter}}[m]=\begin{cases}1,&0\leq m<M,\\
0,&M\leq m<L_{\mathrm{os}}M.\end{cases}\vskip-2.84544pt

(29)

The filtered frequency-domain signal is:

X_{k,\ell}^{(\mathrm{filt})}[m]=X_{k,\ell}[m]\cdot H_{\mathrm{filter}}[m].\vskip-2.84544pt

(30)

Converting back to the time domain yields the filtered signal:

x_{k,\ell}^{(\mathrm{filt})}[i]=\mathrm{IDFT}\bigl\{X_{k,\ell}^{(\mathrm{filt})}[m]\bigr\}[i].\vskip-4.26773pt

(31)

However, filtering may reintroduce peak regrowth, as it restores some of the constructive interference among subcarriers that clipping had disrupted
[
19
]
.

Iterative Clipping and Filtering

To jointly control PAPR and out-of-band emissions, an iterative clipping-and-filtering procedure is commonly employed
[
22
]
. The algorithm proceeds as follows for each symbol
\ell
:

1.

Initialize:
x_{k,\ell}^{(0)}[i]=s_{k,\ell}^{(\mathrm{os})}[i]
;
j=1
.

2.

While
x_{k,\ell}^{(j,\mathrm{clip})}[i]
has out-of-band distortion higher than a threshold:

(a)

Clip:
x_{k,\ell}^{(j,\mathrm{clip})}[i]=\mathcal{C}_{A_{\max}}\bigl(x_{k,\ell}^{(j-1)}[i]\bigr)
.

(b)

DFT:
X_{k,\ell}^{(j)}[m]=\mathrm{DFT}\bigl\{x_{k,\ell}^{(j,\mathrm{clip})}[i]\bigr\}[m]
.

(c)

Filter:
X_{k,\ell}^{(j,\mathrm{filt})}[m]=X_{k,\ell}^{(j)}[m]\cdot H_{\mathrm{filter}}[m]
.

(d)

IDFT:
x_{k,\ell}^{(j)}[i]=\mathrm{IDFT}\bigl\{X_{k,\ell}^{(j,\mathrm{filt})}[m]\bigr\}[i]
.

3.

Output:
x_{k,\ell}^{(\mathrm{ICF})}[i]=x_{k,\ell}^{(J_{\max})}[i]
.

The final transmitted signal is
x_{k,\ell}^{(\mathrm{ICF})}[i]
, which satisfies the peak-power constraint with a certain threshold for out-of-band distortion. The flow diagram of the whole procedure can be found in Fig.
2
.

V
Conclusion

This work highlights an overlooked practical issue in over-the-air computation-federated learning: the instantaneous peak power constraint imposed by the nonlinearity in power amplifiers. This is an issue in over-the-air computation-federated learning since it uses analog transmission and some works consider multi-carrier OFDM transmissions. We applied the classic PAPR reduction methods of iterative amplitude clipping and frequency-domain filtering. Enforcing peak power constraint can indeed degrade aggregation and final accuracy, in some cases, the effects are marginal, and in some other cases (multi-carrier with low noise level), cause divergence. Counterintuitively, the impact is stronger at lower noise for multi-carrier OFDM because the scaling factor from optimization is higher and therefore the in-band distortion is too much amplified. This work should motivate further research in peak power constraint-aware power control and better PAPR reduction techniques for single-carrier and multi-carrier over-the-air computation-federated learning systems.
