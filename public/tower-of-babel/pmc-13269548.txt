A microbiome catalog of Chinese traditional artisanal cheeses provides insights into functional and microbial diversity
Authors: Yang Liu, Pan Huang, Chuan Zhang, Quanbin Dong, Xiuchao Wang, Fengwei Tian, Jianxin Zhao, Zhihong Sun, Lianmin Chen, Wei Chen, Qixiao Zhai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCheese has been consumed globally over millennia and serves as a natural reservoir of diverse microorganisms. Chinese traditional cheeses rely on natural fermentation and have unique physiochemical and microbial characteristics compared to European cheeses. However, there is a major knowledge gap in the understanding of Chinese cheese microbiome. Here, we present a curated Cheese microbiome catalog (cCMC) consisting of 3327 high-quality metagenome-assembled genomes, recovered from metagenomic sequencing of 235 Chinese cheese samples covering all traditional artisanal cheese-producing regions in China, together with 198 publicly available non-Chinese cheese metagenomic datasets. This catalog represents 395 nonredundant species spanning 50 families, including 85 putative novel species. We identified six lactic acid bacteria species enriched in Chinese cheeses, and confirmed that the unique presence of Acetobacteraceae contributes to improving the nutritional quality of Chinese cheese. A total of 8851 biosynthetic gene clusters were detected from cCMC, with over 57% classified as novel. We demonstrated that SNP-level variations among different Lactobacillus helveticus strains are associated with differences in β-galactosidase thermostability. Using the cCMC database, we developed a synthetic microbial community as the starter culture for Qula, a yak milk-based Chinese cheese produced by the Tibetans. Overall, the cCMC provides a comprehensive resource of cheese to enable future attempts on large-scale industrial production of naturally fermented cheeses with distinctive ethnic features.Subject terms: Metagenomics, Food microbiology, Bacterial genomics, Microbiome

Introduction

Microorganisms are essential to fermented food production, driving key biochemical transformations that contribute to the quality, shelf life and health benefits1. Cheese production and consumption can be traced back thousands of years, with over 2000 varieties now having been developed and more than 60% of people in Europe and America consuming cheese on a daily basis2,3. Significant heterogeneity in raw materials, equipment, production environments, and processing techniques contributes to diverse microbial communities in cheese ecosystems4. These various microorganisms are crucial to shaping the appearance, flavor, quality and safety of cheese.

Abiotic stresses and nutrient fluctuations during food fermentation can inactivate microorganisms or reduce their abundance, which hinders their isolation and cultivation from the final product5. Given these challenges, constructing a genomic catalog of uncultivated bacteria offers a complementary approach to comprehensively surveying the microbial composition of cheese. Shotgun metagenomic sequencing has been recognized as a powerful tool in this context, providing high accuracy resolution to species/strain levels6. Recently, a metagenomic database encompassing over 70 cheese varieties from 10 Western countries has been integrated to deepen our understanding of cheese microbial communities7. However, their current genomic diversity remains limited, largely due to the low number of metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) recovered from Asian sources.

Chinese traditional artisanal cheeses are iconic dairy products of ethnic minorities, with historical roots tracing back to the Ming Dynasty (1368-1644)8. Primary types of these cheeses include Rushan (RS), Rubing (RB), Naidoufu (NDF), Naigeda (NGD) and Qula (QL), which exhibit different characteristics shaped by the specific region and ethnic group responsible for their production. In contrast to European cheeses, Chinese traditional cheeses rely entirely on natural fermentation instead of commercial starter cultures and rennet, and incorporate distinctive raw materials such as yak milk, contributing to their unique flavor profiles and microbial compositions9,10. However, most of the previous studies on the microbiome of traditional Chinese cheeses have been mainly conducted on single cheese types with limited sample sizes and 16S rRNA sequencing11–13. Based on currently available public data, only 4 samples of Chinese traditional artisanal cheese have been analyzed using metagenomic approaches, representing a vast underexplored and uncharacterized biological resource14. Filling this knowledge gap could provide valuable genomic resources for further exploration of cheese microbial diversity, offer insights into associations between uncultured microbial taxa and their hosts, and promote the discovery of functional genetic elements such as biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs).

Here, we sequenced 235 Chinese traditional cheese samples covering all recognized cheese types and production regions. Building on this, we integrated metagenomic datasets from publicly available non-Chinese cheese samples to establish a curated Cheese microbiome catalog (cCMC). This catalog contains 3327 MAGs, which can be clustered into 395 nonredundant species. Incorporating samples from traditional Chinese cheeses into the cCMC resulted in over a 500% increase in the total number of species compared to the dataset containing only non-Chinese cheese samples. Particularly, we further compared cCMC with the largest currently available food metagenomic database (curatedFoodMetagenomicData, cFMD)7, which comprises 2533 food samples from 50 countries, and identified 173 species that had not previously been detected in food ecosystems. We also cultured a total of 1232 MAG-paired live bacteria strain isolated from the collected Chinese cheese samples as a supplement to the cCMC. Based on the cCMC, associations among cheese physicochemical properties, microbiota, and metabolites were characterized, along with the genetic and functional diversity of a representative species Lactobacillus helveticus. Finally, this database enabled the design of a synthetic microbial consortium for a traditional Chinese cheese (QL), thereby providing microbial insights to support its industrialization.

Results

An expanded catalog of microbial genomes from global cheese

We first collected a total of 235 traditional artisanal cheese samples from China (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 1a). These samples originated from 7 provinces across Yunnan (35.74%), Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region (17.02%), Xinjiang (14.47%), Sichuan (12.34%), Qinghai (8.51%), Gansu (6.38%), and Tibet Autonomous Region (5.53%). They encompass five distinct cheese types, each traditionally produced by a specific ethnic group: RS by the Bai people, RB by the Yi people, NGD by the Kazakhs, NDF by the Mongolians, and QL by the Tibetans. In terms of milk source, bovine milk constituted the majority (51.91%), followed by yak milk (32.77%) and goat milk (15.32%). This effort resulted in complete geographic coverage of all traditional artisanal cheese-producing regions in China and included diverse cheese types, raw materials, and ethnic groups involved in their production.

The nutritional and physicochemical profile of each cheese sample was assessed, including pH, protein and sugar contents, and water activity (Fig. 1b, c and Supplementary Fig. 1b). Notably, QL exhibited the most distinctive characteristics among the sampled cheeses, with an average protein content of ~60%, nearly twice that of common non-Chinese cheese varieties such as Cheddar15 and Gouda16. HS-SPME GCMS analysis of 235 Chinese cheese samples identified 99 volatile compounds potentially contributing to flavor characteristics (Supplementary Data 1). Among them, ten volatile compounds consistently detected across all cheese types formed a core flavor set dominated by long-chain esters such as decanoic acid ethyl ester and octanoic acid ethyl ester (Fig. 1d). These core ester compounds prevalent in Chinese cheeses differ significantly from the short-chain esters predominantly enriched in European cheeses, such as ethyl acetate and ethyl butyrate6.

cCMC set was established by performing metagenomic sequencing on 235 Chinese cheese samples and integrating them with 198 publicly available non-Chinese cheese metagenomes (covering all relevant datasets published prior to January 2024, Fig. 1a, Supplementary Data 2). Building upon established workflows17, we implemented a single-sample metagenomic de novo assembly and binning pipeline to reconstruct microbial genomes (Fig. 1e). This approach resulted in the recovery of 11,678 MAGs. Among these, 3327 MAGs met the medium-quality criteria defined by the Minimum Information about a Metagenome-Assembled Genome standard (completeness ≥50%, contamination ≤ 5%)18 with an average completeness of 81.54% (± 15.41%) and an average contamination level of 1.38% (± 1.32%) (Fig. 1f).

The 3327 MAGs with medium- or higher-quality were subsequently clustered into 395 dereplicated species-level genome bins (SGBs) based on average nucleotide identity (ANI) (Fig. 1g). Taxonomic assignment of these genomes was performed using GTDB-Tk19, and a maximum-likelihood phylogenetic tree was constructed to infer their evolutionary relationships. Overall, taxonomic analysis revealed that the SGBs were distributed across 8 phyla, 9 classes, 23 orders, 50 families, and 134 genera (Supplementary Data 3). Members of the phylum Bacillota accounted for the majority of the cheese-associated microbiota (65.55%), followed by Pseudomonadota (20.38%). A total of 245 MAGs, which had an average completeness of 79.53% ± 15.43% and an average contamination of 1.57% ± 1.39%, were not assigned to any known species. Based on dRep analysis, they were grouped into 85 primary clusters, representing 85 putative novel species (Fig. 1g). Notably, more than 90% of these MAGs were derived from Chinese cheeses, particularly from QL-type cheeses, and most of them belonged to the phylum Pseudomonadota (Supplementary Fig. 2a). According to the distribution of Clusters of Orthologous Groups (COG) functional categories, the most highly represented categories were related to amino acid transport and metabolism, transcription and cell wall/membrane/envelope biogenesis (Supplementary Fig. 2b). In addition, our analyses further indicated that these newly identified species possess diverse functional capacities, including the biosynthesis of terpenoids and polyketides and the metabolism of cofactors and vitamins (Supplementary Fig. 2c).

Among the MAGs in the cCMC dataset, 2851 (85.69%) were recovered from newly sequenced Chinese cheese samples (cCMC-CH), and 476 (14.31%) from previously published non-Chinese samples (cCMC-NC). Beyond expanding the number of reconstructed microbial genomes, the inclusion of cCMC-CH MAGs markedly improved microbial diversity, as evidenced by 320 newly detected species in cheese samples (Fig. 1h). We compared the 395 SGBs of cCMC with microbial genomes obtained from cFMD7, one of the largest food-related metagenomic database currently available. Using a 95% ANI threshold, we identified 173 SGBs representing new species contributed by the cCMC and expanding the species diversity of cFMD by 16.70%. Importantly, nearly all of these 173 SGBs (99.42%) were derived from the newly sequenced Chinese cheese samples. Furthermore, comparison of the cCMC with representative human gut microbiome datasets, Chinese Gut Microbial Reference (CGMR)17 and Unified Human Gastrointestinal Genome (UHGG)20, indicated 309 and 300 novel SGBs, respectively. These results emphasize the uniqueness of the microbial communities in Chinese cheese and reveal variations in the microbial community structure between cheese and other niches.

In addition, 1232 bacterial strains were successfully isolated from the collected cheese samples. High-quality genomes of these strains were obtained through whole-genome sequencing and assembly. Taxonomic annotation showed that the genomes were predominantly assigned to 3 phyla, 12 families, 35 genera, and 72 species. We identified one isolated strain, unassigned to any known species, that appears to represent a novel member of the genus Kluyvera based on phylogenetic analysis. Furthermore, comparison of the cheese-derived SGBs with genomes of the cultured strains revealed 71 matches at a 95% ANI threshold, corresponding to 71 species across 35 genera. Notably, these matches were largely associated with dominant lactic acid bacteria in cheese, including Lactococcus lactis, Leuconostoc mesenteroides, and Streptococcus thermophilus, as well as several members of the genus Pediococcus (Fig. 1i).

The uniqueness of the microbiome in Chinese cheeses

Significant differences were observed in the microbial community composition between Chinese and non-Chinese cheeses. (β-diversity; permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA), p <0.001, Fig. 2a). Lactobacillaceae and Streptococcaceae exhibited the highest MAG counts among all bacterial families in cheese samples, with the former being more prevalent in Chinese samples and the latter in non-Chinese samples (Fig. 2b). Additionally, the families Staphylococcaceae, Brevibacteriaceae, and Dermabacteraceae were more commonly detected in non-Chinese cheese samples, while their prevalence was considerably lower in Chinese cheese samples. In this study, predominant species were defined as those with a relative abundance ≥1% and a prevalence ≥10%. Eleven such species were identified in Chinese samples, compared to eight in non-Chinese samples (Fig. 2c, d). Several lactic acid bacteria, including Leuconostoc mesenteroides (97.87%), Lactococcus_A raffinolactis (97.45%), and Lactobacillus kefiranofaciens (86.38%), were prevalent specifically in Chinese cheeses, indicating distinct microbial distribution between cheese samples from varied areas. Besides the predominant species, we also characterized 36 bacterial families exclusively detected in Chinese cheese microbiomes (Supplementary Data 4). Among these, we highlighted a subset of high-abundance species (relative abundance ≥ 0.1%), such as Acetobacter malorum, Lactococcus_A paracarnosus, and Lactobacillus acetotolerans, which may reflect the distinct microbial features of Chinese cheeses (Fig. 2e). In addition, we further applied PERMANOVA to assess the effects of different processing factors, including geographical origin, milk source, pasteurization status, texture, and rind type of cheese, on cheese microbiota. The results showed that all of these factors were significantly associated with cheese microbial community composition (p < 0.001, Fig. 2f, g and Supplementary Fig. 3a–c). Geographical origin showed the strongest association with community variations, followed by rind type (Fig. 2h). Kruskal-Wallis and Wilcoxon tests revealed that 344 of the 395 microbial species (87.09%) differed significantly among the three geographical areas (Supplementary Fig. 3d, Supplementary Data 5). When comparing the three rind types of cheese, 74.68% of species showed notable variations (Supplementary Fig. 3e, Supplementary Data 6). We noticed that the members of the Brevibacteriaceae family were markedly enriched in nature rind cheeses, which were consistent with previous reports4. Moreover, the texture, milk source, and pasteurization status of cheese induced changes in 79.49%, 64.97%, and 62.78% of microbial species, respectively (Supplementary Data 7–9).

We also investigated the functional potential of the cheese microbiome. A total of 693 functional pathways were detected in our data, some of which were shared by all samples, including amino acid biosynthesis, peptidoglycan biosynthesis, nucleotide biosynthesis, and coenzyme A (CoA) biosynthesis (Supplementary Fig. 4a). Marked differences in the function of microbiome between Chinese and non-Chinese cheeses were observed (p < 0.001, Fig. 2i). The technological factors, including rind type, texture, milk source, and pasteurization status, could all significantly affect the function of cheese microbiome (p < 0.001, Supplementary Fig. 4b–f). For the beneficial activities of the cheese microbiome, analysis of vitamin biosynthesis pathways revealed that processing technologies predominantly influenced the synthesis of B-vitamins (Fig. 2j and Supplementary Fig. 4g–j). For example, the vitamin B6 and B7 biosynthesis pathways showed differences among cheeses with different rind types, and these pathways were primarily contributed by members of the Pseudomonas genus. We also analyzed the genes related to flavor development and focused on five proteolytic and lipolytic enzymes, including cell-envelope proteinase, endopeptidase, aminopeptidase N, triacylglycerol lipase and esterase (Supplementary Fig. 5). Compared with cheeses from other areas, Chinese cheeses exhibited a greater enrichment of cell-envelope proteinase and triacylglycerol lipase. In addition, different processing technologies significantly affected the abundance of these two enzymes, with semi hard cheeses showing notably lower levels than cheeses of other textures (p < 0.0001). We additionally performed functional annotation of the 3327 reconstructed MAGs to characterize microbial functions at the genome level. This analysis identified 17,599 genes with annotated functional information. Among them, glnQ and oppA were the two most frequently detected genes, both annotated as components of the ABC transporter system (Supplementary Fig. 6a). Other highly abundant functional genes were involved in a wide range of functions, including DNA replication and repair (dnaB, ssb, rarA), fatty acid and membrane biosynthesis (fabG, acpP), as well as vitamin and cofactor metabolism (thiD, folC). At the functional pathway level, 185 KEGG modules with defined functions were identified. These modules exhibited significant differences in their distribution across bacterial phyla (Supplementary Fig. 6b). For example, compared with other bacteria, members of Pseudomonadota showed a higher enrichment in pathways associated with the metabolism of cofactors and vitamins. We further focused on functional pathways associated with cheese flavor synthesis. The results demonstrated that these functional profiles differed markedly among the families Lactobacillaceae, Streptococcaceae, and Acetobacteraceae, which accounted for the largest proportion of MAGs (Supplementary Fig. 6c).

Diversity and novelty of biosynthetic potential within the cheese microbiome

To explore the potential of the cheese microbiome for novel bioactive compound discovery, we investigated its secondary metabolite biosynthetic capacity. A total of 8851 putative secondary metabolite biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) from cCMC were detected using AntiSMASH. Further analysis using BiG-SCAPE grouped the BGCs into 2088 gene cluster families, of which 1245 were singletons containing only a single BGC (Supplementary Data 10). We also observed that only 16 families (0.77%), comprising 26 BGCs (0.29%), are associated with annotated clusters in the MIBiG 3.1 database21. These findings highlight the diversity of BGCs and reveal the vast unexplored biosynthetic potential of microbes in cheese samples. We observed 6758 BGCs (76.35%) annotated from MAGs derived from Chinese samples, averaging 2.86 BGCs per MAG, in contrast to 4.81 BGCs per MAG observed in non-Chinese samples (Fig. 3a). Among Chinese cheeses, QL exhibited the highest average number of BGCs per MAG (3.10), whereas RS showed the lowest (2.23). Across all cheese types, Ribosomally synthesized and Post-translationally modified Peptides (RiPPs) represented the predominant class of BGCs (Fig. 3b). In addition, NGD was enriched in terpene-type BGCs compared with the other cheeses. Further analysis revealed that more than 70% of gene cluster families (GCFs) were habitat-specific, with QL showing the highest proportion of habitat-specific GCFs (34.36%), followed by non-Chinese cheeses (Supplementary Fig. 7). Previous studies have shown that certain lactic acid bacteria possessing specific types of BGCs not only contribute to flavor development during fermentation but also exert marked inhibitory effects on pathogenic microbes22,23. In addition, terpene-type BGCs have been reported to produce natural terpenoids, such as carotenoids and farnesene24. These compounds influence the volatile flavor profile of cheese, and may further enhance its nutritional value via their antioxidant activity. Ribosomally synthesized and Post-translationally modified Peptides (RiPPs) represented the predominant class of BGCs across all cheese types. The majority of BGCs were derived from Bacillota, Pseudomonadota, and Actinomycetota, and were predominantly annotated as types RiPPs, other polyketide synthase (other PKS), non-ribosomal peptide synthetase (NRPS) and Terpene (Fig. 3c). Furthermore, the data revealed that BGCs produced by Bacteroidota were present across all types of Chinese cheese, whereas BGCs of this origin were nearly absent in non-Chinese cheese samples (Supplementary Data 10).

Further analysis identified 5106 BGCs from the cCMC database as potentially novel BGCs (nBGCs), defined by a distance ≥900 under the query mode of BiG-SLiCE25 (Fig. 3d). These nBGCs account for 56.67% of total detected BGCs, highlighting the cheese microbiome as an underexplored reservoir of bioactive natural products. Overall, RiPPs (26.50%) represented the most enriched class among these nBGCs, followed by Other PKS (21.71%) and Terpene (14.13%) clusters (Fig. 3e). Further analysis revealed that 3913 nBGCs (76.64%) were uniquely identified from Chinese cheese samples. Terpene-type nBGCs, which are responsible for the biosynthesis of volatile aromatic compounds such as linalool and limonene, were ~six times more abundant in Chinese cheeses than in non-Chinese samples. In addition, significant differences in nBGCs were observed among different types of Chinese cheeses, with Saccharides-type nBGCs being exclusively detected in RB.

Association among the metabolites, microbiota and physicochemical properties in cheese

The metabolites of cheese are generally influenced by raw materials, manufacturing procedures, and microbial activity, playing a fundamental role in its texture, flavor, and nutritional quality26,27. Untargeted metabolomic analysis of cheese samples from China was performed using flow-injection time-of-flight mass spectrometry (FI-TOF-MS), resulting in the identification of 1387 metabolites spanning a broad spectrum of lipids, organic acids, organoheterocyclic compounds, and others (Supplementary Data 11). Targeted correlation analyses were performed to investigate the associations of cheese microbiota with metabolites and with physicochemical properties. We identified 1332 significant associations (|r|>0.4, FDR < 0.05) involving 107 species and 464 metabolites (Supplementary Data 12). Among the 34 species significantly associated with at least 10 metabolites, the top three in terms of association count were A. malorum, Gluconobacter_A sp014132155, and a novel Acetobacter species, all of which belonged to the family Acetobacteraceae (Fig. 4a). We further identified 50 robust associations (|r|>0.4, FDR < 0.05) between 39 microbial species and 7 physicochemical properties of cheese (Fig. 4b, Supplementary Data 13). In addition, we performed a correlation analysis between the metabolomic profiles and the novel BGCs predicted from cheese samples and observed 616 significant associations between individual metabolites and annotated BGC types (Supplementary Data 14). For example, the counts of terpene-type BGCs in the cheese samples were positively correlated with the levels of Neoxanthin and 2-Ketospirilloxanthin, two representative terpene-derived compounds, suggesting that these BGCs may contribute to the biosynthesis of the corresponding secondary metabolites (Supplementary Fig. 8).

To evaluate whether cheese physicochemical properties influence metabolite levels through microbial mediation, we applied directional mediation analysis based on 37 microbial species with significant associations to both physicochemical properties and metabolites, and identified 489 significant mediation linkages (FDR < 0.05, Supplementary Data 15). We observed that Acetobacteraceae species (Acetobacter, Gluconobacter and Gluconobacter_A) mediated most of the observed associations and Acetobacter malorum was particularly noteworthy for its exclusive detection in Chinese cheeses and notably high prevalence (87.01%) in QL samples (Fig. 4c). This species mediated the influence of cheese protein and sugar content on 5,10-Methylenetetrahydrofolate (5,10-Methylene-THF) level (p = 0.02) (Fig. 4d, e). 5,10-Methylene-THF has been reported to participate the synthesis of 5-methyltetrahydrofolate (5-MTHF, a biologically active folate derivative that can be directly absorbed by the human body and function as a coenzyme to mediate critical physiological processes) in food and gut-derived bacteria28. Genomic analysis revealed that A. malorum harbors a metF gene, which encodes 5-MTHF synthetase. Further analysis of representative species from the cCMC showed that the metF gene is present in nearly all bacteria of the family Acetobacteraceae, highlighting the key role of this family in cheese nutritional quality (Supplementary Fig. 9). An additional fermentation experiment showed that compared with the control group, inoculation with Acetobacter malorum significantly increased the contents of total folate (p < 0.05, Fig. 4f), especially that of 5-MTHF (Supplementary Fig. 10, p < 0.05) in yak milk. In contrast to Acetobacter malorum, some common cheese bacteria, including Lactobacillus delbrueckii, Lacticaseibacillus paracasei and Leuconostoc mesenteroides, exhibited markedly weaker effects on folate accumulation in samples (p < 0.05). Taken together, our data provide insights into the potential mechanisms underlying the relationships among cheese metabolites, microbiota, and physicochemical properties.

Genetic diversity of typical microbes in cheeses

The phylogenetic analyses of all 16 species with a prevalence of ≥10% across cheese samples were performed and the results indicated that strains from most species did not exhibit a clear clustering pattern according to cheese types (Fig. 5a and Supplementary Fig. 11a). However, it is noteworthy that Leuconostoc falkenbergense strains from cheeses could be distinctly separated based on their geographical origin. Moreover, pangenome analyses revealed extensive differences in the number of shared genes across species, ranging from 892 to 1825 (Supplementary Fig. 11b). Among the four predominant species shared by both Chinese and non-Chinese cheeses, including Lactococcus lactis, Lactococcus cremoris, Streptococcus thermophilus and Lactobacillus helveticus, strains originating from Chinese cheeses harbored significantly more strain-specific genes than those from non-Chinese samples (Supplementary Fig. 11c). These findings suggested that variations in geographic origin and manufacturing processes may shape the genomic diversity of cheese-associated strains. Among these shared species, Lactobacillus helveticus has been widely recognized for its significant contribution to dairy fermentation29. Although the phylogeny constructed from GTDB-defined conserved core genes showed that Lactobacillus helveticus lacked a geographically structured pattern, principal component analysis based on single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNPs) clearly distinguished Chinese strains from non-Chinese ones (Fig. 5b). Through a comparative analysis of SNPs present in ≥10% of all strains, 1625 SNPs exhibited significant differences in mutation frequency between the two Lactobacillus helveticus groups, including 1013 SNPs specifically enriched in Chinese strains (FDR < 0.05, log₂[FC] > 2) (Fig. 5c). From these Chinese-enriched SNPs, the top 28 candidates ranked by mutation frequency, defined as the proportion of strains carrying a mutation at a given SNP site, were selected for functional annotation (Fig. 5d). Their corresponding genes were involved in carbohydrate transport and metabolism (ptsI and lacM), amino acid transport and metabolism (hipO and pepQ), lipid transport and metabolism (plsC), and other related pathways.

As an essential enzyme in lactose catabolism by lactic acid bacteria, β-galactosidase is important during cheese fermentation, where it catalyzes lactose hydrolysis and facilitates the formation of characteristic flavor compounds30. In Lactobacillus helveticus, β-galactosidase constitutes an enzyme complex encoded by the lacL and lacM genes31. A comparative analysis using MEGA1132 revealed distinct amino acid variations in these genes among Lactobacillus helveticus strains of different geographic origin (Supplementary Fig. 12a, b). Interestingly, non-Chinese strains exhibited high sequence similarity in both lacL and lacM, while Chinese strains displayed 12 mutated residues (1.9%) in lacL and 10 mutated residues (3.1%) in lacM, with these mutations being conserved across the Chinese samples (Supplementary Data 16 and 17). Among these, we focused on eight mutated residues that are prevalent in Chinese-derived strains (Fig. 5e) and examined the structural variations of these residues in the two groups of Lactobacillus helveticus (Fig. 5f). A combination of eight mutated residues was introduced simultaneously through site-directed mutagenesis to evaluate their collective functional effects. We obtained the mutant enzyme via heterologous expression and assessed its enzymatic characteristics in comparison with the wild-type enzyme. Circular dichroism analysis revealed that the wild-type enzyme exhibited a melting temperature 2.62 °C higher than that of the mutant (Fig. 5g). Additionally, the half-life times recorded for wild-type enzyme at 45 °C (optimal activity) and 60 °C (heat stress) were ~320 min and ~17 min, compared to ~205 min and ~4 min for the mutant, respectively (Fig. 5h and Supplementary Fig. 12c–e). These findings suggest the enhanced thermal stability of the wild-type enzyme over the mutant and highlight genetic and functional differences in β-galactosidase between Chinese and non-Chinese Lactobacillus helveticus strains.

A synthetic microbial community for a specific Chinese cheese

QL, a traditional Tibetan artisanal cheese made from yak milk, differs from other Chinese cheeses due to its distinctive flavor profile and superior protein content (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 13). In contrast to other Chinese cheeses dominating by limited bacterial families, QL exhibits a more diverse microbial community, with predominant species distributed across Lactobacillaceae, Streptococcaceae, Acetobacteraceae, Bifidobacteriaceae, and Pseudomonadaceae (Fig. 6a). This microbial complexity, while contributing to the desirable characteristics of QL, presents significant barriers to the development of standardized starter cultures, which is a critical prerequisite for industrial production. Here, Synthetic microbial communities (SynComs), whose recent advances in fermented food production have demonstrated innovative potential, may offer a promising solution by recapitulating flavor and nutritional profiles while reducing microbial complexity33–35.

Based on the data of cCMC, a total of 20 core species were identified in QL, defined by ≥0.1% relative abundance and ≥80% prevalence, accounting for 74.35% of the total microbiota. Thirteen of these were subsequently selected as candidate SynComs members (Fig. 6b and Supplementary Data 18), while the other strains were not included for the following reasons: phylogenetic redundancy at the species level, potential opportunistic pathogenicity or association with food spoilage. Flavor analysis demonstrated that the SynComs-fermented cheese more closely resembled the flavor profile of the natural sample compared to that produced using the commercial dairy starter cultures (CSC, Fig. 6c). The colonization resistance of SynComs against representative spoilage microorganisms (Staphylococcus aureus and Listeria monocytogenes) in cheese was also evaluated using CSC and a natural microbial community (NMC) as control groups. The results demonstrated that SynComs and NMC exhibited significantly superior inhibitory effects on the proliferation of both pathogens compared to CSC throughout the fermentation process (p < 0.0001, Fig. 6d, e). At three timepoints of fermentation (12 h, 24 h and 48 h), the viable count of S. aureus in SynComs- and NMC-fermented systems was at least 100 times lower than that in the CSC-fermented system. Overall, the constructed database (cCMC) serves as a microbial resource to support the design of SynComs for artisanal cheese, thereby making large-scale, standardized production of such traditional products possible.

Discussion

Here we established the cCMC by reconstructing 3327 medium- or higher-quality MAGs, representing 395 nonredundant species derived from 433 cheese samples. To our knowledge, this is the first dedicated genome catalog of the cheese ecosystem. As a supplement to the cCMC, 1232 live bacterial strains were also isolated from the collected cheese samples, and their whole-genome sequencing data were paired with MAGs. We compared our dataset with a recent large-scale food genome collection (cFMD)7 and identified 173 species-level MAGs representing species newly contributed by the cCMC, thereby enriching food reference genomes and particularly enhancing the representation of cheese-associated microbes. Adding the sequencing data from Chinese cheese samples to the existing cheese metagenomic datasets (all publicly available before January 2024) resulted in an increase of over 500% in the number of observed microbial species. We further provided other metadata from Chinese cheeses, including physicochemical properties, volatile flavor compounds, and metabolites, to support investigations into the relationships between microbial communities and cheese quality.

In contrast to European varieties, Chinese traditional artisanal cheese undergoes spontaneous fermentation driven by native environmental microbiota, contributing to its distinct microbial community and flavor profile6,36. Comparative analysis of the microbial composition of cheeses revealed that specific lactic acid bacteria such as Lactobacillus kefiranofaciens and Leuconostoc mesenteroides are prevalent in Chinese cheese samples (Fig. 2), whereas these species are not commonly detected in cheeses produced in regions outside of China6. This microbial divergence may be associated with differences in manufacturing processes, especially reliance on natural fermentation and the lack of standardized starter cultures in Chinese cheese production. Consistent with our findings, Lactobacillus kefiranofaciens has been reported to be enriched in other naturally fermented dairy products such as kefir37. Another interesting observation is that some unique species are exclusively found in Chinese cheeses, with bacteria from the family Acetobacteraceae comprising the largest proportion (Supplementary Data 4). Members of the Acetobacteraceae family are important industrial fermentation strains that produce beneficial metabolites, such as organic acids, which contribute to the sensory properties of food38,39. Studies have shown that the abundance of Acetobacteraceae is influenced by the environmental oxygen level due to the dependence of these strains on aerobic respiratory pathways for energy production40. Compared to the industrialized fermentation with limited oxygen availability in non-Chinese cheeses, the natural fermentation environment in Chinese cheese may facilitate the enrichment of Acetobacteraceae. Notably, a specific Acetobacteraceae bacterium, Acetobacter malorum, was identified in cheese and found to be significantly enriched in QL (Fig. 2), a yak milk-based Chinese cheese produced by the Tibetans. Acetobacter malorum was initially isolated from fruit and has potential as a starter culture in vinegar production41. Studies have suggested that, compared to cow and goat milk, yak milk contains higher levels of specific amino acids, such as glutamic acid and serine42. These amino acids have been reported to promote the growth of Acetobacter malorum43, which may be responsible for its prevalence in QL.

BGCs in food fermentation systems are highly divergent from those in ecosystems such as the human gut44, ocean45, and soil46, largely driven by unique microbial species exclusively present in food47. The cCMC encompassed great potential in the understanding of BGC diversity, with over half of the BGCs (57.69%) identified as novel (Fig. 3). Particularly, the vast majority (78%) of these novel BGCs were annotated from Chinese cheese samples, which may be partly attributed to the higher sequencing depth (up to 20 GB) in this study. The natural products of RiPPs-type BGCs are widely recognized for their antimicrobial properties48. We observed that this type of BGC predominates within the cheese microbiota, which is consistent with an analysis of BGCs in globally fermented foods47. Bacteroidota-derived BGCs, considering as a main source of flexirubin-like pigment biosynthesis49, were found to be abundantly present in Chinese cheese, which may contribute to the formation of the rind color of these cheese samples. Furthermore, a saccharide-type BGC, previously reported to be enriched in the human gut and oral microbiomes50, was identified for the first time in cheese samples. Although the biological roles of this type of BGC remain underexplored, preliminary studies suggest that it may be involved in the production of cytotoxic or antimicrobial saccharides of bacteria51,52. The evidence presented here supports the cCMC as a valuable resource for discovering novel secondary metabolites and highlights the distinct and rich biosynthetic potential of Chinese cheese microbiota.

Although microbial metabolites associated with cheese flavor and quality have been extensively characterized, the mechanisms linking these metabolites to specific microbes remain elusive. The cCMC, including diverse abiotic information and metabolic profiles, provides a precious reference for examining the associations among cheese physicochemical characteristics, microbial communities, and functional metabolites. We observed extensive associations between Acetobacteraceae and multiple metabolites, including organic acids and lipid compounds. This is likely attributable to the presence of distinctive membrane-bound dehydrogenases in Acetobacteraceae, which rapidly oxidize periplasmic substrates and thereby promote the activity of multiple metabolic pathways, such as alcohol oxidation and organic acid metabolism53. Among Acetobacteraceae, Acetobacter malorum shows the highest number of associations with metabolite production, possibly due to its widespread presence in Chinese cheese. Of further nutritional importance, Acetobacter malorum was observed to mediate the effect of cheese proteins on folate derivatives. Although a previous study demonstrated that Acetobacter malorum can synthesize functional vitamins like vitamin B1254, direct evidence for its ability to produce folate derivatives is still lacking. To investigate this potential, we used the cCMC database as a reference and found that folate derivative biosynthesis genes were present in nearly all Acetobacteraceae species (Fig. 4). Our result offers novel insights into the mechanisms influencing metabolite formation in cheese, highlighting the important role of Acetobacteraceae in shaping Chinese cheese quality.

The cCMC provides a database and platform that facilitates strain-level understanding of functional differences in microbes across diverse cheese environments. Lactobacillus helveticus, a dominant species in cheese microbiota, promotes fermentation by hydrolyzing lactose into glucose and galactose using its β-galactosidase29. A large number of SNPs was identified in Lactobacillus helveticus from the cCMC, revealing widespread intraspecies genetic variation. The differences in allele frequencies among Lactobacillus helveticus strains from geographically distinct sources can be due to varying selective pressures imposed by the fermentation process, which may highlight the environmental adaptability of this species. Recent studies have also revealed that the abiotic environmental selection pressures are associated with genomic diversity within species in lactic acid bacteria, including Lactobacillus helveticus, Lactococcus lactis and Lactobacillus delbrueckii55,56. While many SNPs do not lead to discernible phenotypic alterations, some that result in amino acid change have been shown to affect physiological functions of the strains, such as protein hydrolysis57. In our analyses, we have confirmed that specific amino acid mutations in the gene encoding β-galactosidase can affect the enzymatic thermostability of Lactobacillus helveticus. The insufficient thermostability of β-galactosidase from Chinese strains may result from the lack of selective adaptation to high temperatures or other industrial fermentation stresses.

Our findings reveal that QL contains a higher protein content and displays a distinct profile of ester compounds compared to non-Chinese cheeses (Fig. 1). Additionally, it exhibits distinct flavor profiles, characterized by creamy and fatty notes, differing from the fresh and fruity aromas of European cheeses6. However, the complex microbial community of QL presents a major challenge to its large-scale industrial production. Simplified SynComs have been increasingly employed as model systems to investigate the functions of native microbiota. This is due to their preservation of essential features of natural communities, along with reduced complexity and defined microbial composition58. Recent studies demonstrate that SynComs can successfully perform various functions, such as pathogen resistance59 and biofilm formation60. Emerging evidence indicates that SynComs have also shown potential in other naturally fermented foods, including bean paste33 and sauerkraut61. Based on cCMC platform, a purposeful SynComs was constructed to serve as a starter culture for QL fermentation and shown to effectively replicate the authentic flavor of QL. In addition, it provides colonization resistance against spoilage bacteria similar to that exhibited by the native QL microbiota.

In summary, this work fills significant knowledge gaps in the understanding of microbial diversity and functions in cheese ecosystems and provides a comprehensive database for the design of cheese-related SynComs, thereby offering new possibilities for large-scale industrial production of naturally fermented cheeses.

Methods

Sample collection, isolates and sequencing

Chinese traditional artisanal cheese samples were collected from seven provinces encompassing the Yunnan, Xinjiang, Sichuan, Qinghai, Gansu, Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region and Tibet Autonomous Region between May 2022 and July 2023 (Supplementary Data 2). Samples were collected using sterile bags and kept at 4 °C during transport and storage prior to further analysis. Under sterile conditions, the cheeses were ground and homogenized using a grinder. A subsample of 20 g from each cheese was stored at −20 °C for subsequent flavor analysis, and 10 g was combined with 40 mL sterile water prior to DNA extraction and metabolome analysis. DNA extraction from the cheese samples was performed using the DNeasy PowerFood Microbial Kit (Qiagen, cat.21000). After extracting DNA, libraries were prepared and whole-genome shotgun sequencing was performed on 235 samples using the MGI DNBSEQ-T7 platform. During data processing, low-quality reads and reads aligned to the animal reference genome (including ARS-UCD2.0, BosGru3.1, and ARS1.2) were filtered out by kneadData (v0.10.0) and Bowtie2 (v2.5.1)62. 1232 Cultivated isolates from various Chinese cheese samples were grown in brain heart infusion broth at 37 °C, followed by cell collection through centrifugation at 6000 × g for 10 min. The genomic DNA was extracted and then sequenced by Novogeneusing the Illumina Novaseq platform with paired-end 150 bp. Additionally, metagenomic data from 198 non-Chinese cheese samples were downloaded from public databases and processed identically to data from 235 Chinese cheese samples.

Isolates and metagenomic assembly and binning

Assembly of the 235 metagenomic and 1232 genomic samples was performed with MEGAHIT (v1.2.9)63 using default k-mer parameters, retaining only contigs ≥200 bp in length. Subsequently, quality-controlled reads were aligned to the assembled metagenomes using Bowtie2 (v2.5.1)64 with default parameters, and SAMtools (v1.18)65 was used to convert the resulting alignments into BAM format. Coverage values were determined by applying the ‘jgi_summarize_bam_contig_depths’ script from MetaBAT2 (v2.12.1)66 to the generated BAM files. Metagenomic assemblies were binned using MetaBAT2 (v2.12.1), with contigs shorter than 1.5 kb excluded during the binning process.

Genome quality control

The completeness and contamination of MAGs were assessed using the ‘lineage_wf’ workflow of CheckM (v1.1.3)67. Assemblies were retained if they met quality criteria: for MAGs, a minimum completeness of 50% and contamination below 5%; for cultured isolates, completeness ≥90% and contamination ≤5% were required. After the filtering, we obtained 3327 MAGs and 1232 isolate genomes which were used for the subsequent analyses.

Clustering MAGs into species-level representatives

A total of 3327 MAGs were clustered into 395 representative species-level MAGs by employing dRep (v3.4.2)68 using these settings: ‘-pa’ 0.9, ‘-sa’ 0.95, ‘-cm’ larger, and ‘-con’ 10.

Taxonomic classification and phylogenetic analyses

Taxonomic classification for each assembled genome was conducted using the Genome Taxonomy Database Toolkit (GTDB-Tk, v2.3.0) with reference to GTDB release 207. The ‘classify_wf’ module of GTDB-Tk was executed using designated parameters, and maximum-likelihood phylogenetic trees were constructed from protein sequence alignments generated by the ‘infer’ function with default parameters. Finally, the phylogenetic tree, built upon the 122 archaeal or 120 bacterial universal marker gene sets produced by GTDB-Tk, was visualized using iTOL. ANI between 1,232 bacterial isolates and 395 representative MAGs in the cCMC database was calculated using fastANI69, with pairs exhibiting ANI ≥ 95% considered “successful pairings” representing the same species.

Functional analyses

The functional composition and metabolic potential of the microbial communities were assessed using HUMAnN3 (v3.8) with default parameters, which allows community-level pathway and functional profiling70. In contrast, functional annotation of the MAGs was performed using eggNOG-mapper (v2.1.10), which assigns orthologous groups based on the eggNOG database to predict gene functions. This approach provides COG and KEGG annotations, allowing us to capture the species-specific functional potential of each MAG. Analyses were performed using the default parameters of eggNOG-mapper71.

Biosynthetic gene cluster analysis

Identification of BGCs within the MAGs was performed using AntiSMASH (v7.0.0)72 with default settings. BiG-SCAPE (v1.1.5)73 was used to perform clustering analysis on BGCs predicted in this study together with 2,749 BGCs from the MiBiG database (v3.1)21. Based on sequence similarity networks and applying the default score cutoff (c = 0.3), BGCs were grouped into gene cluster families. To assess the novelty of BGCs, BiG-SLiCE (v2.0.0)74 was executed in query mode using the BiG-FAM database, with a threshold of t = 900. The resulting BiG-SLiCE distance reflected the degree of similarity between each BGC and previously computed gene cluster families, with larger distances indicating greater novelty.

Comparative analysis of Lactobacillus helveticus genomes

A phylogenetic analysis was conducted on 114 metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) annotated as Lactobacillus helveticus. Prokka (v1.14.6) was employed to predict protein-coding genes, and the resulting protein sequences were functionally annotated using eggNOG-mapper (v2.1.10). Core genome alignments were generated with Roary (v3.13.0), followed by SNP calling using Snippy (v3.1) to identify single nucleotide polymorphisms among strains. Only SNPs with a mutation frequency ≥10% in all tested strains were included to ensure that the analyzed sites contained sufficient variations between strains. For each site, the presence of a mutation was coded as 1, and the absence (including missing values) as 0, resulting in a binary (0/1) matrix. This matrix was then used for PCoA to assess strain-level genetic differences based on SNPs.

HS-SPME GCMS analysis

A subsample of 4 g from each cheese were weighed into 20 mL headspace bottles, each sealed with a Teflon-lined cap. The samples were analysed in triplicates. A single DVB/CAR/PDMS fibre (50/30 µm, Supelco, 57328-U) was inserted into a vial and exposed to the headspace for adsorption for 35 min at 60°C to extract the volatile compounds. Finally, the fiber was pulled out and then inserted into the inlet of the GC and desorbed for 5 min at 250 °C. The column oven temperature was initially set to 40 °C and held for 4 min, then increased to 100 °C at a rate of 3 °C/min and held for 2 min, followed by an increase to 150 °C at 4 °C/min, and finally raised to 230 °C at 6 °C/min and maintained for 2 min, resulting in a total GC run time of 55 min. The carrier gas used was helium, maintained at a flow rate of 1 mL/min. Operating at 70 eV in electron ionization mode, the MS detector had its ion source temperature set to 240 °C and the quadrupole was operated in scan mode, collecting total ion currents from m/z 33 to 550 at a scan rate of 0.2 scans/s. Volatile compounds were identified based on their retention indices-determined using straight-chain alkanes (C7-C30)-and by comparison with mass spectra from the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) library. Only compounds with a spectral match score ≥800 were considered for further analysis and recorded.

The determination of the production of folate and its derivatives by Acetobacter malorum

Unfermented yak milk was used as the control, while the experimental groups were prepared by inoculating the milk with Acetobacter malorum and other bacteria commonly found in Chinese cheeses, respectively, including Lactobacillus delbrueckii, Lacticaseibacillus paracasei and Leuconostoc mesenteroides. All strains were activated for three generations and inoculated into yak milk at 2% (v/v). Fermentation was carried out for 48 h, and samples were collected at specific time points for the quantification of folate and its derivatives, which included tetrahydrofolate, 5-methyltetrahydrofolate and 5-formyltetrahydrofolate. These metabolites were extracted and quantified according to previously reported methods, with minor modifications75,76. Briefly, samples were lysed with grinding beads and boiling, then incubated with enzyme solution at 37 °C for 4 h. After centrifugation and acetonitrile purification, the supernatant was freeze-dried, reconstituted, and folate content was quantified by Liquid Chromatography–Tandem Mass Spectrometry.

Metabolome analysis

Cheese samples were thawed on ice and thoroughly vortexed to achieve a homogeneous mixture. A 5 mL aliquot was mixed with 45 mL of 80% methanol and vortexed for 15 s. Incubation at 4 °C for 1 h was carried out to facilitate protein precipitation, and the mixture was subsequently centrifuged at 3200 × g for 30 min. Untargeted metabolomic analysis of cheese samples were performed using flow-injection mass spectrometry (FI-MS) on an Agilent 6550 Q-TOF platform77,78. Briefly, data acquisition was conducted in negative ionization mode with full scan mass spectrometry at a rate of 1.4 Hz, operating in 4 GHz High Resolution mode over an m/z range of 50–1000. The mobile phase consisted of 60:40 isopropanol: water containing 1 mM ammonium fluoride (pH 9.0), supplemented with 10 nM hexakis(1H,1H,3H-tetrafluoropropoxy) phosphazene and 80 nM taurocholic acid for real-time mass calibration. Each sample (100 μL) was injected in randomized sequence into the ionization source. Data were collected in profile mode, converted to centroid format, and processed using MATLAB (MathWorks). Missing values were imputed through recursive search of the raw spectra, and ions were tentatively identified based on high-resolution mass accuracy and isotopic pattern matching after consensus centroid alignment across all samples. Metabolite identification and annotation were conducted by referencing the KEGG, HMDB, and ChEBI databases.

β-galactosidase mutant construction and preparation

Codon-optimized β-galactosidase genes derived from both Chinese and non-Chinese sources were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co., Ltd. and inserted into the NdeI and XhoI restriction sites of the pET-28a (+) expression vector. Escherichia coli DH5α and BL21 (DE3) strains were sourced from our laboratory’s culture collection. For protein expression, Escherichia coli BL21 (DE3) was grown at 37 °C in Luria–Bertani medium supplemented with kanamycin (50 μg/mL). Upon reaching the target optical density, cultures were induced with 0.5 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside and subsequently incubated at 12 °C for 24 h to promote protein expression. Cells were collected by centrifugation at 8000 × g for 5 min, resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline, and disrupted by ultrasonication (300 W, 3 s on/4 s off) for 15 min. The lysate was clarified by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C to remove cell debris. The clarified supernatant was loaded onto a Ni-Trap metal affinity column (GE Healthcare) pre-equilibrated with binding buffer. The column was then washed with 10 mL of buffer containing 300 mM NaCl and 50 mM imidazole to eliminate non-specifically bound proteins. Elution of the target protein was performed using 3-5 mL of buffer comprising 300 mM NaCl and 150 mM imidazole, followed by desalting. The purity of the protein and expression level of β-galactosidase were assessed by SDS-PAGE.

Determination of enzymatic properties and thermostability of β-galactosidase

To determine the optimum reaction temperature, β-galactosidase activity was measured at 30, 35, 40, 45, 50, 55, and 60 °C. The activity at the optimal temperature was set as 100%, and relative activities at other temperatures were calculated accordingly. The half-life (t₁/₂) of purified China-type and non-China-type β-galactosidase were evaluated by incubating the enzymes at 45 °C and 60 °C for various time intervals. Enzyme activity prior to heat treatment was considered 100%.

Circular dichroism (CD) measurements were performed using a Chirascan V100 spectrometer to examine the thermostability of China-type and non-China-type β-galactosidase. CD spectra were recorded three times over the range of 220–320 nm using a 1 mm path-length quartz cell. Background signals from the buffer and β-galactosidase solutions were also measured and subtracted. CD melting analysis was conducted at 220 nm with a heating rate of 1 °C/min.

Preparation of synthetic community for in vitro experiments

Bacterial strains were selected based on microbiome sequencing data obtained from Chinese cheese samples. The mean relative abundance and prevalence of each strain were calculated using 77 samples collected from QL. Strains exhibiting a relative abundance of ≥0.1% in at least 80% of the samples were considered candidates for inclusion in the synthetic community. Following the exclusion of strains exhibiting phylogenetic redundancy at the species level, potential opportunistic pathogenicity, or association with food spoilage, 13 representative isolates were ultimately selected to constitute the synthetic microbial consortium. All selected strains were obtained from public culture collections or academic laboratories, with their sources listed in Supplementary Data 18.

Each strain was inoculated into fresh MRS medium and cultured under appropriate conditions until reaching the logarithmic growth phase. Cultures were adjusted with fresh medium to achieve a final OD₆₀₀ of 0.4. Equal volumes of each normalized culture were pooled to assemble a synthetic community consisting of 13 members. The working inoculum was obtained by centrifuging the mixed culture at 5000 × g for 5 min and resuspending the resulting pellet in an equal volume of phosphate-buffered saline. This inoculum was subsequently introduced into fresh sterile yak milk at a 2:100 (v/v) dilution and incubated at 30 °C until fermentation was complete. Samples were collected at defined time points for flavor profiling and viable plate count of spoilage microorganisms.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using R (v4.4.0) and Python (v3.7.6). Associations between species and sample phenotypes, as well as between species and metabolites, were evaluated using Spearman’s rank correlation. For microbial features significantly associated with both metabolites and cheese phenotypes (FDR < 0.05), we first evaluated the correlation between the metabolites and cheese phenotypes using Spearman’s method (FDR < 0.05). We subsequently conducted mediation analysis using the mediate function from the mediation package (v4.5.0) to investigate the mediating roles of metabolites and microbial features in cheese phenotype variation79. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was applied for pairwise comparisons, while the Kruskal-Wallis test was employed for comparisons involving multiple groups. The Benjamini-Hochberg procedure was applied to control the false discovery rates (FDR). Additional statistical methods are described in the corresponding sections and figure legends.
