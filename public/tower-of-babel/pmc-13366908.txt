Tumor capsule status modifies the survival impact of anatomical versus non-anatomical hepatectomy in early-stage hepatocellular carcinoma: a multicenter retrospective study
Authors: Peng Zhu, Yuxin Tang, Chihyuan cheng, Yongyi Zeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe oncologic superiority of anatomical resection (AR) over non-anatomical resection (NAR) for hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) remains controversial, and the extent to which tumor capsule integrity modifies the survival benefit of AR has not been well defined.MethodsThis multicenter retrospective study included 1,958 patients with early-stage HCC who underwent curative-intent hepatectomy across seven institutions. Patients were classified according to hepatectomy type (AR vs. NAR) and tumor capsule status (complete, incomplete, or absent). Overall survival (OS) and recurrence-free survival (RFS) were analyzed using Cox proportional hazards models. Inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) was applied to balance baseline characteristics. Ordered interaction analyses were conducted to assess effect modification between hepatectomy method and tumor capsule status.ResultsIn IPTW-weighted analyses, NAR was independently associated with worse OS (HR 1.46, 95% CI 1.27–1.67; P < 0.001) and RFS (HR 1.24, 95% CI 1.12–1.38; P < 0.001) compared with AR. Tumor capsule status showed a graded association with survival outcomes, with incomplete and absent capsules associated with progressively poorer OS and RFS. Importantly, a significant ordered interaction between hepatectomy method and tumor capsule status was observed for both OS (HR 1.65, 95% CI 1.35–2.01; P < 0.001) and RFS (HR 1.45, 95% CI 1.24–1.70; P < 0.001). Stratified analyses demonstrated no significant survival difference between AR and NAR in patients with complete capsule, whereas AR conferred a pronounced survival advantage in patients with absent capsule.ConclusionsAnatomical resection is associated with improved oncologic outcomes in early-stage HCC overall; however, this survival benefit is strongly modified by tumor capsule status. These findings suggest that tumor capsule status may serve as an important stratification factor when evaluating the extent of liver resection.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12957-026-04376-6.Keywords: Hepatocellular carcinoma, Anatomical resection, Non-anatomical resection, Tumor capsule, Overall survival, Recurrence-free survival

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide and remains a major public health burden [1–3]. According to the Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) staging system, patients with solitary HCC without macrovascular invasion or extrahepatic spread are classified as early-stage disease (BCLC 0 or A), for whom curative-intent liver resection is recommended as first-line therapy [4]. However, despite apparently favorable tumor stage, long-term survival after resection varies substantially among these patients [5]. This heterogeneity likely reflects underlying differences in tumor biology that are not fully captured by conventional staging systems. Therefore, there remains a need to identify pathological characteristics that better reflect tumor containment and invasive potential, in order to improve prognostic stratification among patients with solitary early-stage HCC.

Tumor capsule is a clinically important pathological feature in hepatocellular carcinoma [6]. The fibrous capsule, predominantly composed of type I collagen (COL1A1), forms a structural barrier between tumor tissue and surrounding liver parenchyma [7–9]. Accumulating evidence indicates that a complete tumor capsule(CTC) is associated with restrained local invasion and a lower propensity for microvascular invasion, whereas tumor capsule incomplete or absence reflects more aggressive tumor behavior [10, 11]. Clinically, multiple studies have consistently demonstrated that complete tumor capsule correlates with improved overall and recurrence-free survival, while incomplete or absent tumor capsule (ITC or ATC) is an independent predictor of early postoperative recurrence [7, 11]. Given that the tumor capsule is directly encountered during liver resection, its status may influence the oncological impact of different surgical approaches.

The optimal extent of liver resection for early-stage hepatocellular carcinoma remains controversial. Anatomical resection (AR) is theoretically favored for its ability to eradicate potential portal venous micro-metastases within the tumor-bearing segment, whereas non-anatomical resection (NAR) emphasizes maximal preservation of functional liver parenchyma and is often preferred in patients with cirrhosis or tumors located in technically challenging regions [12, 13]. However, accumulating evidence across primary and secondary liver malignancies indicates that the oncological benefit of AR over NAR is not uniform and may vary according to underlying tumor biological characteristics [14, 15]. Notably, both tumor capsule status and hepatectomy method have been independently associated with postoperative recurrence and survival in HCC [16, 17]. Despite this, whether tumor capsule status modifies the prognostic impact of AR versus NAR has not been systematically investigated, and evidence guiding surgical strategy according to capsule integrity is currently lacking.

Therefore, this study investigated the interaction between hepatectomy method (AR and NAR) and tumor capsule status(CTC, ITC and ATC) on postoperative outcomes in solitary early-stage HCC. Using a rigorously adjusted retrospective cohort, we evaluated their combined effects on overall survival (OS) and recurrence-free survival (RFS). By determining whether tumor capsule status modifies the prognostic impact of resection type, this work aims to establish evidence-based stratification criteria for surgical decision-making in early HCC.

Materials and methods

Patients

This Multicenter, retrospective cohort study included consecutive patients who underwent curative-intent hepatectomy for solitary hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) at Mengchao Hepatobiliary Hospital of Fujian Medical University, Eastern Hepatobiliary Surgery Hospital of Shanghai and Wuhan No.1 Hospital of Hubei between January 2017 and December 2019. Hepatectomy was classified as anatomical resection (AR) or non-anatomical resection (NAR). AR was defined as en-bloc removal of one or more contiguous Couinaud segments including the tumor and its portal territory, whereas NAR was defined as limited parenchymal resection aimed at achieving an adequate surgical margin regardless of segmental boundaries. Tumor capsule status was assessed on haematoxylin–eosin and Masson-stained sections by two senior pathologists blinded to clinical data. Tumors were classified into three groups based on capsule integrity: complete tumor capsule(CTC), defined as a continuous collagenous rim (COL1A1-rich); incomplete tumor capsule(ITC), defined as a partially interrupted or fragmented rim; and absent tumor capsule(ATC), defined as complete lack of a fibrous capsule. Discrepancies between the two pathologists were resolved by a third expert. This study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Mengchao Hepatobiliary Hospital of Fujian Medical University (No. 2025-196-01) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The requirement for written informed consent was waived due to the retrospective design.The study design and reporting adhere to the STROBE guidelines [18].

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria were patients who (1) underwent primary partial hepatectomy at all of three centers between January 2017 and December 2019; (2) had pathologically confirmed solitary HCC (excluding non-HCC histology) classified as BCLC stage 0 or A; (3) had preoperative Child–Pugh class A or selected B liver function (score ≤ 7); (4) achieved histologically confirmed R0 resection; and (5) had complete preoperative imaging, laboratory results, and at least one postoperative follow-up record.

Exclusion criteria were patients who (1) had R1 or R2 resection margins or macroscopic residual tumor; (2) received any preoperative locoregional or systemic therapy (including TACE, ablation, radiotherapy, or neoadjuvant systemic therapy); (3) had multiple tumors, macrovascular invasion, or lymph-node or extrahepatic metastases; (4) had concurrent or prior malignancy within 5 years (except adequately treated basal-cell carcinoma or cervical carcinoma in situ); (5) suffered severe immunodeficiency disease, cardiac, pulmonary, or renal dysfunction; (6) were lost to follow-up or refused surveillance; or (7) had > 20% missing values in key baseline variables that could not be retrieved from records.

Preoperative evaluation and surgical procedures

All patients underwent preoperative evaluation including contrast-enhanced CT or MRI of the liver, chest CT when clinically indicated, and a standard laboratory panel including Alpha-Fetoprotein (AFP) and Hepatitis B Virus Deoxyribonucleic Acid (HBV-DNA). Liver function and surgical risk were assessed using Child–Pugh classification and indocyanine green retention at 15 min (ICG-R15) by a multidisciplinary team [4]. Hepatectomy was performed as either anatomical or non-anatomical resection under intraoperative ultrasound guidance, with careful handling of the tumor to minimize manipulation and ensure histologically confirmed R0 margins.

Follow-up

Patients were monitored every 2–3 months during the first 2 years after surgery, every 3–6 months during years 3–5, and every 6 months thereafter. Follow-up assessments included serum AFP, liver function tests, and contrast-enhanced CT or MRI of the liver, with chest CT performed when clinically indicated. Tumor recurrence was defined according to AASLD radiological criteria [4, 19] and confirmed by biopsy when imaging findings were equivocal. Overall survival (OS) and recurrence-free survival (RFS) were calculated from the date of surgery to death or first documented recurrence, respectively.

Statistical analysis

Missing baseline covariates were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE). The extent of missingness for baseline variables was generally low to moderate (Supplementary Table S1), and a missing-at-random (MAR) assumption was considered reasonable, as the probability of missingness was likely related to observed clinical and tumor-related variables rather than unobserved outcomes. No strong evidence of systematic missingness patterns was observed across key variables.

The imputation model included all variables used in the primary analyses, including demographic, clinical, and tumor-related covariates, as well as survival outcomes (OS and RFS) and additional auxiliary variables to improve imputation accuracy. Twenty imputed datasets were generated, which is considered sufficient to achieve stable estimates given the proportion of missing data.

Convergence of the imputation process was assessed by visual inspection of trace plots across iterations, confirming stability of parameter estimates. Subsequent univariate and multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression analyses for OS and RFS were performed within each imputed dataset, and the results were combined using Rubin’s rules.

Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analyses for overall survival (OS) and recurrence-free survival (RFS) were performed within each imputed dataset, and the results were pooled according to Rubin’s rules. No collinearity was observed among the covariates included in the final multivariable model (maximum pooled VIF = 2.7; highest Cramér’s V = 0.06).To adjust for potential confounding, inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) based on propensity scores was applied, and weighted Cox regression models were fitted using the pooled datasets to estimate the effects of hepatectomy method and tumor capsule status on survival outcomes. Ordered interaction analyses [20] were further conducted using IPTW-weighted Cox models to assess whether the association between hepatectomy method and survival differed across tumor capsule status. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were generated for visualization purposes using the first imputed dataset before and after IPTW, with log-rank tests employed to compare survival distributions. All analyses were performed using Python (version 3.13.5) for regression modeling and weighting, and R (version 4.3.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing) for Kaplan–Meier curve generation. Two-sided P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Prognostic factors for RFS

Univariable Cox proportional hazards analyses identified several factors significantly associated with recurrence-free survival, including maximum tumor diameter: HR 1.413, 95% CI 1.222–1.633; P < 0.001, absence of tumor capsule: HR 1.443, 95% CI 1.200–1.736; P < 0.001, resection margin < 1 cm: HR 1.535, 95% CI 1.280–1.841; P < 0.001, presence of microsatellite foci (MSF): HR 3.084, 95% CI 2.520–3.774; P < 0.001, intraoperative blood loss (IBL): HR 2.143, 95% CI 1.683–2.729; P < 0.001, microvascular invasion (MVI): HR 1.511, 95% CI 1.299–1.758; P < 0.001, alpha-fetoprotein (AFP): HR 1.359, 95% CI 1.166–1.585; P < 0.001, hepatectomy method: HR 1.163, 95% CI 1.001–1.351; P = 0.049, liver cirrhosis: HR 1.323, 95% CI 1.132–1.545; P < 0.001, Edmondson–Steiner grade (ES): HR 1.371, 95% CI 1.145–1.640; P < 0.001, and incomplete tumor capsule (ITC): HR 1.206, 95% CI 1.042–1.394; P = 0.012 (Table 2). These variables were subsequently entered into a multivariable Cox regression model. In the adjusted multivariable analysis, the type of hepatectomy remained an independent predictor of recurrence-free survival, together with maximum tumor diameter, unfavorable resection margin, absence or incompleteness of tumor capsule, AFP, HBV DNA, and MVI (Table 3).

To account for baseline imbalances between treatment groups, an IPTW-weighted Cox proportional hazards model was applied. In the weighted analysis, NAR (HR 1.249, 95% CI 1.125–1.387; P < 0.001), ITC (HR 1.474, 95% CI 1.299–1.671; P < 0.001), and ATC (HR 1.653, 95% CI 1.405–1.944; P < 0.001) were independently associated with an increased risk of RFS compared with AR and CTC (Table 4).

Kaplan–Meier survival analyses by hepatectomy type and tumor capsule status

Kaplan–Meier analyses stratified by tumor capsule status demonstrated progressively worse overall survival (OS) and recurrence-free survival (RFS) with decreasing capsule integrity. After IPTW adjustment, 5-year OS rates were 92.6%, 79.6%, and 72.4% for patients with complete, incomplete, and absent tumor capsules, respectively, while the corresponding 5-year RFS rates were 72.5%, 57.6%, and 53.6% (log-rank P < 0.001 for both outcomes) (Fig. 2).

Kaplan–Meier curves of overall survival (OS) and recurrence-free survival (RFS) stratified by tumor capsule status. Significant differences in both OS and RFS were observed among the different capsule status groups (log-rank test, P < 0.001 for both outcomes)

When stratified by hepatectomy method, patients undergoing anatomical resection (AR) showed superior survival compared with those undergoing non-anatomical resection (NAR). After IPTW adjustment, the 5-year OS rates were 81.9% for AR and 71.5% for NAR (log-rank P = 0.008), while the corresponding 5-year RFS rates were 60.7% and 54.8%, respectively (log-rank P = 0.051) (Fig. 3). IPTW-adjusted Kaplan–Meier curves stratified by both tumor capsule status and hepatectomy method are provided in the Supplementary Material (Figures S1 and S2).Given the prognostic relevance of tumor capsule status, additional stratified analyses and ordered interaction models were performed to further assess potential effect modification.

Kaplan–Meier survival analysis stratified by tumor capsule status and hepatectomy method

To further evaluate whether the survival benefit associated with hepatectomy method varied according to tumor capsule integrity, Kaplan–Meier analyses were stratified by capsule status. Survival curves were generated for six subgroups defined by capsule status (complete, incomplete, or absent) and surgical approach (AR vs. NAR) (Fig. 4). After IPTW adjustment, the survival advantage associated with AR became more pronounced with decreasing capsule integrity (Supplementary Figure S4).

Kaplan–Meier survival curves of overall survival (OS) and recurrence-free survival (RFS) across six subgroups defined by tumor capsule integrity and hepatectomy type. The stratified curves visualize the interaction pattern between surgical approach and capsule status on survival outcomes

Among patients with a complete tumor capsule, no significant differences in OS or RFS were observed between AR and NAR. In those with an incomplete capsule, a gradual separation of survival curves was noted, although the differences did not reach statistical significance (Supplementary Tables S4 and S5). Notably, a significant survival advantage associated with AR was observed only in patients without a tumor capsule, with higher 5-year OS (68.3% vs. 58.6%) and RFS (45.1% vs. 37.5%) compared with NAR (log-rank P < 0.001 for OS and P = 0.003 for RFS) (Figs. 5, 6 and 7). Similar patterns were consistently observed in IPTW-adjusted analyses presented in the Supplementary Material (Figures S4–S6).

Discussion

Previous studies have generally suggested that anatomical resection (AR) confers better overall survival and recurrence outcomes compared with non-anatomical resection (NAR) in patients with hepatocellular carcinoma [4, 12, 15]. However, the relative benefit of AR versus NAR has been reported to vary according to tumor-specific pathological features, including microvascular invasion and microsatellite foci [5, 14, 21], while the influence of tumor capsule integrity remains unclear.This study on 1,958 patients with single, early-stage HCC investigated the interaction between hepatectomy method and tumor capsule status on long-term overall and recurrence-free survival.

In this multicenter cohort of patients with early-stage HCC, we found that the survival benefit associated with anatomical resection was not uniform but depended on tumor capsule status. While anatomical resection was associated with improved outcomes at the population level, this advantage was primarily observed in patients with incomplete or absent tumor capsules, consistent with a significant interaction between capsule integrity and surgical approach.

Anatomical resection involves excision of the entire portal venous territory harboring the tumor and is therefore considered more effective in removing occult tumor extension beyond the macroscopic lesion, potentially resulting in improved oncologic outcomes [14, 15, 17, 22, 23].However, AR is also accompanied with increased intraoperative blood loss and extensive liver parenchymal resection [24], a balance that has been highlighted in multiple prior studies.A Japanese multicenter study involving a large cohort reported no significant difference in overall survival between anatomical and non-anatomical resection in patients with microportal vein invasion, despite distinct recurrence patterns between the two surgical approaches and similar overall recurrence rates [25]. Another Japanese nationwide survey reported that the recurrence-free survival advantage of anatomical resection was confined to tumors measuring 2–5 cm, with comparable outcomes between anatomical and non-anatomical resection in tumors < 2 cm and > 5 cm [26]. Collectively, the heterogeneity of these results likely reflects variations in patient and tumor characteristics, including liver functional reserve, cirrhosis burden, severity of microvascular invasion, and capsule integrity. In this large multicenter cohort, anatomical resection was associated with superior overall and recurrence-free survival compared with non-anatomical resection in the overall population; however, subgroup analyses demonstrated no significant survival differences between the two approaches in patients with completely encapsulated tumors, whereas anatomical resection conferred significantly improved overall and recurrence-free survival in tumors without a capsule, forming the clinical basis for the observed interaction between tumor capsule status and resection strategy.

Complete tumor capsule in HCC exerts multifaceted biological effects, including suppression of epithelial–mesenchymal transition through the ONECUT2/TGF-β signaling axis, thereby attenuating the metastatic potential of tumor cells [27]. In parallel, complete capsule is associated with lower tumor cell activity, fewer cells with metastatic competence, and constrained parenchymal colonization by residual tumor cells [16]. moreover, complete capsule helps maintain the polarity of peripheral tumor cells, thereby preventing cellular detachment and the acquisition of invasive phenotypes [28]. Multicenter retrospective data by Zhang et al. [17]. identified absent/incomplete tumor capsules as an independent predictor of worsened overall survival (OS) and recurrence-free survival (RFS) in HBV-related HCC with microvascular invasion (MVI). This finding was corroborated by Tang et al. [14]. in a large multicenter cohort, where preserved capsule integrity significantly conferred protective effects on OS/RFS across both HBV and non-HBV populations. Collectively, these observations validate the prognostic significance of capsule status established in our analysis.Multicollinearity among tumor capsule status, MVI, and tumor diameter was minimal (VIF < 3; Cramér’s V ≤ 0.06), supporting their concurrent inclusion in the model.

Extending these observations, we identified a significant interaction between tumor capsule status and hepatectomy type, indicating that the survival benefit of anatomical resection is not uniform but depends on capsule integrity. Specifically, while anatomical resection (AR) was associated with improved overall and recurrence-free survival in the overall cohort, this advantage was not observed in patients with completely encapsulated tumors, whereas a clear survival benefit emerged in those with incomplete or absent capsules.

From a clinical perspective, our findings do not support the routine use of anatomical resection for all patients with early-stage HCC. Instead, the potential benefit of anatomical resection should be interpreted in the context of tumor capsule status. In patients with a complete capsule, non-anatomical resection may be sufficient to achieve favorable oncological outcomes, whereas anatomical resection may be more appropriate in cases with incomplete or absent capsules, where the risk of microscopic tumor spread is higher. As tumor capsule status is currently determined based on pathological evaluation, future efforts should focus on improving its preoperative assessment to better guide surgical decision-making.

This interaction is biologically plausible. A complete tumor capsule may act as a structural barrier that limits peripheral tumor infiltration and reduces the risk of tumor cell dissemination during surgical manipulation [29, 30]. In such cases, non-anatomical resection (NAR) may be sufficient to achieve adequate local tumor control. In contrast, the absence or disruption of the capsule likely reflects a more invasive tumor phenotype with increased potential for microscopic spread along the portal venous territory where AR may provide superior oncological clearance by removing the entire tumor-bearing segment [31].

Together, these findings suggest that tumor capsule integrity functions as an important effect modifier of surgical benefit, and support a more individualized approach to surgical planning in early-stage HCC. While previous studies have independently evaluated the prognostic role of capsule status and the benefit of anatomical resection, our results highlight their interdependent relationship, providing a clinically relevant framework for tailoring surgical strategy.

An important consideration is whether tumor capsule status can be reliably assessed preoperatively. Conventional imaging modalities may provide indirect information regarding capsule integrity, and capsule enhancement is a recognized feature in hepatocellular carcinoma [32–34] ; however, its role in current diagnostic frameworks has not yet been fully standardized.From a clinical perspective, the ability to predict tumor capsule status before surgery would enable surgeons to better tailor individualized surgical strategies. In this regard, emerging approaches such as radiomics and imaging-based machine learning models hold promise for improving preoperative prediction [35–37].Although this was beyond the scope of the present study, our findings provide a clinically relevant rationale for future research aimed at integrating imaging biomarkers with surgical decision-making to optimize outcomes in patients with early-stage HCC.

Several limitations should be acknowledged.First, the retrospective design may introduce residual confounding despite multivariable adjustment and IPTW. Second, assessment of tumor capsule integrity was based on pathological reports and may be subject to interobserver variability across centers.Third, details on perioperative management and adjuvant therapies were not uniformly available, which may have influenced long-term outcomes. Fourth, potential heterogeneity in surgical techniques and perioperative decision-making across participating centers could not be fully captured or adjusted for.Fifth, although IPTW was applied to balance baseline characteristics, unmeasured confounders and selection bias inherent to non-randomized treatment allocation cannot be completely excluded. Sixth, the study population was derived from high-volume centers in China, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other geographic regions and healthcare settings. Finally, molecular and biological correlates of tumor capsule integrity were not directly evaluated, and external validation in independent prospective cohorts is warranted.

Conclusions

this multicenter analysis indicates that anatomical resection is associated with favorable overall and recurrence-free survival in early-stage HCC; however, this association is not uniform and appears to be strongly influenced by tumor capsule status, with limited benefit in well-encapsulated tumors and a greater survival advantage observed in tumors with incomplete or absent capsules.
