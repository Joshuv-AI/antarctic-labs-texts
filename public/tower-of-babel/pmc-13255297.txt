Components and effects of psychosocial support interventions for relatives of patients with delirium in hospitals, systematic review
Authors: Markus Grebe, Martin Nikolaus Dichter, Richard Dano, Sascha Köpke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRelatives of patients with delirium are often confronted with patients’ challenging behaviours such as hyperactivity, aggression, or hallucinations. As a result, feelings of anxiety and depression episodes have been described. The aim is to identify psychosocial support intervention for relatives of patients with delirium in hospitals, describe components and effects, and identify research gaps.MethodsSystematic review based on PRISMA, including psychosocial support intervention studies addressing burden and support needs of adult relatives of hospital patients with delirium. We searched the databases Medline, Web of Sience, CINAHL, Livivo, PsycINFO, and Cochrane Library in 07/2024 and reference lists. We assessed reporting using TIDieR and CReDECI2 and methodological quality using ROB-1 and the JBI tool for quasi-experimental studies. We performed narrative syntheses.ResultsWe identified eight intervention studies addressing relatives of patients with delirium in different settings. Intervention components included provision of information using flyers, videos, web-sites, or telephone calls, and mentoring in the intensive care unit. Reporting of interventions was mostly poor. No study reported a process evaluation. Four of six studies showed a significant difference in relatives’ delirium knowledge. For relatives’ psychological outcomes, no clear effects were shown. Overall, study quality was limited.ConclusionsMore effectiveness studies are needed with better reporting of intervention components, ideally addressing psychological aspects. Feasibility and acceptance of new interventions should be described within accompanying process evaluations.Trial registrationhttps://osf.io/7jbsk.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07750-x.

Background

Delirium is defined as a disturbance of attention, perception and cognition that develops over a short period of time [2] and can result in long-term cognitive impairment [11] increased mortality [37] and a longer length of stay in intensive care units and hospitals [8]. Symptoms of anxiety [31] and depression [15] were identified in relatives of patients with delirium. A prospective observational study showed significant higher levels of anxiety, depression control, intrusion and PTSD (Posttraumatic Stress Disorder) in relatives of patients with delirium compared to families of patients without diagnosed delirium [16]. Studies often report knowledge deficits about delirium [3, 9, 23] and a need for general provision of information, individual instruction and updates in adequate ways [24].

A number of reviews have been performed regarding interventions for delirious patients in ICUs and hospital in general, mostly addressing training interventions, for example, addressing the involvement of relatives in the prevention and detection of delirium [4, 6, 9, 12, 19]. A recently published scoping review focusses on involvement of relatives in the management of delirium. However, the review was restricted to interventions in intensive care and the search was limited to studies published between 2017 and 2024 [18]. Therefore, an overview of intervention studies addressing the information and psychosocial support needs of relatives of patients with previously diagnosed delirium in hospitals is currently lacking.

Thus, such reviews are needed to implement interventions to make sure that relatives receive the information they need and are supported to counteract fear and uncertainty. This could also increase the potential of relatives to prevent and recognise delirium at an early stage. Moreover, the results could also serve as a basis to plan, pilot and evaluate complex interventions based on the framework of the British Medical Research Council (MRC framework) [7, 33] that was developed for this purpose.

Methods

Study design

Systematic review based on the PRISMA guidelines (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) [30] with the review protocol published in advance (https://osf.io/7jbsk). Initially, we had planned to conduct a scoping review in line with the definition of the JBI Scoping Reviews Methodology Group [26]. However, after a first scope of the literature, we decided that a systematic review was more appropriate.

Eligibility criteria

Experimental and quasi-experimental psychosocial support interventions were included if they addressed support needs of adult relatives (aged 18 and over) of hospitalised patients with a diagnosis of delirium or both patients and relatives (dyadic perspective) compared to any other or no intervention. Studies that addressed relatives of patients with alcohol-related delirium and interventions in which relatives were primarily trained to recognise and prevent delirium were excluded. Also, studies conducted in non-hospital settings were excluded. Studies published in English or German assessing any outcome at relatives’ level were included.

Search methods

The database search was conducted in Medline (via PubMed), Web of Science (via EBSCOhost), CINAHL (via EBSCOhost), Livivo, PsycINFO and the Cochrane Library in October 2020. The search was updated in September 2023 and again in July 2024. Reference lists of publications were searched for further studies. Citation tracking was carried out using the Web of Science database. No contact was made with authors. The search strategies are provided as Supplementary Material 1.

Study selection

Title and abstract screening and full-text screening were performed independently by two reviewers using Covidence systematic review software [35]. In case of discrepancies, a consensus was reached by discussion.

Data extraction

For data extraction, templates were created and draft versions were piloted, discussed and adapted. Extracted data included study objective and study population, study design, outcomes and instruments used as well as study effects. Information on reporting of the delivery of the interventions and components of complex interventions were extracted using TIDieR (Template for Intervention Description and Replication) and CReDECI 2 (Criteria for Reporting the Development and Evaluation of Complex Interventions) checklists [14, 25]. Both reporting guidelines were developed in the context of complex intervention research, with TIDieR focussing on intervention reporting and CReDECI 2 on the complete process of complex intervention research based on the MRC framework.

Data synthesis

Data were visually displayed in tabular form and results were presented narratively. Forest Plots were generated to provide an overview about the effects on delirium knowledge. No meta-analysis was performed.

Assessment of study quality

Risk of bias of randomised controlled trials was assessed using the Cochrane Collaboration’s Risk of Bias Tool (ROB) [13]. Quasi-experimental studies were analysed using the checklist for quasi-experimental studies of the Joanna Briggs Institute [34]. The assessment was performed independently by two reviewers. In case of discrepancies, a consensus was reached by discussion.

Results

The first search in October 2020 identified 8553 titles that were included for abstract screening. After the removal of 8,406 irrelevant abstracts and the screening of 147 full texts, six intervention studies [5, 10, 17, 21, 28, 29] were included. The two update searches in September 2023 (search period 2020–09/2023) and July 2024 (search period 2023–07/2024) identified two further intervention studies [1, 36] leading to the inclusion of a total of 8 studies. One study [17] had already been identified in the initial search, newly adding the description of the development of the complex intervention in a further publication [22] (see Fig. 1).

Characteristics of included studies

The included intervention studies had been published between 2002 and 2024 and conducted in Canada [10, 17, 21], Japan [28, 29], the USA [5, 36], and Australia [1]. The study designs included 6 quasi-experimental studies [1, 5, 10, 17, 28, 29] and two randomised controlled trials [21, 36]. The interventions addressed relatives of patients with delirium in palliative settings [10, 28, 29], intensive care units [17, 21, 36], internal medicine [1], and orthopaedics [5]. Relatives included in the studies were on average between 50 and 75 years old (see Table 1). The characteristics of each study are summarized in Table 1.

Methodological quality

All quasi-experimental studies met 50% or more of the criteria of the JBI checklist, which indicates a moderate quality of these studies. The two randomised controlled trials indicate limitations of study quality in different assessment domains. In the study by Wheeler et al., [36], there was insufficient information available regarding relevant assessment domains, leading to a rating of “unclear risk” in the domains of random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of outcome assessment, and selective reporting [36]. The study by Mailhot et al., [21] was generally assessed as having a low risk of bias, except in the domain of allocation concealment, assessed as a high risk of bias [21]. These potential biases limit the certainty of the study findings. The study by [28] was not rated for bias because of the character as a pre-study of the study published by the same authors [29] (see Supplementary Material 2).

Outcomes and measurement instruments

Relatives’ knowledge about delirium was assessed in 6 studies [1, 5, 10, 17, 29, 36]. For this purpose, one study used the Caregiver ICU Delirium Knowledge Questionnaire (CIDKQ) [17] and three studies used the Caregiver Delirium Knowledge Questionnaire [1, 5, 36]. In [10]; the instrument used was not described in detail; in [29]; knowledge was recorded on the basis of agreement regarding 5 reasons for delirium. Anxiety was assessed in 3 studies using the GAD-7 [17], the State Trait Anxiety Inventory [21], and the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale [36]. Psychological distress and relatives’ burden were measured by [1] using the Kessler Psychological Distress Scale (K10) and Caregiver Delirium Burden Scale (DEL-B-C). Relatives’ mood was assessed in the study by [10] with a 0-100 scale (0 “the worst mood”, 100 “perfectly normal mood”) (see Supplementary Material 3).

Intervention components

The interventions included components such as brochures, flyers or booklets with information on delirium [10, 28, 29], video modules addressing relatives [1, 17, 36], training modules delivered via telephone [5], and verbal information, face-to-face and at different consecutive times during ICU stay [20, 21]. In two studies, relatives were also trained to recognise delirium using case vignettes [5, 17] (see Table 1).

Reporting of intervention components

The aim, rationale and how the intervention was delivered were reported in all studies. In two studies, the delivery of the intervention based on a theory was described ([5]; [20–22]). Aspects such as the description of materials and procedures used were not reported or were unclear in individual studies. In 6 studies, the materials used were reported and made accessible [1, 5, 10, 17, 21, 28]. In 5 studies, it was reported who delivered the intervention. Information about modifications during the study and the fidelity of implementation procedures were not reported in 7 and 6 studies respectively (see Fig. 2 and Supplementary Material 4).

Effects of the interventions

The results of the intervention effects are summarised below. See also Figs. 3 and 4; Table 1, and Supplementary Material 3.

Delirium knowledge

In the three before-and-after studies, positive effects were observed with regard to relatives’ knowledge of delirium [1, 5, 17] and was retained two weeks after the intervention [17] or without a significant difference between 2 weeks and 2 months after the intervention [5] (see Fig. 3).

Fig. 3Forest plot about effects on delirium knowledge - Pretest/Posttest studies

Forest plot about effects on delirium knowledge - Pretest/Posttest studies

One of the two studies in the palliative care setting showed significant differences with regard to knowledge about 2 of 5 causes of delirium [29]. In the second study, the intervention did not lead to an increase in knowledge about the frequency of delirium. However, more relatives in the intervention group knew what delirium was [10]. A study carried out in the critical care setting did not show any significant differences in the overall scores of the delirium knowledge questionnaire used [36] (see Fig. 4).

Psychological outcomes

In one study, anxiety scores showed a non-significant advantage for the intervention group on days 4, 15 and 30 after the intervention [21]. No significant differences were found for relatives’ mood between the usual care group and the intervention group [10]. With regard to stress symptoms, one study found no significant differences between the two groups analysed [29]. Also, no significant change was detected in the study of [1] for psychological stress or the burden of the relatives. A non-significant improvement in self-efficacy was demonstrated, particularly among relatives in the intervention group of the study by [21] (see Table 1 and Supplementary Material 3).

Discussion

Summary of results and comparison with previous reviews

The majority of the identified intervention studies were carried out in palliative care settings [10, 28, 29] and intensive care units [17, 21, 36]. Effects on delirium knowledge were reported in three before-after studies using validated instruments. No significant differences were shown with regard to psychological outcomes. Methodological and reporting quality of the studies were limited. The provision of knowledge about delirium was the essential component in most interventions. None of the studies comprised components of psychosocial support, relaxation training or psychotherapeutic approaches as intervention components. Only the study by [21] used a mentoring approach to provide advice, information and feedback to relatives on the implementation of delirium management [21]. To convey information, materials such as flyers or brochures were used in addition to verbal communication, or information was conveyed web-based by video or telephone. A scoping review solely focusing on intervention studies conducted in intensive care units also highlights the aspects of education and mentoring, as well as partnership in the development of training materials [18]. The integrative review by [19] identified three themes of educational interventions: capacity building, resource provision, and enhancement of relationships [19]. Intervention studies that primarily focused on training relatives on detecting and preventing delirium were excluded, although three of the included studies addressed the training of relatives both in the context of prevention and management of delirium [1, 5, 17]. Although we included studies with patients of 18 years or older, the study populations were mostly older people. All study populations had a mean age of at least 50 (range 50.8–75 years), although, where reported, the youngest participant was 22.

Strengths and limitations

The review preparation process from the initial search in October 2020 to the publication date took a long time. However, the search was updated in September 2023 and July 2024 so that this review represents the state of research with regard to intervention studies for relatives of patients with delirium in hospital at this time. The fact that further studies may have been published is a limitation of this review. Also, the restriction to German and English studies could limit the generalizability of this review. However, it should be noted at this point that studies with a main focus on the prevention of delirium were excluded. Originally, the method of evidence synthesis was chosen as part of a scoping review in order to systematically analyse the breadth of available evidence in line with the definition of the JBI Scoping Reviews Methodology Group [26] with regard to pre-intervention studies and intervention studies that have already been conducted and to provide an initial overview. Deviating from this, the intervention studies were reported separately from the intervention preparatory studies at this point and an assessment of the study quality of the identified intervention studies was carried out.

It is standard practice in systematic reviews to use components of the PICO scheme (Population, Intervention, Control group, Outcome) to define the research question and the inclusion criteria [32]. The operationalisation within this context at the beginning within a scoping review were based on the PCC scheme (Population, Context, Concept) which should enable the identification of a very broad body of evidence. However, the included studies were very carefully identified and the selection and data extraction was carried out by two persons, so that the results in combination with a very comprehensive search strategy in six databases represent a sensitive literature search and this in line with standards of good practice of systematic reviews [32].

Recommendations for clinical practice

The findings of this review provide some relevant aspects for geriatric care in hospitals. Importantly, clinicians need to be aware of the importance of involving relatives in the prevention and management of delirium. In high-risk groups relatives should receive information materials as e.g. flyers or brochures that can be used in addition to verbal communication. Also standardised educational sessions for relatives could be considered. In particular, the results show that nurses require specific training regarding how to convey information about delirium and about the required content of information. In addition, specific skills are necessary, for nurses to serve as mentors for family members. Nurses need to possess a number of different skills especially concerning communication and counselling to individually address different phenomena and types of behaviour in the context of delirium.

Recommendations for future research

Accordingly, relatives as a stakeholder that use and benefit from the intervention should also be involved at an early stage in the development of complex interventions [27]. One important aim of the review was to inform the development of future complex interventions on psychosocial support for relatives of patients with delirium. Unfortunately, criteria for the development of complex interventions as addressed by the MRC framework were only partially fulfilled by included studies. In addition, studies should transparently report intervention components and their intended interactions as well as the consideration of contextual factors in intervention development. Importantly, the intervention materials should be made available or at least clearly described to allow for interpretation and replication of the intervention.

Only 2 studies were grounded on a theory of appropriate scope. This shows that at the beginning of the development process a programme theory should be articulated that points to the causal pathways between content of the intervention, intermediate outcomes and long-term goals and how these interact with contextual factors. Also internal and external factors and barriers that have had an influence on the intervention should be described within a theory-based process evaluation [27] while in the included studies insights into factors that promote or inhibit the provision of the interventions could only sporadically be detected. This also include the exploration of costs within the framework of an economic evaluation study [27].

Surprisingly, none of the included studies considered psychosocial support components, relaxation training or psychotherapeutic approaches as intervention components. This provides a starting point for the further development of existing or the development of new complex interventions, as the use of such intervention components appears promising based on the burden on the relatives.

In some cases, the included studies were pilot studies, which suggests that the results should be interpreted with caution. Future studies should preferably choose study designs allowing for valid causal inferences i.e. methodologically strong, high-quality RCTs, possibly conducted as Cluster-RCT on ward level. In contrast, the majority of the included intervention studies were quasi-experimental studies, frequently with methodological weakness that do not allow to make valid conclusions about the effectiveness of the interventions assessed. Also, the two RCTs had important methodological weakness. In particular, since individual studies were conducted with the main objective of feasibility and acceptance.

Standardized outcome assessments applied validated instruments should be used to assess outcomes both for patients and relatives. In the included studies effects were assessed based on the outcomes knowledge about delirium, anxiety, psychological distress and relatives’ burden as well as relatives mood. For some studies, standardised and established instruments were used. However, the applied outcome measures and instruments are still very heterogeneous. In future studies, this should be standardised in order to be able to better compare and synthesize results between the studies.

Conclusion

We detected only a limited number of studies, with methodological shortcomings. The included studies were mostly carried out in palliative care and intensive care settings. The provision of knowledge was the main component of the included studies. Results emphasise the need for more high-quality studies and better description of intervention components and consideration of criteria for the development and evaluation of complex interventions. Although interventions were frequently shown to increase relatives’ knowledge about delirium, these results can only be considered intermediate outcomes and the interventions should be further investigated in robust RCTs with patient- and relative-related outcome measures such as anxiety, psychological stress, relative burden and mood. There is also a need for studies with methodologically well developed and clearly defined intervention components.
