Association Between Micronutrients and Cognitive Performance Among Adolescents and Young Adults Living With Sickle Cell Disease in Kumasi, Ghana: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Albert Dennis Kegya, Mary Amoako, Vivian Paintsil, Kwame Nana Ayisi‐Boateng, Clement Kubreziga Kubuga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimCognitive deficits are commonly reported in patients with sickle cell disease (SCD). Dietary micronutrient intake is a potential modifiable risk factor whose relationship with cognition in SCD patients remains poorly characterised. This study aimed to determine the association between dietary micronutrient intake and cognition among these cohorts.MethodsAmong 128 participants were 64 stable SCD patients (Group‐1) and 64 non‐SCD community controls (Group‐2), aged 13–24 years. A comparative cross‐sectional study was conducted. Recruitment occurred in the same community catchment area served by the KNUST University Hospital SCD clinic. A 24‐h dietary recall and the digit span test (DST) were used for dietary intake and cognition assessment, respectively. Mann–Whitney U test was used for group comparison and Spearman correlation with multiple linear regression for the associations between micronutrients and cognitive score, adjusting for confounding variables. Multiple comparisons were corrected with Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR).ResultsFemales constituted 73.4% of the SCD and 68.8% of the control group. The respective mean ages of Group‐1 and Group‐2 were 19.7 (±1.5) and 20.1 (±1.6) years (p = 0.249). Group‐1 had a lower mean haemoglobin (9.35g/dL) than Group‐2 (12.37g/dL). There was no significant between‐group difference observed in dietary micronutrient intake after FDR correction. The total digit span score was unexpectedly higher in Group‐1 (median 15.5) than Group‐2 (median 13.5; p = 0.006); genotype‐stratified analysis revealed that this was driven by HbSS participants (β = +2.641 vs. HbAA; p = 0.001) and offset by HbSC participants (β = −2.983 vs. HbAA; p = 0.001). In the fully adjusted model, niacin showed a borderline positive association with total digit span (β = +0.791 per doubling of intake; 95% CI: −0.002 to +1.584; p = 0.053), which was robust to confounder adjustment. No association was statistically significant after FDR correction.ConclusionThere was no dietary micronutrient independently associated with cognitive performance after adjustment for multiple comparisons. However, niacin and selenium exhibited biologically plausible trends that warrant further investigation.Trial RegistrationThe authors have confirmed clinical trial registration is not needed for this submission.

Introduction

Sickle cell disease (SCD), as a genetic blood disorder, is one of the neglected chronic diseases, caused by a mutation in position six of the beta‐globin chain [1]. Specifically, the amino acid glutamate is replaced by valine, resulting in distorted red blood cells taking the form of a sickle shape [1]. As a result of red blood cell friction caused by the amino acid replacement, polymerisation and ultimately haemolysis occur. This reduces the red blood cells' average lifespan from 120 days to 15–30 days, which causes anaemia [2].

Areas with high malaria prevalence and population movement show a high distribution of sickle cell traits, which may protect against Plasmodium falciparum infections [3]. Sub‐Saharan Africa, India, the Middle East and parts of the Mediterranean are mostly affected by the disease [2]. In North America and Western Europe, the spread of the disease may be due to population movements, which include the slave trade [4]. About 80% of all SCD cases occur in the sub‐Saharan Africa region, while in Ghana, it is estimated that approximately 1.8% of neonates born per year are affected [5, 6]. The HbSC genotype in West Africa is prevalent [3]. Policy implementation and other programmes, such as national screening of SCD, remain hectic and very challenging for African countries [7, 8].

Cognitive deficits, including impairments in working memory, processing speed and executive function, are reported among SCD patients [9]. These deficits are caused by mechanisms including silent cerebral infarction, chronic anaemia, hypoxia and neuroinflammation. There are also environmental and nutritional factors, such as diet quality, energy expenditure and lifestyle, that can also affect cognitive performance [10]. Jirout et al. noted that environmental and dietary factors can independently influence cognitive capacity, while Peterson et al. reported that silent infarction negatively impacts cognition, particularly working memory, in children with SCD aged 5–21, affecting their performance in subjects like mathematics [9].

In sub‐Saharan Africa, for instance, the empirical or substantial evidence regarding the micronutrient and cognition relationship is scarce despite its biological plausibility in SCD. Many are the micronutrients, including niacin, polyunsaturated fatty acids, vitamin B12, folate, selenium, zinc and iron, that are reportedly known to influence the development and function of the brain, which is crucial for all stages of life [11]. There may be compound existing cognitive vulnerabilities among SCD patients due to the chronic disease‐related depletion of micronutrients. This study aimed to assess dietary micronutrient intake among SCD patients and compare it with that of non‐SCD community controls, to investigate the association between individual micronutrient intakes and cognitive performance.1

Methods

This comparative cross‐sectional study was conducted among SCD patients who attended the SCD clinic at the University Hospital, KNUST, between June 1 and October 14, 2023. The University Hospital serves approximately 200,000 people annually, primarily the KNUST community, including staff and students, as well as nearby areas such as Ayigya, Bomso, Ayeduase and the rest [12]. The hospital is located in Kumasi, Ashanti Region of Ghana, on the northwest of Kumasi‐Accra Road [13]. Participants were divided into two groups: Group‐1, consisting of stable individuals aged 13–24 with SCD genotype (HbSS or HbSC) and no known neurological disorders. Group‐2 comprised non‐SCD community participants without known haemoglobinopathy, confirmed HbAA at enrolment, matched approximately by age and residing in the same community catchment area as Group‐1. By purposive sampling technique, participants of both groups were recruited. Group‐1 was recruited from the SCD clinic register at KNUST University Hospital and Group‐2 from the same residential community catchment area served by that clinic, thereby ensuring that both groups shared socioeconomic and geographical background. All participants at the time of enrolment were not hospitalised, had no acute illness or disease crisis, and no clinical manifestation of haemoglobinopathy. While both groups were predominantly tertiary‐level students, the community‐based recruitment of controls mitigates, though without elimination, concerns about socioeconomic comparability. Females who were pregnant or menstruating were excluded from the recruitment.

The estimated sample size was calculated using z‐tests and independent Pearson's R with G‐Power software (version 3.1.9.4). An alpha level of 0.05, power of 0.95 and an effect size of 0.6 led to a required sample of 64 participants per group, totalling 128 participants. In the study, genotype data for Group‐1 participants were obtained from the medical records at the KNUST University Hospital SCD clinic. These genotypes were initially determined through a routine diagnostic method, specifically alkaline haemoglobin electrophoresis. However, these records were not independently verified for this study. In contrast, for Group‐2 participants, the haemoglobin genotype status was confirmed at the time of enrolment using a validated rapid genotyping test (Golden Care Sickle Cell Test Kit, Tema, Ghana). All control participants tested negative for the HbS allele, indicating an HbAA genotype.

A structured interview‐based questionnaire was employed to collect data on the socio‐demographic and economic status, including age, gender, educational level, parents’ marital status, occupation, ethnicity and religion through closed‐ended questions. The household food security was assessed using validated 6‐Item Food Security Scale, revised in 2012 by the National Centre for Health Statistics. Households were categorised as: high or marginal (Score 0–1), low (2–4), or very low (5–6) food security [14].

Participants' weight was measured in kilograms (kg) using a calibrated Jactermac RGZ‐160 weighing scale, accurate to 0.1 kg. Height was measured in centimetres (cm) with a Jactermac RGZ‐160 stadiometer. To estimate participants’ nutritional status, WHO AnthroPlus 1.0.4 was used to estimate BM‐for‐age for participants under 20 years and body mass index was calculated for those at 20 years and above.

Approximately 2 mL of venous blood was collected for complete blood count analysis (SYSMEX XN‐31 analyser). Blood pressure and pulse were measured with an Omron M2 sphygmomanometer, while peripheral oxygen saturation was assessed with an Omron CMS50N pulse oximeter. A three non‐consecutive 24‐h dietary recalls were conducted per participant over the recruitment period, rather than at every clinic visit, in order to better capture habitual intake variability with household measures. The dietary data were converted to grams using the West Africa Food Composition Tables. Dietary diversity was evaluated using a 9‐food‐group scale per FAO guidelines; a dietary diversity score (DDS) ≥ 4 was considered adequate [15].

Participants' cognitive function was evaluated using forward and backward digit span tests (DST) as outlined in the SOF Visit 9 Operations Manual. In the forward DST, participants repeated sequences of numbers (up to 8 digits, with 9 unique digits) [16]. The Forward DST involved repeating number sequences of up to 8 digits; backward DST required repeating sequences in reverse order (up to 7 digits). The total raw score was the sum of both tests. Raw total scores were standardised to a Z‐score and converted to an IQ‐equivalent using the formula: IQ = 100 + ([x−μ] / SD) × 15, where x is the participant's total score and μ and SD are the sample mean and standard deviation, respectively. IQ‐equivalent scores were then categorised as: above average (≥ 110), average (90–109), or below average (≤ 89) per the Stanford–Binet Intelligence Scales classification [17].

The data were entered into Microsoft Office Professional Plus 2019 workbook, and food analysis template was used to analyse food compositions and nutrients, then exported to IBM SPSS Statistics 20 and R (version 4.5.1) for further analysis and validation. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess the normality of the continuous variables and the Mann–Whitney U tests were used for all between‐groups comparisons of the dietary data and the results are reported as median (IQR). Categorical variables were compared using Pearson chi‐square tests. The 16 micronutrients were all log2 transformed and subjected to variance inflation factor (VIF) analysis to check for multicollinearity (all nutrients VIF < 3), indicating that the results are statistically valid and unaffected by multicollinearity. Using Spearman correlation and multiple logistic regression models adjusted for confounding variables including age, demographic characteristics, health‐related factors and genotype, the association between micronutrient intake and cognitive performance was measured. The overall model fit (R 2) and incremental variance explained (ΔR 2) was valid, indicating that the model appropriately adjusted for the confounding effects and was also not affected by multicollinearity.

The study conducted was subjected to ethical approval from the Committee on Human Research, Publications and Ethics (CHRPE) of the Kwame Nkrumah University of Science and Technology, School of Medical Sciences with approval number: CHRPE/AP/254/23. Written consent was obtained from all individuals who took part in the study. This manuscript was prepared in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) statement for cross‐sectional studies (von Elm et al. 2007).

Genotype‐stratified analysis in the regression model revealed that this counterintuitive group‐level finding was driven by opposing genotype effects: HbSS participants scored 2.641 points higher than HbAA controls (95% CI: 1.10–4.18; p = 0.001), while HbSC participants scored 2.983 points lower (95% CI: −4.62 to −1.35; p = 0.001). The overall Group‐1 advantage is therefore attributable specifically to the HbSS subgroup (67% of Group‐1), consistent with a healthy survivor bias among clinic‐attending HbSS patients, and should not be interpreted as a true cognitive advantage of SCD at the population level.

Fully adjusted regression results are presented in Table 5. The confounder‐only model explained 45.1% of variance in total digit span (R 2 = 0.451, R 2 adj = 0.399); adding all 16 nutrients yielded ΔR 2 = 0.073 (full model R 2 = 0.523), indicating that confounders (particularly gender and genotype) account for the substantial majority of explainable variance. Among confounders, female gender was the strongest independent predictor (β = −2.284; 95% CI: −3.46 to −1.11; p < 0.001). Genotype was highly significant: HbSS versus HbAA (β = +2.641; p = 0.001) and HbSC vs HbAA (β = −2.983; p = 0.001). Haemoglobin showed a borderline negative association (β = −0.306; p = 0.065).

Strongest and most stable association across base and fully adjusted models.

Among the 16 nutrients, niacin showed the most robust association with total digit span in the fully adjusted model (β = +0.791 per doubling of intake; 95% CI: −0.002 to +1.584; p = 0.053; BH‐FDR p = 0.636). This effect was virtually unchanged from the base model (β = +0.955), indicating stability across model specifications and absence of confounding by the additional covariates. Selenium showed a positive trend (β = +0.507; 95% CI: −0.217 to +1.230; p = 0.172; ρ = 0.151), which attenuated from the base model (β = +0.677), suggesting partial confounding by haemoglobin or physical activity. Importantly, phosphorus showed no meaningful association with total digit span in the fully adjusted model (β = −0.547; 95% CI: −1.617 to +0.523; p = 0.319; ρ = −0.024). PUFA (β = −0.290; p = 0.458), SFA (β = −0.371; p = 0.236) and MUFA (β = −0.372; p = 0.273) were similarly non‐significant. No micronutrient association survived BH‐FDR correction across all 16 tests.

Discussion

This cross‐sectional study investigated the association between dietary nutrient intake and cognitive function in 64 adolescents and young adults with SCD and 64 non‐SCD community controls with confirmed HbAA genotype in Kumasi, Ghana. The principal finding is that no dietary nutrient was independently associated with cognitive performance after full confounder adjustment and BH‐FDR correction across 16 simultaneous tests. Niacin showed the most robust biologically plausible trend, stable across model specifications and warrants prospective investigation.

The higher total digit span score observed at the group level in Group‐1 is counterintuitive, given the established literature on cognitive impairment in SCD [20, 21]. Genotype‐stratified analysis provides a mechanistic explanation: the group advantage is driven entirely by HbSS participants (β = + 2.641 vs. HbAA; p = 0.001), while HbSC participants score significantly lower (β = −2.983 vs. HbAA; p = 0.001). The HbSS clinic population likely represents a healthy survivor subgroup, medically stable, adherent to care and health‐engaged, whereas HbSC disease, although clinically milder, may involve distinct neuropsychological risk profiles related to higher blood viscosity and different neurological sequelae [20]. These opposing genotype effects highlight the importance of disaggregating SCD subtypes in cognitive research, and the overall group‐level finding should not be interpreted as evidence of a cognitive advantage conferred by SCD.

The haematological profile of Group‐1 confirmed the clinical characteristics of chronic haemolytic anaemia: significantly reduced haemoglobin, haematocrit, MCV and RBC, alongside elevated WBC and platelet counts reflecting high‐risk susceptibility to infection and inflammation [18, 19]. The borderline negative association between haemoglobin level and total digit span (β = −0.306; p = 0.065) in the regression model likely captures within‐genotype variation in disease severity rather than a direct haematological effect on cognition.

The dietary micronutrient did not differ significantly between groups after BH‐FDR correction, indicating broadly comparable dietary intake in this predominantly tertiary‐educated, urban cohort residing in the same community catchment area. SFA was nominally higher in Group‐1 (p = 0.038 unadjusted), consistent with the higher total fat intake previously reported in this group [22], but did not survive multiple testing correction. The DDSs were adequate in both groups, reflecting access to varied food in the university hospital community environment.

Among all 16 nutrients tested, niacin showed a borderline positive association with total digit span that was stable across base and fully adjusted models (β = +0.791, p = 0.053). The stability of this effect, unchanged by adjustment for gender, haemoglobin, physical activity, education, genotype and other nutrients, is a meaningful indicator of robustness. Niacin (vitamin B3) is a precursor to nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+), which is essential for neuronal energy metabolism, mitochondrial function and DNA repair [23]. Its deficiency is associated with cognitive impairment in multiple populations, and supplementation improves learning and memory in animal models. While the current finding did not survive BH‐FDR correction, a reflection of the study's limited statistical power with 64 participants per group rather than evidence of a null effect, it represents the most scientifically grounded lead in this dataset.

Selenium showed a positive trend (β = +0.507; p = 0.172; ρ = 0.151) that attenuated in the fully adjusted model, suggesting partial confounding by haemoglobin level or physical activity. Selenium is an antioxidant micronutrient essential for neurological function through selenoproteins involved in redox regulation [24, 25]. In SCD patients, in whom oxidative stress is already elevated due to chronic haemolysis, selenium status may influence cognitive outcomes through a complex dose–response relationship. Its narrow therapeutic window, neuroprotective at physiological levels but neurotoxic at excess, warrants biochemical assessment of selenium status in future studies.

The fully adjusted analysis of the data reveals no association existing between cognitive performance and phosphorus (β = −0.547; p = 0.319) as well as PUFA (β = −0.290; p = 0.458) after confounder adjustment on gender, genotype, haemoglobin, physical activity and other confounders accounted for. These null findings do not exclude a physiological role for phosphorus or PUFA in cognition, as previous studies have reported their association with cognitive performance in other populations, but they do not support a direct dietary effect in this study cohort [22, 26, 27, 28].

Gender was the strongest independent predictor of total digit span (β = −2.284; p < 0.001), with males scoring approximately 2.3 points higher than females after full adjustment. This gender difference in working memory performance is well documented in adolescents and young adults [29]. Its magnitude in this sample shows the critical importance of including gender as a covariate in all analyses of cognitive function in this age group. Socioeconomic status, while not comprehensively assessed, was partially controlled by the community‐matched design and the educational homogeneity (> 95% tertiary level) of the sample.

This study applied formal BH‐FDR correction across 16 simultaneous nutrient‐cognition associations in a paediatric and young adult SCD cohort in, Ghana, West Africa and one of the few to use community‐matched, HbAA‐confirmed controls. This methodological rigour, combined with a fully adjusted regression model incorporating nine clinically relevant confounders, represents the study's principal contribution to a sparse evidence base.

There were some limitations to be considered, which include: the use of 24‐h dietary recall method, although conducted on three non‐consecutive days, is subject to recall bias and may not capture habitual intake. Whereas recruitment of participants into both groups from the same community was purposively sampled, strengthening their comparability, the tertiary‐educated cohort predominating in both groups limits generalisability to SCD populations in settings with lower resources, non‐academia or rural levels. Also, biochemical micronutrient status (serum ferritin, transferrin saturation and plasma selenium) was not assessed, and dietary intake may not fully reflect tissue‐level nutritional status, particularly in SCD patients with altered nutrient metabolism. Again, the SCD genotype was based on historical medical records for Group‐1 and was not independently reconfirmed; the impact of HbSS versus HbSC heterogeneity on cognitive outcomes merits dedicated investigation. Lastly, with 64 participants per group and 16 nutrients tested simultaneously, the study was insufficiently powered to reliably detect associations of modest effect size after BH‐FDR correction; the absence of FDR‐corrected significant findings should be interpreted as consistent with insufficient power rather than definitive evidence of no association. Furthermore, the predominantly tertiary‐educated composition of both groups from a single academic community limits generalisability to broader or lower‐resource SCD populations.

This study aimed to explore the association between micronutrients and cognitive performance in adolescents and young adults living with SCD, with a community control. After rigorous confounder adjustment and BH‐FDR correction, no independent micronutrients were found to be associated with cognitive performance. However, niacin showed a biologically plausible confounder‐stable trend associating higher intake with better working memory performance and thus warrants further investigation. Opposing HbSS and HbSC genotype effects explain the counterintuitive group‐level cognitive pattern and underscore the need to disaggregate SCD subtypes in future studies. These findings suggest dietary micronutrient intake, particularly niacin, as a candidate modifiable factor in SCD‐related cognitive outcomes, and establish a methodological template for rigorous nutrition‐cognition research in this population in sub‐Saharan Africa.
