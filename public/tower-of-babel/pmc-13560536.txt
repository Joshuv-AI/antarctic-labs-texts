Regarding emergency medical services activation after epinephrine for anaphylaxis☆
Authors: Sami L. Bahna
Published: 2026

The position paper, World Allergy Organization Anaphylaxis Guidance 2020,1 addressed various aspects of anaphylaxis, including acute treatment but did not provide clear guidance on when to call the Emergency Medical Services (EMS). Globally, for decades, the traditional recommendation for managing anaphylaxis has been to administer epinephrine promptly, then call EMS. Whether EMS should be activated routinely has been debated, with some considering it necessary for safe management of anaphylaxis and others viewing universal activation as unnecessary use of emergency resources when symptoms respond promptly to epinephrine. This question has been addressed by the AAAAI/ACAAI Joint Task Force on Practice Parameters in the 2023 updated anaphylaxis practice parameter.2 The updated recommendation has raised questions and concerns about its interpretation and application by healthcare professionals and patients.

Anaphylaxis is an unpredictable, life-threatening allergic reaction with distinctive characteristics. It can occur suddenly at any age and in any setting, progress quickly, and be caused by almost any trigger and through any route of exposure (Table 1). There is no question that the first-line treatment is prompt epinephrine administration, which rapidly ameliorates symptoms and signs and mitigates the risk of death. Epinephrine has been available worldwide in multiple self-administered forms; all are highly effective when promptly and appropriately administered.3 If needed, epinephrine can be administered every 5 min while watching for its effect and potential adverse effects. The prevailing recommendation has been prompt epinephrine administration followed by calling EMS or rushing the patient to the nearest hospital department (ED) where appropriate services are available and hospital admission can be quickly arranged if needed (Table 2). On occasion, hospitalization may even lead to the identification of the trigger for anaphylaxis. An infant with cow milk allergy was brought to the ED with anaphylaxis despite strict avoidance of milk products.5 He was successfully treated and then hospitalized out of concern about the development of a biphasic reaction. After an uneventful overnight stay, he was discharged, but just before leaving, anaphylaxis occurred immediately after the mother applied a diaper ointment – as had happened on the previous day. Definitive evidence of the advantage of calling EMS or rapid transfer to the ED would require prospective, well-controlled studies, which would be impractical and unethical. In the absence of controlled clinical studies, data must be obtained from the so-called “experiment of nature”. In real-world practice, many patients with anaphylaxis do not follow the advice to call EMS or go to the ED, and most episodes resolve after 1 epinephrine dose, improve after taking an oral antihistamine, or even resolve spontaneously. Furthermore, during the COVID-19 pandemic, many patients with anaphylaxis avoided going to crowded EDs. In a meta-analysis of 86 reports on anaphylaxis (36,557 total episodes), it was noted that in 92.3% of episodes only 1 epinephrine dose was administered.6 However, such a high figure is probably inflated by several factors. Some patients receive epinephrine without anaphylaxis or with just mild symptoms. Most epinephrine prescribers encourage patients to administer epinephrine promptly, arguing that overzealous use is preferable to delayed administration. When an allergic reaction appears as “possible anaphylaxis", it is generally better to err on the side of caution and administer epinephrine.7 In a recent study,8 the medical records of 1127 children (<18 years) who arrived in the ED after receiving epinephrine for food-related anaphylaxis were reviewed. Pre-ED epinephrine was administered once in 84%, twice in 12%, and 3 or more times in 3% of patients. In the ED, 19.4% received additional epinephrine, ie, a total of 34.4% required 2 or more doses – pointing to the advantage of transfer to ED.

In the “Anaphylaxis – A 2023 Practice Parameter Update,“2 the recommendation relevant to anaphylaxis management states: “We suggest that clinicians counsel patients that immediate activation of EMS may not be required if the patient experiences prompt, complete, and durable response to treatment with epinephrine, provided that additional epinephrine and medical care are readily available, if needed. We suggest that clinicians counsel patients to always activate EMS after epinephrine use if anaphylaxis is severe, fails to resolve promptly, fails to resolve completely or nearly completely, or returns or worsens after a first dose of epinephrine.” The recommendation was “Conditional” and with “Very low certainty of evidence”.

Although the Practice Parameter Update and the International Consensus Report provided similar recommendations, some ambiguity was present relevant to EMS activation or transfer to the ED. Placing responsibility for interpretation and application of these recommendations on the patient, caregivers, or whoever is expected to manage the anaphylaxis episode at that time may not be in the patient's best interest. The decision-maker might be a teenage parent, a nurse, a paramedic, a school teacher, a restaurant waiter, or a flight attendant. The decision can also be markedly influenced by the patient's and caretaker's location, circumstances, and comfort and competence in handling the situation. Also, given the risk of rapid progress of anaphylaxis, a shared decision-making may not be in the patient's best interest and might create liability for the physician and/or the caretaker. In a report on 123 episodes of anaphylaxis in 117 children, anaphylaxis occurred at home in 48% of cases.10 In another report of 41 cases occurring on commercial flights,11 21 patients received some treatment while on the plane and 12 after landing. Epinephrine was administered to only 6 on the plane and 2 on the ground.

Recommending waiting for the response to epinephrine before activating EMS may carry substantial risk, including death. Some patients require more than 2 epinephrine doses plus additional supportive therapies in the ED, and some require hospitalization. Media reports have described some striking cases of fatalities despite multiple epinephrine doses. A sesame-allergic 15-year-old girl who purchased a sandwich containing artichoke, olives and tapenade at Heathrow airport.12 She began eating it during the flight to France, but she collapsed and died despite 2 epinephrine doses administered by her accompanying father. Unfortunately, an early transfer to the ED was not feasible. Another example was a peanut-allergic 13-year-old girl who developed fatal anaphylaxis at a campsite near Sacramento, California.13 She died despite 3 epinephrine doses administered by her accompanying father, who is a physician. Multiple epinephrine doses alone may not be adequate treatment for anaphylaxis; additional supportive measures, eg, oxygen, IV fluids, and vasopressors, which are available in the ED but not in the community setting, may be required.

In conclusion, it would be prudent in certain acute allergic reactions, to recommend immediate EMS activation or transport to the nearest hospital ED after epinephrine administration, without waiting for the response. Resolution of symptoms by the time of arrival may be preferable to waiting and observing, particularly in the situations listed in Table 3. In selected cases, waiting to observe the response to epinephrine may be reasonable as part of shared decision-making.
