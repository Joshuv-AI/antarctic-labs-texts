Quantum fluctuations can enhance or reduce positional uncertainty at finite temperature
Harukuni Ikeda

Abstract
The uncertainty principle guarantees a non-zero value for the positional uncertainty, ⟨ Δ ​ x 2 ⟩ > 0 \left\langle\Delta x^{2}\right\rangle>0 , even without thermal fluctuations. This implies that quantum fluctuations inherently enhance positional uncertainty at zero temperature. A natural question then arises: what happens at finite temperatures, where the interplay between quantum and thermal fluctuations may give rise to complex and intriguing behaviors? To address this question, we systematically investigate the positional uncertainty, ⟨ Δ ​ x 2 ⟩ \left\langle\Delta x^{2}\right\rangle , of a particle in equilibrium confined within a nonlinear potential of the form V ⁡ ( x ) ∝ x n V(x)\propto x^{n} , where n = 2 , 4 , 6 , … n=2,4,6,\dots represents an even exponent. Using path integral Monte Carlo simulations, we calculate ⟨ Δ ​ x 2 ⟩ \left\langle\Delta x^{2}\right\rangle in equilibrium as a function of the thermal de Broglie wavelength Λ \Lambda . Interestingly, for large values of n n , ⟨ Δ ​ x 2 ⟩ \left\langle\Delta x^{2}\right\rangle exhibits a non-monotonic dependence on Λ \Lambda : it initially decreases with increasing Λ \Lambda at small Λ \Lambda but increases at larger Λ \Lambda . To further understand this behavior, we employ a semiclassical approximation, which reveals that quantum fluctuations can reduce positional uncertainty for small Λ \Lambda when the nonlinearity of the potential is sufficiently strong. Finally, we discuss the potential implications of this result for many-body phenomena driven by strong nonlinear interactions, such as glass transitions, where the transition densities exhibit a similar non-monotonic dependence on Λ \Lambda .

Introduction

In a classical system, the position is a deterministic variable in the
absence of thermal fluctuations,
i.e.
, the positional uncertainty
is zero,
\left\langle\Delta x^{2}\right\rangle\equiv\left\langle x^{2}\right\rangle-\left\langle x\right\rangle^{2}=0
. In contrast,
for a quantum system, the uncertainty principle ensures
\left\langle\Delta x^{2}\right\rangle>0

[
1
]
, indicating that quantum
fluctuations inherently increase positional uncertainty. What happens at
finite temperatures in equilibrium?
This study systematically
investigates the effects of quantum fluctuations on
\left\langle\Delta x^{2}\right\rangle

in equilibrium using both numerical and theoretical approaches. To
measure the relative strength of quantum fluctuations, we use the
thermal de Broglie wavelength in units of the linear size of the system
L
:

\displaystyle\Lambda=\sqrt{\frac{\beta\hbar^{2}}{mL^{2}}},

(1)

where
\hbar
denotes the reduced Planck constant,
\beta
denotes the
inverse temperature,
m
denotes the mass of the particle, and
L

denotes the linear size of the system.
In this work, we investigate how the system behaves
when
\Lambda
is systematically varied by changing
m
or
\hbar
,
while keeping
\beta
fixed.

A naive intuition suggests that, at a fixed temperature,
\left\langle\Delta x^{2}\right\rangle
should increase with increasing
\Lambda
, owing to tunneling
effects and zero-point energy
[
1
]
. This intuition
holds true in the simplest case: a particle confined in a harmonic
potential,
V(x)\propto x^{2}
, where
\left\langle\Delta x^{2}\right\rangle
increases
monotonically as
\Lambda

increases
[
2
]
. However, this behavior does
not generalize to all systems, as seen in another analytically solvable
example: a particle confined between hard walls. For a classical
particle at finite temperature, the positional distribution is uniform
between the walls. In contrast, for finite
\Lambda
, quantum effects
become significant. The boundary conditions and the continuity of the
wave function suppress the probability of finding the particle near the
walls. As a result,
\left\langle\Delta x^{2}\right\rangle
becomes smaller than its
classical counterpart (
\Lambda=0
), demonstrating that quantum
fluctuations can suppress positional uncertainty.

The observation that quantum fluctuations can suppress positional
uncertainty is rather counterintuitive. Interestingly, similar behaviors
have been reported in many-body quantum systems. For example, quantum
fluctuations often reduce the uncertainty in the rotational degrees of
freedom of hydrogen molecules in solid phases, which stabilizes the
so-called phase II (the partially frozen
phase)
[
3
,
4
,
5
]
.
Related phenomena have also been observed in
crystallization
[
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
]
,
glass
transitions
[
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
,
18
,
19
]
,
and spin-glass
transitions
[
20
,
21
,
22
,
23
]
, where
quantum fluctuations reduce the transition densities. An intuitive
explanation for these phenomena is that quantum fluctuations effectively
increase the particles’ radii, thereby reducing the accessible volume
and lowering the transition
density
[
12
,
13
]
. This situation is analogous to
the case of a single particle confined between hard walls, where quantum
fluctuations, as described earlier, prevent the particle from occupying
regions near the walls. This effectively reduces the accessible volume.

An important and natural question is: under what conditions do quantum
fluctuations suppress positional uncertainty? To address this question,
we systematically study a particle confined in a nonlinear
potential,
V(x)\propto x^{n}
, where
n=2,4,\dots
represents an
even number. By varying
n
, we can systematically change the functional
form from harmonic (
n=2
) to hard walls (
n\to\infty
).

We investigate the model for
2<n<\infty
using path-integral Monte
Carlo simulations
[
24
,
25
,
26
,
27
]
. For weakly nonlinear potentials (
i.e.
, small
n
),

\left\langle\Delta x^{2}\right\rangle
increases monotonically with increasing
\Lambda
,
similar to the behavior observed in the harmonic potential
case. Interestingly, for large
n
,
\left\langle\Delta x^{2}\right\rangle
decreases from
its classical value at moderate
\Lambda
, indicating that quantum
fluctuations suppress the positional uncertainty. This effect becomes
most pronounced in the hard-wall limit (
n\to\infty
), where

\left\langle\Delta x^{2}\right\rangle
decreases monotonically with
\Lambda
. To further
understand this behavior, we perform a semi-classical
calculation
[
24
,
28
]
to determine the
conditions under which quantum fluctuations suppress
\left\langle\Delta x^{2}\right\rangle
. The semi-classical analysis predicts that
\left\langle\Delta x^{2}\right\rangle

increases for
n=2,4
and decreases for
n>4
at small
\Lambda
,
consistent with the numerical results. These findings demonstrate that
quantum fluctuations can generally suppress positional uncertainty at
small
\Lambda
when the potential is strongly nonlinear.

The structure of the paper is as follows. In Sec.
2
,
we introduce the model. In Sec.
3
, we briefly review
two analytically solvable cases: the harmonic potential and hard
walls. In Sec.
4
, we present the numerical results
obtained through path-integral Monte Carlo simulations. In
Sec.
5
, we provide analytical insights derived from
the semi-classical calculation. Finally, Sec.
6
is
dedicated to the summary and discussions, highlighting some potential
implications of our findings for studies of glass transitions.

2
Settings

In this section, we introduce the model and
define key physical quantities.

2.1
Model

We consider a particle in one dimension confined within a potential

V(x)
. The Hamiltonian of the system is given by:

\displaystyle H=\frac{p^{2}}{2m}+V(x),

(2)

where the potential
V(x)
is defined as:

\displaystyle V(x)=k\left(\frac{x}{L}\right)^{n},

(3)

with
n=2,4,6,\dots
representing an even number. The position
x

and momentum
p
satisfy the canonical commutation
relation
[
1
]
:

\displaystyle[x,p]=i\hbar.

(4)

For
n=2
,
V(x)
corresponds to a harmonic potential, while in the
limit
n\to\infty
, it approaches the case of hard walls, as shown in
Fig.
1
.

Figure 1:
Potentials for several
values of
n
. The potential is harmonic when
n=2
, and it becomes
equivalent to hard walls in the limit
n\to\infty
. For simplicity, we
set
k=1
and
L=1
in this figure.

To quantify the positional uncertainty, we observe the variance of
x

in equilibrium:

\displaystyle\left\langle\Delta x^{2}\right\rangle\equiv\frac{1}{Z}\tr\left(e^{-\beta H}x^{2}\right),

\displaystyle Z=\tr e^{-\beta H},

(5)

where
\beta=1/(k_{B}T)
is the inverse temperature, and
k_{B}
is the
Boltzmann constant.

2.2
Nondimensionalization

We introduce dimensionless variables:

\displaystyle\tilde{x}=L^{-1}x,

\displaystyle\tilde{p}=\frac{L}{\hbar}p,

(6)

which satisfy the commutation relation:

\displaystyle[\tilde{x},\tilde{p}]=i.

(7)

With these dimensionless quantities, the partition function can be
rewritten as:

\displaystyle Z=\tr\exp\left[-\Lambda^{2}\frac{\tilde{p}^{2}}{2}-\tilde{\beta}\tilde{V}(\tilde{x})\right],

(8)

where:

\displaystyle\Lambda=\sqrt{\frac{\beta\hbar^{2}}{mL^{2}}},\

\displaystyle\tilde{\beta}=k\beta,\

\displaystyle\tilde{V}(\tilde{x})=\left|\tilde{x}\right|^{n}.

(9)

After nondimensionalization, the system is governed by two control
parameters: the reduced inverse temperature
\tilde{\beta}
and the thermal de
Broglie wavelength
\Lambda

[
2
]
.

3
Solvable Cases

Here we shortly revisit the well known solvable cases.

3.1
Analytical Results for
n=2
(Harmonic Potential)

The model can be solved analytically for
n=2
, where
V(x)
is a
harmonic potential. In this case, the equilibrium distribution of
\tilde{x}

follows a Gaussian
distribution
[
2
,
29
]
:

\displaystyle\rho(\tilde{x})=\frac{1}{Z}\braket{\tilde{x}|e^{-\beta H}|\tilde{x}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\left\langle\Delta\tilde{x}^{2}\right\rangle}}e^{-\frac{\tilde{x}^{2}}{2\left\langle\Delta\tilde{x}^{2}\right\rangle}},

(10)

where the variance is given by:

\displaystyle\left\langle\Delta\tilde{x}^{2}\right\rangle=\frac{\Lambda}{2\sqrt{2\tilde{\beta}}}\coth\left(\Lambda\sqrt{\frac{\tilde{\beta}}{2}}\right).

(11)

As shown in Fig.
2
(a), the distribution
\rho(\tilde{x})

broadens with increasing
\Lambda
, indicating that quantum fluctuations
enhance the positional uncertainty. This result is expected, as
tunneling effects and zero-point energy weaken the confinement imposed
by the potential.

distributions

, we plot
\left\langle\Delta\tilde{x}^{2}\right\rangle
as the blue

\Lambda
.

3.2
Analytical Results for
n\to\infty
(Hard Walls)

In the limit
n\to\infty
, the model corresponds to a particle confined
between hard walls at
\tilde{x}=\pm 1
. For the classical case
\Lambda=0
,
the equilibrium distribution is flat:

\displaystyle\lim_{\Lambda\to 0}\rho(\tilde{x})=\begin{cases}1/2&\left|\tilde{x}\right|<1,\\
0&\left|\tilde{x}\right|\geq 1,\end{cases}

(12)

which results in a positional uncertainty:

\displaystyle\left\langle\Delta\tilde{x}^{2}\right\rangle_{0}=\frac{1}{3}.

(13)

For
\Lambda>0
, the equilibrium distribution is expressed as:

\displaystyle\rho(\tilde{x})=\frac{1}{Z}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{(n\pi\Lambda)^{2}}{8}}\left|\phi_{n}(\tilde{x})\right|^{2},

\displaystyle Z=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{(n\pi\Lambda)^{2}}{8}},

(14)

where
\phi_{n}(\tilde{x})
denotes the
n
-th eigenfunction:

\displaystyle\phi_{n}(\tilde{x})=\sin\left(n\pi\frac{\tilde{x}-1}{2}\right).

(15)

Due to the boundary conditions
\phi_{n}(\tilde{x}=\pm 1)=0
and the
continuity of the wave function, the eigenfunctions’ amplitudes are
significantly suppressed near the walls,
\left|\phi_{n}(\tilde{x})\right|\ll 1
for

\left|\tilde{x}\right|\approx 1
. This results in a narrower distribution of

\rho(\tilde{x})
compared to the classical case, as shown in
Fig.
2
(b). In the limit
\Lambda\to\infty
, the
distribution converges to that of the ground state:

\displaystyle\lim_{\Lambda\to\infty}\rho(\tilde{x})=\left|\sin\left(\pi\frac{\tilde{x}-1}{2}\right)\right|^{2},

(16)

which results in the positional uncertainty:

\displaystyle\left\langle\Delta\tilde{x}^{2}\right\rangle_{\infty}=\frac{1}{3}-\frac{2}{\pi^{2}}.

(17)

In Fig.
3
, we plot
\left\langle\Delta\tilde{x}^{2}\right\rangle=\int d\tilde{x},\rho(\tilde{x})\tilde{x}^{2}
as the red solid line. The positional uncertainty

\left\langle\Delta\tilde{x}^{2}\right\rangle
monotonically decreases with
\Lambda
from

\left\langle\Delta\tilde{x}^{2}\right\rangle_{0}
to
\left\langle\Delta\tilde{x}^{2}\right\rangle_{\infty}
.

4
Numerical Simulation

For
2<n<\infty
, the model cannot be solved analytically. In this
section, we present the numerical results for these cases.

4.1
Path Integral Formulation

To investigate the model numerically, we reformulate the partition
function
Z
using the path-integral
formalism
[
24
,
25
,
27
,
30
]
:

\displaystyle Z=\lim_{N\to\infty}\left(\frac{N}{2\pi\Lambda^{2}}\right)^{N/2}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{N}\int d\tilde{x}_{i}\right)\exp\left[-\tilde{\beta}\Phi(\tilde{x}_{1},\cdots,\tilde{x}_{N})\right],

(18)

where we introduce the effective potential:

\displaystyle\Phi(\tilde{x}_{1},\cdots,\tilde{x}_{N})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[\frac{N}{2\tilde{\beta}\Lambda^{2}}\left(\tilde{x}_{i+1}-\tilde{x}_{i}\right)^{2}+\frac{\tilde{V}(\tilde{x}_{i})}{N}\right].

(19)

The thermal average of a physical quantity
A(\tilde{x})
can then be
expressed as
[
25
]
:

\displaystyle\left\langle A(\tilde{x})\right\rangle=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\langle A(\tilde{x}_{i})\right\rangle,

(20)

where
\left\langle\bullet\right\rangle
denotes the thermal average over the equilibrium
configurations under the effective potential
\Phi
.

4.2
Details of Numerical Implementation

We sample the equilibrium configurations
{\tilde{x}_{1},\cdots,\tilde{x}_{N}}
using
Monte Carlo (MC) simulations with the effective potential
\Phi
in
Eq. (
19
). A single MC step involves
N
local updates
of
\tilde{x}_{i}\to\tilde{x}_{i}+\delta_{x}
for a randomly chosen
i
, along with a
center-of-mass update
R\to R+\delta_{R}
to accelerate
relaxation. Here,
\delta_{x}
and
\delta_{R}
are small random numbers
with
\delta_{x}\in[-0.05\Lambda,0.05\Lambda]
and
\delta_{R}\in[-0.05,0.05]
. Each update is accepted with probability
\min[1,e^{-\tilde{\beta}\Delta\Phi}]
, where
\Delta\Phi
represents the energy change
caused by the update. In this study, we present results for

N=50
. Simulations with
N=100
were also performed to confirm that the
results are independent of
N
. We equilibrated the system using
10^{7}

MC steps, followed by an additional
10^{7}
steps to compute the thermal
average of
\left\langle\Delta\tilde{x}^{2}\right\rangle
.

5
Semi-classical approximation

We employ the semi-classical approximation to get a physical insight
into the non-monotonic behavior for large
n
and small
\Lambda
.
Notably, the decreases of
\left\langle\Delta\tilde{x}^{2}\right\rangle
occur for small

\Lambda
, allowing us to determine whether
\left\langle\Delta\tilde{x}^{2}\right\rangle

decreases or not by analyzing the sign of the leading-order correction
in the semi-classical
approximation
[
24
,
15
]
.

5.1
Partition function

We begin by decomposing
\tilde{x}_{i}
into a center-of-mass term and fluctuations:

\displaystyle\tilde{x}_{i}=R+u_{i},

(21)

where
R=N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\tilde{x}_{i}
represents the center-of-mass, and

u_{i}
are fluctuations around it. For
\Lambda\ll 1
, the fluctuations
are small,
\left|u_{i}\right|\ll 1
, enabling an expansion of
\tilde{V}(\tilde{x}_{i})

around
R
. The partition function then becomes:

\displaystyle Z

\displaystyle\approx\lim_{N\to\infty}\left(\frac{N}{2\pi\Lambda^{2}}\right)^{N/2}\int dR\prod_{i=1}^{N}\int du_{i}\delta\left(N^{-1}\sum_{i}u_{i}\right)

\displaystyle\times e^{-\frac{N}{2\Lambda^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(u_{i+1}-u_{i})^{2}-\tilde{\beta}\tilde{V}(R)-\frac{\tilde{\beta}\tilde{V}^{\prime\prime}(R)}{2N}\sum_{i}u_{i}^{2}}.

(22)

After integrating out the Gaussian fluctuations
u_{i}
, the partition
function reduces to
[
24
,
28
]

\displaystyle Z\propto\int dR\exp\left[-\tilde{\beta}\tilde{V}_{\rm eff}(R)\right],

(23)

where the effective potential is given by

\displaystyle\tilde{V}_{\rm eff}(R)=\tilde{V}(R)+\frac{\Lambda^{2}}{24}\tilde{V}^{\prime\prime}(R).

(24)

The above equation implies that the distribution of the center-of-mass
can be identified with that of a classical particle confined in the
effective potential Eq. (
24
) at the level of the
semi-classical approximation
[
24
,
28
]
.

5.2
Positional uncertainty

The positional uncertainty can be written as

\displaystyle\left\langle\Delta\tilde{x}^{2}\right\rangle=\left\langle R^{2}\right\rangle+\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\langle u_{i}^{2}\right\rangle.

(25)

The first term in Eq. (
25
) is approximated as

\displaystyle\left\langle R^{2}\right\rangle\approx\frac{\int dRe^{-\tilde{\beta}\tilde{V}_{\rm eff}(R)}R^{2}}{\int dRe^{-\tilde{\beta}\tilde{V}_{\rm eff}(R)}}

\displaystyle\approx\left\langle R^{2}\right\rangle_{0}-\frac{\Lambda^{2}}{24}\left[\left\langle\tilde{V}^{\prime\prime}(R)R^{2}\right\rangle_{0}-\left\langle\tilde{V}^{\prime\prime}(R)\right\rangle_{0}\left\langle R^{2}\right\rangle_{0}\right],

(26)

where
\left\langle\bullet\right\rangle_{0}=\left.\left\langle\bullet\right\rangle\right|_{\Lambda=0}

denotes the classical equilibrium average for
\Lambda=0
:

\displaystyle\left\langle\bullet\right\rangle_{0}=\frac{\int dRe^{-\tilde{\beta}\tilde{V}(R)}\bullet}{\int dRe^{-\tilde{\beta}\tilde{V}(R)}}.

(27)

Since
\left\langle\tilde{V}^{\prime\prime}(R)R^{2}\right\rangle_{0}\geq\left\langle\tilde{V}^{\prime\prime}(R)\right\rangle_{0}\left\langle R^{2}\right\rangle_{0}
, the

O(\Lambda^{2})
order term in Eq. (
26
) always give
negative contribution, which reduces the positional uncertainty

\left\langle\Delta\tilde{x}^{2}\right\rangle
. Note that this term appears only for non-linear
potentials since the
O(\Lambda^{2})
order term in
Eq. (
26
) vanishes for the harmonic potential

{\tilde{V}(R)=R^{2}}
. The second term in Eq. (
25
) is
calculated as
[
24
]

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\langle u_{i}^{2}\right\rangle\approx\frac{\Lambda^{2}}{12},

(28)

which gives a positive contribution and enhances the positional
uncertainty. Due to the competition of the terms in
Eq. (
26
) and (
28
), the least-order
contribution changes the sign at a certain value of
n
, which
determines whether
\left\langle\Delta\tilde{x}^{2}\right\rangle
increase or decrease for small

\Lambda
.

is

, invalidating the semi-classical
approximation. Notably, the semi-classical calculation fails to
reproduce the non-monotonic behavior of
\left\langle\Delta\tilde{x}^{2}\right\rangle
observed
for
n>4
. This is expected, as the semi-classical approximation
considers only the
O(\Lambda^{2})
terms, which can describe only
monotonic increases or decreases in
\left\langle\Delta\tilde{x}^{2}\right\rangle
. Higher-order
corrections are necessary to fully account for the non-monotonic
behavior, which we leave as future work.

6
Summary and Discussions

In summary, we have investigated the effects of quantum fluctuations on
the positional uncertainty
\left\langle\Delta x^{2}\right\rangle
of a particle confined in
a non-linear potential,
V(x)=kx^{n}
. Our results demonstrate that
quantum fluctuations suppress
\left\langle\Delta x^{2}\right\rangle
for sufficiently strong
non-linear potentials and small de Broglie wavelengths
\Lambda
. For
larger
\Lambda
, tunneling effects dominate, leading to an increase in

\left\langle\Delta x^{2}\right\rangle
and resulting in a non-monotonic dependence of

\left\langle\Delta x^{2}\right\rangle
on
\Lambda
.

The phenomena observed in our single-particle model may also be relevant
to many-body quantum systems. For instance, previous studies have
reported that quantum fluctuations enhance
crystallization
[
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
]

and
vitrification
[
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
,
18
,
19
]

for small
\Lambda
. An intuitive explanation for these observations is
that quantum fluctuations effectively increase the particle radius,
thereby reducing the free volume
[
12
,
13
]
. This
mechanism resembles the behavior observed in our model, where quantum
fluctuations reduce
\left\langle\Delta x^{2}\right\rangle
for sufficiently strong
non-linear potentials and small
\Lambda
, indicating stronger
confinement of the particle within a narrower region and a corresponding
reduction in free volume
