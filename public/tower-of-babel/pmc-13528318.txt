Why health AI needs protocols, not just pilots
Authors: Bhav Jain, Aman Sinha, Nicholas Chedid, Kedar Mate
Published: 2026

Health systems are rapidly adopting artificial intelligence (AI), with 2 in 3 physicians now using it for tasks like chart summaries and diagnoses—a 78% increase since 2023.1 Yet most clinical AI tools face limited US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) oversight, leading to inadequate evaluation.2 Over half of 1000 FDA-authorised AI devices reported no performance metrics and nearly half documented no clinical studies.3 Unlike pharmaceuticals, which require randomised trials and rigorous surveillance, AI tools are being deployed without comparable standards.

Unfortunately, most health systems still rely on vendor demos and limited pilots for evaluation, ignoring a critical reality: AI performance depends on the data distribution. Patient populations and workflows vary across organisations, so validation in one setting doesn’t guarantee performance elsewhere. While 53% of healthcare AI algorithms include internal validation, fewer than 1% undergo independent external validation.4 AI tools need the same rigour as other interventions: predefined contexts, metrics, pass-fail criteria, deployment guardrails and continuous monitoring.

The case for preregistration

In addition to established clinical trial procedures in medicine, preregistration is standard practice for technology adoption in safety-critical industries beyond healthcare. For example, manufacturers of autonomous vehicles demonstrate the absence of unreasonable risk through safety cases that define operational design domains (ODDs)—the specific environments and scenarios where the system will function—along with hazard scenarios and validation evidence.5 Aviation requires similar discipline: new systems cannot enter service without formal safety risk management.6

Borrowing from these frameworks, health systems deploying AI should formally define what they’re testing, in whom, under what conditions and by what measures. This means specifying the medical ODD—intended users, clinical contexts and inclusion criteria. It means selecting metrics: clinical utility, completeness, readability, equity across subgroups and safety signals like protected health information leakage. It also means defining appropriate evaluation data, wherein privacy and regulatory requirements may necessitate augmenting a local corpus with synthetic data or aggregating large-scale data calibrated to the ODD to ensure adequate analysis. And crucially, it means setting pass-fail thresholds before testing begins.

Consider AI-generated prior authorisation documentation, where a hypothetical hospital may define the following medical ODD: care coordinators use the tool to draft prior authorisation letters for outpatient procedures; it is not used for urgent authorisations, complex multimorbidity cases, or patients under 18 years of age. The hospital preregisters its evaluation: retrospective testing in 200 outpatient prior authorisation cases from their population, including stratified samples of Medicaid and commercially insured adult patients. Success criteria include accurate (>90% recall) capture of diagnoses and medications, appropriate reading levels and equivalent performance across insurance types. If testing reveals, for example, that the tool omits relevant diagnoses in 15% of Medicaid cases, deployment halts before any deleterious outcomes in live patients.

Preregistration enforces discipline: define success criteria upfront, test rigorously against local data and proceed when evidence supports safety and effectiveness. This framework applies equally to internal tools and vendor products. An independent validation of Epic’s 2018 sepsis prediction algorithm found substantially worse performance in local patient populations than what was originally reported from Epic’s internal testing.7 A vendor’s responsibility is to provide tools that can be validated locally—and ideally, to conduct that validation themselves. When vendors do not, health systems must demand and conduct local validation before patient care is affected.

Guardrails and monitoring

Once preregistration requirements are met, organisations should stage rollout with active guardrails to curb risk exposure. Such safeguards protect from known failure vectors: validation checks can flag implausible recommendations (such as contradictory diagnoses or medication doses outside safe ranges), automated PHI redaction can preserve patient privacy from third parties and escalation pathways to subject matter experts can mitigate ambiguity for uncertain cases.

In tandem with guardrails, post-deployment surveillance must track the same metrics defined during preregistration as well as those only available post-deployment, such as override frequency and other user feedback. For widely available systems, monitoring can come from analysis of population-scale datasets, although monitoring is most effective when coupled with intervention. When metrics cross predefined thresholds, protocols activate: pause deployment, investigate root causes and roll back if necessary. Effective monitoring includes both leading indicators (eg, near-misses, unusual override patterns and user anomaly reports) and lagging indicators (eg, adverse events and patient outcomes). Healthcare quality improvement has already demonstrated the value of this pairing: in one large initiative, tracking a leading process measure—the adoption of an obstetric haemorrhage safety bundle—was associated with a 21% reduction in the lagging indicator, haemorrhagic events.8 Early signals of degradation trigger interventions that prevent patient-level harm.

A path forward

Health systems face pressure to adopt AI quickly, making systematic evaluation feel like an obstacle. However, deploying undertested tools in high-risk clinical care environments and discovering problems in live patient care is both dangerous and ultimately slower than preventing them through rigorous upfront validation. Healthcare has solved this before: we don’t accept new medications without trials or surgical techniques without evidence. While AI technology may be novel, the deployment principles are not—define intended use, measure what matters, test before deployment and monitor continuously. Health systems must demand local validation with prespecified criteria, vendors must support this rigour and both must recognise that one-size-fits-all AI is fiction.
