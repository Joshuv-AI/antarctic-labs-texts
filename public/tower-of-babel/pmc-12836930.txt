Mediating role of aging and obesity in the relationship between dietary index for gut microbiota and metabolic syndrome and its potential regulatory mechanisms
Authors: Han Yang, Shuang Zhao, Haijuan Zhao, Zhaojun Pang, Huiqing Yao, Xin Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe relationship between gut microbiota and metabolic syndrome (MetS) has attracted significant attention, with MetS comprising components like abdominal obesity, dysglycemia, dyslipidemia, and hypertension. The dietary index for gut microbiota (DI-GM) has also been developed as a standardized tool for assessing a balanced diet for the gut microbiota. Additionally, the interplay between aging and metabolism is important for understanding disease development. Nevertheless, the intrinsic regulatory mechanisms of factors such as aging in the association between the DI-GM and MetS have not been thoroughly investigated.MethodsParticipants were recruited from multiple cycles of National Health and Nutrition Examination Surveys (NHANES). In this study, weighted logistic regression models, restricted cubic spline (RCS) and subgroup analyses were employed to investigate the association between DI-GM index, categorized as unfavorable or beneficial to gut microbiota, and the prevalence of MetS as well as its components. Mediation analysis models analyzed the mediating roles of biological age advance index (KDM-BAA) and body mass index (BMI). Sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of the models. Candidate genes related to aging and BMI mediators in this association were retrieved using the GeneCards database (evidence: literature/database annotations). GO and KEGG enrichment was conducted to reveal the potential regulatory mechanisms. Top ten topologically central genes were prioritized as potential regulators by protein–protein interaction (PPI) network and cytoHubba-MCC algorithm. GeneMANIA co-expression network and friends analysis refined the functional annotations of these genes, thereby providing clear hypotheses for subsequent experimental validation.ResultsA total of 6086 participants was enrolled. The DI-GM score was significantly negatively correlated with the prevalence of MetS (odd ratio (OR) = 0.91, 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.87–0.95, P < 0.05). Participants with DI-GM scores of 7 or higher had an adjusted OR of 0.69 (95% CI 0.57–0.84) compared to individuals with scores of 0–3. Additionally, unfavorable to gut microbiota were significantly associated with a lower risk of MetS (OR = 0.84, 95% CI 0.79–0.88, P < 0.05). RCS analysis further revealed the linear relationship between DI-GM and the prevalence of MetS as well as its components. Subgroup analyses indicated that alcohol consumption may influence this relationship (P for interaction < 0.05). Mediation analysis revealed that KDM-BAA (mediation proportion: 36.76%) and BMI (mediation proportion: 48.57%) had more prominent mediating effects. Furthermore, candidate genes were primarily associated with stress response, cellular localization, and molecular regulation, and the Pl3K-AKT pathway was the main enriched pathway. Top ten candidate genes were predominantly linked to DNA repair, with TP53 being the most important.ConclusionsHigher DI-GM scores are linearly associated with a lower risk of MetS and its components, with BMI and KDM-BAA index playing prominent mediating roles. Our study demonstrates the important role of DI-GM in potentially preventing the risk of MetS, and we observe the significant influence of unfavorable factors for gut microbiota in this relationship. It also suggests that the improvement of body weight management and modulation of physiological functions during aging may be key mechanisms in this association. Introduction

Metabolic Syndrome (MetS) is composed of a range of metabolic disorder components including abdominal obesity, impaired glucose tolerance, dyslipidemia, and hypertension. These metabolic abnormalities are interrelated and synergistic, significantly raising the risk of diabetes, nonalcoholic fatty liver disease, and cardiovascular diseases such coronary heart disease and stroke, which places a significant economic burden on society and the healthcare system [1, 2]. The prevalence of MetS is 34.7% in the United States [3] and 33.9% in China [4]. Metabolic dysregulation is recognized as a central driver in aging, with aging leading to dysregulation of multiple metabolic pathways, while on the other hand, metabolic changes in turn accelerate cellular senescence and promote age-related diseases. Moreover, obesity-induced metabolic disorders can exacerbate cellular senescence. The relationship among obesity, aging and metabolic diseases has received widespread attention, which is closely related to genetics, diet and environment, especially gut microbiota. Currently, early detection and personalized intervention of MetS have emerged as a pivotal topic in related fields.

The gut microbiome modulates systemic metabolism by influencing energy metabolism, bile acid metabolism and inflammatory responses [5]. Notably, dietary diversity has emerged as a key determinant of microbial community diversity and metabolic function. It has been found that elevated dietary fiber consumption correlates with increased release of short chain fatty acids (SCFAs) in the gut. SCFAs are gut microbial metabolite with beneficial effects on glucose homeostasis, including acetate, propionate, and butyrate. Ingestion of high-fiber foods is sufficient to restore cholesterol levels and regulate the metabolic homeostasis of the host, largely through modulating the abundance of flora such as Faecalibacterium, Alistipes, Oscillobacter, Roseburia, and Pseudofavonifractor [6]. In contrast, L-carnitine, a trimethylamine (TMA) precursor abundant in red meat, exacerbates atherosclerosis in mice by enhancing TMAO production via gut microbial metabolism [7]. Additionally, primary bile acids are metabolized to secondary bile acids by gut microbiota, affecting fat absorption and glucose homeostasis, while cholesterol intake raises total bile acid (BA) levels in the body, and fat and fiber intake significantly effects bile acid metabolic processes and the structure of intestinal microbial communities, such as Firmicutes [8, 9]. Dysbiosis of gut microbiota may accelerate biological aging and promote adipose tissue inflammation, thereby increasing the risk of metabolic disorders [10, 11]. Consequently, optimizing dietary patterns to restore microbiota balance is a critical strategy for mitigating the incidence of metabolic disease.

The dietary index of gut microbiota (DI-GM), a new index to quantify dietary factors that influence the diversity and function of gut microbiome, is effective in identifying dietary patterns that are either beneficial or detrimental to gut microbiota and avoids the low sensitivity of traditional indices such as the healthy eating index and the Mediterranean diet score. DI-GM identifies 14 dietary components that differentially affect the gut microbiota, including both beneficial to gut microbiota (e.g., fermented dairy) and unfavorable to gut microbiota (e.g., red meat) [12]. Herein, given the important role of gut microbiota homeostasis in metabolism regulation, exploring the correlation between DI-GM and MetS risk is essential for developing dietary interventions aimed at maintaining metabolic homeostasis. While prior research has established correlations between diet and gut microbiota in metabolic diseases, most studies have focused on single dietary interventions and lack systematic evaluation of combined dietary effects. Moreover, the majority of evidence derives from animal models and there is a lack of in-depth research on the mechanisms of obesity and aging in the relationship between gut flora and metabolic disease. Thus, it is necessary to investigate further the association between DI-GM and MetS along with its components. This study aims to investigate the association between DI-GM and MetS prevalence using data from the 2007–2018 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES). Additionally, we will explore the potential mediating roles of Klemera-Doubal method biological age advance index (KDM-BAA) and body mass index (BMI), and investigate potential dietary strategies based on gut microbiota modulation for reducing MetS risk.

Given the nature of the data, the current study presents observational findings, establishing important associations between dietary factors, gut microbiota, and MetS risk. However, it is important to emphasize that while these findings contribute to understanding the patterns and relationships between these variables, causality cannot be conclusively established due to the study's cross-sectional design. Future longitudinal studies, which track changes over time and incorporate causal analysis methods, are needed to explore the temporal dynamics and underlying mechanisms of the associations observed in this study. These further investigations will help to clarify whether the observed associations reflect causal pathways or whether they are driven by other confounding factors. Therefore, our study serves as a preliminary exploration that warrants further validation in prospective studies, which could more definitively address the causal relationships between dietary patterns, microbiota, and metabolic outcomes. Our findings may provide novel theoretical insights for developing personalized dietary interventions.

Method

Study design and participants

The analyzed data in this study were derived from the NHANES, a nationally representative cross-sectional program conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS). NHANES employs a stratified, multistage probability sampling design to evaluate the nutritional and health conditions of the U.S. population. The study has been approved by the Ethics Review Board of the NCHS and all participants have signed an informed consent form. More information about NHANES is available on the website (https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/). A total of 59,842 participants from six consecutive NHANES cycles (2007–2018) were included. The exclusion of participants was determined based on the following criteria: (1) age < 20 years (n = 25,072); (2) pregnancy status (n = 372); (3) missing data on DI-GM (n = 4,537); (4) incomplete data of BMI, SII, and KDM (n = 1,303); (5) missing data on the covariates (n = 3,729); (6) missing diagnostic data on MetS (n = 18,743). A total of 6,086 participants were included initially in the subsequent analysis (Fig. 1).

Metabolic syndrome definition

The definition of MetS was based on the diagnostic criteria, which provided in the Adult Treatment Program III of the National Cholesterol Education Program (NCEP) [13]: The diagnosis requires the presence of at least three of the following criteria: (1) Elevated waist circumference (Elevated): WC ≥ 102 cm for men and ≥ 88 cm for women. (2) Elevated Triglyceride (Elevated TG): TG ≥ 150 mg/dL or drug treatment for elevated TG. (3) Reduced high density lipoprotein cholesterol (Reduced HDL-C): HDL-C < 50 mg/dL in females, < 40 mg/dL in males or drug treatment for reduced HDL-C. (4) Elevated fasting blood glucose (Elevated FBG): glucose > 100 mg/dL or drug treatment for diabetes mellitus. (5) Elevated blood pressure (Elevated BP): blood pressure > 130/85 mmHg or drug treatment for raised blood pressure.

Assessment of dietary index for gut microbiota

The DI-GM is a dietary quality indicator used to assess dietary quality associated with maintaining a healthy gut microbiota. It was calculated by taking the average of two independent 24-h dietary recall interviews from NHANES 2007–2018. DI-GM scores are based on intake of 14 specific foods or nutrients (see Table S1). Beneficial components include avocados, broccoli, chickpeas, coffee, cranberries, fermented dairy products, dietary fiber, green tea, soy, and whole grains, while unfavorable components include red meat, processed meats, refined grains, and high-fat diets (≥ 40% of energy from fat) [12, 14].

To assign scores, dietary components were categorized according to sex-specific medians. For each beneficial component, intake was compared to the median value within each sex group. If an individual's intake was equal to or above the median, a score of 1 was assigned, reflecting a higher consumption of beneficial foods; if the intake was below the median, a score of 0 was given, indicating a lower intake. For each unfavorable component, the reverse approach was applied. If an individual's intake was below the median, a score of 1 was assigned, indicating a healthier intake of these components (lower consumption of unfavorable foods); if the intake was equal to or above the median, a score of 0 was assigned, reflecting a higher consumption of unfavorable foods.

The total DI-GM score is the sum of all individual component scores, with a possible range from 0 to 14. Higher DI-GM scores indicate a dietary pattern that is more beneficial to gut microbiota health, suggesting a healthier and more microbiota-friendly diet, characterized by higher intake of beneficial foods and lower intake of unfavorable foods.

Covariates

To effectively control for confounding effects, we included potential covariates associated with MetS from the NHANES based on established literature and clinical expertise. Socioeconomic variables were primarily obtained from household questionnaires, including age (20–30, 31–50, 51–70, > 70), gender, race (non-Hispanic white, Mexican American, non-Hispanic black, other Hispanic, and other race), education level (below high school, high school or above), marital status, and poverty-income ratio (PIR) (non-poor/poor). Participants' lifestyle and medical history was obtained by self-reported questionnaires, including physical activity, smoking, alcohol, sedentary, the history of cancer [15, 16]. Smoking status was defined as never smokers, former smokers and current smokers according to participants who reported smoking less than 100 cigarettes and whether currently smoking or not [17]. The intake of alcohol derived from total nutrient intake during the dietary interviews on the first and second days. Physical activity levels (inactive, moderate, vigorous, or both moderate and vigorous) were based on intensity levels of vigorous physical and moderate physical activity. [18]. Sedentary time (≤ 4 h, 4–8 h, > 8 h) was categorized by tertiles. History of cancer was determined by the self-reported or malignant tumors. Urine albumin and creatinine were measured from random and morning samples, processed at the University of Minnesota. Chronic kidney disease (CKD) was defined as urine albumin/creatinine ratio > 30 mg/g [19, 20]. It should be noted that NHANES does not include data on family medical history, such as a history of diabetes, hypertension, cardiovascular disease, or other metabolic disorders. Family history is a well-established risk factor for metabolic syndrome (MetS) and its components, influencing both genetic predispositions and disease risk. Although family history data would have been valuable in adjusting for potential genetic confounding factors, its absence in this analysis does not diminish the importance of the other covariates included. Future studies incorporating family history data could offer a more comprehensive understanding of how genetic and environmental factors interact in the development of MetS. While genetic predisposition remains an important factor, this study provides important insights into the dietary and environmental factors influencing MetS risk.

Definitions of mediating variables

Height and weight were measured to calculate BMI (weight/height2) at the MEC. Based on WHO criteria, participants were categorized as normal weight (< 25 kg/m2), overweight (25–30 kg/m2), or obese (> 30 kg/m2). Biological age was estimated using the BioAge package via the Klemera-Doubal method (KDM) [21] and KDM-BAA was derived by regressing biomarkers on chronological age, indicating whether an individual's aging is accelerated (positive values) or delayed (negative values) compared to the population average [22, 23].

Mediation analysis

Mediation analysis was performed to examine whether KDM-BAA and BMI mediate the relationship between Dietary Index for Gut Microbiota (DI-GM) and Metabolic Syndrome (MetS). The analysis was conducted using the “mediation” package in R, employing 1,000 bootstrap samples to estimate the direct, indirect, and total effects. In this analysis, DI-GM was the independent variable, KDM-BAA and BMI were the mediators, and MetS was the dependent variable. To ensure the validity of the analysis, potential confounding variables such as sociodemographic factors, lifestyle behaviors, and medical history were controlled for in the models. Key assumptions of mediation analysis, including no unmeasured confounding between the exposure, mediator, and outcome, were carefully considered. Given the cross-sectional nature of the NHANES data, this analysis was exploratory and focused on understanding the relationships between variables rather than drawing causal conclusions.

Screening of candidate genes and functional enrichment analysis

The GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org/) was searched for the keywords “metabolic syndrome”, “biological aging”, “Obesity” and “Short chain fatty acids” to identify relevant targets, and subsequently selected those targets with values exceeding the median for further analysis. The Venn diagram analysis identified overlapping genes among SCFA, metabolic syndrome and mediators (biological aging and BMI), suggesting candidate genes potentially associated with potential co-regulation. Functional annotation of candidate genes into GO categories of biological process (BP), molecular function (MF) and cellular component (CC) for enrichment analysis. For the subsequent GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis, the clusterProfiler package in R [24], was utilized to perform statistical analysis. The enrichment results were visualized with ggplot2 to allow for an intuitive understanding of the overrepresented biological terms and pathways. Given the large number of tests performed during enrichment analysis, we applied Benjamini–Hochberg correction (FDR) to adjust the P-values and control for false discovery rate. Specifically, P-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method, and an adjusted P-value (FDR) < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. This approach ensures that our findings are statistically robust and reduces the likelihood of false positives caused by the large number of multiple comparisons.

PPI network construction

A Protein–Protein Interaction (PPI) network is constructed with search tool for recurring instances of neighbouring genes (STRING) database to identify protein interactions. K-means clustering was applied to interacting proteins, followed by visualization using Cytoscape (version 3.7.1). Top ten candidate genes based on network were discovered by identifying central nodes using the Maximum Clique Centrality (MCC) algorithm in the cytoHubba plugin. Subsequently, co-expression networks were constructed by GeneMANIA (3. 6. 0) (http://genemania.org/) to predict their interactions.

Friends analysis

Following ID conversion, friends analysis was performed with the GOSemSim package (2.22.0) [25] to identify key genes based on their topological importance within the constructed interaction network.

Statistical analysis

Given that NHANES had ensured national sample representativeness through multistage probability sampling, we incorporated primary sampling units, sample weights, and stratification during data analysis, which prevented statistical significance from being overestimated. According to NHANES guidelines, weight selection prioritized representative variables for small group subgroups and then assigned appropriate weights accordingly. Weighted analyses were performed using the “survey” package [26], and multivariate weighted logistic regression models were used to investigate the correlation between DI-GM and MetS along with its components. The odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (95%CI) were calculated. DI-GM was analyzed as a categorical variable and grouped into quartiles (0–4, 5, 6, ≥ 7). Model 1 was unadjusted. Model 2 adjusted for age, sex and race, education, marital, and PIR. Model 3 adjusted for drinking, smoking, physical activity, and sedentary based on Model 2. Model 4 further adjusted for cancer history and CKD based on Model 3.

Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis assessed linear and nonlinear associations between DI-GM and MetS and its components adjusting for Model 4 confounders using the “rms” package [27]. Additionally, interaction and subgroup analyses further evaluated potential confounders of this association. Subgroup analyses were stratified by age, gender, education, PIR, alcohol, smoking, physical activity, sedentary, history of cancer and CKD. The “mediation” package was employed to assess the potential mediating roles of KDM-BAA and BMI in the association between DI-GM and MetS [28]. Due to the cross-sectional nature of the NHANES data, the mediation analyses were conducted as an initial exploration of potential indirect associations between DI-GM and MetS, with an emphasis on understanding the underlying relationships rather than making causal claims. The results should be interpreted as associations rather than definitive causal conclusions. Longitudinal studies will be crucial in future research to establish causality and better understand the directionality of these relationships. Further, we also explored whether KDM-BAA and BMI mediated the association between unfavorable to gut microbiota or beneficial to the gut microbiota and MetS. Sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the reliability of the results. First, we performed weighted linear analyses. Second, logistic regression and RCS analyses were further performed by removing participants with a history of cancer as well as a history of CKD. Finally, unweighted logistic regression and RCS models were used to examine the robustness of these associations.

All analyses were conducted using R (v4.4.0). Normally distributed continuous data were presented as Mean (SE), t-tests were used for comparisons of two independent samples, and categorical data were reported as counts and percentages using chi-square or Fisher's exact probability method. A P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics of study participants

After applying predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, 6,086 people were enrolled, of whom 48.29% (2,941) were male and 51.71% (3,145) were female. MetS components include elevated WC, elevated TG, reduced HDL-C, elevated FBG, and elevated BP. We showed the characteristics of the study population based on whether they have MetS or not. Among them, there were 2,201 participants without metabolic syndrome, accounting for 36.16%, and 3,885 participants with metabolic syndrome, accounting for 63.84%. Compared with non-Mets subjects, the subjects with Mets were older and had higher BMI, SII and KDM-BAA scores. They also exhibited a higher prevalence of CKD (22.07%) and cancer (15.11%). Additionally, significant differences were observed in terms of race, education level, smoking and alcohol status (P < 0.05). Analysis of dietary indicators related to gut microbiota showed a significant reduction in the mean DI-GM score (4.66 ± 0.04) and the mean score of unfavorable to gut microbiota (2.45 ± 0.03) in the Mets group (P < 0.05), but no significant differences were found in the group of beneficial to gut microbiota (Table 1).

Association of DI-GM with metabolic syndrome (MetS) and its components

In multivariate weighted logistic analyses, the investigation focused on assessing the association between DI-GM scores and the prevalence of MetS as well as individual MetS components. Table 2 reveals a significant negative correlation between DI-GM and the prevalence of MetS across all models. For each additional unit of DI-GM, there was an 8% reduction in MetS prevalence in unadjusted model 1 when DI-GM score was used as a continuous variable (OR = 0.92, 95% CI 0.88–0.97). In fully adjusted model 4, each additional unit of DI-GM was associated with a 9% reduction in the risk of MetS prevalence (OR = 0.91, 95% CI 0.87–0.95), all with significant P values (P < 0.001).

For the components of the metabolic syndrome, when the DI-GM score was used as a continuous variable, after adjusting for all variables in Model 4, the significant inverse association remained between DI-GM score and the following independent components, including elevated WC (OR = 0.91, 95% CI 0.86–0.96, P < 0.001), elevated BP (OR = 0.92, 95% CI 0.87–0.96, P = 0.001), elevated TG (OR = 0.95, 95% CI 0.90–0.99, P = 0.048), reduced HDL-C (OR = 0.94, 95% CI 0.90–0.99, P = 0.012), and elevated FBG (OR = 0.91, 95% CI 0.87–0.95, P < 0.001). Additionally, a level of DI-GM greater than or equal to 7 was also significantly and negatively associated with reduced HDL-C and elevated FBG, with a trend P value of < 0.05.

Further analyses showed that higher unfavorable to gut microbiota scores for continuous variables represented lower adverse effects on the gut microbiota. We found that the risk of MetS was reduced by 16% for each unit increase in unfavorable to gut microbiota in fully adjusted model 4 (OR = 0.84, 95% CI 0.79–0.88, P < 0.05). In the study, unfavorable to gut microbiota score was significantly correlated with independent components, such as elevated WC, reduced HDL-C, elevated FBG, and elevated BP, with a P value less than 0.05. The results suggest that there may be a trend of association between elevated TG and unfavorable to gut microbiota scores in model 4 (P = 0.052), but further analysis is still needed by other methods. Whereas, in fully adjusted model 4, we only found that a significantly negative association between the continuous variable beneficial gut microbiota score and elevated FBG (P = 0.045), with no significant correlation with the other components. Also, this negative correlation is also present in model 2 (P = 0.031), model 3 (P = 0.020) in elevated FBG.

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analyses were conducted to further explore the predictive role of DI-GM score on the risk of MetS prevalence in different population subgroups (Fig. 2). Our results illustrated that there was a significant negative correlation between DI-GM and the risk of MetS in most subgroups, including age, gender, education level, PIR, smoking, physical activity, and sedentary. Moreover, no significant interactions were observed (P for interaction > 0.05). Also, no significant interactions were found in cancer history as well as CKD (P for interaction > 0.05) (Fig. S1). The effects of DI-GM on MetS prevalence had no significantly different in these subgroups, indicating the influence of various demographic and health-related factors on the association between DI-GM score and the risk of MetS was relatively independent. Our results demonstrated the applicability and robustness of DI-GM model across different population subgroups. Notably, drinking status could be the factor that significantly modifies the correlation (P for interaction = 0.025), and higher DI-GM scores could be associated with a reduced risk of MetS among drinkers.

Mediation analysis

Further, mediation analyses were conducted to explore the role of KDM-BAA and BMI in this association. Notably, BMI and KDM-BAA played a significant mediating role in the association between DI-GM and MetS, with the mediating proportions explaining 48.57% and 36.76%, respectively (P < 0.0001). The results suggested that the risk of MetS was reduced by multiple pathways working together in this association, 48.57% achieved by lowering of BMI (indirect effect = −0.0087, P < 0.0001), 36.7% achieved by delaying biological age (indirect effect = −0.0066, P < 0.0001), while there were direct protective effects independent of the mediating variable, which collectively contributed to the total effects. Moreover, mediation analysis revealed that the overall effects of unfavorable to gut microbiota on the prevalence of MetS was also significant, BMI and KDM-BAA significantly mediated this association, explaining 56.19% and 26.96%, respectively (P < 0.001). However, the association and the overall effects of beneficial to gut microbiota on MetS risk was not statistically significant (Fig. 4 ).

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the model. First, a weighted linear regression model was performed when the variables were unchanged, which demonstrated the results were consistent with logistic regression results (Table S4). Second, when covariates of history of cancer and CKD were not included, the results of weighted logistic regression and RCS analyses remained robust. (Table S5). Finally, when unweighted logistic regression analyses and RCS analyses were performed, the results were also consistent with the weighted analyses results in the fully adjusted model (Table S6), and both DI-GM score and unfavorable to gut microbiota score were linearly and negatively associated with MetS (Fig. S4, S5). Additionally, unweighted linear regression analyses were performed in the fully adjusted model, and the similar results were obtained, which further illustrated the robustness of the results (Table S7).

Discussion

Based on nationally representative samples of U.S. adults, our study identified significant association between higher DI-GM score and reduced MetS risk. Notably, this association remained consistent after adjusting for potential confounders, and RCS analyses showed linear relationship with varying trends between DI-GM and MetS prevalence as well as MetS components. Additionally, we found that BMI and KDM-BAA had more prominent mediating effects. These findings highlighted the critical role of shaping healthy gut microbiome with dietary interventions in the prevention of MetS risk, and emphasized the importance as well as the feasibility of non-invasive and dietary-based prevention strategies, which contributed to develop personalized dietary recommendations for the prevention of MetS. Furthermore, sensitivity analyses confirmed the stability of these results. Our results suggested that higher DI-GM scores, reflecting balanced diets rich in fiber, whole grains, and fermented foods, are linearly and negatively associated with MetS prevalence, as well as with MetS components. Moreover, KDM-BAA and BMI played a major mediating role in this association. The development of novel dietary intervention strategies based on the DI-GM index is expected to be a potential therapeutic tool for disease onset and progression in MetS population.

Diet is the primary factor driving the composition and metabolic activities of the gut microbiota, surpassing genetics, environment, and other factors in shaping the gut microbiota, which makes dietary modification-based strategies a key intervention strategy for metabolic diseases [29]. Growing evidence from research indicates that maintenance of gut microbiota homeostasis plays an important role in reducing the risk of metabolic syndrome, which provides the basis for individualized interventions. Dysbiosis of the gut microbiota (e.g., Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes) can lead to metabolic dysfunctions. Overproliferation of Firmicutes may promote host fat accumulation by enhancing the efficiency of energy uptake, whereas decrease in bacteroidetes may impair the metabolism of dietary fiber, thereby it affects the production of SCFAs, which play a key role in the regulation of glucose-lipid metabolism and inflammatory responses [30]. Blautia abundance has been significantly inverse correlation with obesity-related metabolic markers [31], which correlates with the accumulation of SCFAs, especially acetate. Previous randomized controlled trials have also found that whole grains facilitate the restoration of intestinal homeostasis and positively improve blood glucose in patients with T2DM [32]. Evidence from a longitudinal study suggests that adherence to a dietary pattern rich in fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and legumes among young adults can reduced insulin resistance and lower risk of MetS [33]. This may be related to synergistic mechanisms that promote changes in the overall composition of HDL cholesterol particles, cholesterol efflux, reduced TG lipase activity, and overall TG synthesis [34, 35]. Our findings align with these studies, with higher DI-GM scores reflecting dietary patterns rich in fiber, whole grains, and fruits being significantly and linearly associated with a reduced risk of MetS and its components. These findings further emphasize the importance of dietary structure in promoting metabolic homeostasis by reshaping gut microbiota composition, offering new avenues for early detection and precision interventions in metabolic diseases.

However, it is important to note that the present study uses cross-sectional data from NHANES, which limits our ability to establish causal relationships. While we have identified significant associations between DI-GM and MetS, these results must be interpreted with caution, as temporality cannot be determined in a cross-sectional analysis. Thus, our claims of causality must be framed carefully, as the association observed does not imply a direct cause-and-effect relationship. Additionally, the mediation analysis presented in this study must be interpreted with the understanding that the observed associations may be confounded by unmeasured factors. Future studies, particularly longitudinal cohort studies, would be necessary to establish the temporal direction and causal pathways underlying the relationship between DI-GM, gut microbiota, and metabolic outcomes.

We found that the high prevalence rate of MetS in the general population cannot be overlooked, as it is significantly associated with an increased risk of chronic diseases such as cardiovascular diseases and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). Although the administration of metformin can improve the symptoms of metabolic syndrome, its effectiveness is always limited by individual differences. Gastrointestinal adverse events occur frequently, and safety remains controversial [36]. Although several studies have emphasized the predictive role of the Dietary Inflammatory Index (DII) or different dietary patterns on MetS risk, few studies have explored the role of aging and obesity in the DI-GM index in relation to MetS and its components. Our study found that KDM-BAA and BMI play an important mediating role in this association. Additionally, we identified TP53 as a potential therapeutic target, which is crucial for developing novel intervention strategies. Pharmacological treatment needs to be combined with lifestyle interventions, and attention needs to be paid to the effects of aging and obesity on metabolic disorders. While biological age reflects the cumulative effects of multisystem aging, approximately 30% of microbiota changes occur during the human lifetime [37], and the aging process further influences the composition of the host's gut microbiota. As individuals age, there is a progressive decline in the diversity and stability of gut microbiota, which is often accompanied by a shift towards a more inflammatory phenotype. This dysbiosis can exacerbate metabolic dysfunction by disrupting the balance of beneficial and harmful microbes, thereby contributing to the development of MetS. Additionally, aging is linked to an increased intestinal permeability and altered gut permeability, which can lead to the translocation of microbial products such as lipopolysaccharides into the bloodstream, triggering systemic inflammation and insulin resistance. This inflammatory process is a key factor in the pathogenesis of MetS, highlighting the importance of dietary interventions aimed at restoring a healthy gut microbiome in aging populations.

This is consistent with earlier studies showing that the Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio of the human microbiota increases from birth to adulthood and is further altered with age [38]. An animal study demonstrated that sustained microbiome rejuvenation in mice could delay or even reverse several markers of aging, including improved coordination and tightening of the gut barrier. Typically, BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 is recognized as obesity by the WHO, and obesity can significantly promote cellular senescence, which leads to metabolic disorders. In older adults, elevated body fat percentage is associated with abnormalities in several biomarkers of aging, including increased plasma activin A, Fas, and other cytokines [39]. This relationship between obesity, aging, and the gut microbiome underscores the importance of managing body weight and promoting a balanced diet as preventive measures against MetS, particularly in elderly individuals. The interplay between obesity, aging, and diet may also explain why metabolic disorders are more prevalent in older populations, as these factors collectively drive metabolic dysregulation.

Oxidative DNA damage has been identified in obese individuals with type 2 diabetes, which may affect intracellular energy metabolism and lead to aging-related metabolic disturbances [40]. As individuals age, the accumulation of oxidative damage in cellular components such as DNA, proteins, and lipids increases, which impairs metabolic function and accelerates aging processes. This cellular aging is compounded by an altered microbiome that may enhance oxidative stress, creating a vicious cycle that exacerbates metabolic disturbances. These aging-related changes underscore the need for dietary strategies that target both microbiota modulation and cellular repair mechanisms.

Notably, a high-fat diet rich in meat protein can decrease the microbiota capable of producing butyrate (e.g., Roseburia) while increasing the release of endotoxins (e.g., lipopolysaccharides), triggering metabolic endotoxemia and chronic low-grade inflammation. This exacerbates insulin resistance and fat accumulation, further supporting our findings that not only higher scores on DI-GM, which reflect overall dietary patterns, but also higher scores on unfavorable to gut microbiota, were significantly associated with a reduced risk of metabolic syndrome [36]. Moreover, a linear relationship was found to exist in this relationship between DI-GM and reduced risk of MetS as well as its components. Similarly, higher unfavorable to gut microbiota scores were also significantly linearly associated with MetS and its components, except FBG. There may be a nonlinear association of unfavorable to gut microbiota score with elevated FBG. Interestingly, no significant association with MetS was found in the beneficial to gut microbiota score. This discrepancy may be attributed to the dynamic nature of the gut microbiota and the chronic nature of metabolic diseases, which warrants further in-depth investigation.

In subgroup analyses, we observed that the protective effect of higher DI-GM scores was more pronounced in specific subgroups, including individuals who were young to middle-aged (30–50 years old), male, better educated, non-poor, never smokers, and former smokers, moderately physically active, and sedentary (4–8 h). This suggests that the impact of DI-GM on metabolic health is multidimensional, involving biological mechanisms, individual behavioral patterns, and social determinants. It can be seen that the gender-dependent nature of the modulation of microbiome functions by dietary disparities [41], may be influenced by hormone levels, showing a stronger protective effect in the male subgroup. Moreover, the metabolic systems of younger and middle-aged groups may be more responsive to changes in microbiota, with their gut microbiota structure being more susceptible to beneficial changes through dietary or lifestyle interventions. Previous studies have confirmed that harmful components in tobacco smoke, such as nicotine and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, can induce dysbiosis by mechanisms like disrupting intestinal barrier function and altering the microbial metabolite profile [42]. This further supports our analysis that the benefits are more pronounced in the subset of never or former smokers, suggesting that smoking cessation interventions in the MetS population may yield more significant gut microecological improvements. Additionally, combining moderate physical activity levels with sedentariness promotes metabolic homeostasis, with moderate exercise potentially amplifying the metabolic benefits of higher DI-GM scores. From a socioeconomic status perspective, including education and income levels, individuals with higher education and economic status generally exhibit better dietary quality and systematic health management. Interestingly, in our results, drinking status emerged as a factor that significantly modifies the correlation (P for interaction = 0.025), with higher DI-GM scores associated with a reduced risk of MetS among drinkers [43]. This suggests that moderate alcohol consumption may have a more favorable impact on metabolic health and gut microbiota, although further prospective cohort studies are required to clarify the complex interactions between alcohol intake, microbiota function, and metabolic phenotype.

Furthermore, the PI3K-Akt pathway maybe play a key role in aging and obesity-mediated effects, as it can regulate metabolic disorders through the modulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α [44]. Additionally, research findings indicate that the prevalence of metabolic syndrome (MetS) is significantly increased among patients with nephropathy. The underlying biological mechanisms are closely associated with the induction of insulin resistance, which is triggered by elevated circulating aldosterone levels, metabolic acidosis, and aberrant concentrations of angiotensin II (Ang II) [45]. These findings align with the protective effect of higher DI-GM scores on MetS observed in a subgroup without a history of cancer or cardiovascular disease. This may be closely related to our discovery of the enriched AGE-RAGE signaling pathway. TP53, a well-known tumor suppressor gene, is increasingly recognized for its role in the regulation of metabolic processes, including inflammation and cellular stress response. In the context of aging and obesity, TP53 plays a central role in cellular senescence, which accelerates the decline of metabolic function and promotes inflammation. TP53 also regulates key cellular processes such as apoptosis and autophagy, which are involved in maintaining cellular homeostasis in response to metabolic stresses. The dysregulation of TP53 signaling may contribute to the progression of MetS by exacerbating systemic inflammation, impairing DNA repair, and increasing oxidative stress, all of which are hallmark features of both aging and obesity.

Furthermore, TP53's role in modulating the gut microbiota has emerged as an area of growing interest. TP53 directly influences the immune response, and alterations in immune function can shift the composition and activity of gut microbiota. In the case of metabolic syndrome, TP53 may modulate microbial communities that influence inflammation and metabolism. For instance, TP53 may affect the balance between pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory microbial species, thereby altering host metabolic responses. As such, TP53 is not only a key player in cellular metabolism but also in the systemic regulation of gut microbiota and its subsequent impact on metabolic disease. These findings open up the potential for targeting TP53 as part of a multi-faceted approach to dietary interventions aimed at reducing the burden of MetS, especially in populations with obesity and advancing age.

Overall, our results revealed a significant correlation between higher DI-GM scores and a reduced risk of MetS, especially in the context of unfavorable gut microbiota, and further highlighted the more prominent mediating effects of KDM-BAA and BMI. However, most gut microbiome therapies are based on animal models, which have limitations in their applicability to human health. In contrast, our study innovatively utilized the DI-GM index as an indicator to assess the quality of diet associated with gut microbiota, conducting the study in a large population, which offers meaningful insights into novel gut microbiome therapies. Finally, sensitivity analyses showed consistent associations, as identified in our main findings. However, there are some limitations to the study. First, the 24-h recall interviews collected data on participants' food intake at mobile examination centers or via telephone interviews, and most of the covariates rely on self-reporting, which may introduce recall bias. Second, the study did not account for changes in MetS over long-term follow-up. While some confounders were adjusted for, there are still factors such as disability and other comorbid conditions that were not considered. Future cohort studies will improve the reliability of the study, and high-throughput sequencing can further explore the dynamic and complex relationships among dietary strategies, gut microbiota, and metabolic syndrome.

Conclusion

Our study revealed a significant association between DI-GM, a dietary quality index reflecting gut microbiota diversity, and the prevalence of MetS and its components. The dose–response relationship suggested a linear negative association between DI-GM and MetS as well as its components. A similar association was observed for dietary patterns unfavorable to gut microbiota, except for the component FBG. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses further confirmed the robustness of the model. Notably, BMI and KDM-BAA played prominent mediating roles in the relationship between DI-GM and MetS, with mediation ratios of 36.76% and 48.57%, respectively, highlighting the importance of managing both obesity and biological aging in the prevention of MetS. TP53 was identified as a potential candidate target for future therapeutic interventions, based on its involvement in the PI3K-AKT pathway. This finding opens up new possibilities for targeted interventions. Therefore, integrating aging and obesity management, along with dietary strategies aimed at modulating gut microbiota, should be considered when personalizing interventions for metabolic syndrome. Future research, particularly longitudinal studies, will be crucial to further explore the dynamics of aging, obesity, and gut microbiota in MetS progression, and to validate the long-term impact of dietary interventions on metabolic health.
