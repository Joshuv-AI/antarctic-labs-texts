Effects of exercise on peripheral blood DNA methylation and related epigenetic markers: a systematic review of human trials
Authors: Yahui Yang, Fengshu Zhu, Aiguo Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRegular exercise promotes health through multiple mechanisms, with DNA methylation serving as a key epigenetic modification involved in the molecular regulation induced by exercise. Peripheral blood, as an accessible tissue, is commonly used to study exercise-induced DNA methylation changes.ObjectiveThis systematic review aims to synthesize evidence from human exercise intervention studies on the effects of exercise on peripheral blood DNA methylation and related epigenetic markers, and to evaluate their potential as biomarkers of exercise efficacy.MethodsThe study protocol was registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251069398). A systematic search was conducted in Pubmed, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, Embase and Scopus databases from database inception through 29 May 2025 for all English-language studies. Eligible studies were included. Risk of bias of randomized controlled trials were assessed using the Cochrane collaboration’s tool (version 5.1.0) and quasi-experimental studies were assessed using the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklists for Quasi-Experimental Studies. The results were summarized using narrative synthesis.ResultsTwenty-nine studies were included, covering both acute and long-term exercise interventions. Acute exercise induced minor and mostly statistically nonsignificant changes in peripheral blood DNA methylation, whereas long-term exercise elicited significant methylation remodeling in genes related to metabolism, inflammation, and immune function. Exercise dose (frequency, intensity, and duration) and population characteristics influenced the magnitude and scope of methylation responses.ConclusionsPeripheral blood DNA methylation provides robust evidence for exercise-mediated systemic epigenetic regulation and shows promise as a biomarker for exercise adaptation and intervention effects. Future research should optimize intervention designs and methodological standards to deepen understanding of exercise epigenetic mechanisms.Keywords: Exercise, DNA methylation, Peripheral blood, Epigenetics, Systematic review

Introduction

Regular physical activity is well established as a cornerstone of health promotion and disease prevention. A growing body of evidence demonstrates that exercise improves cardiovascular fitness, enhances metabolic function, reduces inflammation, promotes mental health, improves cognitive performance and mitigates the risk of numerous chronic conditions [1–5]. Recent evidence underscores that these benefits are partly mediated through epigenetic modifications, particularly DNA methylation, which dynamically regulates gene expression without altering the DNA sequence [6, 7].

Accumulating evidence from both human and animal studies indicates that exercise can lead to differential DNA methylation in a variety of tissues, including brain [8, 9], skeletal muscle [10], adipose tissue [11] and blood [12]. These changes often occur in genes related to inflammation, oxidative stress, mitochondrial function, and neuroplasticity [13]. For example, in the brain, physical activity has been associated with changes in methylation patterns of genes involved in neuroplasticity and mood regulation, potentially mediating exercise-induced cognitive and emotional benefits [14]. In skeletal muscle, exercise-induced methylation changes have been linked to metabolic adaptations and improvements in insulin sensitivity [15]. Moreover, both acute and long-term exercise interventions have been shown to alter the methylation status of key genes such as PGC-1α, BDNF, and IL-6, which regulate metabolism, synaptic plasticity, and immune responses, respectively [16, 17]. These findings highlight DNA methylation as both a biological mediator and potential biomarker of the benefits of physical activity.

Among the accessible human tissues, peripheral blood has become a practical and commonly used surrogate for studying exercise-related changes in DNA methylation. Unlike brain or muscle biopsies, which are invasive or ethically challenging, blood samples are minimally invasive and amenable to repeated sampling. However, the validity of peripheral blood as a surrogate for DNA methylation changes in target tissues remains a topic of ongoing debate. Given the fundamental differences in cellular composition and functional properties between peripheral blood and exercise-sensitive tissues such as muscle or brain, some researchers argue that methylation patterns in blood-derived DNA may not fully reflect those in skeletal muscle, adipose tissue, or the brain, and may be confounded by shifts in white blood cell composition [18, 19].

Nevertheless, several studies provide encouraging evidence supporting the utility of blood-based DNA methylation analysis. In animal models, genome-wide comparisons have identified dozens of shared differentially methylated regions (DMRs) between brain and blood with identical genomic coordinates [20]. In a human neurosurgical sample-matched study, blood showed a relatively high correlation with brain DNA methylation (r = 0.86) and contained the highest proportion of brain-consistent CpG sites (20.8%), outperforming saliva and buccal tissue [21]. Similarly, Horvath and colleagues reported high correlations in DNA methylation between blood and multiple brain regions (r = 0.85–0.91), reinforcing the feasibility of using blood as a surrogate for brain tissue in epigenetic studies [22]. Moreover, during specific developmental stages or disease states, peripheral blood and brain tissue have shown coordinated methylation shifts, such as in antenatal corticosteroid exposure models (r = 0.31) [23]. Furthermore, blood-derived methylation biomarkers—such as epigenetic clocks—have proven effective in estimating biological age and predicting health outcomes, underscoring their biological relevance [24, 25]. Thus, despite its inherent tissue specificity limitations, peripheral blood remains a practical and representative surrogate tissue in exercise epigenetics research.

Given the increasing interest in non-invasive biomarkers and the practical challenges associated with tissue-specific sampling, peripheral blood DNA methylation analysis offers a valuable avenue for evaluating the epigenetic impact of exercise interventions in humans. Despite the growing number of trials assessing this relationship, the findings remain fragmented and heterogeneous with respect to intervention characteristics, participant demographics, and methylation targets. Therefore, the aim of this systematic review is to synthesize evidence from human studies that have examined the effects of exercise on DNA methylation and related epigenetic markers in peripheral blood. By consolidating existing findings, we aim to better understand how exercise modulates systemic epigenetic regulation and to evaluate the potential of peripheral blood methylation profiles as biomarkers of exercise efficacy.

Methods

Search strategy

We performed a systematic search of Pubmed, Web of science, Cochrane library, Embase and Scopus databases from database inception to 29 May 2025 for all studies written in English. Studies were identified by using all possible combinations of the following groups of search terms: (a) “physical activity” OR “physical training” OR “sport” OR “exercise”; (b) “DNA methylation” OR “epigenome” OR “epigenetic”; (c) “whole blood” OR “peripheral blood” OR “PBMC” OR “blood leukocyte”. The specific search was amended as necessary for each database to account for different search functionalities. The reference lists of retrieved articles and grey literature were searched to detect studies potentially eligible for inclusion. The study protocol was registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251069398).

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria were (1) Population: Studies involving human participants of any age, including both healthy individuals and those with clinical conditions. (2) Intervention: Any form of physical activity or exercise intervention, encompassing both acute (short-term) and chronic (long-term) exercise protocols. (3) Comparison: Studies with any type of comparison group or design, including no-treatment controls, standard care controls, comparisons between different populations or intervention types, or within-subject pre-post designs. (4) Outcomes: Studies assessing DNA methylation in peripheral blood—including whole blood, peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), or leukocytes—or evaluating epigenetic markers related to DNA methylation in peripheral blood, such as DNA methyltransferases (DNMT1, DNMT3a, DNMT3b). (5) Study Design: Clinical trials.

Exclusion criteria were (1) Animal studies, in vitro studies, or human studies using non-blood tissues only. (2) Conference abstracts or proceedings lacking sufficient methodological or outcome information regarding the intervention and its effects. (3) Multimodal intervention studies in which the independent effect of physical activity cannot be isolated. (4) Not published in English.

Study selection

Search results were imported into Endnote to remove duplicates. Two reviewers (YY, FZ) independently screened the titles and abstracts of the retrieved articles to exclude irrelevant studies. The remaining articles were then reviewed in full by both reviewers (YY, FZ) to determine the final selection of studies. Disagreements were resolved through consensus or consultation with a third reviewer (AC).

Data extraction

One reviewer (YY) extracted specific characteristics from included studies, including author name, publication year, study design, paticipants characteristics (age, gender), sample size, intervention programme characteristics (mode of exercise, type, duration, frequency), comparison programme and outcomes (including tissue, DNA methylation measurement, and analyzed genes). Another reviewer (FZ) confirmed the content.

Risk of bias assessment

Two reviewers (YY, FZ) assessed risk of bias of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) using the Cochrane collaboration’s tool (version 5.1.0) independently, which includes seven domains: random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of participants and personnel, blinding of outcome assessment, incomplete outcome data, selective reporting, and other sources of bias [26]. Each domain was rated as low risk, high risk, or unclear risk. The overall risk of bias for each study was judged based on these domain-level assessments: a study was considered to have a low risk of bias if all domains were rated as low risk; a moderate risk of bias if one or more domains were rated as unclear but none as high risk; and a high risk of bias if at least one domain was rated as high risk or if multiple domains were rated as unclear, thereby raising concerns about the validity of the study findings. Risk of bias of quasi-experimental studies were assessed using the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklists for Quasi-Experimental Studies independently, which consists of 9 items. Each item was rated as “Yes”, “No”, “Unclear” or “Not applicable” [27]. To determine the overall risk of bias, we calculated the percentage of “Yes” responses out of the total number of applicable items (i.e., excluding “Not applicable” entries from the denominator). Studies were classified as having low risk of bias if ≥ 70% of applicable items were rated “Yes”, moderate risk if 50–69% were “Yes”, and high risk if ≤ 49% were “Yes”. This approach allows for a proportionate evaluation that accounts for variation in study design features. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus or deliberation with a third reviewer (AC).

Data analysis

We summarized the results using narrative synthesis. Meta-analysis was not possible due to the diversity of outcome measures included in the study.

Results

Characteristics and risk of bias of included studies

A total of 29 studies were included in this analysis, exploring the relationship between exercise interventions and DNA methylation. The detailed screening flow is shown in Fig. 1. All studies were RCTs or quasi-experimental designs, with participant numbers ranging from five to several hundred. Several results included in this review originated from sub-studies conducted as part of broader experimental trials [12, 28–31]. These studies encompassed diverse populations, including healthy adults, children, and adults with various health conditions (e.g., obesity, hypertension, cancer, etc.). The studies included both acute and long-term exercise interventions involving various types of exercises such as aerobic, strength, and high-intensity interval training (HIIT). All studies evaluated the effects of exercise on peripheral blood DNA methylation or related epigenetic markers, focusing on changes in gene expression and methylation age (see Table 1 for details).

Effects of acute exercise on DNA methylation in healthy adults

Among the 29 included studies, four examined changes in global DNA methylation or methylation-related genes following acute exercise in athletes or healthy adults. Two studies reported no significant changes in DNMT1 transcripts [32] or DNMT1 and DNMT3b protein levels in PBMCs after a 10-km run [33], with no associations found with physical or biochemical parameters. Robson-Ansley et al. [34] observed mild fluctuations in global methylation immediately after exercise, with 53 CpG sites showing changes (unadjusted p = 0.001), though none remained significant after multiple testing correction. In contrast, Schenk et al. [35] identified 33 significantly altered methylation sites post-exercise, surviving FDR correction, with 57.6% showing decreased methylation and 42.4% increased. These findings suggest that while acute exercise may cause minor and transient methylation changes, most effects are subtle or not statistically significant.

Effects of long-term exercise on DNA methylation in children

One study examined DNA methylation changes in whole blood following a 20-week exercise intervention in children with overweight/obesity [36]. In boys, 485 CpG sites showed significant changes (306 hypermethylated, 179 hypomethylated; P < 0.001), while in girls, 386 sites were affected (108 increased, 278 decreased; P < 0.001). These sites were enriched in pathways related to metabolism, fatty acid processing, and immune function. DNA methylation changes were linked to exercise-induced gene expression changes in 18% of CpG sites in boys and 12% in girls. Affected genes were associated with obesity, metabolic syndrome, and inflammation. These results suggest that DNA methylation mediates the transcriptional response to exercise in children with overweight/obesity, particularly in metabolic and immune pathways. It should be noted that these CpG-level findings were based on unadjusted p-values (P < 0.001), and the significance did not remain after correction for multiple testing (FDR > 0.05).

Effects of long-term exercise on DNA methylation in healthy adults

A total of 11 studies investigated the effects of long-term exercise intervention (ranging from 4 weeks to 1 year) on global or gene-specific DNA methylation in healthy adults. Evidence indicates that long-term exercise interventions can induce changes in global or gene-specific DNA methylation levels in healthy adults, and the dose of intervention may play a critical role in this process. Studies with intervention periods longer than 12 weeks, frequency ≥ 3 times/week, and session duration ≥ 45 min generally reported more pronounced methylation changes. For instance, Smith et al. [28], using a 16-week protocol with three 30-minute sessions per week, found that exercise reversed age-related upregulation of repetitive element expression via genome-wide hypermethylation restoration. Similarly, Gillman et al. [37] observed healthy methylation changes in aging- and cancer-related genes such as AURKA and BCAR1 after 16 weeks of four sessions per week. Rodrigues et al. [12, 38] identified thousands of differentially methylated CpG sites and reductions in epigenetic age after 8 or 14 weeks of moderate-intensity training (60 min per session, three times weekly), with significant sites surviving FDR correction, supporting the systemic epigenetic reprogramming potential of sustained exercise. Chelly et al. [39], with four sessions per week over 8 weeks, also reported increased global methylation and hypermethylation at the RANKL promoter, based on nominal significance thresholds. Denham et al. [40] found that after 8 weeks of three sessions per week of resistance training, tens of thousands of CpG sites showed altered methylation, although these findings were not adjusted for multiple testing. In contrast, studies with lower frequency (≤ 2 times/week) or shorter durations (≤ 4 weeks) showed more limited changes. Dimauro et al. [41] reported slight global hypomethylation after 12 weeks of twice-weekly training, based on nominal p-values, and Denham et al. [13] observed minimal CpG methylation changes after only 4 weeks of training, with analyses including FDR correction. Interestingly, Boyne et al. [42], despite implementing a high-frequency (≥ 5 days/week), year-long program, found no significant differences, possibly due to measurement error arising from failure to adjust for cell-type distribution. Moreover, studies by Zhang [43] and Nakajima [44] indicated that moderate-intensity exercise over 6 months increased promoter methylation of NFκB2 and ASC, suggesting prolonged, consistent exercise may confer anti-inflammatory and anti-aging epigenetic benefits, although these results were derived from nominal p-value thresholds rather than multiple-testing–corrected analyses. In summary, despite some inconsistencies, current evidence supports that long-term exercise can beneficially affect global and gene-specific DNA methylation patterns, contributing to improved health outcomes. However, the epigenetic effects of exercise appear closely tied not only to the total duration but also to frequency and session length, highlighting the importance of optimized dosing in designing effective interventions.

Effects of long-term exercise on DNA methylation in diseased adults

Among 13 studies on long-term exercise interventions in adults with various chronic conditions, most reported significant changes in DNA methylation at global or gene-specific levels, showing condition-specific characteristics. In diabetic and prediabetic populations, a 14-week intervention led to 118 differentially methylated regions (DMRs) in blood, mainly related to inflammation and metabolic pathways, although these findings were based on nominal p-values without multiple-testing correction [31]. In obesity and metabolic syndrome, 986 differentially methylated CpG sites were identified after intervention, most of them hypomethylated and enriched in metabolic pathways [30], surviving FDR correction. Another study in women with polycystic ovary syndrome showed a significant increase in genome-wide methylation, involving lncRNA and ribosomal gene regions, with significant findings after FDR adjustment [29]. In cardiovascular-related diseases, high-intensity interval training increased methylation at the p66Shc promoter, based on nominal significance [45]; in hypertensive patients, repetitive elements (ALU, LINE-1) and genes like EDN1 and NOS2 showed significantly higher methylation after training, also based on unadjusted p-values [46]. Among patients with heart failure, ASC promoter methylation was significantly elevated at 3 and 6 months in the exercise group compared to controls, also based on unadjusted p-values [47]. In cancer survivors, significant methylation changes occurred in 43 genes in breast cancer survivors [48], and 524 genes in colorectal cancer survivors [49], both based on nominal p-values; a slight but non-significant decrease in DNA methylation age was also observed in those with multiple chronic conditions [50]. In older adults with mild cognitive impairment, 248 CpG sites were differentially methylated after 6 months of aerobic training, involving VPS52, NR1H2, etc., with findings surviving FDR correction [51]. For COPD patients, global methylation dropped significantly after the first training session, based on nominal p-values [52]. In patients post-knee arthroplasty, PDK4 promoter methylation changes were concordant in both blood and muscle tissue [53]. In older adults with hematologic malignancy, mPhenoAge decreased in a few individuals post-intervention, although these observations were based on nominal significance thresholds [54].

Current evidence suggests that long-term exercise interventions can induce significant changes in DNA methylation in peripheral blood across various chronic diseases. These changes are disease-specific and involve key biological pathways such as energy metabolism, inflammatory response, and neural regulation, indicating that DNA methylation may serve as a potential mechanistic marker for the physiological benefits of exercise. The number, direction (hyper- or hypomethylation), and functional relevance of methylation changes vary across disease populations, suggesting that exercise may exert distinct epigenetic effects depending on the disease context.

Synthesis across studies

Across 29 studies, a reproducible pattern emerges despite study heterogeneity (Fig. 2): (1) acute bouts typically produce modest, transient methylation shifts often limited to tens of CpGs and seldom survive multiple-testing correction, whereas long-term/regular training more often yields broad, biologically coherent changes (hundreds-thousands of CpGs) enriched for metabolism, inflammation and aging-related pathways (e.g., AMPK, inflammatory signaling, RE/LINE elements). (2) Population context matters. Children and several patient groups frequently show larger or more pathway-focused changes than young healthy adults, suggesting heightened epigenetic plasticity under developmental or perturbed metabolic states. (3) Modality & dose trends. Interventions with greater total dose (duration × frequency) and moderate-to-high intensity more consistently produced extensive methylation remodeling; evidence comparing aerobic versus resistance training is limited but indicates both can induce meaningful changes, sometimes in distinct pathways (cardiometabolic vs. growth/repair genes). (4) Physiological concordance is limited but present. Only a minority of studies tested clinical/fitness correlates, but notable examples (e.g., VO2max vs. BRCA1 or RASSF1) imply that peripheral methylation at specific loci can reflect functional adaptations. Taken together, the balance of evidence supports that persistent, sufficiently dosed exercise is more likely to drive reproducible, functionally relevant blood methylation changes—yet methodological heterogeneity (platforms, cell-type adjustment, batch-control) and sparse reporting of adherence/fitness—methylation associations remain key gaps.

Summary of exercise-induced blood DNA methylation

Discussion

This systematic review included 29 human studies investigating the effects of exercise on peripheral blood DNA methylation, spanning healthy adults, children, and various patient populations. The findings suggest that acute exercise generally induces mild and transient changes in DNA methylation, while long-term interventions—especially those of moderate to high intensity and sufficient duration—consistently lead to extensive methylation remodeling in peripheral blood. These changes often involve genes related to metabolism, inflammation, and neuroregulation, supporting the notion that exercise may mediate health benefits through epigenetic mechanisms, particularly in chronic disease management with disease-specific methylation profiles.

On a mechanistic level, exercise-induced DNA methylation changes are thought to result from transient alterations in cellular energy balance, redox status, and hormonal signaling. During physical activity, increased ATP turnover and oxygen consumption elevate the AMP/ATP ratio and activate energy sensors such as AMPK and SIRT1 [55, 56], which in turn regulate chromatin-modifying enzymes. Enhanced mitochondrial respiration and reactive oxygen species (ROS) production can modify the availability of methyl donors (e.g., S-adenosylmethionine) and alter the redox-sensitive activity of DNA methyltransferases (DNMTs) and ten–eleven translocation (TET) enzymes [7, 57], leading to locus-specific methylation changes. Moreover, repeated bouts of exercise modulate systemic inflammation and stress hormone levels—reducing NFκB2 activation and circulating cortisol—which together may contribute to a more anti-inflammatory and metabolically favorable epigenetic landscape [58, 59]. Although these mechanisms are often proposed in the broader exercise–epigenetics literature, several studies in this review provide direct or partial support. For example, methylation changes in NFκB2-related or IL-6-related loci [43, 44] appeared alongside reductions in inflammatory markers or shifts in immune-related pathways, suggesting that at least part of the methylation response may reflect measurable changes in inflammatory status. Similarly, studies reporting PGC-1α methylation shifts [17, 51] also observed improvements in fitness or metabolic profiles, offering preliminary evidence that epigenetic modulation of mitochondrial biogenesis pathways may have functional relevance.

This review highlights a clear dose–response relationship between exercise and DNA methylation in peripheral blood. Long-term interventions (≥ 8 weeks), with a frequency of ≥ 3 sessions per week and a duration of ≥ 45 min, were more likely to induce statistically significant (FDR-adjusted or unadjusted) and widespread (hundreds to thousands of CpG sites across the genome) changes in DNA methylation [12, 28, 37, 38]. These findings align with prior studies in skeletal muscle, where exercise dose was a key determinant of methylation shifts [15, 16]. In contrast, shorter or lower-frequency protocols generally failed to yield consistent methylation changes, likely due to insufficient physiological stimulation [13, 41]. These results underscore the central role of training dose in epigenetic regulation. Proposed mechanisms include cumulative oxidative stress, metabolic strain, and immune activation that may influence the activity of DNA methyltransferases (DNMTs) and demethylating enzymes (TETs). Some studies also suggest a positive correlation between exercise intensity and methylation responses, implying that more vigorous protocols may trigger stronger epigenetic signaling [14].

Overall, individual characteristics such as age, sex, and disease status appear to shape the magnitude and direction of exercise-induced DNA methylation changes. For instance, in overweight/obese children, 20 weeks of exercise induced widespread methylation shifts in genes related to metabolism and immune function, with sex-specific patterns observed [36]. These findings support earlier studies indicating heightened epigenetic plasticity during development [60]. Among adults, marked differences were observed between healthy and clinical populations. Healthy adults typically showed fewer and more targeted methylation changes, especially in genes associated with aging or metabolic regulation (e.g., LINE-1, AMPK, etc.) [12, 28, 42]. In diabetes, metabolic syndrome, or PCOS patients, methylation changes were mostly found in metabolic and inflammatory pathways [29, 31], while in cancer survivors, genes associated with cell cycle regulation and neurofunction were affected [48, 49]. These patterns indicate that who is exercising—children, healthy adults, or patients—substantially influences both the scale and the biological focus of methylation responses. Such population-dependent variation also reinforces the possibility that exercise may shape distinct “epigenetic trajectories” across developmental and disease contexts, underscoring the value of more personalized intervention strategies in future research.

Taken together, multiple mechanisms may underlie exercise-induced changes in DNA methylation in peripheral blood, including metabolic stress responses, anti-inflammatory signaling, neuroplasticity, and chromatin remodeling. Metabolic regulators such as AMPK and SIRT1 are activated by exercise, promoting mitochondrial biogenesis via PGC-1α, whose promoter methylation changes post-exercise in both skeletal muscle and blood [15, 17]. Anti-inflammatory pathways are also involved—exercise suppresses NFκB2 while enhancing IL-10 expression, accompanied by methylation changes in associated genes [43, 44]. In cognitive contexts, exercise-mediated methylation shifts in BDNF and NR1H2 have been observed in individuals with mild cognitive impairment [51]. Moreover, coordinated changes in microRNA expression and histone modifications may contribute to DNA methylation dynamics during exercise adaptation [17]. However, mechanistic interpretations should be viewed cautiously, as few included studies simultaneously measured methylation and downstream molecular outputs (e.g., cytokine concentrations, mitochondrial markers, insulin sensitivity), leaving most causal links inferred rather than demonstrated.

Although most studies in this review primarily focused on methylation changes, several also examined whether these changes were associated with physiological or functional improvements. Gillman et al. [37] reported that greater increases in VO2max were associated with smaller increases in BRCA1 methylation, while Boyne et al. [42] found an inverse dose–response relationship between VO2max change and RASSF1 methylation. In Ferrari et al. [46], VO2 peak was positively associated with methylation of ALU, EDN1, NOS2 and TNF, and resting blood pressure showed inverse associations with LINE-1, EDN1 and NOS2. Similarly, Butts et al. [47] demonstrated that exercise-induced increases in ASC methylation were positively correlated with six-minute walk test performance and inversely correlated with ASC mRNA and IL-1β. In addition to these studies with formal statistical analyses, several other investigations reported parallel improvements in physiological indicators and alterations in DNA methylation following exercise, suggesting a potential pattern of synchronized biological responses, although these studies did not perform explicit correlation analyses. Collectively, these findings indicate emerging but still limited evidence linking peripheral blood methylation with functional adaptations to exercise, highlighting the need for more systematic evaluation in future work.

Besides physiological outcomes, methodological factors such as adherence and study retention may influence the magnitude and consistency of methylation responses. However, only a few included studies reported compliance or drop-out rates, and none statistically examined whether higher adherence predicted larger methylation shifts. This lack of information limits the ability to determine whether methylation changes serve as reliable biomarkers of exercise efficacy in real-world settings.

In this review, we included studies that examined DNA methylation changes in peripheral blood, as it is a minimally invasive, ethically feasible, and repeatedly accessible tissue commonly used in human epigenetic research. However, concerns remain regarding its ability to reflect tissue-specific methylation changes, particularly in acute exercise studies. For instance, our findings show only mild and often non-significant methylation shifts in blood after acute exercise [34, 35], whereas skeletal muscle shows rapid, significant changes in genes PGC-1α post-exercise [15]. These discrepancies may result from cellular heterogeneity, signal dilution in blood, and temporal lag in systemic methylation responses. Moreover, the type of blood sample used (e.g., whole blood, leukocytes, or PBMCs) can substantially influence observed methylation patterns, as differences in cell-type composition may confound or mask true epigenetic changes. This issue is underscored by studies such as Boyne et al. [42], which did not adjust for leukocyte subset differences, making it difficult to distinguish true methylation shifts from shifts in immune cell proportions. In addition to these biological sources of heterogeneity, methodological variability in blood-based EWAS—including differences in preprocessing pipelines (normalization procedures, probe filtering, batch-effect correction), analytic modeling choices, and adjustment for key covariates (e.g., estimated leukocyte proportions, age, sex, ancestry)—can further contribute to inconsistencies across studies. Future exercise-epigenetic research would benefit from standardized preprocessing workflows, explicit reporting of analytic decisions, and consistent implementation of cell-type deconvolution and multiple-testing correction to improve interpretability and reproducibility. Nevertheless, some evidence supports the representativeness of blood methylation for systemic and chronic responses. For example, Kamo et al. [53] reported consistent methylation shifts in PDK4 in both muscle and blood. Voisin et al. [17] also found shared methylation changes in metabolic genes across tissues. Most notably, long-term exercise trials reveal pronounced methylation changes in peripheral blood across pathways regulating metabolism, inflammation, and epigenetic aging [38, 40]. Thus, despite its limitations, blood remains a practical and biologically meaningful surrogate tissue in human exercise epigenetics research. Future work should continue to clarify when and to what extent blood methylation mirrors target-tissue biology, ideally through multi-tissue designs or analyses integrating methylation with downstream molecular and physiological endpoints.

Limitations

Despite its comprehensive scope, this review has limitations. First, the included studies exhibit considerable heterogeneity in design, intervention duration, intensity, sample size, and methylation analysis platforms, limiting comparability. Second, most studies rely on pre–post comparisons at single time points, lacking longitudinal follow-up to assess the durability of epigenetic changes. Third, some relevant studies may have been missed due to publication bias or database constraints. Differences in methylation platforms (e.g., EPIC vs. 450 K arrays) may also have led to partial or inconsistent coverage of relevant CpG sites across studies. Lastly, while peripheral blood is accessible and widely used, it may not fully capture tissue-specific methylation responses—particularly for pathways regulated in the nervous system or muscle. Thus, caution is warranted when interpreting results outside systemic contexts.

Conclusion

This systematic review indicates that while acute exercise produces limited and transient changes in peripheral blood DNA methylation, long-term interventions—especially those with sufficient frequency, duration, and intensity—can induce significant modifications, particularly in pathways related to metabolism, inflammation, and aging. These results support the potential of peripheral blood DNA methylation profiles to reflect systemic biological adaptations to exercise and serve as accessible biomarkers for evaluating exercise efficacy. Collectively, the evidence strengthens our understanding of how physical activity modulates the human epigenome and highlights the value of peripheral blood in exercise epigenetic research.
