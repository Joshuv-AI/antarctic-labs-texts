Classifying the molecular functions of transcription factors beyond activation and repression
Authors: Jinhong Dong, Michael J Guertin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
In this Outlook, Dong and Guertin discuss a study in this issue of Genes & Development by Rogers et al. that provides mechanistic insight into target gene activation by the transcription factor Notch. They highlight the importance of temporal and scalable approaches to study, at a genome-wide resolution, transcription factor function and interactions that control transcription.Keywords: ATAC-seq, NOTCH1, Notch signaling, PRO-seq, SWI/SNF, TT-seq

Gene regulation is intricate and complex. Each gene is controlled by sophisticated functional relationships between proximal and distal regulatory elements. These relationships change throughout development and in response to stimuli primarily because a cell's repertoire of active transcription factors (TFs) is dynamic. Comprehensive characterization of TF binding sites, accessible chromatin, transcription output, and three-dimensional chromatin interactions have identified the key TFs, cofactors, and genomic regions that potentially regulate the transcriptome (The ENCODE Project Consortium 2012). Although incredibly useful, we cannot accurately predict which TF binding events, physical interactions between proteins, and genetic variants are functional at a given time. Systematic editing and inhibition of enhancers directly test their function, but this is not scalable to the entire genome over many cell types and conditions (Gasperini et al. 2019). An alternative and tractable approach to study the complexity of gene regulation is to characterize the molecular functions of the transcription factors, as Rogers et al. (2024) do for the Notch signaling system.

Transcription factors such as the Notch transcriptional complex (NTC) can influence transcription by recruiting cofactors that facilitate chromatin remodeling, RNA polymerase initiation or termination, pause release, and/or elongation (Core and Adelman 2019). A significant challenge in studying transcription factor function is that traditional activation and depletion methods are prolonged, making it difficult to distinguish direct transcription factor targets from downstream consequences. Rogers et al. (2024) rapidly activate Notch signaling within an SC2 squamous carcinoma cell line that expresses constitutively active Notch1 only in the absence of a γ-secretase inhibitor (GSI). Rapid washout of the GSI in this system triggers Notch signaling, and a time course of metabolic labeling of newly synthesized RNA (TT-seq) identified the primary response genes. TF activation or perturbation typically results in the upregulation or downregulation of many genes. However, TFs such as the NTC act primarily as either activators or repressors, and their direct target genes are more proximal to TF binding sites. Only Notch-activated genes show enrichment for local NTC binding. By integrating kinetic transcriptome profiling upon acute Notch activation and genome-wide NTC localization, Rogers et al. (2024) directly define cis-regulatory targets of the NTC.

After the direct gene targets of a transcription factor are identified, examining local changes in chromatin accessibility and RNA polymerase distribution at target genes offers valuable insight into the transcription factor's specific molecular functions (Danko et al. 2013). Contrary to current models of the NTC's mechanism of gene regulation, Rogers et al. (2024) found minimal changes in chromatin accessibility upon Notch induction despite detecting robust changes in gene expression. Inhibition of SWI/SNF prior to Notch induction demonstrates that SWI/SNF is essential for maintaining chromatin accessibility at inducible Notch binding sites in the Notch-naïve state (Rogers et al. 2024). These observations support a more general mechanism in which the chromatin landscape and the presence of binding motifs predetermine the inducible binding of TFs (John et al. 2011). The relative change in RNA polymerase density at the promoter-proximal pause region, compared with the gene body, can be used to model whether a factor regulates transcriptional initiation and/or pause release (Dutta et al. 2023). Stimulation of pause release in a gene leads to a reduction in RNA polymerase density at the pause region and an increase in density within the gene body, resulting in a decreased pause index for the gene (Gilchrist et al. 2010). Rogers et al. (2024) capture the position of actively transcribing RNA polymerases before and after a time course of Notch activation. RNA polymerase density in the pause region decreased and gene body density increased at the earliest time points, indicating that the NTC directly regulates pause release.

Transcription factors are like any other protein in that they are highly specialized. The most basic functions of sequence-specific TFs are to bind DNA and recruit cofactors that specialize in modulating chromatin structure or the activity of RNA polymerase as it progresses through the transcription cycle. TFs prefer binding to consensus sequences, and deviating from the consensus tends to reduce binding. However, quantifying and predicting interactions between TFs and their protein binding partners is challenging because these interactions are often low affinity and transient. BioID technologies are a powerful tool to characterize potential regulatory cofactor partners of TFs (Branon et al. 2018). Cofactors like histone acetyltransferases, nucleosome remodelers, initiation factors, pause release factors, and elongation factors also specialize, so knowing the interaction partners informs on the molecular function. If a TF recruits P-TEFb, for example, then the TF regulates pause release. Rogers et al. (2024) determined the molecular function of the NTC in regulating pause release using a complementary and more scalable approach: Rapidly modulate a TF's activity and observe changes in RNA polymerase occupancy. Not all systems are amenable to acute activation, but rapidly inducible degron approaches level the playing field in studying TF function (Nabet et al. 2020). If tagging a TF does not disrupt its function, then ATAC-seq and genomic RNA polymerase profiling upon acute degradation can determine whether the TF affects chromatin accessibility, initiation, and/or pause release.

Only after knowing the molecular functions of TFs can we start to develop testable hypotheses for how TFs work together in time and space to regulate expression. For instance, knowing that two TFs compete for the same DNA region and stimulate initiation is essential to propose a mechanism by which the recruitment of an activator can lead to repression. In this scenario, the weaker activator would displace the stronger one, leading to less efficient transcription initiation. At another time in development, initiation at the gene may not be rate-limiting, so recruitment of more highly potent initiation-promoting TFs will not affect transcription output. A comprehensive understanding of gene regulatory systems requires a mechanistic knowledge of individual components so we can begin to develop testable models for how these components work together in a diversity of nuclear contexts.
