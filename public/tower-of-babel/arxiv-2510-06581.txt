Bo Li Fangxiao Wang Shiji Xing

Abstract
We study the fair allocation of indivisible chores among agents with asymmetric weights. Among the various fairness notions, weighted maximin share (WMMS) stands out as particularly compelling. However, whether WMMS admits a constant-factor approximation has remained unknown and is one of the important open problems in weighted fair division [ ALMW22 , Suk25 ] . So far, the best known approximation ratio is O ⁡ ( log ⁡ n ) O(\log n) , where n n is the number of agents. In this paper, we advance the state of the art and present the first constant-factor approximate WMMS algorithm. To this end, we introduce canonical instance reductions and different bounds of agents’ valuations. We also prove that guaranteeing better than 2 2 -approximation is not possible, which improves the best-known lower bound of 1.366.



1
Introduction

Fair allocation of indivisible items has recently attracted considerable interest across fields such as theoretical computer science, computational economics, and multi-agent systems.
This is partly driven by the practical applications where items cannot be divided and must be allocated whole, such as family heirlooms (like jewelry, paintings, and furniture) or office equipment (such as computers, chairs, and printers).
At the same time, traditional fairness concepts like envy-freeness (EF)
[
Fol67
,
Var73
]
and proportionality (PROP)
[
Ste48
]
are not always satisfiable with indivisible items, prompting extensive research into how these criteria can be approximately achieved.
This has led to the study of relaxed fairness notions, most notably envy-freeness up to one item (EF1)
[
Bud10
,
LMMS04
]
and the maximin share (MMS)
[
Bud10
]
, which are now widely accepted in the literature.

Informally, EF1 means that no agent would prefer another agent’s allocation over their own once a single item is removed either from her own or the other agent’s bundle.
It has been proven that an EF1 allocation always exists for both goods and chores, even when agents are asymmetric – that is, when they have different weights in receiving items
[
CISZ21
,
WZZ23
]
.
MMS fairness assigns each agent a threshold value, requiring that every agent receive a bundle at least as valuable as this threshold.
For symmetric agents (where all agents have equal weights), it is known that a constant-factor approximation of MMS fairness can be guaranteed for both goods and chores; see, for example,
[
KPW18
,
GHS+18
,
AG24
]
and
[
HL21
,
HS23
]
.
In the case of asymmetric agents, the best possible approximation ratio for MMS – referred to as WMMS – in the allocation of goods is
n
, where
n
is the number of agents
[
FGH+19
]
.
However, for chores in the asymmetric setting, the optimal approximation ratio for WMMS has remained an open question for a long time.
This is highlighted as one of the important open problems in the fair division of indivisible items, as noted in surveys
[
ALMW22
,
Suk25
]
.

This is not only an interesting theoretical problem; it also has real-world importance.
Wang et al.
[
WLL24
]
justified that, in the context of chore allocation, WMMS can provide a fairer allocation – in the sense of respecting agents’ weights – compared to other weighted fairness notions such as APS, WEF1, PROP1, and CS
[
FH23
,
WZZ23
,
LLW22
]
, in certain scenarios.
The reason is that all these fairness criteria can be satisfied if a single item is allocated to any agent, regardless of the item’s value or the agent’s weight.
For instance, consider a situation with a single chore and two agents, where one agent has a much larger weight than the other. According to these criteria, assigning the chore to the agent with the smaller weight would be considered fair.
However, intuitively, it would be more reasonable to allocate the chore to the agent with the larger weight.
WMMS captures this intuition, as in this example, the only WMMS allocation is to give the item to the agent with the larger weight.
In this way, WMMS better reflects the proportionality of agents’ weights.

Given the above discussion, designing a constant-approximate WMMS algorithm would be intriguing.
To this end, Aziz et al.
[
ACL19
]
first gave an
n
-approximate algorithm and proved that no algorithm can be better than
\frac{4}{3}
-approximate.
They also proved that if an
\alpha
-approximate WMMS allocation exists, then a
4\alpha
-approximate WMMS allocation can be found in polynomial time.
For the case of two agents,
Wang et al.
[
WLL24
]
gave an instance that does not admit better than
\frac{\sqrt{3}+1}{2}\approx 1.366
approximation and designed an algorithm with the matched approximation guarantee.
Thus, the problem is optimally solved for two agents.
For more than two agents, the same work presented two algorithms that are
O(\sqrt{n})
- and
O(\log n)
-approximation, but whether a constant-factor approximate WMMS allocation exists remains open.
In this paper, we answer this problem affirmatively and make progress on both fronts. We design an algorithm that guarantees a 12-approximate WMMS allocation, and prove that a 24-approximate WMMS allocation can be found in polynomial time.
On the negative side, we prove that for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists an instance, for which no allocation is better than
(2-\epsilon)
-WMMS.

Related Work

Fair division has been extensively studied since the seminal work by Steinhaus
[
Ste48
]
.
As proportionality (PROP)
[
Ste48
]
and envy-freeness (EF)
[
Fol67
,
Var73
]
may not be satisfied when items are not divisible, there is ongoing discussion about the most appropriate fairness concepts for such settings.
Several widely recognized and studied notions have emerged, including maximin share (MMS)
[

]
,
proportional up to one (PROP1)
[
CFS17
]
, proportional up to any (PROPX)
[

]
,
envy-free up to one (EF1)
[

,
LMMS04
]
, and envy-free up to any (EFX)
[
GMT14
,
CKM+19
]
.
Our work is most closely related to MMS fairness, which was originally introduced by Budish
[
Bud10
]
for goods and later extended to chores in
[
ARSW17
]
. When agents have equal weights, both goods and chores settings admit constant-factor approximations (see, for example,
[
KPW18
,
GHS+18
]
for goods, and
[
BK20
,
HL21
]
for chores).
The best-known approximation for goods is
\frac{3}{4}+\frac{3}{3836}

[
AG24
]
, and for chores, it is
\frac{13}{11}

[
HS23
]
.
For a more comprehensive overview of fair division with indivisible items, we refer readers to surveys
[
ABFV22
,
ALMW22
]
, and for a detailed discussion on chores, see
[
GLD23
]
.

Farhadi et al.
[
FGH+19
]
were the first to extend the concept of MMS to settings where agents have different weights.
For goods, they constructed an example showing that no allocation can achieve better than a
\frac{1}{n}
-approximate WMMS, and demonstrated that a simple round-robin algorithm guarantees this
\frac{1}{n}
-approximation.
Later, Aziz et al.
[
ACL19
]
adapted the WMMS concept to chore allocation and proved that the best possible approximation ratio lies between
\frac{4}{3}
and
n
.
More recently, Wang et al.
[
WLL24
]
improved both the lower and upper bounds by proving the existence of an
O(\log n)
-approximate WMMS allocation for any number of agents, and showing that the optimal approximation ratio for two agents is 1.366.
Our work builds on this line of research and further advances these bounds.

Other notions of weighted fairness have also been explored in the literature. Babaioff et al.
[
BEF24
]
introduced the AnyPrice Share (APS) as an alternative to MMS and developed a
\frac{3}{5}
-approximation algorithm for goods.
A 2-approximation algorithm for APS was presented in
[
BEF24
]
and
[
LLW22
]
, which was later improved to 1.733 by Feige and Huang
[

]

For goods, a weighted PROP1 allocation is always possible, but a weighted PROPX allocation may not exist
[
AMS20
]
.
In contrast, for chores, a weighted PROPX allocation (which also implies PROP1) always exists and can be efficiently computed
[
LLW22
]
. For both goods and chores, a weighted EF1 allocation can be computed in polynomial time
[
WZZ23
]
, but the existence of weighted EFX allocations is not guaranteed

SHY24
]
.

Very recently, Suksompong provided a survey of the latest developments and key open questions in the area of weighted fair division of indivisible items in

Definitions and Technical Overview

For any positive integer
k
, denote by
[k]=\{1,\ldots,k\}
.
We consider a fundamental fair allocation model by allocating
m
divisible chores
\mathcal{M}=\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{m}\}
to
n
agents
\mathcal{N}=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\}
.
Each agent
a_{i}
has a valuation function
v_{i}:2^{\mathcal{M}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}\cup\{0\}

v_{i}(S)
represents her cost of completing the items in
S\subseteq\mathcal{M}
.
For simplicity, when a bundle contains a single item
e
, denote
v_{i}(e)=v_{i}(\{e\})
.
We assume the cost functions are additive, i.e.,
v_{i}(S)=\sum_{e\in S}v_{i}(e)
for every
S\subseteq\mathcal{M}
.
We sometimes assume, without loss of generality, that the valuations are normalized, i.e.,
v_{i}(\mathcal{M})=1
for all agents
a_{i}
.
An allocation, denoted by
\mathbf{A}=(A_{1},\ldots,A_{n})
, is an
n
-partition of
\mathcal{M}
, where
A_{i}
is the set of items allocated to agent
a_{i}
,
such that
A_{1}\cup\cdots\cup A_{n}=\mathcal{M}
and
A_{i}\cap A_{j}=\emptyset
for all
i\neq j
.
Denote by
\mathcal{A}
the set of all allocations.
An allocation is called partial if
A_{1}\cup\cdots\cup A_{n}\subsetneq\mathcal{M}
.

2.1
Solution Concepts

We are interested in the setting when agents are asymmetric.
Formally, each agent
a_{i}
has a weight
0<w_{i}<1
, representing her share in completing all items.
Assume
w_{1}\geq w_{2}\geq\cdots\geq w_{n}
.
For simplicity, we normalize the agents’ weights such that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}=1
in the algorithms.
Letting
\mathbf{v}=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n})
and
\mathbf{w}=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{n})
, a chore allocation instance is represented by
\mathcal{I}=(\mathcal{N},\mathcal{M},\mathbf{w},\mathbf{v})
.
The items are expected to be allocated in a way that respects the agents’ weights.
Formally, an allocation
(A_{1},\ldots,A_{n})
is proportionally (PROP) fair if
v_{i}(A_{i})\leq w_{i}\cdot v_{i}(\mathcal{M})
every agent
a_{i}
.
However, achieving PROP is often impossible; for example, if a single item with non-zero cost must be allocated between two agents, whichever agent receives the item, the allocation will not be proportional for her.
Given that items may be indivisible, the fairest allocation – one that is as proportional to the agents’ weights as possible – for agent
a_{i}
is the one that minimizes the maximum ratio (aka unfairness ratio) of excess across all agents, i.e.,

\min_{\mathbf{A}\in\mathcal{A}}\max_{j\in[n]}\frac{v_{i}(A_{j})}{w_{j}}.

Then, the weighted maximin share (WMMS) of
a_{i}
is the cost of her weight times the ratio of the above excess, i.e.,

\mathsf{WMMS}_{i}=w_{i}\cdot\min_{\mathbf{A}\in\mathcal{A}}\max_{j\in[n]}\frac{v_{i}(A_{j})}{w_{j}}.

It is clear that
\mathsf{WMMS}_{i}\geq w_{i}\cdot v_{i}(\mathcal{M})
and thus relaxes the requirement of PROP.
It is worth noting that the definition of WMMS does not require the weights to be normalized.
A partition
\mathbf{A}=(A_{1},\ldots,A_{n})
is called a WMMS-defining partition for agent
a_{i}
, if for all
j\in[n]
,

\frac{v_{i}(A_{j})}{w_{j}}\leq\frac{\mathsf{WMMS}_{i}}{w_{i}}.

Definition 1

(
\alpha
-WMMS)
.

Given
\alpha\geq 1
, an allocation
\mathbf{A}=(A_{1},\ldots,A_{n})
is called
\alpha
-approximate WMMS fair (
\alpha
-WMMS) if

v_{i}(A_{i})\leq\alpha\cdot\mathsf{WMMS}_{i}
for all agents
a_{i}\in\mathcal{N}
.
If
\alpha=1
, the allocation is called WMMS fair.

We borrow the following example in
[
WLL24
]
to illustrate the definition of WMMS.

2.2
Technical Overview

Example
1
highlights one of the difficulties in approximating WMMS: allocating items to an agent with a very small weight can severely harm the approximation ratio.
This difficulty also explains why achieving constant-factor approximation for WMMS is more complex than for other fairness notions such as APS, CS, EF1, and PROP1. For these other notions, the fairness criteria are still met even if any single item is given to any agent, regardless of the item’s size or the agent’s weight.
To overcome this difficulty, Wang et al.
[
WLL24
]
proved that, for all
1\leq i<n
, each item after the index
\lfloor\frac{w_{1}}{w_{i+1}}\rfloor+\cdots+\lfloor\frac{w_{i}}{w_{i+1}}\rfloor
has value no more than
\mathsf{WMMS}_{i}
for agent
a_{i}
.
Based on this, their algorithm only allocates items after this index to
a_{i}
.
However, this approach results in agent
a_{1}
receiving more items and potentially incurring a cost as high as
O(\log n)\cdot\mathsf{WMMS}_{1}
.
To achieve a better approximation, it is necessary to begin allocating items to the other agents earlier. However, analyzing such an approach becomes technically challenging.

To this end, we propose a modularization framework and decompose the problem into several components – canonical instances, delegating agents, and proxy cost functions – that make the analysis more manageable.
We first introduce the concept of
canonical
instances. Informally, in a canonical instance, each agent’s weight and each item’s value are powers of
\frac{1}{2}
times
w_{1}
(the largest agent weight), and every agent’s WMMS is equal to her weight.
The structured properties of canonical instances greatly simplify the analysis, allowing us to effectively bound both the value and the number of items in our subsequent algorithm.
We show that reducing any arbitrary instance to a canonical one increases the approximation ratio by at most a factor of 4.

We then design an algorithm for canonical instances, which ensures 3-WMMS.
The algorithm processes items one at a time in decreasing order of value.
Each item can only be given to an agent for whom the item’s value does not exceed her WMMS; such agents are referred to as
active
agents.
If multiple active agents are available, the item is allocated to the agent with the smallest value for it. If there are multiple agents with the same smallest value for the item, then it is allocated to the agent with the smallest weight.
An agent
a_{i}
exits the algorithm (and becomes
inactive
) if her value for her allocated items is no smaller than
3\mathsf{WMMS}_{i}
.
Due to the properties of canonical instances, this value is exactly
3\mathsf{WMMS}_{1}
and cannot exceed it, ensuring the approximation guarantee for exiting agents.
The aforementioned tie-breaking rule plays a critical role: when an agent with a larger weight exits the algorithm, she must have been allocated a bundle of items whose value is very large for agents with smaller weights.
To prove the correctness of the algorithm, we need to prove that, for every item, there is always at least one active agent available, which is the most technically involved aspect of the algorithm.
In this regard, we introduce
delegating
agents and prove that the life window of all delegating agents can span all items via their
proxy cost
– an upper bound of their actual cost.

Taking into account the loss incurred during the reduction to canonical instances, our algorithm achieves a 12-WMMS guarantee.
Our result is primarily existential in nature. While the aforementioned 3-WMMS algorithm operates in polynomial time, the reduction to canonical instances does not, as it requires computing the WMMS partitions for the agents and may generate an exponential number of items.
To address this, we present an alternative linear programming algorithm that runs in polynomial time. We prove that, provided the instance admits an
\alpha
-WMMS allocation, our algorithm can compute a

(2\alpha+\epsilon)
-approximate WMMS allocation, where
\epsilon
is arbitrarily small. Our analysis leverages the rounding technique introduced in
[
ST93
]
and refines the analysis from
[
ACL19
]
.
Combining with our existential result, a
(24+\epsilon)
-WMMS allocation can be found in polynomial time.

Canonical Instances and Polynomial-time Implementation

In this section, we introduce canonical instances.
We show that any instance can be reduced to a canonical instance, with the WMMS approximation ratio increasing by at most a factor of 4.

(Canonical Instances)
.

An instance
\mathcal{I}=(\mathcal{N},\mathcal{M},\mathbf{w},\mathbf{v})

•

Normalized Weights:
w_{1}\geq w_{2}\geq\cdots\geq w_{n}

v_{i}(e_{1})\geq v_{i}(e_{2})\geq\cdots\geq v_{i}(e_{n})

\mathsf{WMMS}_{i}=w_{i}

Claim 2

Moreover, the claim indicates that

6
Conclusion and Discussion

In this paper, we give the first constant-factor approximate WMMS algorithm for indivisible chores
and prove that the best possible approximation ratio is between 2 and 12.
Closing this gap remains an intriguing open problem.
Our algorithm allows an agent to start receiving items only when the item’s value does not exceed her WMMS.
One possible direction for improvement is to permit agents to receive items even earlier, even if the item’s value is larger, potentially achieving a better balance between the value of individual items and the exit threshold.
However, implementing this approach would require extending the analysis to more general instances beyond the canonical ones.
The intuition behind this limitation is that, in canonical instances, any item with a value exceeding an agent’s WMMS is already at least twice as large as her WMMS.
Allocating two such items to an agent would result in an approximation ratio worse than what our algorithm achieves.

There are other interesting future research directions as well.

For example, the
O(\log n)
-approximate algorithm in
[
WLL24
]
is ordinal.
That is, their algorithm does not require the exact numerical values of the items.
Instead, it relies solely on each agent’s ranking of the items by value.
Ordinal algorithms are simple and easy to implement in practice.
In contrast, our algorithm is not ordinal and requires access to the precise values of all items.
It is unknown if the
O(\log n)
approximation ratio can be improved by ordinal algorithms.
This problem has been studied in the unweighted setting.
With ordinal valuations, Amanatidis et al.
[
ABM16
]
designed an
\Omega(\frac{1}{\log n})
-approximate MMS algorithm under ordinal valuations, which has been shown to be asymptotically optimal
[
HS21
]
.
For chores, constant-factor approximate MMS algorithms are given in
[
FH23
]
.

It is also interesting to consider the online setting, where items arrive dynamically over time. In the unweighted case, Zhou et al.
[
ZBW23
]
presented a
(2-\frac{1}{n})
-competitive algorithm under the total value assumption. Without this assumption, it is shown by Song et al.
[
STWZ25
]
and Seddighin and Seddighin
[
SS25
]
that no algorithm can achieve a competitive ratio better than
n
.
When agents have arbitrary weights, Wang et al.
[
WLL24
]
gave an algorithm that is
O(\sqrt{n})
-competitive under the total value assumption. However, whether this ratio can be further improved remains an open question.

Appendix B
Proof of Theorem
2

Proof of Theorem
2
.

The proof is almost identical to that in
[
ACL19
]
, which relies on the rounding technique of
[
ST93
]
.
The key distinction in our proof is the observation that the values of

\mathsf{WMMS}_{i}
can be computed via a PTAS, as shown in
[
HS88
]
.
Since the result in
[
ACL19
]
does not exactly focus on the
\alpha
-WMMS problem, we include a description of the algorithm below for completeness.

First, observe the following linear program:

\displaystyle\sum_{e_{j}\in\mathcal{M}}v_{i}(e_{j})\cdot x_{ij}

\displaystyle\leq\alpha\mathsf{WMMS}_{i},

\displaystyle\forall i\in[n]

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in[n]:v_{i}(e_{j})\leq\mathsf{WMMS}_{i}}x_{ij}

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle\forall j\in[m]

\displaystyle x_{ij}

\displaystyle\geq 0,

\displaystyle\forall i\in[n],j\in[m]

A feasible solution of the above linear program can be viewed as a fractional
\alpha
-WMMS allocation, where
x_{ij}
is the fraction of item
e_{j}
assigned to agent
i
.
Note that this allocation is valid since each item is fully allocated, and each agent can only get a non-negative fraction of an item.
Because there exists an integral
\alpha
-approximate WMMS allocation, this program has at least one feasible solution.
Then, we can find a basic feasible solution to this program and get an
\alpha
-WMMS fractional allocation in polynomial time.
By rounding this fractional solution to an integral one, we can obtain a feasible allocation for the instance. Note that this linear program is in the form of a generalized assignment problem; hence, we can apply standard rounding techniques
[
ST93
]
to find a
2\alpha
-WMMS allocation in polynomial time.

Nevertheless, although we can obtain an allocation in polynomial time from the above program, specifying the program itself proves difficult, because we need to know the WMMS value of each agent, which is intractable.

Finding the WMMS value for an agent
i
is equivalent to computing the minimal timespan on related machines in a job scheduling problem.
Each item
e\in\mathcal{M}
can be viewed as a job with workload
v_{i}(e)
, and each agent with weight
w
can be viewed as a machine with speed
w
.
The total workload of a machine is the sum of the workload of jobs assigned to this machine, divided by its speed.
The goal of this job scheduling problem is to assign all jobs to machines while minimizing the total workload of the busiest machine (the makespan).
If we use
A
to denote a job assignment (
A_{j}
to denote the set of jobs assigned to machine
j
) and use
\mathcal{A}
to denote the set of all possible job assignments, finding the schedule with the minimal makespan is to find
\min_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\max_{j\in[n]}v_{i}(A_{j})
, which happens to be
\mathsf{WMMS}_{i}
. This scheduling problem is known to be NP-Complete.

Fortunately, this problem possesses a polynomial-time approximation scheme (PTAS)
[
HS88
]
. Specifically, for an arbitrarily small
\epsilon>0
, one can find, in polynomial time, an
(1+\epsilon)
-approximation of the minimal makespan, which equals an
(1+\epsilon)
-approximation of
\mathsf{WMMS}_{i}
. Consequently, given a fixed parameter
\epsilon
, one can specify and solve the following linear program in polynomial time:

\displaystyle\sum_{e_{j}\in\mathcal{M}}v_{i}(e_{j})\cdot x_{ij}

\displaystyle\leq(1+\epsilon)\alpha\mathsf{WMMS}_{i},

\displaystyle\forall i\in[n]

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in[n]:v_{i}(e_{j})\leq(1+\epsilon)\mathsf{WMMS}_{i}}x_{ij}

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle\forall j\in[m]

\displaystyle x_{ij}

\displaystyle\geq 0,

\displaystyle\forall i\in[n],j\in[m]

Therefore, by rounding the solution of this program, we can find a
(2\alpha+\epsilon)
-WMMS allocation for any instance in polynomial time.

∎

, one can find a
