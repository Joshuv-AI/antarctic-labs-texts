A combined deep learning framework for mammalian m6A site prediction
Authors: Rui Fan, Chunmei Cui, Boming Kang, Zecheng Chang, Guoqing Wang, Qinghua Cui
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryN6-methyladenosine (m6A) is the most prevalent chemical modification in eukaryotic mRNAs and plays key roles in diverse cellular processes. Precise localization of m6A sites is thus critical for characterizing the functional roles of m6A in various conditions and dissecting the mechanisms governing its deposition. Here, we design a combined framework of Transformer architecture and recurrent neural network, deepSRAMP, to identify m6A sites using sequence-based and genome-derived features. As a result, deepSRAMP achieves a notably enhanced performance compared to its predecessor, SRAMP, the most-used predictor in this field. Moreover, based on multiple benchmark datasets, deepSRAMP greatly outperforms other state-of-the-art m6A predictors, including WHISTLE and DeepPromise, with an average 16.1% and 18.3% increase in AUROC and a 43.9% and 46.4% increase in AUPRC. Finally, deepSRAMP can be successfully exploited on mammalian m6A epitranscriptome mapping under diverse cellular conditions and can potentially reveal differential m6A sites among transcript isoforms of individual genes.

Introduction

Hundreds of types of chemical modifications have been reported to deposit on various RNA species1 and further regulate RNA fate, among which N6-methylation of adenosine (m6A) is the most widespread in messenger RNAs (mRNAs) of mammals. It is estimated that one-fourth of human genes harbor at least one m6A site, and the proportion rises to 60% when removing genes with low expression.2,3 Compared with other RNA modifications, m6A exhibits a distinct dynamic regulation process, which can be installed on RNAs by the complex of METTL3/METTL144 and WTAP5 and can be wiped by m6A demethylases, including the fat mass and obesity-associated (FTO) gene6 and ALKBH5.7 More importantly, the regulation roles of m6A are subjected to the activity of m6A “reader” proteins such as members of the YTH family8 and heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoproteins.9 The impacts of m6A modification on almost all processes of RNA metabolism, including pre-mRNA processing, nuclear export, mRNA stability, and translation, are gradually revealed,10,11 and accordingly, m6A modification is widely implicated in major physiological processes, such as embryo development and differentiation, and pathophysiological conditions, including cancers, cardiovascular diseases, and diabetes mellitus.12,13,14 Given their important roles in diverse biological processes, precise detection of the transcriptome-wide m6A sites is thus critical.

Combined with next-generation sequencing technologies, a variety of strategies using anti-m6A antibodies (MeRIP-seq2,15 and miCLIP16,17), m6A-specific chemistries (m6A-label-seq18), or m6A-sensitive endonucleases (m6A-REF-seq19 and MAZTER-seq20) have enabled the delineation of the m6A landscape. These approaches, especially the ones achieving single-nucleotide resolution, have advanced the exploration of diverse roles of m6A. Meanwhile, the increase in these experimental techniques paves the way for enhancing computational transcriptome-wide m6A mapping, which helps overcome several limitations of experimental techniques, such as labor intensity and complexity of sample preparation. Vast assays of computational tools for identifying m6A sites on mRNAs have been established across different species. Our framework, SRAMP,21 is one of the earliest and most-used tools for predicting mammalian m6A sites at the single-nucleotide level, which is solely dependent on the RNA sequences. Subsequently, multitudinous m6A predictors incorporating genomic features or fusing deep learning techniques, such as WHISTLE,22 Gene2Vec,23 and DeepPromise,24 were proposed. The preference of m6A deposition, such as enrichment in 3′ UTR and near to stop codons,15 makes the genome-derived features advantageous for predicting m6A sites. In addition, deep learning methods are capable of extracting spatial and contextual information from RNA sequences and, as such, usually outperform conventional machine learning methods with manually curated features. While incorporating genome position features enhances prediction accuracy, it reduces the flexibility and ease of use of predictors, making real-time prediction of modification sites on an individual RNA sequence unfeasible. Moreover, when a given site can be mapped on multiple transcript isoforms, existing methods struggle to identify which specific transcript undergoes modification. It is necessary to develop a high-performance algorithm for real-time prediction of m6A sites, especially for isoforms of individual genes, which would help elucidate the association between m6A modification and alternative splicing25 or alternative polyadenylation events.26

Recently, a powerful deep neural network framework called Transformer27 was presented, and it can capture the interconnection of nucleotides alongside the RNA sequences and learn global representations of sequences. This approach has produced tremendous advantages in multiple life science areas, including prediction for RNA two- and three-dimensional structures,28 CRISPR-Cas9 guide RNA cleavage efficiency,29 and noncoding RNA-disease association.30,31 Undoubtedly, the application of the Transformer architecture will enhance the prediction performance of m6A sites.

In this study, we developed an m6A site predictor called deepSRAMP, which integrates sequence-based and genome-derived features into the combination structure of a Transformer encoder and a recurrent neural network (RNN) for learning contextual representatives of proximal regions around m6A sites. More importantly, deepSRAMP incorporates an attention mechanism to learn the weights of multiple transcript isoforms at a given site, thereby enhancing the prediction of the isoform-level m6A modification. deepSRAMP demonstrates extraordinary performance compared to both its predecessor SRAMP and other state-of-the-art m6A predictors. Furthermore, promising results are found when extending deepSRAMP to larger datasets covering 38 mammalian tissues and cells from the m6A-Atlas v2.0 database,32 achieving an average area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC) of 0.648, 0.684, and 0.639 for human, mouse, and rat, respectively. Interpreting the model also reveals the impact of genome position information and preferences of m6A deposition. Finally, deepSRAMP is successfully employed to explore m6A sites on multiple isoforms of an individual gene, and a user-friendly online web server is freely accessible at http://www.cuilab.cn/deepsramp.

Results

Construction of deepSRAMP

Figure 1A shows the architecture of deepSRAMP. The prediction model comprises two modules—the sequence module and the genome position module—which separately generate RNA sequence embedding and genomic feature embedding. In particular, the sequence embedding is fused by a combination of one-hot encoding and RNA word embedding. Then, the results of the two modules are merged and fed to a Transformer block for learning global context information through a self-attention method. Subsequently, an RNN (here, we used a bidirectional gated recurrent unit [BiGRU]) is employed to capture sequential dependencies within the input sequence. Considering that numerous sites can be aligned to multiple transcript isoforms, we pool isoform-level m6A modification probabilities at a given site by the attention mechanism to output the final probability that the corresponding site is modified. To validate the contribution of this hybrid architecture to m6A site prediction, we employed the same training and testing datasets that are used in SRAMP. We focused mainly on delineating the m6A predictor on the mature transcript mode. As SRAMP only used sequence-derived features, we separately retained the sequence module and the genome position module in the prediction model to assess contributions of the deep neural network architecture and the genome-derived features. The process also could directly document the importance of two types of features. As shown in Figure 1B, the deep neural network frameworks based on sequence features or genomic features both achieved improved performance compared to SRAMP. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) between the sequence-based predictive model and the genome-based predictive model could be observed, with 4.6% and 10.6% increases, respectively, compared to SRAMP. Regarding the AUPRC, a popular and effective metric when the dataset is heavily unbalanced, the only sequence-based and the only genome-based model likewise showed 2.4% and 18.5% higher than SRAMP, respectively (Figure 1C). Combined with these two feature types, deepSRAMP exhibited a more significantly improved AUROC of 0.943 and AUPRC of 0.624, with 15.0% and 30.9% increases compared to SRAMP, respectively. Similar to deepSRAMP on the mature transcript mode, the one on the full transcript mode also greatly outperformed SRAMP, as shown in Figure S1.

Furthermore, to comprehensively understand this hybrid framework, we conducted an ablation study by removing the Transformer encoder and the BiGRU component individually. A significant performance difference could be observed between the model with an individual component and deepSRAMP, particularly when the BiGRU component was removed, resulting in a 10.1% decrease in AUROC and 26.7% in AUPRC (Figure S2). These results initially demonstrated the efficiency of our presented architecture on learning representatives of RNA sequence and genome position encoding information to improve the RNA modification prediction.

Comparison of deepSRAMP with other m6A predictors

There are many methods of m6A site prediction that display excellent performance. For example, Chen et al.24 presented a neural network of four convolution layers based on three commonly used sequence encodings called DeepPromise, which stands out from multiple deep-learning-based m6A predictors. An m6A predictor called WHISTLE22 employed sequence-based features and 35 genome-derived features to train a support vector machine (SVM) model and achieved a promising accuracy. Therefore, to fairly compare the performance between deepSRAMP and other state-of-the-art methods, the same datasets as those of WHISTLE were employed to re-construct the approaches mentioned above. Furthermore, since these two predictors only retain a transcript sample when a given site aligns with multiple transcript isoforms, we trained two models—that is, deepSRAMP using data encompassing all transcript isoforms and deepSRAMPsingle based on data keeping a transcript sample at any given site, to ensure fair comparison. The detailed processes for reproduction of WHISTLE and DeepPromise are described in the method details section. Additionally, a cross-validation test across data from different cell lines was executed for a robust assessment. As shown in Figure 2A, WHISTLE introducing genome-derived features exhibited a higher AUROC value than DeepPromise using solely sequence features with a deep learning model (average AUROC: 0.805 vs. 0.783), both of which underperformed in comparison to deepSRAMPsingle, with an average AUROC of 0.955. Notably, deepSRAMP showed an average AUROC value of 0.966, reflecting a 16.1% improvement over WHISTLE. Meanwhile, deepSRAMP also surpassed the other predictors in sensitivity at 0.872, AUPRC at 0.814, and F1 score at 0.707, where these metrics are at 0.825, 0.762, and 0.673 for deepSRAMPsingle; 0.753, 0.375, and 0.406 for WHISTLE; and 0.693, 0.350, and 0.386 for DeepPromise (Figure 2B).Figure 2Comparison of deepSRAMP with WHISTLE and DeepPromise on multiple m6A datasets(A) Performance of the four predictors on m6A datasets from six cell lines based on a cross-validation test. deepSRAMPsingle uses the same data as WHISTLE and DeepPromise, solely retaining a transcript isoform when a given site maps to multiple transcript isoforms.(B) Comparison of average sensitivity, specificity, precision, accuracy, F1, Matthews coefficient (MCC), and AUPRC on six datasets between deepSRAMP and the other two predictors. To further validate the performance of deepSRAMP, we used the binding sites of YTHDF1/2, two established m6A readers that specifically recognize m6A modifiable regions, which were collected and organized by SRAMP. Figures 2C and 2D showed that deepSRAMP (AUROC = 0.967, AUPRC = 0.746) greatly outperformed deepSRAMPsingle (AUROC = 0.953, AUPRC = 0.673), as well as DeepPromise (AUROC = 0.739, AUPRC = 0.228) and WHISTLE (AUROC = 0.782, AUPRC = 0.263), in identifying the YTHDF binding sites from RRACH motifs. Meanwhile, deepSRAMP achieved 24.7% and 49.5% increases for AUROC and AUPRC, respectively, over the baseline of SRAMP, with an AUROC of 0.720 and an AUPRC of 0.251. These results show that deepSRAMP displays superior performance on diverse datasets, suggesting the effectiveness of incorporating the proximal sequence and relative position information of sites on the corresponding transcript and complex network structure.

Generalization performance of deepSRAMP

Abundant techniques have been proposed to carry out m6A profiling at the single-nucleotide level across mammal tissues and cells. To assess the generalization of deepSRAMP, we took advantage of data derived from m6A-Atlas v2.0, which is an m6A epitranscriptome database that collects more than 200,000 m6A sites from Homo sapiens, Mus musculus, and Rattus norvegicus across 25 cell lines and 13 tissues based on 12 techniques. On the whole, the AUPRC values range from 0.379 to 0.869 across multiple datasets of human cells, and the average AUPRC is 0.659 (Figure 3A; Table S1). deepSRAMP still achieved an average AUPRC of 0.648 when training datasets were removed. We also noted several relatively low AUPRC scores, such as 0.379 in hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells with the m6A-SAC-seq technique and 0.401 in HEK293T with the MAZTER-seq technique, which might be attributed to technique-specific biases or laboratory biases. It was reported that the similarity of methylation profiles within the same cell type is lower than that between different cell types from the same experimental batch, based on MeRIP-seq datasets.33 As shown in Figures 3B and 3C, we observed an average AUPRC of 0.658 and 0.624 for mouse and rat tissues, respectively. The datasets from rat tissues were entirely blind to our model. In addition, WHISTLE and DeepPromise were applied to this dataset, resulting in average AUPRC values of 0.164 and 0.143, respectively, for human cell lines (Table S1), which were significantly lower than those achieved by deepSRAMP. The results suggest the superior generalization power of deepSRAMP to predict m6A sites on mammalian mRNAs.Figure 3Generalization power of deepSRAMP on the data from m6A-Atlas v2.0The AUPRC values of deepSRAMP on the m6A modification datasets of (A) human tissues and cells, (B) mouse tissues and cells, and (C) rat tissues.

Interpretation of deepSRAMP

To better understand how input influences the prediction result and determine which features are critical for m6A site prediction, we employed SHAP, an explainable artificial intelligence method, to evaluate the contribution of individual features. The importance scores and directions of the top 20 genomic features were shown in Figure 4A. We first observed that 7 of the 20 features were the splicing site flag at certain positions. Interestingly, non-splicing sites at these positions were associated with an increased probability of the central site being an m6A modification site. It is worth noting that these non-splice junction positions were located within a 100-nt window of the modified site, indicating that splicing sites tend to be at least 100 nt away from the modified site. This result agrees with a recent study suggesting that m6A deposition is excluded from splice-site proximity around 100 nt due to the occlusion of the exon junction complex.34 Except for the features representing the splicing site flag, the remaining important genome-derived features focused on the positions near to the m6A site. To be specific, a high “exon length at −1” had a positive impact on being an m6A site, suggesting that the site within longer exons is more likely to be predicted as modifiable. The sites with a higher “dist to stop codon at −1” and “dist to stop codon at 2” tended to be predicted as m6A sites, demonstrating that the distance to stop codon contributes to the m6A site prediction. A greater “dist to stop codon” represented the site located in the vicinity of the stop codon or 3′ UTR. This was completely consistent with the fact that the m6A site preferred to be located in proximity to the stop codon and in the 3′ UTR. For the type of region close to the site, we found that the 5′ end direction of a m6A site (“CDS at −1”) tended to be a non-coding region and that the 3′ end direction of a m6A site preferred to remain a coding region (“CDS at 1” and “CDS at 2”), in line with previously recognized patterns of CDS/3′ UTR enrichment of m6A sites.2,15 Apart from the above match to existing knowledge, the feature importance plot also revealed that sites far from the 5′ end splicing sites and closer to the 3′ end splicing sites tended to be assigned as m6A sites, hinting that the m6A modification is more likely to deposit near the 3′ end side rather than the 5′ end. Existing high-throughput techniques locating m6A sites at the gene level make it challenging to determine which RNA isoform harbors the given m6A sites. They also hinder the association between m6A sites and their biological functions. Despite the high sequence similarity near an m6A site across different isoforms, its relative location varies at the transcript isoform level. Accordingly, the introduction of genome position information in deepSRAMP enabled the identification of isoform-level m6A modification. The CALM3 gene encoded a member of a protein family that binds calcium35 and regulates cell-cycle and cytokinesis processes. The differential polyadenine length was revealed between three isoforms of CALM3 in the human brain through direct RNA sequencing (DRS).36 We predicted the m6A sites on these three transcripts (ENST00000391918, ENST00000486500, and ENST00000291295) using deepSRAMP. Then, we found that six sites were predicted by the modified one with a stringent threshold in the ENST00000486500 and ENST00000291295 transcripts, while only one site was found in the ENST00000391918 isoform. The differential m6A sites were located on the last exon of the transcripts ENST00000486500 and ENST00000291295, which were not present in ENST00000391918 due to alternative splicing (Figure 4B). Of the predicted m6A sites, five out of six were validated by experimental approaches, while the one remaining was predicted with high probability to be an m6A site. Specifically, the position at 46609857 on chromosome 19 showed a probability of 0.94 of being an m6A site in both ENST00000291295 and ENST00000486500. These differential m6A sites might be involved in the alternative polyadenine process and led to the significantly differentiated polyadenine length between ENST00000391918 and the other two isoforms. The result shows the potential of deepSRAMP in locating m6A sites at the isoform level.

Discussion

In this study, we present a single-nucleotide-resolution m6A predictor, deepSRAMP, which exhibits a markedly improved performance compared to its predecessor, SRAMP, by introducing genome-derived features and a combined deep learning framework. Moreover, with benchmark datasets, deepSRAMP greatly outperforms other representative m6A predictors. On the one hand, deep neural networks can recover underlying sequence motifs, even when only two basic sequence encoding strategies are utilized. On the other hand, genome position features provide a fresh perspective for detecting m6A sites. The combination of these features with RNA sequence information has been demonstrated to improve not only the identification of modification sites but also the prediction of other functional regions on RNAs, such as RNA-binding protein (RBP) binding sites.37

A convenient deepSRAMP online server would facilitate comprehensively characterizing the function and importance of m6A modification in various contexts. At present, there remains a shortage of user-friendly web servers capable of precisely localizing m6A sites on any given RNA sequence, despite the advancements in deep-learning-based m6A classifiers. It is critically necessary to pack the neural network model into a convenient interface for the scientific community. The inclusion of genome-derived features will compromise the feasibility of predictors to some degree, potentially limiting the online server or tool usage. Therefore, the classic sequence similarity search tool BLAST38 has been integrated into the deepSRAMP online server to facilitate the automatic extraction of genomic features, ensuring extraordinary predictive performance while simplifying user input.

It can be observed that the performance of deepSRAMP displays high variability across tissues and experimental technologies. Each of these technologies has its advantages and vulnerabilities. For example, MAZTER-seq is used to detect 16%–25% of m6A sites out of all modified sites, which demonstrates the severe underestimation at m6A sites of antibody-based methods.20 Gold standard data validated by the orthogonal techniques are urgently demanded and can help estimate the performance of computational approaches objectively. Performance disparities across specific tissues are probably due to dynamic regulation in the m6A modification with biological variability. In the future, two primary strategies will contribute to enhancing the performance of m6A predictors under specific conditions. One needs to integrate other omics data, such as gene expression data and RBP or transcription factor activities, to characterize the status of a specific circumstance because m6A deposition is governed by highly variable molecular mechanisms associated with cellular contexts. Linking the omics data to the cellular representatives can be implemented by the advanced deep neural network. The other is utilizing DRS technologies, which are capable of detecting modification sites of a native RNA. Current approaches based on DRS data for mapping m6A sites present a trade-off between precision and sensitivity. Introducing novel features to existing tools based on DRS data, for instance, the same representatives as deepSRAMP, including RNA sequences around m6A sites and genome position information, might further improve the transcriptome-wide mapping of m6A RNA modification.

Limitations of the study

Detecting differential isoform-level m6A modifications remains an essential yet underexplored area. deepSRAMP integrates all features from multiple transcript isoforms at a given site, resulting in an improved performance in predicting m6A sites. Although deepSRAMP can provide the m6A modification probability for any site on a transcript isoform, further high-confidence orthogonal techniques are necessary to confirm the authenticity of isoform-specific m6A sites and to elucidate their underlying functions.

Method details

Dataset preparation and processing

We first obtain the training set and testing set supplied by SRAMP to evaluate the prediction ability of our presented novel framework. The data used in SRAMP originate from GEO: GSE6375317 and GSE71154.39 And 41,808 m6A sites out of 461,488 sites and 10,791 m6A sites out of 119,241 sites are contained in the training set and testing set, respectively. In addition, 10% of the training set is assigned as the validation set to determine hyper-parameters of the model.

Next, to compare deepSRAMP with other state-of-the-art m6A predictors, the same datasets as that utilized in WHISTLE are downloaded, including GEO: GSE63753,17 GSE71154,39 GSE86336,40 and GSE98623,41 with six single-nucleotide-resolution m6A experiments covering five human cell lines. Then all m6A sites from different experiments are aligned to transcriptome using GRCh37 Ensembl annotations (version 87). The strategy of generating positive and negative samples is similar to that of SRAMP, which uses a stricter approach to generate negative samples. Briefly, the m6A sites conforming to RRACH motif are assigned as positive samples, and the other sites with RRACH motif from the same transcript are regarded as negative samples, where 10-fold as much negative samples as positive samples are randomly picked on each cell line. Considering that the randomly selected negative sites on one cell line might be methylated on other cell lines, we remove those samples that appear simultaneously at positive and negative sets between different cell lines from negative sets, resulting in 52,909 positive samples and 527,180 negative samples in total. For the performance evaluation between multiple m6A prediction tools, we adopt an approximate 6-fold cross-validation test, that is, five datasets are used to train predictive models and the remaining one is regarded as the testing dataset for each iteration. Moreover, overlapped samples between the training set and the testing set are deleted from the testing set to ensure its data are blind to the trained model. Besides, the YTHDF binding data as an independent dataset is also used to compare the performance between m6A predictors, which was also organized by SRAMP. Here we only keep the sites that adhere to the RRACH motif. The number of samples on the datasets mentioned above is listed in Table S2.

To further evaluate the generalization of our presented model, we download more m6A epitranscriptome profiles under specific cellular conditions based on diverse m6A detection techniques from m6A-Atlas v2.0.32 And 133,828, 95,547, and 3,954 m6A sites are separately obtained from human, mouse, and rat cells. Likewise, negative samples that are ten times of m6A sites and adhere to RRACH motif are randomly extracted.

Feature encodings

Sequence-based encoding

Various sequence encodings have been presented to describe the DNA or RNA sequences, however, most of them need to be manually extracted. The deep learning approaches have the enormous potential to automatically learn the representatives of RNA sequences. Two encoding schemes to denote sequence features are included, i.e., one-hot encoding and RNA embedding, which are free from the redundant preprocess step. One-hot encoding converts each nucleotide type (i.e., A, G, C, U, N, and gap) into a unique binary vector, and thus transforms a sequence of length L centered around the modification site into an L × 6-dimension vector. As for RNA embedding encoding, it assigns each nucleotide as a unique word and allocates an integer index for each word. The input sequence is encoded using the integer index; and then the integer sequence is transferred into a dense matrix with an L×n size by the ‘embedding layer’ of the model, where n is the dimension of word embedding. In addition, the length of the sequence centered around the modification site is a hyper-parameter and is determined using the validation set.

Genome-based encoding

The installation of m6A modification has a marked regional bias across the transcriptome, therefore, genome position-related features are certainly beneficial for the identification of m6A sites. Here, we extract the absolute and relative positions for each nucleotide of the input sequence, including the exact position on the transcript, the status of whether the site is located on the coding region, the status of whether the site is a splicing junction, the distance to the start codon normalized by the length of each transcript, the normalized distance to the stop codon, the normalized distance to the upstream (5′ end side) splicing site, the normalized distance to the downstream (3′ end side) splicing site, the length of the located exon, and the rank of the located exon normalized by the total number of exons in each transcript. A higher normalized exon rank indicates a site closer to the 3′ end of the transcript. These features are adequate to describe the position information of each site on the corresponding transcript. Moreover, it is reported that m6A sites are inclined to be clustered, accordingly, the distance to the upstream nearest m6A site, the distance to the downstream nearest m6A sites, and the status of m6A modification are introduced in the genome-based encoding. Model design

The model consists of two modules to process the sequence-based features and the genomic features, respectively. Then the merged features from the sequence module and the genome position module are fed into a Transformer encoder and a bidirectional gated recurrent network (BiGRU) to obtain a high-dimension representation for each site. Here, it is worth noting that 65.7% of sites can be aligned to multiple isoform transcripts in the training dataset from SRAMP. Integrating variation in isoform-level features might help address the limitation of identifying isoform-level m6A modification sites. Next fully connected layers are introduced to learn the weights of isoform transcripts at a given site before predicting the isoform-level m6A site probabilities, and attention pooling is used to output the final probability of being an m6A site (Figure 1A). More specifically, in the sequence module, two 1D convolution layers, with 3 and 5 filters respectively, are followed by the one-hot encoding to improve the limitations of its sparse and relatively low-dimension space. For the genome position module, a linear transformation is applied to keep the raw region feature. Then we concatenate the three embeddings, that is, two sequence embeddings and one genome position embedding, and then input the fused embedding to a Transformer block that can effectively capture the global dependencies in the input sequence, with a dropout of 0.2. Besides, a positional encoding is added into the above-merged embedding before passing through the Transformer block. After capturing global context features by the Transformer block, a BiGRU is assembled to further extract representations of m6A sites. The fully connected layer (64, 16, and 8 neuron units) with GELU activation flatten the representation and a linear layer followed by a sigmoid activation function to output the site probability on each isoform transcript and the corresponding weight. Finally, all weights of isoform transcripts at a given site are normalized by a softmax function, and then a weighting sum of all isoform-level site probabilities is calculated as the final probability that the site is modified.

Model training and parameter selection

With a batch size of 256, the model was trained using an Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of 1E−3 to optimize binary cross entropy loss. A total of 31 epochs was trained to prevent the risk of overfitting with a cosine annealing warm restarts learning rate scheduler. The nucleotide length of the flanking window around m6A sites is important for the balance between the computational load and the predictive performance. We tested multiple window sizes ranging from 201nt to 1401nt with a step of 200 and selected the one achieving a balance between predictive performance and mutation load on the validation test, details shown in Figure S3. Here, the window site of 801nt is determined. As for other hyper-parameters, we used a grid search framework to obtain the optimal combination, and detailed parameters of the model are shown in Table S3.

deepSRAMP performance evaluation

Two modes on m6A site prediction for full transcripts and for mature transcripts are separately trained. Given that performing in mature transcript mode remains more challenging than that of full transcript mode, we focus on showing the results of the prediction model on the former data in this study, and the model trained for full transcript also can be utilized in the deepSRAMP online server.

For comprehensively evaluating the performance of deepSRAMP, we separately establish multiple models under different comparison tasks, including (1) the model trained with the dataset derived from SRMAP to compare the performance between SRAMP and deepSRAMP; (2) the model based on 6-fold cross-validation strategy with the same datasets as WHISTLE to compare the performance of deepSRAMP with the congeneric predictors; (3) the model trained with six datasets above to evaluate the performance on the independent dataset (i.e., YTHDF binding sites). And the generalization of deepSRAMP on mammalian tissues and cells is demonstrated using the first model. In addition, the metrics for performance evaluation contains the AUROC, AUPRC, sensitivity, specificity, and F1-score.

Comparison with DeepPromise and WHISTLE

To fairly compare with other state-of-the-art m6A predictors, we selected the same m6A datasets with WHISTLE and reproduced DeepPromise and WHISTLE approaches. To be specific, DeepPromise uses three sequence encoding features, including one-hot encoding, enhanced nucleic acid composition, and RNA embedding, as input to four convolution blocks. It needs to train three CNN models based on those three sequence encodings and employs a weighted sum to integrate the three outputs. Among the models, all hyper-parameters are determined based on our validation set. For the WHISTLE model, we utilize the script provided by the authors to obtain the features used in WHISTLE (http://180.208.58.19/whistle/download/method.zip) and construct the SVM model. Here, since these two predictors are developed without accounting for multiple instances where an m6A site maps to multiple isoform transcripts, we select the longest transcript at a given site for WHISTLE and DeepPromise. Additionally, our neural network is also re-trained using the same data only keeping an individual transcript isoform for those sites with isoform ambiguity, named deepSRAMPsingle.

Interpretation of deepSRAMP

Except for precisely identifying the m6A sites, it is equally critical to interpret how input features contribute to localizing the m6A sites. Here, we used gradient shapely additive explanations (SHAP) to calculate how much each feature contributes to the prediction output.42,43 Inspired by the integrated gradients (IG) method, the gradient SHAP explains the model using the expected gradients based on an extension of classic game-theoretic Shapley values and is more appropriate for the interpretation of deep neural networks. Here, we apply the gradient SHAP on the positive samples of the testing set from SRAMP. By an SHAP beeswarm plot and a bar plot, we can observe the influence of each feature on the output for each sample and the global contributions of each genomic feature. In the beeswarm plot, SHAP values are normalized by the standard deviation of each feature due to the large variability between features.

To investigate the capability of deepSRAMP in identifying isoform-level m6A modifications, we apply deepSRAMP to three CALM3 isoform transcripts to predict their modification sites. Then we select a high-specificity threshold with the probability of 0.90, corresponding to a 95% specificity rate, to label the positive site, which can reduce the occurrence of false-positive results.

Construction of the deepSRAMP web server

The deepSRAMP web server integrates the models trained by the dataset derived from SRAMP on full transcript mode and mature transcript mode, which can be used to predict m6A sites on mammalian mRNAs. To simplify the usage of deepSRAMP, the BLAST38 search strategy is deployed on the online server to extract the genome position information of input sequences. Hence, only corresponding sequences are required for users when they want to detect m6A modification on a specific site or sequence. The online tool is built with the ‘Linux+Apache+Django’ framework and is freely accessed at http://www.cuilab.cn/deepsramp. Furthermore, we build a standalone Python application integrating the trained model for large-scale prediction of m6A modification sites locally, which can be obtained at http://www.cuilab.cn/deepsramp.
