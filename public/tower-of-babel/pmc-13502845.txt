Esophageal Sonography: A Review of the Utility of Ultrasound in Clinical Esophagology
Authors: Daniel L. Cohen, Ayelet Wandel, Alla Khashper, Haim Shirin, Vered Richter
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The utility and availability of ultrasound (US) are increasing. While US has traditionally not been performed on the esophagus, as the entire length of the esophagus cannot be optimally viewed, it does allow for visualization of the cervical and intra-abdominal esophagus. There is growing evidence that esophageal US can be useful in clinical esophagology. This review summarizes the current knowledge concerning esophageal US in various esophageal disorders. Studies of patients with achalasia have shown that US can distinguish patients with achalasia from health controls and patients with esophagogastric cancer. US may be helpful as a screening test for esophageal varices in cirrhotic patients as esophageal wall thickness has been shown to correlate with the presence of varices. US has also been shown to identify the presence of esophageal foreign bodies in case reports in both the adult and pediatric population. In infants, numerous studies have shown its utility in diagnosing gastroesophageal reflux disease and its correlation with pH study results, although the data in adults are less convincing. Taken together, these results suggest that there is a role for US—a noninvasive, cost-effective, and widely available test—in evaluating esophageal disorders. Despite these promising results, it does not seem that esophageal US is frequently used in clinical practice. Barriers to its implementation, including the availability of US and the training required, are discussed, along with the lack of standardized US protocols, equipment, and normal values. Future high-quality studies on the utility of US in esophageal disorders are warranted and will accelerate the implementation of this promising technology.

INTRODUCTION

Ultrasound (US) is useful for evaluating different parts of the human anatomy. However, its utility in evaluating the esophagus and role in esophageal diseases has traditionally been limited because of its inability to easily visualization the entire length of the esophagus. Despite this, there is growing evidence that US of the esophagus can be successfully performed and may be helpful in numerous esophageal disorders.

The use of US is increasing and evolving. Part of this is due to the increased use of point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS). POCUS is the use of a portable US by a nonradiologist physician to evaluate a patient at bedside (1). Although frequently used by emergency room physicians, obstetricians, and orthopedists, it can be used in many gastrointestinal conditions (2).

In addition to POCUS, intestinal ultrasound (IUS) is also rapidly becoming more common. IUS is performed to evaluate and monitor patients with inflammatory bowel disease (3–5). As IUS is performed by a gastroenterologist, it has increased the knowledge of ultrasonography among gastroenterologists and has expanded the number of gastroenterology departments that have access to an US machine.

Given the increasing availability of US machines in gastroenterology and emergency departments, it is worth reviewing the current knowledge of esophageal sonography. This review will discuss the role of transcutaneous US in clinical esophagology in both the adult and pediatric populations. It will mainly detail the different clinical settings in which US has been studied and the current evidence supporting its use (Table 1). It will not discuss endoscopic ultrasound.

To identify studies and case reports for this article, a literature search was performed using PubMed with the following search terms: “ultrasound” along with “esophagus,” “achalasia,” “varices,” “foreign body,” and “reflux.” The search results were then evaluated for their appropriateness toward the subject matter. In addition, all citations listed in the relevant articles were reviewed.

SONOGRAPHIC VISUALIZATION OF THE ESOPHAGUS

US is a noninvasive, cost-effective, and widely available test. It allows for repeat and follow-up examinations because of its lack of radiation exposure, while allowing the provider the opportunity to perform the evaluation in real time, present the findings to the patient, and improve the personal experience of the examination.

Although not typically evaluated on a routine abdominal US, studies have consistently shown that the intra-abdominal esophagus and esophagogastric junction (EGJ) can regularly be seen on transabdominal US. (6–10). With a patient in the supine position and on deep inspiration, the transducer is moved over the xiphoid area (Figure 1). A cross-section of the intra-abdominal esophagus can be seen by applying the transducer sagittally and transversely, directing the beam cephalically through the window of the left lobe of the liver (7). Measurements of the intra-abdominal esophageal length and wall thickness can be obtained, as well as measurements of esophageal distention and the EGJ angle (angle of His) (Figure 2a). A high-frequency linear probe may be used for better resolution when the target is superficial, whereas a curvilinear probe can be used for deeper or broader abdominal assessment. Doppler can be added to assess for varices.

With the patient in the supine position, visualization of the cervical esophagus can be achieved with the ultrasound probe over the neck, while visualization of the distal esophagus can be achieved with the probe over the upper abdomen.

Identifying and measuring the esophagus by ultrasound. (a) The intra-abdominal esophagus (Eso) is identified between the liver (L) and aorta (Ao) using a 1–6 MHz curvilinear transducer (C6-1X, SuperSonic MACH 30, Hologic). (b) The cervical esophagus (Eso) is identified using a 5–18 MHz linear array transducer (L18-5, SuperSonic, MACH 30, Hologic) in the transverse orientation across the anterior neck. In both locations, the esophageal diameter and wall thickness can be measured.

Studies assessing the cervical esophagus by US are more limited (11–14). However, studies have shown that it can be successfully performed (Figures 1 and 2b). After learning how to visualize the cervical esophagus on cadavers, Zhu et al (15) reported that by using both left and right lateral approaches in a cohort of 435 healthy subjects, they were able to identify the cervical esophagus in all cases. A study of pediatric subjects also identified the cervical esophagus in all 93 subjects (16). As the cervical esophagus is not deep, a high-frequency linear probe is usually used.

Visualization of the thoracic esophagus is the most difficult because of the presence of the sternum, ribs, vertebrae, and lungs (17). Owing to this, visualization of most of the thoracic esophagus has been considered difficult and even impossible. The most successful study, using a transthoracic approach, reported that the thoracic esophagus could be identified in 74% of 253 volunteers, although a short portion at the back of the left main bronchus could never be fully seen (17). In a pediatric study, the thoracic esophagus could be identified in only 44% of 93 subjects (16).

ACHALASIA

Achalasia is the prototypical esophageal motility disorder (18). Several studies have assessed the US features of achalasia (Table 2). The first, from Turkey, assessed 35 patients with proven achalasia (19). These patients underwent evaluation of their intra-abdominal esophagus by US and were compared with 3 groups: healthy controls, patients with EGJ cancers, and patients with peptic strictures. The study found that 80% (28 of 35) of the patients with achalasia, despite fasting before the procedure, showed esophageal dilation with residual fluid retention and a smooth narrowing of the distal esophagus, findings that were not seen in any of the healthy controls. In addition, the mean wall thickness in patients with achalasia was thicker than healthy controls (4.8 ± 0.9 mm vs 2.3 ± 0.5 mm, P < 0.001). The EGJ carcinoma patients could be differentiated from the patients with achalasia because their wall thickening was even greater (mean 17.0 mm) and irregular. The peptic stricture patients could be differentiated as their wall thickening was irregular and occupied a longer segment of distal esophagus.

Similar findings were noted on a study from Germany comparing 28 patients with achalasia with healthy controls and patients with EGJ cancer (20). The patients with achalasia had a significantly more dilated esophagus (mean 20.0 mm vs 10.1 mm, P < 0.001) than controls, although esophageal wall thickness was not significantly thicker (3.2 mm vs 2.9 mm). Those with EGJ neoplasms could be differentiated from patients with achalasia because of the presence of a hypoechoic lesion, with US showing 100% sensitivity and 82% specificity for detecting the tumor. A third study, from Taiwan, also compared patients with achalasia with EGJ cancer patients and controls with functional dyspepsia (21). The researchers found that patients with achalasia had a thicker mean esophageal wall thickness than controls (5.1 ± 2.3 mm vs 3.3 ± 1.2 mm, P < 0.001), although there was overlap between the groups, and EGJ neoplasms could be distinguished based on their irregular features.

Taken together, these 3 studies show that patients with achalasia can be identified by having thicker but regular esophageal walls, with esophageal dilation and retained fluid. Despite this, US does not seem to be used frequently in the evaluation of suspected achalasia and has only been cited in several case reports (22–27).

ESOPHAGEAL VARICES

Screening for esophageal varices by upper endoscopy carries certain disadvantages including high financial costs, manpower issues, and the risk of complications. Therefore, several noninvasive screening tests have been evaluated as an alternative to endoscopy (28). Among these has been US. While several studies have assessed whether signs of portal hypertension on a standard abdominal US can help predict the presence of varices (28–31), a few prospective studies have specifically used US to evaluate the distal esophagus (Table 3).

The first report of the use of US to evaluate for esophageal varices was in 1998 (32). Kishimoto et al (32) evaluated 47 patients with cirrhosis or noncirrhotic portal hypertension and correlated US findings with the presence of varices on endoscopy. They found that the mean thickness of the esophageal wall was significantly thicker in the presence of varices (5.7 mm vs 3.6 mm, P < 0.001) and that hepatofugal venous flow on Doppler was specific for varices. Using a diagnostic criteria of wall thickness of at least 5 mm or an irregular wall surface provided a sensitivity of 93%, specificity of 82%, and accuracy of 89% for the presence of varices.

Despite these promising results, only a few other studies have assessed the role of US in variceal screening. Ali et al described 50 patients with cirrhosis and/or portal hypertension compared with 50 controls (33). The intra-abdominal wall thickness was greater in the cirrhotic group (5.7 ± 1.7 mm vs 3.6 ± 0.96 mm, P < 0.001), and there was more irregularity. In another study from 2001, Baek and Lee (34) compared 41 cirrhotic patients with 44 controls. Not only was the mean distal wall thickness was significantly greater in the cirrhotic group compared with controls (5.2 ± 1.8 mm vs 2.7 ± 0.7 mm, P < 0.001), but the mean thickness increased based on the grade of varices (grade I: 4.2 ± 1.2 mm; grade II: 4.9 ± 1.1 mm; grade III–IV: 7.0 ± 1.8 mm).

Finally, a study from Egypt in 2014 evaluated 673 cirrhotic patients using standard US. (35). They too found that the esophageal wall thickness was significantly thicker in patients with varices (mean 7.3 mm) compared with those without varices (3.7 mm), and it was especially thick in those with high-risk varices (8.65 mm). The overall accuracy of US in their cohort was 95%. A subsequent report by the same research team found that the use of US instead of endoscopy for variceal screening would lead to significant cost savings (36). Despite the results of these studies, it seems that the use of US to screen for esophageal varices has not caught on in routine practice (Figure 3).

ESOPHAGEAL FOREIGN BODIES

Foreign body ingestion (FBI) is a common reason for emergency department visits, especially among pediatric patients. The increasing availability and familiarity with POCUS has allowed it become part of the evaluation for many emergency conditions. While US is not part of the standard evaluation for FBI, it has been suggested as a possible alternative to other imaging modalities (37).

Numerous case reports and case series describe the use of US in identifying esophageal foreign bodies (EFBs) in the pediatric population. A recent review article included 14 articles with 52 pediatric cases of FBI identified on US, including in 16 cases in which the foreign body was in the esophagus (38). This included 5 cases of food boluses, 8 coins, and 3 other objects. In 11 of the cases, POCUS was the first imaging modality used. Metallic coins were always hyperechoic and frequently showed posterior acoustic shadowing, while food boluses were more variable.

Case reports are also found in the adult literature. Singleton et al (39) describe 5 cases of EFB identified on US and compared them with 5 healthy controls. The EFB cases had significantly more esophageal dilation (17.5 vs 9.3 mm, P = 0.0011) and persistent air-fluid levels after swallowing, although the actual EFB was only visualized in 3 of 5 cases (60%). Ahn and Sohn (40) describe 3 cases of EFB in the cervical esophagus (2 food impactions and 1 pill). They report seeing hyperechoic or hypoechoic material (the suspected EFB) that did not disappear with swallowing, reverberation artifact in 1 case, and esophageal dilation/bulging proximal to the EFB. Kawai et al also described a hyperechoic appearance of an EFB (41). With POCUS becoming more available and widely used, more cases in which it is used to identify EFB should be expected (Figure 4).

GASTROESOPHAGEAL REFLUX DISEASE

Most studies evaluating the utility of US to diagnose gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) have been conducted in infants and young children. As subjective symptom descriptions of GERD are not reliable from infants and toddlers, objective testing is desired to diagnose GERD in this population (42). However, tests such as upper endoscopy, esophageal pH monitoring, and impedance measurements are all invasive and poorly tolerated. Thus, a simple, noninvasive, well-tolerated test, such as US, would be welcome in this challenging population.

US has been evaluated in several pediatric studies which are summarized in a recent review article by Savino et al (42). US is able to evaluate the anatomy of the distal esophagus and EGJ. It is generally performed after a liquid feed, such as milk, to reproduce a normal feed and for adequate gastric distention. First, the intra-abdominal esophagus is evaluated carefully and its length measured. This length is not constant and may change with respiration and gastric filling, so consistent measurement conditions are required. The intra-abdominal length measurement is important as several studies have directly linked a shorter intra-abdominal esophagus to the presence of GERD (6,43–46). The diameter and wall thickness can also be measured, as larger diameter and wall thickness values have been associated with GERD (47). Next, the gastroesophageal angle (of His) should be measured, as obtuse angles have also been associated with GERD (46). Finally, visualization of reflux of gastric contents can be assessed sonographically. A protracted observation should be performed to assess for this with demonstration of gastric contents entering the esophagus, dilation of the distal esophagus, and vomiting all suggestive of GERD (42).

The accuracy of US in pediatric populations has been directly compared with pH monitoring, the gold-standard for GERD diagnosis, with several studies showing that US has a high sensitivity and positive predictive value. (45,46,48–55). The added use of color Doppler sonography has also been reported to increase the sensitivity of US. (44,48,56,57).

Although US has mostly been studied in the pediatric population, several studies have evaluated its use for diagnosing GERD in the adult population, although with poorer operating characteristics. A study from Turkey attempted to correlate the presence of refluxate identified in the cervical esophagus on US with the presence of GERD (58). They found that the esophageal luminal diameter was the best variable to differentiate GERD from non-GERD patients (5.0 ± 1.3 mm vs 3.9 ± 1.4 mm, P = 0.023), but only with a 79% sensitivity and 61% specificity at diameter of 4.83 mm. In a study by Mohammadi et al (59), 100 patients with reflux esophagitis were compared with 250 GERD patients with a normal endoscopy. They found that the esophagitis patients had significantly thicker esophageal walls, but that using 2.7 mm as a cutoff only had 38% sensitivity, 91% specificity, 63% positive predictive value, and 79% negative predictive value. Saatov et al (60) performed a “fluid load” protocol in which patients drank 200–400 mL of liquid before their examination. They identified regurgitation of liquid into the esophagus, the increase in esophageal lumen diameter, and thickening of the esophageal wall as ultrasonographic signs of GERD. Aghaghazvini et al evaluated 72 suspected GERD patients and found that only the sonographic presence of reflux was correlated with GERD, with a sensitivity of 45% and specificity of 86% (61). Finally, Köroğlu et al found a sonographically visible difference in esophageal wall thickness, hiatus diameters, EGJ diameters, and paraesophageal fat thickness between patients with and without GERD (62).

OTHER ESOPHAGEAL DISEASES

One study evaluated the use of US in patients with systemic sclerosis, which is associated with GERD and esophageal dysmotility, and found differences compared with controls (63). Other case reports have described US diagnosing an intramural esophageal dissection (64) and esophageal cancer (65).

BARRIERS TO IMPLEMENTATION

Despite the significant number of positive studies showing its utility in various esophageal disorders, it does not seem that esophageal US is widely practiced, or that most gastroenterologist are even aware of it. Why is this so? It is likely due to several factors.

First, when evaluating the esophagus, upper endoscopy is often the first procedure that is performed. Nowadays, upper endoscopy is readily available, safe, and can be both diagnostic and therapeutic. Thus, for most of the conditions described above, an upper endoscopy could diagnose and treat the underlying condition, leaving no reason for esophageal US to be performed. Therefore, it may be that in locales where endoscopy is not readily available or is cost prohibitive that esophageal US may have a role.

US machines are not commonly available to gastroenterologists and this may limit their ability to perform this procedure. If esophageal US can only be performed by a radiologist or radiology technician, it may limit the desire and ability of a gastroenterologist to order it. This may also lead to a “turf war” if physicians in both fields are adequately trained. It may be that gastroenterologists can perform US to evaluate for achalasia or varices in patients during routine clinic or office visits, emergency physicians evaluate patients in the emergency department with suspected EFB, and other cases are scheduled for outpatient US to be performed in the radiology department.

In addition, it is not clear how much training is necessary to become proficient in performing esophageal US. For example, one study described how the authors studied on 60 cadavers to learn how to visualize the cervical esophagus, and afterward, they were successful in all study subjects (15). This study, and most of those cited in this review article, were performed by radiologists. As radiologists with expertise in the field of ultrasonography, it was probably much easier for them to learn how to evaluate the esophagus than for a gastroenterologist who has much less experience using and interpreting US. Thus, it remains unclear how much training is required for a gastroenterologist to become proficient in this procedure.

Nor is the amount of time required to perform the procedure described. This likely varies based on the expertise of the practitioner, but if it is considered to be too time-consuming, this would also limit its implementation.

In addition, there may be concerns about the reproducibility of the examination at different times or between different operators or US machines. Thus, data on interobserver and intraobserver agreement are sorely needed before any meaningful clinical application can be considered. Furthermore, there are no standardized diagnostic normal values (such as intra-abdominal esophageal length and diameter, wall-thickness cutoffs, and EGJ angle), examination protocol (including the addition of provocative tests such as fluid-loading), or US machine and settings used (as these vary by study).

Importantly, while many studies and case series have been published, they are severely limited by the small study sizes and overall low study quality. The lack of high-quality data may be another reason why it is not more commonly performed and has not been incorporated into guidelines.

Finally, since many of the studies were performed by radiologists, they were also published in radiology journals. This, too, may explain why the subject is not well-known among gastroenterologists who may not be familiar with this literature.

CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS

There are many benefits to the use of US including its noninvasiveness and cost. Together with the increased use of POCUS, which puts the US in the hands on clinicians actively treating patients, the role of US will only increase in clinical use. It is important for gastroenterologists to be aware of the potential benefits of using US in esophageal diseases.

This review has summarized the current knowledge regarding the use of US in esophageal disorders. There is case series data providing proof-of-concept evidence that US can identify patients with achalasia, although it is unclear how that fits in to the evaluation of patients with suspected achalasia. Although US is not a replacement for manometry, it could be an option in certain populations such as patients who cannot tolerate manometry or in locales where manometry and functional luminal imaging probe are not available. There is also proof-of concept type evidence for the utility and cost-effectiveness of US in screening for esophageal varices in cirrhotic patients. Again, in locales where endoscopy is not readily available or is cost-prohibitive, or for patients who are deemed high-risk for endoscopy and sedation, this may be a good option. Case reports have shown that US can identify EFB. Identifying if an EFB exists and its location after an ingestion, performed by an emergency department physician, can be helpful in planning for endoscopic removal. Finally, there is good evidence that US can be effective in infants with suspected GERD and this should be considered before more invasive testing, while data in adults remain poor (66).

Ideally, the use of esophageal US in the future should be performed under research protocols so that the findings can be reported in a vigorous fashion. High-quality, prospective, multicenter studies are especially needed. For example, prospective studies showing that patients with dysphagia can be easily screened for achalasia with good accuracy, that cirrhotic patients can be screened for varices with high precision, or that EFB can be identified at a high rate will greatly improve its acceptance and will help it find a place in the diagnostic evaluation of these disorders.

In conclusion, US of the esophagus has been shown to be helpful in several esophageal disorders, but how this translates into clinical practice remains unclear. Barriers exist to its use, including the need for training and the availability of US, and it remains unclear how it fits in with the current diagnostic algorithms of esophageal diseases. High-quality studies on the use and benefits of esophageal US in clinical practice are warranted and may speed up the acceptance and integration of this promising technology.
