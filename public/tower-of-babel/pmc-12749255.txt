Green synthesis of quercetin-mediated ZnS quantum dots and their photocatalytic, antimicrobial, and cytotoxic performances
Authors: Büşra Şensoy Gün, Abdülkadir Aydoğan, Musa Çadırcı, Sümeyye Şensoy, Rafig Gurbanov, Belgin Tunalı
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In this study, ZnS quantum dots (QDs) were synthesized via a green, simple, cost-effective, and sustainable method using quercetin as a reducing and stabilizing agent. Successful synthesis was confirmed by UV–vis and Photoluminescence (PL) spectroscopy, revealing characteristic optical properties. Structural and morphological features were analyzed by ATR-FTIR, XRD, SEM–EDX, and TEM, showing crystallinity, surface functionalities, and nanostructure, with TEM confirming particle sizes ≤ 10 nm. The anti-biofilm activity was tested against E. coli ATCC 35218 and P. aeruginosa ATCC 27853, showing significant inhibition, especially against E. coli. Antifungal activity against C. albicans ATCC 10239 (agar disk diffusion) revealed larger inhibition zone (22 mm) than the reference antibiotic (20 mm). The MTT assay revealed high biocompatibility, with L929 fibroblast cell viability remaining above 75% across all test conditions. Under 24 h of UV irradiation, the photocatalytic degradation efficiency reached 85% for Brilliant Green (BG) and approximately 70% for Methylene Blue (MB). Photoluminescence stability was examined between 0–40 °C, showing good thermal stability with minimal structural degradation. These results suggest ZnS QDs possess promising antimicrobial, antifungal, photocatalytic, and biocompatible properties, supporting their potential in biomedical and environmental applications.

Introduction

Quantum dots (QDs) are semiconductor nanocrystals with dimensions in the nanometer scale and attract attention with their unique optical and electronic properties. QDs cause the electronic structure of materials to be organized differently than classical semiconductors1. In nanotechnology, QDs, have a wide range of applications due to their advanced optical properties. In particular, they are of great importance in applications such as high-resolution imaging, biosensors and optical data storage2. QDs also have an important place in the field of health. High photoluminescence efficiency and tunable emission properties are used in biomolecular labeling and imaging in applications such as the detection and treatment of cancer cells3,4.

Zinc sulfide (ZnS) QDs are considered one of the most remarkable materials in nanotechnology. These nano-scale semiconductor crystals play an important role in various application areas due to their distinct optical and electronic properties. ZnS QDs are used in areas such as biomedical imaging, sensors, and optoelectronic devices by providing photoluminescence emission in a wide colour spectrum due to the separation in energy levels5. In particular, the high photochemical stability and solubility properties of ZnS QDs enable these materials to be used effectively in interactions with biomolecules and environmental sensors6. This activity arises from their ability to generate electron–hole pairs, which react with water and oxygen to produce reactive oxygen species (ROS) capable of breaking down complex molecules. Moreover, ZnS QDs possess significant antimicrobial properties, disrupting bacterial cell membranes, inducing oxidative stress via ROS generation, and leading to cell lysis, making them effective against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria7. The strong antimicrobial and photocatalytic properties, along with the biocompatibility of ZnS quantum dots, enable their use in both environmental and biomedical applications.

The synthesis of ZnS QDs is carried out by various chemical and physical methods. Each method provides the production of ZnS nanocrystals for different properties and application areas8. In addition, green synthesis methods have also attracted attention in recent years. Green synthesis aims to produce ZnS QDs using environmentally friendly and sustainable approaches. These methods generally use reactors and solvents obtained from natural or biological sources, which reduces chemical waste and reduces energy consumption9. For example, some studies have shown that green synthesis methods using plant extracts and microorganisms can produce ZnS QDs at lower temperatures and in a shorter time10–12. Green synthesis methods also allow purer and biocompatible ZnS QDs to be obtained, making them more suitable for sensitive areas such as biomedical applications and environmental sensors. The environmentally friendly approach and sustainability advantages make green synthesis superior to other traditional methods13. Moreover, this approach aligns with the principles of green chemistry by minimizing hazardous by-products, reducing carbon emissions, and lowering the overall carbon footprint of nanomaterial production. Therefore, green synthesis not only ensures safer and more sustainable processes but also contributes to global efforts toward environmental protection and climate change mitigation14. Quercetin acts as both a reductant and a stabilizer during green synthesis, increasing the antimicrobial activity of the synthesized nanoparticles while reducing toxicity and improving biocompatibility15. Due to these unique properties, quercetin has been preferred in the green synthesis of ZnS QDs.

Various synthesis routes such as co-precipitation, sol–gel, hydrothermal, and microemulsion techniques have been extensively used for the preparation of ZnS QDs16. The co-precipitation method offers a simple and low-cost route but often results in broad particle size distribution and surface defects that can reduce optical performance17. The sol–gel process provides better control over homogeneity, yet it involves long reaction times and the use of organic solvents that are not environmentally friendly18. Hydrothermal and solvothermal syntheses can produce highly crystalline ZnS QDs with tunable morphology. However, these techniques require high temperature and pressure conditions, as well as specialized equipment, which limit their scalability19. The microemulsion method enables narrow size control due to confinement within micelles, but it typically relies on surfactants and toxic organic solvents, making purification difficult20.

Previous studies have explored green and biocompatible strategies for the synthesis of ZnS nanostructures. For instance, Ayodhya and Veerabhadram (2016) reported a BSA-assisted hydrothermal method that produced uniform ZnS nanoparticles with excellent photocatalytic activity, while Jacob et al. (2019) demonstrated the biosynthesis of ZnS quantum dots using Aspergillus sp., exhibiting strong antibacterial and dye degradation capabilities21,22. Similarly, Mansur et al. (2015) developed ZnS–chitosan nano-immunoconjugates via an aqueous, room-temperature process, yielding ultra-small, highly fluorescent, and cyto-compatible nanocrystals suitable for cancer cell targeting and bioimaging23. In another green approach, Haque et al. (2023) synthesized plant-extract-mediated ZnS QDs, which showed promising antibacterial, cytotoxic, and biosensing performance, including selective detection of bilirubin and rifampicin24. While most recent studies have focused on photocatalysts synthesized through conventional chemical routes, the present work demonstrates a sustainable green synthesis of quercetin-mediated ZnS quantum dots exhibiting highly efficient UV-light-driven photocatalytic degradation, providing an environmentally friendly and reproducible alternative25,26. Despite these advances, green synthesis strategies for ZnS QDs remain limited and relatively scarce in the literature. Another noteworthy feature of the proposed approach is its high reproducibility, especially compared to methods using natural fruit/plant extracts in green synthesis where a large number of variables are difficult to control. To address this gap, the present study reports a simple, cost-effective, and facile synthesis using a single plant-derived component, quercetin, providing an environmentally friendly alternative for ZnS QD production.

In this study, ZnS QD were synthesized using the flavonoid compound quercetin through a simple, environmentally friendly, and sustainable approach. The synthesized ZnS QD were subjected to comprehensive structural, optical, and morphological characterization. Following characterization, biofilm inhibition potential was evaluated against Escherichia coli ATCC 35218 and Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC 27853 strains. Additionally, antifungal activity was assessed against Candida albicans ATCC 10239 using the agar well diffusion method. The cytotoxic effects of ZnS QD were investigated on the L929 fibroblast cell line, and their photocatalytic efficiency was tested against methylene blue and brilliant green dyes. Moreover, the temperature-dependent photoluminescence (PL) behavior of the QD was also explored.

Materials and methods

Quercetin (337951-25G, ≥ 95%), Zinc Nitrate Hexahydrate (TK.920085, Zn(NO3)2.6H2O, extra pure), Sodium Sulfide (Tekkim, TK.170560, Na2SO4, extra pure), Mueller Hinton Broth (MHB, Biolife), Mueller Hinton Agar (MHA, Biolife), Tryptic Soy Broth (TSB, Biolife), Chlorhexidine acetate (CHX, Fluka Analytical, PHR1222-500MG), Methylene Blue (Isolab, 122965–43-9), and Brillantgrün (Merck) were purchased. Solutions were prepared with ultrapure water produced in Human Corporation, Zener 900 (UP: ≦18.3MΩcm) device.

ZnS QD synthesis

For the synthesis of ZnS QD, 0.1 M Zn(NO3)2.6H2O, 0.1 M Na2SO4 and 0.001 M quercetin solutions were prepared with ultrapure water. The prepared Zn(NO3)2.6H2O and Na2SO4 solutions were combined in 1/1 ratios by volume and stirred at 70 °C for 200 rpm and 30 min. The experiment was set up in two groups and at the end of 1 h, 10% 0.001 M quercetin solutions were added dropwise to the solution by volume. The solution to which quercetin was added was stirred at 70 °C for 200 rpm and 60 min and left to cool at room temperature. After the solution cooled, they were centrifuged and washed several times with ultrapure water, and half portion was left in the liquid and other portion was left in the oven to dry at 40 °C. For characterization studies, the liquid and powdered portions were separately used according to the specific techniques.

ZnS QD characterization studies

To determine the optical and electronic properties of the synthesized QDs; Photoluminescence spectra (PL) were examined in the Ocean Optics USB2000 fiber spectrometer device using a 405 nm laser for excitation. UV–Vis spectra ​​(290–800 nm) was collected using a A ELAB UV–VIS spectrophotometer AE-S60 series device. Both PL and UV–Vis spectra are recorded using a 10 mm long quartz cuvette in which ZnS QDs are dispersed in water. According to the two analyses, the QDs that provide the best UV–Vis and PL results was selected and the studies continued on these samples. According to the UV–Vis and PL analysis results, chemical and morphological analyses of the successfully synthesized ZnS QD were carried out. To determine the crystallographic structure and phases of ZnS QDs, X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was carried out using a Malvern Panalytical Empyrean instrument system with Cu Ka radiation having a characteristic wavelength of λ = 1.5406 Å and the data were obtained in the scanning range of 20° < 2θ < 70°. To investigate the functional bonding structure of ZnS QDs, Attenuated Total Reflection Fourier Transform Infrared (ATR-FTIR) spectrum was measured in the range of 4000– 400 cm−1 with a resolution of 4.0 cm−1 by a Spectrometer (Perkin Elmer Spectrum 100) device. The morphology, elemental composition and dimensions of ZnS QDs were determined by Hitachi HighTech HT7700 brand TEM and ZEISS SUPRA 40VP GEMINI SEM–EDX instruments. ZnS QDs were coated by platinum sputtering on the QUORUM–Q ISORES instrument before SEM analysis.

In vitro biological assays

Biofilm inhibition assay

The anti-bofilm activity of ZnS QD against E. coli 35218 and P. aeruginosa ATCC 27853 strains was investigated by incubating the strains in TSB at 37 °C for 24 h. Following the incubation period, the bacterial cultures were adjusted to a density of 106 cells at OD600. Subsequently, 100 μL of the bacterial culture was seeded into the wells of a 96-well plate. The synthesized QDs were diluted to concentrations of 4, 2, 1, and 0.5 mg/ml. These concentrations were added to the bacterial cultures in the wells by serial dilution of 100 µl. The plate was then subjected to an overnight incubation at 37 °C. Following the incubation period, the culture medium from the 96-well plate was removed, and the wells were washed thrice with water. Subsequently, methanol was introduced into the washed wells and maintained for a duration of 15 min to ensure fixation. After the removal of the methanol, 130 μL of 0.1% Crystal violet dye solution was added to the dry wells and maintained for a period of 15 min. The wells were subjected to three cycles of water rinsing in order to eliminate any residual crystal violet. Subsequently, a 30% acetic acid solution was introduced into the wells, and the resulting optical density was measured at OD55027.

Antifungal assay

The antifungal activity of Zns QD against C. albicans ATCC 10239 was evaluated using an agar well diffusion test, a standard method in microbiological analysis. C. albicans culture was diluted to a concentration of 1.5 × 106 CFU/ml following a 24-h incubation in MHB medium at 37 °C. The prepared culture was subsequently applied to the surface of MH agar plates (in accordance with CLSI M44-A2; Mueller Hinton Agar + 2% glucose and 0.5 μg/ml methylene blue) using sterile cotton swabs under aseptic conditions. In this experiment, 40 μL of ZnS QD and CHX antibiotics, prepared at a concentration of 100 ppm, were added to the wells of agar plates, which had a well diameter of 6 mm. The plates were then incubated at 37 °C for 48 h. The antimicrobial activity of the samples was evaluated by determining the diameter of the net inhibition zone around each well, expressed in millimeters (mm)28.

Cell cytotoxicity assay

In order to assess the biocompatibility of ZnS QD, a study was conducted that entailed the execution of a cellular viability assay on L929 cells. The assay employed 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) reduction as a metric for the assessment of cell viability. The cells were cultivated in a specialized medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin–streptomycin, maintained at a temperature of 37 °C in a controlled atmosphere with 5% CO2. A 1 ml volume of cell suspension was seeded into a 96-well plate (approximately 3 × 106 cells ml−1 per well) with fresh medium containing different concentrations of ZnS QD (4, 2, 1, 0.5, 0.25, and 0.125 mg/ml). Following a 24-h incubation period, 10 μL of MTT was added to each well and incubated for an additional 3 h. Cell viability was then evaluated by measuring the optical density at 570 nm in comparison to the control cells29.

Photocatalytic activity assay

The photocatalytic activity of ZnS quantum dots against Methylene Blue (MB) and Brilliant Green (BG) dyes was evaluated by photocatalytic degradation under UV light irradiation. ZnS quantum dots were added to the prepared dye solutions (0.017 g of dye was weighed and dissolved in 100 ml of distilled water) in the ratio of 3/1 and 6/1 as dye/ZnS (v/v). The dye/ZnS solutions, which were taken to a dark place, were kept under a UV A light source with a 0, 1, 2, 5 and 24 h. At the end of each sample hour, UV–Vis absorption measurement was performed and recorded on the A ELAB UV–VIS spectrophotometer AE-S60 series device30.

Result and discussion

The UV–Vis absorption and PL spectra of ZnS QDs synthesized using quercetin are shown in Fig. 1. The method gives direct access to the the optical band gap of nanomaterials and the quantum confinement effect. The UV–Vis spectroscopy also provides information about particle size, purity and optical performance of the synthesis31. PL spectra is another type of analysis that helps to determine the optical properties of QDs and reveal their characteristic features. The widespread use of this method in the field of semiconductor nanostructures is related to its potential to provide detailed information about the electronic and structural properties of these materials32.Fig. 1ZnS Quantum dots (a) PL analysis (b) UV–Vis absorption analysis.

ZnS Quantum dots (a) PL analysis (b) UV–Vis absorption analysis.

The PL emission spectra of ZnS QD at room temperature are shown in Fig. 1a. The PL spectra showed fluorescence emission spectra centered at 613 nm and about 480 nm. The main peak at about 610 nm shows closely spaced double peaks (613 nm and 645 nm). These peaks are previously reported for ZnS QDs and attributed to the following mechanisms; i) 480 nm broad peak is due to the recombination of trapped electrons on sulfur vacancies to interstitial sulfur states, ii) 613 nm is more likely due to sulfur species on the surface of ZnS QDs and iii) 645 nm peak emission originates from the transitions occur between the interstitial zinc states and the zinc vacancies33–35. In the UV–Vis absorption spectrum shown in Fig. 1b, the absorption peaks of ZnS QDs were observed around 298 nm and 370 nm36–40. The large Stoke shift between the absorption and PL peaks is also mainly due to the fact that the PL emission originates from the surface trap states rather than the direct band gap39–42. In the products synthesized with Zn(NO3)2.6H2O and sodium sulfide by a green method, two absorption bands were observed in the UV region. In the studies in the literature, the analysis performed by density functional theory showed that the band edge absorption can originate from both inorganic and organic components, causing a blue shift in the spectrum40,43,44. A broad excitonic peak is initially observed at around 370 nm, but it becomes more pronounced over time and shifts to 298 nm. The absorption peaks of the prepared ZnS nanostructures exhibit a blue shift compared to large-sized ZnS. This shift is explained by the quantum confinement effect due to the very small size of the ZnS quantum dots. In addition, the position and change of the peaks observed in the spectrum reflect the aggregation state of the ZnS particles and support that they exhibit aggregation-induced emission (AIE) behavior.

The Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectrum of ZnS QDs is shown in Fig. 2a. In the spectrum, the broad band around 3300 cm−1 is an indicator of the stretching of the -OH group, which indicates the presence of water molecules or hydroxyl groups45. The bands at 2905 cm−1 and 2831 cm−1 reflect the features of asymmetric C-H stretching in ZnS QDs. In addition, the band at 1637 cm−1 is an indicator of the C-H symmetric stretching. The band at 1388 cm−1 is associated with the stretching of the COOH group46. The prominent band at 1090 cm−1 originates from the resonance interactions between the vibrational modes of the sulfur ions in the crystal structure45. The band observed at 998 cm-1 represents the vibrational modes of the C = C double bond, and this band can be considered as a sign of the possible presence of organic compounds in ZnS QDs47,48. Finally, the band observed at 615 cm−1 belongs to the vibrational modes of the Zn–S (metal sulfide) bond, which reflects the stability and structural properties of the metal-sulfur bond in the ZnS crystal structure45–48. The carbon-related bands observed in the FTIR spectrum may originate from quercetin and are likely attributed to organic functional groups remaining adsorbed on the surface of ZnS QDs after green synthesis, possibly stabilizing the nanoparticles by chelating with ZnS QD29,49.Fig. 2(a) FTIR spectrum and (b) XRD diffraction pattern of ZnS quantum dots.

(a) FTIR spectrum and (b) XRD diffraction pattern of ZnS quantum dots.

The XRD diffraction pattern of ZnS QDs is shown in Fig. 2b. The analysis revealed characteristic diffraction peaks at 2θ = 28.31°, 33.05°, 47.60° and 59.36° positions. These peaks were found to correspond to the (111), (200), (220) and (311) crystal planes, respectively. The observed peaks matched with the diffraction peaks of ZnS QD when scanned in the XRD database (JCPDS No 98–007-7082). Furthermore, the obtained diffraction patterns are consistent with the cubic crystal phase of ZnS49–52.

XRD analysis revealed the synthesis of ZnS QD structures using diffraction patterns. Quercetin appears to play a critical role not only as a surface stabilizer but also as a reducing agent during the synthesis phase. This reducing function becomes particularly important during the initial stages of formation when Na2SO4 is used as a sulfur source. Quercetin, due to the polyphenolic (especially catechol) portions of its structure, tends to release electrons and thus undergo oxidation. It is assumed that QD synthesis provides optimal reducing capacity by oxidizing both hydroxyl groups on the catechol portion of quercetin. During this oxidation, a quercetin molecule loses two electrons (2e-) and two protons (2H+), forming an oxidized quercetin product (a quinone-like structure). These released electrons are used to generate the S2− ion necessary for ZnS formation. Sulfate ion (SO42−), in a high oxidation state, is reduced to the sulfur ion (S2−) before combining with Zn2+ to form ZnS. Eight electrons (8e-) are required for this reaction to occur. In this case, four quercetin molecules must be oxidized to provide the eight electrons and complete the reduction. After the sulfate is converted to S2− with the electrons and protons provided by quercetin, these ions rapidly combine with Zn2+ ions in solution to form ZnS nuclei53,54.

When the morphology of ZnS QDs is examined with SEM analysis, it is seen that the particles are nano-sized and tend to aggregate in places (Fig. 3a). Additionally, EDX analysis confirmed the presence of zinc (Zn) and sulfur (S) elements within the structure of the ZnS QDs (Fig. 3b). This observation supports the successful synthesis of the QDs by indicating that the nanostructures are composed of pure ZnS. Figure 3c presents two transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images of ZnS QDs captured at different magnifications. While SEM analysis reveals aggregated ZnS QDs with a relatively uniform morphology, the TEM images show that the particles are more dispersed. Additionally, the TEM analysis indicates that the size of the QDs is predominantly below 10 nm.Fig. 3(a) SEM image, (b) EDX spectrum, and (c) TEM images of ZnS Quantum Dots.

Temperature-dependent PL spectroscopy sheds light on the carrier relaxation paths for enhancing optical properties for various optoelectronic applications. Therefore, we collected PL spectra of the green synthesized ZnS QDs in the temperature range between 0 °C and 40 °C (Fig. 4a), which is the working temperature of many optoelectronic devices. The PL intensity of the sample significantly decreases as temperature increases, as seen in Fig. 4b. This tendency confirms that phonon-assisted thermal escapes and surface trap states are the principal mechanisms that take place in ZnS QDs55,56. Stated otherwise, as the temperature increases phonon facilitation electrons and holes recombine before radiative recombination, and more electrons and holes trap on the surfaces of QDs. At the same time, as temperature increases the FWHM value gradually broadens reaching about 120 nm from just below 100 nm (Fig. 4c). The main reasons for this trend are probably twofold; exciton-phonon coupling and trap-assisted relaxation mechanisms57–59. PL intensity and FWHM variations as a function of temperature are consistent with the literature data recorded for ZnS QDs synthesized with different methods. For temperature sensing applications, recovering PL intensity upon cooling and heating cycles is crucially important. Therefore, we investigated the PL intensity reversibility after switching the temperature between 0 °C and 40 °C for 4 cycles, as shown in Fig. 4d. Upon heating, due to the thermal escapes and non-radiative surface state mechanisms the PL intensity decreases to about 90 a.u. On the other hand, during the cooling stage, the PL intensity returns to its original value of about 120 a.u. This consistency is observed for all 4 cycles, indicating that the synthesized ZnS QDs exhibit negligible structural degradation and good thermal stability. The high thermal stability of ZnS quantum dots in the temperature range of 0–40 °C increases the potential of these nanostructures in biomedical applications. Since the average human body temperature is around 37 °C, the ability of ZnS QDs to maintain their PL intensity without any structural deterioration up to 40 °C indicates that this material is suitable for long-term use in vivo. This feature will provide great advantages, especially in terms of its use as a bioimaging agent in treatment, diagnosis and monitoring processes.Fig. 4Temperature-dependent normalized PL spectra (a), PL intensity (b), PL peak FWHM (c) and PL intensity reversibility.

P. aeruginosa and E. coli strains are important bacterial pathogens that cause various infections and can form biofilms on surfaces by developing resistance to antibiotics, thereby evading the immune system60. The ability of these bacteria to form biofilms complicates treatment processes. Many studies have highlighted the antimicrobial effects of nanomaterials and surface modifications that prevent biofilm formation by investigating biofilm inhibition61–63. The biofilm assay of ZnS QDs against E. coli ATCC 35218 and P. aeruginosa ATCC 27853 bacteria is shown in Fig. 5. The biofilm formation capacity of these bacteria was evaluated by treating bacterial cultures with different concentrations of ZnS QD (0.5, 1, 2, and 4 mg/ml). The biofilm inhibitory effect of ZnS QD on P. aeruginosa is shown in Fig. 5a. ZnS QD at a concentration of 4 mg/ml reduced biofilm formation by 84% compared to the control group (p ≤ 0.0001). Nevertheless, all tested concentrations retained significant inhibitory effects against biofilm formation when contrasted with untreated control. These findings underscore the dose-responsive anti-biofilm properties of ZnS QDs, with maximal inhibition achieved by increasing the concentration of compound. The biofilm inhibitory effect of ZnS QD against E. coli ATCC 35218 strain is shown in Fig. 5b. Again, a dose-dependent decline in biofilm-production was measured for E. coli. Especially, high concentrations (4 and 2 mg/ml) of ZnS QD caused 83% biofilm inhibition (p ≤ 0.0001). The results suggest that ZnS QDs represent a promising candidate for development as an antimicrobial agent to mitigate biofilm-mediated infections, particularly in combating pathogenic bacterial biofilms. This study underscores their potential utility as a novel therapeutic strategy in addressing biofilm-associated challenges in clinical and industrial settings. Its ability to form biofilms and develop resistance to conventional antifungal agents makes it a critical target for evaluating the efficacy of new antimicrobial compounds. This enhanced antimicrobial effect can be attributed to the nanometer-scale dimensions of quantum dots, which provide a high surface area, their potential to trigger the production of ROS, and their ion release mechanism, as previously reported65–68. ROS generated by ZnS QDs induce oxidative stress on microbial cells by compromising their membranes and internal structures, ultimately leading to cellular damage and death67,68. Even in the absence of light irradiation, the surface defects, sulfur vacancies, and unsaturated coordination sites present on the surface of ZnS QDs can facilitate defect-induced redox reactions69. These defect regions can act as localized electron donors or acceptors, promoting electron transfer to adsorbed oxygen or water molecules and thereby leading to the spontaneous generation of ROS such ashydroxyl radical (•OH) and superoxide anion radicals (•O2−). Moreover, residual quercetin molecules remaining on the QD surface may enhance electron transfer and stabilize radical intermediates. These ROS species induce oxidative stress, damaging the cell membrane and oxidizing intracellular components, thereby reinforcing the overall antimicrobial activity of ZnS QDs60–71. In addition to ROS generation, the antimicrobial activity of ZnS QDs is further supported by their surface properties and the potential release of metal ions66,72,73. These include the release of Zn2+ and S2− ions, which are toxic to cell membranes, DNA, and enzymes, physical damage caused by direct contact of ZnS with the cell membrane, and damage to the electron transport system. All these pathways contribute to the high antimicrobial effect (Fig. 6). Although CHX is a well-established antimicrobial agent, the observation that ZnS QDs produce a larger inhibition zone suggests that these nanomaterials may offer certain advantages over conventional antibiotics. These advantages include efficacy at lower concentrations, broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity, and the ability to inhibit biofilm formation74. Nevertheless, the superior performance of ZnS QDs in this assay may also be influenced by enhanced diffusion properties under the test conditions or by environmental factors that may have impacted the activity of CHX. The results of antimicrobial assays indicate that ZnS QDs have potential as versatile agents against a wide range of pathogens. Further investigation is warranted to optimize the concentrations and formulations of ZnS QDs for clinical applications. Notably, ZnS QDs synthesized via green methods present a promising alternative to conventional antimicrobial strategies, particularly in the context of combating antimicrobial resistance.Fig. 6Antimicrobial mechanisms of ZnS nanoparticles.

Figure 8 shows time evaluations of the UV–Vis spectrum of MB and BG dyes containing ZnS QDs at different ratios, under constant UV irradiation. All spectra show that as the time increases the absorbance are decrease, a clear indication of photocatalytic degradation of the dye molecules (Fig. 8a-d). The absorption peak positions for MB and BG dyes are, respectively, at around 665 nm and 627 nm, and do not shift over the 24 h time period of photocatalytic degradation process. This confirms that the addition of QDs into the dye and UV irradiation do not change the molecular structure of the dyes, but results in a pure photocatalytic action in the structures of the dyes. ZnS is a semiconductor with a wide band gap (3.6–3.7) eV, and this property allows it to exhibit strong photocatalytic activity under UV light. When ZnS quantum dots are irradiated with photons with energies equal to or higher than the band gap energy, electrons in the valence band (VB) are excited to the conduction band (CB), creating positively charged holes (h+) in the valence band. The charge carriers (electrons and holes) formed as a result of this photogeneration initiate a series of redox reactions on the ZnS surface. The electrons (e⁻) in the conduction band react with oxygen molecules (O2) adsorbed on the surface to form O2•−. The holes (h+) in the valence band oxidize H2O or hydroxyl ions (OH⁻) to form •OH. The resulting •OH and •O2- radicals, thanks to their high oxidation potential, break down complex organic dyes such as MB and BG into simpler, environmentally friendly products (CO2, H2O, and inorganic ions). Conclusion

In this study, ZnS QDs were successfully synthesized through an environmentally friendly aqueous green synthesis approach using quercetin as both a reducing and stabilizing agent. Structural and morphological characterizations performed via FTIR, XRD, SEM–EDX, and TEM confirmed the successful formation of ZnS QDs with particle sizes predominantly below 10 nm. The synthesized QDs exhibited remarkable antimicrobial activity against E. coli, P. aeruginosa, and C. albicans. In antibiofilm assays, ZnS QDs achieved 83% and 84% inhibition against E. coli and P. aeruginosa, respectively, while antifungal tests revealed larger inhibition zones compared to CHX, indicating superior antifungal efficacy.

Cytotoxicity evaluation using the MTT assay on L929 fibroblast cells demonstrated over 75% cell viability across all tested concentrations, confirming the biocompatible nature of the ZnS QDs. Moreover, the QDs displayed excellent photocatalytic efficiency in degrading MB and BG dyes, highlighting their potential for environmental remediation. Thermal stability analysis confirmed the structural integrity within the 0–40 °C range, supporting suitability for temperature-sensitive systems.

In conclusion, the green-synthesized ZnS QDs exhibit a multifunctional profile, integrating robust antimicrobial and antifungal efficacy with pronounced photocatalytic activity and inherent biocompatibility. This unique combination establishes them as a highly promising nanomaterial for dual application in both biomedical and environmental remediation fields.
