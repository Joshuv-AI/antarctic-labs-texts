Genome-wide identification of TaeGRASs responsive to biotic stresses and functional analysis of TaeSCL6 in wheat resistance to powdery mildew
Authors: Yuanyuan Guan, Kaige Wang, Junjie Zhao, Xiangyang Miao, Xiangyang Li, Puwen Song, Haiyan Hu, Shengli Zhang, Chengwei Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPowdery mildew is a devastating fungal disease that poses a significant threat to wheat yield and quality worldwide. Identifying resistance genes is highly advantageous for the molecular breeding of resistant cultivars. GRAS proteins are important transcription factors that regulate plant development and stress responses. Nonetheless, their roles in wheat-pathogen interactions remain poorly understood.ResultsIn this study, we used bioinformatics tools to identify and analyze wheat GRAS family genes responsive to biotic stresses and elucidated the function of TaeSCL6 within this family. A total of 179 GRAS genes in wheat were unevenly distributed on 7 chromosomes, and classified into 12 subfamilies based on phylogenetic relationship analysis. Gene duplication analysis revealed 13 pairs of tandem repeats and 142 pairs of segmental duplications, which may account for the rapid expansion of the wheat GRAS family. Expression pattern analysis revealed that 75% of the expressed TaeGRAS genes are responsive to biotic stresses. Few studies have focused on the roles of HAM subfamily genes. Consequently, we concentrated our analysis on the members of the HAM subfamily. Fourteen motifs were identified in the HAM family proteins from both Triticeae species and Arabidopsis, indicating that these motifs were highly conserved during evolution. Promoter analysis indicated that the promoters of HAM genes contain several cis-regulatory elements associated with hormone response, stress response, light response, and growth and development. Both qRT-PCR and RNA-seq data analyses demonstrated that TaeSCL6 responds to Blumeria graminis infection. Therefore, we investigated the role of TaeSCL6 in regulating wheat resistance via RNA interference and barley stripe mosaic virus induced gene silencing. Wheat plants with silenced TaeSCL6 exhibited increased susceptibility to powdery mildew.ConclusionsIn summary, this study not only validates the positive role of TaeSCL6 in wheat resistance to powdery mildew, but also provides candidate gene resources for future breeding of disease-resistance wheat cultivars.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-024-11041-3.Keywords: Wheat, GRAS, TaeSCL6, Powdery mildew, Virus-induced gene silencing, RNA interference

Introduction

Wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) is one of the top three global staples. There is an urgent need to increase wheat production to satisfy increasing global demand. However, sustainable wheat cultivation and production face several environmental threats, including diseases, pests and multiple abiotic stresses [1–4]. Powdery mildew (PM), caused by Blumeria graminis f. sp. tritici (Bgt), is a devastating fungal disease that significantly threatens wheat yield and quality worldwide [5]. Numerous strategies have been proposed to reduce the devastating impact of PM on wheat. The prevalent practice of applying fungicides in the field results in pesticide residues and environmental pollution. Breeding and cultivating resistant wheat varieties has been demonstrated to be a sustainable and effective strategy for disease control. Utilizing the resistance-related genetic determinants is an optimal approach for breeding disease-resistance varieties, encouraging breeders and researchers to identify genes that confer broad-spectrum resistance. Over the past few years, a number of genes involved in defense against B. graminis, such as Pm60 [6], PBDH1607 [7], and Pm5e [8], have been identified. However, most resistance genes are either race-specific or have lost resistance to newly emerged Bgt isolates [9, 10]. Consequently, there remains a pressing need to identify novel resistance genes.

Plants have developed two immune response systems, namely pattern-triggered immunity (PTI) and effector-triggered immunity (ETI), to combat pathogen invasion. These defense responses are mediated by various crucial transcription factors that regulate the temporal and spatial expression of downstream genes. Several large transcription factor families, such as bHLH, MYB, and WRKY, have been reported to participate in stress responses [11–13]. For example, the transcription factors TaMYB391, TaBZR2, and TaLOL2 positively regulate wheat resistance against various pathogens [14–16]. CsWRKY65, SlWRKY8, and GhMYB36 modulate plant resistance to pathogens by regulating the expression of pathogen-related genes (PRs) [17–19]. The NAC family member ONAC083 negatively regulates rice immunity to pathogens by activating the transcription of the target gene OsRFPH2-6 [20]. Collectively, these studies demonstrate that transcription factors are key regulators of plant-pathogen interactions.

The GRAS family is a significant group of plant-specific transcription factors, named after three members: GIBBERELLIN-INSENSITIVE (GAI), REPRESSOR of GAI (RGA) and SCARECROW (SCR) [21], and has been identified in more than 50 plant species. Early studies have demonstrated that GRAS family members play crucial roles in plant development and stress responses [22–25]. For example, the SlGRAS9-SlZHD17 module regulates tomato fruit quality by influencing chlorophyll and carbohydrate metabolism [26]. Two BnGRAS family genes are crucial for rape root development and stress responses [27]. The GRAS transcription factor VaPAT1 positively regulates grape cold tolerance [28]. Overexpression of SlGRAS40 enhances tomato tolerance to abiotic stresses and affects auxin and gibberellin signaling accumulation [29]. These studies highlight the diverse roles of GRAS family members in plant growth and development. Furthermore, several reports on the involvement of GRAS genes in plant disease resistance have been published in recent years. Scarecrow-like (SCL) proteins, belonging to a subfamily of the GRAS family, play crucial roles in plant immunity. For example, OsSCL7 has been reported to positively regulate rice resistance to M. oryzae [30]. Nevertheless, research on the role of the GRAS family in wheat disease resistance remains relatively limited.

In the present study, we identified a gene, TaeSCL6, that is associated with resistance to powdery mildew in wheat. TaeSCL6 is a member of the GRAS family, which prompted a comprehensive genome-wide analysis of GRAS family genes in wheat via bioinformatics techniques. A total of 179 GRAS genes from wheat were clustered and systematically analyzed for phylogenetic classification, conserved domains and motifs, and responses to biotic stresses. Our results indicated that the expression of TaeSCL6 was altered upon infection with Blumeria graminis. To further investigate the role of TaeSCL6 in wheat disease resistance, we silenced TaeSCL6 using RNA interference (RNAi) and barley stripe mosaic virus induced gene silencing (BSMV-VIGS) methods. We found that plants with silencing TaeSCL6 exhibited increased susceptibility to powdery mildew. These findings suggest that TaeSCL6 may serve as a potential target for biotechnological manipulation to develop disease-resistant wheat cultivars.

Materials and methods

Plant and fungal material culture

The common wheat cultivar Aikang58 (AK58) was used for cloning TaeSCL6 and performing RNAi transformation. The common wheat cultivar Bainong207 (with moderate resistance to powdery mildew) was used to analyze the expression pattern of TaeSCL6 and for BSMV-VIGS experiments. The common wheat cultivar Sumai3 was used to maintain the Bgt fungus. All the plants were cultivated in controlled chambers at 22 °C with a 16-h light and 8-h dark photoperiod.

The mixed races of Bgt used in this study were collected from wheat fields in Henan (China) and maintained on Sumai3 (known for high susceptibility to powdery mildew) in an incubator at 23 °C with 14-h-light and 18 °C with 10-h-dark conditions at approximately relative humidity.

Identification of GRAS gene family members in Triticeae species

GRAS family proteins were identified as follows: Firstly, raw GRAS proteins from T. aestivum, Hordeum vulgare, Aegilops tauschii, Triticum dicoccoides, Triticum urartu and Arabidopsis thaliana were downloaded from the Ensembl Plants database (http://plants.ensembl.org/info/data/ftp/index.html). BLASTP comparisons (E-value < 1 × 10− 5) were conducted to identify true GRAS members from the aforementioned putative genes, using GRAS protein sequences from Arabidopsis thaliana as references. Secondly, typical GRAS conserved domains (PF03514), were obtained from the Pfam database (http://pfam.xfam.org/) and used as search models. Proteins containing these conserved domains were selected by searching several different protein databases using the Hidden Markov Model (HMM) implemented in TBtools. When a GRAS gene had multiple transcripts, the longest transcript was retained for further analysis. The integrity of conserved domains of GRAS proteins was confirmed in the NCBI-CDD (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/-cdd/). Thirdly, after removing redundancies among GRAS proteins obtained using the aforementioned two methods, we verified whether the candidate GRAS proteins contained the conserved domains of GRAS family by searching the NCBI CD Search (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure/cdd/wrpsb.cgi), SMART (http://smart.embl-heidelberg.de/) and Pfam databases, subsequently deleting the proteins without GRAS conserved domain. Finally, GRAS proteins in Triticeae species were identified.

We obtained GRAS protein sequences of A. thaliana from TAIR (https://www.Arabidopsis.org, accessed on 8 March 2024) [31, 32].

Phylogenetic analysis of the GRAS genes

To analyze the genetic diversity of the GRAS family in wheat, and multiple sequence alignments of GRAS proteins were conducted using MUSCLE Wrapper Version 5 software of TBtools, and the phylogenetic tree was built using the Maximum likelihood method with 1,000 bootstrap replicates in TBtools software [33]. The phylogenetic tree was visualized using the online tool Evolview (https://www.evolgenius.info/evolview/).

Chromosomal mapping and distribution analysis of the GRAS genes

All the identified genes were mapped to 7 chromosomes according to the chromosomal location information by TBtools software. The number and chromosomal distribution of GRAS genes were analyzed using Excel.

Gene duplications and synteny analyses of GRAS genes

Gene duplications of GRAS genes were predicted using the MCScanX function in TBtools, employing default parameters [34]. MCScanX Diamond output was used to calculate the replication events of the genomes of Triticeae species and A. thaliana. The duplication type of each GRAS gene was analyzed via the Duplicate_gene_classifier program in MCScanX (https://github.com/wyp1125/MCScanX, accessed on 7 September 2024). The synteny of GRAS genes from Triticeae species and A. thaliana was visualized using the One-Step MCScanX in TBtools software. The Dual Synteny Plot for the MCScanX in TBtools software was used to visualize synteny.

Detection of conserved motifs and domains

Analysis of conserved motifs and domains can provide evidence to predict the functions of genes, and the conserved motifs of GRAS proteins were analyzed using the online programs MEME (http://meme-suite.org) and Smart Motif (http://smart.embl-heidelberg.de/), with the known GRAS protein sequences of Arabidopsis thaliana as a control. Conserved motifs of HAM proteins were identified using the following parameters: each protein sequence contained any number of repetitions, maximum number of motifs was 25, the motif width ranged from 6 to 50 [35]. Conserved domains were identified using the online program Batch CD-Search. Gene Structure View (Advanced) in TBtools software was used to visualize the conserved motifs and domains.

Analysis of Cis-acting elements in the HAM promoter

Cis-acting elements are responsible for regulating gene transcription, so the elements of promoters were analyzed. A 2000 bp upstream of the start codon was extracted and used as a hypothetical promoter. These sequences were then submitted to the online tool PlantCARE (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.Be/webtools/plantcare/html/) and PLACE (https://www.dna.affrc.go.jp/PLACE/?action=newplace) to predict putative cis-acting regulatory elements in the HAM subfamily proteins. The final results were analyzed and visualized using TBtools.

Analysis of GRAS expression profiles using RNA-seq data

To identify the wheat GRAS genes responsive to biotic stress, we used RNA-seq data from the public WheatOmics 1.0 dataset and identified 179 GRAS genes in wheat [36]. The data were generated from wheat cultivar N9134 treated with Bgt and Pst, and wheat cultivar Chinese Spring treated with chitin and Flag22. The expression patterns of GRAS genes in response to various stresses (including pathogens-Bgt and Pst, two PAMPs-chitin and Flag22) were visualized as a heatmap using TBtools.

Analysis of expression pattern of TaeSCL6 during Bgt infection by qRT-PCR

The expression patterns of taeSCL6 were analyzed in the leaves upon Bgt infection. Wheat Bainong207 leaves were inoculated with Bgt, when the plants reached the third-leaf stage and subsequently harvested at 2, 6, 12, 24, 48 and 72 hpi. Water-sprayed plants served as controls. All samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C for total RNA extraction and qRT-PCR analysis. Three independent biological replicates were performed for each treatment, and each treatment was repeated three times.

Barley stripe mosaic virus induced gene silencing (BSMV-VIGS)

To explore the function of TaeSCL6 during wheat infected with Bgt, the BSMV-induced gene silencing system was employed to reduce the expression level of TaeSCL6. Gene silencing in wheat leaves using BSMV was conducted according to the method described by Yuan et al. [37]. A 277 bp fragment of the target gene (from + 66 to + 342) was amplified using specific primers and inserted into the BSMV:γ vector via homologous recombination to produce the recombination vectors BSMV: SCL and BSMV: TaPDS, with the BSMV:γ vector serving as a control. The fully expanded second leaves of the wheat cultivar Bainong207 were infected with in vitro-transcribed viruses (mMESSAGE mMACHINE™ T7, Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA) containing BSMV: SCL, BSMV: TaPDS, and BSMV:γ. The completely expanded fourth leaves showing visible virus symptoms at 15 days after infection were collected for Bgt inoculation, and the silencing efficiency was measured via qRT-PCR. Disease symptoms were recorded at 7 days post-inoculation (dpi). All primers used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S8.

Generation of transgenic plants with silencing TaeSCL6 via RNAi

To further investigate the function of TaeSCL6, the transgenic plants were generated via RNAi. A 400 bp fragment containing the sequence of TaeSCL6 was amplified from wheat cDNA via PCR using specific primers, and subsequently cloned and inserted into the vector pMD18-T for sequencing. The PCR products were subcloned into the vector pTCK303 in an inverted orientation using two pairs of restriction endonuclease (Bam H I/Kpn I and Sac I/Spe I), producing the recombinant plasmid pTCK303:TaeSCL6. This plasmid was then transformed into wheat cultivar AK58 via Agrobacterium-mediated genetic transformation to obtain transgenic plants (RNAi-TaeSCL6), following the method described by Wang et al. [38]. Candidate positive transgenic lines were identified using GUS staining, as the vector pTCK303 contained the glucuronidase (GUS) reporter gene. The GUS staining solution was purchased from Hua Yue-yang, Beijing. Leaves were cut and immersed in the solution overnight at 37℃ in the dark, and subsequently decolorized with 75% ethanol. Positive transgenic plants in the T1 and T2 generations were selected and confirmed by quantifying the expression level of TaeSCL6 via qRT-PCR. All primers used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S8.

Resistance analysis

Bgt resistance analysis was performed by infecting detached leaves at the seedling stage. The fully expanded fourth detached leaves from the TaeSCL6-silenced plants and WT plants were cut into two sections, placed on agar media supplemented with 20 mg/L benzimidazole (6-BA), and then inoculated with mixed Bgt races under conditions of 22 °C/16 h light and 18 °C/8 h darkness for 7 days [39]. The disease symptom phenotype was then photographed at 7 dpi.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was isolated from wheat plants using TRizol reagent. cDNA was synthesized using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Takara Bio Inc.) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The qRT-PCR reaction was performed using TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ II (Tli RNase H Plus) (Takara Bio. Inc) and the reaction system was with 40 cycles of 95℃ for 5 s and 60℃ for 20 s on the 7500 Fast Real-Time PCR system (Applied Biosystems). Each gene was determined with three biological and technical replicates for each sample. Tubulin was used as an internal control, and the expression levels of TaeSCL6 were analyzed using the 2−ΔΔCt method for real-time PCR data analysis.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using Microsoft Excel to determine sample averages and standard errors, followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison tests to examine significant differences between samples.

Results

Phylogenetic analysis of the GRAS proteins in wheat

To characterize GRAS family proteins in Triticeae species, we employed a simple HMM search software to identify GRASs, which were subsequently validated using two online tools, SMART and NCBI-CDD. In total, we identified 179, 38, 57, 54, and 108 putative GRAS genes from Triticum aestivum, Triticum urartu, Aegilops tauschii, Hordeum vulgare, and Triticum dicoccoides, respectively. To explore evolutionary relationships of GRAS family proteins, we constructed a phylogenetic tree using the TBtools. Based on the homology of GRAS proteins in Arabidopsis thaliana, the GRAS genes from Triticeae species are categorized into 3 clades and 12 subfamilies, including LISCL, SHR, PAT1, DELLA, SCL3, DLT, SCR, LAS, SCL4/7, HAM, Os4, and Os19 (Fig. 1). The Os4 and Os19 subfamilies contain proteins exclusively from Triticeae species, indicating that these genes may have undergone specialization during the evolutionary process of Triticeae. The remaining subfamilies include proteins from both Triticeae species and Arabidopsis thaliana. Phylogenetic analysis revealed that TaeGRAS proteins have the closest evolutionary relationship with members of T. dicoccoides, a comparable relationship with members of H. vulgare and Ae. tauschii and a slightly distinct relationship with Triticum urartu. Nonetheless, the GRAS proteins from Triticeae species exhibited high similarity, suggesting a relatively conservative evolution of GRAS genes after Triticeae species speciation.

The numbers of GRAS genes in each subfamily across all analyzed species are shown in Table 1. As a heterohexaploid species, wheat results from two rounds of hybridization [40]. Typically, the number of genes in one family in T. aestivum (AABBDD) and T. dicoccoides (AABB) is approximately three times and two times that in the diploid Triticeae species, respectively. In this study, the number of GRAS genes in T. aestivum was 4.71, 3.14, 3.31, and 1.66 times that in T. urartu (AA), Ae. tauschii (DD) and H. vulgare (HH), and T. dicoccoides, respectively. The numbers in T. dicoccoides were 2.84, 1.89, and 2 times that in T. urartu, Ae. tauschii and H. vulgare, respectively. This phenomenon is partly due to the polyploidization of common wheat. However, even when ploidy levels were considered, the number of GRAS genes in hexaploid wheat was significantly greater than that in diploid wheat, whereas the number of GRAS genes in T. dicoccoides was consistent with the expected value.

Numbers of GRAS genes from different Triticeae species in each subfamily

The distributions of GRAS genes among the different subfamilies were significantly different. The largest subfamily, LISCL, comprised 134 genes from Triticeae species, accounting for 31%, followed by PAT1 (73, 15.7%). The distributions of GRAS genes across the chromosomes and subgenomes of the five Triticeae species are shown in Table 2; Fig. 2. The GRAS genes were differentially distributed on the seven chromosomes of the five Triticeae species, with only one gene from common wheat failing to map to any chromosome (Fig. 2). Among the five Triticeae species, chromosome 4 contained the greatest number of GRAS genes, while chromosome 6 presented the lowest number compared to the other chromosomes.

Chromosomal distribution of TaeGRAS genes

The number of GRAS genes (with 179 members) in common wheat was significantly greater than that in T. dicoccoides, T. urartu, Ae. tauschii, and H. vulgare, which had 108, 38, 57, and 54 GRAS genes, respectively. Compared to T. urartu, the number of GRAS genes in subgenome A of common wheat increased by 24 genes. The quantity of GRAS genes in the B and D subgenomes of common wheat was similar to that in T. dicoccoides and Ae. tauschii, respectively, with an increase of 4 in the subgenome B and a decrease of 5 in the subgenome D. These data suggest that the GRAS genes in Triticeae underwent a complex evolutionary process, including gene amplification, retention, and loss.

Gene duplication and syntenic analyses of TaeGRAS genes

To investigate the syntenic relationships of the GRAS family genes within species, we identified a total of 173 pairs of syntenic relationships through intraspecific synteny analysis by MCScanX software and found 13 pairs of tandem duplicated genes among 179 TaeGRAS genes (Fig. 3, Table S1). Furthermore, among the 179 TaeGRAS genes, we predicted 142 genes resulting from whole genome duplication (WGD) or segmental duplication, 21 from tandem replication, 9 from proximal duplication, and 7 from dispersed duplication events (Table S2). The ratio of nonsynonymous (Ka) to synonymous (Ks) factors serves as an indicator of selective pressure in duplication events; thus, we calculated the Ka/Ks ratio for 142 pairs (Table S3). The Ka/Ks values of all pairs were less than 1 (mean value 0.245), indicating that segmental duplication is a major factor in the gene expansion of TaeGRAS family, and that purifying selection significantly impacts the evolution of these members. Conserved motif and domain analysis of HAM proteins

To gain a deeper understanding of the basic information of HAM family members, the motif diversity and motif composition of HAM proteins in Triticeae were further identified via MEME motif scanning software and Smart Motif. A total of 25 motifs were identified and assigned from motif 1 to motif 25 (Fig. 5). Generally, the distribution and characteristics of conserved motifs were similar in the same subfamilies; however, the motif composition of HAM subfamily proteins differed. The number of motifs varies greatly in HAM proteins from Triticeae species. The data showed that TaeSCL6 has 21 motifs, whereas TuG1812G0500005418.01 has only 9 motifs, indicating the complexity and evolution of Triticeae species. All HAM proteins contained motifs 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 15, 17 and 21, suggesting that these 14 motifs are highly conserved in this subfamily and play a decisive role in the HAM subfamily. Motifs 7, 13, 14, 16, 20, 22, 23, 24 and 25 were found exclusively in Triticeae species, and not in Arabidopsis thaliana (Fig. 5B, Table S5), indicating that there is a significant difference in the motif composition between species with distant genetic relationships. The HAM proteins within the same group exhibit similar motifs, which is consistent with the results of the phylogenetic analysis.

We used the Gene Structure View (Advanced) program to conduct a domain analysis of HAM proteins. A total of four types of conserved domains were identified (Fig. 5C). All the HAM genes contain the GRAS superfamily domain. In addition, the domains of HAM members in Triticeae species also include the PHA03378 superfamily, NB-ARC superfamily, and KLF1_2_4_N superfamily, whereas these three conserved domains are absent in Arabidopsis thaliana. NB-ARC superfamily has been reported to have disease resistance, and members of KLF1_2_4-N superfamily can act as transcriptional activators or inhibitors.

Cis-acting regulatory analysis of HAM genes

Cis-regulatory elements (CREs) are responsible for regulating gene transcription during plant growth and development, so the promoters of HAM genes were analyzed to predict their functions. A 2 kb upstream region of the 18 TaeHAM gene promoters was extracted for CRE analysis. The analysis revealed that each HAM promoter contains several CREs that can be classified into four major groups: hormone-responsive elements, environmental signal response components, growth and development elements, and transcription factor binding sites (Fig. 6, Table S6). Almost all HAM promoters contain the CAT-Box element, which is associated with meristem development. For the hormone-responsive elements, nearly all the members contain ABRE, CGTCA-Motif, and TGACG-Motif, while all HAM genes contain G-Box elements related to the light response, STRE related to stress response, and MYB and MYC elements related to environmental adaptability. These data indicate that the functions of HAM proteins, regardless of their specific members, are closely associated with light, stress, and hormonal responses. Additionally, two copies of TaeSCL6 (TraesCS6B02g277400 and TraesCS6D02g229400) and three copies of another gene (TraesCS4A02g088500, TraesCS4B02g113300, and TraesCS4D02g216200) contain defense response elements, suggesting a potential role in wheat immunity.

Expression profiles of GRAS genes in wheat inoculated with pathogens

To explore the potential roles of GRAS genes in wheat response to biotic stresses, their expression patterns were examined. The expression patterns of GRAS genes were analyzed in wheat under two biotic stresses (powdery mildew and stripe rust) and two PAMPs (Flag 22 and chitin) using RNA-seq data from WheatOmics. The data are displayed in a heatmap (Fig. 7). Nearly 50% of the TaeGRAS genes (93) exhibited no or extremely low expression. Genes from the DLT, Os19, LAS, Os4 and SHR subfamilies were completely unexpressed, while the majority of genes in the PAT1 subfamily were expressed. Among the expressed GRAS genes, a total of 66 genes across six subfamilies responded to biotic stresses and PAMPs, such as PAT1 (18), HAM (8), DELLA (6), SCL4/7 (3), SCR (9) and LISCL (22), which are listed in Table S7. Three genes (TraesCS4B02g043100, TraesCS4B02g271000 and TraesCS4D02G040400) in the DELLA subfamily significantly responded to biotic stresses and two PAMPs. All the genes in the SCL4/7 subfamily significantly responded to Flag22 and chitin but did not respond to Bgt or Pst infection. Most genes in the PAT1 and LISCL subfamilies also exhibited significant responsiveness to pathogens and PAMPs. These findings indicate that 75% of the GRAS genes expressed in common wheat might play a role in regulating wheat disease resistance.

Notably, the HAM subfamily in wheat comprises 18 members, of which only nine genes are expressed. Among these genes, six–TraesCS6D02G229400, TraesCS6A02G247200 (TaeSCL6), TraesCS6B02G277400, TraesCS1B02G223400, TraesCS1D02G212800 and TraesCS1A02G209400 were downregulated and upregulated by Bgt and Pst infection, respectively, but these genes were hardly responsive to Flag22 and chitin treatment. Two genes, TraesCS7D02G011400 and TraesCS4A02G482000, responded to Flag22 and chitin treatment but did not respond to Bgt or Pst infection. These findings suggested that these six HAM genes (including TaeSCL6) might be involved in regulating wheat immunity.

Furthermore, we examined the expression patterns of TaeSCL6 in response to Bgt infection using qRT-PCR, which revealed a significant increase in TaeSCL6 expression in wheat seedlings at 6 and 12 h post Bgt infection (Fig. S2). The results showed that TaeSCL6 responded to Bgt fungus infection, highlighting its potential role in disease resistance.

Knockdown of TaeSCL6 increases wheat susceptibility to powdery mildew

To assess the role of TaeSCL6 in regulating the wheat defense response, its expression was silenced in the wheat cultivar Bainong207 through the BSMV-VIGS method to evaluate resistance. The three copies of the TaeSCL6 sequence are highly homologous, with only a few single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and indels; their amino acid sequences are highly similar (Fig. S3). Therefore, the consensus sequence was selected for silencing. The relative expression levels of TaeSCL6 in the silenced plants were determined by qRT-PCR, revealing significant decreases of approximately 4.8-, 3.7-, and 3.1-fold (Fig. 8A). At seven days post-Bgt infection, TaeSCL6-silenced leaves exhibited greater susceptibility to Bgt than did control leaves (BSMV:γ inoculated plants) (Fig. 8B).

To confirm the role of TaeSCL6 in conferring powdery mildew resistance, it was silenced in the wheat cultivar AK58 background via the stable genetic transformation method–RNA interference (RNAi). qRT-PCR experiments revealed that the RNAi lines R1, R2, R3 and R4 lines were effectively silenced, achieving a reduction of 68–79% (Fig. 8C). Resistance analysis revealed that different RNAi-TaeSCL6 transgenic lines (R1, R2, R3 and R4) in the F1 and F2 generations were more susceptible to Bgt and exhibited increased powdery mildew symptoms compared to wild-type (WT) plants (Fig. 8D). The phenotypes of the conidia and mycelial growth were examined under a microscope. The spore germination rate of transgenic plants was faster than that of the WT plants at 24 h post-inoculation (hpi). At 48 and 72 hpi, the hyphal mass was significantly greater in the silenced plants than in the WT plants (Fig. 8E). In conclusion, the results from BSMV-VIGS and RNAi transgenic experiments consistently indicate that TaeSCL6 positively regulates wheat resistance to powdery mildew.

Discussion

Numerous studies have highlighted the diverse roles of GRAS genes in plant growth and response to both abiotic and biotic stresses. The Genome-wide analyses of the GRAS family have been performed in barley, cocoa beans, soybean, and cotton [42–45]. Wheat is a vital cereal crop cultivated worldwide, and the analysis of the GRAS family has been carried out [46, 47]; however, these studies did not identify the specific TaeGRAS genes involved in the response of wheat to biotic stresses. In this study, we performed a comprehensive analysis of 179 GRAS genes in wheat, including their phylogenetic relationships, chromosomal distributions, gene duplications, syntenic relationships, conserved motifs and domains, expression profiles under biotic stresses, and functional analysis of TaeSCL6.

A large number of these genes were identified in wheat (179 GRAS) compared to A. thaliana (33) and T. urartu (38), indicating that wheat possesses a large genome and has evolved with a relatively high ploidy level [48]. Our analysis revealed that GRAS genes from the five Triticeae species were unevenly distributed across the 7 chromosomes. Phylogenetic analysis revealed that TaeGRAS genes can be classified into 12 subfamilies including SCL3, Os19, SCL4/7, LAS, DLT, SCR, SHR, Os4, LISCL, PAT1, DELLA, and HAM. The DELLA and PAT1 subfamilies affect plant development by regulating gibberellin signaling [49, 50]. The SCR/SHR complex is essential for root development [51], indicating that TaeGRAS genes may play diverse roles in plant developmental processes. Tandem and segmental duplications are regarded as the primary mechanisms driving gene expansion and the evolution of plant genomes [41]. The collinearity analysis conducted in our study revealed 13 pairs of tandem duplications and 142 pairs of segmental duplications within the TaeGRAS family, which may play significant roles in gene expansion and evolution of the GRAS family in T. aestivum.

To further identify the TaeGRAS genes responsive to biotic stress, we conducted an expression pattern analysis, which revealed that more than half of the examined TaeGRAS genes were not expressed. Nevertheless, the majority of the expressed TaeGRAS genes appear to respond to pathogen infection or PAMP treatment, including genes in the PAT1, HAM, DELLA, SCR, SCL4/7 and LISCL subfamilies, which are known to be pivotal in plant resistance according to previous research [30, 52, 53]. Therefore, our analysis provides potential gene resources related to biotic stress responses for investigating their functions or breeding wheat resistance cultivars. Numerous studies have investigated the functions of GRAS genes; however, few studies have been conducted on the roles of the HAM subfamily of GRAS in plant resistance. Wheat powdery mildew is one of the most destructive diseases affecting wheat. Cultivating broad-spectrum and durable disease-resistant varieties remains the most promising strategy for reducing Bgt infection. However, the natural evolution of new pathogens can overcome resistance, necessitating continuous exploration of new types of resistance-related genes. Therefore, the identification of disease-related genes remains urgent. In the present study, we focused not only on identifying GRAS genes related to disease resistance but also on investigating the functions of HAM genes in wheat.

Generally, genes within the same subfamily share similar motif compositions [54]. However, the number and types of motifs in HAM proteins vary among different Triticeae species, suggesting that HAM genes have undergone a complex evolution and functional specialization. This raises the question of whether the functions of TaeHAM proteins are conserved across different plant species. In the present study, Cis-acting element analysis revealed that almost all TaeHAM promoters contain CAT-Box elements, which are crucial for regulating meristem development. This finding aligns with previous reports regarding the function of the HAM subfamily in regulating shoot and root development [55, 56]. Importantly, we also found that several TaeHAM promoters possess elements related to stress and defense responses, such as TaeSCL6, suggesting their roles in plant-pathogen interactions. The function of TaeSCL6 is still unclear. According to previous studies, SCLs, a member of the HAM family and the targets of miR171, play important roles in plant immune responses [57–59]. For example, the miR171a-SCL6 module mediates cotton resistance by regulating GhPR1 expression [57]. Osa-miR171b, which targets SCL6, confers resistance to rice stripe virus infection [60]. These studies suggest that SCLs play important roles in plant immunity, leading us to deduce that TaeSCL6 may also contribute to the immune response in wheat.

The expression profile provides valuable insights into protein functions. In this study, RNA-seq data revealed that the expression level of TaeSCL6 was downregulated in the wheat cultivar N9134 upon Bgt infection. We also used qRT-PCR to confirm these results and found that TaeSCL6 was rapidly upregulated after Bgt infection in the wheat cultivar Bainong207 (with moderate resistance to powdery mildew). The disease response of TaeSCL6 differed between the two methods used, potentially indicating distinct resistance response trends in both susceptible and resistant wheat genotypes. Overall, the expression pattern analysis provides further evidence supporting the potential functions of TaeSCL6 in wheat-pathogen interactions.

To investigate the role of TaeSCL6 in wheat resistance to powdery mildew, we employed RNAi and BSMV-VIGS methods to silence its expression. The results indicated that downregulation of TaeSCL6 reduced wheat resistance to powdery mildew, suggesting that TaeSCL6 functions as a positive regulator of wheat immunity. Subsequent studies will further elucidate the mechanisms by which TaeSCL6 operates. Further research will perform the overexpression of TaeSCL6 using molecular techniques to develop wheat cultivars with durable and broad-spectrum resistance. The findings of our study not only contribute to the understanding of the evolutionary process of wheat GRAS genes but also provide gene/genetic resources for breeding wheat disease-resistance cultivars.

Conclusion

In conclusion, a total of 436 GRAS genes were identified across five Triticeae species and classified into 3 clades and 12 subfamilies through phylogenetic analysis. The GRAS genes presented a differential distribution among the seven chromosomes of all five Triticeae species. Gene amplification of TaeGRAS mainly occurs in the subgenome. B. There exists the gene duplication in the wheat GRAS family due to segmental duplication. Expression pattern analysis revealed that almost 50% of the TaeGRAS genes could not be detected, nonetheless, more than 70% of the expressed GRAS genes (66) were responsive to biotic stress, indicating their roles in regulating wheat-pathogen interactions. All HAM subfamily genes contained elements associated with hormone responsiveness, stress response, and light response, while only TaeSCL6 (TraesCS6B02g277400 and TraesCS6D02g229400) and another gene (TraesCS4A02g088500, TraesCS4B02g113300, and TraesCS4D02g216200) contained defense-responsive elements. Additionally, silencing TaeSCL6 reduced powdery mildew resistance in the common wheat cultivars AK58 and Bainong207. These findings not only enhance our understanding of the evolutionary processes of GRAS genes in Triticeae species, but also provide valuable gene resources associated with responses to biotic stress within the GRAS family.
