JVLA Measurement of Grain Size in the Compact Dust Ring around Class I Protostar WL 17 astropy ( Astropy Collaboration et al. 2013 ; Astropy Collaboration et al. 2018 ; Astropy Collaboration et al. 2022 ) , Matplotlib ( Hunter 2007 ) , Numpy ( van der Walt et al. 2011 ) Facilities: JVLA, ALMA
Jun Hashimoto, Hauyu Baobab Liu, Ruobing Dong, Beibei Liu, Takayuki Muto

Abstract
The maximum grain size in protoplanetary disks is a critical parameter for planet formation, as the efficiency of mechanisms like streaming instability and pebble accretion depend on grain size. Even young class 0/I objects, such as HL Tau, show substructures in their disks, indicating the potential for early planet formation. In this study, we investigated the grain size in the dust surrounding the class I object WL 17 using the Karl G. Jansky Very Large Array. Observations were conducted across seven frequency bands (Q, Ka, K, Ku, X, C, and S bands) ranging from 2 to 48 GHz, corresponding to wavelengths of 15 cm to 6.3 mm, with a spatial resolution exceeding 0 . ′′ \farcs 5. While the ring structure at 0 . ′′ \farcs 1 of WL 17 remains unresolved in our data, its emission is clearly detected at all observed frequencies, except at 2 GHz. To estimate the maximum grain size ( a max a_{\rm max} ) within the ring, we compared the observed spectral energy distribution (SED) with theoretical SEDs calculated for various a max a_{\rm max} values using radiative transfer models. Assuming the dust opacity follows the DSHARP model, our analysis suggests that certain structures internal to the ring achieved a maximum grain size of approximately 4.2 mm. Additionally, we discuss the gravitational stability of the ring and the potential planetary core mass that could form through pebble accretion within the structure.



I
Introduction

The discovery of substructures in the HL Tau disk
(
ALMA Partnership et al. 2015
)
represents a significant breakthrough in the field of planet formation. Traditionally, planet formation in the standard core-accretion scenario has been believed to take more than 1 Myr
(
Pollack et al. 1996
, e.g.,)
. However, the presence of substructures in the young HL Tau disk, which is less than 1 Myr, suggests that planet formation occurs much earlier than previously expected, potentially indicating the presence of forming planets in the disk
(
Dong et al. 2015b
)
. The presence of giant planets is often inferred by assuming that most substructures in disks are formed by these planets
(
Paardekooper et al. 2023
, e.g.,)
. Furthermore, recent studies of the atmospheres in directly imaged exoplanets suggest that a solid accretion mass of approximately 100
M_{\earth}
is needed to explain the metal enrichment in these exoplanets
(
GRAVITY Collaboration et al. 2020
, e.g.,
\beta
Pic b;)
. Given that typical protoplanetary disks around Class II objects with a solar mass have a solid mass of approximately 10
M_{\earth}
inferred by assuming optically thin (sub)millimeter emission
(
Ansdell et al. 2017
, e.g.,)
, much less than the total solid mass in typical planetary systems
(
Manara et al. 2018
)
, planet formation earlier than 1 Myr might be necessary to unravel this puzzle.

Nevertheless, recent findings from the eDISK project, The Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array (ALMA) large program in cycle 7, which focuses on searching for substructures around protostars younger than 1 Myr, reported only a few distinct substructures
(
Ohashi et al. 2023
)
. This contrasts with class II objects, where substructures have commonly been observed in disks
(
Andrews et al. 2018
, e.g., DSHARP in)
. The scarcity of substructures around protostars suggests that they may be rare in these early stages of planet formation. The lack of substructure may also be attributed to the disk’s optical depth in ALMA wavelengths at the Class 0/I stage
(
Li et al. 2017
;
Galván-Madrid et al. 2018
;
Ko et al. 2020
;
Liu 2021
;
Xu 2022
, e.g.,)
. This gives additional motivation to explore longer, centimeter wavelengths to determine the amount of material in the disk. By studying the physical and chemical properties of substructures around protostars, we could gain a better understanding of the rapid planet formation processes that occur within a timescale of less than 1 Myr.

The maximum grain size (
a_{\rm max}
) is one of the key parameters in planet formation. The efficiency of mechanisms such as streaming instability
(
Johansen et al. 2014
, e.g.,)
and pebble accretion
(
Ormel & Klahr 2010
;
Lambrechts & Johansen 2012
, e.g.,)
, key steps in planet formation, crucially depend on
a_{\rm max}

(
Liu & Ji 2020
;
Drążkowska et al. 2023
, e.g.,)
.

Two methods exist for determining the sizes of dust particles based on millimeter/centimeter (mm/cm) continuum emission. The first approach involves observing polarized dust continuum emission in the mm/cm range, resulting from scattering
(
Kataoka et al. 2015
, e.g.,)
. The polarization fraction in the dust emission reaches its maximum when the dust grains grow to a size of approximately
a_{\rm max}\sim\lambda/2\pi
, where
\lambda
represents the observing wavelength. This method requires a signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of approximately 100–1,000 in Stokes
I
and is well-suited for studying bright objects like DSHARP disks (Andrews et al. 2018). The second approach involves constructing the spectral energy distribution
(
Liu 2019
, SED, e.g.,)
. This method relies on the fact that grains emit thermal radiation most efficiently at wavelengths similar to their sizes
(
Draine 2006
, e.g.,)
. By analyzing the SED, information about the grain sizes can be inferred.

The grain sizes in disks around class 0/I objects have been estimated to be sub-millimeter sizes by ALMA polarization observations
(
Stephens et al. 2017
;
Lee et al. 2021
;
Aso et al. 2021
;
Sadavoy et al. 2019
, e.g., HL Tau, HH 212, TMC-1A, GSS 30 IRS 1,)
. ALMA observations are particularly sensitive to sub-millimeter-sized grains, whereas the Karl G. Jansky Very Large Array (JVLA) is more suited for detecting millimeter- to centimeter-sized grains. This makes JVLA observations advantageous for investigating grain growth at these larger scales. Prescence of grain growth to centimeter sizes has been suggested in some disks around protostars, such as CB 26 and EC 53
(
Zhang et al. 2021
;
Lee et al. 2020b
, e.g.,)
.

Several class 0/I objects, such as HL Tau, WL 17, GY 91, L1527, Oph IRS 63, HH111 VLA1, TMC-1A, IRAS 04169
+
2702, L1489 IRS, Oph A SM1, have displayed substructures in their disks
(
ALMA Partnership et al. 2015
;
Sheehan & Eisner 2017
;
Sheehan & Eisner 2018
;
Nakatani et al. 2020
;
Segura-Cox et al. 2020
;
Lee et al. 2020a
;
Aso et al. 2021
;
Ohashi et al. 2023
;
Maureira et al. 2024
)
.
Jiang & Ormel 2023
propose that dust rings serve as highly efficient regions for planet formation through pebble accretion. In this paper, we present comprehensive multi-wavelength observations of WL 17 conducted with the JVLA.

WL 17 is an M3-type class-I protostar
(
McClure et al. 2010
)
with a mass of 0.3
M_{\sun}

(
Han et al. 2023
)
, situated at a distance of 135.6 pc in the Ophiuchus molecular cloud
(
Gaia Collaboration et al. 2016
;
Gaia Collaboration et al. 2022
)
, and it is known to possess a dust ring with a low inclination angle of 34 deg
(
Sheehan & Eisner 2017
;
Shoshi et al. 2024
)
while being deeply embedded in its envelope
(
Evans et al. 2009
)
.
Sadavoy et al. 2019
found that WL 17 showed no polarization signal at
\lambda=1.3
mm, with a 3
\sigma
upper limit of
<0.3
%. This suggests that the grain size in WL 17’s ring is either smaller or larger than
a_{\rm max}\sim\lambda/2\pi\sim 200

\mu
m. To further determine the grain size in WL 17, analyzing the SED including ALMA and JVLA data is helpful.

III
Constraints on Maximum Grain Sizes

III.1
Qualitative interpretation for the SED

The
\alpha_{\rm 100\mathchar 45345GHz}=2.30\pm 0.01
result at 100–345 GHz
(
Gulick et al. 2021
)
can be explained by marginally optically thick dust thermal emission (
Hildebrand 1983
).
A plausible interpretation for the 14–48 GHz spectral feature is optically thick dust emission. Although the thermal dust emission at lower frequencies of 14–48 GHz is generally optically thinner, if localized high-density structures are present, the dust emission could be optically thick. The flux density of the optically thinner part decreases with wavelength very rapidly. Consequently, at lower frequencies, the emission from the optically thicker substructures can be seen.

A simple model that explains the
>
14 GHz SED of the WL 17 ring thus must include at least two distinct dust emission component: one low column density disk halo that is not optically thick at
\sim
200–345 GHz, and one high column density component that is optically thick even at
\lesssim
30 GHz.
The optically thinner component has been probed in the spatially resolved spectral index analysis of
Han et al. 2023
.
If these two dust emission components have different spatial distributions (e.g., due to dust segregation), the observations at
>
200 GHz and
<
100 GHz may resolve different morphology.
This is consistent with what was found in the previous high angular resolution ALMA Band 3 and 7 observations
(
Han et al. 2023
)
.

An alternative possibility to explain the low spectral indices at 14–26 GHz is considering the emission of spinning nanometer-sized dust, which might have been detected in the planet-forming disk PDS 70 (
Liu et al. 2024a
) and a few other protoplanetary disks (
Greaves et al. 2018
;
Hoang et al. 2018
;
Greaves & Mason 2022
)
1
1

1

Note that the GBT measurements of flux densities presented in
Greaves et al. 2018
and
Greaves & Mason 2022
, in many cases, deviated largely from the JVLA measurements
(
Chung et al. 2025
)
.

In our present case study of WL 17, the emission of spinning nanometer-sized dust is not considered since
it relies on substantial fine-tuning of the parameters in the radiative transfer modeling (c.f. Section
III.2
) to match the observed SEDs.
In addition, both the theories and the previous claimed detections in the protoplanetary disks remain uncertain.
If the emission of spinning nanometer-sized dust is indeed prominent at
\sim
20–30 GHz, a radiative transfer model without considering this emission mechanism (Section
III.2
) will overestimate
a_{\mbox{\scriptsize max}}
and dust masses (
M_{\mbox{\scriptsize dust}}
).

The
\alpha<
2 results and the time variations of flux densities and spectral indices at
<
14 GHz can be interpreted by time-varying free-free emission, which is moderately common in the Class 0/I stage (e.g.,
Dzib et al. 2013
;
Liu et al. 2014
;
Coutens et al. 2019
).
We do not consider synchrotron emission in our present interpretation since in sources that present bright free-free emission, synchrotron emission has a high chance to be obscured by free electron (
Güdel 2002
).

We quantified our interpretation with a simplified radiative transfer model, which is detailed in the following Section
III.2
.

III.2
SED modeling

III.2.1
Formulation

We follow the approach of
Liu et al. 2019b
;
Liu et al. 2021
to model the invariant dust thermal emission and free-free emission, using the following formulation:

F_{\nu}=\sum\limits_{i}F_{\nu}^{i}e^{-\sum\limits_{j}\tau^{i,j}_{\nu}},

(1)

where
F_{\nu}^{i}
is the flux density of the dust or free-free emission component
i
, and
\tau^{i,j}_{\nu}
is the optical depth of the emission component
j
that obscures the emission component
i
(e.g., the free-free emission around the central protostar may or may not be obscured by the optically thick dust).
The details of how we produced the SEDs of dust and free-free emission are given in Appendix
A
.

In our modelings, we found that the simplest model to fit the observed SEDs (Figure
4
) needs to include two dust emission components that have very different column densities (c.f. Section
III.1
), one free-free emission component for January 27, and two free-free emission components for February 05–21.
We have tried various abstracted geometry by altering the
\tau^{i,j}_{\nu}

III.2.2
Results of modeling

The best-fit parameters of our models are summarized in Table
4
and
5
.
They were obtained using a routine that was based on the the Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) method, which was implemented using the Python-based package
emcee
(
Foreman-Mackey et al. 2013
).
We used 250 MCMC walkers, of which the initial positions were found by interactively searching the parameters that can fit the observational data approximately, which are provided in Tables
4
and
5
.
We then iterated each walker with 50,000 steps, allowing its position to be deviated from the initial position by
\pm 1
order of magnitude.
In these iterations, we employed Gaussian likelihood function and a flat prior function.
In the end, we discarded the initial 10,000 steps as burn-in steps, and determined the best-fit and 1-
\sigma
uncertainty of each parameter based on the median and standard deviation of the MCMC samplers.
The detailed of the fittings and some caveats of the fitting results are elaborated in as follows.

Dust emission components.

The temperatures (

IV
Discussion

In this section, based on the results from our SED modeling in §
III.2
, we discuss the gravitational stability of WL 17’s ring and the potential planetary core mass that could form through pebble accretion within the structure.

IV.1
Gravitational stability

Assuming a gas-to-dust mass ratio of the canonical value of 100
(
Bohlin et al. 1978
)
, the total disk mass (gas
+
dust) of WL 17 is approximately 0.3
M_{\sun}
(Table
4
), while WL 17 itself could have a mass of 0.3
M_{\sun}

(
Han et al. 2023
)
. A large disk-to-star mass ratio exceeding 0.1 can potentially lead to gravitational instability
(
Kratter & Lodato 2016
, e.g.,)
. However, as discussed in §
III.1
, our dust mass estimate likely represents an upper limit. Spatially resolved observations could provide more accurate constraints on the dust mass.

In gravitationally unstable disks, spiral arms are expected to be present in the gas disk
(
Dong et al. 2015a
, e.g.,)
. Additionally, a part of spiral arms can be identified as azimuthal asymmetries in the dust disk
(
Dong et al. 2018
, e.g.,)
.
Indeed, WL 17 might exhibit such azimuthal asymmetries in its dust disk
(
Sheehan & Eisner 2017
;
Gulick et al. 2021
;
Han et al. 2023
;
Shoshi et al. 2024
)
. However,
estimating the Q-value comes with a major uncertainty, namely the gas-to-dust mass ratio. If the ring represents a dust trap, the local gas-to-dust ratio could be significantly lower than 100. To further constrain on the gas mass in the WL 17 system, observations of gas-mass tracers such as HD
(
Bergin et al. 2013
)
and/or analyses of line pressure broadening
(
Yoshida et al. 2022
)
would provide valuable insights.

IV.2
Potential planetary core mass by pebble accretion

Pebble accretion has recently gained attention due to its potential to significantly accelerate the planet core growth
(
Ormel & Klahr 2010
;
Lambrechts & Johansen 2012
)
. Even at large distances from the central star (e.g., at
>50
au), planetesimals can grow into the cores with masses exceeding a range of approximately 5–10
M_{\earth}
within 1 Myr
(
Jang et al. 2022
;
Jiang & Ormel 2023
)
. This mass is referred to as the critical core mass, which is necessary to trigger runaway gas accretion to form gas giant planets
(
Mizuno 1980
, e.g.,)
.

Jiang & Ormel 2023
provided a semi-analytical formula for the typical planet core formed in the ring where pebbles accumulate, described as

V
Conclusion

We observed the WL 17 ring using the JVLA at centimeter wavelengths, ranging from
\lambda=0.6
cm (48 GHz) to 15.0 cm (2 GHz). We successfully detected signals across all wavelengths, except at 15.0 cm. Based on the spectral indices, we attribute the shorter wavelength emission (
\lambda\lesssim 3
cm) to dust rather than free-free emissions. However, the WL 17 ring is not spatially resolved in our observations due to the angular resolution being greater than 0
\farcs
5.

The emission in the frequency range of 2–340 GHz was analyzed using a simple SED model that incorporates two dust components and two free-free emission components. It is important to note that the results of our modeling are influenced by the uncertain properties of the dust. The model includes dust ring components with maximum grain sizes (
a_{\rm max}
) of 4.2 mm and 0.045 mm, as well as free-free emission components with varying electron densities. Our analysis suggests that grain growth in the WL 17 ring has progressed to millimeter-sized grains.

If the gas-to-dust mass ratio is less than approximately 10, pebble accretion within the ring structure might facilitate the formation of a giant planet. However further estimates of the disk mass and the maximum grain size are necessary.

While our SED analysis suggests the grain size of 4.2 mm in the WL 17 ring, the grain size could potentially be larger if the ring exhibits azimuthal asymmetries. Due to the large beam size of more than 0
\farcs
5 in our C-configuration observations, the ring structure is not spatially resolved (Figures
1
–
3
). If azimuthal asymmetries are present at longer wavelengths, thermal emissions from larger dust grains may be required to account for the observed flux density because emitting areas of azimuthal asymmetries are smaller. Future JVLA observations in A-configuration, with a spatial resolution of less than 0
\farcs
1, would provide valuable insights into this possibility.

Appendix A
Emission mechanisms

A.1
Dust emission

The spectral profiles of dust were evaluated based on Equations (10)–(20) presented in
Birnstiel et al. 2018
, which is the analytic solution of the equation of radiative transfer for a geometrically flat isothermal dust slab (c.f.
Miyake & Nakagawa 1993
), which adopted the Eddington-Barbier approximation.
The formulation takes into account dust emission/absorption and self-scattering self-consistently.
Given the assumption of dust opacities (introduced below), the free parameters for each dust component are dust temperature (
T_{\rm dust}
), dust mass surface density (
\Sigma_{\rm dust}
), solid angle (
\Omega_{\rm dust}
), and
a_{\rm max}
.
We assumed that these free parameters do not vary with time (at least, during the observations we compared with).

We adopted the default DSHARP dust opacity model, which assumes a compact dust morphology (
Birnstiel et al. 2018
).
The composition includes water ice
(
Warren 1984
)
, astronomical silicates
(
Draine 2003
)
, troilite, and refractory organics (
Henning & Stognienko 1996
).
As the DSHARP opacity table has been widely applied
(
Hashimoto et al. 2022
;
Hashimoto et al. 2023
;
Liu et al. 2024a
;
Liu et al. 2024b
;
Aso et al. 2024
;
Rivière-Marichalar et al. 2024
;
Guerra-Alvarado et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
, this choice of opacity table allows a straightforward comparison with several previous observational studies.
As the actual dust properties remain largely uncertain, we refer to the Appendix C of
Chung et al. 2024
and Section 5 of
Guidi et al. 2022
for the comprehensive discussion about the effects of assuming different dust compositions and porosity in the SED modeling.

The sizes of dust grains in protoplanetary disks have a broad distribution (
Birnstiel et al. 2018
).
For simplicity, when evaluating the size-averaged opacities, we assumed a power-law grain size distribution function (i.e.,
n(a)\propto a^{-q}
) in between the minimum and maximum grain sizes (
a_{\mbox{\scriptsize min}}
,
a_{\mbox{\scriptsize max}}
), where the values of
q
and
a_{\mbox{\scriptsize min}}
were assumed to be 3.5 and
10^{-4}
mm, respectively.
The size-averaged opacities depend very weakly on
a_{\mbox{\scriptsize min}}
.

A.2
Free-free emission

Free-free emission is the thermal emission of free electrons, which can be described by the electron temperature
T_{e}
and the optical depth
\tau^{\rm ff}
.
The value of
\tau^{\rm ff}
at frequency
\nu
depends on
T_{e}
and the emission measure
EM=\int n_{e}^{2}d\ell
, where
n_{e}
is the electron number volume density.
To evaluate
\tau^{\rm ff}
, we adopted Equation (10) of
Keto 2003
, which is reproduced as follows:

\tau_{\nu}^{\rm ff}=8.235\times 10^{-2}\left(\frac{T_{e}}{\mbox{K}}\right)^{-1.35}\left(\frac{\nu}{\mbox{GHz}}\right)^{-2.1}\left(\frac{\mbox{EM}}{\mbox{pc\,cm${}^{-6}$}}\right),

(A1)

With the formulation we adopted, the spectral indices can range from
-
0.1 (optically thin) to 2.0 (optically thick).
Flux density of free-free emission
F^{\rm ff}_{\rm\nu}
was evaluated as
F^{\rm ff}_{\rm\nu}=T_{e}(1-e^{-\tau_{\nu}^{\rm ff}})

The temperatures of the thermal radio jets have not been very well constrained by observations.
The innermost part of the thermal radio jets may be as hot as
\gtrsim
10
4
K (c.f.,
Reynolds 1986
).
In this work we assumed
T_{e}=
8000 K .
With this assumption and Equation (
A1
), we can see that the turnover frequency where free-free emission is having a transition from the optically thin regime to optically thick regime is determined by
EM
(i.e.,
n_{e}
).
Observing the frequency variations of the spectral indices of free-free emission therefore can constrain the electron density
n_{e}
in the ionized gas source.
We assumed different values of
EM
and
\Omega_{\rm ff}
when fitting the observations taken at different dates to take time variability into consideration.
