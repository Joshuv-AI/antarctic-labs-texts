Multidimensional stigma perception in among adults with obesity: the roles of mental health, social support, and treatment engagement
Authors: Sibel Serçe, Sadık Atıcı, Özlem Ovayolu, Sema Aytaç
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundObesity is a chronic disease that, beyond its physical and psychological repercussions, exposes adult individuals to stigma and negative social attitudes. These experiences may negatively affect self-esteem, mental health, and adherence to treatment regimens. The aim of this study is to evaluate multidimensional stigma perception among of stigma among adults diagnosed with obesity and examine the roles of mental health, social support, and treatment engagement in this context.MethodsThis descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted at a state hospital in the Southeastern Anatolia Region of Türkiye between January and April 2025. The study sample consisted of 139 patients who volunteered to participate in the study and met the inclusion criteria, as determined by a power analysis. Data were collected using a questionnaire and the Chronic Illness Anticipated Stigma Scale (CIASS). Total score of CIASS ranges from 12 to 60, with higher scores indicating greater perceived stigma. Data normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Depending on distribution, Student’s t-test, one-way ANOVA, Mann–Whitney U, and Kruskal-Wallis tests were used for group comparisons. Pearson correlation analysis and multiple linear regression analysis were performed. In the regression model, the CIASS total score was used as the dependent variable, and independent variables were selected based on the literature and univariate analysis results. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Data were analysed using SPSS 23 software.ResultsMost of the participants (74.1%) reported being exposed to negative attitudes, mainly related to physical appearance (55.4%). In addition, 71.2% reported feeling stigmatised, and the majority of them reported receiving no support from family (69.1%) and society (85.6%). The total mean score of CIASS was 34.35 ± 8.02. Those who were exposed to negative attitudes and reported that stigma affected their treatment process had significantly higher CIASS total scores (p < 0.05). Furthermore, the participants who reported having mental health related to obesity had higher CIASS scores (p < 0.05).ConclusionsConsequently, it was determined that a great part of obese adults with obesity experienced stigma and had a lack of social support, resulting in a negative effect on psychological well-being and treatment processes. Interventions aimed at increasing awareness of obesity-related stigma and providing education and support for obese adults are recommended.

Background

Obesity is a rapidly escalating global health condition, affecting public health significantly worldwide [1–3]. The rising prevalence of obesity is a serious source of concern due to the numerous health risks. This not only shortens the life span of individuals but also increases susceptibility to other chronic diseases [2–4]. These problems associated with obesity may lead to individual, social, and socio-economic damages [5].

Independent of physiological needs, increased frequency and amount of eating associated with mental state is usually one of key factors directly affecting obesity [6]. Weight gain may lead to negative conditions including depression, anxiety, stress, low self-esteem, substance abuse, and impaired quality of life [7]. Obesity and its associated physical and psychological problems also result in “dismissive, exclusionary, and discriminatory” behaviours, referred to as stigma within society [8, 9]. Moreover, obese adults are often stereotyped as “unattractive, weak-willed, lazy, selfish, food-loving, unsuccessful, disorganised, unmotivated, worthless, and lacking self-confidence” [10]. This stigma can trigger impaired quality of life, depressive and anxiety symptoms, low self-esteem, and poor body image among these adult individuals [11].

Exposure to stigma may lead to highly negative consequences for health in the short and long term. Contrary to traditional public health beliefs that social pressure motivates obese adults to lose weight, social pressure has been found to raise the risk of obesity in the literature [5]. A systematic review conducted by Pearl and Puhl reported that stigma towards obesity led to poor dietary adherence and a loss of motivation and self-efficacy to follow healthy lifestyle behaviours [12]. Moreover, healthcare professionals hold strong explicit and/or implicit prejudices towards obese adults. Healthcare professionals’ stigma towards obesity is generally characterised by some stereotypes such as indolence, indiscipline, and a lack of willpower, which may negatively affect their behaviours and decision-making processes and the adherence of patients to treatment [13]. Moreover, the majority of studies that examine the level of stigma associated with obesity have been conducted in Western societies [14, 15], and the data on how stigma is experienced in different sociocultural societies appear to be quite limited [15]. It is noteworthy that the stigma associated with obesity has been studied only a limited number of times, particularly in societies, where cultural norms, family structure, and social values play a decisive role in shaping adult individuals perceptions such as Türkiye [16].

The stigma towards obesity can be addressed within the framework of theoretical models that explain the multidimensional nature of stigma. In this sense, the Health Stigma and Discrimination Framework describes stigma as a multi-layered process that manifests itself at the individual, interpersonal, and social levels [17].

Furthermore, as a multidimensional concept, stigma is not limited to individual perceptions but also manifests in different social domains, such as work, family life, and the healthcare system [18]. Indeed, the presence of prejudice and stigmatising attitudes towards obesity has been demonstrated even among healthcare professionals [19]. Therefore, this study aims to examine perceived stigma in adults diagnosed with obesity in a clinical sample in a multidimensional manner, considering its determinants and repercussions across different social domains such as family, occupational and healthcare settings, thereby contributing to the generalisability of existing stigma theories in a non-Western society.

Methods

Aim of the study

This study was conducted to evaluate the stigma level of adults diagnosed with obesity.

Design, setting, and time of the study

This descriptive study was conducted with patients diagnosed with obesity in a state hospital in South-eastern Anatolia Region between January-April 2025.

Population and sample

The population of the study consisted of 200 patients. The sample size was calculated to be minimum 132 participants using the formula of finite population, assuming a confidence interval of 95% and a margin of error of 5%. The default effect size and prevalence were taken into account when calculating the sample size. Additionally, a power analysis was done to obtain sufficient study power [20]. Inclusion criteria were determined as being diagnosed with obesity according to the BMI classification by WHO, having no other conditions causing stigma, having no psychiatric disorders, and being aged 18 years or older. Sequential sampling method was used during the inclusion of participants in the study. Given the potential for data loss, the study was completed with 139 participants who agreed to participate in the study.

Ethical considerations

Permission was obtained from the Gaziantep University Clinical Research Ethics Committee (Decision No: 2024/473) and the institution where the research was conducted. Before the start of the study, patients who volunteered to participate and met the inclusion criteria were informed about the study, and informed consent was obtained from all participants. They were also informed that participation in the study was voluntary and that they could withdraw from the study at any time. All data obtained during and after the research process were kept confidential and used only for scientific purposes, and the principles of the Helsinki Declaration were strictly adhered to throughout the study.

Data collection tools

The data were collected using a questionnaire, prepared by the researchers based on the literature, and the Chronic Illness Anticipated Stigma Scale (CIASS).

Questionnaire

The form, prepared by the researchers, included questions about socio-demographic characteristics (age, gender, marital status, educational level, employment status) and disease-related characteristics (duration of obesity, presence of chronic disease, perception of stigma, exposure to negative attitudes, and the effect of obesity on self-confidence). Variables such as the perceived effect of obesity on self-confidence and psychological well-being were assessed using self-reported questions (e.g., “Does obesity affect your self-confidence?” with response options: Yes/No/Undecided) [21].

Chronic illness anticipated stigma scale (CIASS)

This scale was developed by Earnshaw et al., (2012) [22] and Tünerir (2019) conducted its Turkish validity and reliability study and found that it has good psychometric properties [21]. It has 12 items under three subscales; the first four items assess the anticipated stigma from family and friends, the next four items assess the anticipated stigma from work colleagues, and the last four items assess the anticipated stigma from healthcare workers. Each item is rated between 1 and 5 points using a five-point Likert-type scale. The mean scores of the subscales are calculated separately, resulting in three different stigma values. Total score of the scale is measured by calculating the mean scores of all items. Total score of the subscales varies between “4–20” points while total score of the CIASS varies between “12–60” points. Higher scores indicate that the adult individuals has a higher level of anticipated stigma. The Cronbach’s alpha value of the scale is 0.95. In the study, its Cronbach’s alpha value was found to be 0.809. This scale, used in the study, was selected to assess individuals’ perceived experiences of stigma within the contexts of family, work, and healthcare systems It has been reported in the literature that many of mental and social problems faced by adults with obesity stem from prejudice and discrimination [23]. In this sense, this study aimed to assess perception of stigma among adults with obesity.

Previous studies have confirmed that the scale is psychometrically valid, which is why it was preferred as an appropriate tool for this research population.

Data analysis

The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was run to assess whether or not the data were normally distributed. For normally distributed continuous variables, the Student’s t-test was used to compare two groups and one-way ANOVA was used to compare more than two groups. For non-normally distributed variables, the Mann–Whitney U test and the Kruskal–Wallis test were applied, respectively. No post-hoc analyses were performed for pairwise comparisons following the ANOVA and Kruskal–Wallis tests. The correlations between variables were examined using Pearson correlation analysis, and Multiple Linear Regression analysis was also performed. In the multiple linear regression analysis, the CIASS total score was used as the dependent variable, and the independent variables were included in the model according to the literature and the results of univariate analysis. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 23.0 software, and the significance level of p < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant.

Results

Findings concerning socio-demographic characteristics of the patients and their CIASS total and subscale mean scores

The Body Mass Index (BMI) of the participants was determined as 34.56 ± 3.54. 58.3% of the participants were male, 89.2% were married, and 25.9% were aged between 28 and 37 years. Furthermore, it was found that 48.2% had an income equal to their expenditure, 36% graduated from primary school, and 54% were employed. A great majority of the participants (78.4%) resided in districts, 64% were non-smokers, and 92.8% were non-drinkers (Table 1).

Findings concerning obesity-related characteristics of the patients and their CIASS total and subscale mean scores

Table 2 presents the obesity-related characteristics of the patients. Accordingly, it was found that 37.4% of the participants were diagnosed 7–12 months earlier, 84.2% were unaware of their obesity grade, and 54.7% had no comorbid chronic disease. Furthermore, 45.3% of the participants stated that their obesity was triggered by another disease, while 57.6% reported that they suffered from musculoskeletal problems related to obesity. 51.1% of the participants suffered from mental disorders related to obesity, the vast majority lacked psychological support (95%), did not attend regular medical check-ups (77.7%), and did not follow a diet (84.9%). Moreover, 58.3% of the participants had other obese adults in their family.

When the CIASS scores were compared in terms of variables, a statistically significant difference was found in the scores of the family and friends subscale based on the duration of diagnosis (F = 2.607; p = 0.039), Post-hoc analysis (Tukey HSD) revealed that individuals diagnosed 25 months or more ago scored higher than those diagnosed 1–6 months or 7–12 months ago (p > 0.05). This finding indicates that as the duration of diagnosis increases, individuals’ sensitivity to stigmatizing attitudes from their immediate environment also increases. A statistically significant difference was found in the work colleagues subscale depending on whether obesity was triggered by another disease (Z = 2.597; p = 0.010), and also the participants with obese family members had higher scores on the work colleagues subscale (t = 2.656; p = 0.009).

A significant difference was found between the scores of the family and friends subscale in the comparison of physical problems associated with obesity (F = 3.190; p = 0.015). However, the Tukey HSD post-hoc analysis revealed no statistically significant difference in pairwise comparisons between groups (p > 0.05). This finding indicates that patients have similar levels of stigma perception, regardless of the type of physical complication experienced. Adult individuals who suffered from mental disorders related to obesity had significantly higher total scores on the CIASS, as well as higher scores on the family and friends subscale and work colleagues subscale, whereas no significant difference was found in the “healthcare workers” subscale (p > 0.05).

No statistically significant difference was found between CIASS total and subscale scores in terms of the other variables (p > 0.05) (Table 2).

Findings concerning the obesity-related conditions and the CIASS mean scores of the participants

When examining the participants’ social experiences related to obesity, it was found that 74.1% were exposed to negative attitudes, and the most common cause thereof was criticism about their physical appearance (55.4%). A great majority of the participants stated that they lacked support from their families (69%) and the community (85.6%), whilst 71.2% stated that they felt stigmatised.

According to the results of the statistical analysis, adults exposed to negative societal attitudes had significantly higher CIASS total scores in the sub-dimensions of family and friends and healthcare workers (p < 0.05), while no significant difference was found in the sub-dimension of colleagues (p > 0.05). In comparisons based on the reasons for exposure to negative attitudes, no statistically significant difference was found in CIASS total and sub-dimension scores (p > 0.05).

The scores on the “Healthcare Workers” subscale were examined using the Tukey HSD post-hoc test based on the level of adequate support received from the community, and no significant difference was observed in pairwise comparisons between the groups (p > 0.05). This finding indicates that the perception of community support does not have a decisive effect on the expectation of stigma in the healthcare setting. Furthermore, the ANOVA results also confirmed that the scores on the “Healthcare Workers” subscale did not show a significant difference between the groups (p > 0.05).

Analysis based on the level of societal stigma revealed that adults with this condition had significantly higher CIASS total scores in the “Family and Friends” and “Healthcare Workers” sub-dimensions (p < 0.05), but no significant difference was found in the “Coworkers” sub-dimension (p > 0.05). The Tukey HSD post-hoc test showed that individuals who felt stigmatized had higher CIASS total scores than those who did not; the difference approached the limit of statistical significance (Mean Diff = 3.51; p = 0.068).

In the assessment of the impact of stigma on the treatment process, a significant difference was found in the “Healthcare Workers” sub-dimension and the total CIASS score (p < 0.05), while no significant difference was found in other sub-dimensions (p > 0.05). Tukey HSD post-hoc analysis showed that adults who stated that stigma affected their treatment process had statistically significantly higher total CIASS scores (p = 0.006) and “Healthcare Workers” sub-dimension scores (p = 0.030) compared to those who did not experience this effect. This finding suggests that stigma may affect individuals’ adherence to treatment and their use of healthcare services.

Perceptions regarding the effect of obesity on self-confidence and CIASS scores were analyzed using the Tukey HSD post-hoc test. The results showed that in the “Healthcare Workers” subscale, the stigma scores of the group who stated “Undecided” regarding loss of self-confidence were significantly higher than those who answered “Yes” (p = 0.032) and “No” (p = 0.019). No significant difference was found between the groups in the total scale score and the “Family and Friends” subscale (p > 0.05). Finally, in the comparison of the effect of obesity on self-confidence, it was determined that the sub-dimensions “Family and Friends” and “Healthcare Professionals” had significantly higher CIASS total scores (p < 0.05), but there was no significant difference in the “Coworkers” sub-dimension (p > 0.05) (Table 3).

Multiple linear regression analysis for factors associated with stigma perception

According to the results of the multiple linear regression analysis performed to predict the dependent variable, exposure to negative attitudes (β=-0.255, p = 0.004) and the effect of stigma on treatment (β=-0.182, p = 0.028) were determined to be significant predictors of the dependent variable among the variables included in the model.

It was found that adult individuals exposed to negative attitudes had significantly different scores on the dependent variable, and similarly, individuals who stated that stigma affected the treatment process also showed significant changes in their dependent variable scores.

In contrast, gender (β=-0.020, p = 0.819), BMI (β = 0.001, p = 0.993), and duration of diagnosis (β = 0.040, p = 0.641) did not have a significant effect on the dependent variable. The presence of mental illness also did not have a statistically significant relationship with the dependent variable (β=-0.148, p = 0.085).

Furthermore, the variance increase factor (VIF) values ​​ranging from 1.061 to 1.222 indicate that there is no multicollinearity problem in the model (Table 5).

Discussion

Obesity is a multifaceted condition that causes not only physical problems and psychological well-being and social problems. It has been reported in the literature that many of mental and social problems faced by adults with obesity stem from prejudice and discrimination [23]. In this sense, this study aimed to assess perception of stigma among adults with obesity.

This study revealed that obese adults were exposed to stigma in various domains, such as family, social circles, the workplace, and healthcare, and the levels of stigma were correlated with various socio-demographic and psychosocial factors. These findings indicated that stigma associated with obesity had a multidimensional structure [9, 24].

It has been reported that obese adults in society are frequently stereotyped as “lazy”, “unattractive” or “worthless” [25]. Similar findings have been reported in various studies [26, 27], indicating that stigma is reinforced not only at the social level but also by an individual’s immediate social circle, particularly family and friends [28, 29]. Likewise, this study revealed that obese adults were exposed to stigma by their family members and friends, and therefore their mental states were negatively affected. Similarly, this study revealed that obese adults were exposed to stigma by their family members and friends and that this consequently had a negative impact on their mental health. Interestingly, family support is expected to be a protective factor, but in some cases, it can actually be a source of stigma, highlighting a paradox in interpersonal relationships. Consequently, stigma resulting from interpersonal interactions can negatively affect an individual’s emotional well-being and lead to the development of internalised negative beliefs. This situation can be explained within the context of Earnshaw and Chaudoir’s Framework of Health Stigma and Discrimination, which posits that stigma is a multidimensional process at individual, interpersonal, and societal levels. The increased sensitivity of adults to stigmatizing attitudes from family and friends, particularly as the diagnosis time lengthens, can be interpreted within this framework [30].

Socio-demographic characteristics play a decisive role in exposure to stigma. This study revealed that those who were married and female were more likely to be exposed to stigma. Similarly, the literature has reported that women and young adults with high BMIs are more likely to experience higher levels of stigma [29]. One study reported that women were exposed to more stigma for their weight than men and single individuals were more likely to be exposed to stigma than married individuals [31]. However, some differences between studies indicate that stigma is influenced not only by individual characteristics but also broader contexts, such as cultural norms, media influences, and social expectations [32]. Therefore, stigma associated with obesity should be addressed through a multidimensional assessment.

One of the key findings of the study was that stigma had psychological consequences. Stigma associated with obesity can lead mental health, although it is also considered that this correlation may be bidirectional. On one hand, stigma can undermine self-confidence, lead to social withdrawal, and increase internalised negative beliefs. On the other hand, psychologically vulnerable individuals may be more susceptible to social judgements and perceive stigma more intensely [33–35]. In their study, Clark et al., reported that anxiety and depression were both a cause and a consequence of obesity [36]. Recent studies have also shown that stigma has significant negative effects on individuals’ quality of life and psychological well-being [37]. This study also revealed that adults suffered mental health due to obesity, yet did not seek professional support for such mental health, did not attend medical check-ups related to obesity, and were reluctant to follow a diet. The major barrier preventing obese adults from seeking help is widely regarded as the stigma and negative judgements they are frequently exposed to in society [38]. This interaction highlights the complexity of the correlation between stigma and mental health and emphasises the importance of coping mechanisms and individual resilience.

The visibility of obesity can make adults more vulnerable to social judgement and may increase their stigma experience. In particular, stigma that adults were exposed to in the professional and social life can negatively affect their self-esteem and social participation. This study revealed that adults who reported exposure to negative attitudes in society and stated that they felt stigmatised had significantly higher scores on the stigma scale. Furthermore, the higher level of stigma observed among adults who reported that obesity affected their self-confidence suggests that stigma has negative impacts on individuals’ psychosocial processes. Likewise, the literature indicates that obese adults may be exposed to discrimination in areas such as recruitment, promotion, and pay rises [39, 40]. These findings demonstrate that stigma is not limited to individual perceptions but can cause cumulative impacts across different spheres of life.

The effect of stigma on treatment processes is also significant. This study observed that adults who believed that stigma affected their treatment had higher scores on the stigma scale. This suggests that stigma may hinder treatment adherence and the use of healthcare services [38]. The literature also indicates that stigma poses a significant barrier to accessing healthcare services and may lead individuals to refrain from seeking them [41]. A study conducted by Uyanık et al., with students in the department of health sciences reported that students tended to be prejudiced towards obese adults [42]. In their study, Işık et al., also found the majority of nurses tended to be prejudiced, very few of them were trained on obese patient care, and training lessened their tendencies to be prejudiced against obese patients [43]. Therefore, addressing the issue of stigma not only improves psychological well-being but also contributes significantly to better treatment outcomes in obesity management.

The findings of this study should be evaluated within the framework of theoretical approaches that explain stigma. In their sense, the findings indicate that obese adults may be exposed to different dimensions of these processes. However, no definitive conclusions can be drawn regarding causal relationships between the variables due to the cross-sectional design of the study.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that stigma is prevalent among obese adults in this sample in Türkiye and is more strongly associated with perceived mental health and negative social attitudes than with objective clinical characteristics. The findings highlight the need to increase social awareness, organize training programs for healthcare professionals, and integrate patient-centered approaches into clinical practice.

Study limitations

Some limitations should be taken into consideration while interpreting the findings of this study. First, the fact that the study was conducted at a single public hospital located in a specific region of Türkiye limits the generalisability of the results. Therefore, they may not represent obese adults with different sociocultural characteristics in different regions across Türkiye or at different healthcare institutions. This requires caution when generalising the results to broader populations. Another limitation is that, due to stigma, perceived mental health has been measured through self-report.
