Gut microbiome modulates breast cancer risk in socially isolated mice
Authors: Fabia de Oliveira Andrade, Christopher Staley, Lu Jin, Melike Ozgul-Onal, Maddie McDermott, Sercan Kenanoglu, Karla Andrade de Oliveira, Vivek Verma, Leena Hilakivi-Clarke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBreast cancer risk and mortality are associated with disrupted gut microbiome functions which in turn can affect tumor immune responses. One source of disruption could be stress. Social isolation (SI) stress consistently increases breast cancer risk and mortality in preclinical models and women, but whether SI promotes mammary tumor growth by affecting gut microbiome has not been studied.MethodsWe investigated if increased E0771 mammary tumorigenesis in SI female C57BL/6 mice was associated with changes in their gut microbiome by treating mice with an antibiotic mix that suppresses bacterial abundance and by performing fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) from SI or group-housed (GH) donors to GH host. The effect of SI on anti-tumor CD8 + T and immunosuppressive Foxp3 + Treg cells was also studied.ResultsFecal bacteria that were present at different abundances between GH and SI mice were short chain fatty acid (SCFA) producers, and the most consistent change across three replicate studies was decreased fecal abundance of Akkermansia genus in SI mice. In addition, fecal propionic acid levels were reduced in SI mice, compared with GH mice, in agreement with Akkermansia being propionic acid producer. SI reduced the activation of CD8 + T cells systemically and in the tumor microenvironment, while the levels and activation of immunosuppressive Foxp3 Tregs were increased. Antibiotic treatment reversed increased mammary tumorigenesis and immunosuppression in SI mice but did not affect GH mice. Further, FMT from SI donors increased tumor growth in GH host, compared with FMT from GH donor.ConclusionGut dysbiosis caused by SI may be driving their increased mammary tumorigenesis, potentially through gut dysbiosis induced immunosuppression.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13058-026-02292-x.Keywords: Social isolation, Breast cancer, Gut dysbiosis, Immunosuppression

Background

Social isolation is a stressor that increases the risk of breast cancer and breast cancer mortality [1–3]. Social isolation can be defined as a lack of meaningful social interactions and be caused, for example, by being elderly, being treated unjustly or being diagnosed with a severe disease, such as cancer. Recent data indicate that young adults, between ages of 18 and 29, are the loneliest generation [4]. Co-incidentally, breast cancer incidence is increasing most rapidly among women under 50 [5]. Social isolation is reported to affect 16–41% of breast cancer survivors when assessed within 6–12 months of the diagnosis [6]. According to a survey by American Cancer Society among over 1,000 cancer patients and survivors in 2023, over 50% reported experiencing greater social isolation as a consequence of cancer diagnosis [7]. Socially isolated breast cancer survivors are 43% more likely to recur and 64% more likely to die of breast cancer, compared with socially integrated survivors [6].

The pathways mediating the effects of social isolation on increased disease risk and mortality are not known. Social isolation increases circulating and tissue inflammatory markers [8, 9]. Gut microbiome is the largest immune organ and is also emerging as an essential regulator of host’s health, disease susceptibility and progression, and host’s response to stress [10, 11]. Gut dysbiosis can be defined as a disruption in the mutually beneficial relationship between the host and microbiome. Consequently, the altered gut microbial composition cannot maintain host health. Gut dysbiosis is also defined as a reduced abundance of short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) producing bacteria and fecal SCFA (acetic, butyric and propionic acids) levels [12].

SCFAs have multiple health promoting functions, including their ability to boost cellular and mitochondrial metabolism [13] and to regulate immune cells [14] in a manner that improves anti-tumor immune responses [15, 16]. We report here that social isolation altered the abundance of SCFA producing bacteria, reduced fecal propionic acid levels, and as previously reported [17], induced immunosuppression. Antibiotic treatment reversed the increased mammary tumor burden and immune cell changes in socially isolated mice, and performing fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) from socially isolated mice increased mammary tumorigenesis in group-housed hosts.

Methods

Social isolation and mammary tumorigenesis

Female C57BL/6 mice were purchased from Taconic Biosciences (Rensselaer, NY, USA) and maintained on a 12-h light-dark cycle at 22 °C with free access to laboratory diet (Teklad Global 18% Rodent Diet, 2918, inotiv) and water. At 8 weeks of age, mice were inoculated with 5 × 105 E0771 cells into the 4th mammary gland. After inoculation, mice were divided into group-housed (GH) and socially isolated (SI) mice to mimic cancer-initiated SI. In mice, single housing is a validated model of social isolation [18]. E0771 tumor size was determined by measuring tumor length and width 2 times per week by caliper and the tumor volume was calculated as 0.5 × (length × width2). Mice were monitored for up to 20 days after tumor inoculation, but they were euthanized earlier if the tumor size reached 2,000 mm3.

All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at the University of Minnesota. Mice were euthanized by CO2 inhalation or under isoflurane anesthesia (Piramal Critical Care Inc; NDC Code: 66794-013-25) by cardiac puncture, followed by cervical dislocation.

Antibiotic treatment to ablate gut microbiome

One week before tumor inoculation, 7-week old GH C57BL/6 mice were gavaged with 200 µl water or antibiotic (AB) cocktail composed of 5 mg/ml vancomycin (Cayman), 10 mg/ml Neomycin (Millipore), 10 mg/ml metronidazole (Cayman) and 0.2 mg amphotericin B (anti-fungal; Cayman) for three times weekly to reduce the abundance of gut microbes, as described previously [19]. In addition, their drinking water was supplemented with 1 mg/ml ampicillin (Thermo Scientific) that was changed 2 times per week. E0771 tumor cells (5 × 105) were grafted into the mammary gland. After tumor inoculation, mice receiving AB or water were either isolated or kept group housed. AB treatment continued throughout tumor monitoring period, 3 times per week.

Fecal microbiota transplant (FMT)

Feces collected from mice kept GH or SI for at least 4 weeks were used to prepare the FMT. For the collection, mice were individually transferred to a holding container for a brief time until they generated few fecal pellets. Feces were homogenized with 6× volume of PBS containing 0.05% L-cysteine and centrifuged at 350 g for 5 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was filtered in a 0.25 mm filter and centrifuged at 4500 g for 15 min at 4 °C. The pellet was resuspended in PBS containing 10% glycerol + 0.05% L-cysteine at 1/3 dilution and storage at -80 °C until use. At the time of transplantation, feces were thawed at 37 °C and further kept in ice.

GH C57BL/6 female mice at 7 weeks of age were gavaged with 200 µL of AB cocktail composed of 1 mg/ml of ampicillin, gentamicin (RPI Research products), metronidazole, neomycin and 0.5 mg/ml of vancomycin for three consecutive days [20]. One day after the three-day AB exposure, GH host mice received either 100 µl FMT from GH mice (GH-FMT) or FMT from SI mice (SI-FMT) for three consecutive days. Mice were then allografted with 5 × 105 E0771 cells into the 4th mammary fat pad. After tumor allografting, mice continued to receive FMT two times per week until the end of tumor monitoring period.

Dietary supplementation of partially hydrolyzed guar gum (PHGG) in SI mice to increase SCFA production

Since oral supplementation with SCFAs does not increase fecal SCFAs, and they are rapidly eliminated from circulation, we instead supplemented SI mice with partially hydrolyzed guar gum (PHGG) dietary fiber. We have used this fiber in our earlier studies, since it increases fecal SCFA levels (see Results). Six-week-old C57BL/6 mice were maintained on a modified low-fiber AIN-93G diet (formulated by replacing the standard 5% cellulose with cornstarch; inotiv) beginning two weeks prior to tumor inoculation. Mice were provided ad libitum access to drinking water supplemented with either 0.5% (w/v) PHGG (Sunfiber, Tomorrow’s Nutrition) or maltodextrin (MDX; Millipore Sigma) as a control. Supplemented water was refreshed twice weekly and maintained throughout the duration of the study.

To evaluate the effect of dietary fiber supplementation on mammary tumor growth, mice were grafted with 5 × 105 E0771 cells. Studies done in GH mice were not performed at the same time as studies in SI mice, and thus results are reported separately.

Gut microbiome analysis in GH and SI mice

Four-week-old female C57BL/6 mice were randomized as GH or SI mice and kept in these housing conditions for 4 weeks. At the end of week 4 of GH or SI, feces were collected and snapped frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C. This procedure was performed across three independent studies.

Samples from study 1 were processed and analyzed commercially using shotgun sequencing by ONE CODEX (Wilmington, DE). Samples from studies 2 and 3 were extracted using the DNeasy PowerSoil Pro kit on the automated QIAcube platform using the inhibitor removal technology (IRT) protocol (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany). The V4 hypervariable region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified and sequenced using the 515 F/806R primer set [21] by the University of Minnesota Genomics Center (UMGC; Minneapolis, MN). Samples were paired-end sequenced at a read length of 301 nucleotides (nt) on the Illumina MiSeq platform by UMGC. Raw sequence data are deposited in the Sequence Read Archive at NCBI under BioProject accession numbers SRP346337 (study 2) and SRP404400 (study 3).

Sequence data were processed using mothur ver. 1.41.1 [22] using a modified version of our previously published pipeline [23]. Briefly, samples were trimmed to 170 nt and paired-end joined, following by quality trimming, and alignment against the SILVA database (ver. 138.1) [24]. Chimeras were identified and removed using UCHIME ver. 4.2.40 [25]. Amplicon sequence variants were clustered at 99% similarity using the OptiClust algorithm [26]. Taxonomic classifications were made against the Ribosomal Database Project (ver. 18) [27]. Alpha diversity was calculated via Shannon index using mothur, and beta diversity was evaluated using Bray-Curtis dissimilarities.

Differences in community composition were evaluated using analysis of similarity [28] and visualized by ordination using principal coordinates analysis. To determine donor engraftment in the FMT experiments, we employed the Bayesian algorithm SourceTracker [29]. This software models the distribution of taxa in user-defined sources and predicts the percentage of a sink community attributable to the different source categories provided. To determine which genera were contributing to engraftment, we evaluated the donor contributions from the full results.

DESeq2 R package was used for normalization and Melonnpan 34 was used to predict metabolites relative abundance using the microbiome relative abundance in 16S RNAseq, statistical analysis between GH and SI on the predicted metabolites was produced by limma R package, differentially expressed cutoff was set to fdr < 0.1.

Assessment of fecal SCFA levels

For SCFA analysis, fecal samples were collected and snap-frozen from GH and SI mice. To assess the impact of PHGG on SCFAs, fecal samples were collected from separate groups of tumor-free GH mice fed low-fiber AIN93G diet and kept on MDX or PHGG for 4 weeks. All SCFA analytical standards were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (MO, USA). Optima grade ethyl acetate was purchased from Fisher Scientific (NJ, USA) and used to isolate SCFAs from the aqueous fecal homogenate. Fecal SCFA analysis was performed using a previously described method [30] with modifications. 2-ethylbutryic acid served as the global internal standard for the analysis.

Analytical assessment was performed using a gas chromatography-coupled mass spectrometry (GC-MS) platform. Samples were analyzed using a 1 µL sample injection volume with 30:1 split injection ratio at a constant inlet temperature of 250 °C. Compound separation was performed using an Agilent 7890B GC oven equipped with a DB-FAT WAX ultra inert column (30 m × 0.25 mm i.d., 0.25 μm film, Agilent) maintained with a constant flow rate of 1 mL/min helium gas. The MS detector for the method was a high-resolution Agilent 7200 QTOF instrument operated in electron impact ionization mode at 70 eV electron energy that scanned from 35 to 350 m/z. The platform ion source, quadrupole, and interface were maintained at 230 °C, 150 °C, and 225 °C, respectively. Quantitation of SCFAs was performed using a 7-point calibration curve with appropriate solvent blanks, negative controls, and quality controls samples run throughout the assessment.

Quantitation of SCFAs from the raw GC-MS data was performed using the open-source software Skyline [31]. Calibration curves were fitted independently for each compound by linear in log space regression using the peak ratio of each compound to the global internal standard. No regression weighting was used. All calibration curves were fit with an R2 of at least 0.995 precision. All estimated sample concentrations were normalized by starting lyophilized material weight.

Immunophenotype by flow cytometry

In vitro Naïve CD4 + T Cell Activation: Splenic naïve CD4 + cells were isolated from GH and SI mice (n = 4 per group) using an EasySep™ Mouse Naïve CD4 + T Cell Isolation Kit (STEMCELL). Naïve CD4 + cells (1 × 105 cells/well) from GH and SI mice were incubated with IL-2 (100 UI/ml) and IL-2 + activation cocktail (anti-CD3:1 µg/ml + anti-CD28:0.5 µg/ml) for 72 h. Cells were harvested and analyzed by flow cytometry.

Tissue Processing

Spleens and tumors were processed to prepare a single cell suspension by smashing the tissue against the sterile nylon cell strainers (40 μm) centrifuged and resuspended in T cell media (RPMI media supplemented with 10% FBS, 0.1% β-mercaptoethanol, penicillin (100 U/ml) and streptomycin (100 mg/ml). Approximately 2 × 106 cells from the total spleen and tumor cell homogenate were activated with 50ng/ml of Phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (Sigma-Aldrich), 750 ng/ml of Ionomycin (Sigma-Aldrich) and 10 µg/ml of brefeldin (Biolegend) for 3 h at 37 °C.

All samples were stained with LIVE/ DEAD Fixable Near IR Dead cell kit (Invitrogen) and fixed with fixation buffer containing 4.2% Formaldehyde (BD Biosciences). Panel containing anti-Foxp3 antibody were permeabilized with Foxp3 Permeabilization Kit (BD Biosciences). Other samples were permeabilized with Cytofix/Cytoperm kit (BD Biosciences). The phenotypic distribution of infiltrated cells was determined by staining using appropriately conjugated antibodies against CD3 (clone: 145-2C11), CD8 (clone: 53 − 6.7), CD4 (clone: RM4-5), Foxp3 (clone: MF23), Tbet (clone: O4-46), GATA3 (clone: TWAJ), IFNγ (clone: XMG1.2 ) and IL10 (clone: JES5-16E3) antibodies. Data were acquired using LSRFortessa Flow Cytometer and analyzed by Flowjo 10.9–10.

Statistical analysis

Differences in tumor growth were analyzed by repeated two-way ANOVA. Alpha diversity and similarity to donor were analyzed by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s test. Shapiro-Wilk and D’Agostino & Pearson normality tests were applied, and all other data were analyzed by T-test or Mann-Whitney accordingly to normality test, one-way or two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test when appropriate. GraphPad Prism and R were used to perform statistical analysis. Results with p values ≤ 0.05 were considered statistically significant for all the analyses.

Results

Social isolation (SI) increased mammary tumorigenesis

We used syngeneic female C57BL/6 mice which were grafted with E0771 triple negative breast cancer (TNBC) cells to study the effects of SI on mammary tumorigenesis. Mice were housed singly from the day onwards when they were grafted E0771 mammary tumor cells to mimic SI in women caused by a diagnosis of breast cancer [7] (Fig. 1A). Consistent with the findings obtained in an earlier study [32], starting SI at this point significantly enhanced tumor growth compared to group-housed (GH) mice (p = 0.05; Fig. 1B). This trend was further reflected in the final tumor volume, which was significantly higher in the SI group (p = 0.03; Fig. 1C).

Antibiotic (AB) treatment reduced mammary tumorigenesis in SI mice

AB treatment was administered by oral gavage three times per week, and mice also received continuously ampicillin via drinking water. AB treatment started 7 days before E0771 mammary tumor cells were grafted and continued through the mammary tumor monitoring period (Fig. 1D). Consistent with our initial findings, control (water-treated) SI mice exhibited significantly higher tumor growth (p = 0.005; Fig. 1E) and terminal tumor size (p = 0.01; Fig. 1F) compared with water-treated GH controls. AB treatment significantly attenuated tumor growth (p = 0.025; Fig. 1E) and final tumor size (p = 0.046; Fig. 1F) in the SI group, whereas no such effect was observed in GH mice.

Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) from SI mice increased mammary tumorigenesis in GH mice

In a separate experiment, GH mice were treated with AB for three consecutive days and then they received FMT either from GH or SI donor mice (Fig. 1G). FMTs were administered by oral gavage for 3 consecutive days, after which mice were grafted E0771 mammary tumor cells. FMTs were re-administered twice per week through the mammary tumor monitoring period. FMT from SI donor significantly increased mammary tumor growth (Fig. 1H; p = 0.009) and final tumor size (Fig. 1I; p = 0.048) in the host GH mice, compared with FMT from GH donor.

Social isolation altered the gut microbiome

In three separate experiments, stool samples were collected from C57BL/6 mice that had been housed singly in a cage for 4 weeks or remained group-housed (Fig. 2A). None of these mice had mammary tumors. Fecal bacterial alpha diversity (Shannon index) was not altered between SI and GH mice in two of the three studies and increased in SI mice in study 3 (Additional file 1 A). Beta diversity was significantly different between SI and GH mice in all three experiments (p = 0.022, p = 0.0023, p = 0.0001) (Fig. 2B).

The abundances of several bacterial genera were significantly different between GH and SI mice. However, as shown in Additional file 1B-D and Additional file 2 , the differences were vastly dissimilar across the three replicates. In study 1 done using shotgun sequencing, the abundances of several genera in SI mice were elevated. In study 2 done using16S rRNA sequencing, a notably lower number of significant differences between GH and SI mice was seen. Study 3 with 16S rRNA sequencing again revealed multiple significant differences, and they included both increased and reduced abundance of various bacterial genera. Only one genus was similarly altered in all three comparisons: Akkermansia was significantly reduced in studies 1–3 (Fig. 2C). In two of three replicates, the abundances of the same six genera were elevated and 10 were suppressed in SI mice, compared with GH mice (Table 1A). Common to all elevated and suppressed genera is that they or their families are producers of SCFAs. In addition, recurring bacterial changes were suggestive of inflammation, increased cancer risk, elevated total and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-c) and reduced high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-c) levels (Table 1A).

Changes in the metabolites produced by gut bacteria might be more informative than specific microbial differences in individuals who are at high risk of developing cancer or are at an increased risk of cancer mortality. Since reduced fecal SCFA levels are indicative of gut dysbiosis, we determined them using GC-MS platform. Results indicated that the levels of propionic acid were significantly reduced in SI mice, but no changes in acetic or butyric acid were seen (Fig. 2D). Possible differences in other fecal metabolites were determined using MelonnPan [33] algorithm. Only one metabolite was predicted to be similarly changed in all three experiments: a significant reduction in citrulline in SI mice (Table 1B). Citrulline is a non-dietary amino acid produced mainly in the enterocytes in the gut, but also produced by gut bacteria, such as Corynebacterium glutamicum [34]. Abundances of Corynebacterium were significantly reduced in two of the three replicates (Table 1A). Six other metabolites were predicted to be different in two of the three studies: three were suppressed and the other two were increased (Table 1B).

Effect of AB and FMT on the gut microbiome

AB treatment significantly reduced gut microbial alpha-diversity (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 2E) and suppressed the abundance of gut bacteria that exhibited the highest abundance in non-AB treated mice (Additional file 3). Gut microbiome of AB treated mice contained mostly non-virulent and non-pathogenic lactic acid bacteria Ligilactobacillus (Additional file 3). Alpha-diversity was significantly increased after the first three FMTs and further increased at the end of the tumor monitoring period after several FMTs, reaching pre-AB levels in both GH- and SI-FMT groups (Fig. 2E). Similarity to donor gut microbial composition, as determined using SourceTracker [29], was only 25–30% immediately after first three FMTs but reached 50% at the end of tumor monitoring period in GH-FMT group (Fig. 22F). The similarity index remained below 30% in the SI-FMT group. It is important to note that although FMT is successfully employed to treat many gut inflammatory and other diseases, such as type 2 diabetes, and improves response to cancer immunotherapies, its clinical success is not determined by engraftment rate [35]. One factor contributing to the relatively low similarity index in both groups might have been the presence of mammary tumors in mice that received FMT. It is known that gut microbes influence tumor growth, but tumor also impacts the gut microbiome: individuals with cancer have significant differences in their gut microbiome, compared with matched healthy individuals.

When we interrogated which taxa were engrafting from the donor FMT to host (Fig. 2G), the top 10 taxa were SCFA producers, both from GH- and SI-FMT donors. At the read level, the abundances of these taxa were not statistically different between GH hosts receiving FMT from GH mice and from SI mice (Fig. 2H).

At the end of tumor monitoring period, we compared gut microbiome composition in GH host that received FMT from SI or GH donors. Five bacterial genera were significantly different in GH host with SI -FMT, compared with GH -FMT (Table 2). Of these, Bifidobacterium, Blautia (both increased in SI- FMT host), and Turicibacter (reduced in SI- FMT host) are known SCFA producers. The abundance of pathogenic Streptococcus genus was higher in SI FMT than GH FMT hosts, and Streptohyta_unclassified was reduced in SI- FMT host. Of these Turicibacter was the only one also reduced in SI mice not bearing tumors.

PHGG suppressed mammary tumorigenesis in SI mice

PHGG supplementation for 4 weeks increased fecal SCFA levels, assessed in GH mice (Fig. 3A). However, it did not significantly reduce mammary tumorigenesis in GH mice (Fig. 3B), possibly because normal gut microbiome in GH mice does not influence E0771 mammary tumor growth, even when SCFAs are elevated. In SI mice, PHGG significantly reduced E0771 mammary tumor growth (Fig. 3C).

Differences in tumor growth patterns were analyzed by comparing tumor volumes measured twice a week across the tumor monitoring period using two-way repeated ANOVA. Data are shown as mean ± SEM.

Social isolation increased immunosuppressive cells and reduced CD8 + T cell activation

Since gut microbiome is a key modulator of the host immune system [36], we investigated whether SI altered the frequency and activation of immune cells in vitro and spleen and/or the tumor microenvironment (TME) in vivo.

Naïve CD4 + T cells were harvested from the spleen of SI and GH mice and treated with IL2 and activated with anti-CD3/anti-CD28 antibodies for 72 h (Fig. 4A). A higher number of CD4 + T cells obtained from SI mice exhibited immunosuppressive cytokine IL10 positivity than CD4 + T cells from GH mice (p = 0.03) (Fig. 4B, Additional file 4). Consistent with this finding, a higher percentage of CD4 + T cells from SI were Foxp3 + Treg cells than from GH mice (p = 0.009) (Fig. 4C, Additional file 4).

Discussion

As previously reported in other syngeneic TNBC models [17], in our study social isolation increased the growth of grafted E0771 mammary tumors in female mice. These findings indicate that SI alters mammary gland environment to be more permissible to cancer growth. SI has also been found to increase the risk of developing estrogen receptor positive (ER+) and TNBC mammary tumorigenesis in non-grafted mouse and rat models, including C3 [1]/SV40 T-antigen mice [37], MMTV-neu mice [38], carcinogen-induced mammary tumor models in rats and mice [39, 40], and in rats which developed mammary tumors spontaneously when they aged [41]. Thus, in animal models, SI increases mammary cancer risk and tumor growth.

In our study, the increase in E0771 mammary tumorigenesis in SI mice was linked to changes in the gut microbiome composition. However, in three repeat experiments, the patterns of microbial changes differed from study to study. Like humans, mice exhibit vast inter-individual differences in their gut microbiome composition [42]. The causes of variability in mice are partly genetic, but mostly environmental [43]. This inter-individual variability highlights the difficulty in identifying specific gut bacterial changes predictive of cancer development, or the development of any other disease known to be associated with SI. Only one bacterial genus was consistently changed across the replicates: a significantly reduced abundance of Akkermansia in SI mice. This genus has one dominant species: Akkermansia (A.) muciniphila, a mucin degrading bacterium that generates nutrients to other gut bacteria [44]. A. muciniphila also increases the thickness of the mucus layer by increasing the production of SCFAs, mainly propionate, that in turn increases the number of mucin producing goblet cells [45]. Consequently, A. muciniphila is associated with multiple health benefits that range from reducing inflammatory [46] and metabolic [47] diseases, promoting healthy immune [48] and metabolic responses [49] and improving response to cancer immune therapies [50]. A recent study reported that SI impaired responsiveness to anti-PD1 immunotherapy [51].

Besides reduced abundance of Akkermansia in SI mice, 16 other bacterial genera were similarly changed in two of the three replicate comparisons between tumor-free SI and GH mice. Two bacteria showing increased abundance in SI mice are indicative of gut dysbiosis: Acetatifactor [52] and Duncaniella [53]. Increased abundance of Acetatifactor is also associated with cancer [54]. Unexpectedly, all these 16 genera, or their families, showing either reduced or increased relative abundances in SI mice are SCFA producers. Assessment of fecal SCFA levels in tumor-free mice indicated that only propionic acid, produced by Akkermansia, was significantly reduced in SI mice. There is some evidence that propionic acid inhibits breast cancer cell growth [55].

Three findings obtained in our study suggest that the increase in mammary tumorigenesis in SI mice was at least partly associated with SI-induced changes in gut microbiome. First, suppressing the abundance of fecal gut microbes with AB mix reduced mammary tumor burden in SI mice. Second, FMT from SI donors to GH hosts increased hosts’ mammary tumorigenesis, when compared with hosts receiving FMT from GH donors. Third, supplementing SI mice with fecal PHGG, which increases the production of fecal SCFAs, reduced their tumor burden. Earlier studies have generated mix results regarding AB effect on tumorigenesis: in some studies AB inhibits tumor growth [56] and in some it is referred to cause gut dysbiosis and increase tumor growth [19]. The key difference between the studies reporting opposite effects is that in studies in which AB increases tumor growth, mice have not received any prior treatment that would have increased their baseline cancer risk, while when AB reduces tumorigenesis, it is in mice that are at increased cancer risk. Our findings confirm these observations, since AB reduced tumorigenesis only in SI mice with increased breast cancer risk but did not affect GH mice. The result that PHGG supplementation reduced tumorigenesis in SI, but not in GH mice, further supports this interpretation.

AB treatment also reversed changes in immune cells in SI mice. SI mice exhibited changes indicative of immunosuppression in spleen and TME, i.e., reduced levels of activated CD8 + T cells and increased levels and activation of Foxp3+ Treg cells. It is possible that Th1 immune response bias in C57BL/6 mice impacted results obtained in our study and thus limits data interpretation. However, essentially similar effects of social isolation on 4T1 tumor growth and the modulation of CD8 + T cells and Foxp3 + Tregs have been reported in socially isolated BALB/c mice [17]. Given that BALB/c mice exhibit a Th2-dominant immune response, these findings suggest that the impact of SI on mammary tumorigenesis is not strictly dependent on a specific Th1/Th2 background. Instead, these effects may be mediated by broader mechanisms, such as gut dysbiosis and alterations in other immune signaling, which appear to be conserved across different genetic backgrounds. Inhibition of effector CD8 + T cells and activation of immunosuppressive Foxp3 + Treg cells in spleen of SI mice were eliminated in AB treated SI mice. Nevertheless, since we did not assess the effect of FMT on immune cells, it is not clear whether AB affected immune cells in SI mice via changes in gut microbiome or by directly affecting immune cells.

Conclusions

The results obtained in this study are summarized in Fig. 6 and they show that SI increased mammary tumorigenesis by inducing gut dysbiosis, characterized by reduced fecal abundance of Akkermansia and propionic acid levels, and immunosuppression in TME. Increased mammary tumorigenesis and immunosuppression in SI mice were prevented by treating mice with AB mix, and mammary tumorigenesis was increased in GH host mice receiving FMT from SI donor. Our findings also indicate that supplementing SI mice with PHGG, a dietary fiber that promotes SCFA production, reduced mammary tumorigenesis. Whether breast cancer patients experiencing increased levels of social isolation exhibit gut dysbiosis and whether this gut dysbiosis contributes to increased risk of recurrence and mortality remains to be studied.
