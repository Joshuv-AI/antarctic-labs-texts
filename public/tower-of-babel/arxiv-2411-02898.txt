The connectivity and phase transition in inhomogeneous random graphs of finite types
Hamin Jung

Abstract
A significant generalization of the Erdös-Rényi random graph model is an ‘inhomogeneous’ random graph where the edge probabilities vary according to vertex types. We identify the threshold value for this random graph with a finite number of vertex types to be connected and examine the model’s behavior near this threshold value. In particular, we show that the threshold value is c ​ log ⁡ n n c\frac{\log n}{n} for some c > 0 c>0 which is explicitly determined, where n n denotes the number of vertices. Furthermore, we prove that near the threshold, the graph consists of a giant component and isolated vertices. We also investigate the phase transition and provide an alternative proof of the results by Bollobás et al. [Random Struct. Algorithms, 31, 3-122 (2007)]. Our proofs are based on an exploration process that corresponds to the graph, and instead of relying heavily on branching processes, we employ a random walk constructed from the exploration process. We then apply a large deviations theory to show that a reasonably large component is always significantly larger, a strategy used in both connectivity and phase transition analysis.

The Erdös-Rényi random graph is ‘homogeneous’ in the sense that all the vertices are equivalent. For example, every vertex has the same degree distribution and the same chance of contributing to the largest component. However, this model is not realistic for our real world, since in practical situations, vertices have varying properties. A vertex may be tightly connected to some vertices while having fewer interactions with others. This observation, supported with real experiments and examples, led to the introduction of an ‘inhomogeneous’ random graph model defined as follows.

The number of adjacent vertices of a fixed vertex follows a linear combination of binomial distributions. In
[
3
]
, the authors use a standard exploration process (see Section

The advantage of this approach, first deriving extensive properties of branching processes and readily verifying graphical properties, is that it is applicable to graphical properties other than the component size.

In this article, we present an alternative proof of the phase transition of the giant component. Instead of heavily using branching processes, we mainly use a random walk and large deviations lemmas. Our result is the following.

2.2
Multi-type branching processes

bm

We state some well-known facts about multi-type Galton-Watson branching processes. Most of the results are from
[
8
, Chapter 2]
, and we mostly follow their notations.

Definition 2.1
.

Given an integer
m>0
and distributions
F_{k}\ (k=1,\dots,m)
with state space
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{m}
, a ‘multi-type branching process’ is a vector Markov process
\bm{Z}_{0},\bm{Z}_{1},\dots
where each
\bm{Z}_{t}
is a length
m
vector with non-negative integer components.
\bm{Z}_{0}
is called the initial state, and given
\bm{Z}_{t}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})
,
\bm{Z}_{t+1}
is defined by

\bm{Z}_{t+1}=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\sum_{i=1}^{x_{k}}\bm{\eta}_{i}^{(k)}

where
\bm{\eta}_{i}^{(k)}
are independent random variables with
\bm{\eta}_{i}^{(k)}\sim F_{k}.

As usual, the particles in
\bm{Z}_{t+1}
are thought as the ‘offsprings’ of the particles in
\bm{Z}_{t}
. In this article, the number of types
m
and each distribution
F_{k}
will be clear from the context and we will not write them explicitly. When the initial state is
\bm{Z}_{0}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})
, we write subscripts in the probability and expectation to indicate the fixed initial state. For example, we write
\mathbb{P}_{(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})}
or
\mathbb{P}_{A}
where
A
is a set consisting of
x_{k}
elements of type
k
particles,
k=1,\dots,m
. When
\bm{Z}_{0}=\bm{e}_{k}
for some
k
, we usually write
\mathbb{P}_{x}
where
x
is any particle of type
k
, instead of
\mathbb{P}_{\{x\}}
or
\mathbb{P}_{\bm{e}_{k}}
.

In a 1-type branching process, the expected value of the offspring distribution was the critical value for extinction. In a
m
-type branching process, we use a
m\times m
matrix
\mathbf{M}
called the ‘associated matrix’, whose
(k,l)
-th entry is the expected value of the number of type
l
offsprings from a single particle of type
k
. We write
q_{k}
as the extinction probability of the branching process that starts from one individual of type
k
, i.e.
\bm{Z}_{0}=\bm{e}_{k}
.

Remark 2.2
.

The Perron-Frobenius theorem asserts that given a square matrix
A
with non-negative entries, there exists
\mu\geq 0
, called the ‘Perron root’, such that for every other eigenvalue
\lambda
of
A
we have
|\lambda|\leq\mu
. It also guarantees the existence of an eigenvector corresponding to
\mu
with non-negative components. For our associated matrix
\mathbf{M}
we write the Perron root as
\mu\geq 0
.

We shall exclude branching processes where every individual has exactly one offspring. These trivial processes are called ‘singular’, and we only consider non-singular cases. We call
\mathbf{M}

We introduce two exploration processes on the vertices of

2.3.2
The lower exploration process

Our second exploration process works as a lower bound in future arguments. To distinguish from the upper exploration process, we add a superscript

3
The largest component

We mainly follow the methods given in Sections 2.2 and 2.3 of
[
4
]
.

In this section, we shall always assume Remark
1.4
. Recall that if this assumption does not hold, we may divide the graph
G^{\prime}
into components and divide
V
into sections that have no effect on each other in the exploring process.

Fix a random graph model
\mathbb{G}(n,m,\bm{a},\bm{c},p(n))
. Recall the matrix
\Lambda
defined in Section
1
and Remark
2.2
. Consider the row vector
\bm{u}=(u_{1},\dots,u_{m})
whose transpose is the eigenvector corresponding to the Perron root
\mu
, i.e.

\Lambda\bm{u}^{\dagger}=\mu\bm{u}^{\dagger}\textrm{ or }\bm{u}\Lambda^{\dagger}=\mu\bm{u}.

(3.1)

Remark
2.2
allows us to assume
0\leq u_{i}\leq 1
for all
i=1,\dots,m
. Note that in the homogeneous case, we may consider the eigenvector as
u=1>0
. The Perron-Frobenius theorem only assures that the components of the eigenvector
\bm{u}
are non-negative, but to apply the methods in
[
4
]
, we need that all the components are positive. Fortunately, thanks to our assumption in Remark
1.4
, we have the following.

Proposition 3.1
.

We have
u_{k}>0
for all
k=1,\dots,m
.

Proof.

Suppose without loss of generality that
u_{1}=\dots=u_{l}=0
,
u_{l+1}=\dots=u_{m}>0
, where
1\leq l<m
.

u_{1}=0
implies

u_{1}a_{1}c_{11}+u_{2}a_{2}c_{12}+\dots+u_{m}a_{m}c_{1m}=0,

and since every term in the L.H.S. is non-negative,
a_{k}>0
for all
k
, we have

u_{1}c_{11}=u_{2}c_{12}=\dots=u_{m}c_{1m}=0.

Proceeding with
u_{2}=0,\dots,u_{l}=0
gives

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle u_{1}c_{11}=u_{2}c_{21}=\dots=u_{m}c_{m1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle u_{1}c_{12}=u_{2}c_{22}=\dots=u_{m}c_{m2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\cdots\ =u_{1}c_{1l}=u_{2}c_{2l}=\dots=u_{m}c_{ml}.

Hence we have
c_{kk^{\prime}}=0
for
k=1,\dots,l,\ k^{\prime}=l+1,\dots,m
. This implies that the collapsed graph
G^{\prime}
may be divided into sections
\{1,\dots,l\}
and
\{l+1,\dots,m\}
, which is a contradiction to our assumption in Remark
1.4
. Hence we must have
u_{k}>0
for all
k
.
∎

Denote
u=\min\{u_{1},\dots,u_{m}\}
and
U=\max\{u_{1},\dots,u_{m}\}
. We also define a filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
of
\sigma
-algebras where each
\mathcal{F}_{t}
is the
\sigma
-algebra generated by all sets occuring in the upper exploration process until time
t
. Then we have
1_{v_{t+1}\in V^{(k)}}\in\mathcal{F}_{t}
for all
k=1,\dots,m
. This gives

\mathbb{E}[\bm{S}_{t+1}-\bm{S}_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t}]=(np\Lambda^{\dagger}-I)(1_{v_{t+1}\in V^{(1)}},\dots,1_{v_{t+1}\in V^{(m)}})^{\dagger}

and

\mathbb{E}[\bm{u}(\bm{S}_{t+1}-\bm{S}_{t})|\mathcal{F}_{t}]=(np\mu-1)\bm{u}(1_{v_{t+1}\in V^{(1)}},\dots,1_{v_{t+1}\in V^{(m)}})^{\dagger}.

(3.2)

Now define

X_{0}=\bm{u}\bm{S}_{0},\ X_{t}=\bm{u}\bm{S}_{t}-\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}(np\mu-1)\bm{u}(1_{v_{i+1}\in V^{(1)}},\dots,1_{v_{i+1}\in V^{(m)}})^{\dagger}.

Then (
3.2
) gives
\mathbb{E}[X_{t+1}-X_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t}]=0
, hence
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
-martingale.

Define a stopping time
T=\mathrm{inf}\{t:\bm{S}_{t}=\bm{0}\}
. Since
\bm{S}_{t}\geq\bm{A}_{t}
, we have
T\geq\tau
. Hence the random walk
\bm{S}_{t}
will give an upper bound of our component size. From now on in this section, we shall use
p(n)=\lambda/n
where
\lambda>0
is a constant.

3.1
The subcritical case:
\mu\lambda<1

Assume
\mu\lambda<1
throughout this subsection. Our goal here is to prove part (i) of Theorem
1.7
. Let the upper exploration process start from a vertex
x\in V^{(k)}
. We write
\mathbb{P}_{x},\mathbb{E}_{x}
to indicate that the process starts from
A_{0}=\{x\}
. Then the optional stopping theorem gives

\displaystyle u_{k}=\mathbb{E}_{x}X_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}X_{t\wedge T}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\bm{u}\bm{S}_{t\wedge T}^{\dagger}-\sum_{i=0}^{t\wedge T-1}(\mu\lambda-1)\bm{u}(1_{v_{i+1}\in V^{(1)}},\dots,1_{v_{i+1}\in V^{(m)}})^{\dagger}\right]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle(1-\mu\lambda)u\mathbb{E}_{x}[t\wedge T].

Letting
t\to\infty
, we have

\mathbb{E}_{x}T\leq\frac{u_{k}}{(1-\mu\lambda)u}<\infty.

In particular,
T<\infty\ \mathbb{P}_{x}\textrm{-a.s.}

By using the moment generating function as in
[
4
]
, we may prove that the largest component of our graph
G
has size
O_{C}(\log n)
. But we first state a simple observation that is needed to incorporate (
3.1
) into our calculations. Given a constant
0<a\leq 1
, we have

a(e^{\theta}-1)\geq e^{a\theta}-1,\ \forall\theta\in\mathbb{R}.

(3.3)

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
\alpha=\mu\lambda-1-\log(\mu\lambda)>0
. For
a>1/\alpha u
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left[\max_{x\in V}|\mathcal{C}_{x}|\geq a\log n\right]\to 0

as
n\to\infty
.

Proof.

Let
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
. Since binomial distributions are the sum of i.i.d. Bernoulli random variables, we have from (
3.3
) and (
3.1
)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\theta\bm{u}(\bm{S}_{t+1}-\bm{S}_{t})\right)\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{m}1_{\{v_{t+1}\in V^{(k)}\}}e^{-\theta u_{k}}\prod_{l=1}^{m}(1-p_{lk}+e^{\theta u_{l}}p_{lk})^{a_{l}n}

(3.4)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{m}1_{\{v_{t+1}\in V^{(k)}\}}\exp\left(-\theta u_{k}+\lambda\sum_{l=1}^{m}a_{l}c_{lk}(e^{\theta u_{l}}-1)\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{m}1_{\{v_{t+1}\in V^{(k)}\}}\exp\left(-\theta u_{k}+\lambda\sum_{l=1}^{m}a_{l}c_{lk}u_{l}(e^{\theta}-1)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{m}1_{\{v_{t+1}\in V^{(k)}\}}\exp\left(-\theta u_{k}+\mu\lambda u_{k}(e^{\theta}-1)\right).

Now let

\varphi_{k}(\theta):=\exp\bigl(u_{k}(-\theta+\mu\lambda(e^{\theta}-1))\bigr)

and
C(\theta):=\max_{1\leq k\leq m}\varphi_{k}(\theta)
. Putting
\theta_{0}=-\log(\mu\lambda)>0
gives
\varphi_{k}(\theta_{0})=\exp(-\alpha u_{k})\ \forall k
, hence

Let
C:=C(\theta_{0})
. Then
M_{t}:=\exp(\theta_{0}\bm{u}\bm{S}_{t})/C^{t}
is a
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
-supermartingale. Now suppose that we started the exploration process from a vertex

. Since we know

, the optional stopping theorem implies

e^{u_{k}\theta_{0}}=\mathbb{E}_{x}M_{0}\geq\mathbb{E}_{x}M_{T}=\mathbb{E}_{x}C^{-T},

hence

\mathbb{P}_{x}[T\geq k]\leq C^{k}\mathbb{E}_{x}C^{-T}\leq C^{k}e^{u_{k}\theta_{0}}\leq e^{-\alpha uk}e^{\theta_{0}}.

Now for any

\mathbb{P}\left[|\mathcal{C}_{x}|\geq\frac{(1+\epsilon)\log n}{\alpha u}\right]\leq\mathbb{P}_{x}\left[T\geq\frac{(1+\epsilon)\log n}{\alpha u}\right]\leq n^{-1-\epsilon}e^{\theta_{0}}.

(3.5)

Therefore, for each

, there exists

such that

\mathbb{P}\left[\max_{x\in V}|\mathcal{C}_{x}|\geq a\log n\right]=O(n^{-\epsilon})

and our conclusion follows.
∎

3.2
The supercritical case:
\mu\lambda>1

The supercritical case is more complicated than the previous subcritical case. In
[
4
]
, one of the most crucial tools is a large deviations lemma which guarantees that there are enough active vertices w.h.p. However, this lemma is not immediately applicable to the inhomogeneous model. Therefore, we first start with a large deviations lemma that has limited conclusions but still sufficient to apply to our inhomogeneous model. The following lemma is an observation on the maximal ratio of first taking the exponential and then taking a linear combination versus taking a linear combination first and then applying the exponential.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
a\neq b
, and consider

f_{a,b}(p):=\frac{pe^{a}+(1-p)e^{b}}{e^{pa+(1-p)b}}.

Then, we have

\max_{0\leq p\leq 1}f_{a,b}(p)=f_{a,b}(p_{0})=\frac{\frac{e^{b-a}-1}{b-a}}{\exp(b-a+1-\frac{b-a}{1-e^{-(b-a)}})}

where
p_{0}=\frac{1}{1-e^{-(b-a)}}-\frac{1}{b-a}\in[0,1]
.

We remark that the maximal ratio only depends on the difference
b-a
. Note that the convexity of the exponential function immediately gives
f(p)\geq 1
, but what we need is an upper bound on
f(p)
to prove the following large deviations lemma.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\delta>0
and
Z=X_{1}+\dots+X_{t}

where

is a partition of the probability space

for each

, and all the binomial random variables appearing in the

\sigma(B_{ik}:1\leq i\leq t,\ 1\leq k\leq m)

Put
\gamma(x):=x\log x-x+1
that satisfies
\gamma(x)>0
for all

. Let
x<1<y
. Then we have

\mathbb{P}[Z\leq x\mathbb{E}Z]\leq G(x)^{t}

and

\mathbb{P}[Z\geq y\mathbb{E}Z]\leq G(y)^{t}

for some function
G:\mathbb{R}_{>0}\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
which satisfies
G(x)<1
for
x<1

for

Proof.

For
\theta\in\mathbb{R}

\mathbb{E}\exp(\theta X_{i})\leq\sum_{k=1}^{m}\mathbb{P}(B_{ik})\exp\left(\mu\lambda(1-\delta)u_{k}(e^{\theta}-1)\right)

and the binomial random variables.
Note that

\mathbb{E}X_{i}=\mu\lambda(1-\delta)\sum_{k=1}^{m}\mathbb{P}(B_{ik})u_{k}.

For simplicity we shall write
U_{k}=\mu\lambda(1-\delta)u_{k}

(i) Let

\theta<0

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[Z\leq x\mathbb{E}Z]\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}(\exp\theta Z)}{\exp(\theta x\mathbb{E}Z)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{t}\Bigl(\sum_{k=1}^{m}\mathbb{P}(B_{ik})\exp(U_{k}(e^{\theta}-1))\Bigr)}{\exp(\theta x\mathbb{E}Z)}

(3.7)

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{t}\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{m}\mathbb{P}(B_{ik})\exp(U_{k}(e^{\theta}-1))}{\exp\Bigl(\theta x\sum_{k=1}^{m}\mathbb{P}(B_{ik})U_{k}\Bigr)}.

Putting

\mathbb{P}[Z\leq x\mathbb{E}Z]=\prod_{i=1}^{t}\exp\Bigl(-\gamma(x)\sum_{k=1}^{m}\mathbb{P}(B_{ik})U_{k}\Bigr)\times\prod_{i=1}^{t}\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{m}\mathbb{P}(B_{ik})\exp(U_{k}(x-1))}{\exp\Bigl(\sum_{k=1}^{m}\mathbb{P}(B_{ik})U_{k}(x-1)\Bigr)}.

(3.8)

Now let
A=\max\{U_{k}\}

B=\min\{U_{k}\}
. Since
\sum_{k=1}^{m}\mathbb{P}(B_{ik})=1
for
1\leq i\leq t
, the numerator in (
3.8
) is a linear combination of
\exp\left(A(x-1)\right)
and
\exp\left(B(x-1)\right)
. Since
\gamma(x)>0
and
x<1
, from (
3.8
) we have

\mathbb{P}[Z\leq x\mathbb{E}Z]\leq\exp(-t\gamma(x)B)F(x)^{t}

where

F(x)=\max_{p\in[0,1]}f_{A(x-1),B(x-1)}(p).

Putting

h(x)=e^{-\gamma(x)B}(e^{(A-B)(1-x)}-1),

g(x)=(A-B)(1-x)\exp\Bigl((A-B)(1-x)+1-\frac{(A-B)(1-x)}{1-e^{-(A-B)(1-x)}}\Bigr)

and differentiating gives

h^{\prime}(1)=g^{\prime}(1)=-(A-B),

h^{\prime\prime}(1)=g^{\prime\prime}(1)=(A-B)^{2},

h^{\prime\prime\prime}(1)=-(A-B)^{3}+3B(A-B),\ g^{\prime\prime\prime}(1)=-(A-B)^{3}.

This implies
g(x)-h(x)>0
for
x<1
near
1
, and thanks to Lemma
3.3

G(x):=\exp(-\gamma(x)B)F(x)=\frac{h(x)}{g(x)}<1

for
x<1
near 1, and we have

\mathbb{P}[Z\leq x\mathbb{E}Z]\leq G(x)^{t}

as desired.

(ii) For
y>1
, by a similar argument to (i) with
\theta=\log y
gives

\mathbb{P}[Z\geq y\mathbb{E}Z]\leq\exp(-t\gamma(y)B)F(y)^{t},

and

\mathbb{P}[Z\geq y\mathbb{E}Z]\leq G(y)^{t}.

It is readily verified that
G(y)\to 0
as
y\to\infty
, and in particular we must have
G(y)<1
for
y\gg 1
.
∎

As introduced in
[
4
]
, the main idea to prove the existence of a giant component is to

1.

show that a component of order
\geq\beta\log n
must have order
\Omega_{C}(n^{2/3})
,

2.

then show that if two clusters are sufficiently large they must intersect w.h.p.

For the homogeneous model, the second part is easily done since the probability that two clusters of size at least
n^{2/3}
do not intersect is

(1-p)^{n^{4/3}}\sim\exp(-pn^{4/3})=\exp(-O(n^{-1/3}))=o(1).

But in our situation we have the possibility that
p_{kl}=0
for some
k,l
and that the two big clusters consist only of type
k
vertices and type
l
vertices respectively. In order to solve this issue, we observe that for any
l^{\prime}\in\{1,\dots,m\}
satisfying
c_{kl^{\prime}}>0
, the first big cluster consisting of type
k
vertices must be connected to a large number of type
l^{\prime}
vertices.
The next two lemmas are precise statements of this observation.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
V,W
be sets of fixed labelled vertices of size
\beta n^{2/3}
,
\beta^{\prime}n
, respectively (
\beta,\beta^{\prime}>0
). Let
p=\frac{c}{n}(c>0)
be the probability of a vertex in
V
and a vertex in
W
being adjacent. We assume that all the edges
\eta_{x,y}
are independent for all
x\in V,\ y\in W
. Letting

Y=\#\{y\in W:\exists x\in V\textrm{ s.t. }x,y\textrm{ are adjacent}\},

we have
Y\sim\beta\beta^{\prime}cn^{2/3}
.

Proof.

For each
y\in W
, define
Y_{y}=1
if
y
is connected to
V
and
Y_{y}=0
otherwise. Then

\mathbb{P}[Y_{y}=0]=(1-p)^{|V|}\sim\exp(-\beta cn^{-1/3}).

Note that the
Y_{y}
’s are independent and
Y=\sum_{y\in W}Y_{y}
. Hence

\mathbb{E}Y=\beta^{\prime}n(1-(1-p)^{|V|})\sim\beta\beta^{\prime}cn^{2/3}

and

\textrm{var}(Y)=\beta^{\prime}n(1-(1-p)^{|V|})(1-p)^{|V|}\sim\beta\beta^{\prime}cn^{2/3}.

Applying Chebyshev gives

\mathbb{P}\left[|Y-\mathbb{E}Y|\geq(\log n)(\mathbb{E}Y)^{1/2}\right]=o(1).

∎

By using the same methods as above, we also have the following.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
V,W
be sets of fixed labelled vertices of size
\beta n
,
\beta^{\prime}n
, respectively (
\beta,\beta^{\prime}>0
). Let
p=\frac{c}{n}(c>0)
be the probability of a vertex in
V
and a vertex in
W
being adjacent. We assume that all the edges
\eta_{x,y}
are independent for all
x\in V,\ y\in W
. Letting

Y=\#\{y\in W:\exists x\in V\textrm{ s.t. }x,y\textrm{ are adjacent}\},

we have
Y\sim\beta^{\prime}n(1-e^{-\beta c})
.

We finally prove part (ii) of Theorem
1.7
.
We follow the steps in
[
4
]
, but to present a more rigorous and self-contained explanation, we prove the theorem in 5 steps.

Step 1
.

Fix a
\delta>0
such that
\mu\lambda(1-\delta)>1
. There exists some
\gamma>0
such that the lower exploration process from Section
2.3.2
starting with at least
\gamma\log n
active vertices (i.e.
|\hat{A}_{0}|\geq\gamma\log n
) never dies out w.h.p. More precisely,

\mathbb{P}\left[\bm{W}_{t}=\bm{0}\textrm{ for some }t\right]\leq n^{-2}

for all processes starting with
|\hat{A}_{0}|\geq\gamma\log n
.

Proof.

We use a similar approach used in the proof of Theorem
3.2
. Let
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
and follow (
3.4
) to obtain

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\theta\bm{u}(\bm{W}_{t+1}-\bm{W}_{t})\right)|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]\leq\sum_{k=1}^{m}1_{\hat{v}_{t+1}\in V^{(k)}}\exp\left(-\theta u_{k}+\mu\lambda(1-\delta)(e^{\theta}-1)u_{k}\right).

Now let

\varphi_{k}(\theta):=\exp\Bigl(u_{k}(-\theta+\mu\lambda(1-\delta)(e^{\theta}-1))\Bigr)

and
C(\theta):=\max_{1\leq k\leq m}\varphi_{k}(\theta)
. Since

\varphi_{k}^{\prime}(\theta)=(-u_{k}+u_{k}\mu\lambda(1-\delta)e^{\theta})\varphi_{k}(\theta),

we have
\varphi_{k}^{\prime}(0)=u_{k}(-1+\mu\lambda(1-\delta))>0
for
k=1,\dots,m
. Hence there exists
\theta_{\delta}>0
such that
C(-\theta_{\delta})\leq 1
. Then
M_{t}:=\exp(-\theta_{\delta}\bm{u}\bm{W}_{t}^{\dagger})
is a supermartingale w.r.t.
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
.
We now define
T_{0}:=\textrm{inf}\{t:\bm{W}_{t}=\bm{0}\}
and fix
x\in V^{(k)}
. By the optional stopping time theorem,

e^{-\theta_{\delta}u_{k}}=\mathbb{E}_{x}M_{0}\geq\mathbb{E}_{x}M_{t\wedge T_{0}}\geq\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\exp(-\theta_{\delta}\bm{u}\bm{W}_{T_{0}}^{\dagger});T_{0}\leq t\right]=\mathbb{P}_{x}[T_{0}\leq t]

(3.9)

where as usual the subscript
x
in
\mathbb{E}_{x}
and
\mathbb{P}_{x}
means that the lower exploration process starts with
x
. In the followings,
\mathbb{E}_{l}\ (l\in\mathbb{N})
and
\mathbb{P}_{l}
shall denote the expected value and probability for the exploration process starting with
l
vertices, where any type combination is possible. This is an abuse of notation; to be precise the initial
\hat{A}_{0}
is fixed for each probability space and
\mathbb{E}_{l}
may have different values according to the type combination of the initial state. However, the inequalities below are true regardless of the type combination.

By following the same argument as in (
3.9
) and letting
t\to\infty
, we have

\mathbb{P}_{l}[T_{0}<\infty]\leq e^{-\theta_{\delta}ul}.

Now setting
\gamma=2/u\theta_{\delta}>0
gives

\mathbb{P}_{\gamma\log n}[T_{0}<\infty]\leq n^{-2}

as desired.
∎

Step 2
.

There exists
\beta>0
such that
\mathbb{P}_{A}(0<|A_{\beta\log n}|<\gamma\log n)=o(1)
for all
A\subset V
with
|A|=o(n/\log n)
.

Proof.

Let

S_{t}:=\bm{u}\bm{S}_{t}^{\dagger},\ I_{t}:=\bm{u}\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}(1_{v_{i+1}\in V^{(1)}},\dots,1_{v_{i+1}\in V^{(m)}})^{\dagger}.

Then we may apply Lemma
3.4
with
\delta=0
and
B_{ik}=\{v_{i}\in V^{(k)}\}
for
Z=S_{t}-S_{0}+I_{t}
. Note that

\mathbb{E}Z=\mu\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{t}\sum_{k=1}^{m}u_{k}\mathbb{P}[v_{i}\in V^{(k)}],\ \mathbb{E}I_{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{t}\sum_{k=1}^{m}u_{k}\mathbb{P}[v_{i}\in V^{(k)}],

hence
\mathbb{E}Z=\mu\lambda\mathbb{E}I_{t}
. Take an appropriate
\epsilon>0
such that
G(x)<1
for
x=\frac{1}{\mu\lambda}(\frac{2\epsilon}{u}+1)<1
, where
G
is from Lemma
3.4
.

We want to show that
\mathbb{P}[S_{t}-S_{0}\leq\epsilon t]
is small by using Lemma
3.4
on
Z
. Now, in the homogeneous case, we have
I_{t}=t
deterministically and
\mathbb{E}Z=\mu\lambda t=\mu\lambda I_{t}
. This allows us to immediately apply the large deviations lemma to
Z
since

\mathbb{P}[S_{t}-S_{0}\leq\epsilon t]=\mathbb{P}[Z\leq I_{t}+\epsilon t]=\mathbb{P}[Z\leq(1+\epsilon)t].

However, in our inhomogeneous case,
I_{t}
is a genuine random variable and we obviously do not have
\mathbb{E}Z=\mu\lambda I_{t}
, which is desirable to apply Lemma
3.4
. Therefore we need to apply an additional deviations bound for
I_{t}
. For that reason we fix a
\delta^{\prime}>0
such that
2\delta^{\prime}<1
. Then we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[S_{t}-S_{0}\leq\epsilon t]

(3.10)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[S_{t}-S_{0}\leq\epsilon t,\ I_{t}\geq t^{1/2+\delta^{\prime}}+\mathbb{E}I_{t}\right]+\mathbb{P}\left[S_{t}-S_{0}\leq\epsilon t,\ I_{t}<t^{1/2+\delta^{\prime}}+\mathbb{E}I_{t}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[I_{t}\geq t^{1/2+\delta^{\prime}}+\mathbb{E}I_{t}\right]+\mathbb{P}\left[Z\leq\epsilon t+t^{1/2+\delta^{\prime}}+\mathbb{E}I_{t}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\exp(-2t^{2\delta^{\prime}}/U^{2})+\mathbb{P}\left[Z\leq\epsilon t+t^{1/2+\delta^{\prime}}+\mathbb{E}Z/\mu\lambda\right]

where the last inequality follows from Hoeffding’s inequality. By observing that
\mathbb{E}Z\geq\mu\lambda ut
and applying Lemma
3.4
, the last term in (
3.10
) can be estimated by

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[Z\leq\epsilon t+t^{1/2+\delta^{\prime}}+\mathbb{E}Z/\mu\lambda\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[Z\leq\mathbb{E}Z(\frac{\epsilon t+t^{2\delta^{\prime}}}{\mathbb{E}Z}+\frac{1}{\mu\lambda})\right]

(3.11)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[Z\leq\mathbb{E}Z\left(\frac{\epsilon t+t^{2\delta^{\prime}}}{\mu\lambda ut}+\frac{1}{\mu\lambda}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[Z\leq\mathbb{E}Z\left(\frac{2\epsilon t}{\mu\lambda ut}+\frac{1}{\mu\lambda}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[Z\leq\frac{1}{\mu\lambda}\left(\frac{2\epsilon}{u}+1\right)\mathbb{E}Z\right]\to 0

as
t\to\infty
. Combining (
3.10
) and (
3.11
), we obtain

\mathbb{P}[S_{t}-S_{0}\leq\epsilon t]\to 0\textrm{ as }t\to\infty.

(3.12)

Fix a
y>1
such that
G(y)<1
. Again by Lemma
3.4
,

\mathbb{P}\left[S_{t}-S_{0}+I_{t}\geq y\mu\lambda Ut\right]\leq\mathbb{P}\left[Z\geq y\mathbb{E}Z\right]\to 0\textrm{ as }t\to\infty.

(3.13)

We note that
S_{t}-S_{0}+I_{t}<y\mu\lambda Ut
corresponds to

\bm{u}\bm{U}_{t}^{\dagger}>n\bm{u}\bm{a}^{\dagger}-y\mu\lambda Ut-S_{0}

as
\bm{U}_{t}+\bm{W}_{t}+\bm{I}_{t}=n\bm{a}
for all
t
. Since
\bm{U}_{s}\geq\bm{U}_{s+1}
for all
s
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{A}\left[S_{t}-S_{0}+I_{t}<y\mu\lambda Ut,\ \bm{S}_{t}\neq\bm{A}_{t}\right]

(3.14)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle u^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{A}\left[\sum_{s=1}^{t}\sum_{k=1}^{m}1_{v_{s}\in V^{(k)}}\bm{u}\left(\textrm{Bin}({a_{l}n-|U_{s}^{(l)}|},{p_{lk}})\right)_{1\leq l\leq m}^{\dagger};S_{t}-S_{0}+I_{t}<y\mu\lambda Ut\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle u^{-1}\sum_{s=1}^{t}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\sum_{l=1}^{m}u_{l}(a_{l}n-|U_{s}^{(l})|)p_{lk}\textrm{ with }\bm{u}\bm{U}_{t}^{\dagger}>n\bm{u}\bm{a}^{\dagger}-y\mu\lambda Ut-S_{0}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle Cu^{-1}\sum_{s=1}^{t}\sum_{k=1}^{m}(y\mu\lambda Ut+S_{0})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{Cmt}{nu}(y\mu\lambda Ut+|A|)

for
C=\max\{c_{kl}:1\leq k,l\leq m\}
.
Now choose
\beta>0
such that
\epsilon\beta>\gamma
where
\gamma
is from
Step 1
.
Put
r=\beta\log n
. Combining (
3.12
), (
3.13
), and (
3.14
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{A}[0<|A_{r}|\leq\epsilon r]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{A}[S_{r}-S_{0}+I_{r}\geq y\mu\lambda Ur]+\mathbb{P}_{A}[S_{r}-S_{0}+I_{r}<y\mu\lambda Ur,\ \bm{S}_{r}\neq\bm{A}_{r}]

\displaystyle\mbox{ }+\mathbb{P}_{A}[0<|A_{r}|\leq\epsilon r,\ \bm{S}_{r}=\bm{A}_{r}]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle o(1)+\mathbb{P}_{A}[S_{r}-S_{0}\leq\epsilon r]=o(1).

∎

Step 3
.

All clusters reaching size
\beta\log n
intersect w.h.p.

Proof.

We define
W_{t}:=\bm{u}\bm{W}_{t}^{\dagger}
.
By similar calculations to (
3.12
), (
3.13
), we have for some
\epsilon_{\delta}>0,y>1
,

\mathbb{P}[W_{t}-W_{0}\leq\epsilon_{\delta}t]\to 0,

(3.15)

\mathbb{P}[W_{t}-W_{0}+I_{t}\geq y\mu\lambda(1-\delta)Ut]\to 0

(3.16)

as
t\to\infty
.
Recall from
Step 2
that we put
r=\beta\log n
. Letting
a=a_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge a_{m}
, (
3.16
) implies

\mathbb{P}\left[W_{n^{2/3}}-W_{0}+I_{n^{2/3}}\geq\delta aun-r\right]\to 0

as

, and since

W_{s}-W_{0}+I_{s}\leq\delta aun-r,\ \forall s\leq n^{2/3}.

(3.17)

For
x\in V
, suppose that
\mathcal{C}_{x}
has size
\geq r.
Executing the upper exploration process with

, we have
|A_{r}|>0
. Then

implies that

w.h.p. After these

unexplored vertices. Hence by

In other words, the coupling between

w.h.p.. Combining with (

Step 4
.

\mathbb{P}[|\mathcal{C}_{x}|\leq\beta\log n]\to q_{k}
for fixed

Proof.

Let

We add a superscript
n
merely to avoid confusion. By the natural correspondence between branching processes and random walks (see e.g.
[

T_{0}^{(n)}
is the total progeny of a branching process
\bm{Y}^{(n)}_{t}
with
m
types of individuals, each type
k
having
\textrm{Bin}({a_{1}n},{p_{k1}})\otimes\dots\otimes\textrm{Bin}({a_{m}n},{p_{km}})
as offspring distribution.

Note that there exists a probability space such that

\mathbb{P}\left[\textrm{Bin}({l},{c/l})\neq\textrm{Poi}(c)\right]\leq 2c^{2}/l,

(3.18)

a result from general probability theory. We write
\bm{Z}=\bm{Z}^{\mathbb{G}}
for the branching process defined in Definition
1.5
. Assume that
\bm{Z}_{0}=\bm{Y}^{(n)}_{0}
and all the Poisson distributions occuring in
\bm{Z}_{t}
satisfy (

) with respect to the binomial distributions occuring in
\bm{Y}^{(n)}_{t}

Then, when conditioned on

,

and we have

\mathbb{P}\left[\bm{Y}_{t+1}^{(n)}=\bm{Z}_{t+1}\right]\geq\left(1-C\frac{\log n}{n}\right)^{t+1}\geq 1-C(t+1)\frac{\log n}{n}

(3.19)

for some constant
C>0
. Let
T_{0}
be the total progeny of
\bm{Z}_{t}
. Thanks to (

),

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[T_{0}^{(n)}\leq r_{n}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[T_{0}^{(n)}\leq r_{n},\ \bm{Y}_{t}^{(n)}=\bm{Z}_{t}\ \forall t\leq r_{n}\right]

(3.20)

\displaystyle\mbox{ }+\mathbb{P}\left[T_{0}^{(n)}\leq r_{n},\ \bm{Y}_{t}^{(n)}\neq\bm{Z}_{t}\ \exists t\leq r_{n}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[T_{0}\leq r_{n}\right]+\mathbb{P}\left[\bm{Y}_{t}^{(n)}\neq\bm{Z}_{t}\ \exists t\leq r_{n}\ \big|\ T_{0}^{(n)}\leq r_{n}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[T_{0}\leq r_{n}\right]+\sum_{t=1}^{r_{n}}\mathbb{P}\left[\bm{Y}_{t}^{(n)}\neq\bm{Z}_{t}\ \big|\ T_{0}^{(n)}\leq r_{n}\right]

Similarly, we have

(3.21)

Clearly

, and

is exactly the extinction probability of
\bm{Z}_{t}
, depending on the initial state.

Now fix a vertex

. Since

(3.22)

by (
3.20

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[|\mathcal{C}_{x}|\leq r_{n}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[T_{0}^{(n)}\leq r_{n}\right]+\mathbb{P}\left[T_{0}^{(n)}>r_{n},\ |\mathcal{C}_{x}|\leq r_{n}\right]

(3.23)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[T_{0}^{(n)}\leq r_{n}\right]+\mathbb{P}\left[T_{0}^{(n)}>r_{n},\ \bm{S}_{r_{n}}\neq\bm{A}_{r_{n}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[T_{0}^{(n)}\leq r_{n}\right]+\mathbb{P}\left[S_{r_{n}}-S_{0}+I_{r_{n}}<y\mu\lambda Ur_{n},\bm{S}_{r_{n}}\neq\bm{A}_{r_{n}}\right]

\displaystyle\mbox{ }+\mathbb{P}\left[S_{r_{n}}-S_{0}+I_{r_{n}}\geq y\mu\lambda Ur_{n}\right]

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[T_{0}^{(n)}\leq r_{n}\right]+o(1),

where the last equality owes to (

) and (

) and (

Step 5
.

The giant component has size
\sim n\sum_{k=1}^{m}a_{k}(1-q_{k})
.

Define random variables
Y_{x}:=1_{|\mathcal{C}_{x}|\leq\beta\log n}
for each

.
We shall find an upper bound for the covariance of two random variables
Y_{x}
and
Y_{y}\ (x\neq y)
and use this upper bound to deduce that the giant component size converges in probability.

In order to estimate the covariance of
Y_{x}
and
Y_{y}
, we consider two exploration processes starting from
x
and
y
simultaneously. We first run the upper exploration process with

until time

. Introduce another upper exploration process with

that continues according to (

) until

for some

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[Y_{x}=1,Y_{y}=1]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{C}_{x}\cap\mathcal{C}_{y}=\emptyset,Y_{x}=1,Y_{y}=1]+\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{C}_{x}\cap\mathcal{C}_{y}\neq\emptyset,Y_{x}=1,Y_{y}=1]

(3.24)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[Y_{x}=1]\mathbb{P}[Y_{y}=1]+\mathbb{P}[R_{\beta\log n}\cap R^{\prime}_{\beta\log n}\neq\emptyset]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[Y_{x}=1]\mathbb{P}[Y_{y}=1]+\lambda\max_{1\leq k,l\leq m}c_{kl}\frac{(\beta\log n)^{2}}{n}.

Now we have

\textrm{var}\left(\sum_{x\in V}Y_{x}\right)\leq N+\lambda C{N\choose 2}\frac{(\beta\log n)^{2}}{n}=O(n(\log n)^{2})

where
N=|V|=\sum_{k=1}^{m}a_{k}n
. Chebyshev’s inequality gives

\mathbb{P}\left[\left|\sum_{x\in V}(Y_{x}-\mathbb{E}Y_{x})\right|\geq n^{2/3}\right]=\frac{O(n(\log n)^{2})}{n^{4/3}}\to 0.

(3.25)

Put
Z:=\sum_{x\in V}Y_{x}
. Then
N-Z
is the size of the giant component w.h.p. by
Step 3
. (
3.25
) implies

\mathbb{P}\left[\left|\frac{1}{n}Z-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{x\in V}\mathbb{E}Y_{x}\right|\geq n^{-1/3}\right]\to 0,

and we have

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{x\in V}\mathbb{E}Y_{x}\to\sum_{k=1}^{m}a_{k}q_{k}

by
Step 4
. Thus the giant component, which exists w.h.p., has size
\sim n\sum_{k=1}^{m}a_{k}(1-q_{k})
, and the second largest component has size
\leq\beta\log n
w.h.p.

Recall that we only had
q_{k}<1\ \forall k
for positively regular
\Lambda
(Corollary
2.4
). But we immediately have
q_{k}<1\ \forall k
for
\Lambda
only satisfying our base assumption Remark
1.4
by Lemma
3.6
.
∎

We would like to emphasize that in the supercritical regime, every vertex type has a meaningful contribution to the giant component, i.e. there are
\sim a_{k}(1-q_{k})n
vertices of type
k
in the giant component for all
k=1,\dots,m
.

4
Connectivity

The homogeneous case (Theorem
1.2
) and the general case (Theorem
1.9
) hints that we would expect the threshold for connectivity to be
p(n)=\Theta(\frac{\log n}{n})
. Before we prove our main theorem on connectivity, Theorem
1.10
, we introduce new notations for conciseness. We additionally define
\lambda=\lambda(n)=np(n)
and use the matrix

\Lambda^{\prime}=(a_{l}np_{kl})_{1\leq k,l\leq m}=\lambda\Lambda.

Then
\Lambda
is a matrix of constant entries, and
\Lambda^{\prime}
is a matrix representing the expected number of offsprings of a vertex of a particular type. Let
\mu,\mu^{\prime}
be the Perron roots of
\Lambda,\Lambda^{\prime}
respectively. Then we have
\mu^{\prime}=\mu\lambda
, and there exists a vector
\bm{u}
that is an row eigenvector for
(\Lambda,\mu)
and
(\Lambda^{\prime},\mu^{\prime})
.

We shall assume Remark
1.4
as in the previous section. Then we have Proposition
3.1
, hence

U=\max\{u_{1},\dots,u_{m}\}>0\textrm{ and }u=\min\{u_{1},\dots,u_{m}\}>0.

We also assume
U\leq 1
.

We give a brief motivation on why the critical value is
p=\log n/bn
by noticing the similarities between a homogeneous graph model and a inhomogeneous model. We first turn to the homogeneous model and observe the behavior of
\mathbb{G}_{n,p}
for
p=\log n/n
. Theorem 7.3 of
[
2
]
calculates for

p=\frac{\log n+c+o(1)}{n}\ (c\in\mathbb{R})

that
\mathbb{G}_{n,p}
consists of a giant component and isolated vertices. This is done by calculating the expected number of components of size
2\leq k\leq n/2
. The same calculations are applicable in our inhomogeneous situation with

p=\frac{\log n+c+o(1)}{bn}\ (c\in\mathbb{R})

and
G
consists of a giant component and isolated vertices. This implies that the existence of isolated vertices is the essential cause for not being connected. On the other hand, it is natural to associate the expected value of the degree of a fixed vertex with the probability that it is isolated. This accounts for the appearance of
b
in the critical value.

4.1
The subcritical case

We first give a proof of (i) in Theorem

. Note that every vertex is equivalent in the homogeneous case, but for the inhomogeneous case, it is not guaranteed that every

Proof.

Assume that
\alpha b<1

(4.1)

Now let
I_{n}
denote the number of isolated vertices of type

\mathbb{E}I_{n}\sim a_{l}n^{1-\alpha b_{l}}\to\infty.

(4.2)

We calculate the variance of

\mathbb{P}[d_{x}=0,d_{y}=0]=\left(1-\frac{\alpha c_{ll}\log n}{n}\right)^{-1}\mathbb{P}[d_{x}=0]\mathbb{P}[d_{y}=0],

then

\displaystyle\textrm{var}(I_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a_{l}n\mathbb{P}[d_{x}=0]\left(1-\mathbb{P}[d_{x}=0]\right)

\displaystyle+a_{l}n(a_{l}n-1)\left(\left(1-\frac{\alpha c_{ll}\log n}{n}\right)^{-1}-1\right)\mathbb{P}[d_{x}=0]\mathbb{P}[d_{x}=0]

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle a_{l}n^{1-\alpha b_{l}}+Cn^{1-2\alpha b_{l}}\log n

\displaystyle a_{l}n^{1-\alpha b_{l}}.

By (

∎

4.2
The supercritical case

For the supercritical case, we use similar ideas used in the emergence of a giant component. We shall again execute exploration processes and use a large deviations bound.

Lemma 4.1
.

Under the same settings of Lemma

\mathbb{P}\left[Z\leq x\mu\lambda(1-\delta)ut\right]\leq\exp\left(-\gamma(x)\mu\lambda(1-\delta)ut\right).

4.3
The critical case

Let

\beta n

Lemma 4.2
.

Let

be the probability that for some

r

.

Proof.

We may assume
\lambda=(\log n+c)/b
. Let
q_{r}
be the probability that there exists a component of size
r
. Consider the complete graph
K
formed from the vertex set
V
. We write
T\in K
to indicate that
T
is a tree of order
r
contained in

and
E_{T}
shall denote the edges of

. We also put
\bm{i}^{T}=(i_{k}^{T})_{1\leq k\leq m}

q_{r}=\sum_{T\in K}\left[\prod_{e\in E_{T}}p_{e}\prod_{1\leq k,l\leq m}(1-p_{kl})^{(a_{l}n-i_{l}^{T})i_{k}^{T}}\right],

where
p_{e}
denotes the probability of the edge

in our model

. Now fix

\prod_{1\leq k,l\leq m}(1-p_{kl})^{(a_{l}n-i_{l})i_{k}}=\max_{T\in K}\left[\prod_{1\leq k,l\leq m}(1-p_{kl})^{(a_{l}n-i_{l}^{T})i_{k}^{T}}\right].

,

and
{n\choose k}\leq\left(\frac{ne}{k}\right)^{k}
for every

\displaystyle\sum_{r=2}^{N^{\prime}}q_{r}

\displaystyle\sum_{r=3}^{N^{\prime}}\left[{N\choose r}r^{r-2}\left(\frac{C\lambda}{n}\right)^{r-1}\prod_{1\leq k,l\leq m}(1-p_{kl})^{(a_{l}n-i_{l})i_{k}}\right]

\displaystyle\sum_{r=2}^{N^{\prime}}\left[(Ane)^{r}r^{-2}\left(C\frac{\log n+c}{bn}\right)^{r-1}\exp\left(-\sum_{k,l}p_{kl}(a_{l}n-i_{l})i_{k}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\sum_{r=2}^{N^{\prime}}\exp\Biggl(r(\log n+C^{\prime})+(r-1)(-\log n+\log\log n+C^{\prime})

\displaystyle\sum_{r=2}^{N^{\prime}}\exp\Biggl(r(\log n+C^{\prime})+(r-1)(-\log n+\log\log n+C^{\prime})

For
