The Orator has traditionally had two main tasks. Firstly, to welcome all our medical students, especially those commencing third year. On behalf of the Royal staff you are most welcome and I hope you will enjoy your clinical attachments. My Oration is primarily directed to you and to those in the early stages of their careers although hopefully there is something here for all of us.

My second task of delivering a thought-provoking Oration is somewhat more challenging. I had the privilege of working under Dr Norman Campbell and I love the humorous opening remarks from his oration in 2005 when he said that the Orator has the opportunity of ‘airing views on some topic of interest to himself if not to anyone else’1. My subject is definitely close to my own heart and I hope that my remarks will be of at least passing interest to others.

Can a disease be eliminated?

My interest in hepatitis C is well known. The trajectory of this disease mirrors my own career – it was first identified in 19892, the year I graduated from Queens. As you will hear it may be eliminated before 2030 by which time I expect to be retired - but hopefully not eliminated. My title is derived from phase one of the WHO Global Health Strategy to eliminate viral hepatitis as a major public health threat by 20303. It was published in 2016 and the UK signed up the same year. I want to consider the lessons we can learn from the hepatitis C story and through this to consider what it really means to ‘eliminate a disease’.

WHO had good reason for committing to an elimination strategy for viral hepatitis. In 2015, viral hepatitis was the only major infectious disease with a rising global death rate, overtaking HIV and tuberculosis (Figure 1). This was prior to Covid-19 which I will mention later. The strategy applied to the whole hepatitis alphabet – A, B, C, D and E - but the emphasis was on hepatitis B and C which at that time accounted for 95% of the estimated 1.4 million deaths from viral hepatitis each year.

Several circumstances in 2015 suggested the time might be right for elimination. Vaccines for A and B were well established and work was evolving on hepatitis E vaccination. Strategies were in place for prevention of mother to baby transmission of hepatitis B, reductions in healthcare transmission of hepatitis B and C and harm reduction for people who inject drugs. Most importantly, new potent anti-viral drugs for hepatitis C had just been released. You could not fault the WHO for being optimistic.

Estimated global number of deaths due to viral hepatitis, HIV, malaria and TB, 2000-2015

Of course, the WHO had an excellent track record in this respect, having eradicated smallpox4. For the pedants among you, there is a difference between elimination and eradication. Elimination means reducing the incidence of an infection to zero but requires ongoing interventions for control. Eradication means a permanent reduction to zero with no need for ongoing control.

Like so many infectious diseases, smallpox is known to have existed in ancient Egypt some 3000 years ago. More recent records from the past few centuries show that smallpox had a devastating mortality rate of up to 30% and it is thought to have killed 300 MILLION people in the 20th century alone.

Many of you will know the story of Dr Edward Jenner who noted the protection or immunity to smallpox that milkmaids developed due to their exposure to cowpox which was obviously a much less dangerous but related virus. This led Jenner to develop the first vaccine against variola in 17965.

It took over 150 years but WHO launched an eradication program in 1959. A lesson we must all remember is that despite having an excellent vaccine, the program failed – it was ineffective due to lack of funding and commitment. A well-resourced intensified relaunch in 1967 proved much more successful. The last case in Africa occurred in 1977 and by 1980 WHO declared smallpox eradicated – famously the only infectious disease ever to have been eradicated.

Two cautionary tales are worth noting. Monkeypox or Mpox as it is now known, is from the same orthopoxvirus family as smallpox although thankfully it is much less infectious and less virulent. You may be aware that there was a surge in cases in the UK in 2022. It is thought that the recent resurgence of cases of Mpox worldwide is in part due to the loss of immunity to smallpox. Secondly, the variola virus still exists in two laboratories for research purposes – the CDC in Atlanta Georgia and the VECTOR institute in Russia. I will let you decide whether that makes you feel safer or not.

I must tell you a little bit about the nature of the hepatitis C virus so that you can better understand what follows. HCV is a blood borne virus that damages the liver. The epidemic of hepatitis C in the 20th century has been fuelled by new parenteral routes for blood-to-blood transmission: immunisations, medical treatments, blood transfusions and of course injecting drug misuse which began just over 100 years ago.

Most people are unaware that they have been infected because less than 20% become jaundiced. Yet over 80% of infected individuals fail to clear the virus and develop chronic hepatitis C which over a period of many years can lead on to cirrhosis. After 20 years, 20% of people infected with hepatitis C will have cirrhosis with its serious life-threatening consequences (Figure 2). Therefore, you can see the value in seeking to eliminate such a silent killer.

This quote from Donald Rumsfeld always makes me smile. In 2002, less than 6 months after the horrific 9/11 terrorist attack on America, he said the following in a news conference at the U.S. Department of Defence: ‘… as we know, there are known knowns; there are things we know we know. We also know there are known unknowns; that is to say we know there are some things we do not know. But there are also unknown unknowns—the ones we don’t know we don’t know.’

‘… as we know, there are known knowns; there are things we know we know. We also know there are known unknowns; that is to say we know there are some things we do not know. But there are also unknown unknowns—the ones we don’t know we don’t know.’

The comments led to widespread amusement, ridicule and parody but he was using a well-recognised term, if a little clumsily. Unknown unknowns refer to risks that we don’t know about. We now understand the relevance of this statement to counter terrorism but it applies equally to many other areas and especially to medicine. Hopefully you will soon see the relevance to hepatitis C.

Medical students probably grow tired of being told that in 10 years 90% of what you have been taught will be wrong. But it is remarkable how quickly things change in medicine. The most obvious recent example of this is Covid-19. Until December 2019 there was no knowledge or teaching relating to the nature of this devastating infection. This was truly an ‘unknown unknown’. Yet within 4 months doctors were exchanging emails and WhatsApp’s to learn from other countries about the clinical features and tips for treatment. Our intensive care colleagues learned on the job that nursing patients in the prone position gave a better outcome. Radiologists learned the telltale signs of Covid-19 on CT imaging and physicians noted the quirky changes it caused in routine blood tests. Four years later we have thankfully moved on to a situation where our lives have been transformed by a vaccination program and we rarely have Covid-19 cases in our ICUs. Students and young doctors, you will face massive new challenges that we cannot teach you about – but you should be encouraged by the clear evidence from Covid-19 that the basic training you have received prepares you for these unknown unknowns.

Within hepatology we have not had anything as seismic or newsworthy as Covid-19, but the transformation of hepatitis C across my career has been remarkable – from an unknown condition to one on the verge of elimination. As students we were taught nothing of hepatitis C because it was not identified before I graduated. However, it seems clear that it was not truly unknown prior to 1989. The shadows and footprints of hepatitis C were already very evident for at least a few decades.

I believe I am required to have at least one piece of fine art in my Oration and I do not want to disappoint my colleagues. Epidemics are the rapid spread of a disease to a large number of people within a short period. There are reports of epidemics of jaundice or hepatitis from as far back as China 5000 years ago although early accounts were confused with other illnesses such as malaria. Hippocrates was the first to clearly describe clinical features of epidemic jaundice6. The existence of epidemic hepatitis has been more clearly documented in military campaigns since the 18th century. Defence of the breach at St Jean d’Acre (Figure 3) portrays one of the earliest such examples from the unsuccessful siege by Napoleon of the walled city of Acre. These epidemics seem fairly clearly related to hepatitis A which is transmitted by the faecal-oral route and likely to flourish in battlefield conditions with poor sanitation.

The Siege of Acre in 1799, engraving/print attributed to Francois-Rene Moreaux

But we are not interested in epidemics as that is not the route of transmission for hepatitis C which is blood borne. Serum or post-transfusion hepatitis was suspected for a few centuries but became well established during the second world war. In 1942 there was a major outbreak of hepatitis in the US Navy related to contaminated yellow fever vaccine7. This was one of the earliest clear examples of injections causing hepatitis infection. From the late 1940s it was known that transfusions carried a risk of post transfusion hepatitis and cautions were issued by many including WHO in 1952 who stated that the risk could be reduced by preventative measures. In 1965 there was the landmark discovery of hepatitis B – a virus transmitted through both blood and also bodily fluids such as semen8. Hepatitis B turned out to be the cause of the yellow fever outbreak from contaminated vaccine in 1942. Its discovery led to screening of all blood products in UK by 1972. As a consequence it became clear that patients could acquire a post-transfusion hepatitis from hepatitis B negative blood products – non-A non-B hepatitis. The existence of a non-A, non-B post transfusion hepatitis was confirmed and published in the Lancet in 19749 – we now know the vast majority of such cases were due to hepatitis C. Opportunities to reduce transmission were missed for almost 20 years until 1991 when screening of all blood donations for hepatitis C began (2 years after discovery of hepatitis C)10.

I do not think hepatitis C was an unknown unknown when I was at medical school, more like a known unknown. We knew there was something there. But it was unnamed, had no treatment, no prognosis and no diagnostic test prior to 1989. Those of you at medical school will undoubtedly encounter diseases that were never named while you were at university. In hepatology we now use the term non-ABC hepatitis for acute hepatitis illnesses with no serological diagnosis. The RVH Liver Unit admits several such cases every year. Perhaps in 20 years or less someone in the audience will be standing here giving it a proper name (and hopefully describing a cure).

The Infected Blood Inquiry

I want to move on to the difficult subject of NHS Inquiries.

I know that both those in healthcare and members of the public have been deeply scarred by the findings of inquiries that are very fresh in the memory or indeed ongoing. Inquiry reports are invariably critical. Justice O’Hara who led the hyponatraemia inquiry summarised it well when he said: ‘It is the task of an Inquiry to focus specifically on what has gone wrong, not on what has gone right and such close focus can act as a distorting lens’11.

‘It is the task of an Inquiry to focus specifically on what has gone wrong, not on what has gone right and such close focus can act as a distorting lens’11.

Although we may feel aggrieved by critical conclusions, we need to appreciate that all such inquiries have their origins in patients, relatives or the general public feeling an enormous amount of pain, anger and confusion, and sometimes a need to have someone to blame. We must pause to study these inquiries and learn from them. With that in mind, let us consider The Infected Blood Inquiry which published its report just a few months ago in May 202410. It is a crucial part of the hepatitis C story.

The Inquiry was set up to investigate what Lord Winston described as the ‘worst treatment disaster in the history of the NHS’. Patients received contaminated blood or blood products mostly during the 1970s and 1980s with catastrophic effect. The contaminated blood transmitted HIV, Hepatitis C and to a lesser extent hepatitis B and D. Two main groups were impacted: those with bleeding disorders of whom over 6000 were infected with Hepatitis Cthe general population who were given blood for a variety of reasons, causing almost 27000 to be infected with hepatitis C

It led to more than 3000 deaths and you may wonder how this could have happened. A simple (but inadequate) explanation might be that screening of blood for these infections was not possible. Screening of blood donors for hepatitis C was not introduced in UK until 1991. But there were other factors. A key issue was that the UK was not self-sufficient for blood products and had to import from USA. It was well known that USA used paid donations from high-risk donors (prison populations, iv drug users) and there were widespread recommendations about not using such sources10. As far back as 1952 the WHO stated that the risk of serum hepatitis could be reduced by preventative measures such as donor selection. This was clearly at odds with the policy to use US derived products.

Secondly, there was complacency regarding the risk of non-A, non-B hepatitis. Patients and the public were incorrectly reassured including parliamentary statements despite clear evidence of progressive liver damage from 198012.

Lastly, it is clear that those of us who have been involved in the progressively successful treatment program since the early 2000s are not blameless. We failed to fully comprehend the emotional and psychological trauma caused to patients and families by contaminated blood related infections many years previously. The Infected Blood Inquiry report is an extremely challenging read but I would commend it to everyone present today – the summary is only 6 pages long and I guarantee it will impact and improve your clinical practice.

The Stigma of Hepatitis C

There is a striking contrast between the two main cohorts of patients affected by HCV. In our earliest data for Northern Ireland from the 1990s, we found that roughly half of HCV patients acquired the infection through intravenous drug use or IVDU (Figure 4)13. The majority of the rest came from the group infected through contaminated blood products. Moving forward 25 years over 95% of newly detected cases have intravenous drug use as their main risk factor. Hepatitis C is now inextricably linked with IVDU. You will appreciate that this is a source of great distress for those infected through contaminated blood – to be repeatedly asked if they used IV drugs in the past. Remarkably, I have found over the years that many of our IVDU patients consider hepatitis C to be much more of a stigma than their IV drug use. Patients refer to being ‘dirty’ and this can actually be a motivating factor for chaotic patients to complete HCV treatment. Of course stigma is nothing new in liver disease and the greatest stigma of all for liver patients is alcohol. There is some good reason for being fearful of the label. Thirty years ago a diagnosis of alcohol related liver disease could prevent you from getting an ICU bed. Thankfully those days are long gone. I have deviated from my subject but I hope you will consider this curiosity of why we/the public stigmatise some liver diseases, like hepatitis C, and yet not other lifestyle associated conditions such as cardiovascular disease. We have considered the history of hepatitis C and those most likely to be affected. Now we must turn our attention to how such a disease might be eliminated.

Preventing Hepatitis C

Prevention is better than cure and where better to start than with vaccination, the key contributor to the eradication of smallpox. Alas due to the nature of HCV with its many subtypes and hypervariable regions, very little progress has been made on an effective vaccine. Even the antibodies generated by an infection fail to protect patients from reinfection after successful viral clearance.

The holy grail in preventing HCV transmission in the modern world is to eliminate the scourge of intravenous drug use. Yet events continually conspire against us. Even Covid-19 inadvertently increased the transmission of HCV. Heroin supply chains to Northern Ireland were interrupted and as a consequence intravenous drug usage switched to cocaine which requires multiple injections per day compared to heroin. Multiple opportunities for infection. I am always impressed by the amazing work carried out by our drugs and addictions teams and health inclusion teams across the Province but our data suggests that at best we are in a stalemate and certainly not winning. Unless we overcome the scourge of IVDU, hepatitis C will not be truly eliminated.

If we cannot prevent hepatitis C infections, then what about removing the infected organ. The principle around organ transplantation is to remove a diseased organ and replace it with a healthy one. This is not a practical solution but we must consider it because at one time hepatitis C had a major impact on transplantation. It also allows me to tell you a success story.

By 2004, hepatitis C had become the leading indication for liver transplantation across the UK due to failing cirrhotic livers and drug therapies with a high failure rate15. Projections at that time suggested that by 2020 hepatitis C would account for all available organs for transplantation. Worse still, transplantation did not deliver a good outcome. Recurrent disease in the new liver was the norm, causing rapid progressive damage that was much more aggressive than the original illness. Re-transplantation was often required within 2-3 years. These outcomes have been transformed by modern drug therapies. Contrary to projections in 2004, today in Northern Ireland transplantation is rarely needed for advanced liver disease due to hepatitis C.

Treating hepatitis C

If prevention and transplantation are impractical or impossible solutions, then we must look to curative drug therapies. Figure 7 shows a summary of the amazing progress in drug therapy for hepatitis C genotype 1 (the most common sub type of HCV in UK) over 30 years. The goal of treatment was to achieve long term clearance of the virus also known as a sustained virological response or SVR, meaning that the virus stays away permanently after treatment stops. You could call it a cure if you did not have cirrhosis.

Evolution of drug therapy for hepatitis C genotype 1 in achieving a sustained virological response (SVR)

Interferon monotherapy was actually used for non-A, non-B hepatitis in the late 1980’s before hepatitis C was identified and had been shown to cause some normalisation of LFTs. It became clear that hepatitis C usually relapsed after finishing treatment. The side effects of this subcutaneous injection 3 times per week were quite debilitating, and treatment lasted 6 to 12 months. Worst of all, the SVR was in single figures.

The situation improved in the late 1990s with the addition Ribavirin, an oral antiviral tablet, and there was a substantial step forward in 2002 with the approval of a new once weekly Pegylated Interferon injection alongside daily ribavirin tablets – SVR improved to around 50%. Further increments in SVR occurred with the development of Direct Acting Antiviral tablets or DAAs which were introduced to Northern Ireland in 2011. Nevertheless up until 2015 all treatment was still based around interferon injections with all its associated issues. The dramatic breakthrough came with the release of Sofosbuvir in 2014, a DAA which became the cornerstone of combination tablet-based therapy16. By 2017, we had established treatment for hepatitis C with once daily tablets for 8-12 weeks, minimal side effects and a cure rate of 98% for all. Surely the advent of such potent and safe drug therapies would spell the end for hepatitis C.

It is important to note that the NI commissioners have worked closely with the Regional Liver Unit over the past 15 years to ensure that patients in NI have been given immediate access to the best available treatments for hepatitis C as soon as NICE approves them. They have been one part of a very large team seeking to deliver hepatitis C elimination. In the early 2000’s we realised that hepatitis C patients preferred to engage with our nurse specialists rather than with doctors in a clinic. Our two nurse specialists Karen Patterson and Isabel Stewart helped many patients through difficult times with interferon-based treatments. In more recent years, nurse specialist Orla McCormick has led all patient interface on RVH site and liaison with our treatment partners particularly in NI prison service and addictions services. Prison outreach clinics began in 2017 and the frontline of HCV elimination is now in prison healthcare, drugs and addiction units, health inclusion and homeless services all of whom are delivering treatment to patients. The importance of a team-based approach to HCV elimination was made clear by the Department of Health in the Northern Ireland Elimination Strategy, launched in 202117. Without this multi-faceted and multidisciplinary team, a conventional consultant led hospital-based clinic would fail to reach more than 10% of those we currently treat.

Figure 8 shows the scale of the problem. The number of hepatitis C cases detected in the province each year since 2005 is shown in red (with an estimate for 2024 in yellow) and the number of cases started on treatment each year shown in green. In the decade prior to the launch of tablet-based treatment, only half of those detected actually came through to have treatment. This was partly due to the side effect profile of interferon-based treatment but also due to the chaotic nature of IVDU patients and their inability to engage with conventional services. There was a surge in treatment from 2016 to 2018 due to the introduction of the highly effective tablet based Direct Acting Antiviral (DAA) therapy but then things changed. It became clear that the Regional Liver Unit had exhausted the supply of patients willing to engage with conventional services in a hospital for treatment. The networks became our crucial link to deliver treatment closer to where patients were.

Thankfully, elimination strategies have at last shone a light on the fact that the greater burden of hepatitis C infection is carried by marginalized and vulnerable populations - people who use drugs, those who are incarcerated and the homeless. It is a harsh reality that in our society there is a terrible overlap with these 3 problems. I know that a few of those present today have provided healthcare services within prison. For those of you who have not, experiencing our prisons first hand makes a lasting impression. I am sure you are all familiar with the quote from Dostoevsky that: ‘a society should be judged not by how it treats its outstanding citizens but by how it treats its criminals’.

Perhaps you noticed the recent media outcry about a prisoner release program to create capacity in UK prisons. The thing that caught my eye was Charitable Organisations highlighting that the policy simply resulted in more released prisoners becoming homeless due to flooding the system. Our internal audit data several years ago demonstrated that we lose touch with 90% of prisoners with hepatitis C on their release from prison. To date, we have struggled to resolve the issue of treating the marginalised and vulnerable patients who get hepatitis C, and we as a society must do better.

Before leaving the data in Figure 8, I must explain the upturn in hepatitis C cases in 2021, ironically the same year that we released the Northern Ireland Hepatitis C Elimination Plan. There are two main factors driving the upturn. Firstly, intravenous drug use. I have already mentioned the changes Covid-19 caused in the IVDU community with respect to choice of drug and frequency of injection. In addition, we are now seeing more cases of intravenous drug users re-infecting – see the purple bars in figure 8. A decade ago this was very uncommon but now we are seeing 20-30 cases per year. Even our outreach services are now telling us that they have reached the point our clinics reached several years ago, where they have treated most of the patients who are willing to have treatment. At last count we had over 400 patients on our Liver Unit database with HCV who have declined to attend for treatment.

The second reason for an increase in cases is better detection. Hepatitis C became a notifiable disease in 2021 which has helped. However prior to 2021 we had excellent data on annual HCV incidence through the Regional Virus Laboratory. The more important factor is that our Network and Elimination Strategy has improved detection through more point of care testing in outreach services, especially the prison service. The size of the challenge ahead has become so much clearer, and it is growing!

Conclusion

What do we mean by ‘Eliminating a Disease’? The WHO has been very clever in defining ‘Elimination’. The goal is to eliminate hepatitis C as a public health threat, not to eliminate it completely as was done with smallpox. The overriding targets were to reduce incidence, reduce mortality and seek to treat 80% of those infected3. Perhaps these targets are an acknowledgement that treating everyone with HCV is not possible or practical.

These targets have been updated and whilst we already easily meet the mortality target in Northern Ireland, our current annual incidence (15 per 100,000 population) is more than double the 2030 target. I am confident that we will meet the target to Eliminate Hepatitis C by 2030 using the WHO definitions but we can and should do so much more. Elimination means more than just meeting target numbers.

I have described 4 things that are needed to eliminate hepatitis C:

You need to know the disease. It seems clear that it took us far too long to understand hepatitis C and as the infected blood inquiry showed, much harm was done when warnings of a blood borne hepatitis were not heeded.
