Optimal e-values for testing the mean of a bounded random variable against a composite alternative
Sebastian Arnold, Eugenio Clerico

Abstract
We derive the unique e-values with optimal (relative) growth rate in the worst case for testing the mean of a bounded random variable, hereby contributing with the first application beyond the assumption of mutually absolutely continuous hypotheses of the (RE)GROW quality criteria for e-values originally proposed by Grünwald et al. (2024). For both criteria, we characterise explicitly the alternatives for which it is most difficult to test against, which also admit a meaningful interpretation. We give two important examples of interest where REGROW provides a powerful quality criterion to choose optimal e-variables whereas GROW leads to trivial solutions.



Keywords:
e-variable; optimal growth rate; nonparametric mean-testing; convex envelope.

1
Introduction

E-values are a statistical tool for measuring evidence against a null hypothesis that has attracted increasing interest in recent years as a flexible and safe alternative to p-values; see, e.g.,
4
,
14
, and
9
. Formally, an
e-value
is the observed value (on the data) of an
e-variable
, a non-negative random variable whose expectation is bounded by
1
under the null hypothesis. From this definition, Markov’s inequality ensures that large e-values are unlikely under the null (for any
\alpha\in[0,1]
, the probability that an e-value is larger than
1/\alpha
is at most
\alpha
). As a consequence, the magnitude of an observed e-value directly quantifies statistical evidence against the null hypothesis: the larger an e-value the stronger the evidence in favour of rejection. Thus, by rejecting for sufficiently large values, e-values can be used to obtain valid type-I error control in hypothesis testing problems. Notably, the primary strength of e-values does not lie in the classical testing framework, in which both the data set and the significance level are fixed in advance (and where p-values are often more powerful), but rather in the fact that their validity is preserved under natural operations, such as convex combinations and, under independence, multiplication. This makes e-values a flexible tool for aggregating evidence across multiple experiments, or for adaptive analyses when data come in sequentially over time. For a comprehensive introduction to the theory and use of e-values, we refer to the recent monograph by
10
.

A natural question when testing with e-values is how to choose an appropriate e-variable to use. This choice is typically tackled after specifying an alternative hypothesis: in addition to the null
\mathcal{P}
, we fix a particular alternative hypothesis
\mathcal{Q}
that we consider especially relevant, in the sense that we would like to ensure rejection of
\mathcal{P}
whenever the data are generated from
\mathcal{Q}
. In the classical Neyman-Pearson framework, this leads to selecting a test that, for a fixed significance level, maximises
power
against
\mathcal{Q}
(namely the probability of a rejection under
\mathcal{Q}
) subject to type-I error control. In analogy, we aim to construct valid
\mathcal{P}
-e-variables that are as ‘powerful’ as possible against
\mathcal{Q}
. However, the Neyman-Pearson notion of power is often not the most informative criterion for e-values. Indeed, optimising rejection probability at a fixed significance level
\alpha
ties the construction to that level, thereby discarding information about the magnitude of the evidence, and effectively reduces the output to a binary decision. This is at odds with the interpretation of e-values as quantitative measures of evidence that can be meaningfully accumulated across experiments independent of an accept/reject decision
(
12
)
, and ignores the fact that e-values allow for post-hoc choices of
\alpha

(
3
)
. The classical notion of power is therefore not a suitable criterion for e-values.

When the alternative hypothesis
\mathcal{Q}
is
simple
(namely, it consists of a single distribution
Q
), the most commonly adopted notion of ‘power’ for an e-variable
E
is the
expected growth rate
(or
e-power
)
\mathbb{E}_{Q}[\log E]
, which quantifies the rate at which evidence accumulates under the alternative. Maximising this quantity is well motivated by arguments from gambling theory, information theory, and finance (see, for instance,
4
and
12
, while
6
provides a discussion of alternative utility functions beyond the logarithm). A central result in the theory of e-values states that there exists a (
Q
-almost surely) unique e-variable
E^{\star}_{Q}
maximising
\mathbb{E}_{Q}[\log E]
among all valid e-variables. This optimal e-variable can always be expressed as a generalized likelihood ratio between
Q
and a (sub)probability measure in the effective null, and is referred to as the
growth-rate optimal
(GRO) or
numéraire
e-variable; see
4
and
6
.

Whereas the expected growth rate provides a natural optimality criterion for simple alternatives, its extension to
composite
alternatives (namely families of distributions) is far from straightforward. In this paper, following
4
, we study two possible optimality criteria for e-variables when facing a composite alternative
\mathcal{Q}
:
GROW
(growth rate optimal in the worst case) and
REGROW
(relative growth rate optimal in the worst case).
4
show that
both criteria admit almost surely unique maximisers, referred to as
GROW e-variable
and
REGROW e-variable
, respectively, if we assume that all the measures in both the null
\mathcal{P}
and the alternative
\mathcal{Q}
admit densities with respect to a common reference measure. We refer to this condition as the
absolute continuity assumption
. At present, general existence and characterisation results for GROW and REGROW e-variables are only known under the absolute continuity assumption, and extensions to broader settings of non-dominated hypotheses remain largely unexplored.

In this paper, we consider these criteria for the problem of testing the mean of a bounded real-valued random variable. More precisely, we focus on hypotheses like
\mathcal{P}=\{\text{The mean is ($\leq$) $\mu_{0}$}\}
against
\mathcal{Q}=\{\text{The mean is ($>$) $\mu_{1}$}\}
. Although this pair of hypotheses does not satisfy the absolute continuity assumption, we show that the GROW and REGROW e-variables are nevertheless well defined and admit a simple characterisation. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first result of this kind for non-dominated hypotheses. Existence and characterisation of (RE)GROW e-variables beyond the absolute continuity assumption addresses open problems of current research. Indeed, counter-intuitive examples of e-variables which have infinite expected log-growth, and at the same moment very high probability being strictly smaller than
1
under the alternative (or vice-versa, e-variables with expected log growth
-\infty
, and simultaneously very high probability for being significantly large) show that there are many subtleties in defining
powerful
or
optimal
e-variables and that further research and analysis is needed to fully understand e-power’s advantages and limitations
(
15
)
.

Our analysis builds on recent work by
1
, which shows that the
coin-betting e-class
, a simple subclass of e-variables parametrised by a single scalar parameter, forms the minimal complete e-variable class for the testing problem that we consider. Informally, this implies that every valid e-variable is pointwise dominated by a member of this class. As a consequence, when searching for (RE)GROW e-variables, it is sufficient to restrict attention to the coin-betting e-class. This, together with standard convex analysis tools, allows us to characterise the resulting optimal e-variables analytically.

2
Preliminaries

Notations and definitions.

Let
\mathcal{X}=[a,b]
, for
-\infty<a<b<\infty
, endowed with the standard real topology and its Borel sigma-field. We let
\mathscr{P}
be the set of Borel probability measures on
\mathcal{X}
, and
\mathscr{F}
the family of all Borel measurable functions
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
. For
P\in\mathscr{P}
and
f\in\mathscr{F}
integrable with respect to
P
, we denote by
\mathbb{E}_{P}[f(X)]
(or simply
\mathbb{E}_{P}[f]
) the expectation
\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,\mathrm{d}P(x)
of
f
under
P
. Given a
null hypothesis

\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathscr{P}
, we call
E\in\mathscr{F}
an
e-variable
(with respect to
\mathcal{P}
) if
E\geq 0
, and
\mathbb{E}_{P}[E]\leq 1
for all
P\in\mathcal{P}
. We let
\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{P})\subseteq\mathscr{F}
denote the family of all e-variables with respect to
\mathcal{P}
. We call
P\in\mathscr{P}

absolutely continuous
with respect to a Borel measure
\nu
on
\mathcal{X}
, if
P
admits a density with respect to
\nu
, in which case we write
P\ll\nu
. For
x\in[a,b]
, we let
\delta_{x}
be the Dirac probability measure at
x
.

The absolute continuity (AC) assumption.

Fix two subsets
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
of
\mathscr{P}
, which we will call the
null
and
alternative
hypothesis respectively. If there exists a reference measure
\rho
on
\mathcal{X}
such that each
P\ll\rho
for every
P\in\mathcal{P}
and
Q\ll\rho
for every
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
, we say that the pair
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{Q})
satisfies the
absolute continuity
(AC) assumption. This property often greatly simplifies the theoretical analysis and is therefore commonly assumed in the literature. However, here we will be mostly interested in cases where the AC assumption does not hold.

Convex envelope.

We now introduce a technical tool that will play a key role in our analysis. Given
f\in\mathscr{F}
, we define the c-envelope of
f
as the mapping
f^{c}:\mathcal{X}\to[-\infty,\infty)
given by

f^{c}(x)=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}Q\in\mathscr{P}\,:\\
\mathbb{E}_{Q}[X]=x\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}_{Q}[f]\,,

(1)

where the infimum is restricted to those
Q
for which
f
is integrable. The next lemma gives some useful properties of the c-envelope.

Lemma 1
.

Let
f\in\mathscr{F}
and
f^{c}
be its c-envelope (
1
). Then,
f^{c}
is dominated by
f
, that is
f^{c}\leq f
. If
f
is non-increasing (non-decreasing), then
f^{c}
is non-increasing (non-decreasing). If
f
is convex, then
f^{c}\equiv f
, while if
f
is concave we have that
f^{c}(x)=\frac{b-x}{b-a}f(a)+\frac{x-a}{b-a}f(b)
for all
x
, with the convention
0/0=1
. Moreover, again with the convention
0/0=1
, we have the alternative characterisation

f^{c}(x)=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathcal{X}\\
x_{1}\leq x\leq x_{2}\end{subarray}}\left\{\frac{x_{2}-x}{x_{2}-x_{1}}\,f(x_{1})+\left(1-\frac{x_{2}-x}{x_{2}-x_{1}}\right)f(x_{2})\right\}\,.

(2)

Readers familiar with convex analysis will recognise that
f^{c}
coincides with the convex envelope of
f
(namely, the largest convex function dominated by
f
) of which both the characterisations (
1
) and (
2
), as well as the other properties stated in the lemma, are well known results (see discussion in Chapter 12 of
11
). Yet, our analysis relies only on the facts explicitly stated in
Lemma

1
. For this reason, we avoid invoking additional tools or notions from convex analysis that may be unfamiliar to some reader. A short and self-contained proof of
Lemma

1
is deferred to the appendix.

Testing the mean of a bounded variable.

For fixed
\mu_{0}\in(a,b)
, we consider the null
\mathcal{P}
consisting of all probability measures in
\mathscr{P}
with mean
\mu_{0}
, that is
\mathcal{P}=\{P\in\mathscr{P}\mid\mathbb{E}_{P}[X]=\mu_{0}\}
.

\mathcal{P}
is a
non-parametric
hypothesis, in the sense that it contains discrete, absolutely continuous, and singular continuous measures. For instance, the Dirac measure at
\mu_{0}
, the uniform measure on an open interval contained in
[a,b]
centred at
\mu_{0}
, as well as any convex combination of these two, are all contained in
\mathcal{P}
. In particular,
\mathcal{P}
does not admit a single dominating reference measure, and hence cannot satisfy the AC assumption.

The class of e-variables for
\mathcal{P}
has recently been characterised by
1
, whose results have later been extended by
2
and
5
to more general hypotheses generated by linear constraints. We define

\alpha_{\min}=(\mu_{0}-b)^{-1}<0\,,\quad\alpha_{\max}=(\mu_{0}-a)^{-1}>0\,,\quad\mathrm{and}\quad I_{\mu_{0}}=[\alpha_{\min},\alpha_{\max}]\,.

(3)

It is straightforward to verify that the
coin-betting e-variable

E_{\alpha}\,:\,x\mapsto 1+\alpha(x-\mu_{0})

(4)

is an e-variable for
\mathcal{P}
for any
\alpha\in I_{\mu_{0}}
. We call the set of all e-variables in this form the
coin-betting e-class
.
1
1

1

We refer to
7
for a thorough discussion on why functions in the form (
4
) can be associated with a game of bets on the outcome of a coin (which justifies the names
coin-betting
e-variable and
coin-betting
e-class).
The next result states that these are the only
admissible
e-variables for
\mathcal{P}
, in the sense that any other e-variable is pointwise dominated by a coin-betting e-variable.

Proposition 1

(Corollary of Theorem 1 in
1
)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}=\{P\in\mathscr{P}\mid\mathbb{E}_{P}[X]=\mu_{0}\}
. For any
E\in\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{P})
, there exists
\alpha\in I_{\mu_{0}}
such that
E_{\alpha}(x)\geq E(x)
, for all
x\in\mathcal{X}.

We will also be interested in one-sided mean testing, namely the null hypothesis stating that the mean is only upper bounded by
\mu_{0}
. As a corollary of
Proposition

1
, we can characterise the e-variables for such hypotheses (see the appendix for a proof).

Corollary 1
.

For
\mu_{0}\in(a,b)
, consider

\mathcal{P}=\{P\in\mathscr{P}\mid\mathbb{E}_{P}[X]\leq\mu_{0}\}
.
Then, for every
E\in\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{P})
, there exists
\alpha\in[0,\alpha_{\max}]
such that
E_{\alpha}(x)\geq E(x)
, for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
.

3
GRO, GROW, and REGROW

GRO.

Fix a null hypothesis
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathscr{P}
and assume that the alternative
\mathcal{Q}
is simple, that is
\mathcal{Q}=\{Q\}
for some
Q\in\mathscr{P}
. Given an e-variable
E\in\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{P})
, we define the
e-power
of
E
(under
Q
) as

\mathbb{E}_{Q}[\log E]\,,

where we adopt the convention that this quantity is
-\infty
whenever
\mathbb{E}_{Q}[\max\{0,-\log E\}]=\infty
.
4
showed that under the AC assumption the e-power admits a unique (up to
Q
null-sets) maximiser
E^{\star}_{Q}
, which they called the GRO (growth rate optimal) e-variable for
Q
. Such name is inspired by the fact that the e-power can be interpreted as the expected growth rate of the evidence under repeated independent experiments. They proposed
E^{\star}_{Q}
as the
optimal
e-variable under the alternative
Q
. In a follow-up work,
5
extended the existence and unicity result, showing that the AC assumption is not necessary and that
E^{\star}_{Q}
exists and is well defined under essentially no restriction on
\mathcal{P}
and
Q
. We denote as
\mathrm{GRO}(Q)
the e-power of
E^{\star}_{Q}
,

\mathrm{GRO}(Q)=\sup_{E\in\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{P})}\mathbb{E}_{Q}[\log E]=\mathbb{E}_{Q}[\log E^{\star}_{Q}]\,,

which represents the optimal growth rate achievable under
Q
.

When the alternative
\mathcal{Q}
is not simple and hence consists of several measures (in such case, we speak of
composite alternative
), the problem of how to pick the
best
e-variable becomes more complex. We introduce here the two criteria that were proposed by
4
.

GROW.

The idea behind the growth rate optimal in the
worst
case (GROW) criterion is to consider for each e-variable the
worst-case
e-power
\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\mathbb{E}_{Q}[\log E]
, which leads to define the
worst-case
optimal growth rate as

\mathrm{GROW}(\mathcal{Q})=\sup_{E\in\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{P})}\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\mathbb{E}_{Q}[\log E]\,.

(5)

When a maximiser exists (the first generalisation of Theorem 1 in
4
ensures that, under the AC assumption, this is the case and the maximiser is unique up to
\mathcal{Q}
-null sets), it is called the GROW e-variable under
\mathcal{Q}
, and we denote it as
E^{\mathrm{GW}}_{\mathcal{Q}}
. Existence and uniqueness of
E^{\mathrm{GW}}_{\mathcal{Q}}
in full generality (dropping the AC assumption) remains an open problem; see, e.g., the discussion in
6
. In this paper, we will consider a setting where the AC assumption fails but
E^{\mathrm{GW}}_{\mathcal{Q}}
exists and is well defined.

By definition, the GROW criterion seeks an e-variable that performs well
uniformly
over all alternatives in
\mathcal{Q}
, by maximising the worst case expected growth rate. While this provides a clear and natural optimality principle, it is sometimes too strict and over-pessimistic. Prioritising the least favourable alternative in
\mathcal{Q}
comes at the expense of performance under the other alternative measures in
\mathcal{Q}
, and can push towards a trivial solution that can never reject the null. For a concrete example, consider testing a Bernoulli simple null
\mathcal{P}=\{\mathrm{Ber}(1/2)\}
against the composite alternative
\mathcal{Q}=\{\mathrm{Ber}(q)\mid q>1/2\}
. In this setting, the GROW e-variable is equal to the constant function
1
, which has zero e-power under every
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
; see the example at the end of this section for more details. Although well defined, in this case the GROW criterion does not lead to an informative e-variable.

REGROW.

To address the issue mentioned above,
4
introduced the
relative
growth rate optimal in the worst case (REGROW) criterion. Rather than maximising the worst-case growth rate itself, REGROW compares the e-power of an e-variable to the optimal growth rate achievable, under each single alternative in
\mathcal{Q}
. This leads to the objective

\mathrm{REGROW}(\mathcal{Q})=\sup_{E\in\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{P})}\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\mathbb{E}_{Q}[\log E-\mathrm{GRO}(Q)]\,.

(6)

When a maximiser for
\mathrm{REGROW}(\mathcal{Q})
exists, we call it the REGROW e-variable, and denote it by
E^{\mathrm{RGW}}_{\mathcal{Q}}
. Under the AC assumption,
4
established existence and almost sure uniqueness of the REGROW e-variable.
2
2

2

While restricting to the AC assumption,
4
define the REGROW criterion more generally, with the term
\mathrm{GRO}(Q)
in (
6
) replaced by a generic
offset
function
f:\mathcal{Q}\to\mathbb{R}
of interest. However, since REGROW is mainly motivated and best interpreted for
f(Q)=\mathrm{GRO}(Q)
, we decided to restrict to the definition (
6
).
Without the AC assumption, general existence results remain largely open.

Rather than asking for a guarantee for the e-power holding uniformly under
\mathcal{Q}
, REGROW evaluates performance relative to what is achievable under each alternative:
\mathrm{GRO}(Q)
represents the best possible e-power against
Q
, and REGROW selects an e-variable such that, for every
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
, the e-power is not too far from this benchmark. This comparison ensures that the measures
Q
for which meaningful evidence can be accumulated are not simply discarded because other “worse” alternatives in
\mathcal{Q}
are harder to distinguish from the null. As a result, REGROW does not give up on evidence due to least favourable single alternatives measures, but rather focuses on achieving near optimal performance whenever non-trivial evidence against the null can potentially be obtained.

We now briefly illustrate this with a Bernoulli example, where
E^{\mathrm{GW}}_{\mathcal{Q}}
is powerless while
E^{\mathrm{RGW}}_{\mathcal{Q}}
is non-trivial. Even though the example falls under the AC assumption, it shows a general pattern which we will recognise later on in the paper:
E^{\mathrm{RGW}}_{\mathcal{Q}}
is found by setting an increasing and a decreasing function equal, and is generally not the GRO e-variable for any of the corresponding worst-case distributions.

Example 1

(Testing a Bernoulli)
.

For
fixed
\mu_{0}\in(0,1)
and
\mathcal{P}=\{\mathrm{Ber}(\mu_{0})\}
, we consider the one-sided alternative
\mathcal{Q}_{1}=\{\mathrm{Ber}(\mu)\mid\mu>\mu_{0}\}
and the agnostic alternative
\mathcal{Q}_{2}=\{\mathrm{Ber}(\mu)\mid\mu\neq\mu_{0}\}
. We can identify each e-variable with a vector
Z
in
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{2}
via the canonical embedding
E\mapsto(E(1),E(0))^{\top}
. With this convention, it is easily checked that
\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{P})
consists of all non-negative vectors component-wise dominated by an e-variable of the form
Z_{\varepsilon}=\big(\frac{\varepsilon}{\mu_{0}},\frac{1-\varepsilon}{1-\mu_{0}}\big)^{\top}
, with
\varepsilon\in[0,1]
, and that
Z_{\mu}
is the GRO e-variable for the simple alternative
\{Q_{\mu}\}=\{\mathrm{Ber}(\mu)\}
, for any
\mu\in[0,1]
. Moreover

\mathrm{GRO}(Q_{\mu})=\mathbb{E}_{Q_{\mu}}[\log Z_{\mu}]=\mu\log\frac{\mu}{\mu_{0}}+(1-\mu)\log\frac{1-\mu}{1-\mu_{0}}=\mathrm{kl}(\mu,\mu_{0})\,,

where
\mathrm{kl}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})=\mathrm{KL}(\mathrm{Ber}(\theta)|\mathrm{Ber}(\theta^{\prime}))
, for
\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in[0,1]
.
It follows that the trivial growth rates
\mathrm{GROW}(\mathcal{Q}_{1})=\inf_{\mu>\mu_{0}}\mathrm{GRO}(Q_{\mu})=0=\mathrm{GROW}(\mathcal{Q}_{2})
are achieved by the constant e-variable
E_{\mathcal{Q}_{1}}^{\mathrm{GW}}=E_{\mathcal{Q}_{2}}^{\mathrm{GW}}=Z_{\mu_{0}}=(1,1)^{\top}
. Let us now show that the REGROW criterion provides us with non-trivial e-variables. Fix
\varepsilon\in[0,1]
and
\mu\neq\mu_{0}
. Then,

\mathbb{E}_{Q_{\mu}}[\log Z_{\varepsilon}-\log Z_{\mu}]=\mu\log\frac{\varepsilon}{\mu}+(1-\mu)\log\frac{1-\varepsilon}{1-\mu}=-\mathrm{kl}(\mu,\varepsilon)\,.

By convexity of
\mathrm{kl}
in both its arguments it follows

\displaystyle\mathrm{REGROW}(\mathcal{Q}_{1})

\displaystyle=\sup_{\varepsilon\in[0,1]}\inf_{\mu>\mu_{0}}\big(-\mathrm{kl}(\mu,\varepsilon)\big)=\sup_{\varepsilon\in[0,1]}\min\big\{-\mathrm{kl}(\mu_{0},\varepsilon),-\mathrm{kl}(1,\varepsilon)\big\}\,,

(7)

\displaystyle\mathrm{REGROW}(\mathcal{Q}_{2})

\displaystyle=\sup_{\varepsilon\in[0,1]}\inf_{\mu\neq\mu_{0}}\big(-\mathrm{kl}(\mu,\varepsilon)\big)=\sup_{\varepsilon\in[0,1]}\min\big\{-\mathrm{kl}(0,\varepsilon),-\mathrm{kl}(1,\varepsilon)\big\}\,.

(8)

In both cases, the supremum is achieved by the unique value for which the two terms in the minimum are equal, where uniqueness follows by monotonicity and concavity. For (
8
), we obtain
\varepsilon^{\star}_{2}=1/2
by symmetry, while for (
7
) a direct calculation yields

\varepsilon^{\star}_{1}=\frac{1}{1+(1-\mu_{0})\,\mu_{0}^{\mu_{0}/(1-\mu_{0})}}\,.

This tells us that in general the REGROW e-variables are non-trivial and are given by

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{Q}_{1}}^{\mathrm{RGW}}=Z_{\varepsilon^{\star}_{1}}=\left(\frac{\varepsilon^{\star}_{1}}{\mu_{0}},\frac{1-\varepsilon^{\star}_{1}}{1-\mu_{0}}\right)^{\top}\,,\quad E_{\mathcal{Q}_{2}}^{\mathrm{RGW}}=Z_{1/2}=\left(\frac{1}{2\mu_{0}},\frac{1}{2(1-\mu_{0})}\right)^{\top}\,,

where
E_{\mathcal{Q}_{1}}^{\mathrm{RGW}}
is not the trivial
(1,1)^{\top}
since
\varepsilon^{\star}_{1}>\mu_{0}
, and where
E_{\mathcal{Q}_{2}}^{\mathrm{RGW}}
is not trivial as long as we are not in the symmetric case
\mu_{0}=1/2
.

4
(RE)GROW optimal e-variables for mean testing

In this section, we characterise the (RE)GROW e-variables for testing the mean of a bounded random variable
X\in[a,b]
. First, in
Section

4.1
we consider the case where null and alternative correspond to two given values for the mean:

\mathcal{P}=\{P\in\mathscr{P}\mid\mathbb{E}_{P}[X]=\mu_{0}\}\quad\mathrm{vs.}\quad\mathcal{Q}=\{P\in\mathscr{P}\mid\mathbb{E}_{P}[X]=\mu_{1}\}\,

(9)

for
\mu_{0},\mu_{1}\in(a,b)
.

Section

4.2
deals with the one-sided testing problem, where null and alternative are defined via inequalities:

\tilde{\mathcal{P}}=\{P\in\mathscr{P}\mid\mathbb{E}_{P}[X]\leq\mu_{0}\}\quad\mathrm{vs.}\quad\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}=\{P\in\mathscr{P}\mid\mathbb{E}_{P}[X]>\mu_{1}\}\,,

(10)

for
\mu_{0},\mu_{1}\in(a,b)
with
\mu_{1}\geq\mu_{0}
. For
\mu_{0}=\mu_{1}
, this reminds the first problem studied (
\mathcal{Q}_{1}
) in
Example

1
. Finally, in
Section

4.3
, we focus on the case where the alternative is agnostic, namely consists of everything that is excluded by the null:

\mathcal{P}=\{P\in\mathscr{P}\mid\mathbb{E}_{P}[X]=\mu_{0}\}\quad\mathrm{vs.}\quad\hat{\mathcal{Q}}=\{P\in\mathscr{P}\mid\mathbb{E}_{P}[X]\neq\mu_{0}\}\,,

(11)

for
\mu_{0}\in(a,b)
, which reminds the second problem (
\mathcal{Q}_{2}
) that we studied in
Example

1
.

A key insight is that, from
Proposition

1
and
Corollary

1
, the GROW and REGROW e-variables for the above testing problems can only be (if they exist) coin-betting e-variables of the form (
4
). Therefore, the problem of finding GROW and REGROW e-variables simplifies greatly, and reduces to solving an optimisation in one (GROW) or two (REGROW) dimension(s).

We recall the definitions (
3
):
\alpha_{\min}=(\mu_{0}-b)^{-1}<0
,
\alpha_{\max}=(\mu_{0}-a)^{-1}>0
, and
I_{\mu_{0}}=[\alpha_{\min},\alpha_{\max}]
. Moreover, for
\alpha,\beta\in I_{\mu_{0}}
, we introduce the following functions from
[a,b]
to
[-\infty,\infty)
:

\displaystyle f_{\alpha}(x)

\displaystyle=\log\big(1+\alpha(x-\mu_{0})\big)\,;

\displaystyle F_{\alpha,\beta}(x)

\displaystyle=\log\left(\frac{1+\alpha(x-\mu_{0})}{1+\beta(x-\mu_{0})}\right)\,;

\displaystyle G_{\alpha,\beta}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{b-x}{b-a}\,F_{\alpha,\beta}(a)+\left(1-\frac{b-x}{b-a}\right)F_{\alpha,\beta}(b)\,,

with the conventions
\log 0=-\infty
and
0/0=1
. The function
f_{\alpha}
will be used to analyse GROW,
F_{\alpha,\beta}
will naturally arise when characterising REGROW, while
G_{\alpha,\beta}
is simply the linear interpolation between
F_{\alpha,\beta}(a)
and
F_{\alpha,\beta}(b)
. The following facts are easy to verify:
f_{\alpha}
is strictly concave for every
\alpha\neq 0
(it is simply the constant
0
if
\alpha=0
);
F_{\alpha,\beta}
is strictly increasing if
\alpha>\beta
and strictly decreasing if
\beta>\alpha
(again, the case
\alpha=\beta
is trivial, as
F_{\alpha,\beta}\equiv 0
); and
F_{\alpha,\beta}
is concave for
0\leq\beta\leq\alpha
and
\alpha\leq\beta\leq 0
, and convex for
0\leq\alpha\leq\beta
and
\beta\leq\alpha\leq 0
. The monotonicity of
G_{\alpha,\beta}
clearly follows from the one of
F_{\alpha,\beta}
.

4.1
Testing
\mu_{0}
against
\mu_{1}

The next theorem characterises the GROW and REGROW e-variables for the testing problem (
9
). The proof reveals some important insights discussed later, thus we sketch it in the main text, leaving some technical details to the appendix. Figure
2
illustrates the main ideas behind the proof.

Theorem 1
.

For
\mu_{0},\mu_{1}\in(a,b)
, consider testing the null hypothesis
\mathcal{P}=\{P\in\mathscr{P}\mid\mathbb{E}_{P}[X]=\mu_{0}\}
against the alternative
\mathcal{Q}=\{Q\in\mathscr{P}\mid\mathbb{E}_{Q}[X]=\mu_{1}\}
. Define

\alpha^{\star}_{\mathrm{GW}}=\frac{\mu_{1}-\mu_{0}}{(\mu_{0}-a)(b-\mu_{0})}\,.

(12)

Then,
\alpha^{\star}_{\mathrm{GW}}\in I_{\mu_{0}}
, and the GROW e-variable exists and equals
E^{\mathrm{GW}}_{\mathcal{Q}}:x\mapsto E_{\alpha^{\star}_{\mathrm{GW}}}(x)=1+\alpha^{\star}_{\mathrm{GW}}(x-\mu_{0})
. For
\mu_{1}>\mu_{0}
, there exists a unique
\alpha^{\star}_{\mathrm{RGW}}\in I_{\mu_{0}}
, with
\alpha^{\star}_{\mathrm{RGW}}>\alpha_{\mathrm{GW}}^{\star}>0
, such that

F_{\alpha^{\star}_{\mathrm{RGW}},\alpha_{\max}}(\mu_{1})=G_{\alpha^{\star}_{\mathrm{RGW}},\alpha^{\star}_{\mathrm{GW}}}(\mu_{1})\,.

(13)

Similarly, for
\mu_{1}<\mu_{0}
, there exists a unique
\alpha^{\star}_{\mathrm{RGW}}\in I_{\mu_{0}}
with
\alpha^{\star}_{\mathrm{RGW}}<\alpha_{\mathrm{GW}}^{\star}<0
, that satisfies

F_{\alpha^{\star}_{\mathrm{RGW}},\alpha_{\min}}(\mu_{1})=G_{\alpha^{\star}_{\mathrm{RGW}},\alpha_{\mathrm{GW}}^{\star}}(\mu_{1})
. For
\mu_{1}=\mu_{0}
, we define
\alpha^{\star}_{\mathrm{RGW}}=0
. The REGROW e-variable exists and is given by

E^{\mathrm{RGW}}_{\mathcal{Q}}\,:\,x\mapsto E_{\alpha^{\star}_{\mathrm{RGW}}}(x)=1+\alpha^{\star}_{\mathrm{RGW}}(x-\mu_{0})\,.

Figure 2:
The parameter space
I_{\mu_{0}}^{2}
with the regions where
F_{\alpha,\beta}
is convex (red) or concave (yellow), for
\mu_{1}>\mu_{0}
. By
Lemma

1
,
F^{c}_{\alpha,\beta}\equiv F_{\alpha,\beta}
in the red area and
F^{c}_{\alpha,\beta}\equiv G_{\alpha,\beta}
in the yellow area. For
\alpha>0
, the directional derivatives of the function
\beta\mapsto F^{c}_{\alpha,\beta}(\mu_{1})
are represented as fine arrows. Note that
\beta\mapsto F_{\alpha^{\star}_{\mathrm{RGW}},\beta}(\mu_{1})
has two local minima, achieved at the two black dots in the figure. Formally proving this fact is the main step to show (
15
). In particular, the two black dots are the two unique points simultaneously achieving the sup-inf in (
14
).

Proof.

It is easily checked that
\alpha^{\star}_{\mathrm{GW}}\in I_{\mu_{0}}
. Recalling the definition (
1
) of c-envelope, by
Proposition

1
we have

\mathrm{GROW}(\mathcal{Q})=\sup_{\alpha\in I_{\mu_{0}}}\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\mathbb{E}_{Q}[f_{\alpha}]=\sup_{\alpha\in I_{\mu_{0}}}f_{\alpha}^{c}(\mu_{1})\,.

By
Lemma

1
, the concavity of
f_{\alpha}
implies that
f_{\alpha}^{c}(\mu_{1})=\frac{b-\mu_{1}}{b-a}f_{\alpha}(a)+\frac{\mu_{1}-a}{b-a}f_{\alpha}(b)
. This is a strictly concave differentiable function of
\alpha
, whose maximiser is given by (
12
) (this can be easily derived by setting the derivative equal to zero).

Here we only sketch the proof for REGROW, and defer the technical details for the final step of the proof to the appendix. We only consider the case
\mu_{1}>\mu_{0}
, as
\mu_{1}<\mu_{0}
is essentially analogous, and
\mu_{1}=\mu_{0}
is trivial. For the existence of a unique
\alpha^{\star}_{\mathrm{RGW}}\in(\alpha^{\star}_{\mathrm{GW}},\alpha_{\max})
satisfying (
13
), it is enough to note that
F_{\alpha^{\star}_{\mathrm{GW}},\alpha_{\max}}(\mu_{1})<G_{\alpha^{\star}_{\mathrm{GW}},\alpha^{\star}_{\mathrm{GW}}}(\mu_{1})
,
F_{\alpha_{\max},\alpha_{\max}}(\mu_{1})>G_{\alpha_{\max},\alpha^{\star}_{\mathrm{GW}}}(\mu_{1})
,
\alpha\mapsto F_{\alpha,\alpha_{\max}}(\mu_{1})
is continuous and strictly increasing on
I_{\mu_{0}}
, and
\alpha\mapsto G_{\alpha,\alpha^{\star}_{\mathrm{GW}}}(\mu_{1})
is continuous and strictly decreasing for
\alpha>\alpha^{\star}_{\mathrm{GW}}
.

Proposition
1
implies that
E^{\mathrm{RGW}}_{\mathcal{Q}}
can only be of the form
x\mapsto 1+\alpha^{\star}(x-\mu_{0})
, for some
\alpha^{\star}\in I_{\mu_{0}}
. We need to check that this
\alpha^{\star}
is precisely
\alpha^{\star}_{\mathrm{RGW}}
. Again by
Proposition

1
, we note that, for every
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
,
\mathrm{GRO}(Q)=\sup_{\beta\in I_{\mu_{0}}}\mathbb{E}_{Q}[\log(1+\beta(X-\mu_{0}))]
. By definition (
1
) of c-envelope,

\mathrm{REGROW}(\mathcal{Q})=\sup_{\alpha\in I_{\mu_{0}}}\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\inf_{\beta\in I_{\mu_{0}}}\mathbb{E}_{Q}[F_{\alpha,\beta}]=\sup_{\alpha\in I_{\mu_{0}}}\inf_{\beta\in I_{\mu_{0}}}\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\mathbb{E}_{Q}[F_{\alpha,\beta}]=\sup_{\alpha\in I_{\mu_{0}}}\inf_{\beta\in I_{\mu_{0}}}F^{c}_{\alpha,\beta}(\mu_{1})\,.

(14)

Define
\varphi:I_{\mu_{0}}\to\mathbb{R}
as
\varphi(\alpha)=\inf_{\beta\in I_{\mu_{0}}}F^{c}_{\alpha,\beta}(\mu_{1})
. For
\alpha<\alpha^{\star}_{\mathrm{RGW}}
,

\varphi(\alpha)\leq F_{\alpha,\alpha_{\max}}^{c}(\mu_{1})\leq F_{\alpha,\alpha_{\max}}(\mu_{1})\leq F_{\alpha^{\star}_{\mathrm{RGW}},\alpha_{\max}}(\mu_{1})\,,

where we used
Lemma

1
and the monotonicity of
\alpha\mapsto F_{\alpha,\alpha_{\max}}(\mu_{1})
for the second and third inqualities. For
\alpha>\beta>0
,
F_{\alpha,\beta}
is concave and
F^{c}_{\alpha,\beta}\equiv G_{\alpha,\beta}
by
Lemma

1
. Hence, for
\alpha>\alpha^{\star}_{\mathrm{RGW}}
,

\varphi(\alpha)\leq F_{\alpha,\alpha_{\mathrm{GW}}^{\star}}^{c}(\mu_{1})=G_{\alpha,\alpha_{\mathrm{GW}}^{\star}}(\mu_{1})\leq G_{\alpha^{\star}_{\mathrm{RGW}},\alpha_{\mathrm{GW}}^{\star}}(\mu_{1})=F_{\alpha^{\star}_{\mathrm{RGW}},\alpha_{\max}}(\mu_{1})\,,

where we used the monotonicity of
\alpha\mapsto G_{\alpha,\alpha_{\max}}(\mu_{1})
in the second inequality. Therefore,
F_{\alpha^{\star}_{\mathrm{RGW}},\alpha_{\max}}(\mu_{1})
globally upper bounds
\varphi
. To conclude, it remains to show that this upper bound is actually achieved by
\alpha^{\star}_{\mathrm{RGW}}
, namely

\varphi(\alpha^{\star}_{\mathrm{RGW}})=\inf_{\beta\in I_{\mu_{0}}}F_{\alpha^{\star}_{\mathrm{RGW}},\beta}^{c}(\mu_{1})=F_{\alpha^{\star}_{\mathrm{RGW}},\alpha_{\max}}(\mu_{1})\,.

(15)

The argument for this is simple but slightly lengthy, and is depicted in
Figure

2
. We postpone the details to the appendix.

4.2
Testing for the one-sided hypotheses

Next, we provide explicit results for the GROW and REGROW e-variables for the one-sided testing problem (
10
). Here, we only state the results, deferring the proofs to the appendix as they share many similarities with those of Theorem
1
.

Theorem 2
.

For
\mu_{0},\mu_{1}\in(a,b)
with
\mu_{1}\geq\mu_{0}
, consider testing the null
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}=\{P\in\mathscr{P}\mid\mathbb{E}_{P}[X]\leq\mu_{0}\}
against the alternative
\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}=\{Q\in\mathscr{P}\mid\mathbb{E}_{Q}[X]>\mu_{1}\}
. Let

\alpha^{\star}_{\mathrm{GW}}=\frac{\mu_{1}-\mu_{0}}{(\mu_{0}-a)(b-\mu_{0})}\,.

(16)

Then,
\alpha^{\star}_{\mathrm{GW}}\in(0,\alpha_{\max})
, and the GROW e-variable exists and is
E^{\mathrm{GW}}_{\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}}:x\mapsto E_{\alpha^{\star}_{\mathrm{GW}}}(x)=1+\alpha^{\star}_{\mathrm{GW}}(x-\mu_{0})
.

Moreover, there exists a unique
\tilde{\alpha}^{\star}_{\mathrm{RGW}}\in(0,\alpha_{\max})
with
\tilde{\alpha}^{\star}_{\mathrm{RGW}}>\alpha^{\star}_{\mathrm{GW}}
such that

F_{\tilde{\alpha}^{\star}_{\mathrm{RGW}},\alpha_{\max}}(b)=G_{\tilde{\alpha}^{\star}_{\mathrm{RGW}},\alpha^{\star}_{\mathrm{GW}}}(\mu_{1})\,.

(17)

\tilde{\alpha}^{\star}_{\mathrm{RGW}}
can be expressed in closed form as

\tilde{\alpha}^{\star}_{\mathrm{RGW}}=\frac{(b-\mu_{0})-\frac{(\mu_{0}-a)(b-\mu_{1})}{b-a}r}{(\mu_{0}-a)(b-\mu_{0})\left(1+\frac{b-\mu_{1}}{b-a}r\right)}\,,

(18)

where
r=\big(\frac{\mu_{1}-a}{b-a}\big)^{\frac{\mu_{1}-a}{b-\mu_{1}}}
.

The REGROW e-variable exists and is given by

E^{\mathrm{RGW}}_{\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}}\,:\,x\mapsto E_{\tilde{\alpha}^{\star}_{\mathrm{RGW}}}(x)=1+\tilde{\alpha}^{\star}_{\mathrm{RGW}}(x-\mu_{0})\,.

We remark that for
\mu_{0},\mu_{1}\in(a,b)
with
\mu_{1}<\mu_{0}
, one can show analogously that there exists an unique
\tilde{\alpha}^{\star}_{\mathrm{RGW}}\in(\alpha_{\min},0)
with
\tilde{\alpha}^{\star}_{\mathrm{RGW}}<\alpha^{\star}_{\mathrm{GW}}
such that

F_{\tilde{\alpha}^{\star}_{\mathrm{RGW}},\alpha_{\min}}(a)=G_{\tilde{\alpha}^{\star}_{\mathrm{RGW}},\alpha_{\mathrm{GW}}^{\star}}(\mu_{1})\,

and that the corresponding coin-betting e-variable
E_{\tilde{\alpha}^{\star}_{\mathrm{RGW}}}
is the REGROW e-variable for the symmetric testing problem
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime}=\{P\in\mathscr{P}\mid\mathbb{E}_{P}[X]\geq\mu_{0}\}
vs.
\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}^{\prime}=\{P\in\mathscr{P}\mid\mathbb{E}_{P}[X]<\mu_{1}\}
.

It is worth mentioning that, although when focusing on GROW (
12
) and (
16
) are exactly identical, for REGROW the situation is different, as (
13
) and (
17
) differ in the point where
F_{\tilde{\alpha}^{\star}_{\mathrm{RGW}},\alpha_{\max}}
is evaluated. Although this might seem a minor difference, a key consequence is that
E^{\mathrm{RGW}}_{\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}}
is non trivial for
\mu_{1}=\mu_{0}
for the problem (
10
), while it becomes trivial for (
9
) (cf.
Figure

3
).

4.3
Testing against an agnostic alternative

The next result characterises the GROW and REGROW e-variables for testing the mean without a specific alternative, namely considering as alternative all possible other values of the mean as in (
11
).

Theorem 3
.

For
\mu_{0}\in(a,b)
, consider testing the null hypothesis
\mathcal{P}=\{P\in\mathscr{P}\mid\mathbb{E}_{P}[X]=\mu_{0}\}
against the alternative
\hat{\mathcal{Q}}=\{Q\in\mathscr{P}\mid\mathbb{E}_{Q}[X]\neq\mu_{0}\}
. Then, the GROW e-variable is trivial, namely
E^{\mathrm{GW}}_{\hat{\mathcal{Q}}}\equiv 1
. On the other hand, the REGROW e-variable is given by
E^{\mathrm{RGW}}_{\hat{\mathcal{Q}}}:x\mapsto 1+\hat{\alpha}^{\star}_{\mathrm{RGW}}(x-\mu_{0})
, for

\hat{\alpha}^{\star}_{\mathrm{RGW}}=\frac{\frac{a+b}{2}-\mu_{0}}{(\mu_{0}-a)(b-\mu_{0})}\,.

(19)

Proof.

Recalling the definition (
1
) of c-envelope, by
Proposition

1

\mathrm{GROW}(\hat{\mathcal{Q}})=\sup_{\alpha\in I_{\mu_{0}}}\inf_{\mu\neq\mu_{0}}f_{\alpha}^{c}(\mu)
. For any
\alpha
, by
Lemma

1

f^{c}_{\alpha}
is affine since
f_{\alpha}
is concave, and so
\inf_{\mu\neq\mu_{0}}f_{\alpha}^{c}(\mu)=\min\{f_{\alpha}(a),f_{\alpha}(b)\}\leq 0
. For
\alpha=0
we have
f_{0}^{c}\equiv f_{0}\equiv 0
, so
\mathrm{GROW}(\hat{\mathcal{Q}})=0
, which is achieved by
E^{\mathrm{GW}}_{\hat{\mathcal{Q}}}\equiv 1
.

As for REGROW, again by
Proposition

1
we can write

\mathrm{REGROW}(\hat{\mathcal{Q}})=\sup_{\alpha\in I_{\mu_{0}}}\inf_{\beta\in I_{\mu_{0}}}\inf_{\mu\neq\mu_{0}}F_{\alpha,\beta}^{c}(\mu)\,.

Recalling that
F_{\alpha,\beta}
is non-decreasing for
\alpha\geq\beta
, by
Lemma

1
we have that
\inf_{\mu\neq\mu_{0}}F^{c}_{\alpha,\beta}(\mu)=F^{c}_{\alpha,\beta}(a)=F_{\alpha,\beta}(a)
, whenever
\beta\leq\alpha
(where we used in the last equality that
\delta_{a}
is the only measure in
\mathscr{P}
with mean
a
). Using that
\beta\mapsto F_{\alpha,\beta}(a)
is non-decreasing, we get that
\inf_{\beta\leq\alpha}\inf_{\mu\neq\mu_{0}}F^{c}_{\alpha,\beta}(\mu)=F_{\alpha,\alpha_{\min}}(a)
. With an analogous argument for the case
\beta<\alpha
, we obtain that

\mathrm{REGROW}(\hat{\mathcal{Q}})=\sup_{\alpha\in I_{\mu_{0}}}\min\{F_{\alpha,\alpha_{\min}}(a),F_{\alpha,\alpha_{\max}}(b)\}\,.

(20)

Writing everything explicitly, we have

F_{\alpha,\alpha_{\max}}(b)=\log\left(\frac{\mu_{0}-a}{b-a}\big(1+\alpha(b-\mu_{0})\big)\right)\quad\text{ and }\quad F_{\alpha,\alpha_{\min}}(a)=\log\left(\frac{b-\mu_{0}}{b-a}\big(1+\alpha(a-\mu_{0})\big)\right)\,.

Both these expression are continuous functions of
\alpha
in
I_{\mu_{0}}
,
\alpha\mapsto F_{\alpha,\alpha_{\max}}(b)
is strictly increasing and tends to
-\infty
for
\alpha\to\alpha_{\min}
, while
\alpha\mapsto F_{\alpha,\alpha_{\min}}(a)
is strictly decreasing and tends to
-\infty
for
\alpha\to\alpha_{\max}
. It follows that the supremum in (
20
) is achieved by the unique
\hat{\alpha}^{\star}_{\mathrm{RGW}}\in I_{\mu_{0}}
satisfying
F_{\hat{\alpha}^{\star}_{\mathrm{RGW}},\alpha_{\max}}(b)=F_{\hat{\alpha}^{\star}_{\mathrm{RGW}},\alpha_{\min}}(a)
, which is precisely given by (
19
).
∎

From the proof of
Theorem

3
, we see that any alternative in the form
\hat{\mathcal{Q}}=\{Q\in\mathscr{P}\mid\mathbb{E}_{Q}[X]\in J\}
, where
J\subseteq[a,b]
with
\sup J=b
and
\inf J=a
, would have lead to the same GROW and REGROW e-variables. In particular, this is the case when we test for a minimal effect size
\delta
, that is for alternatives of the form
\hat{\mathcal{Q}}^{\prime}=\{Q\in\mathscr{P}\mid|\mathbb{E}_{Q}[X]-\mu_{0}|>\delta\}
for
\delta>0
such that
\min\{b-\mu_{0},\mu_{0}-a\}>\delta
.

4.4
Worst-case distributions and comparison to the Bernoulli example

Looking at the proofs of our Theorems
1
,
2
, and
3
, we find what are the worst-case distribution in the alternative that achieve the infima over
\mathcal{Q}
in (
5
) and (
6
).

For the testing problem (
9
) studied in
Theorem

1
, the infimum over
\mathcal{Q}
in GROW is achieved by
Q_{1}=q\delta_{a}+(1-q)\delta_{b}
, with
q=(b-\mu_{1})/(b-a)
. Clearly,
E^{\mathrm{GW}}_{\mathcal{Q}}
is the GRO e-variable for the simple alternative
\{Q_{1}\}
. As for REGROW, we see that the infimum is achieved simultaneously by
Q_{1}
and by the measure
Q_{2}=\delta_{\mu_{1}}
. Interestingly, these are the measures with highest and lowest variance in
\mathcal{Q}
. As the worst-case distribution for GROW,
Q_{1}
is the
hardest
alternative to distinguish from
\mathcal{P}
. On the other hand, the deterministic distribution
Q_{2}
constitutes the
easiest
alternative to test against
\mathcal{P}
and thus allows for the most powerful GRO e-variable
E^{\star}_{Q_{2}}=E_{\alpha_{\max}}
over all
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{Q}[\log E^{\star}_{Q}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{Q}[\log(1+\alpha^{\star}_{Q}(X-\mu_{0})]\leq\log\mathbb{E}_{Q}[1+\alpha^{\star}_{Q}(X-\mu_{0})]

\displaystyle=\log[1+\alpha^{\star}_{Q}(\mu_{1}-\mu_{0})]\leq\log[1+\alpha_{\max}(\mu_{1}-\mu_{0})]=\mathbb{E}_{Q_{2}}[\log E^{\star}_{Q_{2}}]\,,

where
\alpha^{\star}_{Q}
denotes the GRO optimal betting parameter for the simple alternative
\{Q\}
. Since REGROW evaluates performance relative to
\mathrm{GRO}(Q)
, alternatives with large
\mathrm{GRO}(Q)
are also those for which a fixed e-variable may incur the largest relative loss, which is why
Q_{2}
emerges as a worst-case distribution for REGROW despite being the easiest in absolute terms.

For the one-sided testing problem (
10
) covered by
Theorem

2
, again the infimum in GROW is “achieved” by
Q_{1}=q\delta_{a}+(1-q)\delta_{b}
, with
q=(b-\mu_{1})/(b-a)
, although this time this is only a
limiting
distribution, in the sense that it is not contained in the alternative
\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}
. Again, for REGROW we have two worst-case distributions: one is
Q_{1}
, while the other is
\delta_{b}
. The reasoning that
\delta_{b}
appears as a second worst-case candidate for REGROW is analogous as above since it allows for the most powerful GRO e-variable amongst all measures in
\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}
.

Finally, for the agnostic alternative (
11
) from
Theorem

3
, the GROW e-variable is powerless, as it is the constant function
1
. It has null e-power against all the measures in the alternative so we cannot speak of a specific
worst
measure for the GROW objective. However, this is not the case for REGROW, where the infimum is achieved by the two measures
\delta_{a}
and
\delta_{b}
. Interestingly, and in contrast to the two other cases, both worst-case distributions do only depend on the boundary of the support of the random variable and not on the specific choice of
\mu_{0}
.

We remark that in all cases the least favourable alternatives are supported on at most two points. In view of the characterisation of the c-envelope in (
2
), this is not surprising. Indeed, for fixed parameters
\alpha
and
\beta
, the map

Q\mapsto\mathbb{E}_{Q}[F_{\alpha,\beta}(X)]

is linear in
Q
. Hence, when minimising over a convex alternative class, the infimum is attained (or approached) at an extreme point of such class. In our case, the alternatives are (unions) of the form
\{P\mid\mathbb{E}_{P}[X]=\mu\}
, and for each of these sets the extreme points are given precisely by the measures supported on at most two points (this is a direct consequence of Carathéodory theorem in dimension
1
).

For Theorems
2
and
3
, the fact that the worst-case distributions for REGROW are supported only on the extremal points
a
and
b
, shows that these corresponds exactly with those that we found in the introductory Bernoulli example, if we set
a=0
and
b=1
. This is also reflected by the fact that (always with
a=0
and
b=1
), the REGROW e-variables satisfy
(E^{\mathrm{RGW}}_{\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}}(1),E^{\mathrm{RGW}}_{\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}}(0))^{\top}=Z_{\varepsilon_{1}^{\star}}
, and
(E^{\mathrm{RGW}}_{\hat{\mathcal{Q}}}(1),E^{\mathrm{RGW}}_{\hat{\mathcal{Q}}}(0))^{\top}=Z_{1/2}
, meaning that the optimal e-variables for the Bernoulli problem are exactly the restriction to
\{0,1\}
of the case
\mathcal{X}=[0,1]
. Notably, this is not the case for the result of
Theorem

1
, as there one of the worst-case distribution is not supported on
\{a,b\}
.

It is also worth highlighting further structural similarity between Theorems
2
and
3
and the Bernoulli example. In all cases, the REGROW e-variable is obtained by equalising the relative growth rates of two worst-case distributions, corresponding respectively to an increasing and a decreasing contribution. Moreover, as already noted in the Bernoulli example, the resulting REGROW e-variables differ from the GRO variables for the respective worst-case distributions. Instead, the REGROW parameter lies strictly between the GRO parameters associated with these two distributions, namely
\alpha^{\star}_{Q_{1}}=\alpha^{\star}_{\mathrm{GW}}<\tilde{\alpha}^{\star}_{\mathrm{RGW}}<\alpha^{\star}_{\delta_{b}}=\alpha_{\max}
and
\alpha^{\star}_{\delta_{a}}=\alpha_{\min}<\hat{\alpha}^{\star}_{\mathrm{RGW}}<\alpha^{\star}_{\delta_{b}}=\alpha_{\max}
, respectively.

5
Discussion

This paper studies the (RE)GROW criteria in a more general setting than originally introduced by
4
. We show that optimal (RE)GROW e-variables exist and can be characterised explicitly in a key example of interest that does not fall under the absolute continuity assumption. For both criteria, we can derive the alternatives for which it is most difficult to test against and show that these worst-case distributions admit a meaningful interpretation. Remarkably, the interpretation of these worst-case candidates, as well as the reasoning underlying the derivation of the (RE)GROW optimal e-variables in this study, align with the much simpler introductory Bernoulli example satisfying the absolute continuity assumption. We hope that our findings shed light on (RE)GROW in full generality and contribute to the current discussion on how to find optimal e-variables for composite alternatives (
15
,
6
).

In
Section

3
, we motivated the REGROW criterion by fact that GROW can be too conservative and lead to powerless e-variables, which aligns with the original motivation given by
4
. However, so far not many explicit examples are known where REGROW is a reasonable criterion whereas GROW is useless, and where the REGROW e-variable can be derived analytically.

4
show that for exponential families with a point null, finding the REGROW e-variable correspond to finding the prior minimizing the worst-case redundancy and that this prior converges to Jeffreys’ prior as the sample size increases.
13
use a simplified version of REGROW in order to find good e-variables for analyzing
2\times 2
contingency tables.
8
study optimal e-variables for testing an invariant parameter of a probabilistic model, and through group invariance they show that in this case the REGROW and GROW e-variables coincide. Importantly, in all of these examples, the absolute continuity assumption is satisfied and the REGROW e-variable is derived in the multi-round setting, where we observe more than one random outcome. Here, we provide simple but relevant examples, where REGROW is a powerful criterion already in the single round setting, whereas GROW does not provide us with a useful criterion; see
Theorem

3
and
Theorem

2
with
\mu_{1}=\mu_{0}
.

The problem of testing for the mean of a bounded random variable has recently been studied by
16
and
7
as a sequential testing problem using (implicitly) e-variables. Their proposed betting algorithms are based on empirical plug-ins of GRO and other criteria
(
16
)
or regret bounds from online-learning theory
(
7
)
, which yield strongly performing confidence sequences (sequences of confidence intervals with a time-unicorn coverage guarantee of the true mean). As in their setting a given (fixed in advance) alternative is not specified, their approach can be interpreted as adaptively learning a “data-matching alternative” as data points are collected over time, ensuring a strategy that performs well against it. By contrast, the optimality criteria studied here are most naturally interpreted in settings where a an alternative is specified a priori (or where one wishes to optimise worst-case performance over such a family). In that case, selecting an e-variable via GRO/GROW/REGROW provides an explicit, alternative-driven notion of optimality, and avoids potential fragility of early plug-in behaviour when only few observations are available. In any case, at the current stage the two frameworks are not directly comparable: the sequential works study multi-round games and optimise long-run accumulation of evidence, whereas our analysis is currently restricted to the single-round (one-shot) mean testing problem.

For future work, it would be of interest to investigate how their proposed betting algorithms compare with approaches based on (RE)GROW optimal betting parameters for the multi-round game (for instance under multi-round agnostic alternatives). Optimality of the coin betting e-class directly extends to the multi round game with
n
outcomes
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
, and optimal e-values are of the form
\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1+\alpha_{i}(x_{i}-\mu_{0}))
; see
1
and the discussion of
5
. However, the optimal betting parameters
\alpha_{i}
’s will generally depend on the previous outcomes
x_{1},\ldots,x_{i-1}
, and thus the optimal e-variables are parametrized by predictable functions rather than simply by one deterministic betting parameter, which makes the problem much less tractable.

Another interesting direction is to investigate whether the existence of (RE)GROW-optimal e-variables extends to other important composite testing problems beyond absolute continuity, and to study optimality criteria beyond log-optimality as for example power utilities recently studied for e-variables by
6
.

Proof.

The fact that
f^{c}
is dominated by

\delta_{x}\in\mathcal{P}

Assume that

in
\mathcal{X}=[a,b]

\lambda=(x_{1}-a)/(x_{2}-a)\in[0,1)

\mathbb{E}_{Q_{2}}[X]=x_{2}
, and define
Q_{1}=\lambda Q_{2}+(1-\lambda)\delta_{a}

Q_{1}\in\mathscr{P}

\mathbb{E}_{Q_{1}}[X]=x_{1}
. We have that
\mathbb{E}_{Q_{1}}[f]=\lambda\mathbb{E}_{Q_{2}}[f]+(1-\lambda)f(a)\leq\mathbb{E}_{Q_{2}}[f]

f(a)=\min f\leq\mathbb{E}_{Q_{2}}[f]
because
f
is non-decreasing. We conclude that
f^{c}(x_{1})\leq f^{c}(x_{2})
.

If
f
is convex, then by Jensen’s inequality we have
f^{c}\geq f
, and so
f^{c}\equiv f
. If
f
is concave, let
\ell\,:\,x\mapsto\frac{b-x}{b-a}f(a)+(1-\frac{b-x}{b-a})f(b)
. Then
f\geq\ell
by concavity, and
\ell(x)\geq f^{c}(x)
for all
x
, since it is realised by the measure
\frac{b-x}{b-a}\delta_{a}+(1-\frac{b-x}{b-a})\delta_{b}
. For any measure
Q
with mean
x

\mathbb{E}_{Q}[f]\geq\mathbb{E}_{Q}[\ell]=\ell(\mathbb{E}_{Q}[X])=\ell(x)
, so
\ell\equiv f^{c}
.

We are left with the final characterisation (
2
). The fact that the RHS is larger than the LHS is obvious, so only the other direction needs to be shown. Fix
x\in\mathcal{X}
. If
f^{c}(x)=f(x)
then this is achieved by (

. So let us assume that

\mathcal{X}
. We can find a measure

, with mean

, such that

. To conclude, it is enough to show that there are

, with
y<x<z
, such as

, where
\theta_{y,z}
is the only probability measure with mean
x
supported on
\{y,z\}
. Without loss of generality, we can assume that
Q
puts no mass on
x
. (If
Q(\{x\})>0
, write
Q=\lambda Q^{\prime}+(1-\lambda)\delta_{x}
with
\lambda\in(0,1)
and
Q^{\prime}(\{x\})=0
. Then
\mathbb{E}_{Q^{\prime}}[X]=x
and
\mathbb{E}_{Q^{\prime}}[f]<\mathbb{E}_{Q}[f]
, so we may replace
Q
by
Q^{\prime}
.) Now, we have that

\displaystyle\int_{z>x}

\displaystyle\int_{y<x}(z-y)\big(\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{y,z}}[f]-\mathbb{E}_{Q}[f]\big)\mathrm{d}Q(y)\mathrm{d}Q(z)

\displaystyle=\int_{z>x}\int_{y<x}\big((z-x)(f(y)-\mathbb{E}_{Q}[f])+(x-y)(f(z)-\mathbb{E}_{Q}[f])\big)\mathrm{d}Q(y)\mathrm{d}Q(z)

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{z>x}(z-x)\mathrm{d}Q(z)\right)\left(\int_{y<x}(f(y)-\mathbb{E}_{Q}[f])\mathrm{d}Q(y)+\int_{z>x}(f(z)-\mathbb{E}_{Q}[f])\mathrm{d}Q(z)\right)

where we used that

. Since we are integrating over a domain where

for every

3

3

To be fully rigorous, one should observe that the double integral manipulation only makes sense if

, which excludes the cases where
