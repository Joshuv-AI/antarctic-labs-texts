Exposure to Air Pollutants and Lewy Body and Parkinson Disease–Related Dementias
Authors: Dimitry S. Davydow, Gregory M. Pontone, Michael S. Okun, Melissa J. Armstrong, Theresa Wimberley Böttger, Camila Geels, Lise Marie Frohn, Jørgen Brandt, Julie Werenberg Dreier, Jakob Christensen, Carsten Bøcker Pedersen, Henriette Thisted Horsdal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionIs exposure to ambient particulate matter with a diameter less than 2.5 µm (PM2.5) and nitrogen dioxide (NO2) associated with increased risk of dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB) and Parkinson disease–related dementia (PDD)?FindingsIn this case-control study including 3024 individuals with DLB and 3808 individuals with PDD matched with 30 240 and 38 080 controls, respectively, greater exposure to ambient PM2.5 or NO2 was associated with significantly increased risk of DLB and PDD. The magnitude of the observed association was stronger for risk of DLB than PDD.MeaningThese findings suggest that exposure to ambient PM2.5 and NO2 may increase risk of DLB and PDD.

Introduction

The incidence and prevalence of α-synucleinopathies, such as Parkinson disease (PD), are increasing.1,2,3,4 PD now represents the fastest increasing neurodegenerative disease worldwide.4 As disease duration and age both increase in individuals with PD, the incidence and prevalence of PD-related dementia (PDD) increase.5 Importantly, PDD or dementia with Lewy body disease (DLB), both α-synucleinopathies, currently represent up to one-quarter of dementia cases worldwide.6 Therefore, understanding the epidemiology of these neurocognitive disorders, including identifying environmental factors, may drive primary prevention.

Toxic environmental exposures may contribute to the development of neurodegenerative diseases.2,7 Notably, air pollutants cross the blood-brain barrier and are associated with an increased neuroimmune response and oxidative stress.8 Furthermore, studies have found significant associations between exposure to ambient particulate matter with a diameter less than 2.5 µm (PM2.5) and increased risks for loss of functional independence in older populations,9 incident PD,10,11 and incident Alzheimer disease (AD).11,12,13

A 2025 study using Medicare claims data identified that long-term exposure to PM2.5 was associated with a 12% increased risk of hospitalization in individuals with DLB and a 17% increased risk of hospitalization in individuals with PDD.14 Using a mouse model, this same study by Zhang et al14 revealed a potential direct neurotoxic effect of PM2.5 possibly mediated by α-synuclein. However, Zhang et al14 did not examine long-term exposure to nitrogen dioxide (NO2), which is primarily emitted from fossil fuel combustion and has been found to be associated with risk of PD and AD,10,13 as a potential risk factor for DLB or PDD.

In this study, we used population-based data from Denmark to examine whether exposures to PM2.5 or NO2 are associated with risk of diagnosis of DLB or PDD. We hypothesized that long-term exposure to either PM2.5 or NO2 would be associated with greater risk of both DLB and PDD, even after adjusting for sociodemographic characteristics and medical and psychiatric comorbidity.

Methods

This case-control study was approved by the Danish Data Protection Agency, and data access was granted by the Danish Health Data Authority and Statistics Denmark. In accordance with Danish legislation, the requirements for informed consent were waived since the study was based exclusively on registry data. The University of Florida institutional review board waived the need for approval after reviewing the study protocol because the study was based on administrative data obtained from Denmark and the study had been approved by the relevant Danish authorities. We followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline and the Reporting of Observation Studies Conducted using Observational Routinely-Collected Health Data (RECORD) statement.

Study Population, Setting, and Data Sources

We conducted a time-matched nested case-control study using information from Danish nationwide registries (eAppendix 1 in Supplement 1). A case-control design is advantageous for studying conditions with less than 1% prevalence, such as DLB and PDD.15 Our sample was constructed using data from the Danish Civil Registration System16 and included all individuals ages 65 to 95 years living in Denmark from January 1, 2001, through December 31, 2021. In this register, Danish residents are each assigned a unique personal identification number that gives accurate linkage to person-level data from all nationwide registries with virtually no loss to follow-up.17

We identified individuals with DLB (International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, Tenth Revision [ICD-10] code G31.8E) or PDD (ICD-10 code F02.3) by first registered diagnosis by physicians in hospital or secondary care settings using data from the Danish National Patient Register18 and/or the Danish Central Psychiatric Research Register.19 We excluded a total of 122 DLB cases and 374 PDD cases due to age, calendar, and residence restrictions (eFigure in Supplement 1).

For each case, we randomly selected 10 population controls. This 1:10 ratio was chosen to enhance statistical efficiency and improve estimate precision. Controls were born within 14 days of the case, the same sex, alive and residing in Denmark at the exact age at which the case was diagnosed (ie, the index date) and free of DLB or PDD. We created separate case-control datasets for each case definition.

Exposures of Interest

Our exposures of interest were long-term exposure to ambient PM2.5 or NO2. We used longitudinal residential address information including geographical coordinates to link to ambient air pollution modeled across Denmark based on the Danish Eulerian Hemispheric Model (DEHM) and Urban Background Model (UBM) systems. DEHM is a regional model using nesting capabilities, including a mother domain covering the northern hemisphere and 3 nested domains to obtain higher resolution over Europe and Denmark.20 UBM is a local scale model, operating at a 1 km × 1 km spatial resolution and hourly temporal resolution covering Denmark and using output from DEHM as boundary conditions.21,22,23 The model system calculates concentrations of gaseous and particulate ambient air pollution, including all major PM2.5 components, such as ash and dust, elemental carbon, primary emitted organic aerosols and sea salt, secondary formed inorganic aerosols (nitrate, sulfate, and ammonium), as well as secondary formed organic aerosols. We computed exposure to PM2.5 or NO2 as the 10-year time-weighted mean concentrations prior to index date (ie, dementia diagnosis or date of matching for controls), accounting for residential changes (if any) for all cases and controls to ensure a period of accumulated exposure with plausible effects and for consistency with prior relevant research.24,25,26 To ensure complete exposure history for all cases and controls, we only included individuals living in Denmark in the entire 10-year exposure period.

Covariates of Interest

We adjusted for age, sex, and calendar period by design. We obtained information on socioeconomic characteristics from registers at Statistics Denmark (eAppendix 1 in Supplement 1). These characteristics included educational level, defined as the highest level attained (primary through lower secondary, upper secondary or vocational, or higher education); employment status (employed, unemployed, retired, disabled); income (quintiles for the entire population specific to age, sex, and calendar year); and family type (married or registered partnership, cohabiting, single). We also included area-level socioeconomic factors defined for 1885 data zones across Denmark,27 including the proportion of inhabitants with mandatory education only, the proportion of unemployed inhabitants, and the proportion of inhabitants with income in the lowest quartile.

We measured medical comorbidity using the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI),28 excluding dementia (eTable 1 in Supplement 1). Prior psychiatric history was defined based on diagnoses registered in the Central Psychiatric Research Register (excluding dementia diagnoses) or any psychiatric medication redemption as registered in the Danish National Prescription Register29 (eAppendix 2 in Supplement 1). Except for age and calendar period, all other covariates were defined at exposure start (ie, 10 years before the index date) to avoid introducing risk of reverse causation.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed from June 2025 through February 2026. To examine the associations of exposure to PM2.5 or NO2 with risk of DLB or PDD, we used conditional logistic regression models to estimate adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% CIs with each case-control set forming a separate stratum. Due to the individually time-matched study design, the derived aORs approximate incidence rate ratio estimates of DLB or PDD.30 We estimated the associations for a fixed exposure contrast in air pollutants assuming a linear exposure-response association: 5 µg/m3 for PM2.5 and 10 µg/m3 for NO2, based on previous research on the impact of these air pollutants on health outcomes.31,32 We fitted models with 1 air pollutant at a time and for each outcome individually. Initially, we fit a model adjusted for age, sex, and calendar period. Subsequently, we fit sequentially adjusted models, initially controlling for sociodemographic characteristics, then CCI and psychiatric comorbidity, and finally for area-level socioeconomic factors.

We conducted 3 sensitivity analyses. First, we fitted our primary models stratified by sex. Second, we repeated conditional logistic regression models modeling the exposures to PM2.5 or NO2 in quintiles. Third we fitted models including both pollutants.

Statistical significance was set at 2-sided P < .05 for all analyses. Analyses were performed using Stata software version 16.1 (StataCorp).

Results

In analyses adjusted for age, sex, and calendar period, each 5 µg/m3 increase in exposure to PM2.5 was associated with significantly greater risk of DLB (aOR, 5.36; 95% CI, 3.98-7.22) and PDD (aOR, 2.60; 95% CI, 2.07-3.26) (Table 2). Furthermore, each 10 µg/m3 increase in exposure to NO2 was associated with significantly greater risk of DLB (aOR, 2.20; 95% CI, 1.95-2.47) and PDD (aOR, 1.23; 95% CI, 1.12-2.35). After adjusting for sociodemographic characteristics, medical and psychiatric comorbidity, and area-level socioeconomic factors, increased exposure to PM2.5 was associated with nearly 4-fold greater risk of DLB (aOR, 3.70; 95% CI, 2.69-5.10) and more than 2-fold greater risk of PDD (aOR, 2.41; 95% CI, 1.88-3.08), while increased exposure to NO2 was associated with a 95% greater risk of DLB (aOR, 1.95; 95% CI, 1.69-2.27) and a 14% greater risk of PDD (aOR, 1.14; 95% CI, 1.01-1.29).

The nature of the associations between exposure to PM2.5 and NO2 and risk of DLB or PDD remained unchanged in sex-stratified analyses (eTable 2 in Supplement 1). The magnitude of the associations between the air pollutants studied with risk of DLB appeared greater among men than women, while for PDD they appeared greater among women than men.

Exposure to PM2.5 remained significantly associated with risk of DLB or PDD when modeling air pollutants in quintiles (Figure 3; eTable 3 in Supplement 1). In fully adjusted models, the highest quintile of PM2.5 exposure was associated with more than 2-fold greater risk of DLB (aOR, 2.31; 95% CI, 1.86-2.88) and 2-fold greater risk of PDD (aOR, 2.02; 95% CI, 1.62-2.52) compared with the group with the lowest quintile of exposure. The associations of exposure to NO2 with risk of DLB or PDD remained significant when NO2 was modeled in quintiles in fully adjusted models (DLB: aOR, 1.96; 95% CI, 1.67-2.30; PDD: aOR, 1.20; 95% CI, 1.03-1.40).

When included together in the same model, PM2.5 and NO2 were both associated with risk of DLB (PM2.5: aOR, 2.09; 95% CI, 1.41-3.09; NO2: aOR, 1.60; 95% CI, 1.33-1.92). Yet, only exposure to PM2.5 remained associated with increased risk of PDD (PM2.5: aOR, 2.89; 95% CI, 2.15-3.88; NO2: aOR, 0.89, 95% CI, 0.74-0.98). However, there was substantial correlation between both exposures (Spearman ρ coefficient among DLB-controls: 0.76, PDD-controls: 0.77).

Discussion

In this nationwide case-control study of Danish older adults, each 5 µg/m3 increase in exposure to the air pollutant PM2.5 was associated with a nearly 4-fold greater risk of later development of DLB and a more than 2-fold greater risk of later developing PDD. Each 10 µg/m3 increase in exposure to NO2 was associated with a nearly 2-fold greater risk of DLB and a smaller but significantly increased risk of PDD. Among men and women, the nature of the observed associations remained the same, although the associated risk of DLB was stronger among men, while among women, the risk of PDD was stronger. Importantly, when exposure to PM2.5 or to NO2 was analyzed in quintiles, there was a clear exposure-response association between PM2.5 exposure and risks of DLB or PDD and between NO2 exposure and risk of DLB, although not PDD. While exposure to PM2.5 and NO2 were both associated with increased risk of DLB when considered together, only PM2.5 exposure was associated with increased risk of PDD. However, caution is warranted in interpreting that each exposure is associated with increased risk of α-synucleinopathy independent of the other due to the substantial correlation between them. Our results bolster prior work that has found collinearity between PM2.5 and NO2 exposure when examining associations with adverse health outcomes.33 Yet, the associations between exposure to PM2.5 or NO2 with later risks of DLB or PDD were independent of socioeconomic factors at the individual or area-level and medical comorbidity or prior psychiatric history, bolstering prior evidence of a potential direct neurotoxic effect of air pollutants.8

Our study strongly supports the epidemiologic findings of Zhang et al14 on the association of exposures to PM2.5 with risk of the first hospital contact for DLB or PDD. The data on NO2 exposure adds an important wrinkle to the air pollution and neurodegenerative disease story. Exposure to NO2 has been associated with risk for AD and vascular dementia13 and risk of psychosis.34,35 Psychosis is common among patients with α-synucleinopathies,36 and olfactory dysfunction has been associated with both psychosis and α-synucleinopathy.37,38,39 Studies have identified an association of exposure to both ambient NO2 and PM2.5 with olfactory dysfunction.40,41 Therefore, the association of air pollution and olfactory dysfunction is important to consider in the context of α-synucleinopathy risk as the olfactory system may be the gateway for these toxicants to the brain.

The magnitude of the associations between air pollutant exposures and each outcome of interest was more pronounced in this cohort than identified by Zhang et al,14 possibly due to differences in study design. In particular, the observed risk was 2-fold greater for DLB than PDD. Previous studies have uncovered that exposure to PM2.5 has been associated with greater burden of cortical amyloid β plaques,42,43 a feature more common in DLB than PDD.44 Yet, the associations we observed between PM2.5 exposure and risk of DLB were larger in degree than those observed with risk of AD in a previously published study using Danish registry data,13 indicating that additional neuropathological processes could possibly underpin the difference.

Neurodegenerative changes induced by long-term exposure to PM2.5 and NO2 could be due in part to neuroinflammation. Prior work has opined that exposure to air pollutants could lead to reprogramming of microglia triggering excessive, chronic production of cytokines, possibly contributing to neuronal dysfunction or loss.45,46 Inflammation may play a role in the pathogenesis of PD,47,48 suggesting that neuroinflammation could potentially mediate the associations of exposure to ambient PM2.5 and NO2 with risk of DLB and PDD. Further research is needed to determine whether neuronal inflammation is the path by which air pollutant exposure leads to α-synucleinopathy and therefore could be a target for novel therapies.

Beyond neuropathological processes explaining the magnitude of association between exposure to ambient PM2.5 and NO2 with risk of α-synucleinopathies, cumulative exposure to multiple types of pollutants could further underlie our findings. Recent work has identified that pesticide exposure may be associated with increased risk of PD.2,49 Therefore, additional research that examines the cumulative impact of exposure to ambient pollutants and pesticides on risk of α-synucleinopathies is warranted.

The public health impacts of linking exposure to ambient PM2.5 and NO2 and DLB or PDD are considerable. In a 2010 analysis, dementias were estimated to cost the US health care system up to $215 billion annually, with costs estimated to increase by 80% by 2040.50 Furthermore, individuals with DLB may have a considerably worse prognosis compared with idiopathic PD without dementia,51 and there is a greater likelihood of nursing home placement and earlier mortality compared to individuals with AD.52 Moreover, individuals with PDD may be at increased risk for earlier mortality than those with PD but without dementia.53 Our study highlights the important role that environmental exposures play in adverse health outcomes and increasing societal costs.

A strength of this study is reliance on well-established national registries providing comprehensive and systematically collected data with virtually complete coverage and follow-up. The study used complete residential address information linked to a high-resolution air modeling system, capable of calculating air pollution levels at high resolution over decades and enabling fine-grained spatial and temporal estimations of the individual exposures to PM2.5 and NO2.

Limitations

Our study has important limitations. Data on DLB or PDD primarily came from the Danish National Patient Register, resulting in potential underestimation of DLB and PDD due to missing less severe cases, ie, those not associated with a hospitalization or emergency department visit. Additional underestimation of DLB cases could be due to lack of formal diagnostic criteria prior to 1995 and the ICD-10 code for DLB not having been introduced in Denmark before 2008.54,55 Yet, if our study underestimated DLB and PDD cases, the magnitude of the risk associated with long-term exposure to PM2.5 and NO2 identified may also be underestimated. Also, while neither DLB nor PDD diagnoses from the registers used have been validated56 and the code used to identify DLB cases may have lacked specificity due to potential inclusion of PDD cases in the DLB category, this code has been used in other studies,14 and these possible limitations would not have affected our findings substantively. Moreover, only individuals who lived long enough to be diagnosed with DLB or PDD could be included in the study. Furthermore, we were unable to account for history of working in occupations at heightened risk of pollutant exposure. Additionally, we lacked data on lifestyle factors that could increase the risk for dementia.

The generalizability of our findings could be impacted by the air pollutant exposure data being limited to a single country. However, the mean annual concentration of ambient PM2.5 in Denmark is comparable to that of the US.57 Furthermore, a previously published study using Danish registry data identified an association between ambient NO2 exposure and risk of PD in urban areas, although not in rural areas,24 suggesting that the associations highlighted in our study may be greater in countries with more densely populated urban areas. Additionally, while our analyses controlled for a range of individual-level and area-level factors, the possibility of residual confounding remains.

Conclusions

In this case-control study using a population-based sample of Danish older adults, we found that exposures to ambient PM2.5 or NO2 were each associated with increased risks of developing DLB and PDD. The magnitude of the observed associations of exposure to PM2.5 or NO2 with risk of DLB and PDD was increased for both diseases but more so in DLB. Considering the substantial public health impacts attributable to both DLB and PDD, additional research is needed to elucidate the mechanisms underpinning the neurodegenerative processes triggered by air pollution.
