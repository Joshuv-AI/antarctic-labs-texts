Repetitions are not all alike: distinct mechanisms sustain repetition in language models
Matéo Mahaut, Francesca Franzon

Abstract
Text generated by language models (LMs) can degrade into repetitive cycles, where identical word sequences are persistently repeated one after another. Prior research has typically treated repetition as a unitary phenomenon. However, repetitive sequences emerge under diverse tasks and contexts, raising the possibility that it may be driven by multiple underlying factors. Here, we experimentally explore the hypothesis that repetition in LMs can result from distinct mechanisms, reflecting different text generation strategies used by the model. We examine the internal working of LMs under two conditions that prompt repetition: one in which repeated sequences emerge naturally after human-written text, and another where repetition is explicitly induced through an in-context learning (ICL) setup. Our analysis reveals key differences between the two conditions: the model exhibits varying levels of confidence, relies on different attention heads, and shows distinct pattens of change in response to controlled perturbations. These findings suggest that distinct internal mechanisms can interact to drive repetition, with implications for its interpretation and mitigation strategies. More broadly, our results highlight that the same surface behavior in LMs may be sustained by different underlying processes, acting independently or in combination.



1
Introduction

Language models (LMs) have achieved remarkable performance in generating human-like text across a wide range of tasks. Still, their outputs can fall into repetitive patterns, in which identical sequences of tokens are generated over and over in cycles.
Previous research has largely treated repetition as a unitary phenomenon, assuming a single underlying mechanism for its emergence. However, repetition can arise in diverse contexts and across different task settings, suggesting that multiple underlying factors can drive it.

In this work, we propose a framework that views repetition as the outcome of multiple interacting mechanisms. Using a controlled set of prompts, we compare two conditions: one in which repetition follows natural language text, and another where it is induced as in-context learning. Adopting a mechanistic interpretability approach, we analyze the model’s internal working during repetition generations from three complementary angles, considering: (a) model confidence, by examining token selection probabilities (Sec.
3
); (b) the involved circuitry, through analysis of attention head activations (Sec.
4
); and (c) generation strategies, by evaluating the effects of targeted mitigation interventions (Sec.
5
).

Our findings show that:

•

Multiple mechanisms can produce the same behavior. These mechanisms may compete, overlap, or act redundantly.

•

The mechanisms at play interact dynamically during the generation. Attention heads contribute differently across two distinct phases: the initial repetitions and the later cycles.

•

The same output can arise with different confidence. Probability distributions vary by prompt type, which in turn affects mitigation strategies.

1.1
Related work

Repetition is a frequent behavior, occurring in models of various sizes and architectures, including the most recent transformer models
(
Holtzman et al. 2019
;
Ivgi et al. 2024
;
Fu et al. 2021
;
Xi et al. 2021
)
.
It emerges across a wide range of tasks, negatively impacting the quality of generated text in LM applications such as summarization, dialogue generation, and translation
(
Shu et al. 2024
;
Xi et al. 2021
;
Wang et al. 2024
)
.
Previous research has investigated its cause and underlying mechanisms, proposing mitigation strategies accordingly
(
Wang et al. 2024
;
Li et al. 2023
;
Fu et al. 2021
)
.

A common feature of repetition is its tendency to persist once initiated, especially when using
greedy decoding
for token selection
(
Holtzman et al. 2019
;
Li et al. 2023
)
.
Prior work attributes the difficulty in escaping repetition cycles to a self-reinforcing dynamic, noting that the probability of repeating the preceding sequences increases across repetitions
(
Xu et al. 2022
)
. The model would assign high probability to the same token, when it is presented with the same context
(
Xu et al. 2022
;
Yan et al. 2023
)
.
Repetition would thus emerge as a context-based strategy of copying the token, implemented as a learned in-context learning skill
(
Wang et al. 2024
;
Yan et al. 2023
)
.
The skill may be supported by specialized skill neurons, dedicated to the repetitions: their activation increases across repetition cycles, as a signature of their involvement in the generation process
(
Hiraoka & Inui 2024
)
.
Repetitive behavior, or repetition skills, may develop during training as a byproduct of the model optimizing to fit high-probability patterns present in the training data
(
Yan et al. 2023
)
. Although cyclical repetition is not a common trait of natural languages as they are generally experienced by speakers
(
Xu et al. 2022
)
, training corpora often contain large amounts of repetitive text
(
Li et al. 2023
)
, which can influence the probability distributions the model learns from.

Once learned, repetition can serve as a fallback behavior that models adopt when uncertain about the next token to generate; for instance, in situations of epistemic uncertainty during knowledge retrieval tasks
(
Ivgi et al. 2024
)
. Compared to more complex fallback behaviors, such as the hallucinations often observed in larger, more extensively trained models, repetition is more commonly used by smaller models and is interpreted as a simpler strategy to implement.

Building on these observations, several mitigation strategies have been proposed to address repetition.
Most rely on adjustments at the training or decoding level, such as nucleus or top-p sampling to reduce overconfidence
(
Holtzman et al. 2019
)
, or the application of token-level and context-based penalties during sampling
(
Zhu et al. 2023
;
Zhu et al. 2024
;
Xu et al. 2022
;
Song et al. 2024
)
. By reducing the self-reinforcement effects driven by likelihood maximization, these methods have shown effectiveness in mitigating repetition.

1.2
Motivations

While existing studies provide valuable insights into
core properties of repetition,
they often overlook the diversity of conditions that trigger it. Repetition can arise across a range of tasks and linguistic contexts, possibly reflecting different underlying causes. For instance, it may be a response to uncertainty, when the model lacks the knowledge required to select the tokens that solve a task; at the same time, paradoxically, it can also result from overconfidence, as the model assigns disproportionately high probability to tokens that form a repeating sequence despite the output is degenerate.

This complex nature suggests that repetition is not tied to a single failure mode, but rather reflects the specific demands of tasks and contexts and the path that the models cross to solve them.
To test this hypothesis, we design a set of experiments comparing repetitions that emerge under different prompting conditions.
To maximize the contrast between generation strategies, one condition simulates natural text generation, where repetition arises spontaneously; the other induces repetition as a procedural copying behavior, characteristic of in-context learning
(
Wang et al. 2024
;
Yan et al. 2023
;
Hiraoka & Inui 2024
)
.

Experimental setup

Models.
We conduct our experiments on three scales of Pythia: 70m, 1.4B, 6.9B,
(
Biderman et al. 2023
)
, using a smaller scale dataset to fit our computational capacity with the biggest scale (as detailed in the Appendix). Results for Pythia-1.4b are presented in the main text, and results for the other models are included in the Appendix.

Repetition cycles
. We formally define the notion of repetition cycles as used in this work.
A sequence of tokens
\{w_{t}\}
generates cycles of length
n
if there exists an index
T
such that for all
t\geq T
:

w_{t}=w_{t+n},\quad\forall t\geq T

where
\{w_{t}\}
is the sequence of tokens.
T
is the starting point of periodicity.
n
is the
cycle length
. This means that for
t<T
, the sequence may be arbitrary, but from
T
onward, it follows a periodic pattern.

Dataset.

We build our dataset to compare two different types of prompts yielding the same token sequences as output.
We select the prompts using the following procedure: we begin with a
raw set
of 16,000 well-formed human sentences, sampled from a minipile partition of the Pile corpus
(
Kaddour 2023
;
Gao et al. 2020
)
. Each sentence is truncated to the first 50 characters (e.g.,
Prophylactic antibiotics for non-laboring patients
.) and used as input to the model. Continuations are generated using greedy decoding, a strategy known to produce repetitive outputs
(
Holtzman et al. 2019
;
Li et al. 2023
;
Xu et al. 2022
)
.

From the initial set, we keep as
Natural
prompts only those sequences that trigger repetitions: specifically, cycles of more than one token repeated at least twice. If a cycle does not start immediately after the original 50-character extract, we extend the prompt by including the model’s output up to the first token in the cycle.

From the Natural prompts, we build
In-Context Learning (ICL)
prompts. Specifically, we take each of the repeated sequences generated in the natural setup, presenting it twice. It should be noted that those sequences tend to be naturalistic, but might not be complete sentences, for example:
iridium complexes. The ruthenium complexes are more efficient catalysts than the iridium complexes. The ruthenium complexes are more efficient catalysts than the
.

This selection ensures that the repeated sequences are identical in both the Natural and ICL sets. As a result, the two datasets are equal in size, and each corresponding pair produces the same cyclical output. Importantly, the obtained datasets are model-specific, as different models may not generate the same repetitive sequences from identical inputs.

Cycle numbering.
In what follows, we indicate the first repetition of a cycle as
cycle 1
. By
cycle 0
, we indicate the input sequence not including the tokens generated by the model. In the natural prompt case, this is solely the prompt. In the ICL case, we set as cycle 0 a single iteration of the repetitive sequence. Cycle 0 in the ICL setup can therefore be considered as a baseline, it is a sequence that is not sufficient to lead to repetition through ICL, and has not been specifically selected to generate repetitions.

Repetition Rate.
During the selection process, we evaluate the model’s natural tendency to greedily generate repetitive sequences. In the initial
raw set
of 16K sentences from the Pile, the sequences generate repetitions in 82% of cases.
The selected ICL prompts lead to repetition in all corresponding cases. This indicates that while the model responds to the ICL structure, it also exhibits a strong tendency to generate repetitions even in response to natural prompts that should not necessarily elicit them.

Task.
The Natural and ICL prompts are input to the model, which generates up to 300 tokens using greedy decoding.

3
Probability analysis

We aim to assess how confident the model is in token selection, based on the idea that different generation strategies may reflect varying levels of model confidence.

Methods.

We track how next-token probabilities evolve over the first 10 cycles within the 300-token greedy generations. We define the probability of a cycle as the average probability of the tokens generated during one complete iteration of that cycle.

As a measure of the certainty of the model for the selected token, we also compute the entropy of the next-token distribution, calculated over the logits. Entropy for a cycle is calculated by averaging the entropy values across all tokens within the cycle. Lower entropy, reflecting a sharper probability distribution on the selected token indicates higher confidence in the model predictions, while higher entropy suggests greater uncertainty.

Results.
Consistently with previous research, we find a self-reinforcing effect of repetition
(
Xu et al. 2022
)
, with probabilities increasing for both natural (orange line with cross markers in Fig
1
, left) and ICL settings (blue line with circle markers), across repetition cycles. The narrow confidence intervals (plotted as the barely visible shaded areas) across all conditions highlight the robustness and consistency of this effect.
Notably, while the exact same output is generated for both datasets, they do not share the same probability distributions over tokens, more evidently with generations involving less cycles.

Probabilities are much lower initially for the ICL setup, leading to a much stronger increase in the initial cycles.
Similarly, the entropy variations plotted in Fig
1
(right) follow a similar pattern: in both cases, entropy converges to low values, with a rapid drop following the second repetition cycle in the ICL case.

In the natural setting, the high probability and low entropy indicate that the model appears to directly generate the cyclical sequence with high confidence. In contrast, in the ICL setting, the model initially exhibits low confidence, which gradually increases over time.
Despite selecting identical output token sequences, the variations in probability and entropy indicate that the model processes the two types of prompts differently
.

4
Attention analysis

The differences observed in token probabilities suggest that there are two processes at play when generating repeating sequences. We assess whether these are implemented through different circuitry: specifically, we focus our analysis on attention heads, which are known to play a key role in performing operations of pattern matching, such as in-context repetition
(
Olsson et al. 2022
;
Crosbie & Shutova 2024
;
Zheng et al. 2024
)
.

Method.
To understand the contribution of each attention head to repetitions, we map each head to the model’s output space using an affine transformation, and the model’s unembedding matrix
(
Belrose et al. 2023
)
. Effectively, we’re looking at what model output token can be predicted from a given attention head probability distribution. We look at this for the first eight cycles generated after each prompt.

If
k
is the number of tokens of a sequence, and each layer
l
has
n
attention heads with input and output dimension of
d
, then the update made to the residual stream by an attention head
a
can be noted:

A^{l,n}=a^{l,n}\left(\text{norm}(x^{l-1})\right)

with
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}
the input from the residual stream, and
norm
layerwise normalization. We train an affine transformation to map each attention head’s output to the unembedding space:

Z^{l,n}=W^{l}_{v}A^{l,n}+b_{\text{vocab}}

with
W_{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
the
trainable weight matrix
mapping the hidden representation to the unembedding space and
b_{\text{vocab}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
the
bias term
added to each logit.

We use the contrastive method from
Ortu et al. 2024
to highlight the contribution of each head to the probability of outputting the next token in a given cycle, rather than the next most likely, non-cyclical token.
In practice we subtract the logits of cycle token
ctok
from the most likely token that is not
ctok
. This next most likely token,
ntok
, corresponds to the token most likely to interrupt the cycle:

contrast=softmax(Z^{l,n}W_{u}^{(ctok)}-Z^{l,n}W_{u}^{(ntok)})

with
W_{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times V}
the unembedding matrix and
V
the vocabulary size.

Positive values of this contrast mean the attention head favors the token that continues the cycle, and negative values mean that it favors another token, that is not in the cycle.

Training.
As in
Belrose et al. 2023
, we find the parameters of the affine transformation using stochastic gradient descent to minimize KL divergence between an attention head’s distribution and the distribution of the final layer, right before the unembedding matrix. We perform this search on a 10k set extracted from minipile
(
Gao et al. 2020
;
Kaddour 2023
)
. To avoid unwanted data contamination, we do not use those 10k samples again in later experiments. We use the final
k
th token to represent the entire sequence.

The contribution of the attention heads to the repetition suggests that two mechanisms come into play. A first, shared mechanism, which activates with both datasets, and a second mechanism, specific to each condition: while in the ICL condition, some attention heads contribute more to the non-cyclical behavior, in the natural prompt setting most heads contribute to some degree to the cyclical behavior, and none significantly to the non-cyclical behavior.

The shared mechanism involves the 4th head of layer 4 (noted L4H4) represented with green squares in Figure

, and L9H9 represented with brown vertical stars. Those two heads activate in both conditions, to different degrees. L4H4 activates strongly and continuously in the ICL setting, reaching
contrast=0.15

contrast=0.15
by layer 8, when at that point in the ICL case L9H9 contrast is below 0.

While this mechanism is present in both cases, it is delayed in the natural prompt setup (left). Both heads take more time to activate to their maximum, and do so abruptly on cycle nº 2, while showing a relatively small increase between cycles 0 and 1. A possible explanation for delayed activation of the same heads following the same patterns in both setups could be that, once the natural prompt has led to a few repetitions, these repetitions in turn activate the ICL behavior. The delay of 2 cycles for maximum activation of L4H4 and L9H9 corresponds to waiting for the prompt to contain 2 identical sequences, sufficient for ICL. The shared contrastive peak co-occurs with repetitive context.

We then come to mechanisms specific to either setup. Each setup have their specific heads. L8H12 (purple diamonds) is strongly positive for the natural setting, converging around
contrast=0.15
and L10H2 (pink triangles) only activates for ICL, in the same amount. That head never activates above 0.05 in the natural setting, and L8H12 is negative in the ICL setting. When additionally taking into consideration all heads (grey lines in Fig.

), the ICL setting has most heads concentrated barely above 0, i.e., they contribute neither to the cyclical nor to the non-cyclical behavior. A few heads also favour non-cyclical behavior, reaching
contrast=-0.18
. The number and strength of negative value heads increases with each cycle. In the natural prompt setting, the opposite is true. There is less concentration around 0, and all heads contribute to varying degrees to the repetitive behavior, while no head contributes significantly to the non repetitive behavior. This suggests that the repetitive behavior in the natural setting is caused by a distributed effect, with multiple heads contributing.

There is a shared mechanism, delayed in the natural setting, that could be linked to the presence of repetitive context. Additionally, different mechanisms appear in the two settings
.
Strong activation of individual heads, along with diffuse contributions from many heads, characterizes the natural setting. In contrast, the ICL setting is distinguished by the emergence of markedly non-cyclical heads.

5
Effect of sampling based mitigation

To further investigate whether the generation processes differ, we examine how repetitions in natural and ICL settings respond to a mitigation strategy
based on sampling
from varying probability thresholds.

Specifically, we use
top-p sampling
, a method previously employed to reduce repetition
(
Holtzman et al. 2019
)
, and compare how it alters generations across the two conditions.
Our use of top-p sampling is motivated by two key observations. First, although greedy decoding may yield the same repeated tokens, the model’s response to sampling can vary depending on the distribution of next-token probabilities, which differs between the natural and ICL settings (see Figure
1
).
Secondly, prior work suggests that repetition is not only a consequence of decoding strategy, even if decoding plays a role (e.g.,
(
Li et al. 2023
;
Xu et al. 2022
;
Song et al. 2024
;
Ivgi et al. 2024
)
).

Method.
Top-p sampling is a decoding method that mitigates repetition from greedy decoding
(
Holtzman et al. 2019
)
. During generation, tokens are sorted from most likely to least likely, and the model samples from the
n
most likely tokens, that combined together exceed a given probability
p
. Formally,

S_{p}=\left\{w_{i}\mid\sum_{j=1}^{i}P(w_{j}\mid x)\leq p\right\}

where
P(w_{i}\mid x)
is the probability of token
w_{i}
given the context
x
. The tokens in
S_{p}
form the smallest subset whose cumulative probability mass exceeds
p
. Once the set
S_{p}
is determined,
probabilities are normalized
within this subset and a token is sampled from the normalized distribution.
In this setup, setting
p
to 0 is equivalent to greedy decoding, whereas setting
p
to 1 is equivalent to sampling from the entire distribution.

Analysis and Results.

We collect two measures to evaluate how top-p sampling affects generation. First, we assess the persistence of repetitive behavior by measuring the
proportion of repeating cycles
in the output. Note that here, a single repetition is sufficient to count the sequence as repetitive, where earlier we checked for cycles until the end of the sequence. This metric captures how often repetition occurs, regardless of whether the repeated sequences match those produced by greedy decoding.
Second, we quantify the
token overlap
between cycles generated with greedy decoding and those produced with top-p sampling, relying on the ROUGE-L metric, which measures text similarity based on the longest common subsequence between generated and reference text
(
Lin 2004
)
.

6
Discussion and Conclusions

This work explores whether behaviorally identical patterns of repetition can arise from different underlying mechanisms within language models. We find that repetitions generated in response to natural language and to ICL prompts elicit signatures of distinct processes in terms of token probabilities, attention head activations, and sampling decoding strategies.

In the
natural
setting, the model exhibits a peaked probability distribution from the early cycles, indicating high confidence in its token selection. This is accompanied by a diffused and early activation of attention heads across the network. These patterns of confidence and diffused activation may relate to the model’s resistance to top-p sampling, in which repetitive behavior will keep occurring, but the cycles themselves will change. We hypothesize that this robustness arises from contribution of multiple heads to reinforcing token choices. In this setting, the model would base its token selection based on broad contextual information, providing more variation to the sampled token sequences, even when the repetitive behavior remains.

In the
ICL
setting, repetition is marked by sparse activation of a few attention heads. Top-p sampling in this context tends to change the specific repeated tokens but not the overall repetitive pattern. Model confidence is initially low and increases notably around the second cycle, suggesting that the model may accumulate just enough structured context to trigger specific attention heads. This supports the idea that, in the absence of rich contextual cues, the model may default to a procedural strategy—such as copying from prior tokens. Once this procedure is initiated (typically after two cycles), it tends to persist regardless of the token content.
The growing activation of inhibited heads, pushing for non repetitive behavior throughout generation, may reflect a mechanism for suppressing alternative procedures or competing continuations, though their exact role remains uncertain. Still, the overall pattern is consistent with a form of self-reinforcement.
As suggested by previous studies
(
Fu et al. 2021
)
, in the ICL setting, the model would base its token selection by performing an operation based on local contextual information.

The
mechanisms occurring across settings
may become more similar in later cycles. Model certainty converges to high levels and attention head activity either decreases or stabilizes even in the natural condition, possibly reflecting a shift toward a strategy of generation based on local information, as the repetitive context expands.

The involvement of
multiple interacting processes in repetition
may help explain why natural prompts result in fewer repetitions than ICL prompts, and why larger, more extensively trained models tend to repeat less
(
Ivgi et al. 2024
)
. Larger models, as well as natural prompts more broadly, can provide richer epistemic and contextual information -allowing to draw on a wider base of evidence to support token predictions. In contrast, smaller models often lack this capacity and may default to repetition as a more accessible strategy which can be applied in nearly any context, since it relies only on local information.
This interpretation is supported by our model size comparison, presented in the Appendix.
From a practical perspective, these results suggest that effective mitigation requires more than surface-level interventions or treating repetition as a purely context-driven operation. Instead, it calls for a deeper understanding of the underlying mechanisms and their complex interactions.

Our findings indicate that repetition can be supported by at least partially independent mechanisms that interact during generation. This aligns with recent proposals suggesting that model behavior may emerge from the interplay or competition of multiple internal processes
(
Ortu et al. 2024
)
. While previous work has identified circuits within language models that carry out structured, cognitive-like operations
(
Olsson et al. 2022
;
Zheng et al. 2024
;
Yin & Steinhardt 2025
)
, these studies often assume a single, unified process driving task performance. Our results challenge that view—even for a relatively simple behavior like repetition—by showing that
different computational pathways can lead to the same output in LMs
.
Since such behavioral complexity seems more pronounced in larger, more capable models, our findings underscores the importance of considering multiple, potentially overlapping mechanisms when interpreting them through the lens of mechanistic interpretability.

Limitations and future work.
Our study focused on small and medium scale models, as they are more affected by repetition
(
Ivgi et al. 2024
)
; however, future research should explore how these mechanisms function across a wider range of sizes, as well as comparing different architectures. Additionally, further work is needed to understand how repetition behaviors emerge during training, and explain whether they result from strategies put in place by the model during learning due to a beneficial effect on performance
(
Springer et al. 2024
;
Yan et al. 2023
)
or are merely undesirable fallback behaviors.
These questions are key to understand the inner functioning of LMs as well as to develop better mitigation strategies, particularly in setups, such as under-resourced languages, where we cannot rely on large training datasets.

Reproducibility Statement

We ran our analyses on freely and publicly available LMs. The code to reproduce all the experiments will be released upon acceptance of the manuscript.

Ethics Statement

While language models can generate highly realistic text, they may also produce inappropriate outputs. The underlying causes of such behaviors remain unclear due to the opacity of their internal mechanisms.
This study contributes to a deeper understanding of their internal workings, focusing on repetition as a case of undesired yet poorly understood behavior. While not inherently harmful, repetition exemplifies the kinds of model outputs that can emerge unpredictably. Gaining insight into its underlying processes is critical for developing methods to efficiently anticipate and mitigate other potentially unwanted behaviors.

We acknowledge the environmental impact associated with the computationally intensive experiments involving large-scale LMs. To mitigate this, we minimized resource consumption by conducting small-scale pilot studies before scaling up to the full dataset, and by running experiments on larger models using memory-efficient tools and reduced datasets.

A.5
Effects of top-p mitigation.

Through the effects of top-p sampling, ICL-prompted sequences in the Pythia-70M model maintain their repetitive structure, even though the specific tokens generated may differ. Despite this variability, ICL-induced repetitions remain more stable than those prompted by natural text. The similarity in behavior across the two settings in Pythia-70M, also reflected in the attention head patterns, suggests that, in smaller models, the underlying mechanisms driving repetition may be more similar across the prompting condition, compared with bigger models.
In the 6.9b setting, top-p sampling has a similar effect to Pythia-1.4b, with very little impact on proportion of repetitions in the dataset overall, and more changes in the actual tokens being repeated in the natural setting. These patterns replicate the one described in Sec.
5
, although being quantitatively different.
