The Complexity of Symmetric Equilibria in Min-Max Optimization and Team Zero-Sum Games Thanks: The authors are ordered alphabetically.
Ioannis Anagnostides, Ioannis Panageas, Tuomas Sandholm, Jingming Yan

Abstract
We consider the problem of computing stationary points in min-max optimization, with a particular focus on the special case of computing Nash equilibria in (two-)team zero-sum games. We first show that computing ϵ \epsilon -Nash equilibria in 3 3 -player adversarial team games—wherein a team of 2 2 players competes against a single adversary—is CLS -complete, resolving the complexity of Nash equilibria in such settings. Our proof proceeds by reducing from symmetric ϵ \epsilon -Nash equilibria in symmetric , identical-payoff, two-player games, by suitably leveraging the adversarial player so as to enforce symmetry—without disturbing the structure of the game. In particular, the class of instances we construct comprises solely polymatrix games, thereby also settling a question left open by Hollender, Maystre, and Nagarajan (2024). We also provide some further results concerning equilibrium computation in adversarial team games. Moreover, we establish that computing symmetric (first-order) equilibria in symmetric min-max optimization is PPAD -complete, even for quadratic functions. Building on this reduction, we further show that computing symmetric ϵ \epsilon -Nash equilibria in symmetric, 6 6 -player ( 3 3 vs. 3 3 ) team zero-sum games is also PPAD -complete, even for ϵ = poly ​ ( 1 / n ) \epsilon=\text{poly}(1/n) . As an immediate corollary, this precludes the existence of symmetric dynamics—which includes many of the algorithms considered in the literature—converging to stationary points. Finally, we prove that computing a non-symmetric poly ​ ( 1 / n ) \text{poly}(1/n) -equilibrium in symmetric min-max optimization is 𝖥𝖭𝖯 \mathsf{FNP} -hard.

Symmetric min-max optimization

As we have seen, symmetry plays a key role in the proof of
Theorem

1.2
, but that result places no restrictions on whether the equilibrium is symmetric or not—this is indeed the crux of the argument. The next problem we consider concerns computing
symmetric
equilibria in
symmetric
min-max optimization problems, in the following natural sense.

Definition 1.4

(Symmetric min-max optimization)
.

A function
f:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
is called
antisymmetric
if
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{Y}
and

f({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})=-f({\bm{y}},{\bm{x}})\quad\forall({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}.

Furthermore, a point
({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
is called
symmetric
if
{\bm{x}}={\bm{y}}
. The associated min-max optimization problem is called symmetric if the underlying function
f
is
antisymmetric
.

\langle{\bm{x}},\mathbf{A}{\bm{y}}\rangle=-\langle{\bm{y}},\mathbf{A}{\bm{x}}\rangle

({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})
. Symmetric zero-sum games are ubiquitous in the literature and in practical scenarios alike.

The study of symmetric equilibria has a long history in the development of game theory, propelled by Nash’s pathbreaking PhD thesis
[
Nas50
]
(

[

Barring major complexity breakthroughs,

Furthermore, as a byproduct of

Team zero-sum games

Finally, building on the reduction of

1.2

In particular, a

\nicefrac{{1}}{{n^{c}}}

6

Unlike our reduction in

It is interesting to note that the class of polymatrix games we construct to prove

Taken together, our results bring us closer to characterizing the complexity of computing equilibria in min-max optimization.

1.2
Further related work

Adversarial team games have been the subject of much research tracing back to the influential work of
[
vK97
]
, who introduced the concept of a
team maxmin equilibrium (TME)
; a TME can be viewed as the best Nash equilibrium for the team. Notwithstanding its intrinsic appeal, it turns out that computing a TME is
\mathsf{FNP}
-hard
[
Bor+10
]
. Indeed, unlike two-player zero-sum games, team zero-sum games generally exhibit a
duality gap
—characterized in the work of
[
SV17
]
.

This realization has shifted the focus of contemporary research to exploring more permissive solution concepts. One popular such relaxation is
TMECor
, which enables team players to
ex ante
correlate their strategies
[
ZFS23
,
ZAC21
,
Bas+17
,
Car+22
,
Far+18
,
ZA20
,
CG18
]
. Yet, in the context of extensive-form games, computing a TMECor remains intractable;
[
ZFS23
]
provided an exact characterization of its complexity. Team zero-sum games can be thought of as two-player zero-sum games but with
imperfect recall
, and many natural problems immediately become hard without perfect recall (
e.g.
,
[
Tew+23
]
). Parameterized algorithms have been developed for computing a TMECor based on some natural measure of shared information
[
ZFS23
,
Car+22
]
. Beyond adversarial team games,
[
Car+23
]
recently explored
hidden-role
games, wherein there is uncertainty regarding which players belong in the same team, a feature that often manifests itself in popular recreational games—and used certain cryptographic primitives to solve them.

In contrast, this paper focuses on the usual Nash equilibrium concept, being thereby orthogonal to the above line of work. One drawback of Nash equilibria in adversarial team games is that the (worst-case) value of the team can be significantly smaller compared to TME
[
Bas+17
]
. On the other hand,
[
Ana+23
]
showed that
\epsilon
-Nash equilibria in adversarial team games admit an
{\mathsf{FPTAS}}
, which stands in stark contrast to TME, and indeed, Nash equilibria in general games
[
DGP09
,
CDT09
]
. This was further strengthened by
[
Kal+23
,
KYP24
]
for computing
\epsilon
-Nash equilibria in adversarial team
Markov
games—the natural generalization to Markov (aka. stochastic) games.

Related to
Definition

1.1
is the natural notion of a
local
min-max equilibrium
[
DP18
,
DSZ21
]
. It is easy to see that any local min-max equilibrium—with respect to a sufficiently large neighborhood of
({\bm{x}}^{*},{\bm{y}}^{*})
—must satisfy
Definition

1.1

[
DSZ21
]
. Unlike first-order Nash equilibria, local min-max equilibria are not guaranteed to exist.

Finally, another related work we should mention is
[
MVY15
]
, where it was shown that in two-player symmetric games, deciding whether a non-symmetric Nash equilibrium exists or not is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard.

Two-player games

In a two-player game, represented in normal-form game, each player has a finite set, let
[n]
, of actions. Under a pair of actions
(i,j)\in[n]\times[n]
, the utility of the
row
player is given by
\mathbf{R}_{i,j}
, where
\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{Q}^{n\times n}
is the payoff matrix of the row player. Further, we let
\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{Q}^{n\times n}
be the payoff matrix of the column player. Players are allowed to randomize by selecting mixed strategies—points in
\Delta^{n}
. Under a pair of mixed strategies
({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\in\Delta^{n}\times\Delta^{n}
, the expected utility of the players is given by
\langle{\bm{x}},\mathbf{R}{\bm{y}}\rangle
and
\langle{\bm{x}},\mathbf{C}{\bm{y}}\rangle
, respectively. The canonical solution concept in such games is the
Nash equilibrium

[
Nas51
]
, which is recalled below.

Definition 2.1
.

A pair of strategies
({\bm{x}}^{*},{\bm{y}}^{*})
is an
\epsilon
-Nash equilibrium
of
(\mathbf{R},\mathbf{C})
if

\langle{\bm{x}}^{*},\mathbf{R}{\bm{y}}^{*}\rangle\geq\langle{\bm{x}},\mathbf{R}{\bm{y}}^{*}\rangle-\epsilon\quad\text{and}\quad\langle{\bm{x}}^{*},\mathbf{C}{\bm{y}}^{*}\rangle\geq\langle{\bm{x}}^{*},\mathbf{C}{\bm{y}}\rangle-\epsilon\quad\forall({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\in\Delta^{n}\times\Delta^{n}.

Symmetric two-player games

One of our reductions is based on
symmetric
two-player games, meaning that
\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{C}^{\top}
. A basic fact, which goes back to
[
Nas51
]
, is that any symmetric game admits a
symmetric
Nash equilibrium
({\bm{x}}^{*},{\bm{x}}^{*})
. Further, computing a Nash equilibrium in a general game can be readily reduced to computing a symmetric Nash equilibrium in a symmetric game
[
Nis+07
, Theorem 2.4]
. In conjunction with the hardness result of
[
CDT09
]
, we state the following immediate consequence.

Theorem 2.2

(
[
CDT09
]
)
.

Computing a symmetric
\nicefrac{{1}}{{n^{c}}}
-Nash equilibrium in a symmetric two-player game is
\mathsf{PPAD}
-hard for any constant
c>0
.

Team zero-sum games

A (two-)team zero-sum game is a multi-player game—represented in normal form for the purposes of this paper—in which the players’ utilities have a certain structure; namely, we can partition the players into two (disjoint) subsets, such that each player within the same team shares the same utility, whereas players in different teams have opposite utilities—under any possible combination of actions. An
adversarial
team game is a specific type of team zero-sum game wherein one team consists of a single player. As in
Definition

2.1
for two-player games, an
\epsilon
-Nash equilibrium
is a tuple of strategies such that no unilateral deviation yields more than an
\epsilon
additive improvement in the utility of the deviator.

We rely on a recent hardness result of

Theorem 3.1

(
[

]
)

-complete.

Now, let
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{Q}^{n\times n}
be the common payoff matrix of a two-player game, which satisfies
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{A}^{\top}
so that the game is symmetric. Without loss of generality, we will assume that
\mathbf{A}_{i,j}\leq-1
for all

the minimum and maximum entry of

Definition of the adversarial team game

Based on

, we construct a
3

u({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}},{\bm{z}})\coloneqq\langle{\bm{x}},\mathbf{A}{\bm{y}}\rangle+\frac{|\mathbf{A}_{\min}|}{\epsilon}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(z_{i}(x_{i}-y_{i})+z_{n+i}(y_{i}-x_{i})\right)+z_{2n+1}|\mathbf{A}_{\min}|.

(2)

The adversary selects a strategy

and

maintains all of the consequences by suitably adjusting the approximation.)

We begin by stating a simple auxiliary lemma (

contains the proof).

Lemma 3.2
.

Let

be an
\epsilon^{2}
-Nash equilibrium of a normal-form game, and

any action of player

In a so-called

Nash equilibrium, every strategy

would be played with probability
0
, but it will be easy to account for that slack.

The first important lemma establishes that, in equilibrium,
{\bm{x}}\approx{\bm{y}}
. The basic argument proceeds as follows. By construction of the adversarial team game (
2
), the adversary would be able to secure a large payoff whenever there is a coordinate

such that

—by virtue of the second term in (

{\bm{x}}
(or symmetrically Player
{\bm{y}}
) can simply neutralize that term in the adversary’s utility by playing

. We provide the precise, quantitative argument below.

Lemma 3.3

(Equilibrium forces symmetry)
.

Consider an
\epsilon^{2}
-Nash equilibrium

of the adversarial team game (

\epsilon^{2}\leq\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}

\left\|{\bm{x}}^{*}-{\bm{y}}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}\leq 2\epsilon

Proof.

For the sake of contradiction, suppose that

for some
i\in[n]
(the case where
y_{i}^{*}-x_{i}^{*}>2\epsilon
is symmetric, and can be treated analogously). Player
{\bm{z}}
could then choose action

(with probability

\displaystyle u({\bm{x}}^{*},{\bm{y}}^{*},a_{i})

\displaystyle=\langle{\bm{x}}^{*},\mathbf{A}{\bm{y}}^{*}\rangle+\frac{|\mathbf{A}_{\min}|}{\epsilon}\cdot(x^{*}_{i}-y^{*}_{i})>\langle{\bm{x}}^{*},\mathbf{A}{\bm{y}}^{*}\rangle+2|\mathbf{A}_{\min}|,

{\bm{z}}

\displaystyle z^{*}_{2n+1}\leq\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{|\mathbf{A}_{\min}|}\leq\epsilon^{2}.

(3)

\displaystyle u({\bm{x}}^{*},{\bm{y}}^{*},{\bm{z}}^{*})

\displaystyle u({\bm{x}}^{\prime},{\bm{y}}^{*},{\bm{z}}^{*})-u({\bm{x}}^{*},{\bm{y}}^{*},{\bm{z}}^{*})

∎

Having established that
{\bm{x}}\approx{\bm{y}}

Lemma 3.4

(Most probability mass in

(6)

Lemma 3.7

3.3
The complexity of determining uniqueness

A natural question arising from

This section characterizes the complexity of computing symmetric first-order Nash equilibria

(

4.1
Problem definitions and hardness results for symmetric equilibria

Given a continuously differentiable function

, we set

to be

F_{\textrm{GDA}}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\coloneqq\prod_{\mathcal{D}}\left[{\bm{x}}-\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}f({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}),{\bm{y}}+\nabla_{{\bm{y}}}f({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\right]\textrm{ for }({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\in\mathcal{D},

the norm of which measures the fixed-point gap and corresponds to the update rule of GDA with stepsize equal to one; we recall that Player
{\bm{x}}
is the minimizer, while Player
{\bm{y}}
is the maximizer. The domain
\mathcal{D}
is a compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
for some

. Moreover, the projection operator

is applied jointly on
\mathcal{D}
.
2
2

2

This is referred to as the “safe” version of GDA because it ensures that the mapping always lies in
\mathcal{D}
. One could also project independently on
\mathcal{D}({\bm{y}})=\{{\bm{x}}^{\prime}:({\bm{x}}^{\prime},{\bm{y}})\in\mathcal{D}\}
and
\mathcal{D}({\bm{x}})=\{{\bm{y}}^{\prime}:({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}^{\prime})\in\mathcal{D}\}
; see
[
DSZ21
]
for further details and the polynomial equivalence for finding fixed points for both versions.
When

can be expressed as a
Cartesian
product

(and the projection can happen independently), otherwise it is called

.

We begin by introducing the problem of computing fixed points of gradient descent/ascent (GDA) for domains expressed as the Cartesian product of polytopes, modifying the computational problem

•

Proof.

(Complexity for symmetric equilibrium)
.

Proof.

We
P
-time reduce the problem of finding approximate symmetric NE in two-player symmetric games to

(15)

We define the

(16)

Assuming that all entries of

As a result

We assume

Remark 4.4

(Comparison with

The argument of

Theorem 4.5

(
\mathsf{PPAD}
-hardness for coupled domains)
.

The problem
GDAFixedPoint
is
\mathsf{PPAD}
-hard when the domain is a joint polytope, even for quadratic functions.

Proof.

The proof follows similar steps with
Theorem

4.2
, namely, we
P
-time reduce the problem of finding approximate symmetric NE in two-player symmetric games to
GDAFixedPoint
with coupled domains.
Given a two-player symmetric game with payoff matrices
(\mathbf{R},\mathbf{R}^{\top})
of size
n\times n
, we set

\mathbf{A}\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbf{R}+\mathbf{R}^{\top}\right)
,

\mathbf{C}\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{R}^{\top}\right)
and define again the quadratic, antisymmetric function

f({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\langle{\bm{y}},\mathbf{A}{\bm{y}}\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\langle{\bm{x}},\mathbf{A}{\bm{x}}\rangle+\langle{\bm{y}},\mathbf{C}{\bm{x}}\rangle.

Moreover, given a parameter
\delta>0
(to be specified shortly), we define the joint domain of
f
to be

\mathcal{D}:=\left\{({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\in\Delta^{n}\times\Delta^{n}:-\delta\leq x_{i}-y_{i}\leq\delta\textrm{ for all }i\in[n]\right\}.

(joint Domain)

Let
({\bm{x}}^{*},{\bm{y}}^{*})
be an
\epsilon
-approximate fixed point of GDA. We will show that
\left(\frac{{\bm{x}}^{*}+{\bm{y}}^{*}}{2},\frac{{\bm{x}}^{*}+{\bm{y}}^{*}}{2}\right)
is an
O(\epsilon^{1/4})
-approximate (symmetric) NE of the game
(\mathbf{R},\mathbf{R}^{\top})
for an appropriate choice of
\delta.

We set
\mathcal{D}({\bm{x}}^{*})=\{{\bm{y}}:({\bm{x}}^{*},{\bm{y}})\in\mathcal{D}\}
and

\mathcal{D}({\bm{y}}^{*})=\{{\bm{x}}:({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}^{*})\in\mathcal{D}\}.

In words,
\mathcal{D}({\bm{x}}^{*})
and
\mathcal{D}({\bm{y}}^{*})
capture the allowed deviations for
{\bm{y}}
and
{\bm{x}}
respectively. It also holds that
f
is
G
-Lipschitz continuous with
G=4n
and also
4n
-smooth (using the same reasoning as in Theorem
4.2
).

Since
({\bm{x}}^{*},{\bm{y}}^{*})
is an
\epsilon
-approximate fixed point of GDA, using
Lemma

A.3
(
Section

A.2
), the following VIs must hold for some positive constant
K<10
and
n
sufficiently large:

\begin{array}[]{cc}\langle{\bm{x}}-{\bm{x}}^{*},-\mathbf{A}{\bm{x}}^{*}+\mathbf{C}^{\top}{\bm{y}}^{*}\rangle\geq-Kn^{3/2}\sqrt{\epsilon}\textrm{ for any }{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{D}({\bm{y}}^{*})\textrm{ and }\\
\langle{\bm{y}}-{\bm{y}}^{*},\mathbf{A}{\bm{y}}^{*}+\mathbf{C}{\bm{x}}^{*}\rangle\leq Kn^{3/2}\sqrt{\epsilon}\textrm{ for any }{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{D}({\bm{x}}^{*}).\end{array}

(17)

Let
\overline{\mathcal{D}}=\left\{{\bm{z}}\in\Delta^{n}:\left\|{\bm{z}}-\frac{{\bm{x}}^{*}+{\bm{y}}^{*}}{2}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{\delta}{2}\right\}.
By triangle inequality, it follows that

\overline{\mathcal{D}}\subseteq\mathcal{D}({\bm{y}}^{*})\cap\mathcal{D}({\bm{x}}^{*}).

We express the VIs of (
17

and common deviation domain:

\begin{array}[]{cc}\langle{\bm{z}}-{\bm{x}}^{*},-\mathbf{A}{\bm{x}}^{*}+\mathbf{C}^{\top}{\bm{y}}^{*}\rangle\geq-Kn^{3/2}\sqrt{\epsilon}\textrm{ and }\langle{\bm{z}}-{\bm{y}}^{*},\mathbf{A}{\bm{y}}^{*}+\mathbf{C}{\bm{x}}^{*}\rangle\leq Kn^{3/2}\sqrt{\epsilon}\textrm{ for any }{\bm{z}}\in\overline{\mathcal{D}}.\end{array}

Multiplying the first inequality by
-1/2
and the second with
1/2
and adding them up gives

\left\langle{\bm{z}}-\frac{{\bm{x}}^{*}+{\bm{y}}^{*}}{2},(\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{C})\frac{{\bm{x}}^{*}+{\bm{y}}^{*}}{2}\right\rangle\leq\frac{1}{4}\left\langle{\bm{x}}^{*}-{\bm{y}}^{*},\mathbf{A}({\bm{x}}^{*}-{\bm{y}}^{*})\right\rangle+Kn^{3/2}\sqrt{\epsilon}.

(18)

Since
{\bm{x}}^{*},{\bm{y}}^{*}\in\mathcal{D}
, it follows that
\left\langle{\bm{x}}^{*}-{\bm{y}}^{*},\mathbf{A}({\bm{x}}^{*}-{\bm{y}}^{*})\right\rangle\leq n\|{\bm{x}}^{*}-{\bm{y}}^{*}\|^{2}_{2}\leq n^{2}\delta^{2}
.
Combining this fact with (
18
), we conclude that

\left\langle{\bm{z}}-\frac{{\bm{x}}^{*}+{\bm{y}}^{*}}{2},(\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{C})\frac{{\bm{x}}^{*}+{\bm{y}}^{*}}{2}\right\rangle\leq n^{2}\delta^{2}+Kn^{3/2}\sqrt{\epsilon}\textrm{ for any }{\bm{z}}\in\overline{\mathcal{D}}.

(VImedian)

(
VImedian
) shows that by deviating from
\left(\frac{{\bm{x}}^{*}+{\bm{y}}^{*}}{2},\frac{{\bm{x}}^{*}+{\bm{y}}^{*}}{2}\right)
to some
{\bm{z}}
in
\overline{\mathcal{D}}
, the payoff cannot increase by more than
\left(n^{2}\delta^{2}+Kn^{3/2}\sqrt{\epsilon}\right)
in the two-player symmetric game with matrices
(\mathbf{R},\mathbf{R}^{\top})
.

We consider any pure strategy
\bm{e}_{j}
for
j\in[n]
. If
\left\|\bm{e}_{j}-\frac{{\bm{x}}^{*}+{\bm{y}}^{*}}{2}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{\delta}{2}
then

and it is captured by (

). Suppose that

\left\|\bm{e}_{j}-\frac{{\bm{x}}^{*}+{\bm{y}}^{*}}{2}\right\|_{\infty}>\frac{\delta}{2}

on the line segment between

Hardness results for symmetric dynamics

Another interesting consequence of
Theorem

4.2
is that it immediately precludes convergence under a broad class of iterative algorithms in general min-max optimization problems.

Definition 4.6

(Symmetric learning algorithms for min-max)
.

Let
T\in\mathbb{N}
. A deterministic, polynomial-time learning algorithm
\mathcal{A}
proceeds as follows for any time
t\in[T]
. It outputs a strategy as a function of the history
\mathcal{H}^{(t)}
it has observed so far (where
\mathcal{H}^{(1)}\coloneqq\emptyset
), and then receives as feedback
\bm{g}^{(t)}
. It then updates
\mathcal{H}^{(t+1)}\coloneqq(\mathcal{H}^{(t)},\bm{g}^{(t)})
.

A
symmetric
learning algorithm in min-max optimization consists of Player
{\bm{x}}
employing algorithm
\mathcal{A}
with history
\mathcal{H}_{x}^{(t)}\coloneqq(\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}f({\bm{x}}^{(t)},{\bm{y}}^{(t)}))_{t=1}^{T}
, and Player
{\bm{y}}
employing the
same
algorithm with history
\mathcal{H}_{y}^{(t)}\coloneqq(-\nabla_{{\bm{y}}}f({\bm{x}}^{(t)},{\bm{y}}^{(t)}))_{t=1}^{T}
.

(A consequence of the above definition is that both players initialize from the same strategy.) Many natural and well-studied algorithms in min-max optimization adhere to
Definition

4.6
. Besides the obvious example of gradient descent/ascent, we mention extragradient descent(/ascent), optimistic gradient descent(/ascent), and optimistic multiplicative weights—all assumed to be executed simultaneously. A simple non-example is
alternating
gradient descent(/ascent)
[
WTP22
,
BGP20
]
, wherein players do not update their strategies simultaneously.

Theorem 4.7
.

No symmetric learning algorithm (per
Definition

4.6
) can converge to
\epsilon
-first-order Nash equilibria in min-max optimization in polynomial time when
\epsilon=\nicefrac{{1}}{{n^{c}}}
, unless
{\mathsf{PPAD}}={\mathsf{P}}
.

Indeed, this is a direct consequence of our argument in
Theorem

4.2
: under
Definition

4.6
and the min-max optimization problem (
16
), it follows inductively that
{\bm{x}}^{(t)}={\bm{y}}^{(t)}
and
\mathcal{H}_{x}^{(t)}=\mathcal{H}_{y}^{(t)}
for all
t\in[T]
. But
Theorem

4.2
implies that computing a symmetric first-order Nash equilibrium is
{\mathsf{PPAD}}
-hard when
\epsilon=\nicefrac{{1}}{{n^{c}}}
.

Assuming that
{\mathsf{P}}\neq{\mathsf{PPAD}}
,
Theorem

4.7
, and in particular its instantiation in team zero-sum games (
Theorem

1.7
), recovers and significantly generalizes some impossibility results shown by
[
KPV23
]
concerning lack of convergence for certain algorithms, such as optimistic gradient descent(/ascent)—our hardness result goes much further, precluding any algorithm subject to
Definition

4.6
, albeit being conditional.

5
Conclusion and open problems

We have provided a number of new complexity results concerning min-max optimization in general, and team zero-sum games in particular (
cf.

Table

1
). There are many interesting avenues for future research. The complexity of computing first-order Nash equilibria (equivalently, the
GDAFixedPoint
problem) remains wide open, but our hardness results suggest a possible approach: as we have seen, in symmetric min-max optimization, computing
either
symmetric or non-symmetric equilibria is intractable. It would be enough if one could establish this using the same underlying function—that is, somehow combine our two reductions into one. With regard to Nash equilibria in adversarial team games, the main question that remains open concerns the
strong
approximation version of the problem, whereby one requires identifying points that are close—in geometric distance—to exact Nash equilibria. Is there some natural subclass of
FIXP
that characterizes the complexity of that problem? Relatedly,
[
EY10
]
provided strong evidence for the intractability of strong approximation—hovering above
\mathsf{NP}
—through a reduction from the
square root sum
problem; it is currently unclear whether such reductions can carry over using adversarial team games.

Lemma A.4
.

Let
(\hat{{\bm{x}}},\hat{{\bm{x}}})
be an
\epsilon
-well-supported NE of the game where
\hat{{\bm{x}}}

{\bm{x}}^{*}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i\in C_{k}}{\bm{e}}_{i}

Proof.

Since
\hat{{\bm{x}}}
is supported on
C_{k}
, let
i
be a coordinate that
\hat{{\bm{x}}}^{*}
puts the least probability mass on; that is,
i\in\argmin_{j\in C_{k}}\hat{x}_{j}.
Considering the utility of playing action
a_{i}
, we have

\displaystyle u({\bm{e}}_{i},\hat{{\bm{x}}})=\hat{x}_{i}\cdot\delta+(1-\hat{x}_{i})\cdot 1=1-\hat{x}_{i}+\hat{x}_{i}\delta.

Moreover, let
j\in C_{k}
be a coordinate such that
\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{j}\geq\frac{1}{k}
. It should hold that

\displaystyle u({\bm{e}}_{j},\hat{{\bm{x}}})=1-(1-\delta)\hat{x}_{j}\leq 1-\frac{1}{k}+\frac{\delta}{k}.

Since
(\hat{{\bm{x}}},\hat{{\bm{x}}})
is an
\epsilon
-well-supported NE, we have

\displaystyle u({\bm{e}}_{j},\hat{{\bm{x}}})\geq u({\bm{e}}_{i},\hat{{\bm{x}}})-\epsilon

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\quad

\displaystyle 1-\frac{1}{k}+\frac{\delta}{k}+\epsilon\geq 1-\hat{x}_{i}+\hat{x}_{i}\delta

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\quad

\displaystyle\hat{x}_{i}\geq\frac{1}{k}-\frac{\epsilon}{1-\delta}.

Thus, for all coordinates

, we have
\frac{1}{k}-\frac{\epsilon}{1-\delta}\leq\hat{x}_{i}\leq\frac{1}{k}+\frac{\epsilon}{1-\delta}\cdot(k-1).

It then holds that

\displaystyle u(\hat{{\bm{x}}},\hat{{\bm{x}}})

\displaystyle\geq u({\bm{e}}_{i},\hat{{\bm{x}}})-\epsilon

\displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{1}{k}+\frac{\epsilon}{1-\delta}\cdot(k-1)\right)\cdot\delta+\left(1-\frac{1}{k}-\frac{\epsilon}{1-\delta}\cdot(k-1)\right)-\epsilon

\displaystyle\geq 1-\frac{1}{k}+\frac{\delta}{k}-\left(\frac{k-\delta}{1-\delta}\right)\epsilon.

∎

Proof of
Lemma

4.14
.

We will prove that
{\bm{z}}_{j}\leq 9\epsilon
for all
j\in[2n]
; the corresponding claim for Player
\hat{{\bm{z}}}
follows similarly. Fix
i\in[n]
.
Lemma

4.13
shows that
|y_{i}^{*}-x_{i}^{*}|\leq 2\epsilon
. We shall consider two cases.

First, suppose that
|y_{i}^{*}-x_{i}^{*}|\leq\nicefrac{{\epsilon}}{{2}}
. Then,

\displaystyle u({\bm{x}}^{*},{\bm{y}}^{*},{\bm{z}}^{*},\hat{{\bm{x}}}^{*},\hat{{\bm{y}}}^{*},a_{2n+1})-u({\bm{x}}^{*},{\bm{y}}^{*},{\bm{z}}^{*},\hat{{\bm{x}}}^{*},\hat{{\bm{y}}}^{*},a_{i})

\displaystyle\geq|\mathbf{A}_{\min}|-\frac{|\mathbf{A}_{\min}|}{\epsilon}\cdot(x^{*}_{i}-y^{*}_{i})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{|\mathbf{A}_{\min}|}{2}\geq\frac{1}{2}

By
Lemma

3.2
, it follows that
\hat{z}_{i}\leq 2\epsilon^{2}
, and similar reasoning yields
\hat{z}_{n+i}\leq 2\epsilon^{2}
. On the other hand, suppose that
|y_{i}^{*}-x_{i}^{*}|>\nicefrac{{\epsilon}}{{2}}
. Without loss of generality, we can assume that
y_{i}^{*}-x_{i}^{*}\geq 0
; the contrary case is symmetric. Since
{\bm{x}}^{*}\in\Delta^{n}
and
{\bm{y}}^{*}\in\Delta^{n}
, there is some coordinate
j\in[n]
such that
y^{*}_{j}-x^{*}_{j}<0
. As before, by
Lemma

3.2
, it follows that
\hat{z}^{*}_{i}\leq\epsilon^{2}
and
\hat{z}^{*}_{n+j}\leq\epsilon^{2}
. Now, we consider the deviation

\displaystyle\Delta^{n}\ni{\bm{y}}^{\prime}={\bm{y}}^{*}+(x_{i}^{*}-y_{i}^{*}){\bm{e}}_{i}+(y_{i}^{*}-x^{*}_{i}){\bm{e}}_{j}.

Then, we have

\displaystyle u({\bm{x}}^{*},{\bm{y}}^{\prime},{\bm{z}}^{*},\hat{{\bm{x}}}^{*},\hat{{\bm{y}}}^{*},\hat{{\bm{z}}}^{*})-u({\bm{x}}^{*},{\bm{y}}^{*},{\bm{z}}^{*},\hat{{\bm{x}}}^{*},\hat{{\bm{y}}}^{*},\hat{{\bm{z}}}^{*})

\displaystyle=\langle{\bm{x}}^{*},\mathbf{A}({\bm{y}}^{\prime}-{\bm{y}}^{*})\rangle+\frac{|\mathbf{A}_{\min}|}{\epsilon}\left(\hat{z}^{*}_{i}(x^{*}_{i}-y^{\prime}_{i}-(x^{*}_{i}-y^{*}_{i}))+\hat{z}^{*}_{j}(x^{*}_{j}-y^{\prime}_{j}-(x^{*}_{j}-y^{*}_{j}))\right)

\displaystyle\phantom{===============}+\frac{|\mathbf{A}_{\min}|}{\epsilon}\left(\hat{z}^{*}_{n+i}(y^{\prime}_{i}-x^{*}_{i}-(y^{*}_{i}-x^{*}_{i}))+\hat{z}^{*}_{n+j}(y^{\prime}_{j}-x^{*}_{j}-(y^{*}_{j}-x^{*}_{j}))\right)

\displaystyle=\langle{\bm{x}}^{*},\mathbf{A}({\bm{y}}^{\prime}-{\bm{y}}^{*})\rangle+\frac{|\mathbf{A}_{\min}|}{\epsilon}\left(\hat{z}^{*}_{i}(y_{i}^{*}-y^{\prime}_{i})+\hat{z}^{*}_{j}(y^{*}_{j}-y^{\prime}_{j})+\hat{z}^{*}_{n+i}(y^{\prime}_{i}-y^{*}_{i})+\hat{z}^{*}_{n+j}(y^{\prime}_{j}-y^{*}_{j})\right)

\displaystyle\leq 4\epsilon|\mathbf{A}_{\min}|+\frac{|\mathbf{A}_{\min}|}{\epsilon}\left(\epsilon^{2}\cdot 2\epsilon+\hat{z}^{*}_{n+i}\cdot\left(-\frac{\epsilon}{2}\right)+\epsilon^{2}\cdot 2\epsilon\right)

\displaystyle\leq-\left(\frac{1}{2}\hat{z}^{*}_{n+i}-4\epsilon^{2}-4\epsilon\right)|\mathbf{A}_{\min}|.

At the same time, since
({\bm{x}}^{*},{\bm{y}}^{*},{\bm{z}}^{*},\hat{{\bm{x}}}^{*},\hat{{\bm{y}}}^{*},\hat{{\bm{z}}}^{*})
is an
\epsilon^{2}
-Nash equilibrium, we have

\displaystyle u({\bm{x}}^{*},{\bm{y}}^{\prime},{\bm{z}}^{*},\hat{{\bm{x}}}^{*},\hat{{\bm{y}}}^{*},\hat{{\bm{z}}}^{*})-u({\bm{x}}^{*},{\bm{y}}^{*},{\bm{z}}^{*},\hat{{\bm{x}}}^{*},\hat{{\bm{y}}}^{*},\hat{{\bm{z}}}^{*})\geq-\epsilon^{2}.

Thus,

\left(\frac{1}{2}\hat{z}^{*}_{n+i}-4\epsilon^{2}-4\epsilon\right)|\mathbf{A}_{\min}|\leq\epsilon^{2}\implies\hat{z}^{*}_{n+i}\leq 9\epsilon.

We conclude that
\hat{z}^{*}_{i}\leq\epsilon^{2}
and
\hat{z}^{*}_{n+i}\leq 9\epsilon
. The case where
x_{i}^{*}-y_{i}^{*}\geq 0
can be treated similarly.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

1.7
.

Suppose that
({\bm{x}}^{*},{\bm{y}}^{*},{\bm{z}}^{*},\hat{{\bm{x}}}^{*},\hat{{\bm{y}}}^{*},\hat{{\bm{z}}}^{*})
is an
\epsilon^{2}
-Nash equilibrium. We have that for any
{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in\Delta^{n}
,

\langle{\bm{x}}^{*},\mathbf{A}{\bm{y}}^{*}\rangle\leq\langle{\bm{x}}^{*},\mathbf{A}{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\rangle+\frac{|\mathbf{A}_{\min}|}{\epsilon}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{z}^{*}_{i}(y^{*}_{i}-y^{\prime}_{i})+\hat{z}^{*}_{n+i}(y^{\prime}_{i}-y^{*}_{i})\right)+\epsilon^{2}.

(35)

Moreover, considering a deviation from
{\bm{x}}^{*}
to
{\bm{y}}^{\prime}
,

\langle{\bm{x}}^{*},\mathbf{A}{\bm{y}}^{*}\rangle+\langle{\bm{x}}^{*},\mathbf{C}\hat{{\bm{x}}}^{*}\rangle\leq\langle{\bm{y}}^{\prime},\mathbf{A}{\bm{y}}^{*}\rangle+\langle{\bm{y}}^{\prime},\mathbf{C}\hat{{\bm{x}}}^{*}\rangle+\frac{|\mathbf{A}_{\min}|}{\epsilon}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{z}^{*}_{i}(y^{\prime}_{i}-x^{*}_{i})+\hat{z}^{*}_{n+i}(x^{*}_{i}-y^{\prime}_{i})\right)+\epsilon^{2}.

(36)

Summing (
35
) and (
36
),

\displaystyle 2\langle{\bm{x}}^{*},\mathbf{A}{\bm{y}}^{*}\rangle+\langle{\bm{x}}^{*},\mathbf{C}\hat{{\bm{x}}}^{*}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\langle{\bm{y}}^{\prime},\mathbf{A}({\bm{x}}^{*}+{\bm{y}}^{*})\rangle+\langle{\bm{y}}^{\prime},\mathbf{C}\hat{{\bm{x}}}^{*}\rangle+\frac{|\mathbf{A}_{\min}|}{\epsilon}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{z}^{*}_{i}(y^{*}_{i}-x^{*}_{i})+\hat{z}^{*}_{n+i}(x^{*}_{i}-y^{*}_{i})\right)+2\epsilon^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\langle{\bm{x}}^{*},\mathbf{A}{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\rangle+\langle{\bm{y}}^{\prime},\mathbf{C}\hat{{\bm{x}}}^{*}\rangle+2\epsilon n|\mathbf{A}_{\min}|+\frac{|\mathbf{A}_{\min}|}{\epsilon}(2n\cdot 9\epsilon\cdot 2\epsilon)+2\epsilon^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\langle{\bm{x}}^{*},\mathbf{A}{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\rangle+\langle{\bm{y}}^{\prime},\mathbf{C}\hat{{\bm{x}}}^{*}\rangle+(38n+2)|\mathbf{A}_{\min}|\epsilon.

(37)

Moreover,

\displaystyle\langle{\bm{x}}^{*},\mathbf{A}{\bm{x}}^{*}\rangle=\langle{\bm{x}}^{*},\mathbf{A}{\bm{y}}^{*}\rangle+\langle{\bm{x}}^{*},\mathbf{A}({\bm{x}}^{*}-{\bm{y}}^{*})\rangle\leq\langle{\bm{x}}^{*},\mathbf{A}{\bm{y}}^{*}\rangle+2|\mathbf{A}_{\min}|n\epsilon.

(38)

Combining (
37
) and (
38
), we get that for all
{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in\Delta^{n}
,

\displaystyle\langle{\bm{x}}^{*},\mathbf{A}{\bm{x}}^{*}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\langle{\bm{x}}^{*},\mathbf{C}\hat{{\bm{x}}}^{*}\rangle\leq\langle{\bm{y}}^{\prime},\mathbf{A}{\bm{x}}^{*}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\langle{\bm{y}}^{\prime},\mathbf{C}\hat{{\bm{x}}}^{*}\rangle+(21n+1)|\mathbf{A}_{\min}|\epsilon.

(39)

Similarly, we can show that for all
\hat{{\bm{x}}}^{\prime}\in\Delta^{n}
,

\displaystyle\langle-\hat{{\bm{x}}}^{*},\mathbf{A}\hat{{\bm{x}}}^{*}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\langle{\bm{x}}^{*},\mathbf{C}\hat{{\bm{x}}}^{*}\rangle\geq-\langle\hat{{\bm{x}}}^{\prime},\mathbf{A}\hat{{\bm{x}}}^{*}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\langle{\bm{x}}^{*},\mathbf{C}\hat{{\bm{x}}}^{\prime}\rangle-(21n+1)|\mathbf{A}_{\min}|\epsilon.

(40)

Taking
{\bm{y}}^{\prime}=\hat{{\bm{x}}}^{\prime}
in (
39
) and summing with (
40
), we get that for all
\hat{{\bm{x}}}^{\prime}\in\Delta^{n}
,

\langle\hat{{\bm{x}}}^{\prime},\mathbf{A}{\bm{x}}^{*}\rangle+\langle\hat{{\bm{x}}}^{\prime},\mathbf{A}\hat{{\bm{x}}}^{*}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}(\langle\hat{{\bm{x}}}^{\prime},\mathbf{C}\hat{{\bm{x}}}^{*}\rangle+\langle\hat{{\bm{x}}}^{\prime},\mathbf{C}{\bm{x}}^{*}\rangle)+(42n+2)|\mathbf{A}_{\min}|\epsilon\geq\langle{\bm{x}}^{*},\mathbf{A}{\bm{x}}^{*}\rangle+\langle\hat{{\bm{x}}}^{*},\mathbf{A}\hat{{\bm{x}}}^{*}\rangle,

where we used the fact that
\mathbf{C}
is skew-symmetric. Now, using the fact that the Nash equilibrium is symmetric, so that
{\bm{x}}^{*}=\hat{{\bm{x}}}^{*}
, we have

\displaystyle\langle\hat{{\bm{x}}}^{\prime},\mathbf{A}{\bm{x}}^{*}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\langle\hat{{\bm{x}}}^{\prime},\mathbf{C}{\bm{x}}^{*}\rangle+(21n+1)|\mathbf{A}_{\min}|\epsilon

\displaystyle\geq\langle{\bm{x}}^{*},\mathbf{A}{\bm{x}}^{*}\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\langle{\bm{x}}^{*},\mathbf{A}{\bm{x}}^{*}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\langle{\bm{x}}^{*},\mathbf{C}{\bm{x}}^{*}\rangle.

(41)

Setting
\mathbf{A}\coloneqq-\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{R}+\mathbf{R}^{\top})
and
\mathbf{C}\coloneqq\mathbf{R}^{\top}-\mathbf{R}
, (
41
) shows that

\langle\hat{{\bm{x}}}^{\prime},\mathbf{R}{\bm{x}}^{*}\rangle\leq\langle{\bm{x}}^{*},\mathbf{R}{\bm{x}}^{*}\rangle+(21n+1)|\mathbf{A}_{\min}|\epsilon

for any
\hat{{\bm{x}}}^{\prime}\in\Delta^{n}
;
ergo
,
({\bm{x}}^{*},{\bm{x}}^{*})
is a symmetric
(21n+1)|\mathbf{A}_{\min}|\epsilon
-Nash equilibrium of the symmetric (two-player) game
(\mathbf{R},\mathbf{R}^{\top})
, and the proof follows from
Theorem

2.2
.
∎
