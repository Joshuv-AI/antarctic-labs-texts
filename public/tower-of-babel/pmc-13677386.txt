Biotechnological upgrading of palm kernel meal for poultry nutrition: Challenges and opportunities
Authors: Ahmed Mijiyawa, Elham Ghashghaei, Uchechukwu Edna Obianwuna, Okasha Hamada, Yendouhamtchié Nadiedjoa, Maxwell Ansong Okai, Lijing Sun, Muhammad Faisal Riaz, Felix Kwame Amevor, Dossêh Jean Apôtre Afayibo, Were Pitala, Hai Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn recent years, fluctuations in the cost of traditional feed ingredients for poultry farming have compelled producers to explore alternative raw materials to reduce costs. Palm kernel meal (PKM), a by-product of the palm oil extraction industry, is one such alternative. Although PKM is often deemed a low-quality ingredient for poultry feed due to its low energy density and high content of non-starch polysaccharides, particularly β-mannans, its availability and lower price relative to other meal products make it a viable option. This review highlights the nutritional challenges and demonstrates how biotechnology can convert a cumbersome waste product into a valuable feed ingredient. The data focuses on the use of exogenous enzymes, with an emphasis on the synergistic action of β-mannanases and multicarbohydrase cocktails. The use of solid-state fermentation (SSF) with fungal and bacterial strains was also discussed. These bioprocesses efficiently break down complex fibrous matrices, increase the content of crude protein through microbial biomass synthesis and release bioactive manno-oligosaccharides (MOS) in situ. In vivo studies demonstrate that incorporating processed PKM maintains and improves production performance. The released MOS act as prebiotics, stimulating the growth of beneficial gut bacteria, preventing the growth of pathogens, and improving intestinal mucosal architecture. Although biotechnological valorization offers a promising way to transform PKM into a high-value functional ingredient for poultry feed, this technology has certain limitations. These include substrate variability and high scaling costs. Further research is needed into industrial-scale SSF processes, as well as the optimization of eco-efficient enzyme combinations and sequential fermentations.Keywords: Enzyme; Gut health; Palm kernel meal; Poultry nutrition, Solid state fermentation

Introduction

The global population is growing exponentially and is expected to rise from seven billion to over nine billion by 2050. The majority of this growth is expected to occur in developing countries in South Asia and sub-Saharan Africa (Institute, 2019). Consequently, the demand for animal products is also expected to rise. Rae and Nayga (2010) observe that the growing global demand for animal protein has led to a sharp increase in poultry production in tropical countries in recent years. This increase is thought to be linked to the preference for this type of protein among millions of consumers worldwide (Ashrafudoulla et al., 2021). Statistically, McLeod et al. (2011) note that poultry meat production has increased sevenfold, while egg production has increased three and a half times over the past 40 years. Furthermore, Faostat (2022b) reports that global egg production tripled between 1980 and 2020, rising from 26.2 to 86.7 million tonnes. The FAO (2025) also reports that, in 2019, global chicken meat production accounted for around 40% of total meat production, making it the most widely produced meat worldwide. It is projected that global poultry meat consumption will be 2.3 times higher in 2050 than in 2010, leading to a significant increase in demand for animal feed and raw materials (Kleyn and Ciacciariello, 2021).

When formulating chicken feed, raw materials are generally classified based on their specific characteristics. For instance, some materials provide energy, protein, calcium, and phosphorus, while others supply minerals, trace elements, and vitamins (Buldgen et al., 1996; Abbas, 2023). According to Ravindran (2013b), the demand for agricultural raw materials (e.g., corn, wheat, soybeans, and soybean meal) in animal feed production is continuously and significantly increasing. Dronne (2018) reports that approximately 630 million tonnes of the world's plant protein production (around 800 million tonnes) is used in animal feed, representing 79% of global plant protein consumption (including forage) and 58% of protein from concentrates. This enables the production of around 68 million tonnes of animal protein. Similarly, Makkar (2018) found that the monogastric sector consumed 155 million tonnes of protein for animal feed in 2013, and estimated that an additional 52 million tonnes would be required by 2030 to meet demand in this sector. Ravindran (2013a) predicts that there will be a significant gap between the supply and demand for raw materials used in animal production in the coming decades. This is because the consumption of plant resources essential for livestock farming directly competes with that of the general population. Consequently, food production costs have risen due to increasing raw material prices. Afolabi et al. (2012) explain that competition between humans and livestock for conventional plant resources has led to shortages and price increases for food ingredients.

From 2019 to 2020, during the initial emergence of the pandemic in Africa, the Caribbean, Asia, and South America, many smallholder farmers faced losses due to high commodity prices, which were exacerbated by urban lockdowns and logistical limitations (Ani et al., 2020; Murray et al., 2021; Uyanga et al., 2021; Mijiyawa et al., 2025). In poultry farming, feed is crucial for maintaining physiological functions and optimizing production performance and egg quality (Bouvarel et al., 2010). According to Loul (1998), Zanu et al. (2012) and Galal et al. (2019), feed accounts for 60–70% of the cost of poultry production operations. Therefore, innovations that enhance nutrient utilization by poultry are essential for developing the poultry sector and increasing its competitiveness on a larger scale.

Alshelmani et al. (2021) suggest that agricultural by-products could serve as valuable alternative feed sources. These by-products can provide protein and energy, replacing soybean meal and yellow corn in the diets of monogastric animals. Given its cost-effectiveness compared to conventional feeds, there is an economic incentive to study the use of PKM in poultry feed across four regions of the world (Asia, the Pacific, South America, and Africa). PKM remains cheaper than soybean meal, even when transportation costs are considered. Global PKM production has grown dramatically, with an annual growth rate of 15% over the past two decades (Faostat, 2022a). However, according to Alimon (2004), PKM contains moderate levels of crude protein (15.5–19.6%), high levels of fiber (13–20%), and a poor amino acid profile (deficient in lysine, methionine, and tryptophan). Additionally, it contains low apparent metabolisable energy (AME) levels of 1,880–2,653 kcal/kg (Sundu et al., 2006; Ezieshi and Olomu, 2007).

Thus, PKM is considered a moderate-quality feed ingredient for ruminants, but not for monogastric animals. Despite its low nutritional value, the availability and low price of PKM compared to other oilseed cakes make it a useful ingredient in poultry feed. This review critically examines the technological breakthrough achieved through the biotechnological valorisation of PKM. Through an analysis of advances in SSF and enzymatic biocatalysis, we demonstrate that these processes not only eliminate anti-nutritional factors, but also transform PKM into a functional ingredient enriched with prebiotic compounds (MOS). We outline a roadmap for the sustainable integration of biotransformed PKM into the diets of broiler chickens and laying hens, while identifying major industrial hurdles, specifically substrate variability and scaling costs.

Nutritional composition of palm kernel meal (PKM) and limitations in poultry (the challenges)

PKM is an agri-food by-product (AIBP) of the palm oil extraction industry. According to O’Mara et al. (1999), the method used to extract the oil, the species of palm nut, and the amount of shell remaining in the meal all affect the nutrient concentration of PKM. The fat content varies depending on the oil extraction method. Chin (2001) states that palm kernels obtained by mechanical extraction contain 5–12% oil, while those extracted by solvent contain 0.5–3%. Babatunde et al. (1975) report that the crude fiber (CF) concentration of PKM ranges from 7 to 20%, depending on the amount of shells and fruit removed from the meal. Furthermore, Alimon (2004) states that PKM contains high levels of fiber (13–20%), has a poor amino acid profile (deficient in lysine, methionine, and tryptophan), and has moderate levels of crude protein (15.5–19.6%). Studies on the standardised ileal digestibility (SID) of PKM show varying results (Table 4). For example, research by Sundu et al. (2008) on six-week-old broiler chickens reported ileal amino acid digestibility ranging from 57% to 82% for Lys and arginine (Arg), respectively. Similarly, Met digestibility was low at around 72%. Furthermore, Blair (2023) conducted SID studies on caecectomised roosters, using ten PKM samples from Colombia, Costa Rica, Mexico, Indonesia and Thailand. Values differed depending on the PKM source. Standardised amino acid digestibility values ranged from 14.6% to 88.2%, depending on the specific amino acid. Among the ten PKM samples, the standardised amino acid digestibility values for Lys, Met, cysteine (Cys) and threonine (Thr) were found to range from 34.5% to 59.7%, 65.5% to 80.3%, 29.3% to 68.8% and 53.5% to 76.9% respectively, demonstrating significant variation. Due to the low Lys and Met content and digestibility of PKM, it is necessary to supplement these two essential amino acids or use significant amounts of protein-rich ingredients when including PKM in the diet at high levels. The reasons for this low digestibility of Lys and Met are difficult to determine, as other amino acids were not similarly affected. Flipot et al. (1971) attribute this to high dietary fibre content, whereas O’Mara et al. (1999) point to heat treatment during processing. There are very few comparative studies on amino acid digestibility of PKM in poultry; however, the existing studies suggest that the species of animal and the source of the PKM affect SID (Sundu et al., 2008; Blair, 2023).

However, in terms of energy, PKM is a highly variable product; its energy value for poultry depends heavily on fat content and the presence of kernel shells, which increase fibre and lignin levels (Jackson and Zumbado, 1996). Previous studies have shown that PKM has a low AME content, ranging from 1,880 to 2,653 kcal/kg (Sundu et al., 2006; Ezieshi and Olomu, 2007). Abdollahi et al. (2015) studied 21- to 25-day-old broiler chickens and determined that the AME and AMEn values for PKM were 1,307 and 1,250 kcal/kg, respectively. Sundu et al. (2005) obtained an AME value of 2,261 kcal/kg, which was higher than previous values. This difference was attributed to the high fat content (111 g/kg) of their PKM sample, linked to variations in oil extraction processes. Furthermore, Ezieshi and Olomu (2007) reported AME values of 2,653, 2,598 and 2,424 kcal/kg respectively using three types of PKM (Okomu, Presco and Envoy) at a 20% inclusion rate in 35-day-old broiler chickens with corresponding fat contents of 94.8, 75.9 and 13.0 g/kg respectively. Similarly, Sundu et al. (2006) obtained AME values of 1,881 and 2,261 kcal/kg in three- and six-week-old broiler chickens, respectively.

Factors limiting the use of feed by-products in animal nutrition

Several factors limit the use of AIBP in animal nutrition, including nutrient variability, the presence of anti-nutritional factors, sensitivity to peroxidation, and high fiber content. According to Yang et al. (2021), anti-nutritional factors commonly found in AIBP impact palatability, digestibility, and animal production performance. Similarly, Alshelmani et al. (2017) and Aftab and Bedford (2018) assert that these factors likely impede bird growth.

Anti-nutritional factors of Palm Kernel meal

Regarding PKM, Düsterhöft et al. (1991) identify the most prevalent anti-nutritional factors as linear mannans with low galactosid substitution (78% of the total NSPs), followed by cellulose (12%), and trace amounts of (4-O-methyl)-glucuronoxylans and arabinoxylans (3% each). Furthermore, according to Moreira and Filho (2008); Han et al. (2010); Van Zyl et al. (2010) there are four types of mannan: linear mannan, galactomannan, glucomannan and galactoglucomannan (Fig. 1). However, Jackson et al. (2004) state that β-mannans in PKM are linear polysaccharides consisting of repeating units of β-1,4-mannose, α-1,6-galactose and glucose linked to a β-mannan backbone. These same authors also state that β-mannans are extremely antinutritional in monogastric animals. Studies by Jackson et al. (2004), Hsiao et al. (2006), and Williams et al. (2014) have demonstrated that β-mannans are surface components of many pathogens and are recognized by animals as pathogen-associated antigens, thereby activating their innate immune system. This immune response, known as the feed-induced immune response, impairs animal performance by diverting energy from growth to this energy-intensive response (Hsiao et al., 2006; Williams et al., 2014). Daud and Jarvis (1992) state that PKM contains hard, crystalline mannans that are highly insoluble in albumen. These factors have been shown to be insoluble in water. Other authors claim that 81% of the carbohydrates in palm kernel meals are non-starch polysaccharides (NSPs) (Knudsen, 1997). The main form of total NSPs in PKM is an insoluble, non-cellulosic polysaccharide representing 33.6% of the dry matter. Furthermore, in addition to the NSPs in PKM, Mok et al. (2013) report a high lignin content, which is likely to result from contamination with nut shells. This is estimated to be 18% of the neutral detergent fibre (NDF), which contributes to the product's gritty and fibrous texture. Therefore, it is important to find ways to improve ingredients containing anti-nutritional factors. Several methods exist to deactivate these factors, including physical, chemical, and biological methods (Yang et al., 2021).

Impact of fibers on intestinal viscosity and nutrient digestibility

PKM is rich in fiber. Research by Panigrahi and Powell (1991), Onifade and Babatunde (1998), and Sundu et al. (2005) has shown that higher levels of PKM in feed decrease the digestibility and retention of nitrogen and calcium. Therefore, it can be concluded that PKM cell walls contain a high proportion of nitrogen or protein. Similarly, the decrease in digestibility with increasing levels of PKM incorporation may be due to broiler chickens' limited ability to digest dietary fiber, such as β-mannan. This is because their digestive tracts lack the necessary enzymes to degrade mannan, which is the main component of NSP in PKM (Sathitkowitchai et al., 2018). Yaophakdee et al. (2018) state that the NSP content of PKM binds water in the lumen, thereby increasing the viscosity of the digestive tract and reducing its flow rate. This results in increased feed intake (FI) and decreased feed digestibility. Annison (1993) also states that soluble fiber increases intestinal viscosity and reduces nutrient digestibility in the small intestine of broiler chickens. Furthermore, in addition to the viscosity effect caused by β-mannans, they can lead to nutrient encapsulation. Specifically, the rigid, insoluble cell wall structure of PKM physically traps nutrients (such as proteins, lipids and starch) inside the cell, preventing the digestive enzymes of monogastric animals from accessing them and reducing overall nutrient digestibility.

However, Brumano et al. (2006) and Olukosi et al. (2007) state that nutrient digestibility increases with age, as the capacity of the digestive tract to digest and absorb nutrients is limited during the starter phase. The ability to digest and metabolise nutrients, particularly compounds containing anti-nutritional factors such as NSPs, tannins, and phytic acid, also varies with age (Del Alamo et al., 2008; Kiarie et al., 2014). This is because the digestive tract matures over time, secreting abundant enzymes. As broiler chickens age, their secretion of digestive enzymes, intestinal surface area, and digestive capacity all increase (Khalil et al., 2023). In the case of PKM, broiler chickens have a limited capacity to digest dietary fiber, such as β-mannan, during the starter phase (Mijiyawa et al., 2025). However, Yaophakdee et al. (2018) found no significant changes in villus length, width, crypt depth, or ileal surface area in 34-day-old broiler chickens, regardless of PKM levels. The authors explain this by stating that adult chicks have well-developed gastrointestinal tracts, enzymes, and microflora that can break down NSPs, enabling them to withstand the negative effects of a high-fiber diet. Patrapan (2013) further supports this finding, stating that growing and finishing chicks can utilise high-fiber diets more efficiently than young chicks. This explains why there is no negative effect on intestinal morphology following the administration of PKM-rich diets, and the low digestibility in the start-up phase.

Incorporation levels and physiological performance

Relatively little research has been conducted on the optimal level of PKM in poultry feed. The suitability of PKM for poultry depends on factors such as the type, age and sex of the chicken, and the source and variation in the oil and hull content of the PKM (Alimon, 2004; Sharmila et al., 2014a). The limitation of the use of PKM in monogastric diets is due to the high level of CF, the main components of which are NSPs and β-mannan. Of the total NSPs, 78% is linear mannan (Düsterhöft et al., 1991; Abdollahi et al., 2016), and the content of the amino acids Met, Tryptophan (Trp) and Cys is low (Fasuyi, 2014).

Growth performance in broilers: Hutagalung (1980) and Yeong (1980) were the first authors to recommend incorporating 20% PKM into broiler chicken feed with no negative impact on performance. Onwudike (1986) conducted studies on different levels of PKM incorporation in starter and finisher feeds for broiler chickens and reported that 28% and 35%, respectively, could be administered without any adverse effects. However, Panigrahi and Powell (1991) and Sundu et al. (2004) recommend incorporating 40% PKM into broiler chicken feed when Met and Lys are added. By contrast, Abdollahi et al. (2016) state that incorporating 16% PKM into broiler chicken feed would have no adverse effect on growth performance, provided amino acid digestibility and AME of PKM are considered in formulation.

The results of Botello et al. (2020) experiment showed that incorporating up to 100 g/kg of PKM into the diet of male Cobb 500 broiler chickens as a partial replacement for corn and soybean meal had no negative impact on growth performance, carcass characteristics or profitability. Furthermore, the same study found that incorporating 300 g/kg of PKM offered the best cost-benefit ratio. Futhermore, Abidah and Wan Nooraida (2017) study concluded that including 10% PKM in starter and finisher feeds for broiler chickens did not negatively affect growth performance, being comparable to the corn-soy-based control diet (0% PKM), and achieving a better feed conversion ratio (FCR) by week 5. However, adding more than 10% PKM resulted in poorer performance in terms of average body weight and FCR. However, in their study on Ross 308 broiler chickens, Setiana and Subekti (2025) recommend modifying the particle size to 1 mm and limiting its incorporation in feed formulations to a maximum of 5% to optimise PKM use and obtain the best performance and carcass quality in broiler chickens.

There has been a considerable divergence in the level of PKM incorporation in the diets of meat chickens from 1980 to 2025. These differences can be attributed to various factors, primarily the source and quality of the PKM, which can vary significantly (Nwokolo et al., 1977; Yeong, 1980) (Table 2). Another plausible reason is the feed formulation. PKM is deficient in essential amino acids, so when Panigrahi and Powell (1991) and Sundu et al. (2004) formulated balanced diets including Lys and Met, they observed an improvement in the incorporation rate. Therefore, when formulating a feed containing a high-fibre ingredient such as PKM, it is crucial to consider the amino acid balance and metabolisable energy. Another possible reason may be related to the breed of broiler chicken used in the experiments, as reflected in the studies by Botello et al. (2020) and Setiana and Subekti (2025).

Laying performance and egg quality: Onwudike (1988) and Perez et al. (2000) reported that including up to 40% PKM in diets for Harco and White Leghorn layers had no negative impact on performance or egg quality. However, a significant decrease in egg production, egg weight, FI and FCR was observed beyond 40–50% PKM (Onwudike, 1988; Perez et al., 2000). Nevertheless, Onwudike (1986) recommends incorporating 30% PKM into pullet feed during the starter and grower phases, which was not be detrimental to the animals' development or future laying performance. Furthermore, Adrizal et al. (2011) suggest incorporating 30% PKM into the diet of local Indonesian laying hens with no negative effects on FI, FCR, egg production, egg weight or mass. The quality characteristics of the eggs produced by hens fed diets containing PKM were comparable to those produced by hens fed a control diet of corn and soybean meal, except for yolk colour, which was paler in the presence of PKM. Nevertheless, the results of Gunawan et al. (2025) study indicate that adding 5% PKM to the diet of Lohman strain laying hens maintains FI, egg production, and energy and protein levels equivalent to those of the control group. Beyond a 5% incorporation rate, however, negative effects on the aforementioned parameters are observed. It is interesting to note that over the years, the level of PKM incorporation in animal feed has decreased. The discrepancy in results between the authors can be attributed to differences in strain, age of laying hens, and experiment duration.

It is important to distinguish between PKM and palm kernel cake (PKC). Although both are by-products of the palm oil extraction industry, the extraction methods differ. Many articles use the terms PKM and PKC interchangeably. However, there is a slight difference in their nutritional values. Consequently, information about PKM can be misleading. As PKM is considered a low-utility ingredient in poultry farming and given the aforementioned results, it is important to find ways to improve it, hence the need for biotechnology.

Biotechnology valorization strategies (the opportunities)

Among the biotechnological approaches to enhancing the nutritional value of PKM are enzyme supplementation, in-situ prebiotic production, SSF, and combined (bio-physico-chemical) treatments. All these approaches aim to neutralise the effect of antinutritional factors (mannans).

Enzyme supplementation (exogenous enzymes)

In the animal feed industry, PKM is supplemented with enzymes that degrade NSPs (NSPase), particularly β-mannanase, when it is included in feed. This is because poultry cannot degrade NSPs due to a lack of endogenous fiber-degrading enzymes. According to Düsterhöft et al. (1991), β-mannan is not the only major NSPs in PKM, as xylan is also present in large quantities. However, due to the complexity of the fibers contained in PKM, a combination of enzymes including β-mannanase, xylanase, cellulase, glucosidase, and galactosidase could be beneficial. Furthermore, according to Chauhan et al. (2012) and Hlalukana et al. (2021), the heterogeneous nature of mannan means that efficient biodegradation may require close association and synergy between the following enzymes: β-mannanase (EC 3.2.1.78), β-mannosidase (EC 3.2.1.25), acetylmannan esterase (EC 3.1.1.6), β-glucosidase (EC 3.2.1.21) and α-galactosidase (EC 3.2.1.22). These enzymes would then cleave the main and side chains of mannan. These combinations are all due to the complexity of the mannans and other antinutritional constituents (such as glucuronoxylans, arabinoxylans and cellulose) found in PKM. Mannans consist of a main chain of β-(1,4)-D-mannose. According to Manna and McAnalley (1993), the 2nd, 3rd or 6th carbon of a D-mannose residue can be acetylated. Furthermore, Reid (1985) states that a D-mannose residue can be substituted by galactose at position 6, forming a galactomannan. The same author states that a glucomannan consists of a main chain of mannoses and glucoses linked in β-(1,4) chains, which are sometimes substituted by galactose residues at α-(1,6) (Fig. 1). For these reasons, β-mannanase alone is insufficient to completely degrade the mannan in PKM. This highlights the importance of an enzymatic cocktail. Furthermore, cell wall polysaccharides are linked to each other by a large number of inter- and intramolecular interactions. Lee et al. (2011) state that these interactions constitute a major barrier to enzyme action. There is a clear trade-off between formulation cost, substrate specificity and overall animal performance when choosing between single enzyme supplementation and multi-enzyme cocktails. Single-enzyme supplementation with β-mannanase targets the main linear β-mannan backbone found in PKM, and β-mannanase efficiently cleaves internal β-1,4-glucosidic bonds when used alone. This primary degradation reduces the viscosity of the intestinal contents and attenuates the innate immune response triggered by soluble mannan structures (Hsiao et al., 2006). The result is moderate improvements in AME and overall poultry performance (Daskiran et al., 2004). From an industrial perspective, incorporating a single enzyme is a simple, cost-effective solution that carries less formulation risk. However, its efficacy is inherently limited as it cannot fully degrade the complex matrix of branched-chain NSPs found in PKM (Sundu et al., 2006). Therefore, multi-enzyme cocktails comprising β-mannanase, α-galactosidase and fibrolytic enzymes offer a synergistic approach to fibre degradation (Meng et al., 2005). While α-galactosidase cleaves galactose residues from the side chains, exposing the underlying mannan backbone to β-mannanase, complementary xylanases or cellulases disrupt the surrounding cell wall matrix. This multi-target mechanism leads to significantly higher oligosaccharide solubilisation, greater AME and improved amino acid digestibility compared to single-component treatments. Despite these clear physiological advantages, the commercial adoption of multi-enzyme cocktails is hindered by their high unit cost. For multi-enzyme cocktails to be economically viable under real-world conditions, the marginal gains in FCR must consistently offset the additional formulation cost (Cowieson et al., 2010). This requires precise optimisation of enzyme proportions based on the level of PKM incorporated into the diet. Several authors state that the enzymes used in polysaccharide degradation are produced by bacteria or fungi (Matte and Forsberg, 1992; Elegir et al., 1994; Belancic et al., 1995), as well as by plants (Cleemput et al., 1997). In the case of β-mannan from PKM, the enzymes cleave β-1,4-glycosidic bonds between two sugars or synthesise glycosidic bonds in order to reduce the antinutritional effects. These hydrolysis products are considered MOS.

In-Situ prebiotic production

Small amounts of natural MOS can be extracted from the structural and storage parts of plants. However, the largest quantities of MOS are now produced by hydrolytically cleaving various natural mannans (Fig. 2). Some agricultural by-products, such as copra meal and PKM, have been shown to be an excellent source of mannan (up to 60%) (Hlalukana et al., 2021). Plant mannans can be found as either homopolymers (linear mannan) or heteropolymers (galactomannan, glucomannan, and galactoglucopolymer), depending on their origin. PKM is known to be rich in β-mannans. According to Arabinda Ghosh et al. (2013) and Shallom and Shoham (2003), β-mannanases (EC 3.2.1.78) are the main enzymes responsible for initiating mannan degradation. They do this by randomly cleaving the peptide backbone, releasing short β-1,4-mannose oligomers and producing new chain ends. According to Gübitz et al. (1996) and Dhawan and Kaur (2007), the products of mannan hydrolysis by β-mannanases are mannobiose and mannotriose. Moreira and Filho (2008) and Van Zyl et al. (2010), however, state that β-mannosidases (EC 3.2.1.25) are exoenzymes that catalyse the hydrolysis of β-1,4-linked mannosides, releasing mannose from the non-reducing end of mannans and MOS. Xiao et al. (2004) and Chauve et al. (2010), however, report that most β-glucosidases are inhibited by glucose and unable to hydrolyse long β-1,4 chains. Finally, Jindou et al. (2002) report that α-galactosidases (EC 3.2.1.22) hydrolyse the α-galactoside substituents of simple oligosaccharides (e.g. raffinose and stachyose) and polymeric substrates (e.g. galactomannans) (Fig. 2). The hydrolysis of mannans requires several glycoside hydrolase (GH) enzymes to act together. Moreira and Filho (2008) identified two types of synergy with respect to mannanolytic enzymes: homeosynergy and heterosynergy. They define homeosynergy as the cooperation between two main-chain cleaving enzymes (e.g. β-mannanase and β-mannosidase), or two side-chain cleaving enzymes (e.g. α-galactosidase and acetylmannan esterase). In contrast, they define heterosynergy as the synergistic interaction between a side-chain cleaving enzyme and a main-chain cleaving enzyme (e.g. β-mannanase and α-galactosidase).

Hydrolysis of mannan with enzymes attacking specific action points to yield sugars (Losada-Garcia et al., 2025).

According to Latham et al. (2016), the hydrolysis of mannan polysaccharides by enzymes reduces antinutritional factors and intestinal viscosity, releases nutrients from cell walls, and breaks down mannan into simple sugars. This allows nutrients and digestive enzymes to circulate more freely, thus improving nutrient absorption. However, research by Saenphoom et al. (2011) suggests that the hydrolysis of PKM by exogenous enzymes can reduce the cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin content and increase the reducing sugar content. Conversely, Olaniyi (2014); Chen et al. (2019) and Mairizal et al. (2019) suggest that hydrolysis by mannanase enzymes can decrease the cellulose, hemicellulose, and CF content of PKM.

Furthermore, Purnawan et al. (2017) state that hydrolysis of PKM by the mannanase enzyme of Streptomyces cyanus produces MOS, which have been identified as mannobiose, mannotriose, mannoterose, mannopentose, mannohexose, and mannoheptose. Based on the preceding information regarding the use of enzymes, either individually or in combination, it is evident that it is possible to improve the suitability of PKM for monogastric animals by reducing its antinutritional factors. Furthermore, the production of MOS during hydrolysis provides an added benefit to animal feed, as these oligosaccharides exhibit prebiotic and/or probiotic properties. In short, using single or combined enzymes has been shown to effectively reduce the antinutritional properties of PKM.

Solid state fermentation (SSF)

Fermentation is a useful method of reducing the antinutritional content of feed ingredients. Cervantes-Pahm and Stein (2010) state that fermentation is a simple, inexpensive method of reducing antinutritional factors to undetectable levels and improving the ingredient's nutritional value. Antinutritional factors such as trypsin inhibitors (TIs), phytates, galactooligosaccharides, lectins, and allergenic proteins are effectively eliminated through fermentation (Hong et al., 2004; Seo and Cho, 2016; Jazi et al., 2018). Furthermore, Sugiharto and Ranjitkar (2019) state that changes occurring during soybean fermentation increase the amount of assimilable and usable nutrients, minerals, and vitamins, thereby improving chicken health and growth. Additionally, Feng et al. (2007) and Park and Kim (2019) found that reduced levels of trypsin and other antinutritional factors enhance digestive enzyme activity and immune and antioxidant capacity, improving overall nutrient utilization.

Several studies have been conducted using biological agents (fungi or bacteria), given the beneficial effect of fermentation, which involves the breakdown of NSPs that chickens cannot digest. In other words, these microorganisms break down complex matrix polysaccharides into sugars, proteins and metabolites through the cocktails of enzymes they produce (Fig. 3). These are therefore easily assimilated by animals. For instance, SSF of PKM with Aspergillus wentii TISTR 3075 increased the crude protein content from 16.99% to 21.36% (Muangkeow and Chinajariyawong, 2009). However, the limiting amino acids in PKM (solvent-extracted; PKM) and fermented PKM (FPKM) with Aspergillus wentii TISTR 3075 were sulfur-containing amino acids (Cys and Met) and Lys. Nevertheless, the AME of the PKM and FPKM in this study was 2,201.83 and 2,080.26 kcal/kg, respectively. One possible explanation for this result is that A. wentii can utilize the nitrogen-free extract (NFE) of the PKM as an energy source during fermentation, resulting in lower metabolizable energy in the FPKM than in the PKM. Similarly, after fermenting palm kernel extract (FPKE) with Aspergillus niger for 66 hours, Marini et al. (2008) observed a significant increase in CP content and a decrease in CF content.

Solid state fermentation with bacteria to improve the nutritional value of PKM (https://BioRender.com).

However, studies involving the FPKM or FPKC with bacteria such as Bacillus have shown improvements in the nutritional profile, enzymatic activity, and performance of broiler chickens. For instance, Pasaribu (2018) found that incubating PKC with Bacillus amyloliquefaciens and Trichoderma harzianum for seven days decreased the CF content. Similarly, Ong et al. (2024) achieved a reduction of over 10% in NDF during 24 hours of PKM fermentation with the Bacillus subtilis F6 strain. These results demonstrate the enormous potential of this Bacillus strain to reduce indigestible fiber and enrich PKM with prebiotics and probiotics, thus transforming it into a functional food ingredient with enhanced nutritional value. Furthermore, Ciptaan (2019) concluded that fermenting PKM with 7% Bacillus subtilis for six days yielded the best results, as evidenced by a CP content of 24.65%, a CF content of 17.35%, a nitrogen retention rate of 68.47%, and a CF digestibility rate of 53.25%. Furthermore, Mirnawati et al. (2023) demonstrated that fermenting PKM with Lactobacillus fermentum increased the CP content by 25.81%, decreased the CF content by 16.90%, and raised the metabolic energy level to 2,743 kcal/kg. Zhu et al. (2024) found that, after 60 hours of SSF of PKM with Bacillus velezensis, Saccharomyces cerevisiae, and Lactobacillus paracasei, SSF effectively improved the nutritional value and antioxidant activity of PKM. This offers a viable solution for increasing the use of PKM in animal feed.

Furthermore, Mirnawati et al. (2025) successfully demonstrated through their studies that a defined bacterial consortium composed of Lactobacillus fermentum and Bacillus subtilis exhibits superior enzymatic, probiotic and growth characteristics compared to monospecific cultures. According to the authors' results, the consortium exhibited synergistic activity in producing cellulase (13.71 U/mL) and mannanase (17.05 U/mL), whereas Lactobacillus fermentum alone displayed the greatest protease activity (11.29 U/mL). Culturing the consortium in a PKM-containing medium at a 1:1 ratio of Lactobacillus fermentum and Bacillus subtilis showed optimal enzymatic production at a PKM concentration of 15–20%. This demonstrates the consortium's potential to improve nutrient digestibility in high-fibre poultry feeds. This bacterial combination also exhibited high tolerance to acidity (70.6%) and bile (62.84%), as well as high hydrophobicity (83.75%) and substantial antibacterial activity against E. coli, S. enteritidis and S. aureus. This confirms its probiotic functionality, as previously discussed. In conclusion, the Lactobacillus fermentum–Bacillus subtilis consortium is a biotechnological innovation with great potential. It combines enzyme production, probiotic function and the sustainable use of feed substrates, offering a practical, environmentally friendly way to improve poultry productivity and intestinal health (Mirnawati et al., 2025).

Numerous studies report substantial increases in CP concentration following SSF of PKM. However, it is important to make a critical analytical distinction between relative CP accumulation and actual nutritional upgrading. The apparent increase in CP is often caused by two distinct mechanisms: 1. The Dry Matter Loss Effect: Microorganisms consume digestible carbohydrates (such as soluble sugars and starches), as well as NSP fractions, for energy, releasing CO₂ and H₂O in the process. This selective loss of organic dry matter concentrates the remaining insoluble fraction, creating an apparent mathematical rise in CP percentage without synthesising new amino acids. 2. Microbial biomass and non-protein nitrogen (NPN): The synthesised protein reflects microbial cell wall constituents, nucleic acids and NPN, rather than balanced plant proteins. In fungal SSF, some of the measured nitrogen is incorporated into chitin and cell wall glucosamines. These contribute to total nitrogen (N * 6.25), but offer limited amino acid value to poultry. Therefore, evaluating SSF efficacy solely on CP enrichment is misleading. Future research and processing validation must rely on standardised true amino acid digestibility (TAAD) and essential amino acid profiling (specifically Lys, Met, and Thr) in poultry.

Although laboratory-scale SSF has clear biochemical benefits, implementing it in commercial feed manufacturing introduces severe operational bottlenecks. The moisture and drying dilemma: optimal SSF requires a substrate moisture content of 40–60%. However, commercial poultry feed requires ingredients with less than 12% moisture to ensure storage stability and prevent mould growth. Thermal drying of FPKM consumes a lot of energy, which significantly increases the processing cost per tonne and risks the thermal denaturation of heat-sensitive micronutrients or beneficial probiotic spores. Bioreactor scale-up and heat dissipation: In large-scale solid-bed fermenters, metabolic heat accumulation creates thermal gradients that exceed the optimal growth temperature of the inoculum, causing localised microbial inactivation or uneven fermentation. Batch-to-Batch Consistency: Variations in the composition of raw PKM (e.g. residual oil content, shell/kernel ratio and particle size) directly affect porosity and water-holding capacity. This leads to inconsistent substrate bioconversion across production batches. Table 5 shows a comparison of the nutritional values of untreated and treated PKM. Combined treatments (bio-physico-chemical)

In order to overcome the severe structural recalcitrance of PKM, a more effective strategy than single-treatment methods is to combine distinct processing modalities. These integrated approaches rely on mechanisms that operate across physical, chemical and biological boundaries. In this context, Sundu et al. (2024) reached several key insights: employing bioconversion technology through fermentation and the addition of ammonium sulphate enhances the physical characteristics of PKM, increases its protein content, and decreases its CF content. The digestibility of protein and CF from the hydrolysis of FPKM is further improved when using a multi-enzyme complex. Additionally, incorporating 20% hydrolysed PKM post-fermentation boosts the AME of the feed. Fermentation with 6% ammonium sulphate, coupled with hydrolysis using a multi-enzyme complex, yields favorable outcomes. Furthermore, Chen et al. (2015b) state that the enzymatic hydrolysis of PKM by SSF has been shown to increase monosaccharide reduction and improve the release of prebiotic oligosaccharides. Pre-treating PKM with mild chemical reagents before enzymatic hydrolysis or microbial inoculation removes structural lignin barriers, significantly enhancing catalytic efficacy (Saenphoom et al., 2013). However, this approach necessitates additional washing or pH-neutralisation steps to prevent the denaturation of exogenous enzymes or the inactivation of fermentative microflora. This adds processing stages and operational expenditure (OPEX).

Physical treatments, such as extrusion and sieving, are an alternative to enzymatic treatment. Extrusion treatment of PKC/PKM has been shown to improve its nutritional value (Hakim et al., 2020), with a reduction in total NSPs and CF accompanied by increased concentrations of mannose, glucose, fructose, 1,4-β-D-mannobiose, 1,4-β-D-mannotriose, 1,4-β-D-mannotetraose and 1,4-β-D-mannopentaose (Roslan et al., 2017). This has also been shown to improve poultry performance. Additionally, Faridah et al. (2020) reported a 6% increase in AME and a 32% increase in CP digestibility in broiler chickens following extrusion. Futhermore, extrusion increases the surface porosity of PKM and reduces particle size, thereby improving bulk density and the rate at which it passes through the avian digestive tract. However, strict temperature control is essential, as excessive thermal exposure can induce Maillard reactions, which bind free amino acids (particularly L-Lys) and reduce overall protein digestibility. Thus, Roslan et al. (2017) found that the optimal extrusion conditions were a temperature of 178°C, a screw speed of 100 rpm, a hopper speed of 5 Hz and a moisture content of 75%.

The most practical multi-modal strategy is thermo-mechanical pretreatments coupled with SSF or targeted enzymatic hydrolysis (Saenphoom et al., 2013; Faridah et al., 2020). Physical disruption decrystallises cellulose and increases the surface area of the substrate, enabling deeper penetration by microbial hyphae and exposing internal β-mannan chains for enzymatic cleavage. While this combination yields superior NSPs solubilisation and increases true metabolizable energy (TMEn) by up to 38%, its industrial adoption is strictly constrained by the high energy requirements of post-fermentation drying to reduce moisture content to below 12%.

Combining thermal and mechanical forces with mild chemical agents (e.g. alkaline swelling using NaOH or aqueous ammonia) effectively cleaves the ester bonds that cross-link lignin to hemicelluloses (Faridah et al., 2020). While this dual approach achieves extensive cell wall breakdown and nutrient de-encapsulation in vitro, its application in poultry nutrition is limited due to risks associated with chemical residues, reduced feed palatability, equipment corrosion and the costs of environmental effluent disposal.

To overcome the economic and operational challenges of multi-stage processes, new green technologies are emerging. Deep eutectic solvents (DES) offer an eco-friendly, selective delignification process that operates under mild thermal conditions and does not destroy functional polysaccharides (Mani et al., 2025). Concurrently, advances in protein engineering and directed evolution are yielding hyper-thermostable β-mannanases capable of withstanding industrial pelleting temperatures (80–90°C). This was demonstrated in a study by Politz et al. (2000), who discovered a highly thermostable endo-(1,4)-β-mannanase produced by the marine bacterium Rhodothermus marinus. Furthermore, precision fermentation and engineered microbial hosts capable of consolidated bioprocessing (CBP) allow for the simultaneous expression of multiple enzymes and the upgrading of biomass in a single step (Yeboah and Söffker, 2026), paving the way for the cost-effective commercial valorisation of PKM. Impacts on production performance and poultry physiology

Adding enzymes is one way to reduce the negative effects of mannan by increasing nutrient availability in poultry rations. According to Sundu et al. (2006), increasing the digestibility and nutritional value of PKM nutrients could be achieved through enzyme supplementation with hemicellulases and mannases. The conference report by Sundu and Dingle (2003) showed increased live weight gain and FI in broiler chickens fed a diet containing 30% PKM and mannanase containing α-galactosidase. Furthermore, the same authors found that combining mannanase containing α-galactosidase with other enzyme cocktails containing cellulase, β-glucanase, phytase, protease, amylase, pectinase, and pentosanase in a broiler chicken feed containing increasing levels of PKM (0–40%) did not improve live weight gain between days 5 and 15, but reduced jejunal viscosity at day 20. The first trial showed an improvement in production parameters with a 30% PKM incorporation rate and the addition of a mannanase containing α-galactosidase. However, the second trial showed no improvement in production parameters on days 5 and 15, despite the addition of an enzyme cocktail. This demonstrates that, even with enzyme supplementation, there are limitations to high PKM incorporation rates depending on age. Furthermore, the digestive system is immature in chicks, and multi-enzyme supplementation remains ineffective, which explains the reported results.

Moreover, Iyayi and Davies (2005) and Abdollahi et al. (2016) showed that adding Avizyme 1300 (100g/t) (Iyayi and Davies, 2005) or a mixture of β-mannanase and NSP-degrading enzymes (200 g/t) (Abdollahi et al., 2016) to diets containing PKM improved the digestibility of protein, fat, and fiber and increased weight gain in broiler chickens. This improvement can be explained by the fact that the enzymes degrade NSPs, releasing β-mannans that are then encapsulated in intact cell walls. These β-mannans can subsequently be depolymerised by β-mannanase, which acts synergistically with the latter (Williams et al., 2014). The depolymerisation of β-mannans by β-mannanase improves nutrient retention (including fibre), thereby contributing to weight gain and enhanced feed efficiency. This partly explains the positive effect of adding enzymes on nutrient utilisation. Sharmila et al. (2014b) reported that adding β-xylanase (200U/kg) to diets containing 20% PKM enhanced the growth performance of male Cobb 500 broiler chickens. However, in their study of 33-week-old Hy-Line Brown laying hens, Choi et al. (2021) concluded that supplementing their diet with 10% PKM and β-xylanase (0.025%, which corresponds to an activity of 1,000,000 U/kg) had little effect on productivity. A study by Kim et al. (2025), which involved the inclusion of mannan-rich ingredients and dietary supplementation with β-mannanase for low levels (0.05% and 0.10%) of dietary energy and protein, was carried out on 26-week-old Hy-Line Brown laying hens. The study showed that most production performance and egg quality were unaffected. Based on the results of Sharmila et al. (2014b) and Choi et al. (2021), we can conclude that the optimal PKM inclusion rate in poultry diets supplemented with β-xylanase varies according to production type and dose of enzyme. Furthermore, comparing the results of Sharmila et al. (2014b) with those reported by Sundu and Dingle (2003) reveals that the latter achieved good results with a PKM inclusion rate of 30%. In contrast, Sharmila et al. determined that an inclusion rate of 20% was optimal. One possible explanation for this discrepancy is the type of enzyme used: although xylan (2 % to 4 % dry matter) is an anti-nutritional component of PKM, mannan (30 % to 39 % dry matter) is the main constituent, which may account for the difference between the two studies.

However, Soltan (2009) found that adding a complex enzyme preparation containing amylase, protease, β-gluconase, lipase, xylanase, and cellulase to a diet containing 20% PKM reduced its negative effect on performance in broiler chickens. Furthermore, Chong et al. (2008) found that laying hens fed a diet containing 25% PKM and supplemented with an NSP enzyme containing mannanase, α-galactosidase, and protease had a lower FCR than control hens. However, the enzymes did improve dry matter digestibility and FCR compared to hens fed PKM without enzyme supplementation (Chong et al., 2008). Zubaidah et al. (2024) reported that a ration supplemented with 10% PKM and enzyme supplements (mannanase at 182 g/tonne, NSPase at 200 g/tonne, and protease at 130 g/tonne) improved the health status of the birds. Furthermore, Lee et al. (2013) and Abdollahi et al. (2016) reported that supplementing the rations of broiler and laying hens with PKM and β-mannase improved their productive performance. As mentioned above, the type of enzyme, dose and the production method (i.e. chicken breed used) can affect the rate at which PKM is incorporated. Furthermore, the source of the PKM, the age of the animals and the level of mannan in the PKM all affect the extent to which the PKM is incorporated into the animals' diet. Despite using an enzyme complex, Soltan (2009) achieved an incorporation rate of just 20%, which is lower than that reported by Sundu and Dingle (2003) at a conference. The latter used a mannanase containing only α-galactosidase enzyme. Furthermore, Chong et al. (2008) achieved 15% more PKM incorporation by using a enzyme complex containing mannanase and α-galactosidase, along with other enzymes, compared to Choi et al. (2021), who used a single enzyme (β-xylanase). The difference in PKM incorporation levels observed confirms the factors affecting PKM incorporation listed above. While this remains lower than the rate presented by Sundu and Dingle (2003), there has been a slight improvement in the production method (i.e. chicken breed used). In conclusion, using an enzyme complex containing specifically mannanase and α-galactosidase allows for the incorporation of more PKM, regardless of the production method.

However, according to Soltan (2009) and Shakila and Reddy (2012), there is limited information regarding the need for enzyme supplementation when high levels of PKM are included in poultry rations. Therefore, it is important to note that the effect of decreased productivity or egg quality can only be mitigated and measured by the addition of enzymes when there is a high incorporation of PKM in a poultry ration.

In light of the aforementioned results, it can be concluded that enzyme supplementation improves PKM utilization by animals (Table 4). Consequently, enzyme supplementation (single or combined) succeeds in breaking down macromolecular nutrients (MSNs), which explains the different results obtained. In addition to these results, differences between authors are observed. These differences can be explained by the enzyme dose used, the combination of enzymes involved, the species of animal used in the experiment, and the source of the PKM. Furthermore, the successful application of biotechnologically enhanced PKM in commercial production systems requires consideration of various feed processing factors, such as conditioning temperature and duration, granulation, pellet quality, and enzyme thermostability. These factors significantly impact nutrient availability and the efficacy of enzyme-based nutritional strategies. For example, Iravani et al. (2024) analysed the impact of conditioning temperature and duration on feed quality and broiler performance and found that, while high temperatures improve pellet quality, they also result in nutrient loss. However, Salahshour et al. (2023) concluded that conditioning starter feed for 60 seconds at 75°C improves the pellet durability index (PDI), as well as performance, villus height and AME. Conversely, the appropriate granulation temperatures and durations for grower and finisher feeds fall within the ranges of 60–65°C and 65–90 seconds, respectively. Taking all these parameters into account during poultry feed manufacturing ensures nutrient availability.Thus, the studies by Wang et al. (2023) provide optimal processing parameters for three techniques: extrusion, fermentation and enzymolysis. They also demonstrate that processing with PKC significantly improves the pellet quality, performance and gut microbiota of growing-finishing pigs. Furthermore, extrusion was found to be particularly effective in terms of feed compared to fermentation and enzymolysis. This result is likely due to the temperature to which PKC is subjected during extrusion. Roslan et al. (2017) state that the optimal extrusion temperature is 178°C, considerably higher than the temperatures used for fermentation or enzymolysis. Other studies have also shown that pelleting and β-mannanase supplementation can improve the digestibility of nutrients, nitrogen utilisation and metabolisable energy in alternative poultry feed ingredients (Keohavong et al., 2026). They explain that pelleting improves nutrient accessibility by physically modifying the feed structure, while β-mannanase improves nutrient utilisation by degrading β-mannan-rich NSPs. Additionally, Thongprajukaew et al. (2013) observed significant effects on the chemical composition of PKM through physical modifications (soaking and microwave irradiation). According to their results, the physical modification of PKM decreased CF content and increased available carbohydrate content, thereby promoting enzymatic hydrolysis. In vitro studies by these authors showed that digestive enzymes extracted from Nile tilapia were most efficient at hydrolysing carbohydrates from soaked PKM, while soaked or microwave-irradiated PKM was most suitable for striped snakefish. These in vitro results suggest that soaking and microwave irradiation could enhance the digestibility of PKM for certain economically significant freshwater fish species. Therefore, these two methods of improving the quality of PKM in aquaculture appear to have significant potential and could perhaps be extended to other animal species.

However, the observed improvement in production performance in both groups could partly be explained by a reduction in energy-intensive immune stimulation, which is associated with a decrease in the concentration of β-mannan in the digestive tract of poultry. Studies by Jackson et al. (2004), Hsiao et al. (2006) and Williams et al. (2014) have demonstrated that β-mannans are surface components of many pathogens and are recognized by animals as pathogen-associated antigens, thereby activating their innate immune system. This immune response, known as the feed-induced immune response, impairs animal performance by diverting energy from growth to this energy-intensive response (Hsiao et al., 2006; Williams et al., 2014). Therefore, adding β-mannanase hydrolyzes high-molecular-weight β-mannans into MOS, which are not recognized by the immune system. Consequently, diet-induced immune responses are reduced, saving energy for production. Several authors have demonstrated the beneficial effects of enzyme supplementation, specifically β-mannanase, in poultry rations (Lee et al., 2003; Daskiran et al., 2004; Jackson et al., 2004; Zou et al., 2006; Williams et al., 2014). Among these benefits, β-mannanase saves energy for production (Williams et al., 2014).

According to Sims et al. (2004), MOS offers benefits such as modification of the gut microbiota, reduced mucosal turnover rates, and modulation of the immune system in the intestinal lumen. Furthermore, MOS is reported to inhibit the adhesion of microorganisms to the cell wall, thereby increasing the immune response of microorganisms in the gut.

According to Yopi et al. (2006), MOS could potentially be used as dietary fibers and prebiotics, as they are indigestible oligosaccharides. Erdoğan et al. (2010) explain that prebiotics are indigestible oligosaccharide mixtures consisting of three to ten carbohydrate monomers that promote the growth, colonisation, or activity of probiotic microorganisms in the gut. However, Gourbeyre et al. (2011) argue that prebiotics can originate from various molecules, although the majority are dietary fibers such as oligosaccharides. The same authors also found that prebiotics influence the intestinal immune system by stimulating the metabolism of certain bacteria. However, Baurhoo et al. (2007) demonstrate that prebiotics impact feed efficiency by increasing the number and size of goblet cells in the jejunum of broiler chickens. Research by Brzóska et al. (2007) and Kathirvelan et al. (2012) reveals that probiotics and prebiotics can increase body and carcass weight and mortality rates in broiler chickens. Based on these results, Alkhalf et al. (2010) and Saad et al. (2013) have proposed using them as an alternative to antibiotics in animal production. Dalloul et al. (2003) state that probiotics containing Lactobacillus species confer resistance to pathogens such as E. coli, Salmonella, Campylobacter, and Eimeria cervulina. Furthermore, a previous study by Baba et al. (1993) involving three compounds (D-mannose, D-galactose and α-methyl-D-glucoside) showed that only D-mannose prevented pathogens from adhering to the intestinal mucosal surface. However, the mannans in PKM consist of a main chain of β-(1,4)-D-mannose. Therefore, if D-mannose alone inhibits pathogen adhesion, the hydrolysis of PKM by an enzyme should increase PKM concentrations in broiler chicken feed, providing a source of prebiotics for probiotic microorganisms.

Although the enzymatic and microbial degradation of non-starchy PKM polysaccharides successfully releases functional β-mannan-derived MOS in vitro, there is a significant discrepancy between these biochemical mechanisms and their efficacy in vivo. Although commercial MOS derived from yeast cell walls (e.g. Saccharomyces cerevisiae) consistently exhibit prebiotic, immunomodulatory and pathogen-excluding properties in poultry, there is limited direct empirical evidence evaluating MOS specifically generated by PKM hydrolysis. The structural heterogeneity, degree of polymerisation and purity of PKM-derived MOS may differ significantly from that of commercial alternatives, which could influence their fermentation kinetics and modulation of the gut microbiota in vivo. Therefore, while the production of MOS represents a promising source of added value for PKM, its ability to consistently improve the performance and intestinal integrity of broiler chickens remains to be proven through rigorous and targeted in vivo trials.

According to Chen et al. (2015a), the gut bacterial community plays a significant role in maintaining intestinal function. However, maintaining digestive function and nutrient absorption requires intestinal structure integrity and microbiota. Aside from MOS obtained during the enzymatic hydrolysis of PKM, which can act as prebiotics or probiotics, plant essential oils also play this role when used as feed additives. While there is no direct link between the use of essential oils and MOS from PKM, adding feed additives is another biotechnology strategy. Combining these processes would be advantageous for feed valorisation.

Aside from the improved nutritional value by SSF, research by Sundu et al. (2021) concluded that broiler chickens fed a diet containing 20% unfermented PKM were smaller than control birds fed diets containing either 10% or 20% Aspergillus niger-fermented PKM. Furthermore, Rizal et al. (2011) conducted studies on broiler chickens fed PKC fermented with Aspergillus niger, demonstrating good animal growth and the beneficial effects of fermenting PKC with Aspergillus niger. Other work by Mirnawati et al. (2020) suggests that incorporating 25% PKM fermented with Bacillus into the feed of broiler chickens has no negative effects. However, laying hens fed PKM that had undergone anaerobic fermentation for eight days exhibited improved FI and stabilized egg production (Dairo and Fasuyi, 2008). The differences observed among authors may be attributed to the microorganisms used to ferment PKM. Bacteria rapidly degrade antinutritional factors, whereas fungi break down stubborn lignin-cellulose networks. However, in PKM, lignin is not the predominant component, unlike in other materials. This explains the results obtained by these authors. Given the wealth of literature on SSF currently available, the research of Olukomaiya et al. (2019); Dawood and Koshio (2020) and Yang et al. (2021) confirms that SSF effectively reduces antinutritional factors and improves the nutritional components and functional properties of feed resources. To provide a broader perspective on fermentation, Li et al. (2022) first examined the in vitro fermentation behaviour of blackberry polysaccharides (BBPs) of different molar masses. They found that molar mass did not influence the final bioaccessibility of BBPs or their ability to produce short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) during fermentation. Furthermore, high-molecular-weight BBPs were extensively degraded during fermentation, generating lower-molecular-weight polysaccharide fragments with a lower utilisation rate. After 24 hours of fermentation, adding BBPs changed the diversity of the bacterial community, primarily resulting in an increased Bacteroidetes-to-Firmicutes ratio. Additionally, low-molecular-weight BBPs modulated the gut microbiota more rapidly than high-molecular-weight BBPs due to their higher utilization rate. In the fermentation process or enzymatic supplementation of PKM, however, high-molecular-weight β-mannans are degraded into low-molecular-weight MOS, which are easily assimilated. Based on these results, we can conclude that the degradation of β-mannans into MOS can modulate the gut microbiota.

In addition to these biotechnological methods, there are complementary nutritional strategies that enhance nutrient utilisation. According to Son et al. (2013) and Almaguer et al. (2014), the phosphorus (P) concentration in PKM ranges from 0.5% to 0.65%. According to these authors, 60–75% of the total P is bound to phytates, and the standardised total tract digestibility (STTD) of P in these products ranges from 35% to 50%. STTD can be increased to between 60% and 75% by adding microbial phytase to the diet given the relatively high phytate concentration (Almaguer et al., 2014). In conclusion, the authors state that the digestible P supply from PKM is comparable to that of soybean meal when microbial phytase is added to the diet. Although the lignin content is low compared to β-mannan, it affects the texture of PKM. Moreover, efficient P utilisation requires its release from the phytate complex. As we can see, PKM contains numerous anti-nutritional factors. However, in poultry nutrition, calcium (Ca) and P are closely linked minerals that interact at digestive, metabolic and skeletal levels such that the level of one directly affects the absorption and utilisation of the other. As previously mentioned, 60–75% of the total P in PKM is bound to phytates. Consequently, using PKM in poultry diets requires this mineral to be made available, given its important role in conjunction with other minerals, particularly Ca. Studies by Salehi et al. (2025) concluded that using phytase in laying hen diets improves feed conversion ratio, as well as eggshell and tibia quality. Furthermore, diets containing phytase and Ca carbonate particles of specific sizes were found to increase blood Ca levels. Lim et al. (2020) attribute these results to phytase hydrolysing phytates in the feed, promoting the digestion and absorption of Ca and P. Although these studies were not conducted on PKM, cereal grains also contain a high proportion of P (60–70%) in the form of phytic acid (Abbasi et al., 2019). Consequently, these results are applicable to PKM. Due to the chelating effect of phytates on minerals, trace elements and proteins, making these nutrients difficult for the intestine to digest and absorb, Mijiyawa et al. (2026) conducted a study on the digestibility of PKM in broiler chickens of different ages. The study used feeds containing either raw PKM or PKM with added phytase (1,000 FTU/kg). Their results showed that age and diet affect the apparent ileal digestibility and SID of certain amino acids. However, regardless of age, the addition of phytase did not improve the digestibility of PKM amino acids. The authors suggested that the low incorporation of the enzyme (0.005%) used in the feed could explain the lack of improvement in amino acid digestibility. Another possibility is that mannan, the main NSP constituent of PKM (Düsterhöft et al., 1991; Abdollahi et al., 2016), could prevent phytase from accessing its site of action by trapping phytate. Furthermore, in an effort to combat the NSP of certain ingredients (corn, wheat, barley, soybean meal, rapeseed meal, PKM, cottonseed meal, copra meal and distillers' dried grains with solubles) in pig feed, Kong et al. (2015) tested the effects of adding EC (containing a combination of Aspergillus niger-derived xylanase, protease and phytase at concentrations of 100, 700 and 300 units/g) on dry matter digestibility in vitro. They found that digestibility varied depending on the ingredient tested and the method used. In light of these results, the effect of phytase on the digestibility of PKM amino acids, particularly in combination with a NSP enzyme, should be studied in more detail.

In terms of the advantages of using biotransformed PKM, Qamar et al. (2024) studies on the combined application of metabolome and metagenome analyses revealed the biosynthetic pathways of novel antioxidant peptides in FPKM. The study emphasised the potential of Lactobacillus fermentation to improve the bioactivity of PKM by promoting the production and release of antioxidant peptides via the enzymatic breakdown of complex carbohydrates and lignocellulosic structures. The results clearly demonstrate that FPKM improves nutritional value by increasing the levels of probiotics, enzymes and beneficial antioxidants while reducing antinutritional factors. This makes FPKM a valuable ingredient in animal feed and a promoter of intestinal health in animals. It is also a multifunctional food component or ingredient in sustainable, plant-based diets for human consumption and a potential culture substrate in the food industry. Furthermore, Mi et al. (2025) demonstrate in their studies that co-fermentation with Aspergillus niger and mannanase effectively improves the nutritional value of PKC. In vivo studies of this co-fermentation have shown that incorporating FPKC into feed improves nutrient digestibility and modulates rumen microbial composition in cattle. This is characterised by a decrease in butyric acid and ammonia nitrogen (NH₃-N) levels without impacting the production of monounsaturated proteins (MCPs) and total volatile fatty acids (TVFAs). This confirms its potential as a valuable alternative feed ingredient. Based on these studies, biotechnologically processed PKM appears to be linked to increased antioxidant levels, enhanced immune function and improved intestinal integrity. While this review focuses on the nutritional benefits of PKM valorisation, there are other biotechnology-based nutritional interventions. Studies in recent years have emphasised the importance of functional feed additives in enhancing antioxidant levels, immune function, intestinal integrity, gut microbiota balance, liver health and resistance to nutritional challenges. For instance, Taghizadeh et al. (2024) were the first to demonstrate the beneficial effects of the combined administration of cholecalciferol (VD) and Satureja rechingeri essential oil (SREO) on the performance and immune system of broiler chickens. According to these authors, this combined diet, at appropriate concentrations, favourably reduced blood triglycerides (TG) and cholesterol (CHO) in chickens. They concluded that adding 200 mg/kg of SREO and 0.1 mg/kg of VD produced favourable results in all treatment groups, potentially leading to the production of a greater number of healthier chickens. Furthermore, Vakili et al. (2025b) found that adding an absorbent compound (bentonite, yeast cell wall extract or saffron petal extract) to toxins mitigated the adverse effects of aflatoxin B1 and ochratoxin A. In addition, saffron petal extract could be used to counteract the effects of toxins in animal feed. The detrimental effect of these toxins on animal feed is well known. Furthermore, improper storage of FPKM during SSF can lead to the development of such toxins, consequently impairing animal performance. Vakili et al. (2025b) therefore state that the most effective dose is 750 mg/kg of saffron petal extract combined with 0.1% yeast cell wall extract and 1% transformed bentonite. Another study by Vakili et al. (2025a) suggests that including 80 ppm of saffron petal extract in the diet of laying hens could provide a functional food source that attenuates cholesterol levels in egg yolk and blood serum while reducing faecal ammonia emissions. Based on these results, we believe that adding these feed additives to PKM, either with enzyme supplementation or after SSF, would be more beneficial for the animals.

Techno-economic challenges and limitations of scaling up

In the face of rising feed costs, it is crucial to identify alternative feeds that are highly nutritious, do not negatively impact performance or costs, and can reduce reliance on corn and soybean meal in poultry feed. This would simultaneously increase the availability of these ingredients for human consumption or biofuel production. As previously mentioned, AIBP are a plentiful and inexpensive alternative to conventional monogastric feed ingredients, such as corn and soybeans. According to Sugiharto et al. (2026), incorporating them promotes sustainability by reducing feed costs, mitigating competition between human and animal feed, and supporting the circular bioeconomy by valorising waste. However, the presence of complex NSPs, lignocellulosic structures, phytates, mannans and other anti-nutritional factors can limit nutrient digestibility and impair intestinal health and reduce animal growth performance when these by-products are used in large quantities (Ravindran and Blair, 1992). Biotechnology, such as the addition of exogenous enzymes or SSF, can effectively mitigate these antinutritional effects, thereby improving intestinal health, growth performance and digestibility. These improvements have led to a considerable increase in the incorporation rate of AIBP. From an economic standpoint, enzyme supplementation often offsets its initial cost through improved feed efficiency, resulting in lower production costs per kilogram of broiler chicken meat (reductions of 7–12% have been observed in several studies) (Sugiharto et al., 2026). Furthermore, techno-economic evaluations indicate that upgrading raw PKM enables higher inclusion rates in poultry diets without negatively impacting performance, effectively offsetting the costs of including soybean meal (Abdollahi et al., 2016). Depending on local enzyme sourcing and energy logistics, replacing a portion of soybean meal with enzymatically pre-treated or FPKM can optimise feed formulation costs. However, opting for direct enzymatic hydrolysis may negate the economic advantage of PKM over soybean meal due to the cost of purchasing commercial mannanases or xylanases.

Studies by Liu et al. (2025) demonstrated a significant enhancement in the nutritional value of PKC through enzymatic hydrolysis over a 12-hour period. In this study, we found that 10 g of PKC required 1 g of mannanase and 0.1 g of xylanase for hydrolysis. Although the PKC value improved, purchasing enzymes in large quantities is not cost-effective. Furthermore, co-fermentation in the same study also demonstrated an improvement in PKC, and the in vivo study produced significant results. In SSF, increasing the substrate volume (PKC/PKM) restricts the dissipation of metabolic heat and the supply of oxygen to the core of the mass. Consequently, this creates temperature gradients that destroy the enzymes or microbial strains used. Unlike submerged liquid fermentation (SLF), strict sterilisation of large quantities of solid PKM is energy-intensive and difficult, increasing the risk of large-scale proliferation of undesirable flora. SSF requires high humidity, often with a water-to-substrate ratio of between 1:1 and 1:2.5. Even though the water content in the study by Liu et al. (2025) was only 20%, incorporating this valuable PKC into poultry feed while ensuring microbiological stability required drying. The energy cost of linking water to the industrial dryer is the main economic constraint. There are several challenges to overcome, particularly with regard to the variability in PKM composition. PKM can vary considerably in fibre content (NDF/ADF), residual lipids (depending on whether it is extracted by pressing or solvent) and hull contamination (Azizi et al., 2021). This biological variability complicates the reproducibility of industrial enzyme kinetics, the specificity and stability of the enzymes, and can lead to inconsistent results from one trial to another and seasonal fluctuations in quality.

However, Olukomaiya et al. (2019) state that SSF is an inexpensive biotransformation technique that can enhance the nutritional value of alternative protein sources. Furthermore, Ong et al. (2024) successfully applied the isolated Bacillus subtilis F6 strain to the fermentation of PKM in their study. This resulted in a significant decrease of at least 10% in NDF content after overnight fermentation. Mannanase activity was detected after just six hours of incubation, indicating a rapid response to contact with PKM. Thus, this B. subtilis F6 strain represents a promising solution for efficiently degrading PKM fibres in a short fermentation period, thereby reducing the contamination and production cost risks mentioned above. According to the same authors, advances in genetic engineering could further improve fibre degradation without requiring prolonged fermentation periods. The characterisation and identification of the GmuG mannanase involved in the hydrolysis of PKM fibres in this study lays the groundwork for future modifications of the bacterial strain.

Although fermentation improves PKM by increasing protein and decreasing fibre, it still presents limiting factors, such as sulfur-containing amino acids (Muangkeow and Chinajariyawong, 2009). Thus, in industrial-scale production, the incorporation of PKM is often limited by lower-cost formulation software due to these imbalances, which limits the return on investment of processing facilities. According to Olukomaiya et al. (2019), there is limited data on the use of alternative protein sources derived from SSF in broiler chicken feed, which necessitates further investigation into their nutritional value, particularly in terms of digestibility and nutrient utilisation. These authors argue that such studies would enhance the competitiveness of these alternative protein sources, offering a promising way for the poultry industry to sustainably meet the growing global demand for animal protein.

As discussed above, transitioning bioprocesses from laboratory settings to industrial animal feed production facilities involves strict regulatory requirements and safety risks. The main risks associated with solid-state fermented feeds are potential microbial contamination and the proliferation of opportunistic pathogens during incubation. There is also a risk of mycotoxin accumulation in the absence of strict humidity and aeration control (Canibe and Jensen, 2012). It is therefore important to implement industrial quality control (QC) protocols incorporating automated monitoring of parameters such as pH, temperature gradients, moisture content and spore density. Additionally, regulatory frameworks such as the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) in the EU or the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) guidelines require full characterisation of fermentative strains, including GRAS (Generally Recognised as Safe) or QPS (Qualified Presumption of Safety) certification, as well as rigorous screening to ensure the absence of antibiotic resistance genes and enterotoxins in the final product (Rychen et al., 2018).

Finally, from an environmental standpoint, the biotransformation of PKM is crucial for promoting circular agriculture and waste valorisation. Life-cycle assessment (LCA) studies show that replacing imported soybean meal with locally produced or processed PKM reduces the carbon footprint (CO₂-equivalent per kg of live poultry weight) by mitigating the impacts of land use change (LUC) associated with soy expansion (Van Zanten et al., 2018). Valorisation efficiency is further maximised when SSF utilises AIBPs without competing for arable land. Quantitative LCA indicators show that incorporating upcycled PKM into poultry rations can reduce the overall global warming potential (GWP) of feed production when processing operations are optimised (Van Zanten et al., 2018).

Conclusion and future perspectives

To reduce reliance on traditional raw materials, integrating PKM into feed remains a viable alternative, significantly supporting the promotion of a circular economy in tropical regions. Although historically regarded as a low-quality ingredient due to its nutritional profile, this review highlights that PKM, when processed using biotechnological methods, offers dual advantages: lowering production costs and enhancing the intestinal health of poultry, all while contributing to sustainable poultry farming. Given these findings, PKM is anticipated to gain wider acceptance in the coming years, evolving from a mere feed supplement to a strategic raw material, driven by advancements in biotechnology. However, bridging the gap between experimental efficacy and commercial viability requires distinguishing between proven achievements and current operational expectations. Although the biological capacity to upgrade PKM is well-established, technological and economic bottlenecks continue to hinder its widespread industrial adoption. Substantial hurdles that temper immediate industrial optimism include high enzyme production costs, energy-intensive post-fermentation drying, batch-to-batch substrate variability, and process scalability. In order to transition biotransformed PKM from a promising laboratory concept to a reliable commercial feedstuff, future research must focus on four priority areas:

1.Optimisation of synergistic enzyme cocktails: Moving beyond single-enzyme treatments to identify the most effective combinations of β-mannanases, xylanases and cellulases, specifically tailored to the structural heterogeneity of PKM cell walls.
