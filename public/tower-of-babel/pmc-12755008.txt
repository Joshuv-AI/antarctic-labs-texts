Adverse childhood experiences and late-life depression in older Chinese adults: the mediating roles of sleep disorders and chronic diseases
Authors: Qianqian Li, Leqin Chen, Ronghui Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdverse childhood experiences (ACEs) are linked to depression in later life; however, the mediating pathways in older Chinese adults remain underexplored. Sleep disorders, highly prevalent in geriatrics, may mediate this pathway through chronic stress mechanisms, yet evidence from Asian cohorts is limited. This study examines the mediating roles of sleep duration, chronic diseases, and other factors in the ACEs-depression associations using a nationally representative sample.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional analysis of data from 6,585 adults aged 60 years and older, obtained from the 2020 wave of the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). ACEs (range 0–12) were assessed via a 12-item scale, and depression via the CES-D-10 (cutoff ≥ 12). Mediators included sleep duration, chronic diseases, and social isolation. Multivariable logistic regression and causal mediation analysis (1,000 bootstraps) were used, adjusting for relevant covariates.ResultsThe prevalence of depressive symptoms was 32.1%. Each additional ACE increased depression risk (adjusted OR = 1.17, 95% CI = 1.11–1.24). Sleep duration mediated 9.4% (β=-0.09) and chronic diseases 4.3% (β = 0.09) of the total associations; social isolation showed negligible mediation (0.03%). Stronger ACEs-depression associations occurred in Central China (OR = 1.35 vs. 1.15 in West, p-interaction = 0.029) and higher-educated groups.ConclusionsACEs are associated with increased late-life depression risk in older Chinese adults, partially mediated by short sleep duration and chronic diseases. These findings suggest the potential value of ACEs screening and targeted interventions addressing sleep and multimorbidity in geriatric care. Longitudinal studies are needed to confirm these pathways.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-025-06740-9.

Introduction

Adverse childhood experiences (ACEs), encompassing abuse, neglect, and household dysfunction, exert profound and lasting effects on both mental and physical health across the lifespan [1, 2]. Depression, a leading global cause of disability, shows particularly high prevalence among older adults, significantly diminishing quality of life and increasing mortality risk [3]. Growing evidence positions ACEs as a critical determinant of late-life depressive symptoms [4, 5], yet the roles of modifiable biological, behavioral, and psychosocial factors in this relationship remain inadequately explored, especially in non-Western populations.

China is confronting pressing public health challenges driven by rapid population aging and a concurrent rise in depression rates among older adults. While ACEs-depression associationss are established in various populations, few studies have focused on Chinese elders, particularly those exposed to unique historical adversities such as mid-20th century warfare and famine [6, 7]. This study addresses this gap by investigating the dose-response relationship between ACEs and late-life depressive symptoms in China, with a balanced examination of three mechanistically distinct, potentially modifiable pathways.

Guided by the biopsychosocial model and life-course frameworks of ACEs [8], we examined three modifiable factors representing distinct pathways: (1) Chronic Disease Burden (allostatic load pathway). ACEs may trigger persistent physiological dysregulation, such as HPA axis dysfunction and chronic inflammation, accelerating multimorbidity (e.g., cardiovascular and metabolic conditions) [9]. This increases depression risk through functional limitations, pain, and treatment burden [10, 11], potentially amplified in Chinese elders with famine exposure due to metabolic programming alterations [12]. (2) Sleep Duration (neurobehavioral pathway). Childhood adversity may disrupt circadian regulation via glucocorticoid receptor changes and HPA axis dysregulation [13, 14], leading to short sleep duration or insomnia in later life. These disturbances impair emotional regulation and prefrontal function, heightening depression vulnerability [15], which may be especially relevant in China’s wartime-exposed cohorts with lifelong sleep issues [16]. (3) Social Isolation (psychosocial pathway). ACEs can undermine social skills and attachment security, increasing later-life isolation [17, 18], which elevates depression risk through reduced support, cognitive disengagement, and heightened stress [19].

Based on these biopsychosocial pathways, we hypothesized that ACEs are associated with late-life depression, partially mediated by: (1) reduced sleep duration (neurobehavioral pathway), (2) increased chronic diseases (allostatic load pathway), and (3) heightened social isolation (psychosocial pathway).

By analyzing data from the nationally representative China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), this research aims to provide evidence for targeted interventions addressing these modifiable pathways to reduce the burden of late-life depressive symptoms in China’s aging population.

Research methods

Data source

This study utilized data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), a nationally representative longitudinal survey of Chinese adults aged ≥ 45 years. Data quality was maintained through the use of trained interviewers and standardized data collection protocols [20]. Briefly, the baseline survey (2011–2012) employed multistage stratified probability proportional-to-size (PPS) sampling, recruiting 17,708 individuals from 10,257 households across 450 communities in 150 counties/districts spanning 28 provinces. Follow-up waves occurred in 2013, 2015, 2018, and 2020.

The 2014 Life History Survey collected retrospective data on childhood experiences from surviving participants interviewed in 2011 and 2013. Our analysis focused on participants aged ≥ 60 years in the 2020 wave who completed both the 2014 and 2020 surveys and had complete data on depressive symptoms, ACEs, mediators, and covariates (N = 6,585 after exclusions for missing primary variables; see Fig. 1 for participant flow).Fig. 1Schematic diagram of the screening process of research subjects

CHARLS received ethical approval from the Peking University Institutional Review Board (IRB00001052-11015), and all participants provided written informed consent. As this is a secondary analysis of publicly available de-identified data, no additional ethical approval was required.

Measurement

Independent variable: ACEs

ACEs were assessed retrospectively using a 12-item scale [2]: seven conventional indicators (e.g., physical abuse, emotional neglect, household substance abuse, mental illness, domestic violence, incarcerated household member, parental separation/divorce) [21]; two expanded indicators (unsafe neighborhood, bullying) [22]; and three novel indicators (parental death, sibling death, parental disability) [23]. Detailed items and definitions are in Supplementary Table 1. Each item was dichotomized (yes/no), and a cumulative score (range 0–12) was calculated and categorized as 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, or ≥ 5 ACEs. Data quality was ensured through trained interviewers and standardized protocols [20].

Dependent variable: depressive symptoms

Depressive symptoms were measured using the 10-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D-10), assessing symptom frequency over the past week. Items included feeling bothered, trouble concentrating, feeling depressed, everything requiring effort, feeling hopeful (reverse-scored), feeling fearful, restless sleep, feeling happy (reverse-scored), feeling lonely, and unable to get going. Scoring: <1 day = 0; 1–2 days = 1; 3–4 days = 2; 5–7 days = 3. Total scores ranged from 0 to 30, with higher scores indicating greater severity. Depression was dichotomized as CES-D-10 ≥ 12 (yes/no). The CES-D-10 has demonstrated good reliability and validity globally [24–26] and in Chinese populations [26, 27].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Python 3.9 (packages: statsmodels 0.13.2, SciPy 1.8.0), with mediation analysis validated in R 4.2.0 using the mediation package (version 4.5.0). Descriptive statistics summarized the sample using frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. Group differences in depression prevalence were tested using Pearson’s χ² test (for categorical variables) and the Kruskal–Wallis test (for ordinal or non-normally distributed continuous variables). A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Primary association analysis

To examine the associations between ACEs and late-life depression, multivariable logistic regression models were constructed sequentially, with increasing adjustment for potential confounders:

Model 0: Unadjusted associations (ACEs only);

Model 1: Adjusted for demographic factors (age, sex);

Model 2: Additional adjustment for socioeconomic factors (marital status, education, rural residence, region, health insurance, pension insurance, household consumption per capita in quartiles);

Model 3: Further inclusion of behavioral factors (smoking status, alcohol consumption, physical activity measured by MET-hours/week, sleep duration);

Model 4: Full adjustment with clinical factors (number of chronic diseases, social isolation level);

Results are reported as odds ratios (ORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Multicollinearity was assessed using Variance Inflation Factors (VIF). All covariates in the fully adjusted model (Model 4) had VIF values below 3, indicating negligible multicollinearity.

Mediation analysis

Mediator selection

Three mediators were selected a priori based on established biopsychosocial pathways linking ACEs to depression, theoretical relevance, and preliminary bivariate associationss (all p < 0.001 with both ACEs and depression): (1) chronic disease burden (allostatic load pathway); (2) sleep duration (neurobehavioral pathway); and (3) social isolation (psychosocial pathway). These align with temporal ordering assumptions (ACEs preceding mediators, which precede depression). Variables such as smoking or marital status were treated as confounders rather than mediators, as they primarily act as shared risk factors rather than intermediary mechanisms.

Analytical approach

Causal mediation analysis was conducted within a counterfactual framework to estimate mediation effects, using the following steps: (1) For each mediator, two regression models were fitted: (i) Mediator model: linear regression of ACEs predicting the mediator, adjusted for all non-mediator covariates; (ii) Outcome model: logistic regression of depression predicted by the mediator and ACEs, adjusted for all non-mediator covariates. (2) The Average Causal Mediation Effect (ACME; indirect effect), Average Direct Effect (ADE), total effect, and proportion mediated were estimated using the product-of-coefficients approach. (3) Statistical significance and 95% CIs were determined using bias-corrected bootstrap confidence intervals based on 1,000 resamples to account for non-normality. (4) All models were adjusted for the same set of 12 covariates (age, sex, marital status, education, residence, region, health insurance, household consumption, pension insurance, smoking, alcohol use, and physical activity). Key assumptions (e.g., no unmeasured confounding, no mediator-outcome confounders affected by exposure) were approximated through covariate adjustment, but violations due to the cross-sectional design (e.g., potential bidirectionality) are acknowledged as limitations.

Effect modification assessment

To evaluate potential effect modification, the following steps were implemented: (1) Subgroup variable selection: Covariates were considered if they showed statistically significant (p < 0.05) or marginally significant (p < 0.10) interaction terms in preliminary fully adjusted models, or if theoretically relevant (e.g., physical activity due to its neurobiological links to depression resilience). Variables with high collinearity (VIF ≥ 5) were excluded to avoid model instability. (2) Interaction testing: Multiplicative product terms (e.g., ACEs × residence) were added to the fully adjusted logistic regression models (Model 4). Interaction effects were formally tested using Wald tests, with a significance threshold of α = 0.05 (two-tailed). Stratified analyses were performed for significant modifiers to report subgroup-specific ORs and mediation proportions.

Sensitivity analyses

To assess robustness, mediation analyses were repeated on the larger dataset before excluding cases with missing covariates (n = 8,928). Additionally, alternative categorizations of sleep duration were tested to evaluate sensitivity to measurement choices.

Results

Descriptive statistics

The analytic sample (N = 6,585) consisted of 3,283 males (49.9%) and 3,302 females (50.1%), with a mean age of ~ 68 years (categorized as 60–64: 26.5%, 65–69: 31.5%, 70–74: 21.4%, ≥ 75: 20.6%). The prevalence of depressive symptoms was 32.1% (n = 2,112), higher among females (38.9% vs. 25.2% males, p < 0.001), rural residents (37.2% vs. 23.2% urban, p < 0.001), and those with lower education (38.4% under primary vs. 17.7% high school+, p < 0.001). No differences by pension insurance (p = 0.154).

Depression was associated with shorter sleep (< 6 h: 44.0% vs. 7–8 h: 21.4%, p < 0.001), more chronic conditions (≥ 5: 38.7% vs. 0: 23.9%, p < 0.001), and higher ACEs (≥ 5: 52.9% vs. 0: 25.9%, p < 0.001). Health behaviors showed inverse associationss: non-drinkers (35.5% vs. 25.5% drinkers, p < 0.001), smokers (33.6% vs. 27.6% non-smokers, p < 0.001), and low physical activity (< 600 MET-min/week: 36.2% vs. >3000: 32.3%, p < 0.001). Significant differences by depression status were observed for most variables (all p < 0.001 except age p = 0.440; see Table 1 for full descriptives).

An examination of the characteristics associated with depression status (Table 1) further clarifies the profile of depressive symptoms in this cohort. The prevalence of depression was markedly higher among those with shorter sleep durations, rising to 44.0% in individuals sleeping less than 6 h per day compared to 21.4% in those with 7–8 h of sleep. A clear gradient was also observed with chronic disease burden, where the depression prevalence increased from 23.9% among those with no chronic conditions to 38.7% for those with five or more. Furthermore, a strong dose-response relationship was evident with ACE exposure: individuals with five or more ACEs had a depression prevalence of 52.9%, double that of those with no ACEs (25.9%). These patterns underscore the close associations between late-life depression and sleep disturbances, multimorbidity, and a history of childhood adversity in this population.

The relationship between ACEs and depression risk

As detailed in Table 2, a dose-response relationship was observed between ACEs and depression risk across all hierarchical regression models. The association remained statistically significant (p < 0.001) after sequential adjustment for demographic, socioeconomic, behavioral, and clinical covariates, with the fully adjusted odds ratio (OR) being 1.17 (95% CI: 1.11–1.24) per additional ACE.

ACEs were linked to mediators: reduced sleep duration (β=−0.09, 95% CI=−0.12 to −0.06), increased chronic conditions (β = 0.09, 95% CI = 0.05–0.13), and slightly higher social isolation (β = 0.01, 95% CI=−0.01 to 0.02). Restricted cubic splines confirmed linearity (p-nonlinearity > 0.05; Fig. 2).

As shown in Fig. 4, ACEs were associated with an elevated risk of depression across all subgroups (all p < 0.001). However, the strength of this association was not uniform. The association was strongest in Central China, where each additional ACE was associated with a 35% increase in depression odds (OR = 1.35, 95% CI: 1.23–1.47).

Similarly, the association was more pronounced among participants with higher educational attainment (e.g., OR = 1.27–1.28) compared to those with lower education (OR = 1.14).

Discussion

Key findings

In our sample of 6,585 older Chinese adults, the prevalence of depressive symptoms was 32.1%, with notable disparities by gender (females 38.9% vs. males 25.2%), residence (rural 37.2% vs. urban 23.2%), and marital status (without spouse 40.7% vs. with 29.5%). Notably, 79.6% reported ≥ 1 ACE, aligning with global estimates [31–33]. A linear dose-response association was observed (fully adjusted OR = 1.17 per ACE, 95% CI = 1.11–1.24, p < 0.001), which remained consistent across statistical adjustments [32–35].

This association was primarily direct but was partially mediated through distinct biopsychosocial pathways: sleep duration (mediating 9.4%) and chronic conditions (mediating 4.3%), while social isolation showed negligible mediation (≤ 0.6%). The specific mediation parameters were as follows: for sleep duration (β=−0.09 for ACEs-sleep association; OR = 0.80–0.82 for sleep-depression association), for chronic conditions (β = 0.09; OR = 1.10 per condition), and for social isolation (β = 0.00–0.01.00.01; OR = 1.07–1.15, p > 0.25).

Effect modification analysis revealed significant variation by geographic region (p-interaction = 0.029), with the strongest association in Central China (OR = 1.35, 95% CI = 1.23–1.47) compared to Western China (OR = 1.15), and a marginal trend by education level (p-interaction = 0.08), showing stronger associations among those with higher education (OR = 1.27–1.28) versus lower education (OR = 1.14). No significant effect modification was found for residence, marital status, or gender (all p-interaction >0.15). These findings highlight the complex multi-level nature of ACEs’ impact on late-life depression, spanning individual biological pathways to broader societal contextual factors [36–38].

Comparison with existing literature

Findings align with evidence linking ACEs to adult depression [39], extending to late life in a Chinese context. The dose-response pattern supports cumulative burden models [2, 40]. The observed depression prevalence (32.1%) and its sociodemographic disparities are consistent with other studies in China [41–45].

To move beyond isolated associations and integrate our findings regarding mediators and moderators, we interpret these results through a socio-ecological framework. The empirical pathways established in Fig. 3 provide the foundation for this theoretical model, situating individual-level mediators within a broader context of interpersonal and societal influences.

At the individual and life-course level, the robust direct association of ACEs with depression, along with the significant mediation through chronic conditions and sleep duration, strongly echoes biopsychosocial and allostatic load models [40, 46–49]. This underscores the long-term biological embedding of childhood adversity via physiological dysregulation and neurobehavioral disruption, representing a direct, internalized pathway from early stress to late-life mental health.

In contrast, at the interpersonal level, the negligible mediation through social isolation presents a notable divergence from some Western studies that emphasize this psychosocial pathway [50]. This suggests that for this generation of older Chinese adults, the internal, physiological sequelae of childhood adversity may constitute a more salient pathway to depression than their contemporaneous social network characteristics [17, 18]. This may reflect cultural norms, cohort-specific resilience strategies, or the possibility that ACEs impair the ability to form supportive bonds later in life.

Finally, the community and societal levels are implicated by the significant effect modification. The stronger association in Central China likely reflects macro-level contextual disparities, such as in economic development and healthcare resource distribution [36, 51]. The trend toward a stronger association among those with higher education challenges conventional protective assumptions and may indicate that greater cognitive awareness or different stress perceptions can shape the psychological impact of early adversity [37, 38, 52], confirming that ACEs’ impacts transcend purely material factors. This multi-level perspective underscores that the connection between ACEs and late-life depression is not a simple direct line but is shaped by the interplay of lifelong biological embedding and contemporary socio-ecological contexts.

Implications for geriatric care and policy

Based on our findings, we propose the following concrete recommendations for clinical practice and public policy.Routine ACEs Screening in Geriatric Care: We recommend the integration of brief, sensitive ACEs screening tools into the primary health assessments of older adults in China. This is particularly crucial for identifying high-risk individuals, such as those with multiple chronic conditions or sleep complaints, for whom a history of ACEs may underlie their current health burden.Targeted Intervention on Modifiable Mediators: Our results highlight sleep duration and chronic disease burden as intervenable pathways. In clinical practice, this translates to:1) Implementing sleep health promotion programs, including cognitive-behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I), as a component of mental health prevention for older adults with a history of adversity. 2) Strengthening integrated care models that concurrently manage chronic physical diseases and mental health in geriatric patients, recognizing their intertwined nature [53].Policy-Level Actions to Address Disparities: To mitigate the stronger ACEs-depression associations observed in Central China and among certain subgroups, resource allocation should be prioritized [36, 51]. This includes: (1) Expanding the coverage and accessibility of mental health services in under-served regions through the training of primary care physicians and the development of telepsychiatry platforms. (2) Incorporating ACEs-aware principles into national healthy aging policies and guidelines for the management of multimorbidity, ensuring a trauma-informed approach to elder care.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths

Strengths include the large, nationally representative sample, comprehensive ACEs assessment (including novel types relevant to the Chinese context), rigorous adjustment for confounders, examination of key moderators (sleep, social isolation, chronic disease), and robust sensitivity analyses.

Limitations

Cross-sectional Design and Recall Bias: ACEs were assessed retrospectively in older adulthood, making the study susceptible to recall bias, especially for subtle forms like emotional neglect. Although our mediation analysis was grounded in well-established life-course theories, the data cannot definitively confirm the pathway from ACEs to mediators and then to depression. For instance, it is plausible that current depression influences the recall of ACEs (recall bias), or that the onset of chronic diseases and sleep disturbances in adulthood preceded and contributed to the development of depression, creating potential bidirectional or reverse-causality relationships that our study design cannot disentangle. Future research

Prospective Multimodal Cohorts: Track ACEs, sleep (using objective measures like PSG), biomarkers (inflammation, HPA axis function, epigenetics), neuroimaging (e.g., resting-state fMRI for social isolation associations), and depression across life stages to elucidate mechanisms and causality.

Intervention Trials: Test targeted interventions (e.g., CBT-I vs. sleep extension protocols for different ACEs/sleep profiles; digital platforms for real-time social isolation monitoring and tailored support; AI-enhanced counseling for underserved areas) specifically in high-ACEs older adults; Validate trauma-awareness counseling in high-education seniors exhibiting amplified ACEs associations; Design cognitive-emotional regulation training to counteract distress hyperawareness.

Methodological Refinements: Use life-history calendars anchored to historical events to improve the accuracy of ACEs recall in older adults. Develop and validate culturally adapted ACEs tools. Employ Mendelian randomization to explore the causal associations of sleep duration on depression. Establish cohorts including institutionalized elders.

Conclusion

This cross-sectional study reveals a robust association between ACEs and late-life depression in a national sample of older Chinese adults, with a linear dose-response pattern (adjusted OR = 1.17 per ACE, 95% CI = 1.11–1.24, p < 0.001). Direct associations predominate (> 90%), with partial mediation through sleep duration (~ 9.4%), chronic conditions (~ 4.3%), and negligibly through social isolation (≤ 0.6%). Effect modification by region and education highlights contextual vulnerabilities. These results emphasize the potential value of incorporating ACEs screening and mediator-focused strategies (e.g., sleep and multimorbidity management) into geriatric primary care, particularly in high-risk groups, to support healthy aging. Longitudinal research is essential to clarify causality and evaluate targeted interventions.
