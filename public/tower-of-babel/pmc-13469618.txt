Cognitive control networks causally support implicit emotion regulation: evidence from dlPFC stimulation and directed functional connectivity
Authors: Agnieszka K. Adamczyk, Miroslaw Wyczesany
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cognitive control networks support adaptive regulation of behavior and affect, yet their role in automatic forms of emotion regulation remains poorly understood. Here, we tested whether large-scale cognitive control networks causally support implicit emotion regulation, using a within-subject design combining offline inhibitory transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) of the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC), EEG-based directed connectivity analyses, and a self-control priming paradigm. Across three sessions (sham, left cathodal, right cathodal), thirty-five female participants passively viewed negative and neutral images under implicit regulation or no regulation conditions. In the sham session, implicit regulation was associated with reduced indices of emotional processing, reflected in attenuated Late Positive Potential (LPP) amplitudes and modulation of prestimulus alpha activity in the visual cortex, indicating modulation of anticipatory and sustained processing stages. These sham-associated neural effects were attenuated (LPP) or altered (prestimulus alpha activity) following inhibitory dlPFC stimulation. Directed connectivity analyses further revealed tDCS-induced alterations in within-network frontoparietal and cingulo-opercular connectivity and cross-network interactions between frontoparietal and visual systems in the theta (3–7 Hz) and beta (15–30 Hz) bands. Together, these findings suggest that implicit regulation recruits large-scale cognitive control networks, with the dlPFC acting as a hub for dynamic coordination between executive and visual systems. Contrary to models that view implicit emotion regulation as independent of executive control, these results indicate that automatic regulatory processes depend on intact top-down control and frequency-specific communication across networks.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53424-4.

Introduction

Emotion regulation (ER) is often conceptualized as an intentional, effortful process. Yet in everyday life, people frequently adjust their emotional responses without deliberate intent—redirecting attention from negative stimuli, suppressing affective displays, or persisting through aversive tasks. Such spontaneous adjustments constitute implicit ER: goal-directed modulation of emotion that unfolds outside of conscious awareness1–5. Although implicit ER is pervasive in daily life and plays a key role in mental health and adaptive functioning6,7, its underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood.

A central open question is whether implicit ER operates independently of cognitive control, or whether it covertly recruits the same top–down processes that support deliberate regulation. Cognitive control involves executive functions such as working memory, inhibition, and flexible attention, which align behavior with internal regulatory goals8,9. While there is strong consensus that explicit ER—such as cognitive reappraisal—relies heavily on executive control2,5,10–12, the role of cognitive control in implicit ER remains debated1,13.

Two competing accounts have been proposed regarding the role of cognitive control in implicit emotion regulation. Traditional accounts conceptualize implicit ER as largely automatic and independent of executive control, assuming that regulation unfolds via associative, habitual, or stimulus-driven mechanisms that do not require prefrontal goal maintenance or top–down modulation2,3. Under this view, disrupting prefrontal control systems should have minimal impact on implicit regulatory outcomes.

In contrast, emerging control-dependent accounts propose that some forms of implicit ER—particularly those involving incidental goal activation—covertly recruit executive mechanisms, despite the absence of conscious regulatory intent1. These accounts predict engagement of dlPFC-centered control networks to maintain goal-relevant representations, allocate attention, and inhibit goal-incongruent appraisals, yielding neural signatures similar to those observed during explicit regulation. Importantly, this perspective reframes the implicit–explicit distinction not as a categorical divide, but as a continuum defined by the degree of reliance on cognitive control. The present study directly tests these competing predictions by examining whether perturbing dlPFC function causally disrupts neural markers and network dynamics supporting implicit ER.

Recent studies suggest that implicit ER may indeed engage controlled processes. For instance, behavioral studies demonstrated that working memory updating predicts the effectiveness of implicit ER14. Moreover, at the neural level, implicit ER has been associated with increased connectivity within frontoparietal and cingulo-opercular networks—core systems for sustained cognitive control15. The frontoparietal network (dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, intraparietal sulcus) supports flexible coordination of cognitive control across domains, whereas the cingulo-opercular network (dorsal anterior cingulate cortex, anterior insula/frontal operculum) monitors performance and signals when adjustments are needed16,17. These networks interact to ensure that control is both effectively deployed and adaptively adjusted as needed. Both networks are also reliably activated during explicit ER5,18–21. Together, these findings point to a potential overlap in the neural architecture supporting explicit and implicit forms of regulation. However, existing evidence is correlational and does not establish a causal role for cognitive control in implicit ER.

The dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC) is a key hub of the frontoparietal network that supports the integration of goal-relevant information with adaptive behavioral responses. It has been consistently implicated in core cognitive control functions including working memory maintenance, response inhibition, and monitoring discrepancies between current and desired goal states22–24. These operations are essential for explicit ER, as demonstrated by noninvasive brain stimulation studies showing that cathodal (inhibitory) stimulation over the dlPFC impairs reappraisal-based regulation12,25, whereas anodal (excitatory) stimulation enhances it26. The same control operations may also be recruited when emotion-regulation goals are activated implicitly—by maintaining goal-relevant information in working memory, shielding it from interference, monitoring progress toward the goal, and adjusting behavior as needed27. Consistent with this view, a previous fMRI study reported increased dlPFC activity following implicit induction of emotional control28. In summary, if implicit ER indeed relies on cognitive control mechanisms supported by the dlPFC, then disrupting dlPFC function should attenuate implicit regulatory effects and alter control-network connectivity. This logic motivates a causal test of whether prefrontal control systems are necessary for implicit ER.

To address this question, we adopted a multimodal causal approach. We used transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) to modulate dlPFC excitability and combined this manipulation with complementary neural, physiological, and behavioral indices of emotion regulation. This design allowed us to assess not only whether implicit ER succeeds or fails at the outcome level, but also how regulatory control is implemented at the level of neural dynamics.

We focused on both local and network-level signatures of control. Event-related potentials (ERPs) provided a well-established index of emotional processing, whereas corrugator electromyographic (EMG) activity indexed expressive components of affective responding. To examine the mechanisms through which control may be exerted implicitly, we analyzed frequency-specific activity and directed functional connectivity within and between cognitive control networks and sensory systems.

These analyses were guided by frequency-specific markers that have been consistently linked to distinct components of top–down control. Theta-band activity has been linked to conflict detection, performance monitoring, and the signaling of control demands within medial frontal and cingulo-opercular systems29. Beta-band synchronization has been proposed to support long-range top–down communication and the maintenance of goal-relevant control states30. Finally, alpha-band activity in visual cortex has been linked to preparatory attention and inhibitory gating of sensory processing, reflecting anticipatory control over incoming information 31.

By combining causal perturbation of the dlPFC with outcome-level measures (ERPs, EMG) and network-level analyses across distinct frequency bands, the present study provides a causal test of whether implicit emotion regulation depends on prefrontal control systems and how such control is implemented across monitoring, top–down signaling, and sensory modulation stages.

Materials and methods

Participants

A power analysis using PANGEA, version 0.2, https://jakewestfall.shinyapps.io/pangea/32, a tool for estimating statistical power in linear mixed-effects designs, indicated that 31 participants would provide 80% power to detect a three-level fixed effect at α = 0.05, assuming a medium effect size (d = 0.5;15). To allow for potential attrition, EEG data loss, or excessive artifacts, we recruited 35 adult women (mean age = 24.0 years, SD = 3.2) in exchange for monetary compensation or course credit. Only female participants were included to control for established gender differences in emotional picture processing33. All participants were right-handed, had normal or corrected-to-normal vision, and reported no history of neurological or psychiatric disorders.

Design and procedure

The study employed a double-blind, sham-controlled, within-subject design: Each participant completed three sessions—Sham, left dlPFC tDCS, and right dlPFC tDCS—with sessions spaced at least three days apart and stimulation order counterbalanced across participants. In each session, participants received 20 min of cathodal tDCS (or sham) immediately followed by an implicit emotion regulation (ER) task, during which EEG and EMG data were recorded. Regulation effectiveness was assessed using subjective ratings of valence (1 = pleasant, 9 = unpleasant) and arousal (1 = aroused, 9 = calm), as well as physiological markers of emotional processing, including the Late Positive Potential (LPP) and corrugator electromyographic (EMG) activity10,34 (Adamczyk et al., 202490).

Stimuli

The task included 60 negative and 30 neutral images selected from the International Affective Picture System (IAPS;35. Based on normative IAPS ratings, negative images had high arousal (M = 6.0, SD = 0.8) and low valence (M = 2.1, SD = 0.5), while neutral images had lower arousal (M = 3.3, SD = 0.6) and mid-range valence (M = 5.2, SD = 0.4). Negative images depicted emotionally aversive content (e.g., suffering individuals, violence, mutilations, accidents, dangerous animals), whereas neutral images depicted people engaged in everyday activities, animals, objects, and neutral landscapes.

Stimuli were divided into four sets of negative images and two sets of neutral images (15 images per set), matched across sets for arousal and valence. To minimize low-level visual confounds in EEG, all sets were equated for luminance and contrast using the SHINE toolbox, version 0.0.2, http://www.mapageweb.umontreal.ca/gosselif/SHINE/36. Equalization was applied separately to each Red, Green, Blue (RGB) channel (converted to LAB color space), followed by recombination into RGB format. Wavelet decomposition37 confirmed that image sets did not differ in spatial frequency content, either at high (> 128 cycles/picture) or low (< 32 cycles/picture) ranges.

Stimuli were presented full screen at a viewing distance of approximately 60 cm, subtending ~ 50° of horizontal visual angle. Presentation was controlled using PsychoPy, version 2022.2.4, https://www.psychopy.org/38 on a 61-cm LED monitor.

Implicit ER task

Participants completed a validated implicit self-control goal priming task15,28, cf.39,40. Each trial began with a scrambled sentence, presented for unscrambling. In the no-regulation (NREG) condition, sentences were semantically neutral (e.g., “He wrote a long letter”), while in the regulation (REG) condition, sentences embedded self-control-related content (e.g., “He reduced his body weight”). All sentences were matched for syntactic complexity and length and presented in scrambled form (e.g., “his reduced He weight body”) to mask their goal-inducing nature.

Participants were instructed to unscramble each sentence and then passively view a negative (NEG) or neutral (NEU) image (Fig. 1). This design yielded three trial types: REG-NEG, NREG-NEG, and NREG-NEU. The complete set of prime sentences is available in Wyczesany et al.28, supplementary materials). tDCS protocol

Transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) was delivered using a 1 × 1 mini-CT stimulator (Soterix Medical, Inc., New York, NY) at 2 mA of cathodal current for 20 min. Stimulation targeted either the left or right dlPFC in separate sessions. The cathodal electrode (5 × 5 cm carbon rubber, saline-saturated SNAPpad™ sponge) was positioned over F3 (left dlPFC) or F4 (right dlPFC) according to the international 10–20 EEG system, while the anodal electrode (5 × 7 cm EASYpad™ sponge) was placed extracephalically over the contralateral deltoid muscle to minimize confounding excitatory cortical effects (see also:41).

Stimulation parameters were within established safety limits for tDCS. With a current intensity of 2 mA and a cathodal electrode area of 25 cm2, current density was 0.08 mA/cm2 and charge density approximately 96 mC/cm2, both well below commonly accepted safety thresholds42.

A standard double-blind sham procedure with ramp-up and ramp-down phases was used41,43,44. Participants were not asked to guess the stimulation condition after each session. Both participants and experimenters were blinded to stimulation condition; stimulation protocols were pre-programmed and concealed by the device. EEG preprocessing was performed using automated procedures, ensuring that data analysis was conducted blind to stimulation condition.

Electrophysiological data recording, preprocessing, and analysis

Electrophysiological data recording

The EEG signal was recorded using a BiosemiTwo system with 64 cap electrodes, arranged according to the international 10–20 system, supplemented by two mastoid electrodes and four electrooculographic (EOG) electrodes. EMG activity of the corrugator supercilii muscle was recorded using Ag/AgCl electrodes with saline-based electrode gel in a bipolar configuration, following the guidelines of Fridlund and Cacioppo45. All signals were sampled at 1024 Hz.

EMG: preprocessing and analysis

The EMG signal was computed as the voltage difference between the two corrugator electrodes, filtered (20–400 Hz), rectified (absolute values transformation), smoothed with a 10-point moving average filter, and downsampled to 512 Hz. Preprocessing steps were implemented in FieldTrip, version 20221223, https://www.fieldtriptoolbox.org/46. Corrugator activity was averaged within 450–2000 ms post-stimulus, relative to a − 200 to 0 ms baseline47,48.

ERP: preprocessing and analysis

EEG data were preprocessed and analyzed using the Atlantis Source Connectivity Toolbox, version 23.12, https://atlantis.psychologia.uj.edu.pl49,93, an open-source software ﻿package. Preprocessing steps included: re-referencing to the linked mastoids; 0.1 Hz high-pass and 47 Hz low-pass filtering using windowed sinc linear phase FIR filters; and bad channel screening via variance thresholding. Eye-movement artifacts were corrected using the recursive least squares method50. Data were segmented relative to stimulus onset (− 0.1 to 2 s), baseline-corrected using the prestimulus period, and subjected to trial-based artifact rejection (or single-channel interpolation if only one electrode was affected). Trials were removed for: excess variance (channel variance threshold: Q1/Q3 ± 5 IQR), range artifacts (> 250 μV), or muscle contamination detected by elevated spectral power between 35 and 47 Hz.

If more than one-third of all trials were rejected, the dataset was excluded from further analysis (3 datasets excluded). Conditions with fewer than 12 trials (minimum required for reliable LPP estimation;51), would also have been excluded, but this was not necessary. On average, 27.4 trials per condition were retained (range: 14–30), with no significant differences as a function of session (p > 0.35) or condition (p > 0.98).

ERP analyses focused on the LPP component defined a priori; earlier components were not analyzed, as they were not part of the hypothesis-driven framework of the study. LPP magnitudes were computed as the mean amplitude from 450 to 2000 ms post-picture onset, measured from electrodes PO3, PO4, PO7, PO8, O1, and O252–54. Electrode selection and LPP onset were determined via a collapsed localizer approach (grand-average waveform and topographical map collapsed across all conditions in the sham session;55). Brain connectivity: preprocessing and analysis

EEG signals were filtered from 2 to 47 Hz using windowed sinc linear phase finite impulse response (FIR) filters and segmented from − 1 to 2 s relative to stimulus onset. Bad channels were identified using an interquartile range (IQR)-based extreme outlier rejection algorithm (variance threshold: Q1/Q3 ± 5 IQR), calculated from temporarily EOG-corrected data (corrected via the recursive least squares method50). Surviving original (non-EOG-corrected) channels were re-referenced to the average of all electrodes.

Trial-based artifact rejection was based on extreme outlier variance (threshold: Q1/Q3 ± 3 IQR), maximum trial voltage range (< 300 μV), and muscle artifact detection (elevated spectral power between 35 and 47 Hz). Remaining trials were decomposed using fastICA (deflation mode, pow3 nonlinearity). Independent components (ICs) were classified as brain or non-brain using a joint probability from two trained classification models—one based on component topography, and another based on spectral and temporal features (e.g., spectral power, pre/post-stimulus variance, correlation and power similarity to EOG signals). The classification models are described in the ASCT manual: https://atlantis.psychologia.uj.edu.pl.

Brain ICs were localized via their weight matrices using minimum norm estimation (MNE;56) with a depth-weighting parameter set to 0.5 and lambda regularization based on noise predetermined system parameter of 2. A realistic head model was generated from the SPM8 MRI template using the five-layer SimBio volume conductor model57. Regions of interest (ROIs) were defined a priori from previous literature and study hypotheses24,58–61: L/R primary visual cortex (V1; − 11 − 81 7 / 11–78 9); L/R intraparietal sulci (IPS; − 32 − 48 44 / 32–52 50); dorsal anterior cingulate cortex (dACC; − 1 − 10 46); L/R anterior insula / frontal operculum (aI/fO; − 35 14 5 / 36 16 4); L/R dlPFC: (− 43 18 29 / 43 18 29); precuneus (Prec; − 2 − 57 42 / 3 − 64 35). The ROI signals were reconstructed as a sum of IC activations in the respective (closest) source dipole separately for all spatial directions. ROI time series were reconstructed as the sum of IC activations from the nearest source dipole in each spatial direction. A single ROI value was obtained by extracting the first principal component from the three spatial components. To mitigate zero-lag correlations caused by source leakage, symmetric multivariate orthogonalization was applied62.

Directed functional connectivity between ROIs was computed for theta (3–7 Hz) and beta (15–30 Hz) frequency bands using the non-normalized directed transfer function63, a multivariate connectivity metric grounded in Granger causality.

Alpha-band analyses were limited to prestimulus power estimates in primary visual cortex, reflecting the well-established role of alpha oscillations in sensory gating and baseline excitability during passive viewing. Alpha-band connectivity was not examined, as such effects have been most consistently reported in paradigms involving explicit or spatially selective attentional modulation64–66. Accordingly, directed connectivity analyses were focused on theta and beta bands, which have been more reliably implicated in the recruitment and maintenance of goal-related control states in tasks that do not involve explicit attentional selection29,30,67,68.

Connectivity analyses focused on a priori, hypothesis-driven directed connections within cognitive control systems. Each tested connection was selected to index a specific control-related function. Importantly, these specific directed connections were preselected based on prior work using the same implicit emotion regulation paradigm, which identified them as core components of control-related network dynamics15.

Theta-band connectivity was examined to capture core control processes, including frontoparietal control (right dlPFC → left IPS; left dlPFC → right IPS) and cingulo-opercular monitoring (dACC → right aI/fO; dACC → left aI/fO). To examine top–down modulation of sensory processing, beta-band connectivity was assessed from the frontoparietal network to visual-associative regions (bilateral IPS → V1; bilateral IPS → Prec).

Analyses were restricted to top–down directed connectivity (e.g., dlPFC → IPS; IPS → visual cortex), consistent with dominant models of cognitive control and emotion regulation1,5,18. Bidirectional connectivity was not exhaustively tested in order to limit multiple comparisons and maintain a hypothesis-driven analytic framework.

Pre-stimulus alpha power

Prestimulus alpha-band activity in visual cortex was used as an index of preparatory attentional state and inhibitory gating of sensory processing31. Alpha power (8–13 Hz) was computed in the − 1 to 0 s interval preceding stimulus onset from the V1 region of interest using Welch’s method. Spectral estimates were log-transformed prior to statistical analysis.

Statistical analysis

Linear mixed-effects models

Data were analyzed in R, version 4.2.2, https://www.r-project.org/69 using linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) implemented in the lme4 package (version 1.1–31;70). Separate models were estimated for each dependent variable: valence and arousal ratings, EMG corrugator activity, LPP amplitudes, prestimulus alpha power, and connectivity between ROIs.

Positive effects for the Regulation contrast indicate reduced responses during regulation relative to no-regulation (NREG–NEG > REG–NEG), corresponding to neural downregulation (e.g., smaller LPP under REG–NEG).

All models included random intercepts for participants to account for repeated measures. Models were fitted using restricted maximum likelihood (REML). p-values were obtained from conditional Type III F-tests with Kenward–Roger correction of degrees of freedom, implemented via Anova from the car package (v3.1.1;71), which calls KRmodcomp from pbkrtest (version 0.5.1;72).

Confidence intervals for fixed effects were estimated using parametric bootstrapping (1000 simulations) with bootMer from lme4, and 95% percentile-based CIs were obtained using boot.ci from the boot package (version 1.3.28;73). Standardized effect sizes (Cohen’s d) were calculated using lme.dscore from EMAtools (version 0.1.4;74). Outliers were excluded based on |df beta|> 2/√N and Cook’s distance > 4/N, where N is the number of grouping units75. All p-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the false discovery rate (FDR) method (p.adjust from stats, version 4.2.2;69) and corrected for stimulation session. Primary analyses focused on within-session planned contrasts testing the presence or absence of implicit emotion regulation effects under Sham and active tDCS conditions. In addition, for outcomes showing significant regulation effects in the Sham session, unified linear mixed-effects models including Session (Sham, Left, Right) × Regulation interactions were fitted to formally assess cross-session differences.

Bayes factor

Following recent recommendations76, we complemented the frequentist LMM analyses with Bayes factor analysis. A Jeffreys–Zellner–Siow prior was used, specifically a folded Cauchy distribution centered at δ = 0 with a fixed-effects scaling factor of r = 0.5. For each analysis, we compared the full model including the fixed effect of interest (H₁) against the null (intercept-only) model (H₀), with “participant” included as a random grouping factor in all models.

Evidence strength was interpreted according to Jeffreys’s (1961)91 taxonomy, as presented in Wagenmakers et al.77. Analyses were conducted in R, version 4.2.2, https://www.r-project.org/69 using the lmBF function from the BayesFactor package (version 0.9.12.4.4;78).

Pre-stimulus alpha power

During the Sham session, prestimulus alpha power in the right primary visual cortex was significantly lower in the REG–NEG compared to the NREG–NEG condition, F(1, 154) = 10.4, pFDR = 0.003 (puncorr. = 0.002), d = 0.52, BF10 = 17.5, indicating strong evidence for alpha suppression during implicit regulation. Under Right tDCS, the numerical pattern differed from the Sham session, with higher prestimulus alpha power in the REG-NEG than in the NREG-NEG condition, F(1, 139) = 12.0, pFDR = 0.002 (puncorr. = 0.001), d = -0.59, BF10 = 34.9, indicating very strong Bayesian evidence for this effect.

Together, these results indicate that prestimulus alpha modulation during implicit regulation was expressed in the right visual cortex under Sham conditions, whereas under active dlPFC stimulation alpha modulation was observed predominantly in the left visual cortex.

Supplementary interaction analyses

Consistent with the planned-contrast analyses, Session × Regulation interactions reached statistical significance for two theta-band control pathways. Specifically, the interaction was significant for theta-band connectivity from the right dlPFC to the left IPS, F(2, 155) = 4.54, p = 0.012, f2 = 0.24, BF10 = 4.27, and for connectivity from the dACC to the right aI/fO, F(2,150) = 3.62, p = 0.029, f2 = 0.22, BF10 = 2.07, with Bayes factors indicating moderate and anecdotal evidence in favor of the interaction. Remaining interaction terms did not reach statistical significance (all ps > 0.05), consistent with the limited statistical power for higher-order interactions in moderate-sized EEG–tDCS samples.

Discussion

This study tested whether implicit emotion regulation (ER)—often assumed to be automatic and independent of executive processes—relies on cognitive control mechanisms. Using inhibitory tDCS over the dlPFC, we found that perturbing prefrontal function altered the expression of neural markers of implicit ER, including prestimulus alpha power and LPP amplitudes, and attenuated frequency-specific connectivity patterns within and between frontoparietal, cingulo-opercular, and visual-associative networks. These findings provide causal evidence that implicit ER engages top-down control processes supported by large-scale cognitive control networks.

Baseline reactivity

tDCS did not substantially alter baseline emotional reactivity: negative stimuli reliably evoked greater unpleasantness, arousal, and LPP amplitudes in all sessions. A marginal difference in EMG corrugator activity was observed in the Sham session, but this contrast was not significant under active tDCS. Given the potential influence of stimulation on expressive baseline activity, EMG was excluded from subsequent regulation analyses. Overall, these results indicate that tDCS did not disrupt baseline emotional reactivity at the subjective or cortical level, allowing subsequent analyses to reliably probe regulatory effects.

Implicit emotion regulation and dlPFC-dependent control

In the Sham session, implicit ER—elicited by incidental activation of a self-control goal—was associated with reduced prestimulus alpha in the right visual cortex and attenuated the LPP amplitudes to negative stimuli, a primary marker of ER efficacy in the present study, which replicated prior work15. Under inhibitory dlPFC stimulation, these neural markers of implicit regulation were no longer observed, indicating that intact prefrontal control is necessary for the expression of these effects. In contrast, subjective ratings of emotional experience were not significantly altered, a pattern that has been consistently reported in previous implicit ER studies15,28.

This neural–subjective dissociation suggests that implicit ER primarily modulates early, automatic stages of emotional processing—such as attentional allocation and sensory gain—indexed here by prestimulus alpha and the LPP, which operate largely outside conscious awareness. In contrast, self-reported affect was assessed retrospectively at the block level and likely reflects a more global, consciously reconstructed evaluation, known to be less sensitive to subtle, nonconscious regulatory effects and influenced by task demands.

Moreover, because implicit regulation in the present paradigm served an instrumental self-control goal rather than a hedonic one its impact on consciously experienced affect may be limited79. Together, these findings indicate that implicit ER can attenuate neural responses to emotional stimuli without substantially altering subjective experience, underscoring the importance of combining neural and experiential measures.

Brain connectivity mechanisms

In the Sham session, implicit ER was associated with increased theta-band connectivity within the frontoparietal and cingulo-opercular networks—specifically from the right dlPFC to the left IPS and from the dACC to the right aI/fO—pathways implicated in goal maintenance and conflict monitoring. Implicit ER was also accompanied by changes in beta-band connectivity from the frontoparietal network to visual-associative regions, including the left IPS to right V1 and the right IPS to left Prec, consistent with top-down modulation of perceptual processing. However, evidence for these beta-band effects was comparatively weaker and primarily supported by planned contrasts, warranting cautious interpretation.

Under cathodal tDCS over either dlPFC, these connectivity patterns were no longer observed, suggesting that intact dlPFC function is required for the expression of frequency-specific coordination between executive control networks and downstream sensory systems during implicit emotion regulation. Notably, the two theta-band control pathways (dlPFC → IPS and dACC → aI/fO) showed convergent support across both planned-contrast analyses and unified Session × Regulation interaction models, strengthening the inference that these connections play an important role in implicit ER. By contrast, effects involving beta-band connectivity should be regarded as more tentative and in need of replication.

Several plausible mechanisms may account for how cathodal tDCS over the dlPFC altered frequency-specific network dynamics during implicit emotion regulation. Neural oscillations are widely thought to support communication and coordination across distributed brain networks80,81, such that perturbations of local cortical excitability can propagate to large-scale network interactions. At the neurochemical level, one possible contributing mechanism is modulation of GABAergic inhibition, which has been linked to cathodal tDCS effects on cortical excitability and may indirectly influence oscillatory synchronization across networks42.

Cathodal tDCS might have reduced theta-band synchronization within control-related networks by attenuating prefrontal contributions to monitoring and control signaling. Frontal theta activity has been consistently linked to cognitive control, performance monitoring, and adaptive behavioral adjustment29, and reduced dlPFC excitability may therefore weaken theta-mediated coordination across control networks.

Similarly, the observed alterations in beta-band connectivity may reflect disrupted long-range top–down communication. Beta-band synchronization has been proposed to support feedback signaling and the maintenance of goal-relevant control states30. By modulating dlPFC excitability, cathodal tDCS may reduce the stability or efficacy of beta-mediated interactions between prefrontal and posterior regions, although this interpretation remains tentative given the relatively weak statistical support for these effects.

From implicit goals to coordinated control: rethinking the boundary between automatic and deliberate regulation

By showing that dlPFC inhibition attenuates the effectiveness of implicit ER and alters connectivity patterns within and between cognitive control networks, this study provides causal evidence that executive mechanisms contribute to automatic emotion-regulation goal pursuit. These findings extend prior correlational work14,28, demonstrating that implicit ER not only engages cognitive-control-related networks15 but is functionally supported by their integrity—paralleling patterns observed in explicit regulation strategies1,5,18.

According to the widely accepted model of cognitive control of emotion (MCCE;5), the dlPFC–parietal network supports explicit ER by maintaining regulatory goals, allocating attention, and manipulating emotional information in working memory. This system is recruited across diverse explicit strategies, including situation selection, selective attention, distraction, reappraisal, and suppression18–21,82. The present findings suggest that some forms of implicit ER strategies, such as incidental activation of a self-control goal, may engage the same control architecture—challenging models that treat implicit ER as fully automatic and cognitively independent (i.e., model-free ER; e.g.,2,3).

Recent theoretical accounts increasingly reject a strict implicit–explicit dichotomy, proposing instead that implicit processes vary in their reliance on cognitive control. While some forms of implicit ER—such as affective conditioning or habitual responses—may proceed automatically and rely on minimal executive input, others, including incidental goal pursuit, appear to engage controlled processes despite the absence of conscious regulatory intent1. These controlled implicit strategies are hypothesized to recruit dlPFC-dependent mechanisms, such as maintaining goal-relevant representations and inhibiting goal-incongruent appraisals. Consistent with this view, implicitly induced self-control goals in the present study elicited frequency-specific connectivity increase within frontoparietal and cingulo-opercular networks, and these effects were attenuated by inhibitory dlPFC stimulation.

Within this architecture, the cingulo-opercular network is thought to monitor performance and signal the need for control, whereas the frontoparietal network implements flexible, goal-directed adjustments. The cingulo-opercular system has been proposed to detect mismatches between reactive emotional states and internal goals, triggering recruitment of cognitive control when discrepancies arise2,83,84. Theta-band signaling from this system may facilitate engagement of frontoparietal circuits, which in turn modulate motivational and attentional subsystems29.

In the present study, such a mechanism may underlie the observed attenuation of later-stage emotional processing, reflected in reduced LPP amplitudes, potentially indexing suppression of goal-incongruent appraisals28 or reduced semantic elaboration of affective meaning12,25. Notably, this later-stage modulation was preceded by changes in preparatory attentional control—indexed by prestimulus alpha attenuation in visual cortex and altered top-down connectivity from prefrontal to posterior regions. The above pattern suggests that downstream modulation of affective processing may arise from active, goal-related control processes that influence sensitivity to incoming stimuli, rather than from purely passive perceptual gating.

These results align with prior fMRI evidence demonstrating overlapping dlPFC engagement across distinct controlled implicit ER paradigms—self-control goal pursuit and implicit reappraisal—both of which also implicated modulation of early visual areas (V1, Prec) and emotion-generative structures such as the amygdala28.

In sum, even without conscious regulatory intent, emotion regulation can recruit executive control systems to implement higher-order forms of affective control. By mapping the large-scale network architecture and frequency-specific dynamics associated with implicit ER, these findings refine mechanistic accounts of spontaneous emotion regulation and highlight the role of coordinated top-down control in regulating emotional responses. These insights may inform future work on affective dysregulation in clinical populations—particularly conditions characterized by heightened emotional reactivity despite preserved explicit regulatory abilities85–89.

Limitations and directions for future research

A first limitation concerns the strength of several connectivity effects, some of which did not survive FDR correction and were supported by weak Bayesian effects. While multiple-comparison correction is essential in high-dimensional EEG studies, it may obscure subtle but meaningful effects in moderate-sized samples90. Importantly, the central finding—that dlPFC–parietal connectivity supporting cognitive control was modulated by implicit ER and disrupted by inhibitory tDCS—was robust and replicated prior work15. By contrast, weaker effects in cingulo-opercular and visual-associative pathways warrant re-evaluation in larger, more statistically powered samples.

Relatedly, the present study was powered to detect within-session regulation effects but was not optimized for higher-order Session × Regulation interactions. Although such interactions reached significance for two core theta-band control pathways, several others did not. The absence of significant interactions for these remaining pathways should therefore be interpreted cautiously and not as evidence against differential stimulation effects.

A second limitation concerns the lack of converging peripheral indices of regulation. Although neural markers captured anticipatory (prestimulus alpha), stimulus-evoked (LPP), and network-level regulatory processes, expressive measures were less stable. Facial EMG effects were weak and inconsistent across conditions, precluding their inclusion in the main regulatory analyses. Future studies could incorporate cardiovascular or electrodermal measures to provide a more comprehensive, multisystem account of implicit regulation.

A further limitation concerns sample homogeneity: the study included only female participants, which limits generalizability. Future studies should examine whether these effects extend across genders.

Finally, spatial specificity was limited by the use of standard electrode montages and a template head model. Although this approach is common in EEG–tDCS studies41,43,44, it entails current spread and reduced anatomical precision. Accordingly, the present findings should be interpreted as reflecting perturbation of dlPFC-centered control networks rather than modulation of a single cortical locus. Future work could improve spatial precision by integrating individual neuroanatomical models for both stimulation targeting and source reconstruction (e.g.,47).

Conclusion

This study provides causal evidence that implicit emotion regulation—often assumed to operate independently of executive control—engages cognitive control networks. Even in the absence of conscious regulatory intent, the brain recruits top-down control mechanisms to align emotional responses with implicitly activated goals, supporting models that frame emotion regulation as a continuum rather than a dichotomy between automatic and deliberate strategies.
