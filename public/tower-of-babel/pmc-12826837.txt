Application of Endoscopic Underwater Surgery for Brain Tumors from Ventricle to Cistern: A Report of Pediatric and Elderly Cases
Authors: Kenichiro IWAMI, Ayako TODA, Masazumi FUJII, Tadashi WATANABE, Ryuta SAITO, Kiyoshi SAITO
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Postoperative brain collapse and subdural fluid collection are potential complications after craniotomy in pediatric and geriatric neurosurgery. We have been using underwater surgery for intraventricular tumors in pediatric patients since 2011 and have recently expanded its use for extra-axial brain tumors in older adult patients. Herein, we introduce our underwater surgical technique and present 3 cases: the first application of underwater surgery for a pediatric intraventricular tumor, and 2 recent cases of cisternal tumors in older adults treated with this technique. We have only used underwater surgery for non-highly-vascularized extra-axial tumors located in the ventricle or cistern, which are considered amenable to hemostasis in the underwater surgical setting. During underwater surgery, the patient is positioned such that the craniotomy site is at the highest position. The craniotomy is limited to 3 cm, and the surgical field is irrigated with artificial cerebrospinal fluid during intradural surgery. In the cases of patients with extra-axial tumors reported herein, underwater surgery was successfully performed without postoperative complications such as subdural fluid collection. Consequently, this surgical method shows promise for preventing postoperative cerebral subsidence and subdural fluid collection in both pediatric and geriatric patients, although its application is contraindicated for large and hemorrhagic tumors.

Introduction

Postoperative subdural fluid collection (SFC) is a potential complication after craniotomy (Fig. 1A).1-4) In pediatric neurosurgery, it is common to encounter SFC following the removal of intraventricular or paraventricular tumors associated with hydrocephalus.5) In a study involving 231 intracranial endoscopic procedures performed in children, Cinalli et al.6) identified several complications, with the most frequent being SFC and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) infections. SFC was observed in 11 patients (4.8%), 7 of whom required subdural shunting.6) Particularly in children with hydrocephalus, SFC following intraoperative brain collapse can be challenging to treat because of the thin cerebral mantle layer.

Pink; intraventricular tumor. CSF: cerebrospinal fluid

We have been applying underwater surgery (UWS) to intraventricular tumors in pediatric patients since 2011, based on the hypothesis that if the brain is not deformed intraoperatively by CSF drainage, brain morphology will be preserved postoperatively and SFC will be prevented. Endoscopic technology has progressed from high-definition to 4K image resolution, significantly enhancing the clarity of the underwater surgical field. Given the aging population, craniotomies for older adult patients have increased.7-9) A commonality between pediatric and geriatric patients is the disparity between the intracranial volume and parenchymal brain volume, that is, increased intracranial volume relative to the parenchymal brain volume (Fig. 1B). Therefore, the same precautions against postoperative brain collapse and SFC are considered necessary in craniotomy for both patient populations. We recently expanded the use of UWS in older adult patients with non-highly-vascularized extra-axial tumors.

Herein, we introduce our underwater surgical technique and present 3 cases: the first application of UWS for a pediatric intraventricular tumor, and 2 recent cases of cisternal tumors in elderly patients treated with this technique.

Technical Note

Surgical technique

The surgical position was determined based on the lesion's location. The supine position was used for patients with lesions other than those in the median occipital and suboccipital regions. The body and head were carefully positioned to elevate the craniotomy site to the highest point, and the head was subsequently immobilized with a Sugita head frame (Mizuho, Japan). Patients with median occipital and suboccipital lesions were placed in the prone position.

In this technique, a craniotomy of approximately 2-3 cm is created following a skin incision over the scalp or eyebrow. After the dura is incised, the ventricle or cistern containing the lesion is approached. The intradural procedures are conducted using a rigid endoscope with an outside diameter of 2.7 or 4 mm (Karl Storz, Tuttlingen, Germany). The endoscope is held and fixed using the Mitaka UniARM (Mitaka Kohki Co, Japan), and these devices are controlled by a second doctor (scopist). When inserting the endoscope into the intradural space, its stability can be enhanced by resting a part of the instrument along the bone edge of the craniotomy. Standard microsurgical instruments are used, although slender instruments are easier to handle. The surgical field is continuously irrigated with artificial CSF (Artcereb, Otsuka Pharmaceutical Factory, Japan) at 37°C using CLEARVISIONⓇ II (Karl Storz). The flow rate is adjusted within the range of 0-100 mL/min to maintain a clear surgical field, while ensuring that excess fluid drains from the dural opening to prevent increase in intracranial pressure. It is critical to ensure that the vessels or lesion is coagulated before transection to prevent contamination of the CSF with blood. After the lesion is resected to the greatest extent, wound closure is performed in the standard neurosurgical fashion.

Regarding the limitations specific to UWS, the most important drawback is blood obscuring the surgical field of view. When bleeding occurs to the extent that the field of vision becomes cloudy, one should consider performing hemostasis after removing the CSF from the surgical field. Conversely, if there is a small amount of bleeding, UWS allows for early detection of bleeding points, and continuous irrigation can have a hemostatic effect. A narrow working space is also a major drawback of UWS for lesions within the subarachnoid space. In comparison with “air view” surgery, during which the brain becomes slack because of CSF drainage, the subarachnoid space remains narrow during UWS. Switching to “air view” surgery should be considered when the working space is too narrow to perform surgical procedures.

Indications and contraindications

Our application of UWS is for extra-axial tumors in the ventricular or cisternal spaces. Ideal indications include cystic tumors necessitating limited internal debulking and cases where hemostasis is readily achieved. For solid or large tumors, the procedure may need to be converted to a standard “air view” approach if tumor debris or hemorrhage during debulking clouds the CSF. Similarly, this technique is not suitable for highly vascular tumors, unless hemostasis can be secured early in the procedure. Moreover, UWS is less suitable for cases requiring a large craniotomy, which can cause endoscope instability and challenges in preventing the intracranial entry of air and blood.

Case presentation

Pediatric case 1

CT: computed tomography; MRI: magnetic resonance imaging

A 25-day-old female infant was referred to us with poor feeding, irregular breathing, large head, and sunset-eye phenomenon. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed severe hydrocephalus and a tumor in the left lateral ventricle with pedicles feeding and draining the blood vessels (Fig. 2A and B). Following ventricular drainage on the same day, the tumor was removed using a 4-mm diameter rigid endoscope (high-definition image resolution) (Fig. 2C). A 1.5-cm cortical incision was made in the temporal lobe from the abnormally widened right squamosal suture to reach the lateral ventricle (Fig. 2B and D), where we coagulated, ligated, and cut the feeding and draining vessels (Fig. 2E and F). The tumor was removed via corticotomy (Fig. 2G). The entire procedure was performed using UWS with continuous irrigation of artificial CSF through a ventricular drainage tube. The brain surface did not sink at the end of surgery. The corticotomy site was sealed with fibrin adhesive. The pathological diagnosis was of choroid plexus papilloma.

The postoperative course was uneventful. Computed tomography (CT) on the next day and MRI 7.5 years after the surgery revealed no SFC or tumor recurrence (Fig. 2H and I). The patient has had healthy growth and development without neurological deficits.

Older adult case 1

Figure 3 and Video 2 present the case of a patient in her 90s with craniopharyngioma who was treated using UWS.

Case 3 A, B) T1-weighted sagittal (A) and T2-weighed coronal (B) MRI showing a cystic craniopharyngioma. C) Schematic diagram of underwater surgery for Case 3. D-F) Intraoperative photographs. Through a right supraorbital craniotomy (D), tumor (green arrowhead) was reduced through the space between the right optic nerve (yellow arrowhead) and the internal carotid artery (red arrowhead) (E, F). G, H) T1-weighted sagittal (G) and T2-weighed coronal (H) MRI after 2 years, revealed no subdural fluid collection and no recurrence of the tumor.

MRI: magnetic resonance imaging

This patient initially presented with mild visual-field loss and underwent MRI in the clinic; however, the visual-field loss became severe as the cystic tumor grew over time (Fig. 3A and B). As the patient had undergone multiple surgeries for bilateral chronic subdural hematomas, we decided to perform UWS to remove the tumor without causing brain deformation (Fig. 3C). Most of the tumor was removed through an eyebrow skin incision and a supraorbital 2-cm craniotomy using a 4-mm diameter rigid endoscope (4K image resolution) (Fig. 3D-F). The entire procedure was performed with continuous irrigation of artificial CSF through the endoscope outer sheath. The surgery lasted approximately 1 hour, and the patient was discharged home 1 week after surgery. Neither SFC nor tumor recurrence were observed on MRI 2 years after surgery (Fig. 3G and H), and the patient has maintained good visual acuity.

CSF: cerebrospinal fluid; MRI: magnetic resonance imaging

The patient presented with dizziness and hearing loss in her left ear. She underwent MRI at the clinic that revealed a multi-cystic tumor, which grew over time and resulted in severe left-side trigeminal neuralgia (Fig. 4A). The patient had age-related parenchymal atrophy of the brain; however, because she was single and wished to continue living alone, we decided to perform UWS to remove the tumor without causing brain deformation (Fig. 4B). Most of the tumor was removed through a 2.5-cm lateral suboccipital craniotomy using a 4-mm diameter rigid endoscope (4K image resolution) (Fig. 4C-F). The entire procedure was performed with continuous irrigation of artificial CSF through the endoscope outer sheath. The surgery lasted approximately 2 hours, and the patient was discharged home 1 week after surgery. A small amount of intracranial air was observed on CT immediately after surgery (Supplementary Figure 1). An MRI 2 years after surgery did not indicate SFC or tumor recurrence (Fig. 4G), and the trigeminal neuralgia has not recurred.

Discussion

Although UWS is now an accepted procedure in endoscopic surgery for intraventricular lesions, its application to the cisterns has been limited. We previously reported its use for microvascular decompression in 2021;10) however, to our knowledge, the present paper is the first to describe its application for cisternal tumors.

In conventional craniotomies using a microscope, the CSF must be drained to improve microscopic visualization of the surgical field. However, endoscopic visualization of the operative field does not require CSF drainage. Moreover, continuous fluid irrigation during UWS keeps the ventricles dilated and prevents brain collapse. In this report, we presented the cases of pediatric and geriatric patients treated using UWS to demonstrate the potential contribution of UWS in preventing postoperative SFC.

Although choroid plexus tumors (CPTs) are among the most common intraventricular tumors, they are rare, accounting for 2-4% of pediatric brain tumors.11) In pediatric neurosurgery, SFC is a common complication after CPT removal and occurs in 18%-32% of cases, requiring appropriate management such as subdural drainage or a subdural peritoneal or ventriculoperitoneal shunt.11-14) Preoperative hydrocephalus and postoperative escape of the CSF into the subdural space through a possible ball-valve mechanism associated with CSF pulsation may lead to SFC expansion. Corticotomy closure with biological adhesives is important after CPT removal, although corticotomy closure alone does not always prevent brain collapse and postoperative SFC.12-14) In 2011, we began using UWS for the removal of pediatric intraventricular tumors in an effort to maintain the thin cerebral mantle layer in position and prevent postoperative SFC. Our experience with CPT is limited; however, we have not observed refractory postoperative SFC in patients successfully treated with UWS. To date, UWS has not been indicated for hemorrhagic tumors. We believe that a key future challenge is the development of a perfusion system that can ensure a clear surgical field, even in the presence of significant bleeding, such as the perfusion system used in ophthalmic vitreoretinal surgery.

Populations are aging rapidly in several countries including Japan, and the number of older adult patients with neurological diseases is increasing. Although careful preoperative evaluation and vigorous postoperative support are crucial for geriatric patients,7) it is certain that in the future, neurosurgeons will increasingly have to perform craniotomies in this patient population. Eichberg et al.15) suggested that advanced age alone should not preclude brain tumor surgery because older patients do not have significant differences in outcomes compared to younger patients regarding complications, length of hospital stay, or changes in functional status. However, it is common knowledge among neurosurgeons that older adult patients have atrophied brain parenchyma and are prone to intraoperative brain collapse and postoperative SFC after craniotomy. Therefore, we have recently broadened the indications for UWS to include the removal of extra-axial tumors in older adult patients at a low risk of bleeding with the goal of preventing postoperative SFC.

One of the common features of our pediatric and geriatric patients was the increased intracranial volume relative to the brain parenchyma volume (Fig. 1B); we indicated UWS for these patients to avoid extreme brain deformities associated with intraoperative CSF drainage based on our hypothesis that if the brain is not deformed intraoperatively, there is a high probability that the brain morphology will be preserved postoperatively and SFC will be less likely to occur. Relying on our limited experience, UWS for pediatric intraventricular tumors with associated hydrocephalus and geriatric extra-axial tumors is a promising method to avoid postoperative SFC. Given that the brain resides in CSF, its intraoperative drainage may have adverse effects both on brain morphology and on its physiological environment and function. Therefore, UWS might be a milestone for which we should aim.

Pure endoscopic removal of brain tumors has been reported; however, large, hard, and vascular tumors remain difficult to remove. Particularly for hemorrhagic tumors, including meningiomas and malignant gliomas, we have not advocated UWS because we anticipate that it will be difficult to obtain an underwater field of view. CPTs tend to be large, fragile, and vascular, making complete surgical resection difficult, with the risk of uncontrollable intraoperative hemorrhage.11,12) The most crucial surgical step is identification and obliteration of the feeding vessels.12) As in Pediatric Case 1, UWS for a potentially hemorrhagic tumor is possible only when the vascular pedicle of the tumor can be approached at an earlier surgery stage.16)

The number of brain tumor surgeries performed in pediatric and geriatric patients is small, and because few facilities perform UWS, we have not yet been able to accumulate a sufficient case sample for comparative analysis to statistically prove the usefulness of UWS. More cases need to be collected and analyze to prove our hypothesis regarding UWS. This is particularly true for its application to extra-axial tumors in older adults, which, to our knowledge, is first described in this report.
