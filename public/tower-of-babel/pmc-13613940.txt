Epidemiological and Phylogenetic Analysis of Reassorted H10Nx Avian Influenza Viruses in Guangdong Province, China
Authors: Jie Liu, Qiyue Zhang, Haiyang Xiang, Yuanguo Li, Jing Liu, Qiucheng Yao, Yuwei Gao, Ye Ge
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsH10 subtype avian influenza viruses circulate widely among wild and domestic birds worldwide. These viruses have crossed species barriers to infect mammals, including humans, with cases of H10N3, H10N5 and H10N8 reported. During active surveillance in Guangdong Province, China, between 2023 and 2025, we isolated 10 H10Nx influenza A viruses from wild birds and domestic ducks. To characterise their epidemiology and evolutionary dynamics, we analysed public sequence and surveillance data and found H10 viruses across all inhabited continents, including more than 400 isolates from China.MethodsBayesian phylogeographic analysis revealed that China is an important transmission hub for Eurasian‐lineage H10 viruses.ResultsPhylogenetic analysis of the 10 H10 genes placed them within the North American lineage. Reassortment analysis indicated that these 10 isolates underwent complex gene exchange with multiple influenza A subtypes, resulting in five distinct genotypes. Some gene segments were identical to those found in avian H10N5 viruses that infect humans, indicating genetic relatedness to human‐infected strains, likely resulting from reassortment events. Notably, these isolates harboured several amino acid substitutions associated with mammalian adaptation. Frequent transmission at the wild bird–poultry interface highlights potential zoonotic risks.ConclusionsOur findings highlight the importance of H10 viruses as a public health risk and the need for intensified active surveillance in wild birds and poultry.

Introduction

Influenza A viruses affect a wide variety of hosts, including wild waterfowl, marine mammals, terrestrial mammals and humans. Wild waterfowl, especially species in the orders Anseriformes and Charadriiformes, are natural reservoirs of avian influenza viruses (AIVs). On the basis of the haemagglutinin (HA) and neuraminidase (NA) glycoproteins, AIVs are classified into H1–H19, H1–H16 and H19 (avian), and H17 and H18 (bats) [1, 2]. Through continuous mutation and reassortment, AIVs can cross species boundaries, posing significant zoonotic threats. Recently, human infections involving multiple subtypes of AIVs containing genetic material from wild birds, such as H3N8, H5N1, H6N1, H7N9, H9N2, H10N5, H10N7 and H10N8, have been reported, emphasising the persistent risk of AIV spillover from wild birds [3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9]. These spillover events underscore the persistent risk of wild bird‐origin IAVs adapting to mammals. Although efficient human‐to‐human transmission has not yet been established, ongoing cross‐species transmission highlights the potential for novel AIV subtypes to emerge as future public health threats [10].

The H10 subtype of influenza A virus was first isolated from chickens in Germany in 1949 [11]. Since then, H10 viruses have become widely distributed in both domestic poultry and wild birds across Eurasia, the Americas and Oceania. These viruses have undergone multiple genetic reassortments, yielding H10N1–H10N9 variants and expanding their host range from birds to mammals, including pigs, mink, seals and humans [12, 13, 14, 15, 16]. Human infections with several H10 subtypes, H10N3, H10N7 and H10N8, have been reported. The first recorded human infection with an H10 subtype involved H10N7 in Egypt in 2004 [17, 18, 19]. Two additional human H10N7 cases occurred in Sydney, Australia, in 2010. In 2013, H10N8 viruses caused three human infections in China, including one fatal case. The first H10N3 human case was reported in Jiangsu Province, China, in April 2021, and another case was reported in Zhejiang Province in 2022. In January 2024, a human coinfection with H3N2 and H10N5 was reported in Zhejiang Province, marking the first documented instance of human infection with avian influenza A (H10N5). An additional H10N3 human case was officially reported in April 2024 [1, 14, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24]. Although there is no indication of human‐to‐human spread, continuous monitoring of H10 viruses is essential.

Phylogenetic analysis revealed that the HA and NA genes of the human H10N5 virus originated from wild bird influenza viruses, with the HA gene belonging to the North American lineage and the NA gene to the Eurasian lineage. Human infections with H7N9, H7N4 and H3N8 viruses, whose genomes are partially or entirely derived from wild bird AIVs, have also been documented in recent years. Some H10Nx AIVs from wild birds and poultry can replicate efficiently in mice without prior adaptation, and a mouse‐adapted avian H10N7 AIV showed increased virulence in mice and acquired neurotropism [16, 25, 26, 27, 28, 29, 30]. A chicken‐derived H10N3 virus was found to be transmissible between guinea pigs via respiratory droplets [17, 31, 32]. Collectively, these findings indicate a relatively high potential for avian H10Nx AIVs to infect humans. Nearly all patients infected with H10N3, H10N8 or H10N7 AIVs had direct or indirect exposure to live poultry, underscoring the risk of spillover from poultry to humans. The threat of continuous interpersonal transmission posed by these novel reassorted AIVs, however, remains uncertain [20, 22, 33, 34, 35].

Recent spillover events involving diverse AIV subtypes from wild birds to humans emphasise the persistent risk of zoonotic transmission and the potential emergence of novel human‐adapted strains. These ongoing threats underscore the critical need for continuous surveillance of AIVs in their natural reservoirs. We report the circulation of H10 viruses among wild birds and poultry along distinct migratory flyways in China from 2022 to 2024. The public health threat risk from H10 AIVs should not be underestimated. Although no evidence of human‐to‐human transmission has been observed, continued monitoring of H10 viruses remains essential.

Materials and Methods

Sample Collection and Virus Isolation

A systematic sampling strategy was applied across reserves and live poultry markets in Guangdong Province, China. For wild birds, fresh faecal droppings were routinely gathered from ground surfaces in their habitats, whereas both oropharyngeal and cloacal swabs were obtained from wild birds captured during banding programmes, and internal tissues were collected from deceased wild birds. For poultry, sampling involved active and passive surveillance. Oropharyngeal and cloacal swabs were collected from healthy live birds (such as those newly introduced to markets), whereas internal tissue specimens (organs) were collected from deceased poultry. All the collected samples were immediately placed in phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) containing antibiotics and glycerol, stored at 4°C for short‐term preservation during transportation and processed within 24 h of arrival at the laboratory. The collected samples were mixed by shaking and clarified by centrifugation at 5000 rpm for 10 min, after which they were screened using real‐time RT–PCR to identify the M gene. Influenza‐positive samples, excluding those positive for highly pathogenic H5 and H7, were inoculated into 9–11‐day‐old SPF chicken embryos (Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Company of Boehringer Ingelheim, China) via the allantoic cavity and incubated at 37°C for 72 h. The embryos were subsequently photographed every 24 h. After 72 h, the embryos were refrigerated at 4°C overnight. Haemagglutination activity in the allantoic fluid was assessed using 1% chicken red blood cells. Allantoic fluid from haemagglutination‐positive embryos was collected for subsequent identification.

Whole‐Genome Sequencing (WGS)

RNA was extracted from allantoic fluid and yolk sac fluid using a viral RNA extraction kit (BayBio, China), followed by reverse transcription with a reverse transcription kit (Takara, Japan). RT–PCR detection was performed using the identification primers M‐229U (TTCTAACCGAGGTCGAAAC) and M‐229L (AAGCGTCTACGCTGCAGTCC), adhering to the national standards. To determine the complete genome, the eight genomic segments of the AIV isolates were amplified via PCR using universal Hoffmann primers. The PCR products were primarily sequenced using standard Sanger sequencing. Sequencing reads were assembled using the SeqMan module in DNAstar software.

Genetic Evolutionary Analysis

The complete genome sequences of H10 AIVs were retrieved from the NCBI Influenza Virus Database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) and GISAID (https://gisaid.org/) databases, with data updated through October 2025. Sequences with duplicates, ambiguous sampling times or unclear host information were excluded using BioAider software for subsequent analysis. Multiple sequence alignment was conducted with MAFFT implemented in Phylosuite software. A maximum‐likelihood tree was constructed with IQ‐TREE, employing ultrafast bootstrapping with support values ≥ 95%. Temporal signals were assessed using TempEst, and sequences without sufficient signals were excluded. The optimal nucleotide substitution model was selected in Phylosuite software. Bayesian evolutionary analysis was performed using BEAST v1.8.4 under strict and relaxed molecular clock models coupled with constant size, exponential growth and Bayesian skyline population models. Three independent Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) runs were conducted, each with 100 million generations, with sampling every 10,000 steps. Convergence was assessed to ensure effective sample sizes (ESS) > 200 after discarding an initial burn‐in of 10%. Subsequently, 1000 trees were resampled from the posterior distribution using LogCombiner. These trees were used for discrete trait analysis of host and subtype evolution in BEAST. Finally, TreeAnnotator was used to remove (burn‐in) the top 10% of the sampled results, and LogCombiner was used to merge the MCMC trees to generate the maximum clade credibility (MCC) trees. Trees were identified using TreeFile software [36]. Functionally important amino acid residues were identified by translation and examination of the open reading frames. Individual gene segments (PB2, PB1, PA, NP, M and NS) were subjected to maximum likelihood phylogenetic reconstruction with 1000 bootstrap replicates.

Spatiotemporal Dynamics Analysis

For phylogeographic reconstruction, the H10 HA gene sequences were analysed under a strict clock and constant population model using Bayesian stochastic search variable selection (BSSVS) [4, 36, 37]. Statistical support for transition rates between locations was assessed using Bayes factors (BFs) in SpreaD3, where a BF ≥ 3 indicated a supported route, a BF ≥ 10 indicated strong support and a BF ≥ 100 indicated decisive support. Finally, MCC trees were generated with TreeAnnotator after the burn‐in and merged tree samples were removed.

Evolutionary Rate Analysis

The full‐genome sequences of H10N5, H10N6 and H10N7 AIVs were aligned with MAFFT, and phylogenetic analysis was performed using IQ‐TREE to exclude potential interference from reassortment events. The coding regions (CDSs) were retained, and gene‐specific dN/dS values were calculated using DnaSP (v6.12.03).

Results

Isolation and Characterisation of H10 Subtype AIVs

Between 2023 and 2025, active surveillance for AIVs in migratory birds was conducted in Guangdong Province, China, including Zhanjiang, Guangzhou, Shenzhen, Zhuhai and Shanwei. Active surveillance for avian influenza in domestic waterfowl was also performed on the Leizhou Peninsula. A total of 14,166 samples were collected, including 12,764 samples from wild birds, mainly consisting of faecal samples, throat swabs, anal swabs and organ tissue samples from dead wild waterfowl, as well as 1402 samples from domestic waterfowl, covering faecal and internal organ samples. A total of 10 strains of the H10Nx AIV were isolated, including H10N5 (7 strains), H10N6 (2 strains) and H10N7 (1 strain). The main hosts were wild ducks and domestic ducks (Table 1). The accession numbers are PZ407452 to PZ407531 in the NCBI database.

Worldwide Prevalence of AIV Subtypes in the H10Nx Subtype

There were 2262 H10 subtype AIV strains in the GISAID database, containing H10N1–H10N9, until 31 October 2025. In terms of distribution, the number of H10Nx strains was greatest in North America, Asia and Europe. South America, Africa and Oceania had the lowest number of cases, with less than 100 (Figure 1a). Asia had 683 H10Nx strains, with China accounting for 448 strains. Within China, these viruses were concentrated in Jiangxi Province, followed by Taiwan and Jiangsu Province. Among the N1–N9 subtypes in China, H10N3 is the most prevalent, followed by H10N8. There have been reports of people being infected with the H10Nx AIV in Jiangsu, Zhejiang, Jiangxi, Guangxi and Yunnan Provinces (Figure 1b). Only one H10 AIV strain from Guangdong Province appears in the GISAID database: an H10N8 virus isolated from a duck in 2012. In the last decade, no H10 AIV strains have been recorded in Guangdong Province.

On a time scale, the number of reported cases of the H10 subtype of AIV was relatively low between 1956 and 2000. Since 2000, the rate has increased dramatically, particularly in 2009, 2013 and 2016, when the number of isolated viruses was highest. Since 2017, the number of isolated viruses has stabilised at approximately 60 strains. Among the nine subtypes, H10N7 was the most abundant (1055 cases), followed by H10N2 and H10N9. As of 31 October 2025, the GISAID database contained 1489 N5 genes, of which H5N5 was the most common (35.6%), followed by H10N5 (12.5%). The database included 8778 N6 genes; H5N6 accounted for 36.6%, whereas 91 H10N6 strains represented 1.04% of all N6 subtypes. There were 2831 N7 genes (covering H1N7–H15N7), with H10N7 comprising approximately one‐third of the N7 genes, at 37% (Figure 1c).

Host Distribution of H10 Subtype AIVs

Influenza A viruses of the H10 subtype exhibit a broad host range, infecting poultry, wild birds, mammals and humans. According to the host‐specific distribution of H10 subtypes, wild birds, particularly those in the orders Anseriformes and Charadriiformes, represent the primary reservoir. Within wild birds, the H10N7 subtype is widely distributed and appears in almost every avian order. In poultry, ducks account for the greatest proportion and diversity of H10 strains, followed by chickens, turkeys and geese, with H10N3 being the most prevalent subtype. Among nonhuman mammals, H10 infections are most frequently documented in harbour seals (predominantly H10N7), followed by bats (H10N8), minks (H10N4) and swine (H10N5). Human cases are relatively rare, with approximately 22 infections reported to date, primarily involving the H10N3, H10N5, H10N7 and H10N8 subtypes (Figure 2a). Historically, H10 viruses have been continuously isolated from wild birds and poultry since 1949, with sporadic spillovers to humans. Human H10 infections were recorded in 2004, 2010 and 2013, followed by a noticeable clustering of cases between 2021 and 2025 (Figure 2b). Within wild birds, H10 viruses are primarily found in Anseriformes and, to a lesser extent, in Charadriiformes, and the H10N7 subtype is widely distributed across avian orders. Human H10 infections occurred in 2004, 2010, 2013 and 2021–2025 (Figure 2c).

Evolution of H10Nx Genes Worldwide

Analysing the evolutionary dynamics of H10Nx across hosts is critical for clarifying its epidemic patterns in different species. On the basis of the collected H10Nx gene sequences, we performed a phylogenetic analysis and constructed evolutionary trees categorised by host type (wild birds, poultry, humans, seals and other mammals).

To elucidate the evolution of H10 AIVs in China, we conducted a phylogenetic analysis of HA genes from the H10 viruses sequenced in this study, along with sequences retrieved from the GISAID database. All HA genes from this study clustered within the North American lineage that circulates among wild birds in Korea and Japan. The pairwise nucleotide identity among these HA genes ranged from 81.5% to 99.8%. The HA genes of the two H10N6 viruses were closely related to those of the human H10N5 strain A/Zhejiang/CNIC‐ZJU01/2023 and grouped on the same branch, which also contained H10N7 strains isolated in Korea in 2022, an H10N4 strain isolated in Japan in 2022 and other wild‐bird H10 viruses isolated in Bangladesh in 2023 (Figure 3a). This novel human‐infecting H10N5 virus is a multisource reassortant, whereas seven of its gene segments—HA, NA, PB1, PB2, NP, M and NS—originated from H10N7 and other low pathogenic AIVs in wild Anseriformes (with HA, M and NS specifically tracing back to H10 and N7 subtypes). Its PA gene was uniquely contributed by H5N2 viruses from domestic Anseriformes [30]. In contrast, the seven strains of H10N5 and H10N7 clustered within the same clade as A/environment/Kagoshima/KU‐24‐4c/2024. This clade also included H10N4 isolated in South Korea in 2023, H10N7 isolated in Qinghai in 2022 and H10N9 isolated in Liaoning in 2020.

We detected multiple NA subtypes within each H10 sublineage and conducted phylogenetic analyses for three major NA subtypes, namely, N5, N6 and N7. All NA genes from H10 AIVs in this study clustered within the Eurasian lineage (Figure 3b–d). N5 genes from the H10N5 strains here were closely related to a human‐origin H10N5 virus, with the duck as the predominant host. The N5 gene cluster showed the highest identity with sequences derived from wild birds. At the geographic clade level, the TMRCA of the Eurasian–Asian N5 clade was 1975 (95% HPD: 1971–1979), which is earlier than that of the North American clade (1981, 95% HPD: 1979–1983), indicating that the Eurasian–Asian region may represent the early divergence and transmission centre for this N5 clade. In this study, the TMRCA of the six duck‐derived N5 genes ranged from June 2022 to February 2024 (95% HPD: 2022–2023), which is consistent with the relationship with the human H10N5 virus. Two H10N6 viruses were included in this study, one isolated from a wild duck and one from a domestic duck; both showed the highest identity to H4N6 and/or H10N6 viruses from wild birds (Figure 3b). The N6 genes belonged to the Eurasian lineage and were closely related to AIVs from multiple regions (e.g., Italy, Ilan, South Korea and Sweden). In this study, the TMRCA of the two N6 genes occurred in June 2024 (95% HPD: February 2024–November 2024) (Figure 3b). Only one strain of the wild duck was isolated in this study (H10N7), and this strain also belonged to the Eurasian lineage. The N7 gene was closely related to AIV H10N7 and H7N7 from wild birds and environmental samples, respectively. Overall, the N7 gene was close to the Eurasian migration lineage (Figure 3c).

Analysis of internal genes revealed complex reassortment among the 10 H10Nx viruses. The five genes, PB2, PA, NP, M and NS, clustered into three groups, whereas PB1 clustered into four groups, reflecting substantial genomic diversity. Notably, the PB2 and NS genes of the H10N5 isolate from ducks and the PA gene of the H10N5 isolate from wild ducks in our study showed high similarity with those of human‐infecting H10N5 (Figure S1). The phylogenetic diversity and respective groupings of each gene segment were analysed, and the 10 isolates were classified into five distinct genotypes (Figure S2). These results indicate that H10Nx viruses naturally present in migratory birds and domestic ducks have evolved into different lineages and undergone complex genetic reassortment between wild birds and poultry, particularly among waterfowl.

Spread of H10 Subtype Influenza Viruses

Wild birds facilitate the global dissemination of AIVs, driving complex spillover and spillback dynamics between wild and domestic bird populations. To trace these pathways, we conducted a discrete phylogeographic analysis based on the HA gene sequences of H10 subtype AIVs. Global‐scale migration reconstruction suggested that Oceania (OC) represents a significant evolutionary source, seeding viral lineages to China (CN, BF > 1000) (Figure 4a). Within East Asia, a highly supported pathway was identified from South Korea (KR) to China (CN, BF > 1000). The virus in China was transmitted from South Korea and Oceania. Subsequently, China acted as a critical epicentral hub, disseminating viruses to North America (NAm, BF > 1000) and to other Asian regions (AS, BF > 1000) and Africa (AF, BF > 100). In turn, viral lineages introduced into North America exhibited spillover pathways to South Korea (KR) and Asia (AS).

On a domestic scale within China, we observed several crucial long‐distance dispersal events. These included highly supported migration pathways spanning from northeastern provinces to the eastern coast, specifically Heilongjiang to Taiwan (HLJ → TW, BF > 100) and Yunnan to Hebei (YN → HeB, BF > 100). The HLJ → TW route likely mirrors the East Asian–Australasian Flyway (EAAF), suggesting that wild waterfowl play a significant role in long‐distance viral transport. Furthermore, Ningxia (NX), Yunnan (YN) and Hebei (HeB) functioned as major transmission hubs, featuring dense networks of inbound and outbound connectivity. These regions likely act as critical interfaces where migratory birds and domestic poultry interact, facilitating viral spillover and subsequent spread through local poultry transport networks. Owing to the lack of definitive BF support for a single root state, the geographic origin of these H10 strains within China cannot be conclusively attributed to Guangdong (Figure 4b).

Selection Pressure Analyses

We evaluated the protein‐coding sequences of H10N5, H10N6 and H10N7 for natural selection using dN/dS ratios. All dN/dS ratios were < 1, suggesting that HA, NA, PB2, PB1, NP, PA, M and NS proteins evolved under purifying selection. Genes encoding surface glycoproteins (HA, N6 and N7), core replication proteins (PA and PB2) and structural matrix proteins (M) exhibited strong purifying selection (dN/dS < 0.2), indicating that these genes are functionally conserved and highly resistant to amino acid substitutions. In contrast to the viral proteins discussed above, the N5, PB1 and NS glycoproteins exhibited substantially higher ω values (between 0.4 and 0.6). Molecular Characterisation

To further characterise these 10 viruses, we analysed molecular markers in their surface and internal genes. All 10 isolates retained the conserved avian‐like receptor markers E190, D193, Q226 and G228 (H3 number), suggesting a baseline preference for avian‐type α‐2,3‐linked sialic acid receptors. Notably, two wild duck‐origin strains, ZJ1610 and ZJ1803, harboured the R137K substitution, which is identical to the human‐infecting H10N3 reference strains (A/Jiangsu/428/2021 and A/SKLID‐ZJU01/2022). All 10 isolates possessed Q222, in contrast to the human H10N3 reference strains, which carried R222 (Figure 5). All 10 isolates shared the amino acid sequence PEIVQGR ↓ GLF at the HA cleavage site, except ZJ282, which carried PEVVQGR ↓ GLF (the same sequence as human‐infected H10N5). This pattern is consistent with the Q/E‐X‐R LPAIV cleavage motif. The PB2 substitutions E627K, A588V and D701N were associated with increased viral polymerase activity and enhanced replication in mammalian cells. None of the 10 H10Nx viruses analysed here carried these substitutions, which indicates that these H10Nx isolates have not acquired increased virulence or transmission potential in mammals. A D‐to‐S substitution at position 66 in PB1‐F2 (D66S) has been linked to increased viral pathogenicity. This change was present in all the strains studied except ZJ282 and ZJ364. All 10 viruses harboured the P42S substitution in NS1 and the N30D and T215A substitutions in M1, mutations that have been implicated in increased virulence in mice. The NS1 proteins of these viruses also contained a C‐terminal ESEV (Glu‐Ser‐Glu‐Val) motif. Taken together, these molecular features suggest that the risk of H10 virus infections from wild birds posing a substantial threat to the general public remains low.

Discussion

Whereas H10 AIVs are found mainly in wild bird reservoirs, their spread to other continents depends heavily on flyways. Ten H10Nx viruses of the North American lineage were detected in domestic ducks and in a wild duck from Guangdong. Because the EAAF is seeded by Eurasian lineage viruses, North American lineage viruses represent transhemispheric gene flow by migration or are introduced by the international live‐bird trade. The high nucleotide identity of the HA gene between our isolates and wild bird strains (Figure 3), combined with the geographic overlap of domestic duck habitats and migratory stopover sites in Guangdong, suggests habitat compression. The spatiotemporal co‐occurrence of high‐density domestic poultry and wild birds carrying exotic lineages was the main driver of the local establishment of these viruses [13, 17, 38].

Domestic ducks act as distinct filters in the evolution of AIV. Our phylogenetic analysis revealed that surface genes (HA and NA) of the H10N5 isolates were homologous to those of a recent human‐infecting H10N5 strain (A/Zhejiang/CNIC‐ZJU01/2023). From a molecular perspective, this suggests that the virus was likely the first adaptation to mammalian receptor binding preference (or at least dual binding) during spillover from wild birds to poultry. Ducks are far from passive reservoirs; rather, they serve as critical hosts where viruses gradually gain enhanced replication capacity and mammalian adaptability [39]. This ‘poultry‐adaptation‐then‐human‐spillover’ pathway potentially explains why live poultry markets serve as critical incubators for novel reassortants. Beyond surface antigenicity, the compatibility of internal gene constellations is a key determinant of zoonotic potential. The human‐infecting H10N5 virus appears to represent a distinct wild‐to‐domestic reassortment pattern, possessing a predominantly wild bird‐origin backbone (seven segments, including PB2, PB1, NP and NS) integrated with a domestic poultry H5N2‐derived PA gene. Notably, five of the six internal genes (PB2, PB1, PA, NP and NS) of our H10 isolates clustered closely with this human H10N5 index strain, suggesting that this specific ‘internal gene cassette’ may have persisted at the local wild bird–poultry interface since at least 2023. This could serve as a risk indicator, implying that the molecular components associated with human infection might not be transient evolutionary anomalies but could be undergoing consolidation within regional poultry populations [40]. Furthermore, this evolutionary pathway seems to differ from that of other typical human‐infecting reassortants in China. For instance, human cases of H10N3, H7N9 and H5N6 were primarily associated with reassorted viruses whose entire internal gene backbones were obtained from endemic poultry H9N2 lineages. In contrast, the H10N5 virus successfully bypassed the H9N2 backbone, demonstrating that a compatible zoonotic genotype can potentially be assembled via wild‐bird LPAIVs combined with specific poultry‐derived segments. Nevertheless, the co‐circulation of these stabilised H10 genotypes alongside endemic H9N2 and highly pathogenic H5/H7 AIVs in poultry hubs poses a continuing challenge. Should these circulating H10 viruses undergo further reassortment—either by donating their established internal cassette to other subtypes or by acquiring highly transmissible segments from H9N2 or H5/H7 lineages—the risk of generating novel variants with pandemic potential could be elevated. Therefore, continuous active surveillance at the wild bird–poultry interface remains of paramount importance [30].

The receptor specificity of AIVs is a critical determinant of host range and zoonotic transmission. Whereas the Q226L and G228S mutations are classic markers of human‐type receptor binding in the H2, H3 and H7 subtypes, structural studies have revealed that H10 subtype viruses utilise distinct mechanisms to interact with eukaryotic receptors. Previous crystallography studies on human‐infecting H10N8 (A/Jiangxi‐Donghu/346/2013) demonstrated that H10 HA can bind to human‐type α‐2,6 receptors despite retaining the avian‐signature Q226 and G228 residues. This dual‐receptor binding capability is largely mediated by structural networks involving the 130‐loop and 220‐loop. In our study, whereas all 10 isolates maintained Q222, Q226 and G228, the wild‐duck isolates ZJ1610 and ZJ1803 possessed the R137K mutation in the 130‐loop. Arg‐to‐Lys substitution at position 137 has been reported to alter the local hydrogen bond network and conformation of the 130‐loop, potentially modulating receptor binding affinity or specificity, as observed in human H10N3 isolates. The presence of R137K in wild duck isolates suggests that H10 viruses circulating in wild birds may already possess molecular precursors that favour mammalian adaptation. Future studies utilising glycan microarrays and animal transmission models are warranted to experimentally evaluate the precise contribution of these structural variations to the zoonotic potential of ongoing H10 avian influenza reassortants.

Our conclusions are limited by existing surveillance biases and uneven spatiotemporal distributions of global AIV data (surveillance gaps). However, the genomic evidence presented here reveals that there is an active H10 gene pool in Guangdong's domestic ducks. In summary, H10 subtype viruses evolve from wild birds to a One Health concern, which is strongly driven by humans. Continuous monitoring of H10 and its internal gene pool should focus on the diversity dynamics of the gene cassette, as these dynamics are key signals for predicting the next cross‐species outbreak.
