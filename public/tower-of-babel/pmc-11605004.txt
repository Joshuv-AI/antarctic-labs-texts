Effect of acute fatigue on force generation accuracy and repeatability in youth athletes
Authors: Agnieszka D Jastrzębska
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe study aimed to evaluate the acute effect of the incremental running test on the accuracy and repeatability of submaximal force generation in adolescent athletes (biathletes and runners) with different training regimes. The isometric force generation was assessed in biathletes and runners by examining left and right elbow extension (LEE; REE); flexion (LEF; REF); left and right knee extensions (LKE; RKE) before and after the test. The mean force of 10 sub-maximal targeted force pulses (Fmean) and force variability parameters: standard deviation and coefficient variation were used for accuracy and repeatability estimation. Biathletes generated forces more accurate and exhibit better repeatability of produced forces than runners. Mean force measured in fatigue conditions, for both groups, did not vary significantly from rest measurements. Before testing biathletes exhibited significantly lower REE and LEE, and after testing lower REF and LEF variability than runners. After testing there was a significant drop of variability in elbows flexion but not extension and RKE in biathletes. Insignificant downward trend of variability parameters were found in runners. The presented findings corroborate sport-specific force generation ability alteration in response to exhaustive effort. Tracking force generation over time may be useful in the monitoring of training loads and specific training adaptations.Keywords: Isometric force, Force repeatability, Fatigue, Adolescents, Biathletes, RunnersSubject terms: Physiology, Neurophysiology

Introduction

The motor coordination is an important factor of efficiency and a predictor of sport performance. The interactions among commends from central nervous system and sensory feedback from muscles, skin, joints, or visuals allow the precise selection of the contraction force, speed, or movement trajectory1. One of the manifestation of motor coordination is the ability of force selection adequate for the performed task2. Its misjudgements can lead to movement imprecision, increased physiological cost, or potential injury.

The factor influencing the force generation capacity is fatigue, multi-factorial phenomenon, centrally and/or peripherally originated, which leads to inevitable consequences including a decrement in absolute force and an increase in isometric force generation variability3,4 and/or inability to generate the adequate force for task performed. The muscle force generation, has been extensively investigated after vary types of muscle work and using different exercise protocols. Most of research showed decrease in force generation patterns (its maximal value, repeatability or matching targeted force level) after: eccentric4–7, isometric tasks4,8,9, however, mainly after selected muscle groups exercise4,6,10 and long-term efforts11–14.

A limited number of studies addressed force generation accuracy after whole-body efforts. Previous reports indicate a notable decline in maximal force ranging from 11 to 18% post-strenuous exercise14–16 increasing up to 30% after prolonged exercise, such as a marathon completed in 3 h17. Force repeatability after whole-body effort assessed in athletes showed an improvement in force generation accuracy in sport-task-specific muscles16,18, and with training experience19. The present study was designed to examine force sense accuracy measured based on the submaximal isometric force repeatability triggered in fatigue conditions without visual feedback, which allowed assessment of force sensation based on internal sensation from the muscles and partly from the skin (skin touch with sensors). Before each force generation, the participants were allowed to adapt the positon of the examined body parts. The author realize that this approach may arouse controversy, because it is rather impossible to grasp the sensor the exact same way, which means that different tactile receptors were stimulated; however, the cutaneous receptors play a minor role during isometric contractions, since there is no movement20. Furthermore, environment, surface, equipment, fatigue, etc. may influence the movement, therefore, balanced muscle activity within force couples should be reached regardless of the situation. Considering that there is an infinite number of strategies utilized to activate muscles during the course of the tasks21, we were interested in the athlete’s ability to reproduce the given force in fatigue condition, regardless of the strategy chosen by the nervous system. This approach remains in contrast with the research oriented to the ability to reproduce targeted forces with respect to maximal force contraction by separated muscle groups after various types of muscle work and in conditions of a non-compliant strap to minimize body movements7,22.

Based on limited existing evidence the hypothesis, that training regime, dependent on sport discipline, affect the force sensation accuracy in fatigue condition was tested. To test this hypothesis, the uncontrolled (without visual feedback), submaximal targeted force generations by upper and lower limbs were examined in youth athletes, representing biathlon and running. As in sport, we usually deal with efforts engaging the whole body, for fatigue development, we decided to use an incremental running test (IRT) to volitional exhaustion due to its similarity to the movement structure of examined athletes. Volitional exhaustion coincide with maximum metabolic and cardio-vascular, as well as motor command23. It was hypothesized that, in runners, force sensation will decrease in task-specific muscles (legs) but remain unchanged in non-task-specific muscles (arms), while in biathletes force sensation will not change in task-specific muscles (legs) and may even be improved in non-task-specific muscles (arms).

Methods

Ethical approval

Prior to commencing the study, informed consent was given by all of the participants and their parents. The study was conducted following the ethical aspects of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by University Senate Research Ethics Commission (24.02.2010) of Wroclaw University of Health and Sport Sciences.

Participants

Thirty-six competitive, healthy athletes U-18 (18 runners (R) and 18 biathletes (B)) volunteered to participate in this study (Table 1). Participants were free of any musculoskeletal injury in the last 12 months (Training years 5.0 ± 1.0 yr). All participants were right handed. The sample size was estimated using G*Power software (version 3.1) The minimum sample size to yield a statistical power of at least .76 with an alpha of .05 and a large effect size (f = 0.8) is 18. The participants were free of any known neuromusculoskeletal impairments and upper or lower extremity injuries within the previous year.

Experimental design

The intervention was a non-randomized controlled before-and-after trial. All the test were performed under the same temperature (21 °C) and humidity (55%) conditions, at the same time of day (9.00–13.00), in the Exercise Laboratory of the University of Sport and Health Sciences (PN-EN ISO 9001:2001 certified), during the pre-start mesocycles.

Incremental running test and physiological measurement

Participants were instructed to refrain from any physical activity 48-hours prior to experiment. The incremental running tests to volitional fatigue (IRT) were performed on an electrical treadmill with initial speed set at 6 km/h and increasing every 3 min by 2km/h. The test ended by participant’s volitional exhaustion (assumed when the participants were not able to run with given velocity; the HR reached predicted value calculated from formula 220-age ± 10rpm24; the oxygen consumption (VO2) decreases with a concurrent increase in minute ventilation and heart rate; the oxygen consumption decreases with a concurrent increase in run speed. VO2 and minute ventilation (VE) were measured continuously throughout the test, using K4b2analyser (Cosmed, Milan, Italy) calibrated in line with the manufacturer’s instructions, and averaged every 30 s. Heart rate (HR) was continuously measured by using a Sport Tester S810 by Polar (Polar, Kempele, Finland). Before and in the third minute after IRT, fingertip blood samples were drawn for the determination of blood lactate concentration (BLa) [mmol] using a LT400 photometer (Dr. Lange, Germany). Before IRT, participants were familiarized with the 6–20 Borg scale25 to rate the level of perceived exertion (RPE). Immediately after the IRT each participant was asked to assess own exertion using the Borg scale.

Isometric force measurement

A custom-designed station (Polish patent #PL213505B1) was used for upper and lower extremities force measurements. Its validity and repeatability was previously verified26,27. The experimental rig consisted of a chair with adjustable back and seat for proper angles in joints maintenance during measurements: elbows at 90°, lower limbs at the knees at 100°, and a foot rest at 90° in relation to the shin, and three force transducers: two stiffly fixed to the chair for measuring the upper limbs, and one situated at the base of chair for measuring the lower limbs. The forearm was positioned in pronation. Force signals were analogue-to-digitally converted at sampling rate of 1000 Hz (CP-168U, V1.2., 8P-232, Moxo Technologies Co., Ltd. Taiwan), and software (Kinestesjometr ver. 1.0) allowing for initial data analysis. The station was calibrated with a 1, 5 and 10 kg weight before each test.

The maximal isometric force (IMVC) and sub-maximal forces were examined before IRT and after 5 min of recovery. First, the participants performed 3 explosive IMVC forward (extension), then backward (flexion) by upper limbs, and next 3 extensions by lower limbs, each separated by 45s of rest. The highest values were taken for analysis.

For force accuracy and repeatability examination, six trials of 10 sub-maximal isometric force pulses with targeted force of 98 N (10 kg): right and left elbow extension (REE and LEE), right and left elbow flexion (REF and LEF), right and left knee extension (RKE and LKE). During the arms examination, the free hand was placed at the lap, and during the legs examination the arms were crossed over the chest. The pelvis was immobilized by the belt to keep participants upright and unable to lean against the backrest, to eliminate additional feedback. Participants were instructed to reach the indexed peak of force (98 N) as fast as possible and relax immediately right after reaching targeted force, and leave the force transducer as fast as possible. At least, in order to familiarize participants with the procedure and the measurement station, participants performed five isometric force pulses by each limb, in each direction with an assumed force of 98 N to memorize the force pattern. The required and triggered force was visualized on a screen placed at a 1 m distance. The pressure force was chosen in accordance with a previous study where the submaximal force accuracy and repeatability was measured concidering three stages of force generated (49 N, 98 N and 147 N) and the results showed that force of 98 N is sufficient for force variability assessment27, and for reference14,16,28.

Statistical analysis

Data were processed using the Statistica 13.1 software package (StatSoft Inc.,Tulsa, OK, USA). Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05 and the data were expressed as mean ± SD. The mean generated force of 10 reps (Fmean) [N] and two measures of variability of force used extensively by many previous studies: the standard deviation of F (FSD), and the coefficient of variation (CV) were calculated. CV was defined as the mean force generated divided by SD and multiplied by 100%. Data normality was assessed through the Shapiro-Wilk test. Once the assumption of normality was confirmed, parametric tests were involved. Student’s paired t-test was performed to test significance between pre- and post-effort trials and Student’s unpaired t-test was performed to test significance between the two examined groups.

Results

The examined groups are similar in anthropological characteristic and fitness level (Table 1). The only significant differences were noted in metabolic reaction between the groups where biathletes reach higher values of hydrogen ions (H+) and blood lactate (BLa) concentration.

There was no significant difference between runners and biathletes in the values of force exerted during an isometric maximum voluntary contraction (IMVC) by the upper limbs, in both pre- and post-test conditions (Fig. 1A). In contrast, for knee extensions significant differences (P < 0.000) were found between two groups, and following IRT, there was a significant, 15–17% reduction of isometric maximum voluntary contraction in biathletes for both right and left knee extension (LKE and RKE respectively) (P < 0.000), and 19% for both right and left knee extension (P < 0.000) in runners (Fig. 1). For upper limbs IMVC decreased by 3–4 N in both biathletes and runners.

Analysis of submaximal isometric force pulses repeatability for both groups in pre-test conditions shows that the mean generated force (Fmean) of 10 pushes was overestimated in the relation to the assumed force of 98 N: 8.5–13% for arms and 28–31% for legs respectively in biathletes and 18–23% for arms and 41–42% for legs respectively in runners. Both groups executed similar forces by arms ± 3 N after IRT comparing to pre-test conditions, 11–15% error rate in biathletes and 20–24% in runners, respectively. The only significant decrease of Fmean for LKE 126.3 ± 12.3 versus 116.8 ± 11.9 N (P < 0.05) in biathletes was found, and the accuracy in this case in reaching force target increased by 7%. Insignificant decrease of submaximal force production by legs, of 4 N, was noted in runners (Fig. 1B).

Biathletes generated the indicated force of 98 N more accurately then runners in both pre- and post-test trials. In rest conditions, significant differences between groups appeared for REF (P < 0.03), LEE (P < 0.02), LEF (P < 0.02), RKE (P < 0.03) and LKE (P < 0.01), and in post-test conditions for LEF (P < 0.04) RKE (P < 0.01) and LKE (P < 0.001) measurements (Fig. 1B).

Student’s unpaired t-test between groups (B/R) (Table 2) showed statistically significant differences in FSD and CV for elbow extension movements (REE: FSD, 9.2 ± 3.5 versus 12.6 ± 3.4 N, P < 0.005; CV, 8.4 ± 2.9 versus 10.9 ± 3.1%, P < 0.02; LEE: FSD, 9.6 ± 3.0 versus 13.6 ± 5.0 N, P < 0.005; CV, 9.0 ± 2.5 versus 11.6 ± 3.9%, P < 0.02) and LKE (FSD, 15.5 ± 5.6 versus 20.8 ± 4.7 N, P < 0.004; CV, 12.2 ± 4.3 versus 15.1 ± 3.5%, P < 0.05) in pre-test conditions. In post-test measurements, the variability parameters (FSD and CV) for elbow flexion movements were smaller in biathletes than in runners (REF: FSD, 8.8 ± 1.6 versus 11.8 ± 4.5 N, P < 0.01; CV, 8.0 ± 1.5 versus 10.1 ± 3.3%, P < 0.02; LEF: FSD, 9.1 ± 2.0 versus 12.4 ± 3.4 N, P < 0.001; CV, 8.3 ± 2.2 versus 10.1 ± 2.1%, P < 0.02) and LKE (FSD 15.0 ± 3.3 versus 18.5 ± 5.0 N, P < 0.02) (Fig. 2A and B).

Discussion

Based on limited existing evidence, the hypothesis that training specificity regiments causes force sensation changes in fatigue condition, was tested. The submaximal targeted force generation was estimated in two youth athletes group after an incremental running graded test (IRT) performed to volitional exhaustion, and the acute effect of IRT on the accuracy and repeatability of submaximal isometric force generation by the upper and lower limbs in athletes. The examination were conducted in second part of pre-start mesocycle, when training load intensity start to increase with concurrent decrease of training volume and specific for sport disciplines training tasks start to dominates. From the two groups tested in this study, runners who generally have less upper body-activated training regimens had higher error in generating forces in relation to the targeted force of 98 N, in both pre- and post-effort conditions. Although the runners’ maximal arm forces in pre- and post-test conditions were similar to biathletes (Fig. 1A), they also exhibited lower force sensation than biathletes, measured by the ability to reproduce the given force (Fig. 1B) and described here by two variability parameters: FSD[N] and CV[%] (Fig. 2A and B). However, the hypothesis that force sensation decreases in runners with fatigue developed by a maximal incremental test (IRT) based on the result obtained herain, may be rejected as mean force and any measured force repeatability parameters did not significantly decrease in this group after effort. Conversely, biathletes shows better accuracy of targeted force production and improvement in its variability for REF and LEF (keeping the rifle during shooting) and RKE (posture stabilization during vertical shooting). It is likely, that any sport has favourable nonspecific effect on improvement sensory, muscular and integrative central nervous components of the force regulation.

Both tested groups were similar in anthropological characteristic and fitness level (Table 1), which allowes for comparisions. The only difference was noticed in H+and BLa accumulation, where the biathletes reached higher values. While the role of lactate in blood acidification is disputed, there is no doubt, that involvement of large muscle groups (which do we have during ITR, results in a higher amount of lactate released into the blood compared to the lactate utilized by less active muscle29 and other tissues30. This allows to consider blood lactate level as a qualitative indicator of glycolysis intensification and metabolic stress.

In both groups the significant reduction in ability to produce maximal force (IMVC) by lower limbs was noticed (P < 0.000). It can be attributed to increased H+ concentration by 33% in runners and 40% in biathletes. Whole body exercise, like running, and the associated elevation of metabolic-by-products, resulted from successively increasing load and, consequently, exercise intensity limits the ability to generate IMVC by the muscle directly involved in exercise. The insignificant influence of increased H+ concentration on IMVC by upper limbs may be attributed to the role of the arms during running, which is swinging for vertical oscillation balance of the body. This results shows, that increased H+ions concentration in blood, does not influence the maximum muscle force generation measured in acute fatigue condition, directly after a strenuous effort, and that fatigue after IRT is rather of peripheral origin than central. The drop in IMVC by lower limbs is resulted rather by intramuscular changes related to increased metabolic-by-products in it31. It has been demonstrated that parameters describing force regulation properties: FSDand CV are related to sports discipline. After the IRT, runners demonstrated no significant changes in force repeatability in both upper and lower limbs. Insignificant decreases in variability parameters with concurrent increase in force error (by next 3–4 N, compared to pre-test condition) in relation to the assumed force of 98 N does not authorize the statement that the runners improved their force sensation as a result of the performed effort. It is possible, however, that the method used in this study may be insufficient to detect changes in force sensation during acute post-exercise fatigue in runners. Expanding the study to include additional levels of submaximal force27 or dynamic movements within a specified range32,33 could help demonstrate the proprioceptive sensitivity in this group of subjects.

Conversely, biathletes showed better accuracy in achieving the target force than runners (Fig. 1B) and force reproducibility (Fig. 2A and B) in both pre- and post-effort conditions. Moreover, biathletes improved in force repeatability in REF and LEF measurements in post-effort conditions, which is in agreement with previous study16. The significant decline of FSD[N] and CV[%] in flexion and insignificant increase in extension after IRT may be linked to neural adaptation on sensory, muscular and integrative central nervous component levels. Competitive requirements in biathlon where arm flexors are strongly engaged during the pooling phase and where the athletes must keep the rifle stable during shooting may promote such adaptation. It was previously reported9,34–36 that group III and IV afferents, stimulated by metabolic-by-products, increasing inhibitory feedback to motor neurons37 for elbow extensor muscles9but not elbow flexor muscles34,35. Also, Golgi organs, specifically engaged in force sensation and regulation, can change their force-signalling properties and become more sensitive to force generation after the effort38. Alternatively, training-induced changes in muscle architecture (such as increases in muscle physiological cross-sectional area, angle pennation, and/or muscle thickness) may contribute to proper force selection39–41.

Considering these data and presented herein results (no changes in force sensation in runners and specific improvements only for REF, LEF and RKE in biathletes), differences in the ability to execute the requested forces by both groups may be derived from the nature of training. In biathlon the complex combination of movement components (poling, one-legged or two-legged ski sliding, balance during skiing and shooting) causes the level of neuromuscular coordination of biathletes to be greater than that of runners, especially in sport-task-specific muscles. The technique of running basically boils down to alternating lower limbs with an integral role of arms swinging for vertical oscillation balance. Likewise, biathletes start shooting in a heavily fatigued state after covering a certain cross-country distance. So they are familiar with exertion of proper forces, isometric contraction when fatigued, while runners start recovery after covering the distance. Similarly, improvement of force generation repeatability in elbow flexion movements in swimmers18 is suggested to be linked to specificity of swimming training and counteracting water resistance while swimming. A recent study28 shows that 1-month sea sailing reduced the lower limb proprioceptive sensitivity in professional and recreational sailors. These results indicate, that spending one month sailing on a seagoing vessel and walking on an unstable surface, while experiencing increased tension in muscles, tendons, and ligaments, can lead to the development of a new strategy for muscle properties. This adaptation may involve reducing tension to enhance body balance in these specific conditions. The mentioned literature results and the results obtained here suggest that training experience, type of load and exercise may modulate muscle force sensing ability.

The influence of training specificity on force sensation is additionally supported by the results of force sensation by the legs. Only, the RKE variability improvement (FSD, P < 0.02; CV, P < 0.05) in biathletes was noticed (Fig. 2A and B). The biathletes precede the swing phase by a phase of ski repulsion from the surface, which additionally increases the surface of resistance; the runners just swing their legs. Moreover, the examined biathletes used their right leg for posture stabilization during shooting. With increasing leg effort load, due to the metabolic changes in working muscles, the force sensation and muscle coordination decrease, and a decline in the ability to generate force can be noticed35. However, specificity of biathlete training may positively affect the force sensation level even in fatigue conditions16.

As the examined subjects were adolescents U18 it shoud be pointed, their adaptive capabilities at the nervous, neurohormonal, and neuromuscular levels in response to training loads are still significant. Althought the sensory maturation is reached at 9 years of age (visual, proprioceptive and finally vestibular)42. Whereas, piramidal systems attains functional maturiry during puberty which in boys in complete at ages 16–17. That is time, when individuals may fully integrate the nervous activity from peripheral sensory outputs and various level of central nervous centers to develop fine coordinated movements, force regulation, postural balance. It is also time when the sport training with its disciplines specificity, start to be more targeted - training load and intensity increases results in proprioceptive sense improvement (e.g. strangh training improves shoulde proprioceptive sense, and the training intensity had a positive impact on level of improvements)43.

One more finding comes from the present research. The performed test engaged the whole body and was executed up to exhaustion. Heavy exercise engaged large muscle groups develops both peripheral and central fatigue, although the duration of the exercise is not negligible. Performed IRT took participants of about 20 min, and the fatigue developed by was acute and rather of peripheral origin, and rather local than general. This may be evidenced by the fact that the IMVC decreases only in the case of lower limbs directly involved in the effort, and not in the case of upper limbs, mainly engaged to stabilize the posture. Moreover, if it was of central origin, we would have observed declines in force reproducibility in both groups. As we did not notice decreases in force reproducibility, it can be postulated that such effort dose not induce central fatigue44,45.

Limitations

The limitation of the study is a relatively small sample size of athletes in each sport group (n = 18) which is inherent to research in sport study.Although, similar training status and fitness level allowed for inter-group comparison. In the case of the small groups engaged in the study, the observed findings may be less conclusive, as an individual variability in the training process, anthropometric parameters, and performance level may influence the results obtained.

The training status without specific characterization of training loads, different type of training completed by the two groups, especially in mesocycle prior the examination also may be treated as a limitation. As training periodization cause fluctuations in level of effort adaptation, and motor coordination in it, which manifests itself in the ability to adjust the precise force generation, future studies in different seasonal training cycles are needed.

Studies involving other sport groups are required to confirm the usefulness of submaximal isometric force generation accuracy, repeatability and/or steadiness in evaluation of sport specific, neuro-muscular adaptation. Further studies include larger cohort and more comprehensive training load monitoring may strength the conclusion.

The other limitation may be the only one level of force accuracy measurement (98 N). Expanding the measurments on more force levels, or/and accuracy in a given movement execution, could give more informative data. It is very likely that in case of releasing of subaximal force at different levels of maximum, certain changes will also appear in runners. Using a few level of force accuracy examination may shed light on the level of proprioceptive sensitivity during acute post-exercise fatigue.

Conclusions

The significant differences between both groups in submaximal force generation (Table 2) with similar fitness levels (Table 1) may suggest sport-specific adaptation in neuromuscular coordination of examined muscle groups. Additionally, the higher acidosis in biathletes (BLa 10.53 ± 1.06; 11.62 ± 1.8 [mM] in runners and biathletes respectively) which reflects metabolic stress, along with the similar level of fatigue (RPE 19.2 ± 0.8; 19.1 ± 0.9) also indicate on better neural control of force generation by biathletes which can be seen in results of variability parameters (FSD, CV%). Biathletes who experience higher acidosis and improved force repeatability in both right and left arm flexion pulses, and knee extension pulses, demonstrate more subtle/greater neuromuscular regulation of muscle groups engaged in tasks in fatigue condition. The lack in force generation variability improvement in runners with concurrent decrease in force generation variability in biathletes after exhaustive incremental test may suggest that receptive sensitivity and feedback can change with a specific training process (dynamic effort with muscle work against the resistance). This hypothesis requires further examination. The present study has also practical approach. The simple measurement of force repeatability may indicate the proper training load adjustment and resulting motor adaptive changes in biathlets, however not in runners. Obtained results in runners may be due to methodological constraints (only one level of force generation accuracy and only isometric force measurments were used for force accuracy measurements). It is maybe, that for runners propoused level of force generation was too insensitive and other should be used.
