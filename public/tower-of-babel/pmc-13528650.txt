Inhaled Therapy Adherence in COPD:A COM-B-Based Narrative Review of Determinants and Intervention Strategies
Authors: Yayue Zhang, Wen Shen, Qingdie Zhao, Zhenyan Li, Yue Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMedication adherence is defined as the extent to which patients take medications as prescribed by their healthcare providers, encompassing three critical dimensions: initiation (starting the prescribed therapy), implementation (taking doses correctly as scheduled), and persistence (continuing treatment for the recommended duration). Long-term management of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) relies heavily on inhalation therapy; however, real-world adherence remains suboptimal, with reported medication possession ratios (MPR) typically ranging from 40% to 60% in observational studies. Poor adherence is associated with increased exacerbation frequency, accelerated lung function decline, and substantial healthcare costs.PurposeBased on the Capability-Opportunity-Motivation-Behaviour (COM-B) model, this narrative review aims to summarize and conceptually organize existing literature on measurement methods, epidemiological status, influencing factors, and intervention strategies for inhalation medication adherence in COPD patients, with particular emphasis on adherence differences between single-inhaler triple therapy (SITT) and multiple-inhaler triple therapy (MITT).MethodsA narrative review methodology was employed, utilizing purposing literature search combined with citation back-tracking. Observational studies and randomized controlled trials from PubMed, CNKI, and other databases (up to December 2024) were included, with influencing factors categorized according to COM-B dimensions. Quantitative estimates reported in this review are derived from individual observational studies with heterogeneous designs and populations, and should be interpreted as illustrative ranges rather than pooled or definitive values. Quantitative estimates are derived from heterogeneous observational studies and should be interpreted as illustrative rather than pooled values. The non-systematic, purposing nature of this review limits comprehensive generalizability.ResultsAcross the heterogeneous observational studies included in this review, MPR ≥80% was achieved by approximately 30% to 50% of patients in reported cohorts, while inhalation technique error rates were reported as illustrative ranges of 30% to 50%. The COM-B framework suggests that adherence deficits arise from the interplay of capability limitations (operational skill deficits), opportunity constraints (with the MITT associated with an illustrative range of approximately 12% to 14% lower MPR compared with SITT in reported real-world studies), and motivational barriers (with depression and anxiety associated with approximately 20% to 25% reductions in adherence in individual cohorts). In observational data, higher adherence has been associated with a reported 51% reduction in exacerbation-related hospitalization risk and an attenuation of forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1) decline by approximately 21 mL/year according to individual observational estimates. SITT was associated with improved treatment persistence compared with MITT in available real-world studies (18-month persistence rates ranging from approximately 2.3% for MITT to 16.5% for SITT). Promising intervention approaches described in the literature include artificial intelligence (AI)-based visual error-correction technology, device simplification strategies, and digital therapeutics, though evidence for some of these strategies remains preliminary.ConclusionInhalation medication adherence in COPD represents a multidimensional behavioral syndrome. This review proposes that transitioning from traditional “one-size-fits-all” education models to precision stratified interventions based on the COM-B framework may enable more individualized chronic disease management, though further empirical validation of phenotype-stratified interventions is warranted. This phenotype-stratified model represents a conceptual framework advanced by the authors to stimulate future research, rather than an empirically validated clinical tool.

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a prevalent chronic respiratory disorder characterized by persistent airflow limitation.1,2 With high prevalence, disability, and mortality rates, COPD poses substantial challenges to global public health systems.3,4 Inhalation therapy serves as the cornerstone of long-term COPD management, effectively alleviating symptoms, improving lung function, and reducing exacerbation frequency.5,6 However, real-world data consistently demonstrate poor adherence to inhalation therapy among COPD patients, with mean medication possession ratios (MPR) of merely 40%–60%, substantially below optimal levels.7,8 Poor adherence not only results in suboptimal symptom control and accelerated lung function decline (approximately 21 mL additional FEV1 loss annually9), but also correlates with increased exacerbation frequency, elevated readmission rates (2.54-fold increased 30-day readmission risk10), and surging healthcare costs.1,7

Despite growing recognition of adherence importance, existing reviews predominantly focus on single dimensions—such as isolated factor analysis or discrete intervention evaluation—lacking integrative theoretical frameworks.11 This fragmented perspective inadequately explains the complex mechanisms underlying poor adherence: why do patients continue exhibiting technique errors despite standard education? Why simplify dosing frequency fail to translate into long-term behavioral change? Why do patients with comorbid depression discontinue treatment despite understanding therapeutic importance? Clearly, inhalation medication adherence in COPD represents a multidimensional behavioral phenomenon involving the interplay of physiological capability, environmental opportunity, and psychological motivation, necessitating systematic deconstruction through behavioral science theory. To clarify the incremental contribution of this review relative to existing literature, we distinguish four domains where prior reviews have left critical gaps. First, existing reviews on COPD inhalation adherence predominantly focus on isolated dimensions—either measurement methodology, epidemiological description, or discrete intervention evaluation—without an integrative theoretical framework that explains how these dimensions interact. Second, while SITT versus MITT comparisons have been reviewed, they have not been systematically analyzed through a behavioral science lens that distinguishes device-simplification effects (Opportunity) from patient-level capability and motivation barriers. Third, reviews of digital adherence technologies have largely reported technological feasibility without mapping specific technologies to theoretically derived behavioral targets. Fourth, and most importantly, no previous review has operationalized the COM-B framework to generate a phenotype-stratified intervention model, enabling clinicians to match patients to dimension-specific interventions. This review addresses these gaps by integrating adherence measurement, epidemiology, COM-B dimensional analysis, BCT mapping, and phenotype-stratified intervention design into a unified conceptual framework, with explicit attention to both high-income and middle-income healthcare contexts.

The COM-B model (Capability-Opportunity-Motivation-Behaviour Model), proposed by Michie et al in 2011, constitutes the core component of the Behaviour Change Wheel (BCW).12 This model emphasizes that behavior generation and maintenance depend upon the synergistic interaction of three elements: Capability (physical and psychological skills required to execute the behavior), Opportunity (external physical environment such as device design and social environment including family support), and Motivation (reflective decision-making such as illness beliefs and automatic impulses including habits and emotional states). The COM-B model has been extensively applied in health behavior research; however, in the COPD medication adherence field, existing studies predominantly employ it as a post-hoc classification tool rather than an intervention design framework.12,13 The selection of COM-B over alternative behavioral frameworks was guided by its specific suitability for complex, habitual health behaviors in chronic disease populations. Unlike the Health Belief Model (HBM), which focuses primarily on individual cognitive perceptions of susceptibility and severity, COM-B explicitly incorporates modifiable environmental factors (Opportunity)—such as device design and regimen complexity—that are central to inhalation therapy adherence. Compared with the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB), which emphasizes reflective intention formation, COM-B additionally captures automatic motivational processes (eg, habit, emotional states, depression) that disproportionately influence elderly patients with cognitive decline. Although the Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF) offers comprehensive coverage of14 behavioral domains, its complexity limits direct translation into clinical intervention design. In contrast, COM-B’s parsimonious three-dimensional structure maps directly onto the Behavior Change Technique (BCT) taxonomy, enabling clinicians to identify specific, actionable intervention targets. This operational clarity makes COM-B particularly advantageous for designing stratified, precision interventions in COPD adherence management.

For COPD patients experiencing acute exacerbation hospitalization, optimizing long-term inhalation regimens at discharge represents a critical clinical decision node. The Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) guidelines recommend individualized treatment escalation based on symptoms and exacerbation risk, with escalation from dual bronchodilators (LABA/LAMA) to triple therapy containing inhaled corticosteroids (ICS/LABA/LAMA) being a common strategy.14,15 It is important to emphasize that escalation to triple therapy should be guided by current guideline-based indications, including a history of moderate-to-severe exacerbations (particularly ≥2 moderate or ≥1 severe exacerbation per year), elevated blood eosinophil counts (≥300 cells/μL) suggesting ICS responsiveness, and concomitant asthma features. Conversely, the decision to initiate ICS-containing regimens requires careful consideration of potential harms, including increased pneumonia risk, especially in older patients and those with prior pneumonia events. The 2024 GOLD report underscores that the benefit–risk ratio of ICS must be individualized, accounting for local pneumonia prevalence, patient comorbidities, and the availability of non-ICS alternatives. Therefore, while this review compares SITT and MITT as device-regimen strategies, it does not advocate universal triple-therapy escalation; rather, it examines how regimen simplification may improve adherence when triple therapy is clinically indicated. Recent real-world studies suggest that single-inhaler triple therapy (SITT) significantly improves medication possession rates and reduces readmission risk compared with multiple-inhaler triple therapy (MITT), with partial mediation through adherence improvement. Nevertheless, systematic comparative evidence regarding device regimen effects on adherence remains scarce in China, and SITT clinical promotion faces implementation science challenges including medical insurance accessibility and primary care availability. It is essential to position this comparison within the broader landscape of COPD pharmacotherapy decision-making. The GOLD 2024 strategy emphasizes that treatment escalation should follow a stepwise approach: initial therapy with bronchodilators (LAMA or LABA/LAMA) for most patients, with ICS-containing triple therapy reserved for those with specific clinical indications as outlined above. SITT and MITT represent alternative delivery mechanisms for the same pharmacological class (ICS/LABA/LAMA), not alternative therapeutic classes. Therefore, the device-level comparison examined in this review presupposes that triple therapy has already been clinically indicated; it does not advocate bypassing guideline-concordant treatment steps. For patients not meeting ICS indications, dual bronchodilator therapy—delivered as single or multiple inhalers—remains the appropriate maintenance strategy. This review’s focus on SITT versus MITT adherence effects is thus nested within, rather than superseding, established pharmacotherapy decision algorithms.

However, real-world evidence regarding the impact of different escalation regimens on readmission rates remains controversial. Recent real-world studies suggest that, among patients receiving ICS-containing triple therapy, single-inhaler triple therapy (SITT) is associated with higher medication possession rates16,17 and lower readmission risk compared with multiple-inhaler triple therapy (MITT),18 with partial mediation through adherence improvement. Nevertheless, systematic comparative evidence regarding device regimen effects on adherence remains scarce in China, and SITT clinical promotion faces implementation science challenges including medical insurance accessibility and primary care availability.

Therefore, based on the COM-B three-dimensional theoretical framework, this review aims to integrate real-world evidence with intelligent monitoring technology advances, sequentially analyzing: (1) gold standard adherence assessment methods and bias sources; (2) domestic and international adherence status and declining trends; (3) interactive mechanisms of influencing factors across “capability-opportunity-motivation” dimensions; (4) associations between adherence and exacerbations, lung function, readmissions, and costs; (5) graded precision intervention strategies (education training corresponding to capability dimension, regimen simplification for opportunity dimension, and feedback reinforcement for motivation dimension); and (6) research gaps and intelligent technology prospects. By integrating behavior change theory, digital health technology, and clinical pharmacy practice, this review aims to provide theoretical foundations for constructing an integrated “education-simplification-feedback” individualized adherence management model, promoting the paradigm shift in COPD chronic disease management from “drug-centered” to “patient-behavior-centered” approaches.

Methods

Literature Search Strategy

This narrative review employed a purposive, non-systematic approach to literature identification, prioritizing conceptual breadth and thematic relevance over exhaustive retrieval. Initial literature sources comprised three channels: (1) the research team’s accumulated COPD management literature database developed over prior studies; (2) citation back-tracking from seminal references (GOLD guidelines, original COM-B model publications, and key adherence measurement reviews); and (3) manual scanning of high-impact respiratory journals (Thorax, Chest, European Respiratory Journal, ERJ Open Research, International Journal of COPD) from 2020 to December 2024. Supplementary targeted searches were conducted to ensure conceptual saturation. The specific purposive search parameters are summarized below to enhance transparency; however, given the narrative review design, these were applied iteratively rather than as rigid, reproducible search protocols.

Purposive Search Parameters

Databases: PubMed/MEDLINE and China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI) were the primary electronic databases consulted for supplementary targeted retrieval. Additional records were identified through manual journal scanning and citation back-tracking, as described above.

Targeted Keyword Combinations: Purposive searches used the following core concept clusters, combined with Boolean operators “AND” and “OR” as appropriate to the database interface.

Population/disease: “COPD,” “chronic obstructive pulmonary disease,” “COPD patients.”

Exposure/intervention: “inhalation therapy,” “inhaled medication,” “inhaler,” “triple therapy,” “SITT,” “MITT,” “digital health,” “smart inhaler,” “electronic monitoring.”

Outcome/behavior: “medication adherence,” “treatment adherence,” “inhaler technique,” “persistence,” “compliance.”

Theoretical framework: “COM-B,” “Behaviour Change Wheel,” “behavior change technique,” “BCT.”

Time Frame: The primary literature search was concluded in December 2024. A limited number of citations from early 2025 were purposively incorporated during manuscript revision to capture rapidly evolving real-world evidence on SITT and digital adherence interventions; these post-hoc inclusions do not alter the narrative review’s non-systematic nature.

Language: English and Chinese.

Inclusion Criteria (applied heuristically): Original research articles (observational cohort studies, retrospective database analyses, randomized controlled trials), systematic reviews, and narrative reviews addressing medication adherence, inhaler technique, regimen complexity, or adherence interventions in COPD populations. Studies utilizing the COM-B model or related behavioral frameworks in chronic disease contexts were also considered when they provided transferable methodological insights.

Exclusion Criteria: Studies exclusively focused on non-COPD populations (eg, asthma-only cohorts without COPD subgroup analysis); conference abstracts, editorials, and commentaries lacking original data; and studies published in languages other than English or Chinese. Studies addressing pharmacological efficacy without adherence or behavioral outcomes were not prioritized.

Study Selection: Identified records underwent independent dual screening by two reviewers against the thematic inclusion criteria outlined above. The PICOS framework was used heuristically to guide relevance assessment rather than as a formal eligibility tool for systematic screening. No formal risk-of-bias assessment or quality-appraisal tool was applied, consistent with narrative review methodology. The principle of conceptual saturation—wherein additional citations no longer introduced new thematic dimensions—guided the final selection, rather than exhaustive inclusion.

Literature Sources and Inclusion

This narrative review employed a purposive literature search strategy combined with citation back-tracking. Initial literature sources comprised three channels: (1) the research team’s accumulated database on COPD management; (2) citation back-tracking from core references (eg, GOLD guidelines, original COM-B model publications); and (3) manual searching of target high-impact journals (Thorax, Chest, ERJ Open Research, International Journal of COPD) published within the past five years. Identified records were assessed for relevance by two reviewers working independently, with thematic inclusion criteria informed by the PICOS framework used to guide conceptual scope rather than as formal eligibility rules for systematic screening. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion. The final set of original studies was used for conceptual integration and evidence mapping within the COM-B framework, rather than for quantitative synthesis. As a narrative review, this study does not claim to have systematically identified all relevant literature. Formal risk-of-bias assessment was not conducted, consistent with narrative review methodology. Quantitative data synthesis was not performed. The PICOS framework was used heuristically to guide thematic inclusion rather than as a formal eligibility criterion for systematic screening. Consequently, no PRISMA-style flow diagram is provided, and the evidence presented reflects interpretive synthesis rather than aggregated effect sizes. Readers should interpret the findings as a theoretically informed conceptual organization of existing evidence, with inherent limitations in comprehensiveness and susceptibility to selection bias.

Definition of High and Low Adherence

Based on commonly used measurement tools in COPD inhalation therapy research, adherence is categorized as follows:

High adherence: MPR or PDC ≥80%, indicating that patients possess or take their prescribed medications for at least 80% of the treatment period. This threshold is widely accepted in chronic disease management as clinically meaningful for reducing exacerbation risk.

Moderate adherence: MPR or PDC 60%–79%, representing partial treatment exposure that may confer limited clinical benefit.

Low adherence: MPR or PDC <60% (or <20% for severely non-adherent subgroups), associated with significantly elevated risks of exacerbations, hospitalizations, and accelerated lung function decline.

For self-reported scales, MMAS-8 scores of 8 indicate high adherence, 6–7 indicate moderate adherence, and <6 indicate low adherence. MARS-A scores follow similar grading principles adapted for inhalation-specific behaviors.

Studies have confirmed that compared with objective measurement methods such as electronic monitoring, The MMAS-8 scale, originally developed by Morisky et al, has been validated against pharmacy fill rates in senior populations.22 Studies have confirmed that compared with objective measurement methods such as electronic monitoring, the MMAS-8 scale tends to overestimate patient adherence levels.22 Furthermore, the limited correlation between pharmacy dispensing data (MPR) and actual medication-taking behavior (electronic monitoring) reminds us that measurement method differences must be considered when evaluating and comparing adherence data across studies.

Current Status of Inhalation Medication Adherence in COPD Patients

International Research Status

Multiple international studies demonstrate universally suboptimal inhalation medication adherence among COPD patients. A large-scale retrospective study based on Swiss health insurance data (2015–2020) found mean MPR of merely approximately 50% among COPD patients, with high adherence (MPR ≥80%) in less than 30%. Further analysis revealed that high-adherence patients exhibited 51% reduced risk of exacerbation-related hospitalization compared with low-adherence patients (MPR <20%) (OR = 0.49, 95% CI: 0.42–0.57).23 Similarly, Vestbo et al identified significant inverse associations between adherence and all-cause mortality and exacerbation hospitalization rates in a prospective cohort study enrolling nearly 6,000 COPD patients.24,25

Multiple real-world studies from Europe and North America consistently report significant adherence decline trends during medium-to-long-term treatment, particularly within 3–6 months post-discharge,26,27 indicating that adherence management should constitute a priority component throughout COPD care continuum.

Beyond European and North American contexts, emerging evidence from the Asia-Pacific and Latin American regions reveals both convergent patterns and important disparities. In Japan, a retrospective claims database study (2019–2021) reported mean PDC of 52.2% for SITT initiators and 39.7% for MITT initiators at 6 months, with only 19.7% of SITT and 10.2% of MITT patients achieving PDC ≥80%; median persistence was merely 2.0 months and 1.0 month, respectively, underscoring the universal challenge of long-term behavioral maintenance even in high-income healthcare systems.28 In Australia, research in the Northern Territory identified particularly low inhalation therapy adherence among Indigenous populations (18%), with 68% of Indigenous patients reporting they had never heard of COPD, highlighting how health literacy and access to culturally safe care compound adherence barriers in marginalized communities.29 In Latin America, a multicenter study across seven countries (Argentina, Chile, Colombia, Costa Rica, Guatemala, Mexico, and Uruguay) reported an overall adherence rate of 54.1% to inhaled therapies among COPD patients.30 In Southeast Asia, a Malaysian study found that 56.1% of COPD patients had good adherence to inhalers, though this varied significantly by disease severity and comorbidity burden.31 These regional variations suggest that adherence barriers and intervention effectiveness identified in European/North American settings may not be directly transferable to healthcare contexts with differing infrastructure, medication financing, and cultural determinants.

Domestic Research Status in China

Inhalation medication adherence among Chinese COPD patients remains equally concerning when contextualized within the broader global landscape. A multicenter study identified mean adherence of merely 45.6%, substantially below optimal levels.8,32 Another retrospective analysis based on electronic prescription data found only approximately 35% of patients maintained MPR >80% within one year post-discharge.7 These figures position China broadly within the mid-range of reported international adherence: comparable to the Swiss mean of 50% and the Japanese SITT PDC of 52.2%, yet lower than the 56.1% observed in Malaysia and the 54.1% reported across Latin American centers. Notably, the proportion of Chinese patients achieving high adherence (MPR >80%) exceeds the 19.7% PDC ≥80% observed for SITT initiators in Japan, suggesting that while overall adherence is suboptimal, the distribution of adherence levels may vary by measurement method and healthcare system structure.

Additionally, domestic research indicates significant adherence disparities across regions and hospital tiers, with rural areas and primary care institutions demonstrating universally lower adherence than urban areas and tertiary hospitals.7 This urban-rural gradient mirrors the disparities observed in other middle-income and geographically diverse settings, such as the pronounced access barriers faced by Indigenous populations in Australia and the regional variation seen across Latin American centers.

Notably, inhaler technique and disease-specific knowledge remain inadequate among domestic patients, further compounding the challenge of poor adherence. A survey of COPD patients in East China revealed over 50% failed to correctly use inhalation devices, with particularly prominent issues among elderly and low-education populations.33,34 This technique error rate is consistent with the 30 %–50% range reported internationally, yet the concentration of errors among elderly and low-literacy subgroups suggests that capability deficits (the COM-B Capability dimension) may be particularly dominant barriers in the Chinese context compared to settings where opportunity constraints (eg, device complexity, reimbursement gaps) predominate.

When compared with the Asia-Pacific region specifically, Chinese adherence patterns appear broadly consistent with those reported in South Korea, where a national cohort study demonstrated that adherence to long-acting inhalers initiated during or after exacerbation was a significant predictor of subsequent exacerbation risk, with high adherers (MPR ≥80%) showing lower risk than low adherers. However, the persistence of poor technique and the steep urban-rural gradient in China highlight structural healthcare delivery challenges—particularly the limited integration of pharmacist-led inhaler education in primary care and uneven access to simplified device regimens—that may be less pronounced in Japan’s universal health coverage system or Malaysia’s hospital-based specialist networks. Comparative implementation research across these middle-income and high-income Asian contexts remains scarce, representing a critical gap for regional adherence optimization.

Multidimensional Influencing Factor Analysis Based on the COM-B Model

Theoretical Framework and Behavioral Mechanisms of the COM-B Model

The COM-B model (Capability-Opportunity-Motivation-Behaviour Model), proposed by Michie et al in 2011, constitutes the core component of the Behaviour Change Wheel (BCW).12 This model posits that behavior generation and maintenance require satisfaction of three core conditions: Capability, Opportunity, and Motivation. These three dimensions operate independently yet interact dynamically; deficiency in any dimension may result in behavioral failure, while multidimensional synergy can generate multiplicative effects for behavior change.

In the COPD inhalation medication adherence context, “behavior” is operationally defined as: patients using prescribed inhalation medications with correct technique within specified timeframes and continuously maintaining this behavioral pattern. This definition encompasses three critical elements—timeliness, technical correctness, and persistence—corresponding to different dimensions of the COM-B model.

Core Theoretical Hypothesis: Poor inhalation medication adherence in COPD patients results not from single-factor causation but from the synergistic interaction of capability deficits (inability to use), opportunity constraints (inconvenience), and motivation weaknesses (unwillingness to use). Traditional interventions predominantly focus on single dimensions (eg, education alone), neglecting dimensional interaction effects, thereby limiting long-term efficacy. Precision interventions based on the COM-B framework require identification of patients’ dominant barrier dimensions for targeted strategy implementation.

Interactive Mechanisms Across COM-B Dimensions

Synergistic Effects Across Dimensions

Boundary Effects of High Motivation Compensating for Low Capability

Research demonstrates that strong medication motivation (Motivation) can partially compensate for operational skill (Capability) deficiencies, but with distinct boundaries. Barnestein-Fonseca et al35 found that when inhalation device error rates exceeded 30%, actual adherence remained significantly lower in patients with high illness cognition and treatment beliefs compared with technically correct patients (MPR 52% vs 71%, P<0.01), despite high motivation. This indicates threshold effects in motivation’s compensatory function for capability—when technical errors severely impede pulmonary drug deposition, psychological drive alone cannot overcome physiological execution barriers.

Mechanism explanation: Repeated operational failures trigger “learned helplessness,” consuming motivation. Elderly patients experiencing multiple failed pMDI attempts due to hand-breath coordination difficulties may abandon treatment due to frustration despite understanding therapeutic importance. Dual-track capability-motivation interventions are required at this stage: technical demonstration (Capability) combined with success experience reinforcement (Motivation).

Social Support Influencing Adherence Through Motivation Mediation

Family support (Opportunity) does not directly affect medication behavior but indirectly improves adherence through enhanced self-efficacy (Motivation). Huang et al’s36 structural equation model demonstrated that 64% of family health’s total effect on adherence was indirectly achieved through the perceived social support → self-efficacy → adherence pathway. Family reminder behaviors accompanied by negative emotions (eg, blame) instead reduce patient autonomous motivation, leading to decreased adherence.

Device Simplification Reshaping Motivation Through Opportunity Creation

Single-inhaler triple therapy (SITT) not only reduces physical burden (Opportunity) but also enhances treatment control perception (Motivation) through “simplification perception.” Real-world database studies demonstrate that SITT is associated with significantly higher medication possession ratios and long-term treatment persistence compared with multiple-inhaler triple therapy (MITT).16,17 Complementing these quantitative findings, qualitative research indicates that patients frequently identify regimen complexity—including the need to coordinate multiple devices with different operating steps—as a major barrier to adherence, whereas simplified once-daily, single-device regimens diminish the cognitive load and emotional burden of disease management.37 This “simplification perception” fosters enhanced self-efficacy and autonomous motivation, as patients experience greater mastery and predictability over their treatment routines. This exemplifies the spillover effect between COM-B model dimensions—opportunity optimization can indirectly reinforce motivation.

Antagonistic Effects Across Dimensions

Compound Impact of Multiple-Device Regimens

Multiple-inhaler triple therapy (MITT) simultaneously creates opportunity deprivation (device burden) and capability deficits (confusion regarding different operational steps), potentially triggering motivation collapse. Halpin et al’s38 study demonstrated MITT patients exhibited merely 2.3% 18-month treatment persistence, significantly lower than SITT’s 16.5%. In-depth analysis revealed MITT patients simultaneously faced triple barriers: memorizing different device use sequences (cognitive load, Capability), multiple daily operations (time cost, Opportunity), and self-efficacy decline following frequent errors (Motivation). These three-dimensional negative factors form a vicious cycle rather than simple superposition.

Cross-Dimensional Erosion by Depression

Depression (Motivation) not only directly reduces treatment willingness but produces cross-dimensional damage through cognitive function decline (Capability) and social withdrawal (Opportunity). Volpato et al’s39 meta-analysis demonstrated COPD patients with comorbid depression exhibited 18% higher inhalation technique error rates and 35% lower family support utilization compared with non-depressed patients. As a core pathological factor in the motivation dimension, depression can systematically dismantle protective factors in other dimensions, representing the most challenging “pivotal barrier” in adherence intervention.

COPD Phenotype-Specific COM-B Barrier Profiles: The distribution of COM-B dimensional barriers varies systematically across COPD clinical phenotypes, reflecting underlying pathophysiological differences. In frequent exacerbator phenotypes (≥2 moderate-to-severe exacerbations/year), repeated hospitalization cycles generate disproportionate motivational barriers—specifically, treatment-related anxiety, depression, and learned helplessness following recurrent acute episodes. The motivation dimension thus dominates adherence deficits in this subgroup, with capability and opportunity barriers playing secondary roles. Conversely, in emphysema-predominant phenotypes characterized by severe airflow limitation and hyperinflation, physical capability barriers assume primacy: reduced inspiratory flow rates compromise dry powder inhaler effectiveness, while dyspnea-induced fatigue limits the cognitive resources available for learning complex device techniques. These phenotype-specific barrier profiles are derived from pathophysiological reasoning and observational patterns rather than empirically validated stratification rules, and should be treated as hypothesis-generating rather than clinically prescriptive.

Limitations of Single-Dimension Interventions

Pure Capability-Dimension Intervention (BCT 6.1+4.1): Traditional inhalation technique training can improve operational correctness to >90%,41 but adherence declines at 6 months post-intervention due to failure to address opportunity (inconvenience of multiple daily doses) and motivation (lack of continuous feedback) barriers. Sanchis et al’s47 systematic review indicated that long-term training effects on DPI adherence were weaker than pMDI because DPI requires greater inspiratory flow; elderly patients may subjectively abandon treatment due to “inability to inhale forcefully” despite mastering technique.

Pure Opportunity-Dimension Intervention (BCT 12.3): Although SITT significantly improves MPR (12–14% increase),17 30–40% of patients still exhibit adherence <80%. Qualitative research reveals some patients reduce doses independently due to “concerns about greater side effects from three drugs combined” (motivation barrier) or “inability to determine whether inhalation is complete” (capability barrier), indicating device simplification requires combined education.

Pure Motivation-Dimension Intervention (BCT 2.3+2.4): Electronic monitoring feedback can improve short-term adherence, but when patients perceive “monitoring” as external control, autonomous motivation is damaged, resulting in “digital fatigue.”Aung et al’s42 meta-analysis demonstrated that pure passive monitoring effect size (d=0.42) was significantly lower than monitoring combined with pharmacist communication (d=0.71), indicating feedback requires interpersonal interaction reinforcement.

Optimal Combinations for Multidimensional Synergistic Intervention

Based on BCTs coding analysis, effective multicomponent interventions typically contain the following core combinations:

“Capability-Opportunity” Dual-Track (BCT 6.1+12.3): Technique training combined with device simplification. In Warunek et al’s41 study, clinical pharmacist education (Capability) combined with post-discharge device replacement with more user-friendly SMI (Opportunity) achieved 78% adherence at 6 months, significantly higher than education-only group (61%).

“Opportunity-Motivation” Linkage (BCT 12.1+2.3): Intelligent reminders combined with self-monitoring. In Sloots et al’s43 eHealth intervention, patients recorded symptoms daily via smartphone (Motivation—self-monitoring), with the system automatically pushing medication reminders (Opportunity—cueing), achieving 91% adherence. Critical design lies in binding reminders with patient active input, avoiding passive reminder “desensitization.”

“Capability-Motivation” Reinforcement (BCT 8.1+10.9): AI error-correction combined with gamified incentives. Future DTx direction42—AI visual recognition of inhalation movement errors (Capability—real-time feedback), with points/badge rewards for correct completion (Motivation—self-reward), forming immediate positive reinforcement loops.

Three-Dimension Full Coverage (BCT 6.1+12.3+2.3): Ideal yet complex intervention mode requiring collaboration among pharmacists (capability training), medical insurance/manufacturers (device simplification), and digital platforms (behavioral feedback). Currently only small-scale feasibility studies48,49 have attempted this model; cost-effectiveness ratios await validation. Cross-disease evidence from hypertension and diabetes management supports the transferability of these BCT combinations to COPD, as the COM-B model and BCT Taxonomy are designed as cross-condition frameworks.

Summary

COM-B model-based in-depth analysis reveals that COPD inhalation medication adherence represents a multidimensional dynamic interactive complex behavioral phenomenon. Capability, Opportunity, and Motivation dimensions contribute independently to adherence while influencing each other through synergy, compensation, and antagonism mechanisms. The limited effectiveness of traditional single-dimension interventions stems from neglecting this interaction. Through BCTs coding, abstract theoretical dimensions can be transformed into operational intervention techniques; through “adherence phenotype” stratification, patient-intervention precision matching can be achieved. Future research should prioritize validating clinical effectiveness and cost-effectiveness of phenotype-stratified interventions, promoting COM-B model transformation from theoretical framework to clinical decision-making tool.

Relationship Between Adherence and Clinical Outcomes: Mechanisms, Gradients, and Heterogeneity

Exacerbations: Dose–Response Relationships and Risk Thresholds

Bischof et al’s study based on Swiss health insurance data (2015–2020, n=18,432) revealed nonlinear dose–response relationships between adherence and exacerbation hospitalization risk.23 This study demonstrated that patients with MPR ≥80% exhibited 51% reduced exacerbation hospitalization risk compared with those with MPR <20% (OR = 0.49, 95% CI: 0.42–0.57). Further analysis indicated this protective effect demonstrated clear gradient characteristics: when MPR was 60%–79%, risk increased approximately 42% compared with ideal adherers; when decreased to 40%–59%, risk amplification expanded to 89%; and when MPR fell below 20%, risk surged 221%. This pattern suggests the existence of clinically significant adherence thresholds—maintaining MPR ≥60% may represent the cost-optimal minimum target, though pursuing >80% remains significantly beneficial in frequent exacerbator phenotypes.

Suh et al’s retrospective cohort study in Korean populations (2022) further validated this association’s generalizability in Asian populations.50 This study found that among patients initiating long-acting inhalers during exacerbation, high adherers (MPR ≥80%) demonstrated significantly lower subsequent exacerbation risk compared with low adherers (HR = 0.61). Notably, this protective effect was most significant within the 90-day “vulnerable window” post-discharge, demonstrating attenuation trends over time, emphasizing the necessity of sustained reinforcement interventions.

Mechanistically, regular medication maintains continuous airway corticosteroid exposure, inhibiting eosinophilic and neutrophilic inflammatory cascade reactions. When drug holidays exceed 7 days, sputum interleukin-8 and tumor necrosis factor-α levels significantly rebound, forming rebound hyperinflammatory states with consequently lowered exacerbation thresholds.

Readmissions: 30-Day Vulnerable Window and Long-Term Trajectories

Readmission represents a core indicator of disease burden and healthcare costs in COPD patients, with poor adherence significantly associated with elevated readmission risk in temporal sequence. Rosen et al (2017) found that patients assessed as low or medium adherence by Morisky scale at admission exhibited 2.54-fold increased 30-day readmission risk compared with high adherers (95% CI: 1.32–4.90).10 This finding highlights intervention urgency in the early post-exacerbation discharge phase—pharmacist telephone follow-up within 48 hours post-discharge and inhalation technique review within 2 weeks may represent critical nodes for blocking readmission chains.

Bogart et al’s study based on 2024 US Medicare big data (n>10,000) provided hard endpoint evidence for single versus multiple-inhaler triple therapy comparison.51 This study demonstrated that SITT initiators exhibited 8.2% 30-day all-cause readmission rate versus 11.7% for MITT initiators, representing 3.5 percentage point absolute difference. More importantly, approximately 67% of SITT’s readmission protective effect was mediated through adherence (SITT group PDC 0.51 vs MITT group 0.37), with the remaining 33% suggesting biological effects of drug synergy independent of adherence. This evidence supports the “design-as-intervention” concept—simplifying treatment regimens themselves can produce clinical outcome improvements.

He et al’s prospective study (2023) further revealed that medication regimen complexity was independently positively associated with readmission risk.52 Each additional inhaler device increased 30-day readmission risk by approximately 40%, with this association particularly significant in elderly (≥75 years) and heart failure comorbidity patients. This suggests that for high-complexity patients, simplifying device numbers (eg, transitioning from MITT to SITT) may more effectively reduce readmissions than education intensification alone.

Lung Function Decline: Phenotype Modification Effects and Individualized Trajectories

Long-term regular medication effects on lung function decline rates demonstrate significant phenotype heterogeneity. Vestbo et al24 (2009) classic study demonstrated that high adherers (MPR ≥80%) exhibited FEV1 decline rate of 28 mL/year versus 49 mL/year in low adherers (<50%), representing 21 mL/year difference. Projected over 5 years, this equates to approximately 100 mL cumulative lung function preservation—approaching one GOLD classification stage, with clear clinical significance.

Celli et al9 (2021) systematic review further refined this relationship. For frequent exacerbator phenotype (≥2 exacerbations/year), adherence intervention demonstrated most significant lung function protective effects, with annual decline rate differences reaching 29 mL; for chronic bronchitis phenotype, difference was 17 mL; while for emphysema-predominant phenotype, difference was merely 8 mL without statistical significance. This finding carries important therapeutics implications: for emphysema-predominant patients, adherence improvement benefits should focus on symptom burden (eg, St. George’s Respiratory Questionnaire score improvement) and exercise endurance (6-minute walk distance enhancement) rather than lung function indicators, avoiding patient frustration and discontinuation due to unimproved FEV1.

Additionally, good adherence is associated with significantly higher health-related quality of life. Saja et al53 (2025) cross-sectional study demonstrated that high adherence group (Morisky scale score = 8) exhibited average 4.2 points lower COPD Assessment Test scores compared with medium-low adherence groups, suggesting regular medication significantly improves patients’ daily activity capacity and psychological status through reduced symptom fluctuation and exacerbation frequency.

Healthcare Costs: Direct Expenditure and Indirect Burden

Poor adherence significantly increases healthcare resource allocation pressure through increased exacerbation frequency and hospitalization episodes Chen et al54 retrospective cohort study based on Chinese population databases (2020) demonstrated that high adherers (MPR ≥80%) exhibited approximately 35% lower COPD-related exacerbation incidence compared with non-adherers, with per-patient annual healthcare cost savings of approximately CNY 8,500. This cost advantage primarily derived from hospitalization expense reduction (accounting for 72% of total savings) rather than increased outpatient medication costs.

Davis et al55 study in US commercial insurance populations (2017) further quantified the adherence–cost relationship. Each 10% improvement in ICS/LABA adherence reduced COPD exacerbation-related costs by approximately 12%. Notably, this cost-effectiveness ratio varied across age strata: patients ≥65 years demonstrated significantly higher adherence improvement cost-effectiveness ratio (approximately 1:4.2) compared with younger patients (1:2.8) due to higher baseline hospitalization risk, suggesting elderly populations should be prioritized for adherence intervention.

Bogart et al51 (2024) health economic analysis demonstrated that despite similar drug costs between SITT and MITT, SITT demonstrated significantly reduced COPD-related healthcare resource utilization due to higher adherence and fewer exacerbations, with favorable incremental cost-effectiveness ratio compared with MITT. This finding supports prioritizing SITT accessibility in medical insurance policy formulation to achieve population-level cost optimization.

Competing Evidence, Harms, and Implementation Considerations

While the preceding sections highlight the benefits of improved adherence and regimen simplification, several countervailing considerations warrant balanced discussion. First, ICS-containing triple therapy—whether delivered as SITT or MITT—carries recognized risks, including increased pneumonia incidence, particularly in elderly patients and those with prior pneumonia history. The 2024 GOLD strategy emphasizes that the decision to escalate to ICS must be individualized based on blood eosinophil levels, exacerbation history, and pneumonia risk, rather than driven solely by adherence optimization goals. Second, SITT may reduce regimen flexibility; patients experiencing adverse effects from a single component cannot easily discontinue that agent without switching the entire device, whereas MITT allows component-level adjustment. Third, the cost-effectiveness of SITT varies substantially across healthcare systems. In settings where SITT is not reimbursed or carries high out-of-pocket costs, the adherence advantage may be offset by access barriers, particularly for low-income populations. Fourth, the evidence base for digital adherence interventions remains concentrated in high-resource, tertiary-care settings with limited generalizability to primary care or rural environments where smartphone penetration and digital literacy are low. Finally, the causal inference linking adherence to clinical outcomes is predominantly observational; residual confounding by disease severity, socioeconomic status, and healthcare access cannot be fully excluded. These limitations underscore that adherence interventions must be evaluated not only for efficacy but also for equity, feasibility, and net clinical benefit within specific healthcare contexts.

Summary

The relationship between adherence and clinical outcomes exhibits multidimensional gradients, phenotypic modification, and temporal dynamics. In available observational studies, acute exacerbation risk demonstrates a nonlinear dose–response association with adherence, with risk increasing sharply when the medication possession ratio falls below 60%; however, this association may be partially confounded by disease severity and healthcare access. Readmission risk peaks within 30 days post-discharge, and SITT has been associated with lower readmission risk in real-world data, though the extent to which this reflects adherence mediation, drug synergy, or selection bias remains incompletely disentangled. Lung function protective effects appear most pronounced in frequent exacerbator phenotypes, while symptom-oriented outcomes may be more relevant in emphysema-predominant phenotypes; cost-effectiveness considerations favor elderly populations but are contingent on healthcare system reimbursement and drug accessibility. These findings suggest that adherence interventions should be precisely stratified based on patient phenotypes, age, comorbidities, and treatment-related risks (including ICS-associated pneumonia), rather than adopting standardized one-size-fits-all approaches. Furthermore, the benefits of improved adherence and regimen simplification must be weighed against potential harms, implementation costs, and equity considerations discussed in the preceding section.

Interventions to Improve Inhalation Medication Adherence in COPD Patients

Medication Education and Skills Training

One-on-one medication education conducted by clinical pharmacists or respiratory specialist nurses constitutes foundational measures for improving adherence. Content includes disease knowledge, drug mechanisms, and correct device usage methods. Research demonstrates that patients receiving systematic training exhibit significantly improved device operational correctness and adherence; a 2021 interventional quasi-experimental study found that after clinical pharmacist education for discharged COPD patients, median operational step correctness rates for all inhalation device types improved from 81.8% to 100%.41

Behavioral Support and Reminder Systems

Traditional interventions such as medication diaries, SMS/WeChat reminders, and telephone follow-up help patients establish regular medication habits. A pilot study in COPD patients with comorbid heart failure demonstrated high adherence to daily electronic symptom diary completion (91%), facilitating active disease monitoring and self-management participation.43 SMS services, as low-cost, easily accessible reminder tools, have been confirmed by multiple studies to improve medication adherence.56 Pharmacist or nurse-conducted telephone follow-up represents effective means of reinforcing medication education and support. Research demonstrates that pharmacist telephone follow-up after COPD medication regimen changes can reinforce patient understanding of adherence and action plan importance.57 Smart pillboxes, mobile health applications, and other emerging technologies are gradually being applied to adherence management, with preliminary studies demonstrating good feasibility and effectiveness.44,58 A 2024 systematic review including 10 studies and 1,432 patients demonstrated that electronic monitoring combined with healthcare provider intervention improved adherence by 18% (95% CI: 9–27) compared with pure passive monitoring, with 85% of patients finding digital platforms user-friendly and 91% considering reminder functions helpful for medication behavior improvement.42 The feasibility of smartphone-based reminder systems in COPD aligns with established evidence from cardiovascular and diabetes chronic disease management, where similar digital adherence technologies have demonstrated effectiveness.56

Family and Social Support Involvement

Encouraging family participation in patient treatment processes provides emotional support and practical assistance such as medication reminders and accompaniment to follow-up visits. Community-based chronic disease management programs also provide continuous supportive environments for patients. Family support and community-based management programs represent two important complementary pillars for improving treatment adherence in chronic disease patients including COPD. Family participation provides personalized emotional and practical assistance, while community pharmacies, general practice clinics, and other institutions provide professional, continuous support services, jointly constructing a robust long-term management support environment for patients.37,59

Individualized Feedback and Goal Setting

Providing regular feedback based on patient adherence data (eg, MPR) helps patients recognize their medication behavior and set achievable adherence goals. This goal-oriented intervention strategy has demonstrated good effectiveness in multiple studies.45,46,60

Multicomponent Comprehensive Interventions

Single intervention measures demonstrate limited effectiveness; multicomponent, individualized comprehensive intervention strategies possess greater advantages. Recent research evidence supports the effectiveness of multicomponent, individualized comprehensive intervention strategies.48 The key to its success lies in the integration of multiple mutually reinforcing elements: individualized education to enhance knowledge and beliefs, continuous behavioral support (eg, pharmacist consultation, skills training) to cultivate habits, family involvement to provide environmental support, and regular feedback to reinforce positive behaviors. This integrated model can more comprehensively address the complex factors influencing adherence, thereby significantly improving medication behavior while bringing substantial improvements in disease control and other clinical outcomes.49 Multicomponent pharmacist-led interventions, originally validated in hypertension and diabetes cohorts, provide transferable behavioral support architectures applicable to COPD adherence management within the COM-B framework.

Research Gaps and Prospects: Precision Intervention Research Agenda Based on the COM-B Framework

Before detailing specific research gaps, it is essential to clarify the evidentiary status of interventions discussed in this review. The COM-B framework itself is a well-established behavioral theory with robust validation across multiple health domains. Similarly, the association between poor adherence and adverse clinical outcomes in COPD is supported by consistent observational evidence from multiple continents. However, several components of this review—particularly the “adherence phenotype” stratification model (COM-B-Based “Adherence Phenotype” Stratification Model: A Conceptual Contribution), the layered intelligent intervention roadmap (Conceptual Framework for Future Intelligent Intervention Research), and the pharmacogenomic extensions discussed in Interdisciplinary Research Frontiers—represent conceptual propositions advanced by the authors rather than empirically validated interventions. Specifically, the phenotype-stratified model and the associated intelligent intervention roadmap represent conceptual frameworks advanced by the authors to stimulate future research, rather than empirically validated clinical tools. These speculative directions are explicitly labeled as such throughout this chapter and should be understood as hypothesis-generating frameworks intended to stimulate future research, not as current clinical practice recommendations.

Structural Deficiencies in Current Evidence Systems

Despite the COM-B model providing an integrative framework for understanding COPD inhalation medication adherence, existing research demonstrates significant gaps in dimensional coverage balance, measurement standardization, and long-term behavioral maintenance:

Dimensional Coverage Imbalance: Current literature over-focuses on “Capability” (inhalation technique training, 42% of included studies) and “Opportunity” (device simplification, 35%), while “Motivation” dimension depth interventions (particularly targeting depression and illness cognition reconstruction) account for merely 23%, mostly small-scale feasibility studies.12 More critically, empirical research on three-dimensional interactions remains virtually blank—no RCTs have simultaneously manipulated Capability (eg, AI error-correction), Opportunity (eg, SITT), and Motivation (eg, DTx feedback) to validate synergistic effects.

Measurement Methodological Heterogeneity: Adherence definition differences between electronic monitoring (gold standard) and pharmacy data (MPR) render effect sizes incomparable. More critically, existing studies predominantly report mean adherence, neglecting differential impacts of medication use patterns (eg, weekend interruptions, holiday discontinuation) on clinical outcomes. Future research requires establishment of time-to-event analysis standards to identify “drug holiday” danger windows.

Behavioral Maintenance “Decay Paradox”: Most intervention studies demonstrate significant adherence improvement during intervention periods (effect size d=0.6–0.8), but effect sizes decay to 0.2–0.3 at 12-month follow-up. This “post-honeymoon failure” suggests short-term intensification cannot translate into long-term habits, requiring investigation into behavioral automaticity neuropsychological mechanisms and maintenance strategies.

The evidence supporting AI-based visual error-correction, prescription digital therapeutics (DTx), and Internet of Things (IoT) monitoring in COPD inhalation adherence remains largely preliminary, derived from feasibility studies, pilot trials, or extrapolation from other chronic disease populations. These technologies are discussed in this review as conceptual future directions and hypothesis-generating frameworks within the COM-B model, rather than as established interventions with proven efficacy for hard clinical endpoints (exacerbations, hospitalizations, mortality) in COPD. Readers should distinguish between the well-supported evidence for traditional interventions (eg, pharmacist-led education, device simplification) and the speculative, high-potential but unvalidated nature of emerging digital strategies.

Sponsorship and Database Limitations: The real-world studies comparing SITT and MITT included in this review (eg, Bogart et al, Halpin et al) were primarily conducted using large commercial or national insurance databases. These studies may be subject to sponsorship bias, as some were funded by pharmaceutical manufacturers of SITT products. Additionally, database studies are susceptible to confounding by indication, channeling bias, and residual confounding from unmeasured variables (eg, disease severity, physician prescribing preferences, patient socioeconomic status). The apparent adherence advantage of SITT may partly reflect that patients prescribed SITT are systematically different from those maintained on MITT in ways not fully captured by statistical adjustment. Readers should interpret SITT-MITT comparisons with appropriate caution, recognizing that observational effectiveness data complement but do not replace randomized evidence.

Stratified design: Stratification by COM-B three-dimensional baseline assessment (high/medium/low), ensuring adequate sample sizes in each stratum for interaction detection.

Endpoint selection:

Primary endpoint: Exacerbation readmission rate (hard endpoint), rather than adherence values alone.

Secondary endpoints: FEV1 annual decline rate, mortality, cost-effectiveness ratio.

Patient-reported outcomes (PROs): Medication Self-Efficacy Scale (MSES), inhalation technique confidence score.

Key Scientific Questions:

SITT vs MITT hard endpoint differences in indicated populations: After controlling for adherence mediation effects, does SITT independently reduce readmissions compared with MITT in patients with guideline-based ICS indications (suggesting biological effects of drug synergy beyond adherence)? Future trials should prespecify subgroup analyses by pneumonia risk, eosinophil status, and treatment history to avoid extrapolating device-simplification benefits to inappropriate populations.

Adherence threshold effect: Does a threshold of “clinically meaningful adherence” exist (eg, MPR ≥75% vs ≥80%), below which the risk of acute exacerbation increases sharply?

Phenotype-intervention matching: Do frequent exacerbator phenotype patients benefit more from intensive motivation interventions (eg, DTx), while emphysema phenotype patients require more opportunity optimization (eg, device simplification)?

Implementation Science Perspective for Translation Research

Transforming effective interventions into routine clinical practice faces an “evidence-practice gap.” Future research must address:

Contextual Adaptation:

Primary care settings: In resource-limited areas where AI-equipped devices are unavailable, low-cost alternatives should be developed (eg, inhaler technique correction videos produced by pharmacists and accessed via QR code scanning).

Rural digital divide: For populations with low smartphone penetration, traditional low-cost interventions (eg, medication calendars plus telephone reminders from family members) should be retained rather than mandating digital solutions.

Implementation Strategies: Adopt RE-AIM framework for intervention scalability assessment:

Reach: Can the intervention reach those who need it most (eg, rural residents and individuals with lower educational attainment)?

Effectiveness: Do interventions remain effective in real-world noisy environments (vs ideal trial conditions)?

Adoption: Are physicians, pharmacists, and hospital administrators willing to adopt (requiring resolution of SITT medical insurance access, smart device procurement processes)?

Implementation: Can trained primary care doctors accurately execute COM-B assessment and stratified intervention?

Maintenance: Do hospitals continue services after funding cessation (requiring sustainable reimbursement models such as pharmacist clinic service fees)?

Policy Research:

Medical insurance payment model innovation: Explore “adherence-based payment”—if hospitals improve patient MPR from 50% to 80%, they receive additional medical insurance rewards covering intervention costs.

SITT priority access policy: ** Based on existing evidence, promote SITT as the first-line choice for COPD maintenance therapy (particularly post-exacerbation) in medical insurance directories, rather than merely third-line escalation option.

However, neurobehavioral modalities such as fMRI and TMS currently lack any direct evidence linking them to improved inhalation adherence or clinical outcomes in COPD populations, and pharmacogenomic-adherence interactions remain hypothetical. Conclusions

Core Findings Synthesis

Based on the COM-B behavior change model, this review systematically integrated measurement methods, influencing factors, and intervention evidence for COPD inhalation medication adherence, deriving the following core conclusions:

First, poor adherence represents a multidimensional behavioral syndrome rather than single patient defect. Capability dimension (30–50% operational error rates), Opportunity dimension (12–14% lower adherence with MITT versus SITT), and Motivation dimension (depression reducing adherence by 20–25%) synergistically interact, forming vicious cycles of poor adherence. Any single-dimension intervention demonstrates effectiveness ceilings of approximately 15–20% adherence improvement; breakthrough requires multidimensional synergy.

Second, among patients with guideline-based indications for ICS-containing triple therapy, device simplification via SITT represents the most evidence-supported “opportunity optimization” strategy within this specific therapeutic context. Real-world data demonstrate that, in this indicated population, SITT is associated with improved medication possession ratio (MPR increase 12--14%) and reduced readmission risk (OR=0.49) compared with multiple-inhaler triple therapy (MITT), with partial mediation through adherence. However, this advantage must be balanced against ICS-related pneumonia risk, regimen inflexibility (inability to discontinue single components), and local reimbursement/access barriers; SITT should not be prioritized in patients without ICS indications or those with recurrent pneumonia.

Third, intelligent technologies provide possibilities for precision stratified intervention, but digital divide vigilance is required. AI visual error-correction, IoT monitoring, and digital therapeutics (DTx) correspond to COM-B three dimensions respectively, but “stratified-escalation” implementation pathways must be established: tertiary hospitals piloting high-precision technologies, primary care promoting low-cost adapted solutions, ensuring equitable accessibility. These are author-proposed conceptual frameworks intended to guide future inquiry and have not been validated in clinical trials.

Fourth, adherence assessment methodology significantly influences reported prevalence and intervention outcomes. Objective measures (electronic monitoring, pharmacy refill data) and subjective scales (MMAS-8, MARS-A) capture different adherence dimensions—acquisition versus actual inhalation behavior—and their discordance underscores the necessity of multi-method assessment in both research and clinical practice.

Fifth, adherence demonstrates robust associations with hard clinical endpoints: higher adherence is associated with reduced exacerbation-related hospitalizations, attenuated lung function decline (particularly in frequent exacerbator phenotypes), lower 30-day readmission rates, and decreased healthcare costs. However, these associations are predominantly observational, and the heterogeneity of effects across COPD phenotypes necessitates individualized outcome expectations rather than uniform adherence targets.

Theoretical Contributions

The contributions outlined in this section represent conceptual and theoretical advances proposed by the authors to integrate existing evidence through the COM-B framework, rather than empirically derived clinical algorithms.

This review’s innovation lies in transforming the COM-B model from descriptive tool to intervention design framework. Through:

Identifying three-dimensional interaction mechanisms (eg, high motivation compensation thresholds for low capability, depression’s cross-dimensional erosion effects);

Constructing six adherence barrier phenotypes (Capability-/Opportunity-/Motivation-impaired and their combinations);

Mapping behavior change techniques (BCTs) to specific intervention components;

This review provides conceptual precision medicine pathways for COPD adherence management, transcending traditional “strengthen education” one-size-fits-all models.

Relative to existing reviews, this review’s specific contributions are threefold. First, it moves beyond descriptive factor listing to operationalize the COM-B framework as an intervention design tool, explicitly mapping BCTs to dimensional targets rather than using COM-B solely as a post-hoc classification scheme. Second, it integrates device-level evidence (SITT versus MITT) with patient-level behavioral analysis, demonstrating how regimen simplification operates through the Opportunity dimension while acknowledging that opportunity optimization alone is insufficient without concurrent capability and motivation support. Third, it proposes a phenotype-stratified intervention model that translates theoretical dimensions into clinically actionable matching rules, pending empirical validation. These contributions are conceptual and organizational rather than empirical; they synthesize existing evidence through a novel theoretical lens to generate testable hypotheses for future research.

Clinical Practice Recommendations

Based on the evidence synthesized in this narrative review, the following tiered recommendations are proposed for clinicians. These recommendations reflect the current state of observational and preliminary interventional evidence, and should be interpreted as pragmatic guidance rather than guideline-level directives.

Established Interventions with Consistent Observational Support:

For the subset of patients with guideline-based indications for triple therapy (eg, frequent exacerbations, blood eosinophil counts ≥300 cells/μL, or asthma features), SITT may be considered as an alternative to MITT to reduce regimen complexity and potentially improve adherence, provided that ICS-related pneumonia risk (particularly in older patients and those with prior pneumonia), regimen inflexibility, patient preference, and local reimbursement/access conditions are carefully weighed. SITT is not recommended for patients without ICS indications or those with recurrent pneumonia; nor should device convenience override pharmacological appropriateness in treatment escalation decisions. Conduct inhalation technique verification using standardized checklists (eg, Sanchis 9-step score) at each prescription or clinical encounter, rather than relying solely on patient self-report.

Interventions with Emerging but Preliminary Evidence:

Integrate family collaborative education and establish home reminder systems for elderly patients and those with lower health literacy. Important contextual note: These recommendations apply specifically to patients for whom triple therapy (ICS/LABA/LAMA) has been clinically indicated according to current GOLD guidelines. They do not imply that SITT should replace dual bronchodilator therapy (LABA/LAMA or LAMA alone) as initial maintenance treatment, nor that device simplification should override pharmacological appropriateness. For patients without ICS indications, the focus should remain on optimizing inhaler technique and adherence within simpler regimens rather than escalating to triple therapy for device-convenience reasons alone.

For patients with comorbid depression or anxiety, consider referral to psychology/psychiatry services or motivational interviewing as adjuncts to standard care, rather than simply emphasizing medication compliance.

Conceptual Directions Requiring Further Validation:

Pilot AI visual error-correction systems and electronic monitoring feedback in well-resourced medical centers as implementation research, with explicit evaluation of cost-effectiveness, digital equity, and scalability.

Participate in or establish real-world cohorts to generate localized, context-specific evidence on adherence interventions in Chinese COPD populations.

Research Prospects

COPD inhalation medication adherence research is transitioning from “problem description” to “precision solution.” The following directions directly address the specific evidence gaps identified in Chapter 8, ensuring that future work builds systematically upon the limitations acknowledged in this review.

Addressing the dimensional coverage imbalance (Structural Deficiencies in Current Evidence Systems): Future research should prioritize empirical validation of motivation-dimension interventions, which currently account for merely 23% of the literature and remain largely limited to small-scale feasibility studies. Specifically, head-to-head randomized controlled trials are needed to compare phenotype-matched multidimensional interventions (Capability + Opportunity + Motivation) against standard education, directly testing the synergistic effects proposed in the COM-B interaction framework (Interactive Mechanisms Across COM-B Dimensions). Such trials should incorporate hard clinical endpoints (exacerbations, hospitalizations, mortality) rather than adherence surrogate measures alone.

Resolving measurement methodological heterogeneity (Structural Deficiencies in Current Evidence Systems): The development of standardized time-to-event analysis protocols is urgently needed to capture “drug holiday” danger windows and their differential impacts on clinical outcomes. Future studies should move beyond mean adherence reporting to characterize medication use patterns (eg, weekend interruptions, holiday discontinuation) using electronic monitoring as the gold standard, while establishing pragmatic equivalence thresholds between MPR and device-recorded adherence for real-world applicability.

Overcoming the behavioral maintenance “decay paradox” (Structural Deficiencies in Current Evidence Systems): Research should investigate the neuropsychological mechanisms of behavioral automaticity in medication-taking, leveraging longitudinal designs with embedded qualitative components to identify why short-term intervention effects decay to 0.2–0.3 at 12-month follow-up. This directly informs the design of sustainable adherence support systems that transcend the “post-honeymoon failure” phenomenon.

Generating localized real-world evidence (Methodological Upgrades for Real-World Research (RWD)): The proposed “China-INHALE Registry” should be established as a prospective cohort with embedded pragmatic RCTs, stratified by COM-B baseline assessment, to validate whether SITT independently reduces readmissions after controlling for adherence mediation (testing the “design-as-intervention” hypothesis) and to identify clinically meaningful adherence thresholds (eg, MPR ≥75% vs ≥80%) below which exacerbation risk increases sharply. This addresses the current scarcity of large-scale domestic real-world data and the unresolved question of phenotype-intervention matching.

Implementing stratified-escalation intelligent interventions (Conceptual Framework for Future Intelligent Intervention Research): Three-layer validation studies are required: (1) multicenter RCTs comparing AI visual error-correction against human pharmacist education for inhalation technique correctness; (2) pragmatic RCTs of IoT smart pillboxes + SITT within medical insurance systems, with health economic endpoints; and (3) adaptive platform trials of prescription digital therapeutics (DTx) with gamification for motivation-impaired phenotypes. These correspond respectively to the Capability, Opportunity, and Motivation dimensions, ensuring that technology deployment aligns with COM-B precision matching rather than indiscriminate adoption.

Bridging the evidence-practice gap (Implementation Science Perspective for Translation Research): Implementation science research must adopt the RE-AIM framework to evaluate scalability, explicitly addressing Reach (rural and low-education populations), Effectiveness (real-world noisy environments), Adoption (physician and pharmacist willingness), Implementation (primary care execution fidelity), and Maintenance (sustainable reimbursement models). Policy research should explore “adherence-based payment” innovations and SITT priority access in medical insurance directories, contingent upon rigorous local cost-effectiveness evidence.

These interdisciplinary extensions remain conceptual frameworks proposed to stimulate future research, but they require dedicated COPD-specific validation before any link to adherence or clinical outcomes can be established.

Ultimate Goal: Transform inhalation medication adherence management from “drug-centered” to “patient-behavior-centered,” achieving true individualization of COPD long-term management through integration of behavior change theory, digital health technology, and healthcare policy innovation—directly addressing the structural deficiencies, measurement gaps, and implementation barriers catalogued in Chapter 8.

Limitations of This Narrative Review

As a purposive narrative review, this study is subject to inherent methodological limitations that should inform the interpretation of its conclusions. The non-systematic literature identification process, while prioritizing conceptual breadth, introduces potential selection bias and cannot guarantee comprehensive coverage of all relevant evidence. Consequently, the quantitative estimates presented herein reflect illustrative ranges derived from heterogeneous observational studies rather than pooled effect sizes, and the proposed COM-B phenotype stratification model remains a conceptual framework awaiting empirical validation. Readers should interpret the findings as theoretically informed interpretive synthesis rather than definitive clinical guidelines.
