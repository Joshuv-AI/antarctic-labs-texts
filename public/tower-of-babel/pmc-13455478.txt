Development and validation of a LASSO-derived nomogram for predicting unfavorable treatment outcomes in drug-resistant pulmonary tuberculosis
Authors: Wei Zhang, Tuantuan Li, Haiqing Liu, Manman Liu, Fangfang Song, Lili Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveDrug-resistant pulmonary tuberculosis (DR-TB) remains a major public health challenge with suboptimal treatment outcomes. This study aimed to identify pre-treatment predictors of unfavorable outcomes in DR-TB patients and develop an interpretable predictive model using routinely available admission variables.MethodsWe retrospectively enrolled 184 DR-TB patients from the Second People’s Hospital of Fuyang City between January 2021 and December 2023. Patients were categorized into favorable outcome group (cured or treatment completed, n = 143) and unfavorable outcome group (treatment failure, death, loss to follow-up, and not evaluated, n = 41). LASSO regression was employed for variable selection from 30 candidate variables, followed by multivariate logistic regression to identify independent predictors. A nomogram was constructed to facilitate individualized risk estimation. SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) analysis was performed to interpret model predictions at global and individual levels. Model performance was assessed using ROC curve analysis, calibration curves, decision curve analysis, and bootstrap resampling. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression was used to examine linearity assumptions for continuous predictors.ResultsLASSO regression selected three admission variables: age, BMI, and albumin. Multivariate logistic regression confirmed these as independent predictors: age (OR = 1.047, 95% CI: 1.018–1.077, P = 0.001), BMI (OR = 0.728, 95% CI: 0.599–0.885, P = 0.001), and albumin (OR = 0.894, 95% CI: 0.826–0.967, P = 0.005). A nomogram was developed based on these three predictors for bedside risk estimation. The model demonstrated good discrimination with AUC of 0.833 (95% CI: 0.758–0.908) and bias-corrected C-index of 0.819 after bootstrap validation. Calibration was satisfactory (Hosmer-Lemeshow P = 0.564), and decision curve analysis confirmed clinical utility across threshold probabilities of 0.1–0.8. SHAP analysis identified age as the most influential predictor, with higher BMI and albumin contributing protectively. RCS analysis confirmed linear associations for all three predictors (P for nonlinear > 0.05 for all), supporting model simplicity.ConclusionThis simple, pre-treatment nomogram, using three routinely available variables, enables early identification of high-risk patients at the time of diagnosis and may support targeted interventions to improve treatment outcomes in drug-resistant pulmonary tuberculosis.Clinical trialNot applicable.Keywords: Drug-resistant pulmonary tuberculosis, Treatment outcomes, Body mass index, Albumin, LASSO regression, SHAP analysis, Nomogram

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) remains a leading cause of death from a single infectious agent worldwide, with drug-resistant TB (DR-TB) representing a persistent threat to global health progress [1]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO) Global Tuberculosis Report 2025, an estimated 390,000 people developed multidrug-resistant or rifampicin-resistant TB (MDR/RR-TB) in 2024, accounting for 3.2% of new TB cases and 16% of previously treated cases [2]. Alarmingly, MDR/RR-TB caused approximately 150,000 deaths globally, with treatment success rates remaining suboptimal at 68–71% despite advances in all-oral regimens [2, 3]. A recent systematic review estimated the global prevalence of MDR-TB at 11.6% among tested populations, highlighting the substantial burden of resistance [4].

China continues to bear a substantial burden of DR-TB, accounting for 7.3% of global MDR/RR-TB cases in 2023 [5]. Although China has achieved notable progress in TB control—transitioning to a medium-low incidence country with an estimated 696,000 new TB cases in 2024, DR-TB remains a critical concern [6]. The estimated number of MDR/RR-TB cases in China was approximately 29,000 in 2023–2024, with treatment success rates of around 66% [7]. The long treatment duration (18–20 months), high pill burden, and severe adverse effects contribute to poor adherence and unfavorable outcomes [8].

Early identification of patients at high risk for unfavorable treatment outcomes is essential for implementing targeted interventions and improving prognosis [9, 10]. Traditional statistical methods have identified several predictors including age, nutritional status, and comorbidities [11, 12]. LASSO regression has been increasingly applied in TB research for variable selection and model development, including prediction of treatment outcomes in MDR/RR-TB populations [13, 14]. However, few studies have integrated machine learning-based approaches with interpretability analysis to develop clinically applicable prediction models specifically for DR-TB treatment outcomes. The SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) framework offers advantages in elucidating individual-level predictions, enhancing clinical utility [15].

Therefore, this study aimed to develop and validate a predictive model for unfavorable treatment outcomes among DR-TB patients using LASSO regression and multivariate logistic regression, integrated with SHAP analysis and restricted cubic spline analysis to verify linearity assumptions for model interpretation. We hypothesized that baseline demographic and laboratory parameters could reliably predict treatment outcomes, thereby facilitating early risk stratification and personalized management.

Subjects and methods

Study population

This retrospective cohort study enrolled patients with DR-TB who were managed in Fuyang City, Anhui Province, between January 2021 and December 2023. Patients were initially identified from the China Disease Prevention and Control Information System. Inclusion criteria were: (1) diagnosis of pulmonary rifampicin-resistant tuberculosis (RR-TB) or multidrug-resistant tuberculosis (MDR-TB), including pre-extensively drug-resistant tuberculosis (pre-XDR-TB), according to the Chinese Industry Standard WS 288–2017 for tuberculosis diagnosis [16], which aligns with the contemporaneous WHO diagnostic recommendations; (2) availability of complete medical records. Exclusion criteria were: (1) comorbid severe psychiatric diseases or malignant tumors, as these conditions could independently determine clinical outcomes through mechanisms unrelated to DR-TB treatment response (e.g., death from malignancy, inability to adhere to treatment due to uncontrolled mental illness); (2) presence of severe concurrent infections at the time of admission, because acute severe infections could transiently and markedly alter the baseline laboratory parameters (e.g., albumin, complete blood count) that constitute the core predictors of our model, thereby introducing measurement bias; (3) absence of inpatient treatment at our hospital, as outpatient-managed patients did not have the standardized, complete baseline laboratory panels required for model development. Accordingly, in this study, “DR-TB” refers specifically to pulmonary RR-TB, MDR-TB, and pre-XDR-TB. Patients with isoniazid mono-resistance or extra-pulmonary tuberculosis were not included.

Of 212 initially identified patients, 28 were excluded, yielding a final cohort of 184 inpatients from the Second People’s Hospital of Fuyang City. Final treatment outcomes were defined according to the 2021 World Health Organization (WHO) definitions for drug-resistant tuberculosis [17]. Favorable outcomes included cure and treatment completion. Unfavorable outcomes comprised treatment failure, death, loss to follow-up, and not evaluated. Patients who experienced adverse drug reactions during treatment but ultimately met the criteria for cure or treatment completion were assigned to the favorable outcome group. Those whose regimen was permanently discontinued due to severe adverse events and who could not be declared cured were classified as treatment failure, in accordance with WHO criteria. The patient selection process is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Flowchart of patient selection

All patients initiated treatment after admission. Treatment was prescribed according to the Technical guidelines for tuberculosis prevention and control in China (2020 edition) [18], based on drug susceptibility testing results and prior treatment history. Patients received standardized all-oral, long-course regimens (18–20 months), typically comprising at least four effective drugs, including fluoroquinolones (e.g., levofloxacin, moxifloxacin) and later-line agents such as bedaquiline, linezolid, cycloserine, and clofazimine, supplemented as needed by pyrazinamide, with individual tailoring. No patient underwent adjunctive pulmonary surgical resection during the study period.

Research methods

Collection of clinical data

Clinical data were retrospectively collected from electronic medical records at the time of admission, prior to initiation of current DR-TB treatment. Collected variables included: demographics (age, gender, BMI); smoking and drinking history; previous TB treatment history (newly diagnosed vs. previously treated); radiologic findings (pulmonary cavity, unilateral vs. bilateral involvement); comorbidities (chronic gastritis, Acute Exacerbation of Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (AECOPD), chronic pulmonary heart disease, nontuberculous mycobacterial disease, chronic hepatitis B, HIV status, chronic kidney disease, hypertension, diabetes); clinical symptoms on admission (cough, wheezing, fever, hemoptysis); treatment details (drug resistance type, number of hospital admissions); and laboratory parameters (AFB smear status, neutrophil count, lymphocyte count, monocyte count, RDW, platelet count, albumin). BMI was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared. Previous TB treatment history was classified as newly diagnosed (no prior anti-TB treatment or < 1 month of prior treatment) or previously treated (≥ 1 month of prior anti-TB treatment).

Laboratory measurements

AFB smear microscopy was performed on sputum specimens collected at admission using the Ziehl-Neelsen staining method, with results classified as negative or positive.

Venous blood samples were collected from all patients after an overnight fast. A 3 mL sample was used to measure albumin levels using a Hitachi 7600 fully automated biochemical analyzer. A 2 mL sample collected in EDTA-K₂ anticoagulant tubes was used for complete blood count analysis, including neutrophil count, lymphocyte count, monocyte count, RDW, and platelet count, employing a Sysmex XE-2100 automated hematology analyzer with its matched reagents. All laboratory measurements were performed on admission prior to treatment initiation.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R software (version 4.4.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Continuous variables with normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and compared using independent samples t-test. Non-normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as median with interquartile range [M (Q₁, Q₃)] and compared using Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages [n (%)] and compared using chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate.

Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression was employed for variable selection from 30 baseline variables, with optimal penalty parameter λ determined by 10-fold cross-validation using the one-standard-error rule. A random seed of 1234 was set to ensure reproducibility of the cross-validation partitioning. Variables selected by LASSO were subsequently included in multivariate logistic regression analysis to calculate odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) for identifying independent predictors of unfavorable treatment outcomes. To facilitate clinical application, a nomogram was constructed based on the final multivariate logistic regression model using the rms package in R. The nomogram assigns points to each predictor and maps the total points to the predicted probability of an unfavorable treatment outcome.

Model discrimination was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, with area under the curve (AUC) and 95% CI calculated. Calibration was assessed using calibration curves and the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was conducted to evaluate clinical net benefit, and clinical impact curves were used to illustrate risk stratification performance. Internal validation was performed using bootstrap resampling with 1,000 iterations to calculate bias-corrected C-index.

SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) analysis was performed to interpret the prediction model. Mean absolute SHAP values were calculated to assess global feature importance, and beeswarm plots and waterfall plots were used to visualize individual-level feature contributions.

Restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression with four knots placed at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles was performed to examine the linearity assumption for continuous predictors, adjusting for other covariates in the model. P for nonlinear was calculated to assess deviation from linearity.

All tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Of the 184 enrolled patients, 41 (22.3%) experienced unfavorable treatment outcomes. Of the 41 patients with unfavorable outcomes, 8 (19.5%) experienced treatment failure, 12 (29.3%) died, 14 (34.1%) were lost to follow-up, and 7 (17.1%) were classified as not evaluated. Compared to the favorable outcome group, patients with unfavorable outcomes were significantly older, had lower BMI and lower albumin levels (all P < 0.001). Additionally, the unfavorable outcome group had a higher proportion of previously treated patients and a higher prevalence of positive AFB smears, bilateral lung involvement, wheezing, chronic gastritis, AECOPD, and chronic pulmonary heart disease, as well as higher neutrophil count, RDW, and PLT, but lower lymphocyte count (all P < 0.05). No significant differences were observed in gender, drug resistance type, or other comorbidities (all P > 0.05). Complete baseline characteristics are presented in Table 1.

LASSO regression

LASSO regression was performed on 30 candidate variables collected at admission. Using 10-fold cross-validation and the one-standard-error rule, the optimal tuning parameter λ was 0.0997. At this λ value, three variables retained non-zero coefficients: age, BMI, and albumin (Fig. 2).

Multivariate logistic regression analysis

The three variables selected by LASSO regression were entered into multivariate logistic regression analysis. As shown in Table 2, all three variables remained independent predictors of unfavorable treatment outcomes. Older age (OR = 1.047, 95% CI: 1.018–1.077, P = 0.001) was associated with increased risk, while higher BMI (OR = 0.728, 95% CI: 0.599–0.885, P = 0.001) and higher albumin levels (OR = 0.894, 95% CI: 0.826–0.967, P = 0.005) were protective factors.

Nomogram for clinical use

To translate the model into a clinically applicable tool, a nomogram was constructed based on the three predictors (Fig. 3). The nomogram allows estimation of the predicted probability of an unfavorable outcome for an individual patient by summing the points assigned to their age, BMI, and albumin values.

Nomogram model

Model performance and validation

The model demonstrated good discrimination with an AUC of 0.833 (95% CI: 0.758–0.908) (Fig. 4a). The optimal cutoff yielded an accuracy of 0.728 (95% CI: 0.658–0.791), with sensitivity of 0.854 (95% CI: 0.745–0.962) and specificity of 0.692 (95% CI: 0.617–0.768). Positive predictive value was 0.443 (95% CI: 0.333–0.553) and negative predictive value was 0.943 (95% CI: 0.898–0.987).

Performance evaluation of the prediction model. (a) Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve with area under the curve (AUC). (b) Calibration curve comparing predicted probabilities with observed outcomes. (c) Decision curve analysis (DCA). (d) Clinical impact curve

Calibration was satisfactory, with the calibration curve closely aligning with the ideal line (Fig. 4b) and a non-significant Hosmer-Lemeshow test (χ² = 6.752, P = 0.564). Internal validation using 1,000 bootstrap resamples yielded a bias-corrected C-index of 0.819.

Decision curve analysis showed positive net benefit across threshold probabilities from approximately 0.1 to 0.8 (Fig. 4c), indicating clinical utility. The clinical impact curve further demonstrated the model’s risk stratification capacity (Fig. 4d).

SHAP interpretability analysis

SHAP analysis was performed to interpret the model predictions at both global and individual levels. Global feature importance (Fig. 5a) showed that age had the highest mean absolute SHAP value (0.67), followed by BMI (0.65) and albumin (0.52), indicating that age contributed most to model output.

SHAP analysis of the predictive model. (A) Feature importance measured by mean absolute SHAP values. (B) Beeswarm summary plot of SHAP values for each feature. Each dot represents a patient, with color indicating feature value (red: high, blue: low). Positive SHAP values correspond to increased risk of unfavorable outcomes, while negative values indicate decreased risk. (C) Waterfall plot for an individual patient, showing how each feature contributed to the final prediction

The beeswarm plot (Fig. 5b) revealed the direction and distribution of feature effects: higher age (red dots) was predominantly associated with positive SHAP values (increased risk), while higher BMI and higher albumin (red dots) were associated with negative SHAP values (protective effect). These patterns were consistent with the logistic regression results.

A representative patient example is illustrated using a waterfall plot (Fig. 5c). For this patient (BMI = 19 kg/m², age = 45 years, albumin = 37.6 g/L), the model output (f(x) = 1.58) was driven by positive contributions from low BMI and low albumin, indicating increased risk, while age had a modest negative contribution. This visualization demonstrates how individual features shift the prediction from the baseline value toward the final outcome probability.

Restricted cubic spline analysis

Restricted cubic spline regression was performed to examine the linearity assumption for continuous predictors, adjusting for the other two variables in the model. As shown in Fig. 6, all three variables demonstrated approximately linear associations with the risk of unfavorable outcomes. The nonlinear components were not statistically significant for age (P for nonlinear = 0.080), BMI (P for nonlinear = 0.818), or albumin (P for nonlinear = 0.412). These findings support the inclusion of these variables as linear terms in the logistic regression model.

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study of 184 patients with drug-resistant pulmonary tuberculosis, we developed and validated a simple predictive model based on three routinely available admission variables: age, BMI, and albumin. The model demonstrated good discriminative ability (AUC = 0.833) and satisfactory calibration, with SHAP analysis providing interpretable insights at both global and individual levels. Restricted cubic spline analysis confirmed linear associations for all three predictors, supporting the model’s simplicity and clinical interpretability.

Our finding that older age independently predicts unfavorable outcomes aligns with previous studies in DR-TB populations [19, 20]. Several mechanisms may explain this association. Aging is accompanied by immunosenescence, characterized by diminished T-cell function and impaired granuloma formation, which compromises the host’s ability to contain Mycobacterium tuberculosis [21]. Additionally, older patients often present with multiple comorbidities that may complicate treatment and increase susceptibility to adverse drug reactions [22]. The linear relationship observed in RCS analysis suggests that each year of age contributes incrementally to risk, without evidence of a threshold effect within the studied age range.

Lower BMI emerged as a significant predictor of unfavorable outcomes, consistent with the well-established role of malnutrition in TB prognosis [23]. Malnutrition impairs cell-mediated immunity, reduces macrophage function, and compromises respiratory muscle strength, all of which may contribute to poor treatment response [24]. The protective effect of higher BMI (OR = 0.728 per kg/m²) underscores the importance of nutritional assessment and support in DR-TB management, particularly in resource-limited settings where malnutrition is prevalent [25].

Albumin, a marker of both nutritional status and systemic inflammation, was independently associated with unfavorable outcomes. Hypoalbuminemia reflects decreased protein synthesis due to malnutrition, as well as increased catabolism driven by chronic inflammation [26]. Previous studies have identified low albumin as a poor prognostic indicator in various infectious diseases, including TB [27]. Our findings extend this evidence to the DR-TB population, demonstrating that admission albumin levels provide incremental predictive value beyond age and BMI alone.

The combination of these three predictors reflects a conceptual framework of baseline physiological and nutritional reserve. Age represents physiological reserve and immune competence; BMI reflects energy stores and muscle mass; albumin indicates visceral protein status and inflammatory burden. Together, these variables capture the patient’s underlying capacity to withstand the prolonged and intensive treatment required for DR-TB [28, 29]. This interpretation is supported by the SHAP analysis, which showed that all three variables contributed meaningfully to individual predictions, with age being the most influential predictor overall.

Compared to previously published models for DR-TB outcomes, our model offers several advantages. First, it relies exclusively on pre-treatment variables that are available at the time of diagnosis, enabling immediate risk stratification before treatment initiation. This contrasts with models incorporating treatment-related variables such as treatment duration or specific drug regimens, which require waiting until after treatment has commenced [30]. Second, all three predictors are routinely measured in clinical practice, low-cost, and readily available even in primary care settings, enhancing the model’s potential for widespread implementation. Third, the model’s simplicity—three variables with linear effects—facilitates clinical adoption without requiring calculators.

The nomogram constructed from our model provides a visual, user-friendly tool for individualized risk estimation at the bedside. By assigning points to age, BMI, and albumin values and mapping the total score to a predicted probability, the nomogram translates the logistic regression equation into a format that requires no electronic calculator or specialized software. This is particularly relevant in resource-limited settings where DR-TB burden is highest and where sophisticated risk prediction tools are seldom feasible. The calibration curve and Hosmer-Lemeshow test (P = 0.564) confirmed good agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes, supporting the reliability of the nomogram for clinical use. Furthermore, decision curve analysis demonstrated positive net benefit across a wide range of threshold probabilities (0.1–0.8), indicating that using the nomogram to guide clinical decisions would provide more benefit than either treating all patients as high-risk or treating none.

The application of SHAP analysis represents a methodological strength of this study. While traditional logistic regression provides odds ratios at the population level, SHAP enables individual-level explanations by quantifying each variable’s contribution to a specific patient’s predicted risk [31]. The waterfall plot in Fig. 4c illustrates how low BMI and low albumin jointly drove a high-risk prediction for an individual patient, while age exerted a modest protective effect. This level of interpretability may enhance clinician trust and facilitate shared decision-making with patients. Furthermore, the consistency between SHAP-derived variable importance and logistic regression results reinforces the robustness of our findings.

Restricted cubic spline analysis confirmed linear associations for all three predictors (P for nonlinear > 0.05 for all), supporting the validity of including them as linear terms in the logistic regression model. While the nonlinear component for age approached statistical significance (P = 0.080), this may reflect limited sample size rather than meaningful deviation from linearity. The absence of nonlinear effects simplifies clinical interpretation: each unit change in these variables corresponds to a constant multiplicative effect on outcome odds, without requiring identification of threshold values [32].

Our model may assist clinicians in several ways. At the time of DR-TB diagnosis, a simple assessment of age, BMI, and albumin can identify patients at elevated risk of unfavorable outcomes. High-risk patients might benefit from intensified nutritional support, closer monitoring for adverse events, more frequent follow-up visits, or consideration of modified treatment regimens when available [33]. In resource-limited settings where sophisticated risk stratification tools are impractical, this model offers a feasible approach to targeting limited resources toward those most in need.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a single-center retrospective study with a relatively modest sample size, which may limit generalizability and statistical power for detecting weaker associations. Second, the study population included both newly diagnosed and previously treated patients; for those with prior treatment history, admission measurements may have been influenced by previous therapy. However, from a clinical perspective, these measurements still represent the patient’s status at the time of initiating our care and are therefore relevant for predicting subsequent outcomes. Third, the unfavorable outcome group encompassed heterogeneous events (treatment failure, death, loss to follow-up, and not evaluated), which may have distinct underlying mechanisms; larger studies are needed to examine these outcomes separately. Fourth, external validation in independent cohorts is necessary before clinical implementation, as internal validation may overestimate model performance. Fifth, we did not collect data on socioeconomic factors, treatment adherence, or Mycobacterium tuberculosis strain characteristics, which may also influence outcomes. Sixth, detailed patient-level treatment data—including exact drug dosages, durations of individual treatment phases, and the timing of drug initiation or changes—were not collected. Although all patients were treated under a unified national guideline with standardized long-course regimens, which substantially constrained within-cohort treatment heterogeneity, residual variability in individualized drug combinations could not be accounted for. We therefore could not stratify the analysis by regimen type. Seventh, our cohort was restricted to inpatients, which may limit the generalizability of the model to outpatient settings where a substantial proportion of DR-TB patients are managed in many regions. Inpatients may have more advanced disease or worse baseline clinical status, and the predictive performance observed here may not directly extend to less severe outpatient populations. External validation in cohorts that include outpatients is therefore needed before wider application.

Despite these limitations, this study has notable strengths. To our knowledge, this is the first study to develop an interpretable machine learning model for DR-TB outcomes using exclusively pre-treatment variables. The combination of LASSO regression, SHAP analysis, and RCS validation provides a comprehensive analytical framework that addresses both prediction and interpretation. The focus on routinely available, low-cost predictors enhances the model’s potential for real-world implementation, particularly in resource-constrained settings where DR-TB burden is highest. Furthermore, by relying only on baseline patient characteristics available at the time of diagnosis, our model provides risk stratification that is independent of the subsequently selected treatment regimen. This makes it conceptually distinct from models that incorporate treatment-related variables, and it supports very early clinical decision-making—before a definitive individualized regimen is finalized.

Conclusions

Age, BMI, and albumin measured at admission are independent predictors of unfavorable treatment outcomes in patients with drug-resistant tuberculosis. The LASSO-derived nomogram, built on these three routinely available, low-cost variables, provides a simple yet robust tool for early risk stratification at the time of diagnosis—before treatment initiation. In settings with high DR-TB burden and constrained resources, such pre-treatment identification of high-risk patients could guide the targeted allocation of nutritional support, closer monitoring, and more intensive follow-up, potentially contributing to improved treatment success rates at the population level. Future prospective multicenter studies are warranted to externally validate this nomogram and evaluate its impact on clinical decision-making and patient outcomes.
