Comparative genomic analysis of Wolbachia strain wCin2USA1 reveals two pairs of cytoplasmic incompatibility factor (cif) genes and multiple intact prophage regions
Authors: Istiaque Zaeem, Nurnabi Azad Jewel, Daniyal Karim, Mohimenul Haque Rolin, Arzuba Akter, Shakhinur Islam Mondal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDengue fever poses an escalating global public health challenge, causing substantial morbidity and economic burden, particularly in tropical and subtropical regions. Wolbachia-mediated biocontrol has emerged as a cost-effective and environmentally sustainable strategy to suppress dengue transmission. However, the vulnerability of currently deployed Wolbachia strains in Ae. aegypti underscores the need to explore novel strains with enhanced stability and efficacy. This study aimed to identify alternative Wolbachia candidates for dengue control through comparative genomic analyses of eight Wolbachia pipientis strains: wMel, wAlbB, wlrr, wHm—C, wCin2USA1, wAnm, ant7, and a wMel variant (wMel_Lab) isolated from Ae. aegypti. Comprehensive analyses were conducted, including phylogenetic reconstruction, metabolic pathway profiling, and detailed characterization of Cytoplasmic Incompatibility Factor (Cif) genes. Among the strains examined, wCin2USA1 was distinguished by its cif gene and prophage complement. This strain exhibited substantial genomic similarity to wMel (98% average nucleotide identity), the strain currently used inAe. aegypti release programs. Notably, wCin2USA1 harbored two distinct pairs of Cif genes from different monophyletic clades, homologous to those found in wMel (Type I) and wAlbB (Type III), respectively. Both pairs are syntenic and comprise uninterrupted open reading frames. This architecture is of a type associated with cytoplasmic incompatibility in other characterized strains, although the CI phenotype of wCin2USA1 itself remains experimentally untested. Additionally, seven prophage regions were identified in wCin2USA1, indicating genomic plasticity; notably, none of the cif loci was located within a predicted prophage region. Collectively, these findings characterize the genomic architecture of an understudied Wolbachia strain and identify wCin2USA1 as warranting experimental investigation; its potential as a biocontrol agent remains a prospect contingent on functional validation. Future studies should prioritize transinfection into Ae. aegypti, crossing and embryonic hatch-rate assays to establish CI phenotype, compatibility testing against wMel- and wAlbB-infected lines, and dengue virus challenge assays.Keywords: Dengue, Wolbachia, Endosymbiont, Comparative genomics, Dengue biocontrol

Introduction

Dengue fever is a vector-borne viral disease transmitted by mosquitoes, especially by Aedes aegypti. The rapid rise of dengue poses a significant global public health challenge, as nearly half of the world's population is now at risk, with an estimated 100 to 400 million infections occurring annually.1 The adoption of numerous countermeasures has been undertaken to control the spread of dengue fever. Present management endeavors concentrate on decreasing populations of the dengue vector using a variety of biocontrol methods.2 Beyond vector-targeted biocontrol, pharmacological strategies such as computational screening of anti-dengue phytochemicals from medicinal plants are also being actively explored as candidate therapeutics against dengue virus proteins.3 In parallel, in-silico approaches have been used to design monovalent and chimeric tetravalent peptide vaccine candidates intended to raise serotype-spanning immunity against all four dengue virus serotypes.4 A major biocontrol strategy includes the introduction of endosymbiotic bacteria that disrupts various biological functions within the hosts' bodies, including dysregulating their sexual cycles by inducing Cytoplasmic Incompatibility (CI) as well as reducing their ability to transmit illnesses, and impeding the growth stages of the vector species.5 Consequently, these alterations lead to suppression of the overall vector population. Notably, one particularly impactful group of bacterial agents employed in this context is Wolbachia. Wolbachia is a genus of Gram-negative bacteria that infects a wide range of arthropod species, typically functioning as an intracellular parasite, though it also exhibits mutualistic relationships in filarial nematodes. It is one of the most prevalent endosymbiotic microbes in arthropods and is recognized as one of the most widespread reproductive manipulators in the biosphere.6 Wolbachia has complex interactions with its hosts, with some host species unable to reproduce or survive without its colonization. Cytoplasmic Incompatibility (CI) caused by Wolbachia is a reproductive barrier where uninfected female Ae. aegypti cannot produce viable offspring when they mate with male carriers of Wolbachia. Although there are no verified cases of Wolbachia being transmitted vertically purely through males, certain unidentified modifications lead to fatal consequences during the early stages of embryo development after fertilization.7 Infected female mosquitoes can produce healthy offspring whether they mate with Wolbachia-carrying or uninfected mates. Infected individuals harboring Wolbachia experience a selective advantage because they can produce viable offspring regardless of whether they mate with Wolbachia-carrying or uninfected males.8 In contrast, uninfected females can only produce viable offspring when mating with uninfected males, placing them at a reproductive disadvantage. This differential reproductive success means Wolbachia-infected females achieve more productive matings, enabling Wolbachia to spread through and replace host populations. It is important to note that Wolbachia infection often incurs fitness costs on the host rather than direct fitness benefits, meaning CI-driven reproductive advantage rather than host fitness improvement, is the primary driver of population replacement.

Genetic studies have identified two genes, CifA and CifB, associated with Cytoplasmic Incompatibility (CI) in Wolbachia. These genes are frequently located together in prophage eukaryotic association modules (EAMs) and are thought to disseminate through horizontal transmission among Wolbachia strains because they exist in prophage regions.9 There are two main models explaining CI: the toxin-antidote (TA) model and the host-modification (HM) model. The TA model suggests that CifB alters paternal chromatin in embryos, countered by maternally inherited CifA, leading to incompatibility between Wolbachia strains with different Cif homologs.10 The HM model proposes that CifB modifications occur during spermatogenesis, with CifA as a co-factor, suggesting rescue without Cif peptide packaging into sperm. This model lacks a clear explanation for the diverse compatibility patterns seen in nature 11.

While the currently established strains, wMel and wAlbB primarily, continue to be used as biocontrol agents to control mosquito populations, their efficiency and stability over long-term is becoming questionable. It is also possible that escape mutations will emerge in dengue or other arboviruses, which will reduce the efficiency of transmission blocking by current Wolbachia strains.12 In this context, it is essential to continue research and extend our understanding about other plausible strains to counter these issues.

According to available studies, wMel is generally more effective in blocking dengue virus replication than wAlbB, although both strains have been reported to decrease the transmission potential of dengue virus.13 Additionally, wMel does not appear to impose a substantial fitness cost on the mosquitoes, whereas wAlbB has been associated with slightly lower fitness benefits compared to wMel.14 However, both strains have proven successful in reducing dengue virus transmission when released in the field.

A concerning fact is number of recent studies have demonstrated that CI, which is an essential quality for the efficacy of wMel, is diminished by the cyclical heat stress that can be a characteristic of field environment.15 Once a threshold of 35 °C is achieved, high temperatures cause the infection to be less efficient and limit the hatch rate of eggs that are infected with wMel. Additionally, high temperatures reduce the density of wMel in adults, which hinders the transfer of the Wolbachia from parent to offspring.15 However, the other strain, wAlbB, has been successfully established in Malaysian populations of Ae. aegypti. It is shown to be less susceptible to high rearing temperatures and has been successfully established in Malaysian populations.16

In events where global warming continues to pose a constant threat, the proportion of the world that is exposed to diseases transmitted by mosquitoes that are fatal, increase because of rising temperatures. Moreover, it may also reduce the usefulness of the Wolbachia-mediated biocontrol by the wMel strain that has been shown to be effective in controlling vector population. Considering these factors, continued exploration of alternative Wolbachia strains is warranted to ensure that Wolbachia-mediated biocontrol remains a resilient technology against mosquito-borne diseases under near-term climate change scenarios.

Here, we examine the genomes of eight strains of Wolbachia pipientis for comparative analysis of genetic characteristics and features of Cytoplasmic incompatibility factor genes or Cif genes that are mainly linked to the capacity to induce cytoplasmic incompatibility (CI) to find more effective alternate strains alongside wMel and wAlbB. Comparative analyses of the Wolbachia genomes have been undertaken to distinguish their distinctive genetic attributes, whereas assessments of Cif genes seek to illuminate characterization of Cif genes instigated by differing Wolbachia strains responsible for disrupting host reproduction. We show that a particular strain, wCin2USA1 shares highly similar genomic properties with wMel strain yet possesses two evolutionarily distinct, syntenic and intact cif gene pairs. Additionally, our study depicts that wCin2USA1 has multiple intact prophage regions, implying its versatility and adaptability to different environments. This study presents the first genomic characterization of wCin2USA1, an understudied Wolbachia strain originally isolated from a non-mosquito host (Rhagoletis cingulata), revealing two evolutionarily distinct cif gene pairs and multiple intact prophage regions not previously described for this strain. Three aspects of this work are new: (i) the first annotation and comparative characterization of wCin2USA1 against the field-deployed reference strains wMel and wAlbB; (ii) the identification of two syntenic cifA–cifB pairs assigned to two distinct monophyletic clades (Type I and Type III), together with three unpartnered cifB loci; and (iii) the observation that all cif loci lie outside predicted prophage regions despite the retention of seven intact prophages. Thereby extending comparative Wolbachia genomics beyond the two field-deployed reference strains, wMel and wAlbB, to identify additional candidate strains for biocontrol applications. Collectively, these genomic features position wCin2USA1 as a candidate strain warranting further experimental investigation as an alternative for mosquito-borne disease control, particularly given concerns regarding the long-term field stability of currently deployed strains.

Materials and methods

Retrieval and curation of Wolbachia genomic sequences

A total of 26 Wolbachia pipientis whole-genome sequences were retrieved from the NCBI database. To reduce redundancy within this dataset, CD-HIT17 was employed to cluster and remove highly similar sequences. A stringent sequence identity threshold of 99% (−c 0.99) was applied to ensure the exclusion of near-identical genomes.

From the resulting non-redundant set, eight genomes were retained against three explicit criteria: (i) assembly completeness: only complete, closed assemblies were retained (ii) supergroup representation: the retained set spans supergroup A (wMel, wMel_Lab, wCin2USA1, wlrr, ant7) and supergroup B (wAlbB, wAnM, wHm-c), so that comparisons are not confined to a single lineage; and (iii) operational relevance: the two strains currently used in field release programmes, wMel and wAlbB, were retained as mandatory benchmarks, together with a second wMel variant recovered from released Ae. aegypti (wMel_Lab). Genome annotation

Total eight Wolbachia genomes including: wMel, wAlbB, wAnm, wlrr, ant7, wHm-c and Ae. Aegypti derived wMel variant wMel_Lab were identified as suitable for analysis, underwent various annotation processes to ensure uniformity and accuracy in functional annotation. It is important to note that the Ae. aegypti isolate (accession GCF_021496235.1, labeled wMel_Lab on NCBI) was originally collected from Ae. aegypti in Cairns, Australia, where wMel-infected mosquitoes were released as part of the Eliminate Dengue program (later renamed the World Mosquito Program). As such, this isolate represents a second wMel variant (wMel_Lab) rather than a novel strain. Its inclusion in the analysis was retained to facilitate examination of intraspecific variation within wMel and to provide comparison with wCin2USA1. PROKKA18 was utilized via the command-line interface with default paramters (minimum contig length of 200 bp, an e-value cutoff of 1e-6, rRNA/tRNA prediction enabled and Rfam search disabled) for functional annotation.

Genome mapping

To generate a BLAST comparison image among all eight strains, the Blast Ring Image Generator (BRIG)19 was utilized. BLASTn comparisons were performed within BRIG using default parameters (maximum identity 70%, minimum identity 50%, BLAST word size 28). All eight genomes were complete assemblies used as submitted in NCBI. For the visualization of genome conservation among all strains, where conservation was assessed based on nucleotide sequence identity (percentage identity) between aligned regions, EasyFig20 was used. FastANI v1.3421 was utilized to visualize pairwise genome comparison between wCin2USA1 strain and other relative strains to associate genome conservation among them.

Phylogenetic tree construction and analysis

To elucidate the evolutionary relationships among Wolbachia strains and closely related bacteria, orthologous and paralogous gene families were assigned for 8 Wolbachia strains and 20 outgroup strains from the class Alphaproteobacteria that are closely related to Wolbachia using OrthoFinder v2.5.522 with default parameters, Diamond all-vs-all protein search and MCL clustering. The orthologous groups containing only one gene in each strain or single-copy genes were selected to construct the phylogenetic tree. The protein sequences of each orthologous group were independently aligned using MAFFT v7.520.23 The phylogenetic tree was constructed based on maximum likelihood (ML) using with the resultant alignment used as input into iq-tree v2.0.6 24 using default parameters, including automated substitution model selection via ModelFinder (−m TEST) and 1000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates (−bb 1000). The tree was visualized using iTOL v6 server (https://itol.embl.de).

Orthologous gene cluster analysis

To identify and visualize orthologous gene clusters among the selected genomes, OrthoVenn3 (https://orthovenn3.bioinfotoolkits.net/home) was used. The protein sequence files (in FASTA format) of the relevant bacterial strains were uploaded to the platform. The default parameters were used: E-value cutoff of 1e-5 and an inflation value of 1.5 for MCL clustering.

The resulting orthologous clusters were then visualized using the interactive Venn diagram provided by the platform. The output Figure was exported directly from OrthoVenn3 and used to illustrate comparative genomic relationships in the final results.

Metabolic pathway construction and COG analysis

To comprehensively analyze the metabolic pathways, all the anticipated protein-encoding genes from these strains were submitted to the EggNOGmapper (v2.1.12)25 and the KEGG Automatic Annotation Server (KAAS)26 for automated functional annotation. The existence and the absence of genes within these pathways were manually evaluated and visualized as a binary presence–absence heatmap constructed in R (v4.3.1) using the ggplot2 package, where filled tiles indicate confirmed gene presence (color-coded by strain) and grey tiles denote absence.

Prophage region prediction

Prophage regions were predicted in all eight genomes using PHASTEST27 with default settings. Predicted regions were classified by completeness score that derived from the number and identity of hallmark phage genes (structural, packaging and lysis modules), region length and gene density as intact (score > 90), questionable (score 70–90) or incomplete (score < 70). Regions were subsequently inspected manually for hallmark structural genes (terminase, portal, head and tail proteins) and for integrases, transposases and recombination-associated proteins. The genomic coordinates of all identified cif loci were then intersected with the predicted prophage boundaries in order to establish whether cif genes were located within or outside prophage regions.

Cytoplasmic incompatibility factor (Cif) genes characterization

The nucleotide sequences for the CI factor (Cif) A and B genes for all five monophyletic types of Wolbachia strains were retrieved from the study conducted by Martinez et al., 2021. Based on high confidence level and accuracy (probability score > 99%, e-value <1E-10, coverage = 100), Cif gene homologs in the eight Wolbachia strains analyzed in this study were identified.

Partially sequenced cif genes were excluded from the analysis. Complete nucleotide sequences of cifA and cifB genes were used to construct a custom database using DIAMOND.28 The eight Wolbachia genomes were then queried against this database using DIAMOND with the –ultra-sensitive parameter to identify cif gene homologs. A cifA and a cifB homologue were designated a “pair” only where the two genes were syntenic, that is, adjacent and in the canonical operonic orientation; cifB loci without an adjacent cifA were recorded separately as unpaired.

The nucleotide sequences for the CifA and CifB genes were aligned separately using the program MAFFT. The separate nucleotide sequence alignments were concatenated using SeqKit.29 Poorly aligned parts were trimmed using TrimAl v1.4.30The concatenated alignment was then used as input for IQ-TREE 1.5.5.24 The Maximum Likelihood tree was inferred using IQ-TREE 1.5.5 and ran with ModelFinder and tree reconstruction. The outputted newick formatted tree was then annotated using using iTOL v6 server (https://itol.embl.de).

Results and discussion

Wolbachia strain wCin2USA1 genomic composition

Our study provided a comprehensive analysis of eight strains of Wolbachia pipientis that have been sequenced on a whole genome level. Among Wolbachia pipientis strains, wMel and wAlbB are the most commonly used in biocontrol efforts to reduce flavivirus transmission in Ae. Aegypti.14

All eight Wolbachia strains show almost similar genome sizes ranging from 1.1 Mb to 1.6 Mb. We can notice a strong genomic conservation between the Ae. aegypti isolate (wMel_Lab) and strains wMel, wCin2USA1, suggesting close evolutionary relationships and supporting the identification of the Ae. aegypti isolate as a wMel variant. These finding highlights intraspecific variation within wMel and illustrates how the same strain can exhibit minor genomic differences depending on the host population from which it is derived. In contrast, wAnm and ant7 exhibit notable divergence, characterized by fragmented identity blocks, indicating more distant evolutionary relationships or ecological specialization. Strains wHm-c and wLtr show intermediate similarity to the wMel_Lab isolate.

Genome comparison revealed that all Wolbachia strains have a very similar GC content percentage, ranging from 33.5% to 35.5%. This narrow range of GC content suggests that GC content among these strains is conserved by purifying selection, and they share a common ancestry.31

The wCin2USA1strain showed an increased gene content when compared to wMel strain. This increased gene content may be due to the acquisition of new genes through horizontal gene transfer (HGT) or the duplication of existing genes, which can lead to functional diversification and adaptation to specific ecological niches.32

It is also important to note that larger genomes may contain more non-coding regions or pseudogenes, which can be difficult to attribute to specific functions as it is seen from Table 1. that the ant7 strain possess 204 pseudogenes, which is very high compared to other strains.

The strain wCin2USA1 harbors only 57 pseudogenes despite its comparatively large genome and high gene count. While a low pseudogene burden is often interpreted as evidence of functional specialization or reductive genome streamlining, this pattern may equally reflect differences in annotation stringency between genome assemblies, distinct evolutionary histories among strains, or strain-specific genome maintenance and repair mechanisms rather than adaptive specialization. Comparative transcriptomic analysis would be required to distinguish between these possibilities. Pending such data, the low pseudogene burden of wCin2USA1 is reported here as an observation rather than as evidence of functional specialization.33

Evolutionary distinctiveness and closeness of wCin2USA1 to wMel strain

According to previously established phylogenetic classifications, Wolbachia infect a wide range of arthropods and certain nematodes, with strains organized into distinct supergroups (A–H and beyond) based on multi-locus gene sequence data, reflecting deep evolutionary divergence34 Among the most extensively studied strains, wMel and wAlbB belong to supergroup A and supergroup B, respectively, both of which are predominantly associated with insect hosts, including mosquitoes of epidemiological significance. Our findings from this study supports this notion as well. The eight Wolbachia strains analyzed in this study form a well-supported monophyletic clade relative to the alphaproteobacterial outgroups, but display deep internal divergence, with a branching pattern that partitions them into two distinct lineages. Their internal branching structure indicates that, while descended from a common ancestor, they have undergone substantial evolutionary diversification (Fig. 2).

The eight Wolbachia strains (bold) form a well-supported monophyletic clade relative to the alphaproteobacterial outgroups.

Within the Wolbachia grouping, the strains are clearly divided into two distinct subclades, corresponding to the well-established Supergroup A and Supergroup B classification of arthropod-infecting Wolbachia. The first subclade, corresponding to Supergroup A, comprises ant7, wIrr, wCin2USA1, the wMel_Lab isolate (from Ae. aegypti), and wMel, all of which show relatively short phylogenetic distances indicative of close evolutionary relationships. The second subclade, corresponding to Supergroup B, includes wAlbB, wAnm, and wHm-c, which cluster separately, suggesting a divergent evolutionary path within the Wolbachia lineage (Fig. 3).

The clustering of the wMel_Lab isolate with wMel is consistent with its identity as a wMel variant.

It suggests that the wCin2USA1 may be heavily similar to these strains compared to the other strains. The wAnm strain belongs to the furthest branch or sub-group from other strains.

The phylogenetic placement of wCin2USA1 offers valuable insights into the evolutionary diversification and symbiotic potential of Wolbachia strains. In our analysis, wCin2USA1 clusters tightly with wMel, a well-characterized strain isolated from Drosophila melanogaster and widely used in vector control programs due to its capacity to induce cytoplasmic incompatibility and suppress arboviral replication. This close evolutionary relationship suggests that wCin2USA1, originally isolated from the cherry fruit fly Rhagoletis cingulata, may share key genomic features and symbiotic traits with wMel, despite their association with phylogenetically distinct insect hosts. The remarkable genomic similarity between wCin2USA1 (from the tephritid fly Rhagoletis cingulata) and wMel (from Drosophila melanogaster) is seemingly inconsistent with the phylogenetic distance between their insect hosts. Horizontal transfer of Wolbachia between distantly related hosts is well-documented and is thought to occur via shared parasitoids, ectoparasites, or other ecological associations. Genomic studies have demonstrated that wMel-like Wolbachia strains have spread rapidly across phylogenetically distant dipteran and hymenopteran hosts, with host-switching events estimated to have occurred over timescales of 104–106 years.11 Furthermore, comparative genomic analysis of wCin2 in Rhagoletis cingulata has itself revealed extensive recombination and horizontal transfer dynamics between Wolbachia strains from related tephritid fly species.35 While a direct horizontal transfer event between Rhagoletis and Drosophila has not been specifically documented, the broader pattern of frequent Wolbachia host-switching among dipterans suggests this as a plausible explanation for the high genomic similarity observed between wCin2USA1 and wMel.

Genomic similarity and structural divergence between wCin2USA1 and the wMel strain

Synteny analysis further demonstrated extensive collinearity, with large, conserved blocks spanning the majority of both genomes (Fig. 5). However, multiple genomic inversions and rearrangements were also observed, indicated by crossing ribbons and shifted syntenic blocks in the alignment. These structural variations, while not extensive, highlight genomic plasticity that could influence gene regulation and expression profiles.36

Despite the high ANI similarity, these structural rearrangements may correspond to differences in the encoded gene complement and regulatory context between the two strains. Such genomic differences could influence phenotypic traits including host-microbe interactions and cytoplasmic incompatibility potential. However, as no proteomic or transcriptomic data were generated in this study, these remain genomic-level inferences rather than demonstrated functional differences.

A total of 748 orthologous gene clusters were identified as conserved across all analyzed Wolbachia strains, representing the species' core genome (Fig. 6). Analysis using an UpSet plot revealed that the strains wMel, wCin2USA1, wlrr, and the wMel_Lab collectively shared 72 orthologous clusters, the highest among all strain combinations. Notably, 47 orthologous clusters were exclusively shared among wMel, wCin2USA1, and the wMel_Lab, representing a significantly enriched subset. Frequent co-occurrence of wMel and wCin2USA1 within shared gene clusters underscores a high degree of genomic similarity between these two strains, suggesting a closer evolutionary relationship compared to other Wolbachia lineages examined.

These results indicate that wCin2USA1 is highly similar to the wMel strain at the genomic level while differing from it in a subset of its predicted protein complement; no proteomic data were generated in this study. Such differences could influence host interactions, reproductive manipulation efficiency, and adaptation dynamics.

Core metabolic pathways of Wolbachia spp

Biosynthetic pathways of interest in the context of Wolbachia-host symbiosis were identified through subsequent visualization and manual annotation (Fig. 7), revealing complete pathways for riboflavin, purines, pyrimidines, and haem biosynthesis. Most of these pathways were found to be present in all Wolbachia genomes. Moreover, all strains were found to contain a suite of metabolite transport and secretion systems including those for haem, zinc, iron (III), lipoproteins, and phospholipids.

Color-wise representation of the presence–absence of various genes in metabolic and secretion/transport system pathways among the selection of Wolbachia genomes. The analyzed genomes are arrayed on the x-axis, with colors of the heatmap representing the various analyzed strains. The y-axis in turn represents different genes and metabolic pathways of interest to Wolbachia studies. Greys in the heatmap represent an absence of a gene within the respective genome.

All Wolbachia strains exhibit consistent metabolic and transport routes, supporting prior research findings. No biosynthetic pathways were uncovered that could suggest a new trait acquired in any of these strains. No pathway was uniquely enriched in wCin2USA1: the differences observed between strains are differences of retention rather than of acquisition. Relevant processes for Wolbachia include haem and nucleotide biosynthesis, as well as transport components like the Type IV Secretion System (T4SS) for secreting protein effectors, shown to be present in all of the strains.

Although wAlbB, wAnm, and wHm-c strains lacked CydA and CydB genes, these genes were found in the remaining five strains. CydA and CydB encode the two subunits of cytochrome bd oxidase, a terminal oxidase of the bacterial respiratory chain involved in oxygen reduction and energy conservation. In intracellular bacteria, this complex is thought to play a role in adaptation to the low-oxygen intracellular environment of host cells, making it potentially relevant to the long-term persistence of Wolbachia within its arthropod hosts.37

On the other hand, the wMel_Lab and wMel strain lack the folB gene, while the other six strains possess it. The wCin2USA1 strain, in addition to wlrr and ant7 strains, demonstrated the preservation of all genes associated with the pathways examined in this study. This indicates that wCin2USA1 retains a more complete and conserved set of metabolic pathway genes relative to the other strains analyzed. This pattern of gene retention is consistent with, but does not directly demonstrate, greater genomic stability or functional versatility. Transcriptomic and functional assays would be needed to confirm whether this genomic completeness translates into a measurable metabolic or fitness advantage.

Additionally, the Type IV Secretion Systems (T4SSs) and Sec-secretion systems were found to be maintained in all eight Wolbachia genomes. The T4SSs are known to play roles in infection and survival for a diverse range of symbiotic and pathogenic intracellular bacteria. It has been predicted that Wolbachia strains in the filarial nematode Brugia malayi utilize their T4SS to secrete protein effector molecules to avoid autophagy pathways and aid in actin cytoskeleton reformation, allowing intracellular mobility.38 Such processes may also be conserved within the Wolbachia strains analyzed in this study.

From the cluster of orthologous groups (COG) heatmap, the predominance of genes in COG category L (replication) within the core can be noticed (Fig. 8). It is the most represented functional COG category in the Wolbachia genomes. The second most represented functional category contains genes involved in translation (J) followed by the S category that comprises of genes with unknown functions. Beyond cytoplasmic incompatibility, the virus-blocking capacity of Wolbachia strains is an equally important determinant of biocontrol efficacy. Several mechanisms have been proposed, including activation of the host Toll, IMD, and JAK/STAT innate immune pathways, induction of reactive oxygen species (ROS)-mediated oxidative stress, competitive sequestration of lipids such as acyl-carnitines that are essential for dengue virus replication, and physical disruption of the endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi membranes required for viral assembly39, 40 Given the close genomic similarity of wCin2USA1 to wMel and the conservation of key metabolic and immune-relevant pathways identified in this study, it is plausible that wCin2USA1 may share virus-blocking capabilities with wMel. The preservation of riboflavin biosynthesis genes, haem pathways, and intracellular transport systems in wCin2USA1 indicates the metabolic infrastructure needed to establish a robust intracellular presence. Future experimental studies should directly test whether wCin2USA1 can block dengue virus replication in Ae. aegypti and evaluate the specific pathways activated upon transinfection.

Cif gene characterization

Our genomic analysis of the Wolbachia strain wCin2USA1, previously uncharacterized and unreported in this context, identified two distinct syntenic cifA–cifB gene pairs, each affiliated with separate monophyletic clades within the cif gene family (Fig. 9). This suggests that wCin2USA1 may harbor functionally and evolutionarily divergent cif operons, possibly acquired through separate horizontal transfer events. Notably, pair-1 of wCin2USA1 shows strong sequence similarity to the cif genes found in the wMel_Lab and the wMel strain, both of which belong to the Type-I monophyletic clade. In contrast, pair-2 of wCin2USA1 exhibits high homology to the cif genes of wAlbB, which fall within the Type-III monophyletic clade (Table 2; Fig. 9A). Both pairs comprise uninterrupted open reading frames of length comparable to their reference homologs, and are therefore described here as intact and putatively functional. This establishes only that the loci are not obviously pseudogenized, and not that they are transcribed, translated or functional. Table 2 additionally records the cifA homologue detected in ant7 (Fig. 9A), however, any syntenic cifB homologue was absent in ant7 strain.

Wolbachia genomes often encode multiple cifA–cifB gene pairs, a configuration thought to underlie complex patterns of bidirectional cytoplasmic incompatibility.41 The presence of two evolutionarily distinct cif operon pairs in wCin2USA1 raises the hypothesis that this strain may possess a combined CI-inducing potential, drawing on mechanisms characterized in both wMel and wAlbB. This remains a genomic prediction, so direct evidence like crossing experiments, embryonic hatch-rate assays, and male-female compatibility studies will be required to confirm whether this genetic architecture translates into a functional advantage in host reproductive manipulation. We emphasize that neither CI strength nor virus-blocking capacity can be predicted from cif copy number, and no comparative claim of stronger cytoplasmic incompatibility is advanced here. The relevant context is comparative rather than functional: multiple cif pairs are associated with more complex, and in some cases bidirectional, incompatibility patterns,42 and supergroup A strains carrying multiple horizontally acquired cif pairs are already documented, including wIrr, which is itself part of the present dataset.43

An operational consequence of this architecture warrants explicit consideration. Under the toxin–antidote framework, rescue requires a CifA antidote cognate to the CifB toxin. A strain carrying both a Type-I (wMel-like) and a Type-III (wAlbB-like) pair might therefore rescue modifications imposed by either lineage, which would be advantageous, permitting introduction into areas where wMel or wAlbB releases have already taken place. Equally, however, divergent cif complements are the recognized basis of bidirectional incompatibility between Wolbachia strains, and the introduction of wCin2USA1 into an established wMel- or wAlbB-infected population could instead generate mutual incompatibility, imposing a reproductive barrier, depressing effective population replacement and potentially destabilizing an existing and functioning release programme. Because wMel and wAlbB are already established at multiple field sites, pairwise crossing experiments between wCin2USA1-transinfected lines and wMel- and wAlbB-infected lines, in both cross directions, are a prerequisite for any deployment consideration rather than an optional follow-up.

Phylogenetic inference indicates that these gene pairs have diverged significantly from each other, aligning with distinct clades characterized in previous studies, such as those described by Martinez et al. (2021). This diversity could point to multiple functional mechanisms or evolutionary strategies for host manipulation.

In addition to the paired genes, three cifB genes were identified without an associated cifA partner in their genomic vicinity (Fig.9). The absence of adjacent cifA genes raises several possible interpretations. One explanation is that these cifB-only loci may represent degenerate or remnant operons, where the cifA gene has been lost due to decay or deletion, while the cifB component remains intact. Alternatively, these unpaired cifB genes might function independently or in conjunction with non-canonical partners. Some studies have proposed that cifB can retain partial or modified functionality even in the absence of cifA, particularly in strains where cytoplasmic incompatibility (CI) is weak or absent.42 Complementing vector-based interventions such as Wolbachia biocontrol, host-gene network analyses have similarly identified natural compounds. For example mangiferin as candidate modulators of dengue virus–host gene interactions, underscoring dengue control as a multi-pronged effort spanning vector, pharmacological, and immunological strategies.44

Prophage regions in wCin2USA1

Analysis revealed that wCin2USA1 maintains multiple prophage regions, including well-defined modules encoding structural phage proteins such as tail, head, portal, and terminase proteins, in contrast to several other Wolbachia strains where such regions are either degenerated or entirely absent. In total, seven prophage regions were identified, of which all seven appear to be intact based on the presence of hallmark phage genes (Fig. 10). This pattern is consistent with prior observations in several Wolbachia strains that exhibit cytoplasmic incompatibility (CI), where prophage insertions remain partially or fully functional and retain conserved gene synteny45. Retained prophage content is not, however, unambiguously advantageous: active mobile elements are equally a source of genomic instability, and prophage induction can be deleterious to the host bacterium.

The presence of multiple integrases, transposases, and recombination-related proteins in wCin2USA1 further supports the notion of ongoing or recent prophage activity, possibly enabling horizontal gene transfer or gene rearrangements within the Wolbachia genome.46 This stands in contrast to strains such as wAnM, where prophage regions are completely absent, a feature previously reported only in nematode-infecting Wolbachia.47

Cytoplasmic incompatibility genes of wCin2USA1

None of the identified cif genes neither the paired nor unpaired are located within predicted prophage regions of the wCin2USA1 genome. This is notable given the well-documented association of cif genes with prophage WO in many Wolbachia strains.48 The decoupling of cif genes from prophage contexts in wCin2USA1 may suggest either a genomic translocation event from their ancestral phage regions or long-term stabilization and domestication of these genes within the Wolbachia chromosome.49

Such observations align with emerging evidence that cif genes, once integrated via phage-mediated transfer, can persist and evolve independently of prophage elements. This separation may allow Wolbachia to retain host-manipulative capabilities even in the absence of active prophage machinery, potentially representing an adaptive benefit.50

The presence of multiple and divergent cif operons independent of prophage regions, alongside solitary cifB genes, underscores the genetic complexity of reproductive manipulation in wCin2USA1. It is plausible that different cif loci contribute to distinct CI phenotypes, or that some are vestigial while others remain functionally active.51 Functional studies would be necessary to assess expression levels, protein interactions, and phenotypic outcomes in host insects.

Scope of inference and future directions

This study is based exclusively on comparative genomic analyses, and the functional conclusions drawn herein should be interpreted as genomically-informed hypotheses rather than demonstrated biological properties. The cytoplasmic incompatibility (CI) potential inferred from wCin2USA1's cif gene pairs has not been functionally validated: in previously characterized Wolbachia strains, CI phenotype has only been confirmed through direct crossing and embryonic hatch-rate assays or through transgenic recapitulation of cif gene pairs52.12 However, no crossing, embryonic hatch-rate or transgenic recapitulation assay has been performed for this strain wCin2USA1. The dengue-biocontrol potential attributed to wCin2USA1 on the basis of its genomic similarity to wMel and wAlbB remains untested practically. Virus-blocking has previously been demonstrated only through direct dengue challenge assays in Wolbachia-infected mosquitoes,53 and no such experiment was conducted in this study. While prophage WO-associated genes are mechanistically linked to CI in other Wolbachia strains48, 49, the contribution of wCin2USA1's specific prophage content to ecological adaptability, environmental resilience, or long-term persistence was not experimentally assessed. Similarly, the low pseudogene count observed in wCin2USA1 may reflect genuine functional retention, but could equally arise from differences in assembly quality, sequencing coverage, or annotation stringency between genomes.54 A concern of particular relevance given that wCin2USA1 was originally isolated from a non-mosquito host, Rhagoletis cingulata challenges the feasibility of establishing wCin2USA1 in Ae. aegypti via transinfection. Previously interspecific and intergeneric transfer of Wolbachia has historically required embryonic microinjection and frequently fails due to incompatibility between the donor strain and the recipient cytoplasmic environment.55 Genomically and functionally validated strains such as wMel have shown reduced CI and reduced field persistence under elevated and fluctuating temperature conditions,15 underscoring that genomic promise alone does not guarantee stable field performance. These limitations should be addressed in future work combining transinfection, crossing, virus-challenge, and thermal-tolerance experiments to functionally validate the candidacy of wCin2USA1 as a potential next-generation Wolbachia biocontrol strain.

Conclusions

Our comparative genomic analysis characterizes Wolbachia strain wCin2USA1 and identifies two features not previously described for it: two evolutionarily distinct, syntenic and intact cifA–cifB pairs assigned to the Type-I and Type-III monophyletic clades, and seven prophage regions, none of which contains a cif locus. This architecture is of a type associated with cytoplasmic incompatibility and with genomic plasticity in other characterized strains. However, these remain genomic predictions that have not been experimentally tested in this study. The strain wCin2USA1 therefore represents a candidate strain warranting future transinfection and functional studies in Ae. aegypti, particularly given concerns regarding the field stability of currently deployed strains. Future work should prioritize crossing and embryonic hatch-rate assays to confirm CI phenotype, dengue virus-challenge assays following transinfection, compatibility testing against established wMel- and wAlbB-infected populations in both cross directions, and assessment of maternal transmission stability and thermal tolerance, in order to determine whether the genomic architecture reported here corresponds to any functional capability in a mosquito host.
