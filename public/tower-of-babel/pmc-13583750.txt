Development and Implementation of a One Health Point of Entry Checklist in Iraq and Jordan
Authors: Alexander G Linder, Kathryn M Hogan, Mohammed J Ahmed, Hanan Abdulghafoor Khaleel, Marha Liwaa Kamoona, Sinan Mehdi Gazi, Nour Abu Elizz, Mohammed Abu-Dalbouh, Ahmad Obaidat, Moutasem Hussainat, Khalid Al Gobori, Alanna S Fogarty, Benjamin Charles Wakefield, Aishwarya Nagar, Rachel Vahey, Thaer S Hussein, Bilal Shtaiyat, Mohammed Alragaad, Ekhlas Hailat, Karim M Al-Zadawi, Erin Sorrell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPoints of entry (POEs) serve as critical hubs in national security efforts, facilitating trade and travel while mitigating threats such as transboundary zoonotic diseases. Given the multisectoral nature of One Health (OH) challenges at POEs, there is a need for a systematic tool to assess preparedness and response capabilities. Existing evaluation frameworks, such as the International Health Regulations (IHR) Monitoring and Evaluation Framework and the World Organisation for Animal Health Performance of Veterinary Services (PVS) Pathway, lack comprehensive OH-specific guidance tailored to POEs.MethodsTo address this gap, we developed and piloted an OH POE Checklist, an assessment tool designed to evaluate human, animal and environmental health capacities at land border crossings and international airports. Collaborating with stakeholders from Iraq and Jordan, we conducted pilot assessments of the checklist at the Trebil, Karameh and Jaber land border points as well as at Baghdad International and Queen Alia International airports.ResultsThe Checklist, structured under the Prevent, Detect, Respond and Evaluate framework, was mapped against existing health security evaluation tools, demonstrating 68% alignment (100/148 indicators) across key indicators from State Party Self-Assessment Annual Reporting, Joint External Evaluation, PVS and IHR POE Annex. Recommendations from these pilots highlighted the need to add questions on methods of international communication between border officials and create a version suitable for mobile-phone completion. Additionally, the pilot highlighted the need to incorporate a secondary evaluation by national and local authorities and broaden the scope to encompass deliberate event competencies.ConclusionsThis study outlines the development, implementation and refinement of the OH POE Checklist, contributing to the integration of OH principles into border security and public health preparedness.Keywords: Disease Outbreaks, Disease Notification, Risk Assessment, Zoonoses

Introduction

Points of entry (POEs) are a vital component of national security efforts to ensure lawful trade and travel, while combating the illegal movement of contraband and people.1 In addition to countering traditional border security threats, POEs must respond to potential One Health (OH)2 events, including the spread of transboundary zoonotic diseases (TZDs) and Transboundary Animal Diseases (TADs). TZDs3 are highly contagious or transmissible zoonotic diseases that can spread rapidly, causing epidemic or pandemic levels of transmission, and with potential for substantial socioeconomic impact while zoonoses are included in the classification TADs4 encompass additional highly contagious epidemic livestock diseases that spread rapidly across borders disrupting livestock production, reducing market access and increasing vulnerability. Given the unique role of POEs at the nexus of national security and the human, animal and environmental interface, it is essential to assess the capacity of POEs to respond to OH threats and interact with public health, veterinary and environmental health sectors, all while aligning with the broader border security responsibilities.

Various international frameworks and guidance documents exist for assessing and categorising responses to chemical, biological, radiological and nuclear events5; ensuring human rights and safety during mass migrations6; and safeguarding human7 and animal health during transport.8 Key international frameworks and evaluation tools such as the International Health Regulation (IHR)9 and components of the IHR monitoring and evaluation framework (MEF) such as the Joint External Evaluation (JEE) tool,10 the State Party Self-Assessment Annual Reporting (SPAR) Tool,11 as well as the World Organisation for Animal Health’s (WOAH) Performance of Veterinary Services (PVS) Pathway,12 are designed to evaluate national public health and veterinary capacities and include sections to assess these capacities at POEs.13 However, none of these tools assess the key factors of an effective, coordinated response to OH events at POEs, (what is available, functional, and actionable at the POE itself) which are critical for managing TZD threats. Moreover, current assessments lack concrete guidance or key corrective actions which consider the unique role border points play in the detection and response to disease events. This gap in existing assessments of POEs hampers effective prevention and response efforts for TZD at these sentinel locales vital to national safety, security, trade and health.

To address this gap, we developed the OH POE Checklist14 (hereafter referred to as the Checklist) as a practical, multisectoral tool designed to assess and strengthen OH preparedness and response capacities at formal border points. While frameworks such as the JEE, SPAR and PVS evaluate national-level systems, they lack operational questions tailored to the multisectoral and cross-border coordination and rapid-response functions required at POEs. The checklist was created in collaboration with national partners to provide a rapid, operationally grounded OH baseline tool for assessing the capabilities and capacities of land border crossings to detect and respond to TZD events; these outputs inform national-level programmes, and where applicable, align with topics that the broader evaluation frameworks like JEE and PVS address. This approach ensures complementarity rather than redundancy, enabling countries to strengthen POE-level OH capacities while contributing to existing global health security frameworks.

Our team collaborated with relevant border authorities and OH experts from the Republic of Iraq and the Hashemite Kingdom of Jordan to develop the Checklist and piloted it at the Trebil border point in Iraq and the Jaber and Karameh border points in Jordan. Initial objectives of the Checklist were to provide security officials and leaders in the human and animal health sectors with a practical tool to assess preparedness and response mechanisms at land border crossings, identify strengths, highlight areas needing improvement, and ensure consistency with international regulations for human and animal health at POEs. In this paper, we will present the process of developing the Checklist; describe the pilots at three land border crossings in Iraq and Jordan and ultimately at two international airports; share the recommendations we received from security personnel for future revisions; and summarise the mapping of the Checklist against existing health security evaluation frameworks.

Methods

Checklist development

Engagement

Prior to developing the Checklist, our team collaborated with national partners to strengthen understanding and coordination around shared TZD threats at Jordan and Iraq’s shared border points. As part of this effort, which included implementing our OH Transboundary Assessment for Priority Zoonoses (OHTAPZ)14 and a tabletop exercise (TTX), we liaised with key experts from human, animal, environmental and border security sectors in both countries. These activities revealed a need to understand existing OH capacities and capabilities at POEs to inform the design of our transboundary simulation exercise (SimEx) and guide the development of national action plans for countering TZDs. To address the absence of operational tools capable of meeting practical, multisectoral and cross-border coordination needs, we developed the Checklist. Experts from the Iraqi Ministries of Health and Agriculture (I-MOH & MOA) and Jordanian Ministries of Health and Agriculture (J-MOH & MOA) served as focal points and coordinated with relevant ministries in each country to ensure that appropriate OH stakeholders (Iraq: Border Commission; Jordan: Ministry of Health, Jordan Centers for Disease Control and Jordan Armed Forces) were consulted during Checklist development.

Establishing requirements for the OH checklist

We grounded development of the Checklist in four key requirements agreed on by all partners. First, the pilot would focus on formal land border crossings, specifically the Karameh and Trebil POEs between Iraq and Jordan, which were the target of our OHTAPZ TTX and SimEx. Second, the Checklist needed to be concise and feasible for completion by POE officials from multiple sectors, including administration, security, health, agriculture and environment. Third, it was designed to assess capacities aligned with the international recommendations and regulations for human, animal and environmental health. Finally, while informed by international guidance, the Checklist should provide concrete, practitioner-focused measures which could be readily evaluated by border officers and staff directly at the POE in accordance with national policies for POE operations.

Developing an OH POE checklist

With partner requirements established, we began developing the Checklist by identifying keywords that captured border security alongside human, animal, and environmental health. These keywords spanned domains: border and POEs; human health; animal health; and environmental health. Keywords were selected by the study team to build on the requirements and operational functions of our national partners identified as relevant at land border crossings. Our first objective was to review existing international guidance on OH responses at POEs. Searches were conducted through general internet search engines and directly searching the publication repositories of relevant international and national bodies with experience in transboundary disease; these searches were supplemented by searching the relevant references list of retrieved and initially identified documents. As the object was to assemble a practical evidence base of authoritative guidance rather than to conduct a systematic review, searches were performed using national language without Boolean syntax. Documents were included if they (1) were issued by an international, regional or national authority; (2) provided guidance, standards or recommendations relevant to the prevention, detection or response to infectious disease or OH events at POEs. We identified 16 documents (online supplemental material 1), which met these criteria and formed the basis for the land border Checklist, including guidance on international trade and travel developed during the SARS, H5N1, Ebola, Zika and COVID-19 outbreaks, as well as recommendations for animal health, vector control and hazardous material transport.

We distilled our findings to formulate 13 core aspects of an effective OH response at POEs, organised under the Prevent, Detect, Respond and Evaluate Framework (table 1). Checklist questions were not directly copied from any single source but were drafted by our team to be practitioner-focused, relevant to land border operations and applicable to the multiple stakeholders contributing to OH responses at land borders. Guided by the identified documents and frameworks, together with focal points from I-MOH & MOA and J-MOH & MOH, we created and piloted the Checklist consisting of 95 questions which test capacities for an effective OH response at land borders (online supplemental material 2). Iterative refinement of the checklist

Checklist development and revision followed an iterative consultative process rather than a discrete round of data collection. Draft versions were circulated to focal points from I-MOH & MOA and J-MOH & MOA, who reviewed them with colleagues in their ministries and returned consolidated comments through a series of virtual working meetings. Proposed changes to question wording, sequence, and scope were incorporated and returned to partners for confirmation before each subsequent round, continuing until the partners agreed the Checklist was ready for pilot implementation. The same cycle was applied after the pilot, drawing on free-text comments accompanying each Checklist question and on structured discussion with sector leads during the SimEx preparation sessions. Comments were compiled by the study team and presented back to partners for prioritisation.

Results

Assessing the OH Checklist against existing health security evaluation frameworks

Although the Checklist was informed by international guidance and best practices on disease prevention, detection and response, it was developed independently to meet the operational and multisectoral needs of POEs. To ensure that the Checklist complemented rather than duplicated existing health security evaluation frameworks, we assessed its alignment with key monitoring tools used by countries implementing the MEF. We checked for alignment with the SPAR Tool, the JEE Tool, the WOAH PVSs tool and the IHR Assessment Tool for Core Capacity Requirements at Designated POEs. Indicators and corresponding capacity areas were extracted from each tool. If at least one checklist question would inform the measurement of an indicator, it was considered aligned, and alignment percentages were summarised by tool and capacity area (table 2). This process provided evidence for how using the checklist can also address key capacities and indicators specified in SPAR, PVS and JEE.

Across the four health security evaluation tools, there were 148 indicators within 43 capacity areas that we assessed. The Checklist aligned with 100 out of the 148 indicators (68%). The Annex had the most overlap with the Checklist, with 90% of the 29 Annex indicators aligning, followed by 69% of the 48 JEE indicators, 60% of the 35 SPAR indicators and 56% of the 36 PVS indicators. All the indicators within a capacity area aligned with the Checklist in six out of 15 capacities for SPAR, nine out of 21 capacities for JEE, and two out of three capacities for core capacities at POE tool. No PVS capacities were fully aligned, as most focused on food safety, animal welfare and veterinary medicines which were beyond the scope of the Checklist.

The 68% alignment underscores the Checklist’s complementarity with existing international evaluation frameworks. While other tools, such as the IHR Benchmarks,15 assist countries in translating MEF findings into national action plans, the Checklist’s objective is to complement the MEF and the IHR Benchmarks effort by operationalising POE disease preparedness and response through an OH lens, providing border authorities with a practical instrument to evaluate and strengthen multisectoral response efforts.

Piloting the OH POE checklist

The Checklist was piloted in March 2024 at the Karameh and Jaber border crossings in Jordan and the Trebil border crossing in Iraq by multisectoral evaluation teams comprised of focal points from the human, animal, environment and security sectors at each POE. The Checklist was available in Arabic and English via Microsoft Excel for ease of use, with response options of ‘Yes’, ‘No’ and ‘Not applicable’ with space for comments for each question. Sectors responsible for disease prevention, detection, and response at the assessed land borders in both countries completed the Checklist, and sectoral responses were collated to develop an overarching, multisector understanding of POE capacities. Feedback on the Checklist design was collected anonymously; we did however record the sector of the respondent to assess how their recommendations for Checklist design addressed sector-specific needs, knowledge, responsibilities, as well as considerations for the variation between sister POEs.

Recommendations from pilot

Sector leads from Border Security, Customs and Immigration, Health, Veterinary Services and Environmental Services provided recommendations to strengthen future Checklist iterations. National sector representatives also contributed input to ensure the tool reflected OH priorities and competencies for formal land border crossings and could be applied to additional POEs. These recommendations were solicited during a series of virtual meetings with in-country coauthors and collaborators during the Checklist development process and as a focus of the session during a wider preparation for an OH SimEx at the Jaber and Trebil border points.16 This work was completed collaboratively and iteratively as guidance and recommendations from the field were provided until the completion of the SimEx. Recommendations were generated through facilitated group discussion and written feedback and are reported here as consolidated positions by sector leads rather than individually attributed responses. These recommendations include:

Developing online and offline versions: For remote border points, respondents recommended an online version of the Checklist accessible on mobile devices and easily translatable into local languages. Such functionality would simplify completion and improve the efficiency of data collection, analysis and action planning.

Refining the POE checklist based on pilot

In response to feedback provided from the land border Checklist pilot, our team created an OH Checklist for international airports. The revised version built on the land border Checklist, adding new questions specific to the unique OH and security context of international airports while retaining the majority of questions that continued to be relevant across all POE types (table 1). The revision process followed a parallel structure used for the land border Checklist but emphasised prevention and detection, which were noted by sector leads as priority areas in an airport setting. The resulting OH Checklist for International Airports drew from 28 international guidance documents, and included 109 questions following the same Prevent, Detect, Respond Framework used in the land border Checklist (online supplemental file 3). Documents for the airport Checklist were identified using the same search approach as described above, with the addition of aviation-specific keywords.

The OH Checklist for International Airports was piloted at Baghdad International Airport (BGW) and Queen Alia International Airport (AMM) in November 2024. Responses from sector specific leads from Border Security, Customs and Immigration, Health, Veterinary Services, Environmental Services and Civil Aviation were collated to provide an overarching assessment of both airports. National leads responsible for aggregating sector responses noted that the Checklist provides an in-depth analysis of airport capabilities to prevent, detect and respond to potential TZDs through an OH lens and provided recommendations for future Checklist refinement. These recommendations include:

Identifying sector-specific questions: Respondents noted that not all questions apply to every sector and recommended delineating sector-specific questions in future iterations and aggregating responses from each sector to generate a full airport checklist assessment.

Expanding security-focused questions related to Joint Criminal-Epidemiological Investigations and responses to deliberate events: Respondents recommended future iterations of the airport checklist expand assessments beyond naturally occurring TZD outbreaks to Joint Criminal-Epidemiological Investigations for potential deliberate misuse of biological specimens which could impact trade and travel. Conclusions

POEs play vital national and regional roles at the intersection of border security and TZD prevention and response. These events require multisectoral coordination, collaboration, and communication across all OH stakeholders. Our POE Checklist puts OH considerations at the centre of understanding the capabilities and capacities at POEs and enables identification of strengths, weaknesses, and priority actions for future capacity building. While POEs vary in their OH competencies based on local trade and travel contexts, the Checklist was designed for broad application across different types of formal border crossings to assess multisectoral readiness and inform national and regional planning.

The Checklist’s alignment with existing frameworks and evaluation tools is due to the fact the SPAR, JEE and PVS were part of its foundation. We recognise that countries are already navigating multiple evaluation and reporting instruments and may face challenges and frustrations implementing multiple tools. Our goal in designing the Checklist was to develop a baseline assessment tool that can evaluate relevant OH capacities at formal POEs while aligning with the requirements of three separate tools (SPAR, JEE and PVS), not to comprehensively cover what existing frameworks already do. As a standalone tool, implementation costs are relatively low; there are no requirements for training, workshops, or external assessment teams as the Checklist is designed to take no more than 45–60 min (per POE) and be completed by existing staff designated to each POE. Therefore, the actual cost of implementation is the staff time to complete the Checklist. Outputs from the assessment can be used by various stakeholders at the border and national level for preparedness and response planning. For example, Checklist responses can inform SWOT analysis to identify targeted action plans to build capacity under National Action Plans for Health Security and/or PVS Pathway. These outputs do require additional time and cost that should be factored depending on the expected output. For example, if a country wanted to follow all five phases of OHTAPZ, the POE Checklist outputs (phase 4) would inform priority functions to be tested in a SimEx (Phase 5) at the target POE(s). Therefore, depending on the country context, framework applied and follow-on activities preimplementation and postassessment costs would need to be incorporated and accounted for.

Although the Checklist shows high alignment with existing evaluation mechanisms, we acknowledge that the Checklist is still in its early stages, and gaps and limitations remain. The Checklist was scoped deliberately to assess OH capacities that are available, functional, and actionable at designated formal POEs, to respond to emerging and novel disease threats, and to be completed by existing POE staff. Capacities located beyond the border or held at the national level, sit at the remit of other instruments, notably the PVS pathway, national veterinary services, and customs and law enforcement mandates; the Checklist is intended to complement rather than to substitute for these tools. While allowing for ease of completion, we acknowledge that the tool relies on POE staff to self-report, which may introduce desirability bias; this could be strengthened with independent verification. Additionally, the Yes/No/Not Applicable format may not capture partial or inconsistent capacity; however, we do provide an area for comments to understand the minutiae of day-to-day operations. This current iteration of the Checklist and its high level of initial alignment with existing frameworks, however, provides opportunities for future efforts focused on building on the land border and international airport tools; incorporating feedback from sector leads in Karameh, Jaber, Trebil, BGW and AMM; implementing a robust review by national authorities to assess POE responses; and including further metrics to assess collaboration between OH sectors and traditional security forces at international airports. These additions could include component areas that have a more deliberate focus such as agroterrorism, smuggling of illegal wildlife and/or bioterrorism events. Further, we hope to continue working on further alignment with existing evaluation frameworks as we refine our questions, with a particular note to improve alignment with the WOAH PVS Pathway.

Developed collaboratively with OH and border security experts in Iraq and Jordan, the OH POE Checklist was created as a living document and part of the larger OHTAPZ toolkit. Available online,15 the OHTAPZ methodology elaborates on the various applications for the Checklist, as noted above. On its own, the Checklist represents a pragmatic step towards systematically understanding OH capabilities and capacities at formal border crossings. The Checklist emphasises practical and real-world considerations for OH responses, while aligning with existing evaluation frameworks and POE assessments. Recommendations from the land border pilot directly informed its adaptation for international airports, ensuring applicability across diverse POEs. As the Checklist continues to be expanded, refined and implemented, it will serve as a resource for integrating OH principles into border security, strengthening multisectoral coordination and advancing preparedness for TZDs, ultimately contributing to more resilient national and regional health security systems.
