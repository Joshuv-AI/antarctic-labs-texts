ALDH3A2-mediated fatty acid synthesis induces ferroptosis and AML drug resistance
Authors: Yun Huang, Yu Zhao, Ping Yan, Zhiquan Long, Chuang Xia, Ling Jiang, Yujiao Zhang, Jiaying Cheng, Fang Liu, Quan Wu, Zhongzhen Zhou, Xuejie Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryChemoresistance in acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is associated with a poor prognosis in patients. Ferroptosis is one of the mechanisms by which anthracycline exerts anti-leukemic effects, with drug-resistant AML cells exhibiting increased resistance to ferroptosis. Elevated expression of aldehyde dehydrogenase 3 family member A2 (ALDH3A2) is correlated with poor prognosis in patients with AML. Patients with chemo-resistant AML exhibit higher levels of ALDH3A2, particularly the V subtype. ALDH3A2 degraded 4-hydroxynonenal and regulated the fatty acid proportion and content of AML cells, protecting AML cells against doxorubicin-induced ferroptosis. Moreover, overexpression of ALDH3A2 changes cell membrane fluidity and reduces drug uptake. Histone deacetylase 2 binds to the ALDH3A2 promoter and regulates its expression. The inhibition of ALDH3A2 or HDAC enhances chemotherapeutic efficacy in AML.

Introduction

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a malignant disease of hematopoietic cells in the bone marrow. The current standard induction therapy for newly diagnosed AML is the “3 + 7” regimen comprising 3 days of anthracycline and 7 days of cytarabine. The complete remission (CR) rate of the standard 3 + 7 regimen is 60%–80% for patients with AML under 60–65 years old.1 Some patients exhibit primary resistance to chemotherapy, defined as failure to achieve CR after two courses of standard induction therapy, with a poorer prognosis compared with others. Insensitivity to chemotherapy remains an important clinical challenge. Although increasing the dose of anthracyclines improves chemotherapeutic efficacy and reduces drug resistance, the side effects limit the increase in dosage.2 One strategy for improving the therapeutic effect of anthracyclines is to increase their efficacy at limited doses.

Accumulating evidence indicates that ferroptosis plays a pivotal role in tumor progression and drug resistance, highlighting its associated targets as promising therapeutic candidates.3 Ferroptosis is an iron-dependent form of regulated cell death characterized by lipid peroxidation, in which phospholipid hydroperoxides (PLOOHs) are generated through the peroxidation of membrane phospholipids and subsequently decomposed into products such as 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE) or malondialdehyde (MDA), thereby inducing cell death. These lipid peroxidation end-products can cause membrane instability and permeabilization, ultimately resulting in cell death.4 Polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) are primary substrates for lipid peroxidation, thereby regulating the sensitivity of the plasma membrane to ferroptosis. Increased saturation of membrane lipids in tumor cells reduces their susceptibility to oxidative stress, thereby conferring resistance against cell death.5 Increased levels of intracellular PUFAs promote ferroptosis, an important antitumor mechanism of anthracyclines.6

Aldehyde dehydrogenase 3 family member A2 (ALDH3A2), known as fatty aldehyde dehydrogenase (FALDH) or ALDH10, is a member of the aldehyde dehydrogenase family. Its main biochemical function is to oxidize medium- or long-chain fatty aldehydes to the corresponding fatty acids.7 ALDH3A2 is considered a pathogenic gene of the autosomal recessive genetic disease Sjogren-Larsson syndrome8; however, recent studies have found that ALDH3A2 is associated with poor prognosis of malignant tumors such as gastric adenocarcinoma, TMPRSS2-ERG gene-positive prostate cancer, and esophageal adenocarcinoma.9,10,11 ALDH3A2 is transcribed into two subtypes, FALDH-N and FALDH-V, depending on the carboxy-terminal sequence splicing. FALDH-N is located in the endoplasmic reticulum and protects cells from arachidonic acid-induced endoplasmic reticulum stress. The FALDH-V subtype, located in the peroxisome and characterized by the presence of a termination codon in exon 9′, does not translate exon 10 from this transcript, resulting in a larger molecular weight than FALDH-N.12 It protects peroxisomes from oxidative stress and is a key enzyme involved in plasmalogen degradation.7,13 Compared with their normal myeloid counterparts, AML cells had stronger survival dependence on ALDH3A2, and knocking down ALDH3A2 makes AML cells more susceptible to oxidative damage and ferroptosis.14

However, the expression profile of ALDH3A2 in real-world AML patients and its impact on the efficacy of chemotherapy have not been investigated. Our study tried to further elucidate this and explored the effect of inhibiting ALDH3A2 activity or expression on enhancing the sensitivity to chemotherapy.

Results

Expression of ALDH3A2 in AML cells

Through the analysis of data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and genotype tissue expression databases, we found that ALDH3A2 gene expression was higher than that in their respective normal tissues in diffuse large B cell lymphoma, pleomorphic glioblastoma, AML, low-grade glioma, and thyroid cancer, while in adrenocortical carcinoma, renal clear cell carcinoma, cutaneous melanoma, and testicular germ cell tumors, ALDH3A2 expression was lower than that in their respective normal tissues (Figure 1A). Analysis of RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) results for 88 leukemia cell lines in the Human Protein Atlas website (HPA) database showed that the top 25% of cell lines with the highest ALDH3A2 expression included fifteen AML cell lines, 4 chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) cell lines, 2 pediatric acute B-lymphoblastic leukemia cell lines, and one T-lymphoblastic leukemia cell line. Leukemia cell lines of myeloid origin accounted for 86.36%, whereas AML cell lines accounted for 78.95%. This indicated that the expression level of ALDH3A2 in AML cells was higher than that in other types of leukemia cells (Figure 1B). To further validate the results obtained from the bioinformatics analysis, we examined the expression of ALDH3A2 in AML cell lines and clinical patient samples. ALDH3A2 protein content was higher in doxorubicin-resistant AML cells (HL-60ADM and K562ADM) than in the parental strains (Figure 1D). We quantified ALDH3A2 protein expression in bone marrow samples from newly diagnosed patients with AML and from those in sustained CR following therapy. ALDH3A2 protein in bone marrow was higher in patients with newly diagnosed AML (n = 3) than in those who had achieved sustained CR after therapy (n = 2). Notably, patients who did not achieve CR after induction chemotherapy (n = 5) exhibited a preference for the FALDH-V subtype, which has a larger molecular weight than that in patients who achieved CR after induction chemotherapy (n = 5).

We collected bone marrow single-nucleus RNA specimens from AML patients who received 3 + 7 standard induction therapy between 2020 and 2021 at initial diagnosis and categorized them based on treatment response into three groups: relapsed/refractory (R/R) group (n = 10), first-course non-remission (C1NR) group (n = 8), and sustained CR group (n = 9) within 2 years. ALDH3A2 expression in bone marrow single-nucleus cells from patients with sustained CR within 2 years was significantly lower than that in the C1NR and R/R groups (Figure 1E). We compared ALDH3A2 mRNA expression in three patients (n = 3) at initial diagnosis and relapse and found increased ALDH3A2 expression at relapse (Figure 1F).

Moreover, we conducted RT-qPCR analysis in bone marrow mononuclear cells to measure the expression of ALDH3A2 mRNA in 66 patients newly diagnosed with AML. As shown in Figure 1G and Table S1, the results revealed the association between ALDH3A2 expression and mutations in CEBPα, FLT3-ITD, and RUNX1. Specifically, 83.33% of patients with RUNX1 mutation exhibited high ALDH3A2 expression, while 66.67% of those with CEBPα mutation showed a similar pattern. In contrast, 84.21% of the patients with the FLT3-ITD mutation had low ALDH3A2 expression. Furthermore, 25% and 20.81% of patients with high ALDH3A2 expression had CEBPα and RUNX1 mutations, respectively, which was higher than that in the low expression group (7.14% and 2.38%, respectively). Conversely, 38.09% of patients with low ALDH3A2 expression had the FLT3-ITD mutation, which was higher than the frequency of this mutation in the high expression group (12.5%).

ALDH3A2 reduced doxorubicin-induced ferroptosis and cytotoxicity through fatty acid synthesis

In our study, treatment with the ferroptosis inhibitor ferrostatin-1 (5 μM) decreased doxorubicin-induced cytotoxicity in HL-60/ADM and U937 cells (Figure S1A). Lipid peroxidation of AML cells increased with increasing doses of doxorubicin (Figure S1B). After treatment with equivalent concentrations of doxorubicin for 48 h, doxorubicin-resistant AML cells exhibited lower lipid peroxidation levels than the parental cells. To achieve comparable lipid peroxidation levels in both parental and resistant AML cells, an increasing concentration of the drug was specifically tailored toward the resistant strains (Figure S1C). This indicates that ferroptosis is frequently observed during treatment with anthracyclines in AML and that resistance to ferroptosis is one of the reasons for drug resistance.

We knocked down ALDH3A2 expression in HL-60/ADM and K562/ADM cells by transducing them with small interfering RNA (siRNA) (Figure 2A). Cells in the control and knockdown groups were treated with equivalent concentrations for 48 h. Assessment of lipid peroxidation levels revealed that ALDH3A2 knockdown led to higher levels of lipid peroxidation (Figure 2B). To further investigate the association between the ALDH3A2-V subtype and resistance to anthracyclines, we generated AML cell lines over-expressing ALDH3A2-V using adenoviral transduction. Immunofluorescence imaging revealed that the red fluorescence marking ALDH3A2 overlapped with the green fluorescence marking the peroxisomal marker protein PMP70 but not with the endoplasmic reticulum marker protein protein disulfide isomerase (PDI), indicating the localization of overexpressed ALDH3A2-V in peroxisomes (Figures 2C and 2D). Subsequent treatment with various concentrations of doxorubicin for 48 h showed that ALDH3A2-V-overexpressing cells exhibited lower levels of lipid peroxidation (Figure 2E) and higher cell viability compared with the control group (Figure 2F). The proliferative activity curve revealed that the IC50 of the ALDH3A2-V overexpressing group was higher than that of the control group (Figure 2G). As demonstrated in the supplementary materials, following treatment with doxorubicin, AML cells exhibited a decrease in the levels of various saturated fatty acids and a concomitant increase in the levels of multiple PUFAs compared to the control group (Figure S2A). We further assessed the fatty acid content in U937 cells overexpressing ALDH3A2-V and in the control cells following doxorubicin treatment using gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS). Orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) revealed significant differences in the levels of heptadecanoic acid (C17:0), oleic acid (C18:1), and linoleic acid (C18:2), which were crucial for distinguishing between the two groups of cells. Following doxorubicin treatment, the ALDH3A2-V overexpressing group exhibited higher levels of these three fatty acids than the control group (Figures 3C–3E). Since fatty acid metabolism is one of the four major pathways regulating ferroptosis, we further investigated whether the role of ALDH3A2 in reducing doxorubicin-induced lipid peroxidation and cytotoxicity in AML was achieved by increasing the level of the three aforementioned fatty acids. HL-60 and HL-60/ADM cells were separately pretreated with heptadecanoic acid (C17:0), oleic acid (C18:1), and linoleic acid (C18:2) for 48 h to increase the intracellular levels of these fatty acids. Subsequently, the cells were treated with doxorubicin, and their proliferative activity and lipid peroxidation levels were assessed after 48 h. The results indicated that pretreatment with heptadecanoic acid (C17:0) and oleic acid (C18:1) enhanced the tolerance of AML cells to doxorubicin and reduced doxorubicin-induced lipid peroxidation, whereas pretreatment with linoleic acid (C18:2) had the opposite effect. Knockdown of ALDH3A2 expression enhanced cellular sensitivity to doxorubicin. Pretreatment of ALDH3A2-knockdown AML cells with fatty acids revealed that C17:0 and C18:1 partially restored drug resistance, whereas C18:2 further reduced resistance (Figures 3F and 3G).

Previous studies have indicated that changes in the fluidity of the cell membrane lipid bilayer are related to the content of saturated fatty acyl chains, phospholipids, and/or cholesterol.17 The incorporation of stearic acid into the membrane enhanced their fluidity. Membrane fluidity can influence the transport of drugs inside and outside cells, as well as the uptake of nutrients, such as glucose, and is involved in tumor cell resistance.18,19 Laurdan is a fluorescent probe that can penetrate the membrane and is sensitive to the spectral properties of the phospholipid phase to which it binds; therefore, it is used to distinguish different lipid phases within the cell membrane and monitor membrane fluidity.20 U937 cells were exposed to heptadecanoic, oleic, or linoleic acid, after which membrane fluidity and the uptake of Cy5 fluorescent-labeled doxorubicin were assessed. Heptadecanoic acid decreased membrane fluidity, whereas oleic and linoleic acids increased it (Figure 4A). Pretreatment with heptadecanoic acid enhanced intracellular doxorubicin accumulation, whereas linoleic acid had the opposite effect (Figure 4B). We stained the cell membranes of ALDH3A2-overexpressing and control cells and found that ALDH3A2high group cells exhibited increased cell membrane fluidity (Figure 4C). Moreover, the overexpressing cells showed lower uptake of doxorubicin after treatment for 4 h (Figure 4D). We examined the relative expression fold of four genes encoding anthracycline-transporting proteins—ABCB1, ABCC1, ABCC2, and ABCG2—in ALDH3A2-overexpressing HL-60 cells and their control counterparts. There are no significant differences in ABCB1 and ABCC2 expression; however, ABCC1 and ABCG2 expression were reduced in the ALDH3A2-overexpression group (Figure S2E). These results indicate that altering the fatty acid content alone can also change the cell membrane fluidity and drug uptake rate. Following the treatment, we assessed the leukemia burden in mice using in vivo imaging, measured the plasma 4-HNE levels using ELISA, and observed their survival periods (Figure 5A). Clinical specimens were collected to validate this finding. Patients were divided into two groups (n = 5) based on ALDH3A2 mRNA expression levels in bone marrow mononuclear cells. Peripheral blood was collected before and after their first idarubicin treatment, and plasma 4-HNE levels were measured. Among the low ALDH3A2 expression groups, patients showed a statistically significant increase in plasma 4-HNE levels after treatment, whereas, in the high ALDH3A2 expression group, patients did not show such statistically significant changes (Figure 5F).

Considering that ALDH3A2 is a potential target for overcoming chemotherapy resistance in AML, we designed and synthesized a candidate inhibitor named X24003, based on the protein structure of ALDH3A2. Molecular docking revealed a strong affinity of X24003 to ALDH3A2, with a calculated binding energy of −7.479 kcal/mol (Figure S3A). We also performed molecular dynamics simulations to assess the binding affinity of X24003 for ALDH3A2. Root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) indicated that X24003 binding effectively suppressed backbone flexibility fluctuations, enhancing overall structural rigidity and compactness (Figure S3B). RMSF, Radius of Gyration (Rg), and Solvent Accessible Surface Area (SASA) parameters collectively demonstrated that the ALDH3A2-X24003 complex maintained stable association (Figures S3C–S3E). Molecular Mechanics/Generalized Born Surface Area (MM/GBSA) calculations revealed that protein-ligand binding was thermodynamically favorable (ΔTOTAL = −34.98 kcal/mol), predominantly driven by van der Waals forces, thereby preserving an energetically favorable state for the complex.

We evaluated the effects of X24003 in both in vivo and in vitro experiments. We next evaluated the efficacy and safety of X24003 in xenograft models. NSG mice were engrafted intravenously with either HL-60 cells (n = 24) or their doxorubicin-resistant derivative HL-60ADM (n = 24); upon confirmed engraftment, animals were randomized to receive (1) PBS vehicle therapy, (2) doxorubicin therapy (1.5 mg/kg/day from day 2 to day 4), (3) X24003 therapy (50 mg/kg from day 1 to day 5), or (4) combination therapy. Three days after treatment completion, peripheral blood was collected for the assessment of hepatic and renal function, and leukemia burden was monitored by in vivo bioluminescence imaging. Consistent with the imaging data, X24003 monotherapy failed to reduce leukemic burden. Doxorubicin alone effectively attenuated disease in HL-60-engrafted mice but showed limited efficacy in HL-60ADM-engrafted animals. In contrast, the combination of X24003 and doxorubicin significantly diminished leukemia load in HL-60ADM-engrafted mice, whereas no additive effect was observed in HL-60-engrafted mice. Bioluminescence images and corresponding quantitative analyses are provided in Figure S3D.

We also evaluated the in vivo safety profile of X24003 at the aforementioned dosing concentration. As shown in Figure S3A, serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and blood urea nitrogen (UREA) levels did not differ significantly among the four treatment groups (n = 6). Serum creatinine (CREA-S) increased under both doxorubicin monotherapy and the doxorubicin-plus-X24003 combination; however, levels in the combination group did not exceed those observed with doxorubicin alone. These data indicate that X24003, administered either as monotherapy or together with doxorubicin, does not elicit hepatic or renal toxicity. Because NSG mice are immunodeficient and thus unsuitable for evaluating potential myelotoxicity, we further assessed X24003 in immunocompetent C57BL/6J mice (n = 5). C57BL/6J mice received 50 mg/kg daily for 5 consecutive days; complete blood counts were obtained before and after treatment. Although the differences in white cell count, hemoglobin, and platelet count were not statistically significant, a downward trend in hemoglobin was noted (Figure S3B).

HDAC2 binds to the promoter of the ALDH3A2 gene, and the HDAC inhibitor reduced ALDH3A2 expression

To further explore the factors regulating ALDH3A2 expression, we predicted the transcription factors regulating ALDH3A2 expression using the online database Cistrome DB. We found that HDAC2 was a predicted molecule. Histones are the spools around which eukaryotic cell DNA is wound, forming chromatin together with DNA. Acetylation modification of histones alters their charge, repelling the negatively charged DNA and leading to chromatin opening, thereby activating the transcription of target genes. HDACs not only induce deacetylation of histones to suppress chromatin transcription but also participate in post-translational modifications of proteins outside of histones, thereby influencing the activity of protein molecules.21 CUT&RUN assays demonstrated that HDAC2 binds to the ALDH3A2 promoter, with enrichment significantly above IgG control (p < 0.01). Treatment with chidamide, which specifically inhibits class I HDAC (including HDAC1, HDAC2, HDAC3, and HDAC8),22 substantially reduced HDAC2 occupancy at the ALDH3A2 promoter (p < 0.05), suggesting that pharmacological inhibition diminishes HDAC2 association with the ALDH3A2 promoter (Figure 7A). It should be noted that although the CUT&RUN assay can demonstrate the binding between proteins and genes, it cannot determine the specific binding form or the specific base sequence. We treated AML cells with doxorubicin, chidamide alone, or their combination for 48 h and found that the expression of ALDH3A2 decreased after chidamide treatment; however, there was no significant difference in the expression of ALDH3A2 in the doxorubicin-treated group. We further verified these results using bone marrow mononuclear cells from newly diagnosed AML patients and relapsed patients with AML, and the results were similar to those obtained from the cell line experiments (Figure 7B).

We evaluated ferroptosis and cell viability in AML cells after treatment with doxorubicin and chidamide alone or in combination. The level of lipid peroxidation increased in the doxorubicin-treated group, no significant difference was observed in the chidamide-treated group, and the combination group had the highest level among all the groups (Figure 7C). This suggested that chidamide was not capable of inducing lipid peroxidation in AML cells; however, it augmented doxorubicin-induced lipid peroxidation. Additionally, cell viability in the combination group was significantly lower than that in the single-drug treatment group. The combination indices (combinatorial index [CI] value) of the two drugs were calculated in all cell lines, indicating that chidamide and doxorubicin have a synergistic anti-AML effect (Figure 7D).

Discussion

Our study found that ALDH3A2, particularly the V subtype, could serve as a biomarker for chemotherapy-resistant AML. Through fatty acid synthesis, ALDH3A2 increased the fluidity of AML cell membranes, reduced drug uptake, and mitigated anthracycline-induced ferroptosis. We have synthetically developed an ALDH3A2 candidate inhibitor and, in parallel, uncovered that histone deacetylase inhibitors (HDACIs) downregulates ALDH3A2 expression, thereby providing two mechanistically distinct potential strategies targeting ALDH3A2 to enhance AML sensitivity to doxorubicin. By inhibiting the activity of ALDH3A2, X24003 increased the sensitivity to doxorubicin in drug-resistant AML cells. The HDAC inhibitor chidamide downregulated the ALDH3A2 expression and had a synergistic anti-AML effect with doxorubicin. These findings suggest that targeting ALDH3A2 may be a promising strategy to overcome chemoresistance in AML.

Lipid and fatty acid metabolism are closely related to the survival and drug resistance of tumor cells. AML exhibits a heightened requirement for the synthesis of very-long-chain fatty acids compared to normal bone marrow hematopoietic cells, which is a characteristic feature of AML lipid metabolism.23 Under identical doses of doxorubicin, the levels of heptadecanoic acid (C17:0), oleic acid (C18:1), and linoleic acid (C18:2) increased in AML cells with high ALDH3A2 expression. These three fatty acids are categorized as saturated acid, monounsaturated acid, and PUFA, respectively. Alterations in fatty acid metabolism and associated changes in membrane properties may represent a contributing mechanism by which certain solid and hematologic malignancies, including AML, modulate sensitivity to anthracycline-based chemotherapy.24 Studies have shown that the enrichment of PUFA on membrane phospholipids increases the sensitivity of AML cells to adriamycin, and the saturation level of membrane fatty acids is related to the uptake and cytotoxicity of adriamycin.25 Increased linoleic acid content is related to sensitivity to doxorubicin.26 Monounsaturated fatty acids displace PUFAs from the plasma membrane phospholipids through the action of acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 3 (ACSL3), thereby reducing ferroptosis.18 Our experiments showed that pretreatment with heptadecanoic acid (C17:0) and oleic acid (C18:1) decreased the sensitivity of AML cells to doxorubicin, whereas pretreatment with linoleic acid (C18:2) increased their sensitivity. However, the role of ALDH3A2 in ferroptosis is inhibitory, likely because of the low content of linoleic acid.

The content of saturated fatty acyl chains in membrane lipids not only affects the susceptibility to ferroptosis but is also associated with alterations in the fluidity of the cell membrane lipid bilayer. Research showed that the incorporation of heptadecanoic acid into the plasma membrane increased the fluidity of the plasma membrane, enhanced its rigidity, and elevated the mechanical threshold of the cation channel Piezo1.27,28 It has been reported that the fluidity of the plasma membrane influences the uptake of drugs by regulating the expression and activity of adenosine triphosphate-binding cassette superfamily transporters in the cell membrane, which are associated with drug resistance.18 However, the observed reduction in ABCG2 expression alongside decreased drug uptake in ALDH3A2-overexpressing AML cells appears paradoxical, as ABCG2 typically functions as an efflux pump. We speculate that ALDH3A2-mediated membrane remodeling may predominate over transporter-mediated effects in reducing drug uptake or that reduced ABCG2 reflects compensatory feedback rather than primary resistance mechanism. It should be noted that our interpretation of the drug uptake data should be tempered, as there may be alternative explanations for the observed reduction in drug uptake beyond ALDH3A2-mediated membrane remodeling such as endocytosis or vesicular trafficking. Further studies are warranted to dissect these complex interactions.

Analysis of individual nuclear cell data from healthy peripheral blood in the Monaco database showed higher ALDH3A2 expression in myeloid cells than in lymphoid cells, particularly monocytes. ALDH3A2 expression was upregulated during the differentiation of granulocyte-monocyte progenitor cells into monocytes.29 Upon analyzing the relationship between ALDH3A2 expression and molecular mutations in patients with AML in our cohort, we found that those with CEBPα or RUNX1 mutations tended to exhibit higher ALDH3A2 expression. CEBPα and RUNX1 are transcription factors with important roles in hematopoiesis and adipogenesis.30,31,32 Further research is needed to reveal how these mutations increase ALDH3A2 expression and to determine whether therapies that inhibit ALDH3A2 activity or expression may benefit patients with CEBPα or RUNX1 mutations.

Our study suggests that increased expression of ALDH3A2 in AML may be a promising biomarker of chemotherapy resistance, which may help clinicians to more accurately discern patients who are unlikely to benefit from chemotherapy and offer a more efficacious treatment. Clinical trial results showed that the combination of HDACIs with chemotherapy had good safety and efficacy, while the efficacy of single-agent HDACIs in AML was limited.33,34 Chidamide with anthracycline was used to treat 27 R/R patients with AML, of which 13 achieved CR, and one achieved partial remission.35 This preliminary evidence that chidamide enhances the chemosensitivity of patients with AML to anthracycline is consistent with our experimental results. Our study also provides a theoretical basis for the application of the HDACI in the treatment of AML. X24003, the ALDH3A2 candidate inhibitor that we designed and synthesized, exhibited potential promising effects in enhancing the sensitivity to doxorubicin in drug-resistant AML. We speculate that X24003 may hold potential as a targeted therapy for AML patients with high ALDH3A2 expression. However, further detailed in vivo experimental validation is required to fully elucidate its therapeutic potential.

Limitations of the study

We must acknowledge that our study has several limitations. First, the causal relationship between ALDH3A2-mediated alterations in membrane fluidity and reduced drug uptake could not be definitively established. Although our experiments demonstrated that ALDH3A2 and fatty acids mediate changes in AML cell membrane fluidity and drug uptake rates, we were unable to identify an optimal approach to directly perturb membrane fluidity and investigate its specific impact on drug uptake. Additionally, although we utilized a considerable number of patient specimens to validate our conclusions, we must acknowledge that the cohort size remains modest; further expansion of the cohort would enhance the reliability of our findings. As for the validation of X24003 efficacy and safety, only preliminary evidence was provided in this study. We intend to conduct further in-depth investigation and optimization, including additional experiments in diverse models. Further experiments such as surface plasmon resonance (SPR), to characterize its specificity and target engagement, would be performed to confirm its potential as a targeted agent.

For the AML-CDX models, 6-week-old female NOD/ShiLtJGpt-Prkdcˆem26/IL2rgˆem26/Gpt (NCG) immunodeficient mice were utilized. These mice were maintained in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) facility at the Laboratory Animal Center of Nanfang Hospital, Southern Medical University. All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Nanfang Hospital, Southern Medical University (approval number: IACUC-LAC-20241016-007), and were performed in compliance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Of note, only female mice were included in this study; therefore, the potential influence of sex differences on the experimental outcomes was not evaluated, which represents a limitation of this study.

For human sample studies, specimens were obtained from a total of 26 newly diagnosed AML patients in the Department of Hematology, Nanfang Hospital, Southern Medical University. All samples were residual diagnostic specimens collected after routine clinical procedures. Participants were over 18 years of age and of Chinese Han ethnicity. Samples were allocated to different groups according to the experimental design(for example: ALDH3A2 expression level, therapeutic response to induction chemotherapy). This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Nanfang Hospital, Southern Medical University (approval number: NFEC-2025-505), and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients or their legal guardians for the use of residual specimens for research purposes. Due to the limited sample size and retrospective nature of sample collection, the potential influence of sex and gender on clinical and experimental results was not systematically analyzed, which should be acknowledged as a study limitation.

All cell lines employed in this study were authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling and confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination. Cells were cultured under standard and consistent laboratory conditions throughout the experiments.

Method details

Bioinformatics analysis

The Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis website (http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/) was used to assess ALDH3A2 expression in various diseases. ALDH3A2 expression in human leukemia cell lines and peripheral blood mononuclear cells from healthy individuals was analyzed using data from the Human Protein Atlas website (https://www.proteinatlas.org/). Clinical data patients related to AML and mRNA expression matrices were downloaded from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), and X-tile software was used to obtain the optimal cut-off value.

Cell culture

AML cell lines HL-60, K562, U937, THP-1, and KG-1α were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection, while doxorubicin-resistant cell lines HL-60ADM and K562ADM were constructed by gradually increasing the concentration of Doxorubicin and inducing repeatedly over one year in HL-60 and K562. The cells were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute-1640 medium at a density of 1x105–1x106 cells/mL and maintained in a cell culture incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2.

Extraction of primary cells from patients with AML

Bone marrow (BM) samples were collected from AML patients and healthy donors after obtaining informed consent following approval by the Medical Ethics Committee of Nanfang Hospital, in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Blood samples aspirated from the bone marrow were gently layered onto Lymphoprep (TBDsciences, Tianjin, China) in a centrifuge tube and centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 20 min. Mononuclear cells at the interface between the separation medium and the plasma were collected using a pipette.

RNA extraction from cells and reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

The cells were lysed using TRIZOL, followed by RNA extraction using chloroform and isopropanol, and then reconstituted in nuclease-free water. The RNA concentration was determined using a UV spectrophotometer (Nanodrop), and reverse transcription was performed using HiScript® III All-in-one RT SuperMix Perfect for qPCR kit. The reaction mixture was prepared with ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix, primers, cDNA, and nuclease-free water according to the Taq Pro Universal qPCR Master Mix manual and then ran on a QuantStudio5. After normalization to a reference gene, Ct values were converted to fold-change relative to the control group using the 2ˆ(–ΔΔCt) method. mRNA Primer for ALDH3A2 GGCAAAGCTTCTCCCTCAGGTATT, CAACCGCAGTGTTTCCCGTATAG.

Protein extraction and western blotting

The protein extraction involved cell lysis with radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer, protease and phosphatase inhibitors, and phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride, followed by sonication and centrifugation. The protein concentration was determined using the bicinchoninic acid method. The protein samples were heated in a water bath at 100°C after adding the loading buffer. The proteins were then separated on a 10% polyacrylamide gel using sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane, blocked with tris-buffered saline with tween 20 (TBST) solution and skim milk, and incubated with specific antibodies overnight at 4°C. Antibodies against GAPDH (#2118, CST) and ALDH3A2 (ab184171, Abcam) were used as loading controls. After washing with TBST, the membrane was incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies, rewashed, and subjected to chemiluminescence detection and imaging. The resulting images were analyzed for grayscale values and statistically evaluated using the ImageJ software.

Detection of lipid peroxidation

The BODIPY 581/591 C11 solution was added to the cell suspension under subdued lighting conditions, achieving a final concentration of 8 μM, gently agitated to ensure thorough mixing before the mixture was incubated at 37°C in the dark for 30 min. Following incubation, cells were centrifuged, washed twice with phosphate buffer saline (PBS), and resuspended for fluorescence analysis using a flow cytometer.

Cell viability assay

Under subdued lighting conditions, 10 μL of cell counting kit-8 (CCK8) solution was added to both control and experimental wells of a 96-well plate. After gentle agitation, the plates were incubated at 37°C in the dark for 1–2 h. Subsequently, the plate was transferred to a microplate reader, and the absorbance at 450 nm was measured to determine cell proliferation.

Detection of 4-HNE using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit

Samples were prepared according to the manufacturer’s instructions, incubated in the wells for 30 min, and then subjected to a series of washing and reagent additions. Finally, the absorbance of each well was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader to quantify 4-HNE levels.

Construction of the ALDH3A2 low-expressing cell line

Cells in the logarithmic growth phase were collected and resuspended in serum-free, antibiotic-free Iscove’s Modified Dulbecco’s Medium to a density of 1 × 107 cells/mL. The knockdown and control groups were then transfected with 20 μg/mL of small interfering RNA (siRNA) targeting the ALDH3A2 gene and negative control siRNA, respectively, using a BTX ECM830 electroporator at 560 V for 1 min. Following electroporation, the cells were transferred to a complete culture medium for further incubation. siRNA sequences used were GGUUACUGCUAAACCAGUUTT, AACUGGUUUAGCAGUAACCTT.

Construction of ALDH3A2 overexpressing cell lines

Cells were first resuspended in fresh complete culture medium at a density of 2 × 105 cells/mL and seeded into a 24-well plate. AML cells were transduced with adenoviral vectors encoding ALDH3A2 or empty-vector controls at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 100, followed by gentle agitation to ensure uniform distribution. The plate was then centrifuged at 500 g for 90 min and placed in a cell culture incubator for 24 h. The medium was then replaced with a fresh, complete culture medium containing 20% fetal bovine syndrome.

Intracellular fatty acid extraction

Cells were treated with 0.04% butylated hydroxytoluene methanol solution, chloroform, and 0.1 M hydrochloric acid. The organic phase was collected, dried using a nitrogen evaporator, and further processed for gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) analysis by adding an internal standard (benzoic acid benzyl ester in n-hexane). This method allows the assessment of fatty acid composition within cells.

Detection of fatty acids

The GC-MS analysis was performed using an Agilent 19091N-133 HP-INNOWax column (30 m x 250 μm x 0.25 μm). The temperature conditions were set as follows: initial temperature of 40°C for 2 min, ramped at a rate of 10°C/min to 60°C, then ramped at a rate of 25°C/min to 200°C, followed by a ramp at a rate of 3°C/min to 230°C and held for 15.4 min. The injection mode was set at a split ratio of 20:1 and a flow rate of 1 mL/min. The injection port temperature was set at 250°C, while the auxiliary heating for the MSD transfer line was set at 250°C. For mass spectrometry, the ionization source was EI with a temperature of 230°C. The MS1 scan range was set at 30–600 with a scan time of 300 ms.

CUT & RUN analysis of RNA-protein binding

Cells were incubated with ConA Beads Pro magnetic beads, followed by overnight incubation with a histone deacetylase-2 (HDAC2) antibody (12922-3-AP, Proteintech). After magnetic separation, a secondary antibody was added and incubated at 26°C for 1 h. Incubation with pA/G-Tnp Pro was performed 1 h after washing. Following another round of washing, the samples were incubated at 37°C for 1 h with TTBL solution for fragmentation. Subsequently, 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate solution and an appropriate amount of DNA Spike-in were added to the fragmented samples and incubated at 55°C for 10 min. The supernatant was collected after magnetic separation, and DNA was collected and extracted using DNA Extract Beads Pro. The extracted samples were quantitatively analyzed using qPCR after releasing the DNA with a stop buffer.

Immunofluorescence

Cells were fixed on slides with 4% paraformaldehyde, and permeabilized with 0.5% TritonX-100 for 20 min at room temperature. After washing with PBS, the cells were blocked with 20% goat serum for 1 h. Primary antibodies (ALDH3A2 (ab184171 Abcam), PMP70(A4172 ABclonal), and protein disulfide isomerase (PDI) (#3501 CST)) were fluorescently labeled using the FlexAble Antibody labeling kit (from Proteintech) and incubated overnight at 4°C. After three washes with PBS, the cells were incubated with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole staining solution in the dark for 5 min, followed by another wash and mounting. Observation and imaging were performed using a fluorescence confocal microscope.

Laurdan staining

Laurdan dye was added to the cell culture medium to achieve a final concentration of 10 nM, and cells were incubated in the dark at 37°C for 30 min. After incubation, cells were collected by centrifugation, fixed on slides using a glass coverslip, observed, and imaged using a fluorescence confocal microscope.

AML mouse xenograft model

GFP/Luciferase-labeled HL-60 cells (HL-60-GFP/Luc, 1.2 × 106) stably expressing either oeCtrl or oeALDH3A2 were transplanted into 6-week-old female NOD/ShiLtJGpt-Prkdcem26/IL2rgem26/Gpt (NCG) mice (GemPharmatech, Guangdong, China) via tail vein injection. After confirming engraftment using bioluminescence imaging, the mice were randomly allocated to different groups. Peripheral blood samples from the mice were collected via the venous plexus of the inner canthus, and the plasma obtained after centrifugation was used to detect 4-HNE.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Experiments were performed in triplicate, and data were expressed as means ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism v8.0 (Graph Pad Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Combinatorial index (CI) values were calculated via the Chou-Talalay method using Compusyn v1.0 software (ComboSyn, Inc., Paramus, NJ, USA), with CI < 1 indicating synergistic, =1 indicating additive, and >1 indicating antagonistic effects. The averaged CI obtained at the effective doses of 50%, 75%, 90%, and 95% were used. Statistical significance was determined using unpaired or paired two-tailed Student’s t-tests for the comparison of two groups and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for multiple-group comparisons. Mouse survival was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
