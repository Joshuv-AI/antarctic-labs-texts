Multimodal graphene oxide nanoplatform integrating proteasome inhibition and phototherapy for synergistic oral cancer treatment
Authors: Shishira P S, Zinataman Sarkar, Varshini Are, Bhavesha Chanchlani, Ankita Ghosh, Swati Biswas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMultimodal therapeutic approaches hold the potential to revolutionize cancer treatment. Herein, we developed a chemo-photothermal-photodynamic therapeutic platform by loading a proteome inhibitor, BTZ, and photodynamic agent, IR820, into polydopamine-coated graphene oxide to form GO@PDA (BTZ, IR820) NPs. The developed NPs exhibit smaller particle size, homogeneous morphology, and laser-mediated drug release. The GO@PDA (BTZ, IR820) NPs exhibited an optimal photothermal conversion efficiency of 54.18%. GO@PDA (BTZ, IR820) NPs enhanced the uptake in the human hypopharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma (FaDU) spheroids and exhibited enhanced cytotoxicity (IC50: 18.53 ng/ml in the presence of laser). In vitro study in FaDU spheroid models also demonstrated mitochondrial dysfunction, reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, and cell cycle arrest, leading to cancer cell death. The developed NPs also exhibited the BTZ-mediated expression of endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress-related proteins, including CHOP, PERK, and BiP, leading to ER stress-mediated cell death in FaDU multicellular spheroids. Introduction

Cancer is one of the major global health issues, and oral cancer ranks as the 11th most malignant type worldwide1. The most common form of oral cancer is head and neck cancer, specifically squamous cell carcinoma, which accounts for 90% of oral cancer cases2. Prognostic factors influencing the disease include lifestyle choices, demographic variables, treatment methods, and tumor characteristics3,4. Unfortunately, about two-thirds of patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage of the disease5. Even with a diagnosis, the survival rate ranges from 50% to 60%. In contrast, the survival rate increases to around 80% when the disease is localized6. The treatment modalities for oral cancer can either be used alone or in combination, such as surgery, chemotherapy, immunotherapy, radiation therapy, phototherapy, etc.7. Patients often experience various side effects from conventional treatments, such as facial deformities that impact their well-being, delayed tissue healing, damage to the salivary glands, and off-target toxic effects. Additionally, prolonged exposure to chemotherapeutic agents can lead to multidrug resistance8.

The ubiquitin-proteasome system plays a crucial role in cell function by removing malfunctioning proteins and eliminating those that are harmful, thereby preventing cell damage9. It exhibits anti-tumor effect by causing endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress through the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway10,11. Inhibiting the activity of the cellular proteasome may serve as a promising therapeutic strategy for cancer treatment. Bortezomib (BTZ) is a proteasome inhibitor12, FDA-approved for multiple myeloma in 2003, and later approved for mantle cell lymphoma in 2006. BTZ is a very potent drug; however, increasing its concentrations can lead to higher toxicity. The boronic groups in BTZ can interact with plasma proteins, resulting in poor stability13,14. While BTZ is approved for treating multiple myeloma, researchers are investigating its effects on solid tumors due to the development of drug resistance from prolonged use of other chemotherapy agents15,16. A new approach to delivering BTZ is needed to reduce toxicity and improve systemic stability.

Phototherapy is an emerging treatment modality for cancer that utilizes light energy at various wavelengths17. Photothermal therapy (PTT) and photodynamic therapy (PDT) are the two most prevalent phototherapies in cancer treatment. PDT generally utilizes light, photosensitizers, and oxygen to undergo a photodynamic reaction, generating cytotoxic reactive oxygen species (ROS)18. PTT utilizes near-infrared (NIR) light to convert light energy into heat energy, achieving local hyperthermia19. IR820 is a commercially available NIR dye that serves as a photothermal agent, commonly used in combination with chemotherapeutic agents to enhance the efficacy of the treatment modality20. IR820 also acts as a theranostic agent as it can induce light-to-heat transition and ROS production upon light irradiation in the NIR region21. While the applications are still limited due to insolubility and reduced ROS generation caused by agglomeration. Undesirable side effects are caused by the aggregation of cyanine present in IR82022. Integrating therapeutic agents with nanoparticles or a nanoplatform can significantly reduce systemic toxicity and off-target effects. Additionally, nanoplatforms can improve the buildup of therapeutic agents at the tumor site, resulting in increased therapeutic effectiveness23.

Graphene oxide (GO) is a widely studied photothermal agent for drug delivery of anti-cancer agents because of its biocompatibility and larger surface area for drug loading through π − π stacking interactions, electrostatic attraction, hydrogen bonding, van der Waals forces, and other surface modifications24. Moreover, GO is a nanomaterial that can induce hyperthermia, increasing its temperature on NIR laser irradiation, ultimately leading to cancer cell death through apoptosis and immunogenic cell death (ICD)25,26. The strong negative charge on the surface of GO limits its efficiency as a drug delivery agent24,27. Therefore, some surface modifications can overcome its limitations, improving drug delivery efficiency. Polydopamine (PDA) is a versatile, biocompatible photothermal agent (PTA) with minimal cytotoxicity28. Due to its chemically active surface, PDA can be easily modified. It offers excellent drug loading efficiency, biodegradability, and controlled drug release capabilities29.

In the present study, GO-coated PDA nanoparticles (NPs) loaded with BTZ and IR820 to form GO@PDA (BTZ, IR820) NPs for the chemo-photothermal-photodynamic therapy of oral cancer have been developed. The GO was prepared by Hummers’ modification method and further coated with PDA (GO@PDA NPs) to enhance its drug loading and drug-delivering efficiency. Further, BTZ was loaded into the GO@PDA NPs. The specific interaction between the boronic group of BTZ and the hydroxyl group of the PDA enhances the drug loading efficiency of the nanoplatform. IR820 was loaded into the GO@PDA (BTZ) NPs, as it is a photodynamic agent and a theranostic agent to form GO@PDA (BTZ, IR820) NPs. The integration of IR820 transforms the photothermal carrier GO and PDA into a multifunctional theranostic system comprising a targeted, diagnostic, and combined PTT-PDT therapeutic platform. Overall, the developed nanoplatform, GO@PDA (BTZ, IR820), combines chemo-photothermal-photodynamic therapy to treat oral cancer. The developed NPs were physicochemically characterized, and their in vitro efficacy was evaluated in an oral cancer spheroid model.

Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization of the NPs

The GO@PDA (BTZ, IR820) NPs for chemo-photothermal-photodynamic therapy for oral cancer were developed in multiple steps, as shown in Fig. 1a. Initially, the GO was synthesized from Graphite using Hummer’s method, and to obtain GO in nano form, the GO was ultrasonicated for 3 h. Further, the GO was coated with PDA to form GO@PDA for enhanced photothermal effect. Proteasome inhibitor BTZ was loaded by incubating with GO@PDA to form GO@PDA(BTZ) NPs. Due to the formation of boronic ester bonds between the boron groups of BTZ and the carboxylic group of the PDA, the loading of BTZ was enhanced and further loaded with IR820 by incubating it with GO@PDA (BTZ) NPs to form GO@PDA (BTZ, IR820) NPs for enhanced synergistic chemo and photothermal effect.

Particle size and zeta potential

The hydrodynamic diameters of the GO@PDA NPs, GO@PDA (BTZ) NPs, and GO@PDA (BTZ, IR820) NPs were 158 ± 2.5 nm, 114 ± 1.24 nm, and 172 ± 1.43 nm, when measured using a Zeta-sizer instrument by the DLS method (Fig. 2f). The zeta potential of GO, GO@PDA NPs, GO@PDA (BTZ) NPs, and GO@PDA (BTZ, IR820) NPs were found to be −27.9 ± 1.4 mV, −19.5 ± 2.3 mV, −14.7 ± 0.91 mV, and 22.2 ± 0.91 mV, respectively, indicating good colloidal stability of the developed NPs (Fig. 2f). Figure S1 represents the particle size and zeta potential of different ratios of GO@PDA (BTZ) and GO@PDA (BTZ, IR820) NPs. Table S1 and Table S2 provide the particle size and zeta potential of different ratios of GO@PDA (BTZ) and GO@PDA (BTZ, IR820) NPs. Furthermore, to characterize the morphology of the developed formulations, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was performed (Fig. 2). GO exhibited sheet-like morphology with multiple layers, ranging from 0.7 to 2 nm in thickness, and a 2D lamellar structure. After coating with PDA, a more granular and compact morphology was observed, suggesting successful functionalization and the formation of GO@PDA. Upon loading with BTZ, a sheet-like morphology formed spherical NPs with a size of 57 ± 2.34 nm, exhibiting a uniform distribution of the GO@PDA (BTZ) formulation. Furthermore, upon IR820 loading, the particle size increased to 89 ± 3.2 nm, and the surface appeared bulkier than that of GO@PDA (BTZ) NPs, confirming dual drug loading and NP formation. Similarly, in the scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) analysis (Figure S2), GO showed sheet-like morphology, and upon PDA coating, GO@PDA exhibited a smooth structure, as observed in the SEM analysis. The particle sizes of GO@PDA (BTZ) NPs and GO@PDA (BTZ, IR820) NPs were 63 ± 3.12 nm and 72 ± 4.22 nm, respectively, with uniform distributions.

Drug loading (DL%) and entrapment efficiency (EE%)

The DL% and EE% % were calculated for GO@PDA (BTZ) and GO@PDA (BTZ, IR820) NPs through UV analysis. Initially, different ratios of BTZ (1 mg) to GO@PDA NPs (5, 10, 15, and 20 mg) were prepared. The GO@PDA (BTZ) NPs obtained 71.3%, 80.12%, 89.2% and 80.5% entrapment efficiency with 11.88%, 7.30%, 5.45% and 3.83% drug loading in the different ratios of GO@PDA (BTZ) NPs (Table S1). Further, to load IR820, a 1:15 ratio of GO@PDA (BTZ) NPs was considered, based on higher entrapment efficiency and smaller particle size compared to other ratios. Similar IR820 (1 mg) to GO@PDA (BTZ) NPs (5/10/15/20 mg) ratios were prepared, with entrapment efficiencies of 65.4%, 82.5%, 79.3%, and 73.5%, and drug loadings of 10.9%, 7.5%, 4.95%, and 3.5%, respectively (Table S2).

Fourier transformation infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), and X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis

Figure 3a, represents the FTIR peaks of the GO@PDA, free BTZ, free IR820, GO@PDA (BTZ) and GO@PDA (BTZ, IR820) NPs. In the GO@PDA, the characteristic peaks of PDA can be observed, such as 3400 cm− 1, 1637 cm− 1, and 1047 cm− 1, indicating -OH/-NH stretching, aromatic C = C stretching, and -CN stretching vibrations. Upon loading with BTZ, GO@PDA (BTZ) NPs exhibited additional peaks around 1650 cm− 1, and 1550 cm− 1, showing the amide (-CO) and amine (-NH) stretching, confirming the BTZ loading. In the GO@PDA (BTZ, IR820) NPs, the characteristic peaks of IR820 were observed at 1100 cm−1and 1600 cm− 1, indicating the vibrational bands of C = C and -CN bands, suggesting successful loading of IR820. Slight shifts in the peaks of the functional groups are observed, indicating strong interactions between the PDA and the drugs loaded (BTZ and IR820), and provide clear evidence for the successful formation of the GO@PDA (BTZ) NPs and GO@PDA (BTZ, IR820) NPs.

The XRD patterns of GO@PDA, free BTZ, free IR820, GO@PDA (BTZ), and GO@PDA (BTZ, IR820) NPs are shown in Fig. 3b. The GO@PDA NPs exhibited 2θ peaks at 10.9° and 26.3°. Ultraviolet (UV) spectrophotometry analysis

UV analysis was performed for GO, GO@PDA, GO@PDA (BTZ), and GO@PDA (BTZ, IR820) NPs, which exhibited successful formation of multimodal nanoplatform as shown in Figure S3. GO exhibited strong absorption around 230 nm due to π-π* transitions of the C = C bonds. GO@PDA showed enhanced, broad absorbance in the visible range, confirming the PDA coating on GO. The GO@PDA (BTZ) NPs showed a shift in peak towards 266 nm, showing the presence of BTZ loading, and GO@PDA (BTZ, IR820) NPs showed an intense absorbance peak around 820 nm due to a high molar extinction coefficient, as IR820 has strong UV absorbance, along with the presence of the BTZ peak, confirming the successful loading.

In vitro drug release study and hemolysis effect

The % cumulative drug release of the GO@PDA (BTZ) and GO@PDA (BTZ, IR820) NPs groups with (+ L) and without (-L) 808 nm/1 W/cm2 laser density was determined in the pH 5.5, pH 6.5, and pH 7.4, respectively, up to 48 h timepoints. At 48 h time periods, about 88%, 78.3% cumulative drug release was observed in the GO@PDA (BTZ) (+ L) and GO@PDA (BTZ) (-L) NPs groups in pH 5.5, whereas GO@PDA (BTZ, IR820) (+ L) and GO@PDA (BTZ, IR820) (-L) NPs showed 80.1% and 73.5% drug release. The graph from Fig. 3d, e suggests better drug release in the presence of 808 nm laser in both the NPs groups compared to the group without laser irradiation. In the hemolysis study, as shown in Fig. 3c, the GO@PDA (BTZ) and GO@PDA (BTZ, IR820) NPs group showed less than 5% hemolysis at 200 µg/ml concentration, indicating non-toxicity of the developed formulations.s.

Photothermal effect

Figure 4a and Figure S4 represent the photothermal effect of the GO@PDA and GO@PDA (BTZ, IR820) NPs at various concentrations (25 µg/ml, 50 µg/ml, 75 µg/ml, and 100 µg/ml) at 808 nm NIR laser/1 W/cm2 density. The GO@PDA (BTZ, IR820) NPs at 25 µg/ml reached 42 °C at 6 min, and those at 100 µg/ml reached 52 °C at 6 min, which remained constant up to 10 min. Similarly, in 50 µg/ml and 75 µg/ml, the temperature showed 45 °C and 49 °C, respectively, as shown in Fig. 4a, c. Whereas the 25 µg/ml of GO@PDA NPs showed 37 °C and 100 µg/ml, 46 °C, and remained constant up to 10 min. Figure 4b, e represents the temperature variation of the GO@PDA (BTZ, IR820) NPs at a fixed concentration of 100 µg/ml, irradiated with 1 W/cm2, 1.5 W/cm2, and 2 W/cm2, 808 nm laser, showing a temperature of 52 °C, 54 °C, and 56 °C rise in temperature with respect to different laser densities. Furthermore, the thermal stability of the GO@PDA (BTZ, IR820) NPs was examined for 60 min, with an ON/OFF cycle of 5 min each, at a constant temperature of 52 °C, as shown in Fig. 4e. The photothermal conversion efficiency of the GO@PDA (BTZ, IR820) NPs group was determined considering the cooling curve (Figure S5). The photothermal conversion efficiency of the GO@PDA (BTZ, IR820) NPs is 54.18%, indicating good photothermal properties.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation and mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) study

Figure 6a, b represents quantitative and qualitative analysis of the ROS generation and MMP depolarization in the spheroid model on treatment with free BTZ (-L), free IR820 (+ L), GO@PDA (+ L), GO@PDA (BTZ) NPs (-L), GO@PDA (BTZ) NPs (+ L), GO@PDA (BTZ, IR820) NPs (-L), and GO@PDA (BTZ, IR820) NPs (+ L) groups. Discussion

In summary, we successfully developed multimodal GO@PDA (BTZ, IR820) NPs for the chemo-photothermal-photodynamic therapy, showing enhanced effect when irradiated with 808 nm NIR-laser for oral cancer treatment. The successful preparation of the nanoplatform was confirmed by various physicochemical characterizations. The improved morphology and size of the GO@PDA (BTZ) and GO@PDA (BTZ, IR820) NPs are evident compared to GO@PDA after the drug loading. The effect of drug release and the anti-hemolytic effect of the GO@PDA (BTZ, IR820) NPs is observed. GO@PDA (BTZ, IR820) NPs exhibited a better photothermal effect, with a temperature increase of up to 52 °C, compared to the GO@PDA group, which reached 46 °C, upon irradiation with an 808 nm NIR laser at a density of 1 W/cm². The photothermal efficiency of the GO@PDA (BTZ, IR820) NPs is 54.18%, suggesting good photothermal properties. The therapeutic effect of the developed formulations and free drugs was evaluated in vitro FaDU cells. In the cytotoxicity assay of the treatment groups, a reduced IC50 of 18.53 ng/ml is observed for the GO@PDA (BTZ, IR820) (+ L) NPs group, indicating a higher efficiency of the formulation. Furthermore, in vitro therapeutic effects were evaluated in the FaDU spheroid model to gain a better understanding of treatment efficacy. GO@PDA (BTZ, IR820) (+ L) NPs group showed enhanced uptake and inhibited spheroidal growth. Further, GO@PDA (BTZ, IR820) (+ L) NPs increased ROS generation and MMP depolarization compared to other treatment groups. In the live/dead assay and Annexin-V/FITC assay, GO@PDA (BTZ, IR820) (+ L) NPs showed enhanced apoptotic rate and cell death. The cell cycle assay revealed cell cycle arrest, inhibiting cell entry into the S phase, as observed in the GO@PDA (BTZ, IR820) (+ L) NPs group. The ER stress protein expression in spheroids treated with the developed formulations showed upregulation in the GO@PDA (BTZ, IR820) (+ L) NPs formulation, indicating higher ER stress-mediated cell death. These combined findings highlight the benefits of multimodal therapy over single-mode treatments in overcoming oral cancer.

Conclusions

The GO@PDA (BTZ, IR820) nanoplatform developed in this study exhibits a synergistic chemo-photothermal-photodynamic effect in oral cancer spheroids, demonstrating promise as a multimodal nanoplatform. By combining chemotherapeutic and photothermal agents, it achieves a smaller size, improved physicochemical properties, and greater photothermal efficiency. The nanoplatform shows effective cellular uptake, stronger growth inhibition, increased ROS production, mitochondrial dysfunction, apoptosis, cell cycle arrest, and elevated ER stress markers. Overall, the findings indicate that the combined use of BTZ and IR820 with the GO@PDA platform significantly enhances therapeutic effectiveness, offering a promising approach for the eradication of oral cancer through multimodal therapy.

Materials and methods

Materials

Graphite and dopamine hydrochloride were obtained from Sisco Research Laboratory (SRL, India). 4′,6-Diamidino-2-phenylindole dihydrochloride (DAPI), agarose, propidium iodide (PI), 5,5′,6,6′-Tetrachloro-1,1′,3,3′ tetraethylbenzimi-dazolocarbocyanine iodide (JC 1 Dye), -(4,5-Dimethylthiazol-2-yl)−2,5-Diphenyltetrazolium Bromide (MTT), dichloro-dihydro-fluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA), and IR820 were procured from Sigma Aldrich (Merck, USA). Annexin V-FITC Apoptosis Detection Kit was obtained from Invitrogen. BTZ was given as a gift sample from Hetero Labs Pvt. Ltd, Hyderabad. Oral cancer cell line FaDU was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, USA). The ER stress primary antibodies: C/EBP Homologous Protein (CHOP), protein kinase R (PKR)-like endoplasmic reticulum kinase (PERK), and Binding Immunoglobulin Protein (BiP), and the housekeeping gene, β-actin (b-actin), and the secondary antibody were purchased from Cell Signalling Technologies (CST, USA).

Preparation of GO@PDA (BTZ, IR820) NPs

GO was synthesized by Hummer’s modified method using the previously reported protocol using pristine graphite powder30. Graphite powder (1 g) was added to the previously agitated sulfuric acid and phosphoric acid (9:1 ratio) solution. Furthermore, potassium permanganate (KMnO4) was gradually added to the above solution, and the solution mixture was stirred continuously for up to 6 h until the solution turned deep green in colour. To eliminate KMnO4, hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) was added to the above solution and agitated for 10–15 min. The exothermic reaction was cooled, and deionized water (H2O) and hydrochloric acid (HCl) were added to the solution. The solution mixture was further centrifuged to decant the supernatant, and the remaining product was dried to obtain dry GO powder.

To coat the GO with PDA, first, PDA was prepared through an oxidation reaction of dopamine HCl under alkaline conditions29. Briefly, dopamine HCl (50 mg) dissolved in deionized H2O (1 ml) was injected into ethanol solution (4 ml) and deionized H2O (9 ml). After adding 250 µl ammonia solution, the colourless solution turned pale yellow, gradually changing to dark brown. The reaction was further incubated overnight, and the PDA was collected by centrifugation, washed three times with H2O, and dried to obtain a dry powder. 100 mg of GO in 5 ml Tris Buffer (pH 8.5) was sonicated for 10 min. 20 mg of PDA was added to the above solution and stirred at 500 rpm overnight to allow coating at room temperature (RT). The solution was centrifuged and washed to obtain GO@PDA NPs.

GO@PDA (BTZ) NPs were prepared by incubating a 1 mg/mL BTZ solution in methanol to pre-sonicate GO@PDA NPs (5, 10, 15, and 20 mg) in a methanol solution. The solution was left under stirring conditions at RT overnight. The solution was centrifuged, and the supernatant was discarded to remove unentrapped BTZ. The pellet was then resuspended in PBS.

Similarly, 1 mg/ml of IR820 solution was loaded into GO@PDA (BTZ) NPs in methanol solution and incubated overnight for loading. Furthermore, the solution was centrifuged, and the supernatant was discarded to remove unentrapped IR820. The pellet was then resuspended in PBS to obtain a GO@PDA (BTZ, IR820) NPs solution.

The surface morphology of the developed NPs was analysed by SEM and STEM by Apreo Lo Vac, FEI, USA. FTIR and XRD analysis

FTIR analysis of the developed NPs and free drugs was performed by the KBr pellet method using FTIR by Jasco-4200, USA, by scanning the pellets at 4000 to 500 cm− 1. To determine the crystallinity of the GO@PDA NPs, GO@PDA (BTZ) NPs, and GO@PDA (BTZ, IR820) NPs, XRD was performed using an X-ray diffractometer (ULTIMA-IV, Rigaku). The scanning angle (2θ) was set from 10˚ to 50˚, 5˚/minute at 40 kV voltage and 30 mA scanning current.

UV analysis

The UV analysis of GO, GO@PDA, GO@PDA (BTZ) NPs, and GO@PDA (BTZ, IR820) NPs was performed to determine the successful formation of the multimodal nanoplatform. All the NPs were dispersed in ethanol and sonicated briefly to ensure a homogenous suspension before the analysis. The spectra measurement was done for all the samples using Jasco Spectrophotometer, and graphs were overlapped using GraphPad Prism 8.0 version (www.graphpad.com) software.

In vitro drug release

The in vitro drug release of GO@PDA (BTZ) NPs and GO@PDA (BTZ, IR820) NPs was performed at pH 5.5, pH 6.5, and pH 7.4, respectively. Briefly, 1 mg/ml of the GO@PDA (BTZ) NPs and GO@PDA (BTZ, IR820) NPs were taken in a dialysis bag and placed in 150 ml of sterile PBS with pH 5.5, pH 6.5, and pH 7.4, and stirred at RT with 100 RPM. At every time point (1 h, 3 h, 6 h, 9 h, 12 h, 24 h, 36 h, and 48 h), 1 ml of each solution was collected and replaced with 1 ml of PBS to maintain the sink condition. For the (+ L) groups, a 1 W/cm2 808 nm NIR laser was irradiated after 12 h for 5 min. The release of BTZ and IR820 was estimated by UV analysis at λmax 267 nm, and λmax 824 nm respectively.

In vitro cell studies

Cell viability assay

An in vitro cell viability assay was performed using FaDU cells in a monolayer using the MTT assay31. Briefly, 1 × 104 cells/well were seeded in 96 96-well plate in DMEM media and allowed to attach overnight. Further, the cells were treated with different concentrations of the free BTZ (-L), free IR820 (+ L), GO@PDA (+ L), GO@PDA (BTZ) NPs (-L), GO@PDA (BTZ) NPs (+ L), GO@PDA (BTZ, IR820) NPs (-L), and GO@PDA (BTZ, IR820) NPs (+ L). The (+ L) groups were irradiated with 1 W/cm2 using an 808 nm laser for 5 min after 12 h of the treatment. After 24 h and 48 h of incubation with the treatment groups, the media were replaced with 100 µl of a 2 mg/ml concentration of the MTT dye. After 4 h of incubation, the MTT dye was replaced with 150 µl DMSO to dissolve the formazan crystals. Avascular FaDU spheroid culture

The FaDU spheroids were developed using the previously reported liquid overlay method32. Briefly, a 1.5% v/v agarose solution in serum-free DMEM media solution was prepared. 50 µl/well of the solution was added to the 96-well plate and allowed to cool. Further, 1 × 104 FaDU cells/well were added and centrifuged at 1,500 RPM for 10 min. The plate was kept in the incubator to form a 3D spheroid assembly.

In vitro cellular uptake study

In vitro cellular uptake of the GO@PDA (BTZ, PDA) NPs was performed in FaDU spheroids. Once the spheroids formed, it was treated with GO@PDA (BTZ, PDA) NPs and incubated for 1 h, 4 h, and 8 h time intervals. After each time point, the spheroids were incubated with DAPI for 15 min and carefully washed with PBS. The quantitative and qualitative cellular uptake measurements were done by flow cytometry (BD-FACS) and confocal microscopy (Leica, Germany).

In vitro spheroid growth inhibition study

In vitro spheroid growth inhibition on treatment with free BTZ, free IR820 (+ L), GO@PDA (+ L), GO@PDA (BTZ) NPs (-L), GO@PDA (BTZ) NPs (+ L), GO@PDA (BTZ, IR820) NPs (-L), and GO@PDA (BTZ, IR820) NPs (+ L) groups were analysed in the FaDU spheroid model. After 12 h of treatment, the spheroids were irradiated with 1 W/cm2 density/808 nm laser. Further, at different time points such as day 0, day 3 and day 6, the spheroid sizes were measured and bright field images were captured using Leica microscope33.

ROS generation study

ROS generation assay was performed to estimate the ROS generation of the developed NPs by using DCFH-DA dye34. Briefly, the spheroids were treated with free BTZ (-L), free IR820 (+ L), GO@PDA (+ L), GO@PDA (BTZ) NPs (-L), GO@PDA (BTZ) NPs (+ L), GO@PDA (BTZ, IR820) NPs (-L), and GO@PDA (BTZ, IR820) NPs (+ L), after 12 h of incubation, the spheroids were treated with 808 nm laser/1 W/cm2 density. After 24 h of the treatment period, the spheroids were washed with PBS and incubated with DCFH-DA (10 µM) dye for 20 min. Further, the quantitative and qualitative analysis for ROS generation was done by flow cytometry and confocal microscopy.

Mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) study

The change in MMP of the FaDU spheroids on treatment with free BTZ (-L), free IR820 (+ L), GO@PDA (+ L), GO@PDA (BTZ) NPs (-L), GO@PDA (BTZ) NPs (+ L), GO@PDA (BTZ, IR820) NPs (-L), and GO@PDA (BTZ, IR820) NPs (+ L) was analysed using a lipophilic JC-1 dye. Similar to the previous method, after 12 h of treatment, the (+ L) group spheroids were irradiated with an 808 nm laser/1 W/cm2. After 24 h of the treatment, the cells were washed with sterile PBS and incubated with JC-1 (5 mM) dye for 15 min and further analysed by confocal microscopy35.

Live/dead assay

To understand the cytotoxic effect of the free BTZ (-L), free IR820 (+ L), GO@PDA (+ L), GO@PDA (BTZ) NPs (-L), GO@PDA (BTZ) NPs (+ L), GO@PDA (BTZ, IR820) NPs (-L), and GO@PDA (BTZ, IR820) NPs (+ L), live/dead assay was performed. The spheroids were treated with the treatment groups. After 12 h of treatment, the (+ L) groups were irradiated with an 808 nm laser. 24 h later, the spheroids were treated with calcein-AM (3 µM) and propidium iodide (PI, 8 µM) and incubated for 15 min in the dark and visualized under a fluorescence microscope.

Annexin V assay for apoptosis

To determine the apoptosis efficiency of the free BTZ (-L), free IR820 (+ L), GO@PDA (+ L), GO@PDA (BTZ) NPs (-L), GO@PDA (BTZ) NPs (+ L), GO@PDA (BTZ, IR820) NPs (-L), and GO@PDA (BTZ, IR820) NPs (+ L), an Annexin V assay was performed. 24 h after the incubation with the treatment groups, the spheroid pellets were collected by centrifugation, and the pellet was resuspended with binding buffer (100 µl) and stained with Annexin V FITC (1 µl) and PI (10 µL) from the TACS® Annexin V-FITC Apoptosis Detection Kit following the manufacturer’s protocol. Further, incubation for 15 min, the binding buffer was added to the control and treatment groups, and cells were analysed using flow cytometry36.

Cell cycle analysis

Cell cycle analysis in free BTZ (-L), free IR820 (+ L), GO@PDA (+ L), GO@PDA (BTZ) NPs (-L), GO@PDA (BTZ) NPs (+ L), GO@PDA (BTZ, IR820) NPs (-L), and GO@PDA (BTZ, IR820) NPs (+ L), treated groups was performed by PI staining30. Briefly, after 24 h of the treatment period, the spheroid pellets were collected by centrifugation. The pellets were resuspended with 70% ethanol and kept in −20 °C for overnight. The pellets were washed with PBS to remove the ethanol and stained with RNase (20 mg/ml), PI (20 µg/ml), and 0.1% Triton-X-100 for 15–20 min. Flow cytometry was performed to analyse the different phases of the cell cycle, and further, the G1, S, and G2/M phases were quantified by FlowJo 10.8.1 software (https://www/flowjo.com )37.

Western blot study

Western blot analysis was conducted to assess the expression of ER stress markers in various treatment groups: free BTZ (-L), free IR820 (+ L), GO@PDA (+ L), GO@PDA (BTZ) NPs (-L), GO@PDA (BTZ) NPs (+ L), GO@PDA (BTZ, IR820) NPs (-L), and GO@PDA (BTZ, IR820) NPs (+ L) in FaDU spheroids. The spheroid pellets were collected after 24 h of treatment. Proteins were isolated using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail, and their concentration was determined using the Bicinchoninic Acid (BCA) Protein Assay Kit. Approximately 30 µg of each protein sample was separated by SDS-PAGE, transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane, and blocked with a BSA blocking buffer for 1 h. The blots were incubated with ER stress primary antibodies: β-actin (1:3000), CHOP (1:1000), PERK (1:1000), and BiP (1:1000) at 4 °C. After overnight incubation, the blots were washed thrice with 1X PBST and blocked with secondary antibody at RT for 1 h. After consecutive washing, the blots were visualized using an electrochemical luminescent (ECL) labeling system (Bio-Rad, USA). The ER stress protein expression was normalized with β-actin, and expression was quantified using ImageJ.exe software, version 8.0 (imagej.net/ij/download/), and the graph was plotted14.

Statistical analysis

All the experiments were conducted in triplicate (n = 3). GraphPad Prism 8.0 version software was used to conduct one-way ANOVA and Student t-test between the groups to determine the statistical significance.
