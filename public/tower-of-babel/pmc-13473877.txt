Integrating heterogeneity into topologically associating domain boundary prediction in large genomic context in human
Authors: Ying Sun, Lars Juhl Jensen, Niels Tommerup, Jan Gorodkin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA fundamental understanding of genome organization relies on accurately annotating topologically associating domains (TADs) and their boundaries. This is crucial for understanding how cis-regulatory elements regulate gene expression. To go beyond calling TADs and boundaries from Hi-C data, several machine learning-based methods have been proposed to go the step further and predict TAD boundaries from genomic sequences. As the growing evidence of TADs and their boundaries, TADs have been proved exhibiting diverse properties, such as differences in replication timing and epigenetic patterns. However, existing methods do not take this heterogeneity into account. To address this, we propose a method called TADBpred for TAD boundary prediction in a large genomic context in humans. TADBpred focuses on TAD boundaries in active and inactive chromatin across cell-lines and tissues, which are GC-rich and AT-rich, respectively. By integrating genomic elements and sequence composition, we designed two models for GC-rich and AT-rich boundaries, respectively. When testing the performance on respective independent held-out datasets, we obtain AUC scores of 0.91 and 0.80. Our results indicate that TADBpred excels in TAD boundary prediction. Additionally, feature importance analysis highlights the essential features for different classes of TAD boundaries, thereby enhancing our understanding of these TAD boundaries.

Introduction

Discovery of highly self-interacting properties of the genome in 2012, led to establishment of topologically associating domains (TADs) as a basic structure in the genome [1–3]. The genomic segments between adjacent TADs are known as TAD boundaries. Enriched with insulator elements (e.g. CTCF), TAD boundaries act as barriers that restrict DNA–DNA interactions between adjacent TADs, creating a highly segregated genomic environment [1]. This architectural organization facilitates long-range cis-regulation, which is crucial for gene expression regulation and DNA replication processes [4, 5]. Disruptions in TAD boundaries can lead to the fusion of neighboring TADs, consequently impacting gene regulation and potentially contributing to diseases such as cancer [6–8]. Therefore, precise annotation of TADs and their boundaries is essential for advancing our understanding of gene regulatory mechanisms.

The annotation of TADs primarily relies on analyzing Hi-C interaction frequency matrices to identify regions of extensive self-interaction across the genome. Dozens of efficient tools, such as DomainCaller [1], InsulationScore [9], and OnTAD [10], have been developed for this purpose [11, 12]. However, the high cost associated with acquiring Hi-C data and the significant computational resources required hinder the widespread detection of TAD structures [13]. Therefore, several machine learning-based methods have emerged for TAD boundary prediction [13–20]. These approaches capitalize on the enrichment of genomic elements at TAD boundaries, such as CTCF and various histone modifications, to distinguish between TAD boundary and TAD regions [13, 14, 18, 20]. Additionally, the enrichment of genomic elements at TAD boundaries suggests that DNA sequences from these regions may possess distinctive features, which can also be applied to TAD boundary prediction [21]. Some of the methods extract DNA sequence information [16, 19] or integrate it with other features [15, 17] for this prediction. However, none of these methods account for the heterogeneity of TAD boundaries. Although there is no universally accepted definition of TADs and their boundaries, their diversity is evident in various aspects [22, 23], such as differences in replication timing [24] (e.g. early/late replication) and epigenetic signals [25] (e.g. CTCF and histone modifications). Incorporating this diversity into TAD boundary prediction methods is essential for enhancing their accuracy and advancing our understanding of genome organization.

In this study, we propose the machine learning-based model TADBpred for TAD boundary prediction across cell-lines and tissues in a large genomic context (40 kb) in human, that accounts for the heterogeneity of TAD boundaries. The TAD boundary datasets were derived from previous work that merged 28 TAD datasets identified by DomainCaller across cell-lines and tissues with distinct GC content distributions, one GC-rich and one AT-rich [21]. These TAD boundaries differ not only in sequence composition but also in epigenetic patterns and TAD hierarchical structures. Therefore, we designed a prediction model that accounts for this GC content difference. The performance of our model, as measured by the area under the curve (AUC) score, was on independent held-out datasets found to be 0.91 for GC-rich boundaries and 0.80 for AT-rich boundaries. Furthermore, examination of the interaction frequency matrix from negative datasets with a high probability of being TAD boundaries revealed their high insulation abilities, indicating that these regions could be defined as TAD boundaries. Additionally, our machine learning-based approach identified important features specific to GC-rich and AT-rich boundaries, enhancing our understanding of these TAD boundaries.

Materials and methods

Datasets

To construct an effective model for predicting TAD boundaries, two distinct datasets were compiled: TAD boundary annotations and TAD boundary-relevant annotation datasets. The well-annotated TAD boundaries were sourced from our previous work [21], wherein we merged 28 TAD annotation datasets from human (hg38) across different cell-lines and tissues from the 3D Genome Browser (version 1.0) [26], called by DomainCaller [1] at a resolution of 40 kb. These 28 TAD annotations are outlined in Supplementary Table S1. Subsequently, TAD boundaries were extracted from their TAD start and end points, extending 40 kb (±20 kb), followed by merging them across samples using our in-house scripts. To investigate the diversity of TAD boundaries, we conducted fuzzy c-means clustering analysis (https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.5497844) based on k-mer embedding of TAD boundaries (see details in following section “k-mer count representation for TAD boundaries”). This yielded two classes: 1732 GC-rich boundaries and 2868 AT-rich boundaries. Additionally, we collected a range of annotation datasets from the UCSC Genome Browser [27] associated with TAD boundaries [14, 28], encompassing repeats, DNase I hypersensitive sites, CpG islands, and transcription factor binding sites (TFBS). These annotation datasets are not cell- or tissue-specific that DNase I hypersensitive sites and TFBS represent a collection from multiple cell-lines and tissues, while CpG islands and repeats are identified from DNA sequence of the hg38 reference genome. Detailed information regarding the TAD boundary-related annotation datasets is listed in Supplementary Table S2.

Considering that the TAD boundary dataset is collected from multiple samples, we excluded 655 TAD boundaries detected in only one sample, since they are not well-represented. In addition, to predict TAD boundaries, an equal number of negative datasets (non-TAD boundary datasets) were generated. These negative datasets comprised 40 kb sequences randomly extracted from TAD regions, maintaining the same GC content distribution as observed in TAD boundaries. Consequently, we obtained 3138 GC-rich datasets and 4752 AT-rich datasets for training GC-rich and AT-rich models, respectively. For each dataset, half of them were positive data points (TAD boundaries) and half of them were negative data points (non-TAD boundaries).

In addition, to evaluate the performance of TADBpred on TAD datasets identified by other TAD callers, we collected 17 processed TAD datasets called by InsulationScore [9] across cell-lines and tissues. These datasets were downloaded from the 4DN Data Portal [29] with a 5 kb resolution. Detailed information is provided in Supplementary Table S1. We then merged them into a well-annotated TAD boundary dataset using our in-house scripts to generate the independent test set for further performance evaluation [21]. To benchmark our method against existing TAD boundary prediction tools without requiring Hi-C data, we also downloaded publicly available ChIP-Seq datasets from ENCODE [30], including nine histone modifications (H3K27ac, H3K27me3, H3K36me3, H3K4me1, H3K4me2, H3K4me3, H3K9ac, H3K9me3, and H4K20me1) and CTCF. Since these prediction tools focus on cell-/tissue-specific prediction and some ChIP-Seq datasets are missing in ENCODE, we chose three well-studied cell-lines as examples for benchmarking, including GM12878, IMR90 and H1-hESC. Detailed information on the ChIP-Seq datasets used is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

TAD boundaries representation by genomic elements and repeats counts

Given lots of evidence suggesting enrichment or depletion of certain genomic elements and repeats at TAD boundaries, such as CTCF binding sites and Alu [1, 14, 28], such annotations hold promise for TAD boundary prediction. Accordingly, we extracted the counts of various genomic elements and repeats for each TAD boundary and non-TAD boundary as features for TAD boundary prediction. This process was performed as follows: we first collected CpG islands, DNase I hypersensitive sites, 340 types of TFBSs, and 54 repeat family annotation files from the UCSC Genome Browser [27], based on the hg38 human reference genome. Annotations were considered to be overlapped a particular region (TAD boundary or non-TAD boundary) if at least 50% of the annotation overlapped with that region. Subsequently, we calculated the number of different types of annotations (including various TFBSs, repeats, and genomic elements) overlapped with each data point, resulting in a vector for subsequent model development. Since TADs are not strand-specific structures, annotations from both strands were counted.

Given that consensus CTCF binding sites with higher peak scores and more experimental support are more closely associated with TAD boundaries [14], we extracted what we refer to as CTCF binding site dataset by applying filters based on peak scores and the number of supporting experiments. The peak score, which is provided by UCSC Genome Browser, indicates the strength of the potential of the signal to bind to the genome ranging from 0 to 1000 [27]. Specifically, this score is calculated based on signal values from ChIP-Seq datasets. Signal values between 0 and the mean plus one standard deviation are normalized to a range of 0–1000, with the maximum value capped at 1000. Therefore, we created CTCF binding site dataset by applying varying thresholds for peak scores and the number of supporting experiments. Specifically, peak score thresholds ranged from 0 to 1000 with increments of 100, while thresholds for the number of experiments ranged from 0 to 100 with increments of 10, resulting in 121 versions of the CTCF dataset. DNase I hypersensitive sites are also widely distributed and collected from multiple experiments, and we similarly extracted DNase I hypersensitive site dataset using different peak scores and experiment number thresholds. Peak scores were filtered from 0 to 1000 with increments of 100, while the number of experiments was filtered from 0 to 250 with increments of 50, resulting in 66 versions of the DNase I hypersensitive site datasets. Then, we counted the number of different versions of CTCF and DNase I hypersensitive sites for each data point.

k-mer count representation for TAD boundaries

We extracted the DNA sequences and computed their k-mer counts (where 1 ≤ k ≤ 20) for each data point in the prediction model, reflecting the occurrences of potential motifs. To reduce the dimensionality of the k-mer count features, we retained only the top 250 k-mer counts for TAD boundaries when k > 4. Since TADs are not strand-specific structures, k-mer counts from both strands were extracted, and only one instance of reverse complementary k-mers with identical counts was retained. Given that most k-mers are not detected in most data points, we selected k-mers with at least 5 counts in over 80% of TAD boundaries. Finally, 1114 k-mers were chosen from TAD boundaries, and their counts were utilized to represent both TAD boundaries and non-TAD boundaries.

Dataset separation for model training

To train the model, each dataset was divided into six partitions based on their similarities, with five partitions for training and the sixth reserved as an independent test set. The dataset integrated all types of features extracted in the above steps, comprising k-mers, repeats, genomic elements, CTCF and DNase I hypersensitive sites. Subsequently, we calculated pairwise Euclidean distances between different data points after normalization using the StandardScaler function in scikit-learn [31]. Data points with Euclidean distances less than the 0.01 percentile threshold were regarded as highly similar. The detailed threshold values were 27.94, 14.77, 31.86, and 29.07 for GC-rich positive and negative datasets, AT-rich positive and negative datasets, respectively. The detailed explanation about identifying highly similar data points is available in Supplementary Text, Supplementary Fig. S1, and Supplementary Table S3. For these highly similar data points, we grouped them together and randomly put the similar ones within one partition to prevent similarity between data points from the validation or test set and those from the training set. For the remaining data points without highly similar data points, we randomly distributed them across different partitions while ensuring a balanced distribution of data points among the partitions. Finally, we obtained 2616 GC-rich and 3960 AT-rich data points for training, with 522 GC-rich and 792 AT-rich data points reserved for independent testing. These data partitions were fixed and consistently used throughout all subsequent model development steps.

Training the model

We proposed two models for predicting whether a genomic region is a TAD boundary or not: the GC-rich model and the AT-rich model, trained on the GC-rich and AT-rich datasets, respectively. We employed the XGBoost classifier [32] for both datasets. XGBoost is a tree-based gradient boosting algorithm that incorporates regularization terms to prevent overfitting. Based on this method, we trained both models with five-fold cross-validation, utilizing five partitions for training and the sixth (held-out) dataset for testing. For each data point, the model output a probability score ranging from 0 to 1, representing the likelihood that the region represents a TAD boundary. If the probability exceeded 0.5, it was classified as a TAD boundary; otherwise, it was classified as a non-TAD boundary. Through grid searching different hyper-parameter combinations, we selected the models with the highest AUC score from cross-validation. The detailed combinations of hyper-parameters are listed in Supplementary Table S4. To prevent overfitting during training, we applied an early stopping strategy; if the performance of the model on the validation set did not improve after 50 iterations, training was halted. The typical number of iterations during training ranged from 1000 to 2500. Since the datasets were represented by different types of features, including k-mers, repeats, genomic elements, CTCF, and DNase I hypersensitive sites, we first trained separate models for each type of features using above procedure on the AT-rich and GC-rich datasets, respectively. Then, we integrated all TAD boundary-relevant features into a single comprehensive feature vector and trained models on the AT-rich and GC-rich datasets using the same procedure. For both GC-rich and AT-rich data, the models trained using all the features achieved a better performance in terms of AUC score than the models trained on the individual types of the features and were therefore selected as the final models. The detailed model performance evaluation is introduced in the next paragraph.

To evaluate the performance of our models for predicting TAD boundaries, we chose the following metrics, including AUC score, accuracy, recall, precision, F1, Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC), and Cohen’s Kappa coefficient (Kappa). Important features for TAD boundary prediction

To investigate the importance of features in TAD boundary prediction, we characterized the importance of features for both the GC-rich and the AT-rich models. Utilizing the SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) method, we quantified the contribution of each feature to the model output [33]. A positive value indicates that the feature positively contributes to the model, whereas a negative value suggests a negative contribution. Here, we chose the held-out test sets for SHAP value calculation, except for the models trained only on CTCF and DNase I hypersensitive site datasets. These models were specifically used to identify high-quality CTCF and DNase I hypersensitive sites, so that the training sets were used for SHAP analysis instead to avoid data leakage.

To examine the distribution of important features relevant to TAD boundaries, we extended both the TAD boundaries and non-TAD boundaries from 40 kb to 400 kb (40 kb ± 180 kb). Subsequently, we partitioned the extended regions into smaller bins using sliding windows with a bin size of 40 kb and a step size of 10 kb. Within each 40 kb bin, we calculated the number of occurrences of the feature of interest. We then computed the mean counts of this feature associated with TAD boundaries across all bins with the same distance to TAD boundaries. Finally, we derived the overall distribution of this feature around TAD boundaries.

Comparison with other machine learning methods

We also trained the models using other machine learning methods, including AdaBoost, random forest, support vector machine (SVM), neural network, naive Bayes, logistic regression, and k-nearest neighbors (k-NN). All these models were implemented using the scikit-learn library [31]. The training process was identical to that used for training the XGBoost model, utilizing five-fold cross-validation on the same training set. Each model was optimized using different hyper-parameter combinations, as detailed in Supplementary Table S5. The models with the highest AUC scores from cross-validation were selected. Subsequently, we applied AUC, accuracy, recall, precision, F1 score, MCC, and Kappa metrics to evaluate their performance.

TADBpred for TAD boundary prediction with GC content into consideration

To account for the heterogeneity of TAD boundaries with different GC content, we developed a two-step prediction model, TADBpred, which performs TAD boundary prediction by selecting the optimal GC-rich or AT-rich model based on the GC content of the target region. In the first step, we calculate the GC content of the 40 kb target genomic region of interest. In the second step, we classify this region as either a TAD boundary or non-TAD boundary using the appropriate model according to its GC content. If the region has a GC content higher than or equal to 0.42, it is predicted using the GC-rich model; otherwise, the AT-rich model is applied. The threshold of 0.42 was determined based on the GC content distributions of the GC-rich and AT-rich datasets, where 99% (1550/1569) of GC-rich boundaries had GC content higher than 0.42, and 99% (2342/2376) of AT-rich boundaries had GC content values lower than 0.42 [21].

To investigate the importance of considering the heterogeneity of TAD boundaries in the prediction model, we combined the GC-rich dataset and the AT-rich dataset to train a unified model using XGBoost method based on all the features. Unlike the two-step prediction model, this unified model does not account for GC content differences and treats all data uniformly. The training process followed the same protocol as the model trained on separate datasets, utilizing five-fold cross-validation and was trained on the same training set. We searched through a series of hyper-parameter combinations and selected the best combination based on the highest AUC score from cross-validation (detailed in Supplementary Table S6). Subsequently, we compared the performance on the independent test set with the model trained on GC-rich and AT-rich datasets separately.

TAD boundary prediction on independent test set from other TAD callers

TADBpred was trained on TAD datasets identified by DomainCaller [1]. However, TAD boundaries annotated by different TAD callers can vary [11, 12]. To evaluate the generalization ability of our model across TADs identified by different TAD callers, we further assessed its predictive performance on TAD datasets identified by InsulationScore [9]. Given that TADBpred is designed to predict TAD boundaries across various cell-lines and tissues, we employed our in-house TAD boundary dataset, which was constructed by merging InsulationScore-based [21] TAD boundaries across cell-lines and tissues. The original TAD boundary dataset, obtained from the 4DN Data Portal [29], had a resolution of 5 kb. To match the input requirement of TADBpred, which predicts whether a large-scale genomic region (40 kb) contains a TAD boundary, we expanded each 5 kb boundary region to 40 kb (5 kb ± 17.5 kb from the center) to construct an independent test set for performance evaluation.

To construct an independent dataset for model performance evaluation, we ensured that the selected TAD boundaries were entirely distinct from those in the training set, both in feature representation and genomic coordinates. First, we calculated pairwise Euclidean distances between data points in the independent dataset and those in the training set, based on feature representations normalized using the StandardScaler fitted on the training data. To avoid potential overfitting due to high similarity, data points in the independent set that were highly similar to any training data points were removed. The thresholds for identifying highly similar data points were determined from the training sets that were used to create the independent dataset. Second, we excluded TAD boundaries whose genomic coordinates overlapped (≥1 nt) with those of the training set. After these filtering steps, 13 891 AT-rich and 7012 GC-rich TAD boundaries remained, which were used as the independent test set. In addition, an equal number of non-TAD boundary regions with the same GC content distribution were selected as a negative dataset for further performance evaluation.

Benchmarking with existing models for TAD boundary prediction on independent test set

To explore whether our model outperforms existing methods for TAD boundary prediction, we performed a benchmarking analysis against other approaches that do not make use of Hi-C data. We are aware of two such methods: HubPredictor [13] and TAD-Lactuca [15]. To assess the generalization ability of these methods, we used TAD boundary datasets identified using InsulationScore and downloaded from the 4DN Data Portal [29]. Since these methods are designed to predict TAD boundaries for specific cell-lines or tissues and require cell- or tissue-specific histone modification data as input, we selected TAD boundaries from well-characterized cell-lines for benchmarking. Due to limited availability of histone modification data in ENCODE [30] for some cell-lines, we chose the TAD boundary datasets from GM12878, IMR90 and H1-hESC for benchmarking. For example, only six types of histone modifications are available for the HFFc6 cell-line in ENCODE, with H4K20me1, H3K4me2, and H3K9ac missing from the database (data collection until 25 March 2025).

To apply HubPredictor [13] and TAD-Lactuca [15] for TAD boundary prediction, we downloaded their source code from their respective GitHub repositories and trained the models using the provided training sets. For HubPredictor, we extended the 40 kb TAD boundary region to 300 kb (40 kb ± 130 kb) to generate the required input signals. Similarly, for TAD-Lactuca, we extended the 40 kb regions to 840 kb (40 kb ± 400 kb). TAD boundaries whose extended regions exceeded chromosome boundaries were excluded from benchmarking. To generate the input features for both tools, we extracted signal values from ChIP-Seq datasets and calculated the mean or summed signal intensities within each extended genomic region using the Python package pyBigWig.

Enrichment of CTCF and histone modifications at TAD boundaries

To investigate the biological relevance of the TAD boundaries predicted by TADBpred, we performed enrichment analysis of CTCF and nine histone modifications (including H3K27ac, H3K27me3, H3K36me3, H3K4me1, H3K4me2, H3K4me3, H3K9ac, H3K9me3, and H4K20me1). We analyzed their signal distributions within ±500 kb regions centered on TAD boundaries. Two widely used TAD callers were selected for comparison, including DomainCaller [1] and InsulationScore [9]. Given that CTCF and histone modification signals are cell- or tissue-specific, we used cell- or tissue-specific TAD annotations from DomainCaller and InsulationScore for the enrichment analysis. To eliminate potential bias caused by differences in Hi-C datasets, we focused on the GM12878 cell-line, for which processed TAD annotations from both DomainCaller and InsulationScore, derived from the same Hi-C dataset [34] are publicly available. For TADBpred, we selected regions from the InsulationScore-based independent test set (on both positive and negative data points) that were predicted as TAD boundaries by TADBpred for enrichment analysis. To compute the signal distributions of CTCF and histone modifications, we divided each TAD boundary and its ±500 kb flanking regions into non-overlapping 5 kb bins, following the settings described in [11] and calculated the total signal within each bin using the Python package pyBigWig. We then computed the mean signal for each bin position relative to the boundary to visualize the enrichment profile. A peak centered at the boundary indicates enrichment, while a valley indicates depletion of the signal at TAD boundaries.

Results and discussion

In this study, we proposed the machine learning model TADBpred, for TAD boundary prediction, taking into account the heterogeneity of TAD boundaries. Here, we focused on TAD boundaries derived from active and inactive chromatin, categorized as GC-rich and AT-rich, respectively, as obtained from our previous work [21]. Initially, we accurately predicted these boundaries by integrating the composition of genomic elements, k-mers, and repeats. Subsequently, we extracted the important features for each type of TAD boundary to elucidate their relationship with TAD boundaries. Through a systematic performance evaluation, TADBpred demonstrated strong predictive ability compared with other non-Hi-C based methods. By examining the Hi-C interaction frequency matrices within regions identified as having a high probability of being TAD boundaries in the non-TAD boundary dataset, we observed that TADBpred can detect the potential TAD boundaries not identified by DomainCaller [1]. These findings suggest that our machine learning-based method for TAD boundary prediction can complement Hi-C interaction frequency matrix-based methods.

Improving the performance of TAD boundary prediction by integrating all types of features

To design models for TAD boundary prediction, we collected three types of features: genomic elements, k-mers, and repeats. Initially, we utilized genomic elements, comprising 340 types of TFBSs, DNase I hypersensitive sites, and CpG islands, to train the models. Each positive or negative data point was represented by a vector indicating the number of occurrences of each genomic element overlapping that data point. Then, we employed the XGBoost algorithm to train the models. XGBoost is a powerful machine learning framework based on gradient boosting, which builds an ensemble of decision trees sequentially to improve predictive performance. Two models were trained separately on GC-rich and AT-rich data using five-fold cross-validation on a dataset divided into five partitions. Following grid search of hyper-parameters, we selected the models with the highest AUC scores on the validation sets. Their performances on the independent test sets were further evaluated using various metrics including AUC score, accuracy, precision, recall, F1 score, MCC, and Kappa. The AUC scores from test sets were 0.8825 and 0.7813 for the GC-rich and AT-rich models, respectively. Further details of the performance are presented in Fig. 1.

Improving the performance of TAD boundary prediction for both GC-rich and AT-rich models by integrating various types of features.

Given that Hong et al. proposed that consensus CTCF binding sites possess higher predictive power for TAD boundaries [14], we incorporated selected CTCF features into our TAD boundary prediction model. We first obtained 121 different versions of CTCF datasets, filtered based on various peak scores and the number of supporting experiments (see detailed filtering steps in the Methods section). Subsequently, we computed the number of CTCF binding sites overlapping with each data point, which were utilized for encoding TAD boundary or non-TAD boundary data. To assess the predictive capabilities of different CTCF datasets for TAD boundaries, we calculated their feature importance scores using SHAP values on the XGBoost model. Our analysis revealed that the CTCF binding site dataset detected in at least 100 experiments with a peak score of not less than 600 exhibited the highest predictive power for GC-rich boundaries, while the CTCF binding site dataset detected in at least 90 experiments with a peak score of not less than 200 was more important for AT-rich boundaries (Supplementary Fig. S2A and B). The CTCF dataset with the highest SHAP values was selected for further model development. To avoid potential data leakage during feature selection, SHAP values were computed exclusively on the training sets. Moreover, models trained only on CTCF datasets achieved AUC scores exceeding 0.77 on the test sets (Supplementary Fig. S2A and B), indicating the crucial role of CTCF in TAD boundary prediction.

Considering the significance of DNase I hypersensitive sites for TAD boundaries [14], which are well-represented across cell-lines and tissues, we conducted a similar analysis on DNase I hypersensitive site datasets. We identified DNase I hypersensitive sites with higher predictive abilities for both GC-rich and AT-rich boundaries, based on their SHAP values from the XGBoost model. We found that DNase I hypersensitive sites with peak scores higher than 200 and detected in more than 50 experiments were more predictive for both GC-rich and AT-rich datasets and they were applied for further training. Following the same procedure used for the CTCF dataset, SHAP values were computed exclusively on the training sets. In addition, models trained solely on DNase I hypersensitive sites also exhibited good performance, with AUC scores exceeding 0.72 for both models on their corresponding test sets (Supplementary Fig. S2D and E).

Subsequently, we utilized these selected high-quality CTCF and DNase I hypersensitive sites with other genomic elements instead of the unfiltered ones to train the model again using the same procedure as described previously. For both the GC-rich and AT-rich models, incorporating these selected high-quality CTCF and DNase I hypersensitive site features resulted in improved performance. The AUC scores obtained from test sets on both models increased by ~0.01. Further details of the performances are provided in Fig. 1.

We also trained our models using k-mer and repeat features, respectively. Each genomic region used as input was represented by a vector indicating the occurrences of different types of repeats or k-mers in that genomic region. Subsequently, the models were trained using XGBoost on the same five-partition dataset, and their performance was evaluated on the test set using the same metrics. Compared to the model trained on the high-quality genomic element dataset, the performances of these models trained on repeats and k-mers were inferior. For the model trained on k-mer features, the AUC scores on the test set for the GC-rich model and AT-rich model were 0.8589 and 0.6913, respectively. Meanwhile, for the model trained on repeat features, the AUC scores on test sets were 0.8064 and 0.6519 for the GC-rich model and AT-rich model, respectively. See detailed performance in Fig. 1.

Among these models trained on different types of features, all GC-rich models demonstrated good performance, with AUC scores higher than 0.8, indicating that genomic elements, k-mers, and repeats are strongly associated with GC-rich boundaries. However, the performance of the AT-rich models was lower compared to the GC-rich models, particularly for the model trained solely on the repeat dataset (AUC = 0.6519). This result suggests the presence of differences in TAD boundaries between active and inactive chromatin, indicating that factors beyond currently collected genomic elements, k-mers, and repeats also contribute to TAD formation in AT-rich regions.

To improve the performance of TAD boundary prediction, we incorporated all available features to train both the GC-rich and AT-rich models. Recognizing the importance of high-quality CTCF and DNase I hypersensitive sites for TAD boundaries, we opted to utilize the high-quality CTCF and DNase I hypersensitive sites with the highest SHAP values for model training, instead of the original datasets. Subsequent to training the models using all features with the XGBoost model, we evaluated their performance on the independent test sets. Compared to models trained on single type of features, both models exhibited notable enhancements in performance, with AUC scores reaching 0.9058 and 0.7983 for the GC-rich and AT-rich models, respectively. Although the accuracy score of the AT-rich model was slightly lower by 0.002 compared to the accuracy from the model trained solely on high-quality genomic elements, there was a clear improvement in the recall of the models by 0.01 and 0.05 for the GC-rich and AT-rich models, respectively. Given that DomainCaller [1] may overlook some boundaries, resulting in a more reliable positive dataset than the negative dataset, we prioritized selecting models that showed a significant increase in recall rather than a slight decrease in accuracy. Detailed performance is listed in Fig. 1. Moreover, the enhanced model performance achieved through feature integration suggests the complexity of TADs and TAD boundary regulation.

Identification of important features for TAD boundary prediction

To investigate the importance of features in TAD boundary prediction, we computed the SHAP values [33] for each feature using the model trained on all available features. Given that genomic elements tend to have a higher contribution compared to repeat and k-mer features, we split the feature importance accordingly based on their feature type. The top 10 most important features for each feature type, along with the comparison of SHAP values between GC-rich and AT-rich models, are illustrated in Fig. 2. Among these important features, some were critical for TAD boundary prediction in both classes, such as CTCF and mammalian-wide interspersed repeats (MIRs). However, other features were critical only for either GC-rich or AT-rich TAD boundary prediction. For example, the top 10 k-mers differed substantially between the 2 models, with only 2 overlapping k-mers identified as critical in both.

Although predictive power is not directly comparable to the enrichment [14], the important features extracted from computational models also reflect their importance for TAD boundaries. Among these genomic elements, CTCF emerges as the most crucial factor for both types of TAD boundaries. It is widely recognized that CTCF, along with the cohesin complex, plays a pivotal role in TAD formation through the loop extrusion model [35, 36]. ZNF143, a well-known transcriptional regulator, exhibited enrichment at AT-rich boundaries. However, recent studies have shown that ZNF143 does not participate in chromatin loop formation [37, 38]. Although less studied, ZNF444 shows strong enrichment at GC-rich boundaries. RAD21 is also important in both GC-rich and AT-rich models. It is a subunit of cohesin complex that contributes to TAD formation [39]. Similarly, TRIM22 also played important roles in both models. It has been reported as a potential loop-mediating protein enriched at chromatin loops [40, 41].

Among the repeat families, MIRs stood out as one of the most essential repeat families for both classes of boundaries. This observation agrees with the result of Dixon et al., that MIRs is depleted at TAD boundaries [1]. Furthermore, Wang et al. showed that MIRs can act as insulators independently of CTCF in different genomic contexts [42]. Additionally, it has been reported that a subset of hAT-Charlies, a type of transposable elements, contributed to loop anchor CTCF sites in humans [43], which might make them more frequently observed in TAD boundaries. While the relevance of CR1 to TADs or TAD boundaries remains unclear, its distribution around TAD boundaries suggests its depletion at TAD boundaries.

To investigate the regulatory relevance of these important k-mers, we compared their genomic coordinates with repeat elements, allowing a minimum overlap of 1 nt. Among the top 10 k-mers in the GC-rich model, all exhibited significant overlap with Alu elements compared with randomly selected sequences, whereas the top 10 k-mers in the AT-rich model showed significant overlap with L1 repeat elements (Supplementary Table S7). These repeat elements have previously been implicated in chromatin organization [44]. In addition, we performed motif analyses for these k-mers. Motifs were derived for each k-mer together with its ±20 nt flanking sequences using MEME (MEME Suite, version 5.5.7) with parameters mod = oops, minw = 3 and maxw = 10 [45]. The resulting motifs were then compared against the HOCOMOCO database (version 11) using Tomtom, also from the MEME Suite (version 5.5.7) [45]. The AATAC/GTATT k-mer matched the binding motif of the HMGA1 transcription factor (q-value = 3.24e-2), whose depletion may alter chromatin structure [46]. The ACCT/AGGT k-mer showed similarity to the binding motif of ZEB1, a zinc-finger homeodomain enhancer-binding protein (q-value = 4.68e-2). Other important k-mers could not be linked to any known motifs, highlighting the complexity and the potential involvement of novel elements in TAD boundary formation.

Comparison performance with other machine learning classifiers

We also trained the models using other machine learning methods, including AdaBoost, random forest, SVM, neural network, naive Bayes, logistic regression, and k-NN models, and compared their performance with the models trained by XGBoost. Since the performance improved through the integration of different types of features, we employed all available features to train these models and assessed their performance on the independent test sets in comparison to the XGBoost model. The comparative results revealed that XGBoost exhibited the best performance on both datasets. However, AdaBoost demonstrated comparable performance to XGBoost on the GC-rich dataset, while SVM exhibited comparable performance to XGBoost on the AT-rich dataset, with a marginal decrease in AUC by only 0.0001. Moreover, random forest and neural network models also presented good performance on both datasets, with their AUC scores decreasing by only <0.04 compared to the XGBoost model. Conversely, naive Bayes, logistic regression, and k-NN models exhibited markedly inferior performance, with the AUC score on the test sets dropping by up to 0.15. Although XGBoost did not outperform other models across all evaluation metrics, such as recall, which was slightly lower by 0.01 compared to the model with the highest recall (random forest for GC-rich and SVM/neural network for AT-rich), we ultimately selected XGBoost to train the final models due to its superior performance on both datasets. Detailed performance metrics are provided in Supplementary Fig. S3.

TADBpred improves TAD boundaries prediction by integrating heterogeneity into the model

We proposed TADBpred, a model for TAD boundary prediction, that accounts for the heterogeneity of TAD boundaries by integrating two submodels: the GC-rich and AT-rich models, trained on GC-rich and AT-rich datasets, respectively. TADBpred operates in two steps to predict whether a given 40 kb genomic region is involved in a TAD boundary. Initially, the model calculates the GC content of the given region. Then it selects the appropriate submodel for prediction based on the GC content. If the GC content is greater than or equal to 0.42, the GC-rich model is used; otherwise, the AT-rich model is applied. Here, the threshold of 0.42 was determined based on the clear separation in GC content between GC-rich and AT-rich boundaries (see details in the “Materials and methods” section).

Compared with other machine learning-based methods for TAD boundary prediction, TADBpred stands out as the first approach to consider the heterogeneity of TAD boundaries in its prediction model. To demonstrate the importance of incorporating this consideration, we trained a unified model on a combined GC-rich and AT-rich dataset using five-fold cross-validation and evaluated its performance on an independent test set. The high-quality CTCF and DNase I hypersensitive site datasets were selected based on SHAP values, which were calculated only from the training sets (Supplementary Fig. S2C and F). We then compared the predictive performance between the TADBpred (a two-step model) and the unified model, with or without consideration of TAD boundary heterogeneity, on separate GC-rich and AT-rich test sets. Incorporating heterogeneity yielded notable improvements: for the GC-rich test set, the AUC increased from 0.8976 to 0.9058, and for the AT-rich test set, from 0.7847 to 0.7983. All evaluation metrics showed improvement, particularly in the AT-rich dataset. Detailed performance comparisons are presented in Fig. 3B.

Performance of TADBpred model for predicting TAD boundaries with different GC contents. (A) Illustration of the TADBpred model with a two-step prediction process: it first calculates the GC content of the given region. If the GC content is greater than or equal to 0.42, it is predicted by the GC-rich model; otherwise, it is predicted by the AT-rich model. (B) Performance improved by accounting for TAD boundary heterogeneity; performance of GC-rich model and AT-rich model on GC-rich dataset (C) and AT-rich dataset (D) split by GC content, respectively. For the GC-rich model predicting AT-rich dataset and AT-rich model predicting GC-rich dataset, the confusion matrix is displayed in the upper right corner.

Given that the TADBpred model predicts different types of TAD boundaries mainly based on their GC content, we conducted an examination of the performance of TAD boundaries with varying GC content for both the GC-rich and AT-rich models. The performance evaluation was conducted on the same test set, which was further stratified into five equal-sized bins based on GC content distribution: (0, 20%), (20%, 40%), (40%, 60%), (60%, 80%), and (80%, 100%). By comparing the performance across datasets with different GC contents, we observed similar performance across the bins. For the GC-rich model, the AUC score ranged from 0.86 to 0.91, with the exception that the boundaries with lowest GC content achieving an AUC score of 0.97. For the AT-rich model, the AUC score ranged from 0.76 to 0.84. A similar pattern was also observed in other evaluation metrics. Detailed performance is shown in Fig. 3C and D. The results suggest that the GC-rich and AT-rich models are not sensitive to GC content variations. Additionally, when utilizing the GC-rich model to predict the AT-rich dataset, over 92% of boundaries were predicted as non-TAD boundaries (Fig. 3C and D). These comparison results underscore the meaningful separation between GC-rich and AT-rich boundaries, emphasizing the importance of considering the heterogeneity of TAD boundaries in TAD boundary prediction.

Cell-/tissue-specific TAD boundary prediction by TADBpred

Given the potential variability of TADs across cell-lines and tissues [21], we further evaluated the performance of TADBpred using TAD samples from individual cell-lines and tissues, even though the model was trained on a merged dataset derived from multiple cell-lines and tissues. In addition, TADBpred was trained using TADs annotated by DomainCaller. However, TAD definitions can differ among different TAD callers [11, 12]. To assess the generalization ability of TADBpred across different TAD callers, we evaluated its performance on TAD boundaries identified by InsulationScore. In total, 17 TAD boundary datasets from various cell-lines and tissues, identified using InsulationScore, were selected for performance evaluation.

To evaluate the performance of TADBpred across various cell-lines and tissues, we constructed independent test sets by systematically removing data points that were highly similar to those in the training set. Specifically, any data point exhibiting high similarity in feature representations to those in the training set, or whose genomic coordinates overlapped (≥1 nt) with training data points, was excluded. The detailed filtering procedures are described in the “Materials and methods” section. For prediction, each 40 kb genomic region was processed as follows: (i) its GC content was calculated; (ii) if the GC content was ≥0.42, the region was evaluated using the GC-rich model; otherwise, the AT-rich model was applied. Based on TAD boundaries annotated by InsulationScore, TADBpred demonstrated consistent performance across multiple cell-lines and tissues. For the GC-rich datasets, the AUC ranged between 0.64 and 0.70, whereas for the AT-rich datasets, they ranged between 0.70 and 0.83 (Fig. 4).

Performance of TADBpred prediction across various cell-lines and tissues on independent test sets identified by InsulationScore.

In addition, we conducted a benchmarking analysis with existing methods for TAD boundary prediction that do not rely on Hi-C data, including HubPredictor [13] and TAD-Lactuca [15]. These methods were selected for benchmarking because they were trained using TAD annotations from DomainCaller, ensuring a fair comparison, as TAD annotations can differ between TAD callers [12]. Since both HubPredictor [13] and TAD-Lactuca [15] depend on ChIP-Seq data for histone modifications and CTCF binding sites, we collected cell-specific histone modification and CTCF datasets from ENCODE [30] for evaluation. Given the limited availability of histone modification data for certain cell-lines, we selected GM12878, H1-hESC, and IMR90 cell-lines for the benchmarking analysis. We trained their models using the code and datasets provided in their respective GitHub repositories and evaluated their performance on independent test sets based on TAD boundaries identified by InsulationScore. Compared with HubPredictor and TAD-Lactuca, TADBpred achieved higher performance on independent test sets, particularly for AT-rich regions. For GC-rich regions, TADBpred achieved AUC scores ranging from 0.65 to 0.70, outperforming TAD-Lactuca (AUC 0.60–0.67) and HubPredictor (AUC <0.50). For AT-rich regions, TADBpred achieved AUC values between 0.76 and 0.83, followed by TAD-Lactuca (0.53–0.66) and HubPredictor (0.43–0.52). Detailed performance is illustrated in Fig. 5.

Benchmarking analysis of TADBpred against existing Hi-C free methods for TAD boundary prediction on independent test sets identified by InsulationScore.

Several other machine learning-based methods have been developed for TAD boundary prediction, but they were not included in the benchmarking analysis due to differences in TAD definitions and species focus. TADs identified by different TAD callers may not be directly comparable [47], making benchmarking with pTADS [17] and nTDP [20] infeasible, since these methods were trained on TADs identified by TADlib [48] and Armatus [49], respectively. Methods such as TADBoundaryDectector [16] and StackTADB [19] focus on non-cell-/tissue-specific sequence information but were designed for fruit flies rather than humans, the different TAD structures between humans and fruit flies rendering them incompatible for benchmarking with TADBpred. Furthermore, several tools aim to predict the Hi-C interaction frequency matrix directly from DNA sequences. These tools, such as Akita [50] and DeepC [51], require the use of TAD callers like DomainCaller or InsulationScore to identify TAD boundaries from the predicted interaction matrices. Because their objectives differ from those of TADBpred, they were not included in the conducted benchmarking analysis.

Enrichment of CTCF and histone modification at TAD boundaries

Since many genomic elements are enriched at TAD boundaries (±500 kb), such as CTCF and various histone modifications [12], we performed an enrichment analysis using ChIP-Seq datasets to better understand the biological relevance of TADBpred predictions. Specifically, we compared the enrichment patterns of CTCF and histone modifications around TAD boundaries predicted by TADBpred or identified by DomainCaller and InsulationScore. Because these ChIP-Seq datasets are cell- or tissue-specific, we performed the enrichment analysis separately for individual cell-lines and tissues. To minimize potential effects of different Hi-C datasets on the enrichment analysis, we used TAD annotations from the GM12878 cell-line derived from [34], of which both processed TADs identified by DomainCaller and InsulationScore are available. Based on the TAD annotations and ChIP-Seq data, we calculated the distribution of CTCF and histone modifications around TAD boundaries within a 1 Mb (±500 kb) window, partitioned into non-overlapping 5 kb bins. Compared with DomainCaller and InsulationScore, TADBpred-predicted boundaries exhibited similar enrichment or depletion patterns (Supplementary Fig. S4). However, the magnitude of enrichment or depletion was lower than that observed for DomainCaller and InsulationScore, likely because TADBpred does not directly rely on histone modification information for TAD boundary prediction.

In addition, upon examination of the prediction results, we found that TADBpred can predict negative examples (non-TAD boundaries) with a high probability (>0.8 or even >0.9) to be TAD boundaries. The probability derived from the XGBoost model ranges from 0 to 1, indicating the confidence that a given sample belongs to a TAD boundary. A probability close to 1 indicates that the region is predicted to be a TAD boundary with high confidence. The false positive rate for both GC-rich and AT-rich regions was ~20% (GC-rich: 51/261; AT-rich: 83/396). To understand why TADBpred identifies these negative regions as potential TAD boundaries, we examined several examples through the Hi-C interaction matrix from GM12878 cell-line [34] by the 3D Genome Browser [26]. Fig. 6A and B show the examples with detailed Hi-C interaction frequency matrix from GC-rich dataset and AT-rich dataset, with the probability of being a TAD boundary 0.98 and 0.93, respectively. In these examples, both “negative” regions, which were not identified as TAD boundaries by DomainCaller [1], exhibit strong insulation properties between neighboring regions, suggesting that they should be considered as TAD boundaries. Through the examination of two examples, we observe that DomainCaller [1] sometimes ignores some TAD boundaries from sub-TADs nested inside others. To verify this, we extracted these negative regions from DomainCaller dataset predicted as TAD boundaries by TADBpred and compared them with TAD boundary datasets identified by InsulationScore. A total of 92% (76/83) of AT-rich regions and 82% (42/51) of GC-rich regions overlapped (≥1 nt) with TAD boundaries identified by InsulationScore. Compared with an equal number of random genomic regions, the observed overlaps with InsulationScore boundaries were significantly higher than expected by chance (one-sided Fisher’s exact test: GC-rich, P-value = 3.63e-3; AT-rich, P-value = 3.40e-7). Therefore, our method, TADBpred, acts as a valuable complement to interaction frequency matrix-based methods in identifying such missed TAD boundaries.

Despite the promising results, some limitations remain in this work. (1) TADBpred was designed to predict TAD boundaries across cell-lines and tissues. However, the existence of cell- or tissue-specific TAD boundaries highlights the need for models capable of accurately predicting such boundaries. Integrating cell- or tissue-specific information and employing advanced machine learning strategies (e.g. transfer learning) may improve prediction accuracy for specific biological contexts. In addition, the lack of clustered histone modification datasets across cell-lines and tissues (similar to the clustered TFBS datasets collected from the UCSC Genome Browser) prevented us from integrating histone modification features into our prediction model. This could be considered in future work when predictions are performed for an individual cell-line or tissue. (2) TADBpred currently considers only the diversity of TAD boundaries between active and inactive chromatin when implementing the machine learning-based prediction model. TAD boundaries can also differ in other aspects. For example, TADs with and without dots have been reported to correspond to different replication timings [24]. Since TADs with or without dots may shift during development, this type of diversity was not considered in the current stage but will be explored in future work. (3) TADBpred focuses solely on predicting TAD boundaries in humans. However, TAD structures vary substantially across species [52]. Currently, most processed TAD datasets are derived from humans. As more high-quality TAD annotations from other species become available, the model could be adapted or extended to enable cross-species TAD boundary prediction. (4) The model might be further improved by incorporating state-of-the-art deep learning architectures, such as long short-term memory networks or pre-trained large language models, which have shown good performance in tasks involving long-range DNA sequences [53–56]. However, the current model is based on tabular features, including k-mer counts extracted from DNA sequences and features derived from epigenetic datasets. For such feature representations, non-deep-learning approaches such as XGBoost can also achieve good performance [57]. An alternative strategy would be to model DNA sequences directly. Nevertheless, the current dataset, with a 40 kb resolution, limits the feasibility of applying such architectures in our present model. In addition, future work could include an evaluation of model robustness, for example through adversarial testing, by introducing controlled Gaussian noise. (5) While we identified several genomic features important for TAD boundary prediction, it remains unclear whether these features are causal factors in TAD formation. For example, CTCF was identified as the most important TFBS, consistent with previous reports [58]. However, ZNF143 also appeared to be important in our model, despite recent studies showing that ZNF143 is not directly involved in chromatin looping [37, 38]. In addition, recent experimental evidence indicates that CTCF depletion is not always sufficient to alter gene expression within TADs [59]. Further functional experiments are still needed to elucidate the biological roles of these factors in TAD formation.

In conclusion, we proposed a machine learning-based TADBpred model for TAD boundary prediction. Compared with other TAD boundary prediction methods, our model is the first to consider the heterogeneity of TAD boundaries, particularly those prevalent in active and inactive chromatin regions. Moreover, our analysis of feature importance, particularly regarding unannotated k-mers, offers valuable insights into the mechanisms underlying TAD formation. Additionally, TADBpred demonstrates an ability to identify boundaries that may be overlooked by DomainCaller [1]. This underscores the role of TADBpred in complementing TAD boundary annotation methods reliant on Hi-C interaction frequency matrices.
