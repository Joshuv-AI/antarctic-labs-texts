Policy Progress in Using Economic Policy Instruments to Improve Nutrition: Challenges and Opportunities
Authors: Anne Marie Thow
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEconomic policy can be a powerful instrument to influence food environments and support improved diets and health. Over the past 20 years, the use of fiscal policy to improve diets has increased dramatically. This paper reviews the trajectory of policy change, and explores the ways in which research has informed three different dimensions of economic policy change in relation to nutrition: policy paradigms, policy instruments and policy settings. The increasing use of fiscal policy contrasts to more limited use of other economic policy instruments, including trade policy, to shape food environments for improved nutrition. Insights from the case studies explored in this paper indicate the important role of research in supporting policy change, including through generating evidence that speaks to sectoral needs and priorities, which can enable policy makers to identify and support potential synergies, as well as providing evidence from real‐world evaluations of policy measures. The case studies also highlight the importance of constructive engagement between nutrition and economic policy makers. Finally, the paper identifies three considerations for research moving forward related to policy innovation, knowledge equity, and gender.

Introduction

The environments in which we make decisions about what we eat have a major influence on what our diets look like. These environments include grocery stores, informal food retail, fast food outlets, social events, workplaces, and our homes. Unhealthy food environments that don't support access to nourishing, affordable food work against improvements to nutrition and health (Pineda, Stockton, et al. 2024b). The world is not on track to achieve the Sustainable Development Goal for “Zero Hunger” (FAO et al. 2024). By 2030, it is projected that 1.2 billion adults will be obese (FAO et al. 2024). Diet‐related risk factors are among the top 5 contributors to deaths globally (Vizhub 2025). Ischemic heart disease alone accounts for 13% of total deaths, and 1 in 9 adults are living with diabetes (IDF 2025). Although recent pharmaceutical developments—most notably Glucagon‐like peptide 1 receptor agonists—have raised questions about the need for dietary intervention to improve health, there has been limited success in shifting risk factors at a population level using these approaches (Pineda et al. 2026). For much of the world, access to pharmaceutical intervention to improve diet‐related health remains highly unequal (Mozaffarian 2024; Spinelli and Oakes 2025).

As a result, there is an urgent need for change to food environments that improves nutrition. Policy action is one critical lever to create such change. Policy is a means to create incentives and supports for behavior—policy creates a framework for action, that translates a societal vision for the future, into the real world (Cabinet Office 1999). However, policy change to improve nutrition has proved challenging globally. Although there has been growing adoption of increasingly strong and innovative policy measures—including fiscal policy, front‐of‐pack labeling and marketing restrictions—these measures have consistently faced opposition from economic interests, within government and the private sector.

This paper is based on the opening keynote given at the International Health Economics Association conference in July 2025. It aims to review learnings from experience with economic policy change for nutrition and to shed light on the role of research in fostering change in all three of these dimensions of policy: in the vision underlying policy; in the actions or content of policy decisions; and in the “translational” function of policy—in other words, in the way in which these decisions seek to turn a vision into action. It then concludes with reflections on opportunities to further strengthen future research.

Using Economic Policy to Improve Diet‐Related Health

The policy changes that can help to address the outcomes of poor nutrition are becoming increasingly clear. The World Health Organization's Acceleration Plan to Stop Obesity, launched in 2023, identified a suite of “best‐practice” policy measures to address obesity and diet‐related non‐communicable diseases or NCDs, with a set of priority measures identified for urgent action. The majority of these policy measures seek to create healthier food environments, that support and incentivize consumers to choose healthier options. These include school food policies, fiscal policies, front‐of‐pack labeling, regulation to protect children from unhealthy food marketing, and upstream incentives that shape food systems.

This paper focusses particularly on the economic policy measures that are recommended to address diet‐related NCDs, because economic policy is a critical component of recommendations to improve diets and health. It shapes upstream incentives related to availability and affordability of food. The paper begins with a detailed case study of fiscal policy to explore factors that both enable and hinder economic policy change for nutrition and NCD prevention. Although this case study is focussed on only one type of policy measure, it allows exploration of some of the broader issues at the interface between economic and health policy. This interface between economic and health policy is also critical to address issues related to health services, pharmaceutical policy, other aspects of NCD prevention like tobacco control, and many others—and thus these reflections also have wider resonance.

Fiscal Policy to Improve Diets and Health

The WHO formally recommended fiscal policy measures for diets and health for the first time in 2004, in the global strategy on diet, physical activity and health (WHO 2004). At the time, there was almost no evidence to support this recommendation—which drew heavily on experience in tobacco control. The first case study of implemented taxes, conducted in 2008, identified only four taxes that had an explicit aim to improve nutrition—taxes on sugar sweetened beverages (SSBs), and some types of confectionary, in Samoa, Nauru, French Polynesia and Fiji (Thow, Quested, et al. 2011b).

Nearly 20 years later, the situation is vastly different. A World Bank analysis from 2023 found 105 national‐level SSB taxes, many of which include a specific health‐related aim (Hattersley and Mandeville 2023; World Bank Group 2023). At least 15 countries now have broader taxes specific to unhealthy foods. In 2024, the WHO released a formal WHO guideline on the use of fiscal policies to promote healthy diets, that included a strong evidence based recommendation for the adoption of SSB taxes, and also conditional recommendations for taxes on unhealthy foods and subsidies for healthy foods (WHO 2024). Fiscal policies to improve nutrition are now also supported in the economic policy sphere. In 2025, the United Nations Tax Committee approved a Handbook on Health Taxes, to support fiscal policy makers to further scale up implementation of health taxes, including SSB taxes and other nutrition taxes (ECOSOC 2025).

Reflecting on this global uptake of the use of taxes to improve nutrition can give us insights into what engenders policy change, and enable us to learn from experience to support strong policy action in the future. The political scientist Peter Hall describes three orders of policy change that characterize decision‐making in public policy (Hall 1993). The first order of policy change relates to settings. In other words, the details of the design of a policy, that enables it to achieve an objective. The second order refers to the selection of an appropriate policy instrument. In this case—the use of taxes. The third order focusses on paradigmatic level changes in the way that governments understand their role and remit.

Third‐Order Change Related to Nutrition Taxation

The wide‐spread, deliberate use of fiscal policy to improve nutrition over the past 20 years required a third order shift—changes in perceptions of what governments can and should do, which have underpinned more specific design considerations. The use of taxes to achieve broader social policy objectives is well established, particularly where market failure is concerned. Recognition of the “market failures” that justify the use of fiscal policy for nutrition has increased over the past 20 years, as more evidence has emerged regarding the health harms described above. Three key aspects of market failure associated with unhealthy diets are externalities, internalities and information asymmetries (Lauer et al. 2023; Thow et al. 2018). The uptake of taxes has been supported by recognition of the appropriateness of using fiscal policy to help address such market failures.

But there is another relevant paradigmatic question, of whether it's appropriate for governments to deliberately seek to modify food prices to try to incentivize healthier food choices and better health outcomes. Food prices are a sensitive issue with political implications (Thow 2024). To talk about taxing food, an essential and daily requirement for human life, raises strong feelings. Unlike tobacco, which kills half of all those who use it as intended, consuming even unhealthy food is better than consuming nothing. These are still very real questions in our current context. In 2023, around three‐quarters of a billion people were undernourished, with certain groups disproportionately disadvantaged: including women, farmworkers, migrants and Indigenous peoples (FAO et al. 2024; HLPE 2023). Malnutrition has risen since 2019, associated with disruption to food systems globally arising from COVID 19 and climatic events over the past few years, and exacerbated by regional conflicts (FAO et al. 2024). This food system disruption is contributing to the broader economic policy challenges of inflation and cost‐of‐living, and reduced affordability of foods due to lower incomes. Healthy diets are unaffordable for large segments of the world population. The UN Food and Agriculture Organization reports that more than a third of the global population cannot afford a healthy diet (FAO et al. 2024).

As a result, addressing equity considerations has been critical in supporting uptake of taxes. Equity impacts depend on the extent to which consumers reduce their consumption of the taxed foods (Grummon et al. 2019). Low income households tend to be more responsive to changes in food and beverage prices, resulting in a minimal tax burden together with relatively higher gains for health, medical costs and economic productivity (Fuchs and Pierola 2022). For example, recent extended cost‐benefit analyses of SSB taxation in Kazakhstan and Ukraine found that SSB taxes tend to benefit the poorest households the most and can have a progressive impact in the medium‐ and long‐run (taking into account health benefits including the associated increase in lifetime income and reduced health care costs) (Fuchs et al. 2020). Evidence indicates that food taxation is one of the few food environment interventions that may reduce socioeconomic inequalities in diets (Løvhaug et al. 2022). Complementing taxation with subsidies on healthier foods, including lowering tax rates on healthy alternatives or introducing consumer subsidies, can also help to address affordability concerns associated with taxation. Consumer subsidies on fruit and vegetables, in particular, have been found to be effective (Andreyeva, Marple, Moore, and Powell 2022).

A third paradigmatic aspect underpinning the uptake of nutrition related taxes has been perceptions of how they impact on industry. A primary mandate of fiscal policy makers is to ensure that the tax framework supports business activity, rather than creating unnecessary red tape. Nutrition‐related taxes face a particular challenge as they apply—at least potentially—to a significant sector of the economy. In Indonesia, for example, the food and beverage sector contributes more than 7% of total GDP, and is growing rapidly (Visi Globalindo Data Utama, P. C. 2024). The agriculture, fisheries and forestry sector contributes over a third of total employment, and more than 14% of GDP (ACIAR 2025). Industry opposition to nutrition‐related taxes has been strong and consistent, globally. The main arguments against nutrition‐related taxes relate to their potential negative economic impact on industry, particularly in relation to employment. Industry also argues that taxes will face problems with cross‐border shopping or compliance, resulting in unfair impacts on domestic industries. An SSB tax in Fiji was rescinded after only 1 year due to industry concerns (Thow, Quested, et al. 2011b). In South Africa, industry successfully lobbied for a halving of the proposed tax, and have successfully prevented a tax increase despite evidence for health impacts, and no evidence of industry harms (Abdool Karim et al. 2020). Concerns regarding industry impacts reflect experience with tobacco control. The tobacco industry has consistently argued that increasing tobacco taxes will lead to a loss of jobs and revenue due to declining smoking rates, despite evidence to the contrary (World Health Organization Regional Office for Europe 2019).

It is true that nutrition related taxes may have some short‐term and sector‐specific impacts related to employment. But there is little evidence to support any negative macro‐economic impacts from SSB or unhealthy food taxes (Mounsey et al. 2020). Real‐world evaluations found no impact on employment in the manufacturing industry in Mexico following the introduction of the energy‐dense food tax (Guerrero‐López et al. 2017). Modeling studies have shown minimal impacts, and in some cases net positive impacts to the economy (Mounsey et al. 2020). These positive impacts can arise because money not spent on unhealthy foods and beverages is often spent on other goods and services. Services, in particular, are a more employment‐intensive sector of the economy (Powell et al. 2014). Evidence that has helped policy makers understand potential economic impacts has been critical in supporting global uptake of nutrition related taxes.

Second‐Order Change Related to Nutrition Taxation

Second order policy change relates to decisions about the instrument. In the 20 years since 2004, uptake of nutrition ‐related taxes has been supported by increasing clarity on what to tax. We have gained a better understanding of the contribution of nutrition to diet‐related NCDs. For example, research into SSBs, particularly in the mid‐late 2000s, showed an independent association between SSB consumption and weight gain, and also between SSB consumption and diabetes risk (Hu 2013; Hu and Malik 2010; Malik et al. 2013; Richelsen 2013). This new evidence provided a strong basis for singling out SSBs as a relatively straightforward object of taxation. This ability to single out a single product was important, because it also provided a straightforward focus for policy proposals, and as a non‐essential product had lower equity implications.

Many of the early recommendations and attempts to tax foods and beverages had a broad focus—such as the fat tax in Denmark (Jensen et al. 2016). This tax was applied based on the content of saturated fat, associated with heart disease risk, and applied to meat, dairy and processed foods. The tax thus raised consumer food prices across a wide range of products, including some core foods that are recommended for consumption in formal government dietary guidelines. This policy incoherence—where governments are both recommending consumption of a food as part of a healthy diet and taxing it—creates mixed messages and leaves a fiscal policy measure vulnerable. Even though in the long term the Denmark tax was shown to be effective in incentivizing consumers to shift toward foods lower in saturated fat, it was repealed due to pressure from industry and consumer concern regarding affordability of a healthy diet. In contrast, SSBs have no nutritional benefits—and even worse, often displace the consumption of healthier beverages (Doherty et al. 2020).

For food taxes, there has been slowly growing clarity on how to identify appropriate foods for taxation that do not apply to core foods. Innovations in the application of nutrient profile modeling, in particular, is supporting a rise in unhealthy food taxes. For example, Colombia recently introduced a tax on processed and ultra‐processed food products that exceed thresholds for sugar, sodium, or saturated fat within selected categories (Cadena et al. 2025).

Increasing uptake of the use of tax as an instrument was also enabled by increasing evidence of their impact on health. As mentioned, there was very little evidence for the initial 2004 WHO policy “suggestion” for fiscal policy. The first systematic review on the effect of fiscal policy on diet, obesity and chronic disease, published in 2010 identified only 24 studies—only about half of which had been peer‐reviewed. Many of the studies were low quality, examining hypothetical taxes and using price elasticity estimates with limitations (Thow, Jan, et al. 2010). In 2022, the systematic reviews that underpinned the formal WHO guideline identified 54 high quality studies of implemented food taxes and subsidies, and 86 articles on sugar sweetened beverages (Andreyeva, Marple, Marinello, et al. 2022; Andreyeva, Marple, Moore, and Powell. 2022). In marked contrast to the situation in 2010, we now have strong, consistent evidence that SSB taxes raise prices and reduce sales of taxed beverages (Andreyeva, Marple, Marinello, et al. 2022; Teng et al. 2019). Experience to date indicates the average pass‐through rate of SSB taxes is 82%, meaning that most of the tax tends to be passed on to consumers in the form of higher retail prices (Andreyeva, Marple, Marinello, et al. 2022). Consumer demand for SSBs tends to be highly sensitive to tax‐induced price increases, with an average estimated price elasticity of demand of −1.59, meaning that a 1% increase in price leads to a reduction in consumption of just over one and half percent. The situation is similar for unhealthy food taxes. Two recent systematic reviews found statistically significant increases in the price and decreases in the sales of taxed products following the introduction of taxes (Andreyeva, Marple, Moore, and Powell 2022; Pineda, Gressier, et al. 2024a).

There is less evidence on more long‐term effects of SSB and food taxes. The UK Soft Drink Industry Levy was associated with an overall 8% relative reduction in obesity levels in girls (Rogers et al. 2023). Mexico's SSB tax was associated with a 1.3% point decrease in overweight or obesity prevalence among adolescent girls within the first 2 years (Gracner et al. 2022). A critical issue has been understanding consumer response—particularly how substitution of taxed products occurs. In relation to SSB taxation, there is little evidence to suggest that consumers substitute SSB consumption by shifting to untaxed beverages (Andreyeva, Marple, Marinello, et al. 2022). However, substitution becomes a much greater concern with unhealthy food taxes. The risk of substitution to untaxed unhealthy items varies based on product availability and the scope and overall design of the tax. Substitution can also be influenced by the tax mechanism. At the moment, governments use a range of tax mechanisms. Specific excise taxes are the preferred instrument for nutrition‐related taxes. In particular, using a specific rather than volumetric tax ensures that cheaper products aren't taxed proportionally less than more expensive products. If a tax is based on the price of the product, the gap between more expensive products and cheaper ones will get larger. This can encourage substitutions to cheaper products, rather than the desired decrease in consumption or shift to healthier products. There is also emerging evidence for longer‐term productivity impacts from nutrition‐related taxes. For example, two modeled studies from Australia indicated significant productivity gains from hypothetical nutrition related taxes: a 20% tax on sugared sweetened drinks was estimated to result in productivity gains equivalent to 1.9% of total annual health expenditure in 2010 (Nomaguchi et al. 2017), and a junk food tax was estimated to avert sufficient premature deaths to account for over 8000 additional working years (Carter et al. 2019).

First‐Order Change Related to Nutrition Taxation

The specific settings of a tax tend to be much more straightforward to change—and as a result, first order change is the point at which we see much more learning and adaptation. An interesting example is the evolution and regional learning that we saw in the European region over the past couple of decades. Many countries in the region introduced SSB taxes relatively early, but have continued to refine and strengthen them, learning from other countries experience. For example, development of a tiered structure based on different sugar content for the UK soft drink levy inspired the change to a tiered tax structure in France and Norway (Thow, Rippin, et al. 2022b).

Examining settings also gives us insights into the importance of the objective in shaping the specific design of the tax. In the UK, the use of tiers was designed to achieve the specific policy goal of reducing sugar, rather than beverage, intakes. Evaluation showed that the volume of sugars sold per capita per day from soft drinks declined by 30% and the mean sugar content of soft drinks fell from five and a half to just under four g per 100 (Bandy et al. 2020). The decision to focus on sugar content in the UK, and subsequent tiered taxes, also resulted in an increase in beverages sweetened with non‐sugar sweeteners, as many industry actors responded by reducing sugar in beverages, and “topping them up” with non‐sugar sweeteners.

This shift highlights one of the ongoing challenges for the “settings” of SSB taxation. There is currently no clear guidance on whether non‐sugar sweeteners should be included in nutrition‐related taxes. On the one hand, excluding these sweeteners from taxes may help to reduce sugar consumption, by encouraging substitution and incentivizing reformulation. Excluding diet drinks may also lower industry opposition to a tax because this allows more scope for reformulation and new product development (Thow, Rippin, et al. 2022b). On the other hand, the WHO recently advised against using artificial sweeteners for weight control and NCD prevention (WHO 2023). One of the most widely used artificial sweeteners, aspartame, has also been classified as “possibly carcinogenic” (Riboli et al. 2023).

It is at the level of first order change that we can often also see most clearly the trade‐offs inherent in policy design. For taxes, these tradeoffs are often between the health objective of the measure, and other outcomes that are important to the finance sector. One trade off that must be considered is between health impacts and revenue generation. Relatively lower taxes tend to generate revenue more consistently over the long term, because they incentivize behavior change less, which means they therefore have a smaller health impact. In contrast, high taxes may have more substantial impacts on consumption and incentivize substitution to untaxed products, leading to less revenue being generated. Where revenue is in some way linked to public investments in health, the public and also politicians are much more likely to support taxes. For example, revenue from nutrition‐related taxes in French Polynesia supported broad‐ranging preventive health initiatives, which benefited the population but also contributed to the objectives of seven different Ministries and so supported political acceptability of the tax (Thow, Heywood, et al. 2011a).

Overall, these improvements in evidence and understanding of taxes across all three orders of policy have fostered uptake of fiscal policy for nutrition.

Trade Policy for Nutrition

As a contrast to the relatively impressive uptake of taxes, I now want to turn to the use of economic policy instruments for nutrition, more broadly. Given the complexity of nutrition problems, there is significant potential for diverse economic policies to address different aspects of the food environment. The 2004 Global Strategy on Diet, Physical Activity and Health also included recommendations to consider economic policies other than fiscal policies, including agriculture, industrial and trade policies in efforts to improve nutrition (WHO 2004). However, in contrast to fiscal policy what we have seen in the intervening 20 years is fairly limited use of agricultural and trade policies for NCD prevention.

The policy innovation that has occurred with respect to these upstream incentives, has been undertaken in countries that aren't often considered in policy learning. I want to highlight the Pacific Island region in particular. The huge burden of non‐communicable diseases in this region—which is known for having the world's highest prevalence of obesity, and was one of the first developing regions to experience the epidemic of diet‐related NCDs. As a result, the region has been a site for innovation in the use of economic policies for nutrition. As I mentioned earlier, the Pacific Island Countries were very early innovators in the use of fiscal policy to promote health. Similarly, in the African region, there has been innovative use of standards, and the intentional use of agricultural subsidies to target nutrient‐rich foods. An example of second order change from both regions is action on the dumping of cheap fatty meat, linked to concerns about cardiovascular disease risk, in Ghana, Samoa and Fiji. In Samoa, a ban on the importation of turkey tails, a commonly consumed high‐fat food, was implemented in 2007 in response to concerns regarding high rates of NCDs (Thow, Jan, et al. 2010). A survey by the nutrition department in the Ministry of Health in 2008 found that in response to the ban about a quarter substituted healthier options such as fish and less than half of consumers switched to other cheap, albeit less fatty, meats (Thow, Swinburn, et al. 2010).

The reorientation of agricultural incentives has similarly been seen to improve nutrition outcomes. For example, the Malawi input subsidy program focussed on maize and legumes led to improvements in dietary diversity and child nutrition (Khonje et al. 2022; Sarpong 2022). However, there is little evidence of significant uptake of recommendations to use such measures for health. The commodities receiving the highest levels of support globally are rice, maize, sugar and meats (FAO et al. 2022).

In relation to the potential for third order change regarding the use of upstream economic policy for nutrition, there are some indications that conceptualizations of the purpose of trade and agricultural policy are beginning to change. The 2022 Flagship report on the State of Agricultural Commodity Markets from the UN Food and Agriculture Organization collated the diverse evidence base on trade and nutrition and included significant new data analysis by the FAO (FAO 2024). This report represented a shift in the paradigm regarding who can contribute to supporting improved nutrition, as it was led by agricultural economists, and was then showcased by the World Trade Organization at their 2024 Annual Agriculture Symposium, which focussed on trade and nutrition. This report showed clearly that trade liberalization has been associated with increased availability and affordability of ultra‐processed foods, and as such, that trade policy makers should be considering nutrition as an outcome. Similarly, the recent World Bank and FAO publications on the potential to repurpose agricultural subsidies globally to create better incentives for healthier and more sustainable food systems have started to expand the purpose of agricultural policy in fundamental ways (FAO et al. 2022; World Bank 2024).

Insights Regarding Research for Policy Change

These case studies—both of change and of limited or partial change—offer insights into how policy change happens in this context. This in turn offers insights into the role that research can play in supporting policy change, in relation to informing policy design, guiding the instruments chosen, and providing insights into the paradigms underlying policy making.

First, evidence that speaks to sectoral needs and priorities can enable policy makers to identify and support potential synergies. This includes evidence to address industry opposition, which usually includes concerns about potential economic impacts and consumer impacts. We can't talk about policy making without talking about political economy—including interests and power. Industry‐funded research often muddies the waters when it comes to evidence for strong health policy measures (Cullerton et al. 2024).

Second, evidence for impact on real people and real businesses is critical. In this context, evidence from real‐world evaluations has been powerful in shaping change. Research to identify innovative solutions is essential to tackle nutrition and diet‐related NCDs. As I mentioned, many of the innovations in policy have not been evaluated, limiting the potential for their scale up.

Finally, these case studies point to the importance of constructive engagement between nutrition and economic policy makers. Forums and incentives for ongoing engagement and mutual exchange between policy makers in different sectors, that is supported by institutional structures is critical. For example, the participation of Ministry of Health staff in Samoa's WTO accession committee ensured that public health was protected and promoted in their accession agreement (Thow et al. 2017). The implementation of good governance mechanisms will be critical for scaling up economic policy action to improve nutrition; in particular, by ensuring that opposition by industry is made visible and that government has tools to manage vested interests (Friel et al. 2023; Patay et al. 2023; Thow et al. 2025). In the context of tobacco control, research has played a role in supporting the development of anti‐lobbying measures and conflict‐of‐interest policies to limit industry influence, and there is significant scope to scale up similar research for nutrition (Gilmore et al. 2025).

Considerations for Future Research

Although there has been progress on policy change at the intersection between economic policy and nutrition, challenges remain. This final section outlines three considerations for research moving forward, to strengthen policy for health.

The first consideration is for research to examine how broader economic policy innovations can be designed to benefit health. As I mentioned earlier, economic policy instruments beyond fiscal policy also shape individual and industry incentives across multiple aspects of health. Economic policy is thus a key area in which to encourage innovation. A challenge arises when a narrow focus on evidence‐based policy inadvertently undermines the policy innovation that we so urgently need to address health challenges. We know we haven't solved these complex challenges, and that we need innovation; but if we recommend that governments only take policy actions that we have cost‐effectiveness evidence for, how will we encourage policy makers to try something new?

In the current global economic policy context, trade rules are being re‐written daily, agreement on tax policy reforms remains challenging, and entrenched interests continue to oppose changes to food and health policies. This is a critical time for us as a research community to be exploring innovative ways for economic policy to create meaningful incentives for public health. As an example, there is increasing global interest in new industrial policy agendas that seek to incentivize industry activity to support sustainable development (Aiginger and Rodrik 2020). There is new evidence that indicates that industrial policy can be effective in generating desired changes (Juhász et al. 2023). But at the moment, there is no indication that health is being considered in industrial policy making. So the potential to integrate health into upstream economic incentives that are shaping nutrition, for example, is being completely missed.

The second consideration relates to knowledge equity. The roots of colonization run deep, and continue to shape research in fundamental ways, including containing and constraining the types of knowledge that we value—often in relatively invisible ways. Knowledge equity refers to equitably valuing diverse, and particularly Indigenous, ways of being [ontology], knowing [epistemology], and doing [methodology] (Abimbola et al. 2024; Sebastian and Giovanangeli 2024; Shiva 1990). It is essential for fair and just policymaking. It is also essential to underpin the innovation and effectiveness of policy that we so desperately need. In his recent book, The Foreign Gaze, Abimbola writes insightfully about being wronged as a knower (Abimbola 2024). This concept relates to the ways in which “knowledge” can be narrowly defined, in such a way as to discount certain sources of knowledge, or certain groups of people who hold knowledge. In nutrition‐related fiscal policy research, addressing knowledge equity and historical legacies of colonization can enable researchers to examine issues related to trust in public institutions, incorporate equity in meaningful ways (Kisselgoff et al. 2023), interrogate ideas and paradigms that perpetuate inequalities (McCoy et al. 2023), and constructively challenge conceptions of when and how vested interests may influence research (Mialon 2023). Examining fiscal policy through a decolonizing lens can also provide new perspectives on the ways in which decolonization can support fiscal capacity and fiscal autonomy of governments (Dharmapala and Suesse 2025; Macnaughton and Ahmed 2023).

There is a challenge—and an opportunity—for us to critically engage with the evidence base in our research fields, and to ask ourselves not just what we know, but who we consider as having knowledge. And then to ask how we can support decolonizing and post‐colonial research. This means looking hard at the practice of our research, and being vulnerable enough to ask others how we can practice better. It means pushing ourselves out of our comfortable theoretical spaces, and reconceptualizing how we conceive of knowledge and who holds it.

The third consideration is for us to think about how we make issues related to gender visible—or invisible—in our research. Selma Mushkin's seminal 1962 paper is an insightful and inspiring work that helped establish the field of health economics (Mushkin 1962). But the way that informal care—which is of course a heavily gendered issue—was described and (not) addressed typifies the often limited consideration in our research of the ways in which gender intersects with economies, cultures and societies. There has been significant progress in this area; for example, detailed consideration of the contribution of informal care in cost‐effectiveness analysis for healthcare interventions (de Groot et al. 2023). But women are still disproportionately impacted by malnutrition and women's health issues are severely underfunded (WEF 2025). Seventy‐five million years of life are lost due to poor health or early deaths of women each year (VaccinesWork 2024). Closing the gap could boost the global economy by $1 trillion by 2040 from fewer deaths and health conditions (WEF 2025). It is critical that we look actively for ways to foreground gender in our research. In nutrition‐related fiscal policy research, foregrounding gender can shed new light on vulnerabilities and local context to support policy that meaningfully addresses inequalities (Mawia et al. 2025; Vercillo et al. 2023). It is critical for us as researchers to be mindful of who is or could be rendered invisible in our research findings, or in our research practice, and take steps to make them visible.

Conclusion

We as researchers have an opportunity to design research that speaks to multiple dimensions of policy change. Research that addresses practical questions about what policy instruments and settings “work” to improve heath is critical, as is research that examines and sometimes challenges paradigms underlying policy and research that fosters policy innovation, to create new approaches to tackle persistent health challenges. Ongoing and shared learning between policy and research communities will be critical to continue to strengthen research. History shows us that change is possible, but we must be mindful that who we learn what from, and how, and with whom, matters.
