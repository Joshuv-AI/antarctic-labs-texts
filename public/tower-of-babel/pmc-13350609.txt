Efficacy Outcomes of Fecal Microbiota, Live-jslm in Preventing Recurrent Clostridioides difficile Infection from Seven Clinical Studies
Authors: Teena Chopra, Daniel Van Handel, Brian Baggott, Robert Orenstein, Kelly Reveles, Beth Guthmueller, Shivam Srivastava, Sahil Khanna
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractClostridioides difficile infection (CDI) is one of the most common healthcare-associated infections in the US, and recurrence remains a major clinical challenge, as standard-of-care antibiotics fail to prevent recurrence in up to one-third of cases. Fecal microbiota, live-jslm (RBL) is the first single-dose, microbiota-based product approved by the US Food and Drug Administration and Health Canada for the prevention of recurrent CDI (rCDI) following standard-of-care antibiotics. The RBL clinical development program is the largest to date for any microbiota-based product, encompassing seven studies and enrolling over 1700 participants to evaluate the safety and efficacy of RBL in preventing rCDI. Although the RBL development program consistently evaluated both efficacy and safety outcomes across all studies, this review summarizes the study designs, with a primary focus on the efficacy outcomes. The program consists of randomized controlled trials, open-label cohorts, retrospective analyses, and real-world data, each contributing unique insights across different patient populations and treatment settings. All studies enrolled adults aged ≥ 18 years with rCDI who received antibiotics for their enrolling CDI episode before RBL administration. Most studies administered RBL rectally, except for an open-label study and a retrospective study, which evaluated colonoscopic administration of RBL. Treatment success was evaluated at 8 weeks, and sustained clinical response was evaluated at 6 or 24 months after RBL and/or placebo administration, depending on the study design. Overall, the study designs for all seven studies were consistent, with slight differences between number of CDI episodes (specifically, 2 studies allowed enrollment after the first CDI recurrence), blinding, controls, and administration routes. Efficacy data of RBL reported across all studies consistently demonstrated RBL is efficacious for the prevention of rCDI in a broad adult population.Trial RegistrationClinicalTrials.gov. Introduction

Clostridioides difficile is an anaerobic, spore-forming, toxin-producing, gram-positive bacterium that affects approximately 500,000 people each year in the USA [1, 2]. C. difficile is acquired from the environment through the fecal-oral route [1]. A healthy, balanced gut microbiome can prevent C. difficile colonization through colonization resistance mechanisms [3]; however, disruption of the gut microbiome (dysbiosis) can reduce this resistance [4, 5]. Factors associated with dysbiosis include antibiotic use, hospitalization, age ≥ 65 years, and underlying comorbidities [1, 6]. Disruption of the gut microbiome facilitates the germination of dormant C. difficile spores and overgrowth of vegetative cells capable of toxin production, which can lead to either asymptomatic colonization or symptomatic C. difficile infection (CDI) [5, 7, 8]. Symptoms of CDI can range from mild to severe diarrhea and complications including toxic megacolon, septic shock, or even death [9].

Antibiotic therapy, including vancomycin, fidaxomicin, and less often metronidazole, remains the current standard-of-care (SoC) treatment for CDI [10]. Although these antibiotic therapies are effective in controlling the acute infection, they are also associated with persistent dysbiosis, which increases the risk of recurrent CDI (rCDI) [11]. Among individuals who develop an initial episode of CDI, up to 35% may experience a recurrence within the first 8 weeks following completion of antibiotic therapy [12–14]. Second and subsequent recurrences are observed in up to 65% of patients who have already experienced a recurrent episode [1, 13–15]. rCDI remains a major clinical challenge, as it leads to higher mortality and healthcare costs [16, 17]. Because current SoC treatments fail to prevent recurrence in up to a third of cases, there is an urgent need for effective strategies to prevent rCDI.

Microbiota-based therapies aim to restore gut microbiome diversity and composition to prevent rCDI. Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) is one such therapeutic option and involves the restoration of the gut microbiome through the instillation of microbes from the stool of healthy donors [18]. The current guidelines from the Infectious Diseases Society of America and Society for Healthcare Epidemiology of America recommend FMT for patients with multiple recurrences of CDI following SoC treatment, recognizing that its administration is under the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) enforcement discretion policy [10, 19]. The updated American College of Gastroenterology clinical guidelines confirm the recommendation of FMT as a second-line option for the prevention of rCDI following SoC treatment [8]. In addition, the 2024 American Gastroenterological Association guidelines similarly recommend FMT for patients with rCDI for whom SoC treatment has failed [20]. However, the routine utilization of FMT is challenged by the lack of standardization in manufacturing processes, product characterization, routes of administration, and potential risks of transmission of pathogenic organisms [10, 19, 21]. To address these challenges, the FDA encouraged the development of regulated, donor-derived, microbiome restoration therapies.

Fecal microbiota, live-jslm (REBYOTA®, abbreviated RBL) is a single-dose broad microbial consortium of spore-forming and non–spore-forming bacteria, including Bacteroidetes [22]. Administration of RBL has been shown to increase both microbial diversity, including Bacteroidia and Clostridia, and secondary bile acids that inhibit C. difficile spore germination and vegetative growth [23]. Engraftment of donor-derived strains has also been associated with restoration of microbiota composition from dysbiosis toward a healthy state, supporting its role in preventing rCDI [24]. RBL was approved in the US by the FDA on November 30, 2022, as the first microbiota-based product for the prevention of rCDI [25]. Health Canada subsequently approved RBL on March 5, 2025, marking an important milestone for microbiota-based therapies in Canada [26]. RBL is administered rectally as a 150-ml microbiota suspension within 24–72 h of completing SoC antimicrobial therapy [22]. It is manufactured according to good manufacturing practices and undergoes standardized donor screening and pathogen testing in alignment with FDA requirements to ensure patient safety [22, 27]. RBL is not indicated for the primary treatment of CDI [22].

The RBL clinical development program is the largest to date for any microbiota-based product, encompassing seven studies with over 1700 participants enrolled and over 1300 participants receiving RBL and/or placebo. The RBL clinical development program consistently evaluated both efficacy and safety outcomes across all studies; this review summarizes the studies within the clinical development program of RBL with a primary focus on the efficacy outcomes observed.

Clinical Development Program of RBL

Overview of the Study Designs

The RBL clinical development program enrolled participants across six prospective clinical studies and one retrospective clinical study (Fig. 1; Table 1). The six prospective clinical studies include three phase 2 studies (PUNCH CD [NCT01925417], PUNCH CD2 [NCT02299570], and PUNCH Open-Label [NCT02589847]) and three phase 3 studies (PUNCH CD3 [NCT03244644], PUNCH CD3-OLS [NCT03931941], and CDI-SCOPE [NCT05831189]) [27–33]. PUNCH is a sponsor-assigned study name used across the clinical development program and is not an acronym. These prospective studies aimed to evaluate the safety and efficacy of RBL in preventing rCDI. Complementing these prospective studies, a retrospective clinical study (AAT study) of participants who received RBL under the FDA’s enforcement discretion guidance assessed the safety and efficacy in a real-world population [34, 35]. These studies enrolled adults aged 18 years or older with rCDI who received antibiotics for their enrolling CDI episode before RBL administration. Safety and efficacy were analyzed in all studies; treatment success was evaluated at 8 weeks and sustained clinical response was evaluated at 6 or 24 months (as applicable) after RBL and/or placebo administration.

Although the study designs shared a core framework, they differed in blinding, controls, and administration routes (Fig. 1; Table 1). PUNCH CD, Open-Label, CD3-OLS, and CDI-SCOPE were open-label studies, whereas PUNCH CD2 and CD3 were randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled studies. The phase 3 study (PUNCH CD3) employed a Bayesian analysis model to confirm the efficacy of a single dose of RBL versus placebo. Additionally, the PUNCH Open-Label study included a historical antibiotic-only control group.

The dosing regimen differed between studies, with two doses of RBL and/or placebo administered 7 ± 2 days apart as the first treatment course for two studies (PUNCH CD2 and Open-Label) and a single dose administered as the first treatment course for four studies (Fig. 1; Table 1). PUNCH CD, CD2, CD3, and CD3-OLS allowed a second course of RBL (one or two doses, depending on the study) if a CDI recurrence was confirmed within 8 weeks of the first course. Most studies administered RBL rectally, except for the CDI-SCOPE study, which evaluated colonoscopic administration of RBL. In the AAT retrospective study, a subgroup of participants also received RBL via colonoscopy. Of the seven studies, two (PUNCH CD3-OLS and AAT) evaluated safety and effectiveness in a broader, real-world population.

These methodological differences should be considered when interpreting findings across all seven studies. The prospective randomized controlled studies offered strong internal validity and a controlled benchmark for efficacy. In contrast, the open-label and retrospective studies added external validity and captured data from real-world populations often excluded from randomized controlled studies, thereby offering important insights into treatment utility in routine clinical practice. In addition, variations in dosing, administration routes, and patient populations may contribute to differences in observed outcomes. Taken together, the inclusion of both controlled and real-world studies strengthens the overall outcomes by supporting both efficacy and generalizability. This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors. Institutional review board approval was not required.

Integration Strategy of the RBL Clinical Development Program

The RBL clinical development program included complementary studies supporting the efficacy, safety, and real-world applicability of RBL, with the sequence and timing of these studies summarized in Fig. 2. As the first study in the clinical development program, PUNCH CD provided early proof-of-concept safety and efficacy data. PUNCH CD2 introduced a placebo control group and investigated dosing strategies (one dose vs two doses of RBL). Results of these studies informed the use of a single-dose approach in subsequent studies. PUNCH CD3 served as the pivotal study for regulatory approval of RBL. This study confirmed the efficacy of RBL using Bayesian hierarchical modeling, which enabled incorporation of data from PUNCH CD2 due to its similar design and population. This approach allowed PUNCH CD3 to reduce the required sample size and decreased the amount of uncertainty in the primary endpoint estimates.

PUNCH CD3-OLS was initiated before the completion of PUNCH CD3 to allow participants who were ineligible for the CD3 study the opportunity to receive RBL and to support the safety of RBL in a broader population. PUNCH CD3-OLS allowed for inclusion of participants with conditions such as inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), irritable bowel syndrome (IBS), and mild-to-moderate immunocompromising conditions.

As the above studies all evaluated rectal administration of RBL, the CDI-SCOPE study and a subgroup within the retrospective AAT study evaluated colonoscopic administration of RBL due to clinical interest in alternative routes of administration. CDI-SCOPE demonstrated the clinical effectiveness of single-dose RBL administered via colonoscopy and complemented previous studies by suggesting colonoscopic delivery as an additional administration option for preventing CDI recurrence in adults [33, 36]. The AAT retrospective study included participants deemed ineligible to participate in a clinical study and therefore provided insights into RBL outcomes in a broader population, mimicking real-world practice. Notably, the AAT study also included a subgroup of ten participants who received RBL via colonoscopy. The findings from the AAT study supported outcomes observed in the prospective studies and offered additional long-term and practical outcomes data.

Taken together, data gathered using this phased approach, from early proof of concept (PUNCH CD) to controlled efficacy studies (CD2/3) to a broad patient population (CD3-OLS) to real-world confirmation (AAT retrospective), strongly support the efficacy and safety of RBL in preventing rCDI.

Population Overview of the RBL Clinical Development Program

The mean age of the participants for all studies was > 60 years, and most participants were female and white. In all studies, the most frequently used antibiotic treatment for the enrolling CDI episode was vancomycin, and participants had three to four CDI episodes before study entry (Table 2). More than 60% of enrolling CDI episodes across all studies were diagnosed via polymerase chain reaction (PCR) test. This diagnostic test is widely used in real-world clinical settings because it is highly sensitive and specific for C. difficile genes and more frequently available than other testing options [37]. Overall, there were no notable differences in the demographics and baseline characteristics of participants across RBL studies, with the exception of PUNCH CD3-OLS, which included participants with comorbidities such as IBD, IBS, and mild-to-moderate immunocompromising conditions. Efficacy Outcomes

Treatment success was defined as the absence of CDI diarrhea through 8 weeks after RBL or placebo administration. PUNCH CD, CD2, CD3, and CD3-OLS allowed participants to receive a second course of RBL if a CDI recurrence was confirmed within 8 weeks of the first course (either one or two doses, depending on the study design). Sustained clinical response was defined as treatment success of the presenting CDI episode and no new CDI recurrences through 6 or 24 months after RBL or placebo administration. Placebo-Controlled Studies

In PUNCH CD2, participants received either two doses of RBL, one dose of RBL plus one dose of placebo, or two doses of placebo, administered rectally [29]. The two-dose RBL group had a similar treatment success rate (55.6%) to the one-dose RBL group (56.8%) at 8 weeks. Regardless of the administration of one or two doses of RBL, both groups had higher treatment success rates than the placebo-only group (43.2%). Given the similarities in efficacy, these results informed the use of a single-dose approach in later studies.

Participants in the PUNCH CD3 study received a single dose of RBL or placebo after SoC antibiotic therapy [31]. A Bayesian primary analysis model, recommended by the FDA and integrating data from PUNCH CD2, estimated a 70.4% treatment success rate for RBL compared with 58.1% for placebo. The posterior probability of success was 98.6%, exceeding the prespecified threshold of 97.5%, confirming statistical and clinical efficacy. The sustained response rate showed that 92.1% of initial responders remained CDI-free through 6 months. Overall, these studies showed consistent RBL efficacy.

Open-Label Studies

PUNCH CD was a single-arm study that demonstrated a treatment success rate of 51.6% at 8 weeks after one course of RBL [27]. PUNCH Open-Label compared RBL outcomes to a historical antibiotic control group [30]. The treatment success rate for those who received RBL was 78.9% at 8 weeks, whereas the treatment success rate was 30.7% in the historical control group. Among those who achieved treatment success, 97.3% maintained a sustained clinical response at 6 months, with 95.3% and 90.7% maintaining sustained clinical response at 12 and 24 months, respectively.

PUNCH CD3-OLS assessed RBL in a real-world population, including participants ineligible for CD3 [32]. At 8 weeks, 73.8% of participants had treatment success; of those, 91.0% remained CDI-free through 6 months.

After the approval of RBL for rectal administration, the CDI-SCOPE study evaluated the efficacy of colonoscopic administration of RBL [33, 36]. At 8 weeks, 95.1% of participants who received RBL via colonoscopy experienced treatment success. Among the participants who achieved treatment success, 97.4% had a sustained clinical response through 6 months.

In the retrospective analysis of participants who received RBL either rectally or via colonoscopy (AAT study), the treatment success rate at 8 weeks for participants who received one dose of RBL was 83.3% versus 82.5% for participants who received two doses [34]. Of the participants who experienced treatment success through 8 weeks, 90.0% who received one dose and 87.9% who received two doses had a sustained clinical response through 6 months. Ten participants who received one dose of RBL for the qualifying event received the product via colonoscopy [35]. Of these, eight participants (80.0%) achieved treatment success; the remaining two did not have comprehensive medical records and were conservatively classified as having treatment failure. Among the eight participants who achieved treatment success, six (75.0%) maintained a sustained clinical response through 6 months [35]. These results were similar to findings from prospective studies and confirm the use of one dose of RBL. The AAT study supports the flexibility of RBL in clinical use, although interpretation of efficacy from retrospective data must be approached cautiously because of potential bias and lack of randomization.

Efficacy Following Second Treatment Courses

The treatment success rates following a second course of RBL at 8 weeks are shown in Fig. 4. PUNCH CD reported treatment success rates of 51.6% for the first course of RBL and 78.6% for the second course [27]. PUNCH CD2 also included participants who received a second course of RBL (one or two doses); however, there were no significant differences in the treatment success rates between the first and second courses of RBL [28, 29]. Although PUNCH CD3 and CD3-OLS observed numerically lower treatment success rates for the second course of RBL, ≥ 80% of these participants had a sustained response through 6 months [31, 32]. Efficacy Outcomes in Populations at High Risk for rCDI

PUNCH CD3-OLS included participants with mild-to-moderate immunocompromising conditions, IBD, and IBS [32, 38–40]; subgroup analyses assessed efficacy outcomes specific to these populations of participants. In a subgroup analysis of immunocompromised participants (n = 141), the treatment success rate at 8 weeks was 75.7%, with 88.7% having a sustained clinical response at 6 months [38]. In participants with IBD, the treatment success rate at 8 weeks was 78.9%, and the sustained clinical response rate at 6 months was 91.1% [39]. In participants with IBS, 68.9% had treatment success at 8 weeks; of those, 82.3% had a sustained clinical response through 6 months [40]. Overall, the efficacy outcomes of participants with mild-to-moderate immunocompromising conditions, IBD, and IBS remained comparable to the rates in participants without these conditions.

Overall, efficacy outcomes were consistent across studies despite differences in design, patient populations, and administration routes. Findings from randomized controlled trials were complemented by similar or, in some studies, higher response rates observed in open-label and real-world studies, supporting both the reliability and generalizability of RBL in preventing rCDI. Variability in outcomes across studies may reflect differences in study populations, dosing strategies, and clinical settings rather than inconsistency in treatment effect.

Future Perspectives

Given the high rates of rCDI despite SoC antibiotic therapy, there remains a need for additional strategies to prevent recurrence [12–14]. Findings from the RBL clinical development program indicate consistent efficacy across multiple study designs, including randomized controlled studies, open-label studies, and real-world analyses [27–35]. Treatment success and sustained clinical response were observed across diverse patient populations, suggesting the observed outcomes are not confined to controlled research settings. The inclusion of both controlled and real-world studies provides context for clinical practice, where patient populations are often heterogeneous; however, differences in study design, patient populations, and clinical settings should be considered when interpreting these findings. Overall, the observed efficacy outcomes suggest that RBL may represent a potential option for addressing this unmet need as a standardized, single-dose product that can be implemented in routine care.

The routine utilization of FMT has been limited by the lack of standardization in manufacturing processes, product characterization, routes of administration, and concerns regarding the transmission of pathogenic organisms [10, 19, 21]. RBL is a regulated microbiota-based product manufactured under standardized conditions with defined donor screening and quality control processes [22, 27]. The availability of a standardized product may support more consistent use of microbiota-based products in clinical practice, with potential implications for reproducibility of outcomes and patient safety.

Interpretations of the findings should consider limitations across included studies. Open-label studies and retrospective analyses may introduce bias. Differences in study populations, dosing regimens, and administration routes may also limit cross-study comparisons. In addition, the seven clinical studies were limited in that participants with fulminant CDI and a life expectancy of < 12 months were excluded from all RBL studies [27–35]. Furthermore, patients with IBD, IBS, and immunocompromising conditions were excluded from all prospective RBL studies except PUNCH CD3-OLS [32].

Further research is warranted to evaluate long-term outcomes beyond 24 months, particularly in broader patient populations, including those with fulminant CDI or severe immunocompromised conditions. Future studies may also examine comparative outcomes with other microbiota-based products and their role within clinical management strategies for rCDI. Consideration of regional differences in clinical practice and access to microbiota-based products may also be relevant for broader implementation.

Conclusion

The clinical development program of RBL consists of randomized controlled studies, open-label cohorts, retrospective analyses, and real-world data, each contributing unique insights across different patient populations and treatment settings. Overall, the study designs for all seven studies were consistent, with slight differences in blinding, controls, and administration routes. These are complementary studies that collectively show the efficacy, safety, and real-world applicability of RBL.

Efficacy of RBL was reported across all studies and demonstrated treatment success at 8 weeks and sustained clinical response through 6 months after RBL administration. Overall, these efficacy data consistently demonstrated RBL to be efficacious for the prevention of rCDI in a broad adult population.
