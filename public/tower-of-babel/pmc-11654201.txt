Long-term impact of maternal pre-pregnancy BMI on the risk of overweight/obesity in preschool children: mediation by infant birth weight and BMI trajectory group from 0 to 2 years
Authors: Ang Mao, Shu-Qin Yang, Ying-Juan Luo, Yan Ren, Xu Yan, Yu-Rong Qiu, Xiao Yang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundObesity is a complex multisystem disease that affects almost every organ system. Studying the early determinants of obesity and implementing targeted interventions have positive implications for promoting long-term health. Therefore, this study was to evaluate the impact of maternal pre-pregnancy overweight or obesity, weight gain rate (WGR) during the second and third trimesters, infant birth weight (IBW) and BMI trajectory group (BMI-TG) from 0 to 2 years on overweight or obesity in preschool children.MethodsA retrospective cohort analysis of data obtained from a hospital was performed using chain-mediated effects to analyze the direct and indirect effects of prepregnancy body mass index (ppBMI)on preschool children’s body mass index (pcBMI), and mediated by three variables: WGR, IBW and BMI-TG.ResultsWe found that the effect of ppBMI and pcBMI was significantly mediated by IBW and BMI-TG. After adjusting for confounding factors, the total effect of ppBMI on pcBMI was estimated to be 0.1965 (bootstrap 95% CI: 0.1154–0.2783), the direct effect was 0.1562 (bootstrap 95% CI: 0.0750–0.2362), and the indirect effect was 0.0403 (bootstrap 95% CI: 0.0219 ~ 0.0651). The mediating effect accounted for 20.5% of the variance, indicating that 20.5% of the impact of ppBMI on pcBMI was mediated by two mediating factors: IBW and the BMI-TG.ConclusionsMaternal overweight/obesity before pregnancy had a significant impact on overweight/obesity in preschool children and was mediated by two main factors: IBW and the BMI-TG.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-20914-x.

Background

Among noncommunicable diseases, overweight and obesity have received increasing amounts of attention because they may offset additional health benefits that contribute to an extended lifespan. Globally, 39% of adults aged 18 years and older were overweight in 2016, and 13% were having obesity. The number of children and adolescents aged 5–19 years worldwide with overweight or obesity has exceeded 340 million in 2016 [1]. Epidemiological studies have demonstrated that overweight/obesity in early life leads to adult and lifelong overweight and obesity [2–4] and is also closely related to a series of diseases, such as early onset [5, 6] obesity in adolescents and adults [7], impaired glucose tolerance, type 2 diabetes [8], decreased lung function [9], cardiovascular disease [10] and some cancers [11]. The longer the duration of childhood overweight and obesity, the greater the impact on the risk of obesity related diseases in adulthood, such as hypertension and hyperglycemia [12]. Therefore, It is of great public health significance to identify the risk factors related to childhood overweight/obesity.

Pre-pregnancy BMI (ppBMI), gestational weight gain rate (WGR), and infant birth weight (IBW) are key factors in childhood overweight/obesity. Mattsson et al. [13] showed that a high maternal pre-pregnancy BMI (ppBMI) is one of the most common risk factors for rapid weight gain in early childhood. A previous meta-analysis, which included 162,129 mother-child pairs from 37 pregnancy and birth cohort studies, found that pre-pregnancy BMI and gestational weight gain are risk factors for childhood overweight/obesity [14]. In addition, Josey et al. [15] quantified the direct and indirect mechanisms of maternal obesity mediated by weight gain in the second and third trimesters and its impact on childhood obesity. Stevens et al. [16] demonstrated that maternal prepregnancy overweight/obesity has a consistent association with offspring overweight/obesity in childhood, with a small part of this association mediated by infant birth weight. Zhang et al. [17] conducted a prospective study on 19 054 children and showed that infants aged 0–2 years who were in a high-growth trajectory group were more likely have overweight and obesity later in life.

A deeper understanding of the relationship between ppBMI, WGR, IBW, and childhood overweight/obesity can help implement targeted measures to reduce the risk of childhood obesity. Zhang et al. [17] demonstrated that maternal gestational weight gain and infant birth weight mediate the effect of pre-pregnancy BMI on the BMI of children at age 2. Heerman et al. revealed the effect of the interaction between ppBMI and GWG on infant growth and found that GWG amplified the effect of ppBMI on outcomes. A study by Josey et al. [15] reported that 8.1% of the effect of maternal obesity on childhood adiposity was mediated by GWG. These studies emphasize the mediating roles of GWG and IBW, with the longest follow-up extending to the BMI of children at age 2, and using a single time point measurement of children’s BMI. However, given the rapid and fluctuating nature of growth and development in early childhood16, and a single time point measurement cannot fully capture the complexities of children’s growth. Therefore, we need for a longer time span to explore the intricate relationships between ppBMI, WGR, IBW, BMI Z score developmental trajectories group (BMI-TG) during the first 2 years of life and preschool children’s BMI (pcBMI), in order to implement targeted interventions to reduce the risk of childhood overweight and obesity.

Methods

Study design

The retrospective birth cohort of mother–child pairs was obtained from the information system of Chengdu Women and Children’s Center Hospital (ICWCCH). This study collected data from basic records of mothers and children born in 2014 at Chengdu Women and Children’s Central Hospital in Qingyang District, Chengdu, and conducted follow-up when the children were aged 6–7 years. The pregnant women’s sociodemographic information, prepregnancy height and weight, prenatal health care data, disease history were extracted from the ICWCCH information system, and data on the gestational age, birth weight, body length and birth defects of the infants were collected from delivery records. Experienced pediatric doctors performed regular physical examinations of the infants and young children, and physical data, such as height and weight, were collected when the children were 1, 3, 6, 8, 12, 18 and 24 months of age. This study was conducted in accordance with the Helsinki declaration and approved from Clinical Research Ethics Committee of the hospitals (No. 202229).

Participants

Our study used the ICWCCH system to select mothers who gave birth at Chengdu Women and Children Central Hospital from January 1, 2014, to December 31, 2014, and their children as the research subjects. The inclusion criteria were as follows: mothers aged between 18 and 35 years in 2014; infants with a first registered gestational age less than or equal to 14 weeks and a gestational age at delivery of 37–42 weeks; singleton infants who had an Apgar score > 7 points at 1 min; and children aged 0–2 years who had participated in the growth and development assessment more than 4 times, including at least 1 time over the age of 1 year. The exclusion criteria were as follows: mothers who conceived through artificial reproductive technology, mothers who suffered from serious illnesses such as infectious diseases or mental illnesses during pregnancy, mothers whose pre-pregnancy weight information was missing, mothers whose weight was measured less than 3 times during pregnancy, and mothers whose children had birth defects or died. Ultimately, 833 mother–child pairs were included in this study. The flow chart of the analysis for participant inclusion is shown in Fig. 1.

Measures of variables

The mothers’ main information included the ppBMI, the WGR, and gestational diabetes status. ppBMI was estimated based on self-reported height and weight at the first prenatal examination, and the BMI was calculated as weight (kg)/ height in meters squared (m2). The calculation of the WGR was performed by subtracting the first measurement result in the second trimester (pregnancy 13 weekend) from the last reported weight before delivery and dividing this result by the gestational interval. Pregnant women are usually screened for gestational diabetes by a 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test with a glucose load of 75 g at 24 to 28 weeks of pregnancy. According to the Guidelines for Diagnosis and Treatment of Hyperglycemia in Pregnancy [18], patients were diagnosed with gestational diabetes if they met any of the following criteria: had a fasting blood glucose level ≥ 5.1 mmol/L, had a 1-hour blood glucose level ≥ 10.0 mmol/L or had a 2-hour blood glucose level ≥ 8.5 mmol/L.

The main measurement information of the children included IBW, body length and weight measured during a physical examination at 0–2 years of age, feeding mode in the first six month, and BMI measured at 6–7 years of age. IBW and length were measured by doctors and included in the birth certificate records of the ICWCCH. The height and weight of the 0- to 2-year-old children were measured by trained nurses and recorded in the ICWCCH information system. Weight was measured to the nearest 0.1 kg while the children were wearing light clothes, and height was measured to the nearest 0.1 centimeters (without a hat) with a body tester. According to the World Health Organization’s child growth standards [19], the standardized BMI z score for sex and age was calculated. Feeding modes within the first six months postpartum were categorized as exclusive and non-exclusive breastfeeding, with artificial feeding and mixed feeding classified under non-exclusive breastfeeding. The BMIs of the children at 6–7 years of age was measured by trained doctors, and BMI was calculated by dividing the weight by the height in meters squared.

Statistical analysis

Considering the characteristics of rapid change and large fluctuations in growth and development during early childhood [20], it is difficult to accurately evaluate the growth and development status of children in early childhood based on single time point measurements or differences between the first and last measurements. Therefore, we used a group-based trajectory model (GBTM) to evaluate the growth and development of children aged 0–2 years to identify clusters with similar developmental trajectories [21]. We fitted 2–6 groups of trajectory models, and each trajectory group in the model was fitted as linear, square or cubic. By comparing the fitting effects of different models and the professional interpretability of the trajectory shape of the model, we selected four groups of models with cubic trajectories and incorporated them into the chain intermediary model in the form of equidistant assignment according to the shapes of the four trajectories and professional knowledge (the trajectory group results are detailed in the supplementary files).

It is well established that chain intermediary effect analysis is a special method for analyzing causality. Our study introduced mediating variables to identify and explain whether the independent variable affected the dependent variable through the mediating variable, decomposed the total effect of the independent variable on the dependent variable into direct and indirect effects, tested whether the effects of each part existed, and determined the size of the effect. To test the statistical significance of the mediating effect, we used the bootstrap normal approximation interval (NA-interval) and bias-corrected and accelerated bootstrap intervals (BCA-intervals) to calculate the 95% interval of the mediating effect [22]. If the 95% interval did not contain zero, the mediating effect was considered to be statistically significant. When the results of the NA-interval and BCA-interval were statistically significant, the results of the BCA- interval were used [23].

We hypothesized that ppBMI can indirectly affect pcBMI through the WGR, IBW, and the BMI-TG; that the WGR can indirectly affect pcBMI through IBW and the BMI-TG; and that IBW can indirectly affect pcBMI through the BMI-TG, all of which can have direct effects on pcBMI. The direct and indirect effects of these four factors on pcBMI were compared, and the intermediary variable that contributed the most to pcBMI was determined. See Fig. 2 for details.

Results

We initially screened 4474 mother–child pairs who met the inclusion criteria in this study, with 833 pairs retained for the mediation analysis after applying the exclusion criteria. It is worth noting that the number of cases of overweight and obesity among mothers before pregnancy was 110, accounting for 13.21% of the sample. The average weight gain rate in the middle and late stages of pregnancy was 0.62 ± 0.27 kg, the average birth weight was 3.36 (SD = 0.40) kg, and the average BMI of preschool children was 15.90 (SD = 2.19) kg/m2 (Table 1). In the model including pcBMI as the outcome variable and IBW and the BMI-TG as the mediating variables (Table 3), after we adjusted for confounding factors, the total effect of the ppBMI on the pcBMI was estimated to be 0.1965 (bootstrap 95% CI: 0.1154 ~ 0.2783), indicating that for every unit (1 kg/m2) increase in the ppBMI (1 kg/m2), the pcBMI increased by 0.1965 kg/m2. After adjusting for IBW and the BMI-TG, the direct effect of the ppBMI on the pcBMI was estimated to be 0.1562 (bootstrap 95% CI = 0.0750 ~ 0.2362), indicating that for every unit (1 kg/m2) increase in the ppBMI, the pcBMI increased by 0.1562 kg/m2. The mediating effect of the ppBMI on pcBMI through the IBW and BMI-TG was also significant, with a mediating effect of 0.0403 (bootstrap 95% CI = 0.0219–0.0651), accounting for 20.5% (0.0403/0.1965) of the variance, indicating that 20.5% of the impact of the ppBMI on the pcBMI was achieved through the IBW and BMI-TG.

Discussion

The results of this study indicated that maternal prepregnancy overweight/obesity has a significant impact on overweight/obesity in preschool children, and we demonstrated that a significant correlation of approximately 20.5% between maternal ppBMI and pcBMI is mediated by IBW and the BMI-TG. ppBMI also had some indirect effects on the BMI-TG, with approximately 34.4% mediated by IBW.

Previous studies have identified high maternal ppBMI as one of the most common risk factors for rapid weight gain in early childhood [25, 26]. Similarly, in the present study, we demonstrated that children exposed to maternal overweight/obesity during pregnancy are more likely have overweight and obesity than children who are not exposed. There is literature indicating that the intrauterine environment may have a profound influence on the development of obesity later in life [27]. One possible mechanism is that a suboptimal intrauterine nutritional environment may modulate a child’s energy balance system by altering the developmental programming of appetite control and the metabolism of adipose tissue and adipocytes in fetuses. Children with modified energy balance systems may be more vulnerable to obesogenic environments, thus increasing their risk of developing obesity during childhood [27, 28]. Recently, Morgen et al. [29] published a study that showed that birth weight was not an important intermediary between maternal ppBMI and the BMI percentile of children [30]. This finding is in contrast to our research, which showed that IBW is an intermediary of this association. In addition, the association between the BMI growth trajectory and obesity in this study is similar to the findings of Aris [31]. This study showed that children with stable high BMI z-scores and an early increase in the BMI z score growth trajectory have a greater risk of obesity at preschool age. The results of these studies indicate that excessive nutritional status and abnormal growth patterns during pregnancy can lead to short-term or long-term unhealthy outcomes for children.

The direct impact of ppBMI on IBW mediated by the WGR is significant. The positive effect of ppBMI on IBW has been reported in other studies [32, 33]. Starling et al. [34] used a linear regression model in a prospective cohort analysis and concluded that maternal ppBMI is positively correlated with neonatal obesity. However, the indirect impact of ppBMI on IBW was not statistically significant. This study also found that women with higher BMI values before pregnancy had lower gestational weight gain. A possible explanation for this finding is that women with higher ppBMIs may have been counseled during prenatal care to control their weight to prevent complications related to the health of the mother and child [35]. With obesity women may benefit from low weight gain during pregnancy [36].

A high ppBMI is associated with an increased risk of being in an accelerated BMI-TG, and a positive indirect impact of IBW has been identified. Previous longitudinal studies have reported a positive correlation between maternal BMI before or during early pregnancy and child growth trajectories characterized by increased BMI [6, 37], and our findings expand the scope of this evidence. These associations can be explained by shared genetic and environmental factors between mothers and their children or long-term changes in fetal endocrine and metabolic processes in having obesity mothers and their children. Fetal malnutrition in the offspring of having obesity mothers may explain, to some extent, the observed association with an accelerated body mass index growth trajectory deviating from greater birth sizes [38, 39].

In this study, compared with the indirect influence of ppBMI on pcBMI, the direct influence of ppBMI was greater. Some studies suggest that the risk of childhood obesity is related to genetics [40], and this may be further influenced by the postpartum environment. Therefore, pre-pregnancy obesity has a greater direct impact on weight-related outcomes in children. In other words, if a mother is exposed to certain factors, leading to obesity development, then her child is more likely to be exposed to all or some of the same factors. Along with weight gain, this process is inevitably accompanied by an increase in skeletal muscle and body fat.

There are several advantages in our study. This was a cohort study spanning 7 years from pre-pregnancy to preschool. We collected data on a series of metabolic and growth development factors during pre-pregnancy and pregnancy and during the period from 0 to 7 years of age, and corrected for possible confounding factors, which can reveal stronger causal relationships. Considering the large fluctuations in BMI in early childhood and the error effect of a single measurement, we used a group-based trajectory model to quantify the growth and development of children aged 0–2 years. Through the mediation analysis of 11 pathways, we found that maternal ppBMI has a natural direct and indirect impact on pcBMI through mediating factors (WGR, IBW, BMI and TG), revealing the relationship between maternal metabolic factors before and during pregnancy and the growth and development of children aged 0–7 years.

This study has several limitations. First, the individuals included in this study were women of appropriate age who underwent prenatal examinations at the Municipal Maternal and Child Health Hospital and agreed to give birth there. Most of these women lived in urban areas, and data from different locations may vary. Therefore, this selection bias can affect the effectiveness of the survey results. Moreover, the pre-pregnancy weight is self-reported, and overweight or obese women may underestimate their own weight. Second, we used only the age-specific BMI of infants as an indicator of child growth, without considering other indicators representing growth and development, such as cortical thickness or upper arm circumference. Finally, mediating effect analysis is a method of exploring the mechanisms and pathways of interactions between variables. The problem of collinearity between independent variables and mediating factors increases the standard error of coefficient estimation and reduces statistical significance.

Conclusions

A high maternal BMI before pregnancy is one of the most common risk factors for early rapid weight gain in children. This was a retrospective birth cohort study. There was a consistent correlation between maternal overweight/obesity before pregnancy and overweight/obesity in childhood, and some of these associations were mediated by IBW and the BMI-TG. Clarifying the association between metabolic factors in early life and obesity in school-age children in the future can help identify populations with a higher risk of obesity and greater body fat content and provide a theoretical basis for early intervention in certain chronic diseases.
