Prioritizing Alignment Paradigms over Task-Specific Model Customization in Time-Series LLMs
Wei Li 1 , Yunyao Cheng 2 , Xinli Hao 1 , Chaohong Ma 1 , Yuxuan Liang 3 , Bin Yang 4 , Christian S. Jensen 2 , Xiaofeng Meng 1 1 Renmin University of China, 2 Aalborg University, 3 Hong Kong University of Science and Technology (Guangzhou), 4 East China Normal University {leeway,xinli_hao,chaohma,xfmeng}@ruc.edu.cn, {yunyaoc,csj}@cs.aau.dk, yuxliang@outlook.com, byang@dase.ecnu.edu.cn

Abstract
Recent advances in Large Language Models (LLMs) have enabled unprecedented capabilities for time-series reasoning in diverse real-world applications, including medical, financial, and spatio-temporal domains. However, existing approaches typically focus on task-specific model customization, such as forecasting and anomaly detection, while overlooking the data itself, referred to as time-series primitives , which are essential for in-depth reasoning. This position paper advocates a fundamental shift in approaching time-series reasoning with LLMs: prioritizing alignment paradigms grounded in the intrinsic primitives of time series data over task-specific model customization. This realignment addresses the core limitations of current time-series reasoning approaches, which are often costly, inflexible, and inefficient, by systematically accounting for intrinsic structure of data before task engineering. To this end, we propose three alignment paradigms : Injective Alignment , Bridging Alignment , and Internal Alignment , which are emphasized by prioritizing different aspects of time-series primitives : domain, characteristic, and representation, respectively, to activate time-series reasoning capabilities of LLMs to enable economical, flexible, and efficient reasoning. We further recommend that practitioners adopt an alignment-oriented method to avail this instruction to select an appropriate alignment paradigm. Additionally, we categorize relevant literature into these alignment paradigms and outline promising research directions.

3.1
Definition

Time-series primitives
are the intrinsic, atomic, and indivisible components of time series data that fundamentally determine their semantics, properties, and structural forms. We identify three primitives of time series data:
(1) Domain Primitive
captures the contextual background and domain-specific temporal semantics of the time series data for essential prior knowledge;
(2) Characteristic Primitive
describes the statistical and dynamic properties of time series data over time, including temporal patterns, and spatial correlations;
(3) Representation Primitive
defines the presented format of time series data, including Number, Text, Number→Text (converted), and other representations. For clarity, we provide an intuitive representation in Figure
4
.

3.2
Representation Primitive

Time series data are commonly represented in numerical or textual forms, each with distinct inductive biases and typically processed independently. Time-series LLMs aim to align the vector representations of both modalities within a shared high-dimensional space. Understanding the respective strengths and limitations of these representations is crucial for developing alignment strategies.

Number
. This numerical representation
\mathbf{X}_{\text{Number}}=\langle x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{t},\ldots,x_{T}\rangle
denotes a sequence of timestamped signal values that capture the temporal dynamics of a time series, where each
x_{t}\in\mathbb{R}
is the observed value at timestamp
t
. This representation offers several strengths:
(1) high fidelity.
Directly preserves precise quantitative information essential;
(2) low overhead.
Minimal transformation overhead compared to textual formats, enabling efficient training and inference;
(3) feature richness.
Naturally contains non-stationarity, trends, and temporal patterns that can be directly learned by models. However, the limitations are as follows:
(1) limited interpretability.
Raw values lack inherent semantic meaning, making it challenging to provide human-understandable insights
[
34
]
;
(2) preprocessing complexity.
Real-world signals often suffer from noise, missing values, and sparsity, necessitating preprocessing such as imputation and anomaly detection
[
10
]
;
(3) complex inter-variable dependencies.
In multivariate settings, intricate dependencies among variables must be explicitly modeled to avoid misleading correlations
[
73
]
.

Text
. This textual representation
{\mathbf{X}_{\text{Text}}=\left\{s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{L}\right\}}
consists of a sequence of discrete symbols
{s_{i}\in\mathcal{V},i=1,2,\ldots,L}
, where each
s_{i}\in\mathcal{V}
comes from a predefined vocabulary
\mathcal{V}
, and
L
denotes the length of the text sequence. It has the following strengths:
(1) semantic interpretability.
Text provides human-readable and structurally meaningful representations, facilitating intuitive understanding and reasoning;
(2) compatibility with LLMs.
This format is well-aligned with LLMs for prompting, reasoning, and generation tasks within LLMs
[
28
]
. However, the limitations are as follows:
(1) preprocessing complexity.
Domain-specific jargon, rare terms, or inconsistent formatting in real-world time-series text often necessitate vocabulary normalization and pruning
[
64
]
;
(2) alignment challenges.
The discrete and low-frequency nature of text may misalign with the continuous and high-frequency characteristics of time series data
[
59
]
.

3.3
Characteristic Primitive

Time series data are typically collected at regular or irregular intervals and are inherently ordered chronologically. They exhibit complex temporal and spatial dependencies that reflect the dynamic nature of the underlying generative processes. We characterize these dependencies from two complementary perspectives: temporal patterns and spatial correlations.

Temporal Patterns.

Consider a univariate time series
\mathbf{X}_{\text{Number}}=\langle x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{t},\ldots,x_{T}\rangle
, where each
x_{t}\in\mathbb{R}
denotes the observed value at timestamp
t
, and
T
is the time-series length. Such a time series can exhibit diverse statistical behaviors, including:
(1) stationarity
is the statistical property, such as mean and variance, remain constant over time, indicating stable patterns;
(2) trend
is the long-term directional patterns, such as gradual increases or decreases in financial or climate data
[
49
]
;
(3) noise
is high-frequency random fluctuations that may obscure meaningful patterns and require denoising
[
31
]
;
(4) period
is recurring patterns at regular intervals, such as daily or seasonal cycles
[
40
]
. Aligning the temporal characteristic of time series data with LLM requires considering:
(1) temporal dynamics.
Capturing both local and global patterns in sequential data while mitigating overfitting and underfitting issues
[
9
]
;
(2) non-stationary.
Adapting to evolving temporal contexts for more robust alignment
[
2
]
;
(3) regularity extraction.
Leveraging learned features to represent trend and periodic patterns that facilitate LLM reasoning
[
102
]
.

Spatial Correlations.

Consider a multivariate time-series
{\mathbf{X}_{\text{Number}}=\left\langle\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{t},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{T}\right\rangle}
consisting of observations
recorded over
T
timestamps. At each timestamp
t\in[1,T]
, the observation
{\mathbf{x}_{t}=\left\langle x_{t}^{1},x_{t}^{2},\ldots,x_{t}^{m},\ldots,x_{t}^{M}\right\rangle}
represents the values of
M
variables, where
x_{t}^{m}
denotes the observation of the
m
-th (
m\in[1,M]
) variable at timestamp
t
. The
T
is the maximum time-series length, and
M
is the maximum number of features. Spatial characteristics concern channel correlations
[
77
]
, including:
(1) multivariate independence.
Treating each variable independently to reduce complexity and avoid spurious correlations;
(2) multivariate dependence.
Multiple variables are processed together by incorporating more information to capture interdependencies of variables. Aligning spatial characteristics with LLMs involves addressing:
(1) multivariate correlation.
Capturing complex inter-variable relationships for coherent and context-aware reasoning
[
37
]
;
(2) noise attribution.
identifying and disentangling noise arising from high-dimensional variable interactions
[
57
]
;
(3) localized shifts.
addressing localized or gradual structural shifts via global dependency modeling across varying temporal scopes
[
41
]
.

Domain Primitive

Time series data spans a broad spectrum of real-world application domains with unique temporal structures. To examine

Temporal-Pattern Diversity.

(1) Medical data.
This domain includes electroencephalogram (EEG) and electrocardiogram (ECG) signals, which typically exhibit strong periodicity and discernible trends with relatively low noise. Interpreting such data requires domain expertise to extract meaningful semantics. For example, specific waveforms correspond to particular brain states

(2) Financial data.
This domain comprises stock prices and trading volumes, which are high-frequency, predictive time series characterized by pronounced noise, significant short-term non-stationarity, and recurring trends. Real-time modeling is often essential for tasks such as risk assessment and market forecasting

(3) Spatio-temporal data.
This domain encompasses sensor readings from multiple sources, often exhibiting strong periodicity and high noise, with less pronounced long-term trends. Data quality is influenced by factors such as sensor type, sampling rate, and hardware-induced noise
[
132
]
.

Spatial-Correlation Complexity.

(1) Medical data.
These datasets are typically low-dimensional, with high-quality EEG and ECG signals collected by professional medical equipment and clinicians. Large-scale acquisition is relatively straightforward
[

. However, physiological data from medical records and diagnoses are subject to strict privacy regulations
[

. Since clinical decisions rely on trust in model outputs, it is crucial for LLMs to provide transparent and interpretable reasoning processes
[

.
(2) Financial data.
These datasets are similarly low-dimensional, and financial time series are governed by rigorous regulatory frameworks to ensure transparency, fairness, and market stability
[

. The interpretation of such data often depends on market-specific context, making it essential to incorporate external knowledge for accurate behavior modeling
[
121
]
.
(3) Spatio-temporal data.
These datasets are generally high-dimensional and heterogeneous, integrating multiple sensor modalities. Effective modeling requires contextual fusion to account for diverse formats and ensure comprehensive interpretation
[
133
]
.

We observe that the design priorities of time-series

with LLMs are influenced by different primitives: the

affects the design of the

(1) Resource Costs.
The resource costs include both computational and labor costs. Computational costs include training time, inference latency, and the scalability of the method across datasets and hardware configurations. Labor costs involve the expertise required to
1
design

(2) Reasoning Improvement.
Effective alignment enables LLMs to
2
leverage auxiliary time-series analysis tools and
3
retrieve extra time-series domain knowledge,
4
activating time-series reasoning capabilities. Acting as a cognitive engine, the LLM can dynamically interface with external modules and heterogeneous information sources, reducing the reliance on manual information retrieval and improving
5

4.3
Injective Alignment

This paradigm is
economical
for specific domains because dynamic domain knowledge is compatible with prompt-based interaction mechanisms in LLMs for easy implementation. It primarily emphasizes alignment with the time-series
Domain Primitive
, and embeds time series into LLM as text data described by
\mathbf{Y}=f_{\theta}(h_{\gamma}(\mathbf{X_{\text{Number}}},\mathbf{X_{\text{Text}}}))
, where
\mathbf{X_{\text{Number}}}
are incorporated into
\mathbf{X_{\text{Text}}}
by injective function
h_{\gamma}
. The prompts are concise statements designed to extract knowledge from an LLM, including descriptions and demonstrations. However, the paradigm is limited by insufficient context length, and the optimization suggestions are as follows:

Prompt Quantization
directly encodes the composite time-series prompt composed of numerical and textual representations into subword tokens, wherein numerical information is injected into the textual input space without modifying the internal structure of LLMs. For instance, TEMPO-GPT
[
9
]
point out that inconsistencies in prompt templates, such as sentence phrasing, variable ordering, and connector usage, can degrade the temporal ability to recognize multivariate dependencies, highlighting the sensitivity of
Injective Alignment
to prompt design. GenG
[
132
]
reduces the dimensionality of high-volume sensor inputs to suppress redundant noise while preserving salient temporal characteristics, thus improving alignment efficiency. Instruct-FinGPT
[
121
]
further adopts a command-style prompt formulation, enabling LLMs to interpret task-specific intentions and generate targeted responses based on financial time series.

Learnable Prompt
involves parameterized tokens that are optimized during training to encode task-relevant or domain-specific knowledge. For example, PromptCast
[
115
]
explores the performance differences between static handcrafted prompts and trainable prompt embeddings, demonstrating the latter’s superior ability to capture context from diverse time series inputs. LeMoLE
[
123
]
further constructs a library of key-value prompt embeddings and employs retrieval to select prompts suited for a given input from candidate prompts based on the highest similarity scores.

This paradigm enables economical alignment with LLMs through prompt-based strategies, but is limited by context length and prompt loss. The instructions given to participants are as follows:

Our position:
Injective Alignment operates at a shallow instructional level, offering a low-cost solution. With minimal resource costs and reasoning improvements, we advocate prioritizing its use in various domain-specific application settings. However, its effectiveness is highly sensitive to the quality of prompt design and the inherent reasoning capabilities of the chosen LLM. Poorly constructed prompts or misaligned model design may lead to high information loss, potentially undermining performance. Practitioners should continue optimization and evaluation with sufficient domain expertise well-suited to craft precise prompts or function calls tailored to the domain context. Moreover, high-capacity LLMs are generally preferred to ensure adequate expressive power for complex reasoning tasks, whereas lightweight models may be more appropriate for simpler tasks to achieve lower resource costs.

4.4
Bridging Alignment

This paradigm is
flexible
for modeling general temporal patterns and spatial correlations due to its ability to leverage complementary semantic information. It primarily emphasizes alignment with the time-series
Characteristic Primitive
, and indirectly leverages LLMs described by
\mathbf{Y}=f_{\theta}(h_{\gamma}(g_{\phi}(\mathbf{X_{\text{Number}}}),\mathbf{X_{\text{Text}}}))
, where the mapping function
g_{\phi}
in external adapter is used to match the similarity of
\mathbf{X_{\text{Number}}}
with a semantically similar textual form
\mathbf{X}{}^{\prime\prime}_{\text{Text}}
. However, the paradigm is limited by complex design, and the optimization suggestions are as follows:

Similarity Retrieval
enhances the alignment between numerical and textual representations by leveraging embedding similarity. Adapters act as a bridging space that projects time series data into an embedding space compatible with LLMs. Specifically, training the
\phi
by loss function
\mathcal{L}
and similarity retrieval by mapping function
g_{\phi}
described by
g_{\phi}(\mathbf{X_{\text{Number}}})\to\mathbf{X}{}^{\prime\prime}_{\text{Text}}
. For instance, Time-LLM
[
42
]
introduces a reprogramming space that transforms time series into text prototypes, effectively bridging the modality gap by aligning numeric representations with textual embeddings. TimeCAP
[
46
]
utilizes a multimodal adapter to retrieve semantically relevant textual summaries as contextual examples, which are then used as contextual prompts for LLM inference.

Trainable Adapter
is inserted between numerical adapters and LLMs. These adapters are designed to learn representations that not only preserve temporal semantics but are also inherently compatible with LLMs, thereby acting as alignment bridges across representations
[
117
]
. For example, TEST
[
91
]
employs contrastive learning at multiple instance-wise, feature-wise, and text-prototype-aligned time series embeddings for alignment. Adapter4TS
[
78
]
learns temporal positional encodings that guide the adapter to preserve chronological structure in the latent space. LeRet
[
35
]
further learns representations of task-specific temporal patterns. TimeXL
[
39
]
jointly learns numerical and textual prototypes in a shared space, encouraging consistent representations across modalities.

This paradigm enables flexible alignment with LLMs through semantic adapters, but is limited by its complexity. The instructions given to participants are as follows:

Our position:
Bridging Alignment operates at an intermediate instructional level, providing a flexible solution. With low resource costs and high reasoning improvements, we advocate prioritizing its use in the general alignment, emphasizing time-series characteristics in application settings. However, it introduces additional inference latency compared to finetuning due to the integration of external adapter modules. Practitioners should carefully evaluate whether the improvements in alignment quality outweigh the reduction in runtime performance. Moreover, effective deployment relies on proficiency in hyperparameter tuning and adapter debugging.

Further Discussion

Acknowledging diverse perspectives and potential curiosities, our perspectives initiate ongoing practical discussion. We posit that the

5.1
Omni-modal Alignment

5.2
Infinite-length Alignment

Streaming time-series data from real-world sensors is typically continuous, unbounded, and non-stationary

(1) storage constraints

Conclusion

In summary, we advocate prioritizing
alignment paradigms
, grounded in intrinsic data primitives, over task-specific customization. To support this perspective, we analyze a broad set of publicly available time-series datasets and identify three essential primitives in time series data: domain, characteristic, and representation. Based on these primitives, we propose three
alignment paradigms
:
Injective Alignment
,
Bridging Alignment
, and
Internal Alignment
. To provide practical instructions for practitioners, we systematically review and classify relevant literature since 2023 within
