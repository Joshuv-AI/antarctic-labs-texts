MIXPRS enables multi-population and multi-method polygenic risk scores using summary statistics
Authors: Leqi Xu, Yikai Dong, Xiaowei Zeng, Zeyu Bian, Geyu Zhou, Leying Guan, Hongyu Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMany multi-population polygenic risk score (PRS) methods have been proposed to improve prediction in underrepresented populations; however, no single method performs best across all scenarios. Although integrating PRSs across multiple methods and populations may improve prediction, this approach is often limited by the need for individual-level tuning data. Here we introduce MIXPRS, a robust framework based on the data fission paradigm for combining multiple multi-population PRS methods using only genome-wide association study summary statistics. MIXPRS uses single nucleotide polymorphism pruning to mitigate linkage disequilibrium mismatch and non-negative least squares regression to estimate combination weights. Across simulations and real-data analyses spanning up to 26 traits, MIXPRS consistently improves prediction accuracy over existing methods. We further extend this framework to MIXPRS+, incorporating functional annotations and clinical PRSs, yielding additional gains in non-European populations. MIXPRS relies only on summary statistics, thus offering broad accessibility and robustness for underrepresented populations.Subject terms: Population genetics, Genetics

Main

Historically, genetic analyses have predominantly focused on European populations owing to the availability of large cohorts1,2. As a result, genetic findings may not generalize well to other populations3,4. To address this data gap, there has been increasing emphasis on expanding genome-wide association studies (GWAS) in non-European populations, alongside the development of multi-population PRS methods5–27. These efforts have improved the accuracy and generalizability of genetic risk prediction across diverse populations.

Despite these advances, no multi-population PRS method consistently demonstrates superior performance across populations and traits14,19–21 because of differences in model assumptions and genetic architectures. Integrating multiple PRS methods can improve robustness, but typically requires individual-level tuning data, which are often unavailable for underrepresented populations. In addition, GWAS meta-analysis yields a single aggregated summary statistics dataset, limiting access to independent data for training and tuning and increasing the risk of overfitting.

To address this challenge, pseudo-GWAS subsampling has been proposed to derive independent training and tuning datasets from a single set of GWAS summary statistics28–30. Recent advances further reformulate PRS integration to operate entirely on summary statistics by transforming individual-level optimization problems into equivalent summary-level formulations29,30. However, these approaches lack a formal statistical framework, are largely limited to single-population settings and often overlook linkage disequilibrium (LD) mismatch between GWAS summary statistics and reference panels.

To overcome these limitations, we introduce MIXPRS, a multi-population PRS integration framework that operates solely on GWAS summary statistics. MIXPRS advances existing approaches in four key ways. First, it formalizes pseudo-GWAS subsampling within the data fission paradigm31–33, providing a principled framework for generating independent datasets. Second, it generalizes PRS integration by incorporating multiple multi-population PRS methods, improving performance in underrepresented populations. Third, it addresses LD mismatch by applying single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) pruning and estimating robust combination weights using non-negative least squares regression (NNLS). Fourth, we extend the framework to MIXPRS+, which incorporates functional annotations and clinical PRSs to further improve prediction performance.

We evaluate MIXPRS through extensive simulations and real-data analyses across up to 26 traits and four non-European populations (East Asian (EAS), African (AFR), South Asian (SAS) and Admixed American (AMR)) using the UK Biobank (UKBB) and All of Us (AoU) datasets. By integrating the strengths of multiple PRS methods, MIXPRS consistently improves prediction accuracy across diverse scenarios. MIXPRS+ further improves performance for traits such as body mass index (BMI) and coronary artery disease (CAD). Given that MIXPRS relies only on GWAS summary statistics, it eliminates the need for individual-level tuning data and enhances robustness and generalizability across populations.

Results

Overview of MIXPRS

MIXPRS is a PRS integration framework based on the data fission paradigm that combines PRS derived from multiple populations and methods using only GWAS summary statistics. The framework consists of three key steps (Fig. 1): step 1, GWAS subsampling to create independent training and tuning datasets; step 2, estimation of combination weights for different PRS methods; and step 3, derivation of the final integrated MIXPRS. Detailed implementations are provided in the Methods.

MIXPRS workflow.

MIXPRS integrates multi-population PRS methods through three key steps. Step 1 comprises GWAS subsampling in MIXPRS. MIXPRS generates subsampled training and tuning GWAS datasets from the original GWAS summary statistics of the target population, using only LD-pruned SNPs with an identity covariance structure. Step 2 involves estimation of MIXPRS combination weights for different PRS methods. This step consists of two substeps: calculation of PRS using subsampled training GWAS along with LD-pruned GWAS summary statistics from other populations using JointPRS-auto and SDPRX; and estimation of PRS combination weights by applying NNLS to the subsampled tuning GWAS and previously calculated PRS. Step 3 is the derivation of MIXPRS. PRSs are computed using the original GWAS summary statistics from all populations using JointPRS-auto and SDPRX, then combined using the PRS combination weights obtained in step 2 to produce the final MIXPRS. Step 1: GWAS subsampling in MIXPRS

This step uses GWAS summary statistics from the target population. MIXPRS applies data fission to partition a single GWAS dataset into independent subsampled training and tuning datasets31,32. To mitigate LD mismatch between GWAS summary statistics and reference panels, SNPs are pruned using the 1000 Genomes Project34, retaining variants with pairwise correlation below 0.5 within 250 kb windows35. MIXPRS adopts an identity covariance structure as a robust approximation. We note that LD-pruned data are used only in step 2 for weight estimation, while the full SNP set from the original GWAS summary statistics is retained in step 3 for PRS construction.

Step 2: estimation of MIXPRS combination weights for different PRS methods

This step consists of two components. First, multi-population PRS methods, including JointPRS-auto14 and SDPRX18, are applied using GWAS summary statistics from both the target and other populations. Subsampled, LD-pruned training GWAS datasets from step 1 are used for the target population, while summary statistics from other populations are restricted to the same SNP set. LD reference panels from the 1000 Genomes Project34 are used to compute PRS effect sizes. Second, the resulting PRS effect sizes are integrated using NNLS regression36, which estimates non-negative weights to combine PRS models and suppresses noisy contributions without additional tuning parameters.

Step 3: derivation of MIXPRS

In the final step, PRS effect sizes are recalculated using the full original GWAS summary statistics from all populations with JointPRS-auto and SDPRX. These are then combined using the weights estimated in step 2 to derive the final integrated MIXPRS, improving prediction performance across populations.

Evaluation of MIXPRS performance and strategy in simulations

We conducted simulation studies to benchmark MIXPRS against existing multi-population PRS methods and evaluate the contribution of its key strategies. Simulations were based on European GWAS summary statistics from UKBB (EUR; n = 311,600)37 and non-European GWAS summary statistics generated from simulated genotypes using 1000 Genomes haplotypes with varying sample sizes19. This hybrid design follows common practice in multi-population PRS studies.

For benchmarking, we compared MIXPRS with JointPRS14, XPASS15, SDPRX18, PRS-CSx17, MUSSEL20, PROSPER21 and BridgePRS22. EUR GWAS used n = 311,600, and non-European GWAS used n = 15,000 and 80,000. Methods requiring tuning data used an additional 10,000 individuals, whereas MIXPRS, XPASS and SDPRX relied only on discovery summary statistics. MIXPRS, JointPRS, PRS-CSx, MUSSEL and PROSPER incorporated GWAS summary statistics from all five populations (EUR, EAS, AFR, SAS and AMR), whereas XPASS, SDPRX and BridgePRS used GWAS summary statistics from the EUR population together with the corresponding non-European target population only.

As shown in Fig. 2 and Supplementary Tables 1 and 2, MIXPRS consistently outperformed other methods across all causal SNP proportions (P = 0.1, 0.01, 0.001 and 5 × 10−4) and sample sizes. On average, MIXPRS improved prediction accuracy by 3.2% over JointPRS, 213.1% over XPASS, 8.7% over SDPRX, 0.4% over PRS-CSx, 19.4% over MUSSEL, 19.5% over PROSPER and 47.5% over BridgePRS across four non-European populations (EAS, AFR, SAS and AMR). Notably, JointPRS and PRS-CSx required additional tuning data, whereas MIXPRS achieved stronger performance using only discovery summary statistics.

We further evaluated three aspects of MIXPRS: (1) comparison with individual-level (IndPRS) and idealized summary-based (IdealPRS) integration; (2) comparison with its component methods; and (3) robustness to model assumptions. These analyses used EUR GWAS (n = 311,600) and simulated non-European GWAS (n = 100,000).

For aspect (1), the simulation design for IndPRS and IdealPRS is described in the Methods. As shown in Fig. 3a, MIXPRS achieved prediction accuracy comparable to both IndPRS and IdealPRS, with IndPRS showing slightly better performance in most settings, indicating that MIXPRS closely matched the individual-level benchmark while relying solely on GWAS summary statistics. As shown in Fig. 3b, ridge, lasso, elastic net and NNLS yielded similar prediction accuracy, with NNLS requiring no hyperparameter tuning.

For aspect (2), we compared MIXPRS with its component methods. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 3, JointPRS-auto performed better in denser settings, whereas SDPRX performed better in sparser settings. MIXPRS consistently matched or exceeded both methods across all scenarios, demonstrating improved stability.

For aspect (3), we evaluated Gaussianity and sensitivity to LD mismatch. Detailed procedures are provided in the Methods. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 2, standardized residuals from the original GWAS closely followed the N(0,1) distribution even at n = 15,000, supporting the Gaussian approximation. We then compared three strategies: RefLD_Full (full SNP set with LD reference panels), RefLD_Prune (LD-pruned SNPs with LD reference panels) and Identity_Prune (LD-pruned SNPs with an identity covariance structure). As shown in Supplementary Fig. 3a and Supplementary Table 4, residual correlations computed from standardized residuals of the subsampled training and tuning GWASs were reduced under both pruning strategies. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 3b and Supplementary Table 5, prediction accuracy reflected a tradeoff between reducing LD mismatch and removing informative SNPs. Gains were largest in sparse settings, whereby Identity_Prune improved prediction by up to 10.20% in EUR and up to 10.50% in non-European populations, whereas effects were minimal or negative in denser settings. Overall, Identity_Prune provided the most robust performance, particularly under sparse architectures or LD mismatch.

Effectiveness and robustness of MIXPRS strategies in UKBB

To evaluate MIXPRS using real data, we performed three analyses: comparison with PRS integration methods using individual-level tuning data; comparison with a summary-level PRS integration baseline; and assessment under different covariance structures and SNP pruning lists. We analyzed traits with available non-European GWAS summary statistics, including 26 traits in EAS, 11 in AFR, four in SAS and four in AMR, using UKBB target cohorts. Sample sizes and SNP counts for discovery GWAS are provided in Supplementary Tables 6 and 7, and UKBB evaluation cohort sizes are given in Supplementary Table 8.

First, we compared IndPRS under four linear combination strategies (ridge, lasso, elastic net and NNLS) with MIXPRS. IndPRS uses fourfold cross-validation to combine component PRS models using individual-level tuning folds and evaluates performance in UKBB by averaging across testing folds. MIXPRS estimates weights directly from discovery GWAS and is evaluated on the same UKBB testing folds. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 4, the four IndPRS strategies yielded similar improvements within each population. Compared with MIXPRS, IndPRS achieved an average improvement of approximately 5% in EAS and AFR, with essentially no improvement in SAS and AMR. These results indicate that individual-level integration provides only modest additional benefit and that MIXPRS captures most of the gains from combining multiple PRS.

Second, we evaluated MIXPRS (Prune_NNLS) relative to a summary-level baseline (Full_Linear). Prune_NNLS uses pruned SNPs with an identity covariance matrix and NNLS, whereas Full_Linear uses all SNPs with an LD reference panel and linear regression. As shown in Fig. 4 and Supplementary Table 9, Prune_NNLS substantially outperformed Full_Linear, improving prediction by 9.48% in EAS, 4.49% in AFR, 33.33% in SAS and 43.52% in AMR. To assess the contribution of each strategy, we compared Prune_Linear with Full_Linear and Prune_NNLS with Prune_Linear. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 5 and Supplementary Table 9, pruning alone improved prediction by 3.57% in EAS, 1.85% in AFR, 27.17% in SAS and 28.51% in AMR. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 6 and Supplementary Table 9, NNLS provided additional gains of 5.73% in EAS, 2.59% in AFR, 4.57% in SAS and 11.47% in AMR. Together, these results show that both SNP pruning and NNLS independently improve MIXPRS performance.

Finally, we assessed robustness to covariance structure and SNP pruning lists. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 7 and Supplementary Table 10, MIXPRS performed robustly using either an identity covariance matrix or an LD reference matrix, with slightly better performance under the identity covariance matrix across populations, suggesting that it is a stable default when LD alignment is imperfect. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 8 and Supplementary Table 11, MIXPRS also showed robust performance across different SNP pruning lists. By default, MIXPRS prioritizes variants with higher non-major allele frequencies (snplist_1), with subsequent lists (snplist_2, snplist_3 and snplist_4) prioritizing the remaining SNPs; among these, snplist_1 yielded slightly better prediction accuracy.

MIXPRS performance benchmarking without tuning data in UKBB

To evaluate prediction accuracy, we benchmarked MIXPRS in the setting without individual-level tuning data. All methods relied exclusively on the GWAS summary statistics in Supplementary Tables 6 and 7 and did not use individual-level data. We compared MIXPRS with four multi-population PRS methods applicable to this setting: JointPRS-auto, SDPRX, PRS-CSx-auto and XPASS. XPASS and SDPRX used GWAS summary statistics from the EUR and target populations, whereas the other methods incorporated all available populations. Prediction accuracy was evaluated using UKBB traits available for each non-European population, comprising 26 traits in EAS, 11 in AFR, four in SAS and four in AMR (Supplementary Table 8).

As shown in Fig. 5 and Supplementary Tables 12 and 13, MIXPRS consistently improved prediction performance across populations in the absence of tuning data. Compared with JointPRS-auto, MIXPRS achieved average improvements of 5.17% in EAS, 14.29% in AFR, 16.25% in SAS and 15.62% in AMR, demonstrating robust performance across populations.

We further compared MIXPRS with its component methods to assess the benefit of integration. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 9 and Supplementary Table 13, MIXPRS achieved the best performance for 18 out of 22 continuous traits and two out of four binary traits in EAS, seven out of nine continuous traits but none of the two binary traits in AFR, all four continuous traits in SAS and three out of four continuous traits in AMR. These results indicate that MIXPRS provides more consistent and stable performance across traits and populations, reducing variability relative to individual methods.

To highlight the advantage of multi-population PRS integration, we compared MIXPRS with PUMAS-EN30. We evaluated two versions differing in LD reference panels and the number of integrated PRS methods (see Methods). PUMAS-EN used the 1000 Genomes LD reference throughout and integrated four single-population PRS methods (lassosum, LDpred2, PRS-CS and SBLUP). PUMAS-EN_paper followed the original implementation, using the 1000 Genomes LD reference for GWAS subsampling and the UKBB LD reference for PRS training and ensemble learning, and integrated eight single-population PRS methods (lassosum, LDpred2, PRS-CS, MegaPRS, SBayesR, DBSLMM, Vilma and SBLUP).

For fair comparison, both methods used a train-to-tune ratio of 3:1 and fourfold Monte Carlo cross-validation (MCCV), with discovery GWAS from the same source (Supplementary Table 6). MIXPRS integrates GWAS summary statistics from five populations, whereas PUMAS-EN uses only the EUR and target population GWAS. In addition, subsampled GWAS sets differ because PUMAS-EN reserves 10% of GWAS for tuning elastic-net hyperparameters, whereas MIXPRS uses NNLS and requires no tuning parameters.

As shown in Supplementary Fig. 10 and Supplementary Table 14, MIXPRS consistently outperformed both PUMAS-EN versions across four lipid traits in three non-European populations (EAS, AFR, SAS); AMR was excluded to match the original PUMAS-EN benchmarking. MIXPRS achieved average improvements over PUMAS-EN and PUMAS-EN_paper of 300.97% and 37.55% in EAS, 101.68% and 14.21% in AFR, and 210.08% and 43.69% in SAS.

The performance gap between PUMAS-EN and PUMAS-EN_paper probably reflects sensitivity to the choice of single-population PRS methods and potential overfitting owing to LD mismatch when using a single LD reference panel. MIXPRS mitigates this issue through SNP pruning while requiring only one LD reference panel and two multi-population PRS methods. The improvement over PUMAS-EN_paper further highlights the advantage of multi-population PRS methods that jointly model genetic effects across populations, improving prediction in non-European populations by leveraging shared genetic information.

MIXPRS performance benchmarking with tuning data in UKBB and AoU

We also benchmarked MIXPRS in settings with individual-level tuning data, considering two scenarios: tuning and testing within the same cohort, and tuning and testing across cohorts. In the same-cohort scenario, we performed fivefold cross-validation in UKBB using all available traits in each non-European population (26 in EAS, 11 in AFR, four in SAS and four in AMR). In the cross-cohort scenario, tuning was conducted in UKBB and testing in AoU using nine continuous traits in AFR and four in AMR. In both scenarios, we compared MIXPRS with JointPRS, XPASS, SDPRX, PRS-CSx, MUSSEL, PROSPER and BridgePRS.

In both scenarios, all methods were trained using discovery GWAS summary statistics (Supplementary Table 6). Methods requiring individual-level tuning data (JointPRS, PRS-CSx, MUSSEL, PROSPER and BridgePRS) additionally used tuning samples to select parameters, either from UKBB folds in the same-cohort setting or from the full UKBB in the cross-cohort setting. By contrast, MIXPRS, XPASS and SDPRX relied solely on discovery summary statistics. XPASS, SDPRX and BridgePRS used GWAS summary statistics from the EUR and target populations, whereas the other methods incorporated all available populations.

As shown in Fig. 6 and Supplementary Tables 12 and 15, MIXPRS consistently improved prediction performance across populations in the same-cohort scenario. Compared with JointPRS, MIXPRS achieved average improvements of 1.70% in EAS, 0.70% in AFR, 4.55% in SAS and 5.98% in AMR.

In the cross-cohort scenario, as shown in Fig. 6 and Supplementary Tables 12 and 16, MIXPRS again demonstrated consistent improvements. Compared with JointPRS, MIXPRS achieved average improvements of 6.07% in AFR and 5.58% in AMR, demonstrating robust performance across cohorts while requiring no individual-level data.

Additionally, we evaluated MIXPRS, IndPRS and SDPRX in AoU. IndPRS integrates PRS across methods and populations using individual-level UKBB data for tuning, followed by evaluation in AoU. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 11 and Supplementary Table 17, MIXPRS achieved performance comparable to IndPRS, indicating minimal information loss when using GWAS summary statistics instead of individual-level data. Both MIXPRS and IndPRS substantially outperformed SDPRX, highlighting the advantage of integrating PRS across methods and populations. Overall, MIXPRS achieved strong performance across scenarios without requiring individual-level tuning data.

Enhancing MIXPRS with clinical PRS and functional annotations

For complex traits such as BMI and CAD, large-scale GWAS meta-analyses have produced clinically oriented PRS, referred to here as ClinicalPRS38,39. In parallel, methods such as SBayesRC incorporate functional annotations into PRS estimation40. Building on MIXPRS, which integrates multi-population PRS methods, we extended the framework to incorporate ClinicalPRS and annotation-informed PRS (SBayesRC), termed MIXPRS+.

As our focus is on improving prediction in underrepresented populations, we evaluated MIXPRS+ only in non-European populations using available GWAS summary statistics. For BMI, GWASs are available for EUR, EAS and AFR, whereas for CAD, only EUR and EAS GWASs are available. Accordingly, we evaluated BMI in EAS and AFR and CAD in EAS. Discovery GWAS datasets are summarized in Supplementary Table 6, and the MIXPRS+ workflow is illustrated in Supplementary Figs. 12–14.

Integrating these components raises two considerations. First, SBayesRC requires genome-wide imputed SNP effects, preventing SNP pruning and the identity covariance approximation used in MIXPRS. MIXPRS+ therefore uses the full HapMap3 SNP set with LD reference panels from the 1000 Genomes Project. Although this may introduce LD mismatch, the integration remains advantageous because MIXPRS, SBayesRC and ClinicalPRS capture complementary information, including cross-population effects, functional annotations and large meta-analysis signals.

Second, ClinicalPRS differs fundamentally from other methods. ClinicalPRS data are derived from large meta-analysis GWAS whose summary statistics are not publicly available and therefore cannot be recalculated under GWAS subsampling. They are incorporated directly as external predictors in the weight-estimation step. Given that these meta-analyses are substantially larger than the publicly available GWAS used in MIXPRS+, the potential for overfitting is minimal. By contrast, JointPRS-auto, SDPRX and SBayesRC are constructed from the same publicly available discovery GWAS and must be recalculated using subsampled training GWAS. To avoid overfitting, their integration weights are then estimated using subsampled tuning GWAS together with ClinicalPRS.

We evaluated MIXPRS+ for BMI in EAS and AFR and for CAD in EAS, comparing it with MIXPRS, ClinicalPRS and SBayesRC. For BMI, we assessed both continuous prediction (R2) and obesity (BMI ≥ 27.5 in EAS and ≥30 in AFR)39. For obesity and CAD, we evaluated AUC, the odds ratio (OR) per s.d., the OR comparing the top and bottom 20% of the PRS distribution and calibration using the Hosmer–Lemeshow (HL) test.

As shown in Fig. 7a, MIXPRS+ achieved the best average rank across BMI in EAS and AFR and CAD in EAS, outperforming MIXPRS, SBayesRC and ClinicalPRS. Although ClinicalPRS, derived from larger meta-analysis GWAS, outperformed MIXPRS in some cases when used alone, MIXPRS+ effectively integrates ClinicalPRS with multi-population and annotation-informed PRS to improve overall performance using only publicly available summary statistics.

As shown in Fig. 7b, MIXPRS+ achieved the strongest overall performance across metrics. For BMI in EAS, MIXPRS+ had the highest R2, AUC, OR per s.d. and top–bottom quintile OR, with all methods well calibrated (HL test P > 0.05). Overall, incorporating ClinicalPRS and functional annotations into MIXPRS improves prediction accuracy, risk stratification and calibration. These results highlight the flexibility of MIXPRS, which can integrate diverse sources of information using only GWAS summary statistics, even when ClinicalPRS are available only as final PRS weights.

Discussion

In this study, we introduced MIXPRS, a multi-population PRS integration framework that combines PRS from multiple methods and populations using only GWAS summary statistics. By incorporating SNP pruning to mitigate LD mismatch and NNLS for robust weight estimation, MIXPRS improves prediction accuracy and stability. Across simulations and real-data analyses involving up to 26 traits in UKBB and AoU, MIXPRS consistently outperformed existing multi-population methods, including JointPRS, XPASS, SDPRX, PRS-CSx, MUSSEL, PROSPER and BridgePRS, as well as the single-population integration framework PUMAS-EN.

Traditional PRS methods often rely on individual-level data for tuning and integration, which can introduce bias and instability when tuning sample sizes are limited19–22. By contrast, MIXPRS, together with recent advances in summary-statistics-based PRS frameworks28–30,41, enables model training and integration using only GWAS summary statistics without requiring individual-level data. This design reduces privacy concerns, avoids noise from small tuning datasets and facilitates standardized benchmarking using publicly available data.

A key challenge addressed in this work is LD mismatch, which can substantially affect PRS performance but remains underexplored in existing methods. By analyzing residual correlations from pseudo-GWAS subsampling and their impact on prediction accuracy, we showed that LD mismatch contributes to residual dependence, leading to overfitting and reduced performance. Although SNP pruning provides a practical solution, alternative approaches, such as shrinkage of the LD matrix (for example, Ledoit–Wolf estimator42) or SNP filtering based on conditional distributions (for example, DENTIST43), may offer further improvements. Developing robust approaches to address LD mismatch remains an important direction for future work.

We further extended MIXPRS to MIXPRS+, which integrates multiple sources of information within a unified framework, including multi-population PRS methods, ClinicalPRS from large meta-analysis studies and functionally informed PRS (SBayesRC). Despite differences in model assumptions, MIXPRS+ effectively combines these components and further improves prediction performance in non-European populations. This extension highlights the flexibility of the framework and its potential to incorporate emerging PRS methods. Future work may extend this approach to integrate PRS across genetically correlated traits, which may provide additional gains44–48.

Several limitations warrant further investigation. First, the current implementation integrates two multi-population methods (JointPRS-auto and SDPRX) that do not require individual-level tuning data. Extending MIXPRS to incorporate additional methods that rely on individual-level data remains an important direction, particularly as summary-statistics-based adaptations of such methods continue to develop28–30,41. Second, our simulations relied on simulated non-European genotypes because of the limited availability of large-scale real datasets. As biobank-scale data in underrepresented populations become more widely available, future studies can further validate these findings. Third, real-data analyses were constrained by the availability of population-specific GWAS summary statistics. For example, limited data availability restricted evaluation of MIXPRS+ to BMI in EAS and AFR and CAD in EAS. Expanding GWAS resources in diverse populations will enable more comprehensive evaluation across traits and populations.

Methods

MIXPRS method

Step 1: GWAS subsampling in MIXPRS

LD pruning strategy implemented in MIXPRS to effectively mitigate LD mismatch

To address potential overfitting induced by LD mismatch, MIXPRS implements an LD pruning strategy. LD mismatch occurs because of slight variations in LD structures across genetic cohorts, which can accumulate significantly given the involvement of millions of SNPs, leading to substantial residual correlations and potential overfitting. Although the exact LD patterns differ slightly between cohorts, the overall LD structure within the same population generally remains consistent. Leveraging this consistency, MIXPRS selects approximately independent SNPs by retaining variants that exhibit pairwise correlation below 0.5 within sliding windows of 250 kb. These LD-pruned SNPs are identified using PLINK35 with a reference genotype panel matched to the target population, such as the 1000 Genomes Project. Subsequently, MIXPRS restricts the GWAS subsampling procedure exclusively to these LD-pruned SNPs and replaces the unknown LD matrix Dk with an identity covariance matrix (I). We adopt this identity approximation given that it is computationally lightweight and empirically robust under the pruned SNP setting. This approach significantly alleviates LD mismatch, enhancing robustness and mitigating overfitting in the GWAS subsampling and subsequent PRS integration steps.

Existing PRS methods

JointPRS(-auto)

JointPRS14 integrates GWAS summary statistics and LD reference panels from multiple populations through a shared Bayesian shrinkage model incorporating genetic correlation structures. When individual-level tuning data are unavailable, JointPRS-auto directly estimates genetic correlations and shrinkage parameters using GWAS summary statistics alone. When individual-level tuning data are available, JointPRS computes two versions: a meta version, which automatically estimates shrinkage parameters; and a tune version, which optimizes shrinkage parameters and performs linear combination across populations. The optimal version (meta or tune) is then selected using a data-adaptive strategy based on prediction accuracy evaluated in the individual-level tuning dataset.

XPASS

XPASS15 jointly integrates GWAS summary statistics and LD structures from two populations using a bivariate Gaussian distribution to model genetic correlation, facilitating information transfer from an auxiliary population to a target population. It also incorporates population-specific effects identified through a P+T procedure. Importantly, XPASS does not require individual-level tuning data.

SDPRX

SDPRX18 uses a hierarchical Bayesian framework to model GWAS summary statistics and LD structures from two populations. It categorizes SNP effects into none, population-specific or shared across populations. The shared component is modeled using mixtures of bivariate Gaussian distributions informed by genetic correlation. SDPRX does not require individual-level tuning data.

PRS-CSx(-auto)

PRS-CSx17, an extension of PRS-CS49, integrates GWAS summary statistics from multiple populations using a shared Bayesian shrinkage prior. In the absence of tuning data, PRS-CSx-auto automatically estimates a global shrinkage parameter through a Bayesian approach. When tuning data are available, PRS-CSx selects the best-performing shrinkage parameter from a predefined set and performs linear combination across populations, optimizing prediction accuracy.

MUSSEL

MUSSEL20 integrates GWAS summary statistics and LD structures from multiple populations using a multivariate spike-and-slab prior capturing genetic correlation. This method necessitates individual-level tuning data to determine parameters, including causal SNP proportion, heritability within populations and between-population correlations. A subsequent super-learning step selects from linear methods (lasso, ridge, elastic net and linear regression) to further optimize predictions.

PROSPER

PROSPER21 uses a penalized linear regression framework, combining lasso and ridge penalties to account for genetic sparsity and population similarities from multiple populations. Individual-level tuning data are required to select optimal penalty parameters. An additional super-learning step selects from linear methods (lasso, ridge, elastic net and linear regression) to integrate PRS generated across penalty parameters and populations, further enhancing prediction performance.

BridgePRS

BridgePRS22 incorporates GWAS summary statistics from two populations by modeling shared and population-specific SNP effects. Initially, it applies Gaussian priors to estimate SNP effects separately for each population. Subsequently, it integrates auxiliary population information to refine effect-size estimates in the target population. Individual-level tuning data are required to optimally combine these estimates into a final PRS score using ridge regression.

PUMAS-EN

PUMAS-EN30 is a single-population PRS integration framework designed to integrate PRS from multiple populations and methods through an ensemble learning approach using GWAS summary statistics and LD reference panels from various populations.

Two versions were considered in our evaluation: the first version, PUMAS-EN, used only the 1000 Genomes Project LD reference panel throughout all integration stages, including GWAS subsampling, PRS training and ensemble learning, closely matching the MIXPRS setup. This version incorporated four commonly used single-population PRS methods: lassosum50, LDpred2 (ref. 51), PRS-CS49 and SBLUP52. The second version, PUMAS-EN_paper, used the 1000 Genomes Project LD reference panel only for GWAS subsampling but used UKBB genotype data for PRS training and ensemble learning. This version included a broader set of eight single-population PRS methods: lassosum, LDpred2, PRS-CS, MegaPRS53, SBayesR54, DBSLMM55, Vilma56 and SBLUP.

Both methods subsequently integrate these diverse PRS models through a penalized regression approach optimized using fourfold MCCV. Ensemble parameters are tuned with subsampled GWAS summary statistics, thus removing any requirement for individual-level data. The final ensemble model weights are determined by averaging coefficients across MCCV folds.

To ensure fair comparisons across methods, we extracted the set of 717,985 SNPs that were common across the LD reference panels of all methods and populations. This intersection ensures that all methods use the same set of SNPs in GWAS.

Simulation integration strategies

We compared MIXPRS with two benchmark strategies: an individual-level integration approach (IndPRS) and an idealized summary-based approach (IdealPRS). MIXPRS and IdealPRS relied on GWAS summary statistics, generating training and tuning GWASs by splitting the effective sample size of the discovery GWAS into 75% and 25% subsets through data fission across four replicates and averaging the resulting weights. MIXPRS estimated weights using LD-pruned SNPs under an identity covariance structure, whereas IdealPRS used the full SNP set with an LD covariance matrix perfectly matched to the discovery samples, representing an idealized GWAS subsampling setting and serving as an upper bound for summary-based integration. By contrast, IndPRS required individual-level data and directly partitioned the discovery individuals into 75% for GWAS and 25% for individual-level tuning data, again repeated four times with averaged weights. This unified design ensured that any performance differences reflected the integration strategies rather than differences in discovery data or component models, thereby enabling a direct comparison of MIXPRS performance with both the individual-level benchmark (IndPRS) and the idealized summary-based benchmark (IdealPRS).

GWAS summary statistics

We compiled GWAS summary statistics for all available traits in each population, including 26 traits in EUR, 26 in EAS, 11 in AFR, four in SAS and four in AMR, from multiple consortia5–9,11,13,59–68. Quality control followed LDHub guidelines using LDSC to exclude duplicate SNPs, strand-ambiguous SNPs (A/T and G/C), insertions and deletions, and SNPs with effective sample sizes less than 0.67 times the 90th percentile threshold69,70. To facilitate fair comparisons across methods while accommodating population-specific genetic variants, SNP sets for each population were further restricted to variants consistently available across all evaluated methods within the corresponding population. The 1000 Genomes Project served as the LD reference panel. Comprehensive information on sample sizes and the number of SNPs for GWAS summary statistics is provided in Supplementary Tables 6 and 7. Given our primary objective to evaluate prediction accuracy specifically in non-European populations (EAS, AFR, SAS and AMR), we verified that no overlap existed between training GWAS datasets and individual-level tuning or testing datasets derived from the UKBB or AoU cohorts in these populations. Although overlaps may exist between the EUR training GWAS and corresponding tuning or testing datasets, any resulting bias is considered negligible for the evaluation within non-European populations.

Access to individual-level data from the All of Us Research Program can be requested through the All of Us data access portal (https://www.researchallofus.org/data-tools/data-access/).

The code for all simulation studies and real-data analyses presented in this paper is publicly available on GitHub (https://github.com/LeqiXu/MIXPRS_analysis) and archived at Zenodo (10.5281/zenodo.19599991)74. Software implementing the proposed MIXPRS method is available at GitHub (https://github.com/LeqiXu/MIXPRS) and archived at Zenodo (10.5281/zenodo.19599993)75. For comparative analyses, implementations of other methods are accessible through their respective repositories: JointPRS (https://github.com/YCSGP/JointPRS), XPASS (https://github.com/YangLabHKUST/XPASS), SDPRX (https://github.com/eldronzhou/SDPRX), PRS-CSx (https://github.com/getian107/PRScsx), MUSSEL (https://github.com/Jin93/MUSSEL), PROSPER (https://github.com/Jingning-Zhang/PROSPER), BridgePRS (https://github.com/clivehoggart/BridgePRS), PUMAS-EN (https://github.com/qlu-lab/PUMAS) and SBayesRC (https://github.com/zhilizheng/SBayesRC).
