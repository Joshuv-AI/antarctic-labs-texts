Application of Digital Technology in Palliative Care in Japan
Authors: Kazuyuki Niki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo provide a comprehensive overview of the use of digital technology in palliative care, focusing on its potential and challenges.MethodsA narrative review of recent literature and clinical applications in Japan was conducted by examining smartphone applications, extended reality (XR), electronic patient-reported outcomes (ePRO), non-wearable sensors, and artificial intelligence.ResultsSmartphone applications provide continuous psychological support and alleviate loneliness. XR (mainly virtual reality) offers distraction therapy for symptom relief and fulfills patients’ “last wishes.” ePRO systems and non-wearable bed sensors enable proactive care through real-time symptom and agitation monitoring. AI assists in predicting non-visible symptoms and formulating personalized care plans. Despite these benefits, challenges such as the digital divide, ethical and legal concerns, and the need for high-quality evidence remain.ConclusionDigital technologies can significantly enhance non-pharmacological interventions in palliative care. Realizing “personalized digital palliative care” requires the integration of these tools according to individual patient needs, along with the resolution of ethical and technical issues.Keywords: Digital technology, Palliative care, Virtual reality, Patient reported outcome measures, Electronics, Artificial intelligence

INTRODUCTION

Palliative care improves the quality of life (QOL) of patients and their families facing life-threatening illnesses by addressing total pain and encompassing physical, psychological, social, and spiritual dimensions. To manage this multidimensional suffering, diverse non-pharmacological approaches are essential, along with pharmacotherapy. Recently, particularly triggered by the coronavirus disease (COVID-19) pandemic, non-pharmacological methods that utilize digital technology have gained significant attention. Digital Therapeutics and Software as a Medical Device are rapidly being developed globally to prevent, manage, or treat diseases. This review focuses on smartphone applications, extended reality (XR), electronic patient-reported outcomes (ePRO), sheet-type bed sensors, and artificial intelligence (AI) as key digital technologies in palliative care. By summarizing the latest clinical research and evidence, primarily from Japan, this study explores specific clinical applications, ethical and social challenges, and prospects for integrating digital technology into humanized care (Table 1).

SMARTPHONE APPLICATIONS

Smartphone applications offer crucial psychological support for patients with cancer with anxiety regarding recurrence. In a decentralized randomized trial, Akechi et al. [1] evaluated a smartphone intervention that provided problem-solving and behavioral activation for breast cancer survivors. At week 8, the intervention group showed significant improvements across multiple measures, including fear of recurrence, depression, and psychological needs, compared to the control group. These improvements were maintained at week 24, with depression scores decreasing further. Given the shortage of available therapists, application-based support is a promising alternative.

EXTENDED REALITY (XR) TECHNOLOGY

XR, particularly virtual reality (VR), contributes significantly to non-pharmacological distress relief through high immersion. The primary proposed mechanism is “distraction therapy,” which diverts attention from unpleasant internal sensations to a virtual environment. Jones et al. [2] reported a significant reduction in Numerical Rating Scale pain scores during a single 5-minute VR session among 30 patients with chronic pain (Before vs. During vs. After: 5.7 vs. 2.6 vs. 4.1), with one-third of patients experiencing complete pain relief. Beyond symptom relief, VR transcends physical limitations to fulfill patients’ “last wishes.” Our preliminary prospective study involving 20 patients with terminal cancer demonstrated that “virtual travel” significantly improved various physical and psychological symptoms, including pain, fatigue, and depression, without severe side effects [3] (Figure 1). VR also serves as a powerful tool for rebuilding family connections during visiting restrictions by providing a sense of security and immersion [4]. Recently, we expanded VR applications to continuous supportive care and found that consecutive 7-day VR interventions (10 minutes a day) delayed the onset and reduced the severity of delayed nausea associated with chemotherapy [5].

ELECTRONIC PATIENT-REPORTED OUTCOMES (EPRO)

ePRO systems electronically collect and manage health status reports directly from patients, transitioning palliative care from a reactive “pull-type” to a proactive “push-type” model. This enables the early detection and treatment of distress. Basch et al. [6] highlighted the profound impact of ePRO, reporting that routine symptom monitoring via ePRO significantly prolonged overall survival in patients with cancer (median: 31.2 vs. 26.0 months) by allowing nurses to intervene promptly with counseling and medication adjustments. Similarly, a Japanese multicenter trial by Kikawa et al. [7] suggested that ePRO monitoring of vital signs can maintain patients’ QOL during chemotherapy. Although ePRO shows high feasibility, challenges remain, including optimizing evaluation items for sensitive themes such as spiritual distress and carefully setting alert thresholds to balance clinical benefits with provider burden [8].

NON-WEARABLE BED SENSORS FOR OBJECTIVE AGITATION MONITORING

Delirium and terminal restlessness are critical concerns in palliative care. However, conventional assessment scales depend on subjective periodic observations by clinicians. To establish a more continuous and objective measurement, Otani et al. [9] conducted a large-scale prospective observational study (N=971 patients, 3,028 assessments) in a Japanese palliative care unit using a sheet-type non-wearable sensor (Nemuri SCAN [NSCAN]) placed under a mattress. The study demonstrated that NSCAN activity scores, calculated per minute based on the frequency and intensity of body movements, were significantly correlated with agitation levels, as assessed using the modified Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale (RASS) (P<0.001). Mean activity scores monotonically increased in accordance with rising agitation levels: 28.9 for RASS -5 (unarousable), 62.6 for RASS 0 (alert and calm), and 118.7 for RASS 3 (very agitated). These findings highlight the ability of continuous 24-hour monitoring via non-wearable sensors to accurately quantify terminal agitation without patient burden, thereby overcoming interpretation biases and providing real-time data to facilitate timely pharmacological or environmental interventions for optimal symptom control.

ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE (AI) IN PALLIATIVE CARE

AI, particularly machine learning, extracts patterns from vast amounts of medical data to improve care quality, ranging from prognostic predictions to personalized care planning. A significant challenge in terminal care is the assessment of non-visible symptoms when patients lose their communication abilities. Shimada and Tsuneto [10] examined 213 patients with cancer who received palliative care team interventions to determine whether machine learning (decision tree analysis) could be used to predict patients’ non-visible symptoms (pain, dyspnea, fatigue, somnolence, anxiety, delirium, insufficient informed consent, and spiritual issues). The maximum and minimum values for predictive accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity, as measures of the overall predictive performance of the model developed by Shimada and Tsuneto [10], were 88.0%/55.5%, 84.9%/3.3%, and 96.7%/24.1%, respectively. Somnolence had the highest predictive accuracy (88.0%) and specificity (96.7%). Pain showed the highest sensitivity (84.9%) among the non-visible symptoms, but the lowest specificity (24.1%). Based on these findings, the authors reported that their predictive model may be useful as a support tool for predicting symptoms that are difficult to assess objectively based on patient background information and easily assessable visible symptoms. Currently, AI applications are being explored to support patient communication and automate life review processes, thereby demonstrating the growing potential of AI in holistic psychological care.
