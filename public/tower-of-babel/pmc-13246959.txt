Chondrocytes reprogram chromatin in hypoxic microenvironments to activate CADM1-AS1/HDAC1 complex-mediated anti-inflammation signals
Authors: Weiyu Ni, Tianyuan Gu, Panyang Shen, Zixuan Mei, Yizheng Wu, Kamuran Yilamu, Nan Pan, Wentao Yang, Gang Liu, Yan Ma, Xiangqian Fang, Shunwu Fan, Haitao Zhang, Shuying Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Maintaining chondrocyte homeostasis is crucial for the effective treatment of osteoarthritis. Chondrocytes reside in a hypoxic environment under physiological conditions. This study, conducted exclusively in male mice, examines the association between epigenetics and chondrocyte homeostasis and observes significant alterations in histone acetylation in chondrocytes under hypoxic conditions. We identify a key enhancer element that regulates the long non-coding RNA CADM1-AS1, which is essential for cartilage homeostasis. This work elucidates a mechanism of epigenetic modulation that contributes to chondrocyte dysfunction in osteoarthritis. Further mechanistic investigations show that CADM1-AS1 recruits histone deacetylase complexes to suppress the transcriptional activation of NOS2, thereby affecting amino acid metabolism in chondrocytes. Conditional knockout of CADM1-AS1 in chondrocytes accelerates osteoarthritis development in mice. Using a hybrid exosome delivery system, we successfully modulate the expression of CADM1-AS1 and effectively reduce cartilage damage. Collectively, these findings reveal the importance of epigenetic regulation via CADM1-AS1-mediated histone deacetylation in the pathogenesis of osteoarthritis. Thus, CADM1-AS1 represents a potential therapeutic target for reducing cartilage damage in osteoarthritis and improving disease management.

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is a leading cause of disability worldwide, predominantly affecting the elderly population. It is characterized by the progressive degeneration of articular cartilage, subchondral bone sclerosis and osteophyte formation1. Although various factors such as aging, mechanical stress, and genetic predisposition contribute to OA development, the molecular and cellular mechanisms that drive disease progression remain inadequately understood. The complexity of OA pathogenesis has hindered the development of disease-modifying treatments, leaving current therapeutic options limited to symptom management and joint replacement surgery in advanced cases2.

At the cellular level, chondrocytes, the resident cartilage cells, play a critical role in maintaining cartilage homeostasis through the synthesis and turnover of the extracellular matrix (ECM)3. However, during OA progression, chondrocytes undergo phenotypic changes, including increased production of catabolic enzymes such as matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and aggrecanases, which degrade key ECM components like collagen type II and aggrecan4. This disruption in ECM integrity is exacerbated by inflammatory cytokines, such as interleukin-1β (IL-1β), IL-6, tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), and multiple chemokines, further accelerating cartilage destruction5. Despite extensive research, the exact molecular pathways that regulate chondrocyte function and ECM remodeling in OA remain elusive.

Recent advances in OA research have shifted attention toward the role of the tissue microenvironment, particularly hypoxia, in regulating chondrocyte behavior. Articular cartilage is inherently avascular, creating a naturally hypoxic environment where oxygen tension plays a vital role in cartilage physiology6. Hypoxia-inducible factors (HIFs), particularly HIF-1α, are central mediators of cellular adaptation low oxygen levels, orchestrating diverse biological processes, including glucose metabolism, autophagy, and ECM production7,8. HIF-1α deficiency impaired ATP maintenance in chondrocytes under hypoxia,and reveals its essential role in metabolic regulation9. HIF-1α has also been shown to promote ECM synthesis while suppressing catabolic enzyme expression, thereby conferring protection against osteoarthritic degeneration10. Moreover, stabilization of HIF-1α under hypoxic conditions promotes mitophagy in chondrocytes, thereby mitigating apoptosis and cellular senescence11. However, the precise molecular mechanisms by which HIF-1α exerts its effects, particularly through epigenetic modifications, are still poorly understood. It is increasingly clear that epigenetic dysregulation plays a fundamental role in shaping gene expression patterns in OA. Central to this regulation are enhancers, distal cis-regulatory elements that drive cell-type-specific transcriptional programs. Their aberrant activity is emerging as a key contributor to disease heterogeneity and progression. Integrating multi-omics approaches now offers unprecedented opportunities to systematically map these regulatory landscapes and unravel their functional roles in OA pathogenesis12. Among these epigenetic mechanisms, histone acetylation, specifically at enhancer regions marked by histone H3 lysine 27 acetylation (H3K27ac), is critical for regulating gene transcription in response to environmental cues, such as hypoxia13,14. Enhancers are dynamic regions of the genome that integrate signals from the cellular environment to control the expression of target genes15. However, the specific enhancers activated in chondrocytes under hypoxia remain largely unexplored.

In recent years, long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) have gained attention as key regulators of gene expression in OA16. These non-coding transcripts can modulate chromatin structure, recruit transcription factors, and interact with epigenetic modifiers to influence gene transcription17,18. Despite growing interest in lncRNAs in the context of OA, the functional role of specific lncRNAs in hypoxia-driven chondrocyte reprogramming has not been fully elucidated.

In this study, we sought to explore the epigenetic landscape of chondrocytes under hypoxic conditions to identify key regulatory elements that contribute to OA pathogenesis. Using integrated transcriptomic and epigenomic profiling, including RNA-seq and ATAC-seq, we discovered a hypoxia-responsive lncRNA, CADM1-AS1, which plays a critical role in ECM remodeling. We demonstrate that CADM1-AS1 interacts with histone deacetylase complexes to modulate transcriptional activity, thereby regulating the expression of crucial OA-related genes. Notably, CADM1-AS1 downregulates Nos2, a gene implicated in inflammatory responses and cartilage degradation, through histone deacetylation.

Despite progress in understanding OA, the mechanisms by which hypoxia shapes the epigenetic landscape of chondrocytes remain poorly defined. Here, we investigate the role of the long noncoding RNA CADM1-AS1 in regulating chondrocyte function under hypoxic conditions and explore its potential relevance to OA pathogenesis. By addressing this gap, our study provides a framework for considering epigenetic regulators as promising targets for therapeutic intervention in OA.

Results

Histone modifications are present in chondrocytes under hypoxia

Initially, we used a high-throughput Illumina sequencing platform to investigate the transcriptome in human chondrocytes (HCs) under normoxia and hypoxia (Fig. 1A). A total of 13789 genes were identified, and we screened out 874 mRNA and 162 lncRNA that exhibited significantly different expression (Figure S1A, B). All 874 mRNA were used as the enrichment background. The gene functional enrichment and annotation analyses using Metascape19 revealed that PID_HIF1_TFPATHWAY was the most affected signaling pathway (Figure S1C, D). We performed Assay for Transposase-Accessible Chromatin using sequencing (ATAC-seq) to investigate the epigenetic modifications of chondrocytes, and the results identified all significantly differential peaks (P < 0.05), including both upregulated and downregulated peaks (Fig. 1B). Under low oxygen levels, there was a higher accumulation of ATAC-seq signals (Figure S2A). By performing an integrated analysis of ATAC-seq and transcriptome sequencing data, we identified a limited proportion of genes that exhibited concurrent increases in chromatin accessibility and transcription levels (Figure S2B). Integration with ATAC-seq data identified genes associated with differential chromatin accessibility peaks, comprising 206 mRNAs and 3 lncRNAs. Further analysis of genes exhibiting increased chromatin accessibility and elevated transcription levels, as well as those with reduced chromatin accessibility and decreased transcription, revealed their involvement in the HIF-1 signaling pathway according to Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis (Figure S2C). Within the dynamic peak changes in ATAC-seq data, we observed that promoters accounted for 2.9%, introns comprised 44.73%, and exons constituted 23.03% of the regulatory landscape (Figure S2D). These findings suggest that a substantial portion of gene transcription regulation in response to hypoxia is potentially mediated through enhancers. Afterward, we identified eight mRNAs and one lncRNA from the RNA-seq dataset that exhibited differential peaks (≥4) through our analysis of ATAC-seq data. This subset of genes is highly indicative of regulation by enhancers. (Figure S2E and F). By interrogating HCs using H3K4me1 and H3K27ac CUT&Tag sequencing libraries, coupled with ATAC sequencing data, we observed a noteworthy overlap between signal segments labeled by H3K27ac and ATAC upregulation bounds under hypoxic conditions (Figure S2G). This finding implicates a potential functional association between actively marked enhancer regions, denoted by H3K27ac, and the accessibility of the underlying chromatin landscape. To investigate the regulatory role of enhancers on genes in chondrocytes, we selected CADM1 as the gene of interest based on its high peak count (≥ 4) and conservation (alignment scores > 100) in the genome (Fig. 1C and Figure S2H). We further analyzed the four peaks and found that peak1 did not show significant overlap between ATAC and H3K27ac sequencing results, suggesting that peak1 may not function as an enhancer (Fig. 1D). Additionally, peaks 2 and 4 correspond to promoter regions of different CADM1 transcripts, indicating they are unlikely to serve as potential enhancers. Consequently, our subsequent studies focused on peak3. To identify the function of peak3, we knocked out peak3 with the CRISPR-Cas9 system in C28/I2 cells. Gene editing was verified using PCR and Sanger sequencing (Figure S3A, B). RT-qPCR analysis revealed that CADM1-AS1 expression, but not CADM1, was regulated by peak3 (Figure S3C, D). Since peak 3 is located 10 kb away from CADM1-AS1, we inferred that it functions as a super enhancer element for the regulation of CADM1-AS1 expression. Next, we investigated the spatial relationship of peak3 in nuclei using chromosome conformation capture (3 C). The 3 C assay detected high interaction frequencies (Fig. 1E), suggesting that the three-dimensional (3D) organization of the chromosome brings peak3 into proximity with the proximal promoter of CADM1-AS1. To evaluate the functional role of gene-distal enhancers, we established two dCas9-based enhancer-targeting epigenetic perturbation systems to regulate enhancer activity in situ. To induce enhancer activity, we generated a fusion construct by combining dCas9 with the core domain of the histone acetyltransferase p300, which catalyzes H3K27ac modifications (Fig. 1F). RT-qPCR analysis confirmed that the peak3-specific gRNA, when coupled with dCas9-p300, significantly activated CADM1-AS1 expression (Fig. 1G). For targeted enhancer repression, we engineered the enCRISPRi cell line by replacing p300 with the KRAB-MeCP2 repressor module (Fig. 1H). RT-qPCR analysis revealed that CADM1-AS1 was decreased upon recruitment of dCas9-KRAB-MeCP2 to peak3 (Fig. 1I). These findings demonstrate that peak3 functionally regulates CADM1-AS1 transcription, suggesting it may act as an enhancer for this lncRNA. Among the 874 genes that exhibited significantly different expression levels under hypoxia in Fig. 1A, Metascape revealed that Hif1-α is the top1 enriched TF based on the TRRUST database (Figure S3E). RT-qPCR analysis confirmed that CADM1-AS1 expression was elevated under hypoxia (Figure S3F). Since HIF-1α and HIF-2α are the main TFs in a hypoxic environment, we then investigated the influence of these two TFs on CADM1-AS1 expression. RT-qPCR analysis identified that overexpression of HIF-1α led to a significant upregulation of CADM1-AS1 expression, whereas HIF-2α had no effect (Figure S3G). According to the JASPAR database, the peak 4 region has seven potential HIF-1α binding sites. Subsequently, we generated luciferase reporter plasmids including a plasmid containing the peak 4 region and seven mutant plasmids. The luciferase assay further validated the involvement of HIF-1αin the regulation of CADM1-AS1 expression, and we hypothesized that motif 3 serves as the HIF-1α binding site (Figure S3H). Expression and functional characterization of CADM1-AS1 in human and mouse cartilage

Cartilage samples were obtained from the tibial plateau of patients with OA who underwent total knee arthroplasty. To reduce the influence of differences between donors on the experimental results, we used the damaged region from a sample as the OA sample and the undamaged region from the same sample as the control. First, we conducted histological analyses to determine the OA status of paired damaged and undamaged tissues in patients. Safranin O/fast green staining revealed reduced proteoglycan (PG) levels in the arthritic damaged areas compared to those in undamaged areas (Fig. 2A). RNA fluorescent in situ hybridization (FISH) staining revealed a reduction in CADM1-AS1 expression in the superficial and deep zone in damaged areas (Figure S4A). The reduced expression of CADM1-AS1 in damaged areas was further confirmed by northern blot analysis. A single band at the expected size (715 nt) was displayed using a CADM1-AS1-specific probe (Fig. 2B). Next, we performed an in vitro transcription and translation assay to confirm that CADM1-AS1 is non-coding (Fig. 2C). RNA FISH analysis showed that CADM1-AS1 expression was predominantly distributed in the nuclei of HCs, with MALAT1 serving as a positive control for nuclear RNA distribution (Fig. 2D). This result was also confirmed by nuclear-cytoplasmic distribution analysis using RT-qPCR (Figure S4B). To assess the involvement of CADM1-AS1 in the regulation of the ECM, we transfected HCs with CADM1-AS1 anti-sense oligonucleotides (ASOs) and verified their efficiency using RT-qPCR (Fig. 2E). When treated with IL-1β for 72 h as a positive control, human chondrocytes showed downregulation of both CADM1-AS1 and HIF-1α. Western blotting and RT-qPCR showed that CADM1-AS1 knockdown led to the upregulation of MMP13 and ADAMTS5 expression in HCs but the downregulation of SOX9, ACAN, and COL2A1 expression (Fig. 2F and Figure S4C). Time-course analysis (0–96 h) revealed a biphasic HIF-1α response, characterized by an early peak at 24 h followed by a significant decline at 48–96 h (Figure S4D, E). Although the CADM1-AS1 gene sequence was conserved in Mus musculus, no conserved transcript was found in the Ensembl database. To verify whether a conserved CADM1-AS1 sequence existed in Mus musculus, we analyzed the available datasets GSM2695465, GSM2695466, GSM2695467, GSM2695468, GSM2692350, and GSM2692351. RNA-seq revealed signals within the conserved region of CADM1-AS1, whereas no corresponding reads were detected in ribosome-associated RNA-seq (Fig. 2G). Furthermore, we blast-searched the sequence of CADM1-AS1 in NONCODE and found that NONMMUT134301.1 (hereafter Cadm1-as1) contained conserved sequences in Mus musculus. The alignment reveals 33.8% sequence identity (highlighted in red), calculated relative to the full-length human CADM1-AS1 transcript (Fig. 2H). RNA-seq showed signals in the CADM1-AS1 conserved region, but they were not observed in imprinting RNA-seq of ribosome-retrieved complexes (Figure S5A). The position of murine Cadm1-as1 within Cadm1 is illustrated in Figure S5B. Using RT-qPCR analysis, we found that Cadm1-as1 was abundant in the colon, skin, and cartilage (Figure S5C). To reveal the correlation between Cadm1-as1 and OA, we investigated Cadm1-as1 expression in naturally aged mice. Safranin O/fast green staining showed that age-related OA progression was exacerbated in experimental mice. This manifested as an increase in OARSI scores, Mankin scores, and thickening of the subchondral bone plate (SBP) (Fig. 2I). Our immunofluorescence (IF) analysis detected elevated P21 expression with advancing age, consistent with established senescence markers in OA. RNA FISH and IF analyses suggested a possible association between Cadm1-as1/HIF-1α downregulation and OA progression (Fig. 2J), though this observational correlation requires further mechanistic validation. Cadm1-as1 regulated ECM remodeling in vitro and in vivo

To investigate the role of Cadm1-as1 in the cartilage of mice, we transfected mouse chondrocytes (MCs) with Cadm1-as1 ASOs. The efficacy of the transfection was confirmed through RT-qPCR analysis (Figure S5D). Western blotting analysis revealed that the knockdown of Cadm1-as1 resulted in upregulated MMP13 and ADAMTS5 expression but downregulated SOX9, ACAN, and COL2A1 expression in MCs (Figure S5E). Adenoviral overexpression of Cadm1-as1 (Ad Cadm1-as1) upregulated SOX9, ACAN, and COL2A1 expression and downregulated MMP13 and ADAMTS5 expression (Figure S5F, G). Furthermore, we knocked out Cadm1-as1 in ATDC5 cells using the CRISPR-Cas9 system. The success of gene editing was confirmed through PCR amplification and subsequent Sanger sequencing (Figure S5H, I). The adenoviral Cadm1-as1 vector was then transfected into KO ATDC cells. Transfection efficiency was validated using RT-qPCR (Figure S5J). Cadm1-as1 knockout increased the expression of the catabolic enzymes MMP13 and ADAMTS5, while overexpression of Cadm1-as1 effectively restored the degradation of the ECM (Figure S5K). To further investigate whether reduced Cadm1-as1 expression accelerated ECM structural loss, we generated Cadm1-as1fl/fl mice. Cadm1-as1fl/fl primary chondrocytes were transfected with control or Cre adenovirus and subsequently cultured for a week. RT-qPCR validated the transfection and western blotting confirmed that Cadm1-as1 could regulate ECM remodeling (Fig. 3A, B, Figure S5L, M). 3D agarose culture, combined with alcian blue staining, was performed to assess the extracellular release of PGs from Cadm1-as1fl/fl primary chondrocytes. The results demonstrated a significant decrease in PGs release with Cadm1-as1 knockdown (Fig. 3C). To verify the function of Cadm1-as1 in vivo, we crossed Cadm1-as1fl/fl mice with ACAN- CreERT2 and Col2-CreERT2 mice to generate Cadm1-as1 conditional knockout (cKO) mice. RT-qPCR and FISH analysis of Cadm1-as1 expression across tissues confirms its successful and specific knockout in the cartilage of cKO mice (Figure S6A, B). Furthermore, RT-qPCR analysis confirmed that Cadm1 expression showed no significant difference between WT and cKO mice (Figure S6C). Next, we established a mouse model of knee OA by destabilizing of the medial meniscus (DMM) surgery. In Cadm1-as1 cKO mice, DMM-induced osteoarthritis pain was markedly exacerbated in the hot-plate test and rotarod analysis, whereas no significant difference was detected in the von Frey assay (Figure S7A, B). Reconstructed 3D coronal and sagittal micro-CT images revealed enhanced osteophyte formation in Cadm1-as1fl/fl, ACAN- CreERT2 and Cadm1-as1fl/fl, Col2- CreERT2mice with DMM-induced OA (Fig. 3D and Figure S7C). Following DMM surgery, Cadm1-as1 cKO mice exhibited more severe subchondral bone remodeling than control mice (Fig. 3E and Figure S7D). Manifestations of DMM-induced OA (cartilage destruction and thickening of SBP) were substantially enhanced in Cadm1-as1 cKO mice, compared with those in control mice after DMM surgery (Fig. 3F and Figure S7E). Additionally, Cadm1-as1 expression was measured using RNA FISH, while MMP13 and ACAN expression were measured using IF assay (Figure S7F, S7G). Moreover, we utilized an age-induced model of OA to investigate the effects of Cadm1-as1 on cartilage degeneration. Each mouse received an injection of Cadm1-as1 adeno-associated virus (AAV) into one knee joint, while the other knee joint was administered the control AAV (Fig. 3G). Histological sections were consistently taken from the medial tibial plateau, and both OARSI and Mankin scores were restricted to the medial compartment. The results demonstrated significant amelioration of cartilage degradation in the medial compartment of aged mouse knee joints following Cadm1-as1 administration. (Fig. 3H and Figure S8A). To further investigate whether Cadm1-as1 attenuated OA in vivo, we generated Cadm1-as1 knock-in (KI) mice at the locus of ROSA26 in C57BL/6JCya by CRISPR/Cas-mediated genome engineering. At this specific site, the transcription of Cadm1-as1 is not constrained by hypoxic conditions, indicating a distinct mechanism at play within this genomic region. As global KI mice, no overt phenotypic abnormalities were observed in Cadm1-AS1 knock-in mice. Body size and body weight were comparable to wild-type littermates at 8 weeks and 20 weeks of age (Figure S8B, C). Following DMM surgery on both WT and KI mice, it was observed that KI mice exhibited milder cartilage destruction (Fig. 3I and Figure S8D). Function of Cadm1-as1 mediated by histone deacetylase complex binding

As Cadm1-as1 is primarily localized in the nucleus, we hypothesize that it functions by targeting specific binding proteins. To identify the binding proteins that bind Cadm1-as1, we performed an RNA pulldown assay. Silver staining, combined with mass spectrometry analysis, demonstrated that biotin-labeled Cadm1-as1 bound to several proteins, including histone deacetylase (HDAC) 1, HDAC2, and pRB-associated proteins p48 (RBAP48), which are components of the histone deacetylation complex (Fig. 4A). Western blotting confirmed that Cadm1-as1 precipitated the main components of the deacetylated histone complex (Fig. 4B). To validate the direct binding of these components with Cadm1-as1, we performed an EMSA, which demonstrated that only HDAC1 can directly interact with Cadm1-as1 in vitro (Fig. 4C). The interaction between Cadm1-as1 and HDAC1 was further validated using a HDAC1 antibody and an EMSA (Fig. 4D). Additionally, the RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) assay provided further evidence of the interaction between Cadm1-as1 and HDAC1 (Fig. 4E). In addition, we have demonstrated through RNA pulldown assays that CADM1-AS1 also binds to HDAC1 in HCs (Figure S9A). Using molecular docking analyses, we found that the domains of HDAC1 have binding affinities for the double-helix hairpin structure of Cadm1-as1 (Fig. 4F). Predictive models and analysis indicated that various hydrogen-bond-forming sites in HDAC1 were important for Cadm1-as1 binding: Asn21, Tyr23, Tyr24, Lys31, His33, Asp99, Tyr201, Glu203, Arg212, Tyr333, Glu225, and Tyr336. To block hydrogen bond formation, we mutated adjacent amino acids of the binding sites to alanine (Figure S9B). The direct interaction between Cadm1-as1 and HDAC1 on Asn21, Tyr23, and Tyr24 was confirmed through a RIP assay (Fig. 4G). Compared with the HDAC1 WT, the distribution of the predicted amino acid residues in the HDAC1 MUT1 differed (Fig. 4H). A stable stem-loop structure of Cadm1-as1 was predicted by the RNAfold web server (Figure S9C). Due to the presence of three stem-loop structures (1–119, 120–250, 251–344), it can be inferred that Cadm1-as1 forms tertiary topological structures responsible for mediating its binding to HDAC1 (Figure S9B). Thus, we constructed stem-loop structure deletion mutant Cadm1-as1 respectively (Fig. 4I). Using a biotinylated RNA pulldown assay, we investigated the binding of Cadm1-as1 to HDAC1 and found that a specific fragment (120–250 nt) was essential for this interaction (Fig. 4J). Consistently, RNA FISH and immunocytochemistry staining confirmed that Cadm1-as1 was co-localized with HDAC1 (Fig. 4K). To determine whether Cadm1-as1 functions via binding with HDAC1 in vivo, WT, or MUT (120–250 nt deletion) Cadm1-as1 AAV was intra-articularly administered to DMM-induced OA mice. Overexpression efficiency was confirmed using an RNA FISH assay (Figure S9D). Cadm1-as1 treatment significantly attenuated cartilage destruction as shown by safranin O/fast green staining after DMM surgery, while MUT Cadm1-as1 treatment did not (Fig. 4L). OARSI scores, Mankin scores and subchondral bone remodeling further confirmed this result (Fig. 4L). Cadm1-as1 inhibits Nos2 transcriptional activation through histone deacetylation

To investigate the impact of Cadm1-as1 on gene transcription, total RNA was extracted from MCs treated with Ad Cadm1-as1 or Ad NC to construct RNA-seq libraries. As shown in the volcano plot, the RNA-seq data revealed that Mmp3 and Mmp13 expression was significantly downregulated while Col2a1 and Acan expression was upregulated in MCs overexpressing Cadm1-as1. Notably, Nos2 exhibited the most substantial fold-change difference among all differentially expressed genes (Fig. 5A). Heatmap showing expression patterns of 30 differentially expressed genes (|log2 fold-change | > 2) in MCs treated with Ad Cadm1-as1 versus or Ad NC (Fig. 5B). Nos2 demonstrated the most significant change, indicating that Nos2 is the primary target gene of Cadm1-as1. Further, the CUT&Tag assay revealed that the promoter locus of Nos2 was enriched near H3K27ac sites in MCs (Fig. 5C) and HCs (Figure S10A). Therefore, we speculate that Cadm1-as1 is a scaffold for the recruitment of the histone deacetylase complex to inhibit Nos2 transcription (Fig. 5D). To demonstrate the interaction between Cadm1-as1 and the Nos2 promoter, we separated this promoter region into ten segments (Fig. 5E). The Chromatin Isolation by RNA Purification (ChIRP) assay combined with RT-qPCR analysis of DNA fragments showed that Cadm1-as1 was specifically enriched in the promoter region (−1200 to −600) of Nos2 (Figure S10B). Furthermore, dot blotting confirmed that Cadm1-as1 bound to the segment (−900 to −600) of the Nos2 promoter via hybridization with a single-stranded DNA segment (Fig. 5F). Through sequence alignment, we observed the presence of paired complementary bases between the Nos2 promoter (−872 to −862) and Cadm1-as1(205 to 215; Fig. 5G). Similar pairing complementary bases also existed in human Nos2 promoter (−1096 to −1088) and CADM1-AS1 (218–226; Figure S10C). To disrupt the binding between the Nos2 promoter and Cadm1-as1 transcript, we generated specific mutations at the binding sites. Subsequently, dot blotting was conducted, which revealed that these mutations indeed resulted in the complete abolishment of the interaction between Cadm1-as1 and Nos2 promoter (Fig. 5H). Additionally, we conducted CUT&Tag-qPCR experiments and confirmed that Cadm1-as1 overexpression increases HDAC1 binding to the Nos2 promoter region and reduces H3K27 acetylation, whereas Cadm1-as1 MUT (120-250 nt deletion) did not (Figure S10D). Genetic perturbation experiments demonstrated that Cadm1-as1 regulates NOS2 expression in both in vivo and in vitro models. Specifically, Cadm1-as1 overexpression suppressed Nos2 mRNA and protein levels, whereas its knockdown produced the opposite effect, confirming its role as a negative regulator of Nos2 (Figure S10E–J). As NOS2 catalyzes the conversion of arginine to citrulline, producing nitric oxide (NO) in vivo (Fig. 5I), we hypothesized that Cadm1-as1 may influence NO production in chondrocytes by regulating NOS2 expression. To test this, we performed targeted metabolomic profiling of arginine metabolism in primary chondrocytes. Following transfection with Ad Cadm1-as1 or Ad NC, significant changes in the levels of many metabolites were revealed. The most prominent changes were observed in the levels of amino acids. (Fig. 5J). Among the identified metabolites, a significant increase in L-arginine levels was observed upon overexpression of Cadm1-as1, while conversely, L-citrulline levels decreased (Fig. 5K, Table S11). To further confirm this process, we determined the arginine and citrulline contents with an ELISA assay (Figure S11A). Overexpression of Cadm1-as1 decreased NO levels in mouse chondrocytes and suppressed the IL-1β–induced increase in NO production (Figure S11B). In addition, we measured arginine, citrulline, and NO levels in primary chondrocytes from Cadm1-as1 knockout and knock-in mice, demonstrating that Cadm1-as1 depletion decreased arginine while increasing citrulline and NO, whereas Cadm1-as1 overexpression had the opposite effect (Figure S11C, D). Using uniformly labeled 13C-arginine, we detected decreased levels of labeled 13C-citrulline in MCs treated with Ad Cadm1-as1 (Fig. 5L). The levels of labeled 13C-urea were not significantly altered, suggesting that Cadm1-as1 overexpression had no significant impact on its metabolism (Fig. 5L). Furthermore, labeled agmatine and creatine were not observed in our analysis. LC-MS analysis of the arginine and citrulline levels in the synovial fluids from patients with different stages of OA was performed (Figure S11E). In patients with severe OA, the levels of arginine were decreased compared to the levels in those with mild OA. However, no significant differences were detected in the citrulline levels when comparing patients from the mild, middle, and severe OA groups. Further examination of the ratio of arginine to citrulline in synovial fluids demonstrated a substantial increase in patients with severe OA compared to the middle OA group (Fig. 5M). To assess the diagnostic potential of arginine, citrulline, and the ratio of arginine to citrulline in OA, we performed a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. The results showed that the ratio of arginine to citrulline exhibited good sensitivity and specificity in distinguishing patients with severe or middle OA from those with mild OA (Fig. 5N). In conclusion, these findings suggest that Cadm1-as1 suppresses of arginine degradation into NO by inhibiting Nos2 expression.

Intra-articular delivery of a hybrid exosome carrying sno vector mitigated OA

To investigate a therapeutic approach involving intranuclear lncRNAs, we constructed a plasmid overexpressing Cadm1-as1 using the sno vector. A “snoVector” is an expression vector designed to stably express RNA sequences of interest and confine their accumulation to the nucleus by flanking the inserted sequence with snoRNA elements, which promote nuclear retention and prevent nucleocytoplasmic export20. The production of EGFP from transfected cells served as an indicator of successful transfection and the proper processing of both EGFP mRNA and sno-lncRNA (Fig. 6A). We successfully engineered chondrocyte-targeting exosomes by introducing a surface-displayed chondrocyte-affinity peptide (CAP) onto the exosomal membrane protein, lysosomal-associated membrane protein 2b (LAMP2B), through genetic manipulation in dendritic cells (DCs). The advantage of this approach is that the N-terminus of LAMP2B extends outside the exosome membrane, allowing the fusion of the targeting peptide without compromising the expression and functionality of LAMP2B (Fig. 6B). Isolation of the exosome was verified using nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA; Fig. 6C) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM; Fig. 6D). Western blotting analysis of whole cell lysates from DCs and protein lysates derived from purified exosomes revealed increased CD63 and TSG101 expression (Fig. 6E). To produce hybrid exosomes for loading the Cadm1-as1 sno vector, we conducted an incubation process involving exosomes, liposomes, and the plasmid. This procedure resulted in the successful encapsulation of the plasmids within the hybrid exosomes (Fig. 6F). The hybrid exosomes were incubated with MCs, and subsequent western blotting revealed that hybrid exosomes containing the Cadm1-as1 sno vector exhibited downregulated MMP13 and ADAMTS5 expression, but upregulated SOX9, ACAN, and COL2A1 expression (Fig. 6G). In the in vivo experiments, male C57BL/6 mice were subjected to intra-articular delivery of the hybrid exosome. To investigate Cadm1-as1 expression, we performed live animal fluorescence imaging analysis of EGFP at 1–5 days post-injection, which confirmed the expression efficiency of Cadm1-as1 (Fig. 6H). After DMM surgery, Safranin O/fast green staining demonstrated that Cadm1-as1 hybrid exosome treatment prevented post-traumatic OA compared with the control group (Fig. 6I). The attenuated cartilage degeneration in the hybrid exosome injection group was confirmed by OARSI scores, Mankin scores, and SBP thickness measurements (Fig. 6I). In addition, RNA FISH and immunohistochemistry confirmed successful NOS2 and Cadm1-as1 expression in vivo (Fig. 6J). Discussion

The conventional treatment strategies employed for patients with OA include oral anti-inflammatory and analgesic medications, glucosamine medications, intra-articular injection of sodium hyaluronate therapy, physical therapy, and surgical therapy21,22. However, current treatment strategies primarily focus on analgesia and anti-inflammation, rather than addressing the pathophysiological changes. In this study, we emphasized the significance of hypoxia as a crucial factor in maintaining chondrocyte homeostasis. Chondrocytes are responsible for modulating ECM synthesis and degradation, which governs the homeostasis of articular cartilage23,24. Furthermore, we identified a mechanism through which CADM1-AS1 is activated under hypoxia through the deacetylation of histone proteins.

Histone acetylation is a key regulator of chromatin structure and is involved in gene transcription, DNA replication, DNA repair, and heterochromatin silencing25,26. Histone acetylation is intricately regulated by the activity of histone acetyltransferases (HATs) and HDACs, which are dynamically balanced27,28. Understanding the intricate interplay between HATs and HDACs holds considerable potential for elucidating fundamental biological mechanisms and identifying therapeutic targets for various diseases that involve the dysregulation of histone acetylation. HATs and HDACs are typically associated with other proteins as they form multiple complexes, each representing a unique function characterized by different site specificities and levels of enzymatic activity29. Among HDACs, the Sin3 core complex is a prominent assembly comprising Sin3A or Sin3B, HDAC1 and/or HDAC2, SAP18, SAP30, RBAP46, and RBAP 48. This complex serves as a critical platform for the recruitment of additional modules that possess enzymatic functions30,31. In this study, we demonstrate that Cadm1-as1 binds to HDAC1 and the NOS2 promoter sequence, thereby recruiting the Sin3a complex to the promoter region. This recruitment mediates histone deacetylation and transcriptional repression of NOS2. To our knowledge, this mechanism has not been reported in chondrocyte degeneration and helps explain the molecular pathogenesis of osteoarthritis.

The NOS2-mediated production of NO from arginine in chondrocytes plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of OA32. In the context of cartilage, NO can induce chondrocyte death through apoptosis. Furthermore, NO has been implicated in reducing PG synthesis and perturbing cell-matrix interactions mediated by fibronectin binding to the α5β1 integrin33–35. The arginine concentration in the blood has been demonstrated to serve as a diagnostic marker for arthritis, with arginine depletion potentially exacerbating the condition36. However, the molecular mechanisms regulating arginine catabolism and anabolism remain unclear. Arginine can be converted into ornithine, a crucial precursor for collagen and polyamine synthesis37. Thus, arginine may facilitate the repair of damaged cartilage by increasing the production of ornithine, proline, and polyamines36. In addition, there was no significant difference in the plasma concentration of citrulline between patients with knee OA and that of the healthy control group36. In our investigation, we made a significant discovery regarding arginine concentration in the synovial fluid of patients with OA. Similar to the findings of the previous study, no significant differences were observed in citrulline levels across the mild, middle, and severe OA groups. However, we noticed that the ratio of arginine to citrulline widely varied between the three patient groups. This finding suggests that assessing the relative levels of arginine and citrulline, rather than their individual concentrations, may hold greater significance in evaluating the metabolic changes associated with different stages of OA. The diagnosis of OA has predominantly relied on clinical symptoms and radiological imaging modalities, such as X-ray, computed tomography, and magnetic resonance imaging38,39. Thus, our findings contribute to the identification of specific biomarkers in synovial fluid, which could aid the diagnosis of OA.

Our preventive treatment regimen, initiated one week after surgical model induction, enabled investigation of early disease mechanisms and therapeutic potential. This approach proved valuable for assessing prophylactic interventions, an essential step toward clinical translation. However, several considerations warrant discussion. The artificially induced and simplified pathogenesis of DMM models differs fundamentally from human osteoarthritis progression. Furthermore, while we observed no significant synovitis, previous reports describe that synovial inflammation in murine DMM models is often transient and anatomically restricted, with mild hyperplasia and cellularity observed primarily in the medial compartment at early timepoints (6 weeks post-surgery) but resolving by 16 weeks40. Our research may have missed these subtle or transient inflammatory changes due to differences in timing or scoring protocols. Moreover, early clinical symptoms of diseases like arthritis are often subtle, making early diagnosis and intervention a significant challenge in clinical practice. These model limitations, combined with the diagnostic challenges of early human disease, emphasize the need for future studies incorporating human tissue validation and more sensitive synovitis detection methods to fully characterize the anti-inflammatory potential suggested by our chondroprotection results. Despite these limitations, our multifaceted experimental approach robustly demonstrates the critical role of CADM1-AS1 in osteoarthritis pathogenesis. Notably, the delivery strategy developed herein provides a potential translational framework for clinical application. To specifically target chondrocytes, we have incorporated a unique component known as CAP into the engineered hybrid exosomes for intra-articular administration. This approach enables precise delivery of the CADM1-AS1 sno vector to the intended chondrocyte population within the joint. The inclusion of CAP in the hybrid exosomes enhances their ability to selectively bind and interact with chondrocytes, facilitating efficient cargo transfer and uptake by these specialized cells41,42. Although the cartilage-targeting exosomes exhibited preferential accumulation within the articular cartilage of the knee joint, we cannot exclude uptake by other joint tissues, as exclusive cartilage specificity could not be definitively demonstrated in vivo. This represents a limitation of the present study and warrants further investigation using quantitative biodistribution and high-resolution in vivo tracing approaches.

In summary, our comprehensive study unveils the importance of epigenetic modifications in OA development. The impact of CADM1-AS1 on histone deacetylation pathways identified here improves our understanding of the underlying molecular mechanisms contributing to OA pathophysiology. Through modulating CADM1-AS1 expression, it is possible to restore proper histone acetylation patterns and mitigate the damaging effects observed in OA joints, ultimately providing directions for more effective management of OA.

Methods

Human participants

Human cartilage samples were obtained from patients with primary OA undergoing total knee arthroplasty. All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment in this study. This process was conducted in adherence to the approved protocol, as outlined by the Ethics Committee of Sir Run Run Shaw Hospital, Zhejiang, China. (Approval No 20230052). A summary of patient information can be found in Table S1. All ethical regulations and approved protocols were meticulously adhered to throughout the duration of this study. For each sample, we selectively obtained undamaged cartilage from the non-loading areas and damaged cartilage with evident lesions from the loading areas. The collected specimens were utilized for various purposes, including RNA extraction, histological analysis, or further processing to isolate chondrocytes, following previously published study43. For chondrocyte isolation, the harvested cartilage was sectioned into small pieces. These fragments were then subjected to a 30 min treatment with 0.25% pronase in PBS, followed by an additional 4-hour incubation at 37 °C with 0.2% collagenase. Subsequently, the digested samples underwent filtration using the 0.075-mm cell strainer. The obtained cells were then cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). The culture format was a monolayer system, with specific cell confluency maintained at 70–80%. Cells were disrupted using trypsin-EDTA. The hypoxic culture conditions were set at 37 °C in 5% CO2 and 2.5% O2. Afterward, the cellular debris was eliminated through a washing procedure using phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The isolated HCs were subjected to continuous culture and utilized for experimental purposes within three passages. All the patients and the public were unaware of the design, conduct, and reporting of this research. The duration for molecular assay under hypoxia was set at 24 h unless otherwise specified. For normoxia-treated cells, HCs were maintained under normoxic conditions (5% CO2 and 21% O2) for 24 h.

For synovial fluid collection, patients were divided into five stages according to the tibiofemoral Kellgren-Lawrence score. Mild OA includes normal and stage I, and middle OA includes stages II and III with stage IV defined as severe OA. In the normal, stage I and stage II groups, synovial fluid was collected during arthroscopic surgery from patients with traumatic anterior cruciate ligament rupture or meniscal injury. In the stage III and stage IV groups, synovial fluid (1 mL per sample) was collected during total knee arthroplasty.

Animal models

Animal experiments were performed according to approved standard protocols by the Laboratory Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of Zhejiang University. The approval number for these protocols was ZJU20220412. The mice were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions and maintained in a controlled environment with a 12 h light/dark cycle at an ambient temperature of 22 °C. Mice were fed a standard laboratory rodent diet and ad libitum access to food and water. Cadm1-as1fl/fl and Cadm1-as1 KI mice were obtained from Cyagen Biology Technology for this study.

For Cadm1-as1fl/fl mice, two guide RNAs (gRNA1: 5′-GGGACTTCATTACTAA-CTGCAGG-3′; gRNA2: 5′-TCATGGGTGACCGACCGGTGAGG-3′) and a donor vector containing loxP sites were co-injected with Cas9 mRNA into fertilized mouse eggs. Founder F0 animals were screened by PCR (Forward primer: 5′-ACGTAAACGGCCACAAGTTC-3′; Reverse primer: 5′-TGATTTCTGTTCTGT-TTCACAGCC-3′) and sequencing for correct targeting. Germline transmission was confirmed by breeding F0 mice with wild-type partners. Heterozygous mice were intercrossed to obtain homozygotes, which were then bred with tissue-specific Cre recombinase-expressing mice to achieve conditional gene deletion. The genotyping strategy and validation gels for founder generation are shown in Figure S12A, C.

Cadm1-as1 knock-in mice were generated by CRISPR/Cas9-mediated homologous recombination at the ROSA26 locus. Briefly, a single guide RNA (gRNA, 5′-CTCCAGTCTTTCTAGAAGATGGG-3′) targeting the mouse ROSA26 gene, together with Cas9 mRNA and a donor vector containing the CAG promoter–Cadm1-as1 cDNA–WPRE–BGH poly(A) cassette, was co-injected into fertilized mouse zygotes. Founder F0 animals were screened by PCR and sequencing for correct targeting. Positive founders were bred to wild-type C57BL/6 mice to establish germline transmission and generate heterozygous F1 offspring. The genotyping strategy and validation gels for founder generation are shown in Figure S12B, D.

For genotyping, genomic DNA was extracted from tail biopsies, and PCR was performed using primer pairs spanning the 5′ and 3′ integration junctions. Agarose gel electrophoresis confirmed the correct banding pattern distinguishing wild-type, heterozygous, and homozygous mice. Mice were maintained on a C57BL/6 J background and genotyped prior to experimental use.

To obtain inducible conditional Cadm1-as1 knockout mice, we performed crossbreeding experiments between Cadm1-as1fl/fl mice and Col2-CreERT2 mice (Jackson Laboratories, USA), as well as ACAN-CreERT2 mice (gifted by Professor Ximei Wu, Zhejiang University). Male mice aged 2 months old (n = 6 to 8 per group) were used for the inducible deletion of Cadm1-as1. The mice were administered Tamoxifen (Sigma, MO, USA) daily via intraperitoneal injections for a period of 5 days. The dosage administered was 100 μg per gram of body weight.

Experimental OA was induced in male mice aged 12 weeks through destabilization of the medial meniscus (DMM) surgery44. Animals were anesthetized with a combination of 10 mg/kg xylazine and 75 mg/kg ketamine before being subjected to DMM surgery. In summary, a skin incision was performed to expose the patella. Subsequently, with the assistance of a microscope, tweezers were implemented to carefully laterally dislocate the exposed patella. Sectioning of medial meniscotibial ligament with micro-surgical scissors. After completing the procedure, the incision site was stitched and closed using sutures. Sham-operated control mice underwent a similar procedure without patellar dislocation or ligament transection. All subsequent murine knee joint histological analyses were specifically performed on the medial compartment sections.

Male C57BL/6 mice were subjected to standard feeding conditions for 6, 12, or 24 months to establish the age-associated osteoarthritis model. Mice aged 12 and 24 months were categorized as aging mice, while 6-month-old mice served as control specimens. All subsequent murine knee joint histological analyses were specifically performed on the medial compartment sections.

In the AAV treatment experiment, mice that underwent either sham or DMM surgery were then subjected to intra-articular injections. These injections comprised AAV control or AAV constructs expressing Cadm1-as1 WT and mutant versions obtained from HANBIO TECH (Shanghai, CHN) at a concentration of 5 × 109 plaque-forming units. The injections were administered using a 10 μL micro-syringe with a total volume of 10 μL, a week after surgery. In aged mouse models, AAV was administered at 12 and 18 months of age; in the DMM model, AAV was injected one week after model induction.

For exosome treatment experiments, mice subjected to DMM or sham surgery received intra-articular injections of either control exosomes or hybrid exosomes (20 µg per injection). Injections were initiated one week after surgery (13 weeks of age) and administered every five days using a 10 µL microsyringe. Osteoarthritis severity was evaluated three months after surgery (24 weeks of age).

All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with institutional ethical guidelines. All animals were monitored daily by trained personnel in accordance with the institutional animal welfare protocol. Mice undergoing surgical procedures or prior to sacrifice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal administration of 1% sodium pentobarbital (0.1 ml per 100 g body weight) to ensure minimal discomfort. Humane endpoints were strictly followed, and any animal exhibiting signs of severe distress or meeting predefined euthanasia criteria was promptly euthanized using approved and ethical methods. The study is reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines.

Cell culture

The procedure for harvesting human chondrocytes followed the established methods mentioned above. To obtain MCs, the articular cartilage from the femoral condyles and tibial plateaus of 5-day-old mice was carefully collected (Cadm1-as1 fl/fl or wild-type C57BL/6). The cartilage underwent a thorough washing process with sterile PBS and was subjected to digestion using 0.2% collagenase type II. Following the digestion process, the samples underwent filtration and were cultured overnight in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS at 37 °C in 5% CO2 and 2.5% O2. The isolated MCs were subjected to continuous culture and utilized for experimental purposes within three passages. For normoxia-treated cells, MCs were maintained under normoxic conditions (5% CO2 and 21% O2) for 24 h.

The human embryonic kidney (HEK)-293 cell line was procured from the American Type Culture Collection (VA, USA), which was first described by F L Graham and A J van der Eb45. The C28/I2 cell line derived from humans was procured from Sigma (MO, USA), which was first described by Goldring et al.46. The ATDC5 cell line, derived from mice, was procured from the Cell Engineering Division (Tsukuba, Japan), which was first described by Atsumi et al.47. The above cell lines were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS at 37 °C in a 5% CO2. The DC2.4 cell line, derived from mice and first described by Shen et al.48 was procured from Fuheng Biotechnology (Shanghai, CHN) and maintained in a special culture medium (Procell, Hubei, CHN).

3D chondrocyte cultures were conducted following previously established protocols, as described in prior studies49. In short, chondrocytes isolated from Cadm1-as1fl/fl were transfected with adenovirus at an MOI of 400 for a period of 72 h prior to the embedding process. To prepare the agarose hydrogel, a mixture of 1 volume of 5× culture medium (containing 5% HEPES, 10% FBS, and 5% penicillin/streptomycin) and 4 volumes of a 2.5% low melting agarose solution was made, resulting in a final concentration of 1× culture medium and 2% agarose. Subsequently, the chondrocytes were embedded in the agarose hydrogel and incubated in a 24-well plate at a density of 2 × 106 cells per well. After 15 min, the agarose gel solidified. The cell/agarose constructs were cultured at 37 °C in 5% CO2 for a week, with the culture medium changed daily. Following the culture period, the samples were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and subsequently embedded in paraffin. The paraffin-embedded samples were sectioned into slices with a thickness of 7 μm. We visualized this matrix through Alcian blue staining of glycosaminoglycans (GAGs) and quantified ECM thickness using Image-Pro Premier software. The analysis involved defining Alcian blue-positive areas via smart segmentation, measuring thickness in pixel units, and converting to micrometer scale using the corresponding magnification scale bar, ensuring standardized and reproducible measurements as previously demonstrated50.

Animal Behavior Analyses

In preparation for hot-plate assay51, the mice were given a minimum acclimation period of 30 min in the procedure room prior to the commencement of the experiment. To evaluate thermal hyperalgesia, individual mice were positioned on a hotplate (Yuyan Instrument, Shanghai, CHN) at 55 °C one at a time. The latency, which refers to the duration until the mouse displayed hind limb licking, jumping, or shaking, was accurately recorded during the experiment. The duration of each trial was limited to a maximum of 45 s. After observing a response, the mouse was promptly withdrawn from the hotplate. Once a response was observed, the mouse was immediately removed from the hotplate. The experiment consisted of three repetitions for each mouse, with a one-day interval between each test session. Subsequently, the mean values were calculated based on the recorded data.

To conduct the rotarod analysis52, each mouse was positioned on the rod (Panlab, MA, USA) for a training duration of no less than 30 min. This training period was implemented to eliminate potential variations in learning capabilities among the mice. Following the training session, the mice were positioned on an accelerating rotarod. We measured and recorded the duration for which each mouse maintained its balance on the rotating rod during the initial ride. Each trial had a maximum duration of 5 min.

Mechanical allodynia was quantified at biweekly intervals using the up-down von Frey filament method53. Mice were acclimated for 15 min in individual plexiglass chambers (10 × 10 × 15 cm) with wire mesh flooring prior to testing. A series of calibrated von Frey filaments (ranging from 0.008 to 2.0 g force) were applied perpendicularly to the plantar surface of the hindpaw for 6 s or until paw withdrawal occurred. Each filament was tested five times at 5-min intervals, with positive responses defined as the paw withdrawal rapidly or there was flinching upon removal of the hair. All behavioral testing was performed by experimenters blinded to treatment groups between 09:00 and 12:00 h under standardized lighting conditions.

Bioluminescence imaging

C57BL/6 mice were sedated with 2% isoflurane administered via a nose cone placed in the imaging platform. The quantification of bioluminescence intensity involved measuring the total emission flux (expressed in photons per second) within the region of interest, which was accomplished by the IVIS Lumina II (Caliper Life Sciences, MA, USA).

MicroCT analysis

The knee joints of mice underwent fixation in a 4% paraformaldehyde solution for a duration of 48 h, followed by thorough washing and storage in 70% ethanol. High-resolution micro-computed tomography (μCT) imaging was conducted using Skyscan 1275 (Skyscan, Aartselaar, Belgium). The scan parameters were set as follows: X-ray voltage of 45 kV; source current of 55 μA; resolution of 9 μm. After acquiring the CT images, a three-dimensional reconstruction was generated. Subchondral bone parameters were analyzed following a standardized protocol to ensure consistent experimental conditions54. Each specimen underwent evaluation by three independent researchers who remained blinded to the experimental design.

Histology and immunohistochemistry

Specimens of cartilage obtained from human donors and mice were subjected to fixation using a 4% paraformaldehyde solution for a period of 48 h. Subsequently, the samples underwent decalcification using 10% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA). Following the required process of decalcification, the samples were then carefully embedded in paraffin and subsequently sectioned at a precise thickness of 3 μm. Subsequently, the sections were subjected to a series of staining procedures as follows: they were immersed in 1% (dissolved in 50% alcohol) fast green solution (Sigma, MO, USA) for a period of 5 min, followed by immersion in 1% acetic acid solution for 5 s. Finally, the sections were stained with 1% (dissolved in 95% alcohol) Safranin O solution (Sigma, MO, USA). For Safranin O-Fast Green staining, three non-consecutive sections per animal were analyzed and the section exhibiting the most severe cartilage wear was selected for quantitative assessment. The femur and tibia were both scored, but only the maximum score was used for statistical analysis. To evaluate cartilage destruction, three independent scorers, who were blinded to the experimental conditions, assessed the samples according to the OARSI scoring system55 and Mankin scoring system56. Additionally, the scorers assessed the thickness of the SBP. Each specimen underwent evaluation by three independent researchers who remained blinded to the experimental design.

For IF staining, the sections were deparaffinized in xylene and hydrated with graded ethanol. After incubating with a sodium citrate antigen retrieval solution (Solarbio, Beijing, China), the sections were incubated successively with 3% H2O2 and 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) (Fudebio, Zhejiang, China) at room temperature (20–25 °C) for 30 min. The sections were then incubated with the indicated antibodies overnight at 4 °C. On the following day, the sections were subjected to three washes with PBS before being incubated with Alexa Fluor 594 goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibodies (dilution ratio of 1:200; procured from Fudebio, Zhejiang, CHN) for 1 h at room temperature (20–25 °C). For nuclear staining, the sections were treated with DAPI for 10 min at RT (20–25 °C). Fluorescence signals were visualized using BX51TRF (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

The following antibodies were used in this study: anti-MMP13 (1:200, ab39012, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), anti-ACAN (1:200, C8035, Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA), anti-NOS2 (1:200, ab178945, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), anti-P21 (1:200, ab109199, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), anti-HIF-1α (1:200, BF8002, Affinity, OH, USA), anti-HDAC1 (1:200, ab280198, Abcam, Cambridge, UK).

Immunocytochemistry

For immunocytochemistry staining, the chondrocytes derived from C57BL/6 mice were subjected to fixation using a 4% paraformaldehyde solution for 10 min and subsequently washed with PBS. Next, the cells were rendered permeable by treating them with 0.1% Triton X-100 for a duration of 5 min, followed by another round of washing with PBS. To block nonspecific binding, the cells were incubated with 5% BSA for 1 h. Subsequently, the cells were incubated with anti-HDAC1 (1:200; Abcam, Cambridge, UK) antibody overnight at 4 °C. Following the initial incubation, the cells were subjected to three washes with PBS prior to being incubated with Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibodies (dilution ratio of 1:200; procured from Fudebio, Zhejiang, CHN) for 1 hour at RT (20–25 °C). For nuclear staining, the cells were treated with DAPI for 10 min at RT (20–25 °C). Fluorescence signals were visualized by BX51TRF (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

siRNAs, ASOs, and adenovirus overexpression

Custom-designed ASOs and siRNAs were synthesized by RiboBio (Guangdong, CHN). Control adenoviruses refer to an empty vector. The culture medium was changed and the chondrocytes were then cultured for 72 h before further analysis.

ATAC-Seq

ATAC-Seq analysis was conducted with the Hyperactive ATAC-Seq Library Prep Kit for Illumina (Vazyme Biotech, Jiangsu, CHN), following the manufacturer’s provided instructions. Subsequently, the lysate was fragmented using TruePrep Tagment Enzyme at 37 °C for 30 min. Following the completion of the reaction, the fragments were incubated with ATAC DNA Extract Beads for 5 min at room temperature (20–25 °C). CUT&Tag assay

The CUT&Tag assay was conducted with the Hyperactive Universal CUT&Tag Assay Kit for Illumina (Vazyme Biotech, Jiangsu, CHN) with modifications. We carried out CUT&Tag assay for one biological replicate. Briefly, 5 × 104 HCs or MCs were collected, washed with wash buffer, and centrifuged. The cell pellets were resuspended in wash buffer and incubated with concanavalin A-coated magnetic beads. Bead-bound cells were then incubated with specific antibodies, followed by washing. Cells were subsequently treated with pA/G-Tnp and Dig-300 buffer. After washing, TruePrep Tagment Enzyme and Proteinase K were added. Chromosome conformation capture (3 C) assay

Chondrocyte 3 C assay were carried out following previously established protocols57. A total of 1 × 107 C28/I2 cells were collected. Chondrocyte cultures were cross-linked using a 1.5% formaldehyde solution in PBS for 10 min. To halt the cross-linking process, the cells were then treated with glycine at RT (20–25 °C) for 5 min. The cells were then lysed with lysis buffer (10 mM NaCl; 10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5; 0.1 mM EGTA; 5 mM MgCl2; 1× complete protease inhibitor). The lysate underwent centrifugation at 1500 g for a duration of 10 min to separate and collect the nuclei. The isolated nuclei were subsequently resuspended in a 1.2× restriction buffer, ready for further experimental procedures. Samples were treated for 1 hour at 37 °C with 0.3% SDS and subsequently subjected to treatment at 37 °C for 1 h, involving the addition of 0.3% SDS and 2% Triton X-100. Following this treatment, 400 U of the PstI (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) enzyme was added, allowing for an overnight digestion of the chromatin. The following morning, the inactivation of the restriction enzyme took place by adding 1.6% SDS and incubating the samples at 65 °C for 25 min. Subsequently, the samples were diluted with 1× ligation buffer and 1% Triton X-100, undergoing a 1-hour incubation at 37 °C. The addition of T4 ligase (1200 U, Takara, Kyoto, Japan) followed, allowing for a 4-hour ligation process at 16 °C, and then an additional 30-minute ligation step at room temperature (20–25 °C). To remove any remaining protein contaminants, proteinase K (300 μg/ml, Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, CHN) was added, and the samples were subjected to overnight incubation at 65 °C. The precipitation of the samples occurred using phenol:chloroform: isoamyl alcohol, followed by purification via three rounds of spinning on Amicon Ultracel 3 K columns (Millipore, MA, USA), with ultrapure water being added between each spin. For the detection of the ligated fragments, a customized TaqMan assay was employed, utilizing reverse primers specific to PstI fragments and combined with a reverse primer and probe for the proximal promoter bait sequence. Normalization of frequencies was carried out using a control template representing a PstI-digested and ligated single Bacterial Artificial Chromosome clone that covered the segment of interest within the genome.

Luciferase reporter assay

The promoter sequence of CADM1-AS1, 2 kb in length, was inserted into the firefly luciferase plasmid (pGL3basic luciferase and renilla_polyA, Addgene 128046). HEK-293 cells were seeded in 24-well plates (1 × 105 cells/well) and transfected with specific or control firefly luciferase plasmid. After an incubation period of 48 h, the Renilla and firefly luciferase activities were evaluated employing a Dual Luciferase Reporter Gene Assay Kit sourced from Beyotime (Shanghai, CHN). Firefly luciferase activity was normalized to the Renilla luciferase activity to determine the ratio.

Genome editing with CRISPR-Cas9 system

CRISPR–Cas9 experiments were performed following previously published protocols58,59. In summary, the design of single guide (sg) RNAs as target sequences was accomplished using the online tool CCTop-CRISPR/Cas9. For the design of oligos used in cloning the sgRNA targeting sequence into a PEP-KO vector. The two oligos (Cadm1-as1 gRNA1:GAATCTAATCTCTAACAGCCTGG, Cadm1-as1, gRNA2:GAATCTAATCTCTAACAGCCTGG) (Peak 3 gRNA1:GGATTTCTATCCCACTATGCAGG, Peak 3 gRNA2:CACACATCATCAGCCCAAGGAGG) underwent phosphorylation and annealing by employing T4 Ligation Buffer and T4 PNK (Takara, Kyoto, Japan) under standard parameters. Following the annealing process, the diluted and annealed oligos (at a 1:200 ratio) were ligated into the PEP-KO vector and transfected into competent cells. The following day, 3-4 single colonies were selected and cultured in 5 mL of Luria-Bertani (LB) broth containing ampicillin (100 μg/ml, Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, CHN). Plasmid DNA was isolated using a standard method from the amplified competent cells. Subsequently, Sanger sequencing analysis was conducted on the obtained plasmid DNA to ensure its accuracy and integrity. Following a standard infection protocol, the PEP-KO plasmid was transfected into ATDC5 or C28/I2 cells at a concentration of 500 ng per well in a 6-well plate. Subsequently, after 48 h of transfection, the cells were subjected to puromycin selection and subsequently replated at low density (50 cells) in a 10 cm dish. Individual colonies were allowed to expand, and a fraction of the cells were collected for further analysis. The sequences of sgRNAs were provided in Table S3.

Generation of enCRISPRa and enCRISPRi cell lines

To establish stable cell lines for inducible enCRISPRi and enCRISPRa, lentiviruses carrying dCas9-effector fusion proteins (dCas9-p300 or dCas9-KRAB-MeCP2) were transduced into C28/I2 cells. Geneticin (300 μg/mL), obtained from Selleck Chemicals (TX, USA), served as the selection agent for enCRISPRa-transfected cells, and blasticidin (5 μg/mL)fromSelleckChemicals(TX,USA)was utilized for selecting enCRISPRi-transfected cells within 24–48 h of transfection. This antibiotic treatment enabled the isolation of cells that successfully integrated the lentiviral vectors carrying the enCRISPRi components. The drug selection was maintained for at least 2 weeks to establish the cell lines.

The design of sgRNAs as target sequences was accomplished using CCTop-CRISPR/Cas9. A human genome non-targeting gRNA was employed as a negative control. Oligonucleotides underwent synthesis, annealing, and subsequent cloning into the lentiviral expression vector Lenti-sgRNA-EFS-GFP. Lentivirus production was carried out by transfecting HEK-293 cells, which were subsequently used to transduce the target cells. Two days later, the GFP-expressing cells were sorted and harvested for RNA extraction and RT-qPCR assay.

Northern blot

Northern blot analysis was carried out utilizing the northern blot kit provided by Ambion™ NorthernMax™ (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). Approximately 30 μg of total RNAs extracted from HCs underwent denaturation using formaldehyde, followed by electrophoresis in a 1% agarose-formaldehyde gel. Following electrophoresis, the RNAs were transferred onto a nylon membrane (Cytiva, Shanghai, CHN). The membrane was then subjected to hybridization with biotin-labeled CADM1-AS1 probes. Finally, the membranes were subjected to X-ray film exposure within a controlled darkroom environment. Probe sequences can be found in Supplementary Table S3.

In vitro transcription and translation assay

The experiments were conducted utilizing the TnT® Quick System (Promega, WI, USA). In brief, a predetermined amount of circular plasmid DNA containing a T7 promoter, along with either the Cadm1-as1 sequence or luciferase sequence (serving as a positive control), was introduced into the reaction buffer. Additionally, 20 μM of methionine and TranscendTM Biotin-Lysyl-tRNA were added to facilitate the incorporation of biotinylated lysine residues into nascent proteins during the translation process. The final volume of the mixture was adjusted to 50 μL using nuclease-free water, followed by an incubation period of 90 min at 30 °C. Following that, the products were detected using western blotting with suitable methods.

ChIRP assay

ChIRP assays were conducted following previously established methods, as described in earlier studies60,61. The antisense DNA probe specifically targeting Cadm1-as1 was custom-designed by RiboBio. Primary MCs were subjected to a 2.5% oxygen culture condition for a duration of 48 h. The cells were treated with 1% formaldehyde at RT (20–25 °C) for 10 min. Following the formaldehyde treatment, glycine was introduced to halt the cross-linking reaction induced by formaldehyde, followed by two washes with PBS. To prepare chromatin fragments, the cells were lysed using Sonication Cell Lysis Buffer (10 mM EDTA, 1% SDS, 50 mM Tris, pH 8.1), and the resulting lysates were sonicated to yield chromatin fragments of approximately 300-500 bp in length. The fragmented chromatin was then diluted with hybridization buffer (1% SDS, 750 mM NaCl, 50 mM Tris, pH 7.0, 15% formamide, 1 mM EDTA). The mixture containing the diluted chromatin was supplemented with biotin-labeled probes, followed by an overnight rotation at 4 °C. Subsequently, immunoprecipitation samples were subjected to washes using low- and high-salt buffers and then eluted with ddH2O. The eluted DNA served as a template for subsequent RT-qPCR analyses.

Biotinylated RNA pulldown assay

A biotinylated RNA pull-down assay was performed to identify proteins interacting with cadm1-as1, using the PierceTM Magnetic RNA-Protein Pull-Down Kit obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific (MA, USA). Negative RNA Control [poly(A)25 RNA] was provided by the kit. In summary, 2 × 107 MCs were harvested, lysed, and sonicated. Prior to use, magnetic beads were with 20 mM Tris buffer. Biotinylated RNAs obtained from GenePharma (Jiangsu, CHN) were incubated with 50 μL of streptavidin magnetic beads at RT (20–25 °C) for 30 min. Subsequently, the samples were subjected to overnight incubation with the biotinylated RNA at 4 °C. Post-incubation, the beads were washed and the resulting complexes were eluted using Elution Buffer at 37 °C for 30 min.

Coomassie Blue Fast Staining

The protein silver staining assay was conducted utilizing the Coomassie Blue Fast Staining Solution obtained from Beyotime (Beyotime, Jiangsu, China) (R2Q17). The proteins isolated from the RNA pulldown assay were heated at 95 °C for 10 min in a loading buffer (Fudebio, Zhejiang, CHN). The denatured proteins were then loaded onto an SDS-PAGE gel for electrophoresis. Following electrophoresis, the gel was washed three times (50 mL per wash) with deionized water. Residual liquid was aspirated, and the gel was stained with 20 mL of Coomassie Brilliant Blue rapid stain solution for 10–30 min. For destaining, the gel was incubated in 100 mL of deionized water with gentle agitation, replacing the solution every 15 min.

Mass spectrometry analysis

The gel bands used were obtained from biotinylated RNA pulldown assay and silver staining experiments, followed by protein identification through mass spectrometry analysis. In brief, gel pieces matching the targeted protein were destained, dehydrated, and treated with reducing and alkylating agents. After further washes and dehydration, gel pieces were digested using trypsin overnight. The resulting peptide fragments were extracted, dried, and resuspended for LC-MS analysis. Subsequently, peptide samples were separated using nano-LC and analyzed using Q Exactive HF-X mass spectrometry (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). The acquired mass spectra were searched against UniProt Mus musculus. Fixed carbamidomethyl modification on cysteine residues, and considered variable modifications such as oxidation on methionine and acetylation on the protein N-terminus. Peptide identification was conducted with a high confidence threshold to ensure reliable results. The differential metabolomic analysis is presented in Table S11.

RIP assay

A total of 2 × 107 MCs were transfected with plasmids overexpressing Flag-HDAC1. Following a 48 h incubation period, RIP assay was conducted with a RIP kit (Millipore, MA, USA) to assess the interaction between RNA and HDAC1. In brief, MCs were lysed using a complete lysis buffer, and a fraction of the cell lysates was reserved as the input sample. The remaining lysates were then subjected to an overnight incubation at 4 °C with either 5 μg of control IgG antibody or anti-Flag-tag antibody. Total RNA was subsequently isolated from the samples for the detection of Cadm1-as1 using RT-qPCR, employing the methodology described above.

Molecular docking RNA

Molecular docking was utilized to predict the interaction between HDAC1 and Cadm1-as1, as well as Cadm1-as1 mutants. To optimize the energy, the Amber14SB force field62 was implemented. Docking between HDAC1 and Cadm1-as1 interaction region was performed using Rosetta63. The protein-RNA docking tool in Rosetta primarily utilizes the Monte Carlo algorithm for conformational search in the entire space. During the low-resolution sampling stage, Rosetta constructs a model of the protein using the backbone atoms and amino acids side-chain centroids. The Monte Carlo algorithm was used for spatial search, and a low-resolution energy function was employed to determine whether a conformation was retained. In the high-resolution optimization stage, all heavy and polar hydrogen atoms were restored, and the Monte Carlo algorithm was used to optimize and insert the side chains. At this stage, a more complete and complex all-atom potential function is used for scoring. Finally, a composite scoring function and binding mode were used to select reasonable docking results.

GST-protein purification and EMSA

Biotin-labeled Cadm1-as1 was synthesized through in vitro T7 transcription by GenePharma (Jiangsu, CHN). The pGEX-6P-2 Vector was utilized for the insertion of the HDAC1 cDNA sequence, creating a fusion protein with GST. The resulting construct was transformed into BL21-competent cells. For the purification of the GST-HDAC1 fusion protein, bacterial pellets were obtained by culturing in LB medium and induced with 0.1 mM Isopropyl β-D-Thiogalactoside for 8 h. Following resuspension in lysis buffer, sonication for 30 min, and centrifugation, the soluble fraction containing the target protein was collected. Incubation with GSTSep Glutathione Magbeads (Yeasen, Shanghai, CHN) facilitated the binding of the fusion protein, which was subsequently eluted using a glutathione elution buffer. The purified protein was then transferred to microtubes and stored in aliquots at −80 °C. For the subsequent binding assay, probes and HDAC1 proteins were mixed in a binding buffer. An RNA-EMSA Kit (Bersinbio, Guangdong, CHN) was employed to perform the EMSA, following the manufacturer’s instructions.

RNA isolation and library construction for RNA-seq

To verify the transcriptional differences in HCs under normoxia and hypoxia conditions (n = 3, reference genome hg38), as well as the effects of Cadm1-as1 overexpression in MCs (n = 3, reference genome mm10), we performed RNA sequencing. The HCs for RNA-seq were harvested at passage 1 and 24 h post-infection. The MCs for RNA-seq were harvested at passage 2, after 24 h of treatment under either hypoxic or normoxic conditions. Total RNA was extracted and purified as described above. For the RNA-seq transcriptome strand library, 2 μg of total RNA was utilized with the TruSeqTM Stranded Total RNA Library Prep Kit from Illumina (San Diego, CA, USA). Ribosomal RNA (rRNA) depletion was carried out using the Ribo-off rRNA Depletion kit. The fragmented RNA served as a template for first-strand cDNA synthesis using random hexamer primers. Following the removal of the RNA template, a replacement strand was synthesized, creating double-stranded cDNA with dUTP in place of dTTP. The second strand was quenched during amplification by the incorporation of dUTP. To avoid self-ligation during adapter ligation, a single ‘A’ nucleotide was added to the 3’ ends of fragments. Multiple indexing adapters were ligated to the prepared double-stranded cDNA, subsequently selecting cDNA fragments ranging from 200 to 300 bp through size selection. Phusion DNA polymerase enabled PCR amplification, generating final libraries that were quantified using TBS380 and subjected to paired-end sequencing on an Illumina novaseq6000 sequencer with 2 × 150 bp read length. Raw paired-end reads from RNA-seq were processed for trimming and quality control using fast with default parameters. The resulting clean reads were then aligned to the reference genome using HIASAT2 software in orientation mode. Mapped reads for each sample underwent assembly using StringTie based on a reference-based approach. Genes exhibiting P < 0.05 and absolute log2(fold changes) > 1 were recognized as differentially expressed genes. Differential lncRNA expression in human chondrocytes under normoxia and hypoxia is presented in Table S5, and differential mRNA expression under the same conditions is presented in Table S6. Genes identified in mouse chondrocytes treated with Ad-Cadm1-as1 or Ad-NC are listed in Table S10.

Dot blotting

For dot blotting analysis, biotin-labeled Cadm1-as1 was produced using in vitro T7 RNA Polymerase transcription by GenePharma (Jiangsu, CHN). The single-stranded DNA was dropped onto a nitrocellulose membrane (Cytiva, Shanghai, CHN) with the Bio-dot apparatus (Bio-Rad, CA, USA), followed by drying the membrane at 120 °C for 15 min. To prevent non-specific binding, the membrane was blocked by incubating it in a TBS-T solution with 5% BSA. Subsequently, the membrane was incubated with the biotin-labeled Cadm1-as1 at RT (20–25 °C) for 30 min. After washing with TBS-T, the membrane was further incubated with streptavidin-HRP (Sigma, MO, USA) for 30 min at RT (20–25 °C). The signal was detected using an Amersham Imager 600 (General Electric Company, MA, USA).

Metabolomics study

Cell lysates from MCs were collected 48 h post-adenoviral transfection for metabolite analysis. Untargeted metabolomics analysis was conducted by Majorbio (Shanghai, CHN). In brief, for cell sample preparation, a methanol/water solution (4:1) containing an internal standard of 2-chloro-l-phenylalanine (0.3 mg/ml) was added to each sample, followed by trichloromethane. Cells were disrupted using an ultrasonic homogenizer for 6 min at 500 W. The extracted samples were then centrifuged, and the resulting supernatant (1 ml) was dried using a freeze-concentration centrifugal dryer. Each dried sample was reconstituted with methanol/water (1:4, v/v), vortexed, and subjected to centrifugation. The resulting supernatants were collected, filtered, and stored in LC vials at −80 °C. Quality control (QC) samples obtained by pooling aliquots from all the samples were used for sample detection. An LC system coupled with a Q-Exactive quadrupole-Orbitrap mass spectrometer was employed for sample analysis. An ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column was utilized, and a gradient elution using water (with 0.1% formic acid) and acetonitrile (with 0.1% formic acid) was performed. Mass analysis was carried out in positive and negative ion modes, with QC samples injected every 10 samples for quality assurance.

Exosome Isolation

DC2.4 cells were cultured until they reached 80–90% confluency, after which the medium was replaced with fresh serum-free medium. Following 48 h of culture, the cell medium was collected and subjected to sequential centrifugation steps at 300 g for 20 min and 2000 g for an additional 30 min to eliminate cell debris. The resulting supernatant was further centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 30 min. This supernatant was then filtered through 0.22 μm pore filters and subjected to ultracentrifugation at 120,000 g for 70 min using a Beckman Coulter OptimaXPN-100 centrifuge. The resulting exosome pellets were resuspended in PBS and stored at −80 °C until further use.

Examination of exosomes by TEM

For TEM analysis, exosomes were deposited onto formvar carbon-coated nickel grids. Samples were prepared by negative staining with a 2% uranyl acetate solution for 10 min. After air-drying, the samples were visualized using a Talos L 120 C transmission electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) to obtain detailed images and characterization.

Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA)

To assess the size distribution of isolated exosomes, the exosomes were appropriately diluted with PBS. A volume of 1 mL of the diluted exosome suspension was loaded into the sample chamber of the Nanosight NS500 (Malvern Panalytical, UK) for analysis. The collected data were subsequently analyzed using NanoSight NTA software V3.3 (Malvern Panalytical, UK).

Exosome-liposome hybridization and plasmid loading

The chondrocyte-affinity peptide CAP (DWRVIIPPRPSA) was identified through phage display screening. CAP-modified polyethylenimine/DNA complexes penetrate the extracellular matrix through both electrostatic interactions (mediated by positively-charged polyethylenimine) and CAP’s targeting capability. These complexes demonstrate specific binding to human and rabbit chondrocytes while showing minimal affinity for synovial cells64, and subsequent studies have consistently validated its specificity and functionality in chondrocyte-targeted applications41. Hybrid exosomes (exosomes loaded with Cadm1-as1 and coated with CAP) were generated by incubation with liposomes, following previously described methods65. Briefly, 10 μg lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) and the specified plasmids (5 μg) were separately diluted in DMEM. Subsequently, they were mixed and incubated at RT (20–25 °C) for 5 min. Next, the resulting complex of plasmids and liposomes was added to the exosomes and incubated at 37 °C for 12 h.

NO assay

The supernatant from MCs was collected 48 h post-adenovirus transfection for NO detection. Nitric oxide levels were measured using an NO detection assay kit (Beyotime, Jiangsu, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. In brief, a series of standards with concentrations ranging from 0 to 100 μM were prepared by dilution. Following collection, the samples were carefully added to individual wells of 96-well plates. Subsequently, Griess Regent I and Griess Regent II were separately introduced into each well. The resulting mixture, consisting of the samples and standards, was then subjected to detection at a wavelength of 540 nm using a Multiskan FC System manufactured by Thermo Fisher Scientific.

Statistical and reproducibility

Statistical analyses were conducted with the GraphPad Prism (version 10.0, CA, USA). The significance of the data was assessed using various tests, including paired Student’s t-test, unpaired Student’s t-test, Kruskal–Wallis test, Mann-Whitney U test, and one-way ANOVA. A h-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

All data associated with this study are present in the paper or the Supplementary Materials. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. Sample sizes for in vitro and in vivo experiments were estimated based on preliminary data and are consistent with those commonly employed in similar studies in the field. No data were excluded from the analyses. Animals were monitored post-operatively for signs of severe distress or persistent lameness; no animals met pre-defined humane endpoint criteria requiring exclusion.
