Phase I study of stereotactic ablative radiotherapy (SABR) in inoperable breast cancer
Authors: C De la Pinta, V Gamero, A Muriel, AB Capuz, R Moris, M Martin, M Alarza, A Montero, MT Muñoz, R Hernanz, ML García Bermejo, S Sancho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeA significant percentage of elderly patients with early-stage breast cancer are not surgical candidates or refuse surgery, and exclusive hormone therapy offers poor local control. SABR, by delivering biologically ablative doses in a few fractions, could be a non-invasive and effective alternative. This phase I clinical trial's primary objective was to determine the safety, tolerability, and maximum tolerated dose (MTD) of SABR as a definitive treatment in this population.Material and methodsA single-center, single-arm, phase I study was designed. Eligible patients were ≥70 years old with unifocal, T1-2N0 M0-1 breast cancer, considered inoperable or who refused surgery. A Fibonacci (3 + 3) dose-escalation design was used to deliver SABR to the primary tumor in 5 fractions. Dose levels were 36 Gy (Level 1), 38 Gy (Level 2), and 40 Gy (Level 3). The primary endpoint was dose-limiting adverse effects, defined as any ≥ Grade 3 events (per RTOG/Harris scales) within the first 6 months. Secondary endpoints included local control (LC), progression-free survival (PFS), and overall survival (OS).ResultsBetween March 2021 and March 2024, 10 patients (11 tumors) with a median age of 91 years (range: 83-96) were enrolled. Three patients received 36 Gy, three received 38 Gy, and four received 40 Gy. With a median follow-up of 27 months (range: 4-40), no dose-limiting adverse events were observed in any cohort, and thus the MTD was not reached. No acute adverse events were recorded. The only late adverse effect reported was one case (10%) of Grade 1 subcutaneous fibrosis at 6 months in the 36 Gy level. Cosmetic outcome was excellent or good in 100% of patients. At 24 months, local control, PFS and OS were 100%, 90% and 90%, respectively.ConclusionDefinitive SABR for primary breast cancer in elderly and/or frail patients is an exceptionally safe and well-tolerated procedure up to a dose of 40 Gy in 5 fractions. Furthermore, it demonstrates promising preliminary efficacy with excellent disease control. SABR stands as a highly effective, non-invasive alternative to surgery or exclusive hormone therapy in this selected patient cohort, strongly supporting further investigation for efficacy in phase II studies.

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most frequently diagnosed cancer in Europe. In Spain, it accounts for nearly a quarter of all cancers in women and is the leading cause of cancer-related mortality, with an incidence rate that is increasing by 2-3% annually. Data indicate that a significant percentage of women over 75 with stage I-III breast cancer decline standard treatment, with refusal rates of 90.8% in 1995 and 69.9% in 2011 [1]. Furthermore, the prevalence of comorbidities rises sharply with age, from 9% in patients <50 years to 56% in those >80 years [2]. Together with the marked increase in life expectancy over the past three decades—from approximately 74–76 years in 1990 to 81–82 years in 2024—population aging has contributed to a steadily rising incidence of breast cancer in older women. In parallel, comorbidities and treatment de-escalation patterns influence management decisions in this population [3].

The standard of care for early-stage breast cancer includes breast-conserving surgery followed by adjuvant radiotherapy and in selected cases where the breast cannot be preserved, mastectomy [4]. Adjuvant treatment typically involves whole-breast irradiation (WBI), with or without a boost to the tumor bed. Moderate hypofractionated schedules delivering doses greater than 2 Gy per fraction over approximately three weeks have now been firmly established as the standard of care, although ultrahypofractionated regimens delivered in a single week are increasingly recommended and used for radiotherapy in early-stage breast cancer [5]. In highly selected patients, treatment can be limited to the lumpectomy cavity, a technique known as partial breast irradiation (PBI) [5].

Traditionally, patient's ineligible for surgery due to comorbidities or high anesthetic risk were offered hormone therapy or palliative radiotherapy for symptomatic control. However, a Cochrane review demonstrated the inferiority of tamoxifen alone compared to surgery [6]. Local progression rates after mastectomy plus tamoxifen, breast-conserving surgery plus tamoxifen, and tamoxifen alone were 8%, 18%, and 64%, respectively [7,8]. The tamoxifen-alone arm was associated with higher mortality, leading to the conclusion that exclusive hormone therapy provides poor local control. To date, no studies in the literature have compared directly surgery (with or without adjuvant treatments) against definitive radiotherapy. Therefore, a local, non-invasive treatment like definitive radiotherapy represents a promising option to be explore to improve the outcomes.

Stereotactic Ablative Radiotherapy (SABR) is a high-precision outpatient technique that delivers ablative doses of >5 Gy per fraction over 1–8 sessions, achieving a higher biologically effective dose (BED) while sparing surrounding tissues. By confining irradiation to the tumor plus a small margin, SABR minimizes risk of adverse effects, directly correlated with treated volume. Compared with the standard approach of surgery followed by whole-breast adjuvant radiotherapy, this strategy may shorten overall treatment duration, avoid general anesthesia and hospitalization in frail patients, and potentially reduce healthcare resource utilization. Based on this rationale, our study implements a dose-escalation protocol increasing the BED from 64.36 Gy to 76.36 Gy, using the Fibonacci method for patient recruitment and dose assignment [9].This study aims to evaluate the safety and tolerability of SABR in patients with inoperable breast cancer, leveraging the radiobiological advantage of high-dose-per-fraction delivery, as validated by the now-standard hypofractionated regimens.

Material and methods

Study design

This was a single-center, single-arm, phase I clinical trial. The study protocol was approved by the ethics committee for clinical research of the Hospital Universitario Ramón y Cajal (CEI 399_20), and all patients provided written informed consent prior to enrollment.

The trial was designed as a phase I study with safety and tolerability as the primary endpoint. A Fibonacci (3 + 3) dose-escalation design was employed, with a maximum planned dose of 40 Gy in 5 fractions. Dose escalation and dose-limiting adverse events assessment were conducted at the patient level. In cases of multiple synchronous tumors within the same patient, all lesions were treated at the same assigned dose level, without intra-patient dose variation, and adverse events evaluation was performed per patient rather than per lesion.

Three patients were enrolled at the first dose level (36 Gy in 5 fractions). If no dose limiting adverse events were observed, the next cohort of three patients was enrolled at the subsequent dose level (38 Gy in 5 fractions). If one patient experienced a dose-limiting adverse events, three additional patients were enrolled at the same dose level. The dose-limiting adverse events was defined as the dose level at which two or more patients in a cohort experienced any Grade ≥3 treatment-related events. Adverse events were assessed for the breast, skin, chest wall, heart, and lung using the RTOG, Harris, and Van Limbergen scales and we re-tabulate adverse events using CTCAE v6 terminology from the source data.

The planned sample size was 9-20 patients, depending on the observed toxicities.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize variables. Local control was assessed based on tumor size changes. OS was calculated from the firt day of SABR to the date of death or last follow-up, and survival curves were generated using the Kaplan-Meier method. A Data and Safety Monitoring Committee (DSMC), composed by a radiation oncologist, a medical physicist, a gynecologist, and a breast surgeon external to the study, was established. The DSMC reviewed patient enrollment, treatment delivery, clinical evolution, and all adverse events, specifically monitoring for dose-limiting adverse events through periodic reports. These evaluations were formally reported to the ethics committee for clinical research.

Objectives

The primary objective was to determine the dose-limiting adverse events and safety profile of SABR for inoperable breast cancer by evaluating acute and late adverse events daily during treatment, and at 1, 3-, 6-, 9-, and 12-months post-treatment. Adverse effects were graded clinically according to the RTOG, Harris, and Van Limbergen scales, and we re-tabulate adverse events using CTCAE v6 terminology from the source data, while cosmetic outcomes were assessed independently by both clinicians and patients. Secondary objectives included local control (LC) of the treated lesion, assessed at 3, 6, 9, 12, 18, and 24 months. Follow-up included clinical examination and ultrasound at 3 and 6 months, followed by imaging per standard clinical practice according to RECIST criteria. Overall survival (OS) at 12 and 24 months was also a secondary endpoint.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Eligible patients were required to have: histologically confirmed unifocal breast carcinoma; T1-2 N0 M0-1 disease (N0 status confirmed by physical examination, axillary ultrasound, fine-needle aspiration (FNA), or MRI) treatment delivered with definitive local tumor control intent aimed at preventing progression-related morbidity; an ECOG performance status of 0-2; age ≥70 years; and a life expectancy >3 months. Patients were considered eligible if they were deemed inoperable, had unresectable tumors, presented with metastatic disease, had prohibitive anesthetic risk, or refused surgery. For SABR eligibility, tumors had to be ≤ 5 cm, located >1 cm from the skin and >0.5 cm from the chest wall, and constitute <50% of the total breast volume.

Exclusion criteria included: systemic treatment within the previous three weeks (cytotoxic chemotherapy or other systemic anticancer therapies; endocrine therapy was allowed as per standard clinical practice); multifocal or multicentric tumors; nodal involvement; tumor volume >50% of the breast; prior radiotherapy to the ipsilateral breast; ipsilateral breast prosthesis; or contraindications to radiotherapy, such as active scleroderma, systemic lupus erythematosus, or dermatomyositis.

Patients were withdrawn from the study for the following reasons: withdrawal of informed consent, patient non-compliance, treatment interruption exceeding five consecutive working days, occurrence of an adverse events as determined by the DSMC, or patient death.

Treatment protocol

Irradiation was delivered using SABR techniques adhering to protocol-specified volume, dose prescription, and dosimetry criteria.

Following treatment indication, a 4D-CT simulation with intravenous contrast was performed. In cases of contraindication to contrast, the target volume was delineated in consultation with a dedicated breast radiologist. The Deep Inspiration Breath Hold (DIBH) technique was used when feasible. The responsible radiation oncologist delineated the Gross Tumor Volume (GTV), Internal Target Volume (ITV) across 10 respiratory phases, and the Planning Target Volume (PTV), defined as the ITV with a 5 mm margin. Organs at risk (OARs)—including lungs, bronchus, trachea, chest wall, spinal cord, heart, esophagus, and skin—were also contoured. Treatment planning was performed using 3D conformal or Intensity Modulated Radiotherapy (IMRT) techniques and had to meet PTV coverage criteria and OAR dose constraints (as detailed in Table 2). Treatment was delivered using 6-15 MV photons on a conventional linear accelerator equipped with Image-Guided (IGRT) and Surface-Guided Radiotherapy (SGRT). A total dose of 36-40 Gy was delivered in 5 fractions, with 2-3 fractions per week, completing the course within 21 days (Fig. 1a and b) (see Table 3).

Adverse events assessment and follow-up

Adverse events were assessed according to RTOG criteria. Cosmetic outcome was evaluated both by physicians using an adapted Harris and Van Limbergen scale and by patients through a validated aesthetic assessment questionnaire, as described by Hoeller et al. [10]. This dual assessment approach allowed comparison between clinician- and patient-reported cosmetic outcomes.

Acute adverse events were defined as adverse events occurring from the start of SABR up to 90 days after treatment completion, and chronic adverse events were defined as adverse events occurring more than 90 days after completion of SABR, in accordance with commonly accepted definitions in radiation oncology.

Tumor response was assessed through serial imaging and clinical examination. Patients were evaluated daily during treatment, at 15 days, and one month after treatment completion, with physical examinations at each visit and laboratory tests starting from the one-month follow-up. Subsequent reviews with physical examination, breast ultrasound, and lab tests were conducted at 3 and 6 months. Thereafter, patients were followed according to standard clinical practice (Fig. 1c).

Objective tumor response classification required imaging confirmation. Physical examination alone was not used to define response but complemented radiological assessment. When clinically indicated, additional imaging was performed to better characterize treatment response or suspected progression.

Local failure was defined as radiological progression of the treated breast lesion within the planning target volume (PTV), based on serial imaging (ultrasound and mammography). Local control was analyzed using Kaplan–Meier methodology, with time calculated from the first day of SABR to the date of documented local progression or last follow-up. Patients without local failure were censored at the date of last clinical or radiological assessment. Progression Free Survival (PFS) was defined as the time from the first day of SABR to the first occurrence of any of the following events: local progression within the treated breast, regional recurrence, distant metastasis, or death from any cause. Patients alive and without documented progression were censored at the date of last clinical or radiological follow-up.

Results

Clinical results

From March 2021 and March 2024, 10 female non metastatic patients with 11 tumors considered inoperable or refusing standard surgery, and with a median age of 91 years (range: 83-96) were enrolled in this study. Three patients received 36 Gy, three received 38 Gy, and four received 40 Gy. The mean tumor volume was 29.97 cc (range 8.34-64.25). Nine patients received aromatase inhibitors as systemic endocrine therapy before, during, and after SABR, while one patient declined endocrine treatment. Tumor response was evaluated according to RECIST criteria at 3, 6, 12, and 24 months (Table 1). At 3 months, 4 patients had stable disease, 3 achieved partial response, and 3 achieved complete response. At 6 months, 4 patients had stable disease, 3 had partial response, and 2 had complete response; one patient died from an unrelated cause and was therefore not evaluable for further response assessments. At 12 months, responses included 1 case of stable disease, 6 partial responses, and 2 complete responses. At 24 months, 1 patient had stable disease, 4 had partial response, and 4 achieved complete response. With a median follow-up of 27 months (range 4-40), follow-up differed across dose cohorts due to the dose-escalation design, with median follow-up times of 40 months for the 36 Gy cohort, 29 months for the 38 Gy cohort, and 25 months for the 40 Gy cohort, actuarial local control and overall survival rates at 24 months were 100% and 90% respectively (Fig. 2). No local or distant progression was observed during follow-up. One patient died before the 24-month evaluation without evidence of local or distant progression and was censored at the time of death.

Treatment related adverse effects

No acute events were reported on follow-up and only a single case of late Grade 1 subcutaneous fibrosis in the CTCAE v6, RTOG and Harris scales, with cosmetic outcomes remaining excellent or good in all patients in Van Limbergen questionnaire.

The dose escalation up to 40 Gy in 5 fractions did not increase acute or early-late adverse effects in this selected population, albeit longer follow-up is precise to address the risk of late complications. The dose-limiting adverse events were not reached, suggesting that higher dose-escalation schemes could be safely explored in the future.

Discussion

Radiotherapy remains a cornerstone of breast cancer management alongside surgery and systemic therapy. However, the growing number of elderly patients and the increasing prevalence of significant comorbidities demand a more individualized therapeutic approach that balances efficacy with tolerability. In selected patients, breast surgery without adjuvant radiotherapy may represent a potential option, particularly in frail or elderly individuals who are not candidates for standard surgical approaches. Nevertheless, surgical procedures may still carry procedural risks and may not be appropriate for all patients with significant comorbidities or very advanced age. In patients who are medically inoperable or decline surgery, exclusive radiotherapy represents a valid alternative, supported by advances in radiobiological understanding and technological precision.

The use of hypofractionation is grounded in radiobiological models suggesting that increasing the dose per fraction can yield outcomes comparable to traditional regimens [[11], [12], [13]]. The response of both normal and tumor tissues to radiation is dose-per-fraction dependent and can be described by the linear-quadratic model, which is characterized by the alpha/beta ratio. Tissues with a low alpha/beta ratio are more sensitive to higher doses per fraction [14]. The radiobiology of breast cancer, which is considered a tumor with a low alpha/beta ratio, supports this approach [10]. Consequently, these shorter regimens improve patient quality of life (QoL) and reduce healthcare costs without increasing adverse events [15,16].

Providing durable local control remains a significant clinical challenge for patients with breast cancer who are ineligible for definitive surgery. While SABR is increasingly adopted for other malignancies, its application in breast cancer remains limited, with existing literature predominantly focused on the neoadjuvant or adjuvant settings [[17], [18], [19]]. Herein, we report the findings of a phase I dose-escalation trial of breast ultrahypofractionated SABR in inoperable patients with technically resectable tumors.

SABR mechanism of action combines direct tumor DNA damage with indirect effects mediated by the tumor vasculature, including endothelial dysfunction, microvascular collapse, and secondary hypoxia, which contribute to extensive tumor cell death. In addition, SABR may induce immunomodulatory effects by promoting the release of neoantigens and damage-associated molecular patterns that enhance antitumor immune activation. The high biologically effective dose delivered with SABR is associated with high rates of local control [20,21]. Our study included ten patients treated exclusively to the gross tumor volume, differing from most published series that delivered whole-breast irradiation with an additional boost [[22], [23], [24]]. The prescribed dose range of 36–40 Gy in five fractions (EQD2 68.84–82.11 Gy) is consistent with commonly reported regimens (30–40 Gy), while higher biological doses have been explored only in isolated cases [[25], [26], [27]].

Clinical outcomes were comparable to those reported in previous studies, with local control rates of 88.6–93.3% at 24 months [23,24,28] and 92% after whole-breast irradiation [24]. Moore-Palhares et al. [23] included 57 patients with breast cancer who were not candidates for surgery (median age 81 years), treated with SABR in 5 fractions. Indications included medical inoperability (35%), metastatic disease (37%), patient refusal of surgery (18%), and unresectable tumors (10%). The treated volumes included breast only (54%), breast and lymph nodes (31%), or lymph nodes only (15%). Most tumors were treated with 35 Gy in 5 fractions (range 35–40 Gy). Zabrocka et al. [24] included 13 non-surgical candidates with primary breast cancer (14 lesions) treated with SBRT, with a median follow-up of 32 months. Indications were medical inoperability, and in 4 cases SBRT was delivered after prior whole-breast radiotherapy (40–50 Gy). The treated volume corresponded to the primary breast tumor. Dose schedules were 30–40 Gy in 5 fractions for SBRT alone and 25–32 Gy in 4–5 fractions when combined with prior whole breast radiotherapy (WBRT). Zabrocka et al. [28] included 21 patients with primary breast cancer who were not surgical candidates (median age 78.4 years). Indications included medical inoperability, with 10 metastatic patients and 11 stage IA–IIIC cases. The treated volume corresponded to the primary breast tumor, with a median lesion size of 3.1 cm and median PTV of 32.4 cc. Treatment consisted of 40 Gy in 5 fractions delivered every other day. Inferior results observed with lower SABR doses (25–32 Gy; 71.6% local control at 24 months) [24] suggest a dose–response relationship. Our cohort—comprising exclusively inoperable or surgery-refusing non metastatic patients, with a median age of 91 years (range 83–96)—represents the oldest population treated with this technique to date. This selection likely contributed to our superior outcomes, including a 24-month progression-free survival markedly higher than the 39.4% reported by Moore-Palhares et al. [23] and a 24-month overall survival of 90%, exceeding those of comparable series (51.8% [21] and 63.8%).

Treatment was well tolerated, with no Grade ≥3 adverse effects, contrasting with the 6.5–38.9% incidence reported by Moore-Palhares et al. [23] and the Grade 3–4 events in other studies [24,28]. Moore-Palhares et al. [23] reported grade 1 adverse events in 46% of treatments, grade 2 in 20%, grade 3 in 13%, and grade 4 in 2%. Grade 2 toxicities mainly consisted of dermatitis with one case of pneumonitis. Additionally, one rib fracture. All grade ≥3 adverse events cases were dermatitis. Zabrocka et al. [24] reported 92.9% acute adverse events included grade 1 in 61.5%, grade 2 in 30.8%, and grade 4 in 7.7%. Late adverse events included grade 2 edema in 7.7% and grade 4 necrosis in 15.4%. Grade 4 necrosis occurred in patients treated with SBRT and WBRT. Zabrocka et al. [28] reported acute adverse events grade 1 in 47.8%, grade 2 in 4.3%, grade 3 in 8.7%, and no grade 4 toxicities. In our series, the rate of grade 1–2 toxicities were very low compared with published data in breast cancer treatments, likely reflecting both the selection of non-superficial lesions and the applied dosimetric optimization. These findings support the principle that reducing the irradiated breast volume, achievable through SABR, lowers the risk of late adverse events [29,30] while maintaining excellent local control in selected patients unsuitable for surgery.

Our findings support the principle that reducing the irradiated breast volume, achievable through SABR, lowers the risk of late adverse events [29,30] while maintaining excellent local control in selected patients unsuitable for surgery. In the absence of dose-limiting adverse events, 40 Gy in 5 fractions appears to be the most suitable regimen for further prospective evaluation, while 36 Gy in 5 fractions may be reserved for cases with unfavorable anatomy (e.g., minimal skin separation) or heightened frailty where stricter dose constraints necessitate de-escalation.

Limitations

We are conscious of the limitations of our protocol. As an exploratory study, the primary role of these findings is to establish the foundation for subsequent, larger-scale trials. Furthermore, a potential bias may arise from the non-absolute criteria used to define inoperability.

Conclusions

To our knowledge, this is the first dose-escalation trial of SABR for primary breast cancer. Our study suggests that definitive ablative radiotherapy is a safe and highly effective treatment for inoperable, early-stage breast cancer. The treatment yields excellent adverse events profiles and cosmetic outcomes, accompanied by ongoing radiographic tumor regression. The durable responses and minimal adverse events observed underscore the potential of this non-invasive approach. Future research, particularly studies evaluating efficacy in phase II and III and predictive biomarkers for radiation response, is warranted to further delineate the optimal patient population for definitive ablative radiation, potentially overcoming the need for surgery in selected and fragile early-stage breast cancer patients. SABR is extrapolable to other clinical scenarios in frail patients with solid tumors, such as renal cell carcinoma or lung cancer.
