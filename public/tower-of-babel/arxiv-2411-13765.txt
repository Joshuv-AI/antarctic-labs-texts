Abstract
. The Schrödinger bridge problem (SBP) seeks to find the measure 𝐏 ^ \hat{\mathbf{P}} on a certain path space which interpolates between state-space distributions ρ 0 \rho_{0} at time 0 0 and ρ T \rho_{T} at time T T while minimizing the KL divergence (relative entropy) to a reference path measure 𝐑 \mathbf{R} . In this work, we tackle the SBP in the case when 𝐑 \mathbf{R} is the path measure of a jump diffusion. Under mild assumptions, with both the operator theory approach and the stochastic calculus techniques, we establish an h h -transform theory for jump diffusions and devise an approximation method to achieve the jump-diffusion SBP solution 𝐏 ^ \hat{\mathbf{P}} as the strong-convergence limit of a sequence of harmonic h h -transforms. To the best of our knowledge, these results are novel in the study of SBP. Moreover, the h h -transform framework and the approximation method developed in this work are robust and applicable to a relatively general class of jump diffusions. In addition, we examine the SBP of particular types of jump diffusions under additional regularity conditions and extend existing results from the diffusion case to the jump-diffusion setting.

1.
Introduction

In this introduction, we will cover preliminaries on the long-celebrated Schrödinger Bridge Problem (SBP) and the framework of jump-diffusion processes under which our work is conducted. We will also explain our main results and the organization of the paper.

1.1.
Schrödinger Bridge Problem: Formulation and Motivation

The original version of this nearly century-old problem was formulated by Erwin Schrödinger in
[
57
,
58
]
. In Schrödinger’s thought experiment, a large number of hot gaseous particles are observed at time
t=0
to have initial distribution
\rho_{0}
. The observer’s prior belief about the behavior of these particles is that their position should evolve according to a Markov process with transition density
q(s,x,t,y)
for
0\leq s\leq t\leq T
. As such, by the law of large numbers, the particle density at a later time
T
should be roughly equal to

\tilde{\rho}_{T}(\cdot)\approx\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}q(0,x,T,\cdot)\rho_{0}(x)dx.

Suppose however that the actual particle density
\rho_{T}
observed at time
T
vastly differs from the expected distribution
\tilde{\rho}_{T}
. Schrödinger believed that a rare event had occurred and that one must find the “most probable explanation” for it. Working from intuition and without a precise statement of the problem, Schrödinger nevertheless correctly deduced that the sought explanation lies in the solution to a pair of integral equations, which would be later known as the
Schrödinger system
.

More than fifty years after Schrödinger’s work, Föllmer obtained in
[
26
]
a rigorous formulation of the SBP using the theory of large deviations
[
67
]
. To formulate the modern version of the SBP, we need to introduce some terminology. Let
\Omega=D([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{n})
be the collection of all càdlàg functions
\omega=(\omega(t))_{0\leq t\leq T}
from the time interval
[0,T]
to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. This is the space of trajectories, and
\omega\in\Omega
is a
sample path
. Let
\Omega
be equipped with the Skorokhod metric.
The resulting Borel
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{F}:=\mathcal{B}_{\Omega}
is generated by the time projections
X_{t}:\omega\in\Omega\mapsto\omega(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
for
t\in[0,T]
. Denote by
\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
the set of probability measures on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
. We will refer to
\mathbf{R}\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
as a
path measure
and
\left\{X_{t}\right\}_{[0,T]}
the stochastic process given by the time projections as the
canonical process
under
\mathbf{R}
. Let
\left\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\right\}_{[0,T]}
be the natural filtration generated by
\left\{X_{t}\right\}_{[0,T]}
and, whenever necessary, we will adopt the
usual augmentation

[
53
, II-67]
of this filtration (by
\mathbf{R}
or another path measure), which we continue writing as
\left\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\right\}_{[0,T]}
.

Let
\omega_{1},...,\omega_{N}\in\Omega
be independently sampled paths according to a “reference” path measure
\mathbf{R}\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
, and denote their empirical distribution by
Z_{N}
. Then as
N\to\infty
, Sanov’s theorem
[
55
]
gives the (asymptotic) likelihood that the empirical distribution of these paths deviates from its true distribution
\mathbf{R}
:

\text{Prob}(Z_{N}\in A)\approx\exp(-N\inf_{\mathbf{P}\in A}\mathrm{KL}(\mathbf{P}||\mathbf{R}))\text{ for }A\subseteq\mathcal{P}(\Omega),

where
\mathrm{KL}(\cdot||\cdot)
is the
Kullback-Leibler divergence
(KL divergence), also known as the
relative entropy
. For probability measures
\mu
and
\nu
, it is defined to be

\mathrm{KL}(\mu||\nu):=\begin{cases}\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}_{\mu}}\left[\log\left(\frac{d\mu}{d\nu}\right)\right]&\text{if }\mu\ll\nu,\\
+\infty&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

In other words, if a large deviation event described by
A
has occurred (such as particles having distribution
\rho_{T}
instead of
\tilde{\rho}_{T}
), finding the “most probable explanation” envisioned by Schrödinger corresponds to finding the minimizer in the decay exponent
\inf_{\mathbf{P}\in A}\mathrm{KL}(\mathbf{P}||\mathbf{R})
. Therefore, the modern formulation of the SBP aims to bridge the two endpoint distributions
\rho_{0},\rho_{T}
while staying as close as possible (in the sense of KL divergence) to the reference path measure
\mathbf{R}
.

For a path measure
\mathbf{P}\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
, let
\mathbf{P}_{t}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be its marginal distribution at time
t
, meaning that for any Borel
B\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\mathbf{P}_{t}(B)=\mathbf{P}(\left\{X_{t}\in B\right\})
. The modern formulation of the SBP states:

Definition 1.1

(Schrödinger Bridge Problem)
.

Given a reference path measure
\mathbf{R}\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
and target endpoint distributions
\rho_{0},\rho_{T}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, determine

(1.1)

\hat{\mathbf{P}}=\arg\min_{\mathbf{P}\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)}\left\{\mathrm{KL}(\mathbf{P}||\mathbf{R})\text{ such that }\mathbf{P}_{0}=\rho_{0},\mathbf{P}_{T}=\rho_{T}\right\}.

We say that the SBP does not have a solution if the “
min

1.1
) is
\infty
.

The optimizer

is called the

between
\rho_{0}
and
\rho_{T}
.

1.2.
Main Results

We consider the SBP (
1.1
) for reference measure
\mathbf{R}
being the law of the jump diffusion associated with the SDE (
1.2
) or the operator (
1.3
) and target endpoint distributions
\rho_{0},\rho_{T}
. As we will review in Section
2
, when
\mathbf{R},\rho_{0},\rho_{T}
satisfy some mild conditions (see Theorem
2.3
for details), (
1.1
) admits a unique solution
\hat{\mathbf{P}}
. Our goal is to conduct a comprehensive study of
\hat{\mathbf{P}}
in the jump-diffusion setting and to develop necessary theoretical elements and technical tools for the interpretation and the analysis of
\hat{\mathbf{P}}
. The main challenge we face is the scarcity of the regularity theory for jump diffusions in comparison with diffusions. Many important properties (e.g., hypoellipticity and heat kernel estimates) are far less understood for general jump diffusions. As a consequence, the extension of the literature on SBP from the diffusion case to the jump-diffusion setting requires careful treatment.

It will also become clear in the review section that
\hat{\mathbf{P}}
is closely tied to the notion of
h
-transform
. Therefore, to interpret
\hat{\mathbf{P}}
properly, we first develop the theory of
h
-transform for general jump diffusions. The topic of
h
-transform is of independent interest on its own. Then, upon establishing
\hat{\mathbf{P}}
in the context of
h
-transform, we further investigate conditions under which
\hat{\mathbf{P}}
possesses similar properties as its counterpart in the diffusion case. We also propose an approximation approach toward
\hat{\mathbf{P}}
under mild regularity requirements.

Assumptions and Results:
We work closely but separately with the SDE (
1.2
) and the operator (
1.3
), as they offer two different angles through which we can examine the SBP and its solution
\hat{\mathbf{P}}
. Furthermore, instead of putting concrete constraints on the coefficients, we adopt basic assumptions that a large family of SDEs and operators are expected to satisfy.

We start with the assumption on the operator, as this is our most general assumption. We assume that the jump-diffusion operator
L
in (
1.3
) satisfies the following:

(
A1
)

The martingale problem for
L
has a strong Markov solution in
\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
. Namely, for every
(s,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
, there exists a path measure
\mathbf{P}^{s,x}\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
such that
\mathbf{P}^{s,x}(\left\{X_{s}=x\right\})=1
and for every
\phi\in C_{c}^{1,2}(\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})

\left\{\phi(t,X_{t})-\phi(s,x)-\int_{s}^{t}(\frac{\partial}{\partial r}+L)\phi(r,X_{r})dr\,:\,s\leq t\leq T\right\}

under
\mathbf{P}^{s,x}
is a martingale with respect to
\left\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\right\}_{[s,T]}

\left\{\mathbf{P}^{s,x}\right\}_{[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}

s\in[0,T]

\mathbf{P}^{\tau,X_{\tau}}

Note that under Assumption

Generality of the Assumptions:
Let us give a brief discussion of our assumptions. Assumption
(A1)
is satisfied when the
martingale problem
for
L
is well posed. The well-posedness of martingale problem for jump-diffusion operators has long been investigated and has generated a rich literature. For example, for general
L
, it has been shown in
[
61
,
36
]
that the martingale problem is well posed if, as functions in
(s,x)
,
\sigma(s,x)
and
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\ell}}|\gamma(s,x,z)|^{2}\nu(dz)
are bounded and continuous, and
b(s,x)
is bounded and measurable. More recently, for
L
with a stable-like jump component, the martingale problem has also been extensively studied
[
7
,
38
,
14
,
8
,
15
,
34
]
.

In fact, in the case of diffusions,
(A1)
also implies
(A2)
as it is a well-known result that the existence of a martingale problem solution for a diffusion operator is equivalent to that of a weak solution to the corresponding Itô SDE
[
62
,
54
]
. However, such an equivalence is not fully established yet for general jump diffusions; some results in this direction are known in the case of time-homogeneous coefficients
[
45
,
42
]
. On the other hand, the SDE (
1.2
) is known to admit a unique
strong
solution under Lipschitz-type conditions on the coefficients (e.g.,
[
1
, Chapter 6.2]
), in which case
(A2)
will be fulfilled.

Assumption
(A3)
is known to hold for diffusions under mild conditions on the coefficients, but analogous results are far less abundant in the jump-diffusion case. A typical scenario in which
(A3)
holds is when the transition distribution
P_{s,t}(x,\cdot)
admits a density
p(s,x,t,\cdot)
that possesses the desired regularity, and thus the regularity of
h
can be inherited from
p
. Such is the case for the two classes of jump diffusions that we will cover in Section
5
. As the literature of jump diffusions is fast developing, we expect that
(A3)
will be shown to hold in more situations.

Novelty of the Work:
Although the SBP for diffusions has been extensively treated and well understood, rigorous studies of the counterpart problem for jump diffusions are still scarce, and a comprehensive mathematical theory for the jump-diffusion SBP is still missing. This work is a step toward filling the gaps. More generally, our work is aligned with the fast-growing body of research that connects/compares diffusions with jump diffusions, and local operators with non-local operators. Below we explain some novelties of this work:

(1) We adopt a general framework that encompasses a wide range of diffusions and jump diffusions. Instead of restricting ourselves to cases with concrete conditions on the coefficients, we build the theory on some basic properties (i.e.,
(A1)
or
(A2)
,
(A3)
) that are known to hold for a large class of diffusions and jump diffusions. This includes models whose coefficients may not be “nice-behaving”, e.g., certain operators with degenerate diffusion components or singular drifts.

(2) In our approach of solving the SBP, we keep separate the “impact” from the target endpoint distributions
\rho_{0},\rho_{T}
and that from the “nature” of the operator
L
, and demonstrate that the latter holds a more essential role in configuring the solution
\hat{\mathbf{P}}
. It is clear from our results that, for a “good-natured”
L
(i.e., supporting the assumptions), we can always access
\hat{\mathbf{P}}
through
h
-transforms, either as a harmonic-
h
-transform (which recovers the classical picture of the diffusion SBP), or as a strong-convergence limit of harmonic-
h
-transforms (which presents a novel approach in the study of
\hat{\mathbf{P}}
).

(3) We develop methodologies that are robust and suitable for the study of jump-diffusion SBPs when the regularity theory is insufficient. For example, the
h
-transform theory proposed here does not require
h
to be strictly positive and is naturally adapted to the zero sets of
h
. The approximation procedure for
\hat{\mathbf{P}}
is also given in terms of explicit formulas and tractable constructions, which makes it more accessible for general jump diffusions.

1.3.
Connections between SBP and Other Fields

Schrödinger’s original motivation for posing his problem was based in quantum physics. Over the years, as the theory behind Schrödinger bridges developed, it has seen applications in various fields. The discovery of the connection between SBP and

In the last decade, these topics have attracted intense attention due to their applications in

,

59

64
]
, where diffusion SBP models are steadily rising in popularity. To cite some examples, diffusion Schrödinger bridges have been adopted for generative modeling tasks
[

]
, their theoretical properties in neural networks have been investigated in
[

, and their numerical approximations have been developed in
[

]
.
While having a vast range of applications, diffusion models also have limitations as they only describe dynamics that are continuous in time. However, in the study of certain phenomena, it is beneficial or even necessary to adopt models that accommodate “jumps”, for which

are natural candidates.
For example, due to their nature of combining continuous and jump dynamics, Lévy processes have been widely adopted in applications to finance as more realistic financial models

More recently, jump diffusions

As an extension of both classical diffusions and Lévy processes, jump diffusions are promising tools in creating richer models in applied scientific fields. Building a comprehensive theory for the jump-diffusion SBP will not only expand the existing literature, but also shed light on potential new areas of applications.

1.4.
Further Questions

In this paper, we provide a detailed analysis of the SBP for jump diffusions from the operator and the SDE perspectives. In fact, there exists a third angle, the
stochastic control
formulation
[
20
]
, which is well-established in the diffusion case. In an ongoing work, we are working on the stochastic control approach to the jump-diffusion SBP.

We also intend to revisit the SBP for processes with killing (known as the
unbalanced SBP
)
[
11
]
and to interpret it as a jump-diffusion SBP, using both the SDE approach and the stochastic control formulation.
More generally, we believe that the jump-diffusion framework allows one to reinterpret many SBP-type problems for (jump) diffusions which possess an additional complexity such as a regime-switching mechanism.

In addition, since the SBP and the Schrödinger system are symmetric with respect to switching the marginals, it is natural to consider the dynamics of the Schrödinger bridge under time reversal. A combination of both forward and backward dynamics has already been applied in some studies of diffusion SBP
[
10
]
. The theories and the techniques developed in this work have the potential to facilitate a similar study for jump diffusions.

On the application side, it is known that, when the reference process admits a positive and continuous transition density, the SBP solution can be obtained via an efficient algorithm (see Theorem
2.4
and the discussion below). While this is the case for a large class of diffusions, only specific types of jump diffusions have been proven to possess densities. Therefore, it is worth exploring whether an effective algorithm can be devised to solve SBPs with relaxed density requirements. In a similar spirit, the special case of the unbalanced SBP for diffusions is studied in
[
11
]
, but results in more general circumstances are missing.

2.
Static SBP and Diffusion SBP

In this section, we will review some important aspects of the theory of the static SBP and the literature of the diffusion SBP. The former will serve as the “start point” of our investigation, and the latter will describe the “picture” we aim to reproduce for jump diffusions.

2.1.
Static SBP and Schrödinger System

Schrödinger’s problem (
1.1
) is also referred to as the
dynamic
SBP because it involves a minimization over the space of path measures. In many situations, it is easier to solve a
static
version of the problem, and to relate the static solution back to the dynamic formulation.

Definition 2.1

(Static Schrodinger Bridge Problem)
.

Given a reference measure
R\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
and target marginals
\rho_{0},\rho_{T}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, determine

(2.1)

\hat{\pi}=\arg\min_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})}\left\{\mathrm{KL}(\pi||R)\text{ such that }\pi_{0}=\rho_{0},\pi_{T}=\rho_{T}\right\}

where
\pi_{0},\pi_{T}
are respectively the marginal distributions of
\pi
in the first and second variable.

We say that the static SBP does not have a solution if the “
min
” in (
2.1
) is
\infty
.

To relate the static SBP to the dynamic problem (
1.1
), let us begin by introducing some notation. For a path measure
\mathbf{P}\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
and
0\leq s\leq t\leq T
, define
\mathbf{P}_{st}
to be its two-dimensional joint marginal distribution at times
s
and
t
.
Moreover, if for
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\mathbf{P}^{xy}:=\mathbf{P}(\cdot|X_{0}=x,X_{T}=y)

is the induced
bridge measure
between
x
and
y
, then we have the decomposition of
\mathbf{P}
as

(2.2)

\mathbf{P}(\cdot)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathbf{P}^{xy}(\cdot)\mathbf{P}_{0T}(dxdy).

For a given choice of
\rho_{0},\rho_{T}
, denote the collection of their couplings by

\Pi(\rho_{0},\rho_{T}):=\left\{\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}):\pi_{0}=\rho_{0},\pi_{T}=\rho_{T}\right\}.

A path measure
\mathbf{P}\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
is called
admissible
if
\mathbf{P}_{0T}\in\Pi(\rho_{0},\rho_{T})
.

Theorem 2.2
.

[
26
]

The Schrödinger problems (
1.1
) and (
2.1
) admit at most one solution
\hat{\mathbf{P}}
and
\hat{\pi}
respectively. Assume that the reference measure
R
in the static problem (
2.1
) and the reference path measure
\mathbf{R}
in the dynamic problem (
1.1
) are such that
R=\mathbf{R}_{0T}
. Then, we have the following relations between the solutions:

(1)

If
\hat{\pi}
is the solution to (
2.1
), then

(2.3)

\hat{\mathbf{P}}(\cdot):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathbf{R}^{xy}(\cdot)\hat{\pi}(dxdy)

is the solution to (
1.1
), which means that
\hat{\mathbf{P}}_{0T}=\hat{\pi}
and
\hat{\mathbf{P}}
shares its bridges with
\mathbf{R}
in the sense that

\hat{\mathbf{P}}^{xy}=\mathbf{R}^{xy}\text{ for $\hat{\pi}$-a.e. $(x,y)$}.

(2)

Conversely, if
\hat{\mathbf{P}}
is the solution to (
1.1
), then
\hat{\pi}:=\hat{\mathbf{P}}_{0T}
is the solution to (
2.1
).

The idea of the proof is simple but worth repeating. Uniqueness follows from the fact that KL divergence is strongly convex and non-negative. As for the relations between the two solutions, we first note that, for any admissible
\mathbf{P}
, the additive property of KL divergence
[
43
, Appendix A]
yields

\mathrm{KL}(\mathbf{P}||\mathbf{R})=\mathrm{KL}(\mathbf{P}_{0T}||\mathbf{R}_{0T})+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathrm{KL}(\mathbf{P}^{xy}||\mathbf{R}^{xy})\mathbf{P}_{0T}(dxdy).

Combining with (
2.2
), it is easy to see that, if
\hat{\pi}
is a solution to (
2.1
), then
\mathrm{KL}(\mathbf{P}||\mathbf{R})
achieves its minimum when
\mathbf{P}_{0T}=\hat{\pi}
and
\mathrm{KL}(\mathbf{P}^{xy}||\mathbf{R}^{xy})=0
for
\mathbf{P}_{0T}
-a.e.
(x,y)
, the latter of which implies that
\hat{\mathbf{P}}^{xy}=\mathbf{R}^{xy}
for
\mathbf{P}_{0T}
-a.e.
(x,y)
, and hence we arrive at (
2.3
). The second statement follows from a similar argument.

The static SBP is often simpler to study. Once a static Schrödinger bridge

is found, a solution

to the dynamic SBP becomes accessible following the theorem above (see

5

for a recent example). The existence and the structure of

(2.6)

\begin{cases}\vskip 5.69046pt\displaystyle\mathfrak{f}(x){\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{R}}}\left[\mathfrak{g}(X_{T})|X_{0}=x\right]=\frac{d\rho_{0}}{d\mathbf{R}_{0}}(x)&\text{ for $\mathbf{R}_{0}$-a.e. $x$},\\

Remark.

Throughout this work, we assume that the conclusion of Theorem

2.2.
Diffusion SBP and Dynamic Schrödinger System

Since Schrödinger’s thought experiment was originally based on the diffusion of hot gaseous particles, it is not surprising that the most extensively studied SBP is the diffusion case, that is, when
\mathbf{R}
is the law of an
\mathbb{R}^{n}

(2.8)

dX_{t}=b(t,X_{t})dt+\sigma(t,X_{t})dB_{t};\,\,0\leq t\leq T,

where
\left\{B_{t}\right\}_{t\geq 0}
is a standard

[
33
,
50
,
20
,
26
]
. In this section, we will review some results on the diffusion SBP that will be extended to the jump-diffusion setting in Section
5
. In particular, we will adopt the setup from
[
33
]
. Throughout this section, we will assume that the endpoint distributions
\mathbf{R}_{0},\mathbf{R}_{T}
admit probability density functions (and hence so do
\rho_{0},\rho_{T}
) and, with slight abuse of notation, we will write these distributions as
\mathbf{R}_{0}(x)dx
, etc.; we also assume that
\mathbf{R}_{0}
,

and

6
6

6

Typically,
\mathbf{R}_{0}
has finite second moment,

[

such that the SDE (

\left\{X_{t}\right\}_{[0,T]}
that is strong Markovian and admits an everywhere positive, continuous transition density

for

and

Let
(\mathfrak{f},\mathfrak{g})
be the solution of the Schrödinger system (
2.6

\hat{\varphi}_{0}:=\mathfrak{f}\cdot\mathbf{R}_{0}

and

(2.9)

\begin{cases}\varphi_{0}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}q(0,x,T,y)\varphi_{T}(y)dy,\\
\hat{\varphi}_{T}(y)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}q(0,x,T,y)\hat{\varphi}_{0}(x)dx,\\
\rho_{0}(x)=\varphi_{0}(x)\hat{\varphi}_{0}(x),\\
\rho_{T}(y)=\varphi_{T}(y)\hat{\varphi}_{T}(y).\end{cases}

The development of the existence of a solution to such a system goes back to Fortet

Theorem 2.4

(Fortet-Beurling-Jamison existence theorem)

Under the setting above, there exists a unique (up to scaling) 4-tuple of functions
(\varphi_{0},\varphi_{T},\hat{\varphi}_{0},\hat{\varphi}_{T})
solving the Schrödinger system (
2.9
).

Although later contributions and proofs are analytic in nature, Fortet’s approach was to develop an iterative scheme based on the four equations in (

[

]
, as well as some modern revisits of the algorithm

In fact, the entropy-regularized optimal transport problem, which is equivalent to the static SBP (

We now switch to a “dynamic” view. Given the solution

are two functions on

\begin{cases}\varphi(t,x):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}q(t,x,T,y)\varphi_{T}(y)dy,\\

then
\varphi,\hat{\varphi}
satisfy the boundary constraints

\varphi(0,\cdot)=\varphi_{0},\;\hat{\varphi}(0,\cdot)=\hat{\varphi}_{0},\;\varphi(T,\cdot)=\varphi_{T},\;\hat{\varphi}(T,\cdot)=\hat{\varphi}_{T}.

In other words, we can rewrite the solution to (

(\varphi(0,\cdot),\hat{\varphi}(0,\cdot),\varphi(T,\cdot),\hat{\varphi}(T,\cdot))

If, in addition,

\rho_{T}(x)=\varphi(T,x)\hat{\varphi}(T,x)\text{ for }x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.\end{cases}

Recall from (

\hat{\mathbf{P}}=\mathfrak{f}(X_{0})\mathfrak{g}(X_{T})\mathbf{R}

\hat{\mathbf{P}}=\frac{\varphi(T,X_{T})}{\varphi(0,X_{0})}\frac{\rho_{0}(X_{0})}{\mathbf{R}_{0}(X_{0})}\mathbf{R}.

As previously noted, this expression corresponds to an

-transform (by the harmonic function

.

Under the setting above, there exists a unique

on

In Section

-transforms, and then study the
h
-transform of a jump diffusion through (1) the generator of the jump diffusion and (2) the SDE corresponding to the jump diffusion. Following each approach, we develop a theory of
h
-transforms that extends the existing literature from the diffusion case (
\gamma=0
) to the jump-diffusion setting. The ‘‘jumping behavior’’ of our model allows us to study a broader class of dynamics, but it also introduces an additional layer of complexity to the problem. In the generator approach, the jump component turns a local operator into a non-local one
7
7

7

Sometimes, the jump-diffusion operator considered in this work is also referred to as a local-nonlocal mixed-type operator in the literature.
, and in the SDE approach, the jump terms appear as new stochastic integrals. In the following subsections, we will devise proper techniques to tackle these changes.

3.1.
h
-Transform: Preliminaries

The
h
-transform was first introduced by Doob in his work on potential theory
[
22
,
23
]
. A common situation in which the
h
-transform arises is when a Markov process is conditioned to exit a ball
B
at a given point
x_{0}\in\partial B
. In its most general sense, the
h
-transform is a combination of conditioning and killing, but we will not consider the latter. Let
L
be the jump-diffusion operator given by (
1.3
) with coefficients satisfying the Assumptions
.
Throughout this subsection we will assume that
(A1)
holds, and we denote by
\mathbf{P}
the solution to the martingale problem for
(L,\rho_{0})
. The coordinate process
\left\{X_{t}\right\}_{[0,T]}
under
\mathbf{P}
is a strong Markov process and for any
0\leq s\leq t\leq T
,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we denote by
P_{s,t}(x,\cdot)
its transition distribution.
Throughout this subsection, we also assume that
h:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to[0,\infty)
is a non-negative and measurable function, and set

(3.1)

\mathcal{A}_{h}:=\left\{(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}:h(t,x)>0\right\}.

We further assume that

(3.2)

\text{if }A_{0}:=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:h(0,x)>0\right\}\text{, then }r_{0}:=\mathbf{P}(\left\{X_{0}\in A_{0}\right\})>0.

Definition 3.1
.

We say that
h
satisfies the mean-value property if for all
0\leq s\leq t\leq T
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

(3.3)

h(s,x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h(t,y)P_{s,t}(x,dy).

Lemma 3.2
.

Suppose that
h
satisfies the mean-value property (
3.3
). Then

\left\{\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{X_{0}\in A_{0}\right\}}\frac{h(t,X_{t})}{h(0,X_{0})}:0\leq t\leq T\right\}

is an
\left\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\right\}
-martingale under
\mathbf{P}
.

Proof.

First note that the mean-value property (
3.3
) guarantees that, for every
t\in[0,T]
,
\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{X_{0}\in A_{0}\right\}}\frac{h(t,X_{t})}{h(0,X_{0})}
is integrable under
\mathbf{P}
, because

\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{P}}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{X_{0}\in A_{0}\right\}}\frac{h(t,X_{t})}{h(0,X_{0})}\right]=\int_{A_{0}}\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h(t,y)P_{0,t}(x,dy)}{h(0,x)}\rho_{0}(dx)=\rho_{0}(A_{0})=r_{0}.

Similarly, for every
t\in[0,T]
and arbitrary
A\in\mathcal{F}_{t}
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{P}}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{X_{0}\in A_{0}\right\}}\frac{h(T,X_{T})}{h(0,X_{0})}\mathbbm{1}_{A}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{P}}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{X_{0}\in A_{0}\right\}}\frac{\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{P}^{t,X_{t}}}\left[h(T,X_{T})\right]}{h(0,X_{0})}\mathbbm{1}_{A}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{P}}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{X_{0}\in A_{0}\right\}}\frac{h(t,X_{t})}{h(0,X_{0})}\mathbbm{1}_{A}\right]\text{ by \eqref{Equation:MeanValueProperty}}.

Thus,
\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{P}}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{X_{0}\in A_{0}\right\}}\frac{h(T,X_{T})}{h(0,X_{0})}\bigg|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]=\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{X_{0}\in A_{0}\right\}}\frac{h(t,X_{t})}{h(0,X_{0})}
, and the claim follows.
∎

Definition 3.3
.

As a result of Lemma
3.2
,

(3.4)

\mathbf{P}^{h}:=\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{X_{0}\in A_{0}\right\}}}{r_{0}}\frac{h(T,X_{T})}{h(0,X_{0})}\mathbf{P}

defines a probability measure on
\Omega
, and
\mathbf{P}^{h}
is called the
h
-transform
of
\mathbf{P}
.

Let us now examine the transition distribution of
\left\{X_{t}\right\}_{[0,T]}
under the path measure
\mathbf{P}^{h}
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Suppose that
h
satisfies the mean-value property (
3.3
) and
\mathbf{P}^{h}
is as in (
3.4
). Then, under
\mathbf{P}^{h}
, the process
\left\{X_{t}\right\}_{[0,T]}
admits the transition distribution

(3.5)

P^{h}_{s,t}(x,dy)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{h(t,y)}{h(s,x)}P_{s,t}(x,dy)&\text{ if }(s,x)\in\mathcal{A}_{h},\\
0&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

Proof.

First, observe that by the mean-value property (
3.3
), if
h(s,x)>0
, then
P^{h}_{s,t}(x,\cdot)
is a probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Next, it suffices to check the finite-dimensional distributions of
\left\{X_{t}\right\}_{[0,T]}
under
\mathbf{P}^{h}
. Let
0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\dots<t_{N}\leq T
and
B_{0},\dots,B_{N}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be arbitrary and define
\tilde{B_{i}}=B_{i}\cap\left\{x_{i}:(t_{i},x_{i})\in\mathcal{A}_{h}\right\}
for
i=0,1,\dots,N
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}^{h}\left(X_{0}\in B_{0},X_{t_{1}}\in B_{1},\dots,X_{t_{N}}\in B_{N}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{P}}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{X_{0}\in A_{0}\right\}}\frac{h(T,X_{T})}{h(0,X_{0})};X_{t_{i}}\in B_{i},i=0,1,\dots,N\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\tilde{B}_{0}}\int_{B_{1}}\dots\int_{B_{N}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{h(T,y)}{h(0,x_{0})}P_{t_{N},T}(x_{N},dy)P_{t_{N-1},t_{N}}(x_{N-1},dx_{N})\dots P_{0,t_{1}}(x_{0},dx_{1})\rho_{0}(dx_{0})

\displaystyle\overset{(\dagger)}{=}

\displaystyle\int_{\tilde{B}_{0}}\int_{\tilde{B}_{1}}\dots\int_{\tilde{B}_{N}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{h(t_{1},x_{1})}{h(0,x_{0})}\frac{h(t_{2},x_{2})}{h(t_{1},x_{1})}\dots\frac{h(T,y)}{h(t_{N},x_{N})}P_{t_{N},T}(x_{N},dy)\dots P_{0,t_{1}}(x_{0},dx_{1})\rho_{0}(dx_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{B_{0}}\int_{B_{1}}\dots\int_{B_{N}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}P^{h}_{t_{N},T}(x_{N},dy)P^{h}_{t_{N-1},t_{N}}(x_{N-1},dx_{N})\dots P^{h}_{0,t_{1}}(x_{0},dx_{1})\rho_{0}(dx_{0}).

To see the equality in
(\dagger)
, suppose that for some
1\leq i\leq N
,
h(t_{i},x_{i})=0
for some
x_{i}\in B_{i}
. Then using (
3.3
),

\displaystyle\int_{B_{i+1}}\dots\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h(T,y)P_{t_{N},T}(x_{N},dy)

\displaystyle\dots P_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}(x_{i},dx_{i+1})

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\dots\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h(T,y)P_{t_{N},T}(x_{N},dy)\dots P_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}(x_{i},dx_{i+1})

\displaystyle=h(t_{i},x_{i})=0.

Thus, the integral

\int_{B_{i}\cap\left\{x_{i}:(t_{i},x_{i})\notin\mathcal{A}_{h}\right\}}\int_{B_{i+1}}\dots\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h(T,y)P_{t_{N},T}(x_{N},dy)\dots P_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}(x_{i},dx_{i+1})P_{t_{i-1},t_{i}}(x_{i-1},dx_{i})

is equal to
0
, and the equality
(\dagger)
follows.
∎

The proof of Lemma
3.4
shows that the points outside of the set
\mathcal{A}_{h}
have no effect on the the transition distributions under
\mathbf{P}^{h}
. In fact,
\mathbf{P}^{h}
is supported on the set of sample paths that stay entirely in
\mathcal{A}_{h}
. To make this statement accurate, we consider the following hitting times:

(3.6)

\text{for every $s\in[0,T]$ and every $n\geq 1$},\tau^{s}_{n}:=\inf\left\{t\geq s:h(t,X_{t})<\frac{1}{n}\right\}.

Then,
\tau^{s}_{n}
’s are increasing
\left\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\right\}
-stopping times
8
8

8

In general,
\tau^{s}_{n}
’s are optional times and hence stopping times when
\left\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\right\}
is taken to be its usual augmentation.
, and so is

(3.7)

\tau^{s}:=\sup_{n\geq 1}\tau^{s}_{n}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\tau^{s}_{n}.

We are now ready to give an equivalent representation of
\mathbf{P}^{h}
.

Proposition 3.5
.

Assume that
h
satisfies the mean-value property (
3.3
). Let
\mathbf{P}^{h}
be as defined in (
3.4
). Then,

(3.8)

\mathbf{P}^{h}=\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{\tau^{0}>T\right\}}}{r_{0}}\frac{h(T,X_{T})}{h(0,X_{0})}\mathbf{P}.

In other words,
\mathbf{P}^{h}
is supported on
\left\{\tau^{0}>T\right\}
, where
(t,X_{t})\in\mathcal{A}_{h}
for all
t\in[0,T]
.

Proof.

First, we claim that for every
0\leq s\leq t\leq T
and every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

(3.9)

\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{P}^{s,x}}[h(t,X_{t})\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{\tau^{s}\leq t\right\}}]=0.

Indeed, since
\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{\tau^{s}_{n}\leq t\right\}}\rightarrow\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{\tau^{s}\leq t\right\}}
as
n\rightarrow\infty
, using Fatou’s lemma and the strong Markov property of
\left\{X_{t}\right\}_{[0,T]}
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{P}^{s,x}}[h(t,X_{t})\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{\tau^{s}\leq t\right\}}]

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{P}^{s,x}}[h(t,X_{t})\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{\tau^{s}_{n}\leq t\right\}}]

\displaystyle=\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{P}^{s,x}}\left[\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{P}^{\tau^{s}_{n},X_{\tau^{s}_{n}}}}[h(t,X_{t})]\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{\tau^{s}_{n}\leq t\right\}}\right]

\displaystyle=\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{P}^{s,x}}[h(\tau^{s}_{n},X_{\tau^{s}_{n}})\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{\tau^{s}_{n}\leq t\right\}}]\text{ by the mean-value property of $h$}

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{P}(\tau^{s}_{n}\leq t)=0\text{ by right-continuity of $\left\{X_{t}\right\}_{[0,T]}$}.

It follows immediately from (
3.9
) that

\mathbf{P}^{h}(\tau^{0}\leq T)=\frac{1}{r_{0}}\int_{A_{0}}\frac{\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{P}^{0,x}}[h(T,X_{T})\mathbbm{1}_{\tau^{0}\leq T}]}{h(0,x)}\rho_{0}(dx)=0,

and hence (
3.8
) holds.
∎

In later sections, in our study of the SBP, we will consider
h
in the form of
h(t,x)=\int g(y)P_{t,T}(x,dy)
,
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
, for some non-negative
g
. The mean-value property is always satisfied by such an
h
, and hence
\mathbf{P}^{h}
is always well defined. We now have two equivalent representations (
3.4
) and (
3.8
) for
\mathbf{P}^{h}
, both of which will be useful in later sections.

We close this subsection by introducing a notion which is closely related to the mean-value property andn which plays an important role in the study of the
h
-transform when
h
possesses a certain level of regularity.

Definition 3.6
.

We say that a function
h:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to[0,\infty)
is
harmonic
if
h
is of class
C^{1,2}
on
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
h
satisfies
{\mathfrak{L}}h=\frac{\partial h}{\partial t}+Lh=0
on
(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Remark.
It is straightforward to see that, under Assumption
(A1)
, when
h
is sufficiently regular, e.g.,
h\in C^{1,2}_{c}
, the notion of
h
being harmonic is equivalent to
h
satisfying the mean-value property. However, our definition of the
h
-transform only requires the latter.

3.2.
h
-Transform: the Generator Approach

In this subsection we study the
h
-transform of a path measure by analyzing the change occurred in the generator. We further derive the martingale problem formulation corresponding to the transformed path measure in the case when
h
is a harmonic function.

We assume that the operator
L
in (
1.3
) satisfies Assumption
(A1)
and
\mathbf{P}
is a solution to the martingale problem for
(L,\rho_{0})
. For a non-negative function
h
of class
C^{1,2}
on
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we define
\mathcal{A}_{h}
as in (
3.1
) and
{\mathfrak{L}}^{h},L^{h}
integro-differential operators as: for
f\in C^{1,2}(\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
,

(3.10)

{\mathfrak{L}}^{h}f:=\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{A}_{h}}\frac{{\mathfrak{L}}(hf)}{h}\text{ and }L^{h}f:=\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{A}_{h}}({\mathfrak{L}}^{h}f-\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}).

As discussed in the previous subsection, when
h
satisfies the mean-value property (
3.3
), we refer to the path measure
\mathbf{P}^{h}
defined in (
3.4
) as the
h
-transform of
\mathbf{P}
. We will show that if, in addition,
h
is harmonic, then
\mathbf{P}^{h}
solves the martingale problem for
L^{h}
with a proper initial distribution. First, let us present the explicit expression of
L^{h}
.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
L
be the jump-diffusion operator defined in (
1.3
),
h
be a non-negative function of class
C^{1,2}
on
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
\mathcal{A}_{h}
be as in (
3.1
). Define, for every
(t,x)\in\mathcal{A}_{h}
,

(3.11)

\displaystyle b^{h}(t,x):=b(t,x)

\displaystyle+\sigma\sigma^{T}\nabla\log h(t,x)+

\displaystyle\int_{|z|\leq 1}\frac{h(t,x+\gamma(t,x,z))-h(t,x)}{h(t,x)}\gamma(t,x,z)\nu(dz),

and

(3.12)

\nu^{h}(t,x;dz):=\frac{h(t,x+\gamma(t,x,z))}{h(t,x)}\nu(dz).

Then for every
f\in C^{1,2}(\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
, at
(t,x)\in\mathcal{A}_{h}
,

L(hf)(t,x)=\left(h\tilde{L}f+fLh\right)(t,x),

where
\tilde{L}
is the operator defined in (
1.3
) with
b
replaced by
b^{h}
, and
\nu
replaced by
\nu^{h}
.
Equivalently, writing
\tilde{{\mathfrak{L}}}:=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+\tilde{L}
, at
(t,x)\in\mathcal{A}_{h}
,

(3.13)

{\mathfrak{L}}(hf)(t,x)=\left(h\tilde{{\mathfrak{L}}}f+f{\mathfrak{L}}h\right)(t,x).

In particular, if
h
is harmonic, then

\tilde{{\mathfrak{L}}}f=\frac{1}{h}{\mathfrak{L}}(hf)={\mathfrak{L}}^{h}f\text{ and }L^{h}f=\tilde{L}f\text{ on }\mathcal{A}_{h}.

All the equations in the lemma are derived by direct computations, and hence the proof is omitted. We will only point out that, under Assumptions
on
\sigma,b,\gamma
, it is easy to verify that for every
(t,x)\in\mathcal{A}_{h}
, the integral in (
3.11
) is finite and for
\nu^{h}(t,x,\cdot)
defined in (
3.12
),

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\ell}}(1\wedge|z|^{2})\nu^{h}(t,x;dz)<\infty,

and hence
b^{h}
is well defined and
\nu^{h}(t,x;\cdot)
is a Lévy measure.

We are now ready to formulate the martingale problem statement involving
\mathbf{P}^{h}
and
L^{h}
.

Theorem 3.8
.

Assume that Assumption
(A1)
holds for the jump-diffusion operator
L
and
\mathbf{P}
is the solution to the martingale problem for
(L,\rho_{0})
. Let
h
be a harmonic function that satisfies the mean-value property, and let
\mathbf{P}^{h}
and
L^{h}
be defined as in (
3.4
) and (
3.10
) respectively. Then
\mathbf{P}^{h}
solves the martingale problem for
(L^{h},\rho^{h}_{0})
, where
\rho^{h}_{0}:=\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{A_{0}}}{r_{0}}\rho_{0}
with
A_{0}
and
r_{0}
defined as in (
3.2
). Moreover, the canonical process
\left\{X_{t}\right\}_{[0,T]}
under
\mathbf{P}^{h}
is a strong Markov process.

Proof.

We first check the initial distribution. Let
B\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be any Borel set, then

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}^{h}(X_{0}\in B)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{P}}\left[\frac{1}{r_{0}}\frac{h(T,X_{T})}{h(0,X_{0})};X_{0}\in(B\cap A_{0})\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{B\cap A_{0}}\frac{1}{r_{0}}\frac{1}{h(0,x)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h(T,y)P_{0,T}(x,dy)\rho_{0}(dx)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r_{0}}\rho_{0}(B\cap A_{0})\text{ by the mean-value property of $h$.}

Now, we verify that for every
f\in C^{1,2}_{c}
,
\left\{f(t,X_{t})-\int_{0}^{t}{\mathfrak{L}}^{h}f(r,X_{r})dr\,:\,0\leq t\leq T\right\}
under
\mathbf{P}^{h}

is a martingale. Let
0\leq s\leq t\leq T
and
A\in\mathcal{F}_{s}
be arbitrary. Then, by Lemma
3.2
,

\displaystyle\int_{A}(f(t,X_{t})-f(s,X_{s}))d\mathbf{P}^{h}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r_{0}}\int_{A}\mathbbm{1}_{A_{0}}(X_{0})\frac{h(T,X_{T})}{h(0,X_{0})}\left(f(t,X_{t})-f(s,X_{s})\right)d\mathbf{P}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r_{0}}\int_{A}\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{A_{0}}(X_{0})}{h(0,X_{0})}\left((hf)(t,X_{t})-(hf)(s,X_{s})\right)d\mathbf{P}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r_{0}}\int_{A}\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{A_{0}}(X_{0})}{h(0,X_{0})}\left((hf)(t,X_{t})-(hf)(t\wedge\tau^{s},X_{t\wedge\tau^{s}})\right)d\mathbf{P}

\displaystyle\hskip 22.76228pt+\frac{1}{r_{0}}\int_{A}\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{A_{0}}(X_{0})}{h(0,X_{0})}\left((hf)(t\wedge\tau^{s},X_{t\wedge\tau^{s}})-(hf)(s,X_{s})\right)d\mathbf{P},

where
\tau^{s}
is as in (
3.7
). We first treat the integral involving the difference in
hf
between
t\wedge\tau^{s}
and
t
, and it suffices to restrict the integral to
\left\{\tau^{s}\leq t\right\}
. That is,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{r_{0}}\int_{A\cap\left\{\tau^{s}\leq t\right\}}\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{A_{0}}(X_{0})}{h(0,X_{0})}

\displaystyle\left((hf)(t,X_{t})-(hf)(\tau^{s},X_{\tau^{s}})\right)d\mathbf{P}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r_{0}}\int_{A\cap\left\{\tau^{s}\leq t\right\}}\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{A_{0}}(X_{0})}{h(0,X_{0})}\left(h(t,X_{t})(f(t,X_{t})-f(\tau^{s},X_{\tau^{s}})\right)d\mathbf{P}

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt+\frac{1}{r_{0}}\int_{A\cap\left\{\tau^{s}\leq t\right\}}\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{A_{0}}(X_{0})}{h(0,X_{0})}\left((h(t,X_{t})-h(\tau^{s},X_{\tau^{s}})\right)f(\tau^{s},X_{\tau^{s}})d\mathbf{P}.

Conditioning on
\mathcal{F}_{\tau^{s}}
inside the second integral above and using the strong Markov property of
\left\{X_{t}\right\}_{[0,T]}
under
\mathbf{P}
, we see that the second integral vanishes due to the mean-value property of
h
. Conditioning on
\mathcal{F}_{s}
inside the first integral and using the boundedness of
f
, we see that the first integral is bounded from above by

\frac{\left\|f\right\|_{\text{u}}}{r_{0}}\int_{A}\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{A_{0}}(X_{0})}{h(0,X_{0})}\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{P}^{s,X_{s}}}[h(t,X_{t})\mathbbm{1}_{\tau^{s}\leq t}]d\mathbf{P},

which also vanishes according to (
3.9
). Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\int_{A}

\displaystyle\left(f(t,X_{t})-f(s,X_{s})\right)d\mathbf{P}^{h}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r_{0}}\int_{A}\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{A_{0}}(X_{0})}{h(0,X_{0})}\left((hf)(t\wedge\tau^{s},X_{t\wedge\tau^{s}})-(hf)(s,X_{s})\right)d\mathbf{P}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r_{0}}\int_{A}\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{A_{0}}(X_{0})}{h(0,X_{0})}\int_{s}^{t\wedge\tau^{s}}{\mathfrak{L}}(hf)(r,X_{r})\,dr\,d\mathbf{P}\text{ by \ref{Assumption:MartingaleProblem-s,x}, since $hf\in C^{1,2}_{c}$}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r_{0}}\int_{A}\int_{s}^{t\wedge\tau^{s}}\mathbbm{1}_{A_{0}}(X_{0})\frac{h(r,X_{r})}{h(0,X_{0})}{\mathfrak{L}}^{h}f(r,X_{r})\,dr\,d\mathbf{P}\text{ since ${\mathfrak{L}}h=0$ and $\left\{(r,X_{r})\right\}_{[s,\tau^{s})}\subseteq\mathcal{A}_{h}$}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r_{0}}\int_{A}\mathbbm{1}_{A_{0}}(X_{0})\frac{h(T,X_{T})}{h(0,X_{0})}\int_{s}^{t\wedge\tau^{s}}{\mathfrak{L}}^{h}f(r,X_{r})\,dr\,d\mathbf{P}\text{ using the martingale property again}

\displaystyle=\int_{A}\int_{s}^{t\wedge\tau^{s}}{\mathfrak{L}}^{h}f(s,X_{s})\,ds\,d\mathbf{P}^{h}=\int_{A}\int_{s}^{t}{\mathfrak{L}}^{h}f(s,X_{s})\,ds\,d\mathbf{P}^{h}\text{ as desired,}

where the last equation is due to the fact that
\mathbf{P}^{h}
is supported on
\left\{\tau^{0}>T\right\}\subseteq\left\{\tau^{s}>t\right\}
.

The strong Markov property of
\left\{X_{t}\right\}_{[0,T]}
under
\mathbf{P}^{h}
can be established following a similar argument.
∎

We have identified
\mathbf{P}^{h}
as the solution to the martingale problem for
(L^{h},\rho_{0}^{h})
. Seeing that
L^{h}
takes the same form as
L
with
b
replaced by
b^{h}
and
\nu
replaced by

, it is tempting to conclude that
(\left\{X_{t}\right\}_{[0,T]},\mathbf{P}^{h})
is a (weak) solution to the SDE

\displaystyle dX_{t}=b^{h}(t,X_{t})dt

4.
SBP for Jump Diffusions: the General Theory

In this section, we return to the Schrödinger bridge problem (SBP) for jump diffusions and study its solution under the theory of
h
-transforms developed in Section
3
. Recall from Theorem
2.3
that, given a Markov reference measure
\mathbf{R}
and target endpoint distributions
\rho_{0},\rho_{T}

(\mathfrak{f},\mathfrak{g})
is the solution to the Schrödinger system (

1.1

\hat{\mathbf{P}}=\mathfrak{f}(X_{0})\mathfrak{g}(X_{T})\mathbf{R}
. We aim at connecting
\hat{\mathbf{P}}
with a certain
h
-transform of the underlying path measure.

In the following subsections, we first discuss a particular type of SBPs that are explicitly “solvable”. Namely, when
\rho_{0}
is a point mass, we can in fact explicitly determine
(\mathfrak{f},\mathfrak{g})
and hence study the property of
\hat{\mathbf{P}}
directly. However, for general
\rho_{0},\rho_{T}
, it is difficult, if not impossible, to derive concrete expressions for
(\mathfrak{f},\mathfrak{g})
. Instead, we will approach
\hat{\mathbf{P}}
from an
h
-transform point of view by considering the function
\mathfrak{h}(t,x):=\int\mathfrak{g}(y)P_{t,T}(x,dy)
and apply the results developed in Section
3
. Although in general we do not have any regularity condition on
\mathfrak{g}

a priori
to guarantee that
\mathfrak{h}
is harmonic, we can establish an approximation theory under Assumption
(A3)
. In particular, we will prove that the desired SBP solution
\hat{\mathbf{P}}
is achieved as the limit, under strong convergence, of a family of path measures all of which are
h
-transforms by harmonic functions.

4.1.
SBP with Explicit Solution

Let us consider the particular case of SBP when the target initial endpoint distribution is a point mass, meaning
\rho_{0}=\delta_{x_{0}}
for some
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

\mathbf{R}
under which the process has the initial distribution

\begin{cases}\vskip 5.69046pt\displaystyle\mathfrak{f}(x_{0}){\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{R}}}\left[\mathfrak{g}(X_{T})\right]=1,\\
\displaystyle\mathfrak{g}(y)\mathfrak{f}(x_{0})=\frac{d\rho_{T}}{d\mathbf{R}_{T}}(y)\text{ for $\mathbf{R}_{T}$-a.e. $y$}.\end{cases}

Assume without loss of generality that
\mathfrak{f}(x_{0})=1
and hence
\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{R}}\left[\mathfrak{g}(X_{T})\right]=1

\mathfrak{g}=\frac{d\rho_{T}}{d\mathbf{R}_{T}}

\hat{\mathbf{P}}=\mathfrak{g}(X_{T})\mathbf{R}=\frac{d\rho_{T}}{d\mathbf{R}_{T}}(X_{T})\mathbf{R}.

To make it concrete, we will examine a simple example that falls outside the usual literature on diffusion SBPs. We also illustrate the results from Section

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636ptdX_{t}=b^{\mathfrak{h}}(t,X_{t-})

This theorem covers the “good case”, where the function

Let us begin by building an approximation to
\mathfrak{g}
. First, since

is non-negative and measurable, there exists a sequence of bounded simple functions
\left\{g^{\prime}_{k}\right\}_{k\geq 1}

where

and the sets

are measurable and pairwise disjoint for different

’s. Fix
k
. By Lusin’s theorem, there exists
g^{\prime\prime}_{k}\in C_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that
\mathbf{R}_{T}(\left\{g^{\prime}_{k}\neq g^{\prime\prime}_{k}\right\})\leq k^{-3}
. We further choose a compact set

such that
\mathbf{R}_{T}(D_{k}^{c})\leq k^{-3}
and choose

such that
\left\|g_{k}\right\|_{\text{u}}\leq k
and
\left\|g_{k}-g^{\prime\prime}_{k}\right\|_{\text{u},D_{k}}\leq k^{-2}

f_{k}
be the non-negative function on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that, for every

f_{k}(x):=\left(\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{R}_{0T}}\left[g_{k}(y)|x\right]\right)^{-1}
if the denominator is positive, and

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
f_{k},g_{k}:k\geq 1
be defined as above. Then

\lim_{k\to\infty}f_{k}(X_{0})g_{k}(X_{T})\frac{d\rho_{0}}{d\mathbf{R}_{0}}(X_{0})=\mathbbm{1}_{S_{0}^{c}}(X_{0})\mathfrak{f}(X_{0})\mathfrak{g}(X_{T})\,\,\text{ $\mathbf{R}$-a.s.},

or equivalently,

\lim_{k\to\infty}f_{k}(x)g_{k}(y)\frac{d\rho_{0}}{d\mathbf{R}_{0}}(x)=\mathbbm{1}_{S_{0}^{c}}(x)\mathfrak{f}(x)\mathfrak{g}(y)\,\,\text{ $\mathbf{R}_{0T}$-a.s. }

Proof.

First note that, by the Borel-Cantelli lemma,
\mathbf{R}_{T}(\left\{g^{\prime}_{k}\neq g^{\prime\prime}_{k}\right\}\text{ i.o.})=0
and therefore

(4.4)

\lim_{k\to\infty}g^{\prime\prime}_{k}=\lim_{k\to\infty}g^{\prime}_{k}=\mathfrak{g}\,\,\mathbf{R}_{T}\text{-a.s.}

Further,

\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{R}_{T}}\left[\left|g_{k}-g^{\prime\prime}_{k}\right|\right]\leq 2k\mathbf{R}_{T}(D_{k}^{c})+\frac{1}{k^{2}}\mathbf{R}_{T}(D_{k})\leq\frac{3}{k^{2}},

and thus for any
\varepsilon>0
,

\mathbf{R}_{T}\left(\left\{\left|g_{k}-g^{\prime\prime}_{k}\right|>\varepsilon\right\}\right)\leq\frac{3}{\varepsilon k^{2}}.

Applying the Borel-Cantelli lemma again, we have that

(4.5)

\lim_{k\to\infty}\left|g_{k}-g^{\prime\prime}_{k}\right|=0\,\,\mathbf{R}_{T}\text{-a.s.}

Combining (
4.4
) and (
4.5
), we conclude that
\lim_{k\to\infty}g_{k}=\mathfrak{g}\,\,\mathbf{R}_{T}\text{-a.s.}
, which is equivalent to

\lim_{k\to\infty}g_{k}(y)=\mathfrak{g}(y)\,\,\mathbf{R}_{0T}\text{-a.s.}

It remains to show that

\lim_{k\to\infty}f_{k}(x)\frac{d\rho_{0}}{d\mathbf{R}_{0}}(x)=\mathbbm{1}_{S_{0}^{c}}(x)\mathfrak{f}(x)\,\,\mathbf{R}_{0T}\text{-a.s.}

Recall that since
\mathfrak{f}
solves the Schrödinger system,

(4.6)

\mathfrak{f}(x)\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{R}_{0T}}\left[\mathfrak{g}(y)|x\right]=\frac{d\rho_{0}}{d\mathbf{R}_{0}}(x)\,\,\mathbf{R}_{0T}\text{-a.s.},

and therefore from the definition of
S_{0}
and
f_{k}
, it suffices to show that

(4.7)

\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{R}_{0T}}\left[g_{k}(y)|x\right]=\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{R}_{0T}}\left[\mathfrak{g}(y)|x\right]\,\,\mathbf{R}_{0T}\text{-a.s.}

By the monotone convergence of conditional expectation,

\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{R}_{0T}}\left[g^{\prime}_{k}(y)|x\right]=\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{R}_{0T}}\left[\mathfrak{g}(y)|x\right].

Note that for every
k\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{R}_{0T}}\left[\left|g_{k}(y)-g^{\prime}_{k}(y)\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{R}_{T}}\left[\left|g_{k}-g^{\prime\prime}_{k}\right|\right]+\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{R}_{T}}\left[\left|g^{\prime}_{k}-g^{\prime\prime}_{k}\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3}{k^{2}}+k\mathbf{R}_{T}\left(\left\{g^{\prime}_{k}\neq g^{\prime\prime}_{k}\right\}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{k^{2}},

which implies that

\lim_{k\to\infty}\left|\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{R}_{0T}}\left[g_{k}(y)|x\right]-\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{R}_{0T}}\left[g^{\prime}_{k}(y)|x\right]\right|=0\;\;\mathbf{R}_{0T}\text{-a.s.}

From here (
4.7
) follows immediately.
∎

We can now express the SBP solution
\hat{\mathbf{P}}
as the limit of a sequence of
h
-transforms by harmonic functions.

Theorem 4.4
.

Suppose that Assumption
(A3)
holds for
\left\{P_{s,t}(x,\cdot):0\leq s\leq t\leq T,x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\right\}
the transition distribution under the strong Markov reference measure
\mathbf{R}
. Let
\mathbf{P},\hat{\mathbf{P}}
be the same as in Lemma
4.1
and
g_{k},f_{k}
,
k\geq 1
be as in Lemma
4.3
. For every
k\geq 1
, we define

(4.8)

h_{k}(t,x):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}g_{k}(y)P_{t,T}(x,dy)\text{ for }(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n},

and

(4.9)

\mathbf{P}^{h_{k}}:=\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{X_{0}\in A_{k}\right\}}}{r_{k}}\frac{h_{k}(T,X_{T})}{h_{k}(0,X_{0})}\mathbf{P},

where
A_{k}:=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:h_{k}(0,x)>0\right\}
and
r_{k}:=\rho_{0}(A_{k})
.
Then
\left\{\mathbf{P}^{h_{k}}\right\}_{k\geq 1}
is a sequence of
h
-transforms by harmonic functions, and
\mathbf{P}^{h_{k}}
converges to
\hat{\mathbf{P}}
in the strong topology in the sense that

\lim_{k\to\infty}\sup_{A\in\mathcal{F}}|\mathbf{P}^{h_{k}}(A)-\hat{\mathbf{P}}(A)|=0.

Proof.

Fix
k\geq 1
. Since
g_{k}\in C_{c}^{\infty}
and
(A3)
holds,
h_{k}
is harmonic. Note that
h_{k}(T,y)=g_{k}(y)
for
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
h_{k}(0,x)=\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{R}_{0T}}\left[g_{k}(y)|x\right]=(f_{k}(x))^{-1}
for
x\in A_{k}
. By combining (
4.9
) with (
4.2
), we can rewrite
\mathbf{P}^{h_{k}}
as

\mathbf{P}^{h_{k}}=\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{X_{0}\in A_{k}\right\}}}{r_{k}}\frac{h_{k}(T,X_{T})}{h_{k}(0,X_{0})}\frac{d\rho_{0}}{d\mathbf{R}_{0}}(X_{0})\mathbf{R}=\frac{1}{r_{k}}f_{k}(X_{0})g_{k}(X_{T})\frac{d\rho_{0}}{d\mathbf{R}_{0}}(X_{0})\mathbf{R}.

Recall that
\hat{\mathbf{P}}=\mathbbm{1}_{S_{0}^{c}}(X_{0})\mathfrak{f}(X_{0})\mathfrak{g}(X_{T})\mathbf{R}
. Therefore, to prove the conclusion of the theorem, it is sufficient to show that
\mathbf{P}^{h_{k}}
is a path measure for each
k\geq 1
and

(4.10)

\frac{1}{r_{k}}f_{k}(X_{0})g_{k}(X_{T})\frac{d\rho_{0}}{d\mathbf{R}_{0}}(X_{0})\xrightarrow{k\to\infty}\mathbbm{1}_{S_{0}^{c}}(X_{0})\mathfrak{f}(X_{0})\mathfrak{g}(X_{T})\text{ in }L^{1}(\mathbf{R}).

First, we apply Lemma
4.3
to get

(4.11)

\lim_{k\to\infty}f_{k}(X_{0})g_{k}(X_{T})\frac{d\rho_{0}}{d\mathbf{R}_{0}}(X_{0})=\mathbbm{1}_{S_{0}^{c}}(X_{0})\mathfrak{f}(X_{0})\mathfrak{g}(X_{T})\,\,\mathbf{R}\text{-a.s.}

Next, we derive that

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{R}}

\displaystyle\left[f_{k}(X_{0})g_{k}(X_{T})\frac{d\rho_{0}}{d\mathbf{R}_{0}}(X_{0})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{R}}\left[f_{k}(X_{0})\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{R}}\left[g_{k}(X_{T})|X_{0}\right]\frac{d\rho_{0}}{d\mathbf{R}_{0}}(X_{0})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{R}}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{X_{0}\in A_{k}\right\}}\frac{d\rho_{0}}{d\mathbf{R}_{0}}(X_{0})\right]\text{ since $f_{k}=(\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{R}_{0T}}\left[g_{k}(y)|\cdot\right])^{-1}$ on $A_{k}$}

\displaystyle=\rho_{0}(A_{k})=r_{k},

which confirms that
\mathbf{P}^{h_{k}}
is a path measure.
Furthermore, by (
4.6
) and (
4.7
), for
\mathbf{R}_{0}
-a.e. (and hence
\rho_{0}
-a.e.)

, if
\frac{d\rho_{0}}{d\mathbf{R}_{0}}(x)>0
, then
\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{R}_{0T}}\left[\mathfrak{g}(y)|x\right]>0
and thus

\lim_{k\to\infty}h_{k}(0,x)=\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{R}_{0T}}\left[g_{k}(y)|x\right]>0.

Therefore, using Fatou’s lemma, we have that

1=\rho_{0}\left(\left\{\frac{d\rho_{0}}{d\mathbf{R}_{0}}>0\right\}\right)\leq\rho_{0}\left(\left\{\lim_{k\to\infty}h_{k}(0,x)>0\right\}\right)\leq\liminf_{k\to\infty}\rho_{0}(A_{k})=r_{k}.

We conclude that as

,

(4.12)

\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{R}}\left[f_{k}(X_{0})g_{k}(X_{T})\frac{d\rho_{0}}{d\mathbf{R}_{0}}(X_{0})\right]=r_{k}\xrightarrow{k\to\infty}1=\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{R}}\left[\mathfrak{f}(X_{0})\mathfrak{g}(X_{T})\right].

Finally, combining (
4.11
) and (

convergence relation (

∎

We close this section with a result stating that the above strong convergence does not only occur at the endpoints

, but also in a “dynamic” manner in terms of the approximation of

Theorem 4.5
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
4.4
, if
\left\{h_{k}\right\}_{k\geq 1}
is the sequence of harmonic functions defined in (

), then for every

,

\lim_{k\to\infty}h_{k}(t,X_{t})=\mathfrak{h}(t,X_{t})\,\,\mathbf{P}\text{-a.s.,}

and

\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{X_{0}\in A_{k}\right\}}}{r_{k}}\frac{h_{k}(t,X_{t})}{h_{k}(0,X_{0})}\xrightarrow{k\to\infty}\frac{\mathfrak{h}(t,X_{t})}{\mathfrak{h}(0,X_{0})}\text{ in $L^{1}(\mathbf{P})$.}

Proof.

Let
g_{k},g^{\prime}_{k}
,
k\geq 1
, be the same as introduced before Lemma
4.3
. For every
k\geq 1
, and every
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
, define

h^{\prime}_{k}(t,x):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}g^{\prime}_{k}(y)P_{t,T}(x,dy).

Clearly,
\left\{h^{\prime}_{k}\right\}_{k\geq 1}
is an increasing sequence that converges to
\mathfrak{h}
. Moreover,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{R}}\left[\left|h_{k}(t,X_{t})-h^{\prime}_{k}(t,X_{t})\right|\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left|h_{k}(t,x)-h^{\prime}_{k}(t,x)\right|P_{0,t}(w,dx)\mathbf{R}_{0}(dw)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left|g_{k}(y)-g^{\prime}_{k}(y)\right|P_{t,T}(x,dy)P_{0,t}(w,dx)\mathbf{R}_{0}(dw)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{R}_{T}}\left[\left|g_{k}-g^{\prime}_{k}\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4}{k^{2}}\text{ by the same argument as in the proof of Lemma \ref{lem:f_k,g_k approximation convergence}}.

It is sufficient for us to conclude that
\lim_{k\to\infty}\left|h_{k}(t,X_{t})-h^{\prime}_{k}(t,X_{t})\right|=0

\mathbf{R}
-a.s., and thus

\lim_{k\to\infty}h_{k}(t,X_{t})=\mathfrak{h}(t,X_{t})\,\,\mathbf{R}\text{-a.s., and hence $\mathbf{P}$-a.s.}

To prove the second statement, we observe that

\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{P}}\left[\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{X_{0}\in A_{k}\right\}}}{r_{k}}\frac{h_{k}(T,X_{T})}{h_{k}(0,X_{0})}\bigg|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]=\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{X_{0}\in A_{k}\right\}}}{r_{k}}\frac{h_{k}(t,X_{t})}{h_{k}(0,X_{0})}

and

\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{P}}\left[\frac{\mathfrak{h}(T,X_{T})}{\mathfrak{h}(0,X_{0})}\bigg|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]=\frac{\mathfrak{h}(t,X_{t})}{\mathfrak{h}(0,X_{0})}.

It is easy to see that the fact (
4.10
) established in Theorem
4.4
is equivalent to

\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{X_{0}\in A_{k}\right\}}}{r_{k}}\frac{h_{k}(T,X_{T})}{h_{k}(0,X_{0})}\xrightarrow{k\to\infty}\frac{\mathfrak{h}(T,X_{T})}{\mathfrak{h}(0,X_{0})}\text{ in }L^{1}(\mathbf{P}).

Therefore, the respective conditional expectations must also satisfy the same
L^{1}(\mathbf{P})
convergence relation.
∎

In this section, we first revisit the Schrödinger system for a jump-diffusion SBP under the general assumption that the transition density exists. Then, we adopt the setting of Kunita

density functions, we obtain results analogous to the diffusion case on the Schrödinger system solution

5.1.
Schrödinger System for Jump Diffusions

As in the previous section, we continue working under the assumption that the conclusion of Theorem
2.3
holds and
(\mathfrak{f},\mathfrak{g})
is a pair of non-negative, measurable functions that solve the Schrödinger system (
2.6
). As in the diffusion case, we assume that the reference endpoint distributions
\mathbf{R}_{0},\mathbf{R}_{T}
and the target ones
\rho_{0},\rho_{T}
all admit probability density functions, which we will write as
\mathbf{R}_{0}(x)dx
, etc, and the transition distribution under
\mathbf{R}
admits a transition density function everywhere and at all time, i.e., for every
0\leq s<t\leq T
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
P_{s,t}(x,dy)=p(s,x,t,y)dy
. We will re-derive the Schrödinger system for the jump-diffusion SBP. Note that for this derivation, we do not need the transition density function to be strictly positive.

Recall that the solution to the SBP takes the form
\hat{\mathbf{P}}=\mathfrak{f}(X_{0})\mathfrak{g}(X_{T})\mathbf{R}
. Expressed in terms of the density functions, this fact yields that

(5.1)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathfrak{f}(x)\mathfrak{g}(y)p(0,x,T,y)\mathbf{R}_{0}(x)dxdy=1.

Further, the Schrödinger system (
2.6
) can be rewritten as

(5.2)

\begin{cases}\vskip 5.69046pt\displaystyle\mathfrak{f}(x)\mathbf{R}_{0}(x)\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathfrak{g}(y)p(0,x,T,y)dy\right)=\rho_{0}(x)&\text{ for $\mathbf{R}_{0}$-a.e. $x$},\\
\displaystyle\mathfrak{g}(y)\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathfrak{f}(x)\mathbf{R}_{0}(x)p(0,x,T,y)dx\right)=\rho_{T}(y)&\text{ for $\mathbf{R}_{T}$-a.e. $y$}.\end{cases}

Therefore, if we further define
\varphi_{T}:=\mathfrak{g}
,
\hat{\varphi}_{0}:=\mathfrak{f}\cdot\mathbf{R}_{0}
, and

\begin{cases}\varphi_{0}(x):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}p(0,x,T,y)\varphi_{T}(y)dy\text{ for }x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\\
\hat{\varphi}_{T}(y):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}p(0,x,T,y)\hat{\varphi}_{0}(x)dx\text{ for }y\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\end{cases}

then (
5.2
) can be interpreted as the 4-tuple of functions
(\varphi_{0},\varphi_{T},\hat{\varphi}_{0},\hat{\varphi}_{T})
being a solution to the Schrödinger system as in (
2.9
).

It is important to note that, although (
5.1
) guarantees that the product
\mathfrak{f}\mathfrak{g}
is integrable with respect to
p(0,x,T,y)\mathbf{R}_{0}(x)=\mathbf{R}_{0T}(x,y)
, it does not imply the respective integrability of
\mathfrak{f},\mathfrak{g}
with respect to
\mathbf{R}_{0},\mathbf{R}_{T}
. As a result, the functions
\varphi_{0},\varphi_{T},\hat{\varphi}_{0},\hat{\varphi}_{T}
may take the value of infinity
[
44
]
. On the other hand, the first equation in (
5.2
) implies that
0<\hat{\varphi}_{0}(x)\varphi_{0}(x)<\infty
for
\rho_{0}
-a.e.
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, which means that
0<\hat{\varphi}_{0}<\infty
and
0<\varphi_{0}<\infty
a.s. under
\rho_{0}
. Similarly, we also have
0<\hat{\varphi}_{T}<\infty
and
0<\varphi_{T}<\infty
a.s. under
\rho_{T}
.

We now ready present a derivation of the probability density function of the marginal distribution of
\hat{\mathbf{P}}
. This is a known result that relies only on the absolute continuity of the initial distribution
\rho_{0}
(with respect to the Lebesgue measure) and the existence of the transition density
p(s,x,t,y)
; we include this derivation for completeness. For every
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we have

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{P}}(X_{t}(\cdot)\in B)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{R}}\left[\mathfrak{f}(X_{0})\mathfrak{g}(X_{T})\mathbbm{1}_{B}(X_{t})\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathfrak{f}(w)\left(\int_{B}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathfrak{g}(y)P_{t,T}(x,dy)P_{0,t}(w,dx)\right)\mathbf{R}_{0}(dw)

\displaystyle=\int_{B}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathfrak{f}(w)\mathfrak{g}(y)p(t,x,T,y)\mathbf{R}_{0}(w)p(0,w,t,x)dydwdx

\displaystyle=\int_{B}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathfrak{g}(y)p(t,x,T,y)dy\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathfrak{f}(w)p(0,w,t,x)\mathbf{R}_{0}(w)dwdx,

showing that the marginal distribution at time
t
, denoted by
\hat{\mathbf{P}}_{t}
, admits a probability density function given by

(5.3)

\hat{\mathbf{P}}_{t}(x):=\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathfrak{g}(y)p(t,x,T,y)dy\right)\left(\int_{S_{0}^{c}}\mathfrak{f}(w)p(0,w,t,x)\mathbf{R}_{0}(w)dw\right).

If
\varphi,\hat{\varphi}
are two functions on
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
given by, for
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

(5.4)

\varphi(t,x):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathfrak{g}(y)p(t,x,T,y)dy

and

(5.5)

\hat{\varphi}(t,x):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathfrak{f}(w)p(0,w,t,x)\mathbf{R}_{0}(w)dw,

then (
5.3
) can be rewritten as
\hat{\mathbf{P}}_{t}(x)=\varphi(t,x)\hat{\varphi}(t,x)
. Further, we observe that the boundary values
(\varphi(0,\cdot),\varphi(T,\cdot),\hat{\varphi}(0,\cdot),\hat{\varphi}(T,\cdot))
coincide with
(\varphi_{0},\varphi_{T},\hat{\varphi}_{0},\hat{\varphi}_{T})
introduced above.

Again, we point out that (
5.3
) only guarantees the finiteness of the product
(\varphi\hat{\varphi})(t,x)
for every

and Lebesgue-a.e.
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, but
\varphi(t,x)
and
\hat{\varphi}(t,x)
alone may be infinite. However, under certain circumstances, we can obtain the finiteness of
\varphi,\hat{\varphi}
individually. For example, if
\mathfrak{f},\mathfrak{g}
are bounded, then it follows immediately from (
5.4
) and (
5.5
) that
\varphi
is bounded and
\hat{\varphi}
is Lebesgue-a.e. finite. Another special case is when the transition density function is
positive
. Indeed, if
p(s,x,t,y)>0
for every
0\leq s<t\leq T
and Lebesgue-a.e.
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then (
5.4
) and (
5.5

Lemma 5.5

There exists a subsequence

\lim_{k\to\infty}h_{k}^{(m_{k})}(t,x)=\mathfrak{h}(t,x)\text{ for every }(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Proof.

For every

\left\{P_{t,T}^{(m)}(x,\cdot)\right\}_{m\geq 1}

Choose a subsequence

such that for all

\left|p_{m_{k}}(t,x,T,y)-p(t,x,T,y)\right|\leq\frac{1}{k^{2}\text{vol}(D_{k})}

and

\displaystyle\left|h_{k}^{(m_{k})}(t,x)-\mathfrak{h}(t,x)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|h_{k}(t,x)-\mathfrak{h}(t,x)\right|+\left|h_{k}^{(m_{k})}(t,x)-h_{k}(t,x)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|h_{k}(t,x)-\mathfrak{h}(t,x)\right|+\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}g_{k}(y)(p_{m_{k}}(t,x,T,y)-p(t,x,T,y))dy\right|,

where the first term converges to 0 as

\displaystyle\int_{D_{k}^{c}}\left|g_{k}(y)\right|\left|p_{m_{k}}(t,x,T,y)\right|dy+\int_{D_{k}^{c}}\left|g_{k}(y)\right|\left|p(t,x,T,y)\right|dy
