Abstract
. Social media platforms are increasingly developing features that display crowdsourced context alongside posts, modeled after X’s Community Notes. These systems, which we term Crowdsourced Context Systems (CCS), have the potential to reshape our information ecosystem as major platforms embrace them as alternatives to top-down fact-checking. To deeply understand the features and implications of such systems, we perform a systematic literature review of existing CCS research and analyze several real-world CSS implementations. Based on our analysis, we develop a framework with three distinct components. First, we present a theoretical model to help conceptualize and define CCS. Second, we identify a design space encompassing six key aspects of CCS: participation, inputs, curation, presentation, platform treatment, and transparency. Third, we identify key normative implications of different CCS design and implementation choices. Our framework integrates these theoretical, design, and ethical perspectives to establish a foundation for future human-centered research on Crowdsourced Context Systems.

1.
Introduction

Social media platforms play an important role as sites of entertainment
(
Herder and Staring 2024
)
, news consumption
(
Chouaki et al. 2024
)
, and interpersonal connection
(
Preece 1999
)
.
At the same time, these platforms must contend with problems stemming from harmful user-uploaded content, which can take the form of abuse, spam, or misinformation
(
Scheuerman et al. 2021
)
.
Specifically, platforms have tried various forms of content moderation
(
Grimmelmann 2015
;
Lampe and Resnick 2004
;
Jhaver et al. 2019
)
to deal with misinformation, including centralized fact-checking efforts to label or remove it
(
Gillespie 2018
)
, as well as distributed approaches where users are empowered to tackle these challenges themselves
(
Seering 2020
)
.
While these approaches have had some success, they have also evoked backlash and accusations of bias from both users and lawmakers, particularly on the political right
(
Jiang et al. 2020
;
Johnson 2023
)
.
Recently, a new approach has emerged: beginning with X/Twitter, major social media platforms are increasingly introducing systems that crowdsource “helpful context” to display alongside potentially problematic posts
(
Coleman 2021
)
(e.g. Figure
1
).
These systems, which we term
Crowdsourced Context Systems
(CCS), have seen growing adoption and impact, with some of the world’s largest platforms, including Meta (Facebook, Instagram, and Threads), TikTok, and YouTube all recently launching their own implementations.
Meta has gone one step further, announcing that their CCS will replace their fact-checking program in the U.S.
(
Kaplan 2025
)
.

As CCS adoption grows, these systems have the potential to reshape our information ecosystem.
Early studies into such systems have produced promising results, finding that CCS can increase user informedness and decrease the spread of misinformation
(
Wojcik et al. 2022
;
Chuai et al. 2024b
;
Drolsbach and Pröllochs 2023
)
,
although it is still unclear whether CCS can replace centralized fact-checking, as some platforms have done or may seek to do
(
Kaplan 2025
)
.
As CCS develop, novel implementation questions also come to the fore, including how to design these systems most effectively, for example, to provide reliable context quickly enough to prevent the spread of the most viral content
(
Chuai et al. 2024b
;
Chuai et al. 2024a
;
Renault et al. 2024
)
.
At this critical moment of change in the information ecosystem, it is important to understand how CCS function, how we should evaluate them, and how these systems may be better designed.

This paper makes two contributions towards the goal of better understanding CCS.
Our first contribution is a
systematic literature review
of existing research studying implementations of CCS.
Our review finds significant research into CCS contributor behavior and the impact of displaying notes.
However, the review highlights the fact that certain aspects of these systems are underexplored by existing research.
We thus set out to identify additional facets of these systems that may affect their impact.
Our second contribution is
an analysis and comparison of real-world implementations of CCS
by several major social media platforms, in order to create a framework
to guide future HCI research on these novel and important systems.
Our framework is composed of three parts.
First, we offer a theoretical model of a CCS that identifies these systems’ core characteristics and what sets them apart from prior crowdsourced systems.
Our model posits that while CCS use familiar moderation tools like crowdsourced information and context labels, they are unique in how they are natively integrated into platforms with the explicit goal of fostering user informedness.
Second, we outline a
design space

(
MacLean et al. 1996
)
for CCS in order to taxonomize the important design decisions made in their implementations.
Design spaces are a conceptual tool used in HCI research that can guide work on complex socioetechnical systems by structuring the space of design possibilities
(
Zhang et al. 2024
;
Lee et al. 2024
;
Card et al. 1990
)
.
We group these design decisions into six high-level
aspects
of CCS that combine and interact to shape their impact: participation, inputs, curation, platform treatment, and transparency.
Third, we identify key normative implications of these systems in order to make sense of the ethical stakes involved in their design.
As systems with the power to shape the information ecosystem, we emphasize three interconnected concerns:
user informedness
,
distribution of power
, and
fairness
.
We discuss the role of CCS within the broader conversation about content moderation and establish a foundation for
future human-centered research on Crowdsourced Context Systems.

2.
Background

In January 2021, Twitter (now X) launched a U.S. pilot for the first CCS, which they called
Birdwatch
, presenting it as, “a community-based approach to misinformation”
(
Coleman 2021
)
.
This system allowed a subset of users to suggest
notes
to, “provide informative context” about Tweets (posts) on the platform.
The same subset of users then rated these suggested notes and these ratings were used as the inputs to a note curation algorithm that aimed to select notes that would be helpful to a broad audience.
In this pilot, these notes were only visible on a separate site, but Twitter announced their plan, “to make notes visible directly on Tweets for the global Twitter audience, when there is consensus from a broad and diverse set of contributors.”

In March 2022, Twitter expanded the pilot, announcing that now, “a small (and randomized) group of people on Twitter in the US will see Birdwatch notes directly on some Tweets”
(
Coleman 2022
)
.
Twitter was purchased by Elon Musk in October 2022, renamed X, and soon Birdwatch was renamed
Community Notes
.
In December 2022, the pilot phase ended and users all over the world began seeing selected notes directly alongside Tweets
(
Community Notes [@CommunityNotes] 2022
)
.
Since then, the system has continued to evolve.
A key feature of X’s Community Notes is its transparency: the note curation code is open source
1
1

1

https://github.com/twitter/communitynotes/tree/main
, as are the crowdsourced notes and ratings data collected by the system
2
2

2

https://x.com/i/communitynotes/download-data
.

In June 2024, YouTube announced a pilot for a similar system, which they called
Notes

(
Team 2024
)
.
In January 2025, Meta announced a pilot for their own implementation, also called
Community Notes
, which would launch on Facebook, Instagram, and Threads
(
Kaplan 2025
)
.
Finally, TikTok announced their own pilot in April 2025, which they called
Footnotes

(
Presser [n. d.]
)
.
According to the platforms, all of these new systems are based on X’s open source implementation of the note curation algorithm.
Unlike X’s implementation, though, none of the new systems are open source or open data.
In fact, while all of these systems serve similar purposes, they differ from each other in crucial ways due to unique design decisions made by each platform.
Since these systems are increasingly prominent features of some of the most important social media platforms, it is critical to define, understand, and reason about them so the HCI community can better inform their implementation and evaluate their impact.

3.
Related Work

While CCS are a relatively new innovation, these systems relate to and touch on multiple bodies of work from the HCI literature, including: research on discourse and deliberation systems, research on fact-checking infrastructures, and research on crowdsourced responses to misinformation.
We describe these areas here; later in the paper (Section
6
) we provide a detailed review of the literature that is specifically addressing CCS.

In a democracy that values free speech, public participation in decision-making via deliberation is considered a social good
(
Small 2021
)
.
One classic concern of HCI research has thus been how to design systems that can support public deliberation among users with diverse, possibly conflicting, perspectives
(
Kriplean et al. 2012
)
.
One such approach was recently termed
bridging systems
, or systems that, “increase mutual understanding and trust across divides, creating space for productive conflict, deliberation, or cooperation”
(
Ovadya and Thorburn 2023
)
.
Such systems can be built with various goals in mind, such as finding common ground between peace-builders in armed conflict
(
Konya et al. 2025
)
, or facilitating participatory approaches to allocate public funding
(
Yang and Bachmann 2025
)
.
CCS can be viewed as highly distributed deliberation systems that aim to allow more informed public participation in social media.
Community Notes, X’s CCS implementation, is one such system.
Indeed, according to its creators, Community Notes was designed to identify crowdsourced content (
notes
), “that both inform understanding (decrease propensity to agree with a potentially misleading claim) and are seen as helpful by a diverse population of users”
(
Wojcik et al. 2022
)
.
CCS thus reflect a long-standing HCI concern with designing systems that support civic deliberation, adapting these ideas to meet the scale, speed, and additional challenges of major social media platforms.

CCS also align with another classic goal of HCI research, which is to help users assesses the veracity of information that they encounter on social media.
Emerging from journalistic practice, one approach to address this issue is to rely on external fact-checking
(
Walter et al. 2020
;
Young et al. 2018
)
, where independent organizations verify public claims and publish their determinations.
While these techniques can have positive effects, they are time-consuming and traditionally require a large amount of professional labor
(
Hassan et al. 2015
;
Juneja and Mitra 2022
)
, and thus may struggle keep up with the speed at which content is published on the internet
(
Micallef et al. 2022
)
.
As a result, researchers have explored more scalable approaches,
such as automated tools to support human fact-checkers
(
Warren et al. 2025
)
, as well as fully-automated approaches
(
Guo et al. 2022
;
Hassan et al. [n. d.]
)
.
While external fact-checks are often found on websites different from where the claim originated, such as Snopes
3
3

3

https://www.snopes.com/
or Politifact
4
4

4

https://www.politifact.com/
, social media platforms may also provide their own fact-checks, which can be presented directly alongside user-posted content
(
How [n. d.]
)
.
Studies have investigated both the most effective ways to present these fact-checks in order to shift belief
(
Clayton et al. 2020
)
and also the psychological characteristics that make some users reluctant to engage with them
(
Tanaka et al. 2023
)
.
In addition to issues of efficacy, and the large cost of running such fact-check programs, social media platforms have struggled with allegations and perceptions that their fact-checks are biased against certain political ideologies
(
Cotter et al. 2022
)
.

While fact-check systems traditionally rely on a central authority, alternative approaches to dealing with information of questionable quality make use of the distributed expertise of a crowd
(
Pennycook and Rand 2019
;
He et al. 2025
)
.
Crowd-based fact-checking is one such approach, which despite being cheaper and faster than professional fact-checking, has often been found to be similarly accurate and effective
(
Allen et al. 2021
;
Martel et al. 2024
;
Zhao and Naaman 2023
)
.
HCI research has explored the practices of those who participate in crowdbased fact-checking
(
He and He 2022
)
, their potential biases
(
Draws et al. 2022
)
, and how fact-checking tasks may be best distributed to a crowd
(
Bhuiyan et al. 2020
)
.
Beyond fact-checking, there are other crowd-based approaches to handling misinformation, such as digital juries
(
Fan and Zhang 2020
;
Zhao and Hobbs 2025
)
, distributed moderation through up/down votes
(
Lampe and Resnick 2004
;
Epstein et al. 2020
)
, and community self-governance
(
Seering 2020
)
.
CCS share much in common with these previous crowdsourced fact-checking systems, but they have a novel set of processes and structures that make CCS stand out as a new type of interactive systems.
Informed by these past bodies of research, we next define what makes these new systems unique.

4.
Generalized Model of Crowdsourced Context Systems

We coin the term
Crowdsourced Context System
to describe these systems, a novel and important addition to the content moderation toolbox.
Such systems have several core characteristics.
First, CCS rely on contributions
crowdsourced
from users of the platform.
Second, these systems are designed to specifically elicit
context
from the crowd in order to help users understand and reflect on the veracity of the original post (as opposed to other types of responses, like, say, the most humorous reply).
Third, these systems supplement user content by displaying additional text, or counterspeech
(
Peng and Grimmelmann 2024
)
, alongside the original posts.
Finally, these systems are natively integrated into social media platforms.
Unlike organic crowd behavior that emerges among users, these systems are built and operated by the platforms, giving platform designers the power to influence how the systems function.

Figure
2
shows a generalized model of a CCS.
Such systems are composed of several core entities, which we denote with
italics
through the rest of the paper.

Users
create
posts
on the platform.

Contributors
then provide context, in the form of text or media snippets called
notes
, which are associated with specific
posts
.
At first these
notes
are only visible to other
contributors
, who assess them and produce
ratings
of their helpfulness.
The platform uses these
ratings
to identify “helpful”
notes
, which are then published and displayed alongside the associated
post
everywhere that it appears on the platform, as seen in Figure
1
.
In implementing this general model, platforms make many individual design decisions, which we will address later.

5.
Methods

To better understand CCS and their design possibilities, we performed two distinct reviews.
First, we performed a systematic literature review of existing research about CCS.
Second, we performed inductive thematic analysis and affinity diagramming on a set of CCS design features extracted from documents describing the real-world CCS implementations of several major social media platforms.

In order to synthesize the research community’s understanding of Crowdsourced Context Systems, we performed a PRISMA-style systematic literature review
(
Page et al. 2021
)
.
Figure
3
shows a flow diagram summarizing our literature search and filtering process.
Our goal was to identify original research that directly studies CCS.
Since Twitter’s implementation was the first and most well-known CCS, we specifically used “Birdwatch” and “Community Notes” as keywords, as well as “Twitter” and “X”, with the expectation that any emerging systems would reference them.
We additionally restricted our search to articles that have been written since 2021, when Birdwatch first launched.
Note that since this is an emerging technology and the set of articles is still limited, we decided to include sources of knowledge that are valued in the HCI community but are sometimes excluded from literature reviews, such as pre-prints and non-full-length conference submissions.
We thus settled on the following inclusion and exclusion criteria:

Inclusion criteria:
the study is about Birdwatch or Community Notes or a similar system, either “in the wild” or in a controlled setting.

Exclusion criteria:
The article was not written in English, does not present original research, is only an abstract/a summary of other research, or is not about the functioning of a CCS.

Search Strategy

We conducted our database search in July 2025.
We searched seven databases that are well-known in the fields of HCI and social media research.
We began with six that were used in another recent HCI literature review of social media research
(
Zhang et al. 2023
)
: ACM Digital Library, IEEE Xplore, SAGE Journals, ScienceDirect (Elsevier), Taylor & Francis Online (Tandfonline), Emerald Insight.
We additionally searched arXiv in order to also include preprints, which are common in these fields.

We searched the full-text, title, abstract, and metadata of articles using the following case-insensitive query string, and limit our results to articles published in 2021 or later:

("community notes" OR "birdwatch") AND ("x" OR "twitter")

This search resulted in n=205 results.

Title Screening

We screened the search results by first considering their title.
One researcher read the title for each result and removed articles that were either duplicates or were obviously off-topic, such as those that were not about social media systems.
This resulted in n=158 articles.

Abstract Screening

One researcher read the abstracts of the remaining articles to further screen the results.
We removed redundant articles that only summarized research that was contained elsewhere in our sample, such as books and book chapters, editorials, abstracts, keynotes, and complete conference proceedings.
We also removed articles that were topically irrelevant, such as those that did not focus on CCS, or that used data from a CCS, but only to answer research questions unrelated to the system.
This resulted in n=34 articles.

Full-Text Screening

Two researchers then read the full-text of the remaining 34 articles and applied the inclusion and exclusion criteria in order to produce the final set of papers.
The researchers met during the process to compare their results and discuss any differences or challenging cases until they were in agreement.
This produced the final set of n=30 articles.

Data Extraction

Two researchers then agreed upon data to extract from each article in the final set, which included the data source, the research questions, the main findings, and any design implications mentioned.
The two researchers independently read the final set of articles and extracted this information.
They then compared values, discussed any areas of disagreement, and came to a consensus for the extracted values for each paper.

Next the two researchers independently grouped like-papers into affinity groups
(
Beyer and Holtzblatt 1999
)
based on the focus of the study.
The researchers reconvened, compared their groups, and discussed the reasons for their groupings.
Several papers were shifted between groupings based on the discussion.
Once the researchers agreed upon the groupings, they came up with labels to describe each group.

Design Feature Identification

In order to identify the design decisions that shape CCS, two authors analyzed four real-world implementations of CCS: Twitter/X’s Community Notes
5
5

5

https://x.com/i/communitynotes/
, Meta’s Community Notes
6
6

6

(which covers Facebook, Instagram, and Threads), TikTok’s Footnotes
7
7

7

https://support.TikTok.com/en/using-TikTok/exploring-videos/footnotes

8

8

Our analysis is based on public, company-written documentation and communications about the systems and their features, which we will collectively refer to as

In addition, we had access to some of the systems, which we used to complement our understanding.
Unfortunately, while we attempted to become Contributors for all of the platforms, some (TikTok, YouTube) did not grant us access.

The researchers independently read through the articles one at a time and extracted design details into a spreadsheet, which resulted in a set of “design features” for each platform.
After independently reviewing the articles for a platform, the researchers convened and performed a round of affinity diagramming

They placed “similar” design features together, and through this inductive method identified “feature groups”.
The researchers then performed thematic analysis

Braun and Clarke 2006

They repeated this process with the articles for each platform, reconvening to do another round of affinity diagramming after each round of feature extraction.
These design features and feature groups were further synthesized into the aspects, dimensions, and options of the design space, which we present below in Section

7.
A Design Space for CCS

Our analysis of the various CCS implementations revealed areas of common characteristics shared between the systems as well as points for variation.
Analyzing these features allows us to construct a
design space
of possibilities.
Within this design space, we identify six key
aspects
of CCS:

(1)

Participation
: Who uses the system and what are different users are allowed to do?

(2)

Inputs
: What shape do contributions to the system take?

(3)

Curation
: How are suggested
notes

(4)

8.
Normative Concerns

CCS are complex sociotechnical systems
(
Cherns 1976
)
that integrate people, algorithms, and speech.
These systems’ growing influence over the information shown on major social media platforms means that the design choices made in their implementation can have significant social consequences
(
Friedman and Nissenbaum 1996
;
Flanagan et al. 2008
)
.
With such high stakes, it is crucial that a human-centered research agenda consider the implications of different design choices, in order to holistically evaluate the impacts of these novel systems.

The design space that we present in Section
7
highlights some of the crucial choices that are made when designing CCS.
How are we to weigh the merits of different designs?
One approach is to identify the primary normative concerns of such a system.
We emphasize three interconnected normative concerns that should guide CCS design:
user informedness
,
distribution of power
, and
fairness
.

8.1.
User Informedness

While social media serves many different purposes, CCS are specifically concerned with social media’s ability to inform the public.
Such systems should thus be evaluated by how well they facilitate user informedness: for example, the degree to which they inform users about the accuracy of information, minimize the spread of misleading
posts
, or otherwise improve information quality on a platform.
Our literature review surfaced ample evidence that the
notes
produced by CCS do have the power to increase user informedness, both by decreasing an individual’s belief in misleading information, and by decreasing the spread of this information
(
Wojcik et al. 2022
;
Renault et al. 2024
;
Slaughter et al. 2025
;
Chuai et al. 2024a
;
Chuai et al. 2024b
;
Drolsbach and Pröllochs 2023
)
.
But while published
notes
have been shown to be effective at promoting understanding, we must also question how effective CCS are at producing these
notes.

Several of these same studies found that Twitter’s implementation of CCS produces
notes
too slowly to prevent the spread of the most viral content
(
Chuai et al. 2024b
;
Chuai et al. 2024a
;
Renault et al. 2024
)
.
There is also a significant gap in the research on the question of coverage, or how many of the misleading
posts
on a platform receive “helpful”
notes
, which is currently unanswered.
The research suggests that there is a CCS design trade-off between speed/coverage and quality/helpfulness: X’s implementation has optimized for the latter, perhaps at the expense of the former.
Whether this is the right decision should be up for debate.

For designers of CCS hoping to improve the system’s speed and coverage, there are several aspects from our design space that could be relevant.
For example, within the
curation
aspect, designers could explore bridging algorithm changes that could lead to faster identification of “helpful”
notes
.
Additionally, design changes within the
participation
and
inputs
aspects, such as those that would allow a larger
contributor
base or that would actively solicit
notes
on the most viral
posts
, could lead to a higher volume of
notes
and
ratings
, and thus quicker selection of helpful
notes
.
When it comes to the difficulty of assessing the coverage of CCS, design changes within the
transparency
aspect could make this easier to study, perhaps by providing easier access to
post
engagement metrics.

8.2.
Distribution of Power

One of the selling points of CCS is that they shift power over content moderation from the platform to the community.
Giving
users
more of a say over the content that they see on their platform can make moderation decisions more legitimate and trusted
(
Shusas and Forte 2024
;
Liu et al. 2023
;
Drolsbach et al. 2024
)
, which is an admirable goal.
Yet such claims require further scrutiny, and CCS should be evaluated to the extent that they actually empower
users
and do not merely allow platforms to shirk responsibility for misleading content.
Evidence from our literature review suggests that bridging-based algorithms, by design, fail to produce
notes
for the most polarizing content
(
Bouchaud and Ramaciotti 2025
)
.
For example, X’s implementation of the bridging algorithm requires polarized
contributors
to agree that a
note
is helpful in order to publish it.
The editorial function of the bridging algorithm can thus limit the type and scope of contributions in two important ways: in terms of framing, the algorithm may prefer centrist points of view and content that could be agreeable to both sides of an issue; in terms of selection, agreement on a
note
may not occur for contentious topics, preventing its publication.
Whether or not
users
at the ends of the ideological spectrum should be empowered to make moderation decisions is a valid question, but it is uncontroversial that the most polarizing content needs moderation, too.
Research has found evidence of harmful content being handled poorly by the system, either because potential
notes
never reach helpful status
(
Chujyo et al. 2025
)
, or because
notes
that do are not actually helpful for certain types of content, such as disinformation that is couched in humor
(
Matamoros-Fernández and Jude 2025
)
.
Zooming out, the low publishing rate for suggested
notes
(around 10% on X
(
Wirtschafter and Majumder 2023
)
and 6% on Meta
10
10

10

https://x.com/guyro/status/1965800300557857053
) gives us pause that the system is actually empowering these
note
authors.
A related, underexplored area for research involves studying which
note contributors
are empowered by CCS.
This research area is difficult to study holistically, as these systems are pseudonymous, but some work has begun to explore it qualitatively
(
Yoon et al. 2025
)
and via partnerships with platforms
(
Allen et al. 2022
)
.
More research is needed to understand if CCS empower
users
of certain demographics or political views over others.

Our design space offers several ways to further empower
users
via CCS.
First and foremost is innovation in the
participation
aspect that would increase the set and diversity of
contributors
.
The challenge for the platforms will be ensuing that quality remains as the participating
contributor
base increases, which is a tension that has been thoroughly studied in the crowdsourcing literature
(
Daniel et al. 2018
)
, for example.
Innovation in the
inputs
aspect may increase the quality of
notes
across the board, perhaps by giving
note
authors helpful feedback
(
Mohammadi and Yasseri 2025
)
, empowering more
users
to make successful contributions to the system.
Another route to shift power to
users
is to disempower the platform.
Though CCS are often described as ways to reduce platforms’ centrality in content moderation, platforms still exert power over the system in important ways, such as by deciding who is admitted as a
contributor
.
Platforms could further guarantee their neutrality through changes to the
transparency
aspect, such as by making their
contributor
selection process public.
But considering that X’s CCS implementation is the only even partially open system that we examined, a first step is for other platforms to follow X’s lead and open source their
note
curation algorithm and the crowdsourced data that they collect.

8.3.
Fairness

CCS are often touted for their potential to implement content moderation in an unbiased way.
Yet these systems are not inherently free from bias.
Results from our literature review detail bias in
contributor
behavior, in terms of the types of accounts that they leave
notes
on, as well as how they rate suggested
notes

(
Allen et al. 2022
)
.
Bridging algorithms have helped mitigate bias in the
note
curation process, with the caveats noted above, but additional bias can still be introduced—for example, in the
note
creation process, or in the selection of
contributors
.
To give a concrete example: X’s bridging algorithm finds agreement among
contributors
,
not
the entire
user
base.
If
contributors
’ beliefs are not representative of
users
’ beliefs then the algorithm’s outcomes may be a biased representation of the community, and not necessarily have broad appeal among
users
.
There is some evidence that so far X’s implementation has not suffered from this problem, but it is worth considering.
Also, accusations of bias in top-down moderation are often due to the opacity of such approaches and may be mitigated by transparency efforts.
Only X’s implementation has provided
any
transparency, and yet the lack of transparency about the
contributor
base has made analyzing them for bias impossible.
Finally, the approach of displaying context rather than removing
posts
is arguably fairer in that it lessens the damage from false positives, by empowering
users
to come to their own conclusions about content’s veracity
(
Peng and Grimmelmann 2024
)
.
However, there is a trade off: leaving up questionable content, even with helpful context, can potentially do harm to those targeted by the content.

Several aspects from our design space can influence the fairness properties of a CCS.
First off, all platforms have taken the approach that it is fairer not to remove misleading content.
However designers may explore other options from the
platform treatment
dimension to identify certain cases where the platform should take action on content flagged by the crowd as misleading.
Second, current implementations tend to display a single, definitive
note
, even if there may be several that are determined to be “helpful”.
The
presentation
aspect offers alternatives that could further the aim of allowing users to come to their own conclusions, such as by showing multiple
notes
when there are multiple with helpful
ratings
.
Additionally showing information about the
note
author could be helpful, as it could help
users
identify the credibility of the source.
This may be at odds with
contributor
privacy, which current implementations have opted to protect.
Finally, innovations in the
curation
aspect could explore other methods of selecting
notes
to display that may be regarded as fairer.
Current implementations of the bridging algorithm use past
ratings
to identify
contributors
with diverse perspectives: perhaps it would be fairer to instead use in-app behavior?
This could provide a fuller picture of a
contributor’s
ideological leanings.
Encouraging more
transparency
would also give assurances of fairness, both in how
contributors
are selected, and how
notes
are published.

9.
Discussion

Through our systematic literature review and analysis of real-world CSS
implementations, we have produced a theoretical framework that can help the HCI research community better reason about, and design, CCS.
These systems were created with the stated goal of addressing the misinformation problem
(
Coleman 2021
)
, but it is unclear if the platforms implementing them are serious about this aim.
For example, many see Meta’s choice to replace their U.S. fact-checking program with a CCS
(
Kaplan 2025
)
as a thinly-veiled effort to curry political favor with a new presidential administration that is critical of fact-checking
(
Nix et al. 2025
)
.
In fact, from a platform’s perspective, CCS have other appealing properties: they cost less than fleets of fact-checkers, they shield platforms from accusations of biased policy enforcement, and they offload the difficult responsibility of distinguishing fact from fiction onto
users
.
It may be expedient for platforms to build CCS in the current moment, but it is unclear if such systems will survive if the political climate shifts.
Still, as long as CCS are in a position to exert such influence over the information ecosystem, it is important for the HCI research community to study them.
Most importantly, we emphasize the need to better understand how CCS perform compared to other content moderation systems.
CCS may perform well in certain cases, but if on the whole they perform worse than the systems they are replacing, for example on the question of coverage, they should be used carefully.

While it has been suggested that they can, CCS cannot effectively replace a fuller spectrum of content moderation tools, and were not designed to do so
(
Elliott [n. d.]
)
.
Content moderation is a broad term that encompasses a variety of different goals, including preventing crimes, mitigating lawful but awful behavior, and promoting high quality content.
The tools used as a part of content moderation are similarly varied and include content take-downs, labeling, and account bans.
Systems that provide context are much narrower in the scope of their goal and mechanism: they aim to better inform
users
by providing them with additional information.
And yet, the empirical results summarized in this paper demonstrate that CCS may play a meaningful role in the content moderation ecosystem, and it is worth exploring how they may integrate with other parts of it.
For example, the research that we reviewed found that notes often build on the work done by professional fact-checkers
(
Borenstein et al. 2025
)
.
Might this relationship flow the other way, as well?
Perhaps data from CCS systems could be used by professional fact-checkers to cover a wider range of issues.
Or, the ability of CCS systems to identify
notes
with broad appeal could help professionals craft their own fact-checks that do the same.

Our design space reveals several ways forward for a HCI research agenda on CCS.
Several aspects are underexplored by current research, particularly the
Participation
aspect.
Encouraging positive participation in collaborative computing systems is a classic topic in HCI research
(
Kraut and Resnick 2012
)
and one in which the community could make strong contributions.
One dimension of participation which has not yet been explored is how platforms may play a more active role in soliciting
notes
and
ratings
from
contributors
.
Past HCI work on how to best make use of the various skills of crowds may be instructive here
(
Bernstein et al. 2015
;
Kittur et al. 2013
)
.
It is worth noting that the more consequential these systems become, the more they will be targets for negative forms of participation as well, primarily gaming
(
Daniel et al. 2018
;
Epstein et al. 2020
)
.
Bridging algorithms are designed to be resistant to certain types of gaming
(
Wojcik et al. 2022
)
, but vulnerabilities may emerge.
One other area for study is the
Presentation
aspect, specifically how different approaches to visually displaying
notes
may affect
user
understanding of misleading claims
(
Gamage et al. 2025
;
Martel and Rand 2023
)
.
Another promising area is the
Inputs
aspect, where recent work has begun to explore the ways in which LLMs can supplement human effort and increase the quality and quantity of
notes

(
De et al. 2025
;
Mohammadi and Yasseri 2025
)
.
Of particular interest is X’s recent decision to allow AI
note contributors
via a new API
(
Li et al. 2025
)
.
How such a decision affects the quantity and quality of
notes
on the platform should be a near-term focus of HCI research.
Of course, studying how changes to CCS impact information dynamics across a platform will be difficult to study without more innovations in the
Transparency
aspect.
X’s approach to transparency in their Community Notes system has been a boon for for studying CCS.
Hopefully other platforms will soon adopt similar approaches to support ongoing studies of CCS effectiveness.

10.
Conclusion

Crowdsourced Context Systems are an increasingly prevalent part of the content moderation strategies of many prominent platforms.
To understand these systems, their impacts, and how they may be better designed, we conducted a systematic literature review of existing research studying implementations of CCS and analyzed several major social media platforms’ implementations of such systems.
Despite recent trends towards the adoption of these systems, we emphasize that they are only one piece of a larger content moderation suite, and suggest against using them as drop-in replacements for traditional fact-checking approaches.
The framework we produced can be used to reason about these systems, and consists of a theoretical model, a design space, and several key normative concerns.
Such systems have potential to address the challenge of misinformation, but to realize this potential, future design research from the HCI community must focus on ways to understand and improve these systems’ performance.
