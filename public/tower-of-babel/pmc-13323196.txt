Comparison of short-term outcomes among hand-sewn, stapled, and two-stage Turnbull–Cutait pull-through anastomoses in TaTME for low rectal cancer
Authors: Q Guo, G Chen, Q Teng, W Huang, L Mao, D Bai, Y Xie, D Zhou, M Ren, Y Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough transanal total mesorectal excision (TaTME) has been recognized as an important surgical method for low rectal cancer, optimal anastomotic techniques remain debated. This study compared short-term outcomes of hand-sewn coloanal anastomosis (CAA), single-stapled anastomosis (SSA), and two-stage Turnbull–Cutait pull-through coloanal anastomosis (TCA).MethodsA retrospective analysis of 147 patients who underwent TaTME for low rectal cancer (tumor distal margin ≤ 5 cm from anal verge) was conducted between July 2020 and July 2023. The patients were categorized into CAA, SSA, and TCA groups based on the anastomosis. The primary endpoint was anastomosis-related complications that included anastomotic leakage, anastomotic bleeding, and anastomotic stricture. The secondary endpoints included neorectal prolapse, pathological outcomes, perioperative outcomes, stoma-related outcomes, and functional outcomes assessed through the low anterior resection syndrome (LARS) scoring system.ResultsAmong 147 patients with low rectal cancer, 42 patients underwent CAA, 69 patients underwent SSA, and 36 patients underwent TCA. Operative time was shortest in the SSA group and longest in the TCA group (p = 0.023). TCA demonstrated a lower protective stoma rate (p = 0.005), higher splenic flexure mobilization (SFM) rate (p = 0.014), and extended postoperative hospital stay (p < 0.001) compared to CAA and SSA. Pathologically, TCA resulted in longer length of resected bowel (p < 0.001), with similar distal resection margins and lymph node yield. The complication rates were similar among the three groups, although the TCA group had a higher incidence of neorectal prolapse (p < 0.001). LARS scores improved gradually in all groups postoperatively, with SSA potentially reducing its incidence.ConclusionIn TaTME, SSA may reduce the incidence of LARS, whereas TCA avoids the need for a stoma at the expense of longer bowel resection and an increased risk of neorectal prolapse. Patient-specific selection of the anastomotic technique remains recommended.Keywords: Transanal total mesorectal excision, Low rectal cancer, Anastomosis, Turnbull–Cutait pull-through, Complication

Introduction

Total mesorectal excision (TME) has become the standard surgical procedure for rectal cancer and has been effective in reducing local recurrence rates [1]. However, low rectal cancer remains challenging for surgeons as a result of limited surgical visibility and narrow operating spaces. Transanal total mesorectal excision (TaTME) was first introduced by Sylla in 2010, providing an innovative surgical approach for low rectal cancers [2]. Compared with conventional laparoscopic techniques, the distinctive bottom-up approach in TaTME offers enhanced operative visualization in narrow pelvic anatomy, which may lead to higher negative circumferential resection margin (CRM) rates [3].

Anastomosis for low rectal cancer remains challenging because of anatomical constraints. Existing linear staplers have difficulty achieving a perpendicular rectal transection [4], and multiple firings of linear staplers are often required to complete rectal transection, which may increase the risk of anastomotic leakage [5]. Additionally, the intersection of staple lines (dog ear) obtained after the double-stapled anastomosis technique also increases the risk of anastomotic leakage [6].

Current evidence on different anastomotic techniques in TaTME remains limited. TaTME avoids the necessity of linear staplers for rectal transection and allows completion of anastomosis with a single stapler. The single-stapled anastomosis (SSA) in TaTME is considered to have potential advantages in reducing anastomotic leakage risks [7]. Hand-sewn coloanal anastomosis (CAA), a common anastomotic technique for ultralow rectal cancer [8], has also been applied in TaTME [9]. However, surgeons routinely opt to create a protective stoma which may reduce the risk of anastomotic leakage and its related morbidity [10, 11].

The two-stage Turnbull–Cutait pull-through coloanal anastomosis (TCA), first described in 1961 [12], is considered capable of reducing the incidence of anastomotic leakage without stoma formation [13]; however, this technique has been gradually replaced by the widespread adoption of staplers [14]. With recent advancements in minimally invasive surgery, TCA has regained attention as a viable option for anastomotic reconstruction in low rectal cancer [15]. In 2021, Madbouly et al. reported the application of the TCA technique in TaTME, demonstrating complication rates comparable to primary anastomosis [16].

In rectal cancer surgery, the choice of anastomotic technique is influenced by multiple factors. Stapled anastomosis is generally preferred owing to its relative technical simplicity, shorter operative time, and reduced risk of manual error [17].

However, for patients with ultralow tumors in whom stapled anastomosis is difficult after intersphincteric resection (ISR), CAA serves as an alternative [18, 19]. TCA is generally reserved for patients assessed preoperatively as high risk for anastomotic leakage yet who decline a protective stoma [20, 21]. Given that the selection of anastomotic technique currently depends largely on surgeon preference, systematic comparative analyses of different techniques are warranted.

Patients and methods

Trial design

A retrospective collection of patient data from July 2020 to July 2023 was conducted at the Department of Gastrointestinal Surgery, Beijing Anzhen Nanchong Hospital of Capital Medical University & Nanchong Central Hospital. Most of the patients and their characteristics were obtained from a retrospective review of the prospectively maintained database Chinese taTME Registry Collaborative (CTRC). Establishment of CTRC was approved by the ethics committee of Beijing Friendship Hospital affiliated to Capital Medical University (IRB File No. 2017-P2-181-01). Our center contributed to the collaborative entry of cases into this database, and the data utilized in this study were solely entered by our center, excluding any data from other centers. This study was independently approved by our institutional ethics committee (IRB No. 2016-09).

Participants

Patients meeting all of the following criteria were eligible for inclusion in this study: (1) preoperative pathological confirmation of rectal adenocarcinoma; (2) tumor distal margin ≤ 5 cm from the anal verge (measured by MRI/proctoscopy); (3) histologically confirmed stage I–III disease; (4) completion of TaTME procedure; (5) complete perioperative records and ≥ 12-month postoperative follow-up. Patients meeting any of the following criteria were excluded from this study: (1) presence of synchronous malignant tumors; (2) history of perineal surgical interventions; (3) requirement for emergent surgical management (hemorrhage, perforation, or obstruction). According to the anastomotic approaches, patients were stratified into three groups: hand-sewn coloanal anastomosis (CAA), single-stapled anastomosis (SSA), and two-stage Turnbull–Cutait pull-through coloanal anastomosis (TCA).

The patient selection criteria were guided by a range of considerations, such as the patient’s age, patient’s concerns regarding a protective stoma or TCA technique, neoadjuvant therapy, comorbidities, surgeon preference, intraoperative assessments of anastomotic tension and blood supply. For some patients who are otherwise eligible for SSA based on tumor height alone, CAA or TCA may ultimately be performed as a result of the factors mentioned above.

Surgical procedure and anastomosis technique

All surgical procedures adhered to the principles of TME and the established protocols for TaTME [1, 2].

Hand-sewn coloanal anastomosis

End-to-end hand-sewn coloanal anastomosis was performed using 3–0 Vicryl interrupted sutures, as described in prior literature [22].

Single-stapler anastomosis

As described by Arroyave et al. [23], following transanal specimen extraction, the anvil is positioned in the proximal colon and secured with a purse-string suture before repositioning into the abdominal cavity. A guide tube is inserted into the distal rectal stump, followed by tightening of the purse-string suture. Under laparoscopic visualization, the circular stapler is advanced through the guide tube to dock with the anvil, and activation of the stapler completes the anastomosis [23].

Two-stage Turnbull–Cutait pull-through coloanal anastomosis

Following transanal specimen extraction, the colon is exteriorized 6–7 cm beyond the anal verge. After 1–2 weeks, the exteriorized colon is transected at the level of the anal canal section, followed by hand-sewn coloanal anastomosis using interrupted sutures [24].

Protective stoma

Temporary ileostomy was chosen as the type of protective stoma created. Stoma reversal was routinely scheduled 3 months after stoma creation. Anastomotic integrity was evaluated by both colonoscopy and water-soluble contrast enema prior to reversal. If anastomotic leakage or stricture was identified, stoma reversal was deferred until resolution of the condition. A permanent stoma was considered if the leakage or stricture could not be successfully managed.

Study endpoints

Primary endpoint: Anastomosis-related complications, which included anastomotic leakage, bleeding, and stricture.

Secondary endpoints: protective stoma rate, delayed reversal rate, time of stoma reversal, operative time of stoma reversal, postoperative hospital stay for stoma reversal, stoma-related complications, operative time, splenic flexure mobilization (SFM), anastomotic height, intraoperative bleeding, postoperative hospital stay, lymph node yield, length of resected bowel, length of distal resection margin. Operative time, length of resected bowel, and postoperative hospital stay were calculated as the cumulative sum of statistics from both the first and second surgical stages. All temporal measurements were standardized using video-recorded surgical timestamps.

Data collection and follow-up

Patient characteristics, surgical variables, and pathological results were recorded during the hospital stay. All patients underwent scheduled outpatient evaluations every 3 months during the first six postoperative months to assess anastomotic integrity through digital rectal examination and endoscopy. Subsequently, follow-up evaluations were scheduled at 6-month intervals. The anal functional outcomes were assessed using the low anterior resection syndrome (LARS) score [25] at intervals of 1, 6, and 12 months following the surgical procedure. For patients with a protective stoma, LARS was assessed following stoma reversal. Accordingly, the assessment was deferred for patients with delayed reversal and was omitted for those whose stomas were not reversed. Furthermore, contact information was provided to all patients for immediate communication in case of any changes in their condition. Ultimately, all data were recorded and entered into a comprehensive database.

The following criteria were used for the diagnosis of postoperative complications: (1) The diagnosis of anastomotic leakage adhered to the grading system established by the International Study Group of Rectal Cancer (ISREC) [26]. (2) Anastomotic stricture was defined as the presence of circumferential narrowing confirmed by digital rectal examination, inability to pass a 12-mm diameter endoscope, or radiographic evidence of anastomotic lumen narrowing (< 12 mm) on water-soluble contrast enema [27, 28]. (3) Anastomotic bleeding was defined as the presence of active rectal bleeding with associated hemoglobin reduction, potentially accompanied by hemodynamic instability. (4) Neorectal prolapse was defined as the abnormal protrusion of either the rectal mucosa or the full-thickness rectal wall beyond the anal verge.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables with normal distribution are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while non-normally distributed data are presented as median (range). Intergroup comparisons were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for normally distributed continuous variables, Pearson’s χ2 or Fisher’s exact test for nominal categorical variables, and Kruskal–Wallis H test for non-normally distributed continuous variables or ordinal data. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05 (two-tailed). Statistical analysis were performed using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Baseline characteristics

Between July 2020 and July 2023, a total of 147 patients with low rectal cancer were included in the study. Of these, 42 patients underwent CAA, 69 patients underwent SSA, and 36 patients underwent TCA. The median follow-up time for the entire cohort was 25 (20, 31) months. Follow-up was complete for 91.8% (135/147) of patients. No significant differences were observed in baseline characteristics, including age, sex, BMI, ASA classification, tumor distal margin from anal verge, TNM stage, and receipt of neoadjuvant therapy among the three groups (Table 1).

Intraoperative and postoperative outcomes

The TCA group had the longest operative time, which was significantly longer than that of the SSA group (306.11 ± 91.41 min vs. 259.57 ± 81.84 min, p = 0.010). No statistically significant difference was observed between the CAA group and either the TCA (p = 0.472) or SSA (p = 0.061) groups regarding operative time. Furthermore, the TCA group also had the longest postoperative hospital stay, significantly longer than both the CAA group [16 (14–18) vs. 10 (9–12) days, p < 0.001] and the SSA group [16 (14–18) vs. 11 (9–14) days, p < 0.001]. None of the patients in the TCA group received a protective stoma, a rate significantly lower than that in the CAA group (11/42, 26.2%, p = 0.001) and the SSA group (15/69, 21.7%, p = 0.003). No significant difference was found between the CAA and SSA groups in the rate of protective stoma. Finally, no significant differences were observed in intraoperative bleeding (p = 0.397) or anastomotic height (p = 0.712) among the three groups, and direct comparisons between any two groups similarly revealed no significant differences. Stoma-related outcomes

All patients with protective stomas in the CAA and SSA groups eventually underwent stoma reversal. In one patient from each group, the reversal was delayed because of an anastomotic stricture secondary to anastomotic leakage. The stricture was successfully managed with digital dilation. The patients with anastomotic stricture experienced a 4-week delay in reversal in the CAA group, and 6-week delay in the SSA group. The difference in the incidence of delay reversal was not statistically significant (p = 1.000). Furthermore, no statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups in the time of stoma reversal (p = 0.428), the operative time of stoma reversal (p = 0.431), or the postoperative hospital stay for stoma reversal (p = 0.259). The observed stoma-related complications included stomal stenosis, peristomal infection, parastomal hernia, and surgical site infection after reversal. The overall incidence of these complications was comparable between the CAA and SSA groups (p = 1.000). Detailed stoma-related outcomes are summarized in Table 3.

Pathological outcomes

The TCA group demonstrated a significantly longer total length of resected bowel compared to both the CAA group (216.0 ± 25.1 mm vs. 163.0 ± 33.1 mm, p < 0.001) and the SSA group (216.0 ± 25.1 mm vs. 154.3 ± 42.1 mm, p < 0.001). Complication outcomes

Neorectal prolapse occurred only in the TCA group, with an incidence of 13.9% (5/36), a rate significantly higher than that in both the CAA group (0%, p < 0.001) and the SSA group (0%, p < 0.001). No significant differences were observed in major complications, including anastomotic leakage (p = 0.272), bleeding (p = 0.566), and stricture (p = 0.975), among the three groups, and direct comparisons between any two groups similarly revealed no significant differences. Although the incidence of anastomotic leakage did not differ significantly among groups (p = 0.272), a numerical trend was noted: the rate was highest in SSA (5.8%, 4/69), lowest in TCA (0%, 0/36), and intermediate in CAA (2.4%, 1/42). In addition, no cases of delayed or chronic anastomotic leakage were identified in our patient cohort. Stoma-related complications were included in the overall complication analysis, revealing no statistically significant differences in overall complication rates among the three groups (p = 0.616). Low anterior resection syndrome

No statistically significant differences were observed in the incidence of LARS among the three groups at any time point (1 month, p = 0.347; 6 months, p = 0.305; 12 months, p = 0.441), with all groups showing a decreasing trend over time. However, a notable clinical trend was observed: at all follow-up time points, the SSA group consistently showed the highest proportion of patients reporting “no LARS” (1 month: 3/69, 3.0%; 6 months: 4/69, 5.8%; 12 months: 12/69, 17.4%) when compared with that in the CAA group (1 month: 1/42, 2.4%; 6 months: 2/42, 4.8%; 12 months: 5/42, 11.9%) and TCA group (1 month: 0/36, 0.0%; 6 months: 1/36, 2.8%; 12 months: 4/36, 11.1%). The SSA group also showed the lowest proportion of patients with “major LARS” (1 month: 46/69, 66.7%; 6 months: 36/69, 52.2%; 12 months: 32/69, 46.4%) when compared with that in the CAA group (1 month: 30/42, 71.4%; 6 months: 26/42, 61.9%; 12 months: 22/42, 52.4%) and TCA group (1 month: 28/36, 77.8%; 6 months: 24/36, 66.7%; 12 months: 21/36, 58.3%).

A stratified analysis based on the presence or absence of a protective stoma was performed to explore LARS further. In the subgroup of patients with a protective stoma (n = 26), no significant differences were found between CAA and SSA at any time point (1 month, p = 0.799; 6 months, p = 0.540; 12 months, p = 0.760). In the other subgroup without a stoma (n = 121), comparisons among CAA, SSA, and TCA again revealed no statistical significance (1 month, p = 0.574; 6 months, p = 0.529; 12 months, p = 0.681). The clinically superior trend for SSA observed in the primary analysis was preserved in this stratum. Discussion

This study compared three anastomotic techniques (CAA, SSA, and TCA) in patients undergoing TaTME for low rectal cancer. Key findings included (1) comparable rates of anastomotic leakage, bleeding, and stricture; (2) avoidance of a protective stoma with TCA; (3) a higher incidence of neorectal prolapse in the TCA group; (4) longer operative time, postoperative stay, and bowel resection with TCA; (5) SSA may reduce the incidence of LARS.

In this study, no significant differences in anastomotic leakage rates were observed among the three anastomotic techniques, aligning with previous research: a Cochrane database analysis showed comparable leakage rates between stapled and hand-sewn techniques [29], and an randomized controlled trial demonstrated equivalent leakage incidence between hand-sewn anastomosis and TCA [24]. Current evidence suggests that anastomotic technique selection does not significantly affect leakage rates in TaTME. It is noteworthy that no anastomotic leakage occurred in the TCA group. This observation may be explained by the mechanism proposed by Biondo et al., who suggested that adhesions formed between the colonic serosa, pelvic tissues, and anal canal during the interval between stages in TCA could reduce leakage risk, functioning similarly to a diverting ileostomy [24]. Patients with chronic anastomotic leakage, anastomotic stricture, or local recurrence may require resection of the affected bowel segment and re-anastomosis [25]. In this study, the bowel resection length in the TCA group was significantly longer than that in the CAA and SSA groups, which may result in insufficient remaining colon length, thereby increasing the difficulty of re-anastomosis or even rendering it unfeasible.

Anastomotic stricture is a serious complication following rectal cancer surgery, second only to anastomotic leakage [26]. It may result in symptoms such as fecal urgency, intestinal obstruction, fecal incontinence, and difficult defecation [27–29]. Moreover, it may increase the risk of permanent stoma, thereby severely compromising the patient’s quality of life [30, 31]. This study revealed an overall anastomotic stricture rate of 7.4%, which is consistent with the previously reported range of 3.6–21.1% in the literature [29, 32]. However, there are notable differences in the incidence rates reported across different studies. Cong et al. suggested that such variation comes from inconsistent definitions and diagnostic criteria for anastomotic stricture among studies, and proposed that establishing a standardized definition and grading system could help address this issue [33]. Meanwhile, Hu et al. indicated that postoperative anastomotic stricture is associated with protective stoma and anastomotic leakage. The underlying mechanism may involve the lack of fecal dilation and peristaltic stimulation at the anastomotic site due to a protective stoma, as well as the severe inflammatory response and scar formation caused by anastomotic leakage [34].

The 13.9% rate of neorectal prolapse observed in the TCA cohort warrants particular attention. Postoperative neorectal prolapse following rectal cancer surgery remains sparsely documented, with literature-reported incidence ranging from 1.3% to 8% [35–39]. Regarding the reasons for neorectal prolapse after rectal cancer surgery, Guraieb-Trueba et al. proposed that in order to preserve the sphincter and perform coloanal anastomosis for low and ultralow rectal cancers, complete mobilization of the descending colon or even splenic flexure is often necessary, resulting in excessive mobility of the neorectum, which could be related to neorectal prolapse [39], a mechanism substantiated by the 58.3% SFM rate in our TCA group. Furthermore, intraoperative length assessment during the second-stage procedure proved challenging because of transient postoperative colonic edema and rigidity. Subsequent resolution of inflammatory changes may result in unanticipated redundancy and eventual mucosal prolapse. These findings emphasize the critical need for intraoperative precision in determining distal colonic resection margins.

In our study, the distal resection margin lengths were comparable among the three groups, which can be attributed to the ability of TaTME to achieve precise transection at the predetermined level distal to the tumor [2, 40]. This indicates that the choice of anastomotic technique does not compromise the distal resection margin, thereby ensuring consistent oncological outcomes while alleviating surgeons’ concerns regarding oncological safety when selecting anastomotic techniques.

The role of a protective stoma in preventing anastomotic leakage remains debated, though consensus holds that it mitigates leakage severity and associated morbidity [41, 42]. However, 17.4–24% of stomas become permanent because of complications or disease progression [25, 30, 43], and temporary stomas may impair renal and bowel function [44, 45]. In our study, although no patients required a permanent stoma, stoma-related complications occurred in 4.8% and 4.3% of patients in the CAA and SSA groups, respectively. In contrast, the absence of a protective stoma in the TCA group precluded such complications. The optimal timing for stoma reversal remains controversial. Some argue that early reversal (within 2 weeks) after confirming the absence of anastomotic leakage can reduce patient burden and may prevent colonic atrophy‑related dysfunction [46–48]. Others contend that delayed reversal does not impair bowel function, whereas early reversal might increase the risk of anastomotic leakage [49, 50]. In our study, stoma reversal was routinely performed at 3 months postoperatively, except in two patients in whom reversal was delayed because of anastomotic stricture. Although the TCA group had the longest operative time and postoperative hospital stay, overall complication rates were comparable among the three groups. Notably, the second-stage procedure in the TCA group was completed within 1–2 weeks, resulting in a shorter overall treatment duration than in the SSA and CAA groups.

The requirement for routine SFM during rectal cancer surgical procedures is still a subject of debate. Proponents argue that SFM is essential for reducing anastomotic tension and ensuring adequate vascular supply at the anastomotic site [51, 52]. Conversely, opponents contend that this technique demonstrates no oncological or complication-related superiority while simultaneously prolonging operative time and carrying potential risks of injuries to spleen parenchyma, colonic wall integrity, and mesenteric vasculature [53, 54]. In our study, SFM was selectively performed on the basis of intraoperative assessment of anastomotic tension dynamics and vascular perfusion. Notably, the TCA group demonstrated significantly higher SFM rates compared to CAA and SSA groups. This suggests that the high rate of SFM required by the TCA procedure to achieve tension-free colonic exteriorization may increase surgical risks and procedural complexity.

This study demonstrated no statistically significant differences in LARS incidence among the three anastomotic techniques across all follow-up time points. Madbouly et al. compared functional outcomes between TCA and SSA in TaTME and reported comparable Wexner incontinence scores [16]. Similarly, Kitaguchi et al. found no significant difference in the prevalence of major LARS at 1 year postoperatively between TCA and CAA [55]. These collective findings suggest that functional outcomes following TaTME may be independent of the specific anastomotic technique employed. Notably, we observed that the SSA group consistently showed the highest proportion of minor LARS and the lowest proportion of major LARS. This trend may be related to the slightly higher anastomotic height in the SSA group compared to the CAA and TCA groups. However, no statistically significant difference was observed in anastomotic height among the three groups, indicating that the choice of anastomotic technique did not influence anastomotic height. Given that anastomotic height is closely associated with both the incidence of anastomotic leakage and the severity of LARS [56, 57], the comparable rates of these complications across groups further support the finding. This alleviates surgeons’ concern about height-related complications when selecting an anastomotic technique.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, it is important to note that the present study was not designed as a randomized controlled trial. As a retrospective analysis, it is inherently subject to potential selection bias and unmeasured confounding, despite efforts to adjust for known variables. Second, this study did not perform a sample size calculation and was limited by a small cohort, resulting in insufficient statistical power that may have hindered the detection of subtle differences in complication rates. Third, the follow-up period is insufficient for evaluating long-term oncological and functional outcomes.

Conclusion

In TaTME, SSA may reduce the incidence of LARS, whereas TCA avoids the need for a stoma at the expense of longer bowel resection and an increased risk of neorectal prolapse. Patient-specific selection of the anastomotic technique remains recommended.
