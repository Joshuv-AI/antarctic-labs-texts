Changing Patterns of Specialty Care for Diabetic Foot Ulcers Among Rural and Urban Veterans Following the VA MISSION Act
Authors: Hiroyuki Suzuki, Mary Vaughan‐Sarrazin, Michael Ohl, Bradley Mecham, Kimberly McCoy, Daniel J Livorsi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe Veterans Affairs (VA) Maintaining Internal Systems and Strengthening Integrated Outside Networks (MISSION) Act expanded eligibility for community‐based care particularly for Veterans facing geographic or capacity‐related barriers. Patients with diabetic foot ulcers (DFU) require timely specialty management and are vulnerable to fragmented care, yet little is known about how the MISSION Act reshaped patterns of DFU‐related specialty care within and outside the VA. We conducted a retrospective cohort study of Veterans aged 65 or older with a new DFU diagnosis in VA between 2016 and 2022. We identified DFU‐related specialty encounters (podiatry, vascular surgery, infectious diseases and endocrinology) and categorised community encounters as VA‐paid or Medicare‐paid. We assessed annual trends in community specialty care and changes in the proportion and payer type of care before and after the MISSION Act. Among 76 398 patients, VA‐paid community specialty care increased substantially following the MISSION Act, with larger relative growth among rural Veterans (rural: +119%; urban: +96%). In contrast, Medicare‐paid DFU specialty care declined during the same period (rural: −12%; urban: −13%). Following the MISSION Act, reliance on VA‐paid community specialty care increased markedly for Veterans with DFU, particularly in rural areas, while reliance on Medicare‐paid care decreased.Keywords: cost shift, diabetic foot ulcer, disconnected care, MISSION act, veterans affairs

Introduction

Diabetic foot ulcer (DFU) is a common and serious complication of diabetes mellitus, often necessitating timely and specialised medical and surgical interventions to prevent severe outcomes, such as major lower limb amputation (LLA). The availability and accessibility of specialty care, such as podiatry, vascular surgery, infectious diseases (ID) and endocrinology, are critical aspects of DFU management and impact both quality of care and outcomes.

The Veterans Affairs (VA) healthcare system is the largest integrated provider in the United States, serving over nine million enrolled Veterans [1]. Although VA has improved geographic access to primary care services by building over 900 outpatient clinics since 2010 [2], specialty care remains concentrated within large, predominantly urban medical centres. As a result, rural and those living far from VA specialty clinics frequently obtain specialty care from non‐VA providers through Medicare, Medicaid, private insurance or VA‐paid community care (VACC).

The Maintaining Internal Systems and Strengthening Integrated Outside Networks (MISSION) Act, implemented in 2019, expanded eligibility for VACC and most rural Veterans became eligible for specialty care at a community healthcare system based on several criteria including the distance from their residence to VA medical centres, wait times for care and the availability of specific services [3]. While expanded community care can has a potential to improve access, it may also fragment care between VA and community healthcare systems, thereby creating care coordination challenges [4].

Conditions like DFU, which require timely, multidisciplinary specialty management, are particularly sensitive to such changes in care delivery. Rural Veterans face significant barriers to timely specialty care within VA and may suffer greater consequence after major LLA because homes in rural areas tend to be less accessible and there is less public transit. In fact, rural populations have more major LLA or mortality from DFU than urban populations [5]. Changes in care patterns associated with the MISSION Act therefore have potentially large impacts on the accessibility of care and outcomes for rural Veterans with DFU. However, little is known about how the MISSION Act reshaped patterns of DFU‐related specialty care within and outside the VA.

This study aimed to fill this gap by investigating the trends and patterns of community specialty care via VACC and Medicare among rural and urban Veterans with DFU during the period from 2016 to 2022.

Materials and Methods

This is a retrospective cohort study of Veterans aged 65 or older with a new diagnosis of DFU in the VA and received DFU‐related specialty care (podiatry, vascular surgery, ID or endocrinology) between 2016 and 2022. We focused on VA enrollees age 65 and older because most (> 90%) are enrolled in Medicare [6, 7]. We obtained patient‐level data from the VA Corporate Data Warehouse (CDW) and linked Medicare data. We limited the cohorts to Veterans who received any care at a VA facility within 2 years before their DFU diagnosis so that we include only those who received medical services within VA before their diagnosis.

The diagnosis of DFU was made using International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision (ICD‐10) diagnostic codes for DFU used in our previous study [8]. We excluded Veterans who had either ICD‐9 or ICD‐10 codes for DFU in 2015 to make sure that only new DFU diagnoses in the study period were included in this study. Rurality was determined using the rurality of patients' residential address defined by Rural–Urban Commuting Area Codes and dichotomised as rural (rural or highly rural) or urban [9]. We also collected patient‐level variables at the time of DFU diagnosis, such as patient demographics (age, sex, race and ethnicity), Social Vulnerability Index (SVI), drive time to nearest VA tertiary hospital and VA facility complexity level. The SVI was sourced from the Centres for Disease Control and Prevention website and assigned based on residential census tracts [10]. The drive time from the Veteran's residence to the closest VA tertiary hospital was calculated using data from the Planning Systems and Support Group files. The drive time to nearest VA tertiary care was dichotomised into 0–60 min or > 60 min because the VA MISSION Act enables all Veterans with mean drive times to VA specialty care greater than 60 min to receive that care at a community healthcare system [11]. VA facility complexity level was obtained from the VA Office of Productivity, Efficiency and Staffing homepage.

Community specialty care was defined as receiving podiatry, vascular surgery, ID or endocrinology inpatient or outpatient encounters in a community healthcare system from 3 months before to 1 year after a diagnosis of DFU. These four specialties were selected because they play central roles in the key components of multidisciplinary DFU management: wound care and offloading, treatment of peripheral artery disease (PAD), management of infection and diabetes control [12]. The 3‐month look‐back period prior to the DFU diagnosis was included to identify patients who were already under specialist care before DFU developed, such as those followed by podiatrists for high‐risk foot conditions or by vascular surgeons for PAD. DFU‐related specialty care at a community healthcare system were identified through the VA CDW (consultation tables, Program Integrity Tools Data and Integrated Veteran Care Consolidated Data), Medicare Fee‐for‐Service Claims and Medicare Advantage Encounters data. Community care was further classified as VA‐paid care through VACC programme or Medicare‐paid care. If the date of DFU diagnosis was on or before 6/5/2019, the patient was categorised as pre‐MISSION Act group. Otherwise, the patient was categorised as post‐MISSION Act group.

The yearly trends of community specialty care (via VACC or Medicare) stratified by rurality were described. Characteristics of Veterans were summarised as frequencies and percentages for the overall cohort and subgroups based on rurality. Comparisons of variables between rural Veterans and urban Veterans were conducted using the Chi‐square test. Additionally, the changes in the proportion of type and coverage (VA‐based, VACC or Medicare‐paid community care) for each specialty care before and after the VA MISSION Act were assessed. All analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC). This study was approved by the institutional review boards. Informed consent was waived.

Results

There were 76 398 patients aged 65 years or older with a new diagnosis of DFU who received DFU‐related specialty care between 2016 and 2022. Table 1 describes the characteristics of Veterans stratified by rurality. Rural Veterans accounted for 34.3% of the cohort. The mean age was 73 (standard deviation: 7.3). White Veterans accounted for 78.5% in the total cohort, and the proportion was significantly higher among rural Veterans (Rural: 87.9%, Urban: 73.5%). Similarly, the proportion of non‐Hispanic Veterans was significantly higher among rural Veterans (Rural: 96.1%, Urban: 91.8%). Rural Veterans had smaller proportion of patients categorised in very high or high SVI groups compared to urban Veterans. Additionally, rural Veterans were more likely to reside > 60 min from the nearest VHA tertiary care hospital and to be cared for in a less complex VHA facility.

The proportion was significantly higher among rural Veterans (33.1%) compared to urban Veterans (26.9%) because of the higher proportion with community specialty care through VACC among rural Veterans (Rural: 18.6%, Urban: 11.1%). Over time, specialty care delivered both within VA and through Medicare declined, whereas community specialty care provided through VACC increased except 2020, when visits across all settings declined, likely due to the COVID‐19 outbreak. Discussion

In this VHA nationwide retrospective cohort study of elderly Veterans with a new diagnosis of DFU, there was an increasing trend in VA‐paid community specialty care, especially among rural Veterans. The trend was consistent among four DFU‐related specialties. In contrast, Medicare‐paid community specialty care declined over time.

The increase in VACC and decline in Medicare‐paid community care were similar to previous studies about emergency care [13] or surgical care [14] before and after the MISSION Act implementation. A greater increase in VACC among rural Veterans is expected because eligibility for community specialty care under the MISSION Act often stems from having a drive time exceeding 60 min to the nearest VA medical centre which is a circumstance more common among rural Veterans. Although most patients in our cohort were eligible to obtain community specialty care through either VACC or Medicare, the patterns of community care utilisation differed markedly between the two programmes. While we were not able to determine the exact reason why VACC increased while Medicare use did not, it is possible that those who have a choice may have preferred to receive VACC because VA enrollees generally face lower copayments through VA than through Medicare [15].

The changes in the coverage of community specialty care were consistent among four DFU‐related specialties, but there may be different implications across specialties. For podiatry care, which all patients with DFU should ideally receive, there seemed to be a shift from VA‐based care to VACC. For other specialties that only some DFU patients need to receive, namely ID, vascular surgery and endocrinology, there was an increase in VACC without a decline in VA‐based care. This may reflect an increasing complexity of DFU patients and the need for specialty care. In addition, the MISSION Act may have been successful in expanding access to necessary DFU specialty care for rural Veterans who may not have timely access to these specialties within the VA system. While specialty care tended to shift to community care, VA enrollees frequently retain their relationship with outpatient primary care within the VA system [15]. Therefore, increased community care for DFU‐related specialty care raises concerns about disconnected care [4], as complicated chronic diseases like DFU require care coordination not only between primary care physicians and specialists, but also between different specialists. Future studies will need to evaluate whether the outcomes of DFU have changed in this era of increased community specialty care.

Our study has a few limitations. First, the index date for our study was the date of DFU diagnosis but not the date specialty care was provided. Therefore, the care of some patients in the pre‐MISSION Act group may have happened after the MISSION Act was implemented. However, we do not think the effect was huge because our study period was long enough (about 3 years before and after the MISSION Act). Second, while our study captured DFU‐related community specialty care at a community care hospital paid by VA or Medicare, we could not capture care paid for by Medicaid or private insurance. However, we believe the number of Veterans utilising Medicaid or private insurance was small because most patients in our cohort are enrolled in Medicare [7]. Third, although we focused on the four specialties central to multidisciplinary DFU care, we did not capture involvement from other relevant specialties such as wound care, orthopaedics or interventional cardiology. As a result, our estimates of specialty care utilisation may underestimate the true extent of specialty involvement in DFU management. Fourth, we were unable to determine the specific reasons for each specialty consultation without detailed chart reviews. Some consultations may have been requested for conditions unrelated to DFU. However, because our cohort was restricted to patients with a new DFU diagnosis, we believe that most consultations were likely related to DFU care.

In conclusion, there was a notable increase in VA‐paid community specialty care for elderly patients with a new diagnosis of DFU after implementation of the VA MISSION Act, particularly among rural Veterans. This trend was consistent across all DFU‐related specialties and coincided with a decline in Medicare‐paid community case use. These findings may suggest that the MISSION Act successfully expanded access to specialty care for rural Veterans, though future research should examine its impact on care coordination and clinical outcomes.
