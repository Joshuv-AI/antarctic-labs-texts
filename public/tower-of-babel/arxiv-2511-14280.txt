A graph-informed regret metric for optimal distributed control
Daniele Martinelli, Andrea Martin, Giancarlo Ferrari-Trecate, Luca Furieri

Abstract
We consider the optimal control of large-scale systems using distributed controllers with a network topology that mirrors the coupling graph between subsystems. In this work, we introduce spatial regret , a graph-informed metric that measures the worst-case performance gap between a distributed controller and an oracle which is assumed to have access to additional sensor information. The oracle’s graph is a user-specified augmentation of the available information graph, resulting in a benchmark policy that highlights disturbances for which additional sensor information would significantly improve performance. Minimizing spatial regret yields distributed controllers—respecting the nominal information graph—that emulate the oracle’s response to disturbances that are characteristic of large-scale networks, such as localized perturbations. We show that minimizing spatial regret admits a convex reformulation as an infinite program with a finite-dimensional approximation. To scale to large networks, we derive a computable upper bound on the spatial regret metric whose minimization problem can be solved in a distributed way. Numerical experiments on power-system models demonstrate that the resulting controllers mitigate localized disturbances more effectively than controllers optimized using classical metrics.



I
Introduction

With the increasing scale and complexity of modern engineering systems, the traditional centralized control paradigm can become ineffective due to vulnerabilities such as single points of failure, high computational burden, and limited communication bandwidth
[
1
]
.
For these reasons, over the past few decades, significant effort has been devoted to the design of distributed control architectures, where local controllers are assigned to different subsystems and coordinate their actions through limited communication.
Typical applications include power systems
[
2
]
and cooperative robotics
[
3
]
.

When using linear output-feedback controllers, complying with the communication network topology imposes structural constraints on the controller transfer function matrix in terms of sparsity patterns and fixed communication delays. Designing optimal distributed controllers under such structural constraints is notoriously challenging, as highlighted by Witsenhausen’s counterexample
[
4
]
.
A landmark contribution was given in
[
5
]
, where the authors proposed the Quadratic Invariance (QI), a sufficient
[
5
]
and necessary
[
6
]
condition for synthesizing sparse controllers that minimize closed-loop norms for linear systems.
More recently, it has been shown that QI is always satisfied for network controllers that share the same communication graph as the plant
[
7
,
8
,
9
]
, while for sparsity patterns that do not satisfy QI, convex approximation methods have been developed
[
10
,
11
]
.

Beyond parameterizing distributed policies, a fundamental question in optimal control is how to evaluate the performance of a controller in the presence of disturbances.
Classical control methods rely on closed-loop norms such as
\H 2
and
\Hinfty
, which embed specific assumptions about disturbance characteristics. The
\H 2
norm assumes disturbances are generated by stochastic processes, whereas the
\Hinfty
norm considers worst-case bounded-energy disturbances
[
12
]
.
However, when real disturbances deviate from these assumptions, such metrics may fail to provide meaningful performance characterization.

This limitation is particularly pronounced in distributed control, where structural constraints on the policy further limit the ability to reject disturbances optimally.
Hence, there is a need for alternative performance metrics that aim to quantify the fundamental trade-off between information availability and control performance.
A natural way to proceed toward this goal is to assess how much improvement could be achieved if additional sensing or communication capabilities were available to the distributed controller.
Recent approaches have focused on synthesizing distributed controllers that approximate centralized policies. For example,
[
13
,
14
]
employ neural networks trained to imitate centralized controllers, while
[
15
]
establishes conditions under which decentralized controllers can accurately approximate centralized ones for given initial conditions.
However, it is often infeasible to closely approximate the performance of a fully centralized controller.

Contributions:

To inform classical performance metrics for distributed control with the ideal behavior reachable by a more informed benchmark, we propose
spatial regret
. Spatial regret is a graph-informed metric that measures the worst-case performance difference between a distributed controller and a reference policy called the
oracle
.
The oracle is a controller with additional sensing or communication links. Hence, its communication topology is a user-defined supergraph of the plant network, serving as a hypothetical (“what-if”) reference topology.
Compared to
[
13
,
14
,
15
]
, this topology does not need to be centralized, allowing for a trade-off between the performance improvement the oracle can achieve and how well we can mimic the oracle using only the available sensor measurements.
For example, suppose some nodes in a networked system are severely affected by disturbances. A suitable oracle topology could allow more distant nodes to access the measurements of these affected nodes, which helps reduce the spread of disturbances originating from the affected nodes. In this case, the spatial regret quantifies how much performance is lost due to the limited communication in the real network graph.
Thus, the oracle guides the design effort towards the parts of the system where extra information would have the largest benefit.

We analyze the theoretical properties of this new metric, establishing conditions for the oracle to guarantee spatial regret is
well-posed
in the sense that it is always non-negative.
This ensures the oracle provides a meaningful performance benchmark.
We prove that spatial regret is well-posed as long as the oracle’s information structure contains that of the distributed controller, and the oracle is optimal in terms of
\H 2
,
\Hinfty
, or
\mathcal{L}_{1}
norm.

We then develop methods to synthesize distributed spatial regret-optimal controllers in an infinite-horizon setting. We derive a convex reformulation that casts the spatial regret minimization problem as an infinite-dimensional convex program, whose finite-dimensional approximation leads to a semidefinite program (SDP).

Our approach addresses limitations of existing regret-optimal control methods
[
16
,
12
,
17
,
18
,
19
,
20
,
21
]
, which typically consider noncausal and centralized benchmarks. While recent works achieve optimal regret via Riccati-based formulations
[
12
,
20
]
, these approaches cannot easily incorporate the sparsity constraints required for distributed policies. Conversely, convex optimization-based formulations
[
17
,
18
,
19
]
handle such constraints naturally but remain limited to noncausal benchmarks and finite-horizon problems.

To address the computational scalability challenges of the resulting SDPs for large systems (e.g.,
[
22
]
), we develop an upper bound on spatial regret that measures the tracking error relative to oracle trajectories, thereby maintaining the oracle as a performance benchmark. The key advantage is that minimizing this upper bound leads to a linear program (LP) that can be solved in a distributed manner using the alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM)
[
23
]
, which decomposes the large problem into smaller subproblems that are coordinated to find the global solution.

Finally, we validate our framework through numerical experiments on power grid systems, demonstrating that spatial regret controllers outperform classical
\H 2
and
\Hinfty
benchmarks under localized disturbances.
Using a 16-bus power grid model, we compare spatial regret-optimal controllers against traditional baselines across two scenarios: a 5-bus subsystem (where we obtain the optimal spatial regret controller) and the full 16-bus system (where we minimize the upper bound to the original metric).
The results show substantial performance improvements for multi-frequency disturbances affecting individual nodes, where the spatial regret framework effectively leverages the oracle’s enhanced sensing capabilities.

This paper builds upon our conference version
[
24
]
, where we first introduced the spatial regret framework for finite-horizon optimal distributed control with state feedback.
The present work extends those results in three key directions.
First, we transition from state to output feedback.
Second, we introduce more flexibility in the choice of oracle structure. In
[
24
]
, the oracle communication graph was fixed to guarantee well-posedness of the metric.
In this work, we identify a class of networked plants and restrict the controller to share the plant’s communication structure. Under these conditions, the spatial regret metric is well-posed for any oracle whose communication graph is a supergraph of the plant’s.
This flexibility enables incorporation of user insight into oracle design, broadening applicability to real-world networked systems.
Third, we extend the framework to infinite-horizon settings. The main challenge in this case is to derive tractable, finite-dimensional convex formulations that approximate the original infinite-dimensional programs while remaining dense, that is, capable of recovering the true solution as the approximation order increases.

However, such classical metrics are inherently graph-agnostic: they treat all disturbances equally, irrespective of the network constraints imposed on the controller.

This motivates a different design philosophy: prioritize robustness against disturbances for which information limitations are the main bottleneck, rather than treating all disturbances uniformly. To do so, we consider an oracle controller
\mathbf{\hat{Q}}\in\netstruc(\hat{\graph})_{\textnormal{stab}}

\netstruc(\hat{\graph})_{\textnormal{stab}}\coloneq\mathcal{RH}_{\infty}^{m\times p}\cap\netstruc(\hat{\graph})

\operatorname{SpReg}_{q}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{\hat{Q}})\coloneq\max_{\left\lVert{w}\right\rVert_{q}\leq 1}\left[J_{q}({w},\mathbf{Q})-J_{q}({w},\mathbf{\hat{Q}})\right]\,,

This work addresses two main challenges.
First, we establish conditions ensuring that the selected oracle provides a valid benchmark. To do so, we introduce the following definition.

Definition 2
.

The spatial regret metric
\operatorname{SpReg}_{q}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{\hat{Q}})
is
well-posed
for a given oracle
\mathbf{\hat{Q}}\in\netstruc(\hat{\graph})_{\textnormal{stab}}

\operatorname{SpReg}_{q}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{\hat{Q}})\geq 0\,,\quad\forall\mathbf{Q}\in\netstruc(\graph)_{\textnormal{stab}}\,.

(11)

Condition (
11

\mathbf{Q}
uniformly outperforms the oracle
\mathbf{\hat{Q}}
. When the condition is violated, the oracle ceases to be a meaningful benchmark, and the regret metric becomes misleading. The next scalar example illustrates how this failure can occur.

Example 2
.

Consider the following scalar LTI system:

x_{t+1}=\alpha x_{t}+\beta w_{t}+\gamma u_{t},\>\>\>z_{t}=x_{t},\>\>\>y_{t}=\begin{bmatrix}x_{t}&w_{t}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\!\!\!,

(12)

where
\left|\alpha\right|<1
,
\beta\neq 0
, and
\gamma\neq 0
.
Since the system is already stable, one might naively choose the zero oracle
\mathbf{\hat{K}}=\mathbf{\hat{Q}}=0
.
However, this choice violates well-posedness.
To see why, consider that the static controller
u_{t}=K^{*}y_{t}
,
K^{*}=-\frac{1}{\gamma}\begin{bmatrix}\alpha&\beta\end{bmatrix}
cancels the closed-loop dynamics, yielding
x_{t+1}=0
for any
w
.
In contrast, the zero oracle incurs
J_{q}(w,\mathbf{\hat{Q}})>0
for any nonzero
w

Hence, the oracle is uniformly outperformed by
\mathbf{Q}^{*}=K^{*}(I-\mathbf{P}_{22}K^{*})^{-1}
, making it meaningless to mimic, and this is reflected in
\operatorname{SpReg}_{q}(\mathbf{Q}^{*},\mathbf{\hat{Q}})<0
.

Remark 1

Given

\mathbf{W}(\mathrm{z})\in\netstruc(\graph)

\mathbf{F}_{\ell}(\mathbf{P},\mathbf{K})
with
\mathbf{F}_{\ell}(\mathbf{P},\mathbf{K})\mathbf{W}(\mathrm{z})
in problems (
18
) and (
22
), it is possible to incorporate prior knowledge about disturbance characteristics, as usually done in
\mathcal{H}_{2}
and
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
design
[
25
]
.

III
Main results

This section presents the main theoretical contributions.
First, we establish conditions under which the oracle guarantees well-posedness of the spatial regret metric (
9
). In the next theorem, we show that spatial regret is well-posed when the oracle’s information structure contains that of the distributed controller, and the oracle is optimal with respect to the
\H 2
,
\Hinfty
, or
\mathcal{L}_{1}
performance criteria.

Theorem 1
.

Given a graph
\hat{\graph}
such that

\graph\subset\hat{\graph}
,
assume an oracle
\mathbf{\hat{Q}}^{*}
is computed as the solution of:

\min_{\mathbf{\hat{Q}}}f(\mathbf{\hat{Q}})\;\;\;\st\quad\mathbf{\hat{Q}}\in\netstruc(\hat{\graph})_{\textnormal{stab}},

(13)

where:

f(\mathbf{\hat{Q}})=\begin{cases}\left\lVert\mathbf{F}_{\ell}(\mathbf{P},\mathbf{\hat{Q}})\right\rVert_{\H 2}^{2}\>\text{or}\>\left\lVert\mathbf{F}_{\ell}(\mathbf{P},\mathbf{\hat{Q}})\right\rVert_{\Hinfty}^{2}&\text{if }q=2,\\[4.30554pt]
\left\lVert\mathbf{F}_{\ell}(\mathbf{P},\mathbf{\hat{Q}})\right\rVert_{\mathcal{L}_{1}}&\text{if }q=\infty.\end{cases}

Then,
\operatorname{SpReg}_{q}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{\hat{Q}}^{*})\geq 0
for any
\mathbf{Q}\in\netstruc(\graph)_{\textnormal{stab}}
.

The proof of
Theorem

1
is reported in Appendix
-B
.
Having established the existence of well-posed oracle choices, the next step is to show how to solve problem (
10
) in a tractable manner. To this end, Section
III-A
derives an infinite-dimensional convex reformulation of problem (
10
) for
q=2
, along with a dense finite-dimensional approximation. Then, in Section
III-B
, we present a convex upper bound for the case
q=\infty
, which additionally enables distributed optimization, making the approach scalable and thus more suitable for large-scale systems.

III-A

Convex reformulation of
(
10
)
with
q=2

To proceed with the reformulation of problem (
10
), we introduce the following technical result.

Lemma 1
.

Let
\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{\hat{Q}}\in\mathcal{RH}_{\infty}^{m\times p}
. Then it holds that:

\operatorname{SpReg}_{2}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{\hat{Q}})=\sup_{\omega\in\Omega}\lambda_{\text{max}}(\mathbf{\Psi}(e^{j\omega})),

(14)

where:

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Psi}(e^{j\omega})

\displaystyle\coloneq\mathbf{F}_{\ell}^{*}(e^{j\omega})\mathbf{F}_{\ell}(e^{j\omega})-\mathbf{\hat{F}}_{\ell}^{*}(e^{j\omega})\mathbf{\hat{F}}_{\ell}(e^{j\omega})\,,

(15)

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}_{\ell}(e^{j\omega})

\displaystyle\coloneq\mathbf{P}_{11}(e^{j\omega})+\mathbf{P}_{12}(e^{j\omega})\mathbf{Q}(e^{j\omega})\mathbf{P}_{21}(e^{j\omega})\,,

(16)

\displaystyle\mathbf{\hat{F}}_{\ell}(e^{j\omega})

\displaystyle\coloneq\mathbf{P}_{11}(e^{j\omega})+\mathbf{P}_{12}(e^{j\omega})\mathbf{\hat{Q}}(e^{j\omega})\mathbf{P}_{21}(e^{j\omega})\,,

(17)

\lambda_{\text{max}}(\mathbf{\Psi}(e^{j\omega}))
denotes the largest eigenvalue of the matrix
\mathbf{\Psi}(e^{j\omega})
, and
\Omega\coloneq[-\pi,\pi]
is the frequency spectrum.

The proof of
Lemma

1
is reported in Appendix
-C
and follows a structure similar to that of
[
25
, Th. 4.3]
. However, a direct application of the latter result is not possible for two reasons. First,
[
25
, Th. 4.3]
is stated in continuous time, whereas our setting is discrete time. Second, the presence of the benchmark policy
\mathbf{\hat{Q}}
in (
9
) leads to the difference of two quadratic forms, so the matrix function
\mathbf{\Psi}(e^{j\omega})
may not be positive semidefinite for some
\omega\in\Omega
. As a consequence, a spectral factorization of the form
\mathbf{\Psi}(e^{j\omega})=\bm{\phi}^{*}(e^{j\omega})\bm{\phi}(e^{j\omega})
(required to invoke
[
25
, Th. 4.3]
) is not guaranteed to exist for every
\omega\in\Omega
.

Lemma
1
demonstrates that the regret can be equivalently computed as the maximum eigenvalue of the function
\mathbf{\Psi}(e^{j\omega})
over the frequency spectrum, confirming that the cost
\operatorname{SpReg}_{2}(\mathbf{Q},\hat{\mathbf{Q}})
is convex in
\mathbf{Q}
. Indeed,
\mathbf{\Psi}(e^{j\omega})
is a convex quadratic form in
\mathbf{Q}
, and the function
\lambda_{\text{max}}(\mathbf{\Psi}(e^{j\omega}))
preserves convexity.
The following theorem shows that problem (
10
) with
q=2
can be reformulated as an infinite-dimensional convex program that admits a finite-dimensional approximation.

Theorem 2
.

Consider a networked plant
{P}
that satisfies Assumption
1
.
Given a graph
\hat{\graph}
such that
\graph\subset\hat{\graph}
, assume the oracle
\mathbf{\hat{Q}}
is derived by solving the optimization problem (
13
). Then the spatial-regret problem (
10
) with
q=2
is equivalent to the following convex program:

\displaystyle\min_{\lambda\,,\mathbf{Q}}~

\displaystyle\lambda

(18a)

\displaystyle\st~

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}\in\netstruc(\graph)_{\textnormal{stab}}\,,\quad\lambda>0\,,

(18b)

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}I&\mathbf{F}_{\ell}(e^{j\omega})\\
\mathbf{F}_{\ell}^{*}(e^{j\omega})&\lambda I+\mathbf{\hat{F}}^{*}_{\ell}(e^{j\omega})\mathbf{\hat{F}}_{\ell}(e^{j\omega})\end{bmatrix}\succeq 0,\>\forall\omega\in\Omega\,,

(18c)

where
\mathbf{F}_{\ell}(\textnormal{z})
and
\mathbf{\hat{F}}_{\ell}(\textnormal{z})
are defined in (
16
), (
17
).

Proof.

Notice that the cost function
\operatorname{SpReg}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{\hat{Q}})
can be reformulated equivalently as (
14
) thanks to
Lemma

1
. Furthermore, introduce an auxiliary scalar
\lambda
to bound the largest singular value of
\mathbf{\Psi}(e^{j\omega})
. This results in an equivalent epigraph formulation:

\min_{\lambda,\mathbf{Q}}\lambda\quad\st\>\>\lambda>0\,,\>\>\lambda I\succeq\mathbf{\Psi}(e^{j\omega}),\quad\forall\omega\in\Omega\,,

which gives (
18a
)-(
18c
) via Schur complement. Since all constraints are convex in
(\lambda,\mathbf{Q})
, the result follows.
∎

To approximate the infinite program (
18
), we discretize the frequency-domain constraints (
18c
) and adopt a finite parameterization of
\mathbf{Q}\in\netstruc(\graph)_{\textnormal{stab}}
.
Indeed, constraints (
18c
) can be approximated by enforcing them over a finite set of frequencies
\Omega_{\rho}\coloneq\{\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{\rho}\}\subset\Omega
, with the flexibility to concentrate samples in frequency regions of interest (e.g., near resonance peaks).
We then derive a finite-dimensional representation of the constraint
\mathbf{Q}\in\netstruc(\graph)_{\text{stab}}
in (
18b
).
While Definition
1
characterizes the network structure in state space, the constraint
\mathbf{Q}\in\netstruc(\graph)_{\text{stab}}
requires a frequency-domain characterization, provided by
[
7
]
.

Lemma 2

(
[
7
]
)
.

Given a system
Q
such that
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathcal{R}_{p}^{m\times p}
, then
Q
is network-structured on
\graph
if and only if each block
\mathbf{Q}^{[i,j]}(\mathrm{z})
satisfies

\mathbf{Q}^{[i,j]}(\textnormal{z})=\begin{cases}\textnormal{z}^{-l(j,i)}\mathbf{H}_{i,j}(\textnormal{z})&\textnormal{ if }l(j,i)<\infty,\\
0&\textnormal{ otherwise,}\end{cases}

(19)

where
\mathbf{H}_{i,j}(\textnormal{z})\in\mathcal{R}_{p}^{p_{i}\times m_{j}}
and
l(j,i)
is the shortest path length from node
j
to node
i
. If no path exists, we set
l(j,i)=\infty
; by convention,
l(i,i)=0
.

Every
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathcal{RH}_{\infty}^{m\times p}
admits the impulse response expansion:

\mathbf{Q}(\textnormal{z})=\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}{Q}_{t}\>\textnormal{z}^{-t}\,,\quad Q_{t}\in\real{m\times p},

(20)

under which the condition
\mathbf{Q}\in\netstruc(\graph)
becomes an infinite collection of linear constraints on the matrices
Q_{t}
.

Proposition 1
.

A transfer function
\mathbf{Q}(\textnormal{z})
as in (
20
) satisfies
\mathbf{Q}\in\netstruc(\graph)_{\textnormal{stab}}
if and only if
\forall i,j=1,\dots,N
:

Q_{ijt}=0\,,\quad\forall t:\>\>0\leq t<l(j,i)\,,

(21)

where
Q_{ijt}\in\real{m_{i}\times p_{j}}
is the
(i,j)
block of
Q_{t}
.

This characterization supports a convex, finite-dimensional approximation of
\mathbf{Q}\in\netstruc(\graph)_{\textnormal{stab}}
. Indeed, truncating (
20
) to
f
terms yields the finite impulse response (FIR) model
\mathbf{\tilde{Q}}(\textnormal{z})=\sum_{t=0}^{f}Q_{t}\>\textnormal{z}^{-t}
, for which (
21
) reduces to a finite number of linear constraints on
Q_{t}
for
t=0,\dots,f
.
Finally, problem (
18
) can be approximated by solving the finite-dimensional SDP:

\displaystyle\min_{\lambda\,,\{Q_{ijt}\}}\quad\lambda

\displaystyle\st~\eqref{eq:conditions_delay_structured_FIR}\quad\forall i,j=1,\dots,N\,,\forall t=0,\dots,f\,,

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}I&\mathbf{F}_{\ell}(e^{j\omega_{k}})\\
\mathbf{F}_{\ell}^{*}(e^{j\omega_{k}})&\lambda I+\mathbf{\hat{F}}^{*}_{\ell}(e^{j\omega_{k}})\mathbf{\hat{F}}_{\ell}(e^{j\omega_{k}})\end{bmatrix}\succeq 0,\>\forall\omega_{k}\in\Omega_{\rho}\,,

where the optimization variables are the scalar
\lambda
and the
(f+1)
matrices
Q_{ijt}\in\real{m_{i}\times p_{j}}
for
i,j=1,\dots,N
.

Remark 3
.

Problem (
10
) could theoretically be solved using the bisection method from
[
12
, Sec. IV]
.
At each iteration, however, this approach requires solving a suboptimal
\Hinfty
minimization problem and factorizing and inverting the oracle closed-loop transfer function matrix, which is computationally intractable for large-scale systems (e.g.,
[
26
,
27
]
).
Our method in
Theorem

2
avoids these bottlenecks by eliminating expensive matrix operations and iterative procedures.

While the
q=2
case admits an exact SDP formulation, its computational complexity grows rapidly with system size (e.g.,
\mathcal{O}(X^{6.5})
for
X
variables using interior-point methods
[
28
]
), making it impractical for large-scale systems.
To address this, we derive an upper bound on
\operatorname{SpReg}_{\infty}
whose minimization can be solved using ADMM, enabling scalability to large networks.
In contrast, the
q=2
case cannot be distributed due to the frequency-domain constraints in (
18c
) that couple all controller variables at each frequency.

III-B

Convex reformulation of
(
10
)
with
q=\infty

Computing
\operatorname{SpReg}_{\infty}
, as per (
9
), requires evaluating the maximum difference of maximum absolute values over an infinite set of disturbances, which is computationally intractable. Unlike the
q=2
case, there is no direct analogue of
Lemma

1
that simplifies the formulation.
Instead, we address this computational challenge by deriving a convex upper bound that still extrapolates information from the dynamics of the oracle.

Proposition 2
.

Let
\mathbf{\hat{Q}}^{*}
solve (
13
) with objective
f(\mathbf{\hat{Q}})=\|\mathbf{F}_{\ell}(\mathbf{P},\mathbf{\hat{Q}})\|_{\mathcal{L}_{1}}
. Then the optimal value of the problem:

\min_{\mathbf{Q}}\>\left\lVert\mathbf{F}_{\ell}(\mathbf{P},\mathbf{Q})-\mathbf{F}_{\ell}(\mathbf{P},\mathbf{\hat{Q}}^{*})\right\rVert_{\mathcal{L}_{1}}\>\text{s.t.}\>\>\mathbf{Q}\in\netstruc(\graph)_{\textnormal{stab}},

(22)

provides an upper bound to (
10
) when
q=\infty
in (
9
). Moreover, problem (
22
) can be expressed as the following LP:

\displaystyle\min_{\lambda,\{Q_{ijt}\},\{\nu_{ijt}\}}\quad\lambda

(23a)

\displaystyle~\text{s.t.}\quad\eqref{eq:tf_as_sum_impulse_response}\,,\>\>\eqref{eq:conditions_delay_structured_FIR}\,,\quad\forall(i,j)\in\mathcal{I},\>\>t\geq 0:

\displaystyle~~~~-\nu_{ijt}\leq\bar{P}_{11}^{[i,j]}(t)+(P_{12}*Q*P_{21})^{[i,j]}(t)\leq\nu_{ijt}\,,

(23b)

\displaystyle~~~~\sum_{j=1}^{n_{w}}\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\nu_{ijt}\leq\lambda\,,\quad\quad\nu_{ijt}\geq 0\,,

(23c)

where
\mathcal{I}\coloneq\{1,\dots,n_{z}\}\times\{1,\dots,n_{w}\}
, and
\nu_{ijt}\in\real{n_{z}\times n_{w}}
for
t\geq 0
. The terms
\bar{P}_{11}^{[i,j]}(t)
and
(P_{12}*Q*P_{21})^{[i,j]}(t)
denote the
(i,j)^{th}
impulse responses at time
t
of
\mathbf{\bar{P}}_{11}\coloneq-\mathbf{P}_{12}\mathbf{\hat{Q}}\mathbf{P}_{21}
and the convolution
\mathbf{P}_{12}*\mathbf{Q}*\mathbf{P}_{21}
, respectively.

Proof.

Let
z
and
\hat{z}
denote the outputs of
\mathbf{F}_{\ell}(\mathbf{P},\mathbf{Q})
and
\mathbf{F}_{\ell}(\mathbf{P},\mathbf{\hat{Q}})
, respectively, under a common disturbance
w
.
By the triangle inequality,
\left\lVert z\right\rVert_{\infty}-\left\lVert\hat{z}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\left\lVert z-\hat{z}\right\rVert_{\infty}
for any
\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{\infty}<\infty
where both
\left\lVert z\right\rVert_{\infty}
and
\left\lVert\hat{z}\right\rVert_{\infty}
are finite due to the stability of
\mathbf{F}_{\ell}(\mathbf{P},\mathbf{Q})
and
\mathbf{F}_{\ell}(\mathbf{P},\mathbf{\hat{Q}})
.
Then, taking the maximum over all disturbances
\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq 1
leads to:

\underbrace{\max_{\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq 1}\Big[\left\lVert z\right\rVert_{\infty}-\left\lVert\hat{z}\right\rVert_{\infty}\Big]}_{=\operatorname{SpReg}_{\infty}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{\hat{Q}})}\leq\underbrace{\max_{\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq 1}\left\lVert z-\hat{z}\right\rVert_{\infty}}_{=\left\lVert\mathbf{F}_{\ell}(\mathbf{P},\mathbf{Q})-\mathbf{F}_{\ell}(\mathbf{P},\mathbf{\hat{Q}})\right\rVert_{\mathcal{L}_{1}}}.

This establishes the upper bound claim.
For the LP formulation, observe that
\mathbf{F}_{\ell}(\mathbf{P},\mathbf{Q})-\mathbf{F}_{\ell}(\mathbf{P},\mathbf{\hat{Q}})=\mathbf{\bar{P}}_{11}+\mathbf{P}_{12}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{P}_{21}
where
\mathbf{\bar{P}}_{11}\coloneq-\mathbf{P}_{12}\mathbf{\hat{Q}}\mathbf{P}_{21}
. Since both closed-loop maps are stable, their difference is stable, and the
\mathcal{L}_{1}
norm of the difference can be expressed as:

\left\lVert\mathbf{F}_{\ell}(\mathbf{P},\mathbf{Q})-\mathbf{F}_{\ell}(\mathbf{P},\mathbf{\hat{Q}})\right\rVert_{\mathcal{L}_{1}}=\\
\max_{i=1,\dots,n_{z}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{w}}\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\left|\bar{P}_{11}^{[i,j]}(t)+(P_{12}*Q*P_{21})^{[i,j]}(t)\right|.

(24)

Standard epigraph reformulation of cost (
24
) with slack variables
\{\nu_{ijt}\}
leads to (
23
), an LP in the variables
\{\nu_{ijt}\}
and the impulse response coefficients
\{Q_{ijt}\}
.
Finally, the constraint
\mathbf{Q}\in\netstruc(\graph)_{\text{stab}}
is expressed as (
21
) thanks to
Proposition

1
.
∎

Problem (
23
) is infinite due to the infinite number of time indices
t
in constraints (
21
), (
23b
), and (
23c
).
A finite-dimensional approximation is obtained by truncating the time horizon to
t=0,\dots,f
for some finite
f>0
. This truncation is justified since both
\mathbf{Q}\in\netstruc(\graph)_{\textnormal{stab}}
and the closed-loop difference
\mathbf{\bar{P}}_{11}+\mathbf{P}_{12}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{P}_{21}
are stable, which ensures that their impulse response coefficients decay exponentially to zero.

The upper bound
\left\lVert\mathbf{F}_{\ell}(\mathbf{P},\mathbf{Q})-\mathbf{F}_{\ell}(\mathbf{P},\mathbf{\hat{Q}}^{*})\right\rVert_{\mathcal{L}_{1}}
in
Proposition

2
penalizes the worst-case trajectory mismatch between the regulated outputs of
\mathbf{Q}
and
\mathbf{\hat{Q}}
, rather than their worst-case difference in performance (as in
\operatorname{SpReg}_{\infty}
). However, minimizing this trajectory mismatch still makes the approach graph-informed through the choice of the oracle supergraph
\hat{\graph}
.

III-C

Distributed computation

The LP in (
22
) has computational complexity scaling polynomially with
2(n_{z}n_{w}f)+1
variables, making it prohibitive for large-scale systems.
We address this by showing that problem (
22
) can be solved using distributed ADMM
[
23
,
29
]
, which decomposes the large problem into
M
smaller subproblems solved in parallel, coordinated through dual variable updates.
To distribute problem (
22
), we employ the system-level parametrization (SLP)
[
29
]
. Given
n_{r}\coloneq n+m
and
n_{c}\coloneq n+p
, introduce the auxiliary variable
\mathbf{\Phi}\in\mathcal{RH}_{\infty}^{n_{r}\times n_{c}}
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Phi}

\displaystyle\coloneq\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{\Phi}_{xx}&\mathbf{\Phi}_{xy}\\
\mathbf{\Phi}_{ux}&\mathbf{\Phi}_{uy}\end{bmatrix}

(25)

\displaystyle\coloneq\begin{bmatrix}\left(\textnormal{z}I-A-B_{2}\mathbf{K}C_{2}\right)^{-1}&\mathbf{\Phi}_{xx}B_{2}\mathbf{K}\\
\mathbf{K}C_{2}\mathbf{\Phi}_{xx}&\mathbf{K}(I-\mathbf{P}_{22}\mathbf{K})^{-1}\end{bmatrix}\,.

Then,
\mathbf{F}_{\ell}(\mathbf{P},\mathbf{K})
can be expressed in terms of
\mathbf{\Phi}
as
[
29
]
:

\mathbf{F}_{\ell}(\mathbf{P},\mathbf{\Phi})\coloneq\begin{bmatrix}C_{1}&D_{12}\end{bmatrix}\mathbf{\Phi}\begin{bmatrix}B_{1}\\
D_{21}\end{bmatrix}+D_{11}\,.

(26)

Under Assumption
1
, we have
\mathbf{\Phi}_{uy}=\mathbf{Q}
, enabling direct imposition of network constraints on
\mathbf{\Phi}_{uy}
.
The difference
\mathbf{\Delta}(\mathbf{\Phi},\mathbf{\hat{\Phi}})\coloneq\mathbf{F}_{\ell}(\mathbf{P},\mathbf{\Phi})-\mathbf{F}_{\ell}(\mathbf{P},\mathbf{\hat{\Phi}})
becomes:

\mathbf{\Delta}(\mathbf{\Phi},\mathbf{\hat{\Phi}})=\begin{bmatrix}C_{1}&D_{12}\end{bmatrix}(\mathbf{\Phi}-\mathbf{\hat{\Phi}})\begin{bmatrix}B_{1}\\
D_{21}\end{bmatrix},

(27)

where
\mathbf{\hat{\Phi}}
corresponds to the oracle
\mathbf{\hat{Q}}
.
As shown in
[
29
]
, the set of achievable closed-loop responses in (
25
) is the set of all and only maps
\mathbf{\Phi}
that satisfy:

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Phi}\begin{bmatrix}\textnormal{z}I-A\\
-C_{2}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}I\\
0\end{bmatrix},

(28a)

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\textnormal{z}I-A&-B_{2}\end{bmatrix}\mathbf{\Phi}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}I&0\end{bmatrix}.

(28b)

Problem (
22
) can be equivalently rewritten in terms of
\mathbf{\Phi}
as:

\min_{\mathbf{\Phi}}\left\lVert\mathbf{\Delta}(\mathbf{\Phi},\mathbf{\hat{\Phi}})\right\rVert_{\mathcal{L}_{1}}\>\>\text{s.t.}\>\>\>\eqref{eq:achievability_constraints_SLP},\>\>\mathbf{\Phi}_{uy}\in\netstruc(\graph)_{\textnormal{stab}}.

(29)

Given the optimal
\mathbf{\Phi}^{*}
, the controller is recovered as
\mathbf{K}^{*}=\mathbf{\Phi}_{uy}^{*}(I+\mathbf{P}_{22}\mathbf{\Phi}_{uy}^{*})^{-1}
.
In
[
29
]
, the authors propose an ADMM-based approach to solve classical optimal control problems in a distributed manner. Their method applies to problems with cost functions and constraints that depend separately on partitions of the optimization variable.
The constraints in (
28
) exhibit these required separability properties. Constraint (
28a
) is
row-separable
, meaning that for any partition
r\coloneq\{r_{1},\ldots,r_{M}\}
of the row indices
\{1,\ldots,n_{r}\}
, it decomposes as:

\mathbf{\Phi}^{[r_{i},:]}\begin{bmatrix}\textnormal{z}I-A\\
-C_{2}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}I\\
0\end{bmatrix}^{[r_{i},:]},\quad\forall i=1,\ldots,M.

Similarly, constraint (
28b
) is
column-separable
for any partition
c\coloneq\{c_{1},\ldots,c_{M}\}
of the column indices
\{1,\ldots,n_{c}\}
. The network constraint
\mathbf{\Phi}_{uy}\in\netstruc(\graph)_{\textnormal{stab}}
imposes element-wise sparsity on impulse response coefficients (
Proposition

1
) and is both row- and column-separable.
We then partition the constraints of problem (
29
) into row- and column-separable sets:

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{(r)}

\displaystyle\coloneq\left\{\mathbf{\Phi}\in\mathcal{RH}_{\infty}^{n_{r}\times n_{c}}:\eqref{eq:achievability_constraints_SLP_row_wise},\mathbf{\Phi}_{uy}\in\netstruc(\graph)_{\textnormal{stab}}\right\},

(30)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{(c)}

\displaystyle\coloneq\left\{\mathbf{\Phi}\in\mathcal{RH}_{\infty}^{n_{r}\times n_{c}}:\eqref{eq:achievability_constraints_SLP_column_wise}\right\}.

(31)

Similarly to constraint sets, a function
f(\mathbf{\Phi})
is
row-separable
for partition
\{r_{1},\ldots,r_{M}\}
of
\{1,\ldots,n_{r}\}
if:

f(\mathbf{\Phi})=\sum_{i=1}^{M}f^{(r)}_{i}(\mathbf{\Phi}^{[r_{i},:]}).

(32)

For instance, the
\H 2
norm is row- and column-separable:

\left\lVert\mathbf{\Phi}\right\rVert_{\H 2}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\left\lVert\mathbf{\Phi}^{[r_{i},:]}\right\rVert_{\H 2}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{M}\left\lVert\mathbf{\Phi}^{[:,c_{j}]}\right\rVert_{\H 2}^{2}.

However, the objective in (
29
) is an
\mathcal{L}_{1}
norm, which represents the maximum value in time over all outputs of a system. This norm couples all variables and prevents direct decomposition as in (
32
), causing our case to fall outside the standard framework of
[
29
]
.
Nevertheless, under structural assumptions on the regulated output matrices, problem (
22
) can still be solved in a distributed manner.

Assumption 2
.

There exists a permutation matrix
\Pi\in\real{n_{r}\times n_{r}}
such that

\begin{bmatrix}C_{1}&D_{12}\end{bmatrix}\Pi
is block-diagonal.

This assumption holds for many practical cases, including the LQG cost
x^{\top}Qx+u^{\top}Ru
where
C_{1}^{\top}=\begin{bmatrix}Q^{\top/2}&0\end{bmatrix}
and
D_{12}^{\top}=\begin{bmatrix}0&R^{\top/2}\end{bmatrix}
are already block-diagonal.

Theorem 3
.

Let
r\coloneq\{r_{1},\ldots,r_{M}\}
and
c\coloneq\{c_{1},\ldots,c_{M}\}
be row and column partitions of
\{1,\ldots,n_{r}\}
and
\{1,\ldots,n_{c}\}
.
Define the functional
\mathcal{F}^{(r)}
as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{(r)}(\gamma,\mathbf{\Phi},\mathbf{\Psi},\mathbf{\Lambda},\mathbf{\hat{\Phi}})\coloneq

(33)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\left\lVert\mathbf{\Phi}-(\mathbf{\Psi}-\mathbf{\Lambda})\right\rVert_{\H 2}^{2}&\textnormal{if }\mathbf{\Phi}\in\mathcal{S}^{(r)},\>\left\lVert\mathbf{\Delta}(\mathbf{\Phi},\mathbf{\hat{\Phi}})\right\rVert_{\mathcal{L}_{1}}\leq\gamma,\\
+\infty&\textnormal{otherwise,}\end{cases}

where
\mathbf{\Delta}_{r_{i}}
collects the components of
\mathbf{\Delta}(\mathbf{\Phi},\mathbf{\hat{\Phi}})
that depend only on
\mathbf{\Phi}^{[r_{i},:]}
(as induced by the permutation matrix
\Pi
).
Moreover, define for each
j\in\{1,\ldots,M\}
the following functionals:

\mathcal{F}^{(c)}_{j}(\mathbf{\Psi}_{j},\mathbf{\Phi},\mathbf{\Lambda})\coloneq\begin{cases}\left\lVert\mathbf{\Psi}_{j}-(\mathbf{\Phi}+\mathbf{\Lambda})^{[:,c_{j}]}\right\rVert_{\H 2}^{2}&\text{if }\mathbf{\Psi}_{j}\in\mathcal{S}^{(c)}_{j},\\
+\infty&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

(34)

where
\mathbf{\Psi}_{j}\in\mathcal{RH}_{\infty}^{n_{r}\times|c_{j}|}
and
\mathcal{S}^{(c)}_{j}
represents the restriction of the column constraint set
\mathcal{S}^{(c)}
to the variables
\mathbf{\Psi}^{[:,c_{j}]}
.
Under Assumption

V
Conclusion

This paper introduced spatial regret, a graph-informed metric for quantifying the performance loss caused by distributed information constraints relative to an oracle with enhanced sensing. Unlike classical
\mathcal{H}_{2}
and
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
norms, spatial regret focuses on disturbances for which additional sensor information leads to the greatest improvement, such as localized disturbances affecting individual nodes.

We established well-posedness conditions to ensure the oracle provides a meaningful benchmark, and developed two synthesis approaches: an exact SDP formulation for
q=2
, and a scalable distributed algorithm for
q=\infty
tailored to large-scale systems. Numerical experiments on power grid models demonstrated 15-27% performance improvements over classical controllers for localized multi-frequency disturbances, validating the effectiveness of our framework.

These results open several directions for future research. One avenue is the development of more efficient algorithms for synthesizing spatial regret-optimal controllers, avoiding SDPs for
q=2
and improving upper bounds for
q=\infty
. Further, exploring the joint exploitation of non-causality and sparsity in oracle selection may yield additional benefits. Incorporating model uncertainty through robust spatial regret formulations, or designing controllers based on data-driven frequency responses, are also promising directions.

Overall, spatial regret represents a concrete step toward integrating network structure and information limitations into performance metrics, enabling targeted and effective synthesis of distributed controllers for complex networked systems.

\max_{\lVert{w}_{1}\rVert_{2}\leq 1}[J_{2}({w}_{1},\mathbf{\bar{Q}})]\geq\max_{\lVert{w}_{2}\rVert_{2}\leq 1}[J_{2}({w}_{2},\mathbf{\hat{Q}})]\geq J_{2}({w},\mathbf{\hat{Q}}).

Proof of Lemma

Let

\hat{z}
denote the outputs of the two closed-loop systems
\mathbf{F}_{\ell}(\mathbf{\tilde{P}},\mathbf{Q})
and
\mathbf{F}_{\ell}(\mathbf{\tilde{P}},\mathbf{\hat{Q}})
, respectively, when affected by the same disturbance
w
with
\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{2}\leq 1
. Using the definition (
9
) and Parseval’s theorem, we derive:

\displaystyle\operatorname{SpReg}_{2}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{\hat{Q}})=\max_{\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{2}\leq 1}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{+\pi}\!\!\!\mathbf{w}^{*}(e^{j\omega})\mathbf{\Psi}(e^{j\omega})\mathbf{w}(e^{j\omega})d\omega

\displaystyle\leq\max_{\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{2}\leq 1}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{+\pi}\lambda_{\textnormal{max}}\big(\mathbf{\Psi}(e^{j\omega})\big)\mathbf{w}^{*}(e^{j\omega})\mathbf{w}(e^{j\omega})d\omega

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\omega\in\Omega}\lambda_{\textnormal{max}}\big(\mathbf{\Psi}(e^{j\omega})\big)\max_{\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{2}\leq 1}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{+\pi}\mathbf{w}^{*}(e^{j\omega})\mathbf{w}(e^{j\omega})d\omega

\displaystyle=\sup_{\omega\in\Omega}\lambda_{\textnormal{max}}\big(\mathbf{\Psi}(e^{j\omega})\big)\max_{\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{2}\leq 1}\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=\sup_{\omega\in\Omega}\lambda_{\textnormal{max}}\big(\mathbf{\Psi}(e^{j\omega})\big)\,.

To complete the proof, we construct a disturbance
\bar{w}
with
\left\lVert\bar{w}\right\rVert_{2}\leq 1
achieving equality.
Let
\omega_{0}\in\arg\sup_{\omega\in\Omega}\lambda_{\textnormal{max}}\big(\mathbf{\Psi}(e^{j\omega})\big)
, assuming
\omega_{0}\neq\pm\pi
. Since
\mathbf{\Psi}(e^{j\omega_{0}})
is Hermitian, it admits the decomposition:

\mathbf{\Psi}(e^{j\omega_{0}})=\lambda_{\textnormal{max}}v_{1}(e^{j\omega_{0}})v_{1}^{*}(e^{j\omega_{0}})+\sum_{i=2}^{\psi}\lambda_{i}v_{i}(e^{j\omega_{0}})v_{i}^{*}(e^{j\omega_{0}})\,,

where

\mathbf{\Psi}(e^{j\omega_{0}})

v_{i}\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{w}}
have unit length.
Ideally, using

\bar{w}

v_{1}(e^{j\omega_{0}})=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha_{1}e^{j\theta_{1}}&\cdots&\alpha_{n_{w}}e^{j\theta_{n_{w}}}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\,,

where for consistency
\alpha_{i}\in\real{}
is such that
\theta_{i}\in]-\pi,0]

.
Denote
\gamma\coloneq\frac{\omega_{0}}{\tan{(\nicefrac{{\omega_{0}}}{{2}}})}

\beta_{i}\geq 0

\theta_{i}=\angle{\left(\frac{(\beta_{i}-\gamma)e^{j\omega_{0}}+(\beta_{i}+\gamma)}{(\beta_{i}+\gamma)e^{j\omega_{0}}+(\beta_{i}-\gamma)}\right)}\,,

\beta_{i}\to\infty

\mathbf{f}_{f}(\textnormal{z})

\omega_{0}

|\mathbf{f}_{f}(e^{j\omega}))|=\left\{\begin{aligned} \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2\varepsilon}}\quad&\textnormal{if }|\omega-\omega_{0}|<\varepsilon\textnormal{ or }|\omega+\omega_{0}|<\varepsilon\,,\\
0\quad&\textnormal{otherwise.}\end{aligned}\right.

where
\varepsilon>0
is small.
Then, pick the disturbance
\bar{w}

\mathbf{\bar{w}}(\textnormal{z})\coloneq\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\alpha_{1}\frac{(\beta_{1}-\gamma)\textnormal{z}+(\beta_{1}+\gamma)}{(\beta_{1}+\gamma)\textnormal{z}+(\beta_{1}-\gamma)}\\
\vdots\\
\alpha_{n_{w}}\frac{(\beta_{n_{w}}-\gamma)\textnormal{z}+(\beta_{n_{w}}+\gamma)}{(\beta_{n_{w}}+\gamma)\textnormal{z}+(\beta_{n_{w}}-\gamma)}\end{bmatrix}}_{\coloneq\mathbf{f}_{v_{1}}(\textnormal{z})}\mathbf{f}_{f}(\textnormal{z})\,.

The function
\mathbf{f}_{v_{1}}

Substituting
\mathbf{\bar{w}}(z)

\displaystyle\left\lVert z\right\rVert_{2}^{2}-\left\lVert\hat{z}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{+\pi}\mathbf{\bar{w}}^{*}(e^{j\omega})\mathbf{\Psi}(e^{j\omega})\mathbf{\bar{w}}(e^{j\omega})d\omega

which shows that

For the case
\omega_{0}=\pm\pi

\mathbf{f}_{f}(z)

Proof of Theorem
3

Problem (
29
) can be rearranged as follows:

\min_{\mathbf{\Phi},\mathbf{\Psi}}\left\lVert\mathbf{\Delta}(\mathbf{\Phi},\mathbf{\hat{\Phi}})\right\rVert_{\mathcal{L}_{1}}\text{s.t.}\>\>\mathbf{\Phi}\in\mathcal{S}^{(r)},\>\>\mathbf{\Psi}\in\mathcal{S}^{(c)},\>\>\mathbf{\Phi}=\mathbf{\Psi}.

(45)

Applying ADMM
[
29
]
to (
45
) yields the iterations:

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Phi}_{k+1}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\!\argmin_{\mathbf{\Phi}\in\mathcal{S}^{(r)}}\!\left\lVert\mathbf{\Delta}(\mathbf{\Phi},\mathbf{\hat{\Phi}})\right\rVert_{\mathcal{L}_{1}}\!\!\!\!+\frac{\rho}{2}\!\left\lVert\mathbf{\Phi}\!-\!\mathbf{\Psi}_{k}\!+\!\mathbf{\Lambda}_{k}\right\rVert_{\H 2}^{2}\!,

(46a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Psi}_{k+1}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\argmin_{\mathbf{\Psi}\in\mathcal{S}^{(c)}}\frac{\rho}{2}\left\lVert\mathbf{\Psi}-\mathbf{\Phi}_{k+1}-\mathbf{\Lambda}_{k}\right\rVert_{\H 2}^{2},

(46b)

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Lambda}_{k+1}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\mathbf{\Lambda}_{k}+\mathbf{\Phi}_{k+1}-\mathbf{\Psi}_{k+1}.

(46c)

The convergence of ADMM follows from problem convexity and feasible set non-emptiness
[
23
,
29
]
.
Each step in (
46
) can be solved in a distributed manner, as we now explain.
Problem (
46b
) exhibits column separability: both the constraint set
\mathcal{S}^{(c)}
and the
\H 2
norm are column-separable. This allows decomposition into
M
independent subproblems, yielding Steps
6
–
7
via the definition of
\mathcal{F}^{(c)}_{j}
in (
34
).
The dual update (
46c
) is a sum that requires no distributed computation.
The main challenge lies in problem (
46a
), which contains the non-separable cost
\left\lVert\mathbf{\Delta}(\mathbf{\Phi},\mathbf{\hat{\Phi}})\right\rVert_{\mathcal{L}_{1}}\!\!\!.

To enable a distributed solution, we introduce an epigraph variable
\gamma\geq 0
and reformulate (
46a
) as:

\min_{\gamma}\gamma+\frac{\rho}{2}\Bigg(\underbrace{\begin{aligned} \min_{\mathbf{\Phi}\in\mathcal{S}^{(r)}}\left\lVert\mathbf{\Phi}-\mathbf{\Psi}_{k}+\mathbf{\Lambda}_{k}\right\rVert_{\H 2}^{2}\\
\text{s.t.}\quad\left\lVert\mathbf{\Delta}(\mathbf{\Phi},\mathbf{\hat{\Phi}})\right\rVert_{\mathcal{L}_{1}}\leq\gamma\end{aligned}}_{=\min_{\mathbf{\Phi}}\mathcal{F}^{(r)}(\gamma,\mathbf{\Phi},\mathbf{\Psi}_{k},\mathbf{\Lambda}_{k},\mathbf{\hat{\Phi}})}\Bigg).

2

[
29

This enables parallel minimization of
\mathcal{F}^{(r)}
over
M
independent subproblems corresponding to the row blocks

of
\mathbf{\Phi}

2

The outer optimization (
47

\gamma
(Step
