On the quantum dynamics of long-ranged Bose-Hubbard Hamiltonians
Marius Lemm, Carla Rubiliani, Jingxuan Zhang

Abstract
. We study the quantum dynamics generated by Bose-Hubbard Hamiltonians with long-ranged (power law) terms. We prove two ballistic propagation bounds for suitable initial states: (i) A bound on all moments of the local particle number for all power law exponents α > d + 1 \alpha>d+1 in d d dimensions, the sharp condition. (ii) The first thermodynamically stable Lieb-Robinson bound (LRB) for these Hamiltonians. To handle the long-ranged and unbounded terms, we further develop the multiscale ASTLO (adiabatic space time localization observables) method introduced in our recent work [ 32 ] .

Introduction

In this paper, we study the quantum dynamics of macroscopically large quantum systems at positive density with strong, long-range interactions. These models are relevant, e.g., for describing the physics of ultracold gases in optical lattices, which are the main platforms on which experimental physicists can perform quantum simulation
[
4
,
17
,
26
]
.

We consider a system of
N
bosonic quantum particles whose positions are restricted to a discrete, finite subset
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{d},\,d\geq 1
. The state of the
N
-boson system is mathematically given by a permutation-symmetric element of the Hilbert space
\bigotimes^{N}_{j=1}\ell^{2}(\Lambda)
. It can be identified with a function

(1.1)

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\psi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}),\qquad\textnormal{with }x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\in\Lambda

satisfying
\psi(x_{\pi(1)},\ldots,x_{\pi(N)})=\psi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})
for all permutations
\pi\in S_{N}

The system state at time
t
is obtained by solving the time-dependent Schrödinger equation

(1.2)

i\partial_{t}\psi_{t}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})=H\psi_{t}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}).

where
H
, the Hamiltonian, is a linear self-adjoint operator of the form

(1.3)

H=\sum_{i=1}^{N}J^{i}+\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq N}V(x_{i},x_{j}).

Here
J^{i}
acts as a fixed
|\Lambda|\times|\Lambda|
Hermitian matrix
J
on the
i
-th particle; it represents the hopping of the
i
-th particle between lattice sites
1
1

1

In physics,
\Lambda

x,y\in\Lambda
.
Moreover,

represents the interaction between particles. The precise definitions can be found in Section

We are interested in systems which combine a few analytical challenges:

(a)

We are interested in
macroscopically large systems at positive density
, i.e., we aim for bounds that are stable in the thermodynamic limit
N,\,\left\lvert\Lambda\right\rvert\to\infty
with fixed density
N/\left\lvert\Lambda\right\rvert=\rho>0
. The main large parameter is the number of particles
N
. While the PDE (
1.2
) is linear, the presence of
N
affects the number of tensor powers that is taken in (
1.1
) and so the problem is effectively one in extremely large dimension. This is widely known as the “curse of dimensionality” of the quantum many-body problem. Meaningful results thus need to be independent of
N
, i.e., of dimension.
2
2

2

For few-body dynamics, i.e., when
N
is held fixed, standard PDE techniques allow to derive ballistic bounds, but the methodology therein does not extend to systems at positive spatial density that we consider here. This means that even deriving a ballistic propagation bound that is uniform in
N
lies outside the scope of usual PDE techniques (e.g., the well-known Radin-Simon ballistic upper bound
[
37
]
). Instead, one needs to exploit the non-trivial local tensor structure that is inherent in quantum many-body physics through the choice of Hilbert space (
1.1
) and Hamiltonian (
1.3
).

(b)

The Bose-Hubbard Hamiltonians (
1.3
) are
strongly interacting
. Mathematically, this means that the interaction term
\sum_{i,j}V(x_{i},x_{j})
does not have any kind of small parameter in front of it that would weaken the effect of interactions (e.g., in the widely studied mean-field regime one places a prefactor
\frac{1}{N}
in front of
\sum_{i,j}V(x_{i},x_{j})

[
24
,
16
,
39

[
10
,
11
,
6

. Since our systems are not associated with any small parameter, the kind of effective evolution equations (and other expansion techniques around weakly interacting systems) that are commonplace in the literature are not available to us. In fact, they are not meaningful here because there is
no small parameter
to expand in.

(c)

We will also allow for both
J^{i}
and
V
to be
long-ranged
(or “heavy-tailed”), i.e., we allow for power-law decay. More precisely, recalling that
d
denotes the spatial dimension, we make the following power-law decay assumptions

(1.4)

|J^{i}_{x_{i}y_{i}}|\leq C|x_{i}-y_{i}|^{-\alpha},\quad|V(x_{i},x_{j})|\leq C|x_{i}-x_{j}|^{-\alpha},\qquad\textnormal{for all $1\leq i\leq N$},

Here
\alpha>0
is a fixed power-law exponent that we aim to choose as small as possible, so that the results hold with the heaviest possible tails.

(d)

For bosons, the local interactions are strong in another, non-parametric sense: they are effectively
unbounded
3
3

3

More precisely, the size of local interactions can grow in the total particle number
N
, which amounts to unboundedness in the limit
N\to\infty
.
even on a lattice, because arbitrarily many of the
N
bosons can accumulate at a single lattice site.

We are interested in the unitary Schrödinger dynamics
e^{-\mathrm{i}tH}
generated by the self-adjoint operator (
1.2
) via Stone’s theorem.
Our goal is to derive
robust propagation bounds
that hold for the whole class of Hamiltonians (
1.3
) and that are thermodynamically stable, i.e., that are uniform as
N\to\infty
.
From a PDE perspective, our goal is to derive
a priori
bounds which capture the fundamental limitations of these quantum many-body systems to rapidly transport either particles and/or correlations. Such propagation bounds on correlations are commonly known as “Lieb-Robinson bounds” after
[
33

The wide interest in quantum propagation bounds stems from the fact that they are decisive tools both for structural results in the context of operator algebras (e.g., they provide the existence of the thermodynamic limit

Due to these various uses and others, propagation bounds have had profound impact on the area of mathematical physics that investigates quantum many-body systems through analytical methods. Accordingly, deriving these essential bounds for systems that lie beyond the existing analytical methods has developed into a dynamic research area within mathematical physics and our paper falls within this area.

The original Lieb-Robinson bound

•

Problem 1: Uniform particle transport bound.
Controlling the growth of high moments of the local boson number

By developing a multiscale version

However, considering a fixed

•

Problem 2: Thermodynamically stable Lieb-Robinson bound (LRB).
On the one hand, for finite-range Bose-Hubbard Hamiltonians, thermodynamically stable LRBs (meaning LRBs that are independent of the total particle number) have been proved for bounded-density initial states
[

,

]
, but with velocities that are growing in time. On the other hand, for initial states satisfying stronger assumptions, namely states with particle-free regions, bounded-velocity (“ballistic”) LRBs have been proved in

Addressing these open problems would rigorously establish for the first time that ballistic propagation of particles and correlations is a rather universal property that extends to the dynamics of many-body Hamiltonians for which all four methodological obstacles (a)–(d) are present.

In this paper, we resolve both of these open problems with the following results.
Specifically, let
H_{\Lambda}
be a Hamiltonian of the form (

A few remarks on the results are in order. First, the condition
\alpha>d+1
for the uniform particle propagation is known to be sharp by studying the non-interacting case (

)
[

. Second, our results yield an explicit universal bound,
\kappa
, on the maximal speed of particle and information propagation. This

is naturally tied to the norm of one-particle group velocity operator. This is to be compared to
[

show any of this unusual behavior and instead adhere to the usual physical principle of locality: propagation is controlled by fixed maximal speed that depends on system parameters and does not grow with time.

Third, regarding the assumption on the initial states, notice that they are different between our two results: our uniform particle propagation bounds assume upper and lower bounds on the density. This includes the experimentally relevant Mott state with unit filling
[
8
]
. For the second result, the bounded-speed Lieb-Robinson bounds, we require an initial particle free region. Indeed, since protocols exist even in the short-range case
[
29
]
that show super-ballistic information propagation for general bounded-density initial states, an additional assumption on the initial state is needed to obtain a bounded-speed LRB. It is a separate problem to derive LRBs for bounded-density initial states under the power-law condition

[

. This would likely require complementing our uniform particle propagation bound with to-be-developed long-range variants of certain methods in
[

27
]
. Since super-ballistic LRBs belong to a different physical regime, we consider this a separate problem that we will consider in a separate work.

We achieve our results by further developing the analytic toolkit known as the ASTLO method, an acronym that stands for adiabatic space time localization observables developed in the many-body context in

[

, for the first time in the context of Lieb-Robinson bounds. We explain our proof strategy in more details after we present the setup and main theorems. We expect that the new methodology we develop here will be useful in related quantum problems in which both technical obstacles (unbounded and long-ranged) are present.

2.
Setup and Main Results

We fix a finite connected subset
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
which we will call lattice. The entire paper generalizes to any graph embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
which has polynomial volume growth and satisfies that any two distinct sites are separated by a distance greater than one.

In the following, we use the convenient Fock space formalism, also known as second quantization. Throughout, we work with a fixed particle number
N
. We emphasize that working in Fock space is completely equivalent and does not affect the difficulty of the mathematical problems we consider in any way; it is mainly useful because the algebra takes care of some bookkeeping.

We consider a system of
N
bosons whose positions lie in
\Lambda
a finite discrete subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Mathematically, we work on the Hilbert space that is defined as the bosonic Fock space

(2.1)

\mathcal{F}(\ell^{2}(\Lambda))=\bigoplus_{N=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{S}_{N}\left(\bigotimes_{j=1}^{N}\ell^{2}(\Lambda)\right),

where
\mathcal{S}_{N}
denotes the projection onto the permutation-symmetric subspace of
{\bigoplus_{N=1}^{\infty}\bigotimes_{j=1}^{N}\ell^{2}(\Lambda)}
.
On this Fock space, we consider a realization of the canonical commutation relations, i.e.,

[a_{x},a_{y}^{*}]=\delta_{x,y},\quad[a_{x}^{*},a_{y}^{*}]=[a_{x},a_{y}]=0,\qquad\forall x,y\in\Lambda.

On
\mathcal{F}(\ell^{2}(\Lambda))
, we consider Bose-Hubbard type Hamiltonians of the form

(2.2)

\displaystyle\boxed{H_{\Lambda}:=\sum_{x,y\in\Lambda}J_{xy}a_{x}^{*}a_{y}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in\Lambda\end{subarray}}V_{xy}a_{x}^{*}a_{y}^{*}a_{y}a_{x}.}

Here,

J=(J_{xy})
is a Hermitian
|\Lambda|\times|\Lambda|
matrix representing the energy of individual particles, and

V=(V_{xy})
is a real-symmetric matrix, i.e.
V_{xy}=\overline{V_{xy}}=V_{yx}
.

For background on the second quantization formalism, see e.g.
[
38
]
. Moreover,
[
14
, App. A]
proves that
H_{\Lambda}
is self-adjoint on
the dense domain

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(H_{\Lambda})=\{(\psi_{N})_{N\geq 0}\in\mathcal{F}(\ell^{2}(\Lambda))\,:\,\sum_{N\geq 0}\|H_{\Lambda}\psi_{N}\|^{2}<\infty\}.

We consider solutions
\psi_{t}
to the time-dependent many-body Schrödinger equation

(2.3)

\displaystyle i\partial_{t}\psi_{t}=H_{\Lambda}\psi_{t}\quad\textnormal{ on }\quad\mathcal{F}(\ell^{2}(\Lambda)),

with initial datum satisfying the well-posedness condition

(2.4)

\displaystyle\psi_{0}\in\mathcal{D}(H_{\Lambda})\cap\mathcal{D}(N_{\Lambda}^{p/2}),\quad p\geq 1.

Here and below, for any subset
X\subset\Lambda
, we define the particle number operator

(2.5)

\displaystyle N_{X}:=\sum_{x\in X}n_{x}.

Since
H_{\Lambda}
is self-adjoint, it generates a unitary group
e^{-\mathrm{i}tH_{\Lambda}}
on
F(\ell^{2}(\Lambda))
via Stone’s theorem. This unitary group encodes the solution operator to the PDE (
2.3
) and we will study its propagation properties. As usual, we will use a dual formulation in which the unitary acts by conjugation on operators (known as the “Heisenberg picture” in physics).

2.1.
Power-law decay assumption

Let
\alpha>d+1
and set

(2.6)

\displaystyle C_{J,\alpha}:=

\displaystyle\sup_{x,y\in\Lambda}\left\lvert J_{xy}\right\rvert(1+\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert)^{\alpha},

(2.7)

\displaystyle C_{V,\alpha}:=

\displaystyle\sup_{x,y\in\Lambda}\left\lvert V_{xy}\right\rvert(1+\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert)^{\alpha}.

These constants then capture the power-law decay because, for any
x\neq y
,

(2.8)

\displaystyle\left\lvert J_{xy}\right\rvert\leq C_{J,\alpha}\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert^{-\alpha},\qquad\left\lvert V_{xy}\right\rvert\leq C_{V,\alpha}\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert^{-\alpha}.

Our main results below depend on (
2.6

2.7
) for appropriate values of
\alpha

are

\Lambda
.

A central role is played by the first moment of the hopping matrix,

(2.9)

\kappa:=\sup_{x\in\Lambda}\sum_{y\in\Lambda}\left\lvert J_{xy}\right\rvert\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert.

By Schur’s test, it is easy to check that
\kappa
is an upper bound of the
1

Using
\left\lvert J_{xy}\right\rvert\leq C_{J,\alpha}(1+\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert)^{-\alpha}
for
\alpha>d+1
, we see that
\kappa
is bounded by a dimensional constant times
C_{J,\alpha}
, uniformly in
\Lambda
.

In the remainder of this paper we consider
\alpha
to be fixed and so we will drop the dependence of (
2.6
)–(
2.7
) on
\alpha
whenever no confusion arises.

Remark 2
.

(i)

The particle-free shell condition (

), which goes back to

, stipulates that there exists an empty shell region of width

.

(ii)

The particle-free shell condition can be dropped, but at the steep price of adding an additional term on the right-hand side of the form

{C_{1}\big\langle N_{X_{2\xi}\setminus X}N_{\Lambda}\big\rangle_{0}},\qquad C_{1}=C_{1}(\alpha,d,C_{J,\alpha},C_{V,\alpha},v,X).

See Section

for details. We do not discuss this extension further.

(iii)

The dependence on
X
in the constant

in these results comes from (

). The latter, in turn, depends only on the number of balls needed to cover the boundary of

; see the proof of Theorem

for details. By adjusting the geometric construction of the ASTLO, it is possible to remove the

-dependence altogether.

(iv)

In
[

]
, the authors obtained Lieb-Robinson bounds that are independent of the strength of

(i.e.,

from (

This maneuver utilizes the finite-range assumption in a fundamental way and is not expected to hold in the same way for long-range interactions. As our focus in the present paper is on long-range Hamiltonians, it is natural to expect that dependence on the interaction

enters in our bounds through

(v)

Through the technical lemmas in Section

Proof of Lemma
6.1

Since
A
and
B
commute, there exists a common orthonormal basis of eigenvectors
\left\{\phi_{\alpha}\right\}_{\alpha=1}^{n}
and relative eigenvalues,
\left\{\lambda_{\alpha}\right\},\,\left\{\mu_{\alpha}\right\}
, with
\lambda_{a},\mu_{\alpha}\geq 0
, such that

\displaystyle A\phi_{\alpha}=\lambda_{\alpha}\phi_{\alpha}\qquad B\phi_{\alpha}=\mu_{\alpha}\phi_{\alpha}.

Given a general

it can be written w.r.t. this basis as

\displaystyle\psi=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}c_{\alpha}\phi_{\alpha}\qquad\text{where }c_{\alpha}\coloneqq\big\langle\psi,\phi_{\alpha}\big\rangle.

This way we can write

\displaystyle\big\langle\psi,AB\psi\big\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{n}c_{\alpha}^{*}c_{\beta}\big\langle\phi_{\alpha},AB\phi_{\beta}\big\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{n}c_{\alpha}^{*}c_{\beta}\mu_{\beta}\lambda_{\beta}\big\langle\phi_{\alpha},\phi_{\beta}\big\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}\left\lvert c_{\alpha}\right\rvert^{2}\mu_{\alpha}\lambda_{\alpha}.

Since
p^{-1}+q^{-1}=1
, we can write
2=2/p+2/q
, using Hölder inequality for numbers, and the fact that
\mu_{\alpha},\lambda_{\alpha}\geq 0
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}\left\lvert c_{\alpha}\right\rvert^{2}\mu_{\alpha}\lambda_{\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}\left\lvert c_{\alpha}\right\rvert^{2}\mu^{p}_{\alpha}\right)^{1/p}\left(\sum_{\beta=1}^{n}\left\lvert c_{\beta}\right\rvert^{2}\lambda^{q}_{\alpha}\right)^{1/q}

\displaystyle=\big\langle\psi,A^{p}\psi\big\rangle^{1/p}\big\langle\psi,B^{q}\psi\big\rangle^{1/q}.

A.3.
Proof of Lemma
3.3

By the mean value theorem, for any
x\leq y
there exists
\xi\in[x,y]
s.th.

(A.18)

\displaystyle f(x)-f(y)=f^{\prime}(\xi)(x-y).

Write
f^{\prime}(\xi)=f^{\prime}(x)+(f^{\prime}(\xi)-f^{\prime}(x))
and
u:=\sqrt{f^{\prime}}
, equation (
A.18
) becomes

\displaystyle f(x)-f(y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(x)(x-y)+(f^{\prime}(\xi)-f^{\prime}(x))(x-y)

(A.19)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle u(x)u(y)(x-y)+u(x)\left(u(x)-u(y)\right)(x-y)+(f^{\prime}(\xi)-f^{\prime}(x))(x-y).

Recalling definition (
3.5
), we see that for every function
f\in\mathcal{E}
, the derivative
f^{\prime}
is compactly supported in
(1/2,1)
and Lipshitz, and similarly for
u\equiv\sqrt{f^{\prime}}
. It follows that the Hölder semi-norm

(A.20)

\displaystyle\left\lvert g\right\rvert_{0,\epsilon}\leq\left\lvert g\right\rvert_{0,1},\qquad g\in\left\{f^{\prime},u\right\},\,0<\epsilon\leq 1.

Inserting (
A.20
) into (
A.19
) yields

\displaystyle\left\lvert f(x)-f(y)\right\rvert\leq u(x)u(y)\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert+C_{f}\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert^{1+\epsilon}\qquad 0<\epsilon\leq 1,

with

(A.21)

\displaystyle C_{f}:=\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\left\lvert u\right\rvert_{0,\epsilon}+\left\lvert f^{\prime}\right\rvert_{0,\epsilon}.

The desired estimate (
3.13
) follows from here.
∎
