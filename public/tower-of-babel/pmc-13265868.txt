Conjoint tendon split approach for humeral head extraction in acute anterior-to-inferior glenoid rim–incarceration fracture-dislocation and chronic locked anterior dislocation
Authors: Naoya Hirosawa, Kazuki Kuniyoshi, Daisuke Kajiwara, Nobuyasu Ochiai, Hiroyuki Sugaya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn acute anterior fracture-dislocation with humeral head incarceration at the anterior-to-inferior glenoid and in chronic locked anterior shoulder dislocation, the humeral head often lies immediately beneath the conjoint tendon, and a standard deltopectoral approach may provide insufficient working space for safe humeral head exposure and extraction. We evaluated the feasibility and neurologic safety of the conjoint tendon split approach (CSA) used as an adjunct for humeral head extraction in these challenging conditions. We hypothesized that CSA would facilitate humeral head extraction under direct visualization without increasing the risk of new postoperative musculocutaneous or axillary nerve deficits.MethodsThis single-center retrospective case series included 11 consecutive patients (11 shoulders) in whom CSA was used as an adjunct for humeral head extraction and who had ≥6 months of follow-up. Patients were classified as acute (within 3 weeks with bony incarceration of the humeral head or head fragment at the anterior-to-inferior glenoid on computed tomography) or chronic (≥3 weeks with locked, functionally irreducible anterior dislocation because of scarring and capsular contracture). Primary outcomes were feasibility (successful humeral head extraction using CSA) and neurologic safety (new musculocutaneous or axillary nerve deficits at final follow-up). Data are reported as median (range) or number (percentage).ResultsThe median age was 84 years (59-88), 10 patients were female (90.9%), and median body mass index was 23.0 kg/m2 (20.4-31.6). Eight shoulders were acute and 3 were chronic. Reverse total shoulder arthroplasty was performed in 9 shoulders (81.8%) and hemiarthroplasty in 2 (18.2%). Pre-operative neurologic palsy was present in 2 patients (18.2%) (axillary nerve, n = 1; radial nerve, n = 1), with no musculocutaneous nerve palsy; both resolved during follow-up. CSA enabled successful humeral head exposure, mobilization, and extraction in all cases (feasibility, 100%). Median final follow-up was 13 months (6-36), and no new post-operative musculocutaneous or axillary nerve deficits infections, or early implant-related complications were observed.ConclusionIn this series, CSA provided reliable access for humeral head extraction in acute anterior-to-inferior glenoid incarceration fracture-dislocation and chronic locked anterior dislocation, without new musculocutaneous or axillary nerve deficits.

Proximal humeral fractures and anterior shoulder dislocation are common injuries encountered in routine clinical practice, particularly after low-energy trauma in older adults.10,15,19 In comminuted 3-part/4-part fractures and fracture-dislocations, poor fixation and humeral head osteonecrosis are more likely because of osteoporosis and compromised humeral head perfusion, making hemiarthroplasty or reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (rTSA) viable treatment options.9,26,27

Conversely, among anterior fracture-dislocations, there is a distinct pathologic condition in which the humeral head (or a humeral head fragment) is deeply impacted into the anterior-to-inferior glenoid rim, rendering closed reduction in the emergency department difficult and allowing reduction attempts to further exacerbate the fracture.1,8,14,30 In addition, in chronic anterior shoulder dislocation, progressive humeral head deformity and bone loss, along with scarring and contracture of the capsule and surrounding soft tissues, develop over time, often necessitating open reduction or arthroplasty.7,11,20,22 In these “difficult exposure/difficult reduction” cases, the humeral head is frequently located deep in the anterior aspect immediately beneath the conjoint tendon, and a standard deltopectoral approach alone often provides insufficient working space directly above the humeral head, forcing predominantly tactile maneuvers. Excessive traction or levering can increase the risk of secondary fracture and nerve injury,1,25 and in chronic cases, contracted anterior soft tissues may further impede mobilization.24

However, for pathologies in which the humeral head (or a humeral head fragment) is incarcerated and fixed along the anterior-to-inferior glenoid rim, reports that practically organize and present specific anterior approaches and techniques to safely and reliably expose and extract the humeral head under direct visualization remain limited.12 Therefore, beyond the choice of definitive procedure, introducing an adjunctive technique that ensures dependable access to the humeral head and safe extraction has substantial clinical value. Against this background, we have used the conjoint tendon split approach (CSA), in which the conjoint tendon is longitudinally split in a limited manner along its fiber direction to provide direct access to the humeral head located immediately beneath it. This approach preserves continuity with the standard deltopectoral approach while enabling short-distance, direct-visualization exposure of the humeral head under the conjoint tendon, and we considered it a more practical anterior exposure technique for these challenging conditions. The purpose of this study was to retrospectively analyze a consecutive series of patients with acute anterior fracture-dislocation with humeral head impaction into the anterior-to-inferior glenoid and chronic anterior shoulder dislocation in whom CSA was used as an adjunct, and to clarify: (1) the pathologic background that necessitated selection of CSA, (2) the extent to which humeral head exposure and extraction were reliably achieved (feasibility), and (3) neurological complications, particularly involving the musculocutaneous and axillary nerves (safety). We hypothesized that CSA would provide reliable direct-visualization access for humeral head extraction without causing new post-operative musculocutaneous or axillary nerve deficits.

We included 11 consecutive patients (11 shoulders) treated at our institution in whom CSA was used as an adjunct for humeral head extraction and who were available for follow-up for at least 6 months post-operatively. Pathoanatomic classification and indications for conjoint tendon split approach

Based on pre-operative computed tomography (CT) findings and time from injury, patients were classified into 2 groups. The acute group was defined as an anterior fracture-dislocation within 3 weeks of injury in which the humeral head or head fragment was bony incarcerated at the anterior-to-inferior glenoid on CT (acute glenoid-incarceration fracture-dislocation).24 The chronic group was defined as cases ≥3 weeks after injury presenting with a locked dislocation that was functionally irreducible because of scarring of the anterior soft tissues and capsular contracture, confirmed by CT and intraoperative findings (chronic locked anterior dislocation).24,29 The 3-week threshold was adopted from definitions used in prior reports on chronic anterior dislocation and was applied as an operative definition for the present analysis.24,29 In both conditions, the humeral head was located deep in the anterior aspect, directly beneath the conjoint tendon. Because we judged that a standard deltopectoral approach alone would provide insufficient working space directly over the humeral head, making direct visualization and extraction difficult, CSA was used in all cases.

Mechanism of injury

Mechanism of injury was categorized based on chart documentation into 3 types: walking fall, bicycle fall, and unwitnessed fall in a care facility. All cases were treated as low-energy trauma (definitions according to Supplementary Table I).

Pre-operative neurologic assessment

Pre-operative neurologic assessment focused on the musculocutaneous and axillary nerves was performed by the treating surgeons who were shoulder-specialized orthopedic surgeons experienced in shoulder examination. Neurologic status was determined using routine clinical judgment on the basis of light-touch sensation in the respective cutaneous territories and motor findings, including deltoid and biceps contraction using manual muscle testing.13,21 In addition, screening of the radial, median, and ulnar nerves was performed. Pre-operative neurologic palsy was defined as the presence of sensory disturbance on light-touch testing or a clear decrease in manual muscle testing strength and was recorded as yes/no in Table I.

Pre-operative imaging and fracture classification

All patients underwent pre-operative shoulder radiography (true anteroposterior and scapular Y views) and CT. In the acute group, proximal humeral fracture patterns were classified according to the Neer classification and the Arbeitsgemeinschaft für Osteosynthesefragen/Orthopaedic Trauma Association (AO/OTA) classification. Given that the utility of CT for fracture classification remains under discussion,3 we referenced CT findings for classification in all cases. In the acute group, 3 Hertel ischemia risk predictors—calcar segment length <8 mm, medial hinge disruption >2 mm, and a head-split and/or an anatomic neck component—were assessed on preoperative CT as yes/no4,9 and are presented in Table II. In acute cases, CT was used to confirm anterior-to-inferior glenoid incarceration of the humeral head (or head fragment). Surgical technique

Setup and approach

All procedures were performed with the patient in a slightly head-up position of approximately 30° (Figs. 1 and 2). A standard deltopectoral approach was used, and the cephalic vein was retracted medially to avoid inadvertent injury during subsequent manipulation. Access through the conjoint tendon interval

A subtle bulge corresponding to the humeral head (or head fragment) is identified deep to the conjoint tendon. Only the superficial aponeurotic layer of the conjoint tendon is incised longitudinally over the bulge (approximately 3-4 cm) (Figs. 1B and 2B). Blunt dissection is performed in line with the muscle fibers to separate the muscular components of the conjoint tendon (the short head of the biceps and the coracobrachialis), thereby exposing the humeral head (or head fragment). If the musculocutaneous nerve is unexpectedly encountered within the interval, it can be preserved because dissection is performed bluntly along the fiber direction. In our series, the musculocutaneous nerve did not appear in the operative field in any case.

Mobilization and extraction of the humeral head fragment

If the subscapularis tendon remains attached, no. 2 Ethibond traction (stay) sutures are placed in the tendon (Figs. 1B and 2C). The sutures are then passed laterally around the conjoint tendon and gently tensioned to translate the fragment outward, enlarging the working space directly over the humeral head and facilitating controlled mobilization and extraction (Figs. 1B and 2D). If no subscapularis attachment remains, the fragment is directly grasped and removed through the same interval. In chronic anterior shoulder dislocation, the humeral head is first delivered through the split interval, and preliminary humeral head osteotomy is performed to permit subsequent release of the inferior capsule and surrounding adhesions, which facilitates controlled reduction maneuvers and improves exposure for the subsequent arthroplasty procedure (Fig. 2E). Because the superficial aponeurotic layer of the conjoint tendon is incised longitudinally in line with the fiber direction, repair of the conjoint tendon is not required.

Post-operative management and outcome measures

No post-operative immobilization was used, and range-of-motion exercises were initiated early within the limits of pain tolerance. The primary outcome measures were feasibility, defined as successful humeral head extraction using CSA, and safety, defined as the presence or absence of new musculocutaneous or axillary nerve deficits. At final follow-up, neurologic status was reassessed by the treating surgeons using the same clinical examination protocol as pre-operatively to determine whether any new post-operative neurologic deficits were present. Because baseline characteristics and pre-operative function varied substantially and the purpose of this study was to evaluate the feasibility and safety of the approach, standardized outcome scores such as the American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons score and the Constant score were not included in the analysis.

Statistical analysis and ethical considerations

Given the limited sample size and the absence of a comparative control group, data were summarized using descriptive statistics. Continuous variables are presented as median (range), and categorical variables as number (percentage). This study was approved by the institutional review board of our hospital (approval No. NCH2025-02), and informed consent was obtained using an opt-out process through institutional public notice in accordance with the requirements for retrospective research.

Results

Eleven consecutive patients (11 shoulders) treated with adjunctive humeral head extraction using CSA and followed for ≥6 months were analyzed (Table I). The median age was 84 years (range, 59-88); 10 patients were female (90.9%). Median body mass index was 23.0 kg/m2 (20.4-31.6). The right shoulder was involved in 5 cases (45.5%). The median interval from injury/dislocation to surgery was 8 days (1-300). All injuries were low-energy falls (Table I; Supplementary Table I).

Eight cases were classified as acute and three as chronic (Table I). In the acute group, fracture patterns and ischemia-related radiographic characteristics are summarized in Table II. Briefly, Neer classification was 3-part in 3 cases (37.5%) and 4-part in 5 cases (62.5%), whereas AO/OTA classification was 11C31 in 7 cases (87.5%) and 11C32 in 1 case (12.5%). All acute cases demonstrated the Hertel ischemia-related predictors assessed in this study (Table II). Closed reduction had been attempted in the emergency department in 1 acute case (12.5%) (Table II).

rTSA was performed in 9 shoulders (81.8%) and hemiarthroplasty in 2 (18.2%): the acute group included 6 rTSA and 2 hemiarthroplasties, whereas all chronic cases underwent rTSA (Table I). All arthroplasties were performed using the Equinoxe Shoulder System (Advita Ortho, Inc., Gainesville, FL, USA).

Pre-operative neurologic palsy was present in 2 patients (18.2%)—axillary nerve in 1 and radial nerve in 1—with no musculocutaneous nerve palsy (Table I). Both palsies resolved during follow-up.

CSA enabled successful humeral head exposure, mobilization, and extraction in all cases (feasibility, 100%). No intraoperative new nerve injury, vascular injury, or excessive bleeding occurred. At final follow-up, clinical assessment revealed no new post-operative musculocutaneous or axillary nerve deficits. The median final follow-up was 13 months (6-36) (Table I). No infections or early implant-related complications occurred during follow-up.

Discussion

In this study, adding CSA to a standard deltopectoral approach enabled successful exposure, mobilization, and extraction of the humeral head in all cases of difficult anterior pathology, including acute anterior fracture-dislocation with anteroinferior glenoid incarceration of the humeral head and chronic locked anterior dislocation. The cohort comprised fractures in which the humeral head (or head fragment) was incarcerated anterior or inferior to the glenoid, as well as long-standing chronic anterior dislocations—conditions in which a deltopectoral approach alone was anticipated to provide limited working space directly over the humeral head and to make safe extraction difficult. Despite these challenges, no permanent neurologic deficits, including new musculocutaneous or axillary nerve palsy, were observed intraoperatively or post-operatively. Thus, within this series, CSA demonstrated feasibility and neurologic safety.

The focus of this study was not the definitive choice of procedure, but rather a surgical bottleneck in these pathologies: how to secure a direct-visualization working space immediately over the humeral head to complete extraction safely when the humeral head is deeply incarcerated anteriorly or fixed by scarring and contracture.12,18,29 In acute cases, bony incarceration and the risk of fracture propagation with reduction attempts are concerns,8,30 whereas in chronic cases, scarring and contracture of the anterior soft tissues can impede mobilization.5,29 Specific stepwise anterior exposure techniques for these difficult conditions have been reported only sporadically.12,18,22 The present series is meaningful in that it clarified the pathologic background that necessitated CSA and demonstrated its feasibility and neurologic safety.

A first reason CSA was effective in this series is that it allowed creation of a “straight anterior tunnel” directly above the humeral head beneath the conjoint tendon. With a conventional deltopectoral approach, the working space is often constrained by the lateral border of the conjoint tendon. In contrast, CSA longitudinally splits the conjoint tendon beneath the coracoid process near its midline, and entry through the split interval shifts the working space approximately 1 cm medially, directly over the humeral head. Importantly, in “rigid” conditions such as acute bony incarceration or chronic scar contracture, this difference can translate into a decisive improvement in visualization and instrument maneuverability. Consistent with this concept, studies of glenoid exposure during shoulder arthroplasty have emphasized that even small degrees of humeral translation or additional release can produce only millimeters of increased space, yet still influence instrument handling and the accuracy of glenoid component placement.16 The approximately 1 cm increase in working space provided by CSA may therefore be clinically meaningful and aligns with these observations.

Second, CSA can facilitate stepwise management of anterior soft-tissue contracture, which is a major obstacle in chronic cases, by allowing direct access from immediately over the humeral head. Prior reports have described that interposition and tethering of soft tissues, including the subscapularis, can be factors that prevent reduction in irreducible anterior dislocation.2,11 In our chronic cases, adding CSA provided direct visualization and mobilization of the humeral head, and performing an initial in situ osteotomy enabled safe progression of scar release and transition to rTSA. Within the scope of our data, the key point is that a preliminary osteotomy facilitated subsequent reduction and reconstruction; this study does not compare CSA with alternative concepts such as coracoid transfer procedures.

Third, CSA may offer an anatomic safety advantage with respect to the musculocutaneous nerve. Multiple anatomic studies have demonstrated variability in the course of the musculocutaneous nerve and the safe zone around the coracoid region.17,23 Accordingly, careful dissection based on anatomic landmarks is essential to avoid nerve injury during anterior shoulder and pericoracoid procedures.6,23 CSA is based on longitudinal incision of the aponeurosis and blunt splitting along the direction of the muscle fibers, which may help minimize excessive medial traction stress. Furthermore, because neurologic injury associated with shoulder dislocation has been reported at a certain frequency,6,13,21 defining neurologic safety as a primary outcome, as in this study, is reasonable for a report addressing challenging cases.

This study has limitations. First, it is a small, single-center retrospective case series without a comparison group. Second, neurologic assessment was based on surgeon-performed clinical examination, including light-touch sensory testing, and electrodiagnostic studies were not routinely obtained. Third, patient-reported outcome measures and standardized functional outcomes were not evaluated; therefore, this study does not permit conclusions regarding patient-perceived benefit or broader post-operative shoulder function. In addition, baseline characteristics and pre-operative function varied substantially across patients, and the cohort included both acute fracture-dislocations and chronic dislocations. Finally, although the value of CT for fracture classification and interobserver reliability has been debated,3 this study was not designed to assess classification reproducibility. Nevertheless, this consecutive series suggests that for acute anterior fracture-dislocation with anteroinferior glenoid incarceration of the humeral head and for chronic locked anterior dislocation, CSA may serve as a useful adjunct that simplifies humeral head extraction while maintaining neurologic safety.

Conclusion

In this consecutive series, the CSA served as a practical adjunct to the deltopectoral approach to provide direct-visualization access for humeral head exposure and extraction in acute anterior-to-inferior glenoid rim incarceration fracture-dislocation and chronic locked anterior dislocation. No new musculocutaneous or axillary nerve deficits occurred during follow-up. Larger comparative studies with standardized functional outcomes are warranted to further define the role of this approach.
