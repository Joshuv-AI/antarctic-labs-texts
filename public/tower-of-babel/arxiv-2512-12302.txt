From Human Intention to Action Prediction: A Comprehensive Benchmark for Intention-driven End-to-End Autonomous Driving
Huan Zheng, Yucheng Zhou, Tianyi Yan, Jiayi Su, Hongjun Chen, Dubing Chen, Wencheng Han, Runzhou Tao, Zhongying Qiu, Jianfei Yang, Jianbing Shen

Abstract
Current end-to-end autonomous driving systems operate at a level of intelligence akin to following simple steering commands. However, achieving genuinely intelligent autonomy requires a paradigm shift: moving from merely executing low-level instructions to understanding and fulfilling high-level, abstract human intentions. This leap from a command-follower to an intention-fulfiller, as illustrated in our conceptual framework, is hindered by a fundamental challenge: the absence of a standardized benchmark to measure and drive progress on this complex task. To address this critical gap, we introduce Intention-Drive, the first comprehensive benchmark designed to evaluate the ability to translate high-level human intent into safe and precise driving actions. Intention-Drive features two core contributions: (1) a new dataset of complex scenarios paired with corresponding natural language intentions, and (2) a novel evaluation protocol centered on the Intent Success Rate (ISR), which assesses the semantic fulfillment of the human’s goal beyond simple geometric accuracy. Through an extensive evaluation of a spectrum of baseline models on Intention-Drive, we reveal a significant performance deficit, showing that the baseline model struggle to achieve the comprehensive scene and intention understanding required for this advanced task.

3
Intention-Drive Benchmark

3.1
Task Definition

We formally define the task of intention-driven end-to-end autonomous driving.
The primary objective is to generate a safe, feasible, and contextually appropriate driving trajectory that semantically satisfies a high-level human intention expressed in natural language.
This task requires an agent to move beyond simple navigational command following and instead perform complex reasoning that grounds linguistic concepts in the dynamic, multi-modal driving environment.

Formally, let the state of the environment at timestep
t
be represented by a set of multi-modal sensor observations
O_{t}
, including multi-view camera images
\{I_{t}^{1},\dots,I_{t}^{N}\}
and LiDAR point clouds
L_{t}
.
Given a history of observations over a time horizon
H
, denoted as
\mathcal{O}_{t-H:t}
, and a high-level human intention articulated as a natural language instruction
I_{\text{lang}}
, the goal is to learn a policy
\pi
that predicts a future driving trajectory
T_{t+1:t+K}
.
The trajectory consists of
K
waypoints in the bird’s-eye-view (BEV) coordinate frame,
T_{t+1:t+K}=\{p_{t+1},\dots,p_{t+K}\}
, where each waypoint is defined as
p_{k}=(x_{k},y_{k})
. The policy is parameterized by
\theta
and can be expressed as:

T_{t+1:t+K}=\pi(\mathcal{O}_{t-H:t},I_{\text{lang}}|\theta)

(1)

Input:
A tuple
(\mathcal{O}_{t-H:t},I_{\text{lang}})
, where
\mathcal{O}_{t-H:t}
is a sequence of multi-modal sensor data capturing the recent history of the driving scene, and
I_{\text{lang}}
is a free-form natural language string describing the human intention.

Output:
A future trajectory
T_{t+1:t+K}
for the ego-vehicle, represented as a sequence of
K
2D waypoints.

This task presents several fundamental challenges that distinguish it from conventional end-to-end driving.
First, it demands robust semantic grounding, requiring the model to associate abstract linguistic concepts (e.g., “the blue building”, “behind the truck”, “a safe spot”) with their corresponding physical entities and spatial relationships in the 3D world.
Second, it necessitates sophisticated compositional reasoning to deconstruct complex instructions into a sequence of executable driving maneuvers.
Finally, evaluating success cannot rely solely on geometric metrics like L2 distance to a ground-truth trajectory, as multiple distinct paths could validly fulfill the same intention.
This highlights the critical need for evaluation protocols that assess the semantic fulfillment of the specified goal, a core focus of our proposed benchmark.

3.2
Dataset

To systematically evaluate the ability to comprehend and execute high-level human intentions, a dedicated and comprehensive benchmark is essential.
Existing datasets primarily focus on trajectory prediction from low-level navigational commands or sensor data alone, lacking the rich, abstract, and context-dependent language that characterizes intention-driven driving.
To bridge this critical gap, we introduce Intention-Drive, a multi-modal benchmark specifically designed for the task of intention-driven autonomous driving.
The construction of Intention-Drive is centered on enriching realistic driving scenarios with a hierarchical set of natural language instructions, spanning from simple atomic commands to complex, abstract human intentions.

3.2.1
Data Collection

The creation of the Intention-Drive dataset follows a principled, multi-stage pipeline designed to generate realistic and diverse intention-action pairs.
Our process leverages OpenScene dataset
27
for authentic driving scenarios and employs advanced Large Language Models (LLMs) for hierarchical language annotation.
The pipeline consists of the following key steps:

Sourcing Foundational Driving Scenarios.

Our data collection process begins with sourcing high-fidelity, real-world driving scenarios from the large-scale OpenScene dataset
27
, where 1359 and 600 scenarios are selected for training and testing, respectively.
We selected OpenScene as our foundation due to its diverse range of complex urban environments, comprehensive multi-modal sensor data, and accurate ground-truth annotations, including the ego-vehicle’s trajectory.
For each selected scenario, we extract a temporal sequence of synchronized sensor inputs and the corresponding future trajectory of the ego-vehicle, which serves as the ground-truth driving action.

We employ InternVL3.0-2B as the baseline model to evaluate the Intention-Drive benchmark.

Hierarchical Instruction Generation.

To generate a rich spectrum of language instructions that reflect varying levels of abstraction, we devised a hierarchical annotation strategy powered by an advanced VLM.
This process synthesizes two distinct levels of linguistic instructions for each driving scenario, ensuring a comprehensive evaluation of a model’s language understanding capabilities, as shown in Figure
2
.
Specifically, we designed a pipeline to obtain the instructions:

•

Low-Level Atomic Instructions:
We first define the most granular, standardized driving primitives representing the fundamental actions of the ego vehicle.
For each timestamp, a VLM is employed to infer the primary maneuver from a predefined vocabulary of atomic actions, such as
Straight
,
Turn Left
,
Change Lane Right
, and
Stop
.
Concurrently, a speed status (e.g.,
Accelerate
,
Decelerate
,
Maintain Speed
) is assigned, reflecting the vehicle’s instantaneous speed profile.
This initial layer ensures a precise and unambiguous representation of the vehicle’s movement at the most basic level, forming the foundational understanding for subsequent high-level generations.

•

High-Level Human Intentions:
Next, leveraging the comprehensive understanding derived from low-level atomic instructions, alongside the multi-modal sensor inputs, these instructions capture natural language utterances from a passenger’s perspective.
These represent abstract goals or observations that require a higher degree of contextual reasoning and semantic interpretation.
This high level focuses on mirroring the intuitive way humans communicate driving intentions, often blending direct commands with observations or warnings, and critically depends on the consistent and semantically rich information provided by the preceding layer.

Data Verification and Refinement.

To ensure the quality, relevance, and consistency of the generated language annotations, we implemented a rigorous verification process.
After the hierarchical generation of low-level atomic instructions and high-level human intentions, these multi-layered instructions undergo a crucial consistency check using an advanced VLM.
Specifically, for each driving scenario, the VLM is tasked with evaluating the logical coherence and semantic alignment among the two generated instruction levels.
Any inconsistencies identified by the VLM trigger a refinement loop, where human annotators review the problematic segment and make necessary corrections.

3.3
Baseline

To systematically evaluate the proposed Intention-Drive benchmark, we establish a strong baseline.
Unlike traditional E2E models that rely on simple steering command, the intention-fulfilling task demands a model capable of deep semantic grounding, mapping abstract linguistic concepts to specific visual features and geometric actions.
To this end, we adopt InternVL3.0-2B
48
as our foundational architecture, which integrates a powerful vision encoder with a LLM. The overall pipeline is illustrated in Figure
3
.

Visual-Linguistic Encoding.

Perceiving fine-grained scene details is a prerequisite for understanding complex human intentions.
As shown in Figure
3
(a), the baseline utilizes a Vision Transformer (ViT) to encode the front-view driving image or video sequences.
To preserve spatial fidelity, the visual features undergo a Pixel Unshuffle operation followed by an MLP projector, converting high-resolution images into a sequence of compact visual tokens.
Simultaneously, the high-level human intention
I_{\text{lang}}
is tokenized via a text tokenizer.
These visual and textual tokens are concatenated to form a unified multimodal input sequence, projecting the driving scene and user intent into a shared embedding space.

LLM-based Reasoning and Planning.

The core decision-making module is powered by Qwen2.5, a state-of-the-art LLM.
By initializing with weights from ReCogDrive
16
, the model inherits a preliminary understanding of driving physics.
In the context of Intention-Drive, the LLM functions as a reasoning agent that interprets the fused multimodal inputs.
It explicitly models the causal relationship between the visual observation and the linguistic intention.

Trajectory as Language Generation.

Our baseline is the formulation of planning as an auto-regressive token generation task.
The model outputs the future trajectory
T_{t+1:t+K}
directly in text format.
As depicted in Figure
3
(b), the system is prompted to generate a response structured as: “Here is the planning trajectory [PT,
(x_{1},y_{1}),\dots,(x_{K},y_{K})
]”.
These textual coordinates represent the waypoints in the BEV coordinate system, which are subsequently parsed into numerical values for execution.

3.4
Evaluation Protocol

To comprehensively assess the performance of autonomous agents in intention-driven scenarios, we propose a two-fold evaluation protocol. This protocol distinguishes between the basic physical execution of driving (Safety & Geometric Metrics) and the semantic fulfillment of the user’s high-level goals (Intent Success Rate).

Safety & Geometric Metrics
. Before an agent can fulfill complex intentions, it must ensure basic driving safety and geometric stability. We employ two standard metrics to evaluate this fundamental capability:

•

Average Displacement Error (ADE):
This metric measures the geometric fidelity of the predicted trajectory. It is calculated as the average
L_{2}
distance between the predicted waypoints and the ground truth trajectory over the time horizon. While a low ADE indicates stable motion generation akin to the human demonstration, it is insufficient alone for intention evaluation, as multiple distinct paths may validly fulfill the same abstract intention.

•

Collision Rate (CR):
This serves as a hard safety constraint. It is defined as the percentage of test scenarios where the ego-vehicle collides with any environmental obstacles or road boundaries. A collision represents a critical failure of the driving system.

Intent Success Rate (ISR)
. Standard geometric metrics fail to capture the semantic validity of a trajectory (e.g., a car might drive safely but park in the wrong spot). To address this, we introduce the Intent Success Rate (ISR) as the primary metric for our benchmark.
ISR assesses whether the agent successfully translates the natural language intention into a correct driving maneuver. A scenario is considered a “Success” if and only if the generated trajectory meets two hierarchical conditions simultaneously.

•

Safety Condition(
C_{safe}
): The trajectory is collision-free and kinematically feasible.

•

Semantic Condition (
C_{sem}
): The trajectory logically satisfies the high-level human intention. This is verified via an automated VLM-based assessment.

Formally, the ISR is calculated as:

\displaystyle ISR=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Pi(C_{safe}^{(i)}\wedge C_{sum}^{(i)}),

(2)

where
N
is the number of scenarios and
\wedge
denotes the logical AND operation. This ensures that a trajectory is counted as successful if it is both safe and semantically correct.

Experiments

4.1
Implementation Details

We utilize InternVL3.0-2B
48
as our baseline model, initializing it with the pre-trained weights from the first stage of ReCogDrive
16
.
The model is fine-tuned using the AdamW optimizer with a learning rate of
2\times 10^{-5}
and a weight decay of
0.05
. We employ a cosine learning rate scheduler with a warmup ratio of
0.03
.
The training is conducted for 1 epoch with a total batch size of 4 distributed across 4 GPUs.
To process high-resolution driving scenes, we utilize a dynamic image size strategy with a base resolution of
448\times 448
and a maximum of 16 dynamic patches.
For parameter efficiency, we apply Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) with a rank of
r=32
to both the vision backbone and the LLM.
The training process is optimized using DeepSpeed with BF16 precision and gradient checkpointing.

Safety and Geometric Stability.

Conclusion

In this work, we identify and address a critical limitation in current end-to-end autonomous driving systems: the inability to transcend basic steering command following to achieve true intention fulfillment.
To bridge this gap, we introduce Intention-Drive, a novel paradigm and benchmark designed to evaluate the capability to understand and execute high-level, abstract human intentions in complex dynamic scenarios.
By constructing a new dataset enriched with hierarchical language instructions and proposing the Intent Success Rate (ISR) metric, we provide the first standardized framework for assessing the semantic consistency between human goals and vehicle actions.
Our experiments reveal a critical disparity: while the baseline models demonstrate robust kinematic safety, they struggle significantly with semantic intention understanding.
