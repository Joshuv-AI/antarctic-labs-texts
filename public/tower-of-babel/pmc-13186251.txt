The co-occurrence of obesity with cancer-related fatigue and their impact on physical function among breast cancer survivors
Authors: Lindsey J Mattick, Po-Ju Lin, Francisco Cartujano-Barrera, Deborah J Ossip, Umang Gada, Allison M Magnuson, Luke J Peppone, Jeremy McGuire, Judith O Hopkins, Janos Molnar, J Scott Maul, Karen M Mustian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurpose:To evaluate the associations of obesity and cancer-related fatigue (CRF) among breast cancer survivors, and the impact of their co-occurrence on physical function.Methods:In a nationwide randomized clinical trial, we assessed body mass index (BMI; kg/m2), CRF via the Brief Fatigue Inventory (BFI), and physical function via hand grip dynamometry (muscular) and 6-minute walk test (cardiorespiratory) among breast cancer survivors at baseline.Results:Among 456 participants with a breast cancer diagnosis (stage I–IIIC, mean age 55.9 years, 23.5 months post first-treatment, 86% white), 43.9% have obesity (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2), and 32.7% have overweight (BMI 25–29.9 kg/m2). Compared to those with a normal weight, survivors with obesity are more than twice as likely to experience moderate-severe CRF (BFI total score ≥ 4; OR [95%CI] = 2.21 [1.35–3.63]). Survivors with co-occurring overweight/obesity and moderate-severe CRF have significantly worse cardiorespiratory function than those with either condition alone.Discussion:Overweight/obesity and CRF are highly prevalent among breast cancer survivors. Survivors with obesity experience worse CRF compared to those with normal weight. Survivors with co-occurring overweight/obesity and severe CRF experience worse cardiorespiratory function, and this persists post-diagnosis. Given the long-term detrimental impact of these conditions on QOL and survival, these findings underscore the need for targeted interventions.Keywords: Breast cancer, obesity, cancer-related fatigue, survivorship

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most common cancer among women globally, and one in eight women in the United States will develop the disease during their lifetime [1,2]. Owing to advances in treatment and early detection, 90% of women diagnosed with breast cancer survive for at least five years post-diagnosis [2]. As survival rates continue to improve, there is an increasing need to focus on improving the long-term quality of life for these individuals.

Studies indicate that over 63% of patients with breast cancer gain weight at an average of 20 lbs in the first 8 years post-diagnosis [3,4] – a rate 2.5–5 times greater than that of women without cancer [3,5]. In addition, more than half of breast cancer survivors experience chronic cancer-related fatigue (CRF), a distressing, persistent, and subjective sense of tiredness or exhaustion that is disproportionate to recent activity, not relieved by rest or sleep, and interferes with usual functioning [6]. This condition can persist for years into survivorship [7–9].

CRF and obesity are commonly co-occurring and separately linked to impaired physical function, which limits daily activities such as personal hygiene, meal preparation, physical activity, work, and maintaining relationships with friends and family [10–13]. Beyond impacting overall quality of life, the development of obesity, CRF, and impaired physical function are also associated with higher all-cause mortality among breast cancer survivors [14,15].

These conditions are further exacerbated by adjuvant endocrine therapies, which are prescribed to over 80% of breast cancer survivors, and are associated witho both CRF and weight gain [16–18]. While the standard duration for adjuvant endocrine therapies was previously up to 5 years post-diagnosis, updated clinical guidelines now recommend extending treatment up to 10 years post-diagnosis to further reduce the risk of recurrence [19,20]. As a result, survivors are left in a difficult position – balancing the benefits of prolonged adjuvant endocrine therapy to prevent recurrence against the ongoing side effects that significantly impact their quality of life [21]. Despite the well-documented prevalence and consequences of obesity, its co-occurrence with CRF and impaired physical function among breast cancer survivors has received limited research attention, though it is better-studied among patients receiving primary treatment [22–25].

Understanding the risk factors contributing to functional impairment and how they interact is therefore critical. We hypothesize that CRF and obesity may interact synergistically to worsen physical function, particularly cardiorespiratory and muscular capacity. Identifying such synergistic contributors could enhance risk prediction, guide more effective clinical care, and support the development of tailored interventions. To address this gap, we analyzed data from a nationwide randomized clinical trial of breast cancer survivors to assess the co-occurrence of CRF and obesity and their combined effects on cardiorespiratory and muscular function.

Materials and methods

Study design

This is a secondary analysis of baseline measures from 456 breast cancer survivors recruited as part of a nationwide randomized clinical trial.

Study population

We conducted a multi-center, blinded, phase III randomized clinical trial aimed to compare the effectiveness of Yoga for Cancer Survivors (YOCAS©®), Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Insomnia (CBT-I), and a behavioral placebo survivorship health education (SHE) to treat insomnia. Adult cancer survivors (n = 740) were recruited from 23 community oncology practices across the United States, which were affiliated with the University of Rochester Cancer Center NCI Community Oncology Research Program (URCC NCORP) Research Base (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT00397930) [26,27]. Survivors were recruited directly by their community oncology practice in cohorts of 15–30 people, and then all members of the cohort were randomized. All baseline assessments took place prior to randomization.

To be eligible for the initial trial, the participants must: (1) have a diagnosis of cancer (no distant metastasis), (2) have received surgery, chemotherapy, and/or radiation therapy, (3) have completed primary treatment within the last 2–60 months, (4) have insomnia, (5) be at least 18 years of age, (6) have no contraindications to functional testing or yoga, (7) not be currently practicing yoga or planning to start, and (8) not have sleep apnea or restless leg syndrome.

Among the 537 trial participants with breast cancer, 68 did not complete physical function tests, nine did not complete self-reported CRF measures, and four had underweight (body mass index [BMI] < 18.5 kg/m2) and were therefore excluded from the final analyses, resulting in a final analytic sample of 456 (Figure 1).

Baseline study measures

Anthropometric data were measured by trained study staff, including height (m) and weight (kg). BMI was calculated as weight divided by height squared (kg/m2). A BMI of 18.5–24.9 kg/m2 was considered normal weight. Those with a BMI of 25.0–29.9 kg/m2 or ≥ 30 kg/m2 were classified with overweight and obesity, respectively.

CRF was measured via two validated questionnaires. The Brief Fatigue Inventory (BFI) is a validated 9-item, 11-point rating scale questionnaire. The BFI is a widely accepted and succinct tool to assess the severity of CRF and its interference with daily life [28]. A BFI total score was generated from the average of all nine questions. A total score of 4 or more indicates the presence of moderate to severe CRF. The Multidimensional Fatigue Symptom Inventory – Short Form (MFSI-SF) is a validated 30-item self-report questionnaire [29]. The MFSI-SF has a total score as well as five subscales that assess general, physical, emotional, and mental fatigue, as well as the vigor (energy level) subdomains of CRF. The total score ranges from −24 to 96. Higher total, general, physical, emotional, or mental subscale scores indicate greater fatigue; a higher score on the vigor subscale indicates less fatigue. The MFSI-SF is therefore a comprehensive assessment of not only CRF severity (total score), but also the breadth of its subdomains (subscales).

Two physical function tests were conducted by trained study coordinators. The 6-minute walk test (6MWT) is a sub-maximal exercise test used to assess aerobic capacity and endurance [30]. The participants were instructed to walk as fast as they can to cover as much distance in six minutes along a marked course, and trained study staff observed and recorded the distance walked. The distance covered in feet was used as the outcome by which to compare changes in cardiorespiratory function.

Muscular function was assessed via hand grip dynamometry [30]. Grip strength is a measure of muscular strength, or the maximum force/tension generated by one’s forearm muscles. Hand-grip dynamometry is standard in the field of exercise physiology as a proxy measure for overall muscular fitness [31]. The participants squeezed the handle of a hand grip dynamometer with maximum force three times. If one of these three measured a ± 10% difference from the other two measures, a fourth measure was taken. An average of the dominant hand scores was utilized as the measure of hand grip strength, unless the dominant arm was the site of an axillary biopsy, in which case the non-dominant hand measures were used.

Statistical analyses

Selected sociodemographic and clinical variables were evaluated according to BMI category (normal weight: 18.0–24.9 kg/m2, overweight: 25.0–29.9 kg/m2, and obesity: ≥ 30 kg/m2). Comparisons were made via one-way ANOVA for continuous variables and chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables, as appropriate.

To assess the relationship between BMI and overall CRF and its subdomains, we performed both bivariate and multivariable analyses. In bivariate analyses, we conducted age-adjusted partial Pearson correlations between BMI (continuous; kg/m2) and MFSI-SF scores (continuous; total and subscales).

In the multivariable analyses, covariates were identified based on bivariate association with both the predictor and the outcome (p < 0.10). Potential covariates evaluated included age, race, ethnicity, education, marital status, physical activity, stage at diagnosis, comorbidities (i.e. diabetes, hypertension, hypothyroidism, and congestive heart failure), time since first treatment (months), and whether they received chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or adjuvant endocrine therapy.

We used multivariable linear regression models adjusted for age (continuous) and whether they previously received radiation therapy (categorical; yes/no) to examine the associations between BMI category (three-category predictor; normal weight as the reference group compared with obesity and with overweight) and MFSI-SF scores (continuous outcome; separate models for total and subscales).

We utilized multivariable logistic regression to evaluate the associations of BMI category (three-category predictor; normal weight as the referent group compared with obesity and with overweight) and reported moderate to severe CRF (measured by BFI; binary categorical outcome) adjusted for age (continuous) and whether they previously received radiation therapy (categorical; yes/no).

To evaluate the combined impact of BMI category and CRF on physical function, we combined participants with either overweight or obesity into one category (overweight/obesity). We then cross-classified participants according to BMI and CRF category (a total score of 4 or more on the BFI indicates the presence of moderate to severe CRF): (1) normal weight with none to mild CRF, (2) overweight/obesity with none to mild CRF, (3) normal weight with moderate to severe CRF, and (4) overweight/obesity with moderate to severe CRF. We calculated marginal mean cardiorespiratory function (6MWT distance) and muscular function (grip strength) adjusted for age (continuous), race (categorical; indicator variables [white, black, and other]), stage at diagnosis (categorical; indicator variables [stages 1 and 2, stage 3, and unknown]), marital status (categorical; indicator variables [married and partnered, or divorced, separated, single, and widowed]), and diabetes (binary categorical; yes/no) for all four categories using the Least Squares Means Method. Tukey-adjustment for multiple comparisons was utilized to examine overall and between-group differences in the cross-classification table.

Results

The overall sample of 456 breast cancer survivors were, on average, 55.9 ± 10.6 years of age, 23.5 ± 19.7 months since first treatment, 86.0% were white non-Hispanic, and 80.3% were diagnosed with early-stage disease (stage I/II).

There were few differences between survivors across BMI categories in regard to age, time since first treatment, stage at diagnosis, marital status, education level obtained, ethnicity, adjuvant endocrine therapy use, or whether they received chemotherapy (Table 1; all p > 0.05). However, breast cancer survivors with obesity were significantly more likely to experience moderate to severe CRF compared to those with overweight or normal weight (69.0% versus 60.4% and 50.4%, respectively; p = 0.006). Survivors with obesity, compared to those with overweight or normal weight were also more likely to have received radiation therapy (76.0% versus 69.6% and 57.0%, respectively; p = 0.003), be Black (16.0% versus 8.1% and 5.6%; p = 0.016), and have a diagnosis of diabetes (17.0% versus 10.7% and 3.8% p = 0.003).

In bivariate analyses, BMI was significantly but weakly correlated with overall CRF (MFSI-SF total; r = 0.09; p = 0.049) and several subscales (physical fatigue r = 0.15, p = 0.001; general fatigue r = 0.13, p = 0.005; and vigor r = −0.12, p = 0.014; higher score indicates worse fatigue, except for the vigor subscale).

Following adjustment for covariates (Table 2) breast cancer survivors with obesity had, on average, worse overall CRF (total β [se] = 5.30 [2.50]; p = 0.036); indicating survivors with obesity reported an average MFSI-SF total score 5.3 points higher than those with normal weight. Subscale analyses demonstrated more granular associations. We report worse physical fatigue (β [se] = 1.64 [0.64]; p = 0.010), general fatigue (β [se] = 2.25 [0.72]; p = 0.002), and vigor (β [se] = −1.17 [0.60]; p = 0.051) subscale scores among survivors with obesity compared to those with normal weight. While overweight was similarly associated with worse CRF compared to their counterparts with normal weight, these associations were non-significant (all p > 0.05).

Fifty percent (228/456) of our study sample had overweight or obesity and reported moderate to severe CRF, while 26% (121/456) had normal weight with moderate to severe CRF, as measured by the BFI. The remaining participants were evenly distributed between those with normal weight and none to mild CRF (12%; 53/456) and those with overweight or obesity with none to mild CRF (12%; 54/456).

There were significant differences in cardiorespiratory function between these four categories (p-trend < 0.001; Figure 2). Specifically, breast cancer survivors who faced the dual burden of overweight/obesity (BMI ≥ 25.0 kg/m2) with moderate to severe CRF (measured via BFI) had an average (mean ± se) 6MWT distance of 1310.1’ ± 33.9’. This represents significantly worse cardiorespiratory function compared to any other group, including those with overweight/obesity and none to mild CRF (1409.6’ ± 38.5’; p-diff = 0.001), those with normal weight but moderate to severe CRF (1462.3’ ± 49.9’; p-diff = 0.002), and those with normal weight and none to mild CRF (1555.7’ ± 50.3’; p-diff < 0.001). There was no significant differences (p-trend = 0.660) in mean handgrip strength between any of the four categories (overweight/obesity with moderate to severe CRF = 18.6 ± 1.0 lbs., overweight/obesity and none to mild CRF = 20.2 ± 1.1 lbs., normal weight and moderate to severe CRF = 17.9 ± 1.5 lbs., and normal weight and none to mild CRF = 19.0 ± 1.5 lbs; all p > 0.05).

Discussion

This study highlights the link between obesity and chronic, severe CRF among breast cancer survivors and is one of the first to examine the compounded impact of both conditions on physical function in this population. Our robust analyses revealed that survivors with obesity reported significantly worse overall CRF and are more than twice as likely to experience the more severe form of CRF, when compared to their counterparts with normal weight, exceeding the thresholds for clinical and statistical significance. Obesity was especially linked to worse outcomes in the physical manifestations of CRF (i.e. physical, general, and vigor subscales). We further demonstrated that breast cancer survivors with co-occurring obesity and moderate to severe CRF exhibit significantly worse cardiorespiratory function compared to those with only one or neither of these conditions.

These novel findings, derived from a large, nationally recruited sample with a high prevalence of both overweight/obesity (77%) and moderate to severe CRF (62%), underscore the disproportionate burden of these co-occurring conditions. Notably, this impact persists years after primary breast cancer treatment (mean time since first treatment = 23.2 months), highlighting an urgent need for targeted interventions to mitigate chronic side effects of treatment among long-term survivors.

Our findings on the association of obesity and CRF among breast cancer survivors align with the existing, albeit limited, literature. Four prior studies also found that a higher BMI was linked to worse CRF [22–25]. While breast cancer survivors with obesity consistently reported worse CRF than their counterparts without obesity, one study also indicated that survivors with overweight experienced worse CRF than those with normal weight, suggesting that overweight, not just obesity, may increase CRF [25]. Our own findings regarding breast cancer survivors with overweight were in a similar direction, though non-significant, possibly due to differences in model adjustments or sample size.

To our knowledge, only one other study has explored the interrelationships of BMI, CRF, and cardiorespiratory function among breast cancer survivors [32]. Unlike our approach, the authors treated cardiorespiratory function as a predictor of CRF, and reported non-significant inverse associations. Though their inverse findings mirror our own, their smaller sample size (n = 257) and longer time since diagnosis (mean = 5 years, up to 18 years) may explain the discrepancies in statistical significance with our findings. Our study was unique in its comprehensive evaluation of physical function, assessing both cardiorespiratory and muscular function in tandem. Although we found no association between muscular function (as measured by grip strength) and CRF or BMI, we did observe significantly lower cardiorespiratory function (as measured by 6MWT) among those with overweight/obesity and moderate-severe CRF. Though hand-grip strength is widely accepted in exercise physiology as a practical proxy measure for overall muscular fitness, there is evidence to suggest that grip strength alone may not accurately reflect lower limb muscle strength in older women [31,33] and is weakly associated with functional outcomes in this population [34]. Future studies should incorporate more comprehensive assessments of muscular function, such as knee extension strength or a relative strength index that incorporates BMI [35,36].

Several mechanisms have been proposed to explain the link between CRF and obesity. The “adipokine hypothesis” posits that adipose tissue functions similarly to an endocrine organ by secreting pro-inflammatory cytokines [37]. Among breast cancer patients with obesity, excess adipose tissue leads to greater secretion of dysfunctional endocrine and inflammatory factors. These effects may be amplified by systemic treatments, which are known to promote inflammation [38]. While not well-studied in populations with cancer diagnoses, the link between cardiopulmonary capacity and dysregulation of inflammation has been studied in the fields of cardiovascular disease and metabolic syndrome research [39,40]. Coupled with the hypothesized role of inflammation as a key driver of CRF and the associations of both obesity and systemic cancer treatment with chronic low-grade inflammation, a biological link between obesity, CRF, and impaired cardiorespiratory function in survivors is highly plausible [41]. Breast cancer survivors are particularly vulnerable to this connection, as over 80% are prescribed adjuvant endocrine therapy for up to 10 years post-treatment to prevent recurrence. This medication is associated with weight gain [16] and this increase in adiposity may contribute to lingering inflammation even after cessation of systemic treatment, further exacerbating CRF.

This study has several notable strengths, including the use of validated tools to measure CRF, enabling a detailed exploration of its overall associations and subdomains. The study’s large sample size is also drawn from community oncology clinics nationwide, with a high prevalence of moderate to severe CRF. Further, we obtained objective measures of physical function, enhancing the robustness of our findings.

As with any study, the interpretation of these results must consider their limitations. This cohort predominantly consists of non-Hispanic white women with breast cancer and at least mild insomnia, so our findings may not be generalizable to other ethnic and racial groups, men, or survivors of other types of cancer. Additionally, the cross-sectional design of the study prevents us from establishing a temporal relationship between BMI, CRF, and impaired physical function. We relied on the 6MWT distance as a proxy for cardiorespiratory function, which, while it is an objective measure, is not a direct measure of peak VO2 intake. We also lacked direct measures of body composition (e.g. visceral fat, adiposity), which may serve as better indicators of cardiometabolic risk than BMI.

CRF and obesity are highly prevalent within this population, and both lead to detrimental impacts on quality of life, the ability to obtain and maintain gainful employment following treatment, and survival [14,15,42]. Given the increasing number of survivors in the US, there is a clear need for systematic screening and targeted treatment of these conditions. The current American Society of Clinical Oncology Guidelines (ASCO) identify several supportive care strategies to mitigate CRF, weight gain, and other late effects among cancer survivors, including aerobic and resistance exercise, healthy dietary patterns, and mindfulness-based practices such as yoga [43,44]. Although these approaches represent the current gold standard, there is a critical need for novel, practical, and scalable interventions. In addition, our findings point to a need for interventions that address the broad cross-section of long-term symptoms and side effects that breast cancer survivors face concurrently. While our study indicates that obesity, CRF, and impaired physical function frequently co-occur, other conditions, such as insomnia and anxiety, are highly prevalent in this population in comparison to their cancer-free counterparts, and an approach to developing survivorship interventions that address the many co-occurring long-term symptoms and side effects that breast cancer survivors face may be the most practical [45,46]. Such interventions should address both the underlying etiology and the social determinants of health specific to the context and experience of breast cancer survivors in the United States to effectively promote and sustain these behaviors.

In summary, our findings add to the growing body of evidence indicating that excess weight is a significant risk factor for CRF among breast cancer survivors. Moreover, the co-occurrence of obesity and CRF, two of the most prevalent conditions in this population, may synergistically contribute to diminished physical function.
