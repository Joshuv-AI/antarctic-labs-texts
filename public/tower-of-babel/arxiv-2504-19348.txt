Non-Markovian dynamics of bottomonia in the QGP Preprint: TIFR/TH/25-9
Vyshakh B R, Rishi Sharma

Abstract
The evolution of quarkonia in the QGP medium can be described through the formalism of Open Quantum Systems (OQS). In previous works with OQS, the quarkonium evolution was studied by working in either quantum Brownian or optical regime. In this paper, we set up a general non-Markovian master equation to describe the evolution of the quarkonia in the medium. Due to the non-Markovian nature, it cannot be cast in Lindblad form which makes it challenging to solve. We numerically solve the master equation for Υ ⁡ ( 1 ​ S ) \Upsilon(1S) without considering stochastic jumps for Bjorken and viscous hydrodynamic background at energies relevant to LHC and RHIC. We quantify the effect of the hierarchy between the system time scale, τ S ∼ 1 / E b \tau_{{\rm{S}}}\sim 1/E_{b} , and the environment time scale, τ E \tau_{{\rm{E}}} , on quarkonium evolution and show that it significantly affects the nuclear modification factor. A comparison of our results with the existing experimental data from LHC and RHIC is presented.

Consider a system (S) interacting with the environment (E). The combined system and environment are isolated, so the density matrix of the total system evolves as,

e^{ih_{s}t}{\bf{r}}e^{-ih_{o}t}\sqrt{\frac{1}{(N_{c}^{2}-1)}}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\

The medium response functions

\Gamma_{1}(t,t^{\prime})=\frac{g^{2}}{6N_{c}}{\rm{Tr}}_{{\rm{E}}}\Bigl({{\bf{E}}}^{a}(t,{\vec{0}}){\cal{W}}_{ab}(t,t^{\prime}){{\bf{E}}}^{b}(t^{\prime},{\vec{0}})\rho_{{\rm{E}}}\Bigr)

The operators

(13)

where

As reviewed in the introduction, truncating the expansion in Eq.

\displaystyle\tilde{\Gamma}(\omega)=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}dt\;e^{i\omega t}\;\Gamma(t,0)\;,\;\;\lim_{\omega\rightarrow 0}\tilde{\Gamma}(\omega)=\frac{1}{2}(\kappa+i\gamma)\;.

(14)

These are often written in terms of dimensionless variables

,
\hat{\gamma}=\gamma/T^{3}

;
Brambilla et al. 2020

Our goal is to go beyond these approximations and directly solve Eq.
10
. Although linear, it is still formidable to solve due to the large number of degrees of freedom associated with the spatial coordinates in three dimensions. Since we will ultimately look at only states with a definite angular-momentum quantum number
l
, it is useful to work in the angular-momentum basis
\lvert l,m\rangle
. An additional benefit of using this basis is that the transition rules can be written in a simpler form
Brambilla et al. 2017b
;
N. Brambilla, J. Ghiglieri, A. Vairo and P. Petreczky 2008
;
Brambilla et al. 2022a
. Furthermore, it can be shown that when the initial density matrix
\rho_{\rm{S}}(0)
is block diagonal in
|l,m\rangle
basis, then for the evolution equation Eq.
10
it will be block diagonal for subsequent times. Defining the blocks by

\rho^{l}(t)=\sum\limits_{m=-l}^{l}\langle l,m\rvert\rho_{S}(t)\lvert l,m\rangle\;,

(15)

where we have summed over the azimuthal quantum number
m
since we are not considering any observables that are sensitive to transitions between different
m
values for a given
l
. The evolution equation for
\rho^{l}(t)
can be written as,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\rho^{l}(t)}{\partial t}=-i\left[h^{l},\rho^{l}(t)\right]-\int\limits_{0}^{t}ds\sum\limits_{n,l^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\Bigl(\Gamma(t,t-s){T}^{\dagger}_{n}(l\to l^{\prime},0)T_{n}(l\to l^{\prime},-s)\rho^{l}(t)

(16)

\displaystyle-\Gamma(t,t-s){T}_{n}(l^{\prime}\to l,-s)\rho^{l^{\prime}}(t)T^{\dagger}_{n}(l^{\prime}\to l,0)+\textrm{H.C.}\Bigr)\;,

where,

\begin{split}h^{l}&=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}h_{s}^{l}&0\\
0&h_{o}^{l}\end{array}\right)\;,\;\;{\rm{with}}\\
h_{s,o}^{l}&=\frac{1}{M}\Bigl(-\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial r^{2}}r+\frac{l(l+1)}{r^{2}}\Bigr)+v_{s,o}(r)\;.\end{split}

(17)

The summations over
n
and
l^{\prime}
in Eq.
16
indicates a sum over transitions between states with different colour quantum numbers (
s,\;o
) and orbital angular momentum (
l,\;l^{\prime}
), and the corresponding time-dependent transition operators at time
t
are
T_{n}(l\to l^{\prime},t)
. To the order in pNRQCD that we are working in (Eq.
1
) only transitions between angular momentum states
l^{\prime}=l\pm 1
are allowed due to the dipole interaction. The explicit form of the transition operators is given by:

\displaystyle T_{n}(l\to l^{\prime},t)=C_{l,l^{\prime}}\times\begin{cases}e^{ih_{o}^{l^{\prime}}t}re^{-ih_{s}^{l}t}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&0\\
1&0\end{array}\right)\;\;\\
e^{ih_{s}^{l^{\prime}}t}re^{-ih_{o}^{l}t}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&\sqrt{\frac{1}{(N_{c}^{2}-1)}}\\
0&0\end{array}\right)\;\;\\
e^{ih_{o}^{l^{\prime}}t}re^{-ih_{o}^{l}t}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&0\\
0&\sqrt{\frac{N_{c}^{2}-4}{2(N_{c}^{2}-1)}}\end{array}\right)\;\;\end{cases}\;,

(18)

for
n=1,2,3
respectively and
C_{l,l^{\prime}}
is given by:

\displaystyle C_{l,l^{\prime}}=\sqrt{\frac{l+1}{2l+1}}\delta_{l^{\prime},l+1}+\sqrt{\frac{l}{2l+1}}\delta_{l^{\prime},l-1}

(19)

The master equation
16
can be given a simple interpretation similar to that of a rate equation. In Eq.
16
, the first pair of
T_{n}
’s correspond to the process
l\to l^{\prime}=l\pm 1\to l
and the second term corresponds to the contribution from the transitions
l^{\prime}=l\pm 1\to l
. There will be higher-order transitions if one goes beyond the leading-order multipole expansion.

III
Numerical Simulation

Master equations of the form Eq.
16
can be solved using stochastic methods such as stochastic unraveling
Breuer et al. 1999
. We review this technique here.

Consider a general master equation,

\begin{split}\frac{\partial\rho_{\rm{S}}(t)}{\partial t}&=A(t)\rho_{\rm{S}}(t)+\rho_{\rm{S}}B^{\dagger}(t)\\
&+\sum_{i}\Bigl(C_{i}(t)\rho_{\rm{S}}(t)D_{i}^{\dagger}(t)+\textrm{H.C.}\Bigr)\;.\end{split}

(20)

For Eq.
16
,

\begin{split}A(t)&=B(t)=-ih^{l}_{\rm{eff}}(t)\\
&=-ih^{l}-\int\limits_{0}^{t}ds\;\Gamma(t,t-s)\times\\
&\qquad\sum\limits_{n,l^{\prime}}{T}^{\dagger}_{n}(l\to l^{\prime},0)T_{n}(l\to l^{\prime},-s)\;,\\
C_{n,l^{\prime}}(t)&=\int\limits_{0}^{t}ds\;\Gamma(t,t-s)T_{n}(l\to l^{\prime},-s)\;,\\
D_{n,l^{\prime}}(t)&={T}_{n}(l\to l^{\prime},0)\;.~\end{split}

(21)

Before describing the formalism of the general case, it is useful to review it for the Lindblad equation (Eq.
40
) for which
A(t)=B(t)=-iH_{\rm{eff}}=-i(H_{\rm{h}}-iL_{i}^{\dagger}L_{i}/2)
, and
C_{i}(t)=D_{i}(t)=L_{i}/\sqrt{2}
. The master equation can be solved by considering an ensemble of wavefunctions
\{|\psi(t)\rangle\}
each evolving according to the stochastic equation,

\begin{split}d\lvert\psi(t)\rangle=&-i\,dt\;H_{{\rm{eff}}}|\psi(t)\rangle\\
&+\sum\limits_{i}\left(\frac{L_{i}(t)|\psi(t)\rangle}{\lvert\rvert L_{i}(t)|\psi(t)\rangle\lvert\rvert}-|\psi\rangle\right)dN_{i}(t)\;,~\end{split}

(22)

where
\{dN_{i}(t)\}
are a set of infinitesimal independent Poisson processes (called “jumps”) with an ensemble average
dN_{i}(t)dN_{j}(t)=\delta_{ij}\;dN_{i}(t)
, satisfying
{\langle\!\langle}dN_{i}(t){\rangle\!\rangle}=\lvert\rvert L_{i}(t)|\psi(t)\rangle\lvert\rvert^{2}/\lvert\rvert|\psi(t)\rangle\lvert\rvert^{2}
. The density matrix
\rho_{\rm{S}}
is given by the ensemble average
{\langle\!\langle}|\psi(t)\rangle\langle\psi(t)|{\rangle\!\rangle}
and satisfies the required Lindblad equation. See Ref.
Brambilla et al. 2021a
for an implementation of this technique to quarkonia.

To unravel the general master equation (Eq.
20
) a two- component stochastic wavefunction
\lvert\psi(t)\rangle=\bigl(\lvert\psi_{1}(t)\rangle,\lvert\psi_{2}(t)\rangle\bigr)
needs to be defined. The evolution equation for
\lvert\psi(t)\rangle
is given by,

\begin{split}d|\psi(t)\rangle=&-i\,dt\;{\overline{H}}|\psi(t)\rangle\\
&+\sum\limits_{i}\left(\frac{J_{i}(t)|\psi(t)\rangle}{\lvert\rvert J_{i}(t)\lvert\psi(t)\rangle\lvert\rvert}-|\psi\rangle\right)dN_{i}(t)\;\\
&+\sum\limits_{i}\left(\frac{\overline{J}_{i}(t)|\psi(t)\rangle}{\lvert\rvert\overline{J}_{i}(t)\lvert\psi(t)\rangle\lvert\rvert}-|\psi\rangle\right)d\overline{N}_{i}(t)\;,\end{split}

(23)

where,

\displaystyle\overline{H}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}A(t)&0\\
0&B(t)\end{array}\right),\;\;\;J_{i}(t)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}C_{i}(t)&0\\
0&D_{i}(t)\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\overline{J}_{i}(t)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}D_{i}(t)&0\\
0&C_{i}(t)\end{array}\right)

and
\{dN_{i}(t),d\overline{N}_{i}(t)\}
are infinitesimal independent Poisson processes as defined above, satisfying

\begin{split}{\langle\!\langle}dN_{i}(t){\rangle\!\rangle}&=\lvert\rvert J_{i}(t)|\psi(t)\rangle\lvert\rvert^{2}/\lvert\rvert|\psi(t)\rangle\lvert\rvert^{2},\end{split}

(30)

with a similar equation for
{\langle\!\langle}d\overline{N}_{i}(t){\rangle\!\rangle}
.
The density matrix
\rho_{\rm{S}}(t)
is given by the ensemble average
{\langle\!\langle}|\psi_{1}(t)\rangle\langle\psi_{2}(t)|{\rangle\!\rangle}
and satisfies Eq.
20
.

For LO
Brambilla et al. 2021b
and NLO
Brambilla et al. 2022a
;
Brambilla et al. 2023
Lindblad equations, it has been found previously that evolution with
H_{\rm{eff}}
(Eq.
22

\Upsilon(1S)
states, and results obtained by solving the Lindblad equation with and without “jumps” are very close. However, for
\Upsilon(2S)
and

which are not as strongly bound, “jumps” are necessary to obtain the accurate survival probability and is essential for capturing the phenomenon of regeneration. Regeneration is a key process for obtaining a realistic phenomenological description of the suppression of the excited states
Brambilla et al. 2022a
;
Brambilla et al. 2023

1S

Motivated by this observation, in this paper, we focus on
\Upsilon(1S)

This effect is absent in LO. In fact, for RHIC with the parameters above, the decay probability remains substantial for LO even at

V
Conclusions

We solve non-Markovian master equations for the evolution of the
\Upsilon(1S)
state in the QGP. For
\tau_{{\rm{E}}}/\tau_{{\rm{S}}}\rightarrow 0
, these equations reduce to LO Lindblad equations. To study the dependence on
\tau_{{\rm{E}}}
(
\tau_{{\rm{S}}}\sim 1/E_{b}
is fixed) we analyze the survival probability of
\Upsilon(1S)
for
\tau_{{\rm{E}}}=\xi_{{\rm{E}}}/T
for three values of
\xi_{{\rm{E}}}
:
\xi_{{\rm{E}}}=\{1/1.5,1,1.5\}
. We find that as
\xi_{{\rm{E}}}
decreases, the survival probability decreases, but for all
\xi_{{\rm{E}}}\sim 1
, LO
Brambilla et al. 2021a
;
Brambilla et al. 2021b
Lindblad equations significantly overestimate the suppression of
\Upsilon(1S)
(see. Figs.
1
,
2
).

We find that the NLO
Brambilla et al. 2022a
;
Brambilla et al. 2023
equations capture much of this reduction of the suppression for realistic
\xi_{{\rm{E}}}
at
T
’s well above the crossover temperature, but our calculations in Bjorken backgrounds (Fig.
2
) suggest that at temperatures
\lesssim 250
MeV the NLO equations do not agree with non-Markovian master equations suggesting higher-order corrections might be necessary.
R_{\textrm{AA}}
values found using the non-Markovian master equation are not very sensitive to the choice of the freezeout time of the
\Upsilon(1S)
, as long as it is roughly around
200
MeV or below. (Interestingly, temperatures of about
200
MeV are not inconsistent with the extraction of the freezeout time for bottomonium states from statistical models
S. Gupta and R. Sharma 2014
;
Kumar et al. 2023
.)

The key ingredient in our calculation is the chromoelectric correlator whose overall strength is governed by the transport coefficient
\kappa
(Eq.
14
) (we take
\gamma=0
). From our results, one can see that a larger
\tau_{{\rm{E}}}
can be compensated for by a larger
\kappa
, and hence an independent determination of
\kappa
is needed to narrow down the parameter space in the absence of other independent observables in this sector.

Using a realistic hydrodynamic medium for RHIC (right) and LHC (left) in Fig.
4
, we find the final probability of survival of the
\Upsilon(1S)
states in these experiments. While a smaller “effective”
\xi_{{\rm{E}}}
might partly explain the suppression observed at RHIC, there is room for additional effects. We leave a more detailed attempt at phenomenology for future work.

Finally, an obvious next step can be including “jumps” and addressing the phenomenology of the excited bottomonium states, and their feed-down.

Appendix A
LO Markovian approximation

In this section we review how the master equation, Eq.
8
, can be rewritten as a LO Markovian equation if
\tau_{{\rm{E}}}\ll\tau_{{\rm{S}}}
.

The environmental correlation functions
\Gamma_{mn}(t,s)
in Eq.
8
decay on time scales
t-s\sim\tau_{{\rm{E}}}
. If this time scale is short compared to the time scale of the variation of
{\bf{V}}^{n}_{{\rm{S}}}(s)
(
\tau_{{\rm{S}}}
) and the observation time (
t
), then in the RHS of Eq.
8
, the lower limit of the
s
integration can be set to
-\infty
and the variation of
{\bf{V}}^{n}_{{\rm{S}}}(s)
can be ignored (LO in
\tau_{{\rm{E}}}/\tau_{{\rm{S}}}
). This is known as the Markovian approximation. In this approximation, the integral in
s
simply gives the
0
frequency limit of
\Gamma_{mn}(t)
and the RHS of Eq.
8
has a structure,

\begin{split}&-\sum_{m,n}\tilde{\Gamma}_{m,n}(0)\Bigl({\bf{V}}^{m}_{{\rm{S}}}(0){\bf{V}}^{n}_{{\rm{S}}}(0)\rho_{{\rm{S}}}(t)-{\bf{V}}^{m}_{{\rm{S}}}(0)\rho_{{\rm{S}}}(t){\bf{V}}^{n}_{{\rm{S}}}(0)\Bigr)\\
&+{\rm{H.C.}}\;,~\end{split}

(39)

where
H.C.
is the hermitian conjugate of the operator in the previous line.

The form in Eq.
39
is not an accident. Going to the Schrödinger picture and defining a new set of operators
L_{k}
by taking appropriate linear combinations of
{\bf{V}}_{{\rm{S}}}^{n}
, Eq.
3
in the Markovian approximation can be rewritten in a more familiar form,

\frac{\partial\rho_{{\rm{S}}}(t)}{\partial t}=-i[H_{\rm{h}},\rho_{{\rm{S}}}]+\sum_{k}\Bigl(L_{k}\rho_{{\rm{S}}}L_{k}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{L_{k}^{\dagger}L_{k},\rho_{{\rm{S}}}\}\Bigr)\;,~

(40)

where
H_{\rm{h}}
gives the Hermitian evolution of
\rho_{{\rm{S}}}
and
L_{k}
is a set of operators that encode its non-Hermitian evolution in a trace-preserving manner. The evolution of the state depends only on the state at that instant, and the evolution operator does not depend on the history. Eq.
40
is the well-known Lindblad form. For the specific forms of
H_{\rm{h}}
and
L_{k}
calculated for quarkonia under various assumptions, we refer the reader to Refs.
Akamatsu 2022
;
Yao 2021
;
J. P. Blaizot and M. A. Escobedo 2018b
;
Brambilla et al. 2018
;
Brambilla et al. 2022a
.

The physical picture behind the mathematical form above is that if
\tau_{{\rm{E}}}\ll\tau_{{\rm{S}}}
, any memory of the
{\rm{S}}-{\rm{E}}
exchanges is quickly lost, and the
{\rm{S}}
evolution is Markovian, and it is known that any positive definite, trace-preserving, Markovian equation for the time derivative of
\rho_{{\rm{S}}}
can be written as a Lindblad equation
Lindblad 1976
;
Gorini et al. 1976
.

Appendix C
Chromoelectric correlator in the Hard Thermal Loop (HTL) approximation

The chromoelectric-correlator,
\Gamma(t)
(Eq.
12
), in a deconfined medium at temperature
T
governs the evolution of the quarkonium density matrix in the QGP. Since it is a real-time quantity, it is challenging to compute non-perturbatively using lattice QCD. Nonetheless, significant progress has been made in understanding this object. The real and imaginary parts of the zero frequency limit of the (causal) correlator define two important transport coefficients
Brambilla et al. 2018
;
Brambilla et al. 2022a
,
\kappa
and
\gamma
, defined in Eq.
14
.

One can estimate
Brambilla et al. 2019

\gamma
using the shift in the peak of the quarkonium spectral function computed on the lattice
Kim et al. 2018
. More recent lattice results
Larsen et al. 2019
for the shift suggest that
\hat{\gamma}

Brambilla et al. 2024
for temperatures of a few
100
MeV, is small. This motivates us in this study to ignore the imaginary part of
\Gamma(t,0)
, an assumption worth closer investigation in the future. The direct computation of
\hat{\gamma}
is an open problem.

Similarly, a non-perturbative calculation of
\hat{\kappa}
is an open problem. A related quantity, the heavy quark momentum diffusion constant is defined as,

\kappa_{\rm{F}}=\frac{g^{2}}{3N_{c}}\int dt\;{\rm{Tr}}_{{\rm{E}}}\left({\cal{U}}(-\infty,t;{\vec{0}}){\bf{E}}(t,{\vec{0}}){\cal{U}}(t,0;{\vec{0}}){\bf{E}}(0,{\vec{0}}){\cal{U}}(0,\infty;{\vec{0}})\right)\;,

(41)

where
{\bf{E}}={\bf{E}}^{a}t^{a}
and
{\cal{U}}(t_{1},t_{2};{\vec{r}})
is the fundamental Wilson line from time
t_{2}
to
t_{1}
at the spatial location
{\vec{r}}
. This has been computed on the lattice
Banerjee et al. 2012
;
Ding et al. 2011
. (See Refs.
Banerjee et al. 2022b
;
Banerjee et al. 2022a
;
Brambilla et al. 2020
;
Brambilla et al. 2022b
;
Brambilla et al. 2022c
for more recent results.)

It is well known that the leading order (in the strong coupling
g
) perturbative
Moore and Teaney 2005
;
Mustafa 2005
estimate of
{\kappa_{\rm{F}}}
in the QGP phase is roughly an order of magnitude smaller than the non-perturbative result
Banerjee et al. 2012
;
Ding et al. 2011
. The next-to-leading order result
Caron-Huot and Moore 2008
;
C. H. Simon and G. D. Moore 2008
is closer, but the large correction suggests that the series in
g
converges slowly for temperatures of interest for heavy ion phenomenology (few
100
MeV).

Little is known about
\Gamma
at finite frequency non-perturbatively. Therefore, it is useful to gain insight from weak-coupling approaches, even though we do not expect these approaches to be quantitatively reliable. Formally,

\Re e[\Gamma(t,0)]=\frac{g^{2}C_{F}}{3}\int\frac{d^{4}q}{(2\pi)^{4}}e^{-iq^{0}t}\frac{e^{q^{0}/T}}{e^{q^{0}/T}-1}\rho(q^{\mu})\;,~

(42)

where,
C_{F}=(N_{c}^{2}-1)/(2N_{c})
is the colour factor and
\rho(q^{\mu})
is the chromoelectric spectral density.

In the Hard Thermal Loop (HTL) formalism at leading order
Kapusta and Gale 2006
, the contribution to the spectral density from longitudinal gluons is

\rho(q^{\mu})=\frac{\pi m_{D}^{2}qq^{0}}{({\bf{q}}^{2}+m_{D}^{2})^{2}+(\pi m_{D}^{2}q^{0}/2q)^{2}}\;,~

(43)

where
m_{D}\sim gT
is the screening mass.

Performing the
q^{0}
integration in Eq.
42
via contour integration for
t>0
, the integrand in the lower complex plane picks residues from the pole of
\rho
[
q^{0}=-i2q(q^{2}+m_{D}^{2})/m_{D}^{2}
in HTL] and the residues from the poles of the Bose-Einstein distribution [
q^{0}=-i2n\pi/T
, for integral
n\geq 1
]. The real part of the result, therefore, has the form

\begin{split}\Re e[\Gamma(t,0)]_{{\rm{HTL}}}&=\int\frac{d^{3}q}{(2\pi)^{3}}q^{3}\Bigl\{\exp[-2tq(q^{2}+m_{D}^{2})/(\pi m_{D}^{2})]\frac{q^{2}\cot[q(q^{2}+m_{D}^{2})/(\pi m_{D}^{2}T)]}{\pi m_{D}^{2}}\\
+&\sum_{n\geq 1}\exp[-2tn\pi T]\frac{2\pi^{2}q^{3}{m_{D}}^{2}nT^{2}}{-q^{6}-2k^{4}{m_{D}}^{2}-k^{2}{m_{D}}^{4}+\pi^{4}{m_{D}}^{4}n^{2}T^{2}}\Bigr\}\;.\end{split}

(44)

The terms in the sum (second term in Eq.
44
) fall off with
t
as
\exp[-2tn\pi T]
, while the contribution from the pole of
\rho
(first term in Eq.
44
) falls off as
\sim\exp[-m_{D}t]
for
q\sim m_{D}
and is the dominant contribution in weak coupling when
m_{D}\ll\pi T
. For
g\gtrsim 1
, both the first and the second term decay on a time scale
\sim 1/T
. Finally, it is easy to verify that
3
3

3

The analysis is a little formal since the integral over
d^{3}q
is UV-divergent and needs to be regularized by an ultraviolet completion of the theory
Brambilla et al. 2013
. This detail does not change the arguments.

\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\;\Re e[\Gamma(t,0)]_{{\rm{HTL}}}=\frac{1}{2}\int\frac{d^{3}q}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{qm_{D}^{2}\pi T}{(q^{2}+m_{D}^{2})^{2}}=\frac{1}{2}\kappa_{{\rm{HTL}}}\;.~

(45)

This motivates our choice of the time-dependent form of the correlator,

\Gamma(t,0)=\frac{\kappa}{2}\frac{e^{-t/\tau_{{\rm{E}}}}}{\tau_{{\rm{E}}}},\;\;t>0~

(46)

with
\tau_{{\rm{E}}}\sim 1/T

14

\Gamma(t,0)
will have an imaginary part

\gamma=0
