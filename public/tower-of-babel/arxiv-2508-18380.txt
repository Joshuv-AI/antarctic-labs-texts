Information Templates: A New Paradigm for Intelligent Active Feature Acquisition
Hung-Tien Huang, Dzung Dinh, Junier B. Oliva

Abstract
Active feature acquisition (AFA) is an instance-adaptive paradigm in which, at test time, a policy sequentially chooses which features to acquire (at a cost) before predicting. Existing approaches either train reinforcement learning (RL) policies, which deal with a difficult MDP, or greedy policies that cannot account for the joint informativeness of features or require knowledge about the underlying data distribution. To overcome this, we propose Template-based AFA (TAFA), a non-greedy framework that learns a small library of feature templates–a set of features that are jointly informative–and uses this library of templates to guide the next feature acquisitions. Through identifying feature templates, the proposed framework not only significantly reduces the action space considered by the policy but also alleviates the need to estimate the underlying data distribution. Extensive experiments on synthetic and real-world datasets show that TAFA outperforms the existing state-of-the-art baselines while achieving lower overall acquisition cost and computation.



1
Introduction

Many existing machine learning (ML) methods assume access to fully observed feature vectors during both training and inference time; however, such an assumption ignores the fact that acquiring each feature typically comes at a cost (e.g., time or money) in practice. To address this limitation, we consider
active feature acquisition
(AFA)
(
Saar-Tsechansky, Melville, and Provost 2009
)
, where an ML agent sequentially acquires features while considering the tradeoff between accuracy and acquisition cost. In this work, we introduce template-based AFA (TAFA), a novel approach that simplifies AFA according to a learned dictionary of subsets of features in order to improve both the inference time of AFA agents, as well as their accuracy, all whilst avoiding the need for RL-based training.

Consider the following illustrative real-world AFA scenario: diagnosing a patient with chest pain. Rather than ordering every available test immediately, a skilled physician starts with basic vital signs, then progressively orders more specialized tests—ECG, blood work, or cardiac imaging—based on initial findings. Each test has acquisition costs (time, money, patient risk), making the sequence of tests crucial for efficient diagnosis.
The AFA agent’s goal is to sequentially and dynamically (instance-personalized) select the most informative features while considering the acquisition cost.
Beyond healthcare, applications abound in robotics (making decisions with judicious use of sensors), education (assessing current knowledge with judicious question choices), troubleshooting (assessing the issues with judicious choices of diagnostic tests), etc.

Existing works in AFA often utilize RL or deep generative modeling. In practice, RL approaches
(
Dulac-Arnold et al. 2011
;
Shim, Hwang, and Yang 2018
;
Li and Oliva 2021
)
struggle with large action spaces and can suffer from credit assignment problems, while information-theoretic methods
(
Chen et al. 2015
;
Ma et al. 2019
;
Watson et al. 2023
)
require modeling complex multidimensional distributions.

To overcome the challenges of RL and generative modeling,
Valancius, Lennon, and Oliva 2024
proposed Acquisition Conditioned Oracle (ACO), a non-parametric approach that considers all possible future acquisition paths. Although theoretically attractive, ACO’s computational cost grows exponentially: with
D
features, it must evaluate
\mathcal{O}(2^{D})
possible acquisition strategies at each step. Returning to our medical example: if a hospital has 20 available tests, ACO would be required to evaluate over 1M possible test combinations, while not all the combinations are informative.

To dramatically reduce this computational burden, we propose Template-based AFA, a framework that learns a compact library of
feature templates
. A template is a set of features that are jointly informative for some (
not necessarily all
) instances. TAFA framework operates in two phases: (1) during training, we propose a template search algorithm to identify a small collection of useful templates (e.g.,
\{\text{ECG, troponins}\}
for cardiac issues,
\{\text{CBC, blood work}\}
for infection) and (2) at inference time, it infers the anticipated cost/benefit of templates dynamically based on current observations, then accordingly chooses the next feature to acquire from the best anticipated template. This compresses the action space from exponential in dimensionality to linear in the number of templates, while maintaining the non-greedy benefits of considering feature combinations.

Our main contributions are as follows:
1) A novel template-based approach to AFA that achieves orders-of-magnitude speedup over the baseline while maintaining comparable accuracy
2) A mutation-enhanced algorithm for high-quality templates discovery.
3) Theoretical analysis showing template search is submodular, enabling approximation guarantees.
4) Theoretical analysis showing that our guiding cost/benefit criteria lower bounds the true optimum value for the AFA MDP.
5) Develop a student/teacher approach to train parametric policies from the learned templates; Moreover, we show that this approach may be used to learn interpretable decision‑tree-based policies.
6) Comprehensive evaluation demonstrating both computational efficiency and predictive performance across multiple datasets.

Active Feature Acquisition

Let
x=\langle x_{d}\rangle_{d=1}^{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
be a
D
-dimensional input feature vector and
y
be its corresponding response variable of a supervised learning task. We use
\mathbf{o}\subseteq\left[D\right]\equiv\{1,\cdots,D\}
and
\mathbf{o}\in\mathfrak{P}\left(\left[D\right]\right)
to denote a subset of feature indices, where
\mathfrak{P}(\cdot)
is the power set of a set, and
x_{\mathbf{o}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\lvert\mathbf{o}\rvert}
be the corresponding subset feature values indexed by
\mathbf{o}
.
We assume access to a supervised learning dataset
\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}=\left\{x^{(n)},y^{(n)}\right\}_{n=1}^{N}\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\mathcal{P}
where
\mathcal{P}
is the data distribution.

The goal is to make an accurate prediction about the response variable using an informative subset of features that is constructed through sequentially deciding which feature is to be acquired and added to the subset on a per-instance basis. In addition to predictive performance, active feature acquisition considers the cost incurred to acquire features, e.g., money paid or time spent to acquire features.
To achieve the goal, one first needs a predictor
\widehat{y}(x_{\mathbf{o}})
that can make an inference about the response variable using an arbitrary subset of features from the corresponding instance. Suppose
y\in[C]
is a discrete variable of
K
possible values, then output of the predictor lies in a simplex, or
\widehat{y}(x_{\mathbf{o}})\in\triangle^{C-1}
for
C
classification categories. In addition to the predictor function, given previously observed subset features
x_{\mathbf{o}}
, we also need to construct a policy
\pi
that takes currently observed subset feature vector
x_{\mathbf{o}}
as input and outputs either a feature index among the subset of unobserved feature indices or a symbol to terminate acquisition, i.e.
\pi(x_{\mathbf{o}})\in\left(\left[D\right]\backslash\mathbf{o}\right)\cup\left\{\varnothing\right\}
where
\varnothing
is the symbol to signaling the termination of feature acquisition. For any non-termination action
a=\pi(x_{\mathbf{o}})\in\left[D\right]
, we acquire a new feature
x_{\mathbf{o}}=x_{\mathbf{o}}\cup x_{\mathbf{a}}
, whereas we use predictor to make inference
\hat{y}(x_{\mathbf{o}})
when terminating action is chosen
a=\pi(x_{\mathbf{o}})=\varnothing
.

The policy has to balance between the utility of each feature in making the prediction and the incurred cost when making a decision. Let
l(\widehat{y}(x_{\mathbf{o}}),y)
be the task objective function used to measure the predictive performance of the predictor
\widehat{y}
(e.g., cross entropy loss for classification or mean-squared-error loss for regression) and
c(i)
to represent the incurred cost of acquiring
i^{\text{th}}
feature. Mathematically, the policy
\pi
needs to jointly minimize both the task objective function
l(\cdot,\cdot)
and the cost function
c(\cdot)
. Prior works
(
Dulac-Arnold et al. 2011
;
Shim, Hwang, and Yang 2018
;
Li and Oliva 2021
)
have formulated AFA as a Markov decision process (MDP).

3
Related Work

As surveyed in
Rahbar, Aronsson, and Chehreghani 2025
, prior works often formulate AFA as a Markov Decision Problem (MDP) and solve with reinforcement learning (RL)
(
Dulac-Arnold et al. 2011
;
Shim, Hwang, and Yang 2018
;
Li and Oliva 2021
)
or take an information theoretic approach and choose actions greedily w.r.t. some feature utility function
(
Chen et al. 2015
;
Ma et al. 2019
;
Watson et al. 2023
)
.

One common utility is conditional mutual information (CMI); however, computing CMI is generally infeasible without access to the underlying data distribution
\mathcal{P}
and is often mitigated by training generative models to estimate the density function of
\mathcal{P}
. As
Gadgil, Covert, and Lee 2024
noted, training generative models comes with its own challenges, and estimating CMI through generative models can be a slow process. To this end, through a carefully crafted objective function that recovers CMI at optimality,
Gadgil, Covert, and Lee 2024
proposed discriminative mutual information estimation (DIME)
(
Gadgil, Covert, and Lee 2024
)
that uses an easy-to-optimize regression model to directly estimate the incremental improvement in task objective
l
of acquiring a new feature, which completely alleviates the need for a generative density model.

Despite its easy-to-optimize property, as
Valancius, Lennon, and Oliva 2024
highlighted, choosing features greedily as is done in DIME often causes the policy to overlook subsets of features that are jointly informative to the target of interest. Even though works that frame AFA as MDP and solve with RL
(
Dulac-Arnold et al. 2011
;
Shim, Hwang, and Yang 2018
;
Li and Oliva 2021
)
does not suffer from the lack of vision caused by greedy acquisition, training an RL agent can still be challenging due to its poor sample efficiency, lack of training stability, catastrophic forgetting
(
Ding and Dong 2020
)
, large action-space and credit assignment issues
(
Li and Oliva 2021
)
.

Thus,
Valancius, Lennon, and Oliva 2024
proposed an acquisition conditioned oracle (ACO) that is a non-greedy and non-parametric AFA method that requires neither RL training nor generative modeling. At inference, through kNN search, an approximate ACO policy would imagine likely values for those currently unacquired features and choose the most informative yet cost-effective subset of features to acquire, which
Valancius, Lennon, and Oliva 2024
argued that such a policy is non-greedy as it estimates the expected predictive error under the acquisition of
subsets
of features instead of the utility (e.g., CMI) of acquiring an
individual
feature. Moreover, Theorem C.1 of
Valancius, Lennon, and Oliva 2024
showed that the AFA MDP state value function is an upper bound for the ACO objective.

Despite their theoretical novelty, rolling out an ACO policy is computationally prohibitive because at each decision as it must evaluate expected error over exponentially many future acquisition sequences.
Although the approximate ACO variant (AACO) policy restricts the space for imagination to a randomly sampled subset of possible scenarios for acquiring features, the inference remains slow.

In contrast, we circumvent this issue by introducing
templates
: a possible subset of features that are informative at predicting the target when being acquired jointly (for some, note necessarily all) instances. As a result, training a TAFA policy first becomes a matter of identifying a
collection of templates
that the agent should consider in the future before taking actions. In other words, the room for imagination of our TAFA policy is restricted to the identified collection of templates instead of the universe of all possible combinations of features, which is exponential in size. As we show later in

§

4
, our proposed TAFA objective is a lower bound of the true AFA MDP value function.

This framing is motivated by the success of existing static feature selection at building statistical or machine learning models
(
Guyon and Elisseeff 2003
;
Li et al. 2017
)
; that is, if a model can already performs well using one static subset of features, we hypothesize that multiple subset of features would enable a model to use less features without making nay compromise at predictive performance. Indeed, static feature selection is a special case of AFA, in which the policy always chooses features from a constant subset of features until all features in the constant subset have been observed, regardless of values
(
Valancius, Lennon, and Oliva 2024
)
. The process of constructing a collection of templates in our TAFA formulation can be seen as attempting to find multiple static subsets of features.

4
Methodology

We now develop our proposed data-driven template-based approach to AFA. We first construct a collection of feature templates during training time so that the policy can first select a feature template and then acquire features following the chosen template.

4.1
Template Search as Set Optimization

We propose to find an optimal collection
\mathcal{B}^{\star}=\{\textbf{b}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{B}
of
B
feature templates where each template
\textbf{b}^{(i)}\subseteq\left[D\right]
is a potentially informative combination of features that is relevant to the supervised learning task of interest.
Before detailing how to construct this collection, we first provide a high-level overview of how the learned templates are utilized during inference to guide active feature acquisition.
At each round during inference, the policy
\pi
: first picks a template
\textbf{b}\in\mathcal{B}^{\star}

dynamically
that it anticipates to be effective given previously observed information
x_{\textbf{o}}
(see
eq. 5
below for details); after, one chooses a feature
i\in\textbf{b}\setminus\mathbf{o}
from the selected template and acquire the feature
x_{i}
; the acquisition repeats until the chosen template is fully observed, i.e.
\textbf{b}\subseteq\textbf{o}
.

Here, we formulate the problem of finding an optimal collection of templates as a set optimization problem

4.2
Template Search Algorithms

In this section, we present two practical algorithms to approximate the optimal template collection
\mathcal{B}^{\star}
. We begin with a straightforward greedy search algorithm that, despite its simplicity, proves effective on basic datasets while remaining computationally tractable. We then introduce an enhanced iterative mutation-based search that leverages genetic algorithm principles to improve the greedy baseline.

Practical Greedy Search

The core challenge in template search is that evaluating all possible feature subsets is computationally prohibitive. To address this, we propose a practical greedy approach operating on a sampled candidate set.

We first define the subset feature loss function that measures the quality of using a feature subset
b
to make an inference about an instance
x,y
:

e(x_{\textbf{b}},y)=l(\widehat{y}(x_{\textbf{b}}),y)+\lambda\sum_{b\in\textbf{b}}c(b)

(3)

This function combines prediction loss with acquisition cost, directly corresponding to the inner minimization in our objective function
eq. 1
.

An ideal greedy algorithm would evaluate
eq. 3
for all possible feature subsets
\textbf{b}\in\mathcal{C}_{\text{ideal}}\equiv\{\textbf{b}^{\prime}\mid o_{\text{init}}\in\textbf{b}^{\prime}\subseteq[D]\}
and iteratively select the best templates. However, with
|\mathcal{C}|=2^{D-1}
possible subsets, this approach is computationally infeasible. As a result, instead of exhaustive search, we randomly sample a candidate collection
\mathcal{C}=\{\textbf{b}^{(s)}\mid\textbf{b}^{(s)}\sim\mathcal{C}_{\text{ideal}}\}_{s=1}^{S}
of size
S\ll 2^{D-1}
. Our greedy algorithm then operates on this manageable candidate set
\mathcal{C}
.

Our greedy algorithm initialize template collection with the single best template among the collection, i.e.
\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{0}=\left\{\argmin_{\textbf{b}\in\mathcal{C}}\frac{1}{\lvert\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}\rvert}\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}}e(x_{\textbf{b}},y)\right\}
, and iteratively adds templates. At iteration
t
, we select the best candidate
\textbf{b}^{\star}_{t}
to add to our current collection
\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{t-1}
, forming
\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{t}=\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{t-1}\cup\{\textbf{b}^{\star}_{t}\}
. This process continues until we reach the desired number of templates
T
. Specifically, for each remaining candidate
\widetilde{\textbf{b}}\in\mathcal{C}\backslash\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{t-1}
, we evaluate how much the overall loss would decrease if we added this template to our current collection. We select the candidate that yields the minimum average loss:

\textbf{b}^{\star}_{t}=\argmin_{\widetilde{\textbf{b}}\in\mathcal{C}\backslash\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{t-1}}{\frac{1}{\lvert\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}\rvert}\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}}{\!\!\!\!\min\!\left({e\left(x_{\widetilde{\textbf{b}}},y\right),\min_{\textbf{b}\in\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{t-1}}\!\!\!{e\left(x_{\textbf{b}},y\right)}}\right)}}

(4)

We then update our collection as
\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{t}=\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{t-1}\cup\{\textbf{b}^{{\star}}_{t}\}
and repeat until we have selected
T
templates.

4.3
Nearest Neighbors Template AFA Policy

Thus far, we have focused on discovering an optimal collection of feature templates
\mathcal{B}^{\star}
. We now turn to the important question of how to dynamically utilize these templates during inference to guide feature acquisition decisions. At each step, our policy must select which template to follow and which feature within that template to acquire next.

Our deployable policy operates in two stages: template selection and feature acquisition. Given current observations
x_{\textbf{o}}
, we first select the most promising template:

\textbf{b}^{\star}=\argmin_{\textbf{b}\in\mathcal{B}}\left\{\widehat{l_{\textbf{b}}}(x_{\textbf{o}})+\lambda\sum_{j\in\textbf{b}\backslash\textbf{o}}c(j)\right\}

(5)

where
\widehat{l_{\textbf{b}}}(x_{\textbf{o}})
estimates the prediction loss when using template
b
given current observations, and
\lambda\sum_{j\in\textbf{b}\backslash\textbf{o}}c(j)
represents the remaining acquisition cost for unobserved features in template
b
. Once a template is selected, we acquire the cheapest unobserved feature from that template:

\pi(x_{\textbf{o}})=\argmin_{a\in(\textbf{b}^{\star}\cup\{\varnothing\})\backslash\textbf{o}}c(a)

(6)

where
c(\varnothing)=0
terminates acquisition.

The key challenge lies in estimating
\widehat{l_{\textbf{b}}}(x_{\textbf{o}})
without access to ground truth labels. Since acquisition costs
c(j)
are known, this reduces to estimating the prediction loss
l(\widehat{y}(x_{\textbf{b}}),y)
for each template.

To resolve this, we employ a k-nearest neighbor (kNN) approach. That is, for each template
b
and training instance
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in\mathcal{D_{\text{train}}}
, we precompute and cache the loss
l(\widehat{y}(x^{\prime}_{\textbf{b}}),y^{\prime})
. During inference, we estimate
\widehat{l_{\textbf{b}}}(x_{\textbf{o}})
by finding the
k
nearest training instances based on observed features
\lVert x_{\textbf{o}}-x^{\prime}_{\textbf{o}}\rVert_{2}
and averaging their cached losses over the neighbors.

4.4
Parametric Student TAFA Policy

Above, we introduced a deployable nearest neighbors template-based policy, which greatly reduces the search space for improved performance.
This section proposes an imitation learning
(
Hussein et al. 2017
)
approach to clone the behavior of a kNN TAFA policy to any class of computationally efficient parametric estimator (and avoids the need for kNN during inference). Moreover, we show that this technique may be used to learn interpretable policies through decision-tree-based students.

We propose to utilize DAgger
(
Ross, Gordon, and Bagnell 2011
)
with our TAFA policy as the expert.
Initially, DAgger collects state vectors that consist of the observed features concatenated with the feature masks, along with the corresponding TAFA expert actions that serve as the labels to the student. We then train a parametric classifier on the aggregated expert demonstrations.
Training alternates between collecting expert demonstration (on student policy rollouts) and updating student policy, and hence gradually incorporates the student’s own decisions into improving its robustness and further reducing the distribution shift between training and deployment conditions
(
Ross, Gordon, and Bagnell 2011
)
.

Interpretability via Decision Trees

Parametric AFA policies are often black-box,
which makes it difficult to understand and debug in practice; in a safety-critical setting like healthcare, black-box models can even cause both legal and ethical issues.
To alleviate the issue, we propose utilizing the aforementioned DAgger imitation learning
(
Ross, Gordon, and Bagnell 2011
)
to distill the kNN TAFA expert

§

into
an ensemble of compact interpretable decision trees (for different steps of the acquisition process) tailored for the AFA task

The ensemble of decision trees
\{\text{Tree}_{m}\}_{m=1}^{K}

6
Conclusion

In this work, we introduce a novel template-based approach for AFA, where a template is a subset of feature combinations that is potentially worth acquiring jointly. As a result, “training” a template-based AFA (TAFA) policy becomes a problem of searching for a collection of sensible templates that the policy should consider before making a decision. We propose formulating the problem of searching for a collection of templates as a set optimization problem. We show that the objective function is a submodular set function, which comes with a theoretical approximation guarantee. Building on this, we develop an iterative mutation-based search algorithm that further shows it improves the performance of a greedy search, as confirmed by the ablations. For interpretability, we distill the resulting TAFA policy into an ensemble of per-cardinality trees that act on templates rather than individual features. This ensemble not only outperforms standard AFA baselines but also yields markedly shorter - and therefore more transparent - decision paths than existing interpretable RL methods. Moreover, TAFA archives significantly lower inference latency, showing practical efficiency for real-world deployment.
