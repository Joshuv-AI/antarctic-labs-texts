Effect of midwife-led planned education on psychosocial health and obstetric outcomes among pregnant women in rural areas during the COVID-19 pandemic: a quasi-experimental study
Authors: Sabriye Uçan Yamaç, Songül Aktaş
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe COVID-19 pandemic has negatively influenced the physical and psychosocial health, social interactions, and socioeconomic status of pregnant women. This study aimed to evaluate the impact of midwife-led planned education on psychosocial health and obstetric outcomes among pregnant women in rural areas during the COVID-19 pandemic.MethodsThe sample for this quasi-experimental study consisted of 74 primiparous pregnant women (37 experimental, 37 control group) in rural Türkiye. The planned training program comprised four sessions in total, including two sessions conducted during the antenatal period and two sessions in the postpartum period. The training content addressed key topics such as prevention of COVID-19 infection, coping with and adapting to physiological and psychological changes during the perinatal period, birth pain and its management, newborn care, and breastfeeding practices. Each session had an average duration of 30–35 min. The training sessions were performed face-to-face using didactic instruction, demonstration methods, and supplementary written and visual brochures. Statistical analysis was performed with the Chi-Square test, Student-t test, Mann–Whitney U test, Paired t-test, and Wilcoxon test.ResultsRisk perception in pregnancy, pregnancy stress, and perceived trauma related to childbirth were lower in the experimental group than in the control group. Of all vaginal births, 74.3% were performed with the help of a midwife. The experimental group demonstrated higher levels of positive birth perception, satisfaction with the assisting midwife and obstetrician, breastfeeding self-efficacy, and maternal attachment than the control group (p < 0.05). There was no statistically significant difference between the groups in terms of postpartum depressive symptoms (p > 0.05).ConclusionDuring the COVID-19 pandemic, planned education provided by midwives to pregnant women demonstrated a positive impact on psychosocial health and obstetric outcomes. To promote psychosocial well-being and optimize obstetric outcomes among women living in rural areas during the perinatal period, it is essential to implement individualized, continuous, and follow-up-oriented midwifery care. Background

The COVID-19 pandemic has adversely affected the physical and psychosocial well-being, social relationships, and economic conditions of pregnant women. These negative impacts have primarily arisen from heightened concerns about virus transmission to themselves and their fetuses, the enforcement of social isolation measures and quarantine protocols, reduced social support networks, restricted access to essential antenatal, intranatal, and postnatal healthcare services, and difficulties in obtaining support from midwives providing these services [1–3]. Pregnancy and residence in rural areas during the COVID-19 pandemic constitute significant risk factors for adverse obstetric and psychosocial outcomes. To mitigate these risks, it is essential to ensure the provision of high-quality prenatal and postpartum midwifery care, accompanied by continuous and comprehensive midwifery support [4, 5]. During the pandemic, some pregnant women encountered psychosocial challenges, including stress, anxiety, and depression, about their health, the well-being of their baby, breastfeeding, and the choice of birth method [6–8]. Maintaining psychosocial well-being during pregnancy is essential for safeguarding the physical and emotional health of both the mother and the infant. It also establishes a foundation for reducing the risk of mental health difficulties throughout pregnancy and the postpartum period [9].

A lack of adequate information during the pandemic, coupled with the spread of inaccurate or unreliable content, may contribute to increased levels of anxiety and depression among pregnant women [10, 11]. A study conducted by Aktaş et al. (2022) involving 352 pregnant women reported that 22.5% of participants expressed concerns about the accuracy and reliability of the information available to them and perceived their information sources as inadequate. Additionally, 39.6% indicated a need for support from healthcare professionals during their pregnancy [3]. Pregnant women in rural areas experienced lower levels of anxiety and depression than their counterparts in urban centers during the COVID-19 pandemic, which could be attributed to the isolation and reduced risk of virus transmission that rural living offers, creating a sense of safety. However, it is important to note that women in rural areas may also face challenges and increased stress, including limited access to information about pregnancy and childbirth, exposure to inaccurate and unreliable information, difficulties in accessing healthcare services, inadequate availability of basic needs such as education and transportation, and heightened concerns about their own health and the well-being of their unborn child during the pandemic [8, 11]. Increased stress levels and increased risk perception can adversely affect the psychosocial well-being of pregnant women. If these disturbances in psychosocial health persist into the postpartum period, they may lead to several negative outcomes, including weakened maternal–infant attachment, increased parental stress, and a higher risk of emotional and behavioral difficulties in the child’s later life [12].

The geographical conditions in rural areas, coupled with economic challenges and the restrictions imposed by the pandemic and quarantine measures, pose risks to the physical and psychosocial health of pregnant women and mothers. Moreover, in certain rural areas, gender inequality further compounds these difficulties. Women living in such regions often encounter limited access to educational opportunities, early marriage resulting in adolescent motherhood, restricted income-generating employment options, and ongoing socioeconomic hardship [4, 6].

Poverty in rural areas and lack of access to health services are important risk factors for women, mothers, families, and public health [6, 11, 13]. One of the primary objectives of the United Nations’ Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) until 2030 is to “address poverty as a barrier to accessing education and healthcare services, promote gender equality and women’s empowerment, ensure equal education opportunities, and prevent maternal and neonatal mortality”. In the context of the COVID-19 pandemic, the demand for antenatal, intrapartum, and postpartum care has significantly increased for pregnant women and mothers in rural areas [5, 11, 14]. If the healthcare needs of pregnant women are not adequately addressed, their psychosocial well-being may deteriorate during the perinatal period [1, 3, 10, 15]. The deterioration of psychosocial well-being during pregnancy contributes to increased maternal stress. High maternal stress levels are associated with a decline in pregnant women’s birth self-efficacy, reducing their confidence in their ability to successfully manage childbirth [16, 17]. A decrease in childbirth self-efficacy and an inability to effectively cope with challenges encountered during labor—such as labor pain—may result in a greater reliance on medical interventions (e.g., epidural analgesia, synthetic oxytocin), lower maternal satisfaction with the birthing process, increased preference for cesarean delivery [17, 18], and adverse obstetric outcomes, including negative birth experiences and birth trauma [19, 20]. A deterioration in women’s psychosocial health and adverse obstetric outcomes during the perinatal period increase the risk of maternal and neonatal morbidity and mortality [18, 21].

Relevant literature is scarce in terms of structured ‘planned education’ studies that monitor the needs of pregnant women in rural areas during the pandemic, encompassing the entire pregnancy, labor, and postnatal periods, and addressing both psychosocial and obstetric outcomes [21–23]. There is a growing need for experimental studies involving pregnant and postpartum women living in rural areas, both during extraordinary circumstances such as pandemics and natural disasters and under normal conditions [4, 5]. Factors such as experiencing pregnancy and motherhood for the first time, living in rural regions, and living through a pandemic may further increase women’s physical and psychosocial needs and increase their requirement for midwifery support [1, 3, 10, 24]. Accordingly, this study was designed to address this identified gap and investigate the impact of planned education provided by midwives to pregnant women in the third trimester in rural areas during the COVID-19 pandemic on perinatal psychosocial health and obstetric outcomes. This study is significant as it contributes to the continuity of midwifery care in rural areas and supports the protection and improvement of maternal and neonatal physical and mental health through education provided to women during extraordinary circumstances such as the pandemic. The findings of this study are expected to address an existing gap in the literature and provide a basis for future research in this field. Moreover, the study may contribute to the World Health Organization’s policies aimed at improving the quality of antenatal care for a positive pregnancy experience (2016) and intrapartum care for a positive birth experience (2018). In addition, it has the potential to support the 2030 Sustainable Development Goal of “empowering all women and girls”.

Method

The design

This study is a quasi-experimental study with an experimental and a control group.

Participants

The study was conducted with nulliparous women registered at health centers in the rural areas of a province in southern Türkiye. These health centers predominantly deliver preventive health services, with midwives serving as the primary healthcare providers within these settings.

The sample size was determined using power analysis. The power analysis of the study, for α = 0,05 significance level, β = 0,20, 0,80 test power in the experimental and control group, based on equal sample size ≅ 0,70 for each group, n = 35, respectively [25]. The study sample comprised 74 pregnant women, with 37 participants assigned to the experimental group and 37 to the control group. The research process involved identifying pregnant women with a gestational age of 28 weeks or more from 37 health centers. The health centers where the services were delivered were situated at a maximum distance of 120 km from the city center. Among these health centers, 17 were identified as having a total of 81 pregnant women who met the gestational age criteria. However, seven of these women were excluded from the sample due to factors such as twin pregnancy, gestational diabetes, or being non–Turkish-speaking immigrant women. Consequently, the remaining 74 pregnant women who met all inclusion criteria were enrolled in the study, with 37 assigned to the experimental group and 37 to the control group.

In this study, participants were allocated to either the experimental or control group through simple randomization performed using Excel software. The researcher compiled a list of pregnant women from the 17 health centers who met the inclusion criteria and numbered them sequentially from 1 to 74. Random assignment was then executed using computer-generated random numbers to ensure an equal (1:1) distribution between the two groups. The inclusion criteria for pregnant women in the study were as follows: voluntary participation; age 20 years or older; attainment of at least a primary education; ability to speak and understand Turkish; gestational age between 28 and 35 weeks; nulliparity (no previous births); and classification as having a low-risk pregnancy. Pregnant women with known psychiatric disorders, visual impairments, and hearing impairments were excluded from the study.

Intervention: planned education in the antenatal and postnatal periods

Planned education program

The development of the educational program was based on the prenatal, intrapartum, and postpartum care guidelines established by the Turkish Ministry of Health. Pregnant women in the experimental group received the planned educational intervention, whereas those in the control group continued to receive standard routine care services. Various education techniques were employed in this study, including didactic expression, demonstration, and the use of written visual brochures. Didactic expression involved interactive elements such as question-and-answer sessions, discussions, and video demonstrations.

Planned education: trainer and education environment

The planned educational program was developed by two researchers (SA and SUY) and implemented by one researcher (SUY), an academic midwife. Prior to the study, the academic midwife had extensive professional experience in the health centers of the province where the research was conducted, providing familiarity with the local context and community dynamics. During the study period, the academic midwife maintained flexible working hours and collaborated closely with the participants to establish a schedule that accommodated their individual needs and availability. Families in this region were primarily engaged in greenhouse farming. It was also observed that the pregnant women involved in the study actively contributed to greenhouse farming activities alongside their families during the study period. Considering the proximity of their residences to the greenhouses, the educational sessions were conducted either in open-air spaces situated in front of the greenhouses or in the gardens adjacent to the participants’ homes. The educational sessions in this study were conducted through face-to-face interactions between the pregnant women and the educator, creating an interactive and engaging learning environment. In light of the ongoing COVID-19 pandemic, all sessions were carried out in accordance with recommended infection prevention measures to minimize the risk of virus transmission.

Education techniques

Various education techniques were employed in this study, including didactic expression, demonstration, and the use of written visual brochures. Didactic expression involved interactive elements such as question-and-answer sessions, discussions, and video demonstrations. As part of the planned education, a comprehensive educational brochure was created to support the educational program. A newborn baby model was used specifically for demonstrating breastfeeding and newborn bathing techniques. While delivering some topics in education (such as breathing and pushing techniques in labor), video images were used. Demonstration techniques were employed to explain practical aspects such as massage techniques, different birth positions, pushing methods, and breastfeeding practices aimed at reducing birth pain. To complement the educational program, a printed educational brochure was developed and distributed to the participants. Furthermore, following the completion of data collection in both the experimental and control groups, the brochure was also provided to the women in the control group to ensure equal access to the educational content.

Planned education in the antenatal and postnatal periods

In the experimental group, a planned educational program was implemented for pregnant women and mothers during the antenatal and postnatal periods. The term planned education refers to a structured program developed and delivered by two researchers (SA and SUY) with clearly defined objectives and goals, guided by an educational manual. The program incorporated appropriate instructional techniques, promoted active participant engagement, and included interim evaluations to assess learning progress.The planned educational program comprised a total of four sessions, including two antenatal and two postpartum sessions. Each session lasted approximately 30–35 min on average, with some extending up to 40 min depending on the individual needs of the pregnant or postpartum participants. All sessions were conducted face-to-face. Detailed information regarding the content of the educational program, as well as the study materials and data collection forms, is provided in the supplementary file 1.

Control group

The control group received only standard midwifery care covering education and counseling (nutrition, hygiene, vaccination, breastfeeding, etc.) and physical examinations (Leopold’s maneuvers, vaginal examinations, and newborn care practices). All participants in the control group received the same educational brochures that had been distributed to the experimental group after the completion of the data collection process.

The Perception of Pregnancy Risk Questionnaire (PPRQ): The questionnaire, originally developed by Heaman and Gupton (2009) [26], was adapted into Turkish and validated by Evcili and Dağlar (2019) [27]; it consists of nine items and functions as a visual analog measurement instrument. It comprises nine items and is a visual analog measurement tool. The answers to the questions are in the form of a linear line with the expressions “no risk at all” and “extremely high risk” at the bottom of each question. It has two sub-dimensions: “risk perception towards the baby” and “risk perception towards the self”. The total score is obtained by dividing the total score of all items by 9. Minimum and maximum scores can be obtained from the scale. A high score indicates a high-risk perception of the pregnant woman in relation to herself and her baby. The total Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the scale was 0.84.

The scale yields scores ranging from 0 to 144, with higher scores indicating greater levels of perceived prenatal stress. The scale includes 11 reverse-scored items and yields total scores ranging from 39 to 195, with higher scores indicating greater perceived trauma. The Cronbach alpha value of the scale is 0.92. The α value of EPDS was determined as 0.79, and the cutoff score was determined as 12/13. The scale scores range from 0 to 30, with higher scores indicating an increased risk of postpartum depression [35].

The Breastfeeding Self-Efficacy Scale (BSES): The BSES was developed by Dennis and Faux [36], and the first version of the scale included 33 items. Later, Dennis [37] reduced the scale to 14 items and developed the Breastfeeding Self-Efficacy Scale Short Form. The α value of this short form was 0.94 [36, 37]. The Turkish adaptation of the scale was conducted by Tokat et al. (2010), with a reported Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.86. The scale is a 5-point Likert type and is evaluated as 1 “I am not sure at all”, 2 “I am not sure”, 3 “Sometimes I am sure”, 4 “I am sure”, and 5 “I am totally sure”. The test yields scores ranging from 14 to 70, with higher scores indicating greater breastfeeding self-efficacy [38]. We applied to the mothers between the 2nd and 5th postnatal days, the Descriptive Information Form and the BSES. The administration of the MAI, TBPS, and EPDS scales is recommended at four weeks postpartum; therefore, in this study, these scales were administered four weeks after birth.

Ethical considerations

Before the study, approval was obtained from the Turkish Ministry of Health (Ankara) COVID-19 Research Ethics Approval Unit (Date: 02.06.2021, Number: GO 2021/205), and written and verbal consent were obtained from the participants. The study was conducted according to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) 25.0 software. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used for the determination of normal distribution. The frequency and percentage values were used to present the data. The Chi-Square test was used in the intergroup comparisons of the participants’ categorical data, such as education level, family type, and level of satisfaction with childbirth. In two independent group comparisons (such as experimental and control group data), quantitative data showing a normal distribution were evaluated with the Student t-test, and those not showing a normal distribution were evaluated with the Mann–Whitney U test. The paired-sample t-test was used to compare normally distributed dependent groups, while the Wilcoxon test was used to compare non-normally distributed dependent groups. Statistical significance was accepted as p < 0.05 [39].

Discussion

The study demonstrated that pregnant women in the experimental group, residing in rural areas during the COVID-19 pandemic, demonstrated lower levels of pregnancy-related risk perception and stress compared to those in the control group. This finding underscores the positive impact of midwives’ antenatal education on pregnancy and childbirth, combined with their continuous support, in reducing pregnancy-related stress and risk perception, while enhancing the psychosocial well-being of pregnant women. The education program covered various topics such as the effects of COVID-19 on pregnancy and the fetus, measures for protection against COVID-19, psychological changes in the third trimester of pregnancy, and coping strategies for labor pain. The individualized structure of the educational program, combined with the researcher’s provision of 24-h telephone access to the midwife, indicates that continuous accessibility to midwifery support may effectively reduce stress and risk perception among pregnant women and increase their preference for vaginal birth. Similarly, Aksoy et al. (2021) conducted an experimental study during the first wave of the COVID-19 pandemic, which demonstrated that tele-education delivered by midwives effectively reduced prenatal distress (stress, anxiety, depression, etc.) among pregnant women [1]. These findings emphasize the critical role of midwives and other healthcare professionals involved in obstetric care in identifying the underlying causes of stress and levels of risk perception among pregnant women and in delivering appropriate, targeted healthcare interventions accordingly. According to Aktaş et al. (2022), pregnant women’s concerns about their own health and that of their babies increased during the COVID-19 pandemic, and they expressed a need for more information regarding the mode of birth and breastfeeding, along with additional support from healthcare professionals throughout the pandemic [3]. Evidence shows that pregnant women experienced higher levels of stress, anxiety, and depression during the COVID-19 pandemic than in the pre-pandemic period [7, 40, 41].

Our study highlights that being pregnant during the pandemic and living in rural areas should be recognized as significant risk factors for the decline of perinatal mental health and adverse obstetric outcomes. The UN Sustainable Development Goals, with a target to achieve a healthy and high-quality life, inclusive education, and gender equality for all pregnant women and mothers by 2030, support the importance of this issue [42].

The experimental group in this study had higher levels of breastfeeding self-efficacy and maternal attachment than those in the control group. This outcome can be attributed to the inclusion of various topics such as “Mother-infant interaction immediately after birth (skin-to-skin contact, breastfeeding, etc.)” in the antenatal education program and “COVID-19 and breastfeeding, the advantages of breast milk, newborn care” in the postnatal education program, the implementation of demonstration techniques to address breastfeeding challenges, and the delivery of education to mothers during both the antenatal and postnatal periods, which may have decreased mothers’ stress levels and improved their maternal functioning and breastfeeding skills potentially. Turan and Aksoy conducted an experimental study and reported that midwifery care, consisting of three home visits, provided to primiparous mothers resulted in increased posttraumatic growth and maternal attachment [43]. Similarly, in a study conducted in the USA, mothers who gave birth during the COVID-19 pandemic (1611 mothers) experienced higher levels of birth-related stress, lower maternal attachment, and encountered more breastfeeding difficulties than those who gave birth before the pandemic (614 mothers) [20].

Planned education delivered in this study may have contributed to the increase in breastfeeding self-efficacy by reducing anxiety in mothers. In a study conducted by Ay Palancı and Aktaş (2021) with 245 mothers, a positive relationship was reported between breastfeeding self-efficacy and maternal functional status (self-care, social support, adaptation to the maternal role, etc.) of mothers in the postpartum period [44]. The presence of postpartum depressive symptoms was not statistically significant in the experimental and control groups (p > 0.05). In contrast to our study, several studies have demonstrated that education has a positive effect on depression levels [45, 46]. The lack of difference in our study could potentially be attributed to the geographical distance of women living in rural areas, particularly far from metropolitan areas, and the constant care and support provided by midwives in health houses, which might contribute to low levels of postpartum depression in both the experimental and control groups. During the COVID-19 pandemic, concerns and uncertainties surrounding breastfeeding have harmed the motivation of mothers to breastfeed [3]. In a randomized controlled study, pregnant women received breastfeeding education during the COVID-19 pandemic, and the fear of breastfeeding in the first month postpartum was assessed, and it was reported that antenatal breastfeeding education reduced the fear of postpartum breastfeeding [47].

The experimental group in this study had higher rates of vaginal birth, perceived birth satisfaction at a “good” level, and reported higher satisfaction levels with both the midwife and obstetrician than the control group. Additionally, in the experimental group, mothers who gave vaginal birth had lower levels of perceived trauma related to childbirth compared to the control group (p < 0.05), suggesting that the midwifery care provided during the antenatal and postpartum periods had a positive impact on the perception of birth and increased satisfaction with the birthing experience. The study also indicates that including topics such as COVID-19, birth, and coping methods for birth pains in the content of the planned educational program during the antenatal period may be effective in promoting and achieving vaginal birth. Women living in rural areas are reported to prefer vaginal birth more in the relevant literature.

The greater birth satisfaction and increased satisfaction with both midwives and obstetrician observed in the experimental group relative to the control group may be attributable to the structured antenatal education provided. The antenatal education may have improved pregnant women’s coping strategies during childbirth, reduced their fear of childbirth, and enhanced their self-efficacy regarding vaginal birth. Consistent with this, Uludağ et al. (2022) reported that online antenatal education delivered during the COVID-19 pandemic reduced anxiety and childbirth-related fear, increased birth preparedness, and alleviated concerns related to COVID-19 [19].

The study demonstrated that mothers in the experimental group who experienced vaginal birth reported lower levels of traumatic birth perception compared to those in the control group. This finding is consistent with the observation that mothers in the experimental group also reported higher birth satisfaction and reduced perceptions of birth trauma relative to the control group. Various studies have indicated a connection between traumatic birth perception and factors like “birth memories” [48], satisfaction with the midwife, overall birth experience [49, 50], and maternal attachment [3]. In a study by Aydın et al. involving mothers who experienced vaginal birth during the COVID-19 pandemic, a positive correlation was identified between birth satisfaction and maternal attachment, while a negative correlation was observed between traumatic birth perception and maternal attachment. Additionally, the study demonstrated that dissatisfaction with healthcare professionals during vaginal birth was associated with a five-fold reduction in maternal attachment, highlighting the impact of negative experiences with care providers on the mother–infant bond [3]. High birth satisfaction among primiparous women who experience vaginal birth has been shown to significantly influence their preference for vaginal delivery in subsequent pregnancies [24, 51, 52]. Moreover, one study reported that midwifery care during the postpartum period contributed to enhanced posttraumatic growth, highlighting the positive effects of such care on mothers’ psychological well-being and personal development [39].

Limitations of the study

In this study, data collection and individualized, face-to-face planned education were conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic, with adherence to mask use and social distancing guidelines. The participants were women residing in rural (mostly highland) areas of a single province in southern Türkiye. The planned education was delivered by a single midwife (SUY), one of the co-authoring midwives, and the researchers personally covered the expenses associated with reaching these participants.

Conclusion

During the COVID-19 pandemic, planned antenatal and postnatal education delivered by midwives to pregnant women in rural areas was associated with positive effects on perinatal psychosocial health and obstetric outcomes. To optimize psychosocial well-being and improve obstetric outcomes for women in rural settings across the antenatal, intrapartum, and postpartum periods, the provision of individualized, continuous, and follow-up-oriented midwifery care is essential. This approach facilitates personalized support and guidance throughout the pregnancy continuum, contributing to enhanced maternal health outcomes and a more positive childbirth and postpartum experience. Future research is recommended to assess the long-term effects of midwife-led education through longitudinal follow-up studies and to explore the experiences and perspectives of pregnant and postpartum women using qualitative methodologies.
