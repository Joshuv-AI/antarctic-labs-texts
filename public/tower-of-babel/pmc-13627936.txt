The genome sequence of Dermanura phaeotis (Chiroptera, Phyllostomidae, Stenodermatinae; Miller, 1902)
Authors: Melissa R de Waal, Nancy B Simmons, Brian P O'Toole, Ning Zhang, Jonathan L Gray, Erick Duarte, Linelle Abueg, Samuel C Talbot, Kirsty McCaffrey, Bonhwang Koo, Giulio Formenti, Meike Mai, Myrtani Pieri, Erich D Jarvis, Emma C Teeling, Sonja C. Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We present a genome assembly from an individual male Dermanura phaeotis (Chordata; Mammalia; Chiroptera; Phyllostomidae). The genome sequence is 2.2 Gb in span. The majority of the assembly is scaffolded into 30 chromosomal pseudomolecules, with the X and Y sex chromosomes assembled.

Introduction

Dermanura is a clade of small, stripe-faced frugivorous bats in the Subfamily Stenodermatinae. For many years these bats were treated as a subgenus of Artibeus (e.g., Koopman, 1994; Simmons, 2005) but more recently have been treated as distinct at the genus level ( Mammal Diversity Database, 2026; Simmons and Cirranello, 2025). Dermanura bats are common throughout the Neotropics with multiple species often occurring in sympatry ( Emmons and Feer, 1997; Reid, 2009). As shown in Figure 1, Dermanura phaeotis currently includes 3 subspecies, D. p. phaeotis (Atlantic side of Central America from Veracruz [Mexico] to Costa Rica), D. p. nanus (western Mexico from Sinaloa to Guerroro), and D. p. palatinus (Pacific side of Central America from Oaxaca, Mexico, to Costa Rica) ( Koopman, 1994; Simmons and Cirranello, 2025). It does not include Dermanura rava (= ravus), which was previously treated as a subspecies of phaeotis (e.g., Koopman, 1994; Simmons, 2005) but which is now regarded as a distinct species ( Hoofer et al., 2008; Solari et al., 2009; Mammal Diversity Database, 2024; Simmons and Cirranello, 2025). As currently recognized, Dermanura phaeotis occurs in Mexico, Belize, Guatemala, Honduras, El Salvador, Nicaragua, Costa Rica, and possibly in Panama and Colombia ( Koopman, 1994; Mammal Diversity Database, 2024). Reports of this species from elsewhere in northern South America may be in error and may date from the time when rava was included in phaeotis.

Position of Dermanura phaeotis in the phylogeny of Family Phyllostomidae.

Dermanura phaeotis is one of 11 species currently recognized in the genus Dermanura ( Simmons and Cirranello, 2025). Dermanura belongs to the Tribe Stenodermatini in the Subfamily Stenodermatinae, which currently includes 19 genera and 76 species ( Baker et al., 2016; Cirranello et al., 2016; Simmons and Cirranello, 2025). Phylogeny based on Hoofer et al. (2008), Solari et al. (2009), and Rojas et al. (2016).

Like other members of the genus Dermanura, D. phaeotis ( Figure 2) has brown fur and black wing membranes, white facial stripes, a broad muzzle, a horseshoe-shaped upper tooth row, and central upper incisors that are bilobed and less than twice as long as the outer incisors ( Emmons and Feer, 1997; Reid, 2009). Dermanura phaeotis can be distinguished from congeners based on a series of morphological traits including the following: small size (forearm 35–40 mm; greatest length of skull 17–21 mm; 9–15 g); dorsal fur faintly tricolored and sandy brown or gray-brown; fur moderately short and smooth (4–6 mm on upper back; prominent white facial stripes that are crisply outlined with upper stripe extending to the front of the ear; ear pinnae and noseleaf pale brown and edged with yellow or white; horseshoe of noseleaf free from upper lip; uropatagium U-shaped (9–11 mm) with dorsal surface almost naked; uropatagium not fringed though may have a few short hairs; postorbital and preorbital processes absent; last upper and lower molars absent; dental formula 2/2, 1/1, 2/2, 2/2 x 2 = 28 ( Timm, 1985; Koopman, 1994; Reid, 2009).

Dermanura phaeotis is locally common and is found in lowland evergreen and deciduous forests up to 1,200 m, and it also occurs in secondary forest and disturbed habitats ( Timm, 1985; Reid, 2009; Miller et al., 2015). Dermanura phaeotis feeds on fruits from understory trees and shrubs but also occasionally eats pollen and insects ( Reid, 2009; Miller et al., 2015; Ingala et al., 2021). It is a tent-making bat that roosts under modified palm, banana, and Heliconia leaves ( Timm, 1985, 1987; Reid, 2009; Miller et al., 2015; García-Méndez and Santos-Moreno, 2020; López-González, 2020).

Dermanura phaeotis is classified as Least Concern in the IUCN Redlist of Threatened Species because of its wide distribution, presumed large population size, occurrence in a number of protected areas, tolerance to some degree of habitat modification, and because it is unlikely to be declining at the rate required to qualify for listing in a threatened category ( Miller et al., 2015).

Genome sequence report

The genome was sequenced from a single male Dermanura phaeotis (field number BZ-12, catalog number AMNH:Mammalogy:280606 collected at the Lamanai Outpost Lodge, Indian Church, Orange Walk District, Belize, on 8 November 2021. A total of 25-fold coverage in Pacific Biosciences Hi-Fi long reads (contig N50 57.9 Mb) was generated after removal of all reads shorter than 10 kb. Primary assembly contigs were scaffolded with chromosome confirmation Hi-C data. The final assembly has a total length of 2.2 Gb in 106 sequence scaffolds with a scaffold N50 of 154 Mbp ( Table 1 ). The majority, 99.4%, of the assembly sequence was assigned to 30 chromosomal-level scaffolds, representing 14 autosomes (numbered by sequence length, and the X + Y sex chromosomes). Chromosomal pseudomolecules in the genome assembly of Dermanura phaeotis are shown in Table 2. The assembly has a BUSCO ( Simão et al., 2015) completeness of 97.8% using the vertebrata odb10 reference set. The assembly is fully phased, and both haplotypes are deposited therein.

Methods

The Dermanura phaeotis specimen was a male individual collected on an American Museum of Natural History (AMNH) field expedition near Lamanai Archaeological Reserve in the Orange Walk District of Belize. The individual sampled was identified as D. phaeotis based on morphological traits (e.g., noseleaf structure and color, fur color and stiping) as described above. The bat was caught in a ground-level mist net set in the gardens of Lamanai Outpost Lodge (17.75156 N, 88.65376 W). All efforts were made to minimize any distress or suffering by the animal. The individual sample was subjected to minimal handling after capture, and it was held in a clean cloth bag after capture as per best practices for field containment of bats. After species identification, the individual was euthanized humanely the same night it was captured. The animal was euthanized by isoflurane inhalation (Formula CHF 2OCClHCF 3, CAS number 26675–46-7; Manufacturer Piramal Critical Care, Supplier US Pharmacy Systems, Product code 5034-1FL-SOL-ORA), an approved and humane euthanasia method that rapidly causes unconsciousness and eventually death. Bats euthanized by this method are rendered unconscious within seconds due to their high respiration rate, and death occurs within a minute or two with no significant suffering by the animal. Capture and sampling were conducted under Belize Forest Department Permit FD/WL/1/21(16) and samples were exported under Belize Forest Department permit FD/WL/7/22(07). All work was conducted with approval by the AMNH Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (AMNHIACUC-20210614). All data were recorded and reported in accordance with the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines ( Percie du Sert et al., 2020) – see data availability section and Table 1.

Tissues were removed from the subject individual immediately following euthanasia and were flash-frozen in a liquid nitrogen dry shipper, with the cold chain maintained from field to museum to laboratory.

DNA was extracted using Nanobind extraction from liver tissue following the Circulomics Nanobind HMW DNA Extraction Protocol. Pacific Biosciences HiFi libraries were constructed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Hi-C data was generated using the Arima Hi-C+ High Coverage kit from the same tissue sample. Sequencing was performed by the Genomic Operations DNA Pipelines at Paratus Sciences on Pacific Biosciences Sequel IIe (HiFi reads) and Illumina NextSeq 2000 (Hi-C) instruments.

Assembly was carried out following the Vertebrate Genome Project pipeline v2.0 ( Larivière et al., 2024) with a few modifications as follows. An initial QC analysis was performed on the raw BAM file using FastQC ( https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/). BAM files were converted to fastq format and merged for downstream processing. Genome size was estimated using GenomeScope2 ( Vurture et al., 2017). Hifiasm was used for genome assembly ( Cheng et al., 2021). The quality of the assembly was evaluated using Merqury ( Rhie et al., 2020) and BUSCO ( Manni et al., 2021). Scaffolding with Hi-C data ( Rao et al. 2014) was carried out with YaHs ( Zhou et al. 2023). PretextView was implemented to generate Hi-C contact maps. Figure 3 – Figure 6 were generated using BlobToolKit ( Challis et al. 2020). Software utilised for the Dermanura phaeotis analysis are depicted in Table 3.

Ethics

The specimen used for genome sequencing was collected in the Orange Walk District of Belize under Belize Forest Department Permit FD/WL/1/21(16). Samples were exported under Belize Forest Department Permit FD/WL/7/22(07). All animal procedures were conducted with approval from the American Museum of Natural History Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (AMNHIACUC-20210614).

The individual was captured using a ground-level mist net and handled in accordance with established best practices for the safe capture and handling of wild bats. Following species identification, the animal was euthanized humanely using isoflurane inhalation overdose. All efforts were made to minimise animal suffering and distress. The study is reported in accordance with ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines.

The authors present a brief report on the genome assembly of Dermanura phaeotis using the standard VGP bioinformatics pipeline. This is a useful resource for the community with no major issues. I have listed several minor points the authors may want to address below.

- In the Methods, the authors indicate that "Assembly was carried out following the Vertebrate Genome Project pipeline v2.0 (Larivière et al., 2024) with a few modifications as follows." However, it is unclear which modifications these were because most of the steps listed in the following text seem consistent with the VGP 2.0 pipeline (Hifiasm for assembly, Hi-C scaffolding with YaHs, Merqury for quality assessment). I suggest the authors revise the passage to make it clearer which modifications were actually made and which steps are just brief descriptions of the VGP pipeline. Otherwise, the tools and version numbers used are well documented and cited.

- The authors state "As shown in Figure 1, Dermanura phaeotis currently includes 3 subspecies,". However, Figure 1 does not show that D. phaeotis includes any subspecies. Please modify the figure to show this or revise the text.

- As another reviewer pointed out, conformation is misspelt in "Primary assembly contigs were scaffolded with chromosome confirmation".

- Some words in the manuscript may have been accidentally written in bold font ("97.8%" and "liver").

- The Figure 5 y-axis label could be written out to clarify what exactly "input_cov" is.
