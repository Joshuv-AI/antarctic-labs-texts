Noncommutative Distances on Graphs: An Explicit Approach via Birkhoff-James Orthogonality
Pierre Clare, Chi-Kwong Li, Edward Poon, Eric Swartz

Abstract
We study the problem of calculating noncommutative distances on graphs, using techniques from linear algebra, specifically, Birkhoff-James orthogonality. A complete characterization of the solutions is obtained in the case when the underlying graph is a path.



1
Introduction

The notion of
noncommutative distance
emerged naturally from the reformulation of the distance in Riemannian geometry in terms of Dirac operators
[
Con94
, Chap. 6]
. More precisely, if
(\mathcal{M},g)
is a spin Riemannian manifold, the geodesic distance
\delta_{g}(p,q)
between two points on
\mathcal{M}
, defined as the infimum of the length of paths from
p
to
q
, can be expressed as:

\delta_{g}(p,q)=\sup\left\{\left|f(p)-f(q)\right|\>:\>f\in{\mathcal{A}}\>,\>\big\|[\partial_{M},f]\big\|\leqslant 1\right\}

(1)

where
\partial_{M}
is the Dirac operator associated with the spin structure on
\mathcal{M}
, acting as an unbounded self-adjoint operator on the Hilbert space
\mathfrak{H}
of
L^{2}
-spinors on
\mathcal{M}
and
{\mathcal{A}}
is the abelian von Neumann algebra of bounded measurable functions on
\mathcal{M}
, acting as multiplication operators on
\mathfrak{H}
.

This approach readily generalizes to noncommutative spaces. Indeed,
({\mathcal{A}},\mathfrak{H},\partial_{M})
is an example of a spectral triple in the sense of Connes and (
1
) allows one to define a noncommutative distance between pure states of a C*-algebra
A
represented on a Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}
equipped with an operator
D
satisfying certain conditions (see
[
Con94
, Chap. 6]
) by:

d^{D}(\varphi,\psi)=\sup\left\{\left|\varphi(a)-\psi(a)\right|\>:\>a\in A\>,\>\big\|[D,a]\big\|\leqslant 1\right\}.

(2)

The case where
A
is of the form
C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})\otimes\mathfrak{K}({\mathcal{H}})
, where
\mathfrak{K}({\mathcal{H}})
denotes the algebra of compact operators on
{\mathcal{H}}
(possibly finite-dimensional), is of particular relevance for applications to physics, including quantum gravity (see
[
Mar16
]
for a comprehensive survey of the history of the notion as well as recent developments). The so-called
almost commutative
case, where
A=C_{0}^{\infty}\otimes M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
already has meaningful applications, notably to the metric interpretation of the Higgs field
[
CC97
,
Con96
]
in the standard model of elementary particles.

Motivated by these applications to physics, general noncommutative distances have been studied on various discrete and finite spaces. In that context, the operator
D
can be understood as the adjacency matrix corresponding to a graph structure, opening the door to combinatorial arguments. In the finite case, the noncommutative distance between two vertices
i
and
j
of the graph associated with the operator
D
is given by the formula stated in Definition
2.2
:

d^{D}(i,j)=\sup\left\{\left|a_{i}-a_{j}\right|\>:\>A=\operatorname{diag}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\>,\>\big\|[A,D]\big\|\leqslant 1\right\}.

(3)

A systematic approach to noncommutative distances on spaces with
n
points was adopted in
[
IKM01
]
, where general formulas were obtained for
n\leq 3
and proven not to exist for
n=4
. Nevertheless, one may be able to obtain results in some cases by exploiting the properties of the underlying graphs, and study the behavior of the distance under certain modifications of the Dirac operator. For instance, the methods developed in
[
Bes21
]
rely on certain graph manipulations to provide estimates for noncommutative distances and compare them to standard (weighted) geodesic distances. Despite these advances, general results remain elusive; an example of a natural question that remains open at present is that of deciding whether the noncommutative distance between two vertices in a graph increases upon removing an edge.

The purpose of this paper is to bring techniques from modern matrix theory to bear on direct calculations of noncommutative distances on certain graphs. More precisely, we use a characterization of the notion of
Birkhoff-James orthogonality
, due to H. Schneider and the second-named author
[
LS02
]
, to reformulate the problem of calculating (
3
) in the case of path graphs. This method allows us to recover known results for graphs with uniform weights
[
BLS94
,
Atz96
]
as well as explicit formulas obtained for
n\leq 4
in
[
IKM01
]
, and to provide a reduction scheme for the general problem. On the other hand, the complexity of the solution in the case of path graphs provides strong evidence of the intractability of the general problem.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce notation, discuss the problem of noncommutative distance on graphs, make some simplifying observations, and introduce our main tool: Birkhoff-James orthogonality (Theorem
2.5
). In Section
3
, we completely characterize the solution to the noncommutative distance problem for path graphs of length
n
. The main tool is the notion of a
viable vector
, introduced in Definition
3.8
, which leads to a convenient reformulation of the problem (Theorem
3.10
). Finally, in Section
4
, we apply our results to obtain explicit formulas for small values of
n
, while also noting that the number of different formulas one must use for each
n
is related to the number of partitions of
n
into a sum of integers all at least
2
, which gives quantitative insight into the difficulty of solving the noncommutative distance problem in full generality.

2
Reformulation of the Problem and Preliminaries

Let us fix notation and collect various technical facts that will be of use in the remainder of the paper.

2.1
Notation and Terminology

Throughout the paper, we make use of standard notation from matrix theory:
M_{m,n}({\mathbb{F}})
denotes the space of matrices of
m
rows and
n
columns with entries in the field
{\mathbb{F}}
, and
M_{n,n}({\mathbb{F}})
is also denoted by
M_{n}({\mathbb{F}})
or simply
M_{n}
. Vectors will be treated as matrices by means of the canonical isomorphism
M_{n,1}({\mathbb{F}})\simeq{\mathbb{F}}^{n}
and for any vector
x\in{\mathbb{F}}^{n}
, we will denote by
x^{*}
the row vector obtained by transposing and conjugating the column vector associated with
x
under this isomorphism.

We will denote by
E_{i,j}
the matrix (of any size, generally made clear from the context) with
1
as its
(i,j)
entry and
0
elsewhere. The symbol
\oplus
will be used to denote direct sums of vector spaces as well as direct sums of linear maps, generally thought of as diagonal entries in block matrices. Finally, given a matrix
M\in M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
, we denote by
\rho(M)
the spectral radius of
M
.

2.2
Noncommutative Distances on Graphs

Let
G
be a finite simple graph with set of vertices
V=\{1,\ldots,n\}
and set of edges
E
. A spectral triple
({\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{H}},D)
can be associated with
G
as follows. Consider the C*-algebra
{\mathcal{A}}
of diagonal matrices in
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
acting on the Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}={\mathbb{C}}^{V}\simeq{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
. The analogue of a Dirac operator in this context is simply a self-adjoint operator in
\operatorname{End}({\mathcal{H}})\simeq M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
that is compatible with the graph structure,
i.e.
a Hermitian matrix
D=(d_{i,j})_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n}
whose entries satisfy:

i\neq j\>\text{and}\>\{i,j\}\notin E\quad\Rightarrow\quad d_{i,j}=0.

(4)

It is worth noting that the condition expressed in (
4
) encodes the compatibility of the operator
D
with the edge structure of
G
interpreted as a discrete differential structure on
V
(see
[
DMH94
]
).

Remark 2.1
.

The matrix entries
d_{i,j}
of the Dirac operator
D
will generally be assumed to be nonnegative. Condition (
4
) allows for
D
to have nonzero coefficients on the diagonal of
D
, even in the absence of loops in the graph
G
.

Given such a triple, the general procedure of (
2
) allows one to define a
noncommutative distance
between vertices of the graph, as follows.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
G
be a finite simple graph with set of vertices
V=\{1,\ldots,n\}
. The noncommutative distance on
G
associated with a Dirac operator
D
as above is given by:

d^{D}(i,j)=\sup\left\{\left|a_{i}-a_{j}\right|\>:\>A=\operatorname{diag}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\>,\>\big\|[A,D]\big\|\leqslant 1\right\}

(5)

for any
i,j
in
V
.

It will also be convenient to consider the (possibly infinite)
weights
associated with
D
and defined by:

w_{i,j}=|d_{i,j}|^{-1}.

These weights can be used to define a geodesic distance
\ell^{D}
on the graph by considering the infimum of weighted lengths of paths connecting two given vertices. Both the geodesic and the noncommutative distances associated with
D
are generalized distances in the sense that they can assume infinite values. However, it is proved in
[
IKM01
, Proposition 4]
that the noncommutative distance
d^{D}(i,j)
is finite if and only if the vertices
i
and
j
are connected in
G
. Another result of
[
IKM01
]
shows that the noncommutative distance between two vertices is always dominated by the geodesic distance, that is,

d^{D}(i,j)\leqslant\ell^{D}(i,j)

for all
i
,
j
. A survey of general properties can be found in
[
Bes21
]
, along with further estimates and comparisons between
d^{D}
and
\ell^{D}
obtained in relation to structural properties of the graph.

Remark 2.3
.

The natural question of deciding whether the noncommutative distance between two vertices of
G
increases upon removing an edge currently appears to remain open.

2.3
First Reductions

While the general definition of
d^{D}(i,j)
given in (
5
) shows the geometric nature of the question of calculating explicitly the noncommutative distance between vertices in a given graph, the problem can be reformulated in somewhat simpler terms. First, a standard C*-algebraic argument (see
[
IKM01
, Lemma 1]
) allows one to consider only elements
A
that are positive in
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
and an equality condition on the norm of commutators
[D,A]
in (
5
). Therefore, restricting our attention to connected graphs, the distance can be expressed as:

\displaystyle d^{D}(i,j)

\displaystyle=\max\big\{\left|a_{i}-a_{j}\right|\>:\>A=\operatorname{diag}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\>,\>\big\|[A,D]\big\|=1\>,\>a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\geqslant 0\big\}

\displaystyle=\max\big\{a_{i}-a_{j}\>:\>A=\operatorname{diag}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\>,\>\big\|[A,D]\big\|=1\>,\>a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\in{\mathbb{R}}\big\},

where the second formulation holds because both
\|[A,D]\|
and
|a_{i}-a_{j}|
are invariant under replacing
A
by
-A
or by
A+rI
.

Next, let us notice that if
A={\rm diag}\,(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
and
D
satisfies the graph
compatibility condition (
4
), then the commutator
[A,D]
has
(i,j)
-entry

(a_{i}-a_{j})d_{ij}
, and hence is a skew-symmetric matrix. We have the following
canonical form result, e.g., see
[
KL97
, Theorem 5]
for
a simple proof.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
T\in M_{n}
be a real skew-symmetric matrix. There is an orthogonal
matrix
Q
such that
Q^{t}TQ
is a direct sum of

0_{n-2r}
(the zero matrix in
M_{n-2r}({\mathbb{R}})
) and
2\times 2

matrices

\begin{pmatrix}0&s_{j}\cr-s_{j}&0\cr\end{pmatrix},\quad j=1,\dots,r,

where
s_{1}\geqslant\cdots\geqslant s_{r}>0
. Consequently,

T
has nonzero complex eigenvalues
\pm is_{j}
for
j=1,\dots,r
and

s_{1}=\|T\|=\max\{x^{t}Ty:x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n},x^{t}x=y^{t}y=1,x^{t}y=0\}.

By the above proposition, it is clear that

A={\rm diag}\,(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
satisfies
\|AD-DA\|\leqslant 1

if and only if

{\rm tr}[A(Dxy^{t}-xy^{t}D)]=y^{t}(AD-DA)x\leqslant 1

for any orthonormal
pair of vectors
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. It follows that:

d^{D}(i,j)=\max\big\{(a_{i}-a_{j}):A={\rm diag}\,(a_{1},\dots,a_{n}),{\rm tr}(AR)\leqslant 1\hbox{
for all }R\in{\cal S}(D)\big\}

(6)

where

{\cal S}(D)=\{\{Dxy^{t}-xy^{t}D:x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n},\|x\|=\|y\|=1,x^{t}y=0\}.

2.4
Birkhoff-James Orthogonality

Orthogonality is a useful tool in the study of Euclidean spaces and the geometric objects modeled after them. However, not all metric spaces of interest come naturally equipped with such a notion. In order to extend techniques that involve perpendicularity (
e.g.
the optimality of orthogonal projections) one may use characterizations expressed in terms of the metric only. One such characterization, introduced by Birkhoff
[
Bir35
]
and studied by James
[
Jam45
,
Jam47
]
, takes as its starting point the elementary observation that two lines in Euclidean space are perpendicular if and only if no point on the second is closer to a marked point on the first than their intersection.

u
v
v+tu
\mathcal{L}_{1}
\mathcal{L}_{2}

Figure 1:
\mathcal{L}_{1}\perp\mathcal{L}_{2}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
Euclidean if and only if
\|v\|\leq\|v+tu\|
for all
t
.

If one denotes the marked point by the origin, the intersection of the lines by
v
, and a direction vector of the second line by
u
(see Figure
1
), then this orthogonality condition can be reformulated as:

\|v\|\leq\|v+tu\|\quad\text{for any $t\in{\mathbb{R}}$}.

(7)

An overview of Birkhoff-James orthogonality and its applications in various settings can be found in the survey
[
GS21
]
. Its relevance to the study of the problem at hand comes down to the way the definition naturally encapsulates the notion of best approximation to a subspace.

If we focus on a matrix space over
{\mathbb{F}}={\mathbb{R}}
or
{\mathbb{C}}
, we say that
a matrix
A\in M_{m,n}({\mathbb{F}})
is Birkhoff-James orthogonal to a matrix
B\in M_{m,n}({\mathbb{F}})

if

\|A\|\leq\|A+\gamma B\|\quad\text{for any $\gamma\in{\mathbb{F}}$}.

(8)

The relation thus obtained (which, we should note, is generally not symmetric) has been studied by Singer
[
Sin70
]
, as well as by the second-named author and H. Schneider
[
LS02
]
. The results established in
[
LS02
, Theorem 3.1]
characterize Birkhoff-James orthogonality and provide a useful tool for this paper, which can be restated as follows:

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
A,B\in M_{m,n}({\mathbb{F}})
. Then
A
is Birkhoff-James orthogonal to
B
if and only if there exist unit vectors
x\in{\mathbb{F}}^{m}
and
y\in{\mathbb{F}}^{n}
such that
\|A\|=x^{*}Ay
and
x^{*}By=0
.

Finally, we note that Birkhoff-James orthogonality is actually equivalent to a local optimality property, in the sense that (
8
) is equivalent to:

\text{There exists $\epsilon>0$ such that:}\quad\|A\|\leq\|A+\gamma B\|\quad\text{for any $\gamma\in{\mathbb{F}}$ with }|\gamma|<\epsilon.

(9)

To see this, suppose
\|A\|\leq\|A+\gamma B\|
. Then

2\|A\|\leq 2\|A+\gamma B\|=\|A+(A+2\gamma B)\|\leq\|A\|+\|A+2\gamma B\|,

whence
\|A\|\leq\|A+2\gamma B\|
.

Thus, in a manner analogous to maximizing a function by finding its critical points, one can use Birkhoff-James orthogonality to find the locations where
d^{D}(i,j)
might be achieved. With the help of Theorem
2.5
to provide a tractable characterization, we shall see this idea employed as the key tool in the proof of Proposition
3.6
.

3
Path Graphs

Assume
D=(d_{ij})\in M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
with
d_{i,i+1}=d_{i+1,i}>0

for
i=1,\dots,n-1
, and
d_{i,j}=0
otherwise. It is a general fact (see
[
Bes21
, Theorem 3]
) that the noncommutative distance between any two vertices
i
and
j
only depends on the Dirac operator induced by
D
on the subgraph obtained by taking the union of all paths connecting
i
to
j
. As a result, to determine

d^{D}(i,j)=\max\{a_{1}-a_{n}:A={\rm diag}\,(a_{1},\dots,a_{n}),\|AD-DA\|=1,a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in{\mathbb{R}}\},

we may focus on the case
(i,j)=(1,n)
, since the union of all paths joining
i
and
j
is simply the unique induced path joining
i
and
j
.

3.1
Further Reformulation of the Problem for Paths and Viable Vectors

We begin by providing some equivalent characterizations of
d^{D}(1,n)
that will prove more fruitful to work with. To simplify notation, we shall write
d_{i}
for
d_{i,i+1}
for the rest of this section; that is,

D=\begin{bmatrix}0&d_{1}&0&\dots&0\\
d_{1}&0&d_{2}&&\vdots\\
0&d_{2}&\ddots&\ddots&0\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\ddots&d_{n-1}\\
0&\dots&0&d_{n-1}&0\end{bmatrix}.

We will make frequent use of the following matrices related to
D
throughout the remainder of this paper.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
d=(d_{1},\dots,d_{n-1})
be fixed, and let
z=(z_{1},\dots,z_{n-1})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n-1}
. Define
L(d,z)
(or simply
L(z)
, when
d
is understood) to be the symmetric tridiagonal matrix with zeros on the diagonal and
d_{1}z_{1},\dots,d_{n-1}z_{n-1}
on the superdiagonal, and let
T(d,z)
(or simply
T(z)
, when
d
is understood) be the
\lfloor\frac{n+1}{2}\rfloor\times\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor
matrix

T(z)=\begin{bmatrix}d_{1}z_{1}&&\\
d_{2}z_{2}&d_{3}z_{3}&\\
&d_{4}z_{4}&d_{5}z_{5}&\\
&&&\ddots\end{bmatrix}.

(10)

Remark 3.2
.

We remark that
d_{i}z_{i}
will be in row
\left\lceil\frac{i+1}{2}\right\rceil=\left\lfloor\frac{i+2}{2}\right\rfloor
and column
\left\lceil\frac{i}{2}\right\rceil=\left\lfloor\frac{i+1}{2}\right\rfloor
of
T(z)
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Suppose
D\in M_{n}({\mathbb{R}})
is a symmetric tridiagonal matrix with zeros on the diagonal and
d_{1},\dots,d_{n-1}>0
on the superdiagonal. Then,

\displaystyle d^{D}(1,n)=

\displaystyle\max\{a_{1}-a_{n}:A={\rm diag}\,(a_{1},\dots,a_{n}),\|AD-DA\|=1,a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in{\mathbb{R}}\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max\{z_{1}+\dots+z_{n-1}:z_{j}\geq 0,\;\rho(L(z))=1\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max\{z_{1}+\dots+z_{n-1}:z_{j}\geq 0,\;\|T(z)\|=1\}.

Proof.

By compactness, all maxima exist. Writing
z_{j}=a_{j}-a_{j+1}
for
j=1,\dots,n-1
, we have
AD-DA=\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}z_{j}d_{j}(E_{j,j+1}-E_{j+1,j})
.
Let
Q={\rm diag}\,(i,i^{2},\dots,i^{n})
. Then,

iQ^{*}(AD-DA)Q=\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}z_{j}d_{j}(E_{j,j+1}+E_{j+1,j})=L(z).

Since
\|iQ^{*}(AD-DA)Q\|=\|AD-DA\|
, the first maximum equals

\max\{z_{1}+\dots+z_{n-1}:z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n-1},\;\|L(z)\|\leq 1\}.

Furthermore, there exists a matrix
G={\rm diag}\,(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})
, with
g_{1},\dots,g_{n}\in\{1,-1\}
, such that

GL(z)G=\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}g_{j}g_{j+1}z_{j}d_{j}(E_{j,j+1}+E_{j+1,j})=\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}|z_{j}|d_{j}(E_{j,j+1}+E_{j+1,j}).

Since
G
is orthogonal,
\|GL(z)G\|=\|L(z)\|
, so we may assume
z_{j}\geq 0
for all
j
. Since
L(z)
is a real symmetric matrix, its norm equals its spectral radius
\rho(L(z))
, so the first two maxima coincide.

Note that
T(z)
is the submatrix of
L(z)
with
1,3,5,\dots
as row indices and
2,4,6,\dots
as column indices, so
L(z)
is permutationally similar to

\begin{bmatrix}0_{k}&T(z)\\
T(z)^{t}&0_{n-k}\end{bmatrix}

where
k={\lfloor\frac{n+1}{2}\rfloor}
. Thus the eigenvalues of
L(z)
occur in plus/minus pairs, and the nonnegative eigenvalues of
L(z)
are precisely the singular values of
T(z)
, whence the second and third maxima coincide.

\Box

There are now two situations to consider, depending on whether or not
L(z)
and
T(z)
in Lemma
3.3
are both reducible or irreducible. Observe that
L(z)
is irreducible precisely when
z_{1},\dots,z_{n-1}
are all nonzero. We consider this irreducible case first; to concisely state the result, we need to introduce some notation.

Definition 3.4
.

Given a vector of positive numbers
d=(d_{1},\dots,d_{n-1})
, set
m=\lfloor\frac{n+1}{2}\rfloor
. Define vectors
\mu(d)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
and
\nu(d)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n-m}
(or, simply
\mu
and
\nu
when
d
is understood) by defining their entries to be

\mu_{1}=\nu_{1}=1,\qquad\mu_{k+1}:=\prod_{j=1}^{k}\frac{d_{2j-1}}{d_{2j}},\qquad\nu_{k+1}:=\prod_{j=1}^{k}\frac{d_{2j}}{d_{2j+1}}.

(11)

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
\mu
and
\nu
be as in Definition
3.4
. Then,

\mu^{t}T(z)\nu=d_{1}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}z_{i}.

Proof.

First, we remark that

\mu^{t}T(z)=(d_{1}(z_{1}+z_{2}),\frac{d_{1}d_{3}}{d_{2}}(z_{3}+z_{4}),\dots)=(\frac{d_{1}}{\nu_{1}}(z_{1}+z_{2}),\frac{d_{1}}{\nu_{2}}(z_{3}+z_{4}),\dots),

where the last term of
\mu^{t}T(z)
is either
\frac{d_{1}}{\nu_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}}(z_{n-2}+z_{n-1})
or
\frac{d_{1}}{\nu_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}}z_{n-1}
, depending on whether
n
is odd or even. Thus,

\mu^{t}T(z)\nu=d_{1}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}z_{i},

as desired.

\Box

Proposition 3.6
.

Suppose that the maxima in Lemma
3.3
are attained at
z=(z_{1},\dots,z_{n-1})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n-1}
with positive entries. Then the maximum value of
\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}z_{j}
is given by

\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}z_{j}=\frac{\|\mu\|\|\nu\|}{d_{1}}=\frac{1}{d_{1}}\sqrt{1+\mu_{2}^{2}+\dots+\mu_{\lfloor\frac{n+1}{2}\rfloor}^{2}}\sqrt{1+\nu_{2}^{2}+\dots+\nu_{\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor}^{2}}.

(12)

Proof.

Using the notation in Lemma
3.3
, since
z_{j}>0
for all
j
,
L(z)
is an irreducible nonnegative matrix, so by the Perron-Frobenius theorem there exists a unique unit vector
v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
with positive entries such that
L(z)v=\rho(L(z))v=v
; moreover, 1 is a simple eigenvalue. Since the nonnegative eigenvalues of
L(z)
are the singular values of
T(z)
,
T(z)
has a unique norm-attaining vector (modulo scalar multiplication).

Let
m={\lfloor\frac{n+1}{2}\rfloor}
.
Because

\begin{bmatrix}0_{m}&T(z)\\
T(z)^{t}&0_{n-m}\end{bmatrix}

is permutationally similar to
L(z)
, there exist positive vectors
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
and
y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n-m}
such that

\begin{bmatrix}0_{m}&T(z)\\
T(z)^{t}&0_{n-m}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x\\
y\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}x\\
y\end{bmatrix},

so
T(z)y=x
and
T(z)^{t}x=y
. Then
\|x\|^{2}=\|T(z)y\|^{2}=y^{t}T(z)^{t}T(z)y=y^{t}y=\|y\|^{2}
, so without loss of generality we may assume that both
x
and
y
are unit vectors. Then
x,y
are the unique positive unit vectors satisfying
x^{t}T(z)y=1
.

Note
T(z)=\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}z_{j}R_{j}
where
R_{2k}=d_{2k}E_{k+1,k}
and
R_{2k-1}=d_{2k-1}E_{k,k}
. We claim that
\|T(z)+t(R_{j}-R_{j+1})\|\geqslant\|T(z)\|
for all
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
satisfying
|t|<\min\{z_{j},z_{j+1}\}
. By way of contradiction, suppose there exists
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
with
|t|<\min\{z_{j},z_{j+1}\}
such that
\|T(z)+t(R_{j}-R_{j+1})\|=d<1
.
If

(\tilde{z}_{1},\dots,\tilde{z}_{n})=(z_{1},\dots,z_{j-1},z_{j}+t,z_{j+1}-t,z_{j+2},\dots,z_{n})/d,

then

\|T(\tilde{z})\|=\|T(z)+t(R_{j}-R_{j+1})\|/d=1

and

\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\tilde{z}_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}z_{j}/d>\sum_{j-1}^{n-1}z_{j},

which contradicts that
z
gives a maximum, proving the claim.

By (
9
),
T(z)
is Birkhoff-James orthogonal to
R_{j}-R_{j+1}
for each

. Then by Theorem
2.5
there exist unit vectors

such that
u^{t}T(z)v=1
and
u^{t}(R_{j}-R_{j+1})v=0
. By the aforementioned uniqueness we may take

and

. Thus
x^{t}R_{j}y=x^{t}R_{j+1}y
for

, which implies

x_{k+1}=\frac{d_{2k-1}}{d_{2k}}x_{k}\quad\text{ and }\quad y_{k+1}=\frac{d_{2k}}{d_{2k+1}}y_{k}

(13)

for

As in Definition

, let
\mu
and
\nu
be vectors whose entries
\mu_{k}
and
\nu_{k}

are

\mu_{1}=\nu_{1}=1,\qquad\mu_{k+1}:=\prod_{j=1}^{k}\frac{d_{2j-1}}{d_{2j}},\qquad\nu_{k+1}:=\prod_{j=1}^{k}\frac{d_{2j}}{d_{2j+1}}.

(14)

By the recurrence relations (

are positive unit vectors,

,

.

Finally, using Lemma

so

\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}z_{j}=\frac{1}{d_{1}}\sqrt{1+\mu_{2}^{2}+\dots+\mu_{\lfloor\frac{n+1}{2}\rfloor}^{2}}\sqrt{1+\nu_{2}^{2}+\dots+\nu_{\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor}^{2}}.

(15)

In general the maxima in Lemma
3.3
may occur at a nonnegative vector
z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n-1}
with some zero entries. In such a case
T(z)
may be reducible, and the decomposition occurs at the locations where
z_{j}=0
. We introduce further notation.

Definition 3.7
.

Let
z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n-1}
be a nonnegative vector. Recursively define integers
\alpha_{k}
,
\beta_{k}
for
k\geq 1
as follows:

•

\beta_{0}=0
,

•

\alpha_{k}
is the least integer
i>\beta_{k-1}
such that
z_{i}\neq 0
, and

•

\beta_{k}
is the greatest integer
i
such that
z_{\alpha_{k}},\dots,z_{i}
are all nonzero. (Note that
\beta_{k}=\alpha_{k}
is possible.)

Further define

f_{k}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}(d_{\alpha_{k}},\dots,d_{\beta_{k}}),\quad\quad u_{k}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}(z_{\alpha_{k}},\dots,z_{\beta_{k}}),\quad\text{and}\quad T_{k}(z)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}T(f_{k},u_{k}).

In general we do not have
T(z)=T_{1}(z)\oplus\dots\oplus T_{r}(z)
, but we do have
T(z)=\Theta_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus\Theta_{s}
, where each
\Theta_{j}
is either a zero matrix of some dimension, or is some
T_{i}(z)
or
T_{i}(z)^{t}
, possibly padded with some zero rows or columns. We illustrate with an example. Suppose

T(z)=\begin{bmatrix}0&&&&&\\
b&c&&&\\
&d&e&&&\\
&&0&0&&&\\
&&&h&i&&\\
&&&&j&k&\\
&&&&&l&0\end{bmatrix}.

Then,

T_{1}(z)=\begin{bmatrix}b&\\
c&d\\
&e\end{bmatrix}\qquad\text{ and }T_{2}(z)=\begin{bmatrix}h&\\
i&j\\
&k&l\end{bmatrix},

and one could choose
\Theta_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
T_{1}^{t}(z)\\
0\end{bmatrix}
and
\Theta_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}T_{2}^{t}(z)&0\end{bmatrix}
(other possibilities exist). Despite the non-uniqueness of this decomposition, it is however the case that

\|T(z)\|=\|\Theta_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus\Theta_{s}\|=\max_{k}\|T_{k}(z)\|.

Then, Lemma
3.3
gives

\displaystyle d^{D}(1,n)

\displaystyle=\max\{z_{1}+\dots+z_{n-1}:z_{j}\geq 0,\|T_{k}(z)\|\leq 1\}

\displaystyle=\max\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{r}\sum_{j=\alpha_{k}}^{\beta_{k}}z_{j}:z_{j}\geq 0,\|T_{k}(z)\|\leq 1\right\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{r}\max\left\{\sum_{j=\alpha_{k}}^{\beta_{k}}z_{j}:z_{j}\geq 0,\|T_{k}(z)\|\leq 1\right\}.

Thus the problem reduces to applying Proposition
3.6
to
T_{1}(z),\dots,T_{r}(z)
, and the maximum value of
\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}z_{j}
is found by summing the corresponding maxima for
T_{1}(z),\dots,T_{r}(z)
.

Moreover, it turns out that, to achieve the maximum, there cannot be any extra zeros in the
\Theta_{j}
summands of
T(z)
. To summarize all of these results, we first introduce the following definition.

Definition 3.8
.

We say that a nonnegative vector
z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n-1}
is
viable
if the following hold.

(1)

There are no consecutive zeros in
z
, and
z_{1}\neq 0
,
z_{n-1}\neq 0
. (This means that
T(z)
actually equals the direct sum of
T_{1}(z),\dots,T_{r}(z)
, or possibly some of their transposes.)

(2)

For each
k=1,\dots,r
,

T_{k}(z)y_{k}=x_{k}\qquad\text{and}\qquad T_{k}(z)^{t}x_{k}=y_{k},

where
x_{k}=\dfrac{\mu(f_{k})}{\|\mu(f_{k})\|}
and
y_{k}=\dfrac{\nu(f_{k})}{\|\nu(f_{k})\|}
.

Remark 3.9
.

(1)

Note that it is possible that there is no positive vector
z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n-1}
that is viable, since it is possible that no positive vector satisfies the second condition of Definition
3.8
. However, it is not difficult to see that a viable vector always exists: for example, if
n
is even, then

z=\left(\frac{1}{d_{1}},0,\frac{1}{d_{3}},0,\frac{1}{d_{5}},\dots,0,\frac{1}{d_{n-1}}\right)

is viable, and, if
n
is odd, then

z=\left(\frac{d_{2}}{d_{1}\sqrt{d_{1}^{2}+d_{2}^{2}}},\frac{d_{1}}{d_{2}\sqrt{d_{1}^{2}+d_{2}^{2}}},0,\frac{1}{d_{4}},0,\frac{1}{d_{6}},0,\dots,0,\frac{1}{d_{n-1}}\right)

is viable.

(2)

If
z
is viable and
T(z)=\Theta_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\Theta_{r}
, then
\Theta_{k}=T_{k}(z)
if
\alpha_{k}
is odd (since there is a unique nonzero element in the row of
T(z)
containing
d_{\alpha_{k}}z_{\alpha_{k}}
), whereas, if
\alpha_{k}
is even, then
\Theta_{k}=T_{k}(z)^{t}
(since either the row of
T(z)
containing
d_{\alpha_{k}}z_{\alpha_{k}}
contains two nonzero entries or
T_{k}(z)\in M_{1}({\mathbb{R}})
).

Theorem 3.10
.

Let
D\in M_{n}({\mathbb{R}})
be a symmetric tridiagonal matrix with zeros on the diagonal and
d_{1},\dots,d_{n-1}>0
on the superdiagonal.

Then,

d^{D}(1,n)=\max\left\{\sum_{k}\frac{\|\mu(f_{k})\|\|\nu(f_{k})\|}{d_{\alpha_{k}}}:z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n-1}\emph{isviable}\right\}.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.3
, Proposition
3.6
, and the preceding discussion,
d^{D}(1,n)
is achieved at some decomposition with the asserted formula. There remain two things to check: (i) first, we must show that none of the summands
in a direct sum decomposition of
T(z)
has a zero row or column (so that
z
is indeed viable),
and (ii) second, we must ensure that all viable
z
are legitimate candidates, that is, we must ensure that the decomposition of
T(z)
for a viable
z
satisfies the Birkhoff-James orthogonality conditions and the constraints
\|T_{k}(z)\|=1
.

To prove assertion (i), it will be easier to work with
L(z)
as defined in Lemma
3.3
. Because
L(z)
is permutationally similar to

\begin{bmatrix}0&T(z)\\
T(z)^{t}&0\end{bmatrix},

the direct sum decomposition of
L(z)
will have a
1\times 1
zero block if and only if some
\Theta_{j}
in the decomposition
T(z)=\Theta_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus\Theta_{s}
has a zero row or column (i.e.
z
is not viable).

We shall show that if the decomposition of
L(z)
has a
1\times 1
zero block, then we can always combine it with another block to increase the objective function
z_{1}+\dots+z_{n-1}
, so
d^{D}(1,n)
cannot be attained at such a non-viable
z
.

Without loss of generality, up to a permutation similarity, we may assume all zero summands of
L(z)
occur last. To this end, suppose
L_{k+1}(z)
is the first zero summand and
L_{k}(z)
is an irreducible (nonzero) summand. To simplify notation, without loss of generality we may suppose
k=1
and
L_{1}(z)
is an
m\times m
matrix. Then
z_{1},\dots,z_{m-1}>0,z_{m}=0
.

Suppose, to the contrary, that
d^{D}(1,n)
is attained at this decomposition. By Lemma
3.3
,

z_{1}+\dots+z_{m}=\max\{w_{1}+\dots+w_{m}:w_{j}\geq 0,\,\rho(M(w))=1\},

where
M(w)\in M_{m+1}
is the symmetric tridiagonal matrix

M(w)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}d_{j}w_{j}(E_{j,j+1}+E_{j+1,j})
.
Define
z_{m}^{\prime}=2(1-t)(z_{1}+\dots+z_{m-1})
and
z_{j}^{\prime}=tz_{j}
for
j=1,\dots,m-1
, where we choose
t\in(0,1)

to satisfy

4(1-t)d_{m}^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}z_{j}^{2}<1.

(16)

Then
\sum_{j=1}^{m}z_{j}^{\prime}=(2-t)\sum_{j=1}^{m}z_{j}>\sum_{j=1}^{m}z_{j}
, so by our assumption of maximality,
\rho(M(z^{\prime}))>1
. We shall show that in fact
\rho(M(z^{\prime}))\leq 1
, giving the desired contradiction.

Because
M(z^{\prime})
is a real symmetric matrix whose eigenvalues occur in plus/minus pairs, the condition
\rho(M(z^{\prime}))\leq 1
is equivalent to
I+M(z^{\prime})
being positive semidefinite.
Writing
B=\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}z_{j}d_{j}(E_{j,j+1}+E_{j+1,j})
and
v=(0,\dots,0,z_{m}^{\prime}d_{m})^{t}
, we have

I+M(z^{\prime})=\begin{bmatrix}I_{m}+tB&v\cr v^{t}&1\cr\end{bmatrix}.

By our assumption of maximality,
\rho(B)=1
, so
I_{m}+tB
is positive definite, and hence invertible.
Let
S=\begin{bmatrix}I_{m}&(I_{m}+tB)^{-1}v\\
0&-1\end{bmatrix}
, so
S
is an invertible matrix satisfying

S^{t}(I+M(z^{\prime}))S=(I_{m}+tB)\oplus[1-(z^{\prime}_{m})^{2}d_{m}^{2}\xi],

(17)

where
\xi
is the
(m,m)
entry of
(I_{m}+tB)^{-1}
. By writing
B=W^{t}{\rm diag}\,(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{m})W
for an orthogonal matrix
W=(w_{ij})\in M_{m}
, we have

\xi=(W^{t}(I+t{\rm diag}\,(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{m})^{-1}W)_{m,m}=\sum_{j=1}\frac{w_{j,m}^{2}}{1+t\mu_{j}};

note that
|\mu_{j}|\leq 1
because
\rho(B)=1
.

Now
\rho(M(z^{\prime}))\leq 1
is equivalent to
I+M(z^{\prime})
being positive semidefinite; by the congruence (
17
), this is equivalent to
(z^{\prime}_{m})^{2}d_{m}^{2}\xi\leq 1
. Indeed,

\displaystyle(z^{\prime}_{m})^{2}d_{m}^{2}\xi

\displaystyle=4(1-t)^{2}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}z_{j}^{2}\right)d_{m}^{2}\sum_{j=1}\frac{w_{j,m}^{2}}{1+t\mu_{j}}<(1-t)\sum_{j=1}\frac{w_{j,m}^{2}}{1+t\mu_{j}}\qquad\text{ by }\eqref{E:t}

\displaystyle\leq(1-t)\sum_{j=1}\frac{w_{j,m}^{2}}{1-t}=1

because
W
is orthogonal, and we obtain the desired contradiction, proving (i).

To prove assertion (ii), recall that, by Definition
3.8
,
T_{k}(z)y_{k}=x_{k}
and
T_{k}(z)^{t}x_{k}=y_{k}
for a viable
z
. Thus

\begin{bmatrix}0&T_{k}(z)\\
T_{k}(z)^{t}&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x_{k}\\
y_{k}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}x_{k}\\
y_{k}\end{bmatrix};

by the Perron-Frobenius theorem, the spectral radius of the block matrix is 1 (note that the block matrix is nonnegative and irreducible because
T_{k}(z)
is). Since the eigenvalues of the block matrix are plus/minus pairs of the singular values of
T_{k}(z)
, we have
\|T_{k}(z)\|=1
. The Birkhoff-James orthogonality conditions in the proof of Proposition
3.6
then follow from the way
x_{k}
and
y_{k}
are defined.

\Box

3.2
Determination of Viable Vectors

In general, to compute
d^{D}(1,n)
one must find and check all viable
z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n-1}
. Toward this end, we present some results that narrow the search considerably.

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
1<p<n-1
. If

f_{1}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}(d_{1},\dots,d_{p-1}),\;f_{2}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}(d_{p+1},\dots,d_{n-1}),

\mathfrak{a}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\frac{\|\mu\|\|\nu\|}{d_{1}},\;\mathfrak{b}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\frac{\|\mu(f_{1})\|\|\nu(f_{1})\|}{d_{1}},\;\mathfrak{c}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\frac{\|\mu(f_{2})\|\|\nu(f_{2})\|}{d_{p+1}},

then

\mathfrak{a}\geqslant\mathfrak{b}+\mathfrak{c}.

Proof.

Let
\mu_{r}
be the first
\mu_{i}
involving
d_{p+1}
and let
\nu_{s}
be the first
\nu_{i}
involving
d_{p+1}
. Thus
\mu_{r},\nu_{s}
are precisely the products of terms up to, but not including,

d_{p-1}/d_{p}
and
d_{p}/d_{p+1}
. Note
r,s
may equal 1.
Thus,

\mathfrak{b}=\frac{1}{d_{1}}\sqrt{1+\mu_{1}^{2}+\mu_{2}^{2}+\cdots+\mu_{r-1}^{2}}\sqrt{1+\nu_{1}^{2}+\nu_{2}^{2}+\cdots+\nu_{s-1}^{2}},

while

\displaystyle\mathfrak{c}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d_{p+1}}\sqrt{1+(\mu_{r+1}/\mu_{r})^{2}+\dots+(\mu_{\lfloor(n+1)/2\rfloor}/\mu_{r})^{2}}\sqrt{1+(\nu_{s+1}/\nu_{s})^{2}+\dots+(\nu_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}/\nu_{s})^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d_{p+1}\mu_{r}\nu_{s}}\sqrt{\mu_{r}^{2}+\dots+\mu_{\lfloor(n+1)/2\rfloor}^{2}}\sqrt{\nu_{s}^{2}+\dots+\nu_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d_{1}}\sqrt{\mu_{r}^{2}+\dots+\mu_{\lfloor(n+1)/2\rfloor}^{2}}\sqrt{\nu_{s}^{2}+\dots+\nu_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}^{2}}.

Let

u_{1}=1+\mu_{1}^{2}+\cdots+\mu_{r-1}^{2},\qquad u_{2}=\mu_{r}^{2}+\cdots+\mu_{\lfloor(n+1)/2\rfloor}^{2},

v_{1}=1+\nu_{1}^{2}+\cdots+\nu_{s-1}^{2},\qquad v_{2}=\nu_{s}^{2}+\cdots+\nu_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}^{2}.

Then

\mathfrak{a}^{2}-(\mathfrak{b}+\mathfrak{c})^{2}=\frac{1}{d_{12}^{2}}\left(\sqrt{u_{1}v_{2}}-\sqrt{v_{1}u_{2}}\right)^{2}\geqslant 0,

as desired.

\Box

We can use Lemma
3.11
to prove the following, which shows that, once a viable
z
is found, one need not check refinements of the decomposition.

Proposition 3.12
.

Let
D\in M_{n}({\mathbb{R}})
be a symmetric tridiagonal matrix with zeros on the diagonal and
d_{1},\dots,d_{n-1}>0
on the superdiagonal. If
z
and
z^{\prime}
are both viable and
z_{i}^{\prime}=0
whenever

, then

\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}z_{i}\geqslant\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}z_{i}^{\prime}.

Proof.

By induction, it suffices to consider the result when all
z_{i},z_{i}^{\prime}
are positive except
z_{p}^{\prime}=0
for some unique
1\leqslant p\leqslant n-1
. Note that if
p=1
or
p=n-1
, then
z^{\prime}
is not viable, a contradiction.

Thus, we may assume
1<p<n-1
. By Proposition
3.6
,

\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}z_{i}=\frac{\|\mu\|\|\nu\|}{d_{1}},

and if

f_{1}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}(d_{1},\dots,d_{p-1}),\;f_{2}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}(d_{p+1},\dots,d_{n-1}),

then, by Theorem

\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}z_{i}^{\prime}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{p-1}z_{i}^{\prime}\right)+\left(\sum_{i=p+1}^{n-1}z_{i}^{\prime}\right)=\frac{\|\mu(f_{1})\|\|\nu(f_{1})\|}{d_{1}}+\frac{\|\mu(f_{2})\|\|\nu(f_{2})\|}{d_{p+1}},

and the result now follows by Lemma

.

\Box

.

It is possible for

Let

Before proving Proposition

T_{k}(z)T_{k}(z)^{t}\mu(f_{k})=\mu(f_{k})
,
T_{k}(z)^{t}T_{k}(z)\nu(f_{k})=\nu(f_{k})
.

Now, by definition, we have

\mu(f_{1})=\left(1,\frac{d_{1}}{d_{2}}\right)^{t},\quad\mu(f_{2})=\left(1,\frac{d_{4}}{d_{5}}\right)^{t},\quad\mu(f_{3})=\left(1,\frac{d_{7}}{d_{8}}\right)^{t},

\nu(f_{1})=(1),\qquad\nu(f_{2})=(1),\qquad\nu(f_{3})=\left(1,\frac{d_{8}}{d_{9}}\right)^{t}.

Recall from Definition
3.14
that

\mu[\alpha_{k},\beta_{k}]\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\left(\mu_{\left\lceil\frac{\alpha_{k}+1}{2}\right\rceil},\dots,\mu_{\left\lceil\frac{\beta_{k}+1}{2}\right\rceil}\right)^{t},

\nu[\alpha_{k},\beta_{k}]\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\left(\nu_{\left\lceil\frac{\alpha_{k}}{2}\right\rceil},\dots,\nu_{\left\lceil\frac{\beta_{k}}{2}\right\rceil}\right)^{t}.

Noting that

\mu=(\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\mu_{3},\mu_{4},\mu_{5})^{t}=\left(1,\frac{d_{1}}{d_{2}},\frac{d_{1}d_{3}}{d_{2}d_{4}},\frac{d_{1}d_{3}d_{5}}{d_{2}d_{4}d_{6}},\frac{d_{1}d_{3}d_{5}d_{7}}{d_{2}d_{4}d_{6}d_{8}}\right)^{t},

\nu=(\nu_{1},\nu_{2},\nu_{3},\nu_{4},\nu_{5})^{t}=\left(1,\frac{d_{2}}{d_{3}},\frac{d_{2}d_{4}}{d_{3}d_{5}},\frac{d_{2}d_{4}d_{6}}{d_{3}d_{5}d_{7}},\frac{d_{2}d_{4}d_{6}d_{8}}{d_{3}d_{5}d_{7}d_{9}}\right)^{t},

we have

\mu[\alpha_{1},\beta_{1}]=(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})^{t}=\mu_{1}\cdot\mu(f_{1}),

\mu[\alpha_{2},\beta_{2}]=(\mu_{3})=\mu_{3}\cdot\nu(f_{2}),

\mu[\alpha_{3},\beta_{3}]=(\mu_{4},\mu_{5})^{t}=\mu_{4}\cdot\mu(f_{3}),

and further

\nu[\alpha_{1},\beta_{1}]=(\nu_{1})=\nu_{1}\cdot\nu(f_{1}),

\nu[\alpha_{2},\beta_{2}]=(\nu_{2},\nu_{3})^{t}=\nu_{2}\cdot\mu(f_{2}),

\nu[\alpha_{3},\beta_{3}]=(\nu_{4},\nu_{5})^{t}=\nu_{4}\cdot\nu(f_{3}).

This shows that, in each case,

\Theta_{k}\Theta_{k}^{t}\mu[\alpha_{k},\beta_{k}]=\mu[\alpha_{k},\beta_{k}],

\Theta_{k}^{t}\Theta_{k}\nu[\alpha_{k},\beta_{k}]=\nu[\alpha_{k},\beta_{k}],

and hence

T(z)T(z)^{t}\mu=\begin{bmatrix}\Theta_{1}\Theta_{1}^{t}\mu[\alpha_{1},\beta_{1}]\\
\Theta_{2}\Theta_{2}^{t}\mu[\alpha_{2},\beta_{2}]\\
\Theta_{3}\Theta_{3}^{t}\mu[\alpha_{3},\beta_{3}]\\
\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\mu[\alpha_{1},\beta_{1}]\\
\mu[\alpha_{2},\beta_{2}]\\
\mu[\alpha_{3},\beta_{3}]\\
\end{bmatrix}=\mu,

T(z)^{t}T(z)\nu=\begin{bmatrix}\Theta_{1}^{t}\Theta_{1}\nu[\alpha_{1},\beta_{1}]\\
\Theta_{2}^{t}\Theta_{2}\nu[\alpha_{2},\beta_{2}]\\
\Theta_{3}^{t}\Theta_{3}\nu[\alpha_{3},\beta_{3}]\\
\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\nu[\alpha_{1},\beta_{1}]\\
\nu[\alpha_{2},\beta_{2}]\\
\nu[\alpha_{3},\beta_{3}]\\
\end{bmatrix}=\nu.

Proof of Proposition
3.15
.

Let
z
be viable with corresponding decomposition
T(z)=\Theta_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\Theta_{r}
. By Remark
3.9
(2),
\Theta_{k}=T_{k}(z)
if
\alpha_{k}
is odd and
\Theta_{k}=T_{k}(z)^{t}
if
\alpha_{k}
is even.

Recalling Definition
3.14
, we have

\mu[\alpha_{k},\beta_{k}]=\left(\mu_{\left\lceil\frac{\alpha_{k}+1}{2}\right\rceil},\dots,\mu_{\left\lceil\frac{\beta_{k}+1}{2}\right\rceil}\right)^{t},

\nu[\alpha_{k},\beta_{k}]=\left(\nu_{\left\lceil\frac{\alpha_{k}}{2}\right\rceil},\dots,\nu_{\left\lceil\frac{\beta_{k}}{2}\right\rceil}\right)^{t}.

Note that
\mu[\alpha_{k},\beta_{k}]
and
\nu[\alpha_{k},\beta_{k}]
partition
\mu
and
\nu
respectively to conform with the decomposition of
T(z)=\Theta_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\Theta_{r}
so that

T(z)^{t}\mu=\begin{bmatrix}\Theta_{1}^{t}\mu[\alpha_{1},\beta_{1}]\\
\vdots\\
\Theta_{r}^{t}\mu[\alpha_{r},\beta_{r}]\end{bmatrix}\ \text{ and }\;\ T(z)\nu=\begin{bmatrix}\Theta_{1}\nu[\alpha_{1},\beta_{1}]\\
\vdots\\
\Theta_{r}\nu[\alpha_{r},\beta_{r}]\end{bmatrix}.

It hence suffices to prove for each
k
that
\mu[\alpha_{k},\beta_{k}]
is in the
1
-eigenspace of
\Theta_{k}\Theta_{k}^{t}
and
\nu[\alpha_{k},\beta_{k}]
is in the
1
-eigenspace of
\Theta_{k}^{t}\Theta_{k}.

By Definition
3.8
, we have
T_{k}(z)y_{k}=x_{k}
and
T_{k}(z)^{t}x_{k}=y_{k}
, where
x_{k}=\mu(f_{k})/\|\mu(f_{k})\|
and
y_{k}=\nu(f_{k})/\|\nu(f_{k})\|
. This implies

T_{k}(z)T_{k}(z)^{t}x_{k}=x_{k},\qquad T_{k}(z)^{t}T_{k}(z)y_{k}=y_{k}

and hence

T_{k}(z)T_{k}(z)^{t}\mu(f_{k})=\mu(f_{k}),\qquad T_{k}(z)^{t}T_{k}(z)\nu(f_{k})=\nu(f_{k}).

Recall that
\Theta_{k}=T_{k}(z)
if
\alpha_{k}
is odd and
\Theta_{k}=T_{k}(z)^{t}
if
\alpha_{k}
is even.

Consider first the case when
\alpha_{k}
is odd and
\Theta_{k}=T_{k}(z)
. We have

\mu(f_{k})=\left(1,\frac{d_{\alpha_{k}}}{d_{\alpha_{k}+1}},\dots\right)^{t},\qquad\nu(f_{k})=\left(1,\frac{d_{\alpha_{k}+1}}{d_{\alpha_{k}+2}},\dots\right)^{t},

and, since
\alpha_{k}
is odd, by the recursive definitions of
\mu
and
\nu
, we have that

\mu[\alpha_{k},\beta_{k}]=\left(\mu_{\frac{\alpha_{k}+1}{2}},\dots,\mu_{\left\lceil\frac{\beta_{k}+1}{2}\right\rceil}\right)^{t}=\mu_{\frac{\alpha_{k}+1}{2}}\cdot\mu(f_{k}),

\nu[\alpha_{k},\beta_{k}]=\left(\nu_{\frac{\alpha_{k}+1}{2}},\dots,\nu_{\left\lceil\frac{\beta_{k}}{2}\right\rceil}\right)^{t}=\nu_{\frac{\alpha_{k}+1}{2}}\cdot\nu(f_{k}),

and hence
\mu[\alpha_{k},\beta_{k}]
is in the
1
-eigenspace for
\Theta_{k}\Theta_{k}^{t}=T_{k}(z)T_{k}(z)^{t}
and
\nu[\alpha_{k},\beta_{k}]
is in the
1
-eigenspace for
\Theta_{k}^{t}\Theta_{k}=T_{k}(z)^{t}T_{k}(z)
.

Next, consider the case when
\alpha_{k}
is even and
\Theta_{k}=T_{k}(z)^{t}
. Since

\nu(f_{k})=\left(1,\frac{d_{\alpha_{k}+1}}{d_{\alpha_{k}+2}},\dots\right)^{t},\qquad\mu(f_{k})=\left(1,\frac{d_{\alpha_{k}}}{d_{\alpha_{k}+1}},\dots\right)^{t},

and
\alpha_{k}
is even we have

\mu[\alpha_{k},\beta_{k}]=\left(\mu_{\frac{\alpha_{k}}{2}+1},\dots,\mu_{\left\lceil\frac{\beta_{k}+1}{2}\right\rceil}\right)^{t}=\mu_{\frac{\alpha_{k}}{2}+1}\cdot\nu(f_{k}),

\nu[\alpha_{k},\beta_{k}]=\left(\nu_{\frac{\alpha_{k}}{2}},\dots,\nu_{\left\lceil\frac{\beta_{k}}{2}\right\rceil}\right)^{t}=\nu_{\frac{\alpha_{k}}{2}}\cdot\mu(f_{k}).

Thus, when
\alpha_{k}
is even,
\mu[\alpha_{k},\beta_{k}]
is in the
1
-eigenspace for
\Theta_{k}\Theta_{k}^{t}=T_{k}(z)^{t}T_{k}(z)
and
\nu[\alpha_{k},\beta_{k}]
is in the
1
-eigenspace for
\Theta_{k}^{t}\Theta_{k}=T_{k}(z)T_{k}(z)^{t}
. Thus, in any case,
\mu[\alpha_{k},\beta_{k}]
is in the
1
-eigenspace of
\Theta_{k}\Theta_{k}^{t}
and
\nu[\alpha_{k},\beta_{k}]
is in the
1
-eigenspace of
\Theta_{k}^{t}\Theta_{k}
, completing the proof.

\Box

Remark 3.17
.

The entries of
T(z)^{t}T(z)
and
T(z)T(z)^{t}
are all sums of products of at most two
z_{i}
, where
z
is treated as a variable for a fixed
d
. Thus, the system of equations produced by Proposition
3.15
is at most quadratic in the entries of
z
and can be solved by, e.g., Mathematica
[
Res22
]
relatively quickly. (See Example
4.4
for such code.)

Much like in the case when we can find a viable
z
with all positive entries, there are cases when we can guarantee that certain viable
z
are the best possible.

Definition 3.18
.

Let
\mu
,
\nu
be as in Definition
3.4
. Define

x\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\frac{\mu}{\|\mu\|}\qquad\text{and}\qquad y\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\frac{\nu}{\|\nu\|}.

Proposition 3.19
.

Let
x
and
y
be as in Definition
3.18
. There exists a viable
z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n-1}
such that
T(z)y=x
and
T(z)^{t}x=y
if and only if

d^{D}(1,n)=\frac{\|\mu\|\|\nu\|}{d_{1}}.

Proof.

Assume first that such a viable
z
exists. By Lemma
3.5
, we have

x^{t}T(z)y=\frac{d_{1}}{\|\mu\|\|\nu\|}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}z_{i},

and, by hypothesis,

x^{t}T(z)y=\left(T(z)^{t}x\right)^{t}y=y^{t}y=1,

which implies

\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}z_{i}=\frac{\|\mu\|\|\nu\|}{d_{1}}.

By Lemma
3.11
and Theorem
3.10
, this is the largest that
\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}z_{i}
can be.

Conversely, if
d^{D}(1,n)=\|\mu\|\|\nu\|/d_{1}
, then this maximum is attained at some viable
z
by Theorem
3.10
. Using Lemma
3.5
, since we know

x^{t}T(z)y=\frac{d_{1}}{\|\mu\|\|\nu\|}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}z_{i}=\frac{d_{1}}{\|\mu\|\|\nu\|}\cdot d^{D}(1,n)=1,

we have

x^{t}(T(z)y-x)=x^{t}T(z)y-x^{t}x=0

and, similarly,

y^{t}(T(z)^{t}x-y)=0,

since
x
and
y
are unit vectors. Thus, there exist vectors
x^{\perp}
and
y^{\perp}
such that
x^{t}x^{\perp}=y^{t}y^{\perp}=0
and
T(z)y=x+x^{\perp}
,
T(z)^{t}x=y+y^{\perp}
. Furthermore, since
x=\mu/\|\mu\|
,
y=\nu/\|\nu\|
, and
z
is viable, by Proposition
3.15
, we have

y=T(z)^{t}T(z)y=T(z)^{t}x+T(z)^{t}x^{\perp}=y+y^{\perp}+T(z)^{t}x^{\perp},

so
T(z)^{t}x^{\perp}=-y^{\perp}
, and a similar calculation shows that
T(z)y^{\perp}=-x^{\perp}
. So,

\|y^{\perp}\|^{2}=\left(y+y^{\perp}\right)^{t}y^{\perp}=\left(T(z)^{t}x\right)^{t}y^{\perp}=x^{t}T(z)y^{\perp}=x^{t}(-x^{\perp})=0,

and so
y^{\perp}=0
. Moreover,
x^{\perp}=-T(z)y^{\perp}=0
, and hence
T(z)y=x
and
T(z)^{t}x=y
.

\Box

Due to the recursive nature of the definitions of
\mu
and
\nu
, it is possible to solve the linear system
T(z)y=x
and
T(z)^{t}x=y
, and there is a unique such
z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n-1}
satisfying these equations. In order to state this solution, we introduce some notation.

Definition 3.20
.

Following the notation of Definition
3.4
, for
1\leqslant k\leqslant\lfloor\frac{n+1}{2}\rfloor
, define

\mu^{(k)}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{k})^{t},

and, for
1\leqslant k\leqslant n-\lfloor\frac{n+1}{2}\rfloor
, define

\nu^{(k)}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}(\nu_{1},\dots,\nu_{k})^{t}.

Corollary 3.21
.

Let
D\in M_{n}({\mathbb{R}})
be a symmetric tridiagonal matrix with zeros on the diagonal and
d_{1},\dots,d_{n-1}>0
on the superdiagonal. Then,

d^{D}(1,n)=\frac{\|\mu\|\|\nu\|}{d_{1}}

if and only if the vector
z
is viable, where

z_{1}=\frac{\|\nu\|}{d_{1}\|\mu\|},

and, for
i\geqslant 1
,

z_{2i}=\frac{1}{d_{1}\|\mu\|\|\nu\|}\left(\|\nu^{(i)}\|^{2}\|\mu\|^{2}-\|\mu^{(i)}\|^{2}\|\nu\|^{2}\right),

z_{2i+1}=\frac{1}{d_{1}\|\mu\|\|\nu\|}\left(\|\mu^{(i+1)}\|^{2}\|\nu\|^{2}-\|\nu^{(i)}\|^{2}\|\mu\|^{2}\right).

Proof.

This follows from Proposition
3.19
and solving the linear system
T(z)y=x
and
T(z)^{t}x=y
for
z
.

\Box

Remark 3.22
.

The
z
listed in Corollary
3.21
is not guaranteed to be viable; for example, if
\|\nu\|>\|\mu\|
, then
z_{2}<0
.

In the case all the weights on the edges of a path are equal, we can recover the result obtained in
[
Atz96
]
; see
[
Bes21
, Proposition 3]
.

Corollary 3.23
.

Suppose
d=(1,\dots,1)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n-1}
and
z
is a viable vector such that the maximum is attained at
z
. Then one of the following holds.

(i)

n=2k
,
z_{1}+\cdots+z_{n-1}=k
is attained when

z_{j}=1
if
j
is odd, and
z_{j}=0
, otherwise.

(i)

n=2k-1
,
z_{1}+\cdots+z_{n-1}=\sqrt{k(k-1)}
is attained when

z_{2j-1}=j\sqrt{\frac{k-1}{k}}-(j-1)\sqrt{\frac{k}{k-1}},\quad z_{2j}=j\left(\sqrt{\frac{k}{k-1}}-\sqrt{\frac{k-1}{k}}\right),\quad\quad 1\leqslant j\leqslant\lfloor n/2\rfloor.

Proof.

In each case, the proposed
z
satisfies the hypotheses of Proposition
3.19
.

\Box

4
Analysis for Paths of Length
\leq 6
and Outlook

In this section, we include an analysis of paths with
n
vertices, where
n\leqslant 6
. If
n\leqslant 3
, we can always find a
z
that satisfies the hypotheses of Proposition
3.19
, so we have the following.

Corollary 4.1
.

(i)

If
n=2
, then the maximum
d^{D}(1,2)=1/d_{1}
is attained at
z=1/d_{1}
.

(ii)

If
n=3
, then the maximum

d^{D}(1,3)=\frac{1}{d_{1}}\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{d_{1}}{d_{2}}\right)^{2}}

is attained at

z=\frac{1}{d_{1}\|\mu\|\|\nu\|}\left(\|\nu\|^{2},\|\mu\|^{2}-\|\nu\|^{2}\right)=\frac{1}{d_{1}\sqrt{1+\frac{d_{1}^{2}}{d_{2}^{2}}}}\left(1,\frac{d_{1}^{2}}{d_{2}^{2}}\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d_{1}^{2}+d_{2}^{2}}}\left(\frac{d_{2}}{d_{1}},\frac{d_{1}}{d_{2}}\right).

When
n=4
, the situation begins to grow more complicated; see
[
Bes21
, Proposition 3]
.

Corollary 4.2
.

Let
D\in M_{4}({\mathbb{R}})
be a symmetric tridiagonal matrix with zeros on the diagonal and
d_{1},d_{2},d_{3}
on the superdiagonal. Then one of the following holds.

(a)

If
\|\nu\|>\|\mu\|
(or, equivalently, if
d_{2}^{2}>d_{1}d_{3}
), then the maximum

d^{D}(1,4)=\frac{1}{d_{1}}+\frac{1}{d_{3}}

is attained at
z=(1/d_{1},0,1/d_{3})
.

(b)

If
\|\mu\|\geqslant\|\nu\|
(or, equivalently,
d_{2}^{2}\leqslant d_{1}d_{3}
), then the maximum

d^{D}(1,4)=\frac{\|\mu\|\|\nu\|}{d_{1}}=\frac{1}{d_{1}}\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{d_{1}}{d_{2}}\right)^{2}}\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{d_{2}}{d_{3}}\right)^{2}}

is attained at

z=\frac{1}{d_{1}\|\mu\|\|\nu\|}\left(\|\nu\|^{2},\|\mu\|^{2}-\|\nu\|^{2},\|\mu\|^{2}\|\nu\|^{2}-\|\mu\|^{2}\right).

Proof.

By Corollary
3.21
, the
z
listed in case (b) is the unique solution to the linear system
T(z)y=x
and
T(z)^{t}x=y
, and this
z
is viable precisely when its entries are nonnegative. Since
\|\nu\|>1
, the third coordinate is always positive, and the second coordinate is nonnegative
precisely when
\|\mu\|\geqslant\|\nu\|
(or, equivalently, when
d_{2}^{2}\leqslant d_{1}d_{3}
).

In the event that
\|\mu\|<\|\nu\|
(or, equivalently,
d_{2}^{2}>d_{1}d_{3}
), there is no viable
z
with the trivial decomposition by Proposition
3.19
and Corollary
3.21
. The only possible way to obtain a viable
z
with a nontrivial decomposition is with
d_{2}=0
, in which case the result follows by Theorem
3.10
(or Proposition
3.15
).

\Box

For example, if
n=4
,
(d_{1},d_{2},d_{3})=(3,2,1)
, then
the optimal
a_{1}-a_{4}
should be
1/3+1
by Corollary
4.2
. The value from (b),
(1/3)\sqrt{1+(3/2)^{2}}\sqrt{1+2^{2}}
, is larger than
1/3+1
,
but it is not attainable, since there is no viable
z
.

We can also deduce the following result when
n=5
.

Corollary 4.3
.

Let
D\in M_{5}({\mathbb{R}})
be a symmetric tridiagonal matrix with zeros on the diagonal and
d_{1},d_{2},d_{3},d_{4}
on the superdiagonal. Then one of the following holds.

(a)

If
\|\nu\|>\|\mu\|
, then the maximum

d^{D}(1,5)=\frac{1}{d_{1}}+\frac{1}{d_{3}}\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{d_{3}}{d_{4}}\right)^{2}}

is attained at

z=\left(\frac{1}{d_{1}},0,\frac{d_{4}}{d_{3}\sqrt{d_{3}^{2}+d_{4}^{2}}},\frac{d_{3}}{d_{4}\sqrt{d_{3}^{2}+d_{4}^{2}}}\right).

(b)

If
\|\mu\|>\sqrt{1+\frac{d_{1}^{2}}{d_{2}^{2}}}\cdot\|\nu\|
, then the maximum

d^{D}(1,5)=\frac{1}{d_{4}}+\frac{1}{d_{1}}\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{d_{1}}{d_{2}}\right)^{2}}

is attained at

z=\left(\frac{d_{2}}{d_{1}\sqrt{d_{1}^{2}+d_{2}^{2}}},\frac{d_{1}}{d_{2}\sqrt{d_{1}^{2}+d_{2}^{2}}},0,\frac{1}{d_{4}}\right).

(c)

If
\|\nu\|\leqslant\|\mu\|\leqslant\sqrt{1+\frac{d_{1}^{2}}{d_{2}^{2}}}\cdot\|\nu\|
, then the maximum

d^{D}(1,5)=\frac{\|\mu\|\|\nu\|}{d_{1}}=\frac{1}{d_{1}}\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{d_{1}}{d_{2}}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{d_{1}d_{3}}{d_{2}d_{4}}\right)^{2}}\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{d_{2}}{d_{3}}\right)^{2}}

occurs at

z=\frac{1}{d_{1}\|\mu\|\|\nu\|}\left(\|\nu\|^{2},\|\mu\|^{2}-\|\nu\|^{2},\left(1+\frac{d_{1}^{2}}{d_{2}^{2}}\right)\|\nu\|^{2}-\|\mu\|^{2},\|\nu\|^{2}\|\mu\|^{2}-\left(1+\frac{d_{1}^{2}}{d_{2}^{2}}\right)\|\nu\|^{2}\right).

Proof.

The proof is analogous to that of Corollary
4.2
: by Corollary
3.21
, if the vector
z
listed in (c) is viable, then the maximum occurs there. This vector is viable precisely when its entries are nonnegative, i.e., when

\|\nu\|\leqslant\|\mu\|\leqslant\sqrt{1+\frac{d_{1}^{2}}{d_{2}^{2}}}\cdot\|\nu\|.

At most one of these inequalities can fail, corresponding to (a) and (b), respectively.

\Box

Remark 4.5
.

It seems clear that the number of truly distinct subcases one must check increases in conjunction with the number of partitions of
