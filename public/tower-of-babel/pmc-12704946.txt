Knowledge and attitude of parents toward human papillomavirus infection and HPV vaccination in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia
Authors: Abeer Badi Almutairi, Mohammed Alshehri, Raghad Almutairi, Mohammed Aldosari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:This study examined the knowledge and attitudes of parents in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, regarding human papillomavirus (HPV) and its vaccine. Recognizing the critical role of parental awareness in vaccine acceptance, the research aimed to assess current levels of understanding, identify demographic factors influencing knowledge, and explore attitudes toward HPV vaccination.Methodology:Using a convenience sampling technique, this quantitative cross-sectional study was conducted between December 2022 and July 2023. The research engaged 330 participants among parents of both genders in Riyadh. Data were collected using a pretested, semi-structured questionnaire adapted from a prior study in Saudi Arabia’s western region. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., located in Cary, North Carolina, United States), encompassing frequencies, percentages, Chi-square tests, and logistic regression.Results:The demographic analysis revealed a predominantly female (95.5%), married (90.0%), and educated sample. Positive attitudes toward vaccination were prevalent, with 50.3% expressing willingness to vaccinate. However, over half of the participants (54.2%) lacked awareness about HPV, indicating a need for targeted educational interventions. Higher education levels correlated with better knowledge. Concerns about vaccine side effects were prevalent (29.4%), impacting willingness to vaccinate. Positive attitudes were associated with higher knowledge levels, with 68.5% of those with good knowledge expressing willingness to vaccinate.Conclusion:This study revealed critical insights into parental knowledge and attitudes regarding HPV and vaccination in Riyadh. The findings emphasize the urgency of educational interventions to enhance awareness and address specific misconceptions. Tailored public health campaigns, considering diverse sources of information and demographic characteristics, are essential for improving vaccination rates and mitigating health risks associated with HPV.

Introduction

The human papillomavirus (HPV) is regarded as the primary cause of cervical malignancy and is a widely recognized sexually transmitted infection. Cancer is the fourth most prevalent malignancy in women worldwide.[1] In 2018, approximately 85% of women in low-income and middle-income nations died from cervical cancer.[2] HPV is responsible for almost 95% of cervical tumors.[1] Saudi Arabia’s incidence of HPV in invasive cervical carcinoma (82%) is at the lower extreme of the range reported worldwide (85%–99%).[3] Getting vaccinated against HPV types 16 and 18 reduces the risk of developing cervical cancer. There are three types of HPV vaccines (bivalent, quadrivalent, and nonvalent), all highly effective against HPV types 16 and 18.[4] Over the next 20 years, the World Health Organization predicts that if more than 80% of young women receive the HPV vaccination, millions of women in low-income and middle-income nations will be protected.[1] We believe that awareness and knowledge of HPV infections and vaccination significantly contribute to decreasing the incidence and prevalence of HPV infection and its subsequent risk of developing cervical cancer. In the literature, we found extensive research focusing on this matter.

From April 2019 to March 2020, cross-sectional research was carried out at Sidra Medical, Qatar. In this study, almost 60% of parents reported having no idea that HPV may cause cancers of the genitourinary system, including cervical tumors. Seventy-seven percent of participants reported feeling “extremely comfortable” with the idea of providing their children a vaccination to protect them from developing genital tumors.[3] Eventually, around 20% of those who took the survey said they still had doubts regarding the HPV vaccination. In the end, many parents living in Qatar favor the HPV vaccination.[5]

Another study was conducted in Serbia from December 2015 to May 2016. They found that the parents had a median knowledge score of 6.0. The range of possible total scores was between 0 and 10. A relatively small percentage of parents (7.1%) responded correctly to all questions (knowledge score 10 out of 10). A better knowledge score on HPV and HPV vaccination was related to the parent being female, highly educated, employed, and possessing a higher income across the whole sample of parents. In addition, they found that having a female child and a greater understanding of HPV and HPV vaccination were independent predictors of a favorable attitude regarding vaccination among parents of children younger than 9 years old. The suggestion of an HPV vaccine by a doctor was independently related to a favorable attitude regarding HPV vaccination among parents of children 9 years old or older. Furthermore, they found a marginally significant relationship between parental positivity and a better knowledge score on HPV infection and the HPV vaccine, indicating that parents’ education level significantly affects their knowledge and positive attitude toward the vaccine.[6]

Over one-third of participants (35.1%) in a separate survey conducted in Ethiopia were knowledgeable about the HPV vaccination and cervical cancer. 44.8% of individuals were open to receiving the HPV vaccine. Factors such as employment and family history of sexually transmitted diseases were substantially linked with HPV vaccination awareness. The attitude of parents of 9- to 14-year-old children toward the HPV vaccination was strongly influenced by participant age, educational level, fear of HPV infection, and awareness of the HPV vaccine. They found that understanding and willingness to obtain the HPV vaccination were low among parents. Multiple significant variables affected parents’ knowledge and attitudes toward HPV and the vaccine, but education level may have had the most significant effect compared to the other variables.[7]

Similar research in Greece indicated that 99.4% of parents were aware of HPV, and 98.8% were aware of the HPV vaccination. Additionally, 47% of the parents reported that their daughters had received the HPV vaccine. The following factors exhibited a statistically significant connection with HPV vaccination in the logistic regression analysis: perceived ease of developing HPV, degree of faith in the medical profession regarding preventive information, and overall assessment of the value of children’s vaccinations. The internet and social networking sites provided parents with knowledge of the participant’s age and her physician’s advice for HPV vaccination.[8] Researchers in western Saudi Arabia discovered that parents had little understanding of HPV and its vaccination. Around one-third (32.9%) were aware of the HPV vaccine, and doctors were the most common source of their information (38%), but the most prevalent obstacle to vaccination was the belief that they were not at risk (75.2%). Only 7.2% had immunized their daughters. The vast majority of parents were knowledgeable of and motivated to vaccinate their children against HPV. This research emphasizes the need to inform women about cervical tumor susceptibility factors and screening programs.[9] Numerous studies have been conducted on the risk factors, clinical presentations, management, and complications of HPV infection; however, in our particular study, we are interested in assessing caregivers’ knowledge and attitudes regarding HPV infection and its vaccination through a quantitative cross-sectional study to be conducted between December 2022 and July 2023 in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. We believe that our findings will provide valuable data to the education department in Riyadh Health Facilities regarding target population groups for educational sessions. This research would significantly highlight the need for precautionary healthcare by increasing awareness and propagating both screening and human papillomavirus vaccination.

Methodology

This quantitative cross-sectional study was conducted between December 2022 and July 2023 in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, among parents of both genders. The inclusion criteria comprised parents with one or more children, irrespective of gender, residing in Riyadh. The exclusion criteria excluded parents living outside Riyadh and those who were illiterate.

The study design involved a quantitative cross-sectional approach to gather a comprehensive snapshot of parents’ knowledge and attitudes regarding HPV infection and HPV vaccination during the specified timeframe. Calculations were based on an estimated absolute error of 5% at a 95% confidence interval to determine the appropriate sample size, yielding a required size of 384.

The sampling technique employed for participant selection was a convenient method chosen for its practicality and feasibility within the study setting. Data were collected using a pretested, semi-structured questionnaire adapted from a prior study conducted in the western region of Saudi Arabia,[9] explicitly focusing on HPV-related knowledge and attitudes among parents.

Data collection began after participants gave their informed consent and were reassured of the confidentiality and voluntariness of their cooperation. Ethical approval was acquired from the King Abdullah International Medical Research Center’s IRB. Data were subsequently input into an Excel computer program. A dedicated codebook was created, outlining variable descriptions and corresponding codes for efficient data management. The SAS system version 9.4 was utilized for data analysis, encompassing the calculation of questionnaire scores, frequencies, and percentages for categorical data. Numerical data were presented as means with standard deviations.

Statistical analyses involved applying the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test to explore relationships among variables. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was employed when indicated, expressing odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals relative to a reference baseline category. A significance level of P < 0.05 was adopted to determine statistical significance. Comprehensive results were summarized in representative tables and graphical figures, providing a thorough overview of the study’s findings.

Most participants were female (95.5%) and married (90.0%). Regarding education, the highest proportion held a university degree (65.2%), and a significant portion had a monthly income ranging from 10,000 to 20,000 Saudi Riyals (37.0%). Government employment was predominant (53.0%), housewives constituted a substantial portion (35.5%), and 86.4% reported having daughters.

Discussion

Our results explore participants’ knowledge of HPV, highlighting a considerable lack of awareness among the study population. More than half (54.2%) reported no knowledge about HPV, indicating a significant gap in information dissemination. This lack of awareness is further emphasized by the fact that 93.6% of participants believed or thought HPV to be very dangerous, demonstrating concerns about a virus with which many are unfamiliar. The study found that 46.4% correctly associated HPV with cervical cancer, indicating a moderate level of knowledge about the virus’s health implications. However, a substantial proportion (46.7%) was unsure about its impact on fertility, indicating a need for education on the broader consequences of HPV infection. This is similar to what is reported in a previous study conducted in the western region of Saudi Arabia, showing that 34.7% of parents had prior knowledge about HPV, 37.9% thought that HPV is very dangerous, and only 32.7% knew that HPV infection is associated with cervical cancer.[9]

The striking similarities between the current study’s findings and the survey conducted among Saudi medical students at King Faisal University in 2014 highlight a persistent lack of understanding regarding HPV and its associated risks in various segments of the population.[10] In both studies, participants demonstrated limited knowledge, emphasizing the need for targeted educational interventions across different demographics. In the Saudi medical student survey, accurate answers ranged from 43.7% to 55% on average, underscoring the challenges in disseminating information about early warning signs, symptoms, and risk factors for cervical cancer.[10]

Similarly, a study conducted among students aged 17–26 in Poland revealed insufficient overall knowledge of cervical cancer, with HPV infection not recognized as the primary cause.[11] This aligns with the current study’s findings, where a considerable proportion of participants were unsure about the impact of HPV on fertility, emphasizing the need for comprehensive education regarding the virus and its associated health risks. Interestingly, a recent survey among healthcare practitioners in Greece uncovered a significant knowledge gap regarding HPV, with only 30% of the sample aware of HPV’s substantial role in carcinogenesis. This surprising finding underscores the importance of continuous medical education and professional development to ensure that healthcare providers are equipped with up-to-date information about prevalent health issues.[12] Given their influential role in guiding patient decisions, healthcare practitioners need to be well-informed advocates for HPV vaccination and cervical cancer prevention.

The findings from a recent Thai poll further reinforce the global trend of low understanding regarding HPV. This cross-cultural similarity emphasizes the need for a unified, international effort to address knowledge gaps and misconceptions surrounding HPV. While the specifics of cultural beliefs and healthcare infrastructure may vary, the overarching theme remains consistent: there is a need for improved education and awareness campaigns to enhance the understanding of HPV and promote preventive measures.[12]

In light of these global parallels, it becomes evident that addressing knowledge gaps surrounding HPV is a multifaceted challenge that extends beyond geographic boundaries.[13] Collaborative efforts on an international scale are crucial to developing comprehensive educational strategies that are culturally sensitive and tailored to the specific needs of diverse populations.

Awareness about the HPV vaccine was observed in only 43.0% of participants, suggesting that more efforts are needed to promote information about preventive measures. Notably, concerns about vaccine side effects were present in 57.7% of respondents, underscoring the importance of addressing misconceptions and fears associated with vaccination. The study’s results align with a 2014 study in Saudi Arabia, where 67% of participants were unaware of HPV vaccination. This persistent lack of awareness may stem from a focus on mandatory vaccinations, leaving optional vaccines like HPV less recognized.[10] Additionally, insufficient health education in primary care settings could contribute to the knowledge gap. To address this, it is recommended that public awareness be enhanced by encouraging healthcare providers in primary care to actively educate individuals about the importance of the HPV vaccine during routine interactions.

Moreover, our results revealed varied sources of knowledge about the HPV vaccine, with the internet (39.1%) and healthcare professionals (12.4%) playing significant roles. The predominance of the internet as the main source of knowledge is reported in many previous studies.[14151617] This emphasizes the need for accurate and accessible online information and the importance of healthcare providers in delivering education about HPV and its preventive measures.

In terms of attitudes toward vaccination [Table 3], the majority of participants expressed willingness to vaccinate their children (50.3%). However, a significant portion hesitated (24.2%), and 25.5% were undecided, indicating a complex landscape of attitudes. Concerns about side effects (24.7%) and a lack of belief in vaccine effectiveness (13.5%) were identified as reasons for vaccine refusal. Notably, 40.0% attributed their reluctance to insufficient knowledge about the vaccine, highlighting the pivotal role of education in shaping vaccination attitudes. The present study’s findings are consistent with those of the 2016 research conducted in Riyadh, where only 37% of participants declined immunization.[18] Moreover, a recent national study in Saudi Arabia revealed that approximately 55% expressed willingness to receive the HPV vaccination if offered, and 73% expressed intent to recommend the vaccine to others. Similarly, in a study involving Jeddah medical students, over 50% indicated a desire to obtain the HPV vaccination.[1920]

Educational level emerged as a significant factor, with participants holding higher education levels more likely to possess good knowledge about HPV. This result is similar to what was reported in a previous study showing that being highly educated and receiving health information from physicians were determinants of parents’ willingness to vaccinate their children.[9] Additionally, various studies in the literature have shown that a higher level of education is significantly associated with better knowledge of HPV.[212223] This underscores the need for targeted educational interventions, especially for those with lower educational backgrounds. Other demographic factors, including age, gender, marital status, monthly income, and profession, did not exhibit significant associations with knowledge levels.

A statistically significant association was found between knowledge level and vaccination willingness. Parents with good knowledge were more likely to express a positive attitude toward vaccination, which is similar to what has been reported in the literature.[32425262728] This further underscores the importance of education in influencing vaccination decisions.

The study’s findings collectively point toward the critical role of education in shaping knowledge and attitudes toward HPV and vaccination. The significant gaps in awareness about HPV, its health implications, and the preventive measures underscore the need for targeted educational campaigns. Such campaigns should consider the diverse demographic characteristics of the population, with a focus on reaching those with lower educational levels. Furthermore, addressing concerns about vaccine side effects and efficacy is crucial for building trust and promoting vaccine uptake. Healthcare professionals and online platforms are identified as key sources of information, indicating the importance of accurate and accessible communication channels. Collaborative efforts between healthcare providers, educational institutions, and public health organizations can play a pivotal role in improving knowledge and fostering positive attitudes toward HPV vaccination.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the study provides valuable insights into the demographic distribution, knowledge, and attitudes of the population regarding HPV and its vaccine. The identified gaps in awareness and the association between knowledge and vaccination willingness highlight the need for targeted educational interventions to enhance public understanding and promote preventive measures. These findings can guide public health initiatives aimed at increasing HPV vaccination rates and reducing the burden of associated diseases in the community.
