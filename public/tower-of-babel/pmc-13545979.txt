Disrupted cerebellar networks in uncontrolled focal to bilateral tonic–clonic seizures
Authors: John Phamnguyen, Elaine Kuan, Puneet Dheer, Viktor Vegh, David Reutens
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveFocal to bilateral tonic–clonic seizures (FBTC) are associated with significant morbidity and an elevated risk of sudden unexpected death in epilepsy (SUDEP). The cerebellum has been implicated in modulating the frequency and severity of tonic–clonic seizures. The present study aimed to identify alterations in cerebellar functional connectivity (FC) associated with susceptibility to FBTC in individuals diagnosed with temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE).MethodsThis prospective study involved the recruitment of 30 patients with unilateral TLE, who were classified into two groups: active FBTC (≥1 FBTC in the past year) and inactive FBTC (ongoing focal seizures but no FBTC within the past year, and in some cases no lifetime history), each consisting of 15 patients. Additionally, 20 demographically matched control subjects were included. Resting‐state functional MRI (rs‐fMRI) and high‐resolution T1‐weighted imaging were utilised. FC analysis was performed using the CONN toolbox, with a focus on cerebellar and subcortical regions. Group comparisons were executed using Kruskal–Wallis tests, followed by post hoc pairwise analyses.ResultsThe findings indicated that FC between the posterior vermis and lateral cerebellar lobules (Crus I/II) bilaterally were significantly lower in the active FBTC group compared to healthy controls. In comparison to the inactive group, the active FBTC group exhibited significantly lower FC with the contralateral Crus I and Crus II. In comparison to controls, the TLE groups exhibited lower FC between the posterior vermis and the mediodorsal and pulvinar thalamic nuclei as well as between the flocculonodular lobe and mesial temporal structures (hippocampus, parahippocampus, and amygdala).SignificanceThe results demonstrate impaired intracerebellar FC, particularly between the posterior vermis and lateral cerebellar lobules, in individuals with active FBTC, thereby supporting the hypothesis that the cerebellum has a regulatory role in modulating susceptibility to tonic–clonic seizures.Plain Language SummaryFocal to bilateral tonic–clonic seizures are severe seizures that start in one brain region and spread to both sides of the brain. In this study, people with temporal lobe epilepsy who had recent tonic–clonic seizures showed reduced communication between the posterior vermis and lateral cerebellar regions. Alterations in other cerebellar connections with the thalamus and temporal lobe were related to temporal lobe epilepsy itself. These findings suggest that cerebellar networks may influence vulnerability to tonic–clonic seizure spread and may be relevant to future sudden unexpected death in epilepsy risk research.Keywords: cerebellum, focal to bilateral tonic–clonic seizures, functional connectivity, resting‐state functional MRI, temporal lobe epilepsy

INTRODUCTION

Focal to bilateral tonic–clonic seizures (FBTC) represent one of the most severe seizure phenotypes. These seizures increase the risk of seizure‐related injury, 1 reduce quality of life, 2 , 3 predispose patients to unfavorable postsurgical outcomes, 4 , 5 and elevate the risk of sudden unexpected death in epilepsy (SUDEP). 6 , 7 SUDEP remains the leading cause of mortality among patients with epilepsy, 8 , 9 highlighting the importance of elucidating the mechanisms underlying FBTC.

Emerging evidence suggests that the cerebellum plays a crucial role in modulating susceptibility to FBTC. Ictal SPECT studies have revealed significant cerebellar activation during tonic–clonic seizures, 10 while morphological alterations in cerebellar structures have been documented in patients experiencing uncontrolled seizures. 10 , 11 , 12 In tonic–clonic seizures, the cerebellum's involvement likely extends beyond motor control, 13 , 14 encompassing autonomic regulation of blood pressure 15 and ventilatory responses to hypoxia, 16 , 17 , 18 which are implicated in SUDEP pathophysiology.

Therapeutic interventions targeting the cerebellum have demonstrated efficacy in enhancing seizure control. In animal models, cerebellar stimulation has been shown to attenuate epileptic discharges. Additionally, human trials utilizing deep brain stimulation and repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) of cerebellar targets have reported reductions in seizure frequency. 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 Despite these promising findings, the specific neural networks through which the cerebellum influences FBTC remain inadequately understood. Therefore, this study employed resting‐state functional magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) to investigate cerebellar functional connectivity (FC) in groups of temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE) patients exhibiting varying frequencies of FBTC. 24 , 25 By comparing patients with active versus inactive FBTC to healthy controls, we aimed to identify network alterations associated with underlying FBTC susceptibility.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Subjects

Thirty patients with TLE were prospectively recruited from the Royal Brisbane and Women's Hospital Comprehensive Epilepsy Program. The diagnosis and lateralization of TLE were based on clinical history, video‐EEG recordings, MRI and FDG PET brain imaging, ictal SPECT, and neuropsychological evaluation. Exclusion criteria included contraindications to MRI scanning, a history of prior brain surgery, or acquired brain injury.

Patients were prospectively categorized into two groups (15 participants each) based on their seizure history at the time of scanning. Participants with one or more FBTC within the preceding 12 months were classified as the “active” FBTC group. Those who had no FBTC within the past year (and in some cases no lifetime history of FBTC) were classified as the “inactive FBTC” group. Twenty healthy controls were demographically matched to the patients following screening to exclude neurological disorders using a standardized questionnaire. Seizure and epilepsy terminology was applied in accordance with current International League Against Epilepsy classification schemes. The study received approval by the Metro North Hospital Health Service (MNHSS) and the University of Queensland (UQ) ethics committee (HREC/2021/QRBW/74461). The total sample size was determined pragmatically by study funding which supported 50 MRI scans. All participants provided written, informed consent.

Data acquisition

T1‐weighted MRI and functional MRI (fMRI) data were acquired using a 3T Siemens Magnetom Prisma. High‐resolution T1‐weighted anatomical images were obtained with the following parameters: repetition time (TR) = 1900 ms, echo time (TE) = 2.26 ms, flip angle = 9 degrees, field of view = 25.6 cm × 25.6 cm, acquisition matrix = 256 × 256, slice thickness = 1 mm, and a total of 192 slices without interslice gap. Following the acquisition of the T1 images, a 10‐min resting‐state fMRI volume was collected with the parameters: TR = 2000 ms, TE = 23 ms, flip angle = 90°, slice thickness = 3 mm, and a total of 42 slices. Participants were instructed to keep their eyes closed, avoid falling asleep, and refrain from engaging in any specific thoughts.

Data preprocessing

Functional and anatomical data were preprocessed utilizing CONN (version 22.a), 26 , 27 an SPM12‐based software application that runs within MATLAB. The preprocessing steps encompassed the generation of voxel displacement maps from phase and magnitude images, realignment via the SPM realign and unwarp procedure, susceptibility distortion correction, slice‐timing correction, and the identification of regressor files using the Artifact Detection Toolbox (ART) procedure. Outlier scans identified by ART were excluded from subsequent analyses, 28 specifically those with framewise displacement above 0.5 mm or global BOLD signal changes above three standard deviations. 28 , 29 These thresholds correspond to the conservative default settings in CONN (0.5 mm displacement and 3 SD global signal change), rather than the more liberal alternatives (2 mm displacement and 9 SD). A reference BOLD image was computed by averaging the remaining scans, which was subsequently utilized for accurate co‐registration to the high‐resolution structural T1 image. To minimize bias from excessive data loss, datasets with >20% of volumes flagged as motion outliers under the conservative criteria were considered to have insufficient usable data. Two participants exceeded this threshold and were excluded from further analysis. One of these participants underwent repeat scanning with acceptable motion parameters and was retained in the final cohort. An additional participant, matched for age and seizure severity, was subsequently recruited and scanned to maintain group balance. After exclusion and replacement, the proportion of scrubbed volumes did not differ significantly between groups (Kruskal–Wallis; p = 0.473), and the effective degrees of freedom after denoising remained within the expected range.

Functional and anatomical data were normalized to standard MNI space, segmented into gray matter, white matter, and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) tissue classes, and resampled to 2 mm isotropic voxels using a direct normalization procedure that employed SPM's unified segmentation and normalization algorithm with the default tissue probability map template. The functional data underwent spatial smoothing through convolution with a 6 mm full width half maximum (FWHM) Gaussian kernel. This kernel size is widely used in resting‐state fMRI studies to enhance signal‐to‐noise ratio while preserving spatial specificity of network connectivity measures. Although the optimal smoothing kernel depends on voxel size, expected effect sizes, and the spatial scale of the regions of interest, values in the 4–8 mm range are commonly used and represent a compromise between sensitivity and spatial specificity. 30 , 31

Subsequently, the functional data were denoised through a standardized denoising pipeline that included the regression of physiological confounds estimated using aCompCor 32 (five principal components each from white matter and CSF), motion parameters and their first‐order derivatives (12 factors), outlier scans (up to 44 regressors), session effects and their first‐order derivatives (two factors), and linear trends (two factors) within each functional run. Band pass frequency filtering of the BOLD time series was applied between 0.008 Hz and 0.09 Hz. The CompCor noise components associated with white matter and CSF were estimated by calculating the average BOLD signal as well as the largest principal components orthogonal to the BOLD average, motion parameters, and outlier scans within each subject's eroded segmentation masks. Based on the number of noise terms included in this denoising strategy, the effective degrees of freedom of the BOLD signal after denoising were estimated to range from 74.1 to 85.3 (with an average 82.4) across all subjects. This relatively narrow range of effective degrees of freedom, despite variable numbers of outlier volumes, is primarily driven by the band‐pass filtering step, ensuring statistical comparisons remain robust across datasets.

Functional connectivity processing

The Automated Anatomical Labelling Atlas 3 (AAL3) 33 was utilized to parcellate the brain into 166 regions of interest (ROI). ROI‐to‐ROI connectivity (RRC) matrices were estimated to characterize the FC between each pair of ROIs. The strength of FC was represented by Fisher‐transformed Z scores derived from bivariate correlation coefficients obtained from a weighted general linear model, calculated separately for each ROI pair. A Fisher‐Z score was applied to stabilize the variance of the correlation coefficient and improve the normality of connectivity estimates prior to group‐level statistical comparisons. The magnitude of Fisher Z connectivity values observed in this dataset was comparable to those reported in previous resting‐state FC studies in epilepsy patients 34 , 35 or evaluating cerebellar FC 36 using similar ROI correlation‐based approaches.

From the 166 parcellated regions, 45 regions were selected, which comprised networks of interest in TLE. The complete list of included regions of interest is provided in Table S1. This selection included cortical regions, as well as subcortical regions such as the thalamus, basal ganglia, and brainstem structures, along with 17 cerebellar regions. The cerebellar ROIs were defined at the lobule level according to the AAL3 anatomical conventions. These lobules included in both cerebellar hemispheres: Crus I and II, as well as lobules III, IV–V, VI, VII, VIII, IX, and X; the midline vermal regions I–V (anterior vermis), VI–IX (posterior vermis), and X (flocculonodular vermis). A 45 × 45 × 50 three‐dimensional FC strength matrix was reshaped, extracting the lower triangle of the symmetric correlation matrices (excluding self‐connections) to yield 990 unique ROI‐to‐ROI comparisons ([452–45]/2 = 990) per subject. This resulted in a 990 × 50 two‐dimensional matrix for analysis in SPSS. To facilitate comparisons in terms of side of ictal onset, 37 , 38 FC matrices for 10 right TLE patients were flipped along the midsagittal plane. This procedure ensured that all connectivity measures were aligned relative to the epileptogenic hemisphere, allowing group comparisons to be performed along an ipsilateral–contralateral axis. Consequently, in the subsequent sections of the manuscript, structures are denoted as being ipsilateral or contralateral to the seizure focus. Comparisons of demographic and clinical data were conducted using the chi‐squared test for categorical variables and the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables, using IBM SPSS v29. Comparisons of FC strength among all ROIs for the three groups (active, inactive, and controls) were performed using the Kruskal–Wallis omnibus test to identify group differences. In instances where statistically significant group differences were detected, the three pairwise post hoc comparisons were performed with Dunn tests and Holm adjustment. 39 The threshold for statistical significance was established at p < 0.05 for all tests.

RESULTS

Demographics and clinical data

The distribution of age and sex was comparable across the groups. The active and inactive epilepsy groups demonstrated similarities in terms of antiseizure medication usage and duration of epilepsy (Table 1). No statistically significant differences were observed between the epilepsy groups regarding age of onset (p = 0.436), duration of epilepsy (p = 0.325), number of antiseizure medications utilized (p = 0.389), or average monthly frequency of focal seizures (p = 0.713). Within the inactive FBTC group, 12/15 (80%) patients had a remote lifetime history of FBTC but none within the 12 months preceding MRI acquisition, while 3/15 (20%) had no known lifetime history of FBTC. In the active FBTC group, 5/15 (33%) patients had more than three FBTC in the preceding year.

Intracerebellar circuitry

Posterior vermis to lateral cerebellar hemisphere connectivity

Omnibus Testing

The Kruskal–Wallis test revealed significant differences between groups in FC between the posterior vermis and several lateral cerebellar lobules: ipsilateral Crus I (Kruskal–Wallis χ2[2] = 7.91, p = 0.019), ipsilateral Crus II (Kruskal–Wallis χ2[2] = 9.50, p = 0.009), contralateral Crus I (Kruskal–Wallis χ2[2] = 8.92, p = 0.012), and contralateral Crus II (Kruskal–Wallis χ2[2] = 9.48, p = 0.009).

Pairwise Comparisons

Post hoc analyses demonstrated that, compared to healthy controls, the active FBTC group had significantly lower FC between the posterior vermis and ipsilateral Crus I (p = 0.015), ipsilateral Crus II (p = 0.006), and contralateral Crus I (p = 0.036). Compared to the inactive group, the active FBTC group had significantly lower FC between the posterior vermis and contralateral Crus I (p = 0.020) and contralateral Crus II (p = 0.006). No significant differences in FC were observed between the inactive FBTC group and healthy controls. The distributions of posterior vermis–Crus I/II FC values across groups are shown in Figure S1.

Posterior vermis to other cerebellar regions

Omnibus Testing

Significant differences between groups were observed in FC between the posterior vermis and contralateral lobule III/IV/V (Kruskal–Wallis χ2[2] = 7.51, p = 0.023) and contralateral lobule X (Kruskal–Wallis χ2[2] = 10.15, p = 0.006).

Pairwise Comparisons

FC between posterior vermis and contralateral lobule III/IV/V was significantly lower in the active FBTC group compared to controls (p = 0.046). In both epilepsy groups, FC between posterior vermis and contralateral lobule X was lower than in controls (inactive: p = 0.009; active: p = 0.040), with no significant differences being observed between epilepsy groups.

Anterior vermis and Flocculonodular connectivity

No significant group differences in FC were observed between the anterior vermis or flocculonodular lobe and other cerebellar lobules.

Cerebellar‐subcortical connectivity

Posterior vermis–thalamic connectivity

Omnibus Testing

Significant group differences were identified in FC between the posterior vermis and ipsilateral mediodorsal nucleus (Kruskal–Wallis χ2[2] = 15.330, p = 0.001) and ipsilateral pulvinar (Kruskal–Wallis χ2[2] = 11.27, p = 0.004).

Pairwise Comparisons

In both epilepsy groups, FC between posterior vermis and ipsilateral mediodorsal nucleus (inactive: p = 0.003; active: p = 0.010) and ipsilateral pulvinar (inactive: p = 0.012; active: p = 0.010) was significantly lower than in controls but the two epilepsy groups did not differ significantly. Additionally, in the inactive group, FC between posterior vermis and contralateral mediodorsal nucleus was significantly lower than in both control (p = 0.003) and active FBTC groups (p = 0.028). The distributions of posterior vermis–ipsilateral mediodorsal and pulvinar connectivity values are shown in Figure S3.

Cerebellar–temporal connectivity

Flocculonodular‐mesial temporal connectivity

Omnibus Testing

Significant group differences were found between the flocculonodular vermis and mesial temporal structures, including ipsilateral hippocampus (Kruskal–Wallis χ2[2] = 7.83, p = 0.020), ipsilateral amygdala (Kruskal–Wallis χ2[2] = 6.27, p = 0.043), contralateral hippocampus (Kruskal–Wallis χ2[2] = 11.34, p = 0.003), contralateral parahippocampus (Kruskal–Wallis χ2[2] = 8.79, p = 0.012), and contralateral amygdala (Kruskal–Wallis χ2[2] = 8.97, p = 0.011).

Pairwise Comparisons

When all epilepsy patients were compared to healthy controls, the FC for all aforementioned connections was significantly lower in epilepsy patients, but no significant differences were observed between active and inactive FBTC groups. The distributions of flocculonodular vermis–mesial temporal connectivity values across groups are shown in Figure S2.

Lateral cerebellar‐mesial temporal connectivity

Omnibus Testing

Significant group differences were observed in FC between ipsilateral Crus I and mesial temporal structures: ipsilateral hippocampus (Kruskal–Wallis χ2[2] = 10.46, p = 0.005), parahippocampus (Kruskal–Wallis χ2[2] = 10.57, p = 0.005), amygdala (Kruskal–Wallis χ2[2] = 7.54, p = 0.023), and contralateral parahippocampus (Kruskal–Wallis χ2[2] = 6.37, p = 0.042).

Pairwise Comparisons

The only statistically significant group differences in FC between lateral cerebellar lobules and mesial temporal regions were for connectivity with ipsilateral Crus I. FC with this structure was significantly lower in the inactive FBTC group compared to controls for ipsilateral hippocampus (p = 0.015), parahippocampus (p = 0.006), and amygdala (p = 0.020), and for contralateral parahippocampus (p = 0.009). In the inactive FBTC group, FC between ipsilateral Crus I and ipsilateral hippocampus (p = 0.005), parahippocampus (p = 0.007), and amygdala (p = 0.014) was significantly lower than for the active FBTC group.

Anterior vermis connectivity

No significant group differences in FC were found between the anterior vermis and any temporal, basal ganglia, insular, or thalamic regions. The key group differences are summarised in Table 2.

DISCUSSION

This study revealed distinct alterations in cerebellar functional networks in TLE patients with recent and without recent FBTC. In those with active FBTC, intracerebellar FC between the posterior vermis and lateral cerebellar lobules (Crus I and II) was significantly lower than in those with inactive FBTC and in healthy controls. The findings build upon previous research utilizing ictal SPECT 10 and structural imaging, 10 , 11 , 12 which implicate cerebellar involvement in tonic–clonic seizures. Our results pinpoint a specific cerebellar circuit that may influence susceptibility to seizure generalization in patients with TLE. Additionally, observed differences in FC between the cerebellum and thalamus, as well as the temporal lobe, highlight the potential role of cerebellar networks in the broader pathophysiology of TLE (Figure 1).

Schematic illustration of affected functional circuits. Arrows denote specific pathways demonstrating significantly reduced functional connectivity.

The posterior vermis and Crus I/II are anatomically linked with pathways that are implicated in tonic–clonic seizure semiology. The motor posturing observed during the tonic phase of FBTC bears resemblance to decerebrate and decorticate posturing, which are thought to reflect vestibulospinal and rubrospinal activation respectively (Figure 2). The posterior vermis is thought to inhibit the fastigial nucleus, which has excitatory projections to the vestibular nuclei. The lateral cerebellar lobules have extensive projections to the dentate nucleus, which projects to the red nucleus via the dentato‐rubral pathway. 13 , 40 While direct monosynaptic projections between the posterior vermis and Crus I/II have not been described, the differences in FC between different FBTC groups imply differences in coordinated activity between the two regions that correspond to susceptibility to FBTC.

Known spinocerebellar motor pathways from posterior vermis and lateral cerebellar lobules.

The underlying mechanism of this coordinated activity remains uncertain. Both the posterior vermis and Crus I/II are embedded within a broader cerebello‐thalamo‐cortical network which involves prefrontal and temporal cortices, 41 as well as the deep cerebellar (fastigial and dentate) and distinct thalamic nuclei. The dentate nucleus modulates the red nucleus, which has been implicated in the ventilatory responses to hypoxia, 42 , 43 and has indirect connections with brainstem respiratory networks, including the Pre‐Bötzinger Complex. 44 In both animal 45 , 46 , 47 and human functional MRI studies, 48 , 49 the cerebellum has been shown to be involved in the ventilatory response to hypoxia 18 and activated following BTCS in humans 10 and during extinction of convulsive seizures in animal models. 50 These findings are consistent with SUDEP‐related imaging studies which consistently report volume loss in the cerebellar vermis and neocerebellum in SUDEP cases. Atrophy of the vermis has also been identified in MRI studies of patients with high SUDEP risk. 11 Cerebellar metabolism is also altered on interictal FDG PET 51 in patients at high risk of SUDEP because of frequent FBTC. FDG uptake is increased in the deep cerebellar nuclei and cerebellar vermis as part of a broader autonomic and cardiorespiratory network. In relation to volume loss and changes in connectivity, this metabolic pattern may reflect maladaptive network activity or compensatory activation in regions involved in autonomic and respiratory recovery.

Altered FC between the posterior vermis and Crus I/II reflects potentially maladaptive changes in cerebellar networks which we hypothesize to be involved in seizure generalization or peri‐ictal cardiorespiratory recovery. The change in connectivity is unlikely to be a consequence of chronic TLE, chronic seizure burden, or temporal lobe pathology per se, as it was not present when those with inactive FBTC were compared with controls, despite the active and inactive FBTC groups having comparable epilepsy duration, focal seizure frequency, and likelihood of lesional MRI. Furthermore, many patients in the inactive FBTC group had a remote history of FBTC, supporting the interpretation that recent FBTC occurrence, rather than lifetime FBTC history alone, is most closely associated with abnormal cerebellar connectivity.

Differences in connectivity between the posterior vermis and ipsilateral thalamic nuclei (specifically, the mediodorsal and pulvinar nuclei) and between the flocculonodular lobe and mesial temporal structures (including the hippocampus, parahippocampus, and amygdala) were observed in both epilepsy groups compared to controls but did not distinguish between active and inactive FBTC patients. This pattern suggests that these changes represent effects of chronic TLE rather than being specifically related to seizure generalization. The posterior vermis and ipsilateral thalami are known to be seizure propagation pathways in TLE. Intracranial EEG studies have demonstrated ictal activity in the pulvinar thalamus during propagated temporal lobe seizures, 52 structural imaging studies have identified abnormalities in the thalamic nuclei ipsilateral to the epileptogenic temporal lobe, 53 and hyperperfusion in the contralateral cerebellar vermis and ipsilateral basal ganglia are observed on ictal SPECT. 54 The flocculonodular lobe forms part of the extended limbic system known to be critically involved in the pathophysiology of TLE (Figure 3), with connections to the amygdala and hippocampus 55 through the Papez circuit. 57

Limitations of our study include its sample size. While it allowed detection of substantial group‐level differences, larger cohorts might reveal more specific network alterations and permit subgroup analyses to explore the effects of factors such as specific FBTC seizure frequency threshold (e.g. >3/year), medication effects and duration of epilepsy. Although the absolute resting‐state FC values observed in this study may appear modest, this is typical for resting‐state fMRI and the cerebellum, where correlations reflect low‐amplitude spontaneous BOLD fluctuations and interpretation relies on consistent group‐level differences rather than absolute magnitude. While flipping FC matrices to align the epileptogenic zone is a standard methodology to preserve statistical power, it inherently assumes functional symmetry between the left and right hemispheres, and possible residual effects of hemispheric dominance or left–right anatomical differences cannot be excluded. The study's cross‐sectional design does not enable temporal precedence to be established, limiting the interpretation of causality from our findings. Future longitudinal studies would provide conclusive evidence of causality.

Future work should also explore potential clinical implications of our finding of intracerebellar connectivity changes associated with susceptibility to FBTC. These findings raise the possibility that posterior vermis–lateral cerebellar connectivity could serve as a candidate imaging marker associated with susceptibility to FBTC, which may have implications for future SUDEP risk stratification studies. Deep cerebellar nuclei and brainstem regions involved in cardiorespiratory control were not directly parcellated in this current analysis, but our findings suggest that they should be specifically examined in future high‐resolution studies. The relevance of reduced posterior vermis–lateral cerebellar connectivity to neuromodulation remains to be determined. It is possible that disrupted cerebellar network integrity could influence responsiveness to stimulation‐based therapies. Conversely, these structures could be targets to be explored for neuromodulation therapies aimed at remedying changes in cerebellar network activity that predispose to seizure propagation. Animal models of cerebellar stimulation have demonstrated suppression of epileptic discharges and in human trials of deep brain stimulation or repetitive TMS of empirically selected cerebellar targets, reductions in seizure frequency have been observed. 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 The clinical application of cerebellar connectivity to treatment planning or response prediction in neuromodulation requires further prospective validation. Beneficial effects from interventions targeting specific structures identified in our study would provide further evidence supporting their role in modulating susceptibility to FBTC.
