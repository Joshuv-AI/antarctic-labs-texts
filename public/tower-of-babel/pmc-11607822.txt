The relationship between perfectionism and sleep quality in athletes: the mediating role of mental toughness
Authors: Guozhuang Chen, Jiali Qian, Yuhan Qiu, Lei Xu, Kun Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecent research suggested that mental toughness might play a mediating role in the relationship between multidimensional perfectionism and sleep quality. This study sought to examine the associations between multidimensional perfectionism, mental toughness, and sleep quality in young Chinese athletes, with a particular focus on investigating the potential mediating role of mental toughness in the relationship between multidimensional perfectionism and sleep quality. A total of 208 Chinese athletes (129 females and 79 males) aged from 15 to 29 (Mage = 19.9, SD = 2.52) were included. Structural equation modeling (SEM) was used to analyze the potential mediating effect. The results revealed that perfectionism striving was not associated with sleep quality, whereas perfectionism concerning emerged as a negative predictor of sleep quality. Mental toughness could positively predict sleep quality. The mediation analysis demonstrated that mental toughness partially mediated the relationship between perfectionism concerning and sleep quality. This study revealed that perfectionism concerning can reduce sleep quality, and mental toughness played a partial mediating role in the relationship between perfectionism concerning and sleep quality among Chinese young athletes. These findings suggest that enhancing athletes’ mental toughness and reducing perfectionism concerning may be effective strategies for improving sleep quality.Keywords: Athletes personality, Perfectionism, Mental toughness, Health problem

Introduction

In recent decades, sleep quality has garnered increasing attention, as poor sleep quality has been identified as a significant contributing factor to various health problems [1]. Sleep quality is a comprehensive assessment index that includes both sleep process and sleep effect [2]. Its evaluation should take into account both the objective sleep situation and the individual’s subjective perception. Sleep quality is undeniably critical for athletes who face substantial energy demands and training pressures, as it facilitates recovery and optimizes performance in subsequent training sessions [3, 4]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that high-quality sleep is crucial for athletes, as it helps reduce the risk of sports injuries, enhances psychological well-being, and boosts competitive performance [5]. However, although some studies have proposed corresponding strategies for identifying the reasons for poor sleep quality [6–8], sleep problems still exist and require continuous attention in athletes [9, 10].

According to the model linking personality to health problems, there is a direct relationship between personality and personal health problems, and personality can enable individuals to maintain certain specific habits that can affect their state of health [11, 12]. As one of the significant personality traits, perfectionism has raised wide attention in influencing adolescents’ health and has been shown to influence one’s sleep quality [13–15]. Perfectionism is defined as a multidimensional personality trait characterized by setting excessively high standards for oneself and engaging in overblown self-criticism when those standards are not met [16]. Although the classification of multidimensional perfectionism varies [17, 18], one study divided perfectionism into perfectionism striving and perfectionism concerning, which has received support from many scholars [19–21]. Perfectionism striving refers to setting unrealistic standards in the pursuit of perfection and has been shown to have more positive effects on daily life [19]. Perfectionism concerning refers to a person’s preoccupation with making mistakes and not achieving perfection, which has been shown to have more negative effects [19].

In recent years, numerous studies have shown a significant relationship between athletes’ level of perfectionism and their mental health status (such as depression [22], burnout [23]) and athletic performance [24]. Athletes with high perfectionism striving and low perfectionism concerning tend to focus on the competition itself, keeping up the effort when things get tough and staying optimistic when things fail, leading to better performance [24]. While athletes with low perfectionism striving and high perfectionism concerning tend to be concerned of failure when facing difficulties, thus can’t focus on the competition itself, and generate negative emotions when losing to rivals, leading to worse performance [24]. It’s obvious that perfectionism striving tends to keep athletes in a good frame of mind and pursuits in the face of adversity, while perfectionism concerning can bring about bad feelings. Moreover, definite but inconsistent relationships between multidimensional perfectionism and sleep quality were found [15, 25]. Notably, in China, most professional athletes specialize in just this one job and may face the problem of post-retirement employment; they need to improve their living standards by getting good performances in competitive games [26]. Additionally, as expressed in the Chinese proverb, “There is no best, only better.” The pursuit of excellence is part of traditional Chinese culture. Therefore, to win in competition, Chinese athletes may be more perfectionist in training and competition, and their sleep quality may be more affected by perfectionism. However, current research on the relationship between perfectionism and sleep quality has only focused on the general population. Studies focusing on athlete groups, especially Chinese athletes are scarce. As a result, this study aims to further investigate the relationship between perfectionism and sleep quality within a sample of Chinese athletes.

In the past decades, numerous studies have examined the mechanisms through which multidimensional perfectionism influences sleep quality. For example, some research suggested that perceived stress and repetitive negative thinking may mediate the link between multidimensional perfectionism and sleep quality [18, 25], but the role of mental toughness in this relationship has not been proven. Mental toughness is a psychological construct associated with success in competitive sports [27]. Some scholars note that mental toughness probably is one of the most frequently used but least understood terms in applied psychology [28]. Although the past decades have evidenced increasing attention to mental toughness in academic research, there still remains disagreement about its conception and dimensionality. In the past years, numerous scholars have proposed definitions of mental toughness grounded on empirical research and theoretical analysis [29–32]. Some scholars conceptualize mental toughness as a trait [33], while others view it as a state [32]. Earlier definitions emphasized its role in overcoming opponents, whereas more recent definitions highlight its relevance to goal achievement [34]. This study adopted Gucciardi’s concept of mental toughness, as he summarized and integrated most of the characteristics of the previous definitions from a comprehensive perspective [27]. Gucciardi claims that mental toughness refers to the personal capacity to produce consistently high levels of subjective (e.g., personal goals or strivings) or objective performance (e.g., sales, race time) despite everyday challenges and stressors as well as significant adversities [27], which has been supported by numerous scholars [33, 35, 36]. In addition, unlike other previous studies that propose mental toughness is a multidimensional conceptualization, Gucciardi empirically validated mental toughness as a unidimensional construct and developed a measurement instrument known as the “Mental Toughness Inventory (MTI)” [27]. In comparison to other measurement instruments, such as the “Mental Toughness Questionnaire 48,” the MTI conceptualizes mental toughness as a unidimensional construct, aligning with its theoretical framework. It has demonstrated strong validity across three distinct populations: students, athletes, and workers [27]. It means that MTI may be more suitable for applying measurement for different groups of people compared to other measurements. That is the reason why the MTI is proposed to be used in this study, as the participants in this study may involve different athlete groups. In China, athletes belong to various groups, including those in regular schools, sports schools, and professional provincial or municipal sports teams. Different groups of athletes live in different environments and are nurtured in different ways. Therefore, the MTI scale was chosen for this study to measure mental toughness.

Mental toughness has captured the attention of both coaches and practitioners since it was proposed. Some studies indicate that mental toughness is associated with good performance and mental health in athlete groups [37, 38]. Adam R proposed that athletes with higher levels of mental toughness have more problem-coping strategies and be more optimistic when facing failure [39]. Daniel J. indicates that mental toughness may offer athletes protection from the experience of burnout in competitive and tough training [40]. In addition, the significance of mental toughness to an athlete may not change depending on the type of sport, as some scholars indicated that the type of sport an athlete participated in was not significantly associated with mental toughness level [41]. The level of mental toughness is evidently crucial for athletes, as it enables them to sustain high performance over extended periods, even under significant pressure or in challenging circumstances [42]. According to the model linking personality and health problems, coping ability could play a mediating role in this relationship [11, 12], which may be used to explain the effect of multidimensional perfectionism on sleep quality. It can be hypothesized that athletes with higher levels of mental toughness are better equipped to maintain consistent work and rest routines, manage the negative emotions arising from training and competitions, and mitigate pre-sleep negative reactions, thereby enhancing sleep quality. Several papers have explicitly described the link between mental toughness and sleep quality. For example, Serge Brand suggested that adolescents reporting greater mental toughness were more likely to have higher sleep quality [43]; Chunxiao Li claimed that greater mental toughness can improve sleep quality by controlling emotional exhaustion [44]. These findings indicate that among athletes, mental toughness is likely a good predictor of sleep quality.

Furthermore, previous studies also indicate that perfectionism striving and perfectionism concerning may have associations with mental toughness. It was found that athletes with lower mental toughness showed higher levels of anxiety and concerns about making mistakes and negative feedback [45], a pattern closely resembling the characteristics associated with perfectionism concerning. Additionally, we can observe similarities between the meanings of striving for perfection and mental toughness. Perfectionism striving refers to individuals who set high standards of behavior for themselves and put all their efforts to achieve their goals, while mental toughness means performing well under pressure, either subjectively or objectively [19, 42]. From this, we can deduce that individuals’ multidimensional perfectionism may be related to their mental toughness level. In recent studies, the relationship between multidimensional perfectionism and mental toughness has been examined in athlete populations. Richard G indicates that having high expectations for oneself is a good indicator of mental toughness, whereas being worried about making mistakes is a poor indicator [46]. Mohebi proposes that athletes who have positive perfectionism have a higher level of mental toughness, whereas athletes who have negative perfectionism have a lower level of mental toughness [47]. These findings indicate that among athletes, perfectionism striving is likely a predictor of strong mental toughness, while perfectionism concerning is likely a predictor of poor mental toughness.

Based on the foregoing discussion, it is evident that perfectionism, mental toughness, and sleep quality play a significant role in influencing the performance of professional athletes. Therefore, it is necessary to study the relationship between the three variables in athletes. The purpose of this study was to confirm the relationships among them and provide a new understanding of sleep issues among young athletes. The theoretical framework (Fig. 1) to be tested and three hypotheses in this study were as follows: (1) perfectionism striving positively predicts sleep quality, whereas perfectionism concerning negatively predicts sleep quality in athletes; (2) mental toughness positively predicts sleep quality in athletes; and (3) mental toughness mediates the relationship between multidimensional perfectionism and sleep quality in athletes.

Materials and methods

Participant

After informed consent was obtained from all participants, 227 questionnaires were distributed randomly in Jiangsu Province and Shanghai city. A total of 208 valid questionnaires were returned, and the rate of valid questionnaires was 91.6%. Nineteen questionnaires were excluded as they did not meet the inclusion criteria or satisfied the exclusion criteria (specific criteria detailed below). There are 129 (62.0%) females and 79 (38.0%) males in the 208 participants. Among them, 47 (22.6%) were team sports athletes, 65 (31.3%) were individual sports athletes, and 96 (46.1%) were both team and individual sports athletes. The participants were 15 to 29 years old, with an average age of 19.9 years (SD = 2.52). According to the Athletes Technical Classification Standard, 51 (24.5%) were second class athletes, 116 (55.8%) were first class athletes, and 41 (19.7%) were national-class elite and international-class elite athletes. The Athletes Technical Classification Standard is issued by the China General Administration of Sport to provide corresponding standard classifications across sports. For example, in basketball, if an athlete achieves the 4th to 1st position in the National Youth Games, he or she is awarded second-class athlete status. If an athlete achieves the 4th to 1st position in the National Games, he or she is awarded first-class athlete status. If an athlete achieves the 12th to 1st position as a core player in the Intercontinental Competition (e.g., Asian Basketball Championships), he or she is awarded national-class elite athlete status. If an athlete achieves the 16th to 1st position as a core player in international competitions (e.g., Olympic games), he or she is awarded international-class elite athlete status.

Measurement

Multidimensional perfectionism

Multidimensional perfectionism was measured by four subscales encompassing a total of 25 items, namely “Striving for Perfection” (5 items, such as “I strive to be as perfect as possible”), “Negative Reactions to Imperfection” (5 items, such as “I feel extremely stressed if everything does not go perfectly”), “Personal Standards” (7 items, such as “I have extremely high goals for myself in my sport”), and “Concern Over Mistakes” (8 items, such as “People will probably think less of me if I make mistakes in competition”). The “Personal Standards” and “Concern Over Mistakes” subscales were taken from the Sport Multidimensional Perfectionism Scale-2 (SMPS-2) revised by Gotwals et al. [48]. The two subscales of “Striving for Perfection” and “Negative Reactions to Imperfection” were taken from the Multidimensional Inventory of Perfectionism in Sport (MIPS) revised by Madigan [49]. Perfectionism striving was represented by “Striving for Perfection” and “Personal Standards”. Perfectionism concerning was represented by “Negative Reactions to Imperfection” and “Concern Over Mistakes”. The scales used a 5-point Likert-type scale, ranging from “1 = very unconforming” to “5 = very conforming”. The questionnaire has been translated into a Chinese version, and the result showed that the Chinese version of the scale had good reliability and validity [50]. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficients of the four subscales employed in this study were between 0.70 and 0.80. The reliability coefficient of the perfectionism striving dimensions was 0.81, and the reliability coefficient of the perfectionism concerning dimension was 0.84.

Mental toughness

The MTI scale adapted by Gucciardi was used to assess mental toughness, which has been proven to have good validity by previous studies [27, 51]. It comprises a total of 8 items, such as “I believe I have the ability to achieve my goals.” Using a 7-point Likert scale, the responses ranged from “1” (completely disagree) to “7” (completely agree). The higher the score is, the greater the mental toughness. This study utilized the Chinese version of this scale, which has good validity [52]. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the scale in this study was 0.92.

Sleep quality

Sleep quality was measured by the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), which consists of 18 items in seven dimensions, including subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, habitual sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, use of sleep medications, and daytime dysfunction (each with a score of 0–3) [2]. An example of items on the PSQI scale is as follows: “Have you had difficulty actively accomplishing things in the past month?”. The total score is the sum of all items. The global PSQI score was 0–21 points, and scores > 5 points indicated “poor sleep quality”. The validity of this scale has been proven to be good by previous studies [53], and the Chinese version of this scale also has a good validity [54]. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the scale in this study was 0.65.

Procedures and statistical analysis

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee at the author’s university. The questionnaires were distributed online using a two-dimensional barcode system provided by “Wenjuanxing” and “WeChat,” a method recognized as a scientifically valid approach to data collection [34]. On the first pages of our e-questionnaire, informed consent was obtained to notify the participants of the research nature, which is confidential and anonymous. After choosing the “I agree” button, the participants were allowed to answer the formal questionnaire. The questionnaire contained a total of 58 questions, and seven questions were used to investigate the demographic variables (such as gender, age, athlete class, and so on) of the participants. Notably, in the gender survey, our question options include a third option in addition to male and female. It took approximately two and a half minutes to 10 min to complete all the items. After collecting the questionnaires, we excluded data that did not meet the inclusion criteria to ensure a better fit of the data to the research question. The exclusion criteria are as follows: incomplete information or too little time to fill out the questionnaire. The inclusion criteria are as follows: athletes rated at Level II and above; athletes who haven’t experienced a serious shock in life within one month; and athletes’ normal participation in training within the previous two months.

SPSS 23.0 (IBM Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) and AMOS 20.0 (IBM Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) were used as the data analysis software. First, Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was used to evaluate the reliability of each scale. Second, a description of each variable was provided to portray the profiles of each variable. Third, Pearson correlation was used to analyze the relationships among the variables. Finally, SEM was conducted to evaluate the mediating effects of mental toughness in the relationship between multidimensional perfectionism and sleep quality using maximum likelihood estimation.

Testing of the mediation model

To control for potential confounding factors, t-tests and analysis of variance were conducted to examine the differences in demographic variables (gender, age, sport type, athlete class) across perfectionism, mental toughness, and sleep quality. None of these differences were significant (P > 0.05), indicating that demographic variables were unlikely to influence the model.

SEM was used to examine the mediating effects of mental toughness on the relationship between perfectionism concerning and sleep quality. First, a direct effect model without mediating variables was constructed with great model fit (χ2/df = 1.940, RMSEA = 0.067, TLI = 0.877, IFI = 0.914, CFI = 0.911). The standardized coefficient for the path between perfectionism concerning and sleep quality was significant (β = -0.359, P < 0.01). Second, a mediating effect model containing mental toughness was constructed with good model fit (χ2/df = 1.793, RMSEA = 0.062, TLI = 0.884, IFI = 0.918, CFI = 0.915). The standardized coefficients for all paths are shown in Fig. 2, and the effect of each path is shown in Table 3. The path from perfectionism concerning to mental toughness is significant. With the addition of mental toughness, the indirect effect of perfectionism concerning on sleep quality becomes significant, and the direct effect is also significantly enhanced, indicating the existence of a mediating effect of mental toughness. As the total effect without the mediator variable is significant, this mediating effect is considered a partial mediating effect.

Results of path analysis

According to the results of the path analyses (Fig. 2; Table 3), perfectionism concerning had negative and direct effects on sleep quality, whereas perfectionism striving had no relationship with sleep quality. Therefore, hypothesis one was only partially supported. Mental toughness has positive and direct effects on sleep quality, and hypothesis two has been completely supported. Perfectionism concerning had an indirect effect on sleep quality through mental toughness, but the perfectionism striving has no significant relationship with sleep quality. As a result, hypothesis three has not been completely supported.

Discussion

Overall, for the first time, we confirmed the relationship between multidimensional perfectionism and sleep quality among young athletes and examined the mediating role of mental toughness between perfectionism concerning and sleep quality.

The results indicate that not both dimensions of perfectionism were associated with sleep quality. On one hand, perfectionism concerning was found to negatively predict sleep quality among athletes, largely supporting previous research findings [15]. This suggests that athletes with high levels of perfectionism concerning are more likely to experience poor sleep quality. On the other hand, perfectionism striving has no significant relationship with sleep quality among young athletes. This finding is not entirely consistent with previous studies, as few studies have found a positive correlation between perfectionism striving and sleep quality [25]. This discrepancy may likely be attributed to differences in measurement tools and participant characteristics. Athletes may have developed higher standards for themselves over the years compared to the general population, allowing them to more easily manage such expectations. As a result, their sleep quality may be less affected than that of the general population when confronted with training goals that exceed their current performance levels. While perfectionism concerning refers to perpetually worrying about making mistakes and fearing failure, which results in negative emotions and nonadaptive reactions. Perfectionism striving refers to setting high standards of behavior and having a high level of proactivity and adaptability [19]. Studies have indicated that perfectionism concerning is positively correlated with fear of failure, depression, competitive anxiety, training distress, and insomnia among athletes [19, 55, 56], which can contribute to maladaptive behaviors. Therefore, the extreme fear of failure and other negative feelings that might affect the emotional state and interfere with a normal sleep cycle, posing a risk for athletes who have a high level of perfectionism concerning. From this perspective, it is important to assess perfectionism within athlete populations, and coaches should be particularly attentive to athletes with high levels of perfectionism concerning. To mitigate the adverse effects of perfectionism concerning on athletes’ sleep, coaches should strengthen athletes’ concentration in competitions, deal with athletes’ post-competition negative emotions timely, and give more stress coping strategies to athletes. For example, when athletes with high levels of perfectionism concerning experience adverse emotions such as anxiety, coaches can organize progressive relaxation training for them [57].

This study also revealed that mental toughness can positively predict sleep quality, which is consistent with the results of previous studies [44, 58, 59]. Therefore, athletes who have a greater degree of mental toughness are less likely to experience poor sleep quality. Teresa observed that individuals with strong mental toughness generally exhibit better sleep habits than those with poor mental toughness [60]. They are also more adept at managing daytime challenges and regulating their mental state [60]. Brand suggested that higher levels of mental toughness are significantly associated with shorter sleep onset latency, fewer awakenings after sleep onset, longer sleep duration, reduced perceived stress, and fewer depressive symptoms, all of which directly and indirectly contribute to improved sleep quality [59]. Furthermore, as dysfunctional thoughts and nonadaptive behaviors contradict the high levels of self-control implied by high levels of mental toughness, mental toughness may influence sleep positively via reduced stress, reduced hyperarousal, and reduced dysfunctional thoughts [43]. Their conclusions can help us understand our results. Athletes with greater mental toughness have better mental regulation ability and sleep habits, which can provide them with a good state before falling asleep and ensure adequate sleep duration. We also found that perfectionism concerning can negatively predict mental toughness, which is consistent with the findings of previous studies [46]. Some studies indicate that mental toughness is always associated with challenging goal expectations and high standards of behavior [45, 61], similar to perfectionism striving. In contrast, perfectionism concerning leads to an excessive fear of failure and overestimates the difficulty of the task, thus reducing individuals’ ability to persist in the task [46]. As a result, athletes who set high personal standards for perfection tend to remain calm and focused in stressful situations. In contrast, athletes’ reactions to imperfection and concerns about making mistakes can undermine their mental toughness.

With respect to indirect effects, we found that mental toughness can play a partially mediating role in the relationship between perfectionism concerning and sleep quality in young athletes. This finding is consistent with previous studies. Some research indicates that mental toughness is more like the ability to not be affected by negative thoughts and persevering in the face of hardship. Athletes with higher levels of mental toughness are good at quickly disengaging from negative mental states, thus preventing them from having a negative impact on the athletes themselves [61–63]. According to the model linking personality and health problems, coping strategies can mediate the relationship between personality and health problems [11, 12], which can help us explain the mediating effect of mental toughness in the relationship between perfectionism concerning and sleep quality. When athletes’ perfectionism concerning increases, the resulting negative reactions can lead to an abnormal state of mind and body. In this state, their original mental toughness level is significantly reduced, which is not conducive to creating good sleep prerequisites and thus leads to a significant decrease in sleep quality. However, for young athletes, a high level of mental toughness can improve their ability to cope with the negative thoughts, effectively regulate their mental state, and maintain good sleep habits. Both of them are beneficial for athletes’ sleep duration and habitual sleep efficiency.

As a result, coaches and other relevant personnel can improve the level of athletes’ mental toughness to alleviate the problem of poor sleep quality caused by perfectionism concerning. A review study indicates that the development of mental toughness in athletes is influenced by environmental factors, with parents and coaches playing a crucial role in fostering this trait [64]. In order to strengthen the mental toughness of their athletes, coaches can create a relaxed training environment, set appropriate training goals, and build rapport with athletes [64]. Furthermore, the effectiveness of several training programs designed to enhance athletes’ mental toughness has been confirmed in recent years. Ajilchi proposed that the “mindfulness training model” is better for mental toughness improvement than “controls training” among basketball players [65]. Connaughton suggested that athletes’ mental toughness could be strengthened by continually inspiring them to aspire to success [66]. Gucciardi proposed a model of psychological skills interventions to enhance mental toughness in athletes [67]. Coaches can draw on these points to enhance their athletes’ mental toughness.

In summary, this study enriches the theoretical discourse on perfectionism, mental toughness, and sleep quality, meanwhile providing practical insights for intervention strategies. The theoretical significance of this study is as follows: First, the relationship between multidimensional perfectionism and sleep quality has been further demonstrated in athlete groups. Notably, all of the athletes we included had maintained regular training in the month prior to the survey. This allows our findings to better reveal the characteristics of the athlete population in terms of multidimensional perfectionism, mental toughness, sleep quality, and their interrelationships. Second, this is the first study to test whether mental toughness mediates the relationship between multidimensional perfectionism and sleep quality in athlete groups. To date, only one other study has examined the mediating role of mental toughness in the perfectionism-sleep quality relationship [68]. However, the study was not initiated on the basis of some theoretical derivations, and it only used the total perfectionism score as the predictor variable. This approach fails to explore the role of different dimensions of perfectionism in the study of relationships. As a result, it does not reveal the role of different dimensions of perfectionism in this relationship. Our study effectively addressed this issue by employing a theoretical model of personality-health problems. This approach not only confirms the relationship within athlete populations but also enhances the understanding of the interplay between multidimensional perfectionism, mental toughness, and sleep quality. In addition, the practical significance of this study is as follows: This study reveals how perfectionism influences sleep quality through mental toughness in athlete groups and provides coaches with intervention strategies for developing athletes’ mental toughness and coping with perfectionism concerning. These strategies are beneficial for safeguarding athletes’ mental health, enhancing their sleep quality, and improving their performance in the competitions.

Several limitations to this study need to be acknowledged. First, this study was a cross-sectional study, which may not reveal causal relationships between variables well. Relevant longitudinal studies can be conducted in the future. Secondly, as noted earlier, mental toughness mediates the relationship between perfectionism concerning and sleep quality through two pathways: the maintenance of healthy sleep habits and the effective management of negative emotions. However, this study did not quantify the specific impact of mental toughness within each pathway, which warrants further investigation in future research. Finally, this study treated mental toughness as a unidimensional construct, and its measurement was also conducted using a unidimensional approach. However, a number of studies have considered mental toughness to be a multidimensional variable. As a result, when a mental toughness instrument measuring multiple dimensions is used, the relationship between perfectionism, mental toughness, and sleep quality may be different. Future research could conduct further research in this direction.

Conclusions

The findings indicated that perfectionism concerning could have a negative impact on athletes’ sleep quality, and mental toughness played a partial mediating role in the relationship between perfectionism concerning and sleep quality. However, there was no significant relationship between perfectionism striving and sleep quality in the athletes. The results suggested that enhancing athletes’ mental toughness and reducing perfectionism concerning may be effective strategies for improving their sleep quality.
