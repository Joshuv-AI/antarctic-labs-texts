Anticoagulation versus no anticoagulation after intracranial hemorrhage in patients with atrial fibrillation: an updated systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Mohammadreza Babaei, Parisa Fallahtafti, Sina Rashedi, Amir Ghaffari Jolfayi, Zahra Hosseini, Kaveh Hosseini, Masih Tajdini, Hamidreza Soleimani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients with atrial fibrillation (AF) and prior intracranial hemorrhage (ICH) present a therapeutic dilemma, where stroke prevention benefits of anticoagulation must be weighed against recurrent hemorrhage risks. Existing evidence remains inconclusive due to limited sample sizes. This updated meta-analysis synthesizes data from recent randomized controlled trials (RCTs) to determine whether anticoagulation provides net clinical benefit in this population.MethodsWe searched PubMed, Embase, and ClinicalTrials.gov until May 2025 for RCTs comparing anticoagulation versus no anticoagulation in AF patients with prior ICH. The primary efficacy and safety outcomes were ischemic stroke and recurrent ICH, respectively, with secondary outcomes of major adverse ischemic events (composite of ischemic stroke, systemic embolism, pulmonary embolism, and myocardial infarction), all-cause mortality, and major bleeding. Pooled effect estimates (hazard ratios or risk ratios) with confidence intervals. Number needed to treat (NNT) and number needed to harm (NNH) were also estimated to provide clinical context.ResultsFive RCTs with 733 patients (371 in the anticoagulation group and 362 in the no anticoagulation group) were included. Anticoagulation therapy demonstrated an 80% reduction in ischemic stroke risk (RR 0.20, 95% CI 0.06–0.71; NNT = 9) and a 64% reduction in major adverse ischemic events (HR 0.36, 95% CI 0.20–0.64, NNT = 11) while showing a nearly four-fold increased risk of intracranial hemorrhage (HR 3.69, 95% CI 1.54–8.84; NNH = 24) and a significantly increased risk of major bleeding (HR 2.40, 95% CI 1.20–4.81; NNH = 23). No significant difference was observed for all-cause mortality between groups (HR 1.17, 95% CI 0.80–1.72; NNH = 177).ConclusionAnticoagulation in AF patients with prior ICH shows clinically meaningful reductions in thromboembolic events but significantly increases bleeding risks in this population. Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is a common cardiac arrhythmia associated with significant thromboembolic risk, such as ischemic stroke [1]. It often requires anticoagulation therapy based on individual risk stratification to prevent clot formation [1]. However, the use of anticoagulants in specific patient subgroups, particularly those with a history of intracranial hemorrhage (ICH), remains controversial [2–4]. Balancing the benefits of stroke prevention against the potential risk of recurrent bleeding in ICH survivors presents a significant clinical challenge, as evidence guiding this decision is limited and inconclusive. In this context, real-world evidence may provide valuable insights that complement clinical trial data and support the development of clinical guidelines.

While several studies have examined anticoagulation outcomes in AF patients with prior ICH, inconsistent findings persist due to variations in study design, populations, and follow-up durations [5, 6]. The COCROACH individual participant data (IPD) meta-analysis pooled data from randomized controlled trials (RCTs) but yielded inconclusive results due to limited sample size [7]. Although oral anticoagulation significantly reduced ischemic cardiovascular events (HR 0.27, 95% CI 0.13–0.56), it did not show a clear reduction in overall stroke, mortality, or hemorrhagic risk. These equivocal findings highlight the critical need for larger, more definitive studies to inform clinical practice in this high-risk population.

This updated meta-analysis aims to provide the most comprehensive evaluation to date of anticoagulation versus no anticoagulation in patients with atrial fibrillation and prior intracranial hemorrhage. It includes the largest number of RCTs available, enhancing the strength and generalizability of the findings. By reporting number needed to treat (NNT) and number needed to harm (NNH), alongside conventional effect estimates, it adds practical clinical value. The application of the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) framework further strengthens the reliability of the conclusions. Together, these features offer clearer, evidence-based guidance for managing this high-risk population.

Methods

Protocol and registration

This systematic review and meta-analysis was reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guideline [8]. The study protocol was registered prospectively in PROSPERO (CRD420251017350). As only published aggregate data were analyzed, no ethics approval was required.

Search strategy

On May 23, 2025, a comprehensive search was performed across PubMed, Embase, and ClinicalTrials.gov to identify RCTs evaluating the efficacy and safety of anticoagulation compared to no anticoagulation in patients with AF following ICH (Table S1). The search was not limited by language or publication date. To ensure thoroughness, the reference lists of included RCTs and other relevant studies were manually reviewed to identify additional eligible studies.

Eligibility criteria and study selection

ectionWe included studies that specifically focused on adult patients (aged 18 years or older) with AF and a history of spontaneous ICH. Eligible studies compared the use of oral anticoagulants (such as direct oral anticoagulants [DOACs] and vitamin K antagonists [e.g., warfarin]) versus no anticoagulation (with or without antiplatelet therapy). Studies were excluded if they involved patients with ICH caused by trauma, vascular malformations, or hemorrhagic transformation of ischemic stroke. Studies without a control group, case reports, case series, retrospective and prospective cohort studies, and review articles were excluded. Unpublished data (e.g., data from previous individual patient data meta-analysis) were included when sufficient details were available. Two investigators (M.B. and S.R.) independently screened the titles, abstracts, and full texts of the identified studies to assess their eligibility. Any disagreements between the investigators were resolved through discussion and consensus.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes of interest were ischemic stroke and recurrent ICH. Secondary outcomes included a composite of major adverse ischemic events (encompassing ischemic stroke, systemic embolism, pulmonary embolism, and myocardial infarction), all-cause mortality, and major bleeding events (both intracranial and extracranial). ICH and major bleeding events were considered safety outcomes, while the rest were considered efficacy outcomes. These outcomes were selected to evaluate the efficacy and safety of oral anticoagulants (DOACs and warfarin) compared to no anticoagulation (with or without antiplatelet therapy) in adults with atrial fibrillation and a history of spontaneous ICH.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Two independent reviewers (M.B. and S.R.) extracted data using a standardized form. Key demographic and clinical details, such as age, sex, past medical history (including hypertension, diabetes, history of previous ischemic stroke, transient ischemic attack, ICH, myocardial infarction) relevant scores (e.g., CHA2DS2-VASc, HAS-BLED, and Modified Rankin Scale), and anticoagulation use before ICH and location of ICH (lobar versus non-lobar) were recorded. Two independent reviewers (M.B. and S.R.) assessed methodological quality of the included RCTs using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool, version 2 (RoB-2) [9]. This tool evaluates RCTs across five domains: randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of outcomes, and selective reporting. Based on these domains, an overall risk of bias was determined for each study. To evaluate the strength of evidence for each outcome, the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) system was employed [10]. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus.

Statistical analysis

For time-to-event outcomes, we primarily extracted hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and performed meta-analysis using a random-effects model with the inverse-variance method. When HRs were unavailable, we calculated risk ratios (RRs) from the total number of events and population in each treatment arm. Between-study heterogeneity was estimated using the DerSimonian-Laird estimator. For continuous outcomes, we computed pooled mean differences (MDs) with 95% CIs using the inverse-variance method. When means and standard deviations were not reported, we estimated them from available statistics (e.g., sample size, median, interquartile range) using established methods [11]. Heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q test and quantified with the I² statistic, with values interpreted as: <25% (low), 25–50% (moderate), and > 50% (high). Number needed to treat (NNT) and number needed to harm (NNH) were calculated as the reciprocal of the absolute risk difference, derived from pooled event rates. All analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.1) and “meta” package, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.

Sensitivity analysis

We performed sensitivity analyses by sequentially excluding individual trials (leave-one-out method).

Results

After screening 1,193 records, 12 studies were assessed for eligibility. Finally, five RCTs were included in the analysis (Figure S1)—three from original articles [2–4] and two from individual patient data from the COCRAOCH study [7]. These trials enrolled a total of 733 patients with non-traumatic ICH. Of these, 371 patients (50.6%) were randomized to receive anticoagulation therapy while 362 patients (49.4%) were assigned to the no anticoagulation group. All studies had a follow-up duration of at least six months, ranging from 0.7 to 3.1 years. The median age of participants across the five trials was 78.1 (77.1–79.1) and 78.8 (77.8–79.8) in the anticoagulation versus no anticoagulation group, respectively, with 33.7% (247/733) being female. Due to incomplete data availability for baseline characteristics in the NOACs for Stroke Prevention in Patients with Atrial Fibrillation and Previous ICH (NASPAF-AF) and Edoxaban Low-Dose for Elder Care Atrial Fibrillation Patients (ELDERCARE-AF) trials, supplementary data from the COCROACH IPD meta-analysis were utilized to ensure comprehensive analysis. Methodological quality of the included RCTs

The assessment of the risk of bias for the NASPAF-ICH and ELDERCARE-AF trials was not feasible, as their data were extracted from the COCROACH study. The remaining included RCTs were open-label; however, outcome assessors were blinded to the randomization process. Given that our primary outcomes (ischemic stroke and ICH) were objective and not likely influenced by participant or investigator awareness of treatment allocation, the lack of blinding was not considered to significantly impact the reported results. Overall, all trials were judged to have a low risk of bias (Figure S2 and Table S2).

Ischemic stroke

The NASPAF-ICH and ELDERCARE-AF trials reported no ischemic stroke events in anticoagulated patients, while the other three trials consistently favored anticoagulation. Pooled analysis showed an 80% relative risk reduction (RR 0.20, 95% CI 0.06–0.71, p = 0.01) in patients receiving anticoagulation, though with moderate heterogeneity (I²=61%) (Fig. 1, Figure S3). Pooled results yielded a number needed to treat (NNT) of 9 (Table S3).

Intracranial hemorrhage

The NASPAF-ICH and ELDERCARE-AF trials observed no recurrent hemorrhages in either treatment group. In contrast, pooled analysis of the remaining three trials demonstrated a nearly fourfold increased risk with anticoagulation (HR 3.69, 95% CI 1.54–8.84, p < 0.01), with no heterogeneity (I²=0%) (Fig. 2, Figure S4). A number needed to harm (NNH) of 24 was derived from the pooled data (Table S3).

Major adverse ischemic event

Pooled analysis of five trials revealed a 64% risk reduction (HR 0.36, 95% CI 0.20–0.64, p < 0.01) with minimal heterogeneity (I²=16%). Notably, PRESTIGE-AF reported only ischemic stroke/systemic embolism due to data limitations, while other trials included pulmonary embolism and myocardial infarction in this composite outcome. Despite this definitional variation, the direction and magnitude of benefit remained robust across all studies (Fig. 3, Figure S5). The estimated NNT was 11 (Table S3).

All-cause mortality

Pooled analysis of five trials demonstrated no significant mortality difference between groups (HR 1.17, 95% CI 0.80–1.72, p = 0.41), with low heterogeneity (I²=29%). While SoSTART suggested increased risk with anticoagulation (HR 2.25, 95% CI 1.09–4.66), other trials showed neutral effects (Fig. 4, Figure S6). The NNH was estimated at 177 (Table S3).

Major bleeding

Pooled analysis of five trials demonstrated a 2.4-fold increased risk of major bleeding with anticoagulation (HR 2.40, 95% CI 1.20–4.81, p = 0.01), with minimal heterogeneity (I²=9%). While PRESTIGE-AF showed the strongest association (HR 4.72, 95% CI 1.74–12.80), other trials exhibited non-significant trends. (Fig. 5, Figure S7). The estimated NNH was 23.

GRADE certainty assessment

GRADE certainty ratings (Table S4) demonstrated high-certainty evidence for major adverse ischemic events (precise estimates, no limitations), moderate certainty for ICH and all-cause mortality (downgraded for imprecision), and low certainty for ischemic stroke (wide confidence interval) and major bleeding (wide confidence interval and suspected bias).

Sensitivity analyses

The leave-one-out sensitivity analysis demonstrated consistent protection against ischemic stroke across all exclusions (OR range: 0.12–0.29), though precision diminished when excluding PRESTIGE-AF or SoSTART. For ICH, risk estimates varied substantially, ranging from HR 2.64 (0.92–7.59) after excluding PRESTIGE-AF to HR 6.21 (1.74–22.09) after excluding SoSTART. Major adverse ischemic events showed particularly robust effects, maintaining statistical significance in all scenarios (HR range: 0.26–0.46), with the most pronounced benefit observed when excluding PRESTIGE-AF. Major bleeding risks remained stable (HR range: 1.70–2.75), while all-cause mortality estimates showed negligible variation across exclusions (HR range: 1.02–1.34). These findings suggest high confidence in the ischemic and major adverse ischemic event results, while the ICH outcomes require cautious interpretation due to their sensitivity to specific trial exclusions (Table S5).

Discussion

Our meta-analysis illustrates the complex risk-benefit profile of resuming anticoagulation after ICH in patients with AF. Anticoagulation significantly reduced the risk of ischemic stroke by 80% and major adverse ischemic events by 64%, corresponding to NNT of 9 and 11, respectively, indicating substantial absolute benefit in preventing thromboembolic complications. However, this benefit was offset by a nearly fourfold increased risk of recurrent ICH and a 2.4-fold rise in major bleeding, with NNH of 24 and 23, respectively. This trade-off between ischemic protection and hemorrhagic risk represents the core dilemma faced by clinicians managing this high-risk population [12].

The pathophysiology underlying ICH in AF patients is multifactorial, often involving hypertension, cerebral small vessel disease, and cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA) [13]. CAA, a leading cause of spontaneous ICH in the elderly, is characterized by β-amyloid deposition in cortical and leptomeningeal vessels, which progressively weakens the vessel walls and can lead to microaneurysm formation and hemorrhage [13]. Patients with both AF and CAA present a complex therapeutic challenge. While anticoagulation reduces the risk of AF-related ischemic events, it significantly increases the risk of ICH in those with CAA [14]. Given the limitations of standard risk scores such as CHA₂DS₂-VASc and HAS-BLED in capturing this pathology, clinical decisions in such patients should be guided by careful neurological assessment and brain imaging.

Over time, clinical practice has shifted from vitamin K antagonists (VKAs) to DOACs, which have shown lower risk of ICH [15, 16]. However, even DOACs carry a non-negligible risk of ICH recurrence. Historically, evidence on anticoagulation in AF patients with prior ICH has come mostly from observational studies, mainly on VKAs, showing reduced thromboembolic risk and lower incidence of ischemic stroke or systemic embolism [17]. However, safety remains uncertain, with some studies reporting no increase in recurrent ICH and others noting higher major bleeding [18, 19]. Limitations of these observational data, including confounding and selection bias, highlight the need for randomized trials in this population. In an individual patient-level meta-analysis of four RCTs, included patients with ICH and AF primarily managed with DOACs, with minimal VKA exposure (3% in SoSTART), anticoagulation therapy reduced the risk of ischemic major adverse cardiovascular events, though it did not significantly impact the composite outcome of stroke or cardiovascular death [7].

The pivotal PRESTIGE-AF trial marks a major advancement in this field. PRESTIGE-AF is the first completed phase 3 trial to demonstrate that DOACs significantly reduce the risk of ischemic stroke in survivors of ICH with AF [20]. A recent meta-analysis of three RCTs [21](APACHE-AF, SoSTART, PRESTIGE-AF; N = 623) excluded the COCROACH study, which included two additional trials (NASPAF-ICH and a subanalysis of ELDERCARE-AF). Our updated analysis included all five RCTs (N = 733), providing a more comprehensive evidence base. Unlike the earlier meta-analysis, which found no significant reduction in thromboembolic events, we observed significant reductions in ischemic stroke (RR 0.20) and major adverse ischemic events (HR 0.36), though bleeding risks remained similarly elevated. These findings suggest greater ischemic benefit than previously reported, aligning with observational data reporting lower thromboembolic events following anticoagulation resumption [22, 23].

Current recommendations of American Heart Association/American Stroke Association (AHA/ASA) [24] and the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) [1] guidelines advocate for individualized treatment decisions. Such decisions should consider the location and cause of the index ICH, stroke risk as estimated by CHA₂DS₂-VASc, bleeding propensity, and patient preferences. Another important consideration is the optimal timing of anticoagulation resumption. While baseline characteristics, including age, comorbidities, and CHA2DS2-VASc scores, were comparable across trials included they varied significantly in anticoagulation timing: from a median of 45 days (APACHE-AF) to 104 days (SoSTART) post-ICH. A risk-stratified approach, based on ICH severity and etiology, appears most appropriate. For example, patients with deep hypertensive hemorrhage and high thromboembolic risk may benefit from carefully timed DOAC resumption, typically four or more weeks after the initial event [24]. In the ENRICH-AF trial, which compares edoxaban to medical management in ICH survivors with AF, an interim safety review of 699 patients revealed that 25% had lobar ICH and 5% had isolated convexity subarachnoid hemorrhage. Due to safety concerns—largely related to CAA—the trial’s Data Safety Monitoring Board recommended halting enrollment for these subtypes and discontinuing the study drug in affected participants [25]. Overall, our results support more confident, individualized treatment decisions and help define practical thresholds for resuming or replacing anticoagulation after ICH.

Given the inherent risks of anticoagulation in patients with prior ICH, left atrial appendage occlusion (LAAO) has emerged as a promising alternative [26]. Devices such as the Watchman and Amulet offer a middle-ground strategy that may retain stroke prevention benefits without substantially increasing the risk of recurrent ICH. Growing evidence from randomized trials and large registry studies supports the safety and effectiveness of LAAO in general AF populations. In the OPTION trial (N = 1600) in patients who underwent catheter ablation, LAAO reduced major or clinically relevant bleeding by more than half (8.5% vs. 18.1%; HR: 0.44) compared with oral anticoagulation, while maintaining similar rates of death, stroke, or systemic embolism (5.3% vs. 5.8%) over 36 months [27]. These results suggest that LAAO provides a favorable balance of efficacy and safety, making it a viable option for patients at high risk of recurrent ICH. Several RCTs are currently underway to further address this gap, including the STROKECLOSE (NCT02830152) and A3ICH (NCT03243175).

Several limitations should be noted. The total number of randomized participants remains relatively small, with key outcomes driven by a few large trials, particularly PRESTIGE-AF. Some studies, such as NASPAF-ICH and ELDERCARE-AF, reported zero events, limiting statistical power. Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of ischemic outcomes, but bleeding outcomes were more variable and influenced by individual trials. Heterogeneity was generally low for ischemic endpoints but higher for bleeding, reflecting differences in patient selection, outcome definitions, and trial protocols. Generalizability is limited because high-risk subgroups, including patients with lobar ICH or CAA, were underrepresented, and data stratified by ICH location or timing remain sparse. Additionally, publication bias cannot be excluded. Although our study is limited by the inherent variability among the included clinical trials, its strength lies in the comprehensive analysis of data from five RCTs, making it the most extensive meta-analysis to date compared to recent articles that included only three or four trials [28–30].

In conclusion, this meta-analysis synthesizes the best available randomized evidence on anticoagulation in AF patients after ICH. While anticoagulation confers substantial ischemic protection, this comes at the cost of increased bleeding, especially recurrent ICH. The findings suggest the need for individualized, nuanced decision-making that integrates clinical characteristics, patient values, and emerging trial evidence. Ongoing studies and trials evaluating LAAC will be critical in guiding safer and more effective management strategies. Until such data are available, shared decision-making remains essential in this complex clinical scenario.
