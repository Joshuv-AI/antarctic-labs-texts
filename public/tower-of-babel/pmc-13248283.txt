Reviewer recommendations and editorial outcomes in peer review: a longitudinal analysis of agreement and disagreement across review rounds
Authors: Carlos De las Cuevas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPeer review is often portrayed as a consensus-driven process in which reviewer recommendations largely determine editorial decisions. However, manuscript-level evidence describing how reviewer disagreement unfolds across review rounds—and how it is resolved in routine editorial practice—remains limited.MethodsWe conducted a meta-research analysis of anonymized editorial data from Healthcare (MDPI, Basel, Switzerland; ISSN 2227–9032) (2021–2025), including manuscripts that were ultimately published and manuscripts rejected after external peer review.Reviewer recommendations (accept, minor revision, major revision, reject) were examined at the manuscript level across review rounds. Alignment between reviewer recommendations and editorial outcomes was assessed primarily using the majority recommendation in the final review round, applying a conservative tie-breaking rule (reject > major > minor > accept). Reviewer discordance was defined as the presence of two or more distinct recommendation categories within a review round. Longitudinal trajectories from first to final round were analyzed, with sensitivity analyses using alternative definitions of unfavorable recommendations.The analysis was conducted using anonymized editorial records. The author did not access identifiable reviewer or author information, and the dataset was analyzed in aggregated form to minimize the risk of re-identification.ResultsThe analysis included 12,187 published manuscripts and 3,819 rejected manuscripts, corresponding to 21,497 and 5,189 review-round records, respectively. Reviewer discordance was common in the first round for both published and rejected manuscripts (74.8% and 85.8%). Across rounds, discordance declined markedly among published manuscripts (to 29.3% in the final round) but remained high among rejected manuscripts (71.4%). Overall alignment between the majority recommendation in the final round and the editorial outcome was high (≈88%) but not absolute: 6.3% of published manuscripts had a majority recommendation of rejection in the final round, and 8.0% retained at least one rejection recommendation. Longitudinally, discordance was frequently resolved among published manuscripts (53.1% transitioned from discordant to concordant), whereas it often persisted among rejected manuscripts (61.4% remained discordant).ConclusionsIn routine practice, peer review functions less as a voting mechanism and more as an adjudicative process in which editorial judgment integrates heterogeneous reviewer input. The asymmetric resolution of disagreement across outcomes underscores the central role of editorial decision-making when reviewer recommendations diverge.Keywords: Peer review, Editorial decision-making, Reviewer disagreement, Research integrity, Meta-research, Scholarly publishing

Introduction

Peer review remains central to scholarly quality control, intended to inform editorial decisions through independent expert assessment. In practice, reviewer recommendations frequently diverge, reflecting differences in expertise, evaluative thresholds, and perspectives on evidence and contribution. Empirical work has repeatedly documented limited inter-reviewer agreement across disciplines, suggesting that disagreement is a common feature of peer review rather than an exceptional occurrence [1]. Concerns about the reliability and consequences of peer review—both as a gatekeeping mechanism and as a potential constraint on innovation—have been discussed for decades [2, 3].

Most empirical investigations of peer review rely on surveys, experimental designs, simulations, or aggregate journal metrics [4, 5]. While informative, these approaches often obscure how disagreement evolves across successive review rounds and how it relates to eventual editorial outcomes. As a result, peer review is sometimes implicitly conceptualized as a quasi-consensus process, when in reality editorial decisions may require integrating heterogeneous—and occasionally conflicting—judgments under uncertainty [3, 6]. In parallel, calls for greater transparency and reform have emphasized the need for evidence grounded in real-world editorial workflows and outcomes [7, 8].

Existing studies examining the relationship between reviewer recommendations and editorial decisions have provided valuable snapshots of concordance at particular decision points. Although several studies have examined peer review decision-making, including analyses incorporating rejected manuscripts and longitudinal designs [9–11], relatively few have focused on manuscript-level trajectories of reviewer disagreement across successive review rounds, and fewer still have included both published manuscripts and manuscripts rejected after external peer review. This longitudinal perspective is important because disagreement may not be static: it can converge through revision and re-review, persist despite changes, or even emerge later in the process.

Using anonymized editorial data from Healthcare spanning 2021–2025, the present study addresses two questions: (i) to what extent do reviewer recommendations align with final editorial outcomes in routine editorial practice; and (ii) how does reviewer discordance change from the first to the final review round, and how do these trajectories differ between published and rejected manuscripts? By analyzing recommendations across review rounds and explicitly comparing longitudinal patterns by outcome, we aim to provide an empirically grounded account of how disagreement is handled in real-world peer review and the extent to which editorial outcomes reflect—but do not mechanically replicate—reviewer recommendations [3, 6].

Methods

Data sources and study design

We conducted a retrospective meta-research analysis of anonymized editorial data from Healthcare covering the period 2021–2025. The dataset included manuscripts that underwent external peer review and were subsequently either accepted for publication or rejected following review. Desk-rejected submissions were excluded from the analysis.

All manuscripts included in the study followed the journal’s standard peer-review workflow. Submissions considered suitable for external evaluation were assigned by the handling editor to independent reviewers, who provided structured recommendations (accept, minor revision, major revision, or reject), accompanied by narrative comments. Reviewers did not have access to each other’s reports at the time of submission unless shared by the handling editor in subsequent rounds, in accordance with routine editorial practice.

For manuscripts undergoing revision, subsequent review rounds could involve the same or different reviewers, depending on editorial judgment and reviewer availability. Editorial decisions were made by the handling editor, who considered reviewer recommendations together with the content of the reviews and the authors’ responses. Reviewer recommendations were advisory and not binding, and final decisions rested with the editor.

The dataset therefore reflects editorial decision-making under conditions of formal peer review rather than initial editorial screening. All data were fully anonymized prior to analysis, and results were analyzed and reported in aggregated form to minimize the risk of re-identification and to enhance transparency regarding potential competing interests.

Unit of analysis

The primary unit of analysis was the individual manuscript, identified by a unique manuscript identification number. Reviewer reports were grouped by manuscript and by review round. Each review round corresponded to a discrete editorial decision point following receipt of one or more reviewer reports.

Reviewer recommendations

Reviewer recommendations were classified into four standard categories: accept, minor revision, major revision, and reject. For each manuscript and review round, the following derived variables were constructed:

Majority recommendation, defined as the modal recommendation across reviewers. In the case of ties, a conservative hierarchy was applied (reject > major revision > minor revision > accept).

Review rounds

For longitudinal analyses, two review stages were defined for each manuscript:

Manuscripts could undergo one or multiple rounds of review. The number of review rounds therefore varied across submissions, reflecting routine editorial practice.

Editorial outcome

Final editorial outcome was classified dichotomously as published or rejected. Alignment between reviewer recommendations and editorial outcomes was assessed primarily using the majority recommendation in the final review round.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were descriptive and comparative. We quantified the prevalence of reviewer discordance and rejection signals by review round and editorial outcome, examined alignment between reviewer recommendations and editorial decisions, and analyzed longitudinal transitions in discordance from the first to the final review round.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of findings to alternative operationalizations of unfavorable reviewer recommendations. In addition to the primary definition based on majority recommendation in the final round, analyses were repeated using:

Results were examined for consistency across these alternative specifications. No causal inferences were made.

Results

Dataset characteristics

The final dataset comprised 12,187 published manuscripts and 3,819 rejected manuscripts, corresponding to 21,497 and 5,189 individual review-round records, respectively. Each record represented a distinct round of external peer review for a given manuscript. Because manuscripts could undergo more than one round of review, the number of review-round records exceeded the number of manuscripts in both groups.

Most published manuscripts underwent more than one round of review prior to acceptance, whereas rejected manuscripts more frequently exited the process after fewer rounds. Overall alignment between reviewer recommendations and editorial outcomes was high but not absolute. Among published manuscripts, 93.7% had a final-round majority recommendation of accept, minor revision, or major revision, while 6.3% were published despite a majority recommendation of rejection. In addition, 8.0% of published manuscripts retained at least one rejection recommendation in the final review round.

Among rejected manuscripts, 71.7% had a majority recommendation of rejection in the final round, whereas 28.3% were rejected despite a non-rejection majority recommendation.

These findings indicate that, although reviewer recommendations strongly influence editorial outcomes, they do not determine them mechanically. Editorial decisions occasionally diverged from reviewer consensus in both directions, reflecting the discretionary role of editorial judgment.

In the first review round, discordance was observed in 74.8% of manuscripts that were ultimately published and in 85.8% of manuscripts that were ultimately rejected. However, the evolution of discordance differed markedly between the two groups.

Among published manuscripts, reviewer discordance declined substantially over successive rounds, falling to 29.3% in the final review round. In contrast, discordance remained high among rejected manuscripts, decreasing only modestly to 71.4% in the final round.

These patterns indicate that convergence among reviewers is characteristic of manuscripts that progress toward publication, whereas persistent disagreement is more typical of manuscripts that are ultimately rejected.

Among manuscripts that were ultimately published (n = 12,187), the most common trajectory was resolution of disagreement: 53.1% transitioned from a discordant to a concordant state. A substantial proportion either remained discordant throughout the process (21.7%) or were concordant in both the first and final rounds (17.5%). A smaller fraction (7.7%) shifted from concordance to discordance.

In contrast, rejected manuscripts (n = 3,819) showed a markedly different pattern. Most remained discordant across review rounds (61.4%), while only 24.4% transitioned from discordance to concordance. Transitions from concordance to discordance (10%) and stable concordance (4.2%) were relatively uncommon.

These contrasting trajectories suggest that convergence of reviewer opinion is closely associated with eventual publication, whereas persistent disagreement characterizes manuscripts that do not progress to acceptance.

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses yielded results consistent with the primary findings. The overall patterns of alignment between reviewer recommendations and editorial outcomes, as well as the asymmetric resolution of reviewer discordance across review rounds, remained stable when alternative operational definitions of unfavorable recommendations were applied.

Specifically, analyses based on (i) the presence of at least one rejection recommendation and (ii) binary classifications contrasting rejection versus non-rejection majorities produced results that were qualitatively unchanged, indicating that the findings were robust to reasonable variations in analytical assumptions.

Discussion

Our findings extend prior empirical work [9–11] examining editorial decision-making and reviewer agreement by focusing specifically on the evolution of disagreement across review rounds at the manuscript level. This study provides a manuscript-level, longitudinal analysis of how reviewer recommendations relate to editorial outcomes in routine peer review. As shown in Table 2 and Figs. 1–2, reviewer recommendations were strongly aligned with editorial outcomes overall, yet disagreement was common in early rounds and resolved asymmetrically across outcomes. By examining both published and rejected manuscripts across successive review rounds, it offers a detailed view of how reviewer disagreement emerges, evolves, and is ultimately resolved in editorial practice.

Several findings merit emphasis. First, reviewer disagreement was common, particularly in the initial round of review. This observation is consistent with previous literature showing limited inter-reviewer agreement and substantial variability in evaluative judgments [1, 5]. Rather than representing a failure of the review process, such disagreement appears to be a structural feature of peer review, reflecting heterogeneity in expertise, evaluative criteria, and thresholds for publication [12].

Second, although alignment between reviewer recommendations and editorial outcomes was high overall, it was not absolute. A non-negligible proportion of manuscripts were published despite the presence of rejection recommendations, including cases in which rejection constituted the majority recommendation in the final review round. Conversely, some manuscripts were rejected despite predominantly favorable recommendations. These findings reinforce the notion that peer review does not function as a simple voting mechanism, but rather as an advisory process in which editorial judgment plays a central role [3, 6].

A key contribution of this study lies in its longitudinal perspective. Unlike prior analyses that focus on single decision points or aggregate acceptance rates, the present study examines how disagreement evolves across review rounds. The results demonstrate a clear asymmetry: among manuscripts that were ultimately published, reviewer disagreement tended to diminish over time, whereas among rejected manuscripts, discordance often persisted. This pattern suggests that convergence of reviewer opinion is characteristic of manuscripts progressing toward acceptance, while sustained disagreement may signal unresolved epistemic uncertainty that the editorial process ultimately resolves through rejection.

Importantly, these findings do not imply that reviewers who disagree are incorrect or that disagreement reflects low-quality review. Rather, they highlight the inherently interpretative nature of peer review, particularly in contexts where methodological rigor, novelty, and relevance must be weighed simultaneously. In such cases, editorial decision-making involves integrating heterogeneous assessments rather than aggregating them mechanically. From this perspective, editorial judgment functions as a form of adjudication under uncertainty, rather than as a procedural endpoint determined solely by reviewer consensus.

The present findings also help contextualize ongoing debates about transparency and reform in peer review. Calls for greater openness often assume that reviewer agreement represents an ideal state. However, the observed prevalence and persistence of disagreement suggest that divergence of expert opinion is intrinsic to scholarly evaluation. Recognizing this may help recalibrate expectations of peer review and underscore the importance of editorial responsibility in mediating between competing assessments [7, 8, 13, 14].

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the analysis was conducted within a single journal, which may limit generalizability to other editorial contexts with different review practices. Second, reviewer recommendations were analyzed categorically, without access to the qualitative content of reviews, which may contain nuances not captured by recommendation labels alone. Third, editorial reasoning could not be examined directly and was inferred from outcomes rather than observed decision-making processes. Finally, although sensitivity analyses supported the robustness of the findings, alternative operationalizations might yield different absolute estimates.

Conclusions

Reviewer disagreement is a routine and persistent feature of peer review. While reviewer recommendations strongly influence editorial outcomes, they do not determine them mechanically. Instead, editorial decisions reflect a process of adjudication in which divergent expert judgments are weighed and resolved under conditions of uncertainty. By documenting how disagreement evolves across review rounds and how it differs between published and rejected manuscripts, this study provides empirical support for a more nuanced understanding of peer review as an interpretative and editorially mediated process rather than a purely aggregative one.
