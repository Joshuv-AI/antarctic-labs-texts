Microlocal analysis of Radon transforms over quadric surfaces
Gaik Ambartsoumian † , Raluca Felea ⋄ , Venkateswaran P. Krishnan ♭ , Clifford J. Nolan ♯ and Eric Todd Quinto ∗

Abstract
. We study the microlocal properties of generalized Radon transforms over a family of quadric hypersurfaces whose centers lie on an orientable hypersurface S S . The quadric surfaces we consider are level sets of the quadratic form associated to a symmetric, invertible matrix A A , with real entries. We study the singularities of the right and left projections of the canonical relation associated with these operators and show that they are determined by the signature of the matrix A A and the hypersurface S S . If the matrix is positive/negative definite (i.e., the surface of integration is an ellipsoid) and S S is strictly convex, we prove that the singularities are folds. If the matrix is indefinite (i.e., the surface of integration is a hyperboloid-type quadric) and S S is either strictly convex or a cylinder, then cusp, fold, or blowdown singularities are present. We also study the case when the surface of integration is a paraboloid and show that the Bolker condition is satisfied.

The goal of this paper is to undertake a systematic analysis of the microlocal properties of generalized Radon transforms that integrate a compactly supported function
f
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
over quadric hypersurfaces whose centers lie on a prescribed hypersurface
S
. The quadric surfaces of integration are defined via an
n\times n
symmetric, invertible matrix
A
with real entries. We study the singularities of the right and left projections of the canonical relation associated with these operators. In general, a canonical relation describes the relation between the singularities of the data and the singularities of the image. We show that the hypersurface
S
and the signature of the matrix
A
determine the singularities of the projections (i.e., folds, cusps, or blowdowns). If the matrix is positive or negative definite (i.e., the surface of integration is an ellipsoid) and
S
is strictly convex, we prove that the singularities are folds. If the matrix is indefinite (i.e., the surface of integration is a hyperboloid-type quadric) and
S
is either strictly convex or a cylinder, then cusp, fold, or blowdown singularities are present.

We also study the case when the surface of integration is a paraboloid and show that in that case, the Bolker condition is satisfied. Our study takes its initial motivation from the paper
[
51
]
in which several of our transforms are studied on restricted domains for which the canonical left and right projections from the canonical relation do not drop rank; thus, no added singularities/artifacts are present in the normal operator.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we define the types of singularities that come up when analyzing our transforms, and we define some of the basic terms.

Let
f
be a smooth function
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

Let
S_{1}(f)
be the following smooth hypersurface:

S_{1}(f)=\{p\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}:\det(\mathrm{d}f_{p})=0,\ \mathrm{d}(\det(\mathrm{d}f))_{p}\neq 0\}.

We say that the derivative of
f
drop ranks simply by 1 at points
p\in S_{1}(f)
.

Definition 2.1
.

[
26
]
The function
f
has a fold singularity along
S_{1}(f)
if for all
p\in S_{1}(f)
,
\ker(\mathrm{d}f_{p})
is transversal to
T_{p}S_{1}(f)
.

Therefore, if
f
has a fold singularity along
S_{1}(f)
, we may choose local coordinates such that there exists a smooth
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, with
f(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n})=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n-1},h(x))
and we have the following local description of
S_{1}(f)
:

S_{1}(f)=\left\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\frac{\partial h}{\partial x_{n}}(x)=0,\ \mathrm{d}\left(\frac{\partial h}{\partial x_{n}}\right)\left(x\right)\neq 0\right\}.

The fold condition implies
\frac{\partial^{2}h}{\partial^{2}x_{n}}\left(x\right)\neq 0
.

Any map with fold singularities can be put in the local normal form
f(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n})=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}^{2})

[
26
]
.

Definition 2.2
.

[
26
]
The function
f
has a blowdown singularity along
S_{1}(f)
if for all
p\in S_{1}(f)
,
\ker(\mathrm{d}f_{p})\subset T_{p}S_{1}(f)
.

Any map with blowdown singularities can be put in the normal form
[
26
]
:

f(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n})=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n-1},x_{n-1}x_{n}).

Next, we define cusp singularities. We consider a point
p\in S_{1}(f)
where
\ker(\mathrm{d}f)_{p}\subset T_{p}S_{1}(f)
. Consider a vector field
v
along
S_{1}(f)
such that
v\in\ker(\mathrm{d}f)
at all points on
S_{1}(f)
. Let
K
be a smooth function such that
K|_{S_{1}(f)}=0
and
\mathrm{d}K|_{S_{1}(f)}\neq 0
. Then, since
\mathrm{d}K
annihilates vectors tangent to
S_{1}(f)
, the function
v(K)=\mathrm{d}K(v)
has a zero at
p
.

Definition 2.3
.

[
26
]
The function
f
has a cusp singularity at
p
if
\mathrm{d}K(v)
has a simple zero at
p
.

Without loss of generality, assume that
p=0
is a cusp point. Using suitable coordinates defined near
p=0
, we may write
f(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n})=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots x_{n-1},h(x))
, with
h(0)=0
, and locally,
S_{1}(f)=\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\frac{\partial h}{\partial x_{n}}(x)=0,\mathrm{d}(\frac{\partial h}{\partial x_{n}})(x)\neq 0\}
. We say that
f
has a cusp singularity at
p=0
if
\frac{\partial^{2}h}{\partial x_{n}^{2}}(0)=0,\ \ \frac{\partial^{3}h}{\partial x_{n}^{3}}(0)\neq 0
and the matrix
\left[\mathrm{d}(\frac{\partial h}{\partial x_{n}})(0)\ \ \mathrm{d}(\frac{\partial^{2}h}{\partial x_{n}^{2}})(0)\right]

has rank
2
. More generally, if
\ker\mathrm{d}f=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{n}}
is tangent simply to
S_{1}(f)
along

S_{1,1}(f)=\Big\{x:\frac{\partial h}{\partial x_{n}}(x)=\frac{\partial^{2}h}{\partial x_{n}^{2}}(x)=0\ \mbox{and}\ \mathrm{d}\left(\frac{\partial h}{\partial x_{n}}\right)(x),\mathrm{d}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}h}{\partial x_{n}^{2}}\right)(x),\ \mbox{are linearly independent}\Big\},

then
S_{1,1}(f)
is a codimension-
2
submanifold, called the cusp set of
f
. Any map with a cusp singularity can be put into a local normal
form
[
26
]
:

f(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n})=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n-1},x_{n-1}x_{n}+x_{n}^{3}).

Definition 2.4
.

Let
A
be an invertible symmetric
n\times n
matrix. We say
A

is positive definite if all the eigenvalues of
A
are positive and negative definite if all the eigenvalues of
A
are negative. When
A
has both positive and negative eigenvalues,
A
is indefinite.

Definition 2.5
.

An orientable hypersurface
S
is called strictly convex if its second fundamental form is definite at every point of
S
(with respect to a chosen orientation), i.e., its principal curvatures are strictly positive (or negative) at every point (e.g., see
[
48
]
).

Remark 2.6
.

Let
S=\{(x,q(x))\}
be the graph of a
C^{2}
function. The matrix
\mathrm{Hess}(q)
is everywhere positive (or negative) definite if and only if
S
is a locally strictly convex hypersurface (up to orientation); see, e.g.,
[
17
]
or
[
25
]
.

Let
X
and
Y
be open subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n_{X}}
and
\mathbb{R}^{n_{Y}}
respectively,
and let
\varphi
be a nondegenerate phase function
[
18
]
on
X\times Y\times(\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus 0)
, for some
N\geq 1
. Let
a
be an amplitude in

S^{m}_{1,0}

[
18
]
.

Definition 2.7
.

[
34
]

A Fourier integral operator (FIO) is a continuous linear operator
F:\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(Y)\to\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(X)
with the kernel

K_{F}(x,y)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{i\varphi(x,y;\theta)}a(x,y;\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta
.

The order of
F
is
\mu:=m+\frac{2N-n_{X}-n_{Y}}{4}
,
and the canonical relation of
F
is

C=\big\{(x,\mathrm{d}_{x}\varphi;y,-\mathrm{d}_{y}\varphi)\,:\,(x,y;\theta)\in\mbox{supp}(a),\,\mathrm{d}_{\theta}\varphi(x,y;\theta)=0\,\big\}\subset(T^{*}X\setminus 0)\times(T^{*}Y\setminus 0).

We define the canonical left and right projections from
C
to be

\pi_{R}:C\rightarrow T^{*}X,\ \pi_{R}(x,y,\theta)=(y,-\mathrm{d}_{y}\varphi)
and
\pi_{L}:C\rightarrow T^{*}Y,\ \pi_{L}(x,y,\theta)=(x,\mathrm{d}_{x}\varphi)
. We say that a canonical relation
C\subset T^{*}X\times T^{*}Y
satisfies the Bolker condition if
\pi_{L}
is an injective immersion.

3.
The canonical relation of
\mathcal{R}

In this section, we provide the basic notation for our transform. After defining the transform, we calculate its canonical relation and some basic terms.

In the discussion below, we mainly follow the notation from
[
51
]
. Let
S
be a smooth connected hypersurface in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, representing the centers of integration surfaces of the Radon transforms considered in this paper. We assume throughout the article that
S
is a surface parameterized by the smooth function
q:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
for some open
\Omega
in
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
:

(3.1)

y_{n}=q(y_{1},\dots,y_{n-1}),\ \ y^{\prime}=(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n-1})\in\Omega.

However, one can often interchange coordinates to locally parameterize a general manifold
S
by (
3.1
), and many of our theorems will be valid in this case.

We let
A
be an
n\times n
symmetric, invertible matrix with real entries. We use
A
to define certain quadric surfaces of integration and assume that the entries of
A
are constants. In some cases, we assume that
A
is a diagonal matrix, and in many cases, we will generalize to the case when
A
is not diagonal. We also adopt the following convention:

x_{T}=x-s,\ \ \text{where}\ \ s=(y^{\prime},q(y^{\prime}))\in S,\ \ \text{for}\ \ y^{\prime}\in\Omega,

and define the function

(3.2)

\displaystyle\psi(y^{\prime},t,x,\omega)

\displaystyle=\omega(t-x_{T}^{T}Ax_{T}),\;\;t>0.

\displaystyle=\omega\left(t-(x^{\prime}-y^{\prime},x_{n}-q(y^{\prime}))A(x^{\prime}-y^{\prime},x_{n}-q(y^{\prime}))^{T}\right).

where
x^{\prime}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1})
and
y^{\prime}\in\Omega
. We suppress the dependence of
\psi
on
A
since we assume
A
is fixed in each theorem.

The generalized Radon transform that we study is

(3.3)

\mathcal{R}f(y^{\prime},t)=\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\nabla_{x}\psi|f(x)e^{i\psi(y^{\prime},t,x,\omega)}\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}\omega.

We assume that
S
does not intersect the support of
f
. For each

(y^{\prime},t)\in\Omega\times(0,\infty)
,
\mathcal{R}f(y^{\prime},t)
integrates
f
over the set
{x^{T}_{T}}A{x_{T}}=t
, which is a level set of the homogeneous second order polynomial
{x^{T}_{T}}A{x_{T}}
.

The canonical relation associated to
\mathcal{R}
is then given by

C=\Big\{(y^{\prime},x_{T}^{T}Ax_{T},\mathrm{d}_{y^{\prime}}\psi,\omega;x,-\mathrm{d}_{x}\psi):\psi_{\omega}(y^{\prime},t,x,\omega)=0\Big\}.

The variables
(y^{\prime},x,\omega)
parametrize the conic Lagrangian submanifold
C
, based on the definition above.

Let us now compute the terms
\mathrm{d}_{y^{\prime}}\psi
and
\mathrm{d}_{x}\psi
separately. A straightforward computation gives

\mathrm{d}^{T}_{y^{\prime}}\psi=2\omega x_{T}^{T}AB^{T}

with
B
being an
(n-1)\times n
matrix given by

B=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&\cdots&0&\partial_{y_{1}}q\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&1&\partial_{y_{n-1}}q\end{pmatrix}.

Similarly, another straightforward computation gives

\mathrm{d}_{x}\psi=-2\omega A(x^{\prime}-y^{\prime},x_{n}-q(y^{\prime}))=-2\omega x_{T}^{T}A.

Now that we have the canonical relation
C
associated to
\mathcal{R}
, let us
study the canonical left (
\pi_{L}
) and right (
\pi_{R}
) projections to
the respective cotangent bundles. We will show that the
projections drop rank simply by
1
on a codimension 1 submanifold
\Sigma
; see (
4.2
). We will prove that the singularities of the projections are determined by the signature of
A
and the choice of
S
. We distinguish several cases described in sections
4
-
7
.

Now that we have established the preliminaries, we will analyze the transform
\mathcal{R}
when the matrix
A
and
S
have different properties.

4.
Case when
A
is positive definite and
S
is a smooth strictly convex hypersurface

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{R}:\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus S)\rightarrow\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Omega\times(0,\infty))
be the Radon transform defined by (
3.2
) and (
3.3
). Assume
A
is an invertible positive definite matrix, and
Hess(
q
) is definite (either positive or negative definite), then
\mathcal{R}
is an FIO associated to a two-sided fold canonical relation.

Recall that we assume
t>0
in this article (see (
3.2
)). Note that if
A
is negative definite, then there are no solutions to
{x^{T}_{T}}A{x_{T}}=t
for
t>0
, but if we restrict to
t<0
then we get the same result as in Theorem
4.1
.

Proof.

Using the parameterization
(y^{\prime},x,\omega)
of the canonical relation
C
, we have

\pi_{L}(y^{\prime},x,\omega)=(y^{\prime},x_{T}^{T}Ax_{T},2\omega x_{T}^{T}AB^{T},\omega).

We next follow
[
51
]
in computing
\mathrm{d}\pi_{L}
and
\mathrm{d}\pi_{R}
. We have

\mathrm{d}\pi_{L}=\begin{pmatrix}I_{n-1}&0&0\\
-2x_{T}^{T}AB^{T}&2\omega x_{T}^{T}A&0\\
\star&2\omega BA&2BAx_{T}\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix}.

The quantities denoted by
\star
here and henceforth are not consequential to the calculations.

A straightforward calculation gives

(4.1)

\det(\mathrm{d}\pi_{L})=2\omega\det\begin{pmatrix}x_{T}^{T}A\\
BA\\
\end{pmatrix}=2\omega\det\Bigg(\begin{pmatrix}x_{T}^{T}\\
B\\
\end{pmatrix}A\Bigg)=2\omega\det\begin{pmatrix}x_{T}^{T}\\
B\\
\end{pmatrix}\det(A).

Next

\det\begin{pmatrix}x_{T}^{T}\\
B\end{pmatrix}=\det\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}-y_{1}&x_{2}-y_{2}&\cdots&x_{n-1}-y_{n-1}&x_{n}-q(y^{\prime})\\
1&0&\dots&0&\frac{\partial q}{\partial y_{1}}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\dots&1&\frac{\partial q}{\partial y_{n-1}}\\
\end{pmatrix}.

By an easy computation, this turns out to be

\det\begin{pmatrix}x_{T}^{T}\\
B\end{pmatrix}=(x_{n}-q(y^{\prime})-\frac{\partial q}{\partial y_{1}}(x_{1}-y_{1})-\dots-\frac{\partial q}{\partial y_{n-1}}(x_{n-1}-y_{n-1})=(-(\nabla q)^{T}(y^{\prime}),1)\cdot x_{T}^{T}.

Thus,
\mathrm{d}\pi_{L}
drops rank where

, that is, on the codimension 1 hypersurface

(4.2)

\Sigma=\{(y^{\prime},x,\omega):(-(\nabla q)^{T}(y^{\prime}),1)\cdot(x_{1}-y_{1},\cdots,x_{n-1}-y_{n-1},x_{n}-q(y^{\prime}))=0\}.

Since the derivative with respect to

Next, we compute
\ker(\mathrm{d}\pi_{L})
to determine the nature of the singularity of
\pi_{L}

(\delta y^{\prime},\delta x,\delta\omega)\in\ker(\mathrm{d}\pi_{L})
. Then note that
\delta y^{\prime}=\delta\omega=0

\begin{pmatrix}x_{T}^{T}\\
B\end{pmatrix}A\begin{pmatrix}\delta x_{1}\\
\delta x_{2}\\
\vdots\\
\delta x_{n}\\
\end{pmatrix}=0

Let

.
Expanding this system,

\begin{pmatrix}x_{T}^{T}\\
B\end{pmatrix}r=\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}-y_{1}&x_{2}-y_{2}&\cdots&x_{n-1}-y_{n-1}&x_{n}-q(y^{\prime})\\
1&0&\cdots&0&\frac{\partial q}{\partial y_{1}}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&1&\frac{\partial q}{\partial y_{n-1}}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}r_{1}\\
r_{2}\\
\vdots\\
r_{n}\\
\end{pmatrix}=0.

Thus

\displaystyle r_{1}=-\frac{\partial q}{\partial y_{1}}r_{n},\quad r_{2}=-\frac{\partial q}{\partial y_{2}}r_{n},\quad\cdots,\quad r_{n-1}=-\frac{\partial q}{\partial y_{n-1}}r_{n}

r_{1}(x_{1}-y_{1})+r_{2}(x_{2}-y_{2})+\cdots+(x_{n}-q)r_{n}=0.

Replacing
r_{i}
in the last equation, we get

This is equivalent to
r_{n}\langle-\nabla q,1\rangle\cdot x_{T}^{T}=0
which is already true since this is the defining equation of
\Sigma
. We will next investigate if the singularity is a fold. Let us assume that we have a

Since the support of the function

\pi_{R}
has a fold singularity. In the first case, since we are considering points
(y^{\prime},x,\omega)
on
\Sigma
, we have
x_{n}=q(y^{\prime})=q(x^{\prime})
. But this would mean that we have a point common to the support of the function
f
as well as the surface
S
. We get a contradiction. Therefore, if we assume that Hess
(q)
is positive or negative definite,
\pi_{R}
has a fold singularity.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

If Hess
(q)
is identically zero, then
S
is a hyperplane, and
\pi_{R}
has a blowdown singularity.

Remark 4.3
.

Note that the condition defining
\Sigma
is just that
x
is in the
tangent plane to
S
at
(y^{\prime},q)
. Therefore, if the surfaces of
integration given by

(4.5)

T(y^{\prime},t)=\left\{x\,:\,x^{T}_{T}Ax_{T}=t\right\}

do not intersect the tangent plane to
S
at
(y^{\prime},q)
,
then there are no points on
C
where the projections drop rank. This
is one of the main theorems of
[
51
]
.

5.
Case when

is indefinite and

We consider the Radon transform
\mathcal{R}
as before, with

an invertible, symmetric matrix with

positive eigenvalues and

negative eigenvalues. We distinguish two cases: when

and when

.

In some of the results in this section, we will assume

to denote such a matrix.

5.1.
Case
k>1

The main theorem for this subsection focuses on the case when

is a smooth strictly convex hypersurface.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\mathcal{R}:\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus S)\rightarrow\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Omega\times(0,\infty))
be the Radon transform defined by (
3.2
)
and (
3.3
). If
A
is a real symmetric invertible matrix with

k\in\{2,3,\dots,n-1\}
positive eigenvalues and
n-k
negative
eigenvalues, and

is a smooth, strictly convex hypersurface, then

\mathcal{R}
is an FIO associated to a canonical relation with
\pi_{L}
having a
cusp singularity and
\pi_{R}
having a fold singularity.

We will prove this theorem in subsection
5.1.3
.
To make the proof more accessible, we will consider a few simpler
cases in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
:

(1)

A
is a diagonal matrix with entries
\pm 1
and
S
is the sphere,

(2)

A
is diagonal with entries
\pm 1
and
S
is any strictly
convex hypersurface.

We will then consider the general case. The calculations above will be helpful in dealing with this general case. We will consider

\pi_{L}
only since the result and the proof for
\pi_{R}
is the same
as the one in Theorem
5.1
.

5.1.1.
Special case 1:
A
diagonal with entries
\pm 1
and
S
a sphere:

Let us consider the matrix with
k
entries of
1
and
n-k
entries of
-1
along the diagonal with
k\in\{2,\dots,n-1\}
.

Let
q
locally describe the unit sphere. Using coordinates
y^{\prime}=(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n-1})
we have

q(y^{\prime})=\sqrt{1-y_{1}^{2}-\cdots-y_{n-1}^{2}}.

We compute

\displaystyle\langle-\nabla q,1\rangle\delta x

\displaystyle=r_{n}\langle\frac{y_{1}}{\sqrt{1-|y^{\prime}|^{2}}},\cdots,\frac{y_{n-1}}{\sqrt{1-|y^{\prime}|^{2}}},1\rangle A^{-1}\langle\frac{y_{1}}{\sqrt{1-|y^{\prime}|^{2}}},\cdots,\frac{y_{n-1}}{\sqrt{1-|y^{\prime}|^{2}}},1\rangle^{T}

\displaystyle=r_{n}\Big\{\frac{y_{1}^{2}+\cdots+y_{k}^{2}-(y_{k+1}^{2}+\cdots+y_{n-1}^{2})}{1-|y^{\prime}|^{2}}-1\Big\}.

\displaystyle=r_{n}\Big\{\frac{y_{1}^{2}+\cdots+y_{k}^{2}-(y_{k+1}^{2}+\cdots+y_{n-1}^{2})-1+|y^{\prime}|^{2}}{1-|y^{\prime}|^{2}}\Big\}

\displaystyle=r_{n}\Big\{\frac{2(y_{1}^{2}+\cdots+y_{k}^{2})-1}{1-|y^{\prime}|^{2}}\Big\}.

In the complement of the set
\{(y^{\prime},x,\omega)\in\Sigma
such that
y_{1}^{2}+\cdots+y_{k}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\}
, where
\Sigma
is given by (
4.2
), we have fold points as before.

Let us next consider the set
\Sigma_{1}=\{(y^{\prime},x,\omega)\in\Sigma:y_{1}^{2}+\cdots+y_{k}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\}
. More explicitly,

\Sigma_{1}=\Bigg\{(y^{\prime},x,\omega):\langle-\nabla q,1\rangle\cdot\left(x_{1}-y_{1},\cdots,x_{n-1}-y_{n-1},x_{n}-q(y^{\prime})\right)=0;y_{1}^{2}+\cdots+y_{k}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}=y_{k+1}^{2}+\cdots+y_{n}^{2}\Bigg\}.

As in Theorem
4.1
, we only need to consider a vector field in
\ker(\mathrm{d}\pi_{L})
of the form
(0,v_{1},\cdots,v_{n},0)
. We then have, for each
1\leq i\leq k
,

v_{i}=-\frac{y_{i}}{\sqrt{1-|y^{\prime}|^{2}}}v_{n}

and for
k+1\leq i\leq n-1
,

v_{i}=\frac{y_{i}}{\sqrt{1-|y^{\prime}|^{2}}}v_{n}.

Hence
v=(v_{1},\cdots,v_{n})
is of the form

(5.1)

\left(-\frac{y_{1}}{\sqrt{1-|y^{\prime}|^{2}}}v_{n},\cdots,-\frac{y_{k}}{\sqrt{1-|y^{\prime}|^{2}}}v_{n},\frac{y_{k+1}}{1-|y^{\prime}|^{2}}v_{n},\cdots,\frac{y_{n-1}}{1-|y^{\prime}|^{2}}v_{n},v_{n}\right),

with
v_{n}\neq 0
anywhere.
For
p\in\Sigma_{1}
, such a
v|_{p}
is in
T\Sigma_{p}
, as can be seen by considering the defining function
K
from (
4.2
)
and noting that

\mathrm{d}K(v)=v(K)=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k}\frac{-y_{i}^{2}}{1-|y^{\prime}|^{2}}v_{n}+\sum\limits_{i=k+1}^{n-1}\frac{y_{i}^{2}}{1-|y^{\prime}|^{2}}v_{n}+v_{n}=v_{n}\left(\frac{1-2|y^{\prime\prime}|^{2}}{1-|y^{\prime}|^{2}}\right)=0.

Here,
y^{\prime\prime}=(y_{1},\cdots,y_{k})
.

Next, we check the requirement for a simple zero in the definition of cusp points. Let us take
v
to be a smooth vector field along
\Sigma
such that
v\in\ker(\mathrm{d}\pi_{L})
at each point of
\Sigma
. Then
v
has exactly the form given in (
5.1
). Define

G(y^{\prime},x,\omega):=\mathrm{d}K(v)=v(K)=v_{n}\left(\frac{1-2|y^{\prime\prime}|^{2}}{1-|y^{\prime}|^{2}}\right).

Here
v_{n}
is a smooth function of
(y^{\prime},x,\omega)
non-vanishing.
Then for
1\leq i\leq k
,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial G}{\partial y_{i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial v_{n}}{\partial y_{i}}\frac{1-2|y^{\prime\prime}|^{2}}{1-|y^{\prime}|^{2}}+v_{n}\frac{-2y_{i}+4y_{i}(y_{k+1}^{2}+\cdots+y_{n-1}^{2})}{(1-|y^{\prime}|^{2})^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial v_{n}}{\partial y_{i}}\frac{1-2|y^{\prime\prime}|^{2}}{1-|y^{\prime}|^{2}}-2v_{n}y_{i}\frac{1-2(y_{k+1}^{2}+\cdots+y_{n-1}^{2})}{(1-|y^{\prime}|^{2})^{2}}.

At points
p
on
\Sigma_{1}
, the first expression on the right vanishes and since
y_{n}>0
(being described as the graph of the function
f(y^{\prime})=\sqrt{1-|y^{\prime}|^{2}}
),
1-2(y_{k+1}^{2}+\cdots+y_{n-1}^{2})\neq 0
. Hence at least one of the partial derivatives,
\frac{\partial G}{\partial y_{i}}
for
1\leq i\leq k
cannot vanish at points on
\Sigma_{1}
, since
y_{1}^{2}+\cdots+y_{k}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}
. Thus, we get that the set
\Sigma_{1}
consists of cusp points for
\pi_{L}
.

5.1.2.
Special case 2:
A
diagonal with entries
\pm 1
and
S
a smooth strictly convex hypersurface

Let us consider the matrix
A
, with
k
entries of
1
and
n-k
entries of
-1
, along the diagonal,
with
k\in\{2,\dots,n-1\}
.

A=\operatorname{Diag}(1,\dots,1,-1,\dots,-1).

Let
S
be a smooth hypersurface locally given as the graph of the smooth function
q(y^{\prime})
for
y^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
.
Then

\langle-\nabla q,1\rangle A^{-1}\langle-\nabla q,1\rangle^{T}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k}|\partial_{y_{i}}q|^{2}-\sum\limits_{i=k+1}^{n-1}|\partial_{y_{i}}q|^{2}-1.

As before, if we exclude the points where
\langle-\nabla q,1\rangle A^{-1}\langle-\nabla q,1\rangle^{T}=0
, then, in this set, we have fold points.

Next, we study the subset
\Sigma_{1}\subset\Sigma
, where
\Sigma
is defined in (
4.2
), by

\Sigma_{1}=\{(y^{\prime},x,\omega)\in\Sigma:\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k}|\partial_{y_{i}}q|^{2}-\sum\limits_{i=k+1}^{n-1}|\partial_{y_{i}}q|^{2}=1\}.

We would like to show that
\Sigma_{1}
consists of cusp points. We first show that for a point
p\in\Sigma_{1}
,
\ker(\mathrm{d}\pi_{L})_{p}\subset T\Sigma_{p}
.

As in Theorem
4.1
, if
(\delta y,\delta x,\delta\omega)\in\ker(\mathrm{d}\pi_{L})
, then,
\delta y=0=\delta\omega
, and we only need to consider a vector field in
\ker(\mathrm{d}\pi_{L})
of the form
(0,v_{1},\cdots,v_{n},0)
. We then have, for each
1\leq i\leq k
,

v_{i}=v_{n}\partial_{y_{i}}q

and for
k+1\leq i\leq n-1
,

v_{i}=-v_{n}\partial_{y_{i}}q.

Hence
v=(v_{1},\cdots,v_{n})
is of the form

(5.2)

v=(v_{n}\partial_{y_{1}}q,\cdots,v_{n}\partial_{y_{k}}q,-v_{n}\partial_{y_{k+1}}q,\cdots,-v_{n}\partial_{y_{k+1}}q).

For
p\in\Sigma_{1}
, such a
v\in T\Sigma_{p}
, since for
K
the defining function in (
4.2
), we have

\mathrm{d}K(v)=v(K)=v_{n}\left(-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k}|\partial_{y_{i}}q|^{2}+\sum\limits_{i=k+1}^{n-1}|\partial_{y_{i}}q|^{2}+1\right)=0.

Here we note that
v_{n}
is a non-vanishing smooth function of
(y^{\prime},x,\omega)
.

Next, we check the simple zero condition in the definition of cusp points. Let us take
v
to be a smooth vector field along
\Sigma
such that
v\in\ker(\mathrm{d}\pi_{L})
at each point of
\Sigma
. Then
v
has exactly the form given in (
5.2
) with the
y^{\prime}
and
\omega
components being
0
. Define

G(y^{\prime},x,\omega):=\mathrm{d}K(v)=v(K)=v_{n}\left(-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k}|\partial_{y_{i}}q|^{2}+\sum\limits_{i=k+1}^{n-1}|\partial_{y_{i}}q|^{2}+1\right).

Here
v_{n}
is a smooth non-vanishing function of
(y^{\prime},x,\omega)
.
Then for
1<k\leq n-1
,

\partial_{y_{k}}G(y^{\prime},x,\omega)=\frac{\partial v_{n}}{\partial y_{i}}\left(-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k}|\partial_{y_{i}}q|^{2}+\sum\limits_{i=k+1}^{n-1}|\partial_{y_{i}}q|^{2}+1\right)+2v_{n}\left(-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k}\frac{\partial^{2}q}{\partial y_{i}\partial y_{k}}\partial_{y_{i}}q+\sum\limits_{i=k+1}^{n-1}\frac{\partial^{2}q}{\partial y_{i}\partial y_{k}}\partial_{y_{i}}q\right).

At points on
\Sigma_{1}
, the first term vanishes, and since
v_{n}
is non-vanishing, we have a system of equations of the form

\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\partial^{2}q}{\partial y_{1}^{2}}&\cdots&\frac{\partial^{2}q}{\partial y_{1}\partial y_{n-1}}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\frac{\partial^{2}q}{\partial y_{1}\partial y_{n-1}}&\cdots&\frac{\partial^{2}q}{\partial y_{n-1}^{2}}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}-\partial_{y_{1}}q\\
\vdots\\
-\partial_{y_{k}}q\\
\partial_{y_{k+1}}q\\
\vdots\\
\partial_{y_{n-1}}q\end{pmatrix}=\frac{1}{2v_{n}}\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{y_{1}}G\\
\vdots\\
\vdots\\
\vdots\\
\partial_{y_{n-1}}G\end{pmatrix}.

If we assume that the function
q
locally gives the graph of the strictly convex hypersurface
S
, then the Hessian matrix on the left is positive definite. Also,
\nabla q
is non-vanishing, since on the level set we are interested in, that is on
\Sigma_{1}
, we have

-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k}|\partial_{y_{i}}q|^{2}+\sum\limits_{i=k+1}^{n-1}|\partial_{y_{i}}q|^{2}+1=0.

Hence not all components of
\partial_{y_{i}}q
can be
0
on
\Sigma_{1}
.
Therefore,
\nabla G
is non-vanishing at points on
\Sigma_{1}
, showing that
\Sigma_{1}
consists of cusp points.

5.1.3.
General
A
,
k>1
,
S
strictly convex
hypersurface

We now prove the main theorem of this
section, Theorem
5.1
.

Proof of Theorem
5.1
.

Let
A
be a general symmetric matrix with
k>1
positive and
n-k
negative eigenvalues.
As before, if we exclude the points where
\langle-\nabla q,1\rangle A^{-1}\langle-\nabla q,1\rangle^{T}=0
, then in this set, we have fold points.

Let us now consider the subset
\Sigma_{1}\subset\Sigma
, where
\Sigma
is defined in (
4.2
), by

\Sigma_{1}=\{(y^{\prime},x,\omega)\in\Sigma:\langle-\nabla q,1\rangle A^{-1}\langle-\nabla q,1\rangle^{T}=0\}.

We would like to show that
\Sigma_{1}
consists of cusp points.
As before, we only need to consider a vector field in
\ker(\mathrm{d}\pi_{L})_{p}
of the form
(0,v_{1},\cdots,v_{n},0)
.
Denoting
v=(v_{1},\cdots,v_{n})
and then letting
r=Av
, we then have,

\displaystyle r_{1}=-\frac{\partial q}{\partial y_{1}}r_{n}

\displaystyle r_{2}=-\frac{\partial q}{\partial y_{2}}r_{n}

\displaystyle r_{n-1}=-\frac{\partial q}{\partial y_{n-1}}r_{n}.

Hence
r
is of the form

(5.3)

r=(-q_{y_{1}}r_{n},\cdots,-q_{y_{n-1}}r_{n},r_{n})

and
v=A^{-1}r
.
We note that
v|_{p}\in T\Sigma_{p}
, since for
K
in (
4.2
), we have

\mathrm{d}K(v)=v(K)=r_{n}\left(\langle-\nabla q,1\rangle A^{-1}\langle-\nabla q,1\rangle^{T}\right)=0.

We note that
r_{n}
is a non-vanishing smooth function of
(y^{\prime},x,\omega)
.

Next, let us check the simple zero condition in the definition of a cusp. Let us
take
v
to be a smooth vector field along
\Sigma
such that
v\in\ker(\mathrm{d}\pi_{L})
at each point of
\Sigma
. Then
v
has exactly
the form
A^{-1}r
with
r
given in (
5.3
) and with the
y^{\prime}
and
\omega
components being
0
. Define

G(y^{\prime},x,\omega):=\mathrm{d}K(v)=v(K)=r_{n}\left(\langle-\nabla q,1\rangle A^{-1}\langle-\nabla q,1\rangle^{T}\right).

Here
r_{n}
is a smooth function of
(y^{\prime},x,\omega)
non-vanishing.
Taking derivatives of
G
with respect to
y_{k}
for
1\leq k\leq n-1
, we have the following system

\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\partial^{2}q}{\partial y_{1}^{2}}&\cdots&\frac{\partial^{2}q}{\partial y_{1}\partial y_{n-1}}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\frac{\partial^{2}q}{\partial y_{1}\partial y_{n-1}}&\cdots&\frac{\partial^{2}q}{\partial y_{n-1}^{2}}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\left(A^{-1}\right)_{1}\cdot(-\nabla q,1)\\
\vdots\\
\vdots\\
\left(A^{-1}\right)_{n-1}\cdot(-\nabla q,1)\end{pmatrix}=\frac{1}{2v_{n}}\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{y_{1}}G\\
\vdots\\
\vdots\\
\vdots\\
\partial_{y_{n-1}}G\end{pmatrix}.

Here
\left(A^{-1}\right)_{i}
refers to the
i^{\mathrm{th}}
row of
A^{-1}
.
If we assume that the function
q
locally gives the graph of the strictly convex hypersurface
S
, then the Hessian matrix on the left is positive definite. Also the column vector

(5.4)

\begin{pmatrix}\left(A^{-1}\right)_{1}\cdot(-\nabla q,1)\\
\vdots\\
\vdots\\
\left(A^{-1}\right)_{n-1}\cdot(-\nabla q,1)\end{pmatrix}

is non-vanishing since on the level set we are interested in. That is on
\Sigma_{1}
, we have

\langle-\nabla q,1\rangle A^{-1}\langle-\nabla q,1\rangle^{T}=0,

and if we assume that the above column vector is vanishing, then we would have that
\left(A^{-1}\right)_{n}\cdot(-\nabla q,1)=0
as well. Since
A
is invertible, this would imply that
(-\nabla q,1)
is the
0
vector, which is not possible.
Hence, not all components of (
5.4
) can be
0
on
\Sigma_{1}
.
Therefore,
\nabla G
is non-vanishing at points on
\Sigma_{1}
, showing that
\Sigma_{1}
consists of cusp points.

∎

5.2.
Case
k=1
,
A
diagonal, entries
\pm 1

In this section, we consider
A
to be diagonal with exactly one entry being
1
and the rest being
-1
for the case of
S
being a sphere. In this case, we can prove that there are no cusp singularities for the left projection.

We consider the top half of the unit sphere. Let
\Omega
be the open unit disk in
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
and let
q(y^{\prime})=\sqrt{1-y_{1}^{2}-\cdots-y_{n-1}^{2}}
. Let
\tilde{S}
be the open top half of the unit sphere:

\tilde{S}=\left\{y^{\prime},q(y^{\prime})\,:\,y^{\prime}\in\Omega\right\}.

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
\mathcal{R}:\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\tilde{S})\rightarrow\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Omega\times(0,\infty))
be the Radon transform
defined by (
3.2
) and (
3.3
). Let
A
be a diagonal
matrix, with one entry (
k=1
) of
1
and
n-1
entries of
-1
on
the main diagonal. Then
\mathcal{R}
is an FIO
associated to a two-sided fold canonical relation.

Proof.

First we assume that the
1
entry is in the first slot, so
A=\operatorname{Diag}(1,-1,\dots,-1)
.

Repeating the calculations as before, we see from the discussion below (
4.3
) that

\langle-\nabla q,1\rangle\delta x=r_{n}\langle-\nabla q,1\rangle A^{-1}\langle-\nabla q,1\rangle^{T}.

For the case of
A
and
q
at hand, we have

\displaystyle\langle-\nabla q,1\rangle\delta x

\displaystyle=r_{n}\langle\frac{y_{1}}{\sqrt{1-|y^{\prime}|^{2}}},\frac{y_{2}}{\sqrt{1-|y^{\prime}|^{2}}},\cdots,\frac{y_{n-1}}{\sqrt{1-|y^{\prime}|^{2}}},1\rangle\begin{pmatrix}1&0&\cdots&0\\
0&-1&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&0\\
0&0&\cdots&-1\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle\cdot\langle\frac{y_{1}}{\sqrt{1-|y^{\prime}|^{2}}},\frac{y_{2}}{\sqrt{1-|y^{\prime}|^{2}}},\cdots,\frac{y_{n-1}}{\sqrt{1-|y^{\prime}|^{2}}},1\rangle^{T}

\displaystyle=r_{n}\left(\frac{2y_{1}^{2}-1}{1-|y^{\prime}|^{2}}\right).

Consider the intersection of the hyperplanes
y_{1}=\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
with
\tilde{S}
. In the complement of this intersection, we have
r_{n}=0
and following the previous calculation, we have that
\pi_{L}
is a fold.

Next, let us consider the points corresponding to
y_{1}=\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
on
\tilde{S}
. We again recall that
\Sigma
is characterized by the set of points

\Sigma=\{(y^{\prime},x,\omega):(-(\nabla q)^{T}(y^{\prime}),1)\cdot(x_{1}-y_{1},\cdots,x_{n-1}-y_{n-1},x_{n}-q(y))^{T}=0\}.

In our setup, we then have

\Sigma=\{((y^{\prime},x,\omega):(x_{1}-y_{1})\frac{y_{1}}{\sqrt{1-|y^{\prime}|^{2}}}+\cdots+(x_{n-1}-y_{n-1})\frac{y_{n-1}}{\sqrt{1-|y^{\prime}|^{2}}}+(x_{n}-\sqrt{1-|y^{\prime}|^{2}})=0\}.

Choosing
y_{1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
, the set
\Sigma
is characterized by

(5.5)

x_{1}+\sqrt{2}\sum\limits_{i=2}^{n-1}x_{i}y_{i}+x_{n}\sqrt{1-2|y^{\prime\prime}|^{2}}=\sqrt{2}.

Here
|y^{\prime\prime}|^{2}=\sum\limits_{i=2}^{n-1}y_{i}^{2}
.

We also have that
x
should lie on the surface

\left(x_{1}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^{2}-(x_{2}-y_{2})^{2}-\cdots-(x_{n-1}-y_{n-1})^{2}-\left(x_{n}-\frac{\sqrt{1-2|y^{\prime\prime}|^{2}}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^{2}=t.

We substitute

x_{1}=\sqrt{2}-\sqrt{2}\sum\limits_{i=2}^{n-1}x_{i}y_{i}-x_{n}\sqrt{1-2|y^{\prime\prime}|^{2}}

into the above equation. We get

\left(\sqrt{2}-\sqrt{2}\sum\limits_{i=2}^{n-1}x_{i}y_{i}-x_{n}\sqrt{1-2|y^{\prime\prime}|^{2}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^{2}-(x_{2}-y_{2})^{2}-\cdots-(x_{n-1}-y_{n-1})^{2}-\left(x_{n}-\frac{\sqrt{1-2|y^{\prime\prime}|^{2}}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^{2}=t.

Rearranging this, we have

\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}-\sqrt{2}\sum\limits_{i=2}^{n-1}x_{i}y_{i}-x_{n}\sqrt{1-2|y^{\prime\prime}|^{2}}\right)^{2}-(x_{2}-y_{2})^{2}-\cdots-(x_{n-1}-y_{n-1})^{2}-\left(x_{n}-\frac{\sqrt{1-2|y^{\prime\prime}|^{2}}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^{2}=t.

Simplifying this, we get,

2\left(\sum\limits_{i=2}^{n-1}x_{i}y_{i}\right)^{2}-2x_{n}^{2}|y^{\prime\prime}|^{2}+2\sqrt{2}x_{n}\left(\sum\limits_{i=2}^{n-1}x_{i}y_{i}\right)\sqrt{1-2|y^{\prime\prime}|^{2}}-\sum\limits_{i=2}^{n-1}x_{i}^{2}=t.

Adding and subtracting
x_{n}^{2}
, we then have

2\left(\sum\limits_{i=2}^{n-1}x_{i}y_{i}\right)^{2}+x_{n}^{2}\left(1-2|y^{\prime\prime}|^{2}\right)+2\sqrt{2}x_{n}\left(\sum\limits_{i=2}^{n-1}x_{i}y_{i}\right)\sqrt{1-2|y^{\prime\prime}|^{2}}-\sum\limits_{i=2}^{n}x_{i}^{2}=t.

From this, we get,

(5.6)

\left(x_{n}\sqrt{1-2|y^{\prime\prime}|^{2}}+\sqrt{2}\sum\limits_{i=2}^{n-1}x_{i}y_{i}\right)^{2}-\sum\limits_{i=2}^{n}x_{i}^{2}=t.

We can view the first term in (
5.6
) as a dot product of the two vectors

A=(x_{2},\cdots,x_{n})\ \ \text{and}\ \ B=(\sqrt{2}y_{2},\cdots,\sqrt{2}y_{n-1},\sqrt{1-2|y^{\prime\prime}|^{2}}).

We note that

b\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
. Let
S
be a
smooth surface in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
parametrized by
y_{n}=q(y^{\prime})
where

q:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
, and
\Omega

is an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}

\mathcal{R}:\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus S)\to\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Omega\times(0,\infty))
on surfaces

(7.2)

t-({x^{T}_{T}})^{\prime}A^{\prime}({x_{T}})^{\prime}-b(x_{n}-q)=0

with centers on

S
satisfies the Bolker condition.

Note that the center set
S
is an arbitrary smooth manifold
parameterized as a graph. The condition
b\neq 0
is important; if

b=0
, then the manifolds of integration are cylindrical surfaces, and
therefore, they only detect wavefront conormal to the axis of the
cylinder, the

axis. The Bolker condition does not hold since

x_{n}
cannot be determined from
\pi_{L}
, as one can see from equation
(

).

Proof.

Using the notation in the theorem, we see that the
phase function of our transform is

\psi(y^{\prime},t,x,\omega)=(t-({x^{T}_{T}})^{\prime}A^{\prime}({x_{T}})^{\prime}-b(x_{n}-q))\omega,

and our surface is given by

\psi(y^{\prime},t,x,\omega)=0
. Using this phase function, we see that the canonical relation is

C=\left\{\left(y^{\prime},t,\omega(2A^{\prime}({x_{T}})^{\prime}+b\nabla q),\omega;x,\omega\begin{pmatrix}2A^{\prime}({x_{T}})^{\prime}\\
b\end{pmatrix}\right)\,:\,y^{\prime}\in S,\ t=({x^{T}_{T}})^{\prime}A^{\prime}({x_{T}})^{\prime}+b(x_{n}-q),\ \omega\neq 0\right\}.

In coordinates
(y^{\prime},x,\omega)
, we see

(7.3)

\pi_{L}(y^{\prime},x,\omega)=(y^{\prime},({x^{T}_{T}})^{\prime}A^{\prime}({x_{T}})^{\prime}+b(x_{n}-q),\omega(2A^{\prime}({x_{T}})^{\prime}+b\nabla q),\omega)=:(y^{\prime},t,z,\tau)\in T^{*}(\Omega\times\mathbb{R}).

We now explain why
\pi_{L}
is injective. The first coordinate of this
projection gives
y^{\prime}
and therefore
q(y^{\prime})
and
\nabla q(y^{\prime})
. The
last coordinate,
\tau
, specifies
\omega
, and the second to last
(
z
coordinate) gives
({x_{T}})^{\prime}
since
A^{\prime}
is invertible. Finally,
the value of
x_{n}
is given by the
t
coordinate since
b\neq 0
.
This shows
\pi_{L}
is injective.

The formulas that determine
x^{\prime},\ x_{n}
, and
\omega
from
coordinates
(y^{\prime},t,z,\tau)
are all smooth functions, so

\pi_{L}^{-1}
is also smooth. Then,
\pi_{L}
is an immersion since

\mathrm{d}\pi_{L}^{-1}\mathrm{d}\pi_{L}=I_{n}
by the chain rule. Therefore, this transform
satisfies the Bolker condition.

Remark 7.2

(The case
k<n-1
)
.

Now let
k\in\{1,\dots,(n-2)\}
.
We get a completely different result. Following the notation at the
start of this section, including (

),
and using arguments similar to those in the proof of Theorem

\pi_{L}(y^{\prime},x,\omega)=(y^{\prime},({x^{T}_{T}})^{\prime}A^{\prime}({x_{T}})^{\prime}+b^{T}[x^{\prime\prime}-(y_{k+1},\dots,y_{n-1},q)],\omega(w+b_{n}\nabla q),\omega)

Next,

from the canonical relation drops rank by

.
Therefore, if

We give a brief summary about the

Definition 8.1
.

Two submanifolds
M
and
N

intersect
cleanly
if
M\cap N
is a smooth submanifold and
T(M\cap N)=TM\cap TN
.

Definition 8.2
.

[
31
]

S^{p,l}(m,n,k)
is the set of all functions
a(z;\xi,\sigma)\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{k})
such that for every
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
and every
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}_{+},\beta\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}_{+},\delta\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}_{+}

|\partial_{z}^{\alpha}\partial_{\xi}^{\beta}\partial_{\sigma}^{\delta}a(z,\xi,\sigma)|\leq c_{K,\alpha,\beta}(1+|\xi|)^{p-|\beta|}(1+|\sigma|)^{l-|\delta|}

for all
(z,\xi,\tau)\in K\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}
.
