Genetic diversity of Dermacentor nitens (Acari: Ixodidae) collected on horses from western Cuba
Authors: Lisset Roblejo-Arias, Adrian A Diaz-Sánchez, Roxana Marrero-Perera, Evelyn Lobo-Rivero, Belkis Corona-González, Neil B Chilton, Risa Pesapane
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe tropical horse tick, Dermacentor nitens Neumann, 1897, has a wide distribution throughout the Neotropics, and is a vector of protozoan haemoparasites that cause equine piroplasmosis. Despite this, little is known about the genetic diversity of this tick species, particularly in Cuba, where horses are important domestic animals used in agriculture and for transportation. The objective of this study was to assess the magnitude of genetic variation in 115 D. nitens adults (57 females and 58 males) collected from horses in the Cuban province of Mayabeque using sequence data of two mitochondrial genes, cytochrome c oxidase subunit 1 gene (cox1) and NADH dehydrogenase subunit 4 gene (nad4). The results revealed the presence of 7 cox1 haplotypes, 14 nad4 haplotypes, and 23 haplotypes with concatenation of the two genetic markers. However, the results of the rarefaction curve analyses predict the existence of other rare haplotypes in Mayabeque Province. The most common haplotype for each genetic marker was identical to a D. nitens from Brazil, Colombia, Panama, and French Guiana. The star-like haplotype networks, each with a central (ie “ancestral”) and most abundant haplotype, combined with the significant negative values for the Tajima’s D and Fu’s F tests, is consistent with a hypothesis of the introduction of D. nitens on horses from mainland South America (ie a founder effect) followed by demographic expansion. Additional population genetic studies on D. nitens are needed in other countries where this species occurs to examine the phylogeography of this tick species of socioeconomic importance.Keywords: tropical horse tick, population genetics, phylogeography, cox1, nad4

Introduction

The tropical horse tick, Dermacentor nitens Neumann, 1897, has a broad distribution in the Neotropics extending from northern Argentina to southern Mexico, and in the southern regions of Texas and Florida in the United States (Strickland and Gerrish 1964, Guglielmone et al. 2021). It also occurs on islands of the Caribbean and the Galápagos Archipelago (Diaz-Sánchez et al. 2021, Guglielmone et al. 2021). Although equids are the preferred host of this one-host tick, D. nitens is known to parasitize other domestic animals (eg cattle, goats, and sheep) and wildlife (eg deer), and occasionally humans (Strickland and Gerrish 1964, Borges and de Oliveira 2003, Guglielmone et al. 2021). The preferred attachment sites of D. nitens on horses are in the ears and the nasal diverticulum, on the ventral midline, mane and tail, and in the perineal and perianal regions (Strickland and Gerrish 1964, Labruna et al. 2002). Heavy infestations on horses lead to severe lesions in the ear, resulting in permanent cartilage damage, and predisposing the host to secondary bacterial infection and attacks by blowflies and screwworms (Strickland and Gerrish 1964). This tick species is also one of the vectors of the protozoan haemoparasites, Babesia caballi and Theileria equi, the etiological agents of equine piroplasmosis (Roby and Anthony 1963, Kerber et al. 2009, Peckle et al. 2013). Equine piroplasmosis is the most serious tick-borne disease affecting horses worldwide (Wise et al. 2013) and has a major impact on the international movement of horses (Knowles 1996). There are range of clinical symptoms associated with equine piroplasmosis that include fever, anemia, jaundice, hepatomegaly, splenomegaly, and hemoglobinuria (Wise et al. 2013). Infection in stallions can result in temporary or permanent infertility (Wise et al. 2013). Horses that recover from the disease are usually life-long carriers (ie reservoir hosts) of T. equi and B. caballi (Wise et al. 2013).

In Cuba, horses are among the most crucial working animals because of their roles in transportation and agriculture. Molecular analyses of blood samples taken from 100 horses in western Cuba showed that 73% were infected with T. equi and 25% were infected with B. caballi (Diaz-Sánchez et al. 2018). Mixed infections of the two haemoparasites were also detected in blood taken from horses (Diaz-Sánchez et al. 2018). Theileria equi and B. caballi have also been detected in D. nitens removed from horses in western Cuba (Diaz-Sánchez, unpublished data). Horses parasitized by D. nitens is common across several provinces in Cuba (Barros-Battesti et al. 2009, Diaz-Sánchez et al. 2021). It has been hypothesized that the origin of this tick species in Cuba resulted from the importation of horses from other geographical regions that were parasitized by D. nitens (Barros-Battesti et al. 2009). This may have occurred during the 16th century when the Spanish established colonies in Cuba. Despite the important role D. nitens plays as a vector of hemoprotozoan parasites B. caballi and T. equi, there has been no published population genetic or phylogenetic study on this tick species in Cuba, or in any part of its broad distributional range in the Neotropics.

Population genetic studies on ticks, such as D. nitens, provide important information on the magnitude of genetic variation within and among populations, dispersal patterns, reproductive strategies, gene flow between populations, and their phylogeography, which can provide valuable insights into the epidemiology of tick-borne diseases (Tabachnick and Black 1995, Gooding 1996, McCoy 2008, Araya-Anchetta et al. 2015). In this study, we assessed the level of genetic variation in D. nitens adults parasitizing horses in Mayabeque Province (western Cuba) using two mitochondrial (mt) protein-coding genes, cytochrome c oxidase subunit 1 gene (cox1), and NADH dehydrogenase subunit 4 gene (nad4).

Materials and Methods

Study Area and Tick Collection

Ticks were collected from horses in the Cuban province of Mayabeque as part of the national Equine Infectious Anemia (EIA) vaccination and surveillance campaign. This work was conducted between April and June of 2018, during the onset of the rainy season and the major peak of tick activity. During this campaign, horses were brought to one of six vaccination stations: El Perú (23°00′44′′N, 82°03′45′′W), Tapaste (23°01′24′′N, 82°08′06′′W), San Antonio de las Vegas (22°51′09′′N, 82°15′36′′W), San José de las Lajas (22°57′56′′N, 82°09′23′′W), Santa Bárbara (23°03′30′′N, 82°07′10′′W), and Zaragoza (22°56′00′′N, 82°02′09′′W; Fig. 1). Based on the Köppen climate classification, this geographical region has a tropical savanna climate with two marked climatic periods, a dry season from November to April with temperatures varying from 19 to 28 °C, and a rainy season from May to October with temperatures ranging from 23 to 32 °C. Mayabeque Province has an average elevation of 153.8 m above sea level, with a mean annual temperature of 27.7 °C, annual rainfall of 160.73 mm and a relative humidity above 75% (Fonseca et al. 2024).

A total of 115 D. nitens adults (57 females and 58 males) were removed from 68 horses at the six vaccination stations. Ticks were identified morphologically to species based on the taxonomic key of Barros-Battesti et al. (2009). Each tick was placed into a separate 1.5 ml sterile tube containing 70% ethanol and stored at −80 °C until required for molecular analyses.

DNA Extraction and PCR Amplification of Genetic Markers

Thawed ticks were rinsed three times in double-distilled water and dried on sterile filter paper. Each tick was excised in half using a sterile scalpel blade and transferred to a sterile MagNA Lyser tube containing ceramic beads. Phosphate-buffered saline (100 μl) was added to each tube prior to homogenization of the samples with a MagNA Lyser instrument (Roche Molecular Diagnostics, Rotkreuz, Switzerland). Total genomic DNA (gDNA) was extracted from each homogenate using a Wizard Genomic DNA Purification kit (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). Total gDNA was extracted from part of the body of each tick to study genetic variation in D. nitens (present study) and the bacterial pathogens within these ticks (Diaz-Sánchez, unpublished data).

Primers COI-F2 (5′-CTTAATTTTCGGCAGTTGAGCA-3′) and COI-R1 (5′-GTTCTTTTTTTCCTGTGGAAAAAC-3′) were used to amplify 743 bp of cox1 (Kaufman et al. 2018), while primers DnND4-F (5′-CACGTTCAGTAATATTAATACTTC-3′) and DnND4-R (5′-TATTATTTCAATTTGGGGTTCTG-3′) were used to amplify 509 bp of nad4. The sequences of the two nad4 primers were based on the sequence of the complete mt genome of D. nitens on GenBank (accession no. KC503258) (Burger et al. 2014). PCRs were carried out using 25 µl reaction volumes containing 13.5 μl of InvitrogenTM UltraPure DNase/RNase-free distilled water, 5 μl of 5×Phusion HF PCR buffer, 1 U Phusion High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, USA), 500 nM of each primer, 3 mM MgCl2, 200 µM of each deoxynucleoside triphosphate (dNTP) (New England Biolabs Inc., Pickering, ON, Canada), and 2 μl of tick gDNA. The Phusion High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase was used for the PCRs because of its proof-reading activity has an exceptional low error rate compared standard Taq polymerases. The amplification conditions used for cox1 were 98 °C for 3 min followed by 35 cycles of 98 °C for 10 s, 52 °C for 30 s, and 72C for 45 s, then, 72 °C for 7 min. The same amplification conditions were used for nad4 except the elongation time was reduced from 45 s to 30 s. Negative (ie no gDNA) controls were included in each PCR run. Amplicons were subjected to electrophoresis on SYBR-safe™ (Molecular Probes, Eugene, Oregon, United States) pre-stained 1.5% agarose-TBE (89 mM Tris, 89 mM boric acid, 2 mM EDTA, pH 8.3) gels. A 100-bp TrackIt™ DNA ladder (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) was used as a size marker. Amplicons were visualized using UV transillumination. An aliquot (10 μl) of each amplicon was purified by adding 1 μl of a mixture containing 0.1 U of Exonuclease I (New England Biolabs Inc.), 0.05 U of FastAP Thermosensitive Alkaline Phosphatase and 0.1 μl of 10× FastAP buffer (ThermoFisher Scientific). Samples were placed at 37 °C for 15 min, and then, at 80 °C for 15 min. Purified cox1 and nad4 amplicons were subjected to automated sequencing using the PCR primers (COI-F2 and COI-R1 for cox1, and DnND4-F and DnND4-R for nad4) in separate reactions.

Population Genetics Analyses

For this study, it was not possible to confirm that horses examined for ticks at each vaccinated station came from properties/farms located within a radius of approximately 20 km of that station. Given this, and that horses are frequently transported among different locations throughout Mayabeque Province, genetic variation in D. nitens was examined over a broad spatial scale (ie within the province).

DNA sequences were compared to those of the complete mitogenome of D. nitens (accession no. KC503258) and the cox1 and nad4 sequences of D. nitens on GenBank using BLASTn. Haplotype diversity (h) represents the probability that two randomly chosen individuals will not have the same haplotype, while nucleotide diversity (π) is interpreted as the average proportion of nucleotide differences per site between possible pairs of sequences (Nei and Kumar 2000). These two diversity indices along with the number of haplotypes, the number of segregating sites (s), and the number of synonymous and nonsynonymous mutations (θ), were determined for each gene using Arlequin version 3.5 (Excoffier and Lischer 2010). The D statistics of Tajima (1989) and the Fs statistics of Fu (1997) were also determined using Arlequin with bootstrap resampling (1,000 replicates). Significantly negative D and Fs values are indicative of population expansions, whereas significantly positive values are suggestive of population bottlenecks (Aris-Brosou and Excoffier 1996). The level of statistical significance was P < 0.02 for the Fu’s test (Fu 1997, Excoffier and Lischer 2010) and P < 0.05 for the Tajima’s tests Tajima (1989). A rarefaction curve, which provides an estimate of the expected number of haplotypes in an area (Colwell and Coddington 1994), was generated for each gene using iNEXT Online (https://chao.shinyapps.io/iNEXTOnline/) (Chao et al. 2016). As the sampling order affects the shape of the curve, the data set was randomized 1,000 times without replacement of haplotypes. A minimum spanning network tree depicting the relationships between haplotypes was also constructed for each gene using the program TCS (Clement et al. 2000).

Phylogenetic Analyses

Phylogenetic analyses were carried on an alignment of the concatenated cox1 and nad4 sequences using the neighbor-joining (NJ) and maximum parsimony (MP) methods in PAUP v4.0b2 (Swofford 2002). Included in the phylogenetic analyses were cox1 and nad4 sequences for eight other species of Dermacentor for which sequence data were available on GenBank. The cox1 and nad4 sequences of Rhipicephalus sanguineus were used as an outgroup in the analyses. For the MP analysis, a heuristic search was carried out with random addition of sequences (n = 100), tree-bisection-reconstruction (TBR) branch swapping, the MulTrees option in effect, MaxTrees set at 1,000 and saving all equally parsimonious trees. Characters were treated as unordered and were weighted equally. Bootstrap analyses (1,000 replicates) were conducted in the NJ and MP analyses to determine the relative statistical support of each clade in the trees, with nodal support expressed as a percentage.

Results

Population Genetics

The DNA sequences of cox1 (660 bp) were obtained for 113 ticks (56 females and 57 males). There were six variable sites in the aligned cox1 sequences (Table 1). These mutational changes represented four pyrimidine transitions and two transversions. Of the seven cox1 haplotypes, C1 was the most abundant, comprising 90 (80%) of 113 ticks. The next most common haplotype was C2 (n = 12). Two haplotypes (C6 and C7) were singletons (ie each represented by a single tick). The rarefaction curve produced from the analyses of the cox1 sequence data (Fig. 2) is beginning to reach an asymptote at 9 or 10 haplotypes. Therefore, in a sample size of 100 individuals, at least 70% of the predicted number of haplotypes have been detected in western Cuba. Negative values were obtained for both the Tajima’s D and Fu’s F tests (Table 2), but these values were not statistically different from zero. The minimum spanning network tree for the cox1 haplotypes (Fig. 3A), consisted of a star-like pattern with C1 in the center, to which the other six haplotypes were connected. Haplotypes C2-C7 differed from haplotype C1 by one or two nucleotide mutations in DNA sequence. Haplotype C1 was identical in sequence to the cox1 sequences of D. nitens from Brazil (GenBank accession no. KC503258), Colombia (accession no. MF363059), French Guiana (accession no. MH513243), and Panama (accession no. KF200121). The other six haplotypes (C2-C7) are “unique” or “new” in that they have not been previously reported in the scientific literature.

Phylogenetic Analyses

The phylogenetic tree produced from the NJ analysis of the concatenated cox1 and nad4 sequence data is shown in Fig. 4. For the MP analysis, 722 of the 1,112 positions in the aligned sequences of the 10 Dermacentor taxa were identical, whereas 326 of the 390 variable positions were phylogenetically informative. The topology of the strict consensus tree of the MP analysis (not shown), based on 52 equally most parsimonious trees (tree length = 991, consistency index excluding uninformative characters = 0.55, and retention index = 0.81), was very similar to the tree produced by the NJ analyses (Fig. 4). In both the NJ and MP analyses there was total support (ie 100% bootstrap support) for a clade containing the 23 haplotypes of D. nitens from western Cuba and D. nitens from Brazil. In contrast, the sequences of D. albipictus did not represent a monophyletic assemblage. There was also strong statistical support (ie bootstrap values of 97% and 93% for NJ and MP analyses, respectively) for a sister-taxa relationship between D. nitens and lineage 2 of D. albipictus. In addition, there was total statistical support (ie bootstrap values of 100% in the NJ and MP analyses) for a clade containing Dermacentor species from the Americas (ie D. albipictus, D. andersoni, D. nitens, and D. variabilis) to the exclusion of other species in the genus (ie D. everestianus, D. marginatus, D. nuttalli, D. reticulatus, and D. silvarum).

Discussion

The choice of appropriate genetic marker(s) for population genetic and phylogeographic studies of D. nitens is crucial because the rates of mutational change in DNA sequence differs among genes (Araya-Anchetta et al. 2015). The mt large subunit ribosomal RNA gene (16S rRNA) and/or cox1 have been used to determine the genetic diversity within other species of Dermacentor (eg de la Fuente et al. 2005, Patterson et al. 2009, Krakowetz et al. 2010, Leo et al. 2010, Kaufman et al. 2018, Ospina-Pérez et al. 2024, Chilton et al. 2025, Nichol et al. 2025, Orkun et al. 2025, Zhao et al. 2025). However, our preliminary analyses of the mt 16S rRNA gene sequences for D. nitens suggested limited genetic variation (Roblejo-Arias et al., unpublished data), as is the case for D. variabilis (Krakowetz et al. 2010, Kaufman et al. 2018, Chilton et al. 2025). Therefore, nad4 was selected as a second genetic marker for assessing genetic variation in D. nitens based on the magnitude of intraspecific variation in nad4 sequences for D. variabilis (Diaz-Sanchez et al., unpublished data).

Haplotype and nucleotide diversities among D. nitens adults were greater for nad4 than cox1, even when taking the length differences of the two genetic targets into account. For instance, there were 14 haplotypes for nad4 compared to only seven haplotypes for cox1. Also, genetic variation was detected at 14 of the 492 (3%) nucleotides in the DNA sequences of nad4 compared to only 6 of 660 (1%) nucleotides in the DNA sequences of cox1. Yi et al. (2019) also found more genetic variation in nad4 than cox1 among 35 Rhipicephalus microplus adults collected from cattle in four provinces of China. Therefore, nad4 sequence data may provide more insight into the phylogeography of D. nitens than analyses based on cox1 sequences. However, concatenation of sequence data from nad4 and cox1 resulted in 23 haplotypes among the 110 adult D. nitens sequenced for both genetic markers. Sequence data from multiple mt genes or the complete mitogenomes of individual ticks would provide a more comprehensive understanding of population genetics and phylogeography of D. nitens. However, this would also require detailed studies to be conducted on D. nitens from all other regions of the neotropics where this species occurs.

The results of the rarefaction curve analyses conducted in the present study indicate that the extent of the genetic diversity in both markers (ie number of haplotypes) for D. nitens from western Cuba may not have been fully established. The rarefaction curve for the cox1 approached saturation suggesting that most haplotypes present in western Cuba were detected. Sampling of an additional 100 ticks from this geographical region is predicted to reveal the presence of an additional 2 to 3 cox1 haplotypes. In contrast, the rarefaction curve for nad4 curve did not approach saturation with a sample size of 112 ticks, thus, the genetic diversity in D. nitens from Mayabeque Province needs to be explored further. The results of the rarefaction curve analyses predict the existence of at least an additional 11 nad4 haplotypes in western Cuba, and that at least 400 ticks need to be sampled to detect these additional haplotypes. This large sample size is needed to detect the many predicted rare haplotypes in Mayabeque Province. The concatenated cox1 and nad4 sequence data further highlights this, such that sampling of 400 ticks would result in the detection of 40 haplotypes, almost twice the number observed by sampling 110 individuals (ie 23 haplotypes). Similar results were obtained by Chilton et al. (2025) in their study of the genetic variation among D. variabilis adults from a population in southern Canada. In that study, 200 D. variabilis adults were more than sufficient to assess the magnitude of genetic diversity in the mt 16S rRNA gene for that population. In contrast, 33 cox1 haplotypes were detected among 200 D. variabilis individuals, 12 (36%) of which were singletons (Chilton et al. 2025). Increasing the sample size to 600 ticks was predicted to result in the detection of an estimated 45 cox1 haplotypes in that population (Chilton et al. 2025). Based on a comparison of the results for D. variabilis (Chilton et al. 2025) and D. nitens (present study), the extent of genetic diversity, hence the rate of mutational change, within a gene (ie cox1), can differ markedly among related species, and the sample size needed to detect all or a majority of genetic variants (haplotypes) within populations or a geographical region depends upon the genetic marker(s) used. In general, the larger the sample size, the greater the probability that rare haplotypes (ie those occurring at a low frequency) will be detected. Furthermore, the minimum spanning network trees, depicting the relationships of haplotypes of the mt 16S rRNA gene, cox1 and/or nad4 for D. nitens (present study), D. andersoni (Patterson et al. 2009), and D. variabilis (Krakowetz et al. 2010, Chilton et al. 2025), have the most common haplotype(s) positioned in the center of the haplotype trees.

The central haplotype in a minimum spanning network tree with a star-like pattern is generally assumed to represent the “ancestral haplotype.” The central haplotype in all three network trees (ie cox1, nad4, and cox1+nad4) of D. nitens from western Cuba was identical in sequence to a D. nitens from Brazil (Burger et al. 2014). Furthermore, the “ancestral” cox1 haplotype has also been detected in D. nitens from Panama, Colombia (Rivera-Páez et al. 2018, Ospina-Pérez et al. 2024) and French Guiana (Binetruy et al. 2019). This haplotype was also the most common in multiple samples of D. nitens from Colombia (Rivera-Páez et al. 2018). The star-like haplotype network trees for D. nitens in western Cuba, the presence of the “ancestral” haplotypes on the mainland of South America, and significant negative values for Tajima’s D and Fu’s Fs tests of the nad4 and concatenated sequence data, would support the hypothesis that D. nitens into Cuba was transported on horses from South America (ie a founder effect) and then undergone a recent demographic expansion. However, it is also possible that there has been little or no demographic expansion following the founder effect from the source population(s). In this scenario, all D. nitens haplotypes would have been introduced on horses into Cuba and have a relative frequency similar to those in populations of mainland South America. Hence, haplotype 1 would be the most common in D. nitens populations in South America and that rarer haplotypes in Cuba would occur at a low frequency in the source population(s). These hypotheses could be tested by conducting detailed ­population genetic analyses of D. nitens in South America and other islands of the Caribbean.

The introduction of D. nitens into Cuba, and when this tick species became established in different regions of the county remains undocumented. However, given that Cuba was a strategic shipping port during Spanish colonial rule, it is plausible that this tick species was introduced from mainland America with the importation of horses and other livestock (eg cattle) into Cuba for trade and agriculture. The tropical climate of Cuba provides favorable conditions for survival (ie engorged females and their eggs resulting unfed larvae) and development (ie for eggs) of this one-host tick species and increasing availability of hosts over time would have enhanced the dispersal, generation times, population sizes, and long-term stability of D. nitens in the country. Since D. nitens can successfully complete up to five generations per year in southeastern Brazil (Labruna and Faccini 2020), it is probable this tick species can also complete several generations each year in western Cuba with its tropical climate. This, combined with extensive equine movement across the western region of the Cuban archipelago facilitating tick dispersal, would account for the relatively high abundance and wide distribution of D. nitens across Mayabeque Province.

Recent phylogenetic studies (Reynolds et al. 2022, 2023, Ospina-Pérez et al. 2024) have shown that D. nitens belongs to a clade within the genus Dermacentor that only includes species from the Neotropics and the Nearctic. The results of our study, based on the phylogenetic trees produced from the NJ and MP analyses of the concatenated cox1 and nad4 sequence, data agree with this finding. This Neotropic/Nearctic clade includes another one-host tick, D. albipictus, the winter tick or moose tick. These two one-host tick species can be distinguished from D. variabilis, D. occidentalis and D. andersoni, three-host ticks in North America, based on differences in chromosome morphology (Gunn and Hilburn 1990). Crosbie et al. (1998) suggested that the one-host life cycle in the genus Dermacentor may have evolved only once. They tested this hypothesis using NJ and MP analyses of DNA sequence data of the mt 16S rRNA gene. Although D. nitens and D. albipictus formed a distinct cluster on both the NJ and MP trees, there was no statistical support, based on the bootstrap values, for a sister-taxa relationship of these two species (Crosbie et al. 1998). In our study, there was a sister-taxa relationship of D. nitens with D. albipictus. However, the winter tick has been shown previously to represent two genetically distinct mt DNA lineages (Leo et al. 2010, Reynolds et al. 2022). Our results revealed strong support for a sister-taxa relationship between D. nitens and lineage 2 of D. albipictus, but no statistical support for this relationship with lineage 1 of D. albipictus. All other species of Dermacentor included in the phylogenetic analyses, all of which are three-host species, were positioned on branches external to D. nitens and D. albipictus. The same tree topology was obtained by Reynolds et al. (2022) in their analyses of the DNA sequences of the mt 16S rRNA gene and cox1. Our results also demonstrated strong support for a single clade containing all of D. nitens haplotypes from western Cuba. This contrasts with the findings of Rivera-Páez et al. (2018) and Ospina-Pérez et al. (2024) who reported the existence of two clades (or lineages) of D. nitens in different parts of Colombia (ie the Amazon and Orinoquia regions) based on sequence data of the mt 16S rRNA gene and cox1. Ospina-Pérez et al. (2024) also demonstrated that the two lineages represent different morphotypes, suggesting the possiblity of cryptic species. All seven cox1 haplotypes of D. nitens detected in western Cuba fall within the lineage of the Orinoquia region, that also includes D. nitens from Brazil, Panama and French Guiana. There is no evidence that the D. nitens lineage from the Amazon region of Colombia is present in western Cuba. These results further support the importation of D. nitens into Cuba from a specific region in the Neotropics. A more detailed phylogeographic study is needed to infer the geographical origin of D. nitens in Cuba. This would require extensive population genetic studies of D. nitens in countries where this tick species occurs.

In conclusion, this study presents the first population genetic study of D. nitens in Cuba. DNA sequences of cox1 and nad4 can be used to examine the population genetics, phylogeography, and evolutionary relationships of tick species of socioeconomic importance. The results showed that there was greater haplotype and nucleotide diversities for nad4 than cox1. Although cox1 is often used as a valuable genetic marker for population genetic studies on ticks, it remains to be determined if there is a greater level of intraspecific variation in the DNA sequence of nad4 than in cox1 for other tick species, including those in the genus Dermacentor, and thus, provides a useful genetic maker to examine their phylogeographic patterns. The haplotypes networks depicting the relationships of D. nitens in the Cuban Province of Mayabeque based on analyses of cox1, nad4 and the concatenated data, each had a star-shaped pattern with one central (ie “ancestral”) haplotype. This combined with the presence of several rare haplotypes and the significant negative values for the Tajima’s D and Fu’s Fs tests, is consistent with either a founder effect of D. nitens in Cuba followed by recent population expansion or the introduction of D. nitens haplotypes into Cuba from mainland South America, the frequencies of which are similar to those in the source population(s). Knowledge of the genetic diversity of D. nitens may have broader implications for vector ecology, tick-borne disease control, and evolutionary adaptation in tick populations throughout the Neotropics.
