Explainable Cross-Disease Reasoning for Cardiovascular Risk Assessment from LDCT
Yifei Zhang 1, Jiashuo Zhang 2, Mojtaba Safari 3, Xiaofeng Yang 3, Liang Zhao 1,∗

Abstract
Abstract Low-dose chest computed tomography (LDCT) inherently captures both pulmonary and cardiac structures, offering a unique opportunity for joint assessment of lung and cardiovascular health. However, most existing approaches treat these domains as independent tasks, overlooking their physiological interplay and shared imaging biomarkers. We propose an Explainable Cross-Disease Reasoning Framework that enables interpretable cardiopulmonary risk assessment from a single LDCT scan. The framework introduces an agentic reasoning process that emulates clinical diagnostic thinking; first perceiving pulmonary findings, then reasoning through established medical knowledge, and finally deriving a cardiovascular judgment with explanatory rationale. It integrates three synergistic components: a pulmonary perception module that summarizes lung abnormalities, a knowledge-guided reasoning module that infers their cardiovascular implications, and a cardiac representation module that encodes structural biomarkers. Their outputs are fused to produce a holistic cardiovascular risk prediction that is both accurate and physiologically grounded. Experiments on the NLST cohort demonstrate that the proposed framework achieves state-of-the-art performance for CVD screening (AUC=0.919) and mortality prediction (AUC=0.838), outperforming single-disease and purely image-based baselines. Beyond quantitative gains, the framework provides human-verifiable reasoning that aligns with cardiological understanding, revealing coherent links between pulmonary abnormalities and cardiac stress mechanisms. Overall, this work establishes a unified and explainable paradigm for cardiovascular analysis from LDCT, bridging the gap between image-based prediction and mechanism-based medical interpretation.

Lung cancer and cardiovascular disease (CVD) are the two leading causes of death worldwide, jointly accounting for more than one-third of annual mortality. In 2022, CVDs were responsible for approximately 32% of global deaths—over 19 million fatalities—while lung cancer remained the leading cause of cancer-related mortality with about 1.8 million deaths
32

7

22

Low-dose chest computed tomography (LDCT) has become the standard of care for lung cancer screening, with the NLST demonstrating a 20% reduction in lung cancer–specific mortality compared to chest radiography. Subsequent randomized trials, including NELSON and UKLS, and multiple meta-analyses have consistently confirmed this survival benefit
28
;
8
;
9
. Beyond its oncologic utility, LDCT also visualizes cardiothoracic structures such as the coronary arteries, pericardial fat, and thoracic aorta, all of which carry prognostic significance for cardiovascular outcomes. Observational studies from large screening cohorts have underscored this dual potential: in the MILD trial, individuals with coronary artery calcium (CAC)
>400
exhibited nearly 17% 12-year all-cause mortality versus 3.2% for those with CAC
=0
(HR
\sim
3.8)
23
; in BioMILD, both CAC
\geq 400
and quantitative emphysema independently predicted CVD mortality (HR
\sim
4.7)
2
; and in ITALUNG, emphysematous changes were linked to long-term CVD mortality even after adjusting for CAC and traditional risk factors
18
. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that a single LDCT scan provides an underexploited opportunity for dual-purpose screening—capturing shared imaging biomarkers of both pulmonary and cardiovascular disease, as illustrated in Fig.
1
.

Nevertheless, several key challenges hinder this potential. First, most prior research has modeled lung cancer detection and CVD risk stratification as independent tasks, overlooking their shared risk factors and correlated imaging biomarkers. Second, reported discrimination for CVD mortality from LDCT-derived measures has remained only moderate (AUCs
\sim

27

36
, they primarily focus on single-organ or single-disease reasoning and do not model the integrated cardiopulmonary pathway. Finally, current deep learning methods often operate as black boxes

, limiting interpretability and reducing clinical trust. These limitations constrain predictive accuracy and clinical applicability, underscoring the need for a unified and interpretable framework for joint cardiopulmonary analysis.

To address these challenges, we propose an
Explainable Cross-Disease Reasoning Framework
for comprehensive
cardiopulmonary risk assessment
from a single low-dose chest CT (LDCT). The framework integrates three coordinated branches that emulate the workflow of clinical reasoning. A
Pulmonary Perception Module
summarizes thoracic abnormalities from LDCT scans into structured findings. An
Agentic Pulmonary-to-Cardiac Reasoning Module
leverages medical knowledge to infer how these lung-derived abnormalities may propagate into cardiovascular stress, generating causal narratives that bridge pulmonary pathology and cardiac physiology. A
Cardiac Localization Module
extracts localized cardiac subvolumes to capture structural biomarkers associated with cardiovascular outcomes. Finally, a
Multimodal Cardiovascular Risk Assessment Module
fuses reasoning-derived textual representations with imaging-based features to produce physiologically grounded cardiovascular risk predictions. Together, these components form a unified, explainable reasoning architecture that not only predicts outcomes but also elucidates the causal pathways linking lung pathology to cardiovascular vulnerability.

Comprehensive experiments on the NLST cohort demonstrate the effectiveness and robustness of the proposed framework. Compared with state-of-the-art baselines such as Sybil, DeepCAC, and M3FM, our method achieves superior discrimination for CVD screening (AUC 0.919) and mortality prediction (AUC 0.838), with consistent gains across multiple 3D-MedicalNet backbones. Ablation analyses confirm that each module—cardiac localization, pulmonary-to-cardiac reasoning, and multimodal fusion—contributes complementary information, leading to monotonic improvements in sensitivity and specificity. Beyond quantitative performance, qualitative visualizations using Grad-CAM and textual attention further reveal how the framework grounds its predictions in interpretable physiological patterns, highlighting coherent associations between pulmonary findings (e.g., fibrosis, effusion) and cardiac features (e.g., ventricular geometry, pericardial fat). Together, these results establish a principled, explainable paradigm for leveraging LDCT as a dual-purpose imaging modality that unifies accurate prediction with mechanistic understanding of cardiopulmonary disease risk.

2
Related Work

2.1
Lung Cancer Risk Prediction from LDCT

Low-dose chest CT (LDCT) has been firmly established as the most effective modality for lung cancer screening, with large randomized trials such as the NLST, NELSON, and UKLS demonstrating significant reductions in lung cancer mortality
28
;
8
;
9
. This clinical evidence has motivated extensive efforts to develop automated algorithms for lung cancer risk prediction
29
. Early approaches followed a two-stage paradigm, first detecting pulmonary nodules and then estimating malignancy risk using handcrafted or learned features
25
;
19
. With the advent of deep learning, convolutional architectures trained on large-scale datasets such as LIDC-IDRI and LUNA16 achieved robust sensitivity for nodule detection and characterization
1
;
26
. Subsequent studies extended these methods to longitudinal risk estimation, culminating in end-to-end frameworks such as
Sybil
, which directly predicts 1–6 year cancer incidence from volumetric LDCT and has demonstrated strong external generalization across multiple cohorts
20
. Recent benchmarking work further compared 2D and 3D architectures, reporting AUCs ranging from 0.62 to 0.86, with 3D models generally outperforming their 2D counterparts
15
. In parallel, deep learning systems have been validated for incidental nodule detection in non-screening CT populations, underscoring their potential beyond trial settings
13
. Despite these advances, most methods remain narrowly optimized for lung cancer outcomes, leaving the broader cardiothoracic information embedded in LDCT underutilized.

2.2
Cardiovascular Risk Assessment from LDCT

In addition to pulmonary findings, LDCT scans inherently capture the heart and thoracic vasculature, providing an opportunity for opportunistic cardiovascular risk assessment. Traditional analyses focused on quantifying coronary artery calcium (CAC), thoracic aortic calcification, and pericardial fat, all of which are well-established biomarkers of cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk
7
;
22
;
10
. In screening cohorts, CAC burden has consistently been shown to predict all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, often rivaling or exceeding lung cancer as a cause of death
14
;
8
. Building on this foundation, recent deep learning approaches have automated calcium scoring and extended LDCT interpretation toward direct outcome prediction. Examples include DeepCAC
34
, KAMP-Net
11
, and related frameworks for fully automated CAC quantification
16
, which report moderate discrimination (AUC
\sim
0.65–0.72) for cardiovascular mortality in the NLST. Beyond coronary calcification, prospective cohort studies such as MILD, BioMILD, and ITALUNG have highlighted the additional prognostic value of emphysema extent, muscle mass, and fat attenuation as systemic markers of cardiometabolic health
23
;
2
;
18
. Nevertheless, the majority of existing approaches focus primarily on cardiac structures and do not fully integrate pulmonary features that co-vary with cardiovascular outcomes, limiting their ability to capture the multifactorial nature of disease risk in LDCT.

2.3
Multitask and Cross-Disease Modeling from LDCT

Lung cancer and CVD have often been modeled separately, yet their co-occurrence and shared risk factors motivate unified approaches. Early work such as Tri2D-Net demonstrated that LDCT encodes both pulmonary and cardiac signals by jointly leveraging coronary calcium and pericardial fat to predict cardiovascular mortality in the NLST, with performance comparable to dedicated calcium scoring
4
. These findings underscored the potential of cross-disease learning directly from screening scans. More recently, large-scale foundation models have extended this paradigm. M3FM was trained on over 120,000 CT scans with paired reports and achieved strong performance across lung cancer risk prediction, coronary artery disease classification, and CVD mortality
21
. Merlin combined volumetric CT with EHR codes and reports to support hundreds of downstream tasks
3
, while Hamamci et al. introduced a generalist 3D CT foundation model that integrates CT-CLIP and CT-CHAT, aligning CT images with radiology text for zero-shot pulmonary finding recognition and enabling instruction-driven report generation
12
. Related efforts such as MedCLIP
31
, BioMedCLIP
35
, RadFM
33
, and CheXagent
6
illustrate the broader trend toward vision–language pretraining in radiology, though most remain focused on 2D chest radiographs. Together, these models highlight different emphases: M3FM on multi-task clinical endpoints, Merlin on CT–EHR integration, and Hamamci et al. on multimodal alignment with radiology text.

Despite their breadth, existing multitask systems largely function as black boxes. Post-hoc saliency or attention maps provide only coarse attribution, limiting clinical trust. In contrast, indicator-based reasoning seeks to expose intermediate states (e.g., emphysema, fibrosis, pericardial fat) and link them explicitly to outcomes. Our framework builds on these trends by unifying lung cancer and CVD risk prediction from LDCT while externalizing reasoning through intermediate indicators, thereby enhancing transparency and clinical utility.

Methodology

3.1
Overview of the Explainable Cross-Disease Reasoning Framework

(iii) the

The proposed framework transforms a single low-dose chest CT (LDCT) volume
X\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times W\times D}
into an interpretable representation of cardiopulmonary health that is used to predict a
single
clinically relevant outcome: the probability of cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk
\hat{y}_{\text{cvd}}\in[0,1]
. Rather than learning a separate lung-cancer outcome, our approach leverages pulmonary information—structured lung findings, knowledge-guided natural-language reasoning (CVD judgment with rationale), and an optional pretrained malignancy prior—as contextual signals that inform the downstream CVD predictor.

As illustrated in Fig.
2
, the overall architecture comprises three coordinated components that correspond to distinct yet complementary sources of information: a
pulmonary perception module
, an
agentic pulmonary-to-cardiac reasoning module
, and a
cardiac representation module
. Together, these components enable integrated and explainable CVD risk prediction by jointly modeling the physiological interplay between pulmonary abnormalities and cardiovascular health.

3.1.1
Agentic Pulmonary-to-Cardiac Reasoning.

The core innovation of this framework is the
Agentic Pulmonary-to-Cardiac Reasoner
(§
3.2
), which performs knowledge-guided reasoning to connect pulmonary abnormalities with cardiovascular outcomes. Given a structured set of lung findings
R_{\text{lung findings}}
extracted from LDCT, the reasoner generates a concise textual explanation that describes how the detected pulmonary patterns may contribute to cardiovascular dysfunction:

\mathcal{T}_{\text{cross}}:\;R_{\text{lung findings}}\;\Rightarrow\;E_{\text{CVD judgment + rationale}},

where
E_{\text{CVD judgment + rationale}}
denotes a natural-language report combining both a CVD judgment and its explanatory rationale.

3.1.2
Pulmonary Representation and Lung Cancer Risk.

The
Lung Cancer Risk Predictor
(§
3.3
) provides a complementary pulmonary representation that reflects long-term exposure and comorbidity risk. From the LDCT input
X
, it estimates temporal malignancy probabilities:

z_{\text{lung}}=\mathcal{F}_{\text{lung}}(X;\theta_{\text{l}}),\quad z_{\text{lung}}=(\hat{y}^{(1)}_{\text{lung}},\ldots,\hat{y}^{(T)}_{\text{lung}})\in[0,1]^{T}.

This pretrained module remains frozen during training and serves as an auxiliary indicator that contextualizes the relationship between pulmonary condition and cardiovascular vulnerability.

3.1.3
Cardiac Region Extraction.

The
Cardiac Feature Extractor
(§
3.4
) isolates a compact subvolume centered on the heart to capture localized anatomic features relevant to cardiovascular status:

X_{\text{card}}=\mathcal{D}_{\text{card}}(X;\theta_{\text{d}})\in\mathbb{R}^{h\times w\times d}.

This extracted cardiac region provides focused structural information—such as coronary calcification, chamber morphology, and pericardial fat—that complements the pulmonary and reasoning signals.

3.1.4
Multimodal Cardiovascular Risk Assessment.

The outputs of the three modules—reasoning text
E_{\text{CVD judgment + rationale}}
, pulmonary risk
z_{\text{lung}}
, and localized cardiac volume
X_{\text{card}}
—are integrated for end-to-end cardiovascular prediction. The textual reasoning
E
is encoded into a semantic embedding:

z_{\text{reason}}=\mathcal{E}_{\text{text}}(E;\theta_{\text{e}})\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{reason}}},

and the cardiac subvolume is processed through a 3D CNN encoder:

z_{\text{card}}=\mathcal{F}_{\text{card}}(X_{\text{card}};\theta_{\text{c}})\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{card}}}.

These modality-specific embeddings are concatenated to form a unified latent representation:

z_{\text{fusion}}=[\,z_{\text{card}};z_{\text{reason}};z_{\text{lung}}\,],\quad\hat{y}_{\text{cvd}}=\sigma\big(\mathcal{G}(z_{\text{fusion}};\theta_{\text{g}})\big),

(1)

where
\sigma(\cdot)
denotes the sigmoid activation for binary CVD risk estimation.
The fusion head and cardiac encoder are trained jointly using binary cross-entropy:

\mathcal{L}=-\big[y_{\text{cvd}}\log(\hat{y}_{\text{cvd}})+(1-y_{\text{cvd}})\log(1-\hat{y}_{\text{cvd}})\big].

(2)

In summary, the framework forms a unified reasoning pathway from pulmonary observation to cardiovascular risk interpretation.
By combining frozen pretrained modules that encode medical priors with trainable components specialized for CVD prediction, the model achieves both interpretability and robust performance in cardiopulmonary risk assessment from LDCT.

A central novelty of our framework lies in its
agentic pulmonary-to-cardiac reasoning
capability, which enables the model to perform structured, multi-agent inference linking lung observations to cardiovascular outcomes.
As illustrated in Fig.
3
, the framework decomposes this reasoning process into three cooperative agents that emulate clinical cognition:
a
Pulmonary Perception Agent
that identifies key thoracic abnormalities from LDCT,
a
Knowledge Reasoning Agent
that recalls and organizes medical knowledge describing the physiological interactions between lung and heart,
and a
CVD Diagnostic Reasoning Agent
that integrates both to derive a cardiovascular judgment and explanatory rationale.
Through this perception–knowledge–reasoning collaboration, the model not only predicts cardiovascular risk but also explains
why

1) Pulmonary Perception Agent.

The reasoning begins with a
pulmonary perception agent
that summarizes observable thoracic abnormalities from LDCT into structured findings:

R=\{(r_{i},s_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N},\quad s_{i}\in[0,1],

(3)

where each
r_{i}
denotes a clinically recognized abnormality (e.g., opacity, fibrosis, emphysema) and
s_{i}
its confidence score.
This agent functions analogously to a radiologist identifying key lung findings from volumetric CT data.
Findings with
s_{i}\geq 0.5
are retained to form a concise pulmonary report
R_{\text{lung findings}}
that serves as the factual basis for subsequent reasoning.

2) Knowledge Reasoning Agent.

Building on the extracted pulmonary report, the
knowledge reasoning agent
interprets and organizes domain knowledge regarding the physiological interplay between the lung and the heart. Instead of relying on static rules, the agent dynamically analyzes established cardiopulmonary mechanisms—such as

“emphysema
\rightarrow
hypoxemia
\rightarrow
pulmonary hypertension”
and

“fibrosis
\rightarrow
chronic inflammation
\rightarrow
endothelial injury”
—to identify relevant causal pathways.
Through this process, it constructs a contextual medical understanding that links pulmonary pathology to cardiovascular function, forming a knowledge-grounded context that conditions downstream diagnostic reasoning.

3) CVD Diagnostic Reasoning Agent.

Conditioned on both the structured pulmonary findings and the knowledge-derived context, a
CVD diagnostic reasoning agent
performs clinical-style inference to derive an individualized cardiovascular interpretation. This agent integrates the inferred physiological mechanisms, their cardiovascular manifestations, and a final diagnostic conclusion with explanatory rationale. A representative reasoning output is:

“Pleural effusion and fibrotic changes reduce pulmonary compliance and oxygen exchange, leading to chronic hypoxemia and hemodynamic overload that elevate cardiovascular risk.”

The overall reasoning pipeline can thus be formulated as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\text{cross}}:\quad R_{\text{lung findings}}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\;\text{(knowledge context)}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\;E_{\text{CVD judgment + rationale}},

where
R_{\text{lung findings}}
represents the structured pulmonary input, and
E_{\text{CVD judgment + rationale}}
denotes the generated diagnostic report encompassing both the cardiovascular assessment and its causal explanation. This multi-agent reasoning design mirrors the cognitive process of clinical experts—first perceiving, then recalling knowledge, and finally reasoning through evidence—thereby achieving physiologically faithful and interpretable cross-disease inference. 3.3
Lung Cancer Risk Analysis

The first module of our framework estimates lung cancer risk directly from a low-dose chest CT (LDCT), reflecting the pivotal role of LDCT in large-scale screening programs. Early identification of malignancy risk is clinically critical, as most lung cancers are detected at advanced stages when therapeutic options are limited. Integrating a validated malignancy predictor at the entry point of our framework provides both a clinically meaningful signal and a reliable foundation for downstream cardiopulmonary risk modeling.

Given an LDCT volume
X\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times W\times D}
, the
Lung Risk Estimator
produces a malignancy probability:

\hat{y}_{\text{lung}}=\mathcal{F}_{\text{lung}}(X;\theta_{\text{l}}),\quad\hat{y}_{\text{lung}}\in[0,1],

(4)

where
\hat{y}_{\text{lung}}
denotes the predicted likelihood of developing lung cancer within a specified temporal horizon (e.g., 1–6 years).
Optionally, a temporal trajectory
\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{\text{lung}}=(\hat{y}^{(1)},\dots,\hat{y}^{(T)})
may be estimated to represent longitudinal malignancy trends, which can be aggregated or directly integrated into downstream survival-style modeling.

By analyzing the entire volumetric context rather than only visible nodules, this module captures subtle imaging biomarkers that precede clinical detection.
Within our framework,
\hat{y}_{\text{lung}}
serves as a complementary prognostic cue for cardiovascular assessment. Epidemiological evidence has shown that individuals with elevated lung cancer risk—often due to chronic smoking and systemic inflammation—exhibit heightened cardiovascular vulnerability. Conditioning the cardiovascular predictor on
\hat{y}_{\text{lung}}
enables the model to incorporate comorbidity-related context, thereby linking malignancy-associated pathways to downstream cardiovascular outcomes and reinforcing the physiological coherence of our cross-disease reasoning paradigm.

3.4
Cardiac Region Extraction

The second module localizes the cardiac region from the full LDCT volume to enable focused cardiovascular feature learning. Applying a 3D backbone directly to the entire chest CT is computationally intensive and confounded by non-cardiac structures such as ribs and lungs. To address this, a dedicated
Cardiac Localization Module
identifies a compact region of interest (ROI) centered on the heart, ensuring that subsequent feature extraction is anatomically specific and computationally efficient. Formally, given an LDCT scan
X\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times W\times D}
, the localization process yields:

X_{\text{card}}=\mathcal{D}_{\text{card}}(X;\theta_{\text{d}}),\quad X_{\text{card}}\in\mathbb{R}^{h\times w\times d},

(5)

where
(h,w,d)\l(H,W,D)
. This subvolume retains the anatomical neighborhood of the heart while discarding irrelevant thoracic regions.

The extracted ROI substantially reduces computational cost and enhances specificity by focusing on morphological biomarkers directly linked to cardiovascular outcomes, such as chamber geometry, coronary calcification, and pericardial fat distribution. The entire process is fully automated and requires no manual annotations, allowing efficient large-cohort processing. The resulting
X_{\text{card}}
serves as the cardiac input for multimodal fusion in the cardiovascular risk assessment stage, complementing the pulmonary and reasoning representations to achieve physiologically grounded cardiopulmonary assessment.

4
Experiments

4.1
Experimental Setup

4.1.1
Dataset and Preprocessing

All experiments were conducted on the
National Lung Screening Trial (NLST)
dataset
28
, the largest randomized study comparing low-dose chest CT (LDCT) with radiography for lung cancer screening in high-risk populations. We included only LDCT examinations and excluded cases with missing metadata, incomplete follow-up, or corrupted DICOM files. After quality control and preprocessing, the final cohort comprised
32,136 LDCT scans from 10,395 subjects
.
Each participant underwent one to three annual scans reconstructed with multiple scanners and kernels, yielding a heterogeneous but clinically realistic dataset.

All imaging data were stored as Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) series and standardized for consistent representation across modules.
Raw LDCT volumes were rescaled and intensity-normalized following the Sybil preprocessing protocol. For the reasoning module, scans were resampled to isotropic voxel spacing and converted to NumPy arrays to enable CT-CLIP–based pulmonary report generation. For the cardiac extraction stage, DICOM series were converted to NIfTI format, clipped to a Hounsfield Unit range of
[-1{,}000,1{,}000]
, normalized to standard intensity distributions, and resampled while preserving anatomical orientation.

Two cardiovascular prediction tasks were defined following prior work
4
: (1)
CVD screening
, identifying subjects who developed cardiovascular events (e.g., myocardial infarction, stroke, coronary artery disease) during follow-up; and (2)
CVD mortality
, identifying deaths primarily attributed to cardiovascular causes based on ICD-9 codes. The screening task included
6,339 positives
and
25,797 negatives
, while the mortality task included
1,090 cardiovascular deaths
and
31,046 non-CVD or censored cases
. Data were split by subject into 70% training, 10% validation, and 20% test sets. This large-scale and heterogeneous cohort provides a robust foundation for evaluating the predictive performance and interpretability of the proposed cross-disease reasoning framework.

4.1.2
Comparison Methods

We conducted comprehensive comparisons to evaluate both the discriminative performance and robustness of our framework. First, we benchmarked against representative state-of-the-art LDCT-based methods, including classical handcrafted pipelines (AE+SVM),
CVD-specific CNN models (DeepCAC
34
, KAMP-Net
11
, Tri2D-Net
4
), and recent foundation or multimodal models (CT-CLIP
12
, Sybil
20
, M3FM
21
). This comparison situates our approach across the evolution from image-only and handcrafted systems to modern multimodal reasoning models. Second, to assess robustness, we implemented our framework on multiple MedicalNet backbones
5
(3D ResNet-50/101/152/200) and evaluated progressively integrated components—lung risk, cardiac localization, pulmonary-to-cardiac reasoning, and multimodal fusion. Finally, we performed component-wise and interpretability analyses by isolating individual modalities (Sybil risk, reasoning text, and cardiac embedding) and visualizing their contributions via Grad-CAM and textual attribution, verifying that each module contributes complementary and physiologically consistent information.

4.1.3
Evaluation Metrics

Model performance was evaluated using the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve and the corresponding area under the curve (AUC), which quantify overall discriminative ability across decision thresholds. For each task, 95% confidence intervals (CIs) of the AUC were estimated via non-parametric bootstrapping with 1,000 resamples.
ROC curves are presented for visual comparison, illustrating sensitivity–specificity trade-offs across competing methods.

4.1.4
Implementation Details

The
Lung Risk Estimator
was implemented using the pretrained
Sybil

20
model, which predicts future malignancy probability directly from LDCT volumes. It was applied in frozen inference mode to generate the malignancy likelihood
\hat{y}_{\text{lung}}
, serving as a stable comorbidity cue for cardiovascular prediction. The
Cardiac Localization Module
followed a
3D RetinaNet
-based detector
4
to automatically identify a heart-centered region of interest (ROI) from each LDCT scan. The resulting cropped subvolume
X_{\text{card}}
(
128^{3}
voxels) was used for downstream cardiac encoding. Within the
Agentic Pulmonary-to-Cardiac Reasoning Module
, three cooperating agents were instantiated. The
Pulmonary Perception Agent
used the pretrained
CT-CLIP

12
model to summarize thoracic abnormalities into structured pulmonary findings. The
Knowledge Reasoning Agent
employed
GPT-DeepResearch
to recall medical knowledge and infer intermediate cardiopulmonary mechanisms, while the
CVD Diagnostic Reasoning Agent
used
GPT-5-nano
to synthesize final textual explanations describing cardiovascular implications. Both operated in zero-shot inference mode without fine-tuning. Generated reasoning text was encoded using
ClinicalBERT

30
to obtain
z_{\text{reason}}
, which was jointly fine-tuned with the downstream fusion network. Cardiac feature extraction adopted MedicalNet
5
(3D ResNet-50/101/152/200) initialized from Med3D pretraining. All trainable modules—the 3D CNN encoder, ClinicalBERT text encoder, and MLP fusion head—were optimized end-to-end using Adam (
1\times 10^{-4}
base LR,
1\times 10^{-5}
weight decay) with cosine annealing, gradient clipping (norm 1.0), and binary cross-entropy loss. Training used early stopping on validation AUC with patience 10 epochs on one NVIDIA A100 (80 GB).

5
Conclusion

We proposed an
Explainable Cross-Disease Reasoning Framework
for integrated assessment of pulmonary and cardiovascular risk from low-dose chest CT (LDCT). The framework combines pulmonary-to-cardiac reasoning, localized cardiac feature encoding, and multimodal fusion to model the physiological interactions between the lung and heart. Extensive evaluation on a large-scale LDCT cohort shows consistent improvements over existing approaches while maintaining strong interpretability. Beyond predictive accuracy, the reasoning architecture provides clinically coherent explanations that clarify how pulmonary abnormalities contribute to cardiovascular stress, enhancing transparency aligned with medical reasoning. More broadly, this study highlights the potential of opportunistic LDCT analysis, which enables concurrent evaluation of multiple disease risks from a single scan, and establishes a foundation for transparent, generalizable, and physiologically grounded AI systems that can support future cardiopulmonary screening and longitudinal health monitoring.
