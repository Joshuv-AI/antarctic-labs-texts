Sex- and fiber type-specific remodeling of the muscle proteome implicates CSRP3 in resistance training-induced hypertrophy
Authors: Lukas Moesgaard, Roger Moreno-Justicia, Johann Schmalbruch, Søren Jessen, Ben Stocks, Nicoline R. Andersen, Jens J. Nielsen, Caio Y. Yonamine, Jonas T. Treebak, Jens Bangsbo, Clara Prats, Lykke Sylow, Atul S. Deshmukh, Morten Hostrup
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Skeletal muscle hypertrophy is a hallmark of resistance training that improves health and longevity. However, despite physiological differences between sexes and fiber types, the underlying proteome changes with resistance training have not been studied in a sex- and fiber type-specific manner. Herein, we show sex- and fiber type-specific remodeling of the human skeletal muscle proteome following 8 weeks of resistance training. Type II fibers exhibited much greater adaptations across both sexes, whereas the main sex difference was a greater remodeling of intermediate filaments in females. Of proteins that were specifically upregulated in type II fibers, overexpression of CSRP3 increased muscle mass in mice, indicating a role of fiber type-specific upregulation of CSRP3 in muscle hypertrophy. In addition, abundance of proteins involved in translation correlated with fiber hypertrophy and differed between sexes and fiber types. These findings demonstrate sex- and fiber type differences in muscle physiology and their contributions to resistance training-induced adaptions and implicate involvement of CSRP3 in resistance training-induced muscle hypertrophy.

Introduction

Skeletal muscle function is essential for health and longevity. Greater muscle strength is associated with a reduced risk of all-cause mortality, with this association even stronger in females than males1. Resistance exercise training is a key intervention for maintaining health and longevity, via increased muscle mass and strength, as well as improved metabolic health2–4. However, the underlying molecular remodeling in response to resistance training and potential sexual dimorphisms remains incompletely described.

Heterogeneous in nature, skeletal muscle is composed of both type I fibers, which have slow contractile properties and are more oxidative, and type II fibers, which have fast contractile properties and are more glycolytic5. Notably, this mixture of fiber types affects the response to resistance training, as greater muscle hypertrophy occurs in type II fibers than type I fibers6–8. Furthermore, we recently demonstrated sex-specific resistance training-induced hypertrophy of type I fibers9. Thus, our understanding of skeletal muscle adaptations to training can greatly benefit from research in both sexes. Unfortunately, the number of studies in biological research that only include male participants outnumber the number of studies that include females by a large margin10.

Studying skeletal muscle adaptations in a fiber type-specific manner is challenging11, due to the need to isolate and identify specific fiber types. Studies that have investigated the fiber type-specific response to resistance training have used targeted approaches, such as immunohistochemistry12 or western blotting13, to investigate single proteins of interest. Naturally, this must be seen as a limitation, as muscle adaptations are complex, and single proteins are unlikely to be representative of the changes occurring in the entire muscle proteome. By quantifying several thousands of proteins in a single run, the application of mass spectrometry-based proteomics to study resistance training can disentangle complex molecular adaptations with unprecedented resolution. Previous studies that have used this approach to explore resistance training-induced adaptations in bulk muscle tissue have shown that resistance training induces changes in both myofibrillar and non-myofibrillar fractions of the proteome14, and that these adaptations differ between young and old individuals15,16. Despite this, cross-sectional studies have also shown only a few differences in the human skeletal muscle transcriptome17 and proteome18 of resistance-trained individuals compared to untrained individuals, bringing into question the degree of muscle remodeling with this exercise modality. Importantly however, these findings in bulk muscle may not be reflective of changes at the muscle fiber level, given potential differences between fiber types. To elucidate this, we recently developed a muscle fiber proteomics workflow and subsequently used this workflow to identify fiber type differences in the skeletal muscle proteome and fiber type-specific adaptations to both endurance19 and resistance training20. However, as these studies only included male participants, we were unable to decipher how fiber type differences and adaptations to training differ between sexes. Thus, given the importance of muscle fiber types for skeletal muscle adaptations and potential sexual dimorphisms of these fiber types, this warrants a sex- and fiber type-specific investigation.

In this study, we applied our recently developed muscle fiber proteomics workflow19 to investigate sex- and fiber type-specific effects of resistance training. Our analysis revealed that proteome remodeling occurred primarily in type II fibers and that intermediate filament protein abundance primarily increased in females. Furthermore, we discovered differences between sexes and fiber types in the abundance of proteins involved in translation, and identified fiber type-specific upregulation of the mechanosensitive protein CSRP3, which increased muscle mass when overexpressed in mouse skeletal muscle. These findings may have implications for resistance training-induced hypertrophy and demonstrate key differences in male and female skeletal muscle that underline the importance of sex-specific research in the field of physiology.

Results

Study overview and proteomics workflow

Twelve females and 12 males who did not engage in resistance training prior to the intervention completed 8 weeks of supervised resistance training performed 3 times a week, with muscle biopsies sampled from m. vastus lateralis before and 3 days after the last training bout (Fig. 1A). The intervention increased leg lean mass (Fig. 1B) and strength, assessed by 1-repetition maximum (1RM) leg press (Fig. 1C), in both males and females, with no differences between sexes (Fig. 1B, C). After freeze-drying the muscle biopsies, single fibers were dissected and typified using dot blotting with antibodies for MYH2 (type II) and MYH7 (type I). Fibers were then pooled by participant and time point, resulting in pools of 18 ± 6 (mean ± SD) type I and 18 ± 5 type II fibers in females, and 14 ± 6 type I and 23 ± 6 type II fibers in males that were prepared for proteomics analysis. This corresponded to a greater proportion of type I fibers in females and type II fibers in males at baseline (p = 0.035; Supplementary Fig. 1A), with no changes in fiber type distribution from before to after the resistance training intervention (p = 0.671; Supplementary Fig. 1A). For preparation for proteomics analysis, samples were enzymatically digested, and peptides were measured by liquid chromatography tandem mass-spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) using timsTOF DIA-PASEF. This approach detected a total of 3197 proteins, of which 2883 proteins were identified in at least 70% of samples in either male or female type I or type II fiber pools, respectively, and used for bioinformatics analysis (Supplementary Dataset 1). Proteomics validated the purity of type I and type II fiber pools, as characterized by high abundance of MYH7 in type I fibers and high abundance of MYH2 in type II fibers (Fig. 1D). In addition, exploration of the dynamic range of quantified proteins showed very low abundance of markers of other cell types, such as satellite cells, fibro-adipogenic progenitors (FAPs), and endothelial cells (Fig. 1E), demonstrating a minimal contribution of these cell types to the total proteome.

While the majority of the proteome was quantified in each sex and fiber type, 22 proteins were only detected in male fibers, and 6 proteins were only detected in female fibers (Fig. 1F and Supplementary Dataset 2). Proteins only detected in male fibers included UBE2V1 and USP13, which are involved in ubiquitination and deubiquitination, respectively, whereas proteins only detected in female fibers included DST, which is involved in anchoring intermediate filaments to the actin cytoskeleton. In addition, 70 proteins were only detected in type I fibers, which included mitochondrial proteins TIMMDC1, CPOX, and AFG1L, and 58 proteins were only detected in type II fibers (Fig. 1F and Supplementary Dataset 2), which included the E3 ubiquitin-protein ligases TRIM21, HECTD3, and NEDD4. Principal component analysis of baseline samples revealed that fiber types clearly separated along principal component 1, which accounted for 35.5% of total variance between samples, whereas sexes seemed to separate along principal component 2, which accounted for 7.3% of the variance between samples (Fig. 1G). This identifies fiber type and sex as the main determinants of variance in human skeletal muscle.

Fiber type differences in the skeletal muscle proteome

To dive deeper into the differences in the sex- and fiber type-specific proteome, we compared only baseline samples to avoid the confounding effects of resistance training. As expected, myosin heavy chains, myosin light chains, and troponin isoforms were the main drivers of separation between type I and type II fibers within principal component 1 (Fig. 2A). When directly comparing fiber types, we identified 1004 proteins that were differentially expressed between type I and type II fibers in females and 1656 proteins that were differentially expressed in males (FDR < 0.05; Fig. 2B and Supplementary Dataset 3). There was a large overlap in fiber type-specific proteins between males and females, although the difference between fiber types was quantitatively greater in males (Fig. 2C), which suggests that the difference between fiber types appears moderately more distinct in males than females. Notably, the protein phosphatase PPM1F and the E3 ubiquitin ligase CUL2 showed greater abundance in type I fibers in females but greater abundance in type II fibers in males. In both sexes, type I fibers were enriched for gene ontology biological process (GO:BP) terms related to mitochondrial ATP synthesis, while type II fibers were enriched for NADH regeneration, driven by the glycolytic enzymes ENO2, ENO3, PFKL, PFKM, PGAM2, PGK1, PGK2, PKM, and TPI1. Other terms such as mitochondrial gene expression and cytoplasmic translation were only enriched in male type I and type II fibers, respectively, whereas the term fatty acid metabolic process was only enriched in female type I fibers (FDR < 0.05; Fig. 2D). However, only fiber type differences in cytoplasmic translation seemed to be affected by sex (sex × fiber type interaction: p = 0.055), suggesting discrepancies in fiber type differences in proteins involved in translation between males and females. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed that female skeletal muscle had a greater abundance of intermediate filament proteins in both type I and type II fibers (FDR < 0.05; Fig. 3C), and follow up analysis confirmed that this sex difference was irrespective of fiber type (sex × fiber type interaction: p = 0.735; Fig. 3D). To further explore intermediate filament differences between sexes, we acquired permeabilized single muscle fibers from two additional male and female subjects and used confocal microscopy of keratin and desmin to assess structural differences. Here, desmin showed a honeycomb pattern at the subsarcolemmal periphery, while it showed clear transversal striated patterns in the intermyofibrillar regions. Pan-keratin showed diffused distribution of keratin across muscle fibers, while individual keratins (keratin 5, 6 and 14) showed clustering of keratin in vesicle-like structures (Fig. 3E). However, based on visual inspection, structural organization of these proteins did not differ between males and females. As such, sex differences in intermediate filaments seem to be specific to protein abundance, whereas the overall structure of the network is maintained between sexes.

Whereas female fibers were enriched for intermediate filament proteins, male fibers were enriched for GO:BP terms related to mitochondria (FDR < 0.05; Fig. 3C). To follow up on this, we used Mitocarta 3.025 to assess whether this was specific to certain mitochondrial proteins or whether it applies to overall greater mitochondrial density. Protein abundance was higher in males than females for most proteins in all mitochondrial complexes (Fig. 3F), irrespective of fiber type (sex × fiber type interaction: Complex I: p = 0.228; Complex II: p = 0.630; Complex III: p = 0.729; Complex IV: p = 0.876; Complex V: p = 0.290), although the difference was more variable in complex I. Likewise, the abundance of proteins within both mitoribosome subunits was higher in males than females (Fig. 3G), irrespective of fiber type (sex × fiber type interaction: 39S subunit: p = 0.369; 28S subunit: p = 0.838). To further elucidate the functional role of these sex differences in mitochondria, we assessed citrate synthase activity. This showed that the greater abundance of mitochondrial proteins was accompanied by greater citrate synthase activity (Fig. 3H). Taken together, these data indicate that males possess a higher skeletal muscle oxidative capacity than females. This coincided with greater maximal oxygen uptake during a bike ergometer test to exhaustion in males when normalized to bodyweight (Supplementary Fig. 2A), but not when normalized to lean body mass (Supplementary Fig. 2B), suggesting that sex differences in maximal oxygen uptake seem to be driven by greater muscle mass in males26 rather than differences in skeletal muscle mitochondrial content.

Some GO:BP terms were also enriched in both sex- and fiber type-specific manner. This included terms such as translation (sex × fiber type interaction: p = 0.018) and mRNA destabilization (sex × fiber type interaction: p = 0.049) that were specifically enriched in male type II fibers, as well as GO:BP terms related to fatty acid metabolism that were specifically enriched in female type II fibers (FDR < 0.05; Fig. 3C). Interestingly, female type I fibers were enriched for the term skeletal muscle contraction. This term was driven by differences in fast troponin isoforms (sex × fiber type interaction: p < 0.001), where abundance of TNNI2 and TNNT3 in type I fibers was greater in females, but abundance of TNNT3 in type II fibers was greater in males (FDR < 0.05; Fig. 3I). This suggests that females have faster contractile properties of type I fibers, but slower type II fibers, indicating that contractile properties of fiber types are also more distinct in males than females.

Remodeling of the muscle proteome following resistance training is sex- and fiber type-specific

We next assessed whether resistance training would elicit a sex- and fiber type-specific response. Here, we observed remarkable remodeling of the skeletal muscle proteome with many of these changes being specific to sex and fiber type. Overall, resistance training upregulated 313 proteins and downregulated 139 proteins (main effect: FDR < 0.05; Fig. 4A and Supplementary Dataset 6). However, when assessed in a sex- and fiber type-specific manner, only 9 and 7 proteins were regulated in female and male type I fibers, respectively. Of these, S100A13, THY1, and SERPINH1 were the only proteins that were upregulated in both sexes and both fiber types, demonstrating consistent regulation of these proteins as an adaptation to resistance training. Whereas SERPINH1 is likely upregulated due to its role as a chaperone in collagen biosynthesis27, which could explain remodeling of the extracellular matrix during resistance training-induced muscle hypertrophy28,29, less is known about the roles of the calcium-binding protein S100A13 and the glycoprotein THY1 in skeletal muscle. In contrast to only a few regulated proteins in type I fibers, 164 proteins were regulated in female type II fibers and 101 proteins were regulated in male type II fibers (FDR < 0.05; Fig. 4B, C and Supplementary Dataset 7). To elucidate the observed sex- and fiber type-specific remodeling of the skeletal muscle proteome, we used GSEA. This revealed that GO:BP terms related to intermediate filaments were upregulated in both female type I and type II fibers, but only in male type II fibers (FDR < 0.05; Fig. 4D). Furthermore, intermediate filament proteins were upregulated to a larger degree in female skeletal muscle, irrespective of fiber type (Fig. 4E), suggesting a greater role of intermediate filaments in mechanotransduction30,31 in females. In contrast, mitochondrial translation and mitochondrial gene expression were upregulated in male type II fibers only (FDR < 0.05; Fig. 4D). To further explore this, we used Mitocarta 3.025 to assess whether this was indicative of sex- and fiber type differences in resistance training-induced mitochondrial biogenesis. This showed that OXPHOS proteins were regulated in a both sex- and fiber type specific manner (trial × sex × fiber type interaction: p < 0.001), where abundance increased to a similar extent in female type I and type II fibers, but to a greater extent in type II fibers in males, as no changes occurred in male type I fibers (Fig. 4F). Interestingly, this was not mirrored by changes in mitochondrial ribosomal proteins, where abundance increased irrespective of both sex and fiber type (Fig. 4G), suggesting a discrepancy in biogenesis of mitochondrial ribosomes and OXPHOS proteins in male type I fibers specifically. Nevertheless, this indicates that resistance training induces a more oxidative skeletal muscle phenotype that is irrespective of fiber type in females, but specific to type II fibers in males.

Whereas terms related to intermediate filaments and mitochondria were upregulated, terms related to gas transport were downregulated in both fiber types in males, as well as in type II fibers in females (FDR < 0.05; Fig. 4D). Downregulation of these terms was driven by the shared proteins AQP1, HBA2, CA2, HBB, HBD, HBG1, HBG2, and RHAG. This downregulation was greater in type II fibers, irrespective of sex (Fig. 4H), underpinning a greater adaptive response to resistance training in type II than type I fibers.

Upregulation of CSRP3 induces muscle hypertrophy

To explore the fiber type-specific response to resistance training, we investigated the 34 proteins that were regulated in both male and female type II fibers, but not type I fibers (Fig. 4C). Here, we confirmed that 25 of these proteins were regulated to a greater extent in type II fibers specifically (trial × fiber type interaction: FDR < 0.05). Of these proteins, we identified fiber type-specific upregulation of CSRP3 (Fig. 5A), which is associated with the GO:BP term “skeletal muscle tissue development”. In addition, CSRP3 is associated with the GO:BP term “detection of muscle stretch”, which implies a role as a mechanosensor regulating skeletal muscle hypertrophy32. To follow up on this, we used an adeno-associated virus (AAV) to overexpress CSRP3 in the muscle of nine C57BL/6Ntac mice. Mice were injected with rAAV9:CSRP3 into the muscles of one leg while the muscles of the other leg were injected with an empty viral vector, rAAV9:eGFP (Fig. 5B). Overexpression of CSRP3 was verified by detection of the FLAG tag in the injected muscles (Fig. 5C), and correspondingly increased muscle protein abundance of CSRP3 compared to sham after 4 weeks (Fig. 5D, E). This was accompanied by an increase in muscle mass of 4% in gastrocnemius and 6% in tibialis anterior (Fig. 5F), which resembles the changes in muscle mass of gastrocnemius and tibialis anterior seen with weight pulling as a mouse model of progressive resistance exercise33. To validate this finding, we replicated the experiment in the gastrocnemius of four additional mice. All four mice experienced an increase in muscle mass with overexpression of CSRP3 compared to sham (Fig. 5G). Discussion

It is widely recognized that resistance training is the gold standard intervention for inducing muscle hypertrophy34, which is of utmost importance for physical function and metabolic health35. However, despite obvious physiological differences between males and females, as well as the consistent observation of fiber type-specific hypertrophy following resistance training6–8, the underlying sex- and fiber type-specific remodeling of the proteome has yet to be mapped. Herein, we demonstrate fiber type-specific differences between males and females in the skeletal muscle proteome and its remodeling following resistance training. Furthermore, we build on these findings by elucidating differences in the skeletal muscle proteome that likely explain resistance training-induced sex- and fiber type-specific hypertrophy. This led to the discovery of CSRP3 as a protein that was upregulated in a fiber type-specific manner and mediates muscle hypertrophy.

Resistance training resulted in substantial remodeling of the skeletal muscle proteome that was highly fiber type-specific. Here, our findings display that greater hypertrophy of type II fibers6–8 following resistance training is reflected by type II muscle fiber-specific remodeling of the proteome. Importantly however, this remodeling extends far beyond the hypertrophic response, as indicated by the many regulated proteins with broad cellular functions unrelated to muscle hypertrophy. Of proteins that may be involved in the hypertrophic response, CSRP3 was specifically upregulated in type II fibers. CSRP3 is a mechanosensitive protein involved in myogenesis and satellite cell-mediated myonuclear accretion36. Upon activation, CSRP3 translocates from the z-disk of sarcomeres to nuclei and interacts with muscle transcription factors (MFRs) such as MyoD, MRF4, and myogenein37–39. Accordingly, the absence of nuclear CSRP3 blunts mechanical strain-induced protein accretion in cultured neonatal rat myocytes37, and knockdown of CSRP3 in chickens suppresses satellite cell differentiation40. As such, we explored the role of fiber type-specific upregulation of CSRP3 and discovered that overexpression of CSRP3 in mice induced muscle hypertrophy resembling that of mouse models of progressive resistance exercise33. Thus, it is likely that CSRP3 acts as a mechanosensor that plays a role in initiating resistance training-induced hypertrophy, which may contribute to explaining fiber type-specific hypertrophy, as overexpressing CSRP3 mimics the upregulation induced by resistance training, which was fiber type-specific.

While we have previously shown differences in the proteome between type I and type II fibers in males19,20, our results show that the skeletal muscle proteome is both sex- and fiber type-specific. Notably, our results indicate that female type I and type II fibers are less distinct than male fiber types. This applies both to proteins involved in biological processes, such as mitochondrial metabolism and cytosolic translation, but also proteins involved in muscle contraction that were driven by sex differences in fast troponin isoforms. Whereas these differences in abundance of fast troponin isoforms do not directly measure contractile function, sex-differences in contractile properties and fatigue resistance of skeletal muscle has previously been observed41. Previously, these differences have merely been attributed to differences in fiber type composition, as fiber type-specific investigations of sex-differences in skeletal muscle are scarce. A previous study found that specific force differed between fiber types in males but not females42, supporting the notion of less distinct fiber types in females. In contrast, fiber shortening velocity did not differ between sexes42, suggesting that sex-differences in skeletal muscle fiber types affect specific contractile properties of skeletal muscle fibers, but not others.

Our findings also suggested both structural and metabolic sex differences in skeletal muscle. Sex differences in abundance of intermediate filament proteins and mitochondrial proteins were irrespective of fiber type, and suggest that intermediate filament proteins likely play a greater role in providing cellular integrity43 in female skeletal muscle, whereas male skeletal muscle seems to possess a greater oxidative capacity. In contrast to greater mitochondrial content in males, females had a greater abundance of proteins related to fatty acid metabolism in type II fibers. Whereas these metabolic differences in male and female skeletal muscle are consistent with previous findings that suggest a greater reliance on fat oxidation in females44–46, previous studies have indicated either no sex differences in mitochondrial protein content47 and volume density48 or greater mitochondrial volume density in females46. This discrepancy is likely due to a greater area proportion of type I fibers in females49, underpinning the importance of assessing sex differences at the muscle fiber level. However, previous research that tried to investigate sex differences in mitochondrial content in a fiber type-specific manner only used a targeted approach, and found no difference in COX IV staining intensity between sexes and fiber types47, demonstrating the importance of high-resolution approaches, such as mass spectrometry-based proteomics. In support of this notion, our findings are in line with previous reports that mitochondrial respiration rates in permeabilized muscle fibers are lower in females than males at physiologically relevant ADP levels50.

Whereas resistance training increased leg lean mass to a similar extent in males and females, which is a widely accepted observation at the whole muscle level51, remodeling of the muscle fiber proteome was greater in females than males. To explore this beyond significantly regulated proteins, we used GSEA to elucidate more subtle differences in sex-specific changes of biological processes. This showed a greater remodeling of intermediate filaments in females that was irrespective of fiber type. Interestingly, intermediate filament proteins were also more abundant in untrained female skeletal muscle, indicating a greater reliance on intermediate filaments for muscle function in females. Disruption of intermediate filaments via mutation or knockout of desmin induces muscle weakness, disruption of myofibrils and myopathy52,53. In addition, desmin-null muscles also show less nuclei deformation upon mechanical stretch, which leads to the absence of stretch-induced signaling31. Here, we are the first to identify sex-specific abundance and upregulation of intermediate filament proteins in skeletal muscle, despite no sex differences in the structure of the intermediate filament network, suggesting a yet unknown role of intermediate filaments in skeletal muscle.

When trying to untangle the diverse differences between sexes and fiber types that may explain differences in training responsiveness, our data suggest a role of translational capacity in mediating muscle fiber hypertrophy. As proteins involved in translation differed both between sexes and fiber types, this may be a potential mechanism of preferential type II fiber hypertrophy and limited resistance training-induced hypertrophy of type I fibers in females9,54. Likewise, greater translational capacity may also explain preferential type II fiber proteome remodeling in response to resistance training. This is likely due to protein synthesis being highly regulated at the translational level55–57. Therefore, the abundance of ribosomal proteins and eukaryotic initiation/elongation factors could be a limiting factor for resistance training-induced protein synthesis.

While our muscle fiber proteomics workflow enabled yet unprecedented insights into sex- and fiber type-specific adaptations to resistance training, many questions remain unresolved. In this regard, a limitation of the study is that whereas we were able to identify a role of CSRP3 in muscle mass regulation, the overexpression experiment in mice does not provide mechanistic insights into how upregulation of the protein mediates hypertrophy or how this is reflected at the structural level of muscle fibers. Thus, future studies investigating the role of CSRP3 in muscle hypertrophy should employ muscle fiber histology and loss-of-function approaches to elucidate hypertrophy at the fiber level and downstream mechanisms. Likewise, while we identified sex differences in abundance and resistance training-induced regulation of intermediate filament proteins, our data cannot decipher the functional impact of this sex-specific remodeling of intermediate filaments. To follow up on this sex difference, we were limited to an exploratory visual assessment of the organization of intermediate filament proteins in permeabilized single fibers of additional men and women that did not undergo resistance training. Therefore, future studies should assess the structural remodeling of intermediate filaments with resistance training and how this impacts muscle function.

In summary, our proteomics workflow allowed mapping of the first fiber type-specific skeletal muscle proteome in females, and revealed differences to the male skeletal muscle proteome that were fiber type-specific. Likewise, resistance training-induced remodeling of the skeletal muscle proteome was also highly affected by sex- and fiber type, with intermediate filament proteins being heavily upregulated in females compared to males, and fiber type-specific upregulation of CSRP3, which increased muscle mass in mice when overexpressed. These results provide a resource to further investigate mechanisms of skeletal muscle adaptations to resistance training and underline the importance of assessing muscle adaptations in a sex- and fiber type-specific manner. As such, future research in the field of muscle physiology and muscle adaptations should strive to address research in a sex-specific manner, as research in males may not always be applicable to females.

Methods

Subjects

Twelve males (28 ± 7 years; mean ± SD) and 12 females (27 ± 7 years) completed the study. Inclusion criteria were between 18–45 years old, and body mass index (BMI) < 27. Before inclusion, each subject provided their written and oral informed consent. Exclusion criteria were smoking, chronic disease, use of prescription medication, and resistance training more than once per week in the 12 months leading up to the intervention. Subject characteristics are described elsewhere9. The study was approved by the local ethics committee of Copenhagen, Denmark (H-18007889) and registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04136457). All experimental procedures involving human subjects were performed at the Department of Nutrition, Exercise, and Sports, University of Copenhagen. The study complied with the 2013 declaration of Helsinki.

Experimental protocol

The study consisted of an 8-week intervention, where subjects performed resistance training three times a week on non-consecutive days. The resistance training protocol included the exercises leg press and leg extension for targeting the quadriceps muscle, and is described in detail elsewhere9. Briefly, for each exercise, 3 sets of 8–12 repetitions were carried out to momentary muscular failure, defined as the inability to complete another repetition. Sets were separated by 2 min of rest. Before and three days after the intervention subjects attended an experimental day where leg lean mass was assessed by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) (Lunar iDXA, GE Healthcare, GE Medical systems, Belgium), a muscle biopsy was sampled from m. vastus lateralis, and one-repetition maximum (1RM) leg press was assessed. Subjects were instructed to replicate their dietary intake in the 24 h leading up to the experimental days. All experimental procedures were conducted at the same time of the day for each subject.

Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry

Two consecutive DXA scans were performed to account for scan-to-scan variation58. Prior to DXA scans, subjects rested for 10 min in a supine position to ensure even distribution of body fluids59,60. Subjects were placed in the scanner, undressed and euhydrated in a standardized supine position. The scanner was calibrated before scans using daily calibration procedures (Lunar iDXA, GE Healthcare, GE Medical Systems).

One-repetition maximum (1RM)

Prior to assessment of 1RM leg press a warm-up consisting of 6, 4 and 2 repetitions at 50, 70 and 80% of the anticipated 1RM was performed. For 1RM determination, Subjects then performed sets of one repetition of increasing weight until the weight could not be lifted through the full range of motion. Three min of rest were provided between each successive attempt. The highest load successfully lifted was defined as 1RM. All 1RM determinations were made within five attempts.

Measurement of maximal oxygen uptake

Prior to the intervention, maximal oxygen uptake (VO2max) was determined breath by breath with a gas analyzing system (Oxycon Pro, Viasys Healthcare, Hoechberg, Germany) during an incremental test to exhaustion on a bike ergometer (Monark LC7, Monark Exercise AB, Vansbro, Sweden). The ramp test started at 100 W and increased by 1 W every 2 s until exhaustion. Subjects were instructed to keep a cadence of approximately 90 rpm, and exhaustion was defined as the point where cadence fell below 70 rpm, despite strong verbal encouragement.

Muscle biopsies

Muscle biopsies were obtained under local anesthesia (lidocaine without epinephrine, Xylocaine 20 mg/mL, AstraZeneca) using a 4 mm Bergström needle (Stille, Stockholm, Sweden) with suction61. Immediately after collection, muscle biopsies were washed in sterile saline solution (0.9% NaCl) and quickly dissected free of visible blood, connective tissue, and fat. Thereafter, muscle biopsies were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at – 80°.

Preparation of muscle fiber pools

The frozen muscle biopsies were freeze dried for 48 h before dissection of single muscle fibers using fine forceps under a stereomicroscope in a temperature- and humidity-controlled room (humidity of ~ 25%). A total of 40 fibers with a minimum length of 1 mm (1.5 ± 0.4 mm; mean ± SD) were dissected from each biopsy. Each fiber was dissolved in 15 µl of lysis buffer (1% SDC, 50 mM Tris-HCL, pH 8.5, and ddH2O) and boiled for 10 min at 95° in a thermomixer. Thereafter, samples were sonicated using a bioruptor twin (Diagenode) for 10 min. To ensure fibers were at the bottom of the tube, samples underwent centrifugation (20.000 × g, 1 min) between each step. A small fraction of each fiber was used to identify MYH expression by dot blotting with specific antibodies for MYH2 (mouse igM, 1:200, A4.1519, DSHB) and MYH7 (mouse igM, 1:200, A4.840, DSHB). For dot blotting, 1 ul (1/15 of a fiber lysate) was spotted onto two identical PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked in 5% skimmed milk diluted in Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween-20 (TBST) for 15 min, followed by incubation in primary antibody for 2 h at room temperature. One membrane was incubated in primary antibody for MYH2, and the other membrane was incubated in primary antibody for MYH7. Thereafter, membranes were incubated in horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody (goat anti-mouse igG, 1:20.000; Bio-Rad) for 1 h at room temperature. Primary antibodies were diluted in 3% BSA in TBST, and secondary antibodies were diluted in 2% skimmed milk in TBST. Membranes were washed for 3 × 5 min between each step in the protocol. Dots were visualized using chemiluminescence (Immobilon Forte, Millipore) in a ChemiDoc XRS + imaging system (Bio-Rad, CA, USA). The remaining sample of each fiber lysate was pooled according to MYH expression, resulting in pools of type I fibers that expressed only MYH7 and pools of type II fibers that expressed only MYH2. Hybrid fibers that expressed both MYH isoforms were excluded.

Preparation of samples for proteomics analysis

Following determination of protein concentration via BCA protein assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 5 µg of protein from each fiber type-specific lysate underwent reduction with DTT (final concentration of 10 mM) and alkylation with CAA (final concentration of 50 mM). Then, proteins were digested overnight using trypsin (Promega) and LysC (Wako) with an enzyme to protein ratios of 1:100 and 1:500, respectively. The following day, tryptic peptides were desalted using in-house crafted styrenedivinylbenzene reverse-phase sulfonate (SDB-RPS) StageTip columns and 200 ng of the resulting desalted peptides were loaded in Evotips according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry

Proteomics measurements were conducted using LC-MS instrumentation comprising an Evosep One HPLC system62 (Evosep) coupled via electrospray ionization to a timsTOF mass spectrometer (Bruker). Peptides underwent separation on an 8 cm, 150 μM ID column packed with C18 beads (1.5 μm) (Evosep). The ‘60 samples per day’ chromatographic method was used to achieve peptide separation prior to electrospray ionization through a CaptiveSpray ion source and a 10 μm emitter into the MS instrument. Samples were measured using a DIA-PASEF workflow63. Specifically, the DIA-PASEF scan range was set at 400–000 (m/z), the TIMS mobility range to 0.64–1.37 (V cm−2), and ramp and accumulation times were both set to 100 ms. To accommodate the ‘60 samples per day method’, a short-gradient method was used, which included 8 DIA-PASEF scans with three 25 Da windows per ramp, resulting in an estimated cycle time of 0.95 sec.

Raw spectra were analyzed by the search engine DIA-NN (version 1.878) using a library-based approach against an in-house muscle fiber-specific MS library containing ∼ 5000 proteins. Proteotypic peptides were used for protein group quantification, with the neural network set to double-pass mode. “Robust LC (high accuracy)” was chosen as quantification strategy, as well as enabling the match between runs option, while the rest of the parameters remained as default, including peptide length ranging 7–30 amino acids and precursor FDR set to 1%.

Bioinformatics

Bioinformatics were conducted within the R environment (R version 4.3.2). Data were filtered such that only proteins that were quantified in 70% of the samples in at least one group (male type I, male type II, female type I, and female type II) were included for analysis. During filtering, samples from one female subject were excluded due to low protein abundance, suggesting sample loss during sample preparation. Thereafter, data were log2 transformed and normalized by median scaling using the PhosR package (v 1.18.1). Categorical annotations for each protein were supplied in the form of GO terms of biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF), as well as UniProt Keywords. All annotations were extracted from the UniProt database. Principle component analysis was performed using the ppca method from the pcaMethods package. Differential expression analysis was performed using the limma package (v 3.64.1), with a false discovery rate (FDR) set to 5% using the qvalue package (v 2.40.0). Enrichment analysis of GO:BP terms was performed with the clusterProfiler package (v4.16.9) using a ranked protein list, with proteins ranked based on log2fold change obtained from differential expression analysis or pearson’s r from the correlation with change in fiber CSA9. An FDR of 5% was chosen for enrichment analysis. GSEA was used to identify ranked pathway-level trends across the full quantified protein distribution, and there represent coordinated shifts in biological processes rather than enrichment restricted to proteins that individually passed the FDR threshold.

Immunostaining and image acquisition of single muscle fibers

Single muscle fibers were teased from m. vastus lateralis biopsies, fixed and stored64, and incubated for 10 min in 0.1% triton x-100 diluted in immune-buffer (IB - 0.25% BSA, 50 mM glycine, 0.033% saponin, 0.05% sodium azide). After 10 min of wash with IB, single muscle fibers were incubated overnight with primary antibodies for desmin (rabbit igG, 1:500, MA5-38096, Invitrogen), pan keratin (mouse igG1, 1:20, ab8068, abcam), KRT5 (mouse igG1, 1:100, MA5-12596, Invitrogen), KRT6 (rabbit igG, 1:200, MA5-32318, Invitrogen), and KRT14 (mouse igG3, 1:100, MA5-11599, Invitrogen) diluted in IB. Followed by three IB washes of 1 h each, the fibers were incubated overnight with the secondary antibodies diluted in IB. Primary antibodies raised in rabbit were immunostained with a goat anti-rabbit IgG conjugated to Alexa Fluor 568 (goat IgG; 1:400; A11004; Thermo Fisher Scientific), and primary antibodies raised in mouse were immunostained with goat anti-mouse IgG conjugated to Alexa Fluor 488 (goat IgG; 1:400; A11001; Thermo Fisher Scientific). To label nuclei, the fibers were incubated with Hoechst (H3570, Thermo Fisher Scientific) diluted in IB during the first 1-hour wash, followed by two 1-hour washes with IB, and finished with a 20 min wash in PBS. Immunostained fibers were mounted on Vectashield mounting medium (4 µg/ml; H-1000; Vector laboratories) and covered with 170 μm ± 5 μm thick coverslips. Sealing was done with transparent nail polish.

Confocal images were taken with a Carl Zeiss LSM700 microscope, through a Plan-Apochromat 63X/1.4 Oil DIC objective. Hoechst, Alexa Fluor 488 and Alexa Fluor 555 were imaged sequentially, and exited with diode 405 nm, 488 nm, and 555 nm lasers, respectively. Detectors gain and offset were adjusted to avoid saturation and empty pixels, and kept constant for each staining condition. Scaling was set to the Nyquist criterion, with 70 nm x 70 nm pixel size.

Maximal activity of citrate synthase

Maximal activity of citrate synthase was determined from ∼ 0.5 mg of dry weight muscle tissue. Maximal enzyme activity was determined fluorometrically with NAD-NADH coupled reactions (Fluoroscan Ascent, Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 25 °C65.

Intramuscular injections of rAAV6:CSRP3 in mice

Female C57BL/6NBomTac mice (Taconic, Lille Skensved, Denmark) aged 16 weeks were maintained on a 12:12 h light–dark cycle and housed at 22 °C (with allowed fluctuation of ± 2 °C) with nesting material. The mice were group-housed. The mice received a standard rodent chow diet (Altromin no. 1324; Brogaarden, Denmark) and water ad libitum. All mice were monitored daily. Prior to intramuscular injection, mice were placed under general anesthesia (2% isofluorane in O2). For CSRP3 overexpression, intramuscular injections of 1 × 1010 vector genomes of rAAV9:CMV > CSRP3/FLAG:WPRE were administered in a volume of Gelofusine (Tibialis anterior: 30 µL; Gastrocnemius: 50 µL) directly into the muscle compartments of one leg. Contralateral muscles were administered rAAV9:CAG>eGFP:WPRE (VectorID: VB010000-9287ffw, VectorBuilder Inc.). The vector designed to overexpress CSRP3, CMV>mCsrp3[NM_001198841.1]/FLAG:WPRE (VB250205-1384yht), was cloned, packaged into AAV9, and subsequently ultra-purified. All procedures were outsourced to VectorBuilder. Four weeks after AAV9 administration, mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation and skeletal muscles were quickly excised. FLAG tag (rabbit anti-FLAG, 1:1000, F7425, Sigma-Aldrich) and CSRP3 protein content was assessed via western blotting (rabbit igG, 1:1000, ab173301, abcam), and muscle mass was measured. The animal experiment complied with the European convention for protection of vertebrate animals used for experiments and other scientific purposes and was approved by the Danish Animal Experimental Inspectorate.

Statistics

Linear mixed modeling was performed using the R package lme4 (v 1.1.37). For analysis of human data, trial, sex, and fiber type were fixed factors, with subject and protein as random factors. For analysis of data from the overexpression experiment in mice, rAAV9 was the fixed factor with subject as a random factor. Data were analyzed as absolute values and are presented as mean differences and 95%CI with p-values to represent probability.
