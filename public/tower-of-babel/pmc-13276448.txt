Age-inappropriate white matter injury reveals hidden cerebral small vessel disease burden and mortality risk
Authors: Pei-Lin Lee, Wen-Yu Ou Yang, Kun-Hsien Chou, Wei-Ju Lee, Chen-Yuan Kuo, Shing-Huei Lin, Liang-Kung Chen, Ching-Po Lin, Chih-Ping Chung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Normative WMH-based evaluation framework improves early detection and risk stratification of covert CSVD.•Age-inappropriate WMH burden identifies advanced CSVD independent of chronological age.•Higher WMH deviation associates with adverse vascular profiles despite similar or younger age.•WMH deviation shows graded associations with multidomain cognitive impairment and gray matter atrophy.•Severe WMH deviation predicts increased long-term all-cause mortality (HR ∼1.9) independent of age.Keywords: White matter hyperintensities, Covert cerebral small vessel disease, Normative model, Brain chart, Hierarchical Bayesian regression

Introduction

Sporadic cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD) is a major cause of stroke and cognitive impairment, characterized by progressive microvascular injury affecting the neurovascular unit [1]. White matter hyperintensities (WMH), the most common imaging marker of CSVD, are consistently associated with adverse vascular and clinical outcomes [[2], [3], [4], [5], [6]]. However, WMH are also strongly age-dependent and frequently observed in individuals without overt neurological symptoms [6]. This overlap complicates clinical interpretation, particularly in covert CSVD, a preclinical stage in which early identification of high-risk individuals is critical [7].

A fundamental limitation of current WMH assessment is the inability to distinguish physiological aging from pathological, accelerated white matter injury. Conventional approaches, including visual grading and absolute volume thresholds, do not account for age-related variability, potentially underestimating disease burden in younger individuals while overestimating it in older adults. As a result, clinically meaningful, age-inappropriate WMH burden remains difficult to define, limiting both risk stratification and mechanistic understanding of CSVD.

Recent advances in normative modeling enable quantification of individual deviation from age-expected brain measures, providing a means to distinguish pathological white matter injury from physiological aging [8]. Applied to WMH, this approach may provide an age- and sex-contextualized framework for identifying individuals whose WMH burden is disproportionate to normative expectations [8,9]. However, its clinical relevance remains uncertain, as existing models have not been validated in large, community-based populations with longitudinal outcomes.

In this study, we aimed to determine whether age-inappropriate WMH burden identifies clinically relevant CSVD. Using a large multi-center normative model and an independent community-based cohort with long-term follow-up, we examined whether WMH deviation from age- and sex-expected values is associated with vascular risk burden, cognitive performance, brain structural changes, and mortality. We hypothesized that WMH burden disproportionate to age- and sex-expected norms reflects clinically relevant CSVD burden and is associated with adverse vascular, cognitive, structural, and mortality outcomes.

Methods

Study populations

Training cohort for normative model development

To develop a robust age- and sex-referenced normative model of WMH severity, we aggregated brain MRI datasets from five collaborating research centers and medical institutions across Taiwan and Japan, ensuring a sufficiently large sample size and broad age representation (Fig. 1). All participants were community-dwelling adults aged ≥50 years without a history of stroke, dementia, neuropsychiatric disorders, head trauma, or other major neurological conditions. Following standardized image quality control procedures focused on signal-to-noise ratio, motion artifacts, and structural integrity, a total of 2544 neurologically healthy individuals (age range, 50–92.1 years; 1225 males and 1319 females) were included for model training. The training cohort incorporated MRI data acquired across seven scanning protocols from multiple scanner manufacturers and coil configurations, enhancing model robustness and generalizability across heterogeneous acquisition settings. The use of MRI data from all normative-model training sites was approved by the corresponding institutional review boards and complied with the data-use permissions of each contributing cohort. Detailed information on demographic characteristics, scanner types, hardware setups, imaging sequences, and protocol parameters for each contributing dataset is summarized in Supplementary Table S1.

Application cohort - the I-Lan longitudinal aging study (ILAS)

Participants for model validation and application were drawn from the I-Lan Longitudinal Aging Study (ILAS), a prospective, population-based cohort of community-dwelling adults [10,11]. Residents of Yuanshan Township in I-Lan County aged ≥50 years were invited to participate. Individuals unable to communicate or complete interviews, those with functional limitations precluding assessments, life expectancy <6 months, MRI contraindications (e.g., metal implants), or institutionalization were excluded. For ILAS neuroimaging studies, we further excluded participants with major structural brain lesions, including territorial infarction or intracranial tumors. Wave 1 of ILAS (January 2011–July 2014) enrolled 1839 participants who underwent neuroimaging. Of these, 1281 had MRI scans with all eligible sequences required for WMH modeling, validation, and application. After excluding 44 participants due to ineligible brain lesions or insufficient image quality (motion or acquisition artifacts), 1237 participants were included in the final analytic sample. Although one training site and the ILAS cohort were both scanned at NYCU using the same Tim Trio scanner and MPRAGE protocol, the two datasets consisted of independent participants, and no ILAS participants or scans were included in the normative model training dataset.

All participants provided written informed consent. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of National Yang Ming Chiao Tung University, Taipei, Taiwan (IRB No. YM104121F-5).

Brain MRI acquisition

Training cohort

MRI data were acquired across seven MRI protocols at five centers using scanners from different manufacturers and coil configurations. Protocol-specific acquisition parameters are detailed in Supplementary Table S1.

ILAS cohort

All MRI scans in the ILAS cohort were acquired on a single 3T Siemens Magnetom Tim Trio scanner (Siemens Healthcare, Erlangen, Germany) equipped with a 12-channel phased-array head coil at National Yang Ming Chiao Tung University. Participants’ heads were stabilized with cushions to minimize motion during scanning. Two MRI sequences were obtained for each participant: (1) T1-weighted (T1w) imaging - A 3D MPRAGE sequence with TR/TE/TI = 3500/3.5/1100 ms; flip angle = 7°; NEX = 1; FOV = 256 × 256 mm²; matrix = 256 × 256; 192 sagittal slices; isotropic voxel size = 1.0 mm³. (2) T2-weighted (T2w) FLAIR imaging - A multi-shot turbo spin-echo sequence with TR/TE/TI = 9000/143/2500 ms; flip angle = 130°; NEX = 1; FOV = 220 × 220 mm²; matrix = 320 × 320; echo train length = 35; 63 axial slices; voxel size = 0.69 × 0.69 × 2.0 mm³.

WMH and brain tissues volumes quantification

To maximize the training sample size and ensure harmonized measurement across cohorts, we used T1w imaging for WMH quantification, as it was the only sequence consistently acquired at all contributing sites. This approach provided most methodological uniformity across the 2544 healthy participants spanning seven MRI protocols and five research centers.

T1-weighted based WMH quantification

WMH and other brain tissues volumes were quantified from T1w images using the Computational Anatomy Toolbox (CAT12; release r2170) implemented in SPM12 (version 7771) within MATLAB R2021a (MathWorks, Natick, MA). All T1w images were processed using the CAT12 Geodesic Shooting pipeline with default parameters [12]. Preprocessing included adaptive non-local means denoising, bias-field correction, skull stripping, unified segmentation, and spatial normalization to Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space using study-specific tissue templates generated from the training cohort. Each scan was automatically segmented into gray matter (GM), white matter (WM), cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), and WMH using CAT12’s integrated WMH detection algorithm, which applies a region-growing approach to identify WMH lesions, including periventricular WMH. This T1w-based WMH volume quantification method has demonstrated good agreement with conventional T2w FLAIR–based approaches [13]. Tissue modulation was applied to preserve volumetric accuracy following normalization, and normalized tissue maps were resampled at 1.5-mm isotropic resolution. Other tissues volumes - including global GM volume (GMV), WM volume (WMV), CSF volume (CSFV) and total intracranial volume (TIV = GMV + WMV + CSFV + WMH)—were also estimated in native space using CAT12’s global tissue module and expressed in cubic millimeters. For regional gray matter analyses, the bilateral hippocampus and bilateral thalamus were selected a priori as subcortical regions of interest. These structures were chosen because hippocampal and thalamic atrophy have been implicated in CSVD-related brain vulnerability, cognitive impairment, and secondary neurodegenerative changes. Regional GMV of the left hippocampus, right hippocampus, left thalamus, and right thalamus was extracted from modulated GM maps in MNI space using regions of interest defined by the Harvard–Oxford subcortical atlas [14]. No other regional gray matter structures were included in the primary regional GMV analyses. Quality control was performed using CAT12’s automated quality assessment, which generates a weighted image quality rating summarizing noise, inhomogeneity, and spatial resolution metrics [15]. Higher image quality rating values indicate poorer image quality. Following previously published CAT12 quality-control criteria, scans with an image quality rating >2.8 were excluded to ensure adequate image quality for reliable volumetric analysis. Among 2772 initially available training scans, 228 were excluded because of an image quality rating >2.8, leaving 2544 participants for normative model training.

WMH volume quantification from T2-weighted FLAIR images in ILAS

For comparison and validation, WMH volume was also quantified from T2w FLAIR images using our previously established analysis pipeline [16,17]. Because FLAIR imaging was not consistently available across the normative training datasets, FLAIR-derived WMH measures were used only for validation within ILAS and were not used to construct a separate FLAIR-based normative model. WMH lesions were segmented with the Lesion Segmentation Toolbox (LST version 3.0.0; www.statistical-modelling.de/lst.html) implemented in SPM12. Each participant’s FLAIR image was first co-registered to the corresponding T1w image, after which the lesion growth algorithm was applied to generate WMH probability maps and lesion-filled T1w images. WMH probability maps were spatially normalized to MNI space using deformation fields derived from the DARTEL processing of the lesion-filled T1w images. Modulation was applied to preserve true WMH volume following normalization. All segmentations were visually inspected to ensure quality. Total FLAIR-derived WMH volume was calculated in MNI space and expressed in cubic millimeters (mm³). T1w-derived and FLAIR-derived WMH volumes in the ILAS cohort showed excellent correlation (Supplementary Figure S1).

Normative model construction using hierarchical Bayesian regression (HBR)

The normative model was trained on the entire training cohort of 2544 healthy participants. WMH volumes were normalized by TIV and log-transformed (log₁₀[WMH volume/TIV]) to reduce skewness. The normative model was constructed using hierarchical Bayesian regression (HBR) implemented in the PCNtoolkit (version 0.29) with log₁₀(WMHV/TIV) as the response variable and age as the primary covariate [[18], [19], [20]]. HBR was chosen for its capability to effectively handle multisite neuroimaging data by modeling site-related technical variability through hierarchical parameters while simultaneously estimating the biological relationships of interest. This approach uses a partial-pooling strategy with shared prior distributions, allowing sites to borrow strength from one another while accounting for inter-site variance, thereby producing stable normative estimates even when individual sites have modest sample sizes [15,18,19]. To accommodate potential non-linear age effects on WMH accumulation, age was modeled using B-spline basis functions with 3 knots. Site (7 MRI protocols) and sex (male/female) were incorporated as batch effects with random intercepts to account for scanner-related technical variance and sex-related biological differences. The model employed a Sinh-Arcsinh (SHASH) likelihood function to appropriately handle the skewed and heavy-tailed distribution of WMH volumes. Model parameters were estimated using Markov Chain Monte Carlo sampling.

Individualized normative deviation scores

We then applied the established HBR-based WMH normative model to the application cohort (ILAS cohort). For each participant, individualized deviation scores (Z-scores) were computed to quantify the extent to which observed WMH burden deviated from age- and sex-expected normative values. Normative model-based WMH severity classification

WMH burden was quantified using individualized normative deviation scores, reflecting the extent to which observed WMH volume deviated from age- and sex-expected values. For interpretability, ILAS participants were categorized into four WMH deviation groups according to the centile bands derived from the normative model trained on the reference cohort. Specifically, participants were classified as low (<25th model-derived centile), mild (25th to <50th centile), moderate (50th to <75th centile), or high (≥75th centile) WMH deviation. These cut points were defined by the external normative reference model, rather than by fixed raw log₁₀(WMHV/TIV) values or quartile splitting within the ILAS cohort. Therefore, the resulting group sizes in ILAS were not expected to be equal. Because normative expectations vary continuously by age and sex, these thresholds do not correspond to a single absolute WMH volume or a single y-axis value in Fig. 1. These categories represent increasing degrees of deviation from age- and sex-expected WMH burden, rather than absolute lesion burden, and were used to facilitate clinically interpretable stratification of age-inappropriate WMH accumulation. Notably, since the normative model accounts for age, sex, site/protocol effects, and non-Gaussian residual distributions, the corresponding WMH volume cutoffs vary by age and sex rather than being represented by a single absolute WMH volume threshold.

Demographics, neuropsychological assessment, and longitudinal mortality follow-up

Demographic characteristics, including vascular risk factors, were obtained through in-person interviews. All ILAS participants underwent routine biochemical blood testing and anthropometric measurements, including blood pressure, body weight, and height. In addition, trained interviewers conducted face-to-face comprehensive neuropsychological assessments. Global cognition was evaluated using the Mini–Mental State Examination (MMSE). Cognitive test scores were analyzed using their original raw scores rather than standardized scores or domain-level Z scores. Mortality status was ascertained through structured telephone follow-up conducted every three months and supplemented by verification via the hospital health information system for all participants. For the present study, mortality data collected through April 2022 were used.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted using SPSS (version 24). (1) Group Comparisons of Demographic and Vascular Profiles: Group differences in demographic characteristics and vascular risk profiles across the four normative model–defined WMH severity categories were examined using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) for continuous variables and χ² tests for categorical variables. ANCOVA models were adjusted for age and sex. When overall group effects were significant, pairwise post-hoc comparisons were performed using Bonferroni correction. (2) Associations with Cognitive Functions and GMVs: To evaluate whether higher WMH severity was associated with poorer cognitive performance and reduced global or regional gray matter volumes, multiple linear regression analyses were conducted. WMH severity was entered as an ordinal predictor to assess linear associations. Models for cognitive outcomes were adjusted for age, sex, and education, whereas models for brain volumetric measures were adjusted for age, sex, and total intracranial volume (TIV). Regression coefficients (β), standard errors (SE), 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and false discovery rate (FDR)–adjusted p-values were reported. Because modeling WMH deviation groups as a single ordinal predictor assumes an approximately linear relationship across adjacent categories, we performed additional sensitivity analyses treating WMH deviation group as a categorical predictor. The low WMH deviation group was used as the reference category, and adjusted β coefficients with 95% confidence intervals were estimated separately for the mild, moderate, and high WMH deviation groups. Cognitive models were adjusted for age, sex, and education, whereas brain volumetric models were adjusted for age, sex, and TIV. (3) Survival Analysis for All-Cause Mortality: Associations between normative WMH severity and all-cause mortality were examined using Cox proportional hazards regression models. Participants were followed from the date of baseline MRI to death or the end of follow-up (April 2022), whichever occurred first. Survival time (in years) served as the time-to-event variable, and mortality status as the event indicator. WMH severity groups were entered as categorical predictors (reference: low deviation), with age and sex included as covariates. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs were estimated using the enter method. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using log-minus-log survival plots and time-dependent covariate tests, with no violations detected.

Results

A total of 1237 participants (mean age 62.7 ± 8.8 years; 52.9% women) were included (Table 1). Normative WMH deviation revealed that individuals with greater age-inappropriate WMH burden were not older, but instead exhibited a disproportionately adverse vascular and clinical profile. Absolute WMH volumes increased monotonically across deviation categories (ANCOVA p < 0.001; all pairwise post-hoc p < 0.05; Fig. 2), confirming that higher deviation categories still corresponded to greater lesion burden after age- and sex-referencing. Although the group-averaged maps in Fig. 2 illustrate spatial distribution, quantitative total WMH burden was consistently higher when derived from FLAIR than from T1w images, as shown in Table 1. Sex distribution differed significantly, with the low-deviation group having the highest proportion of men, whereas education levels did not differ across groups.

Vascular risk profiles across normative model–defined WMH severity groups

This age- and sex-referenced WMH deviation was associated with a progressively adverse vascular phenotype (Table 1). Individuals with higher WMH deviation had a higher prevalence of hypertension and diabetes, as well as higher body mass index, waist circumference, and blood pressure levels, despite being younger.

Cognitive functions in relation to normative model–based WMH severity

Group-wise cognitive performance across the four WMH severity categories is provided in Supplementary Table S2. Fig. 3 shows that cognitive performance declined stepwise with increasing WMH deviation (detailed results in Supplementary Table S3). Each one-level increase in WMH deviation was associated with lower global cognition (MMSE β = –0.179, 95% CI –0.313 to –0.045, FDR-p = 0.034), poorer verbal memory (CVVLT β = –0.121, 95% CI –0.212 to –0.029, FDR-p = 0.034), and worse visuospatial construction (β = –0.330, 95% CI –0.611 to –0.050, FDR-p = 0.049). Among the cognitive measures, verbal fluency showed a borderline association with higher WMH deviation (β = –0.206, 95% CI [insert CI if available], FDR-p = 0.087), but this finding did not survive FDR correction. Greater WMH deviation was associated with impairments in global cognition, episodic memory, and visuospatial abilities, with visuospatial construction showing the strongest association.

Global and predefined subcortical regional gray matter volumes in relation to normative model–defined WMH severity

Group-wise global and regional GMVs across the four WMH severity categories are also summarized in Supplementary Table S2. Parallel changes were observed in brain structures. Greater WMH deviation was associated with lower global and regional gray matter volumes (Figure 3; detailed results in Supplementary Table S3). Each one-level increase in WMH deviation was significantly associated with reduced global GMV (β = –4.180, 95% CI –5.392 to –2.968; FDR-p < 0.001), as well as lower volumes in key subcortical structures, including the left hippocampus (β = –0.020, 95% CI –0.030 to –0.010; FDR-p < 0.001), right hippocampus (β = –0.018, 95% CI –0.029 to –0.007; FDR-p = 0.002), left thalamus (β = –0.036, 95% CI –0.058 to –0.014; FDR-p = 0.002), and right thalamus (β = –0.045, 95% CI –0.067 to –0.023; FDR-p < 0.001). In contrast, WMH deviation was not associated with global white matter volume (p = 0.666).

To examine whether the associations were dependent on the ordinal linearity assumption, we additionally performed categorical regression analyses using the low WMH deviation group as the reference. In these analyses, the high WMH deviation group also showed lower global GMV and lower bilateral hippocampal and thalamic GMV. Detailed per-group estimates are provided in Supplementary Table S4.

Association between normative model–defined WMH severity and all-cause mortality

During a mean follow-up of 9.4 ± 0.7 years, 120 participants (9.7%) died. Crude mortality incidence was highest in the high WMH deviation group (13.8 per 1000 person-years), whereas the moderate-deviation group had the lowest crude incidence (7.6 per 1000 person-years), consistent with its younger age (Table 2). In the age- and sex-adjusted Cox model, high WMH deviation was significantly associated with increased all-cause mortality compared with low WMH deviation (HR 1.90, 95% CI 1.16–3.09; p = 0.011). This association remained significant after additional adjustment for hypertension and diabetes, although the effect size was modestly attenuated (HR 1.72, 95% CI 1.05–2.82; p = 0.032). Mild and moderate WMH deviation were not significantly associated with mortality risk in either model.

Illustrative examples

Fig. 4 illustrates the clinical value of the normative WMH deviation framework. Individuals with comparable WMH volume ratios were classified differently according to age- and sex-referenced expectations, resulting in distinct clinical profiles. For example, a 53.5-year-old participant with a WMHV ratio of 0.09% was classified as having high WMH deviation despite minimal visual burden and subsequently exhibited cognitive impairment and mortality. In contrast, a 71.7-year-old participant with the same WMHV ratio fell within the age-expected range (low WMH deviation) and demonstrated preserved cognition and survival. A similar pattern was observed at higher WMHV ratios (e.g., 0.69%), where younger individuals were classified as high-deviation WMH due to greater deviation from age norms, whereas older individuals were classified as moderate-deviation WMH and showed better cognitive performance. These examples illustrate that WMH deviation provides an age- and sex-contextualized interpretation of WMH burden and may help identify individuals with clinically relevant disproportionate WMH burden despite modest absolute lesion volume.

Discussion

In this study, we show that deviation from age- and sex-expected WMH burden defines a clinically meaningful dimension of CSVD. Individuals with higher WMH deviation were not older, yet exhibited a disproportionately adverse vascular risk profile, worse cognitive performance, reduced gray matter volumes, and, in the high-deviation group, increased mortality risk. These findings indicate that interpreting WMH burden relative to age- and sex-expected norms provides clinically relevant information in addition to absolute WMH volume.

A key conceptual advance of this study is the use of normative modeling to contextualize WMH burden relative to age- and sex-expected norms, rather than relying solely on absolute WMH volume. Conventional WMH grading approaches, based on absolute lesion burden or visual scales, treat WMH volume as a direct proxy for disease severity and clinical risk, without considering whether the observed burden deviates from age-expected norms. Our findings illustrate that individuals with comparable WMH volumes may have different clinical profiles depending on whether their WMH burden is proportionate or disproportionate to age- and sex-expected norms. Younger individuals with relatively modest WMH volumes but high deviation exhibited adverse vascular, cognitive, and structural features, whereas older individuals with similar absolute burden but within age-expected ranges showed more favorable profiles. These observations suggest that WMH burden disproportionate to age- and sex-expected norms may capture an additional clinically relevant component of microvascular injury beyond demographic variation alone. Importantly, this framework does not imply that age-related WMH accumulation is non-biological or clinically irrelevant. Rather, it identifies the portion of WMH burden that is disproportionate to age- and sex-expected norms and evaluates whether this deviation is clinically informative.

Beyond global cognition, higher WMH deviation was associated with lower performance in verbal memory and visuospatial construction, suggesting that cognitive differences associated with disproportionate WMH burden may extend across multiple domains. This pattern is consistent with prior evidence indicating that cognitive impairment in sporadic CSVD is not limited to executive dysfunction but may involve broader cognitive domains. Given the relatively healthy, community-based nature of our cohort, these findings may reflect clinically detectable cognitive differences in individuals with covert CSVD. However, because the present study was cross-sectional, we cannot infer whether these cognitive differences represent early changes or determine their temporal sequence. Longitudinal studies are needed to clarify how WMH deviation relates to subsequent trajectories of domain-specific cognitive change.

In parallel, greater WMH deviation was associated with reduced global GMV and lower hippocampal and thalamic volumes. These subcortical structures have been implicated in CSVD-related brain vulnerability and cognitive impairment in prior studies. In the present study, however, we did not directly test whether hippocampal or thalamic volumes mediated or explained specific domain-level cognitive differences. Therefore, the cognitive and structural findings should be interpreted as parallel associations with WMH deviation rather than as evidence of a direct structure–function relationship. One plausible interpretation is that disproportionate WMH burden may reflect broader brain vulnerability beyond focal white matter lesions, potentially involving secondary gray matter changes through mechanisms such as disconnection and network-level dysfunction. However, causal relationships and temporal ordering cannot be established from the present cross-sectional data.

These findings have several important clinical implications. First, by identifying individuals with WMH accumulation disproportionate to age- and sex-expected norms, WMH deviation provides a framework for contextualizing lesion burden in covert CSVD, particularly among younger or middle-aged individuals with visually mild WMH. This is particularly relevant for younger or middle-aged individuals in whom WMH may appear visually mild but already reflect disproportionate WMH burden relative to age- and sex-expected norms (Fig. 4). Second, WMH deviation may support individualized risk stratification by contextualizing absolute lesion burden relative to age- and sex-expected norms. This may facilitate more accurate identification of individuals at higher risk of cognitive decline and adverse outcomes, and support more individualized monitoring and preventive strategies in clinical practice. Third, this framework has potential implications for clinical trial design. By helping identify individuals with disproportionate WMH burden relative to age- and sex-expected norms, WMH deviation may help refine participant selection for trials targeting early-stage CSVD, where conventional inclusion criteria based on absolute WMH thresholds may be insufficient. More broadly, these findings support a shift from absolute lesion quantification toward age- and sex-adjusted, individualized assessment of brain injury, aligning with emerging precision-medicine approaches in neurology. By capturing biologically meaningful deviation from normative aging trajectories, WMH deviation may serve as a scalable and clinically translatable marker for early disease detection, risk stratification, and intervention targeting in CSVD.

Although T2w-FLAIR imaging remains the conventional standard for WMH visualization, T1w–based WMH quantification provides a practical and scalable approach for large-scale normative modeling. Because FLAIR images were not uniformly available across the multi-site training cohort, an independent FLAIR-based normative model could not be constructed. Therefore, FLAIR-derived WMH volume in ILAS was used to validate lesion-burden consistency rather than to generate a parallel deviation-based classification. The strong correlation between T1w- and FLAIR-derived WMH volumes, together with the monotonic increase in FLAIR-derived WMH volume across T1w-based deviation categories, supports the robustness of the T1w-based classification with respect to conventional FLAIR-visible lesion burden. Because T1w sequences are consistently acquired across diverse clinical and research protocols, they enable the construction of large, heterogeneous reference datasets essential for estimating age-expected WMH burden. Prior studies have demonstrated that T1w-derived WMH measures are strongly correlated with FLAIR-based estimates despite systematically lower absolute values, and remain robust across imaging platforms and acquisition settings [13]. Consistent with these observations, we observed a high correlation between T1w- and FLAIR-derived WMH volumes in our cohort (Supplementary Figure S1), and T1w-based measures effectively differentiated WMH burden across deviation categories (Table 1; Fig. 2). Importantly, the goal of this framework is not to replace absolute lesion quantification, but to contextualize lesion burden relative to age- and sex-expected norms. In this context, the relative ranking of WMH burden, rather than its absolute magnitude, is most critical. Our findings demonstrate that T1w-based WMH measures, when integrated within a normative modeling framework, are sufficiently sensitive to capture inter-individual differences and age-inappropriate WMH accumulation. This is further supported by illustrative cases in which individuals with subtle or visually inconspicuous lesions were identified as having biologically advanced disease relative to their age (Fig. 4). Prior studies applying T1w-derived WMH measures across aging, Alzheimer’s disease, and CSVD populations further support its ability to capture biologically relevant WMH heterogeneity [[21], [22], [23]]. Together, these results support the validity of using T1w-based WMH quantification to detect the extent of deviation from normative aging trajectories.

Limitations

First, our analyses focused on WMH and did not incorporate other CSVD markers, such as lacunes, cerebral microbleeds, or enlarged perivascular spaces. Although WMH is the most prevalent and sensitive marker in community-based populations, integrating multimodal CSVD features may provide a more comprehensive assessment of disease burden. In addition, lacunes may affect T1-weighted WMH segmentation because lacunar cavities or peri‑lacunar gliotic changes may influence lesion classification or boundary estimation. Therefore, the lack of systematic assessment of lacunes, microbleeds, and enlarged perivascular spaces may have introduced residual measurement uncertainty and limited our ability to fully characterize total CSVD burden. Future studies incorporating multimodal CSVD markers are needed to validate and extend this deviation-based framework. Second, outcomes were limited to all-cause mortality; cause-specific events, including vascular mortality, stroke, and dementia, were not available, limiting mechanistic interpretation. Third, both the normative training cohort and the application cohort comprised Asian older adults. This addresses an important gap in predominantly Western-derived brain charts and provides a reference framework that is demographically closer to the Taiwanese application cohort. However, the training cohort was not evenly balanced across contributing sites, with one Japanese site accounting for a large proportion of the reference sample. Although hierarchical Bayesian regression was used to account for site/protocol-related variability, this site imbalance may still influence the normative estimates. Further validation in larger, more evenly distributed Asian reference datasets and in non-Asian populations is warranted. Fourth, the cross-sectional design of imaging and cognitive assessments precludes inference on temporal relationships between WMH deviation, brain atrophy, and cognitive decline. Future studies should incorporate multimodal CSVD markers, longitudinal cognitive and vascular outcomes, and diverse populations to establish the clinical utility of WMH deviation-based severity grading system for early detection, risk stratification, and intervention targeting. Finally, because the ILAS cohort consisted of relatively healthy, community-dwelling adults without stroke or dementia, some cognitive measures may have shown ceiling effects or skewed distributions, particularly the MMSE. Such distributional constraints may reduce sensitivity to subtle cognitive differences and may have led to underestimation of the associations between WMH deviation and cognitive performance. Future studies using more sensitive cognitive measures or longitudinal cognitive trajectories may better characterize the cognitive implications of age- and sex-inappropriate WMH burden. Importantly, WMH deviation should not be interpreted as isolating a single etiological pathway. WMH burden is likely under- or overdetermined at a given chronological age, and the present deviation-based framework was designed to provide an individualized age-referenced classification rather than to decompose the age-related and age-independent biological sources of WMH accumulation.

Conclusion

WMH deviation from age- and sex-expected norms provides clinically relevant information complementary to absolute lesion burden. It identifies individuals with adverse vascular profiles, multidomain cognitive impairment, subcortical gray matter atrophy, and increased mortality risk despite younger age. This framework supports age- and sex-referenced risk stratification of covert CSVD, with potential implications for early intervention and clinical trial design.
