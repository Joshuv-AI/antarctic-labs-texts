Responding to climate change: assessing the current situation and influencing factors of forest carbon sinks in China
Authors: Lidong Shi, Ming Xu, Yuntao Tan, Yucen Wu, Jiahui Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn response to global climate change and China’s “dual carbon” goals, forest carbon sinks, as a key nature-based solution, have gained importance in balancing human-induced carbon emissions and ecological restoration. This study examines the effectiveness of forest carbon sinks across 31 Chinese provinces from 2003 to 2018, using the forest stock expansion method to quantify the validity of carbon sinks. We explore the spatiotemporal evolution and regional disparities of forest carbon sink validity and identify the influence of research and development intensity, industrial structure upgrading, urbanisation level, government intervention degree, and economic development level factors. A spatial Durbin model is employed to assess both direct and indirect effects of natural and policy factors on the carbon sink’s effectiveness in both local and neighbouring provinces. Our findings reveal that forest carbon sink effectiveness follows a pattern of “higher in the west, faster in the east, and catching up in the central region”. The results indicate that increased research and development investment and optimised industrial structure positively influence carbon sink growth, whereas excessive government intervention hampers development. Urbanisation and economic development were found to have no significant direct effect. The spatial analysis shows that research and development intensity and industrial optimisation yield positive spillover effects on neighbouring provinces’ carbon sink growth, whereas government intervention and urbanisation yield negative, non-significant spillover effects. These findings suggest the need for strengthened regional innovation policies, improved forestry governance, and optimised forestry services to support the high-quality development of the forestry sector.Keywords: Forest carbon sink validity, Spatiotemporal heterogeneity, Spatial Durbin model, Spatial spillover effectSubject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Environmental sciences, Environmental social sciences

Introduction

Global warming represents one of the most pressing challenges facing humanity, with far-reaching consequences that endanger critical areas such as the food production system, public health infrastructure, ecological balance mechanisms, and socio-economic structures1. In response, carbon sinks, particularly forest carbon sinks, have emerged as a crucial strategy for mitigating climate change by absorbing carbon dioxide (CO₂) from the atmosphere. Forest ecosystems play a vital role in the global carbon cycle, fixing atmospheric CO₂ through photosynthesis and sequestering it in plants and soils2. This natural process makes forests key players in the fight against climate change, contributing significantly to the reduction of CO₂ concentrations in the atmosphere3. The concept of forest carbon sinks garnered global attention in the 1970s4–6 and has since evolved to include forest management practices, afforestation, and reforestation activities, which are now integral to climate change policies and sustainable development goals.

However, the effectiveness of forest carbon sinks is influenced by various factors, including regional differences in forest types, management practices, and socio-economic conditions. Enhancing the validity of forest carbon sinks is critical for mitigating global warming, reducing greenhouse gas emissions, and promoting sustainable and green development7. In particular, the spatiotemporal heterogeneity of forest carbon sink validity (FCSV) has become a key area of study, with varying carbon sequestration capacities observed across different regions and over time. It suggests that ecological protection policies, such as land-use restrictions, can help protect forest areas and enhance the efficacy of carbon sinks, ultimately improving food security8. Similarly, the role of data-driven agriculture in boosting carbon reduction efforts and advancing green agriculture is essential9.

Despite the growing recognition of the importance of forest carbon sinks, challenges remain in accurately measuring and optimising their effectiveness. Current measurement methods include biomass, micrometeorological, and volume-based approaches, each with its advantages and limitations. The biomass method, for example, estimates carbon storage based on forest biomass data10, while the micrometeorological method provides high-precision measurements by directly monitoring CO₂ flux11. The volume method, widely used for regional-scale assessments, utilises forest volume data to estimate carbon sequestration12,13. However, these methods often fail to capture the dynamic nature of forest carbon sinks, especially at the provincial scale, and may not fully account for spatial interactions across regions.

The driving FCSV are diverse and include urbanisation, natural disturbances, climate variables like temperature and precipitation, financial support, and policy interventions. Research has shown that urbanization, particularly through road expansion, leads to forest fragmentation and degradation14,15. In addition, environmental disturbances such as natural fires and invasive species have significant impacts on forest carbon stocks16,17. Meanwhile, government investments in ecological compensation are closely tied to the validity of carbon sinks3,18,19, highlighting the role of policy in driving forest conservation efforts. A study shows how environmental regulations, through technological innovation, can mediate the relationship between carbon sequestration and food security20. Moreover, another study discusses the impact of agricultural non-point source pollution on food security, emphasising the importance of regulatory measures to improve both agricultural sustainability and the effectiveness of carbon sinks21.

While existing studies have focused on static measurements of carbon sink effectiveness or on individual regions, there is a lack of research on the dynamic evolution of FCSV and its spatial interactions at the provincial scale. Moreover, the relationship between forest carbon sinks and broader socio-economic factors, such as digitalisation and industrial changes, remains underexplored. Recent studies on the effects of digitalisation in sectors such as animal husbandry22,23 and the enhanced carbon sequestration capacities of alpine grasslands24 suggest that technological advancements and ecosystem protection efforts can play a significant role in improving the validity of carbon sinks. A recent study argues that digital financial inclusion can significantly improve agricultural sustainability, thereby indirectly enhancing forest carbon sinks by promoting green practices25. A study emphasises the importance of the rural digital economy in fostering shared prosperity among farmers, thereby creating synergies with forest conservation efforts26. Additionally, another study highlights the moderating role of green technology innovation in ensuring high-quality economic development through environmental regulations27.

This study aims to address these gaps by exploring the spatiotemporal heterogeneity of FCSV across China, utilising a comprehensive framework that integrates spatial–temporal dynamics and spatial econometric analysis. By investigating the driving factors that influence forest carbon sinks at both the local and neighbouring provincial levels, this research offers valuable insights into how forest conservation and management practices can be optimised to achieve high-quality forestry development and contribute to global climate goals. Moreover, a study discusses the potential of environmental tax reform to promote industrial green development28. This concept aligns with the need for integrated policies that support sustainable forestry and green development.

This research contributes to the literature in three aspects: First, the construction of a provincial-level FCSV dynamic assessment framework to provide data support for differentiated ecological policies. Second, by exploring the driving factors of the forest carbon sink, this exploration enriches theoretical models and field applications in forestry. Third, it explores and reveals the spatial spillover pathways of FCSV and demonstrates the necessity of regional collaborative governance. By deepening understanding of forest carbon sinks, this paper provides valuable theoretical support for the realisation of high-quality, sustainable forestry development.

Research design

Policy background

In the context of the accelerated restructuring of the global climate governance system, forest carbon sequestration, as a core solution to achieving carbon neutrality and addressing climate change, has become the strategic focus of current Chinese and international policy design. Since 1998, China has launched the natural forest resource protection project for the first time, prohibiting commercial logging and implementing ecological compensation mechanisms, which have laid the ecological foundation for improving carbon sink capacity. Since the carbon intensity reduction target was first proposed, China has gradually built a three-in-one forest carbon sink policy system of “forest engineering drive, market mechanism innovation, and legal protection strengthening”. Then, in the National Climate Change Plan, for the first time, forest carbon sink was included in the nationally determined contribution, clarifying the planning goal of increasing forest stock by 4.5 billion cubic meters by 2030 compared with 2005, further promoting the implementation of the Action Plan on Establishing a Market-based and Diversified Ecological Protection Compensation Mechanism, and innovatively proposing the principle of “carbon greening”. It prioritises afforestation projects in areas with high carbon sink potential and promotes the interconnection of regional carbon sink trading markets through technology transfer and financial support.

At the international implementation level, the interaction between international policies and domestic practices has been significantly enhanced. Since 2005, when the EU Emissions Trading System began operating, its carbon offsetting mechanism has covered forestry carbon sequestration projects, forcing China to accelerate the development of a domestic policy framework. Shortly after the Glasgow Convention on Climate Change prioritised forest protection, China issued the Guiding Opinions on Scientific Greening, proposed the principle of “Determining green by carbon”, prioritised afforestation projects in areas with high carbon-sink potential, and established a national standard for carbon-sink measurement and monitoring. In addition, China has also launched the China-EU forest carbon sink certification mutual recognition program with European countries, which has covered 12 key forest areas in China. According to the China Forest Resources Report (2023), the national forest carbon sink has reached 850 million tons/year, accounting for 17.3% of industrial carbon emissions absorption. The contribution rate of policy-driven carbon sink validity improvement to the carbon neutrality goal exceeds the expected value by 12 percentage points. This policy evolution trajectory not only reflects China’s responsibility in global climate governance but also provides an institutional model for resolving the trade-off between ecological protection and economic development.

Definition and description of variables

Explained variables

Forest carbon sink validity (FCSV) is explicitly assessed using the forest stock expansion method. The forest stock expansion method has significant advantages in carbon sink assessment, and its core value is reflected in the four dimensions of data reliability, scientific method, policy adaptation, and technical compatibility. First of all, the method relies on a mature forest resource inventory system, and the stock data have long time series, wide coverage, and high precision, providing a sound basis for carbon sink estimation. Secondly, based on the tree-specific biomass expansion factor (BEF) and the conversion mechanism for the carbon content coefficient, transparent calculation of biomass-to-carbon storage is achieved. Thirdly, the method is highly suitable for policy practice, and the stock data can be directly linked to the measurement standard for forestry carbon sink trading to avoid conflicts in accounting systems. Explanatory variables

Research and development intensity, industrial structure upgrading, urbanisation level, government intervention degree, and economic development level are selected as core explanatory variables in this study, and their selection follows the three-dimensional criteria of theoretical fit, policy relevance, and data availability. First, explanatory variables should reflect the key driving mechanisms of FCSV, such as technological progress and industrial structure transformation. Second, the variables need to correspond to interventional policy tools, such as the impact of direct government intervention, urbanisation, economic development, and other macro policies. In terms of individual items, the selection of R&D Intensity (RDI) as the variable is based on endogenous growth theory, which emphasises that R&D investment can improve resource utilisation efficiency through technological progress, and technological innovation in forestry, such as carbon-sequencing tree species selection and remote sensing monitoring, can directly affect carbon sink density29. The Industrial Structure Upgrading (ISU) is selected because of the environmental Kuznets curve and ecological economics research, which pointed out that the increase of the proportion of the tertiary industry can achieve “structural dividends” by reducing the ecological footprint per unit GDP. The Urbanisation Level (UBL) is selected because urbanisation is an important indicator of a country or region’s economic development, and different urbanisation rates have distinct impacts on the formation of the forest carbon sink30. The Government Intervention Degree (GID) is selected because institutional economics emphasises that government behaviour affects environmental performance through resource allocation. However, excessive intervention may also lead to “government failure”31. The Economic Development Level (EDL) affects the carbon sink through the dynamic game of scale effect and technology effect. The expansion of economic scale may increase resource consumption, but technological upgrading can partially offset the negative impact, and controlling for EDL can eliminate the two-way effect of economic growth and avoid missing-variable bias32.

Data sources

This paper is based on the actual situation of FCSV. In order to reflect the availability and operability of data, the original data are from the National Forest Resources Inventory Report, China Forestry Statistical Yearbook, China Statistical Yearbook, and China Environment Yearbook. Considering that the data of each indicator may be missing in different years, in order to obtain more complete data resources as much as possible and reflect the latest situation of the FCSV level in China, the sample data were set as 2003, 2008, 2013, and 2018 according to the availability of forest resources inventory data. In selecting sample regions, due to the absence of data from the Hong Kong Special Administrative Region, Macao Special Administrative Region, and Taiwan Region of China, the remaining 31 provinces (including municipalities and autonomous regions) were selected. Space–time evolution trend

In order to further clarify the trend of FCSV over time and by region, this study conducts a detailed analysis of FCSV using a tabular format.

Estimation of nuclear density

In this study, Kernel Density Estimation was utilised to analyse the distributional dynamics of FCSV. Kernel density estimation is a non-parametric method that uses changes in random variables over time to describe the distribution pattern of regional disparities and estimate the probability density function of the data by placing a kernel function around each data point, then weighting and summing these kernel functions. The basic idea of kernel density estimation is that the density near a data point is higher, while it is lower farther away. The results of 3D kernel density estimation (Fig. 1) show that, based on the distribution position, the FCSV kernel density curve in China shows a continuous shift to the left. With the continued promotion of forest quality improvement projects, the proportion of low-value FCSV areas has been greatly reduced, and the spatial agglomeration of high-value areas has been enhanced, indicating that the continued development of FCSV in China is attributable not only to the Chinese government’s strong attention to forest protection and ecological construction. It also confirms the implementation effect of the Master Plan of Major Projects for the Protection and Restoration of National Important Ecosystems (2021–2035). Especially in economically developed areas such as the Yangtze River Delta and the Pearl River Delta, FCSV has been on the rise through the establishment of a three-tier system of “urban forest, country park, and ecological corridor”.

Based on the distribution trend, the central peak of the FCSV horizontal core density curve in China continues to rise, and its width is gradually increasing. This coexisting trend of “peaking” and “broad base” has dual policy implications. On the one hand, the increase in the height of the central peak confirms that the ecological function of the core forest area has been continuously enhanced by the project of returning farmland to the forest; On the other hand, the extension of the distribution width reflects that the ecological compensation mechanism makes the overall level of FCSV in our country present a trend of continuous improvement, showing a wider range of distribution, proving that our country’s emphasis on forest construction and protection is increasing.

From the perspective of distribution ductility, the kernel density curve of FCSV in China became longer, and distribution ductility tended to slow, indicating that the improvement rate of FCSV in China gradually slowed during the study period. This may be due to the significant investment in the initial stage of forest construction and protection in China, which has led to rapid growth in FCSV. As time goes by, the improvement of FCSV in our country has gradually become more stable, though it may be affected by limiting factors such as the scarcity of land resources, climate change, and so on.

From the trend of distribution polarisation, the bimodal feature of the nuclear density curve gradually appeared, the second peak moved from 2.4 to 3.1, and the peak interval expanded from 1.6 to 2.3. This bimodal distribution reveals a gradient in ecological construction effectiveness. The lengthening of the FCSV kernel density curve and the slowing down of distribution ductility may mean that it is more difficult to further improve FCSV, and more effective measures and strategies need to be taken to promote the further development of forest protection and ecological construction. For example, by strengthening the technology-enabling system.To use the stress-resistant tree species cultivation project to build an intelligent monitoring and early warning platform to break through the bottleneck of natural constraints.

Therefore, facing the goal of “double carbon”, it is suggested to build a differentiated upgrading path: implement the GEP accounting system in the ecological surplus area, and establish a cross-regional ecological product trading market; Implement precision improvement projects in ecologically fragile areas, and break resource constraints through new technologies such as bacteria-grass sand fixation and photovoltaic forest and grass. At the same time, it is necessary to improve the connection mechanism between ecological compensation transfer payment and carbon sink trading, and incorporate the increment of forest carbon sink into the provincial green development assessment system, to stimulate the endogenous impetus of FCSV improvement and help the ecological civilisation construction to move forward to the stage of high-quality development. By strengthening forest resource management and ecological protection, continuously improving FCSV levels in China is of great significance to China’s response to climate change and the realisation of sustainable development goals.

According to the regression results, the RDI is not significant in the basic models (1) and (3) without controlling for fixed effects. However, after introducing individual fixed effects, the models (2) and (4) show significant positive effects of 0.492 and 0.478, respectively. This suggests significant individual heterogeneity in the impact of R&D investment on carbon sink enhancement. The reason for this may be that technology-intensive regions can overcome the bottlenecks of traditional forest management efficiency through investments in technologies such as forest variety improvement and remote sensing monitoring. However, the lag effect of technological transformation prevents short-term models from capturing its effect, highlighting that the impact of R&D input on carbon sink effectiveness is often time-lagged and requires sustained investment and technological accumulation to manifest more clearly.

The ISU passed the 1% significance test in all three models, with coefficient values ranging from 0.463 to 0.687, verifying the positive effect of industrial structure upgrading on carbon sink enhancement. This is consistent with the structural dividend hypothesis in ecological economics, which argues that the service industry’s low consumption of land resources can effectively reduce the ecological footprint34. Meanwhile, new business models such as green finance and eco-tourism can also feed back into forest conservation through market mechanisms. However, attention must be paid to the non-significant coefficient of 0.167 in model (1), which suggests that optimising industrial structure may need to align with the regional development stage. Premature “de-industrialisation” could weaken the economic foundation and restrict ecological investment. Therefore, in promoting industrial structure optimisation, it is crucial to plan according to local economic development levels and resource endowments to avoid excessive deviation from traditional industrial foundations.

The effect of UBL shows spatial stage differences. The basic model (1) showed a significant negative effect of -0.470, confirming the crowding-out effect of land development on forest land resources in the early stage of urbanisation35. However, in model (4), controlled by dual fixed effects, the coefficient approaches zero, indicating that, with the maturation of urbanisation, cities with suitable conditions can achieve a “smart shrinkage” of carbon sink effects through compensation mechanisms such as three-dimensional greening and ecological corridors. This result expands the application of the “compact city” theory and emphasises the need to carefully consider how to promote a dynamic balance between urbanisation and carbon sink effects.

The sign reversal of the GID coefficient deserves further discussion. The positive effect of 0.283 in model (1) reflects the short-term effectiveness of incentive policies such as fiscal subsidies36. However, after controlling for spatial–temporal effects, significant negative values of approximately -0.7 appear in models (2) and (4), suggesting that excessive administrative intervention may distort market mechanisms. From an institutional economics perspective, command-and-control policies tend to lead to the shifting of compliance costs, and the model differences indicate that the absence of market-based tools weakens policy effectiveness. Government intervention should be moderate, avoiding excessive interference that disrupts market mechanisms. In particular, when designing policies, it is important to consider how to enhance the long-term effectiveness and adaptability of policies through market-oriented measures.

The effect of EDL shows a significant negative correlation of -0.091 in the basic model (1), which is consistent with the Environmental Kuznets Curve hypothesis. This suggests that economic growth generally increases environmental pressure and resource consumption37. However, the non-significant coefficient in the fixed-effect model reveals the complexity of its effects. This may be because, after accounting for individual and time effects, the scale effect of economic growth and the technological effect cancel each other out, which echoes the controversy of the “decoupling theory.” This reveals that relying solely on economic growth to improve carbon sink effectiveness is difficult. Therefore, policies should focus on balancing economic growth with environmental protection to avoid the negative ecological effects brought about by unchecked economic development.

Spatial regression model

Spatial autocorrelation test

Before building the model, it is necessary to investigate whether there is a spatial correlation in FCSV. In the spatial econometric analysis, the Global Moran’s I index is used to describe the global spatial autocorrelation, which is usually used to test the spatial correlation. According to the test results in (Table 4), Moran’s I index of FCSV all passed the significance test of 5% or above, indicating that there were significant spatial autocorrelations in China’s FCSV in 2003, 2008, 2013, and 2018. In order to further explore the positive distribution characteristics of FCSV, it is necessary to use the Moran index scatter plot to conduct a re-analysis of provinces in each quadrant.

Table 5 shows that the number of provinces in the High-High cluster increased from 6 in 2013 to 8 in 2018, reflecting the spatial spillover effects of projects such as natural forest protection and returning farmland to forest. Neimenggu shifted from the High-Low outlier quadrant in 2003 to the continuous High-High cluster, illustrating the effectiveness of the Three-North Shelterbelt System. The increase in its carbon sink density has driven neighbouring provinces to exhibit positive spatial dependence, highlighting regional heterogeneity in spillover effects from large-scale ecological projects.

The heterogeneity of spillover effects across the eastern, central, and western regions can be observed as follows: In the eastern region, provinces like Hainan and Chongqing transitioned quickly to the High-High cluster, driven by both government policies and industrial upgrades. The dense infrastructure and regional cooperation have facilitated the rapid diffusion of positive spillover effects from ecological projects. In the central region, the “economic-ecological” paradox is evident in Low-Low clusters. Provinces such as Beijing-Tianjin-Hebei, the Yangtze River Delta, and six central provinces have remained in the Low-Low quadrant, reflecting the inhibiting effects of industrialisation and urbanisation on carbon sinks39. These regions, with per capita GDP above the national average, exhibit “economic highland—ecological depression” characteristics. The spillover effects here are limited due to the negative spatial influence of industrialisation on ecological projects. In the western region, spatial heterogeneity in transitional quadrants (the Low–High and High-Low outliers) is notable. Provinces like Qinghai and Xinjiang, limited by the ecological carrying capacity of arid areas, have low carbon sink background values. However, proximity to high-carbon-sink provinces such as Xizang and Sichuan enables cross-domain ecological compensation policies, enhancing regional cooperation and spillover effects. In the High-Low outlier, Fujian stands out with its top-ranked forest coverage rate. Surrounded by industrialised provinces such as Guangdong and Zhejiang, Fujian forms a “high-value island,” highlighting how spillover effects in coastal areas are shaped by both economic development and ecological advantages.

Spillover effects vary across the eastern, central, and western regions, influenced by local economic structures, industrialisation, and ecological policies. While eastern regions experience rapid, positive spillover effects due to strong infrastructure and economic integration, the central region faces challenges from the conflict between industrial growth and ecological conservation. In contrast, the western region benefits from inter-regional cooperation, where ecological advantages spill over to neighbouring areas, demonstrating how regional dynamics shape the effectiveness of forest carbon sinks.

First, regarding the technology diffusion and knowledge spillover effect of R&D intensity. In the SDM model in Table 6, the direct regression result of RID is 0.283 at the 5% level, while the coefficient of spatial lag term is 0.440, which is also significant at the 5% level, indicating that local R&D investment not only directly improves carbon sink validity through technological innovation, such as forest carbon sequestration gene improvement and remote sensing monitoring technology application, but also generate knowledge spillover through geographical proximity and promote the improvement of technological efficiency in neighboring provinces30. The coefficients of RID in the SEM and SAR models are 0.339 and 0.367, which are significantly higher than the direct effect observed in the SDM. This difference indicates that the traditional model may overestimate the independent contribution of R&D intensity by ignoring the spatial interaction effects of the explanatory variables. The results in Table 7 are largely consistent with those in Table 6, confirming the spatial spillover effect of R&D intensity.

Second, there exists a marked difference between the adjacency matrix and the distance matrix in terms of industrial impact. Under the 0–1 matrix, which represents adjacency relationships, the results fully align with the “structural dividend” theory. An increase in the tertiary industry share within a region not only reduces local ecological pressure and promotes carbon sequestration, as evidenced by a positive direct effect, but also drives neighbouring regions to benefit collectively through industrial linkages and demonstration effects, which is reflected in a positive spillover effect. Under the distance matrix, a competitive “zero-sum” effect emerges. Industrial upgrading in a region characterised by an increased tertiary sector share may be accompanied by the relocation of high-energy-consuming, high-polluting industries to geographically adjacent areas. This results in carbon sink benefits for the originating region, indicated by a negative direct effect that signifies reduced pressure on carbon sinks, but leads to carbon sink losses for neighbouring regions, shown by a positive spillover effect that creates a contradiction. One possible explanation is that the industrial-upgrading region disseminates green services, technologies, or demand to surrounding areas, thereby partially offsetting the negative effects of industrial relocation. However, the net effect still depends on the overall impact. This inconsistency indicates that the spatial effects of industrial structure are highly complex, potentially involving both cooperative and competitive mechanisms simultaneously.

Third, the impact of government intervention exhibits strong “spatial context dependency.” Under the adjacency matrix, government interventions, such as ecological compensation and forestry investment, demonstrate powerful positive effects. They enhance local carbon sinks while generating significant positive spatial spillovers through policy coordination and joint ecological protection and governance, thereby benefiting neighbouring areas. Under the distance matrix, government interventions show pronounced negative spatial spillover effects. This implies that when a region intensifies forestry ecological interventions, it may shift economic activities or environmental pressures to geographically adjacent areas, thereby suppressing forest carbon sinks in surrounding regions. This partially corroborates the risk of “government failure,” where fragmented local policies may trigger interregional “beggar-thy-neighbour” effects. The lack of significant direct effects suggests that local benefits may be offset or inherently unstable.

Spatial Durbin model effect decomposition

The spatial interaction terms of SDM are explained in detail above. However, the value of the regression coefficient cannot be used directly to discuss the marginal effects of various driving factors on the forest carbon sink, because simple point regression analysis of spatial spillover effects across regions incorrectly estimates and requires a partial differential explanation of variable changes. That is, direct and indirect effects are used to explain the influence of independent variables in a given region on the local region and on dependent variables in other regions.

The spatial Durbin model effect decomposition results in Tables 8 and 9 indicate that the influence of various driving factors on FCSV exhibits significant spatial heterogeneity. The interaction mechanism between their direct and indirect effects profoundly shapes the evolutionary pathways of regional carbon sink patterns. Local R&D improves carbon sequestration validity through technological penetration, such as forest tree genetic improvement and pest control, while spatial spillover realises cross-domain knowledge sharing by relying on regional innovation networks. For example, forestry technology patents in the eastern coastal provinces spread to the central provinces through geographical proximity, thereby challenging the traditional linear regression view of “isolated innovation”.

The total effect of ISU is as high as 1.400, of which the indirect effect accounts for 66.9%, highlighting the core value of regional industrial collaborative optimisation. The increase in the proportion of local tertiary industry directly protects forest land resources by reducing demand for industrial land, while the industrial upgrading of neighbouring provinces generates spatial spillovers through the reconstruction of the green supply chain and jointly improves the validity of the carbon sink. From the perspective of distance matrices, signs of “beggar-thy-neighbour” competition may emerge, where local upgrades could come at the expense of surrounding areas. Policy formulation must emphasise interregional industrial coordination planning and ecological compensation mechanisms to ensure that industrial upgrading is both green and collaborative.

The total effect of UBL is -0.738. Although UBL does not pass the significance test, the strength of its indirect effect is -0.584, which reveals the possible spatial risk. Core urban expansion intensifies forest land fragmentation in neighbouring provinces through the “siphon effect”, while the inefficient spread of small and medium sized towns blocks ecological corridors. This negative cycle of “centre-periphery” confirms the heterogeneity of spatial panel quantile regression. From the perspective of the distance matrix, this result further confirms the conclusions drawn from previous analyses. The net impact of urbanisation on forest carbon sinks is statistically indeterminable, as its effects may be absorbed by other variables or involve complex nonlinear relationships. Within the current model framework and variable system, it does not constitute a robust driving factor.

The strong negative total effect of GID is −2.071, presenting a double dilemma of “local distortion—regional deterioration”. The direct effect is −0.653, reflecting the interference of administrative directives with market mechanisms, while the indirect effect is −1.418, exposing the inter-provincial “race to the bottom” triggered by policy imitation. The effect decomposition of EDL fails the significance test, further falsifying the universality of the environmental Kuznets curve. From the distance matrix, the negative effects are even more pronounced. The direct effect is significantly negative, while the indirect effect is −4.587 and the total effect is −5.250, exhibiting extremely large and significant negative values. This is a crucial finding. The current model of government intervention generally inhibits forest carbon sequestration and exhibits strong negative externalities. The substantial negative spillover effects observed in the distance matrix indicate that government interventions in one region may displace development pressures or destructive activities into geographically adjacent areas, resulting in greater overall ecological losses.

Conclusions and policy recommendations

Conclusions

The FCSV exhibits a steady upward trend over time, with clear regional and spatio-temporal characteristics. It shows a typical “higher in the west, faster in the east, and catching up in the central region” pattern, indicating that forest carbon sinks in China are likely to continue increasing both presently and in the future. The benchmark regression reveals that enhancing R&D and optimising the industrial structure significantly promote the growth of carbon sinks, while excessive government intervention significantly hinders this growth. Additionally, urbanisation and economic development have no significant impact on carbon sink validity. Spatial effect regression results show that both increased R&D intensity and industrial structure optimisation have positive indirect effects on neighbouring provinces’ carbon sink development. In contrast, government intervention inhibits the development of carbon sinks in adjacent regions, while the indirect effects of economic development and urbanisation are insignificant.

Policy recommendations

Based on the above conclusions, this paper proposes the following policy recommendations:

First, strengthen forestry infrastructure. To promote the modernisation of the basic forestry system, it is crucial to establish a coordinated national, provincial, and county-level forestry ecological security network. This should integrate systems for forest fire prevention, pest control, and ecological monitoring, with a focus on deploying intelligent monitoring nodes in western China’s natural forests. In parallel, projects aimed at improving plantation forest quality and carbon sink output, particularly in the eastern regions, should be prioritised. This includes enhancing soil quality and water management, as well as increasing technological input and innovation in forestry science. Support for advanced forestry technologies and better forest management will boost both the carbon sink potential and forest sustainability.

Second, establish inter-provincial coordination mechanisms. Coordination mechanisms should be developed in key urban agglomerations such as the Yangtze River Delta, Pearl River Delta, and Chengdu-Chongqing regions, with a focus on integrating neighbouring provinces’ forest carbon sink development into regional ecological assessments. To incentivise positive spillover effects, ecological compensation funds, green financial support, and other market-based mechanisms should be introduced. Furthermore, it is essential to balance forest land conversion with compensatory mechanisms during urbanisation. A strict examination and approval system should be implemented for forest land use in construction projects, along with an ecological restoration plan post-occupation. This ensures the orderly supplementation of forest land resources and supports cross-regional ecological compensation efforts, guiding the sustainable use of forest resources.

Third, optimise financial and ecological compensation policies. Building a market-oriented ecological compensation system is vital for advancing forest carbon sink projects. The government should take a leading role in creating conditions for market mechanisms through policy frameworks, planning guidance, and financial support. A clear ownership and value realisation mechanism for ecological products, such as forest carbon sinks, should be established, including quantitative assessments of ecosystem services. By integrating forest carbon sinks into the national carbon emission trading market, their ecological value can be monetised through market pricing. This will provide continuous economic incentives for ecological conservation. Additionally, green financial tools like green bonds, green funds, and carbon sink-based financing products should be developed to enhance financial support for green and low-carbon development.

Research deficiencies and prospects

Future research can be in-depth from the following two levels: First, deepen the sample data. The data samples used in this study are at the provincial level, which allows us to conduct macro-level analysis. However, to have a more detailed and in-depth discussion of the impact of forest carbon sinks in deeper geographical areas, future studies can refine the granularity of forest carbon sink data and further expand the sample to prefecture-level cities or even county-level data. This will help us to more accurately show the differences in forest carbon sink under different geographical, economic backgrounds, and resource endowment conditions. The second is further to evaluate the driving factors of the forest carbon sink. Although the fixed effect model is used in this paper to study the relationship between forest carbon validity, there is still a lack of consideration for the future driving factors of the forest carbon sink. Future studies should introduce more detailed research indicators and use more comprehensive measurement models to explore the long-term effects and potential impacts of the forest carbon sink.
