Self-fueling catalysis-driven membrane destabilization triggers CSC-enriched tumors ablation
Authors: Lin Huang, Basheng Hu, Guochao Wu, Wenjie Ma, Jiaoyang Qiu, Jiaoyang Zhu, Jing Yang, Haobin Cai, Qingdeng Fan, Bin Ren, Chunmei Chen, Zehui Wang, Jie Feng, Yitong Li, Sugeun Yang, Chenggong Yan, Xiangrong Yu, Zhigang Liu, Zheyu Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEradication of cancer stem cells (CSC) enriched tumors remains a formidable challenge due to their intrinsic drug resistance and robust cholesterol-driven anti-ferroptotic defenses. Herein, we report a cholesterol oxidase (COD)-loaded hollow mesoporous zinc-copper sulfide (COD@HMZCS-HA) nanomedicine designed to eliminate CSC via self-fueling catalysis-driven membrane destabilization. After targeted tumor accumulation, the released components function synergistically to overcome therapeutic resistance. Specifically, COD-mediated cholesterol depletion acts as an indispensable sensitizing step by dismantling the biophysical membrane barrier of protective lipid rafts and inactivating the 7-dehydrocholesterol (7-DHC)-mediated endogenous “molecular brake” on LPO. Concurrently, a self-fueling catalytic cycle, involving Cu+-mediated •OH generation, Zn2+-induced •O2⁻ accumulation and H2S-triggered hypoxia relief and intracellular acidification, drove the massive amplification and propagation of lethal LPO storm. Through simultaneous sustainment of local oxygen availability and abrogation of cholesterol-dependent membrane defenses, COD@HMZCS-HA effectively bypasses classical resistance pathways, culminating in irreversible CSC ferroptosis. In vitro and in vivo studies demonstrate that the COD@HMZCS-HA shows potent antitumor and antimetastatic efficacy due to the extensive ablation of CSC coupled with the disruption of invasive lipid rafts. Collectively, the developed self-fueling catalysis strategy simultaneously overcomes the hypoxic TME barrier and disrupts cholesterol-dependent anti-ferroptotic defenses, offering a promising therapeutic paradigm for elimination of CSC-enriched tumors.Graphical Abstract Cholesterol oxidase (COD)-loaded hollow mesoporous zinc-copper sulfide (COD@HMZCS-HA) nanomedicine was developed to enhance the therapeutic efficacy against CSC-enriched tumors through a self-fueling catalysis-driven membrane destabilization strategy that integrates hypoxic relief, cholesterol catabolism, lipid raft disruption, intracellular LPO storm propagation, and CSC stemness suppression. Introduction

Cancer recurrence and metastasis, which are largely driven by therapy-resistant cancer stem cells (CSC), account for approximately 90% of cancer-related mortality [1]. CSC possesses formidable self-renewal, multipotency, and tumorigenic capacities, underpinning intratumoral heterogeneity, relapse, and dissemination [2–4]. Conventional therapies often fail to eradicate CSC [5–7]. Although differentiation therapies (e.g., all-trans retinoic acid, ATRA) show potential against CSC, their clinical utility is hampered by off-target effects on normal stem cells and ineffectiveness against residual non-CSC, which may reacquire stemness and drive tumor recurrence [8, 9]. Thus, novel strategies capable of selectively and effectively eliminating CSC represent an urgent and unmet clinical need.

The reported ferroptosis therapy (FT), involving excessive lipid peroxidation (LPO) accumulation to induce plasma membrane rupture, highlights the vulnerability of CSC because of their iron addiction for stemness maintenance and self-renewal activation [10–12]. However, the tumor FT is robustly constrained not only by intrinsic antioxidant defenses (e.g., the typical glutathione (GSH) peroxidase 4 (GPX4) system), but also by dysregulated cholesterol metabolism. Specifically, CSC exhibit hyperactivation of the mevalonate pathway, leading to aberrant cholesterol deposition in the plasma membrane [13].

Cholesterol is essential for membrane rigidity, integrity and fluidity [14], and its aberrant accumulation in tumor microenvironment (TME) promotes proliferation, metastasis, and immune evasion [15]. Crucially, the elevated cholesterol in tumor cell membranes erects a robust biophysical barrier by stabilizing lipid rafts, increasing membrane order (greater rigidity and reduced fluidity) that impedes drug penetration and curtails the propagation of LPO [16]. Moreover, 7-dehydrocholesterol (7-DHC), a key intermediate in cholesterol biosynthesis, plays a function as a potent endogenous “molecular brake” through its ability to scavenge free radicals and quench LPO, thereby promoting ferroptosis escape [17]. Collectively, cholesterol metabolism emerges as a master regulator dictating ferroptosis sensitivity, representing a compelling therapeutic target, particularly against CSC.

Prior studies have reported that cholesterol oxidase (COD)-mediated cholesterol depletion offers a potential strategy by converting cholesterol to cholestenone and H2O2, which can promote Fenton or Fenton-like reactions and tumor ferroptosis [18]. Nonetheless, the efficacy of COD-based therapy is severely constrained by its strict oxygen dependence [19, 20]. This limitation is particularly critical for targeting CSC, which predominantly reside in the hypoxic TME. Under such conditions, hypoxia-inducible factor 1α (HIF-1α) signaling actively maintains CSC stemness, dormancy, and resistance to conventional therapies (including FT), consequently precluding COD-mediated intracellular cholesterol catabolism from initiating the intended catalytic enhancement and disruption of the cholesterol-derived membrane barrier [21, 22]. In addition, as COD is a labile biomacromolecule that is easily deactivated, its effective delivery requires strategies enabling high loading content, targeted accumulation, and TME-responsive release [20]. Therefore, developing a strategy that can simultaneously supply oxygen/ROS, overcome hypoxia, and stabilize COD to fuel sustained cholesterol depletion remains a major hurdle.

In this study, we propose a self-fueling catalysis-driven membrane destabilization strategy that can simultaneously overcome the hypoxic TME barrier to sensitize CSC and disrupt cholesterol-dependent membrane defenses against ferroptosis, thereby achieving potent therapeutic efficacy for CSC-enriched tumors. To realize this strategy, hollow mesoporous zinc-copper sulfide (HMZCS-HA) nanocapsules, featuring unique hollow cores (~ 67 nm) and optimal pore size (~ 8 nm), were synthesized via a Kirkendall effect-driven one-pot approach utilizing hyaluronic acid (HA) as a stabilizer. Subsequently, COD was encapsulated into the core space to form COD@HMZCS-HA nanomedicines (Scheme 1a). The HMZCS-HA shell can prevent COD from premature deactivation and minimize off-target toxicity from unwanted leakage.

(b-e): Schematic diagram for the therapeutic mechanism of COD@HMZCS-HA against CSC-enriched metastatic malignancies, including the key processes: (b) cascaded amplification and propagation of ferroptosis via self-fueling inner cyclic catalysis; (c) disruption of the cholesterol-derived biophysical barrier and “molecular brake” (7-DHC); (d) CSC elimination through sustainment of intratumoral oxygen availability and ROS surge; (e) the damage to invasive lipid rafts due to cholesterol exhaustion. Surface anchoring of COD@HMZCS-HA with HA enables selective binding to CD44 receptors overexpressed on tumor cells, thereby enhancing endocytosis and promoting tumor-specific accumulation. Upon internalization into tumor cells including CSC, the acidic environment of endosomes/lysosomes triggers a pH-responsive release of COD, H2S, Cu+, and Zn2+ from COD@HMZCS-HA. Mechanistically, the released components drive a sequential therapeutic cascade. The released COD acts as the initiator by exhausting intracellular cholesterol pools to continuously supply endogenous H2O2. The in situ generated H2O2 serves as a substrate for Cu+, which mediates potent Fenton-like reactions to produce highly cytotoxic hydroxyl radicals (•OH). Concurrently, intracellular Zn2+ overload precipitates severe mitochondrial stress, thereby exacerbating the accumulation of •O2−. Furthermore, the released H2S functions as a specific microenvironmental modulator that not only accelerates nanomedicines disassembly and Cu+-catalyzed kinetics via intracellular acidification but also synergistically inhibits mitochondrial respiration (Scheme 1b).

Notably, while COD is an oxygen-dependent enzyme typically inactive in hypoxic CSC niches, our COD@HMZCS-HA system overcomes the above limitation via dual “oxygen-saving” and “oxygen-producing” pathways. Specifically, H2S suppresses cytochrome c oxidase (COX IV) to reduce endogenous oxygen consumption, while Cu2+ produced through Cu+-mediated Fenton-like reaction reacts with Zn2+-induced mitochondrial •O2− to regenerate Cu+ and simultaneously produce O2. This self-fueling catalysis cycle sustains local oxygen availability, thereby reactivating COD-mediated cholesterol catabolism even under hypoxic conditions (Scheme 1b). The resulting cholesterol depletion disrupts the biophysical membrane barrier via lipid raft destabilization and increased membrane fluidity, and inactivates the “molecular brake” on LPO by reducing 7-DHC levels, which together ultimately promote the amplification and propagation of ferroptosis (Scheme 1c). The sustainment of oxygen availability coupled with the ROS surge within the TME continuously eliminates CSC (Scheme 1d). The ablation of CSC, together with extensive disruption of invasive lipid rafts, synergistically suppresses tumor invasion and metastasis (Scheme 1e). To sum up, the self-fueling catalysis-driven membrane destabilization resulted from COD@HMZCS-HA can eliminate CSC-enriched tumors, whose mechanism includes sustainment of local oxygen availability, cholesterol catabolism, lipid raft disruption, intracellular LPO storm propagation, and CSC stemness suppression (Scheme 1f).

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of COD@HMZCS-HA nanomedicine

The schematic fabrication route of COD@HMZCS-HA nanomedicine is illustrated in Scheme 1a. The synthesis begins with the formation of HMZCS-HA nanocapsule through a Kirkendall effect-driven one-pot process, in which asymmetric diffusion of metal cations and sulfide anions dictates the structural formation [23]. Zinc-doped Cu2O precursors, synthesized in the presence of sodium hyaluronate (NaHA), were subsequently reacted with Na2S solution to form a bimetallic sulfide shell on the precursor surface. The slower inward diffusion of S2− ions relative to the outward migration of Zn2+ and Cu2+ ions induces the formation of Kirkendall vacancies, resulting in well-defined hollow cores with mesoporous sulfide shells.

HMZCS-HA1-4 nanocapsules were synthesized with various concentrations of feeding Na2S, whose detailed synthesis conditions and characterization data are summarized in Table S1. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images (Fig. 1a-d) reveal that the synthesis remains diffusion-limited at lower Na2S concentrations (0.33–1.32 M), enabling controlled cavity formation. In contrast, higher concentrations (2.64 M) shift the growth mechanism towards a reaction-dominated regime, resulting in thick shells, suppression of void formation, and particle aggregation. Notably, HA serves dual roles as both capping agent and stabilizer, facilitating surface functionalization for targeted drug delivery applications.

Among HMZCS-HA1-4 nanocapsules, HMZCS-HA3 characterized by the unique hollow structure (hollow core diameter: ~ 67 nm; shell thickness: ~ 38 nm) and narrow size distribution, were selected as the optimal candidates for further drug loading. Quantitative analysis using inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES) reveals that Zn and Cu contents are 15.7% and 41.2%, respectively. Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra (Fig. S1) confirms the presence of surface-bound HA for HMZCS-HA3, as indicated by the characteristic absorption bands at 1643 cm− 1 (amide I, C = O stretching) and 1422 cm− 1 (COO− symmetric stretching) [24].

Subsequently, the therapeutic agent COD was loaded into HMZCS-HA3 cavities by varying the initial feeding concentration, yielding COD1-3-HMZCS-HA3. The results of UV-vis spectrometry (Fig. S2a, b) identify that COD2@HMZCS-HA3 has a relatively high drug loading efficiency (81.1%) and drug loading content (20.3%) (Table S2).

TEM (Fig. 1e) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (Fig. 1f) confirm that the synthesized COD2@HMZCS-HA3 nanomedicine possesses uniform morphology and monodispersity with ~ 100 nm of particle size. Compared to HMZCS-HA3, which displays a distinct hollow mesoporous architecture, COD2@HMZCS-HA3 exhibits less pronounced internal cavities and darker contrast, suggesting successful COD encapsulation within the pores. Elemental mapping by high-angle annular dark-field scanning TEM (HAADF-STEM) demonstrates homogeneous distributions of Cu, Zn, S, and O across the shell (Fig. 1g), and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) analysis corroborates the presence of Cu, Zn, S, O, N, and C signals (Fig. S3), which both demonstrate the successful fabrication of COD2@HMZCS-HA3.

From X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern of COD2@HMZCS-HA3 (Fig. 1h), seven prominent diffraction peaks are observed, corresponding closely to the (111), (220), (311), and (331) lattice planes of ZnS, as well as the (103), (006), and (116) planes of CuS, which confirm the coexistence of ZnS and CuS crystalline phases within the nanostructure [25].

The surface elemental composition and oxidation states of metal ions were further analyzed using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). The survey spectrum of COD2@HMZCS-HA3 (Fig. 1i; Fig. S4a-e) reveals characteristic signals for Zn 2p, Cu 2p, O 1s, C 1s, N1s and S 2p, which are consistent with the previously described EDX results. High-resolution Cu 2p spectra exhibit binding energy peaks at 931.9, 933.5, 951.7, and 953.3 eV, corresponding to Cu 2p3/2 and Cu 2p1/2 spin-orbit components (Fig. 1j). Peak deconvolution indicates that Cu+ and Cu2+ species respectively account for approximately 79.9% and 20.1% based on integrated peak area analysis.

Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) analysis (Fig. 1k, l) reveals that pristine HMZCS-HA3 nanocapsules have 7.8 nm of pore size and 349.8 m2 g− 1 of specific surface area. These properties theoretically enable efficient encapsulation of COD (35–40 kDa, ~ 4 nm hydrodynamic diameter) to protect its bioactivity and minimize off-target effects. As expected, a reduced pore diameter (5.6 nm) and specific surface area (142.2 m2 g− 1) are observed in COD2@HMZCS-HA3, further validating the successful loading of COD into the nanocapsule pores.

Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis (Fig. 1m) reveals 131.7 and 137.9 nm of hydrodynamic diameters (dh) for HMZCS-HA3 and COD2@HMZCS-HA3 with 0.151 and 0.187 of polydispersity indices (PDI). Additionally, a shift of Zeta potential from − 28.9 mV (HMZCS-HA3) to -22.3 mV (COD2@HMZCS-HA3) is also observed (Fig. S5). The alterations of dh and Zeta potential also demonstrate the successful preparation of COD2@HMZCS-HA3. Furthermore, the negatively charged surface of COD2@HMZCS-HA3 significantly enhances colloidal stability in aqueous physiological media (e.g., H2O, PBS, complete cell culture medium), maintaining favorable dispersion even after 10 days of incubation at 37 °C (Fig. S6a-c).

TME-responsive release, degradation, and enhanced Fenton-like catalytic activity

Figure 2a-c depict the release profiles of COD, Cu ions, and Zn ions from COD2@HMZCS-HA3 under different pH conditions. The data show a markedly accelerated release in acidic environments, with over 80% of drugs released within 12 h at pH 5.5. In contrast, the release in neutral PBS (pH 7.4) remains minimal, below 10% over the same period at 37 °C. Fig. S7 demonstrates that negligible H2S is released at pH 7.4, whereas substantial H2S generation is observed at pH 6.7 and 5.5. These findings validate the pH-responsive nature of COD2@HMZCS-HA3, highlighting its potential for targeted tumor release and therapeutic efficacy (Fig. 2d).

Figure 2e depicts the TME-responsive degradation characteristics of COD2@HMZCS-HA3 after exposure to an acidic PBS solution (pH 6.7) for varying time intervals. TEM observations reveal a progressive degradation of COD2@HMZCS-HA3 within the acidic milieu, culminating in almost complete decomposition after 24 h of incubation.

Given the promising TME-responsive biodegradability and controlled release properties of COD2@HMZCS-HA3, the Fenton-like catalytic activity was evaluated through in vitro •OH generation assays. Specifically, the chromogenic oxidation of 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) and the decolorization of methylene blue (MB) were employed as indicators of ROS production under varying conditions. As shown in Fig. 2f, TMB oxidation yields blue-colored oxidized TMB (oxTMB), which exhibits a characteristic absorbance peak at 652 nm. The concurrent decolorization of MB further indicates ROS generation. Notably, robust •OH production occurs exclusively under acidic conditions (pH 6.7 or 5.5) and requires the simultaneous presence of COD2@HMZCS-HA3 and H2O2. The increase of TMB absorbance at 652 nm with decreasing pH indicates a pH-dependent enhancement of •OH generation by COD2@HMZCS-HA3 (Fig. 2g). Additionally, Fig. 2h illustrates the concentration- and time-dependent accumulation of •OH, emphasizing the catalytic robustness of COD2@HMZCS-HA3.

Similarly, pronounced degradation of MB is observed in acidic solutions containing both COD2@HMZCS-HA3 and H2O2, as evidenced by gradual color fading over time (Fig. 2i, j). These findings are consistent with the results of the TMB oxidation assays. The strong Fenton-like catalytic activity of COD2@HMZCS-HA3 is further validated by electron spin resonance (ESR) spectroscopy. Enhanced •OH signal is found when COD2@HMZCS-HA3 is treated with weak acid solutions (Fig. 2k), providing additional evidence of targeted ROS generation in the TME.

In addition to Cu+-mediated Fenton-like reactivity, the GSH consumption ability of COD2@HMZCS-HA3 through the Cu2+-driven mechanism was further investigated using the 5,5′-dithiobis(2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB) assay, which reacts with GSH to form a yellow product with maximum absorbance at 412 nm (Fig. 2l). As shown in Fig. 2m, the absorbance at 412 nm decreases progressively with acidification, correlating with pH-dependent Cu2+ release and concurrent GSH oxidation by COD2@HMZCS-HA3. Notably, the GSH consumption performance of COD2@HMZCS-HA3 exhibits a concentration-dependent pattern (Fig. 2n).

Tumor-specific targeting and in vitro antitumor potency

Substantial evidence supports the ability of HA to selectively target tumor cells via CD44 receptor-mediated endocytosis [26, 27]. To evaluate whether HA enhances the cellular uptake of our COD2@HMZCS-HA3 in a CD44-dependent manner, the internalization of rhodamine 6G (R6G)-labeled COD2-HMZCS-HA2 (R6G-COD2-HMZCS-HA2) in CD44-positive 4T1 cells and CD44-negative HUVECs was investigated using laser scanning confocal microscopy (LSCM) and flow cytometry (FCM). LSCM images show pronounced cytoplasmic R6G fluorescence in 4T1 cells after 4.0 h of incubation with R6G-COD2@HMZCS-HA3, while HUVECs display minimal intracellular signals (Fig. 3a, b). Notably, pretreatment with a 200-fold of excess free HA to block CD44 receptors prior to nanomedicine exposure significantly reduces fluorescence intensity in 4T1 cells, demonstrating HA-CD44 interaction-dependent cellular uptake (Fig. 3b). Quantitative FCM analysis further corroborated these findings (Fig. 3c). The mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of 4T1 cells treated with R6G-COD2@HMZCS-HA3 is significantly higher than that of 4T1 cells incubated with the same formulation in the presence of excess HA (***P < 0.001), and also higher than that of HUVECs under both non-blocking and HA-blocked conditions ((***P < 0.001). These results underscore the importance of HA-CD44 interactions in promoting selective cellular internalization.

Figure 3d shows the intracellular copper distribution in 4T1 cells treated with COD2-HMZCS-HA and visualized using the SiR-azide Cu2+ probe. The COD2@HMZCS-HA3 group exhibits strong pink Cu2+ fluorescence localized within tumor cells, demonstrating robust CD44-mediated active targeting. This effect is significantly attenuated when cells were pre-incubated with excess HA, which further confirms the mechanism of HA-CD44 dependent cellular uptake. Fig. S8 reveals the lysosomal escape efficiency of R6G-COD2@HMZCS-HA3 in 4T1 cells using Lyso-Tracker Green probe. Compared to the control group, nanomedicine-treated cells exhibit distinct discrete red fluorescence signals that partially colocalized with green lysosomal regions (Pearson’s coefficient = 0.63), demonstrating effective lysosomal escape of the COD2@HMZCS-HA3.

The therapeutic potential of COD2@HMZCS-HA3 was initially assessed through cytotoxicity studies. MTT assays demonstrate favorable biosafety in normal HUVECs (viability > 80% at 20.6 µg/mL of Cu) (Fig. 3e). In contrast, COD2@HMZCS-HA3 exhibits potent concentration-dependent antitumor activity against 4T1 cells, reducing the cell viability to 18.9 ± 3.7% at 20.6 µg/mL of Cu (****P < 0.0001 vs. HUVECs). Quantitative analysis of 4T1 cell cytotoxicity across different treatment durations (6.0–24 h) is shown in Fig. S9. NaHS or COD alone show minimal cytotoxicity, HMZCS-HA3 induces notable tumor cell death, and COD2@HMZCS-HA3 shows the most pronounced tumoricidal activity.

Live/dead staining (calcein-AM/PI) reveal substantial cell death (red fluorescence) in 4T1 cells treated with HMZCS-HA3 for 24 h, and enhanced cytotoxicity upon COD loading (COD2@HMZCS-HA3) (Fig. 3f; S10). In contrast, NaHS or COD alone results in minimal red fluorescence, which is consistent with their limited cytotoxicity. Furthermore, quantitative FCM analysis of cell apoptosis (Fig. 3g, h) show that the apoptosis rate in the COD2@HMZCS-HA3 group reaches 65.8%, which is 1.4-fold higher than that in the HMZCS-HA3 group (46.5%, ***P < 0.001) and 16.0-fold higher than that in the PBS group (4.1%, ***P < 0.0001). These results further corroborates the potent antitumor activity of our COD2@HMZCS-HA3.

Figure 3i presents the results of the EdU staining assay, which assessed the proliferative activity of 4T1 cells across treatment groups. As expected, control groups (PBS, NaHS, or COD) exhibit strong EdU-positive nuclear staining, confirming high proliferative activity. In contrast, HMZCS-HA3 or COD2@HMZCS-HA3-treated cells respectivley show reduced or the weakest EdU fluorescence, indicating significant proliferation suppression. These findings align with complementary data from MTT assays, live/dead imaging, and apoptosis profiling by FCM, collectively demonstrating the potent antitumor capability of COD2@HMZCS-HA3.

To investigate whether COD2@HMZCS-HA3 can trigger intracellular acidification, the pH changes were monitored using the fluorescent probe BCECF-AM. LSCM images reveal a pronounced decrease of green fluorescence in 4T1 cells treated with NaHS, HMZCS-HA3, or COD2@HMZCS-HA3 (Fig. 4b), demonstrating H2S-mediated intracellular acidification. Flow cytometric analysis further confirm these results, showing significantly reduced BCECF fluorescence intensity in cells exposed to H2S-generating formulations (Fig. 4c, d). These findings indicate that COD2@HMZCS-HA3 can effectively enhance intracellular acidification, thereby promoting nanomedicine dissociation.

LSCM images (Fig. 4e) reveal intracellular O2 levels in 4T1 cells measured by the oxygen-sensitive probe Ru(dpp)3Cl2, whose fluorescence is inversely correlated with O2 concentration. Compared with PBS and COD groups, H2S-generating treatments (NaHS, HMZCS-HA3, and COD2@HMZCS-HA3) show significantly attenuated red fluorescence, demonstrating substantial O2 accumulation. This effect stems from H2S-mediated inhibition of mitochondrial respiration, which decreases cellular oxygen consumption [28, 29].

To validate H2S-mediated mitochondrial dysfunction, the expression of cytochrome c oxidase subunit IV (COX IV), a critical electron transport chain component [30–32], was assessed. Immunofluorescence staining results show prominent COX IV downregulation in 4T1 cells after treatment with H2S-releasing formulations (Fig. S12), confirming that COD2@HMZCS-HA3-derived H2S effectively inhibits COX IV activity and disrupts mitochondrial respiration, thereby preserving residual oxygen.

COD2@HMZCS-HA3 treatment reduces basal oxygen consumption rate (OCR) by 32.6% and maximal OCR by 48.9% in 4T1 cells (Fig. S13a). Consistently, the dissolved oxygen levels in the culture medium are significantly elevated after treatment with COD2@HMZCS-HA3 compared to the control (Fig. S13b), which demonstrates that COD2@HMZCS-HA3-derived H2S can suppress mitochondrial respiration and consequently decrease cellular oxygen consumption.

Figure 4f, g presents quantitative FCM analysis of ROS levels in 4T1 cells after treatment with formulations I-V, and staining with the 2’,7’-dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) probe. Notably, COD2@HMZCS-HA3 induces a significantly higher fluorescence intensity compared to HMZCS-HA3 and other control groups (***P < 0.001), demonstrating its enhanced capacity to induce ROS production in tumor cell. The concrete mechanism underlying COD2@HMZCS-HA3-mediated intracellular ROS production was subsequently elucidated.

The positive correlation between COD concentration and H2O2 production in 4T1 cells (Fig. 4h) is attributed to the catalytic ability of COD to oxidize intracellular cholesterol into H2O2. Both COD and COD2@HMZCS-HA3 treatments significantly elevate H2O2 levels in 4T1 cells (Fig. 4i), further confirming the cholesterol metabolism-driven H2O2 self-supply mechanism. Notably, dihydroethidium (DHE) staining reveal substantially enhanced •O2− generation in HMZCS-HA3- and COD2@HMZCS-HA3-treated cells, manifested by intense red fluorescence (Fig. 4j). The elevated level of •O2⁻ can be ascribed to the release of Zn2+ from HMZCS because overloaded Zn2+ can directly promote •O2− generation by disrupting mitochondrial respiratory chain complexes and inducing electron leakage [33]. •O2⁻ levels in 4T1 cells augments significantly with rising Zn2+ concentration (Fig. S14), which corroborates the previous reports [34, 35]. The mitochondrial •O2− induced by Zn2+ can react with Cu2+ to regenerate Cu+ and simultaneously produce O2, establishing a self-fueling catalytic cycle that progressively amplifies oxidative stress.

LSCM images (Fig. 4k) of DCFH-DA-stained 4T1 cells reveal marked green fluorescence signal in the COD and HMZCS-HA3 groups, indicating strong ROS generation. Mechanistically, the COD group exhibits ROS production through cholesterol oxidation-coupled H2O2 generation, whereas the HMZCS-HA3 group induces ROS via a Cu+/Zn2+-mediated Fenton-like reaction. Notably, COD2@HMZCS-HA3 treatment tiggers the most intense ROS signal, confirming that COD-mediated H2O2 self-supply synergistically amplifies the self-fueling inner cycle catalysis.

The intrinsic O2-dependent catalytic activity of COD drives both cholesterol depletion for endogenous H2O2 generation and local O2 consumption [36–38], leading to impaired catalytic performance in hypoxic microenvironments (particularly within CSC-enriched tumor niches) and further exacerbation of local hypoxia. Intracellular H2S generation is diminished in 4T1 cells treated with COD2@HMZCS-HA3 combined with the H2S scavenger hypotaurine (HTAU). Such suppression attenuates hypoxia relief, as verified by increased fluorescence intensity of the oxygen-sensitive probe RuDPP (Fig. S15a, b). Treatment with specific metal ion chelators (i.e., the copper chelator ammonium tetrathiomolybdate [TTM] and the zinc chelator tetrakis-(2-pyridylmethyl)ethylenediamine [TPEN]) markedly reduces intracellular oxygen levels compared to the COD2@HMZCS-HA3 group. The reduction in oxygenation may arise from Zn2+ depletion-mediated inhibition of •O2− production (Fig. S15c), coupled with concurrent copper deprivation that disrupts the self-fueling catalytic cycle for O2 generation. Mechanistically, our COD@HMZCS-HA can effectively overcome this limitation via synergistic oxygen-conserving and oxygen-generating strategies. The released H2S specifically targets and inhibits the mitochondrial respiratory chain (COX IV) to markedly reduce endogenous oxygen consumption in tumor cells [39], thereby preserving residual oxygen in the hypoxic TME to sustain COD-mediated catalytic reactions. Importantly, the Cu2+ generated through Cu+-mediated Fenton-like reactions reacts with Zn2+-triggered mitochondrial •O2− to regenerate Cu+ while producing O2. The established self-fueling catalytic cycle sustains local O2 availability to reactivate COD-mediated cholesterol catabolism. Together, these two complementary mechanisms maintain sufficient local oxygen supply, ensuring that released COD retains robust catalytic efficiency even within the hypoxic, CSC-enriched tumor niches.

Subsequently, the ability of endogenous cholesterol catabolism was evaluated by Filipin III staining assays. Both FCM (Fig. 4l, m) and LSCM (Fig. 4n) analyses reveal substantial intracellular cholesterol reduction in COD- and COD2@HMZCS-HA3-treated 4T1 cells, with negligible changes observed in other groups.

Given the potent cholesterol-depleting capacity of COD2@HMZCS-HA3, the effect on lipid raft integrity was investigated through cholera toxin B subunit-FITC (CTB-FITC) staining because cholesterol is recognized as an essential structural component of lipid rafts [40]. Figure 4o demonstrates that both COD and COD2@HMZCS-HA3 treatments induce prominent disruption of membrane lipid rafts in 4T1 cells, evidenced by a pronounced decrease in green fluorescence intensity. This observation is further quantitatively validated by FCM analysis (Fig. 4p), which confirms that the inhibition of lipid rafts by COD2@HMZCS-HA3 is directly correlated with its cholesterol depletion. Furthermore, as anticipated from lipid raft destabilization, COD2@HMZCS-HA3-mediated cholesterol depletion results in a statistically evident increase in membrane fluidity (Fig. S15d).

Membrane destabilization-driven LPO storm and tumor ferroptosis enhancement

Intracellular LPO accumulation induced by COD@HMZCS-HA3 was monitored using C11-BODIPY581/594, which shifts its fluorescence emission from red to green upon oxidation [41]. LSCM images (Fig. 5a) and FCM analysis (Fig. 5b, c) show markedly elevated green fluorescence intensity in HMZCS-HA3- and COD2@HMZCS-HA3-treated 4T1 cells, indicating robust LPO. Notably, HMZCS-HA3 induces greater LPO accumulation than PBS, NaHS, or free COD, attributing to its Cu+/Zn2+-mediated catalytic activity. Strikingly, COD2@HMZCS-HA3 further amplifies LPO accumulation by 2.4-fold compared to HMZCS-HA3 alone (***P < 0.001), demonstrating synergistic enhancement of ferroptotic oxidative damage through cholesterol oxidation-coupled Fenton-like reactions.

The potent cholesterol-depleting capacity of COD released from COD2@HMZCS-HA3 was hypothesized to activate compensatory metabolic mechanisms that accelerate 7-DHC conversion to cholesterol, thereby reducing cellular 7-DHC pools. This hypothesis is substantiated by the notable downregulation of 7-DHC levels in COD- and COD2@HMZCS-HA3-treated 4T1 cells versus other groups (***P < 0.001, Fig. 5d), demonstrating that cholesterol-depleting treatments can downregulate 7-DHC levels (i.e., inactivation of cholesterol-derived “molecular brake”).

The protective role of 7-DHC against tumor ferroptosis was further assessed by pharmacologically elevating its levels using the FDA-approved drug cariprazine [42], followed by evaluation of LPO in 4T1 cells. Notably, co-treatment with cariprazine markedly reduces the LPO-specific fluorescence signal in COD2@HMZCS-HA3-treated cells (Fig. 5a-c). This inhibitory effect on LPO was further confirmed by quantifying malondialdehyde (MDA), a classic LPO biomarker. Minimal MDA production is observed in the PBS and NaHS groups, while COD induces a moderate increase in MDA levels due to O2-limited catalytic activity. The HMZCS-HA3 group also triggeres a modest elevation in MDA levels in 4T1 cells. In contrast, COD2@HMZCS-HA3 treatment induces the most robust MDA accumulation (Fig. S16d).

Benefiting from synergistic oxygen conservation and generation, COD2@HMZCS-HA3 treatment maintains local oxygen availability to restore the catalytic activity of COD and induce cholesterol depletion in hypoxic 4T1 cells (Fig. S16a). Conversely, COD-mediated cholesterol consumption is significantly attenuated in the presence of specific inhibitors (HTAU, TTM, or TPEN), and the inhibitory effect is further exacerbated by their combinations (HTAU + TTM, HTAU + TPEN, or HTAU + TTM + TPEN). These results confirm that the H2S/self-fueled catalytic cycle-driven O2-sustaining network acts as an essential upstream prerequisite for COD activation and subsequent cholesterol depletion.

Cholesterol catabolism-derived H2O2 facilitates Cu+-mediated Fenton-like reactions to generate abundant •OH. As shown in Fig. S16b, the HPF fluorescence signal of •OH is drastically quenched in cells treated with COD2@HMZCS-HA3 plus TTM, HTAU + TTM, HTAU+TPEN, or HTAU + TTM+TPEN, verifying that Cu bioavailability, COD-mediated H2O2 production and Zn2+-triggered •O2− generation serve as critical fuels to sustain the Cu2+/Cu+ redox cycle and drive continuous •OH production. Impaired •OH generation markedly reduces intracellular LPO accumulation in the abovementioned groups (Fig. S16c). Similarly, cariprazine (7-DHC inducer) pretreatment substantially decreases LPO levels induced by COD2@HMZCS-HA3, which is ascribed to the LPO-scavenging effect of upregulated 7-DHC. Additionally, pretreatment of 4T1 cells with cariprazine, TTM, TPEN, or HTAU attenuates COD2@HMZCS-HA3-induced oxidative damage by 74.9%, 65.0%, 54.1%, and 39.2%, respectively (Fig. S16d), further delineating the individual contribution of each key component to amplified LPO accumulation.

In summary, the robust LPO storm triggered by COD2@HMZCS-HA3 is driven by a highly synergistic mechanistic cascade. H2S-mediated preservation of local oxygen availability restores the catalytic activity of COD to initiate cholesterol depletion, H2O2 production and 7-DHC downregulation, which activates Cu+/Zn2+ dual-catalytic ROS amplification and ultimately augments LPO accumulation to elicit potent tumor ferroptosis. Meanwhile, the self-fueling catalytic cycle with O2 generation further amplifies the above cascade. In this process, COD-mediated cholesterol depletion serves as a critical ferroptosis-sensitizing event by sustaining endogenous H2O2 supply, disrupting lipid raft membrane integrity, and abrogating the 7-DHC-mediated intrinsic “molecular brake” on LPO to compromise cellular antioxidant defenses. Furthermore, the self-fueling bimetallic catalytic network, including Cu+-catalyzed •OH generation, Zn2+-induced •O2− accumulation and H2S-mediated microenvironment modulation (hypoxia relief and intracellular acidification), is the primary driver of ferroptosis. The aforementioned synergistic cascade drives the robust antitumor efficacy, culminating in amplified and extensive tumor ferroptosis (Fig. 5e). The potent ferroptosis induction is further confirmed by powerful GSH consumption and GPX4 inactivation in COD2@HMZCS-HA3-treated 4T1 cells, as demonstrated by LSCM imaging (Fig. 5f, g; Fig. S17-18) and quantitatively validated using corresponding assay kits (Fig. 5h, i).

LSCM imaging of JC-1-stained 4T1 cells demonstrates a pronounced loss of mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) in group V, evidenced by intensified green fluorescence (Fig. 5j). MitoTracker Red staining further confirms a significant reduction in mitochondrial mass after treatment with COD2@HMZCS-HA3 (Fig. S19). FCM analysis quantitatively reveals near-complete MMP collapse, with JC-1 monomer levels increasing from 0% (PBS control) to 97.1% (COD2@HMZCS-HA3 group) (Fig. 5k). Bio-TEM imaging (Fig. 5l) of 4T1 cells treated with nanomedicine reveals hallmark ferroptotic mitochondrial damage [43], including membrane fragmentation and cristae disintegration. Consistently, intracellular ATP levels are significantly depleted (Fig. S20), which collectively demonstrates COD2@HMZCS-HA3-induced mitochondrial dysfunction via amplified ferroptosis.

LSCM images of DIO-stained 4T1 cells (Fig. 5m) show preserved membrane integrity for PBS- or NaHS-treated cells, partial membrane disruption for COD or HMZCS-HA3-treated cells, and severe membrane damage after COD2@HMZCS-HA3 exposure, which indicates a synergistic effect on plasma membrane destabilization. COD2@HMZCS-HA3 treatment significantly increases LDH release from 4T1 cells, whereas NaHS or COD alone causes minimal LDH release (Fig. S21). This enhanced LDH efflux is consistent with loss of plasma membrane integrity during ferroptosis.

γ-H2AX immunofluorescence staining reveals treatment-triggered DNA damage in 4T1 cells (Fig. S22). COD2@HMZCS-HA3 induces the most intense nuclear γ-H2AX foci, demonstrating significantly greater DNA damage than other formulations. This •OH-mediated toxicity correlates with the observed ferroptotic effects, which suggests that ROS-induced nuclear damage contributes to the anticancer mechanism. Fig. S23 shows the cytotoxic effect of COD2@HMZCS-HA3 in the presence of specific inhibitors including GSH (antioxidant), TTM (copper chelator), or cariprazine (7-DHC inducer) against tumor cells. Notably, 4T1 cell viability is significantly restored upon pretreatment with above inhibitors (***P < 0.001 or ****P < 0.0001), providing further evidence that the antitumor activity of COD2@HMZCS-HA3 is mediated through cholesterol depletion-induced ferroptosis propagation.

It is noted that while the nanomedicine induces multifaceted cellular stress (e.g., mitochondrial dysfunction and DNA damage), integrated findings confirm that ferroptosis is the primary cell death mechanism in the COD2@HMZCS-HA3 system, driven by the self-fueling catalysis-driven membrane destabilization. Specifically, COD-mediated depletion of intracellular cholesterol pools disrupts the biophysical membrane barrier via lipid raft destabilization and increased membrane fluidity, and inactivates the “molecular brake” on LPO by reducing 7-DHC levels. Consequently, the robust •OH from Cu+/Zn2+-mediated Fenton-like reactions is thus selectively channeled into vulnerable lipid bilayers, triggering an irreversible LPO storm. Key ferroptosis biomarker analyses in our study, including massive accumulation of LPO and MDA, noticeable downregulation of the classic ferroptosis defender GPX4, and unique upstream 7-DHC consumption and lipid raft disruption, collectively and conclusively validate the ferroptotic cascade.

Additionally, the ferroptotic nature of COD2@HMZCS-HA3-induced 4T1 cell death was also confirmed using specific inhibitors (Fig. S24), as evidenced by the strong protective effect of Ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1, ferroptosis inhibitor), in contrast to the limited effects of Z-VAD-FMK (Z-VAD, apoptosis inhibitor) and Necrostatin-1 (Nec-1, necroptosis inhibitor). Unlike apoptosis relying on energy-dependent caspase cascades and apoptotic body formation, or necrosis lacking specific metabolic regulation [44, 45], 4T1 cell death induced by our nanomedicine is a metabolism-driven, metal-catalyzed ferroptotic process. Rapid and irreversible membrane rupture driven by synergistic lipid raft destabilization and robust LPO accumulation circumvents slower apoptotic signaling cascades, and is expected to culminate in potent ferroptotic cell death that effectively eradicates apoptosis-resistant cancer cells.

Suppression of migration, invasion and tumor stemness

Targeting ferroptosis in CSC holds great promise for overcoming multidrug resistance in refractory tumors [46]. Unlike normal cancer cells, CSC show hyperactive cholesterol metabolism to drive malignant proliferation, which promotes lipid raft formation and stabilizes a ferroptosis-resistant phenotype [47]. Our system, distinct from previously reported COD-based nanomedicines [37, 48], leverages self-fueling catalysis-driven membrane destabilization to target the intrinsic metabolic vulnerability of CSC, providing a potent and precise strategy for CSC-enriched tumors.

Wound healing assays demonstrate that COD2@HMZCS-HA3 potently inhibits 4T1 cell migration, reducing wound closure to just 8.9% compared to partial closure with COD or HMZCS-HA3 alone (Fig. 6c, d). Furthermore, transwell invasion assays (Fig. 6e, f) show remarkably suppressed invasion of 4T1 cells after COD treatment, likely resulting from cholesterol catabolism-induced disruption of invasive lamellipodia. These findings are consistent with the wound healing results, supporting the antimetastatic potential of COD2@HMZCS-HA3 via effective suppression of both migration and invasiveness.

The hypoxic TME maintains CSC stemness through HIF-1α-mediated signaling and enhanced antioxidant defenses, which jointly preserve redox homeostasis [49–52]. Thus, the simultaneous disruption of hypoxia-driven pathways and induction of ROS accumulation represents a promising strategy to eradicate therapy-resistant CSC (Fig. 6g).

Figure 6h shows the robust penetration of R6G-labeled COD2@HMZCS-HA3 into 4T1 CSC spheroids, with strong fluorescence observed throughout the 3D structure. Compared to the PBS group, COD2@HMZCS-HA3 treatment results in a 90.5% decrease in the number of tumorspheres and an 88.0% reduction in size at 7.0 days, confirming its potent anti-stemness activity.

Notably, COD2@HMZCS-HA3 group induces more intense ROS fluorescence throughout the spheroid depth, which is attributed to cholesterol catabolism-mediated amplification of catalytic activity.

Therapeutic efficacy against 4T1 subcutaneous xenografts

Given the favorable in vitro results, the in vivo efficacy of COD2@HMZCS-HA3 was further evaluated in 4T1 tumor-bearing mice (Fig. 7a). Pharmacokinetic analysis of Cu levels in 4T1 tumor-bearing mice after intravenous (i.v.) injection of COD2@HMZCS-HA3 (Fig. S26) reveals a blood circulation half-life (t1/2) of 6.2 h. Fluorescence imaging (Fig. 7b) and corresponding quantitative data (Fig. 7c) demonstrate that Cy7-labeled COD2@HMZCS-HA3 achieves maximal tumor accumulation at 12 h post-injection with sustained retention even at 24 h, which is attibuted to its prolonged blood circulation. Furthermore, ICP-OES analysis of Cu (Fig. 7d) and Zn levels (Fig. S27) in harvested organs and tumor tissues reveals high tumor accumulation of COD2@HMZCS-HA3, reaching 13.0% I.D. g− 1 of Cu and 9.5% I.D. g− 1 of Zn at 12 h post-injection. These findings demonstrate the robust tumor-targeting capability of COD2@HMZCS-HA3, which can be ascribed to the combined effects of the EPR and HA-mediated active targeting to CD44-overexpressing tumor cells.

As shown in Fig. 7e, f, tumors in the PBS and NaHS groups grow rapidly, whereas COD treatment only moderately delays tumor progression. In contrast, marked tumor suppression is observed in group IV and V. Notably, the COD2@HMZCS-HA3 group exhibits the most potent antitumor effect with a tumor inhibition rate of 94.3%, significantly higher than that achieved by HMZCS-HA3 (61.5%, ***P < 0.001; Fig. 7g). Tumoral cholesterol content is significantly reduced in the COD2@HMZCS-HA3 group (Fig. 7i), resulting from COD-mediated catalytic consumption. In comparison, free COD treatment shows a weaker cholesterol-lowering effect due to its poor tumor accumulation. Furthermore, the hypoxia alleviation demonstrated by COD2@HMZCS-HA3 in vitro would enhance catalytic activity of COD, thereby promoting cholesterol consumption. Representative H&E (Fig. 7j) and TUNEL images (Fig. 7k) demonstrate that COD2@HMZCS-HA3 elicits the most extensive tumor damage, characterized by nuclear condensation, loss of membrane integrity, and the highest density of TUNEL-positive cells, which indicates robust apoptosis and structural disintegration.

The robust antitumor activity of COD2@HMZCS-HA3 is associated with pronounced LPO, as demonstrated by intense LPO staining and elevated MDA levels (Fig. 7l; Fig. S29). These findings support the key ferroptosis therapeutic mechanism, i.e., cholesterol catabolism sensitizes tumor membranes to oxidative damage. Furthermore, immunofluorescence staining of the stemness-related marker CD133 shows markedly reduced expression in both the HMZCS-HA3 and COD2@HMZCS-HA3 groups (Fig. 7m). Notably, COD2@HMZCS-HA3 induces the most potent CSC killing effect, indicating its potential to effectively suppress tumor recurrence and metastasis.

During the therapeutic period, all treatment groups exhibit a slight increase in mouse body weight (Fig. S30), suggesting the absence of significant systemic toxicity. Encouragingly, COD2@HMZCS-HA3 exhibits excellent blood compatibility, with hemolysis rates remaining below 2.0% at high concentrations up to 400 µg/mL (Fig. S31). Hematological parameters (Fig. S32) and serum biochemistry (Fig. S33) remain within normal ranges after i.v. administration of COD2@HMZCS-HA3 (Cu dosage of 5.0 mg/kg). Histopathological assessment of major organs reveals no significant tissue damage or abnormalities in either PBS- or COD2@HMZCS-HA3-treated groups (Fig. S34). These results collectively demonstrate the excellent biosafety profile of COD2@HMZCS-HA3 in vivo.

Consistent with prior observations, COD2@HMZCS-HA3 treatment induces extensive tumor damage, characterized by severe plasma membrane rupture and the highest levels of cell necrosis and apoptosis among all groups. Furthermore, LPO staining reveals the most intense LPO signals in the COD2@HMZCS-HA3 group, indicative of elevated ferroptotic activity due to robust cholesterol catabolism-driven LPO and enhanced ferroptosis propagation.

To directly assess intratumoral hypoxic stress, pimonidazole (a hypoxia-specific probe) immunofluorescence staining was performed on tumor tissues from 4T1 tumor-bearing mice after the indicated treatments (groups I-V). As expected, severe hypoxic stress is observed in the PBS group. Notably, COD treatment further exacerbates intratumoral hypoxia due to its inherent oxygen-consuming catalytic nature. In contrast, both HMZCS-HA3 and COD2@HMZCS-HA3 treatments substantially signaling within the tumor (Fig. S35). Besides, ELISA-based quantification of HIF1-α expression levels shows a notable reduction in the COD2@HMZCS-HA3-treated group (Fig. 8f). These results are consistent with the RuDPP staining data (Fig. 4e) and dissolved O2 measurements in the culture medium of hypoxic 4T1 cells (Fig. S13b). Together, these data suggest that the synergistic effects of H2S-mediated oxygen conservation and continuous in situ O2 generation driven by the self-fueling catalytic cycle potentially attenuate intratumoral hypoxia, thereby enhancing the catalytic efficacy of COD and suppressing tumor stemness. Considering the inherent heterogeneity of solid malignancies, advanced future investigations remain essential to precisely map absolute intratumoral oxygen distributions.

Given the vital role of CSC in tumor metastasis, the antimetastatic potential of COD2@HMZCS-HA3 was further evaluated. As shown in lung photographs (Fig. 8g) and H&E-stained sections (Fig. 8h), mice in the PBS and NaHS groups exhibit numerous pulmonary metastatic nodules, whereas the COD group shows only moderate suppression of nodule formation. In contrast, HMZCS-HA3 treatment significantly reduces metastatic burden, and COD2@HMZCS-HA3 almost completely suppresses the formation of metastatic foci. Quantitative analysis reveals that COD2@HMZCS-HA3 treatment reduces the number (Fig. 8i) and area (Fig. 8j) of lung metastatic foci by more than 90% and 75%, respectively. These results strongly support the potent anti-metastatic efficacy of COD2@HMZCS-HA3, mediated through a self-fueling catalysis-driven membrane destabilization strategy that simultaneously relieves hypoxia to sensitize CSC and disrupts cholesterol-dependent membrane defenses against ferroptosis for CSC elimination.

Although the above findings demonstrate promising antitumor effects and minimal in vivo toxicity for COD2@HMZCS-HA3 in both subcutaneous and CSC-enriched metastatic 4T1 models, several challenges still need to be addressed before its further application. First, the current safety profile relies on a short-term observation window of 36 days. Given the sustained release of Cu+ and Zn2+ heavy metal ions and H2S, the long-term tissue accumulation, potential chronic toxicity, and liver/renal clearance pathways require comprehensive evaluation in non-rodent animal models in future work. Second, a major translational concern pertains to the utilization of non-human enzymes. While encapsulation within the HA-stabilized HMZCS shell and its surface HA layer partially shields COD from premature immune recognition, repeated administration may still elicit anti-drug antibodies and accelerate systemic clearance. To minimize immunogenicity for long-term treatment, future system designs may incorporate surface engineering strategies including dense PEGylation and biomimetic cell-membrane coating, or utilize recombinant humanized enzymes.

Conclusions

In summary, we propose a self-fueling catalysis-driven membrane destabilization strategy that simultaneously overcomes the hypoxic TME barrier to sensitize CSC and disrupts cholesterol-dependent membrane defenses against ferroptosis, thereby achieving potent therapeutic efficacy for CSC-enriched tumors. To realize this strategy, hollow mesoporous zinc-copper sulfide (HMZCS-HA) nanocapsules, featuring unique hollow cores (~ 67 nm) and optimal pore size (~ 8 nm), were synthesized via a Kirkendall effect-driven one-pot approach utilizing HA as a stabilizer. Subsequently, COD was encapsulated into the core space to form COD@HMZCS-HA nanomedicines. The HMZCS-HA shell can prevent COD from premature deactivation and minimize off-target toxicity from unwanted leakage. The optimized COD2@HMZCS-HA3 can efficiently accumulate in tumors through the EPR effect and HA-dependent binding to CD44 receptors overexpressed on the surface of tumor cells. Upon exposure to the weakly acidic environment, the COD2@HMZCS-HA3 releases COD, H2S, Cu+ and Zn2+, activating the “OFF-to-ON” antitumor activity.

Both in vitro and in vivo studies have confirmed the pronounced antitumor and antimetastatic efficacy of COD2@HMZCS-HA3, demonstrating its ability to augment ferroptosis and eradicate CSC through the following cascade mechanisms: (1) COD released from COD2@HMZCS-HA3 exhausts cholesterol pools in tumor cells (including CSC) and thus generates H2O2, which reacts with Cu+ via Fenton-like reactions to produce •OH and Cu2+; Cu2+ reacts with mitochondrial-derived •O2− induced by overload of Zn2+, regenerating Cu+ and O2 to form a self-fueling inner cycle catalysis that continuously enhances oxidative stress. (2) H2S acidifies the intracellular milieu, accelerating nanomedicine degradation and Cu+-mediated •OH production while blocking mitochondrial respiration to further sustain local oxygenation, thereby potentially attenuating tumor hypoxia. (3) Cholesterol catabolism by COD disrupts the biophysical membrane barrier via lipid raft destruction and enhanced membrane fluidity, and inactivates the “molecular brake” through 7-DHC reduction, which promotes the amplification and propagation of ferroptosis. (4) The self-fueling catalysis-driven membrane destabilization continuously eradicates CSC within the TME by simultaneously sensitizing CSC via sustainment of intratumoral oxygen availability and disrupting cholesterol-dependent membrane defenses against ferroptosis. (5) The ablation of CSC, together with extensive disruption of invasive lipid rafts, synergistically suppresses tumor invasion and metastasis. These findings reveal a promising strategy for the treatment of CSC-enriched metastatic malignancies with effective suppression of therapeutic resistance, metastasis and cholesterol-mediated ferroptosis escape of CSC.
