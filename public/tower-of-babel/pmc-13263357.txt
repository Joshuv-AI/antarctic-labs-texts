NR5A2 controls gene expression and chromatin contacts of essential circadian metabolic genes in the liver
Authors: Abraham Román-Figueroa, Juan Carlos Gómora-García, Luis Tenorio-Hernández, Rosario Pérez-Molina, Karina Jácome-López, Mayra Furlan-Magaril
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Circadian regulation of gene expression relies on the coordinated action of core clock components, tissue-specific transcription factors, and epigenetic mechanisms, including dynamic enhancer–promoter interactions. Although rhythmic chromatin looping has been described in the liver, the transcription factors that mediate these temporal interactions remain poorly defined. Here, we identify the nuclear receptor NR5A2 as a key regulator of circadian enhancer–promoter communication in the mouse liver. We show that NR5A2 protein levels fluctuate over the 24-h cycle, peaking around Zeitgeber time 0 (ZT0). Genome-wide analysis reveals that NR5A2 preferentially binds H3K27ac-enriched enhancer regions, many of which engage in chromatin looping with promoters of metabolic genes. High-resolution 4C-seq analyses demonstrate that promoters of Elovl5 and Ppp1r3c dynamically interact with NR5A2- and H3K27ac-enriched enhancers in a time-dependent manner, with maximal interactions at ZT0 and reduced contacts at ZT12. Pharmacological inhibition of NR5A2 in vivo leads to alterations in hepatic glycogen and cholesterol accumulation, attenuates the rhythmic expression of circadian metabolic genes, including Ppp1r3c, Cyp8b1, and Elovl5, and disrupts enhancer–promoter contact frequencies at the Elovl5 locus. Together, our findings provide in vivo evidence that NR5A2 integrates circadian and metabolic regulation by modulating enhancer–promoter chromatin interactions, thereby shaping rhythmic liver gene expression.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00018-026-06250-w.

Introduction

The circadian clock coordinates the temporal activity of physiological and behavioral processes. At the molecular level, a group of core-clock genes, working in coordination with tissue-specific transcription factors (TFs), control the rhythmic transcription of hundreds of tissue-specific genes [1, 2]. Beyond transcriptional networks, diurnal temporal regulation is further shaped by epigenetic mechanisms, including the rhythmic recruitment of distal enhancer elements [1, 3–9].

Enhancers are cis-regulatory elements that orchestrate spatio-temporal gene expression programs [10]. Enhancers activate distal genes by physically contacting their target promoters through chromatin looping, a process orchestrated by cell–type–specific transcription factors [10–12]. These interactions typically occur within larger structural units known as Topologically Associating Domains (TADs). TADs are domains with high interaction probabilities whose boundaries are enriched for architectural proteins such as CTCF and the Cohesin complex, consistent with the loop extrusion model [10–12]. By facilitating enhancer–promoter communication while restricting aberrant interactions across adjacent domains, TADs provide a structural framework for understanding enhancer specificity toward their target genes [10–13].

In the mouse liver, thousands of enhancers exhibiting circadian rhythmicity have been identified, highlighting the important function of these elements in the temporal regulation of circadian gene expression [3, 4]. Moreover, time-resolved chromosome conformation capture experiments have shown that circadian gene promoters dynamically engage in interactions with enhancers over the 24 h [5–9]. However, the mechanisms and the TFs mediating these diurnal fluctuations in enhancer–promoter interactions remain largely elusive. In this context, our previous work identified a strong enrichment of the NR5A2 DNA binding motif at both stable and dynamic interacting partners of circadian gene promoters [9].

NR5A2 is a nuclear receptor expressed in endoderm-derived tissues, including the intestine, pancreas, and liver. It is critical in various biological processes, such as development, reproduction, immunity, and metabolism [14]. In the mouse liver, NR5A2 coordinates gene programs controlling bile acid synthesis, reverse cholesterol transport, and glucose homeostasis [15]. Genetic ablation of NR5A2 results in liver steatosis [16], while increasing its activity using agonists such as DLPC improves insulin resistance and reduces liver steatosis in obese mice [17]. Despite its potential as a druggable target for metabolic diseases, the precise mechanisms by which NR5A2 regulates gene expression remain unclear.

Here, we report that the NR5A2 protein abundance exhibits fluctuations in the liver during the 24 h, with a maximum accumulation around ZT0. Analysis of ChIP-seq data against NR5A2 reveals that it preferentially binds to genomic regions enriched for H3K27ac corresponding to putative enhancers genome-wide. Analysis of promoter capture data from mouse liver shows that these regulatory elements establish chromatin loops connecting them to thousands of key metabolic gene promoters. By performing 4C-seq experiments, we further detailed and increased the resolution of the chromatin contacts formed by three important metabolic gene promoters, including Elovl5, Ppp1r3c, and Gsk3a at ZT0 and 12. We found that Elovl5 and Ppp1r3c gene promoters establish chromatin contacts with enhancers enriched in NR5A2 and H3K27ac at their transcriptional peak (ZT0), and these are significantly decreased at ZT12, correlating with the NR5A2 protein nadir in the liver. In vivo inhibition of NR5A2 dampens the circadian gene expression of metabolic genes, including Elovl5, Ppp1r3c, and Cyp8b1, and results in alterations in liver glycogen and cholesterol accumulation during the day. Notably, NR5A2 inhibition reduces enhancer–promoter contact frequencies at the Elovl5 locus. Together, these findings provide the first in vivo evidence that NR5A2 shapes rhythmic chromatin interactions and liver metabolic gene expression by regulating enhancer–promoter communication.

Results

The nuclear receptor NR5A2 protein abundance fluctuates throughout the day and occupies enhancer elements

Previous work showed that circadian gene promoters in mouse liver establish both stable and rhythmic chromatin contacts with regulatory elements, with a notable enrichment of the NR5A2 DNA binding motif at these interacting sites [9]. To investigate whether NR5A2 itself exhibits diurnal fluctuations at the protein level, we measured its abundance at ZT0, ZT6, ZT12, and ZT18. This four-point design captures expected rhythmic transitions, including the feeding/fasting switch (ZT0), mid-day (ZT6), onset of the fasting/feeding switch (ZT12), and mid-night (ZT18), enabling robust detection of temporal changes while maintaining feasibility for in vivo sampling. Cosinor analysis [18] of total protein extracts reveals NR5A2 protein levels exhibit a significant fluctuation around the 24 h (p-value: 0.0201) with an acrophase around ZT0, and an amplitude of 0.43 and this is also observed using nuclear protein extracts (Fig. 1A, B). To understand how NR5A2 may influence genome regulation, we analyzed publicly available NR5A2 ChIP-seq data from adult mouse liver [19] alongside time-resolved ChIP-seq datasets for histone marks (H3K9ac, H3K27ac, H3K4me1, H3K4me3). Our analysis reveals NR5A2 peaks were mainly distributed across promoters (27%), intergenic regions (29%), and introns (35%) (Fig. 1C). Genome-wide correlation analyses revealed a strong association between the NR5A2 cistrome and the active enhancer marks H3K27ac and H3K4me1, with a weaker association with the promoter mark H3K4me3 (Fig. 1D). Histone mark enrichment around NR5A2 peaks showed minimal variation across the day at both promoter-proximal and distal intergenic regions (Fig. 1E). We next examined circadian enhancers defined by eRNA production [3] and found that 52.2% overlapped with NR5A2 peaks (Fig. 1F). Notably, these enhancers exhibited maximal transcriptional activity near dawn (Fig. 1G), coinciding with high nuclear NR5A2 abundance. Analysis of core circadian transcription factors chromatin binding, including BMAL1, CLOCK, NPAS2, PER1/2, CRY1/2 [20], RORα/γ [21], and REV-ERBα/β [22] revealed that NR5A2 binding strongly overlap with the nuclear receptors RORα and γ and REV-ERBα/β (Fig. 1H). Given that NR5A2 peaks around ZT0 in nuclei and chromatin, this finding suggests coordinated binding with RORα/γ in chromatin. Co-occupancy of NR5A2 and RORα/γ is illustrated at the metabolic gene loci: Ppp1r3c and Fads2 (Supplementary Fig. 1 C and D).

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that NR5A2 protein abundance fluctuates around the 24 h, peaking around ZT0, and that genome-wide it preferentially binds to active enhancers. The strong spatial and temporal concordance between NR5A2 and RORα/γ suggests coordinated regulation at common cis-regulatory sites.

NR5A2 ocuppies thousands of chromatin loops connecting promoters of key metabolic genes with their regulatory elements

NR5A2 regulates the transcriptional activity of key metabolic genes in the liver [15]. To determine whether this regulatory role involves long-range chromatin interactions, we analyzed promoter-centric interaction profiles of key metabolic genes. We curated a set of 489 promoters from genes regulating glucose, cholesterol, bile acid homeostasis, as well as de novo lipogenesis from the mouse collection of the Molecular Signatures Data Base (gsea-msigdb.org). Using publicly available promoter capture Hi-C data [9], we identified 4,953 interactions involving this set of metabolic gene promoters, and 3,247 of these interactions overlapped with H3K27ac-enriched regulatory elements. Motif analysis of these H3K27ac-interacting regions revealed a striking enrichment of the NR5A2 motif (Fig. 2A, E-value = 4.2 × 10–53). Taken together, these observations suggest that NR5A2 may modulate gene expression through long-range chromatin contacts—an idea we tested next using high-resolution, time-resolved 4C-seq.

Enhancer–promoter looping at circadian metabolic genes fluctuates in synchrony with NR5A2 protein levels

To gain more detailed information on metabolic gene promoter interactions, we performed 4C-seq experiments at ZT0 and ZT12. 4C-seq provides high-resolution, genome-wide quantification of DNA fragments physically interacting with a genomic region called a viewpoint [23]. Viewpoints were positioned at two circadian metabolic gene promoters with ZT0 transcriptional acrophases (Elovl5, Ppp1r3c) and at one with a ZT12 transcriptional acrophase (Gsk3a) as a temporal control. We also included the Bmal1 gene promoter as a core-clock-regulated control, also transcriptionally peaking at ZT0. Elovl5, Ppp1r3c and Gsk3a encode enzymes that play key roles in hepatic lipid and glycogen metabolism and display a well characterized circadian gene expression in the liver (Supplementary Table 3) [3, 9, 24]. Elovl5 encodes an enzyme required for the elongation of very long–chain fatty acids, a critical pathway in lipid biosynthesis [25]. Its mRNA exhibits a strong circadian rhythm peaking around ZT0 (Fig. 3B), and its transcription is regulated by REV-ERBα, SREBPs, MRG15, and NR5A2 [16, 19, 26]. Ppp1r3c encodes a regulatory subunit that activates Glycogen Synthase 2 (GYS2), the rate-limiting enzyme in glycogen synthesis [27]. Its transcript also peaks near ZT0 (Fig. 3G). In contrast, Gsk3a, which encodes a conserved serine/threonine kinase that inhibits GYS2 activity [28], shows the opposite temporal pattern, with mRNA levels maximal between ZT6 and ZT12 (Supplementary Fig. 2B). Finally, Bmal1 encodes a core clock circadian transcription factor that, together with CLOCK and tissue-specific regulators, drives the rhythmic expression of hundreds of hepatic genes [29]. These metrics confirm the high quality of our 4C-seq experiments [19]. 4C-seq analysis from the Elovl5 promoter identified two prominent interactions with intergenic regions located 65 kb upstream and 118 kb downstream. While the downstream interaction was present at both ZT0 and ZT12, the upstream interaction was ZT0-specific (Fig. 3A). To characterize these interacting regions, we integrated datasets, including H3K27ac, H3K4me3 and NR5A2 ChIP-seq [19, 20], GRO-seq [3], ATAC-seq [30], and TAD coordinates [9]. Our analysis reveals that both interactions correspond to intergenic enhancers enriched in H3K27ac that overlap NR5A2 peaks (Fig. 3A). Both enhancers exhibit increased contact frequencies and H3K27ac accumulation at ZT0 (Figs. 3C and D). Examination of 4 C and H3K27ac signals across the Elovl5 TAD showed increased frequency of chromatin interactions and increased accumulation of H3K27ac at ZT0 (Fig. 3E), and 47% of these regions overlap NR5A2 peaks (Supplementary Fig. 2E). We corroborated this behavior when analyzing 4 C and H3K27ac signals around NR5A2 peaks across the Elovl5 TAD (Supplementary Fig. 2I and M) with an increase in both at ZT0 versus ZT12.

We next examined the 4 C profiles of Ppp1r3c at ZT0 and ZT12. Our analysis revealed two downstream interactions located 100 kb and 150 kb from the promoter (Fig. 3F). Although both were detected at both time points, normalized interaction frequencies were consistently higher at ZT0 (Figs. 3H and 3I). The 100 kb interaction overlaps an NR5A2-bound enhancer cluster within the Hectd2os gene, exhibiting dynamic features: increased ZT0 contact frequency, elevated H3K27ac at CT0, elevated bidirectional transcription at ZT22, and higher accessibility at ZT20 (Fig. 3F, Fig. 3H, and Supplementary Fig. 3D). These dynamics parallel the NR5A2 protein rhythm and the highest Ppp1r3c mRNA abundance (Fig. 3G). The 150 kb interaction lacks enhancer-associated marks and NR5A2 ChIP–seq signal but overlaps a stable CTCF binding site (Supplementary Fig. 3 F). Due to the overall drop in contact frequency after this site (Fig. 3F), we hypothesize that this region might represent the TAD boundary that increases the contact probability between the Ppp1r3c gene promoter and its enhancer located ~ 100 kb downstream, through a mechanism that is independent of NR5A2. Across the Ppp1r3c TAD, H3K27ac-marked regions also displayed increased interaction frequency at ZT0 and elevated H3K27ac levels at CT0, with 74% coinciding with NR5A2 peaks (Fig. 3J; Supplementary Fig. 2 F). We corroborated this behaviour around NR5A2-bound regions with both chromatin interactions and H3K27ac signal increasing at ZT0 versus ZT12 (Supplementary Fig. 2 J and N).

Analysis of the 4C-seq Bmal1 experiments revealed a ZT0-specific interaction with an intronic enhancer located 50 kb downstream. This element overlaps several NR5A2 peaks (Fig. 3K). Its chromatin accessibility, H3K27ac levels, and contact frequency all rise near NR5A2’s chromatin peak, and the highest level of Bmal1 expression at ZT0 (Supplementary Fig. 3 A, Fig. 3M). Notably, 72% of H3K27ac sites overlap NR5A2 peaks surrounding Bmal1 (Supplementary Fig. 2H). Also, NR5A2-bound regions across the Bmal1 TAD also show increased contact frequency at ZT0 and elevated H3K27ac deposition at CT0 (Fig. 3N, Supplementary Fig. 2L and P).

Finally, examination of the Gsk3a gene promoter revealed an interaction with a region 78 kb upstream corresponding to the Megf8 promoter, which overlaps with a small NR5A2 peak (compared to the occupancy at Elovl5 enhancers, for example) (Supplementary Fig. 2 A). Gsk3a gene expression peaks around ZT6–ZT12 (Supplementary Fig. 2B) and this coincides with a slight increase in H3K27ac at ZT12 at the Megf8 interacting region. However this region shows stable accessibility, stable H3K4me3 deposition, and stable contact frequency with the Gsk3a gene promoter over the 24 h (Supplementary Figs. 2 C, Supplementary Fig. 3 C). 4C-seq signal and H3K27ac accumulation across the Gsk3a TAD similarly remain constant between ZT0 and 12 (Supplementary Fig. 2D). Of the H3K27ac peaks in this locus, 49% overlap with NR5A2. However most of them also overlap with H3K4me3 at promoters of different genes surrounding Gsk3a and present a slight increase in 4C-seq signal and slight increase in H3K27ac at ZT12 compared to ZT0 (Supplementary Fig. 2G, K and O). Together, these data indicate that enhancer–promoter communication fluctuates during the day in agreement with the transcriptional peak of Elovl5, Ppp1r3c, and Bmal1 genes with achrophase at ZT0, coinciding with NR5A2 protein accumulation at enhancers.

To determine whether NR5A2 drives chromatin looping and circadian gene expression in the mouse liver, we inhibited its activity in vivo using the inverse agonist SR1848, which has been previously used for this purpose with high specificity [31–33]. Given that the NR5A2 protein peaks around ZT0, we administered mice with vehicle (10% DMSO, 10% Tween-80) or 30 mg/kg of SR1848 compound intraperitoneally at ZT20 for five days (Fig. 4A) (see methods). To validate NR5A2 inhibition, we measured chromatin-bound NR5A2 and quantified hepatic glycogen and cholesterol in vehicle- and SR1848-treated mice at ZT0 and ZT12. SR1848 reduced chromatin-associated NR5A2 protein by ~ 60% at ZT0 in contrast to vehicle samples (Fig. 4B). Liver glycogen varied more than twofold between ZT0 and ZT12 with higher levels at ZT0 in the vehicle samples. NR5A2 inhibition lowered glycogen levels at ZT0 and thus blunted its daily rhythm (Fig. 4C). Discussion

The circadian clock controls the temporal activity of physiological processes by modulating different regulatory layers of gene expression. Previous work in the adult mouse liver has revealed circadian fluctuations in enhancer activity and the three-dimensional organization of the genome (Supplementary Table 3) [4, 5, 7–9, 20, 37]. Functional studies unveiled that REV-ERBα modulates dynamic enhancer-promoter interactions by rhythmically repressing chromatin looping [8]. However, the precise mechanisms controlling distal enhancers and their tissue-specific chromatin looping to circadian gene promoters remain poorly understood.

In this work, we uncover the function of NR5A2 in modulating rhythmic expression of key circadian metabolic genes through enhancer occupancy and chromatin interactions. Our findings reveal that NR5A2 protein levels in the liver fluctuate during the 24 h, peaking around ZT0 and decreasing around ZT12. We corroborate this behavior in total, cytoplasm, nuclei, and chromatin extracts. Supporting these findings, a recent work reports a diurnal fluctuation in NR5A2 total protein levels in the mouse liver, peaking at ZT22 and reaching a minimum at ZT10 (Supplementary Table 3) [19]. Additional experiments demonstrated that the temporal feeding pattern controls this behavior [19], suggesting that, in the liver, NR5A2 transduces nutritional signals into rhythmic gene expression profiles.

Analysis of available ChIP-seq data shows that NR5A2 preferentially binds distal intergenic enhancers (enriched for H3K27ac and H3K4me1) in the mouse liver and co-occupies these regions with RORα/γ. Given that, like RORα/γ, NR5A2 peaks between ZT18 and ZT0 in chromatin extracts, we hypothesize that NR5A2 temporally directs the molecular clock to liver-specific regulatory elements. Additional experiments will clarify this spatio-temporal relation.

Promoter-capture Hi-C analysis revealed that NR5A2 occupies thousands of enhancers interacting with metabolic genes controlling glucose, cholesterol, bile acid, as well as de novo lipogenesis. These findings point to a broad regulatory function for NR5A2 in shaping circadian metabolic gene networks in the liver by long-range chromatin contacts. Genome wide chromatin studies will be needed to fully answer this question.

High-resolution 4C-seq experiments further reveal that genes with transcriptional peaks around ZT0 such as Elovl5 and Ppp1r3c enhancer–promoter looping and enhancer activation are synchronized with the peak of the NR5A2 protein. Given NR5A2's pioneer transcription factor activity in other contexts [33], we propose that it rhythmically binds and opens regulatory elements in the liver to temporally coordinate enhancer–promoter communication and circadian gene expression.

We also identified differences in the circadian interactomes of the liver metabolic genes Elovl5, Ppp1r3c, Gsk3a, and the core-clock gene Bmal1. While the metabolic gene promoters engage in stable and dynamic contacts with regulatory elements, the Bmal1 gene promoter establishes a highly dynamic contact with an intronic enhancer. Supporting these findings, recent works show that liver circadian genes establish stable and oscillating interactions with regulatory elements around the day [7, 9]. Moreover, experiments conducted on Bmal1 knockout mice showed that dynamic interactions rely on the molecular clock, whereas stable interactions do not [7]. We found that the NR5A2 binds both dynamic and stable chromatin interactions for all analyzed interactomes. These findings suggest that NR5A2 can act in coordination with the molecular clock to bring regulatory elements together at specific times of day. In other cases, it appears to exploit pre-established chromatin structures to control target genes at a distance. By inhibiting NR5A2 in vivo, we observed a significant reduction in gene expression of Elovl5, Ppp1r3c, and Cyp8b1, metabolic genes peaking around ZT0, but not a reduction in Bmal1 gene expression also peaking at ZT0. Rhythmic gene expression control of an essential core-clock gene such as Bmal1 might not require tissue specific modulation by NR5A2 and could be solely provided by robust action of the core-clock transcription factors [26]. We also did not observe an impact upon NR5A2 inhibition on other metabolic gene expression, including Gsk3a and Cyp7a1, with transcriptional peak around ZT6—12, time at which NR5A2 is not abundant in the liver nuclei. Finally, NR5A2 inhibition also significantly reduced chromatin interactions between the Elovl5 promoter and its NR5A2-bound enhancers, accompanying the loss of its normal transcriptional diurnal rhythm.

Together, these findings provide the first in vivo evidence that NR5A2 orchestrates chromatin architecture and rhythmic metabolic gene expression in the liver by modulating enhancer–promoter communication (Fig. 6) and its inhibition dampens transcriptional rhythmicity of its targets ultimately leading to defects in glycogen and cholesterol metabolism. Materials and methods

Experimental model

All applied procedures were approved by the animal welfare practices according to the Institute of Cellular Physiology-National Autonomous University of Mexico animal facility board. Male C57BL/6 J mice were housed under 12 h of light:12 h (LD12:12) dark with food and water ad libitum for at least three weeks. Mice were then euthanized at ZT0, ZT6, ZT12, and ZT18, and livers were collected, snap-frozen, or fixed with 2% formaldehyde for further processing. Experiments were performed on three to five biological replicates per ZT. Mice used in this study were between 8 to 12 weeks old. All animal care and handling were performed following the Institute of Cellular Physiology-National Autonomous University of Mexico’s animal facility guidelines.

In vivo NR5A2 inhibition experiments

Male C57BL/6 J mice were housed under 12 h light:12 h dark (LD12:12) with food and water ad libitum for two weeks. The mice were then administered intraperitoneally with 30 mg/kg−1 of the SR1848 compound (Sigma, SML1513) or vehicle (10% DMSO, 10% Tween 80 in PBS) at ZT20 every 24 h for five days. After the last dose, animals were euthanized at ZT0 and ZT12. Livers were dissected, snap-frozen, or fixed with formaldehyde for further processing. A total of five animals per group were studied. All animal care and handling were performed following the Institute of Cellular Physiology-National Autonomous University of Mexico’s animal facility guidelines.

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted with TRIzol following the manufacturer’s instructions. The RNA integrity was validated by visual inspection of RNA bands on agarose gel electrophoresis. cDNA synthesis was performed using random primers with the Jena Bioscience SCRIPT RT cDNA synthesis kit (PCR-511S). qPCRs were carried out using the qPCR Green Master Mix with UNG/High ROX kit (PCR-374S). The sequences of primers used to amplify specific transcripts are provided in Supplementary Table 1. Relative quantification was estimated with the 2 −ΔΔCT method, using the Albumin mRNA as an endogenous control. Three to five biological replicates were performed, each with technical triplicates.

Cytoplasmic, nuclear, and chromatin fractionation of mouse liver tissue

For the subcellular fractionation of mouse liver tissue, 300 mg of frozen liver was transferred to a pre-chilled Petri dish on ice, and 500 µl of ice-cold Nuclei Isolation Buffer with protease inhibitors (NIB) (250 mM Sucrose, 10 mM HEPES, 1.5 mM MgCL, 10 mM KCL, 0.001% Igepal, 0.2 mM DTT) was added. The liver tissue was minced into small fragments (< 1 mm3) using a scalpel blade. An additional 1 ml of NIB was added, and the material was transferred to a pre-chilled douncer. The tissue was further disrupted by applying five strokes with a tight-fitting pestle.

The homogenate was passed through a 70 µm cell strainer placed over a 50 ml conical tube. The douncer was washed with 1 ml of NIB, and the wash was also filtered through the strainer. The strainer was subsequently washed with an additional 1 ml of NIB to ensure complete recovery of the homogenate.

The filtrate (total of 3.5 ml) was transferred to a 15 ml conical tube and centrifuged at 1000 g for 10 min at 4 °C using a fixed-angle rotor centrifuge. The supernatant, representing the cytoplasmic fraction, was carefully collected and aliquoted. The pellet was resuspended in 1 ml of NIB to wash residual material from the original tube, and the suspension was transferred to a new 15 ml conical tube. The sample was centrifuged again at 500 g for 10 min at 4 °C.

After centrifugation, the supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was resuspended in 500 µl of Nuclei Resuspension Buffer (NRB) (1% BSA, 2 mM MgCL on PBS1X). The suspension was filtered through a 40 µm cell strainer into a low-bind 1.5 ml microcentrifuge tube to further purify the nuclear fraction. The sample was centrifuged at 500 g for 10 min at 4 °C. The pellet was washed again with 500 µl of NRB, followed by another centrifugation at 500 g for 10 min at 4 °C. The resulting pellet was resuspended in 50 µl of No Salt Buffer (3 mM EDTA, 0.2 mM EGTA), incubated on ice for 30 min with occasional vortexing.

Following the incubation, the sample was centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C. The supernatant, representing the soluble nuclear fraction, was collected and aliquoted. The remaining pellet, containing chromatin, was resuspended in Radioimmunoprecipitation Assay (RIPA) buffer (10 mM Tris–CL, 1 mM EDTA, 0.5 mM EGTA, 1% Triton X-100, 0.1% Sodium Deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS, 140 mM NaCL) with protease inhibitors and processed for subsequent analyses.

Western blot

100 mg of liver tissue was lysed with cold RIPA buffer (10 mM Tris–Cl, 1 mM EDTA, 0.5 mM EGTA, 1% Tritón-X100, 0.1% Sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS, 140 mM NaCl). Homogenates were centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C. Protein content was determined using the Bradford reagent (BioRad), following the manufacturer’s instructions. 50 μg of protein per sample was separated in a 10% SDS-PAGE gel and transferred to a PVDF membrane. Membranes were blocked with 5% w/v non-fat milk in 0.05% PBS-Tween for two hours. Subsequently, membranes were washed thrice with 0.05% PBS-Tween and incubated overnight with the corresponding primary antibody at 4 °C. The primary antibodies used in this study are as follows: mouse anti-human LRH-1 antibody (PP-H2325—00), mouse anti-NR5A2 antibody (sc—393,369), mouse anti-histone H3 antibody (ab1791), and rabbit anti-GAPDH antibody (ab9485).

Then, membranes were washed three times with 0.05% PBS-Tween and incubated with the corresponding HRP-conjugated secondary antibody for two hours at room temperature (RT).

Finally, proteins were detected with a chemiluminescent substrate (P36599 [7], Millipore) on a C-DiGit Blot Scanner (LI-COR). Western blots were performed for two or three independent biological replicates.

Hepatic glycogen measurement

Ten milligrams of liver tissue were washed with cold 1X PBS, followed by dounce homogenization in 1 mL of molecular biology-grade water using a tight pestle. Homogenates were boiled for 10 min and centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 10 min. Supernatants were collected and aliquoted. Samples were diluted 1:100 in water, and 5 µL of each was used for glycogen quantification with a Glycogen Assay Kit (Abcam, ab65620) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Glycogen content was measured at 570 nm using a microplate reader and expressed as mg/g of protein.

Hepatic cholesterol measurement

Twenty milligrams of liver tissue were washed once with cold 1X PBS. The samples were then dounce homogenized in 1 mL of a solution containing chloroform, isopropanol, and igepal at a 7:11:0.1 ratio. The homogenates were centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 10 min to collect the organic phase. After evaporating overnight at 60 °C, the lipid residue was dissolved in 200 µL of Cholesterol Assay Buffer. Hepatic cholesterol was then quantified using the Cholesterol/Cholesteryl Ester Quantitation Assay Kit (Abcam, ab65359) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Cholesterol content was expressed as mg/g of protein.

4C-seq

4 C template preparation

Fixed adult liver tissue from two biological replicates per condition and ZT was sieved through a 70-μM cell strainer and dounce homogenized in 5 ml of ice-cold lysis buffer (10 mM Tris–Cl, 0.2% Igepal, 10 mM NaCl, 1X LAB, 1X PMSF, and 1X pepstatin) with a tight pestle for a total of 60 strokes on ice. Nuclei were washed and permeabilized with 0.3% SDS for 45 min at 37 °C with shaking. Triton X-100 was added to a 1.68% final concentration. Chromatin was then incubated overnight with 200 units of DpnII (NEB) at 37 °C with shaking. An additional 200 units of DpnII were added the next day for 8 h. Digestion efficiency was evaluated by visual inspection of DNA fragments on agarose gel. After heat inactivation, chromatin was ligated with 30 units of T4 DNA ligase (Invitrogen). Ligation efficiency was validated by visual inspection of DNA fragments on agarose gel. Then, cross-link reversal of chromatin was carried out using RNAase A and Proteinase K. 3 C DNA template was purified with phenol/chloroform extraction and resuspended in TLE buffer. 3 C template efficiency was validated by amplifying an internal control and a long-range interaction by end-point PCR. Subsequently, the 3 C material was digested with 20 units of Csp6I (NEB) or 10 units of the NlaIII (NEB) enzymes. After digestion efficiency validation and heat inactivation of enzymes, DNA fragments were ligated with 3000 units of the T4 DNA ligase (NEB) on a final volume of 5 ml. Finally, the 4 C template was purified with phenol/chloroform extraction and AMPure beads and resuspended in TLE buffer.

4C-inverse PCR

Eight hundred nanograms of 4 C template were used for PCR amplification using Roche's Expand adaptors (Supplementary Table 2). The reaction was carried out on a final volume of 50 μL in the following PCR protocol: 2 min at 94 °C followed by 20 cycles of 10 s at 94 °C, 1 min at 61 °C, and 3 min at 68 °C, and a final extension of 3 min at 68 °C. Four parallel reactions of 200 ng were pooled per sample. Subsequently, an aliquot of 50 μL of the PCR pool was purified with the AMPure XP beads to eliminate the excess primers. The purified PCR products were resuspended in TLE buffer.

4C-seq library preparation

The purified 4C-PCR products were further amplified with the Nextera XT primers to introduce barcodes. This reaction was performed with the Phusion HF DNA polymerase (NEB) in the following PCR protocol: 30 s at 98 °C, followed by eight cycles of 10 s at 98 °C, 30 s at 55 °C, 30 s at 72 °C, and a final extension of 10 min at 72 °C. The final libraries were purified with the AMPure XP beads system to eliminate the excess primers and resuspended in TLE buffer. Libraries were analyzed with a Bioanalyzer and sequenced on NovaSeq equipment.

4C-seq analysis

FASTQ files were trimmed, mapped, and quantified using the pipe4C processing pipeline with default parameters [23]. Briefly, 4 C data were normalized by scaling all reads mapped to the viewpoint chromosome to one million, and interaction profiles were smoothed using a running mean over small windows of adjacent fragments to reduce the influence of overrepresented fragments.

Significant chromatin interactions were identified using peakC [38], which models the background interaction profile on both sides of the viewpoint using monotonic regression. For each fragment end, peakC computes the ratio and the difference between the observed running mean coverage and the expected background signal. Interactions were called significant using the following parameters: wSize = 21, defining the number of fragment ends used to calculate the running mean; qWr = 1 and qWd = 1.5, setting the thresholds for the ratio and difference, respectively; and alphaFDR = 0.1, specifying the false discovery rate used to control for multiple testing.”Visual inspection and overlapping of 4 C profiles on a genomic window of ~ 500 Kb surrounding baits were performed using pyGenomeTracks [39]. Quantification of 4 C reads on specific regions was performed using BEDTools [40]. Venn diagrams showing the extent of overlap between H3K27ac and NR5A2 peaks within TADs were generated using Intervene [41]. Finally, metaplots showing 4 C signal around NR5A2 peaks within TADs were generated using deeptools [42].

Metabolic gene selection

To compile a rigorous list of metabolic regulators, we retrieved gene sets from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB, Mouse collection). Specifically, we used version 2025.1. Mm (latest release as of June 2025) gsea-msigdb.org. We selected the following curated gene sets: GOBP Energy Reserve Metabolic Process, HALLMARK Bile Acid Metabolism, HALLMARK Cholesterol Homeostasis, HALLMARK Fatty Acid Metabolism, and REACTOME Glucose Metabolism. By merging and de-duplicating these gene lists, we obtained a final set of 494 unique metabolic genes.

Promoter capture Hi-C analysis

Publicly available promoter–capture Hi-C data from mouse liver were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO; accession GSE155153). BEDPE files containing promoter interactions at ZT0, ZT6, ZT12, and ZT18 were intersected with a BED file of HindIII fragments overlapping promoters of metabolic genes using a custom Python script. To characterize regulatory features associated with these interactions, a BED file comprising shared and ZT-specific promoter-interacting regions was intersected with H3K27ac peaks at CT0 using BEDTools [40]. Corresponding FASTA sequences for H3K27ac peaks at shared and ZT-specific interactions were extracted from the mm39 mouse genome. Motif enrichment analysis was performed using MEME-ChIP with default parameters [43].

ChIP-seq and GRO-seq retrieval and processing

Publicly available ChIP-seq data for mouse liver were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. Big Wig data sets were obtained under the following accession numbers: H3K4me1 CT0 (GSM982784), H3K4me1 CT12 (GSM982787), H3K27ac CT0 (GSM982805), H3K27ac CT12 (GSM982808) H3K4me3 CT0 (GSM982791), H3K4me3 CT12 (GSM982794), H3K9ac CT0 (GSM982798), ROR-α ZT22 (GSE101116), ROR-γ ZT22 (GSE101116), REV-ERB-α/β ZT10 (GSE36375), CTCF ZT0 (GSM4696318), CTCF ZT12 (GSM4696320). The BED files were obtained under the following accession numbers: BMAL1 (GSE39860), CLOCK (GSE39860), NPAS2 (GSE39860), PER1 (GSE39860), PER2 (GSE39860), CRY1 (GSE39860), CRY2 (GSE39860), ROR-α ZT22 (GSE101116), ROR-γ ZT22 (GSE101116), REV-ERB-α/β ZT10 (GSE36375).

Bam file data set for the NR5A2 was obtained from the National Omics Data Encyclopedia (NODE), under the accession code (OEP000757).

GRO-seq data for mouse liver were obtained under the following accession numbers: GRO-seq ZT22 positive strand (GSM1437742), GRO-seq ZT22 negative strand (GSM1437742), GRO-seq ZT10 positive strand (GSM1437738), GRO-seq ZT10 negative strand (GSM1437738).

ATAC-seq data for WT mouse liver were obtained under the following accession numbers: ZT20 WT (GSM4682365), ZT08 WT (GSM4682367).

All datasets were converted to the mm39 mouse genome assembly using CrossMap [44]. For the NR5A2 track, peak calling and bedgraph file generation were performed using MACS2 [45] with default parameters.

NR5A2 ChIP-seq analysis

Genome annotation of the NR5A2 peaks was carried out using ChIPseeker [46].

Spearman correlation plots were generated using the multiBigwigSummary and plotCorrelation tools from deepTools [42], analyzing 1 Kb bins across the whole genome. Metaplots showing time-resolved histone marks around NR5A2 peaks, were carried out using computeMatrix and plotProfile of deepTools [42].

Visualization of ChIP-seq data tracks on specific regions was done with pyGenomeTracks [39].
