Autoantibody-Based Models for the Diagnosis and Activity Assessment of Takayasu Arteritis
Authors: Jianxiong Wu, Jing Cui, Fengjuan Li, Xin Tan, Xue Wang, Yuan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe precise diagnosis and activity assessment of Takayasu arteritis (TA) remain challenging. Using a two-phase approach, we screened serum from 9 TA patients and 10 healthy controls via HuProt microarrays, identifying three novel autoantibodies (anti-Table 2, anti-TRAC, anti-LILRA5). These were validated by ELISA in 175 samples (61 active TA, 42 inactive TA, 72 controls). Logistic regression models were developed from training (70%) and test (30%) sets. For diagnosis, Model 1 achieved AUCs of 0.88 and 0.86, outperforming CRP (0.58) and ESR (0.61). For activity assessment, Model 2 achieved AUCs of 0.78 and 0.79, superior to CRP (0.62) and ESR (0.58). Introduction

Takayasu arteritis (TA) is a rare autoimmune systemic arteritis that primarily affects the aorta and its major branches and is associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular complications [1]. Although TA affects people of all ethnicities worldwide, the disease exhibits a disproportionately high incidence in East Asia and predominantly affects women between the ages of 20 and 40 years [2]. Chronic inflammation of the arterial injury in TA may lead to arterial stenosis, occlusion, and aneurysm formation, which remain asymptomatic or result in life- or organ-threatening infarctions, such as acute myocardial infarction, hypertension, myocardial infarction, and ischemic stroke [3].

Currently, TA diagnosis relies on the classification criteria established by the American College of Rheumatology (ACR) in 2022 [4]. These criteria emphasize typical clinical manifestations, such as onset age ≤ 60 years, limb claudication, diminished pulses, asymmetrical blood pressure, vascular bruits, and characteristic arterial angiography abnormalities. TA diagnosis depends heavily on imaging confirmation and physician experience, making it complex and subjective, and it is prone to misdiagnosis. Therefore, the identification of reliable biomarkers could greatly simplify and standardize the diagnostic process. Although commonly used inflammatory markers, such as C-reactive protein (CRP) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), may reflect disease activity, they lack specificity and are not suitable for establishing a definitive diagnosis of TA [5, 6]. Therefore, there remains an urgent need for more sensitive and specific diagnostic methods for TA.

Autoantibodies have emerged as valuable biomarkers in autoimmune diseases, offering a minimally invasive, accessible, and reproducible means for diagnosis and disease monitoring, as well as providing insights into disease pathogenesis [7–9]. HuProt arrays, a type of protein chip technology, enable unbiased comprehensive detection of autoantibodies at the human proteome level [10]. Several serum autoantibodies have been identified and validated as biomarkers for autoimmune diseases and cancers using HuProt assays [11–15].

In this study, we applied a multi-phase approach to identify and validate novel autoantibodies for TA. Using human proteome microarray technology, we screened serum samples to discover candidate autoantibodies associated with TA. These candidates were verified by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) in an independent cohort. Based on these validated biomarkers, we developed two models, which demonstrated higher clinical application value than existing clinical inflammatory markers, including ESR and CRP, for TA diagnosis and activity assessment.

Methods

Study Design

This study consisted of two phases to identify novel autoantibodies: discovery and verification. Based on the findings, two models were developed for TA diagnosis and disease activity assessment, respectively (Fig. 1). In the discovery phase, unbiased autoantibody screening was performed using HuProt microarrays in a discovery cohort of 9 TA patients and 10 healthy controls. A total of 176 candidate proteins were identified based on fold change > 2 and a TA positive rate ≥ 33.33%. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was then conducted and explore 12 candidate proteins associated with immune-related pathways. To further improve diagnostic efficiency, we performed AUC analysis and selected antigens with AUC values > 0.7, ultimately identifying three candidate proteins (Table 2, TRAC, LILRA5) for subsequent validation. In the verification phase, antibody levels against these three antigens were measured by ELISA in a larger independent cohort comprising 103 TA patients (61 active TA patients and 42 inactive TA patients) and 72 healthy controls. To develop and validate the diagnostic model, the entire verification cohort (n = 175) was randomly partitioned into a training set (70%, comprising 73 TA patients and 51 healthy controls) and a test set (30%, comprising 30 TA patients, 21 healthy controls). To develop and validate the disease activity assessment model, the entire TA patients in the verification cohort (n = 103) was randomly partitioned into a training set (70%, comprising 43 active TA patients and 30 inactive TA patients) and a test set (30%, comprising 18 active TA patients and 12 inactive TA patients). The models were fitted using the training set and their performance was subsequently evaluated on the test set.

Human Serum Samples

The serum samples used in this study were obtained from patients who attended Beijing Anzhen Hospital, Capital Medical University, between July 2016 and August 2019. All patients met the 1990 American College of Rheumatology criteria for TA [16] and had undergone extensive diagnostic evaluations to exclude conditions that could mimic the disease (Behcet disease, Cogan syndrome, Kawasaki disease, sarcoidosis, Marfan syndrome, and infective aortitis). Healthy individuals undergoing physical examination were included as the healthy control group with matching for sex and age.

Criteria for disease activity were assessed as previously described [17]. Two independent rheumatologists classified disease as active or inactive TA in each patient. Criteria for active disease were detection of one or more new vascular lesions (e.g., aneurysms, stenosis or occlusions, and/or new arterial-wall irregularities) in arteries (e.g., aorta, innominate, subclavian, axillary, common and internal carotid, vertebral, superior and inferior mesenteric, renal, common iliac, and celiac axis) examined by vascular images in patients. Alternatively, using the NIH criteria, criteria for active disease were new onset or worsening of two or more of the following features: (1) systemic features without another cause; (2) elevated ESR; (3) indications of vascular ischemia or inflammation; or (4) typical angiographic features, including any imaging method in addition to conventional angiography [17, 18]. A positive finding on edema-weighted MRI alone was not regarded as an indicator of active disease. Criteria for inactive disease were as follows: (1) absence of any features of active disease for at least the previous 3 months; (2) absence of new vascular lesions and stability or improvement of one or more existing vascular lesions during imaging assessment. Of note, we documented the cumulative medication use for each patient up to the time of sampling, including glucocorticoids, immunosuppressants, and biologics, reflecting cumulative treatment exposure rather than a single time‑point assessment.

Serum samples were prepared as described previously [19]. Briefly, fasting blood samples were collected from the cubital vein in the morning and were drawn with an EDTA tube, before being centrifuged at 1,000 g for 15 min. The supernatants were aliquoted (200 µL/aliquot) to avoid repeated freeze–thaw cycles and subsequently stored at − 80 °C until analysis. Before the detection step, no hemolysis and no visible precipitation occurred in the serum samples. The study was approved by the Beijing Anzhen Hospital Ethics Review Board, and all patients provided written informed consent. The study was registered in the Clinical Trial Registry (NCT03372980).

Serum Autoantibody Profiling of HuProt Protein Arrays

We performed comprehensive serum autoantibody profiling using the HuProt Human Proteome Microarray, which features 21,155 purified full-length human proteins. The microarrays were provided by CDI Laboratories and purchased from Shenzhen Huada Genomics Co. Ltd. (Huada, Shenzheng, China). We provided 10 serum samples from healthy individuals and nine serum samples from patients with TA in the discovery phase for the HuProt arrays and separately detected autoantibody signals. The standard procedure for the HuProt arrays has been described previously [20]. The serum samples were diluted 1:200 in binding buffer (1% BSA in PBST) and incubated with the arrays.

Recombinant Proteins and Autoantibody Detection By ELISA

Two proteins (Table 2, TRAC) were purchased from CUSABIO (Wuhan, China), and another protein (LILRA5) was purchased from Sino Biological (Beijing, China). The concentration, purity, and molecular weight of all proteins were confirmed by sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis. ELISA was performed with a coating concentration of 5 µg/mL for Table 2, TRAC, and LILRA5. A secondary antibody dilution of 1:10,000 was used for Table 2, TRAC, and LILRA5. The details of the ELISA procedure have been described previously [21]. Optical density (OD) values were measured at a wavelength of 450 nm on an ELISA plate reader. Two blanks and six quality controls were set up on each ELISA plate for in-plate quality control and standardization on different plates, respectively.

Statistical Analysis

The OD values of the samples were normalized according to the quality control values of each plate. Continuous variables conforming to normal distribution are presented as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD), while those not conforming to normal distribution are presented as median (interquartile range, IQR). Categorical variables are expressed as n (%). For comparisons between two groups, the unpaired Student’s t-test was used for normally distributed continuous variables, and the Mann–Whitney U test was used for non-normally distributed continuous variables. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test. For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed. The cutoff value for each autoantibody or model was selected to achieve a specificity > 0.85 while maximizing the Youden index. Metrics including sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, Youden index, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, and AUC were calculated to evaluate diagnostic performance. Logistic regression analysis was performed to develop the models. Differences between the two AUCs were compared using the DeLong test. Data were analyzed and graphed using R (version 4.2.3) and GraphPad Prism (version 10.0). P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participants’ Characteristics

The baseline demographic information of the participants in the discovery phase is shown in Table 1. Healthy individuals were matched to the mean age and sex distribution of the TA group. None of the participants enrolled in the study had other autoimmune or known inflammatory diseases. Apart from the prevalence of hypertension, which was higher in the TA group than in the healthy control group (P = 0.033), the groups were balanced in terms of comorbidities. Most laboratory indicators were also expressed at higher levels in TA group, except white blood cell (P = 0.027) and neutrophil (P = 0.032) counts, which could reflect the degree of inflammation. Table 2 displays the clinical characteristics of patients with TA according to disease activity in the verification phase. Patients with active TA had a higher incidence of pulse and blood pressure inequality, fever, myalgia, arthralgia and arthritis (all P < 0.05). Regarding common inflammatory markers, no significant differences were observed in ESR and CRP levels between patients with active TA and inactive TA. Compared with healthy controls, TA patients exhibited elevated ESR levels (P = 0.013), whereas CRP levels showed no significant change.

Candidate Antigen Identification Based on the HuProt Array

In the discovery phase, 176 priority candidate proteins were screened based on the criteria of fold change > 2 and TA positive rate (PR) ≥ 33.33% (Fig. 2a). First, we performed KEGG pathway enrichment analysis on the 176 candidate proteins and visualized the top 20 pathways using circular plots (Fig. 2b-c). The results revealed that these pathways encompassed multiple biological processes, including metabolism, cell cycle, and immune response. To identify potential autoantibodies associated with TA, we focused on the immune‑related pathways that have been well documented to be involved in the pathogenesis and progression of TA. To systematically characterize all proteins encompassed by these immune-related pathways, we constructed an UpSet plot and performed heatmap analysis, which clearly illustrated the complex intersection patterns of 12 proteins across 9 immune-related pathways (Fig. 2d-e).

Functional pathway enrichment analysis of 176 candidate proteins identified from HuProt microarray screening

To identify novel biomarkers, ROC curve analysis was conducted among the 12 candidate antigens, with an AUC ≥ 0.7 considered to indicate significant predictive value. Among the antigens, Table 2, TRAC, and LILRA5 demonstrated superior diagnostic performance, with the AUC ranging from 0.74 to 0.76, sensitivity ranging from 0.33 to 0.56, and a specificity of 0.90 (Table S1). Finally, three autoantibodies against the antigens (Table 2, TRAC, and LILRA5) were selected as candidate autoantibodies for subsequent experiments (Fig. 2a).

Serum Autoantibody Concentrations in the Verification Phase

In the verification phase, 175 serum samples (61 patients with active TA, 42 patients with inactive TA, and 72 healthy controls) were utilized to assess the autoantibody concentrations against the three candidate antigens. The results revealed that the three autoantibodies (anti-Table 2, anti-TRAC, and anti-LILRA5) exhibited significant differences between the TA and healthy control groups (P < 0.0001) (Fig. S1). Moreover, the anti-Table 2 concentration was positively correlated with TA activity status (P < 0.05), and anti-TRAC was specifically elevated in the active TA group compared with the other groups (P < 0.0001). Anti-LILRA5 was consistently higher in the TA group than in the healthy control group, regardless of disease activity status (P < 0.001) (Fig. S1).

Diagnostic Performance of the Three Autoantibodies for Distinguishing TA From Healthy Controls and Classifying TA Activity

In the verification phase, ROC curve analysis of the three autoantibodies (anti-Table 2, anti-TRAC, and anti-LILRA5) was conducted, revealing that the diagnostic AUC values for distinguishing between patients with TA and healthy controls were 0.79 (95% confidence interval [CI] 0.73–0.86), 0.74 (95% CI 0.67–0.81), and 0.78 (95% CI 0.71–0.85); sensitivity ranged from 0.35 to 0.40; and specificity was 0.86 (Fig. S2; Table 3). These results were consistent with those in the discovery phase and demonstrated that the three novel autoantibodies exhibited high diagnostic value, with AUC values > 0.7.

When comparing the active TA group with the inactive TA group, anti-TRAC demonstrated the highest differential diagnostic value, with an AUC of 0.76 (95% CI 0.67–0.86), a sensitivity of 0.44, and a specificity of 0.86. Conversely, anti-Table 2 and anti-LILRA5 exhibited the lowest diagnostic value for active TA, with diagnostic AUC values of 0.69 (95% CI 0.58–0.79) and 0.56 (95% CI 0.52–0.74), respectively (Fig. S2; Table 3).

To evaluate whether prior treatment exposure influenced the circulating levels of the three autoantibodies, we compared the levels of these antibodies among treatment‑naïve patients (n = 32), treated patients with active TA (n = 30), and treated patients with inactive TA (n = 41). As shown in Fig. S3, given that majority of treatment‑naïve patients were in the active phase, we primarily assessed the differences between the treatment‑naïve group and the treated active TA group. No significant differences were observed between the two groups for any of the three autoantibodies. These findings suggest that prior disease duration did not substantially interfere with the autoantibody profiles in our study cohort.

We further performed Spearman’s rank correlation analysis to evaluate the potential relationship between disease duration and the levels of the three autoantibodies. The median disease duration (IQR) for the entire cohort of 103 patients was 4 (1–16) years, with 5 (1–14) years for patients with active disease and 3 (1–16.5) years for those with inactive disease. As shown in Table S2, no significant correlations were observed between disease duration and any of the three autoantibody titers (all P > 0.05), indicating that disease duration did not substantially influence the autoantibody profiles in our study cohort.

Diagnostic Performance of Model 1 for Precise TA Diagnosis

Based on the results of the ROC curve analysis, the three autoantibodies all demonstrated good diagnostic performance for distinguishing patients with TA from healthy controls. To improve the diagnostic accuracy, the three autoantibodies were introduced into the logistic regression equation for multi-factor multiple logistic regression analysis (Fig. 3a). The logistic regression equation was as follows: PRE (PTA) = 1 ÷ (1 + EXP (−(− 20.591 + 3.45 × Table 2 + 0.97 × TRAC + 3.30 × LILRA5)). The performance evaluation of the established model in the training set resulted in an AUC of 0.88 (95% CI 0.82–0.94), with a sensitivity of 0.69 and a specificity of 0.86. Similarly, in the test set, the AUC was 0.86 (95% CI 0.76–0.96), with a sensitivity of 0.60 and a specificity of 0.86. The Delong test demonstrated no significant difference in the discriminatory ability of the model between the training and test sets (P = 0.85) (Fig. 3b-c; Table 4). Notably, existing clinical inflammatory markers (ESR and CRP), for which the diagnostic AUC values were 0.61 (95% CI 0.53–0.69) and 0.58 (95% CI 0.50–0.66), sensitivity ranged from 0.28 to 0.35, and specificity ranged from 0.88 to 0.89, showed poorer diagnostic performance than Model 1 (Table 4).

Diagnostic Performance of Model 2 for TA Activity Assessment

Based on the varying associations of the three autoantibodies with disease activity, we subsequently reconstructed a diagnostic model for distinguishing the TA activity classification (Fig. 4a). The logistic regression equation was as follows: PRE (PTA−active) = 1 ÷ (1 + EXP (−(− 9.986 + 1.44 × Table 2 + 1.34 × TRAC + 0.74 × LILRA5)). The performance evaluation of Model 2 in the training set resulted in an AUC of 0.78 (95% CI 0.67–0.88), with a sensitivity of 0.65 and a specificity of 0.87. Similarly, in the test set, the AUC was 0.79 (95% CI 0.62–0.95), with a sensitivity of 0.56 and a specificity of 0.92. The Delong test demonstrated no significant difference in the discriminatory ability of Model 2 between the training and test sets (P = 0.69) (Fig. 4b-c; Table 4). Furthermore, Model 2 showed better ability to differentiate between different stages of disease activity than ESR and CRP, which had diagnostic AUC values of 0.58 (95% CI 0.47–0.69) and 0.62 (95% CI 0.50–0.72), respectively, sensitivity ranging from 0.33 to 0.36, and a specificity of 0.86 (Table 4).

Discussion

This study identified and validated three novel autoantibodies (anti-Table 2, anti-TRAC, and anti-LILRA) in TA and developed two models—one for precise TA diagnosis and one for TA activity assessment. The two models demonstrated superior diagnostic value compared with conventional clinical inflammatory markers (ESR and CRP) for TA diagnosis and activity assessment.

The current assessment system of TA relies heavily on imaging examinations, which are both costly and technically complex. Moreover, imaging findings often fall behind actual pathological inflammatory activity, failing to provide accurate or reliable prediction of disease progression prior to, or in early-stage, disease. By contrast, circulating autoantibodies have long served as established diagnostic biomarkers in autoimmune diseases owing to their direct link to immune pathogenesis [22]. Autoantibody testing offers a rapid, operator-independent, and minimally subjective approach, which may help reduce diagnostic delays and misdiagnosis. Importantly, specific autoantibodies can act as either effector molecules or indicators of key pathological pathways, offering potential for more convenient and precise TA diagnosis. For example, Mutoh et al. identified EPCR and SR-BI as TA-associated autoantigens with high specificity among autoimmune rheumatic diseases. Mechanistic studies revealed that autoantibodies against these receptors block their negative regulatory functions, thereby promoting endothelial activation and Th17-mediated inflammation [23]. These findings suggest that autoantibody testing may offer both diagnostic value and insights into the molecular mechanisms of TA.

While prior studies have reported autoantibodies, such as anti-SPATA7, anti-QDPR, and anti-PRH2, in TA, they have failed to differentiate the active and inactive states of TA [24]. Other antibodies, including anti-endothelial, anti-aorta, anti-ferritin, anti-annexin V, and anti-monocyte, were proven to be associated with TA, but they lack diagnostic specificity [25–29]. In comparison, the newly identified autoantibodies in the present study not only showed significant diagnostic performance, but they also demonstrated utility in distinguishing TA disease activity.

Notably, CRP and ESR, as traditional inflammatory markers, are widely used in the clinical management of TA [5]. We found that, compared with healthy controls, the TA group showed no significant elevation in CRP levels, while ESR levels were mildly increased. In the disease subgroup analysis, active TA patients exhibited no significant change in CRP and ESR compared with inactive patients. This finding further highlights the inherent limitations of CRP and ESR as traditional inflammatory markers. Their levels can be elevated in various inflammatory conditions, reducing their diagnostic specificity [30]. Furthermore, approximately one third of patients with TA in the active phase may present with normal CRP and ESR levels, failing to accurately reflect disease activity in a timely manner [5]. Collectively, these observations not only underscore the inadequacy of current biomarkers but also indirectly reinforce the central objective of our study, namely, to identify novel biomarkers with greater sensitivity or complementary value to compensate for the shortcomings of existing inflammatory indicators.

It is noteworthy that, in the validation cohort of this study, a subset of patients received hydroxychloroquine, which was used in combination with prednisone, other immunosuppressants, and biologic agents in all cases, rather than as monotherapy. Although hydroxychloroquine is not a standard therapeutic agent for TA, a study has demonstrated that hydroxychloroquine can enhance the anti-inflammatory effects of conventional therapy and attenuate radiographic progression in TA [31]. Additionally, other studies have suggested a potential adjunctive role for hydroxychloroquine in the management of TA [32]. These findings provide some rationale for clinicians considering hydroxychloroquine in selected patients. We also reviewed the relevant guidelines for TA. Although hydroxychloroquine is not recommended as a standard therapeutic agent for TA, the guidelines indicate that high‑quality evidence is currently lacking regarding the selection of conventional synthetic disease‑modifying antirheumatic drugs (csDMARDs). Therefore, the choice of appropriate agents should be based on a comprehensive consideration of multiple factors, including disease activity, severity, complications or comorbidities, fertility requirements, drug safety, and treatment costs [4]. This suggests the feasibility of hydroxychloroquine application in TA.

Our candidate autoantibodies were selected through a structured screening process informed by the immune pathogenesis of TA. Although the precise mechanisms of TA remain incompletely understood, immune dysregulation is recognized as central to its development [23, 33]. Table 2 functions as an adaptor protein in the Toll-like receptor/interleukin-1 signaling pathway and interacts with Beclin-1 to regulate autophagy, acting as a molecular switch in inflammatory responses [34, 35]. TRAC is integral to T-cell receptor assembly and antigen recognition; its dysfunction can alter T-cell receptor-mediated immunity and has been linked to disorders such as graft-versus-host disease [36–38]. LILRA5, a member of the leukocyte immunoglobulin-like receptor family, mediates pro-inflammatory responses in autoimmune conditions like rheumatoid arthritis [39]. Notably, the related soluble receptor LILRA3 has been implicated in TA pathogenesis, where its deletion may lead to natural killer cell overactivation [40].

Initially, we individually evaluated the value of each of the three antibodies in diagnosing the disease and distinguishing disease activity. The three antibodies achieved AUC values of 0.74–0.79 with accuracies of 56%–58.9% for disease diagnosis, and AUC values of 0.63–0.76 with accuracies of 55.3%–61.2% for distinguishing disease activity, indicating that each antibody alone has limited value in both diagnosis and activity assessment. In this regard, combining multiple biomarkers has proven effective in enhancing diagnostic accuracy in autoimmune diseases [15, 41]. For instance, a 44-autoantibody panel has improved diagnostic performance in rheumatoid arthritis [15]. In the present study, the three novel autoantibodies represent a relatively complete immune response chain, from pathogen sensing and innate immune activation (anti-Table 2) [42, 43] to antigen-specific adaptive recognition (anti-TRAC) [43] and pathogen clearance (anti-LILRA5) [44, 45]. To better reflect the activation of inflammatory pathways in TA and enhance the clinical value of autoantibodies, logistic regression was used to construct two models based on the three autoantibodies. Model 1, designed for TA diagnosis, showed robust performance with AUC values of 0.88 (training set) and 0.86 (test set). This significantly surpassed the diagnostic efficacy of conventional markers, including CRP (AUC 0.58) and ESR (AUC 0.61). Furthermore, Model 1 demonstrated higher accuracy under conditions of specificity agreement. Given the complex pathophysiology of TA activity, we developed a separate model (Model 2) for activity assessment, which achieved AUC values of 0.78 (training set) and 0.79 (test set). Model 2 may be more useful for the assessment of TA activity than CRP (AUC 0.62) and ESR (AUC 0.58), and it exhibited higher accuracy under conditions of specificity agreement. These results suggest that a multi-autoantibody approach can significantly enhance both diagnosis and activity assessment in TA.

In this study, we identified three novel TA-associated autoantibodies through a high-throughput proteomic screen followed by ELISA validation. The establishment of two models based on these novel autoantibodies has the potential to enhance TA diagnosis and activity assessment, with greater diagnostic performance than existing inflammatory indicators in clinical practice, including ESR and CRP. Our cohort included patients across different disease activity stages to more thoroughly evaluate the utility of these biomarkers in TA.

The study adopted a rigorous methodology and a relatively large sample size to ensure reliability. Several limitations of the present study should be addressed. First, the single‑center cross‑sectional design may limit the generalizability of our findings with respect to geographic, ethnic, and treatment variables. Accordingly, further validation through multicenter studies is required. Second, to further establish the specificity of these novel antibodies for TA, validation in patients with giant cell arteritis, other vasculitides, and autoimmune diseases is required. Future multicenter studies incorporating diverse disease controls are therefore needed to confirm the specificity of our findings. Third, due to the retrospective design of this study, information regarding the timing and dosage of therapeutic agents was unavailable for a subset of patients, future studies with standardized treatment protocols are needed to clarify its impact on autoantibody profiles.

Conclusion

In summary, we identified three novel autoantibodies and developed two models incorporating these autoantibodies as biomarkers, offering good accuracy for distinguishing TA from healthy controls and in evaluating disease activity. The models may have potential as valuable new tools for the clinical diagnosis of TA and disease activity assessment.
