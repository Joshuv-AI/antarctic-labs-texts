Understanding eating disorders in the Middle East: body dissatisfaction and westernization in Saudi Arabia and Turkey
Authors: Bernou Melisse, Hayriye Gulec, Lot Sternheim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionBody dissatisfaction is one of the maintaining factors of eating disorders and a well-known risk factor for their development. It was assumed that body dissatisfaction did not afflict non-Western countries. However, body dissatisfaction is reported in non-Western countries, and to what extent westernization is associated with body dissatisfaction and subsequent eating disorders is unknown. The purpose of the present study is to examine the association between Western acculturation and body dissatisfaction in Saudi Arabia and Turkey, and to determine the measurement invariance of the Body Shape Questionnaire.MethodsThe Body Shape Questionnaire and the adapted version of the Acculturation Rating Scale for Mexican–Americans II were administered to convenience samples in Saudi Arabia (n = 746) and Turkey (n = 363). A hierarchical multiple linear regression analysis was conducted with body dissatisfaction as the dependent variable and cultural orientation as the independent variable. Citizenship status, BMI, and sex were included as covariates. A multi-group CFA compared the item intercepts of the Body Shape Questionnaire in the two non-Western samples with a Dutch community sample (n = 1651).ResultsThe Body Shape Questionnaire scores differed (p = 0.036) between individuals who had lived abroad in a Western country (M = 88.2, SD = 38.1) for at least six months and those who had not (M = 82.0, SD = 36.5). The BMI, but not cultural orientation, was associated with body dissatisfaction (B = 2.4, R = 0.4, R square = 0.18; p < 0.001). The multi-group CFA indicated that the Body Shape Questionnaire had a single-factor structure, and the item intercepts were similar across samples (CFI = 0.92; RMSEA = 0.059; RMSEA 90% CI 0.50-0.68; SRMR = 0.07).DiscussionFuture research should evaluate preventative programs targeting westernization-driven sedentary lifestyles. The Body Shape Questionnaire can be utilized across cultures and may help further the understanding of body dissatisfaction and eating disorders in the Middle East.Keywords: Body dissatisfaction, Western acculturation, Saudi Arabia, Turkey, Body shape questionnaire, Measurement invariance, BMI

Introduction

The Middle East reports a surge in eating disorders, yet research into the sociocultural mechanisms, including acculturation processes, remains scarce [1]. Body dissatisfaction, defined as the subjective negative evaluation of one’s physical body [2, 3], is recognized in Western cultures as one of the maintaining factors of eating disorders and a well-known risk factor for their development [4]. Understanding body dissatisfaction in Middle Eastern countries may contribute to the global understanding of eating disorders. Body dissatisfaction is a culturally bound syndrome [5], and cultural factors are essential to understanding its development [6]. Historically, body dissatisfaction is associated with Caucasian females in Western countries [7], and related research is heavily concentrated on Western samples [8]. In Western societies, body dissatisfaction is often associated with narrow beauty standards that prioritize slenderness and low body fat [3]. In contrast, traditional beauty ideals in Middle Eastern countries are considered more attainable [9], as they favor a curvier body, often associated with fertility and wealth [7, 8]. This contrast suggests that Middle Eastern females experience lower levels of body dissatisfaction, as Western beauty standards, primarily focused on female thinness, are associated with body image concerns [10, 11].

Recent research highlights that up to one third of female adolescents and young adults in the Middle East experience body dissatisfaction [12–14]. The expected modesty and body coverage in the Middle East can reduce appearance-focused pressures and body surveillance, potentially protecting against body dissatisfaction. However, modesty and sex norms are also associated with internal conflict, especially when balancing dress codes with global beauty ideals [15]. This tension may increase discomfort and self-monitoring for some females [16]. In a community-based sample in Saudi Arabia, high scores are reported on the shape concern subscale of both the interview and the self-report version of the Eating Disorder Examination, with 27% reporting body dissatisfaction as a symptom of an eating disorder [17, 18]. In Turkey, body image concerns are associated with weight-related behaviors in adolescents and emerging adults [9, 19–21]. Similar to Western studies [22], body dissatisfaction is associated with a range of psychological issues, including anxiety, depressive symptoms, low self-esteem, stress, and particularly eating disorder pathology [23–28]. These findings highlight that it is important to understand body dissatisfaction in non-Western samples, which could be attributable to exposure to Western values and beliefs regarding beauty ideals [29].

Western acculturation is the process by which non-Westerners adopt the language, lifestyle, values, and beliefs of Western cultures, encompassing aspects such as fashion, dietary habits, and media use [30]. It is not a unidimensional construct between traditional or westernized cultural orientations but rather a bi-dimensional construct where identification with one cultural orientation does not preclude the identification with the other [31, 32]. Recent literature in the Middle East indicates that acculturation extends beyond language learning and adaptation (e.g., Emiratis within the United Arab Emirates are not vocal in Arabic). Similar to the Western and immigration context, it is associated with increased psychological distress [33] including depression, anxiety [34], body dissatisfaction [35], and eating disorder pathology [36, 37]. Some scholars suggest that body dissatisfaction is especially prevalent in cultures in transition as they adopt Western values [5, 6, 36]. This view offers a broader framework for examining Westernization in cultures characterized by sociocultural changes [32]. It also helps address critiques from scholars who argue that findings from a single country in non-Western regions (e.g., Asia) are often over-generalized, highlighting that the role of westernization varies across nations depending on their exposure to Western culture and norms [38]. Considering these cultural dynamics, the present study explores the association between Western acculturation and body dissatisfaction in different sociocultural contexts in the Middle East.

Saudi Arabia and Turkey undergo sociocultural changes toward westernization, albeit from historically different pathways. In Saudi Arabia, economic prosperity driven by the oil boom lead to rapid societal and cultural shifts associated with a rise in body mass index (BMI, kg/m2) [7]. These changes disrupt traditional lifestyles, including dietary habits, an increase in the consumption of fast food, higher in salt, sugar, and fat [39], and a more sedentary lifestyle [1]. Consequently, approximately 70% of the Saudi population has a high BMI (BMI > 25) [40], and the scarce studies available suggest that the interplay between the rise in both BMI and internalization of the thin-ideal contributes to increased levels of body dissatisfaction [18, 41]. In contrast, Turkey has a long-lasting tradition of being in contact with Western ideologies [42, 43], including exposure to Western beauty ideals emphasizing thinness [44]. A high BMI remains a significant health concern, with an average weight increase of 12.5 kg for both females and males between 1974 and 2010, as reported by the Turkey National Nutrition and Health Surveys [45, 46]. Furthermore, higher BMI is particularly prevalent in the West and Central regions, where westernization and urbanization are more pronounced [45]. Similar to studies in the West, elevated BMI and thin-ideal internalization are risk factors for body dissatisfaction and, consequently, eating disorders [14]. Given these patterns, understanding the relationship between Western acculturation and body dissatisfaction in Saudi Arabia, which is undergoing a rapid transformation, and Turkey, which is experiencing a more gradual shift toward westernization and urbanization, may provide valuable insights into how sociocultural influences are associated with body image concerns in diverse cultural contexts in the Middle East.

Various measures are available to assess body dissatisfaction in Turkey [47, 48]. However, in Saudi Arabia, the Body Shape Questionnaire is the only assessment instrument to measure this construct [17]. The Body Shape Questionnaire was developed to measure body dissatisfaction, especially in individuals with an eating disorder, and was initially developed for Western samples [49]. Though its psychometric properties are examined in previous research [17, 50, 51], evidence concerning its measurement invariance across cultures remains limited. Measurement invariance refers to the psychometric equivalence of a construct across groups or time as measured by a psychological instrument [52]. It has not yet been assessed whether the items of the Body Shape Questionnaire are interpreted similarly in two Middle Eastern samples, each dealing with various degrees of sociocultural changes, compared to a Western sample. Unraveling these issues could help determine the suitability of assessment tools measuring body dissatisfaction in individuals with an eating disorder developed in Western countries to citizens in Middle Eastern countries.

Taken together, it is unclear whether Western/traditional cultural orientation, or other factors such as high BMI, female sex, or the varying degrees of sociocultural transition toward westernization across countries, are relevant to understanding body dissatisfaction in the Middle Eastern context. Despite recent literature on body dissatisfaction in the Middle East, there is a notable lack on research examining factors associated with acculturation in this region. Consequently, factors associated with body dissatisfaction remain insufficiently understood. Establishing the measurement invariance of the Body Shape Questionnaire is important to validate its use in assessing body dissatisfaction across cultures. The present study examined the associations of acculturation-related variables with body dissatisfaction in two Middle Eastern countries, Saudi Arabia and Turkey. Acculturation was conceptualized using a bi-dimensional approach that measured the extent of identification with Western culture and the maintenance of the original culture. Citizenship (Saudi Arabia vs. Turkey), BMI, and sex were examined as covariates in these associations, and the analyses were repeated for females and males separately. The history of living abroad was also evaluated as a proxy for Western acculturation for the citizens of both countries. As a secondary aim, the measurement invariance of the Body Shape Questionnaire among Saudi, Turkish, and Dutch samples was determined. The item intercepts were examined to determine whether the construct of body dissatisfaction could be interpreted similarly across cultures.

Materials and methods

Participants and procedure

In the current study, three convenience samples were collected from three different countries: Saudi Arabia, Turkey, and the Netherlands (see Table 1). All samples were part of larger studies. Data were collected anonymously, using a purposive community-based convenience sample and snowballing, chosen because experience showed that Middle Eastern citizens (Saudi Arabian and Turkish samples) are less inclined to participate in research [18]. Consequently, given the socially reclusive nature [53], the aim was to recruit a representative group within the available population. Convenience sampling was further driven by logistical constraints and the need to reach as many participants as possible within a limited time frame. Participants were informed about the study and could anonymously access the web-based self-report survey through a link or QR-code. They could tick a box to provide informed consent, and were debriefed after completion of the self-reports.

The Saudi sample was recruited in Riyadh, Dammam and Jeddah through various channels such as universities [King Saud University; Princess Noura bint Abdulrahman University (PNU)], high schools (Sixth High School for Quran Memorization), gyms (NuYu gym, Sukoun yoga studio), through social media (X, Facebook, and Snapchat), and the principal investigator’s (BM) network. Participants could earn study credit for their participation, providing an incentive for enrollment. Participants had to be Saudi passport holders, aged ≥ 18 years old, and literate. The self-reports were completed in Survey Monkey [54]. Data collection took place between April 2017 and May 2018. Ethical approval was given on May 7th, 2017 (17–0097), by the ethical review board of PNU in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia.

For the Turkish sample, psychology bachelors’ at Bursa Uludag University were recruited and received one study credit for participation. In addition to university-based recruitment, calls for participation were disseminated through various social media platforms (X, Facebook, Snapchat, and LinkedIn) and the second author’s (HG) professional network. Participants had to be Turkish passport holders, aged ≥ 18 years, and literate. The self-report measures were administered through the online survey platform Qualtrics [55], and data collection took place between June and August 2024. Ethical approval was granted on March 22nd, 2024, by the Social Sciences Research and Publication Ethics Committee of Bursa Uludag University (reference number 2024-03).

The Dutch sample was partially recruited through Tilburg University, where 11 students were tasked with 80 h of data collection as part of their Master’s thesis, as described elsewhere in detail [56]. Participating students received one study credit. In addition, participation was encouraged via social media (X, Facebook, and LinkedIn), health care facilities (general practitioners, hospitals, and mental health care centers), and through the principal investigator’s (BM) network, targeting both individuals with and without self-reported eating disorder pathology. Participants had to be Dutch citizens, aged ≥ 18 years, and literate. Similar to the Turkish sample, data were collected in Qualtrics [55]. Data collection took place between December 2023 and June 2024. Ethical approval was granted on 12-12-2023 by the ethical board of Tilburg University (reference number TSB_RP1246).

Measures

Demographic data

Demographic data were collected by self-report. Participants were requested to provide demographics such as nationality, sex, age, highest level of education, marital status, and history of living abroad for at least six months. Finally, BMI was calculated as self-reported weight in kg/ squared height in meters.

Body shape questionnaire

The Body Shape Questionnaire is a self-report questionnaire measuring body-shape dissatisfaction during the past four weeks, and is widely used among individuals with an eating disorder. The Body Shape Questionnaire entails 34 items, which were answered on a 6-point Likert frequency scale (1: never, to 6: always) related to, e.g., fear of weight gain, desire to lose weight, and self-devaluation pertaining to physical appearance. The total score is the sum of all items (34–204) [49]. Scores above the cut-off indicate clinically significant body-shape dissatisfaction in the context of an eating disorder. The BSQ is validated in Saudi Arabia, Turkey and the Netherlands. The Saudi Arabian version of the Body Shape Questionnaire has a cut-off of > 114 [17], the Dutch version of the Body Shape Questionnaire has a proposed cut-off of > 97 [50], and the British original has a cut-off of > 110 [49]. There is no availability for a Turkish clinical cut-off [51]. A sex neutral version of the self-report was used in all samples. In addition, the cultural adaptations made to the Saudi version of the Body Shape Questionnaire were reported elsewhere in detail [17]. No information was provided regarding the cultural adaptation process of the Turkish version [51]. However, further cultural adaptations of the BSQ items (beyond the minor Saudi adjustments) deemed not required, as all other items are direct measures of body shape dissatisfaction. The Body Shape Questionnaire has good psychometric properties, such as good test-retest reliability (r = 0.88) and high internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.96) (Pook, 2008; Welch et al., 2012). However, in the present study, item 26 (“Have you vomited to feel thinner?“) and item 32 (“Have you taken laxatives in order to feel thinner?“) were excluded from the analyses due to factor loadings below the recommended threshold of 0.50, indicating insufficient contribution to the latent construct [57]. This item was likely less relevant for community samples, as vomiting to feel thinner is more frequently observed in clinical eating disorder samples [58]. All other items were retained. Internal consistencies in the present study were acceptable (Saudi sample: McDonald’s ω = 0.75; Turkish sample: McDonald’s ω = 0.98), to excellent (Dutch sample: McDonald’s ω = 0.98).

Acculturation rating scale for Mexican–Americans II

The revised version of the Acculturation Rating Scale for Mexican–Americans (ARMSA-II) [31, 59] was only conducted in Saudi Arabia and in Turkey. The ARMSA-II measures westernization through two unrelated dimensions: (i) identification with a recently introduced Western culture and (ii) maintenance of the original culture. The ARMSA was initially established to measure acculturation among Mexican immigrants. Western acculturation and Mexican orientation were each assessed in 12 behaviorally based questions on a four-point frequency-based Likert scale (1: never to 4: very often) on four domains: language, media, food, and consumer goods, such as ‘How often do you wear Western (such as jeans, t-shirts, and skirts)/ traditional clothing?’. The total scores on the subscales ranged between 0 and 36 [31]. Some of the adaptations made to the ARMSA-II for use in Saudi Arabia are: language preferences involving Arabic as mother tongue, common Arabic restaurants such as Najd Village and Isteraha (desert-camp), and traditional Saudi dishes such as kabsah, maqluba, dates, samboosa and dolmah, are added. Traditional markets are mentioned as souks, as are traditional clothes, jallabiyah (for females) and thoob (for males) respectively. Further adaptations are explained elsewhere in detail [18]. For the Turkish sample, ARMSA-II was first translated into Turkish by HG and then back-translated into English by a PhD student in Psychology. After the research team checked the adequacy of the back-translation, the scale items were revised to reflect Turkish culture with examples of language, media, food, and consumer goods. The Turkish adaptation of the ARMSA-II include the changes of mother tongue preference to Turkish and the addition of examples for traditional Turkish foods for lunch and dinner (e.g., imambayıldı, karnıyarık), snacks (e.g., simit, börek), and desserts (e.g., baklava, helva). Also, traditional Turkish coffee houses and restaurants (e.g., kahve, meyhane), traditional clothing (e.g., başörtüsü, haşema), and traditional markets (e.g., pazar, aktar) were mentioned. Other studies showed good internal consistency: Cronbach’s alphas were 0.82 − 0.84 for the scale measuring identification with the original culture and 0.90 for identification with the recently introduced culture [18, 60]. Internal consistencies in the present study for the Western acculturation subscale were good: McDonald’s ω = 0.79 in Saudi Arabia, and McDonald’s ω = 0.76 in Turkey. Internal consistencies for the traditional cultural orientation subscale were acceptable: McDonald’s ω = 0.75 in Saudi Arabia, and McDonald’s ω = 0.69 in Turkey.

Statistical analysis

Software, assumptions, and internal consistency

The analyses for the primary aim were performed in SPSS version 29 [61]. Data were examined for multicollinearity, homoscedasticity, and normal distribution of residuals. Internal consistencies of the measures were measured by McDonald’s ω (≥ 0.70 acceptable, ≥ 0.90 excellent) [62].

Association between cultural orientation and body-shape dissatisfaction

A correlation matrix was calculated. Subsequently, citizenship and sex were nominal variables: Saudi Arabia (1) and Turkey (2), and male (0) and female (1). Furthermore, hierarchical multiple regression analyses using the stepwise method were performed with the following independent variables: citizenship, BMI, and sex were covariates and therefore entered first, followed by the subscale scores of the ARMSA measuring Western acculturation or a traditional cultural orientation as well as the scores on the various domains (language, media, food, and consumer goods) of the ARMSA subscales. The dependent variable was body dissatisfaction, which was measured with the Body Shape Questionnaire total score. Analyses were repeated for females and males separately. Regression coefficients of 0.30–0.39 were considered weak, 0.40-0.69 moderate, and > 0.70 large [63]. In addition, an independent samples t-test was conducted to compare the Body Shape Questionnaire scores between individuals who had lived abroad in a Western country for at least six months and those who had not.

Item interpretation

A multi-group Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) was performed in AMOS version 29 to assess if the Body Shape Questionnaire yielded similar factor solutions over the three samples. The Dutch sample was included as a Western reference group to assess whether the scale functions similarly across culturally distinct contexts, rather than to serve as a direct sociocultural comparison group. The single-factor solution was confirmed when the following criteria were met: Comparative Fit Index (CFI) ≥ 0.90, Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) ≤ 0.08, with the RMSEA 90% confidence interval (CI) not exceeding 0.08, and Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR) ≤ 0.08. The changes in fit indices, ΔSRMR, ΔCFI, and ΔRMSEA, were minimal, indicating a good model fit. Finally, measurement invariance was examined to assess if the items were interpreted similarly over the three samples [64].

Results

Participants

Table 1 summarizes the demographic characteristics of the three samples. In Saudi Arabia (N = 787), 5.2% had missing data and were excluded. Consequently 746 participants (M = 23.5 years, SD = 5.5) were included. The majority were female (85.9%), single (72.7%), and university attendees (39.5%). In the Turkish sample (N = 392) 7.6% were excluded due to missing data, resulting in 363 participants, predominantly female (82.4%), single (46.0%), and university students (79.9%). In the Netherlands (Dutch citizens, N = 1689), 2.2% were excluded for missing data, resulting in 1651 participants: 76.7% female, 71.6% single, and 53.0% university attendees.

Body dissatisfaction

The mean Body Shape Questionnaire total score in the Saudi sample was M = 86.0 (SD = 35.7). This was M = 77.4 (SD = 38.7) in the Turkish sample and M = 81.9 (SD = 38.4) in the Dutch sample. Table 1 shows the number of participants who scored above the national clinical cut-off and the cut-off of the British original. Multiple comparisons (F = 6.3, p = 0.002) showed that the Body Shape Questionnaire scores differed over the three samples. A Bonferroni posthoc analysis showed that the Body Shape Questionnaire total score was higher in Saudi Arabia compared to Turkey (p = 0.002) and the Netherlands (p = 0.038). There were no differences between the Turkish and Dutch samples (p = 0.165). Furthermore, the Body Shape Questionnaire scores did differ (p = 0.036) between individuals who had lived abroad in a Western country (M = 88.2, SD = 38.1) for at least six months and those who had not (M = 82.0, SD = 36.5).

Associations between variables

Table 2 shows a moderate association between BMI and body dissatisfaction in females and males in Saudi Arabia and in Turkey. Table 2 also shows that Western acculturation and traditional orientation were moderately associated. No other significant associations were detected between variables.

Association of western acculturation with body dissatisfaction and its covariates

Although the complete model was significant, step 1 of the hierarchical regression analysis (F (3, 1041) = 48.4; p < 0.001) indicated that being a Saudi or a Turkish citizen and sex were not associated with body dissatisfaction. Only BMI was moderately and positively associated with body dissatisfaction (B = 2.4, 95% confidence interval 2.1–2.7; R = 0.4, R square = 0.18; t = 15.0; partial r = 0.4; p < 0.001). Subsequently, in Step 2 of the hierarchical regression analysis, the four domains of the subscales and the subscale scores of Western acculturation and traditional cultural orientation were entered. After adjusting for BMI, none of the ARMSA subscales or their domains were associated with the Body Shape Questionnaire score. Citizenship, sex, the ARMSA Western acculturation subscale and its domains, and the ARMSA traditional orientation subscale and its domains did not contribute to the model and were therefore excluded. Finally, the analysis was repeated for females (B = 2.6; 95% confidence interval 0.8-2.4; R = 0.2; R square = 0.04; t = 11.6; partial r = 0.4; p < 0.001) and males (B = 1.5, 95% confidence interval 2.1-3.0; R = 0.04; R square = 0.002; t = 3.9; partial r = 0.4; p < 0.001) separately, which yielded similar results, only BMI was associated with body dissatisfaction.

Multi-group confirmatory factor analysis

The results of the multi-group CFA indicated that the Body Shape Questionnaire had a single-factor structure (CFI = 0.92; RMSEA = 0.059; RMSEA 90% CI 0.50-0.68; SRMR = 0.07; ΔSRMR = 0.01; ΔCFI = 0.005; ΔRMSEA = 0.011), irrespective of citizenship. Item intercepts were similar, and items were interpreted similarly over the three cultures [64].

Discussion

The present explorative cross-sectional study examined the associations of acculturation-related variables with body dissatisfaction in two Middle Eastern countries: Saudi Arabia and Turkey. It is essential to examine variables associated with body dissatisfaction in cultures in transition, given its association with eating disorders, especially in light of the more recent reports on the prevalence of eating disorders in the Middle East [1]. Acculturation-related variables assessed participants’ identification with Western culture and the extent to which they maintained their original cultural identity. In both countries, BMI, sex, and citizenship status were included as covariates, as prior research showed that these factors, particularly higher BMI, female sex, and sociocultural transitions toward westernization, appeared to be associated with increased body dissatisfaction [10, 29]. Additionally, the analysis involved a comparison of body dissatisfaction between individuals in Saudi Arabia and Turkey, with and without a history of living in a Western country. As a secondary aim, the measurement invariance of the Body Shape Questionnaire was examined by comparing its item intercepts in Saudi and Turkish samples with those of a Dutch sample to determine whether the Body Shape Questionnaire performs similarly across Western and Middle Eastern samples.

Western acculturation, measured by the extent of adopting Western cultural norms and values, was not associated with body dissatisfaction. This finding contrasts with prior research suggesting an association between body dissatisfaction and westernization in the Gulf [35], parts of Asia [65], and immigrant populations [66, 67]. This lack of association may stem from several factors. Firstly, there were methodological differences from the other studies examining Western acculturation and body dissatisfaction. Namely, Brokhoff and colleagues (2012) included adolescents and used the Vancouver Index of Acculturation [68] to assess cultural identification, whilst AlShebali et al. (2020) developed their own measure: the Internalization of Western Values Scale. Therefore, the differences in sample characteristics and measurement instruments may account for the contradictory findings.

Western acculturation was measured via the ARMSA-II in the present study. The ARMSA-II was initially developed in line with the acculturation framework by Berry (2005), which delineated two separate acculturation processes: maintenance of the original culture and development of relationships with the recently introduced culture [69]. These processes were measured with questions involving language use and preference, ethnic identity and classification, cultural heritage, ethnic behaviors, and ethnic interactions, as is reflected in the ARMSA-II items. Although the ARMSA-II has been validated in a migrant population [59], prior studies used the ARMSA-II in subgroups in a country rather than immigrant populations [60, 70]. Whilst the acculturation experienced by the cultural subgroups and the cultural majority of a country may be similar within a population, studies examining this issue are lacking. Experiences may, in fact, be very different, and hence, until the ARMSA-II is validated in non-immigrant populations, results should be interpreted with caution.

Secondly, there is an overlap between concepts such as westernization, acculturation, globalization, and urbanization, all under the umbrella of modernization [71]. The ARMSA-II items may reflect various elements of these varying constructs, not just Western acculturation. The associations with factors such as socioeconomic status, historical connection to the West (colonization), level of urbanization, and situational factors such as easy web access may all be associated with the current findings. Although the ARMSA-II offered a broad assessment of acculturation, it may not fully capture the complexity of cultural orientation related to Western contexts. Nevertheless, the ARMSA-II is recognized as a good measure for acculturation [72, 73].

The Body Shape Questionnaire was the only available instrument in Saudi Arabia and, therefore, was utilized to assess body dissatisfaction. The Body Shape Questionnaire evaluates a specific dimension of body image, namely dissatisfaction with one’s shape and size in the context of an eating disorder, which may not encompass salient culturally specific aspects of appearance. Broader facets of physical appearance, such as different body parts, skin color, body hair, or facial features, may hold greater relevance in the context of westernization and related beauty norms [11, 58, 74, 75]. These features are often subject to sociocultural ideals that extend beyond weight and shape. Indeed, a recent review concluded that culture is strongly associated with body image, highlighting important cultural differences even across regions of a single country [75]. Future research would benefit from including assessment tools that address a wider range of appearance-related concerns, such as the Body Uneasiness Test [76], to provide a more comprehensive understanding of body image across diverse cultural settings. However, the absence of validated instruments assessing such a wider range of appearance-related concerns at the time of data collection precluded its inclusion in the present study.

The roles of citizenship, sex, and BMI were evaluated as associated covariates in the present study. In the regression analyses, citizenship status was not associated with body dissatisfaction. However, univariate analyses indicated that body dissatisfaction was higher in the Saudi Arabian sample than in the Turkish and Dutch samples, with no differences between the latter two groups. Recent reports have indicated high levels of body dissatisfaction in Saudi Arabia [18], which were associated with various eating disorder pathologies [18, 77], underscoring the urgent need to address body image concerns in this context.

In accordance with other studies [18, 66, 78], the data showed that Saudi and Turkish citizens with a history of residing in a Western country for at least six months reported higher levels of body dissatisfaction compared to those who had not lived abroad. This finding indicated that citizens of two Middle Eastern countries with a sojourner experience in a Western country were similar and more likely to experience body dissatisfaction than their counterparts who did not have such an experience, and therefore supports the association between immigration-related stress and body dissatisfaction.

Prior research indicated an association between body dissatisfaction and westernization [35, 79]. In the present study, a moderate association was observed between Western acculturation and traditional orientation in both countries, suggesting the concurrent retention of elements from both original and Western cultures. Acculturation is a complex, bidirectional process, with individuals not necessarily prioritizing one culture over the other but integrating elements from both [31, 32]. In other words, perhaps in these samples, the Western beauty ideals that may be associated with body shape and size dissatisfaction have not taken over the traditional values (yet). However, it is equally plausible that traditional beauty ideals in Saudi Arabia and Turkey may already reflect Westernization, reducing the association with further acculturation. Moreover, the concept of “Western culture” should be recognized as heterogeneous, encompassing diverse and sometimes conflicting beauty norms across different Western contexts. For example, a recent study examining beauty norms across five European cities found that whilst male beauty standards were nationally specific, primarily based in racial and sex ideologies, female beauty standards did not vary across countries, but along lines of (emerging) cultural capital and sex [80]. Therefore, references to Western beauty ideals in the present study were not intended to imply a singular or uniform standard, but rather reflected generalized patterns commonly represented in globalized media and prior literature. In accordance with this, Western acculturation was assessed based on domains concerning the preference of language use, media, food, and consumer goods.

Other factors that were not included in the current study may also be associated with body image perceptions, like family, religion, or social media [36, 81, 82]. Furthermore, thin-ideal internalization has been identified as a central construct in explaining the association between westernization, body dissatisfaction, and consequent eating disorders in a prior review [1]. However, the absence of validated instruments for assessing thin-ideal internalization in Saudi Arabia at the time of data collection precluded its inclusion in the present study. As a result, a potentially critical pathway in the association of acculturation and body dissatisfaction could not be examined. These observations underscore the complexity of the relationship between acculturation and body dissatisfaction, and point to the need for further investigation into nuanced dimensions of westernization across culturally related national and regional contexts. Additionally, one could also speculate that with westernization, changes in fashion include all-body covering clothing items being replaced by items that cover much less of the body. Subsequently, females are more exposed to their own body but also to others, which may start the process of social comparison, which in turn has been associated to a more negative body image [83]. Specifically, future studies into body image in the context of westernization should include measures of internalization of Western beauty ideals (i.e., the thin ideal).

Sex was not a significant covariate in the association between Western acculturation and body dissatisfaction. However, as the samples were over 85% female, results may be female-sex-driven, and conclusions were hard to draw. It should be noted that similar results were found when the analyses were repeated for females and males separately, though the sample of males was significantly smaller than the sample of females. Future research should therefore aim to include larger groups of males. Body dissatisfaction was positively but moderately associated with BMI, in accordance with various other studies examining the association between BMI and body dissatisfaction in non-Western samples [18, 84–86], as well as in Western ones [87].

Furthermore, the association between BMI and body dissatisfaction was consistent, irrespective of citizenship, sex, and the degree of sociocultural changes toward westernization. Whilst previous cross-cultural research reported greater body dissatisfaction in countries with greater levels of westernization, the association was no longer significant when BMI was included as a covariate [29]. Modernization, including westernization, tends to coincide with a more sedentary lifestyle, associated with a rise in BMI [88, 89]. The rise in BMI is especially a concern in Saudi Arabia, with 70% of the population reporting a BMI above 25 [18]. A study by the Republic of Turkey Ministry of Health General Directorate of Public Health (2017) revealed that the prevalence of a BMI > 25 and a BMI > 30 above the age of 15 was 34% and 31%, respectively. In accordance with this, various other studies found that a high BMI determined the association of eating disorder pathology with body dissatisfaction, stress, and low self-esteem in Western and non-Western samples [6, 85, 86, 90]. In other words, a high BMI, and potentially associated negative beliefs could be associated with the development of other psychopathologies, especially eating disorders.

Therefore, preventative programs addressing a sedentary lifestyle could benefit individuals living in the Middle East. Notably, such programs should address maladaptive weight loss strategies to prevent the development of an eating disorder [91]. Effective programs to improve body dissatisfaction, such as the Body Project implemented in Saudi Arabia, seem essential to reduce the negative consequences of body dissatisfaction [3, 92]. Examining the efficacy of such programs in Turkey is also recommended in future studies.

The present study also examined whether the Body Shape Questionnaire [49], initially designed for Western use, applies to non-Western samples. The analysis revealed a single-factor structure for the Body Shape Questionnaire across all samples, and item interpretations were consistent [64]. This finding indicated that body dissatisfaction is understood and measured similarly across these countries despite their varying levels of westernization. In other words, body dissatisfaction is a consistent construct in these diverse cultural contexts, and the Body Shape Questionnaire items may be interpreted uniformly, demonstrating the cross-cultural validity of the Body Shape Questionnaire [49]. However, future research is recommended to continue to validate the Body Shape Questionnaire in other contexts and to explore how cultural nuances might be associated with body dissatisfaction beyond the general framework provided by the Body Shape Questionnaire. Finally, based on the results of the present study and prior research [93, 94], it is recommended that item 26 (vomited to feel thinner) be removed when conducting the Body Shape Questionnaire in community samples. In addition, item 32 (laxative use to feel thinner) should be carefully evaluated for suitability in community-based populations, as its performance may vary across cultural contexts. In the present study, the exclusion of these items improved model fit without compromising construct validity, as the remaining items continued to capture core aspects of body dissatisfaction.

Several limitations should be considered while interpreting the present study’s findings. A significant limitation was that the data were collected through self-report measures. Specifically, the BMI, based on self-reported weight and height, might be biased because social desirability could have led participants to report weights that were different from their actual weights [95]. In addition, due to the unavailability of validated self-reports, the Body Shape Questionnaire was used to measure body dissatisfaction. In contrast, other measures include broader notions of appearance ideals. The Body-Esteem Scale for Adolescents and Adults (BESAA) [96], assesses individuals’ perceptions of how others evaluate their physical appearance, or the Body Uneasiness Test [15, 76, 97] measures satisfaction with skin and various body parts. The BESAA was only validated among children in Turkey [48] and among Tunisian Arab speaking populations [98]. Whilst the ARMSA-II is developed as a measure for acculturation, it has only been validated in immigrant populations and may not fully comprehend Western acculturation specifically, versus closely associated concepts such as modernization, globalization, and urbanization. The absence of measurements assessing the internalization of the thin-ideal represents a significant limitation. Although we controlled for key covariates (sex, BMI, and citizenship), other potentially relevant factors such as socioeconomic status, media exposure, or urbanization were not included due to data availability and the need to maintain statistical power. Future research would benefit from incorporating a broader range of sociocultural and contextual variables to more fully disentangle the interplay between acculturation processes and body image outcomes.

The data were collected from convenience community samples, while data from clinical samples are generally considered more reliable. In addition, convenience and snowball sampling, limited the generalizability of the findings due to possible self-selection bias and other unknown sampling errors. These limitations are common in Middle Eastern psychological research owing to recruitment and infrastructural challenges [18, 77, 99–101]. While Saudi Arabia’s socio-cultural context is broadly comparable to other Gulf Cooperation Council states in terms of rapid acculturation, traditional norms, and modernization policy, each country within the region has unique social, legal, and demographic characteristics that may modulate the observed effects [102, 103]. Moreover, while Saudi Arabia and Turkey exemplify different acculturation tempos and contexts, they do not represent the entire region. Although the findings of the present paper can not be automatically generalized to all Middle Eastern countries, the comparisons made offer novel insights in the association of acculturation tempo and context in this underrepresented region. In addition, the Dutch sample may differ from other Western populations due to distinctive cultural, health, and social contexts which can be associated with body dissatisfaction, eating behaviors, and related attitudes [104]. This uniqueness suggests caution when generalizing our findings to other Western groups. Future research should however, use probability-based sampling and include additional countries to increase generalizability.

In all samples, the participants were primarily female, single, and attending university, meaning that the sample composition differed from the general population. However, body dissatisfaction is most prevalent among female adolescents [3], and the similarities between the samples facilitated comparisons across them and enhanced generalizability to single females attending university. In addition, although the samples sizes were adequate for the planned analyses, the unequal sample sizes across the countries could potentially influence cross-cultural comparisons. Therefore, the results should be interpreted cautiously, as their generalizability to the broader Middle Eastern population is limited. We can not rule out that the respondents may have been more likely to participate because of their greater interest in healthcare, mental health, body dissatisfaction, or eating disorder pathology compared to the general population [105].

It is recommended that future studies examine the mediating and moderating roles of BMI and thin-ideal internalization to delineate the association between westernization and body dissatisfaction in non-Western countries. Finally, since the present study could only examine associations between variables, its cross-sectional design does not permit inferences about causal relationships. For instance, it is unknown if Western acculturation is a risk factor for increased BMI. Therefore, research using longitudinal data is recommended.

Despite its limitations, the current study has several strengths. It is one of the few large-scale studies conducted in two Middle Eastern countries, each experiencing various degrees of westernization. The sample sizes were relatively large, particularly considering the reserved nature of the population, which can be a barrier to study participation [106, 107]. This study, the first of its kind in Saudi Arabia, used a validated self-report instrument to measure body dissatisfaction. Furthermore, while many studies assume homogeneity within ethnic groups, both within- and between-group variances were explored by examining ethnicity and Western acculturation versus traditional cultural orientation. The interpretation of Body Shape Questionnaire items was also compared across three samples with varying levels of westernization. It revealed that a similar interpretation of the Body Shape Questionnaire across these cultural contexts was psychometrically valid, enhancing the generalizability of its findings.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the present study examined the association between Western acculturation and body dissatisfaction in two Middle Eastern countries with differing levels of westernization. No association between Western acculturation and body dissatisfaction was found, but this may reflect the difficulty of assessing acculturation to Western beauty ideals and using different assessment measures, or assessing different elements of acculturation or body image may have had different outcomes. BMI was positively associated with body dissatisfaction, reflecting global research findings. Furthermore, the Body Shape Questionnaire performed similarly across all samples, indicating its suitability for both Western and non-Western populations and suggesting its usability for further body image and eating disorder research in Middle Eastern countries.
