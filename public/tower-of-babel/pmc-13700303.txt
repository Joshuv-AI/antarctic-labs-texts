Itaconate-based hydrogels for immunomodulation and infection control: design strategies, biomedical applications, and translational challenges
Authors: Min Wang, Jiachen Wang, Guoqiang Wang, Mi Chen, Jing An, Hao Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractItaconate is a bioactive immunometabolite with anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and antimicrobial properties. Its unsaturated dicarboxylic structure allows for versatile polymerization and chemical functionalization. These characteristics make it a promising building block for bioactive hydrogels. However, current research on itaconate-based hydrogels is fragmented across immunology, polymer chemistry, and biomedical engineering, with a lack of a unified design framework. In this review, we summarize the biological properties of itaconate and its derivatives relevant to biomaterial design, including immunomodulatory effects and antimicrobial activity. We categorize itaconate-based hydrogels into two main strategies: those directly fabricated from itaconate or its analogues, and those loaded with itaconate or itaconate derivatives such as 4-octyl itaconate (4-OI) and dimethyl itaconate (DMI). We explore the relationships between composition, crosslinking chemistry, responsiveness, biological functions, and therapeutic applications in drug delivery, tissue repair, inflammatory diseases, and anti-infective therapy. We also highlight critical limitations, including insufficient structure-function comparisons, overreliance on small-animal models, limited mechanistic validation, and challenges in manufacturing, stability, biosafety, and clinical translation. This review presents a conceptual framework for the rational design of itaconate-based hydrogels and outlines key priorities for advancing these materials toward clinical translation as bioactive and immunomodulatory hydrogel platforms.Graphical Abstract Keywords: Itaconate, Endogenous immunometabolite, Immunometabolic regulatory, Antimicrobial biomaterials, Biomedical hydrogel

Introduction

Extensive advances in immunometabolism have highlighted the importance of endogenous metabolites as active regulators of inflammation, host defense, and tissue homeostasis [1, 2]. Among them, itaconate has emerged as a representative immunoregulatory metabolite produced predominantly by activated macrophages through immune response gene 1 (IRG1/ACOD1)-dependent decarboxylation of cis-aconitate [3, 4]. Beyond its metabolic origin, itaconate has attracted growing interest because of its unsaturated dicarboxylic structure and multifunctional biological activities, including anti-inflammatory and antimicrobial effects. These properties make it particularly attractive for developing bioactive biomaterials that are designed to modulate pathological microenvironments rather than merely serve as passive structural matrices [5–8].

Hydrogels have become the most promising biomaterial platforms for biomedical applications because of their high-water content, tunable network structure, injectability, biocompatibility, and capacity for localized therapeutic delivery [9, 10]. Incorporating itaconate into hydrogel systems offers a unique opportunity to integrate intrinsic biological activity with adaptable material properties. Structurally, the α, β-unsaturated dicarboxylic acid moiety of itaconate provides versatile chemical reactivity for polymer synthesis and network construction. Biologically, its immunomodulatory effects and antimicrobial activities may enable hydrogels to actively regulate inflammation, oxidative stress, and infection-associated tissue damage [11, 12]. Therefore, itaconate-based hydrogels represent a promising class of functional biomaterials that can be used in wound healing, infected tissue repair, drug delivery, and regenerative medicine.

Recent studies have greatly improved our understanding of the biological effects of itaconate [13–15]. Mechanistically, itaconate and its derivatives have been reported to suppress excessive inflammatory responses through multiple pathways, including inhibition of succinate dehydrogenase (SDH) activity, modulation of glycolytic metabolism, activation of nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (NRF2)-related antioxidant programs, and restriction of NOD-, LRR-, and pyrin domain-containing protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome activation [6, 16, 17]. In addition, accumulating evidence suggests that itaconate may participate in antibacterial defense and tissue repair processes, further broadening its biomedical relevance [18]. However, the therapeutic performance of itaconate within hydrogel systems is likely influenced by multiple design parameters, including hydrogel composition, the mode of itaconate incorporation, network architecture, and release behavior [19–23]. Thus, translating the biological functions of itaconate into robust hydrogel platforms is not only a simple incorporation, but also requires a mechanism-guided material design.

Although interest in itaconate has expanded rapidly, current literature remains fragmented. Existing discussions often emphasize either the immunometabolic biology of itaconate or general advances in functional hydrogels, whereas a focused analysis of itaconate-based hydrogels remains limited [5, 21–24]. Therefore, the relationships between itaconate chemistry, biological function, hydrogel fabrication strategy, and biomedical performance have not yet been systematically integrated. Moreover, important challenges remain insufficiently addressed, including how to balance bioactivity with material stability, how to compare different hydrogel design strategies, and how to evaluate translational feasibility in a realistic biomedical environment.

In this review, we focus specifically on the engineering of itaconate-based hydrogels. We summarize the biological properties of itaconate most relevant to biomaterials design, discuss current strategies for constructing itaconate-based hydrogel systems, review their emerging biomedical applications, and critically examine the key limitations and translational challenges facing the field. By integrating immunometabolic rationale with hydrogel engineering, this review aims to provide a clearer framework for the future development of clinically relevant itaconate-based biomaterials.

Biological properties of itaconate relevant to hydrogel engineering

Itaconate, an endogenous metabolite synthesized by activated macrophages, is increasingly recognized as a biomaterial-relevant immunometabolite with anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, antimicrobial, and antiviral activities [6, 7, 11, 25]. For hydrogel engineering, these activities are most valuable when they are translated into local and controllable material functions, particularly in pathological microenvironments characterized by persistent inflammation, redox imbalance, infection, and impaired tissue repair, such as chronic wounds and infected tissues [21, 22, 26].

Immunomodulatory effects

Itaconate functions as an endogenous metabolic signal that limits excessive macrophage activation and supports tissue homeostasis [5, 13–15]. Because native itaconate has limited membrane permeability, cell-permeable derivatives, especially dimethyl itaconate (DMI) and 4-octyl itaconate (4-OI), are frequently used in mechanistic studies. However, these compounds are not fully interchangeable with endogenous itaconate. DMI is more electrophilic but is not directly converted into itaconate, whereas 4-OI can release itaconate after esterase-mediated hydrolysis [27–29]. This distinction is important for hydrogel design: native itaconate is more suitable as a polymerizable or ionizable material motif, whereas DMI and 4-OI are better considered as releasable bioactive cargoes for intracellular immunomodulation [8, 21, 26, 30].

The immunomodulatory actions of itaconate can be summarized as follows (Fig. 1) [6, 16, 17]. First, itaconate rewires inflammatory metabolism by inhibiting SDH activity, altering mitochondrial respiration, and reducing the production of pro-inflammatory mediators such as interleukin-1β (IL-1β), IL-6, nitric oxide (NO), and hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α) [31, 32]. Itaconate and 4-OI can also suppress glycolysis through covalent modification of enzymes such as fructose-bisphosphate aldolase A (ALDOA), lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA), and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) [33–36]. Second, itaconate attenuates inflammatory signaling by inhibiting NLRP3 inflammasome activation, which involves disruption of the NLRP3–NEK7 interaction and subsequent reductions in IL-1β and IL-18 release [37]. Third, itaconate and 4-OI activate cytoprotective redox programs through KEAP1 modification and NRF2 stabilization [29, 38, 39]. Additional pathways, including ATF3-mediated inhibition of IκBζ, competition with α-ketoglutarate for TET2 binding, modulation of PPAR signaling, restriction of cGAS-STING activation, regulation of IFN-β responses, inhibition of IRAK4, and suppression of COX2-dependent prostaglandin production, further support the broad but context-dependent immunoregulatory profile of itaconate derivatives [40–50]. Additionally, chemoproteomic analyses have identified multiple cysteine-reactive targets of itaconate in inflammatory macrophages, providing molecular support for its broad effects on immune signaling, transcriptional regulation, and cell death pathways [51]. At the cellular level, these signaling pathways are associated with reduction of macrophage M1 polarization, promotion of regulatory T-cell responses, suppression of Th17 and CD8+ T-cell activity, and inhibition of neutrophil extracellular trap formation [5, 52]. The research evidence in inflammatory and immune-mediated disease models further supports the therapeutic potential of itaconate and its derivatives [53–56]. By activating the NRF2 pathway, inhibiting NLRP3 inflammasome activation, and modulating cGAS-STING signaling, these compounds can regulate macrophage polarization, reduce oxidative stress, suppress inflammatory cytokine production, and limit pathological cell death [57–72]. For hydrogel engineering, these findings provide a theoretical basis for the local delivery, sustained release, or covalent presentation of itaconate and its derivatives to achieve precise spatiotemporal regulation of the immune microenvironment and tissue repair [26, 30, 73].

Antimicrobial and antiviral activities

In addition to immunomodulatory effects, itaconate also participates in the host defense process of the body against microbial infection (Fig. 2) [7, 18, 74–77]. Its antibacterial activity is influenced by environmental pH and is mechanistically related to interference with microbial and host metabolic pathways [78]. The key targets of itaconate include bacterial isocitrate lyase (ICL) in the glyoxylate shunt and propionyl-CoA carboxylase (PCC), thereby impairing carbon assimilation and energy metabolism in susceptible bacteria [79, 80]. Itaconate and its derivatives also enhance macrophage-mediated pathogen clearance by regulating SDH activity, glycolysis, autophagy, phagosome maturation, and TFEB-dependent lysosomal biogenesis [81–84]. Host transporters, including solute carrier family 13 member 3 (SLC13A3) and adenosine triphosphate (ATP)-binding cassette transporter G2 (ABCG2), regulate itaconate uptake and export, influencing antibacterial efficacy [85, 86]. Other host-defense mechanisms include activation of oxoglutarate receptor 1 (OXGR1) to enhance mucociliary clearance, and spleen tyrosine kinase inhibition to reduce hypervirulent Klebsiella pneumoniae-induced tissue damage [87, 88]. Itaconate derivatives also display antiviral activity. For example, 4-OI has been reported to inhibit viruses such as SARS-CoV-2, influenza A virus, Zika virus, and Herpes simplex virus by altering host metabolism, blocking viral ribonucleoprotein export, and modulating antiviral signaling components including MAVS, IKKβ, and JAK1 [89–94]. From a materials-design perspective, these findings suggest that itaconate-based hydrogels may be useful in infected or infection-prone tissues, but their anti-infective efficacy should not be over-attributed to itaconate alone. Notably, in many itaconate-based hydrogel systems, robust antimicrobial performance also depends on the introduction of complementary components such as cationic groups, metal ions, nanoparticles, or photoresponsive elements [95–99]. Therefore, the core value of itaconate in anti-infection hydrogel is more likely to be reflected in the integration of infection control, inflammation regression, and tissue repair, rather than as an independent antibacterial agent.

Biological properties relevant to hydrogel design

The biological properties of itaconate should be interpreted as functional design cues for hydrogel engineering rather than as isolated pharmacological effects. Rational itaconate-based hydrogel design should therefore follow four principles. First, the material function should match the dominant pathological cues of the target microenvironment, such as inflammation, oxidative stress, acidity, infection, or impaired healing. Second, itaconate-related bioactivity should be integrated into the hydrogel architecture through controlled release, covalent incorporation, ionic interactions, or responsive degradation. Third, the hydrogel should provide lesion-responsive functional adaptation, allowing the material to interact dynamically with biochemical and physicochemical signals in diseased tissues. Finally, biological mechanisms and hydrogel engineering should be co-designed to achieve synergistic optimization of material structure, biological function, and therapeutic outcome (Fig. 3).

Among these biological activities, anti-inflammatory and antioxidant functions are the most directly relevant to hydrogel-based tissue repair. SDH activity inhibition, NLRP3 inflammasome suppression, and KEAP1/NRF2 activation provide mechanistic support for designing itaconate-based hydrogels for inflammatory and oxidative stress-related microenvironments, including diabetic wounds, osteoarthritis, infected tissues, and other sites of impaired healing. Anti-infective mechanisms can further benefit applications where inflammation and infection coexist, such as chronic wounds, diabetic ulcers, gastrointestinal injury, and biomaterial-associated infection [5, 12, 21, 30, 76, 100]. Thus, itaconate-based hydrogels should be viewed not as passive carriers, but as localized immunometabolic material platforms capable of coordinating inflammation control, pathogen-management support, and tissue regeneration.

Native itaconate and its derivatives also play different roles in material construction. Because of its dicarboxylate structure and polymerizable unsaturation, native itaconate can function as a structural monomer, ionizable network component, or reactive site for further chemical functionalization in directly constructed or functionalized hydrogels [21, 23, 95]. In contrast, DMI and 4-OI are more suitable as encapsulated or conjugated cargoes when intracellular pharmacological activity after local release is required [22, 26]. This distinction helps to achieve a reasonable match between the molecular form of itaconate and the function of the hydrogel: native itaconate mainly contributes network functionality and physicochemical responsiveness, whereas DMI and 4-OI primarily contribute post-release bioactivity. Despite the above advantages, itaconate-based hydrogels still need to solve several key problems before realizing transformation applications. At present, the structure-activity relationship of itaconate and its derivatives has not been fully elucidated, and the relative roles of free, released, and covalently bound itaconate in biomaterial systems are still unclear. In addition, signals related to itaconate may produce adverse effects that depend on specific biological contexts, including interference with erythropoietic heme biosynthesis, altered antitumor immunity, or undesirable effects on lineage-specific cell differentiation [101–104]. Therefore, future itaconate-based hydrogel studies should define dose windows, release kinetics, cell specificity, degradation behavior, and long-term safety in parallel with material performance.

Design strategies for itaconate-based hydrogels

Itaconate was initially considered a highly promising platform compound for biomass sources. Although its early industrialization process fell behind several competitive bio-based alternatives, there has been a significant increase in attention from academia towards itaconate in recent years [105]. This renewed research enthusiasm is mainly due to its dual properties of multifunctional chemical precursors and endogenous immunometabolites. From the perspective of molecular structure, itaconate contains both dicarboxylic acid groups and α, β-unsaturated double bonds, so it has a wide range of chemical reactivity and synthesis scalability, and can be further used to prepare renewable resins, polyester, and multi-functional hydrogel systems. The above structural and functional advantages make itaconate an important molecular platform for connecting sustainable chemistry, polymer engineering, and advanced biomaterial design [11]. From the perspective of hydrogel engineering, itaconate-based hydrogels are more appropriately classified according to how biological activity is introduced into the network rather than being regarded as a single material class. In general, current systems can be understood within two major design modes (Fig. 4). The first comprises hydrogels directly fabricated from itaconate or its analogues, in which itaconate primarily serves as a structural component of the network. These materials are typically explored for pH-responsive drug delivery, antimicrobial platforms, and infected-wound healing, owing to the ionizable and chemically functional nature of the itaconate motif [106, 107]. The second comprises carrier-based hydrogels loaded with itaconate or its derivatives, in which therapeutic activity is achieved mainly through the controlled release of bioactive molecules such as 4-OI or DMI. Such systems have been more widely developed for inflammatory disease and tissue repair applications, where sustained local delivery is required to regulate pathological microenvironments [26, 108]. It should be emphasized that the differences between these two design patterns are not only reflected in their preparation and formulation strategies, but also in their inherent biological logic, evaluation criteria, and translational challenges. For directly fabricated hydrogels, itaconate is mainly used to endow materials with physicochemical responsiveness, network construction ability, degradability, and potential scaffold-level bioactivity. In contrast, carrier-based hydrogels are more dependent on the loading of active substances, controlled release, intracellular targeting, dose-related safety, and the pharmacological activity of released itaconate derivatives. This distinction is particularly important because the material advantages of directly fabricated hydrogels, such as biological source, pH responsiveness, and controllable network structure, do not necessarily mean that they can become an effective immunopharmacological delivery platform. Conversely, even biologically active 4-OI- or DMI-loaded hydrogels may have limited translational potential if their release profiles, formulation complexity, or long-term in vivo fate remain insufficiently controlled [21–23, 26, 30]. Therefore, classifying itaconate-based hydrogels according to the design pattern helps avoid generalizing different systems and provides a clearer analytical framework for comparing their respective advantages, limitations, and biomedical application values.

Directly fabricated hydrogels from itaconate or its analogues

The most direct strategy for fabricating itaconate-based hydrogels is to use itaconate or its analogues as the structural unit of the hydrogel network. This design utilizes the unique α, β-unsaturated dicarboxylic architecture of itaconate, allowing it to participate in both free-radical polymerization (FRP) and the formation of polyester networks. In such systems, itaconate is not only introduced as a releasable active load, but is covalently integrated into network-building units, thereby endowing the material scaffold with the potential to participate in biological functional regulation [11, 109, 110]. Despite these advantages, the intrinsic chemistry of native itaconate imposes important constraints on direct network construction. In particular, the free-radical homopolymerization (FRhP) of itaconate in aqueous solution is limited by relatively low reactivity, strong pH dependence, and poor monomer conversion. FRhP is nearly suppressed above pH 4 because of carboxylate ionization, whereas copolymerization with monomers such as acrylic derivatives markedly improves polymerization efficiency and pH tolerance [11, 23, 111]. Further studies have shown that the most favorable polymerization conditions are achieved when itaconate is present in its half-deprotonated form, in which aqueous solubility is maximized and efficient homopolymerization becomes possible at higher monomer concentrations. At the same time, both the polymerization rate and the molecular weight of the resulting polymer decrease significantly as the degree of ionization increases at a given monomer concentration [112]. Thus, although native itaconate remains limited in direct FRP because of the low reactivity of its double bond and its poor homopolymerization tendency, it continues to be regarded as a promising low-cost bio-based platform chemical because it can be strategically converted into more reactive derivatives suitable for FRP, thereby expanding its utility in hydrogel design (Fig. 5) [21, 113, 114].

Consistent with this chemical flexibility, hydrogels have been directly fabricated using itaconate or its analogues copolymerized with alkene-containing compounds via various physical and chemical initiation strategies (Table 1). Physical methods include γ-ray, ultraviolet (UV), and microwave irradiation, whereas chemical initiation has been achieved using persulfates or cerium ammonium nitrate. These fabrication routes provide substantial control over network density, swelling behavior, and drug loading/release performance [106, 107, 115–117]. For example, under γ-ray-induced free-radical copolymerization (FRcP), a series of copolymeric hydrogels has been successfully synthesized from itaconate with N-isopropylacrylamide (NIPAAm), N-vinyl-2-pyrrolidone (NVP), or 2-hydroxyethyl methacrylate (HEMA). These systems exhibited tunable drug loading capacities that depended on itaconate content, together with drug release profiles modulated by environmental pH, temperature, and network crosslinking density [117–119]. Similarly, UV-induced FRcP has been used to fabricate pH-responsive hydrogels based on itaconate and poly(ethylene glycol) (PEG)-derived grafts, including tetra(ethylene glycol) dimethacrylate (TEGDMA) and PEG methyl ether methacrylate (PEGMMA) [23]. In addition, the widely used camphorquinone/tertiary amine initiating system has enabled in situ blue-light gelation of itaconoyl chloride and PEG in the presence of dimethylaminoethyl methacrylate (DMAEMA) and HEMA. The resulting hydrogel displayed high gelation yield, moderate swelling, favorable compressive strength, excellent chondrocyte compatibility, and enhanced glycosaminoglycan (GAG) synthesis, indicating its promise as an injectable photocurable carrier for cartilage repair [120]. Likewise, a hydrogel fabricated by grafting itaconate and lactic acid onto tragacanth gum via microwave irradiation exhibited antioxidant activity together with controlled drug delivery potential [121]. Chemical initiator-induced FRcP has likewise played an important role in optimizing itaconate-based hydrogel systems. Initiators such as potassium persulfate (PPS) or ammonium persulfate (APS), together with crosslinkers including PEG diacrylate (PEGDA), ethylene glycol dimethacrylate (EGDMA), N, N’-methylene bisacrylamide (MBA), or N, N-dimethyl acrylamide (DMA), have enabled the preparation of semi-interpenetrating networks (semi-IPNs) hydrogel and interpenetrating polymer networks (IPNs) hydrogel with tunable drug/protein release profiles and good biocompatibility. For instance, persulfate-initiated crosslinking of itaconate with PEGDA produced superabsorbent hydrogels with superior swelling properties [122]. An EGDMA-crosslinked p(IA-co-HEMA) semi-IPN hydrogel incorporating polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) exhibited dual temperature- and pH-responsive swelling together with antimicrobial activity, and further copolymerization with chitosan generated an IPN hydrogel suitable for controlled drug delivery [123, 124]. Similarly, MBA-crosslinked p(IA-co-NIPAAm), itaconate/chitosan, and itaconate/sodium alginate hydrogels demonstrated excellent swelling, temperature/pH responsiveness, and protein release while preserving enzymatic activity. Notably, incorporation of itaconate increased acidic group density and thereby improved swelling behavior compared with acrylate-based hydrogels [125–127].

Among itaconate-derived precursors, itaconic anhydride (IAn) occupies a particularly important position because it contains both an unsaturated double bond and a reactive anhydride functionality. Under alkaline conditions, IAn can react directly with amino groups in collagen or gelatin to form amide linkages, after which FRP of the double bonds generates hydrogel networks [128–130]. Modification of gelatin with IAn has been shown to enhance mucoadhesive properties through Michael-type addition between the double bonds of the anhydride-derived moiety and thiol groups in mucin cysteine residues [130]. Building on this reactivity, poly(thioether amide) hydrogels with abundant pendant carboxyl groups have been developed via combined thiol-ene click Michael addition and amine-anhydride ring-opening reactions with cysteine, followed by integration with chitosan [131]. These examples highlight the versatility of IAn as a precursor for expanding the structural and functional diversity of directly fabricated itaconate-based hydrogels.

In general, the directly fabricated itaconate hydrogel has the advantages of relatively simple composition and highly adjustable physicochemical properties, so it has strong application potential. Its carboxyl-rich network structure can endow materials with pH-responsive swelling and controllable release ability, while polyester-based or itaconate derivative-based network systems can further achieve degradability, mechanical modulation, and bio-based network construction. Compared with traditional acrylic, methacrylic, or polysaccharide hydrogels, this strategy establishes a more direct relationship between renewable monomer chemistry and the behavior of microenvironmentally responsive materials [21, 107]. However, a key limitation of this strategy is that its mechanism of action still has some ambiguity. The covalent introduction of itaconate may help improve its structural integration and network stability in material networks, but it may also reduce the availability of free itaconate, which is typically necessary to trigger classical intracellular immune metabolic signals. Therefore, directly fabricated hydrogels are most convincingly positioned as bio-based, responsive, and relatively simple scaffolds [21]. In contrast, to further define it as a therapeutic platform based on immunometabolites, more rigorous validation is still needed, including direct comparison with similar materials without itaconate, quantitative analysis of their degradation behavior, standardized release studies, and providing clearer evidence to establish causal relationships between network chemical composition and specific biological mechanisms. In addition, the preparation method will also significantly affect the conversion application potential of such hydrogels. Physical initiation systems usually have the advantages of relatively clean initiation conditions and easy regulation of the gel process, but may rely on special equipment, precise irradiation parameter control, or formulation systems with good photocompatibility [119, 120]. Chemical initiation systems are usually easier to implement and have greater potential for large-scale preparation, but strict evaluation of residual initiators, crosslinking agents, and unreacted monomers is necessary to ensure their cell compatibility and long-term safety [115, 124]. These factors are particularly important for clinical transformation, because the application performance of hydrogels not only depends on their swelling characteristics or release behavior, but also is jointly affected by preparation repeatability, sterilization suitability, and regulatory acceptability. From the perspective of transformation application, the directly fabricated itaconate hydrogels may be more suitable for application scenarios that give priority to local microenvironment response, scaffold stability, degradability, and preparation simplicity, but not for systems that take the precise intracellular delivery of itaconate derivatives as the primary goal.

Carrier-based hydrogels loaded with itaconate or itaconate derivatives

The second major design mode uses hydrogels as local delivery systems for itaconate or its bioactive derivatives, rather than as the primary structural basis of the network. In this context, therapeutic efficacy depends predominantly on the encapsulation, retention, and controlled release of active species such as 4-OI and DMI, followed by their intracellular action after local administration [26, 108]. This strategy is particularly relevant because many of the anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and cytoprotective effects of itaconate derivatives depend on intracellular activity, which is not necessarily retained when itaconate is covalently incorporated into a polymer network. Therefore, carrier-based hydrogels provide a direct means of translating the pharmacology of itaconate derivatives into biomaterial-based therapy.

A principal feature of this strategy is the combination of local hydrogel-mediated retention with the biological activity of membrane-permeable derivatives. Sustained release can increase local bioavailability, extend residence time at diseased sites, and limit systemic exposure, thereby improving therapeutic selectivity in inflammatory and degenerative disorders. This rationale has been widely applied in diabetic wound healing, osteoarthritis, osteochondral repair, bone regeneration, intervertebral disc degeneration, and related tissue-repair settings characterized by oxidative stress, persistent inflammation, and immune dysregulation. In these systems, DMI- or 4-OI-loaded hydrogels have been developed to modulate macrophage polarization, suppress inflammatory cytokine production, attenuate oxidative injury, and promote tissue remodeling [26, 108, 132–134]. Compared with directly fabricated itaconate hydrogels, carrier-based hydrogels have a clearer pharmacological rationale because therapeutic effects can be attributed more directly to released itaconate derivatives. This advantage makes them particularly suitable for disease settings requiring intracellular regulation of inflammatory signaling, redox balance, mitochondrial function, and macrophage phenotype [5, 26]. However, this strategy also transfers the main design challenge from network construction to release engineering. The hydrogel must provide sufficient retention to avoid rapid clearance, but also permit release at a rate that maintains therapeutically relevant intracellular exposure. Excessively rapid release may lead to burst dosing and potential local toxicity, whereas overly slow release may fail to achieve effective concentrations in target cells [116, 133]. Therefore, the therapeutic success of this design mode depends on matching the release kinetics of 4-OI or DMI with the temporal dynamics of the target disease microenvironment. This design mode is also compatible with a broad range of advanced hydrogel formats. Depending on the therapeutic goal, hydrogel carrier systems have been engineered with multifunctional properties, including injectability, tissue adhesion, self-healing, nanocomposite reinforcement, and stimuli-responsiveness, to optimize drug release kinetics and enhance local retention [135–138]. In many cases, DMI or 4-OI is combined with auxiliary components, including nanoparticles, exosomes, metal-organic frameworks (MOFs), nanovesicles, black phosphorus, and other bioactive elements, to further enhance regenerative, antibacterial, or antioxidant performance. Such combinations have substantially expanded the functional scope of carrier-based hydrogels and account for many of the most effective formulations reported to date. At the same time, increased compositional complexity complicates mechanistic interpretation, since the observed therapeutic effects may derive not only from the released itaconate derivative, but also from synergistic or even dominant contributions of secondary components [30, 98, 108, 134, 139].

This trade-off is particularly important when evaluating translational potential. Simple carrier-based hydrogels are generally easier to manufacture, sterilize, characterize, and regulate, but they may offer limited control over intracellular delivery or disease-responsive release. In contrast, nano-enabled or multicomponent carrier-based hydrogels can improve spatiotemporal control, cell-specific exposure, and microenvironment-responsive release, but they also increase the burden of quality control, batch-to-batch reproducibility, component-level safety assessment, and regulatory classification [98, 108, 134]. Thus, the most sophisticated formulation is not necessarily the most translatable one. For clinical translation, the added value of each functional component should be demonstrated through well-controlled comparisons with simpler formulations. Despite their therapeutic promise, carrier-based hydrogels still face several unresolved challenges, including burst release, limited control over the effective dose, uncertain long-term release behavior, and difficulty distinguishing cargo-specific effects from carrier-mediated effects [140]. Compared with conventional hydrogels loaded with anti-inflammatory drugs, antibiotics, antioxidants, or growth factors, the potential advantage of itaconate-derivative delivery lies in localized immunometabolic reprogramming rather than simple symptomatic suppression. After release, membrane-permeable derivatives such as 4-OI and DMI can act on macrophages, endothelial cells, and other target cells to regulate oxidative stress, inflammatory signaling, and mitochondrial function. This mechanism supports the positioning of itaconate-based hydrogels as bioactive and immunomodulatory hydrogel platforms [26, 30, 73]. Nano-enabled delivery strategies, including nanoscale carriers, nanovesicles, MOFs, nanogels, and nanocomposite networks, may further enhance cell-specific exposure and microenvironment-responsive release [30, 98, 134, 139]. However, because DMI and 4-OI differ from native itaconate in membrane permeability, electrophilicity, intracellular processing, and potential off-target effects, their optimal release profiles and therapeutic windows are unlikely to be identical across disease settings [28]. Therefore, future development of carrier-based hydrogels requires more rigorous release engineering, clearer definition of cargo-specific mechanisms, reduced formulation complexity where possible, and systematic evaluation of the contribution of each functional component. At present, carrier-based hydrogels represent one of the most therapeutically advanced branches of itaconate-based hydrogel research. However, these hydrogels should not be regarded merely as systems for small-molecule delivery. Rather, they are pharmacologically informed biomaterial platforms whose design must be matched to both the properties of the encapsulated itaconate derivative and the pathological features of the target microenvironment.

Structure-property-function relationships and design considerations for biomedical applications

Although the two principal design strategies for itaconate-based hydrogels differ fundamentally in how itaconate-derived functionality is incorporated, their biological performance can be interpreted within a shared structure-property-function framework. This framework comprises four interdependent design variables, namely hydrogel composition, the mode of itaconate incorporation, network architecture, and release behavior (Table 2). Together, these variables form a sequential yet closely coupled relationship in which molecular composition establishes the physicochemical basis of the hydrogel, incorporation mode determines whether itaconate functions primarily as a structural motif or as a pharmacologically available bioactive species, network architecture regulates mechanical performance and mass transport, and release behavior ultimately defines the magnitude, duration, and spatial distribution of biological exposure [11, 21, 26, 30, 95, 133, 141].

Major design variables governing the structure-property-function relationships of itaconate-based hydrogels

Hydrogel composition establishes the molecular basis of material performance. The type and proportion of itaconate-containing monomers or derivatives, polymers, crosslinkers, and auxiliary components collectively determine hydrophilicity, carboxylate density, surface charge, swelling, degradation, and interactions with proteins and cells. In directly fabricated hydrogels, the carboxylic acid groups of itaconate can enhance water affinity, ionization, pH responsiveness, and electrostatic interactions with charged molecules. However, these effects depend not only on the nominal itaconate content but also on the fraction and chemical state of carboxyl groups retained after polymerization or modification. Therefore, effective dicarboxylate density, rather than monomer feed ratio alone, should be considered when interpreting biological performance [112, 141–143]. The mode of itaconate incorporation determines whether itaconate acts mainly as a structural component or as a pharmacologically available bioactive species. In directly fabricated systems, itaconate-containing units may contribute to network formation, degradation, cargo immobilization, and microenvironmental responsiveness, with biological effects arising largely from changes in material properties. By contrast, carrier-based hydrogels deliver free itaconate or derivatives such as 4-OI and DMI through physical encapsulation, cleavable conjugation, or incorporation into nanoparticles, nanovesicles, or MOFs. In these systems, biological activity depends on release, cellular uptake, intracellular availability, and molecular reactivity. Thus, the presence of an itaconate-related moiety within a hydrogel does not necessarily indicate pharmacologically relevant intracellular exposure [21, 30, 95, 132, 144, 145]. Network architecture further translates molecular composition into mechanical and transport properties. Crosslinking density, pore structure, mesh size, polymer-chain mobility, and reversible interactions collectively regulate stiffness, injectability, self-healing, tissue adhesion, degradation, and cargo retention. Loosely crosslinked networks generally favor swelling, diffusion, and cell infiltration but may show limited mechanical stability, whereas densely crosslinked networks improve structural integrity and residence time at the cost of slower transport and release. In carrier-based hydrogels, the relationship between mesh size and carrier dimensions is particularly important because excessive confinement may improve retention while limiting carrier disassembly and cell-carrier interactions [21, 124, 141, 146]. Release behavior provides the final connection between hydrogel design and biological response. Therapeutic performance depends not only on the total amount of itaconate incorporated but also on its local concentration-time profile. Release may be governed by diffusion, degradation, bond cleavage, swelling, ion exchange, or carrier disassembly. Rapid burst release may produce an immediate pharmacological effect but can shorten the therapeutic window, increase the risk of local toxicity, and diminish the benefits of hydrogel-mediated delivery. Sustained release can prolong local immunomodulatory activity and reduce dosing frequency, whereas excessively slow release may fail to achieve therapeutically effective intracellular concentrations [30, 119, 133]. Stimuli-responsive release can improve spatiotemporal control, although the contribution of the carrier or responsive matrix should be distinguished from that of itaconate itself [147, 148]. Because these four variables are closely coupled, optimizing one parameter often produces trade-offs in others. Increasing itaconate content may enhance hydrophilicity, pH-responsive swelling, ionic binding, and drug-loading capacity, but excessive incorporation can disrupt polymerization, reduce network homogeneity, weaken mechanical integrity, accelerate degradation, or promote burst release [111, 112, 143]. Likewise, increasing crosslinking density generally improves mechanical stability and residence time but reduces mesh size, molecular diffusion, cargo release, and cell infiltration. Faster degradation may facilitate cargo liberation and tissue integration, yet premature network breakdown can compromise structural support and cause uncontrolled exposure [145, 149]. Therefore, the optimal formulation should not maximize any single property. Instead, it should balance chemical functionality, incorporation efficiency, mechanical stability, degradation, mass transport, and biological exposure according to the intended therapeutic application. Notably, the structure-property-function relationships become more difficult to interpret when additional functional carriers or bioactive components are incorporated into the hydrogel. Although exosomes, MOFs, black phosphorus, metal ions, nanoparticles, and other components may enhance antibacterial, antioxidant, photothermal, angiogenic, or regenerative performance, the resulting therapeutic effects may reflect additive or synergistic interactions among the hydrogel matrix, itaconate derivative, carrier, and auxiliary cargo [97, 98, 108, 132, 150]. Consequently, the biological response of a multicomponent system should not be attributed to itaconate alone. Mechanistic evaluation should include an itaconate-free hydrogel with comparable physicochemical properties, an equivalent dose of free itaconate or its derivative, an empty carrier-loaded hydrogel, and the complete composite formulation. Key parameters, including effective itaconate content, retained dicarboxylate density, loading efficiency, crosslinking density, degradation rate, mechanical properties, release kinetics, cellular uptake, and local tissue exposure, should then be correlated with relevant biological outcomes to establish robust structure-function relationships.

This structure-property-function framework also clarifies the relative advantages of the two principal design strategies. Directly fabricated itaconate-based hydrogels are most suitable when therapeutic performance depends on intrinsic network properties, such as pH responsiveness, swelling, tissue adhesion, barrier formation, mechanical support, or controlled degradation. They are therefore attractive for wound dressings, gastrointestinal delivery, and tissue scaffolds in which matrix-level functions are central [21, 107, 142, 151]. However, they may be less suitable for applications requiring precise intracellular delivery because covalently incorporated itaconate units may not generate pharmacologically relevant intracellular exposure. By contrast, carrier-loaded hydrogels are preferable when sustained local delivery of bioactive derivatives such as 4-OI or DMI is the primary objective. Their advantages include improved local retention, cargo protection, reduced systemic exposure, and the possibility of stimulus-responsive release, particularly in macrophage-rich inflammatory environments [22, 26]. These benefits must nevertheless be balanced against potential burst release, variable loading, carrier-hydrogel interactions, formulation complexity, and uncertainty regarding long-term in vivo fate. The optimal design is application-dependent. Gastrointestinal systems benefit from preserved ionizable dicarboxyl groups and pH-responsive swelling, whereas tumor-responsive systems often rely on combined effects of acidic pH, carrier destabilization, and photothermal or multicomponent interactions [142, 148]. For immunomodulatory tissue repair, sustained local exposure to bioactive derivatives is generally more important than maximizing swelling [133]. In anti-infective wound healing, antibacterial activity must be balanced with exudate management, cytocompatibility, inflammation resolution, angiogenesis, and tissue regeneration [95]. Thus, directly fabricated and carrier-loaded hydrogels should be regarded as complementary rather than interchangeable platforms.

From a translational perspective, itaconate-based hydrogels must demonstrate advantages over established platforms under comparable conditions. Directly fabricated systems should be benchmarked against non-itaconate hydrogels matched for charge density, swelling, crosslinking, degradation, and mechanical properties [112, 143, 145]. Carrier-loaded systems should be compared with hydrogels delivering conventional anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, antibacterial, or regenerative agents using equivalent models, doses, release profiles, and endpoints. Pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic analyses are also needed to distinguish itaconate-specific activity from the general benefits of local retention and controlled delivery [98, 133, 134, 150]. The distinctive potential of itaconate-based hydrogels lies in the possibility of coordinating material engineering with host-directed immunometabolic regulation [5, 11]. Unlike conventional hydrogels that function mainly as passive depots for exogenous drugs, these systems can integrate network chemistry, microenvironment responsiveness, controlled release, and the intrinsic biological activity of itaconate derivatives. When these elements are rationally matched, the resulting therapeutic platform may provide localized and sustained regulation of inflammatory signaling, redox homeostasis, mitochondrial function, macrophage polarization, and infection-associated metabolic responses [26, 30, 142, 152, 153]. Therefore, their relevance to nanobiotechnology extends beyond the simple addition of nanoparticles and instead lies in the rational integration of nanoscale carriers, nanostructured transport pathways, responsive interfaces, and immunometabolically active hydrogel networks within a unified therapeutic system.

Biomedical applications of itaconate-based hydrogels

The two major design strategies discussed above provide a conceptual framework for understanding the biomedical applications of itaconate-based hydrogels. Directly fabricated hydrogels are mainly characterized by their intrinsic network functionality, physicochemical responsiveness, and structural simplicity, whereas carrier-based hydrogels are primarily designed to enable the controlled release of bioactive itaconate derivatives and to amplify their post-release pharmacological effects [21, 30]. These two strategies should not be regarded as mutually exclusive application categories, but rather as complementary design approaches that can be tailored to specific therapeutic needs and pathological microenvironments. Accordingly, the biomedical value of itaconate-based hydrogels lies in their capacity to integrate material properties, immunometabolic activity, and release behavior within a single therapeutic platform [11, 12]. Unlike conventional immunomodulatory hydrogels, which mainly serve as reservoirs for exogenous bioactive agents such as cytokines, growth factors, antioxidants, antibiotics, or metal ions, itaconate-based hydrogels exploit the inherent immunomodulatory activity of itaconate derivatives to achieve host-directed immunometabolic intervention [5, 30, 75, 95]. Through rational material design, these systems may modulate NRF2 signaling, inflammatory responses, reactive oxygen species (ROS) homeostasis, mitochondrial function, macrophage polarization, and infection-associated metabolic pathways [26, 96, 150, 154]. Furthermore, the incorporation of nanoscale carriers, including nanogels, MOFs, nanovesicles, exosomes, and nanocomposite networks, may expand their ability to regulate the spatiotemporal availability of itaconate derivatives and optimize therapeutic outcomes [30, 97, 150, 153]. Collectively, these features position itaconate-based hydrogels as emerging bioactive and immunomodulatory hydrogel platforms with potential relevance to nano-enabled therapeutic delivery. Gastrointestinal and colon-targeted delivery

In gastrointestinal and colon-targeted delivery systems, itaconate often serves as a principal pH-responsive building block, and its dicarboxylic groups are largely retained within the hydrogel network. Under acidic gastric conditions (e.g., pH 1.2), protonation of the carboxyl groups reduces charge density and weakens electrostatic repulsion between polymer chains, thereby limiting swelling and suppressing premature drug release. By contrast, under neutral or mildly alkaline intestinal conditions (e.g., pH 7.4), deprotonation generates negatively charged carboxylate groups, increases osmotic pressure and interchain repulsion, and promotes network expansion and drug diffusion. This ionization-dependent behavior provides the physicochemical basis for the use of itaconate-rich hydrogels as oral and colon-targeted delivery platforms. Consistent with this mechanism, several itaconate-based hydrogels have been developed for gastrointestinal delivery. A persulfate-initiated, MBA-crosslinked p(IA-co-AAM) nanogel showed 10-fold higher swelling and 3-fold greater doxorubicin release in simulated intestinal fluid (pH 7.4) than in gastric fluid (pH 1.2) [142]. A hyaluronic acid-grafted p(IA-co-AAM) nanogel similarly protected acyclovir under gastric conditions and enabled sustained release in colonic fluid, thereby improving bioavailability and prolonging half-life [157]. Three semi-IPN hydrogels incorporating carbopol, aloe vera, or sterculia gum into p(IA-co-AAM) further improved colon-targeted delivery by combining pH-responsive swelling with enhanced mechanical and mucoadhesive properties [141, 152, 158]. A semi-IPN hydrogel based on microcrystalline cellulose and itaconate likewise exhibited greater swelling under alkaline conditions and was proposed as an oral insulin carrier [146]. Additionally, an MBA-crosslinked hydrogel prepared from itaconate, HEMA, and PEGMMA protected ursolic acid in gastric fluid while allowing rapid intestinal release [159]. TEGDMA-crosslinked hydrogels based on itaconate with PEGMMA or NVP, as well as EGDMA-crosslinked hydrogels fabricated from itaconate, methacrylic acid (MAA), and PVA, were also developed as oral delivery platforms for drugs and proteins, reducing premature leakage and enzymatic degradation in the stomach while promoting intestinal release [23, 145, 160]. Moreover, a DMA-crosslinked hydrogel synthesized from itaconate and double bond-modified vanillin showed higher cumulative release of 5-fluorouracil in simulated intestinal fluid, while increasing itaconate content enlarged pore size and swelling capacity but reduced mechanical strength [143]. Notably, itaconate-based nanocomposite systems have been developed to prolong release and improve sustained delivery, including grafted starch-, carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC)-, or pectin-based hydrogels containing ZnO/cellulose nanofibers, Cloisite 30B, or multiwall carbon nanotubes. Collectively, these studies indicate that preservation of itaconate as a major structural component with intact dicarboxylic functionality typically yields the desired pH-responsive release profile for oral and colon-targeted administration, characterized by low release in acid and high release at neutral/alkaline [147, 161, 162].

Tumor-responsive drug delivery

However, this pH-responsive trend is not universal across all itaconate-based delivery systems. In tumor-targeted formulations, the release behavior often reflects the integrated response of multicomponent networks, in which itaconate may no longer be the dominant ionizable motif. In many such systems, itaconate is introduced in esterified, copolymerized, or otherwise chemically transformed forms, and its dicarboxylic groups may be partially consumed or structurally subordinated within the overall network. As a result, pH-triggered release is frequently governed by the collective behavior of all acid-responsive components, such as polyhistidine, tertiary amine-containing polymers, chitosan, or other protonatable segments, rather than by itaconate alone. This distinction is particularly evident in chemotherapeutic delivery. A carrier composed of polyitaconate, PEG, folic acid, and polyhistidine showed greater doxorubicin release at pH 5.0 than at pH 7.4 and was efficiently internalized by HeLa cells through folate receptor-mediated endocytosis, thereby enhancing antitumor activity [163]. To reduce doxorubicin-associated toxicity, methoxy PEG-poly((2-dimethylamino) ethyl methacrylate-co-itaconate)-modified gelatin nanoparticles were developed for the co-delivery of doxorubicin and betaine, resulting in reduced toxicity together with enhanced apoptotic effects in breast cancer cells [164]. In another example, a dual thermo-/pH-sensitive hydrogel based on p(IA-co-NIPAAm), chitosan, and glycerophosphate was used for 5-fluorouracil delivery and significantly inhibited proliferation while promoting apoptosis in triple-negative breast cancer models [148]. Similarly, polymeric nanocapsules prepared from p(IAn-co-NVP) and chitosan via interfacial condensation exhibited high 5-fluorouracil loading and controlled release, leading to enhanced apoptosis in A549 lung cancer cells [165]. These studies suggest that acidic release in tumor-oriented systems often arises from cooperative protonation, network relaxation, or carrier destabilization within hybrid formulations designed for the acidic tumor microenvironment, rather than being simplistically attributed to the intrinsic dicarboxylic chemistry of itaconate.

Anti-infective delivery systems

Itaconate-based hydrogels have also been investigated as anti-infective delivery platforms because they combine drug-loading capacity with tunable release behavior and, in some cases, intrinsic antibacterial contributions. Hydrogels prepared by grafting itaconate and lactic acid onto CMC or tragacanth gum via microwave irradiation showed stronger activity against Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) than Escherichia coli (E. coli) and enabled rapid amoxicillin release under acidic conditions, indicating potential for gastric anti-infective delivery [121, 166, 167]. Using persulfate initiation and MBA crosslinking, itaconate-modified tragacanth gum nanohydrogels were developed for controlled ampicillin release with enhanced antibacterial activity against E. coli, while porous and biocompatible p(IA-co-AAM)-grafted chitosan or CMC hydrogels were also prepared as promising drug-delivery matrices [168–170]. ZnO nanoparticle-incorporated itaconate-based nanocomposite hydrogels fabricated either from itaconate, NVP, and chitosan via γ-ray irradiation or from polyitaconate-grafted chitosan via CAN initiation enabled controlled release of amoxicillin or ciprofloxacin while exhibiting enhanced broad-spectrum antibacterial activity against S. aureus, E. coli, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (P. aeruginosa), highlighting their potential as dual-functional platforms for antibacterial therapy and drug delivery [171, 172]. Beyond polymeric systems, a chromium-based itaconate-functionalized metal-organic framework (Cr-IA MOF) served as a sustained-release carrier for ibuprofen, amoxicillin, and metronidazole, and the metronidazole-loaded formulation showed synergistic antibacterial activity against both S. aureus and E. coli [153]. These examples broaden the scope of itaconate-based delivery systems, but also indicate that antibacterial performance often derives from multiple factors, including co-loaded drugs, inorganic additives, and matrix composition, rather than from itaconate itself alone.

Wound-associated local release platforms

Itaconate-based delivery systems have further been extended to wound-associated applications, where local retention and stimulus-responsive release are especially valuable. An epichlorohydrin-crosslinked itaconate-modified starch hydrogel containing Fe3O4 magnetic nanoparticles enabled dual pH/magnetic-responsive release of guaifenesin and promoted wound repair [173]. Another nanocomposite hydrogel based on p(IA-co-NVP), hyaluronic acid, and PVA supported sustained release of curcumin-loaded liposomes and significantly improved wound healing [174]. These findings suggest that itaconate-based hydrogels can function not only as passive reservoirs but also as adaptable local delivery matrices whose responsiveness can be tailored through compositional integration with bioactive polymers and nanoparticles.

Despite the promise of itaconate-based hydrogels for oral, tumor-responsive, anti-infective, and wound-associated delivery, their design advantages should be interpreted in relation to established pH-responsive and local-release hydrogel platforms. Compared with conventional carriers such as poly(acrylic acid), MAA copolymers, chitosan, alginate, or PEG-based networks, itaconate-based hydrogels may offer additional value through their dicarboxylate-rich chemistry, tunable ionization behavior, and potential integration of bio-based responsiveness with local therapeutic delivery [112, 147]. However, this advantage is not universal. Burst release remains a recurring problem in many pH-responsive systems, compromising site specificity and long-term efficacy [140]. Moreover, enhanced swelling and enlarged pore size often improve drug diffusion but may simultaneously weaken mechanical integrity, as illustrated by formulations in which increased itaconate content promoted swelling at the expense of structural strength [143]. The contrasting pH-responsive behaviors observed in gastrointestinal and tumor-targeted systems further indicate that hydrogel performance cannot be inferred from the presence of itaconate alone. Instead, it depends on the chemical state of itaconate, retention of dicarboxylic groups, protonatable co-monomers, crosslinking density, network architecture, and the dominant structural role of each component [142, 163]. Therefore, future delivery studies should move beyond release-profile description and include release modeling, non-itaconate control hydrogels, mechanical benchmarking before and after swelling, cytocompatibility assessment under disease-relevant exposure windows, and direct comparison with mature pH-responsive carriers. Such benchmarking is essential to determine whether itaconate chemistry provides a true design advantage or merely reproduces functions already achievable with established hydrogel systems.

Immunomodulatory hydrogels for inflammatory diseases and tissue repair

Itaconate-based hydrogels have emerged as active immunomodulatory biomaterials for inflammatory diseases and tissue repair. Beyond serving as passive delivery carriers, these hydrogels can reshape pathological microenvironments by releasing itaconate derivatives or integrating itaconate-containing functional networks, thereby attenuating oxidative stress, modulating macrophage polarization, and supporting tissue regeneration. This design is particularly relevant to diseases characterized by persistent inflammation, ROS accumulation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and impaired healing. Cardiovascular and soft tissue repair

In cardiovascular diseases, itaconate acts as an inflammatory mediator and therapeutic target, regulating oxidative stress, inflammation, and abnormal glycolysis to offer promising therapeutic potential [175, 176]. Itaconate-based hydrogels and scaffolds are primarily designed to suppress post-infarction inflammation while improving cell survival and regenerative signaling. DMI-loaded PCL nanofibers promoted myocardial repair by reprogramming macrophage polarization, reducing inflammation, and improving ventricular function after infarction [154]. Similarly, a hydrophilic p(IA-co-NIPAAm) hydrogel enhanced cardiac stromal cell proliferation, spheroid formation, and growth factor secretion, and in combination with stromal cells promoted angiogenesis and reduced apoptosis in myocardial infarction models [177]. Beyond inflammatory regulation, itaconate-based hydrogels have also been used as cell-instructive substrates. Laminin-derived peptides grafted onto itaconate-PVA hydrogels supported pluripotent stem cell adhesion, proliferation, and cardiomyocyte differentiation, while PEG nanosegments incorporation further improved peptide efficiency and stem cell adhesion and proliferation [151, 178]. Together, these studies suggest that in cardiovascular applications, itaconate-based hydrogels can function both as local immunoregulatory carriers and as regenerative matrices that enhance cell-material interactions.

Chronic inflammatory wound healing

Chronic inflammatory wounds, especially diabetic wounds, are characterized by the pathological coexistence of hyperglycemia, hypoxia, ROS overproduction, chronic inflammation, and vascular impairment, making them a primary application target for 4-OI-loaded hydrogels [179]. 4-OI-loaded hydrogels enable local retention of the therapeutic agent while amplifying its anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects within this complex wound microenvironment. For example, a self-healing Ag-thiol coordinated hydrogel loaded with 4-OI activated the KEAP1/NRF2 pathway, protected mitochondria, suppressed bacterial growth and inflammation, promoted neovascularization, and accelerated diabetic wound closure [22]. 4-OI-loaded electrospun membranes similarly improved diabetic wound healing by modulating macrophage polarization, enhancing angiogenesis, and promoting re-epithelialization [180]. More complex hybrid systems have further expanded this strategy. Methacrylated gelatin (Gel-MA)-based hydrogels incorporating 4-OI-modified black phosphorus nanosheets showed combined antibacterial, antioxidant, and pro-angiogenic activity [108]. Meanwhile, Gel-MA-based hydrogels loaded with 4-OI-containing hybrid nanovesicles improved delivery to both M1 macrophages and endothelial cells, thereby enhancing immune regulation and vascular repair. The combined therapeutic effects significantly improve diabetic wound repair and regeneration, potentially reducing diabetes-related amputation and mortality rates [30]. Notably, endothelial cell damage and ferroptosis have been identified as key factors impairing diabetic wound healing [181]. 4-OI effectively mitigates high glucose-induced endothelial oxidative stress and ferroptosis by regulating the KEAP1/NRF2/GPX4 signaling pathway. When encapsulated in exosomes and incorporated into hydrogels, 4-OI significantly accelerates diabetic foot ulcer healing and improves healing quality by suppressing glucose-induced endothelial ferroptosis [139]. Beyond diabetic wounds, a dextran-PEG hydrogel carrying 4-OI-loaded micelles reduced peritoneal fibrosis and prevented postoperative adhesions by regulating macrophage function and mesothelial differentiation [182]. Likewise, a hydrogel containing 4-OI-loaded nanostructured lipid carriers alleviated atopic dermatitis by reducing inflammation, oxidative stress, and barrier dysfunction [183]. Overall, these studies highlight the versatility of hydrogel-assisted delivery of itaconate derivatives in soft tissue disorders. However, their efficacy often reflects the synergy between local retention, carrier design, and auxiliary functional components, rather than the action of 4-OI alone.

Osteoarticular and bone regeneration

Osteoarthritis, a chronic degenerative joint disease, has seen a rising global incidence due to population aging and increasing obesity rates. In 2020, approximately 595 million people worldwide were affected by osteoarthritis, making it a significant public health burden [184]. Osteoarthritis and osteochondral injury are important targets for itaconate-based hydrogels, as these disorders are characterized by chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, metabolic dysregulation, and progressive matrix degeneration [149, 185, 186]. In this context, the value of itaconate-based hydrogels lies not simply in drug encapsulation but in their ability to enhance local retention and provide sustained immunometabolic regulation within the joint microenvironment. For example, a pH-responsive MOF constructed from zinc ions, 2-methylimidazole, and itaconate, and subsequently encapsulated in Gel-MA hydrogel microspheres, effectively attenuated osteoarthritis progression by improving intra-articular bioavailability and delivering anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects to chondrocytes [132]. Likewise, a zinc-based supercontainer loaded with 4-OI showed marked therapeutic efficacy in acute arthritis models by scavenging excessive ROS and modulating mitochondrial metabolism in synovial macrophages [187]. To further improve the delivery efficiency of 4-OI, sustained-release systems have been developed through both hydrogel encapsulation and covalent conjugation strategies. For instance, alginate-based hydrogel incorporating 4-OI exhibited significant anti-inflammatory and cartilage-protective effects in osteoarthritis models [133]. In another approach, covalent grafting of 4-OI onto chitosan followed by hydrogel formation enabled sustained release and promoted macrophage polarization from the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype toward the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, resulting in pronounced inflammation resolution [188].

This immunoregulatory strategy has also been extended to osteochondral regeneration, where inflammation control must be integrated with structural and metabolic support. A hydrogel prepared from itaconate, glycerin, and succinate promoted ECM synthesis, reduced ROS, preserved mitochondrial morphology, and enhanced cartilage repair in a rabbit defect model [113]. A bone adhesive fabricated by photopolymerizing hydroxyapatite with a polyitaconate ester synthesized from itaconate, citric acid, 1,3-propanediol, and 1,12-dodecanedicarboxylic acid exhibits excellent adhesive properties and bone repair induction, demonstrating significant potential for bone repair applications [189]. More advanced systems have incorporated DMI- or 4-OI-based immune regulation into mechanically functional scaffolds. An engineered hierarchical hydrogel containing DMI-loaded zwitterionic nanogels combined immune-responsive release with targeted mitochondrial transfer. Its osteogenic-range stiffness (Young’s modulus, 27.81 kPa) provided a favorable ECM-like microenvironment for bone repair. By releasing DMI under inflammatory conditions and promoting mitochondrial transfer from macrophages to bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs), the hydrogel reduced ROS, enhanced ATP production and oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) activity, and synergistically promoted bone regeneration [26]. Similarly, 4-OI-loaded hydrogels or scaffolds have been used to enhance regenerative microenvironments for cartilage and bone repair by suppressing inflammation and promoting macrophage polarization [190, 191]. A nanocomposite hydrogel was constructed from acrylate-modified β-cyclodextrin and gelatin and co-loaded with 4-OI and copper covalent organic frameworks. It achieved coordinated immunomodulatory and pro-regenerative effects through rapid 4-OI release and sustained Cu²⁺ delivery. This dual-release pattern promoted M2 macrophage polarization, BMSC osteogenic differentiation, and endothelial angiogenesis, ultimately significantly improving fracture healing [192]. In diabetic infected bone defects, multifunctional hydrogels loaded with 4-OI and exosomes reduced ROS damage, protected mitochondrial function, provided antibacterial activity, and promoted neovascularization and osteogenesis [150]. Notably, related concepts have also been applied to intervertebral disc degeneration, where an itaconate-loaded MOF embedded in hyaluronic acid hydrogel enabled sustained release under acidic conditions and delayed disc degeneration in vivo [134]. Collectively, these studies indicate that in osteochondral repair, itaconate-based hydrogels must satisfy dual requirements, including immunometabolic control of inflammation and effective support for mechanically demanding regenerative processes.

In immunomodulatory applications, itaconate-based hydrogels may offer a distinctive advantage by enabling localized immunometabolic intervention in macrophage-rich pathological environments, such as diabetic wounds, osteoarthritis, and osteochondral defects. Compared with conventional immunomodulatory hydrogels that mainly deliver anti-inflammatory drugs, cytokines, antioxidants, or growth factors, itaconate-based systems are potentially more mechanism-directed because they target interconnected metabolic and redox pathways involved in inflammation resolution, mitochondrial protection, macrophage polarization, and tissue regeneration. This feature may make them particularly suitable for chronic inflammatory microenvironments in which inflammation, oxidative stress, and impaired repair coexist [26, 30, 132, 188, 189]. Nevertheless, the translational significance of this advantage remains difficult to evaluate because the strongest reported effects often occur in hybrid systems containing exosomes, nanoparticles, MOFs, bioactive ions, or other regenerative components. In such multicomponent formulations, the dominant therapeutic effect may arise from 4-OI/DMI, the hydrogel carrier, the auxiliary component, or their combined action. This creates a major interpretive limitation and makes direct comparison with existing immunomodulatory hydrogel systems essential [97, 98, 132, 139]. Therefore, future studies should incorporate factorial formulations, matched unloaded carriers, non-itaconate analogues, dose-response analysis, pathway-specific validation, and head-to-head comparison with clinically or experimentally mature immunomodulatory hydrogels. In addition, long-term biosafety, degradation-product fate, disease-specific dosing windows, reproducible manufacturing, and sterilization compatibility must be systematically evaluated before these systems can be considered clinically mature.

Anti-infective and wound-healing applications

Bacterial infection remains a major cause of mortality and healthcare burden worldwide, and the misuse of antibiotics has further accelerated antimicrobial resistance, particularly in post-surgical, trauma-related, and chronic wound infections. In this context, antibacterial hydrogels have attracted considerable interest because they can combine local infection control with defect coverage, exudate management, and tissue repair while reducing reliance on systemic antibiotics [193, 194]. Itaconate-based hydrogels are particularly promising in this field because their chemical versatility enables tunable mechanical properties, controlled release, and multiple modes of antimicrobial functionalization (Table 5).

Intrinsically antibacterial itaconate-based hydrogels

One important strategy is the direct construction of intrinsically antibacterial itaconate-based polymers or hydrogels. For example, a hydrogel derived from alkynyl-modified itaconate, DMI, and an azide-bearing thiazole monomer was synthesized through FRcP and click chemistry to yield a copolymer with tunable cationic charge density, which showed pronounced antibacterial activity against Gram-positive bacteria, including S. aureus and S. epidermidis, while maintaining negligible hemotoxicity and good biocompatibility [195]. Similarly, IAn-modified collagen hydrogels exhibited enhanced antibacterial efficacy against S. aureus [129]. Although many itaconate-based systems show stronger activity against Gram-positive bacteria than against Gram-negative strains, chemical modification can broaden their antibacterial spectrum. For instance, quaternized thiazole-functionalized polyitaconate retained efficacy against S. aureus while extending activity to E. coli and P. aeruginosa [196]. These results indicate that the antimicrobial activity of itaconate-based hydrogels depends not only on the itaconate backbone itself, but also on the incorporation of cationic or membrane-active moieties that strengthen bacterial targeting.

Functionalized itaconate-based anti-infective systems

A second major design strategy is to combine itaconate-based matrices with metal ions, nanoparticles, or external stimuli to achieve broader-spectrum antimicrobial effects. Copper (II)-functionalized p(IA-co-AA) nanohydrogels displayed strong antibacterial activity against multiple clinically relevant dental pathogens while maintaining favorable cytocompatibility [97]. Mechanistically, p(IA-co-AA) can facilitate the valence transition of copper from Cu2+ to Cu+, thereby increasing hydroxyl radical generation and enhancing antibacterial efficacy [197, 198]. Likewise, p(IA-co-HEA) hydrogels have been used as controlled-release carriers for silver, copper, and zinc ions, enabling selective antibacterial activity against different strains [199]. Incorporation of silver or gold nanoparticles further improved activity against S. aureus, Bacillus cereus (B. cereus), E. coli, and P. aeruginosa, while also enhancing antifungal efficacy against Candida albicans (C. albicans) and Aspergillus niger [98, 144, 200]. In addition, itaconate can form supramolecular metallogels with cobalt (II), copper (II), and zinc (II) ions, which exhibit notable antimicrobial activity against Gram-positive bacteria (Bacillus subtilis and Listeria monocytogenes), Gram-negative bacteria (E. coli and S. typhimurium), and fungi (C. albicans and Aspergillus flavus) [201].

Itaconate-derived materials have also been integrated into non-antibiotic antibacterial strategies. A peptide-mimicking antimicrobial polymer prepared by copolymerizing asymmetrically disubstituted itaconate with DMA has been proposed as a promising alternative to conventional antibacterial agents [202]. Moreover, photodynamic therapy (PDT) and photothermal therapy (PTT) exhibit remarkable potential for bacterial infection treatment by generating ROS and localized heat, respectively, which effectively disrupt bacterial structures with minimal risk of resistance development [203]. Notably, the combined application of PTT and PDT demonstrates synergistic antibacterial effects, as thermal-induced membrane damage enhances ROS permeation and promotes protein leakage [99]. This dual-mode approach has been successfully implemented in a methacrylated gelatin-based hydrogel incorporating 4-OI-loaded black phosphorus nanosheets, which effectively suppressed the growth of S. aureus and Escherichia coli through concurrent PTT and PDT actions [108]. Together, these studies show that the anti-infective potential of itaconate-based hydrogels often arises from cooperative design, in which the itaconate matrix functions as both a carrier and a functional platform for metal- or stimulus-assisted killing.

Infected wound dressings and regenerative hydrogels

Infected wound healing is one of the most active application areas for itaconate-based hydrogels because these materials can simultaneously suppress bacterial growth, modulate inflammation, and support tissue regeneration [204]. An unsaturated polyester synthesized from itaconate, ricinoleic acid, and 1,6-hexanediol exhibited antibacterial activity against S. aureus while promoting fibroblast adhesion and proliferation, indicating potential for wound repair [205]. More notably, a single-component polyitaconate hydrogel was prepared through stepwise esterification and subsequent photopolymerization, which effectively inhibits bacterial infection and inflammation by suppressing bacterial ICL activity and blocking IκBζ translation, while simultaneously promoting angiogenesis and collagen deposition to accelerate the healing and regeneration of methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA)-infected wounds [21]. Likewise, an itaconate-grafted Pluronic hydrogel exhibited antibacterial, antioxidant, and anti-inflammatory functions and accelerated MRSA-infected wound healing through ROS scavenging, inflammatory suppression, and enhanced angiogenesis [95]. These examples suggest that itaconate-derived wound dressings may exert therapeutic effects through both direct antibacterial action and host-directed immunomodulation.

Compared with synthetic polymers, natural polymers such as hyaluronic acid, chitosan, and gelatin have been widely applied in wound healing due to their excellent biocompatibility, biodegradability, and ease of modification [206]. Natural polymer-based wound dressings have further expanded the design space of itaconate-containing anti-infective hydrogels. An itaconate-grafted chitosan/PVA hydrogel membrane fabricated by freeze-thaw cycling showed improved antibacterial activity against both E. coli and S. aureus after vanillin incorporation [207]. A sodium alginate/phytic acid hydrogel containing a newly synthesized itaconate-based polymer and lavender essential oil also displayed enhanced antioxidant and antimicrobial properties, supporting its use as an antimicrobial wound dressing [208, 209]. In addition, p(IA-co-AAM)-grafted nanogels have been applied to natural polymer systems to improve structural integrity, pH responsiveness, swelling behavior, and antimicrobial activity for wound healing applications [157, 210, 211]. Particularly, p(IA-co-AAM)-grafted hyaluronic acid nanogels showed particularly favorable mechanical strength, adhesion, biocompatibility, and dual antimicrobial/antioxidant activity, thereby accelerating wound healing in both diabetic and non-diabetic settings through regulation of oxidative stress, inflammation, and angiogenesis [212]. Likewise, hydrogels incorporating antimicrobial itaconate-derived carbon dots have shown promise in burn wounds infected with P. aeruginosa by reducing ROS, preserving mitochondrial membrane potential, decreasing apoptosis, and promoting angiogenesis and wound closure [213].

The strong swelling behavior of many itaconate-based hydrogels further enhances their suitability for wound management, as highly swellable networks can absorb exudate, maintain a moist healing environment, and enable controlled release of therapeutic agents [214]. A pH-/salt-responsive hydrogel synthesized from itaconate, diethylene glycol, and acrylic acid (AA) showed high swelling capacity, good biocompatibility, biodegradability, and antibacterial activity against S. aureus, B. cereus, and C. albicans, highlighting its potential as a wound dressing for preventing secondary infection [215]. A γ-ray-induced p(IA-co-NIPAAm) hydrogel with thermo/pH responsiveness showed increased pore size and swelling with higher itaconate content, and incorporation of silver nanoparticles improved both mechanical strength and antibacterial activity against S. aureus and E. coli through rapid initial and sustained Ag+ release [216, 217]. Similarly, γ-ray-synthesized p(IA-co-HEMA) hydrogels exhibited dual thermo- and pH-responsive properties and functioned as effective penetration barriers against S. aureus and E. coli [218]. A pH-responsive p(IA-co-HEA) hydrogel crosslinked with EGDMA showed maximal swelling at neutral pH, favoring exudate absorption during the dynamic pH changes of wound healing, and when loaded with Zn2+ displayed significant antibacterial activity against E. coli [219]. Overall, these systems show that the value of itaconate-based wound dressings lies not only in antimicrobial delivery but also in their ability to coordinate absorbency, responsiveness, and tissue repair.

Barrier patches and other anti-infective biomaterials

Beyond cutaneous wound dressings, itaconate-derived antimicrobial materials have also been explored as barrier patches and protective biomaterials for tissue repair. A dopamine-modified azo-initiator-initiated p(IA-co-HEA) hydrogel was combined with hyaluronic acid to develop an adhesive patch with excellent skin adhesion properties, which could be utilized for the transdermal delivery of ketoprofen [220]. A bio-based polyurethane patch crosslinked from polyitaconate and chalcone thioglycerol showed antibacterial activity against E. coli and S. aureus together with favorable thermodynamic stability and biocompatibility, suggesting suitability for gastrointestinal rupture repair [100]. Related polyurethanes synthesized from itaconate, citric acid, and epichlorohydrin also exhibited broad-spectrum antibacterial and antifungal activity, while polyitaconate-based polyurethanes incorporating 4, 4’-dithiodiphenylamine showed additional advantages in reprocessability, mechanical robustness, solvent resistance, and UV stability [221, 222]. These examples indicate that the anti-infective potential of itaconate-derived materials extends beyond classical hydrogels to more durable barrier-type biomaterials, although their relevance to wound-healing hydrogels is comparatively indirect.

Itaconate-based hydrogels show considerable anti-infective potential, but their antibacterial performance and mechanisms are highly formulation-dependent. Native or polymerized itaconate systems often exhibit stronger activity against Gram-positive bacteria, whereas broad-spectrum antibacterial effects usually require additional functionalization with cationic groups, metal ions, nanoparticles, essential oil, photothermal agents, or photodynamic components [97, 108, 195, 196, 200, 209]. Compared with conventional antibacterial hydrogels, the distinctive promise of itaconate-based systems lies not merely in direct bactericidal activity, which can already be achieved by many established antimicrobial materials. Rather, their potential advantage lies in coupling infection control with host-directed inflammation resolution, ROS regulation, immune microenvironment remodeling, and tissue repair [209]. This integrated anti-infective and immunoregulatory logic is particularly relevant for chronic or infected wounds, where bacterial clearance alone is often insufficient to restore normal healing [21, 205, 212]. However, broad-spectrum antibacterial claims should be made cautiously because secondary components may dominate the antimicrobial effect, while the independent contribution of itaconate remains insufficiently defined in many formulations. Therefore, future studies should quantify the specific role of itaconate through component-deletion experiments, compare performance against clinically relevant antibacterial hydrogel controls, evaluate resistance risk and microbiome effects, and balance antimicrobial potency with cytocompatibility, angiogenesis, collagen remodeling, and scar-quality outcomes. From a translational perspective, the most promising anti-infective itaconate-based hydrogels will likely be those that demonstrate not only strong antibacterial activity but also reproducible manufacturing, acceptable safety, and clear superiority over existing wound dressings or antibacterial hydrogel platforms.

Since the introduction of glass ionomer cements (GICs) by Wilson et al. in the 1970s, these materials have become widely used due to their excellent adhesion, biocompatibility, and sustained fluoride release, particularly in dental applications such as fissure sealants, restoratives, and adhesives [223, 224]. The γ-ray-irradiated p(IA-co-AA) copolymer, when combined with fluoro-aluminosilicate glass, forms a GIC with excellent compressive strength and acid resistance, making it ideal for dental adhesives [225]. The molecular weight of p(IA-co-AA) significantly impacts the mechanical properties of GIC. To control molecular weight and viscosity, precipitation photopolymerization has been successfully used to synthesize p(IA-co-AA) copolymers with varying molecular weights [226]. Additionally, photoinitiator-induced polymerization with thioglycolic acid as a chain transfer agent produced a series of p(IA-co-AA) copolymers with different molecular weights, where the concentration of thioglycolic acid notably influenced both molecular weight and polydispersity. Importantly, the resulting GICs demonstrate tunable setting times and mechanical properties [227].

Itaconate-based hydrogel has emerged as a novel superabsorbent material due to its excellent liquid absorption and water retention properties. Under hydrothermal conditions, the superabsorbent hydrogel formed by itaconate, chitosan, and urea demonstrated remarkable absorption capacities of approximately 100 g/g for distilled water and 71 g/g for sodium chloride solution (0.01% w/v), while maintaining reversible water absorption characteristics [228]. Two novel itaconate-based crosslinkers were successfully synthesized through functional modification with either glycidyl methacrylate or epichlorohydrin. These crosslinkers were subsequently copolymerized with AA via FRP to produce two types of highly absorbent itaconate-based hydrogels [229]. Furthermore, a superabsorbent polymer was synthesized using itaconate, AA, and 2-acrylamido-2-methyl-1-propanesulfonic acid through persulfate-initiated polymerization with 1,4-butanediol as the crosslinker. This approach significantly enhanced the low absorbency under load and liquid permeability of the itaconate-based superabsorbent polymer while maintaining high gel strength and excellent biocompatibility [230].

In the realm of flexible electronics, itaconate-based materials have advanced electronic skin technology. A persulfate-initiated p(IA-co-HEA) polymer was incorporated into the collagen fiber network of natural aluminum-tanned sheepskin, creating a high-strength bionic electronic skin with superior conductivity, adhesion, antibacterial properties, and environmental stability. This electronic skin effectively monitors motion, physiological signals, and environmental conditions such as temperature and humidity [231]. Similarly, a photopolymerized p(IA-co-AA) ionic conductive elastomer, crosslinked with PEGDA and DMA, exhibits excellent mechanical strength, transparency, self-recovery, and antibacterial properties, making it a promising candidate for next-generation wearable electronics and strain sensors [232].

Critical challenges and translational outlook

Despite growing interest in itaconate-based hydrogels for a broad spectrum of biomedical applications, several critical challenges must be addressed before their translational potential can be fully realized. Current studies have substantially advanced the synthesis, functionalization, and proof-of-concept validation of these materials. However, many reports remain centered on demonstrating favorable material properties or therapeutic outcomes, while providing limited critical comparison with established hydrogel platforms. As a result, it remains difficult to determine when itaconate chemistry provides a genuine design advantage and when it simply functions as another carboxylate-containing or bioactive modifier [21, 95]. A more rigorous translational framework is therefore needed to distinguish between directly fabricated itaconate-containing hydrogel and carrier-based hydrogels loaded with itaconate derivatives. These strategies differ substantially in their mechanisms of action, controllability, safety concerns, manufacturing complexity, and regulatory feasibility. Directly fabricated hydrogels may offer compositional simplicity, pH responsiveness, and bio-based network construction, but their immunometabolic activity is often mechanistically ambiguous [24, 142]. Carrier-based hydrogels can more directly deliver active derivatives such as 4-OI or DMI, but they depend heavily on release control, intracellular bioavailability, and dose-related safety [26, 30]. Nano-enabled or hybrid systems may further improve spatiotemporal delivery and multifunctionality, but their complexity can complicate mechanism attribution, reproducibility, and clinical translation [139, 192]. Therefore, future development should move beyond descriptive performance reporting toward systematic comparison of advantages, limitations, and translational feasibility across different design strategies. In particular, unresolved structure-function relationships, incomplete understanding of degradation behavior and long-term in vivo fate, insufficient comparative benchmarking against mature hydrogel systems, limited manufacturing and quality-control evaluation, and the restricted predictive value of current preclinical models collectively hinder clinical translation (Fig. 7). In the following sections, these challenges are discussed from five perspectives.

Unresolved structure-function relationships

A fundamental challenge in the development of itaconate-based hydrogels lies in the insufficient understanding of their structure-function relationships. Although variations in monomer composition, crosslinking density, network architecture, incorporation strategy, and release behavior have been shown to affect physicochemical and biological properties, the underlying correlations remain incompletely defined. This limitation restricts the rational design of hydrogel systems with predictable performance for specific biomedical applications [111, 112, 143]. Currently, itaconate-based hydrogels can be broadly classified into two primary design strategies: hydrogels directly fabricated from itaconate or its analogues, and carrier-based hydrogels loaded with itaconate or itaconate derivatives. Although both approaches are promising, they introduce bioactivity through fundamentally different mechanisms. Directly fabricated hydrogels mainly rely on network chemistry, carboxylate-rich structures, physicochemical responsiveness, and degradation behavior, whereas carrier-based hydrogels are designed to regulate the local or intracellular availability of bioactive derivatives such as 4-OI or DMI [21, 26, 30]. Therefore, biological outcomes observed in one class should not be directly generalized to all itaconate-containing systems. This distinction is particularly important for polymerized systems. Upon covalent incorporation into a polymeric network, itaconate loses its free molecular form, and its capacity to engage intracellular targets becomes uncertain, as demonstrated for small molecules like itaconate, 4-OI, or DMI [21]. Although some studies attribute the full immunomodulatory and antimicrobial activity of free itaconate to polymerized forms, such claims may only hold under specific conditions, such as when bioactive fragments are released upon degradation or when local chemical reactivity is preserved. However, direct mechanistic evidence supporting these assumptions is frequently lacking [95]. Therefore, future studies should clearly distinguish structural contributions, stimuli-responsive physicochemical behavior, controlled-release effects, and true pharmacological activity. Their unique value over conventional immunomodulatory or anti-infective hydrogels should not be defined merely by the presence of itaconate units, but by whether material design can be mechanistically linked to immunometabolic regulation. Ideally, network chemistry, degradation behavior, release kinetics, and nanoscale carrier architecture should be correlated with local exposure to itaconate derivatives and subsequent biological effects, including NRF2 activation, redox homeostasis, mitochondrial function, macrophage polarization, and infection-associated inflammatory responses [26, 30, 150, 187]. However, many current studies report desirable material properties, such as enhanced swelling, increased pore size, ROS scavenging capacity, or improved antibacterial effects, without establishing causal links between these parameters and therapeutic efficacy [142, 143]. For example, increasing itaconate content may enhance pore size and swelling capacity but compromise mechanical integrity [143]. Similarly, improved antibacterial activity may arise from cationic groups, metal ions, nanoparticles, or photothermal components rather than from itaconate itself [97, 108, 196]. Anti-inflammatory effects may be mediated by released small molecules, enhanced local retention, or auxiliary components rather than by the intrinsic properties of the hydrogel network [21, 182]. Therefore, future research should prioritize systematic structure-property-function mapping rather than descriptive performance reporting alone. Key parameters, including itaconate content, derivative loading, dicarboxylate retention, crosslinking density, mesh size, degradation rate, mechanical stiffness, release kinetics, and intracellular bioavailability, should be quantitatively correlated with biological outcomes. Such mechanistic clarification is essential for establishing itaconate-based hydrogels as rationally designed bioactive hydrogel platforms rather than broadly defined itaconate-containing biomaterials.

Degradation, biosafety, and long-term in vivo fate

Another critical issue concerns the degradation behavior, biosafety, and long-term in vivo fate of itaconate-based hydrogels. While many studies have demonstrated acceptable short-term biocompatibility, systematic investigations into degradation products, clearance pathways, local tissue responses, repeated-dose safety, and long-term biosafety remain limited. These knowledge gaps make it difficult to comprehensively assess their suitability for prolonged implantation, repeated administration, or chronic disease treatment [209, 230]. The safety concerns differ among different hydrogel design strategies. For directly fabricated itaconate-containing hydrogels, key unanswered questions include whether degradation releases free itaconate, structurally modified fragments, oligomeric species, or non-bioactive degradation products. It also remains unclear whether degradation-induced local pH changes, residual unsaturated groups, or acidic fragments may influence surrounding tissue metabolism, immune-cell behavior, or inflammatory responses over time. Therefore, the safety profile of directly fabricated hydrogels cannot be inferred solely from short-term cytocompatibility or histological assessment [21, 95, 142]. For carrier-based hydrogels, additional concerns arise from the local and systemic exposure of 4-OI, DMI, or related derivatives. The therapeutic window of these molecules may vary across tissues, disease states, and cell types. Burst release could cause excessive local exposure, whereas prolonged low-level release may induce insufficient efficacy or unintended immune modulation [133, 153, 158, 162]. Several broader biological concerns associated with itaconate and its derivatives also warrant consideration, including inflammation-associated anemia, context-dependent tumor-promoting or immune-evasive effects, and the inhibitory impact of 4-OI on myogenic differentiation and antigen presentation [101–104]. The safety evaluation becomes even more complex for nano-enabled or multicomponent itaconate-based hydrogels. Many advanced systems incorporate 4-OI or DMI together with black phosphorus, copper-containing frameworks, nanogels, exosomes, responsive nanovesicles, metal ions, photothermal components, or antimicrobial agents. Although such combinations may enhance therapeutic efficacy, they also make it difficult to identify the source of both beneficial and adverse effects. The overall biological response may reflect the released itaconate derivative, the carrier matrix, the nanomaterial component, or interactions among all components [30, 97, 108, 139, 142]. Therefore, sophisticated formulations require more rigorous component-level safety evaluation than simple hydrogel systems. Highly complex systems may demonstrate strong efficacy in proof-of-concept studies but face substantial barriers in safety testing, quality control, regulatory classification, and manufacturing. By contrast, single-component or simplified itaconate-based hydrogels may offer clearer mechanistic interpretation, more tractable safety profiling, and a more straightforward path toward clinical translation [21, 95]. This does not mean that multicomponent systems should be avoided, but their added complexity must be justified by clear therapeutic benefits over simpler formulations. Therefore, future studies should include not only standard cytocompatibility assays and short-term animal histology, but also degradation tracking, metabolite analysis, extractables and leachables assessment, immunotoxicity evaluation, repeated-dose studies, biodistribution analysis where relevant, and longer-term functional follow-up. These studies should be designed according to the specific hydrogel strategy being evaluated, because the major safety risks of directly fabricated hydrogels and derivative-loaded hydrogels are not identical.

Insufficient comparative benchmarking against established hydrogel platforms

The translational value of itaconate-based hydrogels is also constrained by the lack of rigorous comparative benchmarking against established hydrogel platforms. Many studies demonstrate that itaconate-based hydrogels exhibit desirable properties such as pH responsiveness, biodegradability, antibacterial activity, anti-inflammatory effects, or tunable mechanics [133, 142, 147, 213]. However, relatively few studies address the more critical question of whether itaconate confers any distinct advantage over mature platforms, including AA, methacrylate, alginate, hyaluronic acid, Gel-MA, or chitosan-based systems. This is particularly important for pH-responsive drug delivery, where ionizable carboxyl groups are already common in established polymers [233]. The same issue arises in wound healing and anti-infective applications, where itaconate often serves as just one component within complex composites that include cationic modifications, metal ions, nanoparticles, or photothermal agents [97, 108, 196]. While these systems yield promising results, they obscure the specific contribution of itaconate. Without proper controls, such as comparisons between hydrogels with and without itaconate or between native itaconate and 4-OI-loaded systems, it remains unclear whether itaconate is a central functional element or simply a modifier.

From the perspective of bioactive and immunomodulatory biomaterials, comparative benchmarking is particularly important because the claimed advantage of itaconate-based hydrogels should extend beyond conventional material performance. Compared with established immunomodulatory or anti-infective hydrogels that mainly function as reservoirs for exogenous cytokines, growth factors, antioxidants, antibiotics, or metal ions, itaconate-based systems are expected to offer a more mechanism-oriented strategy by integrating material engineering and host-directed immunometabolic regulation [5, 11, 24]. Therefore, their advantages should be evaluated not only by conventional material indicators, such as swelling ratio, degradation rate, mechanical strength, and antibacterial efficiency, but also by mechanism-relevant biological endpoints, including NRF2 signaling, inflammatory cytokine profiles, ROS homeostasis, mitochondrial function, macrophage phenotype, intracellular delivery efficiency, and infection-associated metabolic remodeling. For directly fabricated hydrogels, benchmarking should include non-itaconate analogues with comparable charge density, crosslinking density, stiffness, and degradation behavior. Such controls are necessary to determine whether observed effects arise from itaconate-specific chemistry or from general carboxylate-rich network properties. For carrier-based hydrogels, comparison should include hydrogels delivering conventional anti-inflammatory agents, antioxidants, antibiotics, growth factors, or other immunomodulatory molecules under identical disease models, dosing conditions, and evaluation endpoints.

More rigorous benchmarking could strengthen the field in several ways. First, it would clarify when itaconate provides functional advantages beyond serving as an alternative monomer or carboxylate-containing modifier. Second, it would identify therapeutic contexts in which its chemical-biological identity, especially its connection to immunometabolic regulation, provides benefits that are difficult to achieve using conventional hydrogel platforms. Third, it would help determine whether the added formulation complexity of nano-enabled or multicomponent hydrogels is justified by measurable improvements in efficacy, safety, or translational feasibility. Finally, such comparisons would enhance translational credibility by demonstrating whether itaconate-based hydrogels provide superiority over simpler and more established biomaterial systems. Therefore, future studies should include direct head-to-head comparisons with clinically or experimentally mature hydrogel platforms under identical disease models, dosing regimens, material doses, drug-loading levels, and evaluation endpoints. Only through such systematic benchmarking can the field determine whether itaconate-based hydrogels represent a meaningful advance in immunomodulatory biomaterials and nano-enabled therapeutic delivery, rather than primarily a chemical variation of existing hydrogel systems.

Reproducibility, scalability, and manufacturing considerations

Reproducibility and manufacturing feasibility are central barriers to the clinical development of itaconate-based hydrogels. Most current studies are performed at laboratory scale under highly controlled conditions, whereas batch-to-batch consistency, raw-material variability, process robustness, quality-control criteria, sterilization compatibility, storage stability, and large-scale production are seldom systematically evaluated. For translational development, these parameters are not secondary technical details but core determinants of product quality, regulatory compliance, cost, and clinical usability [24, 205, 213].

Manufacturing reproducibility must be evaluated across both chemical synthesis and hydrogel fabrication, and the key quality-control requirements differ substantially among hydrogel strategies. Directly fabricated itaconate-based hydrogels require careful control of monomer conversion, degree of functionalization, polymerization efficiency, crosslinking density, residual monomers or initiators, and degradation profiles [21, 111, 112, 143]. Carrier-based hydrogels loaded with 4-OI, DMI, or related derivatives further require control over loading efficiency, cargo distribution, burst release, long-term release kinetics, and derivative stability. Nano-enabled or multicomponent systems introduce additional variables, including nanoparticle size, surface chemistry, nanovesicles or exosome quality, metal-ion content, photothermal properties, and interactions among components. These challenges are amplified by the fact that itaconate-based hydrogels may involve multi-step functionalization, free-radical polymerization, photo-crosslinking, ionic coordination, nanoparticle incorporation, or biologically derived components, each of which can introduce variability in pore architecture, mechanical strength, swelling ratio, degradation rate, and release behavior [30, 133, 139, 144]. Residual persulfates or other initiators may complicate purification and safety evaluation, whereas γ-ray or photopolymerization can reduce chemical residues but create process sensitivity related to irradiation dose, light penetration, exposure time, and sample geometry [23, 115, 119]. Therefore, as formulation complexity increases, so does the burden of analytical characterization and quality control. Future studies should report process parameters in sufficient detail and establish acceptance criteria for critical quality attributes, including chemical composition, degree of functionalization, gelation time, mechanical properties, sterility, endotoxin level, degradation behavior, and drug or derivative loading efficiency.

Scalability is another unresolved issue. A formulation that performs well in small molds or thin films may not retain the same network homogeneity, curing efficiency, sensitivity, thermal profile, or release kinetics after scale-up. Injectable and in situ-forming hydrogels introduce additional requirements, including reproducible mixing, manageable viscosity, predictable gelation under physiological conditions, compatibility with delivery devices, and consistent performance after storage and transport [120, 133]. Multifunctional nanocomposite systems may face even greater scale-up barriers because exosomes, nanovesicles, MOFs, metal ions, or photoresponsive nanoparticles are difficult to standardize and may increase batch heterogeneity [30, 97, 108, 134, 150]. By contrast, modular systems based on clinically familiar matrices with selective itaconate functionalization or controlled loading of DMI/4-OI may offer a more realistic manufacturing path.

Sterilization and storage stability should be integrated into hydrogel formulation design rather than treated as downstream manufacturing issues. Because sterilization effects depend on polymer chemistry, crosslinking, network structure, bioactive components, and processing conditions, no single method is universally suitable. Thermal, irradiation-based, or chemical treatments may alter polymer integrity, crosslink density, swelling, mechanics, degradation, release, self-healing, and biological performance [234, 235]. Therefore, macroscopic integrity alone is insufficient. For example, autoclaving-sterilized PVA-MA hydrogels remained handleable but showed reduced stiffness and directional mechanics [236]. Sterilization conditions should thus be selected early and validated for both sterility and retention of application-specific critical quality attributes. Storage is especially challenging for dynamically crosslinked hydrogels because continued bond exchange or secondary crosslinking may change modulus, mesh size, injectability, and release behavior over time. Hydrated systems may also undergo dehydration, network collapse, microbial contamination, or payload degradation [235, 237]. In itaconate-based formulations, 4-OI may hydrolyze or lose bioactivity, whereas polyitaconate may continue to crosslink; ultra-low-temperature storage may slow this process [21, 29]. Lyophilization and protective packaging may improve stability, but rehydration may not fully restore the original network [235]. Therefore, real-time and accelerated studies under relevant temperature, humidity, light, freeze–thaw, and packaging conditions are needed to define shelf life and determine whether the product should be supplied as a preformed hydrogel, lyophilized matrix, precursor solution, or two-component kit.

The upstream supply of itaconate should also be considered. Chemical synthesis can be limited by cost, yield, and environmental burden, whereas microbial biomanufacturing using engineered strains such as E. coli and Y. lipolytica provides a more sustainable alternative. Industrial production using A. terreus has reached large scale, and metabolic engineering continues to improve productivity [238, 239]. However, biomedical translation requires not only bulk availability but also medical-grade purity, impurity profiling, traceability, and consistency of raw materials. Overall, materials that provide only marginal biological improvement but require highly complex manufacturing are unlikely to succeed clinically. Future translational efforts should prioritize simplified formulations, scalable production, validated sterilization methods, stable storage formats, standardized quality-control frameworks, and clear justification for each added functional component. From a translational perspective, the most promising itaconate-based hydrogel may not be the most complex formulation, but the one that achieves a favorable balance among therapeutic efficacy, manufacturability, safety, reproducibility, and clinical usability.

Preclinical, regulatory, and commercialization barriers

Finally, the predictive value of current preclinical evidence remains insufficient to fully support clinical translation. Itaconate-based hydrogel remains dominated by in vitro studies and small-animal models, which is a significant limitation since many target indications, such as chronic wounds, osteoarthritis, bone defects, and gastrointestinal repair, are sensitive to scale, tissue mechanics, immune context, and disease chronicity. A hydrogel that works well in short-term murine models may not perform similarly in larger, more clinically relevant settings [22, 26, 100, 132]. Another challenge is model selection. Many studies use simplified or acute models, while clinically relevant conditions are often chronic, heterogeneous, or complicated by infection and metabolic factors. For example, Rodent diabetic wound models do not fully recapitulate human diabetic wounds because of species-specific differences in skin architecture, vascularization, immune responses, microbiota, and healing dynamics [240]. Osteoarthritis involves long-term biomechanical remodeling, cartilage degeneration, synovial inflammation, and subchondral bone changes that cannot be captured by short-duration studies alone [186]. Current infected-wound models typically reproduce only selected aspects of human wound infection and often fail to fully capture polymicrobial biofilm communities, dynamic host-microbe interactions, and the long-term persistence of infection [241]. Preclinical evaluation should also be better aligned with the intended hydrogel strategy. For directly fabricated hydrogels, studies should assess local tissue integration, degradation behavior, scaffold stability, mechanical function, and long-term inflammatory responses. For derivative-loaded carrier hydrogels, studies should evaluate pharmacokinetic profiles, local retention, intracellular delivery, dose-response relationships, and potential systemic exposure. Moreover, for nano-enabled or multicomponent systems, additional attention should be paid to biodistribution, component-level safety, immune responses to nanomaterials or nanovesicles, and long-term clearance. Across all strategies, endpoints should extend beyond short-term histology, wound closure, or gross tissue repair to include infection control, tissue integration, mechanical function, pain, immune normalization, and quality of repair.

Regulatory positioning is a key translational consideration. Depending on their composition and intended application, itaconate-based hydrogels may be classified as drug-device combination products, biologic-containing products, or advanced wound-care materials [21, 26, 231]. Accordingly, product classification and primary mode of action will shape the required regulatory evidence package, which may include chemistry, manufacturing, and controls or technical documentation, sterility assurance, risk-based biocompatibility and chemical characterization, degradation and extractables/leachables profiling, pharmacokinetic or toxicokinetic assessment of released constituents, and evaluation of local and systemic toxicity [242, 243]. Systems incorporating DMI, 4-OI, exosomes, nanoparticles, MOFs, or antimicrobial agents are likely to encounter more complex regulatory pathways than single-component or matrix-functionalized hydrogels [95, 132, 139, 199]. Accordingly, early alignment of material design, safety, and performance testing with the anticipated regulatory classification and approval pathway may reduce downstream translational uncertainty and facilitate a smoother route to clinical adoption [243, 244].

Commercialization challenges are also substantial. Itaconate-based hydrogels must demonstrate not only biological efficacy but also a credible value proposition compared with lower-cost and clinically familiar products such as alginate dressings, hyaluronic acid gels, collagen matrices, chitosan-based dressings, and Gel-MA-like platforms. Commercial viability will depend on manufacturing cost, supply-chain reliability, sterilized shelf life, ease of use, compatibility with clinical workflows, reimbursement potential, and evidence of superiority in clinically meaningful endpoints [206, 242, 245]. Therefore, future translational studies should incorporate disease-relevant large-animal models, longer follow-up, standardized comparators, functional endpoints, safety margins, manufacturing feasibility, and health-economic considerations. The objective should not merely be to demonstrate efficacy in an experimental model, but to establish why an itaconate-based hydrogel is preferable to existing platforms within a clearly defined clinical context.

Taken together, itaconate-based hydrogels have progressed from exploratory chemical systems to biologically sophisticated multifunctional biomaterials, but their translational pathway remains incomplete. Key priorities include clarifying structure–function relationships, defining long-term degradation and biosafety profiles, benchmarking performance against mature hydrogel platforms, simplifying formulations where feasible, establishing reproducible and scalable manufacturing processes, validating sterilization and storage stability, defining appropriate regulatory pathways, and demonstrating clinical and commercial value in relevant models. Addressing these challenges will be essential for transforming itaconate-based hydrogels from promising experimental materials into clinically credible therapeutic products.

Conclusion

Itaconate-based hydrogels represent a distinctive class of bioactive biomaterials because they bring together two attributes that are often treated separately: the chemical versatility required for hydrogel construction and the immunometabolic activity required for therapeutic regulation. Current evidence indicates that the greatest value of itaconate lies not simply in introducing dicarboxylate groups or α, β-unsaturated double bonds into polymer networks, but in enabling localized and potentially programmable control of inflammation, oxidative stress, host defense, and tissue repair. This dual identity gives itaconate-based hydrogels a particularly attractive position at the interface of sustainable polymer chemistry, biomaterials science, immunology, and regenerative medicine. Their key advantage over many existing immunomodulatory or anti-infective hydrogel systems lies in the ability to connect material design with immunometabolic regulation. Specifically, network chemistry, nanoscale carrier architecture, and release kinetics can be engineered to control local exposure to itaconate derivatives, thereby modulating macrophage polarization, redox signaling, mitochondrial function, and infection-associated inflammatory responses. This mechanism-oriented design logic distinguishes itaconate-based hydrogels from hydrogel systems that primarily function as passive reservoirs for anti-inflammatory drugs, antibiotics, growth factors, or other exogenous agents.

However, the field is still at an early developmental stage. Current studies remain heterogeneous in material design, mechanism validation, and biological interpretation. Directly fabricated itaconate-based hydrogels and carrier-based hydrogels loaded with itaconate or itaconate derivatives offer distinct advantages, but they should not be viewed as mechanistically interchangeable. Directly fabricated systems are strongest when network responsiveness, biodegradability, and scaffold simplicity are the main design goals, whereas carrier systems are more suitable when intracellular immunometabolic signaling by 4-OI, DMI, or related derivatives is required. Nano-enabled and multicomponent systems may further improve spatiotemporal delivery, cell-specific exposure, and multifunctional therapy, but they also introduce additional challenges in mechanism attribution, reproducibility, safety evaluation, manufacturing, and regulatory translation. Therefore, future development should move beyond descriptive demonstrations of favorable swelling, release, antibacterial, or anti-inflammatory properties. Greater emphasis should be placed on mechanism-informed classification, quantitative structure-property-function analysis, and direct comparison with established hydrogel platforms. In particular, future studies should clarify how itaconate content, derivative loading, dicarboxylate retention, crosslinking density, mesh size, degradation behavior, mechanical stiffness, release kinetics, and intracellular bioavailability correlate with biological outcomes such as NRF2 activation, ROS homeostasis, mitochondrial protection, macrophage phenotype, inflammatory cytokine profiles, infection control, and tissue regeneration. Such analyses are essential for determining whether itaconate provides a true functional advantage over conventional pH-responsive, immunomodulatory, or antibacterial hydrogel systems. From a translational perspective, several priorities are especially important: comprehensive evaluation of degradation products and long-term in vivo fate; rigorous biosafety assessment under repeated or prolonged treatment conditions; head-to-head benchmarking against clinically or experimentally mature hydrogel platforms; simplification of overly complex formulations; scalable and reproducible manufacturing; validated sterilization and storage strategies; and early consideration of regulatory classification and commercialization feasibility. With these advances, itaconate-based hydrogels may evolve from promising experimental materials into clinically credible platforms for immunomodulatory biomaterials, anti-infective therapy, and regenerative medicine.
