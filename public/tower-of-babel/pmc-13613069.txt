Molecular and structural basis for replication initiation and strand separation by human mitochondrial DNA polymerase γ
Authors: Viktoriia Sokolova, Gina Buchel, Sarah Strock, Ashok R Nayak, Dmitry Temiakov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDefects in human mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) replication can lead to somatic mutations associated with a range of devastating mitochondrial diseases. However, the molecular mechanisms governing the earliest steps of mtDNA replication and their fidelity remain poorly understood. Here, we found that DNA polymerase gamma (Polγ) forms stable complexes with RNA–DNA primer–template substrates, exhibiting greater stability and lower misincorporation than on DNA-primed substrates. Structural analysis revealed that Polγ interacts with the 2′-OH groups of ribose within the first four nucleotides of the primer, explaining the stability of complexes that utilize RNA primers. Although Polγ requires TWINKLE to extend RNA primers, its intrinsic strand-displacement activity allows it to extend DNA primers independently. Structural data further show that the strand-separation mechanism in human Polγ is distinct from that of its yeast paralog, Mip1, and involves previously unresolved elements—the catcher and a GP loop in the exonuclease domain—that support intrinsic strand-displacement synthesis by Polγ. Structure-guided mutagenesis of elements involved in strand separation supports these structural observations. Together, our study provides mechanistic insight into mtDNA replication initiation and strand separation and has implications for understanding the molecular basis of mitochondrial disease.

Introduction

The proteins that constitute the human mitochondrial replisome are distinct from those involved in nuclear DNA replication and instead share structural and mechanistic similarities with the bacteriophage T7 replication machinery [1]. The primary enzyme responsible for mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) synthesis and maintenance is DNA polymerase gamma (Polγ). Polγ performs several essential functions in mitochondria, including mtDNA synthesis, 3′–5′ proofreading, and base-excision repair, and has also been implicated in RNA-associated activities such as reverse transcription, RNA-primed DNA synthesis, and ribonucleotide incorporation [2–6].

Polγ is a heterotrimeric complex composed of one catalytic subunit, Polγ-A, and two accessory subunits, Polγ-B [7, 8]. Crystal and cryo-EM structures of human Polγ have established the overall architecture of the holoenzyme and provided important insight into its catalytic mechanism, proofreading, processive DNA synthesis, disease-associated mutations, and antiviral nucleotide toxicity [9–12]. Polγ belongs to the Pol A family of DNA polymerases, which also includes a closely related bacteriophage T7 DNA polymerase (T7 gp5), bacterial DNA polymerase I, and human DNA polymerases Theta and Nu. Polγ-A contains characteristic exonuclease and polymerase domains of this family [4, 13, 14], whereas Polγ-B enhances the processivity of DNA synthesis and is essential for full holoenzyme activity [6, 7].

Polγ requires the mitochondrial replicative helicase TWINKLE and single-stranded DNA binding protein, mtSSB, for processive synthesis through duplex DNA [15–18]. TWINKLE unwinds double-stranded mtDNA in an ATP-dependent manner and is essential for mtDNA maintenance, as loss of TWINKLE function causes severe defects in mitochondrial genome replication and embryonic lethality in mice [15, 18, 19]. During initiation of replication of human mitochondrial genome, Polγ must utilize RNA primers synthesized by mitochondrial RNA polymerase, POLRMT [20]. At the heavy-strand origin, OriH, these early replication events require TWINKLE, which unwinds the downstream duplex DNA to support primer extension. In contrast, replication from the light-strand origin, OriL, appears to be TWINKLE-independent because OriL is exposed as single-stranded DNA during heavy-strand synthesis [21]. However, whether TWINKLE is strictly required for all strand-separation events during mtDNA replication remains unresolved. Biochemical studies have produced apparently conflicting conclusions regarding the intrinsic strand-displacement capacity of human Polγ. Several reports found that wild-type (WT) Polγ displays little or only weak strand-displacement activity on duplex-containing substrates, whereas exonuclease-deficient Polγ variants can synthesize more efficiently into downstream duplex DNA [22–24]. These observations suggested that the 3′–5′ exonuclease activity of Polγ may oppose strand displacement, possibly by promoting idling or partitioning of the primer terminus away from the polymerase active site. In contrast, more recent single-molecule studies demonstrated that human Polγ can destabilize the first base pair at a fork junction, indicating that the enzyme itself has an intrinsic ability to promote strand separation [25]. However, this activity is strongly limited by reannealing of the parental DNA strands and is therefore greatly enhanced by mtSSB, which stabilizes the displaced single-stranded DNA [26].

These findings raise a key mechanistic question: does human Polγ contain dedicated structural elements that support intrinsic strand displacement, and, if so, how are these elements coordinated with its polymerase and exonuclease functions? Recent structural studies of yeast mtDNA polymerase Mip1, a Polγ paralog, provided important insight into this problem [27]. Mip1 is capable of TWINKLE-independent strand-displacement synthesis, consistent with the absence of a TWINKLE homolog in yeast mitochondria. Cryo-EM structures of Mip1 captured sequential intermediates of strand separation and identified several structural elements that contribute to this activity, including the strand-displacement loop (SD loop), the wedge helix, and a mobile “catcher” element that stabilizes the downstream DNA during duplex opening [27]. Interestingly, related structural elements are present in human Polγ, and several residues implicated in strand separation are conserved between Mip1 and human Polγ. Nevertheless, the extent to which these elements contribute to human Polγ strand displacement, and whether human Polγ uses the same structural mechanism as Mip1, has not been established. In this study, we investigated the earliest steps of primer extension and strand separation by human Polγ using complementary biochemical and structural approaches.

Materials and methods

Protein expression and purification

N-terminal histidine-tagged human Polγ-B (residues 26–485) was expressed and purified as described previously [28]. Variants of N-terminal histidine-tagged human Polγ-A (residues 26–1239) were obtained by site-directed mutagenesis (QuikChange, Agilent). WT and exonuclease-deficient (Exo−, D198A, E200A) Polγ-A were expressed and purified as previously described with modifications [28]. Polγ-A was purified by affinity chromatography using Ni-NTA beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific), followed by affinity chromatography using a TSKgel Heparin-5PW column (Tosoh Bioscience). The heparin column was equilibrated in buffer A (40 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.9, 150 mM NaCl, 5% glycerol, 5 mM of 2-mercaptoethanol), and Polγ-A was eluted by 0%–70% linear gradient of buffer B (40 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.9, 1.5 M NaCl, 5% glycerol, 5 mM of 2-mercaptoethanol). Peak fractions eluted at 33 mS/cm were collected and analyzed using polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE), concentrated, and stored at −80°C.

Polγ holoenzyme complex was reconstituted by incubating Polγ-A fractions after Ni-NTA chromatography with a two-fold molar excess of purified Polγ-B for 10 min at room temperature. Polγ was purified by affinity chromatography on a Heparin-5PW column, as described above. Peak fractions eluted at 40 mS/cm were collected and analyzed using sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE), concentrated, and stored at −80°C.

Human TWINKLE (residues 43–684) having an N-terminal histidine tag was cloned into the pProEx vector, and variants were obtained by site-directed mutagenesis (QuikChange, Agilent). TWINKLE variants (WT, K421A) were expressed in RIPL cells (Millipore) and purified as follows. The cells were grown in filtered LB supplemented with 0.8% glucose until the OD600 reached 0.6 units, and TWINKLE expression was induced by addition of 0.15 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) for 16 h at 25°C. Cells were lysed in buffer containing 40 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.9, 1.2 M NaCl, 5% glycerol, 1 mM ATP, 5 mM MgCl2, and 5 mM of 2-mercaptoethanol, DNA was precipitated by 0.25% polyethyleneimine, and the supernatant proteins were precipitated by 50% ammonium sulfate overnight. The pellet was then resuspended in buffer containing 40 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.9), 1 M NaCl, 5% glycerol, 0.2 mM ATP, 5 mM MgCl2, and 5 mM of 2-mercaptoethanol and purified by affinity chromatography using Ni-NTA beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific). All TWINKLE concentrations refer to the hexameric form.

Human mtSSB (residues 17–148) having a C-terminal histidine tag was expressed and purified as described previously [28]. MtSSB concentration is reported for the tetrameric form.

To anneal the nucleic acid templates, the oligonucleotides were diluted in water, heated for 7 min at 95°C, and cooled down (1°C/min) for 1 h to 25°C in a thermocycler.

Turnover assays for determining stability

The complexes of Exo− Polγ (0.25, 0.5, 0.75, or 1 μM) with labeled primer–template (1 μM, RNA28/DNA28TS or DNA28/DNA28TS) were assembled in a buffer containing 40 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.9), 60 mM NaCl, 10 mM MgCl2, and 20 mM of 2-mercaptoethanol in the presence of bovine serum albumin (BSA; 0.1 mg/ml) for 5 min at room temperature. The incoming cognate dNTP (0.1 mM dATP) was added to the reaction and incubated for 2 min at room temperature and stopped by the addition of an equal volume of 95% formamide/0.05 M ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA). The products of the reaction were resolved by 20% PAGE containing 6 M urea and visualized by autoradiography using a PhosphorIm ager (GE Healthcare).

Primer extension assay

The primers were 5′-labeled using [γ-32P]ATP (3000 Ci/mmol) and T4 polynucleotide kinase (NEB). The complexes of Polγ or Polγ-A (50 nM) with labeled primer–template constructs were assembled in a buffer containing 40 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.9), 60 mM NaCl, 1 mM MgCl2, and 20 mM of 2-mercaptoethanol in the presence of BSA (0.1 mg/ml) for 5 min at room temperature. Primer extension was performed in the presence of 0.1 mM dNTPs, or as indicated. All reactions were carried out for the indicated periods of time at room temperature (unless otherwise indicated) and stopped by the addition of an equal volume of 95% formamide/0.05 M EDTA. The products of the reaction were resolved by 20% PAGE containing 6 M urea and visualized by autoradiography using a PhosphorImager (GE Healthcare).

Strand-displacement assay

The primers were 5′-labeled using [γ-32P]ATP (3000 Ci/mmol) and T4 polynucleotide kinase (NEB). Polγ (40 nM) was preincubated with labeled primer–template constructs (20 nM) in a buffer containing 40 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.9), 50 mM NaCl, 1 mM MgCl2, and 20 mM of 2-mercaptoethanol in the presence of BSA (0.1 mg/ml) for 5 min at room temperature. Primer extension was performed in the presence of 20 μM dNTPs (unless otherwise indicated). All reactions were carried out for the indicated periods of time at 37°C (unless otherwise indicated) and stopped by the addition of an equal volume of 95% formamide/0.05 M EDTA. The products of the reaction were resolved by 20% PAGE containing 6 M urea and visualized by autoradiography using a PhosphorImager (GE Healthcare).

Replication assay

The primers were 5′-labeled using [γ-32P]ATP (3000 Ci/mmol) and T4 polynucleotide kinase (NEB). Replication complexes of Polγ (20 nM) and TWINKLE (40 nM) with labeled primer–template (20 nM) were assembled in a buffer containing 40 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.9), 80 mM NaCl, 10 mM MgCl2, and 20 mM of 2-mercaptoethanol in the presence of BSA (0.1 mg/ml). Replication was performed in the presence of 0.1 mM dNTPs and 4.5 mM ATP, unless otherwise indicated. For reactions with mtSSB, increasing amounts (20, 40, 100 nM) were added, as indicated. Replication reactions were carried out for 30 min at 37°C, unless otherwise indicated, treated with Proteinase K (0.2 mg/ml) for 1 h at 55°C, followed by the addition of an equal volume of 95% formamide/0.05 M EDTA. The products of the reaction were resolved by 20% PAGE containing 6 M Urea and visualized by autoradiography using PhosphorImager (GE Healthcare).

Processive DNA synthesis

The DNA template was prepared by annealing a 20-nt M13 forward primer to single-stranded circular M13mp18 DNA (Bayou Biolabs). WT Polγ or the indicated Polγ variants (60 nM) were added to the primer–template construct (2 nM) in buffer containing 40 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.9), 50 mM NaCl, 10 mM MgCl₂, and 20 mM of 2-mercaptoethanol, in the presence of BSA (0.1 mg/ml), and incubated for 5 min at room temperature. Primer extension was initiated by adding dNTPs to a final concentration of 20 μM. Reactions were carried out at 37°C for the indicated times and stopped by the addition of 0.05 M EDTA. The reaction products were resolved on a 0.8% agarose gel containing ethidium bromide and visualized using a Bio-Rad ChemiDoc imager.

Electrophoretic mobility shift assay

The 5′-Cy3-labeled primer (DNA24, IDTDNA) was annealed to DNA template strand (DNA38TS). To perform electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA), complexes of Polγ/DNA were assembled in a buffer containing 40 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.9), 60 mM NaCl, 10 mM MgCl2, 5% glycerol, 20 mM of 2-mercaptoethanol in the presence of BSA (0.1 mg/ml) and incubated with 100 nM of 5′-Cy3-labeled templates for 10 min at room temperature. The reactions were resolved in 0.5% agarose gels run in 0.5× TBE buffer for 30 min at 100 V at 4°C. The products of the reactions were visualized using Bio-Rad ChemiDoc™ imager and quantified using ImageJ. For each reaction, the fraction of bound DNA was determined as the intensity of shifted DNA divided by the sum of free and shifted DNA signals (n = 3 independent experiments).

Preparation of Polγ complexes for CryoEM

To assemble the Polγ RNA–DNA primer–template complex, 2 μM Exo− Polγ (D198A, E200A) was incubated with the RNA20/DNA20TS template at a 1:1:1 molar ratio in a buffer containing 10 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.9), 100 mM NaCl, 10 mM dithiothreitol (DTT), and 2 mM MgCl2 and incubated for 5 min at room temperature. Following incubation at room temperature for 5 min, ddATP and dTTP were added to final concentrations of 0.1 and 1 mM, respectively, and the primer was extended for 10 min at room temperature, followed by overnight dialysis in the same buffer at 4°C.

To assemble the strand-separation Polγ complex, WT Polγ (2 μM) was incubated with the DNA20/DNA40TS/NT23flap4T template at a 1:1 molar ratio in the presence of 0.5 mM dGTP and ddTTP for 20 min at room temperature to allow primer extension and chain termination. The complex was then dialyzed overnight at 4°C against buffer containing 10 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.9), 100 mM NaCl, 10 mM DTT, and 5 mM MgCl₂. After dialysis, dATP (an incoming nucleotide) was added to a final concentration of 0.1 mM, and the complex was incubated at room temperature for an additional 10 min prior to transferring to ice and grid preparation.

The complexes were applied to negatively glow-discharged 300 mesh UltraAufoil 1.2/1.3 holey-gold grids (Quantifoil). Grids were blotted with ash-free Whatman® Grade 540 filter paper in a Vitrobot Mark IV (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 4–5 s at 4°C and 95%–100% humidity, and then vitrified in liquid ethane. The sample quality and distribution were assessed using Glacios Transmission Electron Microscope equipped with a Falcon 4 direct electron detector.

Single-particle data acquisition and image processing

High-resolution single-particle cryo-EM datasets were collected at the Stanford-SLAC Cryo-EM Center (S2C2) and the National Cryo-Electron Microscopy Facility (NCEF) at the Frederick National Laboratory for Cancer Research. Data acquisition was performed on a Titan Krios G3 transmission electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific) operating at 300 kV, equipped with a Gatan BioQuantum energy filter operating in zero-loss mode (slit width 20 eV). Movies were recorded on a Gatan K3 direct electron detector (Ametek) operating in super-resolution mode at a nominal magnification of 105 000× or 130 000×, corresponding to super-resolution pixel sizes of 0.428 Å/pixel and 0.342 Å/pixel (physical pixel sizes of 0.856 Å/pixel and 0.684 Å/pixel after 2× Fourier cropping during motion correction), respectively. A 100-µm objective aperture was inserted to enhance contrast for the Polγ RNA–DNA heteroduplex dataset. Micrographs were acquired over a target defocus range of −0.4 to −1.8 µm. Specimens were exposed to a total accumulated electron dose of 60–70 e−/Å² over an exposure time of 1.66–2.6 s, fractionated into 60–70 frames at 1 e−/Å²/frame. Automated data collection was performed using EPU software (Thermo Fisher Scientific) or Latitude S (Ametek Gatan). A total of 12 703 and 7236 movies were acquired for the Polγ RNA–DNA heteroduplex and Polγ strand-displacement catalytic complexes, respectively. Comprehensive data collection parameters are summarized in Supplementary Table S1.

All image processing was performed in CryoSPARC v4.4–4.6 [29]. Movie stacks were gain-normalized, motion-corrected, and dose-weighted using Patch Motion Correction, and CTF parameters were estimated for each micrograph using Patch CTF estimation. Micrographs containing crystalline ice or ethane contamination, exhibiting excessive frame motion, or yielding CTF fits worse than 5 Å were excluded using Curate Exposures job. Polγ particles were initially picked using Blob Picker with a particle diameter range of 80–130 Å, and particles separated by <30 Å were removed as duplicates. Following multiple rounds of 2D classification on 4× binned particles, well-resolved 2D classes were pooled and re-extracted at the full pixel size. An initial reference map was generated by ab initio reconstruction using an input resolution range of 5–7 Å. The resulting map was low-pass filtered to 30 Å and used as the reference for non-uniform refinement of the unbinned particle stack.

Heterogeneous refinement of the Polγ RNA–DNA heteroduplex dataset revealed no substantial conformational heterogeneity among the resulting 3D classes, except for minor differences in the upstream DNA duplex trajectory. Consequently, a single consensus reconstruction was generated and refined to a nominal resolution of 2.6 Å, and this map was used for subsequent model building and refinement.

For the Polγ strand-displacement complex, an initial homogeneous particle population free of disordered and junk particles was isolated by ab initio reconstruction followed by heterogeneous refinement into four classes. The dominant Polγ-like class was subsequently refined by non-uniform refinement to an overall resolution of 3.0 Å. A second round of heterogeneous refinement into four classes resolved conformational heterogeneity within this population, enabling separation of the strand-displacement catalytic state from particles in which the primer–template duplex was not positioned at the polymerase active site either by Polγ translocation or partitioned toward the exonuclease site, representing intermediates along the proofreading pathway [12]. Reference-based motion correction was then applied to particles belonging to the strand-displacement catalytic class to further improve the signal-to-noise ratio. To improve the local resolution of the Polγ, including the downstream DNA region and adjacent protein regions, soft-edged masks were generated independently for the catalytic subunit (Polγ-A) and the accessory homodimer (Polγ-B), followed by separate local refinements for each masked region. The resulting focused maps were merged into a composite reconstruction using the vop maximum operation in UCSF Chimera [30] guided by the consensus map. The composite map enabled improved visualization of previously poorly resolved features, including the downstream template strand, the strand-displacement module (SD and GP loops), and the catcher domain. The final composite density map was sharpened using a transformer-based deep learning framework, CryoTEN [31]. The CryoTEN-sharpened map was used only as a visualization aid; model refinement, validation, and map-to-model statistics were performed using the unsharpened/composite maps. Reported cryo-EM resolutions are based on the FSC = 0.143 criterion [32]. Map anisotropy was assessed using the 3DFSC server [33], and angular distribution and local resolution plots were generated in UCSF ChimeraX [34].

Model building and structure refinement

An AlphaFold2-predicted Polγ model and primer–template duplex were docked into the respective unsharpened cryo-EM maps of the two catalytic complexes, and the polypeptide chains were morph-fitted using Coot v0.9.8.5 [35]. For the Polγ RNA–DNA structure, an RNA primer strand was fitted into the cryo-EM density. Chain-terminating nucleotides dideoxy AMP (2′,3′-dideoxyadenosine-5′-monophosphate) or dideoxy TMP (2′,3′-dideoxythymidine-5′-monophosphate), together with incoming nucleotides dTTP (2′-deoxythymidine-5′-triphosphate) or dATP (2′-deoxyadenosine-5′-triphosphate) and Mg²⁺ ions, were placed in the catalytic pocket for the Polγ RNA–DNA and Polγ strand-displacement structures, respectively. DNA-B and RNA-A restraints generated in Coot were used to model the template DNA and RNA primer, respectively. The local fit of Polγ and the nucleic acid regions to the density was iteratively improved through manual model fitting in Coot, followed by real-space refinement in PHENIX. The C-terminal five residues of the GP-loop (residues 319–346), together with the catcher domain (residues 990–1052), showed improved density in the focused map and were therefore modeled in the strand-displacement structure. In the RNA–DNA Polγ structure, catcher subdomain residues 992–1012 and 1031–1042 were omitted because of insufficient density. Density for the catcher domain improved in the strand-displacement structure, allowing most of this region to be modeled, except for residues 1005–1012 and 1031–1034, which remained poorly resolved, and their side chains were truncated to alanine.

Real-space refinement was performed using secondary-structure and Ramachandran restraints for the polypeptide chain, together with base-pairing and base-stacking restraints for the nucleic acids. Comprehensive model validation was carried out using PHENIX and the PDB validation server. Map-to-model Fourier shell correlation (CCmask) values were calculated in PHENIX [36]. Angles between the RNA and DNA primer axes were measured in Chimera, and protein–nucleic acid interactions were analyzed using the Protein–Ligand Interaction Profiler (PLIP) server [37]. Model refinement and validation statistics are summarized in Supplementary Tables S1 and S2. Figures were prepared using PyMOL and ChimeraX.

Results

Structural basis for RNA-primed replication initiation by Polγ

To determine the structural basis for RNA primer recognition and utilization, we used cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) and single-particle analysis to solve the structure of Polγ assembled on an RNA primer–template in the presence of the incoming substrate dTTP (Supplementary Fig. S1, Supplementary Table S1, and Fig. 1A and B). The 2.6 Å structure (Fig. 1C and D) reveals that Polγ adopts a catalytic conformation similar to that previously observed with a DNA primer–template [11], with an overall RMSD of 1.035 Å over 615 Cα atoms in the palm subdomain (Supplementary Fig. S1H).

(B) Schematic of the primer–template duplex (RNA20/DNA20TS) used to assemble the Polγ complex for cryo-EM studies. The RNA primer (coral) was extended by incorporating a chain-terminating residue, ddAMP, in the presence of the next incoming substrate, dTTP (yellow). (C) Cryo-EM density of the Polγ RNA–DNA primer–template complex. Major domains are indicated. (D) Structure of the Polγ RNA–DNA primer–template complex. (E) Close-up view of interactions between the thumb subdomain and the minor groove of the RNA–DNA heteroduplex. Blue dashed lines indicate distances (in Å) between side chains of thumb residues and the edges of the RNA–DNA base pairs. (F) Close-up view of Polγ interactions with the RNA primer. Potential hydrogen bonds with the 2′-OH groups of the RNA are shown as blue dashed lines, with distances (Å) indicated. Oxygen atoms of the 2′-OH groups are shown in red, and catalytic Mg²⁺ ions are shown in pink. The Sensor loop, which contains key residues interacting with the 2′-OH groups of RNA, is colored purple.

Minor conformational changes are observed in flexible regions of Polγ, including the fingers domain (residues 942–983) and the Guide loop (residues 757–784). We also observed a notable reorganization of the RNA–DNA heteroduplex (spanning positions n − 1 to n − 5), characterized by a ∼5° shift in the helical axis compared to the DNA–DNA primer–template duplex (PDB: 5C51) [38]. This structural change is accompanied by localized widening of the minor groove of the heteroduplex, which improves shape complementarity with the thumb helix (residues 787–809) and strengthens interactions with residues N803 and R807 (Fig. 1E).

In addition to these structural changes, the RNA-primed complex revealed specific interactions between Polγ-A and the 2′-OH groups of the first four nucleotides of the RNA primer within the polymerization site (Fig. 1F). Because a chain-terminating ddATP was used to obtain the RNA–DNA–Polγ ternary complex, the terminal n − 1 nucleotide lacks a 2′-OH group. To evaluate potential interactions that would occur with a natural RNA primer terminus, we modeled a ribose O2′ at this position and subjected the local model to real-space refinement. The refined model places the terminal 2′-OH within hydrogen-bonding distance of R853, with an O2′–R853 distance of 2.8 Å, supporting a direct interaction between R853 and the terminal ribose 2′-OH.

In the refined model, the 2′-OH group of the n − 1 nucleotide is positioned to interact with both H1134 and R853 (Fig. 1F). The ribose 2′-OH groups of the n − 2 and n − 3 nucleotides interact with the backbone carbonyl oxygens of L860 and T861, respectively. Together with R853, these residues are located in the Sensor Loop of Polγ, a structural element previously implicated in the proofreading mechanism [11, 12] (Fig. 1F). Finally, the 2′-OH group of the n − 4 nucleotide is positioned for a longer-range electrostatic interactions with the backbone carbonyl oxygen of S799 in the Polγ thumb domain (Fig. 1F).

Inspection of the Polγ catalytic site further revealed density consistent with three Mg²⁺ ions positioned around the primer terminus and the incoming nucleotide (Supplementary Fig. S2). Two Mg²⁺ ions occupy positions corresponding to the canonical A- and B-site metals of Pol A family DNA polymerases [38, 39]. The B-site Mg²⁺ is coordinated by the triphosphate moiety of the incoming nucleotide and nearby catalytic residues, whereas the A-site Mg²⁺ is positioned between the primer-terminal ddAMP, the incoming dTTP, and the acidic residue E1136, consistent with its expected role in organizing the reactive center. In addition, we observed a weaker density feature near D890, D892, and the γ-phosphate oxygen of the incoming nucleotide. We modeled this feature as a putative auxiliary Mg²⁺ ion (Mg3) because of its proximity to ligands capable of metal coordination. This assignment is tentative, as the density is of lower occupancy than the canonical catalytic metals; however, its position suggests that it may stabilize the incoming nucleotide or a transient catalytic-state intermediate in the RNA–DNA Polγ complex.

Topology of primer–template constructs affects the properties of Polγ complexes

Our further objective was to examine the stability of Polγ on primer–template constructs containing either an RNA or DNA primer, representing initiation of replication and productive DNA synthesis, respectively (Fig. 2A). Using turnover primer extension assays with an exonuclease deficient (Exo−) variant of Polγ to assess stability, we found that Polγ is more stable when using an RNA primer as a substrate and is less error-prone compared to constructs containing DNA primers (Fig. 2B).

The RNA primer is colored coral and the DNA primer red throughout the manuscript. (B) Turnover primer extension assays were performed to assess the stability of Polγ on DNA- or RNA-primed templates. Left: RNA primer extension; right: DNA primer extension. In a stable complex at a 0.5 to 1 ratio of Exo− Polγ to a template, ∼50% of primer extension is expected, as observed for the RNA-primed template. Note the high misincorporation rate of Polγ on the DNA-primed template. Primer extension assays were performed with Exo− Polγ to identify misincorporation of a non-cognate dNTP (C) and a cognate rNTP (D) when utilizing a DNA or RNA primer. (E) Primer extension assays were performed on a primer–template in the presence of limited dNTPs (0.1 mM dATP and dTTP) to extend the primer by only 5 nt. As expected, RNA primer extension by 1 nt was observed in the presence of dATP (Fig. 2B,left panel). In contrast, when extending a DNA primer, Polγ misincorporated up to four dAMP residues (Fig. 2B, right panel). Extension of a primer by up to six mismatched nucleotides has been previously observed in Pol A family of DNA polymerases [40]. Moreover, Polγ exhibited significantly reduced misincorporation when extending an RNA primer, as it did not readily incorporate a non-cognate dCMP (Fig. 2C, left panel) or a cognate ribonucleotide (rAMP) (Fig. 2D, left panel). In contrast, efficient DNA primer extension occurred with non-cognate dCTP (nearly complete, Fig. 2C, right panel) and cognate rATP (∼50%, Fig. 2D, right panel).

Polγ-B has been shown to decrease the holoenzyme’s fidelity [41] and contribute to replisome function [42], however, these studies did not examine replication initiation and, more specifically, extension of an RNA primer. Given Polγ’s higher stability and reduced misincorporation on RNA-primed templates, we next tested whether Polγ-B was strictly required for primer extension during the initial stages of replication initiation. In the presence of Polγ-B, both WT and Exo− Polγ-A were able to extend RNA and DNA primers (Fig. 2E, lanes 2,4,7,and 9). Interestingly, we found that Polγ-B was absolutely required for replication initiation, as neither WT Polγ-A nor Exo− Polγ-A could efficiently extend an RNA primer (Fig. 2E, lanes 3 and 5). However, both variants of the holoenzyme were able to extend a DNA primer under the same conditions, though extensive DNA primer degradation was observed in the case of WT Polγ-A (Fig. 2E, lanes 8 and 10).

To further assess the increased stability and reduced misincorporation of RNA-primed Polγ complexes, we compared the rates of in situ misincorporation in complexes assembled on a DNA primer versus those assembled on an RNA primer following the incorporation of three cognate dNTPs (Fig. 2F). As shown above (Fig. 2C, lanes 4–8), the DNA20 primer readily misincorporates dGMP after incorporating three cognate dGMP residues (Fig. 2F, lane 2). High misincorporation rates were also observed when the RNA20 primer first incorporated three cognate dGMPs to convert to a chimeric 23-nt RNA/DNA product, which then misincorporated an additional residue (Fig. 2F, lane 4). This contrasts with the earlier observation (Fig. 2C, lanes 1–4), where no misincorporation was detected during extension of the RNA20 primer in the presence of a non-cognate nucleotide, underscoring the importance of Polγ-A interactions with the 2′-OH groups of the first three 3′-terminal nucleotides of the RNA primer, as revealed by structural studies (Fig. 1F).

Polγ possesses intrinsic strand-displacement activity

Previous studies have demonstrated that Exo− Polγ, but not the WT enzyme, possesses strand-displacement abilities [22, 23, 25], however, it is not known whether the nature of the primer (RNA versus DNA) influences this function. We therefore examined Polγ replication activity using a “fork” primer–template construct containing either an RNA or DNA primer (Fig. 3A and B). The construct features a 28 bp RNA–DNA duplex, a gap of four nucleotides to promote loading of Polγ [22], and a single-stranded DNA “flap” of 40 nt at the 5′ end of the non-template strand of DNA to enable loading of TWINKLE, if required.

(A) Primer extension assay using increasing concentrations of WT or Exo− Polγ, performed at 37°C on an RNA-primed fork (RNA28/DNA28TS/40T_20DuplexNT). (B) Primer extension assay using increasing concentrations of WT or Exo− Polγ, performed at 37°C on a DNA-primed fork (DNA28/DNA28TS/40T_20DuplexNT). Note a non-templated nucleotide addition by Exo− Polγ. (C) Replication assay performed with 20 nM DNA template, 20 nM WT Polγ in the absence and presence of increasing amounts of WT TWINKLE (lanes 3–5), or K421A TWINKLE (lanes 6–8). (D) DNA primer extension using WT or Exo− Polγ performed at 22°C, 32°C, and 37°C on a DNA-primed fork (DNA28/DNA28TS/40T_20DuplexNT).

At a physiologically relevant temperature (37°C) and dNTP concentration (20 μM), neither WT nor Exo− Polγ was able to efficiently unwind the downstream DNA duplex when using an RNA primer (Fig. 3A). Strand-displacement activity by Exo− Polγ was observed only when the enzyme was present at 5- to 10-fold molar excess over DNA substrate (Fig. 3A, lanes 8 and 9). In contrast, both WT and Exo− Polγ displayed strand-displacement abilities when utilizing a DNA primer (Fig. 3B), consistent with recent single-molecule studies [25].

We next probed whether the addition of TWINKLE to the RNA–DNA–Polγ complex can enable replication initiation (Fig. 3C). As expected, in the presence of all dNTPs, WT Polγ was able to extend the RNA primer only in the presence of WT TWINKLE, as a complete extension of the primer to generate a run-off product was observed, indicating efficient strand separation and extension by the Polγ–TWINKLE complex (Fig. 3C, lanes 3–5). Consistently, when the ATP hydrolysis-deficient variant of TWINKLE was used, K421A TWINKLE [43], Polγ could not extend the primer through the duplex region of the template (Fig. 3C, lanes 6–8). In contrast to the rolling-circle replication system, mtSSB is not needed when utilizing a short fork substrate (Supplementary Fig. S3A and B), agreeing with previous studies [24].

Because prior reports have indicated that WT Polγ lacks efficient strand-displacement activity [23, 24, 42], we explored whether the observed activity is temperature-dependent on the given fork construct (Fig. 3D). Indeed, at 22°C neither WT nor Exo− Polγ was able to extend the DNA primer to the end of the template (“run-off”) (Fig. 3D, lanes 2 and 3). However, at 32°C, Exo− Polγ extended most of the primer to run-off, whereas WT Polγ remained largely unable to unwind the downstream duplex (Fig. 3D, lanes 5 and 6), consistent with previously reported data [23].

Finally, we repeated the strand-displacement assay using a construct previously employed by Falkenberg and coworkers to examine Polγ gap-filling and strand-displacement activities (Supplementary Fig. S3C). This substrate contains a 30-bp downstream duplex region and a 20-nt gap between the 3′ end of the primer and the 5′ end of the non-template strand [23]. As in the experiments described above, WT Polγ efficiently filled the gap at 22°C and 32°C but exhibited robust strand-displacement activity only at 37°C (Supplementary Fig. S3C).

Structural basis for strand displacement by Polγ

To understand the mechanism of strand-displacement activity of human Polγ, we used a strategy previously applied successfully to its yeast homolog, Mip1, in which productive DNA synthesis was used to drive the polymerase from the primer–template junction into the downstream duplex, thereby promoting strand displacement during active DNA synthesis (Fig. 4A) [27]. However, unlike Cryo-EM studies of Mip1-driven strand separation, in which four major 3D classes representing consecutive steps of DNA displacement were identified, we detected only one major 3D class with human Polγ relevant to the process (Fig. 4B, Supplementary Figs S4 and S5, and Supplementary Tables S1 and S2). This class yielded a 2.9 Å structure that captures the strand-displacement state.

The structure reveals that, as expected, the DNA primer was extended by four nucleotides and that the next incoming substrate, dATP, is accommodated in the insertion site of Polγ. Although the starting DNA construct contained fully double-stranded downstream DNA, the cryo-EM maps lack density for the non-template strand. Instead, the space normally occupied by the non-template bases at positions n + 1, n + 2, and n + 3 is filled by structural elements of Polγ: a previously unresolved loop in the exonuclease domain termed here the GP loop (residues 335–346), named for the conserved G339–P340 motif, and the strand-displacement (SD) loop (residues 1083–1092), an element implicated in strand displacement in the Mip1 DNA polymerase [27] (Fig. 4C and D). The absence of ordered density for the non-template strand near the active site, together with the clear positioning of the downstream template strand and the occupation of the expected non-template strand path by Polγ structural elements, is consistent with displacement of the non-template strand in the captured complex. Notably, the “catcher,” a structural element previously implicated in strand displacement by Mip1, becomes better resolved in the cryo-EM map only in this strand-displacement structure and is positioned adjacent to the downstream DNA, supporting its role in the intrinsic strand-displacement activity of human Polγ (Fig. 4C and D).

The organization of the downstream template strand and the absence of ordered density for the displaced non-template strand near the active site are consistent with a locally melted downstream duplex. This conformation suggests that the Polγ structure captures a late strand-displacement state, similar to the late intermediate observed in Mip1[27], in which the GP loop and SD loop occupy the expected path of the non-template strand and are positioned to prevent reannealing of the DNA strands. This likely involves insertion of residues E1090 and E1091 of the SD loop, the bulge formed by the invariant in mammalian species residues G339 and P340 of the GP loop (Fig. 4E) and the non-conserved residue R338 of the GP loop into the space normally occupied by the non-template strand (Fig. 4D). In contrast, the universally conserved in eukaryotes residue F1092 of the SD loop, which plays a key role in Mip1 strand-displacement activity (Fig. 4E), does not appear to directly participate in strand displacement in the human Polγ structure obtained here (Fig. 4D). Notably, the wedge helix (residues 309–316), a structural element implicated in the intrinsic strand-displacement activity of Mip1, appears to be positioned too far from the downstream DNA in the Polγ complex to play a direct role in strand displacement.

The catcher subdomain of human Polγ, which is inserted within the mobile fingers domain, is shifted into close proximity to the downstream DNA, similar to the movement of the corresponding loop observed during strand displacement in Mip1 [27]. In this position, the catcher is well placed to engage the downstream duplex and stabilize the displaced DNA configuration. Positively charged residues K990, R993, R1026, R1030, and R1047 are oriented toward the phosphate backbone of the downstream DNA, suggesting that electrostatic interactions may help anchor the DNA and maintain the geometry required for strand separation (Fig. 4D). Thus, the catcher subdomain appears to act together with the SD and GP loops to support the strand-displacement state by stabilizing the downstream DNA and limiting reannealing of the non-template strand.

To probe the roles of these structural elements in intrinsic strand displacement, we generated Polγ variants containing deletions or substitutions of the key residues within the proposed strand-displacement module. To test the contribution of the GP loop, we deleted two central residues, G339-P340, generating the ΔGP variant. These residues are positioned to clash with the non-template DNA strand during displacement. In the SD loop, we introduced the E1090A/E1091A substitutions to assess the contribution of these acidic residues to strand displacement. Because F849 plays an important role in strand displacement in yeast Mip1, we also generated the corresponding F1092A variant to evaluate its role in human Polγ. Finally, we deleted the catcher subdomain residues 998–1044 and reconnected the remaining segments with a three-glycine linker (Supplementary Fig. S6).

We first examined the strand-displacement activity of Polγ variant containing mutations in GP and SD loops (Fig. 5A–D and Supplementary Fig. S6). All these mutants retained catalytic activity, as evidenced by nearly complete extension of a 30-nt primer on templates with a single-stranded downstream region (Supplementary Fig. S6, B–D). In contrast, although ΔGP Polγ efficiently filled the 20-nt gap on a template containing a partially double-stranded downstream region, its ability to displace the non-template DNA strand was reduced by ~2.5-fold, consistent with the role of this structural element predicted by our structural observations (Fig. 5A and E). This phenotype was also evident at the lower end of the physiological dNTP concentration range (Fig. 5B). In contrast, mutations in the SD-loop had only limited effect on strand displacement: F1092A retained ~50% of WT activity, whereas E1090A/E1091A showed marginal reduction in strand-displacement activity (Fig. 5A,C and E).

Deletion of the catcher subdomain produced the most severe defect in strand-displacement activity (Fig. 5D,E). Similar to the other Polγ variants, the catcher-deletion variant efficiently extended the primer on single-stranded DNA templates (Supplementary Fig. S6D), however, its strand-displacement activity was reduced by approximately five-fold on templates containing a downstream double-stranded DNA region (Fig. 5D,E). Because the catcher subdomain is positioned to interact with downstream DNA, we next tested whether its deletion affected DNA binding using EMSA. Kd(app) measurements revealed an approximately 2-fold reduction in binding of the catcher-deletion variant to the primer–template DNA (Supplementary Fig. S6E,F). These results suggest that the reduced strand-displacement activity of this variant is at least partly explained by impaired DNA binding.

Finally, we examined how mutations in the strand-displacement module of Polγ affect enzyme’s processive DNA synthesis using an M13 primer-extension assay (Fig. 5G–I). A short 25-nt primer was annealed to single-stranded M13 DNA (∼7 kb), and Polγ was allowed to synthesize the complementary strand, thereby converting the phage DNA into a double-stranded form. The E1090A/E1091A and ΔGP variants showed processivity comparable to WT Polγ on primed M13 DNA (Fig. 5H and I). In contrast, processivity was severely reduced for both the F1092A and catcher-deletion variants (Fig. 5H and I).

Discussion

Previous in vitro studies of mitochondrial replication have used rolling-circle substrate systems containing DNA primers to examine the functional interplay between TWINKLE and Polγ [15, 44–46]. However, replication systems that use RNA primers—the physiological substrate for replication initiation—have yet to be utilized for mitochondrial replication studies. The RNA-primed fork substrate used here directly models the physiological substrate used during replication initiation and is fully compatible with structural methods. The network of interactions observed in the Polγ structure is specific to ribonucleotides in the primer and likely contributes to the increased stability, and decreased misincorporation during initial DNA synthesis observed in our biochemical experiments (Figs 1 and 2).

In Pol A family DNA polymerases, discrimination between correct and mismatched base pairs is strongly influenced by active-site residues that monitor the primer terminus [40, 47]. In Polγ, R853 is positioned to interact with the n − 1 nucleotide of the primer (Fig. 1F) and has been implicated in mechanism of substrate selection [12, 40]. Recent structural and biochemical studies showed that R853 coordinates an active-site interaction network in Polγ and contacts the sugar moiety of the incoming nucleotide, thereby promoting correct reactant alignment, Watson–Crick base pairing, and active-site closure [48]. In our RNA–DNA Polγ structure, R853 is positioned to form an additional interaction with the 2′-OH group of the n − 1 ribose, while H1134 contacts the same ribose region (Fig. 1F). Together with additional 2′-OH-dependent contacts involving the upstream RNA nucleotides, these interactions stabilize the primer terminus in the product site and provide a structural basis for the reduced misincorporation observed during RNA-primed synthesis (Fig. 2). Consistent with this idea, studies of human Polα have shown that primer structure can influence polymerase active-site geometry and fidelity during RNA-primed DNA synthesis [49].

Our data also clarify an important issue in the mtDNA replication field. Previous biochemical studies concluded that human Polγ lacks significant intrinsic strand-displacement activity and requires TWINKLE to synthesize through duplex DNA [22–24]. Our results show that, under physiological temperature and nucleotide conditions, Polγ can displace a downstream non-template strand in vitro. A recent study reported that intrinsic strand-displacement activity of human Polγ is most efficient at physiological Mg²⁺ concentrations [50]. Thus, the combined structural and biochemical data demonstrate that human Polγ has intrinsic strand-displacement activity on model DNA substrates.

By capturing Polγ during strand-displacement synthesis, we identified structural elements that stabilize the separated DNA strands and prevent reannealing of the displaced non-template strand. Classification of the cryo-EM dataset revealed a single well-defined catalytic state (Figs 4A and 6A). This state most closely resembles the late strand-displacement intermediate previously observed in yeast Mip1, in which three downstream base pairs are separated (Fig. 6B) [27]. In the Mip1 study, several conformations along the strand-displacement pathway were captured within the same dataset, including complexes with intact downstream DNA, with one downstream base pair melted, and with three downstream base pairs melted. In the late Mip1 intermediate, the downstream duplex is kinked between the n + 3 and n + 4 positions, a distortion that likely promotes melting of three base pairs (Fig. 6B).

The human Polγ strand-displacement complex shows a similar kink in the downstream template strand between the n + 3 and n + 4 positions, supporting the interpretation that the captured state corresponds to a complex with at least three downstream base pairs separated (Fig. 6A). Because the displaced non-template strand is not resolved beyond the separation point, the exact number of melted base pairs cannot be determined. However, Polγ structural elements occupy the space normally taken by the non-template strand, indicating that this conformation would prevent reannealing and maintain the downstream duplex in a locally melted state.

The overall organization of the human Polγ strand-displacement module is related to that of yeast Mip1, with the SD loop and wedge helix adopting a similar spatial arrangement (Fig. 6). However, the elements that appear to drive strand separation differ between the two enzymes. In Mip1, the SD loop and wedge helix are the principal strand-separation elements. Conserved residues in these regions, including F849, N847, and R265, clash with the n + 1 base during the initial stage of strand displacement and contribute directly to duplex melting [27]. In human Polγ, the corresponding SD loop is positioned near the separation point and may contribute to this process, but our structural and biochemical data indicate that its role is limited. Mutations of F1092 and E1090/E1091 produced only mild effects on strand-displacement activity (Fig. 5). In addition, the wedge helix in human Polγ is shorter, and the conserved R309 residue is not positioned to directly promote strand separation, in contrast to the arrangement observed in Mip1 (Fig. 6).

Instead, human Polγ appears to use an additional loop within the exonuclease domain as a major strand-separation element. This GP loop is significantly shortened in yeast Mip1 and lacks the GP motif, but is conserved in mammalian Polγ, suggesting that mammalian enzymes have evolved a distinct structural element to support strand displacement (Fig. 6). In the human Polγ strand-displacement complex, the GP loop extends toward the downstream duplex and is positioned to clash with the n + 1, n + 2, and n + 3 bases of the displaced non-template strand. This placement makes the GP loop a strong candidate for maintaining the separated state of the downstream duplex and preventing reannealing during synthesis. Indeed, deletion of the central G339–P340 residues of the GP loop reduced strand-displacement activity while preserving efficient gap filling and primer extension on single-stranded templates (Fig. 5).

Deletion of the catcher subdomain produced the strongest defect in strand-displacement activity and also caused a marked decrease in processive DNA synthesis on single-stranded M13 DNA (Fig. 5). These results indicate that the catcher subdomain plays an important role in stabilizing the downstream DNA during synthesis. In the captured human Polγ structure, the catcher is not fully engaged with DNA, unlike what has been observed in the yeast Mip1 complex [27]. However, this region is expected to be flexible and to undergo repeated cycles of engagement and disengagement with the downstream DNA during polymerase translocation. Thus, the catcher subdomain likely forms transient contacts with the DNA backbone during Polγ translocation and strand displacement. Together, the structural and biochemical data identify the GP loop, SD loop, and catcher subdomain as components of the human Polγ strand-displacement module, with the GP loop and catcher subdomain making the dominant functional contributions to intrinsic strand-displacement synthesis.

The intrinsic strand-displacement activity of Polγ may be relevant to mitochondrial long-patch base-excision repair (LP-BER). In this pathway, limited strand-displacement synthesis by Polγ could generate a short 5′ flap for subsequent processing by mitochondrial nucleases prior to ligation. The strand-displacement elements identified here may therefore also contribute to localized DNA synthesis during mtDNA repair.

Although our data demonstrate that Polγ has intrinsic strand-displacement activity on model DNA substrates, these findings should not be interpreted to mean that Polγ acts independently of TWINKLE during mtDNA replication in vivo. TWINKLE is likely required to increase processivity, coordinate leading-strand synthesis, and support efficient progression through long duplex regions of mtDNA. Indeed, our RNA-primed replication–initiation assays show that Polγ critically depends on TWINKLE during the early stages of replication when the primer is RNA rather than DNA (Fig. 3). Whether this intrinsic activity is used during mitochondrial replication, LP-BER, or both in vivo, remains to be determined. Future studies should focus on reconstituting higher-order mitochondrial replisome assemblies containing Polγ, TWINKLE, mtSSB, and physiologically relevant RNA- and DNA-primed fork substrates. Such studies will be essential for defining how the intrinsic strand-displacement activity of Polγ is integrated with helicase-driven unwinding during mitochondrial genome replication.
