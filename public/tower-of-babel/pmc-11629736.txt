Fast and simple fluorometric measurement of phloem loading exposes auxin‐dependent regulation of Arabidopsis sucrose transporter AtSUC2
Authors: Yunjuan Ren, Ziyu Zhang, Diana Zhanakhmetova, Wenhui Li, Shaolin Chen, Tomáš Werner, Johannes Liesche
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYThe rate of sucrose export from leaves is a major factor in balancing whole‐plant carbon and energy partitioning. A comprehensive study of its dynamics and relationship to photosynthesis, sink demand, and other relevant processes is hampered by the shortcomings of current methods for measuring sucrose phloem loading. We utilize the ability of sucrose transporter proteins, known as SUCs or SUTs, to specifically transport the fluorescent molecule esculin in a novel assay to measure phloem loading rates. Esculin was administered to source leaves and its fluorescence in the leaf extract was measured after 1 or 2 h. Dicot plants with an active phloem loading strategy showed an export‐dependent reduction of esculin fluorescence. Relative leaf esculin export rates correlated with leaf export rates of isotopic carbon and phloem exudate sucrose levels. We used esculin experiments to examine the effects of phytohormones on phloem loading in Arabidopsis, showing, for example, that auxin induces phloem loading while cytokinin reduces it. Transcriptional regulation of AtSUC2 by AUXIN RESPONSE FACTOR1 (ARF1) corroborated the link between auxin signaling and phloem loading. Unlike established methods, the esculin assay is rapid and does not require specialized equipment. Potential applications and limitations of the esculin assay are discussed.

INTRODUCTION

Sucrose is the central unit of carbon and energy in most plant species (Lemoine, 2000; Smith & Stitt, 2007). As the primary product of photosynthesis, substantial amounts of sucrose are transported from leaves to carbon sink tissues. Transport takes place in the phloem sieve elements, in which the osmotic potential differences drive the mass flow of the sugary phloem sap from source to sink tissues (Münch, 1930). Accordingly, the transport rate primarily depends on the rate at which sucrose is loaded into, and unloaded from, the phloem sieve elements (Smith & Stitt, 2007; Srivastava, Ganesan, Ismail, & Ayre, 2009; Liesche & Patrick, 2017). Phloem unloading typically happens by diffusion through plasmodesmata connecting sieve elements and surrounding cells (Liesche and Patrick, 2017). In contrast, sucrose loading into the source phloem in most crop plants, and the model plant Arabidopsis thaliana, depends on active transport (Rennie & Turgeon, 2009). Sucrose diffuses via plasmodesmata toward the phloem parenchyma cells, from where these sugars are sequestered into the apoplast by SUGARS WILL EVENTUALLY BE EXPORTED TRANSPORTERS (SWEETs) before being taken up by SUCROSE TRANSPORTERS (SUTs or SUCs) into the sieve element‐companion cell complex (Chen et al., 2012). It has long been assumed that the activity of the phloem loading SUT is responsible for the dynamic regulation of whole‐plant carbon allocation according to leaf status and environmental conditions that have been observed in Arabidopsis, maize, and other plant species (Baker et al., 2016; Knox, Paterlini, Thomson, & Oparka, 2018). Indeed, mRNA levels of phloem loading SUTs were found to mirror changes in phloemloading activity induced by abiotic stress exposure (Durand et al., 2018; Knox et al., 2018; Xu, Chen, Yunjuan, Chen, & Liesche, 2018). However, no transcription factors that could link environmental conditions and SUT expression have been identified. Moreover, preliminary data indicates a finely grained, multi‐faceted landscape of post‐transcriptional regulation (Liesche et al., 2011; Xu & Liesche, 2021), but only a few examples have been validated (Xu et al., 2020). Besides SUTs, other proteins could directly influence the phloem loading rate, but their relevance for the regulation of leaf sugar export has not been investigated (Xu & Liesche, 2021). The lack of knowledge on the molecular mechanism of phloem loading regulation prevents an understanding of the various environmental influences on whole‐plant carbon allocation as well as the dynamic interplay of photosynthesis, carbon storage, and sugar transport.

The investigation of phloem loading regulation has been hindered by the lack of a simple and reliable method to measure phloem loading. Tracing of isotopic carbon after supplying it to a leaf as 14CO2 or 11CO2 can provide accurate values for assimilating export from leaves (Christy & Fisher, 1978; Madore & Lucas, 1987; Minchin & Thorpe, 2003). However, the required specialized equipment and difficulties associated with handling radioactive material limit the practicability of these approaches. Using the stable isotope 13C instead requires destructive sampling and analysis on specialized equipment (Dersch et al., 2016; Xu et al., 2018). The primary alternative has been EDTA‐facilitated exudation followed by measurement of sucrose concentrations. While this approach can be prone to artifacts (Liu, Chao, & Turgeon, 2012), it proved sufficiently reliable for comparative analysis of phloem loading activity (Xu et al., 2018). However, sample processing and measurements typically require 5–8 h (Xu, Ren, & Liesche, 2019).

Esculin, a fluorescent coumarin β‐glucoside, has been identified as a substrate for various SUTs (Gora, Reinders, & Ward, 2012; Sivitz et al., 2007). Esculin has been instrumental in characterizing SUT activity in protoplasts (Rottmann et al., 2018) and leaf epidermal cells (Xu et al., 2020). After its phloem mobility was demonstrated (Knoblauch et al., 2015), esculin has been used to measure phloem transport velocity in Arabidopsis seedlings (Knox et al., 2018). Here, we establish esculin fluorescence measurements as a tool for the relative measurement of phloem loading activity. We demonstrate the value of the method by testing different plant species for SUT‐mediated phloem loading and by investigating the hormone‐dependent regulation of phloem loading in Arabidopsis.

Hormones control many developmental programs and environmental responses that involve the adjustment of whole‐plant carbon allocation. For example, Arabidopsis drought stress responses, principally controlled by ABA and auxin, involve adjustment of the soluble sugar levels and sucrose transport in source and sink tissues (Hummel et al., 2010; Lemoine et al., 2013; Xu et al., 2018). During the shade avoidance response of potato plants, the phloem loading rate is adjusted by GA and ethylene signaling (Chincinska et al., 2008). Transcription factors or other signaling molecules that link hormone action with phloem loading of sucrose have not been identified.

Shedding light on how hormones affect sucrose transport contributes to a more comprehensive understanding of how plants perceive and respond to their environment. This knowledge can have practical implications for crop improvement, as it may inform strategies for optimizing plant growth, enhancing stress tolerance, and increasing agricultural productivity (Kanno et al., 2016; Min et al., 2014; Zhu et al., 2017). Furthermore, the esculin assay described here could enable a broad range of questions regarding whole‐plant carbon allocation to be addressed with increased efficiency, not only in model plants.

RESULTS

Esculin is exported from leaves in angiosperms with active phloem loading strategy

Esculin infiltrated on the abaxial side of a leaf (Figure 1A) led to strong increases in esculin fluorescence in leaf extracts of all tested plant species. In only a few species, such as tomato, spraying with esculin solution led to significant esculin uptake into the leaf. To be consistent, we chose infiltration as our standard procedure for esculin application.

Our hypothesis was that esculin in the leaf extract would decrease over time in species with active apoplasmic phloem loading strategies. Indeed, esculin fluorescence in leaf extracts that were obtained 2 h after infiltration was lower than esculin fluorescence in leaf extracts obtained 1 h after infiltration in the active apoplasmic loaders Arabidopsis, tomato, tobacco, and Vinca major (Figure 1B). SUT‐dependent esculin transport can be tested by co‐applying sucrose at high concentration, which reduces esculin transport due to substrate competition (Rottmann et al., 2018). When we co‐infiltrated esculin and sucrose, no significant reduction of esculin fluorescence between the 1 and 2 h samples was observed in Arabidopsis, tobacco, tomato, and V. major (Figure 1B). No significant reduction in esculin levels was also observed for plant species with phloem loading strategies that do not directly depend on SUT activity. The active symplasmic loader squash (Cucurbita pepo) and the passive symplasmic loader maple (Acer saccharum) did not show significant differences in esculin fluorescence in leaf extract after 1 and 2 h (Figure 1C). Despite being active apoplasmic loaders, wheat (Triticum aestivum) and maize (Zea mays) also did not show a change in esculin fluorescence with sampling time (Figure 1D). Monocot SUTs HvSUT1, ShSUT1, OsSUT1, and OsSUT5 were previously shown not to transport esculin (Sivitz et al., 2007; Sun, Reinders, LaFleur, Mori, & Ward, 2010). However, a certain esculin uptake capacity has been indicated for wheat transporters TaSUT1, TaSUT2, TaSUT3, TaSUT6, and TaSUT7 (Prasad, Jung, & Seo, 2023).

The results indicate that the infiltrated esculin is loaded into the phloem of angiosperm species with an active apoplasmic phloem loading strategy. Testing for esculin fluorescence in the extract of sink leaves in tomato showed an increase in fluorescence over time that supports this interpretation (Figure 1E).

Esculin‐based measurement of phloem loading

Can esculin export from leaves be used as a reliable indicator of phloem loading activity and, thereby, the leaf carbon export rate? To answer this question, we performed the esculin assay on Arabidopsis and S. lycopersicum in parallel with the conventionally used methods of measuring the sucrose content of leaf phloem exudate and measuring leaf 13C‐levels after pulsing with 13CO2 (Xu et al., 2018). In all experiments, values after 2 h were measured and the difference between control conditions and sample conditions was evaluated.

The measurements of phloem exudate sucrose and 13C‐export from leaves have previously indicated a positive association of phloem loading rate with light levels, as long as these remain below the stress‐inducing level (Xu et al., 2018). These results were validated in our experiments in Arabidopsis and tomato (Figure 2). The 13C‐content of the labeled source leaves, an indicator for the non‐exported carbon, scaled negatively with light intensity. The sucrose content of phloem exudate, an indicator for exported sucrose, showed a positive scaling relationship (Figure 2). Measurements of the esculin content of source leaves showed similar dynamics as the measurements of 13C‐content (Figure 2).

We used the fluorescent symplasmic tracer carboxyfluorescein‐diacetate (CFDA) as a control for SUT‐independent phloem loading (Han et al., 2022; Turgeon & Oparka, 2010; Zhang, Tolstikov, Turnbull, Hicks, & Fiehn, 2010). It was applied and its fluorescence was measured in the same way as esculin. In contrast to esculin, the light level had no significant influence on the CFDA content of Arabidopsis or tomato leaves 2 h after labeling (Figure 2D,H).

Investigating hormonal influences on phloem loading

We utilized the relative simplicity of the esculin assay for a systematic investigation of short‐term influences on the phloem loading of a wide range of hormones and their respective inhibitors. These substances were co‐infiltrated with esculin in Arabidopsis source leaves and the esculin fluorescence of leaf extract was measured after 2 h. A total of 165 tests were conducted across 16 treatment groups. Of the results, five values were excluded as outliers. To confirm the relevance of our measurements for phloem loading activity in the context of hormone applications, we performed parallel measurements of sucrose in phloem exudates. The expected strong negative correlation between esculin fluorescence of source leaf extract and phloem exudate sucrose content was detected (Figure 3A). The time needed to prepare 48 samples of phloem exudate and perform sugar measurements via HPLC was several days longer than esculin extraction and measurement of 165 samples on a fluorometer. The costs, too, were lower for the esculin assay compared to the phloem exudate sucrose measurements. With 8–16 samples for the esculin assay and three samples for exudate sucrose measurements, the average standard deviation of biological replicates normalized to the mean was 13% for the esculin assay and 19% for the exudate measurements.

Looking at the effects on esculin loading of the different hormones or their inhibitors indicates that some hormones increase phloem loading activity within the 2 h after treatment, while others induce a decrease or do not affect loading rates (Figure 3F). Significant increases of phloem loading activity were observed for the auxin indole‐acetic acid (IAA), the ethylene precursor ethephon, abscisic acid (ABA), jasmonic acid, strigolactone, and salicylic acid (Table 1, Figure 3F). A significant decrease was observed for the auxin transport inhibitor NPA, the GA3 synthesis‐inhibitor chlormequat chloride (CCC) as well as the cytokinins zeatin and kinetin (Table 1, Figure 3F).

As indicated in the correlation analysis, most hormones had similar effects on phloem loading, leaf soluble sugar content, and photosynthesis rate (Table 1). However, there were exceptions. Strigolactone and salicylic acid treatment were followed by a strong increase in phloem loading activity, but no significant change to soluble leaf sugar content and, in the case of salicylic acid, no increase in photosynthesis rate.

In the following, we corroborated the link between hormonal signaling and phloem loading activity by elucidating a molecular mechanism for the potential auxin‐dependent AtSUC2 regulation. We chose auxin because of the consistent effects of IAA and NPA on phloem loading (Figure 3F) and relatively well‐established signaling pathways.

AUXIN RESPONSE FACTOR 1 (ARF1) can bind to the AtSUC2 promoter

In Arabidopsis, a central role in determining the phloem loading rate has been ascribed to AtSUC2 (Gottwald et al., 2000). Checking for transcription factor binding sites in the AtSUC2 promoter (2000 basepairs upstream of the first exon) in the Plant Cis‐acting Regulatory DNA Elements (PLACE) database yielded several transcription regulator candidates with links to auxin signaling. Notably, the well‐established auxin response element (AuxRE, TGTCTC) for binding of AUXIN RESPONSE FACTORs (ARFs) is present in the AtSUC2 promoter at two positions (Figure S1). Since this motif is conserved in the promoters of phloem loading SUCs across a broad range of species (Liang et al., 2020; Figure S1), we continued to experimentally verify the binding capacity, focusing mainly on ARF1 and ARF5, the two primary candidates according to PLACE.

The yeast‐one‐hybrid assay, in which the leucine gene necessary for growth on selective medium was controlled by the AtSUC2 promoter, indicated that ARF1 and ARF5 can bind to the AtSUC2 promoter (Figure 4A). Growth of the cells coexpressing AtSUC2‐promoter and ARF combinations was similar to the positive control, while the negative control showed no growth (Figure 4A). In addition, we used luciferase reporter gene assays to verify that ARF1 and ARF5 can activate AtSUC2 transcription. The firefly luciferase (LUC), cloned behind the AtSUC2 promoter, was active when coexpressed in Nicotiana benthamiana leaves with ARF1 or ARF5, but showed only low activity when coexpressed with the empty vector control (Figure 4B). Without a promoter (“Empty:LUC” in Figure 4B), LUC signal was negligible. After normalization to internal control (activity of Renilla luciferase expressed from the same vector), a fourfold increase in LUC activity was detected for AtSUC2:LUC coexpression with ARF1 or ARF5 compared to AtSUC2:LUC coexpressed with the empty vector control (Figure 4C). Coexpression with a third ARF, ARF6, resulted in the similarly low activity of luciferase (Figure 4B). When quantified, the average intensity across experiments with ARF6 was significantly lower than the LUC background activity (Figure 4C).

The capacity for binding of ARF1 to the specific binding site (TGTCTC) of the AtSUC2 promoter was validated in vitro using electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) experiments. The combination of GST‐labeled ARF1 with the biotin‐labeled AuxRE‐DNA‐probe led to the band shift relative to the GST‐control (Figure 4D). The band's intensity decreased with the addition of unlabeled probe (“cold probe”) that competes with the labeled probe for binding (Figure 4E). Moreover, the band was diminished when a mutated AuxRE‐probe (TTTTTT) was used (Figure 4D).

Taken together, these results are consistent with the function of ARFs as direct regulators of AtSUC2 expression.

ARF1 is involved in the auxin response of phloem loading

Arabidopsis plants carrying different ARF1 frameshift mutations introduced by CRISPR/Cas9‐technology (Figure S2), showed slightly larger rosette diameters than wild‐type plants 3 weeks after germination (Figure 5A; Figure S3). The AtSUC2 mRNA level was reduced in one of the two arf1 mutant lines (Figure 5B). Notably, IAA treatment led to a much smaller increase of AtSUC2 mRNA levels in the mutant lines compared to wild‐type plants (Figure 5B). While the abundance of AtSUC2 mRNAs increased fourfold in the wild‐type, it increased by less than 2 times in the arf1 mutants (Figure 5B). Esculin assays indicated decreased phloem loading activity in arf1 mutant lines under control conditions compared to wild‐type plants (Figure 5C). Importantly, IAA treatment did not increase esculin export from leaves of the arf1 mutants as much as it did in wild‐type plants (Figure 5C). Whereas wild‐type plants exported about 50% more esculin within 2 h after IAA treatment, mutants exported, respectively, about 25% and 35% more than mock‐treated plants (Figure 5C).

In addition to the arf1 mutants, we also analyzed plant lines expressing ARF1 under the control of the constitutive 35S promoter (ARF1‐overexpressor). These plant lines showed smaller rosette size than wild‐type plants after 4 weeks (Figure 5D). AtSUC2 mRNA levels were strongly increased in leaves of ARF1‐overexpressor lines, and appeared to scale with ARF1 mRNA level (Figure 5E). Esculin phloem loading was increased in ARF1‐overexpressors compared to wild‐type plants (Figure 5F).

DISCUSSION

Sucrose phloem loading in source leaves is a crucial aspect of whole‐plant metabolism. Therefore, its measurement is highly relevant for a broad range of scientific inquiries (Turgeon, 2010). However, there has been a lack of convenient and cost‐effective methods to measure phloem loading activity that would allow high‐throughput analysis in non‐specialized laboratories (Gersony, McClelland, & Holbrook, 2021). Esculin is a fluorescent coumarin glucoside that has become popular as a sucrose analog because it is specifically transported by SUTs, including the phloem loading SUTs of angiosperms (Knoblauch et al., 2015; Knox et al., 2018; Nieberl et al., 2017; Rottmann et al., 2018). This study demonstrates that the application of esculin can aid in the investigation of phloem loading.

The transport of esculin out of source leaves was observed only in active apoplasmic loading species. That means that our method can be used to quickly identify active apoplasmic loading in species whose loading type was previously unknown. The broad application could supplement our rudimentary understanding of the evolution of phloem loading (Davidson, Keller, & Turgeon, 2011; Liesche, 2017). However, the method appears to be limited to eudicots and its suitability might even vary within this group. Phloem loading SUTs in monocots are phylogenetically different from those in eudicots (Reinders, Sivitz, & Ward, 2012) and the inability of monocot SUTs to transport esculin has been observed in heterologous expression systems for barley HvSUT1, sugarcane ShSUT1, and rice OsSUT1 (Sivitz et al., 2007; Sun et al., 2010). While wheat TaSUT1‐mediated import of esculin into protoplasts has been indicated recently (Prasad et al., 2023), these experiments lacked appropriate controls. The phloem‐loading sucrose transporter GhSUT1‐L2 from cotton, a dicot with active apoplasmic phloem loading strategy, showed only minor esculin transport activity when expressed in yeast cells and cotton leaves did not accumulate esculin in the veins (Yadav et al. 2022). Accordingly, the suitability of the esculin assay needs to be established empirically in uncharacterized plants.

The direct comparison of esculin experiments with established methods to measure phloem loading in Arabidopsis and tomato validated the usefulness of esculin assays for generating relative data on phloem loading activity. While the trends are consistent across methods, their dynamic range varies considerably. One reason could be, that the three methods all measure different parameters: (i) the export of esculin from leaves, (ii) the export of isotopic carbon from leaves, some of which is not built into carbohydrates destined for export, and (iii) a change in exudate sucrose content, which is an indicator of phloem sap sucrose content (Liu et al., 2012). The esculin loading dynamics could differ from sucrose loading dynamics due to a lower esculin‐affinity of SUTs (see Figure S11 in Xu et al., 2020) or attachment of esculin to intracellular structures (see Figure 5I in Xu et al., 2020). However, our experiments showed a higher responsiveness to environmental conditions than 13C‐export measurements, indicating that the influence of SUC affinity and esculin attachment is limited. Exudate sucrose content showed surprisingly strong variation under different light levels, falling down to 20% of the maximum while esculin export from leaves only fell to 50%. The measurements on exudate might generally underestimate sucrose concentrations as some sucrose is broken down into hexoses during the 4–5‐h‐long sample collection time (Liu et al., 2012; Xu et al., 2019). Moreover, samples contain an unknown level of hexoses and sucrose originating from non‐phloem tissues (Liu et al., 2012).

A comparison of esculin assay and exudate analysis illustrates the advantages of the esculin assay: relatively high sensitivity and utilization of common equipment. While it also has a low time requirement, some species or leaf types might require a relatively high number of repetitions because of high sample‐to‐sample variation. For Arabidopsis, we typically pooled leaf extracts from two to four leaves for esculin measurements to reduce leaf‐to‐leaf variation within a plant. For other plants, measurements on single leaves were feasible. Obtaining enough exudate for reliable sucrose concentration measurements on a well‐optimized HPLC requires the pooling of about 10 Arabidopsis leaves (Xu et al., 2019). Typically, this means collecting leaves from more than one plant. Esculin extraction takes about 1 h and fluorescence measurement only adds a few minutes to the total analysis time. This compares favorably to the several hours of exudate collection followed by another 30 min to 2 h of sucrose measurement. The advantage of not requiring specialized equipment counts mainly in comparison to isotope‐based methods, as exudate sucrose measurements could be performed via simple enzymatic experiments (Chincinska et al., 2008). It should be noted that esculin fluorescence can be conducted even without a fluorometer. We have successfully used a fluorescence microscope for this purpose. However, this requires some careful adjustment of the focus to ensure comparability across samples, for example, through the addition of a bead or fiber.

The usefulness of the esculin assay was demonstrated here through our systematic analysis of hormone effects. The results point to several hormones playing a role in regulating the rate of phloem loading in Arabidopsis. The auxin effect on esculin export from leaves that we observed matches with previous studies that reported stimulation of assimilate transport in the phloem of Phaesolus vulgaris by IAA (Patrick & Steains, 1987) and increased transcript abundance of StSUT1 in potato source leaves after 2,4‐d application (Harms, Wöhner, Schulz, & Frommer, 1994). Results for ethylene and the ethylene response inhibitor AgNO3 similarly matched expectations based on the observation of ethephon‐treated potato plants having higher StSUT1 transcript abundance and higher phloem exudate sucrose levels (Chincinska et al., 2008).

For GA, however, there is some discrepancy between the literature and our results. GA was shown to enhance sucrose phloem loading in potato (Chincinska et al., 2008) and Vicia faba (Aloni, Wyse, & Griffith, 1986). While we found esculin export from leaves to be reduced by the GA‐inhibitor CCC, it was also slightly, but significantly, reduced by GA3 (Figure 3F). The difference between our results in Arabidopsis and experiments in potato and V. faba is unlikely to be due to technical issues of the esculin assay as our plants also displayed reduced photosynthesis rate, leaf sucrose levels and phloem exudate sucrose levels after GA3 treatment.

Our simple experimental analysis of hormone effects on esculin export from leaves serves as a first indicator of the hormonal regulation of phloem loading. We selected auxin signaling to explore a potential molecular link between hormone action and AtSUC2 activity in Arabidopsis. Auxins, including IAA, are major regulators of plant growth and development, with most responses being mediated by transcriptional regulators (Roosjen, Paque, & Weijers, 2018). ARFs are one of the major families of transcription factors that regulate the expression of auxin‐responsive genes. They play a crucial role in auxin signal transduction by specifically binding to auxin response elements (AuxRE) in the promoter regions of target genes, activating or repressing their expression (Guilfoyle & Hagen, 2007). We focused on ARFs due to the presence of conserved AuxREs in the AtSUC2 promoter. SUC gene regulation by an ARF transcription factor has been previously shown in Rosa hybrida for RhARF7 and RhSUC2 during petal abscission (Liang et al., 2020).

We used three methods to confirm the binding capacity of ARFs to the AtSUC2 promoter. These indicated that ARF1, ARF5, and ARF6 can principally regulate AtSUC2 transcriptional activity. Which of these ARFs, or others not considered here, are relevant for AtSUC2 regulation in planta remains to be determined. We focused on ARF1, which has been originally characterized as a transcription repressor based on protoplast experiments (Ulmasov, Hagen, & Guilfoyle, 1999). However, our promoter‐binding assays indicated that ARF1 can activate AtSUC2. Moreover, arf1 mutants showed a diminished auxin response of AtSUC2 mRNA levels and esculin loading, while ARF1‐overexpressor plants showed increased AtSUC2 mRNA levels and increased esculin loading rates. There have been indications that the same ARF can act as an activator and repressor. For example, ARF7 and ARF19, which were originally classified as activators (Ulmasov et al., 1999) were suggested to act on certain target genes as repressors (Okushima et al., 2005). Similarly, ARF1 might act as a transcriptional activator of AtSUC2 while repressing other target genes. However, the apparent straightforwardness of our results does not exclude the possibility of complexity, for example regarding cofactor action (Freire‐Rios et al., 2020).

A previous study from our lab showed that post‐translationally regulated AtSUC2 activity positively correlated with plant growth (Xu et al., 2020). However, the relationship between AtSUC2 transcriptional regulation and Arabidopsis growth appears more complex. Three‐week‐old arf1 mutants were significantly larger than wild‐type control plants despite having similar, or lower AtSUC2 mRNA levels. Three‐week‐old ARF1‐overexpressor lines were smaller than wild‐type plants despite higher AtSUC2 mRNA levels. These results are in agreement with results from Arabidopsis plants overexpressing AtSUC2 under the control of a general or companion cell‐specific promoter. These AtSUC2‐overexpressors showed increased phloem loading, but reduced growth, mainly due to a perceived phosphate limitation (Dasgupta et al., 2014; Lei et al., 2011). Our observation corroborates Dasgupta et al.'s conclusion that AtSUC2 transcriptional activity can be uncoupled from productivity in Arabidopsis (Dasgupta et al., 2014). However, it remains to be determined if such a situation occurs under physiological conditions. Moreover, regarding ARF1‐related phenotypes, it should be considered that the ARF1 function is partly redundant, as indicated previously (Ellis et al., 2005; Li, Johnson, Stepanova, Alonso, & Ecker, 2004) and that our IAA treatments only partly abolished the phloem loading response. Moreover, ARF1 has many target genes that could have an influence on AtSUC2 activity themselves, and other auxin‐responsive transcription factors and co‐regulation with other hormones can play a role, too. It should also be noted that auxin could affect AtSUC2‐facilitated transport rates via alteration of the trans‐membrane proton gradient. Auxin is known to influence proton pump (ARABIDOPSIS H + ‐ATPASE, AHA) activity through post‐translational modifications, although this has not been shown for the companion‐cell specific AHA3 (Haruta, Gray, & Sussman, 2015; Miao, Russinova, & Rodriguez, 2022).

In conclusion, our study illustrates the value of the esculin‐based method for investigating the regulation of phloem loading in dicots. Due to its relative simplicity, it is broadly applicable and should enable the medium‐ to high‐throughput studies that are necessary to uncover the molecular regulation of a process that is integral to plant function (Küstner, Nägele, & Heyer, 2019).

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Plant growth conditions

Arabidopsis seeds (A. thaliana ecotype Col‐0) were planted on a 1:1 mixture of soil (Nature'sgro, Scotts Miracle‐Gro Company, Marysville, OH, USA) with vermiculite (Pindstrup, Midtjylland, Denmark) after seed sterilization and vernalization. Arabidopsis plants were grown in growth chambers at 16 h light/8 h dark periods, with light levels of 200 μmol photons m−2 s−1 and a constant temperature of 21°C. Experiments were carried out on 4‐week‐old (rosette‐stage) Arabidopsis plants. Tobacco (N. benthamiana), tomato (Solanum lycopersicum cv Micro‐Tom), and maize (Z. mays cv inbred line B73) were grown in growth chambers at 350 μmol photons m−2 s−1 irradiation, 16 h light/8 h dark periods, and temperatures of 25°C. Tomato and tobacco plants were used 28 days after germination and maize after 10 days. Wheat (T. aestivum L. cv Chinese spring) seedlings were grown on soil at 12°C in a growth chamber with 500 μmol photons m−2 s−1 and a 16‐h‐light/8‐h‐dark photoperiod. Experiments were carried out on wheat plants 10 days after germination. V. major and C. pepo were cultivated in a greenhouse with 14 h of light and 10 h of darkness at the temperature of 22–25°C. V. major plants were used in experiments at 6 week‐old and C. pepo 4‐week‐old. All plants were watered daily to keep the soil humid. A. saccharum experiments were carried out on 10 m‐high trees grown on the campus of Northwest A&F University (34°16′59″N, 108°04′30 ″ E).

For experiments on light acclimation of phloem loading in Arabidopsis, light levels were adjusted to darkness (0 μmol photons m−2 s−1), low light (100 μmol photons m−2 s−1), and high light (400 μmol photons m−2 s−1), while all other parameters remained unchanged. For experiments on tomato, light levels were darkness, low light (150 μmol photons m−2 s−1), and high light (550 μmol photons m−2 s−1).

Measurement of 13C‐assimilate export from leaves

Large source leaves of 4‐week‐old Arabidopsis plants and r4‐week‐old tomato plants were selected for labeling. A plastic bag was sealed around the leaf. Inside the bag, 13CO2 gas was generated by mixing 50 mg of NaH13CO3 (99% 13C, Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) with 100 μL of saturated citric acid using a syringe. The bag was taken off 30 min after labeling and plants were transferred to the respective light conditions. After 2 h, the labeled leaves were harvested and dried in an oven at 75°C for 100–120 h. The samples were ground to a fine powder in a mortar. The powder was shipped to Huake Stable Isotope Laboratory (Shenzhen, China), which carried out the 13C‐measurement on an isotope ratio mass spectrometer (Delta V Advantage, ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

Measurement of phloem exudate sugar concentrations

Phloem exudate was sampled according to Xu et al. (2019). Source leaves were cut off at the petiole and immediately placed in a petri dish containing 20 mM EDTA solution so that the petioles were submerged. While submerged, petioles were re‐cut at about 1 mm. For one sample, 20 leaves from three to four plants were collected. After 1 h in a dark and humid environment, the leaves were rinsed with distilled water and transferred to a single test tube containing 1 mL of distilled water. After 5 h of incubation in a dark and humid chamber, leaves were carefully removed and the solution filtered through a 0.22 μm filter. If necessary, samples were stored at −80 °C. The exudate sugar concentration was measured on an ion chromatography system (Dionex ICS 5000A+, ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) using a Carbo Pac™ PA10 column (4 × 250 mm, ThermoFisher Scientific), 200 mM NaOH solution as mobile phase, and a flow rate of 0.5 mL min−1. Chromatographic grade standards of glucose, fructose, sucrose, and maltose (Solarbio, Beijing, China) were used at five concentrations each. The instrument software Chromeleon 7 (ThermoFisher Scientific) was used to draw standard curves and calculate the sugar concentrations.

Measurement of phloem loading with esculin assay

Esculin solution was freshly prepared by diluting 17 mg esculin powder (TCI, Tokyo, Japan) in 50 mL of distilled water for a 1 mM concentration. Esculin solution was infiltrated into leaves through the abaxial epidermis using a 1 mL or a 5 mL syringe without a needle (Figure 1A). Solution was infiltrated until it filled the intercellulars of ≥95% of the leaf lamina, as indicated by the darker color of the wet leaf (Figure S4). For Arabidopsis, V. major and wheat leaves, this typically required about 0.5 mL of esculin solution per leaf. For tobacco leaves, we applied about 1 mL, for maize about 1.5 mL, for A. saccharum about 2 mL, and for leaves of C. pepo about 3 mL of esculin solution. We generally chose the largest leaves on a plant. Consistent infiltration can contribute to a low sample‐to‐sample variation. In this regard, users should be aware of potential differences in infiltration amounts that could be caused by differences in leaf anatomy and, potentially, stomata opening. For plants with small leaves (<5 cm2), several leaves of the same individual were pooled for sampling. For 3–4‐week‐old Arabidopsis plants, typically two to four leaves were labeled and pooled before extraction of esculin.

Two hours after the esculin application, the labeled leaves were cut off and transferred to 2 mL test tubes. Before freezing the samples in liquid nitrogen, they were weighed on a laboratory scale (PWC124, ADAM, Milton Keynes, UK). Samples were immediately crushed using a high‐flux tissue grinder (SCIENTZ‐48, Ningbo Scientz Biotechnology, Ningbo, China). After adding 1.5 mL MWC solution (60% methanol, 60% chloroform, and 15% water, v/v), samples were incubated in a water bath at 50°C for 30 min, followed by centrifuge for 20 min at 12 000 g. The supernatant was transferred to a new tube. For some species, samples required dilution with pure water to fall in the optimal range of the fluorometer. For example, 300 μL water was added to the 600 mL supernatant derived from a tomato source leaf. Esculin fluorescence in these samples was measured by transferring 200 μL into 500 μL disposable cuvettes in a fluorometer (Infinite M200 Pro, Tecan, Männedorf, Switzerland). The excitation wavelength was 335 nm and the emission detection range was 460 ± 5 nm. Fluorescence readings were normalized to leaf weight. Autofluorescence was not taken into account, because mock treatment tests showed very limited variation across leaves and plant species and levels significantly below 1/10th of the esculin sample values (Figure S5).

Hormone and response inhibitor treatments

Hormone and inhibitor solutions were prepared using phosphate‐buffered saline (pH 5.6). If necessary, substances were first dissolved in small volumes of DMSO before dilution in PBS to final concentrations. Solutions were infiltrated on the abaxial leaf surface of 4‐week‐old plants using a syringe without a needle. The following concentrations were used: indole‐3‐acetic acid (IAA, 10 μM), naphthylacetic acid (NAA, 5 μM, 10 μM), 2,4‐dichlorophenoxyacetic acid (2,4‐D, 5 μM, 10 μM), abscisic acid (ABA, 40 μM), strigolactone (SL, 5 μM), ethephon (ETH, 50 μM), salicylic acid (SA, 0.5 mM, 1 mM), chlormequat chloride (CCC, 0.6 mM), naphthylphthalamic acid (NPA, 10 μM), gibberellic acid 3 (GA31.4 mM), uniconazole (Uni, 40 μM), methyl jasmonate (MeJA, 10 μM), brassinosteroid (BR, 0.1 μM), brassinazole (BL, 0.1 μM), zeatin (10 μM), and kinetin (10 μM). Hormones/inhibitors were infiltrated into the abaxial side of a leaf using a 5 mL syringe. The effects were analyzed 2 h after infiltration. For experiments in which phloem loading was assessed using the esculin assay, working solutions of hormones/inhibitors were prepared with esculin solution, meaning that hormones/inhibitors and esculin were co‐infiltrated into the leaf.

RNA extraction and mRNA quantification

Total RNA was isolated using the Trizol (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) extraction method according to manufacturer instructions. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from 1 μg of total RNA using the EasyScript One‐Step gDNA Removal and cDNA Synthesis SuperMix Kit (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China). One microliter of cDNA was used as the template for the quantitative real‐time PCR (RT‐qPCR) reaction using SYBR Green Premix Pro Taq HS qPCR Kit (Accurate Biotechnology, ChangSha, China). The RT‐qPCR reaction procedure consisted of 40 cycles, pre‐denaturation at 95°C for 30 sec, denaturation at 95°C for 5 sec, and annealing at 55°C for 30 sec, with a total reaction volume of 20 μL. mRNA levels for the Arabidopsis genes were normalized to the housekeeping genes ACTIN12 (ACT12) and UBIQUITIN10 (UBQ10) (Xu et al., 2018). The primers used in this study are listed in Table S1. The relative fold changes of target gene mRNA levels were calculated according to the 2−ΔΔCt method (Livak & Schmittgen, 2001).

Measurement of photosynthetic rate and leaf sugar concentrations

Photosynthetic rates of Arabidopsis leaves under different treatments were measured using a YX1102G portable photosynthetic detector (Beijing Yaxin Li Instrument, Beijing, China). The chamber area was set as 1.5 cm2, the light source conditions were set to 1100 μmol photons m−2 s−1, and the airflow rate to 0.3 L/min. The concentration of CO2 was kept at 380–400 μmol mol−1 air (μg ml−1), and the automatic detection time was 20 sec with 5 repeats. At least three biological replicates were set up in this experiment.

Total soluble leaf sugars were extracted using the ethanol extraction method (Xu et al., 2020). The leaves were placed in a 2 mL test tube, quickly frozen in liquid nitrogen, and then crushed by a high‐flux tissue grinder (frequency 40 Hz, 2 times 20 sec, SCIENTZ‐48, Ningbo Scientz Biotechnology, Ningbo, China). After adding 80% ethanol solution to each EP tube, they were incubated at 80 °C in a water bath for 30 min. Samples were centrifuged for 20 min (12 000 g, room temperature) and the supernatant was transferred to a new tube. The fluid was evaporated in a vacuum concentrator (Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany) and the residue dissolved in 1.5 mL pure water. After filtering using a 0.22 μm filter, sugar concentrations were measured as described for phloem exudate sugars above.

Yeast one‐hybrid assay

The AtSUC2 promoter (2000 bp upstream of the start codon) was inserted into the pAbAi vector, which was then introduced into the yeast one‐hybrid gold strain (Weidi, Shanghai, China) using the lithium‐acetate transformation method (Gietz & Woods, 2002). Transformed cells were grown on dropout medium (SD/‐Ura) plus 600 ng ml−1 aureobasidin A (AbA). The transcription factors, ARF1 and ARF5 were cloned into the pGADT7 vector. Yeast cells containing the AtSUC2‐promoter construct were transformed with the ARF‐containing vectors and grown on dropout medium (SD/−Leu) plus 700 ng ml−1 aureobasidin A (AbA) for 3 days. In addition, yeast cells with pAbAi‐p53RE and pGADT7‐p53 were generated. The strains containing an empty pGADT7 vector served as a negative control for AtSUC2 activity. The combination of pAbAi‐p53RE and pGADT7‐p53 served as positive control (Li et al., 2020). Relevant primers are listed in Table S1.

Luciferase reporter assay

We inserted the cDNAs of three ARF genes (ARF1, ARF5, and ARF6) into the pBI111 vector and the AtSUC2 promoter into a pGreen II 0800 vector carrying the LUC gene and the Renilla reniformis luciferase gene (REN) (Miaoling Biology, China). Promoters were introduced upstream of the LUC gene. Empty vectors served as negative controls. Agrobacterium strain GV3101 was transformed with all vectors using the freeze–thaw method (Jyothishwaran et al., 2007). The transformed bacteria were cultured in a liquid YEP medium containing suitable antibiotics at 28 °C overnight. A second culture was inoculated with 1 mL of the original culture and cells were harvested when this culture reached an OD600 of 0.6. After centrifugation (4000 rpm, 5 min), cells were resuspended in 1 mL infiltration buffer solution (10 mM MgCl2, 150 μM acetosyringone, 10 mM MES‐KOH, pH 5.6) and incubated at room temperature for 3 h on a rotary shaker. The Agrobacterium populations containing the effector and the reporter constructs were mixed at a ratio of 1:9.

This solution was infiltrated in the abaxial side of large leaves of 30‐day‐old N. benthamiana plants. Typically, three leaves per plant were used. After 3 days, the infiltrated leaves were cut off and sprayed with 1 mM d‐luciferin potassium salt (M8873, AbMole, Houston, TX, USA). Luminescence imaging was carried out on a Lumazone Pylon 2048B imaging system (Princeton Instruments, Trenton, NJ, USA) at −120 °C. For quantification of luminescence, a dual luciferase reporter assay (Promega Corporation, Madison, WI, USA) was used according to the manufacturer's instructions and the resulting luminescence was measured with GloMax 20 luminometer (Promega Corporation). The dual luciferase assay offers higher accuracy and better comparability through normalization of the promoter‐dependent reporter intensity of LUC to the intensity of the control reporter intensity of REN. Primers used in this experiment are listed in Table S1.

Electrophoretic mobility shift assay

The Lightshift Chemiluminescent EMSA kit (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) was used for the EMSA experiments. The ARF1 gene was cloned into the pET41a vector with a GST tag. Cells of the Escherichia coli strain BL21 (DE3) (Tsingke, Beijing, China) were transformed with this vector using the chemical transformation method (Sambrook, Fritsch, & Maniatis, 1989) to produce recombinant protein. E. coli containing recombinant vectors were cultured in a shaker at 37 °C overnight (12 h), and then the overnight cultured bacterial solution was transferred into a new liquid LB medium at a ratio of 1:100 and continued to be cultured for about 2 h until the OD600 value of the bacterial solution was 0.6.

Expression of the recombinant GST‐ARF1 protein was induced by the addition of 0.5 M isopropyl β‐d‐thiogalactoside (IPTG) to achieve a final IPTG concentration of 0.75 mM. After 4‐h incubation at 37 °C, GST‐ARF1 was extracted using GST 4FF prepacked chromatographic columns (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) according to manufacturer instructions. A series of biotinylated probes were produced by PCR using manufacturer‐provided primers (Sangon Biotech) and labeled with a 5′ biotin signal. Primer sequences are provided in Table S1 and probe designs are specified in Table S2.

For pre‐electrophoresis, 0.5× TBE was run on a 6% non‐denatured polyacrylamide gel at 100 volts for 1 h. To prepare the reaction system, 10× binding buffer, 1ug uL−1 poly(dI·dC), 50% glycerol, 1% NP‐40, and 100 mM MgCl2 were combined, followed by the addition of 2 μL purified target protein ARF1 and gentle mixing. After 20 min of incubation at room temperature, the competitive probe (unlabeled cold probe or mutational probe) was introduced and the reaction proceeded for 15 min. The biotin‐labeled probe was then added, and 2–3 μL of 10× loading buffer was introduced after a 15‐min incubation at room temperature. Following the reaction, the sample was loaded onto the pre‐electrophoretic polyacrylamide gel and subjected to electrophoresis at 120 volts on ice for 1.5 h. The sample was then transferred to a positively charged nylon membrane (FFN10, Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) using wet transfer and sealed with UV light at room temperature for 15 min. After removing the blocking solution, the membrane was incubated with a blocking solution containing streptavidin‐HRP at room temperature for 15 min. Subsequently, the membrane was washed three to four times with washing buffer for 5 min each time, followed by treatment with equilibrium buffer for 5 min. Finally, imaging of the membrane was performed using a chemiluminescent imager (ChemiDocXRS+, Bio‐Rad, Hercules, CA, USA).

Construction of transgenic plants

Two distinct arf1 mutant strains were generated via CRISPR/Cas‐9 technology editing (Xing et al., 2014). Protospacer adjacent motif (PAM) and off‐target site information were collected using CRISPR online primer design software (http://crispr.mit.edu/). Two sgRNAs were designed and cloned into pKSE401 to construct the sgRNA‐Cas9 vector. Wild‐type Arabidopsis (Col‐0) was transformed with the sgRNA‐Cas9 constructs by floral dipping (Clough & Bent, 1998). The genomic DNA of transgenic plants was extracted using a plant genomic DNA extraction kit (EP011, ELK Biotechnology, Wuhan, China). Sequencing with primers designed to bind on both sides of the PAM site was used to determine whether the target gene was edited. This was followed by PCR‐based screening for homozygous mutants. Primers used in vector construction and the identification of transgenic plants are listed in Table S1. The sgRNA sequences and mutant sequence information are provided in Figure S2.

For the overexpression plants, 35S:ARF1 was introduced into the vector pCambia 2300‐35S and Arabidopsis wild‐type plants were transformed by floral dipping (Clough & Bent, 1998). ARF1 mRNA levels were quantified as described above. Three lines with ARF1 mRNA levels more than ten times higher than wild‐type plants were selected for further analysis.
