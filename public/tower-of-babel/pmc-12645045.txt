Assessment of traffic related air pollution effects on indoor air quality in educational buildings
Authors: Fahad K. Alqahtani, Yousif I. Deeb, Mohamed Sherif, Ahmed Gouda Mohamed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study assesses the impact of traffic-related air pollution (TRAP) on indoor air quality (IAQ) within a Riyadh school. The main objectives are to pinpoint factors influencing IAQ and to evaluate the effectiveness of existing ventilation and filtration systems. Air quality Egg sensors monitored nitrogen dioxide (NO2), carbon monoxide (CO), and particulate matter (PM1.0, PM2.5, PM10) levels both indoors and outdoors over 30 days. Findings revealed average indoor PM2.5 and PM10 concentrations of 14.09 µg/m3 and 18.15 µg/m3, respectively, while outdoor concentrations averaged 20.63 µg/m3 and 27.88 µg/m3. A strong positive correlation was observed between indoor and outdoor PM levels, with Spearman coefficients ranging from 0.81 to 0.94. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) indicated that particulate matter is the primary contributor to IAQ issues, followed by humidity and gases like NO2 and CO. Notably, indoor NO2 averaged 43.36 ppb, surpassing the outdoor average of 38.66 ppb, suggesting indoor sources and ventilation shortcomings. These results highlight the need for strategic school siting, enhanced HVAC systems, and routine IAQ monitoring. Recommendations emphasize locating schools away from major traffic areas and adopting advanced air purification technologies. Expanding research across schools could further clarify IAQ impacts, supporting healthier environments for children.

Introduction and background

Sustainability aims to conserve resources that enhance human life and health. The utilization of renewable substances and materials is also related to sustainability. Pollution levels surpass World Health Organization (WHO) recommendations in 91% of the world’s population. Along with building and electrical pollution, traffic-related air pollution (TRAP) accounts for 26% of total greenhouse gas emissions1,2.

It is a known fact that indoor air quality (IAQ) is influenced by both Outdoor Air Quality (OAQ) and indoor components such as construction techniques, building systems, building materials, furnishings, moisture, building occupants, and activities within the building3. In addition, outdoor air ventilation across the building can introduce TRAP in an indoor environment. It is well established that sources of air pollutants that cause poor IAQ depend on air pollutant type, amount, the rate at which it is released from its source, and the degree of ventilation available to remove it from indoors4. Moreover, a range of health consequences in both adults and children are also associated with indoor biological and chemical pollutants. Various studies have been conducted to evaluate the impact of TRAP on human health, which has a deleterious effect on the respiratory system5.

Although TRAP impacts many sites, schools are among the most vulnerable buildings to air pollutants due to the increased traffic density around them, especially at entry and exit times or school buildings on roadsides6. It has been noticed that long-term exposure leads to a decline in children’s attention and memory development7. Also, traffic pollution around the school significantly impacts the kids’ health. Since children’s lungs take in more air, potentially increasing their exposure to contaminants8, and since their organs are still developing, they are more vulnerable to health problems caused by indoor pollution9.

The health implications of prolonged exposure to poor indoor air quality (IAQ), particularly in environments influenced by traffic-related air pollution (TRAP), are well established. Fine particulate matter (PM2.5) and nitrogen dioxide (NO2) are known to exacerbate respiratory illnesses such as asthma, bronchitis, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), especially in children and elderly populations10,11. Long-term exposure has been linked to reduced lung function growth, cardiovascular dysregulation, and increased all-cause mortality12. Cognitive impacts have also been observed; recent studies found significant associations between elevated TRAP exposure and diminished attention and memory performance among primary school children13,14. Children’s susceptibility is notably higher due to their higher respiratory rates, underdeveloped detoxification systems, and the ongoing maturation of their pulmonary and immune systems12,13. These findings emphasize the urgency of characterizing IAQ within school settings, particularly in traffic-congested urban environments.

Many studies have studied the IAQ of different places and found that dust or particulate matter (PM), sulfur dioxide (SO2), nitrogen dioxide (NO2), carbon monoxide (CO), carbon dioxide (CO2), and heavy metals are the primary air pollutants. Chen et al.15 reported that PM concentration increased by 16–21% in indoor air when the building was less than 10 m from the adjacent road15. The closer the building is to the road, the higher the air particles and contaminants concentration in indoor air. The study suggested that the building has a mechanical ventilation system that supplies fresh air from the top in the opposite direction of traffic and the same wind direction. This solution will increase oxygen concentration and reduce air contaminants inside the building15,16. Swiercz16 suggested using façade ventilation devices to improve IAQ. In comparison, Swamy17 pointed to using a particular type of air filtering and purification to improve IAQ and reduce toxic gases. The study showed that the CO level was significantly reduced by 40% after using the synthesized filter media.

In another study conducted by Reche et al.18, the impact of TRAP on the IAQ of 39 school buildings in Barcelona was assessed. It showed that the concentration of air particles increased in those schools near an adjacent road, and vice versa, with a high level of fine particles (PM2.5), nitrogen dioxide (NO2), and equivalent black carbon (EBC).

Locally, few studies have been conducted to measure the indoor air quality (IAQ) in significant university hospitals, governmental elementary schools, educational buildings, commercial and office buildings, hotels, family restaurants, and takeaway restaurants located in the Eastern and Western provinces of Saudi Arabia19–26.In these studies, there was a consensus that outdoor traffic substantially influences Indoor Air Quality within healthcare facilities, schools, buildings, and restaurants. The levels of pollutants in facilities located on high to moderate-traffic streets were considerably higher than those located on low-traffic streets. Also, most of these studies19–21,24,25 reported that the levels of pollutants (i.e., TSP, PM1, PM2.5, PM10, CO, CO2, NO2, SO2) were higher than the recommended air quality standards specified in different standards, such as the WHO. For instance, El-Sharkawy24 and Alghamdi25 concluded that the levels of pollutants inside schools increased with the presence of students and increased with the presence of traffic activities at different levels, varying between 36.7 and 50% higher than the allowable limits specified by the World Health Organization (WHO)20,25.

This study presents a focused and contextually significant investigation into the impact of outdoor traffic-related air pollution (TRAP) on indoor air quality (IAQ) within a functioning school environment in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. As urbanization intensifies and vehicular density increases across cities in the Middle East, understanding the extent to which traffic-derived pollutants infiltrate indoor spaces—particularly schools—is of growing importance. The novelty of this research lies in its real-time, simultaneous monitoring of indoor and outdoor air pollutants within a school adjacent to a high-traffic corridor. This setting provides an ideal platform to evaluate pollutant transfer mechanisms, identify indoor exposure risks for children, and assess the performance of existing ventilation and filtration systems under realistic operational conditions.

The primary aim of the study is to analyze the spatiotemporal behavior of key air pollutants, namely nitrogen dioxide (NO2), carbon monoxide (CO), and particulate matter (PM1.0, PM2.5, and PM10), to determine how outdoor traffic emissions influence indoor air quality and whether the current building systems are adequate in mitigating this impact. The study adopts a structured methodology beginning with carefully selecting a school building located in a traffic-intensive area, ensuring classroom conditions that reflect typical regional infrastructure. Air Quality Egg (AQE) sensors were deployed to continuously monitor pollutant concentrations indoors and outdoors over 30 days, enabling high-resolution, real-world data collection.

The study applies a multi-layered analytical approach to derive meaningful interpretations from the dataset. Descriptive statistical analyses capture the central tendencies and variability of pollutant levels. In contrast, Spearman’s rank correlation analysis examines the strength of association between indoor and outdoor pollutant concentrations. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) is used to reduce the dimensionality of the data and identify dominant pollutant contributors influencing air quality patterns. Biplot visualizations derived from PCA components enhance the interpretability of the pollutant loading structures. At the same time, Quantile–Quantile (Q–Q) plots are utilized to assess the normality of pollutant distributions and highlight any anomalies or outliers.

Importantly, the study does not merely report pollutant levels; it investigates their time-dependent fluctuations with school operational schedules, particularly during high-traffic periods such as student arrival and departure times. This temporal disaggregation identifies pollutant infiltration patterns and exposure peaks that may pose health risks to students and staff. The observed lag between outdoor pollutant peaks and subsequent indoor elevations provides evidence of infiltration through the building envelope and HVAC systems, underscoring the role of external vehicular emissions as a primary contributor to indoor air quality deterioration.

By anchoring the findings within the specific environmental and architectural context of a school in Riyadh, this research offers novel insights into the localized effects of TRAP on IAQ in arid, rapidly urbanizing regions. The study concludes by providing practical, evidence-based recommendations for reducing indoor exposure to outdoor pollutants in school settings. These include integrating high-efficiency particulate air (HEPA) filters, improving natural and mechanical ventilation strategies, and establishing continuous real-time air quality monitoring within educational institutions. Furthermore, the findings emphasize the importance of traffic management near schools and the potential for urban planning interventions—such as green buffer zones—to mitigate the ingress of vehicular emissions.

Research methodology

This study employed a robust and integrated methodology to assess the influence of outdoor traffic-related air pollution (TRAP) on indoor air quality (IAQ) within a school located in Riyadh, as illustrated in Fig. 1. The selected school, "Abdul Aziz bin Omair Intermediate School," is in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. The school was in a traffic-dense zone and characterized by overcrowded classrooms, ensuring the research site reflected conditions where students are most vulnerable to high outdoor pollution levels and compromised indoor air environments (45 m away from the main road and 15 m from the secondary road, as shown in Fig. 2). Continuous air quality monitoring was conducted over 30 days using calibrated Air Quality Egg sensors positioned indoors and outdoors. These sensors recorded hourly concentrations of nitrogen dioxide (NO2), carbon monoxide (CO), and particulate matter fractions (PM1.0, PM2.5, PM10), yielding a high-resolution dataset for both temporal and spatial comparison.Fig. 1Proposed research methodology.Fig. 2The location of the school is in relation to the surrounding roads.

Proposed research methodology.

A multi-tiered statistical approach was applied to analyze the collected data. Descriptive statistics were used to examine the central tendencies and variability of pollutant concentrations, while correlation analyses, particularly Spearman’s rank-based coefficients, were utilized to assess the strength and direction of relationships between indoor and outdoor environmental parameters. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was employed to reduce dimensionality and identify the most influential pollutants contributing to air quality variation. Complementing these methods, biplot visualizations were developed to map the interaction among variables, and Quantile–Quantile (Q–Q) plots were generated to evaluate data distribution and detect deviations from normality.

This comprehensive methodological framework enabled a nuanced and data-driven analysis of the interactions between indoor and outdoor air pollutants in the context of school environments impacted by vehicular emissions. The integration of time-resolved monitoring, multivariate analysis, and visual diagnostics enhanced the study’s ability to characterize pollutant behavior and provided critical insights into exposure dynamics affecting students and staff in high-risk educational settings.

Selection criteria

The research data were collected from an elementary school located in Riyadh. The selected school should fulfil the criteria. The selection criteria for this current study are that the school must be located near a traffic-prone area, and the classrooms must be overcrowded (approximately 38 students). The school has a student population of 38 or more. The classrooms are equipped with air conditioning. The type of board used in the classrooms is pen-based. The classrooms are located on the ground floor. The floors are made of concrete. The ceiling material is also concrete. The view from the classrooms overlooks the street.

Air quality measurement procedure

Air Quality Egg, which was used to collect the data, was made by Kickstarter Company, USA27. It is a WiFi-enabled device that uses sensors to record changes in NO2, CO, and PM (1.0, 2.5, and 10) contaminants in the air and automatically uploads them via cloud service into the Egg devices. Later, the data on each factor that can cause pollution was downloaded by connecting the device to the computer. Two sensor devices were installed in the school for at least 30 days, one placed inside the classroom and the other outside the school, as shown in Fig. 3. It is important to note that the school utilizes wall-mounted split-type HVAC systems in all classrooms, which operate in recirculation mode without mechanical outdoor air intake or high-efficiency filtration. During the monitoring period, windows were kept closed, and no natural ventilation pathways were observed. These conditions suggest low ventilation rates, which may have contributed to the elevated indoor pollutant concentrations, particularly nitrogen dioxide (NO2). While exact air change rates (ACH) could not be measured, the observed pollutant accumulation patterns are consistent with limited indoor-outdoor air exchange.Fig. 3Sensors installed inside and outside of the school building.

Sensors installed inside and outside of the school building.

The primary purpose of the outdoor sensors is to read the changes in the outdoor air, whereas the indoor sensor was fixed inside the enclosed room to examine the effects of outdoor air on IAQ. Both sensors collected the change in the weather on an hourly basis. The indoor air sensor setup was installed away from the window or air outlet to guarantee more accurate results and placed on a fixed steel stand. Also, it was considered that the sensor must be at least 2 m away from the heat generator device (if any is present). Moreover, following the reported protocols, the electrical source was enclosed in a steel box to eliminate any interruption in the sensors’ power feeding27. As for outdoor air sensor installation, exposure to direct sunlight was avoided. Also, a proper distance was maintained from the air suction side of the Air Conditioning (AC), and the sensor was placed on a fixed steel stand. The methodology followed in this study is according to the standard of “ISO 12103-1 AI Test Dust; ASHRAE”28. Air quality egg (AQE) sensors were factory-calibrated before deployment, based on ISO 12103-1 AI Test Dust protocols to ensure measurement accuracy and consistency. A 24-h co-location test was performed within the school grounds, during which both indoor and outdoor sensors were operated side-by-side to assess inter-device reliability. The observed deviation across NO2, CO, and PM pollutants remained within ± 5%, confirming high measurement consistency. While calibration against regulatory-grade instruments was not conducted on-site, the AQE devices are widely validated in peer-reviewed literature for comparative urban air quality studies. Sensors were installed on fixed steel stands, protected from direct sunlight and heat sources, and powered via shielded electrical boxes. These procedures provided a stable monitoring setup for capturing real-time trends in both indoor and outdoor pollutant concentrations.

The air quality monitoring campaign was conducted continuously for 30 days during a single season, coinciding with a typical academic period when the school was fully operational. The study period was purposely selected to avoid academic holidays or atypical operating schedules that could affect indoor occupancy and pollutant generation. However, it is acknowledged that this single-season snapshot does not encompass the full range of meteorological variations that occur across the year. Seasonal shifts in temperature, humidity, wind speed, and atmospheric stability can significantly affect pollutant dispersion, accumulation, and indoor infiltration. Furthermore, occupant behaviors and building ventilation dynamics—including HVAC use and window-opening frequency—may change seasonally, potentially altering the magnitude and character of indoor air pollution. Therefore, while the data collected provide high-resolution insight into indoor-outdoor pollutant dynamics during a representative period, they may not fully reflect the annual variability in air quality conditions.

Data representation

The collected data represents the influence of occupant built-up and TRAP on IAQ and OAQ during the arriving/ leaving and working hours compared to weekend and non-working days. Moreover, the impact of environmental effects (dust storms) was also studied. A sample of the data collected using Egg sensors is shown in Fig. 4. For all cases, outdoor parameters that can affect the IAQ over the period were compared in a given manner, i.e., arriving/leaving hours versus weekend arriving/leaving hours, working hours versus non-working hours, and dust storm versus non-working hours. Although direct traffic count data for the study site were unavailable during the monitoring period, temporal traffic proxies were utilized to approximate traffic intensity. These included predefined periods of known vehicular activity patterns around the school—specifically, Arriving and Leaving Hours (A&LH: 6:00–7:00 and 12:00–13:00), Working Hours (WH: 7:00–12:00), and Non-Working Hours (NWH). Analysis of outdoor NO2 concentrations across these timeframes revealed temporal variations, notably elevated levels during A&LH. This temporal pattern aligns with expected traffic congestion associated with school drop-off and pick-up times. Additionally, weekend measurements showed comparatively lower NO2 levels, consistent with reduced traffic flow. These observations suggest that outdoor NO2 concentrations are significantly influenced by traffic volume, supporting the inference of a strong association between vehicle emissions and pollutant levels even in the absence of direct traffic count data.Fig. 4Sample of the data collected from the Egg sensor.

Eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the covariance matrix were then calculated, with the principal components (PCs) corresponding to the largest eigenvalues. PCA helped identify critical contributors to indoor and outdoor air quality variance, clearly visualizing the most influential factors.

Biplot visualization

Biplots were created to visualize the results of the PCA. These plots represented the position of different parameters concerning the first two principal components (PC1 and PC2), which exhibited the maximum variance. The biplots allowed for the interpretation of the contribution of each parameter to the overall variance, facilitating a better understanding of the data structure. A PCA and biplot-based data visualization were carried out using the Scikit-learn machine learning library and the yellow-brick visualizer in Python.

Quantile–quantile (Q–Q) plot analysis

Quantile–Quantile (Q–Q) plots were generated to compare indoor and outdoor data distribution against a theoretical normal distribution. This analysis helped identify deviations from normality, which could indicate potential issues with the data distribution. In Q–Q plots, the quantiles of the observed data were plotted against the quantiles of a standard normal distribution. Deviations from the identity line indicated non-normality, providing insights into the variability and potential outliers in the data.

Influence of traffic activity on indoor air quality

Beyond statistical trends, a deeper temporal interpretation of the data provides strong evidence that traffic-related outdoor air pollution (TRAP) plays a significant role in determining indoor air quality in the studied school environment. The temporal structure of the dataset—specifically its segmentation into Arriving and Leaving Hours (A&LH), Working Hours (WH), Non-Working Hours (NWH), and weekends—allowed us to examine pollutant behavior in close alignment with daily traffic patterns.

Outdoor concentrations of PM1.0, PM2.5, PM10, and NO2 peaked consistently during the A&LH intervals (6:00–7:00 and 12:00–13:00), coinciding with student drop-off and pick-up periods when traffic congestion around the school perimeter is at its highest. This pattern was mirrored indoors, with particulate matter levels increasing shortly after outdoor peaks—typically within a 15–30 min lag—suggesting active infiltration of vehicular emissions through the building envelope or HVAC systems.

Indoor PM2.5 concentrations, for example, rose from an average of 12.0 µg/m3 during Non-Working Hours to over 18.5 µg/m3 during A&LH periods. Outdoor PM2.5 peaked at 26.5 µg/m3 during the same periods, indicating a pollutant transmission efficiency of approximately 70%. This trend supports the hypothesis that outdoor traffic emissions primarily contribute to elevated indoor particulate matter levels, particularly in classrooms near drop-off zones.

The case of NO2 is even more critical. Although outdoor NO2 also peaked during traffic hours, indoor NO2 levels remained elevated for longer durations, often exceeding outdoor levels. This is likely due to the relatively lower air exchange rates indoors and possible accumulation effects. The indoor NO2 average during A&LH reached 49.3 ppb, compared to 41.2 ppb outdoors—values exceeding WHO recommendations and strongly suggesting indoor entrapment of traffic-emitted NO2.

Weekend comparisons further confirm the influence of traffic on indoor air quality. Across all monitored pollutants, indoor and outdoor levels dropped significantly during weekends, when traffic volume was noticeably reduced. For instance, weekend indoor PM2.5 levels averaged 9.6 µg/m3—approximately 32% lower than weekday working hours. Without significant indoor source activity, such temporal variance highlights traffic emissions as the dominant pollutant source during school days.

Correlation analysis supports these observations. The Spearman correlation coefficient between indoor and outdoor PM2.5 was strongest during A&LH (ρ = 0.89), compared to WH (ρ = 0.74) and weekends (ρ = 0.51). Similar patterns were found for PM10.0 and NO2. These correlations demonstrate the synchronicity of pollutant levels between indoor and outdoor environments and the temporal coupling of air quality degradation with peak traffic flow.

Taken together, this time-resolved analysis underscores how vehicular emissions, particularly during periods of concentrated traffic, directly compromise indoor air quality in school environments. These results highlight the urgent need for exposure mitigation strategies, including enhanced filtration, traffic re-routing, and creating buffer zones around educational buildings.

Implications for school building design and indoor air quality management

Regular monitoring of indoor air quality and timely maintenance of air filtration systems are essential to ensure a healthy indoor environment. This includes implementing advanced air purification technologies and adopting best practices in building design to enhance natural ventilation while minimizing pollutant ingress. The HVAC system’s role in maintaining a stable indoor pressure environment is critical, as it helps control the entry of outdoor air, reducing the penetration of pollutants such as PM, CO, and NO2. By maintaining a slightly higher outdoor pressure, the HVAC system ensures that indoor air remains cleaner, even when outdoor air quality deteriorates due to traffic or environmental events.

The study indicates that particulate matter is a primary concern for indoor air quality, with strong loadings for PM1.0, PM2.5, and PM10. Effective filtration systems and regular cleaning are essential to reduce indoor PM levels. Also, managing humidity and gaseous pollutants like CO through dehumidifiers and adequate ventilation can improve indoor air quality. The correlation between outdoor and indoor air quality highlights the importance of situating schools away from primary traffic sources to minimize TRAP infiltration. Practical barriers, such as green buffers and advanced building designs, can help mitigate the impact of outdoor air quality on indoor environments.

Continuous monitoring of critical pollutants (PM, CO, NO2) and environmental parameters (temperature, humidity, pressure) is crucial. Real-time data can inform adaptive management strategies, such as adjusting HVAC operations based on outdoor conditions. Installing high-efficiency particulate air (HEPA) filters and ensuring regular maintenance of ventilation systems can help maintain optimal indoor air quality.

Furthermore, outdoor air quality management through traffic management policies, strategic urban planning to locate schools away from primary traffic sources, and infrastructure improvements that enhance natural ventilation and incorporate green barriers can significantly reduce outdoor pollutant ingress, ultimately improving outdoor and indoor air quality in school environments.

Conclusion

The present study offers a detailed, real-world investigation into the influence of traffic-related air pollution (TRAP) on indoor air quality (IAQ) within an occupied school environment in a densely trafficked urban zone. Utilizing a month-long continuous monitoring campaign and real-time data collection across five critical pollutants—PM1.0, PM2.5, PM10.0, nitrogen dioxide (NO2), and carbon monoxide (CO)—this research provides empirical evidence on pollutant behavior and indoor-outdoor interactions under operational school conditions. The study is distinctive in its use of time-segmented analysis that mirrors the school’s daily rhythms, allowing for temporal mapping of pollutant concentrations during arriving and leaving hours (A&LH), working hours (WH), non-working hours (NWH), and weekends. This time-stratified approach enabled the identification of peak exposure windows for students and staff, thereby enriching our understanding of IAQ variability in response to environmental and behavioral inputs.

The data revealed a consistent and strong relationship between outdoor and indoor particulate matter levels. Spearman correlation coefficients for PM1.0, PM2.5, and PM10.0 between indoor and outdoor environments ranged from 0.79 to 0.89, indicating a high degree of infiltration. Despite indoor concentrations being consistently lower—e.g., PM2.5 measured at 14.09 µg/m3 indoors versus 20.63 µg/m3 outdoors—these values still raise concern, particularly considering WHO’s daily exposure guidelines and the vulnerability of the exposed population: children. The relatively high indoor values suggest that even with some degree of attenuation through building structures, existing ventilation and envelope systems are not sufficiently effective in filtering particulate matter, especially during peak traffic periods. This reflects pollutant ingress, insufficient air exchange rates, and a lack of advanced filtration.

Furthermore, the study highlights the inadequacy of school HVAC systems that operate in recirculation mode without a mechanical fresh air supply. The inability of such systems to dilute or displace incoming pollutants during high-exposure windows is a critical concern. These results show that schoolchildren are exposed outdoors during commutes and indoors during the early part of the school day—when pollutant levels are still stabilizing due to internal air retention. This dual exposure dynamic must be accounted for in school health policy and environmental regulation.

A particularly significant and novel finding is the indoor-outdoor relationship of nitrogen dioxide. Unlike particulate matter, indoor NO2 concentrations exceeded outdoors, with a recorded average of 43.36 ppb indoors versus 38.66 ppb. This inversion is a critical outcome, suggesting either internal sources of NO2—such as off-gassing from building materials, cleaning agents, or malfunctioning HVAC equipment—or pollutant entrapment due to insufficient ventilation. The relatively moderate indoor-outdoor correlation for NO2 (Spearman’s ρ ≈ 0.45) further supports this explanation. That indoor NO2 levels exceeded the WHO’s annual mean guideline value of 40 ppb within a school setting is particularly troubling, given young children’s sensitivity to oxidizing pollutants. Chronic exposure to NO2 has been linked to the development and exacerbation of asthma, reduced lung function growth, and increased absenteeism in school-aged children.

The analysis of carbon monoxide (CO) presented a different behavioral profile. CO concentrations were generally low (indoor average ≈ 0.06 ppb; outdoor ≈ 0.08 ppb), and the correlation between indoor and outdoor values was weak (ρ ≈ 0.23). This could be attributed to CO’s fast dispersion characteristics and the episodic nature of its emission, mainly from vehicle idling or combustion engines. Its minimal indoor presence could also indicate a lack of indoor sources or dilution through passive ventilation. However, despite CO not being a dominant pollutant in this case, its temporal profile still aligned with traffic activity peaks, underscoring its potential as an early warning indicator for TRAP influence during specific hours of the school day.

Methodologically, this study advances the field of IAQ monitoring in educational settings by leveraging low-cost air quality sensors in a scientifically structured manner. The sensors were factory calibrated, validated through side-by-side pre-deployment testing, and installed with environmental controls to avoid solar radiation, HVAC interference, or micro-environmental distortion. While not as precise as reference-grade instruments, these devices enabled high-resolution, continuous monitoring that offered more profound insights into pollutant fluctuations, indoor-outdoor dynamics, and time-of-day exposure profiles often absent in snapshot or survey-based IAQ studies.

Beyond empirical findings, this study contributes a critical narrative to the broader dialogue on environmental justice and children’s health in urban settings. School environments are assumed protective, yet this research illustrates how external air pollution—specifically vehicular emissions—can penetrate these spaces and expose children to health-compromising pollutant levels. This is particularly urgent in developing regions and rapidly urbanizing areas where traffic volumes are increasing and educational infrastructure may not meet modern air quality standards.

That said, the study is not without limitations. The data were collected at a school in a single season (late autumn), limiting generalizability across different climatic conditions, urban topographies, and architectural designs. Seasonal meteorological factors—such as increased air stagnation in winter, higher temperatures in summer, and shifts in wind direction—influence air pollutant behavior, building ventilation patterns, and human exposure. The lack of seasonal variability data prevents us from capturing these broader atmospheric interactions. Likewise, the school’s building characteristics and HVAC design may not reflect other institutions’ characteristics, especially in newer or retrofitted schools. To broaden the applicability of the findings, future investigations should include multi-seasonal monitoring across diverse school buildings and urban typologies to evaluate how seasonal variability, ventilation strategies, and climatic conditions influence the indoor exposure to outdoor air pollutants in educational environments.

To address these gaps, future research should adopt a longitudinal, multi-season, and multi-location design to explore how IAQ varies across geographic regions, building types, ventilation strategies, and outdoor pollution loads. Such research should ideally integrate additional environmental variables, including CO2 levels (as a proxy for ventilation rate), relative humidity, occupant density, and HVAC performance metrics. More importantly, coupling environmental exposure data with health records—such as respiratory symptom reports, absenteeism trends, and academic performance metrics—would create a robust evidence base for policy intervention and school health reforms.

From an applied perspective, this study highlights a critical need for policy-oriented solutions and technical upgrades within school systems. These include installation of higher-grade filtration systems (e.g., MERV-13 or HEPA filters), implementation of continuous IAQ monitoring systems with real-time alerts, inclusion of IAQ metrics in school inspection and safety protocols, and urban planning strategies that prioritize buffer zones or vegetative barriers between traffic corridors and school campuses. Public awareness campaigns about peak exposure times and risk mitigation behaviors for parents and school administrators could help reduce cumulative pollutant exposure during high-risk intervals.
