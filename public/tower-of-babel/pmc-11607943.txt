CRHBP, a novel multiple cancer biomarker connected with better prognosis and anti-tumorigenicity
Authors: Wonbeak Yoo, Hyunji Choi, Jieun Lee, Yeeun Lee, Kyung Chan Park, Kyunghee Noh
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe corticotropin-releasing hormone-binding protein (CRHBP) plays a crucial role in regulating corticotropin release. Little is known about the role of CRHBP, a major regulator of neuroendocrine, autonomic, and stress adaptation, in tumors. In this study, we aimed to investigate the clinical and molecular landscapes of CRHBP in various types of tumors.MethodsWe investigated the role of CRHBP in different types of tumors using publicly available databases and performed a comparative expression analysis of CRHBP-related genes in pan-cancer prognosis using methylation profiling, tumor-infiltrating immune cell expression analysis, gene enrichment analysis, and protein-protein interaction analysis, identified common pathways, and in vitro evaluation.ResultsWe evaluated CRHBP expression across tumor and corresponding normal tissues using the data from The Cancer Genome Atlas and the Genotype-Tissue Expression database. CRHBP was downregulated in most tumors and was identified as an important factor for predicting the prognosis of patients with cancer. Intracellular metabolic pathways and hormone-related processes were involved in the functional mechanisms of CRHBP. Mechanistically, the downregulation of CRHBP was attributed to the upregulation of four miRNAs in most tumors, and CRHBP expression was related to tumor-infiltrating immune cells in tumors. Overexpression of CRHBP significantly inhibited cell proliferation of LUAD, LIHC, and KIRC cell lines, while inhibition of cell mobility was found only in KIRC and HCC cells.ConclusionsThis study provides a comprehensive summary of the systemic role of CRHBP expression in various types of tumors, highlighting the prognostic importance and clinical significance of tumors. Furthermore, CRHBP decreases cell proliferation and mobility in cancer cell lines associated with OS and DFS, further research is needed to understand the underlying mechanisms and explore clinical applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12935-024-03562-4.Keywords: CRHBP, Prognosis, Genetic alteration, Immune cell infiltration, Enrichment analysis, Cell proliferation

Introduction

Corticotropin-releasing hormone-binding protein (CRHBP), also known as corticotropin-releasing factor-binding protein, is located on chromosome 5q13.3. CRHBP is a 37 kDa secreted glycoprotein that regulates adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) release [1, 2]. In vertebrates, ACTH plays an important role in stress responses and physiological activities such as steroid hormone biosynthesis and adrenal development [3, 4]. Specifically, CRHBP influences the hypothalamus-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, which binds to corticotropin release hormone (CRH) and regulates its bioavailability, including neuroendocrine, autonomous, and behavioral adaptations to stress [5–7] .

CRHBP may function physiologically or metabolically, such as in the genetic variation in antenatal corticosteroid (ACS) metabolism in cases of small for gestational age (SGA) and in the regulation of biological activities by binding to the corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) complex [8, 9]. In addition, downregulation of CRHBP is associated with poor prognosis and an increased risk of hepatocellular carcinoma [10, 11]. Similarly, CRHBP levels were confirmed to be low in kidney and prostate cancers [12, 13]. However, the role of CRHBP has been evaluated in only a few cancers, and its function in other tumor types remains unclear.

Despite significant advancements in cancer therapies since the 1960s, the challenge of cancer heterogeneity remains. Ongoing research continues to explore new strategies for more effective treatments [14]. Accordingly, there has been an increasing focus on Pan-cancer analyses on tumor progression and prognosis in recent years. These analyses, NGS and microarray-based analysis technologies, allow the identification of biomarkers on a transcriptomic scale in cancer research. Additionally, the comprehensive multi-omics data analysis provides a potential evidences for diagnosis and prognosis as well as for therapeutic approaches in various types of cancer [15, 16]. Therefore, the use of pan-cancer projects helps to comprehensively understand cancer diagnosis, prognosis, and immunological parameters [17–19].

In this context, based on the special significance of CRHBP in various tumors, it is necessary for us to conduct a pan-cancer analysis will become increasingly attractive as an understanding and therapeutic approach to cancer. In the present study, we utilized multiple datasets, including those from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), to explore the expression profile and prognostic value of CRHBP in various tumor types. We further investigated the correlation between CRHBP and gene alterations, methylation, microRNA (miRNA) interactions, and immune cell infiltration. Furthermore, we performed a functional enrichment analysis of CRHBP-correlated genes and proteins to explore their potential roles in biological and molecular processes. Based on this comprehensive analysis and experimental validation, we aimed to demonstrate the clinical value of CRHBP in various tumors.

Results

CRHBP expression in various cancers

First, we used the TIMER database to analyze CRHBP expression in TCGA pan-cancer data. As shown in Fig. 1A, CRHBP was downregulated in most types of cancers compared to its expression in adjacent normal tissues. This included bladder urothelial carcinoma (BLCA), breast invasive carcinoma (BRCA), cervical squamous cell carcinoma and endocervical adenocarcinoma (CESC), cholangiocarcinoma (CHOL), colon adenocarcinoma (COAD), glioblastoma multiforme (GBM), head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (NHSC), renal hepatocellular carcinoma (KICH), kidney renal clear cell carcinoma (KIRC), kidney renal papillary cell carcinoma (KIRP), liver hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC), lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC), pheochromocytoma and paraganglioma (PCPG), prostate adenocarcinoma (PRAD), rectum adenocarcinoma (READ), stomach adenocarcinoma (STAD), thyroid carcinoma (THCA), and uterine corpus endometrial carcinoma (UCEC). Because TCGA lacks information on the corresponding normal tissues for some tumors, we combined the data from TCGA and GTEx databases for further analysis. As shown in Fig. 1B, the extent of downregulation of CRHBP in the normal tissues compared to the expression in the corresponding tumor tissues of adrenocortical cancer (ACC), BRCA, CESC, CHOL, GBM, KICH, KIRC, KIRP, LIHC, PCPG, sarcoma (SARC), THCA, UCEC, and uterine carcinosarcoma (UCS) were lower than that in the normal-tumor tissue pairs sourced from the GTEx database. Acute myeloid leukemia (LAML), however, showed the opposite trend. Nevertheless, BLCA, COAD, lymphoid neoplasm diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL), esophageal carcinoma (ESCA), NHSC, brain lower grade glioma (LGG), LUAD, LUSC, ovarian serous cystadenocarcinoma (OV), PAAD, PRAD, READ, skin cutaneous melanoma (SKCM), STAD, testicular germ cell tumors (TGCT), and THCA did not show any significant differences in terms of CRHBP expression between normal and tumor (Supplementary Fig. 1). To investigate CRHBP expression in different tumors in detail, we performed a protein expression analysis using the CPTAC database. As shown in Fig. 1C, the total protein levels of CRHBP in the primary tumor tissues of KIRC, LIHC, and GBM were lower than those in normal tissues. In addition, based on the “Pathological Stage Plot” module of GEPIA2, we analyzed the relationship between CRHBP expression and the pathological stages of the tumors. As shown in Fig. 1D and Supplementary Fig. 2, in BRCA, KIRC, PAAD, and THCA tissues, the RNA expression levels of CRHBP were significantly negatively associated with the later clinical stages of these diseases.

Prognostic evaluation of CRHBP in pan-cancer

To investigate the prognostic significance of CRHBP in various tumors, a heatmap of the CRHBP gene expression depicting its significant prognostic value was generated using the GEPIA2 database, and the samples were divided based on the median expression value of CRHBP. As shown in Fig. 2A, we found that high expression of CRHBP was associated with better overall survival (OS) in patients with KIRC (p = 2.6e-08), MESO (p = 0.022), PAAD (p = 0.0014), and SARC (p = 0.0016). However, high CRHBP expression was associated with a poor prognosis in LAML (p = 0.021), UCEC (p = 0.027), and UVM (p = 0.0068). In addition, disease-free survival (RFS) analysis showed that high expression of CRHBP was correlated with better prognosis for KIRC (p = 0.00041), LIHC (p = 0.0088), and PAAD (p = 0.0078), whereas high expression of CRHBP was associated with poor RFS in UVM (p = 0.0038) (Fig. 2B) but not in others (Supplementary Figs. 3 and 4). Subsequently, we used the Kaplan–Meier plotter tool to analyze the survival and prognosis of patients with different tumors. As shown in Fig. 2C, high CRHBP expression was associated with poor OS in patients with BLCA (p = 0.048) and UCEC (p = 0.025) but with better OS in patients with KIRC (p < 0.001), LIHC (p = 0.0096), LUAD (p < 0.001), PAAD (p = 0.0027), and SARC (p = 0.016). In addition, high CRHBP expression was significantly associated with poor relapse-free survival in patients with ESCA (p = 0.012) or KIRP (p = 0.018). In contrast, low CRHBP expression was significantly associated with better relapse-free survival in patients with PAAD (p = 0.011) (Fig. 2D). These results demonstrate that CRHBP expression levels are correlated with the prognosis of various tumors, despite the variations in prognostic values.

Genetic and epigenetic alterations of CRHBP in various cancers

First, CRHBP genetic alteration profiles from all tumor types were gathered from the cBioPortal TCGA cohort. As shown in Fig. 3A, the CRHBP gene had the highest alteration frequency (> 3%) with ‘amplification’ in patients with ACC. In case of UCEC, the main type was ‘mutation’, and the alteration frequency was ~ 3%. Similar to that in UCEC, most alterations linked to deep deletions were in the OV (~ 3% frequency). The types, loci, structural domains, and corresponding cases of CRHBP alterations are shown in Fig. 3B. Splice/missense mutations were found to be the main type of genetic alteration, particularly, an X182_splice/P182S mutation in CRHBP (seen in 1 case of LUAD, 2 cases of BRCA, and 1 case of CSEC). Additionally, one CRHBP phosphorylation site (206th residue) was present in the main domain without any genetic alterations. Because UCEC showed the highest genetic alteration frequency, we further analyzed the association between CRHBP and clinical attributes in UCEC data from TCGA. In the detailed cancer type analysis, uterine mixed endometrial carcinoma showed the highest mutation frequency than that shown by other types, including uterine serous carcinoma/uterine papillary serous carcinoma and uterine endometrioid carcinoma (Fig. 3C). Simultaneously, the tumor mutation burden (TMB) (median value: 8 vs. 1.28) and polymerase epsilon (POLE) were significantly higher in patients with tumors harboring CRHBP-altered group than in those without CRHBP alterations in UCEC (Fig. 3D and E). However, prognostic analysis showed no significant changes in DSS, OS, or PFS in patients with UCEC, regardless of alterations in CRHBP (data not shown). Since epigenetic changes have been identified in primary human tumors, differences in CRHBP methylation levels were compared between normal and tumor tissues using the SMART database for various types of tumors. As shown in Fig. 3F, CpG-aggregated methylation value of CRHBP was significantly higher in tumor tissues than in corresponding normal tissues in BRCA (p ≤ 0.0001), CESC (p ≤ 0.001), CHOL (p ≤ 0.05), COAD (p ≤ 0.01), ESCA (p ≤ 0.0001), HNSC (p ≤ 0.0001), KIRC (p ≤ 0.0001), KIRP (p ≤ 0.0001), LUAD (p ≤ 0.0001), LUSC (p ≤ 0.01), PAAD (p ≤ 0.0001), PRAD (p ≤ 0.0001), READ (p ≤ 0.05), THCA (p ≤ 0.01), and UCEC (p ≤ 0.0001). Promoter methylation plays an important role in regulating gene expression, and hypermethylation of promoter downregulates gene expression. Therefore, the promoter methylation levels of CRHBP were analyzed using the ULCAN database in different tumor tissues. We found that the promoter methylation levels of CRHBP in the primary tumor tissues of BLCA, BRCA, COAD, CESC, HNSC, KIRC, KIRP, LIHC, LUAD, LUSC, PCPG, READ, TGCT, and UCEC were lower than those in the corresponding normal tissues but not in other tissues (Fig. 3G and Supplementary Fig. 5). These results suggest minimal association of CRHBP expression, which is downregulated in various tumors, with its promoter methylation level.

CRHBP-related gene enrichment analysis data

In addition, we further explored the biological role of CRHBP-interacting genes using Pathway Commons (Fig. 4A). The gene expression profile of the interacting genes in various tumor and normal tissues, generated via the GEPIA2 database, is presented in Fig. 4B. CCNB2, UCN, and COA7 levels were higher in tumor tissues than in the corresponding normal tissues, whereas PLS1, TAF1, UBR1, and UBR2 showed the opposite trend. Moreover, we used Enrichr to obtain the top 10 CRHBP-enriched pathways using ontological analyses, to identify cellular and biological functions. During the pathway enrichment analyses, CRHBP was significantly associated with class B/2, the event MECP2 regulates transcription factors, and the G2/M DNA replication checkpoint in Reactome 2022 as well as miRNA regulation of DNA damage response, cyclin B2-mediated events, and CREB phosphorylation through the activation of adenylate cyclase in BioPlanet 2019 (Fig. 4C). During ontological analysis, CRHBP was significantly linked with the cellular response to oxygen-containing compounds, negative regulation of protein autophosphorylation, and cellular response to leucine in GO Biological Process 2023 and leucine binding, amino acid binding, and ubiquitin-protein transferase activity in GO Molecular Function 2023 (Fig. 4D). In addition, we used STRING to identify CRHBP-interacting proteins, the results of which were supported by those of gene set enrichment analyses. As shown in Fig. 4E and F, the top CRHBP-interacting proteins were used to analyze the biological and molecular processes. The biological process results showed that the CRHBP-correlated proteins were involved in the regulation of endocrine processes, hormone secretion, and related pathways. The molecular process results suggest that CRHBP-correlated proteins are linked to corticotropin-releasing hormone receptor 2 binding, neuropeptide receptor binding, and hormone activity.

Prediction of CRHBP upstream miRNA and enrichment analysis

miRNAs play a crucial role by being negatively associated with mRNA expression. As CRBHP is downregulated in various tumors, regulatory miRNA expression should be higher in these tumors. Therefore, the target miRNAs of CRHBP were predicted using miRDB, TargetScan, and miRWalk, and 22 common miRNAs were predicted as candidate targets using a Venn diagram (Fig. 5A). To identify potent candidates, a meta-profile heatmap of the differential expression profiles of the 22 selected miRNAs in tissue samples from various patients with cancer and healthy controls was constructed. As shown Fig. 5B and C, hsa-miR-550a-3p, hsa-miR-200b-3p, hsa-miR-429, and hsa-miR-92a-3p showed significant differential expression between the tissue samples of patients with cancer and healthy controls and also carried binding sites for CRHBP 3′-UTR. Thus, these four miRNAs were selected as regulators of CRHBP expression for further analysis. Next, an expression relationship analysis was performed to examine the interactions between the four candidate miRNAs and CRHBP expression using pan-cancer data from Starbase. CRHBP expression was negatively associated with hsa-miR-550a-3p expression in nine tumors (Fig. 6A), with hsa-miR-200b-3p in 16 tumors excluding UVM and LGG (Fig. 6B), with hsa-miR-429 in 18 tumors excluding KIRC (Fig. 6C), and with hsa-miR-92a-3p in 16 tumors excluding LAML and TGCT (Fig. 6D). Thus, our results showed that CRHBP could be directly targeted by the four miRNAs, thus reducing its expression.

To determine the functional connections and underlying roles of three candidate miRNAs, hsa-miR-200b-3p, hsa-miR-429, and hsa-miR-92a-3p, functional enrichment analysis was conducted using the Starbase module. As shown in Tables 1, 2 and 3, the top five hits of enrichment analysis and KEGG pathway analysis showed that the three miRNAs were associated with cancer, actin cytoskeleton, and focal adhesion. During disease ontology analysis, gene-disease association, tumor, and cancer were identified. Other important molecular and biological function pathways were enzyme binding, ribonucleotide binding, transcription regulator activity, regulation of biosynthetic process, and intracellular signal transduction. These results indicate that candidate miRNAs may play an important role in the action of CRHBP via metabolism, signal transduction, or transcriptional regulation in cancer.

Immune infiltration analysis of CRHBP

Because immune cell infiltration plays an indispensable role in cancer progression, we next investigated whether CRHBP expression was correlated with immune cell infiltration in various tumors (Fig. 7). Our results indicated that CRHBP expression was positively associated with an abundance of CD8+ T cells, macrophages, and cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) in BLCA, BRCA, COAD, HNSC, KIRP, LUAD, PAAD, READ, SKCM, STAD, and UVM, whereas it was not significantly correlated in CESC, CHOL, DLBC, GBM, KICH, MESO, PCPG, THYM, UCEC, or UCS. In COAD, NHSC, LUSC, PAAD, and STAD, a positive association with B cells was identified, whereas no clear association with natural killer (NK) cells was observed. Interestingly, CRHBP expression showed a significant negative association with myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) in almost all types of cancers, excluding CHOL, DLBC, LGG, SKCM, THYM, and UCEC. These results suggest that CRHBP levels significantly correlate with immune cell infiltration in various tumors.

CRHBP decrease cell proliferation and mobility in vitro

Since CRHBP expression was significantly associated with prognosis in pan-cancer, we further investigated the function of CRHBP in KIRC, LUAD, and LIHC cell lines. As shown in Fig. 8A-C, the expression of CRHBP was downregulated in all KIRC, LUAD, and LIHC cell lines compared to the expression in the corresponding normal cell lines of HEK293, BEAS-2B, and HepaRG, respectively. Based on CRHBP expression in various cancer cell lines, the human KIRC cell lines ACHN and Caki-1, LUAD cell lines A549 and H460,and LIHC cell lines FOCUS and SK-HEP-1 were selected, and then these cell lines were further validated the biological role of CRHBP overexpression (Fig. 8D-F). Experiments showed that CRHBP overexpressing cells decreased the ability in cell proliferation and colony formation across all cancer cells (Fig. 8G-I). We further investigated the role of CRHBP in cell mobility using wound healing assay. The results indicated that overexpression of CRHBP reduced the migration ability of KIRC cells, including ACHN and Caki-1, as well as LIHC cells, including FOCUS and SK-HEP-1, compared to the control transfected group (Fig 9A and B). Simultaneously, the levels of the epithelial marker E-cadherin were significantly increased in CRHBP-overexpressed ACHN, FOCUS, and SK-HEP-1 cells compared to the control group. However, CRHBP-overexpressed A549 and H460 cells did not show significant changes in cell migration and associated epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) markers (Fig. 9C). These findings indicate that CRHBP primarily regulates cell proliferation, and its impact on cell motility may vary depending on the type of cancer (Fig. 10).

Discussion

CRHBP, a type of secreted glycoprotein expressed in the hypothalamic corticotropin region and pituitary gland, is involved in the regulation of CRH receptor activity as well as stress response, pregnancy, and binge drinking [20–22]. A functional connection has been suggested between CRHBP and tumors. Specifically, low CRHBP expression is associated with poor prognosis and carcinogenesis in HCC, and hypermethylation of CRHBP is correlated with its mRNA expression and clinicopathological parameters in KIRC [10, 11, 23]. However, the role of CRHBP in tumorigenesis remains unclear. To address this, we performed pan-cancer analyses of CRHBP expression across different cancer types based on the data from the TCGA, CPTAC, and GTEx databases, including molecular characteristics of gene expression, genetic modification, methylation, candidate miRNA profiling, and immune penetration. Subsequently, we validated CRHBP overexpression markedly reduced the capacity of cell proliferation and colony formation of KIRC, LUAD, and LIHC cells, and cell migration at least partly dependent on MET (Fig. 10).

Recently, precision molecular technologies, including genomic and multi-omics analysis, have been developed to enhance the understanding and establishing of disease and to establish therapeutic strategies across various research fields [24, 25]. These techniques are considered to have significant value, along with traditional analytical methods such as Immunohistochemistry (IHC), Immunofluorescence (IF), polymerase chain reaction (PCR), and Western blot, which are widely used in the biological and medical fields. Particularly in the field of oncology, the utilization of extensive information, from comprehensive genomic profiling to protein detection, accessed through public databases, can provide a foundation for the development of therapeutic interventions as well as a more accurate molecular description of tumors [26–28].

Based on the aforementioned, the present study identified CRHBP was expressed at lower levels in 15 of 33 tumors, namely, ACC, BRCA, CESC, CHOL, GBM, KICH, KIRC, KIRP, LAML, LIHC, PCPG, SARC, THCA, UCEC, and UCS, sourced from TCGA and GTEx databases, indicating an adverse role for CRHBP in tumor progression. The results of the prognostic analysis further demonstrated that high expression of CRHBP was linked to good prognosis of OS for KIRC, MESO, PAAD, and SARC, whereas LAML, UCEC, and UVM showed the opposite trend. Sequentially, the prognosis of KIRC, LIHC, and PAAD, in terms of RFS, increased with high expression of CRHBP, whereas UVM showed poor prognosis. These prognostic values were also verified using a Kaplan–Meier plotter, and the results revealed poor prognosis for BC and UCEC, while KIRC, LIHC, LUAD, PAAD, and SARC showed the opposite. In addition, ESAD and KIRC showed poor relapse-free survival; however, LIHC and PAAD showed better prognoses. The downregulation of CRHBP has been previously demonstrated to be associated with poor prognosis in patients with cancers such as LIHC or KIRC [10, 23]. Although the survival prognosis based on CRHBP expression is tumor-specific, no studies have reported on tumors other than LIHC and KIRC. From our study, it is inferred that the differential expression of CRHBP is closely related to the survival prognosis for most tumors.

To understand the molecular mechanisms underlying CRHBP downregulation in various tumors, genetic alteration analyses, methylation profiling, and miRNA screening were performed. This study demonstrated that ACC has the highest alteration frequency for gene amplification, followed by UCEC for gene mutation. In particular, the TMB and POLE levels were significantly higher in the altered group than in the unaltered group in UCEC. TMB, defined as the number of somatic mutations per megabase of gene sequence, may be a predictive biomarker of tumor response to immune checkpoint inhibitors in several cancer types, as it is considered a key driver for the production of immunogenic neopeptides (MHCs) on tumor cell surfaces [29]. Notably, according to the TCGA molecular classification of UCSC in 2013, POLE hypermutation was associated with the best prognosis [30, 31]. Although our study demonstrated that TMB and POLE were significantly related to genetic alterations in CRHBP with clinical implications, further studies are needed to support our findings. Methylation profiling analysis demonstrated that almost all tumors showed hypermethylation based on CpG-aggregated methylation values compared to that in normal tissues, whereas primary tumor tissues showed hypomethylation in the promoter region. Because hypermethylation at the promoter site lowers gene expression [32, 33], the downregulation of CRHBP in various tumor tissues was not considered dependent on promoter methylation in this study. miRNA gene silencing is a characteristic of tumors and can regulate gene expression and DNA methylation [34, 35]. Hence, we considered the possible involvement of miRNAs in downregulating CRHBP. We identified upregulated hsa-miR-550a-3p, hsa-miR-200b-3p, hsa-miR-429, and hsa-miR-92a-3p as negative regulators of CRHBP by interacting with CRHBP 3′-UTR. In addition, the expressions of these four miRNAs were negatively associated with CRHBP expression in various tumors, implying that the miRNAs destabilized CRHBP, thereby contributing to its downregulation. miRNA enrichment analysis was associated with increased cancer through gene-disease association, focal adhesion, transcription regulator activity, and intracellular signal transduction, which are believed to play important roles in tumor progression.

The immune microenvironment and infiltrating immune cells are essential components that play important roles in tumor development via intercellular interactions [36, 37]. However, each of these biomarkers has limitations, and as our understanding of the interactions between cancer and the immune system advances, new potential biomarkers are emerging. These biomarkers can be broadly classified into intrinsic tumor cell-related and microenvironment-related categories.

Therefore, we investigated the relationship between CRHBP expression and tumor-infiltrating immune cells to clarify the relationship in the tumor microenvironment of different cancer types. Interestingly, CRHBP expression was positively associated with CD8+ T cells, macrophages, and CAFs in most tumor types, such as BLCA, BRCA, COAD, HNSC, KIRP, LUAD, PAAD, READ, SKCM, STAD, and UVM, whereas it was negatively correlated with MDSCs. In the tumor microenvironment, CD8+ T cells play a major role in tumor immunity, indicating their cytotoxicity to tumor cells [38]. In contrast, cancer-related fibroblasts and macrophages play pivotal roles in tumor growth and progression [39]. In addition, MDSCs are generated in the bone marrow, one of the major components of the tumor microenvironment, and their main characteristic is strong immunosuppressive activity [40]. In this study, CRHBP expression was positively correlated with CAFs associated with tumor activation and CD8+ T cells associated with tumor suppression, whereas it was negatively correlated with MDSC associated with tumor development. Despite performing a comprehensive analysis for interaction between various tumor-infiltrating immune cells and CRHBP expression, the differential contribution of CRHBP to the tumor microenvironment must be further investigated.

Through the analysis of CRHBP-binding and expression-related genes, we identified 10 genes enriched in ‘CREB signaling,’ ‘cell cycle regulation,’ and ‘intracellular response.’ Network analysis using the STRING database confirmed that 10 proteins interacted with CRHBP, and CRHBP-related proteins were highly enriched in the regulation of endocrine processes and secretion of hormones, such as corticotropin and glucocorticoids. These results were consistent with those of previous observational studies. For example, Khadka et al. reported that glucocorticoid signaling may vary depending on the type of cancer; consequently, it also had varying effects [41]. Sato et al. suggested that corticotropin-releasing hormone levels correlated with OS in patients with pancreatic cancer [42]. Following that, we further investigated the possible mechanism of how the CRHBP expression influences cancer progression. The in vitro experiment shows that CRHBP overexpression strongly impaired proliferation ability of KIRC, LUAD, and LIHC cells. In the cell migration experiments, we also found that CRHBP inhibited cell motility of KIRC and LIHC cells via MET transition, reflecting the result as we analyzed by the in silico data. These findings may help understand the important role of CRHBP and its related signaling pathways in the pathogenesis of tumors.

Nevertheless, several limitations of our study should be acknowledged. First, most of our findings were based on publicly available datasets used to define the molecular activity related to the gene signature composition of CRHBP, and further prospective studies are needed to verify their clinical significance. Additionally, although we have validated the effects on cell proliferation and motility in several cancer cell lines in vitro, there remains a lack of specific associations for individual cancer types. Moreover, further analysis of the relationship between CRHBP-related cellular functions and carcinogenesis is required in larger sample sizes. Ultimately, a more systematic approach is necessary for a comprehensive understanding of CRHBP, which will require additional experimental validation.

Despite these limitations, it is important to recognize that a thorough study of CRHBP could have significant implications for patient prognosis. Due to its role in low regulatory pathways across various cancers, downregulation of CRHBP demonstrates strong molecular characteristics that may help identify favorable response roles in patients with cancer.

Conclusions

In this study, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of CRHBP expression and identified that it is downregulated in most tumors, and CRHBP expression was significantly correlated with the clinical prognosis of cancer patients, genetic alterations, epigenetic regulation, dysregulation of miRNAs, immune cell infiltration, enrichment analysis, and experimental validation. Thus, our study contributes to clarifying the role of CRHBP from various perspectives. These results were based on the data from databases; therefore, further clinical and mechanism studies are necessary to validate the role of CRHBP in different tumor types.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition and processing

GEPIA2 was used to identify gene expression profiles and perform interactive analyses based on TCGA and GTEx data [43] (accessed on 4 August 2023). This database allowed us to assess OS and RFS based on the expression of CRHBP and perform further validation using the Kaplan–Meier Plotter (KM plot) [44] (accessed on 4 August 2023). Hazard ratios (HR) were calculated with 95% confidence intervals, and p-values (from the log-rank test) were calculated online. The UALCAN web [45] was used to compare the expression levels of total protein and phosphorylation (CPTAC, Clinical Proteomic Tumor Analysis Consortium) between normal and primary tumor tissue data from TCGA (accessed on 14 August 2023). The cBioportal for Cancer Genomics database was used to analyze the genetic alterations in CRHBP in various cancers (TCGA, PanCancer Atlas, accessed on 18 August 2023). All statistical analysis was automatically calculated online.

Comprehensive analysis of CRHBP methylation

The Shiny Methylation Analysis Resource Tool (SMART) [46] was used to analyze the differential expression of methylated CRHBP in different cancers (accessed on 28 August 2023). In addition, the UALCAN database facilitated the analysis of CRHBP promoter methylation (accessed on 28 August 2023).

Bioinformatics analysis of miRNA target prediction

To identify potential targets of CRHBP, candidate miRNAs were predicted using miRDB [47], TargetScan [48], and miRWalk [49] (accessed on 4 September 2023). Using the data of 22 candidate miRNAs, meta-profiling heatmaps of various cancers were generated to observe the expression levels of promising miRNA biomarkers based on dbDEMC 3.0 [50] (accessed on 5 September 2023). Pan-cancer expression correlations and enrichment analyses of candidate miRNAs with CRHBP were conducted using the Starbase database [51] (accessed on 7 September 2023).

Exploring tumor microenvironment in pan-cancer data from TCGA

The tumor immune estimation resource database (TIMER 2.0) was used to analyze and visualize immune infiltrates from TCGA in TIMER Database [52] (accessed on 28 August 2023). In addition to exploring the association between tumor-infiltrating immune cells and CRHBP gene expression, the degree of infiltration of CD8+ T cells, macrophages, B cells, NK cells, MDSCs, and CAFs was analyzed using the TIMER database. The strength of the correlations was evaluated using a heatmap with based on the purity-adjusted Spearman’s rho coefficient across various cancer types in TCGA, and the statistical significance was estimated using TIMER 2.0. Statistical analysis was performed automatically using the online database.

Functional and pathway enrichment analysis

The 16 genes with the strongest correlation with CRHBP were selected using Pathway Commons [53] and this result was further analyzed to determine their mRNA expression in various cancers. To determine their functional and pathway significances, pathway-enriched analysis (including Reactome 2022, BioPlanet 2019, and KEGG 2021 Human) and ontological analysis (including GO Biological Processes 2023, CO Cellular Component 2023, and GO Molecular Function 2023) were performed using Enrichr for 16 selected genes forming a cluster with CRHBP [54] (accessed on 11 September 2023). The p-values were calculated online using Fisher’s exact test.

Cell culture

The HEK293, A704, Caki-1, Caki-2, SN1C, BEAS-2B, FOCUS, SK-HEP-1, HepG2 and Hep3B cell lines were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM). The SNU-333, SNU-349, SNU-482, SNU-1272, A549, H441, H460, H520, H1299, H1437, H1975, HCC827, SNU-475, SNU-449, and Huh7 cell lines were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI)-1640. MRC5 cells were cultured in Eagle’s minimum essential medium (EMEM). All cell lines were incubated in a growth medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% antibiotics at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2.

Lentivirus production and transduction

The transgene plasmids CRHBP-pIRES-ZsGreen1 and pIRES-ZsGreen1 were constructed using conventional molecular biology techniques. The 293T cell line was cultured in DMEM with 10% FBS and 1% antibiotics and incubated in a 5% CO2 incubator at 37 °C. Lentivirus particles were produced by co-transfecting 293T cells with VSV-G, gag-pol, and transgene plasmids, using PEIpro (Polyplus, France). 48 h after transfection, the supernatants containing lentivirus were harvested, filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane filter (Nalgene, USA), and stored at -80 °C. For transduction, cells were seeded in each well of a 6-well plate. 16 h post-seeding, the cells were transduced with the CRHBP-pIRES-ZsGreen1 or pIRES-ZsGreen1 lentiviral particles and replaced with a fresh culture media after 8 h of transduction.

Cell proliferation assay

Cells were seeded in a 96-well plate at a density of 2 × 103 cells/well and cell viability was assessed at 24, 48, 72, and 96 h using the WST-1 assay (EZ-Cytox, Korea). The plate reader was used to measure the absorbance at 450 nm.

Cell wound healing assay

The A549, H460, FOCUS, SK-HEP-1, ACHN, and Caki-1 cells were seeded into 24-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well. After 16 h of incubation, a wound was created by scratching the cell monolayer using a 200-µl pipette tip. The cells were then thoroughly washed with PBS to remove debris, and the culture medium was replaced. Wound healing was monitored under an optical microscope at 0, 4, 8, and 12 h.

Cell colony formation assay

The A549, H460, FOCUS, SK-HEP-1, ACHN, and Caki-1 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at 1000 cells per well and incubated for 10 days. Cells were rinsed with PBS and stained with 0.5% crystal violet solution for 30 min at room temperature. The colonies were then counted, photographed, and quantified.

RNA isolation and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen, USA), and first-strand cDNA was synthesized using the RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. qRT-PCR was performed using SYBR Green master mix (Thermo). Primers were designed using Primer3 software, and their sequences are described in Supplementary Table 1. The mRNA expressions levels of the target genes were normalized to the reference gene human GAPDH and calculated using the 2 − ΔΔCt method [55, 56].

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was calculated automatically based on the online database above. Student’s t-test implemented by GraphPad Prism (Version 10). Correlations were analyzed by Spearman and Pearson’s correlation. A P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
