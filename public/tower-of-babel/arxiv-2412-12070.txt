Simultaneous and multiplicative Diophantine approximation on missing-digit fractals
Sam Chow, Han Yu

Abstract
. We investigate the metric theory of Diophantine approximation on missing-digit fractals. In particular, we establish analogues of Khinchin’s theorem and Gallagher’s theorem, as well as inhomogeneous generalisations.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
11J83 (primary); 28A80, 42B05 (secondary)

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Two foundational results in metric Diophantine approximation

We begin by recalling the foundational results of Khinchin and Gallagher. We write
{\lambda}_{k}
for
k
-dimensional Lebesgue measure, and write
\|x\|
for the distance between a real number
x
and the set of integers. For
\psi:\mathbb{N}\to[0,1)
, we denote by
W_{k}^{\times}(\psi)
the set of
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})\in[0,1)^{k}
such that

\|nx_{1}\|\cdots\|nx_{k}\|<\psi(n)

has infinitely many solutions
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Similarly, we denote by
W_{k}(\psi)
the set of
\mathbf{x}\in[0,1)^{k}
such that

\max\{\|nx_{1}\|,\dots,\|nx_{k}\|\}<\psi(n)

holds for infinitely many
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Theorem 1.1

(Khinchin’s theorem
[
38
,
39
]
)
.

Let
\psi:\mathbb{N}\to[0,1)
be non-increasing, and let
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Then

{\lambda}_{k}(W_{k}(\psi))=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\psi(n)^{k}<\infty\\
\\
1,&\text{if }\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\psi(n)^{k}=\infty.\end{cases}

Theorem 1.2

(Gallagher’s theorem
[
34
]
)
.

Let
\psi:\mathbb{N}\to[0,1)
be non-increasing, and let
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Then

{\lambda}_{k}(W_{k}^{\times}(\psi))=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\psi(n)(\log n)^{k-1}<\infty\\
\\
1,&\text{if }\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\psi(n)(\log n)^{k-1}=\infty.\end{cases}

Khinchin’s theorem gives the simultaneous approximation rate of a generic vector, whilst Gallagher’s theorem gives the generic multiplicative approximation rate. The latter is a strong form of a famous conjecture of Littlewood, except that the approximation rate is only valid for almost all vectors.

Conjecture 1.3

(Littlewood’s conjecture in
k
dimensions)
.

Let
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
,
where
k\geqslant 2
. Then

\liminf_{n\to\infty}n\|nx_{1}\|\cdots\|nx_{k}\|=0.

1.2.
Diophantine approximation on fractals

Now that we have seen the classical results of Khinchin and Gallagher, it is natural to ask what happens if one replaces Lebesgue measure with some other measure. A probability measure
\mu
is
Khinchin
if Khinchin’s theorem holds with
\mu
in the place of
\lambda_{k}
, and
Gallagher
if Gallagher’s theorem holds with
\mu
in the place of
\lambda_{k}.

The philosophy is that a ‘natural’ probability measure
\mu
should be Khinchin and Gallagher, unless there is a simple obstruction.

A natural class of measures is given by induced Lebesgue measures on manifolds.
The following landmark result is the culmination of decades of devoted research; see the major breakthroughs by Kleinbock and Margulis
[
41
]
and by Beresnevich
[
6
]
, as well as
[
8
,
10
,
12
,
15
,
27
,
54
]
.

Theorem 1.4

(Beresnevich–Yang
[
14
]
, Beresnevich–Datta
[
7
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a non-degenerate submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
, where
k\geqslant 2.
Let
\mathcal{U}
be an open subset of
\mathcal{M}
, and let
\mu
be a smooth probability measure supported on
\mathcal{U}\cap[0,1]^{k}
. Then
\mu
is Khinchin.

For the Gallagher property, much less is known. Badziahin–Levesley
[
3
]
solved the convergence theory for planar curves, subject to curvature. For affine hyperplanes, see
[
9
,
20
,
21
,
22
,
24
,
36
]
, and finally
[
25
]
, where the problem was largely solved when
k\geqslant 9
.
For our purposes, the most relevant result is as follows.

Theorem 1.5

(Chow–Yu
[
25
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a hypersurface in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
, where
k\geqslant 5.
Let
\mathcal{U}
be an open subset of
\mathcal{M}
, in which the Gaussian curvature is non-zero, and let
\mu
be a smooth probability measure supported on
\mathcal{U}\cap[0,1]^{k}
.
Then
\mu
is Gallagher.

In this article, we investigate
missing-digit measures
. These generalise the canonical measure on the middle-third Cantor set, and we will define them in §
2.1
. A motivating goal is as follows.

Conjecture 1.6

(Dream Theorem for missing-digit fractals)
.

Let
k
be a positive integer, and let
\mu
be a missing-digit measure on
\mathbb{R}^{k}
that is not supported on any affine hyperplane. Then
\mu
is Khinchin and Gallagher.

Remark 1.7
.

The so-called Dream Theorem was introduced in a survey article
[
11
]
, referring to Theorem
1.4
before it was proved. Conjecture
1.6
is analogous, but also includes the Gallagher property. We would expect it to extend to self-similar measures
[
45
]
.

A measure on
\mathbb{R}^{k}
is
split
if it is a Cartesian product of Borel probability measures on
\mathbb{R}
. Our main result is as follows.

Theorem 1.8

(Main theorem)
.

Let
\mu
be a Borel probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{k}.
If

(1.1)

\mathrm{dim}_{\ell^{1}}(\mu)>k-\frac{k-1}{k+1},

then
\mu
is Khinchin. If moreover

\mu=\mu_{1}\times\cdots\times\mu_{k}

is split, and

(1.2)

\mathrm{dim}_{\ell^{1}}(\mu_{j})>1-\frac{1}{k+1}\qquad(1\leqslant j\leqslant k),

then it is Gallagher.

The Fourier
\ell^{1}
dimension
\mathrm{dim}_{\ell^{1}}
was used in
[
56
]
to establish a Jarník–Besicovitch-type theorem for fractal measures; we will define it in §
2.2
. Computing Fourier
\ell^{1}
dimension for missing-digit measures is not a simple task. In fact, it is not even immediately clear why there are proper missing-digit measures that satisfy (
1.1
). For this reason, we will in §
A.1
provide concrete examples where Theorem
1.8
applies. In particular, we will see that if the ‘digit structure’ of a missing-digit measure is simple enough, then its Fourier
\ell^{1}
and Hausdorff dimensions differ only slightly. Fourier
\ell^{1}
dimension was used in
[
57
,
58
]
to deduce various results on the continuity of projections of missing-digit measures in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
, where
k\geqslant 2
.

Recently, in
[
23
]
, a precise method to compute
\mathrm{dim}_{\ell^{1}}(\mu)
for an arbitrary missing-digit measure
\mu
on
[0,1]
was presented. We discuss a higher-dimensional analogue in §
A.2
.

1.3.
Related work

Similar conjectures can be found in the articles of Levesley–Salp–Velani
[
43
]
and Bugeaud–Durand
[
19
]
, and the problems are also discussed in the articles of Mahler
[
44
]
and Kleinbock–Lindenstrauss–Weiss
[
40
]
. Recent progress towards Conjecture
1.6
can be found in
[
23
,
26
,
28
,
37
,
51
,
56
]
. We highlight the following breakthrough.

Theorem 1.9

(Khalil–Luethi
[
37
]
)
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
\mu
be a missing-digit measure on
[0,1]^{k}
. Then there is an effectively-computable constant
{\varepsilon}>0
depending only on
k
such that if
\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{H}}(\mu)>k-{\varepsilon}
then
\mu
is Khinchin.

Khalil and Luethi’s method applies not only to missing-digit measures but also to more general self-similar measures with rational parameters and the open set condition. The Hausdorff dimension
\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{H}}(\mu)
of a Borel measure
\mu
is defined, for example, in
[
30
, Chapter 2]
, but we do not require the notion in full generality. If
\mu
is the Cantor–Lebesgue measure of a missing-digit fractal
\mathcal{K}
, see §
2.1
, then

\mathrm{dim}_{\ell^{1}}(\mu)\leqslant\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{H}}(\mu)=\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{H}}(\mathcal{K}).

In this setting, we will see in §
A.1
that the difference between
\mathrm{dim}_{\ell^{1}}(\mu)
and
\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{H}}(\mu)=\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{H}}(\mathcal{K})
is often small, in which case Theorem
1.8
goes beyond Theorem
1.9
.

In the special case
k=1
, there has been even more dramatic recent progress.

Theorem 1.10

(Bénard–He–Zhang
[
5
]
)
.

Let
\mu
be a self-similar probability measure on
[0,1]
whose support is not a singleton. Then
\mu
is Khinchin.

Theorems
1.9
and
1.10
both use random walks on homogeneous spaces. Our Fourier-analytic methodology, here and in
[
25
,
56
]
, is very different.

1.4.
More refined results

In the course of proving Theorem
1.8
, we establish some finer results. In order to state them, we introduce some more refined notions. For
\psi:\mathbb{N}\to[0,1)
and
\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},\dots,y_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
, we denote by
W_{k}(\psi,\mathbf{y})
the set of
\mathbf{x}\in[0,1)^{k}
such that

\max\{\|nx_{1}-y_{1}\|,\dots,\|nx_{k}-y_{k}\|\}<\psi(n)

has infinitely many solutions
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and write
W_{k}^{\times}(\psi,\mathbf{y})
for the set of
\mathbf{x}\in[0,1)^{k}
such that

\|nx_{1}-y_{1}\|\cdots\|nx_{k}-y_{k}\|<\psi(n)

holds for infinitely many
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Definition 1.11
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
\psi:\mathbb{N}\to[0,1)
be non-increasing. A probability measure
\mu
on
[0,1]^{k}
is

•

inhomogeneous convergent Khinchin
if

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\psi(n)^{k}<\infty\implies\mu(W_{k}(\psi,\mathbf{y}))=0\quad(\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}),

•

inhomogeneous divergent Khinchin
if

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\psi(n)^{k}=\infty\implies\mu(W_{k}(\psi,\mathbf{y}))=1\quad(\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}),

•

inhomogeneous convergent Gallagher
if

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\psi(n)(\log n)^{k-1}<\infty\implies\mu(W^{\times}_{k}(\psi,\mathbf{y}))=0\quad(\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}),

•

inhomogeneous divergent Gallagher
if

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\psi(n)(\log n)^{k-1}=\infty\implies\mu(W^{\times}_{k}(\psi,\mathbf{y}))=1\quad(\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}).

We establish the following new results. Theorem
1.8
follows from these by specialising
\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{0}
in the conclusions.

Theorem 1.12

(Convergence theory)
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
\mu
be a Borel probability measure on
[0,1]^{k}
satisfying

(1.3)

\mathrm{dim}_{\ell^{1}}(\mu)>k-\frac{k}{k+1}.

Then
\mu
is inhomogeneous convergent Khinchin. If moreover

\mu=\mu_{1}\times\cdots\times\mu_{k}

is split and satisfies (
1.2
),
then it is inhomogeneous convergent Gallagher.

Theorem 1.13

(Divergence theory)
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
\mu
be a Borel probability measure on
[0,1]^{k}
satisfying (
1.1
). Then
\mu
is inhomogeneous divergent Khinchin and inhomogeneous divergent Gallagher.

Observe that, for the divergent Gallagher property and for the convergent/divergent Khinchin properties, we do not require
\mu
to be split. We only require
\mu
to be split for the convergent Gallagher result.
Note also that if
k=1
then the assumption (
1.1
) cannot be met. We will see in §
A.1
that if
k\geqslant 2
then (
1.1
) can hold.

1.5.
Methods

Observe that sets like
W_{k}^{\times}(\psi)
are limit superior sets. To show that they have full measure, we use the divergence Borel–Cantelli lemma
[
13
]
. This requires us to estimate sums of measures of arcs around rationals, and of the intersections of these arcs. We achieve this using Fourier analysis, specifically via the framework of ‘moment transference principles’ that we recently developed in
[
25
]
. Unlike in the case of non-degenerate hypersurfaces
[
25
]
, the Fourier coefficients
\hat{\mu}(\mathbf{k})
do not decay pointwise:
\hat{\mu}(\mathbf{k})\not\to 0
as
\|\mathbf{k}\|_{\infty}\to\infty
. We instead exploit their average decay using the Fourier
\ell^{1}
dimension — developed in
[
56
]
and
[
23
]
— as discussed in §
1.2
. Divisibility also plays a key role, since the
n^{-1}
-periodicity of the arcs manifests as
n
-divisibility in Fourier space
\mathbb{Z}^{k}
. To establish the variance transference principle, we ultimately count solutions to a system of Diophantine equations.

1.6.
A bound on the Hausdorff dimension

Our expectation transference principle is strong enough to establish a Hausdorff dimension bound for simultaneous approximation on missing-digit fractals. To put this into context, we recall Levesley’s inhomogeneous version of the classical Jarník–Besicovitch theorem. For
t\geqslant 0
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\psi_{t}(n)=n^{-t}
.

Theorem 1.14

(Inhomogeneous Jarník–Besicovitch
[
42
]
)
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in\mathbb{R}
. Then

\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{H}}(W_{k}(\psi_{t},\mathbf{y}))=\min\left\{\frac{k+1}{t+1},k\right\}.

Our result is as follows.

Theorem 1.15
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
.
Then there exists
{\varepsilon}>0
such that the following holds whenever

t<\frac{1}{k}+{\varepsilon}.

Let
\mathcal{K}
be a missing-digit fractal in
[0,1]^{k}
whose Cantor–Lebesgue measure
\mu
satisfies
(
1.3
).
Then

\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{H}}(W_{k}(\psi_{t},\mathbf{y})\cap\mathcal{K})\leqslant\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{H}}(W_{k}(\psi_{t},\mathbf{y}))+\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{H}}(\mathcal{K})-k.

Remark 1.16
.

This upper bound was first conjectured with equality in
[
19
]
, when
\mathcal{K}
is the middle-third Cantor set and
\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{0}
. Despite the recent progress made in
[
56
]
and
[
23
]
, the problem remains open. The conjectured threshold differs for larger values of
t
, transitioning to
\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{H}}(\mathcal{K})/(t+1)
.

1.7.
Counting rational points on/near missing-digit fractals

For any compact set
\mathcal{K}\subset\mathbb{R}^{k}
, and real numbers
Q\geqslant 1
and
{\delta}\geqslant 0
, we define

\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{K}}(Q,\delta)=\{(\mathbf{a},q)\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}\times(\mathbb{Z}\cap[1,Q]):{\mathrm{dist}}(\mathbf{a}/q,\mathcal{K})\leqslant\delta/Q\}.

Observe that
\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{K}}(Q,0)
comprises rational points in
\mathcal{K}
of height at most
Q
. A challenging problem is to show that if
\mathcal{K}
‘lacks linear structure’ but has a naturally-associated dimension
\mathrm{dim}(\mathcal{K})
then, for any
\varepsilon>0,

\#\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{K}}(Q,0)\ll_{\varepsilon}Q^{\mathrm{dim}(\mathcal{K})+{\varepsilon}}.

For missing-digit fractals, we have
\mathrm{dim}(\mathcal{K})=\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{H}}(\mathcal{K}).

For irreducible projective varieties, the problem is known as Serre’s dimension growth conjecture
[
18
,
50
]
. It was solved by Salberger in 2009, and that solution was recently published
[
48
]
.

For the middle-third Cantor set, the conjecture was made by Broderick, Fishman, and Reich
[
17
]
. The statement naturally extends to missing-digit fractals. In the case
k=1
, progress was made recently in
[
23
]
, but not until now has substantial progress been made for
k\geqslant 2
.

For
{\delta}\in(0,1)
, heuristic reasoning suggests that

(1.4)

\#\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{K}}(Q,\delta)\asymp\delta^{k-\mathrm{dim}(\mathcal{K})}Q^{\mathrm{dim}(\mathcal{K})+1}.

The critical threshold, corresponding to Dirichlet’s approximation theorem, is
{\delta}=Q^{-1/k}
. The problem becomes significantly more demanding when
\delta
is smaller than this, and the estimate can even break down when
\delta
is extremely small.
A related problem was introduced by Mazur
[
46
]
, who asked how close a rational point can be to a smooth planar curve and still miss.

When
\mathcal{K}
is a suitably curved manifold, we now have some good counting estimates with
\delta
below the
Q^{-1/k}
threshold, especially for hypersurfaces, see for instance
[
12
,
35
,
49
,
52
]
.
For certain missing-digit fractals on
[0,1]
, such a result was obtained in
[
23
]
. Our present methods deliver (
1.4
) beyond the critical threshold for missing-digit fractals on
[0,1]^{k}
.

Theorem 1.17
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
\mathcal{K}
be a proper missing-digit fractal in
[0,1]^{k}
with Cantor–Lebesgue measure
\mu
satisfying (
1.3
). Then, for some constant
\eta>0,
we have (
1.4
)
whenever
Q
is sufficiently large and
{\delta}\gg Q^{-\eta-1/k}
.

We can apply Theorem
1.17
to count rational points on missing-digit fractals.

Corollary 1.18
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
\mathcal{K}
be a proper missing-digit fractal in
[0,1]^{k}
with Cantor–Lebesgue measure
\mu
satisfying (
1.3
). Then, for some constant
\eta>0,

\#\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{K}}(Q,0)\ll Q^{(1+1/k)\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{H}}(\mathcal{K})-\eta}.

Proof.

By Theorem
1.17
with
\eta/(k-\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{H}}(\mathcal{K}))
in place of
\eta
,

\displaystyle\#\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{K}}(Q,0)

\displaystyle\leqslant\#\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{K}}(Q,Q^{-\eta-1/k})

\displaystyle\ll Q^{-\eta}(Q^{-1/k})^{k-\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{H}}(\mathcal{K})}Q^{\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{H}}(\mathcal{K})+1}=Q^{(1+1/k)\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{H}}(\mathcal{K})-\eta}.

∎

This generalises the
k=1
case, which was demonstrated in
[
23
]
. Counting rationals in the middle-third Cantor set is considered to be a difficult problem, see for instance
[
47
]
. As explained in
[
23
]
, the condition (
1.3
) fails for the middle-third Cantor set. The condition holds, for instance, when

k=1,\qquad b\geqslant 5,\qquad\#\mathcal{D}=b-1.

See §
A.1
for some situations in which
k\in\mathbb{N}
is arbitrary the condition holds.

The assumption (
1.3
) is essential for the proof of Theorem
1.17
, but we suspect that the weaker assumption
\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{H}}(\mu)>k-\frac{k}{k+1}
should suffice for the result. It is easy to see that such a condition cannot be dropped without imposing other conditions, e.g. that
\mu
is not supported in any proper affine subspace. We illustrate with the following example, using the notation of §
2
.

Example 1.19
.

Suppose
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{K}_{b,\mathcal{D}}
is a missing-digit fractal in
[0,1]^{k}
with

\mathcal{D}\supseteq\{0,1,\ldots,b-1\}^{k-1}\times\{0\}.

As
\mathcal{K}
contains
[0,1]^{k-1}\times\{0\}
, we have

\#\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{K}}(Q,0)\gg Q^{k}.

By (
2.1
), such a set
\mathcal{K}
exists with

\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{H}}(\mathcal{K})<k-\frac{k}{k+1}.

If moreover
{\delta}\leqslant Q^{-1/k}
, then

\delta^{k-\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{H}}(\mathcal{K})}Q^{\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{H}}(\mathcal{K})+1}\leqslant Q^{(k+1)\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{H}}(\mathcal{K})/k}=o(Q^{k}).

The constant
\eta
in Theorem
1.17
can be chosen according to a lower bound for
\mathrm{dim}_{\ell^{1}}(\mu)
, which can be effectively computed using Theorem
A.2
. In special cases, we obtain a bound for
\#\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{K}}(Q,0)
with an exponent that is arbitrarily close to best possible.

Theorem 1.20
.

Fix
\eta>0.
Then there exists a missing-digit fractal
\mathcal{K}\supseteq[0,1]^{k-1}\times\{0\}
such that

\#\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{K}}(Q,0)\ll Q^{k+\eta},\qquad\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{H}}(\mathcal{K})>k-\eta.

Note that if
\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{H}}(\mathcal{K})>k-\frac{k}{k+1}
then the estimate

\#\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{K}}(Q,0)\ll Q^{k+\eta}

does not follow trivially from Corollary
1.18
.
Since
\mathcal{K}\supseteq[0,1]^{k-1}\times\{0\}
, the bound is sharp up to a factor of
O(Q^{\eta})
. However, we suspect that better estimates hold for missing-digit fractals that do not concentrate too much on affine subspaces.

1.8.
Intrinsic Diophantine approximation

Although the main focus of this paper is extrinsic Diophantine approximation, we also establish new results in the intrinsic setting, wherein we approximate by rational vectors in
\mathcal{K}
. The terminology was introduced by Mahler in the influential article
[
44
]
. Let
\mathcal{K}\subsetneq[0,1]^{k}
be a missing-digit fractal with Cantor–Lebesgue measure
\mu
, and let
\psi:\mathbb{N}\to[0,1)
. We denote by
W^{\mathcal{K}}_{k}(\psi)
the set of
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\in[0,1)^{k}
such that

\max\left\{\left|x_{1}-\frac{a_{1}}{n}\right|,\dots,\left|x_{k}-\frac{a_{k}}{n}\right|\right\}<\frac{\psi(n)}{n}

holds for infinitely many
(a_{1},\dots,a_{k},n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}\times\mathbb{N}
for which

\left(\frac{a_{1}}{n},\dots,\frac{a_{k}}{n}\right)\in\mathcal{K}.

For
\tau>0,
we write
W_{k}^{\mathcal{K}}(\tau)=W_{k}^{K}(n\mapsto n^{-\tau}).
It follows from a result of Weiss
[
55
]
that

(1.5)

\mu(W_{1}^{\mathcal{K}}(\tau))=0\qquad(\tau>1).

More generally, it follows from a result of Kleinbock–Lindenstrauss–Weiss
[
40
]
that if
\mu
satisfies an affine irreducibility condition then

(1.6)

\mu(W_{k}^{\mathcal{K}}(\tau))=0\qquad(\tau>1/k).

Conjecture 1.21

(Broderick–Fishman–Reich
[
17
]
)
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
\mathcal{K}
be a proper missing-digit fractal in
[0,1]^{k}
that is not contained in any proper affine subspace. Then, for all
\tau>0,

\mu(W_{k}^{\mathcal{K}}(\tau))=0.

This conjecture was originally posed for the middle-third Cantor set, but we believe that it should hold in general. A recent result in
[
23
]
asserts that if
\mathrm{dim}_{\ell^{1}}(\mu)>1/2
then there exists
\eta>0
such that

(1.7)

\mu(W_{1}^{\mathcal{K}}(\tau))=0\qquad(\tau>1-\eta).

This refined (
1.5
). Presently, we extend (
1.7
) to higher dimensions, thereby refining (
1.6
).

Theorem 1.22
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
\mathcal{K}
be a proper missing-digit fractal in
[0,1]^{k}
with Cantor–Lebesgue measure
\mu
satisfying (
1.3
).
Then, for some
{\varepsilon}>0
,

\mu(W_{k}^{\mathcal{K}}(\tau))=0\qquad\left(\tau>\frac{1}{k}-{\varepsilon}\right).

We will see that this is a simple consequence of Corollary
1.18
. Note that if (
1.3
) holds then
\mu
cannot be supported in any proper affine subspace.

1.9.
Further related work

One can simplify the problems of counting rationals and intrinsic approximation by using the intrinsic height
[
32
]
instead of the denominator. For the middle-third Cantor set, see the recent article

Preparation

2.1.
Missing-digit fractals and measures

Let
k\geqslant 1
and
b\geqslant 2
be integers, and let
\mathbb{P}
be a probability measure on

\{0,1,\ldots,b-1\}^{k}
.
A
missing-digit measure
is the probability measure
\mu_{b,\mathbb{P}}
whose distribution is that of the random variable

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathbf{d}^{(j)}/b^{j},

where the
\mathbf{d}^{(j)}\in\{0,1,\ldots,b-1\}^{k}
are i.i.d. with distribution
\mathbb{P}
. A
missing-digit fractal
is the support of a missing-digit measure. Equivalently, a missing-digit fractal is

\mathcal{K}_{b,\mathcal{D}}=\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathbf{d}^{(j)}/b^{j}:\mathbf{d}^{(j)}\in\mathcal{D}\right\}\subseteq[0,1]^{k},

where
k,b
are as above and
\mathcal{D}\subseteq\{0,1,\ldots,b-1\}^{k}
.
The
Cantor–Lebesgue measure
of a missing-digit fractal
\mathcal{K}_{b,\mathcal{D}}
is

\mu_{b,\mathcal{D}}=\mu_{b,\mathbb{P}},\qquad\text{where}\qquad\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{d})=\#\mathcal{D}^{-1}\quad(d\in\mathcal{D}).

Example 2.1
.

The Cantor set is

\mathcal{K}_{b,\{0,2\}}
.
The Cantor measure is the Cantor–Lebesgue measure of the Cantor set.

A missing-digit fractal
\mathcal{K}
is
proper
if
\#\mathcal{K}\geqslant 2
and
\mathcal{K}\neq[0,1]^{k}
. A missing-digit measure is
proper
if its support is proper.

2.2.
Notions of dimension

We begin our discussion with Hausdorff measure and dimension, which are standard concepts in fractal geometry
[
29
]
. For
\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{k}
, we write
|\mathcal{U}|
for the diameter of
\mathcal{U}
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{k}
. For
s\geqslant 0
and
{\delta}>0
, define

H^{s}_{\delta}(\mathcal{F})=\inf\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}|\mathcal{U}_{i}|^{s}:\mathcal{F}\subseteq\displaystyle\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{U}_{i},\quad|\mathcal{U}_{i}|\leqslant{\delta}\>\forall i\right\}.

The
Hausdorff
s
-measure
of
\mathcal{F}
is

H^{s}(\mathcal{F})=\lim_{{\delta}\to 0}H^{s}_{\delta}(\mathcal{F}),

and the
Hausdorff dimension
of
\mathcal{F}
is

\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{H}}(\mathcal{F})=\inf\{s\geqslant 0:H^{s}(\mathcal{F})=0\}.

Hausdorff measure refines Lebesgue measure, in that
H^{k}(\mathcal{F})=c_{k}{\lambda}_{k}(\mathcal{F})
for some constant
c_{k}>0
. Note that

(2.1)

\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{H}}(\mathcal{K}_{b,\mathcal{D}})=\frac{\log\#\mathcal{D}}{\log b}.

We equip ourselves the following basic tool, which is commonly used to establish upper bounds for Hausdorff measure and dimension.

Lemma 2.2

(Hausdorff–Cantelli lemma)
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{2},\ldots
be hypercubes in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
, and put
\mathcal{E}_{\infty}=\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{E}_{n}
. Let
s>0
, and suppose

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}|\mathcal{E}_{j}|^{s}<\infty.

Then
H^{s}(\mathcal{E}_{\infty})=0
and
\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{H}}(\mathcal{E}_{\infty})\leqslant s
.

Proof.

This is
[
16
, Lemma 3.10]
.
∎

Next, we recall the Fourier
\ell^{t}
dimensions, which were brought into this subject in
[
56
]
. Let
\mu
be a Borel probability measure supported on
[0,1]^{k}
. For
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}
, the
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\mathrm{th}}

Fourier coefficient
of
\mu
is

\hat{\mu}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})=\int_{[0,1]^{k}}e(-{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\cdot\mathbf{x}){\mathrm{d}}\mu(\mathbf{x}).

The
Fourier
\ell^{t}
dimension
of
\mu
is

\mathrm{dim}_{\ell^{t}}(\mu)=\sup\left\{s\geqslant 0:\sum_{\|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\|_{\infty}\leqslant Q}|\hat{\mu}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})|^{t}\ll Q^{k-s}\right\}.

It follows from the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality that

\frac{\mathrm{dim}_{\ell^{2}}(\mu)}{2}\leqslant\mathrm{dim}_{\ell^{1}}(\mu)\leqslant\mathrm{dim}_{\ell^{2}}(\mu).

For
s>0
, we say that
\mu
is
s
-regular
if

\mu(B_{r}(\mathbf{y}))\asymp r^{s}\qquad(\mathbf{y}\in{\mathrm{supp}}(\mu),\quad 0<r\leqslant 1).

Note that the Cantor–Lebesgue measure of a missing-digit fractal
\mathcal{K}
is
s
-regular with
s={\mathrm{\mathrm{dim}_{H}}}(\mathcal{K})
, see
[
31
, Theorem 3.9]
. By mimicking the continuous analogue in
[
45
, §3.8]
, it is readily confirmed that if
\mu
is
s
-regular then

\mathrm{dim}_{\ell^{2}}(\mu)=\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{H}}({\mathrm{supp}}(\mu))=s.

Note also that if
\mu
is a missing-digit measure and
\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{H}}({\mathrm{supp}}(\mu))=s
then

(2.2)

\mu(B_{r}(\mathbf{y}))\ll r^{s}\qquad(\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{k},\quad 0<r\leqslant 1).

2.3.
Moment transference principles

For any Borel probability measure
\mu
on
[0,1]^{k}
and any
f\in L^{1}(\mu)
, we write

\mu(f)=\int_{[0,1]^{k}}f{\mathrm{d}}\mu.

Let
{\lambda}
be Lebesgue measure on
[0,1]^{k}
. Let
\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{2},\ldots
be bounded Borel subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
, and put
\mathcal{E}_{\infty}=\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{E}_{n}
. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
f_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{k}\to[0,\infty)
be compactly supported and measurable.

Define

\displaystyle E_{N}(\mu)=\sum_{n\leqslant N}\mu(f_{n}),\qquad V_{N}(\mu)=\int_{[0,1]^{k}}\left(\sum_{n\leqslant N}(f_{n}(\mathbf{x})-{\lambda}(f_{n}))\right)^{2}{\mathrm{d}}\mu(\mathbf{x}).

The
expectation transference principle
(ETP) holds if

E_{N}(\mu)=(1+o(1))E_{N}({\lambda})+O(1)\qquad(N\to\infty).

The
variance transference principle
(VTP) holds if

V_{N}(\mu)=V_{N}({\lambda})+o(E_{N}(\mu)^{2})+O(1),

along a sequence of
N\to\infty
.

Lemma 2.3

(General convergence theory conclusion)
.

Let
\psi:\mathbb{N}\to[0,1)
. Assume that
f_{n}\gg 1
on
\mathcal{E}_{n}
for all sufficiently large
n\in\mathbb{N}
, that

(2.3)

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}{\lambda}(f_{n})<\infty,

and that we have ETP for this sequence of functions. Then
\mu(\mathcal{E}_{\infty})=0
.

Proof.

Replace
A_{n}^{\times}
by
\mathcal{E}_{n}
in the proof of
[
25
, Lemma 2.6]
.
∎

Lemma 2.4

(General divergence theory conclusion)
.

Let
C\geqslant 1
and
\psi:\mathbb{N}\to[0,1)
. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, suppose
f_{n}
is supported on
\mathcal{E}_{n}
. Suppose we have ETP and VTP for this sequence of functions, as well as

(2.4)

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}{\lambda}(f_{n})=\infty

and

(2.5)

\sum_{m,n\leqslant N}{\lambda}(f_{m}f_{n})\leqslant CE_{N}({\lambda})^{2}+O(1).

Then
\mu(\mathcal{E}_{\infty})\geqslant 1/C
.

Proof.

The ETP and the divergence of
\sum_{n}{\lambda}(f_{n})
give

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mu(f_{n})=\infty.

By
[
25
, Lemma 2.5]
,

\sum_{m,n\leqslant N}\mu(f_{m}f_{n})=\sum_{m,n\leqslant N}{\lambda}(f_{m}f_{n})+o(E_{N}(\mu)^{2})+O(1),

along a sequence of
N\to\infty
. Combining this with (
2.5
) and the ETP yields

\sum_{m,n\leqslant N}\mu(f_{m}f_{n})\leqslant(C+o(1))E_{N}(\mu)^{2}+O(1)

along this sequence. Functional divergence Borel–Cantelli
[
25
, Lemma 2.7]
completes the proof.
∎

2.4.
Setup

Our analysis involves decomposing the problem into rectangles, scaling bump functions accordingly, and passing to Fourier space. Let
w:\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]
be a non-zero bump function supported on
[-2,2]
. This has the property that

(2.6)

\hat{w}(\xi)\ll_{L}|\xi|^{-L},

for any
L\geqslant 1
. The function
w
will approximate
\{x:|x|\leqslant 1\}
or
\{x:1/2\leqslant|x|\leqslant 1\}
.

Next, we adapt
w
to certain rectangles. Let
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
and
\mathbf{d}\in(0,1/2]^{k}
, and define

\displaystyle A_{n}(\mathbf{d})

\displaystyle=\{\mathbf{x}\in[0,1]^{k}:\|nx_{j}-y_{j}\|<nd_{j}\quad(1\leqslant j\leqslant k)\},

\displaystyle A^{\circ}_{n}(\mathbf{d})

\displaystyle=\{\mathbf{x}\in[0,1]^{k}:nd_{j}/2<\|nx_{j}-y_{j}\|<nd_{j}\quad(1\leqslant j\leqslant k)\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{d})

\displaystyle=[-d_{1},d_{1}]\times\cdots\times[-d_{k},d_{k}],

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{\vee}(\mathbf{d})

\displaystyle=[-1/d_{1},1/d_{1}]\times\cdots\times[-1/d_{k},1/d_{k}],

\displaystyle w_{\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{d})}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\prod_{j\leqslant k}w(x_{j}/d_{j}).

Then

\widehat{w_{\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{d})}}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})=\prod_{j\leqslant k}d_{j}\hat{w}(d_{j}\xi_{j}).

Note that
w_{\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{d})}
is normalised so that

\widehat{w_{\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{d})}}(\mathbf{0})\asymp d_{1}\cdots d_{k}.

If
\mathbf{d}
is clear from the context, then we will simply write
\mathcal{R}
for
\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{d})
and
\mathcal{R}^{\vee}
for
\mathcal{R}^{\vee}(\mathbf{d})
. By (
2.6
), we thus have

(2.7)

\frac{\widehat{w_{\mathcal{R}}}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})}{d_{1}\cdots d_{k}}\ll 2^{-mL}\qquad({\boldsymbol{\xi}}\in 2^{m}\mathcal{R}^{\vee}\setminus 2^{m-1}\mathcal{R}^{\vee}).

We see that
A_{n}(\mathbf{d})
and
\mathcal{R}
have the same shape, and note that
\mathcal{R}^{\vee}
is the dual rectangle of
\mathcal{R}.
The bump function
w_{\mathcal{R}}
is supported on

2\mathcal{R}:=\{2\mathbf{x}:\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{R}\}.

Its Fourier transform
\hat{w}_{\mathcal{R}}
is essentially supported on
\mathcal{R}^{\vee}.
For any shift
\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in\mathbb{R}^{k},
the shifted function
w_{\mathcal{R}}(\cdot-\boldsymbol{\gamma})
is supported on
\mathcal{R}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}
, and its Fourier transform is still essentially supported on
\mathcal{R}^{\vee}.
These considerations can be used to establish ETP and VTP for unions of rectangles.

We will work with a smooth, periodically extended version of the indicator function of
A_{n}(\mathbf{d})
, namely

(2.8)

A^{*}_{n}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{d})=\sum_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}}w_{\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{d})}\left(\mathbf{x}-\frac{\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{y}}{n}\right),

where
w
approximates
\{x:|x|\leqslant 1\}
. By choosing
w
to instead approximate
\{x:1/2\leqslant|x|\leqslant 1\}
, we can alternatively use this same expression to approximate
A_{n}^{\circ}(\mathbf{d})
.

For the simultaneous theory, we use
A_{n}(\mathbf{d})
with
\mathbf{d}=(d,d,\dots,d)
for some
d>0.
Thus, the corresponding
\mathcal{R}
and
\mathcal{R}^{\vee}
are all hypercubes.

For the multiplicative theory, the situation is more complicated, as we need to consider boxes of different shapes in order to approximate

A_{n}^{\times}:=\{\mathbf{x}\in[0,1)^{k}:\|nx_{1}-y_{1}\|\cdots\|nx_{k}-y_{k}\|<\psi(n)\}.

We write

h_{i}=2^{-i}

for each integer
i
in some range. The range is given by

\frac{\psi(2^{m-1})}{2^{m-1}}\leqslant h_{i}\leqslant\frac{1}{2^{m-1}}

in the convergence setting, for

n\in D_{m}:=[2^{m-1},2^{m}).

Fixing a small constant
\tau>0
, the range in the divergence setting is

\frac{1}{2^{(1+(1+\tau)/k)m}}\ll h_{i}\ll\frac{1}{2^{(1+(1-\tau)/k)m}}.

Writing

hh_{i_{1}}\cdots h_{i_{k-1}}=\frac{\psi(2^{m})}{2^{km}},\qquad Hh_{i_{1}}\cdots h_{i_{k-1}}=\frac{\psi(2^{m-1})}{2^{k(m-1)}}

and

\displaystyle\mathfrak{B}_{n}

\displaystyle=\bigsqcup_{h_{i_{1}},\ldots,h_{i_{k-1}}}A_{n}^{\circ}(h_{i_{1}},\ldots,h_{i_{k-1}},h),

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}_{n}

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{h_{i_{1}},\ldots,h_{i_{k-1}}}A_{n}(h_{i_{1}},\ldots,h_{i_{k-1}},2^{k-1}H),

we then have

\mathfrak{B}_{n}\subseteq A_{n}^{\times}\subseteq\mathfrak{C}_{n}.

Admissible set systems and admissible function systems

For each
m\in\mathbb{N}
, we choose an index set
I_{m}
and a threshold

\mathfrak{d}_{m}\gg\frac{1}{n^{1+(1+\tau)/k}}\qquad(n\in D_{m}).

For each

and each

d_{i,j}=d_{i,j,m}
such that

n^{-\tau}\mathfrak{d}_{m}\ll d_{i,j}\ll n^{\tau}\mathfrak{d}_{m}\qquad(n\in D_{m}),

does not depend on

is a sequence

obtained in this way.

For the Khinchin theory, we choose

to be a singleton for each

3.
Expectation transference principle

In this section, we establish some ETP results. For notational convenience, we write

\tilde{E}_{N}(\mu)=E_{2N-1}(\mu)-E_{N-1}(\mu)-E_{2N-1}({\lambda})+E_{N-1}({\lambda}).

Theorem 3.1

(Dyadic split ETP)
.

Let
N\in\mathbb{N}
and
\mathbf{d}\in(0,1/2]^{k}
. For each
n\in[N,2N)
, we associate
A_{n}^{*}
to
\mathbf{d}
as in §
2.4
. Let
\mu=\mu_{1}\times\dots\times\mu_{k}
be a product of probability measures on
[0,1]
with
\mathrm{dim}_{\ell^{1}}(\mu_{j})=\kappa_{j}
for each
j.
Then, for any
{\varepsilon}>0
,

\tilde{E}_{N}(\mu)\ll_{\varepsilon}N^{k+{\varepsilon}}\prod_{j=1}^{k}d_{j}^{{\kappa}_{j}-{\varepsilon}}.

Proof.

By Parseval,

\displaystyle\tilde{E}_{N}(\mu)

\displaystyle=\sum_{N\leqslant n<2N}\int(A_{n}^{*}(\mathbf{x})-{\lambda}(A_{n}^{*})){\mathrm{d}}\mu(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{N\leqslant n<2N}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\neq\mathbf{0}}\widehat{A_{n}^{*}}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})\hat{\mu}(-{\boldsymbol{\xi}}).

To proceed, the key intuition is that
\widehat{A_{n}^{*}}
is essentially supported on the multiples of
n
in the box

\mathcal{R}^{\vee}_{N}:=[-1/d_{1},1/d_{1}]\times\cdots\times[-1/d_{k},1/d_{k}].

Indeed, as
A_{n}^{*}
is
n^{-1}
-periodic, its Fourier coefficients vanish away from multiples of
n
.
Moreover, we can see from (
2.7
) that if
u\in\mathbb{N}
and
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\in 2^{u}\mathcal{R}^{\vee}_{N}\setminus 2^{u-1}\mathcal{R}^{\vee}_{N}
then

\widehat{A_{n}^{*}}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})\ll_{L}2^{-uL}N^{k}d_{1}\cdots d_{k},

for any constant
L\geqslant 1
.
Thus,

\displaystyle\frac{\tilde{E}_{N}(\mu)}{N^{k}d_{1}\cdots d_{k}}\ll\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\mid{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\in\mathcal{R}^{\vee}_{N}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}\\
N\leqslant n<2N\end{subarray}}|\hat{\mu}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})|+\sum_{u=1}^{\infty}2^{-uL}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\mid{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\in 2^{u}\mathcal{R}^{\vee}_{N}\setminus 2^{u-1}\mathcal{R}^{\vee}_{N}\\
N\leqslant n<2N\end{subarray}}|\hat{\mu}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})|.

Next, we exploit the product structure of
\mu
. Observe that there are
O(N^{\varepsilon})
many integers
n
such that
n\mid{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
, whence

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\mid{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\in\mathcal{R}^{\vee}_{N}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}\\
N\leqslant n<2N\end{subarray}}|\hat{\mu}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})|

\displaystyle\ll N^{\varepsilon}\prod_{j\leqslant k}\left(\frac{1}{d_{j}}\right)^{1-{\kappa}_{j}+{\varepsilon}}.

For
u\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\mid{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\in 2^{u}\mathcal{R}^{\vee}_{N}\setminus 2^{u-1}\mathcal{R}^{\vee}_{N}\\
N\leqslant n<2N\end{subarray}}|\hat{\mu}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})|

\displaystyle\ll(2^{u}N)^{\varepsilon}\prod_{j\leqslant k}\left(\frac{2^{u}}{d_{j}}\right)^{1-{\kappa}_{j}+{\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle\leqslant 2^{Ku}N^{{\varepsilon}}\prod_{j\leqslant k}\left(\frac{1}{d_{j}}\right)^{1-{\kappa}_{j}+{\varepsilon}},

where
K=(k+1)(1+{\varepsilon})
. Choosing
L>K
then gives

\displaystyle\frac{\tilde{E}_{N}(\mu)}{N^{k}d_{1}\cdots d_{k}}\ll N^{{\varepsilon}}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\left(\frac{1}{d_{j}}\right)^{1-{\kappa}_{j}+{\varepsilon}}.

∎

We also need an ETP result for general measures, rather than product measures. For this, we require a strong assumption on
\mathbf{d}.

Theorem 3.2

(Dyadic general ETP)
.

Let
N\in\mathbb{N}
and
0<d\leqslant 1/2
. For each integer
n\in[N,2N)
, we associate
A_{n}^{*}
to
\mathbf{d}=(d_{1},\ldots,d_{k})
as in §
2.4
, where
\tau>0
is fixed and

N^{-\tau}d\ll d_{j}\ll N^{\tau}d.

Let
\mu
be a Borel probability measure on
[0,1]^{k}
with
\mathrm{dim}_{\ell^{1}}(\mu)=\kappa
. Then, for any fixed
{\varepsilon}>0,

\tilde{E}_{N}(\mu)\ll N^{k+(2k+{\varepsilon})\tau+{\varepsilon}}d^{\kappa-{\varepsilon}}.

Proof.

Imitating the proof of Theorem
3.1
gives

\displaystyle\frac{\tilde{E}_{N}(\mu)}{N^{k}d_{1}\cdots d_{k}}\ll\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\mid{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\in\mathcal{R}^{\vee}_{N}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}\\
N\leqslant n<2N\end{subarray}}|\hat{\mu}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})|+\sum_{u=1}^{\infty}2^{-uL}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\mid{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\in 2^{u}\mathcal{R}^{\vee}_{N}\setminus 2^{u-1}\mathcal{R}^{\vee}_{N}\\
N\leqslant n<2N\end{subarray}}|\hat{\mu}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})|,

where

R_{N}^{\vee}=[-1/d_{1},1/d_{1}]\times\cdots\times[-1/d_{k},1/d_{k}].

A similar argument to the remainder of that proof then delivers

\frac{\tilde{E}_{N}(\mu)}{N^{(1+\tau)k}d^{k}}\ll N^{\varepsilon}(N^{\tau}/d)^{k-{\kappa}+{\varepsilon}}.

∎

4.
Variance transference principle

In this section, we establish the VTP.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\mu
be a probability measure on
[0,1]^{k}
satisfying (
1.1
), and let
(f_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
be an admissible function system. Then VTP holds for
(f_{n})_{n}
and
\mu
.

Proof.

We wish to estimate

V_{N}(\mu)=\int_{[0,1]^{k}}\left(\sum_{n\leqslant N}(f_{n}(\mathbf{x})-{\lambda}(f_{n}))\right)^{2}{\mathrm{d}}\mu(\mathbf{x}).

As
f_{n}
is a Schwartz function, we may replace it by its Fourier series to obtain

\displaystyle V_{N}(\mu)

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,1]^{k}}\left(\sum_{n\leqslant N}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\neq\mathbf{0}}\hat{f}_{n}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})e({\boldsymbol{\xi}}\cdot\mathbf{x})\right)^{2}{\mathrm{d}}\mu(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,1]^{k}}\sum_{n,n^{\prime}\leqslant N}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\prime}\neq\mathbf{0}}\hat{f}_{n}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})\hat{f}_{n^{\prime}}({\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\prime})e(({\boldsymbol{\xi}}+{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\prime})\cdot\mathbf{x}){\mathrm{d}}\mu(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{n,n^{\prime}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\prime}\neq\mathbf{0},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}+{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\prime}=\mathbf{0}}\hat{f}_{n}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})\hat{f}_{n^{\prime}}({\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{n,n^{\prime}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}+{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\prime}\neq\mathbf{0}}\hat{f}_{n}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})\hat{f}_{n^{\prime}}(\xi^{\prime})\hat{\mu}({\boldsymbol{\xi}}+{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=V_{N}({\lambda})+\sum_{n,n^{\prime}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}+{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\prime}\neq\mathbf{0}}\hat{f}_{n}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})\hat{f}_{n^{\prime}}({\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\prime})\hat{\mu}({\boldsymbol{\xi}}+{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\prime}).

The final equality is gotten by running the same calculation for
V_{N}({\lambda})
. It is crucial that all of the Fourier series converge absolutely, which is the case as our functions are Schwartz. We are left with the contribution of the non-zero coefficients:

V_{N}(\mu)=V_{N}({\lambda})+E,

where

E=\sum_{n,n^{\prime}\leqslant N}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}+{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\prime}\neq\mathbf{0}}\hat{f}_{n}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})\hat{f}_{n^{\prime}}({\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\prime})\hat{\mu}({\boldsymbol{\xi}}+{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\prime}).

We sum over
n,n^{\prime}
dyadically, noting that

|E|\leqslant\sum_{m,m^{\prime}\leqslant\frac{\log N}{\log 2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in D_{m}\\
n^{\prime}\in D_{m^{\prime}}\end{subarray}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}+{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\prime}\neq\mathbf{0}}|\hat{f}_{n}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})\hat{f}_{n^{\prime}}({\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\prime})\hat{\mu}({\boldsymbol{\xi}}+{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\prime})|,

where
D_{m}=[2^{m},2^{m+1}).
We normalise the Fourier coefficients by dividing by

{\lambda}(f_{n})=\sum_{i\in I_{m}}n^{k}\prod_{j\leqslant k}d_{i,j}\asymp 2^{km}\sum_{i\in I_{m}}\prod_{j\leqslant k}d_{i,j}=:w_{m}

and

{\lambda}(f_{n^{\prime}})\asymp w_{m^{\prime}}.

Writing

a_{n}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})=\frac{\hat{f}_{n}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})}{{\lambda}(f_{n})},\qquad a_{n^{\prime}}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})=\frac{\hat{f}_{n^{\prime}}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})}{{\lambda}(f_{n^{\prime}})},

we now have

\displaystyle E

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{m,m^{\prime}\leqslant\frac{\log N}{\log 2}}w_{m}w_{m^{\prime}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in D_{m}\\
n^{\prime}\in D_{m^{\prime}}\end{subarray}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}+{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\prime}\neq\mathbf{0}}|a_{n}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})a_{n^{\prime}}({\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\prime})\hat{\mu}({\boldsymbol{\xi}}+{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\prime})|

\displaystyle=\sum_{m,m^{\prime}\leqslant\frac{\log N}{\log 2}}w_{m}w_{m^{\prime}}S(m,m^{\prime}),

where

S(m,m^{\prime})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in D_{m}\\
n^{\prime}\in D_{m^{\prime}}\end{subarray}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}+{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\prime}\neq\mathbf{0}}|a_{n}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})a_{n^{\prime}}({\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\prime})\hat{\mu}({\boldsymbol{\xi}}+{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\prime})|.

To proceed, we examine the coefficients
a_{n},a_{n^{\prime}},
which satisfy

|a_{n}|,|a_{n^{\prime}}|\leqslant 1.

The support of
f_{n}
is essentially a union of rectangles with different side lengths but the same volume, and its Fourier series is the sum of the Fourier series of those rectangles.
In what follows, we may assume that
I_{m}
is non-empty, since otherwise it will not contribute anything to the sums.

We write

a_{n}=\sum_{i\in I_{m}}a_{n,i},

where

{\lambda}(f_{n})a_{n,i}

is the Fourier transform of
\mathbf{x}\mapsto A_{n}^{*}(\mathbf{d}^{(i)};\mathbf{x})
. Note that

{\lambda}(f_{n})\asymp w_{m}=(\#I_{m})2^{km}\prod_{j\leqslant k}d_{i,j}

for all
i\in I_{m}
, whence

a_{n,i}\ll(\#I_{m})^{-1}.

We now see that

S(m,m^{\prime})\ll\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in D_{m}\\
n^{\prime}\in D_{m^{\prime}}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in I_{m}\\
i^{\prime}\in I_{m^{\prime}}\end{subarray}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}+{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\prime}\neq\mathbf{0}}|a_{n,i}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})a_{n^{\prime},i^{\prime}}({\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\prime})\hat{\mu}({\boldsymbol{\xi}}+{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\prime})|.

The function
a_{n,i}
is essentially supported on the multiples of
n
in

\mathcal{R}_{m,i}^{\vee}:=[-1/d_{i,1},1/d_{i,1}]\times\cdots\times[-1/d_{i,k},1/d_{i,k}].

Indeed, we have the following estimate
[
25
, Lemma 5.3]
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let

n\in D_{m},\quad i\in I_{m},\quad L,t\geqslant 1,\quad{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}\setminus t\mathcal{R}_{m,i}^{\vee}.

Then

a_{n,i}(\mathbf{x})\ll_{L}t^{-L}(\#I_{m})^{-1}.

Similarly, the function
a_{n^{\prime},i^{\prime}}
is essentially supported on the multiples of
n^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{R}^{\vee}_{m^{\prime},i^{\prime}}
.
The upshot is that

S(m,m^{\prime})\ll_{L}\sum_{u,u^{\prime}=0}^{\infty}S_{u,u^{\prime}}(m,m^{\prime}),

where

S_{u,u^{\prime}}(m,m^{\prime})=\frac{2^{-L(u+u^{\prime})}}{\#I_{m}\#I_{m^{\prime}}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in I_{m}\\
i^{\prime}\in I_{m^{\prime}}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\boldsymbol{\xi}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}+{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\prime}\neq\mathbf{0}\\
n\mid{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\in 2^{u}\mathcal{R}_{m,i}^{\vee}\\
n^{\prime}\mid{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\prime}\in 2^{u^{\prime}}\mathcal{R}_{m^{\prime},i^{\prime}}^{\vee}\end{subarray}}|\hat{\mu}({\boldsymbol{\xi}}+{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\prime})|.

Here
L\geqslant 1
is a constant that we will describe later.

Write

T=\begin{pmatrix}t_{1}&t_{1}^{\prime}\\
t_{2}&t_{2}^{\prime}\\
\vdots&\vdots\\
t_{k}&t_{k}^{\prime}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mathbf{n}=\begin{pmatrix}n\\
n^{\prime}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mathbf{x}=\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}\\
x_{2}\\
\vdots\\
x_{k}\end{pmatrix}

For
\mathbf{0}\neq\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}
, let
N(\mathbf{x})=N(\mathbf{x};m,m^{\prime},u,u^{\prime},i,i^{\prime})
count solutions
(\mathbf{t},\mathbf{t}^{\prime},\mathbf{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{2k+2}
to

T\mathbf{n}=\mathbf{x}
such that

\displaystyle n\in D_{m},\qquad n^{\prime}\in D_{m^{\prime}},\qquad\mathbf{t},\mathbf{t}^{\prime}\neq\mathbf{0},

\displaystyle|t_{j}n|\leqslant 2^{u}/d_{i,j,m},\quad|t^{\prime}_{j}n^{\prime}|\leqslant 2^{u^{\prime}}/d_{i^{\prime},j,m^{\prime}}\qquad(1\leqslant j\leqslant k).

Then

\displaystyle S_{u,u^{\prime}}(m,m^{\prime})=\frac{2^{-L(u+u^{\prime})}}{\#I_{m}\#I_{m^{\prime}}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in I_{m}\\
i^{\prime}\in I_{m^{\prime}}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\mathbf{0}\neq\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}}|\hat{\mu}(\mathbf{x})|N(\mathbf{x}).

This is a finite sum, since
N(\mathbf{x})=0
for large
\mathbf{x}
. Indeed, if
N(\mathbf{x})\geqslant 1
then

(4.1)

|x_{j}|\leqslant\frac{2^{u}}{d_{i,j,m}}+\frac{2^{u^{\prime}}}{d_{i^{\prime},j,m^{\prime}}}\ll\frac{2^{u+\tau m}}{\mathfrak{d}_{m}}+\frac{2^{u^{\prime}+\tau m^{\prime}}}{\mathfrak{d}_{m^{\prime}}}.

For
j=1,2,\ldots,k
, we can bound the number of possibilities for
t_{j},t_{j}^{\prime}
by

r_{j}:=\frac{2^{u+2-m}}{d_{i,j,m}}+1,\qquad r_{j}^{\prime}:=\frac{2^{u^{\prime}+2-m^{\prime}}}{d_{i^{\prime},j,m^{\prime}}}+1,

respectively. Note that

r_{j}\ll\frac{2^{u+(\tau-1)m}}{\mathfrak{d}_{m}},\qquad r_{j}^{\prime}\ll\frac{2^{u^{\prime}+(\tau-1)m^{\prime}}}{\mathfrak{d}_{m^{\prime}}}.

To estimate
N(\mathbf{x})
, we decompose
N(\mathbf{x})=N_{1}(\mathbf{x})+N_{2}(\mathbf{x})
, where
N_{1}(\mathbf{x})
counts solutions with
\mathbf{t},\mathbf{t}^{\prime}
parallel. Without loss of generality, we assume that
m\leqslant m^{\prime}
.

4.1.
The partial-rank case

Since
\mathbf{x}\neq\mathbf{0}
we may, by symmetry, assume that
x_{1}\neq 0
. As
n\mathbf{t}+n^{\prime}\mathbf{t}^{\prime}=\mathbf{x}
for any solution counted by
N_{1}(\mathbf{x})
, the vectors
\mathbf{t}
and
\mathbf{t}^{\prime}
must be multiples of
\mathbf{x}
, with
t_{1}t_{1}^{\prime}\neq 0
. The count
N_{1}(\mathbf{x})
is therefore bounded by the number of solutions to

t_{1}n+t_{1}^{\prime}n^{\prime}=x_{1}.

Choosing the value of
t_{1}n\notin\{0,x_{1}\}
, then applying the divisor bound to determine
t_{1},n,t_{1}^{\prime},n^{\prime}
, gives

N_{1}(\mathbf{x})\ll 2^{\tau m}\left(\frac{2^{u+\tau m}}{\mathfrak{d}_{m}}+\frac{2^{u^{\prime}+\tau m^{\prime}}}{\mathfrak{d}_{m^{\prime}}}\right).

4.2.
The full-rank case

Since
\mathbf{t},\mathbf{t}^{\prime}
are not parallel we may, be symmetry, assume that the first two rows of
T
are linearly independent. As
(t_{1},t_{2})\neq(0,0)
, we may also assume that
t_{1}\neq 0
. We begin by choosing
t_{1}\neq 0
, as well as
t^{\prime}_{1}
and
t_{2}
. Using that

t_{1}n+t^{\prime}_{1}n^{\prime}=x_{1},\qquad t_{2}n+t^{\prime}_{2}n^{\prime}=x_{2},

we see that

(t_{2}t^{\prime}_{1}-t_{1}t^{\prime}_{2})n^{\prime}=t_{2}x_{1}-t_{1}x_{2}.

The left hand side is non-zero, so

n^{\prime}\mid t_{2}x_{1}-t_{1}x_{2}\neq 0.

Thus, by the divisor bound, the number of possibilities for
n^{\prime}
is at most
O(2^{(m+m^{\prime})\tau}).
After choosing
n^{\prime}
, we can determine at most one possibility for
n
via

t_{1}n+t^{\prime}_{1}n^{\prime}=x_{1},

and at most one possibility for
t^{\prime}_{2}
via

t_{2}n+t^{\prime}_{2}n^{\prime}=x_{2}.

Choosing
t_{3},\dots,t_{k}
will then determine
t^{\prime}_{3},\dots,t^{\prime}_{k}
in at most one way. The upshot is that

N_{2}(\mathbf{x})\ll r_{1}r_{1}^{\prime}r_{2}r_{3}\cdots r_{k}2^{(m+m^{\prime})\tau}\ll\left(\frac{2^{u+(\tau-1)m}}{\mathfrak{d}_{m}}\right)^{k}\frac{2^{u^{\prime}+(\tau-1)m^{\prime}}}{\mathfrak{d}_{m^{\prime}}}2^{(m+m^{\prime})\tau}.

4.3.
VTP conclusion

Combining our bounds on
N_{1}(\mathbf{x})
and
N_{2}(\mathbf{x})
with (
4.1
) furnishes

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{x}\neq\mathbf{0}}|\hat{\mu}(\mathbf{x})|N(\mathbf{x})\ll 2^{O(\tau)m^{\prime}}\left(\frac{2^{u}}{\mathfrak{d}_{m}}+\frac{2^{u^{\prime}}}{\mathfrak{d}_{m^{\prime}}}\right)^{k-\mathrm{dim}_{\ell^{1}}(\mu)}\left(\frac{2^{u-m}}{\mathfrak{d}_{m}}\right)^{k}\frac{2^{u^{\prime}-m^{\prime}}}{\mathfrak{d}_{m^{\prime}}}.

Now, taking
L\geqslant 1
sufficiently large,

S(m,m^{\prime})\ll 2^{O(\tau)m^{\prime}}\left(\frac{1}{\mathfrak{d}_{m}}+\frac{1}{\mathfrak{d}_{m^{\prime}}}\right)^{k-\mathrm{dim}_{\ell^{1}}(\mu)}\left(\frac{2^{-m}}{\mathfrak{d}_{m}}\right)^{k}\frac{2^{-m^{\prime}}}{\mathfrak{d}_{m^{\prime}}}.

Therefore

\displaystyle E

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{m,m^{\prime}\leqslant\frac{\log N}{\log 2}}w_{m}w_{m^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\qquad\cdot\left(2^{O(\tau)m^{\prime}}\left(\frac{1}{\mathfrak{d}_{m}}+\frac{1}{\mathfrak{d}_{m^{\prime}}}\right)^{k-\mathrm{dim}_{\ell^{1}}(\mu)}\left(\frac{2^{-m}}{\mathfrak{d}_{m}}\right)^{k}\frac{2^{-m^{\prime}}}{\mathfrak{d}_{m^{\prime}}}\right)

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{m,m^{\prime}\leqslant\frac{\log N}{\log 2}}w_{m}w_{m^{\prime}}2^{O(\tau)m^{\prime}}(2^{m^{\prime}(k+1)/k})^{k-\mathrm{dim}_{\ell^{1}}(\mu)}2^{m}2^{m^{\prime}/k}.

Since

E_{N}({\lambda})\asymp\sum_{m\leqslant\frac{\log N}{\log 2}}2^{m}w_{m}

whenever
N
is a power of two, and since
\tau
is small, it remains to show that

(1+1/k)(k-\mathrm{dim}_{\ell^{1}}(\mu))+1/k<1.

This is nothing more than a rearrangement of (
1.1
).
∎

Proof of Theorem

For the convergent Khinchin statement, we may assume that

\psi(n)\geqslant(n\log^{2}n)^{-1/k}\qquad(n\geqslant 2).

Indeed, we may replace
\psi
by
\tilde{\psi}
, where
\tilde{\psi}(n)=\psi(n)+(n\log^{2}n)^{-1/k}
for

, since

and
\sum_{n}\tilde{\psi}(n)^{k}<\infty
.

We use
f_{n}(\mathbf{x})=A_{n}^{*}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{d})
from (
2.8
) where, for

\mathbf{d}=(d,\ldots,d),\qquad d=\psi(2^{m-1})/2^{m-1}.

Then

so, by the Cauchy condensation test and the convergence of
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\psi(n)^{k}

2.3
). Moreover, we have
f_{n}\gg 1
on

\{\mathbf{x}\in[0,1)^{k}:\max\{\|nx_{1}-y_{1}\|,\ldots,\|nx_{k}-y_{k}\|\}<\psi(n)\}.

By Lemma
2.3
, it remains to confirm the ETP.

For any
{\varepsilon}>0

gives

\sum_{n\in D_{m}}(\mu(f_{n})-{\lambda}(f_{n}))\ll_{\varepsilon}(2^{m-1})^{k+{\varepsilon}}(\psi(2^{m-1})/2^{m-1})^{{\kappa}-{\varepsilon}}.

For

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{m\leqslant M-1}(2^{m-1})^{k-{\kappa}+2{\varepsilon}}\psi(2^{m-1})^{{\kappa}-{\varepsilon}},

where

On the other hand,

Applying this to

\psi
by
\tilde{\psi}
, where

from §

on

and, since

we have (
2.3
) by the Cauchy condensation test and the convergence of
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\psi(n)(\log n)^{k-1}

2.3
, it remains to confirm the ETP. Note that
f_{n}
is a linear combination of

It therefore suffices to prove that

\sum_{n\in D_{m}}(\mu(A_{n}^{*})-{\lambda}(A_{n}^{*}))=o(2^{m-1}m^{k-1}\psi(2^{m-1})).

Recall that
\mu=\mu_{1}\times\cdots\times\mu_{k}

), there exists
{\kappa}
in the range

For any fixed

, Theorem

gives

\displaystyle\sum_{n\in D_{m}}(\mu(A_{n}^{*})-{\lambda}(A_{n}^{*}))

\displaystyle\ll(2^{m-1})^{k+{\varepsilon}}\sum_{i\in I_{m}}(d_{1}\cdots d_{k})^{{\kappa}}

\displaystyle\ll(2^{m-1})^{k+{\varepsilon}}\left(\frac{\psi(2^{m-1})}{2^{k(m-1)}}\right)^{{\kappa}}.

Writing

This is equivalent to

n^{k(1-{\kappa})-1+{\varepsilon}}=o(\psi(n)^{1-{\kappa}}),

which follows from the inequalities

\psi(n)\geqslant\frac{1}{n^{1+{\varepsilon}}},\qquad 1-{\kappa}<\frac{1}{k+1}

and the fact that

is small. This completes the proof of Theorem
1.12
.

5.3.
Proof of Theorem

The proof is similar to that of the Khinchin part of Theorem

3.2

\mu

Our setup is similar to that of the Khinchin part of Theorem

, the smooth function
f_{n}
is supported on a union of balls of radius
2\psi(2^{m-1})/2^{m-1}
, and

f_{n}\gg 1
on at set
\mathcal{S}_{n}
containing

A_{n}:=\{\mathbf{x}\in[0,1)^{k}:\max\{\|nx_{1}-y_{1}\|,\ldots,\|nx_{k}-y_{k}\|\}<\psi(n)\}.

Moreover, if
\mathbf{z}\in A_{n}
then
B_{r}(\mathbf{z})\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{n}
, where

r=r_{m}=\psi(2^{m-1})/2^{m}.

As
\mu
is
{\kappa}_{2}
-regular, we now see that
\bigcup_{n\in D_{m}}A_{n}\cap\mathcal{K}
is covered by a collection
\mathcal{B}_{m}
of hypercubes
B
of diameter
|B|\asymp r

centred in
\mathcal{K}
such that

\sum_{B\in\mathcal{B}_{m}}\mu(B)\ll\sum_{n\in D_{m}}\mu(\mathcal{S}_{n})\ll\sum_{n\in D_{m}}\mu(f_{n}).

Thus, by Theorem
3.2
and the
{\kappa}_{2}
-regularity of
\mu
, we have

\displaystyle r_{m}^{{\kappa}_{2}}\#\mathcal{B}_{m}\ll_{\varepsilon}2^{m-1}\psi(2^{m-1})^{k}+(2^{m-1})^{k+{\varepsilon}}r_{m}^{{\kappa}-{\varepsilon}}

for any constant
{\varepsilon}>0
,
where
{\kappa}=\mathrm{dim}_{\ell^{1}}(\mu)
.

Now, for any fixed
s>0
,

\sum_{B\in\mathcal{B}_{m}}|B|^{s}\ll r_{m}^{s}\#\mathcal{B}_{m}\ll r_{m}^{s-{\kappa}_{2}}(2^{m-1}\psi(2^{m-1})^{k}+(2^{m-1})^{k+{\varepsilon}}r_{m}^{{\kappa}-{\varepsilon}}).

By Theorem
1.14
and Lemma
2.2
, it remains to show that

(5.1)

\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}2^{m-1}r_{m}^{s-{\kappa}_{2}}\psi(2^{m-1})^{k}<\infty

and

(5.2)

(2^{m-1})^{k+{\varepsilon}}r_{m}^{{\kappa}-{\varepsilon}}\ll 2^{m-1}\psi(2^{m-1})^{k},

whenever
{\varepsilon}
is sufficiently small and

\frac{1}{k}<t<\frac{1}{k}+{\varepsilon},\qquad s>\frac{k+1}{t+1}+{\kappa}_{2}-k.

By the Cauchy condensation test, we have (
5.1
) if and only if

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(\psi(n)/n)^{s-{\kappa}_{2}}\psi(n)^{k}<\infty.

The latter indeed holds, since

(\psi(n)/n)^{s-{\kappa}_{2}}\psi(n)^{k}=n^{(t+1)({\kappa}_{2}-s)-tk}

and

\displaystyle(t+1)({\kappa}_{2}-s)-tk<(t+1)\left(k-\frac{k+1}{t+1}\right)-tk=-1.

With
n=2^{m-1}
, we can rewrite (
5.2
) as

n^{k+{\varepsilon}}(\psi(n)/n)^{{\kappa}-{\varepsilon}}\ll n\psi(n)^{k},

which is equivalent to

\psi(n)^{k-{\kappa}+{\varepsilon}}\gg n^{k-1+2{\varepsilon}-{\kappa}}.

By (
1.3
), the latter holds for any sufficiently small
{\varepsilon}
, since
\psi(n)=n^{-t}
and

\frac{{\kappa}-k}{k}>k-1-{\kappa}.

This confirms (
5.2
) and completes the proof of Theorem
1.15
.

Proof of Theorem

Let us write

{\kappa}=\mathrm{dim}_{\ell^{1}}(\mu),\qquad{\kappa}_{2}=\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{H}}(\mathcal{K}).

For the upper bound we use the smooth function

from (
2.8
), where

\mathbf{d}=({\delta}/N,\ldots,{\delta}/N),\qquad\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{0},

for each

and

\displaystyle\#\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{K}}(2N-1,{\delta})-\#\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{K}}(N-1,{\delta})

be the Cantor–Lebesgue measure of

Observe that

Appendix A
More about the Fourier
\ell^{1}
dimension

In this appendix, we further discuss Fourier
\ell^{1}
dimension for missing-digit measures.

A.1.
The Fourier
\ell^{1}
dimension in special cases

In a natural, wide class of special cases, we can rigorously estimate the Fourier
\ell^{1}
dimension. Building on
[
23
, Theorem 2.6]
, the following estimates were demonstrated in
[
58
, Theorem 2.15]
.

Theorem A.1

(Yu
[
58
]
)
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
\mu_{b,\mathcal{D}}
be the Cantor–Lebesgue measure of a missing-digit fractal
\mathcal{K}_{b,\mathcal{D}}
on
[0,1]^{k}
.

(a)

Let
t\in\mathbb{N}
. Then

\liminf_{\begin{subarray}{c}b\to\infty\\
\#\mathcal{D}\geqslant b^{k}-t\end{subarray}}\mathrm{dim}_{\ell^{1}}(\mu_{b,\mathcal{D}})=k.

In particular, if
{\varepsilon}>0
and
b
is sufficiently large, then

\mathrm{dim}_{\ell^{1}}(\mu_{b,D})>k-{\varepsilon}

holds whenever

\#\mathcal{D}=b^{k}-1.

(b)

Let
b\geqslant 4
be an integer, and suppose
\mathcal{D}
has the form

\mathcal{D}=[a_{1},b_{1}]\times[a_{2},b_{2}]\times\cdots\times[a_{k},b_{k}]\cap\{0,\dots,b-1\}^{k}.

Then

\mathrm{dim}_{\ell^{1}}(\mu_{b,\mathcal{D}})\geqslant\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{H}}(\mathcal{K}_{b,\mathcal{D}})-\frac{k\log(2\log b)}{\log b}.

A.2.
Estimating the Fourier
\ell^{1}
dimension

Here we provide a general algorithm to estimate the Fourier
\ell^{1}
dimension of a missing-digit measure
\mu=\mu_{b,\mathbb{P}}
on
[0,1]^{k}.
The strategy comes from
[
23
]
. For
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
, define

g(\mathbf{x})=\left|\sum_{\mathbf{d}\in\{0,1,\ldots,b-1\}^{k}}\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{d})e(\mathbf{d}\cdot\mathbf{x})\right|.

Since the Fourier transform of the distribution of a sum of independent random variables is the product of the Fourier transforms of the individual distributions, we have

(A.1)

|\hat{\mu}(\mathbf{x})|=\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}g(b^{-j}\mathbf{x}).

For
L\in\mathbb{N}
and
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
, define

S_{L}(\mathbf{x})=\prod_{j=0}^{L-1}g(b^{j}\mathbf{x}).

Theorem A.2
.

If
L\in\mathbb{N}
then

\mathrm{dim}_{\ell^{1}}(\mu)\geqslant\frac{-\log\left(\displaystyle\sup_{\mathbf{x}}b^{-kL}\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}=0}^{b^{L}-1}S_{L}\left(\mathbf{x}+\frac{\mathbf{i}}{b^{L}}\right)\right)}{\log(b^{L})}.

Proof.

We may assume without loss of generality that
L=1
. Indeed, if
L\geqslant 2
, then we can replace
b
by
b^{L}
and
\mathbb{P}
by the distribution of

\sum_{j=0}^{L-1}\mathbf{d}^{(j)}b^{j},

where the
\mathbf{d}^{(j)}
are independent random variables distributed according to
\mathbb{P}
. This leaves
\mu
unchanged, and replaces
S_{L}
by
S_{1}=g
.

It now suffices to prove that

\sum_{\|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\|_{\infty}\leqslant Q}|\hat{\mu}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})|\ll Q^{k-s}\qquad(Q\in\mathbb{N}),

where

s=\frac{-\log\left(\displaystyle\sup_{\mathbf{x}}b^{-k}\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}=0}^{b-1}g\left(\mathbf{x}+\frac{\mathbf{i}}{b}\right)\right)}{\log b}.

Furthermore, we may assume that
Q=b^{N}-1
for some
N\in\mathbb{N}
. Thus, it remains to show that

\sum_{\|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\|_{\infty}<b^{N}}|\hat{\mu}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})|\ll\left(\displaystyle\sup_{\mathbf{x}}\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}=0}^{b-1}g\left(\mathbf{x}+\frac{\mathbf{i}}{b}\right)\right)^{N}.

Arguing by induction, we establish the sharper and more general inequality

\displaystyle\sup_{\mathbf{y}}\sum_{\|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\|_{\infty}<b^{N}}|\hat{\mu}(\mathbf{y}+{\boldsymbol{\xi}})|\leqslant\left(\sup_{\mathbf{x}}\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}=0}^{b-1}g\left(\mathbf{x}+\frac{\mathbf{i}}{b}\right)\right)^{N},

for every integer
N\geqslant 0
.
The case
N=0
is simply the fact that
|\hat{\mu}(\mathbf{y})|\leqslant 1
for all
\mathbf{y}
.

Let us now take
N\geqslant 1
, and assume the inequality with
N-1
in place of
N
. By (
A.1
),

|\hat{\mu}(\mathbf{x})|=g(b^{-1}\mathbf{x})|\hat{\mu}(b^{-1}\mathbf{x})|\qquad(\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}).

Writing
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}={\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{(1)}+b{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{(2)}
now gives

\displaystyle\sum_{\|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\|_{\infty}<b^{N}}|\hat{\mu}(\mathbf{y}+{\boldsymbol{\xi}})|

\displaystyle=\sum_{\|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{(1)}\|_{\infty}<b}g(b^{-1}\mathbf{y}+b^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{(1)})\sum_{\|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{(2)}\|_{\infty}<b^{N-1}}|\hat{\mu}(b^{-1}\mathbf{y}+b^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{(1)}+{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{(2)})|.

Thus, by the inductive hypothesis,

\displaystyle\sum_{\|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\|_{\infty}<b^{N}}|\hat{\mu}(\mathbf{y}+{\boldsymbol{\xi}})|

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{\|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{(1)}\|_{\infty}<b}g(b^{-1}\mathbf{y}+b^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{(1)})\left(\sup_{\mathbf{x}}\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}=0}^{b-1}g\left(\mathbf{x}+\frac{\mathbf{i}}{b}\right)\right)^{N-1}

\displaystyle\leqslant\left(\sup_{\mathbf{x}}\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}=0}^{b-1}g\left(\mathbf{x}+\frac{\mathbf{i}}{b}\right)\right)^{N}.

This closes the induction and completes the proof.
∎

We expect that replacing the maximum with a minimum would give rise to an upper bound. However, we also expect that a proof of this would be technical and lengthy. Moreover, the lower bound is what is needed for applications. For these reasons, we do not establish the upper bound.

Note by periodicity that the lower bound in Theorem
A.2
is effectively computable.
