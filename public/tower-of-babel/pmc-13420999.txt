Multiecho Fat‐Water Spiral MR Elastography With Distributed Encoding for Simultaneously Imaging Brain and Skull Displacement
Authors: Alexa M. Diano, Alex M. Cerjanic, Olivia M. Bailey, Mary K. Kramer, Joy Mojumder, Ruth J. Okamoto, Philip V. Bayly, Dzung L. Pham, Curtis L. Johnson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurposeTo introduce a novel multiecho, multishot spiral MR elastography (MRE) sequence to image brain and skull displacement simultaneously using a joint fat‐water image reconstruction and distributed motion encoding.MethodsMultiple echo times were implemented into a high‐resolution multishot spiral MRE sequence to capture water‐based brain signal and fat‐based skull signal in a single acquisition. A joint fat‐water reconstruction was optimized for brain‐skull imaging, and a distributed motion encoding technique was incorporated to reduce acquisition time. This work demonstrates the feasibility of this sequence to quantify relative brain‐skull displacement and brain tissue mechanical properties.ResultsBrain and skull displacement were successfully imaged in a single acquisition. This technique was validated through comparison to a reference EPI MRE scan in which no significant differences were found in mechanical properties derived from each scan. A preliminary analysis of brain and skull displacement over time revealed that relative brain‐skull displacement was variable across the surface of the brain, with a higher amplitude of brain displacement at the anterior of the head.ConclusionThe proposed multiecho fat‐water spiral MRE sequence is a promising technique for quantifying brain‐skull displacement in vivo to improve computational modeling of traumatic brain injury.

Introduction

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) is a leading cause of death and disability affecting almost 70 million people worldwide each year [1, 2]. Despite the severity and prevalence of TBI, the brain's response to trauma and the mechanisms involved with injury remain unclear. A major limiting factor in TBI research is the lack of available methods to observe the in vivo response of brain tissue to a mild injury and effectively study the resulting brain‐skull kinematics. As such, finite element models are the primary tool used to understand TBI and how forces from a head impact or blast are transmitted to the brain, causing strain deformation, and ultimately tissue damage [3]. These brain biomechanical models have become more accurate in recent years through the incorporation of data from advanced imaging methods [4]. Previous work has shown that the brain‐skull interface, consisting of the meninges, plays an important role in the protection of the brain against injury [5], though measuring the mechanics of this interface in vivo is challenging and the transmissibility of motion from the skull to the brain remains undefined [6]. Researchers have observed the presence of traumatic meningeal enhancement in mild TBI patients, emphasizing the need to characterize the mechanics at the brain‐skull interface [7, 8, 9, 10, 11]. Gaining a better understanding of the relationship between an injury‐inducing skull acceleration, subject‐specific brain biomechanics, and the resulting brain deformation is an essential component for accurately modeling TBI.

Magnetic resonance elastography (MRE) is a noninvasive imaging technique that measures the mechanical properties of soft tissue in vivo [12, 13]. MRE has previously been shown to be a sensitive imaging modality for assessing brain tissue viscoelasticity across different aging milestones, with changes in tissue stiffness observed during development and across later life [14, 15]. Additionally, MRE has been instrumental for capturing spatial heterogeneity through in vivo regional evaluation, showing notable differences in shear stiffness between white matter, cortical gray matter, and subcortical gray matter, as well as significant variability even between healthy participants [15, 16, 17]. This subject individuality, coupled with the known viscoelastic changes from aging, disease, and injury, highlights the importance of a method able to capture subject‐specific brain mechanics for understanding brain injury risk. As previous studies have shown that the incorporation of brain mechanical properties has improved outputs in computational models of TBI, it is particularly important for these properties to accurately reflect subject‐specific in vivo brains [18, 19, 20].

The motion‐sensitive nature of MRE enables it to capture how brain tissue responds to an external force in the small‐strain regime, which, when coupled with subject‐specific mechanical properties, creates a dynamic contribution to computational models of injury [21]. Prior studies have evaluated MRE‐derived strain fields for use in determining regions of the brain tissue that may be more vulnerable to injury [22, 23]. Beyond evaluating strain deformation, researchers have used MRE with multiple wave patterns to reveal unique directionally dependent characteristics, crucial for modeling various types of injury [24, 25]. MRE provides insight regarding brain tissue displacement; however, motion resulting from head impacts primarily initiates in the skull, and MR technology is currently limited in its ability to image skull displacement. Imaging the cortical bone of the skull requires an ultrashort echo time, which is not compatible with existing MRE sequences. The ability to analyze the kinematics of both brain and skull in response to an external force would greatly benefit models of injury mechanics.

The objective of this work is to enhance an existing high‐resolution MRE sequence to image both brain and skull displacement using a chemically encoded spiral imaging protocol and a joint fat‐water image reconstruction. An important study conducted by Yin et al. imaged skull motion through an MRE sequence sensitized to signal from the fatty bone marrow within the medullary cavity of the skull [26]. With this protocol, this group investigated repeated head impacts illustrating the potential for its use as a sensitive imaging biomarker for TBI [27, 28]. Using a single‐shot echoplanar imaging (EPI) sequence, they acquired sequential scans with water or fat excitation separately to image brain and skull displacement, respectively. We build on this idea with multishot spiral readouts which provide advantages over EPI sequences that are constrained by distortion and low SNR from long readouts [29, 30]. These limitations are further amplified by the high spatial resolution necessary to image the thin geometry of the skull. Furthermore, the use of fat excitation to image skull marrow may lead to signal contamination from the neighboring scalp region, which also has high fat content, highlighting the need for a technique capable of acquiring images with high spatial resolution and low distortion. For this reason, we incorporated a spiral k‐space trajectory to achieve higher spatial resolution while maintaining SNR and minimizing image artifacts from magnetic field inhomogeneity.

Here, we propose a multiecho spiral MRE sequence with distributed motion encoding, which will enable researchers to acquire high‐resolution images of brain and skull displacement in a reasonable acquisition time. Outlined below is a description of the implementation of multiecho imaging with distributed‐encoding MRE and the incorporation of a joint fat‐water image reconstruction. We further investigate the feasibility of the proposed technique in four participants to validate the quality of displacement data acquired in vivo and demonstrate the ability to capture high‐quality maps of mechanical properties through comparison to a traditional MRE sequence.

Methods

Multiecho Spiral Sequence and Joint Fat‐Water Image Reconstruction

To achieve data necessary for simultaneous imaging of fat and water, to capture skull and brain displacement, we implemented a 2D multishot spiral spin‐echo MRE sequence with two echo times, shown in Figure 1A. Here, the first echo time (TE1) is at the spin echo when fat and water signal are in‐phase and the second (TE2) is an asymmetric spin echo when a phase shift is present between fat and water, providing sufficient contrast for the joint reconstruction described below. Following preliminary experimentation, we chose an echo spacing of 0.5 ms, which was determined empirically to provide good fat‐water separation in the proposed sequence. Spiral MRE imaging parameters included: 240 × 240 mm2 FOV; 120 × 120 matrix size; 2 mm isotropic resolution; 40 slices; TR = 4000 ms; TE1/TE2 = 55.0/55.5 ms; six designed constant‐density interleaved spiral shots with a reduction factor of Rxy = 2. MRE displacements were encoded with a motion encoding gradient amplitude of 30 mT/m. Total acquisition time for the proposed scan was 4 min 24 s.

(A) Pulse sequence diagram for the proposed multiecho spiral MRE sequence with multiple echo times (TE1 and TE2) for encoding fat and water signal, and (B) sample motion encoding directions for traditional MRE and MRE with distributed generalized encoding.

Images were reconstructed jointly using data acquired at both echo times to simultaneously resolve water and fat signals. We adapted an iterative MR image reconstruction approach for multishot spiral imaging, where an image is estimated by minimizing the difference between the measured data and the image estimate from a forward model [31]: (1) x^=argminx‖y−ASx‖22+βR(x)

The system matrix, A, incorporates the signal model for non‐Cartesian spiral sampling [32] and spatially varying off‐resonance information obtained from an auxiliary field inhomogeneity map [31]. Operator S incorporates coil sensitivities for parallel imaging with SENSE [33], R denotes the roughness penalty, and 𝛽 is the regularization weighting parameter. To enable fat‐water separation, we modified the system matrix to include water and fat as separate components, Aw and Af, by accounting for the chemical shift between the two [34]: (2) Aw(ΔTE)mn=ei2πk⃑m·r⃑neiΔωntm+ΔTE (3) Af(ΔTE)mn=ei2πk⃑m·r⃑neiΔωn+Δωftm+ΔTE here Δωn is the field inhomogeneity at spatial position n, Δωf is the chemical shift of fat relative to water, and tm is the sampling time of the k‐space point m. We define sampling time within an individual trajectory, relative to the echo time, and denote the difference in echo time relative to the symmetric spin echo as ΔTE. In this work, a single‐peak model for the fat signal was used with a shift of 430.71 Hz from the water peak, determined based on the specific field strength of the 3 T scanner at our center.

The resulting objective function equation for resolving both fat and water images, x^f and x^w, is then solved using a penalized least squares solver via conjugate‐gradient descent [35]: (4) x^wx^f=argminxw,xfyΔTE1yΔTE2−AwΔTE1SAfΔTE1SAwΔTE2SAfΔTE2Sxwxf22+βwRxw+βfRxf

Thus, each echo is modeled as containing contributions from both water and fat, and signals from both components are jointly estimated from the combined data at both TE1 and TE2. In our implementation, the first echo time is the spin echo where fat and water are in‐phase and ΔTE1 = 0, and ΔTE2 = 0.5 ms represents the asymmetric second echo. The forward and adjoint operations of Aw and Af, incorporating off‐resonance, were implemented via the nonuniform fast Fourier transform and time segmentation [31] with a segment length of 0.5 ms of the readout for computational tractability. The proposed image reconstruction method generates images from both water and fat signals, x^w and x^f, which both have phase accumulated via MRE motion encoding. Specific reconstruction parameters, including the chemical shift frequency, number of time segments, and regularization weighting, were optimized in preliminary experiments to balance complete fat‐water separation and SNR.

Distributed Encoding and Displacement Estimation

In this proposed sequence we adopt the distributed, generalized motion encoding method previously described by Kramer et al. [36] Compared to traditional MRE motion encoding, in the distributed encoding method, motion encoding gradients are applied along several nonorthogonal directions distributed over the surface of a sphere. Each encoding direction is also sampled with a different phase offset, such that motion encodings are uniquely distributed in both space and time. This allows us to estimate full 3D displacements from a reduced number of samples with minimal redundancy. Traditional MRE acquires 24 total images which account for motion encoding across three orthogonal directions, at positive and negative gradient polarities to remove unwanted background phase, with each sampled at a minimum of four evenly spaced phase offsets to capture wave propagation through time. For distributed encoding, each direction has a different phase offset, which is uniformly distributed over one period of applied vibration. In the proposed sequence, we use 10 encoding vectors distributed in space and time to reconstruct the displacement fields in both brain and skull regions in an accelerated scan time (Figure 1B). Specific directional components of each encoding vector can be found in Table A1. Although seven directions is the theoretical minimum number needed to resolve displacements with this technique, preliminary testing showed that 10 directions provided a sufficient reduction in scan time while more accurately resolving displacements in the presence of large rigid‐body motion from skull vibrations, which are difficult to unwrap. Traditional brain MRE with high sensitivity to motion can cause substantial phase wrapping in the skull region, which primarily consists of rigid‐body motion, and is difficult to unwrap spatially. Previous approaches have used multiple scans with both high and low sensitivity motion encoding, such as the dual‐sensitivity motion encoding scheme described in Yin et al. [26]. However, with the distributed encoding technique, the entire ensemble of encodings contains both high and low components of motion encoding sensitivity in each direction, allowing for data to be “unwrapped” during displacement estimation [36], which ultimately allows us to resolve rigid‐body motion in brain and skull using the proposed approach.

To resolve the displacement fields, the brain regions were first automatically segmented from the water images using the brain extraction tool from the FMRIB Software Library (FSL) [37]. Skull, represented by marrow signal, and scalp regions of interest were segmented from the fat images using manual delineation. Fat and water phase images for each region of interest were processed using the harmonic motion estimator for distributed encoding, described by Kramer et al. [36]. For the brain images, regularization was applied during the estimation with a roughness penalty weighting of λ = 2. After the solution converged, this weighting was lowered to λ = 0.001 to maintain spatial resolution. The scalp images were estimated using the same parameters as the brain, whereas for the skull, we increased the roughness penalty weighting to λ = 20, as it is expected to have more uniform displacement due to its rigidity. This process resulted in full vector displacement fields in the brain, skull, and scalp images for each participant.

Participants and Data Acquisition

We evaluated the proposed approach by scanning four male human subjects (26–40 years old). This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. All data was collected on a Siemens 3 T Prisma MRI scanner with a 20‐channel head/neck receive coil (Siemens Healthineers; Erlangen, Germany). To acquire MRE data, small vibrations were applied to the head, at a frequency of 50 Hz, using a pneumatic actuator (Resoundant Inc., Rochester, Minnesota) connected to a soft pillow passive driver, placed beneath the participant's head, with foam padding to minimize spurious motion. Supplemental scans included a spin echo coil‐sensitivity and field mapping scan for use in the MRE image reconstruction, and high‐resolution T1‐weighted MPRAGE and Dixon fat‐water structural scans for registration and anatomical comparison. Imaging parameters for the MPRAGE scan included: 240 × 240 mm2 FOV; 0.9 mm isotropic resolution; TR = 2300 ms; TE = 2.32 ms. Imaging parameters for the Dixon scan included: 180 × 180 mm2 FOV; 0.6 × 0.6 × 2.0 mm3 resolution; 40 slices; TR = 706 ms; TE = 12 ms.

Motion Data Analysis

Brain, skull, and scalp displacement fields, determined via harmonic motion estimation from the distributed encoding data [36], were processed to separate rigid‐body (bulk) motion from wave motion. Skull and scalp displacements derived from the fat images were fit to a model of rigid‐body motion that included both translational and rotational motion around a center of mass, defined for each participant as the volumetric center of the brain [38]. Brain displacements from the water images were also fit to a model of rigid‐body motion with the same center of mass to remove bulk translation and rotation, allowing for the separate analysis of wave motion in the brain. Rigid body displacements (u‾) were estimated using the equations below where u‾O is the translational component and θ is the rotational component, and r is the voxel position relative to the center of the brain [38, 39]: (5a) u‾LR=u‾LRO+θAPrSI−θSIrAP, (5b) u‾AP=u‾APO+θSIrLR−θLRrSI, (5c) u‾SI=u‾SIO+θLRrAP−θAPrLR.

To evaluate the dynamics of the brain and skull over time, we selected points along the exterior of the brain at four positions corresponding to the anterior, posterior, left, and right regions of the head. We calculated an “expected” rigid‐body displacement field throughout the entire brain based only on the coefficients of skull motion [40]. The relative displacement field on the brain surface is estimated from the magnitude of the difference between actual brain motion and expected rigid‐body motion on the brain surface. Relative displacement was normalized by total skull bulk motion for each participant. Analysis of this field provides quantitative information on local brain‐skull coupling in different regions of interest.

To confirm that we sufficiently captured displacement of the skull and avoided any signal contamination from the scalp or brain regions, we calculated the percentage of rigid‐body motion in the skull and scalp regions of the fat images. We expect the skull to be primarily rigid whereas the scalp should have more deformation. Previous studies have observed a difference between scalp and skull displacement [39], and Yin et al. established the presence of a slip interface between the scalp and skull, suggesting that the two neighboring regions have independent patterns of displacement [26].

For a regional analysis of relative brain‐skull displacement across the surface of the brain, we conducted cortical segmentation on each dataset using FreeSurfer with the T1‐weighted MPRAGE anatomical scan. Each participant's MRE magnitude image was registered to its respective anatomical image through FSL's FLIRT software [41, 42, 43]. We then obtained the inverse rigid transformation and applied this to each bilateral cortical region. All resulting images were visually inspected to ensure successful registration and accurate alignment between the displacement data and the cortical regions. The brain coverage from the imaging volume varied due to subject head size and position; therefore, only cortical regions containing data for at least three subjects were included in calculations of the mean.

Brain Mechanical Properties

Brain mechanical properties were estimated using nonlinear inversion (NLI) of the water‐derived displacement fields from the multiecho spiral MRE scan [44]. NLI is used to estimate maps of the complex shear modulus, G*=G′+iG′′, where G′ is the storage modulus, and G′′ is the loss modulus. We then used the complex shear modulus to calculate shear stiffness, μ=2G*2/G′+G*, and damping ratio, ξ=G′′/2G′, which are commonly reported metrics in MRE [45].

For validation, this sequence was compared to a standard EPI MRE sequence with traditional motion encoding, resulting in 24 total images, using the same resolution and imaging coverage but only one echo time. Imaging parameters included: 240 × 240 mm2 FOV; 120 × 120 matrix size; 2 mm isotropic resolution; 40 slices; TR = 4800 ms; TE = 56 ms; 50 Hz actuation frequency; four phase offsets; motion encoding gradient amplitude = 30 mT/m. This was used to validate the water images acquired in the brain by providing a comparison for the mechanical property maps. Phase images from the EPI sequence underwent a complex subtraction to remove background phase, phase unwrapping with FSL PRELUDE [46], and temporal Fourier filtering, resulting in displacement maps in each of three orthogonal directions. NLI and parameters were consistent between spiral and EPI datasets for comparison. Multiscale structural similarity index (MSSIM) and percent difference between the spiral and EPI shear stiffness and damping ratio images were calculated for each participant.

Results

Figure 2 presents fat and water images from the proposed MRE sequence. The magnitude images show good agreement with the Dixon fat‐water anatomical scan. Displacement images show motion resolved in both the brain and skull. The highest amplitudes of displacement are seen in the anterior–posterior direction (Y), with the lowest amplitudes in the left–right (X) direction. Displacement in the superior–inferior direction (Z) shows motion in the anterior and posterior parts of the skull out of phase due to the “nodding” motion of the head from applied vibration. These patterns are all consistent with vibration applied to the back of the head [22, 47, 48].

In Figure 3, we analyze motion in the skull compared to the scalp, which is also present in the fat image. Across all subjects, we observed a high percentage of bulk motion in the skull (91%–97%). This is expected as we assume the skull to move primarily as a rigid body. In contrast, scalp displacement comprises only 16%–30% bulk motion, which is consistent with a previous analysis that showed the scalp moves differently from the skull and significantly deforms during an MRE experiment [26]. This analysis also demonstrates that there is minimal signal contamination from the scalp in the skull region due to residual distortion and blurring, which could cause the analyzed motion of the skull to appear more similar to the scalp.

Measured displacements in the anterior–posterior (AP) and superior–inferior (SI) directions were the highest for each analyzed point. Figure 6 shows resulting maps of relative brain‐skull displacement, highlighting variation across regions of the brain. Specifically, we observed an average normalized relative brain‐skull displacement of 0.094 in the frontal lobe and 0.19 in the temporal lobe indicating higher brain displacement compared to skull. In the parietal and occipital lobes, the average normalized relative displacements were −0.016 and −0.061 respectively, suggesting increased skull displacement, or less motion transmission to the brain. An average positive displacement ratio in the frontal lobe compared to the negative displacement apparent in the posterior of the brain further reinforces the likelihood of a reduced anterior brain‐skull coupling. Discussion

In this work we introduce a novel sequence that uses multiecho fat‐water MRE with distributed encoding to image skull and brain displacement for quantifying the brain mechanics critical to understanding and modeling TBI. Previous work has shown that fat‐water imaging is a promising method for quantifying skull displacement [26, 27, 28]. Yin et al. used sequential EPI MRE scans with fat and water excitation to image skull and brain motion, respectively [26]. Here, we similarly imaged motion in both brain and skull by implementing a multiecho spiral MRE sequence with iterative image reconstruction. The incorporation of multishot spiral imaging enabled us to increase spatial resolution to 2 mm while the iterative reconstruction corrected for field inhomogeneity, ultimately reducing distortion and enhancing our ability to resolve displacement in the thin skull region. By acquiring brain and skull displacement in a single scan, we also eliminate any potential issues or artifacts due to subject motion between scans, providing a direct measurement of skull‐to‐brain force transmission.

We can compare the proposed sequence with the previous approach described by Yin et al., which uses sequential EPI MRE acquisitions utilizing different water‐selective or fat‐selective spatial‐spectral excitation pulses [26, 27, 28]. Their approach allowed for both fat and water images to be acquired with 3 mm isotropic resolution in 3 min 20 s. In contrast, we achieved higher resolution at 2 mm isotropic voxel size by tuning a multishot spiral MRE sequence with field inhomogeneity correction to minimize distortion, and incorporation of multiple echo times into a single sequence. We offset the additional scan time for multiple spiral shots through parallel imaging and the incorporation of the distributed motion encoding scheme [36]. Distributed encoding allows for accurate estimation of 3D motion fields from a reduced set of samples, such as the 10 samples we used in this work versus the 24 samples used in traditional MRE, which resulted in a total acquisition time of 4 min 24 s. This proposed sequence offers further flexibility to improve resolution via number of spiral shots designed and used, and the use of multiple echo times could also be incorporated into a full 3D acquisition, such as those recently described for brain MRE [49, 50, 51], and reconstructed with a similar algorithm.

Previous studies have sought to measure brain and skull dynamics to assess the importance in its relation to TBI. Badachhape et al. analyzed skull and brain displacement in vivo using MRE to measure brain displacement and accelerometers to measure skull displacement [38, 39]. While this technique was limited in its ability to accurately capture brain‐skull coupling due to different measurement modalities, it provided a framework for leveraging the motion‐sensitivity of MRE to assess the biomechanical response of brain tissue to an external force. A crucial component of brain‐skull kinematics is the rigid‐body motion of the brain and skull. The scale of bulk motion in MRE is significantly larger than harmonic wave motion, which can cause issues with temporal unwrapping. Previous analyses of bulk motion in the brain have used an additional scan to guide the unwrapping [38], or a dual‐sensitivity encoding technique [26] to capture both harmonic and bulk motion in MRE. In this work, our incorporation of distributed motion encoding [36] not only led to a significant reduction in scan time but also allowed for the acquisition of high and low sensitivity motion encoding in a single scan. This enabled the spatial and temporal unwrapping necessary to account for the increased displacement in the skull compared to brain tissue, ultimately capturing rigid‐body motion of the skull and generating sufficient wave propagation throughout the brain tissue to quantify mechanical properties.

We analyzed motion captured from the proposed sequence and compared it with previous studies examining brain and skull displacement. The trajectories of brain and skull motion, shown in Figure 4, demonstrated similar trends to those reported in Badachhape et al., specifically in the observation of higher amplitude brain displacement at the anterior of the head [38]. However, our findings suggest that the brain and skull are more closely coupled than previous work has shown, as they appear to move in phase with each other at similar amplitudes of displacement. While Yin et al., who also used MRE‐based measurements of both brain and skull, found a similar relationship between brain and skull in translational motion, they observed a greater difference between brain and skull rotational displacement [26]. We observed a similar trend, with similarities in brain and skull translation, while the skull had much higher rotational displacement than the brain (Figure 5). Overall, the similarity of our findings to previous works supports the data quality achievable with the proposed approach.

Relative brain‐skull displacement was calculated from the difference between total brain motion and expected bulk motion of the skull on the surface of the brain, similar to previous studies of brain‐skull displacement in a minipig model [40, 52]. We observed consistent amplitudes of relative displacement across participants, but this varied significantly across the surface of the brain (Figure 6). We know from previous work that brain tissue mechanical properties vary across subject populations and regionally within the brain [53, 54]. This work suggests similar variability exists in the mechanics of the brain‐skull interface and emphasizes an increased need to characterize this relationship across a larger number of participants. As TBI occurs under unique loading conditions and produces a high degree of variability in injury profiles [55, 56], incorporating these distinctions in mechanical properties and brain‐skull mechanics is crucial for developing computational models that represent a broad population.

Mechanical property estimates of the brain were also used for validation of this proposed sequence. We found that properties derived from our novel scan, including shear stiffness and damping ratio, were consistent with previously reported values for healthy adults in this age range [16]. No significant differences were observed between the images produced from the proposed sequence and the standard EPI MRE acquisition (Figure 7). This demonstrates that the multiecho spiral sequence and joint fat‐water image reconstruction approach preserves the image quality necessary to capture high‐quality maps of brain mechanical properties. Brain tissue material properties, such as those derived from MRE, are an important component of modeling TBI. The ability to capture these properties while simultaneously measuring relative brain‐skull displacement will provide additional advantages to model input parameters. While the acquired water‐based images were validated using a standard EPI MRE sequence with traditional motion encoding, demonstrating the success of the joint fat‐water reconstruction, we were not able to perform direct validation of the fat‐based MRE images beyond comparison of estimated skull kinematics with previous literature.

The preliminary measurements presented in this study primarily served to demonstrate performance of the proposed sequence and therefore we had a relatively small sample size for an in vivo experiment. Although resulting displacements and mechanical properties were consistent across subjects, a larger cohort will be needed to assess variability across a wider population. Interestingly, initial testing revealed significant variation in the concentration of fat in the bone marrow of the skull between individuals. This variability limited the study population to adults with higher fat content in the skull marrow. While research regarding the transition from red to yellow bone marrow in the human skull is limited, previous work conducted has shown that, in the vertebral marrow of the lumbar spine, there is a significant dependence on age and sex [57]. In male subjects, they observed a steady increase of fat content in the bone marrow with age while female subjects consistently had lower marrow adiposity than males until postmenopause [58]. Further work is needed to explore the transition from red to yellow bone marrow across different demographical groups and determine how these factors may influence the applicability of fat‐based MRE measurements of skull motion.

Traditional fat‐water imaging acquires two images with fat and water “in phase” and “out of phase” by using an echo spacing of approximately 1.1 ms to achieve a phase difference of π radians between fat and water at 3 T. However, our preliminary testing found that an echo spacing of 0.5 ms produced better fat‐water separation with the proposed MRE sequence, which we then adopted for the implementation presented in this work. We hypothesized that, because MRE is a phase contrast‐based technique and both fat and water components have significant phase accumulation from motion encoding, that less of a phase shift from a smaller echo gap allowed for better fat‐water separation in an MRE acquisition. Future work could seek to identify an optimal prescription of echo times for this application, which may also include additional echo time samples, though at the cost of increased scan time. Additionally, we used a single‐peak fat model which was sufficient for resolving skull fat and determining kinematics of skull motion, though a multipeak fat model could be incorporated which may additionally improve quantification of fat.

The benefits of MRE‐derived mechanical properties of the brain and kinematics of the brain‐skull interface for computational models of TBI can be further expanded by the inclusion of multiple frequencies or directions of actuation. Prior studies have highlighted the importance of frequency and direction of a force in the biomechanical response of the brain tissue [23, 48]. The incorporation of distributed motion encoding to accelerate our MRE sequence allows for the acquisition of more data within a single scan session, enabling us to collect data at multiple frequencies and directions of actuation in a reasonable time to avoid any subject discomfort. In addition to expanding the study population, future work aims to include a wider range of applied frequencies and different directions of mechanical actuation to comprehensively quantify the force transmission from skull to brain.

Conclusions

In this work we introduced a novel multiecho, multishot spiral MRE acquisition that leverages chemical‐shift‐induced fat‐water separation and distributed generalized motion encoding to simultaneously quantify brain and skull displacement. With this sequence we were able to capture both high‐sensitivity brain motion and low‐sensitivity bulk skull motion within a single scan. This work demonstrates the feasibility of advanced MR techniques to quantify brain‐skull displacement noninvasively and provide computational models of TBI with accurate measures of brain biomechanics.
