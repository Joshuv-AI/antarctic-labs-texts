Beyond Quadratic Costs: A Bregman Divergence Approach to H ∞ Control
Joudi Hajar, Reza Ghane, Babak Hassibi

Abstract
This paper presents a novel extension of the H ∞ H_{\infty} control framework that generalizes the traditional quadratic cost formulation to accommodate strictly convex, nonquadratic functions for the state, control input, and disturbance. This new formulation not only captures additional noise characteristics but also supports a range of performance objectives—including sparse control, safety constraints, and other tailored behaviors—beyond what is possible with quadratic costs. We derive a closed-form solution of a central controller that minimizes the worst-case performance ratio under the proposed cost structure. Furthermore, we develop Riccati-like equations that impose necessary and sufficient conditions on the nonquadratic cost functions, thereby ensuring the existence of a robust solution. Finally, we rigorously establish Lyapunov stability for the closed-loop system. The proposed framework bridges robust control theory with modern approaches in machine learning and signal processing, offering enhanced flexibility and improved performance in complex control scenarios.



I
Introduction and Related Works

Robust control has evolved significantly over recent decades, transitioning from classical formulations to sophisticated frameworks that handle increasingly complex systems. Beginning with the landmark work of
[
1
]
in 1981, the
H_{\infty}
control paradigm has been refined to address linear state-space models in the 1980s
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
and subsequently extended to nonlinear models in the 1990s
[
5
]
. This body of work has become a cornerstone of robust control theory, with numerous applications in diverse areas such as robotics
[
6
]
, electric machines
[
7
]
, and even traffic control
[
8
]
. In the traditional
H_{\infty}
framework, the main objective is to design a controller that mitigates the effects of worst-case disturbances by minimizing the maximum gain of the ratio between the cost incurred on the state and control input and the energy of the disturbance. This ratio is closely linked to the
L_{2}
gain of the system. However, a notable limitation of the classical quadratic
H_{\infty}
formulation is its inability to leverage additional side-information about the noise characteristics—such as its sporadic or sparse occurrence or the presence of a bounded
\ell_{\infty}
norm, and the inherent limitation imposed by the quadratic costs on both the state and the controller.
At the same time, disciplines such as machine learning and signal processing have progressed beyond quadratic cost functions. Techniques like the
\ell_{1}
regularizer are widely used in compressed sensing to induce sparsity, and cross-entropy loss functions are common in classification tasks. Despite these advances, control theory has largely maintained the reliance on quadratic costs to obtain closed-form controller expressions in both
H_{2}
and
H_{\infty}
settings. In our companion paper
[
9
]
, we demonstrated the benefits of incorporating Bregman divergences within the
H_{2}
control framework by exploiting their inherent “completion-of-squares” property. Concurrently, the present work develops a complementary theory for
H_{\infty}
control by generalizing its quadratic cost to a broader class of strictly convex functions. Notably, while the Bregman divergence appears directly in the cost formulation for the
H_{2}
problem, its role in the
H_{\infty}
setting is more nuanced, requiring a careful reformulation to preserve the robustness guarantees.

The primary contributions of this paper are as follows:

•

Generalization of the
H_{\infty}
Cost:
We extend the classical quadratic cost framework to incorporate strictly convex functions of the state, control input, and disturbance. This generalization allows the formulation to naturally integrate additional noise characteristics that are often overlooked in traditional approaches, while also emphasizing the benefits of nonquadratic cost functions for achieving more sophisticated and desirable controller behaviors.

•

Controller Construction:
For fully or overactuated systems, we derive the infinite-horizon (i.e., time-invariant) central controller corresponding to the new nonquadratic
H_{\infty}
cost, ensuring that it robustly minimizes the worst-case performance ratio.

•

Riccati-like Equations:
To guarantee the existence of the robust controller, we formulate Riccati-like equations tailored to the nonquadratic functions involved. These equations impose necessary conditions on the chosen cost functions, bridging the gap between classical quadratic designs and the more general setting.

•

Stability Analysis:
Our formulation guarantees Lyapunov stability, and the central controller is proven to be stabilizing.

Moreover, we note that game-theoretic approaches have been employed
[
10
]
where the central controller for the sub-optimal
H_{\infty}
problem emerges as the solution to a min-max game. Our results reveal that a similar interpretation holds in the nonquadratic regime. However, we note that the min-max game formulation is essentially a contrived problem that only considers causal disturbances. In contrast, the classical
H_{\infty}
control formulation is designed to guarantee robustness against
any
bounded-energy disturbance, regardless of causality. In our nonquadratic extension, even though a similar min-max interpretation can be drawn, we deliberately depart from this artificial game-theoretic framework, and we derive not only sufficient conditions, but also necessary ones for the existence of a
family
of
H_{\infty}
controllers. We employ a Bregman divergence formulation which allows for completion-of-squares, a key technique used in the derivation of the classical
H_{\infty}
controller
[
11
]
. We thus ensure robust performance in the true
H_{\infty}
sense.

In the following sections, we detail the theoretical underpinnings of our approach, outline the derivation of the central controller, and discuss the implications of our generalized framework across various application domains.

II-B

Quadratic
H_{\infty}
: Framework and Established Results

In this paper, we consider the discrete-time linear system described by

x_{k+1}=Ax_{k}+Bu_{k}+w_{k},

(1)

where
x_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
denotes the state,
u_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the control input, and
w_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
represents the disturbance. The disturbance sequence is assumed to have bounded energy, i.e.,

\|w_{k}\|_{2}^{2}<\infty
for every
k\geq 1
. For simplicity, the initial state is taken to be zero, i.e.,
x_{0}=0
.

Within the quadratic
H_{\infty}
control framework, our objective is to design a sequence of causal control laws that guarantees robust performance against any disturbance sequence
\{w_{k}\}_{k=0}^{T}
satisfying the above energy bound. This leads to the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{causal}\\
\{u_{k}\}\end{subarray}}\;\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{w_{k}\}\\
\|w_{k}\|_{2}^{2}<\infty\end{subarray}}\;J_{Q},

(2)

\displaystyle J_{Q}:=\frac{x_{T+1}^{\top}P_{T+1}x_{T+1}+\sum\limits_{k=0}^{T}\left(x_{k}^{\top}Q_{k}x_{k}+u_{k}^{\top}R_{k}u_{k}\right)}{\sum\limits_{k=0}^{T}w_{k}^{\top}S_{k}w_{k}}.

Given the inherent complexity of this formulation, a common approach is to consider a relaxation. Specifically, for a prescribed performance level
\gamma>0
, the problem is reformulated as follows.

Problem 1
.

For a given
\gamma>0
, find a sequence of causal controllers
\{u_{k}\}
such that for every disturbance sequence
\{w_{k}\}_{k=0}^{T}
satisfying
\|w_{k}\|_{2}^{2}<\infty
, the following performance bound is achieved:

\frac{x_{T+1}^{\top}P_{T+1}x_{T+1}+\sum\limits_{k=0}^{T}\left(x_{k}^{\top}Q_{k}x_{k}+u_{k}^{\top}R_{k}u_{k}\right)}{\sum\limits_{k=0}^{T}w_{k}^{\top}S_{k}w_{k}}\leq\gamma.

(3)

By employing a completion-of-squares argument along with appropriate matrix factorizations, one can derive not only a nominal (or “central”) controller but also a family of controllers that ensure the desired robust performance, as described by the following result from
[
11
]
.

Theorem 1
.

A full-information causal controller strategy
\check{u}_{k}=\mathcal{F}_{k}(w_{0},w_{1},\dots,w_{k})
,
that solves Problem
1
and achieves the desired
H_{\infty}
performance exists if and only if, for each time step
k
, the matrix

\Delta_{k}:=-\gamma^{2}S_{k}+P_{k+1}^{c}-P_{k+1}^{c}B\,R_{B^{c},k}^{-1}B^{\top}P_{k+1}^{c}

(4)

is negative semidefinite, i.e.,
\Delta_{k}\preceq 0
, where
R_{B^{c},k}:=Q_{k}+B^{\top}P_{k+1}^{c}B,
and the sequence
\{P_{k}^{c}\}
is obtained by the backward Riccati recursion

P_{k}^{c}=A^{\top}P_{k+1}^{c}A+R_{k}^{c}-K_{c,k}^{\top}R_{e,k}^{c}K_{c,k},

(5)

with the feedback gain defined by

K_{c,k}=\bigl(R_{e,k}^{c}\bigr)^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}B^{\top}\\
I\end{pmatrix}P_{k+1}^{c}A,

and

R_{e,k}^{c}=\begin{pmatrix}Q_{k}+B^{\top}P_{k+1}^{c}B&B^{\top}P_{k+1}^{c}\\
P_{k+1}^{c}B&-\gamma^{2}S_{k}+P_{k+1}^{c}\end{pmatrix}.

In this setting, all possible full-information causal controllers
\check{u}_{k}=\mathcal{F}_{k}(w_{0},\cdots,w_{k})
are given by those that satisfy

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{k=0}^{T}(\check{u}_{k}-\bar{u}_{k})^{\top}\!R_{B^{c},k}(\check{u}_{k}-\bar{u}_{k})

\displaystyle+\sum\limits_{k=0}^{T}\!(w_{k}-\hat{w}_{k})^{\top}\!\Delta_{k}(w_{k}-\hat{w}_{k})\!<\!0

(6)

where
\begin{pmatrix}\hat{u}_{k}\\
\hat{w}_{k}\end{pmatrix}=-K_{c,k}x_{k},

and
\bar{u}_{k}=-R^{-1}_{B^{c},k}B^{\top}P_{k+1}^{c}(Ax_{k}-w_{k}).

The central controller is given by
\check{u}_{k}=\bar{u}_{k}.

Theorem
1
links the existence of a robust
H_{\infty}
controller (for disturbances with bounded energy) to the solution of a Riccati recursion. It provides necessary and sufficient condition for a full-information controller to exist and provides a complete parametrization of all controllers achieving the desired performance. For further details, see
[
11
]
.

II-C

Bregman Divergence

The Bregman divergence is a measure of deviation of function from its linear approximation. For a strictly convex and differentiable function
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, and any two points
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
we define the Bregman divergence as:

D_{\phi}(x,y)=\phi(x)-\phi(y)-\nabla\phi(y)^{\top}(x-y).

(7)

This divergence quantifies the difference between the value of the convex function at
x
and its first-order Taylor approximation around
y
evaluated at
x
. We present a popular example of the bregman divergence.

Example 1
.

Squared Euclidean Distance:
Let
\phi(x)=\frac{1}{2}\|x\|_{2}^{2}
. Substituting into the definition of Bregman divergence yields:

D_{\phi}(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}\|x\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|y\|_{2}^{2}-y^{\top}(x-y)=\frac{1}{2}\|x-y\|_{2}^{2}.

Bregman divergence possesses several key properties that facilitate its application in optimization and control theory. The following lemma summarizes these properties, where the ”Completion of Squares”and ”Duality” properties lie at the heart of the proofs in this work.

Lemma 1
.

The following holds:

•

Non-Negativity
: For all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
we have that
D_{\phi}(x,y)\geq 0
, with
D_{\phi}(x,y)=0\Leftrightarrow

x=y
.

•

Asymmetry
: In general,
D_{\phi}(x,y)\neq D_{\phi}(y,x)
.

•

Convexity
: For a given
y
, the function
D_{\phi}(\cdot,y)
is convex in its first entry.

•

Law of Cosines:
For any three points
x,y,z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have:

\displaystyle D_{\phi}(x,y)=D_{\phi}(x,z)

\displaystyle+D_{\phi}(z,y)

\displaystyle-\left(\nabla\phi(y)-\nabla\phi(z)\right)^{\top}(x-z).

•

Duality:

D_{\phi}(x,y)=D_{\phi^{\ast}}(\nabla\phi(y),\nabla\phi(x))

•

Completion of Squares:
For two convex functions
\phi_{1}
and
\phi_{2}
, and points
x,y,z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have the following:

\displaystyle D_{\phi_{1}}(x,y)+D_{\phi_{2}}(x,z)

\displaystyle=D_{\phi_{1}+\phi_{2}}\left(x,x^{\ast}\right)

\displaystyle+D_{\phi_{1}}\left(x^{\ast},y\right)+D_{\phi_{2}}\left(x^{\ast},z\right),

(8)

where
x^{\ast}
satisfies:
\nabla(\phi_{1}+\phi_{2})(x^{\ast})=\nabla\phi_{1}(y)+\nabla\phi_{2}(z).

III
H_{\infty}
Control in the Infinite Horizon with Non-Quadratic Costs

We aim to move beyond conventional quadratic cost functions by incorporating more general
convex
penalties on the state
x_{k}
, the control
u_{k}
, and the disturbance
w_{k}
. In this extended framework, we derive infinite-horizon closed-form controllers for fully or overactuated systems—akin to those in the quadratic case—that are applicable even with non-quadratic cost functions. This generalization enables the design of control strategies that exhibit richer and more desirable behaviors, effectively addressing a broader range of performance objectives in practice.

III-A

Problem Setting and Assumptions

We consider the discrete-time system in (
1
). We extend the

H_{\infty}
problem to accommodate more general (non-quadratic) cost functions, in
an infinite-horizon framework
. In our formulation, the designer specifies the state cost
q(x_{k})
, the control cost
r(u_{k})
, and the disturbance cost
s(w_{k})
, where each function is assumed to be
strictly convex, even, and nonnegative
.

To capture the cost incurred after any finite horizon, we introduce a terminal cost (or storage function)

p(x),

Unlike the other cost functions,
p(x)
is
not arbitrarily chosen
by the designer; instead, it is determined by the infinite-horizon optimality conditions, and it is assumed to be strictly convex and positive. This property helps ensure both system stability and the well-posedness of the performance measure. In the quadratic
H_{\infty}
where all cost functions are quadratics, one recovers
p(x)=x^{\top}Px
with
P\succ 0
as the unique stabilizing solution of the
H_{\infty}
algebraic Riccati equation.

Problem 2
.

Original Problem Statement:

We consider the following infinite-horizon robust control problem. Our goal is to design a causal time-invariant control policy
\{u_{k}\}
where

u_{k}=\mathcal{F}(w_{0},w_{1},\dots,w_{k}),
which minimizes the worst-case
H_{\infty}
cost ratio over all disturbance sequences
\{w_{k}\}
having finite energy. That is,

\displaystyle\min_{\text{causal }\{u_{k}\}}\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\max_{\{w_{k}\}:\;s(w_{k})<\infty}J_{G},

(9)

\displaystyle J_{G}:=\frac{\,p(x_{T+1})+\sum\limits_{k=0}^{T}\bigl[q(x_{k})+r(u_{k})\bigr]}{\sum\limits_{k=0}^{T}s(w_{k})}.

(10)

Here,
q
,
r
, and
s
are designer-specified cost functions that are strictly convex, positive, and even. The storage function
p
, arises from infinite-horizon optimality conditions, and is also assumed to be strictly convex.

Relaxed Problem:

For a given performance level
\gamma>0
, find a causal time-invariant control policy
\{u_{k}\}
such that, for every finite horizon
T\geq 1
,

\max_{\{w_{k}\}:\;s(w_{k})<\infty}\;\frac{\,p(x_{T+1})+\sum\limits_{k=0}^{T}\bigl[q(x_{k})+r(u_{k})\bigr]}{\sum\limits_{k=0}^{T}s(w_{k})}\leq\gamma^{2},

(11)

which is equivalent to

\frac{\,p(x_{T+1})\!+\!\sum\limits_{k=0}^{T}\bigl[q(x_{k})\!+\!r(u_{k})\bigr]}{\sum\limits_{k=0}^{T}s(w_{k})}\leq\!\gamma^{2},\text{ }\forall\{w_{k}\}\!:\!\;\!\!s(w_{k})<\infty.

Final Statement:

For a given performance level
\gamma>0
, find a causal time-invariant control policy
\{u_{k}\}
such that, for every finite horizon
T\geq 1
, and for all admissible disturbance sequences
\{w_{k}\}
(i.e., with finite energy),

J_{T}:=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{T}\left[\gamma^{2}\,s(w_{k})-q(x_{k})-r(u_{k})\right]-p(x_{T+1})\geq 0.

(12)

This formulation extends the conventional quadratic cost setup to more general convex penalties, thereby enabling the synthesis of controllers that can achieve richer performance characteristics.

Assumptions

To facilitate the problem, we impose the following assumptions.

The convex, even, and nonnegative functions

,

,

\displaystyle p(0)=q(0)

The system matrix

Assumption 3
.

The input matrix

Note that Assumption

.

Lemma 2

Consider the following deterministic system:

\displaystyle x_{k+1}=Ax_{k}+Bu_{k}

\{u_{k}\}

IV-C

Discussion on the Controller Synthesis

Theorem
2
presents an explicit formulation for an optimal nonlinear state-feedback controller applicable to linear systems when minimizing non‐quadratic cost functions. This result is particularly valuable for practical applications, as it considerably broadens the range of feasible controller designs beyond the traditional quadratic paradigm.

In practice, the state, control, and disturbance cost functions must adhere to the conditions specified in Theorem
2
. Ideally, a designer would select the cost functions
q
,
r
, and
s
and then seek a strictly convex function
p
that satisfies the Riccati-like equation (
15
). However, identifying such a function
p
for given
q
,
r
, and
s
can be challenging. To overcome this difficulty, we propose a methodology that allows the designer to directly choose only two out of the three cost functions.

The following section outlines our proposed approach. In each case, the designer begins by fixing the structure of either
m(\cdot)
or
g(\cdot)
. For simplicity, in the first two approaches, we assume that
m(\cdot)
is fixed to a quadratic form

m(x)=x^{\top}Mx,

with
M\succ 0
, ensuring its strict convexity. In the third approach, we instead fix the structure of
g(\cdot)
to a quadratic form,

g(x)=x^{\top}Gx,
with
G\succ 0
. The designer may then proceed using one of the following methods.

1.

State Cost and Disturbance Specification:
The designer selects the state cost
q(\cdot)
and the disturbance cost
s(\cdot)
. For a given
M\succ 0
, the control cost
r(\cdot)
is then determined using Equations (
19
) and (
20
). In this setting,
M
must be chosen carefully to ensure that the resulting
r(\cdot)
is both convex and positive. Thus, the design problem reduces to finding an
M\succ 0
that satisfies these criteria, a task that can be addressed using (possibly convex) optimization techniques.

2.

Control Cost and Disturbance Cost Specification:
Here, the designer chooses the control cost
r(\cdot)
and disturbance cost
s(\cdot)
. With
M
specified, the state cost
q(\cdot)
is computed via the same equations. The challenge lies in finding an
M\succ 0
that ensures the resulting
q(\cdot)
is convex and positive, again potentially addressed through (convex) optimization.

3.

Control Cost and State Cost Specification:
In this approach, the designer fixes the control cost
r(\cdot)
and state cost
q(\cdot)
. With a selected
G\succ 0
, the disturbance cost
s(\cdot)
is then derived. The design problem is to choose
G
such that the computed
s(\cdot)
remains convex and positive, which can similarly be achieved using optimization techniques.

In all the approaches above, a critical requirement is that the designer must also ensure that
\gamma^{2}s(\cdot)-g(Ax+(\cdot))
is convex in
(\cdot)
, as stated in Theorem
2
.

Remark 3
.

In each of the three cases presented, the designer may choose to fix the structure of either
m(\cdot)
or
g(\cdot)
. In our examples, we fix
m(\cdot)
(with
m(x)=x^{\top}Mx
,
M\succ 0
) for the first two cases, and
g(\cdot)
(with
g(x)=x^{\top}Gx
,
G\succ 0
) for the third. Similar results and conditions can be derived for either choice.

This flexible framework facilitates the design of advanced control strategies, including a principled way of safety control, bang-bang control, and sparse control. Consequently, Theorem
2
opens up a new landscape of possibilities in control engineering, enabling the development of more tailored and efficient controllers for a wide range of applications.

IX
Conclusion

This work extends the

-A

Proof Idea of Theorems
3
,
4
and
5

The proofs rely on the following ideas:

1.

Convexity via Hessian Positivity:

A function
f
is convex if

\nabla^{2}f(x)\succ 0\quad\forall x.

2.

By imposing Assumption
1
as a constraint, convexity implies positivity of the functions.

3.

Fenchel Duality and the Inverse Function Theorem:

For a convex function
f
and its Fenchel conjugate
f^{\ast}
, one obtains (via the inverse function theorem)

\nabla^{2}f\bigl(\nabla f^{\ast}(\xi)\bigr)\,\nabla^{2}f^{\ast}(\xi)=I.

This crucial relation shows that locally the Hessian of
f
is the inverse of the Hessian of
f^{\ast}
.

Combining these ideas along with some matrix inequalities provides sufficient conditions for the overall functions to be convex.

-B

Proof of Theorem
3

In order for equation (
15
) to hold, we need to ensure
\gamma^{2}s(\cdot)-g(Ax+(\cdot))
is convex for a fixed
x
, and
p(\cdot)
and
q(.)
are convex (which will imply
p(\cdot)
and
q(.)
are positive as will be seen in the proof).

To ensure the convexity of
p(.)
, we utilize the following result from convex analysis:

Lemma 4
.

If a function
f
is convex, positive, and
f(0)=0
, then its Fenchel dual
f^{\ast}
is also convex, positive and
f^{\ast}(0)=0
, and vice versa.

Then, using Lemma
4
, we will observe that
p^{\ast\ast}
is convex, even, positive and by choosing
p:=p^{\ast\ast}
, the results follow.
Thus, for the quadratic function
m(x)=x^{\top}Mx
, convexity of
p
is equivalent to

\nabla^{2}p^{\ast}\succeq 0,

(73)

and from
15
we have:

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}p^{\ast}(\xi)=A\nabla^{2}m^{\ast}(A^{\top}\xi)A^{\top}+\gamma^{2}\nabla^{2}s^{\ast}(\gamma^{-2}\xi)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad-B\nabla^{2}r^{\ast}(B^{\top}\xi)B^{\top}

(74)

thus we need:

(75)

\displaystyle B\nabla^{2}r^{\ast}(B^{\top}\xi)B^{\top}-\gamma^{2}\nabla^{2}s^{\ast}(\gamma^{-2}\xi)\preceq A\nabla^{2}m^{\ast}(A^{\top}\xi)A^{\top}.

(76)

Now, after ensuring convexity of
p^{\ast}
, note that its gradient at the origin is given by

\nabla p^{\ast}(0)=A\,\nabla m^{\ast}(A^{\top}0)\,A^{\top}+\nabla s^{\ast}(\gamma^{-2}0)-B\,\nabla r^{\ast}(B^{\top}0).

By Assumption
1
,
\nabla s^{\ast}(0)=\nabla r^{\ast}(0)=0
, and since
\nabla m^{\ast}(x)=\tfrac{1}{2}M^{-1}x
we have
\nabla m^{\ast}(0)=0
. Hence
\nabla p^{\ast}(0)=0
, which for a convex function implies that the origin is its global minimizer and therefore
p^{\ast}(x)\geq 0
for all
x
. Similarly, because
\nabla q(0)=\nabla p(0)-\nabla m(0)=0
, convexity of
q
also guarantees
q(x)\geq 0
for all
x
. We thus turn to establishing the convexity of
q
.

To ensure the convexity of
q
, we require

\nabla^{2}p(x)-2M\succeq 0,\quad\forall x

(77)

Using the inverse function theorem (see Section
-A
), we then have

\nabla^{2}p^{\ast}(\nabla p(x))\preceq\tfrac{1}{2}M^{-1},\quad\forall x,

(78)

Taking
\tilde{x}:=\nabla p(x)
, we observe that since (
78
) holds for every
x

and
\nabla p
is bijective,

\nabla^{2}p^{\ast}(\tilde{x})\preceq\tfrac{1}{2}M^{-1},\quad\forall\,\tilde{x}.

(79)

which gives

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}p^{\ast}\!(\cdot)\!=\!\tfrac{1}{2}AM^{-1}\!\!A^{\top}\!\!+\!\gamma^{2}\nabla^{2}s^{\ast}\!(\gamma^{-2}(\cdot))\!-\!B\nabla^{2}r^{\ast}\!(B^{\top}\!(\cdot))B^{\top}\!

\displaystyle\preceq\tfrac{1}{2}M^{-1}

(80)

or equivalently,

\displaystyle B\nabla^{2}r^{\ast}(B^{\top}(\cdot))B^{\top}\;-\;\gamma^{2}\nabla^{2}s^{\ast}(\gamma^{-2}(\cdot))

\displaystyle\succeq\tfrac{1}{2}AM^{-1}A^{\top}-\tfrac{1}{2}M^{-1}.

(81)

Therefore, combining (
-B
) and (
79
) gives the two-sided bound which ensures convexity of both
p
and
q
:

\displaystyle L\preceq B\nabla^{2}r^{\ast}(B^{\top}\xi)B^{\top}-\gamma^{-2}\nabla^{2}s^{\ast}(\gamma^{-2}\xi)\preceq U,\quad\forall\xi,

(82)

where

L:=\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(AM^{-1}A^{\top}-M^{-1}\bigr),\qquad U:=\tfrac{1}{2}AM^{-1}A^{\top}.

Finally, note that if

\inf_{\xi}\bigl[B\nabla^{2}r^{\ast}(B^{\top}\xi)B^{\top}-\gamma^{-2}\nabla^{2}s^{\ast}(\gamma^{-2}\xi)\bigr]=0,

then (
82
) forces

\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(AM^{-1}A^{\top}-M^{-1}\bigr)\preceq 0,

implying stability of
A
since
M\succ 0
. This justifies the stability assumption in the first statement of the theorem.

Ensuring the convexity of
\gamma^{2}s(\cdot)-g(Ax+(\cdot))
via (
54
) in Theorem
3
follows a similar line of arguments (convex analysis and Inverse function theorem) and will be ommitted.

Strong Convexity and Smoothness

Under the extra assumption that
r^{\ast}
and
s^{\ast}
are both strongly convex and smooth, the second part of the theorem follows easily. Recall the elementary fact: if two matrix‐valued functions satisfy

X_{\min}\;\preceq\;X(t)\;\preceq\;X_{\max}
\text{and }Y_{\min}\;\preceq\;Y(t)\;\preceq\;Y_{\max}\quad\forall\,t,

then a sufficient condition for
X(t)\preceq Y(t)
for all

is

\sup_{t}X(t)\;\preceq\;\inf_{t}Y(t),\quad\text{i.e.}\quad X_{\max}\;\preceq\;Y_{\min}.

We apply this observation to obtain every inequality in (
56
) by bounding each matrix function above and below by its extremal values. The only exception is

B\,U_{r}^{-1}B^{\top}\;-\;\gamma^{2}\,L_{s}^{-1}\;\succ\;0

which must be imposed directly when
A
may be unstable.

This concludes the proof of Theorem
3
.
