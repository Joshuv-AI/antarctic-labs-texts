Atractylenolide III mitigates polycystic ovary syndrome by activating FDX1-mediated proliferation of ovarian granulocyte cells via PI3K/AKT/mTOR
Authors: Nan Zhou, Wei Tai, Wenqiang Lv, Qing He, Shiwen He, Kexin Chen, Qing Wang, Jingbo Zhang, Suyan Li, Yijuan Cao, Xueyan Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPolycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is the most common endocrine and metabolic disorder among women of reproductive age worldwide. This study aimed to investigate the therapeutic potential and molecular mechanisms of Atractylenolide III (ATL-III), a bioactive compound derived from Atractylodes macrocephala Koidz., in PCOS.MethodsFollicular fluid and granulosa cells (GCs) were collected from women with PCOS. A PCOS mice model was established using dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA). The body weight, estrous cycle, glucose tolerance, insulin tolerance, and ovarian morphology were measured to assess ovarian function. The knockdown and overexpression of ferredoxin 1 (FDX1) were conducted in human granulosa-like tumor cells (KGN). Viability and proliferation of GCs were evaluated by CCK-8 and EdU staining. RT-qPCR, Western blotting, and immunohistochemical staining were performed to detect molecule expressions in the PCOS patients, PCOS mice, and KGN cells.ResultsFDX1 expression was significantly decreased in both follicular fluid and GCs of PCOS patients. In PCOS mice, ATL-III was first found that improved the estrous cycle, disordered folliculogenesis, glucose tolerance, insulin resistance, and ovarian morphology, and upregulated ovarian FDX1, and proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA) expression. DHEA-induced KGN cells demonstrated FDX1 suppression associated with proliferation inhibition, whereas FDX1 overexpression rescued proliferative capacity. ATL-III restored KGN cell viability and proliferation through FDX1 upregulation. Mechanistically, ATL-III activated PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling by regulating FDX1 in both PCOS mice and KGN cells.ConclusionOur findings establish ATL-III as a novel regulator of FDX1 that alleviates PCOS via PI3K/AKT/mTOR-mediated GCs proliferation enhancement, positioning it as a promising therapeutic agent for PCOS treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13048-025-01941-7.Keywords: Polycystic ovary syndrome, Ferredoxin 1, Atractonolide III, Granulosa cells, Proliferation

Background

Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is the most prevalent reproductive and metabolic disorder, affecting approximately 6–10% of women of childbearing age globally [1]. Common symptoms of PCOS include hyperandrogenism, polycystic ovaries, ovulatory dysfunction, obesity, and insulin resistance [2]. The PCOS diagnostic criteria established in Rotterdam in 2003 are currently the most widely used internationally [3]. To date, the pathogenesis of PCOS remains unclear, involving various mechanisms such as genetics, hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian (HPO) axis dysfunction, and hyperinsulinemia. Therefore, finding medicines with precise efficacy and high safety and formulating reasonable and effective treatment plans are urgent issues in the clinical treatment of PCOS.

PCOS patients exhibit irregular ovulatory function, indicating that PCOS is a major cause of anovulatory infertility [4]. In PCOS, growing preantral follicles resemble those in normal ovaries; however, when the follicle diameter reaches 5–8 mm, development often stalls, preventing the maturation and ovulation of oocytes [5]. Granulosa cells (GCs) proliferation plays a role in participating in abnormal follicle development in patients with PCOS [6–8]. Hyperandrogenism in human and mouse GCs caused H3K27Ac aberrations, which are associated with CK2α nuclear translocation and HDAC2 phosphorylation, resulting in inhibited cell proliferation and finally causing abnormal follicle development in PCOS [9]. Besides, GLP-1/GLP-1R axis acts on PCOS ovarian granulosa cells to promote their viability in vivo and in vitro, thus enhancing the proliferation and anti-apoptosis of PCOS ovarian MGCs, thereby contributing to oocyte maturation in PCOS [10]. These findings collectively indicate the involvement of GCs in the occurrence and development of PCOS, positioning them as molecular targets for PCOS treatment and providing a novel theoretical foundation for future therapeutic interventions.

The FDX1 gene encodes ferredoxin 1 (FDX1), a small iron-sulfur protein, which plays a crucial role in the reproductive process [11]. The expression pattern of FDX1 in PCOS remains controversial, with studies reporting both increased and decreased levels in patient samples. Recent evidence suggests that FDX1 expression is significantly downregulated in the GCs of women with PCOS and PCOS rats [12]. However, there is also research indicating that during the maturation process of normal mouse oocytes, the expression of the FDX1 gene is upregulated [11]. Moreover, FDX1 has been demonstrated to be highly expressed in GCs of patients with PCOS, and it also affects their cellular functions [13]. Besides, the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway is one of the essential signaling pathways that regulate the normal functions of GCs, mediating cell survival and differentiation, proliferation, and apoptosis [14, 15]. It has been reported that Catalpol protects ovarian GCs from apoptosis in rats by regulating PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway [16], similar to Bu-shen-zhu-yun decoction [17].

Atractylodes macrocephala Koidez has long been used both in Traditional Chinese Medicine and as a dietary supplement [18, 19]. It has been included in the list of health foods published by the National Health Commission of the People’s Republic of China [20]. Atractylenolide-III (ATL-III) is a sesquiterpene lactone and the main bioactive component found in Atractylodes macrocephala Koidez. Research has revealed that ATL-III exhibits diverse pharmacological effects, including anticancer, anti-inflammatory, and antiallergic responses, anti-apoptosis, as well as neuroprotection [21–25]. Above those, ATL-III has been closely associated with cellular proliferation and apoptosis [21]. However, the effect of ATL-III on PCOS is still unclear. Thus, we hypothesized that AT-III might have a therapeutic effect against PCOS by regulating cellular proliferation.

The purpose of this study was to investigate the mechanism of ATL-III in the treatment of PCOS. We collected follicular fluid and GCs from PCOS patients and then confirmed proliferation disorder in the ovarian GCs of PCOS patients and the potential roles of FDX1 in this process. A dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA) -induced PCOS mice model was established to examine protective effect of ATL-III on PCOS. Meanwhile, KGN cells were applied to explore the mechanism of ATL-III in regulating the proliferation of GCs in PCOS through PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway mediated by FDX1.

Methods

Chemicals and reagents

ATL-III was purchased from Chengdu Herbpurify Co., Ltd (Chengdu, China). Dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA) and metformin were obtained from MedChemExpress (Shanghai, China). Antibodies against FDX1(BS71332), proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA, BS42666), and GAPDH (AP0063) were acquired from Bioworld Technology (Nanjing, China). Tubulin (AP0064) antibody was purchased from ABclonal (Wuhan, China). PI3K(AF6242), p-PI3K (AF3242), AKT(AF6261), p-AKT (AF0061), mTOR (AF6308), and p-mTOR (AF3308) antibodies were obtained from Affinity Biosciences (Jiangsu, China).

Clinical samples

The PCOS patients and healthy women included in this study were all recruited from the Reproductive Medicine Center of Xuzhou Central Hospital. This study, following the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki, was approved by the Ethics Committee of Xuzhou Central Hospital (Approval No. XZXY-LK-20210901-024) and registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (Registration number: ChiCTR2300067599, Registration Date༚2023-01-13). The inclusion criteria for PCOS patients were as follows: (1) diagnosis of PCOS based on the Rotterdam criteria and undergoing in vitro fertilization-embryo transfer (IVF-ET), while other conditions causing hyperandrogenism and ovulation disorders were excluded; (2) aged between 18 and 35 years old; (3) no other treatments within the past three months apart from IVF. The inclusion criteria for the control group were: (1) aged between 18 and 35 years old; (2) undergoing IVF-ET due to tubal factor infertility or male factor infertility; (3) regular menstrual cycles (21–35 days), normal basal reproductive hormone levels/ovarian function, and no clinical manifestations of hyperandrogenism such as hirsutism. The exclusion criteria for all participants were: (1) age less than 18 or more than 35 years old; (2) presence of other causes of menstrual disorders and/or excessive androgen levels, such as Cushing’s syndrome, hyperprolactinemia, congenital adrenal hyperplasia, or other adrenal diseases, thyroid dysfunction, and androgen-secreting tumors; (3) use of hormonal contraception, lipid-lowering drugs, or other medications affecting insulin sensitivity or serum androgen levels within the past three months.

After oocyte retrieval, the follicular fluid containing GCs was pooled and centrifuged at 800 rpm for 10 min to precipitate granular cells, and the supernatant (follicular fluid) after centrifugation was collected [26]. The portion containing GCs was carefully aspirated, then suspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and centrifuged at 250 g for 5 min. Finally, the supernatant was removed, and GCs were collected and stored at -80 °C.

Animal experimental design

The animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Xuzhou Medical University. Three-week-old female BALB/C mice were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). The mice, weighing 10–12 g, were housed in the SPF(Specific Pathogen Free) facility of the Experimental Animal Center at Xuzhou Medical University. All mice were randomly divided into six groups (n = 6): control group, PCOS group, 10 mg/kg ATL-III group, 20 mg/kg ATL-III group, 40 mg/kg ATL-III group, and 200 mg/kg metformin group. The PCOS mouse model was induced using DHEA dissolved in sesame oil, with a daily subcutaneous injection of 60 mg/kg DHEA 60 mg/kg for 28 consecutive days. Concurrently, ATL-III (10, 20, 40 mg/kg) or metformin (200 mg/kg) was orally administered by gavage following dilution in 0.5% CMC-Na solution for 28 consecutive days.

Oestrous cycle determination

After the 21 days with DHEA injections, we collected vaginal smears from mice each day. Staining was performed using 0.1% methylene blue solution, and cell morphology was observed under a bright-field microscope to determine the stage of the estrous cycle. During the estrous cycle, the estrus phase is characterized by a predominance of squamous epithelial cells, the proestrus phase is mainly composed of round nucleated epithelial cells, and the metestrus phase is dominated by many leukocytes along with epithelial cells.

Intraperitoneal glucose tolerance test (IPGTT) and insulin tolerance test (ITT)

After 28 days of continuous gavage treatment, the mice were fasted for 12 h before conducting the glucose tolerance test. The mice were intraperitoneally injected with glucose at 2 g/kg, and blood samples were collected from the tails of the mice. The first drop of blood was discarded after gently squeezing the tail from the middle towards the tip. Blood glucose levels were measured at 0, 15, 30, 60, 90, and 120 min by a glucose meter. A 4-hour fasting period was followed by an insulin tolerance test, during which insulin was injected intraperitoneally at a dose of 1.5 IU/kg. Blood glucose concentrations were measured at 0, 15, 30, 45, and 60 min by glucose meter. Subsequently, all mice were euthanized, and ovarian tissues and blood samples were collected for further experiments.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining of ovary

Fixed unilateral ovarian tissues in 4% paraformaldehyde were embedded in paraffin, and 4-micrometer-thick ovarian tissue sections were prepared. After deparaffinization and rehydration, the ovarian sections were mounted on glass slides, stained with H&E, and subjected to routine histological examination under an optical microscope (Olympus, Japan).

Ovarian immunohistochemical staining (IHC)

Collecting paraffin-embedded ovarian sections from the same side, the sections underwent deparaffinization, antigen retrieval, washing, and blocking. Subsequently, they were incubated overnight with specific antibodies against FDX1 (1:500) or PCNA(1:1000). The sections were stained with 3,3’-Diaminobenzidine and hematoxylin, followed by gradient dehydration in ethanol and sealing with neutral resin. IHC images were observed and captured under a microscope. The Image J software was employed to measure the optical density and perform quantitative analysis (density = IOD/area).

Cell culture and treatment

The KGN cells were cultured in a DMEM medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. The cells were maintained in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2 at 37 °C. KGN cells were treated with different concentrations of DHEA (0, 1 µM, 10 µM, 100 µM) to establish an in vitro model of PCOS. To investigate the effect of ATL-III on KGN cells, cells were pre-treated with 100 µM DHEA for 24 h, followed by co-culture with different concentrations of ATL-III (0, 12.5, 25, 50 µg/ml) for another 24 h. The positive control group was treated with 1mM metformin.

Cell transfection

According to the lentiviral transduction protocol, KGN cells were transduced with lentivirus (virus titer 3.5 × 109 TU/mL) in serum-free DMEM medium for 16 h, followed by incubation in DMEM medium containing serum for an additional 72 h. Subsequently, virus-infected cells were exposed to puromycin (1 µg/ml) for 48 h to select colonies positive for viral infection. All lentiviruses were synthesized by Shanghai Gene Chem Co., Ltd.

RNA extraction and RT-PCR

Total Tissue RNA was extracted using RNA-easy isolation Reagent (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd, China), following the manufacturer’s instructions, and then converted to cDNA using the HiScript l All-in-one RT SuperMix Perfect for qPCR Kit (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd, China). Quantitative reverse transcriptase PCR analysis was carried out employing the Light Cycle 96 software version 1.1 (Roche, USA). The relative expression levels were determined utilizing the 2−ΔΔCq method. The specific primers used in the study were as follows: FDX1 Forward: 5’-AACCCTGGCTTGTTCAACCTGTC-3’, Reverse: 5’-CCAACCGTGATCTGTCTGTTAGTCC-3’; GAPDH Forward: 5’-CAGGAGGCATTGCTGATGAT-3’, Reverse: 5’-GAAGGCTGGGGCTCATTT-3’.

Western blotting

Protein samples were extracted from KGN cells and mouse ovarian tissues using lysis buffer, and protein concentrations were determined by the BCA Protein Assay Kit. Proteins were separated by 15% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred to nitrocellulose (NC) membranes. After blocking with 3% bovine serum albumin at room temperature for 1 h, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: FDX1 (1:500), PCNA (1:1000), GAPDH (1:1000), p-PI3K/PI3K (1:1000), p-AKT/AKT (1:1000), p-mTOR/mTOR (1:1000), Tubulin (1:10000). Subsequently, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:10000) for 1 h. Protein signals were quantified by densitometry and normalized to GAPDH or tubulin expression as controls.

Cell counting Kit-8 (CCK-8)

According to the manufacturer’s instructions, cell viability and proliferation were analyzed by CCK-8 colorimetric assay (Dojindo, Japan). KGN cells were seeded in a 96-well plate at a density of 3 × 103 cells/well. After different treatment conditions, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C in a light-protected incubator for 2 h. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Spectra Max M5, Molecular Devices, USA).

5‑Ethynyl‑2’‑deoxyuridine (EdU) staining

Following the operational guidelines provided by Guangzhou Ruibo Biotechnology Co., Ltd. for the EdU cell proliferation detection (imaging detection), KGN cells were seeded at a density of 4 × 103 cells per well in a 96-well plate. After treating the cells with 50 µM EdU, they were incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. Subsequently, Apollo staining and DAPI staining were performed, and the proportion of EdU-positive cells was assessed under a fluorescence microscope. The calculated proliferation ratio was determined as follows: Proliferation ratio = Number of EdU-positive cells / Number of Hoechst-stained cells.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS (version 22.0) and GraphPad Prism (version 9.0) software. Normality and homogeneity of variance were assessed for all quantitative data. The data were presented as Mean ± SEM or median (interquartile range). Independent sample t-test for parametric data and Mann–Whitney U test for non-parametric data were conducted. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was used for multiple comparisons. p < 0.05 was deemed as indicative of statistical significance.

Moreover, a remarkable reduction in FDX1 levels was observed in the follicular fluid of PCOS patients in contrast to the control group. (Fig. 1D).

Abnormal proliferation of GCs is closely associated with poor follicular development and ovulatory dysfunction, which represents one of the primary causes of infertility in PCOS. To investigate this association, Western blot experiments were conducted to assess the expression of the proliferation marker PCNA in GCs from both healthy women and PCOS patients. The results showed a significantly lower proliferative capacity of GCs in PCOS patients compared to the control group (Fig. 1E-F), indicating the occurrence of abnormal GCs proliferation in PCOS.

ATL-III ameliorated the occurrence and progression of PCOS in DHEA-induced mice

PCOS mice model was induced by DHEA and treated with ATL-III to investigate the effects of ATL-III on PCOS phenotypes. Metformin treatment was used as the positive control group. As shown in Fig. 2A and B, the body weights of mice in the PCOS group were higher than those in the control group, while ATL-III or metformin treatment reduced weight gain. IPGTT and ITT results revealed that PCOS mice had abnormal glucose tolerance/ insulin tolerance, and their blood glucose levels were significantly higher than those of normal mice. Meanwhile, treating PCOS mice with 20 and 40 mg/kg ATL-III significantly reduced the blood glucose levels and remarkably improved the impaired glucose and insulin tolerance (Fig. 2C-F).

The normal estrous cycle of mice in the PCOS group was disrupted, primarily during the estrus phase, whereas ATL-III treatment partially restored the disordered estrous cycle of these mice (Fig. 2G-H). H&E staining results showed that PCOS mice displayed a typical polycystic ovarian morphology, characterized by fewer corpora lutea, increased cystic follicles, and a reduced granulosa cell layer. Treatment with ATL-III partially reversed these changes, particularly with significant therapeutic effect in the high-dose group (Fig. 2I), indicating that ATL-III improved the ovarian morphology and follicular development of PCOS mice.

ATL-III increased FDX1 expression and promoted proliferation of ovarian granulosa cells in PCOS mice

We measured the expression of FDX1 in PCOS mice ovaries. As shown in Fig. 3A, the abundance of FDX1 protein in the ovaries of PCOS mice was significantly reduced, and the protein level of PCNA was also significantly decreased. Moreover, IHC staining showed that the protein levels of FDX1 and PCNA were significantly lower in the ovarian GCs of PCOS mice (Fig. 3C and F), consistent with the findings in clinical GCs samples. Meanwhile, 20 and 40 mg/kg ATL-III significantly decreased the FDX1 expression both in the ovarian tissues and the ovarian GCs (Fig. 3C and E). Meanwhile, 20 and 40 mg/kg ATL-III significantly increased the FDX1 expression both in the ovarian tissues and the ovarian GCs (Fig. 3C and E). These results indicated that ATL-III increases the protein level of FDX1 and promotes the proliferation of ovarian GCs.

As shown in Fig. 4A-B, after treatment with different concentrations of DHEA (0, 1, 10, 100 µM) in KGN cells for 24 h, the expression of FDX1 decreased in a concentration-dependent manner. After the regulatory effect of ATL-III on FDX1 in DHEA-induced KGN cells was discovered, we further examined the impact of ATL-III on the proliferation of ovarian granulosa cells. The results showed that the DHEA-induced decrease in PCNA protein expression was significantly alleviated after treatment with ATL-III (Fig. 4C-E). CCK8 assays demonstrated a significant decrease in the cell growth viability of the KGN cells treated with DHEA. However, after ATL-III treatment, cell viability showed a dose-dependent increase with increasing duration of administration (Fig. 4F-G). Similarly, EdU assays revealed that the proportion of proliferating cells in the DHEA group was significantly lower than control group. On the contrary, compared with DHEA group, there was a significant increase in the proportion of proliferating cells in 25 and 50 µg/ml ATL-III groups, which was in line with the CCK8 results (Fig. 4H-I). The results indicate that ATL-III exerts a certain promoting effect on FDX1 expression and cell proliferation in KGN cells, especially 50 µg/ml ATL-III group.

The efficiency of gene knockdown and overexpression was assessed using Western blotting. The results demonstrated that FDX1 was effectively knocked down by the Sh-111,961 sequence, which exhibited the highest efficiency in subsequent investigations within three candidate sequences (Fig. 5A). Similarly, the expression of FDX1 significantly increased in KGN cells following lentiviral transduction with FDX1 overexpression constructs (Fig. 5B).

The expression of the proliferation marker PCNA after FDX1 gene knockdown and overexpression was detected by Western blotting. PCNA expression was significantly reduced in KGN cells after FDX1 knockdown, while PCNA expression was upregulated after FDX1 overexpression (Fig. 5C). Moreover, we assessed KGN cell proliferation capability by EdU and CCK8 assays. The results showed that that Sh-FDX1 decreased the proportion of proliferating cells, thus inhibiting cell proliferation, while the OE-FDX1 group exhibited the opposite effect (Fig. 5D-F). As shown in Fig. 5G, the FDX1 protein level and PCNA expression level in KGN cells significantly increased after ATL-III treatment in DHEA-induced KGN cells, whereas Sh-FDX1 inhibited the effect of ATL-III in the activation of FDX1 and PCNA. Taken together, these findings suggest that FDX1 promotes GCs proliferation, highlighting its potential as a molecular target for the treatment of PCOS. In addition, ATL-III may promote KGN cell proliferation by upregulating FDX1 expression.

ATL-III facilitated PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathways in PCOS mice and KGN cells

As is well known, the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway has been considered as an important pathway mediating cell proliferation. Thus, we assessed the protein expression of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway to investigate whether it is involved in the protective effects of ATL-III in PCOS. As shown in Fig. 6A, the phosphorylation levels of PI3K, AKT, and mTOR in the ovaries of PCOS mice were significantly lower than those of control group, which were adversely up-regulated by ALT-III or Metformin. As expected, 40 mg/kg ATL-III significantly increased the phosphorylation level of PI3K, AKT, and mTOR in the PCOS mice (Fig. 6B-D).

Discussion

The cumulus-oocyte complex consists of a secondary oocyte surrounded by a unique group of granulosa cells, also known as cumulus granulosa cells. Granulosa cells establish connections through gap junctions and paracrine secretion with the oocyte, providing it with nutrients such as pyruvate, amino acids, and cholesterol, along with growth regulators to support its development [27, 28]. Moreover, during folliculogenesis and meiotic resumption, the oocyte heavily relies on GCs for energy production until it dissociates from the cumulus complex [29]. Inhibition of GCs proliferation results in insufficient nutrition and communication for oocyte development [30]. Therefore, GCs play a crucial role in follicular development and ovulation. The proliferation dysfunction of GCs is closely associated with poor follicular development and ovulatory dysfunction and is one of the main causes of infertility in PCOS.

Previous studies have reported that FDX1 is involved in steroid metabolism and mitochondrial cytochromes and plays a critical role in female folliculogenesis. The transformation of primordial follicles into primary follicles (PPT) marks the initial stage of follicular growth within the ovary. Research indicates that the transcription factor SP1 facilitates the transition from primordial to primary follicles, with SP1 in GCs being involved in cholesterol metabolism by regulating the transcription of FDX1. Overexpression of FDX1 in vitro can rescue the slowed PPT phenotype resulting from SP1 gene knockout. Furthermore, under physiological conditions, the expression of FDX1 steadily increases with ovarian growth, suggesting its involvement in supporting ovarian development [31]. PCOS is a highly heterogeneous disorder. Differences in patient phenotypes, metabolic status, or the specific PCOS model used could lead to opposite expression patterns. FDX1 expression and function might be regulated by complex feedback loops or post-translational modifications that lead to different observable outcomes in different experimental systems or patient cohorts. Our results showed that a significant decrease of FDX1 expression in women with PCOS, which was consistent with previous literature reports [12, 32]; this phenomenon was also found in PCOS mice and KGN cells induced by DHEA. Meanwhile, PCNA, a protein associated with cell proliferation, exhibited decreased expression levels in PCOS groups, including human GCs, mouse ovaries, and KGN cells. The crucial role of FDX1 in the proliferation of PCOS by PCNA was confirmed by the overexpression and knockdown of FDX1 in KGN cells. As expected, knockdown of FDX1 inhibited KGN cells proliferation, whereas overexpression of FDX1 facilitated their proliferation. These findings indicate that FDX1 is a crucial factor contributing to the reduced proliferation of GCs, leading to follicular arrest and impaired ovarian development.

PCOS is a lifelong condition in women that not only significantly impacts reproductive and metabolic health but also imposes a substantial economic burden. Therefore, there is an urgent need to study the molecular mechanisms involved in the pathogenesis of PCOS and to develop new effective therapeutic targets [33]. Conventional medicine treatment mainly focuses on symptomatic treatment or achieving stage-specific treatment goals, such as reducing androgens, regulating menstrual cycles, and improving insulin resistance, while they only have moderate therapeutic effect and are not able to thoroughly prevent complications [34]. Traditional Chinese medicine or ingredients extracted from herbs showed curative effects on PCOS by improving follicular development, attenuating the hormonal imbalance, and ameliorating glucolipid metabolism alterations [35, 36]. ATL-III, isolated from a Chinese herbal medicine Atractylodes macrocephala Koidz, have been reported to possess neuroprotective and anti-inflammatory activities. In the present study, ATL-III was first demonstrated to have a therapeutic effect on DHEA-induced PCOS mice, including reduction in body weight, improvement in disordered folliculogenesis, restoration of normal estrous cycle, and alleviation of impaired glucose tolerance and insulin resistance, which may be the important biological events related to ATL-III in the treatment of PCOS.

Based on our confirmation of the role of FDX1 in the pathogenesis of PCOS, we hypothesized that ATL-III affects the proliferation of GCs through FDX1, thus providing a basis for the treatment of PCOS. In the present study, DHEA was used to pre-treat KGN cells to simulate the occurrence of PCOS. As expected, the results revealed that moderate and high doses of ATL-III significantly upregulated the expression levels of FDX1 and PCNA in KGN cells. We further evaluated the effect of ATL-III on KGN cell viability and proliferation capacity, and the results demonstrated a dose-dependent reversal of decreased cell viability and proliferation capacity induced by DHEA treatment. In addition, further mechanism experiments involving FDX1 knockdown by lentivirus in KGN cells confirmed that ATL-III significantly upregulated FDX1 expression, thereby promoting the expression of PCNA in the PCOS model. Consistent with these studies, FDX1 and PCNA expression were decreased in PCOS mice, and intervention of ATL-III restored these changes caused by DHEA. Therefore, the findings confirmed that ATL-III promotes the proliferation of GCs in PCOS via rescuing the expression of FDX1 in KGN cells. However, in the animal experiment, ovarian tissue rather than ovarian granulosa cells was used to investigate the alteration of signaling pathways. Further studies are necessary to explore the therapeutic efficacy of ATL-III in clinical settings. It is worth noting that the functional experiments in this study were conducted using the KGN cells. Although this is a widely accepted model for studying the function of granulosa cells, KGN cells have genetic and functional differences from normal primary granulosa cells. Therefore, our research findings will be validated in untransformed models such as primary granulosa cells or SVOG cells in the future.

The PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway is a key intracellular signaling system that drives cellular growth and survival [37]. In this study, we found a correlation between PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway and the beneficial effects of ATL-III in PCOS mice, suggesting that ATL-III administration could alter the phosphorylation levels of PI3K, AKT, and mTOR. In addition, we found that ATL-III could upregulate these proteins induced by DHEA in KGN cells. After knocking out FDX1 in KGN cells of PCOS, ATL-III could rescue the expression of PI3K/AKT/mTOR. The fact that ATL-III can further enhance signaling even in the context of FDX1 knockdown suggests a complementary mechanism. We propose that ATL-III may also directly activate the PI3K and enhance the phosphorylation activity of PI3K (i.e., increased p-PI3K), thereby initiating the phosphorylation cascade of downstream AKT and mTOR to directly restore pathway expression. Besides, ATL-III may directly modulate upstream regulators of the PI3K/AKT pathway (e.g., growth factor receptors or IRS proteins) or inhibit negative regulators such as PTEN. These mechanisms require further research and exploration. The above results suggested that ATL-III promotes ovarian GCs proliferation through regulating phosphorylation of PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway.

Conclusion

In summary, this study suggests that FDX1 is a molecular target of PCOS and regulates the proliferation of ovarian granulosa cells. We have demonstrated for the first time that ATL-III has potential therapeutic effects on PCOS through alleviating PCOS phenotypes and promoting the proliferation of GCs via upregulating phosphorylation of PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway. Therefore, ATL-III could serve as a new potential therapeutic agent for the treatment of PCOS.
