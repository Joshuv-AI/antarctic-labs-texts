Solar Reflected Dark Matter under the Influence of Dark Magnetic Field
Haoming Nie

Abstract
The scattering of dark matter particles within the Sun’s hot plasma can lead to acceleration of dark matter, producing a high-energy solar-reflected DM flux detectable in ground-based experiments. In the vector portal model, interactions between dark matter and Standard Model particles are mediated by a hidden vector field–referred to as “dark photon”–which kinetically mixes with the conventional photon through a small mixing angle. Furthermore, the solar plasma generates intense magnetic fields. Due to the photon-dark photon mixing, this simultaneously sources a “dark magnetic field”. For sufficiently low dark photon masses, this dark magnetic field is capable of deflecting dark matter particles traversing the Sun. We found that if the dark magnetic force is sufficiently strong, the dark magnetic field becomes a wall, preventing the dark matter particles from reaching the deep core region, suppressing their reflected flux. This scenario correct the sensitivity of solar-reflected dark matter detection, offering critical insights for ground-based experiments aiming to probe dark matter.

Introduction

The standard model (SM) is a well-established theory for describing the universe. However, there are still many reasons for us to extend this structure. One major front of beyond standard model physics is dark matter (DM). From astrophysical observations, we realize that a large potion of matter in the universe cannot be explained by the standard model. To reveal the nature of DM, various direct detection experiments are set up to capture the feeble signal of DM-SM interaction. Currently, the experimental exclusion line of galactic halo DM is reaching the threshold of elastic scattering of neutrino background (the “neutrino fog”)
XENON:2024ijk
;
PandaX:2024muv
. Therefore, there is increasing interest in alternative DM models other than the traditional WIMP paradigm, especially MeV scale light DM
SENSEI:2023zdf
;
DAMIC-M:2023gxo
;
SuperCDMS:2023sql
;
XENON:2022ltv
;
LUX:2020car
;
LZ:2023poo
;
PandaX:2022xqx
;
CDEX:2022kcd
;
CDEX:2022dda
. For light DM with MeV mass, the scattering between nuclei is inefficient due to large mass hierarchy. Therefore, detection through electron recoil signal is a better choice. However, the recoil energy is still small and often falls below the detection thresholds of experiments, making direct detection of light DM a tough job, especially when the mass of DM is even smaller than MeV.

In the last decade, it has been proposed in
An:2017ojc
that DM particles can be accelerated via elastic scattering with keV-energy electrons in the hot, dense plasma of the solar interior. Since the mass of electron and MeV-scale light DM particle are of the same scale, the energy transfer is efficient, producing a solar reflected DM (SRDM) spectrum with a significant keV-energy tail. Considering the SRDM flux, exclusion limits derived from direct detection experiments are established in
An:2017ojc
;
An:2021qdl
;
Emken:2021lgc
;
Emken:2024nox
with cross section around
\sigma_{e}\sim 10^{-38}\text{cm}^{2}
for contact interaction, and milicharge around
Q_{\text{eff}}\sim 10^{-9}
for interactions mediated by a massless dark photon.

On the theoretical side, the direct detection of both halo DM and SRDM requires a coupling (“portal”) between DM and SM particles. Currently we knew three portals to connect the DM and the standard model: neutrino portal, Higgs portal and vector portal. The vector portal involves an additional vector field
V^{\mu}
, often called the dark photon
1982DP
;
Holdom:1985ag
;
HOLDOM198665
;
HOLDOM1991329
;
Dienes:1996zr
, that mixes with
\mathrm{U}(1)_{Y}
hypercharge field by a mixing angle denoted by
\kappa
. After electroweak symmetry breaking this provides a mixing between dark photon and normal photon. Recently, the study of dark photon has became a prosperous front for both theorists and experimentalists. Dark photons with masses larger than 10MeV are subjected to traditional collider search, while light dark photons are more limited by astrophysical and cosmological bounds. A thorough illustration of dark photon constraints can be found in
Caputo:2021eaa
.

In vector portal model, the scattering of dark matter off electron is mediated by the exchange of dark photon-photon mixed state. When the dark photon mass significantly exceeds the characteristic momenta,
m_{V}\gg p_{e},p_{\chi}
, the interaction reduces to an effective contact interaction. Conversely, if
m_{V}\ll p_{e},p_{\chi}
, then the scattering is well approximated by massless mediator scattering. In previous studies, the mass of dark photon appeared solely as a parameter in the propagator within the scattering amplitude. For
m_{V}
much smaller than the temperature of the Sun (
\sim\text{keV}
), the dark photon is treated as massless and any
m_{V}
gives the same result. However, in this paper, we point out that DM-SM interactions represent only one aspect of the dark photon’s phenomenology. When the mass of dark photon corresponds to an astronomical scale,
m_{V}\sim R_{\odot}^{-1}
, where
R_{\odot}
is the radius of the Sun, the large-scale dark photon field significantly influences the motion of SRDM. The Sun sustains a strong standard model magnetic field, generated by the electric current induced by dynamical plasma inside. Due to the dark photon-photon mixing, such an electric current simultaneously sources a “dark magnetic field”. Any DM particle approaching the Sun will be affected by a dark magnetic force with a strength roughly equivalent to
\sim\kappa e_{D}v|\nabla^{2}{\bf B}|/m_{V}^{2}
, where
e_{D}
is the “dark charge” carried by DM and
v
is the velocity of the DM particle. For
m_{V}\lesssim 10^{-14}\text{eV}
and
\kappa e_{D}>10^{-9}
, the dark magnetic force is large enough to significantly redirect DM inside the Sun, giving rise to a new phenomenology of SRDM. In our simulations, the effect of dark magnetic field is double-edged. The dark magnetic force will bend the trajectory of DM particles, causing them to stay longer inside the Sun, potentially inducing more scattering. On the other hand, the deflection prevents DM particles from reaching the core region of the Sun, thus suppressing the scattering probability with energy transfer
\gtrsim 1\,\text{keV}
. Our simulations reveal that the high energy tail of SRDM is suppressed, while the
\sim 10\,\text{eV}
part of the spectrum is enhanced. By taking the dark magnetic force into account, we can draw more appropriate lines of constraints on millicharge
Q_{\text{eff}}
for different
m_{V}
.

This paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we derive the formula of dark electric and magnetic field induced by electric charge and current. In section 3, we build a model of dark magnetic field generated by solar dynamics, and show the behavior of DM particle in dark magnetic field. In section 4, we briefly explain how SRDM flux is generated by simulations. In Section 5, we calculate the signal induced by SRDM in the detection target of experiments and show the exclusion line with the correction of dark magnetic field. Then in the last section we give a summary of this paper.

2
Dark Photon Equations of Motion

After electroweak symmetry breaking, the dark photon model can be described as an additional vector field with a Stuckelberg mass term, mixed with the usual electromagnetic field with mixing angle
\kappa
. The Lagrangian can be written as

\mathcal{L}=-\frac{1}{4}F_{\mu\nu}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\kappa F_{\mu\nu}V^{\mu\nu}-\frac{1}{4}V_{\mu\nu}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}m_{V}^{2}V^{\mu}V_{\mu}-e_{D}J_{D}^{\mu}V_{\mu}-eJ^{\mu}V_{\mu},

(1)

where
V_{\mu}
is the dark photon field,
V^{\mu\nu}=\partial^{\mu}V^{\nu}-\partial^{\nu}V^{\mu}
is the dark photon field strength,
J_{D}^{\mu}=(\rho_{D},{\bf J}_{D})
is the dark 4-current,
\rho_{D}
is the dark charge density,
{\bf J}_{D}
is the dark charge current. The classical equations of motion can be easily derived through Lagrangian equation:

\displaystyle\partial_{\mu}\left(F^{\mu\nu}+\kappa V^{\mu\nu}\right)

\displaystyle=eJ^{\nu}

(2)

\displaystyle\partial_{\mu}\left(\kappa F^{\mu\nu}+V^{\mu\nu}\right)+m_{V}^{2}V^{\nu}

\displaystyle=e_{D}J_{D}^{\nu}.

(3)

Writing in 3D components, the equations of motion are

\displaystyle-\partial_{t}\tilde{{\bf E}}+\nabla\times\tilde{{\bf B}}-\kappa\partial_{t}{\bf E}+\kappa\nabla\times{\bf B}+m_{V}^{2}{\bf V}

\displaystyle=e_{D}{\bf J}_{D}

(4)

\displaystyle-\partial_{t}{\bf E}+\nabla\times{\bf B}-\kappa\partial_{t}\tilde{{\bf E}}+\kappa\nabla\times\tilde{{\bf B}}

\displaystyle=e{\bf J}

(5)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\tilde{{\bf E}}+\kappa\nabla\cdot{\bf E}+m_{V}^{2}V^{0}

\displaystyle=e_{D}\rho_{D}

(6)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot{\bf E}+\kappa\nabla\cdot\tilde{{\bf E}}

\displaystyle=e\rho,

(7)

where
\tilde{{\bf E}}=-\nabla V^{0}-\partial_{t}{\bf V}
and
\tilde{{\bf B}}=\nabla\times{\bf V}
is dark electric and magnetic field. For massive vector field, we must have
\partial_{t}V^{0}+\nabla\cdot{\bf V}=0
. Taking the curl of eq.
4
, assuming static fields and neglecting dark charges, we have

m_{V}^{2}\tilde{{\bf B}}-\nabla^{2}\tilde{{\bf B}}=\kappa\nabla^{2}{\bf B}.

(8)

Therefore, for
m_{V}
not too smaller than
1/R_{B}
, where
R_{B}
is the spatial variation scale of
{\bf B}
, we can approximately write
\tilde{{\bf B}}\sim\kappa/m_{V}^{2}\times\nabla^{2}{\bf B}\sim\kappa B/m_{V}^{2}R_{B}^{2}
.

In expression of scalar and vector potentials, the equations
4
\sim
7
can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{2}V^{0}-\nabla^{2}V^{0}+\frac{m_{V}^{2}}{1-\kappa^{2}}V^{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{e_{D}}{1-\kappa^{2}}\rho_{D}-\frac{\kappa e}{1-\kappa^{2}}\rho

(9)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{2}{\bf V}-\nabla^{2}{\bf V}+\frac{m_{V}^{2}}{1-\kappa^{2}}{\bf V}

\displaystyle=\frac{e_{D}}{1-\kappa^{2}}{\bf J}_{D}-\frac{\kappa e}{1-\kappa^{2}}{\bf J}.

(10)

Here, it is clear that the normal electric charge and current are also sources of dark electric and magnetic fields, but the coupling is suppressed by a millicharge
Q_{\text{eff}}=\kappa e_{D}/e
. In the same virtue, we can also derive the equations for normal potentials:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{2}\phi-\nabla^{2}\phi

\displaystyle=\frac{e}{1-\kappa^{2}}\rho-\frac{\kappa e_{D}}{1-\kappa^{2}}\rho_{D}-\frac{\kappa m_{V}^{2}}{1-\kappa^{2}}V^{0}

(11)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{2}{\bf A}-\nabla^{2}{\bf A}

\displaystyle=\frac{e}{1-\kappa^{2}}{\bf J}-\frac{\kappa e_{D}}{1-\kappa^{2}}{\bf J}_{D}-\frac{\kappa m_{V}^{2}}{1-\kappa^{2}}{\bf V}.

(12)

The dark photon field enters as a source for normal electromagnetic potential. However, because of the smallness of
\kappa
, the influence on usual electromagnetic field is always negligible in real situations where DM is rare
\rho_{D},{\bf J}_{D}\approx 0
: When
m_{V}\gg 1/R_{B}
,
V^{0}\approx-(\kappa e/m_{V}^{2})\rho
,
{\bf V}\approx-(\kappa e/m_{V}^{2}){\bf J}
. Therefore, the third terms on the right side in eq.
11
and eq.
12
are
\kappa^{2}e\rho
and
\kappa^{2}e{\bf J}
, which are much smaller than the first terms on the right side. Conversely, when
m_{V}\ll 1/R_{B}
, the equations of
V^{\mu}
become the same as
A^{\mu}
, except for the fact that dark photon coupled with normal electric charge by
\kappa e
. Hence
V^{0}\approx\kappa\phi
and
{\bf V}\approx\kappa{\bf A}
. The third terms on the right side in eq.
11
and eq.
12
are
\kappa^{2}m_{V}^{2}\phi
and
\kappa^{2}m_{V}^{2}{\bf A}
, which are always much smaller than the terms on the left side.

In reality, DM particles are very few,
\rho_{D}\approx 0
,
{\bf J}_{D}\approx 0
. For qualitative estimation, we only consider static field, then

\displaystyle V^{0}-\frac{1-\kappa^{2}}{m_{V}^{2}}\nabla^{2}V^{0}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\kappa e}{m_{V}^{2}}\rho

(13)

\displaystyle{\bf V}-\frac{1-\kappa^{2}}{m_{V}^{2}}\nabla^{2}{\bf V}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\kappa e}{m_{V}^{2}}{\bf J}.

(14)

These are standard massive boson equations of motion with source. The solution should be the famous Yukawa potential:

\displaystyle V^{0}({\bf x})

\displaystyle=-\frac{\kappa}{4\pi(1-\kappa^{2})}\int d^{3}x^{\prime}\,e\rho({\bf x}^{\prime})\frac{e^{-(m_{V}/\sqrt{1-\kappa^{2}})\left|{\bf x}-{\bf x}^{\prime}\right|}}{\left|{\bf x}-{\bf x}^{\prime}\right|}

(15)

\displaystyle{\bf V}({\bf x})

\displaystyle=-\frac{\kappa}{4\pi(1-\kappa^{2})}\int d^{3}x^{\prime}\,e{\bf J}({\bf x}^{\prime})\frac{e^{-(m_{V}/\sqrt{1-\kappa^{2}})\left|{\bf x}-{\bf x}^{\prime}\right|}}{\left|{\bf x}-{\bf x}^{\prime}\right|}.

(16)

Because of the decaying exponential factors, the integrals are usually hard to perform analytically. In this paper, we calculate them by numerical integration. After
{\bf V}
is calculated, we can derive
\tilde{{\bf B}}=\nabla\times{\bf V}
directly.

In the presence of dark photon field, the DM particle with velocity
{\bf v}
will experience a force just like electrons in normal electromagnetic field,

\mathbf{F}_{\text{dark}}=e_{D}(\tilde{{\bf E}}+{\bf v}\times\tilde{{\bf B}}).

(17)

Therefore, we can make an estimation of the force strength,
F_{\text{dark}}\sim\kappa e_{D}vB/m_{V}^{2}R_{B}^{2}
. The cyclotron radius corresponding to this “dark Lorentz force” is

R=\frac{m_{\chi}m_{V}^{2}R_{B}^{2}v}{\kappa e_{D}B},

(18)

where
m_{\chi}
is the mass of DM
\chi
. If we want the effect of this force to be significant, this radius should not be larger than the radius of the Sun. Consequently, we derive an estimation for the parameters:

\frac{m_{\chi}m_{V}^{2}}{\kappa e_{D}}\lesssim B\frac{R_{\odot}}{R_{B}^{2}v}.

(19)

Current direct detection constraint is
\kappa e_{D}\sim 10^{-10}
. We choose the benchmark parameters
m_{\chi}\sim 0.1\,\text{MeV}
,
v\sim 0.002
,
R_{B}\sim 0.04R_{\odot}
,
B\sim 0.4\,\text{G}
, the estimation of the order magnitude of
m_{V}
is

m_{V}\lesssim 10^{-14}\,\text{eV}.

(20)

In actual simulations (see Section 4) we found that around
m_{V}\sim 10^{-14}\,\text{eV}
, there are significant changes on trajectories if
\kappa e_{D}\sim 10^{-9}
.

Solar Dark Magnetic Field

It is commonly believed that the magnetic field of the Sun is generated by the solar dynamo mechanism in the interior
Parker1955a
;
Parker1955b
. Traditionally, this is suspected to be closely related to certain plasma motion patterns in a thin layer called tachocline (thickness
\sim 0.04R_{\odot}
) with a radius around
R_{\text{tac}}\approx 0.7R_{\odot}

Strugarek2011tacho
;
Charbonneau2014
. Recently, new studies suggest that the solar dynamo may originate just below the surface
vasil2024solar
. The detailed mechanism of magnetic field generation is beyond the scope of this paper. To highlight the main idea of dark magnetic field deflection and give a qualitative estimation, we neglect the small structure eddies and begin with a simple model, assuming there is a thin layer of electric current circling around the Sun in the tachocline region:

e{\bf J}_{\text{SD}}(r,\theta,\phi)=\frac{3\mathfrak{m}}{4\pi R_{\text{tac}}^{3}}\sin\theta\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma_{\text{tac}}}e^{-\frac{(r-R_{\text{tac}})^{2}}{2\sigma_{\text{tac}}^{2}}}\hat{\phi},

(21)

Here we choose the Gaussian thickness of the current layer to be
\sigma_{\text{tac}}=0.02R_{\odot}
. This will generate a dipole magnetic field outside tachocline:

{\bf B}(r,\theta,\phi)=\frac{\mathfrak{m}}{4\pi r^{3}}(2\cos\theta\,\hat{r}+\sin\theta\,\hat{\theta}),

(22)

assuming that the magnetic field fits the observed average value on the surface,
2\mathfrak{m}/4\pi R_{\odot}^{3}\sim 0.4\,\text{G}

howard1974studies
. This seems to be a strong assumption, but since the DM particles come from all directions, the averaging effect will cause the reflected flux to be irrelevant to the angular distribution of the dark magnetic field, and only the average magnitude at different radius is important. Therefore, this simple model is enough for illustrating the idea qualitatively.

The dark photon field
{\bf V}
profile can be numerically calculated via eq.
16
, the resultant dark magnetic field
\tilde{{\bf B}}=\nabla\times{\bf V}=\tilde{B}_{r}\hat{r}+\tilde{B}_{\theta}\hat{\theta}
is shown in fig.
2
. It can be clearly seen that as
m_{V}
becomes smaller, the magnitude of dark magnetic field becomes larger, the distribution in space becomes extensive and approaches a dipole field like the normal magnetic field.

Numerically calculated
\tilde{{\bf B}}
, decomposed into radial and azimuthal angle part
\tilde{B}_{r}
and

. The left graph has
m_{V}=1\times 10^{-15}\,\text{eV}
, while the right graph has
m_{V}=1\times 10^{-14}\,\text{eV}

Before conducting simulations, we anticipate that the influence of the dark magnetic field on SRDM will exhibit a dual nature. Firstly, the dark magnetic force induces curvature in the trajectories of DM particles, causing them to spiral within the Sun. This extended path length increases the probability of scattering with solar electrons. Conversely, if the incident angle between an incoming DM particle’s velocity vector and the local dark magnetic field line exceeds a critical value, the particle will be deflected outward, preventing its penetration into the hot, dense core region. These competing effects complicate the estimation of the resultant flux spectrum. Generally, DM particles with higher initial velocities possess a higher likelihood of reaching the solar core. The trajectories simulated by our program is illustrated in fig.
3
. Consequently, for moderate values of kinetic mixing and
m_{V}\lesssim 10^{-14}\,\text{eV}
, we expect that the low energy part of the SRDM spectrum is suppressed while the high energy tail is enhanced.

3.1
Deflection by Dark Magnetic Field

In order to discuss the deflection of DM by dark magnetic field qualitatively, we imitate the procedure in plasma dynamics: divide the motion of DM into the motion of guiding center and gyromotion of the particle:

\mathbf{r}(t)={\bf R}(t)+\mathbf{r}_{L}(t).

(23)

This approximation requires the dark magnetic field to be weakly inhomogeneous slowly changing in a gyroradius
R_{LD}=m_{\chi}v_{\perp}/e_{D}\tilde{B}
. In our estimation, this is not always satisfied by solar dark magnetic field, but the analysis can still be fruitful for intuition. By expanding everything with
\mathbf{r}_{L}
around
{\bf R}
, we arrive at an equation for guiding center:

\frac{e_{D}}{m_{\chi}}\langle\dot{\mathbf{r}}_{L}\times[\mathbf{r}_{L}\cdot\nabla\tilde{{\bf B}}({\bf R})]\rangle=-\frac{\mathcal{M}_{D}}{m_{\chi}}\nabla\tilde{B},

(24)

where
\mathcal{M}_{D}=\pi R_{LD}^{2}J_{D}
is the dark magnetic moment of gyromotion. This derivation can be found in most textbooks on plasma physics, for example
somov2012plasma
. Equivalently, the guiding center is experiencing a “force” proportional to the gradient of dark magnetic field
\mathcal{F}_{D}=-\mathcal{M}_{D}\nabla\tilde{B}
. This means that if we keep the strength of the real force (proportional to
\kappa e_{D}/m_{V}
) constant, the larger
m_{V}
is, the more slopy the
\tilde{{\bf B}}
should be, and the stronger the equivalent “force” on guiding center should be. Consequently, we expect that DM is more likely to be directly bounced away like hitting a wall rather than wandering around in the solar interior in the case of strong dark magnetic force.

4
Solar Reflected Dark Matter

The plasma in the solar interior is composed of fast-moving electrons and ions, with temperatures ranging from 1 eV on the surface to 1 keV in the core. The solar temperature and density profiles are still debated, but for our purpose, the difference is negligible. Generally, the value of the scattering rate is governed by

\Gamma_{\chi-i}=\frac{1}{2k_{1}^{0}}\int\frac{{\rm d}^{3}k_{2}}{2k_{2}^{0}(2\pi)^{3}}\int\frac{{\rm d}^{3}p_{1}}{2p_{1}^{0}(2\pi)^{3}}f_{i}(p_{1})\int\frac{{\rm d}^{3}p_{2}}{2p_{2}^{0}(2\pi)^{3}}(2\pi)^{4}\delta^{4}(k_{1}+p_{1}-k_{2}-p_{2})\sum_{\text{spin}}|\mathcal{M}|^{2},

(25)

where
i
stands for particle type and
f_{i}(p_{1})
is the thermal distribution

f_{i}(p_{1})=n_{i}({\bf x})\left(\frac{2\pi}{m_{i}T({\bf x})}\right)^{3/2}e^{-p_{1}^{2}/2m_{i}T({\bf x})},

(26)

in which
n_{i}({\bf x})
and
T({\bf x})
are the particle number density and the temperature at a given point
{\bf x}
in the Sun.
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
are the initial and final momenta of the DM particle,
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
are the initial and final momenta of charged particles in the Sun.
\mathcal{M}
stands for the scattering amplitude, which is averaged over initial spins and summed over final spins. In this paper, we consider light DM with
m_{\chi}
around MeV. In numerical simulation, the interior of the Sun is divided into
\sim
2000 radial layers, each layer is accompanied by temperature and number density value, so that the scattering rate can be computed at any position where the DM particle is present.

The explicit form of the DM-electron(ion) scattering rate in massless mediator case is calculated in
An:2021qdl
in detail. Here we only mention the crucial parts in the calculation. The amplitude of the scattering including electron Debye screening effect is

\mathcal{M}=\frac{\kappa e_{D}e\langle J^{0}_{D}J^{0}\rangle}{\varepsilon_{L}(q^{2}+m_{V}^{2})},

(27)

where the longitudinal permittivity is given by

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{L}(A)=

\displaystyle 1+\frac{e^{2}n_{e}}{Tq^{2}}\left[1-2A\int\frac{x{\rm d}x}{(2\pi)^{1/2}}\tanh^{-1}(x/A)e^{-x^{2}/2}\right]

(28)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+\frac{e^{2}n_{e}}{Tq^{2}}F_{1}(A),

(29)

where for electron,
A_{e}=(q^{0}/q)(m_{e}/T)^{1/2}
. If the effect of ions with label
i
is also considered, the expression changes to

\varepsilon_{L}=1+\frac{e^{2}n_{e}}{Tq^{2}}\left[F_{1}(A_{e})+\sum_{i}\frac{Z_{i}^{2}n_{i}}{n_{e}}F_{1}(A_{i})\right],

(30)

where
A_{i}=(q^{0}/q)(m_{i}/T)^{1/2}
. Following the derivation in
An:2021qdl
, the rate of DM-electron scattering can be written as

\Gamma_{\chi-e}=\frac{n_{e}\kappa^{2}e^{2}e_{D}^{2}}{(2\pi T)^{3/2}m_{e}^{1/2}}\int{\rm d}x_{q}{\rm d}c_{q}\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{2}x_{q}+A_{e}\right)^{2}\right]\frac{1}{|x_{q}^{2}+\frac{e^{2}n_{e}}{Tq^{2}}F_{1}(A_{e})|^{2}}\frac{x_{q}^{5}}{(x_{q}^{2}+\frac{m_{V}^{2}}{m_{e}T})^{2}},

(31)

where

A_{e}=v_{1}\left(\frac{m_{e}}{T}\right)^{1/2}c_{q}+\frac{m_{e}}{2m_{\chi}}x_{q},

(32)

and
x_{q}=q/\sqrt{m_{e}T}
,
v_{1}=k_{1}/m_{\chi}
,
c_{q}=\cos\theta_{qk_{1}}
. In the simulation program, Eq.
31
is used as a distribution to generate a random momentum transfer
q
and accompanied direction
\cos\theta_{qk_{1}}
. As mentioned in
An:2021qdl
, when
m_{V}\rightarrow 0
, the total rate of Coulomb-like scattering diverges. Even with the screening effect, the total rate is still too large for conducting simulations. However, the divergent behavior is contributed mainly by forward scatterings, which has little effect on the DM energy or trajectory. For the purpose of SRDM, such possibilities should be cut off. Therefore, we introduce an IR cutoff represented by
\zeta
to
x_{q}
:

x_{q}=\frac{q}{\sqrt{m_{e}T}}=\frac{m_{\chi}\Delta v_{\chi}}{\sqrt{m_{e}T}}>x_{q,\text{cut}}=\frac{m_{\chi}\zeta v_{\chi 0}}{\sqrt{m_{e}T}}=\zeta\frac{m_{\chi}}{m_{e}}v_{\chi 0}\sqrt{\frac{m_{e}}{T}}.

(33)

In this formula
v_{\chi 0}
is a reference velocity, often taken as the average halo DM velocity
v_{\chi 0}=220\,\text{km}/\text{s}
. As shown in
An:2021qdl
, for scattering in the Sun, taking
\zeta=5
is enough to capture the soft scattering behavior.

The numerical results of SRDM are derived through a Monte Carlo simulation program. The simulation follows similar steps as
An:2021qdl
, but is modified in order to account for the dark magnetic force:

1.

Randomly generate initial impact parameter
b\in A_{\rho}
(
A_{\rho}
is the impact disc), velocity at infinity
v_{\infty}
and the angle of velocity according to the halo DM distribution; and then send the DM particle near the Sun (
r=4R_{\odot}
), taking account for energy and angular momentum conservation. Since the solar dark magnetic field is axial-symmetric, the angle between velocity at infinity and solar magnetic axis,
\theta_{\infty}
, and the angle of aiming point on the impact disc,
\nu_{\infty}
, are also important. Therefore,
\theta_{\infty}
and
\nu_{\infty}
are also generated at random evenly. A geometrical illustration of these initial parameters is shown in fig.
4
.

2.

Draw the trajectory of the DM particle step by step. The main difference with the procedure in
An:2021qdl
is that apart from gravity, dark magnetic force (eq.
18
) is also considered. Because the distribution of dark magnetic field is direction dependent and the force is perpendicular to the velocity, the 1D coordinate used in
An:2021qdl
is not appropriate and we are forced to use 3D Cartesian coordinates.

3.

At every step, generate a random number in the range
[0,1]
and compare it to the scattering probability
P=\Gamma_{\chi-e}\Delta l/v
(where
\Delta l
is the step length and
v
is the velocity of the DM particle) to determine whether scattering happens or not. If scattering happens, then use eq.
31
to generate
q
and
\cos\theta_{qk_{1}}
. With the value and direction of the momentum transfer, the momentum of DM particle is changed, sending the DM particle into a new trajectory with new energy value. Return to step 2 until the DM particle is far enough from the Sun (
r>4R_{\odot}
).

4.

Collect the total energy
E_{\chi}
of the DM particle at infinity, draw a histogram and normalize it. Finally, we get the normalized flux
F_{A_{\rho}}(E_{\chi})
.

After the normalized flux spectrum is generated, the SRDM flux is related to the normalized flux through

\frac{{\rm d}\Phi_{\text{SRDM}}}{{\rm d}E_{\chi}}=\Phi_{\text{halo}}\times\frac{F_{A_{\rho}}(E_{\chi})A_{\rho}}{4\pi r_{\oplus}^{2}},

(34)

where
r_{\oplus}\approx 1\,\text{A.U.}
is the distance between the Sun and Earth. An illustration of reflected flux when
m_{V}=1\times 10^{-16}\,\text{eV}
is shown in fig.

, compared to the case
m_{V}=1\times 10^{-13}\,\text{eV}
where the effect of dark magnetic field is negligible. The graph demonstrates that when the dark magnetic field is present, the reflected flux is enhanced around
E_{R}\sim 10\,\text{eV}
, while suppressed for
E_{R}>10\,\text{eV}
. This modification confirms the dual role of the dark magnetic field in SRDM physics. Trajectories of DM particles are redirected by the dark magnetic force inside the Sun, increasing their residence time and consequently the probability of more scatterings. At the same time, the hotter and denser core region is shielded by the dark magnetic field, suppressing keV energy scatterings. Our simulations indicate that the shielding effect will eventually dominate, resulting in suppression of the keV-tail, and enhancement of the
\sim 10\,\text{eV}
spectrum. This core exclusion is quantified in

5
Direct Detection

Finally, we can use the reflected flux to calculate the signal in ground-based direct detection experiments, such as XENONnT
XENON:2017vdw
and CDEX-10
CDEX:2022kcd
. Generally, the averaged scattering cross section of the ionization process can be calculated by an integral of momentum transfer
q

Essig:2011nj
:

\frac{{\rm d}\langle\sigma_{e}\rangle}{{\rm d}\ln E_{e}}=\frac{\bar{\sigma}_{e}}{8\mu_{e}^{2}}\int{\rm d}q\left[q|F_{\text{DM}}(q)|^{2}|f_{e}(p_{e},q)|^{2}\eta(E_{\text{min}}(q,\Delta E))\right].

(35)

Here, the recoil energy is
E_{e}=p_{e}^{2}/2m_{e}
, where
p_{e}
is the outgoing electron momentum;
q
and
\Delta E
are the momentum and energy transfer;
\bar{\sigma}_{e}=\kappa^{2}e^{2}e_{D}^{2}\mu_{e}^{2}/\pi(q_{0}^{2}+m_{V}^{2})^{2}
is a reference cross section. The reference momentum transfer is often chosen to be
q_{0}=\alpha_{\text{EM}}m_{e}
.
|F_{\text{DM}}(q)|^{2}=1/q^{2}
is the DM form factor. For our interest,
m_{V}<1\times 10^{-13}\,\text{eV}
, and the mass of dark photon can always be ignored.
|f_{e}(p_{e},q)|^{2}
is the electron form factor that depends on the material and initial electron states. The
\eta
function is the averaged squared inverse velocity,

\eta(E_{\text{min}})=\int_{E_{\text{min}}}dE_{\chi}\frac{m_{\chi}}{2E_{\chi}}\frac{1}{\Phi_{\text{halo}}}\frac{d\Phi_{SR}}{dE_{\chi}},

(36)

where

E_{\text{min}}=\frac{1}{2}m_{\chi}v_{\text{min}}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}m_{\chi}\left|\frac{\Delta E}{q}+\frac{q}{2m_{\chi}}\right|^{2}.

(37)

Therefore, the total ionization rate is

\frac{dR_{e}}{d\ln E_{e}}=N_{T}\Phi_{\text{halo}}\frac{d\langle\sigma_{e}\rangle}{d\ln E_{e}},

(38)

where
N_{T}
is the number of atoms in the target.

In the XENON experiment, electron recoils in xenon target produce ionization signals (S2). For recoil energies above
1\,\text{keV}
(
E_{e}\gtrsim 1\,\text{keVee}
), they additionally produce scintillation signals (S1). In this paper, we employ XENONnT S1+S2 data from
XENON:2022ltv

|f_{nl}(p_{e},q)|^{2}
for atomic orbitals labeled

Essig:2017kqs
by atomic wave functions from
Bunge1993RHF

i

(39)

where

6
Summary

Traditionally, the mass of dark photon is solely treated as a parameter in the propagator of DM scattering amplitude. In this paper we demonstrate that dark photon with very small mass can potentially cause astrophysical-scale phenomena simultaneously. Due to the small mixing between dark photon and normal photon, any current coupled to normal electromagnetic field will also source dark photon field. The resulting dark magnetic field follows the shape of Yukawa potential, decaying with a length scale corresponding to
m_{V}^{-1}
. Therefore, if
m_{V}
is comparable to an astronomical scale (the solar radius), corresponding to
m_{V}\sim 10^{-15}\text{eV}
, then the dark magnetic field generated by the internal dynamics of the Sun is sufficiently strong to alter the trajectories of DM particles approaching the Sun. The effect of the dark magnetic field exhibits a double nature: on the one hand, it will bend the trajectories of DM particles, forcing DM particles to travel in longer paths inside the Sun, potentially enhancing opportunities for high-energy scatterings. On the other hand, if the incident DM particle has an unfavorable initial pitch angle relative to the dark magnetic field, then the DM particle has a large probability of being deflected away and prevented from entering the hotter core region of the Sun. Consequently, this will suppress useful keV-energy DM-electron scatterings. In order to find out the overall effect, we developed a Monte Carlo simulation program based on pre-existing works on SRDM, and incorporated new ingredients to capture the effect of dark magnetic field. It turns out that the core-shielding effect dominates, and the high energy tail of SRDM is always suppressed compared to the case without dark magnetic field in the parameter space region we are interested in. Consequently, the sensitivity of ground-based experiments such as CDEX-10 and XENONnT is significantly weakened for
m_{V}\lesssim 10^{-15}\text{eV}
and
m_{\chi}\lesssim 0.1\,\text{MeV}
. Although the solar dynamics and the distribution of dark magnetic field are greatly simplified in this paper, the main point is still enlightening in the sense that the global configuration of dark photon field is as important as its excitations. Future work should integrate realistic solar magnetohydrodynamics–including transient features like sunspots and flares–to refine predictions of SRDM flux and its implications for direct detection.
