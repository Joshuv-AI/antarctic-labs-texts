Recovering All Coefficients in the Schrödinger Equation With Finite Sets of Boundary Measurements
Shitao Liu and Antonio Pierrottet

Abstract
. We consider an inverse problem of recovering all spatial dependent coefficients in the time dependent Schrödinger equation defined on an open bounded domain in ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} , n ≥ 2 n\geq 2 , with smooth enough boundary. We show that by appropriately selecting a finite number of initial conditions and a fixed Dirichlet boundary condition, we may recover all the coefficients in a Lipschitz stable fashion from the corresponding finitely many boundary measurements made on a portion of the boundary. The proof is based on a direct approach, which was introduced in [ 11 ] , to derive the stability estimate directly from the Carleman estimates without any cut-off procedure or compactness-uniqueness argument.



Keywords
: Inverse problem, Schrödinger equation, finite measurements, Carleman estimates, Lipschitz stability

2010 Mathematics Subject Classifications
: 35R30; 35Q41

Introduction and Main Results

In this paper we focus on the inverse problem for the Schödinger equation defined on an open and bounded domain
\Omega

\mathbb{R}^{n}

) boundary

, along with initial condition

(1)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}iw_{t}+q_{2}(x)\Delta w+\mathbf{q}_{1}(x)\cdot\nabla w+q_{0}(x)w=0&\text{in }\ Q^{T}\\[5.69054pt]

Here we assume the electric potential
q_{0}(x)
, magnetic potential

, and the coefficient

appearing in the principal part are all real-valued and satisfy

(2)

\begin{cases}q_{0}=q_{0}(x)\in L^{\infty}(\Omega),\ \mathbf{q}_{1}=\mathbf{q}_{1}(x)\in\left(C^{1}(\Omega)\right)^{n},\ \mbox{and}\\[5.69054pt]

In the following we also use the admissible set
\mathcal{A}_{M}
defined as

(3)

\mathcal{A}_{M}=\{f\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega):\ \|f\|_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}\leq M\}.

where

In addition, we denote

as the open subset of

\Gamma_{1}
as the open subset of
\Gamma

\Gamma=\overline{\Gamma_{0}\bigcap\Gamma_{1}}
and
\Gamma_{0}\cap\Gamma_{1}=\varnothing
.

We then consider the following inverse problem for the system (
1

q_{0}(x)
,
\mathbf{q}_{1}(x)

q_{2}(x)
from measurements of Neumann boundary traces of the solution

\Gamma_{1}
and over the time interval
[0,T]

\Sigma_{1}^{T}=[0,T]\times\Gamma_{1}

To make the observed part of the boundary

\Omega
and the unobserved part of the boundary
\Gamma_{0}

(A1) There exists a strictly convex function

such that the following two properties hold true:

(i) The normal derivative of

of the boundary is non-positive. Namely,

\frac{\partial d}{\partial\nu}=\langle{D}d(x),\nu(x)\rangle\leq 0,\quad\forall x\in\Gamma_{0},

where

for the scalar function
d
is the gradient vector field on
\Omega
with respect to the metric
g
,
\langle X,Y\rangle=g(X,Y)
,
\forall X,Y\in T_{x}\Omega
, where

is the tangent space at

, and
\nu
is the unit outward normal field along the boundary
\Gamma

(ii) There exists a positive constant
\rho>0
such that the Hessian of

(a second-order tensor),

{D}^{2}d(X,X)=\langle{D}_{X}({D}d),X\rangle\geq\rho\langle X,X\rangle=\rho|X|_{g}^{2},\ \forall X\in T_{x}\Omega

is the covariant derivative of a vector field with respect to

(A2)

has no critical point on

Remark 1.1
.

(1) The geometrical assumptions above permit the construction of a vector field that enables a pseudo-convex function necessary for allowing a Carleman estimate containing no lower-order terms for the time dependent Schrödinger equation (
1
). In fact, this applies to the general Schrödinger equation defined on a Riemannian manifold with a general metric

(see Section 2). In our present case,

becomes a Riemannian manifold with the metric

and hence the condition (i) is actually equivalent to the Euclidean normal derivative of

on
\Gamma_{0}
is non-positive since

is assumed to be strictly positive.

(2) The above type of geometrical assumptions were formulated as early as in Hörmander’s classical work on partial differential operators of principal types
[
10
]

Specifically they were also formulated in
[
27
]
under the general Riemannian geometry framework. For examples and detailed illustrations of large general classes of domains and boundaries

satisfying the aforementioned assumptions we refer to

Inverse source problem.
The first step to solve the above inverse problem is to convert it into the corresponding inverse source problem. Indeed, let

(6)

\begin{cases}f_{2}(x)=p_{2}(x)-q_{2}(x),\ \mathbf{f}_{1}(x)=\mathbf{p}_{1}(x)-\mathbf{q}_{1}(x),\ f_{0}(x)=p_{0}(x)-q_{0}(x)\\[5.69054pt]

\displaystyle\begin{cases}iu_{t}+q_{2}(x)\Delta u+\mathbf{q}_{1}(x)\cdot\nabla u+q_{0}(x)u=S(t,x)&\text{in }Q^{T}\\[5.69054pt]

Recovering coefficients in partial differential equations with single or finitely many boundary measurements is an important type of inverse problems and has been well studied in the literature, especially in the context of hyperbolic type equations and systems, see for instance the monographs
[
7
,
13
,
14
,
17
,
18
,
24
]
and the substantial lists of references therein. For the time dependent Schrödinger equation considered in the present paper, it is known that one can recover the electric potential
q_{0}
from a single partial boundary measurement
[
1
,
22
,
25
,
28
]
, even when
q_{0}
is complex valued
[
12
]
, or non-compactly supported
[
6
]
. In the case of the vector valued magnetic potential
\mathbf{q}_{1}
, one can recover it alone or together with the electric potential from properly making finite sets of partial boundary measurements
[
8
,
12
,
15
]
.

Recovering the variable unknown coefficient
q_{2}
appearing in the principal part of the Schrödinger equation is more challenging and much less studied in the literature. Nevertheless, the similar inverse problem for the wave equation plays a significant role in many practical applications and it has been well investigated
[
3
,
23
,
26
]
. The typical approach is to rewrite the equation on an appropriate Riemannian manifold so that the principal part becomes differential operators with constant coefficients. In our proof below such a step is also carried out at the beginning of Section 3.

The standard solution methods for recovering coefficients of partial differential equations from a single or finitely many boundary measurements require using the Carleman-type estimates for the underlying equations. To certain extent, all such approaches can be seen as variations or improvements of the so called Bukhgeim–Klibanov (BK) method which was originated in the seminal paper
[
9
]
, see also
[
16
]
. Our approach to solve the present inverse problem uses a more recent variation of the BK method that combines a Carleman estimate for the Schrödinger equation
[
27
]
and a post Carleman estimate route introduced in
[
11
]
.

Another motivation of our paper is our recent work
[
21
]
where we considered the same inverse problem of recovering all coefficients in the general second-order hyperbolic equation from finite sets of boundary measurements. In particular, in
[
21
]
we derived the uniqueness of all the coefficients first based on Carleman estimates, and then use the uniqueness result to achieve stability estimate based on a compactness-uniqueness argument. In the present paper, however, we employ a novel approach to directly derive the stability estimate from Carleman estimates. As mentioned above, this approach is motivated by the recent work
[
11
]
. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first paper treating all the aforementioned coefficients for the Schrödinger equation (
1
) with a direct approach to derive the stability estimate without having to use any cut-off procedures or compactness-uniqueness arguments.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we recall some necessary tools to solve the inverse problems. This includes the Carleman estimate for the general Schrödinger equation defined on Riemannain manifold, as well as the energy estimate and regularity theory for the Schrödinger equation with Dirichlet boundary condition. In Section
3
we provide the proofs of Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
, and in Section
4
we give some examples where the positivity condition (
5
) is satisfied and some concluding remarks. We also provide a proof of the energy estimates for the Schrödinger equation in the appendix at the end.

2.
Carlemen Estimate, Energy Estimate and Regularity Theory for the Schrödinger Equation

Consider a Riemannian manifold
(\mathcal{M},g)
with the metric
g(\cdot,\cdot)=\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle

\displaystyle iw_{t}(t,x)+\Delta_{g}w(t,x)+G(w)=F(t,x),\qquad(t,x)\in Q=[-T,T]\times\Omega

\displaystyle G(w)(t,x)=\langle\mathbf{P}_{1}(t,x),Dw(t,x)\rangle+P_{0}(x)w(t,x).

\displaystyle|G(w)(t,x)|^{2}\leq C_{T}[|w(t,x)|^{2}+|Dw(t,x)|^{2}],\qquad\forall(t,x)\in Q

Pseudo Convex Function
.
Having chosen the function

\displaystyle\phi(-T,x)

Some Examples and Concluding Remarks

In this last section we first provide some concrete examples such that the key positivity condition (
5
) is satisfied, and then we give some general remarks.

Example 1
. Consider the following functions
w_{0}^{(i)}(x)
,
{x}=(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})\in\Omega
,
i=1,\cdots,n+2
, defined by

\begin{cases}w_{0}^{(1)}(x)=1,\ w_{0}^{(2)}(x)=x_{1},\ w_{0}^{(n+2)}(x)=\frac{1}{2}x_{1}^{2}\\[5.69054pt]
w_{0}^{(i)}(x)=x_{i-1},\ i=3,\cdots,n+1.\end{cases}

Then we may easily see that the matrix
W_{\bf w_{0}}(x)
is a lower triangular matrix with all
1
’s along the diagonal. Thus the determinant of the
W_{\bf w_{0}}(x)
matrix is
1
for all
x\in\Omega
and therefore the positivity condition (
5
) is satisfied.

Example 2
. Consider the following functions
w_{0}^{(i)}(x)
,
x=(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})\in\Omega
,
i=1,\cdots,n+2
, defined by

w_{0}^{(1)}(x)=1,\ w_{0}^{(i)}(x)=e^{x_{i-1}},\ i=2,\cdots,n+1,\ w_{0}^{(n+2)}(x)=e^{-x_{1}}.

Then the matrix
W_{\bf w_{0}}(x)
becomes

\displaystyle W_{\bf w_{0}}(x)=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0&0&\cdots&0&0\\[5.69054pt]
e^{x_{1}}&e^{x_{1}}&0&0&\cdots&0&e^{x_{1}}\\[5.69054pt]
e^{x_{2}}&0&e^{x_{2}}&0&\cdots&0&e^{x_{2}}\\[5.69054pt]
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
e^{x_{n}}&0&0&\cdots&0&e^{x_{n}}&e^{x_{n}}\\[5.69054pt]
e^{-x_{1}}&-e^{-x_{1}}&0&\cdots&0&0&e^{-x_{1}}\\[5.69054pt]
\end{bmatrix}

Here
W_{\bf w_{0}}(x)
is not a lower triangular matrix. However, subtract the 2nd, 3rd,
\cdots
, (n+1)th columns from the last column does transform it into a lower triangular matrix with nonzero diagonal elements. It is easy to see that the determinant is given by
2\prod_{i=2}^{n}e^{x_{i}}
. As
\Omega
is a bounded domain, we hence have the condition (
5
) is satisfied.

Example 3
. As a general formula which contains the above two examples as special cases, we may consider for
x=(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})\in\Omega
,

w_{0}^{(1)}(x)=1,\ w_{0}^{(i)}(x)=w_{0}^{(i)}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{i-1}),\ i=2,\cdots,n+1,\ w_{0}^{(n+2)}(x)=w_{0}^{(n+2)}(x_{1}).

where the functions
w_{0}^{(i)}(x)
,
i=2,\cdots,n+2
, satisfy that there exist positive constants
r_{1},\cdots,r_{n+1}
such that

(36)

\begin{cases}|\partial_{x_{i-1}}w_{0}^{(i)}|\geq r_{i-1}>0,\ i=2,\cdots,n+1;\\[5.69054pt]
|\partial_{x_{1}}w_{0}^{(1)}\partial_{x_{1}x_{1}}w_{0}^{(n+2)}-\partial_{x_{1}}w_{0}^{(n+2)}\partial_{x_{1}x_{1}}w_{0}^{(1)}|\geq r_{n+1}>0.\end{cases}

In this case, the matrix
W_{\bf w_{0}}(x)
is given by

\displaystyle W_{\bf w_{0}}(x)=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0&0&\cdots&0&0\\[5.69054pt]
w_{0}^{(2)}&\partial_{x_{1}}w_{0}^{(2)}&0&0&\cdots&0&\partial_{x_{1}x_{1}}w_{0}^{(2)}\\[5.69054pt]
w_{0}^{(3)}&\partial_{x_{1}}w_{0}^{(3)}&\partial_{x_{2}}w_{0}^{(3)}&0&\cdots&0&\Delta w_{0}^{(3)}\\[5.69054pt]
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
w_{0}^{(n+1)}&\partial_{x_{1}}w_{0}^{(n+1)}&\partial_{x_{2}}w_{0}^{(n+1)}&\partial_{x_{3}}w_{0}^{(n+1)}&\cdots&\partial_{x_{n}}w_{0}^{(n+1)}&\Delta w_{0}^{(n+1)}\\[5.69054pt]
w_{0}^{(n+2)}&\partial_{x_{1}}w_{0}^{(n+2)}&0&0&\cdots&0&\partial_{x_{1}x_{1}}w_{0}^{(n+2)}\\[5.69054pt]
\end{bmatrix}

Note if we multiply the second row by
\frac{\partial_{x_{1}}w_{0}^{(n+2)}}{\partial_{x_{1}}w_{0}^{(2)}}
and subtract it from the last row, we will make the second entry in the last row zero. Then by simple row operations between the 2nd, 3rd,
\cdots
, (n+1)th row and the last row may make the last column all zero except the last entry, which equals to

\displaystyle\partial_{x_{1}x_{1}}w_{0}^{(n+2)}-\frac{\partial_{x_{1}x_{1}}w_{0}^{(2)}\partial_{x_{1}}w_{0}^{(n+2)}}{\partial_{x_{1}}w_{0}^{(2)}}.

Therefore we arrive at a lower triangular matrix and we may easily compute the determinant as

\displaystyle\prod_{i=2}^{n+1}\partial_{x_{i-1}}w_{0}^{(i)}\left[\partial_{x_{1}x_{1}}w_{0}^{(n+2)}-\frac{\partial_{x_{1}x_{1}}w_{0}^{(2)}\partial_{x_{1}}w_{0}^{(n+2)}}{\partial_{x_{1}}w_{0}^{(2)}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{i=3}^{n+1}\partial_{x_{i-1}}w_{0}^{(i)}\left[\partial_{x_{1}}w_{0}^{(2)}\partial_{x_{1}x_{1}}w_{0}^{(n+2)}-\partial_{x_{1}x_{1}}w_{0}^{(2)}\partial_{x_{1}}w_{0}^{(n+2)}\right].

The assumption (
36
) then guarantees the positivity condition (
5
) is satisfied.

Finally, let us mention some general comments and remarks.

(1) It is also possible to set up our inverse problem by assuming Neumann boundary condition
\frac{\partial w}{\partial\nu}
on
\Sigma^{T}
and making measurements of Dirichlet boundary traces of the solution
w
over
\Sigma_{1}^{T}
. This, however, would require more demanding geometrical assumptions on the unobserved portion of the boundary
\Gamma_{0}
. For example, we may need to assume
\langle Dd,\nu\rangle=0
on
\Gamma_{0}
in the geometrical assumption to account for the Neumann boundary condition
[
27
]
. In addition, the more delicate regularity theory for Schrödinger eqaution with nonhomogeneous Neumann boundary condition will also need to be invoked. Nevertheless, the main ideas of solving the inverse problem remain the same.

(2) An important first step in our proof is to extend the solution of the Schrödinger equation (
7
) to
[-T,0]
, as denoted in (
22
). Consequently, we need to assume all the coefficients in the Schrödinger equation are real-valued. As such an extension is needed in most of the solution methods based on Carleman estimates, there were very few work on recovering complex coefficients with single or finitely many boundary measurements for the Schrödinger equation. The recent work
[
12
]
showed that it is possible to recover a complex electrical potential from a single Neumann boundary measurement under certain uniformly boundedness assumption on the difference between two electrical potentials. On the other hand, let us also remark that one does not need the coefficients to be real-valued in order to derive Carleman estimates, although certain boundedness conditions like (
13
) on the coefficients will be needed.

(3) The inverse problem formulated in this paper is often referred as the so-called “single measurement” type inverse problem. Let us mention that there is another standard “many measurements” formulation for inverse problem for the Schrödinger equation. In such formulation one seeks to recover coefficients of the Schrödiner equation from all possible boundary measurements, which are often modeled by the Dirichlet to Neumann map. Although more measurements are required, an advantage in this set up is that one does not need certain positivity assumption on the initial condition in order to solve the inverse problem. In particular, complex-valued coefficients may also be considered. The standard solution methods in the many measurements formulation often involve using the geometric optics solutions
[
4
,
5
]
or the Boundary Control (BC) method developed by Belishev
[
2
]
. For the latter we also refer to the monograph
[
19
]
.

Appendix: Energy Estimate for the Schrödinger equation

In this appendix we provide a proof for the energy estimate of the general Schrödinger equation defined on a Riemannian manifold as in Section 2. This should be a standard estimate, but we did not find any good reference so we decided to include a short proof here.

Theorem 4.1

(Energy Estimate)
.

For the Schrödinger equation (
11
) with the energy level differential term
G
defined as in (
12
) and (
13
), and forcing term
F\in H^{1}(-T,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
. Suppose we have the homogeneous Dirichlet boundary condition
w=0
on
\Sigma=[-T,T]\times\Gamma
for some fixed positive
T>0
, and the initial condition
w_{0}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
. Then we have

(37)

\mathbb{E}_{w}(t)\leq C\left(\|w_{0}\|^{2}_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}+\|F\|^{2}_{H^{1}(-T,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}\right),\ \forall t\in[-T,T]

where
C
depends on
\Omega,\ T,\ \mathbf{P}_{1}
and
P_{0}
.

Proof.

First, let us consider the case where
t\in[0,T]
.
We start with the Schrödinger equation (
11
). Multiply (
11
) by
\overline{w}
, integrate over
Q^{t}=[0,t]\times\Omega
, and take the imaginary part, we get

\displaystyle\int_{Q^{t}}\text{Im}\left[iw_{t}\overline{w}\right]+\text{Im}\left[\Delta_{g}w\overline{w}\right]+\text{Im}\left[\left\langle\mathbf{P}_{1},Dw\right\rangle\overline{w}\right]+\text{Im}\left[P_{0}|w|^{2}\right]dQ^{t}=\int_{Q^{t}}\text{Im}\left[F\overline{w}\right]dQ^{t}.

Apply Green’s identity along with eliminating any purely real part or zero boundary terms, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{Q^{t}}\text{Im}\left[\frac{i}{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}|w|^{2}\right]+\text{Im}\left[\left\langle\mathbf{P}_{1},Dw\right\rangle\overline{w}\right]dQ^{t}=\int_{Q^{t}}\text{Im}\left[F\overline{w}\right]dQ^{t}.

Then by Cauchy–Schwartz inequality and (
13
), we arrive at

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|w(t,x)|^{2}d\Omega\leq\|w_{0}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+C\int_{Q^{t}}\left|F\right|^{2}+\left|Dw\right|^{2}+\left|w\right|^{2}dQ^{t}.

Applying Gronwall’s inequality we then get

(38)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|w(t,x)|^{2}d\Omega\leq Ce^{Ct}\left(\|w_{0}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|F\|^{2}_{L^{2}(Q^{t})}+\|Dw\|^{2}_{L^{2}(Q^{t})}\right).

Similarly, now we multiply (
11
) by
\overline{w}_{t}
, integrate over
Q^{t}
and take the real part we have

(39)

\displaystyle\int_{Q^{t}}\text{Re}\left[i|w_{t}|^{2}\right]+\text{Re}\left[\Delta_{g}w\overline{w}_{t}\right]+\text{Re}\left[\left\langle\mathbf{P}_{1},Dw\right\rangle\overline{w}_{t}\right]+\text{Re}\left[P_{0}w\overline{w}_{t}\right]dQ^{t}=\int_{Q^{t}}\text{Re}\left[F\overline{w}_{t}\right]dQ^{t}

Apply Green’s identity, eliminating the purely imaginary and zero boundary terms, and use the equation (
11
) to rewrite the
\overline{w}_{t}
term in the third integral term on the left-hand side, we get

\displaystyle\int_{Q^{t}}-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}|Dw|^{2}-\text{Re}\left[i\left\langle\mathbf{P}_{1},Dw\right\rangle\Delta_{g}\overline{w}\right]-\text{Re}\left[i\left\langle\mathbf{P}_{1},Dw\right\rangle P_{0}\overline{w}\right]

(40)

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt+\text{Re}\left[i\left\langle\mathbf{P}_{1},Dw\right\rangle\overline{F}\right]+P_{0}\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}|w|^{2}dQ^{t}=-\int_{Q^{t}}\text{Re}\left[F_{t}\overline{w}\right]dQ^{t}.

Now for the second term on the left-hand side of (
40
), by a combination of Green’s identity and the following identity (see, for instance,
[
29
, Lemma 2.1]
)

\left\langle D\overline{w},D\left\langle H,Dw\right\rangle\right\rangle=\left\langle D_{D\overline{w}}H,Dw\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\left\langle H,D\left(\left|Dw\right|^{2}\right)\right\rangle

where
H:\mathcal{M}\to T_{x}\mathcal{M}
, and eliminate purely imaginary terms, we get

(41)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\text{Re}\left[i\left\langle\mathbf{P}_{1},Dw\right\rangle\Delta_{g}\overline{w}\right]

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}\text{Re}\left[i\langle D_{D\overline{w}}\mathbf{P}_{1},Dw\rangle\right],

where
\langle D_{X}\mathbf{P}_{1},DY\rangle
is the Hessian of
\mathbf{P}_{1}
evaluated at
X,Y\in T_{x}\mathcal{M}
. Applying (
41
) to (
40
), we have

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|Dw(t,x)|^{2}d\Omega

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|Dw_{0}|^{2}+P_{0}|w(t,x)|^{2}-P_{0}|w_{0}|^{2}d\Omega-\int_{Q^{t}}2\text{Re}\left[i\langle D_{D\overline{w}}(\mathbf{P}_{1}),Dw\rangle\right]dQ^{t}

\displaystyle+\int_{Q^{t}}-2\text{Re}\left[i\left\langle\mathbf{P}_{1},Dw\right\rangle P_{0}\overline{w}\right]+2\text{Re}\left[i\left\langle\mathbf{P}_{1},Dw\right\rangle\overline{F}\right]+2\text{Re}\left[F_{t}\overline{w}\right]dQ^{t}.

Then by (
13
), (
38
) and the Poincaré inequality, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|Dw(t,x)|^{2}d\Omega\leq Ce^{Ct}\left(\|w_{0}\|^{2}_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}+\|F\|^{2}_{H^{1}(0,t;L^{2}(\Omega))}+\int_{Q^{t}}|Dw|^{2}dQ^{t}\right).

With Gronwall’s inequality we then get

(42)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|Dw(t,x)|^{2}d\Omega\leq Ce^{Ct}\exp(Ce^{Ct})\left(\|w_{0}\|^{2}_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}+\|F\|^{2}_{H^{1}(0,t;L^{2}(\Omega))}\right).

Using (
38
) and (
42
) on
\mathbb{E}_{w}(t)
and we have,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{w}(t)=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left|w(t,x)\right|^{2}+\left|Dw(t,x)\right|^{2}d\Omega

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle Ce^{Ct}\exp(Ce^{Ct})\left(\|w_{0}\|^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega)}+t\|F\|^{2}_{H^{1}(0,t;L^{2}(\Omega))}\right).

This implies the desired estimate (
37
) with
t\in[0,T]
. In an almost identical fashion we can also get the same estimate for
t\in[-T,0]
.
∎
