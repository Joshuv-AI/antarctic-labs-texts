Bridging the intention‒behavior gap in herpes zoster vaccination: A nationwide survey in a self-payment setting
Authors: Fang-Yu Liou, Chang-Ying Chen, Yin-Fan Chang, Ru-Hsueh Wang, Wei-Chen Shen, Cheng-Kuo Huang, Chin-Sung Chang, Chih-Hsing Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHerpes zoster (HZ) causes substantial morbidity, yet global uptake remains suboptimal where the vaccine is excluded from universal coverage. Taiwan exemplifies such a self-payment setting, with the HZ vaccine self-financed and local government subsidies restricted to limited eligible groups. This study analyzed data from a nationwide, web-based cross-sectional survey of adults aged ≥18y fielded in 2025, examining factors associated with HZ vaccination behavior, intention, and willingness to pay (WTP). Of 1690 respondents, 281 (16.6%) were vaccinated; among 1409 unvaccinated, 1140 (80.9%) were willing to vaccinate. Factors associated with HZ vaccination included age ≥65y (OR: 1.59; 95% CI: 1.12–2.25), prior HZ infection (OR: 1.62; 1.23–2.15), prior receipt of influenza, pneumococcal, or respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) vaccines (OR: 3.32; 2.26–4.89), and awareness of local subsidy programs (OR: 2.30; 1.70–3.12). Among unvaccinated respondents, intention was most strongly associated with high concern about HZ (OR: 3.62; 2.67–4.90). When each prior vaccine was examined separately, prior receipt of self-paid RSV (OR: 5.35; 3.44–8.31) and pneumococcal vaccines (OR: 3.48; 2.54–4.75) showed stronger associations with HZ vaccination than coronavirus disease 2019 (OR: 1.50; 1.04–2.16) or influenza vaccines (OR: 1.51; 1.13–2.02). The median WTP rose stepwise across vaccination statuses (unvaccinated-unwilling: USD 8.2, unvaccinated-willing: USD 32.8, vaccinated: USD 65.6; p < .001), yet all values remained below the market price. In this self-payment setting, HZ vaccination was constrained by affordability rather than lack of motivation. Expanding coverage will require policy action on cost, while prior receipt of self-paid adult vaccines offers a clinically identifiable marker for targeted counseling.

Introduction

Herpes zoster (HZ), the clinical reactivation of latent varicella-zoster virus, affects approximately one in three persons during their lifetime, with the highest burden among older adults.1–4 Acute herpetic neuralgia and postherpetic neuralgia (PHN) – the most common long-term complication – substantially impair quality of life and functional independence.5 Beyond the established age-related risk, recent evidence indicates that prior coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) infection further increases the one-year risk of HZ (hazard ratios, 1.59 to 2.16), broadening the population at risk.6,7

Two efficacious vaccines are available for primary prevention. The live attenuated zoster vaccine reduces the incidence of HZ and PHN by 67% and 83% in the first year following administration.8 The recombinant zoster vaccine (RZV), now the preferred option in most national programs,9 reduces the risk of HZ and PHN by 97.2% and 91.2%, respectively, with a complete two-dose regimen.10 Despite this efficacy, global uptake remains suboptimal,11–13 in part because HZ vaccination is excluded from universal national health coverage in many countries and remains primarily an out-of-pocket expense.14 Uptake varies markedly by funding mechanism. Coverage reaches approximately one-third of eligible older adults where HZ vaccination is publicly funded and nationally recommended (32% among adults aged ≥65 y in Australia15), but is substantially lower where the vaccine remains an out-of-pocket expense (0.79% among adults aged ≥40 y in China16). This contrast underscores the value of examining the determinants of uptake specifically under self-payment conditions, where the decision to vaccinate is also a decision to pay.

Taiwan provides an instructive case of such a self-payment setting, where the HZ vaccine is predominantly funded out-of-pocket rather than through universal coverage. The HZ vaccine is not included in Taiwan’s National Health Insurance program or the national immunization schedule. Although some local governments offer partial subsidies, eligibility is mostly restricted to economically disadvantaged individuals, leaving most adults to pay the full cost out-of-pocket. Understanding what drives vaccination uptake under such self-payment conditions is therefore relevant not only to Taiwan but also to the many regions where comprehensive public funding has yet to be established. Behavioral theory anticipates that motivation alone may not suffice in such settings. Both the Health Belief Model and the 5C framework of vaccination psychology treat cost as a structural barrier capable of overriding favorable attitudes,17–20 and the resulting intention–behavior gap is well documented across health behaviors.21 We therefore analyzed vaccination intention and behavior as separate outcomes.

Two questions central to expanding HZ vaccination coverage in a self-payment setting remain inadequately addressed. First, it is unclear whether adults who have received other vaccines are more likely to receive the HZ vaccine. Prior vaccination behavior may reflect general health-seeking tendencies, vaccine confidence, or regular healthcare contact, which can predict future vaccination decisions.22,23 It is also unknown whether this association differs depending on whether the prior vaccine was publicly funded or paid out-of-pocket. Adults who have already paid out-of-pocket for one vaccine may be more willing to do so for additional self-paid vaccines such as HZ, but this hypothesis has not been tested. Second, the determinants of willingness to pay (WTP) for the HZ vaccine remain poorly understood, particularly whether WTP is influenced by vaccination behavior, intention, and prior adult vaccination history.

To address these gaps and to provide insights applicable to similar self-payment settings worldwide, we analyzed data from a nationwide, web-based cross-sectional survey of adults in Taiwan, with the aim of identifying what separates the intention to vaccinate from the act of vaccinating when the cost is borne by the individual. Specifically, we aimed to (1) determine the prevalence of HZ vaccination and the intention to vaccinate among unvaccinated adults; (2) identify factors independently associated with vaccination behavior and intention, with particular focus on the influence of prior adult vaccination history; and (3) assess WTP for the HZ vaccine, including its variation by vaccination behavior, intention, and prior adult vaccination history.

Methods

Study design and respondents

This study is an analysis of a dataset from the 2025 National Survey on Herpes Zoster Knowledge and Vaccination Attitudes, a nationwide survey commissioned by the Taiwan Association of Family Medicine as a public-opinion poll. The survey was conducted from August 20 to September 2, 2025, by an independent professional survey research organization among adults aged 18 y and older. Participants were recruited through active digital distribution across various online platforms, including e-mails, websites, social media, and search engines. To ensure a representative sample, stratified random sampling was performed based on sex, age, and residential area. This recruitment methodology was widely validated and utilized in recent large-scale epidemiological web surveys.24,25 To prevent duplicate responses and ensure the uniqueness of each participant, digital behavioral profiling and user tagging techniques were integrated into the data collection process. Self-reported demographic information was cross-verified using digital behavioral data matching to ensure data accuracy and reliability.26 Participation in the parent survey was voluntary; respondents were informed of its purpose and agreed to participate before answering the questionnaire, and the survey collected no personally identifiable information. The present analysis of this existing dataset was approved by the Institutional Review Board of National Cheng Kung University Hospital before any analysis was undertaken, with a waiver of informed consent (No. B-ER-115–034, April 2, 2026). This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Questionnaire survey

The survey instrument was developed for the parent survey by an expert panel convened by the Taiwan Association of Family Medicine. It was adapted from two previously published studies27,28 and further refined through expert consensus. Each item was reviewed for relevance, accuracy, and clarity by a multidisciplinary panel of specialists in family medicine, geriatrics, and preventive medicine. The panel review established content validity and confirmed cultural and linguistic appropriateness for the Taiwanese setting. Because the instrument used single-item behavioral, attitudinal, and economic measures rather than multi-item latent-construct scales, content validity rather than internal consistency was the appropriate validation standard. The final instrument consisted of 15 items in five sections: (1) demographics and medical history; (2) disease knowledge and risk perception; (3) HZ vaccination behavior, intention, and barriers; (4) prior adult vaccination history; and (5) awareness of local subsidy programs and WTP. Items were presented in true/false, multiple-choice, and 10-point numeric rating scale formats. The questionnaire was developed in Chinese. Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics are presented as counts and percentages. Comparisons across groups were conducted using χ2 tests. Clinical health status was dichotomized based on the diagnosis of at least one of 10 chronic conditions. Perception scales (pain severity, impact on daily life, and concern level) were rated on 1–10 numeric rating scales and dichotomized into low (1–6) and high (7–10) groups, consistent with the commonly used cutoff of ≥7 to denote a high level on numeric rating scales.

Multivariable logistic regression models were employed to identify independent factors associated with two primary outcomes: (1) HZ vaccination behavior (vaccinated vs. unvaccinated) among the total population, and (2) HZ vaccination intention (willing vs. unwilling) among unvaccinated individuals. These models were adjusted for sex, age, chronic medical illness, history of HZ, concern level regarding HZ, and awareness of local subsidy programs. Six logistic regression models were constructed for each primary outcome, each containing a different prior vaccination variable. This approach evaluated the influence of prior immunization at multiple levels while avoiding multicollinearity. Models 1 to 4 examined the influence of each individual prior vaccine separately, with prior receipt of COVID-19 vaccine (Model 1), influenza vaccine (Model 2), pneumococcal vaccine (Model 3), and RSV vaccine (Model 4) as the predictors of interest. Models 5 and 6 used composite measures. Model 5 served as the primary composite analysis, examining prior receipt of any non-COVID-19 adult vaccine (influenza, pneumococcal, or RSV); this composite was designed to isolate the influence of vaccinations requiring more active health-seeking behavior, given that the near-universal COVID-19 vaccination coverage in Taiwan would otherwise obscure such associations. Model 6 was conducted as a sensitivity analysis, examining prior receipt of any adult vaccine (COVID-19, influenza, pneumococcal, or RSV), to assess whether the composite findings remained robust when the universally funded COVID-19 vaccine was included. As a further sensitivity analysis, the primary multivariable models were repeated with the sample restricted to respondents aged ≥50 y, the principal target age for HZ vaccination.

For the WTP analysis, cumulative distribution curves were generated to visualize the maximum out-of-pocket amounts participants were willing to pay, an approach adopted from previous vaccine WTP studies.29,30 For comparisons among three groups stratified by HZ vaccination behavior and intention (vaccinated, unvaccinated-willing, and unvaccinated-unwilling), the Kruskal‒Wallis test was used, followed by Dunn’s post-hoc test with Bonferroni correction for pairwise comparisons. For comparisons between two groups stratified by history of other non-COVID-19 adult vaccinations (yes vs. no), the Mann‒Whitney U test was used. Effect sizes were reported as eta-squared (η2) for Kruskal‒Wallis and r (|Z|/√N) for Mann‒Whitney U tests.

All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 27.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A p value less than .05 was considered statistically significant.

Factors associated with herpes zoster vaccination behavior and vaccination intention

Multivariable logistic regression analysis demonstrated that HZ vaccination behavior was significantly associated with age ≥65 y (odds ratio [OR]: 1.59, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.12–2.25), a history of HZ (OR: 1.62, 95% CI: 1.23–2.15), a history of other non-COVID-19 adult vaccinations (OR: 3.32, 95% CI: 2.26–4.89), and awareness of local subsidy programs (OR: 2.30, 95% CI: 1.70–3.12) (Table 2). Sex, chronic medical illness, and concern levels did not show significant associations with actual vaccination behavior. Regarding vaccination intention, significant factors included age 50–64 y (OR: 1.46, 95% CI: 1.02–2.08), age ≥65 y (OR: 2.26, 95% CI: 1.43–3.59), high concern levels (OR: 3.62, 95% CI: 2.67–4.90), a history of other non-COVID-19 adult vaccinations (OR: 2.54, 95% CI: 1.89–3.42), and awareness of local subsidy programs (OR: 1.64, 95% CI: 1.22–2.22). In contrast, sex, chronic medical illness, and a history of HZ were not significantly associated with the intention to vaccinate. In a sensitivity analysis restricted to the 913 respondents aged ≥50 y (Supplementary Table 1), the principal associations were largely preserved: prior receipt of other non-COVID-19 adult vaccinations remained the strongest correlate of both vaccination behavior (OR: 2.48, 95% CI: 1.52–4.03) and intention (OR: 3.82, 95% CI: 2.40–6.10); high concern remained associated with intention only (OR: 3.39, 95% CI: 2.10–5.48); and awareness of local subsidy programs remained associated with behavior (OR: 2.16, 95% CI: 1.46–3.19), with a similar but borderline association with intention (OR: 1.56, 95% CI: 0.98–2.48). Discussion

In this nationwide cross-sectional survey of adults examining HZ vaccination behavior and intention in a predominantly self-payment setting, four principal findings emerged. First, vaccination intention substantially exceeded actual uptake, indicating a pronounced intention–behavior gap. Second, the correlates of intention and behavior diverged: concern about HZ was associated with intention only, and a prior HZ episode with behavior only. Third, prior receipt of self-paid adult vaccines showed the strongest association with vaccination behavior. Fourth, the public’s WTP fell well below the market price of HZ vaccine and was lowest among individuals with lower vaccine acceptance. Together, these findings indicate that intention and behavior are associated with distinct factors, a pattern consistent with motivational factors relating to intention and economic factors relating to uptake.

In this self-payment setting, HZ vaccination coverage and intention were comparable to or higher than most international reports, yet this favorable engagement did not translate proportionally into actual uptake. The HZ vaccination coverage in this study was 21.0% among adults aged ≥50 y and 26.2% among those aged ≥65 y. This exceeds the coverage rates reported in China (0.79% among those aged ≥40 y in 2024)16 and the United States (17% among those aged ≥50 y in 2021)13 but remains lower than in Australia (32% among those aged ≥65 y in 2019–2020).15 Notably, four in five unvaccinated respondents (1140 of 1409, 80.9%) were willing to vaccinate, substantially exceeding the 55.74% pooled global willingness rate reported in a 2023 systematic review and meta-analysis.31 The contrast between this strong vaccination intention and the suboptimal actual coverage indicates that public attitude is unlikely to be the primary obstacle. Rather, a substantive intention–behavior gap separates the willing from the vaccinated.

Concern level regarding HZ was the factor most strongly associated with vaccination intention but was not associated with actual behavior. Among the unvaccinated respondents, those with high concern levels had more than three times the odds of expressing willingness to vaccinate (OR: 3.62; 95% CI: 2.67–4.90). However, concern level was not independently associated with actual HZ vaccination. This discrepancy suggests that while concern accompanies the intention to be immunized, concern alone appears insufficient to translate intention into actual vaccination under self-payment conditions. Whereas multiple cross-sectional studies have demonstrated that higher perceived risk is associated with greater willingness to receive the HZ vaccine,32–35 our analysis extends these findings by showing that this motivational pathway does not appear to translate into actual uptake when cost remains a barrier.

Conversely, a prior HZ infection was associated with actual vaccination but not with future intention among unvaccinated individuals. Stated differently, among those who had experienced HZ but remained unvaccinated, prior infection was not associated with willingness to vaccinate. This null association can be explained by the pattern of self-reported reasons for non-vaccination shown in Figure 1. Among unvaccinated respondents, “perceived lack of need due to prior HZ history” was cited as a barrier more than three times as often by the unwilling group than the willing group (18.6% vs. 5.9%, p < .001; Figure 1). This suggests that a substantial proportion of people with prior HZ believe a single episode confers lasting protection and therefore see no reason to vaccinate. This belief, however, overlooks the well-documented potential for HZ recurrence, and current Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices (ACIP) guidelines explicitly recommend HZ vaccination irrespective of prior HZ history.9,36,37 Public education should be targeted rather than generic. Adults with a prior HZ episode are identifiable in routine practice and remain at risk of recurrence, and counseling at diagnosis or subsequent visits could address recurrence risk, post-episode vaccine efficacy, and the related misconceptions of low susceptibility and severity.

Whereas prior HZ history reflects direct disease experience, prior receipt of other adult vaccinations represents a different behavioral pathway – one that emerged as an even stronger predictor of HZ vaccination uptake. A history of other non-COVID-19 adult vaccinations was the strongest predictor of HZ vaccination behavior and an independent predictor of intention. The vaccine-specific findings revealed a clear gradient by funding mechanism of each prior vaccine. The entirely self-paid RSV vaccine showed the strongest association (Model 4, OR: 5.35), followed by the recently transitioned pneumococcal vaccine (Model 3, OR: 3.48), and then the long-publicly-funded influenza and COVID-19 vaccines (Models 2 and 1: OR: 1.51 and 1.50, respectively). This gradient suggests that the association reflects individuals’ out-of-pocket spending on preventive care rather than vaccination habits per se. To our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate a funding-mechanism gradient in prior vaccination as a predictor of HZ vaccine uptake, providing a novel framework for understanding vaccination decisions in self-payment settings.

Clinical risk groups warrant separate consideration. Diabetes is associated with an approximately 40% increase in HZ incidence.38,39 Immunocompromising conditions and immunosuppressive therapies raise the risk of HZ, postherpetic neuralgia, disseminated disease, and HZ-related hospitalization.40 ACIP therefore recommends RZV for immunodeficient or immunosuppressed adults from age 19.9 Nonetheless, chronic medical illness was not associated with vaccination behavior or intention. This null association persisted across all six regression models (Tables 2 and 3) and in the analysis restricted to adults aged ≥50 y (Supplementary Table 1). Elevated clinical risk therefore did not distinguish those who vaccinated from those who did not. This finding should be interpreted with caution. Individual conditions were too sparse for stable estimation and were analyzed as a single composite indicator. This indicator grouped severely immunosuppressed patients with those having more common conditions, and such aggregation may have diluted a genuine association among the most immunocompromised. Even so, the absence of any signal suggests that clinical risk is not currently a principal driver of vaccination in this self-payment setting. Clinician-initiated counseling could address this gap.

Across these findings, affordability emerged as the central obstacle separating intention from behavior, and our data build a cumulative case for this conclusion through two lines of evidence. First, cost was the most frequently cited barrier in both unvaccinated subgroups and was cited more often by the willing group than by the unwilling group (62.1% vs. 40.9%, p < .001; Figure 1). This pattern is consistent with prior literature identifying high vaccine cost as the primary barrier to HZ vaccination28,33,41,42 and indicates that cost is the proximal obstacle particularly for those who would otherwise vaccinate. Second, our WTP analysis provides direct quantitative evidence that financial constraints are not merely perceived but objectively present, and that this constraint scales with vaccine acceptance. Figure 2(A) reveals a stepwise WTP gradient across groups defined by HZ vaccination behavior and intention (vaccinated USD 65.6 > unvaccinated-willing USD 32.8 > unvaccinated-unwilling USD 8.2; all pairwise p < .001). Figure 2(B) also shows a parallel pattern when respondents were stratified by history of other non-COVID-19 adult vaccinations (with vs. without history: USD 32.8 vs. USD 16.4, p < .001). Together, these analyses demonstrate that individuals with lower vaccine acceptance – whether defined by HZ behavior and intention or by prior adult vaccination history – consistently report lower WTP. Critically, even in the highest stratum, the median WTP reached only about one-eighth of the approximately USD 520 retail price for a two-dose RZV regimen in Taiwan.

Our findings suggest a five-part strategy for narrowing the intention–behavior gap in self-payment settings. (1) Education. Misconceptions about post-episode protection, susceptibility, and severity were more common among unwilling respondents; opportunistic counseling at diagnosis and follow-up could reframe prior disease experience as a motivator. (2) Subsidies and their communication. WTP fell far short of market price, and awareness of existing subsidies was associated with both outcomes, yet nearly half of respondents were unaware such programs existed. Broader or tiered subsidies, paired with systematic outreach through primary care, pharmacies, and public health channels, may therefore increase uptake. (3) Clinical integration. Prior self-paid vaccination signals a willingness to bear out-of-pocket costs and may identify candidates for targeted counseling. Co-administration with other adult vaccines is safe and offers a low-friction opportunity to reach them.43–46 (4) Barriers. Simplifying access and clarifying vaccine safety could help convert intention into action. (5) Surveillance. Ongoing monitoring of coverage and emerging barriers would allow refinement over time. These recommendations rest on cross-sectional associations and are offered as testable intervention targets rather than established causal levers.

This study possesses several methodological strengths. First, the survey employed a nationwide, stratified random sampling design based on sex, age, and residential area, yielding 1690 valid responses for analysis. Second, we analyzed vaccination behavior and intention as distinct outcomes rather than a composite hesitancy measure. Third, we stratified the analysis by specific adult vaccines, yielding clinically actionable differentiation by subsidization status. Fourth, we quantitatively compared public WTP against the current market price, providing direct evidence of the affordability gap.

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Second, the web-based survey may introduce selection bias, potentially underrepresenting older and digitally less literate adults who are at highest risk for HZ despite stratified sampling. However, because online respondents likely skew toward greater health literacy, this bias would inflate rather than deflate coverage estimates, so the low coverage and wide intention–behavior gap we observed are likely conservative. Third, reliance on self-reported clinical and vaccination histories is subject to recall bias and misclassification. Future prospective studies incorporating electronic medical records or national health insurance claims data are required to validate these findings. Fourth, 46.0% of respondents were aged 18–49 y, so aggregate estimates should be interpreted accordingly. A sensitivity analysis restricted to adults aged ≥50 y confirmed the principal associations. Fifth, individual income and educational attainment were not collected, so residual confounding by socioeconomic status cannot be excluded. Residential area (metropolitan vs. non-metropolitan) was the only available proxy and was not associated with either outcome (Table 1). Future studies incorporating income and education are needed to distinguish affordability from the broader socioeconomic influences on HZ vaccination.

Conclusion

In this self-payment setting, lower HZ vaccination uptake was associated more with cost barriers than with a lack of motivation. Measured WTP fell substantially below the market price of RZV. Concern about HZ was associated with vaccination intention; however, this association alone did not appear sufficient to close the intention–behavior gap. These findings suggest that expanding coverage will likely require policy action on cost. In parallel, prior receipt of self-paid adult vaccines may offer a clinically identifiable marker for targeting counseling and co-administration efforts.
