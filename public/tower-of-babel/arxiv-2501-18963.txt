Time-Varying Bayesian Optimization Without a Metronome
Anthony Bardou, Patrick Thiran

Abstract
Time-Varying Bayesian Optimization (TVBO) is the go-to framework for optimizing time-varying, expensive, noisy black-box functions. However, most asymptotic guarantees for TVBO algorithms assume observations are acquired at a constant frequency. Since streaming GP inference scales quadratically with dataset size, this assumption is unrealistic in the long run. We relax it and derive the first upper regret bound that explicitly accounts for changes in observation sampling frequency. This analysis yields practical recommendations for dataset sizes and stale-data policies. Experiments on synthetic and real-world problems show that an algorithm (BOLT) following these recommendations outperforms state-of-the-art TVBO methods.

Introduction

Bayesian Optimization (BO) uses a surrogate model of the objective function
f
, typically a Gaussian Process (GP), to efficiently learn and optimize it. Its sample efficiency has made BO the standard approach to black-box optimization across diverse applications
(
Marchant and Ramos, 2012
;
Bardou et al., 2025

)
and motivated extensions to challenging settings such as multi-objective optimization
(
Daulton et al., 2022
)
and high-dimensional input spaces
(
Bardou et al., 2024a
)
. Yet only a handful of works study Time-Varying Bayesian Optimization (TVBO), where

also depends on time

, despite the ubiquity of time-varying black-box optimization problems, with occurrences in unmanned aerial vehicles
(

)
, online clustering
(

)
, and network management
(
Kim et al., 2019
)
.

TVBO differs substantially from its static counterpart. In particular, recent work identifies the

Background

2.1
Time-Varying Bayesian Optimization

Like most BO algorithms, a TVBO algorithm uses a centered GP with covariance function
k
(denoted by
\mathcal{GP}\left(0,k\right)
) as prior placed on the objective function
f
with observational noise
\sigma^{2}_{0}
. Conditioned on a dataset of
n
observations
\mathcal{D}=\left\{\left(\bm{x}_{i},t_{i},y_{i}\right)\right\}_{i\in[n]}
, where
y_{i}=f(\bm{x}_{i},t_{i})+\epsilon,\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}_{0})
for any
i\in[n]
, the posterior satisfies
f(\bm{x},t)|\mathcal{D}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\mu_{\mathcal{D}}(\bm{x},t),\sigma^{2}_{\mathcal{D}}(\bm{x},t)\right)
for any
(\bm{x},t)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{T}
, where

\displaystyle\mu_{\mathcal{D}}(\bm{x},t)

\displaystyle=\bm{k}((\bm{x},t),\mathcal{D})\bm{\Delta}^{-1}\bm{y},

(1)

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{\mathcal{D}}(\bm{x},t)

\displaystyle=k((\bm{x},t),(\bm{x},t))-\bm{k}^{\top}\left((\bm{x},t),\mathcal{D}\right)\bm{\Delta}^{-1}\bm{k}\left((\bm{x},t),\mathcal{D}\right),

(2)

where
\bm{\Delta}=\bm{K}+\sigma^{2}_{0}\bm{I}_{n}
,
\bm{K}=\bm{k}(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{D})
,
\bm{k}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})=\left(k((\bm{x}_{i},t_{i}),(\bm{x}_{j},t_{j}))\right)_{(\bm{x}_{i},t_{i})\in\mathcal{X},(\bm{x}_{j},t_{j})\in\mathcal{Y}}
,
\bm{y}=\left(y_{i}\right)_{y_{i}\in\mathcal{D}}
and where
\bm{I}_{n}
is the
n\times n
identity matrix.

At the
i
-th iteration occurring at time
t_{i}
, a TVBO algorithm looks for a query
(\bm{x}_{i},t_{i})
that achieves the best exploration-exploitation trade-off. In the BO framework, this problem is addressed by maximizing an acquisition function
\alpha_{\mathcal{D}}:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{T}\to\mathbb{R}
, so that
\bm{x}_{i}=\argmax_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{S}}\alpha_{\mathcal{D}}(\bm{x},t_{i})
. Many acquisition functions have been proposed, the most popular being GPUCB
(
Srinivas et al., 2012
)
, Expected Improvement
(
Mockus, 1994
)
and Probability of Improvement
(
Jones et al., 1998
)
.

At the
i
-th iteration, the instantaneous performance of a TVBO algorithm is measured by the instantaneous regret
r_{i}=f(\bm{x}^{*}_{i},t_{i})-f(\bm{x}_{i},t_{i})
, where
\bm{x}^{*}_{i}=\argmax_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{S}}f(\bm{x},t_{i})
. The performance up to an horizon
T
is usually measured by the cumulative regret
R_{T}=\sum_{i=1}^{T}r_{i}
.

2.2
The Three Time Scales of a TVBO Problem

A TVBO problem involves three interacting time scales (which are not given to the algorithm): (i) a temporal lengthscale
l_{T}
that characterizes the rate of variation of
f
, (ii) the time horizon
H
of the optimization task, and (iii) the response time
R(n)
of the algorithm, which includes the time needed to observe
f
and to make a new query using a dataset of size
n
. In the following, we denote by
N_{H}=\left\lfloor H/R(0)\right\rfloor
the maximal number of observations that could be collected if the computational overhead of GP inference were negligible.

Because
R(n)
grows with
n
, every TVBO algorithm must implicitly address a trade-off between the information it retains and the frequency at which it can sample
f
. The literature resolves it in three ways: by retaining every observation (growing datasets), by discarding the dataset as a whole at chosen instants (resets), or by discarding selected observations while keeping the rest (removal). Which of these strategies is appropriate depends on the relative magnitudes of
l_{T}
,
H
and
R(n)
. We discuss three particular regimes here.

Near-Constant Response Time.

Suppose that
R(N_{H})\approx R(0)
. The computational overhead remains negligible even if the algorithm retains every observation that can be collected up to the horizon
H
. This regime typically occurs when objective evaluation is substantially more expensive than GP inference. Growing datasets are then computationally viable, and controlling the dataset size offers little benefit in terms of sampling frequency. Differences between algorithms are therefore driven primarily by the quality of statistical models rather than by their response times.

Quasi-Static Problems.

Suppose that
l_{T}\gg R(n)
for a reasonable dataset size
n\ll N_{H}
. The algorithm can collect many observations before the objective changes substantially. Resets may then remain competitive by treating the problem as a sequence of approximately static optimization tasks, although each reset may discard observations that are still informative. Growing datasets can also model the objective accurately, but their response times may eventually become prohibitive when the horizon is long.

General Long-Horizon Problems.

The most challenging regime occurs when the horizon is long, the response time grows noticeably with the dataset size
n
, and
l_{T}
is comparable to
R(n)
for practically useful values of
n
. Growing datasets may then issue observations at an increasingly low frequency, while resets may repeatedly discard the dataset before enough observations have been collected to build an informative surrogate. Removal is the only one among the three strategies that can, in principle, keep the response time bounded without sacrificing the observations that remain informative, but it raises two questions that the other two strategies answer trivially: how many observations to retain, and which ones to discard.

This paper targets this third regime, and Section
3
answers both questions in turn.

summarizes the main TVBO solutions along the axes that the preceding discussion identifies as decisive: how the response time enters the analysis of each method, its stale-data policy, and how its dataset size is controlled.

Existing regret analyses have primarily considered two models of temporal variation. The first one assumes that the objective follows a persistent Markov evolution. In this setting, the expected cumulative regret is bounded from below linearly, and algorithms with upper bounds of the same asymptotic order, up to polylogarithmic factors, have been proposed
(
Bogunovic et al., 2016
;
Brunzema et al., 2025
)
. The second one assumes that the cumulative temporal variation of the objective is sublinear. Sublinear regret can then be achieved when this variation grows sufficiently slowly with the horizon
(
Zhou and Shroff, 2021
;
Deng et al., 2022
)
. Such condition excludes environments with a nonvanishing temporal variation.

We do not include these regret rates in Table
1
, because they hold under fundamentally different models of nonstationarity. Moreover, in a bounded-reward setting, a linear upper bound alone does not distinguish between algorithms. The central distinction relevant to this work is instead whether an algorithm explicitly accounts for the dependence of its response time on its dataset size. Existing algorithms with regret guarantees assume
R(n)\in\Theta(1)
, which amounts to placing themselves in the near-constant regime, whereas methods without this assumption do not model the effect of
R(n)
on their performance.

Mapping these methods onto the three regimes makes the gap visible. Growing datasets (TV-GPUCB, ABO, W-GPUCB) implicitly assume a near-constant response time, while resets (R-GPUCB, ET-GPUCB) and fixed-size sliding windows (SW-GPUCB) suit quasi-static problems. W-DBO is, to the best of our knowledge, the only method performing relevancy-based removal, but the size of its dataset follows from a user-specified removal budget rather than from an analysis of
R(n)
.

This paper closes that gap. Our analysis considers a GPUCB algorithm equipped with a FIFO policy, which isolates the trade-off between retaining more observations and sampling the evolving objective more frequently. It answers both questions raised above: how many observations to retain (Section 3.2) and which ones to discard (Section 3.3).

3
Main Results

3.1
Core Assumptions and Problem Setting

In this section, we introduce the core assumptions underpinning this work. Assumption
3.1
is made in all GP-based BO papers as it ensures the existence of the surrogate model. Assumption
3.2
is widely used in the TVBO literature
(
Bogunovic et al., 2016
;
Nyikosa et al., 2018
;
Bardou et al., 2024b
)
. It captures spatio-temporal dynamics with two correlation functions
k_{S}
and
k_{T}
. Assumption
3.3
requires that
k_{S}
and
k_{T}
verify some properties. It holds for most kernels used in practice (e.g., Matérn, RBF, Rational Quadratic, sinc) and the condition (
3
) is further discussed in Remark
A.4
. Assumption
3.4
introduces some regularity about the objective function in the spatial domain
\mathcal{S}
. It is used for most regret proofs in the literature
(
Srinivas et al., 2012
;
Bogunovic et al., 2016
)
. This is satisfied by sufficiently smooth GPs (e.g., built with Matérn with
\nu>2
, RBF) but it can fail with rougher GPs (e.g., Ornstein-Uhlenbeck processes built with Matérn kernels where
\nu=1/2
).

Assumption 3.1

(Surrogate Model)
.

The objective function
f:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{T}\to\mathbb{R}
is a sample path of
\mathcal{GP}\left(0,k\right)
, where
\mathcal{S}=[0,1]^{d}
and
k:\left(\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{T}\right)^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
is a covariance function.

Assumption 3.2

(Covariance Separability)
.

The covariance function
k
has the following form:

k((\bm{x},t),(\bm{x}^{\prime},t^{\prime}))=\lambda k_{S}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})k_{T}(t,t^{\prime})

where, without loss of generality,
\lambda=1
is the signal variance while
k_{S}:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{S}\to[-1,1]
and
k_{T}:\mathcal{T}\times\mathcal{T}\to[-1,1]
are correlation functions in the spatial and temporal domain, respectively.

Assumption 3.3

(Covariance Properties)
.

The correlation function
k_{S}
(resp.,
k_{T}
) is real, even, isotropic, stationary and can therefore be rewritten as
k_{S}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})=k_{S}(\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime})
for all
\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}
(resp.,
k_{T}(t,t^{\prime})=k_{T}(|t-t^{\prime}|)
for all
t,t^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}
). Furthermore, there exists a nonnegative, nonincreasing function
g_{T}
such that
|k_{T}(\tau)|\leq g_{T}(\tau)
for all
\tau\geq 0
and such that

G=\sup_{m\geq 1}\frac{1}{R(m)}\int_{0}^{mR(m)}g_{T}(\tau)d\tau<\infty,

(3)

where
R
is the response time of the TVBO algorithm (Definition
3.5
). Finally,
\min_{\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}k_{S}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})>0
.

Assumption 3.4

(Lipschitz Continuity in
\mathcal{S}
)
.

Let
g\sim\mathcal{GP}(0,k_{S})
. Then, for any
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{S}
, any
L>0
and any
i\in[d]
,

\mathbb{P}\left[\left|\frac{\partial g(\bm{x})}{\partial x_{i}}\right|>L\right]\leq ae^{-(L/b)^{2}}.

Finally, let us properly define the response time
R(n)
of a TVBO algorithm, which models the combined costs of observing
f
and of updating/conditioning the GP on a dataset of size
n
. It satisfies three natural properties: (i) the objective function cannot be observed at an arbitrarily high frequency because querying it takes a nonzero amount of time, (ii) the response time is an increasing function of the dataset size
n
and (iii) when the dataset size
n
diverges, the response time diverges as well.

Definition 3.5

(Response Time)
.

The response time
R:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
is a function that returns the time separating two consecutive iterations of a TVBO algorithm with a dataset of size
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that (i)
R(0)>0
, (ii)
\forall n\in\mathbb{N},R(n+1)\geq R(n)
and (iii)
\lim_{n\to\infty}R(n)=+\infty
.

Based on Definition
3.5
, we study TVBO algorithms in a realistic, wall-clock–constrained regime where obtaining an informative posterior and issuing the next query are not instantaneous because both function observation and GP inference require non-negligible amounts of time. Concretely, the algorithm queries as soon as it is able to, so that the query times satisfy

t_{0}=0,\qquad t_{m}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}R(n_{i})\quad(m\geq 1),

where
n_{i}
is the dataset size of the TVBO algorithm at iteration
i
.

With this greedy-in-time policy
(
Nyikosa et al., 2018
;
Bardou et al., 2024b
)
, we consider the
average regret

R_{m}/m
as the metric of performance to minimize after
m
iterations. This formulation is natural and well-posed: it makes explicit the fundamental trade-off in TVBO between (i) spending more compute per query to obtain a high-quality posterior (larger
R(n)
) and (ii) issuing more frequent queries to track temporal changes in
f
. Any theoretical or empirical comparison of algorithms in this setting must therefore account for the growth and variability of
R(n)
and for the intrinsic time-scale of variation of
f(\cdot,t)
. Ignoring either one of these properties yields conclusions that may be irrelevant in wall-clock constrained, time-varying applications.

3.2
Regret Analysis Without a Metronome

We now analyze the regret of a TVBO algorithm under the assumptions and definitions introduced in Section
3.1
. As the cumulative regret of a TVBO algorithm is linear in the worst case when
R(n)\in\Theta(1)

(
Bogunovic et al., 2016
)
, one can expect a similar result when
R(n)\in\omega(1)
.

Understanding how the temporal correlation structure
k_{T}
and the response time
R(n)
jointly affect the regret is essential for designing TVBO algorithms. Existing regret analyses that explicitly manage stale observations have mainly considered policies that reset the entire dataset, either periodically
(
Bogunovic et al., 2016
)
or after an event is triggered
(
Brunzema et al., 2025
)
, while assuming a constant response time
R(n)\in\Theta(1)
. Such resets are relatively aggressive, as they discard all past observations at once, including recent observations that may remain informative. We consider instead a gentler First-In, First-Out (FIFO) policy: once the dataset reaches a maximal size
n
, each new observation replaces the oldest one, so that the dataset always contains the
n
most recent observations. This setting allows us to study the trade-off between the information retained from past observations, governed by
k_{T}
, and the sampling frequency, governed by
R(n)
. This trade-off is formalized by the asymptotic regret bound established in the following theorem.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a TVBO algorithm that uses the GPUCB acquisition function. Let
R(s)
be the response time of
\mathcal{A}
for a dataset size
s\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
n
be the maximal dataset size of
\mathcal{A}
and let
\|\bm{u}_{n}\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{T}^{2}(iR(n))
. Let
\delta\in(0,1)
and let
R_{T}
be the cumulative regret of
\mathcal{A}
after
T
iterations. Then, with probability
1-\delta
,

R_{T}\leq\sqrt{TC_{1}\beta_{T}\left(\gamma_{n}+\frac{1}{2\sigma_{0}^{2}}(T-n)(1-C_{2}\|\bm{u}_{n}\|_{2}^{2})\right)}+\frac{\pi^{2}}{6},

(4)

where
C_{1}
,
C_{2}\in\mathcal{O}(1)
,
\gamma_{n}\in\mathcal{O}(n)
and
\beta_{T}\in\mathcal{O}(\log T)
are defined in Appendix
A
.

The proof of Theorem
3.6
is provided in Appendix
A
. The gist of the proof is to first bound the cumulative regret of the TVBO algorithm from above with an expression that involves the mutual information
\gamma_{n}=I(\bm{f}_{n},\bm{y}_{n})
, where
\bm{f}_{n}=\left(f(\bm{x}_{1},t_{1}),\cdots,f(\bm{x}_{n},t_{n})\right)
and
\bm{y}_{n}=\left(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n}\right)
, and the posterior variance of the surrogate model with maximal dataset size
n
. This is achieved with proof techniques introduced by
Srinivas et al. (2012)
;
Bogunovic et al. (2016)
. Next, an upper bound is derived for the posterior variance that involves
\|\bm{u}_{n}\|^{2}_{2}
. Combining these two upper bounds yields (
4
).

Dependence of the constants and non-negativity.

The constants
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are independent of
n
and
T
, but they are not universal. In particular, they depend on the observational noise
\sigma^{2}_{0}
and on the spatial and temporal kernels
k_{S}
and
k_{T}
. Furthermore, the quantity under the square root in Theorem
3.6
is shown in Appendix
A
to be always non-negative (see Lemma
A.3
).

Interpretation.

For fixed maximal dataset size
n
and
T\to\infty
,
\gamma_{n}
is constant and
\beta_{T}
grows polylogarithmically, yielding
R_{T}\in\widetilde{O}(T)
. This does not establish sublinear regret under persistent temporal variation, consistently with the TVBO literature
(
Bogunovic et al., 2016
;
Brunzema et al., 2025
)
. The bound instead reveals how the response time
R(n)
and temporal kernel
k_{T}
jointly affect the choice of maximal dataset size.

In the static case,
k_{T}(t,t^{\prime})=1
and
\gamma_{n}
is the mutual information associated with
k_{S}
. Setting
n=T
(no dataset cap) in (
4
) yields
R_{T}\leq\sqrt{TC_{1}\beta_{T}\gamma_{T}}+\pi^{2}/6
, recovering the standard sublinear GPUCB bound of Theorem 2 in
Srinivas et al. (2012)
.

For fixed
n
and
T\gg n
,
\gamma_{n}/T
vanishes and
T-n\sim T
. The leading term in (
4
) therefore depends on
n
through
\sqrt{1-C_{2}\|\bm{u}_{n}\|_{2}^{2}}
. Maximizing
\|\bm{u}_{n}\|_{2}^{2}
thus minimizes the leading asymptotic term of the bound, motivating the following recommendation.

Recommendation 1.

In the long-horizon regime, a TVBO algorithm should maintain a dataset of size
n^{*}
, where

\displaystyle n^{*}

\displaystyle=\argmax_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\|\bm{u}_{n}\|_{2}^{2}=\argmax_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{T}^{2}(iR(n)).

(5)

Computability of Recommendation 1.

Assumptions
3.1
-
3.4
do not guarantee that
\|\bm{u}_{n}\|^{2}_{2}
admits a maximizer over
\mathbb{N}
(see the right panel of Figure
1
for an empirical example, and Appendix
B
for sufficient conditions). This has no practical consequence: at iteration
T
, the dataset contains at most
T
observations, so any maximal size larger than
T
has the same effect as
n^{*}=T
, and the search in (
5
) can be restricted to
n\in[T]
, over which the maximum is always attained. This range widens as the experiment goes on: it settles on the finite maximizer of
\|\bm{u}_{n}\|^{2}_{2}
when one exists, and otherwise keeps recommending a larger dataset, that is, retaining every observation collected so far.

does not scale with the dataset size

(i.e.,

),
\|\bm{u}_{n}\|_{2}^{2}

T
is recommended.

3.3
Removal of Irrelevant Observations

In the previous section, we used a FIFO policy to study the joint effect of the temporal kernel
k_{T}
, the response time
R(n)
, and the maximal dataset size
n
. This analysis led to Recommendation 1. The simplicity of the FIFO policy makes it an analytically tractable reference, but it might be suboptimal because it needs not retain the most informative observations.

Given the maximal dataset size prescribed by Recommendation 1, the remaining question is therefore to select the observation to remove whenever this size is exceeded.
Bardou et al. (2024b)
propose to select
\bm{o}_{i}\in\mathcal{D}
that minimizes the integrated 2-Wasserstein distance
(
Kantorovich, 1960
)
between the GP surrogate
\mathcal{GP}_{\mathcal{D}}\left(\mu_{\mathcal{D}},\sigma_{\mathcal{D}}^{2}\right)
conditioned on
\mathcal{D}
and the surrogate
\mathcal{GP}_{\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}}\left(\mu_{\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}},\sigma_{\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}}^{2}\right)
conditioned on
\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}=\mathcal{D}\setminus\{\bm{o}_{i}\}
. This policy achieves good empirical performance
(
Bardou et al., 2024b
)
, but its connection to the subsequent optimization decisions has not been formally established. In this section, we show that minimizing this distance controls the perturbation of the acquisition function induced by removing an observation, thereby providing a theoretical justification for the policy under the following assumption.

Assumption 3.7

(Lipschitz Acquisition)
.

At iteration
T\in\mathbb{N}
, the acquisition function
\alpha:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{T}\to\mathbb{R}
is built from the posterior mean
\mu(\bm{x},t)
and the posterior standard deviation
\sigma\left(\bm{x},t\right)
of the GP surrogate, that is,
\alpha(\bm{x},t)=g_{T}(\mu(\bm{x},t),\sigma\left(\bm{x},t\right))
for some function
g_{T}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
. Furthermore,
g_{T}
is a Lipschitz continuous function, that is,

|g_{T}(\bm{u})-g_{T}(\bm{v})|\leq L_{T}||\bm{u}-\bm{v}||_{2},\text{ }\forall\bm{u},\bm{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

(6)

Assumption
3.7
holds for many acquisition functions. For example, if
\alpha
is GPUCB
(
Srinivas et al., 2012
)
, that is,

Recommendation 2.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we developed a regret analysis that explicitly accounts for the dependence of computational overhead and observation sampling frequency on the dataset size of a TVBO algorithm. Our main result, Theorem
3.6
, establishes an asymptotic upper regret bound for GPUCB applied to a spatio-temporal GP with a FIFO policy. To the best of our knowledge, it is the first result to jointly relate TVBO regret to the temporal covariance structure and the algorithm’s response time, yielding a principled recommendation for the maximal dataset size. Our second result, Theorem
3.8
, bounds the acquisition-function perturbation induced by observation removal using the integrated Wasserstein distance between GP surrogates, providing theoretical support for the policy of
Bardou et al. (2024b)
. Together, these results inform two complementary decisions: how many observations to retain and which to remove. We combined these recommendations in BOLT, which consistently achieves competitive or superior performance across diverse synthetic and real-world benchmarks.

Future work will extend this analysis beyond Assumptions
3.2
and
3.3
. In particular, nonseparable spatio-temporal kernels could capture dynamics whose temporal behavior varies across the spatial domain and could be applied to more complex spatio-temporal black-box optimization problems.

Proof.

Let us start by recalling the definition of the posterior variance in (

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{\mathcal{D}}(\bm{x},t)

\displaystyle=\bm{v}^{\top}\bm{\Lambda}^{-1}\bm{v}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{v_{i}^{2}}{\lambda_{i}+\sigma^{2}_{0}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}+\sigma^{2}_{0}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}k((\bm{x},t),(\bm{x}_{j},t_{j}))\phi_{ij}\phi_{ik}k((\bm{x},t),(\bm{x}_{k},t_{k}))

(27)

where
\bm{v}=\bm{Q}^{\top}\bm{k}((\bm{x},t),\mathcal{D})
while (
27
) is the quadratic form
q_{n}(\bm{x},t)
rewritten with sums instead of matrix products and using the fact that
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{i}
for all
i\in[n]
. Furthermore, note that the largest eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}
of the Gram matrix
\bm{K}
built from observations collected every
R(n)
unit of time can be bounded from above:

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\max_{i\in[n]}\bm{K}_{ii}+\sum_{j\neq i}\left|\bm{K}_{ij}\right|

(28)

\displaystyle\leq 1+2\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\left|k_{T}(jR(n))\right|

(29)

\displaystyle\leq 1+2\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}g_{T}(jR(n))

(30)

\displaystyle\leq 1+\frac{2}{R(n)}\int_{0}^{(n-1)R(n)}g_{T}(\tau)d\tau

(31)

\displaystyle\leq 1+2G

(32)

where (
28
) is an application of Gershgorin’s theorem for real and symmetric matrices, (
29
) holds because all the diagonal entries of
\bm{K}
are equal to one, and because for all
j\in[n]
, there are at most two entries
k_{T}(jR(n))
at each temporal lag
jR(n)
, and (
30
) holds under Assumption
3.3
. Inequality (
31
) is an application of the quadrature rule on the truncated range
[0,(n-1)R(n)]
: because
g_{T}
is nonnegative and nonincreasing,
R(n)g_{T}(jR(n))\leq\int_{(j-1)R(n)}^{jR(n)}g_{T}(\tau)d\tau
for every
j\geq 1
, and summing these inequalities over
j=1,\cdots,n-1
yields (
31
). Finally, (
32
) holds because
g_{T}
is nonnegative, so that
\int_{0}^{(n-1)R(n)}g_{T}(\tau)d\tau\leq\int_{0}^{nR(n)}g_{T}(\tau)d\tau
, and because of the definition (
23
) of
G
.

Let
\bar{\lambda}=1+2G
. Then, rearranging the sums in (
27
), we have

\displaystyle q_{n}(\bm{x},t)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\bar{\lambda}+\sigma^{2}_{0}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}k((\bm{x},t),(\bm{x}_{j},t_{j}))k((\bm{x},t),(\bm{x}_{k},t_{k}))\underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi_{ij}\phi_{ik}}_{\delta_{jk}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\bar{\lambda}+\sigma^{2}_{0}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}k^{2}((\bm{x},t),(\bm{x}_{i},t_{i}))

(33)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\bar{\lambda}+\sigma^{2}_{0}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{S}^{2}(\bm{x},\bm{x}_{i})k_{T}^{2}(t,t_{i})

(34)

where
\delta_{jk}
is the Kronecker delta with value
1
if
j=k
and
0
otherwise, (
33
) is due to the orthonormality of the eigenvectors of
\bm{K}
and (
34
) is due to Assumption
3.2
.

Let
\kappa=\min_{\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}k_{S}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})
. Then,
\kappa>0
as per Assumption
3.3
and we finally have

\displaystyle q_{n}(\bm{x},t)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\kappa^{2}}{\bar{\lambda}+\sigma^{2}_{0}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{T}^{2}(t,t_{i})

\displaystyle=C_{2}\|\bm{u}_{n}\|^{2}_{2},

where
C_{2}=\kappa^{2}/(\bar{\lambda}+\sigma^{2}_{0})
.

Noting that
\sigma^{2}_{\mathcal{D}}(\bm{x},t)=1-q_{n}(\bm{x},t)\leq 1-C_{2}\|\bm{u}_{n}\|_{2}^{2}
concludes the proof. Recalling that
\sigma^{2}_{\mathcal{D}}(\bm{x},t)\geq 0
also yields that
0\leq 1-C_{2}\|\bm{u}_{n}\|_{2}^{2}
, confirming that
1-C_{2}\|\bm{u}_{n}\|_{2}^{2}
is non-negative.
∎

Remark A.4
.

The constant
G
can be finite even when
g_{T}
is not integrable. Suppose that
g_{T}(\tau)\leq c(1+\tau)^{-p}
and
R(m)\geq c_{R}m^{q}
for some
c,c_{R},p>0
and
q\geq 0
. Then
G<\infty
whenever
p(1+q)>1
, a condition that does not require
p>1
. In particular, temporal correlation functions whose tails decay as
\Theta(\tau^{-1})
, such as sinc or a Rational Quadratic with
\alpha=1/2
, satisfy it for every
q>0
, although no integrable
g_{T}
dominates them (see Appendix
B
for a detailed discussion on these two cases).

Finally, let us prove Theorem
3.6
.

Proof.

Combining Lemmas
A.2
and
A.3
, we get

\displaystyle R_{T}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{C_{1}T\beta_{T}\left(\gamma_{n}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=n+1}^{T}\log(1+\sigma^{-2}_{0}\left(1-C_{2}\|\bm{u}_{n}\|_{2}^{2}\right))\right)}+\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{C_{1}T\beta_{T}\left(\gamma_{n}+\frac{\sigma^{-2}_{0}}{2}(T-n)\left(1-C_{2}\|\bm{u}_{n}\|_{2}^{2}\right)\right)}+\frac{\pi^{2}}{6},

(35)

where (
35
) uses
\log(1+x)\leq x
for any

.
∎

For the sake of completeness, we conclude this appendix by showing that the information gain
\gamma_{n}
scales linearly with
n
in the time-varying setting.

Lemma A.5
.

For all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\gamma_{n}\in\mathcal{O}(n)
.

Proof.

Recall that
\gamma_{n}
can be recovered from the covariance matrix
k(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{D})
and its spectrum. More specifically, denoting
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
the ordered spectrum of
k(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{D})
, we have

\displaystyle\gamma_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(1+\sigma^{-2}_{0}\lambda_{i})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{-2}_{0}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}

(36)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{-2}_{0}\Tr(k(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{D}))

(37)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{-2}_{0}n,

(38)

where (
36
) uses the property
\log(1+x)\leq x
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, (
37
) uses the property that the trace of a matrix is the sum of its eigenvalues, and (
38
) holds for any kernel that is stationary and normalized (i.e., that satisfies
k((\bm{x},t),(\bm{x},t))=1
for all
(\bm{x},t)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{T}
).

One immediately concludes from (
38
) that
\gamma_{n}\in\mathcal{O}(n)
, which also concludes the proof.
∎

Appendix B
Sufficient Conditions for Benefiting from a Maximal Dataset Size

In this appendix, we derive sufficient conditions for
\|\bm{u}_{n}\|^{2}_{2}
to admit a finite maximizer
n^{*}
. According to Recommendation 1, these become sufficient conditions for characterizing when capping the dataset size of a TVBO algorithm is beneficial. Throughout,
p
denotes the polynomial decay exponent of the temporal correlation function
k_{T}
and
q
the polynomial growth exponent of the response time
R
, while
c_{T}
and
c_{R}
denote the associated multiplicative constants.

Proposition B.1
.

Suppose that there exist
p,q,c_{T},c_{R}>0
such that

|k_{T}(\tau)|\leq c_{T}(1+\tau)^{-p}\quad\text{and}\quad R(n)\geq c_{R}n^{q}

for every
\tau\geq 0
and
n\geq 1
. Then

\|\bm{u}_{n}\|_{2}^{2}\leq c\,n^{-2pq}\sum_{i=1}^{n}i^{-2p}

for some constant
c>0
. Consequently,

\|\bm{u}_{n}\|_{2}^{2}=\begin{cases}\mathcal{O}\left(n^{-2pq}\right),&p>1/2,\\[2.84526pt]
\mathcal{O}\left(n^{-q}\log n\right),&p=1/2,\\[2.84526pt]
\mathcal{O}\left(n^{1-2p(1+q)}\right),&p<1/2.\end{cases}

In particular, if
2p(1+q)>1
, then
\|\bm{u}_{n}\|^{2}_{2}\to 0
and the sequence admits a finite maximizer.

Proof.

Since
\tau\mapsto c_{T}(1+\tau)^{-p}
is nonincreasing and
R(n)\geq c_{R}n^{q}
, we have, for every
n\geq 1
and every
i\in[n]
,

k_{T}^{2}(iR(n))\leq c_{T}^{2}\left(1+ic_{R}n^{q}\right)^{-2p}\leq c_{T}^{2}\left(ic_{R}n^{q}\right)^{-2p}=c\,n^{-2pq}i^{-2p},

where
c=c_{T}^{2}c_{R}^{-2p}
. Summing this inequality over
i=1,\ldots,n
gives the first claim. The three asymptotic regimes then follow from the standard behavior of the partial sums of
i^{-2p}
, which are bounded when
2p>1
,
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
when
2p=1
, and
\mathcal{O}(n^{1-2p})
when
2p<1
.

For the last claim, observe that
2p\geq 1
implies
2p(1+q)\geq 1+q>1
, so that the single condition
2p(1+q)>1
includes the first two regimes. In those two regimes,
\|\bm{u}_{n}\|^{2}_{2}
is
\mathcal{O}(n^{-2pq})
and
\mathcal{O}(n^{-q}\log n)
respectively, both of which vanish because
q>0
. In the third regime,
2p(1+q)>1
makes the exponent
1-2p(1+q)
negative, so
\|\bm{u}_{n}\|^{2}_{2}\to 0
as well. A nonnegative sequence converging to
0
attains its supremum at a finite index, which concludes the proof.
∎

The condition
2p(1+q)>1
admits a simple interpretation: capping the dataset size is beneficial when the temporal correlation decays fast enough (large
p
) relative to how fast the response time grows (large
q
). It fails only when
2p(1+q)\leq 1
, which, since
q>0
, forces
p<1/2
. The next proposition shows that, in this regime,
\|\bm{u}_{n}\|^{2}_{2}
diverges when the polynomial decay of
k_{T}
is tight.

Proposition B.2
.

Suppose that there exist
p,q,c^{\prime}_{T},c^{\prime}_{R}>0
such that

k_{T}(\tau)\geq c^{\prime}_{T}(1+\tau)^{-p}\quad\text{and}\quad R(n)\leq c^{\prime}_{R}n^{q}

for every
\tau\geq 0
and
n\geq 1
. If
2p(1+q)<1
, then
\|\bm{u}_{n}\|^{2}_{2}\to+\infty
, and the sequence admits no finite maximizer.

Proof.

For every
n\geq 1
and every
i\in[n]
, we have
1\leq in^{q}
, hence
1+iR(n)\leq 1+ic^{\prime}_{R}n^{q}\leq(1+c^{\prime}_{R})in^{q}
. Therefore,

k_{T}^{2}(iR(n))\geq c^{\prime 2}_{T}\left(1+iR(n)\right)^{-2p}\geq c^{\prime}\,n^{-2pq}i^{-2p},

where
c^{\prime}=c^{\prime 2}_{T}(1+c^{\prime}_{R})^{-2p}
. Since
q>0
, the assumption
2p(1+q)<1
implies
2p<1
, so that

\sum_{i=1}^{n}i^{-2p}\geq\int_{1}^{n+1}x^{-2p}dx=\frac{(n+1)^{1-2p}-1}{1-2p}.

Summing the pointwise bound over
i=1,\ldots,n
therefore gives
\|\bm{u}_{n}\|^{2}_{2}\in\Omega\left(n^{1-2p(1+q)}\right)
, which diverges because
1-2p(1+q)>0
. A diverging sequence admits no finite maximizer.
∎

Remark B.3
.

Propositions
B.1
and
B.2
leave out the edge case
2p(1+q)=1
, at which the upper bound of the former is
\mathcal{O}(1)
and the lower bound of the latter is
\Omega(1)
. At this order the analysis is inconclusive, and whether
n^{*}
is finite or not has to be determined by another method.

The remaining regime
2p(1+q)<1
is, however, unreachable under Assumption
3.3
. Indeed, suppose that the envelope satisfies
g_{T}(\tau)=\Theta(\tau^{-p})
and that the response time satisfies
R(m)=\Theta(m^{q})
with
q>0
. Then the finiteness of
G
required by Assumption
3.3
already imposes
p(1+q)\geq 1
, hence
2p(1+q)\geq 2
. Assumption
3.3
is therefore more demanding than the condition
2p(1+q)>1
of Proposition
B.1
, so that the divergence regime of Proposition
B.2
and the edge case
2p(1+q)=1
both fall outside the setting of this paper.

Consequently, under Assumptions
3.1
-
3.4
and a response time growing polynomially with
q>0
, every temporal correlation function with a polynomially decaying envelope admits a finite
n^{*}
: capping the dataset size is always beneficial in this setting. This does not, however, confine the analysis to the first regime of Proposition
B.1
. Because Assumption
3.3
constrains the product
p(1+q)
rather than the decay exponent
p
alone, all three regimes remain reachable: an envelope with
p=1/5
combined with a response time with
q=4
, for instance, satisfies
p(1+q)=1
, falls in the third regime, and yields
\|\bm{u}_{n}\|^{2}_{2}\in\mathcal{O}(n^{-1})
.

Two borderline examples: sinc and RQ-1/2.

Let us instantiate the above discussion on the two temporal correlation functions of Figure
1
that sit exactly at the boundary of validity of Assumption
3.3
. The sinc correlation function
k_{T}(\tau)=\sin(\tau/l_{T})/(\tau/l_{T})
satisfies
|k_{T}(\tau)|\leq\min(1,l_{T}/\tau)
, while the Rational Quadratic correlation function
k_{T}(\tau)=\left(1+\tau^{2}/(2\alpha l_{T}^{2})\right)^{-\alpha}
decays as
\Theta(\tau^{-2\alpha})
. Both therefore admit a polynomial envelope with exponent
p=1
, the former exactly and the latter for
\alpha=1/2
. Neither is integrable, so neither is dominated by an integrable envelope
g_{T}
. This is precisely why Assumption
3.3
is stated through the quantity
G
rather than through
\int_{0}^{\infty}g_{T}(\tau)d\tau
.

With
p=1
, Assumption
3.3
holds as soon as
p(1+q)=1+q>1
, that is, as soon as
q>0
, and Proposition
B.1
applies because
2p(1+q)=2(1+q)>1
. The resulting rate is
\|\bm{u}_{n}\|^{2}_{2}\in\mathcal{O}(n^{-2q})
, so both correlation functions admit a finite
n^{*}
as soon as the response time grows polynomially with the dataset size. For the response time
R(n)\in\Theta(n^{3})
used in the left panel of Figure
1
, this gives
\|\bm{u}_{n}\|^{2}_{2}\in\mathcal{O}(n^{-6})
, which is consistent with the finite maxima plotted there. More generally, for a RQ correlation function with parameter
\alpha
, Assumption
3.3
requires
\alpha>1/(2(1+q))
while Proposition
B.1
requires
\alpha>1/(4(1+q))
. For
q=3
, these thresholds are
\alpha>1/8
and
\alpha>1/16
respectively, so that
\alpha=1/2
lies comfortably inside both.

The only case in which these two correlation functions fall outside Assumption
3.3
is
q=0
, that is, a response time that does not grow with the dataset size. Then
\int_{0}^{mR(m)}g_{T}(\tau)d\tau
grows logarithmically in
m
while
R(m)
remains constant, so that
G
diverges. However, in that setting,
\|\bm{u}_{n}\|^{2}_{2}
is nondecreasing and Recommendation 1 returns
n^{*}=T
irrespective of the temporal correlation function. Capping the dataset size is not beneficial in that regime, so nothing is lost by excluding it.

Appendix C
Recommended Stale Data Management Policy

In this appendix, we prove Theorem
3.8
by connecting the
L^{2}
-distance between Lipschitz-continuous acquisition functions and the integrated 2-Wasserstein distance between GP posteriors.

Proof.

Let us start by considering the effect of Assumption
3.7
on the distance at a single point
(\bm{x},t)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{T}
.

\displaystyle|\alpha_{\mathcal{D}}(\bm{x},t)-\alpha_{\tilde{\mathcal{D}}}(\bm{x},t)|

\displaystyle\leq L_{T}||\left(\mu_{\mathcal{D}}(\bm{x},t)-\mu_{\tilde{\mathcal{D}}}(\bm{x},t),\sigma_{\mathcal{D}}(\bm{x},t)-\sigma_{\tilde{\mathcal{D}}}(\bm{x},t)\right)||_{2}

\displaystyle=L_{T}\sqrt{\left(\mu_{\mathcal{D}}(\bm{x},t)-\mu_{\tilde{\mathcal{D}}}(\bm{x},t)\right)^{2}+\left(\sigma_{\mathcal{D}}(\bm{x},t)-\sigma_{\tilde{\mathcal{D}}}(\bm{x},t)\right)^{2}}.

(39)

We can now bound the squared
L^{2}
distance between the two acquisition functions
\alpha_{\mathcal{D}}
and
\alpha_{\tilde{\mathcal{D}}}
on any subset
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\times\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
of the spatio-temporal domain
\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{T}
:

\displaystyle||\alpha_{\mathcal{D}}-\alpha_{\tilde{\mathcal{D}}}||^{2}_{2}

\displaystyle=\oint_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}\int_{\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}\left(\alpha_{\mathcal{D}}(\bm{x},t)-\alpha_{\tilde{\mathcal{D}}}(\bm{x},t)\right)^{2}d\bm{x}dt

\displaystyle\leq\oint_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}\int_{\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}L_{T}^{2}\left(\left(\mu_{\mathcal{D}}(\bm{x},t)-\mu_{\tilde{\mathcal{D}}}(\bm{x},t)\right)^{2}+\left(\sigma_{\mathcal{D}}(\bm{x},t)-\sigma_{\tilde{\mathcal{D}}}(\bm{x},t)\right)^{2}\right)d\bm{x}dt

(40)

\displaystyle=L_{T}^{2}\oint_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}\int_{\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}\left(\left(\mu_{\mathcal{D}}(\bm{x},t)-\mu_{\tilde{\mathcal{D}}}(\bm{x},t)\right)^{2}+\left(\sigma_{\mathcal{D}}(\bm{x},t)-\sigma_{\tilde{\mathcal{D}}}(\bm{x},t)\right)^{2}\right)d\bm{x}dt

\displaystyle=L_{T}^{2}W_{2}^{2}(\mathcal{GP}_{\mathcal{D}},\mathcal{GP}_{\tilde{\mathcal{D}}}),

(41)

where (
40
) follows from (
39
) and (
41
) from the definition of the integrated 2-Wasserstein distance on the domain
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\times\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
. Applying a square-root on both sides yields the desired result.
∎

WLAN.

This benchmark aims at maximizing the throughput of a Wireless Local Area Network (WLAN). 27 moving end-users are associated with one of 6 fixed nodes and continuously stream a large amount of data. As they move in space, they change the radio environment of the network, which should adapt accordingly to improve its performance. To do so, each node has a power level that can be tuned for the purpose of reaching the best trade-off between serving all its users and not causing interference for the neighboring nodes.

The performance of the network is computed as the sum of the Shannon capacities for each pair of node and associated end-users. The Shannon capacity
(
Kemperman, 1974
)
sets a theoretical upper bound on the throughput of a wireless communication. We denote it
C(i,j)
, we express it in bits per second (bps). It depends on
S_{ij}
the Signal-to-Interference plus Noise Ratio (SINR) of the communication between node
i
and end-user
j
, as well as on
W
, the bandwidth of the radio channel (in Hz):

C_{ij}(\bm{x},t)=W\log_{2}(1+S_{ij}(\bm{x},t)).

Then, the objective function is

f(\bm{x},t)=\sum_{i=1}^{6}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}C_{ij}(\bm{x},t),

where
\mathcal{N}_{i}
are the end-users associated with node
i
.

For the numerical evaluation, we optimized the power levels
\bm{x}
in the domain
[10^{0.1},10^{2.5}]^{6}
. For this experiment, the TVBO solutions were evaluated with a Matérn-5/2 for the spatial covariance function and a Matérn-1/2 for the temporal covariance function. The results are provided in Figure
13
.
