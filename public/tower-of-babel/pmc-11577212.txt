Effect of black soldier fly (Hermetia illucens) larvae meal and oil on the performance, biochemical profile, intestinal health and gut microbial dynamics in laying hens☆
Authors: Samiullah Khan, Xiaoli Shi, Renlian Cai, Zhao Shuai, Wei Mao, Ibrar Muhammad Khan, Ayman A Swelum, Jianjun Guo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigated the effect of incorporating black soldier fly (BSF) larvae meal and oil on laying hens' performance, egg quality, serum profile, intestinal structure, and gut health. A total of 378 Lohmann laying hens (age 48 wk) were randomly assigned to 6 treatments with 3 replicates of 21 hens each. Following 7 d acclimation, the trail was conducted for 8 weeks. The dietary groups include: basal corn-soybean meal diet (S) without BSF (BSO) oil (S+BSO 0), S with BSF oil (S+BSO 100), BSF meal (9 %) without BSF oil (BSF 9+BSO 0), BSF meal (9 %) with BSF oil (BSF 9+BSO 100), BSF meal (18 %) without BSF oil (BSF 18+BSO 0), and BSF meal (18 %) with BSF oil (BSF 18+BSO 100). The results showed that the BSF 18 + BSO 100 diet significantly reduced egg weight (P < 0.001) compared to other dietary treatments. The addition of BSF meal reduced feed intake (P < 0.001) and the Haugh units (P < 0.05) in hens fed 18 % BSF meal with and without BSO. The jejunum villus area, crypt depth, and intestinal wall thickness increased with the increase in the inclusion of BSF larvae meal (P < 0.001). The ileum villus height, crypt depth and intestinal wall thickness increased (P < 0.001) at 9 % BSF meal and then decreased at 18 % BSF meal with and without BSF oil. The bacteria genera Ruminococcus, Clostridiales, Bacteroidales, Ruminococcus torques, and Intestinimonas were positively associated with the dietary treatments, while Prevotellaceae UCG-001, Clostridium, and Faecalibacterium were negatively associated with the dietary treatments. The inclusion of BSF meal and oil enriched the functional network of several pathways, including ascorbate and aldarate metabolism, D-arginine and D-ornithine metabolism, and fatty acid metabolism, highlighting the positive effects of BSF larvae meal and oil on the chicken gut microbiota. In conclusion, BSF meal at 9 % with BSF oil and BSF meal at 18 % without BSF oil can be incorporated into the diet without impairing the performance and gut health of laying hens.Keywords: Black soldier fly larvae, Performance, Intestinal structure, Gut microbiota, Laying hen

Introduction

Soybean meal and oil are important sources of protein and fat in poultry nutrition worldwide. The negative environmental impacts, including deforestation, excessive water use, and significant reliance on soybean meal imports, have led to the exploration of alternative protein-rich sources for sustainable poultry production (Hartinger et al., 2022). One potential approach to enhance poultry farming efficiency involves the use of black soldier fly (BSF) larvae meal as a substitute for soybean meal and oil in poultry feed (Ewald et al., 2020; de Souza Vilela et al., 2021). BSF larvae, due to its high crude protein content (58–67 %) and essential amino acids (valine, 2.8 %; arginine, 2.0 %; methionine, 0.9 %), have emerged as a valuable alternative ingredient for feeding monogastric animals (Dabbou et al., 2018; de Souza Vilela et al., 2021). In addition, the abundant amount of lauric acid present in the BSF larvae has beneficial effect in terms of reducing pathogenic bacteria (Fortuoso et al., 2019; Zulkifli et al., 2022). Furthermore, the chitin in BSF larvae has immunomodulatory properties by modulating the immune system of food animals (Mwaniki et al., 2020; Hartinger et al., 2021).

Previous studies have shown no detrimental effects when substituting BSF larvae at 15 % on the performance of laying hens (Zawisza et al., 2023). The complete or partial substitution of BSF larvae meal did not reduce the laying hen performance (Maurer et al., 2016). Marono et al. (2017) observed a reduced body weight gain, egg production, and feed intake at 17 % incorporation of BSF larvae meal in a laying hen diet. Mwaniki et al. (2018) fed 0, 5 %, and 7.5 % BSF larvae meal to young hens aged 19 weeks and observed an adverse effect on hen day production at the 5 % inclusion. It is also crucial to assess the commercial potential of oil derived from BSF larvae to ensure its effective integration into poultry diets (Cullere et al., 2019; Heuel et al., 2022). The partial and complete replacement of soybean oil with BSF larvae fat showed no adverse effect on broiler performance, cecal microbiome, or metabolic health (Schäfer et al., 2023). In laying hens, the partial replacement of soybean meal with full-fat BSF larvae and the complete substitution of soybean oil with BSF oil did not have a negative effect on performance or egg quality (Patterson et al., 2021).

The animal gut microbiota with a diverse bacterial array play a central role in enhancing the physiological function and thus health and performance (Schäfer et al., 2023). The incorporation of BSF larvae meal in layer pullet diets resulted in an increased abundance of beneficial bacterial populations, including Bacteroides and Lactobacillus (Ndotono et al., 2022). Huang et al. (2024) observed an enhanced relative abundance of Ruminococcus torques, Parabacteroides, and Clostridia vadinBB60 group with increased inclusion of live BSF larvae in laying hens diet. The changes in the bacterial population may be associated to the chitin in BSF larvae's exoskeleton, which can be degraded by short-chain fatty acid-producing bacteria and used as a substrate by other microorganisms (Luparelli et al., 2022).

Despite the existence of several studies investigating the effects of BSF larvae meal and oil separately, to our knowledge, no studies in laying hens have assessed the intestinal histomorphology and microbial effects of incorporating both components simultaneously. This study aimed at including BSF larvae meal and oil in laying hens and assess their effect on the performance, intestinal consequences, and gut communities in the cecal digesta.

Material and methods

Birds, housing and experimental diets

The Animal Care and Use Committee of Guizhou University, Guiyang, China, approved all procedures involving animals in the experiment (No. EAE-GZU-2023-T095).

In total, 378 commercial Lohmann laying hens at 48 wk of age with uniform laying rate and body weight were divided into six dietary treatments. Each group consists of three replicates, with 21 birds in each replicate. The six dietary groups were as follow: soybean meal (S) without BSF oil, (S+BSO 0); S with BSF oil, (S+BSO 100); BSF larvae meal (9 %) without BSF oil, (BSF 9+BSO 0); BSF larvae meal (9 %) with BSF oil, (BSF 9+BSO 100); BSF larvae meal (18 %) without BSF oil, (BSF 18+BSO 0) and BSF larvae meal (18 %) with BSF oil, (BSF 18+BSO 100). Laying hens in the S+BSO 0 group were offered a basal diet of corn-soybean meal. BSF meal and oil were purchased from Sichuan Qianlang Ecological Agriculture Co., Ltd., Chengdu, China. The nutrient concentrations of the BSF meal used during the trail are shown in Supplementary Table 1. The fatty acid profile of BSF oil is provided in Table 1. Table 2 shows the ingredient compositions of all the dietary treatments. The trial lasted for nine weeks with one week of adaptation period.

Performance

The performance parameters, including individual egg weight and egg production were recorded daily, and feed intake was recorded weekly. To identify possible variations in the early and later phases of the experimental trail, these data were used to calculate the average egg weight (AEW), laying rate, average daily feed intake (ADFI), and feed conversion ratio (FCR) at the stages of 48 to 52 wk, 52 to 56 wk, and 48 to 56 wk. To observe the effects of dietary treatments on laying hen performance, these data were also analyzed on a weekly basis.

Egg quality characteristics

At the end of experiment, 30 eggs from each treatment (n = 10 eggs per replicate) were selected for the assessment of egg quality characteristics. An electronic Vernier caliper DL3944 (Deli, Ningbo, Zhejiang, China) was used to measure length and width of all these collected eggs. The DET-6000 egg quality tester (NABEL, Kyoto, Japan) was used to measure the eggshell strength. The eggshell thickness, calculated as the average of the top end, lower end, and middle, was measured using the ETG-5200 eggshell thickness gauge (Robotmation, Tokyo, Japan). The Haugh units and albumen height were quantified using an automatic egg quality analyzer (Robotmation, Tokyo, Japan). The egg yolk weight was measured with an electronic balance with BL-320H (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan).

Serum biochemical profile

At the end of the experiment, blood samples (n = 6 samples per treatment) were collected from the wing vein into 5 mL tubes following the wing vein by selecting one hen from each replicate. These blood samples were then centrifuged at 3000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the serum was stored at -20 °C. The parameters including amylase (AM), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), azelaic transaminase (AST), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), total protein (TP), albumen (ALB), and triglycerides (TC) were quantified by a BS-240 Vet chemistry analyzer (Mindray, Shenzhen, Guangdong, China).

Intestinal histomorphology

The intestinal sections, including the jejunum and ileum (n = 3 samples per treatment for each section), were treated with 4 % paraformaldehyde (Wuhan Service Technology, Wuhan, Hubei, China), trimmed, and embedded in paraffin wax. For morphometric analysis, samples of ileum and jejunum were stained with haematoxylin and eosin. The slides were examined under a microscope at 40X combined magnification with a Nikon Eclipse Ci-L (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan), and Image-Pro Plus 6.0-analysis software (Media Cybernetics, Silver Spring, MD) was used to generate the results. The parameters analyzed for the ileum and jejunum include villus height, villus width, crypt depth, mucosa, muscle thickness, and intestinal wall thickness.

Gut sample collection and 16S rRNA sequencing

The cecal contents (n = 3 samples per treatment) were taken immediately in a sterile tube and stored in liquid nitrogen before being transferred to -80°C for 16s RNA sequencing. The library preparation and sequencing were performed (LC Bio Technology, Hangzhou, Zhejiang, China) and total DNA of the microbiome was extracted from each sample using the CTAB. V3-V4 fragments were amplified using primers: 341F (5′-CCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG-3′) 805R (5′-GACTACHVGGGTATCTAATCC-3′). The Illumina HiSeq PE250 platform was employed to purify DNA for sequencing (Mingke Biotechnology, Hangzhou, Zhejiang, China). Subsequently, the obtained samples were subjected to sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq platform.

Unique barcodes were utilized to allocate pair end readings to their corresponding samples, which were abbreviated by the barcode and primer sequence. The high quality clean tags were obtained after filtering the raw reads according to the fqtrim (v0.94) technique. By employing the Vsearch software (v2.3.4), the chimeric sequences were filtered. Using DADA2 after conducting dereplication, a feature table and sequence were obtained. For ensuring the accuracy, the alpha as well as beta diversity were attained through normalizing to the similar sequence. The SILVA (release 138) classifier was employed to normalize feature abundance, taking into account the relative abundance of each sample. Alpha diversity was utilized to assess the complexity of species diversity including Chao1, observed species, Goods coverage, Shannon, and Simpson, which were calculated using QIIME2. The QIIME2 software was employed to compute the beta diversity, with the R package being used to generate the graphs. The blast algorithm was employed to align the sequences, with the SILVA database being used to annotate the feature sequences for each representative sequence.

Statistical analysis

The performance, egg quality, serum analysis, and intestinal measurements were analyzed using a one-way ANOVA through SPSS 18.0 statistical software. In cases where ANOVA revealed significant differences, the Tukey HSD test was used to separate between treatment means. Data were presented as means of three replicate samples, followed by their pooled standard errors (SEM). P value less than 0.05 indicates a significant difference, denoted by different lowercase letters. The AEW, laying rate, ADFI and FCR were compared weekly using the GraphPad Prism v.7 programme. All bar charts and box plots for the egg quality, serum analysis and intestinal histomorphology were generated in RStudio (v1.1.453, RStudio, Inc) with the 'ggplot2′ package. The gut microbiota was analyzed using the OmicStudio tools (https://www.omicstudio.cn/tool).

Result

Performance of laying hens fed BSF larvae meal and oil

The performance metrics for laying hens, including AEW, laying rate, ADFI, and FCR, fed a diet including BSF meal and oil for 8 weeks, are presented in Fig. 1 and Table 3. The individual weekly data for each performance measure is also provided in Supplementary Tables 2 and 3. A significant reduction (P < 0.001) in AEW was recorded in hens fed at 18 % BSF meal with BSF oil compared to the control basal-diet during wk 48 to 56. There were no significant differences (P > 0.05) in AEW of hens fed at 9 % inclusion of BSF with or without BSF oil throughout the entire experimental period. During 48 to 56 wk, laying hens fed a diet containing 18 % BSF meal without BSF oil showed a reduced laying rate (P < 0.05) compared to the control basal-diet. The hens receiving an 18 % BSF meal, both with and without BSF oil, exhibited a significant reduction in feed consumption (P < 0.001) during the entire experimental period.

Intestinal morphology of laying hens fed BSF larvae meal and oil

The jejunum exhibited an increase (P < 0.001) in villus height, villus width, crypt depth, mucosa, muscle thickness, and intestinal wall thickness (Fig. 3). A decreased muscle thickness was observed for the BSF meal with oil (BSF 9+BSO 100 and BSF 18+BSO 100) when compared with the soybean meal with BSF oil (S+BSO 100). No differences for the jejunum villus area and crypt depth were observed among the dietary treatments (P > 0.05).

Gut microbial dynamics of laying hens fed BSF larvae meal and oil

A total of 1,532,652 raw readings were collected from the samples, yielding 1,272,910 effective reads. The sequences found during the study of microbial ASVs from cecum samples were dispersed throughout 823 ASVs. Alpha diversity was assessed using the QIIME software.

Diversity of microbial community in laying hens

Improved mean values for Chao1 and Shannon index observed in chicken fed with BSF larvae meal inclusion (Table 4). The results demonstrated the following values for the Chao1 index: S+BSO 0 (1529.84 ± 156.99), S+BSO 100 (1569.6 ± 88.20), BSF 9+BSO 0 (1594.82 ± 56.29), BSF 9+BSO 100 (1589.14 ± 135.47), BSF 18+BSO 0 (1567.25 ± 149.06) and BSF 18+BSO 100 (1583.08 ± 165.46). The mean values for Shannon index were S+BSO 0 (9.11 ± 0.10), S+BSO 100 (9.17 ± 0.15), BSF 9+BSO 0 (9.06 ± 0.24), BSF 9+BSO 100 (9.21 ± 0.16), BSF 18+BSO 0 (8.95 ± 0.18), BSF 18+BSO 100 (9.08 ± 0.17). According to the principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) analysis, there were significant differences in gut microbiota composition between the experimental groups and the S+BSO 0 group (Fig. 5A; Supplementary Fig. 1). The Bray-Curtis distance matrix-based (PCoA) plot revealed that beta diversity was influenced by the inclusion of BSF larvae meal and oil in the dietary treatment.

Composition of gut microbiota at taxonomic levels

As illustrated in Fig. 5B, the dominant phyla were Firmicutes, Bacteroidota, Actinobacteriota, and Proteobacteria in all the dietary treatments. However, the Rikenellaceae RC9 gut group, Bacteroidales, Clostridiales, Ruminococcaceae, Bacteroides, and Faecalibacterium were dominant at the genus level (Fig. 5C). Population analysis showed the presence of the abundance of the same genera and phyla among the dietary groups, but their relative abundance varied (Supplementary Figs. 2 and 3).

At the genus level, the incorporation of BSF larvae and oil led to an increased abundance of the Rikenellaceae RC9 gut group, Ruminococcus torques group, Bacteroidales, and Clostridia vadinBB60 group; however, these changes were not statistically significant. In a similar manner, the abundance of Clostridium, Faecalibacterium, and Prevotellaceae UCG-001 at the genera level showed a significant decrease (P < 0.005) with the inclusion of BSF meal and oil (Fig. 6).

Linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEFSe)

The Kruskal-Wallis rank sum test was utilized in this study to identify characteristic species that exhibited differences in abundance across distinct treatments (Fig. 8A, Fig. 8B). The Wilcoxon rank-sum test applied to ascertain whether the subspecies of these species exhibited convergence towards a uniform taxonomic level, followed by linear discriminant analysis (LDA) for finalizing the selection of biomarker species. A cladogram produced from the LEfSe analysis displayed differentially abundant taxa enriched in the microbiota between experimental treatments. The findings indicated that the composition and population structure of the cecal microbiota were altered by BSF meal, both with and without BSF oil treatment.

Functional prediction of gut microbiota

The effects of the BSF larvae meal and oil on gut microbial metabolic pathways were assessed by performing functional predictions analysis PICRUSt2 (Phylogenetic Investigation of Communities by Reconstruction of Unobserved States) using the KEGG orthologues (level 3 KEGG pathway) database. Functional metabolic pathways demonstrated an increase in flavone and flavonol biosynthesis, as well as D-arginine and D-ornithine metabolism, when comparing the BSF 9+BSO 0 and BSF 18+BSO 0 groups to the S+BSO 0 group. Enhanced ascorbate and aldarate metabolism, along with increased penicillin and cephalosporin biosynthesis, were noted in the BSF 9+BSO 100 and BSF 18+BSO groups relative to the S+BSO 100 group. The S+BSO 100 group exhibited an increase in steroid hormone biosynthesis, geraniol degradation, inositol phosphate metabolism, fatty acid metabolism, and peroxisome activity compared to the S+BSO 100 group (Fig. 9).

The BSF dietary treatments, in comparison to the S+ BSO 0 group, demonstrated a significant enhancement in the superpathways of the microbiota in laying hens. These includes superpathway of L-threonine biosynthesis, superpathway of demethylmenaquinol-6 biosynthesis I, creatinine degradation I, superpathway of demethylmenaquinol-9 biosynthesis, and superpathway of histidine, purine, and pyrimidine biosynthesis. In the functional prediction data, the significant enrichment of metabolic pathways, such as L-threonine biosynthesis, superpathways of menaquinol-10, and teichoic acid biosynthesis, indicates that incorporating BSF meal and oil improved gut functions (Fig. 10).

Discussion

The performance parameters were not affected either by adding BSF meal at 9 % with and without BSF oil or BSF meal at 18 % without BSF oil in the present study. Surprisingly, the BSF meal at 9 % both with and without BSF oil improves the AEW of laying hens. The incorporation of BSF larvae meal as a protein source is consistent with the recent findings, which suggest that including 5–15 % BSF larvae meal resulted in comparable or even increased animal performance (Zawisza et al., 2023). The current study resulted in a decreased egg weight and feed intake with BSF larvae meal inclusion at 18 % into the laying hen diet. Morano et al. (2017) observed a decrease in egg weight and feed intake following the complete replacement of soybean meal with BSF larvae meal. Similarly, 8 and 16 % BSF larvae meal incorporation in laying hen feed showed no adverse effect on the performance the complete replacement of soybean meal with BSF larvae meal reduced the feed intake and egg production of the chickens (Patterson et al., 2021). The variations in performance may be attributed to factors such as the age and breed of the bird, the duration of the experimental trial, and the substrate utilized for rearing the BSF larvae (Khan et al., 2024).

The egg quality traits in the current research such as eggshell thickness, eggshell strength, albumen height, egg yolk weight were resistant against the exchange of the BSF meal and oil. This is crucial for the implementation of BSF meal and oil in feeding practice. The albumen height, eggshell thickness and eggshell strength showed no differences among the dietary treatments, in line with the previous studies (Heual et al., 2021; Patterson et al., 2021). Additionally, the dietary treatments did not effect the yolk weight of laying hens in the current study. The high levels of fatty acids in BSF meal and oil, including myristic and palmitic acid, might be transferred to the yolk; however, they did not influence yolk weight (Heuel et al., 2021b). The present study indicated that the inclusion of BSF larvae meal and oil enhanced eggshell thickness, although no significant differences were observed among the dietary treatments. Mwaniki et al. (2018) observed an increased eggshell thickness following the incorporation of 5 to 7.5 % BSF larvae meal into the laying hen diet. The fermentation of chitin in the hindgut may have led to an increase in calcium absorption, which subsequently caused an increase of eggshell thickness (Park et al., 2021).

Blood biochemistry is a crucial factor in assessing the physiological condition of animal health. The findings of this study indicated no significant differences in serum parameters, including AM, ALT, AST, ALP, TP, ALB, and TC, across the various dietary treatments. Previous studies showed no effect on the blood biochemical indices of laying hens fed defatted BSF larvae meal (Zawisza et al., 2023). Khan et al. (2024) observed no differences in serum parameters; however, the AM level increased and then subsequently decreased with the increased inclusion of BSF larvae meal. In our study, the AM levels also increased at 9 % BSF meal with BSF oil followed by a decreased AM levels, however, these differences were not significant. The abundant amount of lauric acid and chitin present in the BSF larvae may facilitated the breakdown of starch by enhancing the α-amylase activity in ruminants (Borland et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024). The drop in AM levels recorded in our research might be related to decrease feed intake resulting from the fibrous nature of BSF meal, resulting in a lower demand for the initiation of enzyme production (Khan et al., 2024).

The intestinal tracts of poultry consist of complex, diverse, and dynamic microbial communities that significantly influence host health and production performance (He et al., 2021). The current study showed notable alterations during the histomorphological analysis of the laying hen jejunum and ileum fed BSF larvae meal. The jejunum measurements except villus width exhibited a positive relation with BSF larvae inclusion. An upward trend was observed in the villus height, crypt depth, and mucosa at 9 % BSF larvae with and without oil, followed by a decreased trend in chicken fed 18 % BSF meal with and with BSF oil treatments. Previous studies demonstrated that the inclusion of BSF larvae calcium salt in the diets of laying hens improved jejunal villus height, villus width, and crypt depth (Chen et al., 2022; Al Anas et al., 2024), consistent with the findings of the current study. In contrast, there were no alterations in the histomorphology observed with BSF oil supplementation (Schiavone et al., 2018; Spranghers et al., 2018). Similarly, BSF meal did not showed any effect on intestinal morphology, including villus height and crypt depth (Chu et al., 2020).

The ileum parameters including villus area and muscle thickness increased with the BSF meal inclusion, however, the villus height, crypt depth increased and then decreased at 18 % BSF larvae meal incorporation with and without BSF oil. Cutrignelli et al. (2018) also showed a decreased villus height of ileum during the complete replacement of soybean meal with the 17 % BSF larvae meal in the chicken diet. The anti-inflammatory properties of BSF larvae may be responsible for the observed changes in intestinal shape, which in turn affect the hen's gut microbiota. The bioactive substances found in BSF larvae have anti-inflammatory qualities and support a healthy gut microbiome. Furthermore, components derived from BSF promote growth factors and increase barrier function, which together improve intestinal shape and health. The observed pattern indicates the potential for elevated cellular activity within the intestinal mucosa region, specifically in vicinity of the villi. This activity serves to facilitate the renewal of the villi, which in turn enhances nutrient absorption (Donaldson et al., 2021). Birds fed lauric acid showed improved immune response, increased productivity and better digestive tract function, including villus expansion and barrier function (Wu et al., 2021).

The evaluation of microbial community diversity utilizing phylogenetic diversity indexes, Chao1, Simpson, and Shannon observed amplicon sequence variants provides an in-depth analysis that effectively identifies both the evenness and richness dimensions (Biasato et al., 2023). Despite the complexity and dynamic nature of chicken digestive tracts, these communities are vital to both the chicken's health and production performance (Yadav and Jha, 2019). The current showed an improved Chao 1 as well as Shannon index with the inclusion of BSF larvae meal and oil. Ndotono et al. (2022) reported an increase alpha diversity by incorporating 10 % BSF larvae meal into the diet of Pullets. Similarly, the complete replacement of soybean meal with the defatted meal of BSF larvae at 17 % showed an increased Shannon index (Borrelli et al., 2017).

The current findings demonstrate a positive association among several bacterial genera, including Ruminococcaceae, Clostridiales, Bacteroidales, Ruminococcus torques, Intestinimonas, and the Olsenella group, in relation to the incorporation of BSF larvae meal and oil. Conversely, Lactobacillus, Faecalibacterium, Lachnospiraceae, and Clostridium exhibited a negative relationship with BSF meal and oil. Ruminococcaceae play a crucial role in the degradation of various polysaccharides and fibers, leading to the production of short-chain fatty acids that support intestinal health (Lv et al., 2022). In line with current findings, incorporating live BSF larvae meal into the laying hen diet increased the abundance of Ruminococcus torques (Huang et al., 2024). Lactobacillus, which decreased in our study, is in line with the previous studies (Józefiak et al., 2018; Huang et al., 2024). Józefiak et al. (2018) observed that even a small amount of 0.05 to 0.2 % of the BSF meal inclusion in broiler diet could reduce the abundance of the Lactobacillus. Similar results were also obtained with reducing Lactobacillus abundance in laying hen fed live BSF larvae meal (Huang et al., 2024). Regarding modulation of the gut microbiota, we observed a gradual increase in relative abundance of Ruminococcus torques group, Oscillospiraceae, UCG-005, and Clostridia vadinBB60 group with increased provision of BSF larvae. Previous research showed similar findings by the gradual increase in the abundance of these bacteria with the inclusion of live BSF larvae in the diet of laying hens (Huang et al., 2024). Studies showed that these bacteria are commonly associated with immune regulation of the animal (Liu et al., 2021; Dehau et al., 2023).

Functional metabolic pathways showed an increased flavone and flavonol biosynthesis, D-arginine and D-ornithine metabolism. D-Arginine and D-ornithine metabolism is a module of D-Amino acid metabolism. Bacitracin and putrescine are the two key products from this module. Bacitracin is a natural polypeptide antibiotic that inhibits the peptidoglycan production of Gram-positive microbes. The production of flavones and flavonols can produce a range of flavonoids, which have a variety of biological characteristics including anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial (Hu et al., 2022). Creatinine degradation I, super pathway of demethylmenaquinol-9 biosynthesis, and superpathway of histidine, purine, and pyrimidine biosynthesis. In the functional prediction data, the significant enrichment of metabolic pathways, such as L-threonine biosynthesis, superpathways of menaquinol-10, and teichoic acid biosynthesis, indicates that black soldier fly meal supplementation improved gut functions. These pathways ensures the integrity of bacterial membrane that collectively contributing to enhanced chicken gut.

Conclusion

This study confirms that the performance of laying hens can be improved by partial replacement of BSF meal with and without complete replacement of soybean oil in the diet of laying hens. The inclusion of BSF larvae meal at 9 % with and without BSF oil slightly increased the production performance; however, major replacement of SBM with BSF meal (18 %) resulted in reduced egg weight and egg width. Enhanced chao1 and Shannon and certain bacterial population genera Ruminococcus, Clostridiales, Bacteroidales, Ruminococcus torques group, and Olsenella group were positively associated with BSF meal and oil inclusion, while Lactobacillus, Faecalibacterium, Lachnospiraceae and Clostridium were negatively associated with increased BSF meal and oil in laying chicken feed. Functional prediction enriched metabolic pathways, such as ascorbate and aldarate metabolism, super pathways of L-threonine metabolism, flavone and flavonol biosynthesis, D-arginine and D-ornithine metabolism, steroid hormones biosynthesis, fatty acid metabolism, highlights the beneficial effects of BSF larvae meal and oil on the chicken gut microbiota. In conclusion, BSF larvae meal at 9 %, both with and without BSF oil, as well as BSF meal at 18 % without BSF oil can be included into the diet of laying hens without any adverse effect on performance.
