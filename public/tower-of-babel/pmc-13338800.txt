Deciphering Physicochemical Properties of Urinary Proteins from Stone Formers as Macromolecular Prolithogenic Factors to Drive Calcium Oxalate Stone Development
Authors: Sudarat Hadpech, Paleerath Peerapen, Visith Thongboonkerd
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A recent study has identified several urinary proteins from calcium oxalate (CaOx) stone formers (patients with CaOx kidney stones) that promote stone development. Nevertheless, the physicochemical properties responsible for their prolithogenic effects remained undefined. In this study, stone formers’ urinary proteins were fractionated into 15 fractions (SF1-SF15) using chromatographic methods and analyzed through a series of CaOx crystal assays, followed by protein identification via tandem mass spectrometry. Physicochemical properties of all proteins identified in individual fractions, along with their crystal-promoting activities, were then analyzed and subjected to the Pearson correlation test. The majority of protein fractions exhibited crystal-promoting effects. Crystallization-promoting activity positively correlated with the proportion of oxalate-binding proteins, while aggregation-promoting activity positively correlated with the number of oxalate-binding sites/protein and isoelectric point (pI). Crystal adhesion-promoting activity negatively correlated with the number of oxalate-binding sites/protein, pI, proportion of basic proteins, and proportion of alpha-helix structure but positively correlated with the proportion of acidic proteins and proportion of random-coil structure. None of the physicochemical properties correlated with crystal growth-promoting activity. In conclusion, some physicochemical properties of urinary proteins from stone formers are associated with their CaOx stone-promoting activities and may serve as predictors for their prolithogenic potential.

Introduction

Protein properties may be determined by its amino acid sequence, which dictates how a protein folds into its three-dimensional structure. The arrangement of different amino acid types, such as hydrophobic, hydrophilic, and acidic and basic amino acids, within the polypeptide chain is critical for governing protein function and stability. Therefore, any changes in amino acid sequence, such as mutations and modifications, can alter protein folding, resulting in impaired, aberrant, or new protein function as often observed in viral proteins bound to receptors. − Upon protein folding, surface properties, such as charge and hydrophobicity, become key determinants of how interactions occur between a protein and other molecules, including its partners, substrates, inhibitors, and receptors as well as ions. ,− Misfolded proteins, however, often exhibit functional alterations and form toxic aggregates. ,

Urine contains a diverse array of plasma proteins filtrated through the glomeruli and proteins secreted from cells within the renal and urinary tract. Alterations in their levels can reflect pathological conditions in various organ systems. , Kidney stone development is an intricate biomineralization process that involves mineral crystallization and interactions among urinary macromolecules (i.e., proteins), ions, and crystal surfaces, all of which can be incorporated into the stone matrix. − Stone patients (stone formers) often have abnormal levels of key urinary proteins due to genetic predispositions or other acquired conditions. , For example, reduced excretion of crystal-inhibitory proteins or increases in crystal-promoting proteins can tip the balance toward stone development. − Although many proteins in stone formers’ urine have been identified by proteomics, , their functional characteristics and mechanistic roles in modulating stone formation remain largely unexplored.

A recent study has highlighted the inhibitory effects of proteins derived from the urine of healthy (nonstone) individuals against calcium oxalate (CaOx) stone formation. A subsequent study has further uncovered that several physicochemical properties of urinary proteins are critical determinants of their inhibitory roles. In light of these findings, our recent research has identified several urinary proteins from stone formers’ urine that may exert promoting activities to enhance CaOx stone development. Nonetheless, the physicochemical properties responsible for their prolithogenic effects remained undefined. Herein, we expanded the list of urinary proteins excreted by stone formers. The complexity of whole urinary proteins was reduced by fractionation using diethylaminoethyl anion exchange followed by size-exclusion chromatography (SEC). Urinary proteins present in all 15 fractions were identified by tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS). Additionally, physicochemical properties of urinary proteins from stone formers’ urine that determined their stone-promoting activities were elucidated. Also, a series of CaOx crystal assays were done more systematically to obtain the degree of CaOx crystal-promoting activities of each fraction in individual assays to define the correlation between the degree of crystal-promoting activities and individual physicochemical properties.

Results

Degrees of Crystal-Promoting Activities of Individual Urinary Protein Fractions

The schematic summary of all methodological approaches is provided in Figure . First, urine samples collected from 30 stone formers were pooled and subjected to protein isolation and fractionation via diethylaminoethyl anion exchange, followed by SEC. All 15 protein fractions, designated as SF1-SF15, were then evaluated for their modulatory effects on CaOx stone development using four distinct crystal assays. Degrees of crystal-promoting activities of individual urinary protein fractions in each assay were relative to crystal parameters under the control condition (see Supporting Information Methods). The positive value denotes the stone-promoting activity, while the negative one reflects an inhibitory effect.

Most of these protein fractions had crystallization-promoting activities, except SF4, SF8, and SF9, which showed negative effects (Figure A). Similarly, almost all of the protein fractions demonstrated crystal growth-promoting activities, except SF5 with a minimal degree of inhibitory activity (Figure B). Nine out of 15 fractions, including SF1, SF2, SF7, and SF10-SF15, exerted crystal aggregation-promoting activities, while the rest had negative effects (Figure C). Interestingly, all 15 fractions promoted crystal adhesion without an inhibitory effect observed (Figure D). Overall, these crystal assays revealed that urinary proteins from CaOx stone formers predominantly exerted CaOx crystal-promoting activities, thereby serving mainly as pro-lithogenic factors to enhance CaOx stone development.

Protein Identification by Tandem Mass Spectrometry

Proteomics was employed for the protein identification in each protein fraction. nanoLC-ESI-Qq-TOF MS/MS successfully identified 23, 17, 19, 12, 30, 36, 29, 39, 3, 39, 99, 3, 17, 6, and 25 in SF1-SF15 fractions, respectively (Supporting Information Table S1).

Physicochemical Properties of Proteins Found in Each Fraction

Protein properties were examined to infer their modulatory activities and various degrees of crystal modulation observed in individual crystal assays. Crystal formation is an intricate sequential process, in which calcium and oxalate ions play crucial roles. Thus, calcium- and oxalate-binding sites of the proteins found in each fraction were first analyzed. The results showed that <25% of proteins in almost all fractions were calcium-binding proteins, except SF12, in which 33% calcium-binding proteins were found (Figure A). None of the urinary proteins in SF9 and SF14 were identified as calcium-binding proteins (Figure A), resulting in no number of calcium-binding domains being reported for these fractions (Figure B). For other fractions, the number of calcium-binding domains found in each protein was ≤8 (with a maximum in SF5). Note that although there were some calcium-binding proteins detected in SF2 and SF12, the information on specific calcium-binding domains and their number was not available for these proteins in the database (Figure B). In contrast to calcium-binding proteins, >60% of urinary proteins found in all 15 fractions were oxalate-binding proteins (Figure C). Interestingly, 100% of proteins found in fractions SF4, SF12, and SF14 were oxalate-binding proteins (Figure C). Furthermore, at least one oxalate-binding sites/protein was detected in all the protein fractions with a maximum found in SF12 (Figure D).

To gain insights into additional protein properties that might be influential for their promoting roles in stone development, other various parameters including isoelectric point (pI), instability index, hydropathy (GRAVY score), residual components, and secondary structures were also analyzed. Analyses revealed that average pI values of proteins identified across 15 fractions ranged from 5 to 8 (Figure A). Proportions of acidic proteins (pI < 7) and basic proteins (pI > 7) in each fraction were determined. The observed pI values corresponded to the relative distribution of acidic and basic proteins within each fraction. Fractions SF3, SF6, SF7, and SF14 exhibited high proportions of acidic proteins (more than 80%), while SF9 and SF12 were predominated by basic proteins (more than 60%) (Figure B).

Given that the pI can influence protein structural stability, proteins in each fraction were assessed for their instability and stability. Proteins exhibiting an instability index of <40 were considered stable, whereas those with an index of >40 were considered unstable. The results demonstrated that all 15 fractions contained proteins with an average instability index of >40, suggesting that these proteins were mainly unstable (Figure C). The proportion of stable versus unstable proteins in each fraction confirmed that unstable proteins predominated, comprising >60% of proteins found in each fraction (Figure D).

Hydropathy, which can be assessed by the GRAVY score, was another protein property examined herein. Notably, only fraction SF12 exhibited a positive average GRAVY score, while the other 14 fractions had negative average scores, ranging from −0.3 to −0.5 (Figure E). The GRAVY score was also used to determine hydrophilic (GRAVY < 0) and hydrophobic (GRAVY > 0) proteins in each fraction. The data showed that hydrophilic proteins were predominant in almost all fractions, except SF12, in which hydrophobic proteins were predominant (Figure F).

Amino acid profiles of proteins constituted in each fraction were subsequently analyzed. Aromatic amino acids were present in all 15 fractions, ranging from 5% to 8% (Figure A). Polar amino acids constituted 25–29% (Figure B), while nonpolar amino acids constituted 38–49% (Figure C). Positively charged amino acids constituted 7–13% (Figure D), whereas negatively charged amino acids constituted 7–15% (Figure E).

Subsequently, four distinct conformational states were examined. The results showed that alpha-helix, extended-strand, beta-turn, and random-coil structures constituted approximately 21–55%, 5–15%, 2–4%, and 37–65%, respectively, of proteins found in all 15 fractions (Figure A–D).

Correlations between the Degree of Crystal-Promoting Activities and Individual Physicochemical Properties

All aforementioned physicochemical properties of proteins in each fraction were subjected to correlation analyses with the degree of crystal-promoting activities in the corresponding fraction. All of the data obtained from the Pearson correlation test are summarized in Table . From these, only significant correlations are depicted in Figure . Crystallization-promoting activity positively correlated with the proportion of oxalate-binding proteins (Figure A), while aggregation-promoting activity positively correlated with the number of oxalate-binding sites/protein (Figure B) and pI (Figure C). Crystal adhesion-promoting activity negatively correlated with the number of oxalate-binding sites/protein (Figure D), pI (Figure E), proportion of basic proteins (Figure G), and proportion of alpha-helix structure (Figure H), but it was positively correlated with the proportion of acidic proteins (Figure F) and proportion of random-coil structure (Figure I). However, none of the physicochemical properties correlated with crystal growth-promoting activity of the proteins.

Discussion

Urolithiasis results from the formation of crystalline materials in the urinary system and is influenced by both genetic and environmental factors. − Among various organic and inorganic components in the urine, proteins play pivotal roles in modulating CaOx stone development processes. ,, The urinary proteome consists of a wide array of proteins, some of which function as inhibitors of CaOx stones, while others promote their development under pathological conditions. − , In stone formers, some urine proteins undergo physicochemical alterations that may shift the balance toward an environment conducive to stone development. , One of the most critical aspects of this phenomenon is the structural and chemical properties of urinary proteins, such as their folding, post-translational modifications, charge state, hydropathy, and ability to interact with calcium and oxalate ions. − Additionally, protein aggregation not only expands their sizes to larger complexes but also can create nucleation sites or scaffolds that promote crystallization. , Moreover, the loss of activities of key inhibitory proteins by compositional or conformational changes can shift the balance of an environment to favor stone development. ,

This study characterized the physicochemical properties of urinary proteins from stone formers to identify key features involved in their promoting effects on CaOx stone development. Urinary proteins were fractionated using diethylaminoethyl anion exchange and size-exclusion chromatography (SEC). Thereafter, CaOx crystal-modulatory activities of each protein fraction that reflected the combined activities of all protein constituents (not a single protein) within that fraction were examined. Pearson correlation analysis was then performed to find the correlation between the degree of crystal-promoting activities and individual physicochemical properties. The results demonstrated that the proportion of oxalate-binding proteins within each urinary protein fraction was a critical determinant for the CaOx crystallization-promoting activity of a protein fraction (Figure A). This finding is consistent with previous experimental evidence demonstrating that HSP90, an oxalate-binding protein, exhibits promoting activity in crystallization and other crystal assays. Our results also showed that the number of oxalate-binding sites/protein significantly correlated with the degree of crystal aggregation-promoting activity (Figure B). This finding is consistent with the data reported in a previous study on the crystal aggregation-promoting activity of fibronectin, which contains six oxalate-binding sites. This previous study has also demonstrated that fibronectin can bind to CaOx crystal surfaces and thus act as an adhesive bridge among individual crystals. Moreover, we found that the crystal aggregation-promoting activity correlated with the protein pI, which is generally based on the type and number of amino acids constituted in a protein. , The more basic the pI, the higher the crystal aggregation-promoting activity of the protein. This is in contrast to the urine pH, which negatively correlates with the adhesive ability of CaOx crystals to bind to renal cells (the more basic the pI, the lower the degree of crystal adhesion to the cells).

Crystal attachment to tubular cell surfaces is another critical step to initiate stone development. The adhesive force between crystals and cells is influenced by the physicochemical properties of both crystal surfaces and epithelial cell membranes. − However, urinary proteins can affect (enhance or reduce) the interaction between crystals and cells, most likely via crystal-protein binding prior to cell adhesion. , Herein, our findings showed an inverse correlation between the crystal adhesion-promoting activity and various physicochemical properties of the proteins, including the number of oxalate-binding sites/protein (Figure D), pI (Figure E), proportion of basic proteins (Figure G), and proportion of alpha-helix structure (Figure H). The binding between a protein and a crystal that leads to a decline of crystal-cell adhesion might be explained by the occupancy of crystal surfaces after crystal-protein binding, resulting in less available space on the crystal surfaces for cell adhesion. Although the precise mechanism underlying this observation needs further elucidation, a similar phenomenon has been reported in many previous studies revealing that coating CaOx crystals with calcineurin B, Tamm–Horsfall protein, or other various molecules can reduce crystal-cell adhesion. ,,,, On the other hand, binding of other groups of urinary proteins can enhance crystal-cell adhesion by the interaction between the crystal-bound protein and the cell surfaces that may express a specific receptor for such crystal-bound protein. Even with much fewer studies to support the latter phenomenon, binding of HSP90 on the crystal surfaces enhances crystal adhesion to the cells, which express surface receptors for HSP90. ,,

An inverse correlation between the crystal adhesion-promoting activity and protein pI was also observed (Figure E), consistent with the negative correlation between the adhesive ability of CaOx crystals to bind to renal cells and urine pH reported in a previous study. This might be from electrostatic interactions. While direct experimental evidence on the specific influence of protein pI in this context is limited, the role of pH and pI in modulating electrostatic forces is well-established. For example, a study on bovine serum albumin has demonstrated that alterations in buffer pH influence net charge, charge distribution, interfacial charged groups, and zeta potential. Proteins with lower pI values carry more negative charges than those with higher pI values. , Our results revealed that most protein fractions possessed pI values lower than the pH (7.4) used in the crystal assays (see Figure A and Supporting Information Methods), indicating that most of these urinary proteins were negatively charged. The negative charge enables electrostatic interactions with the positively charged calcium-rich crystal surfaces. The crystal–protein complex might enhance the crystal’s ability to adhere to cell surfaces via the proteins’ binding molecules on cell membranes.

On the other hand, basic proteins typically contain many basic amino acids like lysine and arginine. CaOx, especially its monohydrate form, has negatively charged locales on its surfaces due to the presence of oxalate. The positive charges of basic proteins allow them to bind to the negatively charged locales of the crystal. This binding can mask other reactive calcium locales that normally bind to calcium-binding proteins or the negative charge-rich regions on cell membranes, , thereby reducing the crystal’s ability to interact with cell membranes.

In contrast to the mentioned inverse correlations, positive correlations were noted between the crystal adhesion-promoting activity and proportion of acidic proteins (Figure F) as well as proportion of random-coil structure (Figure I). At physiological pH, acidic proteins’ charges are typically negative due to their higher proportion of acidic amino acids, such as aspartic acid and glutamic acid. Our findings indicated that SF3, SF6, and SF7 fractions, which contained high proportions of acidic proteins (>80%), exhibited high crystal adhesion-promoting activities. These data suggested that negative charges of acidic proteins in these fractions critically facilitated their interactions with the calcium-rich surfaces on CaOx crystals, likely modifying the crystal surfaces in a manner that enhanced their ability to adhere to the cells. Note that biological systems are inherently multifunctional, and similar phenomena have been observed in protein-mediated interactions between breast cancer cells and bone apatite crystals. It is plausible that the crystal-cell adhesion is mediated, at least in part, by protein–protein interactions. Specifically, proteins adsorbed onto crystal surfaces may facilitate their binding to specific cellular receptors, particularly if these proteins serve as ligands for the receptors.

A random-coil is a type of protein secondary structure, in which the polypeptide chain lacks regular repeating conformation, such as alpha-helix or beta-sheet. A previous study has reported that the random-coil structure is involved in calcium-binding affinity. The random-coil structure found in stone formers’ urine in the present study significantly correlated with the degree of the crystal adhesion-promoting activity (Figure I). The promoting effect on crystal adhesion observed in this study might be due to the fact that proteins with random-coil structures are quite flexible for their interactions, , allowing them to bind to CaOx crystals and cell surfaces more effectively.

Collectively, physicochemical analyses of proteins in stone formers’ urine suggested that each protein property influenced CaOx stone development through multiple mechanisms. For example, the oxalate-binding property could affect crystallization (Figure A), crystal aggregation (Figure B), and crystal adhesion (Figure D). Other properties, such as protein pI (acidic or basic) (Figure C,E–G), and secondary structures (particularly alpha-helix and random-coil) (Figure H,I) could also determine CaOx crystal-modulatory activities of the stone formers’ urinary proteins. These findings underscore the multifaceted roles of proteins in the causation of urolithiasis. However, none of the physicochemical properties showed a significant correlation with the crystal growth-promoting activity. This observation implicates that the protein-mediated modulation of CaOx stone formation is driven more predominantly in some processes, such as nucleation, aggregation, and crystal-cell adhesion, rather than crystal growth kinetics.

As aforementioned, each fraction of the samples analyzed in this study comprised a complex mixture of urinary proteins. This approach remained challenging to attribute the observed CaOx stone-promoting activities to any single protein. Therefore, additional efforts were directed toward identifying key urinary proteins with potential CaOx stone-promoting activities by integrating the present findings with existing scientific evidence. To this end, a total of 13 proteins previously ranked as candidates for potential CaOx stone promoters from our recent study were further analyzed for their differential levels in stone formers’ urine vs normal urine. We expected that the valid stone promoters would have greater levels in the stone formers’ urine. As we initially anticipated, several lines of evidence have confirmed that all these candidate proteins in the list have greater levels in the stone formers’ urine compared with the normal urine (Table ). These candidates included alpha-1-acid glycoprotein 2, alpha-1-antitrypsin, apolipoprotein D, CD44 antigen, complement C4-B, endosialin, fibronectin, fibrinogen alpha chain, interleukin-18-binding protein, kallikrein-1, retinol-binding protein 4, titin, and vitamin D-binding protein. In addition, physicochemical properties of these key urinary proteins with potential CaOx stone-promoting activities were retrieved and matched to those with significant correlations with the degree of crystal-promoting activities (Figure ). The data revealed that each of these strong candidates had most of the physicochemical properties that significantly correlated with the degree of crystal-promoting activities (Table ), strengthening their potential roles as the CaOx stone promoters.

Again, major limitations of this study were from the inherent complexity and variability of urinary proteins that make it challenging to establish definitive causal relationships between specific protein properties and CaOx stone development. For instance, there were negative effects observed for a few fractions in Figure A–C and a contradictory GRAVY score of SF12 in Figure E, as compared with several other fractions, which consistently showed the same tendency. These contradictory results were unlikely to be attributed to a single protein or specific protein properties; rather, they reflected the net effects of a complex mixture of proteins and protein properties within each fraction. These fractions were likely enriched with some proteins possessing inhibitory functions that might counterbalance or override crystal-promoting activities. Given that each fraction comprised multiple proteins with potentially opposing roles, the observed outcomes represented the integrated effects of all constituent components. Therefore, extensive laboratory investigations using purified or recombinant urinary proteins are needed to address these causal relationships. Also, the dynamic nature of urinary protein excretion and proteins’ post-translational modifications and interactions within the urinary environment might not be fully captured by solely in vitro or ex vivo analyses. Moreover, while physicochemical analyses offered valuable insights, they might not be able to reflect intricate biological interactions that occur in vivo, including the influence of renal epithelial cells and other macromolecular and micromolecular mediators.

Also, it should be noted that the present study employed diethylaminoethyl anion exchange prior to SEC fractionation, which might lead to the loss of cationic urinary proteins. However, the anion exchange step was, in fact, helpful to purify urinary proteins to be ready for effective fractionation by SEC (as we did not implement other isolation, concentration, or desalting methods to purify urinary proteins before fractionation). More importantly, the vast majority of urinary proteins are anionic, with a tiny number of cationic proteins. , Therefore, the cationic protein loss, if any, would minimally affect our analyses, which targeted the vast majority of urinary proteins. Another limitation of this study was the absence of a healthy control group. Moreover, the investigations were performed entirely on the pooled urine, without consideration for interindividual variabilities, particularly those related to age and gender. However, the primary objective was not to perform comparative or subgroup analyses but rather to investigate the functional effects of urinary protein fractions derived from the pooled urine of stone formers (the pooling was essential to obtain a sufficient amount of total recovered proteins for all the crystal assays and protein identification via tandem mass spectrometry). Therefore, further elucidations of these CaOx stone-promoting urinary proteins in individual subjects and in stone formers compared with healthy (nonstone) controls and subgroup analyses with respect to their age and gender would solidify and increase the value of our findings.

Conclusions

This study extensively examined urinary proteins excreted by the CaOx stone formers. The proteins from pooled urine were fractionated into 15 fractions (SF1-SF15) using chromatographic methods and analyzed through a series of CaOx crystal assays, followed by protein identification via tandem mass spectrometry. Physicochemical properties of all proteins identified in individual fractions, along with their crystal-promoting activities, were then analyzed using the Pearson correlation test. The majority of protein fractions exhibited crystal-promoting effects. Crystallization-promoting activity positively correlated with the proportion of oxalate-binding proteins, while aggregation-promoting activity positively correlated with the number of oxalate-binding sites/protein and pI. Crystal adhesion-promoting activity negatively correlated with the number of oxalate-binding sites/protein, pI, proportion of basic proteins, and proportion of alpha-helix structure but positively correlated with the proportion of acidic proteins and proportion of random-coil structure. None of the physicochemical properties correlated with crystal growth-promoting activity. In conclusion, some physicochemical properties of urinary proteins from stone formers are associated with their CaOx stone-promoting activities and may serve as predictors for their prolithogenic potential.

Materials and Methods

Ethical Approval and Sample Collection

This study was approved by the institutional ethical review board with approval # Si415/2023. Random midstream urine samples were obtained from 30 patients with CaOx stones (34–70 years old) with signed consent forms. Then, 100 mL of urine from each was clarified by a brief centrifugation at 500g before pooling.

Urine Processing and Protein Fractionation by Diethylaminoethyl Anion Exchange Followed by Size-Exclusion Chromatography

The pooled urine sample was desalted by dialysis with deionized water and dried by lyophilization. The proteins were then dissolved in 10 mM Tris–HCl with 50 mM NaCl (pH 7.3). Fractionation of urinary proteins was performed by diethylaminoethyl anion exchange followed by SEC as described in detail in refs − and Supporting Information Methods.

Systematic Analyses of CaOx Crystal-Promoting Activities in SF1-SF15 Urinary Protein Fractions

A series of crystal assays employed in this study encompassed four key phases of kidney stone formation: (i) crystallization, (ii) crystal growth, (iii) aggregation, and (iv) crystal-cell adhesion. Methodologies for all these crystal assays have been described in detail in refs − and Supporting Information Methods. Degrees of CaOx crystal-promoting activities of individual protein fractions were calculated as detailed in Supporting Information Methods.

Identification of Urinary Proteins in Each Fraction by nanoLC-ESI-Qq-TOF MS/MS

See details in refs − and Supporting Information Methods.

Defining Physicochemical Properties of Urinary Proteins in Each Fraction

Various tools were employed to define the physicochemical properties of urinary proteins found in each fraction. Calcium- and oxalate-bindings are essential features of proteins for their interactions with calcium and oxalate in free forms and on the crystal surfaces. ,,,, Calcium-binding domains in individual proteins were identified via the UniProt (https://www.uniprot.org/) tool, whereas oxalate-binding sites were identified via the OxaBIND (https://stonemod.org/oxabind.php) tool. Other protein properties, such as isoelectric point (pI), stability, hydropathy (GRAVY score), and residual components, were analyzed via the ProtParam (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/) tool. Their secondary structures were investigated via the SOPMA (https://npsa-prabi.ibcp.fr/cgi-bin/npsa_automat.pl?page=/NPSA/npsa_sopma.html) tool.

Statistics and Correlation Analyses

Multiple comparisons were performed with ANOVA with Tukey’s posthoc test for normally distributed data; otherwise, Kruskal–Wallis tests were done. Correlations between the degree of crystal-promoting activities and individual physicochemical properties were assessed by Pearson correlation analysis. Statistical significance was defined as a p-value of <0.05.
