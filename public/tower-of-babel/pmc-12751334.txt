Advanced biometrical strategies for genetic analysis and heterosis assessment in maize germplasm
Authors: Muhammad Faheem Jan, Ming Li, Nahaa M Alotaibi, Waqas Liaqat, Asia Maqbool, Muhammad Tanveer Altaf, Saman Arshad, Aisha Umar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMaize (Zea mays L.) is a cornerstone of global agriculture, contributing significantly to food security and economic stability as one of the world’s most important cereal crops. Breeding programs enhancing maize productivity depend on strategically exploiting genetic variation and heterosis. This study employed biometrical approaches to analyze combining ability, heterosis, and heritability in 29 genotypes, including 9 parental lines and 20 F1 hybrids, developed through a Line × Tester mating scheme. Significant genetic variability was observed, with P4 (B73) and P1 (Zheng58) identified as superior combiners for nitrate reductase (NR) activity, glutamine synthetase (GS) activity, and grain yield. Testers P8 (Mo17) and P9 (PH4CV) exhibited strong combining abilities for NR activity, ear length, and grain yield, indicating the importance of parental selection. Additionally, hybrids P1 × P9 (Zheng58 × PH4CV) and P5 × P7 (PH6WC × 178) exhibited strong specific combining ability (SCA) effects, signifying both additive and non–additive gene actions in trait improvement. High mid–parent heterosis (MPH) and better–parent heterosis (BPH) were observed, with MPH ranging from 61.91% to 272.26% and BPH from 32.77% to 216.29% for grain yield, showing the potential for hybrid vigor. High heritability for grain yield, NR activity, and other traits suggests a strong genetic foundation for breeding. These findings highlight the integration of genetic variability, combining ability, and heterosis, optimizing hybrid performance and enhancing parental selection in future breeding programs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07782-2.Keywords: Maize breeding, Line × tester analysis, Nitrate reductase, Heritability, Hybrid performance, Parental line selection

Introduction

Maize (Zea mays L.) is one of the most significant staple crops worldwide, contributing nearly 37% of global cereal production in 2023 [1]. Its production has surged in recent decades due to rising demand, technological advancements, and yield improvements, positioning it as the leading cereal by production volume [2]. While the expansion of agricultural land has been modest (8% increase from 1961 to 2020), the global yield per unit area has risen by 134%, making maize one of the most cultivated crops [3]. In China, maize plays a pivotal role in food security, with Northeast China accounting for over 31% of the country’s maize production, particularly in Heilongjiang, which contributes 15.16% of the national yield (National Bureau of Statistics, 2023, https://www.stats.gov.cn/). This region’s temperate climate and vast arable land make it a key area for maize cultivation, with significant implications for global food security. Hybrid maize varieties dominate commercial production due to their superior traits, including higher yields and resilience under diverse environmental conditions [4]. Maize breeders utilized advanced breeding programs focused on hybridization to develop varieties that meet the increasing global demand. A core aspect of these breeding programs is understanding combining ability, heterosis, and heritability, which are key to selecting optimal inbred lines and parental combinations [5, 6]. Combining ability, particularly the concepts of general combining ability (GCA) and specific combining ability (SCA), is essential for predicting hybrid performance and ensuring the success of maize breeding programs [7]. These concepts help breeders identify parental lines with the potential to produce high–yielding hybrids, emphasizing the need to consider both additive and non–additive genetic effects [7–9].

Heterosis or hybrid vigor is a critical factor for improving maize yield and other important traits, such as stress tolerance and nitrogen use efficiency. It is influenced by genetic distance between parents, environmental conditions, and developmental stages [10, 11]. While heterosis in maize has been studied extensively, understanding its full potential remains a research challenge [12, 13]. Incorporating genetic variability, combining ability, and heterosis into breeding programs offers a comprehensive approach to enhancing hybrid performance and selecting superior parental lines. Genetic parameters like heritability and genetic advance play a vital role in guiding breeders to select traits for improvement and predict potential gains in successive generations [14, 15]. These estimates help identify traits that are genetically stable and amenable to selection, contributing to more efficient crop improvement strategies. The Line × Tester mating design, first proposed by [16], has proven effective for assessing GCA and SCA effects in maize. This design enables breeders to evaluate the genetic potential of parental lines and select those with the highest hybridization potential [17, 18]. This study employs this design to investigate the combining ability and heterosis in 29 maize genotypes, including 9 parental lines and 20 F1 hybrids, focusing on traits related to nitrogen metabolism, yield, and overall plant development. The findings aim to enhance parental selection and optimize hybrid performance, particularly in temperate maize–growing regions like Northeast China.

Field experiment and data collection

In the summer of 2023, a total of 20 first–generation (F1) direct cross combinations, along with their nine original parent lines, were evaluated using a Randomized Complete Block Design (RCBD) in a split–plot arrangement with three replications at Chenggaozi Town, Xiangfang District, Harbin, Heilongjiang, China. The site is located at 126.86° E and 45.69° N, within a temperate continental monsoon climate zone (Fig. 1). Two nitrogen treatments were applied: 0 kg N/ha and 150 kg N/ha. Nitrogen treatment was assigned to the main plot, while genotypes were assigned to the subplot. Plant and row spacing were set at 25 cm and 65 cm, respectively, with 12 plants per row. Conventional agronomic practices for local maize cultivation were used to manage the experimental plots. At the silking stage, measurements were taken for N–metabolic enzymes, including nitrate reductase (NR) and glutamine synthase (GS), as well as chlorophyll content and chlorophyll fluorescence parameters (Fv/Fm and Y(II)). Ear leaves were collected from three representative plants, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C. Enzyme activities involved in nitrogen metabolism, specifically NR and GS, were measured using commercial kits from Suzhou Grace Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Suzhou, China). Absorbance readings were taken with a Thermo Scientific Multiskan FC microplate reader (Waltham, MA, USA). Similarly, a 0.1 g sample of fresh ear leaf tissue was ground in 95% ethanol and transferred into centrifuge tubes. The extracts were incubated in the dark at 4 °C for 48 h to ensure complete pigment extraction. After incubation, the samples were centrifuged, and the absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 470, 649, and 665 nm using a UV–5500 spectrophotometer (Shanghai Chemical Laboratory Equipment Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). Chlorophyll fluorescence parameters, including Fv/Fm (maximum photochemical efficiency of photosystem II) and Y(II) (effective quantum yield of photosystem II), were recorded on the middle portion of the leaves using a chlorophyll fluorometer (PhotosynQ, MultispeQ; PhenoTrait Technology Co., Ltd., Waltham, MA, USA). At physiological maturity, plant height (PH), ear height (EH), stalk diameter (SD), as well as six yield–associated traits: ear length (EL), ear diameter, rows per ear (RPE), grains per row (GPR), 100–kernel weight (100–KW), and grain yield (GY) were measured, with measurements taken from 5 plants per row to ensure accurate representation of each trait.

Results

Genetic parameters, including genetic variability, combining ability, and heterosis, are fundamental to the success of maize breeding programs. Estimates of combining ability are crucial for identifying potential parental lines and cross combinations that enhance trait development. In this study, we analyzed 29 maize genotypes, including nine parents (P1–P9) and their 20 F1 crosses, using statistical procedures such as analysis of variance (ANOVA) to evaluate genotypic differences. The results obtained by performing ANOVA provided insights into the genetic variability present in the traits, demonstrating significant differences among the genotypes for several key characteristics. Combining ability analysis was performed to assess general combining ability (GCA) and specific combining ability (SCA), essential for identifying superior parental lines and hybrid combinations. Heterosis analysis was also performed to evaluate the potential for hybrid vigor, particularly for traits related to yield. Additionally, heritability and genetic advance estimates were evaluated to understand the genetic control of the traits and their potential for improvement in successive generations.

Genetic variability among genotypes

The analysis of variance (ANOVA) for morphological, biochemical, and yield–related traits across 29 genotypes, including 20 hybrids resulting from crosses between 5 lines and 4 testers, revealed highly significant differences for all traits examined (Table 3). The ANOVA also identified significant variability among genotypes, parental lines, crosses, as well as interactions between parents and crosses, and between lines and testers, for nearly all traits. These findings indicate the substantial genetic variability present in the traits studied, which is critical for the identification of superior genotypes and optimal crosses in maize breeding programs.

General combining ability

General combining ability (GCA) measures the average performance of a line across multiple cross combinations, providing a crucial metric for the selection of superior parental lines in hybrid breeding programs (Table 4). The results indicate that lines P4 and P1 are prominent general combiners, exhibiting positive effects on a range of key physiological and yield–related traits. Notably, lines P4 and P1 showed positive GCA effects for N metabolism enzymes and chlorophyll fluorescence traits, highlighting their potential to enhance photosynthetic efficiency through improved N metabolism and photochemical quantum yield of photosystem II. Additional positive GCA values for P4 were observed for Fv/Fm, PH, EH, ED, RPE, GPR, and GY, marking it as an excellent general combiner for these traits. Line P1 showed a significant positive GCA effect for 100–KW, suggesting P1 as a strong contributor to seed size and weight. Therefore, P4 and P1 are identified as excellent general combiners for most of the traits, especially 100–KW and GY. In contrast, line P2 exhibited negative GCA effects for several traits, including N metabolism enzymes, ear traits, and grain yield. The negative GCA effects observed in P2 for several traits were unexpected, suggesting that non–additive genetic interactions or epistatic effects may have influenced its performance. This highlights the complexity of genetic interactions in maize, where additive effects alone do not always predict hybrid performance. Environmental factors may also have played a role, emphasizing the need to consider both genetic and environmental factors in hybrid breeding.

Among the testers, P8 and P9 emerged as commendable general combiners, with positive GCA effects across traits (Table 4). P8 and P9 exhibited a considerable positive GCA effect for N metabolism enzymes, growth, ear traits, and grain yield, solidifying their role in enhancing both yield and physiological traits crucial for high-performance hybrids. Tester P6 showed a positive GCA effect for growth and ear diameter, contributing significantly to improved plant structure and productivity. Consequently, P8 and P9 were identified as good general combiners across traits. In contrast, tester P7 exhibited negative GCA effects for N metabolism enzymes, ear traits, and grain yield, showing their limited utility for enhancing positive traits in hybrid combinations.

Specific combining ability

The specific combining ability (SCA) analysis revealed significant non–additive genetic interactions across several key physiological, morphological, and yield–related traits (Table 5). Results indicated that hybrids with positive SCA effects on N metabolism enzymes. Notably, hybrids P3 × P8, P1 × P9, and P5 × P7 demonstrated positive SCA effects for N metabolism enzymes, marking their potential for enhanced N utilization. Conversely, hybrids such as P5 × P8, P3 × P6, P1 × P8, and P4 × P9 exhibited negative SCA effects for N metabolism enzymes, indicating reduced N efficiency. In terms of the photochemical quantum yield of photosystem II, hybrids P3 × P8 and P5 × P6 showed positive SCA effects, indicating their potential to enhance photosystem II activity. In contrast, P5 × P8 and P3 × P6 showed negative SCA effects and exhibited limited photosystem II activity. Further, hybrids such as P1 × P9 and P5 × P7 showed positive SCA effects for PH growth, ear traits, and grain yield. These hybrids exhibited strong potential for improving yield and yield traits, which are crucial for high productivity. Similarly, P5 × P6 emerged as a high–performing hybrid, with positive SCA effects on ED and 100–KW, emphasizing its potential for grain yield. In contrast, hybrids such as P2 × P7, P5 × P8, and P3 × P6 showed considerable negative SCA effects, exhibiting reductions in growth, ear traits, and grain yield. These negative SCA effects suggest these hybrids may not be suitable for high–performance breeding initiatives, as they exhibit reduced growth and yield potential.

Ratio of GCA/SCA variance

The ratio of GCA/SCA variance is an important metric in plant breeding, providing insights into the relative contributions of additive versus non–additive genetic effects in controlling the expression of various traits (Table 6). Traits with high GCA/SCA ratios indicate that additive gene action is more important. Traits with low ratios suggest non–additive effects are significant. Differences in GCA/SCA ratios among traits could be due to the nature of genetic control (additive vs. non–additive) for each trait. Several traits, including growth and ear traits, exhibited high GCA/SCA variance ratios, reflecting a strong influence of additive genetic effects. These traits are primarily controlled by additive genetic factors, with non–additive effects being comparatively less influential. For grain yield, the GCA/SCA ratio (0.72) indicates a balanced contribution of both additive and non–additive effects, suggesting that both genetic components influence this trait. Similarly, traits such as Chl a + b, Fv/Fm, and Y (II) were also influenced by both additive and non–additive effects, reflecting the importance of specific allele interactions in their optimal expression. Nitrate reductase activity, with a GCA/SCA ratio of 0.58, showed a strong non–additive influence, suggesting that its expression is sensitive to genetic background. In contrast, traits such as PH, EH, SD, RPE, and GPR, with high GCA/SCA ratios, are largely driven by additive genetic effects. Traits with a GCA/SCA ratio less than 1 tend to exhibit more variability across different genetic backgrounds and environments, as their expression is significantly influenced by specific allele combinations.

Mid parent heterosis

Mid-parent heterosis (MPH) analysis revealed significant effects across various traits (Supplementary Table S1). The NR activity exhibited both positive and negative heterosis values, with the highest values observed in P1 × P7 (17.80%) and P5 × P7 (10.45%), indicating a substantial enhancement in N metabolism in these hybrids. In contrast, hybrids like P3 × P6 and P3 × P9 showed negative heterosis values (–15.50% and − 9.83%), suggesting reduced NR activity. For GS activity, the highest positive heterosis was recorded in P3 × P8 (13.67%) and P4 × P6 (12.21%), demonstrating the potential of these hybrids to improve N assimilation. However, hybrids such as P2 × P9 and P3 × P7 exhibited negative heterosis (–15.50% and − 13.68%), indicating a reduction in GS activity. Chlorophyll a + b content showed a wide range of heterosis values, from − 26.53% to 26.61%, with P2 × P6 (19.12%) and P5 × P8 (16.56%) exhibiting positive heterosis, while P5 × P7 and P3 × P8 showed negative effects (–26.53% and − 16.55%). This suggests that certain hybrids can enhance photosynthetic capacity, while others may have limitations in chlorophyll content. For Fv/Fm, P2 × P7 showed the highest positive heterosis (7.32%), with P3 × P8 (4.73%) also showing significant improvements. In contrast, hybrids like P3 × P7 and P4 × P7 exhibited negative heterosis, highlighting potential limitations in photosynthetic efficiency in these crosses. Regarding Y (II), the highest MPH was in P1 × P7 (17.80%) and P2 × P7 (9.77%), indicating their potential for enhancing photosystem II efficiency. However, P3 × P6 had the lowest heterosis value (–15.50%). For morphological traits such as PH, EH, and SD, P1 × P6 (61.68%), P1 × P9 (136.28%), and P2 × P6 (34.57%) exhibited the highest MPH, suggesting that these hybrids have considerable potential for improving plant structure. In contrast, P2 × P7 (21.79%), P4 × P8 (32.17%), and P3 × P9 (3.49%) showed the lowest values. In terms of yield–related traits, the highest MPH for EL and ED was observed in P3 × P8 (50.06% and 45.59%), while P2 × P7 had the lowest heterosis for these traits (6.67% and 16.38%). For RPE, GPR, and 100–KW, P3 × P9 (45.71%), P2 × P6 (91.85%), and P4 × P8 (81.01%) exhibited the highest heterosis, demonstrating their potential to enhance yield components. For grain yield, MPH ranged from 61.91% (P5 × P9) to 272.25% (P2 × P8), with P2 × P8, P3 × P8, and P2 × P6 showing the highest heterosis (272.25%, 250.84%, and 210.84%, respectively), indicating significant potential for yield improvement. In contrast, P5 × P8 (73.28%) and P5 × P9 (61.91%) exhibited the lowest heterosis values.

Better parent heterosis

Better parent heterosis (BPH) analysis across various traits showed significant effects (Supplementary Table S2). NR activity showed both positive and negative heterosis values, ranging from − 18.07% to 16.67%, with the highest values in P1 × P7 and P5 × P6, indicating substantial enhancement in N metabolism in these hybrids. In contrast, hybrids such as P3 × P6 and P3 × P9 exhibited negative heterosis values (–18.07% and − 14.08%), suggesting reduced NR activity. For GS activity, heterosis values ranged from − 21.05% to 10.78%, with the highest positive heterosis observed in P3 × P6 and P1 × P9, indicating potential for improved N assimilation. However, hybrids such as P2 × P9 and P3 × P7 exhibited negative heterosis (–21.05% and − 15.62%). Chlorophyll a + b content ranged from − 31.92% to 26.03%, with P5 × P6 and P2 × P6 exhibiting the highest heterosis. In contrast, P5 × P7 and P3 × P8 showed negative heterosis values (–31.92% and − 26.53%), suggesting that certain hybrids enhance photosynthetic capacity, while others exhibit limitations in Chl content.

Heterosis for Fv/Fm peaked at 4.29% in P2 × P7, with P3 × P8 (2.98%) showing positive heterosis. In contrast, hybrids like P4 × P7 and P3 × P9 exhibited negative heterosis (–7.52% and − 4.97%), highlighting potential limitations in photosynthetic efficiency in these crosses. For Y (II), the highest BPH was observed in P1 × P7 (16.66%) and P3 × P8 (13.36%), indicating their potential to enhance photosystem II efficiency. However, P3 × P6 showed the lowest heterosis. Regarding morphological traits, the highest BPH for PH, EH, and SD was observed in P3 × P6 (60.61%), P1 × P9 (115.30%), and P2 × P6 (24.96%), respectively. These hybrids have considerable potential for improving plant structure. In contrast, P2 × P9, P4 × P9, and P3 × P9 showed the lowest values. For yield–related traits, the highest BPH for EL and ED was observed in P2 × P8 and P3 × P8, while P2 × P7 and P4 × P8 recorded the lowest heterosis for these traits. For RPE, GPR, and 100–KW, the highest heterosis values were observed in P3 × P8, P2 × P6, and P4 × P8, indicating their potential to enhance yield components. The lowest values were observed in P1 × P7, P5 × P8, and P3 × P7 (1.31%).

Mean mid and better parent heterosis across hybrids

Mean MPH and BPH across the 20 hybrids exhibited a range from − 0.74 to 173.44% across various traits (Fig. 2). Notably, the highest MPH was observed in GY (150.20%), followed by EH (74.02%), GPR (56.25%), and NR activity (54.05%), indicating substantial hybrid vigor in these traits. Conversely, the lowest MPH was evident in GS activity (–0.74%), f Fv/Fm (–0.19%), Y (II) (3.94%), and Chl a + b (5.24%), suggesting limited heterosis. The highest MPH was recorded in hybrids P2 × P6, P2 × P8, P3 × P8, P1 × P9 and P2 × P9 across studied traits, demonstrating their superior performance. Similarly, the mean BPH across hybrids ranged from − 4.54 to 112.37% for all traits. The highest BPH was recorded in GY (112.37%), followed by EH (59.04%), NR activity (41.53%), and GPR (39.22%). Conversely, the lowest BPH was observed in GS activity (–4.54%), followed by chl a + b (–0.97%), Y (II) (1.07%), and RPE (1.45%), indicating limit heterosis for these traits. The highest BPH values were recorded in hybrids P2 × P8, P3 × P8, P1 × P9, and P3 × P9, suggesting these hybrids as superior for targeted breeding traits.

Notably, NR and GS activities were positively correlated with Fv/Fm, PH, EH, and GY, indicating the critical role of N metabolism in enhancing photosynthetic efficiency and promoting plant development, ultimately contributing to higher yield. Strong correlations were also recorded between NR and EH (0.73), PH and EH (0.68), and ED with GY (0.69), suggesting that taller plants with higher ear placement are associated with increased yield. Conversely, negative correlations were recorded for 100–KW with PH (–0.49) and EH (–0.39), indicating that larger kernels may be associated with reduced plant and ear height. Similarly, GPR is negatively associated with ED (–0.24) and RPE (–0.13), suggesting that higher grain numbers may be associated with smaller ear size and row count. Significant positive correlations with GY were also recorded for ED (0.69), RPE (0.51), 100–KW (0.45), NR (0.42), GS (0.41), and EL (0.30), indicating that heterosis in yield is closely related to these traits. The results suggest that BPH depends on multiple interrelated traits, highlighting the importance of selecting traits that optimize growth, ear morphology, and yield performance.

Correlation analysis of MPH across traits (Fig. 3b) revealed similar patterns to BPH, with some distinct trends. NR and GS activities remained essential, showing positive correlations with Fv/Fm, PH, EH, and GY, emphasizing the critical role of N metabolism in driving photosynthesis and improving agronomic traits. However, Y (II) showed a negative correlation with (EL) (–0.36) and (GPR) (–0.32), suggesting potential trade–offs in photosynthetic efficiency and grain number. Likewise, 100–KW exhibited negative correlations with PH (–0.16) and EH (–0.17), reflecting a need to balance kernel weight with plant and ear height for optimal hybrid performance. Heritability and genetic advance

Heritability estimates across the studied traits ranged from 0.58 to 0.99 (Supplementary Table S3). Notably, traits such as GY (0.99), PH (0.99), EH (0.98), and ED (0.97) exhibited high heritability, indicating strong genetic control. Conversely, Fv/Fm showed comparatively lower heritability (0.50), indicating a greater influence of environmental factors have a greater influence on this trait. Lower heritability indicates that selection for this trait might be less effective or more challenging because environmental influences play a significant role. The genetic advance (GA) as a percentage of the mean ranged from 4.41% to 78.05%. Grain yield showed the highest GA (78.05%), indicating that genetic factors play a substantial role in its expression, followed by EH (55.75%), GPE (44.06%), and NR (43.27%). The lowest GA was recorded for Fv/FM (4.41%), indicating the trait’s greater dependence on environmental factors. These results highlight the variation in genetic control and potential for genetic improvement among traits, offering valuable insights for breeding programs aiming to enhance maize productivity.

Discussion

Hybrid breeding has revolutionized crop improvement, particularly in maize, by enabling the development of genetically diverse, trait–specific inbred parental lines. However, understanding the genetic mechanisms underlying heterosis and combining ability remains a significant challenge. This study aimed to assess the performance of lines through an in–depth analysis of their combining abilities, which could guide future breeding programs to parental line selection and fully harness heterosis. The success of hybrid development depends on selecting parental lines with favorable genetic attributes and their ability to produce superior hybrids. This can be effectively evaluated by combining ability analysis, heritability estimates, and genetic advance evaluations [22, 23].

Genetic variability and combining ability

Combining ability analysis is a critical tool for assessing parental performance and understanding the genetic mechanisms governing trait expression. It has been effectively employed in plant breeding programs [24] to select the most promising parental lines. GCA estimates were in a favorable direction, indicating the potential of parents to produce promising breeding populations. On the other hand, SCA, which is influenced by non–additive gene action, plays a key role in identifying heterotic crosses that exhibit desirable traits [18, 25]. Thus, the SCA effect is a crucial criterion for evaluating hybrids and selecting trait–specific cross–combinations [26]. Several studies have reported that the variance component for grain yield in maize due to GCA was much higher than that due to SCA [27, 28]. In contrast, other studies [29, 30] concluded that SCA was more important than GCA. A third group of researchers, including [31, 32], reported that both components were equally important. The genetic variability of parental lines is crucial for selecting inbred lines, which are essential for targeted maize improvement [9].

This study evaluated nine genetically diverse inbred lines and their 20 F1 hybrids to identify promising parental lines and hybrids for maize improvement. Analysis of variance revealed significant genetic variability across all traits, indicating the genetic potential of the selected inbred lines and hybrids. GCA was significant for traits such as NR activity, GS activity, plant height, ear height, ear diameter, and grain yield. Lines P4 and P1 emerged as general combiners, P4 showing positive GCA effects for NR, GS, ear diameter, and grain yield, suggesting their potential to enhance physiological efficiency and yield–related traits. These findings align with Kumar et al. [33], who emphasized the importance of high GCA values for yield improvement. Genotypes with high GCA effects can also serve as valuable sources for developing improved inbred lines or as donors for further trait enhancement [34]. In contrast, P2 exhibited significant negative GCA effects for multiple traits, limiting its potential for developing high–yielding hybrids. This contradicts findings by Gaballah et al. [10], who emphasized the value of lines with positive GCA. This discrepancy may be due to non–additive genetic interactions (SCA effects) or environmental factors not accounted for in other studies.

Hybrid performance and specific combining ability

Hybrid performance can be predicted by integrating various parental line performance, combining ability, heterosis, and phenotypic data from field trials [35, 36]. Specific combining ability, determined by non–additive genetic effects, also significantly influenced hybrid performance. Hybrids such as P5 × P7, P1 × P9, P3 × P8, and P5 × P6 exhibited significant positive SCA effects for traits like NR, GS, PH, EL, ED, GPR, 100–KW, and GY, in the desirable direction, except for Chl a + b and RPE (Table 5). These results emphasize the role of non–additive genetic interactions in enhancing specific trait performance, consistent with findings from [18, 37], who indicated the importance of SCA in identifying hybrids with superior performance for targeted traits. The significant SCA effects observed in these hybrids highlight their potential for high productivity and suitability for breeding programs focused on yield improvement and other desirable traits.

The complementary roles of GCA and SCA reinforce the idea that breeding programs should leverage both additive and non–additive genetic effects to optimize hybrid performance. Our study also provides a novel perspective on the GCA/(GCA + SCA) variance ratio, showing that traits under study were influenced by both additive and non–additive effects, with additive effects being dominant. This is consistent with previous research on maize, where traits such as plant height, ear length, ear diameter, rows per ear, grains per row, kernel weight, and grain yield were similarly affected by additive effects [9, 38]. A GCA/(GCA + SCA) ratio nearing one suggests that GCA plays a more significant role than SCA in maize hybrid breeding (Table 6). Inbred lines exhibiting high GCA could be effectively used as parental lines to produce high–performing hybrids. Our findings suggest that traits like grain yield and ear height are more influenced by additive effects, as indicated by the high GCA/SCA ratio. This is consistent with Tesfaye et al. [39], who similarly found that additive gene effects were dominant for these traits. A low value of GCA, whether positive or negative, suggests that the average performance of a parent in hybrid combinations does not significantly differ from the overall mean of the crosses [10]. A high value of GCA, conversely, reflects significant gene transfer from parents to progeny, highlighting the prominence of additive genetic effects [40, 41].

Line × tester mating design and genetic divergence

The ANOVA following the Line × Tester mating design revealed significant genetic variation across all traits, indicating a wide genetic diversity. Notable variation was observed between the lines and testers for most traits, NR, Chl a + b, Fv/Fm, Y (II), ED, and GY (Table 3). These findings suggest substantial genetic divergence between the lines and testers [42–44]. The interaction between lines and testers was highly significant for most traits, excluding RPE (Table 3), indicating distinctiveness in productivity, particularly for grain yield. These results support the application of Line × Tester design in hybrid development [45, 46]. Our results align with [33, 47], demonstrating the effectiveness of this method in capturing genetic variability and assessing combining ability in maize hybrids.

Heritability and genetic advance

Heritability and genetic advance analyses provided further insights into the genetic control of traits. High heritability values for traits such as grain yield, plant height, and ear diameter indicate strong additive genetic effects and suggest that these traits can be effectively improved through selection [48]. Kashiani et al. [15] reported that days to silking, plant height, number of days to tasseling, and ear height were found to be the most heritable traits in tropical sweet corn inbred lines, with heritability of 80.5%, 79.9%, 66.9%, and 63.7%, respectively. Entringer et al. [49] reported high heritability in three super sweet corn populations, ranging from 61.53% (plant height) to 76.19% (grains per row). Grain yield exhibited the highest genetic advance, indicating substantial potential for genetic gain through selection. Traits with high heritability and genetic advancement, such as grain yield, are ideal for genetic improvement, as they reflect favorable additive genetic variance [50, 51].

Heterosis across hybrids

Heterosis, resulting from allelic diversity and the degree of dominance in parental lines’ traits, was significant in this study. The observed MPH (61.91% to 272.26%) and BPH (32.77% to 216.29%) for grain yield indicate substantial hybrid vigor. These findings align with a study by Mekasha et al. [52], who reported MPH values ranging from 115.33% to 329.88%, which reflects a similar extent of hybrid vigor. Additionally, Abera et al. [53] and Wegary et al. [54] reported a high level of heterosis in maize hybrids. Environmental factors, such as soil fertility, temperature, and precipitation, are known to influence heterosis expression [55], which may account for the observed variation. The genetic background of parental lines also plays a role, with high–performance lines showing consistent hybrid vigor due to allelic diversity at loci related to yield components [54]. The significant correlation between GCA and hybrid performance suggests that GCA could reliably predict hybrid success, with heterosis primarily dependent on SCA effects.

Correlation of traits and breeding implications

Traits such as NR, GS, EL, ED, RPE, and GPR exhibit significant positive correlations with GY across both MPH and BPH, indicating their potential for improvement through heterotic group selection. The positive correlation between NR / GS activity and grain yield reflects the importance of efficient nitrogen metabolism in maize. NR facilitates nitrate reduction, while GS synthesizes glutamine, both of which are essential for protein synthesis and overall plant growth. Higher NR and GS activities improve nitrogen use, enhancing photosynthesis and plant development, ultimately leading to increased grain yield. In breeding, selecting for higher NR and GS activity in parental lines can result in hybrids with improved nitrogen use efficiency, better yield potential. Interestingly, 100–KW showed negative or near–zero correlations with most traits in both datasets, suggesting an inverse relationship between kernel size and other agronomic traits in the context of heterosis. The strong and positive correlation between SCA, MPH, and BPH suggests that SCA can be a reliable predictor of hybrid performance [9, 56, 57]. These findings reinforce the idea that breeding lines with significant GCA and additive effects for traits such as NR, GS, PH, EH, ED, RPE, GPR, and GY contribute to the development of high–performing hybrids. The stable and positive correlations for traits like ED and GY suggest their potential for long–term improvement through heterosis breeding programs.

Limitations and future research

This study was conducted at a single location and over one year, limiting the ability to generalize results to other environments. Environmental factors such as temperature, rainfall, and soil type can significantly impact heterosis and hybrid performance. Future research should test these hybrids across multiple locations and years to evaluate their adaptability and consistency in diverse conditions. Additionally, the study lacks molecular or genomic validation of the observed genetic effects. Incorporating genomic tools like QTL mapping or GWAS would provide deeper insights into the genetic basis of heterosis and enhance breeding program efficiency.

Conclusion

The present study successfully identified key lines, testers, and hybrid combinations with significant genetic potential for enhancing maize yield and associated agronomic traits. Among the inbred lines, P4 (B73) demonstrated consistently superior general combining ability for critical traits such as N metabolism enzymes, chlorophyll fluorescence, ear traits, and grain yield, followed by P1 (Zheng 58), indicating their strong additive genetic effects. These lines are identified as promising candidates for the development of high–yielding hybrids. Among the testers, P8 (Mo17) and P9 (PH4CV) emerged as robust general combiners, particularly for N metabolism enzymes, chlorophyll content, and grain yield, signifying their value in hybrid breeding. Hybrid combinations such as P1× P9 (Zheng58 × PH4CV), P5 × P7 (PH6WC × 178), P3 × P8 (444 × Mo17), and P5 × P6 (PH6WC × Chang 7–2) exhibited outstanding performance for multiple yield–related traits, demonstrating significant heterosis. These hybrids represent strong candidates for commercial cultivation, benefiting from both additive and non–additive genetic effects. To further exploit hybrid vigor, reciprocal recurrent selection can be employed, focusing on both GCA and SCA to improve parental lines and their hybrids. For traits with high GCA/SCA ratios, selecting parents based on GCA is essential, as it emphasizes additive genetic effects. Conversely, for traits with low GCA/SCA ratios, specific crosses should be tested for SCA to exploit non–additive genetic effects. Marker–assisted selection or pedigree selection can be utilized to pyramid favorable alleles, ensuring gene stacking for multiple traits. Back–crossing and line breeding could also be employed to fix favorable alleles in elite lines. High heritability estimates, particularly for nitrate reductase, plant height, ear height, ear diameter, and grain yield, coupled with significant genetic advance, further emphasize the potential for selection gains in these traits. These findings suggest that B73, Zheng58, Mo17, and PH4CV are key parental lines for future breeding programs aimed at enhancing maize grain yield.
