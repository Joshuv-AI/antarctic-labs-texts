Selective Preconcentration and Mass Spectrometry Profiling of Charged Extracellular Modified Ribonucleosides by Borate Complexation and Temperature-Assisted Ionic Liquid Microextraction
Authors: Gabriella M. Floro, Colin P. McCarragher, Yu-Li Shih, Kevin D. Clark
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Extracellular modified ribonucleosides have recently emerged as potent cell-to-cell signaling molecules, but their low abundance in complex biofluids has impeded progress toward understanding the full complement of their functions. A major bottleneck is the lack of sample preparation techniques that can extract, purify, and preconcentrate extracellular modified nucleosides from biological fluids. Here, we address this limitation by developing a borate complexation, temperature-assisted ionic liquid microextraction (BTAIL-ME) technique. BTAIL-ME capitalizes on the formation of boronate esters with the cis-diols of ribonucleosides and the in situ production of an IL solvent to permit rapid and selective extraction of modified ribonucleosides. We synthesized and investigated the extraction performance of 8 IL solvents and optimized key parameters of BTAIL-ME, including temperature, extraction time, and IL concentration. The optimized BTAIL-ME method leveraged a dicationic IL 1,10-di­(3-benzylimidazolium)­decane bis­[(trifluoromethyl)­sulfonyl]­imide ([C10(BzIM)2]2+ 2­[NTf2]−) that displayed preconcentration factors of approximately 27-fold for N7-methylguanosine (m7G) and 30-fold for N1-methyladenosine (m1A), compared to no enrichment of these nucleosides using conventional solid-phase microextraction approaches for nanomolar concentration samples. Importantly, for broad applicability of BTAIL-ME, we developed a mass spectrometry-compatible method to recover nucleosides from the IL phase into an ammonium acetate buffer for direct injection into liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC-MS) instrumentation. We then applied BTAIL-ME to investigate extracellular modified nucleosides released from primary cultures of Aplysia californica neural tissues, which enabled downstream LC-MS detection of three methylated, positively charged ribonucleosides. BTAIL-ME introduces an extraction approach for profiling select extracellular modified ribonucleosides by LC-MS, paving the way toward understanding their functions in biological systems.

Introduction

The epitranscriptome is comprised of more than 170 post-transcriptional modifications of RNA, ranging in chemical complexity from methylation to conjugation with metabolites and complex biopolymers. − These modifications are dynamic and in some cases reversible, impacting the binding, localization, and translation of substrate RNAs. While the functions of cellular RNA modifications are increasingly investigated, − very little is known about the metabolism and downstream biological effects of modified ribonucleosides after their release into extracellular fluids. − , Recently, extracellular modified ribonucleosides (EMNs) have emerged as potential disease biomarkers ,,,− and ligands in cell-to-cell signaling. , EMNs are often present at low concentrations in biofluids, ranging from fM to μM levels in plasma, cerebrospinal fluid, and urine, ,, and their abundances may reflect a pathophysiological state of various forms of cancer, , neurodegenerative disease, ,, and infection. , Very recently, modified adenosine nucleosides, including N1-methyladenosine (m1A) and N6-methyladenosine (m6A), displayed potent activation of purinergic receptors with implications in immune cell function. , These findings raise questions about whether the cell-to-cell signaling properties of EMNs are generalizable to other systems. For example, in the central nervous system (CNS), canonical nucleosides are well-established neuromodulators, , but nothing is known about the concentration profiles or signaling properties of EMNs in the brain. Although sensitive analytical methods exist for profiling intracellular modified nucleosides in the CNS, , analogous approaches that overcome the low abundance of EMNs in complex biological samples are lacking.

Solid-phase extraction (SPE) and liquid–liquid extraction (LLE) are conventional techniques for purifying and isolating EMNs. These exhaustive extraction methods often require large volumes of organic solvent and are labor- and time-intensive, involving lengthy in vacuo drying and resuspension steps. Additionally, SPE and LLE are limited by the commercial availability of sorbents and/or solvents compatible with biological samples. The extraction of ribonucleosides has thus relied on custom materials, such as TiO2-based sorbents, phenylboronate gels, magnetic nanoparticles, or boronate affinity media that limit extraction to an individual modified nucleoside or class of modified nucleosides. An ideal extraction technique would leverage an extraction phase designed specifically for EMNs to maximize their preconcentration, while minimizing the solvent volume, time, and user intervention required.

Microextractions comprise a family of sample preparation techniques that are faster, use less solvent, and often afford superior preconcentration of analytes compared to exhaustive SPE or LLE techniques. When combined with selective extraction phases, microextraction approaches may be particularly useful for profiling EMNs due to their low endogenous concentrations. For example, a microextraction approach using a magnetic sorbent was applied for the extraction of two methylated nucleosides from urine before analysis by HPLC, enabling enrichment factors >100-fold after solvent evaporation and resuspension. Although this approach was highly selective, new methods and extraction phases capable of rapidly preconcentrating a broader profile of EMNs are needed.

Ionic liquids (ILs) are molten salts comprised of organic/inorganic cations and anions with melting temperatures below 100 °C and are attractive alternative solvents for sample preparation. Owing to the customizable functional groups readily incorporated into IL structures, an estimated ∼1018 unique ILs can be synthesized. ILs display unusual physicochemical properties, including low vapor pressure at ambient conditions, a broad range of polarities, high thermal stability, high ionic conductivity, and multiple solvation interactions. The structural tunability of ILs renders them useful for a broad range of applications, including energy storage, catalysis, and drug delivery. ILs have experienced growing success in analytical chemistry, particularly as task-specific solvents and sorbents for the extraction and preconcentration of a variety of analytes, including DNA, , RNA, phenol, perfluoroalkyl substances, and neurotransmitters. When coupled with microextraction approaches such as in situ IL-dispersive liquid–liquid microextraction (IL-DLLME), high analyte enrichment factors are readily achieved in microliter volumes of IL extraction phase. ,− Given these advantages, we hypothesized that IL solvents would be useful for preconcentrating and profiling the multitude of modified nucleosides that have been previously reported.

Here, we have developed a borate complexation, temperature-assisted IL microextraction technique (BTAIL-ME) for the selective extraction and preconcentration of three EMNs from aqueous biological supernatants for downstream liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis. BTAIL-ME capitalizes on borate complexation with the cis-diols of ribonucleosides to facilitate partitioning of positively charged EMNs into an IL extraction phase formed in situ. Our BTAIL-ME approach outperformed conventional C18-μSPE by ∼20- to 30-fold on the basis of preconcentration factors for m1A, N3-methylcytidine (m3C), and N7-methylguanosine (m7G) and required less time for the overall workflow (∼30 min). Important for broad application of the method, we show that BTAIL-ME can be combined with LC-MS/MS for the extraction of three EMNs from aqueous samples containing high concentrations of nonvolatile salts. Finally, we demonstrate that BTAIL-ME enables profiling of three EMNs released from primary CNS tissue cultures from the neurobiological model animal Aplysia californica, paving the way toward understanding the roles of EMNs in biological systems.

Reagents and Materials

The reagent 4-methoxybenzyl bromide was obtained from AOBChem (Los Angeles, CA, USA). Lithium bis­[(trifluoromethyl)­sulfonyl]­imide (LiNTf2) was obtained from Chem-Impex International (Wood Dale, IL, USA). For additional modified nucleoside standards, m1A was purchased from Biosynth (Staad, Switzerland), m6A was obtained from Selleck Chemicals (Houston, TX, USA), and m3C was purchased from Cayman Chemical (Ann Arbor, MI, USA). All chemicals had a minimum purity of 98% and were used without further purification.

Synthesis of Ionic Liquids

Three monocationic ILs, butylmethylimidazolium bromide ([BMIM]+[Br]−), octylmethylimidazolium bromide ([OMIM]+[Br]−), and decylmethylimidazolium bromide ([DMIM]+[Br]−), were synthesized following previously reported procedures (Scheme S1, Supporting Information). Five dicationic ILs including 1,10-di­(3-methylimidazolium)­decane bromide ([C10(MIM)2]2+2­[Br]−), 1,12-di­(3-methylimidazolium)­dodecane bromide ([C12(MIM)2]2+2­[Br]−), 1,10-di­(3-butylimidazolium)­decane bromide ([C10(BIM)2]2+2­[Br]−), 1,8-di­(3-benzylimidazolium)­octane bromide ([C8(BzIM)2]2+2­[Br]−), 1,10-di­(3-benzylimidazolium)­decane bromide [C10(BzIM)2]2+ 2­[Br]−), and 1,10-di­(3-p-methoxybenzylimidazolium)­decane bromide ([C10(pMBzIM)2]2+2­[Br]−) were synthesized according to previously reported procedures (Scheme S1) with slight modifications for the [C10(pMBzIM)2]2+ 2­[Br]− IL (Scheme S2). All ILs were characterized with 1H NMR, 13C NMR, and high-resolution MS. Characterization data for the [C10(pMBzIM)2]2+ 2­[Br]− and [C10(BzIM)2]2+ 2­[Br]− ILs (Figures S1–S6) are shown in the Supporting Information.

Borate Complexation Temperature-Assisted Ionic Liquid Microextraction (BTAIL-ME) or C18-μSPE Treatment of Nucleoside Standards

BTAIL-ME of nucleosides from aqueous samples was conducted as follows. Samples were prepared in polypropylene microfuge tubes either from nucleoside stock solutions (1000 ppm) at the desired concentrations or isolated directly from cell cultures (see below). Next, an 80 μL aliquot of a 50 mg/mL hydrophilic IL bromide salt solution was added to the sample for a final volume of 400 μL. The mixture was placed in a dry bath at 55 °C for 15 or 60 min, for the high-throughput or high enrichment factor method, respectively. Then, the LiNTf2 metathesis reagent (1 g/mL) was added at an IL: LiNTf2 molar ratio of 1:2 to facilitate the formation of a turbid solution comprised of finely dispersed hydrophobic IL microdroplets. The sample was agitated with a vortex mixer (∼115g) for 30 s and then centrifuged at 21,000g for 15 min at 4 °C to sediment the analyte-enriched hydrophobic IL phase. The supernatant was removed and diluted for HPLC-UV analysis to assess depletion of nucleosides from the aqueous phase. To the analyte-enriched IL phase, 20 μL of 500 mM ammonium acetate and 20 μL of ethyl acetate were added for back-extraction of nucleosides. The immiscible liquids were vortexed for 10 s and then centrifuged at 21,000g and 4 °C for 5 min, after which an aliquot of the aqueous layer was subjected to HPLC analysis to determine nucleoside recoveries. For interfacing BTAIL-ME with LC-MS/MS analysis of nucleosides, residual IL in the back-extraction solution was removed using pipet tips packed with C18 functionalized particles prior to sample injection. The 20 μL back-extraction solution was aspirated through the pipet tip 20 times. For C18-μSPE sample treatment, solvent choice, solvent volume, and sample aspiration steps followed the protocol provided in the user manual.

To determine the quantity of nucleosides extracted from aqueous samples by BTAIL-ME, matrix-matched external calibration curves were constructed with authentic nucleoside standards and used to compare nucleoside concentrations in the aqueous sample solution before and after extraction using either HPLC or LC-MS analysis (Figures S7 and S8A). For depletion experiments, a 100 μL aliquot of the aqueous sample was removed after extraction, diluted to 250 μL with HPLC-grade water, and a 10 μL aliquot was injected for HPLC analysis. Analysis of Ribonucleosides by HPLC-UV and LC-MS/MS

Nucleosides were separated using an Agilent 1260 Infinity II Prime LC System or an Agilent 1260 Infinity II HPLC with diode array detection (DAD) fitted with an Agilent ZORBAX Eclipse Plus C18 column (4.6 × 150 mm, 5 μm) and a ZORBAX Extend C18 guard column (4.6 × 12.5 mm, 5 μm). Mobile phase A was 5 mM ammonium acetate (pH 5.5) in water, and mobile phase B consisted of a 60/40 mixture of mobile phase A and ACN. The separation was conducted at a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min in gradient elution mode with an initial hold at 1% B for 5 min, an increase to 5% B over 4 min, and then an increase to 7% B over 2 min. The mobile phase composition was then linearly ramped to 10% B over 2 min, 15% B over 19 min, 70% B over 8 min, and 100% B over 4 min. The mobile phase composition was held at 100% B for 10 min before re-equilibrating the column with 3 column volumes at the initial mobile phase composition prior to the next injection. UV detection was set to 260 nm for nucleosides and 210 nm to monitor elution of IL from the column. After 10 injections of BTAIL-ME samples, the column was washed with 50/50 ACN/H2O for 1 h to prevent accumulation of IL in the HPLC system.

Mass spectrometry analysis of nucleosides was performed on either an Agilent 6530B quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometer (qTOF-MS) or Agilent 6230 TOF using electrospray ionization in positive mode. For BTAIL-ME-treated samples, flow from the HPLC was directed through a diverter valve set to waste from 0 to 3 min and 25–54 min of analysis to prevent nonvolatile salts and other contaminants from entering the ion source. For standards or C18-μSPE-treated samples, the diverter valve was set to waste for 0–3 min. For MS analysis on either instrument, parameters included a capillary voltage of 4 kV, N2 drying gas flow rate of 5 L/min at 300 °C, and a mass range of m/z 200–580. For targeted MS/MS analysis on the qTOF-MS instrument, m/z 258.1090 (m3C), 282.1202 (m1A, m6A, and Am), and 298.1151 (m7G) were selected for collision-induced dissociation (CID) using a narrow isolation width (m/z 1.3). Collision energies for these targeted ribonucleoside ions are shown in Table S1.

Animals and Cell Culture

Aplysia californica (∼150–200 g) were purchased from the National Resource for Aplysia (Miami, FL, USA) and maintained in an aquarium with circulating, aerated artificial seawater (ASW; Instant Ocean, Blacksburg, VA, USA) at 14 °C for a minimum of 72 h before experiments. Animals were anesthetized by injection of a chilled magnesium chloride solution (390 mM) into the body cavity using a volume equivalent (in mL) of 40% of the animal’s mass (in g). Major ganglia from the CNS were surgically removed, washed in ASW, and cultured in 2.5 mL of homemade ASW with 10 ppm kanamycin sulfate (GoldBio, St. Louis, MO, USA) in a 12-well plate at 13 °C for 6 days. Homemade ASW consists of 460 mM NaCl, 10 mM KCl, 10 mM CaCl2, 22 mM MgCl2, and 26 mM MgSO4 in deionized water. The tissue was then removed, and the culture medium was subjected to BTAIL-ME to prepare the sample for downstream analysis using HPLC-UV or LC-MS/MS analysis.

Results and Discussion

Ionic Liquid Solvents for the Preconcentration of Modified Ribonucleosides

The low abundance of EMNs in complex biological samples precludes their direct detection by analytical instrumentation, limiting our ability to characterize the biological and diagnostic importance of these compounds. Due to the structural tunability of ILs for specific tasks, we hypothesized ILs would be ideally suited for the selective extraction and preconcentration of EMNs. We initially investigated the nucleoside extraction properties of a series of monocationic alkylmethylimidazolium ILs using an in situ IL-DLLME technique (Figure S8A and B). For these extraction experiments, a standard mixture containing m1A, m7G, A, and Am nucleosides (Figure A) was initially chosen, as these nucleosides display a wide range of polarities and have been previously detected in extracellular fluids. , Specifically, m1A is positively charged at physiological pH and known to activate purinergic receptors in immune cells. m7G is another methylated purine nucleoside also bearing a positive charge. Adenosine was included in initial experiments due to its abundance in extracellular CNS fluids, while Am was selected as a representative, relatively nonpolar methylated positional isomer that blocks the 2′-O hydrogen bond donor group. The in situ IL-DLLME of nucleosides with [BMIM]+[NTf2]− and [OMIM]+[NTf2]− resulted in modest E F values between 20 and 40 for m1A and m7G, while little to no extraction of A and Am was observed (Figures S8C and D). E F values reflect the theoretical concentration of nucleoside in the IL solvent after extraction and are useful for initial method optimization. Although longer alkyl chains have previously been shown to improve EE values for DNAs, this effect was not observed for the [DMIM]+[NTf2]− IL, possibly due to its low critical micelle concentration (CMC) promoting polar nucleosides to distribute into the aqueous solution instead of the hydrophobic core of the micelle. , We reasoned that a dicationic IL with two imidazolium groups tethered by a long alkyl chain would increase CMC values and afford greater structural tunability, so we synthesized and applied a series of dicationic ILs for IL-DLLME of nucleosides (Figure B). Generally, lower EE and E F were observed for the dicationic ILs with pronounced decreases in m1A and m7G extraction (Figure C). We suspected the poor extraction of m1A and m7G observed with the studied dicationic ILs was due to electrostatic repulsion between the multiple imidazolium groups and positively charged methylated nucleosides, so we explored borate-ribonucleoside complexation to mitigate this effect. The cis-diols of ribonucleosides reversibly complex with borate to form a bidentate boronate ester bearing a negative charge that may interact favorably with the cation of the IL. , We thus integrated borate complexation into the IL-DLLME workflow by adding sodium tetraborate to the sample prior to in situ generation of the IL extraction phase. Gratifyingly, enrichment of m1A by the [C10(MIM)2]2+ 2­[NTf2]− IL dramatically improved from negligible E F without borate to 27.2 ± 1.8 (Figure C). Low EE and E F values for Am were observed (Figures C and S9), which may be explained by the 2′-O-methyl group impeding formation of the cyclic boronate ester. The extraction of A was also poor, but it is important to note that A is an abundant nucleoside in extracellular fluids and many other biological samples, so its negligible enrichment into the IL phase may be advantageous for selective analysis of EMNs. Since m1A and m7G bear positively charged nucleobases under the studied conditions, borate complexation results in the formation of a zwitterion that appears to be important for IL-based extraction. 11B NMR spectra support the formation of monodentate and bidentate boronate ester complexes with m1A and m7G in solution (Figure S10A-D), and the relative intensities of the peaks corresponding to the nucleoside-borate complexes decrease in the presence of the IL cation (Figure S10D), suggesting interaction between the nucleoside-borate complex and IL cation that may improve extraction performance.

A major advantage of IL solvents compared to commercially available extraction media is the tunability of IL cation/anion structures for selective analyte enrichment. After observing high ribonucleoside E F values using borate complexation and dicationic imidazolium IL extraction solvents, we investigated the relationship between IL structure and nucleoside extraction by varying the functional groups at the N3 position of the imidazolium ring and the alkyl linker length between imidazolium cations. Increasing the N3-alkyl chain length from methyl to butyl showed little improvement in EE values for m1A, A, and Am, but substantially improved the EE of m7G from 2.9% to 33.3% (Figure S9). We thus explored a dicationic IL with an N3-octyl group [C10(OIM)2]2+ 2­[NTf2]− structure, but this IL was not compatible with our extraction method due to emulsion formation at IL concentrations required for BTAIL-ME. We then thought to increase π-π stacking interactions between the IL and ribonucleosides with a benzyl-functionalized IL ([C10(BzIM)2]2+ 2­[NTf2]−) and observed higher EE and E F for m1A (Figure C and Figure S9). The benzyl structure [C10(BzIM)2]2+ 2­[NTf2]− outperformed an IL bearing allyl groups ([C10(AIM)2]2+ 2­[NTf2]−), potentially due to enhanced cation-π and π–π stacking interactions through its aromatic structure. When functionalizing the benzyl group with a para-methoxy moiety, we observed no enrichment, which we attribute to the relatively more viscous nature of the IL. We therefore determined that a benzyl group was optimum at the imidazolium N3 position for extraction of these nucleosides.

Next, we studied the alkyl chain linker length, which is known to alter the physical properties of the dicationic ILs, such as viscosity, surface tension, and density. Upon decreasing the alkyl linker length from a decyl to an octyl group in the [C8(BzIM)2]2+ 2­[NTf2]− IL, we observed lower E F for all nucleosides (Figure C). ILs with linkers C12 ([C12(MIM)2]2+ 2­[Br]−) and longer formed emulsions in solution and were therefore not employed in BTAIL-ME. Overall, the dicationic IL [C10(BzIM)2]2+ 2­[NTf2]− showed the highest E F values for m1A and m7G and was selected for further experiments.

We then investigated the effect of temperature on the combined borate complexation and in situ IL-DLLME method, as it is well established that temperature affects IL viscosity and mass transfer of analytes into the IL phase. , Over a range of 0–75 °C, we found the optimum extraction temperature to be 55 °C, which resulted in E F for m1A and m7G of 51.4 ± 2.1 and 87.1 ± 0.9, respectively (Figure D). Incubating the sample at a higher temperature of 75 °C resulted in degradation of m1A and m7G due to Dimroth rearrangement and depurination, respectively (Figures S11 and S12). We selected 55 °C as the optimum temperature because the EE and E F values improved significantly while the chemical alterations to m1A and m7G were negligible (<3%) at 15 min (Figures S11 and S12). The combined borate complexation, temperature-assisted IL microextraction (BTAIL-ME) for the selective extraction of modified ribonucleosides is shown in Figure and was subsequently further optimized to enhance ribonucleoside extraction.

BTAIL-ME Method Optimization

We optimized the BTAIL-ME method to maximize the selective extraction and enrichment of modified ribonucleosides, minimize sample handling steps, reduce extraction times, and interface with LC-MS/MS instrumentation. IL concentration, sample incubation time, and agitation time are key parameters for reducing extraction times while maintaining high EE and E F values. Sample incubation time is defined here as the time that the nucleosides sample is incubated with the hydrophilic IL bromide salt and sodium tetraborate before adding the metathesis reagent. IL concentrations from 8.1 to 32.4 mM (yielding 3–14 μL of sedimented IL extraction phase) and incubation times from 15 to 60 min were optimized in a multivariate experimental design. The highest E F values (up to 131.3) were observed when employing low IL concentrations and longer incubation times (Figure S13A-B), which would enable analysis of EMNs at <100 nM concentrations (270 ± 58 nM m1A in plasma10). Alternatively, when high sample throughput is desired, the user may opt for a shorter BTAIL-ME method deploying a 15 min incubation. For small-volume or low-abundance samples, a 60 min incubation produces a 3 μL sedimented IL layer to maximize nucleoside preconcentration. These results demonstrate that the BTAIL-ME method can be tailored to balance nucleoside extraction with sample throughput, depending on the needs of the analyst.

Next, we investigated the effect of the concentration of LiNTf2 metathesis reagent and sodium tetraborate on BTAIL-ME of ribonucleosides. Ionic strength is a key factor influencing metathesis reactions and sedimented phase volume in in situ IL-DLLME methods, thereby affecting EE and E F values. Figure S13C shows heatmap results from the multivariate optimization of LiNTf2 and sodium tetraborate concentrations. Inspection of the heatmaps reveals that different BTAIL-ME conditions were optimal for m1A and m7G, in some cases resulting in EE values that differ by more than 50% (Figure S13C). A and Am showed negligible extraction (<5%) regardless of reagent concentration (data not shown). BTAIL-ME displays the highest EE for m7G when there is an excess of NTf2 – and borate, while m1A partitions to the IL phase in lower salt conditions, possibly due to the differential impact of ionic strength on nucleoside solubility and electrostatic interactions. These results suggest that BTAIL-ME conditions can be tuned for the extraction of select EMNs.

Agitation time was also optimized to identify conditions that would promote thorough mixing and maximize extraction phase contact with the sample. Vortex agitation times from 30 to 500 s showed little to no difference in EE or E F values (Figure S13D-E). An agitation time of 30 s was chosen to shorten the workflow while maintaining the selectivity for charged ribonucleosides, as we observed a slight decrease in the extraction of m1A and m7G perhaps due to partitioning of inorganic salts to the IL phase. We also observed a corresponding increase in the extraction of A and Am, which may be explained by the coextraction of more hydrophobic analytes at higher salt concentrations, as inorganic salts increasingly partition into the IL over time. Ultimately, the optimized BTAIL-ME method facilitates rapid ribonucleoside extractions (∼35 min) and enables preconcentration of select ribonucleosides with evaluation factors of ∼50–60.

LC-MS Compatibility of BTAIL-ME for Preconcentration of EMNs from a Complex Sample and Comparison to Solid-Phase Microextraction

Extracellular biological fluids like cerebrospinal fluid, urine, and cell culture supernatants are high in salt content and contain a broad range of modified ribonucleosides at fM−μM concentrations, requiring preconcentration strategies prior to HPLC-UV or LC-MS/MS. ,, High salt concentrations can also damage MS instrumentation if directly injected. We thus investigated the LC-MS/MS compatibility of BTAIL-ME, which would permit the discovery and identification of modified ribonucleosides in extracellular fluids. We reasoned that modified ribonucleosides may be recovered from the IL phase into an MS-compatible buffer. Following BTAIL-ME of a 10 ppm ribonucleosides mixture in artificial seawater (ASW), we diluted the analyte-enriched IL layer in a small volume (∼10 μL) of ethyl acetate and back-extracted nucleosides into 10 μL of varying concentrations of ammonium acetate (10 mM, 500 mM, and 1 M) with 30 s of vortex agitation (Figure A). We found that higher concentrations of ammonium acetate increased nucleoside recoveries (Figure S14), suggesting that disruption of electrostatic interactions between the IL and nucleoside-borate complex facilitates recovery. As 500 mM ammonium acetate provided the best precision and highest recoveries, we chose this concentration for our back-extraction method. Gratifyingly, extracted nucleosides recovered with the 500 mM ammonium acetate buffer were detectable by LC-MS and showed substantially larger peak areas compared to nucleosides in the initial sample solution (Figure B). We then used this recovery method following application BTAIL-ME of a more complex nucleosides matrix and compared our approach to a conventional method.

Solid-phase extraction using a pipet tip packed with C18 particles (C18-μSPE) is a popular approach to desalting samples prior to MS analysis. Figure C and D show a comparison of C18-μSPE and BTAIL-ME for the extraction of a mixed standard of 17 ribonucleosides with individual concentrations ranging from 0.025 to 1.000 μM prepared in a high ionic strength sample matrix of ASW that is typically used for neuronal cell and tissue cultures from the neurobiological model Aplysia californica. Table S2 provides a list of the studied nucleosides (Supporting Information). While C18-μSPE showed little to no preconcentration using the manufacturer’s suggested conditions (Table S2 and Figure C), we observed high PFs for positively charged nucleosides like m3C, m1A, and m7G, particularly at lower nucleoside concentrations (Table S2 and Figure D). For example, m3C was preconcentrated from a 0.025 μM sample by a factor of 19.8 ± 3.4. Note that given a sample volume of 400 μL and recovery volume of 10 μL, the theoretical maximum PF is 40. As expected, PF values decreased as nucleoside concentration increased, which may be explained by saturation of the IL with nucleoside analytes. These results indicate that BTAIL-ME is compatible with preconcentrating modified ribonucleosides from a complex matrix and outperforms conventional techniques.

We then compared BTAIL-ME and μSPE using applicability and sustainability metrics. We employed the blue applicability grade index (BAGI) on a scale of 25–100, and the sample preparation metric of sustainability (SPMS) on a scale of 0–10.61. , Both tests depict the scores in the center of the pictograms shown in Figure S15, with darker blue values for BAGI indicating favorable parameter scores, while SPMS follows a green-red spectrum in which green corresponds to more favorable scores that are weighted into the final value. BAGI evaluation scored BTAIL-ME as 65.0 and C18 μ-SPE as 55.0 (Figure S15A). BTAIL-ME outperformed C18 μ-SPE in terms of one-step preconcentration into the IL solvent, while C18 μ-SPE has a practicality advantage due to their ready-made commercially available kits. SPMS evaluation scored BTAIL-ME as 5.79 and C18 μ-SPE as 5.05 (Figure S15B). These scores show moderate advancements for BTAIL-ME in terms of miniaturization, as sample volumes of less than 1 mL are required, approximately 1 mL of waste generated per sample, and many samples can be analyzed in parallel (∼24) with our equipment. The weighted scoring system of SPMS showed slightly higher scores for BTAIL-ME due to the additional steps and apparatus required for C18 μ-SPE.

We then evaluated the feasibility of using BTAIL-ME for quantitative experiments and the linearity of BTAIL-ME for the three nucleosides with the highest preconcentration factors. Figure S16 shows that when samples containing increasing concentrations of m1A, m7G, and m3C are processed by BTAIL-ME, a corresponding increase in extracted ion chromatogram (EIC) peak areas for each recovered nucleoside is observed. Correlation coefficients (R 2) were 0.66, 0.71, and 0.92 for m3C, m1A, and m7G, respectively, for the concentration range of 25–1000 nM. Since the IL solvent becomes saturated with nucleoside analyte at higher nucleoside concentrations, it is conceivable to improved linearity would be observed at lower nucleoside concentrations. Nonetheless, our results show that BTAIL-ME enables qualitative profiling of select modified nucleosides and shows great promise for future quantitative experiments.

Application to Extracellular Modified Nucleosides Released from A. californica Neurons

Modified nucleosides represent an emerging class of cell-to-cell signaling molecules, , but their abundances and functions have yet to be characterized in the brain. To this end, we cultured A. californica primary CNS tissues, collected the supernatant, and applied our optimized BTAIL-ME technique coupled with downstream LC-MS analysis (Figure A). For comparison, we analyzed CNS tissue culture supernatants by C18 SPE and a desalting method using 70% ethanol. The conventional C18 SPE and organic solvent-assisted desalting approaches did not yield detectable modified nucleosides from tissue culture supernatants, despite successfully enriching nucleoside standards from samples prepared at 75 μM and 0.075 μM in ASW (Figure S16). This is likely due to the extremely low EMN concentrations and complexity of the sample matrix due to cell-secreted interferences. For BTAIL-ME, the base peak chromatogram (BPC) showed no detectable early eluting salt/buffer peak (Figure B), indicating BTAIL-ME purified the sample from salts that would otherwise hinder analyte ionization by ESI and saturate the MS detector. Components eluting at 15 min were not identified but likely represent coextracted cellular metabolites. Extracted ion chromatograms (EICs) for m1A, m6A, and m3C derived from BTAIL-ME of CNS tissue releasates were confirmed based on elution order and high mass accuracy with peak intensities above the signal-to-noise ratio (Table S2 and Figure C). Intriguingly, the nucleosides m1A and m6A have previously been reported as ligands in purinergic receptors but have never been reported as EMNs in the CNS. The detection of m3C further illustrates the selectivity BTAIL-ME displays for polar, charged nucleosides. To further confirm EMN identities, we pooled CNS tissues from two animals into a single 6 day culture, performed BTAIL-ME of the supernatant, and analyzed the recovered nucleosides using a targeted LC-MS/MS method. We observed the expected product ions for m3C, m1A, and m6A (Figures D and S17), confirming detection of these nucleosides. Given that m6A is uncharged under the extraction conditions, it is possible that this nucleoside is either generated by Dimroth rearrangement of m1A, is present at high levels in A. californica CNS releasates, or a combination of mechanisms. It is conceivable that other nucleosides were extracted by BTAIL-ME but were lost to waste due to the diverter valve method we set up to prevent contamination of the instrument with residual IL solvent. Nevertheless, BTAIL-ME enabled detection of EMNs from CNS tissue culture supernatants, underscoring the need for further investigation into the profile and functions of EMNs in the brain, where other biological fluids may contain higher concentrations of protein or lipids that preclude the detection of EMNs.

Conclusions

In this study, we developed BTAIL-ME for the extraction and preconcentration of EMNs from aqueous samples. BTAIL-ME utilizes in situ generation of an IL extraction phase to enrich borate-complexed ribonucleosides for downstream LC-MS analysis. Optimizing the BTAIL-ME method revealed an IL concentration and time-dependent enrichment performance, where the user can choose to minimize sample preparation time or maximize E F for select modified ribonucleosides. The BTAIL-ME method outperforms C18-μSPE in terms of enrichment, with m1A preconcentrated 30-fold using BTAIL-ME compared to no detectable extraction using C18-μSPE. We applied the optimized BTAIL-ME method to primary A. californica CNS tissue culture releasates and detected three EMNs, including m1A, m3C, and m6A. Overall, BTAIL-ME addresses limitations of conventional sample preparation methods to enable profiling of select EMNs and future investigation into their biological functions.

Although BTAIL-ME provides high PF values for select EMNs, the method is not without limitations. Here, we demonstrate the application of BTAIL-ME to a biological cell culture supernatant for the preconcentration of modified nucleosides, but other biofluids such as plasma and CSF would be intriguing to explore. To apply this method to biological fluids that contain higher concentrations of modified nucleosides, we envision sample dilution may enable quantification within the linear range of BTAIL-ME. Additional method optimization considerations would likely be required to overcome the relatively higher protein concentrations in these samples. Next, BTAIL is highly selective for the extraction of positively charged modified nucleosides. This high selectivity comes at the cost of universality, which further highlights the need to develop new IL structures to capture a broad range of EMNs. Lastly, while BTAIL-ME compares favorably to μSPE for extraction and preconcentration of select EMNs, there is room for process improvements. For example, minimizing sample handling by eliminating the back-extraction step and reducing lab apparatus requirements by investigating alternative heat sources (e.g., heat packs) would broaden the applicability of BTAIL-ME, potentially to field settings.

Finally, we envision that judicious selection of IL solvents for BTAIL-ME will permit LC-MS/MS profiling of EMNs from even smaller volume samples, such as releasates from individual ganglia or single neurons. Profiling intracellular MNs for individual ganglia , and single neurons from the A. californica CNS revealed heterogeneous RNA modification profiles, suggesting the intriguing possibility that EMN profiles may also be tissue and cell specific. We anticipate IL microextraction techniques will play a prominent role in efforts to assay these small-volume samples and may be further enhanced by coupling with microfluidic devices and state-of-the-art high-resolution mass spectrometers.
