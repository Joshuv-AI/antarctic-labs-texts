Spatial Partitioning of Phenylpropanoid- and Auxin-Related Metabolites in Sparganium stoloniferum Tubers
Authors: Mengru Sang, Qinan Liu, Ying Dong, Zheng Jiang, Jingjie Dang, Siyi Liu, Chenyan Lu, Qinan Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Phenolic acids and flavonoids are major bioactive constituents of Sparganium stoloniferum tubers (SL), yet their spatial organization within this medicinal organ remains insufficiently characterized, limiting understanding of how anatomical structure relates to metabolite distribution. To address this, we integrated mass spectrometry imaging (MSI), non-targeted LC–MS and targeted LC–MS/MS metabolomics, and reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT–qPCR) analysis to characterize the spatial patterns of phenylpropanoid- and auxin-related metabolites and selected transcripts in SL. MSI and non-targeted LC–MS profiling showed that phenylpropanoid- and flavonoid-related metabolites were preferentially accumulated in the peripheral cortex rather than in the stele, although the stele constitutes the dominant internal tissue of the tuber. Targeted LC–MS/MS further confirmed that representative hydroxycinnamic acids and caffeoylquinic acid derivatives were enriched in the cortex and, for most validated metabolites, in the cell wall-enriched fraction. Notably, spatial metabolomic profiling also revealed a contrasting stele-biased distribution of indole-related metabolites, including indole-3-acetamide-related features detected by MSI and non-targeted LC–MS and indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) enrichment validated by targeted LC–MS/MS. RT–qPCR analysis showed that phenylpropanoid biosynthetic genes were generally more highly expressed in the cortex and cell wall-enriched fraction, whereas auxin-related genes showed higher expression in the stele. Together, these results show cortex-biased phenylpropanoid accumulation, preferential association of most validated phenylpropanoid-related metabolites with the cell wall-enriched fraction, and stele-associated IAA accumulation and auxin-related transcript expression. This study provides a spatial framework for understanding metabolite partitioning in medicinal aquatic storage organs and highlights the importance of integrating anatomical, metabolomic, and gene expression information in medicinal plant research.

1. Introduction

Sparganium stoloniferum tubers (SL) are the botanical source of Sparganii Rhizoma, a traditional medicinal material [1]. In a nationwide population-based study in Taiwan, Sparganii Rhizoma was the most commonly prescribed single herb among patients with uterine fibroids [2]. It is also included in Fufang Banmao Capsule, an approved multi-herb Chinese patent medicine whose use in patients with hepatocellular carcinoma has been documented in a real-world study [3]. Phenolic acids and flavonoids are major bioactive constituents of this medicinal material. Previous studies have linked their accumulation to tuber development and stress responses [4,5] but have largely relied on bulk analyses of whole tubers. Consequently, it remains unclear how these metabolites are partitioned between the cortex and stele and whether they are preferentially associated with particular parenchyma-derived fractions. Resolving this spatial heterogeneity would provide a basis for tissue-informed sampling and chemical evaluation of Sparganii Rhizoma.

Phenylpropanoid biosynthesis links aromatic amino acid metabolism to stress-responsive and structural products [6,7], and phenylpropanoid-derived compounds contribute to lignification and cell-wall formation [8,9]. These established roles provide a rationale for examining both tissue distribution and fraction-level association. Mass spectrometry imaging (MSI) enables localized metabolic patterns to be visualized in situ [10,11], whereas LC–MS and gene expression analyses provide complementary chemical and transcriptional evidence. However, to our knowledge, these approaches have not previously been integrated to resolve phenylpropanoid-related metabolites in SL. Thus, the key knowledge gap concerns the spatial partitioning of these metabolites rather than their occurrence in the plant.

SL are particularly suitable for this analysis because the peripheral cortex and the parenchyma-rich central stele differ in environmental exposure and storage-related features. Their anatomical organization is relevant to the aeration adaptations of wetland plants and the source–sink relationships of storage organs [12,13]. Previous work in SL further linked tuber maturation to parenchyma densification, cell-wall remodeling, and starch accumulation [14]. Auxin provides relevant developmental context because it regulates cell expansion and tissue patterning [15,16], auxin biosynthesis contributes to monocot development [17], and tuber formation is governed by interacting developmental and source–sink signals [18]. This background provides developmental context for interpreting the auxin-related patterns that emerged from the profiling data.

In this study, we integrated MSI, non-targeted LC–MS, targeted LC–MS/MS, fractionation of stele parenchyma, and RT–qPCR to characterize spatial partitioning between the cortex and stele and between the cell wall-enriched (CW) and cellular inclusion-enriched (CI) fractions. Stele-biased auxin-related signals first observed by MSI, together with complementary non-targeted LC–MS evidence, prompted exploratory follow-up through targeted IAA measurement and analysis of selected auxin-related transcripts. The integrated analyses revealed cortex-biased phenylpropanoid- and flavonoid-related metabolites, relative association of most validated phenylpropanoids with the CW fraction, and stele-associated IAA accumulation and auxin-related transcript expression.

2. Results

2.1. Anatomical and Morphological Characterization of the Stele, Cortex, and Parenchyma-Derived Fractions in SL

SL exhibited a clearly defined anatomical organization. The frontal view of the tuber showed an overall conical morphology, with small root scars arranged in horizontal rings (Figure 1A). The basal view showed a slightly flattened structure typical of a monocot tuber, with the outer white region corresponding to the cortex and the central pale-yellow region representing the stele, within which vascular bundles were scattered (Figure 1B). The stele occupied most of the internal portion of SL and consisted of densely packed, thin-walled parenchyma cells containing abundant starch granules (Figure 1B–D). In contrast, the cortex formed the peripheral region and exhibited a looser, more aerated tissue architecture (Figure 1B–D). A transverse section was prepared for MSI analysis and used to annotate regions of interest corresponding to the stele, cortex, cell wall-associated regions, and cellular inclusion-associated regions (Figure 1C). MSI data were visualized using a three-dimensional hyperspectral uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP), in which each pixel was encoded according to its metabolite abundance profile. For a more detailed representation of pixel-wise spectral variation, a UMAP scatter plot of the same tissue section is shown separately in Figure S1A, where the distance between points reflects similarity in metabolic features. Clear metabolic differences were observed between the stele and cortex, together with additional heterogeneity within the stele that supported the annotation of cell wall-associated and cellular inclusion-associated subregions for subsequent MSI analysis. Microscopic examination showed that stele parenchyma cells were interspersed with vascular bundles, whereas the cortex exhibited a looser and more aerated tissue organization (Figure 1D,E). Stele parenchyma tissues were subsequently separated into a CW fraction and a CI fraction. The CW fraction contained abundant cell wall fragments (Figure 1F), whereas the CI fraction contained intracellular granules with distinct morphological features (Figure 1G). Together, these anatomical and morphological observations established a structural framework linking MSI-based spatial annotation with subsequent LC–MS analysis of the corresponding enriched fractions.

2.2. MSI-Based Visualization of Tissue- and Region-Level Metabolic Heterogeneity in SL

Spatial segmentation separated the SL section into ten metabolically defined segments, reflecting heterogeneous ion distributions across the tissue (Figure 2A). For each segment, Figure 2B shows the segment mask, a t-statistic profile, and a representative ion image. These views summarize relative associations between ion features and individual segments and do not imply that the displayed ions are unique to those segments. Among these segments, Segment 3 corresponded mainly to the central stele and showed enrichment of a putatively annotated indole-3-acetamide (IAM)-related ion feature at m/z 175.1187, whereas several peripheral segments corresponded to cortex-associated metabolic features. The segment masks, t-statistic profiles, and representative ion images further showed distinct ion-distribution patterns between the stele and cortex. High-dimensional MSI data were further visualized using a three-dimensional t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) projection (Figure 2C). Pixels with similar spectral profiles clustered together (Figure S1B), and the resulting color-coded image revealed distinct metabolic differences not only between the central stele and peripheral cortex but also between cell wall-associated and cellular inclusion-associated regions within the stele. Representative cortex-enriched ion images included 2-feruloyl-6-(4-hydroxycinnamoyl)glucoside at m/z 541.1062, 1,3-dicaffeoylquinic acid at m/z 539.1671, caffeoyl myricitrin at m/z 627.1403, 2-(3,4-dihydroxy-phenylethanolyl)-5-O-feruloyl-xylose-glucoside at m/z 475.1607, N-feruloyltryptamine at m/z 359.1377, and an additional phenylpropanoid-related ion feature at m/z 655.1933 (Figure 2D). These ion images illustrated the preferential accumulation of phenylpropanoid- and flavonoid-related metabolites in the peripheral cortex. In contrast, the IAM-related ion feature at m/z 175.1187 was strongly localized in the central stele, indicating stele-associated accumulation of indole-related metabolites (Figure 2E). In addition to tissue-level differences, representative region-associated ion images further revealed fine-scale metabolic heterogeneity within stele parenchyma tissues. Ion features associated with cell wall regions included 4-(3-hydroxybutyl)-2-methoxyphenol at m/z 197.1001 and lansiumamide C at m/z 266.1543, suggesting preferential localization of phenolic and phenolamide-related metabolites in these regions (Figure 2F). In contrast, ion features associated with cellular inclusion regions included citrulline at m/z 158.0888, glutaminylvaline at m/z 246.0485, Gln-His at m/z 266.1031, N-acetylneuraminic acid at m/z 292.0788, and Tyr-Gly-Gly at m/z 296.1137, indicating enrichment of amino acid- and peptide-related metabolites in cellular inclusion-associated regions (Figure 2G). These MSI results showed ion-distribution differences between the cortex and stele and between cell wall-associated and cellular inclusion-associated regions within the stele. Phenylpropanoid-related ion features were predominantly cortex-enriched and partly associated with cell wall regions, whereas the putatively annotated IAM-related ion feature showed a stele-biased distribution. These observations provided the spatial rationale for subsequent LC–MS-based comparisons between the stele and cortex and between the CW and CI fractions.

2.3. Non-Targeted LC–MS Profiling of Differential Metabolites Between the Stele and Cortex

Non-targeted LC–MS metabolomic profiling was performed on manually separated stele and cortex tissues to investigate tissue-specific metabolic differences. Quality control samples clustered tightly in the principal component analysis (PCA) score plot, indicating high analytical stability and reproducibility (Figure 3A). Both PCA and orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) showed clear separation between stele and cortex samples, reflecting substantial metabolic differences between the two anatomical regions (Figure 3A,B; Tables S1 and S2). Differential analysis identified numerous metabolites with significantly different abundances between the stele and cortex. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis showed that phenylpropanoid biosynthesis exhibited the strongest enrichment, followed by flavonoid biosynthesis, with several related specialized metabolic pathways also significantly represented (Figure 3C). Network visualization of the enriched KEGG pathways and associated differential metabolites further showed close connections among phenylpropanoid-, flavonoid-, and related specialized metabolic pathways (Figure 3D). Relative abundance analysis revealed broad cortex-biased accumulation of phenylpropanoid-derived metabolites. Most representative phenylpropanoid-related metabolites showed higher abundance in the cortex, including cinnamic acid, p-coumaric acid, caffeic acid, 5-hydroxyferulic acid, sinapic acid, cryptochlorogenic acid, sinapoyl aldehyde, and several flavonoid-related metabolites, including luteolin, quercetin, isoquercetin, and apigenin. In contrast, ferulic acid and vanillic acid were more abundant in the stele, whereas sinapyl alcohol showed no significant tissue difference (Figure 3E). This pattern was consistent with the MSI results showing preferential localization of hydroxycinnamoyl-derived metabolites in the peripheral cortex. Hierarchical clustering and subcluster analysis further resolved these tissue-specific metabolic patterns (Figure S2A,B). A major cortex-biased cluster contained multiple phenylpropanoid-, flavonoid-, coumarin-, and catechin-related metabolites, including caffeic acid, sinapic acid, 3-feruloylquinic acid, 5-hydroxyferulic acid, sinapoyl aldehyde, coumarin, quercetin, and catechin-related derivatives. This cluster extended the trend observed for the representative metabolites shown in Figure 3E and indicated that cortex-biased accumulation involved a broader range of phenolic and flavonoid-related compounds. In contrast, indole-related features annotated as L-tryptophan, IAM, indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), 4-O-(indole-3-acetyl)-D-glucopyranose, and indole-3-carboxaldehyde were more abundant in the central stele (Figure 3F), indicating preferential accumulation of indole-related metabolites in the dominant internal tissue. These results showed contrasting tissue distributions, with phenylpropanoid- and flavonoid-related metabolites generally more abundant in the cortex and annotated indole-related features more abundant in the stele.

2.4. Non-Targeted LC–MS Profiling of Differential Metabolites Between Cell Wall-Enriched and Cellular Inclusion-Enriched Fractions

Non-targeted LC–MS metabolomic profiling was performed on the CW and CI fractions isolated from stele parenchyma tissues to investigate fraction-level metabolic differences. Quality control samples clustered tightly in PCA score plots, indicating high analytical stability and reproducibility of the dataset. PCA analysis showed clear separation between CW and CI fractions along the first principal component, reflecting substantial differences in their metabolic profiles (Figure 4A; Table S3). This separation was further confirmed by OPLS-DA, indicating robust discrimination between the two fractions (Figure 4B; Table S4). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed that differential metabolites between the CW and CI fractions were enriched in multiple metabolic pathways, with phenylpropanoid biosynthesis showing the strongest enrichment (Figure 4C). These pathways were mainly associated with specialized metabolism, carbohydrate metabolism, amino acid metabolism, and lipid-related metabolism, indicating distinct fraction-level metabolic differentiation. To further resolve the metabolic organization underlying these pathway-level differences, relative abundance analysis was performed to evaluate the distribution patterns of selected differential metabolites between the two fractions (Figure 4D). Several phenylpropanoid-related metabolites, including 2-hydroxycinnamic acid, p-coumaric acid, caffeic acid, ferulic acid, sinapoyl aldehyde, and sinapyl alcohol, showed significantly higher relative abundance in the CW fraction than in the CI fraction, whereas 5-hydroxyferulic acid showed no significant difference between the two fractions. These patterns support the preferential association of multiple phenylpropanoid-derived metabolites with the CW fraction. Hierarchical clustering and subcluster analysis further resolved the fraction-specific accumulation patterns of differential metabolites (Figure S3A,B). A major CW-biased group contained phenylpropanoid- and flavonoid-related metabolites such as caffeic acid, p-coumaraldehyde, sinapoyl aldehyde, coumarin, 3-feruloylquinic acid, and isosilybin, whereas other subclusters showed CI-biased or mixed patterns. These results showed distinct metabolite profiles between the CW and CI fractions, with multiple phenylpropanoid-derived metabolites more abundant in the CW fraction and other metabolites showing CI-biased or mixed patterns.

2.5. Targeted LC–MS/MS Validation of Tissue- and Fraction-Specific Metabolite Accumulation

Targeted LC–MS/MS analysis was performed to validate the tissue- and fraction-specific accumulation patterns of key metabolites identified by MSI and non-targeted metabolomics. IAA was significantly more abundant in the stele than in the cortex, supporting stele-biased accumulation of indole-related metabolites in SL (Figure 5A). However, IAM was not detected under the present targeted LC–MS/MS conditions because no chromatographic peak met both the retention-time and qualifier-ion-ratio criteria. Targeted quantification further showed that several phenylpropanoid-related metabolites were significantly enriched in the cortex relative to the stele (Figure 5B). These included the hydroxycinnamic acids p-coumaric acid, caffeic acid, ferulic acid, and sinapic acid, together with the caffeoylquinic acid derivatives chlorogenic acid, neochlorogenic acid, and cryptochlorogenic acid. This tissue-level pattern supported the preferential accumulation of phenylpropanoid-related metabolites in the cortex and was broadly consistent with the MSI and non-targeted LC–MS results. Further fraction-resolved analysis showed that most of these metabolites were also significantly enriched in the CW fraction relative to the CI fraction (Figure 5C). This pattern was observed for p-coumaric acid, ferulic acid, sinapic acid, chlorogenic acid, neochlorogenic acid, and cryptochlorogenic acid, whereas caffeic acid showed no significant difference between the two fractions. These results indicate that the association of phenylpropanoid-related metabolites with the cell wall-enriched fraction is metabolite-dependent rather than uniformly shared by all validated compounds. Overall, targeted LC–MS/MS confirmed higher IAA abundance in the stele and higher abundances of selected phenylpropanoid-related metabolites in the cortex. Most of the latter were also more abundant in the CW fraction, although the fraction-level pattern varied among metabolites.

2.6. RT–qPCR Analysis of Phenylpropanoid Biosynthetic and Auxin-Related Gene Expression

The phenylpropanoid biosynthetic pathway illustrated in Figure 6A was reconstructed based on transcriptomic evidence from SL. Only enzymatic steps supported by detectable gene expression were included in this model, thereby providing a species-specific metabolic framework for SL. Although the KEGG phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway assigns the direct conversion from p-coumaric acid to caffeic acid to coumarate 3-hydroxylase (C3H), no corresponding C3H transcript was identified in SL. Therefore, this step was reconstructed through an alternative route involving shikimate or quinate ester intermediates. In this reconstructed pathway, p-coumarate-related intermediates are converted toward caffeate-related derivatives through the coordinated action of shikimate O-hydroxycinnamoyl transferase (HCT), p-coumaroyl shikimate/quinate 3′-hydroxylase (C3′H), and caffeoyl shikimate esterase (CSE), consistent with the transcriptomic evidence detected in SL. Phylogenetic analyses were used to support the functional annotation of candidate genes involved in indole-derived auxin biosynthesis, transport, and signaling. Phylogenetic trees for the auxin-related candidate genes are shown in Figures S4–S7. These phylogenetic results were considered together with KEGG annotation and expression profiles during the selection of representative auxin-related genes for RT–qPCR validation. RT–qPCR analysis revealed gene-specific expression patterns among the selected phenylpropanoid-related genes. Phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL), cinnamate 4-hydroxylase (C4H), 4-coumarate:CoA ligase (4CL), HCT, C3′H, and ferulate 5-hydroxylase (F5H) were more highly expressed in the cortex than in the stele, whereas CSE and caffeic acid O-methyltransferase (COMT) showed the opposite pattern (Figure 6B). Overall, the expression profile of the selected phenylpropanoid-related genes was predominantly cortex-biased and was broadly consistent with the preferential accumulation of phenylpropanoid-related metabolites in the cortex. At the fraction level, most examined phenylpropanoid-related transcripts were recovered at higher abundance from the CW fraction than from the CI fraction, although gene-specific variation was observed (Figure 6C). This overall pattern was consistent with the preferential association of multiple phenylpropanoid-related metabolites with the CW fraction. For indole-derived auxin metabolism, the schematic in Figure 6D summarizes the putative AMI-mediated conversion of IAM to IAA, together with the downstream auxin transport and signaling components selected for RT–qPCR validation. Genes associated with indole-derived auxin biosynthesis, transport, and signaling, including amidase (AMI), auxin resistant 1 (AUX1), auxin/indole-3-acetic acid (AUX/IAA) family members, and small auxin-up RNA (SAUR) family members, showed higher expression in stele tissues than in cortex tissues (Figure 6E). This expression pattern was broadly consistent with the stele-biased indole-related metabolite profile, including the IAM-related feature visualized by MSI, features annotated as IAM and IAA by non-targeted LC–MS, and the enrichment of IAA confirmed by targeted LC–MS/MS. Overall, most selected phenylpropanoid-related genes showed higher expression in the cortex, and their transcripts were generally recovered at higher abundance from the CW fraction than from the CI fraction, although gene-specific variation was observed. The selected AMI, AUX1, AUX/IAA, and SAUR genes showed higher expression in the stele than in the cortex.

3. Discussion

Phenolic acids and flavonoids have long been regarded as major bioactive constituents of SL, and previous studies have linked these compounds to phenylpropanoid- and flavonoid-related defense responses during tuber development and stress exposure [4,5]. By integrating MSI, non-targeted LC–MS, targeted LC–MS/MS, and RT–qPCR, we resolved metabolite and gene expression patterns across anatomical tissues and parenchyma-derived fractions. The principal finding was a cortex-biased distribution of phenylpropanoid- and flavonoid-related metabolites, with most targeted phenylpropanoid-related metabolites also enriched in the CW fraction. In contrast, stele-associated IAA accumulation and higher expression of selected auxin-related genes were observed. Together, these findings show contrasting spatial patterns at the tissue and fraction levels.

The cortex is an environment-facing tissue with a looser, more aerated architecture than the stele. In aquatic plants, cortical aeration facilitates gas exchange under oxygen limitation [19], and specialized metabolites often accumulate in spatially restricted tissues involved in local defense or environmental interactions [20,21,22]. The observed cortex enrichment may therefore represent a localized defense-related metabolic domain, consistent with earlier evidence that phenylpropanoid and flavonoid metabolism in SL responds to development and stress [4,5]. Importantly, the three analytical platforms supported this interpretation at complementary levels rather than by detecting an identical compound set. MSI visualized mainly putatively annotated conjugated derivatives, non-targeted LC–MS covered a broader pool of free hydroxycinnamic acids, flavonoids, and monolignol-related intermediates, and targeted LC–MS/MS quantified selected hydroxycinnamic acids and caffeoylquinic acid derivatives. Their agreement lies in the shared cortex-biased pattern, not in compound-by-compound overlap.

Preferential phenolic accumulation in the cortex raises the possibility that expansion of the starch-rich stele during growth contributes to the decline in whole-tuber phenolic content [4,5,14]. This dilution hypothesis is consistent with growth–defense and source–sink coordination [23,24,25], although it was not directly tested here. The stele also exhibits coordinated cell-wall and starch maturation in its parenchyma [14], and the observed indole-related distribution represents a distinct metabolite pattern within this internal storage tissue. Evidence for this pattern was again platform-dependent. MSI detected a putatively annotated IAM-related feature, non-targeted LC–MS detected features annotated as IAM and IAA, and targeted LC–MS/MS confirmed IAA enrichment but did not detect IAM. Accordingly, the IAM assignments remain putative. Together, the confirmed IAA enrichment and higher expression of selected auxin-related genes show stele-associated IAA accumulation and auxin-related transcript expression. These patterns may be relevant to parenchyma development, tissue expansion, or storage-organ formation [15,16,17,26,27], but this possibility remains a hypothesis requiring direct functional validation.

At the fraction level, non-targeted and targeted LC–MS showed preferential association of several phenylpropanoid-derived metabolites with the CW fraction. Targeted analysis confirmed this pattern for p-coumaric acid, ferulic acid, sinapic acid, chlorogenic acid, neochlorogenic acid, and cryptochlorogenic acid, whereas caffeic acid did not differ significantly between fractions. The CW and CI fractions are operationally enriched samples rather than purified cellular compartments, and some cross-contamination during preparation cannot be excluded. Their differences therefore indicate relative fraction association and should not be interpreted as exclusive subcellular localization. Fraction-resolved RT–qPCR results likewise reflect transcript abundance recovered from these enriched preparations rather than transcription occurring within the cell wall or cellular inclusions. Nevertheless, the enrichment of hydroxycinnamate-related metabolites in the CW fraction is consistent with the occurrence of ferulate and p-coumarate in monocot cell walls and with their roles in lignin–carbohydrate associations and wall assembly [9,28]. Direct analysis of wall-bound conjugates will be required to establish chemical incorporation into wall polymers.

RT–qPCR provided transcriptional support for these spatial patterns. Most selected phenylpropanoid-related genes were more highly expressed in the cortex and, at the fraction level, in the CW fraction, whereas AMI, AUX1, AUX/IAA, and SAUR genes were more highly expressed in the stele. The phenylpropanoid scheme was reconstructed from SL transcriptomic evidence. No C3H transcript was identified, so conversion toward caffeate-related derivatives was represented by a route supported by the detected HCT, C3′H, and CSE transcripts. These expression patterns are consistent with the metabolite distributions, but they do not establish enzyme activity, metabolic flux, or direct regulatory relationships.

Together, these results show contrasting tissue- and fraction-associated patterns in SL, including cortex-biased distributions of phenylpropanoid- and flavonoid-related metabolites, stele-associated IAA accumulation and auxin-related transcript expression, and preferential association of selected phenylpropanoids with the CW fraction. Sparganii Rhizoma is prepared from the whole tuber, and its bulk chemical profile may therefore reflect both the relative proportions of cortex and stele and their distinct metabolite compositions. These findings may inform future tissue-informed sampling and chemical quality evaluation, although their relevance to pharmacological efficacy and developmental function remains to be established.

4. Materials and Methods

4.1. Plant Materials and Sample Preparation

SL were collected from the Botanical Garden of Nanjing University of Chinese Medicine in December 2024, during the pharmacopoeial harvest period for Sparganii Rhizoma. The plant material was authenticated at Nanjing University of Chinese Medicine, and voucher specimens were deposited in the Herbal Medicine Specimen Museum under voucher number YC20241201. After removal of adhering soil and roots, SL samples of uniform size and similar developmental status were selected for subsequent analyses. For MSI, SL were transversely sectioned and subjected to microscopic observation, mass spectrometry imaging, and region-of-interest annotation to ensure spatial correspondence between anatomical structures and metabolite distributions. Based on the anatomical organization of SL, each transverse section was divided into the stele and cortex. Parenchyma regions within the stele were further annotated as cell wall-associated regions and cellular inclusion-associated regions. For fraction-resolved LC–MS metabolomic analysis and RNA extraction, the peripheral cortex was carefully separated using a blade, and the remaining central tissue was collected as the stele. The isolated stele was transversely sectioned at a thickness of 10 μm. The sections were washed three times with 1 mL of double-distilled water per wash. After washing, the residual section material was collected as the CW fraction. The washing suspensions from the three washes were pooled and centrifuged at 500× g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the resulting pellet was collected as the CI fraction. The morphological characteristics of the CW and CI fractions were examined using an Axio Scope.A1 microscope (Zeiss, Jena, Germany). The cortex, stele, CW fraction, and CI fraction designated for non-targeted LC–MS metabolomic analysis were freeze-dried and stored at −80 °C until extraction. Separate aliquots designated for RNA extraction and targeted LC–MS/MS analysis were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C without lyophilization. Samples used for RNA extraction were ground into a fine powder under liquid nitrogen before RNA isolation, whereas samples used for targeted LC–MS/MS analysis were weighed and analyzed on a fresh-weight basis.

4.2. Mass Spectrometry Imaging and Analysis

SL were embedded in 2% carboxymethyl cellulose sodium (Aladdin, Shanghai, China). Tissue sections mounted on indium tin oxide (ITO)-coated glass slides were sprayed using a TM-Sprayer (HTX Technologies, Chapel Hill, NC, USA) with 15 mg/mL 2,5-dihydroxybenzoic acid (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) dissolved in acetonitrile/water (9:1, v/v). The sprayer temperature was set to 60 °C, with a flow rate of 0.12 mL/min and a pressure of 8 psi. A total of 25 matrix passes were applied to the slides, with 5 s of drying time between each pass. MALDI timsTOF MSI experiments were performed on a prototype Bruker timsTOF flex MS system (Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany) equipped with a 10 kHz smartbeam 3D laser. Laser power was set to 80% and then fixed throughout the whole experiment. The mass spectra were acquired in positive mode. The mass spectral data were acquired over a mass range from m/z 50–1300. The imaging spatial resolution was set to 5 μm for the tissue, and each spectrum consisted of 400 laser shots. MALDI mass spectra were normalized with the root mean square (RMS), and the signal intensity in each image was shown as the normalized intensity. MS/MS fragmentation spectra acquired on the timsTOF fleX system were used to support putative structural annotation of representative MSI ion features. Region-of-interest (ROI) segmentation was performed based on anatomical structures and MSI signal patterns. To further characterize the metabolic architecture, spatial segmentation analysis was conducted using the spatially aware nearest shrunken centroids clustering method implemented in the R package Cardinal (v3.0.1), with a neighborhood smoothing radius of r = 2 and s = 0. This allowed pixels with similar spectral profiles to be categorized into distinct clusters to guide ROI selection. For each spatial segment, a t-statistic profile was generated to show the relative association of individual ion features with that segment. The same ion feature could therefore be represented in the profiles of more than one segment. Additionally, non-linear dimensionality reduction was applied to the high-dimensional MSI data. t-SNE was performed using Python 3 with scikit-learn (v1.2.2), following mean centering and initial PCA to preserve local data features. UMAP was also implemented in Python 3 using umap-learn (v0.5.3) to retain both local and global structures while enhancing computational efficiency. For both methods, the first three embedding dimensions were mapped to the RGB color channels to generate hyperspectral visualizations of the tissue sections. Furthermore, cell wall-associated and cellular inclusion-associated regions within stele parenchyma were annotated as additional regions of interest. Representative ROI-associated ion features were exported for downstream comparison and used to guide tissue selection for LC–MS-based metabolomic analysis.

4.3. Non-Targeted LC–MS Metabolomic Analysis

Approximately 100 mg of freeze-dried sample was extracted with 400 μL of methanol/water extraction solution (4:1, v/v) containing L-2-chlorophenylalanine (Adamas-beta, Shanghai, China) as an internal standard at a final concentration of 0.02 mg/mL. Samples were homogenized using a Wonbio-96c frozen tissue grinder (Wanbo, Shanghai, China) for 6 min at −10 °C and 50 Hz, followed by low-temperature ultrasonic extraction using an ultrasonic bath for 30 min at 5 °C and 40 kHz. The extracts were kept at −20 °C for 30 min and then centrifuged at 13,000× g for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was transferred to injection vials for LC–MS analysis. LC–MS analysis was conducted at Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) using a UHPLC system coupled to a Q Exactive HF-X mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Chromatographic separation was performed on an ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm i.d., 1.7 μm; Waters Corporation, Milford, MA, USA). Mobile phase A consisted of water/acetonitrile (98:2, v/v) containing 0.1% formic acid, and mobile phase B consisted of acetonitrile containing 0.1% formic acid. Acetonitrile was purchased from Fisher Chemical (Pittsburgh, PA, USA), and formic acid was purchased from CNW (Düsseldorf, Germany). The flow rate was set to 0.40 mL/min, the column temperature was maintained at 40 °C, and the injection volume was 3 μL. The gradient elution program was as follows: 0–0.5 min, 98% A and 2% B; 0.5–7.5 min, 98–65% A and 2–35% B; 7.5–13.0 min, 65–5% A and 35–95% B; 13.0–14.4 min, 5% A and 95% B; 14.4–14.5 min, 5–98% A and 95–2% B; and 14.5–16.0 min, 98% A and 2% B. The Q Exactive HF-X mass spectrometer was equipped with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source and operated in both positive and negative ion modes. The optimized MS parameters were as follows: sheath gas flow rate, 50 arb; auxiliary gas flow rate, 13 arb; heater temperature, 450 °C; capillary temperature, 320 °C; spray voltage, 3.5 kV in positive ion mode and −3.0 kV in negative ion mode; S-lens RF level, 40; and stepped normalized collision energy, 20, 40, and 60%. Data were acquired over a mass range of m/z 70–1050, with resolutions of 70,000 for full MS and 17,500 for MS/MS. Each group comprised six biological replicates.

The raw LC–MS data were preprocessed using Progenesis QI software (v3.0; Waters Corporation, Milford, MA, USA), and a three-dimensional data matrix in CSV format was exported. The matrix included sample information, metabolite names, and mass spectral response intensities. Internal standard peaks and known false-positive peaks, including noise, column bleed, and reagent-derived peaks, were removed from the data matrix, followed by redundancy removal and peak merging. Metabolites were annotated by searching against the in-house plant-specific metabolite database of Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). These database-based assignments were treated as putative annotations. The resulting data matrices were merged, deduplicated, and uploaded to the Majorbio Cloud Platform (Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) for subsequent data analysis. Data preprocessing was performed as follows. Metabolic features detected in at least 80% of samples in any group were retained. After filtering, missing values were imputed using the minimum value in the data matrix, and peak intensities were normalized to the total ion intensity using sum normalization. Variables in quality control (QC) samples with a relative standard deviation (RSD) greater than 30% were removed, and the resulting data were log10-transformed to generate the final data matrix for subsequent analysis. Multivariate statistical analysis was performed using the R package ropls (v1.6.2). PCA and OPLS-DA were conducted, and sevenfold cross-validation was used to evaluate model stability. Metabolites with variable importance in projection (VIP) values > 1 and p values < 0.05, as determined by Student’s t-test, were considered significantly different metabolites. Differential metabolites between the two groups were mapped to biochemical pathways based on the KEGG database for metabolic enrichment and pathway analysis. These metabolites were classified according to their associated pathways or biological functions. Enrichment analysis was conducted to determine whether specific functional nodes were significantly represented among the differential metabolites. The Python package scipy.stats (v1.18.0) was used for enrichment analysis to identify the biological pathways most relevant to the experimental comparisons.

4.4. Targeted LC–MS/MS Metabolomic Analysis

Targeted quantification of selected metabolites was performed using an AB Sciex ExionLCTM AD UHPLC system coupled to an AB Sciex QTRAP 5500 mass spectrometer (AB Sciex, Framingham, MA, USA). The chromatographic and mass spectrometric conditions were adapted from previously published methods with minor modifications [5,29]. Frozen, non-lyophilized samples were accurately weighed to 200 mg on a fresh-weight basis and extracted with 1 mL of 80% methanol containing 0.1% formic acid. Samples were sonicated in an ice bath for 30 min at 40 kHz, incubated at −20 °C for 30 min, and centrifuged at 13,000× g for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was transferred to LC–MS/MS vials. For IAM and IAA, chromatographic separation was conducted on an Agilent ZORBAX RRHT SB-Aq column (3.0 mm I.D. × 100 mm, 1.8 µm; Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA) with mobile phases consisting of methanol (solvent A) and 0.1% formic acid in water (solvent B). The solvent gradient changed according to the following conditions: from 0 to 1 min, 95% B; from 1 to 9 min, 95% B to 40% B; from 9 to 11 min, 40% B to 5% B; from 11 to 13 min, 5% B; from 13 to 13.2 min, 5% B to 95% B; from 13.2 to 15 min, 95% B for system equilibration. For p-coumaric acid, caffeic acid, ferulic acid, sinapic acid, chlorogenic acid, neochlorogenic acid, and cryptochlorogenic acid, chromatographic separation was conducted on an Athena UHPLC C18 column (3.0 mm I.D. × 150 mm, 1.8 µm; CNW, Düsseldorf, Germany) with mobile phases consisting of methanol (solvent A) and 0.1% formic acid in 5 mM ammonium acetate (solvent B). The solvent gradient changed according to the following conditions: from 0 to 5 min, 98% B; from 5 to 10 min, 98% B to 87% B; from 10 to 12 min, 87% B; from 12 to 17 min, 87% B to 75% B; from 17 to 25 min, 75% B to 67% B; from 25 to 27 min, 67% B to 65% B; from 27 to 29 min, 65% B to 50% B; from 29 to 31 min, 50% B to 5% B; from 31 to 36 min, 5% B; from 36 to 36.1 min, 5% B to 98% B; from 36.1 to 40 min, 98% B for system equilibration.

The sample injection volume was 5 µL, and the flow rate was set to 0.3 mL/min. The column temperature was maintained at 40 °C. During analysis, all samples were stored at 8 °C. The mass spectrometer was equipped with an electrospray ionization source and operated in either positive or negative ion mode. The optimized conditions in positive ion mode were as follows: CUR 20 psi, TEM 500 °C, GS1 40 psi, GS2 40 psi, ISVF 4500 V, CAD 9 psi, and EP −10 V. The optimized conditions in negative ion mode were as follows: CUR 20 psi, TEM 500 °C, GS1 40 psi, GS2 40 psi, ISVF −4500 V, CAD 9 psi, and EP −10 V. Authentic standards were purchased commercially, and detailed information is provided in Table S5. Multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) transitions and compound-dependent parameters were optimized for each analyte, and the final optimized mass spectrometric conditions are provided in Table S6. Calibration curves were generated using authentic standards, and the regression equations, correlation coefficients, and linear ranges are provided in Table S7. Data were analyzed using SCIEX OS-Q software (v1.4.0.18067). Targeted identification required agreement with the authentic standard in retention time and qualifier-ion ratio. Analytes failing to meet either criterion were considered not detected. Metabolite contents were calculated using the corresponding calibration curves and expressed as ng/g fresh weight. Each group comprised three biological replicates.

4.5. RNA Extraction and Quantitative Real-Time PCR Analysis

Total RNA was extracted from the stele, cortex, CW fraction, and CI fraction using the FastPure Universal Plant Total RNA Isolation Kit (#RC411, Vazyme, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA quality and concentration were assessed by a Nano-300 NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Allsheng, Hangzhou, China). First-strand cDNA was synthesized using the HiScript III 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (#R312, Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Candidate genes related to the biosynthesis or metabolism of selected metabolites were identified based on previously generated transcriptome data, KEGG pathway annotation, sequence annotation, and prior RT–qPCR and phylogenetic analyses performed using the same transcriptomic dataset. Phenylpropanoid-related genes were selected from previously characterized candidate genes [4], whereas auxin-related genes were selected according to transcriptome annotation, pathway information, and their putative roles in indole-derived auxin biosynthesis, transport, and signaling. Each auxin-related candidate transcript was queried against the NCBI nucleotide database using BLASTN (v2.17.0), and representative plant sequences with consistent annotations and relatively high sequence similarity were selected. Sequences were aligned using MUSCLE in MEGA (v11.0.13). Neighbor-joining trees were constructed using Maximum Composite Likelihood distances, incorporating transitions and transversions with uniform rates among sites. Gaps were treated by pairwise deletion, and branch support was assessed using 1000 bootstrap replicates. Gene-specific primers were designed using Primer Premier (v5.00) and are listed in Table S8. Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using Hieff qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (#11202ES03, Yeasen, Shanghai, China) on a QuantStudio 3 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). ACT3 (TRINITY_DN501_c1_g1, NCBI: XP_010912922.1) was used as the internal reference gene. RT–qPCR assays were conducted following established protocols [4], and relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method [30]. Each group comprised three biological replicates, with three technical replicates per biological replicate. Expression patterns of candidate genes were compared among the tissue and fraction samples and integrated with targeted metabolomic data to identify genes potentially associated with tissue- and fraction-specific accumulation of candidate metabolites.

4.6. Statistical Analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical significance between groups was evaluated using Student’s t-test, and p values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses and graph generation were performed using GraphPad Prism software (v9.0).
