Prenatal exposure to per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances and neurodevelopment trajectory: towards early warning via cohort- and metabolomics-based risk ranking
Authors: Chang Gao, Ruonan Li, Hongyan Wu, Zhenzhen Xie, Weitian Tang, Yuanyuan Hou, Lin Tao, Shengmei Zhang, Jia Lv, Tianrui Gao, Xiu-Hong Meng, Maohua Miao, Wei Yuan, De-Xiang Xu, Hong Liang, Yichao Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrenatal exposure to per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) may adversely affect neurodevelopment, but comparative risks and metabolic disruptions remain unclear.ObjectiveTo integrate various levels of evidence and illustrate the relationship between prenatal PFAS exposure and neurodevelopmental trajectories in children.MethodsIn the Shanghai-Minhang birth cohort, maternal PFAS were measured from plasma collected at 12–16 weeks’ gestation. Neurodevelopment was assessed at 6, 12 and 48 months using the Ages and Stages Questionnaire-3 and group-based trajectory modelling was performed. Associations between PFAS (co-)exposure and neurodevelopment were evaluated using logistic regression and mixture model effects. Metabolomic perturbations of PFAS with potential neurodevelopmental effects were analysed in PC12 neuronal cells.FindingsAmong 412 mother-child pairs, higher PFAS exposure was linked to suboptimal development in communication (highest vs lowest tertile: ORPFHxS=2.96, 95% CI 1.05 to 8.35, ptrend=0.064; ORPFOS=3.45, 95% CI 1.27 to 9.43, ptrend=0.012), personal-social (highest vs lowest tertile: ORPFNA=2.41, 95% CI 1.05 to 5.56, ptrend=0.036), and total score (highest vs lowest tertile: ORPFOS=2.32, 95% CI 1.09 to 4.91, ptrend=0.028) across early childhood. Metabolomic analysis revealed disrupted excitatory/inhibitory (E/I) neurotransmission. Integrated toxicological assessment, including exposure risk information, toxicogenomic signatures, bioassay-inferred toxicity and in vitro neurotoxicity profiles, ranked PFOS as the most neurotoxic, followed by perfluorooctanoic acid, perfluorohexanoic acid, perfluorohexanesulfonic acid and 6:2 chlorinated polyfluorinated ether sulfonate. An E/I imbalance-based screening identified 8.3% of children as high-risk for PFAS-related neurodevelopmental deficits.ConclusionsPrenatal PFAS exposure is associated with suboptimal neurodevelopment, particularly in communication and social interaction.Clinical implicationsMaternal exposure profiles during early gestation that are associated with disrupted E/I neurotransmission may serve as early indicators of increased risk for suboptimal neurodevelopment, thereby informing timely preventive interventions.Keywords: Neurodevelopmental Disorders

Background

The foetal origin of the mental health hypothesis, framed under the well-accepted Developmental Origin of Health and Diseases theory, emphasised the critical role of in utero adversities on the psychopathology of children.1 Prenatal exposure to environmental contaminants has been associated with a broad range of adverse neurological developmental outcomes in children.2 3 Among the thousands of exogenous chemicals that have been identified thus far, per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) received considerable attention because of their long half-life and bioaccumulation property. To date, various types of legacy and replacement emerging PFAS have been detected in biological samples collected from pregnant mothers4 and women of childbearing age,5 stressing the need for understanding the impact of in utero exposures to PFAS on the development of children.

Using various neurodevelopmental assessment and/or diagnostic tools for neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs), previous studies revealed the relationships between prenatal exposure to PFAS and increased risk of autism spectrum disorder (ASD) and/or attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder,6,8 lowered intelligence quotients9,11 and other neurobehavioural problems.12 13 Given the rapid and ongoing nature of children’s learning and adaptation, employing repeated measures and regular re-evaluations may be crucial for uncovering potential associations between prenatal PFAS exposure and the dynamic progression of neurodevelopment. To the best of our knowledge, there have only been three studies examining the associations between prenatal PFAS exposure and neurodevelopmental trajectories in children thus far,14,16 which either specifically focused on language development or did not extend the research beyond toddler age. Therefore, a comprehensive profiling of prenatal PFAS exposure, coupled with a longitudinal follow-up on the neurological development of the children, is essential to address this question.

Identifying key chemicals related to suboptimal neurodevelopment in children is essential for subsequent chemical prioritisation. This process supports government decision-making by pinpointing the most hazardous chemicals and guiding regulatory actions. In this context, toxicological data that integrates both epidemiological evidence and animal experimental findings is indispensable. In recent years, researchers have been advocating for developing new approach methodologies (NAMs) in toxicological research to reduce the reliance on animals for sustainable research.17 Computational toxicology with the aid of artificial intelligence, the use of cell cultures and organ-on-a-chip, and high-throughput screening have all contributed to an important step towards the next generation of toxicological research.

Objective

We aimed to integrate various NAMs to conjointly illustrate the impact of prenatal PFAS exposure on the progression of neurodevelopment of children through early childhood for a grounded and evidence-based chemical prioritisation (online supplemental Figure S1).

Methods

Study design and population

Between April and December 2012, pregnant women between 12 and 16 weeks of gestation who attended antenatal services at Maternal and Child Health Hospital of Minhang District in Shanghai were invited to participate in the Shanghai-Minhang birth cohort (SMBC) study.18 In brief, pregnant women who were registered residents of Shanghai, free from chronic diseases and planned to deliver at the designated study hospital were invited to participate. Exclusion criteria were referral to other hospitals, twin pregnancies and abortion or stillbirth. Children of SMBC were followed up postnatally with scheduled home visits at 6, 12 and 48 months by trained personnel to gather information regarding their physical and neurological development. Self-administered questionnaires were collected at each follow-up, gathering the lifestyle and dietary information of the mother-infant pair.

Participating mothers of SMBC provided fasting blood samples at enrolment; plasma was separated and stored at −80°C prior to analysis. Among the 1292 enrolled participants, 67 of whom were excluded due to various reasons, and only 676 participants had surplus fasting blood samples for reanalysis of PFAS (online supplemental Figure S2). Among these participants, 412 children, of whom neurodevelopmental scores assessed by the Age and Stage Questionnaire-3 (ASQ-3) were available at any two time points of the three (6, 12 and 48 months), were therefore included.

Measurement of PFAS exposure

Based on our previously reported method,19 PFAS were extracted from 50 μL of thawed plasma using a mixture of acetonitrile and water (v/v=1/9) followed by a mixture of methanol and acetonitrile (v/v=1/1). PFAS separation and identification were performed using high-performance liquid chromatography coupled with a Triple Quad 5500+ mass spectrometer (LC-MS/MS, Sciex, Framingham, Massachusetts, USA). Procedural blank, blank spiking and matrix spiking tests were performed for quality assurance and control purposes. The limit of detection (LOD) was defined as three times the signal-to-noise ratio. The recovery rates and LODs of all tested PFAS are available in online supplemental Table S1.

Neurodevelopmental assessment and trajectory modelling

ASQ-3 is a widely accepted tool for assessing the neuropsychological development of children in infancy and early life from five domains, including communication, gross motor, fine motor, problem solving and personal-social. ASQ-3 consists of 30 questions (six for each domain); higher cumulative scores indicate overall more advanced neuropsychological development. The Chinese version of ASQ-3 was introduced in 2015, which demonstrated a satisfactory level of performance in regard to internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha reliability coefficient of 0.8), inter-rater reliability and test-retest reliability (Pearson correlation coefficient of 0.8 for both), sensitivity (87.5%) and specificity (84.5%).20 ASQ-3 was performed by the parent (84%–95% by the mother) or other caregiver of the child during a home visit, who was blinded to the exposure status of the participating mother. Among the 412 children included, ASQ data were imputed for 20, 43 and 69 children at 6, 12 and 48 months of age using predictive mean matching, which was performed with 10 multiple imputations based on 20 iteration cycles. The imputation model included PFAS exposure, relevant covariates, and available ASQ data in order to account for variables predictive of the outcome and missingness, while preserving the relationships among variables used in the analysis.

We performed group-based trajectory modelling (GBTM) to describe the neurodevelopmental trajectories of children, which assumes that individuals of the population share similar growth patterns and can be grouped based on distinct underlying trajectories. For each ASQ domain and total ASQ score, we identified two trajectories (low vs high), where the high group was used as a reference for further analyses.

In vitro experiments and metabolomic profiling

Based on PFAS screened through a population study, in vitro experiments were performed to study the neurotoxic effect of screened PFAS exposure on the functionality of neuronal cells through a leave-one-out strategy. Neuronal PC12 cells derived from rat pheochromocytoma were cultured at 37°C/5% carbon dioxide, in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium containing 10% foetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin and streptomycin. The low, medium and high concentrations correspond to the median, 95th percentile and 10 times the 95th percentile value as measured in our study population.

After 48 hours of treatment, the number of live cells was measured with an automated cell counter (JIMBIO FIL, Jiangsu Jimbio Technology Co, Jiangsu, China). Amino acids and their relevant amides (AA) were analysed based on our previously published methods,21 due to their roles as neurotransmitters or precursors, which are fundamental for normal neurobehavioural function. In brief, cells were gently rinsed with phosphate-buffered saline (pH=7) twice and dissociated from the cell cultures with pancreatin. Collected cells were added with 800 μL solvent (methanol/acetonitrile, v/v=1/1) containing internal standard (citric acid-d4, 2 μg/mL) for extraction. Analysis was performed by Triple Quad 5500 LC-MS/MS, Framingham, Massachusetts, USA) coupled with an ACQUITYTM Premier HSS T3 Column (Waters, Massachusetts, USA).

Statistical analysis

Covariate selection

At enrolment, participating women completed a self-administered questionnaire seeking their demographics, past pregnancy experiences, use of nutritional supplementations and depressive symptoms. The Centre for Epidemiological Studies-Depression scale was used to assess depressive symptoms in early pregnancy. Univariate linear regressions were performed to screen for potential confounders, and variables that were statistically significantly related to any of the ASQ domains at any of the time points were further considered. Based on biological considerations and results from the regression modelling, the following covariates were included: maternal age, maternal pre-pregnancy body mass index, parity, maternal education level, monthly household income, prepregnancy passive smoking, prepregnancy folic acid supplementation, early pregnancy depression, paternal education level, sex of child and gestational age at birth.

GBTM fitting

We first attempted to identify the optimal number of trajectories (between 1 and 5) for each ASQ domain and total ASQ score based on quadratic fitting. To ensure the validity of trajectories, there should be no less than 5% of the study population per group with an average posterior inclusion probability (PIP) of greater than 0.7 for each individual group. The optimal number of trajectories was chosen where the Bayesian information criteria (BIC) and sample-size adjusted BIC (SABIC) were smallest, while entropy was closest to one. Entropy is a measure of the precision of modelling, where a value close to 1 indicates the exclusivity of participants being classified into one group. We then attempted linear, quadratic, cubic and quartic fitting to identify the optimal shape of the trajectory based on BIC, SABIC and entropy. Detailed information regarding the goodness-of-fit for model selection, including BIC, SABIC, entropy, proportion per group and average PIP per group is shown in online supplemental Table S2.

Single-analyte and mixture effect analyses

Differences in the demographic characteristics of included and excluded participants were assessed using χ2 and Mann-Whitney U test for categorical and continuous variables, respectively. For measured PFAS, values below LOD were replaced by LOD/√2. A total of 15 PFAS with detection frequency (DF) greater than 40% were natural log-transformed for further analyses. Spearman’s correlation was used to assess the correlations between individual PFAS.

Using a generalised linear regression model, associations between prenatal PFAS exposure and ASQ scores of children at different time points were assessed in both complete cases and in the imputed population. Next, the relationships between prenatal exposure to selected PFAS and the risk of being classified with suboptimal neurodevelopmental trajectories in early childhood were analysed using a logistic regression model, which adjusted for the same set of confounders as in the generalised linear regression model. Multiple comparisons were corrected using the Benjamini-Hochberg method for the neurodevelopmental trajectory analyses. Sex-stratified analysis was conducted for the neurodevelopmental trajectory of the total score.

Natural log-transformed PFAS were further Z-score transformed to ensure the stability of mixture modelling. Considering the potential linear and non-linear associations between PFAS mixture and neurodevelopmental outcomes in children, both Quantile-based G Computation (QGC) and Bayesian Kernel Machine Regression (BKMR) were used. The same set of covariates used in the single analyte model was used for mixture effect analysis. QGC estimates the marginal effect on the health outcome of all mixtures by simultaneously increasing their concentration by one quantile. The effect of individual chemicals in the mixture can be either positive or negative; significant results are found when the effect size of one side outweighs the other. The model was performed using 20 000 bootstraps across four quantiles; the overall effect was referred to as the QGC index. BKMR stands for another tool to assess the joint effect of concurrent chemicals towards shared health outcomes, but with more flexibility in allowing non-parametric modelling. We performed BKMR analysis with 30 000 Markov chain Monte Carlo iteration cycles. Model convergence was confirmed by visually inspecting the trace plot, autocorrelation plots and density plots, and by assessing the Gelman–Rubin convergence statistics (all Rhat <1.05, online supplemental Table S3 and Figure S3). We further calculated group PIP and conditional PIP to assess the relative importance of perfluoroalkyl carboxylic acids (PFCA) and perfluoroalkyl sulfonic acids (PFSA), as well as individual PFAS, towards the total observed effect, respectively.

The relative contribution towards observed effects measured using BKMR and QGC was used to screen effect-driving PFAS for further in vitro evaluations. By multiplying the group and conditional PIP for individual PFAS, the BKMR weight of each PFAS is defined as that divided by the summation of PFAS. The average BKMR and QGC weight was then calculated and normalised on a scale between 0 and 10 for individual PFAS, and the top nine ranking PFAS out of fifteen were further used.

Assessment of neurotoxicity for in vitro experiments

Concentration of AA was corrected by cell numbers, and relative changes of excitatory/inhibitory (E/I) ratio to control and/or mixture were calculated based on absolute concentrations. For further analysis based on principal component analysis (PCA), data were normalised by the median value. Deviation of the neurotransmitter profiling from the control group was used as an indicator of neurotoxicity. Principal component 1 (PC1) scores of all groups were first standardised against the control group and then normalised on a 0–10 scale. All data were pooled together for the relative neurotoxicity, while normalised within the dosage group for the single chemical effect. To further assess the interaction of individual PFAS with the mixture and clarify the direction of the effect, we calculated the standardised PC1 score for each group and its difference from the mixture group at the three dosage levels, then normalised these values on a scale from 0 to 10. An antagonistic effect of PFAS was identified when the PC1 score of the mixture group was lower than that of the mixture excluding the PFAS, whereas a synergistic effect was indicated when the PC1 score of the mixture group was higher. The differences in the abundance of AA were performed by one-way analysis of variance, and enrichment analysis was performed based on the Kyoto Encyclopaedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database; multiple comparisons were corrected by the Benjamini-Hochberg method.

Integration of exposure risk, toxicogenomics, bioassay-inferred toxicity and in vitro toxicity analysis

For chemical prioritisation through integration of data from the four domains, the exposure risk, toxicogenomic, bioassay-inferred toxicity and in vitro toxicity, each (sub)domain was based on a 0–10 scale, where a higher value indicates greater needs for prioritisation. For exposure risk, DF and concentration were rated based on values of the current study, while transplacental transfer efficiency (TTE) were obtained from our previous publication.4 For toxicogenomic analysis, we searched the Comparative Toxicogenomic Database (CTD, https://ctdbase.org/) and identified a total of 12 582 PFAS congeners-related genes and 18 751 unique NDD traits-related genes; the latter overlapped with 461 genes in the DisGeNET database (https://disgenet.com/). An intersection of genes was retrieved from PFAS and NDD traits-related genes, and a chemical–gene–disease interaction network was identified through CTD for toxicogenomic scoring.

The reference dosage used for the bioassay-inferred toxicity domain was sourced from the human biomonitoring health-based guidance value dashboard (https://www.intlexposurescience.org/i-hbm/). The biological half-life of each PFAS was identified from literature for perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA) and perfluorooctanesulfonic acid (PFOS),22 for perfluorohexanesulfonic acid (PFHxS) and perfluoroheptanesulfonic acid (PFHpS),23 for perfluorononanoic acid (PFNA), perfluoroheptanoic acid (PFHpA) and perfluoroundecanoic acid (PFUnDA),24 for perfluorohexanoic acid (PFHxA)25 and for 6:2 chlorinated polyfluorinated ether sulfonate (6:2 Cl-PFESA).26 For chemical–phenotype interactions, based on genes common to both the exposure and the outcome, a total of 651 phenotypes were further identified through KEGG and Gene Ontology for scoring.

The following formula was used to calculate the overall toxicity rating, where D indexes the different domains, and n for the number of subscales within each domain, and i represents the raw value for each subscale.

All the above-mentioned analysis, including trajectory modelling and multiple imputation, was performed with R (V.4.3.2) with the following packages: ‘bkmr’, ‘bkmrhat’, ‘qgcomp’, ‘lcmm’ and ‘mice’. A two-tailed p<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Findings

Dozens of PFAS were highly detected in the study population

There were no statistically significant differences regarding the basic demographic characteristics between the participants included (n=412) and excluded (n=813) from the original study, despite the fact that more of the fathers in the excluded group received a high school degree or below (18.9% vs 25.0%, p=0.021, online supplemental Table S4). Of the included participants, the median age of mothers at conception was 28 years, and the prepregnancy body mass index was 20.2 kg/m2 (online supplemental Table S4). Most mothers were primiparous (87.3%) and attained a college degree or above (77.9%). More than half of the participating children were male (58%), and the population was mostly born at term (96.1%).

The distribution of PFAS exposure burden of the studied population is shown in online supplemental Table S5). No statistical differences in the PFAS concentration between included and excluded participants were found (online supplemental Table S6). PFOA, PFOS, PFHxS, PFHpS, PFNA, perfluorodecanoic acid (PFDA) and 6:2 Cl-PFESA were detected in all study populations (figure 1A, online supplemental Table S5). Strong correlations were observed between several legacy and emerging PFAS, especially among alternative PFCAs and PFOS (RhOPFOS-PFUnDA: 0.73, RhOPFOS-PFDA: 0.79, RhOPFOS-PFNA: 0.71; online supplemental Table S7).

Prenatal PFAS exposure was associated with poorer neurodevelopmental status in children.

Online supplemental Table S8 shows the distribution of ASQ scores (for each domain and total) of individual time points, which remained consistent prior to and after imputation. The total score of individuals was calculated upon completion of imputation. Associations between prenatal PFAS exposure and ASQ-3 composite and total score at 6, 12 and 48 months of age were comparable in the complete case and imputed population (online supplemental Table S9-S11). With the lowest exposure tertile serving as reference, prenatal PFAS exposure was mostly related to poorer development in communication and personal-social domains, as well as in total ASQ score (figure 1B and online supplemental Table S12-S14). Using GBTM, there were 35 (8.5%), 65 (15.8%), 54 (13.1%), 50 (12.1%), 46 (12%) and 55 (13.3%) children being classified as having the suboptimal trajectory for the development of communication, gross motor, fine motor, problem solving, personal-social and total score, respectively (figure 2A and online supplemental Table S2). More boys were defined with suboptimal development in all assessed domains except for communication and gross motor (online supplemental Table S15). Similar to single-time point analyses, prenatal PFAS exposure was mostly related to suboptimal development in communication, personal-social and total scores (figure 2A and online supplemental Table S16). Prenatal exposure to the middle and highest tertiles of PFHxS was both associated with increased risk of low communication development in the first 4 years of life (medium: OR 3.06, 95% CI 1.06 to 8.81; high: OR 2.96, 95% CI 1.05 to 8.35). Prenatal exposure to the highest tertile of three of the five PFAS was associated with an overall compromised development pattern in early life following a dose-response manner (PFHpS: OR 2.68, 95% CI 1.22 to 5.85, ptrend=0.014, false discovery rate (FDR)=0.070; PFOS: OR 2.32, 95% CI 1.09 to 4.91, ptrend=0.028, FDR=0.073; 6:2 Cl-PFESA: OR 2.72, 95% CI 1.24 to 5.98, ptrend=0.012, FDR=0.070), and these associations remained borderline statistically significant after FDR adjustment (online supplemental Table S16). Sex-stratified analysis showed that the effect was more evident in female offspring (online supplemental Table S17).

Neurotoxic risk rating of PFAS through mixture effect modelling

A non-monotonic association between PFAS exposure and suboptimal neurodevelopment trajectory was revealed by the BKMR model (figure 2B and online supplemental Table S18), and the relative contribution of PFSA was greater than that of PFCAs with PIP of 0.7299 and 0.4995, respectively (online supplemental Table S18). Within each group, PFOS and PFHpS, as well as PFNA and PFUnDA, contributed most significantly towards the observed effect. Similarly, through the QGC model, PFHpA, PFNA, 6:2 Cl-PFESA, PFHpS and PFHxS ranked in the top five towards the joint effect on increased risk of falling in the compromised total score trajectory, respectively (online supplemental Table S19).

For the overall neurotoxic risk score rating, relative importance/weight derived from the BKMR and QGC models were averaged out and normalised on a scale of 0–10 (online supplemental Table S20), of which the top-ranking PFHpS, PFNA, 6:2 Cl-PFESA, PFHpA, PFOS, PFHxS, PFUnDA, perfluorohexanoic acid (PFHxA) and PFOA were then used to study their toxicological effect and interactions in an vitro model.

In vitro PFAS exposure causes disruption of neurotransmitter metabolism

The treatment group, dosage and cell counts are shown in online supplemental Table S21). The concentration of AA after 48-hour treatment is available in online supplemental Table S22. Based on PCA, the neurotransmitter profiles of cells treated by PFHxA, PFUnDA and PFHxS were distinctive from those of the control and other PFAS treatment groups (figure 3B). Figure 3C displays The top 20 AA that were differentially abundant between the control and treatment groups, including ureidopropionic acid, l-anserine, S-methyl-l-cysteine, l-aminoadipic acid, 5-methoxytryptophan and creatinine, are displayed in figure 3C (online supplemental Table S23 and Figure S4) and were mostly enriched in the glycine and serine metabolism, methionine metabolism, and phenylalanine and tyrosine metabolism (figure 3D and online supplemental Table S24). Compared with the control, an elevation in the glutamate (excitatory) to γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA, inhibitory) ratio in PC12 cells was observed after treatment with a single PFAS and a mixture of PFAS and removing any one of the PFAS from the mixture would reduce such effect (figure 3E and online supplemental Tables S25 and 26). Deviation in the neurotransmitter profile from the control group was used as an indicator of PFAS-induced neurotoxicity. To assess the relative neurotoxicity of individual PFAS at different dosage levels, the PC1 score of all treatment groups was first standardised against the control group and then normalised on a 0–10 scale (online supplemental Table S27). PFHxA at high dose displayed the strongest neurotoxicity (figure 3F). For most of the PFAS chemicals tested, either individually or in a mixture, the relative neurotoxicity increased with the dosage used, except for PFOA and PFNA, where a U-shaped response was identified (figure 3F). At a population-relevant dose, the lowest dosage, the neurotoxicity of PFHxA ranked top, followed by PFHpS, PFOA and 6:2 Cl-PFESA. It is also worth noting that the neurotoxicity of the mixture is equal to or less than that of most single PFAS exposures, suggesting potential interactions between these chemicals. All PFAS demonstrated antagonistic effects with the mixture, where PFOS and PFHxS were rated highest of the interaction score at low and medium doses, respectively (figure 3G and online supplemental Table S28).

NAM-based neurodevelopmental-related PFAS risk prioritisation

The exposure risk domain considered the prenatal PFAS exposure prevalence and level, as well as TTE and PFOA, which were rated highest for exposure risk due to high DF and absolute concentrations. For bioassay-inferred toxicity, we included biomonitoring data (reference dosage and biological half-life) of the screened PFAS, along with the chemical–phenotype interaction identified through CTD; PFOS scored highest in this domain. PFHxA topped the in vitro toxicity domain due to the highest level of neurotoxicity and moderate interactions with other PFAS. Lastly, for the toxicogenomic analysis based on data sourced from CTD, PFOS outscored the rest of PFAS (figure 4A–E and online supplemental Table S29). Overall, besides the three PFAS that have already been included in the Stockholm Convention, PFHxA and 6:2 Cl-PFESA were next on the top prioritisation list for chemical control using NAMs of this study. Five co-exposure patterns that induced an imbalanced E/I ratio in the in vitro model were used to identify individuals at risk for NDDs using exposure profiling determined in the SMBC study population, suggesting that approximately 8.25% of the population may be at risk (figure 4F and online supplemental Table S30).

Discussion

Adverse in utero exposures and their long-term effects on neurodevelopmental outcomes in offspring have emerged as a significant public health concern. In this study, we investigated the impact of prenatal PFAS exposure on the neurodevelopment of children during early childhood, using repeated assessments of the ASQ at 6, 12 and 48 months. Through mixture modelling, we identified nine PFAS chemicals that drive neurotoxic effects and further explored their toxicities at population-relevant doses in neuronal cells. These PFAS not only disrupted AA metabolism but also exhibited strong interactions with each other. To facilitate the prioritisation of chemicals based on their neurotoxic potential, we integrated in vitro toxicity data from the current study with exposure risk information, bioassay-inferred toxicity data and a comprehensive toxicogenomic score. Through this methodology, we identified the next generation of PFAS chemicals (eg, PFHxA and 6:2 Cl-PFESA) that should be closely monitored for regulatory control and potential elimination or restriction from products used by pregnant women.

Following the mandatory withdrawal of legacy PFAS from the global market, temporal changes in the PFAS exposure level of the global population have been observed. Using groundwater and surface water collected from multiple regions across the world, the PFAS concentration of waters collected from several coastal cities in North America, East Asia and Australia exceeded relevant guidelines.27 Samples for the current study were collected from pregnant women residing in the port city of Shanghai. The reported level of legacy PFAS was nearly 10 times higher compared with that of a previous Canadian cohort study (PFOA: 17.318 vs 1.68 ng/mL) with samples collected at approximately the same time,10 reflecting a regional difference. Another Chinese birth cohort study from Shanghai reported much lower PFOA levels (4.61 ng/mL) in maternal plasma collected 5 years after the restriction of use.16

Emerging evidence highlights sex-specific effects of prenatal PFAS exposure, influencing critical offspring health domains such as obesity susceptibility,28 body composition and adiposity,29 30 pubertal development31 and neurodevelopmental progression.32 Using a trajectory-based analysis method, our finding is in agreement with two previous reports on the associations between prenatal PFHxS exposure and increased risk of the children being classified the suboptimal trajectory for the communication domain using ASQ-3.15 16 Similar to our findings, prenatal exposure to PFHxS was associated with increased risk of falling in the persistently low communication development trajectory (OR 2.55, 95% CI 1.20 to 5.45) in female offspring only, while null effects were observed in boys.15 A recent systematic review with meta-analysis quantitatively synthesised available evidence regarding prenatal PFAS exposure with executive function and IQ in children33. In contrast, pooled evidence from three studies showed that prenatal PFAS exposure was adversely associated with performance IQ in male offspring (βPFOS=−1.56, 95% CI −2.96 to −0.17; βPFOA=−1.87, 95% CI −3.29 to −0.46; βPFHxS=−2.02, 95% CI −3.23 to −0.81).33 Unlike the Bayley Scales of Infant and Toddler Development (BSID) and Gesell Developmental Schedules (GDS), which require administration by trained professionals for formal evaluation of neurodevelopmental stages and delays, the ASQ-3 is a parent-completed screening tool valued for its efficiency, accessibility, and cost-effectiveness and has been widely applied in large cohort studies.34,35 Although ASQ is not a recognised diagnostic tool for NDD, it has been demonstrated with strong correlation with BSID-III in identifying developmental delay in communication.36 One of the largest studies to date, with 2257 mother-infant pairs to date showed that prenatal PFHxS (Q4 vs Q1: β=−2.0, 95% CI −3.7 to −0.2, ptrend=0.02), PFNA (Q4 vs Q1: β=−4.4, 95% CI −6.1 to −2.6, ptrend<0.01), PFDA (Q4 vs Q1: β=−3.9, 95% CI −5.7 to −2.2, ptrend<0.01) and PFUnDA (Q4 vs Q1: β=−3.8, 95% CI −5.6 to −2.1, ptrend<0.01) were negatively associated with language development assessed by BSID at 2 years of age.37 In another Canadian birth cohort, despite low maternal PFOS levels (median 3.63 ng/mL), the association between prenatal PFOS exposure and poorer adaptive behaviour determined via BSID-III at age two was evident.38 Only two Chinese birth cohort studies have assessed child neurodevelopment in infancy using the GDS.13 Zhang et al reported that prenatal PFOA exposure was negatively associated with developmental quotient z-scores in boys (β=−0.327, 95% CI −0.616 to −0.038),39 while Yao et al observed that PFBS exposure was adversely associated with gross motor and adaptive developmental quotients, being more pronounced in boys.13

Non-targeted metabolomic studies suggest that exposure to legacy and emerging PFAS is associated with altered amino acid and lipid metabolism across various bodily fluids.40 The dual role of AA, as neurotransmitters or their precursors and as energy substrates, makes them central to PFAS-related NDDs and the focus of this study. Foetal brain development follows a strict, sequential process from neuronal proliferation, differentiation and migration to synapse formation and pruning. Disruptions at any of these critical stages can result in irreversible damage.41 Neurotransmitters derived from the placenta, maternal circulation and foetal synthesis collectively support early foetal development by facilitating the formation and maturation of synapses and neural circuits.42 Data from both epidemiological studies and animal experiments support the impact of protein deprivation during early pregnancy on the development of schizophrenia in adulthood,43 highlighting the critical importance of AA in normal brain development and psychological diseases. A recent study showed that co-exposure to 17 highly prevalent PFAS-induced neurological alterations in mice through the disturbed metabolism of neurotransmitters.44 In this study, we demonstrated that neuronal cells treated with single or mixed PFAS showed distinctive AA patterns when compared with those of the control group, especially among those AA with neuroprotective potentials, including l-anserine, S-methyl-l-cysteine and 5-methoxytryptophan. A tilted E/I balance in early life is considered the underlying cause of several NDDs later in life.45 Glutamate and GABA are the primary excitatory and inhibitory neurotransmitters, respectively, and their ratio is a widely recognised proxy for E/I balance.46 Our work showed that mixed PFAS exposure disrupted the E/I balance in neuronal cells, which was restored upon their removal. In genetically modified animals that resemble ASD-like phenotypes, researchers were able to alleviate the social deficits through shifting the E/I balance towards inhibitory transmissions.47

There is no universal approach to the chemical prioritisation of different health conditions, as the filtering function of key biological barriers within the human body must not be overlooked. In the context of prenatal exposure and the associated health risks in the offspring, the immature foetal blood–brain barrier means that TTE could directly influence the biodistribution of maternal exposures to the fetus. Although previous research examined the neurotoxicity of a range of chemicals extracted from the blood of pregnant women through non-targeted screening approaches, TTEs of these chemicals were neglected.48 Additionally, biological half-life, rather than environmental half-life, was considered in the chemical prioritisation rating, due to the bioaccumulation and persistence of PFAS chemicals, which are likely to induce long-term health impacts when compared with other substances that can be readily conjugated and excreted. Cell culture-based metabolomic analysis, as one of the NAMs, has been advocated to study the toxicity of common environmental toxicants49 due to its unique strength in deciphering the complex biological process in a high-throughput and cost-effective manner. Considering the low elimination rate and long biological half-life of PFAS and the fact that all measured PFAS can readily cross the placental barrier,4 the population-based doses given are able to better reflect the long-term exposure of the fetus. Given the limited scope of a single study, existing data generated from other studies were also used for the analysis of chemical–phenotype and toxicogenomic interactions to estimate the toxicity of various substances under a broader disease term. However, it must be noted that 6:2 Cl-PFESA and PFHpS were rated 0 for the chemical–phenotype and toxicogenomic analysis due to lack of existing data, rather than indicating an absolute low risk. Especially considering that 6:2 Cl-PFESA was of high exposure risk and the biological half-life of it is an order of magnitude longer than that of other substances, this also calls for further research in relation to the health risks of these substances.

The strength of this study builds on several key aspects. First, repeated measurements and the trajectory analysis allowed for investigating the longitudinal effects of prenatal PFAS exposure. Next, for the in vitro experiment, we used functional alteration, rather than survival of neuronal cells, as the indicator for cell toxicity, which better reflects the nuance and long-lasting impacts the PFAS might have. Lastly, when integrating evidence from different dimensions, we included several key parameters that were overlooked in previous studies for chemical prioritisation in relation to diseases, which provided a more thorough evaluation. However, the findings of this study must be interpreted with its limitations. Maternal plasma samples were collected at a single time point in the early second trimester of pregnancy, which limits the ability to identify sensitive windows of exposure. However, it is important to note that PFAS exhibits small variation over the course of pregnancy. This study defined a suboptimal neurodevelopmental trajectory based on longitudinal repeated measures by ASQ, which is a screening tool, rather than clinical diagnostic standards. Future work focusing on benchmarking it against the clinical diagnostic threshold would be valuable. It must be acknowledged that the sample size of the epidemiological analysis in the current study was relatively moderate due to loss of follow-ups for long periods; multiple imputations were performed to retain the sample size. Although we have included a range of covariates from both prenatal and postnatal periods, it is possible that unmeasured confounding factors were not fully adjusted. Especially considering potential associations between childhood PFAS exposure and incidence of neuropsychiatric disorders.50-52 PFAS doses used in our in vitro analysis were derived from population-based data. However, given the regional exposure hotspot and that the minimum dose corresponds to the 50th percentile of the population, these doses may not accurately represent chronic exposure scenarios in regions with lower exposure profiles. Chemical prioritisation scores obtained under the current scheme included parameters that relied on existing information from public databases, which could potentially induce bias for those newly developed or regionally specific chemicals. However, we used the average score across different domains instead of the cumulative score, which minimises the differences.

Clinical implications

The findings of this study lead to three main implications. First, the use of neurodevelopmental trajectories based on repeated early childhood measurements revealed an association between prenatal PFAS exposure and a persistent suboptimal developmental trajectory. This highlighted the need for longitudinal follow-up and regular monitoring to assess the health impacts of these persistent chemicals. Second, our study demonstrated how NAMs can be effectively integrated with epidemiological evidence in toxicological research, providing a more comprehensive analysis of health risks, which could be applied to other disease models. Finally, the chemical prioritisation strategy considered key factors related to substances and specific health conditions, offering a framework to identify emerging chemicals for closer monitoring and to distinguish region-specific threats, thus enabling more targeted regulatory controls.
