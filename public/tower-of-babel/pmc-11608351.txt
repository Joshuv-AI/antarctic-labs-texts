Self-supervised spectral super-resolution for a fast hyperspectral and multispectral image fusion
Authors: Arash Rajaei, Ebrahim Abiri, Mohammad Sadegh Helfroush
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractHyperspectral-multispectral image fusion (HSI-MSI Fusion) for enhancing resolution of hyperspectral images is a hot topic in remote sensing. An important category of approaches for HSI-MSI Fusion is based on deep learning. The main challenges in deep learning based fusion methods include the lack of training data, poor generalization to various datasets, and high computational costs. This paper suggests a new approach to tackle these difficulties by introducing an innovative technique for HSI-MSI fusion. The proposed method involves training a tiny deep neural network that can reconstruct high-resolution hyperspectral images through spectral super-resolution of high-resolution multispectral images. This method does not require high resolution training data and they are artificially generated based on the spatial degradation model of the input observation images. Therefore, the problems of data scarcity and poor generalization are addressed, and also the computational burden is significantly reduced. After conducting thorough experiments, it was found that the proposed method provides promising results. The source code of this method is available at https://github.com/rajaei-arash/SSSR-HSI-MSI-Fusion.Subject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing, Computational science

Introduction

Hyperspectral images (HSI) contain numerous contiguous spectral channels having narrow bandwidth. They offer a significant amount of detailed spectral data for distinguishing and categorizing various substances due to their high spectral resolution. The extensive spectral resolution of HSIs enables their utilization in diverse fields including agriculture1, surveillance2, target detection3, and more. The spatial resolution of HSIs is hindered by hardware limitations, despite their high spectral resolution. This compromise between spectral and spatial resolution in remote sensing images restricts the usability of HSIs in high level tasks like classification4, semantic segmentation5, object identification6, etc. Consequently, enhancing the resolution of HSIs has become a major concern of remote sensing.

The availability of high-resolution multispectral images (MSI) of a specific area prompts the concept of fusing HSI with MSI (HSI-MSI fusion), aiming to achieve improved spatial resolution in hyperspectral images.

In the last decade, deep learning methods including Deep Neural Networks and Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN) have demonstrated exceptional ability in addressing image processing tasks. They have become widely utilized for enhancing the resolution of images. In comparison to traditional approaches that rely on manually crafted priors for HSI-MSI fusion, CNNs have demonstrated superior performance due to their ability of learning the effective priors automatically.

However, one limitation of CNNs is their reliance on training data, which necessitates large amounts of data for training. This can be a challenge in remote sensing applications where training data is often limited. Additionally, CNNs may have poor generalization due to the specific imaging models and the inherent characteristics of the remote sensing data. Moreover, despite the use of modern GPUs, training deep neural networks takes hours due to the immense amount of training data and the large sizes of these networks.

A novel method namely self-supervised spectral super-resolution (SSSR) is proposed in this article to address the issues mentioned above. This method involves using a tiny deep neural network to estimate the spectrum of high-resolution HSI (HRHSI) pixels from the spectrum of MSI pixels. This method eliminates the need for HRHSI training data. Since the necessary training data is generated from existing data, the problem of poor generalization is eliminated. Moreover, the training and reconstruction time is very short. Our contribution is:

A novel approach for HSI-MSI fusion based on deep learning has been proposed that fuses HSI and MSI at the pixel level.

Related works

When remote sensing platforms are simultaneously equipped with both high-resolution multispectral sensors and hyperspectral sensors, they can capture images at the same time without the need for co-registration7. This feature makes them suitable for HSI-MSI fusion. In the last few decades, several techniques for HSI-MSI fusion have been developed, including methods based on modeling8, matrix9 or tensor10 decomposition, and deep learning11.

Model-based methods involve creating a model by considering prior knowledge and utilizing the observed images. The issue is then resolved either through a Bayesian framework or maximum a posteriori estimation7,12.

Data decomposition methods can be categorized as Matrix Factorization, Dictionary/Sparse based Representation, and Tensor Representation approaches. The CNMF method based on matrix factorization work based on spectral unmixing13. In dictionary-based data representation methods, the fused images are reconstructed by combining elements of a dictionary that was learned from the data14. Tensor representation-based approaches aim to estimate HRHSI by decomposing the 3-D data into a kernel tensor and three dictionaries that represent width, height, and spectral details. These methods utilize different hand-made priors to improve the representation of data15.

Recently, due to the strong performance of CNNs in solving machine vision problems, they are widely applied in HSI-MSI fusion tasks16. One group of studies in this category is based on supervised learning. For instance, the HRMSI is concatenated with upsampled LRHSI, and fed as input to a CNN. Then, the network reconstructs HRHSI by learning the mapping between inputs and fused output images17. To minimize the computational burden,18 employed principal component analysis (PCA) for dimensionality reduction, and the fusion was carried out using the above approach. In Ref.19, 1-D and 2-D convolutions extract spectral and spatial priors, and HRHSI is reconstructed using these priors. In Ref.20, a U-shaped network extracts the latent priors of the LRHSI and HRMSI and uses them for fusion. In another approach21, the fusion is performed by solving an optimization problem that is regularized with the priors extracted by a CNN.

In methods based on supervised learning, it is assumed that HRHSI training data are available and they are used to train the network. But in remote sensing, training data for HRHSI is usually not available. Therefore, unsupervised/self-supervised learning methods are considered to adress this deficiency.

In methods based on unsupervised/self-supervised learning, an encoder-decoder network reconstructs the fused image using the relationships that exist between the observed (LRHSI/HRMSI) and the target (HRHSI) images. For instance, in Ref.22, three coupled autoencoders were employed to extract endmembers and abundances from LRHSI and HRMSI images using linear mixture model for unmixing. Additionally, the spatial and spectral degradation models were estimated, and the extracted information was then fused to perform image fusion using an unsupervised approach. In Ref.23, two coupled autoencoders performed unmixing of LRHSI and HRMSI data to extract the necessary information for hyperspectral image super-resolution. The proposed method by Li et al.11 blindly extracts the correlation between LRHSI and HRMSI data in three stages and performs HRHSI reconstruction. This method employs three different neural networks to estimate spatial and spectral degradation models. The proposed method by Gao et al.24 employs two types of losses for feature extraction: self-supervised loss and separable loss. In the self-supervised loss section, it utilizes the images projected onto the PCA-derived subspace to generate self-supervised labels. In the separable loss section, the spatial error and spectral error are treated separately for backpropagation to reconstruct the output image. In Ref.25, fusion is performed by an encoder-decoder network in which special layers are implemented to carry out spectral and spatial degradation according to the degradation models. By changing the strategy, in Ref.26, a CNN with a self-supervised approach is used. Here, the data is artificially generated by Gram-Schmidt Adaptive (GSA) transformation of the input images. consequently, applying spatial and spectral constraints on the output as loss functions are used for network training. Hong et al.27 employed self-supervised decoupled-and-coupled networks to progressively fuse spatial and spectral information of images at the sub-pixel level. This method utilizes spectral unmixing and linear mixture model as prior knowledge to extract sub-pixel similarities and differences between LRHSI and HRMSI. In the method proposed by Wang et al.28, prior knowledge is obtained by representing LRHSI in a subspace and using the first eigen-image to train a deep network in a self-supervised manner. This prior knowledge is then utilized as a regularizer in solving the inverse problem, which is solved using the ADMM algorithm.

In summary, supervised learning-based methods achieve high performance but require labeled training data, which can be scarce in remote sensing applications. On the other hand, unsupervised/self-supervised learning-based methods do not require labeled training data but rely on prior knowledge, such as spatial-spectral degradation models or linear mixing model (LMM) for spectral unmixing. These methods can be effective when the prior knowledge is valid, but they may fail otherwise.

We have implemented a novel self-supervised approach to address the scarcity of labeled training data. Our approach employs a lightweight deep neural network with less than 10K parameters, making it computationally efficient and fast. The method reconstructs HRHSI in a pixel-wise manner, enabling the full spatial resolution of HRMSI to be utilized for HRHSI reconstruction.

Overview of the proposed framework

Given the scarcity of remote sensing data, a self-supervised approach has been devised to address the inverse problem of equation (2), involving the synthetic generation of data required for neural network training directly from the input pair LRHSI and HRMSI. Synthetic data generation is repeated for each new pair of remote sensing images to be fused.

Figure 1 illustrates the entire process of LRHSI-HRMSI fusion. In the initial stage, a synthetic training dataset is generated using LRHSI and HRMSI images. This dataset is constructed based on the inherent relationship between hyperspectral and multispectral images, considering the characteristics of the acquisition sensors, as detailed in subsection Generation of the synthetic training data. In the second stage, a neural network is trained using the generated data. The network learns the spectral correlation between pixels in corresponding hyperspectral and multispectral images. Through the training process, the neural network acquires the ability to solve the inverse problem associated with equation (2). The third stage entails HRHSI reconstruction. Here, the high-resolution hyperspectral image is reconstructed pixel-wise by feeding the neural network with spectral vectors from the high-resolution multispectral image, which then reconstructs the corresponding spectral vectors of the high-resolution hyperspectral image.

Workflow of the proposed method for HRHSI reconstruction. The fusion process by the proposed method consists of three stages. Stage 1: Preparing data for self-supervised learning. Training pairs are prepared according to the spatial degradation model. j and n are the index and the number of pixels in LRHSI/LRMSI, respectively. Stage 2: Model training. In this stage, the model learns the spectral relationship between HSI and MSI. Stage 3: HRHSI reconstruction using the trained model.

Two key points are worth noting. (1) All aforementioned stages must be repeated for each new fusion task. Unlike conventional supervised methods where network training is conducted once, and the trained network can subsequently fuse new LRHSI-HRMSI pairs, this method necessitates repeating all three stages (i.e. synthetic data generation, network training from scratch, and HRHSI reconstruction) for each new LRHSI-HRMSI pair to be fused. (2) Network training commences from scratch for every new fusion task and no information from previous dataset is retained for use in subsequent fusion. This is due to the distinct spatial-spectral characteristics of different remote sensing datasets. Even datasets captured under identical atmospheric conditions, using the same sensors, and of the same scene but with a time difference of a couple of hours can exhibit varying spectral characteristics owing to changes in solar angle and intensity. In such scenarios, transfer learning does not contribute to performance improvement. Therefore, there is no need to use transfer learning or pre-training in this method. Consequently, for each new LRHSI-HRMSI pair, new training data must be generated, and the neural network weights must be initialized randomly and trained from scratch.

The neural network structure

Deep learning-based supervised fusion techniques effectively balance spectral accuracy and spatial detail. However, their performance is highly reliant on the quality of training data30. In contrast, unsupervised methods, while free from training data (ground truth), often struggle to capture fine-grained textures and spatial details present in multispectral images31. Alternatively, spectral super-resolution (SSR) enhances the spectral detail of input data while preserving spatial information31.

The proposed method in this study utilizes SSR as a tool for hyperspectral and multispectral image fusion. Unlike conventional methods that reconstruct the output image either globally or patch-wise, the proposed method reconstructs the output image pixel-wise (Fig. 1). This allows for maximal exploitation of the spatial resolution of the multispectral image. The advantage of pixel-wise reconstruction is a sharper image with maximum spatial resolution. Conversely, the introduction of noise into the spectrum of the reconstructed image is a limitation of this approach.

Furthermore, a consequence of pixel-wise reconstruction of the output image is that the proposed method does not directly utilize the spatial relationship from neighboring pixels for output reconstruction. However, in the first stage of the fusion process, where synthetic training data is generated, is produced using a linear combination of neighboring pixels (according to a specific pattern that the hyperspectral sensor follows for image acquisition). Therefore, the proposed method indirectly leverages the spatial correlations between neighboring pixels. Please refer to subsection 3.2 “Generation of the Synthetic Training Data.”

The neural network suggested here estimates the spectral vector of an HSI pixel with L elements from the spectral vector of an MSI pixel with l elements . Given the limited sizes of the input and output data, a neural network of relatively small size is sufficient to solve this problem, eliminating the need for huge deep neural networks. During the design and testing stages, neural networks with different dimensions were tested. It was observed that a three-layer network is suitable for this purpose and increasing the network dimensions did not improve its efficiency, as shown in ablation study. Therefore, unlike common methods that utilize deep networks with hundreds of thousands or even millions of parameters, the neural network used in the proposed method is very small, consisting of only a few thousand parameters (Table 10).

The network uniformly increases the spectral channels of input to reach the desired output channels. The number of nodes in the layers are L/3, 2L/3, and L respectively. The mean squared error (MSE), spectral similarity, and mean absolute error were compared as loss function. We employed the mean squared error (MSE) loss function due to its superior performance demonstrated in the ablation study.

The aforementioned datasets were scaled to [0, 1] and the results were used as ground-truth (GT). Similar to most previous studies, the HRMSI and LRHSI are simulated by spectral and spatial degradation of GT, respectively32. To simulate the LRHSI, GT was downsampled using Gaussian interpolation with scaling factor of 8, following the Wald’s protocol. Wald’s Protocol is used to evaluate the quality of fused images when high-resolution reference images are unavailable. It involves: (1) Degrading the originals: The original high-resolution PAN and multispectral images are intentionally lowered in resolution. (2) Comparing the fused image: After fusion, the lower-resolution fused image is then compared to the only true reference available, i.e. the original, undegraded MSI dataset. (3) Synthesis Property: This comparison assesses the “synthesis property”. Ideally, the fused image at the lower resolution should closely resemble the image the sensor would capture at that resolution (if it existed). The HRMSI was simulated by integrating the spectral channels of GT using the spectral SRF of IKONOS multispectral sensor (Fig. 7b).

Comparison with the state-of-the-art methods

The SSSR is compared with seven different advanced approaches, each belonging to a different category of fusion approaches. These approaches include Coupled Nonnegative Matrix Factorization (CNMF)13, Hyperspectral Subspace regularization (HySure)29, Coupled Sparse Tensor Factorization (CSTF)33, Self-Supervised Fusion by Deep Neural Network (SSFDNN)26, Two-Stream Fusion Network (TFNet)34, Fusion via Self-supervised Loss and Separable Loss (SuvLss)24, and Self-Supervised Fusion by Eigenimage to Eigenimsge prior learning (E2E-Fus)28. The CNMF, HySure, CSTF, TFNet, and E2E-Fus codes have been made available to the public. The SuvLss and SSFDNN methods were diligently implemented following the original documentations to ensure a fair comparison. The parameter settings recommended in the original documents were utilized for implementing the aforementioned methods. The fusion was performed using the above methods at scaling factors 4 on the aforementioned datasets. Complexity analysis

Table 10 presents the number of parameters, FLOPs (floating-point operations), and training time for the deep learning models, along with the total fusion time for all methods investigated in this work. The proposed method (SSSR) with 10K parameters exhibits tens of times fewer parameters compared to the two other deep learning-based methods, namely SSFDNN and E2E-Fus, with 480K and 2.4M parameters, respectively. This enables training and fusion of the proposed method using a CPU, while the computations for the other methods require a GPU.

SSSR performs the fusion process in three stages: data generation, network training, and image reconstruction. Each stage takes approximately 0.5, 80, and 5 seconds, respectively, for a high-resolution hyperspectral image (HRHSI) with dimensions of . As shown in Table 10, SSSR achieves a fusion time comparable to traditional fusion methods, while maintaining an accuracy level on par with deep learning approaches (Tables 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5).

Conclusion

SSSR, a novel approach was proposed for HSI-MSI fusion. This approach uses a tiny deep neural network to estimate the spectrum of HSI pixels based on the spectrum of MSI pixels. This method does not require high resolution HSI data, and the data required for training is simulated using the spatial degradation model and therefore, it has no generalization problem. SSSR is very fast and has extremely low computational burden, while its accuracy is comparable to deep learning methods. This method has results competitive with the advanced methods.

This method is not entirely blind and uses a spatial degradation model to generate training data. Not knowing the spatial degradation model leads to a severe reduction in the efficiency of SSSR. Therefore, the estimation of spatial degradation model using the relations that exist between MSI and HSI will be the subject of future work.
