Sharp constants relating the sub-Gaussian norm and the sub-Gaussian parameter
Lasse Leskelä, Matvei Zhukov

Abstract
We determine the optimal constants in the classical inequalities relating the sub-Gaussian norm ‖ X ‖ ψ 2 \|X\|_{\psi_{2}} and the sub-Gaussian parameter σ X \sigma_{X} for centered real-valued random variables. We show that 3 / 8 ⋅ ‖ X ‖ ψ 2 ≤ σ X ≤ log ⁡ 2 ⋅ ‖ X ‖ ψ 2 \sqrt{3/8}\cdot\|X\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq\sigma_{X}\leq\sqrt{\log 2}\cdot\|X\|_{\psi_{2}} , and that both bounds are sharp, attained by the standard Gaussian and Rademacher distributions, respectively.

Introduction

A real-valued random variable
X
is said to be sub-Gaussian if its tails decay at least as fast as those of a Gaussian random variable. Two standard ways to quantify such behaviour are via the
sub-Gaussian norm

\|X\|_{\psi_{2}}\ =\ \inf\left\{K>0\colon\mathbb{E}e^{X^{2}/K^{2}}\leq 2\right\},

and the
sub-Gaussian parameter

\sigma_{X}\ =\ \inf\left\{\sigma\geq 0\colon\mathbb{E}e^{s(X-\mathbb{E}X)}\leq e^{\sigma^{2}s^{2}/2}\ \text{for all }s\in\mathbb{R}\right\}.

It is well known that
\|X\|_{\psi_{2}}<\infty
if and only if
\mathbb{E}|X|<\infty
and
\sigma_{X}<\infty
, and these equivalent conditions are both commonly used as definitions of a sub-Gaussian random variable
[
1
,
14
,
15
]
.
Moreover, there exist universal constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that for every centered random variable
X
,

c_{1}\,\|X\|_{\psi_{2}}\,\leq\,\sigma_{X}\,\leq\,c_{2}\,\|X\|_{\psi_{2}}.

Precise knowledge of these universal constants is important in high-dimensional probability and statistical applications where sharp tail bounds are crucial. However, their exact values do not appear to be explicitly known in the literature. The main goal of this note is to identify them precisely.

Theorem 1.1
.

For any centered random variable
X
,

\sqrt{3/8}\cdot\|X\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq\sigma_{X}\leq\sqrt{\log 2}\cdot\|X\|_{\psi_{2}},

and both inequalities are sharp.

Sharpness is demonstrated by the standard Gaussian and Rademacher distributions, as shown below.

Example 1.2
.

Let
X\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. Then
\sigma_{X}=1
and
\|X\|_{\psi_{2}}=\sqrt{8/3}
, showing that the lower bound in Theorem
1.1
is tight.

Example 1.3
.

Let
X\in\{-1,1\}
with
\mathbb{P}(X=\pm 1)=1/2
. Then
\sigma_{X}=1
and
\|X\|_{\psi_{2}}=1/\sqrt{\log 2}
, showing that the upper bound in Theorem
1.1
is tight.

The optimal upper bound
\sigma_{X}/\|X\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq\sqrt{\log 2}\approx 0.83

in Theorem
1.1
improves upon the best previously known estimate,
to our knowledge, namely
(3/2)^{1/2}(\log 2)^{1/4}\approx 1.12
reported in
[
8
]
.
Earlier, weaker upper bounds include
\sqrt{3}\approx 1.73

[
11
,
13
]
,
2

[
1
,
17
]
,
\sqrt{2e}\approx 2.33

[
4
]
, and
\sqrt{8}\approx 2.83

[
10
]
.

The optimal lower bound
\sigma_{X}/\|X\|_{\psi_{2}}\geq\sqrt{3/8}\approx 0.61
appears implicitly in earlier works
[
2
,
8
,
15
]
, where it can be derived by evaluating specific constants in general sub-Gaussian inequalities. However, to our knowledge, its sharpness has not been previously identified. As demonstrated above, this bound is attained by the standard Gaussian distribution. Other explicit, albeit weaker, lower bounds reported in the literature include
\sqrt{1/6}\approx 0.41

[
10
,
11
,
13
]
,
\sqrt{1/8}\approx 0.35

[
1
]
, and
\sqrt{(\log 2)/8}\approx 0.29

[
17
]
.

Lemma 2.3
.

For any number
s\in\mathbb{R}
and for any probability measure
\mu

on
\mathbb{R}
such that
\int e^{x^{2}}\mu(dx)\leq 2
,

\displaystyle\int e^{{\lvert sx\rvert}}\,1({\lvert x\rvert}\geq K)\,\mu(dx)

\displaystyle\ \leq\ 2e^{-K^{2}/2},\qquad K\geq 2{\lvert s\rvert},

(2.3)

\displaystyle\int{\lvert x\rvert}\,1({\lvert x\rvert}\geq K)\,\mu(dx)

\displaystyle\ \leq\ 2e^{-K^{2}/2},\qquad K\geq 2.

(2.4)

Proof.

Fix
K\geq 2{\lvert s\rvert}
.
Observe that
x^{2}-{\lvert sx\rvert}\geq\frac{1}{2}x^{2}
for
{\lvert x\rvert}\geq 2{\lvert s\rvert}
.
Hence,

e^{{\lvert sx\rvert}}\ =\ \frac{e^{x^{2}}}{e^{x^{2}-{\lvert sx\rvert}}}\ \leq\ \frac{e^{x^{2}}}{e^{x^{2}/2}}\ \leq\ \frac{e^{x^{2}}}{e^{K^{2}/2}}\qquad\text{for ${\lvert x\rvert}\geq K$}.

Because
\int e^{x^{2}}\mu(dx)\leq 2
, inequality (
2.3
) follows by noting that

\int e^{{\lvert sx\rvert}}\,1({\lvert x\rvert}\geq K)\,\mu(dx)\ \leq\ e^{-K^{2}/2}\int e^{x^{2}}\mu(dx)\ \leq\ 2e^{-K^{2}/2}.

Inequality (
2.4
) follows by
noting that
{\lvert x\rvert}\leq e^{{\lvert x\rvert}}
and applying
(
2.3
) with
s=1
.
∎

Lemma 2.4
.

For any
s\in\mathbb{R}
,
\mu\mapsto M_{\mu}(s)

is a continuous real functional on
\mathcal{H}
in the topology of weak convergence.

Proof.

Assume that
\mu_{n}\to\mu_{\infty}
weakly, for some
\mu_{n},\mu_{\infty}\in\mathcal{H}
.
By a Skorohod coupling
[
6
, Theorem 4.30]
, there exist random variables
X_{n},X_{\infty}

defined on a common probability space
such that

\operatorname{Law}(X_{n})=\mu_{n}
,

\operatorname{Law}(X_{\infty})=\mu_{\infty}
,
and

X_{n}\to X_{\infty}
almost surely.
Then
e^{sX_{n}}\to e^{sX_{\infty}}
almost surely.
The random variables
e^{sX_{n}}
are uniformly integrable
due to (
2.3
).
It follows
[
6
, Lemma 4.11]
that

M_{\mu_{n}}(s)=\mathbb{E}e^{sX_{n}}\to\mathbb{E}e^{sX_{\infty}}=M_{\mu_{\infty}}(s).

∎

Lemma 2.5
.

The set
\mathcal{H}\subset\mathcal{P}
is compact in the topology of weak convergence.

Proof.

Let us first verify that
\mathcal{H}
is closed.
Assume that
\mu_{n}\to\mu
weakly for some
\mu_{n}\in\mathcal{H}
and
\mu_{\infty}\in\mathcal{P}
.
By a Skorohod coupling
[
6
, Theorem 4.30]
,
there exist random variables
X_{n},X_{\infty}
defined on a common probability space such that

\operatorname{Law}(X_{n})=\mu_{n}
,

\operatorname{Law}(X_{\infty})=\mu_{\infty}
,
and

X_{n}\to X_{\infty}
almost surely.
Then by Fatou’s lemma
[

, Lemma 4.11]
,

\mathbb{E}e^{X_{\infty}^{2}}\ =\ \mathbb{E}\liminf_{n\to\infty}e^{X_{n}^{2}}\ \leq\ \liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}e^{X_{n}^{2}}\ \leq\ 2.

(2.5)

The random variables
X_{n}
are uniformly integrable
due to (

).
It follows
[
6
, Lemma 4.11]
that
\mathbb{E}{\lvert X_{\infty}\rvert}<\infty

\mathbb{E}X_{\infty}\ =\ \lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}X_{n}\ =\ 0.

Because

By (

2.1.2
Reduction to finitely supported distributions

Because the set of probability measures
\mathcal{H}
defined in (
2.1
)
is convex, we expect the linear functional
\mu\mapsto\int e^{sx}\mu(dx)

to be maximised in the extremal points of
\mathcal{H}
.
An element
\mu
in
\mathcal{H}
is called
extremal

if it cannot be written as a convex combination
\mu=(1-\lambda)\mu_{1}+\lambda\mu_{2}

for some
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\in\mathcal{H}
and
\lambda\in(0,1)
.
We denote by
\operatorname{ex}(\mathcal{H})
the extremal elements of
\mathcal{H}
.
We will apply Winkler’s theorem
[
16
]
to reduce
our optimisation problem to from general probability measures
to probability measures of finite support.
Note that both the original and the reduced feasible set are infinite-dimensional.

Lemma 2.6
.

The set of extremal elements of
\mathcal{H}
equals

\operatorname{ex}(\mathcal{H})=\mathcal{H}_{1}\cup\mathcal{H}_{2}\cup\mathcal{H}_{3}.

Proof.

Using functions
f_{1}(x)=x
and
f_{2}(x)=e^{x^{2}}
, we see that

\mathcal{H}\ =\ \{\mu\in\mathcal{P}\colon\mu(f_{1})\leq 0,\,\mu(-f_{1})\leq 0,\,\mu(f_{2})\leq 2\}.

(2.7)

By applying
[
16
, Theorem 2.1:(a), Example 2.1:(a)]

to the moment set (
2.7
),
we see that all extremal elements of
\mathcal{H}
can be expressed in the form

\mu\ =\ \sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}\delta_{x_{i}},

where
1\leq m\leq 4
,
p_{1},\dots,p_{m}>0
,
p_{1}+\cdots+p_{m}=1
, and the vectors

(1,x_{i},-x_{i},e^{x_{i}^{2}})
,
i=1,\dots,m
, are linearly independent.
By noting that
\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}(1,x_{i},-x_{i},e^{x_{i}^{2}})=0

if and only if
\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}(1,x_{i},e^{x_{i}^{2}})=0
,
it follows that the last requirement is equivalent to
stating that the vectors

(1,x_{i},e^{x_{i}^{2}})
,
i=1,\dots,m
, are linearly independent.
Because these vectors are elements of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, we must have

1\leq m\leq 3
.
In light of Lemma
A.1
, we also see that
for
m=3
, linear independence is equivalent to
x_{1},\dots,x_{m}
being distinct.
The same is obviously true for
m=1,2
, so the claim follows.
∎

Lemma 2.7
.

For any real number
s\in\mathbb{R}
, there exists a probability
measure

\mu_{*}\in\mathcal{H}_{2}\cup\mathcal{H}_{3}

such that

M_{\mu_{*}}(s)\ =\ \sup_{\mu\in\mathcal{H}}M_{\mu}(s).

Proof.

Fix
s\in\mathbb{R}
, and
denote
\alpha=\sup_{\mu\in\mathcal{H}}M_{\mu}(s)
.
By Proposition
2.2
, there exists
a probability measure
\bar{\mu}\in\mathcal{H}
such that
M_{\bar{\mu}}(s)=\alpha
.
By recalling the representation (
2.7
)
and applying
[
16
, Theorem 3.1]
, we see that every probability measure
in
\mathcal{H}
, and in particular
\bar{\mu}
, can be
represented as a convex combination

\bar{\mu}(B)\ =\ \int_{\operatorname{ex}(\mathcal{H})}\mu(B)\,\pi(d\mu)

for some probability measure
\pi
on the set
\operatorname{ex}(\mathcal{H})
equipped with the
sigma-algebra of evaluation maps
\mu\mapsto\mu(B)
,
B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})
.
It follows that

\alpha\ =\ \int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{sx}\bar{\mu}(dx)\ =\ \int_{\operatorname{ex}(\mathcal{H})}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{sx}\mu(dx)\,\pi(d\mu)\ =\ \int_{\operatorname{ex}(\mathcal{H})}M_{\mu}(s)\,\pi(d\mu).

Therefore,

\int_{\operatorname{ex}(\mathcal{H})}(\alpha-M_{\mu}(s))\,\pi(d\mu)\ =\ 0,

and because the integrand is nonnegative, we conclude that

M_{\mu}(s)=\alpha
for
\pi
-almost every
\mu
.
In particular, the set
\{\mu\in\operatorname{ex}(\mathcal{H})\colon M_{\mu}(s)=\alpha\}

is nonempty. We conclude that there exists a probability measure
\mu_{*}\in\operatorname{ex}(\mathcal{H})

such that

M_{\mu_{*}}(s)\ =\ \sup_{\mu\in\mathcal{H}}M_{\mu}(s).

By Lemma
2.6
, we find that

\operatorname{ex}(\mathcal{H})=\mathcal{H}_{1}\cup\mathcal{H}_{2}\cup\mathcal{H}_{3}.

The unique element in
\mathcal{H}_{1}
is
\delta_{0}
. By Jensen’s inequality

M_{\delta_{0}}(s)\leq M_{\mu}(s)
for all
\mu\in\mathcal{H}
,
so that
\mu\mapsto M_{\mu}(s)
is minimised, not maximised, at
\delta_{0}
.
Therefore, we conclude that
\mu_{*}\in\mathcal{H}_{2}\cup\mathcal{H}_{3}
.
∎

The functions

\displaystyle\ =\ \big(e^{sx_{1}},e^{sx_{2}},e^{sx_{3}},\,p_{1}se^{sx_{1}},p_{2}se^{sx_{2}},p_{3}se^{sx_{3}}\big),

\nabla g_{0}(p,x)

\nabla g_{1}(p,x)

The gradients can also be written as

for all

.

We see that

that

,
we know that
\sup_{\mu\in\mathcal{H}}M_{\mu}(s)=M_{\mu_{*}}(s)
for some
probability measure
\mu_{*}
in
\mathcal{H}_{2}\cup\mathcal{H}_{3}
.
Lemma
2.8
implies that
\mu_{*}\notin\mathcal{H}_{3}
. Hence
\mu_{*}\in\mathcal{H}_{2}
.
∎

2.2
Upper bound for binary distributions

Recall the definitions of
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}
in (
2.1
)–(
2.2
).
We denote the sub-Gaussian parameter of a probability measure
\mu\in\mathcal{H}
by

\sigma_{\mu}\ =\ \inf\left\{\sigma\in[0,\infty):\int e^{sx}\mu(dx)\leq e^{\sigma^{2}s^{2}/2}\ \text{for all $s\in\mathbb{R}$}\right\}.

Proposition 2.9
.

\sigma_{\mu}\leq\sqrt{\log 2}
for all
\mu\in\mathcal{H}_{2}
.

Proof.

Fix a
\mu\in\mathcal{H}_{2}
.
Being a probability measure supported on two points, we find that

\mu=p\delta_{x_{1}}+(1-p)\delta_{x_{2}}
with
p\in(0,1)
and
x_{1}\neq x_{2}
.
By swapping
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
if necessary, we may assume without loss of generality that

p\in[1/2,1)
.
Because
\mu
is centered, we find that
x_{2}=-\frac{p}{1-p}x_{1}
.
For convenience, let us parametrise
p=\frac{u}{1+u}
in terms of
u\in[1,\infty)
.
Then
\int e^{x^{2}}\mu(dx)=G(u,x_{1}^{2})
where

G(u,t)\ =\ \frac{u}{1+u}e^{t}+\frac{1}{1+u}e^{u^{2}t}.

In this case we know
[
3
, Theorem 3.1]

that

\sigma_{\mu}^{2}\ =\ \begin{cases}x_{1}^{2}\frac{u^{2}-1}{2\log u},&\qquad u\neq 1,\\
x_{1}^{2},&\qquad u=1.\end{cases}

Assume first that
u=1
. Then

\int e^{x^{2}}\mu(dx)=e^{x_{1}^{2}}
,
so that the condition
\int e^{x^{2}}\mu(dx)\leq 2

implies that

\sigma_{\mu}^{2}=x_{1}^{2}\leq\log 2
.

Assume next that
u>1
.
Denote
\alpha(u)=2\log 2\frac{\log u}{u^{2}-1}
.
Then our claim amounts to verifying that

x_{1}^{2}\leq\alpha(u)
for all
x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}

such that
G(u,x_{1}^{2})\leq 2
.
Because
G
is strictly increasing in its second input,
this is equivalent to verifying that
F(u)=G(u,\alpha(u))
satisfies
F(u)\geq 2
.
We note that
F
is continuously differentiable on
(1,\infty)
, with
F(1+)=2
.
Therefore, it is sufficient to show that
F^{\prime}(u)\geq 0
for all
u>1
.

Observe that

F^{\prime}(u)\ =\ \partial_{1}G(u,\alpha(u))+\alpha^{\prime}(u)\partial_{2}G(u,\alpha(u)),

(2.10)

where

\displaystyle\partial_{1}G(u,t)

\displaystyle\ =\ (1+u)^{-2}\Big(e^{t}+(2u(1+u)t-1)e^{u^{2}t}\Big)

\displaystyle\partial_{2}G(u,t)

\displaystyle\ =\ u(1+u)^{-1}\Big(e^{t}+ue^{u^{2}t}\Big),

\displaystyle\alpha^{\prime}(u)

\displaystyle\ =\ 2\log 2\,u^{-1}(u^{2}-1)^{-2}\Big(u^{2}-1-2u^{2}\log u\Big).

By substituting the above equations into (
2.10
), and simplifying, we find that

F^{\prime}(u)\ =\ \frac{h_{1}(u)e^{\alpha(u)}}{(1+u)(u^{2}-1)^{2}}+\frac{h_{2}(u)e^{u^{2}\alpha(u)}}{(1+u)(u^{2}-1)^{2}},

(2.11)

where

\displaystyle h_{1}(u)

\displaystyle\ =\ 2\log 2\cdot(u^{2}-1-2u^{2}\log u)+(1+u)(u-1)^{2},

\displaystyle h_{2}(u)

\displaystyle\ =\ 2\log 2\cdot(u^{3}-u-2u\log u)-(1+u)(u-1)^{2}.

Differentiation shows that
h_{2}(1)=h_{2}^{\prime}(1)=h_{2}^{\prime\prime}(1)=0
, and that

h_{2}^{\prime\prime\prime}(u)=2\log 2\cdot(6+2/u^{2})-6\geq 0

for
u\geq 1
. Therefore,
h_{2}(u)\geq 0
.
Because
e^{u^{2}\alpha(u)}\geq e^{\alpha(u)}
,
we find by (
2.11
) that

F^{\prime}(u)\ \geq\ \frac{h(u)e^{\alpha(u)}}{(1+u)(u^{2}-1)^{2}},

(2.12)

where
h(u)=h_{1}(u)+h_{2}(u)
.
Differentiation shows that

h(u)\ =\ 2\log 2\cdot\Big(u^{3}+u^{2}-u-1-2u^{2}\log u-2u\log u\Big)

satisfies
h(1)=h^{\prime}(1)=h^{\prime\prime}(1)=0
,
together with
h^{\prime\prime\prime}(u)=2\log 2\cdot\big(6-\frac{2}{u^{2}}-\frac{4}{u}\big)\geq 0
.
Therefore,
h(u)\geq 0
for
u>1
. It follows that
F^{\prime}(u)\geq 0
for all
u>1
due to (
2.12
).
∎

2.3
Concluding the proof of the upper bound

Proof of Theorem
1.1
: Upper bound.

Let
X
be a random variable such that
\mathbb{E}X=0
and
\lVert X\rVert_{\psi_{2}}<\infty
.
Choose a number
K>\lVert X\rVert_{\psi_{2}}
, and define
Y=X/K
.
Then
Y
is a centered random variable such that

\mathbb{E}e^{Y^{2}}\ =\ \mathbb{E}e^{X^{2}/K^{2}}\ \leq\ 2.

We conclude that the probability distribution
\mu
of
Y
belongs to
\mathcal{H}
.

Fix
s\in\mathbb{R}
, and denote by
M_{\mu}(s)=\mathbb{E}e^{sY}
the moment generating function of
Y
.
By Proposition
2.1
, we know that
for some
\mu_{*}\in\mathcal{H}_{2}
,

M_{\mu_{*}}(s)\ \geq\ M_{\mu}(s).

By Proposition
2.9
,
the sub-Gaussian parameter of
\mu_{*}
is bounded by

\sigma_{\mu_{*}}\leq\sqrt{\log 2}
,
so that
M_{\mu_{*}}(s)\leq e^{\log 2\cdot s^{2}/2}
.
We conclude that the moment generating function of
Y

is bounded by

\mathbb{E}e^{sY}\ \leq\ e^{\log 2\cdot s^{2}/2}\qquad\text{for all $s\in\mathbb{R}$}.

(2.13)

By applying (
2.13
) with input
sK
,
it follows that for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
,

\mathbb{E}e^{sX}\ =\ \mathbb{E}e^{(sK)Y}\ \leq\ e^{\log 2\cdot(sK)^{2}/2}\ =\ e^{(\sqrt{\log 2}\,K)^{2}s^{2}/2}

Because the above bound holds for all
K>\lVert X\rVert_{\psi_{2}}
, we conclude that

\sigma_{X}\leq\sqrt{\log 2}\,\lVert X\rVert_{\psi_{2}}
.
∎

3
Proof of lower bound

For completeness, we include a short proof of the lower bound in Theorem
1.1
, even though the result is implicit in earlier works
[
2
,
8
,
15
]
and can be obtained, e.g., by evaluating
[
2
, Lemma 1.6]
or by tracking constants in the proof of
[
15
, Theorem 2.6]
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
: Lower bound.

Fix a number
K>\sqrt{2}\sigma_{X}
,
and recall that
the moment generating function
of the standard Gaussian distribution with
the probability density function

\phi(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-x^{2}/2}

is given by

e^{t^{2}/2}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{tu}\phi(u)du.

By substituting
t=\sqrt{2}X/K
, taking expectations, and
applying Fubini’s theorem,
we find that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}e^{X^{2}/K^{2}}\ =\ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}e^{(u\sqrt{2}/K)X}\phi(u)\,du.

The definition of the sub-Gaussian parameter implies that

\mathbb{E}e^{(u\sqrt{2}/K)X}\leq e^{\sigma_{X}^{2}u^{2}/K^{2}}.

Observe also that

e^{\sigma_{X}^{2}u^{2}/K^{2}}\phi(u)=\phi(u/\tau)

where
\tau=(1-2\sigma_{X}^{2}/K^{2})^{-1/2}
.
It follows that

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}e^{(u\sqrt{2}/K)X}\phi(u)\,du\leq\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\phi(u/\tau)\,du=\tau,

so that

\mathbb{E}e^{X^{2}/K^{2}}\ \leq\ (1-2\sigma_{X}^{2}/K^{2})^{-1/2}.

This above inequality reveals that

\mathbb{E}e^{X^{2}/K^{2}}\leq 2

whenever
K>\sqrt{8/3}\,\sigma_{X}
.
Therefore,

\lVert X\rVert_{\psi_{2}}\leq\sqrt{8/3}\,\sigma_{X}
,
or equivalently,
\sigma_{X}\geq\sqrt{3/8}\,\lVert X\rVert_{\psi_{2}}
.
∎

Appendix A
Auxiliary results

Lemma A.1
.

For any
x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\in\mathbb{R}
, the matrix

V=\begin{bmatrix}1&1&1\\
x_{1}&x_{2}&x_{3}\\
e^{x_{1}^{2}}&e^{x_{2}^{2}}&e^{x_{3}^{2}}\\
\end{bmatrix}

is invertible if and only if
x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}
are distinct.

Proof.

If
x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}
are not distinct, then neither are the
columns of
V
, and hence
V
cannot be invertible.

For the converse implication, it suffices to verify that
the rows
v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}
of
V
are linearly independent
when
x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}
are distinct. To do this,
assume that

c_{1}v_{1}+c_{2}v_{2}+c_{3}v_{3}=0

for some scalars
c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}
.
Then the function

f(t)=c_{1}+c_{2}t+c_{3}e^{t^{2}}

vanishes at three distinct points
x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}
.
We note that

f^{\prime\prime}(t)\ =\ 2c_{3}(1+2t^{2})e^{t^{2}}.

If
c_{3}>0
, then
f
is strictly convex, and a strictly convex function cannot have three distinct zeros.
If
c_{3}<0
, then
-f
is strictly convex, and similarly,
f
cannot have three distinct zeros.
Therefore,
c_{3}=0
, which implies that

f(t)=c_{1}+c_{2}t.

Because
f
vanishes at points
x_{1}\neq x_{2}
, and

0=f(x_{2})-f(x_{1})=c_{2}(x_{2}-x_{1}),

we see that
c_{2}=0
.
It follows that
c_{1}=f(x_{1})=0
.
Thus,
c_{1}=c_{2}=c_{3}=0
, which confirms that the rows

v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}
are linearly independent.
∎
