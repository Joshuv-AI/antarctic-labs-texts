Sudden unexpected death in epilepsy: An Italian expert consensus statement on risk, mechanisms, prevention, and therapeutic strategies
Authors: Vincenzo Belcastro, Fedele Dono, Giovanni Falcicchio, Lorenzo Gigli, Angela La Neve, Sara Matricardi, Barbara Mostacci, Angelo Pascarella, Leandro Pecchia, Emilio Russo, Pasquale Striano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveSudden unexpected death in epilepsy (SUDEP) is a leading cause of premature mortality in people with epilepsy, particularly in those with tonic–clonic seizures (TCSs), developmental and epileptic encephalopathies (DEEs), and drug‐resistant epilepsy. Although awareness of SUDEP has increased, major uncertainties remain regarding individualized risk stratification, mechanistic interpretation, and implementation of preventive strategies in routine care. This Italian expert consensus statement was developed to formalize a shared multidisciplinary position on SUDEP risk profiles, pathophysiological mechanisms, and preventive and therapeutic strategies.MethodsThe consensus was supported by a focused narrative appraisal of current evidence, rather than a systematic review, and developed through a structured multidisciplinary process based on a nominal group technique. The panel comprised 11 Italian experts: nine epileptologists from the Italian League Against Epilepsy (LICE) and its SUDEP Working Group, one cardiologist with expertise in epilepsy‐related cardiac complications and SUDEP, and one biomedical engineer with expertise in seizure‐detection devices and wearable technologies. Statements were generated across three macro‐areas, discussed in working groups and plenary sessions, prioritized through an initial in‐person vote, refined after discussion, and submitted to a second online round. Consensus was predefined as at least 75% agreement on a 5‐point Likert scale.ResultsA final set of 33 statements was generated, of which 26 reached consensus; two of these (serotonergic modulation as a preventive strategy and fenfluramine's mechanisms beyond seizure control) reached the threshold only narrowly and are discussed as hypothesis‐generating rather than as established clinical guidance. The resulting framework supports an extension of seizure‐centered prevention toward an integrated, individualized, and mechanism‐informed approach, in which reduction of convulsive seizure burden remains central while post‐ictal respiratory, autonomic, cardiac, arousal, genetic, and treatment‐related vulnerabilities are considered in selected high‐risk patients.SignificanceThis consensus provides the first formalized Italian multidisciplinary position on SUDEP, offering an evidence‐informed and mechanism‐aware framework intended to support more consistent risk communication, individualized risk stratification, and preventive care in clinical practice.Plain Language SummarySudden unexpected death in epilepsy (SUDEP) is uncommon but is more likely in people with frequent tonic–clonic seizures and some severe epilepsy syndromes. An Italian multidisciplinary expert panel developed consensus statements on SUDEP risk, possible mechanisms, monitoring, and prevention. Reducing convulsive seizures and supporting treatment adherence remain the main preventive priorities; SUDEP information should be offered broadly, while counseling and additional measures should be tailored to individual risk. These statements reflect expert agreement and available evidence and are not formal clinical practice guidelines.

INTRODUCTION

Sudden unexpected death in epilepsy (SUDEP) is defined as the sudden, unexpected, witnessed or unwitnessed, non‐traumatic and non‐drowning death of a person with epilepsy, with or without evidence of a seizure, excluding documented status epilepticus, in whom post‐mortem examination does not reveal a toxicological or anatomical cause of death. 1 , 2 Although relatively infrequent at the individual level, SUDEP represents one of the most devastating epilepsy‐related outcomes and a major contributor to premature mortality in people with epilepsy. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4

The burden of SUDEP is not evenly distributed across the epilepsy population. The most robust clinical predictor is the presence and frequency of tonic–clonic seizures (TCSs), particularly when seizures are nocturnal or unwitnessed. 1 , 2 , 5 Epidemiological data also indicate increased vulnerability in people with drug‐resistant epilepsy and in severe DEEs, particularly Dravet syndrome (DS) and, within a broader high‐risk clinical framework, Lennox–Gastaut syndrome (LGS). In these patients, convulsive seizure burden may coexist with syndrome‐specific biological vulnerability and complex comorbidity. 3 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8

For this consensus, “high‐risk patients” is described using a tiered structure to avoid indiscriminately including most drug‐resistant patients. The highest risk could be, in particular, defined as: frequent or recent nocturnal/unwitnessed TCSs, DEEs (DS, LGS). In addition, risk‐modifying factors include: drug resistance, intellectual disability, poor adherence, limited supervision, cardiorespiratory/genetic markers. 1 , 2 , 5 , 6

The pathophysiology of SUDEP remains only partially understood. Current evidence supports a multifactorial model in which seizure‐related dysfunction of respiratory, autonomic, cardiac, and arousal networks leads to progressive post‐ictal cardiorespiratory collapse. 2 , 9 , 10 Available monitored cases, particularly the MORTEMUS study, suggest a typical sequence after a TCS, frequently during sleep, followed by post‐ictal respiratory dysfunction, autonomic instability, bradyarrhythmia, and terminal asystole. 9 , 11

Accordingly, SUDEP can be conceptualized as a network‐based post‐ictal failure: a seizure‐triggered breakdown of interacting brainstem, respiratory, autonomic, cardiac, and arousal systems rather than the consequence of a single isolated respiratory or cardiac mechanism. 9 , 10 , 12 , 13

Within this framework, the post‐ictal window represents a crucial time for prevention after a potentially dangerous seizure, because supervision, repositioning, stimulation, rescue medication when clinically indicated, and targeted cardiorespiratory monitoring may act on a still evolving and potentially reversible collapse cascade. 5 , 12 , 13 , 14 This framing does not displace seizure reduction, surgical or neuromodulatory options, dietary therapy when appropriate, and optimized antiseizure treatment as primary preventive targets; rather, it identifies the post‐ictal phase as an actionable interval once a high‐risk seizure has occurred.

Further progress in SUDEP prevention will require prospective validation of preventive strategies and better translation of mechanistic insights into therapeutic decision‐making. 2 , 5 , 15 Despite existing international guidance, implementation of SUDEP communication, individualized risk assessment, monitoring, and preventive strategies remains heterogeneous in routine care, and a structured Italian multidisciplinary position integrating epilepsy, cardiology, and monitoring perspectives has been lacking. Against this background, this expert consensus statement aims to formalize a shared Italian position on SUDEP clinical features, mechanisms, and preventive and therapeutic strategies, complementing rather than replacing existing international recommendations.

METHODS

The SURE (Statement & Unified Recommendations by Experts) Working Group was convened as a multidisciplinary Italian expert panel to develop consensus on key clinical and research aspects of SUDEP. The panel comprised 11 experts: nine epileptologists affiliated with LICE and/or its SUDEP Working Group, one cardiologist with specific expertise in epilepsy‐related cardiac complications and SUDEP, and one biomedical engineer with expertise in seizure‐detection devices and wearable technologies. Panelists were selected purposively to combine clinical, scientific, and technological expertise relevant to SUDEP, DEEs, cardiology, seizure‐detection devices, and wearable technologies. The initiative was designed to integrate available scientific evidence with expert opinion and to formulate consensus statements across three predefined macro‐areas: clinical characteristics and risk factors, pathophysiological mechanisms, and preventive and therapeutic strategies.

The consensus process, described in Figure 1, was based on a structured nominal group technique (NGT). 16 Before the meeting, panel members independently reviewed the relevant literature and received a structured list of key questions covering SUDEP epidemiology, risk assessment, pathophysiology, preventive strategies, and future research priorities. These questions served as the basis for subsequent discussions and consensus voting. During the meeting, experts were assigned to working groups corresponding to the three macro‐areas. Within each group, participants first generated preliminary statements considered relevant for clinical practice and future research (silent generation of ideas). These preliminary statements were then presented in plenary session following a round‐robin format and collectively discussed during a clarification phase to refine wording, improve conceptual consistency, and identify overlap or redundancy across statements. Following the plenary discussion, a first in‐person voting round was conducted to establish the priority of the preliminary statements. The statements generated by the working groups were subjected to a structured prioritization process using a ranking approach, in line with the NGT. Each expert was asked, independently and anonymously, to classify each statement into one of three categories: not a priority, important but not a priority, or high priority to be submitted for consensus voting. This step was aimed at selecting the statements considered most relevant by the panel for inclusion in the subsequent consensus phase, which consisted of agreement rating using a 5‐point Likert scale.

The supporting evidence appraisal was narrative and intended to contextualize the predefined consensus questions rather than to generate formal evidence‐based recommendations. Panel members independently reviewed literature considered relevant to these questions; this step did not use a predefined systematic or database‐specific search protocol. It did not follow a PRISMA process or include duplicate study selection, formal risk‐of‐bias assessment, or evidence grading.

The highest‐priority statements underwent iterative revision to improve clarity, eliminate overlap, and better reflect the available evidence and expert discussions. Following review by the methodologist and the scientific lead, Prof. Pasquale Striano (P.S.), the final wording of each statement was submitted for a second online voting round.

During the in‐person meeting, the panel collectively agreed on the use of a 5‐point Likert scale (strongly disagree, disagree, neither agree nor disagree, agree, strongly agree) to assess the level of agreement, as it is widely used in consensus processes and considered appropriate to capture both disagreement and intermediate positions without introducing excessive complexity. The 5‐point Likert scale was used for the final consensus agreement‐rating phase. Consensus was predefined as at least 75% of respondents rating a statement as either 4 (“agree”) or 5 (“strongly agree”), calculated based on the frequency of these responses over the total number of voters for each statement. Considering that commonly adopted cut‐offs in structured consensus methods range between 66.6% and 80%, the 75% threshold was deemed appropriate given the specialized nature of the topic, the multidisciplinary composition of the panel, and the relatively small number of experts involved. Consensus percentages quantify agreement among participating experts only; they do not indicate certainty of evidence, effect size, or strength of a clinical recommendation.

Given the multidisciplinary composition of the panel, participants were allowed to refrain from voting on statements addressing highly specialized clinical, genetic, or technological aspects outside their primary area of expertise. Consequently, the denominator for agreement varied across items, with 9 to 11 respondents per statement.

Statements addressing invasive cardiac monitoring, genetic channelopathy testing, and device implantation (B4, B7, B8) were generated and voted on with input from a single cardiologist with epilepsy‐specific expertise; broader cardiogenetic or electrophysiological input was not available, and the voting results for these items should therefore be interpreted in light of this limitation.

A formal GRADE evidence‐to‐decision framework was not used as this project was designed as an NGT‐based expert consensus exercise rather than guideline development. Future updates may benefit from a systematic evidence review and a formal evidence‐to‐decision framework.

The voting pattern also identified the statements that generated greater debate and, therefore, require cautious interpretation. Consensus was not reached for poor SUDEP awareness as a direct driver of modifiable risk behaviors (A5), universal post‐ictal pulse oximetry or vital‐parameter monitoring for relatives (A7), the assumption that wearables necessarily create a false sense of protection (A8), loop recorder implantation after ECG or Holter abnormalities (B7), mandatory invasive cardiac monitoring in genetically high‐risk patients (B8), beta‐blockers as part of a multimodal SUDEP‐prevention strategy (Ca2), and universal use of anti‐suffocation pillows in patients with convulsive or nocturnal seizures (Cb6). Statements on serotonergic modulation (B9) and fenfluramine mechanisms beyond seizure control (Ca3) reached consensus only narrowly and are therefore discussed as biologically plausible and hypothesis‐generating rather than as direct clinical evidence of SUDEP prevention.

DISCUSSION

SUDEP clinical profile and high‐risk populations

The first macro‐area reframes SUDEP as a major epilepsy‐related outcome with discernible patterns of risk across identifiable clinical subgroups, rather than as a rare and largely unpredictable complication. DEEs with a high burden of convulsive seizures, particularly Dravet syndrome and, within a broader high‐risk clinical framework, Lennox–Gastaut syndrome, emerge as priority settings for proactive prevention. The evidence base is stronger for Dravet syndrome, whereas SUDEP‐specific data in Lennox–Gastaut syndrome remain more limited despite its high mortality and clinical complexity. 1 , 2 , 3 , 6 In addition, a recent Bayesian meta‐analysis of 37 RCTs and 15 extension studies (3757 patients) estimated an overall SUDEP incidence of 4.32/1000 person‐years in DEEs, with the highest rate observed in Dravet syndrome (7.59/1000 person‐years) and a substantial risk also in Lennox–Gastaut syndrome (3.40/1000 person‐years), confirming that SUDEP represents approximately half of all premature deaths in these disorders. 17

The panel also converged on two major unmet needs: the absence of validated predictive biomarkers and the lack of integrated clinical‐genetic models for individualized risk stratification. Current risk assessment still depends largely on clinical history, seizure burden, nocturnal occurrence, adherence, supervision, and comorbidity, while genetic information is increasingly relevant but not yet prospectively validated as a stand‐alone SUDEP predictor. 1 , 2 , 18

The voting results reinforce seizure control as the principal modifiable preventive target while highlighting nocturnal risk, supervision, treatment adherence, syndrome‐specific vulnerability, and post‐ictal recovery as clinically relevant contextual modifiers (Box 1). 1 , 2 , 3 , 5 , 6 , 19 These domains should be interpreted together rather than as independently validated predictors.

Published literature supports SUDEP counseling as part of good clinical care, 1 , 20 , 21 whereas the panel vote specifically addressed implementation in higher‐risk settings. The lack of consensus that poor awareness alone directly promotes higher‐risk behaviors should therefore not be interpreted as evidence against informing lower‐risk or newly diagnosed patients. Counseling should remain individualized, family‐centered, and embedded in broader educational, therapeutic, and support pathways rather than treated as a stand‐alone behavioral intervention.

A similar caution applies to monitoring and device‐related statements. Lack of consensus on universal post‐ictal pulse oximetry or vital‐parameter monitoring should not be read as evidence against selected use. Rather, in line with the available literature, the panel favored risk‐adapted monitoring in high‐risk contexts and avoided universal recommendations or deterministic assumptions about device‐related behavior. 5 , 14 , 15 Taken together, the first macro‐area combines a clear focus on high‐risk populations with appropriate caution where direct outcome evidence is limited.

Pathophysiology and involved mechanisms

The statements in the second macro‐area are consistent with the current interpretation of SUDEP as the result of dynamic interaction among seizure activity, respiratory control, cardiovascular regulation, autonomic function, and impaired post‐ictal recovery. Rather than treating respiratory, autonomic, and cardiac abnormalities as isolated phenomena, the panel interpreted them as interconnected components of a temporally linked post‐ictal cascade, in line with the sequence of events described in monitored SUDEP cases. 2 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14

Within this framework, the immediate post‐ictal period emerges as a critical window of vulnerability. The panel reached consensus on the importance of targeted post‐ictal cardiorespiratory monitoring in high‐risk individuals with convulsive seizures and considered post‐ictal heart‐rate monitoring a reasonable component of a broader surveillance strategy. These statements are clinically important because they emphasize the post‐ictal period rather than the seizure alone, as evidence from monitored SUDEP cases suggests that this is the phase in which the fatal sequence most clearly unfolds. The implication is not that monitoring alone prevents SUDEP, but that the post‐ictal window is the phase in which deterioration may be recognized and response pathways can still be activated. 5 , 12 , 13 , 14

Respiratory dysfunction remains central to the model, while cardiac abnormalities may act as downstream effectors, co‐determinants, or markers of broader cardiorespiratory vulnerability in susceptible individuals. Overall, the evidence supports an integrated model involving brainstem, autonomic, respiratory, and cardiac networks rather than a single isolated mechanism. 10 , 12 , 13 , 22

The importance given to the cardiological dimension is reflected in the statements supporting structured cardiological evaluation in selected high‐risk patients, including interictal 12‐lead ECG, Holter‐ECG, post‐ictal ECG when feasible, and ECG evaluation during video‐EEG monitoring when prior abnormalities are present. These findings reflect expert opinion rather than trial‐derived evidence and support consideration of a risk‐stratified approach to cardiological evaluation in selected high‐risk patients, pending prospective validation; non‐invasive assessment is endorsed as a reasonable first step, while more advanced investigations should be considered on an individualized multidisciplinary basis when clinical context, electrocardiographic abnormalities, or genetic findings raise concern. 2 , 12 , 23 , 24 , 25

The statement on genetic testing recognizes that, particularly in drug‐resistant epilepsies and DEEs, etiological genetic diagnosis may provide clinically relevant information beyond syndrome classification. In selected patients, it may help contextualize potential neurocardiac vulnerability, an area supported by emerging evidence on genes expressed in both the central nervous system and the cardiovascular system. However, the current role of genetic findings in prospective SUDEP risk prediction remains incompletely defined. 2 , 13 , 25

Finally, the panel reached only narrow consensus on serotonergic modulation (B9). Published preclinical and mechanistic literature provides biological plausibility for an influence on post‐ictal respiratory recovery and seizure‐induced respiratory arrest, 9 , 10 , 12 , 26 but direct clinical confirmation is limited. This statement should therefore be regarded as hypothesis‐generating and is not sufficient to support clinical guidance for SUDEP prevention.

Taken together, the second macro‐area distinguishes mechanistic plausibility from clinical implementation: post‐ictal monitoring and selected cardiological assessment may be considered in higher‐risk phenotypes, while genetic and serotonergic hypotheses require prospective validation before they can inform broader practice.

Therapeutic strategies, preventive care, and future directions

Therapeutic strategies

The strongest area of agreement is that SUDEP risk reduction currently relies mainly on reducing the burden, severity, and duration of TCSs, particularly in high‐risk patients. This approach is consistent with the most robust evidence currently available and should include optimized syndrome‐informed antiseizure treatment and, when appropriate, surgical, neuromodulatory, dietary, and rescue strategies. 1 , 5

Within pharmacological strategies specifically discussed for SUDEP prevention, beta‐blockers were evaluated as a potential multimodal option. Consensus was not reached on this statement (Ca2); their use should therefore remain individualized and restricted to specific neurocardiological contexts rather than presented as broadly applicable. 2 , 5

Among the therapeutic options considered, fenfluramine (FFA) is discussed as two consensus statements that specifically addressed this agent (Ca3 and Ca4). FFA has an established antiseizure role in clinically appropriate patients with DS and LGS, with randomized and long‐term evidence showing clinically meaningful reductions in seizure burden. 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 In addition, emerging evidence suggests potential benefits on selected non‐seizure outcomes in these syndromes. 32 Beyond these clinical effects, FFA has a distinctive multimodal mechanism of action involving serotonergic pathways and sigma‐1 receptor modulation. 33 , 34 This provides a biologically plausible rationale for a potential influence on SUDEP‐related vulnerability, further supported by preclinical evidence showing that FFA can reduce seizure‐induced respiratory arrest in a mouse model of SUDEP. 35 In addition, pooled Dravet syndrome exposure data reported a lower all‐cause/SUDEP mortality rate during FFA treatment (1.7 per 1000 person‐years) than historical estimates. 36 Together, these mechanistic, preclinical, and clinical observations support further investigation of a potential role of FFA in SUDEP‐related pathways. However, the evidence remains hypothesis‐generating, and the potential role of FFA in SUDEP prevention requires prospective confirmation.

Preventive care pathways and monitoring technologies

Published guidance and recent communication evidence support offering SUDEP information broadly to people with epilepsy and their families. 1 , 21 The panel identified the need for proactive, explicit, individualized, and repeated counseling in patients at increased risk, as determined by clinical evaluation. For patients at lower current risk, SUDEP information should remain available, with the timing, depth, and repetition of discussion tailored to individual circumstances and clinical judgment. Counseling should remain family‐centered and integrated with adherence support, rescue planning, supervision, and individualized risk assessment.

Selective use of seizure‐detection devices, AI‐supported tools, wearables, and multimodal monitoring may be appropriate in patients with active epilepsy and higher SUDEP risk, particularly those with recurrent convulsive seizures, when the goal is to improve detection, supervision, and timely response. 5 , 15 , 23 Here, active epilepsy refers to ongoing seizures despite treatment or recurrent clinically relevant seizures requiring continuing management. Wearable and AI‐supported technologies should be interpreted primarily as tools to improve detection and response in selected high‐risk patients, rather than as stand‐alone SUDEP‐preventive interventions.

Night‐time supervision should be discussed with patients with nocturnal or convulsive seizures. By contrast, no consensus was reached regarding the routine use of anti‐suffocation pillows. The panel recognized that their potential role in SUDEP risk mitigation still requires stronger supportive evidence and raised concern that such devices could create a false sense of security if used in isolation; therefore, they should not be considered a substitute for established preventive strategies focused on seizure control, supervision, and individualized risk assessment. 5 , 14

Future directions

Prospective registries remain a key priority for capturing rare outcomes, defining high‐risk subgroups, and integrating clinical, genetic, physiological, treatment, counseling, and monitoring variables into more refined SUDEP risk models. 2 , 24 Because SUDEP is a rare outcome, adequately powered interventional studies using SUDEP as a direct endpoint are difficult to conduct, particularly in rare DEEs. Well‐designed registries and real‐world evidence studies may therefore provide a practical framework for evaluating syndrome‐specific risk modifiers, counseling pathways, monitoring strategies, peri‐ictal interventions, and treatment‐related clinical signals over time.

LIMITATIONS

The present consensus should be interpreted in light of several methodological and contextual considerations. Because SUDEP is a rare outcome, adequately powered prospective and interventional studies using SUDEP as a primary endpoint are challenging to conduct. Many statements therefore draw on observational evidence, biological plausibility, and clinical experience rather than dedicated prospective trials. In addition, the supporting evidence appraisal was narrative rather than systematic, no formal risk‐of‐bias assessment or GRADE evidence grading was performed, and consensus percentages quantify expert agreement rather than certainty of evidence or recommendation strength.

In addition, although the panel included experts from different disciplines and areas of expertise, it was relatively small, entirely Italian, and predominantly epilepsy‐oriented. The predominance of epileptologists and the absence of direct patient and caregiver involvement may limit the generalizability, acceptability, feasibility, and patient‐centeredness of statements concerning counseling, nocturnal supervision, caregiver response, respiratory assessment, and monitoring technologies. These domains would benefit from validation with direct patient and caregiver involvement and broader representation from nursing, psychology, respiratory medicine, genetics/cardiogenetics, health economics, and implementation science.

Furthermore, the statements reflect consensus within a specific national healthcare context and may not fully account for differences in healthcare systems, resources, and clinical practice across countries. Core principles such as seizure control, adherence support, broad provision of SUDEP information, individualized risk assessment, and periodic reassessment are broadly transferable, whereas cardiological pathways, caregiver training, emergency‐response systems, and monitoring technologies should be adapted to local resources and organization of care.

Despite these limitations, the consensus provides a structured framework that integrates current evidence with clinical experience and may support more consistent risk communication, risk stratification, and preventive care. Future prospective registries, multinational collaborative datasets, and real‐world studies will be important to validate the statements, refine risk assessment, and evaluate preventive interventions in routine practice; future consensus updates may also benefit from a systematic evidence review and a formal evidence‐to‐decision framework to more explicitly link evidence certainty, benefits and harms, feasibility, stakeholder values, and recommendation strength.

CONCLUSIONS

SUDEP remains one of the most serious complications of epilepsy, particularly in individuals with tonic–clonic seizures, drug‐resistant epilepsy, and developmental and epileptic encephalopathies. While seizure control remains the cornerstone of prevention, a broader approach incorporating post‐ictal vulnerability, treatment adherence, individualized counseling, and selected monitoring strategies may further support risk reduction.

This Italian multidisciplinary consensus identifies areas of expert agreement while acknowledging heterogeneity in the supporting evidence. Convulsive seizure control and adherence remain the principal modifiable targets; SUDEP information should be offered broadly, with the intensity of counseling and additional preventive measures tailored to individual risk and clinical context. Cardiological assessment, post‐ictal monitoring, wearable technologies, and mechanism‐based therapeutic hypotheses require selective application and further prospective validation. These consensus statements are not formal clinical practice guidelines and should be interpreted and implemented according to individual clinical circumstances, local resources, and care pathways.
