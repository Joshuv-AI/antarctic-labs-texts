Analysis of clinical characteristics of 38 patients with dextrocardia: An observational study
Authors: Yongguang Li, Chong Wang, Xiaokai Li, Yu Fan, Biao Zhang, Chengzhen Rong, Susu Li, Zihan Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dextrocardia is an extremely rare congenital anomaly, and survival into adulthood is exceptionally uncommon. This study aimed to systematically analyze the clinical, imaging, laboratory, and functional characteristics of patients with Dextrocardia to improve understanding of its natural history and prognosis. A retrospective review of 38 patients diagnosed with Dextrocardia at a single center from 2012 to 2025 was conducted. Baseline demographic data, associated anomalies, laboratory parameters, imaging findings, cardiac function assessed by the New York Heart Association (NYHA) classification, and follow-up outcomes were collected and analyzed. Of 38 patients, 21 (55%) were male and 17 (45%) were female; the majority were over 61 years of age (36.8%). All patients had Dextrocardia with the heart located entirely within the right hemithorax and no pericardial defects. Laboratory tests showed mild myocardial stress but preserved systemic homeostasis. Imaging demonstrated that computed tomography (CT) was the predominant diagnostic tool, providing detailed anatomical information, while chest X-radiation (X-ray) and echocardiography served as supplementary modalities. Most patients had preserved or mildly impaired cardiac function, around NYHA Class I–II. During a mean follow-up of 21.9 months, all patients survived without mortality. Dextrocardia without major anomalies may represent a distinct subtype with favorable outcomes. Chest CT is the most valuable imaging modality, and functional assessment is essential for prognosis. These findings broaden the clinical spectrum of Dextrocardia and challenge the traditional view that survival is universally poor.

1. Introduction

Ectopia cordis is a rare congenital malformation in which the heart is located partially or completely outside the thoracic cavity due to a failure of midline fusion during embryonic development.[1] The condition is typically diagnosed at birth, with an estimated incidence of 1 in 50,000 to 80,000 live births.[2] Most affected infants present with severe associated anomalies and have poor prognoses, often leading to early mortality.[3] Cases of ectopia cordis are sporadic, and their true prevalence remains unknown due to the limited number of reports in the literature.

It is classified into 4 major types based on the anatomical location of the ectopic heart: cervical, thoracic (also known as suprasternal), thoracoabdominal, and abdominal.[4] Thoracic ectopia cordis is the most common form of cardiac positional anomaly. It is characterized by right-sided positioning of the cardiac apex within the thoracic cavity, accounting for approximately 60 to 65% of cases, followed by thoracoabdominal and abdominal forms.

Dextrocardia is the most common form of cardiac positional anomaly. Dextrocardia is exceedingly rare, and cases that survive into adulthood often present with atypical symptoms or may be incidentally discovered during imaging for unrelated conditions.[5] Due to long-term compensatory mechanisms and variable severity, some individuals remain asymptomatic or experience only mild cardiopulmonary compromise.

To date, most studies on Dextrocardia have focused on neonatal or pediatric populations, with limited information available regarding its presentation, progression, and imaging characteristics. The natural history of Dextrocardia beyond infancy remains poorly understood, and adult cases are typically reported only as isolated case reports. This study represents an analysis of Dextrocardia, integrating thoracic computed tomography (CT) and functional features of this rare condition. By addressing a significant gap in the literature, our research provides valuable insights into the long-term survivability, clinical variability, and diagnostic imaging approaches in patients, thereby enhancing awareness and supporting informed decision-making for future diagnostic and therapeutic purposes.

Understanding the imaging features and clinical course of Dextrocardia is crucial for accurate diagnosis, risk assessment, and appropriate clinical management. The findings from this study may help clinicians distinguish Dextrocardia from other mediastinal or structural anomalies, facilitating timely intervention when necessary. Moreover, our work lays the foundation for the development of standardized diagnostic protocols and may serve as a reference for future multicenter or longitudinal studies on this rare condition.

2. Methods

2.1. Study design and population

This retrospective study was conducted at a single center from 2012 to 2025. Patients diagnosed with Dextrocardia were included in the study. The diagnosis was confirmed based on imaging findings from X-radiation (X-ray), thoracic CT, and transthoracic echocardiography. Patients without complete imaging data or with secondary cardiac malformations were excluded. This study aims to systematically analyze the clinical and imaging characteristics of Dextrocardia, a rare congenital cardiac anomaly with limited data available in this population. This study was reviewed and approved by the

Institutional Review Board of Lixin County People’s Hospital (LXXRMYY-2025LW019).

2.2. Inclusion and exclusion criteria

We have clearly defined the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Specifically, patients were included if they:

Inclusion criteria included: Age ≥ 18 years at the time of enrollment; Had imaging-confirmed Dextrocardia based on thoracic CT and/or echocardiography. Had complete clinical and imaging records available.

Exclusion criteria included: Prior cardiac corrective surgery; Incomplete imaging or clinical data; Concomitant structural heart diseases could confound positional assessment.

2.3. Data collection

Baseline demographic information was collected for all patients, including gender, age at diagnosis, and family history. These parameters were used to characterize the study population and analyze potential correlations with clinical outcomes. Family history was assessed based on documented medical records and clinician-recorded admission histories. A positive family history was defined as congenital heart disease or structural cardiac abnormalities in first-degree relatives.

2.4. Associated anomalies and cardiac function

Associated congenital anomalies, complications, and cardiac function were systematically documented for each patient. Cardiac function was evaluated using the New York Heart Association (NYHA) classification (Class I–IV), which provided a standardized measure of symptom severity and functional status. We have specified that the NYHA functional class was determined by attending cardiologists at the time of admission, based on standard clinical evaluation and in accordance with established guideline definitions.

2.5. Laboratory examinations

Laboratory examinations focused on myocardial injury markers to evaluate cardiac status. Key indicators included creatine kinase-MB (CK-MB) and pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP). Serum electrolytes (sodium) were measured using standard laboratory methods. These biomarkers helped to assess ongoing myocardial damage or stress, providing valuable information to correlate biochemical findings with imaging and clinical data.

2.6. Imaging examination

All patients underwent imaging examinations to confirm the diagnosis of Dextrocardia and to evaluate associated anatomical abnormalities. Initial screening was performed using a chest X-ray to identify abnormal cardiac position and thoracic deformities. Chest CT and echocardiography scans were subsequently conducted in most patients to provide detailed anatomical information, including the relationship between the ectopic heart and adjacent thoracoabdominal structures, the presence of pericardial defects, and the extent of associated congenital malformations. Imaging findings were systematically recorded and compared with surgical or clinical outcomes when available to ensure diagnostic accuracy.

2.7. Follow-up information

The follow-up period ranged from initial diagnosis to the most recent clinical evaluation or patient contact. Follow-up data were collected to assess patient outcomes, including survival status and duration of follow-up. Tracking these outcomes provided important insights into the natural history and long-term prognosis of Dextrocardia, facilitating assessment of disease progression and effectiveness of clinical management strategies.

2.8. Statistical analysis

Given the rarity of Dextrocardia and the retrospective, single-center design, the statistical analysis was descriptive. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), depending on distribution. Categorical variables are summarized as frequencies and percentages. No formal hypothesis-driven inferential statistical comparisons were performed due to the limited sample size, and the results were interpreted as exploratory.

3.2. Laboratory parameters for the assessment of cardiac function

This table (Table 2) presents the laboratory parameters measured to assess cardiac function in male and female patients with Dextrocardia. Biomarkers included NT-proBNP, creatine kinase (CK), CK-MB, and Na+. NT-proBNP levels were higher in the female group (803.7 ± 1129.3, n = 3) compared with the male group (438.5 ± 408.3, n = 9). CK-MB levels were slightly lower in females (13.5 ± 7.4, n = 9) than in males (17.3 ± 11.1, n = 16). Similarly, CK concentrations showed a small difference between groups, with mean values of 73.9 ± 54.1 (n = 9) in females and 81.5 ± 46.2 (n = 16) in males. Overall, these findings indicate that both groups exhibited relatively stable electrolyte balance, while mild variations were observed in cardiac stress and myocardial injury markers. These laboratory findings provide objective evidence for evaluating myocardial function and systemic status in this patient cohort. Given the small and uneven subgroup sizes, the laboratory findings are presented descriptively and should be interpreted with caution. No inferential statistical comparisons were performed.

3.4. Cardiac function

Cardiac function was evaluated using the NYHA classification. Most patients were classified as Class I or II, indicating preserved or mildly impaired function. In the male group, 19.0% were in Class I, 47.6% in Class II, 23.8% in Class III, and 9.5% in Class IV. In the female group, 29.4% were classified as Class I, 47.1% as Class II, and 23.5% as Class III, with no patients classified as Class IV. Most patients maintained stable cardiac performance, while a small proportion of males exhibited more advanced functional impairment (Table 4). These results describe the distribution of functional status within this cohort without implying differences between sexes.

3.5. Follow-up outcomes

The mean follow-up duration was 22.9 ± 5 months for male patients and 20.8 ± 6.6 months for female patients. At the end of the follow-up period, all 38 patients (21 males and 17 females) were alive, and no deaths were reported in either group. The absence of mortality, combined with generally preserved cardiac function, indicates a favorable short- to mid-term prognosis for this cohort, and long-term survival with appropriate management (Table 4).

4. Discussion

Dextrocardia is a rare congenital positional anomaly in which the cardiac apex points to the right side of the thorax. Its estimated incidence ranges from 1 in 10,000 to 1 in 12,000 live births. Dextrocardia may occur as an isolated condition or in association with situs inversus, heterotaxy syndrome, or structural congenital heart defects. Isolated Dextrocardia without major anomalies generally has a favorable prognosis, whereas cases accompanied by complex intracardiac malformations carry increased morbidity and mortality. Dextrocardia is one of the most frequently encountered congenital cardiac positional anomalies.[6] In contrast, our cohort of 38 patients represents one of the largest single-center series of adolescent and adult Dextrocardia, providing new insights into the clinical spectrum, imaging features, laboratory findings, and outcomes.

In this study, 21 patients (55%) were male and 17 (45%) were female, with the majority older than 61 years (36.8%). Previous reports have emphasized neonatal Dextrocardia, with survival into adulthood considered exceptional. The wide age distribution observed reflects the age profile of this cohort.

X-rays were used in about one-third of cases, primarily for initial screening. Chest CT was the predominant modality, applied in over 70% of patients. In this study, echocardiography was performed in 7 males (23.3%) and 9 females (36%). It provided a real-time, noninvasive assessment of cardiac structure and function. However, in Dextrocardia, abnormal heart position and chest wall defects often limit acoustic windows, reducing image quality and diagnostic accuracy. Additionally, advanced echocardiographic techniques are costly, limiting widespread use. Chest CT provided detailed anatomical information regarding the ectopic heart and surrounding thoracoabdominal structures.[7] These findings are consistent with prior reports emphasizing its role in anatomical assessment.

In this paper, we present a case of Dextrocardia, and none of the cases demonstrated pericardial defects or major associated anomalies. This finding contrasts sharply with earlier literature, in which Dextrocardia is often accompanied by severe intracardiac malformations, abdominal wall defects, or craniofacial anomalies, all of which contribute to the poor outcomes historically reported.[8] The absence of such malformations in our cohort may partially explain the relatively favorable prognosis and preserved cardiac function observed. From a clinical perspective, recognizing this heterogeneity is very important as prognosis should not be considered uniformly poor but rather stratified according to the presence or absence of major associated malformations.

In evaluating Dextrocardia, cardiac functional assessment is paramount. While imaging modalities primarily provide anatomical information, the clinical course and prognosis of patients are largely determined by the functional status of the heart. Assessment of cardiac function enables early identification of hemodynamic compromise, detection of subclinical myocardial dysfunction, and evaluation of the patient’s tolerance to potential surgical or interventional treatment.[9] Functional parameters also help stratify risk, guide therapeutic decision-making, and more accurately predict long-term outcomes than anatomical findings alone.

Moreover, standardized tools such as the NYHA classification provide a simple, reproducible means to quantify symptom burden and functional limitation, thereby facilitating comparisons across studies.[10] In this context, incorporating cardiac function evaluation into the routine assessment of Dextrocardia is essential for comprehensive patient management, as it directly influences both immediate clinical care and long-term prognosis.[11]

In the research, the laboratory evaluation of Dextrocardia patients included NT-proBNP, CK-MB, CK, and serum sodium, which together provided an objective reflection of cardiac stress and metabolic status. NT-proBNP showed wide variability among individuals, especially in males, suggesting heterogeneous levels of ventricular wall tension and functional reserve.[12] CK-MB and CK levels were modestly elevated, consistent with mild myocardial strain rather than overt injury.[13] Serum sodium concentrations remained within physiological ranges, indicating preserved systemic homeostasis and absence of advanced heart failure-related electrolyte disturbance.

Cardiac function was systematically evaluated using the NYHA classification, which stratifies patients into functional classes based on symptom burden and activity limitations.[14] In this cohort, most patients, more than 60%, were categorized as Class I and II, reflecting preserved or only mildly impaired functional capacity. Such stability is clinically important, as functional status often predicts exercise tolerance, treatment feasibility, and long-term survival more accurately than anatomical findings alone.

Unlike neonatal cases of Dextrocardia, where mortality is commonly attributed to severe cardiac dysfunction and failure within days or weeks, long-term survivors in our series demonstrated relatively stable cardiac function.[15] This contrast underscores the heterogeneity of Dextrocardia and highlights the potential existence of a less severe clinical subtype. The favorable functional profile observed suggests that prognosis is not universally poor, but rather dependent on the extent of associated anomalies and the degree of cardiac impairment. These findings broaden the clinical understanding of Dextrocardia and emphasize the need for comprehensive functional assessment in all cases.

During a mean follow-up of 21.9 months, all patients survived, with no deaths recorded. This contrasts sharply with historical data, which describe survival of only hours to weeks. The excellent prognosis in our cohort may reflect uniform Dextrocardia, absence of septic-pia cordisvere anomalies, and preserved cardiac function. These findings challenge the perception of Dextrocardia as universally lethal and suggest that selected patients can achieve good long-term outcomes.

This paper highlights the variability in clinical presentation in Dextrocardia. Clinically, a comprehensive evaluation, including imaging and functional assessment, remains important for individualized management.

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective and single-center design may introduce selection bias and limit generalizability. Second, although this represents one of the larger adult series from a single institution, the overall sample size remains modest, and laboratory data were incomplete in a subset of patients, resulting in small subgroup numbers for certain parameters. Consequently, statistical power was limited, and the findings were interpreted as exploratory and descriptive rather than confirmatory. Genetic or molecular investigations were unavailable, limiting insight into underlying developmental mechanisms. Further multicenter studies with larger cohorts and extended follow-up are warranted to validate and expand upon these findings.

5. Conclusions

In summary, this cohort of 38 patients with thoracoabdominal Dextrocardia provides important evidence that not all forms of Dextrocardia are associated with poor outcomes. Most patients maintained preserved cardiac function, and survival was excellent during follow-up. Chest CT was the most informative imaging modality, while laboratory and functional assessments supported clinical evaluation. These findings broaden the clinical spectrum of Dextrocardia and highlight the need for multicenter studies to better define prognosis and underlying mechanisms.
