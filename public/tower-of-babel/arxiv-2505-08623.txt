Abstract
. We propose a tractable extension of the rough Bergomi model, replacing the fractional Brownian motion with a generalised grey Brownian motion, which we show to be reminiscent of models with stochastic volatility of volatility. This extension breaks away from the log-Normal assumption of rough Bergomi, thereby making it a viable suggestion for the Equity Holy Grail — the joint SPX/VIX options calibration. For this new (class of) model(s), we provide semi-closed and asymptotic formulae for SPX and VIX options and show numerically its potential advantages as well as calibration results.

Introduction

Fractional Brownian motion has a long and famous history in probability, stochastic analysis, Physics and their applications to diverse fields
[

,
48
,

It was recently rejuvenated in the form of fractional volatility models in mathematical finance.
First introduced by Comte and Renault

]
,
later studied theoretically by Djehiche and Eddahbi
[

]
,
Alòs, León and Vives
[

]
and Fukasawa
[

]
, they were properly empirically justified and promoted by Gatheral, Jaisson and Rosenbaum
[
36
]

and Bayer, Friz and Gatheral
[
12
]
.
Since then, a vast literature has pushed the analysis in many directions
[

,

,

,

,
13
,
18
,
37
,
46
]
,
leading to theoretical and practical challenges (and exciting progress) to understand and implement these models.

While these rough stochastic volatility models may ultimately not be the universally perfect models they may have seemed at first — and new ones in the form of multifactor or path-dependent models are fascinating competitors — they nevertheless helped re-design a new framework for volatility modelling,
spearheading new advances in the joint calibration of SPX and VIX options, the so-called
Holy Grail
of Equity modelling.
Among these rough volatility models, the rough Bergomi
[
12
]
model, an extension of the classical Bergomi model
[
15
]
, was first highlighted for its tractability.
Indeed, as with its classical cousin, the variance process is log-Normal (though non-Markovian) and thus more readily amenable to computations.
Unfortunately, this property also implies that the squared VIX, as a conditional expectation of the integrated variance, is also close to log-Normal, implying an almost flat VIX volatility smile,
markedly different from the upward curve observed on the market.
One may, of course — and this has indeed been done — construct more sophisticated versions, such as rough Heston
[
29
,
30
]
, though at the cost of losing tractability (even though the simplified version
[
37
]
appears very promising).

With this in mind, we introduce an extension of the rough Bergomi (rBergomi) model, which we call the Grey Bergomi (gBergomi) model:
it preserves self-similarity and stationary increments while relaxing the log-Normality constraint.
This is inspired by and reminiscent of (rough) stochastic volatility of volatility models or volatility-modulated models, as studied in
[
10
,
32
,
41
,
42
]
.
We develop the mathematical background, the model and its pricing characteristics in Section
2

and gather the required numerical algorithms in Section
3
.
Since some pricing formulae may be complicated and cumbersome, we develop some asymptotic closed-form approximations in Section
4

and gather all these tools in Section
5
to examine the calibration properties of this model on SPX and VIX options data.

The grey Bergomi model

This allows us, in Section

2.1.
Generalised grey Brownian motion

For
\beta>0
, the standard Mittag-Leffler function
\mathcal{E}_{\beta}
is defined as an entire function via the series representation

(2.1)

\mathcal{E}_{\beta}(z):=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{z^{n}}{\Gamma(\beta n+1)},\qquad\text{for all }z\in\mathbb{C},

where
\Gamma
is the Gamma function and, for
\beta\in(0,1]
,
the
\mathrm{M}
-Wright function
\mathcal{M}_{\beta}
reads

(2.2)

\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(z):=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{(-z)^{n}}{n!\Gamma(-\beta n+1-\beta)},\quad\text{for }z\in\mathbb{C}.

Remark 2.1
.

Recall that the Gamma function is defined for negative values via its analytic continuation

\Gamma(-x)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(x+1)}\frac{\pi}{\sin(\pi(x+1))},

where
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

The choice
\beta=\frac{1}{2}
reduces the
\mathrm{M}
-Wright function to the Gaussian density,
as a simple computation shows that

\mathcal{M}_{\frac{1}{2}}(z)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\exp\left\{-\frac{z^{2}}{4}\right\}
.
The two functions
\mathcal{M}_{\beta}
and
\mathcal{E}_{\beta}
are related through the Laplace transform

(2.3)

\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-su}\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(u)\mathrm{d}u=\mathcal{E}_{\beta}(-s),\qquad\text{for any }s\in\mathbb{R}.

Finally, a random variable
Y_{\beta}
is said to follow the (one-sided)
\mathrm{M}
-Wright distribution
if it is supported on the positive half line and admits
the
\mathrm{M}
-Wright function (
2.2
)
as probability density.
Note in particular that its moment of order
\kappa>-1
exists
[
58
]
and

(2.4)

\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{\beta}^{\kappa}\right]=\frac{\Gamma(1+\kappa)}{\Gamma(1+\beta\kappa)}.

We are now ready to introduce the generalised grey Brownian motion.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
\beta\in(0,1]
,
\alpha\in(0,2)
.
A generalised grey Brownian motion
B^{\beta,\alpha}
defined on a complete probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
is a one-dimensional continuous process starting from

B^{\beta,\alpha}_{0}=0

\mathbb{P}
-almost surely,
such that, for any
0\leq t_{1}<\ldots<t_{n}<\infty
, its joint characteristic function is given by

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left\{\mathrm{i}\sum_{k=1}^{n}u_{k}B^{\beta,\alpha}_{t_{k}}\right\}\right]=\mathcal{E}_{\beta}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}^{\top}\Sigma_{\alpha}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}\right),\quad\text{ for any }\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

where

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\alpha}:=\frac{1}{2}\left(t_{k}^{\alpha}+t_{j}^{\alpha}-\left|t_{k}-t_{j}\right|^{\alpha}\right)_{k,j=1}^{n}
.

Remark 2.3
.

The existence of the distribution of the generalised grey Brownian motion
B^{\beta,\alpha}
is a consequence of Minlos Theorem
[
52
, Theorem 1.1 and Equation (37)]

Note that
\Sigma_{\alpha}
is the covariance matrix of a (Mandelbrot-Van Ness, or Type-I) fractional Brownian motion with Hurst exponent
\frac{\alpha}{2}
and is symmetric positive definite.

Remark 2.4
.

When
\beta=\alpha=1
, the generalised grey Brownian motion reduces to a standard Brownian motion, as one can indeed check that

\mathcal{E}_{1}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}^{\top}\Sigma_{1}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}\right)=\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}^{\top}\left(\min\{t_{i},t_{j}\}\right)_{i,j=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}\right\}.

In the Physics literature pertaining to anomalous diffusions,
the
\frac{1}{2}
factor is not present in
\Sigma_{\alpha}
,
as Physics conventions normalise Brownian motion to a variance of
2
at time
1

[
44
, Chapter 10]
as opposed to a normalisation of
1
,
standard in probability theory
[
52
]
.

By inverse Fourier transform, the joint characteristic function above is integrable and decays rapidly; therefore the distribution is absolutely continuous and the joint density of
(B^{\beta,\alpha}_{t_{1}},\ldots,B^{\beta,\alpha}_{t_{n}})
reads
[
24
]

f_{\beta}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}})=\frac{(2\pi)^{-\frac{n}{2}}}{\sqrt{\operatorname{det}\Sigma_{\alpha}}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\tau^{-\frac{n}{2}}\exp\left\{-\frac{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}^{\top}\Sigma_{\alpha}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}}{2\tau}\right\}\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(\tau)\mathrm{d}\tau,\qquad\text{for all }\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

By simple computations,
for any
s,t\geq 0,n\in\mathbb{N}
,
odd moments of
B_{t}^{\beta,\alpha}
are null and

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(B_{t}^{\beta,\alpha}\right)^{2n}\right]=\frac{(2n)!t^{n\alpha}}{2^{n}\Gamma(\beta n+1)},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbb{E}\left[B_{t}^{\beta,\alpha}B_{s}^{\beta,\alpha}\right]=\frac{t^{\alpha}+s^{\alpha}-|t-s|^{\alpha}}{2\Gamma(\beta+1)}.

Furthermore, for each
t,s\geq 0
, the characteristic function of the increments reads

(2.5)

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left\{\mathrm{i}u\left(B_{t}^{\beta,\alpha}-B_{s}^{\beta,\alpha}\right)\right\}\right]=\varphi_{t-s}(u),\qquad\text{for all }u\in\mathbb{R},

where

(2.6)

\varphi_{\delta}(u):=\mathcal{E}_{\beta}\left(-\frac{u^{2}}{2}|\delta|^{\alpha}\right),\quad\text{for all }\delta,u\in\mathbb{R}.

Since the Mittag-Leffler function
\mathcal{E}_{\beta}
is not quadratic,
the marginals of
B^{\beta,\alpha}
are not Gaussian,
and (
2.5
) shows that it is
\frac{\alpha}{2}
-self-similar with stationary increments.
A careful analysis
[
23
]
further shows that the sample paths of
B^{\beta,\alpha}
have finite
p
-variation for any
p>\frac{2}{\alpha}
, thereby implying that it is not a semimartingale whenever
\alpha\in(0,1)
.
When
\alpha\in(1,2)
, an argument similar to
[
56
]
shows that it cannot be a semimartingale either since its quadratic variation is null
(which would imply finite variation, incompatible with its
1
-variation being infinite).
We highlight one interesting property that will be key for our computations and which will help provide financial intuition later on:

Lemma 2.5

(Proposition 3 in
[
51
]
)
.

The process
B^{\beta,\alpha}
has the same finite-dimensional distributions as
\sqrt{Y_{\beta}}B_{t}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}
, where
B^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}
is a Mandelbrot–Van Ness fractional Brownian motion with Hurst parameter
\frac{\alpha}{2}
and
Y_{\beta}
an independent one-sided
\mathrm{M}
-Wright random variable.

Remark 2.6
.

The decomposition in Lemma
2.5
holds in finite-dimensional
distribution and not pathwise, so the Hölder regularity of
B^{\alpha/2}
does not transfer
directly to
B^{\beta,\alpha}
along paths, but follows from Kolmogorov’s continuity theorem.
By stationarity of the increments and Lemma
2.5
,

B^{\beta,\alpha}_{t}-B^{\beta,\alpha}_{s}
and
\sqrt{Y_{\beta}}B^{\alpha/2}_{t-s}

are equal in distribution for each
t
,
whence, for any integer
n\geq 1
,

\mathbb{E}\big[|B^{\beta,\alpha}_{t}-B^{\beta,\alpha}_{s}|^{2n}\big]=\mathbb{E}\big[Y_{\beta}^{n}\big]\mathbb{E}\big[|B^{\alpha/2}_{t-s}|^{2n}\big]=\frac{(2n)!}{2^{n}\Gamma(\beta n+1)}|t-s|^{n\alpha},

using
\mathbb{E}[Y_{\beta}^{n}]=\Gamma(n+1)/\Gamma(\beta n+1)
from (
2.4
) together with
the Gaussian moments of
B^{\alpha/2}

(with variance
|t-s|^{\alpha}
).
Choosing
n>\frac{1}{\alpha}
, Kolmogorov’s continuity theorem provides a modification of
B^{\beta,\alpha}
that is almost surely locally Hölder continuous of any order
\gamma<\frac{n\alpha-1}{2n}
.
Letting
n\uparrow\infty
,

B^{\beta,\alpha}
admits a modification that is locally Hölder continuous of any order

\gamma<\frac{\alpha}{2}=H
, as for the fractional Brownian motion itself.

Given the characteristic function (
2.5
)-(
2.6
)
for
0<s<t
, define its analytic extension

(2.7)

\mathfrak{M}_{t-s}(u)\coloneqq\varphi_{t-s}\left(-\mathrm{i}u\right)=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{e}^{u\left(B_{t}^{\beta,\alpha}-B_{s}^{\beta,\alpha}\right)}\right]=\mathcal{E}_{\beta}\left(\frac{u^{2}}{2}|t-s|^{\alpha}\right),\quad\text{for all }u\in\mathbb{R},

well-defined and positive,
thus fully characterising the moment-generating function.

2.2.
The grey Bergomi model

Our starting point is a simple variation of the rough Bergomi model for an underlying stock price
S
,
originally proposed by Bayer, Friz and Gatheral
[
12
]
, with risk-neutral dynamics (assuming no interest rate)

(2.8)

\begin{array}[]{r@{\;}lc}\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}S_{t}}{S_{t}}&=\displaystyle\sqrt{V_{t}}\mathrm{d}W_{t},&S_{0}=1,\\
V_{t}&=\displaystyle\xi_{0}(t)\mathcal{E}^{\lozenge}(\eta\mathrm{G}_{t}),&V_{0}>0,\end{array}

where
\xi_{0}(\cdot)>0
denotes the initial (forward) variance curve,
\eta>0

is a (volatility of volatility) parameter, and
\mathrm{G}
a continuous non-degenerate Gaussian process.
Standard Gaussian theory ensures the existence of a standard Brownian motion
B
,
correlated with
W
with correlation
\rho\in[-1,1]
,
such that

\mathrm{G}_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}K(t-s)\mathrm{d}B_{s}
for some kernel function
K\in L^{2}((0,\infty))
.
Here
\mathcal{E}^{\lozenge}
denotes the Wick exponential defined as
\mathcal{E}^{\lozenge}(Z)\coloneqq\exp\{Z-\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[Z^{2}]\}

(note that the classical Doléans-Dade is not well defined for fractional Brownian motion as the latter is not a semimartingale
[
56
]
for
H\neq\frac{1}{2}
).
This version of the rough Bergomi model exhibits VIX marginals that closely resemble a log-Normal distribution, hence implying an almost flat VIX smile;
this was already noted in
[
12
]
,
but is not consistent with the observed upward-sloping behaviour of the VIX smile, and we showcase some empirical evidence in Appendix
C
.
In a stochastic volatility framework,
evidence has highlighted that
the volatility of the volatility parameter should not be deterministic
[
10
,
32
]
,
leading some to believe that a stochastic formulation could help reconcile the calibration of SPX and VIX smiles.
Furthermore, the inclusion of additional factors for an adequate joint fit is well-documented and supported by the literature:
in particular Rømer
[
57
]
investigated calibration problems of one-factor rough volatility models over 2004–2019 and concluded that the joint calibration problem is largely solvable with two-factor volatility models.
This was further documented for rough volatility models
[
46
]
,
regime-switching dynamics
[
39
]
, and path-dependent volatility models
[
40
]
.

In this spirit, we introduce a new, strictly positive, factor as a random volatility of volatility
\widetilde{\eta}
, so that the rough Bergomi variance process in (
2.8
) is replaced by

V_{t}=\xi_{0}(t)\mathcal{E}^{\blacklozenge}\left(\widetilde{\eta}\mathrm{G}\right)_{t},\qquad\xi_{0}(t)>0,\quad t\in[0,T],

where, for any
\eta>0
,

(2.9)

\mathcal{E}^{\blacklozenge}(\eta Z)_{t}\coloneqq\exp\left\{\eta Z_{t}-\log\mathfrak{M}_{t}(\eta)\right\},\quad\text{ for }t\in[0,T],

with
\mathfrak{M}
introduced in (
2.7
).
Note that this collapses to
\mathcal{E}^{\lozenge}(\eta Z)

for
Z
centred Gaussian.
By definition, the forward variance reads
\xi_{s}(t):=\mathbb{E}[V_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{s}^{\mathrm{G}}]

for
0\leq s\leq t
, and

\xi_{0}(t)=\mathbb{E}[V_{t}]
is
guaranteed as
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{E}^{\blacklozenge}(\eta Z)\right]=1
.
We may in particular choose
\widetilde{\eta}=\eta\sqrt{Y_{\beta}}
with
\eta>0

and
Y_{\beta}
an independent non-negative random variable with density
\mathcal{M}_{\beta}
in (
2.2
),
in which case, by Lemma
2.5
,
this
grey Bergomi

can be viewed as an extension of (
2.8
)
with randomised volatility of volatility, where

(2.10)

V_{t}=\displaystyle\xi_{0}(t)\mathcal{E}^{\blacklozenge}\left(\eta B^{\beta,\alpha}_{t}\right),\qquad V_{0}>0.

where
B^{\beta,\alpha}
is a generalised grey Brownian motion from Definition
2.2
.
For computational reasons however, the fractional Brownian motion
B^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}
has some drawbacks,
in particular the fact that its integral (Mandelbrot-van Ness) representation against a standard Brownian motion
requires a rather cumbersome kernel.
We thus amend the above setup slightly (similar to
[
12
]
),
replacing
B^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}
by a Riemann-Liouville fractional Brownian motion.
This change does not preserve the stationary increment structure outlined in Section
2.1
, and with the use of Lemma
2.5
we propose an alternative system to that of (
2.10
)

(2.11)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rll}\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}S_{t}}{S_{t}}&=\displaystyle\sqrt{V_{t}}\left(\rho\mathrm{d}B_{t}+\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}\mathrm{d}W_{t}\right),&S_{0}=1,\\
V_{t}&=\displaystyle\xi_{0}(t)\mathcal{E}^{\blacklozenge}\left(\eta\sqrt{Y_{\beta}}B^{H}_{t}\right),&V_{0}>0.\end{array}\right.

where from now on,
B^{H}
denotes a Riemann-Liouville fractional Brownian motion with Hurst parameter
H\in(0,1)

(from the results in Section
2.1
, we have the correspondence
\alpha=2H
), admitting the representation

B^{H}_{t}=\mathfrak{c}\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{H_{-}}\mathrm{d}B_{s},\qquad\text{for all }t\geq 0,

with the following convenient notations from now on:

Notations 2.7
.

\displaystyle\mathfrak{c}:=\frac{1}{\Gamma(H_{+})}
,

H_{\pm}:=H\pm\frac{1}{2}
,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{b}:=\frac{\eta^{2}\mathfrak{c}^{2}}{4H}
.

Remark 2.8
.

Explicitly, the renormalising factor in (
2.11
) is the unconditional
moment generating function of the grey driver: since
B^{H}_{t}
(conditional on
\sigma(Y_{\beta})
) is centred Gaussian with variance
\mathfrak{c}^{2}t^{2H}/(2H)
,

\mathfrak{M}_{t}(\eta):=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\{\eta\sqrt{Y_{\beta}}B^{H}_{t}\}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\{\mathfrak{b}Y_{\beta}t^{2H}\}\right]=\mathcal{E}_{\beta}\left(\mathfrak{b}t^{2H}\right),

the last equality being the
\mathrm{M}
-Wright moment generating function (
2.4
). Hence the variance process (
2.11
) admits the explicit form

(2.12)

V_{t}=\xi_{0}(t)\mathcal{E}^{\blacklozenge}\left(\eta\sqrt{Y_{\beta}}B^{H}_{t}\right)=\frac{\xi_{0}(t)}{\mathcal{E}_{\beta}\left(\mathfrak{b}t^{2H}\right)}\exp\left\{\eta\mathfrak{c}\sqrt{Y_{\beta}}\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{H_{-}}\mathrm{d}B_{s}\right\}.

Here
\eta>0
is the deterministic volatility-of-volatility parameter and the randomisation
enters solely through
\sqrt{Y_{\beta}}
; the composite
\widetilde{\eta}=\eta\sqrt{Y_{\beta}}

introduced above is used only for motivation and not carried further. Because the
renormalisation
\mathcal{E}_{\beta}(\mathfrak{b}t^{2H})
is deterministic,
\mathbb{E}[V_{t}]=\xi_{0}(t)
holds unconditionally.

In the sequel, we denote for any
t>0
the sigma-algebras
\mathcal{F}^{Z}_{t}\coloneqq\sigma(Z_{u},u\leq t)
for
Z\in\{W,B\}
,
\mathcal{F}^{\mathscr{B}}_{t}\coloneqq\sigma(Y_{\beta})\lor\mathcal{F}_{t}^{B}
and
\mathcal{F}_{t}\coloneqq\mathcal{F}^{W}_{t}\lor\mathcal{F}^{\mathscr{B}}_{t}
.
Finally, the filtrations are denoted by
\mathbb{F}^{Z}:=\cup_{t\geq 0}\mathcal{F}^{Z}_{t}
for
Z\in\{W,B\}

and
\mathbb{F}\coloneqq\mathbb{F}^{W}\lor\mathbb{F}^{\mathscr{B}}
.

Remark 2.9
.

We refer to (
2.11
) as the gBergomi model despite the use of the Riemann-Liouville representation for the fractional Brownian motion.

In order for this model to make sense, the Fundamental Theorem of Asset Pricing requires the stock price to be a true martingale:

Theorem 2.10
.

If
\rho\leq 0
, the stock price
(S_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, solution to (
2.11
), is a true
\mathbb{F}
-martingale.

Proof.

Following the ideas developed in
[
35
,
49
]
, clearly
S
is a non-negative local martingale, hence a supermartingale.
With
\tau_{n}:=\inf\{t>0,B^{H}_{t}=n\}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
, then

S_{0}=\mathbb{E}\left[S_{T\land\tau_{n}}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[S_{T}\mathbbm{1\hskip-5.97508pt{1}}_{\{T\leq\tau_{n}\}}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[S_{\tau_{n}}\mathbbm{1\hskip-5.97508pt{1}}_{\{\tau_{n}\leq T\}}\right],

and therefore

S_{0}-\mathbb{E}\left[S_{T}\right]=\lim_{n\uparrow\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[S_{\tau_{n}}\mathbbm{1\hskip-5.97508pt{1}}_{\{\tau_{n}\leq T\}}|\sigma(Y_{\beta})\right]\right].

Define on
\mathcal{F}_{t\wedge\tau_{n}}
the measure
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}
(conditional on

\sigma(Y_{\beta})
) through the density

\frac{\mathrm{d}\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}}\bigg|_{\mathcal{F}_{t\wedge\tau_{n}}}:=S_{t\wedge\tau_{n}}=\exp\left\{\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{n}}\sqrt{V_{s}}\big(\rho\mathrm{d}B_{s}+\overline{\rho}\mathrm{d}W_{s}\big)-\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{n}}V_{s}\mathrm{d}s\right\},

with
\overline{\rho}:=\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}

which is a
\mathbb{P}
-martingale since
V
is bounded on
[0,\tau_{n}]
and
S_{0}=1
.
By Girsanov’s theorem, the processes

\widehat{B}^{(n)}_{t}:=B_{t}-\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{n}}\rho\sqrt{V_{s}}\mathrm{d}s\qquad\text{and}\qquad\widehat{W}^{(n)}_{t}:=W_{t}-\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{n}}\overline{\rho}\sqrt{V_{s}}\mathrm{d}s,

are independent
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}
-Brownian motions and hence

\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathbb{E}\big[S_{\tau_{n}}1\hskip-5.97508pt{1}_{\{\tau_{n}\leq T\}}\big|\sigma(Y_{\beta})\big]\Big]=S_{0}\mathbb{E}\big[\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}(\tau_{n}\leq T)\big].

The volatility driver
B^{H}_{t}=\mathfrak{c}\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{H_{-}}\mathrm{d}B_{s}
reads, under
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}
,

B^{H}_{t}=\widehat{B}^{H}_{t}+\rho\mathfrak{c}\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{n}}(t-s)^{H_{-}}\sqrt{V_{s}}\mathrm{d}s,\qquad\text{with}\qquad\widehat{B}^{H}_{t}:=\mathfrak{c}\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{H_{-}}\mathrm{d}\widehat{B}^{(n)}_{s}.

Since
(t-s)^{H_{-}}\sqrt{V_{s}}\geq 0
, the drift is non-positive precisely when

\rho\leq 0
(and vanishes when
\rho=0
, where
B^{H}\equiv\widehat{B}^{H}
); hence

B^{H}_{t}\leq\widehat{B}^{H}_{t}
for all
t\leq\tau_{n}
a.s.,
so
\tau_{n}\geq\tau_{n}^{0}:=\inf\{t>0:\widehat{B}^{H}_{t}=n\}
. By Dominated Convergence and since
\widehat{B}^{(n)}
is a
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}
-Brownian motion, then

\lim_{n\uparrow\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}\left(\tau_{n}^{0}\leq T\right)\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\lim_{n\uparrow\infty}\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}\left(\tau_{n}^{0}\leq T\right)\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\lim_{n\uparrow\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}B^{H}_{t}\geq n\right)\right]=0.

Therefore

S_{0}-\mathbb{E}[S_{T}]=0
,
which concludes the proof.
∎

In the context of option pricing, in particular for American options,
the following result, adapted from
[
38
]
,
guarantees that the model can be used:

Lemma 2.11
.

For any
t\geq 0
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{u\in[0,t]}S_{u}\right]<\infty
.

Proof.

In light of Theorem
2.10
,
we first assume that
\rho\leq 0
.
Using the Riemann-Liouville fractional Brownian motion representation and Lemma
2.5
,
the variance under (
2.11
) reads

V_{t}=\frac{\xi_{0}(t)}{\mathcal{E}_{\beta}(\mathfrak{b}t^{2H})}\exp\left\{\eta\mathfrak{c}\sqrt{Y_{\beta}}\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{H_{-}}\mathrm{d}B_{s}\right\}=:f(t,Z_{t}),

with

f(t,z):=\frac{\xi_{0}(t)\mathrm{e}^{z}}{\mathcal{E}_{\beta}(\mathfrak{b}t^{2H})}
,

Z_{t}:=\int_{0}^{t}K_{H,\eta}(t,s)\mathrm{d}B_{s}

and
K_{H,\eta}(t,s):=\eta\mathfrak{c}\sqrt{Y_{\beta}}(t-s)^{H_{-}}
.
From
[
38
, Theorem A.4]
(strong existence and uniqueness for stochastic Volterra equations) applied as in the rough Bergomi example of
[
38
, Section 3.1]
,
we conclude that

\widetilde{Z}_{t}=Z_{t}+\int_{0}^{t}K_{H,\eta}(t,s)\rho\sqrt{\frac{\xi_{0}(s)}{\mathcal{E}_{\beta}(\mathfrak{b}s^{2H})}}\exp\left\{\frac{\widetilde{Z}_{s}}{2}\right\}\mathrm{d}s

admits a unique strong solution.
Furthermore, since
f(t,\cdot)
is non-decreasing for each
t
,

0\leq f(t,\widetilde{Z}_{t})\leq\frac{\xi_{0}(t)\exp\{Z_{t}\}}{\mathcal{E}_{\beta}(\mathfrak{b}t^{2H})}.

The existence of a strong and unique solution to
\widetilde{Z}_{t}
allows to use
[
38
, Theorem 2.5]
;
when coupled with the inequality above, we obtain for some
\kappa,\gamma>0
,

(2.13)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{u\in[0,t]}S_{u}\Big|\sigma(Y_{\beta})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\kappa+\gamma\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left[f(s,\widetilde{Z}_{s})|\sigma(Y_{\beta})\right]\mathrm{d}s

(2.14)

\displaystyle\leq\kappa+\gamma\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\xi_{0}(s)}{\mathcal{E}_{\beta}\left(\mathfrak{b}s^{2H}\right)}\exp\left\{\mathfrak{b}Y_{\beta}s^{2H}\right\}\mathrm{d}s<\infty.

Taking expectations results in

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{u\in[0,t]}S_{u}\right]\leq\kappa+\gamma\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left[f(s,\widetilde{Z}_{s})\right]\mathrm{d}s\leq\kappa+\gamma\int_{0}^{t}\xi_{0}(s)\mathrm{d}s<\infty.

∎

Remark 2.12
.

The integrability bound above shows
\sup_{u\leq t}S_{u}\in L^{1}
, so the non-negative local
martingale
S
is therefore a true martingale. This gives an alternative proof of Theorem
2.10
under the same hypothesis
\rho\leq 0
, independent
of the change-of-measure argument (the proof of the bound uses only the explicit form of
V
, not the martingale property).

2.3.
VIX

Now that the basic properties for the stock price have been set, we move on to studying the VIX under the grey Bergomi model.

2.3.1.
VIX Dynamics

The continuously monitored version of the VIX is defined as

\VIX^{2}_{T}:=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{\Delta}\int_{T}^{T+\Delta}V_{s}\mathrm{d}s\middle|\mathcal{F}^{\mathscr{B}}_{T}\right],\qquad\text{for any }T\geq 0,

with
\Delta
corresponding to one month.
To streamline the results, introduce the quantities

(2.15)

\mathcal{V}_{s}^{T}:=\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}(s-u)^{H_{-}}\mathrm{d}B_{u}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathcal{V}_{s,T}:=\displaystyle\int_{T}^{s}(s-u)^{H_{-}}\mathrm{d}B_{u},

for any
0\leq T\leq s
.
The following proposition derives an expression for it:

Proposition 2.13
.

The VIX dynamics under (
2.11
) are given, for any
T\geq 0
, by

\VIX_{T}^{2}=\frac{1}{\Delta}\int_{T}^{T+\Delta}\frac{\xi_{0}(s)}{\mathcal{E}_{\beta}(\mathfrak{b}s^{2H})}\exp\left\{\eta\mathfrak{c}\sqrt{Y_{\beta}}\mathcal{V}_{s}^{T}+\mathfrak{b}Y_{\beta}(s-T)^{2H}\right\}\mathrm{d}s.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{V}_{t}:=\mathcal{V}_{t}^{t}
. Since
Y_{\beta}
and
\mathcal{V}_{s}^{T}=\int_{0}^{T}(s-u)^{H_{-}}\mathrm{d}B_{u}
are both

\mathcal{F}^{\mathscr{B}}_{T}
-measurable, while
\mathcal{V}_{s,T}=\int_{T}^{s}(s-u)^{H_{-}}\mathrm{d}B_{u}
is independent of
\mathcal{F}^{\mathscr{B}}_{T}

(future
B
-increments, and
Y_{\beta}\perp B
) and centred Gaussian with variance

\tfrac{(s-T)^{2H}}{2H}
, then

\displaystyle\VIX_{T}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Delta}\int_{T}^{T+\Delta}\frac{\xi_{0}(s)}{\mathcal{E}_{\beta}(\mathfrak{b}s^{2H})}\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\{\eta\mathfrak{c}\sqrt{Y_{\beta}}\mathcal{V}_{s}\}\middle|\mathcal{F}^{\mathscr{B}}_{T}\right]\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Delta}\int_{T}^{T+\Delta}\frac{\xi_{0}(s)}{\mathcal{E}_{\beta}(\mathfrak{b}s^{2H})}\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\{\eta\mathfrak{c}\sqrt{Y_{\beta}}(\mathcal{V}_{s}^{T}+\mathcal{V}_{s,T})\}\middle|\mathcal{F}^{\mathscr{B}}_{T}\right]\mathrm{d}s.

Taking the Gaussian expectation over
\mathcal{V}_{s,T}
alone,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\{\eta\mathfrak{c}\sqrt{Y_{\beta}}(\mathcal{V}_{s}^{T}+\mathcal{V}_{s,T})\}\middle|\mathcal{F}^{\mathscr{B}}_{T}\right]

\displaystyle=\exp\{\eta\mathfrak{c}\sqrt{Y_{\beta}}\mathcal{V}_{s}^{T}\}\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\{\eta\mathfrak{c}\sqrt{Y_{\beta}}\mathcal{V}_{s,T}\}\middle|\mathcal{F}^{\mathscr{B}}_{T}\right]

\displaystyle=\exp\{\eta\mathfrak{c}\sqrt{Y_{\beta}}\mathcal{V}_{s}^{T}+\tfrac{1}{2}\eta^{2}\mathfrak{c}^{2}Y_{\beta}\tfrac{(s-T)^{2H}}{2H}\Big\}

\displaystyle=\exp\{\eta\mathfrak{c}\sqrt{Y_{\beta}}\mathcal{V}_{s}^{T}+\mathfrak{b}Y_{\beta}(s-T)^{2H}\big\}.

Substituting back, the proof follows from

\VIX_{T}^{2}=\frac{1}{\Delta}\int_{T}^{T+\Delta}\frac{\xi_{0}(s)}{\mathcal{E}_{\beta}(\mathfrak{b}s^{2H})}\exp\{\eta\mathfrak{c}\sqrt{Y_{\beta}}\mathcal{V}_{s}^{T}+\mathfrak{b}Y_{\beta}(s-T)^{2H}\big\}\mathrm{d}s.

∎

2.4.
Skew-Stickiness Ratio (SSR)

While joint SPX/VIX calibration is the primary objective, a model’s term-structure dynamics should also be assessed. A standard diagnostic is the Skew-Stickiness Ratio (SSR)
of Bergomi
[
16
]
(see also
[
17
]
), which measures how the
at-the-money implied volatility skew responds to movements in the underlying.
Let
[X,Y]_{[t,s]}
denote the quadratic covariation of two Itô processes
X,Y
over
[t,s]
,
and let
\sigma_{t}(\tau)
and
\mathcal{S}_{t}(\tau)
denote, respectively, the at-the-money-forward
implied volatility and its skew at time
t
for maturity
\tau
. The SSR at time
t
and
maturity
\tau
is defined as

\mathcal{R}_{t}(\tau):=\frac{1}{\mathcal{S}_{t}(\tau)}\frac{\derivative{s}[\log S,\sigma_{\cdot}(\tau)]_{[t,s]}\big|_{s=t}}{\derivative{s}[\log S]_{[t,s]}\big|_{s=t}},

that is, the instantaneous covariation between the log-price and the ATM volatility,
divided by the instantaneous variance of the log-price and normalised by the skew.
Replacing
\sigma
with the variance-swap implied volatility
\sigma_{\mathrm{VS}}
gives the
variance-swap SSR
[
17
]
.
For the gBergomi model both versions share the same short-maturity limit.

Proposition 2.16
.

For the gBergomi model (
2.11
),

\displaystyle\lim_{\tau\downarrow 0}\mathcal{R}_{t}(\tau)=H+\tfrac{3}{2}.

The variance-swap case follows exactly as in
[
17
, Proposition 20]
, and the
standard case from
[
33
, Corollary 5.6]
.

3.
Numerical algorithms

3.1.
Numerical implementation of VIX process

Simulating the VIX under (
2.11
) requires generating the Volterra process
\mathcal{V}^{T}
in (
2.15
) on a discrete grid
\mathfrak{T}=\{\tau_{j}\}_{j=0,\ldots,N}
over the interval
[T,T+\Delta]
.
Since
\mathcal{V}^{T}
is a centred Gaussian process, its law on the grid is fully determined by the covariance matrix
C_{jk}:=\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{V}^{T}_{\tau_{j}}\mathcal{V}^{T}_{\tau_{k}}]
, which can be computed explicitly via (
3.1
).
The naïve approach – computing a full Cholesky factorisation of the
N\times N
matrix
C
– has
\mathcal{O}(N^{3})
complexity and, more critically, becomes numerically unstable for small values of
H
due to the near-singularity of
C
.
To circumvent this, we adopt the
truncated Cholesky
scheme of
[
45
]
, which proceeds in two stages.
In the first stage, the process is simulated exactly at the
first
l
grid points
\{\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{l}\}

via a standard Cholesky decomposition of the
l\times l

leading sub-matrix of
C
. This small exact block absorbs the ill-conditioning that arises from grid points close to
T
,
where the kernel
(t-s)^{H_{-}}
is singular. In the second stage, the remaining points
\{\tau_{l+1},\ldots,\tau_{N}\}
are generated recursively using a one-step Markovian approximation:
each
\mathcal{V}^{T}_{\tau_{j}}
is drawn conditionally on
\mathcal{V}^{T}_{\tau_{j-1}}
alone, exploiting the fact that, sufficiently far from
T
, the correlation between
consecutive points is close to one and the conditional
distribution is well approximated by a bivariate Gaussian
projection.
Concretely, writing
\rho_{j-1,j}:=\mathrm{Corr}(\mathcal{V}^{T}_{\tau_{j-1}},\mathcal{V}^{T}_{\tau_{j}})

for
j=l+1,\ldots,N
, the recursion reads

\mathcal{V}^{T}_{\tau_{j}}=\sqrt{\mathbb{V}[\mathcal{V}^{T}_{\tau_{j}}]}\left(\frac{\rho_{j-1,j}\mathcal{V}^{T}_{\tau_{j-1}}}{\sqrt{\mathbb{V}[\mathcal{V}^{T}_{\tau_{j-1}}]}}+\sqrt{1-\rho_{j-1,j}^{2}}\;Z_{j}\right),\qquad Z_{j}\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,1).

This reduces the per-path cost of the second stage
to
\mathcal{O}(N)
while retaining the exact covariance
structure at each consecutive pair.

Remark 3.1
.

We set
l=8
as in
[
45
]
to avoid numerical issues with a small determinant.

Recall that the covariance structure
of
\mathcal{V}^{T}
in (
2.15
) reads

(3.1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{V}_{t}^{T}\mathcal{V}_{s}^{T}\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}[(t-u)(s-u)]^{H_{-}}\mathrm{d}u

(3.2)

\displaystyle=\frac{(s-t)^{H_{-}}}{H_{+}}\left\{t^{H_{+}}\mathrm{F}\left(\frac{-t}{s-t}\right)-(t-T)^{H_{+}}\mathrm{F}\left(\frac{T-t}{s-t}\right)\right\},

for any
t<s
, where
{}_{2}F_{1}
is the Hypergeometric function
[
5
, Chapter 15]
and

\mathrm{F}(u):={}_{2}F_{1}\left(-H_{+},H_{+},1+H_{+},u\right).

The full simulation procedure is summarised below.

Algorithm 3.2

(VIX simulation)
.

Fix a grid
\mathfrak{T}=\{\tau_{j}\}_{j=0,\ldots,N}
on
[T,T+\Delta]
, and
l\in\mathbb{N}
.

(i)

Compute the covariance matrix of

(\mathcal{V}_{\tau_{j}}^{T})_{j=1,\ldots,l}
using (
3.1
);

(ii)

compute
\rho_{j-1,j}:=\mathrm{Corr}(\mathcal{V}_{\tau_{j-1}}^{T},\mathcal{V}_{\tau_{j}}^{T})
by Cholesky decomposition for
j=l+1,\ldots,N
;

(iii)

generate
\{\mathcal{V}_{\tau_{j}}^{T}\}_{j=l+1,\ldots,N}
via

\mathcal{V}_{\tau_{j}}^{T}=\sqrt{\mathbb{V}[\mathcal{V}_{\tau_{j}}^{T}]}\left(\frac{\rho_{j-1,j}\mathcal{V}_{\tau_{j-1}}^{T}}{\sqrt{\mathbb{V}[\mathcal{V}_{\tau_{j-1}}^{T}]}}+\sqrt{1-\rho_{j-1,j}^{2}}\ Z_{j}\right),\textup{ for }j=l+1,\ldots,N,

where
\{Z_{j}\}_{j=l+1,\ldots,N}
are centred i.i.d. Gaussian with unit variance;

(iv)

compute
\VIX_{T}
by numerical integration, for example with a trapezoidal rule:

\text{VIX}_{T}\approx\sqrt{\frac{1}{\Delta}\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\frac{Q^{2}_{T,\tau_{j}}+Q^{2}_{T,\tau_{j+1}}}{2}(\tau_{j+1}-\tau_{j})},

where
\displaystyle Q^{2}_{T,t}\coloneqq\frac{\xi_{0}(t)}{\mathcal{E}_{\beta}(\mathfrak{b}t^{2H})}\exp\left\{\eta\mathfrak{c}\sqrt{Y_{\beta}}\mathcal{V}_{t}^{T}+\mathfrak{b}Y_{\beta}(t-T)^{2H}\right\}.

4.
Asymptotics of the SPX & VIX smiles

We now develop asymptotic closed-form expressions for the implied volatility smile of the SPX and the VIX,
in particular for the ATM short-time level, skew, and curvature,
following the approach in
[
7
, Chapter 6-8]
.
We proceed as in
[
46
]
and consider a square-integrable strictly positive process
\left\{A_{t}\right\}_{t\in[0,T]}
, adapted to the filtration
\mathbb{F}
introduced in Section
2.2
.
We further introduce the
\mathbb{F}
-martingale conditional expectation process

\mathfrak{F}_{t,T}^{A}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\left[A_{T}|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right],\quad\text{ for all }t\in[0,T],

which is nothing else than a time
t
-price of a Future contract on
A_{T}
.

We use
\mathbb{D}
to denote the domain of the Malliavin operators
\mathrm{D}^{i}
for
i\in\{1,2\}
with respect to the Brownian motion
Z^{i}

and write
\mathbb{L}^{2}\coloneqq L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{D})

(and refer the interested reader to
[
53
]
for an in-depth introduction to Malliavin calculus).
Assuming
A_{T}\in\mathbb{D}
, the Clark-Ocone formula
[
53
, Theorem 1.3.14]
reads,
for each
t\in[0,T]
,

\mathfrak{F}_{t,T}^{A}=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathfrak{F}_{t,T}^{A}\right]+\sum_{i=1}^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\mathfrak{f}_{s}^{i}(A_{T})\mathrm{d}W_{s}^{i},

where
Z^{1}\coloneqq W
and
Z^{2}\coloneqq B
and
\mathfrak{f}_{s}^{i}(A_{T})\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{D}^{i}_{s}A_{T}|\mathcal{F}_{s}]
.
Now, since
\mathfrak{F}_{t,T}^{A}
is an
\mathbb{F}
-martingale, this can be further written as

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathfrak{F}_{t,T}^{A}=\mathfrak{F}_{0,T}^{A}+\sum_{i=1}^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\mathfrak{F}_{s,T}^{A}\phi_{s}^{i}\mathrm{d}Z_{s}^{i},\quad\text{ with }\quad\phi_{s}^{i}\coloneqq\frac{\mathfrak{f}_{s}^{i}(A_{T})}{\mathfrak{F}_{s,T}^{A}},}

which is well defined since
A
is strictly positive,
and hence so is
{\mathfrak{F}_{s,T}^{A}}
. Finally, for
\boldsymbol{\phi}\coloneqq(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})\in\mathbb{L}^{2}
,

4.1.
Small-time VIX asymptotics

Throughout this section,
W
,
B
and

are mutually independent, defined on the product space

W
and

-Wright random variable.
Malliavin differentiation is taken only in the directions

\mathcal{F}_{0}

Regarding membership in

(1)

(2)

[

(3)

\mathcal{F}_{0}

(5)

since

is Lipschitz on

and

Since

, Theorem 1.3.14]
reads, for each

where

Since

almost surely, let

, so that

and the representation takes the form of the general framework above with only the

component active.

The SPX smile is primarily a crash-skew market. It reflects the fact that market participants pay for volatility to rise when the index falls, and therefore it requires a strong negative spot–volatility correlation. In contrast, the VIX smile is primarily a volatility-convexity market. It prices optionality on future variance itself becoming extremely large, whether or not this variance shock is explained one-for-one by the contemporaneous equity move. In the one-factor gBergomi model, the same grey parameter
\beta
is forced to serve both purposes. When
\beta\to 1
, the grey randomisation disappears and the model reduces to the rough Bergomi limit. This is the regime preferred by the SPX surface: the SPX smile wants leverage-driven skew, generated through a large negative correlation
\rho
, and does not require an additional independent variance-tail randomisation. Conversely, when
\beta
is small, the M-Wright random variable introduces a heavy-tailed volatility-of-volatility mixture. This is precisely the mechanism preferred by the VIX surface, since VIX options are highly sensitive to convexity in future variance. Thus, the economic conflict is that SPX wants the grey tail switched off because it interferes with the leverage-skew mechanism, whereas VIX wants the grey tail switched on because it generates the variance-tail convexity priced by VIX calls. In market terms, SPX options are primarily protection against equity downside and crash beta, while VIX options are closer to pure volatility-spike convexity. These are related risks, but they are not identical risks. A single grey factor is therefore asked to play two incompatible roles. This suggests a natural interpretable extension. Rather than assigning separate models to SPX and VIX, one may keep a single common variance process but separate the leverage-skew channel from the variance-tail-convexity channel. One possible specification is

\log\left(\frac{V_{t}}{\xi_{0}(t)}\right)=\eta_{\mathrm{lev}}c_{H}X_{t}+\eta_{\mathrm{tail}}c_{H}\sqrt{Y_{\beta}}Z_{t}-\Lambda(t),

where

X_{t}:=\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{d}B_{s}
and

Z_{t}:=\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{d}B_{s}^{\perp}
.
The normalisation term
\Lambda(t)
is chosen such that that
\mathbb{E}[V_{t}]=\xi_{0}(t).
The equity index is driven by

\frac{\mathrm{d}S_{t}}{S_{t}}=\sqrt{V_{t}}\mathrm{d}W_{t},

with

\mathrm{d}\langle W,B\rangle_{t}=\rho\mathrm{d}t

and

\mathrm{d}\langle W,B^{\perp}\rangle_{t}=0
.
In this specification, both SPX and VIX options are written on the same variance process
V_{t}
. The model does not assign one factor to SPX and another factor to VIX in an instrument-specific way. Instead, the common variance process contains two economically distinct channels. The loading
\eta_{\mathrm{lev}}
controls the spot-correlated leverage-skew channel, while
\eta_{\mathrm{tail}}
and
\beta
control the grey variance-tail-convexity channel. The parameter
\rho
controls the strength of the equity crash–volatility feedback, while
\beta
controls the heaviness of the independent volatility-of-volatility tail. This extension separates two risks that traders already distinguish in practice. The first is crash-directional volatility risk: when the index sells off, volatility rises. This is the risk primarily embedded in the SPX skew, and it is naturally represented by the spot-correlated rough factor
X_{t}
. The second is pure variance-tail risk: the possibility that future variance itself becomes extremely high. This is the risk more directly priced by VIX options, and it is naturally represented by the grey factor
\sqrt{Y_{\beta}}Z_{t}
. The one-factor gBergomi model forces these two risks to be controlled by the same loading. Empirically, this appears too restrictive. The SPX calibration drives the model toward the rough Bergomi limit,
\beta\to 1
, because it wants a clean leverage-skew mechanism. The VIX calibration drives the model toward small
\beta
, because it wants heavy-tailed variance convexity. A two-loading model keeps the same common variance process but allows crash skew and variance-tail convexity to be priced separately. The proposed extension remains economically interpretable. One loading measures how violently volatility reacts to equity selloffs, while the other measures how much independent volatility-spike convexity is priced in the variance market. This provides a more transparent alternative to highly flexible multi-factor specifications whose parameters are difficult to interpret economically. We leave the development, analysis, and calibration of such multi-factor model for our follow-up paper.

(Comparison with rough Bergomi and rough Heston)
.

It is instructive to position the gBergomi model against the two most closely related

Rough Bergomi.
The gBergomi model nests rough Bergomi as boundary case
\beta=1
.
The comparison is therefore not

Reducing

\beta
does not merely add a degree of freedom: it activates a variance-tail channel that is structurally absent from rough Bergomi, which is the empirical flexibility the extension is designed to provide.

Rough Heston.
Rough Heston instead retains an affine square-root variance process, which confers analytical tractability that gBergomi does not share: a semi-explicit characteristic function through the fractional Riccati equation, hence Fourier-based pricing, together with well-developed finite-dimensional Markovian

[
3

Rough Heston preserves transform-based tractability and Markovian lifts but shares the one-factor joint-calibration limitation; gBergomi forgoes that tractability in exchange for a heavy-tailed, M-Wright-driven variance channel that directly targets VIX convexity. This is consistent with our calibration findings above and with the two-loading extension we propose, which retains the grey variance channel while restoring an independent leverage-skew mechanism.

Appendix B
Markovian Approximation of grey Bergomi

Since the variance process in gBergomi is not Markovian, simulation schemes are computationally more expensive. A workaround is to consider Markovian approximations
[
2
,
11
,
21
]
.
Writing the power law kernel
K(t):=\frac{1}{\Gamma(H_{+})}t^{H_{-}}
as the Laplace transform

K(t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-tx}\mu(\mathrm{d}x),\quad\text{with }\mu(\mathrm{d}x):=\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\Gamma(H_{-})\Gamma(H_{+})x^{H_{+}}},

an application of the stochastic Fubini theorem yields

\int_{0}^{t}K(t-s)\mathrm{d}B_{s}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{-(t-s)x}\mathrm{d}B_{s}\mu(\mathrm{d}x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}Y_{t}^{x}\mu(\mathrm{d}x),

where
Y_{t}^{x}:=\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{-(t-s)x}\mathrm{d}B_{s}
is an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process.
Approximating the measure
\mu
by a finite sum of Dirac masses yields

K(t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-tx}\mu(\mathrm{d}x)\approx\sum_{i=0}^{N}w^{N}_{i}\mathrm{e}^{-x^{N}_{i}t},

where
(w^{N}_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
are positive weights and
(x^{N}_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
are mean-reverting speeds. Applying this to the gBergomi model results in the Markovian approximations
S^{N}
and
V^{N}
,

(B.1)

\displaystyle S^{N}_{t}=S^{N}_{0}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}V^{N}_{s}\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\sqrt{V^{N}_{s}}\left(\rho\mathrm{d}B_{s}+\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}\mathrm{d}B^{\bot}_{s}\right)\right\},

(B.2)

\displaystyle V_{t}^{N}=\frac{\xi_{0}(t)}{\mathcal{E}_{\beta}(\mathfrak{b}t^{2H})}\exp\left\{\eta\sqrt{Y_{\beta}}\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{i=0}^{N}w^{N}_{i}\mathrm{e}^{-x^{N}_{i}(t-s)}\mathrm{d}B_{s}\right\},

where
S^{N}_{0}=S_{0}=1
and
V_{0}^{N}=V_{0}=\xi_{0}(0).
Given the positive weights
(w^{N}_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
and the mean-reverting speeds
(x^{N}_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
, one can simulate the Markovian approximation of the gBergomi model.
Carefully choosing weights and mean-reverting speeds can drastically improve the value of
N
one needs in order to obtain a given accuracy. An example of this can be seen in comparing the methodology in
[
11
]
and
[
59
]
, where in
[
11
]
one can afford to use a considerably smaller
N
, thus, decreasing simulation times.
