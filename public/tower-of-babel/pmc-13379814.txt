Endurance athletes demonstrate a distinctive atrial substrate characterized by biatrial dilation, heightened vagal tone, and long-term hemodynamic stress. These adaptations, while physiologically advantageous for performance, increase the susceptibility to atrial fibrillation (AF). Persistent AF is particularly burdensome in athletes, as even small impairments in rate response or atrial function can produce disproportionate declines in exercise capacity.

Although pulmonary vein isolation (PVI) remains the cornerstone of rhythm-control therapy, non–pulmonary vein (non-PV) triggers such as a persistent left superior vena cava (PLSVC) may contribute to refractory or recurrent AF. Historically, ablation within the PLSVC has carried elevated risk when performed with radiofrequency or cryothermal energy due to the proximity of the phrenic nerve, conduction tissue, and coronary structures. Pulsed field ablation (PFA) offers a nonthermal, myocardial-selective method that may improve safety in anatomically complex areas.

This report describes the first known case of PLSVC isolation using PFA in a master's endurance athlete with persistent AF, highlighting the intersection between sports cardiology, individualized rhythm control, and emerging ablation technology.

History of Presentation

A 60-year-old lifelong endurance athlete presented with persistent AF and progressive decline in exercise performance. He trained regularly as a master's swimmer, completing 1-hour sessions at approximately 2:30 per 100 yards, and competed in gravel cycling events with a functional threshold power (FTP) of around 220 W (3.1 W/kg). He noted increasing exertional fatigue and difficulty sustaining workloads that previously felt effortless. His swim pace slowed to approximately 3:00 per 100 yards, and his FTP dropped to roughly 175 W. He estimated a 20% to 25% decline in performance, which he found distressing and incompatible with his athletic lifestyle.

On examination, his rhythm was irregularly irregular, with a normal rate and no signs of congestive heart failure.

Past Medical History

He had no history of structural heart disease or cardiovascular risk factors. Six months before presentation, he underwent PVI and cavotricuspid isthmus ablation for persistent AF. Sinus rhythm lasted only 3 days. He underwent repeat cardioversion with transient restoration of sinus rhythm. Flecainide caused intolerable adverse effects, and sotalol had no meaningful impact on arrhythmia burden. He began experiencing dizziness, and an implantable loop recorder was inserted for tailored management. A PLSVC had been identified on his echocardiogram and during his prior ablation procedure.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis for recurrent AF in this athlete included PV reconnection, non-PV triggers, autonomic-mediated AF related to high vagal tone, and atrial myopathy from lifelong endurance training. The rapid recurrence following initial PVI favored the presence of a dominant non-PV trigger. The known PLSVC and its anatomical connections to the coronary sinus and left atrium suggested that it could serve as a source of arrhythmogenic conduction.

Investigations

Electrocardiography and implantable loop recorder data demonstrated persistent AF. Prior transthoracic echocardiography revealed normal ventricular function with biatrial enlargement. Laboratory evaluation, including thyroid studies and a metabolic panel, was unremarkable. His symptoms and abrupt performance decline raised concern for a non-PV driver of persistent arrhythmia.

The 2024 HRS Expert Consensus Statement on Arrhythmias in the Athlete emphasizes the importance of early rhythm control when AF leads to impaired exercise capacity. These principles guided further management.

Management

Given his high functional expectations, intolerance of antiarrhythmic medications, recurrent AF after prior PVI, and evidence of atrial myopathy with biatrial enlargement and persistent AF substrate, a shared decision was made to pursue repeat ablation with targeted evaluation of non-PV triggers. PVs remained connected, although ablation was redone with PFA. Because the patient had persistent AF and an abnormal atrial substrate consistent with atrial myopathy, posterior wall isolation was also performed empirically as part of substrate modification rather than in response to a discrete mapped posterior wall trigger. PFA was selected because of its myocardial selectivity and favorable collateral tissue profile. Circumferential PFA lesions were delivered with the Sphere-9 ablation catheter (Medtronic, Affera) within the PLSVC. Because of the known potential for PFA-induced coronary vasospasm when ablating near the coronary system, prophylactic nitroglycerin was administered. The PLSVC was successfully isolated entirely with PFA, confirmed with both entrance and exit block without procedural complications. Circumferential ablation near the proximal aspect of the PLSVC was performed first without initial isolation, requiring additional ablations throughout to the distal aspect. Ablation was also deliberately avoided in the most proximal coronary sinus because of its close relationship to the atrioventricular node and the need to minimize the risk of conduction system injury.

Outcome and Follow-Up Data

The patient recovered uneventfully and experienced substantial improvement in symptoms. His implantable loop recorder demonstrated a marked reduction in AF burden, with only rare, brief paroxysmal episodes now out to 7-month follow-up (Figure 2). Within 2 months, he returned to swimming at his baseline pace of approximately 2:30 per 100 yards and regained an FTP near 220 W. He resumed competitive-level cycling and swimming without the use of antiarrhythmic medications. Discussion

This case illustrates several important points relevant to both sports cardiology and contemporary AF management. Endurance athletes develop structural and autonomic adaptations that predispose them to AF, and persistent AF frequently produces an outsized effect on exercise performance. The 2024 HRS consensus recognizes that early rhythm control, particularly with catheter ablation, may be preferred in athletes when symptoms impair training or competition, as rate control strategies often fail to meet their physiologic demands.1

The presence of a PLSVC is uncommon in the general population but more prevalent among patients with AF.2 Myocardial sleeves within the PLSVC can serve as powerful non-PV triggers. Ablation in this structure using thermal energy has historically been challenging due to the proximity of the phrenic nerve, coronary arteries, and conduction tissue, exposing patients to elevated procedural risk.3,4 PFA offers a nonthermal method that preferentially ablates cardiomyocytes while sparing adjacent noncardiac tissues. These characteristics make PFA particularly well suited for anatomically complex areas such as the PLSVC.5,6 In this patient, PFA facilitated safe and effective isolation of the PLSVC, resulting in significant clinical improvement.

A further consideration, particularly relevant in endurance athletes, is the risk of stiff left atrial (LA) syndrome with cumulative ablation. Extensive LA lesion sets, regardless of energy source, can reduce atrial compliance and lead to elevated filling pressures, exertional dyspnea, pulmonary hypertension, and reduced exercise capacity.7,8 These physiologic consequences are especially problematic in athletes who depend on efficient diastolic filling and high cardiac output during sustained exertion. Prior work demonstrates that ablation-related fibrosis can contribute to symptomatic limitation even when arrhythmia is controlled.9 In this case, that risk was weighed carefully; however, the patient's recurrent persistent AF, marked symptoms with reduced exercise capacity, prior failed PVI, and evidence of atrial myopathy supported a substrate-based strategy, including empiric posterior wall isolation. Accordingly, the potential benefit of more durable rhythm control was judged to outweigh the recognized risk of impaired LA compliance, while reinforcing the need for thoughtful lesion selection and avoidance of unnecessary ablation.

To our knowledge, this is the first documented case of PLSVC isolation using PFA in a master's endurance athlete with persistent AF. It highlights the importance of evaluating non-PV triggers in athletes with recurrent arrhythmia and supports the potential role of PFA in improving the safety of ablation in challenging anatomical regions.

Conclusions

PFA provided a safe and effective means of isolating an arrhythmogenic PLSVC in a master’s endurance athlete with persistent AF. The procedure resulted in substantial improvement in exercise performance and quality of life.
