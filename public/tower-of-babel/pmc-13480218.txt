Tumor organoids as a revolutionary platform for advancing cancer nanomedicine
Authors: Chenyu Wu, Jingyao Chen, Chuanyi Zhang, Jiecheng Ji, Yinhua Tan, Zhichang Liu, Qiang Wu, Xiuli Zheng, Kui Luo, Kai Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNanomedicine has revolutionized oncology through targeted drug delivery and theranostic applications. However, its clinical translation remains a formidable challenge, primarily due to the translational gap created by conventional preclinical models. These models often fail to recapitulate patient-specific tumor heterogeneity and the complex dynamics of the tumor microenvironment. This review posits that emerging tumor organoid platforms, particularly tumor organoid-on-a-chip systems, represent a transformative solution. A key contribution of this review is the introduction of a comprehensive evaluation framework for assessing nanomedicine efficacy, safety and clinical correlation using organoid platforms, offering a critical comparison with conventional models. Furthermore, we identify pivotal challenges including simulation of tumor immune microenvironment, material compatibility, and integration with artificial intelligence, and propose innovative bioengineering approaches to address them. By faithfully bridging the gap between in vitro models and human pathophysiology, tumor organoid platforms are poised to unlock the full clinical potential of next-generation cancer nanomedicine.Keywords: Cancer nanomedicine, Tumor organoids, Tumor organoid-on-a-chip, Preclinical models, Clinical translation, Precision medicine

Introduction

The global incidence of malignant tumors and related mortality continue to rise, underscoring an urgent need for more effective and tailored cancer control strategies [1]. Current standard cancer therapeutic modalities, including surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, targeted therapy and immunotherapy have markedly improved patient outcomes, yet each is constrained by significant limitations. Surgical resection is frequently ineffective against microscopic metastases, leading to high rates of recurrence. Systemic chemotherapy is plagued by dose-limiting toxicities and the inevitable emergence of multidrug resistance [2]. While radiotherapy remains effective for certain tumors, achieving maximal tumor damage while minimizing harm to healthy tissues presents an ongoing challenge [3]. Furthermore, immunotherapy faces substantial clinical hurdles in many cancer types, largely due to the tumor immunosuppressive microenvironment and the risk of immune-related adverse events in some patients [4]. Collectively, these challenges highlight a critical unmet need for more effective, precise, and safer therapeutic strategies.

Over the past decades, nanomedicine has undergone unprecedented development. By encapsulating therapeutic agents in nanoscale carriers, nanomedicines can enhance drug stability, prolong circulation time, and enable targeted delivery to tumor tissues, thereby reducing off-target toxicity [5]. Targeting strategies have evolved from passive accumulation to active targeting and even organelle-specific delivery, demonstrating considerable promise in oncology [6, 7]. Currently, more than 80 novel nanomedicines for cancer therapy are under clinical evaluation worldwide [8]. Despite these advances and a robust clinical pipeline, the translation rate of cancer nanomedicine from bench to bedside remains disappointingly low—a paradox wherein success in conventional preclinical models often fails to predict efficacy in human patients.

A primary culprit for this translational failure is the inadequacy of existing preclinical models. Traditional assessment of nanomedicine has relied heavily on animal models and two-dimensional (2D) cell cultures, while these systems exhibit significant limitations in capturing the complexity of human tumors. In response, advanced in vitro models have been developed specifically for evaluating cancer nanomedicine. Among these, three-dimensional (3D) organoid technology represents a paradigm shift in preclinical research, emerging as an indispensable tool for both basic investigation and translational applications. Organoids are sophisticated in vitro models that accurately recapitulate the 3D architecture and physiological functions of human organs, and they have demonstrated remarkable fidelity to their in vivo counterparts. They can be generated from diverse cellular sources, including adult stem cells (ASCs) and pluripotent stem cells (PSCs) [9].

Tumor-derived organoids preserve the genetic, pathological, and histological characteristics of the original malignancy. The integration of microfluidic engineering has further advanced this technology through tumor organoid-on-a-chip (TOoC) systems. By culturing tumor organoids within a precisely controlled microfluidic environment, these platforms create pathologically relevant tumor models that offer dual advantages: scalable in vitro expansion of tumor organoids, and faithful recapitulation of critical tumor features, including tumor heterogeneity and a complex tumor microenvironment (TME) [10]. The synergy of biological authenticity and engineering control in these hybrid systems provides unprecedented predictive power, scalability, and physiological relevance for evaluating next-generation cancer nanomedicines (Fig. 1).

Tumor organoids facilitate the clinical translation of nanomedicine by enabling: (1) high-throughput drug response screening, (2) optimization of delivery routes (e.g., intravenous, inhaled, oral), (3) real-time monitoring via integrated sensors, (4) precision diagnostics, and (5) evaluation of combination therapies

In this review, we examine recent advances at the intersection of cancer nanomedicine and organoid technology. We first evaluate the distinct advantages of tumor organoids and TOoC systems over conventional models, emphasizing their potential in bridging the gaps between nanomedicine development and clinical implementation (Fig. 2). We then outline the current landscape and translational challenges in cancer nanomedicine. Finally, we discuss the key hurdles that must be overcome, including the faithful recreation of the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME), issues of standardization, and the integration of complex data with artificial intelligence (AI), and propose future directions to fully realize the translational potential of these innovative platforms.

Organoid technology platforms

Origin and derivation of organoids

While conventional 2D cell cultures have been fundamental for in vitro cell studies, they fail to recapitulate the architectural and functional complexity of human organs. To bridge this gap, a shift has been made towards a 3D culture system to capitalize on the self-organizing properties and stemness of mammalian cells to generate organoids. Organoids are primarily derived from two fundamental classes of stem cells: ASCs harvested from differentiated tissues, and PSCs, which include both induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) and embryonic stem cells (ESCs) [17]. Both ASC- and PSC-derived organoids can form 3D models that partially replicate structural and functional attributes of their in vivo counterparts, but they are developed through distinct derivation methodologies. ASC-derived organoids rely on a mixture of growth factors within the culture medium to mimic the natural stem cell niche, supporting self-organization and lineage specification. In contrast, PSC-derived organoids are typically generated through a guided, sequential differentiation process. This involves the timed addition of specific morphogens and growth factors to simulate embryonic developmental signaling pathways, thereby directing cell fate toward the desired tissue lineage [18].

ASC-derived organoids

ASC-derived organoids with a 3D epithelial structure are derived from stem cells that are capable of self-renewal and differentiation into organ-specific epithelial cells [19]. They are typically created by embedding dissociated ASCs or single-cell suspensions within extracellular matrix (ECM) hydrogels such as Matrigel (Fig. 3) [20]. This approach was pioneered in mouse systems and subsequently adapted for human cells. A landmark study by Sato et al. demonstrated the long-term expansion and differentiation of purified Lgr5⁺ intestinal stem cells in laminin-rich Matrigel, generating organoids with distinct crypt-villus structures [21]. The resulting organoids contained all intestinal cell types and they were structurally similar to those derived from entire crypts.

Generation of organoids from adult stem cells (ASCs) and induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs). Organoids can be derived from either ASCs isolated from tissue samples or iPSCs generated via somatic cell reprogramming. Following induction, both cell types undergo 3D culture in a defined medium supplemented with lineage-specific morphogens and small molecules. Key regulators, including WNT agonists (e.g., CHIR99021), growth factors (e.g., FGF, EGF, HGF), and niche components (e.g., Noggin, R-spondin 1, B27) are used in distinct combinations to direct differentiation toward specific organ lineages, such as brain, liver, kidney, intestine, stomach, and lung

Next, Ootani et al. reported an alternative method for culturing intestinal organoids. They cultured neonatal tissues in 3D collagen gels with an air–liquid interface, which supported long-term intestinal proliferation and multilineage differentiation regulated by RSpo1-Fc, a Wnt agonist [22]. Building on these pioneering studies, a variety of organoids, including those from Lgr5+ stem cell-containing organs, have been subsequently created, such as colon [23], gastric [24], and liver organoids [25]. Furthermore, human organoid models, encompassing airway [26], gastric [27], pancreatic [28], kidney [29], prostate [30], lung [31], and brain organoids [32], have been established from stem cells or progenitor cells obtained from diverse tissue sources.

PSC-derived organoids

Organoids can also be generated from PSCs, including ESCs and iPSCs. PSCs are characterized by unlimited self-renewal and the capacity to differentiate into any cell type. Therefore, they can be used for the creation of organoids comprising not only epithelial but also mesenchymal and endothelial components. These organoids are unique for modeling human organogenesis and early development. The Sasai laboratory pioneered this field. They generated retinal organoids from mouse ESCs [33]. However, the use of human ESCs is constrained by stringent ethical regulations, and their clinical and research application have been hampered [34]. Consequently, iPSCs are frequently employed for organoid generation. Compared to ESCs, the derivation of organoids from iPSCs requires an additional reprogramming step to convert somatic cells into PSCs, followed by exposure to germ layer- and tissue-specific patterning factors and embedding in Matrigel to support three-dimensional structure formation (Fig. 3) [35].

Recapitulating key developmental pathways is critical for the generation of PSC-derived organoids. Directed differentiation into the three germ layers is achieved by adding specific growth factors and small molecules to the culture medium [36]. For instance, Activin A and Wnt agonists induce endoderm differentiation, giving rise to various endodermal organoids, including gastric [37], lung [38], thyroid [39], and intestinal [40] organoids. Mesoderm differentiation, induced by BMP and FGF, has been successfully used to generate kidney organoids that recapitulate key features of human fetal kidneys [41]. Ectoderm induction, typically achieved using BMPs to suppress endoderm and mesoderm fates while promoting ectoderm-specific gene expression, has enabled the creation of organoids for the central nervous system, including brain [42], retinal [43], and hippocampal models [44], significantly advancing research in neurodevelopment.

Tumor organoids

There are very few in vitro models that faithfully recapitulate the profound inter- and intratumoral heterogeneity of human cancers, which has become a major bottleneck in cancer nanomedicine development. Patient-specific variations in the tumor architecture and microenvironment have a direct, profound impact on blood circulation, vascular extravasation, accumulation, penetration, cellular uptake, drug release, and therapeutic efficacy of nanomedicine [45]. Patient-derived tumor organoids can partially recapitulate these heterogeneous features of tumors, and they have been used for enhancing mechanistic understanding of tumorigenesis and evaluating therapeutic responses of nanomedicine [16]. Building upon established methods for culturing healthy epithelial organoids, Sato and colleagues pioneered a significant advancement by successfully establishing long-term organoid cultures directly from colorectal cancer patient tissues [46]. Subsequent multi-omics analyses have validated that these tumor-derived organoids preserve the molecular characteristics and heterogeneity of the original tumors over extended cultivation periods.

Tumor organoids can be derived from diverse sources, including primary or metastatic tumors, circulating tumor cells (CTCs), and malignant cells isolated from body fluids [47]. The preparation typically follows two principal approaches: complete enzymatic or mechanical dissociation of samples into single-cell suspensions, or the processing of tissue into millimeter-sized fragments. These cells or tissue fragments are then embedded within a 3D extracellular matrix (e.g., Matrigel) and cultured under defined conditions optimized to support stemness and proliferation, ultimately forming self-organizing tumor organoids [48]. Alternatively, tumor organoids can be engineered by introducing specific oncogenic driver mutations into healthy organoids to model the sequential steps of tumor initiation and progression [46]. Irrespective of the derivation method, large-scale tumor organoid biorepositories have been established for numerous cancer types, including colorectal [49], breast [50], gastric [51], pancreatic [52], and sarcoma [53] (Table 1). These living biobanks serve as invaluable high-throughput platforms for nanomedicine screening and therapy evaluation, making substantial contributions to the advancement of personalized oncology.

Despite these significant advances, conventional tumor organoid models often fail to fully recapitulate the complex TME, predominantly due to the lack of key stromal components. For example, they do not possess a functional vascular system, therefore, they can not be used for elucidating the in-depth mechanisms of overcoming systemic physiological barriers by nanomedicine, such as inefficient extravasation from the vascular endothelium into the tumor interstitium, as well as rapid clearance by the bloodstream, the reticuloendothelial system, and the kidneys. Furthermore, although primary tumor-derived organoids initially contain stromal cells, such as immune cells and fibroblasts, these cells undergo progressive loss during prolonged culture [47]. The depletion of these stromal cells in the tumor organoids leads to compromised or diminished tumor barriers, which are essential for assessing the effectiveness in delivering nanomedicine. To address these gaps, the TOoC platform that integrates perfusion, endothelial barriers, and stromal/tumor co-culture have been increasingly employed for the evaluation of nanomedicine.

Tumor organoid-on-a-chip (TOoC) systems

Compared to tumor organoids, microfluidic-based TOoC systems have emerged as a transformative strategy. TOoC platforms, a cutting-edge 3D biomimetic technology, have synergistically integrated microfluidic engineering principles, tissue fabrication techniques, and patient-derived tumor specimens [93]. By co-culturing endothelial cells (ECs) with tumor cells within precisely engineered microarchitectures, TOoC platforms enable the formation of perfusable, lumenized vascular networks that recapitulate tumor-associated vasculature [94]. By incorporating endothelial barriers and physiological flow conditions, direct visualization and quantification of nanomedicine blood circulation, vascular extravasation, tumor-targeted accumulation and penetration, as well as cellular uptake, are realized in these platforms [95].

Furthermore, the incorporation of patient-derived immune components such as T lymphocytes and tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), facilitates physiologically relevant modeling of immune cell–tumor interactions [96] and enables evaluation of the interplay between nanomedicines and the host immune system, which static organoids cannot recapitulate. Moreover, these platforms promote the establishment of structured culture systems in which stromal cells and tumor cells can be co-localized within 3D hydrogels. This configuration provides a sophisticated platform for studying tumor-induced angiogenesis and lymphangiogenesis, both of which are critical for tumor growth and metastasis.

In addition, this advanced experimental paradigm enables precise spatiotemporal control over physicochemical parameters within the microenvironment, achieving high-fidelity recapitulation of native tumor pathophysiology. By integrating 3D spheroids with microfluidic technology, TOoC platforms allow precise regulation of nutrient flow, oxygen gradients, shear stress, and interstitial pressure [97]. The gradients created in these TOoC platforms could be mapped to those in a TME, such as the oxygen gradient from the normoxic surface to the hypoxic core of a tumor. These gradients are critical for assessing nanomedicine uptake, drug release from stimuli-responsive carriers, and the emergence of drug resistance. For example, the hypoxic core of solid tumors was accurately replicated in a TOoC platform, and this platform was used to evaluate the penetration of nanomedicine into hypoxic tumor regions [98], which is a critical test that static models fail to provide. To date, a diverse array of human tumor organoid chips has been developed, encompassing models of lung [99], brain [100], liver [101], colorectal [102], breast [103], and pancreatic [104] cancers. These chips capture key features of the TME, including biochemical gradients, niche factors, and the dynamic interplay between tumor cells and the ECM.

Advantages of tumor organoids and TOoC platforms

For decades, conventional animal models and 2D cell cultures have been extensively utilized in biomedical research and have contributed to notable successes of drug for cancer therapy. While 2D cultures offer simplicity and high-throughput compatibility, they fail to recapitulate the complex tissue architecture and heterogeneity of in vivo systems. Although animal models can partially address these challenging issues, they often display interspecies discrepancies, which may compromise the predictive validity of preclinical data for human clinical application [105]. In this context, tumor organoids and TOoC platforms have emerged as significant advances, offering more physiologically relevant systems that capture inter- and intra-tumoral heterogeneity, recapitulate key elements of the TME (including stromal cells and ECM), and enable the fluidic interconnection of multiple organ chips to simulate multi-organ interactions and systemic pharmacokinetics [106] (Fig. 4). Together, they form a robust toolbox for the preclinical evaluation of next-generation nanomedicines.

Preservation of tumor heterogeneity

Tumor heterogeneity, encompassing both inter-tumor (variation between patients) and intra-tumor (variation within a single tumor), represents a major obstacle in developing effective, personalized therapies. A comprehensive understanding of the heterogeneity is pivotal for devising precision therapeutic strategies, and robust in vitro models capable of faithfully recapitulating tumor heterogeneity are essential for evaluating these strategies. In a comparative computational study, 657 cancer cell lines, 415 patient-derived xenografts, 26 genetically engineered mouse models, and 131 tumor organoids were assessed against 22 natural tumor types and 36 subtypes. The analysis revealed that tumor organoids exhibit superior transcriptional fidelity compared to xenografts and cell lines. Notably, several patient-derived tumor organoids displayed classification scores comparable to natural tumors, underscoring their exceptional potential for accurately mapping tumor heterogeneity [107]. Patient-derived tumor organoids have been widely employed to model this complexity. For instance, Jacob et al. established glioblastoma organoids that faithfully replicated the histological features, cellular diversity, gene expression profiles, and mutation landscapes of the original patient tumors, highlighting their utility as highly representative preclinical models [108].

The TOoC technology further enhances the ability to model tumor heterogeneity with greater physiological relevance. Microfluidic techniques allow precise control over microscale environmental parameters, offering a dynamic context to model intricate processes such as tumor invasion and extravasation during metastasis. Chen et al. developed a transwell-integrated organoid chip platform to simulate tumor metastasis. This system accurately mimicked key steps of invasion and extravasation, and its multi-chamber design enabled high-throughput, real-time analysis of metastatic processes [109]. Additionally, TOoC systems designed for culturing precision-cut tumor slices have been shown to maintain native tissue heterogeneity while significantly enhancing viability and culture longevity. This approach enables prolonged study of tumor progression, invasion, and metastasis, and provides a predictive platform for assessing nanomedicine efficacy and toxicity [110].

Tumor organoids and TOoC platforms can preserve the molecular and phenotypic diversity of the original tumor, allowing the assessment of differential treatment responses of nanomedicine across distinct tumor subpopulations. A recent study utilized single-cell-derived tumor organoids to evaluate the sensitivity of colorectal cancer subpopulations to conventional chemotherapy, thereby facilitating the phenotypic characterization of drug-resistant subpopulations [111]. Furthermore, RNA sequencing analysis of resistant organoids identified candidate drugs targeting resistance-related pathways on an individual patient basis. These findings confirm that organoid-based approaches can reveal subpopulation-specific drug responses and resistance mechanisms. The organoids approach to conventional chemotherapy can be directly transferable to nanomedicine screening. Heterogeneous cellular uptake and efficacy across tumor subclones are critical for the development of personalized nanomedicine.

Dynamic reconstruction of the TME

The TME is a sophisticated and dynamic ecosystem composed of diverse cellular and acellular elements. Cellular components include tumor cells, ECs, CAFs, mesenchymal stem cells, and immune cells, while the acellular constituents encompass the ECM, soluble signaling factors, and interstitial fluid pressure. Collectively, these elements critically influence tumor metabolism, angiogenesis, and immune evasion, playing pivotal roles in tumorigenesis, progression, and metastasis [112]. Faithfully reconstructing the TME within 3D models is therefore essential, as it not only captures critical tumor-stroma dynamics but also establishes a predictive platform for evaluating nanomedicine efficacy. While co-culturing CAFs and immune cells with tumor organoids represents a step toward preserving TME integrity, such static co-culture approaches often fall short of fully recapitulating its complexity. In contrast, microfluidic-based systems, with their precisely engineered chambers and channels, enable precise control over fluid flow, cell positioning, and chemical gradients, offering a more accurate and dynamic mimicry of the native TME [113].

The vascular system, lined with vascular endothelium, is essential for tumor growth and metastasis. Tumor cells and stromal cells secrete pro-angiogenic factors that stimulate neovascularization, enhancing oxygenation and nutrition delivery. Integrating functional, perfusable vascular networks into organoids is vital to meet their metabolic demands and to study delivery barriers. In the early 3D microvascular systems, EC suspensions or microbeads coated with ECs were encapsulated in extracellular matrix gels to form microvessels, serving as valuable models for angiogenesis [114]. Microfluidic advancements have enabled the development of vascular networks through two primary methods: patterning ECs within predefined microchannels to guide self-organization and inducing stable anastomosis between adjacent vascular structures. These techniques are crucial for elucidating angiogenesis dynamics and for constructing advanced TOoC systems. For instance, Paek et al. innovatively combined microfluidic 3D cell culture with angiogenic self-assembly to create perfusable 3D microvascular beds, which were then integrated with lung cancer cells to establish a vascularized organoid model of human lung adenocarcinoma [115].

As the most abundant cell type in the TME, CAFs are recognized as key architects of tumor progression. Activated CAFs secrete a plethora of paracrine factors that promote tumor and vascular growth while contributing to a tumor immunosuppressive microenvironment. To functionally interrogate tumor-stroma crosstalk, Liu et al. established a 3D co-culture model of liver cancer organoids with CAFs [116]. Their findings demonstrated that CAFs promote tumor organoid growth through both direct cell–cell contact and paracrine signaling. Conversely, cancer cells secrete factors to modulate CAF activation. Notably, tumor organoids exhibited increased resistance to clinical anticancer drugs when co-cultured with CAFs or treated with CAF-conditioned medium, underscoring the critical role of CAFs in driving liver cancer progression and chemoresistance [117].

With the rise of immunotherapy as a mainstream cancer treatment, recreating the TIME using organoids has gained significant attention. Immune cells in the TIME exert dual roles, either suppressing or promoting tumor progression. TAMs, derived from circulating monocytes, are among the most abundant immune populations and are key modulators of therapeutic responses [118]. Current methods for generating immune-competent organoids primarily involve adding specific immune cell types to epithelial cultures or maintaining native immune populations from patient biopsies [113]. For example, Zhou et al. generated cholangiocarcinoma organoids by co-culturing tumor cells with peripheral blood mononuclear cells or T cells, and demonstrated effective anti-tumor immune responses using flow cytometry and live-cell imaging [119].

Furthermore, immune cells have been successfully integrated into microfluidic chips to dissect their spatial and functional interactions with cancer cells. Ye et al. utilized a TOoC platform to study macrophage polarization in tumors, finding that M1 macrophages inhibited tumor growth and angiogenesis via CXCL9/CXCL10, whereas M2 macrophages promoted tumor cell motility through MMP7 and ANGPT2 [120]. In another study, Jose et al. employed a microfluidic model to examine natural killer (NK) cell immunotherapy and antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity. Their model revealed that NK cells infiltrate tumor spheroids more rapidly than therapeutic antibodies, a process guided by chemokine receptors activated by factors such as CXCL12 and CCL28 [121]. These advanced models provide comprehensive, dynamic insights into the TIME, thereby accelerating the development of more effective immunotherapeutic strategies.

By faithfully recapitulating the cellular and acellular complexity of the TME, tumor organoid and TOoC platforms have refined the multidimensional assessment of nanomedicine behavior. As discussed earlier, the integration of perfusable vascular networks enables direct evaluation of nanomedicine from multiple dimensions. Furthermore, the incorporation of CAFs and their secreted ECM in tumor organoid and TOoC platforms creates a biomimicking barrier, providing a more physiologically relevant context. This barrier can be leveraged for assessing the nanomedicine penetration depth and therapeutic efficacy in a TME. In addition, the inclusion of immune cells has opened the door to functional validation of immunomodulatory nanomedicines. For instance, a recent study using pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma organoids evaluated the immune activation, specificity, and combinatorial synergy of a nanovaccine, which could not be assessed in conventional models [122]. Collectively, these advances support that tumor organoids and TOoC platforms can recapitulate the TME characteristics in vitro that are essential for nanomedicine evaluation.

Modeling multi-organ interactions

Dynamic and multifaceted interactions between organs epitomize the systemic complexity of human physiology. This complexity is profoundly amplified in cancer. Tumor initiation and progression often arise from orchestrated coordination of multiple organ systems. Therefore, it is crucial that in vitro models are capable of recapitulating the intricate crosstalk between tumors and distant organs. The TOoC technology allows the creation of a sophisticated platform to simulate systemic, inter-organ communication. By integrating multiple organoids or tissue models within a single, interconnected microfluidic circuit, body‑on‑a‑chip platforms can be employed for pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic modeling, ADME profiling (absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion), quantitative systems pharmacology, and other physiology‑grounded computational studies [123]. For instance, a human four‑organ system comprising heart, liver, skeletal muscle, and neural modules has been developed to assess long‑term drug toxicity via continuous monitoring of cellular functions [124]. More recently, Wang et al. constructed an 18‑organ micro-physiological system by coupling a vascular network with an excretory system to replicate in vivo blood distribution and waste elimination. This system successfully captured the dynamic relationship between the accumulated drug concentration and the level of organ‑specific toxicity [125].

These advances enable the use of TOoC platforms to evaluate the ADME of nanomedicines in vivo, thereby addressing critical questions related to prodrug activation, on‑target accumulation, and metabolite‑mediated toxicity. Moreover, real‑time assessment of functional changes in each organoid can be achieved through various analytical techniques, while omics analyses can uncover signaling pathways by identifying inter‑organ secretions and metabolites. Mathematical modeling and AI‑based data analytics further allow quantification of multiple inter‑organ interactions and prediction of complex modes of action [126]. Together, these capabilities facilitate the analysis of nanomedicine targets and mechanisms of action, helping to resolve key issues associated with target accumulation and metabolite‑mediated toxicity.

Advances in nanomedicine-based cancer diagnosis and therapy

The biomedical research landscape is witnessing a significant upsurge in nanomedicine development. These meticulously engineered formulations are designed to enhance therapeutic delivery, improving efficacy while mitigating associated risks. Nanomedicines are engineered to execute a multi-stage process [127]. Current research extends to a wide spectrum of multifunctional “smart” nanocarriers capable of delivering diverse therapeutic payloads. Various nanocarrier platforms, including inorganic nanoparticles, lipid-based systems, polymeric constructs, and bio-derived vehicles, have been extensively explored and systematically evaluated for their drug delivery potential (Fig. 5). Through meticulous optimization of physicochemical properties (e.g., size, geometry, stiffness, surface characteristics), these carriers are engineered into sophisticated, multifunctional delivery systems. The resulting nanomedicines can significantly enhance the therapeutic index of anticancer agents, marking a pivotal stride in cancer therapy.

Cancer diagnosis

Early cancer detection relies heavily on high sensitivity and specificity [128]. Nanomedicine significantly enhances the capture efficiency of circulating biomarkers (e.g., exosomes, circulating tumor cells) and improves tumor boundary visualization through high surface-area-to-volume ratio [129], active and passive tumor targeting [130], and multimodal imaging capabilities [131]. Innovations such as stimuli-responsive nanoprobes further amplify diagnostic signals specifically at pathological sites [132]. For example, pH-sensitive nanoprobes and serum protease-sensitive nanoprobes [133] have both demonstrated high sensitivity and specificity. However, the actual efficacy of these nanotheranostic tools is highly dependent on the complex physiological environment. In solid tumors, a dense ECM severely restricts nanoprobe tissue penetration, while phenotypic heterogeneity can compromise ligand-receptor targeting specificity [134]. Conventional 2D cultures cannot replicate these 3D spatial barriers or signal attenuation effects. Consequently, nanoprobes that perform exceptionally well in 2D assays often suffer a steep drop in signal-to-noise ratio in vivo. Tumor organoids, which faithfully preserve 3D architecture and heterogeneity, provide a much-needed preclinical platform to validate the tissue penetration depth and true targeting specificity of nanomedicine diagnostics.

Cancer therapy

Despite continuous advancements in conventional modalities non-specific biodistribution, systemic toxicity, and acquired resistance remain persistent bottlenecks in clinical oncology. Nanomedicine addresses these challenges through sophisticated engineering across multiple biological dimensions [127]. In chemotherapy, nanomedicines improve tumor targeting through both passive and active strategies [135]. They can also resensitize drug-resistant tumors by reprogramming the TME [136]. In radiotherapy, high-atomic-number nanomedicines enhance radiosensitivity by amplifying radiation energy deposition in tumors via secondary electron cascades. Moreover, they can overcome radiotherapy resistance by alleviating tumor hypoxia [137] and through targeted modulation [138].

As nanomedicine continues to advance, tumor-targeted therapy has been extended to subcellular organelles such as mitochondria [139], the endoplasmic reticulum [140], and the nucleus [141]. However, monotherapy often leads to acquired resistance through secondary mutations, compensatory activation of signaling pathways, and phenotypic plasticity. Nanomedicine platforms address this by enabling the co-delivery of two or more agents, thereby achieving potent synergistic or synthetic lethality effects and mitigating drug resistance [142]. Cancer immunotherapy, on the other hand, faces challenges including off-target activation, an immunosuppressive TME, and rapid therapeutic degradation. Nanomedicines can prolong antibody half-life, enable targeted delivery [143], and reprogram the immunosuppressive TME to improve outcomes [144]. Beyond these, nanomedicine allows the co-delivery of multiple agents with synchronized pharmacokinetics and spatiotemporal tumor accumulation, overcoming the pharmacokinetic disparities and asynchronous biodistribution that often limit conventional drug combinations [145, 146].

However, the success of these highly complex, multi-mechanistic strategies is fundamentally dictated by multicellular interactions and dynamic physical barriers within the TME. Because conventional 2D models lack critical stromal components, vascular structures, and dynamic immune crosstalk, they may lead to overestimated nanomedicine efficacy or misinterpreted mechanisms of action. Consequently, tumor organoid and TOoC platforms that accurately reconstruct these intricate, multi-barrier microenvironments have become essential for reliably evaluating and translating advanced nanotherapeutics.

Deciphering the in vivo fate of nanomedicines through kinetics

To systematically bridge the gap between nanomedicine design and clinical translation, it is essential to understand their in vivo fate through distinct kinetic phases. These phases, including pharmacokinetics, cellular uptake, payload release, and route dependence, represent the critical biological questions that organoid platforms are uniquely positioned to answer.

Pharmacokinetics and tissue penetration

The blood circulation half‑life of nanomedicines critically influences their tumor accumulation. Upon intravenous administration, nanomedicine-protein corona formation often leads to accelerated macrophage clearance [147]. While surface PEGylation or mechanical stiffness modulation can mitigate sequestration by the mononuclear phagocyte system (MPS) [148], successful extravasation remains collectively shaped by abnormal tumor vasculature and the perivascular TME [149]. Once at the tumor site, uniform tissue penetration is critical. Penetration depth is governed by nanomedicine size and binding affinity [149], but is frequently thwarted by dense stromal matrices, elevated interstitial fluid pressure, and impaired lymphatic drainage [150]. TOoC platforms, which incorporate perfusable vascular networks, have emerged as powerful tools to recapitulate these complex physical barriers, enabling real-time assessment of nanomedicine distribution that conventional 2D cultures cannot provide.

Uptake kinetics

Cellular internalization is a prerequisite for therapeutic activity. Most nanomedicines are taken up via endocytic pathways [151], with efficiency largely dictated by physicochemical properties such as surface charge [152] and ligand density [153]. Following internalization, endosomal escape remains a critical bottleneck for effective cytoplasmic drug release. To promote endosomal escape, increasing efforts have focused on designing organelle‑targeting nanomedicines via covalent surface modification [154]. Nevertheless, the challenge of crossing organellar membrane barriers during intracellular trafficking remains to be fully addressed. Interestingly, emerging evidence suggests that cancer cell mutations may also influence nanomedicine internalization [149]. However, direct evidence remains scarce, largely because current in vitro models fail to capture the heterogeneous features of tumor cells in vivo. This highlights an urgent need for next-generation preclinical models to dissect these underexplored cell-nanomedicine interactions.

Release kinetics

Ideally, nanomedicines should release their therapeutic payload exclusively at the tumor site, yet premature leakage during systemic circulation remains a challenge. To circumvent this issue, stimuli-responsive nanomedicines, triggered by endogenous signals (e.g., pH variations [155], enzymatic activity [156], reducing conditions [157]) or exogenous stimuli (e.g., ultrasound [158], light [159], magnetic fields [160]) have been engineered. Despite these advances, the translational potential of such smart systems is limited by the inability of static models to accurately mimic pathological gradients. TOoC platforms, capable of recapitulating physiologically relevant oxygen gradients and pH landscapes, offer a powerful in vitro setting for rigorously validating stimuli-responsive release mechanisms.

Route dependence

Nanomedicines can be delivered via a wide range of administration routes, including oral [161], intravenous [162], intrathecal [163], inhalation [164], intranasal [165], intraperitoneal [166], intratumoral [167], topical [168], intradermal [169], subcutaneous [169], and intravesical injection [170]. Nanomedicine efficacy is highly route-dependent, requiring careful alignment of physicochemical properties with specific physiological barriers. For instance, the blood–brain barrier (BBB) severely restricts systemic access to brain tumors and intrathecal administration bypasses this barrier but demands that nanomedicines remain stable in the high-salt cerebrospinal fluid [171], intraperitoneal injection leverages local high concentrations for peritoneal malignancies [172], and intratumoral injection faces limitations with microscopic metastases [167]. Different routes yield distinct targeting efficiencies and biodistribution profiles. Tumor organoid and TOoC platforms, capable of recapitulating key barriers such as the BBB and dense tumor stroma, offer a powerful preclinical tool to systematically compare administration routes and optimize delivery strategies for specific nanomedicine formulations.

Current landscape and clinical translation of nanomedicine

Synergistic advances in disease biology and material science have established nanomedicine as a pivotal enabler of precision oncology. Several nanomedicines have received regulatory approval and are currently deployed in clinical practice (Table 2). These agents enhance cancer therapy through a range of distinctive advantages: (1) tunable surface chemistry and a high surface-area-to-volume ratio that improve the drug loading capacity, therapeutic efficacy, and safety profiles [173]; (2) enhanced pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics by improving drug stability, solubility, circulation half-life, and tumor accumulation [174]; (3) support for both active and passive targeting, enabling spatially controlled drug delivery to tumors and subcellular compartments [175]; (4) deep tumor penetration and uniform intratumoral drug distribution [176]; (5) co-delivery of multiple therapeutic agents with high precision [177]; (6) development of a theranostic platform [178]; (7) overcoming conventional drug resistance [179]; (8) controlled release profiles to improve dosing regimens and patient adherence [180]; (9) effective antigen and adjuvant delivery in synthetic vaccine design [181]; and (10) remodeling of the pre-metastatic niche to suppress metastasis [182].

Despite these promising features, the clinical application of nanomedicine is severely hampered by unpredictable in vivo behavior, primarily driven by suboptimal toxicity profiles, biodistribution, and delivery efficiency. The long-term biocompatibility, degradation pathways, and excretion profiles of many nanocarriers remain incompletely characterized, necessitating rigorous preclinical evaluations. Furthermore, although nanomedicines generally exhibit superior tumor accumulation compared to free drugs, their performance is heavily constrained by complex systemic physiological barriers and dense tumor tissue barriers that conventional 2D models fail to accurately recapitulate. In molecular imaging, their prolonged circulation and non-specific accumulation can also generate false-positive signals. These issues underscore an urgent need to move beyond general promises of “better prediction” and instead establish physiologically relevant platforms that can systematically dissect nanomedicine-biology interactions.

In this context, recent breakthroughs in tumor organoid and TOoC technologies provide compelling, actionable solutions. By retaining key tumor characteristics and microenvironmental cues under precisely controlled conditions, these platforms allow us to deconstruct the translational gap into distinct, practical functions. Specifically, integrating organoid-based models into nanomedicine development defines five discrete translational functions: discovery-stage screening to identify lead candidates through high-throughput evaluation of nanomedicine libraries [184]; mechanistic validation by dissecting tumor organoids at single-cell resolution to decode resistance pathways [111]; patient stratification using patient-derived organoids to identify responder subsets [185]; toxicity modeling utilizing matched healthy organoids to predict off-target toxicities [186]; and route optimization leveraging region-specific TOoC barriers to quantitatively compare administration routes [187]. Together, these functions enable comprehensive evaluation and screening of nanomedicines, supporting precision cancer therapy, optimization of administration routes, improvement of diagnostic nanomedicines, and the design of effective combination regimens (Fig. 6). Ultimately, this targeted deployment will enhance clinical predictability and bridge the critical gap between promising in vitro designs and successful clinical applications.

Rather than merely providing a setting for isolated case studies, this integration establishes a foundational framework for synthesizing recurring principles across pharmacokinetics, safety, and therapeutic efficacy. Evaluating nanomedicine safety and toxicity

Conventional toxicity assessment of nanomedicine is often constrained by a lack of reliable in vitro models, with complete toxicological profiles frequently emerging only during clinical trials. A recurring lesson from early organoid-based toxicity studies is that the physical configuration and cellular composition of the organoid model fundamentally dictate the predictive accuracy of nanotoxicity readouts. For instance, comparative studies have demonstrated that suspension-cultured organoids exhibit superior nanoparticle uptake and heightened sensitivity compared to ECM-embedded cultures, establishing them as a simplified yet more predictive tool for baseline safety assessment [190]. Furthermore, temporal dynamics play a critical role; studies using iPSC-derived brain organoids revealed highly complex, dose-dependent effects of silver nanoparticles (AgNPs), ranging from paradoxical proliferative effects at low doses to severe cytoskeletal disruption at higher doses [189].

However, synthesizing these findings reveals that conventional monoculture organoids, regardless of optimization, remain fundamentally insufficient for comprehensive nanotoxicity profiling, harboring several critical blind spots that must be explicitly addressed. First, the absence of stromal and immune cells renders these models incapable of evaluating immune-mediated toxicity (e.g., complement activation cascades, hypersensitivity reactions, and macrophage-driven pro-inflammatory cytokine release) [122]. Second, the typical experimental durations (days to weeks) fail to capture the chronic tissue damage caused by long-term exposure to clinically persistent nanomedicines. Third, the lack of systemic clearance pathways in static organoids renders them inadequate for modeling the long-term bioaccumulation of non-degradable nanomaterials. Finally, batch-to-batch variability and the lack of validated correlations with in vivo endpoints preclude standardized toxicity benchmarks. Furthermore, Rodriguez Mier et al. showed that organoid assays alone may not reliably predict inter-patient variability. Consequently, the field must therefore move beyond proof-of-concept (PoC) screening. Future platforms should incorporate prolonged culture viability, immune/stromal components, and standardized toxicity benchmarks to truly enhance the predictive value of preclinical safety assessments.

Screening of cancer nanomedicines

Given the vast diversity of nanomedicine physicochemical properties and their resulting pharmacokinetic variations [191], robust in vitro screening is essential. However, a well-designed screening pipeline requires more than isolated examples of successful drug testing; it demands a standardized, principle-driven workflow. Based on recurring lessons from current practices, we propose a five-component integrated framework to guide future screening: (1) histopathological and genetic sequencing validation to confirm organoid fidelity to the original tumor; (2) high-content imaging and other techniques to monitor nanomedicine stability and targeting efficacy; (3) penetration analysis to measure the diffusion depth of nanoparticles into hypoxic core regions; (4) uptake mapping to quantify cell-type-specific internalization; and (5) orthogonal toxicity assessment in matched healthy organoids to predict biocompatibility. Adopting this framework will standardize cross-study comparisons and boost the predictive validity of organoid-based screening for in vivo and clinical testing.

Applying this framework illustrates its utility. For example, patient-derived gastric cancer organoids were established from surgically resected specimens [192]. Histopathological and molecular characterization (including H&E staining, immunohistochemistry, Alcian blue staining, and whole-exome sequencing) confirmed high fidelity to the primary tumors. Using this platform, two clinically relevant paclitaxel nanomedicines, albumin-bound PTX and liposomal PTX, were comparatively evaluated. RNA sequencing of differentially responding organoids revealed treatment-specific gene expression changes, while live-dead staining and confocal microscopy mapped spatiotemporal drug distribution, which successfully predicted in vivo efficacy. Similarly, integrating TOoC technology into this framework has provided unprecedented microenvironmental control. A glioblastoma TOoC model incorporating a simulated blood–brain barrier demonstrated that macrophage-mediated nanomedicine delivery significantly enhanced tumor infiltration and cytotoxicity compared to free drugs [193].

Despite these advanced workflows, a critical synthesis of current literature reveals a persistent gap: the vast majority of screening studies omit step 5 (healthy organoid controls). This oversight is particularly dangerous for nanomedicines, which carry distinct risks of off-target toxicity and immunogenicity. The few studies that have incorporated paired healthy organoids (e.g., demonstrating the selective cytotoxicity of MoS₂ nanosheets in colorectal cancer models [186]) highlight the necessity of this step for establishing a true therapeutic window. Moreover, even when tumor-selective effects are observed, organoid assays alone may not reliably predict inter-patient variability due to unaccounted systemic factors [194]. To fulfill the promise of organoid-based screening, the field must mandate comprehensive, standardized pipelines that integrate healthy controls with multi-omics and pharmacokinetic modeling.

Optimizing drug delivery pathways

The efficacy of nanomedicine is inextricably linked to the route of administration, with each route presenting a distinct profile of biological and physical barriers [195–197]. Therefore, selecting the optimal administration route is crucial for maximizing nanomedicine performance (Fig. 9).

Administration route-dependent delivery of nanomedicines and their associated biological barriers. The therapeutic efficacy of nanomedicine is critically determined by the chosen administration route, each of which presents a distinct set of physiological and cellular barriers. a Oral delivery must sequentially overcome the intestinal mucus layer, the epithelial barrier, and hepatic first-pass metabolism to achieve systemic bioavailability. b Intravenous injection, while bypassing first-pass metabolism, faces challenges including rapid clearance by the mononuclear phagocyte system, hemodynamic shear forces, and the requirement for effective vascular extravasation to reach tumor tissue. c Inhalation requires an optimal aerodynamic particle size to avoid mucociliary clearance and to facilitate penetration across the alveolar-epithelial barrier

The intestinal epithelium serves as both a critical barrier and a metabolic interface in the gastrointestinal tract, playing a decisive role in oral drug absorption and bioavailability. The expression of drug-metabolizing enzymes and transporters in enterocytes significantly influences the efficacy of oral administered nanomedicine, motivating the development of intestinal tumor organoid and TOoC models for evaluation. Sokolova et al. employed colorectal cancer organoids and normal intestinal organoids to assess the uptake and distribution of ultrasmall gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) [198]. Results confirmed efficient internalization of fluorescently labeled Au-AF647 nanoparticles. Treatment with doxorubicin-loaded AuNPs (Au-Dox-AF647) induced significant cell death in CT-26 tumor organoids, whereas control nanoparticles showed no cytotoxicity, demonstrating therapeutic potential.

Beyond biochemical barriers, physical factors such as intestinal peristalsis considerably impact nanomedicine absorption. Fang et al. developed a microfluidic chip with an array of lateral micropores surrounded by pressure channels that periodically compress cultured human colon tumor organoids [199]. This system successfully recapitulates the mechanical stimuli associated with colonic peristalsis, providing a physiologically relevant platform for evaluating nanomedicine behavior under dynamic conditions.

For pulmonary diseases including lung cancer, inhalation represents a highly attractive strategy for localized drug delivery while minimizing systemic exposure. Lung-on-a-chip models have emerged as valuable tools for investigating inhaled nanomedicines, as they replicate key structural and functional features of the pulmonary microenvironment, such as the air–liquid interface and alveolar-capillary barrier. Zhang et al. developed a three-channel microfluidic device accommodating alveolar epithelial cells, ECM, and human vascular ECs to mimic the human alveolar-capillary unit [200]. Introduction of TiO₂-NPs and ZnO-NPs into the alveolar channel simulated nanoparticle inhalation, revealing dose-dependent barrier disruption characterized by reactive oxygen species generation, apoptosis, and increased permeability. Consistent with prior reports, ZnO-NPs displayed greater toxicity than TiO₂-NPs, validating the biological relevance and sensitivity of the TOoC platform.

To extract a deeper, more actionable message from these individual examples, the field must adopt a unified metric framework for rational route selection. TOoC platforms now enable the direct measurement of route-specific, quantifiable parameters that synthesize these physical and biochemical factors under strictly controlled in vitro conditions. For oral delivery, gut-liver-on-a-chip systems can directly measure apparent permeability coefficients and hepatic first-pass metabolism [201]. For inhalation, lung-on-a-chip models equipped with air–liquid interfaces and cyclic mechanical stretch can reliably quantify deposition efficiency, mucociliary clearance rate, and trans-epithelial permeation. For intravenous administration, vascularized TOoC platforms enable the assessment of pharmacokinetics and uptake kinetics. Furthermore, the advent of multi-organ-chip systems now allows for the simulation of inter-organ pharmacokinetics, providing a systemic view of route-dependent effects [202]. By shifting from descriptive route testing to the application of these standardized quantitative metrics, researchers can rationally identify the optimal administration strategy for any given nanomedicine.

Real-time monitoring of nanomedicine

The dynamic growth and development of tumors involve intricate changes in proliferation and metabolism, which significantly reshape the TME. Tumor organoids accurately recapitulate the oxygen and nutrient gradients observed in vivo, making them ideal systems for modeling cellular proliferation, metabolism, and therapeutic responses. Real-time monitoring and analysis of these organoids can therefore provide profound insights into tumor progression and their responses to nanomedicine. Various imaging and analytical techniques have been employed to study organoids, including bright-field microscopy for structural assessment [203], fluorescence-based assays for viability and drug response [204], genomic profiling for treatment-induced gene expression alterations [73], and assays for secreted protein quantification in the extracellular milieu [205]. However, these methods are often limited by potential cellular damage or inherent technical constraints when applied to complex 3D constructs.

To overcome these limitations, the field is converging on a core principle: real-time monitoring must transition from isolated endpoint readouts to the continuous, non-invasive mapping of dynamic physiological and pharmacological gradients (Fig. 10). Currently, these advancements remain largely confined to PoC stages. For instance, integrating surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy with organoid models has demonstrated the ability to continuously map steeper glucose gradients from the core to the periphery of stromal-rich spheroids, a level of metabolic profiling impossible with conventional assays [206]. Furthermore, tracking nanomedicine behavior requires the simultaneous observation of a cascade of physiological barriers (circulation, extravasation, penetration, and release) [207]. A recurring lesson from microfluidic studies is that nanomedicine stability and disassembly kinetics must be evaluated across these cascading barriers in real-time, rather than in isolated static wells. By coupling tumor-on-a-chip platforms with environmentally sensitive dyes, researchers have spectrally distinguished between assembled and disassembled micelle states during perfusion, revealing that micelle stability is a prerequisite for successful extravasation [208].

Fluorescently labeled nanomedicines are then perfused through the integrated vascular channel. Distinguishing these PoC achievements from genuinely scalable applications highlights a major translational bottleneck. To transition from theoretical feasibility to routine, broadly applicable laboratory platforms, the field must prioritize a different set of engineering principles. Specifically, future systems must move away from bespoke setups toward standardized, industrially replicable chip architectures. Furthermore, they must integrate commercially accessible or “plug-and-play” sensors, coupled with harmonized, automated protocols for image acquisition and data analysis. Only by explicitly addressing these scalability requirements by separating them from the biological PoC milestones, can real-time organoid monitoring fulfill its promise as a routine tool in nanomedicine development.

Advancing cancer diagnosis

While nanomedicine offers exceptional tunability for cancer diagnosis, translating these agents requires a fundamental shift in how in vitro models are utilized. Synthesizing current literature reveals a foundational principle: the evaluation criteria for diagnostic nanomedicines must be decoupled from therapeutic metrics. Unlike therapeutics, where penetration depth and cytotoxicity are paramount, diagnostic assessment necessitates a focus on signal-to-background ratio (SBR), limit of detection (LOD), specificity, and the spatial fidelity of the signal within the 3D architecture, with the ultimate goal of achieving clinically relevant thresholds [209, 210]. In this context, tumor organoids serve not merely as 3D cell masses, but as rigorous platforms for validating this spatial and molecular accuracy, overcoming the inability of conventional 2D monolayers to replicate the complex tissue barriers and optical scattering properties that targeted probes must navigate in vivo.

Applying the criteria reveals three distinct, synergistic advantages of organoid-based diagnostic validation (Fig. 11). First, they enable the validation of spatial targeting fidelity, ensuring that targeted probes selectively accumulate in biomarker-high regions, such as the enhanced uptake of cMet-targeting peptides in colorectal adenomas compared to normal controls, providing superior contrast evidence over traditional binding assays [211]. Second, organoids provide a physiologically relevant secretome and metabolic environment for evaluating dynamic sensing nanotechnologies, such as utilizing liver organoids to validate continuous nanoprobe monitoring of albumin fluctuations [212]. Third, their multicellular heterogeneity allows for precise molecular subtyping in a 3D context, where multiplexed nanosensors can simultaneously target specific mRNA or protein signatures to distinguish tumor subtypes, effectively bridging the gap between simple in vitro fluorescence and complex in vivo imaging [213].

Adapted with permission [212, 213]. Copyright 2024 American Chemical Society.

Ultimately, the successful integration of organoids into diagnostic nanomedicine development will rely on establishing standardized workflows based on these principles. By systematically benchmarking SBR, LOD, and spatial resolution against clinical thresholds, organoid platforms can accelerate the transition from proof-of-concept to clinical diagnostic kits. As sensor technologies are increasingly coupled with microfluidic organ-on-a-chip systems, these physiologically relevant models are poised to become the gold standard for translating next-generation diagnostic nanotechnologies into early detection and precision oncology.

Developing combination therapy regimens

Cancer combination therapy, defined as simultaneous or sequential use of multiple treatment modalities, leverages synergistic interactions to enhance antitumor efficacy, overcome drug resistance, and improve patient outcomes [214]. While nanomedicine fundamentally resolves the pharmacokinetic mismatch inherent in conventional combination therapies by ensuring the co-delivery and synchronized biodistribution of synergistic agents, optimal dose ratios and administration sequences can vary substantially even among patients with the same cancer type [215]. Therefore, tumor organoids and TOoC platforms are no longer merely supplementary validation tools; they have become indispensable high-throughput testing beds for rationally designing personalized combination regimens (Fig. 12).

Systematic optimization of combination therapy regimens using TOoC platforms. a Initial screening of diverse therapeutic modalities (e.g., chemotherapy, immunotherapy, targeted therapy) to identify synergistic and safe drug combinations within a patient-specific organoid model. b-c Subsequent optimization of critical administration parameters, including dose ratios, treatment timing, and sequential drug-release profiles from nanomedicines, to achieve an enhanced therapeutic index

Several nanomedicine-assisted combination therapies have demonstrated clinical promise. Notably, CPX-351, a liposomal formulation co-encapsulating cytarabine and daunorubicin at a fixed 5:1 molar ratio [216]. Building on this, a core–shell nanomedicine for co-delivering a chemotherapeutic agent with siRNA exhibited synergistic antitumor activity in breast cancer models [217]. In another breast cancer study, a PEG-lipid-based system co-delivered curcumin and methotrexate, the latter serving as both a cytotoxic agent and a targeting ligand via folate receptor binding [218].

However, systematically optimizing drug ratios, sequences, and release kinetics remains a formidable challenge. Tumor organoids have been utilized to systematically map the multidimensional parameter space of combination therapies, rather than serving as simple binary viability assays. For instance, Ramzy et al. identified an optimized low-dose drug combination in colorectal cancer organoids that outperformed standard chemotherapy regimens [219]. Similarly, a microfluidic 3D organoid culture system was employed to test various combinations and sequential administrations in pancreatic cancer organoids, revealing patient-specific differences in treatment responses [220]. These studies validate the utility of organoids for rational combination therapy design.

Extending this paradigm, Ferreira et al. used mouse pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma organoids to evaluate the combination of gemcitabine with a chitosan nanoparticle vaccine containing the cancer-testis antigen NY-ESO-1 [221]. The combination of NY-ESO-1 nanoparticle-stimulated peripheral blood mononuclear cells and gemcitabine led to a pronounced reduction in organoid size, whereas gemcitabine alone had a minimal effect. This work illustrates how organoid models can facilitate the development of nanoparticle-assisted combination therapies within a clinically relevant timeframe.

Ultimately, the exponential growth of the combination parameter space (drug types, ratios, sequences, and release triggers) highlights a formidable bottleneck in current screening methods. This points toward an emerging overarching principle: the convergence of AI-driven data synthesis with organoid technology is the inevitable next step for personalized combination design. Recent work integrating machine learning with large microfluidic organoid arrays has proven that AI can efficiently navigate this complexity, discovering sequential administration regimens that achieve comparable efficacy to concurrent dosing while substantially reducing total drug dose [222]. Together, these advances indicate that AI will not merely assist in drug screening, but will become a core component in designing personalized, nanomedicine-based combination regimens at the individual patient level.

Challenges and limitations

Recent years have witnessed remarkable progress in nanomedicine, particularly in oncology. The development of cancer nanomedicine typically follows a standardized pipeline encompassing rational design, synthesis, comprehensive physicochemical characterization, in vitro validation using cell-based assays, and preclinical evaluation in murine models. However, the limited throughput and physiological relevance of these conventional platforms contribute to high attrition rates in clinical trials, with Phase II/III discontinuation rates exceeding 60% for oncology nanomedicines. The emergence of organoid technology presents a transformative approach to addressing these limitations. A landmark development occurred in August 2022 when the FDA approved a novel investigational drug (NCT04658472) for clinical trials based exclusively on organoid-derived preclinical data [223]. This milestone underscores the potential of organoid platforms to accelerate nanomedicine translation.

Nevertheless, translating this promise into routine practice requires moving beyond the mere listing of technological hurdles. To drive meaningful translation, this section reframes key challenges as concrete, actionable priorities: establishing standardized readouts, defining inter-laboratory reproducibility metrics, achieving harmonized organoid characterization, and developing minimal reporting standards for nanomedicine testing in organoid systems (Fig. 13).

Emerging frontiers in nanomedicine-organoid integration. a Remodeling the TME using biomimetic nanomaterial scaffolds (e.g., nanofiber hydrogels) to guide organoid maturation and simulate dynamic ECM remodeling, while nanomedicines locally modulate cellular processes such as angiogenesis. b Engineering precise disease models via nanocarrier-mediated delivery of gene-editing tools (e.g., CRISPR-Cas9) to introduce specific oncogenic mutations or correct genetic defects in organoids. c Leveraging AI and machine learning to analyze high-content organoid-nanomedicine interaction data, enabling the rational design and virtual screening of next-generation nanotherapeutics. d Incorporating immune organoids into TOoC platforms to systematically elucidate and modulate nanomedicine-immune system crosstalk, thereby advancing the development of combination immunotherapies

Lack of standardized manufacturing protocols and reporting standards

A fundamental challenge hindering the clinical translation of organoid-nanomedicine platforms is the absence of standardized protocols, which currently prevents the establishment of inter-laboratory reproducibility metrics. Addressing this requires a parallel, approach that tackles the distinct sources of variability in both components, while defining minimal reporting standards.

For nanomedicine, critical quality attributes (e.g., particle size, polydispersity index, drug-loading efficiency), are highly sensitive to synthesis conditions. Minor fluctuations in process conditions can introduce substantial batch-to-batch variabilities, complicating the transition from laboratory-scale preparation to industrial-grade manufacturing [224]. Emerging technologies such as microfluidics and template-assisted microprinting, offer scalable and well-controlled nanomedicine production [225]. However, to ensure inter-laboratory reproducibility, these advancements must be coupled with standardized release kinetics assays and mandatory reporting of synthesis parameters (e.g., flow rates, temperature, precursor concentrations, and purification methods).

Similarly, organoid technology suffers from heterogeneity in starting cell populations, leading to genetic variability and instability in the resulting models [226]. Inconsistencies in culture medium composition, growth factor types and concentrations, and differentiation protocols significantly impact the reliability and comparability of organoid models [227]. Techniques such as gene editing and microfluidics-enabled intracellular delivery can enhance genetic uniformity by establishing homogeneous cell lineages. Furthermore, advances in organoid bioprinting [228] and engineered hydrogel matrices [229] enable spatial control over the tissue architecture and high-throughput generation under defined biochemical and mechanical conditions. However, the reliance on costly media and signaling molecules often impedes scalability. Synthetic biology offers a promising alternative [230]. By incorporating synthetic gene circuits, signaling pathways, and regulatory elements, precise spatiotemporal control over organoid development and function could be achieved. This engineering not only improves the reproducibility and complexity of organoid models but also reduces their reliance on expensive exogenous factors, allowing for direct morphogenetic programming and more accurate disease modeling.

Platform‑specific material challenges, most notably the absorption of small hydrophobic molecules by polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) in TOoC platforms (up to 60% within 24 h) [231], further compromise reproducibility. Progress requires that studies utilizing PDMS-based TOoCs include PDMS-drug absorption coefficients as a mandatory reproducibility metric, while the field actively prioritizes the development of composite material substitutes. Ultimately, realizing the full potential of this integration ultimately hinges on adopting a minimal information standard for organoid-nanomedicine studies (Table 3), ensuring that every published study provides the necessary control parameters for cross-study comparison.

Simulating the complexity of the TIME and harmonized characterization

Tumor development and progression are governed by intricate and dynamic crosstalk between cancer cells and the TIME. The TIME is a pivotal regulator for malignant progression and has become a central focus in cancer nanomedicine development. Developing physiologically relevant in vitro models that accurately recapitulate the TIME holds significant translational value. Such models provide critical insights into nanomedicine-immune system interactions, and serve as a powerful platform for optimizing immune-modulatory nanomedicine formulations. Recent advances in the TOoC technology have enabled the reconstruction of some TIME components. However, these systems still face fundamental challenges in maintaining long-term viability and functional coordination of diverse cellular constituents, including stromal cells, immune subsets, and tumor cells, while preserving their native spatial organization and signaling networks. Beyond local tumor immunity, there is growing interest in the systemic interplay between lymphoid organs and nanomedicine pharmacokinetics. The unique architectural complexity of immune organs, characterized by highly specialized 3D tissue organization and precisely regulated cellular trafficking, presents formidable engineering challenges. Although current organoid technologies have made significant strides in modeling epithelial tissue, they can not yet reconstitute the full spectrum of immune functions, particularly the dynamic lymphocyte priming and trafficking processes that occur in secondary lymphoid organs [232]. Integrating immune organoids with tumor organoids to study the interactions between tumor cells, nanomedicines, and immune cells has emerged as a vital research direction. Elucidating these mechanisms will help circumvent unintended immune activation and guide the development of safer, more effective nanomedicines, ultimately improving clinical success rates.

Furthermore, incorporating vasculature and the ECM poses substantial engineering challenges, as most hydrogels lack essential bioactive factors, and microfluidic approaches alone fail to simulate tumor-induced angiogenesis [233]. Nanomedicine itself can be employed to address these limitations. On one hand, nanofibers can serve as culture scaffolds that, precisely mimic the topological structure and mechanical properties of the native ECM, providing contact guidance for cells [234]. On the other hand, nanomedicine can be designed to deliver factors such as VEGF and EGF, facilitating the generation of mature and stable tumor vasculature within organoids and, thereby constructing more physiologically relevant vascular barriers.

However, as these models become increasingly complex, they risk becoming bespoke, irreproducible systems. To translate these advanced TIME models into reliable screening tools, a concrete priority must be the establishment of a harmonized characterization checklist. This includes mandatory reporting of immune cell subset viability over time, ECM density metrics, and vascular perfusion permeability coefficients under baseline conditions. Only through such standardized characterization can we reliably evaluate whether a nanomedicine is genuinely modulating the TIME or exploiting artifacts of an unstandardized model.

Perspectives and new opportunities

Advanced analytical techniques beyond conventional bulk readouts are essential for unveiling the intrinsic heterogeneity of tumor organoids for nanomedicine evaluation. Recent advances in single-cell omics, spatial transcriptomics, multiplexed imaging modalities, and AI-driven high-content screening platforms may collectively revolutionize the assessment of nanomedicine behavior within organoid models. These advancements enable the examination of cell-type-specific responses, the revelation of spatial heterogeneity, and the dynamic monitoring of drug-tissue interactions in an integrated manner.

Emerging characterization technologies for in-depth revelation of interactions between organoids and nanomedicine

Single-cell omics, particularly single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), have been employed for the dissection of molecular subtypes and the tumor heterogeneity in organoids [235]. This tool can be leveraged to reveal differences in cellular uptake and responses across distinct cell subpopulations in organoids after exposure to nanomedicine, thereby identifying sensitive or resistant subsets, tracing lineage-specific uptake patterns, and ultimately guiding rational nanomedicine design. In addition, spatial transcriptomics preserves the 3D spatial information while profiling gene expression at a high resolution during the assay process, directly revealing the correlation between the cellular function and the spatial microenvironment. In a recent review, the integration of spatial omics with 3D models, such as organoids and TOoC platforms, is systematically discussed [236]. This technique can help decode cell positioning dynamics and microenvironmental responses. Therefore, nanomedicine-induced transcriptional changes could be mapped onto specific structural regions within 3D models, offering a novel perspective for precisely evaluating the penetration, distribution, and mechanisms of action of nanomedicine within tumor organoids.

While spatial transcriptomics provides global molecular landscapes, multiplexed immunofluorescence imaging allows direct visualization of protein-level spatial organization within 3D models. This technique enables simultaneous detection of multiple protein markers while preserving the 3D architecture of tumor organoids, thus allowing direct observation of cellular uptake of nanomedicine by specific cell types, intracellular transportation of nanomedicine within these cells, and drug release from nanomedicine at the intended intracellular target [237]. By integrating automated image acquisition, multiparametric analysis, and machine learning algorithms, the high-content imaging platform can simultaneously analyze an array of morphological and functional parameters, including the organoid size, fluorescence intensity distribution, and subcellular nanomedicine localization [238]. HCS-3DX, a breakthrough in this field, is a next-generation AI-driven automated high-content screening system. It addresses the resolution limitation of current imaging systems, achieves single-cell resolution imaging and analysis, and reliably performs 3D high-content screening at a single-cell level [239]. Consequently, its application may markedly enhance the accuracy and efficiency of the nanomedicine screening process. To maximize impact, the community should adopt standardized imaging and analysis pipelines that enable cross-study comparison of high-content data.

Role of AI in establishing reproducibility and predictive metrics

A particularly attractive direction lies at the convergence of organoid-based evaluation and AI. As an advanced computational tool, AI employs machine learning algorithms to identify complex patterns, construct predictive models, and classify high-dimensional biological data [240]. The rapidly expanding biobanks of tumor organoids provide an unprecedented resource for AI-driven analysis. By analyzing large-scale data from single-cell omics and spatial transcriptomic of tumor organoids, AI can uncover novel therapeutic targets, thereby accelerating the rational design and development of nanomedicines. For example, the molecular and cellular landscape of colorectal cancer was comprehensively characterized by integrating single-cell profiling with bulk transcriptomics and multiplexed immunohistochemistry, and FOLR2+ macrophages were identified as a target for improving chemotherapy responses [241]. This finding could be leveraged for the design of macrophage-targeting nanomedicine.

Beyond target discovery, an AI-driven integration platform that combines cell culture and high content analysis enables the analysis at a single-cell resolution. The platform can streamline the processing of large datasets generated from tumor organoid platforms, thereby remarkably enhancing the throughput and efficiency of nanomedicine screening. Furthermore, AI can systematically analyze imaging and genomic data obtained from nanomedicine-treated organoids, accurately predict therapeutic efficacy of nanomedicine, and identify specific genetic mutations in organoids associated with drug resistance [242]. This capability is poised to accelerate the realization of personalized cancer therapy. In a recent study, an AI-based multimodal approach was used to combine genomic, transcriptomic, and TME features for the prediction of responses to immune checkpoint inhibitors and tyrosine kinase inhibitors, and the power of AI in guiding treatment selection was demonstrated [243].

Collectively, these advances illustrate that the synergy between AI and organoid platforms is not merely incremental but transformative. By enabling high-resolution, data-driven predictions of nanomedicine behavior and patient-specific responses, this integration holds the potential to bridge the gap between preclinical models and clinical outcomes. To realize this potential, the field must prioritize the development of open-access, well-annotated organoid datasets and benchmarked AI models that can be validated across independent laboratories.

Conclusions

Cancer nanomedicine represents a transformative therapeutic frontier, with a steadily expanding pipeline of candidates advancing through clinical trials. However, its clinical transition remains significantly constrained by the limited physiological relevance of conventional preclinical models. Organoid technology, which faithfully recapitulates in vivo tissue architecture and key functional attributes, offers a powerful platform to bridge this translational gap. This review has systematically outlined recent advances in cancer nanomedicine and detailed methodologies for cultivating tumor organoids. More importantly, it provides a holistic perspective on the synergistic integration of these two fields, reframing this integration from a collection of PoC studies into the establishment of a rigorous, metric-driven evaluation framework.

Tumor organoids and TOoC platforms have emerged as transformative models for high-throughput, precise evaluation of nanomedicine efficacy and safety. To fully realize their disruptive potential and accelerate clinical adoption, future research must transition from merely identifying technological hurdles to implementing concrete, field-wide priorities. These include: establishing mandatory material-compatibility metrics to eliminate drug-absorption artifacts in microfluidic systems; adopting harmonized organoid characterization protocols and minimal reporting standards to guarantee inter-laboratory reproducibility; and leveraging AI not just for target discovery, but as the computational backbone for defining standardized, multidimensional readouts across diverse platforms. By translating these priorities into actionable methodological norms, the organoid-nanomedicine interface will provide the robust, predictable foundation required to ultimately deliver personalized, next-generation cancer therapies to the clinic.
