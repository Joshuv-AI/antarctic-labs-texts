On the computation of the infinity Wasserstein distance and the Wasserstein Projection Problem
Gennaro Auricchio, Gabriele Loli, Marco Veneroni

Abstract
Computing the infinity Wasserstein distance and retrieving projections of a probability measure onto a closed subset of probability measures are critical sub-problems in various applied fields. However, the practical applicability of these objects is limited by two factors: either the associated quantities are computationally prohibitive or there is a lack of available algorithms capable of calculating them. In this paper, we propose a novel class of Linear Programming problems and a routine that allows us to compute the infinity Wasserstein distance and to compute a projection of a probability measure over a generic subset of probability measures with respect to any p p -Wasserstein distance with p ∈ [ 1 , ∞ ] p\in[1,\infty] . Keywords: Infinity Wasserstein distance, Wasserstein Projection Problem, Discrete Optimal Transport, Numerical Algorithms for Optimal Transport.

Introduction

Given a Polish space
X
endowed with a metric
d:X\times X\to[0,\infty)
, the
p

X
, namely
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(X)

W_{p}(\mu,\nu):=\bigg(\min_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int_{X\times X}d^{p}(x,y){\rm d}\pi(x,y)\bigg)^{\frac{1}{p}},

(1)

where
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
is the set of transportation plans between
\mu
and
\nu
, that is,

\begin{split}\Pi(\mu,\nu):=\Big\{\pi\in\mathcal{P}(X\times X):\pi(A\times X)=\mu(A),\\
\pi(X\times B)=\nu(B),\quad\forall A,B\subseteq X\Big\}.\end{split}

(2)

Owing to their appealing mathematical properties, the Wasserstein distances and Optimal Transport problems have been used across several applied fields, ranging from Machine Learning

[

.
The growing popularity of these distances generated an increasing demand for efficient ways to solve the minimization problem that defines them.
Nowadays, there are different approaches to the minimization problem on the right-hand side of (

the Sinkhorn’s algorithm (see

Despite the variety of methods developed to compute Wasserstein distances, there are still classes of subproblems connected to Optimal Transport that are either prohibitive to solve or have remained unaddressed.

Preliminary Notions and Notation

In this section, we fix our notation and recall the basic notions of Optimal Transport.
For a complete introduction to Optimal Transport we refer the reader to

2.1
Basic Notions about Discrete Optimal Transport

Let
X
be a discrete and finite set of points.
For simplicity, we assume that
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
for a suitable
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and endow
X
with the Euclidean distance

|x-y|:=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_{i}-y_{i})^{2}}.

(3)

Given
f:X\to[0,+\infty)
and
g:X\times X\to[0,+\infty)
, we set

|f|_{X}=\sum_{x\in X}f_{x}\quad\text{and}\quad|g|_{X\times X}=\sum_{(x,y)\in X\times X}g_{x,y}.

Moreover, we denote with
\mathcal{M}(X)
the set of nonnegative real functions on
X
and with
\mathcal{P}(X)
the subset
\{\mu\in\mathcal{M}(X):|\mu|_{X}=1\}
; analogously to continuous domains, we refer to these spaces as
positive measures
and
probability measures
, respectively.

Given two probability measures
\mu
and
\nu
, we define the set of transportation plans between
\mu
and
\nu
as

\Pi_{\mu,\nu}:=\bigg\{\pi_{x,y}\geq 0:\sum_{x\in X}\pi_{x,y}=\nu_{y};\sum_{y\in X}\pi_{x,y}=\mu_{x}\bigg\}.

We define the
p
-transportation cost functional
\mathbb{T}_{p}:\Pi_{\mu,\nu}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
as
\mathbb{T}_{p}(\pi):=\sum_{(x,y)\in X\times X}|x-y|^{p}\pi_{x,y}
.
The
p
-Wasserstein distance between
\mu
and
\nu
is then defined as

W_{p}^{p}(\mu,\nu):=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi_{\mu,\nu}}\mathbb{T}_{p}(\pi).

(4)

Since we are considering a
n
-dimensional finite set of points, the infimum in (
4
) is a minimum, which is characterized as the solution to the following Hitchcock-Koopmans transportation problem
[
45
]
:

\displaystyle W_{p}^{p}(\mu,\nu):=\min\quad

\displaystyle\sum_{(x,y)\in X\times X}|x-y|^{p}\pi_{x,y}

(5)

s.t.

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in X}\pi_{x,y}=\nu_{y},

\displaystyle\forall y\in X,

(6)

\displaystyle\sum_{y\in X}\pi_{x,y}=\mu_{x},

\displaystyle\forall x\in X,

(7)

\displaystyle\pi_{x,y}\geq 0

\displaystyle\forall(x,y)\in X\times X.

(8)

2.2
The Infinity Wasserstein distance

Given a transportation plan
\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
, we define
\mathbb{T}_{\infty}(\pi)
as it follows

\mathbb{T}_{\infty}(\pi):=\max_{x,y\in X\times X,\;\text{s.t.}\;\pi_{x,y}>0}|x-y|,

so that
\mathbb{T}_{\infty}(\pi)
is the longest mass movement described by the transportation plan
\pi
.
We then define the infinity Wasserstein distance between
\mu
and
\nu
as

W_{\infty}(\mu,\nu):=\min_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\mathbb{T}_{\infty}(\pi),

(9)

hence
W_{\infty}(\mu,\nu)
describes the minimal longest transportation that can be accomplished with a transportation plan.
It is easy to check that
W_{\infty}(\mu,\nu)
is equal to the limit of
W_{p}(\mu,\nu)
when
p\to\infty
,
[
46
]
.
Notice that, unlike the Wasserstein distance, the infinity Wasserstein distance between two probability measures cannot be characterized as the minimum value of a minimum cost-flow problem.

In Theorem
1
we will address the problem of computing the
W_{\infty}
distance between any couple of probability measures.

2.3
The Wasserstein Projection Problem

It is well known that
W_{p}
induces a metric over
\mathcal{P}(X)
for every
p\in[1,\infty]
,
[
44
]
.

Definition 1
.

Given a subset
K\subset\mathcal{P}(X)
and an element
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(X)
, we say that
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(X)
is a projection of
\mu
over
K
, with respect to
W_{p}
, if

\nu\in K\quad\text{and}\quad W_{p}(\mu,\nu)\leq W_{p}(\mu,\rho)\quad\forall\rho\in K.

(10)

In what follows, we focus on a specific class of sets
K
.
Given
f\in\mathcal{M}(X)
such that
|f|_{X}\geq 1
, we set

K_{f}:=\bigg\{\rho\in\mathcal{P}(X):\rho_{x}\leq f_{x},\quad\forall x\in X\bigg\}.

(11)

Given any
f\in\mathcal{M}(X)
, the set
K_{f}
is closed, which ensures the existence of at least one projection. With respect to the continuous setting, uniqueness is not to be expected in the discrete one, due to a lack of convexity. We recall that a standard geodesic between two measures
\mu_{0}
and
\mu_{1}
in the
p
-Wasserstein space on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is given by

\mu_{t}=((1-t)P^{1}+tP^{2})_{\sharp}\gamma,\quad t\in[0,1]

where
P^{i}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the projection on the
i^{th}
component,
\gamma
is an optimal transport plan between
\mu_{0}
and
\mu_{1}
, and the ‘
♯
’ symbol denotes the push-forward measure. The set
K_{f}
is convex (in the classical sense, i.e.
t\mu+(1-t)\nu\in K_{f}
for all
\mu,\nu\in K_{f}
and
t\in[0,1]
) but, since we are considering the case in which measures are supported over a grid,
K_{f}
is not geodesically convex with respect to any
W_{p}
distance as long as the grid contains more than two points, since
(1-t)P^{1}+tP^{2}\notin X
for a generic
t\in(0,1)
and therefore the geodesic is not defined.
As a consequence, there might be more than one measure satisfying conditions (
10
), as the following example shows. Let

X=\{0,1,2\}\subset\mathbb{R},\quad\mu=\delta_{1},\quad f=\frac{1}{2}(\delta_{0}+\delta_{1}+\delta_{2}).

Then, for

\nu_{t}=\frac{1}{2}(t\delta_{0}+\delta_{1}+(1-t)\delta_{2}),\quad t\in[0,1]

a direct computation shows that

W_{p}(\mu,\nu_{t})=\left(\int_{X}|x-1|^{p}\ d\nu_{t}(x)\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}=\left(\frac{1}{2}(t+0+(1-t))\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}=2^{-\frac{1}{p}}

so that all measures
\nu_{t}
, for
t\in[0,1]
are minimizers of
W_{p}(\mu,\cdot)
, for all
p\geq 1
.

In Theorem
3
we will address the problem of computing a projection of a measure
\mu
on
K_{f}
, with respect to the
W_{1}
metric.
Noticeably, the argument used in Theorem
3
can be extended to the projection problem induced by any
W_{p}
with
p\in(1,\infty)
.
The case
p=\infty
is handled in Corollary
1
.

3
The Maximum Nearby Flow Problem

Given a threshold
t\geq 0
, we define the set of nearby points as

N_{t}:=\big\{(x,y)\in X\times X:|x-y|\leq t\big\},

moreover, given
x\in X
, we denote the radius
t
ball centred in
x
as

B_{t}(x):=\big\{y\in X:|x-y|\leq t\big\}.

For every
t\in[0,\infty)
, a utility function over
N_{t}
is a collection of nonnegative values
s^{(t)}:=\{s^{(t)}_{x,y}\}_{(x,y)\in N_{t}}
.
Given
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(X)
,
\nu\in\mathcal{M}(X)
,
t\geq 0
, and a utility function
s^{(t)}
, the Maximum Nearby Flow (MNF) problem, introduced in
[
47
]
, is defined as

\displaystyle\max_{\eta\in\mathcal{N}^{(t)}_{\mu,\nu}}

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}^{(t)}_{s}(\eta),

(12)

where

\mathbb{B}^{(t)}_{s}(\eta):=\sum_{(x,y)\in N_{t}}s_{x,y}^{(t)}\eta_{x,y}

and
\mathcal{N}^{(t)}_{\mu,\nu}
is the set of nearby flows between
\mu
and
\nu
, that is

\begin{split}\mathcal{N}^{(t)}_{\mu,\nu}:=\bigg\{\eta\in\mathcal{M}(X\times X):\ &\eta_{x,y}=0\;\;\text{if}\;\;(x,y)\notin N_{t},\\
\sum_{x\in X}\eta_{x,y}\leq\nu_{y}\ &\forall y\in X,\sum_{y\in X}\eta_{x,y}\leq\mu_{x}\ \forall x\in X\bigg\}.\end{split}

(13)

Note that, for all
\eta\in\mathcal{N}^{(t)}_{\mu,\nu}
, for all
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(X)
and all
\nu\in\mathcal{M}(X)
,

|\eta|_{X\times X}=\sum_{x\in X}\sum_{y\in X}\eta_{x,y}\leq\min\Big\{\sum_{x\in X}\mu_{x},\sum_{y}\nu_{y}\Big\}\leq\min\{1,|\nu|_{Y}\}.

(14)

For the sake of simplicity and without loss of generality, from now on, we assume that
|\nu|_{Y}\geq 1
, so that
|\eta|_{X\times Y}\leq 1
.
Since
\mathcal{N}^{(t)}_{\mu,\nu}
is nonempty (it contains the null nearby flow,
\eta_{x,y}=0
) and
N_{t}
is a finite set, the MNF problem is feasible and admits a solution.

In what follows, we denote by
\Theta^{(t)}_{\mu,\nu,s}
the set of optimal solutions to the MNF problem with threshold
t\geq 0
, utility function
s^{(t)}
, probability measure
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(X)
and measure
\nu\in\mathcal{M}(Y)
.
When the utility function is clear from the context, we omit
s
from the index of
\Theta^{(t)}_{\mu,\nu,s}
.
Even though the Maximum Nearby Flow problem is well defined for any utility function
s^{(t)}
, in what follows we focus on two specific classes of utilities:

•

the
constant utility

s^{(t)}_{x,y}=1
for every
t\geq 0
and every couple
(x,y)\in N_{t}
, which enables us to compute the infinity Wasserstein distance
W_{\infty}
and a projection with respect to
W_{\infty}
. In this case, by (
14
)

\max_{\eta\in\mathcal{N}^{(t)}_{\mu,\nu}}\mathbb{B}^{(t)}_{s}(\eta)=\max_{\eta\in\mathcal{N}^{(t)}_{\mu,\nu}}\sum_{(x,y)\in N_{t}}s_{x,y}^{(t)}\eta_{x,y}=\max_{\eta\in\mathcal{N}^{(t)}_{\mu,\nu}}|\eta|_{N_{t}}\leq 1,

(15)

and

•

the
complementary cost utility
, defined as
s_{x,y}^{(t)}=t-|x-y|
for every
(x,y)\in N_{t}
, which enables us to solve the Wasserstein Projection Problem. Notice that, since
|x-y|\leq t
for every
(x,y)\in N_{t}
, then
s^{(t)}_{x,y}\geq 0
.

If a nearby flow
\eta\in\mathcal{N}^{(t)}_{\mu,\nu}
satisfies
|\eta|_{X\times X}=1
,
the inequalities in (
14
) are equalities and therefore
\eta
is a transportation plan between
\mu
and
\nu
, that is,
\eta\in\Pi_{\mu,\nu}
.
We now turn to the problem of finding the smallest value
t
for which there exists a solution
\bar{\eta}
to the MNF such that
|\bar{\eta}|_{N_{t}}=1
.
For this reason, given a utility function
s^{(t)}_{x,y}
, we introduce the
minimal saturation threshold
.

Definition 2

(Minimal Saturation Threshold)
.

Given a probability measure
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(X)
, a measure
\nu\in\mathcal{M}(X)
such that
|\nu|_{X}\geq 1
, and a utility function
s^{(t)}_{x,y}
, we define the
minimal saturation threshold
as

\tau_{s}(\mu,\nu):=\min\bigg\{t\geq 0\text{ s.t. }\exists\eta\in\Theta^{(t)}_{\mu,\nu}\text{ s.t. }|\eta|_{X\times X}=1\bigg\},

(16)

where
\Theta^{(t)}_{\mu,\nu}
is the set containing all the optimal nearby flows between
\mu
and
\nu
for threshold
t\geq 0
.

Notice that, since
s^{(t)}_{x,y}\geq 0
by definition, if we set
t=\max_{(x,y)\in X\times X}|x-y|
, then there exists at least one optimal nearby flow such that
|\bar{\eta}|_{X\times Y}=1
.
In particular, the right-hand side of (
16
) is the infimum of a non-empty set, thus
\tau_{s}(\mu,\nu)
is well defined and finite.

3.1
Characterizing the Infinity Wasserstein distance via the Minimal Saturation Threshold

We now use the Minimal Saturation Threshold to characterize the infinity Wasserstein distance induced by the constant utility function, that is
s_{x,y}^{(t)}=1
for every
t\geq 0
and for every
(x,y)\in N_{t}
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\mu
and
\nu
be two probability measures on
X
and let
s^{(t)}
be the constant utility function, so that
s^{(t)}_{x,y}=1
for every
t\geq 0
and every
(x,y)\in N_{t}
.
Then,

W_{\infty}(\mu,\nu)=\tau_{s}(\mu,\nu).

Proof.

Let
t\geq 0
be such that there exists
\eta\in\Theta^{(t)}_{\mu,\nu}
for which
|\eta|_{X\times X}=1
.
Then,
\eta\in\Pi_{\mu,\nu}
, and since supp
(\eta)\subseteq N_{t}
,
\mathbb{T}_{\infty}(\eta)\leq t
.
Therefore,

W_{\infty}(\mu,\nu)\leq\min\left\{t\geq 0\text{ s.t. }\exists\eta\in\Theta^{(t)}_{\mu,\nu}\text{ s.t. }|\eta|_{X\times X}=1\right\}=\tau_{s}(\mu,\nu).

In order to conclude we need to prove the inverse inequality.
Let
t=W_{\infty}(\mu,\nu)
,
then there exists
\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
such that

\mathbb{T}_{\infty}(\pi)=t,\qquad\text{i.e.,}\qquad\pi_{x,y}=0\qquad\text{if}\qquad|x-y|>t.

Therefore,
\pi\in\mathcal{N}^{(t)}_{\mu,\nu}
and
|\pi|_{N_{t}}=1
. By (
15
),
\pi\in\Theta^{(t)}_{\mu,\nu}
, and therefore

\min\Big\{t\geq 0\;\text{ s.t. }\;\exists\eta\in\Theta^{(t)}_{\mu,\nu}\;\text{ s.t. }\;|\eta|_{N_{t}}=1\Big\}\leq\mathbb{T}_{\infty}(\pi)=W_{\infty}(\mu,\nu),

which concludes the proof.
∎

3.2
Solving the Projection Problem via the Maximum Nearby Flow problem

In this section we show that the minimal saturation threshold associated with the complementary cost utility, defined as
s^{(t)}_{x,y}=t-|x-y|
for
t\geq 0
and
(x,y)\in N_{t}
, is connected to the computation of the Wasserstein Projection.
Given a threshold
t\geq 0
, we denote with
|\cdot|_{t}
the
t
-truncated Euclidean norm, defined as

|x-y|_{t}:=\min\{|x-y|,t\}.

We denote with
W_{p}^{(t)}
the Wasserstein distance obtained by using the
p
-th power of the
t
-truncated Euclidean distance
|\cdot|_{t}
rather than the standard Euclidean distance in (
5
), that is

W_{p}^{(t)}(\mu,\nu):=\left(\min_{\pi\in\Pi_{\mu,\nu}}\sum_{(x,y)\in X\times X}|x-y|^{p}_{t}\pi_{x,y}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}.

Since
|x-y|_{t}\leq|x-y|
for all
x,y\in X
, then

\sum_{(x,y)\in X\times X}|x-y|^{p}_{t}\pi_{x,y}\leq\sum_{(x,y)\in X\times X}|x-y|^{p}\pi_{x,y}\qquad\forall\pi\in\Pi_{\mu,\nu},\ \forall\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(X)

and thus

W_{p}^{(t)}(\mu,\nu)\leq W_{p}(\mu,\nu)\qquad\forall\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(X).

(17)

Remark 1
.

It was shown in
[
47
]
that if
\mu
and
\nu
are two probability measures and
s_{x,y}^{(t)}=t-|x-y|
is the complementary cost utility, then

W_{1}^{(t)}(\mu,\nu)=t-\max_{\eta\in\mathcal{N}^{(t)}_{\mu,\nu}}\sum_{(x,y)\in N_{t}}s_{x,y}^{(t)}\eta_{x,y},

(18)

and that a similar result applies to every
W_{p}^{(t)}
distance.

As a preliminary result, we show an
a-posteriori
upper bound on the error we make by approximating
W_{1}
with
W_{1}^{(t)}
.

Theorem 2
.

Let be given a threshold
t\geq 0
,
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(X)
, and the complementary cost utility function
s^{(t)}_{x,y}=t-|x-y|
.

(i)

If there exists
\eta^{*}\in\Theta^{(t)}_{\mu,\nu}
such that
|\eta^{*}|_{N_{t}}=1
, then

W_{1}^{(t)}(\mu,\nu)=W_{1}(\mu,\nu).

(19)

(ii)

For all
\eta\in\Theta^{(t)}_{\mu,\nu}
such that
|\eta|_{N_{t}}<1

\displaystyle W_{1}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle-W_{1}^{(t)}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{(x,y)\in X\times Y}(|x-y|-t)_{+}\frac{\Big(\mu_{x}-\sum_{y\in B_{t}(x)}\eta_{x,y}\Big)\Big(\nu_{y}-\sum_{x\in B_{t}(y)}\eta_{x,y}\Big)}{1-\sum_{(x,y)\in X\times X}\eta_{x,y}},

where
(\;\cdot\;)_{+}:=\max\{\cdot,0\}
is the positive part of
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

If
\eta^{*}\in\Theta^{(t)}_{\mu,\nu}
satisfies
|\eta^{*}|_{N_{t}}=1
,
then
\eta^{*}
is a transportation plan between
\mu
and
\nu
. Since by definition
\eta^{*}=0
if
|x-y|>t
, using (
18
) in the last equality, we obtain

\displaystyle W_{1}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{(x,y)\in X\times X}|x-y|\eta^{*}_{x,y}=\sum_{(x,y)\in N_{t}}|x-y|\eta^{*}_{x,y}

\displaystyle=\sum_{(x,y)\in N_{t}}(|x-y|-t+t)\eta^{*}_{x,y}

\displaystyle=t-\max_{\eta\in\mathcal{N}^{(t)}_{\mu,\nu}}\sum_{(x,y)\in N_{t}}s_{x,y}^{(t)}\eta_{x,y}=W_{1}^{(t)}(\mu,\nu),

which, together with (
17
) concludes the proof of (i).

Let now
\eta\in\Theta^{(t)}_{\mu,\nu}
satisfy
|\eta|_{N_{t}}<1
and let
(\bar{x},\bar{y})\in N_{t}
satisfy
|\bar{x}-\bar{y}|<t
. We now show that if
\sum_{x\in B_{t}(\bar{y})}\eta_{x,\bar{y}}<\nu_{\bar{y}}
, then
\sum_{y\in B_{t}(\bar{x})}\eta_{\bar{x},y}=\mu_{\bar{x}}
(and, in the same way, if
\sum_{y\in B_{t}(\bar{x})}\eta_{\bar{x},y}<\mu_{\bar{x}}
, then
\sum_{x\in B_{t}(\bar{y})}\eta_{x,\bar{y}}=\nu_{\bar{y}}
).
Toward a contradiction, assume that

\quad\sum_{x\in B_{t}(\bar{y})}\eta_{x,\bar{y}}<\nu_{\bar{y}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\sum_{y\in B_{t}(\bar{x})}\eta_{\bar{x},y}<\mu_{\bar{x}}.

(20)

Then, there exists a
\varepsilon>0
such that

\sum_{x\in B_{t}(\bar{y})}\eta_{x,\bar{y}}+\varepsilon<\nu_{\bar{y}}\quad\quad\text{and}\quad\quad\sum_{y\in B_{t}(\bar{x})}\eta_{\bar{x},y}+\varepsilon<\mu_{\bar{x}}

and define
\eta^{*}
as

\eta^{*}_{x,y}:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\eta_{x,y}+\varepsilon&\quad\mbox{ if }(x,y)=(\bar{x},\bar{y}),\\
\eta_{x,y}&\quad\;\mbox{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

It is easy to see that
\eta^{*}\in\mathcal{N}^{(t)}_{\mu,\nu}
, moreover, since
s^{(t)}_{\bar{x},\bar{y}}=t-|\bar{x}-\bar{y}|>0
, we have that

\displaystyle\sum_{(x,y)\in N_{t}}s^{(t)}_{x,y}\eta^{*}_{x,y}=\sum_{(x,y)\in N_{t}}s^{(t)}_{x,y}\eta_{x,y}+\varepsilon s^{(t)}_{\bar{x},\bar{y}}>\sum_{(x,y)\in N_{t}}s^{(t)}_{x,y}\eta_{x,y},

which contradicts the maximality of
\eta
. We conclude that (
20
) holds.

Let
\zeta\in\mathcal{M}(X\times X)
be defined as

\zeta_{x,y}=\frac{\Big(\mu_{x}-\sum_{v\in B_{t}(x)}\eta_{x,v}\Big)\Big(\nu_{y}-\sum_{u\in B_{t}(y)}\eta_{u,y}\Big)}{1-|\eta|_{N_{t}}}.

Owing to (
20
), we have that
\zeta_{x,y}=0
if
|x-y|<t
, while
\eta_{x,y}=0
if
|x-y|>t
. When
|x-y|=t
both measures can be non zero.
Define

\pi\in\mathcal{M}(X\times X),\qquad\pi_{x,y}:=\eta_{x,y}+\zeta_{x,y}.

(21)

For
y\in X
we compute

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in X}\pi_{x,y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in B_{t}(y)}\eta_{x,y}+\sum_{x\in X}\zeta_{x,y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in B_{t}(y)}\eta_{x,y}+\frac{\left(\nu_{y}-\sum_{u\in B_{t}(y)}\eta_{u,y}\right)}{1-|\eta|_{N_{t}}}\sum_{x\in X}\left(\mu_{x}-\sum_{v\in B_{t}(x)}\eta_{x,v}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in B_{t}(y)}\eta_{x,y}+\nu_{y}-\sum_{u\in B_{t}(y)}\eta_{u,y}=\nu_{y}.

Similarly, for all
x\in X
,
\sum_{y\in X}\pi_{x,y}=\mu_{x}
, thus
\pi\in\Pi_{\mu,\nu}
.

Lastly, let
\pi^{*}_{x,y}
be an optimal transportation plan between
\mu
and
\nu
with respect to the Euclidean metric and let
\pi
be the transportation plan defined in (
21
). We have

\displaystyle\sum_{(x,y)\in X\times X}

\displaystyle|x-y|\pi^{*}_{x,y}\leq\sum_{(x,y)\in X\times X}|x-y|\pi_{x,y}

\displaystyle=\sum_{(x,y)\in X\times X}\bigg(\max\{|x-y|-t,0\}+\min\{|x-y|,t\}\bigg)\pi_{x,y}

\displaystyle=\sum_{(x,y)\in X\times X}(|x-y|-t)_{+}\zeta_{x,y}+\sum_{(x,y)\in X\times X}\min\{|x-y|,t\}\pi_{x,y}

\displaystyle=\sum_{(x,y)\in X\times X}(|x-y|-t)_{+}\zeta_{x,y}+t\sum_{(x,y)\in X\times X}\zeta_{x,y}+\sum_{(x,y)\in N_{t}}|x-y|\eta_{x,y}

\displaystyle=\sum_{(x,y)\in X\times X}(|x-y|-t)_{+}\zeta_{x,y}+t\bigg(1-\sum_{(x,y)\in N_{t}}\eta_{x,y}\bigg)+\sum_{(x,y)\in N_{t}}|x-y|\eta_{x,y}

\displaystyle=\sum_{(x,y)\in X\times X}(|x-y|-t)_{+}\zeta_{x,y}+t-\sum_{(x,y)\in N_{t}}s_{x,y}^{(t)}\eta_{x,y},

so that

W_{1}(\mu,\nu)\leq\sum_{(x,y)\in X\times X}(|x-y|-t)_{+}\zeta_{x,y}+t-\sum_{(x,y)\in N_{t}}s^{(t)}_{x,y}\eta_{x,y}.

Since
\eta\in\Theta^{(t)}_{\mu,\nu}
, from Remark
1
, we infer

W_{1}(\mu,\nu)\leq\sum_{(x,y)\in X\times X}(|x-y|-t)_{+}\zeta_{x,y}+W_{1}^{(t)}(\mu,\nu),

which allows us to conclude the proof.
∎

Remark 2
.

The upper bound proposed in Theorem
2
can be generalized to an upper bound on the relative error.
Indeed, since
W_{1}^{(t)}(\mu,\nu)\leq W_{1}(\mu,\nu)
, given any
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(X)
,
\eta\in\Theta^{(t)}_{\mu,\nu}
, and
t\geq 0
, we have

\dfrac{|W_{1}(\mu,\nu)-W_{1}^{(t)}(\mu,\nu)|}{|W_{1}(\mu,\nu)|}\leq\dfrac{\sum_{(x,y)\in X\times X}(|x-y|-t)_{+}\tilde{\mu}_{x}\tilde{\nu}_{y}}{\Big(1-\sum_{(x,y)\in N_{t}}\eta_{x,y}\Big)W_{1}^{(t)}(\mu,\nu)}

(22)

where
\tilde{\mu}_{x}=\mu_{x}-\sum_{y\in B_{t}(x)}\eta_{x,y}
and
\tilde{\nu}_{y}=\nu_{y}-\sum_{x\in B_{t}(y)}\eta_{x,y}
.

We are now ready to state the main result of this section, which ties the minimal saturation threshold, defined in (
16
), to the Wasserstein Projection problem defined in Section
2.3
.

Theorem 3
.

Let
t\geq 0
be a threshold,
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(X)
, and
f\in\mathcal{M}(X)
with
|f|_{X}\geq 1
.
Given the complementary cost utility function
s^{(t)}_{x,y}=t-|x-y|
, we consider the following problem:

\displaystyle\max_{\eta\in\mathcal{M}(X\times X)}

\displaystyle\sum_{(x,y)\in N_{t}}s^{(t)}_{x,y}\eta_{x,y}

(23)

subj.

\displaystyle\sum_{y\in B_{t}(x)}\eta_{x,y}\leq\mu_{x}\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{x\in B_{t}(y)}\eta_{x,y}\leq f_{y}\quad\forall x,y\in X.

If
\eta\in\mathcal{M}(X\times X)
is a solution to (
23
) such that
|\eta|_{X\times X}=1
, then the second marginal of
\eta

\zeta_{y}=\sum_{x\in B_{t}(y)}\eta_{x,y},\quad\quad\quad\forall y\in X

(24)

is a projection of
\mu
on
K_{f}
, with respect to
W_{1}
.
That is,

\zeta\in K_{f}=\left\{\rho\in\mathcal{P}(X):\rho_{x}\leq f_{x},\quad\forall x\in X\right\}

and

W_{1}(\mu,\zeta)\leq W_{1}(\mu,\rho)\quad\quad\quad\forall\rho\in K_{f}.

(25)

Proof.

Let
\eta
be a solution to (
23
) such that
|\eta|_{X\times X}=1

then
\eta\in\Pi_{\mu,\zeta}
, where
\zeta
is defined in (
24
).
Note that
\zeta\in\mathcal{P}(X)
, since
\zeta_{y}\geq 0
and

\sum_{y\in X}\zeta_{y}=\sum_{y\in X}\sum_{x\in X}\eta_{x,y}=\sum_{x\in X}\mu_{x}=1.

Moreover,
\zeta\in K_{f}
by definition of
\zeta
and the fact that
\eta
satisfies the constraints in (
23
).
Owing to Theorem
2
, we obtain

W_{1}^{(t)}(\mu,\zeta)=W_{1}(\mu,\zeta).

(26)

Lastly, we show that (
25
) holds.
Toward a contradiction, let us assume that there exists
\rho\in K_{f}
such that
W_{1}(\mu,\rho)<W_{1}(\mu,\zeta)
, then

W_{1}^{(t)}(\mu,\rho)\leq W_{1}(\mu,\rho)<W_{1}(\mu,\zeta),

where the first inequality comes from the inequality
|x-y|_{t}\leq|x-y|
.
Moreover, owing to (
26
)

W_{1}^{(t)}(\mu,\rho)\leq W_{1}(\mu,\rho)<W_{1}(\mu,\zeta)=W_{1}^{(t)}(\mu,\zeta),

thus, from Remark
1
, we find a nearby flow
\eta^{*}\in\mathcal{N}^{(t)}_{\mu,\rho}
such that

W_{1}^{(t)}(\mu,\rho)=t-\sum_{(x,y)\in N_{t}}s^{(t)}_{x,y}\eta^{*}_{x,y}<t-\sum_{(x,y)\in N_{t}}s^{(t)}_{x,y}\eta_{x,y}

and therefore

\sum_{(x,y)\in N_{t}}s^{(t)}_{x,y}\eta_{x,y}<\sum_{(x,y)\in N_{t}}s^{(t)}_{x,y}\eta^{*}_{x,y}.

Since
\rho\in K_{f}
, we conclude that
\eta^{*}
is a feasible solution for problem (
23
), which contradicts the optimality of
\eta
.
∎

Remark 3
.

Notice that all the arguments used so far can be adapted to the case in which
|x-y|
is replaced with any positive and bounded cost function
c_{x,y}
in the utility function
s^{(t)}_{x,y}
and
|x-y|_{t}
with
(c_{x,y})_{t}:=\min\{c_{x,y},t\}
.
In this case, we are able to compute the
W_{c,\infty}
distance, defined as

W_{c,\infty}(\mu,\nu)=\min_{\gamma\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\max_{\gamma_{x,y}>0}c_{x,y}.

Similarly, we can compute the solution to the minimization problem

\argmin_{\nu\in K_{f}}W_{c}(\mu,\nu)=\argmin_{\nu\in K_{f}}\min_{\gamma\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}c_{x,y}\gamma_{x,y}.

In particular, Theorem
3
can be generalised to compute the infinity Wasserstein distance induced by any
l_{p}
norm and the a projection of a probability measure on
K_{f}
with respect to any
p
-Wasserstein distance
W_{p}
, where
p\in[1,\infty)
.

To conclude the section, we adapt Theorem
3
to the case
p=\infty
.

Corollary 1
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(X)
be a probability measure and
f\in\mathcal{M}(X)
be a measure such that
|f|_{X}\geq 1
.
Let
\tau_{s}(\mu,f)\in\mathbb{R}
be the minimal saturation threshold between
\mu
and
f
with respect to the constant utility function
s^{(t)}_{x,y}=1
.
Then,

\tau_{s}(\mu,f)=\min_{\nu\in K_{f}}W_{\infty}(\mu,\nu).

(27)

Moreover, let
\eta
be a solution to

\displaystyle\max_{\eta\in\mathcal{M}(X\times X)}

\displaystyle\sum_{(x,y)\in N_{T}}\eta_{x,y}

(28)

subj.

\displaystyle\sum_{y\in B_{T}(x)}\eta_{x,y}\leq\mu_{x}\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{x\in B_{T}(y)}\eta_{x,y}\leq f_{y}\quad\forall x,y\in X,

where
T=\tau_{s}(\mu,f)
.
Then
\zeta_{y}=\sum_{x\in B_{T}(y)}\eta_{x,y}
is a projection of
\mu
on
K_{f}
, with respect to
W_{\infty}
.

Proof.

First, we show that
\min_{\nu\in K_{f}}W_{\infty}(\mu,\nu)\leq\tau_{s}(\mu,f)
.
Let
t
be such that there exists
\eta\in\mathcal{M}(X\times X)
with
|\eta|_{N_{t}}=1
,

\sum_{y\in B_{t}(x)}\eta_{x,y}\leq\mu_{x},\quad\quad\text{and}\quad\quad\sum_{x\in B_{t}(y)}\eta_{x,y}\leq f_{y}.

Then
\eta
is a transportation plan between
\mu
and
\zeta
, where

\zeta_{y}=\sum_{y\in B_{t}(x)}\eta_{x,y}.

Then
W_{\infty}(\mu,\zeta)\leq t
and therefore
W_{\infty}(\mu,\zeta)\leq\tau_{s}(\mu,f)
.

We now prove the other inequality.
Let
\zeta\in K_{f}
be a projection of
\mu
on
K_{f}
with respect to
W_{\infty}
.
Let
T:=W_{\infty}(\mu,\zeta)
and, towards a contradiction, let us assume that
T<\tau_{s}(\mu,f)
. Then there exists
\pi\in\Pi_{\mu,\zeta}
such that
\pi_{x,y}=0
if
|x-y|>T
.
Let
\eta^{*}_{x,y}=\pi_{x,y}
for every
(x,y)\in N_{T}
,
then
\eta^{*}
is feasible and optimal for problem (
28
).
We then conclude that
\tau_{s}(\mu,f)\leq T
and thus the proof.
∎

Remark 4
.

It is important to note that the projection problem on the right-hand side of (
27
) generally does not have a unique solution.
Therefore, Corollary
1
identifies one possible projection among many.

4
Experimental Results

In this section, we introduce the Climbing Algorithm, a routine capable of retrieving the Minimal Saturation Threshold.
We show that it can be used to compute the infinity Wasserstein distance and to solve the Wasserstein projection problem.
In the numerical experiments, all optimisation models were solved using IBM CPLEX Optimisation Studio (version 22.1.1) through its Python API. The test instances were derived from greyscale images (represented as two-dimensional histograms) provided by the DOTMark benchmark
[
48
]
.

4.1
The Climbing Algorithm

First, we define the routine of the Climbing Algorithm.
The Climbing Algorithm takes as input two probability measures
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(X)
, a utility function
s^{(t)}
over
N_{t}
, and a set of positive thresholds
\mathcal{T}:=\{t_{i}\}_{i=1,\dots,n}
, with
t_{i}<t_{i+1}
, and returns the smallest element of
\mathcal{T}
for which there exists a solution to the Nearby Flow Problem (defined as in (
12
)) i.e., the smallest
\bar{t}\in\mathcal{T}
for which there exists
\eta\in\Theta^{(t)}_{\mu,\nu}
such that
|\eta|_{N_{\bar{t}}}=1
.
To accomplish this, we iteratively solve the Maximum Nearby Flow for growing values of
t\in\mathcal{T}
, and we stop when we find a solution with total mass equal to
1
.
In Algorithm
1
, we report the pseudo-code of the Climbing Algorithm.

Notice that if
\mathcal{T}
is the image of
c_{x,y}
for every
(x,y)\in X\times X
, the Climbing Algorithm always returns the minimal saturation threshold.

In our experiments, we start from
t_{0}=\min\mathcal{T}
and, at each iteration, we set
t_{k+1}=\min\{c_{x,y}\;\text{s.t.}\;c_{x,y}>t_{k}\}
.
While there are alternative approaches to search for the optimal value
t\in\mathcal{T}
, we opted for this simple search routine as it well-suited to search for minimal saturation thresholds that are expected to be small.
In general, depending on the problem at hand, it would be possible to improve the performances of the Climbing Algorithm by tuning the searching method (
e.g.
it could leverage a multiscale approach or a bisection approach).

5
Conclusion

In this work, we addressed the computational problems associated with the infinity Wasserstein distance and the projection of probability measures on closed subsets of probability spaces.
These problems are crucial in various applied fields, but often remain impractical due to their computational complexity or the absence of algorithms.
To overcome these limitations, we introduced a novel class of Linear Programming formulations and a computational routine capable of efficiently determining both the infinity Wasserstein distance and the projections with respect to any
p
