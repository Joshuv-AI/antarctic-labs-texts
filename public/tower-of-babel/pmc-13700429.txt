Association between Toxoplasma gondii infection and diabetes in pregnant women: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Seyyed Amir Mohammad Mirmomeni, Azam Moslemi, Reza Ghasemikhah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundToxoplasma gondii is a globally prevalent zoonotic parasite that can cause significant maternal and neonatal complications during pregnancy. Diabetes mellitus, a condition associated with impaired immune function, may increase susceptibility to such infection. Despite the severe consequences of toxoplasmosis in immunocompromised individuals, no systematic review has previously evaluated its association with diabetes in pregnant women.MethodsFollowing the PRISMA 2020 guidelines, a comprehensive search was conducted across PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science and Google Scholar (updated in August 2025). Eligible human observational studies reporting Toxoplasma gondii infection among pregnant women with gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), type 1 diabetes, or type 2 diabetes were included. Data extraction and quality assessment were performed using Hoy et al.’s tool and Newcastle–Ottawa Scale. Meta-analysis was conducted using Stata version 17.ResultsOut of 198 retrieved articles, 9 studies (7 case–control and 2 cross-sectional) met the inclusion criteria. In cross-sectional studies, pooled IgG seroprevalence among diabetic pregnant women was 29% (95% CI: 0.22–0.35), with low heterogeneity (I² = 23.63%). IgM seroprevalence was 7% (95% CI: 0.02–0.12). Case–control meta-analysis revealed a significant association between IgG seropositivity and diabetic pregnancy (OR = 2.07, 95% CI: 1.66–2.58; p < 0.001) without evidence of publication bias or heterogeneity. IgM seropositivity was not significantly different between diabetic and healthy pregnant women (OR = 1.81, 95% CI: 0.97–3.35; p > 0.05).ConclusionDiabetic pregnant women exhibit a significantly higher prevalence of chronic T. gondii infection (IgG) compared to non-diabetic controls, highlighting a potential association that warrants further investigation. However, the association with acute infection remains unclear. More prospective, geographically diverse studies are needed to clarify causality, underlying mechanisms, and clinical outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12879-026-14081-0.

Background

Toxoplasma gondii is one of the most widespread intracellular zoonotic pathogens, with a global seroprevalence estimated between 30% and 50%, making it a major focus of medical and public health research [1–3].

Sexual reproduction of this parasite occurs exclusively within the epithelial cells of the intestinal tract of felids, which serve as the definitive hosts. In contrast, a wide range of warm-blooded vertebrates, including humans, act as intermediate hosts. Human infection with T. gondii can occur through several transmission routes: Ingestion of water, vegetables, or soil contaminated with oocysts; Consumption of raw or undercooked meat containing tissue cysts; and vertical (congenital) transmission of tachyzoites from infected mothers to the fetus during pregnancy [4, 5].

In healthy individuals, toxoplasmosis is generally asymptomatic or presents mild and nonspecific symptoms. However, the infection can lead to severe and life-threatening complications in immunocompromised patients, fetuses, and other vulnerable groups. For example, immunosuppressed patients may develop fatal encephalitis, while infants congenitally infected with T. gondii may suffer from central nervous system defects, intellectual deficit, encephalitis and blindness [6–8]. Several studies have reported considerable worldwide seroprevalence rates in immunocompromised populations, including 30.8% in cancer patients [9], 26% in kidney transplant recipients [10] and a seroprevalence of 26.22% has been reported among Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV)-infected patients [11].

Pregnancy itself is associated with physiological immunomodulation, placing women at increased risk for various infections [12]. Systematic reviews of this vulnerable population indicate that the global prevalence of Toxoplasma infection ranges from 33% to 36.6% [13–15].

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is recognized as a common cause of immune system dysfunction and is influenced by factors such as genetic susceptibility, autoimmune disorders, insulin resistance linked to metabolic syndrome, and hormonal abnormalities. The hallmark of diabetes is glucose intolerance and persistent hyperglycemia [16]. In 2021, an estimated 529 million people worldwide were affected by diabetes, and this number is predicted to rise to 1.3 billion by 2050, prompting the World Health Organization (WHO) to classify diabetes among the major noncommunicable diseases requiring priority control [17, 18].

Findings from previous review studies suggest an inconclusive association between T. gondii infection and type 1 diabetes, with reported seroprevalence rates ranging from 16.5% to 28.6% depending on study design. However, a statistically significant association has been reported in patients with type 2 diabetes, with a prevalence of 47.8% [19–22]. Notably, these studies are limited by small sample sizes and geographic restriction as most were conducted in the Middle East. Although the exact relation between toxoplasmosis and diabetes remains unclear, an investigation comparing T. gondii-infected and uninfected mice demonstrated destruction of the insulin-producing β-cells of the islets of Langerhans during both acute and chronic infections. Histopathological examination revealed enlarged islets with edema and leukocyte infiltration during acute infection, whereas chronic infection was characterized by degenerated islets and CD8 + T-cell infiltration. These findings suggest an exaggerated immune response that may predispose individuals to the development of type 1 diabetes [23]. Furthermore, the chronic inflammatory state associated with toxoplasmosis has also been proposed as a potential predisposing factor for development of type 2 diabetes [24].

Diabetes during pregnancy, whether pregestational (type 1 or type 2) or gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), is associated with adverse outcomes for both the mother and the fetus. Infants born to diabetic mothers are at increased risk of cardiovascular anomalies, musculoskeletal abnormalities, neurodevelopmental impairments, craniofacial malformations, trisomy 21, and limb deformities [25, 26]. Diabetic pregnant women are predisposed to preeclampsia, miscarriage and progression of pre-existing retinopathy and nephropathy [27, 28]. Globally, the prevalence of pregestational diabetes has been estimated to range from 0.5% to 2.4% and has doubled in recent years due to increasing obesity rates. Meanwhile, the prevalence of GDM is approximately 14% and is typically detected during the second and third trimesters due to metabolic and endocrine alterations [29–31].

Although several investigations have examined the prevalence and potential association of T. gondii infection among pregnant women with diabetes, the existing evidence remains fragmented and inconclusive. Given the increased susceptibility of this population and the lack of comprehensive synthesis in the literature, conducting a systematic review and meta-analysis to estimate the seroprevalence of toxoplasmosis among diabetic pregnant women was warranted [32–35].

The present review was designed to quantify the burden of T. gondii infection in diabetic pregnant women and to evaluate whether diabetes mellitus is associated with an increased risk of seropositivity compared with non-diabetic pregnant controls. Such evidence-based insights could support clinicians, researchers and health policymakers in improving prenatal care strategies and reducing the risk of toxoplasmosis in this high-risk group.

Methods

This study was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA 2020) guideline. The protocol was registered on 11 July 2025 in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under registration number (CRD420251102488).

The literature search was initially performed in July 2025 and updated in August of 2025 across following electronic databases: PubMed, Web of Science and Scopus. In addition, Google Scholar was used as a supplementary database because it indexes a wide range of grey literature and materials not always available in traditional databases. The reference lists of all included articles were also manually screened by the investigators to identify additional relevant studies.

Two investigators (R.G and S.A.M) independently searched each database using a combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text terms connected by the Boolean operators “AND” and “OR” as follows:

“Toxoplasma gondii “,“toxoplasmosis”, “Toxoplasma”, “diabetes”, “diabetes mellitus”,” gestational diabetes”, “type 1 diabetes”, “type 2 diabetes”, “pregnancy”, “pregnant women”, “prevalence”, “seroprevalence”.

The complete database-specific search strategies are provided in Supplementary File 1.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Human observational studies, including cross-sectional and case-control studies, that reported either direct prevalence or sufficient laboratory-confirmed data on T. gondii infection in pregnant women with diabetes (gestational, type 1, or type 2 diabetes) were included. No cohort studies were identified. Editorials, commentaries, systematic reviews, abstracts without full texts, animal studies, duplicate publications, case reports, letters, and studies without accessible full texts were excluded. Studies focusing on clinical populations other than diabetic pregnant women or investigating parasites other than T. gondii were also excluded. No restrictions were applied regarding language, geographic region, serological assay type, or publication date.

Study selection and quality assessment

After removing duplications using Endnote software, two reviewers (R.G and S.A.M) independently screened titles and abstracts of all identified articles. Full-text screening followed for potentially eligible studies. Each disagreement was resolved through discussion and consensus between two reviewers. The remaining eligible studies were then subjected to quality assessment. The methodological quality of cross-sectional studies was assessed using the Hoy et al. tool [36], with scores of 7–10 indicating low risk of bias, 4–6 moderate risk, and 0–3 high risk. The quality of case-control studies was evaluated using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale [37], where the scores of 7–10 represented low bias risk, 6 indicated moderate risk, and 5 or below denoted high risk of bias.

Data extraction and meta‑analysis

Data extraction was performed independently by two investigators (R.G. and S.A.M.), and any disagreements resolved by discussion. For each study, the following information was extracted: the first author, publication year, country, study design, diagnostic method, sample size of diabetic pregnant women, number of T. gondii-positive samples, subgroup characteristics, and demographic data. Complete data from control groups were also collected in case-control studies.

The analysis was performed based on the standard errors and pooled odds ratios (OR) of IgG and IgM measurements, with corresponding 95% confidence intervals. All statistical analyses we conducted using STATA version 17.0 (StataCorp, USA) with the metan package.

Heterogeneity among studies was assessed by Cochran’s Q test (with p-value < 0.10 indicating statistical significance) and the I² index. An I² value between 50% and 75% was considered substantial heterogeneity whereas values exceeding 75% indicated considerable heterogeneity [38].

Publication bias was evaluated using funnel plots and Egger’s regression test.

Results

According to the study selection process illustrated in Fig. 1, a total of 198 articles were retrieved from PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar. After removing 24 duplicates, 174 unique records remained. Of these, 141 articles were excluded as unrelated based on title and abstract screening, while 33 were considered potentially relevant. Following eligibility assessment, 15 studies were reviewed in detail, and ultimately, 9 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). Several studies investigating the association between Toxoplasma gondii infection and diabetes were excluded during full-text screening because they were conducted in diabetic populations other than pregnant women and therefore did not meet the predefined eligibility criteria. The included studies enrolled women with different diabetes subtypes (gestational diabetes mellitus, type 1 diabetes, type 2 diabetes, or mixed diabetic populations), introducing clinical heterogeneity across the pooled analyses. Geographically the available evidence is predominantly derived from Middle Eastern populations. the included studies originated from Iran (n = 4), Iraq (n = 3), Turkey (n = 1) and China (n = 1). Of these, seven employed a case–control design [22, 33, 34, 39–42], while two utilized a cross-sectional design [43, 44] (Tables 1 and 2).

For the detection of Toxoplasma gondii immunoglobulins, Saki et al. [38] utilized both enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) and indirect immunofluorescence assay (IFA) techniques. In the remaining studies, ELISA was the primary diagnostic method employed, except for one conducted by Sanay et al. [40], which did not provide sufficient methodological details.

According to the risk of bias assessment tool developed by Hoy et al. (2012), both cross-sectional studies were classified as having a moderate risk of bias by achieving 6 of 10 scores. The seven case–control studies were assessed using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) and were judged to have a low risk of bias, with scores ranging from 7 to 8.

Cross-sectional studies

In total, 234 diabetic pregnant women were enrolled across the two cross-sectional studies. Among these participants, 67 were positive for IgG antibodies against Toxoplasma gondii. Using the DerSimonian–Laird random-effects model in Stata version 17, the pooled prevalence of IgG antibodies was estimated at 29% (95% CI: 0.22–0.35) with low heterogeneity (I² = 23.63%) (Fig. 2A). Furthermore, the pooled prevalence of IgM antibodies was calculated at 7% (95% CI: 0.02–0.12), demonstrating moderate heterogeneity (I² = 45%) (Fig. 2B).

Case-control studies

In the case–control analysis, Egger’s test revealed no evidence of publication bias for IgG (p = 0.83), and the funnel plot demonstrated a visually symmetric distribution (Fig. 3). The heterogeneity assessment indicated complete homogeneity among the included studies (Q = 4.31, p = 0.64, I² = 0%). Consequently, a fixed-effects model was applied, producing a pooled odds ratio (OR) of 2.07 (95% CI: 1.66–2.58; p < 0.001). This finding suggests a significantly higher IgG seropositivity among diabetic pregnant women compared with controls (Fig. 4). Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis confirmed the stability of the results, as the exclusion of any individual study did not materially affect the overall estimate (Fig. 5).

For IgM seropositivity, Egger’s test likewise indicated no evidence of publication bias (p = 0.97), and the funnel plot exhibited a symmetric distribution (Fig. 6). No heterogeneity was observed among the included studies (Q = 2.00, p = 0.73, I² = 0%). Consequently, a fixed-effects model was applied, yielding a pooled odds ratio (OR) of 1.81 (95% CI: 0.97–3.35; p > 0.05). These findings indicate no statistically significant difference in IgM seropositivity between diabetic and non-diabetic pregnant women (Fig. 7).

Discussion

This study represents the first systematic review and meta-analysis to quantify both the prevalence of Toxoplasma gondii infection and its association with diabetes among pregnant women. Analysis of two cross-sectional studies indicated an IgG seroprevalence of 29% among diabetic pregnant women, a finding comparable to the 28.6% and 23.3% rates reported respectively by Asgari et al. [21] and Khattab et al. [45] (both studies surveyed seroprevalence of T. gondii in ype-1 diabetic patients). However, due to the limited number of cross-sectional studies identified, our findings should be interpreted cautiously and reassessed as additional studies become available.

The meta-analysis conducted on case-control studies demonstrated a statistically association between IgG seropositivity and diabetic pregnancies (OR: 2.07), suggesting an association between prior exposure to T. gondii and diabetes in pregnant women. in this target population. This observation aligns with findings in non-pregnant populations. For example, a case–control study conducted in Egypt reported higher IgG seroprevalence among patients with type 2 diabetes compared to controls with seropositivity correlating with clinical complications [46]. Similarly, a study from Iran identified significantly elevated levels of both IgG and IgM among type 2 diabetic patients relative to healthy controls [47]. Furthermore, a recent meta-analysis study by Catchpole et al. [48] suggested that T. gondii seropositive individuals have approximately a 2.7-fold higher risk of developing type 2 diabetes mellitus. A systematic review and meta-analysis evaluating the relationship between T. gondii infection and type 1 diabetes mellitus reported an increased odds ratio for T. gondii infection in diabetic patients compared with controls (pooled OR ≈ 2.45; CI 0.91–6.61), becoming stronger after exclusion of one outlier study (OR ≈ 3.38). These findings are consistent with our IgG-related results in pregnant diabetic women, suggesting an association between chronic toxoplasmosis and diabetes [19]. Additional case-control studies reported markedly higher IgG seroprevalence among diabetic individuals. For instance, the study entitled “Probable Association between Chronic Toxoplasma gondii Infection and type 1 and type 2 Diabetes Mellitus” revealed significantly higher IgG in both T1DM and T2DM subjects versus controls respectively with odds ratio of 4.41 and 6.44 [49]. Nevertheless, some preliminary studies have found no association between acute or chronic T. gondii infection and diabetes [50, 51]. A matched case‐control study in Durango, Mexico, identified IgG seropositivity in 6.4% of diabetic cases and 3.2% of controls, without statistical significance [51]. These findings suggest that geographic region, endemicity, study design, or target population may influence the observed results. The observed association could reflect shared inflammatory or immunological pathways rather than a direct causal effect: immune modulation, chronic inflammatory state, oxidative stress or potential effects on insulin sensitivity. For example, in Ansari-Lari et al. [33] study, T. Gondii seropositivity (IgG) was not significantly more frequent among diabetic cases than controls; however inflammatory markers (leptin, TNF‐α) showed differences between groups and appeared to interact with T. gondii infection status. These results suggest that T. gondii infection may be associated with inflammatory pathways that are also involved in diabetes through inflammation or immune‐modulation rather than direct damage against endocrine system. Nonetheless, the exact pathological mechanisms remain poorly understood and warrant further investigation.

In contrast, our analysis found no statistically significant association between IgM seropositivity and diabetic pregnancy. However, this finding should be interpreted with caution. The limited number of included studies, the relatively wide confidence interval, and the resulting reduction in statistical power may have hindered the detection of a true association. Therefore, the absence of statistical significance should not be interpreted as evidence of the absence of an effect. Additional large-scale studies are needed to clarify the potential relationship between recent T. gondii infection and diabetes during pregnancy.

Several studies have similarly reported low IgM seropositivity in both diabetic and non-diabetic individuals, limiting statistical power and reducing the stability of IgM-related findings [21, 52]. Nevertheless, other reports have identified significant associations between IgM seroprevalence and diabetes [48], indicating that further research is required to clarify this relationship.

Because all included studies were either case–control or cross-sectional in design, establishing causality or determining temporal relationships remains challenging. Consequently, the mechanisms underlying the observed association between chronic toxoplasmosis and diabetes during pregnancy cannot be conclusively determined and require investigation through longitudinal and mechanistic research.

One of the major limitations of this review is the clinical heterogeneity of diabetic conditions among the included studies. The analyzed populations comprised women with different forms of diabetes, including type 1 diabetes, type 2 diabetes, and gestational diabetes mellitus, while some studies did not provide sufficient information for stratified analyses. Because of the limited number of eligible studies, subgroup analyses according to diabetes type were not feasible. Given the distinct pathophysiological mechanisms, immunological profiles, and metabolic characteristics of these conditions, future studies should evaluate the association between T. gondii infection and each diabetes subtype separately to determine whether the observed relationship differs across diabetic disorders. Therefore, the pooled estimates should be interpreted as representing diabetic pregnancy overall rather than any specific type of diabetes.

Several important confounding factors, including socioeconomic status, hygiene practices, trimester of pregnancy, comorbid conditions, exposure to cats or contaminated soil, dietary habits such as consumption of raw or undercooked meat, educational level, and area of residence, may influence both toxoplasmosis and diabetes risk through various mechanisms. However, because only a limited number of studies included in our study provided adequate analytical data regarding these variables, subgroup analyses could not be performed.

Most included studies originated from Middle Eastern countries, a region known to have one of the highest prevalences of toxoplasmosis among pregnant population, alongside Africa and Latin America [15]. In particular, prevalence rates of 41.1% and 51% respectively in Iranian and Iraqi pregnant women [53, 54]. According to International Diabetes Federation (IDF), 27% of Middle Eastern and North African pregnant women have GDM which is significantly higher compared to the global prevalence of 14% [55]. Nevertheless, another study focused on Middle East and North Africa reported a remarkably different rate of 13% (11.5% in Iraq and 11.4% in Iran) which demonstrates the need of more dedicated research in the region for assessment of diabetes prevalence among pregnant population [56]. Given the relatively high burden of both toxoplasmosis and diabetes in the Middle East, the association observed in the present review may not fully reflect that of regions with different epidemiological profiles. Therefore, caution is warranted when generalizing these findings to populations with lower prevalence of toxoplasmosis, diabetes, or both, and further studies from other geographic regions are needed to evaluate the consistency of the observed association.

It should be noted that the small number of included studies may reduce the statistical power of Egger’s regression test to detect publication bias Therefore, the absence of statistically significant publication bias should not be interpreted as evidence that publication bias is absent.

Given the observed association between chronic T. gondii infection (IgG seropositivity) and diabetes during pregnancy, screening for T. gondii infection in pregnant women may be considered, particularly in endemic regions. However, because evidence regarding IgM seropositivity remains inconclusive, routine screening specifically for acute toxoplasmosis may be less justified unless clinical suspicion exists or population-level risk is high. Importantly, most included studies focused primarily on latent infection, highlighting the need for additional research investigating acute toxoplasmosis in pregnant diabetic populations.

Future studies should ideally be prospective cohort designs, following women from early pregnancy or even preconception to assess whether T. gondii infection either preexisting or newly acquired is prospectively associated with the subsequent development of diabetes.

To further elucidate the potential causal mechanisms linking Toxoplasma gondii infection with diabetes during pregnancy, future studies could incorporate measurements of inflammatory biomarkers, indices of insulin resistance and molecular detection of T. gondii (e.g., Polymerase Chain Reaction). For instance, a study from Iraq that utilized PCR targeting the B1 gene, together with assessments of leptin and ferritin levels in patients with type 2 diabetes and healthy controls found that molecular detection rates were lower than serological positivity rates, whereas biochemical markers were markedly elevated among infected diabetic individuals [57].

Finally, controlling for potential confounders, applying uniform standardized diagnostic criteria, and consistently reporting both IgG and IgM seroprevalence with corresponding confidence intervals in future investigations will enhance comparability and strengthen the evidence base regarding the association between chronic toxoplasmosis and diabetes.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrated a significant association between chronic Toxoplasma gondii infection, as indicated by IgG seropositivity, and diabetes in pregnant women. Diabetic pregnant women were approximately twice as likely to be seropositive compared to non-diabetic controls, suggesting that latent toxoplasmosis is associated with altered immune or metabolic regulation, although the direction and causality of this relationship remain uncertain during pregnancy. In contrast, IgM seropositivity showed no significant association, implying that acute infection was not significantly associated with diabetic status among pregnant women. However, this conclusion should be interpreted cautiously due to the limited number of available studies and the need for further research specifically focused on acute T. gondii infection and its relationship with diabetes in pregnant populations. Several limitations should be acknowledged, including the predominance of studies conducted in the Middle East, the small number of cross-sectional investigations, and inadequate adjustment for potential confounders such as type of diabetes, socioeconomic status, environmental exposures, and dietary habits. These limitations may restrict the generalizability of our findings and highlight the need for more diverse and methodologically rigorous studies. Future studies should adopt prospective cohort designs and incorporate assessment of inflammatory pathways, insulin resistance markers, and molecular detection of T. gondii to clarify potential causal mechanisms. Such data would enhance understanding of interactions between chronic toxoplasmosis and diabetes, support the development of targeted screening and preventive strategies for high-risk pregnant populations, and ultimately contribute to greater public health awareness regarding the global burden of toxoplasmosis.
