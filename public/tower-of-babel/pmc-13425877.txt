Current reported practices in surgical antibiotic prophylaxis and infection prevention in primary arthroplasty: an open web-based survey among orthopedic specialists in Türkiye
Authors: Tahir Burak Sarıtaş, Çağla Keskin Sarıtaş
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposePeriprosthetic joint infection (PJI) remains a major complication after primary arthroplasty. We descriptively assessed current perioperative prophylaxis and infection-prevention practices in Türkiye by benchmarking reported practices against guideline recommendations and describing areas of variability that may warrant further evaluation.MethodIn this anonymous, open web-based descriptive cross-sectional survey distributed through professional networks in Türkiye (December 2025–January 2026), orthopedic and traumatology specialists reported their professional profile, systemic prophylaxis practices, and preoperative/perioperative preventive measures. Analyses were descriptive (counts, percentages).ResultsA total of 278 specialists participated. Cefazolin was the first-choice prophylactic agent (100%); in β-lactam allergy, clindamycin was most preferred (73.4%). For MRSA (methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus) colonization, 50.4% reported adding a glycopeptide. Initial cefazolin dosing was mostly within 60 min before incision (98.2%). Nevertheless, 53.6% extended prophylaxis beyond 24 h, 57.2% continued antibiotics until drain removal, and 62.2% prescribed oral antibiotics at discharge. Only 46.0% reported a written institutional prophylaxis protocol. Routine MRSA and MSSA (methicillin-susceptible Staphylococcus aureus) screening rates were low (19.4% and 15.1%), while asymptomatic bacteriuria screening remained high (50.0%). Preoperative bathing was recommended by 70.9%; hair removal was mainly performed with electric clippers (92.8%), usually immediately preoperatively (72.3%). Antibacterial suture use was low (9.0%), whereas antibiotic-loaded bone cement use was common (64.7%).ConclusionReported practice appeared to align with guideline recommendations for agent selection and first-dose timing, but variability was observed in prophylaxis duration, drain/discharge-related decisions, screening-decolonization workflows, and local preventive strategies. Given the voluntary open web-based sampling strategy, unknown denominator and response rate, descriptive design, and absence of outcome correlation, these findings should be interpreted only as self-reported practices among respondents, not as representative estimates of national practice or evidence supporting practice change. Further studies with defined sampling frames, audit-based practice verification, and joint-specific outcomes are needed to evaluate how these reported variations relate to actual practice and clinical outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-026-10036-3.Keywords: Primary arthroplasty, Antibiotic prophylaxis, Periprosthetic joint infection, Surgical site infection prevention, Survey study, Türkiye

Introduction

Total joint arthroplasty is one of the most commonly performed procedures in orthopedics, and case volumes continue to rise with population aging and expanding indications. Concurrently, periprosthetic joint infection (PJI) persists as one of the gravest consequences of arthroplasty surgery regarding mortality, functional impairment, and healthcare expenditures [1]. Patients who acquire PJI frequently necessitate numerous major surgical interventions, prolonged intravenous antibiotic therapy, and prolonged rehabilitation, imposing a significant socioeconomic cost on both individuals and healthcare systems [2].

International consensus statements and guidelines strongly advocate, based on robust evidence, that in primary arthroplasty patients without allergies, first- or second-generation cephalosporins should be administered intravenously within 60 min prior to incision, re-dosed according to operative duration and blood loss, and typically discontinued within 24 h [3–5]. International guidelines also emphasize that standardizing prophylaxis and related perioperative infection-prevention practices is the practical strategy to reduce PJI risk [6–8].

Nonetheless, survey and audit studies from several countries have demonstrated significant guideline discordance and considerable variance in the selection of preventive agents, scheduling of the first dosage, and especially the duration of postoperative administration [9–11]. Reports indicate that in numerous centers, prophylaxis is administered for more than 24 h, and essential infection-prevention measures—such as screening for asymptomatic bacteriuria, MRSA/MSSA screening and decolonization, preoperative bathing, hair removal, surgical-site preparation, and local antibiotic application—are inadequately standardized [5, 12–15]. Nonetheless, comprehensive surgeon-based surveys that evaluate both systemic prophylactic regimens and essential preoperative/perioperative infection-prevention strategies in primary arthroplasty are scarce. This open web-based study aimed to descriptively characterize self-reported systemic prophylaxis and key preoperative/perioperative infection-prevention practices among responding orthopedic surgeons in Türkiye and to benchmark these reported practices against guideline recommendations.

Materials and methods

This study was a descriptive, cross-sectional, anonymous survey conducted online among orthopedic and traumatology specialists in Türkiye. The study was conducted in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Marmara University Faculty of Medicine (Decision No: 09.2025–25.0904). Clinical trial number: not applicable. Eligible participants were orthopedic and traumatology specialists practicing in Türkiye. Responses were included only if all mandatory sections were complete. Incomplete or ineligible responses were excluded. Electronic informed consent was obtained before questionnaire access. No directly identifiable personal data (name, national ID, email, IP, or location) were collected. The questionnaire was administered via Google Forms and was accessible from December 2025 to January 2026. The form settings restricted submissions to one response per user account. The survey link was disseminated via national and regional professional email groups, appropriate society email distribution lists, and professional peer communication channels, with periodic reminder messages issued. This was an open, non-probability web-based sampling strategy rather than a registry-based random sample. Because the survey link could be forwarded across professional networks and the exact number of eligible orthopedic and traumatology specialists who received or viewed the invitation was unknown, the denominator of the invited population could not be determined and a formal response rate could not be calculated. Accordingly, the 278 complete responses should be interpreted as the final respondent sample rather than a representative sample of all orthopedic and traumatology specialists in Türkiye. Given the descriptive aim and open distribution strategy across Türkiye, no formal sample-size calculation was performed; all eligible complete responses received during the study period were analyzed.

Survey items were formulated in alignment with the three most recent guidelines issued by the World Health Organization (WHO), the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), and the Society for Healthcare Epidemiology of America (SHEA)/Infectious Diseases Society of America/Association for Professionals in Infection Control and Epidemiology (APIC)/American Hospital Association (AHA) [6–8]. The questionnaire was developed specifically for this study to descriptively map self-reported practices rather than to generate a psychometric scale or a composite score. Therefore, the items were primarily structured as practice-based questions addressing specific prophylaxis and infection-prevention behaviors.

Before distribution, a pilot test was conducted with 10 orthopedic surgeons to assess clarity, comprehensibility, feasibility, estimated completion time, and technical functionality of the online form. Wording was revised according to the feedback received from the pilot participants. These pilot responses were not included in the final analysis. However, no formal psychometric validation was performed, including test–retest reliability, internal consistency testing, or construct validity assessment. The final English version of the questionnaire is available as Supplementary File 1 to improve transparency and reproducibility. The final questionnaire consisted of four key sections: (1) introduction and electronic informed consent; (2) professional profile; (3) practices concerning systemic surgical antibiotic prophylaxis in primary arthroplasty cases; and (4) preoperative/perioperative infection control practices. Survey items were framed for primary arthroplasty in general; respondents were not asked to provide separate answers for hip, knee, shoulder, or other joint-specific arthroplasty procedures.

To make evidence-based comparisons more transparent, selected survey items were mapped to a structured evidence-benchmark framework based on current guideline recommendations, consensus statements, and high-quality contemporary evidence, including randomized trials, systematic reviews, and meta-analyses relevant to surgical antibiotic prophylaxis and infection prevention in arthroplasty. For each domain, the relevant evidence-based benchmark was summarized and compared with the corresponding aggregate survey response. Findings were contextualized descriptively as reported alignment with, difference from, or variability around the selected evidence-based benchmark, depending on whether the reported practice was consistent with, inconsistent with, or not directly addressed by a clear recommendation or consistent evidence base. These categories were used only to describe the relationship between survey responses and the selected benchmark and were not intended to assess surgeon knowledge, quality of care, clinical appropriateness, or patient outcomes. When evidence was heterogeneous or recommendations allowed local adaptation, variation was interpreted as acceptable practice diversity rather than as guideline deviation. No composite compliance or adherence score was calculated, because the questionnaire was not designed or psychometrically validated for scoring, and the relative weighting of individual domains was not pre-specified.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were descriptive; categorical variables are presented as n/N (%). For multiple-response items, responses were summarized descriptively and were not interpreted as indicating a hierarchy, priority, or primary decision criterion. Missing data were not imputed. No subgroup, comparative, or multivariable analyses were performed, because the study was designed as an aggregate descriptive survey and was not intended to identify independent determinants of practice variability. We used Microsoft Excel, version 2016, software (Microsoft, Redmond, WA) to summarize the responses to all survey questions and present descriptive statistics.

Results

A total of 278 complete responses were included in the final respondent sample and analyzed descriptively. Among the respondents, 80.9% were specialists (n = 225), 11.2% were associate professors (n = 31), and 7.9% were professors (n = 22). The predominant duration of specialist practice reported was 0–5 years (44.2%, n = 123). About 45.7% of the participants were employed at training and research hospitals (n = 127) (Table 1).

In primary arthroplasty surgeries, all respondents indicated cefazolin as their primary prophylactic antibiotic (100.0%, n = 278). In cases of β-lactam allergy, clindamycin emerged as the predominant option, recommended by 73.4% (n = 204) of respondents. The predominant strategy for MRSA colonization involved the incorporation of a glycopeptide into the usual regimen (50.4%, n = 140). The administration timing of the initial cefazolin dose prior to incision was primarily recorded as ≤ 30 min (56.1%, n = 156) and 31–60 min (42.1%, n = 117). In glycopeptide (vancomycin/teicoplanin) prophylaxis, infusion initiation times were documented as follows: ≤30 min in 35.3% (n = 98), 31–60 min in 35.3% (n = 98), and > 120 min in 1.1% (n = 3). For the redosing criterion item, respondents could select more than one option; therefore, responses do not indicate the primary or prioritized criterion used in clinical decision-making. The most frequently selected option was after more than one antibiotic half-life, reported by 57.2% of respondents (n = 159). Although “>2 antibiotic half-lives” (25.9%, n = 72) and “1500 mL blood loss” (8.6%, n = 24) were reported, only 6 respondents (2.2%) selected both criteria simultaneously. Postoperative prophylaxis beyond 24 h was reported by 53.6% of respondents (n = 149). A documented institutional protocol for surgical antibiotic prophylaxis was reported by 46.0% (n = 128). In instances involving drain utilization, 57.2% (n = 159) asserted that antibiotics were continued until drain removal. Oral antibiotic prescription at discharge was reported by 62.2% of respondents (n = 173) (Table 2).

Routine MRSA screening was indicated by 19.4% of respondents (n = 54), whereas MSSA screening was reported by 15.1% (n = 42). Among respondents screened for MRSA, the predominant decolonization method reported was intranasal mupirocin combined with chlorhexidine bathing/wiping (63.0%, n = 34), whereas only 3.7% indicated a 5-day decolonization period (n = 2). Routine urine screening for asymptomatic bacteriuria was reported by 50.0% of respondents (n = 139). Preoperative dental consultation was reported by 22.3% of respondents (n = 62). Preoperative bathing/washing was recommended by 70.9% of respondents (n = 197), with the majority suggesting that it be performed the evening before surgery (90.4%, n = 178/197). Among respondents recommending preoperative bathing/washing, chlorhexidine was selected as the cleansing material by 56.8% (n = 112/197). Electric clippers were the most commonly reported hair-removal method, selected by 92.8% of respondents (n = 258), and hair removal was most often reported immediately before surgery (72.3%, n = 201). Laminar airflow in the operating room was reported by 87.8% of respondents (n = 244). Povidone-iodine was the most commonly reported surgical skin antiseptic (82.7%, n = 230), followed by alcohol combined with povidone-iodine (12.2%, n = 34). Iodophor-impregnated incise drape use was reported by 73.4% of respondents (n = 204). Normal saline (NS) was the most frequently utilized solution for intraoperative irrigation, accounting for 69.1% (n = 192), followed by a combination of povidone-iodine and NS at 17.3% (n = 48). Antibacterial suture use was reported by 9.0% of respondents (n = 25). Application of antibiotic powder to the implant was reported by 15.8% (n = 44), with vancomycin being the most often selected agent (n = 42/44). Antibiotic-loaded bone cement use was reported by 64.7% of respondents (n = 180), with gentamicin (n = 100/180) and vancomycin (n = 66/180) being the most commonly reported agents (Table 3).

Discussion

This open web-based survey, distributed through professional networks in Türkiye, describes self-reported infection prophylaxis and preventive practices among 278 responding orthopedic specialists. Reported alignment with selected evidence-based benchmarks was high for prophylactic agent selection and first-dose timing, whereas substantial heterogeneity was observed in prophylaxis duration and drain/discharge-related antibiotic decisions. In contrast, several adjunctive preoperative/perioperative strategies showed context-dependent variability in domains where evidence is heterogeneous or local adaptation is acceptable. Because the study was descriptive, based on self-reported data, and conducted through an open web-based distribution strategy with an unknown eligible denominator, the findings should be interpreted as respondent-level estimates rather than nationally representative prevalence estimates or evidence of causal determinants of variability. To reduce subjectivity in evidence-based interpretation, a structured evidence-benchmark comparison is presented in Table 4; therefore, the discussion focuses on the main interpretive patterns, potential system-level contributors to variation, and implications for future implementation research rather than repeating each recommendation in detail.

Guidelines recommend that, in patients without β-lactam allergy, a first- or second-generation cephalosporin be administered intravenously within 60 min before incision, with prophylaxis generally limited to 24 h [4, 6–8]. Some studies further suggest that cephalosporin prophylaxis may be optimal when given closer to incision, ideally 10–25 min pre-incision [16]. In our study, reported cefazolin selection and pre-incision timing were consistent with the selected guideline-based benchmark; however, these findings should be interpreted cautiously because they were self-reported rather than verified through pharmacy records, anesthesia documentation, surgical checklists, or institutional audits. Clindamycin was the expected alternative in β-lactam allergy [38]. The reported addition of a glycopeptide for MRSA colonization is broadly consistent with selective, screening- and risk-based prophylaxis rather than routine vancomycin use [7, 20, 39]. Because glycopeptides require prolonged infusion, administration should be planned early, generally up to 120 min before incision, to ensure adequate tissue levels [7]. In our cohort, most respondents reported starting infusion within ≤ 60 min, suggesting potential workflow or coordination constraints rather than individual-level factors alone.

Responses regarding intraoperative redosing and postoperative prophylaxis showed variability in reported antibiotic management. Redosing is generally guided by prolonged operative duration, commonly > 2 antibiotic half-lives, and/or major blood loss [4, 17]; however, the redosing item allowed multiple responses without requiring respondents to rank or prioritize criteria. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted as heterogeneity in reported criteria rather than as direct evidence of clinical decision-making patterns or knowledge gaps. Prolonged prophylaxis remained common: 53.6% reported prophylaxis beyond 24 h despite recommendations for discontinuation within 24 h in primary arthroplasty [6–8, 18]. Similarly, 57.2% continued antibiotics until drain removal, and 62.2% prescribed oral antibiotics at discharge. Although these practices may be intended to reduce perceived PJI risk, prolonged prophylaxis may increase antimicrobial resistance, adverse events, and costs [19]. Such patterns may reflect habit-based prescribing, medico-legal concerns, limited antimicrobial stewardship integration into orthopedic pathways, and the absence of written institutional protocols rather than individual decision-making alone.

Routine MRSA and MSSA screening rates were low, although standardized screening–decolonization pathways are relevant in arthroplasty and generally include intranasal mupirocin combined with chlorhexidine bathing/wiping for approximately 5 days before surgery [20, 21]. Among respondents who screened, mupirocin plus chlorhexidine was the most common decolonization approach, but only a small proportion reported a standard 5-day duration, suggesting variability in how decolonization protocols are reported or operationalized. The present descriptive data cannot determine whether clearer institutional pathways would reduce PJI or SSI rates.

Routine preoperative screening for asymptomatic bacteriuria was reported by 50%, a practice that differs from current recommendations. Evidence does not support routine screening or treatment before elective non-urologic surgery; testing and treatment should be limited to symptomatic urinary tract infections [14, 22]. Preoperative dental consultation was less frequently reported; given the limited evidence supporting universal dental screening before arthroplasty, this finding may reflect a more selective approach rather than clear non-adherence [23, 24]. These patterns may be influenced by microbiology capacity, access to preoperative screening pathways, infectious diseases collaboration, and persistence of traditional preoperative clearance routines.

Preoperative bathing was recommended by 70.9% of respondents, which is broadly consistent with guidance to reduce skin microbial burden [7, 8]. However, the choice of cleansing material varied, and current evidence does not consistently demonstrate the superiority of chlorhexidine over plain soap [6, 25]. Hair removal practices were more consistent with selected evidence-based benchmarks: electric clippers were preferred by most respondents, and hair removal was usually performed immediately before surgery, consistent with recommendations to avoid hair removal unless necessary and to prefer clipping over shaving when removal is indicated [8, 26]. These findings suggest relatively strong reported alignment in hair removal practices, while bathing protocols may remain more institution-dependent.

Several adjunctive operating-room practices showed context-dependent variability. Laminar airflow use was high, although current evidence does not consistently demonstrate an independent reduction in SSI/PJI risk [27]. For surgical site antisepsis, povidone-iodine alone was most frequently reported, whereas alcohol-based antiseptic preparations are favored in many protocols because of their broad and rapid antibacterial activity [6, 28]. Recent randomized evidence suggests that alcohol-based povidone-iodine may be non-inferior to alcohol-based chlorhexidine, supporting the view that standardized application technique, adequate contact time, full coverage, and proper drying are critical regardless of agent choice [29, 30]. Similarly, iodophor-impregnated drape use and irrigation practices varied, but evidence for routine SSI reduction with incise drapes remains inconsistent, and no single irrigation strategy is universally established [31–33]. Therefore, variation in these domains should be interpreted as practice diversity in evidence-evolving areas rather than uniform guideline deviation.

Local antibiotic-related strategies also varied. Antibiotic powder use was uncommon, mostly involving vancomycin, and recent evidence does not consistently support routine vancomycin powder use in primary arthroplasty [34]. By contrast, antibiotic-loaded bone cement was frequently reported, predominantly with gentamicin, consistent with its widespread use in registry-based reports [35]. However, routine use of antibiotic-loaded cement is not generally recommended for all primary arthroplasty cases and should be individualized according to joint type, fixation method, patient risk profile, institutional policy, and local microbiology [36]. Antibacterial sutures were infrequently used, and arthroplasty-specific randomized evidence has not consistently shown SSI reduction with triclosan-coated sutures [37]. Overall, these local strategies should be viewed as context-dependent practices requiring clearer decision pathways rather than uniformly inappropriate or guideline-discordant practices. This lack of joint-specific data is particularly relevant for local strategies such as antibiotic-loaded bone cement, antibiotic powder, and antibacterial sutures, because their use may differ according to joint type, fixation method, implant selection, and patient risk profile.

Several Türkiye-specific contextual and system-level factors may plausibly contribute to the observed patterns. Arthroplasty services in Türkiye are delivered across a mixed healthcare structure, including state, training and research, university, and private hospitals, where operating-room infrastructure, microbiology capacity, pharmacy involvement, purchasing policies, infection-control staffing, and stewardship support may vary [40]. The lack of written institutional prophylaxis protocols in more than half of respondents’ settings may allow individual preference and local habits to shape practice. In addition, several recommendations require multidisciplinary coordination among orthopedics, anesthesia, infectious diseases, microbiology, pharmacy, and operating room teams, particularly for timely glycopeptide infusion, intraoperative redosing, MRSA/MSSA screening–decolonization, and postoperative antibiotic discontinuation. Resource availability, local microbiology, purchasing policies, and workflow constraints may also influence context-dependent practices such as antisepsis products, irrigation solutions, antibiotic-loaded cement, and antibacterial sutures. However, because these factors were not directly measured and no subgroup or multivariable analyses were performed, they should be interpreted as plausible implementation-related explanations rather than proven determinants. Variability should not be interpreted as a direct measure of care quality; in domains with clear recommendations, it may indicate differences from selected benchmarks, whereas in evidence-uncertain or context-dependent areas, practice may reasonably vary. Future studies linking reported practices with institutional protocols, resource indicators, infection-control infrastructure, audit data, and patient-level outcomes are needed to clarify the drivers and clinical consequences of variability.

This study has several limitations. As a voluntary, open web-based cross-sectional survey disseminated through professional networks rather than a closed national registry, it is vulnerable to selection and nonresponse bias, and the eligible denominator and response rate could not be determined; therefore, the reported percentages should be interpreted as respondent-level estimates rather than nationally representative prevalence estimates. Self-selection of surgeons with greater interest in arthroplasty, infection prevention, or antimicrobial stewardship cannot be excluded, and the direction or magnitude of this bias is unknown. The questionnaire was newly developed and not formally psychometrically validated; although pilot testing improved clarity and feasibility, measurement error, item misinterpretation, and limited reproducibility remain possible. All data were self-reported and were not verified against institutional audits, pharmacy records, anesthesia records, surgical checklists, antibiotic administration data, or patient-level outcomes; thus, social desirability bias, recall bias, and misreporting may have occurred, particularly in domains with high reported guideline alignment. Analyses were descriptive only, without subgroup comparisons or multivariable modeling, and Türkiye-specific contextual factors such as hospital level, stewardship support, pharmacy involvement, microbiology capacity, infection-control infrastructure, resource availability, purchasing policies, and protocol implementation were not directly measured. The structured evidence-benchmark comparisons should therefore be interpreted only as descriptive contextualization of self-reported practices, not as measures of surgeon knowledge, quality of care, clinical appropriateness, or patient outcomes. Some items, such as redosing criteria, allowed multiple non-hierarchical responses, limiting interpretation of clinical decision-making priorities. Finally, primary arthroplasty was evaluated as a single category, without joint-specific or revision-specific data, limiting procedure-level interpretation because prophylaxis and infection-prevention workflows may differ across hip, knee, shoulder, other primary arthroplasty, and revision procedures. Any implications derived from these findings should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than evidence supporting practice-changing interventions.

Conclusion

This open web-based survey among orthopedic specialists in Türkiye found high reported alignment with selected guideline-based benchmarks for prophylactic agent selection and initial-dose timing in primary arthroplasty, but variability was observed in prophylaxis duration; drain/discharge-related antibiotic use; screening-decolonization workflows; asymptomatic bacteriuria management; surgical field preparation and irrigation; and local antibiotic strategies. Not all variation should be interpreted as guideline discordance; some differences may reflect acceptable practice diversity in evidence-uncertain or context-dependent domains. As a voluntary, self-reported descriptive survey without a known denominator, response rate, audit verification, patient-based data, or outcome correlation, this study cannot assess quality of care, provide representative estimates of national practice, support causal inferences, or justify practice-changing recommendations. The absence of joint-specific data for hip, knee, shoulder, or other arthroplasty procedures, as well as revision-specific variables, further limits procedure-level interpretation. Therefore, these findings should be viewed as respondent-level descriptive data and as a basis for hypothesis generation. Future studies with defined sampling frames, audit-based practice verification, hierarchical survey items, joint-specific data, and patient-level outcomes are needed to evaluate how these reported variations relate to actual practice and clinical outcomes.
