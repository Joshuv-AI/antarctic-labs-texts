Lived experience of organizational silence among nursing faculty: a phenomenological study
Authors: Begum Yalçın, Güzin Ayan, Nilgün Göktepe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionOrganizational silence in nursing academia limits communication, weakens collaboration, and harms faculty well-being and educational quality. Hierarchical structures, fear of negative consequences, lead faculty to withhold ideas and concerns. This study aims to explore the experiences, reasons, and consequences of silence among nursing faculty members.MethodsA phenomenological descriptive qualitative design was employed for this study. Nineteen participants were recruited through a purposeful snowball sampling strategy. Data were collected via semi-structured online interviews and analyzed using Colaizzi’s phenomenological method, with the COREQ checklist guiding reporting rigor and transparency.ResultsFindings revealed three overarching thematic areas describing participants’ lived experiences: navigating silence within hierarchical academic structures, reasons for remaining silent and experiencing personal and organizational consequences of remaining silent. Participants described staying silent to avoid being labeled as troublemakers, to prevent retaliation or increased workload, and because they believed that speaking up would not lead to meaningful change, which contributed to feelings of learned helplessness. Silence also served as a psychological self-protection mechanism.ConclusionRemaining silent significantly shapes both the academic work environment and the well-being of nursing faculty members. Remaining silent undermines faculty well-being and the quality of nursing education. Promoting psychological safety and open communication is essential to support faculty voices and improve academic and clinical outcomes.Keywords: Nursing faculty members, Voice, Silence, Academic work environment

Introduction

An academic setting is expected to encourage freedom of speech and foster a culture in which all opinions are valued [1]. Creating open forums for discussion, encouraging feedback, and respecting diverse perspectives support professional growth, collaboration, and academic excellence. In nursing education, these expectations carry additional weight, as nursing faculty are responsible for advancing nursing science, contributing to the profession, and serving as role models for students and the broader community [2]. Many nursing faculty, however, enter academia with strong clinical expertise but limited preparation for the multifaceted academic roles of teaching, research, and service, which can be more challenging to adapt to in the academic environment [3].

Healthcare professionals, including academic faculty, also have a moral, ethical, and legal obligation to contribute to a respectful, safe, and supportive work environment [4, 5]. Fostering respectful communication, providing conflict-resolution resources, and promoting empathy within academic teams are essential elements of a healthy academic climate, one that supports learning, enhances student outcomes, protects faculty well-being, and contributes to institutional success.

Despite these ideals, faculty members may withhold their ideas, concerns, and criticisms for many reasons, including fear of retaliation, hierarchical academic systems, interpersonal conflict, and a lack of institutional support [6]. The literature suggests that silence among faculty can weaken collaboration, restrict innovation, and negatively affect academic team functioning [7]. However, research that focuses specifically on silence within academic nursing faculties and, the unique challenges nursing faculty face when navigating academic hierarchy, remains limited. This gap highlights the need for a deeper understanding of how silence manifests and its implications for nursing academia.

To guide this study, Morrison and Milliken’s [8] Theory of Organizational Silence was adopted as the theoretical framework. The theory explains how structural and cultural conditions, such as hierarchical power structures, low trust in leadership, and beliefs about the futility of speaking up, suppress employee voice, creating climates where silence becomes the default response. In nursing academia, where hierarchical structures and title-based authority are pronounced, these conditions can restrict open dialogue and undermine faculty well-being. Repeated experiences of being ignored or discouraged may lead to learned helplessness and foster collective norms that inhibit the expression of voice [9]. Evidence from Turkish academia further reflects these dynamics, documenting a reluctance to express opinions or propose new ideas in rigid hierarchical environments [1, 10, 11].

Organizational silence carries substantial consequences. At the individual level, it can lead to emotional exhaustion, burnout, diminished professional identity, and reduced job satisfaction [7, 12]. At the organizational level, silence undermines problem-solving, innovation, and collaboration, while weakening trust and commitment within academic teams. In nursing academia, these outcomes pose a threat to both faculty retention and the overall quality of nursing education.

Given these concerns, understanding why nursing faculty remain silent, how they experience silence, and what consequences arise from this silence is essential. This study was conducted in Türkiye due to its unique academic culture and the prevalence of hierarchical structures in its academic institutions. Despite the centrality of faculty voice to academic success, limited research has explored the lived experiences of silence specifically among nursing faculty. This study was therefore conducted to address this gap by examining the experiences, reasons, and consequences of silence among nursing faculty in Türkiye. Using Morrison and Milliken’s [8] theoretical framework provides a comprehensive lens to understand the antecedents, reinforcing mechanisms, and outcomes of silence. It offers insight into how more open, psychologically safe, and collaborative academic environments can be cultivated.

Methods

Aim

This study aims to investigate nursing faculty members’s experiences, reasons, and consequences they face for remaining silent.

Study design

This study employed a phenomenological descriptive qualitative design to understand nursing faculty members’ experiences of silence in academic settings. Phenomenology was chosen because it focuses on exploring and interpreting individuals’ lived experiences and the meanings they ascribe to a particular phenomenon [13]. This approach aligned with the purpose of the study, allowing for an in-depth examination of how nursing faculty perceive, experience, and respond to silence within their professional roles. By capturing the essence of these experiences, phenomenology provided a rich and nuanced understanding of the structural, relational, and emotional factors that contribute to organizational silence in nursing academia.

The Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist was used to guide the design, conduct, and reporting of the study [14]. Adherence to COREQ ensured methodological transparency, rigor, and comprehensive reporting across all stages of data collection and analysis. This structured approach strengthened the credibility and trustworthiness of the study while maintaining alignment with phenomenological research principles.

Setting and participants

The study population consisted of nursing faculty members working at universities with bachelor’s degree programs. In Türkiye, 189 institutions (136 public universities and 63 private universities) provide undergraduate nursing education. The inclusion criteria were: (1) being employed as a faculty member in a nursing school, (2) holding a graduate degree in nursing or currently enrolled in a nursing graduate program. Participants were required to teach nursing courses, ensuring they were directly involved in academic nursing roles. Recruitment was not limited to a single geographic region; nursing faculty from different cities and institutional types (public/private) across Türkiye were eligible to participate. Both full-time and part-time faculty members were included, provided they met the above criteria.

A purposeful snowball sampling strategy was employed to ensure participants could provide rich, relevant data on the study topic. Snowball sampling was selected because it enables access to individuals who may otherwise be difficult to identify within academic settings [15]. Recruitment began when one of the researchers, respecting the participants’ privacy and autonomy, contacted nursing faculty members from her former institution who met the inclusion criteria. Although the researcher knew these initial participants, they were informed that participation was voluntary, confidential, and unrelated to any professional relationship, minimizing potential influence on participation or data collection. At the conclusion of each interview, participants were asked to refer colleagues who also met the inclusion criteria and had experienced silence in academia.

Referrals were contacted individually by email, which included an invitation to participate, a brief description of the study, and the inclusion criteria. Individuals who expressed interest were screened by the research team prior to scheduling the interview to confirm that they met all eligibility requirements. This multistep approach ensured that all participants were appropriate for the phenomenological exploration of organizational silence.

This approach aligns with the principles of phenomenological research, which prioritize participants’ lived experiences with the phenomenon to enable in-depth exploration and accurate thematic representation [16]. Although phenomenological research typically includes between 3 and 25 participants, sample size was determined by the principle of data saturation—defined as the point at which no new themes or information emerged. After interviewing 19 participants, the research team observed strong repetition of experiences and no emergence of new themes, confirming that we had reached a point where additional interviews were unlikely to yield new insights, thus achieving data saturation.

Data collection tools

Semi-structured interview questions were meticulously prepared by researchers with extensive experience in qualitative research and the topic of silence, drawing on existing literature [8, 9, 17, 18] (Table 1). In addition to the interview guide, socio-demographic and professional information of the participants (age, gender, professional experience, institutional experience, field of specialization, administrative duty status) were also noted. Two pilot interviews were conducted to test the comprehensibility of the questions, and the semi-structured interview form was finalized after the interviews. The two pilot interviews were excluded from the study, but their contribution to the process was valuable.

Data collection

The data was collected by one of the researchers who has extensive expertise in qualitative inquiry and organizational silence. Two pilot interviews were conducted, although excluded from the final analysis, informed the refinement of the wording and flow of the questions.

Data were collected through online semi-structured interviews conducted via Google Meet. Prior to the interview, participants received study information and confirmed their eligibility through email. Verbal informed consent was obtained at the beginning of each session, consistent with ethical guidelines for minimal-risk online research. Written identifiers were not collected, and participants were reminded that participation was voluntary and that they could withdraw at any time without penalty.

Given the sensitivity of workplace experiences and the potential risks to employment, several confidentiality measures were implemented. Participants were encouraged to keep their cameras off, only audio recordings were taken, and all audio files were anonymized and permanently deleted after transcription to further reduce identifiability [19].

All interviews were conducted by a female nursing faculty member with a PhD and substantial experience in qualitative research. Reflexive notes were taken throughout the conversations to support depth and maintain analytic rigor. Interviews averaged 40 min. Data saturation was achieved at the 19th interview. In this study, the authors aimed to recruit a diverse group of nursing faculty with different academic titles working in public and private universities. Initial code saturation, defined as the point at which no new codes emerged, was observed around the 15th interview during the early stages of analysis. In line with Colaizzi’s phenomenological method [20], data collection and analysis proceeded concurrently, allowing emerging meanings and themes to be continuously examined and refined [13, 20]. Additional interviews were conducted to ensure depth, capture variation across participants, and confirm the consistency of the identified themes. By the final interviews, no new themes, sub-themes, or meanings were identified, indicating that meaning saturation had been achieved. Data collection was completed between February and June 2024.

Data analysis

Within the first three days of the interviews, voice recordings were transcribed verbatim using the Microsoft 365 Word Dictation feature. The first author then listened to each recording in full to verify the accuracy of the transcriptions. All authors, who hold PhD degrees in nursing management, are faculty members, and have extensive training and experience in qualitative research, bring a wealth of expertise to this study, ensuring its credibility.

Data were meticulously analyzed following Colaizzi’s [20] seven-step method. First, transcripts were read and reviewed in their entirety to gain familiarity with the participants’ accounts. Significant or relevant statements were underlined, reflecting experiences, reasons, and consequences of silence. In keeping with Morrison and Milliken’s [8] organizational silence framework, particular attention was paid to identifying statements that reflected antecedents (e.g., fear, hierarchical barriers), processes (e.g., withholding ideas, strategic withdrawal), and consequences (e.g., reduced commitment, psychological strain) (Fig. 1).

Conceptual framework of organizational silence among nursing faculty, adapted from Morrison and Milliken (2000)

Each researcher independently coded the transcripts, identifying and labeling meaning units. The research team then met to compare initial codes, examining consistencies and differences, and to explore connections between codes in relation to the theoretical framework. Through iterative discussions, codes were clustered into subthemes and themes, ensuring that interpretations aligned both with the data and with the conceptual lens provided by Morrison and Milliken’s [8] model. The team deliberated on each subtheme and theme until consensus was reached, at which point the thematic structure was finalized.

This combined inductive and theory-informed approach allowed for both emergent insights from participants’ lived experiences and a structured understanding of how organizational silence operates in the nursing academic context.

Rigor

Lincoln and Guba’s [21] four criteria were applied to the data to attain rigor. Since the interviewer had no administrative ties to the participants, credibility was established. All participants gave their agreement to participate in the study, and the interviewer encouraged them to answer all questions freely because there were no right or incorrect answers. In order to achieve conformance, one researcher performed the interviews while the other worked independently and actively on the data analysis process. Dependability was made possible by using a semi-structured interview guide to ensure consistency. By providing detailed explanations and verbatim remarks, transferability was attained, allowing readers to determine whether the current study’s findings apply to their situations.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by Kutahya Health Sciences University Ethical Board (2023/07–17) and follow the Declaration of Helsinki standards. Verbal consent was obtained from all participants for their participation in the study and record the interviews. Confidentiality and anonymity of the interviews were ensured, and participants were coded with the letter P (P1-P19) in the transcript of the interviews.

Themes

Data analysis generated three overarching themes that reflect the participants’ lived experience of organizational silence within nursing faculties in Türkiye (1) navigating silence in a hierarchical academic culture, (2) silence as a strategy for protection and survival and (3) the emotional and organizational consequences of silence.

Theme 1. Navigating silence in a hierarchical academic culture

Participants described silence as a constant, embodied part of their professional life, not just an isolated decision. The experience was often accompanied by emotional strain, tension in relationships, and a sense of walking on eggshells. Many participants spoke about modifying their behavior to “keep the peace” in meetings, avoiding eye contact, or withholding suggestions, even when they saw clear issues.

Especially when you start a family and have a child, the dynamics change… I try to minimize the problems as much as possible, and this causes me to remain silent.(P16)

Silence often took place in public settings (department meetings, clinical practice discussions) but had private emotional consequences, such as frustration, resignation, or guilt.

When I tried to give feedback to my manager, her mood changes. Then you feel that this will not end well… Because this will come back as a workload. That’s why we were remaining silent. (P4)

Theme 2. Reasons for remaining silent

This theme reflects how participants used silence as a deliberate method to protect themselves from conflict, retaliation, job loss, increased workload, or psychological harm. It also encompasses learned helplessness, in which repeated failures to create change led participants to silence themselves intentionally.This overarching theme includes five subthemes: (1) avoiding trouble, (2) fear of speaking up, (3) workload avoidance, (4) lack of belief in change and (5) silence for self-protection.

Subtheme 1: Avoiding trouble

Silence was used to maintain harmony and avoid being labeled a troublemaker. This was particularly important for those with family responsibilities or those who valued stability over confrontation.

I know that when a colleague was on maternity leave, for example, she went to lecture because her dean wanted her to so she accepted because she did not want that her routine to be disrupted. (P10)

There are people who accept everything. Therefore, you cannot be very free in such an environment. You cannot express everything freely in order not to cause problems because you think that when you say something, it will all come back to me in the end! (P6)

Subtheme 2: Fear of speaking up

Fear was a central reason for silence. Many participants reported economic concerns, fear of dismissal, or concerns about harming their academic career.

For example, I go to my clinical practice every day… it exhausts me a lot, but unfortunately, I cannot speak out or ask for a regulation on this because we have economic concerns; we keep silent not to lose our jobs. (P15)

A friend of mine, she did not submit the petition about our salaries because she was afraid of being fired. (P4)

Subtheme 3: Workload avoidance

Some participants noted that speaking up resulted in punitive increases in workload, while others remained silent intentionally to avoid additional duties.

If possible, I keep quiet during the meetings so that another workload is not placed on my shoulders. (P7)

When I tried to give feedback to my manager, her mood changes. Then you feel that this will not end well… Because this will come back as a workload. That’s why we were remaining silent. (P4)

Subtheme 4: Lack of belief in change

Repeated experiences of being ignored created a sense of learned helplessness. Participants described giving up raising concerns because they no longer believed change was possible.

If I say this now, nothing will change. I know nothing would change. So, it’s a bit like learned helplessness… then we say why should I raise any of my concerns and then we all remain silent. (P10)

I usually do not hesitate to raise my voice. But I don’t for the last one or two years… Most of my concerns in the previous years were not heard. I don’t make any suggestions or raise any concerns anymore. (P9)

We talked a lot, but nothing changed. We have become silent now. (P11)

Subtheme 5: Silence for self-protection

Participants described a need to protect their emotional and psychological well-being when their efforts to speak up were minimized or dismissed.

What difference does it make if I bring it up? If I bring it up, it’s nothing more than a gossip for them… I don’t even make a sound. (P7)

I saw myself on the path of remaining silent in order to psychologically protect my health because we did not want to be psychologically involved in this war anymore. (P16)

Theme 3. Personal and organizational consequences of silence

Remaining silent had profound personal and organizational impacts. These consequences reflected emotional exhaustion, decreased loyalty, and even decisions to withdraw from positions or leave institutions entirely. This theme included two subthemes: (1) personal consequences and (2) organizational consequences.

Subtheme 1: Personal consequences

Participants described emotional distress, feelings of inadequacy, and strain that extended into personal life.

This made me feel inadequate about my job. (P16)

One participant described a traumatic moment during the pandemic when accumulated workplace pressure affected her relationship with her child:

At the end I took the toy. I broke it! My daughter was very scared of me! … I said to my husband, that I can’t do it, I shouldn’t continue to work there. (P6)

Subtheme 2: Organizational consequences

Participants reported decreased loyalty, disengagement, and decisions to leave their positions due to unresolved systemic issues.

These feeling decreases loyalty to the organization. (P4)

Keeping silent in the face of an injustice we experience reduces loyalty to the organization. (P1)

…I left because these problems were not solved even though we voiced them. (P10)

Discussion

The findings of this study offer a comprehensive understanding of how organizational silence is experienced and reinforced within nursing academia in Türkiye. Consistent with Morrison and Milliken’s [8] Theory of Organizational Silence, the three overarching themes identified in the results demonstrate how antecedents, processes, and consequences interact to sustain silence as a persistent cultural pattern rather than an individual choice.

Navigating silence in a hierarchical academic culture theme highlights silence as a continuous emotional and relational experience embedded in daily academic life. Participants described tension in meetings, hesitancy to share ideas, and behavioral withdrawal, echoing Edmondson’s [22] concept of low psychological safety and Bowen and Blackmon’s [17] concept of a silence climate. This aligns with Morrison and Milliken’s [8] argument that silence is a relational phenomenon shaped by perceived risks and fears of futility. Broader research similarly shows that academic hierarchies, prestige-based interactions, and competitive environments can amplify fear and discourage dissenting views [7, 12]. In such climates, expressing concerns may be perceived as a threat to one’s academic standing, further reinforcing silent behavior [23, 24]. The findings of the research must be interpreted in the context of Turkiye’s unique cultural setting, which has a significant impact on nursing faculty members’ real encounters with silence. Turkiye’s is characterized by collectivism and substantial power distance, according to Hofstede’s [25] cultural features. In these contexts, hierarchical structures are highly valued, and “superiors” are often viewed as authoritarian paternalists [25]. According to a study by Yalçın and Ayan [9], nursing faculty indicated that silence is often due to fear of harming one’s academic career. This cultural mindset may increase acquiescent silence, especially among junior faculty, who may view dissent as disrespectful or risky for their job security.

Faculty members deliberately remain silent to avoid trouble, prevent retaliation, escape increased workload, or protect their emotional well-being. These experiences mirror the central antecedents described by Morrison and Milliken [8], including fear of negative consequences, low trust, and the belief that speaking up is futile. Prior research also shows that toxic or authoritarian leadership exacerbates these fears, fostering climates where silence becomes a coping mechanism rather than a passive behavior [26, 27]. A significant contribution of this study is the shift identified in participants’ motivations: unlike earlier findings that emphasized silence as a way to protect one’s career [1, 28, 29], participants in this study prioritized protecting their psychological well-being. This supports Seligman’s [30] learned helplessness theory and aligns with the “processes and mechanisms” stage in Morrison and Milliken’s [8] model, where repeated dismissals reinforce beliefs that speaking up is pointless.

The third theme demonstrates the profound impact of silence on faculty well-being and institutional functioning. Participants described experiencing emotional exhaustion, inadequacy, and a spillover into their personal lives, consistent with occupational health frameworks, such as the spillover effect [31]. Organizational consequences included disengagement, reduced loyalty, and decisions to leave institutions, supporting Morrison and Milliken’s [8] proposition that silence is self-reinforcing and detrimental to organizational learning. Broader literature also highlights how systemic academic pressures, such as an overemphasis on research output and competitive structures, can heighten feelings of inadequacy and further suppress voice [23, 32].

Together, these findings reveal a cyclical dynamic in which hierarchical structures, fear, toxic leadership, and perceived futility reinforce silence, while silence itself produces psychological harm and organizational decline. This study extends previous literature by showing that silence among nursing faculty is not only a professional avoidance strategy but also an emotional survival mechanism shaped by limited psychological safety and entrenched academic norms. Nursing faculty members should be a role model to their students. In this context, faculty silence may be implicitly modeled as acceptable behavior, shaping students’ perceptions of when and whether to speak up in clinical settings. Students may internalize silence as a norm, particularly in hierarchical environments where questioning authority is discouraged. One of the most important nursing role is advocacy. Therefore, the absence of visible advocacy behaviors among faculty may weaken students’ development of professional voice and responsibility. If the students do not learn the importance of advocating for their patients particularly for patient safety issues or when to speak up about their rights would be harmful for the future nurses. This may have direct implications for clinical safety, as future nurses may hesitate to report errors, question unsafe practices, or escalate concerns. Such patterns may contribute to the normalization of silence in clinical environments, potentially compromising patient safety outcomes. Thus, faculty silence not only affects academic environments but may also extend into clinical practice through the professional socialization of nursing students. Breaking this cycle requires intentional institutional strategies, such as enhancing psychological safety, fostering supportive communication cultures, providing safe platforms for expression, and offering leadership training that focuses on emotional intelligence and inclusive decision-making [33, 34]. Addressing faculty silence is therefore essential not only for faculty well-being but also for cultivating a culture of speaking up among future nurses.

Limitations

The study’s findings can not be generalized since it is not always possible to generalize qualitative research findings. Participants may feel compelled to censor themselves because the ideas of voice and silence are contentious.

Conclusion

The findings of this study demonstrate that remaining silent has profound and multifaceted effects on both the academic work environment and the well-being of nursing faculty members. Consistent with Morrison and Milliken’s (2000) framework, silence arises through the interaction of several antecedents, such as hierarchical academic structures, fear of retribution, and a diminished belief that speaking up will produce meaningful change, and the subsequent processes through which faculty deliberately withhold concerns, ideas, and feedback. Although silence may serve as a short-term protective strategy, it results in substantial personal and professional consequences, including psychological strain, emotional exhaustion, reduced organizational commitment, and professional stagnation. Over time, these consequences extend beyond individual experiences, contributing to a broader academic culture that limits collaboration, innovation, and the overall development of nursing education. Creating environments where faculty feel psychologically safe, valued, and supported in expressing their perspectives is essential for strengthening academic functioning and advancing the nursing profession.

Implications for nursing policy

The results of this study underscore the urgent need for nursing institutions and policymakers to address organizational silence as a critical threat to faculty well-being and educational quality. Policies should prioritize psychological safety, transparency, and supportive communication cultures to counteract the effects of silence driven by fear, hierarchical pressures, or perceived futility. At the institutional level, nursing schools should adopt clear policies that protect faculty who express concerns, establish confidential reporting mechanisms, encourage structured opportunities for dialogue, and require leadership training that emphasizes emotional intelligence, conflict resolution, and inclusive decision-making. Ensuring that faculty participation in governance is meaningful and protected from retaliation is essential for strengthening voice and engagement, especially for junior faculty members, so that they can speak up without fear that their academic careers will be harmed. Faculty management should create a safe environment by including junior faculty in faculty commissions and encouraging them to be active members. In addition, providing orientation about their new roles, including the university’s mission and vision, is important. Providing guidance for transitioning from clinical practice to academia may also help to develop bottom-up strategies to enhance open communication. At the individual and peer level, junior faculty members can also contribute to creating “micro-spaces” of psychological safety within their immediate work environments. For example, they may form small peer support groups, engage in informal reflective discussions with trusted colleagues, and create safe opportunities for sharing concerns in low-risk settings. Such micro-level interactions can help normalize speaking up, build collective confidence, and gradually challenge the broader culture of silence.

At the national policy level, regulatory and accreditation bodies should integrate expectations for healthy communication climates and psychological safety into program standards. Policies promoting equitable leadership practices, workload regulation, and anti-bullying protections can help reduce power imbalances that contribute to silence. Because faculty well-being is closely tied to student outcomes, nursing policy should also support multi-institutional and multicultural research examining silence, voice, and organizational culture across diverse academic contexts. Strengthening communication systems and institutional support structures will not only safeguard nursing faculty but also contribute to the development of an empowered academic workforce and a more resilient nursing profession.
