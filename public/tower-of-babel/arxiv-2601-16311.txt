A Non-Autonomous Model for Parabolic Implosion
Katelynn Huneycutt, Samantha Sandberg-Clark, Liz Vivas

Abstract
. Orthogonal polynomials appear naturally in the study of compositions of Möbius transformations. In this paper, we consider several classes of orthogonal polynomials associated to non-autonomous perturbations of a parabolic Möbius map. Our results can be viewed as instances of non-autonomous parabolic implosion, including a random perturbative regime in which convergence holds almost surely.



1.
Introduction

The study of parabolic implosion, starting with the work of Lavaurs
[
8
]
, has had striking consequences on the study of dynamics in one complex variable. In recent years, this study has been extended quite successfully to several complex variables.

Parabolic implosion in several variables has been developed in multiple settings. Starting with a map with a semi-attracting fixed point, Bedford, Smillie, and Ueda
[
5
]
described a parabolic implosion phenomenon, later generalized by Dujardin and Lyubich
[
7
]
. Similarly Bianchi studied parabolic implosion for a class of maps on dimension
2
tangent to the identity
[
6
]
.

Another setup in which parabolic implosion has been generalized, is to the case of skew-product maps that are tangent to the identity. Recent work by Astorg, Buff, Dujardin, Peters, Raissy
[
3
]
exploit a parabolic implosion phenomenon in this setup to prove the existence of wandering Fatou components. Similar results have been obtained by Astorg and Boc-Thaler
[
4
]
as well as by Astorg and Bianchi
[
2
]
.

Although our core estimates are one-dimensional, they are designed to be applied to skew-product dynamics in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
, thus describing new cases of parabolic implosion. We follow the approach of Vivas and study the map
F(z)=\frac{z}{1-z}
and its perturbations. As in
[
9
]
, we transform the problem to a multiplication of matrices and use orthogonal polynomial theory. We should point out that in our new setup we require a different class of orthogonal polynomials than the ones used in
[
9
]
. See section
3
for more details.

Let us start by describing our results in more detail. The classical theorem of Lavaurs involves the perturbation of any given map
f
as below.

Theorem 1

(Lavaurs)
.

Let
f
be defined in a neighborhood
V
of the origin and be of the form
f(z)=z+z^{2}+O(z^{3})
. Consider the perturbation of f as follows: given
\epsilon>0
let
f_{\epsilon}(z)\coloneqq f(z)+\epsilon^{2}
. If we take a sequence of numbers
N_{\epsilon}\to\infty
and
\epsilon\to 0
, such that
N_{\epsilon}-\frac{\pi}{\epsilon}\rightarrow 0
. Then we obtain the following:

\displaystyle(f_{\epsilon})^{N_{\epsilon}}\rightarrow\mathcal{L}_{f}

uniformly on compacts on the basin of attraction of
f
. Here
\mathcal{L}_{f}
is the Lavaurs map of
f
.

The Lavaurs map of
f
is a transformation describing the discrepancy between the incoming and outgoing linearizing coordinates for
f
on their respective incoming and outgoing basins. We will refer to this phenomenon as
autonomous parabolic implosion
for
f
.

1.1.
Non-autonomous perturbations

The natural question is what happens if the perturbation varies at
each step. Given a sequence
\{\epsilon_{k}\}_{k=1}^{N}
, define
f_{k}(z)\coloneqq f(z)+\epsilon_{k}^{2}
. We are interested in under which conditions on
\{\epsilon_{k}\}_{k=1}^{N}
does

\displaystyle f_{N}\circ f_{N-1}\circ\cdots\circ f_{2}\circ f_{1}\rightarrow\mathcal{L}_{f}.

This “non-autonomous” parabolic implosion shows up naturally when studying parabolic implosion in several variables. In effect, Astorg et al.
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
have proven results of this type.

In
[
9
]
, Vivas focuses on the case of the initial map to be
f(z)=z/(1-z)
since in this case each perturbation is also a Möbius transformation, and the Lavaurs map is the identity.

1.2.
Our results

We extend the results of
[
9
]
by allowing both multiplicative and additive perturbations of the model map.
Specifically, given sequences
\{\rho_{k},\epsilon_{k}\}_{k=1}^{N}
, we consider

f_{k}(z):=\rho_{k}\frac{z}{1-z}+\epsilon_{k}^{2},\qquad\rho_{k}=e^{2\pi i\theta_{k}},\ \theta_{k}\in\mathbb{C},

and ask under what conditions the composition

f_{N}\circ f_{N-1}\circ\cdots\circ f_{1}

converges to the identity.

Our main theorems show that this convergence persists under several regimes:

•

Purely multiplicative perturbations (
\epsilon_{k}=0
).
Convergence holds provided the sequence
\{\theta_{k}\}
approximates
1/N
closely enough, with controlled error terms (Theorem
A
).

•

Combined multiplicative and additive perturbations.
More general families of convergent sequences arise when
\{\theta_{k}\}
and
\{\epsilon_{k}\}
are simultaneously perturbed (Theorem
B
).

•

Random additive perturbations (
\rho_{k}=1
).
If the additive errors fluctuate randomly with mean
\frac{\pi}{N}
, then convergence still holds almost surely (Theorem
D
).

These results highlight the delicate balance in the non-autonomous setting.
Errors of order
1/N^{2}
already require strong correlation conditions, but introducing randomness allows one to relax these restrictions.
Note also that although multiplicative and additive perturbations may look analogous, they are not directly reducible to each other: in the additive case each perturbation can be conjugated to a rotation, but the conjugacy varies with
k
, so the multiplicative case cannot be deduced from the additive one.

In fact, we will see later that the rotation case (
\rho_{k}\neq 1
) cannot be deduced directly from the additive perturbative case (i.e.,
\rho_{k}=1
).

Let us state our theorems now:

Theorem A

(Multiplicative perturbations)
.

Let

f_{k}(z):=\frac{\rho_{k}z}{1-z},\qquad\rho_{k}=e^{2\pi i\theta_{k}},\qquad\theta_{k}\in\mathbb{C}.

Suppose
\{\theta_{k}\}
satisfies any of the following:

(1)

\displaystyle\theta_{k}=\frac{1}{N}+O\!\left(\tfrac{1}{N^{3}}\right)
;

(2)

\displaystyle\theta_{k}=\frac{1}{N}+\frac{c_{k}}{N^{2}}
with
c_{k}
uniformly bounded and
\;c_{k}+c_{k+1}=O\!\left(\tfrac{1}{N}\right)
for odd
k
;

(3)

\displaystyle\theta_{k}=\frac{1}{N}+\frac{Ce^{2\pi ik/N}}{N^{2}}
where
C
is any constant in
\mathbb{C}
.

Then

f_{N}\circ f_{N-1}\circ\cdots\circ f_{1}\longrightarrow\mathrm{Id}

as
N\to\infty
, uniformly on compact subsets of
\mathbb{C}
.

Theorem B

(Combined multiplicative and additive perturbations)
.

Let

f_{k}(z):=\frac{\rho_{k}z}{1-z}+\epsilon_{k}^{2},\qquad\rho_{k}=e^{2\pi i\theta_{k}},\ \ \theta_{k}\in\mathbb{C}.

Suppose
\{\theta_{k}\}
and
\{\epsilon_{k}\}
satisfy any of the following:

(1)

\displaystyle\theta_{k}=\frac{1}{N}+O\!\left(\tfrac{1}{N^{3}}\right)
and
\displaystyle\epsilon_{k}=O\!\left(\tfrac{1}{N^{2}}\right)
;

(2)

\displaystyle\theta_{k}=\frac{1}{N}+\frac{c_{k}}{N^{2}}
with
c_{k}
uniformly bounded and
\,c_{k}+c_{k+1}=O\!\left(\tfrac{1}{N}\right)
for odd
k
, and
\displaystyle\epsilon_{k}=O\!\left(\tfrac{1}{N^{2}}\right)
;

(3)

\displaystyle\theta_{k}=\frac{1}{N}+\frac{Ce^{2\pi ik/N}}{N^{2}}
and
\displaystyle\epsilon_{k}=O\!\left(\tfrac{1}{N^{2}}\right)
; where
C
is any constant in
\mathbb{C}
.

(4)

\displaystyle\theta_{k}=0
and
\displaystyle\epsilon_{k}=\frac{\pi}{N}+O\!\left(\tfrac{1}{N^{3}}\right)
;

(5)

\displaystyle\theta_{k}=0
and
\displaystyle\epsilon_{k}=\frac{\pi}{N}+\frac{c_{k}}{N^{2}}
with
\,c_{k}+c_{N-k}=O\!\left(\tfrac{1}{N}\right)
.

Then

f_{N}\circ f_{N-1}\circ\cdots\circ f_{1}\longrightarrow\mathrm{Id}

as
N\to\infty
, uniformly on compact subsets of
\mathbb{C}
.

Remark
.

Conditions (2)–(3) show that errors of order
1/N^{2}
in the multiplicative phase
\theta_{k}
are admissible provided they satisfy summation/cancellation constraints, while the additive terms remain at scale
1/N^{2}
. Conditions (4)–(5) cover the purely additive regime near the critical scale
\pi/N
familiar from Lavaurs theory, again allowing
1/N^{2}
-level modulations subject to symmetry constraints.

We also will prove that the multiplicative case is inherently different than the additive one. We do that by exhibiting an example in which one sequence composition converges to the identity; however, a conjugate sequence, does not.

Theorem C

(Difference between additive and multiplicative)
.

There exist sequences
\{\epsilon_{k}\}
and
\{\rho_{k}\}
for
1\leq k\leq N
, such that:

(1)

For each
k
, the maps
\displaystyle{f_{k}(z)=\frac{\rho_{k}z}{1-z}}
and
\displaystyle{g_{k}(z)=\frac{z}{1-z}+\epsilon_{k}^{2}}
are conjugated.

(2)

We have
g_{N}\circ g_{N-1}\circ\cdots\circ g_{1}
converges uniformly on compacts to the Identity.

(3)

The composition
f_{N}\circ f_{N-1}\circ\cdots\circ f_{1}(z)
does not converge to
z
for any
z
.

Finally, we study what happens if we apply a random composition. We can weaken the conditions if we allow for a sequence of independent and bounded random variables with
0
mean as the scaling factors of
1/N^{2}
. More explicitly:

Theorem D

(Random perturbations)
.

For fixed
N
, let
\{\eta_{k}\}_{k=1}^{N+1}
be independent, bounded, mean-zero random variables, and set

\epsilon_{k}=\frac{\pi}{N}+\frac{\eta_{k}}{N^{1+\delta}}\quad(\delta>0),\qquad f_{k}(z)=\frac{z}{1-z}+\epsilon_{k}^{2}.

Then

f_{N}\circ f_{N-1}\circ\cdots\circ f_{1}\longrightarrow\mathrm{Id}

as
N\to\infty
, uniformly on compact subsets of
\mathbb{C}
, with probability one.

Remark
.

The random theorem shows that decreasing the error scale to
N^{-(1+\delta)}
(any
\delta>0
) and imposing only boundedness and zero mean suffices for almost sure convergence, without the arithmetic correlations required in the deterministic
1/N^{2}
-scale cases.

The paper is organized as follows: in Section
2
we link iterations of a sequence of linear transformations to orthogonal polynomials.
In Section

, we review the relevant results on orthogonal polynomials.
In Section
4
, we prove Theorems
A
and
B
.
In Section

and

We prove Theorem

2.
Compositions of Linear Transformations

In this section we describe how the compositions of linear fractional transformations can be expressed in terms of orthogonal polynomials.

Lemma 1
.

Given

f_{k}(z)=\frac{\alpha_{k}z+\beta_{k}}{\gamma_{k}z+\delta_{k}},

an infinite sequence of non-trivial linear fractional transformations for
k\geq 1
(i.e., assume
\alpha_{k}\delta_{k}\neq\beta_{k}\gamma_{k}
for all
k
). Assume that
\gamma_{k}\neq 0
for all
k
.

Denote by
F_{N}:=f_{N}\circ f_{N-1}\circ\cdots\circ f_{1}
. Then we have the following formula:

\displaystyle F_{N}(z)=\frac{A_{N}z+B_{N}}{C_{N}z+D_{N}},

where the coefficients are given by the following recurrence formulas:

\displaystyle C_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\left(\delta_{k+1}+\frac{\gamma_{k+1}\alpha_{k}}{\gamma_{k}}\right)C_{k}-\frac{\gamma_{k+1}}{\gamma_{k}}\left(\alpha_{k}\delta_{k}-\beta_{k}\gamma_{k}\right)C_{k-1},

\displaystyle C_{0}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle C_{1}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{1};

(1)

\displaystyle D_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\left(\delta_{k+1}+\frac{\gamma_{k+1}\alpha_{k}}{\gamma_{k}}\right)D_{k}-\frac{\gamma_{k+1}}{\gamma_{k}}\left(\alpha_{k}\delta_{k}-\beta_{k}\gamma_{k}\right)D_{k-1},

\displaystyle D_{0}

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle D_{1}

\displaystyle=\delta_{1};

and

\displaystyle A_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\gamma_{k+1}}(C_{k+1}-\delta_{k+1}C_{k});

(2)

\displaystyle B_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\gamma_{k+1}}(D_{k+1}-\delta_{k+1}D_{k}).

Proof.

For all
k
, by the definition of
C_{k}
and
D_{k}

\displaystyle C_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{k+1}A_{k}+\delta_{k+1}C_{k};

\displaystyle D_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{k+1}B_{k}+\delta_{k+1}D_{k}.

By solving for
A_{k}
and
B_{k}
, we find (
1
).
The formulas for
C_{k+1}
and
D_{k+1}
follow by writing the definitions of
C_{k+1}
and
D_{k+1}
, substituting in the definition of
A_{k}
and
B_{k}
respectively, then finally replacing the new
A_{k-1}
and
B_{k-1}
terms using the formulas from
equations (
1
). Rearranging the remaining terms yields the result.
∎

Remark
.

(1)

Note that we can also obtain expressions for
A_{k}
and
B_{k}
as recurrence equations, similarly as in equation (
1
).

(2)

Since there is a degree of freedom as to how we write each fractional transformation, we can always normalize them so that one coefficient on
f_{k}
is a given constant (unless that coefficient is
0
to begin with). In the following we will normalize each matrix so that all
\gamma_{k}
are equal whenever
\gamma_{k}\neq 0
for all
k
.

(3)

Now if we make each
\displaystyle{f_{k}(z)=\frac{z}{1-z}+\epsilon^{2}}
, i.e.
\alpha_{k}=1-\epsilon^{2},\beta_{k}=\epsilon^{2},\gamma_{k}=-1,\delta_{k}=1
for all
k
then we obtain the following equations for
C_{k}
and
D_{k}
:

\displaystyle C_{k+1}

\displaystyle=(2-\epsilon^{2})C_{k}-C_{k-1},

\displaystyle C_{0}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle C_{1}

\displaystyle=-1;

\displaystyle D_{k+1}

\displaystyle=(2-\epsilon^{2})D_{k}-D_{k-1},

\displaystyle D_{0}

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle D_{1}

\displaystyle=1.

As noted in
[
9
]
, these are precisely the classical Chebyshev polynomials, linking
the iteration of these transformations to orthogonal polynomial theory.

We apply now Lemma
1
to the family of maps that was mentioned in the introduction.

Lemma 2
.

Let
(\epsilon_{k},\rho_{k})_{k\geq 1}
be sequences of complex numbers. Consider the
maps
\displaystyle{f_{k}(z)=\frac{\rho_{k}z}{1-z}+\epsilon_{k}^{2}}
.
Denote by

as above. Then:

\displaystyle F_{N}(z)=\frac{A_{N}z+B_{N}}{C_{N}z+D_{N}},

\displaystyle B_{k}

\displaystyle=r_{k}-r_{k+1};

\displaystyle C_{k}

\displaystyle=-q_{k};

\displaystyle D_{k}

\displaystyle=r_{k};

for:

\displaystyle q_{k+1}

\displaystyle=(1+\rho_{k}-\epsilon_{k}^{2})q_{k}-\rho_{k}q_{k-1},

\displaystyle q_{0}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle q_{1}

\displaystyle=1;

\displaystyle r_{k+1}

\displaystyle=(1+\rho_{k}-\epsilon_{k}^{2})r_{k}-\rho_{k}r_{k-1},

\displaystyle r_{0}

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle r_{1}

\displaystyle=1.

Proof.

This is the specialization of Lemma

to
\alpha_{k}=\rho_{k}
,
\beta_{k}=\epsilon_{k}^{2}
,
\gamma_{k}=-\rho_{k}
,

, with a straightforward computation.
∎

When all

are equal to

.

\displaystyle F_{N}(z)=\frac{A_{N}z}{C_{N}z+1},

\displaystyle C_{k}

\displaystyle=-q_{k};

where:

\displaystyle q_{0}

3.
Orthogonal Polynomials

We now study the orthogonal polynomials arising from the recursive sequences in Lemma
2
.
Recall the sequence:

\displaystyle q_{k+1}

\displaystyle=(1+\rho_{k}-\epsilon_{k}^{2})q_{k}-\rho_{k}q_{k-1},

\displaystyle q_{0}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle q_{1}

\displaystyle=1,

where the coefficients depend on the sequences
(\rho_{k},\epsilon_{k})
.
Our goal is to understand, under which conditions on
\rho_{k}
and
\epsilon_{k}
, the value
q_{N}
tends to
0
as
N
tends to infinity.

Past work
: The case
\rho_{k}\equiv 1

was studied in detail by Vivas
[
9
]
, who obtained conditions on perturbations of the form
\epsilon_{k}=\pi/N+\alpha(k)/N^{2}
to ensure
q_{N}=O(1/N)
. The main tool was to compare
q_{k}
with the Chebyshev polynomials: the sequence
U_{k}
, given by
U_{k+1}=(2-\epsilon^{2})U_{k}-U_{k-1}
.

When the
\rho_{k}
are allowed to vary on the unit circle or close to it, however, this approach no longer works. Comparing to the Chebyshev polynomials
U_{k}
in this case does not lead to useful bounds and do not reflect the structure of the new recurrence.

New sequence
: With that in mind, we compare each
q_{k}
with the recursive family arising in the autonomous rotation case (see Corollary
1
), that is where each
\rho_{k}=\rho
. Denote this sequence by
T_{k}
, defined by:

\displaystyle T_{k+1}

\displaystyle=(1+\rho)T_{k}-\rho T_{k-1},

\displaystyle T_{0}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle T_{1}

\displaystyle=1.

By induction, we can show

\displaystyle T_{k}=1+\rho+\rho^{2}+\dots+\rho^{k-1}=\frac{1-\rho^{k}}{1-\rho}.

In particular, when
\rho=e^{2\pi i/N}
we can rewrite:

(3)

\displaystyle T_{k}=e^{\frac{i\pi(k-1)}{N}}\frac{\sin(\pi k/N)}{\sin(\pi/N)}.

From this formula, we will frequently reference that
T_{N}=0
and
T_{N+1}=1
, as illustrated in Figure
1
, and that
|T_{k}|
is maximized when
k=\tfrac{N}{2}
, with the maximum approximately
N/\pi
when
N
is large.

Figure 1.
Graph of
T_{k}
and
|T_{k}|
for
N=10,000
.

As a first application we observe the following example:

Example 1
.

Let
\displaystyle{f_{k}(z)=\frac{\rho z}{1-z}}
for all
k\geq 1
, for
\rho=e^{2\pi i/N}
for a fixed
N
. Then by Corollary
1
and equation (
3
), the coefficients of
F_{N}
are given by

\displaystyle A_{N}=T_{N+1}-T_{N}=1,\ B_{N}=0,\ C_{N}=-T_{N}=0,\ D_{N}=1.

Thus,
F_{N}(z)=z
.

We use the orthogonal polynomials
{T_{k}}
to derive meaningful bounds for the sequence
{q_{k}}
. This is achieved through a formula that expresses the difference between
{q_{k}}
and
{T_{k}}
. We first state this result in a general form by considering any sequences of
a_{k},
and
b_{k}
in
\mathbb{C}
as additive perturbations to the coefficients in the recursive formula.

Lemma 3
.

Consider the recursive polynomials

\displaystyle T_{k+1}

\displaystyle=(1+\rho)T_{k}-\rho T_{k-1},

\displaystyle T_{0}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle T_{1}

\displaystyle=1;

(4)

\displaystyle q_{k+1}

\displaystyle=(1+\rho+a_{k})q_{k}-(\rho+b_{k})q_{k-1},

\displaystyle q_{0}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle q_{1}

\displaystyle=1.

Their difference is given by

\displaystyle q_{k}-T_{k}=\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}[a_{j}q_{j}-b_{j}q_{j-1}]T_{k-j},\quad k\geq 2.

Proof.

This follows by induction by subtracting the two relationships.
∎

Remark
.

In our applications of this lemma, the sequence
{b_{k}}
represents how far our multiplicative rotation deviates from the root of unity
e^{2\pi i/N}
; that is,
b_{k}=\rho_{k}-\rho
. The sequence
{a_{k}}
is defined by
a_{k}=b_{k}-\epsilon_{k}^{2}
.

In the case where
\epsilon_{k}\equiv 0
for all
k
, the expression for
a_{k}
simplifies to
a_{k}=b_{k}
. Otherwise, the sequence
{a_{k}}
incorporates an additional perturbation term
\epsilon_{k}^{2}
.

We will use the estimate above repeatedly in the following lemmas.

3.1.
Quadratic errors

Fix
\rho=e^{2\pi i/N}
. Our goal is to identify conditions on
a_{k}
and
b_{k}
that guarantee the errors

|q_{N}|=|q_{N}-T_{N}|\quad\text{and}\quad|q_{N+1}-1|=|q_{N+1}-T_{N+1}|

remain small.

Note that if we suppose
a_{k}=b_{k}
and

\rho+b_{k}=\rho+a_{k}=e^{2\pi i/(N+1)},

then
a_{k}=b_{k}=O(1/N^{2})
. We will refer to such a choice of
a_{k}
and
b_{k}
as quadratic or the resulting orthogonal polynomials as having quadratic errors. In this case, the recursion yields

q_{k+1}=(1+e^{2\pi i/(N+1)})q_{k}-e^{2\pi i/(N+1)}q_{k-1},

which allow us to explicitly compute the formula
q_{k}=e^{\frac{i\pi(k-1)}{N+1}}\frac{\sin(\pi k/(N+1))}{\sin(\pi/(N+1))}
and the values:

|q_{N}|=1\quad\text{and}\quad q_{N+1}=0.

In particular, the mere condition
b_{k},a_{k}=O(1/N^{2})
does not suffice to ensure that
|q_{N}-T_{N}|=o(1)
.

Nevertheless, when
a_{k}
and
b_{k}
are quadratic and comparable, we can still establish a uniform control on successive differences.

Lemma 4
.

Fix
N\in\mathbb{N}
and set
\rho=e^{2\pi i/N}
. Consider the recurrence (
4
)

\displaystyle q_{k+1}

\displaystyle=(1+\rho+a_{k})q_{k}-(\rho+b_{k})q_{k-1},

\displaystyle q_{0}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle q_{1}

\displaystyle=1,

for
1\leq k\leq N+1
.
Assume there is a constant
C>0
, independent of
N
, such that for all
k

a_{k}=\frac{c_{k}}{N^{2}}+E_{1,k},\qquad b_{k}=\frac{c_{k}}{N^{2}}+E_{2,k},

with
|c_{k}|\leq C
and
|E_{j,k}|\leq C/N^{4}
(
j=1,2
).

Then:

•

There exists a constant
\tilde{C}>0
, independent of both
k
and
N
, such that

|q_{k}-q_{k-1}|\leq\tilde{C}\quad\text{for all $1\leq k\leq N+1$}.

•

There exists a constant
C^{\prime}>0
, independent of
k
and
N
, such that

|q_{k}-T_{k}|\leq C^{\prime}\quad\text{for all $1\leq k\leq N+1$}.

Proof.

Let
\tilde{C}>2
satisfy
\frac{\max_{\ell}|c_{\ell}|}{N}\cdot\tilde{C}+\frac{4C}{N}<\tilde{C}
and work using induction.
First, note that
|a_{j}|<\epsilon
for some
0<\epsilon<1
.
For the base case, observe that
q_{0}=0
,
q_{1}=1
, and
q_{2}=1+\rho+a_{1}
from which it follows
|q_{0}|=0\leq 2N
,
|q_{1}|=1\leq 2N
, and
|q_{2}|\leq 1+\epsilon<2N
.
We also have

\displaystyle|q_{1}-q_{0}|

\displaystyle=1<\tilde{C},

\displaystyle|q_{2}-q_{1}|

\displaystyle=|\rho+a_{1}|\leq 1+\epsilon<\tilde{C}.

Step 1:
|q_{j}|\leq 2N
for all
1\leq j\leq k
implies
|q_{k+1}-q_{k}|\leq\tilde{C}
.
Now suppose
|q_{j}|\leq 2N
and
|q_{j}-q_{j-1}|\leq\tilde{C}
for all
1\leq j\leq k
.
Then using Lemma
3
and induction,

\displaystyle|q_{k+1}-q_{k}|

\displaystyle=|(q_{k+1}-T_{k+1})+(T_{k+1}-T_{k})+(T_{k}-q_{k})|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{j=1}^{k}[a_{j}q_{j}-b_{j}q_{j-1}]T_{k+1-j}+\rho^{k}-\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}[a_{j}q_{j}-b_{j}q_{j-1}]T_{k-j}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{j=1}^{k}[a_{j}q_{j}-b_{j}q_{j-1}]\rho^{k-j}+\rho^{k}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left[\frac{c_{j}}{N^{2}}(q_{j}-q_{j-1})\right]\rho^{k-j}+\sum_{j=1}^{k}[E_{1}q_{j}-E_{2}q_{j-1}]+\rho^{k}\right|

\displaystyle\leq N\cdot\frac{\max_{\ell}|c_{\ell}|}{N^{2}}\cdot\tilde{C}+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left(\frac{C}{N^{4}}\cdot|q_{j}|+\frac{C}{N^{4}}\cdot|q_{j-1}|\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\max_{\ell}|c_{\ell}|}{N}\cdot\tilde{C}+\frac{4C}{N^{2}}

\displaystyle<\tilde{C}.

Step 2:
Under the same assumptions,
|q_{j}|\leq 2N
and
|q_{j}-q_{j-1}|\leq\tilde{C}
for all
1\leq j\leq k
, and Lemma
3
,

\displaystyle|q_{k+1}|

\displaystyle=\left|T_{k+1}+\sum_{j=1}^{k}(a_{j}q_{j}-b_{j}q_{j-1})T_{k+1-j}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|T_{k+1}+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left[\frac{c_{j}}{N^{2}}(q_{j}-q_{j-1})+E_{1}q_{j}-E_{2}q_{j-1}\right]T_{k+1-j}\right|

\displaystyle\leq N+N\cdot\left[\frac{\max_{\ell}|c_{k}|}{N^{2}}\cdot\tilde{C}+\frac{2C}{N^{2}}\right]\cdot N

\displaystyle=N+\max_{\ell}|c_{\ell}|\tilde{C}+2C

\displaystyle<2N,

for sufficiently large
N
.

Thus we conclude
|q_{j}|\leq 2N
and
|q_{j}-q_{j-1}|\leq\tilde{C}
for all
1\leq j\leq k+1
.
By induction,
|q_{j}|\leq 2N
and
|q_{j}-q_{j-1}|\leq\tilde{C}
for all
1\leq j\leq N+1
.
∎

3.2.
Cubic errors

We use now the estimates above when the errors are cubic, i.e.
a_{k},b_{k}=O\left(\frac{1}{N^{3}}\right)
.

Proposition 1
.

Let
N\in\mathbb{N}
and set
\rho=e^{2\pi i/N}
.
Consider the recurrence in (
4
):

\displaystyle q_{k+1}

\displaystyle=(1+\rho+a_{k})q_{k}-(\rho+b_{k})q_{k-1},

\displaystyle q_{0}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle q_{1}

\displaystyle=1,

where for each
1\leq k\leq N+1
:

\displaystyle a_{k}=\frac{c_{k}}{N^{3}}+O\left(\frac{1}{N^{4}}\right),\qquad b_{k}=\frac{c_{k}}{N^{3}}+O\left(\frac{1}{N^{4}}\right)

with
c_{k}\in\mathbb{C}
and
|c_{k}|<C
for some constant
C>0
independent of
N
and
k
, and the
O(\tfrac{1}{N^{4}})
bounds uniform in
k
and
N
.
Then there exists a constant
C^{\prime}>0
, independent of
N
, such that

|q_{N}|\leq\frac{C^{\prime}}{N}\quad\text{and}\quad|q_{N+1}-1|\leq\frac{C^{\prime}}{N}.

Proof.

Using Lemma
3
we obtain

\displaystyle q_{k}-T_{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}[a_{j}q_{j}-b_{j}q_{j-1}]T_{k-j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\left[\left(\frac{c_{j}}{N^{3}}+{O\left(\frac{1}{N^{4}}\right)}\right)q_{j}-\left(\frac{c_{j}}{N^{3}}+{O\left(\frac{1}{N^{4}}\right)}\right)q_{j-1}\right]T_{k-j}

(5)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\frac{c_{j}}{N^{3}}(q_{j}-q_{j-1})T_{k-j}+\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\left[{O\left(\frac{1}{N^{4}}\right)}q_{j}-O\left(\frac{1}{N^{4}}\right)q_{j-1}\right]T_{k-j}.

Using Lemma
4
,
|q_{j}-q_{j-1}|\leq\tilde{C}
for some
\tilde{C}>0
and
|q_{j}|\leq 2N
for all
1\leq j\leq N+1
. Therefore for
k\leq N+1
equation (
5
) becomes

\displaystyle|q_{k}-T_{k}|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\frac{c_{j}}{N^{3}}(q_{j}-q_{j-1})T_{k-j}+\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\left[{O\left(\frac{1}{N^{4}}\right)}q_{j}-O\left(\frac{1}{N^{4}}\right)q_{j-1}\right]T_{k-j}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\left|\frac{c_{j}}{N^{3}}\right||q_{j}-q_{j-1}||T_{k-j}|+\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\left[O\left(\frac{1}{N^{4}}\right)|q_{j}|+O\left(\frac{1}{N^{4}}\right)|q_{j-1}|\right]|T_{k-j}|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\frac{|c_{j}|}{N^{3}}\tilde{C}N+\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}O\left(\frac{1}{N^{3}}\right)N

\displaystyle\leq O\left(\frac{1}{N}\right).

Since
T_{N}=0
and

we know

\displaystyle|q_{N}|=|q_{N}-T_{N}|=O\left(\frac{1}{N}\right)\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad|q_{N+1}-1|=|q_{N+1}-T_{N+1}|=O\left(\frac{1}{N}\right).

∎

3.3.
Explicit condition for convergence

If we want the error to be of quadratic order, we have to impose some conditions on it. We have the following:

Proposition 2
.

Let
N\in\mathbb{N}
and set
\rho=e^{2\pi i/N}
.
Consider the recurrence in (
4
):

\displaystyle q_{k+1}

\displaystyle=(1+\rho+a_{k})q_{k}-(\rho+b_{k})q_{k-1},

\displaystyle q_{0}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle q_{1}

\displaystyle=1,

where for each
1\leq k\leq N+1
:

\displaystyle{a}_{k}=\frac{c_{k}}{N^{2}}+O\left(\frac{1}{N^{4}}\right),\ {b}_{k}=\frac{c_{k}}{N^{2}}+O\left(\frac{1}{N^{4}}\right),

where all
c_{k}\subset\mathbb{C}
are uniformly bounded in
N
, and the
O(\tfrac{1}{N^{4}})
terms uniform in
k
and
N
.
If, in addition, there exists a constant
C>0
, independent of
N
, such that

(6)

\left|\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}a_{k}T_{k}\right|\leq\frac{C}{N}

then there exists
C^{\prime}>0
, independent of
N
, such that

|q_{N}|\leq\frac{C^{\prime}}{N}\quad\text{and}\quad|q_{N+1}-1|\leq\frac{C^{\prime}}{N}.

Proof.

Suppose
{a}_{k}=\frac{c_{k}}{N^{2}}+O\left(\frac{1}{N^{4}}\right)
and
{b}_{k}=\frac{c_{k}}{N^{2}}+O\left(\frac{1}{N^{4}}\right)
, so
b_{k}=a_{k}+O(1/N^{4})
.
Then observe that Lemma
3
gives

\displaystyle q_{k}-T_{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}[{a}_{j}q_{j}-{b}_{j}q_{j-1}]T_{k-j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}a_{j}(q_{j}-q_{j-1})T_{k-j}+\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}(a_{j}-b_{j})q_{j-1}T_{k-j}.

Taking the second summand in modulus, we have

\left|\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}(a_{j}-b_{j})q_{j-1}T_{k-j}\right|=\left|\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}O(1/N^{4})q_{j-1}T_{k-j}\right|\leq O(1/N).

Hence, in what follows, we work exclusively with the first summation
\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}a_{j}(q_{j}-q_{j-1})T_{k-j}
.

Let
k=N
and observe that (
3
) gives

\displaystyle T_{N-j}

\displaystyle=e^{i\pi(N-j-1)/N}\frac{\sin((N-j)\pi/N)}{\sin(\pi/N)}

\displaystyle=-e^{-i\pi(j+1)/N}\frac{\sin(j\pi/N)}{\sin(\pi/N)}.

We also have

\displaystyle q_{j}-q_{j-1}

\displaystyle=(\rho+{a}_{j-1})q_{j-1}-(\rho+{b}_{j-1})q_{j-2}=(\rho+{a}_{j-1})(q_{j-1}-q_{j-2})+O\left(\frac{1}{N^{4}}\right)q_{j-2}.

We first prove the estimate under the simplifying assumption that
a_{k}=b_{k}
. We then show that
b_{k}-a_{k}=O(1/N^{4})
will contribute a sufficient small order term. Since
a_{k}=O(1/N^{2})
we may choose a branch of logarithm for
\rho+a_{k}
. Then:

\displaystyle q_{j}-q_{j-1}

\displaystyle=\prod_{k=1}^{j-1}(\rho+{a}_{k})=\prod_{k=1}^{j-1}e^{(2\pi i/N+c_{k}/N^{2}+O(1/N^{4}))}

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}\frac{2\pi i}{N}+\frac{c_{k}}{N^{2}}+O\left(\frac{1}{N^{4}}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{\frac{2\pi i(j-1)}{N}+\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}c_{k}+O\left(\frac{1}{N^{3}}\right)\right\}.

Then

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}a_{j}(q_{j}-q_{j-1})T_{N-j}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}a_{j}e^{2\pi i(j-1)/N+\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}c_{k}/N^{2}+O(1/N^{3})}e^{-i\pi(j+1)/N}\frac{\sin(\pi j/N)}{\sin(\pi/N)}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}a_{j}e^{\pi i(j-1)/N}\frac{\sin(\pi j/N)}{\sin(\pi/N)}e^{\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}c_{k}/N^{2}+O(1/N^{3})}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}a_{j}T_{j}\left(1+\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}\frac{c_{k}}{N^{2}}+O\left(\frac{1}{N^{3}}\right)\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}a_{j}T_{j}\right|+O\left(\frac{1}{N}\right).

Consequently,

|q_{N}|=|q_{N}-T_{N}|\leq\left|\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}a_{j}T_{j}\right|+O\left(\frac{1}{N}\right),

and
|q_{N}|\leq\frac{C^{\prime}}{N}
if
\left|\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}a_{j}T_{j}\right|\leq\frac{C}{N}
.

An analogous argument can be made to show:

|q_{N+1}-1|=|q_{N+1}-T_{N+1}|\leq\frac{C^{\prime}}{N}.

Let us work on the general case now where
b_{k}-a_{k}=O(1/N^{4})
.
Then
q_{j}-q_{j-1}=(\rho+a_{j-1})(q_{j-1}-q_{j-2})+(b_{j-1}-a_{j-1})q_{j-2}
.
Let us write for simplicity
r_{j-1}:=(b_{j-1}-a_{j-1})q_{j-2}
.
From Lemma
4
and the conditions on
a_{j}
and
b_{j}
, we have that each
q_{j}
is of order at most
N
and
b_{j-1}-a_{j-1}=O(1/N^{4})
. Then each term
|r_{j}|<C/N^{3}
where
C
is uniform.
Hence,

\displaystyle q_{j}-q_{j-1}=\prod_{k=1}^{j-1}(\rho+{a}_{k})+R_{j-1}

where
R_{j-1}:=r_{j-1}+r_{j-2}(\rho+a_{j-1})+r_{j-3}(\rho+a_{j-2})(\rho+a_{j-1})+\cdots+r_{1}(\rho+a_{2})(\rho+a_{3})\cdots(\rho+a_{j-1})
.
Because
|\rho+a_{j-1}|=1+O(1/N^{2})
, any product of less than
N
terms of this form is of order
O(1)
.
Therefore, for each
1\leq i<j\leq N-1
we obtain:

\displaystyle|r_{j-i}\prod_{k=1}^{i-1}(\rho+a_{j-k})|<C/N^{3},

which implies that
|R_{j-1}|<C^{\prime}/N^{2}
.

Then

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}a_{j}(q_{j}-q_{j-1})T_{N-j}\right|\leq\left|\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}a_{j}T_{j}\right|+\left|\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}a_{j}R_{j-1}T_{N-j}\right|+O\left(\frac{1}{N}\right).

We can check that

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}a_{j}R_{j-1}T_{N-j}\right|<N\frac{C}{N^{2}}\frac{C^{\prime}}{N^{2}}N=O(1/N^{2}).

∎

In the following Corollaries, we provide examples of sequences
(c_{k})
that satisfy the summation condition (
6
).

Corollary 2
.

Let
(c_{k})\subset\mathbb{C}
be a bounded sequence such that

for

Proof.

Let
(c_{k})\subset\mathbb{C}
be bounded such that
c_{k}+c_{k+1}=O\left(\frac{1}{N}\right)
for
k
odd.
Expanding the expression (
6
)

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}a_{j}T_{j}\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\left|\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}c_{j}T_{j}\right|=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\left|\sum_{1\leq j\leq N-1,j\textrm{ odd }}(c_{j}T_{j}+c_{j+1}T_{j+1})\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\left|\sum_{1\leq j\leq N-1,j\textrm{ odd }}(c_{j}+c_{j+1})T_{j}+c_{j+1}(T_{j+1}-T_{j})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{N^{2}}\left(\sum_{1\leq j\leq N-1,j\textrm{ odd }}|c_{j}+c_{j+1}||T_{j}|+\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}|c_{j+1}||T_{j+1}-T_{j}|\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{N^{2}}\left(N\frac{C}{N}N+NC\right)=\frac{2C}{N}.

∎

Similarly we have:

Corollary 3
.

Let
(c_{k})\subset\mathbb{C}
such that

.
Then the coefficients

We can rewrite

Then the expression in (

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}C\rho^{k}\frac{\rho^{k}-1}{\rho-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{C}{N^{2}}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\frac{\rho^{2k}}{\rho-1}-\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\frac{\rho^{k}}{\rho-1}\right)=0.

∎

3.5.

We now consider the other recursive polynomial in Lemma

\displaystyle r_{k+1}

\displaystyle=(1+\rho+a_{k})r_{k}-(\rho+b_{k})r_{k-1},

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle=1.

As before, we write our polynomials in a more general form.

.

and

4.
Non-Autonomous Implosions

In this section we prove all our theorems related to non-autonomous parabolic implosion. We use the estimates obtained in Section
3
, as well as the results explained in Section
2
, that link coefficients of compositions of Möbius transformations.

We will separate our theorems into two categories. As before, starting from the map
\displaystyle{f(z)=\frac{z}{1-z}}
, we first investigate multiplicative perturbations of
f
. On our second set of theorems we focus on perturbations that are of both multiplicative and additive form.

Theorem A
.

Let

f_{k}(z):=\frac{\rho_{k}z}{1-z},

where
\rho_{k}=e^{2\pi i\theta_{k}}
and
\theta_{k}
satisfies any of the following conditions:

(1)

\displaystyle{\theta_{k}=\frac{1}{N}+O\left(\frac{1}{N^{3}}\right)}
; or

(2)

\displaystyle{\theta_{k}=\frac{1}{N}+\frac{c_{k}}{N^{2}}}
where
c_{k}+c_{k+1}=O\left(\frac{1}{N}\right)
for
k
odd; or

(3)

\displaystyle{\theta_{k}=\frac{1}{N}+\frac{c_{k}}{N^{2}}}
where
\displaystyle{c_{k}=Ce^{2\pi ik/N}}
for
C\in\mathbb{C}
.

Then:

\displaystyle q_{1}

5.
Difference with the Additive Condition

In the previous sections we analyzed the multiplicative setting, where compositions of maps of the form

f_{k}(z)=\frac{\rho_{k}z}{1-z}

exhibit delicate convergence properties depending on the sequence
\{\rho_{k}\}
.
We now contrast this with the additive case, which at first sight appears closely related but leads to very different asymptotic behavior.

Recall the following family of maps:

\displaystyle g_{k}(z)=\frac{z}{1-z}+\epsilon_{k}^{2}

where we fix
N
.
Maps of this type were considered in
[
9
]
where it is proven that:

g_{N}\circ g_{N-1}\circ\cdots\circ g_{1}\to\mathrm{Id},

under specific conditions on the sequence
\{\epsilon_{k}\}
.

Each individual
g_{k}
is analytically conjugated to a map
f_{k}
of the form

\displaystyle f_{k}(z)=\frac{\rho_{k}z}{1-z},

provided that the parameters satisfy

\sqrt{\rho_{k}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\rho_{k}}}=2-\epsilon_{k}^{2}.

However, the following theorem shows that, even when the maps are pairwise conjugated, the convergence of their compositions may behave very differently.

Theorem C
.

There exist sequences
\{\epsilon_{k}\}
and
\{\rho_{k}\}
for
1\leq k\leq N
, such that:

(1)

For each
k
, the maps
\displaystyle{f_{k}(z)=\frac{\rho_{k}z}{1-z}}
and
\displaystyle{g_{k}(z)=\frac{z}{1-z}+\epsilon_{k}^{2}}
are conjugated.

(2)

We have
g_{N}\circ g_{N-1}\circ\cdots\circ g_{1}
converges uniformly on compacts to the Identity.

(3)

The composition
f_{N}\circ f_{N-1}\circ\cdots\circ f_{1}(z)
does not converge pointwise to
z
for any
z\neq 0
.

This example illustrates that conjugacy at the level of individual maps does not imply comparable asymptotic behavior under composition.

Proof.

As in the one variable situation, the relationship for the conjugacy is satisfied when:

\rho_{k}=\exp(2i\theta_{k})\textrm{ and }\epsilon_{k}=2\sin(\theta_{k}/2).

Assume for simplicity that
N=2m
. We choose:
\theta_{k}=\frac{\pi}{N-1}
for
1\leq k\leq m
and
\theta_{k}=\frac{\pi}{N+1}
for
m+1\leq k\leq 2m=N
.

In
[
9
]
, it is proven that: if
\epsilon_{k}=\frac{\pi}{N}+\frac{\alpha_{k}}{N^{2}}
such that
\alpha_{k}+\alpha_{N-k}=O\left(1/N\right)
, then (
2
) holds. It is clear that this condition is satisfied for the choice of
\epsilon_{k}
.
In fact, the choice of
\epsilon_{k}
implies that

\alpha_{k}=\begin{cases}\pi+O(1/N)&\text{if $1\leq k\leq m$}\\
-\pi+O(1/N)&\text{if $m+1\leq k\leq 2m.$}\end{cases}

Therefore
\alpha_{k}+\alpha_{N-k}=O(1/N)
and (
2
) holds.

We now prove (
3
). For simplicity let us rename
s=\exp(2i\pi/(N-1))
and
t=\exp(2i\pi/(N+1))
. We use the formula
q_{k+1}=(1+\rho_{k})q_{k}-\rho_{k}q_{k-1}
for
q_{N}
and see that

\displaystyle q_{N}

\displaystyle=1+s+s^{2}+\ldots+s^{m-1}+s^{m}\left(1+t+t^{2}+\ldots+t^{m-1}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{s^{m}-1}{s-1}+s^{m}\frac{t^{m}-1}{t-1}.

For the first term, we have the following:

\displaystyle\frac{s^{m}-1}{s-1}=\frac{e^{iN\pi/(N-1)}-1}{e^{2i\pi/(N-1)}-1}=\frac{i}{\pi}(N-1)+\frac{1}{2}+O(1/N).

Likewise:

\displaystyle\frac{t^{m}-1}{t-1}=\frac{e^{iN\pi/(N+1)}-1}{e^{2i\pi/(N+1)}-1}=\frac{i}{\pi}(N+1)+\frac{3}{2}+O(1/N).

Putting it all together, along with the fact that
s^{m}=-1-\frac{i\pi}{N}+O(1/N^{2})
:

\displaystyle q_{N}=-\frac{2i}{\pi}+O(1/N).

Consequently,

\displaystyle|q_{N}-T_{N}|

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\pi}.

for
N
large enough.
Thus, we do not get convergence to the identity.
∎

6.
Bifurcations for Skew-Products

A standard way in which non-autonomous parabolic phenomena arise in higher dimension is through
skew-product
maps

F(z,w)=(f_{w}(z),g(w)),

where the base dynamics
w\mapsto g(w)
generates a parameter sequence

w_{0},w_{1},\dots,w_{N-1}

and hence a non-autonomous composition in the fiber,

f_{w_{N-1}}\circ\cdots\circ f_{w_{1}}\circ f_{w_{0}}.

In the parabolic context, one is interested in regimes where the fiber maps are small perturbations of the model parabolic Möbius map

z\mapsto\frac{z}{1-z}

and where the induced non-autonomous composition exhibits a
bifurcation-like
effect as the base point
w_{0}=w_{0}(N)\to 0
and the number of iterates
N\to\infty
.

The results of Section
4
provide a flexible mechanism for producing such examples: if along the base orbit one can write the fiber maps in the form

f_{k}(z)=\rho_{k}\,\frac{z}{1-z}+\epsilon_{k}^{2},\qquad\rho_{k}=e^{2\pi i\theta_{k}},

with
\theta_{k}=\frac{1}{N}+O(N^{-2})
(or
\theta_{k}=0
in the additive scaling regime) and
\epsilon_{k}=O(N^{-2})
in one of the admissible patterns from Theorems
A
–
B
, then the induced
N
–step fiber composition converges to the identity.
In particular, in each of the examples below, we choose a base map
g
and an initial parameter
w_{0}=w_{0}(N)
so that the resulting sequence
\{\theta_{k},\epsilon_{k}\}
falls under one of our theorems, yielding

F^{N}(z,w_{0})\longrightarrow(z,0)\text{ as }N\to\infty,

uniformly on compact subsets in the
z
–variable.

Example 2

(Constant base parameter)
.

Consider

F(z,w)=\left(e^{2\pi iw}\frac{z}{1-z},\,w\right).

Then
F^{N}(z,w_{0})\to(z,0)
for
w_{0}=\frac{1}{N}
.

Proof.

If
w_{0}=\frac{1}{N}
, then the base coordinate is constant:
w_{k}=w_{0}
for all
k
. Hence the fiber map at each step is

f(z)=e^{2\pi i/N}\frac{z}{1-z}.

Therefore

F^{N}\!\left(z,\frac{1}{N}\right)=\left(f^{\circ N}(z),\frac{1}{N}\right)=\left(z,\frac{1}{N}\right)

by Example
1
, and the claim follows.
∎

Example 3

(Alternating multiplicative perturbations)
.

Consider

H(z,w)=\left(e^{2\pi i(\frac{1}{N}+w)}\frac{z}{1-z},\,-w\right).

Then
H^{N}(z,w_{0})\to(z,0)
for
w_{0}=-\frac{1}{N^{2}}
.

Proof.

Set
w_{0}=-\frac{1}{N^{2}}
and define
c_{k}\in\{\pm 1\}
by

c_{k}=\begin{cases}1,&k\ \text{odd},\\
-1,&k\ \text{even}.\end{cases}

Then
w_{k}=c_{k}w_{0}
and the
k
th fiber map is

f_{k}(z)=e^{2\pi i\theta_{k}}\frac{z}{1-z},\qquad\theta_{k}=\frac{1}{N}+w_{k}=\frac{1}{N}+\frac{c_{k}}{N^{2}}.

Since
c_{k}+c_{k+1}=0
for all
k
, Theorem
A
(2) applies and gives

f_{N}\circ\cdots\circ f_{1}\to\mathrm{Id}.

Also
w_{N}=(-1)^{N}w_{0}\to 0
. Hence
H^{N}(z,w_{0})\to(z,0)
.
∎

Example 4

(Rotating multiplicative perturbations)
.

Consider

G(z,w)=\left(e^{2\pi i(\frac{1}{N}+w)}\frac{z}{1-z},\,e^{2\pi i/N}\,w\right).

Then
G^{N}(z,w_{0})\to(z,0)
for
w_{0}=\frac{e^{2\pi i/N}}{N^{2}}
.

Proof.

With
w_{0}=\frac{e^{2\pi i/N}}{N^{2}}
we have

w_{k}=\frac{e^{2\pi i(k+1)/N}}{N^{2}}.

Define

\theta_{k}=\frac{1}{N}+\frac{e^{2\pi ik/N}}{N^{2}},\qquad f_{k}(z)=e^{2\pi i\theta_{k}}\frac{z}{1-z}.

Then
\theta_{k}=\frac{1}{N}+w_{k-1}
, so the
k
th fiber map along the orbit is precisely
f_{k}
. By Theorem
A
(3),

f_{N}\circ\cdots\circ f_{1}\to\mathrm{Id}.

Moreover
|w_{N}|=1/N^{2}\to 0
, so
G^{N}(z,w_{0})\to(z,0)
.
∎

Example 5

(Including additive perturbations: constant base)
.

Consider

F_{1}(z,w)=\left(e^{2\pi iw}\frac{z}{1-z}+w^{4},\,w\right).

Then
F_{1}^{N}(z,w_{0})\to(z,0)
for
w_{0}=\frac{1}{N}
.

Proof.

If
w_{0}=\frac{1}{N}
, then
w_{k}=w_{0}
for all
k
, and each fiber map equals

f(z)=\rho\,\frac{z}{1-z}+\epsilon^{2},\qquad\rho=e^{2\pi i/N},\qquad\epsilon^{2}=w_{0}^{4}=\frac{1}{N^{4}}.

Thus
\theta=\frac{1}{N}
and
\epsilon=O(N^{-2})
, so Theorem
B
(1) gives

f^{\circ N}\to\mathrm{Id}.

Hence

F_{1}^{N}(z,w_{0})=\bigl(f^{\circ N}(z),w_{0}\bigr)\to(z,0).

∎

Example 6

(Including additive perturbations: alternating base)
.

Consider

G_{1}(z,w)=\left(e^{2\pi i(\frac{1}{N}+w)}\frac{z}{1-z}+w^{2},\,-w\right).

Then
G_{1}^{N}(z,w_{0})\to(z,0)
for
w_{0}=-\frac{1}{N^{2}}
.

Proof.

Let
w_{0}=-\frac{1}{N^{2}}
and define
c_{k}
as in Example
3
, so
w_{k}=c_{k}w_{0}
and
w_{k}^{2}\equiv\frac{1}{N^{4}}
. The induced fiber maps are

f_{k}(z)=e^{2\pi i\theta_{k}}\frac{z}{1-z}+\epsilon_{k}^{2},\qquad\theta_{k}=\frac{1}{N}+w_{k}=\frac{1}{N}+\frac{c_{k}}{N^{2}},\qquad\epsilon_{k}^{2}=w_{k}^{2}=\frac{1}{N^{4}}.

Since
c_{k}+c_{k+1}=0
and
\epsilon_{k}=O(N^{-2})
, Theorem
B
(2) applies and yields

f_{N}\circ\cdots\circ f_{1}\to\mathrm{Id}.

Also
w_{N}=(-1)^{N}w_{0}\to 0
, hence
G_{1}^{N}(z,w_{0})\to(z,0)
.
∎

The examples above illustrate how the abstract non-autonomous convergence results proved in this paper naturally arise in the study of skew-product dynamics.
In each case, the dynamics of the base variable
w
produces a controlled drift through a parabolic regime in the fiber, leading to a non-autonomous composition whose asymptotics are governed by Theorems
A
and
B
.

While these examples do not by themselves yield global dynamical consequences such as the existence of wandering Fatou components, they capture the local bifurcation mechanisms that underpin such constructions in higher dimensional complex dynamics.

This perspective complements the work of Astorg and collaborators
[
3
,
4
]
, where similar skew-product configurations arise implicitly, and suggests that orthogonal polynomial techniques may offer a useful analytic framework for studying more intricate skew-product bifurcations, including random or weakly correlated perturbative regimes.

We expect that these methods can be further adapted to settings where the base dynamics is no longer periodic or deterministic, potentially leading to new examples of non-autonomous parabolic phenomena in several complex variables.

7.
Random Compositions

In this section, we return to the purely additive setting studied by Vivas
[
9
]
, where the fiber maps are given by

f_{k}(z)\coloneqq\frac{z}{1-z}+\epsilon_{k}^{2},

but we now allow the perturbation parameters
\{\epsilon_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
to be
random
.
Our goal is to show that, while deterministic perturbations at the critical scale
1/N^{2}
require delicate arithmetic cancellations, introducing randomness allows one to recover convergence under significantly weaker assumptions.

Throughout this section we restrict to the autonomous multiplicative case
\rho_{k}\equiv 1
, so that the dynamics is governed entirely by additive perturbations.
In this setting the
N
th composition

F_{N}:=f_{N}\circ f_{N-1}\circ\cdots\circ f_{1}

can be expressed as a Möbius transformation whose coefficients are determined by two recursive sequences
\{q_{k}\}
and
\{r_{k}\}
.
This representation, introduced in
[
9
]
, allows us to reduce the problem of convergence of
F_{N}
to quantitative estimates on these sequences.

When
\rho_{k}\equiv 1
, we have
\rho_{k}=\rho+b_{k}\equiv 1
for all
k
, and hence

\rho+a_{k}=1-\epsilon_{k}^{2}
.
Specializing Lemma
2
to this case recovers Lemma 1 of
[
9
]
, which shows that

F_{N}:=f_{N}\circ f_{N-1}\circ\cdots\circ f_{1}

is a Möbius transformation with coefficients determined by the sequences
\{q_{k}\}
and
\{r_{k}\}
defined by

\displaystyle q_{k+1}

\displaystyle=(2-\epsilon_{k}^{2})q_{k}-q_{k-1},

\displaystyle q_{0}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle q_{1}

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle r_{k+1}

\displaystyle=(2-\epsilon_{k}^{2})r_{k}-r_{k-1},

\displaystyle r_{0}

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle r_{1}

\displaystyle=1.

The relationship between
\{q_{k}\}
and
\{r_{k}\}
is given by Lemma
5
.
In particular, when
\rho_{k}\equiv 1
, this reduces to Lemma 6 of
[
9
]
.

Lemma 6
.

If
\rho_{k}\equiv 1
, then

r_{k+1}=q_{k}-s_{k-1},

where the auxiliary sequence
\{s_{k}\}
is defined by
s_{0}=0
,
s_{1}=1
, and

s_{k+1}=(2-\epsilon_{k+1}^{2})s_{k}-s_{k-1}.

The key idea is to compare the random recursion satisfied by
q_{k}
with the deterministic Chebyshev recursion, and to show that random fluctuations remain sufficiently small with overwhelming probability.

We compare the sequence
\{q_{k}\}
with the Chebyshev polynomials of the second kind
\{U_{k}\}
.

Lemma 7

(
[
9
, Lemma 4]
)
.

Let

\displaystyle q_{k+1}

\displaystyle=(x+d_{k})q_{k}-q_{k-1},

\displaystyle q_{0}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle q_{1}

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle U_{k+1}

\displaystyle=xU_{k}-U_{k-1},

\displaystyle U_{0}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle U_{1}

\displaystyle=1.

Let
x=2\cos\theta
.
If there exist
\epsilon>0
and
m\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}|d_{j}q_{j}|\leq\epsilon\sin\theta,

then
|q_{n}-U_{n}|\leq\epsilon
for all
1\leq n\leq m
.

The proof begins with the identity

\sin\theta\,(q_{n}-U_{n})=-\textrm{Im}\left(\delta_{n}e^{-in\theta}\right),\qquad\delta_{n}:=\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}d_{j}q_{j}e^{ij\theta}.

We now assume that the coefficients
d_{k}
are random.
Throughout this section we fix
\delta>0
and write

(7)

\displaystyle d_{k}=\frac{\xi_{k}}{N^{2+\delta}},

where
\xi_{k}
are any independent, bounded, mean-zero random variables.

Lemma 8
.

Let

\delta_{n}:=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}d_{k}\,q_{k}\,e^{ik\theta},\qquad n\geq 1.

Then
\{\delta_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
is a martingale with respect to the filtration

\mathcal{F}_{n}=\sigma(\xi_{0},\dots,\xi_{n-1})
.

Proof.

Since
q_{k}
depends only on
\xi_{0},\dots,\xi_{k-1}
, the variable
\delta_{n}
is
\mathcal{F}_{n}
-measurable.
Moreover,

\mathbb{E}|\delta_{n}|\leq\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}(|d_{k}||q_{k}|)<\infty,

because the
\xi_{k}
are bounded.

Finally,

\delta_{n}=\delta_{n-1}+d_{n-1}q_{n-1}e^{i(n-1)\theta}.

Conditioning on
\mathcal{F}_{n-1}
and using
\mathbb{E}(\xi_{n-1})=0
gives

\mathbb{E}(\delta_{n}\mid\mathcal{F}_{n-1})=\delta_{n-1},

so
\{\delta_{n}\}
is a martingale.
∎

Recall from Lemma 5 of
[
9
]
that the condition on
d_{k}
immediately gives us the bound
q_{k}
bounded by
N
for all
1\leq k\leq N
with probability one. Therefore we have the following:

Lemma 9
.

Assume
|\xi_{k}|\leq M
almost surely and
|q_{k}|\leq N
with probability one, for
k\leq n
.
Then for any
\lambda_{n}>0
,

\mathbb{P}(|\delta_{n}|\geq\lambda_{n})\leq\exp\!\left(-\frac{\lambda_{n}^{2}N^{2(1+\delta)}}{2M^{2}n}\right).

Consequently,

\mathbb{P}\bigl(|q_{n}-U_{n}|\geq\tfrac{\lambda_{n}}{\sin\theta}\bigr)\leq\exp\!\left(-\frac{\lambda_{n}^{2}N^{2(1+\delta)}}{2M^{2}n}\right).

Proof.

Since

|\delta_{n}-\delta_{n-1}|=|d_{n-1}q_{n-1}|\leq\frac{M}{N^{1+\delta}},

Azuma’s inequality (see for instance
[
1
]
) yields the stated bound.
The estimate for
|q_{n}-U_{n}|
follows from the identity above.
∎

Proposition 5
.

Let
\theta=\pi/N
,
x=2\cos\theta
.
With probability at least
1-\exp(-N^{\delta})
,

\displaystyle|q_{N-1}-1|

\displaystyle=O\!\left(N^{-\frac{1}{2}(1+\delta)}\right),

\displaystyle|q_{N}|

\displaystyle=O\!\left(N^{-\frac{1}{2}(1+\delta)}\right),

\displaystyle|q_{N+1}+1|

\displaystyle=O\!\left(N^{-\frac{1}{2}(1+\delta)}\right),

\displaystyle|s_{N-2}-2\cos\theta|

\displaystyle=O\!\left(N^{-\frac{1}{2}(1+\delta)}\right),

\displaystyle|s_{N-1}-1|

\displaystyle=O\!\left(N^{-\frac{1}{2}(1+\delta)}\right).

Proof.

Let
\theta=\pi/N
and
x=2\cos\theta
. We apply Lemma
9
with the choice

\lambda_{n}:=\sqrt{2n}\,N^{-(3/2+\delta/2)}.

Then for each
1\leq n\leq N+1
, Lemma
9
gives

\mathbb{P}\bigl(|\delta_{n}|\geq\lambda_{n}\bigr)\leq\exp\!\left(-c\,\frac{\lambda_{n}^{2}N^{2(1+\delta)}}{n}\right)=\exp\!\left(-c\,N^{\delta}\right),

for some constant
c>0
depending only on the uniform bound on
|\xi_{k}|
.

Using the identity
\sin\theta\,(q_{n}-U_{n})=-\textrm{Im}(\delta_{n}e^{-in\theta})
, we obtain

|q_{n}-U_{n}|\leq\frac{|\delta_{n}|}{\sin\theta}.

Since
\sin(\pi/N)\sim 1/N
, there is a constant
C>0
such that

|q_{n}-U_{n}|\leq CN\,|\delta_{n}|.

Therefore, on the event
\{|\delta_{n}|\leq\lambda_{n}\}
we have

|q_{n}-U_{n}|\leq CN\lambda_{n}\leq C\sqrt{2n}\,N^{-(1/2+\delta/2)}=O\!\left(N^{-\frac{1}{2}(1+\delta)}\right),\qquad 1\leq n\leq N+1.

By a union bound over
1\leq n\leq N+1
,

\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\max_{1\leq n\leq N+1}|\delta_{n}|>\lambda_{n}\Bigr)\leq(N+1)\exp(-cN^{\delta})\leq\exp(-c^{\prime}N^{\delta})

for some
c^{\prime}>0
and all
N
large. Hence, with probability at least

1-\exp(-c^{\prime}N^{\delta})
we have simultaneously for all
1\leq n\leq N+1
,

|q_{n}-U_{n}|=O\!\left(N^{-\frac{1}{2}(1+\delta)}\right).

Now use the explicit values of
U_{n}
at
\theta=\pi/N
:

U_{N-1}(2\cos(\pi/N))=\frac{\sin((N-1)\pi/N)}{\sin(\pi/N)}=1,\quad U_{N}(2\cos(\pi/N))=\frac{\sin(\pi)}{\sin(\pi/N)}=0,

U_{N+1}(2\cos(\pi/N))=\frac{\sin((N+1)\pi/N)}{\sin(\pi/N)}=-1.

This yields the first three estimates:

|q_{N-1}-1|=O\!\left(N^{-\frac{1}{2}(1+\delta)}\right),\quad|q_{N}|=O\!\left(N^{-\frac{1}{2}(1+\delta)}\right),\quad|q_{N+1}+1|=O\!\left(N^{-\frac{1}{2}(1+\delta)}\right).

Finally, the same argument applies to the shifted sequence
s_{n}
(as noted in the remark preceding Lemma
8
), giving

|s_{n}-U_{n}|=O\!\left(N^{-\frac{1}{2}(1+\delta)}\right)

uniformly for
n\leq N+1
on the same high-probability event. In particular,

|s_{N-2}-U_{N-2}|=O\!\left(N^{-\frac{1}{2}(1+\delta)}\right),\qquad|s_{N-1}-U_{N-1}|=O\!\left(N^{-\frac{1}{2}(1+\delta)}\right).

Since

U_{N-2}(2\cos(\pi/N))=\frac{\sin((N-2)\pi/N)}{\sin(\pi/N)}=\frac{\sin(2\pi/N)}{\sin(\pi/N)}=2\cos(\pi/N),

and
U_{N-1}(2\cos(\pi/N))=1
, we obtain

|s_{N-2}-2\cos(\theta)|=O\!\left(N^{-\frac{1}{2}(1+\delta)}\right),\qquad|s_{N-1}-1|=O\!\left(N^{-\frac{1}{2}(1+\delta)}\right),

as claimed.
∎

We are ready now to prove our main theorem in this section.

Theorem D
.

For fixed
N
, let
\{\eta_{k}\}_{k=1}^{N+1}
be independent, bounded, mean-zero random variables, and set

\epsilon_{k}=\frac{\pi}{N}+\frac{\eta_{k}}{N^{1+\delta}}\quad(\delta>0),\qquad f_{k}(z)=\frac{z}{1-z}+\epsilon_{k}^{2}.

Then

f_{N}\circ f_{N-1}\circ\cdots\circ f_{1}\longrightarrow\mathrm{Id}

as
N\to\infty
, uniformly on compact subsets of
\mathbb{C}
, with probability one.

Proof.

For
\epsilon_{k}
as above, we have:

2-\epsilon_{k}^{2}=2-\frac{\pi^{2}}{N^{2}}-\frac{2\pi\eta_{k}}{N^{2+\delta}}-\frac{\eta_{k}^{2}}{N^{2+2\delta}}.

For
x=2\cos(\pi/N)
, we have

x+d_{k}=2-\epsilon_{k}^{2}
when

d_{k}=-\frac{2\pi\eta_{k}}{N^{2+\delta}}-\frac{\eta_{k}^{2}}{N^{2+2\delta}}+O(1/N^{4}).

We see that this satisfies conditions of equation
7
.
For the composition of the linear transformations, we use the
Lemma
2

F_{N}(z)=\frac{A_{N}z+B_{N}}{C_{N}z+D_{N}},

with

A_{N}=q_{N+1}-q_{N},\quad B_{N}=r_{N}-r_{N+1},\quad C_{N}=-q_{N},\quad D_{N}=r_{N}.

Using Lemma
6
and Proposition
5
, we obtain

A_{N}\to-1,\quad B_{N}\to 0,\quad C_{N}\to 0,\quad D_{N}\to-1

almost surely.
Thus
F_{N}\to\mathrm{Id}
uniformly on compact sets.

Since the failure probabilities are summable, Borel–Cantelli implies that these bounds hold for all sufficiently large
N
almost surely.
∎

In the random setting, independence and zero mean suffice to ensure almost sure convergence. This suggests that non-autonomous parabolic implosion may be robust under stochastic perturbations, including in higher-dimensional settings.
