Prevalence of arrhythmias and associated factors among COVID-19 patients in the intensive care unit at COVID-19 treatment centers, Addis Ababa, Ethiopia, 2022. A cross-sectional study
Authors: Gobu Letemu, Zuriyash Mengistu, Emebet Berhane WoldeMariam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCoronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19), predominantly a respiratory illness, has been a global human threat that causes a multisystem disease, specifically cardiac arrhythmia commonly occurs. Currently, studies about the prevalence of Arrhythmias among COVID-19 patients are limited in low-income countries, including Ethiopia.ObjectiveTo assess the prevalence of arrhythmias and associated factors among COVID-19 patients in the ICU.MethodAn institution-based cross-sectional study design was employed to assess the prevalence of arrhythmias. A study was conducted on a random selected 422 COVID-19 adult patients in the ICU treatment center. Addis Ababa. Ulla Walfridsson’s “Arrhythmia-Specific Checklist " was used to collect data. The study was conducted from February 15 to April 15, 2022. Data were described using mean, median, range, and frequency. Bivariate and multivariable analyses were computed. Patient confidentiality is maintained.ResultFrom a total of 422 COVID-19 participants, the prevalence of cardiac arrhythmias was 193(45.7% ; 95% CI: 40.90–50.60).There were 79(18.7%) deaths. The study found that 18.7% of the total COVID-19 patients died. Electrolyte imbalance, high serum chloride, and high Calcium levels were significantly associated with cardiac arrhythmias. Introduction

COVID-19 is a highly contagious and pro-arrhythmogenic disease that threatens people in all countries around the world [1]. Studies reported that over 433 million people have been infected globally, with over 5.9 million deaths by the end of 2022 [2, 3]. Particularly, at the regional level, the Western Pacific Region reported unique findings where the number of new weekly cases and deaths increased by 32% and 22%, respectively, including Africa [2, 3]. A systematic review and meta-analysis found that the national pooled prevalence of COVID-19 infection in the Ethiopian population was 8.83% [2].

Infection with COVID-19 has been associated with many pulmonary and extrapulmonary manifestations. Mainly, arrhythmias have been described throughout the pandemic in COVID-19 patients [2, 3]. Arrhythmias in COVID-19 patients may manifest in viral myocarditis through arrhythmia mechanisms, including ischemia caused by endothelial dysfunction, electrolyte imbalance due to the destruction of the myocardial membrane of the myocardium, and reduced expression of gap proteins in the myocardium [4, 5].

Arrhythmia in myocarditis can also be associated with angiotensin II receptors. There is cardiovascular fibrosis and intensified inflammatory cytokines, especially tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and interleukin (IL) [6–9]. The study reported that, besides the direct cardiac effects, systemic inflammation might additionally predispose to long QT syndrome/TdP as a result of indirect mechanisms [6, 7]. Foremost, inflammatory cytokines can induce cardiac sympathetic system hyperactivation, which in turn represents a well-recognized trigger for life-threatening arrhythmic events. Second, IL-6 inhibits cytochrome, particularly CYP3A4, thereby increasing the bioavailability of several medications, including QT-prolonging drugs [10].

A systematic review and meta-analysis study conducted in China in 2020 reported a pooled prevalence of arrhythmia that occurred in 30.1% of COVID-19 patients [11]. A study in Iran reported that arrhythmia was 61.8% of COVID-19 patients [12]. A study revealed that 44% of COVID-19 patients in the ICU developed arrhythmia [13], and another reported that 37% [14] and 60% had cardiac arrhythmia [15]. A study in Italy identified that 24.5% of COVID-19 patients had a history of atrial fibrillation (AF) [16]. AF is quite common among hospitalized older COVID-19 patients (≥ 60 years of age and men, 65% [17–24].

Factors like age, arterial hypertension, history of myocardial infarction, renal dysfunction, and a higher value of D-dimer acted as possible cofactors for the new onset of AF in patients with COVID-19 [25, 26]. A study reported that the presence of chronic renal disease in COVID-19 patients may synergize with pre-existing cardiovascular risk factors [27]. Arrhythmias in COVID-19 may occur as a result of hypoxia [28–31]. In Ethiopia, eight out of ten hospitalized COVID-19 patients recovered and were discharged from inpatient care [32]. Another study revealed African patients were hospitalized with more severe COVID-19 cases and had poorer outcomes [33].

There is a scarcity of research conducted in our country regarding the prevalence of cardiac arrhythmias and related factors. Therefore, this study was to investigate the prevalence of arrhythmias that co-exist with COVID-19 and factors related to arrhythmias.

Methods and materials

Study setting, study design, and period

The study was conducted in Addis Ababa City, the capital city of Ethiopia. There are six federal and three city administration COVID-19 treatment centers. The study used treatment centers that had COVID-19 treatment centers and COVID-19 ICU admissions as the study area. The federal treatment centers include Bole Millennium, St. Peter Specialized Hospital, St. Paul Specialized Hospital, Alert Specialized Hospital, Tikur Anbessa Hospital, and Eka Kotebe General Hospital. The city administration hospital includes Tirunesh Beijing, Yekatit 12 Hospital, and Zewditu Memorial Hospital.

Among these, a total of nine COVID-19 treatment centers, including St. Peter Specialized Hospital, St. Paul’s Specialized Hospital, Zewditu Memorial Hospital, and Millennium Hall, were selected through the lottery method.

An institution-based cross-sectional study design was employed to assess the prevalence of arrhythmias among COVID-19 patients in the ICU.

The study was conducted from February 15 to April 15, 2022.

The COVID-19 patients admitted to the ICU from January 2021 to April 2022 were included.

Source and study participants

The population sources for this study were all intensive care unit (ICU) COVID-19-positive patients’ charts in COVID-19 treatment centers.

The study population was the ICU-admitted COVID-19-positive patients’ charts from whom the study subjects were obtained at selected COVID-19 treatment centers.

The study unit was each actual study participant’s chart of the intensive care unit COVID-19 patient’s chart, which was drawn from the study population.

Inclusion criteria

In this study, COVID-19 patients proposed for ICU admission were consecutively analyzed. A COVID-19 patient’s chart that had the data of admitted patients with ages greater than or equal to 16 years confirmed a positive COVID-19 test by polymerase chain reaction testing of a nasopharyngeal or oropharyngeal sample. Patients presenting with an oxygen peripheral saturation (SpO₂) lower than 85% and/or dyspnea and/or mental confusion were eligible for ICU admission. Electrocardiogram (ECG) and electrolyte results were included.

We excluded patients with Do-Not-Resuscitate (DNR) orders, those under isolation precautions that limited cardiac monitoring, and individuals who were asymptomatic or had only mild symptoms. These exclusions were applied to reduce heterogeneity, ensure reliable arrhythmia detection, and maintain comparability of outcomes among ICU-admitted patients with clinically significant COVID-19.

Sample size determination and sampling procedure

The required sample size of eligible participants for the study is determined by using a single population proportion formula. n = (Za/2)²*p (1-p)/d².

Where: n = the desired sample size. In the previous study, the prevalence of arrhythmia in ICU patients was 48% [34].

n = 422 COVID-19 patients’ chart.

Using a proportional size allocation formula, a total of 422 samples were distributed to the randomly selected list of four COVID-19 centers in Addis Ababa, including 42 charts from St. Peter’s Specialized Hospital, 148 charts from St. Paul’s Specialized Hospital, 21 charts from Zewditu Memorial Hospital, and 211 charts from Millennium Hall. Finally, we used a systematic random sampling technique by creating a numerical order list of the ICU-admitted COVID-19-positive patients’ charts. We divide the total number of charts by a calculated sample size of 422. The computed interval would be 4. A simple random sampling was used to start at a randomly chosen point of the first four lists of charts. Then, we selected every 4th chart interval until we had 422 charts.

Dependent variable

Arrhythmias

Arrhythmia is a disturbance of the normal heart rhythm in which the heart is beating too fast, too slow, or irregularly compared to the normal sinus rhythm. This is supposed to be due to alteration in impulse generation or disturbance in impulse conduction or a combination of all these factors.

The prevalence of arrhythmia refers to the proportion of COVID-19 patients who had abnormal arrhythmia during their ICU stay period of time as recorded in the document. The type of arrhythmia involved in this study is the following.

The prevalence of arrhythmia was identified from the patient’s chart, which has a confirmed diagnosis using an electrocardiogram (ECG) result with the interpretation of cardiologists. The standard ECG was done for all the ICU-admitted COVID-19 patients.

Independent variables

Age, gender, electrolytes, hypertension, heart disease, renal disease, diabetes mellitus, and hypoxia.

Measurement tools

A baseline ECG was conducted upon admission, which was interpreted by the expert physician on duty. The ECG findings can be reviewed with a cardiologist as needed. An interpretive ECG recorder diagnoses arrhythmias by analyzing the morphology of the ECG waveform in specific leads. The interpretation is validated through established standard ECG guidelines in combination with the patient’s clinical context, symptoms, and medical history to help differentiate between normal variations and pathological findings. Based on the patient’s clinical condition, repeated ECGs were performed. In complex cases, especially when there was uncertainty about interpreting an ECG finding, a cardiologist reviewed the tracing to confirm the diagnosis.

To identify the cause of arrhythmia in patients, aside from Covid-19, some measures have been taken, including patient history, clinical condition, fluid and electrolyte test, and echocardiogram to examine the structure and function of the heart for anomalies.

The arrhythmia-specific symptoms and electrolyte results were included.

A checklist is prepared to assess the prevalence of arrhythmias and associated factors among COVID-19 patients. The checklist is adapted from Ulla Walfridsson’s “validated new Arrhythmia-Specific questionnaire in Tachycardia and Arrhythmia“ [35]. The questionnaire was developed and validated using arrhythmia patients in Sweden by an expert panel consisting of cardiologists and nurses working daily with arrhythmia patients. The checklist contains 37 questions. Five personal information, seven health histories, and five laboratory results. The rest of the questions are about arrhythmias and related questions. The patients’ records were reviewed. The data were collected by data collectors using a checklist that indicated the arrhythmia burden of symptoms.

Data collection procedure

To collect the data, four BSC nurses and one supervisor were trained for 3 days by the principal investigator about how to extract data from the chart, the inclusion and exclusion criteria, ethical issues, and the rights of participants. Four BSc nurses were assigned to the selected four hospitals independently to collect the data. The entire data collection period was supervised by one trained health professional and the principal investigator.

Data quality assurance

A pretest was conducted on 5% of the sample in the Eka Kotebe General Hospital COVID-19 center two weeks after the actual data collection, and the patients’ records were excluded from the study. The principal investigator was actively involved in the supervision of the data collection. The supervisor checked for completeness daily. Data cleaning was done manually by removing the questions with missing values.

Data processing and analysis

The collected data was checked for completeness and inconsistencies. The data were cleaned by Epi Info version 7 and analyzed by using SPSS version 25 software. The descriptive statistics, frequency, percentage, median, and interquartile range were calculated. The association between dependent and independent variables was calculated using bivariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses.

Multicollinearity was assessed before logistic regression analysis using collinearity diagnostics in SPSS. Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) values ranged from 1.11 to 1.24, and tolerance values ranged from 0.81 to 0.98, all within acceptable limits.

The Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test was not significant (χ² = 8.04, df = 8, p = 0.24), indicating adequate model fit.

Ethical consideration

Ethical approval was obtained from the Addis Ababa University, College of Health Sciences, School of Nursing and Midwifery Institutional Review Board (IRB) (Protocol No. SNM/09/2021). An official letter of cooperation was issued to the COVID-19 treatment centers in Addis Ababa, and permission to conduct the study was obtained from the medical directors of each Intensive Care Unit.

All participants were informed about the study objectives, and informed consent was obtained according to IRB-approved procedures. Written consent was obtained from participants who could read and write, while oral consent was obtained from those unable to do so, or from their surrogates. All consent procedures were documented in patients’ medical records. Confidentiality of participant information was strictly maintained, and the study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Result

Sociodemographic and physiological characteristics

A total of 422 patients diagnosed with COVID-19 participated in this study. The majority of participants were men (270; 64.0%). The mean (± SD) age of the respondents was 59 ± 15 years, with a median (IQR) age of 60 (50–70) years. The participants’ ages ranged from 16 to 100 years. The study participant accounting for, 274(64.9%) were aged over 54 years.

There were 79(18.7%) deaths among all participants. Cardiac arrest occurred in 86 (20.4%) of the total study population, out of cardiac arrested patients, 78 (98.7%) participants died.

The body temperature of patients ranged from 34.0 °C to 38.3 °C, with a median (IQR) of 36.8 (36.4–37.0)°C. For patients on mechanical ventilation, a high-flow nasal cannula (HFNC) was used, providing flow rates up to 60 L/min and FiO₂ levels between 21% and 100%. The median (IQR) of FiO₂ was 92 (70–100) (Table 1).

Prevalence of cardiac arrhythmias

The presence of cardiac arrhythmia among patients with COVID-19 was determined using patient history, physical examination, electrolyte test results, and electrocardiographic (ECG) findings. The overall prevalence of cardiac arrhythmia was 193 (45.7%, 95% CI: 40.9–50.6). Among these, 108 (56.0%) were male patients (Fig. 1).

The diagnosis of arrhythmia was primarily based on electrocardiogram (ECG) findings, focusing on the pattern of P waves, QRS morphology, and rhythm regularity. For example, ventricular tachycardia (VT) was diagnosed mainly using ECG criteria—such as wide QRS complexes, atrioventricular (AV) dissociation, capture or fusion beats, and characteristic morphology—along with supporting clinical context, including structural heart disease, and confirmed by additional investigations (e.g., imaging and laboratory tests) when necessary.

In intensive care unit (ICU) patients, arrhythmia duration was assessed through continuous telemetry monitoring, with events verified using 12-lead ECG recordings when available. Telemetry strips were reviewed by trained clinicians to determine the onset, termination, and cumulative duration of multiple arrhythmic episodes.

Risk factors related to cardiac arrhythmias

Out of all patients, 309 patients’ records revealed that they have a history of comorbidities. Of the patients accounted for, 168 (39.8%) had one comorbidity, followed by 94 (22.3%) patients who had two comorbidities. Almost half of COVID-19 patients (47.2%) had hypertension, followed by diabetes (42.9%). Out of the total Covid-19 patients, about 101 (23.9%) individuals undergo echocardiography. Among individuals who had echocardiography findings show that 22 (27.8%) patients had left ventricular (LV) wall motion abnormalities. Association between the prevalence of cardiac arrhythmia and the independent variable

Binary logistic regression analysis revealed that age, sex, heart disease, chronic renal disease, serum calcium level, and serum chloride level were significantly associated with cardiac arrhythmia.

Individuals aged 35–54 years were 73% less likely to experience cardiac arrhythmias compared with those aged 16–34 years (COR = 0.27, 95% CI: 0.12–0.60). Male patients were 47% less likely to develop cardiac arrhythmia (COR = 0.53, 95% CI: 0.35–0.80). Patients with heart disease were nearly twice as likely to develop cardiac arrhythmias compared with those without heart disease (COR = 1.66, 95% CI: 1.07–2.60).

Patients with chronic renal disease were four times more likely to develop cardiac arrhythmias compared with those without renal disease (COR = 4.06, 95% CI: 1.30–12.67). Individuals with elevated serum chloride levels were five times more likely to develop cardiac arrhythmias compared with those with normal serum chloride levels (AOR = 5.32, 95% CI: 1.94–14.00). Likewise, patients with high serum calcium levels were nearly four times more likely to develop cardiac arrhythmias compared with those with normal calcium levels (COR = 3.60, 95% CI: 1.07–12.12).

Variables with a p-value < 0.025 and a 95% confidence interval in the binary logistic regression were included in the multivariable logistic regression analysis (Table 4). After adjustment, individuals aged 35–54 years remained 68% less likely to experience cardiac arrhythmias compared with those aged 16–34 years (AOR = 0.32, 95% CI: 0.14–0.74). Male patients were 47% less likely to develop cardiac arrhythmia (AOR = 0.53, 95% CI: 0.34–0.81). Patients with elevated serum chloride levels were four times more likely to develop cardiac arrhythmias compared with those with normal levels (AOR = 4.08, 95% CI: 1.26–13.17). Similarly, those with high serum calcium levels were five times more likely to develop cardiac arrhythmias (AOR = 5.31, 95% CI: 1.73–16.27).

Discussion

COVID-19 triggers a complex interplay between the autonomic nervous system and multiple neuroendocrine pathways. An imbalance between the sympathetic and parasympathetic nervous systems can lead to inappropriate immune activation and cardiovascular dysregulation. Activation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenocortical (HPA) axis increases cortisol production, which initially supports immune function but may cause immune suppression when sustained [16–19]. Critically ill COVID-19 patients may develop Cytokine Release Syndrome (CRS), a hyperinflammatory condition characterized by excessive cytokine release (e.g., IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α). These cytokines can cause tissue and organ damage. Hemodynamic complications such as vasodilation and capillary leakage contribute to hypotension, often progressing to shock [16–22].

This study investigated the prevalence of arrhythmias in COVID-19 patients and associated risk factors. Among the 422 participants, the majority, 274 (64.9%), were aged 55 years and above, with a mean age of 59 ± 15 years and a median (IQR) age of 60 (50–70). In terms of sex distribution, 270 (64.0%) were male.

The overall prevalence of cardiac arrhythmias was high (45.7% ; 95% CI: 40.90–50.60), which exceeds findings from studies conducted by the British Cardiac Society (17%), Denmark (37%, 95% CI: 30.0–45.0) [14], and Iran (23.6%) [12], but is slightly lower than the prevalence reported in a study from China (60%) [22]. Differences in technological advancement, healthcare infrastructure, and study timelines likely account for this variation. During the early phase of the pandemic, limited awareness of COVID-19’s cardiac implications may have contributed to underreporting. Additionally, factors such as limited diagnostic capacity, a higher proportion of patients with comorbidities and older age (> 54 years), and less developed arrhythmia management in our setting may explain the higher observed prevalence [11, 19–22].

Among patients who developed arrhythmias, most (60.1%) had sinus tachycardia. This result was lower than findings from global studies reporting higher rates of atrial arrhythmias (81.8%) [22]. The prevalence of atrial fibrillation/flutter in our study was 17.1%, slightly lower than the 24.5% reported in a Texas study [16], likely due to differences in the inflammatory responses associated with the disease.

Individuals aged 35–54 years were 68% less likely to experience cardiac arrhythmias compared with those aged 16–34 years (AOR = 0.32, 95% CI: 0.14–0.74). These findings are consistent with global data indicating that older age significantly predicts COVID-19 severity [23, 24]. One global survey reported a median age of 62 years [22], suggesting that older adults are more vulnerable to COVID-19-related arrhythmias. Male patients were 47% less likely to develop cardiac arrhythmias (AOR = 0.53, 95% CI: 0.34–0.81).

Patients with high serum calcium levels were five times more likely to develop cardiac arrhythmias (AOR = 5.31, 95% CI: 1.73–16.27). Similarly, those with elevated serum chloride levels were four times more likely (AOR = 4.08, 95% CI: 1.26–13.17). These results are consistent with a previous study conducted in Egypt [4]. The involvement of multiple organ systems in COVID-19 may impair electrolyte regulation, and electrolyte imbalances—particularly in potassium, magnesium, and calcium—are known contributors to arrhythmogenesis [30, 31]. This may be due to disease-related alterations in physiological and electrolyte balance. Although magnesium data were incomplete, and potassium showed clinical relevance, statistical significance was not observed.

A total of 168 participants (39.8%) had at least one comorbidity, with hypertension being the most common (47.2%). These findings suggest that chronic diseases may exacerbate cardiovascular risk in COVID-19 patients [27].

In this study, the prevalence of arrhythmias among ICU-admitted COVID-19 patients was 45.7%. Sinus tachyarrhythmias and atrial fibrillation were the most commonly observed types. Our findings are consistent with a study conducted in Italy, which reported that 24.5% of COVID-19 patients had a history of atrial fibrillation (AF), as well as with other studies conducted on ICU cohorts [6, 7].

The proposed mechanisms underlying COVID-19–associated arrhythmias include cytokine-mediated ion channel alterations, such as IL-6 inhibition of the hERG channel and disruption of repolarizing currents [19, 22]. Additionally, systemic inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1, and IL-6) may affect ion channel expression, gap junction integrity, and action potential duration [19–22]. Multiscale electrophysiological modeling has further demonstrated that inflammation may induce QT prolongation and create an arrhythmogenic substrate [16–18].

Other studies have reported similar findings, with cardiac arrhythmia prevalence rates of 37% [14] and 60% [15] among COVID-19 patients. However, most clinical investigations do not simultaneously assess inflammatory biomarkers, ion channel function, and drug concentrations in relation to arrhythmic events. Consequently, these proposed mechanisms remain literature-supported hypotheses rather than empirically verified mechanisms [18–22].

In an African cohort study, ECG findings revealed significantly prolonged QTc intervals in the study population. In our study, cardiac arrest occurred in 22.4% of COVID-19 patients, a higher rate than that reported in the African cohort (7.5%) [33]. The mortality rate in our study was 18.7%, exceeding that of a meta-analysis reporting 14.3% inpatient mortality among COVID-19 patients [32] and that of non-African cohorts (14.2%) [33], but consistent with the overall mortality rate reported among African participants (18.2%) [33]. In the African study, abnormal QTc prolongation was identified as an independent predictor of sudden cardiac death, supporting the conclusion that cardiac arrhythmia significantly contributes to poor outcomes in hospitalized COVID-19 patients.

This higher mortality and cardiac arrest rate may reflect limited access to advanced diagnostic technologies, continuous cardiac monitoring, and timely management of the complex pathophysiological processes associated with COVID-19 in resource-limited settings.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it included only patients treated in Addis Ababa City, and may not represent rural populations. Second, data regarding COVID-19 medications and ICU length of stay were not collected. Key laboratory and clinical parameters, such as troponin levels, echocardiography results, serum magnesium, APACHE/SOFA scores, and vasopressor requirements, were incomplete. The small number of patients who underwent echocardiography may limit the ability to fully assess underlying structural or functional cardiac abnormalities. Third, the study did not distinguish between the characteristics of different subtypes of cardiac arrhythmias. Fourth, the systematic sampling method resulted in underrepresentation of younger patients (16–34 years) and overrepresentation of older patients with comorbidities. Finally, potential selection bias related to the exclusion of COVID-19 patients treated outside the ICU and the restriction to four public hospitals may further limit generalizability.

Conclusion

This study found that nearly half (45.7%, 95% CI: 40.9–50.6) of ICU-admitted COVID-19 patients developed cardiac arrhythmias. The overall mortality rate was 18.7%. Electrolyte imbalances—specifically elevated serum calcium and chloride levels—were positively associated with cardiac arrhythmias. Patients aged 35–54 years were 68% less likely to develop arrhythmias compared with younger age groups.

These findings highlight the need for early recognition and management of arrhythmias in COVID-19 patients, as well as the importance of electrolyte monitoring and age-specific risk assessment to improve patient outcomes in critical care settings.

Recommendation

COVID-19 treatment centers in Addis Ababa should work more on cardiac arrhythmias for high-quality care. It is also crucial to optimize diagnostic services through ongoing training, and enhanced support for practitioners working in intensive care units (ICUs).

Healthcare professionals should closely monitor serum electrolyte levels, particularly calcium and chloride, upon admission of COVID-19 patients. Prompt identification and correction of abnormalities are essential to reduce the risk of arrhythmia.

Future studies should focus on the treatment and management of cardiac arrhythmias in COVID-19 patients, identify key determinants, and explore outcomes across different arrhythmia subtypes.
