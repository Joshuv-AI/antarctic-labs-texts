Problems and needs in palliative care questionnaire – caregiver form (PNPC-c): a cross-cultural adaptation and validation into Spanish
Authors: Jaime Martín-Martín, Stella Martín-de-las-Heras, Pablo García-Pérez, Leticia Rubio, María Dolores Pérez-Cárceles
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCaregivers are the essential mainstay of care for patients under palliative treatment, and they experience problems and needs for which they require support, but these are rarely assessed. The Problems and Needs in Palliative Care questionnaire – caregiver form (PNPC-c) evaluates these issues, but it has not been adapted into Spanish. The aim of this study was to translate, cross-culturally adapt, and evaluate the psychometric properties to validate the PNPC-c into Spanish (PNPC-c SV®).MethodsThe translation and a cross-cultural adaptation of the PNPC-c comprised direct and reverse translation, revision by a Delphi panel and a pilot study. The validation of the psychometric properties of the PNPC-c SV® instrument were analyzed. The PNPC-c SV® was validated with a group of 230 caregivers of palliative care patients. A response frequency analysis was performed to identify the 10 main problems and needs among caregivers. An inferential analysis was performed to compare the problems identified between those over and under 50 years of age and between men and women.ResultsThe PNPC-c SV® developed led to the reduction of the questionnaire from the initial 67 items to 48 items per section (problems and needs). The main problems reported were continuing their social activities (78.3%) and fear of an unpredictable future (77.3%). The need that required the most attention from caregivers was fear of an unpredictable future (56.1%), followed by not wanting to leave the patient alone (50%). Significant differences were observed in the identification of problems between men and women as well as between caregivers older and younger than 50 years of age. The internal consistency using Cronbach’s α was 0.959, with problems and needs presenting a value of 0.907 and 0.940, respectively. The PNPC-c SV® is a useful tool to assess the problems and needs of Spanish-speaking caregivers of palliative care patients.ConclusionsMapping the problems and needs of caregivers allows planning and organizing effective care services, thereby improving the quality of life of caregivers and patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12904-026-02166-1.Keywords: Ethics, Clinical Quality of Healthcare, Caregiver, Validation Studies, Psychometrics, Cross-Sectional Studies, PNPC-c

Introduction

Population aging has led to an increase in the number of multi-pathological patients requiring specialized attention in palliative care [1]. Palliative care does not only occur in the moments close to death; it can have a long duration, impacting the quality of life of the patient, their environment and their caregivers [2]. Therefore, there are multiple areas and aspects of both the patient and the caregiver that should be evaluated to improve their quality of life and the professional support and assistance to patients and caregivers [3].

According to the National Cancer Institute, a caregiver is defined as a person who provides care to individuals who need assistance with daily activities at home, in a hospital, or in another healthcare setting [4]. The role of the caregiver in palliative care is essential, since they are the link between the health system and the patient [5]. Caregivers can be formal (people with adequate training to provide care) or informal (usually family members). In both cases, they focus on keeping and/or improving the quality of life of these patients [6].

The majority of informal caregivers aren’t trained in health care and must constantly face new challenges, requiring specific attention for themselves [7]. The patient’s loss of autonomy with the consequent decrease in the quality of life of a loved one, the appearance of new symptomatology, and the psychological changes suffered by the patient are fundamental factors that produce overload in the caregiver, and there are several instruments that evaluate this specific factor [8]. However, many caregivers deny their own fundamental problems and needs to provide better care for the family member, which makes it difficult for them to recognize their own needs [9]. Nevertheless, understanding the needs and problems of caregivers in palliative care is essential for providing quality, family-centered care.

Questionnaires are the most commonly used instruments to assess the subjective perception of professionals, patients and relatives in the main areas of approach in patients requiring palliative care. Factors such as the patient’s physical ability [10], fatigue [11], professional skills in the area [12, 13] or quality of life at the end of life [14] have been extensively studied by means of validated questionnaires. The quality and application of questionnaires depend to a great extent on the analysis of their psychometric properties: reliability, validity and responsiveness; improving the quality of the results obtained [15].

Recognizing the central role that caregivers play, the World Health Organization includes caregivers’ well‑being as a key component of its definition of palliative care, underscoring the importance of addressing their needs within clinical practice. However, the structured assessment of caregivers’ needs is still not routinely implemented, despite its relevance for improving the quality of care and supporting families throughout the palliative process. In addition, these data are essential for planning and organizing effective and efficient care provision [16] guiding the development of tailored interventions and informing service allocation within palliative care systems.

Questionnaires such as “Family Inventory of Needs” and “Home Caregiver Need Survey” (HCNS) have been developed and validated to assess some of the needs of informal caregivers of palliative care patients [17]. The “Carer Support Needs Assessment Tool – Intervention (CSNAT-I)”, is a brief evidence-based tool developed to identify family caregivers’ support needs for end-of-life care research and practice. The CSNAT-I has been rigorously validated and has demonstrated good face, content and criterion validity [18]. However, the “Problems and Needs in Palliative Care questionnaire-caregiver” (PNPC-c) is designed to comprehensively assess the problems and needs of caregivers [19]. The purpose of this questionnaire is to assess, through 67 items replicated in two sections, the existence of problems in caregivers of palliative care patients and whether they need more attention for these problems. The PNPC-c questionnaire was originally developed in German and translated into English [19] and used in other countries such as Netherlands [16, 20], but its psychometric properties have not been fully analyzed [16, 19]. However, it has not been translated, adapted and validated into Spanish. To address this gap and to provide an overview of the problems that caregivers experience as well as to identify those for which they would appreciate greater professional attention, the aim of this study was twofold: first, to adapt and translate the PNPC‑c into Spanish; and second, to validate the Problems and Needs in Palliative Care questionnaire–Caregiver Spanish Version® (PNPC‑c SV®) by assessing its psychometric properties.

Methods

Study design

This study was designed in three phases. In phase 1, the “Problems and Needs in Palliative Care questionnaire-caregiver” was translated and cross-culturally adapted to Spanish in accordance with international standards [20]. Phase 2 involved an initial pilot study and, subsequently, an analytical cross-sectional study to identify the main problems and needs of caregivers. Phase 3 consisted of the validation of the psychometric properties of the PNPC-c SV® in caregivers of patients requiring palliative care.

Participants

A total of 230 Spanish caregivers of palliative care patients participated in this study. Participants were recruited over a 12‑month period (September 2023–2024) from two randomly selected Home Palliative Care Support Teams in Murcia (Spain). A consecutive sampling strategy was used, including all caregivers who met the inclusion criteria during the study period. The duration of the recruitment period was determined based on the estimated inclusion rate in this clinical context, yielding an average of 10–12 new participants per month and allowing the planned sample size to be reached (n = 230). Recruitment and data collection were conducted by trained researchers in collaboration with the clinical teams, following standardized procedures to ensure data quality and ethical compliance.

The inclusion criteria were being a Spanish‑speaking primary caregiver of a palliative home care patient. Exclusion criteria were refusal to provide informed consent and inability to understand the questionnaire items.

Measures

Socio-demographic survey

A questionnaire was created to gather participants’ sociodemographic information: sex, age, marital status, educational level, health-related training, employment status, changes in employment for patient care, time spent caring for the patient, patient needs (day and night care), previous care experience, relationship with the patient and profession of the caregiver. The data obtained from patients were sex, age, main diagnosis and dependence in activities of daily living.

PNPC-c instruments

The PNPC-c questionnaire was designed through a panel of experts to assess the support needs of caregivers. The questionnaire was originally developed in German and back-translated into English; structured in 67 items that are replicated in two main sections: “Is this (item) a problem?” with three answer choices (Yes - Somewhat - No), and the second section “Do you want (professional) attention for this (item)?” with three answer choices (Yes, more – As much as now – No). Each section has two main theoretical constructs: “Quality of care aspects”, with three dimensions (taking care of the patient, problems in consultation, overriding problems), and “Quality of life of the caregiver”, with nine dimensions (instrumental activities of daily living, autonomy, own physical symptoms, financial issues, administrative issues, spiritual issues, relationship with the patient, social support, psychological issues). The theoretical construct “Quality of care aspects” includes two additional dimensions, in which the need for support from the professional is evaluated (Concerning the General Practice and Concerning the Specialist) “Do you want his/her attention for this?”, but not the caregiver’s own needs related to the patient [19]. The psychometric properties validity and reliability were satisfactorily evaluated in the PNPC-c. Reliability was evaluated by means of Cronbach’s α coefficient for internal consistency, obtaining positive values (α˃0.70) in seven of the dimensions, with the remaining four presenting also a positive but lower value (α = 0.60–0.70). The validity of the proposed dimensions was correlated with the COOP-WONCA questionnaire [21] with substantial correlations (Spearman´s Rho ˃0.40) [19].

Translation and cross-cultural adaptation

The translation and cross-cultural adaptation of the PNPC-c questionnaire into Spanish version (PNPC-c SV®) was carried out based on the work of Osse (2006) [16]. The translation and adaptation process followed the standardized method of direct and reverse translation [22]. Authorization was obtained from the original authors of the questionnaire for its translation and adaptation. The questionnaire was translated from the English version into Spanish by two independent palliative care specialists with extensive knowledge of English. Both translations were shared with a Delphi panel of 10 palliative care experts (physicians and nurses); the two versions were compared to identify the most appropriate expressions to improve the understanding of caregivers and to obtain a single consensus by making the necessary cultural adaptations [23]. Finally, an official native English translator back-translated the questionnaire into English to compare it with its original published version. After this process, the preliminary version of the questionnaire was piloted with a group of caregivers (n = 10). This process concluded with the PNPC-c SV® questionnaire (Supplementary material 1).

Validation of PNPC-c Spanish version®

Descriptive statistics based on response frequency were performed for qualitative sociodemographic variables and a descriptive study of means and standard deviation was conducted for quantitative variables. The problems and needs identified by the participants based on the PNPC-c SV® were presented in frequency response rates. An inferential analysis of contingency tables was performed using Fisher’s test to compare the problems identified between men and women and between younger (˂50 years) and older (˃50 years) caregivers. The age of 50 years was established based on the median age of the caregivers.

The psychometric properties of the PNPC-c SV® instrument were analyzed to evaluate its internal consistency, internal structure and factor analysis. Internal consistency was evaluated by using Cronbach’s α Coefficient [24].

Sampling adequacy was assessed using the Kaiser-Mayer-Olkin test (KMO) measure for the overall questionnaire and separately for the sections “Is this a problem?” and “Do you want professional attention for this?” [25]. Bartlett’s test of sphericity was applied to test whether the correlation matrix differed significantly from an identity matrix; significant results (p˂0.05) indicated that the data were suitable for factor analysis [26]. Based on the results of the KMO measures and Bartlett’s test, Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) [27] was conducted. Number of factors analysis was based on Parallel analysis by Factors eigenvalues, factors were extracted using Maximum Likelihood Extraction (MLE) and an oblique promax rotation method was applied to clarify the factor structure. Factor retention criteria included eigenvalues ≥ 1 [28], scree plot inspection, and a proportion of explained variance greater than 10%.

The appropriate sample size is five participants per item evaluated [26]. The analysis of the psychometric properties of the questionnaire was carried out for the two sections separately: “Is this (item) a problem?” and “Do you want (professional) attention for this (item)?”. Data were entered and processed using the statistical program JASP version 0.18.1 [29].

Ethical aspects

This study was performed in line with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The Provincial Research Ethics Committee of Malaga granted approval (Date: May 11, 2020; Number CPAL-2020). All participants signed the informed consent [30]. All participant information was anonymized before analysis, and any identifying details were removed or coded to ensure confidentiality. The dataset was stored on secure, password‑protected institutional servers with restricted access limited to the research team. No data was transferred to external devices or third‑party platforms. To maintain the integrity of the analysis, we used standardized procedures and validated statistical software. Only the final anonymized dataset was used for analysis, and all steps of the analytic process were documented to ensure traceability and reproducibility.

Results

Translation and cross-cultural adaptation

The PNPC‑c questionnaire underwent a rigorous process of cultural adaptation, translation, and validation for its use in the Spanish context, resulting in the PNPC‑c SV®. As part of this methodological process, which included a comprehensive cross‑cultural adaptation and evaluation by an expert panel, the instrument was refined to enhance clarity, relevance, and conceptual equivalence. This review led to a reduction in the number of items from the original 67 per Sect. (134 items in total) to 48 items per Sect. (96 items in total) in the adapted version. Specifically, nine items from the original published questionnaire were removed, and eighteen items were merged into nine due to conceptual overlap or redundancy. Importantly, these modifications were carried out while preserving the original dimensional structure of the instrument, ensuring that the adapted version maintained fidelity to the conceptual framework of the PNPC‑c (Supplementary Table 1).

Structural validity and internal consistency

The construct validity for the PNPC-c SV® questionnaire calculated by means of the KMO test was 0.515, and Bartlett’s Test of Sphericity was significant (p=˂0.001); for the overall scale. Regarding construct validity for the section “Do you consider this to be a problem?” the KMO test result was 0.7, and Bartlett’s Test of Sphericity was significant (X2 = 5376.399; degrees of freedom (df) = 1128; and p=˂0.001). These values indicate that both the sampling and the variance of the results are adequate and allow performing the EFA. EFA oblique with rotation method promax showed that items 24, 42 and 43 do not have a significant loading on the factors in this factor solution with values below 0.3 (Supplementary Table 3). It was also observed that items 1, 4, 10, 15, 16, 17, 20, 28, 30, 32, 35, 37, 38, 46, 47 and 48 produced cross loading on two or more factors. The EFA detected 13 components with an eigenvalue higher than 1 (Supplementary Fig. 1), which explained 54% of the variance of the result based on the initial eigenvalues.

The construct validity for the section “Do you need attention for this problem?” analyzed with the KMO was 0.795, and Bartlett’s Test of Sphericity was significant (X2=6659.017; degrees of freedom (df) = 1128; and p=˂0.001). Based on the established cut-off point of 0.3 factor loadings, factor 1 included 13 items. EFA oblique with rotation method promax showed cross loadings for items 2, 4, 5, 7, 10, 11, 17, 23, 24, 30, 32, 33, 36, 38, 39 and 46 on two or more factors. The factor distribution obtained does not correspond to the distribution of theoretical dimensions established by the originally published PNPC-c questionnaire (Supplementary Table 4). In this sense items 19, 28, 43, 44 do not have a significant loading on the factors in this factor solution with values below 0.3 (Supplementary Table 4). The exploratory factor analysis showed 10 factors with eigenvalues above 1 that explained 54.7% of the variance of the results (Supplementary Fig. 2). More specifically, the exploratory factor analysis showed the existence of 13 factors for the section “Do you consider this to be a problem?” and 10 for the section “Do you need attention for this problem?” (Supplementary Table 3).

The internal consistency of the PNPC-c SV® was analyzed based on the theoretical dimensions established by the original authors of the questionnaire using Cronbach’s α with a value of 0.959. The sections “Do you consider this to be a problem?” and “Do you need attention for this problem?” of the questionnaire obtained values of 0.907 and 0.940, respectively (Table 5). Likewise, each of the theoretical dimensions of the questionnaire were analyzed according to the original distribution. For the sections “Do you consider this to be a problem?” and “Do you need attention for this problem?”, the dimension with the highest value was “Financial Issues” (α = 0.785 and α = 0.903, respectively) and the one with the lowest value was “Spiritual Issues” (α = 0.366 and α = 0.47, respectively).

Discussion

In our study, the PNPC-c questionnaire was cross-culturally adapted, translated and validated in the Spanish context (PNPC-c SV®). During this process, the number of items was reduced from 67 per section to 48, due to the removal and merging of items with similar characteristics, without modifying the initial structure of dimensions. Furthermore, the psychometric properties; internal consistency and construct validity are positive.

The validation of the PNPC-c SV® questionnaire was performed in a sample of 230 caregivers. Although no formal a priori sample size calculation was performed, the final sample exceeds the minimum recommended thresholds for exploratory factor analysis according to COSMIN criteria, ensuring adequate power for the psychometric analyses conducted. The original published version of the PNPC-c [19] was applied to a sample of 76 informal caregivers. Analyzing these data with the current COSMIN criteria [31] for questionnaire validation, the sample size currently required should be larger, thus the results of the psychometric properties should be cautiously considered.

In our study, the main problems and needs experienced by caregivers (Table 2) show similarities with the original published study [16], demonstrating that caregivers have common concerns. Significant differences were observed according to the sex of the caregiver (Table 4) possibly due to cultural differences [32]. In general, the problems are significantly greater for younger caregivers, suggesting that younger caregivers found it more difficult to manage the disruption to their life due to caring for the patient [16].

The psychometric properties of the original published version showed results of feasibility with a mean missing response of 3 ± 7 in the section “Do you consider this to be a problem?” and 5.4 ± 13.1 in the section “Do you need attention for this problem?”, being significantly associated with the age of the participants [19]. However, in this study, no loss of response values was observed in any of the items. The loss of response in these items could be associated with the lack of comprehensiveness; a total of 76 comments made by 40 of the participants in the original published study were observed [19].

The structural validity analysis of the PNPC‑c SV® questionnaire for the section “Do you consider this to be a problem?” identified a 13‑factor structure (Supplementary Table 3). Although this number of factors aligns with the original published version, the distribution of the items within each factor according to the theoretical distribution of the original published questionnaire are not comparable [16]. It should be noted that the original study did not perform a psychometric analysis of these characteristics, thus a comparison cannot be established. Furthermore, this factor solution showed an adequate adjustment in its significant values of KMO = 0.7 and Bartlett’s Test of Sphericity: close to the reference values [31].

However, for the section “Do you need attention for this problem?”, the number of factors was 10, as opposed to the 13 defined in the original published version of the questionnaire [16]. The adjustments for the exploratory factor analysis were the same as for the previous section, thus the present factor solution could be accepted (Supplementary Table 5) [31]. It is noteworthy that the distribution of items and factor loadings observed in the present study cannot be compared to those obtained in the original published version for neither of the two sections, since this analysis was not performed in the original study [16].

In the original published instrument,, in the section “Do you consider this a problem?”, the reliability levels based on the theoretical dimensions of the questionnaire were acceptable (α ≥ 0.7) in seven dimensions (caring for the patient, predominant problems in the quality of care, autonomy, economic issue, relationship with the patient, social support and psychological issues) and questionable (α ≥ 0.6) in four others (problems in consultations, own physical symptoms, administrative issues and spiritual issues). Low levels of internal consistency were only observed in the dimension “Instrumental activities of daily living” (α = 0.56) [19]. Furthermore, in the section “Do you need care for this problem?”, two dimensions (relative to general practice, and relative to the specialist) obtained excellent internal consistency values (α ≥ 0.9), ten dimensions (Patient care, problems in the consultation, predominant problems in the quality of care, autonomy, own physical symptoms, economic problems, spiritual problems, relationship with the patient, social support, psychological problems) obtained good internal consistency values (α = 0.8-0. 9), one dimension (admission problems) obtained acceptable internal consistency values (α = 0.76), and one dimension (instrumental activities of daily living) obtained unacceptable levels of internal consistency (α = 0.49) [19]. These levels of internal consistency are similar to the results obtained in the present study (Table 5).

Prue et al. (2014) compiled the instruments validated to date that assess the needs of informal caregivers in cancer patients, where the PNPC-c questionnaire is not included [32]. As the results obtained in the present study and in the original published study [19], the validation studies of the different questionnaires showed positive results in the analysis of internal consistency [32], with the exception of the Needs Assessment of Family Caregivers-Cancer (NAFC-C), whose values ranged between 0.56 and 0.86 [33]. A differential element of the instruments that assess the needs of caregivers is their length, from the 27 items of the NAFC-C questionnaire [33] to the 90 items of the HCNS [34], which has an impact on the number of dimensions assessed, ranging from 4 to 9 [32]. All the questionnaires included were developed based on a panel of experts [32], although only two of them performed test-retest analyses of the results obtained [35, 36]. As with the original published version of the PNPC-c questionnaire, correlation analyses were performed with the COOP questionnaire [37], the HCNS questionnaire was correlated with the Karnofsky Performance Scale and the NAFC-C with the caregiver’s quality of life [33]. Beyond all these questionnaires, the “Carer Support Needs Assessment Tool – Intervention (CSNAT-I)” constitutes a key reference instrument in caregiver‑needs assessment. It is a brief, evidence‑based tool specifically designed for palliative and end‑of‑life care at home, covering 14 domains of support needs identified directly from caregivers through qualitative research. Its validation demonstrated clear associations with caregiver strain, distress, preparedness, and patient symptom burden, highlighting its capacity to capture meaningful aspects of the caregiving experience [18]. The CSNAT-I has been adapted and validated in different contexts and languages [38–40]. This has made it a widely used tool in clinical practice and research, reinforcing the importance of developing culturally adapted tools, such as the PNPC-c SV®, to meet the specific needs of carers within different healthcare systems.

Despite the development of all these instruments, to the best of our knowledge, no instruments were identified that assess the problems and support needs of informal caregivers of palliative care patients as the PNPC-c SV® does. This 48-item instrument allows identifying the needs and problems of caregivers in order to provide support from the health and social services, which will directly improve the quality of life of caregivers and indirectly improve the quality of life of patients.

This study has some limitations. It was not possible to analyze all psychometric properties through tests such as test-retest, criterion-related or concurrent validity and confirmatory factor analysis. Future research should incorporate a comprehensive content validity assessment to further strengthen the evidence supporting the content validity of the PNPC‑c SV®. The study was also limited by the gender homogeneity of the sample and the potential non‑randomized sampling, factors that should be considered when interpreting the results. However, the sample size allowed analyzing both the internal consistency using Cronbach’s alpha and the structural validity through exploratory factor analysis, in both psychometric properties for independent sections of the questionnaire. In addition, the translation and cross-cultural adaptation (PNPC-c SV®) allowed reducing the number of items, thus facilitating its application. Further studies with a larger sample size will allow performing a confirmatory factor analysis and increasing the number of analyzed psychometric properties of the PNPC-c SV®.

Conclusions

The PNPC-c SV® is a useful tool to assess the problems and support needs of informal caregivers of palliative care patients. This study therefore has several implications for clinical practice. Physicians can play a crucial role—and benefit substantially—by acknowledging the challenges faced by family caregivers, communicating effectively, supporting decision‑making, facilitating home care, addressing caregivers’ emotional well‑being, and recognizing their grief. We hope this study motivates professionals to engage proactively with caregivers in palliative care and to systematically assess and identify the areas in which they need additional support.

The PNPC-c SV® is the only instrument in Spanish in this field that is used exclusively for this purpose; it can be extrapolated to Spanish speakers with the necessary cross-cultural adaptations. The study of needs through this instrument enables the design of specific courses for caregivers who lack training in this area and who play a fundamental role for these patients.
