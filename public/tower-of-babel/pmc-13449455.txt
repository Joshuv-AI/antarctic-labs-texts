Extranodal natural killer-cell lymphoma presenting to the emergency room with abdominal pain: a case report

Background

Extranodal natural killer (NK)/T-cell lymphoma, nasal type (ENKL), is a rare and aggressive subtype of non-Hodgkin lymphoma characterized by a strong association with Epstein–Barr virus (EBV) and a predilection for extranodal sites [1–4]. The nasal cavity and nasopharynx are the most frequent primary sites, whereas the skin, gastrointestinal tract, and testes are less commonly involved. Primary gastrointestinal ENKL is exceptionally rare, accounting for less than 1% of all malignant lymphomas, and typically presents with nonspecific symptoms that hinder early diagnosis. Although gastrointestinal bleeding and perforation are uncommon, their occurrence indicates an extremely poor prognosis [4].

We describe a rare case of primary ileal ENKL that initially presented with abdominal pain that rapidly progressed to intestinal perforation, highlighting the diagnostic challenges and critical importance of timely recognition and intervention.

Case presentation

Case history

A previously healthy Japanese 20-year-old male, with no relevant medical history, also no family and psychosocial history developed sudden-onset abdominal pain while outdoors. He was asymptomatic until this episode. The pain was continuous with intermittent exacerbations, followed by a single episode of bloody stool. He was unable to walk because of the severity of pain and was transported to our emergency department by ambulance.

On arrival, his vital signs were: temperature 38.0 °C, blood pressure 108/40 mmHg, pulse 97 beats/minute, respiratory rate 31 breaths/minute, and full consciousness. Physical examination revealed midline lower abdominal tenderness, without peritoneal signs. Laboratory tests revealed slight elevations in the levels of inflammatory markers (Fig. 1). Digital rectal examination was negative for occult blood. The contrast-enhanced abdominal CT is shown in Fig. 2A. Despite the administration of paracetamol (1000 mg), flurbiprofen (50 mg), and pentazocine (15 mg), his pain persisted, and he required hospitalization for observation. No antibiotics were administered because neither CT nor laboratory results suggested an intra-abdominal infection. Although the pain was severe, the idea of performing a laparoscopy for diagnostic purposes was difficult to consider on the first day, given that the cause of the pain was still unknown.

On day 2 of hospitalization, he continued to experience severe abdominal pain. Early morning repeat CT revealed intraperitoneal free air (Fig. 2B), which was consistent with a gastrointestinal perforation. Emergency laparotomy revealed a perforated lesion with semi-circumferential ulceration located 100 cm proximal to the terminal ileum (Fig. 3). Partial small bowel resection was performed. No lymphadenopathy requiring resection was observed. The postoperative course was uneventful. On postoperative day 8, the histopathological examination suggested malignant lymphoma. On day 12, immunohistochemistry revealed diffused EBV-encoded RNA positivity, CD3 positivity, and CD20 negativity, confirming the diagnosis of ENKL. This patient was diagnosed with stage 3 ENKL and assessed as having an intermediate risk according to the PINK-E score (Prognostic Index for Nodal Marginal Zone Lymphoma with Extranodal Involvement).

Outcome and follow-up

The patient was referred to a specialized oncology center on day 18, and received two courses the SMILE regimen; this name of regimen is derived from the following pharmacological agents: S: Dexamethasone (a corticosteroid), M: Methotrexate, I: Ifosfamide (an alkylating agent), L: L-asparaginase (an enzyme that depletes asparagine), E: Etoposide (a topoisomerase inhibitor). The selection of the SMILE regimen for this patient was based on its established efficacy against the highly aggressive and chemo-resistant nature of ENKL.

During the hematopoietic recovery phase following the second course of SMILE, the patient manifested a persistent fever and rapidly progressive acute liver failure. Given the positive EBV-PCR results and the underlying EBER(Epstein-Barr virus-Encoded small RNA)-positive malignancy, a diagnosis of EBV-related hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) was established. Despite aggressive intervention with steroid pulse therapy, high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin, cyclosporine, and etoposide, the patient's condition continued to deteriorate, leading to a fatal outcome within three months of the initial presentation.

Discussion

We report a rare case of ENKL diagnosed after a small intestinal perforation that resulted in a fatal outcome. This highly instructive and clinically significant case underscores the necessity of including rare diseases such as ENKL in the initial differential diagnosis of gastrointestinal complaints and symptoms.

Natural killer cell-derived lymphomas were first identified in the late 1980s. In the fifth edition of the World Health Organization classification of hematolymphoid tumors, ENKL are listed among EBV-positive extranodal natural killer (NK)/T-cell lymphoma [1]. Epidemiologically, the incidence of ENKL varies geographically, with a typical age of onset of approximately 50 years. Most cases present as nasal tumors, often accompanied by localized ulcerations [2, 3].

ENKTLs are of NK-cell origin. Approximately 25% of patients with newly diagnosed ENKL present with systemic dissemination. Advanced-stage disease and localized disease arising outside the nasal cavity are generally managed with chemotherapy alone; however, the 5-year overall survival rate remains poor at approximately 40% to 50% [4].

Contrastingly, ENKL arising outside the upper aerodigestive tract is rare, with gastrointestinal involvement accounting for only 3.8% of cases [5]. The most frequent symptom in these cases is abdominal pain (~ 90%), whereas intestinal perforation and B symptoms are reported in a minority of cases [6]. Less commonly, systemic symptoms or pancytopenia led to a diagnosis [3]. Because extranodal primary sites are classified as advanced-stage disease, the 5-year overall survival rate is even lower at approximately 34% [7]. Among the patients undergoing surgery for intestinal perforation, our review identified 24 previously reported cases (Table 2), of which only one patient survived beyond 1 year [6, 8–24]. These findings indicate that although the prognosis is poorer in advanced presentations, the ability to suspect a diagnosis at an early stage is crucial for prolonging survival.

Gastrointestinal endoscopy is commonly used to investigate the source of bleeding in hemodynamically stable patients presenting with hematochezia and abdominal pain. Colonoscopy has a reported diagnostic yield of 45–90% for lower gastrointestinal bleeding [6]. Even when the initial colonoscopy is negative for non-small bowel bleeding, repeat examination can identify the source in approximately 30% of cases [8]. When small bowel bleeding is suspected, video capsule endoscopy is often performed. Contrast-enhanced CT can detect active bleeding and mass lesions with high sensitivity [9]; however, it frequently fails to visualize mucosal lesions or small ulcers, necessitating complementary evaluation using endoscopy or other modalities. Consequently, in an emergency department setting, a diagnosis often cannot be established during initial management, and early consultation with appropriate specialists is warranted because of the broadness of the differential diagnosis.

Our patient was a young man with ENKL who presented with intermittent abdominal pain and was diagnosed with intestinal perforation. He was younger than the usual age of onset, and the nonspecific nature of his symptoms rendered the diagnosis challenging until the perforation occurred.

In retrospect, close attention to the characteristics of the abdominal pain might have suggested an impending extension of the mucosal lesion beyond the intestinal wall, such as a perforation (Fig. 4). In this case, pain exhibited both intermittent and continuous components, consistent with a mixture of visceral and somatic pain. Contrast-enhanced CT revealed no abnormal mesenteric findings such as increased fat stranding. This presentation could thus be interpreted as intermittent visceral pain due to mucosal involvement, accompanied by the onset of continuous somatic pain as the process extends beyond the intestinal wall (that is, the visceral peritoneum). Nevertheless, even with such insights, it remains inherently challenging to make such a diagnosis during the initial evaluation.

Conclusions

Herein, we report a rare case of ENKL diagnosed after perforation of the terminal ileum. In patients presenting with persistent abdominal pain and hematochezia but without obvious abnormalities on contrast-enhanced CT, ulcerative diseases of the small intestine with a risk of perforation, including rare diseases such as ENKL, should be considered in the differential diagnosis at presentation. Prompt and thorough evaluation, including early consultation with specialists, is essential for improving the prognosis of patients with ENKL.
