Dichroism from Chiral Thermoelectric Probes: Generalized Sum Rules for Orbital and Heat Magnetizations
Baptiste Bermond, Lucila Peralta Gavensky, Anaïs Defossez, Nathan Goldman

Abstract
We introduce a unified framework that relates orbital and heat magnetizations to experimentally accessible excitation spectra, through thermoelectric probes and generalized sum rules. By analyzing zero-temperature transport coefficients and applying Kramers-Kronig relations, we derive spectral representations of magnetization densities from thermoelectric correlation functions. Excitation rates under chiral thermoelectric drives then naturally emerge as direct probes of these Kubo-type correlators, placing orbital and heat magnetizations on equal footing with the topological Chern number. As a direct consequence of our formalism, we introduce a hierarchical construction that organizes orbital and heat magnetizations into distinct physical contributions accessible through sum rules, and also derive real-space markers of these magnetizations. From an experimental standpoint, we propose concrete implementations of thermoelectric dichroic measurements in quantum-engineered platforms based on modulated strain fields. These results establish thermoelectric dichroic measurements as a versatile route to access and disentangle fundamental ground-state properties.

Introduction

. Indeed, the quantum metric and the Berry curvature – as well as their associated topological invariants – can be expressed in terms of sum rules involving dissipative responses upon driving

;

;

;

;
Repellin and Goldman 2019
;
Schüler et al. 2020
;
Goldman and Ozawa 2024
;
Verma and Queiroz 2024
;
Bermond et al. 2025
. This suggests practical methods to probe these geometric properties in a wide range of physical settings, including solid-state devices

;
Tan et al. 2019
;
Klees et al. 2020
;
Chen et al. 2022
;
Beaulieu et al. 2024
;
Kang et al. 2025
and ultracold gases
Asteria et al. 2019
. Besides, sum rules have also emerged as fundamental identities for establishing bounds on material properties
Kruchkov and Ryu 2023
;
Onishi and Fu 2024a
;
Onishi and Fu 2024b
;
Verma and Queiroz 2024
;
Kruchkov and Ryu 2025
;
Shinada and Nagaosa 2025
;
Ji et al. 2025
.

Despite their utility in capturing various aspects of quantum matter
Yu et al. 2024
, these sum rules mostly involve the electric conductivity tensor and its integrals over frequencies,
\int_{0}^{+\infty}\omega^{n}\sigma^{\mu\nu}(\omega)d\omega
. As a result, this framework has been essentially confined to properties related to electric transport, or, from the perspective of Kubo’s linear response theory
Kubo 1957
, to electric current-current correlation functions
\langle\hat{J}^{\mu}_{e}\hat{J}^{\nu}_{e}\rangle
. A natural extension would be to explore a broader class of sum rules that encompass transport phenomena of a different nature, such as thermal transport
Cooper et al. 1997
;
Qin et al. 2011
;
Auerbach and Bhattacharyya 2024
. This direction is particularly compelling in systems hosting neutral collective excitations, such as spin waves (magnons) or propagating Majorana modes in spin systems
Katsura et al. 2010
;
Matsumoto and Murakami 2011a
;
Matsumoto and Murakami 2011b
;
Nasu et al. 2017
. In this context, the relevant correlation functions become
\langle\hat{J}_{e}^{\mu}\hat{J}_{Q}^{\nu}\rangle
(electric-heat current correlations) and
\langle\hat{J}_{Q}^{\mu}\hat{J}_{Q}^{\nu}\rangle
(heat-heat current correlations), where
\hat{J}_{Q}
denotes the heat current. Identifying which physical quantities are encoded in sum rules built from thermoelectric correlations remains an intriguing challenge, offering rich opportunities from both fundamental and applied perspectives.

Exploring generalized sum rules associated with thermal transport and probes naturally brings us to the notions of orbital and heat magnetizations. These fundamental ground-state properties are central to connecting thermal transport coefficients with Kubo-type correlation functions
Cooper et al. 1997
;
Qin et al. 2011
;
Auerbach and Bhattacharyya 2024
. In Bloch-band systems, such magnetizations admit a clear interpretation in terms of electric or heat magnetic moments – arising from the self-rotation of electronic wave packets – and circulating edge currents
Xiao et al. 2005
;
Thonhauser et al. 2005
;
Ceresoli et al. 2006
;
Thonhauser 2011
;
Qin et al. 2011
. Specific physical contributions to these magnetizations can be formulated as sum rules involving the electric conductivity tensor
Kubo 1957
;
Souza and Vanderbilt 2008
. For example, the self-rotation component of orbital magnetization can be obtained via the dichroic

f
-sum rule
Oppeneer 1998
;
Verma and Queiroz 2024
;
Souza and Vanderbilt 2008
;
Ji et al. 2025
. Yet, how the total orbital and heat magnetizations emerge from sum rules, and how their individual contributions can be systematically accessed through thermoelectric dichroic measurements, has been largely overlooked. Addressing this gap constitutes a primary motivation for this work.

In this work, we establish a unified framework that connects ground-state topological and magnetization properties to experimentally accessible excitation spectra. By analyzing the zero-temperature limit of transport coefficients, and making use of the Kramers–Kronig relations, we demonstrate that orbital and heat magnetization densities admit spectral representations derived from thermoelectric and thermal correlation functions. Building on this insight, we propose that frequency-integrated excitation rates under suitably engineered chiral drives serve as powerful probes for extracting Kubo-type coefficients, thereby enabling experimental access to the full set of ground-state properties. In particular, while purely electrical drives yield the topological Chern number of an insulating state (i.e. its quantized Hall response), mixed and purely thermal drives reveal the orbital and heat magnetization densities, placing these quantities on equal footing within integrated dichroic spectra. Moreover, we discuss how physically distinct contributions to the orbital and heat magnetizations can be identified by combining a proper set of sum rules. We present a generalization of this partitioning through a hierarchical construction, which organizes generalized (higher-order) magnetizations according to sum rules. We illustrate our results on realistic models, and describe how this thermoelectric dichroic approach can be implemented in quantum engineered settings, such as cold atoms in optical lattices.

II
Transport coefficients, sum rules and dichroic measurements: a brief illustration

We first motivate our approach by illustrating the interconnections between sum rules for transport coefficients and dichroic measurements, which form the foundation of the generalized sum rules investigated in this work; see also Refs.
Bennett and Stern 1965
;
Souza and Vanderbilt 2008
;
Tran et al. 2017
;
Goldman and Ozawa 2024
.

Let us consider a two-dimensional insulator subjected to a circularly polarized electric field,
\bm{E}_{\pm}\!=\!2E_{0}(\cos(\omega t),\pm\sin(\omega t),0)
. In the dipolar approximation, one can include this perturbation in the Hamiltonian through a vector potential
\bm{A}_{\pm}\!=\!(2E_{0}/\omega)(\sin(\omega t),\mp\cos(\omega t),0)
minimally coupled to the electric current operator,

\displaystyle\delta\hat{\mathcal{H}}^{\pm}

\displaystyle=\frac{2E_{0}}{\omega}\left[\sin(\omega t)\hat{J}^{x}_{e}\mp\cos(\omega t)\hat{J}^{y}_{e}\right],

(1)

where
\hat{J}_{e}^{\mu}\!=\!-e\hat{v}^{\mu}
denotes the components of the single-particle electric current operator. Following Bennett and Stern
Bennett and Stern 1965
, the power absorbed
\mathcal{P}^{\pm}(\omega)
by an isotropic system of area
A
subjected to the circularly polarized electric field
\bm{E}_{\pm}
can be expressed in terms of the absorptive part of the optical conductivity tensor,
\sigma^{{\rm abs}}(\omega)=(\sigma(\omega)+\sigma^{\dagger}(\omega))/2
, according to

\mathcal{P}^{\pm}(\omega)\!=\!4E_{0}^{2}A\left({\rm Re}\left[\sigma^{{\rm abs},xx}(\omega)\right]\mp{\rm Im}\left[\sigma^{{\rm abs},xy}(\omega)\right]\right),

(2)

where
{\rm Re}/{\rm Im}
denote real/imaginary parts. Hence, the absorptive part of the conductivity tensor can be related to the differential power absorbed,
\Delta\mathcal{P}(\omega)\!=\!(\mathcal{P}^{+}(\omega)-\mathcal{P}^{-}(\omega))/2
, through the useful relation

\displaystyle{\rm Im}\left[\sigma^{{\rm abs},xy}(\omega)\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{\Delta\mathcal{P}(\omega)}{4AE_{0}^{2}}=-\frac{\hbar\omega\Delta\Gamma(\omega)}{4AE_{0}^{2}}.

(3)

This dissipative part of the conductivity tensor thus reflects the circular dichroism of the medium. For later purposes, we express the power absorbed in terms of an excitation rate,

\Gamma^{\pm}(\omega)=\mathcal{P}^{\pm}(\omega)/\hbar\omega,

(4)

which reflects the rate at which a photon
\hbar\omega
is absorbed by the system, and we introduced the corresponding differential rate
\Delta\Gamma\!=\!(\Gamma^{+}-\Gamma^{-})/2
in Eq. (
3
).

We now apply Kramers-Kronig relations
Bennett and Stern 1965
to the imaginary part of the conductivity tensor in Eq. (
3
),

\displaystyle\sigma^{xy}

\displaystyle=\underset{\omega\to 0}{\lim}{\rm Re}[\sigma^{xy}(\omega)]=\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{{\rm Im}[\sigma^{{\rm abs},xy}(\omega)]}{\omega}d\omega

\displaystyle=-\frac{\hbar}{2\pi E_{0}^{2}A}\Delta\Gamma^{{\rm int}}\,,

(5)

where we introduced the integrated differential rate

\Delta\Gamma^{{\rm int}}\!\equiv\!(1/2)\int_{0}^{\infty}\Gamma^{+}(\omega)-\Gamma^{-}(\omega)d\omega.

(6)

The sum rule in Eq. (
5
) provides an explicit relation between the DC Hall conductivity
\sigma^{xy}
and the circular dichroism of the medium, captured by the differential rates
\Delta\Gamma\!=\!(\Gamma^{+}-\Gamma^{-})/2
.

For a two-dimensional Chern insulator, the Hall conductivity is quantized according to the many-body Chern number
Niu et al. 1985
, such that the integrated differential rate
\Delta\Gamma^{{\rm int}}
in Eq. (
5
) is quantized
Tran et al. 2017
;
Repellin and Goldman 2019
;
Goldman and Ozawa 2024
. This quantized dissipative effect was first observed in an ultracold atomic gas
Asteria et al. 2019
. For a wave packet prepared in a Bloch band, this circular-dichroic approach gives access to the local Berry curvature of the band
Tran et al. 2017
;
Schüler et al. 2020
, as was experimentally demonstrated in Refs.
Beaulieu et al. 2024
;
Bac et al. 2025
.

The excitation rates
\Gamma^{\pm}(\omega)
can be explicitly evaluated within the framework of Fermi’s golden rule
Tran et al. 2017
;
Goldman and Ozawa 2024
, considering the time-dependent perturbation in Eq. (
1
)

\displaystyle\Gamma^{\pm}(\omega)\!=\!\frac{2\pi}{\hbar}\sum_{n\in\textrm{occ}}\,\sum_{m\in\textrm{unocc}}\,|\mathcal{V}_{mn}^{\pm}|^{2}\,\delta(\varepsilon_{m}\!-\!\varepsilon_{n}\!-\!\hbar\omega),

(7)

\displaystyle|\mathcal{V}_{mn}^{\pm}|^{2}=\left(E_{0}/\hbar\omega\right)^{2}\bigg|\bigg\langle m\bigg|\hat{J}^{x}_{e}\!\pm\!i\hat{J}^{y}_{e}\bigg|n\bigg\rangle\bigg|^{2},

where
\{|n\rangle\}
(resp.
\{|m\rangle\}
) denotes the set of occupied (resp. empty) single-particle eigenstates of energy
\varepsilon_{n}
(resp.
\varepsilon_{m}
). By substituting the expression (
7
) into the sum rule given in Eq. (
5
), one recovers Kubo’s formula for the Hall conductivity, expressed as a current-current correlation function
Kubo 1957
:

\sigma^{xy}=\frac{1}{iA}\sum_{n\in\textrm{occ}}\,\sum_{m\in\textrm{unocc}}\frac{\langle n|\hat{J}^{x}_{e}|m\rangle\langle m|\hat{J}^{y}_{e}|n\rangle-(x\leftrightarrow y)}{(\varepsilon_{m}\!-\!\varepsilon_{n})^{2}}.

(8)

This example illustrates how Kubo-type responses, linked to ground-state transport properties, can be connected to a dissipative dichroic response and experimentally identified by monitoring the excitation rates
\Gamma_{\pm}(\omega)
– or power absorbed
\mathcal{P}^{\pm}(\omega)
– under external driving.

We close this introductory section by remarking that the circular perturbation in Eq. (
1
) can be equally introduced through a direct coupling to the electric polarization,

\displaystyle\delta\hat{\mathcal{H}}^{\pm}

\displaystyle=2E_{0}\left[\cos(\omega t)\hat{P}^{x}_{e}\pm\sin(\omega t)\hat{P}^{x}_{e}\right],

(9)

where
\hat{P}^{\mu}_{e}\!=\!-e\,\hat{r}^{\mu}
denotes the components of the single-particle electric polarization operator. This circular drive can be naturally realized in neutral atomic gases by periodically shaking the lattice
Struck et al. 2011
;
Jotzu et al. 2014
. In these engineered settings, the resulting excitation rates can be extracted from band-mapping images, which reveal the redistribution of atoms among the Bloch bands under driving
Asteria et al. 2019
.

III
Thermoelectric transport coefficients and sum rules

Linear response theory provides a systematic way to express electrical and thermal transport coefficients in terms of equilibrium correlation functions. Within this framework, the DC electric (
\bm{J}_{e}
) and heat (
\bm{J}_{Q}
) currents are related to applied static electric fields
\bm{E}
and temperature gradients
-\bm{\nabla}T
via

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\bm{J}_{e}\\
\bm{J}_{Q}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\sigma&\alpha_{\mathrm{th}}\\
T\,\overline{\alpha}_{\mathrm{th}}&\kappa\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\bm{E}\\
-\bm{\nabla}T\end{pmatrix}.

(10)

Here
\sigma
denotes the electrical conductivity tensor,

\alpha_{\mathrm{th}}
and
\overline{\alpha}_{\mathrm{th}}
the thermoelectric tensors leading to Seebeck and Peltier effects, and
\kappa
the thermal conductivity tensor. Following Refs.
Cooper et al. 1997
;
Qin et al. 2011
;
Auerbach and Bhattacharyya 2024
, these transport coefficients can be obtained from the zero-frequency limits of the corresponding Kubo correlation functions as:

\displaystyle\sigma

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{L}_{ee}^{\mathrm{DC}}

\displaystyle\alpha_{\mathrm{th}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\left(\mathbb{L}_{eQ}^{\mathrm{DC}}-c\,\bm{\mathcal{M}}\times\right)

\displaystyle\overline{\alpha}_{\mathrm{th}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\left(\mathbb{L}_{Qe}^{\mathrm{DC}}-c\,\bm{\mathcal{M}}\times\right)

\displaystyle\kappa

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\left(\mathbb{L}_{QQ}^{\mathrm{DC}}-2\,\bm{\mathcal{M}}^{Q}\times\right),

(11)

where
\bm{\mathcal{M}}
and
\bm{\mathcal{M}}^{Q}
denote the orbital and heat magnetization densities of the system. In these expressions,
\bm{\mathcal{M}}\times
represents the linear operator associated with the cross product by
\bm{\mathcal{M}}
; in components
(\mathcal{M}\times)_{\mu\nu}=\epsilon_{\mu\rho\nu}\mathcal{M}_{\rho}
, and similarly for
\bm{\mathcal{M}}^{Q}\times
.

The DC limit of the Kubo matrices is defined as

\mathbb{L}_{\alpha\beta}^{\mathrm{DC}}=\lim_{\omega+i\epsilon\to 0}\lim_{A\to\infty}\mathrm{Re}[\mathbb{L}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)],

(12)

where the finite-frequency correlation functions are defined as
Auerbach and Bhattacharyya 2024

\mathbb{L}_{\alpha\beta}^{\mu\nu}(\omega)=-\frac{i}{\hbar A}\sum_{nm}\frac{\langle n|\hat{J}_{\alpha}^{\mu}|m\rangle\langle m|\hat{J}_{\beta}^{\nu}|n\rangle}{\omega+i\epsilon+(\omega_{n}-\omega_{m})}\frac{f_{n}-f_{m}}{\omega_{n}-\omega_{m}}.

(13)

For simplicity, we restrict the discussion to non-interacting fermionic systems, where
|n\rangle
are single-particle eigenstates of the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
with energies
\varepsilon_{n}=\hbar\omega_{n}
and
f_{n}
denotes the Fermi–Dirac occupation. Spatial indices
\mu,\nu\in{x,y}
label Cartesian components of the current operators
\hat{\bm{J}}_{\alpha}
, while the indices

\alpha,\beta=e,Q,

(14)

distinguish between electric and heat currents. We note that the electric-electric response tensor coincides with the optical conductivity introduced in Sec.
II
, namely
\mathbb{L}^{\mu\nu}_{ee}(\omega)\equiv\sigma^{\mu\nu}(\omega)
.

The subtractions involving orbital and heat magnetizations in Eq. (
11
) ensure that the transport coefficients describe the response of true transport currents to external fields, excluding contributions from circulating magnetizations
Qin et al. 2011
;
Auerbach and Bhattacharyya 2024
;
Smrcka and Streda 1977
;
Cooper et al. 1997
. As we will show in the following, the Kubo approach provides a natural starting point for deriving exact frequency-integrated constraints, or
sum rules
, which follow from causality and the Kramers–Kronig relations.

The current operators
\hat{\bm{J}}_{\alpha}
can be expressed as time derivatives of the corresponding polarization operators
\hat{\bm{P}}_{\alpha}
,

\hat{J}_{\alpha}^{\mu}=\frac{i}{\hbar}[\hat{H},\hat{P}_{\alpha}^{\mu}],

(15)

which provides a convenient framework for formulating response functions in terms of polarization matrix elements. Under open boundary conditions, the one-body polarization operators are well-defined and take the real-space forms

\displaystyle\hat{P}_{e}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=-e\,\hat{r}^{\mu},

(16a)

\displaystyle\hat{P}_{Q}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\!\left\{\hat{r}^{\mu},\hat{H}-\mu\right\},

(16b)

corresponding to electric and heat polarizations, respectively.

We will focus on insulating states, for which only interband matrix elements (
n\neq m
) contribute to the Kubo response in Eq. (
13
).
Using the relation
\langle n|\hat{J}_{\alpha}^{\mu}|m\rangle=i\omega_{nm}\langle n|\hat{P}_{\alpha}^{\mu}|m\rangle
, the frequency-resolved correlation functions can then be written as

\mathbb{L}_{\alpha\beta}^{\mu\nu}(\omega)=\frac{i}{\hbar A}\sum_{n\neq m}f_{nm}\omega_{mn}\frac{\langle n|\hat{P}^{\mu}_{\alpha}|m\rangle\langle m|\hat{P}^{\nu}_{\beta}|n\rangle}{\omega-\omega_{mn}+i\epsilon},

(17)

where
\omega_{nm}=\omega_{n}-\omega_{m}
and
f_{nm}=f_{n}-f_{m}
. The product of matrix elements of the polarization operators in Eq. (
17
) can be further decomposed into symmetric and antisymmetric parts as

\langle n|\hat{P}^{\mu}_{\alpha}|m\rangle\langle m|\hat{P}^{\nu}_{\beta}|n\rangle=P^{+,\mu\nu}_{\alpha\beta,nm}+i\frac{P_{\alpha\beta,nm}^{-,\mu\nu}}{2},

(18)

with

\displaystyle P^{+,\mu\nu}_{\alpha\beta,nm}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}\left[\langle n|\hat{P}^{\mu}_{\alpha}|m\rangle\langle m|\hat{P}^{\nu}_{\beta}|n\rangle\right]

\displaystyle P^{-,\mu\nu}_{\alpha\beta,nm}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\mathrm{Im}\left[\langle n|\hat{P}^{\mu}_{\alpha}|m\rangle\langle m|\hat{P}^{\nu}_{\beta}|n\rangle\right].

(19)

We note that the
+
(
-
) part is symmetric (antisymmetric) in both the indices
m\leftrightarrow n
and
\mu\leftrightarrow\nu
.
Using the Sokhotski–Plemelj identity, one obtains explicit expressions for the real and imaginary parts of the Kubo correlators in Eq. (
17
):

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}\left[\mathbb{L}_{\alpha\beta}^{\mu\nu}(\omega)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\hbar A}\sum_{n\neq m}f_{nm}\omega_{mn}\left(2\pi P^{+,\mu\nu}_{\alpha\beta,nm}\delta(\omega-\omega_{mn})-P^{-,\mu\nu}_{\alpha\beta,nm}\,\mathrm{p.v.}\left[\frac{1}{\omega-\omega_{mn}}\right]\right)\,,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Im}\left[\mathbb{L}_{\alpha\beta}^{\mu\nu}(\omega)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\hbar A}\sum_{n\neq m}f_{nm}\omega_{mn}\left(\pi P^{-,\mu\nu}_{\alpha\beta,nm}\delta(\omega-\omega_{mn})+2P^{+,\mu\nu}_{\alpha\beta,nm}\,\mathrm{p.v.}\left[\frac{1}{\omega-\omega_{mn}}\right]\right)\,,

(20)

where
\mathrm{p.v.}
denotes the principal value.

To isolate the bulk contribution and preserve the insulating character free from boundary effects, it is convenient to consider crystalline systems with periodic boundary conditions. In this case, the response can be evaluated in the Bloch basis
|u_{n\bm{k}}\rangle
, with
\bm{k}
the crystal quasimomentum and where
n
now represents a band index. In this representation, the off-diagonal matrix elements of the polarization operators are well-defined and given by

\displaystyle\langle u_{n\bm{k}}|\hat{P}_{e}^{\mu}|u_{m\bm{k}}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-ie\langle u_{n\bm{k}}|\partial_{k_{\mu}}u_{m\bm{k}}\rangle,

(21)

\displaystyle\langle u_{n\bm{k}}|\hat{P}_{Q}^{\mu}|u_{m\bm{k}}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{i}{2}(\varepsilon_{n\bm{k}}+\varepsilon_{m\bm{k}}-2\mu)\langle u_{n\bm{k}}|\partial_{k_{\mu}}u_{m\bm{k}}\rangle,

highlighting their direct connection to the geometric structure of the Bloch wavefunctions.

From Eqs. (
12
) and (
20
), one finds that the DC limit of the Kubo correlation functions is governed solely by the spatially antisymmetric component and can be written as

\displaystyle\mathbb{L}_{\alpha\beta}^{\mathrm{DC},\mu\nu}\!\!

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\!\mathrm{Re}\left[\mathbb{L}_{\alpha\beta}^{\mu\nu}(0)\right]\!\!=\!\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!d\omega\frac{\mathrm{Im}\left[\mathbb{L}_{\alpha\beta}^{\mu\nu}(\omega)\right]}{\omega}\,,

(22)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\!\frac{1}{\hbar}\!\!\sum_{n\neq m}\!\int\!\!\!\frac{d^{2}k}{(2\pi)^{2}}\!\!\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\!\!d\omega f_{n}P^{-,\mu\nu}_{\alpha\beta,nm}(\bm{k})\delta(\omega-\omega_{mn}(\bm{k})),

where

P^{-,\mu\nu}_{\alpha\beta,nm}(\bm{k})=2\mathrm{Im}\left[\langle u_{n\bm{k}}|\hat{P}^{\mu}_{\alpha}|u_{m\bm{k}}\rangle\langle u_{m\bm{k}}|\hat{P}^{\nu}_{\beta}|u_{n\bm{k}}\rangle\right].

(23)

The spectral sum rules in Eq. (
22
) directly follow from the Kramers–Kronig relations
Bennett and Stern 1965
and the odd-frequency property
\mathrm{Im}\left[\mathbb{L}_{\alpha\beta}^{\mu\nu}(\omega)\right]=-\mathrm{Im}\left[\mathbb{L}_{\alpha\beta}^{\mu\nu}(-\omega)\right]
. Importantly, these sum rules impose integral constraints on the system’s absorption spectrum.

Using Eqs. (
21
)-(
23
), and taking the zero-temperature limit, the DC Kubo coefficients in the insulating ground state can be compactly written as

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!d\omega\frac{\mathrm{Im}\left[\mathbb{L}_{ee}^{\mu\nu}(\omega)\right]}{\omega}=\mathbb{L}_{ee}^{\mathrm{DC},\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=-\frac{e^{2}}{h}\,\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho}C_{\rho},

(24a)

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!d\omega\frac{\mathrm{Im}\left[\mathbb{L}_{eQ}^{\mu\nu}(\omega)\right]}{\omega}=\mathbb{L}_{eQ}^{\mathrm{DC},\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=-c\,\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho}\mathcal{M}_{\rho},

(24b)

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!d\omega\frac{\mathrm{Im}\left[\mathbb{L}_{QQ}^{\mu\nu}(\omega)\right]}{\omega}=\mathbb{L}_{QQ}^{\mathrm{DC},\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=-2\,\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho}\mathcal{M}^{Q}_{\rho},

(24c)

where
C_{\rho}
,
\mathcal{M}_{\rho}
and
\mathcal{M}_{\rho}^{Q}
denote, respectively, the ground-state Chern number, and the zero-temperature orbital and heat magnetization densities. These quantities are given by the following
\bm{k}
-space integrals
Xiao et al. 2005
;
Ceresoli et al. 2006
;
Shi et al. 2007
;
Matsumoto and Murakami 2011a
;
Qin et al. 2011

\displaystyle C_{\rho}

\displaystyle=\frac{i\epsilon_{\rho\mu\nu}}{2\pi}\int d^{2}k\sum_{n\in\mathrm{occ}}\langle\partial_{k_{\mu}}u_{n\bm{k}}|\partial_{k_{\nu}}u_{n\bm{k}}\rangle,

(25a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\rho}

\displaystyle=\!\frac{\epsilon_{\rho\mu\nu}}{2i\Phi_{0}}\!\int\!\!\frac{d^{2}k}{2\pi}\!\!\!\sum_{n\in\mathrm{occ}}\langle\partial_{k_{\mu}}u_{n\bm{k}}|\hat{H}_{\bm{k}}+\varepsilon_{n\bm{k}}-2\mu|\partial_{k_{\nu}}u_{n\bm{k}}\rangle,

(25b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{Q}_{\rho}

\displaystyle=\!\frac{i\epsilon_{\rho\mu\nu}}{2\hbar}\!\!\!\int\!\!\!\frac{d^{2}k}{(2\pi)^{2}}\!\!\!\!\sum_{n\in\mathrm{occ}}\!\langle\partial_{k_{\mu}}u_{n\bm{k}}|\left(\!\!\frac{\hat{H}_{\bm{k}}\!+\!\varepsilon_{n\bm{k}}\!-\!2\mu}{2}\!\!\right)^{2}\!\!\!|\partial_{k_{\nu}}u_{n\bm{k}}\rangle,

(25c)

where
\Phi_{0}\!=\!hc/e
denotes the flux quantum.

Equations (
11
) and (
24
) make explicit that all thermoelectric and thermal transport coefficients vanish in the zero-temperature limit (
\alpha^{\mu\nu}=\overline{\alpha}^{\mu\nu}=\kappa^{\mu\nu}=0
), consistent with the absence of entropy and heat transport required by the third law of thermodynamics. The magnetization subtractions in Eq. (
11
) are essential to ensure this behavior and to eliminate unphysical divergences that would otherwise appear in this limit. The only transport response that can remain finite at zero temperature is the electrical Hall conductivity,
\sigma^{\mu\nu}=-e^{2}\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho}C_{\rho}/h
, which remains topologically quantized according to the ground-state Chern number
Niu et al. 1985
.

Interestingly, the Kramers–Kronig relations in Eq. (
24
) imply that all the ground-state quantities in Eq. (
25
) admit a spectral representation, establishing a direct link between static ground-state properties and the system’s excitation spectrum. While this connection is well-established for the Chern number
Tran et al. 2017
;
Goldman and Ozawa 2024
, the fact that the
full
orbital and heat magnetization densities can also be obtained from thermoelectric and thermal correlation functions has been largely overlooked.

For later use, it is convenient to introduce the absorptive (Hermitian) part of the Kubo correlation functions:

\displaystyle\mathbb{L}^{\mathrm{abs},\mu\nu}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)\!

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbb{L}^{\mu\nu}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)+\mathbb{L}^{\nu\mu*}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)\right)

(26)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\frac{\pi}{\hbar}\!\!\int\!\!\frac{d^{2}k}{(2\pi)^{2}}\!\!\sum_{n\neq m}f_{nm}(\bm{k})\omega_{mn}(\bm{k})\delta(\omega-\omega_{mn}(\bm{k}))

\displaystyle\!\times\left(P_{\alpha\beta,nm}^{+,\mu\nu}(\bm{k})+i\frac{P_{\alpha\beta,nm}^{-,\mu\nu}(\bm{k})}{2}\right)\,.

This quantity represents the component of the response that is genuinely associated with optical absorption. Therefore, it is the quantity that naturally appears in sum rules and experimentally observed transition rates. In particular, we note that the Kramers-Kronig relations in Eqs. (
22
) and (
24
) can equivalently be expressed in terms of
\mathrm{Im}[\mathbb{L}_{\alpha\beta}^{\mathrm{abs},\mu\nu}(\omega)]
.

Interestingly, the sum rules in Eqs. (
22
) and (
24
) also admit a real-part analogue. In the zero-temperature limit, this reads

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\!d\omega\frac{\mathrm{Re}\left[\mathbb{L}_{\alpha\beta}^{\mathrm{abs},\mu\nu}(\omega)\right]}{\omega}\!

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\frac{\pi}{\hbar}\!\int\!\!\frac{d^{2}k}{(2\pi)^{2}}\!\!\!\sum_{n\in\textrm{occ}}\sum_{m\in\textrm{unocc}}\!\!\!\!P^{+,\mu\nu}_{\alpha\beta,nm}(\bm{k})

(27)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{\pi}{\hbar}\int\!\!\frac{d^{2}k}{(2\pi)^{2}}g_{\alpha\beta}^{\mu\nu}(\bm{k}).

This expression isolates the real components of the matrix elements in Eq. (
18
), thereby defining the symmetric tensors
g_{\alpha\beta}(\bm{k})
. One readily recognizes the well-known Souza-Wilkens-Martin sum rule
Souza et al. 2000
by taking
\alpha\!=\!\beta\!=\!e
in Eq. (
27
), and noting that
g_{ee}^{\mu\nu}(\bm{k})\!=\!e^{2}g^{\mu\nu}(\bm{k})
, with
g^{\mu\nu}(\bm{k})
the quantum metric associated with the occupied Bloch bands
Kolodrubetz et al. 2017
;
Yu et al. 2024
;
Ozawa and Goldman 2019
.

Furthermore, we note that Eq. (
27
) is directly related to the quadratic quantum fluctuations of the corresponding polarization operators in the ground state,

\int\frac{d^{2}k}{(2\pi)^{2}}g_{\alpha\beta}^{\mu\nu}(\bm{k})\!=\!\frac{1}{A}\!\left(\!\frac{\langle\hat{P}_{\alpha}^{\mu}\hat{P}_{\beta}^{\nu}+\hat{P}_{\beta}^{\nu}\hat{P}_{\alpha}^{\mu}\rangle_{0}}{2}-\langle\hat{P}_{\alpha}^{\mu}\rangle_{0}\langle\hat{P}_{\beta}^{\nu}\rangle_{0}\!\right),

(28)

where
\langle\dots\rangle_{0}
denotes the ground-state expectation value. We remark that the symmetric tensor

\displaystyle g_{QQ}^{\mu\nu}(\bm{k})\!

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\sum_{n\in\textrm{occ}}\,\sum_{m\textrm{unocc}}\mathrm{Re}\left[\langle u_{n\bm{k}}|\hat{P}_{Q}^{\mu}|u_{m\bm{k}}\rangle\langle u_{m\bm{k}}|\hat{P}_{Q}^{\nu}|u_{n\bm{k}}\rangle\right]

(29)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\sum_{n\in\textrm{occ}}\,\sum_{m\textrm{unocc}}\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{n\bm{k}}+\varepsilon_{m\bm{k}}-2\mu}{2}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\times\mathrm{Re}\left[\langle\partial_{k_{\mu}}u_{n\bm{k}}|u_{m\bm{k}}\rangle\langle u_{m\bm{k}}|\partial_{k_{\nu}}u_{n\bm{k}}\rangle\right],

is semi-positive defined with respect to the scalar product in the Cartesian indices. The quantity
g_{QQ}^{\mu\nu}
can thus be identified as a “heat quantum metric”.

Relation to the dichroic
f
-sum rule

Dichroic approaches entirely based on the electrical conductivity tensor, such as the dichroic
f
-sum rule
Oppeneer 1998
;
Verma and Queiroz 2024
;
Souza and Vanderbilt 2008
, only capture
partial
contributions to the orbital magnetization, missing relevant terms that arise naturally from thermoelectric and thermal correlations. Concretely, the dichroic
f
-sum rule
Oppeneer 1998
;
Verma and Queiroz 2024
;
Souza and Vanderbilt 2008
takes the form

\int_{0}^{\infty}\!d\omega\,\mathrm{Im}\,\left[\mathbb{L}_{ee}^{\mathrm{abs},\mu\nu}(\omega)\right]=\epsilon_{\mu\nu\rho}\frac{\pi ec}{\hbar}\mathcal{M}_{\rho}^{{\rm SR}},

(30)

where the ground-state property

\mathcal{M}^{{\rm SR}}_{\rho}=\frac{1}{2i\Phi_{0}}\epsilon_{\rho\mu\nu}\!\!\int\!\!\frac{d^{2}k}{2\pi}\!\!\sum_{n\in\mathrm{occ}}\braket{\frac{\partial u_{n\bm{k}}}{\partial k_{\mu}}|\hat{H}_{\bm{k}}-\varepsilon_{n\bm{k}}|\frac{\partial u_{n\bm{k}}}{\partial k_{\nu}}}\,,

(31)

accounts for the intrinsic magnetic-moment (or
self-rotation
) contribution of the Bloch states to the orbital magnetization
Chang and Niu 1996
;
Xiao et al. 2005
;
Xiao et al. 2010
.

It is well known that the total orbital magnetization of Bloch bands can be expressed in terms of two contributions
Chang and Niu 1996
;
Xiao et al. 2010
;
Matsumoto and Murakami 2011a
,

\bm{\mathcal{M}}\!=\!\bm{\mathcal{M}}^{{\rm SR}}+\bm{\mathcal{M}}^{{\rm COM}},

(32)

where the
center-of-mass
contribution

\mathcal{M}_{\rho}^{{\rm COM}}=-\frac{1}{\Phi_{0}}\int\frac{d^{2}k}{2\pi}\sum_{n\in\mathrm{occ}}\left(\varepsilon_{n\bm{k}}-\mu\right)\Omega_{n\bm{k}}^{\rho}\,,

(33)

involves the Berry curvature of the occupied bands

\Omega_{n\bm{k}}^{\rho}=i\epsilon_{\rho\mu\nu}\braket{\frac{\partial u_{n\bm{k}}}{\partial k_{\mu}}|\frac{\partial u_{n\bm{k}}}{\partial k_{\nu}}}.

(34)

We thus conclude that this center-of-mass contribution to the orbital magnetization, which is absent in the dichroic
f
-sum rule in Eq. (
30
), is captured by thermoelectric correlations in the generalized sum rule (
24b
).

As a corollary, we conclude that the two distinct contributions to the orbital magnetization in Eq. (
32
) can be individually measured, by combining the two sum rules in Eqs. (
30
) and (
24b
). A similar conclusion can be reached for the heat magnetization, as we further discuss in Section
VI
.

In the following Section
IV
, we will show how the absorptive part of the frequency-resolved Kubo response functions can be accessed experimentally by monitoring excitation rates under suitably engineered thermoelectric probes, providing a direct and practical route to extract the full set of ground-state properties discussed above.

IV
Thermoelectric dichroism and spectral sum rules from integrated differential rates

In this Section, we show that the dichroic approach introduced in Refs.
Tran et al. 2017
;
Repellin and Goldman 2019
;
Asteria et al. 2019
;
Goldman and Ozawa 2024
and reminded in Section
II
– originally proposed to extract the many-body Chern number of an insulator by monitoring Bloch-band excitation rates under a circular drive – can be naturally extended to access the full equilibrium orbital and heat magnetization densities.

We consider a crystalline lattice subjected to a chiral thermoelectric drive of the form

\delta\hat{H}_{\alpha\beta}^{\pm,\mu\nu}(t)=\frac{2}{\omega}\left(V_{0\alpha}\sin(\omega t)\hat{J}_{\alpha}^{\mu}\mp V_{0\beta}\cos(\omega t)\hat{J}^{\nu}_{\beta}\right),

(35)

where the dimensional prefactors
V_{0\alpha}
are chosen so that lowest-order time-dependent perturbation theory remains applicable. As in Section
III
, the indices
\alpha,\beta\!=\!e,Q
specify the nature of the thermoelectric components entering the probe, hence providing a direct generalization of the electric probe in Eq. (
1
). Here
V_{0e}
carries units of electric field, while
V_{0Q}
carries units of inverse length.

Following Fermi’s golden rule, and considering a fermionic system at zero temperature, the excitation rate from occupied to unoccupied Bloch states for a driving frequency
\omega
can be obtained as

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\alpha\beta}^{\pm,\mu\nu}(\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2\pi A}{\hbar^{2}}\!\!\int\!\!\frac{d^{2}k}{(2\pi)^{2}}\!\!\sum_{n\in\textrm{occ}}\,\sum_{m\in\textrm{unocc}}\!\!\frac{\delta(\omega-\omega_{mn}(\bm{k}))}{\omega^{2}}

(36)

\displaystyle\times|\langle u_{m\bm{k}}|V_{0\alpha}\hat{J}_{\alpha}^{\mu}\pm iV_{0\beta}\hat{J}_{\beta}^{\nu}|u_{n\bm{k}}\rangle|^{2}.

The difference between excitation rates obtained for right (

\Delta\Gamma_{\alpha\beta}^{\mu\nu}(\omega)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\Gamma_{\alpha\beta}^{+,\mu\nu}(\omega)-\Gamma_{\alpha\beta}^{-,\mu\nu}(\omega)\right),

(37)

which evaluates to

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta\Gamma^{\mu\nu}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)}{A}\!

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!-\frac{2\pi}{\hbar^{2}}\!\!\int\!\!\frac{d^{2}k}{(2\pi)^{2}}\!\!\sum_{n\in\textrm{occ}}\,\sum_{m\in\textrm{unocc}}\!\!\!V_{0\alpha}V_{0\beta}

(38)

\displaystyle\times P_{\alpha\beta,nm}^{-,\mu\nu}(\bm{k})\delta(\omega-\omega_{mn}(\bm{k})).

We note that, while the frequency-resolved excitation rates
\Gamma_{\alpha\beta}^{\pm,\mu\nu}(\omega)
generally depend on the lattice embedding (i.e. the relative position of orbitals), the differential rates
\Delta\Gamma^{\mu\nu}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)
are independent of the lattice geometry
Simon and Rudner 2020
.

Combining Eq. (
38
) with the definition of the absorptive correlator in Eq. (
26
) yields, for
\omega>0
,

\mathrm{Im}\left[\mathbb{L}_{\alpha\beta}^{\mathrm{abs},\mu\nu}(\omega)\right]=-\frac{\hbar\omega\Delta\Gamma_{\alpha\beta}^{\mu\nu}(\omega)}{4AV_{0\alpha}V_{0\beta}}.

(39)

Equation (
39
), which generalizes Eq. (
3
) to thermoelectric responses, is a remarkable relation: it shows that the imaginary part of the absorptive Kubo tensor can be directly extracted from the frequency-resolved differential rates measured under circularly polarized (chiral) drives.

Combining Eqs. (
22
) and (
38
), one finds a direct correspondence between the integrated differential rates and the DC Kubo coefficients in Eq. (
24
):

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta\Gamma^{\textrm{int},\mu\nu}_{\alpha\beta}}{A}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\frac{\Delta\Gamma^{\mu\nu}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)}{A}

(40)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{2\pi}{\hbar}V_{0\alpha}V_{0\beta}\mathbb{L}_{\alpha\beta}^{\mathrm{DC},\mu\nu},

such that

\displaystyle\Delta\Gamma^{\textrm{int},\mu\nu}_{ee}/A

\displaystyle=V_{0e}^{2}\frac{e^{2}}{\hbar^{2}}\epsilon_{\mu\nu\rho}C_{\rho}\,,

(41a)

\displaystyle\Delta\Gamma^{\textrm{int},\mu\nu}_{eQ}/A

\displaystyle=V_{0e}V_{0Q}\frac{2\pi c}{\hbar}\epsilon_{\mu\nu\rho}\mathcal{M}_{\rho}\,,

(41b)

\displaystyle\Delta\Gamma^{\textrm{int},\mu\nu}_{QQ}/A

\displaystyle=\frac{4\pi}{\hbar}V_{0Q}^{2}\epsilon_{\mu\nu\rho}\mathcal{M}_{\rho}^{Q}.

(41c)

The frequency-integrated differential rates therefore act as direct experimental probes for the corresponding DC Kubo coefficients of insulators, linking measurable excitation asymmetries (i.e. generalized circular dichroism) to intrinsic ground-state properties. In particular, purely electrical drives yield the Chern number, while mixed and purely thermal drives (
eQ
and
QQ
) respectively give access to the full orbital and heat magnetization densities. This establishes a unified framework in which topological and magnetization properties emerge on equal footing from integrated dichroic spectra obtained under appropriately designed chiral drives.

V
Open boundary systems: Local markers and experimental implementations

The formal relations between differential excitation rates and the DC Kubo coefficients derived in Section
IV
rely on periodic boundary conditions, which ensure that the excitation processes involve only bulk transitions between extended Bloch states. In experimentally relevant settings, however, systems necessarily have open boundaries, where localized edge states may appear within the bulk gap and contribute to the excitation rates
Tran et al. 2017
;
Ünal et al. 2024
. Understanding how these boundary contributions modify the dichroic excitation spectra is essential to connect this theoretical formulation with measurable observables.

Real-space formulation and local markers

A natural way to address this problem is to reformulate the chiral thermoelectric driving protocol [Eq. (
35
)] directly in terms of the electric and heat polarization operators

\delta\hat{H}_{\alpha\beta}^{\pm,\mu\nu}(t)=2\left(V_{0\alpha}\cos(\omega t)\hat{P}_{\alpha}^{\mu}\pm V_{0\beta}\sin(\omega t)\hat{P}^{\nu}_{\beta}\right),

(43)

hence generalizing the chiral probe in Eq. (
9
) to the present thermoelectric framework. According to Fermi’s golden rule, the frequency-resolved rates then read

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\alpha\beta}^{\pm,\mu\nu}(\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2\pi}{\hbar^{2}}\sum_{n\in\textrm{occ}}\sum_{m\in\textrm{unocc}}\delta(\omega-\omega_{mn})

(44)

\displaystyle|\langle m|V_{0\alpha}\hat{P}^{\mu}_{\alpha}\pm iV_{0\beta}\hat{P}_{\beta}^{\nu}|n\rangle|^{2},

with the corresponding differential rates yielding

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta\Gamma^{\mu\nu}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)}{V_{0\alpha}V_{0\beta}}\!

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!-\frac{2\pi}{\hbar^{2}}\sum_{n\in\textrm{occ}}\sum_{m\in\textrm{unocc}}P_{\alpha\beta,nm}^{-,\mu\nu}\delta(\omega-\omega_{mn}).

The antisymmetric components
P_{\alpha\beta,nm}^{-,\mu\nu}
are here entirely expressed in their real-space representation, see Eqs. (
16
) and (
19
). The corresponding integrated differential rates take the form

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta\Gamma^{\textrm{int},\mu\nu}_{ee}}{V_{0e}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{2}}{\hbar^{2}}\epsilon_{\mu\nu\rho}\int d^{2}r\,\mathfrak{C}_{\rho}(\bm{r})

(46a)

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta\Gamma^{\textrm{int},\mu\nu}_{eQ}}{V_{0e}V_{0Q}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi c}{\hbar}\epsilon_{\mu\nu\rho}\int d^{2}r\,\mathfrak{M}_{\rho}(\bm{r})

(46b)

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta\Gamma^{\textrm{int},\mu\nu}_{QQ}}{V_{0Q}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{4\pi}{\hbar}\epsilon_{\mu\nu\rho}\int d^{2}r\,\mathfrak{M}^{Q}_{\rho}(\bm{r}),

(46c)

where
\mathfrak{C}_{\rho}(\bm{r})
,
\mathfrak{M}_{\rho}(\bm{r})
and
\mathfrak{M}_{\rho}^{Q}(\bm{r})
denote, respectively, the local markers for the Chern, orbital and heat magnetizations:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}_{\rho}(\bm{r})=

\displaystyle 2\pi i\epsilon_{\rho\mu\nu}\langle\bm{r}|\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{r}^{\mu}\hat{\mathcal{Q}}\hat{r}^{\nu}\hat{\mathcal{P}}|\bm{r}\rangle\,,

(47a)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{M}_{\rho}(\bm{r})=

\displaystyle\frac{\pi\epsilon^{\rho\mu\nu}}{i\Phi_{0}}\langle\bm{r}|\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{r}^{\mu}\hat{\mathcal{Q}}\hat{H}\hat{\mathcal{Q}}\hat{r}^{\nu}\hat{\mathcal{P}}-\hat{\mathcal{Q}}\hat{r}^{\mu}\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{H}\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{r}^{\nu}\hat{\mathcal{Q}}|\bm{r}\rangle+\frac{\mu}{\Phi_{0}}\mathfrak{C}_{\rho}(\bm{r})\,,

(47b)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{M}_{\rho}^{Q}(\bm{r})=

\displaystyle\frac{i\epsilon^{\rho\mu\nu}}{8\hbar}\langle\bm{r}|2\hat{H}\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{r}^{\mu}\hat{\mathcal{Q}}\hat{H}\hat{\mathcal{Q}}\hat{r}^{\nu}\hat{\mathcal{P}}+\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{r}^{\mu}\hat{\mathcal{Q}}\hat{H}^{2}\hat{\mathcal{Q}}\hat{r}^{\nu}\hat{\mathcal{P}}-\hat{\mathcal{Q}}\hat{r}^{\mu}\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{H}^{2}\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{r}^{\nu}\hat{\mathcal{Q}}|\bm{r}\rangle+\mu\frac{c}{e}\mathfrak{M}_{\rho}(\bm{r})-\frac{\mu^{2}}{4\pi\hbar}\mathfrak{C}_{\rho}(\bm{r})\,.

(47c)

Here
\hat{\mathcal{P}}=\sum_{\varepsilon_{n}<\mu}|n\rangle\langle n|
is the ground-state projector and
\hat{\mathcal{Q}}=\hat{\mathcal{I}}-\hat{\mathcal{P}}
its complement. Notably, in this real-space formulation, the familiar markers for the Chern and orbital magnetizations
Bianco and Resta 2011
;
Bianco and Resta 2013
;
Seleznev and Vanderbilt 2023
emerge naturally, together with an analogous expression for the heat magnetization, which, to our knowledge, has not been previously reported. Thanks to the exponential locality of
\hat{\mathcal{P}}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime})
in insulators, the bulk-averaged values of these markers coincide with those on the torus in the thermodynamic limit. Importantly, while the total Chern number vanishes in an open boundary sample
Bianco and Resta 2011
;
Bianco and Resta 2013
;
Tran et al. 2017
;
Tran et al. 2018
– as a direct consequence of the bulk-edge correspondence
Ünal et al. 2024
– the total orbital and heat magnetizations remain finite and correspond to their value on the torus
Wang et al. 2022
.

Implementing dichroic probes in lattice systems

We now discuss how the generalized chiral probes in Eq. (
43
) can be implemented in engineered lattice systems, described by a tight-binding Hamiltonian:

\hat{\mathcal{H}}=\sum_{ij}t_{ij}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{i}\hat{c}_{j}+U_{j}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{j}\hat{c}_{j}\,,

(48)

where
\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{j}
creates a particle at lattice site
j
, where
t_{ij}
denotes the hopping matrix element connecting lattice sites
i
and
j
, and where
U_{j}
denotes an onsite potential. The corresponding electric polarization operator takes the form of a linear gradient potential,

\hat{P}^{\mu}_{e}=-e\sum_{i}r^{\mu}_{i}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{i}\hat{c}_{i}\,,

(49)

while the heat polarization introduced in Eq. (
16
) takes the form of linear “gravitational” potential, or strain,

\hat{P}^{\mu}_{Q}=\sum_{i,j}\frac{r^{\mu}_{i}+r^{\mu}_{j}}{2}t_{ij}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{i}\hat{c}_{j}\,+\sum_{j}(U_{j}-\mu)r_{j}^{\mu}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{j}\hat{c}_{j}\,.

(50)

Hence, the perturbations
\delta\hat{H}_{eQ}^{\pm}(t)
and
\delta\hat{H}_{QQ}^{\pm}(t)
in Eq. (
43
) involve a spatially-dependent time-modulation of the bare hopping amplitudes of the form

t_{ij}\to t_{ij}\left(1+V_{0Q}\left(r^{\mu}_{i}+r^{\mu}_{j}\right)\cos(\omega t)\right),

(51)

where the strength of the drive should satisfy
(1/V_{0Q})\!\gg\sqrt{A}
, in order to monitor excitation rates within the perturbative regime. Such a local and time-dependent control over tunneling matrix elements can be achieved in ultracold atomic gases trapped in optical lattices under a quantum-gas microscope; see Refs.
Impertro et al. 2024
;
Nixon et al. 2024
;
Impertro et al. 2025
. The excitation rates in Eq. (
44
) could then be measured by monitoring the dynamical repopulation of the Bloch bands through band-mapping, as was previously demonstrated in the dichroic measurement of Ref.
Asteria et al. 2019
. We anticipate that similar driving schemes could also be implemented in circuit QED platforms, where high control over tunneling matrix elements can be achieved
Roushan et al. 2017
;
Wang et al. 2024
. In solid-state materials, time-modulated strain could be activated by exciting phononic modes; see the recent Ref.
Li et al. 2025
on phonon dichroisms and their relation to heat magnetic moments.

Finally, we remark that the perturbations
\delta\hat{H}_{eQ}^{\pm}(t)
and
\delta\hat{H}_{QQ}^{\pm}(t)
also potentially involve a time-modulated gradient, through the onsite potential entering the heat polarization
\hat{P}^{\mu}_{Q}
; see Eq. (
50
).

Measuring individual contributions to the orbital magnetization

As previously reminded in Eq. (
32
), the total orbital magnetization can be partitioned in terms of two contributions,
\bm{\mathcal{M}}\!=\!\bm{\mathcal{M}}^{{\rm SR}}+\bm{\mathcal{M}}^{{\rm COM}}
, which can be individually addressed by combining two sum rules; see Section
III
. In practice, one would first extract the total orbital magnetization
\bm{\mathcal{M}}
through the sum rule in Eq. (
46b
), i.e. by subjecting the system to the chiral probe
\delta\hat{H}_{eQ}^{\pm,\mu\nu}(t)
and monitoring the corresponding dichroic response. Then, the self-rotation contribution
\mathcal{M}^{{\rm SR}}
would be extracted by measuring the dichroic response resulting from the conventional chiral probe
\delta\hat{H}_{ee}^{\pm}(t)
, and using the relation

\frac{1}{A(V_{0e})^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\,\omega\,\Delta\Gamma^{\mu\nu}_{ee}(\omega)d\omega=-\epsilon_{\mu\nu\rho}\frac{4\pi ec}{\hbar^{2}}\mathcal{M}_{\rho}^{{\rm SR}},

(52)

which directly derives from the
f
-sum rule in Eq. (
30
) and Eq. (
39
). The center-of-mass contribution would then be obtained through the difference
\bm{\mathcal{M}}^{{\rm COM}}\!=\!\bm{\mathcal{M}}-\bm{\mathcal{M}}^{{\rm SR}}
.

VI
Generalized heat magnetizations and their sum-rule partitions

In Sections
III
-
IV
, we established that the self-rotation contribution to the orbital magnetization can be derived from the conventional dichroic
f
-sum rule, whereas the total orbital magnetization is governed by a distinct sum rule involving a thermoelectric probe. In this Section, we extend this sum-rule partitioning to the heat magnetization and its higher-order generalizations. In particular, we reveal a hierarchical structure of sum rules that generalizes the physical partitioning of the orbital magnetization to heat magnetizations.

Partitioning the heat magnetization

Let us start by partitioning the total heat magnetization as

\bm{\mathcal{M}}^{Q}=\bm{\mathcal{M}}^{Q,1}+\bm{\mathcal{M}}^{Q,2}+\bm{\mathcal{M}}^{Q,3}\,,

(53)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\rho}^{Q,1}

\displaystyle=\!-\frac{i\epsilon_{\rho\mu\nu}}{8\hbar}\!\!\!\int\!\!\!\frac{d^{2}k}{(2\pi)^{2}}\!\!\!\!\sum_{n\in\mathrm{occ}}\!\langle\partial_{k_{\mu}}u_{n\bm{k}}|\left(\!\hat{H}_{\bm{k}}\!-\!\varepsilon_{n\bm{k}}\!\right)^{2}\!\!|\partial_{k_{\nu}}u_{n\bm{k}}\rangle

(54a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\rho}^{Q,2}

\displaystyle=\!\frac{i\epsilon_{\rho\mu\nu}}{2\hbar}\!\!\!\int\!\!\!\frac{d^{2}k}{(2\pi)^{2}}\!\!\!\!\sum_{n\in\mathrm{occ}}\!\langle\partial_{k_{\mu}}u_{n\bm{k}}|\left(\!\!\frac{\hat{H}_{\bm{k}}\!+\!\varepsilon_{n\bm{k}}\!-\!2\mu}{2}\!\!\right)\!\!\!

(54b)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\times\left(\!\hat{H}_{\bm{k}}\!-\!\varepsilon_{n\bm{k}}\!\right)|\partial_{k_{\nu}}u_{n\bm{k}}\rangle

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\rho}^{Q,3}

\displaystyle=\!\frac{1}{2\hbar}\int\frac{d^{2}k}{(2\pi)^{2}}\sum_{n\in\mathrm{occ}}\left(\varepsilon_{n\bm{k}}-\mu\right)^{2}\Omega_{n\bm{k}}^{\rho}\,.

(54c)

First, we remind that the total heat magnetization
\bm{\mathcal{M}}^{Q}
can be expressed in terms of the sum rule in Eq. (
41c
), which involves the heat probe
\delta\hat{H}_{QQ}^{\pm}(t)
.

Then, we note that the first contribution
\bm{\mathcal{M}}^{Q,1}
entering Eq. (
53
) can be expressed in terms of a sum rule associated with the conventional electric probe
\delta\hat{H}^{\pm}_{ee}(t)
,

\frac{1}{A(V_{0e})^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\,\omega^{2}\,\Delta\Gamma^{\mu\nu}_{ee}(\omega)d\omega=-\epsilon_{\rho\mu\nu}\frac{16\pi e^{2}}{\hbar^{3}}\mathcal{M}^{Q,1}_{\rho}\,.

(55)

In fact, this sum rule was originally introduced by Kubo
Kubo 1957
: considering free particles of mass
m
subjected to a background magnetic field
B_{z}
, this sum rule was expressed in terms of the electric conductivity tensor as

\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\,\omega{\rm Im}\left[\sigma^{xy}(\omega)\right]=\frac{ne^{3}}{m^{2}c}B_{z},

(56)

where
n
denotes the particle density. A comparison with Eq. (
55
) is obtained by noticing that
{\rm Im}\left[\sigma^{xy}(\omega)\right]\sim\omega\Delta\Gamma^{xy}_{ee}(\omega)
; see Eq. (
3
).
We point out that similar sum rules were derived in the context of rotating Bose-Einstein condensates
Zambelli and Stringari 1998
;
Pitaevskii and Stringari 2016
, relating the angular momentum of a rotating atomic gas to the dynamical structure factor relative to quadrupolar excitations.

The second contribution
\bm{\mathcal{M}}^{Q,2}
accounts for the intrinsic heat-magnetic-moment (or
heat-self-rotation
) contribution to the heat magnetization
Zhang et al. 2020
. In direct analogy with the dichroic
f
-sum rule in Eqs. (
30
) and (
52
), this contribution can be extracted by measuring the dichroic response resulting from the chiral thermoelectric probe
\delta\hat{H}^{\pm}_{eQ}(t)
, through the relation

\frac{1}{AV_{0e}V_{0Q}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\,\omega\,\Delta\Gamma^{\mu\nu}_{eQ}(\omega)d\omega=-\epsilon_{\rho\mu\nu}\frac{4\pi e}{\hbar^{2}}\mathcal{M}^{Q,2}_{\rho}\,.

(57)

Interestingly, from Eq. (
41b
), we notice that the integrand on the LHS of Eq. (
57
) can be interpreted as the frequency-resolved orbital magnetization,
\mathcal{M}_{\rho}(\omega)
, formally defined through

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\,\mathcal{M}_{\rho}(\omega)d\omega=\mathcal{M}_{\rho},

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\rho}(\omega)\equiv\frac{1}{AV_{0e}V_{0Q}}\frac{\hbar}{4\pi c}\epsilon_{\mu\nu\rho}\,\Delta\Gamma^{\mu\nu}_{eQ}(\omega).

(58)

Hence, as far as the self-rotation contribution
\mathcal{M}^{Q,2}
is concerned, the
heat
magnetization can be understood as resulting from a sum rule involving a frequency integral of the
orbital
magnetization

\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\,\omega\mathcal{M}_{\rho}(\omega)d\omega=-\frac{2\pi}{\Phi_{0}}\mathcal{M}^{Q,2}_{\rho}\,,

(59)

where we combined Eqs. (
57
)-(
58
).

Altogether, in analogy with the center-of-mass contribution to the orbital magnetization in Eq. (
33
), the third contribution
\bm{\mathcal{M}}^{Q,3}
constitutes the “new” piece of information, not directly accessible through a dedicated sum rule. This “center-of-mass” contribution can nevertheless be evaluated through the three sum rules listed above, combined with the relation
\bm{\mathcal{M}}^{Q,3}\!=\!\bm{\mathcal{M}}^{Q}\!-\!\bm{\mathcal{M}}^{Q,1}\!-\!\bm{\mathcal{M}}^{Q,2}
.

General hierarchical construction

The partitioning of the orbital and heat magnetizations in terms of sum rules and “center-of-mass” contributions can be systematically generalized to a whole hierarchy of higher-order heat magnetizations. In the present construction, the “order” refers to the power with which heat enters the generalized magnetizations, as we now describe.

Our construction starts by introducing the generalized polarization operators

\hat{P}^{\mu}_{n}=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\begin{pmatrix}n\\
k\end{pmatrix}\left(\hat{H}-\mu\right)^{k}\hat{r}^{\mu}\left(\hat{H}-\mu\right)^{n-k},

(60)

where one recognizes the conventional electric polarization
\hat{P}^{\mu}_{e}\!=\!-e\hat{P}^{\mu}_{0}
and heat polarization
\hat{P}^{\mu}_{Q}\!=\!\hat{P}^{\mu}_{1}
.

Then, inspired by the Kramers-Kronig relations in Eq. (
24
), one defines the
n
th-order heat magnetization density as

-\,\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho}\mathcal{M}^{(n)}_{\rho}\equiv\mathbb{L}_{\alpha\beta}^{\mathrm{DC},\mu\nu},\quad n=\alpha+\beta,

(61)

where the correlation functions
\mathbb{L}_{\alpha\beta}^{\mathrm{DC}}
[Eq. (
17
)] now include the generalized polarization operators
\hat{P}^{\mu}_{\alpha}
and
\hat{P}^{\mu}_{\beta}
defined in Eq. (
60
).

In Bloch representation, these quantities are explicitly given by the
\bm{k}
-space integral

\mathcal{M}^{(m)}_{\rho}=\!\frac{i\epsilon_{\rho\mu\nu}}{\hbar}\!\!\!\int\!\!\!\frac{d^{2}k}{(2\pi)^{2}}\!\!\!\!\sum_{n\in\mathrm{occ}}\!\langle\partial_{k_{\mu}}u_{n\bm{k}}|\left(\!\!\frac{\hat{H}_{\bm{k}}\!+\!\varepsilon_{n\bm{k}}\!-\!2\mu}{2}\!\!\right)^{m}\!\!\!|\partial_{k_{\nu}}u_{n\bm{k}}\rangle\,.

(62)

By considering the first orders
m\!=\!0,1,2
, one recognizes the expressions for
C
,
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{M}^{Q}
, namely,
\mathcal{C}_{\rho}=\frac{2\pi}{\hbar}\mathcal{M}^{(0)}_{\rho}
,
\mathcal{M}_{\rho}=\frac{-e}{c}\mathcal{M}^{(1)}_{\rho}
and
\mathcal{M}^{Q}_{\rho}=\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{M}^{(2)}_{\rho}
.

We then generalize the chiral probe
\delta\hat{H}_{\alpha\beta}^{\pm}(t)
in Eq. (
43
) so as to encompass the generalized polarization operators,
\hat{P}^{\mu}_{\alpha}
and
\hat{P}^{\mu}_{\beta}
, with
\alpha,\beta\!\in\!\mathbb{N}
. We find that the corresponding dichroic responses yield the higher-order heat magnetizations
\mathcal{M}^{(\alpha+\beta)}
through the relation

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta\Gamma^{int,\mu\nu}_{\alpha\beta}}{A}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\,\frac{\Delta\Gamma^{\mu\nu}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)}{A}

(63)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi}{\hbar}V_{0\alpha}V_{0\beta}\epsilon_{\mu\nu\rho}\mathcal{M}^{(\alpha+\beta)}_{\rho}.

Importantly, similarly to the orbital and heat magnetizations discussed above, the information carried by these higher-order heat magnetizations is redundant in the sense that most of their contributions can be inferred from sum rules involving (frequency-resolved) lower-order magnetizations. To see this, let us introduce the quantity

\displaystyle m_{\rho}^{a,b}

\displaystyle=\!\frac{i\epsilon_{\rho\mu\nu}}{\hbar}\!\!\!\int\!\!\!\frac{d^{2}k}{(2\pi)^{2}}\!\!\!\!\sum_{n\in\mathrm{occ}}\!\langle\partial_{k_{\mu}}u_{n\bm{k}}|\left(\!\!\frac{\hat{H}_{\bm{k}}\!+\!\varepsilon_{n\bm{k}}\!-\!2\mu}{2}\!\!\right)^{a}\!\!\!

(64)

\displaystyle\times\left(\!\hat{H}_{\bm{k}}\!-\!\varepsilon_{n\bm{k}}\!\right)^{b}|\partial_{k_{\nu}}u_{n\bm{k}}\rangle.

One then identifies an insightful partitioning of the
n
th-order heat magnetization expressed as

\mathcal{M}_{\rho}^{(n)}=m_{\rho}^{n,0}=\mathcal{M}^{{\rm COM},(n)}_{\rho}-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\begin{pmatrix}n\\
k\end{pmatrix}\left(\frac{-1}{2}\right)^{\!\!k}m_{\rho}^{n-k,k}.

(65)

Importantly, all the contributions entering Eq. (
65
) can be expressed as generalized sum rules involving lower-order (frequency-resolved) dichroic responses,

\displaystyle m_{\rho}^{a,b}

\displaystyle=\epsilon_{\rho\mu\nu}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\,\left(\hbar\omega\right)^{b}\frac{\Delta\Gamma^{\mu\nu}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)}{V_{0\alpha}V_{0\beta}},\quad\alpha+\beta=a,

(66)

except for the “center-of-mass” contribution

\mathcal{M}_{\rho}^{{\rm COM},(m)}=\frac{1}{\hbar}\int\frac{d^{2}k}{(2\pi)^{2}}\sum_{n\in\mathrm{occ}}\left(\varepsilon_{n\bm{k}}-\mu\right)^{m}\Omega_{n\bm{k}}^{\rho},

(67)

which contains the “new” information at the
n
-th order, not captured by a dedicated sum rule involving lower-order quantities.

We stress that Eq. (
66
) generalizes the sum rules for the individual contributions
\bm{\mathcal{M}}^{Q,1}
and
\bm{\mathcal{M}}^{Q,2}
to the heat magnetization in Eqs. (
55
) and (
57
), as well as the dichroic
f
-sum rule for the self-rotation contribution
\bm{\mathcal{M}}^{{\rm SR}}
to the orbital magnetization in Eq. (
52
). The general partitioning expressed in Eq. (
65
) thus emerges as a foundational principle governing the sum rules established in this work.

VII
Concluding remarks

This work presented a unified framework connecting the orbital and heat magnetizations to experimentally accessible chiral probes via generalized sum rules, placing these fundamental ground-state properties on the same footing as the topological Chern number.

We have set a particular emphasis on the dichroic response to chiral probes
\delta\hat{H}_{\alpha\beta}^{\pm}(t)
, as defined in Eq. (
43
), which naturally lead to the extraction of the total orbital and heat magnetizations. As we discussed, this framework represents a significant extension to the dichroic
f
-sum rule and to similar dichroic responses leading to the Berry curvature and Chern number of Bloch band systems
Souza and Vanderbilt 2008
;
Tran et al. 2017
;
Repellin and Goldman 2019
;
Asteria et al. 2019
;
Goldman and Ozawa 2024
. However, more general probes and sum rules could be considered within this framework. Indeed, the Kramers–Kronig relations in Eq. (
24
) can be extended by considering a broader class of integrals of the form

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\!d\omega\frac{\mathrm{Im}\left[\mathbb{L}^{\mathrm{abs},\mu\nu}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)\right]}{\omega^{n}},\quad\int_{0}^{\infty}\!d\omega\frac{\mathrm{Re}\left[\mathbb{L}^{\mathrm{abs},\mu\nu}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)\right]}{\omega^{n}},

(68)

where
n
is an integer, and where the (absorptive) correlation functions are defined in Eq. (
26
). Setting
n\!=\!1
naturally leads to generalizations of the quantum geometric tensor
Kolodrubetz et al. 2017
;
Shinada and Nagaosa 2025
, with the real and imaginary parts giving access to possible analogues of the Berry curvature and quantum metric, respectively. As for the conventional quantum geometric tensor, the real and imaginary parts would be extracted by monitoring excitation rates under linear and circular drives, respectively
Ozawa and Goldman 2018
;
Ozawa and Goldman 2019
;
Yu et al. 2020
.
Establishing generalized quantum geometric tensors – for instance, to identify geometric bounds of ground-state properties
Shinada and Nagaosa 2025
– and further elucidating the properties of the “heat quantum metric” introduced in Eq. (
29
) represent compelling directions for future investigation.

Our study has primarily examined insulating phases at zero temperature in the absence of interactions. A natural extension of the framework would be its application to correlated insulators. While the sum rules derived in this work [e.g., Eqs. (
24
) and (
41
)] are expected to remain valid in the presence of interactions, the corresponding heat-current and heat-polarization operators would necessarily incorporate these processes explicitly
Cooper et al. 1997
. Moreover, it would be interesting to elucidate how our momentum-space relations [e.g., Eqs. (
25
)-(
29
)] would be reformulated using twisted boundary conditions, which provide an appropriate parameter space for many-body settings
Souza et al. 2000
;
Ozawa and Goldman 2019
;
Goldman and Ozawa 2024
.

As noted above, our framework relies on zero-temperature transport coefficients, rendering finite-temperature generalizations an intriguing challenge.
Within this context, elucidating the geometric content of thermoelectric sum rules at finite temperature – particularly the fate of the heat quantum metric in Eq. (
29
) when extended to mixed states – emerges as a compelling open problem
Ji et al. 2025
.

Moreover, while the physical quantities analyzed here are integrated over the entire Brillouin zone, it would be valuable to examine their momentum-resolved components, accessible either through the dichroic response of localized wave packets
Tran et al. 2017
or momentum-resolved spectroscopy
Schüler et al. 2020
;
Beaulieu et al. 2024
. We further note that the applicability of our thermoelectric dichroic approach may also prove relevant in the study of bosonic systems
Bermond et al. 2025
;
Tesfaye and Eckardt 2025
.

Another promising direction concerns the study of edge contributions to ground-state properties and thermoelectric dichroic signals, for instance, by making use of effective low-energy edge theories
Ünal et al. 2024
. Finally, it would be worthwhile to identify concrete dichroic schemes for extracting the orbital magnetization density of Floquet systems, where this quantity is quantized according to Floquet winding numbers
Nathan et al. 2017
;
Peralta Gavensky et al. 2025
;
Gavensky et al. 2025
.
