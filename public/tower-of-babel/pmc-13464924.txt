Clinical decision-making skill in diagnosing of oral diseases among medical interns
Authors: Ava Farzan, Abtin Heidarzadeh, Shadman Nemati, Hossein Khoshrang, Maryam Basirat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOral mucosal diseases are frequently misdiagnosed due to overlapping clinical presentations. Given the important role of physicians in the initial assessment of oral conditions, evaluating diagnostic reasoning among medical trainees is essential. This study aimed to assess the clinical decision-making skills of medical interns in diagnosing oral diseases.MethodsThis cross-sectional analytical study was conducted among 66 medical interns at Guilan University of Medical Sciences using a census sampling approach. Data were collected using a two-part questionnaire consisting of demographic information and 19 Key Feature–based clinical scenarios, most accompanied by visual aids. The instrument was validated using a two-round Delphi process and demonstrated acceptable reliability (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.70; ICC = 0.861). Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, independent t-tests, and linear regression analysis.ResultsThe mean diagnostic performance score was 7.14 ± 2.9 (range: 1.73–13.77). Male interns had higher scores than females (p = 0.003). No significant association was found between age and diagnostic performance, while GPA showed a significant positive correlation (p < 0.001). Diagnostic accuracy was higher for conditions with more recognizable clinical features, such as oral candidiasis and denture stomatitis, whereas lower accuracy was observed for conditions with non-specific presentations, including reflux-related lesions, burning mouth syndrome, and xerostomia.ConclusionMedical interns demonstrated suboptimal diagnostic performance in oral diseases. These findings suggest a need for enhanced training in oral diagnosis and clinical reasoning within medical curricula. Future educational strategies incorporating case-based learning and interdisciplinary training may improve diagnostic competence.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09589-7.

Introduction

In many cases, physicians are the first members of the healthcare team that patients consult regarding oral lesions, often prior to visiting a dentist [1, 2]. General practitioners can play a vital role in promoting oral health care [3]. Unfortunately, communication between dentists and other health professionals remains limited, both in terms of scientific collaboration and treatment coordination [3, 4]. Effective oral health management and risk factor mitigation require a multidisciplinary approach. Some patients initially seek diagnosis and treatment of oral issues from general practitioners [1]. Consequently, routine medical visits may represent the only opportunity to identify serious oral lesions. Furthermore, many systemic diseases manifest orally, and the initial presentation of certain serious conditions—such as pemphigus vulgaris and lichen planus—may appear exclusively in the oral cavity [2, 5].

Oral and dental health are integral components of overall well-being, closely linked to systemic health conditions. Therefore, collaboration between medical and dental professionals is essential for comprehensive healthcare. Interprofessional collaboration involves a partnership among healthcare providers and patients, fostering a coordinated approach to decision-making regarding health and social issues. Continuing education programs should highlight the importance of such collaboration and equip providers with strategies to confidently identify and appropriately refer patients [6, 7].

While previous studies have largely assessed dentists’ diagnostic knowledge [8–10], limited evidence exists regarding medical interns’ performance. Our study addresses this gap by evaluating diagnostic reasoning within a medical cohort.

The range of diseases affecting the oral cavity, jaw, and face is extensive, and many lesions share overlapping clinical features. Due to their nonspecific presentation and high resemblance, many oral lesions are frequently misdiagnosed. Recognizing the pivotal role of physicians in early diagnosis and the need for targeted educational enhancements for medical students, we initiated a study during 2023–2024 aimed at evaluating the diagnostic performance in oral disease of medical interns at Guilan University of Medical Sciences concerning oral diseases.

Method and materials

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional-analytical study was conducted after obtaining ethical approval [IR.GUMS.REC.1402.202] on 66 medical students from Guilan University of Medical Sciences, all of whom were in their internship phase between March 2023 and February 2024 and expressed willingness to participate in present study.

Characteristics of participants

census sampling was employed, and all eligible medical interns at Guilan University of Medical Sciences were invited to participate. Exclusion criteria included being a transferred or visiting student at Guilan University of Medical Sciences. Incomplete questionnaires or responses exceeding the predetermined maximum completion time based on pilot testing were considered invalid.

Questionnaire

In the initial phase of the study, a list of diseases that general practitioners should be able to recognize was compiled based on expert input from eight faculty experts in oral medicine. Using the list, the key symptoms and clinical features were identified through a two-round Delphi process. Consensus for each item was predefined as ≥ 80% agreement among experts. Items not reaching consensus in the first round were revised and redistributed for the second round. All items achieved the required level of agreement, and none were deleted.

The finalized content was transformed into 19 case-based Key Feature questions to assess critical steps in diagnostic reasoning. The selected 19 conditions represent a spectrum of common, clinically relevant oral diseases with varying levels of diagnostic complexity, including infectious, inflammatory, neoplastic, and systemic-related oral manifestations that general practitioners are expected to recognize.

The questionnaire comprised two sections: first, demographic information and second, Key Feature- based clinical scenarios, most accompanied by visual aids. certain diagnostically challenging conditions, cases such as lupus erythematosus and gastroesophageal reflux were intentionally included to assess awareness of systemic conditions which are considered essential competencies for medical graduates.

The questionnaire, developed through a comprehensive review of literature, textbooks, and documentary sources.

Content validity was assessed using the Content Validity Ratio (CVR) and Content Validity Index (CVI) based on ratings from eight experts. CVR values ranged from 0.53 to 1.00, CVI scores ranged from 0.84 to 1.00. Items scoring between 0.80 and 0.90 were revised, while those above 0.90 were retained.

Internal consistency was assessed in a pilot sample of 20 medical interns, yielding a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.70, indicating acceptable reliability.

Test–retest reliability was evaluated over a 10-day interval in 15 medical interns, yielding ICC = 0.861 and r = 0.99, indicating good stability. These relatively high values may partly reflect the short retest interval and sample homogeneity and were therefore interpreted cautiously.

Scoring procedure

The final questionnaire consisted of 19 Key Feature–based case scenarios, each carrying one point (raw score range: 0–19). Each item included one or more essential correct options along with four to five distractors. Participants were instructed to select 3–6 responses per case. Credit was awarded only when all essential options were correctly identified within the permitted range. Selecting more than the allowed number resulted in no credit.

To emphasize patient safety and discourage guessing, predefined unsafe or inappropriate management options (“killer options”) resulted in loss of credit for that item.

For ease of interpretation, raw scores were linearly rescaled to a 0–20 scale. Scores were categorized as poor (< 50%), moderate (50–75%), and good (> 75%). A predefined scoring rubric and answer key were developed through expert consensus (≥ 80% agreement). Written responses were independently scored by two faculty raters, and discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus. Expert opinions from fields such as community and family medicine, oral and maxillofacial medicine, dermatology, otolaryngology, neurology, pathology, internal medicine, rheumatology, and pediatrics guided the scoring.

Data collection

Data were collected through an on-line survey platform. Participant completed the questionnaire voluntarily after receiving information about the study objectives.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using STATA version 16. Continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation and categorical variables as frequency (percentage). Independent t-tests were used for group comparisons. Simple linear regression analysis was conducted to examine associations between diagnostic performance and predictor variables (e.g., GPA, gender). Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

The study included 66 medical interns, with a mean age of 26.11 ± 1.6 years. Of the participants, 30 (45.5%) were female and 36 (54.5%) were male. Their GPA was 16.63 ± 0.76. Regarding university affiliation, 45 participants (68.2%) were enrolled at the Faculty of Medicine in Rasht, and 21 students (31.8%) at the International Campus of Bandar Anzali.

The overall mean diagnostic performance score was 7.14 ± 2.9, with scores ranging from 1.73 to 13.77. Male interns had higher mean scores than females (p = 0.003). No statistically significant differences were observed between participants from Rasht and Bandar Anzali (Table 1).

Discussion

Clinical examination remains fundamental for diagnosing common oral conditions; however, many oral lesions present with non-specific features, making accurate diagnosis highly dependent on clinicians’ reasoning skills and clinical exposure [11–14]. Despite the importance of diagnostic reasoning, most previous research has focused on dentists, with comparatively limited attention to medical interns or general practitioner [5, 6, 14–16]. Because patients frequently consult general physicians, otolaryngologists and dermatologists for oral complaints [3, 15–17], assessing diagnostic performance among medical interns is crucial for reducing misdiagnosis and improving patient care. The aim of the study was to identify patterns of diagnostic performance in oral disease, rather than provide a detailed analysis of each individual condition.

The present study demonstrated that medical interns exhibited limited diagnostic accuracy with an average score of 7 out of 20 in identifying oral diseases. This finding aligns with previous studies reporting suboptimal performance among both dental and medical trainees [8, 9, 18, 19], suggesting a broader gap in clinical diagnostic training. Several factors may explain this gap. First, undergraduate medical curricula often provide limited exposure to oral diseases, reducing familiarity with their clinical presentations. Second, the separation between medical and dental education may hinder interdisciplinary learning. Third, insufficient emphasis on visual diagnostic training may impair recognition of oral lesions. Finally, exam-oriented learning approaches may limit the development of applied clinical reasoning skills. An additional factor that may have influenced the overall performance is the scoring design of the Key Feature questions. To emphasize patient safety and discourage guessing, selection of clearly unsafe (“killer”) options resulted in loss of credit for the corresponding item. While this approach aims to reflect real-life clinical consequences of inappropriate decisions, it may also have contributed to lower total scores. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with consideration of this scoring strategy.

No significant association was observed between age and their diagnostic performance, likely due to the narrow age range of participants. Although a statistically significant difference was observed between male and female participants, this finding should be interpreted cautiously, as previous studies have reported inconsistent results [10, 19]. Such differences may reflect contextual or behavioral factors rather than true variations in diagnostic ability.

Participants performed better in diagnosing conditions with recognizable or familiar clinical presentations— such as oral candidiasis (including HIV-related manifestations), denture stomatitis, aphthous stomatitis, and salivary gland tumors. Higher diagnostic performance for these conditions may be explained by their relatively distinctive clinical features, higher prevalence and greater emphasis during undergraduate training and clinical rotations. Repeated exposure to these common conditions likely facilitates diagnostic performance and strengthens diagnostic confidence among students. conversely, conditions with subtle or overlapping features—such as gastric reflux-related oral lesions, burning mouth syndrome, and xerostomia were most misdiagnosed [20]. Similar difficulty has been noted in previous research, such as Poelman et al., where dentists demonstrated wide variation in diagnostic accuracy for different oral mucosal conditions [19]. Furthermore, medical students in Gunjal’s study showed limited ability to identify early signs of oral cancer [10], In the present sample, conditions such as primary herpetic stomatitis were correctly identified by only about 40% of participants, likely due to confusion with other viral illnesses presenting with similar prodromal symptoms. Moreover, a key distinguishing feature—herpetic gingivostomatitis—may be overlooked during clinical examination.

Low accuracy was also observed for systemic or life-threatening conditions such as pemphigus and squamous cell carcinoma highlighting the potential consequences of delayed or incorrect diagnosis. These challenges are consistent with previous research demonstrating variability in diagnostic accuracy for oral mucosal and orofacial conditions [10, 19, 20].Diagnostic challenges also extended to pain-related disorders, including temporomandibular disorders and neuralgias, Differentiating odontogenic from non-odontogenic orofacial pain—such as neuralgia or sinusitis—requires a structured diagnostic approach that may be less familiar to medical trainees. Misinterpretation of such conditions as dental pathology can lead to unnecessary interventions and delays in appropriate management [20].

Limited diagnostic performance of lesions, such as oral lichen planus and inflammatory papillary hyperplasia was also observed, in line with prior reports that these lesions are frequently overlooked [8]. Although one-third of medical interns indicated referral to oral medicine specialists, this proportion may be insufficient given the complexity of many oral conditions. Consistent with previous research, conditions with more recognizable clinical features were diagnosed more accurately, whereas those with non-specific or overlapping presentations posed greater challenges [8, 9, 19]. This pattern highlights the importance of structured diagnostic frameworks and repeated clinical exposure. The findings underscore the importance of strengthening oral health education within medical curricula. Integrating case-based learning, visual training modules, interdisciplinary teaching, and short clinical rotations in oral medicine may enhance diagnostic competence and improve patient care.

This study has several limitations. The sample size was relatively small and derived from a single institution, which restricts generalizability. The cross-sectional design does not capture developmental changes in clinical reasoning skills over time. Although clinical scenarios included key elements of patient history, the absence of real patient interaction and dynamic history taking may limit ecological validity. Additionally, variations in participants’ prior clinical exposure may have influenced performance.

Future studies should incorporate multicenter designs, longitudinal follow-up, and real-patient or simulation-based assessments to better capture clinical decision-making processes.

Conclusion

Medical interns demonstrated limited diagnostic performance in oral diseases. Strengthening oral health education, integrating clinical reasoning training, and promoting interdisciplinary learning may improve diagnostic performance. Further research is needed to evaluate the effectiveness of such interventions.
