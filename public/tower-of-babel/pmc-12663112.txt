Garvicin Q-producing Lactococcus garvieae LG3092: its role in mitigating dysbiosis and H. pylori-associated gastritis
Authors: Yanyan Shang, Yuwei Wu, Ying Zhang, Xinyu Zhao, Juan Wang, Yu Ding, Hui Zhao, Moutong Chen, Ying Li, Qingping Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
H. pylori induces gastritis and promotes gastric carcinogenesis. Antimicrobial therapy against H. pylori often causes gastrointestinal dysbiosis, with side effects like vomiting, diarrhea, and antibiotic resistance, hindering effective eradication. This study investigated the effects and mechanisms of probiotics in balancing microbiota to alleviate H. pylori-related gastritis. Using in vivo and in vitro gastritis models with various H. pylori virulence strains, the study employed 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing and qPCR to link gastric microbiota with inflammation. Genomic mining and microbiota reconstruction identified Lactococcus garvieae LG3092 and GarQ as key microecological-targeting regulators. Results showed that different H. pylori induce varying levels of gastritis in vivo, with elevated IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α levels linked to pro-inflammatory bacteria. LG3092 secretes GarQ, specifically targeting Man-PTS receptors on pro-inflammatory bacteria, disrupting their membranes and modulating the gastric microbiota, highlighting the potential of probiotics in combination with other therapies to managing H. pylori infection and related gastritis.

Introduction

Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) is an important human pathogen, and its urease production capability enables it to survive and colonize in the acidic environment of the stomach for extended periods1, contributing to chronic gastric mucosal inflammation, stomach pain, stomach distension, duodenal ulcer, etc., which can progress to gastric cancer2,3 in about 92% of cases4. Compared with the risk of gastric cancer, the rapid improvement of gastritis might be the main concern. The immune evasion mechanisms5 of H. pylori lead to sustained inflammation and chronic infection6, making early eradication crucial for reducing gastric discomfort and the risk of cancer. The effectiveness of traditional treatments is declining due to increasing bacterial resistance, and antibiotic therapy can exacerbate the gastric and intestinal flora dysbiosis, worsening inflammation, and causing side effects like nausea and vomiting. This often leads to premature treatment discontinuation. Therefore, alternative therapies, such as antimicrobial biologics, are urgently required to combat H. pylori infection while preserving microbial homeostasis7.

The pathogenicity of H. pylori is associated with mechanisms such as adhesion8, virulence factors (VacA and CagA), host signaling pathways, alterations in microbial communities, and the initiation of gastric mucosal inflammation9. CagA and VacA exert complementary roles during pathogenesis, particularly in inflammation. Both CagA and VacA upregulate IL-8 via the NF‑κB signaling pathway10,11. Among these, CagA is the dominant inducer of IL‑8, with its effect enhanced by VacA12,13. Strains expressing s1/m1-type VacA may induce greater IL‑8 production, although its inflammatory potential remains lower than that of CagA. The primary pathogenic roles of VacA lie in vacuole formation, mitochondrial disruption, and ion channel regulation14,15, while CagA interferes with host cell signaling16, contributing to the characteristic hummingbird-like cell elongation12,13. The persistent inflammation from H. pylori infection is a major cause of chronic gastritis, and the microbial imbalance may further intensify the inflammatory response, lead to symptoms such as indigestion, diarrhea, and irritable bowel syndrome. Therefore, targeted treatment addressing the diverse pathogenic mechanisms mentioned above is are effective strategies to prevent and control H. pylori, reduce H. pylori-related inflammation, and alleviate host gastrointestinal discomfort.

Probiotics, especially Lactobacillus strains, have shown promise in suppressing urease activity17, inhibiting clinical H. pylori growth in vitro18, reducing infection by competing for adhesion sites in the gastric mucosa19, and mitigating gastric mucosal damage by modulating the immune system and reducing inflammation20. Currently, novel mechanisms for inhibiting H. pylori and related pathogenic bacteria, such as regulating gastrointestinal microbiota balance in synergy with other beneficial bacteria remain relatively underexplored. There is a lack of systematic studies examining how variations in individual gastric environments and microbiota composition impact the efficacy of probiotics against H. pylori. Lactococcus strains, known to produce bacteriocins that antagonize pathogenic bacteria, may also contribute to balancing the gastrointestinal microbiota, but their effects on H. pylori and gastric diseases remain unclear21. Beyond traditional probiotics such as Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium, the next-generation probiotics have the potential to modulate the gastrointestinal microbiota to improve related diseases and is a potential tool for personalized therapies22.

In this study, we propose that H. pylori-induced microecological disruption, beyond its virulence factors, plays a significant role in gastritis development. H. pylori infection induces elevated levels of IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, and TNF-α primarily through activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway, with IL-8 being particularly prominent. Other pro-inflammatory bacteria further increase the expression of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, which contributes significantly to the exacerbation of gastritis and the onset of gastric pain. These pro-inflammatory bacteria can exacerbate mucosal inflammation through various mechanisms. For instance, their cell wall components, such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS), can persistently activate the host’s immune system, triggering inflammatory cascades23–25. Furthermore, some of these taxa secrete toxins that directly damage gastric epithelial cells26. The synergistic action of these microbes disrupts mucosal homeostasis27 and may, together with H. pylori, create a vicious cycle that perpetuates chronic inflammation. Therefore, we aimed to investigate the H. pylori-associated pro-inflammatory bacteria activity and potential of Lactococcus strains in reducing inflammatory response, and to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying their alleviation of H. pylori-related gastritis. Studies have shown that Lactococcus garvieae (L. garvieae) secretes the bacteriocin GarQ, which specifically targets the Man-PTS on the membranes of pro-inflammatory bacteria, causing membrane disruption and a reduction in the relative abundance of pro-inflammatory bacteria28. In the present study, strain LG3092 utilizes this same GarQ-mediated, selective antibacterial mechanism to enable anti-inflammatory bacteria, such as Lactobacillus, to gain competitive and adhesive advantages in the gastrointestinal tract, thereby restoring microbial balance and alleviating inflammation. This research might provide insights for developing effective therapeutic strategies against H. pylori and restoring gastric microbiota homeostasis.

In vivo, H&E staining showed that both H. pylori SS1 and GZ6B5 caused inflammation in mice gastric tissue. The gastric mucosa of Group SS1 showed chronic mild to moderate active gastritis, glandular atrophy, and some intestinal metaplasia (Fig. 1d). The gastric mucosa of Group GZ6B5 developed chronic severe active gastritis, glandular atrophy, and high-grade intraepithelial neoplasia of focal glands (Fig. 1d). Compared to the Group NC, H. pylori-infected mice (Group SS1 and GZ6B5) exhibited varying degrees of upregulation pro-inflammatory factor expression. TNF-α (p = 0.001), IL-1β (p = 0.0002), and IL-6 (p = 0.0014) were significantly increased in SS1-infected mice, while IL-1β (p = 0.0056) and TNF-α (p = 0.0012) expression were elevated in GZ6B5-infected mice (Fig. 1e). Notably, no higher inflammatory expression was observed in GZ6B5-infected mice than in SS1-infected mice, which was inconsistent with our in vitro results. These findings suggest that H. pylori SS1 and GZ6B5 induce different levels of inflammation in vivo and in vitro.

The core microbiota in the NC group primarily consisted of Lactobacillales, Bacillales, Bacteroidales, and Lachnospirales. The SS1 group exhibited five additional core microbiota—Oscillospirales, Clostridiales, Erysipelotrichales, Burkholderiales, and Coriobacteriales compared to the NC group. The GZ6B5 group had two additional core microbiota relative to the NC group, specifically Oscillospirales and Veillonellales (Fig. 2c). Correlation analysis revealed that Lactobacillales abundance was negatively correlated with pro-inflammatory factors, especially IL-8 (p < 0.01), while Bacteroidales was positively correlated with IL-8 (p < 0.05). Furthermore, Lachnospirales and Oscillospirales showed positive correlations with IL-6 (p < 0.05) and IL-8 (p < 0.01), respectively. Veillonellales abundance demonstrated a significant positive correlation with TNF-α (p < 0.0001), while Coriobacteriales was positively correlated with IL-6 expression (p < 0.05). Additionally, Clostridiales, Erysipelotrichales, and Burkholderiales exhibited positive correlations with pro-inflammatory factors, whereas Bacillales displayed a negative correlation, though not statistically significant (Fig. 2d). Studies indicate that most Lactobacillales are classified as anti-inflammatory bacteria, however, certain genus within this order, Streptococcus and Enterococcus may cause infections under specific conditions. Notably, studies showed that Bacillales belonged to anti-inflammatory bacteria, but many members of this order are recognized as pathogenic. Examples include Listeria monocytogenes, Bacillus cereus, Bacillus licheniformis, Bacillus subtilis, and Bacillus pumilus, suggesting that Bacillales should be considered as an opportunistic pathogen. Furthermore, Bacteroidales, Lachnospirales, Oscillospirales, Clostridiales, Erysipelotrichales, Burkholderiales, Coriobacteriales, and Veillonellales are classified as pro-inflammatory bacteria.

The microbial interaction network (Fig. 2e) indicates that in group NC, the anti-inflammatory Lactobacillales were negatively correlated with the pro-inflammatory Bacteroidales, Lachnospirales, Oscillospirales, Clostridiales, and Coriobacteriales, while Bacillales were negatively correlated with the pro-inflammatory Veillonellales. In H. pylori-infected groups, the correlation between the anti-inflammatory Lactobacillales and the pro-inflammatory Clostridiales, Coriobacteriales was disrupted, instead correlated negatively with Bacillales, suggesting H. pylori infection partially disrupts the balance between anti-inflammatory Lactobacillales and certain pro-inflammatory bacteria. Bacillales, under H. pylori infection, exhibited a pro-inflammatory response, potentially due to increased pathogenic genus. Notably, in the H. pylori-infected groups, Staphylococcales, Rhizobiales, Corynebacteriales, and Enterobacterales were positively correlated with H. pylori (Campylobacterales), a trend not observed in group NC.

A separate experiment using an artificial mixed bacterial system (Table S4) showed no significant inflammatory change in AGS cells cultured with the st-NC bacterial mixture compared to untreated AGS cells (Group Blank). However, AGS cells cultured with the st-SS1 and st-GZ6B5 artificial bacterial mixtures exhibited up-regulation of inflammatory factors compared with Group st-NC. IL-8 (p = 0.0011), IL-6 (p = 0.0022), and TNF-α (p = 0.0356) were significantly elevated in the st-SS1 group, while IL-6 (p = 0.0347) and IL-8 (p = 0.0388) were up-regulated in Group st-GZ6B5 (Fig. 2f). These results suggest that both SS1 and GZ6B5 exacerbate gastric inflammation by inducing dysbiosis. Therefore, inhibiting H. pylori-associated pro-inflammatory bacteria and increasing the abundance of anti-inflammatory bacteria may help restore microbial balance and alleviate gastric inflammation.

The cell-free supernatant (CFS) of the above 12 strains of LABs was adjusted to pH = 6, and inhibition zones against pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory bacteria were re-evaluated. Most LAB strains completely lost their antibacterial activity, with only a few strains still inhibiting pro-inflammatory bacteria. L. garvieae LG3092 exhibited strong inhibition against pro-inflammatory bacteria Enterococcus faecium and Salmonella typhimurium, and mild inhibition of Listeria monocytogenes, while showing slight inhibition against the anti-inflammatory bacterium Lacticaseibacillus paracasei. L. garvieae ST5_1 demonstrated mild inhibition of both Enterococcus faecium (pro-inflammatory) and Lacticaseibacillus paracasei (anti-inflammatory). L. garvieae 2055_2 mildly inhibited Listeria monocytogenes (pro-inflammatory) and L. garvieae (anti-inflammatory). L. plantarum 0101160_1_Z showed mild inhibitory effects on the pro-inflammatory Enterococcus faecium and Listeria monocytogenes, as well as on the anti-inflammatory Lacticaseibacillus paracasei, L. garvieae, and Lactobacillus lactis. L. plantarum SR28_2 exhibited mild inhibition of Listeria monocytogenes (pro-inflammatory) (Table S7). Thus, under higher pH conditions, LG3092 demonstrated optimal inhibition of pro-inflammatory bacteria with minimal inhibition of anti-inflammatory bacteria, a capacity not shared universally among the tested strains.

LG3092 modulates microbiome balance in H. pylori-associated gastritis and alleviates inflammatory responses

To comprehensively investigate the anti-inflammatory effects of L. garvieae LG3092 and its underlying mechanisms, this study was conducted from three key perspectives. First, both in vitro and in vivo experiments demonstrated that LG3092 can modulate the gastric microbial balance, specifically by suppressing certain pathogenic bacteria and promoting a healthier microbial community structure. Second, we confirmed that LG3092 effectively alleviates inflammation induced by H. pylori and associated pathogens in both cellular and animal models. This anti-inflammatory effect is attributed not only to its direct antagonistic activity against pro-inflammatory bacteria but also to its regulatory influence on the gastric microbiota. Finally, our findings further reveal that the anti-inflammatory efficacy of LG3092 is closely linked to its competitive inhibition of H. pylori colonization in vivo, suggesting that LG3092 confers its beneficial effects through multiple synergistic pathways.

Studies29 showed that Enterococcus and Staphylococcus proliferate in stomach following H. pylori infection, contributing to gastric inflammation and the disease progression associated with H. pylori. To verify the effect of the CFS of LG3092 on the gastrointestinal microbiota in vitro, Listeria, Enterococcus, and Salmonella were added to the artificially mixed bacterial system based on Table S4. After 6-hour incubation with CFS, compared to Group st-SS1 + CFS (0 h), the st-SS1 + CFS (6 h) showed a slight decrease in the average relative abundance of Lactobacillus (from 0.7796 to 0.7794), a decrease in Enterococcus (from 0.0204 to 0.009), Listeria (from 0.0243 to 0.0108), Clostridium (from 0.0227 to 0.0195) and Salmonella (from 0.0004 to 0.0000). The average relative abundance of Bacillus increased from 0.1514 to 0.1711, Escherichia increased from 0.0005 to 0.0011, Helicobacter increased slightly from 0.0007 to 0.0010. After 6 h incubation, the average relative abundance of Lactobacillus decreased from 0.6191 to 0.5884 in Group st-GZ6B5 + CFS, while Enterococcus decreased from 0.00284 to 0.0009, Listeria decreased from 0.0011 to 0.0004, and Salmonella decreased from 0.0004 to 0.0000. The average relative abundance of Bacillus (from 0.3686 to 0.3852), Escherichia (from 0.0005 to 0.0018), Staphylococcus (from 0.0069 to 0.0076), and Helicobacter (from 0.0007 to 0.0010) increased slightly compared to st-GZ6B5 + CFS (0 h) (Fig. 3b).

In vivo validation experiment (Fig. 3e), L. garvieae LG3092 intervention led to an increase in alpha diversity, particularly in the Shannon index, indicating enhanced species diversity, richness, and evenness in the LG3092_GZ6B5 group (p < 0.01), reflecting improved ecosystem health and stability (Fig. 3c). PCoA analysis revealed distinct clustering of gastric microbiota composition based on four different treatments, with the LG3092_SS1 and LG3092_GZ6B5 groups clustering similarly, and showing significant differences from the infection groups. These results suggest that LG3092 intervention helped normalize the microbiota structure in infected mice (Fig. 3d).

At the order level, compared to infected mice, LG3092 intervention reduced the relative abundances of Lachnospirales, Oscillospirales, and Clostridiales, though not significantly. Conversely, the relative abundance of Bacteroidales increased, particularly in the LG3092_GZ6B5 group (p = 0.0148). Lactobacillales, Bacillales, and Veillonellales slight increased in SS1-infected mice, while decreases were observed in GZ6B5-infected mice. The abundances of Erysipelotrichales, Burkholderiales, and Coriobacteriales decreased in SS1-infected mice, with a slight increase in GZ6B5-infected mice (Fig. 3f). These results suggest that LG3092 can inhibit the growth of specific microorganisms and promote a balanced microbiota both in vitro and in vivo.

Pathological observations of H&E-stained gastric tissues revealed that H. pylori-infected mice exhibited thinner gastric mucosa, atrophy of glands, partial mucosal detachment, brush margin presence, and inflammatory cell infiltration in the gastric propria layer (Fig. 4b). In contrast, H. pylori-infected mice treated with LG3092 (Group LG3092 + SS1 and Group LG3092 + GZ6B5) showed no significant inflammation, the gastric tissue was more similar to the normal group, with neatly arranged, plump, intact epithelial cells, no mucosal shedding, and normal submucosal morphology. Inflammatory cell infiltration and lesions were alleviated (Fig. 4b). Pro-inflammatory factor measurements in gastric tissue showed that in the LG3092 intervention groups, the expression of gastric pro-inflammatory factors nearly normalized, showing a significant downward trend compared to the infection groups (Fig. 4c). These findings suggest that L. garvieae LG3092 can modulate the expression of pro-inflammatory factors and reduces inflammation induced by H. pylori and its associated pathogens.

The anti-inflammatory effect of LG3092 is related to its competitive inhibition of H. pylori in vivo

A standard curve correlating CFU with Ct values was established using the LG3092_specific primers to analyze the colonization level of LG3092 in gastric tissue from mice in each group30. The results showed that LG3092 could successfully colonized the stomach of mice, and H. pylori infection, especially H. pylori GZ6B5 could impede LG3092 colonization to a certain extent. This may be related to competition for gastric mucosal colonization sites (Fig. S2).

According to previous studies31, H. pylori was quantitatively measured, and the results demonstrated that both H. pylori SS1 and GZ6B5 successfully colonized the stomach of mice. Notably, H. pylori SS1, a domesticated standard strain, exhibited a higher colonization rate in the stomach. In mice fed with LG3092 and subsequently infected with H. pylori, colonization levels decreased from 103.17 to 102.33 for SS1 (p < 0.0001) and from 102.95 to 102.18 for GZ6B5 (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 4d). Compared with the H. pylori GZ6B5 with higher pathogenicity, LG3092 has a better inhibitory effect on H. pylori SS1.

To elucidate the selective bactericidal mechanism of GarQ and identify its potential receptors on bacterial membranes, comparative genomic analysis was performed across bacteria exhibiting differential susceptibility to GarQ. The results revealed that GarQ-sensitive Enterococcus faecalis harbors a complete Man-PTS system. In contrast, Listeria monocytogenes lacks the PTS mannitol-specific enzyme IIABC component, while Salmonella typhimurium and Lacticaseibacillus paracasei are deficient in the PTS maltose transporter subunit IIABC. However, all three strains remain sensitive to GarQ, suggesting that the absence of these specific IIABC components does not impair GarQ’s ability to target these bacteria. Notably, Bacillus subtilis and Escherichia coli lack the PTS mannose transporter subunit IID, Enterobacter sakazakii is deficient in subunit IIC, and Bacillus mycoides, H. pylori, and Staphylococcus aureus lack all Man-PTS subunits. These strains uniformly exhibit resistance to GarQ (Figs. 5d and S5 and S6). These findings suggest that the presence of Man-PTS subunits IIC and IID may be critical for GarQ-mediated bacterial targeting and recognition.

GarQ demonstrated resistance to pH variations but was sensitive to enzymatic activity. Kinetic growth curves of bacteria revealed that pH had little effect on antibacterial activity of GarQ, whereas enzymatic treatments nearly inactivated GarQ, as evidenced by the similar bacterial growth curves for enzyme-treated and untreated GarQ.

Discussion

H. pylori is a major cause of chronic gastritis, peptic ulcer, and gastric cancer, with infection rates and pathogenicity differing between developed and developing countries due to variations in virulence factors. CagA, a key virulence factor, induces cytoskeletal rearrangements in host cells, leading to the “hummingbird” phenotype, a precursor to tumor development. Several studies indicated that East Asian-type CagA causes more severe cytoskeletal changes and has a stronger association with gastric cancer than Western-type CagA29. Additionally, the therapeutic effects of probiotics vary among different strains of H. pylori. In this study, we compared the effects of Western-type H. pylori SS1 and East Asian-type H. pylori GZ6B5, finding that GZ6B5 induced more pronounced cellular hummingbird deformation and inflammatory responses in AGS cells, suggesting higher pathogenicity of East Asian type H. pylori (Fig. 1a, b). This difference may be attributed to variations in the CagA types present in H. pylori SS1 and GZ6B5. Furthermore, the cytopathic effects could be influenced by duration and dosage of infection (Fig. S1). Currently, there is limited research on the use of highly virulent East Asian isolates in animal models, and their pathogenicity and mechanisms in vivo remain unclear. Therefore, investigating GZ6B5 infection in mice is essential.

Previous studies have explored the impact of pathogenic bacteria, including H. pylori, on inflammatory factors, revealing that pathogens such as Staphylococcus aureus33, Escherichia coli34, and Salmonella35 activate host immune cells through LPS or other toxins, promoting the upregulation of inflammatory factors. However, the specific trends of these changes have not been thoroughly detailed. This study is the first to refine the relationship between pro-inflammatory bacteria and changes in inflammatory factors. In vivo, infection with H. pylori SS1 and GZ6B5 caused differing levels of gastric inflammation. Contrary to expectations, GZ6B5 causes no more severe inflammation than SS1. In vitro, H. pylori infection mainly increased IL-8 expression (not obvious in vivo), while in vivo, the expression of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α was significantly elevated. The pro-inflammatory effects of GZ6B5 were unstable, even lower than those induced by SS1 (Fig. 1b, e), indicating that other factors may influence the inflammatory response in vivo. Microbial composition analysis revealed that SS1 infection led to more pro-inflammatory bacteria in its core microbiota and more pronounced microbial imbalance than GZ6B5, which may explain the more severe inflammatory response observed with SS1 (Fig. 2c, d). In the absence of H. pylori, Bacillales was negatively correlated with the pro-inflammatory Veillonellales, and anti-inflammatory Lactobacillales was negatively correlated with Clostridiales, Coriobacteriales, indicating low abundance of pathogenic genus within Bacillales and Lactobacillales, both contributing to inflammation suppression. Under H. pylori infection, however, Bacillales showed a negative correlation with anti-inflammatory Lactobacillales, while the correlation between Lactobacillales and the pro-inflammatory Clostridiales, Coriobacteriales disappeared, indicating that H. pylori invasion disrupts the original microbial balance, enhancing pathogenic genus within Bacillales and Lactobacillales, diminishes their anti-inflammatory effects, and elevates inflammation levels. Thus, following H. pylori infection, it is essential to suppress associated pro-inflammatory bacteria while augmenting anti-inflammatory bacteria. Additionally, H. pylori infection resulted in a positive correlation between Campylobacterales and Staphylococcales, Rhizobiales, Corynebacteriales, and Enterobacterales, suggesting synergistic effects that accelerate gastric disease progression under conditions of microbial imbalance (Fig. 2e).

There is reason to suspect that H. pylori is the main cause of the significant up-regulation of IL-8 expression, and the expression of pro-inflammatory factors IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α is mainly affected by other pro-inflammatory bacteria associated with H. pylori, such as Staphylococcales, Enterobacterales, Bacillales, Clostridiales, and etc. (Fig. 7). To better understand the link between these microbial shifts and gastric inflammation, we elucidated the key pro-inflammatory characteristics of the identified taxa. A primary mechanism is the presentation of lipopolysaccharide (LPS) from the outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria. As a potent pathogen-associated molecular pattern (PAMP), LPS can trigger the release of numerous pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6, IL-8) from host cells via the Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) pathway, a common feature in orders such as Enterobacterales, Campylobacterales, and Veillonellales23–25. This LPS-driven inflammatory cascade likely acts synergistically with H. pylori’s own pro-inflammatory factors (e.g., CagA) to exacerbate immune-mediated damage to the gastric mucosa. Beyond LPS, many of these taxa directly disrupt host barriers and functions by secreting specific toxins. For instance, pathogenic species within Clostridiales produce toxins that compromise cell membranes26, while S. aureus from Staphylococcales secretes various hemolysins and exotoxins36. These toxins not only cause direct cell death, releasing damage-associated molecular patterns, but also recruit neutrophils, further amplifying local inflammation. Furthermore, the ecological behavior of certain bacteria is critical. For example, Veillonella species are adept at forming multi-species biofilms27, which could provide structural support and protection for primary pathogens like H. pylori, hindering their clearance. In addition to α-toxin-mediated activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, Staphylococcales employ biofilm formation as a strategy to activate the TLR2/NF-κB pathway, thereby inducing chronic inflammation36. Concurrently, taxa such as Coriobacteriales37 and Clostridiales38 have been linked to the disruption of intestinal barrier integrity, a mechanism that, if active in the stomach, could increase mucosal permeability and allow microbial products to access deeper tissues, thus perpetuating chronic inflammation. These findings highlight that the observed increase in these pro-inflammatory taxa (microbial imbalance) points towards a vicious cycle following H. pylori infection that intensifies inflammation and disrupts mucosal homeostasis through multiple mechanisms. The probiotic intervention in our study likely mitigates gastritis by suppressing these specific microbial groups, thereby breaking this cycle. It is noteworthy that the two H. pylori strains used, SS1 and GZ6B5, differ markedly in host adaptation and strain-specific characteristics. The mouse-adapted SS1 strain, after multiple in vivo passages, exhibits enhanced adhesion to the gastric mucosa and is capable of stable colonization, leading to sustained inflammatory stimulation39,40. In contrast, the clinical human-derived strain GZ6B5 is not fully adapted to the murine gastric environment, displays low colonization efficiency, and is poorly recognized in vivo41. Moreover, inflammation in the mouse model involves complex immune cell networks42,43. Therefore, even highly virulent human strains may fail to robustly activate immune cells in mice, whereas the AGS cell model yields more direct and pronounced inflammatory responses. Conversely, in human-derived AGS cell lines, GZ6B5 exhibits better compatibility with cell surface receptors, potentially triggering stronger inflammatory responses than SS1. These factors—beyond virulence factors (CagA and VacA) or microbial dysbiosis—likely contribute to the observed discrepancies between in vivo and in vitro inflammatory responses. Future studies could employ in vivo adaptation or selective screening of human-derived H. pylori strains to enhance colonization and recognition compatibility in the murine gastric environment. Additionally, co-culture systems incorporating THP-1 macrophages, PBMCs, or neutrophils into the AGS model may provide a closer approximation of immune responses in vivo44.

H. pylori produces urease, increasing gastric pH not only to promote survival and colonization through immune escape mechanisms but may also lead to abnormal gastric acid secretion, disrupting the gastric mucosal barrier and triggering acute gastritis and stomach pain. Additionally, the use of proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) in H. pylori treatment also causes abnormalities in gastric acid secretion; the disruption of the gastric acidic environment allows for the proliferation of pathogenic bacteria, such as Proteus and Staphylococcus, which typically do not colonize the stomach. This further complicates the eradication of H. pylori and the improvement of inflammation. Several studies have explored the impact of PPIs use on the elevation of gastric pH and the subsequent reduction in probiotic efficacy. One study45 on the effects of PPIs on the gastrointestinal microbiome found that PPI excessive use leads to gastrointestinal microbiota dysbiosis and an increase in gastric pH, which in turn reduces the effectiveness of probiotics, particularly Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium. That is to say, although the PPI-induced increase in gastric pH can enhance probiotic survival and competitive inhibition of H. pylori, it may also reduce the efficacy of probiotics that rely on organic acids as their primary antimicrobial agents. In response to this issue, some studies have proposed potential solutions, such as using PPIs at lower doses or intermittently, combining antibiotics as an adjunct, and utilizing gastric acid protectants (e.g., bismuth compounds)46. However, no studies have systematically explored probiotic-based strategies to address this issue. The development of probiotics that can modulate microbial homeostasis while maintaining functionality under wide-pH conditions remains an unmet need. This study highlights the effectiveness of LG3092 and its secreted GarQ even in the presence of elevated pH, thus overcoming the challenge of reduced antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory efficacy. In addition, regulating inflammatory factors and preventing immune imbalance is an effective approach for preventing persistent H. pylori infection and alleviating gastritis-related pain. Probiotics or their metabolites may exert immunomodulatory effects, reducing IL-8 production and inflammatory chemokines, as well as decreasing lymphocyte infiltration18. Correct use of probiotics as adjuncts in H. pylori eradication therapy could enhance eradication efficacy and minimize adverse reactions, especially in multidrug-resistant cases47. Our in vitro results show that L. garvieae LG3092 inhibits pro-inflammatory bacteria in the core microbiota of NC, SS1, and GZ6B5 groups with minimal impact on anti-inflammatory bacteria (Fig. 3a). In a simulated high-pH gastric environment, CFS of LG3092 modulated the composition of the artificial mixed microbial system, reducing inflammation in AGS cells (Fig. 3b, Fig. 4a), suggesting its potential for improving microbial dysbiosis and remains effective during PPI use.

In vivo studies showed that, compared to Group NC, the addition of LG3092 led to a trend toward normalization of the relative abundances of Bacillales, Lachnospirales, Oscillospirales, Clostridiales, Erysipelotrichales, and Coriobacteriales in H. pylori-infected mice, indicating that LG3092 intervention partially ameliorated the dysbiosis induced by H. pylori infection by adjusting the relative abundances of most pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory bacteria toward normal ranges. The relative abundance of Lactobacillales exhibited minimal changes before and after LG3092 intervention. In contrast, the relative abundances of certain pro-inflammatory bacteria, including Bacteroidales, Veillonellales, and Burkholderiales, did not meet expectations, suggesting that the gastric flora imbalance caused by H. pylori was more severe and complex, highlighting the need for prolonged microbiota modulation (Fig. 3f). Additionally, LG3092 was observed to alleviate H. pylori-induced gastric gland atrophy, mucosal shedding, brush border disruption, and inflammatory cell infiltration (Fig. 4b), reducing the IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, and TNF-α expression compared to the H. pylori-infected groups (Fig. 4c). Overall, H. pylori promotes the progression of gastric diseases by increasing the abundance of opportunistic pathogens and decreasing the abundance of probiotics, while L. garvieae LG3092 prevents H. pylori-induced gastric inflammation from worsening and improves gastric microenvironment through a reduction in the abundance of pro-inflammatory bacteria48 and regulation microflora balance.

GarQ, a type IId bacteriocin, exhibits pH stability and protease sensitivity (Fig. 5e), making it highly valuable in treating bacterial infections. Previous studies have indicated that the selective antibacterial activity of GarQ is mediated by the mannose-specific phosphotransferase system (Man-PTS), with its mode of action, potency, and underlying mechanisms varying depending on the bacterial species and their Man-PTS structure21,28. Among the four Man-PTS subunits, only IIC and IID have been identified as class II bacteriocin receptors and are considered essential for GarQ sensitivity49, consistent with the findings of this study. Comparative genomic results showed that GarQ-sensitive strains harbor a complete Man-PTS subunit, while the insensitive bacteria lack some components (most notably subunits IIC or IID) or the entire Man-PTS system (Figs. 5d and S5 and S6). Variations in the amino acid sequences and structural conformations of Man-PTS may account for the difference in the inhibitory curves obtained by GarQ acting on LABs and other pathogenic bacteria (listeria monocytogenes, Enterococcus faecalis, and Salmonella typhimurium) in this study (Fig. S5). This specificity and narrow antibacterial spectrum allow GarQ to target certain pathogens without significantly disturbing microbiota balance (Fig. 7). In addition, the reasons for the different inhibition efficiency of different bacteria may also be related to the amount of GarQ, the initial bacterial number, and the co-incubation time. In this study, the method of measuring the growth kinetics curve was used to explore the bacteriostatic spectrum of GarQ, which could not only determine the minimum bacteriostatic concentration but also indicate the active state of the bacteria.

Although GarQ does not directly eliminate H. pylori (Fig. S6), it effectively inhibits lactic acid bacteria, listeria monocytogenes, Enterococcus faecalis, and Salmonella typhimurium (Fig. 5c), which supports LG3092 to regulate gastric microflora. In this study, GarQ was able to reshape the flora abundance of the artificial mixed bacterial system in a simulated gastric fluid environment and reduce the inflammation of AGS cells (Fig. 6a and b). Studies have shown that lactic acid bacteria generally provide health benefits to the human50. However, excessive amounts may have adverse effects, particularly on the stomach and digestive system. An overabundance of LABs may lead to lactic acid accumulation and increased gastric acid, causing various gastrointestinal discomforts, including nausea, bloating, heartburn, and gastric ulcers. In patients with pre-existing gastric conditions, an excess of LABs may elevate risk of certain gastric diseases; for instance, in individuals with gastric ulcers, excessive lactic acid can irritate ulcer sites, delay healing, and intensify pain. In severe cases, this can lead to gut microbiota imbalance and a reduction in microbial diversity. Thus, more is not necessarily better when it comes to probiotics like lactic acid bacteria; maintaining appropriate supplementation is crucial to support a balanced microenvironment. GarQ has been shown to delay the logarithmic growth phase of lactic acid bacteria, helping to avoid adverse effects from improper dosing while ensuring sufficient levels of probiotics to produce active metabolites and exert beneficial properties. Therefore, it is reasonable to speculate that GarQ may indirectly contribute to the management of H. pylori infection by altering microbial diversity, interaction networks, and microecological balance, as well as inhibiting other gastric bacteria, thereby creating an environment less conducive to the survival of pathogenic bacteria. Regrettably, this study did not explore the specific mechanisms underlying GarQ secretion, target recognition, or binding, nor did it evaluate its in vivo antibacterial or anti-inflammatory efficacy. These aspects warrant focused investigation in future research.

The GarQ/LG3092-mediated regulation of microbial balance is only one of the important contributors to its anti-inflammatory effect; this study focused primarily on microbial equilibrium, potentially overlooking other anti-inflammatory mechanisms. LG3092 exhibits strong colonization potential in the gastrointestinal tract and can competitively inhibit pathogenic bacteria, including H. pylori. GarQ specifically targets and suppresses certain pro-inflammatory pathogens. Both LG3092 and GarQ act to antagonize pathogenic bacteria while minimizing disruption to beneficial microbes, thereby preserving microbial homeostasis (Fig. 7). In addition, probiotics are known to reduce virulence factor load, mitigate oxidative stress, modulate host immunity, and produce beneficial metabolites such as short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs)51,52. As a probiotic, LG3092 may exert anti-inflammatory effects through multiple pathways, and future studies should investigate these alternative mechanisms in greater detail. In addition, this study explored only the dose-response relationship of GarQ’s antagonistic effects against pro-inflammatory bacteria, without evaluating the dose-response of LG3092 CFS or GarQ in relation to inflammation modulation. This could lead to conceptual ambiguity. Some studies have reported that bacteriocins produced by probiotics possess immunomodulatory activity, including the promotion of anti-inflammatory cytokines and regulation of both innate and adaptive immunity53,54. However, to date, there is no direct evidence demonstrating that GarQ alone can alleviate inflammation. Future research should investigate the dose-dependent anti-inflammatory effects of GarQ or probiotic fermentation products in inflammation models, which would be essential for validating their clinical application potential. Such work could also provide a theoretical foundation for the development of next-generation postbiotic products based on microbial metabolites.

This study is the first to detail the relationship between H. pylori and other pro-inflammatory bacteria and acute inflammatory factors. This highlights the importance of a comprehensive assessment that combines multiple inflammatory factors, emphasizing that, aside from IL-8, other acute inflammatory factors in the body also warrant attention. This study aims to elucidate the potential mechanisms by which probiotics combat gastric H. pylori infection, providing insights for the application of next-generation probiotics in the treatment of H. pylori infections.

Methods

Lactic acid bacteria (LAB) and Pathogenic microbes

LAB strains were isolated and cultured as described by Li et al.55. In brief, 275 LAB strains were obtained from soil, fermented foods, herbal products, animal and human feces, and gastric samples. They were cultured in De Man Rogosa Sharpe (MRS) medium (Huankai, Guangzhou, Guangdong, China) for 48 h at 37 °C in an anaerobic workstation (Don Whitley Scientific, W Yorkshire, UK). Cell-free supernatant (CFS) was prepared by centrifugation at 4 °C, 10,000 × g for 2 min, followed by filtration through a 0.22 μm sterile filter.

Two H. pylori strains were employed: the mouse-adapted SS1 strain (ATCC 43504 derivative; EPIYA-ABC CagA, s2/m2 VacA), obtained from the Guangdong Microbial Culture Collection Center (GDMCCC), and the clinical isolate GZ6B5 (EPIYA-ABD CagA, s1/m1 VacA), sourced from a chronic gastritis patient in Guangdong, China56. Both strains were cultured in Columbia-based serum medium (Huankai) under microaerophilic conditions (5% O2, 10% CO2, 85% N2) for 48-96 h at 37 °C57. Other bacterial strains, including Salmonella typhimurium ATCC 14028, Listeria monocytogenes ATCC 19115, Enterococcus faecalis ATCC 29212, Bacillus subtilis ATCC 6633, Bacillus mycoides ATCC 10206, Escherichia coli ATCC 25922, Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 29213, and Bacillus cereus ATCC 14579, were purchased from GDMCC and cultured in LB broth (HuanKai) at 37 °C for 24 h. Clostridium difficileZHCD1203, kindly provided by Prof. Jianhui Chen in Sun Yat-sen University and was cultured anaerobic at 37 °C for 48 h in Reinforced Clostridium Medium (RCM) (HuanKai).

Bacterial identification and phylogenetic analysis

Taxonomic analysis of the isolated strains was performed by Sanger sequencing (Genewiz, Suzhou, China) of the 16S rRNA gene as described by Chen et al.58. In brief, genomic DNA of the bacteria was extracted using a genomic DNA extraction kit (Magen Biotech, Guangzhou, China). The 16S rRNA gene was amplified using universal primer 27 F and 1492 R (Table S1)59. The 16S rRNA gene sequence was compared with GenBank (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/BLAST) reference sequences and constructed the phylogenetic tree.

Cells and coculture

Human gastric adenocarcinoma cells (AGS) were donated by Prof. Jianhui Chen (Sun Yat-sen University) and cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, USA) with 10% (vol/vol) fetal bovine serum (Gibco, Waltham, USA) and an antibiotic-antifungal mixture (100 U penicillin, 100 μg/mL streptomycin, 0.25 μg/mL amphotericin B; UT HyClone, USA), in a 5% CO2 incubator (Thermo Fisher) at 37 °C.

Co-incubation of AGS cells with pathogenic bacteria (especially H. pylori) was performed as described by Nemidkanam V60, using a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 1:100 for 24 hours. The culture medium and culture conditions were the same as those used for AGS cell culture. AGS cells without bacteria served as control (Group Blank). After co-incubation, cells were washed with sterile PBS, and cell morphological changes were observed under an optical microscope (DM6 B, Leica, Wetzlar, Germany). The cells were then collected for qPCR analysis of IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, and TNF-α expression.

Screening of the antibacterial potential of LABs

Well diffusion method, as described by Li et al.61, was used to screen the anti-pathogenic potential of LAB strains. Briefly, 100 μL of bacterial suspensions (1×108 CFU/mL) were cultured on agar plates, and 100 μL CFS of the candidate LABs was added into Oxford cups, refrigerated for 6 h, and cultured under the same conditions as the tested bacteria described in the section “Lactic acid bacteria (LAB) and Pathogenic microbes”. The inhibition zone was measured after 48 h62,63. A scoring system was applied based on the inhibition diameter and bacterial strain type according to the criteria in Table S2.

Determination of inflammatory factors expression

Expression of IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, and TNF-α in AGS cells60 and mice gastric tissues64 was quantified using real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR). Total RNA was extracted using a RNeasy Mini kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) and converted into cDNA with the Evo M-MLV reverse transcription reagent master mix (Accurate Biology, Hunan, China). RT-qPCR was performed with SYBR Premix Ex Taq II (Accurate Biology), and primers of the targeted genes are listed in Table S1. The mRNA expression levels were normalized to GAPDH based on 2−△△CT method65.

Effect of GarQ and CFS on inflammation induced by artificial mixed bacteria system

Based on the literature and animal experimental results, an artificial mixed bacteria system was configured. The bacteria used included Lactobacillus, Entercoccus, Bacillus, Listeria, Clostridium, Escherichia, Salmonella66, Staphylococcus, and Helicobacter. The culture system was artificial simulated gastric juice (LB liquid medium + 10% fetal bovine serum + 3.0 g/L pepsin). The relative abundance of each bacterial strain was adjusted, with CFS (pH = 6) added at a concentration of 100 μL/mL and GarQ at a final concentration of 125 μg/mL. After incubation at 37 °C under microaerophilic condition for 6 h, mixed bacteria centrifuge 10,000 × g for 2 min and suspension with PBS. The mixed bacterial system treated with GarQ at 0 h underwent the same centrifugation and resuspension procedures as the 6 h GarQ-treated group and served as the control. After co-culturing the various treated mixed bacterial systems with AGS cells for 12 hours, the expression levels of inflammatory cytokines in the cells were measured.

Quantification evaluation of the gastric microbes

The method of Chen et al.67 was used to calculate the relative abundance of bacteria (Total bacteria, Enterococcus, Clostridium67, Lactobacillus68, Bacillus69, Listeria70, Escherichia, Staphylococcus71,Salmonella71, and Helicobacter31), H. pylori, and LG3092. The primers used are listed in Table S3. Quantification assays of the gastric microbes were conducted on Roche LightCycler® (Roche, Nutley, NJ, USA). The value of 2−∆Ct was calculated for the quantification of each species of bacteria.

Experimental animal and treatment protocols

6-8 weeks old female SPF C57BL/6 mice (Guangdong Medical Laboratory Animal Center, Guangzhou, China) were stable for 1 week prior to the experiment. The animals were housed in a climate-controlled facility (24 °C, 50% humidity, 12-hour light/dark cycle) with free access to food and water72. Following a 3-day triple antibiotic therapy (Ampicillin 3 mg/d, gentamicin 0.36 mg/d, Azithromycin 3 mg/d)73, 30 mice were randomly assigned to various experimental groups74, 6 mice was considered as one experimental unit. From day 4, proton pump inhibitors (omeprazole 3 mg/kg/day) were administered 2 h before gastric filling with H. pylori/LG3092/PBS suspension75.

To evaluate the effects of different H. pylori strains, mice in groups of SS1 and GZ6B5 were orally infected with 5 × 108 CFU of H. pylori every 2 days for 3 times, while the control group (group NC) received PBS (Fig. 1c). L. garvieae LG3092 (5 × 108 CFU/day) was administered for 6 consecutive days to evaluate its anti-H. pylori effects in vivo. Mice in groups of SS1, GZ6B5, LG3092 + SS1 and LG3092 + GZ6B5 were infected by oral gavages with H. pylori, and the SS1 and GZ6B5 groups served as controls receiving PBS instead of LG3092 (Fig. 3e). On day 16, all C57BL/6 mice were euthanized under deep anesthesia induced by intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital (100 mg/kg), followed by cervical dislocation. And gastric tissue was equantly divided into 4 parts for histological analyses, microbiome sequencing, inflammatory factor analysis, and H. pylori culture.

The animal experiments were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Guangdong Provincial Academy of Medical Sciences, under approval number GT-IACUC202407152.

Histological analyses

Paraformaldehyde-fixed stomach tissues were embedded in paraffin, sectioned at 5 mm, and then stained using a hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining kit (Solarbio, Beijing, China) according to the manufactor’s instruction. The pathological damage and inflammation of stomach tissues were observed with a optical microscope (Leica, DM6 B, Germany).

Microbiome 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing

The gastric microbiome was analyzed by 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing according to the methods of Chen et al.76. Microbial DNA was extracted using QIAamp PowerFecal Pro DNA Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). 338 F and 806 R (Table S1) were used to amplify the V3-V4 region of 16S rRNA. The amplicon sequencing was conducted on the Illumina MiSeq PE300 platform (Illumina, San Diego, USA). The noise of the optimized sequence after the quality control stitching was reduced using DADA277 in the Qiime2 process, generating an Amplicon sequence variant (ASV). Based on the SILVA database (v138), ASVs were analyzed using the Naive bayes classifier in Qiime2. Moreover, functional prediction analysis of microbial communities was performed using PICRUSt278.

Correlation analysis and regulation of core microflora

Alpha diversity, beta diversity, and microbial abundance were analyzed on the Majorbio cloud platform (www.majorbio.com). Core microflora (present in 90% of samples, with relative abundance >1%) were identified and analyzed via MicrobiomeAnalyst79. Correlation analysis with inflammatory cytokine assessed the impact of distinct microbial communities on H. pylori-associated gastritis. The bacterial interaction network was constructed using MicrobiomeAnalyst and visually refined with Cytoscape software80.

Quantification of H. pylori and LG3092 within Mice Stomachs

Pan-genomic analysis of L. garvieae LG3092 and 119 other L. garvieae isolates in the NCBI database using Prokka (v1.11) and Roary (v3.11.2) software31. Once the core genome was obtained, Gubbins (v2.4.1) was used to identify genes containing a higher density of base substitutions, and the specific sequence of L. garvieae LG3092 was obtained which was different from that of other LABs. Primers were designed for the specific sequence using Oligo (v7) software (Molecular Biology Insights, USA).

Quantification assays of H. pylori31 and LG309230 performed using qPCR, with primers listed in Table S181. Assays were conducted on Roche LightCycler® (Roche, Nutley, NJ, USA) with the annealing temperature of 56.5 °C for H. pylori and 68 °C for LG3092. Read amplification results using the LightCycler®96 SW 1.1 (Roche).

Whole genome sequencing

Genomic DNA libraries were both constructed by AMT Rapid DNA-Seq Kits (CISTRO, Guangzhou, China) and Rapid Barcoding Sequencing Kit (Nanopore, Oxford, UK) for sequencing on the Nextseq 550 platform (Illumina, CA, USA) as well as the Nanopore MinION platform (Nanopore, Oxford, UK). The Illumina and Nanopore reads were aligned into de novo assembled contigs using Unicycler software (v0.4.8)82.

Genome annotation and comparative genomics analysis

Pan-genome analysis was performed on Prokka output using Roary (v3.11.2) with a BLASTP identity cut-off of 95%, and Gubbins was used for recombination analysis and remove putative recombined regions83. Comparative genomic analysis of various bacterial strains was conducted, including L. garvieae, Salmonella typhimurium, Listeria monocytogenes, Enterococcus faecalis, Enterobacter sakazakii, Bacillus subtilis, Bacillus mycoides, Escherichia coli, Staphylococcus aureus, Lactiplantibacillus plantarum, Lacticaseibacillus paracasei, and H. pylori.

Bioinformatics analysis and preparation of the bacteriocin

The amino acid sequences of bacteriocin were analyzed using Protein BLAST and CDD (Conserved Domain Database) in NCBI (www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure/cdd/wrpsb.cgi) to identify homologous proteins and predict conserved domains. The SWISS-MODEL (swissmodel.expasy.org) was used to predict three-dimensional structures of bacteriocin.

The expression of bacteriocin coding gene was evaluated by PCR amplification according to the method of Malesevic M, and the primers Bac_F and Bac_R used are shown in Table S184. GarQ, with over 95% purity, was synthesized chemically (PepMic, Suzhou, China) and dissolved in 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid (Sigma, Darmstadt, Germany) before use.

Inhibition kinetics of bacterial growth and the minimum antibacterial concentration of bacteriocin

GarQ was tested at different concentrations (1000 μg/ml, 500 μg/mL, 250 μg/mL, 125 μg/mL, 62.5 μg/mL, 31.3 μg/mL, 15.6 μg/mL, 7.8 μg/mL) against bacterial suspensions in the logarithmic growth period. The OD600 value was measured over time (0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 24, and 48 h) under aerobic, anaerobic, and microaerophilic conditions at 37 °C using a Multiskan Sky Microplate Reader (Thermo Scientific).

Stability of the bacteriocin

The pH of GarQ was regulated from 2 to 9, incubate at 37 °C for 2 h, the reset to pH 7. It was also treated with proteases (pepsin, trypsin, papain, proteinase K) at 1 mg/mL, incubated at 37 °C for 1 h, and added to Enterococcus faecalis ATCC 29212 cultures at the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC). Bacteriostatic activity was assessed by measuring OD600 at 9 time points (0 h, 2 h, 4 h, 6 h, 8 h, 10 h, 12 h, 24 h, 48 h).

Residue of bacteriostatic activity (%) =[1 - (TOD600 – NTOD600)/NCOD600] × 100%。TOD600 is the absorbance value of the culture supplemented with the treated bacteriocin, NTOD600 is the absorbance value of the culture supplemented with the untreated bacteriocin, NCOD600 is the absorbance value of the culture without the addition of the bacteriocin.

Preliminary study on the mechanism of bacteriocin

Cell leakage was measured by adding GarQ at its MIC to cultures of Salmonella typhimurium (1 × 102 CFU/mL) and Enterococcus faecalis (1 × 102 CFU/mL), incubated at 37 °C, 120 r/min. Conductivity was measured at various time points (0, 2, 4, 8, and 12 h) to assess cell membrane disruption. The bacterial solution without bacteriocin was used as blank control.

For visualization of cell damage, Salmonella typhimurium was incubated with GarQ at its MIC for 6 h. The bacterial cells were stained using LIVE/DEADTM BacLightTM Bacterial Viability Kit (Thermo Scientific) and examined under a LSM 700 Laser Confocal Microscope (ZEISS, Oberkochen, Germany).

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed by unpaired t-test or by one-way ANOV A using GraphPad Prism® software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, America). Normality tested using Shapiro–Wilk test. The p-values of < 0.05 (*), < 0.01 (**), and < 0.001 (***) were considered significant. The mean ± standard deviation (SD) for at least three independent experiments was calculated.
