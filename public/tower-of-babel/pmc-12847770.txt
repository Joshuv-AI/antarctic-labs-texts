The adaptive tasks and trade-offs that drive the human value system
Authors: Noam Markovitch, Dan Hilman Amir, Rony Zer Kavod, Ariel Knafo-Noam, Yuval Hart
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Human values inform behaviors, attitudes, and beliefs, but what shapes the human value system? Here, we employ Pareto analysis on the European Social Survey data (N = 411,904). Pareto analysis relies on an optimization framework to extract the drivers that shape people’s individual variations. We found that individual differences in values are linked to balancing trade-offs between three adaptive tasks: Self-enhancement, Growth, and Conservation. Notably, value combinations that represent non-adaptive trade-off solutions, though considered theoretically possible, are absent from the data. These adaptive tasks are robust across two global samples, multiple countries, different religiosity levels, and age groups. Beyond identifying adaptive tasks, Pareto analysis provided a framework for assessing trade-off shifts across development and religiosity levels. This work paves the way for investigating the tasks’ etiology and their underlying mechanisms. More broadly, Pareto analysis offers a principled approach to understanding individual differences in humans, revealing the adaptive tasks and trade-offs that drive complex psychological systems.

Introduction

Many psychological and social systems show systematic individual differences. An important example is the human value system, where individuals display significant variation in what they consider important1. What might be the underlying forces driving individual differences within a system? One potential driver of such differences is the organism’s need to balance between different competing tasks. Schwartz, whose theory of values is widely applied, postulated that values are derived from three universal requirements inherent to human existence2,3: the basic needs of individuals as biological entities, the demands for coordinated social interaction, and the survival and well-being of groups. However, this hypothesis was never directly examined, and it remains unclear whether values are truly driven by different adaptive tasks, what these tasks are, and how individual differences stem from the need to balance such tasks.

To explore these questions, we rely on Pareto optimality theorem4–7, which is widely applied to biological, economic, and engineered systems, and reveals the underlying adaptive tasks that drive individual differences within a system or organism. Applying this theorem to individual differences in human values can address fundamental questions about the adaptive tasks governing human values. In this context, we use the term “adaptive tasks” to refer to functional adaptive objectives, which can be inferred as drivers of individual variation using the Pareto framework.

Pareto optimality theorem4–7 posits that individual differences in the phenotypic space represent different optimal solutions for trade-offs between competing adaptive tasks. According to Pareto optimality, optimal phenotypes of organisms that need to balance competing tasks reside within a low-dimensional polytope6,7. For two competing tasks, the polytope is a line, for three tasks, it is a triangle, for four tasks, a tetrahedron, and so on. A vertex of the polytope is called an archetype, and it represents the optimal phenotype for a single adaptive task. For example, Darwin’s finches6,8 have three dietary sources (i.e., adaptive tasks): hard seeds, soft seeds, and insects and nectar. Each dietary source has an optimized beak shape (i.e., an archetype). In each island, there is a different composition of nutrients that dictates different balances between the three archetypal beak morphologies. Thus, depending on their specific niche, different organisms (e.g., finches) may exhibit a myriad of different (beak) phenotypes, that Pareto optimality will attribute to different balances of trade-offs between competing (dietary) tasks. One can reverse this logic: if a dataset falls within a polytope, Pareto optimality suggests it may correspond to phenotypes optimizing competing tasks, represented by the vertices of the polytope. The enriched (overrepresented) features of the data points near the polytope’s vertices characterize the adaptive tasks and trade-offs the organism adheres to7.

Unlike clustering methods (e.g., k-means, latent class analysis), which group individuals based on similarity and assign them to clusters defined by a central profile or centroid, Pareto analysis does not classify individuals into discrete types. Instead, it assumes continuous variation that cannot be distinctly separated and is guided by the principle that individuals balance competing adaptive tasks according to their unique ecological or social niches7. Therefore, two main differences arise: First, the Pareto approach is especially well suited to continuous datasets, where forcing a categorical separation between close-by data points might be misleading and statistically underpowered (see also ref. 7). Second, conceptually, Pareto analysis is a hybrid of theory-driven and data-driven approaches, as it relies on the theoretical assumption that trade-offs between competing adaptive tasks are the main source of individual variation, with each individual optimizing the balance between these adaptive tasks.

Pareto analysis has been highly fruitful in studying the driving forces behind individual differences in biological systems, such as: animals’ morphology6, mice behaviors9, the differentiation of gut cells10, breast cancer cells7, immune cells11, dietary patterns12, and time preferences for reward13. Here, we ask whether such an approach can be used in psychological sciences to discern the competing adaptive tasks underlying individual differences in human values.

Personal values represent what people deem important and worthy; they are broad and desirable goals that are relevant across situations and serve as guiding principles in individuals’ lives14,15. For each person, some values are more important than others. Based on individuals’ needs and societal survival requirements, Schwartz identified ten core values, organized in a circular structure1. The circular structure represents compatibilities between neighboring values and conflicts between opposing values (see Fig. 1A). These values are grouped into four higher-order values: self-enhancement versus self-transcendence, and openness to change versus conservation. Additionally, their circular organization is structured by two organizing principles that span two important axes: the distinction between values that express growth versus self-protection motivations, and between values focused on personal versus social outcomes (see Fig. 1A). Schwartz’s circular structure has been demonstrated repeatedly, with hundreds of samples from dozens of cultures. Within the domain of values, adaptive tasks may compete at both cognitive and behavioral levels. For example, considering Schwartz’ hypothesized universal tasks2,3, a competition between basic needs of the individual as a biological entity and the survival and well-being of groups may influence both competing motivations (e.g., ego-based versus empathy-based) and competing actions (e.g., advancing one’s own status versus cooperating with the group).Fig. 1Schwartz’s theory of values and study overview.A Schwartz’s theory of values1: definitions and structure, demonstrating the 10 basic values, four higher-order values, and two underlying organizing principles (adapted from Schwartz, 201664). Here, we used Pareto analysis7 on 411,904 individuals from 38 countries, retrieved from the European Social Survey (ESS; nine rounds between 2002 and 201816–24), to infer the adaptive tasks and trade-offs underlying individual differences in the value system. We also replicated our analysis using a large global sample (N = 104,445; 52 countries) from the World Values Survey (WVS)25,26.

Methods

Data and participants

The ESS is a biennial cross-sectional multi-country survey. We retrieved data from nine ESS rounds between 2002 and 201816–24, which comprised a representative sample from 38 countries, with a 95% age range between 17 and 82. As no new data were collected in this study and the analysis was conducted solely on publicly available data, we did not obtain informed consent ourselves, nor did we preregister the study. This research was approved by the Faculty of Social Sciences Ethics Committee at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem.

For each round of the ESS (Nrange per round = 40,185–56,752, Ntotal = 430,870), we first calculated the ten value scores, as outlined in the section below. For each dataset of each ESS round, we excluded participants who reported identical value scores across all values (range across rounds: 132–600, total: 2286), as such responses likely reflect either careless answering or a lack of value prioritization, the focus of the current study. We note that these excluded individuals would result in data points at the center of the data and thus would not affect the outcomes of the Pareto analysis. Additionally, rows containing missing mean value scores were eliminated (range across rounds: 837–4824, total: 16,680). The distinct ESS rounds were then combined into a single composite sample of 411,904 participants, serving as the basis for subsequent analyses. Because outliers can have a significant influence on the results of the data structure found using the Pareto task inference method (ParTI; see Supplementary Fig. 9 in ref. 7), we used a peeling method. Peeling is specifically designed to reduce the influence of extreme points in multidimensional space by iteratively removing the outer layers of the data (the points spanning the convex hull, i.e., the envelope of the data)7. This approach better preserves the underlying geometric structure that Pareto analysis aims to reveal, as well as the correlations between the different dimensions. Therefore, the core dataset for all analyses was a peeled dataset, where we removed the ten outer layers of the convex hull of the data, resulting in a base sample of 411,570 participants (removing 0.08% of the data). For the analysis of the entire sample, we peeled an additional 30 layers (i.e., 40 peels in total, N = 409,862) to avoid simplexes that are created by outliers only. When subsamples were analyzed (e.g., different ESS rounds, different religiosity levels), in addition to the ten-layer peeling, we also removed outliers from each group separately, peeling an additional 20 to 30 layers, depending on group sample sizes in each analysis.

We also aimed to replicate our findings in an additional global dataset. To this end, we analyzed data from Waves 5 and 6 of the WVS25,26, a multi-wave cross-national survey. These waves include a 10-item value battery inspired by Schwartz’s framework1. In total, 161,671 participants had available data on values, from 66 countries spanning Europe, the Americas, Africa, the Middle East, and the Asia–Pacific region. After applying the same procedure and exclusion criteria described above, 104,445 participants remained, and following peeling, the final sample included 102,727 individuals. Demographic information for the sample is presented in Table 1.

Measures

Values

The ESS measures individuals’ values using the 21-item version of the Portrait Values Questionnaire (PVQ-2127), which measures values based on Schwartz’s theory of basic human values1. The PVQ presents participants with short descriptions of what is important to a specific individual (e.g., “It is important to him to be rich. He wants to have a lot of money and expensive things”). Participants were requested to rate the degree to which the individual described is similar to themselves, on a scale from 1 (“Very much like me”) to 6 (“Not like me at all”). Ten scale scores were calculated based on these ratings, for each value from Schwartz’s value theory. For each individual, their mean response was calculated and subtracted from the calculation of each of their scale scores, to correct for differences in response scale use and according to the PVQ’s instructions. Then, value scores were standardized within each ESS round (after excluding participants with identical value scores throughout, see Data and participants). Finally, to improve interpretability, we reversed the archetypes’ value profiles such that a higher value score indicates a higher importance of the value.

Statistical analysis

Pareto analysis of values

To determine the effective dimensionality of the values data, we applied the “elbow” method to the explained variance curve. The “elbow” method identifies the optimal number of components by plotting the cumulative explained variance against the number of components and selecting the point where additional components yield diminishing returns, creating an “elbow” in the curve. Using the minimal number of dimensions that capture a substantial portion of the variability balances information retention with noise reduction and improves the interpretability of the results. The “elbow” method indicated that the values dataset is well described by two dimensions (representing 47% of the variance; Supplementary Fig. 1). We therefore implemented the ParTI method7 on the first two principal components of the data. Unless specified otherwise, most analyses were conducted using the SISAL algorithm28, and implemented in Python version 3.8.

Despite the advantages of attaining such a large dataset (409,862), it also introduces computational complexity in the calculation of the minimal simplex. To achieve tractable computations in finite times, we used a bootstrapping approach that focuses the analysis on the highest density regions of the datasets:we sampled subsamples randomly (with replacement) and computed the simplex structure. We next outline the general method used for all analyses, with specific details provided in the Supplementary Methods and figure legends. In each analysis, we sampled 200 samples of 15,000 to 60,000 data points (depending on the original sample size for each specific analysis) to extract the distribution of archetypes’ positions across the different repetitions. Subsequently, we determined the average archetype position within each archetype position distribution and identified, among the 200 samples, the sample with archetypes closest to the computed average archetypes. We calculated the t-ratio (the simplex volume divided by the convex-hull volume7), and performed 1000 permutations of the dataset, each involving the calculation of the minimal bounding simplex and its corresponding t-ratio value. The p value of the t-ratio test was determined as the proportion of instances where the permuted t-ratio was smaller or equal to the unpermuted t-ratio, indicating a better bounding ratio between the simplex and the data’s convex hull.

Enrichment analysis of socio-demographic characteristics associated with each adaptive task

Enrichment analysis uses complementary data (socio-demographic information) on each individual to link specific features to specific adaptive tasks, examining whether these features are maximally (or minimally) enriched at the areas closest to each archetype7. An enriched feature means that this specific feature is more likely to appear among individuals who are closer to the archetype compared to its probability in the rest of the population. We tested enrichment patterns for the continuous variables: age, years of education, household total net income, number of people living in the household, and religiosity; and for the categorical variables: having children (yes/no), marital status, gender, and country. For the purposes of estimating enrichment, we calculated the density profile of each feature as a function of distance from the different archetypes. To that aim, we rank data points (individuals) based on their proximity to each archetype and for each archetype, we then partitioned the data points into 20 bins of distance from the archetype, each containing 5% of the sample. The bins are arranged such that the first bin comprises individuals closest to the archetype, while the last bin consists of those farthest from it.

To estimate enrichment patterns of continuous variables, we calculated the mean feature value in each of the bins. The significance of enrichment is calculated based on a Mann–Whitney test29, comparing the first bin to all the remaining bins. To estimate enrichment patterns of the categorical variables, we first transformed each level of the feature to a Boolean feature (i.e., individuals receiving a value of 1 if the category applies to them, or 0 otherwise). We then calculated the fold enrichment—the relative density of the feature in each of the bins compared to the density in the entire sample. The significance of enrichment was calculated based on the hypergeometric test, comparing the density of the first bin to the density in the remaining bins. The significance of depletion patterns was calculated by flipping the Boolean values (individuals being assigned a value of 1 if the category does not apply to them, and 0 otherwise). Significance tests for all enrichment features are reported after a Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction30 (α = 0.1).

In the Pareto analysis algorithm, the computation of enrichment patterns involves evaluating the enrichment of a feature within the first bin relative to the remaining bins7. To further assess the monotonicity of each enrichment pattern relative to its distance from the archetype, we calculated a correlation coefficient. For continuous features, we calculated Spearman correlations. For categorical features, we calculated the rank-biserial correlation. The correlation coefficients indicate the effect size of the feature’s enrichment.

Trends of archetypes’ value scores across religiosity levels and age

To estimate the statistical significance of trends in archetypes’ value scores across different religiosity levels and age groups, we calculated the archetypes’ positions for each religiosity/age group. We then converted these archetype positions into the 10 value scores for each adaptive task within each group. Next, we calculated the Spearman correlations between religiosity level/age group and the value scores for each value independently, for each task separately. We repeated this calculation for each one of the 200 bootstrapped samples and calculated the mean correlation and its corresponding 95% confidence interval.

Results

Values data structure falls within a robust triangle with three archetypes

To understand if individual differences in values are driven by trade-offs between adaptive tasks, we started by asking whether the values data falls within a well-defined polytope as expected from Pareto optimality theorem (Fig. 1B)6,7. Employing a principal components analysis (PCA) and the “elbow” method7, we determined that the data is well-explained by the first two components of the data (explaining 47% of the variance; Supplementary Fig. 1). Notably, the two principal components reflect the two organizing principles from Schwartz’s theory31, with one component reflecting values focused on personal versus social outcomes and the second component reflecting values that express growth versus self-protection motivations (Supplementary Fig. 1). In two dimensions, the bounding polytope should be a triangle. We then employed Pareto analysis to test the triangular structure of the data. A triangle with three vertices significantly fitted the data (Fig. 2A; t-ratio test7,32: p = 0.028, see Methods). Statistical tests of Pareto analysis using a different algorithm (i.e., PCHA33) and on higher-dimensional data supported the finding that a triangle best represented the data (Supplementary Figs. 1, 2 and Supplementary Methods 1). While the PCHA algorithm indicated that only a two-dimensional triangle significantly fit the data (p < 0.01; four-archetype solution: p = 0.70; five-archetype solution: p = 0.33), the SISAL algorithm also identified four- and five-archetype solutions with significant or marginally significant fits (four-archetype solution: p = 0.06; five-archetype solution: p = 0.03). However, the additional archetypes in these higher-dimensional solutions produced value profiles that were not coherent with Schwartz’s well-established value circle1 (i.e., adjacent values sometimes displayed opposite patterns; Supplementary Fig. 1). Moreover, the three-archetype solution showed greater structural stability: the mean variance in archetype positions (normalized to the mean simplex size in the corresponding dimension) was 0.08 across different bootstrap runs, compared to 0.21 and 0.36 in the four- and five-archetype solutions, respectively. We conducted several tests to confirm the robustness of archetypes’ positions: First, archetypes’ positions were stable across different numbers of peeled layers (see Methods and Supplementary Fig. 3). Second, we analyzed the data from each round of ESS separately (every 2 years between 2002 and 2018; Supplementary Methods 2) and found similar archetype positions across ESS rounds (Supplementary Fig. 4). In addition, to test the robustness of these findings across different aggregation levels of the data, we repeated the Pareto analysis using either the original item-level data or a previously suggested alternative value aggregation method to seven values34–37, finding similar archetypes across all aggregation levels (see Supplementary Fig. 5). Finally, we replicated the results of our Pareto analysis using global data from the WVS25,26, which includes a ten-item value battery inspired by Schwartz’s framework, and again recovered the same triangular, three-archetype structure, supporting the generalizability of our findings (see Supplementary Fig. 6).

The position of each archetype in the values space indicates the characteristics of the adaptive task it optimizes (Fig. 2B): The first archetype, termed Self-enhancement, emphasizes mainly values that focus on the interests of the self (i.e., power and achievement) and deemphasizes values that focus on the interests of others (i.e., benevolence and universalism). The second archetype, termed Growth, emphasizes values that express growth motivations (i.e., self-direction, benevolence, universalism, stimulation, and hedonism), and deemphasizes values expressing self-protection motivations (i.e., conformity, security, and power). Finally, the third archetype, termed Conservation, emphasizes values that focus on preserving the status-quo (i.e., tradition, conformity, and security), and deemphasizes values that focus on personal outcomes (i.e., stimulation, hedonism, self-direction, and achievement). While some values are emphasized to varying degrees across multiple archetypes, each archetype was defined by the values it emphasizes most. These dominant patterns align with Schwartz’s theory1 and informed our labeling of the archetypes (see Fig. 1A). The value profiles reveal the trade-offs underlying value priorities: (1) Social dominance in relation to the group (Self-enhancement) versus keeping the group’s social fabric intact (Conservation). (2) Progress through change (Growth) versus maintaining the status-quo (Conservation), and (3) Focus on the self regardless of others (Self-enhancement) versus focus on the self as part of a global community of individuals (Growth).

Importantly, while Schwartz’s view on values allows any value profile to exist as long as it adheres to the circular structure1 (nearby values can co-exist, opposite direction values cannot co-exist), the triangular structure of the dataset implies that specific regions in the phenotypic values space are inhabitable. These value profiles, while possible in theory, according to the Pareto optimality theorem represent non-adaptive balancing between the competing adaptive tasks and thus do not exist in the phenotypic values space (see Fig. 2C). For example, a value profile that emphasizes Self-transcendence values (i.e., universalism and benevolence) but not openness to change values (i.e., self-direction and stimulation) is theoretically possible, yet it does not appear within the phenotypic value space.

Enrichment analysis reveals the socio-demographic characteristics associated with each adaptive task

The Pareto optimality theorem posits that the data’s confinement within a low-dimensional polytope indicates that the tasks represented by the archetypes are adaptive6,7. If a task is indeed adaptive, we would also expect a phenotype optimized for performing that task to be more prevalent in environments aligned with it. Returning to Darwin’s finches6,8, we would expect that a beak shape best suited for eating hard seeds would likely be more common in environments where such seeds are the primary dietary resource. Similarly, we would expect, for example, the value profile optimized for the task Conservation to be more prevalent in contexts relevant for this task—for instance, in more religious countries or among older individuals.

Enrichment analysis uses complementary data on each individual to test associations between specific features to specific adaptive tasks, allowing us to assess whether tasks are indeed more prevalent in contexts aligned with them. To this end, we examined the enrichment patterns for each archetype—we quantified the levels of each feature versus the distance from the archetype. The underlying assumption is that features related to the specific adaptive task should be maximally enriched (or depleted) near the archetype, and will show a significant decrease (increase) in feature levels as the distance from the archetype grows7. In other words, enrichment can be viewed as an overrepresentation, where overrepresentation means that a specific feature is more likely to appear among individuals that are closer to the archetype compared to its probability in the rest of the population.

We found that each adaptive task was associated with an overrepresentation (enrichment) or underrepresentation (depletion) of several notable socio-demographic features (see Fig. 3, Supplementary Fig. 7, and Supplementary Table 2 for full results). The Self-enhancement task showed an overrepresentation of features such as younger age, unmarried status, not having children, and living in larger households. The Growth task showed an overrepresentation of features such as higher education, younger age, lower religiosity, and unmarried status. Lastly, the Conservation task showed an overrepresentation of features such as older age, higher religiosity, and married status. Additionally, different countries showed enrichment patterns near the archetypes. To exemplify these patterns, we highlight some countries with the strongest effect sizes for each archetype: Self-enhancement showed overrepresentation in countries such as Serbia, Lithuania, and the Russian Federation; Growth showed overrepresentation in countries such as Iceland, Sweden, and Denmark; and Conservation showed overrepresentation in countries such as Poland, Romania, and Spain. Values adaptive tasks are robust across different countries

Considering the different enrichment patterns observed across various countries for the adaptive tasks, one could argue that the tasks actually reflect different priorities that are culture-dependent. Indeed, it is repeatedly found that cultures show substantial variation in value priorities14,15,38–41 (but also similarities between cultures in value hierarchies14,15,38,42,43). In such a case, different cultures may push individuals towards different sets of values, such that the triangular structure only reflects different culture types at each vertex rather than functional trade-offs between competing adaptive tasks (Fig. 4A). To test this, we created three groups, each with the data of countries enriched for each adaptive task separately (e.g., a group containing participants from all countries that were significantly enriched for the adaptive task Growth, see Fig. 3D and Supplementary Methods 3). If the tasks we found are merely a depiction of different cultures, there would be no significant Pareto-like structure (a triangle) in the distinct groups of enriched countries (Fig. 4A). Contrary, if the tasks and their trade-offs are robust across different countries, the structure of each group would be a similar triangle (with the same value profile for each adaptive task). For each of the three country groups we found that a triangular structure best describes the data (Self-enhancement: p = 0.027; Growth: p < 0.001; Conservation: p = 0.014), and that the tasks’ value profiles are similar to those found in the entire data (Fig. 4B and Supplementary Fig. 8). Values adaptive tasks are robust across religiosity levels

Another alternative explanation for the triangular structure is that it reflects restrictions and norms created by religion, such that individuals who are more religious will show one set of values and less religious individuals will show a different set of values. This alternative is especially important to examine due to the repeatedly found associations between values and religiosity levels (regardless of the religion itself, among monotheistic religions)14,15,41,44,45.

Religiosity level and age shift the balance between self-enhancement and conservation

While we found that the triangular structure remains robust across different religiosity levels and ages, we also found that religiosity and age modulate the value profiles of the adaptive tasks (Figs. 5B, 6B and Tables 2, 3, respectively). These trends in value importance across different religiosity levels and ages are consistent with previously established population-level associations between values and religiosity or age14,15,41,44–48. However, the Pareto framework allows for a finer-grained view of these effects. For example, it was previously found that there is an increased importance of conservative higher-order values (tradition, conformity, and security) with age48. Yet, examining these trends through the prism of the Pareto framework (Supplementary Methods 4, 5) revealed that the effects of religiosity and age manifest in shifting the balance of the Self-enhancement-Conservation trade-off towards Conservation (see Tables 2, 3 for correlations with religiosity and age, respectively). This resulted in the Conservation value profile becoming more pronounced as individuals age or are more religious, while the Self-enhancement value profile becomes more moderate as individuals age or are more religious. That is, for the Conservation task, religiosity level had significant correlations with the values security, tradition, stimulation, hedonism, and achievement, and age showed significant correlations with all values except for power. For the Self-enhancement task, both religiosity level and age had significant correlations with all values except for the correlations with power. Discussion

We used Pareto analysis to reveal the adaptive tasks and trade-offs that drive individual differences in the human value system. The rich dataset of the ESS, spanning a multitude of countries, years, and socio-demographic groups, allowed for a fine-grained characterization of the underlying competing adaptive tasks. We found that individual differences in the value system are linked to three adaptive tasks: Self-enhancement, Growth, and Conservation. These adaptive tasks were robust across different global samples (ESS and WVS) and robust to variation in countries, religiosity levels, and age, emphasizing their fundamental functionality. The three adaptive tasks also show distinct trade-offs between them, relating to social dominance versus in-group cohesion, progress versus status-quo, and self-focus versus the self as a part of a group. Our findings extend Schwartz’ seminal circular values structure1,14,15, in showing that there are some combinations of values that are possible according to Schwartz’ circle but, according to Pareto analysis, represent non-optimal balancing between the competing adaptive tasks, and thus do not exist in the data.

Our findings align with several existing theories and insights from the values literature. First, the two primary dimensions revealed by the PCA align with Schwartz’s proposed organizing principles of personal versus social outcomes and growth versus self-protection motivations31. Furthermore, Schwartz hypothesized that each value originates from one to three universal human requirements2,3: the basic needs of individuals as biological entities, the demands for coordinated social interaction, and the survival and well-being of groups. While the adaptive tasks we found share similarities with these requirements, they do not map directly onto them, as Schwartz thought of these requirements as potential sources for the existence of each value across different cultures, and not as sources for individual differences in the value system. Second, the value profiles we identified for the adaptive tasks show similarities with value profiles obtained through cluster analysis for various groups, but also exhibit varying degrees of divergence, ranging from minor differences in emphasis on specific value priorities, to more substantial differences49–53. A substantial difference concerns the number of profiles: while we found three robust adaptive tasks, most clustering studies report four to five clusters. These additional clusters, include, for example, an undifferentiated profile with moderate scores across all higher-order values49,51, or profiles that share similar value characteristics52. These discrepancies may stem from differences in sample characteristics—as most clustering studies focused on children or adolescents and included fewer countries, limiting cultural variability—or from their predominant focus on the four higher-order values instead of the ten individual values. Importantly, while we discussed value profiles corresponding to each of the identified adaptive tasks, these profiles do not represent groups of people, as is typically the case with clustering methods. Clustering approaches assign each observation to a distinct cluster, characterized by the cluster’s centroid. In contrast, Pareto analysis assumes continuous data that cannot be distinctly separated and is guided by the principle that individuals balance several competing adaptive tasks based on their unique niches7. Therefore, each individual embodies a different optimal balance among the adaptive tasks. Finally, the trade-offs we found align with the functional theory of values46,54, calling for a tension between survival versus thriving (Conservation versus Growth and Self-enhancement), and personal versus social (Self-enhancement versus Growth and Conservation).

Even though we found that value importance hierarchy remained the same in all three adaptive tasks across different religiosity levels and ages, we also demonstrated that religiosity levels and age have moderating effects on Self-enhancement’s value scores and intensifying effects on Conservation’s value scores. These trends in religiosity and age align with previous findings regarding the population-level associations between religiosity levels and age with values14,15,41,44–47,55, e.g., the importance of the higher-order value conservation (i.e., tradition, conformity, and security) increases with age. However, the Pareto framework allowed for a finer-grained view of the effects of religiosity and age on values, demonstrating that these effects shift the balance of the Self-enhancement-Conservation trade-off towards Conservation, leaving the Growth task unaffected.

Our work complements previous works that explored the role of trade-offs in shaping human behavior, mainly in the cognitive sciences56,57. The rational allocation of limited cognitive resources constrains the possible normative mechanisms of various cognitive processes, such as human decision-making with its reward-complexity trade-off58, task decomposition and planning that balance utility and computational cost59,60, and learning under uncertainty, which involves an accuracy-speed trade-off61, to name a few. The Pareto framework allows to infer the competing adaptive tasks from individual differences, thereby providing a foundation for further normative investigations even if initially these competing goals are unknown.

Limitations

We note that while the three adaptive tasks show robust patterns across two global samples, most of the countries included in our main analysis are European. Future research should further examine the generalizability of the current findings to other cultures (though the replication analysis on non-European countries supports our findings). Another limitation of this study arises from the sole reliance of the ESS on the PVQ-21 questionnaire27. Other value measures and quantification methods of people’s values15,62 should be tested as well on a mass scale. Moreover, the SISAL algorithm identified four- and five-archetype solutions with significant or marginally significant fits and non-coherent value profiles. Although we found consistent evidence supporting a three-archetype solution, future work is needed to determine whether such profiles capture meaningful psychological patterns or statistical noise. In addition, while our findings suggest some changes in the balance between adaptive tasks across different ages, to truly understand individual dynamics on the Pareto front, researchers should employ longitudinal designs from childhood to late adulthood. Furthermore, the Pareto framework allows estimation of how closely an individual’s value profile aligns with each adaptive task by measuring their distance from each archetype. An important question for future research is how these alignments relate to other psychological constructs, individual development, life outcomes, or behaviors (see link to a dedicated script at the Code availability section). To better understand the etiology of the Pareto front, future research should explore which psychological and societal forces influence it63, and how. Finally, a core question remains as to the mechanisms and potential sources underlying tasks’ competition—whether the basis for competitions between the adaptive tasks stem from conflicts between different motivations (cognitive level), or from conflicts between alternative actions (behavioral level).

Conclusion

Using Pareto analysis, our work provides a principled approach to infer the driving forces of individual differences in human values. These insights pave the way for inquiries and theories concerning the etiology of each adaptive task and its underlying mechanisms. More broadly, our findings underscore the potential of utilizing Pareto analysis in psychological and social sciences, as it may reveal important insights about the underlying functionality of individual differences in complex psychological constructs, such as human values.
