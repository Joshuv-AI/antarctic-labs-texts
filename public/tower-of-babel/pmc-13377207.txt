Genotype-dependent sulfur depletion is linked to antioxidant dysfunction and growth responses to As, Cd, and Pb in Alnus glutinosa
Authors: Greta Striganavičiūtė, Vaida Sirgedaitė-Polikaitienė, Milana Šilanskienė, Inesa Kniuipytė, Marius Praspaliauskas, Irena Vaškevičienė, Egidijus Lemanas, Dorotėja Vaitiekūnaitė
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlnus glutinosa L. (black alder) is a promising phytoremediation candidate due to its N fixation, high biomass, and adaptability, yet responses to As, Cd, and Pb remain poorly understood. Three alder genotypes were exposed to increasing metal(loid) concentrations under controlled conditions hydroponically, evaluating growth, metal(loid) and elemental accumulation, antioxidant enzymes and secondary metabolites. Generalized Linear Model and Principal Component analyses revealed substantial genotype- and metal-dependent effects, with Cd causing the strongest inhibition and oxidative stress, while low-dose As and Pb induced hormetic responses (~ 20% shoot height, ~ 10% chlorophyll increases). Cd showed root accumulation with low shoot translocation; Pb was almost exclusively root sequestered. Notably, S levels declined universally. Cumulative results suggest that S depletion forms part of a broader stress-response pattern in which metal-specific uptake and tissue partitioning are followed by genotype-specific antioxidant impairments, potentially through putative effects on the ascorbate-glutathione cycle. Random Forest analysis identified glutathione-S-transferase and chlorophyll as strong metal-type predictors, while lipid peroxidation and ascorbate peroxidase best distinguished genotypes. Results support a hierarchical response pattern in which metal-specific accumulation and nutrient disruption are followed by downstream oxidative stress and growth inhibition, although the underlying sulfur-related mechanisms are inferred and require further validation. These findings highlight the potential importance of S metabolism’s critical role in A. glutinosa metal(loid) tolerance and inform future phytoremediation breeding strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-50825-3.Keywords: Black alder, Phytoremediation, Ascorbate-glutathione cycle, Hydroponics, Half-sibSubject terms: Biochemistry, Environmental sciences, Plant sciences

Introduction

Phytoremediation, particularly using woody plants, has emerged as a promising, environmentally friendly strategy to mitigate metal-contaminated sites1. Trees provide advantages over herbaceous species due to their long lifespan, high biomass, and ability to explore deep soil layers2. Among these, Alnus glutinosa L. (black alder) stands out for its fast growth, symbiotic association with nitrogen-fixing Frankia bacteria, and adaptability to disturbed environments1–4. These characteristics make it a strong candidate for phytostabilization and ecosystem rehabilitation on metal(loid)-polluted sites.

Despite this potential, scientific knowledge on A. glutinosa responses to individual exposure to Cd, As, and Pb remains limited, especially under controlled hydroponic conditions that isolate environmental variables4. Previous research has focused primarily on soil-based systems, with minimal investigation into genotype-specific physiological and biochemical responses. Furthermore, the role of antioxidant enzymes, secondary metabolites, and nutrient homeostasis in mediating tolerance to these metal(loid)s in A. glutinosa is poorly understood.

This study aimed to address these knowledge gaps by exposing three genetically distinct A. glutinosa genotypes to increasing concentrations of Cd, As, and Pb in a hydroponic system. Growth responses, metal(loid) accumulation and translocation, antioxidant enzyme activity, levels of soluble sugars and secondary metabolites (SMs), and nutrient element profiles were assessed. Additionally, multivariate statistical methods to identify key traits associated with metal-specific and genotype-specific responses were applied. It was hypothesized that genotypes would differ in their response mechanisms. Moreover, antioxidant and metabolic adjustments were expected to vary by genotype and metal exposure.

Materials and methods

Seeds and initial growth environment

Seeds of 3 A. glutinosa half-sib families (genotypes) (13-99-1 K, 26-133-6 K, 41-65-7 K) were gathered in spring 2021 in Lithuania in the seed plantation of the Šiauliai City Forest Department and kept at a temperature of + 4 °C until the experiment commenced. Germination was carried out in moist rockwool cubes previously soaked in distilled water adjusted to pH 5.6 using HCl and NaOH. Seedlings were cultivated in a modified, full-strength (100%) Hoagland’s nutrient solution composed of the following components: 6 mM KNO₃, 2.32 mM Ca(NO₃)₂·4 H₂O, 1.86 mM MgSO₄·7 H₂O, 1 mM NH₄H₂PO₄, 46 µM H₃BO₃, 9 µM MnCl₂·4 H₂O, 8.99 µM C₁₂H₁₂Fe₂O₁₈, 0.76 µM ZnSO₄·7 H₂O, 0.5 µM CuSO₄·5 H₂O, and 0.58 µM Na₂MoO₄·2 H₂O. Plants were maintained under controlled environmental conditions: day/night temperatures of 25/20°C, fluorescent lighting at an irradiance of 94.5 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹, and a 16-hour light/8-hour dark photoperiod. After ~five weeks of cultivation, seedlings reached the BBCH14 growth stage, characterized by the development of four true leaves5. Throughout the growth period, irrigation was provided as needed using half-strength (50%) Hoagland’s solution.

Hydroponic conditions and experimental groups

Following the attainment of the BBCH14 growth stage, seedlings were transferred into a hydroponic setup consisting of opaque plastic containers (dimensions: 42 × 35 × 11 cm; 12 L volume). The lids of these containers were fitted with openings to hold plastic aquatic pots. Each unit was filled with 8 L of 100% Hoagland’s nutrient solution, and the rockwool cubes containing the seedlings were inserted into the aquatic pots such that their lower halves remained fully submerged. The experimental design included ten treatment variants for each of the three A. glutinosa half-sib families (genetic groups): one control and nine metal(loid)-treated groups. Prior to transplantation, nine containers were amended with heavy metal(loid)s (HMs) - arsenic (AsH₁₅Na₂O₁₁, 98% purity; Thermo Fisher, Germany), lead (Pb(NO₃)₂, 99% purity; VWR International GmbH, Austria), and cadmium (CdSO₄, 99% purity; Thermo Fisher). Each half-sib family received three concentrations of each metal: As at 25, 50, and 100 µM; Pb at 200, 400, and 600 µM; and Cd at 5, 25, and 50 µM. These concentrations were chosen based on preliminary screening and data on hydroponic tests with As, Cd and Pb in other woody species6–8. Continuous aeration was ensured using air pumps, which were connected to flexible tubing and 18 cm oxygen diffusers positioned at the bottom of the containers. Seedlings were cultivated for a period of ~four weeks under semi-controlled environmental conditions in a growth chamber, with day/night temperatures maintained at 25/20°C, fluorescent lighting at an irradiance of 94.5 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹, and a 16 h light/8 h dark cycle.

Growth measurements and sampling

Seedling height was recorded immediately upon transfer to the hydroponic system and again after four weeks to assess height increment during the treatment period. Root length measurements, limited to the longest root per seedling, were conducted only at the end of the experiment. A modified version of the tolerance index (TI), based on shoot height, was used to quantify the relative capacity of plants to grow under contaminant exposure9.

Aboveground and root biomass were harvested for subsequent biochemical and elemental composition analyses. For biochemical assays, six fresh samples (approximately 0.1 g each) were collected from leaf tissue and another six from roots for each of the 30 experimental groups. These were semi-composite samples, created by pooling material from several individuals within a group. The collected samples were immediately frozen and stored for later processing.

For elemental composition analysis, shoot and root tissues were completely dried at 60 °C in a forced-air drying oven until constant weight was achieved. Dry weights (DW) were determined using an analytical balance scale. From each group, five dry samples from a pooled specimen (approximately 0.3–0.5 g each) were prepared for further analysis.

Biochemical analyses

Quantification of seedling pigments and stress-related metabolites was performed using spectrophotometric analysis. This included measurements of total chlorophyll (CHL), carotenoids (CAR), and SMs such as total phenolic content (TPC) and total flavonoid content (TFC). Oxidative stress was assessed via lipid peroxidation levels, expressed as malondialdehyde (MDA) concentration, and soluble sugars (SS) were also determined. In addition, the activities of key antioxidant enzymes - catalase (CAT), superoxide dismutase (SOD), guaiacol peroxidase (POX), ascorbate peroxidase (APX), glutathione reductase (GR), and glutathione S-transferase (GST) - were evaluated. All analyses were conducted using a SpectroStar Nano microplate reader (BMG Labtech, Offenburg, Germany) with 96-well microplates. All of these procedures were based on previously established and validated protocols7, which were applied without modification in the present study.

Extract preparation for photosynthetic pigment, SM, MDA, and sugar analyses

From each experimental group, 6 samples of 0.1 g were processed using a tissue homogenizer (Precellys 24, Bertin Technologies, France) operating at 1956×g for 30 s, with two metal beads added to facilitate grinding. Following initial homogenization, 1.5 mL of 80% ethanol (v/v in water) was added to each sample, and the homogenization step was repeated under identical conditions. The homogenates were subsequently centrifuged at 16,090×g for 30 min at 4 °C. The supernatants were collected and used for the spectrophotometric determination of CHL, CAR, TPC, TFC, MDA, and SS.

Total phenol content

In short, the crude extract for TPC determination was combined with Folin-Ciocalteu reagent (VWR International GmbH) (diluted 1:9 (v/v) with water) and allowed to react for 5 min. Following this, a 10% sodium carbonate (Na₂CO₃) solution was added, and the mixture was incubated in the dark for one hour. Absorbance was recorded at 725 nm. The specific formulas and calibration procedures used for this assay have been detailed previously10.

Total flavonoid content

For TFC determination, the crude extract was mixed with a reaction buffer consisting of absolute ethanol (MERCK, Germany), aluminum chloride (Alfa Aesar, Germany), potassium acetate (Sigma Aldrich, USA), and distilled water. The reaction mixture was incubated in the dark for 30 min. Absorbance was subsequently measured at 415 nm. Detailed formulas and calibration procedures for the assay are available in a previously published protocol10.

Chlorophyll a and b, and carotenoid content

Absorbance readings of the crude extract were taken at wavelengths of 470, 648, and 664 nm. The calculations and calibration methods used for pigment quantification followed established protocols described in detail elsewhere10. CHL content was determined as the sum of chlorophyll a and chlorophyll b concentrations.

Malondialdehyde content

MDA levels were determined when the supernatant was combined with a reaction solution containing trichloroacetic acid (Molar Chemicals Kft, Hungary) and thiobarbituric acid (Alfa Aesar), then incubated at 95 °C for 30 min. After incubation, the samples were rapidly cooled on ice. Absorbance measurements were taken at 440, 532, and 600 nm. The calculation formulas and additional procedural details have been previously described11.

Soluble sugar content

Quantification of SS was performed using the anthrone method. The sample was mixed with anthrone reagent (Carl Roth, Germany), which had been prepared by dissolving anthrone in concentrated sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄; Chempur, Poland), following the procedure outlined by Čėsnienė et al.11. The mixture was incubated at 90 °C for one hour, after which absorbance was measured at 620 nm.

Potassium phosphate buffer preparation

Potassium phosphate buffers at different pH levels were prepared by combining stock solutions of 1 M K₂HPO₄ (Carl Roth) and 1 M KH₂PO₄ (Chempur). Buffers with pH values of 6.5, 7.0, 7.4, and 7.8 were made as needed for the experiments.

Extract preparation for enzyme analysis

For enzyme activity measurements, 6 samples of 0.1 g of fresh biomass from each experimental group were ground to a fine powder in liquid N using a mortar and pestle. The powder was then suspended in an extraction buffer (5 ml per 0.1 g) composed of potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.8), Triton X (Carl Roth) (1%), polyvinylpolypyrrolidone (PVPP; Sigma Aldrich) (6 g w/v), and ascorbic acid (ASC; Chempur) (25 mM). The mixture was centrifuged at 14,020×g for 1 h at − 4 °C, and the resulting supernatant was collected for determination of protein concentration as well as CAT and SOD activities. For assays of APX, POX, GR, and GST, the supernatant underwent further purification through Sephadex G-25 chromatography columns (PD-10, Cytiva, UK), and the bulk filtrate was used for these analyses. All steps were performed on ice to maintain enzyme stability. Detailed formulas and procedures are described elsewhere10.

Total protein quantification

Protein concentration was assessed by combining the crude extract with Biuret reagent-prepared from CuSO₄, sodium potassium tartrate, and sodium carbonate dissolved in sodium hydroxide-followed by the addition of Folin-Ciocalteu reagent. The mixture was incubated at room temperature for 50 min, after which absorbance was measured at 660 nm. Detailed calculation methods and additional procedural information are provided in a previous study10.

Enzyme activity

CAT activity was determined by mixing the crude extract with potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7) and 0.59% hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂). Absorbance at 240 nm was measured at regular intervals to track the enzyme activity. Calculation methods and further procedural details are provided in a previous publication10.

SOD activity was measured by combining the crude extract with a reaction mixture containing potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.8), methionine (AppliChem, Germany), nitro blue tetrazolium (NBT) (VWR International GmbH), ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) (Chempur), and riboflavin (AppliChem). The mixture was exposed to white light with an irradiance of 30 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ until the samples exhibited visible darkening relative to controls. Absorbance at 550 nm was then recorded to assess SOD activity. Detailed formulas and methodology are described elsewhere11.

POX activity was evaluated by mixing the filtered extract with a reaction solution consisting of pyrogallol (Chempur), 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 6.5), and 0.59% H₂O₂. Absorbance at 430 nm was monitored at regular time intervals to measure enzyme activity. Further details and calculation procedures are available in the literature10.

APX activity was assessed by incubating the filtered extract with ASC solution, 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0), and 0.59% H₂O₂. Absorbance at 290 nm was recorded periodically to monitor the reaction. Additional procedural details and formulas have been previously published10.

GST activity was determined by mixing the filtered extract with a reaction buffer containing potassium phosphate buffer (pH 6.5), 1-chloro-2,4-dinitrobenzene (CDNB) (Thermo Fisher), and reduced L-glutathione (GSH) solution (Sigma Aldrich). Absorbance at 340 nm was measured at regular intervals to assess enzyme activity. The methods and formulas are detailed in prior work12.

For GR activity, the filtered extract was combined with a reaction buffer composed of HEPES buffer (pH 8) (Sigma Aldrich), EDTA, and NADPH (Carl Roth). After adding oxidized L-glutathione (GSSG) (Applichem), absorbance changes at 340 nm were recorded over time. Calculation methods and procedural details are described elsewhere10.

Elemental composition analyses

From each experimental group 5 dry samples of 0.3–05 g each were ground. The resulting powder was digested with a mixture of 65% nitric acid (HNO₃), 30% hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂), and 48% hydrofluoric acid (HF) in a volume ratio of 8:3:1. Digestion was carried out using a high-pressure microwave digestion system (Anton Paar, Multiwave GO Plus, Austria). Concentrations of Cd, As, Pb, P, S, Mg, K, Ca, Mn, Zn, Cu, and Fe in both shoot and root samples were quantified via inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES; Optima 8000, PerkinElmer, USA). To ensure analytical accuracy, certified reference material CRM-BCR-129 (hay powder) from the Community Bureau of Reference (CBR) was employed. To monitor signal stability during ICP-OES measurements, a control solution at 5 mg/L concentration was analyzed after every 20 samples. Calibration was repeated whenever values exceeded a 10% deviation threshold from predefined limits. Each sample was measured in triplicate to ensure reproducibility. Elemental calibration involved four calibration points using PerkinElmer standard solutions: Multi-Element Quality Control Standard 21 for Cd, As, Pb, Mg, Ca, Mn, Zn, and Fe; Quality Control Standard 7 A for K; and pure standards for P and S. The precision of elemental quantification was validated by achieving a linear correlation coefficient (R²) of at least 0.99913.

In addition, to evaluate plant responses to HM exposure, several indices were calculated following Yongpisanphop et al.9. The bioconcentration factor (BCF) was calculated for shoots and roots to quantify HM accumulation relative to the nutrient medium, while the translocation factor (TF) was used to assess the transfer efficiency of HMs from roots to shoots.

Statistical analysis

Generalized linear model

The data were compiled, and tables were generated using Microsoft Office Excel. Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Due to violations of assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance (normality was assessed on raw data, and data transformations did not improve distributional assumptions) as determined by Shapiro-Wilk and Levene’s tests, respectively, a Generalized Linear Model (GzLM) with a normal distribution and identity link function was employed to assess the effects of three factors on all biochemical and growth variables (SMs, enzymes, shoot height, root length), and HM and element (P, Ca, K, etc.) accumulation in plant tissues: metal type and dose (combined into a single fixed factor: Metal_Dose), Genetic_Group (half-sib family) and Tissue_Type (shoot vs. root; just for HM and element accumulation). Datasets are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for all measured parameters, except for elemental composition which is expressed as percentage differences to the corresponding untreated control.

The Metal_Dose factor comprised combinations of three HMs (Pb, Cd, As) each applied at three different concentrations, along with an untreated control. The genetic group factor included three distinct genotypes of A. glutinosa, while there were two types of tissues. The GzLM tested the main effects of Metal_Dose, Genetic_Group and Tissue_Type. The significance of model effects was evaluated using the Wald Chi-square test. For biochemical traits, each treatment × genotype group included 12–18 replicates, while growth parameters were measured on 20 seedlings per treatment × genotype. HM and element accumulation analyses were performed using five pooled samples per treatment × genotype, separately for shoots and roots, derived from the total biomass of each treatment group. Each genotype was treated and analyzed independently.

Post hoc pairwise comparisons of estimated marginal means were performed (only when the overall model effect was significant and were two-tailed) using the Least Significant Difference (LSD) adjustment to control for multiple testing (done for all except element accumulation). A significance level of p < 0.05 was used throughout. Exact p values are reported in the text where appropriate, while differences among treatments in tables are indicated using letter-based groupings derived from the corresponding statistical tests.

Spearman’s Rank correlation analysis

A non-parametric Spearman’s rank-order correlation analysis was performed. The analysis included HM concentration and the measured levels of nutrient elements (P, Ca, K, etc.) in A. glutinosa roots and shoots combined. The analysis was based on pooled HM and element accumulation data, with five replicates per treatment × genotype × tissue type. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS. The Spearman’s rho correlation coefficient (ρ) test was selected due to its suitability for non-normally distributed datasets and its ability to detect monotonic relationships between variables without assuming linearity. A two-tailed significance test was applied to assess the probability of type I error. Correlation coefficients (ρ) were reported for all tested relationships. Significance thresholds were set at p < 0.05. Statistically significant correlations are highlighted in bold in the correlation matrix.

Principal component analysis

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was performed to explore the multivariate relationships among 14 biochemical and growth traits of black alder seedlings. Prior to analysis, the data were inspected and cleaned to remove any missing values. PCA was conducted on scaled (standardized) trait data using the prcomp function in R (version 4.3.1) to reduce dimensionality and visualize patterns of variation. The PCA biplots were generated using the factoextra R package. Seedling samples were grouped according to HM treatment categories (As, Cd, Pb) and genotypes for visual interpretation. To summarize group-level variation, centroids for grouped data were calculated. 95% confidence ellipses were added to highlight group clustering. PCA was used as an exploratory, descriptive tool and no hypothesis testing or p values were derived from this analysis.

Random Forest classification

To identify key physiological and biochemical traits predictive of metal contamination (As, Cd, Pb) and genotype, Random Forest (RF) classification using the randomForest package was performed in R. The dataset comprised 14 numeric traits. Data rows with missing values were excluded prior to analysis. Two separate models were trained with 500 trees to classify samples by metal or genotype based on the selected traits. Variable importance was assessed using the Mean Decrease in Gini index. The analysis was performed in RStudio using randomForest, dplyr, and ggplot2 packages. Variable importance was visualized with a horizontal bar plot ranking traits by their contribution to classification accuracy. RF was used as a machine-learning classification approach and does not provide p values; therefore, no significance testing was associated with this analysis.

Results

Statistical overview of treatment effects

GzLM analyses revealed that, for the majority of the measured dependent variables – including CHL, CAR, TPC and TFC, MDA, SS, antioxidant enzymes (CAT, SOD, POX, APX, GR, and GST), as well as shoot height, root length, and HM and element accumulation, there were statistically significant effects of Metal_Dose (combined factor from HM type and their respective doses), and Genetic_Group (half-sib family/genotype) (p < 0.05; Wald Chi-square test). However, there were exceptions, two antioxidant enzymes, SOD and GR, and HM, P, K and Cu accumulation did not show significant variation among genotypes (p > 0.05). Also, both HM and 8 out of 9 element accumulation were statistically dependent on Tissue_Type (p < 0.001; except for S).

GzLM post hoc pairwise comparisons of estimated marginal means indicated significant differences between dose levels within each metal type (As, Cd, Pb) across many biochemical, growth (Tables S1-S3) and HM accumulation factors (Tables 1, S4, S5) (p < 0.05), but not necessarily in all tested groups, confirming robust dose-dependent responses in the tested parameters.

Growth responses and tolerance indices

Growth inhibition and tolerance indices provide an integrative measure of plant performance under metal(loid) stress. Plant growth responses varied substantially among genotypes and metal treatments, with most exposures resulting in growth inhibition (Table 1, S1-S5, Figs. 1 and 2, S1, S5, S6). Among the tested genotypes, positive growth responses (TI > 100%) were observed in a few specific metal-dose combinations. Notably, genotype 13-99-1 K exhibited a strong growth stimulation under Pb 200 µM, with a tolerance index of 120.74% (Tables 1, S5), while genotype 26-133-6 K exhibited a TI of 103.64% (Table S5). Similarly, genotype 41-65-7 K showed enhanced shoot height under As 50 µM, with a TI of 104.64% (Table S4). For the majority of treatments, however, metal exposures resulted in growth inhibition compared to control conditions (Tables 1, S1-S5, Figs. 1 and 2, S1, S5, S6). No other genotypes or treatments exceeded TI of 100%, with Cd treatment having the most negative effect (TI ranging from 8% to 34% in all genotypes) (Tables 1, S4, S5). Adverse effects were also clearly noticeable in the roots of all three genotypes (length reduced by up to 73%); again, Cd impact being the most toxic (Tables S1-S3, Figure S6).

Physiological stress indicators

Photosynthetic pigments, lipid peroxidation, and soluble sugars are widely used indicators of physiological stress and oxidative damage under metal(loid) exposure. CHL showed metal-specific and dose-dependent responses across genotypes (Tables S1-S3, Figure S11). At low doses As (25 µM) occasionally promoted CHL production, particularly in genotypes 26-133-6 K and 41-65–7 K (10% and 11% respectively) (Tables S2, S3, Figure S11). In contrast, Cd consistently reduced CHL levels across all genotypes (by up to 67%) (Tables S1-S3, Figure S11). Pb had intermediate effects, with only mild reductions or slight increases at the lowest dose (200 µM) (Tables S1-S3, Figure S11). Notably at the lowest Pb concentration genotype 13-99-1 K, while showing a TI of 120% (Table 1), as mentioned before, exhibited no substantial differences in CHL levels (Table S1, Figure S11).

CAR responses complemented CHL patterns but showed enhanced protective functions (Tables S1-S3, Figure S12). CAR tended to increase under low to moderate As or Pb exposure in some genotypes (up to 14% and 12% respectively) (Tables S1, S3, Figure S12), while Cd consistently reduced them (up to 54%) (Tables S1-S3, Figure S12).

Lipid peroxidation, measured as MDA content, revealed distinct genotype-specific oxidative stress patterns. MDA decreased in genotypes 41-65-7 K and 13-99–1 K almost across the board (decreases up to 44%) (Tables S1, S3, Figure S10), while in genotype 26-133-6 K, some concentrations increased the MDA levels (Table S2, Figure S10).

Cd and Pb exposure led to SS accumulation (Tables S1-S3, Figure S9). Exposure to As had more variable or suppressive effects, especially in genotype 26-133-6 K (Table S2, Figure S9). Genotypes 41-65-7 K and 13-99–1 K showed the most pronounced sugar accumulation (up to 228%) (Tables S1, S3, Figure S9).

Secondary metabolite responses

Secondary metabolites such as phenolics and flavonoids can contribute to metal(loid) stress mitigation through antioxidant functions. SM production was substantially enhanced under HM stress. TPC levels generally increased in response to HMs, with the highest increases in Cd-treated groups (up to 134%), across all genotypes (Tables S1-S3, Figure S7). TFC responses were more variable and genotype-dependent (Tables S1-S3, Figure S8), with some increases under low/moderate stress and declines under higher metal loads or in sensitive genotypes, e.g., Pb-treated alders exhibited enhanced TFC (increases up to 25%) in genotypes 41-65-7 K and 13-99–1 K (Tables S1, S3, Figure S8), but were reduced in genotype 26-133-6 K by up to 21% (Table S2, Figure S8).

Antioxidant enzyme activity

Antioxidant enzymes form the primary defense against metal(oid)-induced oxidative stress. Antioxidant enzyme responses revealed complex, dose-dependent, and genotype-specific patterns under HM stress. Low concentrations of As generally stimulated enzyme activity, while high concentrations showed more variable effects (Tables S1-S3, Figures S13-S18). Genotype 26-133-6 K showed the most positive responses to As (Table S2, Figures S13-S18). Cd exhibited generally inhibitory effects, particularly in genotypes 26-133-6 K and 41-65–7 K (Tables S2, S3, Figures S13-S18). The highest Cd concentration consistently showed the strongest inhibitory effects. Genotype 13-99-1 K showed more tolerance to Cd stress overall (Table S1, Figures S13-S18). Pb had mixed effects depending on concentration and genotype; however, 200 µM often showed stimulatory effects (Tables S1-S3, Figures S13-S18). Higher concentrations (400, 600 µM) were more inhibitory. Genotype 26-133-6 K was most responsive to treatments, showing the largest increases in APX and POX (Table S2, Figures S14, S15), while genotype 41-65–7 K generally showed decreased enzyme activity across treatments (Table S3, Figures S13-S18). Genotype 13-99-1 K showed intermediate responses with better tolerance to HM stress (Table S1, Figures S13-S18).

Multivariate analysis of genotype-specific responses

To integrate the complex physiological responses across genotypes, multivariate and machine-learning approaches were applied. PCA was conducted to explore overall variation in biochemical markers and growth parameters among the three genotypes (Figure S2). The PCA biplot reveals distinct patterns of trait clustering and genotype-specific responses to HM exposure. The first two principal components explained 32% (Dim1) and 18.4% (Dim2) of the total variance, respectively. Although considerable overlap exists among the three genotypes, distinct clustering patterns and cluster centroid positions were observed. The vector projections and cluster positions show that genotype 13-99-1 K clusters more towards the negative side of Dim1 and positive side of Dim2, closely associated with changed values TFC and SS. Genotype 26-133-6 K is clustered more towards the positive side of Dim1, linked with changes antioxidant, pigment, growth and MDA levels. Genotype 41-65-7 K cluster is similarly placed, but more tightly clustered along the Dim1 axis.

To quantify which physiological traits most effectively distinguish between genotypes, an RF classification model was applied using all measured biochemical and growth variables. Feature importance, expressed as mean decrease in Gini impurity, revealed that MDA and APX were the most informative predictors of genotype, with importance scores of 36.84 and 36.28, respectively (Fig. 3). Other highly ranked traits included SS (30.84) and CAR (28.52). Moderate contributions were observed for GST, POX, CHL, root length, TPC, and TFC. Traits with the lowest importance included CAT, SOD, shoot height, and GR, supporting earlier GzLM findings where SOD and GR showed no significant genotype effects. These results also corroborate the earlier PCA analysis, as seen in the genotype clustering patterns.

Metal accumulation and translocation

As the efficiency of phytoremediation is closely linked to metal uptake and compartmentalization, As, Cd, and Pb accumulation in alder tissues and calculated related indices were quantified (Tables 1, S4, S5). Root accumulation of As increased dose-dependently across all genotypes (55–342 mg/kg DW), with moderate shoot accumulation (3.6–36.2 mg/kg DW) (Table 1, S4, S5). Genotype 13-99-1 K showed the highest As accumulation in shoots at 100 µM (Table 1), and genotype 26-133-6 K showed the highest As accumulation in the roots at 100 µM (Table S5). Cd-treated alders exhibited extremely high root BCFs (up to 966.01), with root accumulation reaching 521 mg/kg DW (Tables 1, S4, S5). Shoot accumulation was moderate (1.8–139 mg/kg DW), with genotype 41-65-7 K showing the highest Cd tolerance (TI – 34.4) (Table S4). Cd accumulation in the shoots increased dose-dependently. Pb exhibited massive root accumulation (2.620–9.445 mg/kg DW) with very poor translocation to shoots (TF 0.01–0.09) (Tables 1, S4, S5). Overall, data indicated strong root sequestration for all 3 HMs. TFs were low across the board. TIs were mostly below 100%, with Cd having the most negative impact (exceptions mentioned earlier in section “Growth responses and tolerance indices”).

Metal-specific physiological profiles

A second PCA was performed to assess how different metal exposures (As, Cd, Pb) and untreated control conditions influenced the biochemical and growth profiles of A. glutinosa. The biplot (Figure S3) explains 32.0% and 16.4% of the total variance along the first (Dim1) and second (Dim2) principal components, respectively. Samples show clear grouping patterns corresponding to the type of metal exposure, indicating treatment-specific physiological responses.

The control group is clustered near the origin, representing baseline levels for all traits. In contrast, samples treated with As cluster more toward the positive side of Dim1, closely associated with changed values of growth parameters (shoot height, root length), CHL, CAR, and antioxidant system parameters. The Cd group tightly clusters separately on the negative side of Dim1, aligning most strongly with changed SS and TPC. Meanwhile, the Pb group shifts slightly toward the positive side of Dim2, correlating with increased enzymatic activity. These PCA results complement the earlier GzLM analysis, which showed considerable Metal_Dose effects on biochemical and physiological traits – a pattern echoed here.

RF classification was applied to identify which physiological traits most effectively distinguish between different metal exposures (Figure S4). Using all measured biochemical and growth variables, feature importance revealed that GST and CHL were the most informative predictors of metal exposure, with importance scores of 42.09 and 40.74, respectively. Other highly ranked traits included SS (30.65) and shoot height (29.55), followed by root length (27.58). Moderate contributions were observed for APX (19.71), CAR (19.42), MDA (17.32), and TPC (16.83). Traits with the lowest importance included POX (15.25), CAT (11.48), GR (10.48), SOD (10.27), and TFC (9.87).

Nutrient homeostasis under metal stress

HM exposure substantially disrupted essential nutrient homeostasis. Specific nutrient responses revealed consistent patterns across genotypes. Across all genotypes, S levels decreased considerably in both roots and shoots (Tables 2, S6, S7). However, genotype 26-133-6 K was an exception, as S was not depleted in As-treated groups in either roots or shoots (Table S6). Conversely, Ca and K levels were consistently higher in the shoots in genotypes 13-99-1 K (Table 2) and 41-65-7 K (Table S7), while Mg levels were increased in all three genotypes (Tables 2, S6, S7). In all three genotypes, the levels of Fe, Mn, Cu, and Zn were also considerably reduced (Tables 2, S6, S7). This reduction was more pronounced in shoots than in roots. The two lower doses of As increased Zn levels in the roots (Table 2, S6, S7). Overall, Cd is the most disruptive metal across all genotypes, while As at low doses can stimulate nutrient uptake.

Discussion

This study provides detailed metal(loid) (As, Cd, Pb) accumulation patterns and trait-level understanding of HM tolerance in 3 A. glutinosa genotypes at an early stage, highlighting the interplay between growth, oxidative stress, metabolic adjustment, and elemental homeostasis.

Hormetic and dose-dependent physiological responses

Across all measured physiological, biochemical, and growth parameters, A. glutinosa displayed clear dose-dependent responses to As, Cd, and Pb exposure. Metal-specific trends were evident as well: Cd elicited strong inhibitory effects on growth, chlorophyll content (reductions up to 67%), and antioxidant enzyme activity, particularly at higher concentrations. In contrast, low concentrations of As and Pb occasionally stimulated growth and chlorophyll levels, indicative of a hormetic effect. For example, genotype 13-99-1 K showed enhanced shoot height (TI − 120.7%) under Pb 200 µM, while a low-dose As (25 µM) increased chlorophyll content in 2 genotypes by ~ 10%. Moreover, low doses boosted TPC levels by ~ 21% and CAR levels by ~ 12% in genotype 13-99-1 K (Pb 200 µM). Furthermore, SS increased with metal dose, and together with increased CAR, this may suggest activated osmoprotective and antioxidant responses. Similar low-dose stimulation under HM exposure has been previously documented as a hormetic response in plants, characterized by biphasic dose-responses where low stress levels activate protective mechanisms and moderate growth enhancement14. In Lonicera japonica Thunb. Cd applied at low doses increased plant root length, biomass, height and photosynthetic pigment levels15. Similarly in corn, Pb was shown to increase growth and chlorophyll content significantly16.

Metal accumulation patterns and tissue distribution

Furthermore, in the present study, A. glutinosa exhibited clear, dose-dependent accumulation patterns for As, Cd, and Pb, with marked differences in tissue distribution. All 3 HMs accumulated to the highest concentrations in root tissues (following this general trend: Pb > As> Cd), suggesting strong root sequestration, especially for Pb and As. These patterns are consistent with previous reports identifying A. glutinosa as a non-hyperaccumulator excluder species with phytostabilizing potential17–19. A proteomic study on two clones each of A. incana (L.) Moench and A. glutinosa under polymetallic HM stress indicated that in A. glutinosa, the response was localized almost exclusively to the roots20. However, collectively, there is a lack of studies on tissue-specific quantification and verified singular metal(loid) accumulation data for Pb and Cd, and especially for As in A. glutinosa. Moreover, a critical gap persists in the literature regarding controlled or hydroponic experiments assessing the uptake and translocation of As, Cd, and Pb in this species.

Nutrient disruption and transporter interference

Exposure to HMs disrupted nutrient profiles, especially for divalent cations, with Cd causing the most pronounced declines in essential micronutrients such as Fe, Mn, Cu, and Zn, particularly in shoots, with a similar reaction from the two higher Pb concentrations. HMs can inhibit uptake or translocation of essential nutrients by competitive inhibition at ion-transport sites21, e.g., in this study, Cd2+ and Pb2+ may have competed with Ca2+, Mg2+, Fe2+, Mn2+, Cu2+, and Zn2+ for shared transporters or channels, reducing nutrient entry into roots or xylem loading. This mechanism was demonstrated by a study with rice, where Pb and Cd showed reduced entry to the plants by providing them with Ca2+ or Mg2+, while at the same time, there was no evident competition for entry by K+21. This is similarly in part confirmed by the lack of the same response to the As5+ ion, as well as strong positive correlations between these nutrients in the current study. Furthermore, Thomas M. (2021) showed that Fe and Zn significantly influenced Cd uptake through both deficiency-induced effects and competitive mechanisms for cellular uptake in barley22. Analogue competition pathways were further reported for maize23.

This suggests that divalent cation transporters (possibly ZIP (Zrt-/Irt-like protein) family or heavy-metal ATPases) may represent the shared entry pathway in this case23–25, as As5+ likely uses different transport mechanisms (phosphate or arsenate transporters)26, avoiding direct competition with these divalent essential metals. In the current study, the levels of these elements were almost all genotype-dependent (except for Cu – p = 0.053). Thus, considering the likely shared transporter pathway for the rest, the differences may be due to the transporter expression levels, transporter variants with different affinities, or regulatory pathway variation, as suggested by other studies27,28.

P and K levels under HM exposure, on the other hand, were not genotype-dependent. This suggests P and K homeostasis likely operates through separate, possibly a more tightly regulated variety of pathways that collectively show less genotypic variation under HM stress, which is likely due to their multiple critical roles.

Genotype-specific stress cascades involving S and APX

Multivariate analyses further suggest that genotype-dependent tolerance is influenced more by downstream stress handling than by how much metal they accumulate. In this context, the current data support a two-stage interpretation: first, metal-specific uptake and tissue partitioning establish a shared physiological stress background; second, genotype-specific antioxidant and metabolic responses shape the eventual tolerance outcome (Fig. 4). The RF analysis revealed that MDA and APX levels were the most divergent parameters among the three A. glutinosa genotypes under As, Cd, and Pb stress, followed by SS and CAR concentrations. These results suggest that genotypic variation in A. glutinosa under HM stress is primarily manifested through differential oxidative stress responses rather than through major differences in accumulation at the used doses. The prominence of APX over SOD or other antioxidant enzymes in this study might reflect that other enzymes have broader substrate specificity and thus may show less genotype-specific variation, while APX might show greater divergence because it operates specifically in the ascorbate-glutathione (AsA-GSH) cycle29. It was shown that Cd’s primary route for toxicity is the depletion of GSH and bonding to sulfhydryl groups of proteins30.

Conceptual model of heavy metal(loid) (HM)-induced stress responses in Alnus glutinosa. Exposure to As, Cd, and Pb resulted in similar metal(loid) accumulation and tissue partitioning patterns across the three genotypes. The shared upstream conditions were associated with disruption in nutrient homeostasis, particularly for divalent cations, and a widespread decline in sulfur levels across genotypes. They were further followed by genotype-specific downstream physiological and biochemical responses, primarily reflected in antioxidant and metabolic adjustments (including ascorbate peroxidase (APX) activity, soluble sugar (SS), and carotenoid (CAR) levels), which in turn influenced oxidative damage levels (malondialdehyde (MDA)) and ultimately growth outcomes. Random Forest analysis identified MDA, APX, SS, and CAR as key discriminators of genotype-specific responses. The dashed box represents inferred sulfur-dependent detoxification pathways (e.g., glutathione (GSH), phytochelatins, and the ascorbate–glutathione (AsA–GSH) cycle), which may contribute to the observed antioxidant responses but were not directly measured in this study. The model integrates experimental results with literature-supported mechanisms and should be interpreted as a hypothesis-generating framework.

Within this downstream response phase, the almost universal S depletion suggests that HMs may interfere with S metabolism or uptake, while also increasing demand for sulfur-containing detoxification compounds. One well-known physiological role of S in plants is as an essential component of GSH, phytochelatins (PCs), and various S-containing enzymes critical for detoxifying HMs30–32. Thus, under HM stress, increased synthesis of these S-based compounds may deplete overall S pools30,32. Because genotypes showed different degrees of S depletion despite broadly similar accumulation patterns, the present data suggest that S homeostasis may contribute to the observed differences in downstream antioxidant performance rather than acting as an isolated upstream driver. Also, as GR levels were not genotype-dependent and were therefore low on the RF list, this may suggest that Alnus genotypes differ in their GSH synthesis rather than in their capacity to regenerate GSH via GR as per their respective metabolic roles29. This pattern may explain why APX, rather than CAT or POX (which don’t depend on the AsA-GSH cycle), emerged as one of the most genotype-discriminating parameters. Supporting this interpretation (proposed model – Fig. 4), a study on two Salix varieties differentially accumulating Cd showed that AsA-GSH metabolic pathways played essential roles in Cd detoxification28. Moreover, testing of two Populus nigra L. genotypes demonstrated that Cd tolerance may be attributed to genotype-dependent differences in S-containing PCs and other thiol compounds33. Importantly though, the present study does not directly quantify sulfur-related metabolites or enzymatic activities, and therefore the involvement of sulfur-dependent detoxification pathways should be interpreted as an inference rather than a demonstrated mechanism.

Oxidative damage and secondary antioxidant compensation

Following the proposed two-stage cascade (Fig. 4), genotypes experiencing stronger downstream stress disruption would be expected to show reduced antioxidant efficiency, irrespective of broadly similar exposure and accumulation backgrounds. In the present study, this pattern is reflected most clearly by APX and MDA, which were the strongest genotype-discriminating traits in the RF analysis. This is further supported by SS and CAR levels serving as moderate differentiators in the RF analysis, as they can serve as osmoprotectants and alternative antioxidant and protective compounds when primary antioxidant systems are compromised34,35. Overall, this supports a conceptual framework in which sulfur depletion may contribute to the antioxidant network, affecting both enzymatic (APX) and non-enzymatic (CAR, SS) defense systems, with membrane damage (MDA) as the ultimate biochemical consequence (Fig. 4). However, the present data more directly support differential downstream oxidative-stress handling than a single confirmed sulfur-driven mechanism.

Trait-based differentiation of metal type

The second RF analysis provides insights into metal-specific responses versus genotype-specific responses. The parameters that differentiate by metal type are quite different from those that differentiate by genotype. RF classification identified GST activity and CHL content as the most informative traits for differentiating A. glutinosa responses to As, Cd, and Pb. This outcome is consistent with known physiological and biochemical effects of these metals. Cd exposure is well-documented to impair chlorophyll biosynthesis and stability by displacing Mg at the chlorophyll core and inducing oxidative damage to thylakoid membranes, leading to significant pigment degradation36, while Pb has several direct and indirect impacts on the photosynthetic apparatus37, and As might have entirely different effects on the photosynthetic machinery too38. In contrast, other antioxidant enzymes such as SOD, CAT, and GR exhibited more uniform responses and ranked lower in importance, likely due to their broader roles in general oxidative stress responses rather than metal(loid)-specific signaling29. Traits such as SS accumulation, shoot height, and root length were also moderately ranked, reflecting their sensitivity to stress intensity but lesser discriminatory power between metal types.

Conclusions

This study suggests that genotypic variation in A. glutinosa HM tolerance operates through downstream metabolic capacity rather than differential uptake, as all genotypes showed broadly similar accumulation patterns, yet showed distinct physiological responses. Analyses indicate that S homeostasis may represent one important component of this downstream response network, alongside APX-, MDA-, SS-, and CAR-associated stress differentiation. Rather than supporting a single S-driven mechanism, the present data support a two-stage model in which metal-specific uptake and tissue partitioning are followed by genotype-specific oxidative-stress outcomes. These findings support a shift away from traditional exclusion-focused approaches, instead suggesting that intracellular detoxification capacity may represent the primary constraint, where divalent cation transporters likely contribute to competitive nutrient disruption while metal-specific GST pathways may influence cellular responses. This suggests that breeding programs may benefit from prioritizing metabolic resilience over exclusion traits and that S supplementation or selection for enhanced S metabolism may improve phytoremediation performance. Future research should focus on validating these findings through direct measurements of sulfur-related metabolites and enzymatic activities, including correlation analyses between these parameters and sulfur content, as well as validating these findings in long-term soil-based systems, identifying genes controlling S homeostasis for marker-assisted selection, and determining whether S-GSH constraints are conserved across other excluder species. This work positions A. glutinosa as a model for understanding metabolic constraints on HM tolerance and provides a foundation for future mechanism-based environmental remediation approaches.
