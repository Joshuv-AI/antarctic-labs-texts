Predicting the incidence of common intestinal infectious diseases in Changzhou, China based on environmental factors and deep learning
Authors: Xianzhi Zheng, Lei Qiao, Hao Hong, Yixin Zhang, Qinhui Fan, Jinglan Dai, Jingyi Zhao, Fang Yao, Sipeng Shen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIntestinal infectious disease is a common infectious disease that is closely related to meteorological conditions and air pollution factors. We aim to construct a short-term prediction model for the daily incidence of common intestinal infectious diseases in Changzhou city.MethodsThe daily incidence data of hand, foot, and mouth disease and other infectious diarrhea in Changzhou and the daily meteorological data and air pollutant data in the same period were collected from May 13, 2014 to December 31, 2024. The meteorological data consisted of temperature, humidity, wind speed, air pressure, etc. Air pollutant data included PM2.5, PM10, SO2, NO2, O3, and AQI indicators. Three models, Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM), Transformer, and a hybrid model combining seasonal trend decomposition with Transformer, were constructed and compared. Additionally, an advanced STL-T-L hybrid model (Seasonal-Trend decomposition using Loess, Transformer, and LSTM) was proposed for further analysis. Bayesian optimization was used to determine the hyperparameters of the deep learning model. The model integrated historical incidence data, environmental factors, and engineered time characteristics, lag terms, and rolling statistics. The root mean square error (RMSE), mean absolute error (MAE), mean absolute percentage error (MAPE), and mean absolute scale error (MASE) were calculated on the independent test set to evaluate the prediction performance of the model.ResultsAmong all the evaluated models, the STL-T-L hybrid model showed the best prediction performance on the test set, with RMSE of 6.337, MAE of 4.524, MAPE of 58.482%, MASE of 0.638. The prediction model built in this study considered the historical incidence of the disease and incorporated various meteorological and air pollution factors. The results showed that the STL-T-L model incorporating these features achieved the best prediction results.ConclusionThe STL-T-L model can effectively predict the common intestinal infectious diseases and can be used as a tool for monitoring and early warning of intestinal infectious diseases in Changzhou.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25998-7.Keywords: Intestinal infectious diseases, Deep learning, Prediction model, Environmental factors

Introduction

Intestinal infectious diseases refer to infectious diseases caused by a variety of pathogens, such as bacteria, viruses, or parasites, which enter the human body through the digestive tract and cause nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, and abdominal pain as the main symptoms. It is one of the most common diseases in the world [1]. Intestinal infectious diseases pose a threat to people's health and bring a heavy disease burden to the whole society [2, 3]. China is one of the Asian countries with a high incidence of intestinal infectious diseases [4].

In China, eight intestinal infectious diseases, including cholera, poliomyelitis, dysentery, typhoid and paratyphoid fever (TAP), viral hepatitis A, viral hepatitis E, hand-foot-mouth disease (HFMD), and other infectious diarrhea, are prescribed by law. According to the data of the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention in 2024, HFMD and other infectious diarrhea are the most common diseases, accounting for more than 95% of all intestinal infectious diseases [5]. While the incidence of some serious intestinal infectious diseases such as dysentery, cholera, typhoid, and paratyphoid has decreased significantly, the disease burden of HFMD and other infectious diarrhea is still rising [6]. Therefore, the prevention and control tasks of common intestinal infectious diseases are still severe and need further attention.

In recent years, many studies have focused on the risk factors of intestinal infectious diseases. Epidemiological studies have confirmed that there is a close relationship between meteorological factors and the risk of various intestinal infectious diseases [7–9]. As one of the intestinal infectious diseases, the relationship between HFMD and meteorological factors has also received extensive attention because of its high incidence [4, 10–16]. Studies have generally shown that temperature and humidity are important factors affecting intestinal infectious diseases. In addition, other studies have also explored the effects of meteorological factors on different types of intestinal infectious diseases, such as bacillary dysentery [17], typhoid or paratyphoid [18], rotavirus, and norovirus infection [19, 20]. A HFMD study in Guangzhou found that temperature and humidity significantly affect intestinal infectious diseases. Specifically, for every 1 °C rise in temperature, the risk increases by 1.86%, and for every 1% rise in humidity, the risk increases by 1.42% [21]. These studies emphasized that local meteorology and its changes need to be fully considered when predicting intestinal infectious diseases [11, 12, 14].

In addition to meteorological factors, the increasingly severe air pollution problem may also play a role in the occurrence and development of a variety of intestinal infectious diseases. While the respiratory tract is the primary portal of entry, evidence indicates their impact is systemic. In particular, ultrafine particulate matter (e.g., PM2.5) is capable of translocating from the alveoli into the systemic circulation, allowing for its distribution to distal organs like the gastrointestinal tract. This process occurs via two primary biological pathways: a rapid, direct passage across the thin alveolar-capillary barrier, and a slower, cell-mediated transport by immune cells that have phagocytosed the particles [22, 23]. Indeed, preliminary studies have provided some important clues. Relevant epidemiological investigations have observed that the concentration of air pollutants can affect neonatal diarrhea [24]. Some studies have also found that air pollutants are associated with inflammatory bowel disease [25, 26]. These clues suggest that air pollution may be another environmental factor that cannot be ignored in the risk of intestinal infectious diseases, and may work together with meteorological factors [27].

Forecasting the trajectory of infectious diseases is a cornerstone of public health preparedness. In recent years, with the rise of computational power and data availability, Artificial Intelligence (AI) and machine learning methods have emerged as powerful alternatives. Advanced architectures, particularly deep learning models, are adept at learning the intricate, non-linear patterns and long-range dependencies inherent in epidemiological data, which traditional models often miss. A key advantage of these AI-driven approaches is their capacity to integrate and learn from diverse data sources offering the potential to build more accurate and robust prediction systems [28, 29].

Changzhou city is located in the eastern part of China, and the prevalence of intestinal infectious diseases has specific spatial and temporal characteristics [30]. However, this setting presents a unique challenge, as Changzhou is a major industrial city with over 5 million people, characterized by a subtropical monsoon climate and a complex mix of air pollutants. Fortunately, the city's high-quality, real-time disease surveillance system provides the robust data necessary to build and validate advanced prediction models in such a representative urban environment. Here, we construct a prediction model based on meteorological and air pollution indicators to predict the trend of intestinal infectious diseases shortly.

Methods

Data sources

The monitoring data of common intestinal infectious diseases, including HFMD and other infectious diarrhea, were confirmed from the clinically diagnosed cases reported by various medical institutions in Changzhou City from 2014 to 2024 in the China Disease Prevention and Control Information System. The infectious disease report card was checked by name and ID number to eliminate duplicate reported cases. The original data contained daily case report information. Data from the year 2020 was deliberately excluded from this analysis. This decision was made due to the unprecedented impact of the COVID-19 pandemic, which altered public healthcare-seeking behaviors, introduced interventions such as social distancing, and potentially affected the consistency of data reporting.

The national air quality data was collected from the national urban air quality real-time publishing platform of China Environmental Monitoring Station. The time period was from May 13, 2014 to December 31, 2024. The data included PM2.5 (μg/m3), PM10 (μg/m3), SO2 (μg/m3), NO2 (μg/m3), O3 (μg/m3), CO (mg/m3), and air quality index (AQI) real-time value. We used the mean value of each feature within 24 h. The air quality data included real-time monitoring values for the air quality index (AQI). For the pollutants PM2.5, PM10, SO2, NO2, and CO, both 1-h and 24-h mean concentrations were provided. For O3, the dataset contained its 1-h mean, 24-h mean, 8-h rolling mean, and the 24-h maximum of the 8-h rolling mean. Daily concentrations of air pollutants were calculated by averaging the readings from eight official monitoring stations to generate a city-wide daily mean.

The meteorological data were collected every three hours from the Liyang station, Changzhou City, in the National Climate Data Center (NCDC). The meteorological elements included temperature (Celsius), air pressure (hPa), dew point (Celsius), wind direction and wind speed (meter/second), cloud cover, and precipitation (millimeter). The above characteristics were added, and the daily temperature difference was calculated.

Transformer model

This study uses the Transformer's Encoder architecture to predict the number of cases. Its core advantage lies in its self-attention mechanism. The self-attention mechanism can effectively capture the dependencies within the sequence. Transformer is different from the RNN or LSTM, which relies on loop processing. It can process all time points in the sequence in parallel and improve the computational efficiency [32].

Input representation and position coding

Input representation and position coding

The input sequence is regarded as a series of vectors, because the self-attention mechanism itself does not process the order information of the elements in the sequence, and needs to superimpose a position coding vector for each element of the input sequence. The position coding provides the model with information about the position of each element in the sequence.

(2)Transformer encoder layer

Transformer encoder layer

The core of the model consists of one or more stacked Transformer encoder layers. Each encoder layer mainly consists of two sub-layers.

The multi-head self-attention sublayer is the key to Transformer. It allows the model to simultaneously pay attention to the information of all other time steps in the input sequence when calculating the representation of a time step in the sequence, and dynamically aggregate this information according to the calculated attention weight. Each element of the input sequence is linearly projected to obtain three vectors: query (Q), key (K) and value (V). The attention weight is obtained by calculating the dot product similarity of Q and all K, and then normalized by the Softmax function. Then, these weights are used to weight and sum V to form the attention output of the time step. The multi-head mechanism allows the model to perform multiple attention calculations in different subspaces at the same time, stitch their respective results, and perform linear transformation again to capture the dependency.

The position feedforward network sublayer is a fully connected feedforward neural network, which is independently applied to the output of the attention sublayer at each time step. It is generally composed of two linear transformations and an activation function for nonlinear transformation of the features extracted by the attention mechanism.

The output of each sub-layer adopts the residual connection method, that is, the input of its sub-layer is directly added to the output of the sub-layer, and then the layer normalization is performed [33]. These techniques can alleviate the problem of gradient disappearance and stabilize the training process.

The Global Average Pooling operation is to average the output vectors of all time steps. The pooled vector is then fed into a fully connected linear output layer (Dense Layer), which has only one neuron to predict the standardized next-day incidence.

Bayesian optimization to determine the hyperparameters

The performance of deep learning models such as LSTM and Transformer depends largely on the selection of hyperparameters, such as the number of network layers, the number of units, and the learning rate. Manually adjusting the hyperparameters of the deep learning model is time-consuming and inefficient. Bayesian optimization provides a more efficient global optimization strategy, which is especially suitable for evaluating objective functions with high computational costs [34]. Bayesian optimization relies on the construction of a probabilistic surrogate model to approximate the objective function. By using the acquisition function to guide the location of the next evaluation, the purpose of balancing exploration and exploitation and finally finding the global optimal solution is achieved.

The principle of Bayesian optimization is to establish a probability model to fit the observed 'hyper-parameter combination-performance score' data. This probabilistic model can predict the performance of different hyperparameter combinations and evaluate the uncertainty of these predictions. Then, based on the prediction results of the probability model, the next hyper-parameter combination to be evaluated is selected by balancing the strategy of exploring high-uncertain regions and using the current optimal region.

Uncertainty quantification using Monte Carlo dropout

Deterministic point forecasts from deep learning models are insufficient for actionable public health early-warning systems, as they fail to convey the inherent uncertainty and risk associated with epidemiological predictions. To address this limitation, we selected the Monte Carlo (MC) Dropout technique as a principled and computationally tractable method for approximating Bayesian inference in our deep learning models[35]. Unlike standard inference where dropout is deactivated, MC Dropout leverages the continued activation of dropout layers during the prediction phase. This procedure treats dropout not merely as a regularizer, but as a method to perform approximate variational inference. Consequently, each stochastic forward pass through the network can be interpreted as a single draw from the posterior distribution of the model's parameters.

For each time step in the test set, we performed T = 100 stochastic forward passes to generate an empirical predictive distribution of the case count. The final point forecast was defined as the mean of this distribution. The 95% prediction interval was subsequently constructed by extracting the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles from this distribution, providing a robust estimate of the model's confidence for each forecast.

Model training and evaluation criteria

The number of daily reported cases of intestinal infectious diseases was collected. We checked the continuity of the date sequence. When a certain date was missing from the original report, we set the case count to 0, indicating no disease incident. Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE) was used to fill in the missing values of numerical covariates (AQI, temperature, PM2.5, PM10, SO2, NO2, O3, CO) in the data [36]. The predictive mean matching (PMM) method was used to obtain a complete data set for subsequent analysis. The final data (a total of 3463) were divided into a training set (70%, 2425), tuning set (for the selection of model parameters and the calculation of integrated weights, 15%, 520), and test set (for the final model evaluation, 15%, 520).

The final LSTM and Transformer models were used to predict on independent test sets. Since the model predicted the number of standardized cases, the prediction results were de-normalized back to the original case number scale. The predicted value of the original scale was compared with the true number of original cases in the test set, and the key performance indicators were calculated. The time series diagram of the predicted value and the true value was drawn. The key performance indicators included root mean square error (RMSE), mean absolute error (MAE), mean absolute percentage error (MAPE), and mean absolute scaled error (MASE).

Results

Characteristics of the intestinal infectious disease population

The incidence data of HFMD and other infectious diarrhea from May 13, 2014 to December 31, 2024 in Changzhou were collected. The number of daily cases ranged from 1 to 162, with an average of 17.67 cases and a standard deviation of 16.28. Of them, HFMD accounted for the major fraction (82.47%). The cases were mainly children (4.93 years old on average), and 96.66% cases were younger than 18 years old. Males (58.65%) were larger than females (41.35%). Few cases had multiple viral infections (Supplementary Table 1).

From 2014 to 2024, the number of intestinal infectious diseases in Changzhou showed a certain degree of volatility, with no obvious long-term continuous upward or downward trend. In general, it remained in a relatively stable range, with some significant fluctuations in the middle (Supplementary Fig. 3). It could be seen that some similar fluctuations existed every year with some seasonal characteristics.

Characteristics of air quality and meteorological data

In the time series diagram of air quality and meteorological data (Supplementary Fig. 4), air pressure and temperature exhibited periodic fluctuations. The wind speed was relatively stable overall, while the temperature difference and NO2 exhibited an apparent fluctuation trend. The overall fluctuation of AQI was more frequent; O3 had noticeable seasonal fluctuation, higher in summer and lower in winter. PM2.5 and PM10 showed large fluctuations.

Verification of stationarity

To extract effective information and make the data applicable to the deep learning model, the ADF unit root test was used to evaluate the stationarity of all numerical variables (Supplementary Fig. 5). Only the first-order difference was performed on the non-stationary daily average temperature, and the difference result (P ≤ 0.01) was used for modeling (Supplementary Fig. 6). Other variables were tested to be stable, without differential treatment, and the original value was retained.

Parameter selection and prediction results of ARIMA model

As a widely used basic model, the ARIMA model is commonly used in the prediction of infectious diseases. In the study, the ARIMA model was first used to predict the incidence of intestinal infectious diseases. The parameters of ARIMA were determined with the lowest RMSE by combining the training set, and these parameters were used to predict the test set. The final model obtained by the function was ARIMA (1,1,1) (1,0,2) [7].

After model training and prediction, the relevant fitting graph and prediction graph were obtained (Figs. 2a and 3a). The RMSE, MAE, MAPE, and MASE of the ARIMA model on the combined training set (training set and tuning set) were 8.146, 5.191, 43.496% and 0.732, respectively. In the test set, the RMSE was 60.637, the MAE was 59.045, the MAPE was 1294.899%, and the MASE was 8.329.

Hyperparameter selection and prediction results of the LSTM, Transformer, and STL-Transformer models

We used a single-layer LSTM structure to construct the LSTM model without bidirectional processing. The key hyperparameters of the final LSTM model included 128 units, a dropout rate of 0.4, a time step of 15, and a learning rate of 0.00096. The LSTM model had an RMSE of 1.888, an MAE = 1.430, an MAPE = 17.801%, and an MASE = 0.202 in the combined training set. In the test set, the RMSE was 8.405, the MAE was 5.529, the MAPE was 94.891%, and the MASE was 0.780 (Figs. 2b, 3b and 4a).

The optimal hyperparameters for the Transformer model were set as follows: 3 encoder layers, 7 attention heads, 84 units in the feed-forward network, a dropout rate of 0.297, 7 timesteps for the input sequence, and a learning rate of 0.00024. The performance evaluation index of the final Transformer model on the training set and the test set: RMSE = 6.587, MAE = 4.598, MAPE = 37.765%, and MASE = 0.660 in the training set; RMSE = 6.714, MAE = 4.598, MAPE = 51.906%, and MASE = 0.649 in the test set (Figs. 2c, 3c and 4a).

In the STL-Transformer model, the time series was first decomposed using Seasonal-Trend decomposition using Loess (STL) with a weekly seasonal period (frequency = 7) and a periodic seasonal window (s.window = "periodic"). For the Transformer component of the STL-Transformer model, the optimal hyperparameters were set to 2 encoder layers, 1 attention head, 208 units in the feed-forward network, a dropout rate of 0.271, an input sequence length of 8 timesteps, and a learning rate of 0.00023. Finally, the performance of STL-Transformer model received RMSE = 8.780, MAE = 5.790, MAPE = 49.720%, and MASE = 0.817 in the training set; RMSE = 6.547, MAE = 4.868, MAPE = 63.938%, and MASE = 0.687 in the test set (Figs. 2d, 3d and 4a).

STL-T-L outperformed other models

Following the STL-Transformer model, an LSTM network was employed to correct the prediction residuals, configured with 32 units, a dropout rate of 0.2, an input sequence length of 7 timesteps, and a learning rate of 0.001. Using the models described above, we constructed a STL-T-L hybrid model (Seasonal-Trend decomposition using Loess, Transformer, and LSTM), which was capable of making 1-day ahead forecasts.

The hyperparameter selection and prediction results of the final STL-T-L model were evaluated: RMSE = 7.554, MAE = 4.978, MAPE = 45.063%, and MASE = 0.702 in the training set; RMSE = 6.337, MAE = 4.524, MAPE = 58.482%, and MASE = 0.638 in the test set (Figs. 2e, 3e and 4a).

From the graph of the prediction results, the basic model ARIMA showed deficiencies in predicting the incidence of intestinal infectious diseases, indicating that it could not accurately predict the number of intestinal infectious diseases (Fig. 4a). In the early stage of prediction, the prediction of ARIMA model fluctuated slightly, but there was still a gap compared with the actual value. It showed that there was a nonlinear relationship between the number of cases, and the ARIMA model cannot effectively deal with it.

The STL-T-L model achieved the lowest RMSE and MAE on the test set, indicating its high prediction accuracy and strong performance (Fig. 4a, Supplementary Table 2). However, STL-T-L performed worse than the Transformer models regarding the MAPE index of relative error. Although the STL-T-L model minimized the error as a whole, it might produce relatively large errors at some points with smaller true values, indicating that STL-T-L pursued accuracy, while Transformer showed better stability in relative proportion.

Because of the sensitivity of RMSE to large errors, it could effectively help identify the most robust models that avoid extreme errors. In all our model evaluations, RMSE was selected as the final criterion to measure the prediction performance of the model. According to the results of RMSE, the STL-T-L model had certain advantages in controlling and reducing prediction errors, and was the most accurate and reliable model in this study.

To quantify the prediction uncertainty of the final STL-T-L model, we employed the Monte Carlo (MC) Dropout method, a technique that provides a Bayesian approximation for deep learning models. During the prediction phase on the test set, we performed 100 stochastic forward passes through the model for each data point. This process generated a distribution of predictions for each point, reflecting the model's uncertainty. Finally, the 95% prediction interval was constructed by taking the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the prediction distribution for each time step (Fig. 3).

Finally, to address the "black-box" nature of deep learning models which can obscure the influence of key environmental factors, we utilized permutation importance to identify and rank the influential predictors within our best-performing model. We conducted this importance analysis separately for the main component of the STL-T-L model, yielding results for the Transformer parts. For the Transformer component, the analysis revealed that autoregressive features—especially, the number of cases from the previous day, the 7-day rolling average of cases, and AQI ranked as the top three factors in importance. These were followed by the number of cases from the same day last week, PM2.5, PM10, NO2, average temperature, and the 7-day rolling average temperature. Notably, O₃ exhibited negative importance scores (Fig. 4b).

Discussion

Intestinal infectious disease is still an important challenge in the field of global public health. In recent years, the potential impact of environmental factors, especially meteorological conditions and air pollution on intestinal infectious diseases has received increasing attention [37, 38]. This study constructed a variety of time series prediction models. Finally, the model integrated with STL, Transformer, and LSTM showed the best prediction performance on the test set, which outperformed the single deep learning model and the model integrating STL and Transformer.

While the STL-T-L model achieved the lowest RMSE and MAE, indicating superior performance in terms of absolute error, it is noteworthy that the Transformer model yielded a lower MAPE. This discrepancy suggests that while the STL-T-L model provides more stable predictions across the entire range of incidence values, the Transformer model may be more accurate in predicting relative changes, especially during periods of low incidence. The choice of the optimal model may therefore depend on the specific public health objective, whether it is minimizing absolute case prediction errors or improving the accuracy of relative trend forecasts.

The proposed STL-T-L model demonstrated exceptional predictive performance on the test set. While daily infectious disease forecasting is inherently challenging due to high volatility and non-stationarity, our model not only achieved robust absolute error metrics (RMSE of 6.337) but, more importantly, substantially outperformed the classical benchmark. Compared to a similarly calibrated ARIMA model (RMSE = 60.637), the STL-T-L model achieved a remarkable 89.5% reduction in error. This significant performance gain highlights the practical value of our hybrid architecture in capturing complex temporal patterns that simpler statistical models miss.

This superiority was further confirmed by the mean absolute scaled error (MASE), a more robust scale-independent metric, where the STL-T-L model also achieved the minimum value (0.638). Finally, the 95% prediction interval plot revealed that the vast majority of actual case counts fell within the forecasted bounds, indicating that the model's uncertainty estimates are reliable and that it effectively captures the incidence trend of intestinal infectious diseases.

In the process of model construction, we found that the historical dynamics of the disease itself played a leading role. This finding is consistent with previous studies, which have established that historical incidence data was an important predictor [39–41]. In addition to the historical data of the disease itself, specific environmental factors also affect the prediction results. The response of the model to temperature changes shows that temperature is an important factor affecting intestinal infectious diseases. On a biological level, systematic reviews and meta-analyses have established that higher ambient temperatures accelerate the proliferation and survival of enteric bacterial pathogens in environmental reservoirs [42, 43]. On a behavioral level, temperature modulates human activities that influence exposure risk; for instance, warmer weather often leads to changes in food handling practices and increased participation in recreational water activities [44, 45] Therefore, the strong temperature signal captured by our model likely represents the integrated effect of these complex biological and behavioral pathways.

This study also incorporates a variety of air pollution indicators, such as PM2.5, SO2, AQI, CO, NO2, O3, and PM10, as features into the model. A significant finding of this study is that air pollution indicators, particularly PM2.5, are valuable predictors of intestinal infectious disease incidence. Additionally, ultrafine particles (e.g., PM2.5) are small enough to penetrate the alveolar-capillary barrier and directly enter the bloodstream [22]. Once blood-borne, these particles and their toxic constituents are distributed throughout the body, ultimately reaching the gastrointestinal tract [46, 47]. Collectively, this compromised gut environment provides a compelling explanation for how exposure to air pollution can systemically weaken the body's defenses against enteric pathogens, thereby validating the importance of PM2.5 as a key predictor in our model.

Notably, O3 exhibited negative importance scores, which indicates that permuting these features—thereby nullifying its predictive contribution—led to a marginal improvement in the model's RMSE on the test set. This phenomenon is likely attributable to the effects of multicollinearity. The negative score for O3 likely reflects the complex intercorrelations among air pollutants and meteorological factors. Ground-level ozone formation is highly dependent on temperature and the presence of precursor pollutants like nitrogen oxides (NOx). Consequently, its concentration is often strongly correlated with other included inputs, such as temperature, NO2 and PM2.5 [48]. Therefore, we think that while these features are theoretically relevant, their practical contribution is negligible or even slightly detrimental in the context of the current model and in the presence of other strong predictors.

We systematically compared the performance of LSTM, Transformer, STL-Transformer, and STL-T-L models in the prediction of common intestinal infectious diseases in Changzhou. In the process of constructing the model, a variety of meteorological and air pollution indicators are combined to understand and predict the trend of disease from a more comprehensive perspective. The entire research process follows a more complete and standardized time series prediction step, including the use of Bayesian optimization to adjust the hyperparameters and evaluate the model to ensure the reliability of the results [49]. Despite these strengths, we acknowledge several limitations that offer avenues for future research. First, while Bayesian optimization is a powerful tool, it does not guarantee convergence to a global optimum. Second, the spatial representativeness of our exposure data presents a limitation. Meteorological data were derived from a single site in Liyang, and while air quality exposure was estimated by averaging eight stations, both methods rely on the assumption of uniform exposure for all Changzhou residents. This may not accurately reflect exposure levels across different areas, and future work could create more granular exposure maps using multi-site data. Next, this study is the exclusion of data from 2020. This partial data gap may potentially affect the model's comprehensive understanding of long-term trends, and future iterations could be improved by incorporating pandemic adjustment factors. Furthermore, while our model demonstrates strong overall performance, the high MAPE highlights challenges in predicting periods of very low incidence. The implementation of advanced techniques such as weighted loss functions, or quantile regression presents a promising opportunity to enhance prediction stability and utility for real-time public health alerts.

This study provides a technical basis for the short-term early warning of intestinal infectious diseases under the influence of environmental factors in Changzhou. The model combines real-time environmental monitoring data to predict the incidence of intestinal infectious diseases and warn the public health department. Future research should consider adding more potential influencing factors, such as population mobility data, information on social media, or more detailed etiological monitoring data. In addition, there may be differences in the spread of intestinal infectious diseases in different regions, and it is essential to develop predictive models that take into account spatial and temporal dynamics.

In conclusion, the application of the STL-T-L model can effectively predict the number of intestinal infectious diseases, which contributes to early warning and prediction of common intestinal infectious diseases in Changzhou.
