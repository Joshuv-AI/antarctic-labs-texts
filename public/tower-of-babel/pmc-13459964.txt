Asymptomatic right atrial cement embolism after vertebral kyphoplasty requiring surgical extraction: a case report

Introduction

Intracardiac cement embolism is a rare but potentially life-threatening complication of vertebral augmentation procedures such as vertebroplasty and kyphoplasty. Migration of polymethylmethacrylate through the vertebral venous plexus may lead to embolization to the right-sided cardiac chambers and pulmonary arterial circulation. Although pulmonary cement embolism is more frequently reported, intracardiac embolization may cause arrhythmias, valvular injury, cardiac perforation, tamponade, right heart failure, or sudden death. Management remains controversial because standardized therapeutic recommendations are lacking.1–3

We report an asymptomatic patient with a large mobile right atrial cement embolus and pulmonary cement emboli after kyphoplasty, successfully treated by surgical extraction.4,5

Case presentation

A 68-year-old man with multiple myeloma and dorsolumbar compression fractures underwent percutaneous vertebral kyphoplasty on 13 April 2026 (Figure 1; Supplementary material online, Video S1). Following partial improvement in back pain, he developed fever 4 days later while participating in a clinical trial for multiple myeloma. Transthoracic echocardiography performed during the diagnostic work-up incidentally revealed a mobile hyperechoic right atrial mass (Figure 2). Left ventricular systolic function was preserved, with no significant valvular or other structural abnormalities. The patient had no history of cardiovascular disease and remained asymptomatic, with an unremarkable physical examination. No previous echocardiograms were available for comparison. He was referred to the emergency department for further evaluation.

Fluoroscopic image obtained during percutaneous vertebral kyphoplasty. The arrow indicates cement leakage beyond the vertebral body during injection.

Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrating a mobile elongated hyperechoic mass within the right atrium, consistent with intracardiac cement embolism.

Computed tomography (CT) confirmed a 25 × 11 mm hyperattenuating intracardiac structure within the right atrium, consistent with migrated cement following kyphoplasty. Additionally, multiple linear hyperattenuating opacities were identified within the segmental pulmonary arteries of the right upper, middle, and lower lobes, consistent with pulmonary cement emboli, as well as within the paravertebral venous plexus (Figure 3).

Contrast-enhanced chest computed tomography. (A) Coronal reconstruction showing a linear hyperattenuating cement fragment within the right atrium extending towards the tricuspid valve (arrow). (B) Axial image confirming the intracardiac location of the cement embolus adjacent to the right atrial wall (arrow).

Given the size, mobility, and embolic risk of the intracardiac cement embolus, surgical extraction was indicated despite the absence of symptoms (see Supplementary material online, Video S2). Median sternotomy was performed under general anaesthesia. Following systemic heparinization, the initial operative strategy was to perform the procedure on a beating heart without cardiopulmonary bypass, aortic cross-clamping, or cardioplegic arrest, as only removal of the right atrial cement embolus was anticipated. However, intraoperative transoesophageal echocardiography unexpectedly identified a patent foramen ovale (PFO). Given the potential risk of paradoxical embolization and the opportunity to repair the defect during the same procedure, the surgical strategy was revised. Cardiopulmonary bypass was subsequently established via ascending aortic and bicaval cannulation, followed by aortic cross-clamping and antegrade cardioplegia, allowing both safe cement extraction and concomitant PFO closure.

Right atriotomy revealed an elongated rigid cement fragment measuring ∼2.5 cm, firmly adherent to the pectinate muscles of the anterior right atrial wall. The cement fragment was carefully extracted en bloc to avoid fragmentation and distal embolization, followed by careful inspection of the atrial cavity to exclude residual material (Figure 4). Surgical closure of the PFO was subsequently performed.

Intraoperative view following right atriotomy demonstrating an elongated intracardiac cement fragment adherent to the pectinate muscles of the anterior wall of the right atrium.

No surgical intervention on the pulmonary arterial tree was undertaken because the embolized cement fragments were located distally within the lobar and segmental pulmonary arteries, making surgical retrieval technically unfeasible in the absence of haemodynamic or respiratory compromise.

Follow-up

The postoperative course was uneventful. Follow-up transthoracic echocardiography before discharge demonstrated no residual intracardiac foreign material. The patient was discharged on oral anticoagulation because of the residual distal pulmonary cement emboli. At the 3-month follow-up visit, he remained asymptomatic and repeat transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated preserved biventricular function with no evidence of residual or recurrent intracardiac foreign material. Following multidisciplinary reassessment, continuation of oral anticoagulation with acenocoumarol was recommended to complete a total treatment duration of 6 months, after which anticoagulation will be discontinued.

Discussion

Intracardiac cement embolism is an uncommon but potentially life-threatening complication of vertebral augmentation procedures. Leakage of polymethylmethacrylate into the vertebral venous plexus may result in migration to the right-sided cardiac chambers and pulmonary arteries. Although cement leakage is relatively common, clinically significant intracardiac embolization remains rare and may cause arrhythmias, valvular dysfunction, myocardial perforation, cardiac tamponade, right ventricular failure, or haemodynamic collapse.1–4

Clinical presentation ranges from incidental findings to severe cardiovascular compromise and may occur months or years after vertebral augmentation procedures. Multimodality imaging is essential for diagnosis and treatment planning. Echocardiography identifies intracardiac foreign material and evaluates embolus mobility and haemodynamic consequences, whereas CT better defines embolus morphology, extension, and associated pulmonary cement emboli.3,4

Management remains challenging because no standardized therapeutic recommendations currently exist, and available evidence is mainly limited to case reports and small case series. Recent systematic reviews have confirmed that conservative management, percutaneous retrieval, and open surgical extraction have all been successfully employed, with treatment decisions largely guided by embolus size, mobility, location, adherence, associated complications, and patient-specific characteristics. Percutaneous retrieval has been successfully reported in selected patients; however, large, rigid, or adherent cement fragments may increase the risk of fragmentation, distal embolization, or cardiac injury during endovascular manipulation. Consequently, open surgical extraction should be strongly considered in selected patients with high-risk intracardiac cement emboli.2–6

In the present case, surgical extraction was considered the safest therapeutic option following multidisciplinary discussion between the cardiology and cardiac surgery teams, despite the absence of symptoms. Several anatomical and procedural features favoured surgery over conservative or percutaneous management. First, the embolus was large and highly mobile, increasing concern regarding further embolization or mechanical injury to intracardiac structures. Second, intraoperative findings confirmed firm adherence to the pectinate muscles of the right atrial wall, making spontaneous stabilization unlikely and potentially limiting the feasibility and safety of percutaneous retrieval. Third, the unexpected intraoperative identification of a PFO provided an opportunity for simultaneous surgical closure, thereby eliminating a potential route for future paradoxical embolization without substantially increasing procedural complexity. Collectively, these anatomical characteristics shifted the risk–benefit balance towards definitive surgical extraction.

Strengths and limitations

This case has several limitations. As a single case report, its findings cannot be generalized, and treatment decisions should be individualized according to patient and embolus characteristics. In addition, long-term imaging follow-up of the residual pulmonary cement emboli is not yet available. Its strengths include multimodality imaging, intraoperative confirmation of embolus adherence, multidisciplinary decision-making, and favourable short-term clinical and echocardiographic follow-up.

To our knowledge, reports of asymptomatic large right atrial cement emboli requiring surgical extraction remain limited. This case highlights that management should be guided by embolus size, mobility, adherence, embolic risk, and not symptoms alone. It also illustrates the value of multimodality imaging and multidisciplinary decision-making in guiding treatment when standardized recommendations are lacking.

Patient perspective

The patient remained asymptomatic throughout hospitalization and experienced an uneventful postoperative recovery. He expressed satisfaction with the diagnostic process, surgical treatment, and clinical outcome.
