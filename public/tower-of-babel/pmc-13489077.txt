Antibacterial Efficacy of 6‐Gingerol Alone and in Combination With Enrofloxacin Against Multidrug‐Resistant Staphylococcus aureus Isolated From Goats
Authors: Maria Khan, Arshad Zahoor, Shabana Naz, Caterina Losacco, Sohail Ahmad, Ibrahim A. Alhidary, Luciana Rossi, Rifat Ullah Khan, Huda A. Alqahtani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundGoat mastitis is an economically important disease that reduces milk yield and quality while also posing a public health risk due to antimicrobial‐resistant pathogens.ObjectivesThis study aimed to evaluate mastitis diagnostic techniques, isolate Staphylococcus aureus, detect methicillin‐resistant S. aureus (MRSA) and assess the antibacterial potential of 6‐gingerol alone and in combination with antibiotics.MethodsA total of 2000 aseptically collected milk samples were obtained from lactating goats presented at the Civil Veterinary Hospital, Chargano Chowk and the Veterinary Teaching Hospital, The University of Agriculture Peshawar, Pakistan. Samples were screened using the Surf Field Mastitis Test and confirmed by the California Mastitis Test (CMT). Positive samples were cultured on selective media, and S. aureus was identified through Gram staining, catalase and coagulase tests. MRSA detection was performed using PCR amplification of the mecA gene. Antimicrobial susceptibility was assessed using the Kirby–Bauer disc diffusion method according to CLSI guidelines. The antibacterial activity of standard and extracted 6‐gingerol, selected antibiotics and enrofloxacin plus 6‐gingerol combination therapy was evaluated using inhibition zone diameter and minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) assays.ResultsThe Surf Field Mastitis Test showed high diagnostic performance with excellent sensitivity, moderate specificity and substantial agreement with the CMT. S. aureus isolates exhibited varying resistance patterns, with confirmed presence of MRSA strains via mecA gene detection. Among treatments, enrofloxacin demonstrated the highest individual antibacterial activity, while extracted 6‐gingerol showed moderate inhibitory effects. The combination of enrofloxacin with 6‐gingerol exhibited the strongest antibacterial activity, surpassing all single‐agent treatments. MIC analysis confirmed a concentration‐dependent inhibitory effect of 6‐gingerol against S. aureus. Correlation analysis showed a strong negative relationship between increasing compound concentration and bacterial growth, while combination therapy significantly enhanced antimicrobial efficacy.ConclusionThese findings demonstrate that rapid field diagnostics are effective for mastitis screening and suggest that 6‐gingerol, particularly in combination with enrofloxacin, may serve as a promising adjunct antimicrobial strategy for controlling resistant S. aureus in goat mastitis.

Introduction

Goat farming plays an important role in the livelihood of rural communities by providing milk, meat and income, particularly in developing countries (Nedumaran and Muthuveni 2023; Purwaningsih et al. 2024). Goat milk is valued for its nutritional quality, digestibility and medicinal properties, making it an important component of household nutrition and small‐scale dairy enterprises (Biswas et al. 2024; Mansoor et al. 2025). However, the productivity of dairy goats is greatly affected by mastitis, one of the most common and economically significant diseases of the udder (Bessembayeva et al. 2024; Tibebu et al. 2025). Mastitis is an inflammatory condition of the mammary gland usually caused by bacterial infection, leading to reduced milk yield, poor milk quality, treatment costs and premature culling of animals (Dorgham et al. 2024; Lima et al. 2025). Both clinical and subclinical mastitis occur in goats, but subclinical mastitis is more difficult to detect because it shows no visible signs while silently decreasing milk production and increasing bacterial contamination (Aliyi 2023).

Among the bacterial pathogens associated with caprine mastitis, Staphylococcus aureus is considered one of the most important causative agents worldwide (Gherardi 2023; Susanty et al. 2025). This organism is highly adaptable, contagious and capable of colonizing the skin, teat canal and mammary tissue (Javed et al. 2024; Campos et al. 2022). Infected animals often develop persistent or recurrent intramammary infections that are difficult to eliminate. S. aureus can produce toxins, biofilms and several virulence factors that enhance tissue damage and resistance to host immune defences (Touaitia et al. 2025).

A growing concern in veterinary and human medicine is the emergence of methicillin‐resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) (Abd El‐Razik et al. 2025; Zafar et al. 2025). MRSA strains carry the mecA gene, which encodes an altered penicillin‐binding protein (PBP2a) that reduces the efficacy of β‐lactam antibiotics such as methicillin, penicillin and related drugs (Olanipekun et al. 2025). The spread of MRSA in dairy animals is alarming because it limits treatment options, increases therapeutic failure and may facilitate transmission of resistant bacteria between animals, farm workers, veterinarians and the environment (Pal et al. 2024). The detection of the mecA gene by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) is considered a reliable method for confirming methicillin resistance and identifying MRSA isolates.

The treatment of mastitis in goats often relies on the use of commonly available antibiotics such as enrofloxacin, amoxicillin, gentamicin and tetracycline (Lima et al. 2025). However, frequent or indiscriminate use of antimicrobials has contributed to the development of resistance among mastitis pathogens (Gherardi 2023). Therefore, regular antimicrobial susceptibility testing is necessary to guide effective therapy and reduce unnecessary antibiotic usage (Aqib et al. 2017; Rasheed et al. 2023; Touaitia et al. 2025). The agar disc diffusion method based on Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines remains a widely accepted approach for evaluating susceptibility patterns of bacterial isolates.

In recent years, interest has increased in natural antimicrobial compounds as supportive or alternative approaches to combat resistant bacteria (Rasool et al. 2024; Fitriadi et al. 2024; Sultan et al. 2025). One such compound is 6‐gingerol, the principal bioactive constituent of ginger (Zingiber officinale). 6‐Gingerol possesses antibacterial, antioxidant, anti‐inflammatory and immunomodulatory properties (Yücel et al. 2022; Zimazi et al. 2025). Previous studies have reported its inhibitory effects against Gram‐positive and Gram‐negative bacteria, including S. aureus (Hasan et al. 2026). Combining plant‐derived compounds with conventional antibiotics may enhance antibacterial activity through synergistic interactions and potentially reduce required antibiotic doses.

Despite the importance of goat mastitis, limited data are available regarding the prevalence of MRSA and antimicrobial susceptibility of S. aureus isolates in many regions, including Peshawar. Local epidemiological information is essential for designing evidence‐based control strategies, selecting effective therapeutic agents and reducing the spread of resistant pathogens. Therefore, the present study was conducted to determine the prevalence of mastitis in goats using screening tests, isolate and identify Staphylococcus aureus from mastitic milk samples, detect MRSA using the mecA gene and evaluate the antimicrobial susceptibility of isolates against commonly used antibiotics. In addition, the antibacterial activity of 6‐gingerol alone and in combination with antibiotics was assessed to explore its potential as an alternative or complementary therapeutic option against mastitis‐associated S. aureus.

Materials and Methods

Study Area and Duration

The present study was conducted in District Peshawar, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Pakistan. Milk samples were collected from goats presented to two major veterinary healthcare centres: Civil Veterinary Hospital, Chargano Chowk, and Veterinary Teaching Hospital, The University of Agriculture, Peshawar.

Sample Collection

A total of 2000 goat milk samples were collected aseptically for the detection of mastitis and bacterial isolation. Of these, 1000 samples were collected from Civil Veterinary Hospital and 1000 samples from the Veterinary Teaching Hospital. Milk samples were obtained from lactating goats suspected of clinical or subclinical mastitis. Before sampling, teats were washed with clean water, dried with disposable towels and disinfected using 70% ethanol. The first few streams of milk were discarded, and approximately 10–15 mL milk was collected into sterile screw‐capped tubes. Samples were immediately placed in an ice box and transported to the laboratory for further analysis.

Screening of Mastitis

Surf Field Mastitis Test

The Surf Field Mastitis Test was used as a rapid field screening method for mastitis detection. Equal volumes of milk and commercial surf solution were mixed in a clean test paddle or tube. Formation of gel or thickening indicated a positive reaction.

California Mastitis Test

The California Mastitis Test (CMT) was used as the reference test for confirmation of subclinical mastitis. Equal amounts of milk and CMT reagent were mixed in the wells of a CMT paddle. The mixture was gently rotated for 10–15 s and scored according to gel formation. Samples showing visible gel formation were considered positive.

The diagnostic performance of the Surf test was evaluated against CMT by calculating sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), accuracy and Cohen's kappa coefficient.

Isolation and Identification of Staphylococcus aureus

Milk samples positive for mastitis were cultured for bacterial isolation. A loopful of milk was streaked onto Mannitol Salt Agar (MSA) and Blood Agar plates and incubated aerobically at 37°C for 24–48 h. Colonies showing golden yellow pigmentation on MSA and β‐haemolysis on blood agar were presumptively identified as Staphylococcus aureus. Pure colonies were subjected to Gram staining and biochemical tests including catalase and coagulase tests. Gram‐positive cocci in clusters, catalase‐positive and coagulase‐positive isolates were considered S. aureus.

Molecular Detection of MRSA (mecA Gene)

Confirmed S. aureus isolates were screened for methicillin resistance by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) targeting the mecA gene.

Bacterial suspension equivalent to 0.5 McFarland standard was swabbed onto Mueller–Hinton agar plates. Antibiotic discs were placed on the inoculated surface and plates incubated at 37°C for 24 h. Zones of inhibition were measured in millimetres and interpreted as resistant, intermediate or sensitive according to CLSI breakpoints.

Preparation and Confirmation of 6‐Gingerol Extract

Both standard 6‐gingerol and plant‐derived 6‐gingerol extract were used in the study. Fresh ginger (Zingiber officinale) rhizomes were procured from the local market and taxonomically authenticated by the Department of Weed Science, The University of Agriculture, Peshawar, Pakistan.

For extraction, the rhizomes were thoroughly washed to remove soil and debris, peeled and rinsed again with clean water. The cleaned rhizomes were then cut into small pieces and crushed with methanol using a pestle and mortar. The homogenized mixture was transferred into Falcon tubes and centrifuged at 4500 rpm for 15 min. The supernatant was collected, filtered through Whatman filter paper and transferred into sterile tubes.

Stock solutions of both standard 6‐gingerol and the plant extract were prepared by dissolving accurately weighed quantities in sterile dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). Working solutions (100 µg/mL) were prepared by further dilution with methanol under aseptic conditions.

Confirmation and quantification of 6‐gingerol in the extract were performed using High Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC) equipped with UV detection. Separation was achieved on a Betasil C18 column (150 mm × 4.6 mm, 5 µm particle size). The mobile phase consisted of HPLC‐grade water (solution A) and acetonitrile (solution B) in gradient ratios, with a constant flow rate of 1 mL/min. Detection was carried out at 254 nm. Identification of 6‐gingerol was based on comparison of retention time and photodiode array spectra of the sample with those of the authentic standard.

Antibacterial Activity of 6‐Gingerol and Combination Therapy

The antibacterial activity of antibiotics, standard 6‐gingerol, extracted 6‐gingerol and Enrofloxacin + 6‐gingerol combination was evaluated using agar well diffusion or disc diffusion assay.

Fresh bacterial inoculum of S. aureus was spread on Mueller‐Hinton agar plates. Wells/discs containing test compounds were applied and incubated at 37°C for 24 h. Zones of inhibition diameter (IZD) were measured in mm. Each treatment was tested in triplicate and results expressed as mean ± standard deviation.

Minimum Inhibitory Concentration of 6‐Gingerol

Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of 6‐gingerol was determined by broth microdilution method using serial twofold dilutions ranging from 25 to 0.0488 µg/mL in sterile microtiter plates.

Each well received bacterial inoculum adjusted to 106 CFU/mL and was incubated at 37°C for 24 h. Wells were examined for visible turbidity. The lowest concentration showing no visible growth was recorded as the MIC.

Discussion

The present findings provide an integrated evaluation of mastitis screening methods and antimicrobial interventions against Staphylococcus aureus, one of the most important mastitis‐associated pathogens in dairy animals (Muça et al. 2026). Collectively, the results indicate that rapid field diagnosis combined with optimized antimicrobial strategies—particularly combination therapy involving enrofloxacin and 6‐gingerol—may significantly improve mastitis management. These findings are highly relevant because S. aureus mastitis remains difficult to control due to biofilm formation, intracellular persistence and increasing antimicrobial resistance (Rasheed et al. 2023; Javed et al. 2024). Recent literature also emphasizes the need for improved diagnostics and novel adjunct therapies in dairy medicine (Muzammil et al. 2022; Javed et al. 2023; Ramuada et al. 2024; Mustafa et al. 2025).

The equal distribution of milk samples between the Civil Veterinary Hospital (Chargano Chowk) and the Veterinary Teaching Hospital, The University of Agriculture, Peshawar, strengthens the representativeness of the dataset by minimizing location bias. Balanced sampling from two independent veterinary centres increases external validity and suggests that the observed diagnostic and antimicrobial patterns are not restricted to a single facility. This is particularly relevant in mastitis studies, where management practices, hygiene status and antimicrobial usage can vary substantially between institutions. Recent mastitis surveillance studies indicate that environmental conditions, milking hygiene, herd size and regional antibiotic practices strongly influence mastitis prevalence and bacterial resistance profiles (Iraguha et al. 2026). Therefore, using two separate veterinary institutions increases the generalizability of the present findings for district Peshawar and surrounding dairy systems.

The use of CLSI interpretive criteria ensures that susceptibility testing was standardized according to internationally accepted breakpoints. Variation in breakpoint values among antibiotics reflects differences in pharmacodynamics, diffusion rates in agar and clinically achievable serum/tissue concentrations. For example, fluoroquinolones such as enrofloxacin often have distinct interpretive thresholds because of their concentration‐dependent killing and excellent tissue penetration, whereas tetracycline breakpoints differ due to its primarily bacteriostatic action. The presence of intermediate categories for selected drugs also indicates that some antimicrobials possess a narrower transition range between susceptibility and resistance, which is important for treatment decision‐making (Wang et al. 2025).

The Surf test demonstrated perfect sensitivity relative to the CMT, meaning it successfully identified all positive mastitis samples. This is highly desirable in herd‐level screening because false negatives may allow infected animals to remain untreated, increasing production losses and pathogen transmission. Although specificity was moderate, indicating some false positives, such over‐detection may be acceptable in preliminary screening programs where confirmatory testing follows (Iraguha et al. 2026). Thus, the Surf test may represent a practical, economical substitute for routine field surveillance in Pakistan. A recent review on bovine mastitis diagnostics confirms that low‐cost field tests remain highly valuable in developing dairy systems where automated SCC counters are unavailable (Ramuada et al. 2024).

The substantial Cohen's kappa agreement between the Surf test and CMT indicates strong consistency between methods. This suggests that the Surf test may serve as a practical low‐cost alternative for field conditions, especially in resource‐limited areas. Similar observations have been reported in developing dairy systems where household detergents or surfactants have shown acceptable screening value due to their ability to lyse somatic cells and react with DNA/protein‐rich mastitic milk, producing gel formation analogous to the CMT reaction (Leach et al. 2008).

The strongest antibacterial effect against Staphylococcus aureus was observed with the enrofloxacin + 6‐gingerol combination therapy, followed by enrofloxacin alone, gentamicin, standard 6‐gingerol, tetracycline and crude extract. This pattern is biologically logical and reflects the different mechanisms of action and resistance profiles of the tested agents. Enrofloxacin acts by inhibiting DNA gyrase and topoisomerase IV, thereby rapidly blocking bacterial DNA replication. Its superior single‐agent activity suggests that the isolates remained susceptible to fluoroquinolone treatment. Gentamicin exerts its antibacterial action by binding to the 30S ribosomal subunit, leading to mistranslation and bacterial death; however, its uptake may decline in low‐oxygen or inflamed tissues, which may explain its slightly lower efficacy compared with enrofloxacin. The lower activity of tetracycline likely reflects widespread resistance mechanisms such as efflux pumps, ribosomal protection proteins and enzymatic inactivation. The finding that purified 6‐gingerol outperformed crude extract indicates that concentration and purity of the active phenolic compound are critical determinants of antibacterial activity. Recent experimental studies confirmed that 6‐gingerol possesses measurable antibacterial effects against S. aureus and other pathogens, supporting the present findings (Elfaky et al. 2024).

The enhanced activity of the enrofloxacin + 6‐gingerol combination likely reflects synergistic mechanisms, as also reported by Sahoo et al. (2024), who demonstrated that 6‐gingerol acted as an effective adjuvant by enhancing the susceptibility of multidrug‐resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae to colistin. This suggests that 6‐gingerol may similarly potentiate enrofloxacin activity against Staphylococcus aureus through complementary antibacterial mechanisms. As a lipophilic compound, 6‐gingerol can disrupt bacterial membrane integrity, increasing membrane permeability (Elfaky et al. 2024). This membrane damage may facilitate greater intracellular penetration of enrofloxacin, thereby improving its antibacterial effect. In addition, many phytochemicals are known to inhibit bacterial efflux systems, allowing antibiotics to remain inside the cell for longer periods (AlSheikh et al. 2020). S. aureus mastitis frequently involves biofilm formation within mammary tissue, and ginger‐derived compounds may interfere with quorum sensing and biofilm development, rendering bacterial cells more vulnerable to treatment (Shehata et al. 2024). Furthermore, while enrofloxacin disrupts DNA replication, 6‐gingerol simultaneously disturbs membrane function and oxidative balance (Li et al. 2019), producing multi‐target stress that overwhelms bacterial defence systems. Recent studies reported synergistic use of 6‐gingerol with conventional antibiotics against multidrug‐resistant bacteria, strongly supporting this interpretation (Molzahn et al. 2026).

The MIC findings demonstrated a clear concentration‐dependent response, with complete growth inhibition at higher concentrations and progressive regrowth as the concentration declined. This is expected for phytochemicals because they often require threshold concentrations to destabilize membranes, inhibit metabolic enzymes and impair virulence factors. Recent work also described dose‐dependent antibacterial activity of 6‐gingerol against both Gram‐positive and Gram‐negative pathogens (Alfuraydi et al. 2024). Therefore, the present MIC data validate the genuine antimicrobial potential of 6‐gingerol rather than incidental inhibition.

The ranking results statistically confirmed the order of efficacy as Combination > Enrofloxacin > Gentamicin > 6‐Gingerol > Tetracycline > Extract. This suggests that phytochemicals alone may not replace conventional antibiotics, but they can significantly enhance their activity (Khare et al. 2021). Modern antimicrobial research increasingly favours such antibiotic adjuvant strategies to reduce resistance pressure and restore the efficacy of existing drugs.

The strong negative correlation between concentration and bacterial growth confirms classical dose‐response pharmacology, where increasing concentration suppresses microbial proliferation. The positive correlation between treatment type and IZD indicates that stronger therapies consistently produced larger bacterial clearance zones. These findings support the biological consistency of the agar diffusion assay used in the present study. The greatest pairwise differences were observed between combination therapy and all single treatments, indicating that the improvement was not marginal but statistically meaningful. Smaller differences among antibiotics suggest relatively similar baseline efficacy, whereas the addition of 6‐gingerol created a distinct therapeutic advantage. These findings are highly relevant for bovine mastitis management. The Surf test may offer an inexpensive option for rapid herd‐level screening, while the enrofloxacin + 6‐gingerol combination may improve cure rates against S. aureus. Combination therapy may also allow lower antibiotic doses, thereby reducing drug residues in milk and limiting resistance development. Plant‐derived compounds such as 6‐gingerol may therefore emerge as valuable veterinary antimicrobial adjuvants. Overall, the present results indicate that combining conventional antibiotics with natural bioactive compounds represents a promising strategy for improving mastitis treatment and combating antimicrobial resistance.

Conclusion

Overall, the results support a dual strategy for mastitis control: efficient screening through Surf testing and improved antimicrobial therapy through antibiotic‐phytochemical combinations. The strong performance of enrofloxacin + 6‐gingerol suggests a promising alternative approach against S. aureus, particularly in the era of increasing antimicrobial resistance. Further in vivo studies, toxicity assessments and pharmacokinetic evaluations are recommended before routine clinical application.
