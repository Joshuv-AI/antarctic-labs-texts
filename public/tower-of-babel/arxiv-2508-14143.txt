Beyond Turing: Memory-Amortized Inference as a Foundation for Cognitive Computation
Xin Li, Department of Computer ScienceUniversity at Albany xli48@albany.edu

Abstract
Intelligence is fundamentally non-ergodic: it emerges not from uniform sampling or optimization from scratch, but from the structured reuse of prior inference trajectories. We introduce Memory-Amortized Inference (MAI) as a formal framework in which cognition is modeled as inference over latent cycles in memory, rather than recomputation through gradient descent. MAI systems encode inductive biases via structural reuse, minimizing entropy and enabling context-aware, structure-preserving inference. This approach reframes cognitive systems not as ergodic samplers, but as navigators over constrained latent manifolds, guided by persistent topological memory. Through the lens of delta-homology , we show that MAI provides a principled foundation for Mountcastle’s Universal Cortical Algorithm , modeling each cortical column as a local inference operator over cycle-consistent memory states. Furthermore, we establish a time-reversal duality between MAI and reinforcement learning: whereas RL propagates value forward from reward, MAI reconstructs latent causes backward from memory. This inversion paves a path toward energy-efficient inference and addresses the computational bottlenecks facing modern AI. MAI thus offers a unified, biologically grounded theory of intelligence based on structure, reuse, and memory. We also briefly discuss the profound implications of MAI for achieving artificial general intelligence (AGI).



Keywords:

memory-amortized inference (MAI), non-ergodicity, time-reversed reinforcement learning, entropy-reversibility duality, generalized spatial bootstrapping, Mountcastle’s Universal Cortical Algorithm, non-ergodicity, energy bottleneck, artificial general intelligence (AGI)

II
Non-Ergodicity as the Foundation of Intelligence

The concept of
non-ergodicity
provides a unifying lens through which memory, inference, and intelligent behavior can be understood. In contrast to ergodic systems, where all accessible states are explored given sufficient time, intelligent systems exhibit structured, path-dependent behavior, reusing past experiences to guide future inference. This structural departure from statistical uniformity underpins adaptive decision-making and memory-based cognition.

II-A

Definition: Non-Ergodicity in Dynamical Systems

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a phase space and
T^{t}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}
a dynamical evolution operator. A system is
ergodic
if for any observable
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
, the time average equals the ensemble average:

\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}f(T^{t}x)\,dt=\int_{\mathcal{X}}f(x)\,d\mu(x)

where
\mu
is the invariant measure of the system.
A system is
non-ergodic
if this equality fails, i.e., its long-term behavior depends on its initial condition or historical path. Non-ergodicity implies that the system explores only a subset of its possible states, shaped by memory or structural constraints.

Intelligence is inherently
non-ergodic

[
18
]
. Unlike purely stochastic or memoryless agents, intelligent systems exhibit structured, history-dependent dynamics that break the assumption of uniform exploration
[
19
]
. Rather than treating each new observation or decision as independent, intelligent behavior leverages temporal coherence and structural memory to bias its future evolution
[
20
]
. This reuse of temporally coherent, structurally encoded memory trajectories has direct implications for the classic
bias–variance dilemma
in learning systems
[
13
]
.
Specifically, intelligent agents:

•

Reuse structured memory to bias inference trajectories.
Prior experiences are not discarded but actively recalled and embedded into the inference process, enabling the agent to bypass costly recomputation by reusing previously learned representations or latent paths
[
2
]
.

•

Preserve contextual history to guide simulation and prediction.
Context is not a passive backdrop but a dynamic influence that modulates attention, expectation, and planning. The ability to simulate likely futures or reconstruct latent causes depends critically on maintaining a coherent history of the past
[
21
]
.

•

Converge toward preferred states shaped by prior experience.
Through repeated exposure and reinforcement, intelligent agents develop habitual trajectories or attractor basins in latent space that stabilize future inference. These attractors encode inductive biases that constrain exploration and enhance generalization
[
22
]
.

Taken together, these properties imply that cognitive dynamics are not governed by ergodic, memoryless sampling of the latent state space. Instead, they are shaped by
persistent topological structures
, such as memory cycles, homology classes, and attractor submanifolds, that define a context-sensitive flow over the agent’s internal representation space
[
23
]
. This structured, non-uniform recurrence supports adaptive behavior by enforcing coherence, efficiency, and reuse across time. Rather than treating memory as a static buffer or cache, intelligence operationalizes memory as a dynamic substrate/loop for inference, one that allows the system to generalize from fewer examples, infer from partial information, and coordinate action across extended timescales
[
24
]
. From a broken symmetry perspective
[
6
]
, the brain doesn’t just pick a winner; it builds a loop that keeps track of the choice and its alternatives, enabling recall, switching, or reuse later. This is why loops are the natural consequence of broken symmetry: they embody what remains invariant after selection. Formally, we have

Lemma 1

(Symmetry Breaking Implies Cycle Formation)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
denote the latent space of neural states with symmetry group
G
acting transitively on admissible configurations. Suppose a neural dynamics
\varphi_{t}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}
breaks
G
down to a subgroup
H\subset G
, leaving invariant only a restricted set of configurations. Then we have:
1) The quotient
\mathcal{X}/H
admits nontrivial homology generators
[\gamma]\in H_{k}(\mathcal{X}/H)
corresponding to recurrent orbits of
\varphi_{t}
;
2) Each generator
[\gamma]
encodes a cycle that persists under recurrent dynamics, i.e.,
\varphi_{t}(\gamma)\simeq\gamma
, forming a memory trace.
Thus, broken symmetry produces residual topological invariants in the form of nontrivial cycles, which act as
structural memory units
. These cycles capture what remains invariant after the collapse of symmetric alternatives and provide the substrate for persistent perception and action.

In other words, cycle formation is the algebraic footprint of broken symmetry: when multiple possibilities collapse into a structured subset, the residue forms a loop that is nontrivial in homology
[
7
]
. Intelligent systems operate in fundamentally
non-ergodic
environments, where certain states are rarely, if ever, revisited, and where prior experiences must be retained despite nonstationary or sparse inputs
[
25
]
. In such settings, memory must be treated not as a transient buffer but as a structured, persistent substrate for inference. Learning cannot be uniformly applied across all possible experiences; instead, it must be guided by the topological structure and recurrence patterns encoded in memory. Non-ergodicity provides the theoretical foundation for resolving the stability-plasticity dilemma
[
26
]
. By rejecting the assumption of uniform state-space sampling and embracing memory as a dynamic, structure-preserving substrate for inference, non-ergodicity enables continual learning systems to generalize efficiently while maintaining long-term coherence.

These insights motivate the need for a principled computational framework that captures how structured memory, contextual flow, and topological persistence jointly underpin intelligent behavior. We argue that such a framework is provided by
Memory-Amortized Inference
(MAI), which reconceptualizes inference not as re-computation over flat, ergodic priors, but as a reuse of contextually embedded memory cycles and structural trajectories in latent space
[
27
]
. MAI formalizes intelligence as inherently non-ergodic: it operationalizes memory as a topologically constrained substrate for inference, allowing systems to simulate, adapt, and generalize through structure-aware navigation. The following subsection develops this perspective in detail.

II-B

Memory-Amortized Inference as Structured Non-Ergodicity

In the MAI framework, inference proceeds via cyclic reuse of latent trajectories:

\Phi_{t+1}=\mathcal{F}(\Phi_{t},\Psi_{t}),\quad\Phi_{t}\approx\mathcal{R}(\Phi_{t+1},\Psi_{t})

This structure defines a non-ergodic dynamics over a latent manifold
\mathcal{Z}
, where certain cycles are preferentially reused and reinforced. Memory is not a static trace, but a
topological filter
that biases future inference away from unstructured exploration
[
28
]
.
Let
\gamma\subset\mathcal{Z}
be a set of persistent memory cycles. Then the inference trajectory satisfies:

\text{Prob}(\Phi_{t+1}\in\gamma|\Phi_{t})\gg\text{Uniform}(\mathcal{Z})

indicating a structural concentration over reused paths. Formally, we have the following result.

Lemma 2

(Structured Non-Ergodicity of Inference)
.

Let
(\Phi_{t},\Psi_{t})
be a sequence of amortized inference steps over a latent manifold
\mathcal{Z}
. Suppose:
1) The transition dynamics preserve latent cycles:
\Phi_{t+T}\approx\Phi_{t}
for some minimal cycle length
T
;
2) Context transitions
\Psi_{t}
induce non-trivial loops in a nontrivial homology generator
H_{1}(\mathcal{Z})
on a latent manifold of cognitive states;
3) The entropy of future states is minimized by reentry into prior cycles:
H(\Phi_{t+1})<H(\Phi_{t+1}|\Phi_{t}\notin\gamma)
.
Then the system exhibits structured non-ergodicity and supports MAI over persistent latent trajectories.

Sketch.

If cycles are preserved and reused (condition 1), and if context transitions maintain topological integrity (condition 2), then the entropy-minimizing behavior (condition 3) will bias inference toward recurrent structures in
\gamma\subset\mathcal{Z}
. Thus, the system avoids uniform exploration and converges toward stable inference paths, i.e., it is non-ergodic in a structured, memory-preserving sense.
∎

MAI and the Bias-Variance Tradeoff.

Traditional statistical learning theory frames generalization as a tradeoff: systems with low bias may flexibly adapt to new data but risk overfitting due to high variance, while systems with strong inductive bias generalize more reliably but may underfit novel patterns. In ergodic or memoryless learners, the lack of structured priors often leads to over-reliance on recent observations, resulting in unstable inference and poor transfer across tasks. By contrast, intelligent agents mitigate this dilemma through
pre-wired structural biases

[
13
]
, in the form of persistent memory cycles, schema hierarchies, or latent topologies, that constrain learning dynamics to a small, meaningful submanifold of the full hypothesis space.

Within the MAI framework, inductive bias is not externally imposed; instead, it emerges from the internal reuse of memory structures shaped by prior experience. These biases enforce low-dimensional consistency across contexts, effectively reducing variance without sacrificing plasticity
[
29
]
. In this way, MAI reframes generalization not as a balance between bias and variance, but as a consequence of topological coherence: inference remains robust and efficient precisely because it unfolds along trajectories that preserve the agent’s structural memory of the world.

MAI and the Stability-Plasticity Tradeoff.

The classical
stability-plasticity dilemma
in continual learning
[
26
]
refers to the need for a cognitive system to preserve previously acquired knowledge (stability) while remaining adaptable to new information (plasticity). In many learning architectures, especially those based on gradient descent
[
30
]
and uniform sampling, this tradeoff is difficult to manage: learning new tasks often leads to catastrophic forgetting of old ones
[
31
]
, whereas rigid memorization impairs generalization. We argue that this dilemma arises from an implicit assumption of
ergodicity
, the idea that over time, all states or experiences will be revisited, making uniform learning sufficient.

MAI addresses this challenge by formalizing inference as reuse over structured memory cycles rather than re-computation from scratch. Stability is achieved through the persistence of topological features in memory, such as cycles, homology classes, and attractor submanifolds, which encode prior knowledge in a robust, reusable form. Plasticity, in turn, is localized to regions of the latent space where the current memory topology fails to explain new inputs, enabling targeted updates without overwriting global structure
[
27
]
. In this way, MAI introduces a principled mechanism of
structural gating

[
32
]
: new information is integrated only when it generates a genuine topological novelty.

Non-ergodicity

3)

In short,

III
Theoretical Framework

We formalize
memory-amortized inference
(MAI) as a general strategy for reducing the computational cost of inference by storing and reusing structured latent representations. The key idea is to construct a memory of prior inference results such that new inference problems can be approximated by querying and adapting from this memory, rather than solving the full problem from scratch.
Let
\Psi\in\mathcal{X}
denote the observable context and
\Phi\in\mathcal{S}
the latent content to be inferred. Let
\mathcal{L}(\Psi,\Phi)
denote a loss or cost function encoding the fidelity or predictive value of
\Phi
under context
\Psi
. We assume that inference corresponds to solving the following optimization:

\Phi^{*}=\arg\min_{\Phi\in\mathcal{S}}\left[\mathcal{L}(\Psi,\Phi)\right]
.
Formally, we start with the following definition.

Definition 1

(Memory-Amortized Inference)
.

Let
\mathcal{M}=\{(\Psi^{(i)},\Phi^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^{N}
be a memory of prior context–content pairs, and let
\mathcal{R}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{S}
be a retrieval-and-adaptation operator and
\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{S}
be the bootstrapping update operator implemented via generative simulation. Inference is said to be
memory-amortized
if it is formulated as a structural cycle between
content

\Phi
and
context

\Psi
, where memory acts as a reusable substrate for inference:

\Phi_{t+1}=\mathcal{F}(\Phi_{t},\Psi_{t}),\quad\Phi_{t}\approx\mathcal{R}(\Phi_{t+1},\Psi_{t})

in lieu of directly optimizing
\Phi^{*}
, such that the expected cost satisfies

\mathbb{E}_{\Psi}\left[\mathcal{L}(\Psi,\hat{\Phi})\right]\leq\mathbb{E}_{\Psi}\left[\mathcal{L}(\Psi,\Phi^{*})\right]+\varepsilon
,
for some amortization gap
\varepsilon\ll\mathcal{L}(\Psi,\cdot)
, and where the runtime cost of
\mathcal{R}
is substantially lower than full inference.

The Retrieval-and-Adaptation Operator
\mathcal{R}
.

The retrieval-and-adaptation operator
\mathcal{R}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{S}
serves as the core mechanism by which inference avoids re-computation. Given an input query (typically latent or perceptual),
\mathcal{R}
retrieves relevant elements from the memory
\mathcal{M}=\{(\Psi^{(i)},\Phi^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^{N}
and performs a lightweight adaptation to generate a candidate solution
\hat{\Phi}
.
Operationally,
\mathcal{R}
consists of two stages:

•

Retrieval:
Identify a relevant subset of memory entries
\{(\Psi^{(j)},\Phi^{(j)})\}\subset\mathcal{M}
based on similarity to the current context
\Psi_{t}
. This can be performed via kernel-based attention, similarity search in latent space, or topological proximity under homological constraints.

•

Adaptation:
Modulate or interpolate the retrieved
\Phi^{(j)}
values conditioned on
\Psi_{t}
, resulting in a candidate
\hat{\Phi}_{t}=\mathcal{R}(\Phi_{t+1},\Psi_{t})
. This step often involves gradient-free adjustments (e.g., feature warping, parameter blending) and is significantly cheaper than full inference.

The
retrieval-and-adaptation operator

\mathcal{R}
in MAI generalizes the classical notion of key-value memory used in neural attention and memory-augmented models. In conventional key-value memory systems
[
33
,
34
]
, memory is structured as a set of key-value pairs:

\mathcal{M}=\{(\Psi^{(i)},\Phi^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^{N}
,
where a context vector
\Psi
acts as a
key
to retrieve values
\Phi
via similarity-based soft addressing:

\hat{\Phi}=\sum_{i}w_{i}\Phi^{(i)},\quad w_{i}=\frac{\exp(-d(\Psi,\Psi^{(i)}))}{\sum_{j}\exp(-d(\Psi,\Psi^{(j)}))}
.
This model supports one-shot retrieval but lacks structural consistency or bidirectional inference.
By contrast, the operator
\mathcal{R}(\Phi_{t+1},\Psi_{t};\mathcal{M})
in MAI performs a more general operation: it
retrieves
a candidate latent representation from memory based on both the current context
\Psi_{t}
and a target latent code
\Phi_{t+1}
, and then
adapts
it to produce a consistent approximation of the preceding latent state
\Phi_{t}
. This supports inference in reverse time and satisfies the memory-amortized constraint:

\Phi_{t}\approx\mathcal{R}(\Phi_{t+1},\Psi_{t}),\quad\Phi_{t+1}=\mathcal{F}(\Phi_{t},\Psi_{t})
.
The operator
\mathcal{R}
thereby enables cycle-consistent inference, crucial for temporal coherence and structural reuse. Unlike key–value memory, which operates over flat vector spaces,
\mathcal{R}
may act over structured memory (e.g., graphs, latent manifolds, or topological complexes) and is inherently adaptive.

The
retrieval-and-adaptation operator

\mathcal{R}
allows the system to approximate inference by projecting current context onto a previously learned latent manifold, leveraging structural memory to maintain both generality and efficiency.
Once a candidate memory trace is retrieved, it may be refined through a lightweight adaptation process, e.g., gradient-based adjustment, local smoothing, or symbolic substitution, yielding an amortized estimate
\hat{\Phi}=\mathcal{R}(\Psi;\mathcal{M})

The Bootstrapping Update Operator
\mathcal{F}
.

The bootstrapping operator
\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{S}
governs the internal dynamics of inference by iteratively updating the latent content representation
\Phi_{t}
given the context
\Psi_{t}
. It defines a recurrence:

\Phi_{t+1}=\mathcal{F}(\Phi_{t},\Psi_{t})
,
where
\mathcal{F}
encodes the system’s structural prior, capturing the directionality, topology, and dynamic consistency of inference over time. Unlike standard update rules that minimize a loss from scratch,
\mathcal{F}
performs bootstrapping: each update is initialized from a prior memory-induced state, often already close to the optimal solution due to cycle recurrence.
Here are several key properties of
\mathcal{F}
:

•

Cycle-Consistency:
If
(\Phi_{t},\Psi_{t})\in\gamma
for some memory cycle
\gamma\subset\mathcal{Z}
, then
\Phi_{t+T}\approx\Phi_{t}
, enabling amortization via structural recurrence.

•

Structural Biasing:
Updates follow latent paths constrained by prior topology (e.g., flow fields over homology classes or attention-modulated latent graphs), enforcing low-entropy generalization.

•

Minimal Cost Gradient
: Because the initialization
\Phi_{t}
already lies near an attractor, the subsequent update
\Phi_{t+1}
requires only a small corrective shift, further amortizing the inference process.

The bootstrapping update operator
\mathcal{F}
in MAI is structurally analogous to the
half-step down
trick used in Q-learning
[
38
]
and temporal difference (TD) methods
[
8
]
. In Q-learning, the value function is updated by approximating the current value via a one-step lookahead:

Q(s_{t},a_{t})\leftarrow r_{t}+\gamma\max_{a^{\prime}}Q(s_{t+1},a^{\prime})
,
which yields the approximation
Q(s_{t})\approx Q(s_{t+1})
. This forward-directed value propagation allows reinforcement learning agents to estimate long-term outcomes without simulating entire trajectories.

By contrast, MAI reverses the time direction: the update operator
\mathcal{F}
bootstraps latent inference forward using structured memory and contextual cues:

\Phi_{t+1}=\mathcal{F}(\Phi_{t},\Psi_{t})
,
and this is inverted by retrieval:

\Phi_{t}\approx\mathcal{R}(\Phi_{t+1},\Psi_{t})
.
This dual relationship forms the backbone of the MAI half-step trick: the current latent content
\Phi_{t}
generates the next-step prediction
\Phi_{t+1}
, which in turn can be used to reconstruct
\Phi_{t}
. While Q-learning bootstraps value via reward-driven transitions, MAI bootstraps inference through latent memory and context, yielding a cycle-consistent structure that reduces entropy. Both approaches use bootstrapping to manage uncertainty and amortize computational cost, but in opposite directions, highlighting a deeper time-reversed duality between learning and inference.

In summary,
\mathcal{R}
and
\mathcal{F}
jointly implement MAI (refer to Fig.
2
): the former retrieves and reuses structurally consistent priors; the latter ensures dynamic stability and coherence through latent bootstrapping. Together, they replace full optimization with memory-guided navigation over constrained latent trajectories. This complementary interplay between
\mathcal{R}
and
\mathcal{F}
gives rise to a principled mechanism for inference under uncertainty: rather than recomputing content from scratch, the system reuses latent structure from memory and refines it through bootstrapped updates. This recursive formulation enables stable inference trajectories that converge toward contextually relevant attractors, effectively amortizing the cost of learning across time. The underlying dynamics of this process can be formalized as a contractive map over a structured retrieval cycle, leading to provable convergence under mild assumptions. Assume

with small reconstruction errors (denote them collectively by

MAI entropy minimization induces reversibility.

The MAI objective minimizes conditional uncertainty,

\min\;\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MAI}}\;\;:=\;\;H(\Psi_{t}\mid\Phi_{t})\;+\;H(\Phi_{t}\mid\Psi_{t})
,
subject to cycle-consistency.
By the chain rule and the nonnegativity of mutual information,

H(\Psi_{t})\;=\;I(\Psi_{t};\Phi_{t})+H(\Psi_{t}\mid\Phi_{t})\Longrightarrow I(\Psi_{t};\Phi_{t})\;=\;H(\Psi_{t})-H(\Psi_{t}\mid\Phi_{t})

Thus, decreasing

is
equivalent to

a nontrivial structure.

Under cycle-consistency, the forward generative map

is approximately sufficient for
\Psi_{t}

H(\Psi_{t}\mid\Phi_{t})

implies near-deterministic prediction (up to
\varepsilon_{t}

for any function

\Psi_{t}

I(\Phi_{t};h(\Psi_{t}))\;\geq\;I(\tilde{\Psi}_{t};h(\Psi_{t}))\;\approx\;I(\Psi_{t};h(\Psi_{t}))

Hence
\Phi_{t}
retains the predictive information necessary to

the flow: given
(\Phi_{t+1},\Psi_{t})

\mathcal{R}
reconstructs
\Phi_{t}

(up to
\varepsilon_{t}

is the inverse (up to
\varepsilon_{t}

\mathcal{F}

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MAI}}
yields an
approximately reversible

persistent latent cycles. In RL terms, this realizes

the information needed to back up value from
s_{t+1}
to
s_{t}

\Phi_{t}
and can be transported backward via the MAI inverse.

Conversely, suppose reverse value propagation holds along the forward trajectory,

such that relevant

within
\Phi_{t}
and
s_{t}
within
\Psi_{t}

then existence of a stable backward map implies that

sufficient low-entropy statistic

\Psi_{t}

H(\Phi_{t}\mid\Psi_{t})
must also be small for stable reuse. Therefore,
reverse value propagation forces the MAI conditional entropies to be minimized.
This establishes the claimed duality:

\text{Minimize }\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MAI}}\Longleftrightarrow\text{Enable reversible (backward) value propagation.}

Entropy bound for reversibility.

Let
\Delta H:=H(s_{t+1})-H(\Phi_{t-1})

By the chain rule and the law of total entropy,

H(s_{t+1})\;=\;I(s_{t+1};\Phi_{t})\;+\;H(s_{t+1}\mid\Phi_{t})

Under cycle-consistency,
H(s_{t+1}\mid\Phi_{t})
is controlled by the forward
prediction error (bounded by
\varepsilon_{t}

H(s_{t+1}\mid\Phi_{t})\leq\epsilon

H(\Phi_{t-1})\;=\;I(\Phi_{t-1};\Psi_{t-1})\;+\;H(\Phi_{t-1}\mid\Psi_{t-1})\;\geq\;I(\Phi_{t-1};\Psi_{t-1})

so, using time-shifted stationarity/notational alignment and writing

A:=I(\Phi_{t};\Psi_{t})

reused each cycle,

\Delta H=H(s_{t+1})-H(\Phi_{t-1})\;\leq\;\big[I(s_{t+1};\Phi_{t})+\epsilon\big]\;-\;A

Since
I(s_{t+1};\Phi_{t})\leq A
whenever the predictive information flows through
(\Phi_{t},\Psi_{t})

(the MAI bottleneck), we obtain the bound

\Delta H\;\leq\;\epsilon\;\leq\;A

i.e., reversibility holds if and only if the forward–backward entropy gap is bounded by the

amortized structural information

made available by the MAI cycle.
Intuitively, the latent memory must carry at least as much reusable structure as the
uncertainty introduced by one forward step for the backward (value) propagation to be feasible.

Conclusion.

Parts (a) and (b) establish the equivalence

\text{MAI entropy minimization}\iff\text{reversible (backward) value propagation}

and part (c) gives the stated entropy bound. This proves the theorem.
