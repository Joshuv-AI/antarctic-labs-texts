Amine-rich cross-linking templates enabling toughened self-assembled monolayer for stable perovskite solar modules
Authors: Zhinan Zhang, Sifan Chen, Chuan Peng, Yinghao Xu, Shaofu Wang, Wenbo Li, Yang Zheng, Wei Wang, Shengjie Du, Fuyi Zhou, Haimeng Xin, Sixiong Li, Xingzhong Zhao, Ti Wang, Zhenyi Ni, Zhenhua Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
As the most popular hole-selective materials in efficient p-i-n perovskite solar cells (PSCs), self-assembled monolayers (SAMs) still remain great challenges in long-term stability due to their frail construction and week bonding. To form a toughened donor-acceptor interface, here, we adopt an amine-rich cross-linking templates for SAM layer growth. The enhanced Coulombic interaction between polymer amines and SAMs promotes the formation of SAM growth networks, resulting in reduced self-aggregation of SAMs and the uniform SAM films with high coverage. This design minimizes substrate exposure and suppresses perovskite degradation. Consequently, the unencapsulated mini-module retains 94.19% of their initial efficiency after aging for 10,190 hours under the ISOS-D-1 condition and 93.37% of their initial efficiency for 744 hours under the ISOS-L-2 condition. The optimized interface and perovskite films minimizes carrier transport loss, which result in the champion efficiency of 27.07 (certified 26.39%), 24.03, and 18.48% for PSC, mini-module and submodule (aperture area of 14.4 and 700 cm2), respectively.

INTRODUCTION

Over the past decades, perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have witnessed substantial progress and been widely recognized as a promising class of technological routes for the commercialization due to the excellent power conversion efficiency (PCE) (1–3) and cost-effectiveness (4). The development of large-area perovskite solar modules (PSMs) with high efficiency and stability is a crucial step toward commercialization. So far, the PSCs based on self-assembled monolayer (SAM) have exhibited PCE exceeding 27% (5, 6). However, the substantial efficiency and stability gap persists between small-area PSCs and large-area modules, primarily stemming from the unsatisfied quality and uniformity of large-area SAM films and perovskite films (7–10). In principle, the SAM molecules contain an anchoring group that bonds to hydroxyl groups on the transparent conductive oxides (TCOs) substrates, terminal groups and spacer groups (11–13). SAMs processed by wet deposition methods encounter challenges due to their intrinsic hydrophobicity, which often leads to self-aggregation, low coverage, and inhomogeneous distribution on TCO substrates (7, 14–18). During large-area coating techniques such as slot-die or blade coating, SAM molecules possibly tend to stack in the nanosized valleys of fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO) substrate or the glass portion of the P1 scribed trenches, which leads to a multiple-layer stacking with poor SAM coverage and random orientation of hydrophobic SAM head clusters (7, 19). Low coverage enhances direct contact between perovskite and TCO substrates, while molecular stacking may also cause SAM molecule desorption, reducing operational stability. These issues also severely hinder the uniform coating of large-area perovskite films, inevitably introducing pinholes that potentially cause current leakage and induce perovskite degradation at buried interfaces (20, 21). Therefore, it is crucial to successfully achieve the uniform deposition of SAM molecules to enhance the SAM stability.

To address this issue, several approaches have been explored, such as interface engineering and Co-SAM strategies (21, 22). The typical commercialized approach involves sputtering NiOx onto TCO glass to enhance the wettability of the precursor solution, but this method substantially increases costs (23–25). Additives used in Co-SAM strategies primarily consist of small-molecule additives containing carboxylic or phosphoric acids (26, 27), SAM molecules (28), and dipole molecules (29–31) to enhance the monodispersity and coverage of SAM molecules. However, studies focusing on achieving near-monolayer SAM structures and suppressing SAM desorption during operation remain limited. In addition, the design and modification of SAM molecules have also been investigated. Chen et al. (32–34) designed and synthesized a series of polymer SAM molecules that exhibited strong binding to TCO substrates, resulting in enhanced ultraviolet (UV) stability and reverse bias stability. Jiang et al. (1) reported an additive that uses azide groups to cross-link with SAM molecules, enhancing the stability of SAM molecules against external stresses. However, it remains necessary to systematically develop additives with enhanced wettability and biocompatibility to minimize inhomogeneity and low coverage on large-size substrates, thereby enhancing the performance and stability of the module for industrial production.

In this work, we present a buried interface based on cross-linkable polymer templates for the SAM layer, combined with slot-die coating, which enhances the uniformity and stability of SAM films. Specifically, we use polyethylenimine, 80% ethoxylated solution (PEIE) to interact with the phosphonic acid groups of the SAM via proton exchange, forming a PEIE-SAM composite layer through enhanced electrostatic attraction. The constructed PEIE-SAM acts as a network template, which effectively suppresses self-aggregation of SAMs on the substrate and facilitates the formation of uniform and dense SAM films over large areas. The resulting high surface coverage and improved wettability provide a prerequisite for the crystallization of perovskite films, leading to high-quality, large-area perovskite films with effectively suppressed pinhole formation. The optimized interface and perovskite films promote the transport of photo-generated charges and minimize substrate surface exposure, suppressing perovskite degradation. As a result, the unencapsulated mini-modules exhibit enhanced thermal and operational stability, retaining 94.19% of their initial efficiency after aging for 10,190 hours under the ISOS-D-1 condition and 93.37% of their initial efficiency for 744 hours of continuous operation under 1-sun illumination at 55° to 65°C (ISOS-L-2 condition). The submodule also retains more than 90.96% of their initial efficiency for 25 days under ISOS-O-2 condition. The solely SAM-based inverted PSMs fabricated by slot-die coating achieve champion efficiency of 24.03 and 18.48% with aperture area of 14.40 and 700 cm2. Notably, this PEIE-assisted strategy also proves effective for small-area devices, with a spin-coated PSC reaching a champion PCE of 27.07% (certified 26.39%). This work provides a new insight for utilizing wet deposition processes to fabricate stable, large-area SAM-based modules, highlighting the potential of SAM technology for industrial-scale production.

RESULTS

The formation of high-quality, large-area SAMs and perovskite films

To address the challenges of large area SAM films with suppressed self-aggregation and high coverage, we mixed the PEIE polymer into the [2-(3,6-dimethoxy-9H-carbazol-9yl)ethyl]phosphonic acid (MeO-2PACz) solution, followed by slot-die coating onto the TCO substrates. Unless otherwise specified, SAM refers to the pure MeO-2PACz (control), and PEIE-modified SAM is referred to as PEIE, respectively. As shown in Fig. 1A, the amine group of the PEIE molecule undergoes a proton transfer reaction with phosphonic acid in the MeO-2PACz, forming the ionic bond. This interaction is reflected in the calculated electrostatic potentials. The electrostatic potential is depicted in Fig. 1B, compared to MeO-2PACz (1.41 D), SAM + PEIE exhibits a larger dipole moment (2.97 D), which enhances electrostatic attraction within the composite and modulates the substrate work function (1). The enhanced dipole moment may be attributed to the aspect ratio differences caused by molecular asymmetry and the charge separation at the proton transfer region, which favors the formation of robust dipole interface (35). The dynamic light scattering (DLS) is shown in Fig. 1C, the average particle size of the control sample is 301.10 nm. Following the introduction of polymer PEIE molecules into the SAMs, the PEIE samples achieve the reduced average particle size of 48.89 nm, which is attributed to the polymer molecules acting as a self-assembly template for SAM molecules. This effectively reduces the self-aggregation effect and promotes the formation of a large-area, dense SAM. To investigate the chemical interactions between SAM and PEIE molecules, 1H–nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) measurement is carried out. The 1H-NMR spectrum reveals a low-field shift upon addition of PEIE, from 3.73 to 3.79 parts per million, confirming proton transfer (Fig. 1D). Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra of the SAM and SAM + PEIE powders provide further evidence. The bending vibrations of P-OH shift from 1024.03 to 1060.66 cm−1 and from 944.42 to 977.73 cm−1, indicating strong intermolecular interactions between SAM and PEIE (Fig. 1E). The peak at 1130.08 cm−1 is attributed to the P─O bond, further confirming proton transfer and the formation of an ionic bond.

The x-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) further supports the chemical interactions between SAM and PEIE. As shown in figs. S1 to S3, with the introduction of PEIE, the P 2p peak and O 1s peak shift by 0.23 and 0.28 eV to lower binding energy, respectively. The PEIE treatment also induces a 0.27 eV shift of In 3d peak toward lower binding energy (fig. S2). Moreover, XPS analysis reveals an increase in the P/In atomic concentration ratio from 14.76 for the control to 16.09 for the PEIE-treated sample. After DMF rinsing, the P/In ratio of the control sample decreased to 6.32. However, the ratio for the PEIE-modified substrate showed a smaller difference, decreasing only to 8.40 (figs. S4 and S5 and table S1). This higher ratio indicates enhanced SAM coverage on the TCO substrate, facilitated by the PEIE network template (36). The XPS spectra of C 1s further support these results. As shown in fig. S6, the C─P bond shifts toward a higher binding energy, possibly resulting from proton transfer processes and the redistribution of the electron cloud around the P atom (35). The tensile stress measurement is performed, and the device structure is indium tin oxide (ITO)/SAM (SAM + PEIE)/perovskite/epoxy adhesive/glass (inset of Fig. 1F). The polymer PEIE enhances the interfacial tensile strength at the buried interface from 385.67 to 487.53 N, indicating improved adhesion (Fig. 1F). This may be attributed to the strong interactions between PEIE molecules and the SAM or perovskite.

The surface properties and film quality of SAM are systematically evaluated. The atomic force microscopy (AFM) images are shown in Fig. 2 (A and B), the ITO/SAM substrate exhibits a root mean square (RMS) roughness of 3.45 nm. With the introduction of PEIE, the RMS roughness of ITO/SAM + PEIE substrate decreases to 3.13 nm. The surface potential of ITO/SAM and ITO/SAM + PEIE substrates is evaluated by Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM). The ITO/SAM + PEIE substrate shows contact potential difference values of 283.66 mV, which is narrower than that of the ITO/SAM substrate (335.69 mV). This indicates a more uniform and concentrated surface potential distribution for the PEIE-modified sample (figs. S7 and S8). To further analyze the spatial distribution of pristine MeO-2PACz and MeO-2PACz + PEIE on the substrate, photo-induced force microscopy (PiFM) measurement is performed. As illustrated in Fig. 2 (C to F), and figs. S9 to S12, the peaks at 1147 and 1221 cm−1 correspond to C─O bond and the stretching vibrations of C─N bond, respectively (37). The peak position at 1287 cm−1 is associated with the stretching vibrations of the carbazole ring. In addition, the peak of P─OH, P═O, and P─O bonds correspond to 1024, 1191, and 1114 cm−1, respectively (38). The control samples exhibit an inhomogeneous potential distribution, accompanied by visible self-aggregation and pinholes on the substrate. Compared to control samples, PEIE samples have more uniform anchoring on ITO substrate due to minimized self-aggregation and enhanced coverage. The PEIE molecule, acting as a growth template for the SAM, markedly promotes the condensation reaction between phosphate groups in the SAM and hydroxyl groups on the ITO surface. Furthermore, these hydrophilic amine groups substantially improve the substrate wettability, reducing the water contact angle from 74.72° to 42.59° (fig. S13). These effects promote the formation of a uniform and dense SAM layer, which provides an optimal substrate for the subsequent deposition of high-quality perovskite films.

To investigate the morphology and thickness of PEIE, PiFM, and x-ray reflectivity are carried out. The device structure is Glass/PEIE with different concentrations. The peaks at 1630 and 1030 cm−1 correspond to the PEIE molecules (39). As shown in figs. S14 and S15, the PEIE molecules (0.2 mg/ml) form a discontinuous film on the substrate, and the PEIE layer is very thin, measuring only 0.99 nm. These results indicate that PEIE acts solely as a growth template for the SAM, effectively minimizing the impact of the insulator on the series resistance and charge carrier transport of the device (40, 41). Compared to pristine SAM (5.44 nm), SAM + PEIE exhibits a thinner film thickness (3.09 nm), suggesting reduced self-aggregation (fig. S15). The distance between the farthest-apart atoms in MeO-2PACz is approximately 1.23 nm, indicating a multilayered structure for the SAM. After perovskite deposition, we removed the perovskite film and the remaining thicknesses of SAM and SAM + PEIE layers were determined to be 2.72 and 1.12 nm, respectively (fig. S16). We speculate that the SAM + PEIE layer forms a dense monolayer structure after perovskite deposition, whereas the SAM tends to aggregate without PEIE. Moreover, PiFM measurements were conducted on the exposed SAM substrates after removing the perovskite layer. The peaks at 1024 and 1114 cm−1 correspond to P─OH bond and P─O bond, respectively. As shown in fig. S17, the PEIE sample exhibited a more homogeneous potential distribution compared to the control sample, indicating that the introduction of PEIE notably suppresses the aggregation of SAM on the substrate.

To further validate the above results, devices with PEIE molecules on various modified interfaces are fabricated. Compared to the control device, devices based on ITO/SAM/PEIE and ITO/SAM + PEIE substrates exhibit enhanced efficiency, demonstrating that the discontinuous and ultrathin PEIE layer prevents the passivation-carrier transport trade-off in the devices (fig. S18).

In addition, the perovskite films are further characterized to investigate the influence of PEIE treatment on interfacial properties. The peel-off process is performed to expose the bottomed interface of the perovskite film with a detailed schematic illustration shown in fig. S19 (21, 42). The XPS spectra of the perovskite bottom interface is first characterized. As shown in fig. S20, with the introduction of PEIE, the Pb 4f peak and I 3d peak shift by 0.26 and 0.24 eV to lower binding energy, respectively. The N 1s peak also shifts by 0.26 eV toward lower binding energies, indicating strong interactions between the PEIE molecule and the perovskite (fig. S21). The cross-section scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images reveal fewer voids upon PEIE treatment (fig. S22). The SEM images of the bottom interface and top surface further illustrates this improvement. As depicted in Fig. 2G and figs. S23A and S24, perovskite films deposited on the ITO/SAM substrate exhibit numerous pinholes at both the bottom interface and top surface, which can increase leakage current and degrade photovoltaic performance. With the introduction of PEIE molecule, perovskite films on the ITO/SAM + PEIE substrate are markedly more uniform and denser, with markedly reduced pinhole density, which can be attributed to the improved quality of the SAM film and the enhanced wettability of the precursor solution on the substrate (Fig. 2H and figs. S23B and S25). On the basis of the AFM and KPFM measurements, compared to the control sample [RMS = 5.88 nm, full width at half-maximum (FWHM) = 40.78 mV], a reduction in surface roughness and concentrated potential distribution are observed in the PEIE sample, with RMS of 4.51 nm and FWHM of 23.65 mV, further confirming the enhanced quality of the perovskite film (Fig. 2I and figs. S26 and S27). The x-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns are shown in fig. S28, the enhanced crystallinity is achieved for perovskite films deposited on ITO/SAM + PEIE substrates, accompanied by a slight increase in the peak intensity of the (001) face from 20,085 to 21,450 a.u. compared with the control sample (fig. S29). The XRD results are consistent with the AFM and KPFM results.

On the basis of the above characterization, a detailed mechanism has been put forward for achieving large-area SAM films with higher coverage and high-quality perovskite films. As shown in Fig. 2J, during the slot-die coating of the control SAM solution, large-sized micelles are observed because of self-aggregation effects. The low specific surface area of these large micelles hinders efficient condensation reactions with hydroxyl groups on the TCO surface, resulting in low surface coverage and an inhomogeneous distribution of SAM molecules on the substrate. This inhomogeneity is further extended to the subsequently deposited perovskite film, leading to pinhole formation that creates shunting paths and degrades device performance. In contrast, polymer PEIE molecules are introduced to form a dense and uniform network via proton transfer reactions with the SAM, serving as a template for SAM assembly. This strategy effectively enhances the monodispersity of micelles in solution and achieves high-quality, large-area SAM films with higher coverage. Moreover, the hydrophilic amine groups of PEIE substantially improve the wettability of the perovskite precursor solution on the modified substrate. Through the synergistic effects of high-coverage SAM films and enhanced surface wettability, large-area, high-quality perovskite films have been achieved and pinholes have been essentially eliminated.

The carrier behavior at buried interfaces

The UV photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) is performed to investigate the energy band structure. As shown in fig. S30, the work function (WF) of ITO/SAM substrate and ITO/SAM + PEIE substrate are 4.67 and 4.73 eV, respectively. Obviously, the work function of the ITO/SAM + PEIE substrate is more matched to the valence band maximum of perovskite, which reduces the energy barrier at the buried interface and minimizes interfacial energy loss (fig. S31 and table S2). The shift in work function may be attributed to the increase in dipole moment and surface coverage, as well as the change in SAM layer thickness. To evaluate the spatial uniformity of the perovskite film and interfacial charge extraction, a 6 cm × 6 cm perovskite film is divided into five equal sections, and steady-state photoluminescence (PL) measurements are performed. The glass/ITO/SAM/perovskite structure is tested while the laser is focused on the glass side. As shown in Fig. 3 (A and B), the PL peak intensities of the control samples exhibit substantial variations across five measured positions, which may be attributed to the inhomogeneity of the large-area SAM layer. In contrast, enhanced uniformity and reduced intensity of the PL peak are observed in perovskite films deposited on ITO/SAM + PEIE substrates, indicating that PEIE facilitates the formation of high-quality perovskite films and promotes hole extraction (43). Time-resolved PL (TRPL) spectra of perovskite films are carried out and fitted by a double-exponential equation. As shown in Fig. 3C and table S3, compared to the control film (τave = 353.00 ns), the PEIE film also exhibits a markedly reduced carrier lifetime (τave = 169.70 ns), consistent with the PL results. The fast decay component (τ1) in the TRPL curve is associated with charge extraction. The τ1 lifetime of the control film is 16.61 ns, while that of the PEIE sample is reduced to 13.72 ns, indicating enhanced charge extraction (44). The PL quantum yield (PLQY) is further used to investigate carrier dynamics, and the results are presented in Fig. 3D. The PLQY values for the control and PEIE samples are determined to be 6.77 and 7.74%, respectively. The calculated quasi-Fermi level splitting (QFLS) also increases from 1.20 to 1.21 V (table S4). Obviously, the perovskite films of PEIE samples show an enhancement in PLQY and QFLS value, further confirming the beneficial effect of PEIE treatment in reducing nonradiative recombination. The open-circuit voltage loss caused by nonradiative recombination (∆VOC−impnonrad) is also calculated by the formula: ∆VOC−impnonrad=−KBTln(PLQY)/q, where KB, T, and q are the Boltzmann constant, temperature, and elemental charge, respectively. Following the introduction of PEIE, ∆VOC−impnonrad decreased from 70.03 to 66.53 mV, which benefits the increase in the open-circuit voltage (VOC) of the device (table S4).

PL mapping measurements are conducted to investigate the quality of the bottom interface in perovskite films. Over the 80 × 80 μm area, the perovskite film based on PEIE exhibits uniform and enhanced PL map intensity compared to control samples, indicating reduced nonradiative recombination and improved perovskite film quality (figs. S32 and S33). Transient absorption (TA) spectroscopy is also used to investigate charge carrier dynamics at the buried interface, with the pump and probe beams incident through the glass substrate. The change in absorption induced by the pump excitation is primarily attributed to the ground-state photobleaching (GSB) peak. The pseudo-color TA plots of the control and PEIE sample is shown in fig. S34, the GSB peak, observed at approximately 766 nm, originates from band filling by photoexcited carriers. The decay of the GSB peak is shown in Fig. 3E. The decay of the perovskite film deposited on the ITO/SAM + PEIE substrate exhibits a substantial reduction from 10,454.88 ps to 2995.48 ps, further supporting the enhanced hole extraction. The τ1 lifetime decreases from 244.93 ps of the control film to 116.06 ps of the PEIE film, consistent with the TRPL results. The dark current density-voltage (J−V) measurement is shown in fig. S35, the dark current density of the PEIE mini-module is markedly suppressed, demonstrating reduced nonradiative recombination and leakage of currents. Light intensity dependent VOC experiments indicate that the ideal factor (n) has decreased from 1.72 KBT/q to 1.26 KBT/q (Fig. 3F). Last, to further investigate the uniformity of 30 cm × 30 cm perovskite films by slot-die coating process, 30 cm × 30 cm perovskite film is divided into nine equal sections, and PL mapping measurements of the bottom interfaces are carried out (fig. S36). As shown in Fig. 3 (G and H), compared to the control sample, perovskite films deposited on ITO/SAM + PEIE substrates exhibit markedly enhanced PL intensity and more concentrated intensity distribution, indicating that large-area perovskite films achieve higher uniformity. The above results demonstrate that the PEIE polymer not only effectively optimizes the transport of photogenerated carriers at the bottom interface but also promotes the formation of high-quality perovskite films and SAM films with high uniformity and high coverage.

Photovoltaic performance and stability for PSCs and PSMs

To investigate the applicability of polymer network templates in various processes, PSCs are first prepared via a spin-coating process, and the device structure is TCO/SAM + PEIE/PVK/fullerene (C60)/SnO2/Cu. The PEIE-treated PSCs also achieve an enhanced average PCE value of 25.68 ± 0.52%, with corresponding average values of short-circuit current density (JSC), open-circuit voltage (VOC), and fill factor (FF) at 25.86 ± 0.15 mA/cm2, 1.173 ± 0.01 V, and 84.63 ± 0.93%, respectively (table S5 and fig. S37). The J-V curve of the champion device is shown in Fig. 4A, compared to the control device (PCE = 24.39%), the PEIE-treated PSC exhibits a champion efficiency of 27.07%, accompanied by JSC value up to 26.18 mA/cm2, VOC value up to 1.192 V, FF value up to 86.73% (active area, 0.09 cm2). The PEIE-treated PSC is tested by the National Photovoltaic Industry Measurement and Testing Center to report a certified efficiency of 26.39% with VOC value of 1.186 V, JSC value of 25.63 mA/cm2, and FF value of 86.81% (Fig. 4B). The steady-state certified efficiency is 26.02% (fig. S38). The FF loss calculations indicate that the PEIE modification strategy synergistically suppresses nonradiative recombination and reduces charge transport losses at the buried interface (fig. S39). The integral current density derived from external quantum efficiency (EQE) spectra is 24.50 and 25.07 mA/cm2 for the control and PEIE-treated PSC, which are consistent with the J-V results (fig. S40). Meanwhile, the enhanced quantum conversion efficiency is observed in the EQE spectra of PEIE-treated PSC, particularly in the short-wavelength region, demonstrating improved extraction of photogenerated carriers at the bottom interface (45). The bandgap of perovskite is calculated to be 1.55 eV based on the first derivative of the EQE spectrum (fig. S41). The PEIE-treated PSC maintains a higher stable output efficiency of 26.42% of maximum power point (MPP) tracking over 300 s of continuous operation (fig. S42).

In addition, by using the above optimized buried interface, high-quality SAM and perovskite films are also slot-die coated onto TCO substrates. The laser-scribing technology is employed to connect the subcells in series, and the size and arrangement of the P1-P2-P3 structure formed by laser scribing are shown in fig. S43 and the inset of Fig. 4D. A narrow dead area width of 239 μm is achieved, resulting in a high geometric FF of about 96.70%. The mini-module with an aperture area of 14.40 cm2 is successfully fabricated, and the statistical data of the photovoltaic parameters are presented in Fig. 4C and fig. S44. The control modules exhibit an average PCE value of 17.46 ± 0.95%, with corresponding average values of JSC, VOC, and FF at 3.995 ± 0.08 mA/cm2, 6.814 ± 0.22 V, and 64.16 ± 4.05%, respectively. The PEIE modules achieve an enhanced average PCE value of 20.23 ± 1.09%, with corresponding average values of JSC, VOC, and FF at 4.073 ± 0.04 mA/cm2, 6.924 ± 0.13 V, and 71.71 ± 2.91%, respectively (table S6). The distribution of photovoltaic parameters is markedly more concentrated for the PEIE module, demonstrating the effectiveness and reproducibility of this bottom interface strategy. The J-V curves illustrated in Fig. 4D demonstrate that the PEIE module achieves a champion efficiency of 24.03%, substantially surpassing the 20.22% efficiency of the control module. The champion mini-modules with PEIE exhibit VOC value of 7.177 V, JSC value of 4.131 mA/cm2, and FF value of 81.04%. These optimized values obtained in PEIE-based PSMs are primarily attributed to enhanced extraction of photogenerated carriers and improved quality of large-area perovskite films. The dependence of device performance on PEIE concentration is further detailed in fig. S45. In addition, Fig. 4E demonstrates that the PEIE mini-module maintains a stabilized output efficiency of 23.47% under MPP tracking over 360 s of continuous operation. To investigate the reproducibility of this strategy on a larger scale, we fabricate a submodule with an aperture area of 700 cm2 using the slot-die coating process assisted by the PEIE network template. The current-voltage (I-V) curves and photograph of the submodule are illustrated in Fig. 4F. The submodule, comprising 44 series-connected subcells, achieves the champion efficiency of 18.48%, with VOC value of 51.79 V, short-circuit current (ISC) value of 0.39 A, and FF value of 64.05% (aperture area of 700 cm2).

The stability of the module and PSC is investigated under the International Organic Solar Stability Summit (ISOS) protocol. First, the long-term operational stability of the mini-module is performed through MPP tracking under continuous one sun equivalent illumination, following the ISOS-L-2 protocol. During the testing period, the unencapsulated mini-module is continuously exposed to a temperature of 55° to 65°C. The spectra of the illumination source are shown in fig. S46, consisting of white light-emitting diodes (LEDs) calibrated to one sun intensity. As shown in Fig. 4G, the unencapsulated PEIE-based mini-module retains 93.37% of its initial PCE after 744 hours of MPP tracking under 1-sun illumination, whereas the control mini-module drops below 73% of its initial PCE after 500 hours. In addition, we further perform dark stability testing of the mini-module following the ISOS-D-1 protocol. The unencapsulated mini-module is maintained under dark conditions and open-circuit voltage state at a temperature of 15° to 35°C. The unencapsulated PEIE-based mini-module preserves 94.19% of its initial PCE after 10,190 hours, while the control mini-module remains only 81.60% of its initial PCE after 3340 hours (Fig. 4H). To further investigate the reproducibility of the network template strategy on stability, the operational stability of PSCs is also evaluated. As shown in fig. S47, the PEIE-based PSC also retains over 98.50% of its initial PCE after 2420 hours of MPP tracking under 1-sun illumination (ISOS-L-2). The operational stability of the PSC under humidity and heat conditions is also further performed at a test temperature of 85°C and a humidity range of 55 to 75% RH. Under the ISOS-L-3 protocol, the PEIE-based PSC maintains over 88.92% of its initial PCE after 504 hours of MPP tracking (fig. S48), demonstrating the effectiveness and reproducibility of the PEIE strategy. Last, the PEIE-based submodule is exposed to outdoor conditions to evaluate its operational stability under real-world conditions, following the ISOS-O-2 protocol. The submodule is encapsulated by a cover glass, and the encapsulation materials are poly-olefin and butyl rubber. During testing, the outdoor temperature and relative humidity are 15° to 30°C and 55 to 75% RH, respectively. As shown in Fig. 4I, the submodule preserves 90.96% of its initial PCE after 25 days of MPP tracking in outdoor conditions.

To elucidate the mechanism of enhanced stability, we characterize the aged films by time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (TOF-SIMS) to investigate the desorption and ion migration of SAM molecules. As shown in Fig. 4J and fig. S49, after aging, the enhanced PO2− signal in the perovskite of the control sample indicates desorption and migration of SAM molecules into the perovskite layer. In contrast, PEIE-modified SAMs maintain stable bonding with the TCO substrate after aging, accompanied by fewer PO2− signals in the perovskite layer (18). Fewer PO2− signals are also observed at the surface of PEIE-modified perovskite films, further confirming the reduced desorption of SAM molecules (Fig. 4, K and L). To investigate the interfacial distribution of PEIE, the PiFM measurements were conducted on the exposed SAM + PEIE substrate after removing the perovskite layer. The peaks at 1630 and 1030 cm−1 correspond to PEIE molecules. As shown in fig. S50, PEIE-related signals were uniformly distributed across the interface, suggesting that PEIE remains within the SAM film after perovskite removal. Moreover, PEIE exhibits low solubility in DMF, and the solution becomes turbid at a concentration of 4.5 mg/ml (fig. S51). Although the possibility that a small fraction of PEIE molecules penetrated into the perovskite layer cannot be completely excluded, such penetration does not notably affect the electrical properties.

Furthermore, PiFM measurements were performed on SAM-modified substrates after light-soaking treatment to investigate the changes of SAM during the aging process. As shown in fig. S52, after 1 week of continuous light soaking, the aged control substrates exhibited a highly heterogeneous potential distribution, with micrometer-scale agglomerations and pinholes on the surface. In contrast, the PEIE-modified substrates maintained a relatively uniform potential distribution after aging. Subsequently, we fabricated PSCs using aged SAM substrates. The statistical distributions of the photovoltaic parameters are summarized in fig. S53. Compared with the fresh control devices, the aged control devices exhibited noticeable degradation in all photovoltaic parameters, with the average PCE decreasing from 23.66 ± 0.55% to 19.22 ± 1.24%. In contrast, the PEIE-treated devices exhibited enhanced photostability, with the average PCE decreasing from 25.55 ± 0.39% to 23.39 ± 0.75% after aging. We attribute the enhanced stability to the cross-linking interaction between PEIE and SAM. The polymer PEIE molecules at the buried interface enhance the packing of SAM molecules on the TCO substrate and improve the quality of the perovskite film. This strategy minimizes contact between the perovskite and TCO substrate and the desorption of SAM molecules during operation, thereby suppressing nonradiative recombination at the interface and the degradation of the perovskite (1, 46, 47).

DISCUSSION

In summary, we have developed an effective cross-linkable template strategy to simultaneously enhance the crystallization of perovskite films and the stability of SAM. The amine group of PEIE molecules can undergo proton transfer reactions with the phosphonic acid groups of SAM molecules, forming an interfacial network through ionic interactions. The network structure of PEIE molecules facilitates the alignment of SAM molecules, resulting in reduced self-aggregation and uniform, large-area SAM films with high coverage. The uniform SAM layer and the introduction of additional amine groups effectively enhance the wettability, facilitating the formation of dense and pinhole-free perovskite films. The resulting optimized interface and film quality enhance charge transport and minimize substrate surface exposure, suppressing perovskite degradation. As a result, this strategy exhibits broad applicability, the champion efficiency of 27.07 (certified 26.39%), 24.03, and 18.48% is achieved for spin-coated PSC (0.09 cm2), slot-die-coated mini-module (aperture area of 14.4 cm2) and submodule (aperture area of 700 cm2), respectively. Furthermore, the corresponding mini-modules and PSCs exhibit enhanced stability under both dark storage (ISOS-D-1) and continuous illumination (ISOS-L-2) conditions. This work highlights the potential of cross-linkable SAMs to fabricate highly efficient and stable PSMs.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Materials

Formamidinium iodide (FAI) was purchased from greatcell solar. Propane-1, 3-diammonium iodide (PDAI2, ≥98%), lead chloride (PbCl2, 99.999%), cesium iodide (CsI, 99.999%), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; anhydrous, 99.9%), N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF; anhydrous, 99.8%) and N-methylpyrrolidone (NMP; anhydrous, 99.8%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Phenylmethylammonium chloride (PMACl, ≥99.5%), methylammonium chloride (MACl), and methylammonium bromide (MABr) were purchased from Xi’an Yuri Solar. Lead iodide (PbI2, 99.99%) and dimethoxy-9H-carbazol-9-yl)ethyl]phosphonic acid (MeO-2PACz, 98.0%) were purchased from TCI. Copper (Cu), anhydrous ethanol was purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent. Ultradry chlorobenzene (CB), N-(4-carboxyphenyl)guanidine hydrochloride (GuaBCl, 99%) were purchased from Innochem. Isopropyl alcohol (IPA, 99.5%) and PEIE were purchased from Macklin. Fullerene (C60, 99.9%) was purchased from Advanced Election Technology. Lead bromide (PbBr2, 99.999%) was purchased from Leyan. All chemicals were not further purified.

Preparation of the perovskite precursor solution

For the perovskite precursor solution of solar cell, the 1.5 M solution with the chemical formula Cs0.05MA0.05FA0.90PbBr0.1I2.9 was prepared via dissolution of 18.37 mg of CsI, 197.86 of mg FAI, 6.76 mg of MABr, 8.88 mg of MACl, 577.15 of mg PbI2, 22.2 of mg PbBr2 in 873 μl of DMF, and DMSO solution (v/v = of 4: 1) and stirring 3 hours at room temperature. For the perovskite precursor solution of solar module, the 0.8 M solution with the chemical formula Cs0.05MA0.05FA0.90PbBr0.1I2.9 was prepared in DMF and NMP solution (v/v = of 7: 3). Then, PMACl (1.1 mg/ml), PbCl2 (2.1 mg/ml), and GuaBCl (0.4 mg/ml) were added into the perovskite precursor solution and stirred 30 min. Last, the precursor solution was filtered with 0.45-μm poly(tetrafluoroethylene) filters.

PSC fabrication

The ITO glass substrates were washed in IPA and acetone by ultrasonic treatment for 20 min, respectively. The ITO substrates were treated by UV-ozone (UVO) for 15 min. The ethanol solution of MeO-2PACz or MeO-2PACz + PEIE (0.1, 0.2, and 0.4 mg/ml) were spin-coated onto the ITO glass substrates at 5000 rpm for 20 s, followed by annealing at 110°C for 10 min to fabricate the SAMs. Then, the hole-selective-layer–coated ITO was washed with anhydrous ethanol by dynamic coating at 5000 rpm for 20 s, followed by annealing at 110°C for 10 min to eliminate unbound molecules. The perovskite precursor solution was spin-coated on SAMs at 1000 rpm for 5 s; then, 120 μl of CB was rapidly added to the top of the perovskite at 3000 rpm for 30 s, 15 s before the end of the spin-coating process. The film was annealing at 110°C for 15 min. For surface passivation, The IPA solution of PDAI2 was cast onto 5000 rpm spun perovskite films, followed by annealing at 110°C for 10 min. Last, 18-nm C60, 20-nm ALD-SnO2, and 80-nm copper electrode were sequentially thermal evaporated onto these perovskite films.

Perovskite mini-module fabrication

The perovskite mini-modules consisting of six subcells connected in series were fabricated on ITO glass substrates with dimensions of 6.0 cm × 6.0 cm. The series interconnection of the module was accomplished by means of P1, P2, and P3 lines, which were scribed with a laser scribing system of 1064 and 532 nm at a power of 20 W. The P1 lines (a width of 18 μm) were prepatterned on the TCO glass substrates by using 28% of a laser power under a speed of 400 mm/s with a frequency of 100 kHz and a pulse duration of 120 ns. The TCO substrate was cleaned with organic solvent and treated with UVO for 15 min. The SAMs and perovskite film were fabricated by a slot-die technique, with a gap of 100 μm. The coating velocity was 15 mm s−1, coupled with a liquid output of 20 μl s−1. The fresh SAM films were annealed at 110°C for 10 min. The fresh perovskite film was prepared by the same process and was quickly placed in a vacuum flashing chamber, which is pumped to a pressure of less than 10 Pa and kept for 30 s. The film was annealing at 110°C for 15 min. For surface passivation, the IPA solution of PDAI2 (3 mg/ml) was cast onto 5000 rpm spun perovskite films, followed by annealing at 110°C for 10 min. Subsequently, 20-nm C60 and 20-nm ALD-SnO2 were sequentially thermal evaporated and atomic layer deposition onto these perovskite films. Before the copper electrode deposition process step, the P2 lines (a width of 93 μm) were patterned. This was accomplished at an average laser power of 29% under a speed of 300 mm/s with a frequency of 500 kHz and a pulse duration of 120 ns. When the 100-nm-thick copper electrode was deposited, the P3 lines (a width of 39 μm) were patterned following the same scribing conditions as the P2 lines.

Perovskite submodule fabrication

The perovskite submodules consisting of 44 subcells connected in series were fabricated on FTO glass substrates with dimensions of 30.0 cm × 30.0 cm. The series interconnection of the module was accomplished by means of P1, P2, and P3 lines, which were scribed with a laser scribing system of 1064 and 532 nm at a power of 20 W. The P1 lines (a width of 18 μm) were prepatterned on the TCO glass substrates by using 28% of a laser power under a speed of 400 mm/s with a frequency of 100 kHz and a pulse duration of 120 ns. The TCO substrate was cleaned with organic solvent and treated with UVO for 15 min. The SAMs and perovskite film were fabricated by a slot-die technique, with a gap of 100 μm. The coating velocity was 15 mm s−1, coupled with a liquid output of 20 μl s−1. The fresh SAM films were annealed at 110°C for 10 min. The fresh perovskite film was prepared by the same process and was quickly placed in a vacuum flashing chamber, which is pumped to a pressure of less than 10 Pa and kept for 60 s. The film was annealing at 110°C for 15 min. For surface passivation, The IPA solution of PDAI2 (1 mg/ml) was cast onto 1800 rpm spun perovskite films, followed by annealing at 110°C for 10 min. Subsequently, 30-nm C60 and 30-nm ALD-SnO2 were sequentially thermal evaporated and atomic layer deposition onto these perovskite films. Before the copper electrode deposition process step, the P2 lines (a width of 93 μm) were patterned. This was accomplished at an average laser power of 29% under a speed of 300 mm/s with a frequency of 500 kHz and a pulse duration of 120 ns. When the 100-nm-thick copper electrode was deposited, the P3 lines (a width of 39 μm) were patterned following the same scribing conditions as the P2 lines.

Electrical measurements

Current density and voltage curves (J-V) were performed by using a Keithley 2450 primary meter, and the light source was the simulated AM1.5G irradiation (100 mW/cm2) generated via the xenon-lamp–based ZOliX SS150 solar simulator. The light intensity was calibrated by standard silicon solar cell. All J-V curves were obtained in reverse scan mode. The EQE measurements were carried out with the Enli QE measurement kit (Enli Technology Co. Ltd) and samples were calibrated by standard reference silicon solar cell with a known EQE. Unless specified, all PSCs had an operating area of 9 mm2, which was determined by the overlap area of the ITO and Cu electrodes. All perovskite solar was encapsulated by using epoxy, UV adhesive and cover glass in nitrogen-filled glove box. All measurements of the encapsulated devices were performed in air environment.

Stability testing of the mini-module

For the ISOS-D-1 aging test, the unencapsulated mini-modules were placed at dark storage and open-circuit voltage with temperature of 15° to 35°C.

For the ISOS-L-2 aging test, the unencapsulated mini-modules were fabricated using the method described above with a structure of ITO/MeO-2PACz or MeO-2PACz and PEIE/perovskite/C60/ALD-SnO2 (30 nm)/Cu. The operational stability of the unencapsulated mini-module was measured by a home-made stability test system operating in the MPP tracking mode. Specifically, the illumination source is white LED with its intensity calibrated to match 1-sun conditions by a silicon reference cell. The spectrum of LED light source was shown in fig. S46. The humidity was around 20 to 30% RH, and the measured temperature was around 55° to 65°C. During the light soaking of unencapsulated mini-modules, the modules were connected to a resistor such that they worked at their MPP at the beginning of the test. After light soaking for different durations, the devices were taken to the solar simulator for J-V measurement. The J-V measurements were performed by using a Keithley 2450 primary meter, and the light source was the simulated AM1.5G irradiation (100 mW/cm2) generated via the xenon-lamp–based ZOliX SS150 solar simulator. The J-V curve was measured in reverse scan mode, with a testing interval of 24 hours. The voltage ranges from 7.2 to −0.2 V, with a scan rate of 0.1 V/s. The testing method for PSCs is consistent with the mini-module.

For the ISOS-L-3 aging test, test temperature is 85°C, test humidity is 55 to 75% RH, and the testing method for PSCs is consistent with the mini-module. For the ISOS-O-2 aging test, test temperature is 15° to 30°C, test humidity is 55 to 75% RH, and the testing method for submodule is consistent with the mini-module.

Characterization

The SEM images were taken by using ZEISS GeminiSEM 500. The XRD was taken from Rigaku Smartlab with x-ray generator at 3 KW assisted by copper Kα radiation (λ = 1.5418 Å) with the scanning range and scanning speed are 5° to 50° and 5°/min. The PL spectrum and TRPL spectrum were carried out on HORIBA Delta Flex fluorescence spectrometer. A 372-nm laser was used as excitation source to collect signals at 700 to 900 nm. AFM and KPFM images were performed by Bruker Dimension Icon XR equipment in Scanasyst-Air and Peak force KPFM mode. XPSs were characterized by Thermo Fisher Scientific K-Alpha and XPS spectra were calibrated by the reference C1s level at 284.80 eV. UPS was conducted in Thermo Fisher Scientific K-Alpha system with a He Iα photon source (hν = 21.22 eV) and a bias voltage of −10 V. All of the UPS and XPS spectra were processed and fitted via Advantage software. The DLS were characterized by Malvern Zetasizer Pro. FTIRs were measured by Thermo Fisher Scientific Nicolet iS50. NMR was measured by Bruker AVANCE III HD 500 MHZ. The force-displacement curve was measured by Electronic Universal Testing Machine CMT6104.

PiFM measurements

PiFM results were acquired using a VistaScope microscope from Molecular Vista Inc. All PiFM experiments were excited by a pulsed quantum cascade laser (Block Engineering) with a gap-free narrowband tunable wave number of 760 to 1950 cm−1. The spectral linewidth is ∼2 cm−1 with a wave number resolution of 0.5 cm−1. The PiFM experiment here was operated at the sideband excitation with the laser-frequency modulated at fm = f1 − f0, where f0 is the first mechanical eigenmode resonances of the cantilever that is used for PiF signal detection, while f1 denotes the second ones recorded for the AFM topography of the sample. The probe is an Pt-coated tip with a resonant frequency of ∼350 kHz (PPP-NCHPt-MB, Nanosensors).

Ultrafast TA

The output of a femtosecond laser (repetition rate of 40 kHz, 1030 nm central wavelength, and pulse duration of 120 fs, PHAROS, Light Conversion) was split into two parts. One went through optical parametric amplification (ORPHEUS twins, Light Con version) and then became a pump to excite perovskite thin films. The other went through the delay stage and produced white light via sapphire acting as the probe light. The pump and probe were focused on the sample with a spot size of about 64 mm. Owing to the small angle between the two beams, the pump was blocked by the pinhole. The probe was therefore collected with a spectrometer. The above configurations were carried out with an ultrafast TA spectrometer (HARPIA, Light Conversion).

Time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry

Depth profiling of perovskite films was carried out by using an ION-TOF TOF-SIMS V spectrometer. Analysis was completed by using a 30-kV Bi primary ion gun. High-mass-resolution depth profiles were completed with a 30-KeV Bi3+ primary ion beam (1.0-pA pulsed beam current) and the analysis area was 100 × 100 μm. The primary ion beam dose density was kept below 7.2 × 1013 ions/cm2 to remain under the static-sims limit. Sputter depth profiling was accomplished with a 1-keV O2 ion beam (110-nA sputter current) and the sputter area was 300 × 300 μm.
