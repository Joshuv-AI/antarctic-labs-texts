Evolution of strangeness and hyperons in quarkyonic matter
Yuki Fujimoto, Toru Kojo, Larry McLerran

Abstract
We study the evolution of matter composition from nuclear to quark densities in the confining regime, by extending an ideal model of Quarkyonic matter, IdylliQ model, to multi-flavor systems including strangeness. The model provides a dual description of quark and baryon occupation probabilities which are determined by minimizing the energy of the system. Saturation of low-momentum quark states drives the formation of quark matter and constrains baryon distributions, inducing statistical repulsion among baryon species. Applying the model to charge-neutral matter composed of neutrons, Λ 0 \Lambda_{0} , and Σ 0 \Sigma_{0} hyperons, we find that, for typical size of baryons, d d -quark saturation occurs before hyperons appear, delaying their onset and shifting the threshold density from ∼ 2 \sim 2 – 3 ​ n sat 3n_{\rm sat} to ∼ 5 \sim 5 – 6 ​ n sat 6n_{\rm sat} ( n sat ≈ 0.16 ​ fm − 3 n_{\rm sat}\approx 0.16\,{\rm fm^{-3}} : nuclear saturation density). After hyperons emerge, low-momentum hyperon states remain only sparsely occupied due to the quark saturation. These features mitigate the hyperon puzzle, in which the appearance of hyperons softens neutron star equations of state significantly by increasing energy density with little pressure increase. Our results highlight the key role of quark saturation in dense baryonic matter and provide new insights into the interplay between quark dynamics and hyperon physics in neutron stars.

Introduction

Describing the evolution of matter from hadronic to quark matter is one of the most important challenges in quantum chromodynamics (QCD) and modern nuclear physics.
The problem includes the evolution of matter compositions such as baryon species and lepton fraction.
In neutron star phenomenology, the understanding of matter content at baryon densities above
n_{B}\gtrsim 2n_{\mathrm{sat}}
(
n_{\mathrm{sat}}\approx 0.16\,\text{fm}^{-3}
: nuclear saturation density) is crucial to delineate not only the equation of state (EoS) but also transport phenomena such as cooling.
The need for stiffening of the EoS at a relatively low density, around
1.5~n_{\mathrm{sat}}
, is established phenomenologically
[
1
]
.
For the recent development at
n_{B}\lesssim 2n_{\mathrm{sat}}
, see Ref.
[
2

To bridge nuclear and quark matter descriptions, seminal works have elaborated Quarkyonic matter models, applying them to neutron-star phenomenology
[
3
]
and also to nuclear matter
[
4
,
5
]
.
Quarkyonic matter
[
6
]
is a matter composed of the quark Fermi sea with the baryonic Fermi surface
[
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
]
, and color confinement remains important until the color screening caused by quarks cuts off confining gluons.
Quarks near the Fermi surface are trapped into baryons, while the bulk of the Fermi sea is filled equally by quark states at different colors and hence is color singlet.
The range of the quark chemical potential
\mu
for Quarkyonic matter descriptions is estimated to be
\Lambda_{\rm QCD}\lesssim\mu\lesssim\sqrt{N_{c}}\Lambda_{\rm QCD}

[
6
]
with
\Lambda_{\rm QCD}
and
N_{c}
being the typical non-perturbative scale in QCD and the number of colors, respectively.

Recently, the present authors have constructed an ideal model of Quarkyonic matter, IdylliQ model
[
11
]
, which allows us dual descriptions for the occupation probabilities of states for quarks and baryons.
The advantage of this dual model is that one can keep track of both degrees of freedom, which are dual to each other, all the way from nuclear to quark matter densities.
The quark matter formation is characterized by the saturation of quark states which begins at low momenta, triggering the rapid stiffening of the EoS, i.e., rapid increase in the pressure as a function of the energy density.
In the dual baryonic language, the quark Pauli blocking constraint leads to strong suppression of the baryon occupation densities at low momenta while baryons at higher momenta are free of such constraint;
the emerging picture is a momentum space shell structure of baryons
at the Fermi surface with the bulk part of the Fermi sea filled by quarks
[
3
,
6
,
12

, as originally conjectured in Ref.
[
6
]

The previous IdylliQ model is restricted to isospin symmetric matter with equal amount of protons and neutrons.
In this paper we extend our previous study to three-flavor matter including strange quarks, elaborating the framework to describe the evolution of matter composition.
This extension clarifies the importance of the quark physics in baryonic matter more vividly than in the flavor symmetric case.
In particular, the quark Pauli blocking alone induces statistical repulsions among different baryon species.
For simplicity and illustration of key ideas, in this paper we focus on charge neutral matter composed of charge neutral baryons, neutron

We also examine the appearance of

Our descriptions should have important implications on the hyperon puzzle, if there is one, which states that the appearance of hyperons softens neutron star EoS significantly

In this paper, we focus on the neutron-rich regime where

The main results of this paper are two-fold:

(i) The

This additional

(ii) Softening associated with the appearance of

The

-quark saturation constraint allows hyperons to occupy low momentum states only with small probability of

Hence, hyperons must occupy higher momenta soon after their emergence.
Meanwhile,

This density may be beyond those achievable in neutron stars.

Although the present work considerably simplifies matter with hyperons by e.g., omitting detailed descriptions of baryon-baryon interactions and structural change of baryons, we believe that the above mentioned quark saturation effects have some generic features emerging from the quark matter formation, and our statistical considerations should help to infer or constrain the trend of in-medium many-baryon forces.
We expect that convoluting the knowledge of the bare baryon-baryon interactions, being studied in various methodologies, with our statistical considerations eventually leads to the full solution for the hyperon puzzle.

II
Preliminaries: Flavorless Ideal Dual Quarkyonic (IdylliQ) Model

In this section, we review the details of the ideal dual Quarkyonic (IdylliQ) model, which is necessary to understand the content of the current paper.
This is an elaboration of the work presented in Ref.
[
11
]
, with some extra detail included.
The results of this section will be needed for our later analysis.

II.1
Model

We assume a duality relation between the momentum distribution function for quarks with a given color
f_{q}(q)
and the baryon distribution
f_{B}(k)
as (we denote:
\int_{k}\equiv\int\frac{d^{d}\boldsymbol{k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}
with
d=3
)
[
45
,
46
]
.

\hskip-8.5359ptf_{q}(q)=\int_{k}\varphi\big(\boldsymbol{q}-\tfrac{\boldsymbol{k}}{N_{c}}\big)f_{B}(k)\,.

(1)

Here
\varphi
represents a single quark momentum distribution in a single baryon state.
As quarks are confined in a spatial domain of the baryon size
\sim\Lambda_{\mathrm{QCD}}^{-1}
,
\varphi(q)
is spread to momenta of
\sim\Lambda_{\mathrm{QCD}}
.
Adding the quark contributions from each baryon leads to quark distributions in dense matter.
Here, for symmetric nuclear matter, we include a spin-isospin degeneracy factor
g=4
.
For pure neutron matter, the factor is
g=2
.
The extension for multi-flavors and multi-baryon species will be discussed in the next section.

The normalization is
\int_{q}\varphi(q)=1
.
The dual expression of the baryon number that follows from Eq. (
1
) is

n_{B}=g\int_{k}f_{B}(k)=g\int_{q}f_{q}(q)\,.

(2)

Here
f_{q}
is defined for a fixed color,
f_{q}\equiv f_{q}^{R}=f_{q}^{G}=f_{q}^{B}
with which
n_{B}=n_{q}^{R}=n_{q}^{G}=n_{q}^{B}
.

For a Hamiltonian or energy density, we neglect all interactions other than that tacitly included to confine quarks into a baryon.
The energy densities are simply

\varepsilon_{B}[f_{B}]=g\int_{k}E_{B}(k)f_{B}(k)\,.

(3)

The energy of a baryon is
E_{B}(k)=\sqrt{M_{B}^{2}+k^{2}}
.
If we assume a simple additive quark model, one can write the baryon energy as

E_{B}(k)=N_{c}\int_{q}E_{q}(q)\varphi\big(\boldsymbol{q}-\boldsymbol{k}/N_{c}\big)\,,

(4)

where
E_{q}
is defined through this relation.
The following relation readily follows

\varepsilon_{B}[f_{B}]=g\int_{q}E_{q}(q)[N_{c}f_{q}(q)]=\varepsilon_{q}[f_{q}]\,.

(5)

This is the duality for the energy density.
The expression with
f_{q}
is more useful when we examine EoS from the quark matter viewpoint.

In this work, we keep using the same
\varphi
from low and high densities.
In reality,
\varphi
can be modified by quark exchanges as well as structural changes in baryons.
All these effects will be considered elsewhere.

In order to determine the optimized distributions for baryons and quarks, we minimize the energy density functional with a given
n_{B}
by varying
f_{B}
(or
f_{q}
) at each momentum.
It is tempting to introduce the Lagrange multiplier to impose the fixed
n_{B}
constraint, but the current problem with infinitely many variables (
f_{B}
at each
k
) does not satisfy the conditions requested for the Lagrange multiplier method which demands the existence of the multiplier
\lambda
such that
\delta\varepsilon/\delta f_{B}(\boldsymbol{k})=\lambda\delta n_{B}/\delta f_{B}(\boldsymbol{k})
for all
\boldsymbol{k}
.
Hence we use the canonical method in which we manifestly fix
n_{B}
. The constraint is

0=\int_{k}\delta f_{B}(k)\,.

(6)

A variation which satisfies the constraint is

\delta f_{B}(\boldsymbol{k}_{1})+\delta f_{B}(\boldsymbol{k}_{2})=0\,,

(7)

which simply means we move a particle from
\boldsymbol{k}_{2}
to
\boldsymbol{k}_{1}
.
The change of energy associated with such change is

\displaystyle g^{-1}\delta\varepsilon_{B}

\displaystyle=E_{B}(\boldsymbol{k}_{1})\delta f_{B}(\boldsymbol{k}_{1})+E_{B}(\boldsymbol{k}_{2})\delta f_{B}(\boldsymbol{k}_{2})

\displaystyle=\big[E_{B}(\boldsymbol{k}_{1})-E_{B}(\boldsymbol{k}_{2})\big]\delta f_{B}(\boldsymbol{k}_{1})\,.

(8)

If
E_{B}(\boldsymbol{k}_{1})<E_{B}(\boldsymbol{k}_{2})
, the energy is reduced by increasing
f_{B}(\boldsymbol{k}_{1})
and reducing
f_{B}(\boldsymbol{k}_{2})
at the same amount.
In particular, if we set
|\boldsymbol{k}_{2}|
to
k_{\rm sh}
which is the largest possible momentum for nonzero
f_{B}
, then a positive
\delta f_{B}(\boldsymbol{k})
with
|\boldsymbol{k}|\leq k_{\rm sh}
always reduces the energy.
This describes a process in which a particle with the momentum
\boldsymbol{k}_{\rm sh}
decays into a particle with
\boldsymbol{k}
.

With
|\boldsymbol{k}_{2}|=k_{\rm sh}
, the number-conserving variation of the energy is defined to be

\displaystyle g^{-1}\frac{\,\delta^{\prime}\varepsilon_{B}\,}{\,\delta^{\prime}f_{B}(\boldsymbol{k})\,}

\displaystyle\equiv g^{-1}\bigg(\frac{\,\delta\varepsilon_{B}\,}{\,\delta f_{B}(\boldsymbol{k})\,}-\frac{\,\delta\varepsilon_{B}\,}{\,\delta f_{B}(\boldsymbol{k}_{\rm sh})\,}\bigg)

\displaystyle=E_{B}(k)-E_{B}(k_{\rm sh})\,.

(9)

The slope is positive for
k\geq k_{\rm sh}
, so
f_{B}(k)=0
is favored. For
k\leq k_{\rm sh}
, larger values of
f_{B}(k)
are favored, but whether
f_{B}(k)
reaches 1 (the maximum) or not depends on the quark Pauli blocking constraint. But expressing the constraint in terms of
f_{B}
is a nontrivial problem and this is the major difficulty to solve the minimization problem with the constraint.
In the IdylliQ model, we choose a specific form of
\varphi
that allows us to invert the sum rule and express
f_{B}
in terms of
f_{q}
.
Then the minimization problem with the constraint can be solved explicitly.

II.2
A solvable model and its solution for the phase-space distributions

In the IdylliQ model, we choose the following distribution for
\varphi
,

\varphi(\boldsymbol{q})=\frac{2\pi^{2}}{\Lambda^{3}}\frac{e^{-q/\Lambda}}{q/\Lambda}\,,~~~~~~\hat{L}[\varphi(\boldsymbol{q})]=\frac{\,(2\pi)^{2}\,}{\Lambda^{2}}\delta(\boldsymbol{q})\,

(10)

where
\hat{L}=-\nabla_{q}^{2}+\frac{1}{\Lambda^{2}}

with
\Lambda
being some scale parameter characterizing the size of baryons.
Applying this operator to Eq. (
1
), we find a local relation between
f_{B}
and
f_{q}
(
d=3)
,

f_{B}(N_{c}q)=\frac{\Lambda^{2}}{N_{c}^{d}}\,\hat{L}\big[f_{q}(q)\big]\,.

(11)

The candidates of local solutions for
f_{q}
are the boundary values
f_{q}=1
and those dual to
f_{B}=0
and
1
.
The
f_{q}
dual to
f_{B}=0
and
1
are determined as follows.
The most general solution for
f_{B}=0
is

\displaystyle f_{q}^{f_{B}=0}(q)=c_{+}y_{+}(q)+c_{-}y_{-}(q)\,,

(12)

where
y_{\pm}(q)=e^{\pm q/\Lambda}/q
satisfying
\hat{L}[y_{\pm}]=0
.
Meanwhile
f_{B}(N_{c}q)=1
can be obtained as

\displaystyle f_{q}^{f_{B}=1}(q)=N_{c}^{d}+d_{+}y_{+}(q)+d_{-}y_{-}(q)\,.

(13)

The range of constants
c_{\pm}
and
d_{\pm}
are constrained by the condition
0\leq f_{q}(q)\leq 1
.

We now put the candidates of solutions together.
Before quark states are saturated, however, we do not have to consider the quark Pauli blocking constraint and one can readily determine the baryon distribution as

f_{B}(k)=\Theta(k_{\rm sh}-k)\,,

(14)

and we can substitute it into the sum rule.
The solution for
f_{q}(q)
takes the form

f_{q}(q)=f_{q}^{f_{B}=1}(q)\Theta(q_{\rm sh}-q)+f_{q}^{f_{B}=0}(q)\Theta(q-q_{\rm sh})\,,

(15)

with coefficients
c_{\pm}
and
d_{\pm}
determined from the sum rule.
We will relate
k_{\rm sh}
and
q_{\rm sh}
shortly, and it will turn out
k_{\rm sh}=N_{c}q_{\rm sh}
.
The expression is valid for sufficiently small
k_{\rm sh}
with which
f_{q}(0)\leq 1
. Beyond some value of
k_{\rm sh}
, the extrapolation of Eq. (
15
) for large
k_{\rm sh}
would violate the condition
f_{q}(0)\leq 1
.
The saturation at
q=0
first occurs at
k_{\rm F}\approx\sqrt{2/N_{c}}\Lambda
or
n_{B}/n_{\mathrm{sat}}=2.58\times\big(\Lambda/0.4\,\text{GeV}\big)^{3}
for
N_{c}=3
.
Note that this implies the saturation density is parametrically small compared to the QCD scale
\Lambda^{3}
.

When quark states are filled at low momenta the solution should take the form

\displaystyle f_{q}(q)=\Theta(q_{\rm bu}-q)

\displaystyle+f_{q}^{f_{B}=1}(q)\Theta(q_{\rm sh}-q)\Theta(q-q_{\rm bu})

\displaystyle+f_{q}^{f_{B}=0}(q)\Theta(q-q_{\rm sh})\,.

(16)

For
q\leq q_{\rm bu}
the quark states are saturated.
Beyond
q\geq q_{\rm bu}
the quark distribution is dual to the saturated baryon distribution; here the baryons become free from the Pauli blocking constraint and hence
f_{B}
can take the maximal value 1.
For
q\geq q_{\rm sh}
, the quark distribution is dual to the baryon distribution where
f_{B}
is zero.
Applying
\hat{L}
, we find

\displaystyle\frac{\Lambda^{2}}{N_{c}^{d}}\,\hat{L}\big[f_{q}(q)\big]=\frac{1}{\,N_{c}^{d}\,}\Theta(q_{\rm bu}-q)

\displaystyle+\Theta(q_{\rm sh}-q)\Theta(q-q_{\rm bu})

\displaystyle+\frac{\Lambda^{2}}{N_{c}^{d}}R\,,

(17)

where
R
comes from the application of derivatives on step functions,

\displaystyle R=

\displaystyle\big[1-f_{q}^{f_{B}=1}(q_{\rm bu})\big]\delta^{\prime}(q_{\rm bu}-q)

\displaystyle+\big[f_{q}^{f_{B}=1}(q_{\rm sh})-f_{q}^{f_{B}=0}(q_{\rm sh})\big]\delta^{\prime}(q_{\rm sh}-q)

\displaystyle-\nabla f_{q}^{f_{B}=1}(q_{\rm bu})\delta(q-q_{\rm bu})

\displaystyle+\nabla\big[f_{q}^{f_{B}=1}(q_{\rm sh})-f_{q}^{f_{B}=0}(q_{\rm sh})\big]\delta(q-q_{\rm sh})\,.

(18)

The function
R
would contain the
\delta
- and
\delta^{\prime}
-functions which violate the condition
0\leq f_{B}\leq 1
. This problem can be avoided by demanding
f_{q}
and
df_{q}/dq
to be continuous at each junction point. The resulting
R
vanishes and we get (
k=N_{c}q,k_{\rm bu}=N_{c}q_{\rm bu},k_{\rm sh}=N_{c}q_{\rm sh}
)

f_{B}(k)=\frac{1}{\,N_{c}^{d}\,}\Theta(k_{\rm bu}-k)+\Theta(k_{\rm sh}-k)\Theta(k-k_{\rm bu})\,.

(19)

The rest is the determination of
k_{\rm bu}
that follows from the continuity conditions of
f_{q}
. This in turn requires the determination of
c_{\pm}
and
d_{\pm}
. One can set
c_{+}=0
to satisfy
f_{q}\rightarrow 0
for

.
So four conditions are sufficient to determine
k_{\rm bu}
,
c_{-}
, and
d_{\pm}
.

With four conditions from two junction points, one can express
c_{-}
,
d_{\pm}
,
and
\Delta_{q}=q_{\rm sh}-q_{\rm bu}
as functions of
q_{\rm sh}
.
The conditions are

\displaystyle\begin{split}1&=f_{q}^{f_{B}=1}(q_{\rm bu})\,,\\
0&=\frac{df_{q}^{f_{B}=1}(q_{\rm bu})}{dq}\,,\\
f_{q}^{f_{B}=1}(q_{\rm sh})&=f_{q}^{f_{B}=0}(q_{\rm sh})\,,\\
\frac{df_{q}^{f_{B}=1}(q_{\rm sh})}{dq}&=\frac{df_{q}^{f_{B}=0}(q_{\rm sh})}{dq}\,.\end{split}

(20)

the four coefficients are entirely determined by these four matching equations.

N_{c}^{3}=\frac{\mathcal{X}_{N_{c}^{3}}}{\mathcal{Y}}\,,\qquad d_{\pm}=\frac{\mathcal{X}_{d_{\pm}}}{\mathcal{Y}}\,,\qquad c_{-}=\frac{\mathcal{X}_{c_{-}}}{\mathcal{Y}}\,,

(21)

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{N_{c}^{3}}

\displaystyle=-[y_{+}^{\prime}(q_{\rm sh})y_{-}(q_{\rm sh})-y_{+}(q_{\rm sh})y_{-}^{\prime}(q_{\rm sh})]y_{-}^{\prime}(q_{\rm bu})\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{d_{+}}

\displaystyle=-y_{-}^{\prime}(q_{\rm bu})y_{-}^{\prime}(q_{\rm sh})\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{d_{-}}

\displaystyle=y_{+}^{\prime}(q_{\rm bu})y_{-}^{\prime}(q_{\rm sh})\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{c_{-}}

\displaystyle=y_{+}^{\prime}(q_{\rm bu})y_{-}^{\prime}(q_{\rm sh})-y_{-}^{\prime}(q_{\rm bu})y_{+}^{\prime}(q_{\rm sh})\,,

(22)

and the common denominator is

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}

\displaystyle=[y_{+}^{\prime}(q_{\rm bu})y_{-}(q_{\rm bu})-y_{+}(q_{\rm bu})y_{-}^{\prime}(q_{\rm bu})]y_{-}^{\prime}(q_{\rm sh})

\displaystyle-[y_{+}^{\prime}(q_{\rm sh})y_{-}(q_{\rm sh})-y_{+}(q_{\rm sh})y_{-}^{\prime}(q_{\rm sh})]y_{-}^{\prime}(q_{\rm bu})\,.

(23)

Here we display only the equation to determine

, which is obtained from the relation

;

\frac{\Lambda+q_{\rm bu}}{\Lambda+q_{\rm bu}-(\Lambda+q_{\rm sh})e^{-\Delta_{q}/\Lambda}}=N_{c}^{3}\,.

(24)

Using

the baryon distribution in (

\displaystyle n_{B}

\displaystyle=4\int_{k_{\rm sh}-\Delta}^{k_{\rm sh}}\frac{d^{3}\boldsymbol{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}+\frac{4}{N_{c}^{3}}\int_{0}^{k_{\rm sh}-\Delta}\frac{d^{3}\boldsymbol{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,,

(25)

\displaystyle\varepsilon

\displaystyle=4\int_{k_{\rm sh}-\Delta}^{k_{\rm sh}}\frac{d^{3}\boldsymbol{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\sqrt{k^{2}+M_{N}}

\displaystyle~~~+\frac{4}{N_{c}^{3}}\int_{0}^{k_{\rm sh}-\Delta}\frac{d^{3}\boldsymbol{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\sqrt{k^{2}+M_{N}}\,.

(26)

This is the flavor symmetric, two-flavor EoS in the IdylliQ model.

The EoS in the IdylliQ model is similar (but not quite identical) to the one proposed in McLerran-Reddy (MR) model
[
3
]
.
In the second integral in
\varepsilon
, we make the change of the variable
\boldsymbol{k}\to\boldsymbol{q}=\boldsymbol{k}/N_{c}

and assume the relation
M_{N}\rightarrow N_{c}M_{q}
, finding the form in Ref.
[
3
]
,

\displaystyle n_{B}^{\rm MR}

\displaystyle=\frac{3}{2\pi^{2}}\left[k_{\rm sh}^{3}-(k_{\rm sh}-\Delta)^{3}+\frac{(k_{\rm sh}-\Delta)^{3}}{N_{c}^{3}}\right]\,,

(27)

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\rm MR}

\displaystyle=4\int_{k_{\rm sh}-\Delta}^{k_{\rm sh}}\frac{d^{3}\boldsymbol{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\sqrt{k^{2}+M_{N}^{2}}

\displaystyle~~~+4N_{c}\int_{0}^{\frac{k_{\rm sh}-\Delta}{N_{c}}}\frac{d^{3}\boldsymbol{q}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\sqrt{q^{2}+M_{q}^{2}}\,.

(28)

The only difference in EoS between the IdylliQ and MR models
lies in the explicit form of the quark kinetic energy.
The two EoS become completely identical if one replaces

\sqrt{q^{2}+M_{q}^{2}}
with
E_{q}^{\rm IdylliQ}(\boldsymbol{q})

defined
through the relation

\sqrt{k^{2}+M_{N}^{2}}=N_{c}\int_{q}E_{q}^{\rm IdylliQ}(\boldsymbol{q})\varphi\big(\boldsymbol{q}-\tfrac{\boldsymbol{k}}{N_{c}}\big)\,.

(29)

Applying the operator
\hat{L}
acting on the variable
\boldsymbol{k}
,
one can explicitly derive the form of
E_{q}^{\rm IdylliQ}(q)
which reflects
that quarks are confined into a baryon.

II.3
Comparison with the previous model of Quarkyonic Matter

Comparison of the previous model of Quarkyonic matter and IdylliQ model.
In the previous modeling,
there is a discrepancy between the Fermi momenta of
u
and
d
quarks, so the
u
-quark distribution has a mismatch with the nucleon distribution. Meanwhile, in the IdylliQ picture, this mismatch is naturally resolved by considering the half-occupied distribution for the
u
quark that is dual to the nucleonic description.

The extension of the above-mentioned flavor symmetric model
[
3
]
to the flavor asymmetric one is not straightforward.
The previous attempts extended the form (
28
)
by generalizing the flavor symmetric quark part into the flavor asymmetric ones.
But without including the physics that quarks are bound to baryons,
such extension leaves a mismatch between baryon and quark momentum distributions.
For instance, in pure neutron matter,
typical attempts to fill
u
- and
d
-quark states with the probabilities
1

leads to the
u
- and
d
-quark Fermi sea at different size,
see the leftmost panel in Fig.
1
.
Then, picking
u
- and
d
-quarks near the Fermi surfaces result in a baryon having
u
- and
d
-quark momentum distributions
considerably deformed from those in ordinary baryons.
In domains not very far from nuclear matter densities, however,
we expect that highly deformed baryons are energetically disfavored.
In this work, we instead assume that confining forces remain strong enough to
maintain the overall structure of baryons,
and as a result the quarks have more general momentum distribution than in a simple Fermi sea,
as shown in the rightmost panels in Fig.

.

III
IdylliQ model with multiple flavors

In this section, we extend the IdylliQ model reviewed in the previous section to the case with multiple flavors.
The matter composition can be determined by minimizing the energy density for a fixed baryon density.
Equivalently, one can determine the composition by satisfying the chemical equilibrium condition.

III.1
Simplified setup

Here we consider adding the hyperons
\Lambda^{0}
and
\Sigma^{0}
on top of pure neutron matter.
As already mentioned, we assume the mass of
\Lambda^{0}
and
\Sigma^{0}
are degenerate, i.e.,
M_{Y}\equiv M_{\Lambda^{0}}=M_{\Sigma^{0}}
, and denote the degeneracy factor as
d_{Y}=2
.
Throughout this paper, we use the value
M_{N}\approx 0.94\,\text{GeV}
and
M_{Y}\approx 1.12\,\text{GeV}
.
In this simple setup, we have only two types of variables,
f_{N}(k)
and
f_{Y}(k)\equiv f_{\Lambda^{0}}(k)=f_{\Sigma^{0}}(k)
.

The energy density described in terms of the momentum distribution of hadrons
f_{i}(k)

(i=n,Y)
is

\displaystyle\varepsilon=g\int_{\boldsymbol{k}}\left[E_{N}(k)f_{n}(k)+d_{Y}E_{Y}(k)f_{Y}(k)\right]\,,

(30)

A factor
g=2
in front of the integral is the degeneracy of spins, and we use the ideal dispersion relation
E_{i}(k)=\sqrt{k^{2}+M_{i}}
as before.
The number density of neutrons and hyperons are

\displaystyle n_{n}=g\int_{\boldsymbol{k}}f_{n}(k)\,,\quad n_{Y}=g\int_{\boldsymbol{k}}d_{Y}f_{Y}(k)\,.

(31)

The charge neutrality
n_{Q}=0
is fulfilled trivially as we only consider the charge-neutral baryons.

Now, consider the momentum distribution of quarks:
f_{q}(q)

(q=u,d,s)
.
The confinement relation that sets the duality transformation between
f_{B}(k)
and
f_{q}(q)
is

\displaystyle f_{q}(q)=\sum_{i=n,\Sigma^{0},\Lambda^{0}}\int_{\boldsymbol{k}}\varphi\left(\boldsymbol{q}-\frac{\boldsymbol{k}}{N_{c}}\right)B_{q}^{i}f_{i}(k)\,,

(32)

where
B_{q}^{i}
is the baryon number carried by a quark of flavor

q
inside the baryon species
i
.
Explicitly,

\displaystyle\hskip-5.69046ptf_{d}(q)

\displaystyle=\int_{\boldsymbol{k}}\varphi\left(\boldsymbol{q}-\frac{\boldsymbol{k}}{N_{c}}\right)\left[B_{d}^{n}f_{n}(k)+d_{Y}B_{d}^{Y}f_{Y}(k)\right]\,,

(33)

\displaystyle\hskip-5.69046ptf_{u}(q)

\displaystyle=\int_{\boldsymbol{k}}\varphi\left(\boldsymbol{q}-\frac{\boldsymbol{k}}{N_{c}}\right)\left[B_{u}^{n}f_{n}(k)+d_{Y}B_{u}^{Y}f_{Y}(k)\right]\,,

(34)

\displaystyle\hskip-5.69046ptf_{s}(q)

\displaystyle=\int_{\boldsymbol{k}}\varphi\left(\boldsymbol{q}-\frac{\boldsymbol{k}}{N_{c}}\right)\left[d_{Y}B_{s}^{Y}f_{Y}(k)\right]\,,

(35)

where
B_{d}^{n}=2/3
and
B_{d}^{Y}=1/3
are the baryon number carried by constituent
d
quarks in neutrons and the average baryon number carried by constituent
d
quarks in hyperons.
Also,
B_{u}^{n}=1/3
and
B_{u}^{Y}=1/3
for
u
quarks in baryons, and
B_{s}^{Y}=1/3
for
s
quarks in baryons.

We consider the case in which the
d
quark state saturates.
The low-
k
regions of
f_{n}(k)
and
f_{Y}(k)
are subject to the saturation of
d
-quark states
f_{d}(q)
at
q\leq q_{\rm bu}
.
Applying
\hat{L}
to Eq. (
33
) with
f_{d}=1
,
we find the constraint

\displaystyle B_{d}^{n}f_{n}^{\rm bulk}(k)+d_{Y}B_{d}^{Y}f_{Y}^{\rm bulk}(k)=\frac{1}{N_{c}^{3}}\,,

(36)

at
k\leq k_{\rm bu}=N_{c}q_{\rm bu}
.
With the constraint, we optimize the
f_{n}
and
f_{Y}
to minimize the energy.

We consider the energy minimization with
n_{B}
fixed. As before we discuss how the particles with the maximal energy transform into particles with less energy.
We assume that the shell part is saturated by neutrons while the bulk may be a mixture of neutrons and hyperons

\displaystyle f_{n}(k)

\displaystyle=f_{n}^{\rm bulk}(k)\Theta(k_{\rm bu}-k)+\Theta(k-k_{\rm bu})\Theta(k_{\rm sh}-k)\,,

(37)

\displaystyle f_{Y}(k)

\displaystyle=f_{Y}^{\rm bulk}(k)\Theta(k_{\rm bu}-k)\,,

(38)

where
f_{n}^{\rm bulk}(k)
and
f_{Y}^{\rm bulk}(k)
satisfy the
d
-quark saturation condition Eq. (
36
).
To optimize the composition in the bulk, we consider the conversion
n(k)\rightarrow Y(k)
at
k<k_{\rm bu}
.
Here we have introduced the notation
B(k)
which represents a baryon
B
with the momentum
k
.

III.2
Determination of the bulk composition

As done in Sec.
II
for isospin symmetric matter, we vary
f_{n}
and
f_{Y}
in the bulk to minimize the energy functional for a fixed
n_{B}
.
Unlike the previous case where the application of
\hat{L}
to
f_{q}
directly determines
f_{N}
,
here we further must determine the composition through the energy consideration.

The saturation condition imposes important constraints on the particle conversion processes.
Even when
\mu_{n}
exceeds
M_{Y}
, the weak decay
n(k_{\rm sh})\rightarrow Y(k=0)
does not necessarily occur
if the
d
-quark states are saturated.
An extra process to open
d
-states in the bulk is needed for such decays, demanding an extra energy cost.

When one neutron converts into one hyperon,
n(k)\rightarrow Y(k)
, this opens the phase space for another hyperon to come to fill the state
Y(k)
,
since neutrons contain two
d
-quarks while each of
\Sigma^{0},\Lambda^{0}
contains only single
d
-quark.
This extra hyperon can be brought by the conversion of neutrons in the shell into hyperons in the bulk,
n(k_{\rm sh})\rightarrow Y(k)
,
and this decay reduces the energy of the system.
Through these sequences of the processes, not only the bulk but also the shell structure in momentum space are reorganized.

One can examine the energy gain and cost for each process while keeping
n_{B}
fixed.
This can be manifestly done by varying
f_{n}
and
f_{Y}
as in Eq. (
7
).
The calculations are rather lengthy and we postpone the step-by-step calculations to the Appendix
C
.
Below, we show quicker calculations by focusing on the chemical equilibrium conditions.

The key observation to skip complications of number-conserving energy variation is that, when we reach the ground state, the conversion processes reach the equilibrium; knowing this fact allows us to impose the
\beta
-equilibrium condition from the very beginning for an efficient search for the ground state.
We prepare several candidates of the solution (parametrization of
f_{n}
and
f_{Y}
),
demand the
\beta
-equilibrium condition to fix the parameters in each candidate, and then single out the minimum solution at the same
n_{B}
.

For simplicity, we consider a sufficiently low density close to the hyperon thresholds.
We can safely assume that those hyperons appear at low momenta, with the occupation probability being constant as we justify shortly.
Therefore, we take the following ansatz for the solution:

\displaystyle f_{n}(k)

\displaystyle=\frac{h_{n}}{N_{c}^{3}}\Theta(k_{Y}-k)\Theta(k)

\displaystyle~~~+\frac{1}{B_{d}^{n}N_{c}^{3}}\Theta(k-k_{Y})\Theta(k_{\rm bu}-k)

\displaystyle~~~+\Theta(k-k_{\rm bu})\Theta(k_{\rm sh}-k)\,,

(39)

\displaystyle f_{Y}(k)

\displaystyle=\frac{h_{Y}}{N_{c}^{3}}\Theta(k_{Y}-k)\Theta(k)\,.

(40)

From Eq. (
36
), the parameters
h_{n}
and
h_{Y}
satisfies

B_{d}^{n}h_{n}+d_{Y}B_{d}^{Y}h_{Y}=1\,.

(41)

This implies that
0\leq h_{n}\leq 1/B_{d}^{n}
and
0\leq h_{Y}\leq 1/(d_{Y}B_{d}^{Y})
.
Using these distributions and eliminating
h_{n}
by using Eq. (
41
) in the above equations, the thermodynamic quantities are

\displaystyle\frac{\,n_{n}\,}{g}

\displaystyle=-d_{Y}\frac{h_{Y}}{N_{c}^{3}}\frac{B_{d}^{Y}}{B_{d}^{n}}\int_{0}^{k_{Y}}\!\!\!\!\!\!dk\,D(k)

\displaystyle~~~-\left(1-\frac{1}{B_{d}^{n}N_{c}^{3}}\right)\int_{0}^{k_{\rm bu}}\!\!\!\!\!\!dk\,D(k)+\int_{0}^{k_{\rm sh}}\!\!\!\!\!\!dk\,D(k)\,,

(42)

\displaystyle\frac{\,n_{Y}\,}{g}

\displaystyle=d_{Y}\frac{h_{Y}}{N_{c}^{3}}\int_{0}^{k_{Y}}\!\!\!\!\!\!dk\,D(k)\,,

(43)

\displaystyle\frac{\,\varepsilon\,}{g}

\displaystyle=d_{Y}\frac{h_{Y}}{N_{c}^{3}}\int_{0}^{k_{Y}}\!\!\!\!\!\!dk\,D(k)\left[E_{Y}(k)-\frac{B_{d}^{Y}}{B_{d}^{n}}E_{N}(k)\right]

\displaystyle~~~-\left(1-\frac{1}{B_{d}^{n}N_{c}^{3}}\right)\int_{0}^{k_{\rm bu}}\!\!\!\!\!\!dk\,D(k)E_{N}(k)

\displaystyle~~~+\int_{0}^{k_{\rm sh}}\!\!\!\!\!\!dk\,D(k)E_{N}(k)\,,

(44)

where the density of states is given by
D(k)=k^{2}/(2\pi^{2})
.

Now we have four parameters,
h_{Y},k_{Y},k_{\rm bu}
, and
k_{\rm sh}
to be determined.
As we mentioned, we impose the
\beta
-equilibrium condition.
The (candidates of) chemical potentials
\mu_{n}
and
\mu_{Y}
are given by

\displaystyle\mu_{n}=\left(\frac{\partial\varepsilon}{\partial n_{n}}\right)_{n_{Y}}\,,\quad\mu_{Y}=\left(\frac{\partial\varepsilon}{\partial n_{Y}}\right)_{n_{n}}\,.

(45)

The chemical potential for neutrons
\mu_{n}
and
\mu_{Y}
can be calculated by using the method of the Jacobian.
The details of calculations are given in Appendix
A
.
The neutron chemical potential is found to be

\displaystyle\mu_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{-DE_{N}\big|_{k_{\rm bu}}\left(1-\frac{1}{B_{d}^{n}N_{c}^{3}}\right)\frac{\partial k_{\rm bu}}{\partial k_{\rm sh}}+DE_{N}\big|_{k_{\rm sh}}}{-D(k_{\rm bu})\left(1-\frac{1}{B_{d}^{n}N_{c}^{3}}\right)\frac{\partial k_{\rm bu}}{\partial k_{\rm sh}}+D(k_{\rm sh})}\,,

(46)

which is independent of hyperon parameters
k_{Y}
and
h_{Y}
; it is essentially a function of
k_{\rm sh}
as
k_{\rm bu}
is fixed through the condition
f_{n}(k)=1
for
k_{\rm bu}\leq k\leq k_{\rm sh}
, in the similar way as Eq. (
20
).
Meanwhile, the hyperon chemical potential is

\displaystyle\mu_{Y}

\displaystyle=E_{Y}(k_{Y})-\frac{B_{d}^{Y}}{B_{d}^{n}}E_{N}(k_{Y})+\frac{B_{d}^{Y}}{B_{d}^{n}}\mu_{n}

\displaystyle=E_{Y}(k_{Y})-\frac{1}{2}E_{N}(k_{Y})+\frac{1}{2}\mu_{n}\,,

(47)

where in the last line, we used
B_{d}^{Y}=1/3
and
B_{d}^{n}=2/3
.

Recalling that
\mu_{n}
is a function of
k_{\rm sh}
, the chemical equilibrium condition
\mu_{n}=\mu_{Y}=\mu_{B}
turns into the relation between
k_{\rm sh}
and
k_{Y}
for a given
h_{Y}
,
which reads

\mu_{B}(k_{\rm sh})=2E_{Y}(k_{Y})-E_{N}(k_{Y})\,.

(48)

This is the necessary condition for the ground state. As we have derived the condition at a given
h_{Y}
, the parameters
k_{\rm sh}
and
k_{Y}
should be regarded as functions of
h_{Y}
.

Finally, we determine
h_{Y}
by choosing the lowest energy solution out of candidates satisfying Eq. (
48
).
The variation of
h_{n}
while holding
n_{B}
fixed yields

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial\varepsilon}{\partial h_{Y}}\right)_{n_{B}}\!\!=\frac{\,gd_{Y}\,}{N_{c}^{3}}\!\int_{0}^{k_{Y}}\!\!\!\!\!dkD(k)\left[E_{Y}(k)-\frac{\,E_{N}(k)+\mu_{B}\,}{2}\right]\,.

(49)

We note that the integrand is a monotonically increasing function of
k
,

\frac{\partial}{\partial k}\big[E_{Y}(k)-\frac{1}{2}E_{N}(k)\big]=\bigg(\frac{1}{E_{Y}(k)}-\frac{1}{2E_{N}(k)}\bigg)k>0\,,

(50)

provided that
E_{Y}(k)<2E_{N}(k)
. This condition holds for
M_{Y}<2M_{N}
, valid for realistic baryon masses.
Now we found that the integral is always negative for
k<k_{Y}
and vanishes at
k=k_{Y}
, see Eq. (
48
).
Therefore
(\partial\varepsilon/\partial h_{Y})_{n_{B}}<0
, favoring a larger
h_{Y}
.
Consequently,
h_{Y}
takes the largest value possible; the domain of
k<k_{Y}
is fully saturated by hyperons.

Now our solution takes the form

\displaystyle f_{n}(k)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{B_{d}^{n}N_{c}^{3}}\Theta(k-k_{Y})\Theta(k_{\rm bu}-k)\

\displaystyle~~~+\Theta(k-k_{\rm bu})\Theta(k_{\rm sh}-k)\,,

(51)

\displaystyle f_{Y}(k)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d_{Y}B_{d}^{Y}N_{c}^{3}}\Theta(k_{Y}-k)\,.

(52)

with Eq. (
48
) for
h_{Y}=1/(d_{Y}n_{d}^{Y})
,

\mu_{B}=2E_{Y}(k_{Y})-E_{N}(k_{Y})\,.

(53)

The solution is valid for sufficiently large
n_{B}
.
We recall that the RHS of Eq. (
53
) is an increasing function of
k_{Y}
so that
\mu_{B}
at the onset of hyperons is

\mu_{B}^{\rm onset}=2M_{Y}-M_{N}\,,

(54)

below which it is not possible to satisfy the chemical equilibrium; neutrons completely dominate the system.
A snapshot of the phase space distributions for baryons and quarks is shown in Fig.
2
.

III.3
Equation of state

In Fig.

, we show the EoS (
42
)-(

).
The explicit form of the EoS is given in Appendix

.
The onset of hyperons is shown with dash-dotted lines in each figure.

There are two remarkable consequences of the quark saturation:

First, hyperons emerge in the system at density
\sim(5
–
6)n_{\rm sat}
, significantly higher than the usual estimate, (
2
–
3)n_{\rm sat}
.
We note that the typical core density of two-solar mass neutron stars is about
\sim 5n_{\mathrm{sat}}
; if our estimate on the hyperon onset is valid, the high mass is established before hyperons soften the EoS.
Second, even after the appearance of hyperons, the softening is mild as the contribution of the hyperons is suppressed by a factor
1/N_{c}^{3}
.
Since hyperons cannot largely occupy the phase space at low energy, increasing the hyperon density makes hyperons energetic more quickly than theories without the saturation;
the relativistic regime for hyperons is reached at lower density.

We emphasize that all these dramatic effects arise solely from the statistical considerations; no detailed discussions of interactions are used.
We expect that the baryon-baryon interactions, which seem to be repulsive in most flavor channels, further stiffen the EoS.

Softening may be more significant when we add
\Xi^{0}
, which is a heavier baryon not subject to
d
-quark saturation condition, and lies slightly above the threshold of hyperons with
S=-1
.
This issue will be explained in the next section.

Including heavier baryons: the case of

Here, we discuss that

\Delta^{0}
from the relation
\mu_{\Delta^{0}}=(\partial\varepsilon/\partial n_{\Delta^{0}})_{n_{n},n_{Y}}
:

\displaystyle\mu_{\Delta^{0}}

\displaystyle=E_{\Delta^{0}}(k_{\Delta^{0}})-\frac{B_{d}^{\Delta^{0}}}{B_{d}^{Y}}E_{Y}(k_{\Delta^{0}})+\frac{B_{d}^{\Delta^{0}}}{B_{d}^{Y}}\mu_{Y}\,,

h_{\Delta^{0}}
.

\beta
-equilibrium condition regardless of the value of
h_{\Delta^{0}}
.
In the
\beta
-equilibrium, one finds
\mu_{Y}=\mu_{B}
and
\mu_{\Delta^{0}}=\mu_{B}
.

The variation of the energy with respect to
h_{\Delta^{0}}
reads

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial\varepsilon}{\partial h_{\Delta^{0}}}\right)_{n_{B}}

\displaystyle=d_{\Delta^{0}}\frac{g}{N_{c}^{3}}\int_{0}^{k_{\Delta^{0}}}dkD(k)

\displaystyle~~~~~~\times\left[E_{\Delta^{0}}(k)-\frac{B_{d}^{\Delta^{0}}}{B_{d}^{Y}}E_{Y}(k)-\left(1-\frac{B_{d}^{\Delta^{0}}}{B_{d}^{Y}}\right)\mu_{B}\right]

\displaystyle=d_{\Delta^{0}}\frac{g}{N_{c}^{3}}\int_{0}^{k_{\Delta^{0}}}dkD(k)\left[E_{\Delta^{0}}(k)-2E_{Y}(k)+\mu_{B}\right]>0\,.

(62)

One can easily verify that the integrand is positive for
k<k_{\Delta^{0}}
. From this argument, one can say that
h_{\Delta^{0}}=0
is the minimum energy solution. This means that the appearance of the
\Delta^{0}
baryons is disfavored in this setup.

IV.3
Applicability of the solution

The aforementioned solution is valid when the following four conditions are fulfilled:
(i)
\Lambda\lesssim 740~\text{MeV}
.
(ii)
k_{Y}<k_{\rm bu}
.
(iii)
f_{u}(q)<1
and
f_{s}(q)<1
for any
q
.
(iv)
\mu_{B}\lesssim N_{c}^{3/2}\Lambda_{\rm QCD}
.

First, the ansatz for
f_{n}
and
f_{Y}
(
39
,
40
) is valid as long as the saturation of the
d
-quark distribution occurs at
\mu_{B}<M_{Y}
.
When
k_{\rm sh}^{Y}\equiv\sqrt{\mu_{B}^{2}-M_{Y}^{2}}
exceeds
k_{\rm bu}
, neutrons at
k_{\rm sh}
can decay into hyperons with the momentum
k_{\rm sh}^{Y}(\geq k_{\rm bu})
.
Removing a neutron at
k_{\rm sh}
reorganizes the shell structure and reduces the energy by
\mu_{B}(>E_{n}(k_{\rm sh}))
, and the baryon number conservation demands the system to accommodate another baryon.
Adding a hyperon in the shell domain costs at least the energy of
\sqrt{M_{Y}^{2}+k_{\rm bu}^{2}}
.
When the conversion of neutrons to hyperons within the shell is energetically preferred, the
S=-1
hyperons also develop the shell structure like in the neutron distribution.
Such a situation may be realized when
\Lambda
is large and the saturation of the quark distribution occurs at low
\mu_{B}
.
It occurs when
\Lambda\approx 740~\text{MeV}
or larger.
This is derived from the Fermi momentum at which the saturation of the
d
-quark distribution occurs
k_{\rm sat}\approx(2/N_{c})^{1/2}\Lambda
, and the condition that the
d
-quark distribution saturates below the hyperon onset
\mu_{\rm sat}=\sqrt{k_{\rm sat}^{2}+M_{N}^{2}}>M_{Y}
.

Secondly, when
k_{Y}
exceeds
k_{\rm bu}
, the solution (
51
,
52
) is no longer valid.
One has to also consider the shell structure in
S=-1
hyperon distribution
f_{Y}
as in the neutron distribution.
This applicable limit is plotted with the dotted lines in Figs.
3
and
5
.

Thirdly, when the
u
and
s
quark distributions,
f_{u}(q)
and
f_{s}(q)
, are saturated,
the
\Xi^{0}
hyperon will also have the low occupation at low momentum and the shell structure at high momentum.
Such a situation demands us to manifestly consider
u
- or
s
-quark saturation conditions.
We note that, once either
u
- or
s
-quark states are saturated together with
d
-quarks, no baryon octet can be free from the saturation conditions; all baryon octets are suppressed at low momenta.

Finally, when
\mu_{B}\gtrsim N_{c}^{3/2}\Lambda_{\rm QCD}
, the color screening suppresses the confining gluons so that Quarkyonic picture may no longer be valid.

IV.4
Toward a more realistic neutron-star equation of state

In this paper, we have modeled the neutron star matter with only the charge-neutral baryons, and there are two important effects we have neglected here: the lepton contribution and the interaction.

Regarding the first point, a neutron star in reality is composed not only of strongly interacting particles but of leptons as well.
The charge neutrality condition together with the
\beta
-equilibrium introduces a certain fraction of protons and leptons.
Since the chemical potential of leptons is finite in such a realistic environment, the chemical potential for the charged baryon can be modified.
Therefore, the threshold for the negatively charged baryons may be shifted to lower density by the charge chemical potential
\mu_{Q}
.
Nevertheless, the shift in the hyperon threshold in this paper holds regardless of whether there is a finite
\mu_{Q}
.
As long as the
d
-quark states are saturated, there is a systematic shift in the threshold, and such shift is of the order of
\sim 0.1~\text{GeV}
while the effect of
\mu_{Q}
may be of the order of
\sim 0.01~\text{GeV}
.
So, even if
\mu_{Q}
is nonzero, which can lower the charged hyperon threshold, the picture we have presented in this paper remains intact.

Regarding the second point, although we have taken into account some fraction of the interaction by taking into account the confinement effect, essentially the low-energy neutrons are treated as an ideal gas, at which the effect of the interaction is shown to be already important.
The interaction may be modeled simply by the excluded volume effect
[
12
,
47
]
as in the van-der-Waals EoS
[
48
,
49
]
.
Work in these directions is in progress.

V
Summary and conclusion

In this paper, we demonstrated the extension of ideal dual Quarkyonic (IdylliQ) model to the case of multiple flavors.
We pointed out that by considering the quark substructure of baryons, there may be an additional mechanism that may also work toward the direction of mitigating the hyperon puzzle.
This mechanism arises because, in neutron matter, one cannot insert a strangeness
S=-1
and charge neutral
Q=0
baryon without that baryon having a down quark in it, but in Quarkyonic Matter, the down quark Fermi sea is filled.
The first available baryon state with strangeness that is electrically neutral and has no down quark in it is the
\Xi^{0}
baryon composed of two strange quarks and an up quark.
To show this mechanism clearcut, we ignored small isospin splitting in the mass, e.g.,
M_{\Lambda^{0}}=M_{\Sigma^{0}}
.

The main results of this paper are two-fold:

1.

The threshold of the
S=-1
hyperons is shifted from
\mu_{B}=M_{Y}
to
2M_{Y}-M_{N}
, where
M_{Y}
and
M_{N}
are the mass of hyperons with strangeness
S=-1
and nucleons, respectively.
This can be understood as follows:
In order to keep the minimum energy state with the
d
-quark distribution saturated, one has to replace one neutron, which contains two
d
’s, with two hyperons, which contains one
d
.

2.

The EoS is softened mildly by
S=-1
hyperons, but not as strongly as would be the case if the quark substructure of baryons was not properly accounted for.
The bulk of the softening in the EoS comes from the
\Xi^{0}
hyperon.

Both these points make the hyperon contribution to the EoS feeble and thus works toward the resolution of the hyperon puzzle.
Hyperons at low momenta appear in the system with a low probability and thus affect the EoS only weakly.
In this regard, a large number of hyperon species is no longer crucial because hyperons that have a
d
-quark in them suppress one another to occupy the limited phase space for
d
-quarks.
Meanwhile, hyperons at high momenta are almost free from the pre-occupied phase space, but those hyperons at high momenta not only increase energy density but also the pressure significantly, and hence do not soften the EoS much.
Once the
\Xi^{0}
appears, there is a significant softening in the EoS.
Nevertheless, the shifted threshold is high enough so that it may not greatly affect the maximum mass of neutron stars.

We note that the interaction and realistic structures of baryons are missing in the aforementioned picture, therefore we do not claim that this is not yet the full solution of the hyperon puzzle.
The current mechanism can be studied in more realistic setup by integrating this into the current advanced phenomenological studies of hyperons.
We believe such directions worth further investigation, but they are left as future works.

Appendix A
Details of calculations using Jacobian

Here, we give details of calculations using Jacobian for derivatives that appear in the main text.

We first take up the computation of neutron chemical potential. We take the derivative of energy density holding
n_{Y}
fixed. It can be written

\mu_{n}=\bigg(\frac{\partial\varepsilon}{\partial n_{n}}\bigg)_{n_{Y}}=\frac{\partial(\varepsilon,n_{Y})}{\partial(n_{n},n_{Y})}=\frac{\partial(\varepsilon,n_{Y})}{\partial(k_{\rm sh},k_{Y})}\bigg/\frac{\partial(n_{n},n_{Y})}{\partial(k_{\rm sh},k_{Y})}\,.

(63)

Each determinant reads

\frac{\partial(\varepsilon,n_{Y})}{\partial(k_{\rm sh},k_{Y})}=\bigg(\frac{\partial\varepsilon}{\partial k_{\rm sh}}\bigg)_{k_{Y}}\bigg(\frac{\partial n_{Y}}{\partial k_{Y}}\bigg)_{k_{\rm sh}}-\bigg(\frac{\partial\varepsilon}{\partial k_{Y}}\bigg)_{k_{\rm sh}}\bigg(\frac{\partial n_{Y}}{\partial k_{\rm sh}}\bigg)_{k_{Y}}\,,

(64)

and

\frac{\partial(n_{n},n_{Y})}{\partial(k_{\rm sh},k_{Y})}=\bigg(\frac{\partial n_{n}}{\partial k_{\rm sh}}\bigg)_{k_{Y}}\bigg(\frac{\partial n_{Y}}{\partial k_{Y}}\bigg)_{k_{\rm sh}}-\bigg(\frac{\partial n_{n}}{\partial k_{Y}}\bigg)_{k_{\rm sh}}\bigg(\frac{\partial n_{Y}}{\partial k_{\rm sh}}\bigg)_{k_{Y}}\,.

(65)

Noting
(\partial n_{Y}/\partial k_{\rm sh})_{k_{Y}}=0
since holding
k_{Y}
and
h_{Y}
fixed does not change
n_{Y}
, we find

\displaystyle\mu_{n}=\left(\frac{\partial\varepsilon}{\partial k_{\rm sh}}\right)_{k_{Y}}\bigg/\left(\frac{\partial n_{n}}{\partial k_{\rm sh}}\right)_{k_{Y}}\,.

(66)

Explicitly,

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial\varepsilon}{\partial k_{\rm sh}}\right)_{k_{Y}}

\displaystyle=-D(k_{\rm bu})E_{N}(k_{\rm bu})\left(1-\frac{1}{B_{d}^{n}N_{c}^{3}}\right)\frac{\partial k_{\rm bu}}{\partial k_{\rm sh}}

\displaystyle~~~+D(k_{\rm sh})E_{N}(k_{\rm sh})\,,

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial n_{n}}{\partial k_{\rm sh}}\right)_{k_{Y}}

\displaystyle=-D(k_{\rm bu})\left(1-\frac{1}{B_{d}^{n}N_{c}^{3}}\right)\frac{\partial k_{\rm bu}}{\partial k_{\rm sh}}+D(k_{\rm sh})\,.

(67)

The chemical potential for hyperons
\mu_{Y}
can be computed in the same way,

\displaystyle\mu_{Y}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial(\varepsilon,n_{n})}{\partial(k_{\rm sh},k_{Y})}\bigg/\frac{\partial(n_{Y},n_{n})}{\partial(k_{\rm sh},k_{Y})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(\frac{\partial\varepsilon}{\partial k_{Y}}\right)_{k_{\rm sh}}}{\left(\frac{\partial n_{Y}}{\partial k_{Y}}\right)_{k_{\rm sh}}}-\frac{\left(\frac{\partial n_{n}}{\partial k_{Y}}\right)_{k_{\rm sh}}}{\left(\frac{\partial n_{Y}}{\partial k_{Y}}\right)_{k_{\rm sh}}}\mu_{n}\,,

(68)

where

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial\varepsilon}{\partial k_{Y}}\right)_{k_{\rm sh}}

\displaystyle=gd_{Y}\frac{h_{Y}}{N_{c}^{3}}D(k_{Y})\left[E_{Y}(k_{Y})-E_{N}(K_{Y})\frac{B_{d}^{Y}}{B_{d}^{n}}\right]\,,

(69)

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial n_{Y}}{\partial k_{Y}}\right)_{k_{\rm sh}}

\displaystyle=gd_{Y}\frac{h_{Y}}{N_{c}^{3}}D(k_{Y})\,,

(70)

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial n_{n}}{\partial k_{Y}}\right)_{k_{\rm sh}}

\displaystyle=-gd_{Y}\frac{B_{d}^{Y}}{B_{d}^{n}}\frac{h_{Y}}{N_{c}^{3}}D(k_{Y})\,.

(71)

Using these we obtain the chemical potentials in the main text:

\displaystyle\mu_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{-DE_{N}\big|_{k_{\rm bu}}\left(1-\frac{1}{B_{d}^{n}N_{c}^{3}}\right)\frac{\partial k_{\rm bu}}{\partial k_{\rm sh}}+DE_{N}\big|_{k_{\rm sh}}}{-D(k_{\rm bu})\left(1-\frac{1}{B_{d}^{n}N_{c}^{3}}\right)\frac{\partial k_{\rm bu}}{\partial k_{\rm sh}}+D(k_{\rm sh})}\,,

(72)

\displaystyle\mu_{Y}

\displaystyle=E_{Y}(k_{Y})-\frac{B_{d}^{Y}}{B_{d}^{n}}E_{N}(k_{Y})+\frac{B_{d}^{Y}}{B_{d}^{n}}\mu_{n}\,.

(73)

Next we compute the derivative of
\varepsilon
with respect to
h_{Y}
for a fixed
n_{B}
:

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial\varepsilon}{\partial h_{Y}}\right)_{n_{B}}=\frac{\partial(\varepsilon,n_{B})}{\partial(h_{Y},n_{B})}=\frac{\partial(\varepsilon,n_{B})}{\partial(h_{Y},k_{\rm sh})}\bigg/\frac{\partial(h_{Y},n_{B})}{\partial(h_{Y},k_{\rm sh})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(\frac{\partial\varepsilon}{\partial h_{Y}}\right)_{k_{\rm sh}}\left(\frac{\partial n_{B}}{\partial k_{\rm sh}}\right)_{h_{Y}}-\left(\frac{\partial n_{B}}{\partial h_{Y}}\right)_{k_{\rm sh}}\left(\frac{\partial\varepsilon}{\partial k_{\rm sh}}\right)_{h_{Y}}}{\left(\frac{\partial n_{B}}{\partial k_{\rm sh}}\right)_{h_{Y}}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\partial\varepsilon}{\partial h_{Y}}\right)_{k_{\rm sh}}-\left(\frac{\partial n_{B}}{\partial h_{Y}}\right)_{k_{\rm sh}}\bigg(\frac{\partial\varepsilon}{\partial n_{B}}\bigg)_{h_{Y}}

\displaystyle=d_{Y}\frac{g}{N_{c}^{3}}\int_{0}^{k_{Y}}dkD(k)

\displaystyle~~~\times\left[E_{Y}(k)-\frac{B_{d}^{Y}}{B_{d}^{n}}E_{N}(k)-\left(1-\frac{B_{d}^{Y}}{B_{d}^{n}}\right)\mu_{B}\right]\,.

(74)

Appendix B
Expression of the equation of state

The equation of state (EoS) is always a function of the Fermi momentum
k_{\rm sh}
.
Below the hyperon threshold, the EoS is described by the pure neutron matter with the low momentum part of the
d
quark states saturated.
Above the hyperon threshold, the EoS is a mixture of neutrons and degenerate hyperons.

B.0.1
Below the saturation and below the hyperon threshold

The EoS is that of an ideal gas in this regime:

n_{B}=g\frac{k_{\rm sh}^{3}}{6\pi^{2}}\,,~~~~\varepsilon=ge_{3}(k_{\rm sh},M_{N})\,,

(75)

with
\mu_{B}=\sqrt{k_{\rm sh}^{2}+M_{N}^{2}}
.
The function
e_{3}
is defined as

\displaystyle e_{3}(k,M)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{k}dx\,D(x)\sqrt{k^{2}+M^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{16\pi^{2}}k\sqrt{k^{2}+M^{2}}(2k^{2}+M^{2})

\displaystyle~~~+\frac{1}{16\pi^{2}}M^{4}\ln\left(\frac{\sqrt{k^{2}+M^{2}}-k}{M}\right)\,.

(76)

B.0.2
Saturation condition for pure neutron matter

The saturation takes place when
f_{d}(q=0)=1
.
This happens when

\hskip-5.69046ptf_{d}(q=0)=B_{d}^{n}N_{c}^{3}\left[1-e^{-k_{\rm sh}/(N_{c}\Lambda)}\left(1+\frac{k_{\rm sh}}{N_{c}\Lambda}\right)\right]\,.

(77)

B.0.3
Above the saturation and below the hyperon threshold

Above the saturation, one has to solve the equation to obtain the shell thickness
\Delta
or the fermi momentum for the under-occupied bulk part
k_{\rm bu}=k_{\rm sh}-\Delta
.
The equation is

\displaystyle\hskip-5.69046pt\frac{N_{c}\Lambda+k_{\rm sh}-\Delta}{N_{c}\Lambda+k_{\rm sh}-\Delta-(N_{c}\Lambda+k_{\rm sh})e^{-\Delta/(N_{c}\Lambda)}}=B_{d}^{n}N_{c}^{3}\,.

(78)

Then, from
\Delta
determined above, the EoS is obtained as

\displaystyle n_{B}

\displaystyle=-g\left(1-\frac{1}{B_{d}^{n}N_{c}^{3}}\right)\frac{k_{\rm bu}^{3}}{6\pi^{2}}+g\frac{k_{\rm sh}^{3}}{6\pi^{2}}\,,

(79)

\displaystyle\varepsilon

\displaystyle=-g\left(1-\frac{1}{B_{d}^{n}N_{c}^{3}}\right)e_{3}(k_{\rm bu},M_{N})+ge_{3}(k_{\rm sh},M_{N})\,,

(80)

\displaystyle\mu_{B}

\displaystyle=\frac{-k_{\rm bu}^{2}E_{N}(k_{\rm bu})\left(1-\frac{1}{B_{d}^{n}N_{c}^{3}}\right)\frac{\partial k_{\rm bu}}{\partial k_{\rm sh}}+k_{\rm sh}^{2}E_{N}(k_{\rm sh})}{-k_{\rm bu}^{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{B_{d}^{n}N_{c}^{3}}\right)\frac{\partial k_{\rm bu}}{\partial k_{\rm sh}}+k_{\rm sh}^{2}}\,,

(81)

where in the last line, we use the relation

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Delta}{\partial k_{\rm sh}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{N_{c}\Lambda\Delta}{(N_{c}\Lambda+k_{\rm sh})(k_{\rm sh}-\Delta)}\,,

(82)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial k_{\rm bu}}{\partial k_{\rm sh}}

\displaystyle=1-\frac{\partial\Delta}{\partial k_{\rm sh}}=\frac{k_{\rm sh}(N_{c}\Lambda+k_{\rm sh}-\Delta)}{(N_{c}\Lambda+k_{\rm sh})(k_{\rm sh}-\Delta)}\,.

(83)

B.0.4
Above the saturation and above the hyperon threshold

After the
d
-quark saturation occurs and
\mu_{B}
exceeds the hyperon threshold, the EoS are

\displaystyle\frac{\,n_{B}\,}{g}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{c}^{3}}\left(\frac{1}{B_{d}^{Y}}-\frac{1}{B_{d}^{n}}\right)\frac{k_{Y}^{3}}{6\pi^{2}}-\left(1-\frac{1}{B_{d}^{n}N_{c}^{3}}\right)\frac{k_{\rm bu}^{3}}{6\pi^{2}}+\frac{k_{\rm sh}^{3}}{6\pi^{2}}\,,

(84)

\displaystyle\frac{\,n_{S}\,}{g}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{B_{d}^{Y}N_{c}^{3}}\frac{k_{Y}^{3}}{6\pi^{2}}\,,

(85)

\displaystyle\frac{\,\varepsilon\,}{g}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{c}^{3}}\left[\frac{e_{3}(k_{Y},M_{Y})}{B_{d}^{Y}}-\frac{e_{3}(k_{Y},M_{N})}{B_{d}^{n}}\right]-\left(1-\frac{1}{B_{d}^{n}N_{c}^{3}}\right)e_{3}(k_{\rm bu},M_{N})+e_{3}(k_{\rm sh},M_{N})\,,

(86)

with
\mu_{B}
same as in Eq. (
81
).
And the
k_{Y}
is determined through the
\beta
-equilibrium condition

\displaystyle\mu_{B}=2E_{Y}(k_{Y})-E_{N}(k_{Y})\,.

(87)

Appendix C
Stepwise minimization with fixed baryon density

Figure 6:

Compositional changes initiated with the conversion of neutrons into hyperons which in turn triggers structural changes in the neutron momentum shell. The
d
-quark states are saturated at low momenta before and after these processes. Each conversion process conserves the baryon number.
(Left)
Neutrons at the momenta
k
transform into
\Lambda^{0}
or
\Sigma^{0}
,
n(k)\rightarrow\Lambda(k)
or
\Sigma^{0}(k)
with the energetic cost. Hyperons contain fewer
d
-quarks than neutrons so that the conversion
n\rightarrow Y
in the bulk leaves the phase space open for
d
-quarks or other hyperons;
(Right) The open phase space is filled by hyperons converted from neutrons in the shell. This process continues until
d
-quark states get saturated. Removing the baryon number from the shell, the shell structure is reorganized by reducing
k_{\rm sh}
and
k_{\rm bu}
as
k_{\rm bu}^{(1)}\rightarrow k_{\rm bu}^{(2)}
and
k_{\rm sh}^{(1)}\rightarrow k_{\rm sh}^{(2)}
. This conversion reduces the energy of the system.
Although we show these processes sequentially, they can occur at once. Whether the energy of the system decreases or not depends on the value of
k
.

In this appendix we consider the stepwise variation of energy, manifestly holding
n_{B}
fixed.
We consider the conversion of
n(k)\rightarrow Y(k)
for baryons with momentum
k\leq k_{\rm bu}
and subsequent modification of the shell.
The advantage of this strategy is that one can identify which processes cost and gain the energy,
see Fig.
6
.

We first consider the process
n(k)\rightarrow\Lambda^{0}(k)
or
\Sigma^{0}(k)
. The variation of each density at momentum
k
is

\delta n_{n}(k)+\delta n_{\Lambda^{0}}^{(1)}(k)+\delta n_{\Sigma^{0}}^{(1)}(k)=0\,,

(88)

where we defined

\delta n_{i}(k)\equiv\delta f_{i}(k)D(k)dk\,,

(89)

with
D(k)=k^{2}/2\pi^{2}
being the density of states at
k
.
Assuming
\Lambda^{0}
and
\Sigma^{0}
are generated at equal probability, in this first conversion process the density of each hyperon species changes as

\delta n_{Y}^{(1)}(k)\equiv\delta n_{\Lambda^{0}}^{(1)}(k)=\delta n_{\Sigma^{0}}^{(1)}(k)=-\frac{\delta n_{n}(k)}{2}\,.

(90)

After this conversion, the
d
-quarks are no longer saturated.
This is because we lose a single
d
-quark for each
n(k;udd)\rightarrow Y(k;uds)
process. We also note that this first process costs the energy
E_{Y}(k)-E_{n}(k)
for each conversion and never occurs unless another energy reduction process becomes possible by this conversion.

To fill the hole in the
d
-quark states in the bulk, we consider the second conversion process in which the neutrons in the shell decay into hyperons in the bulk. In this second process, the hyperon densities increase until the
d
-quark states are saturated,

\delta n_{Y}^{(2)}(k)=\delta n_{\Lambda^{0}}^{(2)}(k)=\delta n_{\Sigma^{0}}^{(2)}(k)=\delta n_{Y}^{(1)}(k)\,.

(91)

Thus, the amount of decaying neutrons is

-\delta n_{n}^{\rm shell}=\delta n_{\Lambda^{0}}^{(2)}(k)+\delta n_{\Sigma^{0}}^{(2)}(k)=-\delta n_{n}(k)\,.

(92)

Reducing the number of neutrons in the shell, the shell structure is reorganized until the optimized (saturated) distribution is achieved again.
As we have explicitly calculated in isospin symmetric matter, the momenta
k_{\rm bu}
and
k_{\rm sh}
for the saturated momentum shell are uniquely related.
The
k_{\rm bu}
and
k_{\rm sh}
before (index 1) and after (index 2) satisfy the relation

F_{\rm sat}(k_{\rm bu}^{(1)},k_{\rm sh}^{(1)})=F_{\rm sat}(k_{\rm bu}^{(2)},k_{\rm sh}^{(2)})=0\,.

(93)

and hence the relation between
dk_{\rm bu}=k_{\rm bu}^{(2)}-k_{\rm bu}^{(1)}(<0)
and
dk_{\rm sh}=k_{\rm sh}^{(2)}-k_{\rm sh}^{(1)}(<0)
is also uniquely specified.
Now we can write the variation of neutron number in the shell as

\delta n_{n}^{\rm shell}=-\bigg(1-\frac{1}{\,B_{d}^{n}N_{c}^{3}\,}\bigg)D(k_{\rm bu})dk_{\rm bu}+D(k_{\rm sh})dk_{\rm sh}\,.

(94)

Here we assumed that the bulk just below
k_{\rm bu}
is saturated by neutrons; in the interval
[k_{\rm bu}-dk_{\rm bu},k_{\rm bu}]
the amplitude increases from
1/B_{d}^{n}N_{c}^{3}
to
1
, while the amplitude in
[k_{\rm sh}-dk_{\rm sh},k_{\rm sh}]
is reduced from
1
to
0
.

Now we examine how the energy density changes. Since we write the variations within infinitesimal phase space, it is sufficient to multiply the energy at each phase space,

\displaystyle\delta\varepsilon^{(1+2)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{Y=\Lambda^{0},\Sigma^{0}}E_{Y}(k)\delta n_{Y}^{(1+2)}(k)

\displaystyle~~~~~+E_{n}(k)\delta n_{n}(k)+\delta\varepsilon_{n}^{\rm shell}

\displaystyle=\delta n_{n}(k)\bigg(-2E_{Y}(k)+E_{n}(k)+\frac{\delta\varepsilon_{n}^{\rm shell}}{\delta n_{n}^{\rm shell}}\bigg)

(95)

where in the last step we used

\delta n_{Y}^{(1+2)}(k)=-\delta n_{n}(k)
and
\delta n_{n}^{\rm shell}=\delta n_{n}(k)
. Here
\delta\varepsilon_{n}^{\rm shell}
is

\delta\varepsilon_{n}^{\rm shell}=-E_{n}D\big|_{k_{\rm bu}}\bigg(1-\frac{1}{\,B_{d}^{n}N_{c}^{3}\,}\bigg)dk_{\rm bu}+E_{n}D\big|_{k_{\rm sh}}dk_{\rm sh}\,.

(96)

At chemical equilibrium, the expression
\delta\varepsilon_{B}^{\rm shell}/\delta n_{B}^{\rm shell}
corresponds to the neutron chemical potential as shown in the main text.
We recall that
\delta n_{n}(k)<0
corresponds to the increase of
f_{Y}(k)
. Looking at the coefficient of
\delta n_{n}(k)
, we conclude that increasing
f_{Y}(k)
reduces the energy when

2E_{Y}(k)-E_{N}(k)\leq\frac{\,\delta\varepsilon_{B}^{\rm shell}\,}{\,\delta n_{B}^{\rm shell}\,}\,.

(97)

In the main text, we have seen that the LHS is an increasing function. Hence the inequality holds (i.e., hyperons appear) only at low momenta.
Hence we conclude that the distributions
f_{n}
and
f_{Y}
take the form

\displaystyle f_{n}(k)

\displaystyle=\frac{3}{\,2N_{c}^{3}\,}\Theta(k-k_{Y})\Theta(k_{\rm bu}-k)

\displaystyle~~~+\Theta(k-k_{\rm bu})\Theta(k_{\rm sh}-k)\,,

(98)

\displaystyle f_{Y}(k)

\displaystyle=\frac{3}{\,2N_{c}^{3}\,}\Theta(k_{Y}-k)\,,

(99)

where
k_{Y}
is defined to be

2E_{Y}(k_{Y})=E_{N}(k_{Y})+\frac{\,\delta\varepsilon_{B}^{\rm shell}\,}{\,\delta n_{B}^{\rm shell}\,}\,.

(100)

Since the distribution has the lowest energy, there should be no further conversion. Hence this condition to determine
k_{Y}
is equivalent to the
\beta
-equilibrium condition. One can manifestly compute
\mu_{n}
and
\mu_{Y}
using Jacobian method and check that
\mu_{n}=\mu_{Y}
indeed holds. The results coincide with those in the main text.
