Sufficiently Regularized Nonnegative Quartic Polynomials are Sum-of-Squares
Coralia Cartis and, Wenqi Zhu

Abstract
Hilbert’s 17th problem famously established that not all nonnegative polynomials admit an SoS representation. Hilbert also identified a few special classes in which nonnegativity and SoS are equivalent, such as univariate polynomials, quadratic polynomials, and bivariate quartic polynomials. In this paper, we extend this equivalence to several new subclasses of multivariate quartically-regularized polynomials and characterise the NP-hardness boundary of these special-structure polynomials. Namely, we consider the global optimization of multivariate symmetric cubic polynomials regularised by weighted quartic powers of the Euclidean norm. These special-structure polynomials arise as iterative subproblems in high-order tensor methods for nonconvex optimisation problems. We consider shifting these polynomials by their global optimum so as to make them nonnegative, and show that for sufficiently high regularization and under mild assumptions, these polynomials admit a Sums-of-Squares (SoS) representation. We also identify several structured subclasses of quartically-regularized cubic polynomials for which global optimality of the model implies that its nonnegativity is certified by an SoS decomposition, for all values of the regularization parameter (such as quadratic-quartic polynomials and quartic polynomials containing a special cubic term that can be decomposed into a product of a quadratic norm and a linear form). We provide counterexamples using quartic separable norms that showcase the crucial role of the Euclidean norm in these representations. We illustrate how to use our SoS-based certificates for the Taylor subproblems inside high-order tensor methods for nonconvex optimization, with encouraging results.

3.3

The Schnabel Model

Schnabel et al.
[
66
]
proposed a formulation for unconstrained optimization based on a fourth-order model of the objective function
f
. The model is given by

m_{\mathcal{S}}(s):=f_{0}+g^{T}s+\frac{1}{2}H[s]^{2}+\frac{1}{6}\sum_{j=1}^{k}(a_{j}^{T}s)^{2}(b_{j}^{T}s)+\frac{1}{4}\sum_{j=1}^{k}{\sigma_{j}}(a_{j}^{T}s)^{4}

(Schnabel Model)

where
\sigma_{j}>0
,
a_{j},b_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
are specific vectors which correspond to the low–rank approximations of the third and fourth-order derivatives and are built by interpolating function and gradient information from past iterates.
Schnabel et al. showed that although (
Schnabel Model
) is a fourth-order polynomial in
n
variables, it can be reduced to the minimization of a fourth-order polynomial in
k
variables plus a quadratic in
n-k
variables. Note that the quartic term in
m_{\mathcal{S}}
does not necessarily ensure that

m_{\mathcal{S}}(s)\to+\infty
as
\|s\|\to\infty
in all directions.
Therefore, the Schnabel model may not be bounded below
and cannot guarantee the existence of a global minimizer of the model function.
In
[
66
,
67
]
, Schnabel et al. used this model within a trust-region framework to obtain global convergence of the overall algorithm.

Let

.
We begin with a technical lemma that provides alternative equivalent expressions for

Examples and Preliminary Numerical Results

In this section, we begin by examining several well-known quartic examples due to Nesterov and Ahmadi (

and
Section

4.2
). These problems are known to be NP-hard to globally minimize and are not SoS-exact at the zeroth level of the Lasserre hierarchy. We compare these examples with their quartically regularized counterparts and analyze—both theoretically and numerically—how different regularization norms and problem constraints affect SoS representability.

In
Section

We test this method on the Moré–Garbow–Hillstrom (MGH) test set

and provide preliminary numerical observations on several representative and challenging examples, including the Nesterov–Chebyshev function, the Beale function, and the sensor network localization problem.

Sensor Network Localization Problem

Quartic optimization problems arise in various engineering applications such as independent component analysis
[
14
]
, blind channel equalization in digital communication
[
40
]
, and sensor network localization
[
10
]
.
We consider the sensor localization problem, where
{\cal A}
and
{\cal S}
denote the sets of anchor nodes and sensor nodes, with cardinalities
|{\cal A}|
and
|{\cal S}|
, respectively.
The goal is to estimate the sensor positions
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
for
i\in{\cal S}
by minimizing the quartic objective

\min_{x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{3},\,i\in{\cal S}}f_{{\cal S}}(x):=\sum_{i,j\in{\cal S}}\big(\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|^{2}-d_{ij}^{2}\big)^{2}+\sum_{i\in{\cal S},\,j\in{\cal A}}\big(\|x_{i}-s_{j}\|^{2}-d_{ij}^{2}\big)^{2},

(59)

where
d_{ij}
are (possibly noisy) distance measurements between nodes,
s_{j}
are the known positions of the anchors, and

x=[x_{1}^{T},x_{2}^{T},\dotsc,x_{|{\cal S}|}^{T}]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{3|{\cal S}|}.

By homogenizing (
59
), one obtains a quartic minimization problem with a single quadratic constraint, fitting the structure of (
54
).
As a resuult, (
59
) is known to be NP-hard
[
39
]
11
11

11

Indeed, even deciding whether the optimal value of (
59
) is zero is NP-hard.
. The relative sizes of the sensor and anchor sets strongly influence the difficulty of the localization problem.
When anchors outnumber sensors (
|{\cal A}|>|{\cal S}|
), the system is overdetermined and relatively easier.
In practical settings, however, only a few anchors are available (e.g., GPS-equipped nodes), and the common regime is
|{\cal A}|\leq|{\cal S}|
.
This underdetermined case is significantly more challenging: the resulting optimization landscape is highly nonconvex, often sensitive to measurement noise, and typically contains many spurious local minima.
Moreover, due to geometric symmetries, multiple global minimizers may exist.
For example, even in the simplest case with two anchors and two sensors, the problem admits several symmetric global solutions, and the rank condition required for moment-SDP solvers (e.g., GloptiPoly/MSDP) fails—preventing extraction of the global minimizer from the relaxation.

The quartic sensor–localization model is well suited for third-order methods.
Although the problem is highly nonconvex, its third-order tensor has a structured (and often low-rank) form determined by the anchor geometry. Moreover, the third-order tensor can be applied to directions without explicitly forming the full dense tensor; see
Section

C.3
for details. We test four methods on this problem:
AR3 + SoS
,
fminunc
,
ARC
, and
AR3 + ARC
.

We try on the smallest “hard” case, with
|{\cal S}|=|{\cal A}|=2
a various level of noise
10^{-5}-10^{0}
. In this setting,
f_{{\cal S}}
is a quartic polynomial in
n=2\times 3=6
variables. Our experiments show that, from the same initialization,
AR3+SoS
usually reduces the objective value the most giving near zero function value (below
O(10^{-5})
for noise level
10^{-5}-10^{-1}
). In contrast,
ARC
,
AR3 + ARC
, and
fminunc
typically converge to a higher objective function value, which correspond to a local minimizers. It is worth noting that, we also tried reformulate the
6

Scalability and CPU time considerations.

Our experiments are limited to relatively small-scale problems because the inner subproblems are solved using moment-SDP relaxations, which are practical only for small polynomial systems (
n<10
). In comparison with other methods,
AR3 + SoS
is more expensive in terms of CPU time per iteration.
Although SoS relaxations offer a principled route to global optimality, the SDP size

The focus of this paper is on establishing a theoretical framework that enables a higher-order Newton-type algorithm to achieve global convergence guarantees while maintaining polynomial-time complexity per iteration. In particular, our work—together with the recent results of Ahmadi

5

Conclusion

In this paper, we studied the interplay between regularization, convexification, and Sum-of-Squares (SoS) representability for quartically regularized cubic polynomials arising from high-order adaptive regularization methods. We showed that, under sufficiently strong regularization, a nonconvex nonnegative quartically regularized polynomials admits an SoS representation. This establishes, for the first time, explicit SoS certification results for a family of nonconvex quartically regularized models.

In addition, we identified several structured subclasses of quartically regularized cubic polynomials for which global optimality of the model implies that its nonnegativity is
certified
by a Sum-of-Squares (SoS) decomposition, for all values of the regularization parameter
\sigma
.
In this sense, our results extend Hilbert’s classical characterizations of nonnegative polynomials to a new subfamily motivated by high-order tensor methods.
We further propose an algorithmic framework that combines SoS-based Taylor models with adaptive regularization techniques.

Several directions for future research naturally arise from this work. These include establishing global complexity guarantees for the AR
3
+SoS algorithmic framework, and investigating tight semidefinite relaxations with reduced dimensionality. In particular, recent work by
[
73

leverages global sufficient optimality conditions for cubic quartically regularized (CQR) polynomials

to derive a tight SDP formulation of size
n+1
.
Another promising direction is to exploit additional structural properties to develop more scalable variants using alternatives
