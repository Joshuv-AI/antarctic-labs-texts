Calibration method for the optical system of infrared CMOS-theodolite in astronomical measurement
Authors: Conghai Ruan, Houtian Zhang, Chonghui Li, Shuai Tong, Shaojie Chen, Chunlin Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe all-day astronomical measurement system, which integrates a theodolite with an infrared sensor, offers substantial advantages in miniaturization, automation, speed, measurement accuracy, and efficiency, enabling comprehensive all-day astronomical observations. High-precision instrument calibration is a core prerequisite for obtaining accurate astronomical measurement results, which directly determines the reliability and effectiveness of the system’s observation data. However, the system features a removed eyepiece and a small optical field of view; in addition, its complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor (CMOS) sensor is equipped with low pixel resolution, making existing conventional calibration methods incompatible and unable to meet high-precision calibration requirements. This study utilizes a self-developed astronomical measurement system as a platform, fully integrating the theodolite’s angle measurement data to propose a novel calibration method. The approach initially determines the target point’s direction value and partial camera parameters through two-face measurement, which then serves as known information for computing principal point pixel coordinates using face I/II data. To verify the effectiveness and reliability of the proposed method, calibration experiments are first conducted to obtain a set of well-calibrated parameters with high precision. On this basis, astronomical positioning experiments are carried out in two typical observation environments: daytime and nighttime. Preliminary experimental results show that the proposed calibration method can stably and reliably acquire high-precision calibration parameters of the optical system, providing solid technical support for subsequent astronomical observations. Compared to established astronomical reference points, the root mean square errors (RMSEs) for daytime astronomical longitude and latitude measurements were within 0.33″ and 0.34″, respectively, whereas night-time measurements achieved RMSEs of 0.26″ and 0.29″, respectively. These findings highlight the method’s potential for improving the accuracy, robustness, and practical applicability of astronomical measurements across diverse observational environments, contributing to advancements in modern astronomical observation technologies.Keywords: All-day astrometry, Image-assisted total station (IATS), Instrument calibration, Optical system, Image measurement, Astronomical positioningSubject terms: Astronomy and planetary science, Mathematics and computing, Optics and photonics, Physics

Introduction

Astronomical geodesy determines the astronomical longitude and latitude of a ground point or the astronomical azimuth in a specified direction by observing the position of celestial bodies1. With advantages such as robust autonomy, good concealment, robust anti-jamming capability, and high orientation accuracy, it plays a critical role in professional fields, including ground reference system positioning, geoid shape determination, and engineering orientation. Astronomical geodesy also underpins national economic development and military security2–6.

Scientific and technological progress has transformed astronomical geodetic equipment from traditional optical instruments to modern electronic systems. Bulky, cumbersome traditional instruments (e.g., WILD T4), which rely heavily on surveyors’ visual observation, are being replaced by lightweight, portable, and reliable electronic theodolite-based systems-such as the ICARUS system7(ETH Zurich) and the Field Astronomical Geodetic System8(University of Information Engineering). Integrating computer technology with theodolite instrumentation, these systems significantly enhance measurement efficiency and accuracy9–11, but remain semi-automated due to their reliance on operator proficiency, which causes result variations among different users.

With the development of image sensor technology, sensors can replace human eyes in observation, reducing manual observation errors. Digital Zenith Instruments, which mount charge-coupled device (CCD) cameras on the telescope’s focal plane, achieve high astronomical positioning accuracy-for instance, ETH Zurich’s DIADEM system and the University of Hannover’s TZK2-D can reach 0.08″ vertical deviation accuracy after 20 min of observation12. However, their application is limited by narrow sky coverage, high cost, and inability to perform simultaneous astronomical orientation and positioning13.

Integrating image sensors into theodolites or total stations and replacing visual star observation with camera-based stellar imaging eliminates human-induced errors while maintaining instrument compactness, flexibility, and portability. Representative systems include ETH Zurich’s QDaedalus, which replaces the eyepiece of a Leica total station with a CCD sensor to deliver 0.2″ positioning accuracy14–18. The Information Engineering University has also advanced astronomical geodesy using high-precision Leica TS50i and TS60 image-assisted total stations, achieving 0.2″ positioning accuracy, vertical deviation standard deviations of 0.13″-0.19″, and RMSE below 0.2″19,20.

The fusion of imaging sensors, computing, and geodetic instrument technology brings key benefits of miniaturization, automation, and high efficiency, strongly enhancing measurement precision and throughput. Nevertheless, such systems rely on visible-spectrum sensors, whose imaging quality degrades under strong sunlight, cloud attenuation, and atmospheric scattering, preventing all-day astronomical observation21,22. Since sky radiance in the infrared band is far lower than in the visible, and infrared radiation penetrates thin clouds, smoke, and haze more effectively, integrating an infrared image sensor into a high-precision theodolite and extending stellar observation to the infrared band offers a viable path to all-day operation.

This work presents the AT02 system, which embeds an IMX991 infrared CMOS sensor into its telescopic assembly. With quantum efficiency covering 900–1700 nm and an image resolution of 752 × 520 pixels, the system enables all-day astronomical measurement. As with all precision instruments, calibration is essential prior to deployment. The AT02 calibration framework includes two core modules: theodolite calibration and optical system calibration. Theodolite calibration follows well-established standards and protocols; the main technical challenge lies in optical system calibration.

Extensive calibration studies have been conducted for visible-band optical systems. Bürki et al.14 developed DAEDALUS, a clip-on CCD adapter for total stations that enables automated prism-free target detection. Their system was calibrated using two-face observations and iterative least-squares adjustment to solve for the principal point and affine parameters, compensating for mounting errors, scale bias, and focus drift. The system achieved 0.2″-0.3″ astrogeodetic accuracy and better than 10 μm industrial precision. However, it neglected optical distortion and used a simplified affine model, while the visible sensor remained unsuitable for all-day observation. Shi et al.19 introduced an automated astronomical survey method using the built-in CCD of a Leica TS50i total station. They derived a polynomial transformation between pixel coordinates and angular readings via indoor calibration to reduce personal and instrumental errors. The method achieved 0.7″ horizontal and 0.6″ vertical conversion accuracy, meeting first-class astronomical field requirements. Yet it relied only on basic polynomial fitting without estimating intrinsic camera parameters or optical distortion, lacked a strict geometric model, and still required manual aiming for reference angles. Zhang et al.23 focused on camera mounting errors in image-assisted total stations (IATS) and derived a physically interpretable error correction model featuring image plane rotation (κ) and tilt errors (α, β) to replace conventional affine transformation. Their analysis indicated that a 2″ tilt error could introduce horizontal and vertical angular errors up to 3.11″ and 2.00″ at 50° elevation, which must be corrected for high-precision astronomy. However, this work remained at the level of theoretical modeling and quantitative analysis, with no real calibration or field validation. Zhou et al.24,25 developed a calibration approach for the telescope camera of the Leica MS50 IATS using automatically detected coded targets. This method performs a 2D coupled calibration between image pixels and angles by solving four affine transformation parameters and one radial distortion parameter, without relying on control points, distance observations, or absolute orientation information. It is mainly applied to high-precision, close-range, multi-target relative deformation monitoring. However, it can only establish a relative angular relationship between image points and the collimation axis, and cannot obtain the absolute spatial direction of targets in the total station or world coordinate system. Furthermore, it has limitations including the idealized assumption of a fixed principal point, strong dependence on coded targets, and applicability only to single-station setups.

Significant differences exist between the calibration methods for the all-day astronomical measurement optical system and conventional approaches. Due to the system’s narrow field of view, obtaining a sufficient number of control points within a single observation field is challenging unless the target is placed at a considerable distance from the system. However, positioning the calibration object too far away results in an unsatisfactory control network shape, negatively impacting the accuracy of the calibration parameters. Additionally, the system’s image sensor is sensitive only to infrared light, making it impossible to obtain clear images of conventional checkerboard patterns and other standard calibration objects. These limitations render traditional calibration methods for photogrammetry, such as the indoor three-dimensional calibration field method26 and the Zhang et al. calibration method27, unsuitable for this system.

In astronomical measurements, the direction value of a target star measured by the theodolite serves as a key parameter for astronomical positioning and orientation, and the angular measurement accuracy of the theodolite is extremely high. Therefore, establishing a measurement and calibration model based on theodolite angle data is not only intuitive and effective, but also highly practical and cost-efficient. Although studies by Shi et al.19, Bürki et al.14, and Zhou et al.24,25 have provided valuable references for calibration methods, they still exhibit obvious limitations. Some methods are developed only for specific scenarios and cannot satisfy the high-precision requirements of astronomical measurements. Most methods require high-precision absolute direction data of target points; some rely on oversimplified models with excessively stringent constraints, and others adopt pure polynomial fitting, resulting in calculated parameters without clear physical meaning. In addition, some methods directly use factory nominal parameters without performing measured calibration on key indicators, which inevitably reduces the final calibration accuracy. To overcome the deficiencies of existing calibration methods, this paper presents a dedicated calibration scheme for small-field-of-view, all-day astronomical optical systems. The method requires neither external measurement information nor manual target aiming. First, two-face observations are adopted to decouple the principal point pixel coordinates from the absolute directions of target points. Then, the principal point pixel coordinates in the face Ⅰ and face Ⅱ positions are solved separately.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 mainly introduces the optical path configuration of the all-day astronomical measurement system, the commonly used coordinate systems, and the derivation of the image measurement model. Section 3 proposes the calibration strategy of this paper, analyzes the influences of installation errors and distortions on angle measurement accuracy, and constructs the calibration model. On this basis, the detailed procedure of parameter decoupling is presented combined with parameter correlation analysis. Section 4 designs and conducts systematic calibration experiments, elaborates the experimental scheme and implementation steps, quantitatively analyzes the accuracy of calibration parameters, and finally obtains a set of high-precision calibration parameters suitable for astronomical measurements, as well as discusses their application effect in astronomical positioning. Section 5 concludes the whole paper, summarizing the core research achievements, main innovations and future research directions.

Image measurement model

Astronomical measurements are conducted by observing the zenith distances or azimuths of stars. These observations, when combined with supplementary data such as time, enable the determination of the astronomical longitude and latitude of a ground point or the astronomical azimuth of a ground edge.

In the all-day astronomical measurement system, observations consist of the direction values of the collimation axis and the pixel coordinates of the corresponding image point on the image plane of the infrared star point, obtained through digital image processing. Based on these two types of observations, an image measurement model is established to calculate the direction value of the star point in the theodolite coordinate system.

Calibration method

Calibration model

Installation errors are inevitable for the infrared CMOS and reflecting mirror during assembly. Such errors manifest as image plane rotation, tilt, and non-parallelism between the principal optical axis and the collimation axis. Zhang et al.23 proved that the influences of image plane tilt and reflecting mirror installation deviation on measurement are equivalent, and can be decomposed into error angles β and α in two orthogonal directions, while image plane rotation can be represented by parameter κ.

By incorporating these two parameter types into the measurement models and following the derivation procedure in Sect. 2.3, it can be demonstrated that for angular errors α and β- which are practically controllable in assembly-the solution from two-face observation is unaffected by such deviations.

Notably, the horizontal and vertical tilt components β and α are fully coupled with the principal point coordinates and , respectively, and thus can be treated as integrated entities separately during the calibration process. The presence of the rotation angle κ will alter the image plane coordinates of the image points, so it can be incorporated into the measurement model.

Considering that the field of view of the camera in the all-day astronomical measurement system is small, only 1°, the influence of optical distortion on imaging measurement is relatively limited. In existing studies, Ref14. does not consider the effect of distortion on measurement results under a camera field of view of 0.75°; Refs24,25. only introduce first-order radial distortion for error compensation. Combined with the actual manufacturing process and field of view conditions of the camera in this paper, a first-order radial distortion model is adopted to compensate for imaging distortion28 to further improve measurement accuracy. Obtaining high-precision direction values and of target points is the original intention of calibration. If and are treated as unknown parameters to be solved, minor measurement errors will cause them to deviate from their true values due to the coupling effect between parameters. Therefore, it is necessary to consider separating them first. Taking the average of multiple manual aiming results as the true value is an effective method, but it still introduces aiming errors. Meanwhile, since the eyepiece of the total station is removed in this system, the true direction values of target points cannot be determined by manual aiming.

As proven in Sect. 2.3, additional constraints can be provided by theodolite observations in the direct and reverse positions, thereby separating the direction values of target points. Accordingly, the calibration strategy of this paper is as follows: first, the mean values of the two-face calibration data are used to separate parameters , , f, , and κ, with the principal point and of the image located at the center of the image plane at this stage; then, based on the calibration results of the parameters obtained in the first step, the principal points and of the image in the face Ⅰ and face Ⅱ positions can be solved respectively.

Calibration experiment

Experiment overview

To verify the correctness and validity of the proposed research method, a calibration experimental platform, as illustrated in Fig. 6, was designed. The platform consists of an infrared theodolite and an infrared light source installed on the observation squat, with a distance of no less than 400 m between them, as depicted in Fig. 6(a, b), respectively. Additionally, an automated calibration software system was developed. This system allows a computer to control the infrared theodolite, and the core procedure of data acquisition is as follows: the software actuates the alidade or telescope of the theodolite to adjust the orientation of the optical axis toward different directions, so as to capture images of the infrared light source. The platform realizes the automatic execution of data acquisition and processing, which not only improves the measurement efficiency but also enhances the accuracy of parameter calibration.

Automatic data acquisition and processing platform: (a) Control measurement terminal. (b) Infrared light source.

Theoretically, only three different observation directions are required to observe the fixed infrared light source. This allows for the listing of six equations to complete the calibration. However, to minimize random errors in the observation process and improve the reliability of the solution for unknown parameters, the actual number of observation directions should exceed three. Additionally, to enhance the accuracy of the first-order radial distortion coefficient, the image points should be uniformly distributed across the entire image plane.

High-precision target centroid extraction is a prerequisite for obtaining excellent calibration parameters. Fig. 7 shows the target imaging results at different calibration periods. By comparison, the imaging quality of the target at night is better with a pure background and less interference. In contrast, affected by solar radiation during the daytime, the image is often accompanied by blocky noise interference besides the target image points. Therefore, when processing the star images collected for calibration, conventional star image preprocessing can be omitted, and the star boundary traversal search can be directly performed on the whole image.

Calibration star images acquired at different time periods: (a) Nighttime calibration star image. (b) Daytime calibration star image.

In this paper, the boundary search algorithm proposed in Ref.30. is adopted. The star signal is first enhanced by Gaussian template convolution, and then the connected domain analysis is used to traverse the star image to extract the target boundary. For daytime images, the boundary search process will inevitably extract noise regions simultaneously, which can be eliminated according to the area characteristics of the extracted targets (noise regions usually have a larger area). After extracting the effective star boundaries, the gray centroid algorithm is used to calculate the star centroid.

To facilitate program design, the number of observation directions m, is set to a perfect square. Furthermore, to improve the reliability of the image information, the infrared light source is photographed n times in each observation direction. The average value of the pixel coordinates of the image points across multiple images, after removing coarse data, is taken as the final pixel coordinate value of the target image points during data processing.

During the measurement process, operations such as instrument rotation, angle measurement, face flipping, and image capture are automated. Commands are sent from the computer to the measurement system, which executes them autonomously upon receiving the instructions. To guarantee the timely acquisition of calibration parameters, the data processing module is integrated with the data acquisition module. The complete processing workflow is illustrated in Fig. 8.

Data collection and processing flow.

As illustrated in Fig. 8, the data acquisition and processing process can be performed in the following steps:

Preparation for calibration. This includes the placement of the instrument, leveling, preheating, communication settings, and parameter settings.

Data Acquisition. The computer program controls the theodolite to observe the target point from different directions, and the target point is imaged on the image plane sequentially in the order from left to right and from top to bottom. The steps for single-direction on two-face measurement are: face I aiming, face I angle measurement, face I photographing, switching face I to face II, face II angle measurement, and face II photographing.

Module jumping. Following the data acquisition, the text file of the direction values of the collimation axis and image file would be generated under the corresponding path. Through the module jumping button in the data acquisition interface, it can realize jumping from the data acquisition interface to the data processing interface and transferring the parameters such as the number of observation directions m and the number of photographed images n, which can be used as the basis of data checking in the data processing module.

Data processing. The two types of observation files obtained from the measurement are loaded and checked, and an error message would pop up if any abnormality is found. After passing the data check, the batch processing of infrared star map is completed according to the designed star point extraction algorithm, and the centroid pixel coordinates of the corresponding infrared image points are obtained; based on the two types of pre-processed data, the two-face, face I, and face II calibration are performed in order; the reprojection errors of the parameters are presented in the form of a scatterplot after the completion of the calibration and the results of calibration are stored in the form of a text file.

Analysis of experimental calibration results

To verify the correctness of the calibration model and the stability of the instrument during the calibration process, a total of four groups of experiments were designed and conducted in Zhengzhou. In Groups 1 to 3, the infrared theodolite and the infrared light source were mounted on the same observation piers respectively, and all these experiments were completed on August 30, 2024. Experiment 1 was carried out from 9:40 to 10:40 on the same day, with an air temperature of 26 ℃ and an atmospheric pressure of 754.56 mmHg. Experiment 2 was carried out from 18:40 to 19:40 on the same day, with an air temperature of 32 ℃ and an atmospheric pressure of 753.81 mmHg. Experiment 3 was carried out from 21:05 to 22:05 on the same day, with an air temperature of 29 ℃ and an atmospheric pressure of 756.06 mmHg. Experiment 4 was performed from 19:20 to 20:20 on May 7, 2024, with an air temperature of 27 ℃ and an atmospheric pressure of 757.56 mmHg. These experiments involved two-face measurement with an infrared theodolite observing a fixed infrared target point from 49 different directions. For each observation, a total of 10 images were captured, 5 from face I and 5 from face II. Simultaneously, the direction values of the collimation axis were recorded.

Experimental verification of astronomical positioning

The current mainstream astronomical positioning method is the altitude method. When this method is used to simultaneously determine astronomical longitude and latitude, the accuracy of the finally obtained astronomical latitude is closely related to the altitude angle measurement error and the quality of the geometric configuration of the observed stars; while the accuracy of astronomical longitude depends on the accuracy of the positioning latitude31. During the experiment, the good geometric configuration of the observed stars can be guaranteed by the star selection algorithm; therefore, the altitude angle data corrected by the calibration parameters becomes the core factor determining the positioning accuracy.

To verify the accuracy and reliability of the measurement model and calibration parameters, an astronomical positioning experiment was carried out on September 21, 2024, at a known measurement benchmark point in Beijing. The experiment was conducted in two periods: daytime and nighttime, with clear and cloudless weather on the day to ensure stable observation conditions. Among them, the daytime positioning experiment was carried out from 14:03 to 14:58, with an ambient temperature of 25.8℃ and an air pressure of 760.3 mmHg; the nighttime positioning experiment was carried out from 19:03 to 19:58, with an ambient temperature of 19.4℃ and an air pressure of 759.9 mmHg. Twelve groups of positioning experiments were completed in each period, with 10 to 12 observed stars selected for each group. The star selection configuration approximately followed a uniform distribution, and the duration of a single group of experiments was about 5 min. Table 7 shows the positioning results obtained using the calibration parameters of the method proposed in this paper. It can be seen from the table that the RMSE of longitude and latitude in the 12 groups of daytime positioning experiments are 0.33″ and 0.34″, respectively; the RMSE of longitude and latitude in the 12 groups of nighttime positioning experiments are 0.26″ and 0.29″, respectively.

Conclusion

Traditional astronomical measurement equipment can only conduct observations under clear night conditions. In contrast, the independently developed astronomical measurement system, which combines a CMOS and a theodolite, enables all-day observations both in the daytime and at night-time. This study conducted in-depth research on the measurement model and calibration method of the system, and verified their correctness through calibration experiments and astronomical positioning experiments. The following key conclusions are drawn:

There is a complete correlation between the absolute direction of the target point and the coordinates of the principal point of the image. If the observation conditions are limited, treating both as unknown parameters to participate in calibration will not yield high-precision camera calibration parameters.

The use of the theodolite’s two-face observation method can provide additional constraint conditions to achieve the decoupling of the absolute direction of the target point and the coordinates of the principal point of the image. This method first uses the two-face measurement data to decouple the absolute direction of the target point and the pixel coordinates of the principal point of the image, and solves some camera parameters simultaneously, then solves the coordinates of the principal point of the image for the faceⅠ and face Ⅱ positions respectively.

A set of calibration data collection and camera parameter self-calibration software was developed. By adopting the method of rotating and shooting a single infrared target point, the collection and calibration of multiple groups of camera parameters were completed, and finally a set of camera parameters with high precision and good stability was obtained.

Using this set of parameters, daytime and nighttime positioning experiments were carried out at a known benchmark point. The experimental results show that the RMSE of longitude and latitude in the 12 groups of daytime positioning experiments are 0.33″ and 0.34″, respectively; the RMSE of longitude and latitude in the 12 groups of nighttime positioning experiments are 0.26″ and 0.29″, respectively. In contrast, if nominal parameters are used for calculation, significant deviations will occur in the positioning results. This result fully indicates that the method proposed in this paper can provide high-accuracy calibration parameter support for high-precision astronomical measurements.
