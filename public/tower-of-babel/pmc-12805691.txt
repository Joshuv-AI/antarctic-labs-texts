Distribution of Staphylococcus aureus drug resistance genes, biofilm formation and cell wall characteristics in dairy cattle from dairy farms in Northeast China
Authors: Wei Wu, Hongzhe Zhao, Shize Hao, Guiwei Li, Xihuai Xue, Jiaxuan Li, Wen Cui, Yanping Jiang, Yingying Ma, Xinyuan Qiao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe spread of drug-resistant Staphylococcus aureus(S.aureus) in cattle herds not only threatens livestock production but also becomes an important route for the transmission of drug-resistant bacteria to humans, posing significant public health risks and epidemiological significance. In this study, a total of 282 clinical samples were collected form cattle farms in Heilongjiang, Jilin, and Inner Mongolia, and isolation of S. aureus from 62 samples. Through spa typing and disk diffusion assays of 62 isolates, the predominant spa type was identified as t034. Notably, 54 isolates exhibited multidrug resistance, with a penicillin resistance rate as high as 87.1%, and 34 isolates were identified as methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA). Among the 11 resistance genes tested, the detection rate of tetM gene was 100%. The Congo red plates and crystal violet staining assay showed that the isolated strains all produced biofilms, with no significant changes in the cell wall. In addition, icaA, SarA, and CidA genes identified as critical regulators of biofilm formation. Results from this study suggest that the feces and milk of dairy cows in Northeast China were contaminated with S. aureus including MRSA strains, and these strains carry a variety of resistance genes and have the ability to form biofilms, showing a resistance phenotype to a variety of antimicrobial agents.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12917-025-05180-2.Keywords: Bovine-derived S. aureus, Antibiotic resistance, Resistance genes, Biofilm

Introduction

S. aureus is a widely distributed opportunistic pathogen in nature, primarily transmitted to humans through environmental contact and contaminated food. It frequently colonizes the skin and mucosal surfaces of both healthy individuals and animals. According to epidemiological studies, approximately 30% of the healthy human population carries this bacterium in their nasal passages. Beyond localized colonization, S. aureus is capable of causing a range of infections, including pneumonia and osteomyelitis, as well as severe, life-threatening conditions such as sepsis and infective endocarditis [1, 2]. Notably, bovine-derived S. aureus has become a potential source of human infections. Logue et al. discovered that MRSA strains may arise from humans or specific strains associated with cattle. Kreausukon et al. detected MRSA in bulk tank milk, revealing the food safety risks posed by contaminated dairy products entering the human food chain [3, 4]. This pathogen exhibits significant genetic and phenotypic diversity, which confers remarkable environmental adaptability and antibiotic resistance [5]. In the livestock sector, bovine-derived S. aureus is a leading cause of bovine mastitis, incurring substantial economic losses. As a zoonotic pathogen, it threatens both food safety and public health. Transmission occurs through contaminated dairy products or direct contact, potentially leading to foodborne illnesses and severe infections in humans [6, 7].

MRSA was first discovered in the UK in 1961, signaling a new era in antimicrobial resistance. Its global prevalence then grew steadily in the subsequent years. In 1972, MRSA entered the veterinary record with its identification in bovine mastitis cases [8], a disease with serious consequences for animal health and milk production, compounded by the risk of bacterial toxins to humans. By 1975, Devriese et al. reported MRSA in a broader host range, including swine, cattle, and cats [9]. A pivotal confirmation of zoonotic transmission occurred in 1988, when Scott et al. isolated MRSA from a pet cat belonging to an MRSA-positive patient, providing definitive proof of human-animal transmission [10, 11].

The interaction between bovine and human S. aureus involves host-adapted lineage evolution, bidirectional spillover, and the exchange of antimicrobial resistance and adaptive genes. During spillover, the pathogen adapts by shedding virulence factors and acquiring new host-specific genes. This adaptation reveals complex transmission dynamics and establishes cattle as both recipients and sources of MRSA [12], making bovine-derived S. aureus a major One Health challenge that requires enhanced surveillance and targeted interventions.

The development of antimicrobial resistance in S. aureus is a multifactorial process, primarily involving the inappropriate use of antibiotics, acquisition of resistance genes, and biofilm formation. Resistance and virulence genes are frequently carried by mobile genetic elements (MGEs), facilitating their dissemination through horizontal gene transfer (HGT) among bacterial populations, thereby accelerating the spread of resistance and enhancing pathogenic potential [13–15]. Of particular significance is the role of biofilm formation as a key strategy in S. aureus resistance. Biofilms, structured bacterial communities encased in extracellular polymeric substances (EPS) comprising proteins, DNA, and polysaccharides, protect host immune responses and confer tolerance to antimicrobial agents, making biofilm-associated infections particularly challenging to eradicate [16, 17]. Additionally, clinical and laboratory-derived vancomycin-intermediate S. aureus (VISA) strains often exhibit characteristic cell wall thickening and attenuated virulence [18], suggesting adaptive evolutionary strategies in response to antibiotic pressure. However, research on S. aureus cell wall modifications and their relationship to resistance remains limited, hindering a comprehensive understanding of its resistance mechanisms [19].

Current research on S. aureus remains largely centered on strains from mastitis and dairy products, with limited focus on microbial communities in cattle manure [20]. Yet, under intensive farming systems, contaminated manure may serve as a transmission route for S. aureus via water, feed, or environmental exposure. Inadequate manure management could further establish these pathogens as reservoirs for mastitis and other infections, while facilitating the dissemination of antibiotic resistance genes. Enhanced surveillance of these pathways is thus essential not only for sustainable livestock production but also for controlling the spread of resistant bacteria, ensuring food safety, and protecting public health [21]. More systematic research and targeted interventions are urgently needed.

Therefore, to systematically investigate the prevalence of S. aureus exhibiting multidrug resistance (including MRSA) and robust biofilm-forming ability in dairy farm environments (feces) and at the product source (raw milk), and to assess the resulting public health threat, we conducted this survey and analysis on dairy farms in Northeast China. This study provides a scientific foundation for developing targeted interventions at the source to enhance dairy cattle production performance, ensure the safety of animal-derived foods, and contain antimicrobial resistance dissemination.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and isolation of S. aureus strains

From 2017 to 2021, a total of 282 clinical samples were collected from Northeast China, including 263 fecal samples and 19 mastitis case samples.All sampled animals were lactating dairy cows aged between 3 and 7 years, clinically healthy at the time of sampling, and had not received any antibiotic treatment within 14 days prior to sample collection. The fecal samples were obtained from cattle farms in Heilongjiang and Jilin provinces during two periods (2017–2018 and 2020–2021), with the feces being loose and yellow-black in color. The mastitis samples were collected from dairy farms in Inner Mongolia and Heilongjiang province between 2020 and 2021, with most of the milk samples appearing chylous and some showing a light yellow color. A total of 8 dairy farms were included in the study, located across three provinces in Northeast China: Heilongjiang (n = 3), Jilin (n = 3), and Inner Mongolia (n = 2). All samples were stored in 50 mL centrifuge tubes at 4 °C, transported to Northeast Agricultural University, and frozen at −80 °C for further processing.

Milk samples (200 µL) were spread onto blood agar plates (Qingdao Haibo Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) and incubated at 37 °C for 18 h. Colonies exhibiting β-hemolysis were selected and streaked onto fresh blood agar for purification. For fecal samples, 1 g of feces was homogenized in 1 mL of PBS buffer, and 100 µL of the suspension was inoculated into 10% NaCl-supplemented LB broth (Qingdao Haibo Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) followed by incubation at 37 °C with shaking (180 rpm) for 18 h. The cultures were then streaked onto Baird-Parker agar (Qingdao Haibo Biotechnology Co., Ltd.), and black colonies surrounded by transparent zones were subcultured on blood agar.

The isolates were subjected to Gram staining, coagulase test and biochemical identification. Genomic DNA was extracted using a bacterial DNA extraction kit, and nuc gene of S. aureus was specifically amplified using nuc in Table 1. The PCR assay was carried out in a 25 µL reaction volume, consisting of 12.5 µL of 2× EsTaq MasterMix (Toyobo Co., Ltd., Osaka, Japan), 2 µL of template DNA, 1 µL of each primer (nuc-F and nuc-R, 10 µM), and 8.5 µL of nuclease-free water. The thermal cycling program included an initial denaturation at 94 °C for 5 min, followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 30 s, annealing at 59 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 30 s, with a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min, and termination at 4 °C. Finally, the isolate was stored in LB medium containing 60% (Vol/vol) glycerol and stored at −80 ° C for future use.

Primer sequences

Staphylococcal protein A typing

The staphylococcal protein A (spa) gene was amplified using spa primers in Table 1. The PCR products were purified and subsequently sequenced by Jilin Kumei Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Changchun, China). The obtained sequences were analyzed in the 5′→3′ direction to identify the X-region of the spa gene, which was flanked by the conserved sequences RCAMCAAAA- (upstream) and -TAYATCGT (downstream). The X-region sequences were segmented into 24-bp repeat units and submitted to the Ridom SpaServer (https://www.spaserver.ridom.de/) for automated spa typing analysis.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing of S. aureus

The antibiotic susceptibility profiles of the isolates were determined using the standard the agar disk diffusion on Mueller-Hinton agar (MHA) according to CLSI guidelines (CLSI M100-S30 2020). A panel of 12 antimicrobial agent representing nine distinct classes was tested: rifampicin (RIF, 5 µg), co-trimoxazole (SXT, 23.75/1.25 µg), penicillin G (PEN, 10 µg), oxacillin (OXA, 1 µg), cefdinir (FOX, 30 µg), amikacin (AMI, 30 µg), gentamicin (GEN, 10 µg), ciprofloxacin (CIP, 5 µg), tetracycline (TET, 30 µg), clindamycin (CLI, 2 µg), chloramphenicol (CHL, 30 µg), and erythromycin (ERY, 15 µg) (Qingdao Haibo Biotechnology Co., Ltd.).Bacterial cultures were grown to the logarithmic phase, and the turbidity of the suspensions was adjusted to a 0.5 McFarland(OD625: 0.08–0.125) standard using sterile physiological saline. The suspensions were then uniformly swabbed onto MHA plates under aseptic conditions. Antibiotic disks were aseptically placed on the agar surface with appropriate spacing, and the plates were incubated at 35 ± 2 °C for 18 ± 2 h. Following incubation, the inhibition zone diameters were measured and interpreted according to CLSI breakpoints. S. aureus ATCC25923 as quality control strains in each assay.

Identification of antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs)

To assess the antimicrobial resistance profiles, 11 antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) were screened in the isolated S. aureus strains using PCR.The targeted genes encompassed resistance determinants for several antibiotic classes: aminoglycosides [aac(6’)/aph(2’’) and aph(3’)-III], tetracyclines (tetK and tetM), β-lactams (mecA and blaZ), rifampin (ropB), quinolones (grlB and gyrB), and macrolides (ermA and ermC). PCR reactions were performed in a 25 µL volume containing 12.5 µL of 2× EsTaq MasterMix (Toyobo Co., Ltd., Osaka, Japan), 1 µL each of forward and reverse primers (sequences and annealing temperatures provided in Table 1), 2 µL of DNA template, and 8.5 µL of nuclease-free water. The amplification protocol consisted of an initial denaturation at 94 °C for 2 min, followed by 30 cycles of denaturation (94 °C, 30 s), annealing (gene-specific temperature, 30 s), and extension (72 °C, 30 s), with a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. Amplification products were separated by electrophoresis through 1% agarose gels.

Congo red test

The biofilm-forming ability of the isolated bacterial strains was assessed using Congo red agar (CRA) plates, which was prepared by dissolving glucose (20 g), agar (15 g), peptone (10 g), NaCl (5 g), beef extract (3 g), and Congo red (0.8 g) in 1000 mL of deionized water. The pH was adjusted to 7.0–7.2 before autoclaving. Bacterial cultures were streaked onto CRA plates and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Taking the standard strain S. aureus ATCC25923 as the standard strain, the colonies that produce biofilms are black, dry and crystalline, while those that do not form biofilms are pink or red.

Semi-quantitative crystal Violet (CV) assay

Bacterial strains were cultured in TSB(Qingdao Haibo Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) supplemented with 1% glucose. Aliquots (200 µL) of bacterial suspension were dispensed into 96-well polystyrene microtiter plates, with S. aureus ATCC 43,300 serving as the positive control and sterile 1% glucose TSB as the blank control. After 48 h of incubation at 37 °C, non-adherent cells were removed by washing three times with sterile PBS. Biofilms were fixed with 200 µL of 95% methanol (Qingdao Haibo Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) for 20 min, washed twice with PBS, and stained with 200 µL of 0.1% crystal violet (Solarbio, Beijing, China) for 30 min. Excess stain was removed by three PBS washes, and the bound dye was solubilized in 200 µL of 33% acetic acid.The optical density at 570 nm (OD570) was measured using a SpectraMax ABS microplate reader (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA).

Real time-qPCR

Bacterial DNA was extracted using a genomic DNA kit (Cowin Biotech, China). qPCR was performed using Power SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) with the following cycling conditions: 95 °C for 10 min; 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 1 min. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method [22]. Primer sequences are listed in Table 1.

Electron microscopy

Overnight cultures grown in LB broth at 37 °C with shaking (180 rpm) were harvested by centrifugation (5,000×g, 3 min) and washed three times with PBS. Bacterial pellets were fixed overnight at 4 °C in 2.5% glutaraldehyde, followed by three washes with 0.1 M PBS (pH 7.2). Post-fixation was performed with 1% osmium tetroxide for 2 h at 4 °C. After additional PBS washes, samples were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (50%, 70%, 90%, and 100%), followed by a 1:1 mixture of 100% ethanol and 100% acetone (3 min per step at 4 °C). Final dehydration was accomplished with pure acetone for 5 min at room temperature.For embedding, samples were infiltrated with epoxy resin using incremental acetone-resin ratios (1:1 for 45 min, 1:2 for 90 min, and 1:4 overnight at room temperature). Using a Leica ultra-thin slicer, samples were sequentially sectioned and subjected to dual staining: first with 2% uranyl acetate, then with lead citrate. Subsequently, the prepared samples were examined using a transmission electron microscope (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted in triplicate to ensure reproducibility and reliability. The data are presented as mean values ± standard error (SE). Statistical analyses and graphical representations were performed using Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, USA). The t-test was used to compare the optical density (OD) values in the biofilm experiment. X2-test was conducted on the data of antimicrobial resistance.The ΔΔCT values of qPCR gene expression analysis were analyzed by t-test. P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, ***P ≤ 0.001, **P ≤ 0.01, *P ≤ 0.05 and ns > 0.05.

Results

Prevalence of S. aureus strains

In this study, a total of 282 clinical samples were collected from cattle farms across three northern provinces in China (Heilongjiang, Jilin, and Inner Mongolia). The samples included 160 fecal samples from cattle farms in Heilongjiang during 2017–2018, 103 fecal samples from cattle farms in Heilongjiang and Jilin during 2020–2021, and 19 mastitis milk samples from dairy farms in Inner Mongolia and Heilongjiang during 2020–2021. The prevalence of S. aureus is illustrated in Fig. 1, with a total of 62 S. aureus isolates identified. Among these, the positive rates were 21.9% (35/160) for fecal samples collected in 2017–2018, 17.5% (18/103) for fecal samples collected in 2020–2021, and 47.4% (9/19) for mastitis milk samples collected in 2020–2021. The isolation and identification results of S. aureus are shown in Figure S1. The results showed that S. aureus was more prevalent in mastitis samples than in fecal samples.

Discussion

Existing research has primarily focused on the characteristics of strains in mastitis cases and dairy products, with insufficient attention paid to bovine feces. Notably, the persistent presence of S. aureus in the mammary environment is closely related to its unique biological characteristics, including biofilm formation, intracellular survival mechanisms, and phenotypic switching [23]. These traits contribute to treatment failures and recurrent mastitis episodes. More importantly, bovine populations serve as a significant reservoir for antibiotic-resistant S. aureus, with these resistant strains capable of being transmitted to humans through direct or indirect contact [24, 25]. Research indicates that S. aureus can achieve cross-species adaptation by losing original host-related genes and acquiring new host-specific genes. Among these, livestock-associated MRSA (LA-MRSA) is considered an important source of community-associated MRSA (CA-MRSA) [26, 27]. Multiple epidemiological studies have further confirmed the transmission risk of bovine-derived S. aureus. A systematic review by Camino González-Machado et al. revealed that MRSA was detected in 68.8% of dairy product studies and 73.8% of bulk milk surveys, suggesting that contamination may originate from the animals themselves or the processing environment [28]. Additionally, a study by Asmaa Sadat et al. involving 700 raw milk samples found S. aureus in 288 of those samples, with 113 strains carrying the PVL toxin gene. This affirmed that the milking process, contamination from sick animals, and improper handling are significant sources of contamination. These findings underscore that S. aureus in bovine feces is a crucial source of dairy product contamination, highlighting the necessity and urgency for intensified related research [29]. In this study, the high detection rate of S. aureus in bovine mastitis samples underscores its role as a primary pathogen in this condition. Additionally, the presence of S. aureus in dairy cattle feces provides valuable data supporting potential infection routes [30].

Typing techniques for S. aureus, including phenotypic and molecular methods, are essential for understanding strain-relatedness, tracing pathogen origins, and conducting epidemiological investigations [31]. Among these, Spa typing is a widely used molecular typing method due to its simplicity and cost-effectiveness. Compared to studies from other regions, Spa typing of S. aureus may exhibit regional and host-specific variations [32–34]. In this study, the main epidemic type of S. aureus isolated was in contrast to the findings of Parisa asadollahi et al. This contrasts with findings by Parisa Asadollahi et al., who reported t037, t030, and t002 as the predominant types in Asia, with t030 being the most prevalent in China [35]. This discrepancy may reflect regional differences in the distribution of S. aureus types across China. These findings highlight the importance of regional surveillance and typing of S. aureus to better understand its epidemiology and inform targeted control measures. The predominance of t034 in this study suggests potential regional specificity in strain distribution, which warrants further investigation to elucidate the underlying factors driving these differences.

With the widespread use of antimicrobial agent, the problem of drug resistance of S. aureus is becoming increasingly serious, especially with the emergence of MRSA, which aggravates its harm. Therefore, the monitoring of S. aureus drug resistance has become particularly important [36]. Studies have shown that MRSA shows high resistance to a variety of antimicrobial agent, especially β-lactam antibiotics (such as penicillin), with a resistance rate of up to 100%, and MRSA has significantly higher multiple resistance than MSSA. For example, Ting Gan et al. found that MRSA showed varying degrees of resistance to 11 antibacterial drugs, while the multiple resistance rate of MSSA was 80.66% [37]. Meng Dan also showed that MRSA had a higher resistance rate to penicillin, cefoxitin, and tetracycline, but a lower resistance rate to rifampin and nitrofurantoin [38].MRSA isolated in this study also showed resistance to a variety of antibiotics, and its resistance was more serious than MSSA. Therefore, it is particularly important to monitor S. aureus and MRSA in real time.

The development of drug resistance in S. aureus is mainly attributed to the irrational use of antimicrobial agent, the acquisition of drug-resistance genes, and the formation of biofilms. Resistance genes can be carried by chromosomes or plasmids and transferred under antibiotic induction by mobile genetic elements, encoding inactivating enzymes, modifying enzymes, efflux pump genes, or protective proteins, thereby making bacteria resistant to antimicrobial agent [39–42]. However, some strains carry resistance genes but are sensitive to related antibiotics, which may be related to their heterogeneous resistance or transient gene expression [43].In this study, multiple drug resistance genes were detected in the isolated S. aureus. Among them, the carrier rate of the tetracycline resistance gene tetM was the highest (100%), followed by the aminoglycoside resistance gene aph(3’)-Ⅲ (98.4%). The carrying rates of other genes such as tetK, ermC, aac(6’)/aph(2”), grlB and ropB ranged from 74.2% to 85.5%. The results indicated that these strains had a rich reserve of resistance genes to TET, AMI and QNS. Mechanism analysis indicates that tetM (ribosomal protective protein), tetK (effector pump), and aph(3’)-Ⅲ and aac(6’)/aph(2”) (chemically modified enzymes) are the main mechanisms leading to resistance to tetracyclines and aminoglycosides, respectively. However, a key finding is that although the detection rates of genes such as tetM, aph(3’)-Ⅲ and aac(6’)/aph(2”) are extremely high, the corresponding antibiotic phenotypic resistance rates are relatively low, that is, there are a large number of gene-positive but phenotypic sensitive strains.This indicates that the detection of resistance genes alone cannot fully predict the phenotypic outcome, and its expression may be affected by regulatory mechanisms or epigenetic factors.

Biofilms protect bacteria from antibiotics through mechanisms such as physical barriers, quorum-sensing systems, and efflux pumps [44, 45]. Studies have shown that intercellular polysaccharide adhesin (PIA) and regulatory genes (such as icaA, SarA, CidA) play a key role in biofilm formation [46–48]. In this study, the expression levels of icaA, SarA and CidA genes in medium and strong adhesion biofilm strains were significantly higher than those in weak adhesion strains, suggesting that these genes may be the main regulators of biofilm formation. In this study, the drug resistance of strains forming biofilms with different degrees of adhesion was compared. The resistance rates of strains forming strong adhesion biofilms to RIF, SXT, PEN, CHL, OXA and FOX were higher than those forming weak adhesion biofilms, especially rifampicin, with a resistance rate of 47.7% for strains forming strong adhesion biofilms. The proportion of strains that form weakly adherent biofilms is 0%. However, in this study, only four strains produced weakly adherent biofilms. Therefore, more samples need to be obtained for comparison to determine whether the formation of biofilms can lead to multidrug resistanc. In addition, this study found no significant difference in cell wall thickness between MRSA and MSSA through transmission electron microscopy, indicating that changes in cell wall thickness may not be the main factor mediating MRSA. Or only in specific bacteria (e.g., vancomycin-mediated resistant S. aureus) [49, 50].

Furthermore, the results of this study show that there is partial overlap in spa typing between the S. aureus isolated from milk samples and the strains from feces, for instance, both types t034 and t518 were jointly detected. It is worth noting that although milk isolates showed higher levels in terms of the types of drug resistance, the carrying rate of drug resistance genes, and the ability to form biofilms, the drug resistance gene profiles carried by the strains from the two sources were still highly similar. This discovery suggests that under modern intensive farming models, there may be new transmission routes between S. aureus in different habitats (such as milk and feces), thereby promoting the cross-flow of genes related to drug resistance and environmental adaptation.

Conclusion

In this study, S. aureus and MRSA were prevalent in dairy cow samples from Northeast China, with the t034 strain emerging as the dominant type. Most isolates exhibited multiple drug resistance, harbored multidrug resistance genes, and demonstrated biofilm-forming capability. We further identidied icaA, SarA, and CidA genes were identified as key regulators of biofilm formation. Given these findings, optimizing dairy farm management practices and modifying prevention strategies against S. aureus becomes imperative. Our study provides critical insights into the impact of S. aureus contamination on cow health management and the safety of dairy-derived food production.In view of the close contact between farm personnel, animals and the environment, these strains may pose risks to human health and endanger public health and safety through direct exposure or environmental pollution.
