Cardiovascular magnetic resonance-derived left atrial parameters for assessing diastolic dysfunction and prognosis in hypertrophic cardiomyopathy
Authors: Weiwei Liao, Jianping Zhong, Wenjian Tang, Daoxiong Xiao, Yongming Tan, Xiangqiang Liu, Youlin Fu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEchocardiography is the mainstay for assessing left ventricular diastolic dysfunction (LVDD) in hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM). Given that routine cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) lacks guideline-recommended valvular hemodynamic parameters, this study aimed to evaluate the diagnostic and prognostic value of CMR left atrial (LA) parameters for guideline-defined LVDD.MethodsThis dual-center study included 248 HCM patients and 70 controls, all of whom underwent both echocardiography and CMR. Patients were categorized into normal left atrial pressure (LAP) (normal or Grade I LVDD) and elevated LAP (Grade ≥ II LVDD) groups per echocardiographic guidelines. Volumetric and CMR-FT strain analyses quantified left heart function. LA parameters' diagnostic ability for elevated LAP and their prognostic value were assessed.ResultsCompared with controls, HCM patients showed significantly impaired LA reservoir (εs), conduit (εe), and booster pump (εa) strains, which progressively deteriorated with LAP elevation (LVDD progression), while LA volumes progressively increased. LA parameters diagnosed elevated LAP superior to LV indices, with εs showing the highest efficacy [AUC=0.903, cutoff=27.95%]; combining εs with LAVImin improved the AUC to 0.929. Multivariable Cox analysis showed εs (HR: 0.867), LAVImin (HR: 1.025), and LV-LGE burden (HR: 1.103) were independent predictors of heart failure-related composite events, and the combined model demonstrated a C-index of 0.852. Furthermore, both εs and the HCM-AF score independently predicted incident atrial fibrillation, with εs outperforming the HCM-AF score (C-index: 0.802 vs 0.734).ConclusionCMR LA parameters are sensitive markers for LVDD with elevated LAP in HCM, providing significant clinical value for risk stratification and prognostic assessment.Keywords: hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, CMR, CMR-FT, left ventricular diastolic dysfunction, left atrial, left atrial pressure

Introduction

Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) is the most common inherited cardiovascular disorder, with an estimated prevalence ranging from 1:500–1:200 in adults [1], [2], and remains one of the leading causes of sudden cardiac death in young individuals. HCM was once regarded as a classic monogenic sarcomeric disease; however, it is now recognized that the genetic basis of many patients is substantially more complex, with only 30%–40% harboring sarcomeric mutations [2], [3]. Phenotypically, HCM is characterized by heterogeneous patterns of left ventricular (LV) hypertrophy accompanied by myocardial disarray and interstitial fibrosis. Although LV systolic function is often preserved, left ventricular diastolic dysfunction (LVDD) and elevated filling pressures are highly prevalent owing to impaired LV relaxation and increased myocardial and chamber stiffness [4]. Given the close association of LVDD with heart failure with preserved ejection fraction and adverse clinical outcomes in HCM, and the fact that nearly half of patients may progress to develop heart failure symptoms [5], [6], early and accurate assessment of diastolic dysfunction is essential for improving risk stratification and prognosis.

In patients with HCM, transthoracic echocardiography remains the primary non-invasive modality for evaluating LVDD. Clinical assessment follows the 2022 American Society of Echocardiography (ASE) recommendations for HCM [4], which adopt the structured diagnostic algorithm from the 2016 ASE/European Association of Cardiovascular Imaging guidelines [7]. This framework utilizes key parameters—including maximum left atrial (LA) volume index (LAVImax), E/e’ ratio, and peak tricuspid regurgitation velocity—to estimate whether LAP is elevated, thereby assessing LV filling pressures and the degree of LVDD. This streamlined approach is both efficient and robust, with its diagnostic accuracy validated against invasive hemodynamic measurements in large-scale cohort studies [8]. Furthermore, evidence suggests that LA strain is more sensitive than conventional volumes in reflecting diastolic-related LA remodeling, identifying elevated LAP (Grade ≥ II LVDD) with greater precision [9], [10]. Supported by established thresholds, its integration into LVDD assessment is now increasingly recommended [11], [12], [13].

Compared with echocardiography, cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging is regarded as the "gold standard" for quantifying cardiac morphology and function due to its superior spatial resolution, multi-parametric quantification, and independence from acoustic windows [14]. Although LA strain analysis via CMR feature tracking (CMR-FT) is a more recent development, it has demonstrated substantial value in the early detection of functional abnormalities and risk stratification across various cardiovascular diseases [15], [16], [17]. However, CMR-based diastolic evaluation remains relatively limited, largely because routine CMR protocols often lack guideline-recommended valvular hemodynamic parameters required for LVDD assessment. Furthermore, the systematic evolution of CMR-derived LA functional and strain parameters in relation to progressive diastolic worsening has not been fully elucidated. Therefore, this study aimed to quantify CMR-derived LA functional and strain parameters in HCM patients to evaluate their utility in identifying elevated LAP/LVDD and to determine their prognostic value for adverse cardiovascular events.

Methods

Study design and population

This two-center retrospective study consecutively enrolled patients with HCM at Ganzhou People’s Hospital and the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University between January 2018 and June 2024. Inclusion criteria were (1) a clinical diagnosis of HCM, defined as a maximum LV end-diastolic wall thickness ≥ 15 mm (or ≥ 13 mm in those with a confirmed family history) in the absence of other cardiac or systemic conditions explaining the hypertrophy [1]; (2) a preserved LVEF ≥ 50%; and (3) completion of both CMR and echocardiography within a five-day interval. Patients were excluded based on the following: (1) known coronary artery disease, significant valvular heart disease, or prior cardiac surgery; (2) chronic systemic diseases potentially affecting cardiac function (e.g., hyperthyroidism or renal failure); (3) poor CMR image quality or incomplete follow-up data; and (4) insufficient echocardiographic parameters to determine LAP status according to guidelines. Enrolled patients were stratified based on the recommendations for HCM [4], utilizing parameters including E/e’ > 14, peak tricuspid regurgitation velocity > 2.8 m/s, and LA volume index > 34 mL/m². Accordingly, patients were assigned to the HCM (Normal LAP) group (indicating normal or Grade I LVDD) or the HCM (Elevated LAP) group (indicating Grade ≥ II LVDD). Simultaneously, an age- and sex-matched control group of healthy individuals was recruited, all of whom had normal CMR, echocardiography, and electrocardiogram findings, with no history of cardiovascular disease, diabetes, or malignancy. Baseline clinical characteristics and medication history for all participants were retrieved from electronic medical records. Additionally, the HCM-AF score was calculated for each patient with HCM based on baseline parameters, including LA diameter, New York Heart Association functional class, age at diagnosis, and age at clinical evaluation [18], [19].

CMR protocol

All CMR examinations were performed on a 3.0T scanner (Skyra, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) using an 18-channel phased-array body coil and electrocardiographic gating. Participants were scanned in the supine position and underwent breath-hold training prior to the examination. Cine imaging was performed using a balanced steady-state free precession (TrueFISP) sequence, including LV two-chamber and four-chamber long-axis views and contiguous short-axis slices covering the entire LV. Key acquisition parameters were: repetition time (TR)/echo time (TE) = 2.9–3.5 ms/1.3–1.5 ms, flip angle = 42°–55°, field of view = 350 mm × 350 mm, matrix = 256 × 192, and slice thickness = 8 mm. Late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) imaging was conducted 10–15 min after the intravenous administration of a gadolinium-based contrast agent (Gadopentetate dimeglumine [Magnevist], Bayer Healthcare, Leverkusen, Germany) using a phase-sensitive inversion recovery sequence, with slice positions identical to those used for cine imaging. The key LGE parameters included: TR = 750 ms, TE = 2.1 ms, flip angle = 20°, and slice thickness = 8 mm.

CMR image analysis

All CMR images were analyzed offline using CVI42 software (version 5.17.2, Circle Cardiovascular Imaging, Calgary, Alberta, Canada) by two radiologists with over 5 years of cardiovascular imaging experience, both blinded to clinical data. Based on cine sequences, LV endocardial and epicardial contours at end-diastole and end-systole were automatically delineated and manually adjusted on short-axis slices to obtain LVEF, as well as body surface area (BSA)-indexed end-diastolic volume index (EDVI), end-systolic volume index (ESVI), stroke volume index (SVI), cardiac index (CI), and LV mass index (LVMI). Maximal wall thickness (MWT) was manually measured as the maximum end-diastolic thickness across 17 segments. Subsequently, LV three-dimensional global radial (GRS), circumferential (GCS), and longitudinal strain (GLS) were calculated by delineating long-axis contours and integrating them with the previously completed short-axis contours within the strain module (Supplementary Figure S1). LGE was quantified on the LGE short-axis images by defining enhancement as signal intensity ≥ 5 standard deviations (5-SD) above the mean of remote normal myocardium, with the LGE extent expressed as a percentage of total LV myocardial mass.

LA volumes were measured using the biplane area-length method on two-chamber and four-chamber long-axis cine images. After automatic tracking and manual adjustment of LA contours, volume parameters throughout the cardiac cycle were obtained to derive BSA-indexed maximum (LAVImax), minimum (LAVImin), and pre-atrial contraction (LAVIpre) volume indices. LA emptying fractions (EF) were calculated as follows[20]: LA Total EF = (LAVImax − LAVImin) / LAVImax × 100%；LA Passive EF = (LAVImax − LAVIpre) / LAVImax × 100%；LA Booster EF = (LAVIpre − LAVImin) / LAVIpre × 100%. LA strain analysis was performed in the dedicated strain module. Endocardial and epicardial borders were manually delineated, with the LA appendage and pulmonary veins excluded. The analysis yielded reservoir strain (εs), conduit strain (εe), and booster pump strain (εa). Additionally, corresponding peak strain rates were derived, including peak positive strain rate (SRs), early diastolic negative strain rate (SRe), and late diastolic negative strain rate (SRa) (Fig. 2).

Follow-up

The primary endpoint was a heart failure (HF)-related composite endpoint, including HF-related death, HF-related hospitalization, and cardiac transplantation. For patients with multiple adverse events, only the first event was included in the primary analysis. The secondary endpoint was incident atrial fibrillation (AF), assessed independently of nonfatal primary events. Incident AF was defined as newly developed AF (either symptomatic or asymptomatic) documented on a standard 12-lead ECG (10 seconds) or lasting 30 s or more on single-lead or multiple-lead ECG devices during the follow-up period [21]. Outcome data were obtained from electronic medical records and routine telephone follow-up.

Reproducibility analysis

The reproducibility of LV and LA strain parameters was evaluated using images randomly selected from 30 HCM patients and 10 healthy controls. Inter-observer variability was assessed based on the independent measurements of two blinded radiologists. To determine intra-observer variability, a single radiologist repeated the analysis on the same images after a one-month interval.

Statistical analysis

Normality and variance homogeneity were assessed using Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Levene’s tests, respectively. Continuous data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range); three-group comparisons (elevated LAP, normal LAP, and controls) were performed via one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Bonferroni correction or Kruskal-Wallis tests with Dunn’s post-hoc tests. Two-subgroup comparisons used independent-samples t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests. Categorical data [frequencies (percentages)] were evaluated using Chi-square or Fisher's exact tests. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves evaluated CMR LA parameters for identifying elevated LAP, and univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to identify independent predictors of elevated LAP.

For the primary composite endpoint, independent predictors were identified using Cox regression; variables with clinical significance or univariable P < 0.10 were included after eliminating multicollinearity via the variance inflation factor (VIF). Model performance was assessed using Harrell's C-index and time-dependent ROC curves. For incident AF, Fine–Gray models accounted for competing risks of HF-related death and cardiac transplantation. LA parameters' incremental value over the HCM-AF score was evaluated using the ΔC-index and likelihood ratio test. Reproducibility of CMR-FT strain measurements was quantified using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs). Analyses used IBM SPSS (version 27.0) and R (version 4.3.3), with a two-sided P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline clinical characteristics

A total of 318 subjects were finally enrolled, including 70 healthy controls and 248 patients with HCM (comprising 128 in the HCM [normal LAP] group and 120 in the HCM [elevated LAP] group), whose baseline clinical characteristics and medications are summarized in Table 1. The mean age was 49.57 ± 15.74 years (78 males) in the HCM (normal LAP) group, 55.28 ± 13.17 years (67 males) in the HCM (elevated LAP) group, and 51.21 ± 12.78 years (44 males) in the healthy controls. Statistical analysis showed that while both HCM subgroups had significantly higher SBP than the healthy controls, no significant differences were observed among the three groups regarding sex, DBP, HR, BMI, BSA, or history of smoking and alcohol consumption. Further comparison between the HCM subgroups revealed that the HCM (elevated LAP) group was characterized by a significantly higher proportion of hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy (HOCM), an older mean age, and more frequent use of beta-blockers, calcium channel blockers, and statins compared to the HCM (normal LAP) group. However, no significant differences were found between the two HCM subgroups regarding creatinine levels, eGFR, the prevalence of hypertension, diabetes, and hyperlipidemia, or a family history of HCM.

Comparison of CMR parameters

Cardiac structural and functional parameters for all groups are detailed in Table 2. Although LVEF remained preserved and comparable across the three groups, CMR analysis revealed substantial LV remodeling and functional impairment in HCM patients. Particularly in the HCM (elevated LAP) group, EDVI, SVI, LVMI, and MWT were significantly higher than in both the HCM (normal LAP) and healthy control groups (all P < 0.05). Regarding LV mechanics, GLS, GCS, and GRS were significantly impaired in HCM patients compared with healthy controls, demonstrating a significant stepwise deterioration across the three groups (all P < 0.05). Furthermore, the LV-LGE prevalence was significantly higher in the HCM (elevated LAP) group than in the HCM (normal LAP) group (65.8% vs. 46.9%, P < 0.001). Similarly, the LV-LGE burden was significantly more severe in the elevated LAP group, both when evaluated in the whole cohort and specifically among patients with present LV-LGE.

Regarding LA volume parameters (Table 2 and Fig. 3E–F), LAVImax, LAVImin, and LAVIpre were significantly increased in both HCM groups compared with healthy controls, with the most pronounced expansion observed in the HCM (elevated LAP) group (all P < 0.001). Notably, LA strain parameters (Fig. 3A–C) revealed a stepwise deterioration in LA function: εs was significantly reduced across the healthy control, HCM (normal LAP), and HCM (elevated LAP) groups (43.12 ± 6.63% vs. 34.19 ± 5.99% vs. 24.15 ± 4.78%, respectively; all P < 0.001). Similarly, εe (19.58 ± 5.60% vs. 17.09 ± 5.25% vs. 11.86 ± 2.99%) and εa (23.54 ± 5.84% vs. 17.09 ± 3.51% vs. 12.29 ± 3.17%) demonstrated consistent and significant decreasing trends (all P < 0.05). Additionally, analysis of corresponding strain rate parameters showed significant differences among the three groups for SRs and SRe, with the exception of SRa. Collectively, these parameters effectively reflect LA structural remodeling and dysfunction in HCM patients, which show progressive aggravation under conditions of elevated LAP/LVDD.

Diagnostic performance of LA parameters for elevated LAP

ROC curve analysis demonstrated the superior diagnostic value of LA parameters in distinguishing patients with HCM (elevated LAP) from those with HCM (normal LAP) (Table 3 and Fig. 4). Among all individual parameters, LA εs exhibited the highest discriminatory capacity, with an AUC of 0.903 (95% CI: 0.860–0.937). Using a cutoff value of 27.95%, LA εs yielded a diagnostic sensitivity of 89.1% and a specificity of 75.8%, significantly outperforming LA volume indices and LV functional parameters. Notably, the diagnostic performance of LAVImin surpassed that of the guideline-recommended LAVImax (AUC: 0.832 vs. 0.784). Furthermore, a combined indicator integrating LA εs and LAVImin further enhanced diagnostic accuracy, yielding an AUC of 0.929 (95% CI: 0.889–0.958), with a sensitivity of 83.5% and a specificity of 90.6%.

Independent predictive value of LA parameters for elevated LAP

To further verify the independent diagnostic value of LA parameters and adjust for potential confounders, including age and the HOCM phenotype, multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed (Supplementary Table S1). In the univariable analysis, age, HOCM, LVMI, LV-LGE burden, and LV GLS were significantly associated with elevated LAP (all P < 0.05). However, after adjustment for these variables in the multivariable model, LA εs (OR: 0.734, 95% CI: 0.657–0.819; P < 0.001) and LAVImin (OR: 1.157, 95% CI: 1.075–1.245; P < 0.001) remained the only independent predictors of elevated LAP.

CMR parameters and clinical outcomes

During a median follow-up of 28.5 months (IQR: 19.0–52.6 months), 56 patients (22.6% [56/248]) experienced the heart failure-related composite endpoint (incidence rate: 7.3 events per 100 person-years), including 7 HF-related deaths, 1 HF-related heart transplantation, and 48 HF hospitalizations. Compared with the event-free group (Table 4), patients in the event group were significantly older (P = 0.004), had a higher prevalence of HOCM (46.4% vs 18.2%, P < 0.001), and showed a greater prevalence of elevated LAP (83.9% vs 38.0%, P < 0.001).

Regarding CMR parameters, the event group exhibited more pronounced LV remodeling and myocardial injury, as evidenced by significantly higher LVMI and MWT than the event-free group (all P < 0.05). In addition, the event group showed a higher prevalence of LV-LGE (78.6% vs 49.5%, P < 0.001) and a significantly greater LV-LGE burden, along with significantly impaired LV deformation parameters (GRS, GCS, and GLS; all P < 0.05). In the LA assessment, LA volume indices (LAVImax, LAVImin, and LAVIpre) were significantly increased in the event group. Furthermore, except for SRa, all LA strain and strain rate parameters, including εs, εe, εa, SRs, and SRe, were significantly lower in the event group (all P < 0.05).

For the secondary outcome, incident AF occurred in 53 patients (21.4% [53/248]) during a median follow-up of 33.7 months (IQR: 20.6–56.7 months), yielding an incidence rate of 6.32 events per 100 person-years. As shown in Supplementary Table S2, the incident AF group was characterized by older age, a higher proportion of the HOCM phenotype, an elevated HCM-AF score, increased LA volume indices (LAVImax, LAVImin, and LAVIpre), and significantly impaired LA strain parameters (εs, εe, and εa) (all P < 0.05).

Independent predictors and prognostic performance for clinical outcomes

Table 5 summarizes the Cox regression analysis results for the primary composite endpoint. Univariable analysis revealed that age, HOCM, LVMI, MWT, LV deformation parameters (GRS, GCS, and GLS), LV-LGE burden, all LA volume indices (LAVImax, LAVImin, and LAVIpre), and LA strain parameters (εs, εe, and εa) were significantly associated with the primary endpoint. After adjusting for confounding variables and eliminating multicollinearity—with the VIFs of the finally included variables ranging from 1.133 to 3.969—multivariable analysis identified LV-LGE burden (HR: 1.103, P = 0.001), LAVImin (HR: 1.025, P = 0.013), and εs (HR: 0.867, P = 0.005) as independent predictors. The combined model demonstrated excellent prognostic performance with a C-index of 0.852 (95% CI: 0.806–0.899) and robust discriminative stability on time-dependent ROC curves (1-, 2-, and 3-year AUCs: 0.871, 0.884, and 0.879; Fig. 5A). Moreover, Kaplan–Meier analysis stratified by the optimal εs threshold of 27.95% for differentiating elevated LAP showed a significantly lower event-free survival probability in the high-risk group (log-rank P < 0.001; Fig. 5B).

To account for the competing risks of HF-related death and cardiac transplantation, a Fine–Gray model was employed to analyze incident AF (Table 6). Reproducibility of CMR strain parameters

CMR-based strain analysis for both LV and LA parameters demonstrated high reproducibility (Supplementary Table S3). Inter-observer agreement was good to excellent (ICC: 0.839–0.935). Intra-observer reproducibility was even more robust, with all metrics exceeding 0.85 and reaching a maximum of 0.953 for LV GCS.

Discussion

This work provides a comprehensive evaluation of CMR-derived LA parameters and their prognostic value in patients with HCM. Our analysis yielded several primary findings: First, during early diastolic dysfunction, even when LAP remains within the normal range, CMR-derived LA parameters can sensitively detect subclinical structural remodeling and functional impairment in patients with HCM. Second, LA reservoir strain (εs) demonstrated superior diagnostic efficacy in identifying elevated LAP (Grade ≥ II left ventricular diastolic dysfunction), significantly outperforming conventional metrics and LV strain. Third, εs, LAVImin, and LV-LGE burden were identified as robust, independent predictors of heart failure-related composite events in this cohort. Finally, εs independently predicted incident AF, with its discriminative capacity outperforming the clinical HCM-AF score and demonstrating a significant incremental prognostic yield.

Assessment value of CMR LA parameters for diastolic function in HCM

LA is anatomically and hemodynamically coupled with LV, modulating LV filling and overall cardiac performance through reservoir, conduit, and booster pump functions. Assessment of LA morphology and function has attracted increasing attention in the diagnosis and risk stratification of cardiovascular diseases. In HCM and other cardiovascular conditions, structural and functional LV remodeling is frequently concomitant with elevated filling pressures and LVDD. Driven by retrograde pressure transmission and the compensatory requirement to sustain LV filling, the LA undergoes adaptive increases in volume and pressure. Notably, these adaptive alterations in LA function often precede detectable LV dysfunction [22], [23]. Accordingly, LA characteristics represent pivotal biomarkers for the early identification and severity assessment of diastolic impairment. They are integrated into clinical guidelines as essential criteria for identifying elevated LAP/Grade ≥ II LVDD [4], [7] and provide critical prognostic insights in heart failure [23], [24].

LV remodeling in HCM is characterized by hallmarks such as myocardial hypertrophy, fibrosis, and LV outflow tract obstruction (LVOTO); these changes can be reflected through multidimensional quantitative metrics provided by CMR and CMR-FT. Our findings demonstrate that HCM patients, irrespective of LAP status, exhibit significantly higher LVMI and MWT compared to healthy controls, alongside impaired LV strain parameters. As LAP elevation/LVDD progressed, these structural and strain abnormalities became increasingly accentuated, accompanied by an increased LGE burden and LVOTO prevalence. Importantly, in the subclinical phase where LAP remains normal, LA volume parameters were already significantly increased compared to controls and showed further expansion in patients with elevated LAP. This confirms that LA remodeling is initiated during the early stages of functional impairment in HCM [23]. As LV end-diastolic pressure continuously increases, the LA is driven from early compensatory dilation toward late pathological remodeling; this atrioventricular uncoupling ultimately establishes a vicious cycle between LVDD and adverse LA remodeling.

Compared with LA volume metrics—which reflect cumulative structural remodeling secondary to chronic pressure overload—LA strain parameters demonstrate superior sensitivity and accuracy in reflecting diastolic deterioration. Specifically, strain parameters capture early intrinsic myocardial deformation impairment and directly quantify functional alterations across all LA phases. Our study found that, with the exception of SRa, all LA strain metrics (εs, εe, εa, SRs, and SRe) were significantly lower in both HCM subgroups than in healthy controls. This finding highlights that diastolic impairment in HCM comprehensively involves all three LA functional phases, with these strain indices showing a progressive decline as LAP increases. ROC analysis further confirmed that εs was the strongest individual predictor (AUC = 0.903), significantly outperforming conventional volume and LV strain indices. Additionally, LAVImin, reflecting the LA end-emptying status, demonstrated better discriminatory power than LAVImax, which represents the limit of pressure-induced expansion. The combination of εs and LAVImin further enhanced the diagnostic performance to an AUC of 0.929. Notably, multivariable analysis confirmed that this diagnostic value remains independent of the HOCM phenotype, age, LV-LGE burden, and LVGLS, establishing these parameters as reliable imaging biomarkers for identifying LVDD.

Prognostic value of CMR parameters for adverse outcomes in HCM

In clinical research, multiple routine CMR parameters are widely utilized for risk stratification in HCM, among which LGE burden is extensively recognized for its prognostic value. LGE on CMR reflects myocardial replacement fibrosis, a hallmark pathological feature of HCM [25], [26]. Evidence indicates that LGE is detectable in up to 70% of HCM patients, and its extent is closely linked to disease progression, sudden cardiac death, and HF [26], [27]. A previous study of 345 patients with HCM identified CMR-based LV volume and functional parameters alongside LGE as significant prognostic predictors [28]. Our findings reaffirm that LV-LGE burden is a robust, independent predictor of HF-related adverse outcomes in HCM. Regarding LA volumetric metrics, growing evidence suggests that compared with LAVImax, LAVImin reflects elevated left ventricular filling pressures and impaired LA compensatory function with greater sensitivity and accuracy, demonstrating superior correlation with diastolic dysfunction [29], [30] and enhanced prognostic capability for adverse clinical outcomes in HCM [24]. In the present work, LAVImin not only effectively identified diastolic dysfunction but also emerged as a powerful independent predictor of an adverse HF-related prognosis. Given that the measurement feasibility of LAVImin on standard CMR protocols is entirely comparable to that of LAVImax, it holds significant promise as a more sensitive and practical clinical index for detecting subclinical cardiac dysfunction and guiding risk tiering.

Recent evidence suggests that strain parameters offer superior prognostic value compared with conventional volumetric and functional metrics [31], [32], [33]. Furthermore, while LV GLS is established as an important independent predictor of adverse outcomes [33], [34], multivariable analyses across various cardiac diseases indicate that LA strain may possess even greater prognostic efficacy. Di Zhou et al. [22] noted that LA strain is a more sensitive and representative indicator for the early assessment of myocardial injury, holding clear value in predicting HCM outcomes, whereas the prognostic significance of LV strain was less pronounced in certain cohorts. Similarly, Anne et al. [35] demonstrated in patients with dilated cardiomyopathy that CMR-derived LA conduit strain is a strong independent prognostic predictor of adverse events, demonstrating superiority over LV GLS. In our work, after integrating multidimensional information—including LV strain, conventional cardiac functional metrics, and clinical risk markers—LA reservoir strain remained the most robust independent predictor for both HF-related adverse events and incident AF in patients with HCM, a conclusion consistent with the findings of Thomas Zegkos et al.[36] using speckle-tracking echocardiography. Furthermore, the HCM-AF score has recently emerged as a validated, disease-specific clinical tool for predicting AF in HCM. Our findings demonstrate that both LA reservoir strain and the HCM-AF score are independent predictors of incident AF; crucially, LA reservoir strain exhibited superior discriminative capacity over the clinical score, demonstrating a substantial incremental prognostic yield.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be noted. First and foremost, the assessment of elevated LAP relied on echocardiographic algorithms rather than invasive hemodynamic measurements. We acknowledge the inherent variability in echo-derived LAP estimation; early studies reported a moderate correlation (r=0.44) between the LAP predicted by the single-parameter E/e′ ratio and invasively assessed LAP in HCM [37]. While multi-parametric structured assessments recommended by established guidelines show improved diagnostic accuracy (up to 87%) [8], they remain surrogates for the gold standard. Due to the limited number of patients undergoing invasive catheterization in our cohort, future prospective studies are required to further validate the correlation between CMR parameters and invasive LAP. Additionally, despite the dual-center design, the total sample size was relatively limited due to strict inclusion criteria, which may introduce selection bias. Furthermore, owing to the retrospective nature of this study, certain auxiliary parameters for refined diastolic function classification, such as pulmonary vein flow duration, were not consistently available for all patients. Finally, although CMR-FT technology demonstrates high reproducibility, the current manual post-processing workload and inter-vendor algorithmic variations remain potential barriers to its widespread adoption.

Conclusions

This work demonstrates that CMR-derived LA parameters offer exceptional value in identifying cardiac dysfunction and predicting an adverse prognosis in patients with HCM. LA reservoir strain (εs), particularly when combined with LAVImin, is highly effective for identifying left ventricular diastolic dysfunction associated with elevated left atrial pressure; additionally, εs, LAVImin, and LV-LGE burden are robust independent predictors of heart failure-related composite events. Furthermore, εs is also an independent predictor of incident AF. Therefore, LA parameters, particularly reservoir strain, should be integrated into the assessment of left ventricular diastolic function and prognosis in patients with HCM.
