Ethical readiness and trust in artificial intelligence–assisted anaesthesia: A cross-sectional survey study among anaesthesia professionals in India
Authors: Tiajem Jamir, Ram Singh, Prakash Gondode, Kunal Aggarwal, Sudhansu Sekhar Nayak, Prashant Sirohiya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and Aims:Perioperative anaesthesia is progressively integrating decision support systems based on artificial intelligence (AI). Safe AI use requires not only technical validation but also ethical preparedness and trust to uphold patient autonomy, informed consent, and clinician oversight. This study evaluated ethical readiness and trust towards AI integration among anaesthesia professionals using a cross-sectional survey.Methods:Anaesthesia professionals across Indian government, private, and corporate hospitals completed a structured, content-validated, and pilot-tested self-administered questionnaire assessing five domains. AI Ethical Trust and Readiness Index (AETRI) was derived as a composite score. Group differences, associations, and predictors were analysed using Mann–Whitney U/Kruskal–Wallis tests, Pearson's correlation, and multiple linear regression, respectively.Results:A total of 255 anaesthesia professionals participated, including 44.7% junior residents, 36.9% senior residents, 11.0% consultants, and 7.4% faculty. Most (69.4%) worked in government hospitals. The mean ± standard deviation domain scores were: Knowledge (K) 0.73 ± 0.21, Utility and Trust (UT) 3.41 ± 0.56, Ethical Readiness (ER) 3.62 ± 0.61, Transparency and Control (TC) 3.66 ± 0.64, and Institutional Support (IS) 1.86 ± 0.58. The mean AETRI was 57.2 ± 9.43, indicating moderate readiness. Knowledge varied by designation (P = 0.029), with higher scores among faculty and consultants. Faculty demonstrated higher AETRI than residents (P = 0.011). ER and TC were the main predictors of AETRI (R2 = 0.52).Conclusion:Anaesthesia professionals demonstrated moderate AI knowledge and trust but limited institutional readiness. Ethical readiness and transparency, rather than technical knowledge alone, are the primary determinants of clinician preparedness for AI integration.

INTRODUCTION

Artificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly being used in perioperative practice, including hypotension prediction and anaesthesia decision-support. Early studies suggest potential benefit in haemodynamic management, although broader validation using patient-centred outcomes is still needed.[12] As AI moves into clinical practice, anaesthesiologists need to interpret and critically assess its recommendations. However, many AI models functioning as “black box” systems with limited explainability can make unexpected outputs difficult to understand and justify, creating ethical and professional challenges.[3] Uncertainty about disclosing AI use may affect patient autonomy and informed consent,[4] while unclear responsibility can lead to overreliance on AI or its complete rejection.[5] Although clinicians remain cautiously optimistic, concerns persist about data quality, bias, transparency, medicolegal issues, and skill erosion.[6] Reviews in perioperative and critical care settings similarly emphasise the need for continued human oversight, highlighting gaps in ethical governance, data protection, fairness, and accountability even when AI performs well in trials.[7] However, evidence on how anaesthesia professionals, particularly in low- and middle-income settings, perceive and ethically engage with AI remains limited. Existing research has focussed mainly on AI literacy rather than ethical readiness, which includes judging contextual appropriateness, overriding AI outputs, documenting its use, and communicating risks to patients.[8] Variability in digital systems, training programmes, infrastructure, and regulatory frameworks may further influence India’s readiness for AI-enabled perioperative care.

Prior research on AI in anaesthesia has largely focussed on technical performance or general attitudes, with limited evidence on ethical preparedness. In India, diverse healthcare systems, variable digital infrastructure, and evolving regulations emphasise the need to understand clinicians’ trust, preparedness, and support requirements. This study addresses these gaps through AI Ethical Trust and Readiness Index (AETRI). It assesses anaesthesia professionals’ knowledge, attitude, trust, transparency, ethical readiness, and institutional support regarding AI-assisted decision-making. It also examines differences across professional and institution groups, relationship with AETRI, and perceived barriers to AI adoption.

METHODS

The study is a descriptive, cross-sectional, questionnaire-based survey of anaesthesia professionals in India. The analyses were confined to explaining patterns and associations; no causal or deterministic relationships were inferred given the cross-sectional design. The institutional ethics committee provided the ethical clearance for the study (AIIMSA-4746/2025). The study employed a non-probability accessibility sampling approach with elements of snowball dissemination using institutional mailing lists, professional networks, and social media groups. The survey link was disseminated through institutional mailing lists, departmental contacts, professional anaesthesia networks, and anaesthesia-focussed social media groups, including WhatsApp-based academic discussion forums. Because the survey link was openly forwardable across multiple digital platforms, the exact number of institutions, groups, or individuals exposed to the survey could not be reliably quantified. Consequently, a conventional denominator and response rate could not be calculated. To minimise duplicate responses, only one submission per Google account was permitted, sign-in was required for survey submission, and a response to all items was required for submissions. Email identifiers were not exported with responses. Internet Protocol (IP) addresses were not collected. Aggregate metadata were screened for obvious duplicate entries, and no apparent duplicate submissions were identified. All responses were collected anonymously in accordance with institutional ethical standards from September 2025 to the end of December 2025.

The study included anaesthesia professionals (senior residents, consultants, faculty, and junior residents with at least 6 months of supervised clinical anaesthesia experience) who were currently involved in perioperative care, teaching, or decision-making. Only respondents who could understand the English language were eligible. “Prior AI exposure” was defined a priori as one or more of the following: a) Formal training (structured workshop/course/certificate), b) Clinical use (AI-driven monitoring/decision support), c) Academic exposure (research/teaching), and d) General-use tools (e.g., chat-based, analytic tools, large language models). Undergraduate students, interns, non-anaesthesia specialists, and individuals exclusively in administrative roles without active clinical responsibilities were not eligible to participate. In the absence of prior Indian data on ethical readiness towards AI in anaesthesia, we assumed a conservative expected proportion of 50%. We calculated the sample size using the OpenEpi online sample size calculator (version 3) for single proportions. With a 95% confidence level, 10% absolute precision, and a design effect of 1.0, the software estimated a minimum sample size of 97. To account for potential incomplete or unusable responses, we inflated the target sample size to approximately 100–120 participants. Through broad dissemination, we obtained 255 complete post-pilot responses, increasing the precision of estimates.

A content-validated and pilot-tested self-administered questionnaire was developed based on literature on AI in anaesthesia and critical care, ethical frameworks for medical AI (including autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, and justice),[9] World Health Organization (WHO) digital health ethics guidance,[10] and existing surveys assessing clinician attitudes towards AI in perioperative medicine.[6] The items were organised into five domains: Knowledge (K), capturing conceptual understanding of AI tools; Utility and Trust (UT), assessing perceived usefulness, reliability, and willingness to incorporate AI outputs; Ethical Readiness (ER), evaluating comfort with explaining AI use, assessing fairness, and balancing AI recommendations with clinical judgment; Transparency and Control (TC), addressing concerns about “black-box’’ behaviour, explainability, and clinician override; and Institutional Support (IS), reflecting training availability, access to AI platforms, legal clarity, and departmental culture. As ethical readiness for AI-assisted anaesthesia is considered multidimensional, including individual knowledge, trust, ethical judgement, supervisory control, and institutional preparedness, these domains were prioritised. Response formats included dichotomous items, multiple-choice items, and 5-point Likert scales (strongly disagree to agree strongly).

A multidisciplinary panel of senior anaesthesiologists and AI researchers assessed content validity for relevance, clarity, and representativeness. The questionnaire was pilot-tested in 55 participants to ensure clarity and reasonable completion time (≤ 15 min). Cronbach’s alpha was 0.53 for UT, 0.50 for ER, and 0.69 for TC, indicating limited to moderate internal consistency. UT and ER addressed broader concepts of trust and ethical readiness, which likely contributed to the lower alpha values. Cronbach’s alpha was not assessed for the K and IS domain because of their response formats. The pilot data were excluded from the final analysis. The questionnaire was considered content-validated and pilot-tested but not fully psychometrically validated. Given the exploratory nature of the instrument and the multidimensional constructs assessed, this pilot-testing provided an appropriate initial assessment of its internal consistency.

Anonymised data were exported from the Google Forms to Microsoft Excel and analysed using Jamovi (version 2.6). Continuous variables were summarised as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median [interquartile range (IQR)] based on the Shapiro–Wilk test, and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. K was calculated as the mean of four binary items (0–1), while scores for UT, ER, TC and IS were derived from their respective 5-point Likert items. These domains were combined into the AETRI and expressed as a percentage (0–100%). Participants could select multiple barriers and enablers from a list of relevant potential factors. Group differences were assessed using the Mann–Whitney U or Kruskal–Wallis tests, as appropriate. The associations among domains were examined using Pearson’s correlation coefficient, and independent predictors of AETRI were identified using multiple linear regression. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

RESULTS

A total of 255 anaesthesia professionals completed the survey. The mean age of the respondents was 31.4 ± 6.3 years, and 53.7% were female. The cohort comprised junior residents (44.7%), senior residents (36.9%), consultants (11.0%), and faculty (7.4%). Most respondents worked in government hospitals (69.4%), followed by private (17.6%) and corporate/academic institutions (13.0%). 61.2% of participants showed prior AI exposure [Table 1]. Participants revealed moderate to high scores in most domains, except IS, which was lowest across the cohort. The composite AETRI mean score was 57.2 ± 9.43, indicating moderate overall readiness [Table 2].

DISCUSSION

With applications expanding from automated monitoring to haemodynamic prediction, image analysis, and clinical decision support, AI is gradually driving perioperative practice.[11121314] When technologies begin to direct intraoperative management, successful integration requires not only technical reliability but also clinical preparedness, ethical awareness, and institutional readiness.[151617] Here, we underscored prevailing insights and structural barriers by providing a distinctive assessment of ethical trust and readiness for AI adoption among Indian anaesthesia professionals. The majority of participants expressed favourable opinions and a reasonable understanding of AI’s potential, which is in line with international data demonstrating interest in predictive algorithms and AI-assisted monitoring.[61618] However, prior AI exposure did not influence knowledge or AETRI scores. This finding may reflect the nature of current ‘AI exposure’ which may represent superficial or non-clinical interactions such as chat-based tools or passive conference exposure.[319] Such exposure does not necessarily translate into clinical understanding, ethical reasoning, or decision-making competence. Therefore, to improve readiness, structured, context-specific training is likely needed rather than passive exposure. Readiness appears to depend less on familiarity and more on structured training. The high perceived UT in our cohort mirrors international findings that clinicians believe AI can reduce workload and augment vigilance.[1618]

A key finding of this study is that ER and TC were the main predictors of AETRI, suggesting that readiness is more strongly shaped by clinicians’ confidence in supervising AI recommendations than by baseline knowledge. ER encompasses fairness, accountability, bias, and shared decision-making, issues repeatedly emphasised in recent reviews of AI in anaesthesia.[2021] Transparency relates to explainability, interpretability, and the ability to identify algorithmic errors. Studies have shown that opaque “black-box’’ systems undermine clinician trust, hinder risk communication, and complicate informed consent.[42223] Longstanding calls for explainable AI (XAI), clear override mechanisms, and transparent documentation pathways to minimise automation bias are supported by the predictive power of ER and TC.[52122]

The IS domain had the lowest score in the current survey, indicating systemic barriers to AI adoption. Participants noted concerns about a lack of medico-legal clarity, inadequate training pathways, and limited access to validated tools; these issues were echoed in reviews conducted across multiple health systems.[1524] When AI is used to support clinical decisions, even high-income regions acknowledge the uncertainty surrounding governance, operational responsibility, and liability.[52223] These difficulties are intensified in low- and middle-income environments by variable digital infrastructure and evolving legal frameworks.[910] In our study, faculty had higher knowledge and AETRI scores, possibly reflecting their greater clinical experience and exposure to medico-legal and academic discussions concerning AI. Studies from Canada, Europe, the United States, and the Middle East have shown similar designation-linked disparities.[61618] The predominance of residents and the mean age of 31.4 ± 6.3 years reflect that the greater participation of younger professionals may partly be related to higher engagement with the online platforms through which the questionnaire was circulated; however, this was not specifically assessed. The lack of variations among institution types implies that barriers to AI readiness are not sector-specific but rather widespread.

The strong correlation between ER and Transparency (r=0.47) indicates that clinicians who value ethical governance also prioritise explainability. This is consistent with the behavioural science literature that demonstrates how transparency builds trust, supports accountability, and facilitates the acceptance of AI-derived recommendations.[172225] The fact that UT and ethical domains have moderate correlations highlights that trust in AI is fundamentally ethical rather than just technical.

Overall, the findings of our study support the global evidence that safe AI integration requires much more than just clinical interest. When AI is used to guide clinical decisions, reviews often highlight the risks of biased outputs, challenges with informed consent, and persistent uncertainties about accountability and liability.[202122232426] Complexity is increased in India due to variations in infrastructure, documentation practices, and regulatory clarity. These findings support the need for national guidance, institutional governance frameworks, and ethics-focussed training to ensure the safe and responsible use of AI in perioperative care.[5161720212728] While the knowledge, attitudes, and ethical concerns regarding AI have been addressed in previous studies, the novelty of our study focusses on ethical readiness, transparency, clinical judgment, and institutional support as distinct dimensions among Indian anaesthesia professionals. It has highlighted the domain-specific gaps, providing a practical baseline for assessing preparedness for integrating AI-assisted tools into anaesthesia practice.

The survey findings have several limitations that should be addressed. First, selection bias may have been introduced by using a non-probability sampling technique with online distribution, which would have favoured participation by individuals with greater interest in or familiarity with AI. It was not possible to determine the precise number of people who had access to the survey because it used open online dissemination with forwardable links across social media groups and professional networks. Therefore, a conventional response rate and formal assessment of non-response bias were not feasible. The limited representation of senior practitioners may not adequately reflect their views. Although the survey was disseminated nationally, it was not geographically stratified; therefore, balanced representation across regions and cities could not be assumed. The cohort featured a higher proportion of respondents from government institutions, which might have reduced the representation of viewpoints from corporate and private healthcare settings. The results are based on self-reported responses from participants, which may have been influenced by personal bias or a propensity to provide socially acceptable answers. Although the questionnaire underwent content validation, pilot testing, and exploratory internal consistency assessment, comprehensive psychometric evaluation including construct validity assessment, factor analysis, and test–retest reliability was not undertaken. Therefore, the instrument should be viewed as exploratory and may require further validation before larger longitudinal or multicentre deployment. Finally, variations in infrastructure, training systems, and regulatory frameworks across regions may influence the applicability of our results to different healthcare settings.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, this study suggests that anaesthesia professionals in India are broadly receptive to AI-assisted care in perioperative practice but report only moderate overall readiness and limited institutional support. Ethical Readiness and Transparency and Control were the main predictors of AETRI, highlighting the importance of clinician oversight, explainability, and ethical judgement alongside technical competence. However, given the cross-sectional design, and limited psychometric validation of the questionnaire, these associations should be interpreted cautiously and should not be considered causal. Further multicentre studies using robustly validated instruments and longitudinal or implementation-based approaches are needed to determine whether structured ethics-focused training, clearer institutional governance, and defined accountability mechanisms can translate into greater readiness for safe and responsible AI use in anaesthesia.
