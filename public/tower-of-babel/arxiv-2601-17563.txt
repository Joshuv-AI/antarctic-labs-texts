Towards Generalisable Imitation Learning Through Conditioned Transition Estimation and Online Behaviour Alignment Conference: Proc. of the 25th International Conference on Autonomous Agents and Multiagent Systems (AAMAS 2026); May 25 – 29, 2026; Paphos, Cyprus 294
Nathan Gavenski, Matteo Leonetti, Odinaldo Rodrigues

Abstract
. State-of-the-art imitation learning from observation methods (ILfO) have recently made significant progress, but they still have some limitations: they need action-based supervised optimisation, assume that states have a single optimal action, and tend to apply teacher actions without full consideration of the actual environment state. While the truth may be out there in observed trajectories, existing methods struggle to extract it without supervision. In this work, we propose Unsupervised Imitation Learning from Observation (UfO) that addresses all of these limitations. UfO learns a policy through a two-stage process, in which the agent first obtains an approximation of the teacher’s true actions in the observed state transitions, and then refines the learned policy further by adjusting agent trajectories to closely align them with the teacher’s. Experiments we conducted in five widely used environments show that UfO not only outperforms the teacher and all other ILfO methods but also displays the smallest standard deviation. This reduction in standard deviation indicates better generalisation in unseen scenarios.

1.
Introduction

One way to learn how to perform a task is to observe someone else execute it and then attempt to imitate their actions.
In computer science, this is called
imitation learning
(IL), where an agent observes a “teacher”.
Unlike other learning approaches, IL leverages the teacher’s guidance, thus avoiding blindly learning by trial-and-error.

Learning from demonstration
(ILfD) is the most common approach for IL, where the agent uses sequences of states and actions performed by the teacher.
In practice, it suffers from two significant drawbacks: poor generalisation in environments with multiple solutions and the scarcity of action-labelled data
(
11
)
.

Learning from observation
(ILfO) mitigates the latter by learning a task with access to only sequences of states via self-supervision.
This allows agents to use much more easily obtainable data, such as video recordings of game-playing.

ILfO typically employs one of two approaches:
behavioural cloning
(BC) or
adversarial imitation learning
(AIL).
BC seeks to match a teacher’s actions by minimising a supervised loss, while AIL aims to make the agent’s behaviour indistinguishable from that of the teacher.
To avoid ILfD’s generalisation problem, BC assumes each state in the environment can be reached by only one optimal action, which is not always the case.
To address this and correct the agent’s behaviour, AIL collects samples via
online play
(direct environment interaction).
Yet, these strategies still have shortcomings.
BC tends to overfit, and AIL can fall into
behaviour-seeking
mode (blindly mimicking the teacher’s behaviour, irrespective of the actual environment state).
This leads to poor performance in environments that require the underlying action intent or the causal relationships between actions and outcomes for success.

To tackle these shortcomings, recent works
(
12
;
23
)
use self-supervision to approximate the teacher’s behaviour, with others employing an artificial reward function
(
34
;
21
)
.
However, these works still assume that states can be reached only by a single action, rely on an action-based loss function, and exhibit behaviour-seeking due to reliance on the quality and coverage of the data samples.

In this work, we introduce Unsupervised Imitation Learning from Observation (UfO), a novel unsupervised approach to imitation learning from observation.
Unlike prior ILfO methods
(
12
;
23
;
21
;
31
)
, which rely on supervised losses to infer or reconstruct teacher actions, UfO learns without action-based supervision.
Instead, it derives its understanding of behaviour from the causal structure of state transitions.
The main contribution of UfO is the reconstruction stage, where a policy and a generative model are jointly trained to approximate the environment’s dynamics through mutual optimisation: the policy seeks actions that best condition the generative model to reproduce the teacher’s transitions, and the generative model refines its predictions based on environment transitions.
This unsupervised coupling eliminates dependence on action-level supervision while constraining learning to behaviourally meaningful transitions, mitigating the behavioural-seeking bias common in ILfO.
A subsequent adversarial refinement stage further improves policy generalisation through online play, enabling UfO to surpass the teacher’s performance and outperform all competing methods across five benchmark environments.
Together, these results establish UfO as the first method to achieve effective unsupervised imitation purely from observation, without relying on supervised action inference or external rewards.

2.
Background

In this work, we assume the environment to be a Markov Decision Process (MDP)
{M}=\langle{S,A,T,r,\gamma}\rangle
, in which
S
is the state space,
A
is the action space,
T
is a transition function
T:S\times A\rightarrow S
,
r
is the immediate reward function, and
\gamma
is the discount factor
(
28
, Ch. 3)
.

Solving an MDP yields a policy
{\pi}
with a probability distribution
P
over actions.
In each state
s
,
P(a\mid s)
is the probability of taking action
a
in
s
.
For simplicity, we assume that
{\pi}(s)
will determine the action the agent takes in state
s
, i.e., the action
a
such that
P(a\mid s)
is the highest.
We will differentiate policies via appropriate subscripts.

The objective of IL is to generate an agent policy
{\pi_{\theta}}
that approximates the teacher’s policy
{\pi_{\psi}}
from a set of ‘trajectories’ representing the behaviour of the teacher solving the task. Trajectories may consist of sequences of state-action pairs or simply sequences of state transitions. We will use the symbol
\mathcal{T}
to denote the set of all trajectories, irrespective of their type. Notice that IL does not assume access to the teacher’s reward function or discount factor.

If the trajectories include the actions performed by the teacher, imitation learning becomes a supervised learning problem, known as
behavioural cloning
(BC), optimising:

(1)

\argmin_{\theta}\textstyle\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}}\sum_{(s,a)\in\tau}{\mathcal{L}}(a,{\pi_{\theta}}(s)).

Above, we minimise the supervised loss function
{\mathcal{L}}
(such as the mean square error) between the action
a
the teacher chooses at state
s
(annotated in a trajectory
\tau
) and the action
{\pi_{\theta}}(s)
the agent policy thinks it should take.
Yet, relying on annotated actions from a teacher might not be feasible.
In some cases, such as when the teacher’s data consists of video recordings, it might be impossible to retrieve which action was actually taken.
Therefore, ILfO assumes that trajectories contain only sequences of states and generate agent policies based solely on these sequences.
The two most common ILfO approaches are behavioural cloning and adversarial imitation learning (see
13
for details).

2.1.
Behavioural Cloning Methods

Behavioural Cloning for ILfO tries to generate the agent policy using
inverse dynamics models
(IDM) and
forward dynamics models
(FDM).
IDM tries to predict the action responsible for a transition (
s,s^{\prime}
),
where FDM tries to model
T
directly.
The disadvantage of these dynamic models is that although the MDP consists of a single function
T
, they rely on learning one of many possible transition functions
T^{\prime}\subseteq T
.
IL methods that rely on IDM
(
12
;
23
)
use teacher’s trajectories
\tau_{\psi}
to create
self-supervised
actions
\tilde{a}\in\{\text{IDM}(s,s^{\prime})\mid(s,s^{\prime})\in\tau_{\psi}\}
to train their policies using Eq.
1
.
On the other hand, methods that rely on FDM use the teacher’s trajectories coupled with action
(
6
)
or temporal
(
24
)
information to condition the next state generation and optimise the agent using:

(2)

\argmin_{{\theta}}\argmin_{\phi}\textstyle\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}}\sum_{(s,s^{\prime})\in\tau}{\mathcal{L}}(s^{\prime},\mathcal{G}_{\phi}(s,{\pi_{\theta}}(s))),

where
\mathcal{G}_{\phi}
is a generative model approximating
T
, and
{\mathcal{L}}
is still a supervised loss function used to minimise the loss between the actions chosen by the teacher and agent.

These methods rely on the assumption that the teacher transition function is an
injective function
, i.e.,
T(s_{i},a_{i})\neq T(s_{j},a_{j})
, whenever
(s_{i},a_{i})\neq(s_{j},a_{j})
.
However, this rarely holds in practice, resulting in the learning process encouraging the agent to favour a single action during a transition (even when multiple alternatives are viable).
Hence, the agent tends to learn disproportionately skewed policies.
The inability to give the correct weights to action alternatives in unseen scenarios often leads to failure
(
29
)
.
We will show how to tackle this issue in Section
3.1
.

2.2.
Adversarial Imitation Learning Methods

Adversarial imitation learning employs a
discriminator
that attempts to distinguish between the actions chosen by the agent and those chosen by the teacher for states encountered during online play.
Instead of learning an action mapping function similar to BC, it tries to approximate the teacher policy by how the agent behaves.
AIL can be further divided into two categories: (i)
inverse reinforcement learning
, and (ii)
generative adversarial learning
.

Most AIL approaches learn by minimising a variation of the adversarial loss from
15

(
15
)
:

(3)

\begin{split}\argmin_{\omega}\argmax_{\theta}\!\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}}\!\sum_{s\in\tau}log(\mathcal{D}_{\omega}\!\left(s,{\pi_{\psi}}\!\left(s\right)\right)\!+log(1\!\!-\!\!\mathcal{D}_{\omega}\!\left(s,{\pi_{\theta}}\!\left(s\right)\right)\end{split}

where
\mathcal{D}_{\omega}
is a
discriminator model
that identifies whether a pair (
s,a
) is from the teacher or from the agent.
It is important to note that Eq.
3
also does not assume access to
{\pi_{\psi}}
.
It only requires the agent’s policy to follow the behaviour of the teacher’s.
Therefore, to “fool” the discriminator, the agent attempts to follow the teacher’s trajectories as closely as possible.
For inverse reinforcement learning approaches,
\mathcal{D}_{\omega}
trains to predict a value between
\left[0,1\right]
, where
0
means the model thinks
(s,a)
is from the agent and
1
means it is from the teacher.
Generative adversarial learning methods use the gradient from
\mathcal{D}_{\omega}
to teach the policy how to behave as the teacher.
For ILfO methods, Eq.
3
uses state transitions
(s^{\prime},s)
rather than state-action pairs to discriminate.

Dependence on adversarial losses requires IL approaches to rely heavily on the teacher samples’ coverage and quality.
By forcing the agent to behave precisely as its teacher, adversarial methods often fall into behaviour-seeking mode
(
34
)
— trying to emulate the teacher’s behaviour too closely and forcing a trajectory that might not be appropriate in the current state of the environment.
To make things worse, IL methods attempt to reduce the number of teacher samples to lower the learning cost (for greater
efficiency
), amplifying this issue further.
Section
3.2
shows how UfO employs AIL sparingly to refine the agent’s policy without causing it to blindly mimic the teacher’s behaviour.

3.
Unsupervised IL from Observation

In this work, we propose Unsupervised Imitation Learning from Observation, a novel IL technique that avoids the need for action-based supervised optimisation, the reliance on the assumption of injective transition functions and the behaviour-seeking mode.
UfO is an unsupervised imitation learning method composed of:

(i)

a policy model
{\pi_{\theta}}
to learn to predict the best action given a state
s
;

(ii)

a
conditioned
generative model
\mathcal{G}_{\phi}
to generate the next state
\mathcal{G}_{\phi}(s,{\pi_{\theta}}(s))
, given a state
s
and the action
{\pi_{\theta}}(s)
; and

(iii)

a discriminator model
\mathcal{D}_{\omega}
to discriminate between agent and teacher trajectories
\mathcal{D}_{\omega}(\tau_{\pi_{\theta}}||\tau_{\pi_{\psi}})
.

UfO initialises all models with random weights and collects teacher trajectories
\mathcal{T}_{\pi_{\psi}}

In the reconstruction stage (Lines

In the subsequent adversarial stage (Lines

), UfO refines the policy to more closely resemble the teacher’s.

3.1.
Reconstruction Stage

The reconstruction stage (illustrated in Fig.
1
a) trains both generator and policy based on the teacher transition function and the agent transition function
\mathcal{T}_{\pi_{\theta}}
.
It does so, by iteratively
freezing

\mathcal{G}_{\phi}
and
{\pi_{\theta}}
, and training each model by using
\mathcal{T}_{\pi_{\theta}}
and
\mathcal{T}_{\pi_{\psi}}
, respectively.
This part of the training runs for
\text{epochs}_{r}
, which is significantly higher than the second stage.

For the policy portion of the training (Lines
6
-
8
), UfO uses the teacher samples to learn how to properly condition the generator based on the teacher’s transitions following:

(4)

\argmin_{\theta}\textstyle\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}_{\pi_{\psi}}}\sum_{(s,s^{\prime})\in\tau}\mid s^{\prime}-\mathcal{G}_{\phi}\left(s,{\pi_{\theta}}\left(s\right)\right)\mid.

This part aims to enable the policy to learn how to accurately predict an action that allows the generator to determine the teacher’s next state based on its learned transition function.
In other words, given that
\mathcal{G}_{\phi}
learns a sufficiently approximate transition function
T^{\prime}\subseteq T
, if the agent predicts the same action that the teacher would choose, the next state should also be similar –
T(s,{\pi_{\psi}}(s))\approx\mathcal{G}_{\phi}(s,{\pi_{\theta}}(s))
.

For the generator part of the training (Lines
11
-
13
), UfO uses the agent samples collected from the environment to learn how to approximate the MDP’s transitions using:

(5)

\argmin_{\phi}\textstyle\!\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}_{\pi_{\theta}}}\!\sum_{s\in\tau}\mid T(s,{\pi_{\theta}}(s)),\mathcal{G}_{\phi}(s,{\pi_{\theta}}(s)))\mid.

Due to the lack of knowledge about actions, it is impossible to approximate the transition function based on the teacher’s observations alone. This part aims to enable the generator to learn how the MDP’s transition behaves through transitions caused by the agent in the environment.

Therefore, by intercalating policy and generator training, UfO learns an approximate transition function that is leveraged to learn a policy similar to the teacher’s.
As the agent approximates its policy to the teacher, so do its trajectories, leading the generator to fine-tune itself to similar transitions.
In other words, since the agent replicates trajectories close to the teacher and the generator only updates its weights via online play (i.e., by interacting with the environment), UfO ensures a positive correlation between the teacher and agent models.
Specifically, to minimise Eq.
4
, the policy must predict an action that generates the same next state
\mathcal{G}_{\phi}(s,{\pi_{\theta}}(s))=\mathcal{G}_{\phi}(s,{\pi_{\psi}}(s))
, and to minimise Eq.
5
, the generator has to correctly predict the next state based on the current state of the environment and the action predicted by the policy
\mathcal{G}_{\phi}(s,{\pi_{\theta}}(s))=T(s,{\pi_{\theta}}(s))
.
By not having any supervised losses, the policy must learn for itself which actions align more with the teacher’s next expected state and better understand how different values affect the transition function from
\mathcal{G}_{\phi}
.
Hence, the loss function does not introduce bias into the policy’s action predictions, thereby addressing the supervised loss limitations of ILfO approaches.
Moreover, UfO circumvents the reliance on injective transition functions by learning from state transitions
P(s^{\prime}\mid s,{\pi_{\theta}}(s))
rather than direct state-action mappings
P(a\mid s)
.
This is because the gradients updating
{\theta}
originate from the generator itself (purple arrows in Fig.
1
a, and the generator learns from state-action pairs.

Nevertheless, at the end of the reconstructive stage, the policy will have learned only from teacher transitions, and these may not be sufficient to determine how the agent should act in unseen cases.
To mitigate the absence of training data for these cases, we add an adversarial stage with online play to further align the policy with the teacher’s and improve generalisation.

3.2.
Adversarial Stage

The adversarial stage (Fig.
1
b) trains both the policy, based on the agent samples collected from the environment
\mathcal{T}_{\pi_{\theta}}
, and the discriminator
\mathcal{D}_{\omega}
based on both the teacher samples
\mathcal{T}_{\pi_{\psi}}
and the agent’s.
First, UfO freezes
\mathcal{G}_{\phi}
so that the model does not learn to “fool” the discriminator by diverging from the learned approximate transition function from the reconstructive stage.
In this stage, only the policy
{\pi_{\theta}}
and discriminator
\mathcal{D}_{\omega}
are trained.
We freeze the generator
\mathcal{G}_{\phi}
to preserve the dynamics learned during the reconstruction phase.
This ensures that the policy alone is fine-tuned to better reproduce the teacher’s behavioural patterns without altering the environment approximation.
If UfO were not to freeze its generator, given there are more optimisation functions binding
\mathcal{G}_{\phi}
’s optimisation to the MDP’s transition function, the generator would focus on learning how to force the discriminator to misclassify the states it generates, diverging from the learnt transition function
\mathcal{G}_{\phi}=T^{\prime}\nsubseteq T
.

Afterwards, UfO collects samples using
{\pi_{\theta}}
in the environment (Line
17
) and trains both models by optimising a minmax framework:

(6)

\min_{\omega}\max_{{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\mathcal{T}_{\pi_{\psi}}^{\Delta}}\!\!\log(\mathcal{D}_{\omega}(\tau))\!+\!\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\mathcal{T}_{\pi_{\theta}}^{\Delta}}\!\!\log(1\!-\!\mathcal{D}_{\omega}(\tau))

where
\mathcal{T}_{\pi_{\psi}}^{\Delta}
is the sequence of the differences between subsequent states in teacher trajectories, and
\mathcal{T}_{\pi_{\theta}}^{\Delta}
is the sequence of the differences between subsequent states generated by
\mathcal{G}_{\phi}
.
Hence, the
i
th entry from
\mathcal{T}_{\pi_{\psi}}^{\Delta}
is
|s_{i}-T(s_{i},{\pi_{\psi}}(s_{i}))|
, and from
\mathcal{T}_{\pi_{\theta}}^{\Delta}
is
|s_{i}-\mathcal{G}_{\phi}(s_{i},{\pi_{\theta}}(s_{i}))|
.
UfO uses the difference between states to avoid memorising the teacher’s states since they are fixed during training.
Moreover,
\mathcal{D}_{\omega}
can be more applicable to unseen states by using the difference since the differences between states will hold.

It is important to note that the discriminator in UfO is a recurrent neural network.
Thus, it receives the differences between all pairs of states in a trajectory and updates both the policy and its weights.
Using the whole trajectory allows UfO to have a global view of the behaviour of the teacher and agent through the sequence of states and since the discriminator is recurrent, it can correct the policy when it acts optimally locally but not globally.
Nevertheless, the use of an adversarial stage poses some challenges: (i) it is susceptible to entering behaviour-seeking mode, and (ii) heavy updates to the policy can lead to
catastrophic forgetting
, i.e., loss of previously learned information by a pre-trained model
(
20
)
.
These challenges may arise because the adversarial stage encourages the agent to take actions that produce differences resembling those made by the teacher.
However, these differences may not be optimal across all possible trajectories.
This can result in poorer trajectories, causing a compounding error between the policy and the discriminator, significantly altering
{\theta}
.
As a consequence, the policy may lose its previously acquired knowledge.
To address these challenges, UfO uses only
10
epochs in the adversarial stage (
\text{epochs}_{a}
), a drastically lower learning rate, and clips the gradients when updating
{\theta}
.
By avoiding prolonged adversarial training, UfO manages to avoid behavioural-seeking mode, while keeping the acquired knowledge the policy learned from its reconstruction step.

Experimental Results

We compared UfO’s results against the following five key related methods.
Behavioural Cloning from Observation (BCO)
(
30
)
and Generative adversarial Imitation from Observation (GAIfO)
(

)

(

)
, Modality-agnostic Adversarial Hypothesis Adaptation for Learning from Observations (MAHALO)
(

)
, and Off-Policy Imitation Learning from Observations (OPOLO)

We do not compare to other baselines, such as DICE-based methods, such as SMODICE

)

We experimented with five commonly used environments: Ant, Half Cheetah, Hopper, Swimmer, and Inverted Pendulum.

Implementation and Metrics

We used PyTorch to implement our agent and optimise the loss functions in Eq.

via Adam
(

)
.
We used the datasets provided by Imitation Datasets

)

(

We also provided all learning rates in the supplementary material with a link to the official implementation.

We evaluate all methods using the
Average Episodic Reward
(
AER
) metric and
Performance

\mathcal{P}
.

AER({\pi})
is the average accumulated reward for a policy
{\pi}
over
n
trajectories with
t
steps each:

AER({\pi})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{t}\gamma^{t}r(s_{i,j},{\pi}(s_{i,j})).

\mathcal{P}_{\tau}({\pi})
is the reward of the policy
{\pi}
in a trajectory
\tau
, normalised between random and teacher policies, where
0
corresponds to the reward of a random policy
{\pi_{\xi}}
, and
1
to the reward of the teacher policy
{\pi_{\psi}}
:

\mathcal{P}({\pi})=\nicefrac{{AER({\pi})-AER({\pi_{\xi}})}}{{AER({\pi_{\psi}})-AER({\pi_{\xi}})}}.

Finally, we do not report accuracy since achieving high accuracy does not necessarily translate into a high reward for the agent.

To avoid any confusion, we note that although IL methods might achieve
\mathcal{P}
higher than one, the overall objective is not to be as close as possible to the teacher (e.g.,
AER({\pi_{\theta}})\approx AER({\pi_{\psi}})
), but to measure whether the agent is learning a policy that is relatively better than both policies.
Results in Tab.
1
are the average and standard deviation in the five environments.
We do not report accuracy since achieving high accuracy does not necessarily translate into a high reward for the agent (seen in Sec.
6.1
).

5.
Theoretical Underpinning for Convergence

Sec.
4
presents the empirical evidence for the convergence of UfO over five benchmarks.
We now provide the theoretical explanation of convergence for UfO under the reconstruction and adversarial stages.

5.1.
UfO Setup

We first start by considering:

(i)

{\pi_{\theta}}
is the policy model, computed by a neural network parametrised by
{\theta}
. Given a state, it selects the action to be taken;

(ii)

the generative model
\mathcal{G}_{\phi}
is a neural network parametrised by
\phi
. It outputs the state reached by the execution of an action in a state;

(iii)

the discriminator model
\mathcal{D}_{\omega}
is a neural network parametrised by
\omega
. Given a trajectory, it outputs a value in the unit interval, estimating the likelihood that a trajectory belongs to the teacher.

Furthermore, let
\tau_{\pi}\sim\mathcal{T}_{\pi}
be trajectories generated by a policy
{\pi}
(either
{\pi_{\theta}}
or
{\pi_{\psi}}
), and
{\mathcal{L}}
be the squared loss:
{\mathcal{L}}(s^{\prime},\mathcal{G}_{\phi}(s,{\pi_{\theta}}(s)))=\left\|s^{\prime}-\mathcal{G}_{\phi}(s,{\pi_{\theta}}(s))\right\|^{2}
(as stated in Sec.
3
).

5.2.
Reconstruction Stage

We begin by analysing the first stage from UfO: the reconstruction stage.
Lemma
5.1
provides the intuition behind local convergence.

Lemma 5.1.

Let
{\pi_{\theta}}
be the policy model,
\mathcal{G}_{\phi}
be the generative model, and
{\mathcal{L}}
be the squared loss, which interleaves every
k
iteration the parameter updates as follows:

•

{\theta}_{k}\leftarrow\argmin_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{\left(s,s^{\prime}\right)\sim\tau_{\pi_{\psi}}}{\mathcal{L}}(s^{\prime},\mathcal{G}_{\phi_{k-1}}({\pi}_{\theta_{k}}\!(s)))
; and

•

\phi_{k}\leftarrow\argmin_{\phi}\mathbb{E}_{\left(s,s^{\prime}\right)\sim\tau_{{\pi}_{\theta_{k}}}}{\mathcal{L}}(s^{\prime},\mathcal{G}_{\phi_{k}}({\pi}_{\theta_{k}}\!(s)))
.

We assume that
{\pi_{\theta}}
and
\mathcal{G}_{\phi}
are continuously differentiable with Lipschitz-continuous gradients, and that learning rates are bounded so that parameter updates remain within compact sets.

Then the parameters
{\theta}_{k}
and
\phi_{k}
, where
k
is the
k
th iteration from the reconstruction stage, converge to a local stationary point (
{\theta}^{*}
,
\phi^{*}
).
Thus, the alternating update scheme corresponds to block coordinate descent with differentiable and Lipschitz-continuous objectives.
Standard convergence results (e.g.,
2
and
26
) ensure convergence to a stationary point under these assumptions.

Lemma
5.1
ensures convergence of the joint optimisation for the first stage of both neural networks.
We now present Lemma
5.2
, which justifies the role of
\mathcal{G}_{\phi}
individually within this setup.

Lemma 5.2.

Let
{\pi}_{\theta_{k}}
be a fixed neural network trained up to the
k
iteration (
{\theta}_{k}
), and
\mathcal{G}_{\phi_{k-1}}
be a neural network parametrised by
\phi_{k-1}
up to the
k-1
iteration using
\mathbb{E}_{(s,s^{\prime})\sim\tau_{\pi_{\theta}}}{\mathcal{L}}(s^{\prime},\mathcal{G}_{\phi_{k-1}}(s,{\pi}_{\theta_{k}}\!(s)))
with sufficient model capacity and training data, the
k
th iteration of the generative update leads to
\mathcal{G}_{\phi_{k}}(s,{\pi}_{\theta_{k}}(s))\rightarrow\mathbb{E}\left[s^{\prime}\mid s,{\pi}_{\theta_{k}}\!(s)\right]

In other words, this setup is a supervised regression problem where the target is the next state under the transition dynamics
\mathcal{G}_{\phi}(s,{\pi_{\theta}}(s))\approx T(s,{\pi_{\theta}}(s))
.
We note that the reconstruction stage only updates
\mathcal{G}_{\phi}
weights from the environment since it is impossible to predict
\mathcal{G}_{\phi}(s,{\pi_{\theta}}(s))
with no knowledge of how
T(s,{\pi_{\theta}}(s))
occurs.
Therefore, given universal approximation capacity,
\mathcal{G}_{\phi}
converges to the conditional expectation of the MDP’s dynamics (
T
).
Moreover, as
{\pi_{\theta}}
improves and generates trajectories closer to those in
\mathcal{T}_{\pi_{\psi}}
, the distribution
\tau_{\pi_{\theta}}
becomes increasingly aligned with
\tau_{\pi_{\psi}}
, improving the generator’s data quality.

Now, we present Lemma
5.3
, which characterises the role of
{\pi_{\theta}}
individually for the reconstruction stage.

Lemma 5.3.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{\phi_{k-1}}
be the fixed generative neural network trained up to the
k-1
iteration, and
{\pi}_{\theta_{k}}
be the policy neural network parametrised by
{\theta}_{k}
up to the
k-1
iteration.
Assume
\mathcal{G}_{\phi}+\epsilon=T
, where
\epsilon
is some error that inevitably decreases as
k
increases on the support of
{\pi_{\theta}}
.
Then, updating
{\theta}_{k}
via
\mathbb{E}_{(s,s^{\prime})\sim\tau_{\pi_{\psi}}}{\mathcal{L}}(s^{\prime},\mathcal{G}_{\phi_{k-1}}(s,{\pi}_{\theta_{k}}(s)))
leads to
{\pi_{\theta}}
producing teacher-like transitions.

If
\mathcal{G}_{\phi}
accurately approximates the environment’s transition model, then matching
\mathcal{G}_{\phi}(s,{\pi_{\theta}}(s))
to the teacher-observed
s^{\prime}
encourages
{\pi_{\theta}}
to choose actions that align with the teacher’s behaviour.
As
{\pi_{\theta}}
improves and produces more teacher-like state transitions, the generator receives better on-policy samples in future iterations, enhancing its approximation of the environment.
This recursive structure forms a positive feedback loop that enables consistent
unsupervised
updates to
{\theta}
.
It is important to note that UfO learns the teacher’s actions unsupervisedly in an unsupervised way via this process, and
{\pi_{\theta}}
only learns from teacher transitions via
\mathcal{G}_{\phi}(s,{\pi_{\theta}}(s))
.
Therefore, no direct action signal is backpropagated to the neural network at any time.

Lemmas
5.2
and
5.3
show that improvements to the generator reinforce the policy’s ability to mimic teacher behaviour and vice-versa.
We can now explore the convergence of this coupled process.

Lemma 5.4.

Let (
{\theta}_{k}
,
\phi_{k}
) be the parameters at iteration
k
of the alternating optimisation previously defined.
Under boundedness of updates and the assumptions of Lemmas
5.1
-
5.3
, (
{\theta}_{k}
,
\phi_{k}
) converges to (
{\theta}^{*}
,
\phi^{*}
) such that
\tau_{\pi_{\theta}}+\epsilon=\tau_{\pi_{\psi}}
where
\epsilon
is an acceptable error assuming both
\tau_{\pi_{\theta}}
and
\tau_{\pi_{\theta}}
start in the same state
s
.

In other words, Lemma
5.4
follows from the two-timescale stochastic approximation theory
(
3
)
.
The generator adapts quickly to the current policy, and the policy updates using a fixed generator, where the joint process converges to a consistent fixed point.

5.3.
Adversarial Stage

After the reconstruction stage, an adversarial stage refines the policy
{\pi_{\theta}}
using a discriminator
\mathcal{D}_{\omega}
.
Lemma
5.5
explores the discriminator optimisation under this stage.

Lemma 5.5.

Let
\tau
denote a trajectory, which is a finite sequence of states
\tau=\left(s_{0},s_{1},\ldots,s_{t}\right)
,
p_{\pi}(\tau)
denote the probability densities over trajectories by the teacher policy
{\pi_{\psi}}
and the agent’s
{\pi_{\theta}}
, and the discriminator
\mathcal{D}_{\omega}
be trained under the loss function:

\argmax_{\omega}\mathbb{E}_{\tau_{\pi_{\psi}}\!\sim\!\mathcal{T}_{\pi_{\psi}}}\!\log\!\mathcal{D}_{\omega}(\tau_{\pi_{\psi}})+\mathbb{E}_{\tau_{\pi_{\theta}}\!\sim\!\mathcal{T}_{\pi_{\theta}}}\!\log(1\!-\!\mathcal{D}_{\omega}(\tau_{\pi_{\theta}})).

Then, for a fixed policy
{\pi_{\theta}}
and
{\pi_{\psi}}
, the optimal
\mathcal{D}_{\omega}
is:

\mathcal{D}_{\omega}^{*}(\tau)=\frac{p_{\pi_{\psi}}(\tau)}{p_{\pi_{\psi}}(\tau)+p_{\pi_{\theta}}(\tau)}.

Thus, minimising the result value function for
{\pi_{\theta}}
corresponds to minimising the Jensen-Shannon divergence,
JSD({\pi_{\psi}}\mid\mid{\pi_{\theta}})
, between the teacher and agent trajectory distributions.

Lemma
5.5
follows standard results in generative adversarial learning
(
15
)
, where the optimal discriminator for two distributions is known to be
\mathcal{D}_{\omega}^{*}
.
The generator (in our work,
\mathcal{G}_{\phi}(s,{\pi_{\theta}}(s))
) trains to match the teacher by minimising the Jensen-Shannon divergence.

While Lemma
5.5
establishes the objective of the adversarial stage, it does not guarantee that the policy remains stable after reconstruction.
Therefore, Lemma
5.6
ensures that under mild constraints, fine-tuning does not lead to catastrophic forgetting or divergence.

Lemma 5.6.

Assume that during adversarial training, the generator
\mathcal{G}_{\phi}
remains fixed after reconstruction, the discriminator
\mathcal{D}_{\omega}
is near-optimal at each step, and the policy
{\pi_{\theta}}
is updated using small, clipped gradients.
Then the adversarial stage preserves the behavioural properties of the reconstructed policy and improves generalisation without destabilising earlier learning.

In other words, policy updates are clipped and learning rates are kept small to avoid destabilising the pre-trained policy.
Therefore, the fixed generator ensures that the transition model used to compute gradient signals remains stable.
With a near-optimal discriminator, the gradients provided to
{\pi_{\theta}}
reliably reflect the discrepancy between agent and expert trajectories.
Gradient clipping and a reduced learning rate guarantee that updates to
{\pi_{\theta}}
are conservative, preventing large deviations from the pre-trained policy.
Under these conditions, the adversarial stage acts as a regularised adjustment rather than a full retraining, mitigating the risk of catastrophic forgetting
(
14
)
while refining behavioural fidelity.

Finally, we note that these results only assume differentiability, Lipschitz continuity, and bounded updates.
No convexity assumptions are made.
Convergence should be interpreted as local convergence to stationary points, which are typical in deep learning settings.

Discussion

In this section, we consider some key aspects of UfO’s behaviour:

(i)

the interaction between generator and policy in the first stage;

(ii)

how the adversarial step improves UfO overall results; and

(iii)

how sample efficient UfO is.

6.1.
Teacher Action Approximation

It is vital for UfO to estimate action effects using solely the loss in Eq.
4
closely via the process described in Sec.
3.1
.
If the signal from the generator is not strong enough and the policy is unable to approximate its predictions, or the generator is unable to learn a transition function close enough to the MDP’s, the adversarial stage will not work, and UfO will not achieve the desired results.

In Fig.

It displays the error from
{\pi_{\theta}}
according to the teacher’s dataset, the generative error from
\mathcal{G}_{\phi}
according to the teacher’s dataset (split into training and evaluation) and the agent’s collected samples, and the reward accumulated for the agent during each training step.
As expected, the generator learns a transition function that approximates the actual MDP transition function quite early in the training phase (purple line).
However, for the generator to see transitions closer to the teacher’s, the policy has to improve its error, and we notice a direct correlation between the errors of
{\pi_{\theta}}
and
\mathcal{G}_{\phi}
.
We also observe that as the errors of both models decrease, the rewards increase, which shows that Eq.
4
and
5
yield the expected outcome.
Yet, we note that the policy error stagnates after a certain number of epochs, potentially indicating that the gradient signal from the generator is no longer enough to improve the policy’s predictions further.
This behaviour is expected since the generator is not optimised to improve the policy’s predictions themselves, but rather to learn a transition function that can approximate the teacher’s and the MDP’s.
Thus, the adversarial stage is crucial for further improving the policy’s predictions and for stabilising the model’s behaviour.
Finally, we believe that this stage could be further improved by using a curriculum learning approach.
In this approach, the
\mathcal{G}_{\phi}
would receive more samples it predicted poorly, and the policy would receive more meaningful weight updates.

Related Work

The simplest form of imitation learning is BC
(
25
)
, which addresses the IL problem as a supervised learning problem.
It uses teacher samples of the form
(s,a,s^{\prime})
, where
s^{\prime}=T(s,a)
, to learn how to approximate the teacher’s policy.
However, supervised approaches are costly for more complex scenarios, requiring more samples about the actions’ effects in the environment.
For example, solving Atari games requires approximately
100
times more samples than control tasks, such as CartPole.
Generative Adversarial Imitation Learning (GAIL)
(
17
)
matches the state-action frequencies from the agent to those seen in the training data, creating a policy with action distributions closer to the teacher.
GAIL leverages the data structure more efficiently, reducing its dependency on large quantities of data and making it more suitable for complex environments.
It uses adversarial training to discriminate state-action pairs from the agent or the teacher while minimising the difference between them.

Recent self-supervised approaches
(
30
;
23
)
that learn from observations use teacher’s transitions
(s_{\pi_{\psi}},s^{\prime}_{\pi_{\psi}})
and leverage random transitions
(s_{\pi_{\xi}},s^{\prime}_{\pi_{\xi}})
to learn the IDM of the environment, and afterwards, generate self-supervised labels for the teacher’s trajectories.
Generative Adversarial Imitation Learning from Observation (GAIfO)
(
31
)
is an ILfO method based on GAIL.
The discriminator is trained using state transitions, and the policy is optimised using an inverse reinforcement learning strategy.
Imitating Latent Policies from Observation (ILPO)
(
6
)
differs from such work by trying to estimate the probability of a latent action given a state.
Within some environment steps, it remaps latent actions.
More recently, Off-Policy Learning from Observations (OPOLO)
(
34
)
used a dual-form of the expectation function and an adversarial structure to achieve off-policy ILfO.
Modality-agnostic Adversarial Hypothesis Adaptation for Learning from Observations (MAHALO)
(
21
)
unifies ILfO with offline reinforcement learning by learning pessimistic value estimates through adversarially trained reward and critic functions.
It accommodates partially labelled datasets and generalises across several ILfO settings.
Continuous Imitation Learning from Observation (CILO)
(
12
)
used the same IDM structure of earlier methods but introduced an exploration mechanism to further improve its IDM’s predictions.
Path signatures were used as non-parametric encodings to identify trajectories that behave similarly to the teacher efficiently.

8.
Conclusion

In this work, we proposed Unsupervised Imitation Learning from Observation (UfO), an unsupervised ILfO method addressing critical limitations of existing approaches.
Firstly, UfO eliminates reliance on action-based supervised optimisation, reducing the risk of overfitting.
Secondly, it mitigates behaviour-seeking that often lead to suboptimal performance.
Lastly, UfO provides generalisation by more effectively handling environments where transitions can result from multiple actions.
It learns to approximate the teacher’s policy and refine it through adversarial learning.
A two-stage training process ensures the agent captures accurate state transitions and aligns its policy with the teacher’s through online interactions.
By leveraging transitions rather than direct state-action mappings, UfO avoids common ILfO pitfalls and achieves superior results across diverse initial states.

Our experiments highlight the effectiveness of UfO, consistently surpassing the teacher in five benchmark environments.
Unlike traditional methods, UfO achieves state-of-the-art results without sacrificing robustness, making it suitable for real-world scenarios where action labels are unavailable or costly.
It demonstrates strong generalisation, maintaining low trajectory variance as evidenced by its coefficient of variation (
2.64\%
).
Beyond imitation learning, UfO offers a general framework for robust policy learning from indirect supervision.
Its proxy-based learning approach is applicable to offline RL, sim-to-real transfer, and causal representation learning in domains such as healthcare and economics. Moreover, the refinement stage resembles curriculum learning and can be adapted to enhance sample efficiency and robustness in safety-critical systems.

Future work will explore UfO’s application to more complex and dynamic environments, further enhancing sample efficiency through advanced exploration strategies and expanding its applicability as a foundation for observational learning in autonomous systems.

Appendix D
On The Use Of External Data

UfO does not rely on any external data beyond the teacher’s observations.
It collects all additional samples used during training through the agent’s interactions with the environment.
In contrast, ILfO methods commonly use external data to bridge the gap between their initial random policy and the teacher’s policy.
They do so by collecting samples from a random policy to learn the transition dynamics and to utilise teacher samples in a self-supervised manner.
MAHALO
(
21
)
slightly differentiates from this setting.
Although MAHALO is a more recent imitation learning method, its performance in our evaluation is notably lower.
We attribute this to three key differences:

(i)

differences in problem formulation between MAHALO, UfO, and other ILfO methods,

(ii)

the data and hyperparameters used, and

(iii)

our interpretation of the discrepancy between MAHALO’s reported performance and that observed in our setup.

In this section, we examine these differences to understand the role that external data plays in ILfO methods and how UfO differentiates itself from other ILfO methods.

D.1.
Problem Formulation

The core difference between UfO, MAHALO, and other ILfO methods lies in (i) whether they require samples outside the teacher’s ones, and (ii) how they utilise them.
UfO operates in a fully unsupervised manner, relying exclusively on the teacher trajectories and the agent’s own interactions.
It does not leverage any form of labelled data, including action or reward information, nor does it rely on externally sourced trajectories to learn environment dynamics.
In contrast, MAHALO depends on an external offline RL dataset (e.g., D4RL
(
8
)
) that contains labelled transitions, including both actions and rewards.
It utilises this dataset to train an inverse dynamics model, which is then applied to infer actions based on the teacher’s state-only demonstrations.
This positions MAHALO closer to methods such as BCO
(
30
)
, GAIfO
(
31
)
, CILO
(
12
)
, which similarly infer actions or dynamics from external sources before applying them to the teacher data.
A key distinction introduced by this formulation is that MAHALO’s reliance on offline datasets is populated with suboptimal but structured policies.
Unlike purely random trajectories, these suboptimal policies offer meaningful coverage of the environment and make the learning task considerably easier by implicitly providing informative intermediate behaviours.
It is this availability of suboptimal policy data – not simply the act of bridging the distributional gap between random and teacher samples – that underpins MAHALO’s performance advantages.
This departs from the strict observational constraints upheld by UfO and other ILfO methods considered in our main work.

D.2.
Data and Hyperparameters

With this clear distinction in mind, we now outline the hyperparameter selection process employed in our evaluation and the data used for the offline and online training portions.
In their original work,
21
state that the confidence threshold
\beta
, which determines whether inferred actions are accepted based on their likelihood, is tuned individually for each environment.
Unfortunately, no specific
\beta
values are provided for the overlapping environments in our study (
Hopper
and
HalfCheetah
).
Furthermore, our evaluation includes additional environments not present in their original setup (
InvertedPendulum
,
Ant
, and
Swimmer
).
To ensure a fair and robust comparison, we train over all
\beta
values reported in their supplementary material
\{0.1;1;10;100;1,000\}
.
For each environment, MAHALO is trained using the same
700
teacher episodes employed across all baselines (which is approximately
300
episodes short of their original work), and exclusively with random samples (i.e., no suboptimal policies are used from the offline data).
Although UfO does not use any external data, we maintain the use of random policy information to have a fairer comparison with other ILfO methods.
The random dataset contains
10,000
episodes containing a tuple of
\langle{s,a,s^{\prime},r}\rangle

We use the same random dataset for all methods to maintain consistency, and the same teacher datasets, which are the v
