Vacuum-assisted scalp repositioning: a novel temporizing approach to acute sinking skin flap syndrome. Illustrative cases
Authors: Evan Courville, Joshua Marquez, Michael Homma, Michael Conley, Georgios P Skandalakis, Peter Shin, James Botros, Christian Ricks
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDThis report describes the use of a novel approach to address acute sinking skin flap syndrome (SSFS), a postcraniectomy complication arising from brain dysfunction beneath the skull defect. The authors present a case series of two patients, emphasizing the prospective application of an external plaster cast in tandem with a vacuum-assisted closure (VAC) device (wound VAC) to promptly reposition the scalp and relieve brain compression.OBSERVATIONSFollowing intervention, one patient showed immediate neurological improvement, with complete resolution of symptoms within hours. Conversely, the second patient developed nonconvulsive status epilepticus. Computed tomography scans postintervention validated the successful scalp repositioning and mass effect resolution in both instances. This temporary approach proved successful in one patient with moderate symptoms, serving as a bridge to cranioplasty.LESSONSThe integration of an external plaster cast and wound VAC offers a cost-effective and prompt solution for patients with acute SSFS pending cranioplasty. Appropriate patient selection and heightened caution for those with severe symptoms should be exercised.Keywords: sinking skin flap syndrome, vacuum-assisted closure, scalp repositioning, craniectomy, cerebrospinal fluid dynamics, pathophysiologyABBREVIATIONS: CSF = cerebrospinal fluid, CT = computed tomography, EEG = electroencephalography, ICU = intensive care unit, IPR = inpatient rehabilitation, SoT = syndrome of the trephined, SSFS = sinking skin flap syndrome, VAC = vacuum-assisted closure

The syndrome of the trephined (SoT) and sinking skin flap syndrome (SSFS) are distinct conditions specific to neurosurgical patients who have undergone craniectomy.1 The former refers to a generalized neurological decline, whereas the latter manifests as deficits attributable to brain dysfunction directly underlying the skull defect.1 Definitive therapy involves the placement of an internal cranioplasty implant to reestablish the integrity of the skull.2 However, this presents a significant challenge at institutions without immediate access to a bone bank or rapid implant manufacturing. Such limitations can be a disservice to patients with readily reversible neurological deficits while their treatment is limited to hydration and horizontal or Trendelenburg positioning.3

The pathophysiology of SoT and SSFS remains incompletely understood. A widespread misconception is that the concavity of the skin flap and subsequent brain compression arise because of exposure to atmospheric pressure.4–6 However, this is unequivocally refuted in Hakim’s thesis, which asserts that it is directly related to changes in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) pressure.7 Furthermore, removing a patient’s bone flap has been shown to cause in cerebral metabolism, CSF flow dynamics, and cerebral hemodynamics.1,6,8 Interestingly, these changes manifest even in the absence of brain compression, midline shift, or changes in ventricular size; precede the development of neurological symptoms; and are reversible with a cranioplasty implant.1,5,6,8 This emphasizes our limited insight into the pathophysiology of SSFS and supports the views of some experts who suggest that cranioplasty should be performed as soon as cerebral edema has resolved.6

The purpose of this case lesson is to describe the use and outcomes of a novel temporizing measure for patients who present with acute SSFS while awaiting a cranioplasty implant. An external plaster cast with a vacuum-assisted closure (VAC) device (wound VAC) was utilized to reposition the scalp to its natural physiological location for the temporary improvement in paradoxical brain herniation.

Illustrative Cases

Case 1

Clinical Presentation

A 49-year-old male presented with severe headaches, nausea, and vomiting after a fall. He underwent emergency decompressive craniectomy for an enlarging intracerebral hemorrhage with midline shift. His postoperative course was uneventful, and he was discharged to inpatient rehabilitation (IPR) 26 days after admission. During his IPR, he made a full recovery. He was ambulatory, managed all daily activities independently, and his neurological function returned to baseline.

The patient returned to the emergency department 3 months after being discharged. His family reported month-long urinary retention necessitating the placement of a Foley catheter, a 3-day decline in mental status, and 1 day of left-sided hemiparesis. On physical examination, the patient presented with a flexed left elbow and his hand positioned across his chest. There was an absence of voluntary movement in the hand, yet his muscle tone remained intact. His left leg demonstrated antigravity movements. Notably, bradypsychia was observed, and the right-sided surgical flap exhibited significant depression, as depicted in Fig. 1. Head computed tomography (CT) showed a convex deformity of the right frontotemporoparietal region with 1 cm of midline shift. The patient was diagnosed with SSFS.

Treatment

Given his rapid decline in mental status and motor function, urgent neurosurgical intervention was necessary. The patient was immediately placed in the Trendelenburg position and received intravenous hydration. However, an hour of this conservative approach yielded no improvement in his condition, prompting a decision to escalate treatment. Thus, a procedure designed to eliminate the direct compression of his brain by the overlying soft tissue was attempted.

Device Design

An approach similar to that described by Dillen et al.9 was adopted. A plaster cast was molded to the patient’s head and trimmed accordingly to overlap with bone minimally surrounding the defect. A hole was then made at the center of the cast, and a double layer of Medipore tape was applied along its perimeter to protect the skin. The right side of the head was shaved to place the cast over the cranial defect. Last, a wound VAC device was placed over the cast and set to a pressure of 75 mm Hg (Fig. 2).

B: Plaster cast molded to the patient’s craniectomy site, trimmed to overlap minimally with bone. C: A plaster cast with a hole in the center was placed over the shaved area of the cranial defect. Note that a double layer of Medipore tape was applied to the plaster cast along its perimeter to protect the skin. D: Wound VAC placed over the cast and surrounding skin.

Outcome

Remarkably, within 10 minutes, the patient regained volitional movement in his left arm, and by the sixth hour, all of his neurological symptoms, including urinary retention, had resolved. A head CT scan demonstrated near-complete resolution of the sunken flap and midline shift (Fig. 1). The apparatus remained in place for 5 days without causing damage to the underlying skin, and upon arrival of his internal cranioplasty implant, he underwent definitive surgery. In the operating room, after removal of the apparatus, immediate sinking of the skin flap was observed. Internal cranioplasty was performed without difficulty. In the immediate postoperative period, his presenting symptoms had returned, and he had bradypsychia without volitional movement of his left arm. Postoperative head CT was without acute findings with stable midline shift. Two hours postoperatively, volitional movement of his fingers returned, and by the third postoperative day, he had returned to his neurological baseline. A CT scan on the same day showed a reduced ventricular size, suggesting improved CSF dynamics. The patient was then discharged in stable condition.

Case 2

Clinical Presentation

A 46-year-old male presented after a motor vehicle crash. He had multiple facial and skull base fractures as well as bilateral hemorrhagic contusions, diffuse cerebral edema, and extrusion of devitalized brain tissue from the right ear, concerning for a traumatic encephalocele. The patient underwent a left decompressive hemicraniectomy, left temporal lobectomy, and evacuation of intraparenchymal hemorrhage. His postoperative course was uneventful, and he was discharged to IPR 13 days after admission. During his rehabilitation, he showed signs of partial recovery: he remained confined to his bed, could spontaneously open his eyes, had movement in all limbs, spoke in incomprehensible sounds, and needed extensive assistance for daily care activities.

Two months postdischarge, he was readmitted for scheduled placement of a custom left-sided cranioplasty. However, on the second postoperative day, he experienced a sudden deterioration in neurological status and was found to have a large epidural hemorrhage, necessitating a left decompressive hemicraniectomy with removal of his custom implant. Upon discharge to IPR, his neurological condition showed marginal improvement, and he occasionally followed commands.

One month later, the patient returned to the emergency department because of a sudden decline in his neurological status. Clinical examination showed that he was less responsive; his eyes remained open but did not track movement. He localized to painful stimuli with his left side, but only withdrew in response to painful stimuli on his right side. His cranial flap was significantly sunken. Computed tomography demonstrated significant mass effect with midline shift, and he was readmitted for concerns of SSFS versus SoT.

Treatment

The patient was placed in the Trendelenburg position and received intravenous hydration without improvement in neurological function. A treatment approach analogous to that used in case 1 was then used.

Outcome

Shortly after the sunken flap was adjusted, the patient experienced a tonic-clonic seizure. The external cast and wound vacuum were promptly removed. Repeat head imaging revealed a new 3-mm acute subdural hematoma without mass effect, with near-complete resolution of the previously observed paradoxical brain herniation (Fig. 3). The patient was intubated for airway protection and transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU) for monitoring, including long-term electroencephalography (EEG).

The EEG results indicated nonconvulsive status epilepticus. The patient’s seizures remained resistant to multiple oral antiepileptic drugs, high-dose benzodiazepines, and ketamine infusion. Consequently, the patient underwent burst suppression using pentobarbital for several days before gradually being weaned off. Interestingly, despite removal of our device, this patient’s scalp maintained its normal position throughout his admission. After achieving better seizure control and undergoing a custom cranioplasty, his neurological function showed marked improvement. He began to speak and responded to commands. After 1 month of readmission, the patient had stabilized sufficiently to be transferred to a long-term acute care facility.

Patient Informed Consent

The necessary patient informed consent was obtained in this study.

Discussion

We introduce a novel and temporary treatment using an external plaster cast and wound VAC for acute SSFS in two neurosurgical patients.

Observations

This report illustrates that restoring the scalp to its normal physiological position can rapidly alleviate the acute symptoms of SSFS in some patients. Nonetheless, heightened caution should be exercised for patients exhibiting severe symptoms or those with an underlying high risk of seizures. Individuals presenting with extensive cranial defects and severe symptoms might benefit from preemptive administration of antiseizure medications before any repositioning attempts. Additionally, using intermittent suctioning to gradually adjust the skin flap and preemptive admission to the ICU for potential complications could be beneficial.

Similar devices that aim to reposition the scalp have been developed; however, much time and specialized equipment are required for their assembly. These include custom-made helmets, the placement of external metal plates using transcortical screws, and customized three-dimensional–printed external plates.4,10,11 The plaster cast and wound VAC address the crucial issue of time and reduce the technical components and materials required, allowing neurosurgical teams to utilize a simple, easy-to-use device for addressing SSFS symptoms.

Lessons

This study demonstrates the potential benefits of scalp repositioning via an efficacious and innovative method to promptly reverse SSFS symptoms. Unlike alternative techniques, this approach requires minimal assembly time and utilizes equipment that is readily available at most hospitals. It can serve as a valuable interim solution in resource-constrained settings and tertiary medical centers while patients await definitive SSFS management. This report is limited by the number of cases; however, the presented method highlights the potential for innovative, rapid-response strategies in addressing critical neurological complications in SSFS. We emphasize that providers should proceed carefully for those with large cranial defects and severe symptoms.
