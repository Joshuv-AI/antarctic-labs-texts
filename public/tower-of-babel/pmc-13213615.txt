Consumption of four unhealthy commodities and exposure to advertising, sponsorship, and promotion in Bamako, Mali
Authors: Heather E Rosen, Housseini Dolo, Alyssa S Khan, Cheick Oumar Doumbia, Salif Thiam, Brahima Konate, Mountaga Diallo, Seydou Doumbia, Nino Paichadze
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNon-communicable diseases (NCDs) are the leading cause of death and disability globally and risks for many NCDs are associated with commercial determinants of health for tobacco, alcohol, ultra-processed foods (UPFs), and sugar-sweetened beverages (SSBs). Our aim was to understand reported consumption patterns of these four commodities along with reported exposure to advertising, promotion, and sponsorship (APS), focusing on vulnerable groups including young adults (18–24), less wealthy (quintiles 1 and 2), and women. We conducted a systematic randomized household survey of adults at two sites in Bamako, Mali to collect socio-demographic factors and measure reported consumption and exposure to APS for the commodities. We calculated descriptive statistics and performed χ2 tests to assess differences by three vulnerable groups. We surveyed 984 participants and found high reported consumption of SSBs (78%) and UPFs (67%) at least once per week, together with low reported current tobacco use (11%) and low reported alcohol use (1%) in the past 30 days. More than half of participants reported exposure to APS in the past 30 days for SSBs (76%) and UPFs (57%) while tobacco and alcohol reported exposure was lower (38% each). Young adults had higher reported consumption of SSBs and UPFs and higher reported APS exposure for both commodities compared with older groups. Systems-level interventions are recommended including increasing the SSB tax, adding a UPF tax, and strengthening enforcement of the APS ban for tobacco. These findings suggest further research on the usefulness of an APS ban for SSBs and UPFs targeted to young adults.Keywords: non-communicable diseases, commercial determinants of health, risk factors, advertising, sponsorship, Mali

Background

Non-communicable diseases (NCDs) are the leading cause of death and disability globally, with 44.8 million deaths and over 22 000 disability-adjusted life years per 100 000 population in 2023 (Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation 2026). The behavioral risk factors for NCDs such as tobacco use, alcohol use, unhealthy diet, and lack of physical activity are well-defined and widely known (World Health Organization 2023). However, the focus on these individual health behaviors sometimes obscures the role of the larger environment (Marmot and Bell 2019, Bognar 2020). The risks for many NCDs are also associated with commercial determinants such as the production, marketing, and consumption of commercially produced products (Moodie et al. 2013, Buse et al. 2017, Knai et al. 2018). Sugar-sweetened beverages (SSBs), ultra-processed foods (UPFs), and tobacco and alcohol are all considered unhealthy commodities and they are increasingly being consumed in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) as multinational corporations seek to expand into new markets (Stuckler et al. 2012). To understand the role of corporations in the consumption of these four unhealthy commodities (tobacco, alcohol, UPFs, and SSBs), we can apply the commercial determinants of health (CDoH) concept defined as “the systems, practices, and pathways through which commercial actors drive health and equity” (Gilmore et al. 2023).

Corporations utilize a number of strategies to increase demand for and consumption of their products and marketing is one of these key strategies, often in the form of advertising, promotion, and sponsorship (APS) (Gilmore et al. 2023). The impact of tobacco and alcohol APS on consumption has been well established (Paynter and Edwards 2009, Lovato et al. 2011, Jernigan et al. 2017) and limited evidence on unhealthy food and beverage APS also indicates increased consumption of these products, especially in children (Boyland et al. 2016, Sadeghirad et al. 2016). Multinational corporations are very creative in their strategies, including expanding to online and social media advertising, often across national borders, and using below the line marketing practices (e.g. events at bars, loyalty programs, product placement in movies, etc.) (Perez et al. 2012, World Health Organization 2022, Strowger et al. 2024). There is already substantial literature on APS for tobacco, but the study of APS for alcohol, UPFs, and SSBs in LMICs as a CDoH is an emerging field (Huse et al. 2022, Dumbili 2025, Jindarattanaporn et al. 2026, Kandel et al. 2026).

Mali, in West Africa, is one of the fastest growing and fastest urbanizing countries in the world (Gramlich 2019, World Bank 2025). It is among the countries with the highest percentage of youth of secondary school age and this youth bulge is expected to result in a growing number of people in the 15–64 age group and a higher proportion of elderly (65+) in the long run (United Nations Population Fund 2025). Urban environments have been linked to unhealthy lifestyles and increased risk factors for NCDs such as smoking, alcohol use, physical inactivity, and inadequate nutrition (Juma et al. 2019). In 2023, NCDs were responsible for 64 401 deaths in Mali, which is 33% of total deaths and represents an increase of 126% in deaths from 1990 (Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation 2026). Major NCD risk factors (high blood pressure, high fasting plasma glucose, dietary risks, high body-mass index, and tobacco use) were among the top ten risk factors for death in Mali in 2023 (Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation 2026).

There is very limited data available on consumption of tobacco, alcohol, UPFs, and SSBs among adults in Mali. A 2013 WHO STEPwise approach to NCD Surveillance (STEPS) survey reported that in the capital of Bamako, 9% of adults were daily smokers and 2% were alcohol users in the past 12 months (Ministère de la Santé Republique du Mali 2013). The only data on consumption of processed foods comes from an analysis of the living standards measurement study-integrated surveys on agriculture from 2014 to 2015, which found that 60% of food consumed in urban areas of Mali had any processing and 15% of food was highly processed (Smale et al. 2020). A global analysis of adult SSB intake reported that average consumption per person in Mali in 2018 was 2.4 servings (8 ounces each) per week compared with global consumption of 2.7 servings (Lara-Castor et al. 2023). There is currently no information available about the extent of APS for these four unhealthy commodities among adults in Bamako or elsewhere in Mali. Policies for tobacco control are well-developed in Mali, but those for alcohol UPF and SSB control are limited, although there is an existing tax on SSBs (Fig. 1).

The objective of this paper was to describe the consumption patterns of alcohol, tobacco, UPFs, and SSBs, along with exposure to marketing practices (macro associated factors) for these commodities in Mali. Our goal was to better understand consumption of unhealthy commodities among vulnerable groups (young adults, low-income, and women) and the extent to which these groups are targeted by marketing practices for these commodities. Previous research has shown that CDoH drive global health inequity and those young adults, low-income, and women are vulnerable to its effects (Gilmore et al. 2023, Manderson and Jewett 2023, McCarthy et al. 2023, Pitt et al. 2024). The study provides new data to measure consumption and marketing practices for the four unhealthy commodities and can be used to guide design of policies and population-level interventions in Mali. In addition, this study can serve as a model for further research on CDoH in Sub-Saharan Africa and LMICs.

Methods

We conducted a household survey in May and June 2024 in two health districts (Commune I and IV) of Bamako, Djikoroni Para, and Banconi, which are primarily low-income urban areas with populations of 75 804 and 126 162 inhabitants, respectively (CVD-Mali 2024). We selected the sites for convenience since they were part of the Demographic Surveillance Study of the Centre pour le Développement des Vaccins au Mali established in 2006, (Cunningham et al. 2019, CVD-Mali 2024).

Survey tool

The survey instrument was developed through a rigorous process involving literature review, expert consultations, and cognitive interviews. It was designed to measure consumption of four unhealthy commodities, specifically alcohol, tobacco, UPFs, and SSBs. The survey also captured exposure to APS for the four commodities. It also assessed perceptions and attitudes to the commodities such as product appeal, healthiness perception, affordability perception, and availability perception. Given the possibility of recall bias, we took several steps to improve design of the survey questions and encourage better recall during data collection, including conducting cognitive interviews during the survey design phase, reading explanatory paragraphs about SSBs and UPFs to participants, and asking about specific places participants might see advertising, types of promotions they might encounter, or types of events where they might see sponsorship (Khan et al. Manuscript under review). We followed existing surveys identified in the literature review and did not include specific definitions of APS in the survey. The survey was developed in English and translated into French by native speakers.

Sample size

We used the formula n=Z2P(1−P)E2 to calculate sample size where Z is 95%, P is .5, and E is 0.05 to yield a sample of 385 (Cochran 1963). To explore strata-specific estimates of gender (male and female), a sample size of 462 respondents is required for each of the two strata, which accounts for 20% of combined refusals and attrition. Thus, the total sample size requirement is 462×2 = 924 subjects.

Sampling

The two study sites were selected by convenience sampling and we then used systematic random sampling to select households to participate in the survey. Starting from a health facility considered the central point of each area and proceeding in a randomly selected direction, every third household was selected. At each household, a list of potential participants was prepared and a family was randomly selected (if more than one family was present) and then one person over the age of 18 was randomly selected from that family for the survey. Survey participants received a small cash incentive.

Data collection procedures

To engage the community prior to initiation of the survey, a team met with the village chief and local leaders in each community to introduce and engage them in the project and letters were sent to the health district directors to inform them of the project. Ten community interviewers were each partnered with community health workers from the area who would support the sampling process. The interviewers received one day of training to ensure a standard approach including review of the consent process, the survey tool, and the data collection protocol. A field supervisor conducted daily quality control checks and discussed data quality with the interviewers on a regular basis. Data was collected on tablets using REDCap. Because consumption patterns would be different during the fasting period of Ramadan, data collection was initiated more than 30 days after the end of Ramadan.

Defining consumption

Our questions on SSB and UPFs were influenced by the Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System and the Mali Diet Quality Questionnaire, although there are no widely accepted definitions of SSB and UPF consumption (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention 2016, Global Diet Quality Project 2017). For our survey, consumption of SSBs was defined as consuming regular soda or other sugary drinks (sugar-sweetened fruit drinks, sweet tea, sweetened coffee, hot cocoa, sports, or energy drinks) at least once per week. Diet soda and 100% fruit juice were excluded. Consumption of UPFs was defined as consuming any of the following at least once per week: cakes, gateau ni, or sweet cookies; candy, chocolates, caramels, sweet groundnut, sweet sesame, ice cream, popsicles, or rice pudding; chips such as Pringles, Lays, Doritos, or Slice; instant noodles such as Kellogg’s or Indomie; potato fries, sweet potato fries, fried aloko, beignets, or fried chicken; or fast food such as burgers, shawarma, or pizza. We modeled our question on tobacco usage from the Global Adult Tobacco Survey (GATS) and used the definition of alcohol consumption from the STEPS survey (Global Adult Tobacco Survey Collaborative Group 2020, World Health Organization 2026). Thus, consumption of tobacco was defined as being a current user of tobacco products (including electronic cigarettes or smokeless tobacco). Consumption of alcohol was defined as consuming any alcohol in the past 30 days.

Defining exposure to marketing practices

Our questions on APS exposure were modeled after those in GATS, but there are no widely accepted definitions for these items (Global Adult Tobacco Survey Collaborative Group 2020). Exposure to advertising was defined as noticing advertisements or signs promoting products at any of ten specific places or “anywhere else” in the past 30 days. Exposure to promotion was defined as noticing any of seven types of promotion examples in the past 30 days (free samples, sale prices, coupons, free gifts or special discount offers on other products, clothing or other items with brand name or logo, promotions in the mail, and competitions or contests). Exposure to sponsorship was defined as noticing any product brands or companies associated with sports and sporting events or with music, theater, art, or fashion events in the past 30 days. Thus, exposure to APS was defined as exposure to any advertising, promotion, or sponsorship in the past 30 days.

Statistical analysis

Data were exported to Excel from REDCap and then analyzed using R version 4.4.0. Data was collected on 1000 participants, but 16 were excluded from the final analysis due to missing key data (12 missing age and 4 missing wealth variables). We calculated descriptive statistics for the socio-demographic characteristics, consumption patterns, and exposure to APS. Categorical variables were summarized with counts and percentages and continuous variables with means and standard deviations. Relative wealth was calculated in quintiles (1 = lowest, 5 = highest) using the EquityTool developed for Mali by Metrics for Management (Metrics for Management 2024). We performed χ2 tests to assess differences in the consumption and exposure variables by three vulnerable groups: young adults (18–24 years old) vs. older participants (25+ years old), low income (quintile 1 and 2) vs. wealthier groups (quintile 3–5), and female vs. male. A P-value of .05 was considered statistically significant. We did not analyze data separately by study site.

Participants were majority Muslim (99%) from a variety of ethnic groups. One-quarter of the participants were in the bottom two income quintiles and almost two-thirds (66%) used social media once a week or more frequently.

Discussion

We assessed consumption patterns of alcohol, tobacco, UPFs and SSBs as well as exposure to marketing practices for these four commodities in Bamako, Mali. Participants reported high consumption of SSBs (78%) and UPFs (67%) at least once per week, together with low tobacco and alcohol use (11% and 1%, respectively). They reported high exposure to APS in the past 30 days for SSBs (76%) and UPFs (57%) and less so for alcohol (38%), and tobacco (38%). Young adults had significantly higher reported consumption of SSBs and UPFs and higher reported exposure to APS for these two commodities compared with older groups. Women reported significantly lower consumption than men for three of the four commodities and reported lower exposure to APS for all four commodities.

Very little similar data is available on consumption of unhealthy commodities from urban low-income country settings to make comparisons. A study among low-income adults in Kuala Lumpur, Malaysia (an upper-middle-income country), reported that 68% consumed SSBs at least once per week, 35% consumed dessert foods, and 16% consumed fast food (Eng et al. 2022). In a low-income population in Accra, Ghana (a lower-middle-income country), 15% consumed processed meat at least once per week and 54% consumed fizzy drinks (Kushitor et al. 2023). In both these studies, SSB consumption was higher than UPF consumption, which is similar to our results. Unhealthy food and drinks categories are often not well described in research making direct comparisons difficult.

Young adults (18–24 years old) had higher consumption of SSBs and UPFs. People in this age group are in transition to adulthood and experiencing important changes in economics, lifestyle, and culture that can promote changes in diet (Winpenny et al. 2018). The dietary behaviors adopted during this period will likely persist into later life and a diet high in fruits and vegetables in young adults has been associated with lower risk of cardiovascular disease later in life (Craigie et al. 2011, Miedema et al. 2015). Participants in the lower income quintiles also consumed more UPFs than their richer counterparts. Calorie-dense highly processed foods tend to be cheaper than healthier foods and this may be fueling the growth in obesity worldwide (Popkin 2001, Drewnowski and Specter 2004, Headey and Alderman 2019).

There are a number of strategies that have been proposed to address CDoH including behavior change, regulation of business practices, fiscal policy, citizen action, and litigation (Lee and Freudenberg 2022). Our study indicated that measures to decrease SSB and UPF consumption are warranted. Systematic reviews have found that taxation of SSBs is associated with lower consumption (Andreyeva et al. 2022, Fernandes et al. 2025) and limited studies suggest a similar effect for taxation of UPFs (Thow et al. 2014, Sacks et al. 2021). Mali has had a SSB excise tax since 2005, but it is only 10%, well below the 20% recommended by WHO (World Health Organization 2015a, Global Food Research Program 2025). Excise taxes are believed to be more effective than sales taxes in lowering consumption because consumers see the impact in the price when they choose the item, not when paying at the register (Brownell and Frieden 2009). We recommend increasing the existing SSB excise tax and adding an excise tax on UPFs to decrease consumption of these products and encourage healthier eating practices. Since they already impose taxes of this type, the government of Mali has the knowledge and processes in place to enact these new policies. However, political will for these taxes in Mali at present seems weak; for instance, the most recent national strategy to combat NCDs does not mention taxes, sugar, or processed foods at all (Ministere de la Sante et de L’Hygiene Publique 2018). Concerted efforts by policy-makers, researchers, and civil society all working together will be needed to develop and implement these new policies.

The survey indicated that there was strong penetration of corporate brands for SSBs and UPFs into everyday life in Bamako through APS. Unfortunately, there is no comparable data from other LMICs to help us analyze this trend. Young adults were also significantly more likely to report exposure to marketing practices for UPFs and SSBs. Studies of UPF and SSB APS find that exposure is associated with higher product consumption, but the evidence is primarily in those under 18 years of age (Boyland et al. 2016, Arrona-Cardoza et al. 2023, Ilieva et al. 2025). Current initiatives to restrict advertising for these two commodities have primarily focused on targeting to children and adolescents, for example in Chile, Sweden, and South Korea (Dillman Carpentier et al. 2020, Global Food Research Program 2024). However, young adults are an important market segment that is especially vulnerable to APS and could benefit from an advertising ban (Freeman et al. 2016, Northcott et al. 2025). We recommend further research with young adults to determine the usefulness of an on APS ban for SSBs and UPFs.

Reported consumption of both tobacco and alcohol in the Mali survey was low and in line with the 2013 STEPS survey (Ministère de la Santé Republique du Mali 2013). This was expected given the strong influence of Islam in Mali and its prohibition on alcohol and discouragement of tobacco use, although we cannot discount the possibility of social desirability bias in responses (Krumpal 2013). To better study tobacco and alcohol consumption in Mali, we suggest further research should take the form of systematic social observation or a mixed methods study design or a survey with non-probability sampling approach such as the snowball sampling technique (Dumbili 2014, Törnävä et al. 2025, Valiente et al.).

It is particularly concerning that 38% of our survey was exposed to any tobacco APS considering that Mali ratified the World Health Organization Framework Convention on Tobacco Control (WHO FCTC) in 2006 and adopted policies to ban these marketing practices (United Nations n.d). Exposure to APS in West African countries that are also party to the WHO FCTC was much lower; for instance, 18.7% of participants in Nigeria reported exposure to tobacco APS in the past 30 days and 17.0% of participants in Senegal reported the same (World Health Organization 2012, World Health Organization 2015b). To decrease tobacco use, Mali should increase enforcement activities to restrict tobacco APS in line with its commitments to the WHO FCTC. Effective enforcement can be hampered by factors such as loopholes in the legislation that are exploited by the tobacco industry, over-reliance on media platform self-regulation, and lack of capacity to implement the legislation (Uang et al. 2018, Freeman et al. 2022, Ling et al. 2022).

Overall, this study found high reported consumption of SSBs (78%) and UPFs (67%) that appears to be in line with consumption in other similar settings, but data is very limited. Reported exposure to APS for SSBs (76%) and UPFs (57%) was also high and may be contributing to consumption levels. Young adults, who are in an important transition phase of developing dietary habits, were more likely to report consumption of these two unhealthy products and report exposure to APS for them. Reported consumption and reported exposure to APS for tobacco (11% and 38%, respectively) and alcohol (1% and 38%, respectively) were lower, likely reflecting the influence of the culture in Mali, although tobacco APS was relatively higher than other West African countries that are party to the WHO FCTC.

This study has a number of strengths and also important limitations. Strengths of this analysis include the intensive design process of the questionnaire, the comprehensive approach to APS exposure, and the unique nature of the study being the first to measure both consumption and exposure to APS for the four unhealthy commodities that contribute to NCD risk. A key limitation is the use of self-reported data to measure consumption and exposure to APS. Recall bias is driven by a variety of factors including degree of detail and time period, significance of event to participant, social desirability, and personal characteristics, as well as specifics of the interviewing technique (Coughlin 1990). As described above, the likely effect of the bias on measures of alcohol and tobacco consumption was lower rates, especially for women. Under-reporting of energy intake in food surveys has been reported in multiple countries, which suggests that the UPF and SSB consumption rates reported here may also be lower than actual (Scagliusi et al. 2008, Orcholski et al. 2015). There is some evidence that self-reported exposure to digital advertising accurately reflects campaign exposure and that recall of TV advertising is a valid indicator of exposure, although further research is needed in this area to understand potential bias in recall of marketing practices (Niederdeppe 2005, Romberg et al. 2020). We also recommend exploring the use of daily diaries, digital tracking apps, and dietary journals to determine the best methods for capturing alcohol, tobacco, and unhealthy food and drink consumption and exposure to APS (Singh et al. 2025, Swahn et al. 2025). Another limitation is that we did not include a specific definition of the terms APS as part of the survey and this may have led to some confusion among participants. Additionally, there may be limited generalizability of the study findings to non-urban settings where the APS environment is different.

Conclusion

This study is the first in Mali to focus on NCD risk factors through a CDoH lens. It reports novel data on consumption of SSBs and UPFs and exposure to marketing practices for SSBs and UPFs as well as alcohol and tobacco. The findings reflect the nutrition transition underway in low-income countries where unhealthy food and drink consumption is driving higher rates of NCDs. Results confirm that corporations are moving aggressively to market unhealthy food and drink in low-income countries. Systems-level interventions to address these CDoH are recommended including taxation, stronger enforcement, and further research.
