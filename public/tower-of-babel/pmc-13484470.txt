Assessment of phyto-remediation efficiency and crop productivity of castor-based rice mill effluent (RME) wetland system
Authors: Ashutosh Das, R. Sathya, Ranjan Kumar Mallick
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
For major paddy producing countries like India, rice mill effluent (RME) poses a persistent environmental challenge by dint of its high organic and nutrient loads. Among various methods of treatment of various wastewater, of late, including RME, constructed wetland has been emerging as a sustainable treatment option. Usage of Ricinus communis (commonly called Castor) has been explored herewith for being the wetland plant for remediation of RME, so as to achieve simultaneous effluent remediation and biomass production. In the present study the RME and soil samples were procured from Vallam suburb of Thanjavur district of Tamil Nadu (India) for being a part of a rich agro-belt of southern India. The biochemical analyses of the RME and soil indicate elevated TDS, EC, and nitrate levels. RME-pre-soaked castor seeds exhibited enhanced germination percentage (90%) and as high as 12-fold rise in vigor index, in comparison to controls, The results emerged from the wetland trials, moderate effluent dilution (50%) maximized plant height (52 cm), leaf area index (2.4), and removal efficiencies (65–72% for NO3––N and NH4+–N; >70% for COD/BOD). Field validation study further confirmed enhanced plant growth and yield in effluent-enriched soils, with seed yield increasing fourfold (22 → 91 g) and harvest index rising to 60.6%. The system performance was strongly supported by enhanced microbial activity, as indicated by a 77% increase in microbial biomass carbon and a 76% increase in dehydrogenase activity. Overall, these findings suggest that castor-based constructed wetlands have the potential to integrate wastewater phytoremediation with productive land use while maintaining the chemical safety of castor seed biomass within a circular bioresource framework.

Introduction

Rice milling industries play a pivotal role towards food security for major paddy-producing countries like India. However, unless duly treated, the rice mill effluent generated from rice mills, by dint of high organic matter, suspended solids, nutrients, and dissolved salts, may adversely affect soil structure, microbial balance, and crop productivity over time and can pose serious environmental threats and health hazards1. Untreated rice mill effluents are typically characterized by high organic load, suspended solids, ammonia, and dissolved salts, which can lead to eutrophication, oxygen depletion, and long-term soil and groundwater degradation. The conventional effluent treatment methods, such as chemical coagulation, aeration, and biological reactors, are often high energy-driven and cost-intensive, and hence appear unsuitable for small to medium-scale rice mills2.

Constructed wetlands (CWs) are engineered systems designed to mimic natural wetland processes, utilizing synergistic interactions between substrate, microorganisms, and macrophytes to facilitate sedimentation, filtration, microbial degradation, plant uptake, and nutrient transformation3. Microbial communities within constructed wetlands, particularly in the rhizosphere, are widely recognized as the primary drivers of organic matter degradation and nutrient transformation, often contributing more significantly than plant uptake alone4.

The selection of wetland plants often plays a critical role in the phyto-remediation potential and system stability of constructed wetlands2,3. In fact, several researchers have extensively studied traditional wetland macrophysics such as Phragmites australis, Typha latifolia, and Cyperus spp. and reported their effectiveness with regard to different types of wastewater. However, of late, there has been increasing interest in exploring non-traditional plants with added economic or ecological value. In light of this, castor (Ricinus communis), a hardy and fast-growing species, seems particularly promising due to its adaptability to marginal soils, tolerance to pollutants as well as production of usable biomass, oil, and by-products5.

Andreazza et al. have confirmed castor-plant to be an efficient hyperaccumulator of copper (up to 2 805 g/ ha) along with high phyto-mass production in vineyard soils contaminated with Cu mining waste6. Niu et al. further demonstrated the phyto-extraction of cadmium and lead by Ricinus communis, along with enhanced growth under metal stress conditions7. The studies in the author’s laboratory have shown treatment effectiveness of leachate using castor-based vertical flow constructed wetland system (VF-CW’s)8. This study reports not only tolerance of Castor plant to the leachate toxicity but also an enhanced growth of the plant (up to 60% during germination-stage & and up to 40% at post-germination stage).

In light of the preceding discussion, integrating castor plants in constructed wetland systems is likely to provide a novel, sustainable approach to rice mill effluent management. In the present study, we have investigated the operation, and performance of a castor-based wetland system under laboratory and field conditions, with emphasis on soil and water quality changes, plant growth responses, and biomass productivity (esp. the seed). By systematically comparing fresh and effluent-polluted soils, as well as bore-well and wastewater irrigation, the present work is aimed at exploring the potential of castor plant as a phytoremediator and productive crop in wastewater treatment systems in general and rice mill effluent, in particular.

Unlike conventional constructed wetland studies that primarily emphasize pollutant removal efficiency, the present work evaluates a dual-function system that integrates wastewater phytoremediation with crop productivity. However, despite extensive research on constructed wetlands, limited studies have systematically linked laboratory-scale wetland treatment performance with field-scale agronomic outcomes in rice-based agro-ecosystems. In this context, the present study provides a novel integrated assessment of a castor-based wetland system by combining controlled laboratory experiments with field-scale validation of soil quality and crop productivity. In addition, while the present study focuses on major nutrients and selected trace metals, other contaminants such as arsenic (As), which is a critical groundwater pollutant in many agro-ecosystems, were not evaluated and may be considered in future investigations.

The novelty of this study lies in integrating microbial activity indicators (microbial biomass carbon and dehydrogenase activity) with phytoremediation performance and agronomic productivity, thereby providing a more mechanistic understanding of plant–microbe interactions in constructed wetland systems.

Study area and acquisition of samples

The study area for the present research work is Vallam region, belonging to the Thanjavur District of Tamil Nadu, India (10.72°N latitude, 79.08°E longitude). The study area lies at an elevation of 75 m above mean sea level, located on a plateau to the south of Thanjavur (Fig. 2). This area was selected because it is a dominant rice-cultivation area, with secondary castor cultivation and is dotted with several rice mills. The Vallam region is representative of rice-based agro-ecosystems in plain river-network zones of southern India, where agro-processing activities coexist with intensive agriculture and generate persistent effluent-management challenges.

Rationale and strategy for sample acquisition:

Rice Mill Effluent (RME): Thanjavur District, also known as the rice bowl of southern India, exhibits an extensive rice field and numerous rice processing units, releasing rice-mill effluent (RME) to a common pollutant stream, draining to the common sewage treatment plan (STP), meant for handling domestic sewage of the locality, causing overloading and poor performance of STP’s. RME sample was collected from the common stream connecting the rice mills, before being drained to the STP. The representative RME samples were collected during mid-December, representing the major rice harvesting season in this area.

Castor oil-seed: The castor plant (Ricinus communis L.) has been selected as the primary agent for phytoremediation because it is known for its abundance, fast growth and economic potential (being a cash-crop). The castor- seeds were collected from the conventional castor cultivation sites in this area for germination, lab-scale wetland and field study. The growth pattern of the plant was evaluated for these conventional sites as well as the plants grown in a dedicated field, irrigated by RME.

Soil substratum: The typical soil of Vallam suburb of Thanjavur is primarily lateritic soil and cretaceous formations, unlike the aluvial soil in the main Cauvery deltaic region, thereby supporting both rice and castor. The representative soil was collected from the agricultural field, as well as from fields saturated by RME was used for the study.

Soil texture analysis and classification

Representative surface soil samples from virgin and from RME-enriched sites (0–30 cm; 12 Nos from each), collected from the study area using a stainless-steel auger (air-dried, crushed, and sieved through a 2 mm mesh-size). Bouyoucos hydrometer method9,10 was used to estimate sand, silt and clay proportion, wherein the soil (pre-treated with 5% sodium hexametaphosphate dispersant) was poured into a sedimentation cylinder with hydrometer, whose reading was recorded after 40 s and also at 2, representing sand and silt fractions, clay fraction being the remaining percentage out of 100, subject to temperature-correction. Based on the results, USDA soil texture triangle was constructed, where each virgin and effluent-rich field sample was represented by red and blue markers (“ο”), respectively. (vide Fig. 3)

Fig. 3USDA soil texture classification showing distribution of virgin and effluent-affected soil samples.

USDA soil texture classification showing distribution of virgin and effluent-affected soil samples.

The soil texture classification (Fig. 3) indicates that both virgin and effluent-treated soils fall within the loamy sand to sandy loam classes, suggesting that effluent application did not significantly alter the inherent soil texture. However, improvements in soil chemical and biological properties were observed, indicating that effluent irrigation primarily influenced soil functionality rather than its physical texture. This consistency in soil texture ensures that observed changes in plant growth and nutrient dynamics are attributable to effluent-induced chemical and biological modifications rather than inherent physical differences in soil structure.

Seed and wastewater procurement

Castor seeds (Ricinus communis L.) used in the study were collected from local conventional agricultural cultivation sites in the Vallam region, Thanjavur District, Tamil Nadu, India, with permission from the respective landowners/farmers. No protected or genetically modified plant material was used in this study.” Oil-seeds of castor plant of RME-exposed plants (observed to be of an average height of 5.73 ft, close to average height of castor plant cultivated conventionally) were also collected from the study area and were manually inspected by local skilled harvesters to retain only mature and physiologically sound seeds, which are observed to possess a dry seed weight of 0.144 g, confirming acclimatization of castor plant to the biochemical stress induced by the effluent. The seeds were manually cleaned, air-dried and stored in ambient conditions for usage in studies. Seeds were pre-soaked (12 h, 32 °C) in either freshwater (control) or rice mill effluent (as required by the experiments) so as to enhance seed hydration and uniform metabolic as per general germination protocols11,12.

Rice mill effluent samples were collected from the common discharge outlet at multiple time points during active milling operations and composited to capture temporal variability associated with processing intensity and seasonal conditions. The composite samples were preserved in air-tight containers (5 L, 4 °C) to minimize physicochemical alteration before analysis. Soil and water characterization study

The characteristics of soil and water samples were analysed before germination and wetland studies. Soil samples, collected from virgin agricultural field as well as from rice mill effluent–polluted soil (at 0–30 cm depth), both with similar soil types. Collected samples (air-dried, crushed, and sieved to < 2 mm) were analysed at the Soil Analysis Laboratory, Tamil Nadu Agricultural University, Aduthurai, using standard protocols for general parameters such as. texture, pH, electrical conductivity (EC), and CaCO3 content), Macronutrients such as organic carbon (OC), nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), and potassium (K).and the Micronutrients such as Fe, Mn, Zn, Cu, B, and S. Note, in this study, the soil fertility and suitability for castor cultivation were interpreted relative to standard fertilizer equivalents (N, P2O5, K2O, urea, superphosphate, potash, ZnSO4·7 H2O, CuSO4·5 H2O).

All analytical instruments were calibrated using certified standard solutions prior to measurement, and routine quality control procedures, including blanks, duplicate samples, and internal standards, were employed to ensure analytical accuracy and reproducibility. Water samples, collected from the local bore well and from the outlet of rice mill plants, were analysed for pH, colour, EC, TDS, TSS, salinity, NO3––N, NH4+–N, and Cl–, in the author’s laboratory as per APHA standard methods13 (Table 2).

Determination of cation exchange capacity (CEC)

The cation exchange capacity (CEC) of soil samples was determined to assess the nutrient retention potential and buffering capacity of virgin and rice mill effluent (RME)-polluted soils. Air-dried and sieved (< 2 mm) soil samples were analysed using the standard ammonium acetate (NH₄OAc) extraction method at pH 7.0. Briefly, 10 g of soil was saturated with 1 N ammonium acetate solution, followed by removal of excess salt through washing with ethanol. The adsorbed ammonium ions were subsequently displaced using 1 N sodium chloride (NaCl), and the released ammonium was quantified using distillation and titration.

The CEC values were expressed as cmol(+)/kg of dry soil. All analyses were performed in triplicate, and results are reported as mean ± standard error (SE). The measured CEC values were interpreted in relation to soil organic carbon content and effluent-induced changes in soil chemical properties.

Soil respiration measurement

Soil respiration was measured to evaluate microbial activity and biological response of soils under rice mill effluent (RME) exposure. Representative soil samples from virgin and effluent-polluted fields were incubated under controlled laboratory conditions using the alkali absorption method. Briefly, 50 g of moist soil (adjusted to field capacity) was placed in an airtight incubation chamber along with a vial containing 10 mL of 0.1 N NaOH solution to trap evolved CO2. The setup was incubated at 28 ± 2 °C for 24 h.

After incubation, the absorbed CO₂ was precipitated with BaCl₂ and quantified by titration of the residual NaOH with 0.1 N HCl using phenolphthalein as an indicator. Soil respiration rates were expressed as mg CO2–C kg–1 soil day–1. All measurements were carried out in triplicate, and results are presented as mean ± standard error (SE).

This parameter serves as an indicator of microbial metabolic activity and soil biological functioning under varying effluent exposure conditions.

Seed pre-treatment and germination evaluation

The seeds after due surface-sterilisation (through pre-soaking in 1% NaOCl at 32 °C for 12 h) are sown in red lateritic soil, adhering to completely randomized design (CRD) with 30 seeds per treatment (in triplicates) for germination study. Various germination indices, used to quantify viability, rate, and germination vigour, are given below:

The Germination Percentage (GP)12, a direct indicator of seed viability and treatment effectiveness, was calculated as:

where n = the total number of seeds successfully germinated; N = the total number of seeds sown.

The Mean Germination Time (MGT)14, a representation of the rate (i.e., slowness) and degree of uniformity (i.e., lower synchronicity, metabolic efficiency and stress tolerance) of germination was determined using:

where ti = duration (i.e., specific day since sowing) and ni = number of seeds germinated on day ti.

The Germination Index (GI)15, an index that integrates both the speed and spread of germination over time thereby suggesting adaptability to effluent-stress was computed as:

\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$GI = \sum \frac{{{n_i}}}{{{t_i}}}$$\end{document}

Finally, the Seedling Vigor Index (SVI)12 was calculated as:

SVI = GP × L

where GP = germination percentage (%) and L = mean seedling length (root + shoot, cm per seedling on the final day of observation). This composite index reflects the overall vigor, growth potential, and physiological integrity of germinated seedlings, linking early germination success to later establishment potential under variable water quality conditions.

Together, these indices provide a comprehensive assessment of the germination dynamics, physiological robustness, and early growth response of castor seeds under effluent exposure, allowing for comparative evaluation across treatments and environmental regimes.

At day 10, plumule and radicle lengths of ten randomly selected seedlings per replicate were measured with a digital caliper; fresh and dry masses were recorded after oven-drying at 70 °C for 48 h. All values were expressed as mean ± SE. Differences between G1 and G2 were analyzed via one-way ANOVA and Tukey’s HSD (p < 0.05). In fact, prior to statistical analysis, data were screened for outliers and missing values. No systematic missing data were observed, and extreme values, when detected, were verified against original measurements before inclusion or exclusion. All statistical analyses were conducted at a significance level of p < 0.05.

Experimental design of wetland system

A laboratory-scale constructed wetland system was established to evaluate the interactive effects of soil type and water quality on castor (Ricinus communis L.) growth and rice mill effluent treatment efficiency. The factorial experimental design included four treatment configurations (Fig. 4), combining two soil types—red lateritic soil and effluent-polluted soil—with two irrigation media—freshwater and rice mill wastewater. The treatments were arranged in a completely randomized factorial layout with three replicates per treatment to ensure statistical reliability.

Fig. 4Configuration of lab scale wetland studies for rice mill effluent treatment.

Configuration of lab scale wetland studies for rice mill effluent treatment.

Each unit consisted of a rectangular wetland bed containing 10 kg of substrate, with provision for controlled inflow and outflow to simulate semi-saturated wetland conditions. The effluent inflow was regulated to maintain consistent hydraulic retention, and the system was monitored under ambient laboratory temperature and a 12:12 h light–dark cycle. Effluent and soil samples were collected periodically to assess nutrient dynamics, contaminant reduction, and physicochemical changes throughout the study period.

Castor plants from the germination trial were transplanted into the wetland beds and monitored over the experimental duration. Plant growth and physiological performance were assessed using quantitative morphometric parameters, namely plant height, stem diameter, leaf count, and root length.

Plant height was measured from the soil surface to the apical tip of the shoot using a measuring tape, reflecting vertical growth and vegetative vigor.

Stem diameter was determined 1 cm above the soil surface using a digital vernier caliper, avoiding nodal regions; this indicated structural stability, nutrient translocation capacity, and resistance to mechanical stress.

Leaf count involved enumerating fully expanded, non-senescent leaves per plant, representing photosynthetic potential and productivity.

Root length was measured from the stem base to the tip of the longest root after careful uprooting and washing, indicating root system development and adaptability to soil–water interactions.

All measurements were taken from ten representative plants per treatment, and mean values from triplicate readings were used for statistical analysis. These growth indices collectively served as indicators of plant vigor, treatment efficiency, and system performance under varying soil–water combinations. Following the growth period, post-harvest analyses of soil and water were conducted to quantify nutrient fluxes and evaluate the overall phytoremediation potential of the castor-based wetland system.

Field evaluation and yield assessment

A field-scale evaluation was carried out to validate the laboratory findings and assess the productivity of castor (Ricinus communis L.) under contrasting soil conditions—virgin agricultural soil and rice mill effluent–polluted soil. The study site was located in the Vallam region, characterized by a tropical semi-arid climate with mean daily temperatures ranging between 31 and 34 °C and an annual precipitation of approximately 900 mm. Both sites were similar in soil texture (sandy loam) but differed in organic carbon, salinity, and nutrient accumulation owing to effluent exposure.

Prior to land preparation, soil samples (0–30 cm depth) were collected from each field to establish baseline fertility and contamination profiles following APHA12 and TNAU soil testing protocols. Each field was prepared by ploughing, levelling, and delineating uniform experimental plots measuring 3 m × 3 m, separated by 1 m buffer zones. Seeds from the same genetic lot used in the laboratory trials were sown directly at a spacing of 60 × 45 cm, ensuring consistency across treatments. Standard agronomic practices were followed, including manual weeding and irrigation, but no chemical fertilizers were applied to maintain experimental comparability.

Growth monitoring was conducted from the 14th to 24th week after planting (WAP). Plant height, stem diameter, and canopy spread were recorded biweekly using a measuring tape and digital vernier caliper. At maturity, yield attributes—including number of capsules per plant, seed yield per plant (g), and seed yield per hectare (t ha–1)—were determined after oven-drying at 70 °C for 48 h to a constant weight.

To quantify productivity and physiological efficiency, several agronomic indices were computed to provide a detailed assessment of growth performance, yield potential, and biomass allocation in castor plants grown under contrasting soil–effluent conditions.

The Crop Growth Rate (CGR)25 was calculated as:

\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$CGR = \frac{{{W_2}{\mathrm{-}}{W_1}}}{{{t_2}{\mathrm{-}}{t_1}}} \times \frac{1}{A}$$\end{document}

where W₁ and W₂ are the total plant dry weights (g) recorded at times t₁ and t₂, respectively, and A is the ground area (m2) occupied by the plants. CGR reflects the net dry matter accumulation rate per unit area per unit time and serves as a primary indicator of crop productivity and photosynthetic efficiency.

The Relative Growth Rate (RGR)25 was expressed as:

\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$RGR = \frac{{ln{W_2}{\mathrm{-}}ln{W_1}}}{{{t_2}{\mathrm{-}}{t_1}}}$$\end{document}

where W1 and W2 are plant dry weights at two successive time intervals (t1 and t2). RGR indicates the relative increase in biomass per unit of existing biomass, thus representing the efficiency of carbon assimilation and plant metabolic rate.

The Harvest Index (HI)26 was computed using:

This ratio measures the proportion of total plant biomass that is allocated to economic yield, providing insight into the efficiency of assimilate partitioning to reproductive structures.

The Seed Index (SI)27 was determined as:

\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$SI = \frac{{Weight{\mathrm{~}}of{\mathrm{~}}100{\mathrm{~}}Seeds}}{{100}}$$\end{document}

representing the mean seed weight, which indicates seed size and density—important parameters for evaluating reproductive success and genetic stability under different soil and effluent conditions.

The Biological Yield (BY)26 was defined as the total dry biomass (g plant–1 or t ha–1) produced above ground, encompassing stems, leaves, and reproductive structures, while the Economic Yield (EY)28 represented the harvested seed yield (t ha–1). These two parameters together provide a measure of both total productivity and the portion of yield that contributes directly to economic output.

Heavy metal analysis in soil and castor plant tissues

To evaluate the potential accumulation and translocation of trace metals under rice mill effluent–based wetland conditions, heavy metal concentrations were quantified in soil and castor plant tissues (root, shoot, and seed). Composite samples of wetland-treated soil and corresponding plant tissues were collected at harvest from the field validation plots described in section “Field evaluation and yield assessment”. All samples were washed with deionized water, oven-dried at 70 °C to constant weight, ground, and sieved (< 0.5 mm). Acid digestion was performed using a nitric acid–hydrogen peroxide method. Briefly, 0.5 g of dried sample was digested with 10 mL of concentrated HNO3 at 120 °C, followed by oxidation with 2 mL of H2O2 until a clear solution was obtained. The digests were filtered and diluted to 50 mL with deionized water.

Concentrations of Zn, Cu, Cd and Pb were determined using Atomic Absorption Spectrophotometry (AAS) (PerkinElmer model), calibrated with certified multi-element standards. Instrument calibration was verified using procedural blanks and standard reference solutions. All metal concentrations (mg kg⁻¹, dry weight), are reported as mean ± standard error (SE), as based on three independent replicate digestions and AAS measurements. All measurements were performed on composite samples and are reported as mean concentrations. It may be noted that, while routine soil fertility analysis included Fe, Mn, Zn and Cu, the present heavy-metal assessment focused on Zn, Cu, Cd and Pb due to their relevance for food safety and phytoremediation risk evaluation.

The translocation factor (TF)23 and bioaccumulation factor (BAF)24 were calculated to assess metal uptake and internal mobility:

Microbial biomass and enzyme activity analysis

Microbial biomass carbon (MBC) was determined using the chloroform fumigation–extraction method29,30. Fresh soil samples (10 g) were fumigated with ethanol-free chloroform for 24 h under vacuum in a desiccator and subsequently extracted with 0.5 M K2SO4 (soil: extractant ratio 1:4). Non-fumigated samples were extracted similarly. Extractable organic carbon was measured, and MBC was calculated using a kEC factor of 0.45.

Dehydrogenase activity (DHA) was estimated using the triphenyl tetrazolium chloride (TTC) reduction method31–33. Soil samples (5 g) were incubated with 3% TTC at 37 °C for 24 h, and the produced triphenyl formazan (TPF) was extracted using methanol and quantified spectrophotometrically at 485 nm. Both parameters were analysed in triplicate and expressed as mean ± standard error (SE), representing microbial abundance and metabolic activity, respectively.

The baseline soil analysis (Table 4) reveals significant differences between virgin agricultural soil and rice mill effluent-polluted soil. Polluted soil exhibited higher electrical conductivity (2.16 dS/m) and organic carbon (1.02%) compared to virgin soil (0.04 dS/m and 0.61%, respectively), indicating substantial enrichment from effluent inputs. Elevated concentrations of available nitrogen (231.2 mg/kg), phosphorus (68.4 mg/kg), and potassium (425 mg/kg) suggested nutrient accumulation consistent with organic wastewater loading. However, increased EC and soluble salts may also imply potential osmotic stress under prolonged irrigation34. Micronutrient levels (Fe, Mn, Zn, Cu) were similarly higher in polluted soils, enhancing short-term fertility but posing long-term risks of metal accumulation. Rice mill effluent analysis showed slight alkalinity (pH 7.9), elevated EC (2.08 dS/m), TDS (1,320 mg/L), and high NH4+–N (3.2 mg/L) relative to borehole controls. COD (358 mg/L) and BOD (180 mg/L) confirmed substantial organic loading typical of parboiling wastewater (APHA, 2024). These values defined the initial physicochemical context for biological and phytoremediation responses35.

Interestingly, there is a pronounced increase in soil electrical conductivity (53-fold), nitrogen (293-fold), and phosphorus (substantial enrichment, approximately 30-fold increase) after effluent exposure, which reveals possible nutrient accumulation and salinity enrichment, a compositional signature of parboiled rice mill effluent, which is known to be rich in organic and inorganic solutes. On the other hand, there is moderate elevation in organic carbon (67.2%) and, to a relatively lesser degree, potassium (8.4%), which apparently contributes to the short-term improvement in soil fertility, with associated with an increasing acidification regime as well, as evident from reduced pH (upto 10%). Micronutrient variations—particularly Zn and Cu increases (25–72%) seem to reflect effluent-driven mobilization of trace metals36. Although effluent-enriched soils exhibited improved short-term fertility, repeated long-term application of treated effluent may lead to cumulative salinity buildup, nutrient saturation, and shifts in soil microbial communities. Such changes can influence soil structure, permeability, and nutrient cycling, emphasizing the importance of controlled application rates and periodic soil monitoring for sustainable agronomic use37.

To further evaluate soil functional response under effluent exposure, additional parameters including cation exchange capacity and soil respiration were analysed, as discussed in the following section.

Soil functional properties: cation exchange capacity and microbial respiration

In order to further assess soil functional properties under effluent exposure, cation exchange capacity (CEC) and soil respiration were evaluated as indicators of nutrient retention and biological activity, respectively (Table 5). The results show that CEC increased from approximately 6.5 cmol(+)/kg in virgin soil to about 11.8 cmol(+)/kg in effluent-polluted soil, representing a substantial (~ 80%) enhancement in the soil’s capacity to retain exchangeable nutrients. This increase is consistent with the observed rise in organic carbon content and the accumulation of soluble ions derived from rice mill effluent, both of which contribute to increased surface charge and adsorption sites in soil matrices.

The enhanced microbial biomass and enzymatic activity observed in effluent-treated soils provide strong mechanistic support for the pollutant removal efficiency of the wetland system. The high COD and BOD removal (> 70%) can be attributed to microbial oxidation of organic matter, while nitrogen reduction reflects coupled nitrification–denitrification processes mediated by rhizosphere-associated microbial communities.

The increase in dehydrogenase activity, an indicator of intracellular oxidative metabolism, confirms active microbial respiration and substrate utilization. Dehydrogenase activity is widely regarded as a reliable indicator of intracellular microbial oxidative metabolism and overall soil biological activity31–33. In the present study, the elevated microbial biomass suggests that rice mill effluent serves as a carbon-rich substrate that stimulates microbial proliferation. The close agreement between microbial indicators, soil respiration, and nutrient dynamics demonstrates that plant–microbe interactions are central to the functioning of the constructed wetland system.

These processes seem to be primarily mediated by heterotrophic and nitrifying–denitrifying microbial consortia, which operate under aerobic and anoxic microenvironments within the wetland substrate. Similar observations have been reported in constructed wetland systems where microbial consortia, including heterotrophic bacteria and nitrifiers, dominate organic matter degradation and nitrogen transformation processes4,35.

Although metal concentrations in the present study were within permissible limits, long-term monitoring of plant tissues and soil is recommended to ensure compliance with environmental and agricultural safety standards.

The temporal growth trend (Fig. 9) attests a consistent improvement in plant stature and yield accumulation across all growth stages in wetland-treated plots. There is an accelerated seed yield gain observed after the 16th week. This may be because of enhanced nutrient remobilization and reproductive efficiency under moderate effluent-derived fertility44. Besides, the increments in both the yields, i.e., biological (150 g) and economic (90.9 g), reveal the integrated wetland system being actualised in terms of fertility restoration as well as contaminant attenuation.

The present study did not evaluate soil respiration or greenhouse gas emissions (e.g., CO2 or CH4 fluxes), which are important indicators of system-level sustainability and carbon balance. Future investigations incorporating gas flux measurements would provide a more comprehensive evaluation of the environmental performance of such wetland-based agro-ecosystems.

Thus, collectively, the present study demonstrates the scope of the application of treated rice mill effluent, being mediated through a plant-based wetland, has the potential to support enhanced castor productivity as well as the mitigation of nutrient losses and improvement in soil health. The field validation also corroborates the findings from controlled experimental setup, as outlined in preceding sections (viz. sections “Wetland system growth performance and stress response” & “Phytoremediation efficiency and nutrient uptake”), thereby exploring the potential of R. communis be considered a resilient and high-value bioresource for effluent-integrated agro-ecosystems.

Zinc and copper, which are essential micronutrients but potentially toxic at elevated levels, were moderately enriched in soil (48.17 and 25.06 mg kg–1, respectively) and showed substantial accumulation in roots (120.92 and 80.72 mg kg–1). However, their concentrations declined markedly in shoots and seeds, with seed Zn and Cu values of 16.79 and 13.12 mg kg–1, respectively. Cadmium and lead, the most toxic trace metals of regulatory concern, were present at relatively low levels in soil (0.84 and 39.10 mg kg–1) and were largely retained in roots, resulting in very low seed concentrations (0.09 mg kg–1 for Cd and 0.24 mg kg–1 for Pb).

Comparison with FAO–WHO Codex Alimentarius permissible limits indicates that seed concentrations of Cd and Pb remained well below the maximum allowable levels for edible plant materials, while Zn also remained within the safe range. Although Cu showed moderate enrichment in seeds, it is an essential micronutrient, and the observed level remains within acceptable ranges reported for oilseed and non-food industrial crop applications. The low bioaccumulation factors for Cd and Pb in seeds and translocation factors well below unity further confirm the restricted mobility of these metals from roots to reproductive tissues.

These results demonstrate that the castor-based constructed wetland system effectively immobilizes potentially hazardous metals in below-ground biomass while maintaining the chemical safety of economically valuable seed fractions, in consonance with similar findings by researchers46. This behavior supports the dual role of R. communis as a phytoremediator and as a safe biomass crop within effluent-irrigated agro-ecosystems. As outlined in preceding paragraphs, the results indicate that integration of castor into constructed wetland systems enables simultaneous effluent remediation and agronomic productivity, extending the functional scope of wetlands beyond treatment alone. In fact, beyond pollutant removal and crop productivity, the castor-based wetland system contributes to broader environmental sustainability by enabling nutrient recycling, reducing freshwater demand, and promoting safe reuse of agro-industrial effluents. Such integrated systems align with circular bioeconomy models by converting waste streams into productive agricultural inputs.

Role of microbial processes in wetland functioning

The performance of the castor-based constructed wetland system can be understood through the interaction between plant uptake and microbially mediated biogeochemical processes. The significant increase in microbial biomass carbon and dehydrogenase activity indicates that effluent enrichment enhances both microbial abundance and metabolic activity in soil systems29.

Microbial communities in the wetland substrate are responsible for the degradation of organic pollutants, as evidenced by high COD and BOD removal efficiencies, and for nutrient transformations such as nitrification and denitrification. Root exudates from Ricinus communis likely create a biologically active rhizosphere, facilitating microbial colonization and enzymatic activity.

The strong agreement between microbial indicators, soil respiration, and nutrient removal patterns demonstrates that microbial processes are integral to wetland performance and contribute significantly to both pollutant attenuation and soil fertility enhancement.

In such systems, plant roots primarily facilitate microbial activity through oxygen transfer and exudate release, whereas the majority of biochemical transformations are carried out by microbial communities inhabiting the rhizosphere and substrate37,40.

Conclusions

The present study indicates that castor (Ricinus communis)–based constructed wetlands have the potential to reduce nutrient and organic loads from rice mill effluent while supporting biomass production. Laboratory and field observations suggest that moderate effluent dilution (≤ 50%) is associated with improved plant growth, physiological stability, and pollutant attenuation, with removal efficiencies exceeding 70% for COD and BOD and 65–72% for nitrogen species. Field-scale evaluation further showed enhanced growth and yield attributes in effluent-enriched soils, indicating agronomic benefits under the studied conditions. While these results highlight the promise of integrating castor into constructed wetland systems for decentralized effluent reuse, long-term monitoring of soil salinity, nutrient accumulation, and metal dynamics remains necessary to ensure environmental sustainability. In addition, heavy-metal analysis showed that Cd and Pb were largely retained in root tissues, with seed concentrations remaining below FAO–WHO permissible limits, indicating that castor-based wetland systems can achieve remediation without compromising biomass safety. The system thus demonstrates potential as an integrated phytoremediation–agroecosystem model under controlled effluent reuse strategies. While microbial biomass and enzyme activity measurements provide strong indirect evidence of microbial involvement, the absence of microbial community structure analysis (e.g., 16 S rRNA sequencing or functional gene profiling) limits the ability to identify specific microbial taxa and pathways responsible for pollutant transformation.
