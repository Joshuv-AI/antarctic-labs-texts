Examining the roles, relationships and power dynamics shaping universal health system policy processes in high- and upper-middle-income countries: a scoping review
Authors: Luisne Mac Conghail, Sarah Parker, Anne Matthews, Sara Burke
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPolicies for universal health systems aim to provide equitable access to quality healthcare. However, achieving this remains a complex goal in high and upper-middle-income countries. Despite widespread efforts, universal health system reforms vary significantly, shaped by historical, political, and economic contexts. Understanding the policy process, including the roles of various actors and institutions, is essential to improving policy effectiveness and achieving equitable health systems. This scoping review examines the literature on policy processes, stakeholder influences, and contextual factors shaping policies for universal health system reforms.MethodsA systematic search of peer-reviewed and grey literature from 2014 to 2024 was conducted using five academic databases and Google Scholar. Seventy-four studies focused on policies for universal health systems in high and upper-middle-income countries. Data was examined in two phases. First, a descriptive analysis explored the geographic and economic contexts of the studies and their representation across stages of the policy cycle, including agenda-setting, formulation, adoption implementation, and evaluation. Topp et al.’s framework was then used to examine the influence of key actors, focusing on their relationships, power sources, and societal expressions of power.ResultsThe review revealed significant geographical disparities, representing only 30% of eligible countries. Most studies focused on early policy stages, with limited attention to implementation and evaluation. A predominance of qualitative research facilitated contextual insights, yet the underrepresentation of quantitative and mixed methods approaches restricted opportunities for integrated analysis. Crises and ideological shifts were drivers of policy momentum, catalysing changes in universal health system reforms. Governments played a central role, supported or contested by civic groups, professional associations, and academia. Media often influenced public discourse and policy perceptions, amplifying or challenging reform narratives. Persistent challenges included fragmented systems, equity-efficiency tensions, and definitional ambiguities, undermining policy coherence and sustainability.ConclusionsThe review underscores the need for a broader ‘universal’ framework for understanding health system reform and prioritising equity, quality, and sustainability. Adaptive health systems, robust institutions, and standardised frameworks to address political, economic, and ideological barriers are crucial. Future research must evaluate equity impacts, refine policy design, and explore mechanisms to align reforms with universal health system principles and goals.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13773-8.

Background

The central aim of all health systems is to promote, protect and restore the health of populations [1, 2]. Recognised as a fundamental human right, access to healthcare should ensure equitable access to essential services, free from discrimination [3]. This encompasses four critical components: availability (adequate functioning health facilities and services for all), accessibility (eliminating barriers for marginalised groups), acceptability (culturally appropriate people-centred), and quality (safe, effective, and efficient services) [4].

To uphold the principle of health as a human right, policies for universal health systems are globally advocated for as a crucial mechanism to ensure equitable access to essential healthcare services [1, 3–5]. Such policies are considered essential for improving population health, driving economic progress, and promoting social justice [6]. They are also vital for reducing disparities, strengthening comprehensive health governance, and fostering resilient health systems [1]. Policies for universal health systems operationalise the right to health by establishing frameworks that define who is covered, what access they have to health and social care services, and the extent of financial protection. These foundational pillars, underpinned by equity and quality as core objectives, ensure that the principle of health as a human right is translated into practice [7–10].

Throughout this paper, ‘policies for universal health systems’ is used as an inclusive term to capture diverse approaches aimed at achieving universal access to quality healthcare. This terminology avoids a narrow focus on individual elements and reflects the varied terms used in health systems literature such as ‘universal health care’, ‘universal health coverage’, ‘universal health system’, ‘universal healthcare access’, or ‘universal insurance coverage’ [7, 9–11].

The World Health Organisation (WHO) defines universal health coverage as ensuring that all individuals have access to quality health services they need, when and where they need them, without financial hardship. This encompasses the entire continuum of care, from health promotion and prevention to treatment, rehabilitation and palliative care [12]. The WHO supports universal health coverage as a fundamental principle for health systems [8, 13]. The inclusion of universal health coverage in the 2030 Agenda for the Sustainable Development Goals (SDG) has further elevated its prominence. Specifically, SDG Target 3.8 calls for achieving universal health coverage by ensuring financial risk protection, access to quality essential healthcare services, and safe, effective, affordable medicines and vaccines for all [14].

Despite global commitments, addressing health inequalities through policies for universal health systems is yet to be realised. Policy momentum and implementation challenges persist, hindering progress in advancing this agenda [6, 15]. Developing policies to support universal health systems is a highly dynamic and multifaceted process, requiring complex decisions on resource allocation, service coverage, service delivery, and cost management [2, 16]. These decisions demand substantial public resource commitments through revenue generation, fund pooling, and service purchasing or provision, making them inherently political [8, 17]. Consequently, health policy development is driven not only by public health needs but also by the influence of political, economic, and civic stakeholders as well as the broader political-economic context.

Previous research highlighted the importance of understanding the multifaceted and complex dynamics that shape the development of health systems and policy reform [18–22]. These dynamics include the lasting impacts of historical legacies on pathways forward, the influence of the political economy, and the intricate and dynamic relationships among actors within and between state and society [19–21, 23]. Central to this body of work is the role of power and the associated actions of political, economic and civic actors, each bringing their knowledge, values and interests to bear on the health system and policy processes [21, 22, 24].

Previous studies have explored various aspects of universal health system reforms and policies, including mapping evidence on care packages [25], synthesising conceptual literature and global debates [7, 11, 26], exploring implementation approaches [27–29] assessing historical foundations [9, 10, 16] and reviewing political factors [30]. Many studies have highlighted a lack of thorough examination of the complex social environments and political economies where actors shape policy and practice, affecting health system quality and access [20, 31–34]. Few studies have explicitly focused on the roles and influence of key actors or the power dynamics underpinning universal health systems policy processes.

This review aims to address this evidence gap, contributing to a richer understanding of policymaking processes and their underlying mechanisms [31, 35]. Drawing on Topp et al. [21], this review explores how power is expressed, leveraged and experienced to shape health policy and systems.

Methods

Scoping reviews are particularly well suited for synthesising existing knowledge and identifying research gaps. Scoping reviews differ from systematic reviews by focusing less on assessing quality and on the re-analysis of existing study results. Instead, they aim to comprehensively capture, identify, and represent a broader spectrum of literature, discern and examine available evidence, pinpoint knowledge gaps, and identify critical characteristics or factors associated with a given concept [36]. The scoping review followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) extension for scoping reviews checklist [37] to ensure rigour in reporting and adhere to the framework methodology of Arksey & O’Malley [38] including the following stages: (i) identifying the research question, (ii) identifying relevant studies, (iii) study selection, (iv) charting the data, and (v) collating, summarising, and reporting the results.

Identifying the research question

This scoping review examines the nature, volume, and scope of published research on the policy processes related to universal health systems, focusing on how political, economic, and civic actors influence these processes in high and upper-middle-income countries. Identification of relevant studies

The literature search was conducted using the following databases: Embase, Web of Science, PsycINFO, EBSCOhost, and Scopus. It was also supplemented by searching Google Scholar for relevant grey literature, specifically in search of policy reports. The search only included peer-reviewed articles published in English after 2014. It focused on the question’s three main concepts: health policy reform, universal health systems, and the influence of stakeholders. An expert subject librarian aided in the design and refinement of the search strategy. Search terms were piloted to assess the appropriateness of databases and keywords and further refined. To be eligible, articles focused on health reforms aimed at achieving universal health systems in high and upper-middle-income countries, explicitly examining access to quality care, financial coverage, or the governance of these reforms, with explicit connections to policymaking within the specified timeframe. Comparative studies were included only if results for eligible countries were reported separately. Articles documenting healthcare trends, indicators, or general overviews of universal health policies without detailed discussion of reform processes or policy development were excluded. Additionally, papers focused solely on theory building or methodological development without empirical applications of real-world policy processes or reforms were excluded, as this review aims to understand the policy mechanisms behind healthcare reforms through the analysis of original research and empirical syntheses’. Clinical trials, disease-specific research, or sector-specific studies concentrating on a single function or aspect of the health system (e.g., primary care or workforce planning) were excluded. These studies do not align with the broader scope of this review, which aims to examine systemic health reforms integral to universal health policy efforts.

The screening process was facilitated by Covidence (an online open-access screening software tool). Initially, one reviewer (LMC) screened all identified articles by title. LMC then assessed abstracts for eligibility, with 20% independently reviewed by a second reviewer (SP, SB, and AM). Eligible abstracts were retrieved to full text, and two researchers independently reviewed each full-text article. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion within the review team.

Charting the data

Data were extracted from the included articles using a standardised data extraction tool on Covidence, and the main results were charted to align with the study’s objectives. The extracted information included the author, year of publication, title, country, economic status, study aims, study design, stage of the policy cycle, universal health definition used, universal health policy reform under analysis, identified stakeholders and their influence, as well as other barriers and facilitators to the policy process. One reviewer (LMC) performed the initial data charting, and a 10% sample of the charted data was independently reviewed by the other reviewers (SP, SB, and AM) for quality assurance, checking errors, inconsistencies, and discrepancies in the charted data. Discrepancies or disagreements between the primary reviewer’s extracts and the quality reviewers’ assessments were discussed among the team to reach a consensus. In cases where consensus was not immediately achieved, a third reviewer was consulted to resolve any issues. Periodic team meetings were held to standardise interpretations and ensure that the extraction process was consistent across all reviewers. This process ensured that the data extracted were valid and reliable, maintaining the overall quality of the review.

A critical appraisal was not conducted in this scoping review, as the primary objective was to map the breadth and scope of available literature rather than assess the quality of individual studies. Scoping reviews, unlike systematic reviews, focus on identifying themes, gaps, and patterns in the research rather than evaluating the methodological rigour of each study. Given the exploratory nature of this review, which aims to understand how political, economic, and civil actors influence universal health policy reforms, the research team decided that a critical appraisal was not appropriate.

Collating, summarising, and reporting

The data analysis was conducted in two phases. The first phase employed a descriptive analysis approach to identify the characteristics of the studies, focusing on the geographic and economic settings in which policies for universal health system reforms are situated and identifying specific policy reforms across the distinct stages of the policy process.

In the second phase, an inductive approach was first used to identify themes and patterns emerging from the data to address the study’s objectives, which aimed to identify the key actors involved in universal health system reforms and understand their influence on the policy process. Once these themes were established, they were then mapped deductively onto Topp et al.’s [21] framework for identifying and refining discrete areas of inquiry for power-focused health policy and systems. This framework, consisting of three main empirical sites of power: [1] actor relationships and networks, which investigate how individuals and organisations, along with their values and intentions, influence policy decisions and shape outcomes through their interactions; [2] sources of power, which examine the resources actors draw upon, such as material capital, technical expertise, and political or bureaucratic influence impact policy processes; and [3] societal flows and expressions of power, which analyse how power is expressed, leveraged, and experienced to shape health policy and systems, ultimately influencing health equity. This combined inductive-deductive process allowed for both the emergence of new insights and the systematic organisation of results within an established theoretical structure.

The studies included in this review predominantly focus on analysing the conditions under which policies for universal health systems are developed and implemented within specific country contexts (see supplementary file 1). Many employ political economy frameworks and analysis to explore the interplay between economic interests, power structures, and policy outcomes [16, 41–47], while Kingdon’s multiple streams framework [48] is also frequently applied to elucidate how elements, such as political will, public health needs, and policy proposals converge to create critical windows of opportunity for health policy change [41, 44, 49–54].

In addition to these dominant frameworks, a range of other theoretical approaches are utilised to examine universal health policy reform, including stakeholder analysis [41, 42], policy coalitions [58, 59], veto point theory [43, 57, 58], historical institutionalism [59, 60], and path dependencies [57, 60–62]. Most studies adopt a multidimensional approach, integrating several theories to provide a comprehensive understanding of health system reforms [41–43, 47, 53, 60, 63].

Numerous studies emphasise practical lessons drawn from the policy process, illustrating the complexity of reforms and the factors influencing their success or failure [16, 47, 64–76]. A subset of the literature examines the role of media in shaping public opinion and policy discourse, highlighting how media narratives influence the political context surrounding health reform debates [65, 77, 78]. Several studies also consider the role of crisis, including economic shocks [52, 60, 62, 79–81] and the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on universal health policies [82–84]. While other works analyse the impact of privatisation within universal health systems [53, 57, 61, 62, 67, 85–89].

Finally, several studies engaged directly with the public, gathering perspectives on universal health reforms, providing insights into citizens perceptions of universal health reforms, their satisfaction with these policies and their role in shaping these policies [71, 90–97]. Other research focuses on healthcare workers’ views on these reforms [85, 98].

Key factors influencing universal health policy reform

Within the analysis, we identified several themes across the included literature, informed by Topp et al.’s guide [21] for power analysis in health policy and systems research. The review categorises findings into [1] actor relationships and networks [2], sources of power, and [3] the ways power is expressed and experienced in societal contexts. The themes presented below provide critical insights into the universal health system policy process, highlighting the roles of political, economic, and civic actors and how they influence various stages of the policy cycle.

While the term universal health systems are utilised in this paper, in the discussion below, the authors maintain the original terms used by each study’s authors to reflect their specific context and specific aspects of universal health system policy.

Actor relationships and networks

The first theme highlights how networks of individuals and organisations shape health policies through advocacy, values, and alliances. Together, these dynamics illustrate how networks of individuals and organisations with varying beliefs and intentions unite to support, modify, or resist health reforms.

Civic actors

Civic actors, including the public, civil society groups, and labour unions, are cited as playing pivotal roles in shaping policies for universal health systems, particularly during the agenda-setting stage. The literature identifies that the public tends to advocate for an expanded governmental role in ensuring access to healthcare [74, 92, 94, 99, 100]. However, as demonstrated in South Africa, public trust in the government to implement meaningful reforms can be low, as identified by Weimann and Stuttaford [95].

In Chile, Bossert and Villalobos Dintrans [51], and in South Korea, Kim and Lee [56], document how public unrest over persistent healthcare inequality pushed universal health coverage onto the policy agenda. The two-tier health system in these countries, perceived as favouring the wealthy, drove dissatisfaction and demands for reform [51, 56]. Similarly, in Ireland, widespread dissatisfaction with the country’s two-tier system, dividing healthcare access based on financial means, led to public support for universal healthcare [52, 79, 91, 101]. In China, public discontent with increasing healthcare privatisation sparked a grassroots movement advocating reforms to restore equitable access to care [16, 44, 66, 69, 76, 97]. Conversely, Do Rosário Costa and Vaitsman [57], observed that civic activism in Brazil struggled to unify around a comprehensive health agenda, which may have contributed to fragmented and less effective efforts towards health system equity.

Civil society can also counterbalance corporate interests in some contexts. In Malaysia, Croke et al., [42] and Minh et al., [102] highlighted how significant investments in private-sector financing for universal health coverage were blocked by civil society, demonstrating grassroots activism power to prevent market-driven reforms [42, 102].

Professional associations

Professional medical associations often have complex and decisive roles throughout the policy cycle particularly in agenda-setting, policy formulation, implementation and they are also key in resisting certain policies for universal health systems. While their positions may be influenced by professional or economic interests, these associations are also shaped by ideological beliefs and interpretations of how proposed reforms will impact healthcare.

In Turkey, Agartan [50, 103], Agartan and Kuhlmann [85] and Harris [104], highlighted how professional associations resisted the Health Transformation Program and advocated for a national health service model. They opposed financing reforms like premiums, performance-based pay, and privatisation, viewing them as policies which erode equitable access to care [50, 103, 104]. This resistance to market-driven reforms was also evident in Mexico as documented by Gomez-Dantes et al., 2015 and Nigenda et al., 2016 [41, 105], and in South Africa by Bust et al., 2023 [77] where medical professionals opposed health system changes perceived to be based on market principles.

In Thailand, a network of doctors played a pivotal role in the design of the Universal Coverage Scheme (47,56,70). These medical professionals leveraged their politically protected positions to push the reform agenda, demonstrating how doctors can influence policy formulation.

In Uruguay, Ballart and Fuentes [108], noted that political divides within medical associations influenced their stance on universal health coverage, with some fearing reforms would limit their independence. Similar concerns over loss of control and the potential strain on the system were reported in Ireland [52]. Fox and Choi [43] also noted in the United States, that healthcare professionals worried that a single-payer system would lead to longer wait times, overuse, and reduced patient choice, driving up costs and resulting in rationed care.

Polak et al. [93] also emphasised that healthcare providers had a significant influence during reform implementation due to their control over service delivery. They concluded that doctors in Poland support or resist reforms based on how funding mechanisms and service structures align with their ideological views or financial interests, highlighting the role of economic incentives and beliefs in shaping attitudes to policies for universal health systems.

Labour unions

Labour unions, as collective representatives of workers, often influence various stages of the policy cycle by advocating for the protection of employment-based benefits and opposing market-driven reforms, particularly during the policy formulation, adaption and implementation stages. In Chile [51], medical unions and social actors opposed the two-tier health system and market-driven reforms, supporting a tax-funded, NHS-style system. In Vermont, United States, MacNaughton [106, 107] highlighted the Vermont Workers’ Centre’s ‘Health Care is a Human Right’ campaign, which successfully shifted the discourse from economics to human rights, achieving significant progress toward universal, affordable healthcare policies. However, the literature also suggests that labour unions have sometimes resisted policy reforms for universal health systems. Fox and Choi [43] noted in New York in the United States, unions feared that decoupling healthcare from employment would weaken their influence and lead to inferior health insurance for their members, thus favouring the status quo over universal health policy reforms. Machado [46], highlighted in Argentina how state-union coalitions maintained employee-based healthcare, which hindered the development of a truly universal health system. Harris and Libardi Maia [61], documented in Thailand that labour unions pressured companies to provide private health plans, believing the private sector provided better quality care than government services.

Economic interests

The literature also documents, albeit not extensively, the role of economic agents in shaping policy formulation for universal health systems. Béland et al.’s [86] analysis of Canada, Sweden, Denmark and the UK indicates that more mature health systems are increasingly adopting market-based approaches. Minh et al. [99] highlight ASEAN country’s struggle to balance the for-profit private sector’s demands with the goals of equitable healthcare access. In Uruguay, Ballart and Fuentes [100] demonstrate how universal healthcare was achieved through financing private and third-sector providers, reflecting a market-oriented ideology.

In Turkey, Dorlach and Yegen [53] report that private sector resistance to reforms stemmed from concerns over strict regulation and low reimbursement rates, which eventually reshaped policy formulation and final implementation. In Thailand, Harris [104] highlights opposition from private hospitals, which feared reforms would undermine their financial stability and autonomy. Croke et al., [42] note that private and corporate interests, including private hospitals, supported Malaysia’s health reforms to expand their market share in a public sector-dominated system. These reforms aimed to reduce the state’s role in healthcare financing and delivery, favouring privatisation and insurance-based models.

Kim and Lee [56] in South Korea and Yuda [60], and Marzoeki et al. [72] in Indonesia document strong business opposition to the merging of insurance systems, primarily due to concerns about higher employer contributions. Marzoeki et al. [72] further explores how critics framed mandatory reforms as human rights issues, arguing that they infringed on individual freedoms. Some private organisations supported the mandatory nature of the reform but opposed the government’s monopolistic control over its administration. In the United States, national health insurance, faced strong opposition from private sector actors who stood to lose business [43, 100]. McDonough [73] illustrates Vermont’s successful near-universal coverage through a public-private framework, illustrating how balancing ideological and economic preferences was central to gaining political support for universal health. As Gómez-Dantés [41] notes, the development of Seguro Popular was marked by features such as contracting private providers and promoting efficiency competition. However, a change to a left-leaning government brought a shift for health reforms aimed at universality, equity, and anti-corruption, dismantling previous structures in contrast with global trends favouring public-private partnerships [74].

Sources of power

The second theme examines the resources and authority different actors leverage to influence key policy process stages, including agenda setting, formulation, implementation, and evaluation. These sources demonstrate how resources, authority, and institutional roles converge and are strategically leveraged to direct health policy processes.

Leveraging academia and think tanks to influence health policy

The academic community, concerned with pursuing research, education, and scholarship, also play a crucial role in shaping health reforms by evaluating healthcare issues, providing evidence to guide policy formulation, and advocating for reform. However, political leaders and policymakers selectively draw on research that aligns with their ideological perspectives, a common theme across various country contexts in the identified literature. Examples from countries like China, the United States and Mexico demonstrate that entrenched ideological beliefs often resist empirical evidence, with policymaking often shaped by the current political climate [43, 64, 74, 77].

In China, academic institutions have been instrumental in providing empirical data to inform health reforms, particularly in health financing and service delivery. Scholars such as Yu [66], Korolev [97], and Hsiao et al. [44] illustrate how China exemplifies the tension between evidence-based policy and ideologically motivated decision-making. Initially, pro-market and growth-orientated healthcare policies received academic endorsement, with prominent scholars supporting reforms that aligned with this mantra put forward by the then Government. However, a joint report evaluation by the Development Research Centre of the State Council, Beijing University, and the World Health Organisation later highlighted that these policies undermined efficiency and equity. This shift in the use of scholarship and evidence in this report brought legitimacy to critiques of the healthcare system and played a crucial role in shifting the political narrative towards reform [44, 66, 69, 76, 109].

In Ireland, academics contributed to developing reforms, aimed at transitioning to a universal health system as detailed by Burke et al. [79]. A team of experts worked with a parliamentary committee to provide the evidence base for drafting macro-level health system reforms. Similarly, experts set the policy agenda for universal health insurance in Ireland in 2011, though political and financial challenges stalled the initiative [52, 101]. In Indonesia, Yuda, 2023 and Marzoeki et al., 2024 described researchers advocating for universal health coverage, providing empirical evidence and public support for reform [60, 72].

In Turkey, academia both supported and critiqued health reforms. International organisations like the WHO, OECD, and the World Bank collaborated with Turkish academics to collect data and guide the Health Transformation Program. However, as noted by Agartan [50, 63, 103] the heavy involvement of the IMF and World Bank shaped the reform in a market-oriented direction, which some academics contested as inequitable, creating a split in the academic community [50, 63, 103].

The literature establishes the influence of the WHO, World Bank and IMF in promoting universal health coverage, especially in upper-middle-income countries [46, 60, 61, 63, 66–68, 76, 79, 89, 108], providing technical expertise and funding, and shaping reform direction. However, domestic academics often contested these success narratives put forth by these organisations, arguing that the reforms did not fully account for local contexts or achieve universal coverage [49, 98, 103]. For example, Dorlach and Yegen [52] argue that Turkey’s so called ‘achievement’ of universal health coverage remains segmented and inequitable as access to healthcare is contingent on the regular payment of insurance contributions. Baggott and Lambie argue that think tanks and international organisations tend to promote market-based policies [64]. Giovanella et al. [89] extends this critique, noting the WHO’s disproportionate focus on health financing over broader universal health systems considerations.

Interestingly, despite their high use in many countries [110] Hsaiao et al. [44], are the only authors to identify consultancy companies role in deliberating and influencing policies for universal health systems.

The central role of government in health reforms

Governments are consistently the most influential actor across all policy process stages. Agartan [63] emphasises the government’s pivotal role in Turkey’s agenda-setting, policy formulation and adoption. Conservative political parties backed universal health coverage policies to gain public support aligned with neoliberal principles, such as means testing and private sector involvement [44, 99]. Similarly, Gomez and Reich highlight the central role of ruling political parties in shaping Mexico’s health system, where the government sets universal health policies as part of the political agenda [41]. In Indonesia, Yuda [60] concluded that the government leveraged health policy reform toward a universal health system for political gain, aiming to bolster declining public support. Research from Thailand supports this, with Tangcharoensathien [47] arguing that adopting universal health coverage is a political rather than a technical endeavour.

Political polarisation is identified as a threat to the sustainability of universal health reforms. McDonough [72] observed that opposition to universal health insurance often stemmed from partisan divides rather than sector-specific interests. New administrations may also be incentivised to repeal and replace policies from their predecessors [84], as noted in the United States [100] and Mexico [75]. Knaul et al. [84] argue that ensuring the longevity of health reforms requires insulating them from political pressures. This requires regular innovation and adaptation strategies to enforce constitutional provisions, thus safeguarding them from changing administrations. Additionally, broad communication strategies foster ownership among various stakeholders and are essential for maintaining both political and public support for health system reforms [84].

The number of veto points, defined as an actor’s ability to prevent policy from progressing through the legislature, is documented as critical to the success or failure of universal health system reforms. Bossert and Villalobos Dintrans [51] in Chile and Fox and Choi [43] in the United States, concluded that too many veto points within government approval processes often acted as barriers to reform adoption. In contrast, China’s top-down model, with few veto points, allows for rapid reform implementation [44].

Turkey is cited as being successful in overcoming veto points [16, 58, 63, 85, 104, 108]. Its policy adoption success was partly attributed to the involvement of technical experts, such as physicians and economists, enlisted by the Minister for Health. These experts played key roles in targeting veto point actors, negotiating and forming alliances to overcome policy blockages [69, 103, 104]. Ministers of Finance often act as critical stakeholders capable of obstructing reforms, as documented in Turkey [58], Indonesia [72], and China [76]. To overcome this, reform strategies emphasised focus on economic benefits and ensured alignment with broader fiscal goals.

The literature also highlights the importance of minister-driven or presidential-driven reforms, with political champions crucial for reform success. This is evident in studies from Mexico [41, 105], Uruguay [108], South Africa [77], Malaysia [42], and Thailand [55]. Political champions can galvanise commitment and drive action, as demonstrated in China, where Yu [66] and Mao et al. [70] discuss leadership and political commitment to reform success. In contrast, studies from Korea [56] and Ireland [81] reveal a gap between universal health policy and a lack of governmental commitment to actualise it.

In New York, United States, Fox and Choi [43] noted how bureaucratic resistance to reform often focused on concerns about increased administrative burden and restructuring, overshadowing potential health service benefits. This focus on operational challenges, rather than system improvements, is not unique to the United States. Atun et al. [67] found similar bureaucratic inertia in Latin American countries, particularly in the context of insurance regulation. Harris [104] documents a comparable issue in Turkey, where bureaucratic hesitancy has complicated healthcare reform implementation, highlighting the tension between administrative capacities and policy goals.

To implement universal health system reforms, governments often use incremental changes. This strategy minimises perceptions of reform as an immediate threat, allowing for negotiation, adjustment, and evaluation of reforms before scaling up. Such an approach has been documented in Turkey [63], Uruguay [108], Ireland [79], Thailand [47] and South Africa [88] where funding increased incrementally, or pilot projects served as testing grounds for broader reforms.

The European Union (EU) is also identified as a driver in shaping reform, as noted by Agartan [63] and Ali Jadoo [96]. As part of Turkey’s EU accession bid, the country aligned its health reform programme with EU health policies. Similarly, Indonesia received EU technical assistance during its universal health coverage reforms [60]. Policy transfer and learning are documented in other cases, such as Turkey learning from Mexico and Thailand [103], Uruguay learning from Spain and France [108], Germany and the Netherlands, learning from the United States and Canada [111], and Indonesia learning from Germany [60]. However, despite these examples, there is a lack of data on the role of supernational institutions like the EU and ASEAN in universal health system policies. Although often referenced in a comparative context [62, 74, 102] their influence on universal policies has not been explored in depth.

Political change

The literature consistently identifies political transitions as critical in shaping universal health system reforms. Changes in government often result in significant policy shifts, which can open or close policy windows for universal health systems.

The military dictatorships in Latin America, apart from Cuba, severely undermined human rights, suppressed democratic freedoms and curtailed investments in the social sectors, including publicly funded and delivered health systems. These regimes deepened socioeconomic and health inequalities by restricting citizens’ entitlements and marginalising vulnerable populations. In response, movements in Argentina, Brazil, Chile, Peru, Uruguay, and Spain mobilised to restore democracy, address inequalities, and reclaim citizens’ rights to healthcare [59, 62, 67, 74]. In Uruguay, for instance, Ballart and Fuentes [108] report that the election of a left-leaning coalition in the mid-1980s, after decades of authoritarian rule, marked a turning point, paving the way for substantial universal health system reforms. In Brazil, political transitions similarly catalysed significant health policy changes, highlighting the broader impact of political change on health reforms [57].

In Turkey, a change in government similarly created the conditions necessary for major health reforms [63, 103]. Ireland also experienced health reform momentum during political instability, where the absence of a majority government allowed opposition parties to influence the health reform agenda [79, 81, 101]. Mexico’s political landscape shifted after 70 years of one-party rule, with the election of a new party opening the door for transformative health reforms [41, 68, 104]. However, these reforms were later dismantled with the rise of a subsequent government [75]. A government change in China led to a shift from heavily growth and market-oriented policies toward a more socialist focus with an administration prioritising social development and healthcare access [66]. Finally, in Korea, democratisation played a pivotal role in shaping health reforms, as the shift toward democratic governance provided opportunities to address health disparities and expand healthcare access [56].

Broader societal flow and expressions of power

The final theme examines how power is communicated, leveraged, and experienced throughout the policy process within health systems.

A central challenge in the literature is the ambiguity of universal health system policies’ definitions, which fuels ambiguities related to the assumptions, strategies, and intended outcomes. Giovanella et al. [89] argue that these varied terms often obscure the nuances of policy reforms, complicating efforts to achieve comprehensive healthcare reform. A critical distinction the authors argue that often gets blurred in the literature is between health insurance coverage, the enrolment of populations in insurance schemes, and effective health care coverage, ensuring meaningful access to quality care [89]. In Brazil, for example, Giovanella et al. [89] highlight that effective universal coverage requires extensive structural reforms, including improved payment and monitoring systems and stronger accountability measures. Moreover, it involves reorganising and managing healthcare systems to ensure that resources are allocated efficiently and equitably [89]. McNaughton [106, 107] argues that universal healthcare, often seen as an economic endeavour by policymakers, must centralise human rights to achieve its intended impact. This complexity is further illustrated by Frenk and Gomez-Dantes [68], who highlight that despite Mexico’s constitutional right to healthcare, the lack of a clear framework limits its actualisation.

The literature critiques these insurance-based models for universal health systems, where they have been documented as resulting in fragmented, shallow access to care. Atun et al., [67] highlight that employment-based social insurance schemes in Argentina, Chile, Mexico, and Uruguay have created parallel healthcare systems with differing levels of access and quality based on socioeconomic status, hindering the development of equitable universal health systems. Moreover, private sector practices like “cream skimming” worsen these inequities by selectively enrolling low-risk, high-income individuals, increasing costs and fragmenting services [67].

This fragmentation is also documented in Brazil by Machado [46], Harris and Libardi Maia [61] and Giovanella et al. [89] who argue that insurance-based models introduce complex administrative structures that hinder cohesive care delivery. This lack of cohesion creates a fragmented system that struggles to provide equitable access to healthcare [46, 61, 89].

Similarly Reich [75] discusses Mexico’s Seguro Popular program, which is documented as falling short of meeting total healthcare needs, leaving many with significant financial burdens and revealing a gap between nominal coverage and true universal access. China is cited as facing similar challenges, with Meng et al. [109] noting that payment incentives for insurance companies contribute to health system fragmentation, raising concerns about service quality. Hsiao et al. [44] and Mao et al. [70] observe that although universal insurance coverage has been ‘achieved’, disparities in the quality and accessibility of healthcare persist. Financial protection has improved affordability, yet the narrow scope of benefits and unequal distribution of services means many citizens still struggle to access high-quality care.

The issue of shallow coverage is also apparent in Costa Rica, where Sáenz [112] critiques the exclusionary nature of its universal system, which primarily benefits those in formal employment. Long waiting times for essential services such as diagnostics, specialised evaluations, and surgeries push many to seek private healthcare, deepening inequity. In contrast, Harris and Libardi Maia [61] notes that Thailand’s state health insurance programs have reinforced public sector dominance through sustained government investment. Strict underwriting requirements and high premiums for private insurance have limited private sector growth, ensuring the public sector remains the principal care provider.

Decentralisation in universal health systems

The literature frequently debates decentralisation, highlighting the tension between centralised control and decentralised autonomy in health reforms [75]. Reich [77] notes that balancing these approaches is challenging, as each approach has distinct benefits and drawbacks. Decentralisation can enhance local responsiveness and citizen participation, aligning services with local needs, as noted by Atun et al. [67]. However, it also introduces complexities in governance and performance management, with varying local capacities potentially widening inequalities between municipalities and risking politicisation in health decision-making [67].

In countries with decentralised structures, like Brazil, Rao et al. [74] document how autonomous subnational entities are tasked to implement central policies. While this structure enables local adoption, it frequently results in fragmented health service delivery, overlapping responsibilities and inefficiencies that hinder policy implementation. In the United States, decentralisation produces a mixed landscape of health policies that vary by state, allowing states like Massachusetts and New York to expand healthcare access [43, 94]. However, this creates unequal healthcare access nationwide, with outcomes depending on state leadership policies [92].

A similar situation is observed in South Africa, where Michel et al. [113] highlight the top-down nature of policy implementation. Although policies are nominally decentralised, limited authority at district and facility levels prevents local managers from implementing reforms effectively, creating confusion about health system goals and limited accountability.

In Mexico, decentralisation during Seguro Popular was cited by Nigenda et al. [105] as health reforms are being hampered due to weak information systems and inadequate institutional capacity, limiting data use for monitoring implementation. Furthermore, limited negotiation experience among state decision-makers affected labour contracts, with quality and productivity indicators often missing, making monitoring performance challenging. These issues ultimately led to the recentralisation of health services, reflecting a shift toward stronger federal oversight. Reich [75] notes while Seguro Popular allowed flexibility, its decentralised approach led to equity and efficiency issues due to resource and implementation variations at the state level.

China’s experience with decentralisation illustrates both potential and limitations. Yu [66] and Hasio et al. [44] document how delegating financial and political responsibilities to local governments incentivised outreach and enrolment initiatives, with performance targets holding local officials accountable for outcomes. This approach mobilised local engagement, but the variation in local capacities and resources presented governance challenges, in areas with weaker administrative capabilities [44, 66].

Crisis as triggers for policies for universal health systems

Whether economic or public health-related, crises have historically been powerful catalysts for health system reforms, including expanding or retracting universal health policies. Across multiple countries, crises have intensified political and public pressures for reform, highlighting systemic deficiencies and pushing governments to adopt and implement significant policy changes.

In China, various studies highlight the outbreak of SARS as a pivotal moment in health policy reform. The crisis heightened awareness of healthcare inadequacies and increased political pressure to expand public health coverage. The fear of future outbreaks and public dissatisfaction with the state of healthcare galvanised the government to prioritise health reforms, making access to healthcare a critical national issue [44, 66, 69, 109, 114].

More recently, the COVID-19 pandemic underscored disparities within health systems and prompted further reform efforts. In Ireland, for instance, the government used the crisis to build resilience and advance its reform agenda to strengthen the health system for future challenges [82, 83]. In contrast, the pandemic delayed reform in South Africa as resources were diverted to emergency responses [77].

The literature identifies similar patterns in Latin American countries like Mexico, Brazil, Chile, Colombia, and Costa Rica, and ASEAN countries like Thailand, Brunei, Malaysia, and Singapore regarding the pressures from chronic diseases and ageing populations. The anticipated economic cost of addressing these health issues and the elderly population’s growing needs have created an urgent demand for additional resources to expand healthcare coverage and address these challenges [41, 46, 67, 74, 102, 112].

The literature also shows that economic fluctuations frequently catalysed policy reforms for universal health systems. In the United Kingdom, the economic crisis of 1980 and 2008 and subsequent austerity measures triggered reforms in the National Health Service, accelerated trends towards outsourcing and privatisation [62, 86, 87]. Similarly, in Ireland, Spain, and Germany, neoliberal policies of the early 2000s expanded the private sector’s role in healthcare, contributing to healthcare ‘commodification’ [62, 80, 81, 87]. In Latin America, Asia, and Europe, the economic crisis exposed issues of low health coverage and poor public service quality, sparking debates on the state’s role in healthcare provision [16, 52, 55, 67, 79, 108]. In Turkey, Agartan [63] and Harris [104] describe how economic instability drove the government to reform its healthcare system to achieve broader coverage [63, 104]. Conversely, periods of economic growth, as documented in Mexico [105], Uruguay [108] and Ireland [79], mobilised additional public financial resources for social development, including healthcare. These contexts encouraged government leaders to pursue more ambitious healthcare access efforts.

The role of the media

The media plays a pivotal role in shaping the agenda-setting, adoption, and evaluation stages of health policy reforms’, particularly by highlighting critical issues, framing public discourse, and influencing public perceptions of universal health system reforms. This influence is evident across contexts where the media has both polarised and galvanised debates around health systems.

In the United States, the works of Gollust [65, 78] and McDonough [73], highlights how media coverage of health reforms, specifically the Affordable Care Act (ACA), framed it as a political contest rather than fostering policy discussions. Media emphasis on political strategies and partisan dynamics, often obscured the ACA’s content and benefits. This framing led the public to interpret the law through politically charged narratives, limiting a deeper understanding of its implications for healthcare access [65, 73, 78, 90]. Similarly, Fox and Choi [43] detailed how opponents of New York’s single payer-proposals used media to shape public opinion. The insurance industry and business interests led campaigns against single-payer systems, including creating the “Realities of Single Payer” website to highlight perceived flaws [43].

In China, it is well established in the literature that the media amplified public dissatisfaction with the healthcare system. Reports on health system failures, coupled with perceived abandonment of socialist healthcare, sparked debate. Online platforms enabled people to discuss and voice concerns, with analysts using it to critique health system shortcomings. The media-driven public dissatisfaction fuelled national discussions and increased pressure on the government to implement reforms shaping the policy agenda [44, 69, 76, 97].

In South Africa and Turkey, Bust et al. [77] and Argartan [98], respectively, note that media channels were used to express discontent with ongoing healthcare reforms with significant advocacy for private sector roles, reflecting broader debates on balancing public and private healthcare provisions.

Discussion

This scoping review examined the nature, volume, and scope of published research on the policy processes related to universal health systems, highlighting the influence of political, economic, and civic actors in high and upper-middle-income countries. The review aimed to illuminate the research on the relational power dynamics that drive, hinder, or modify reforms toward achieving equitable health systems by documenting the literature on how societal contexts and institutional factors shape these processes.

The analysis presented highlights critical gaps and trends in the universal health system policies literature. The underrepresentation of eligible countries in the reviewed literature underscores significant geographical disparities in research on universal health systems. With only 30% of eligible countries represented, the findings reveal that the evidence base is concentrated in a limited subset of high and upper-middle-income countries. The research concentration in a small subset of countries suggests potential biases shaped by research infrastructure, political visibility, or reform momentum in these regions. Such disparities underscore the need for more inclusive and geographically diverse research, particularly those less frequently studied but equally critical to understanding universal health system policy processes. It is also plausible that countries with well-established universal health systems generate less research overall or focus their scholarship on more specific aspects of system performance and sustainability. While an in-depth examination of this possibility lies beyond the scope of this study, it represents an important avenue for future research. In addition, the predominance of studies focusing on early policy stages, such as agenda-setting, formulation, and adoption, over later stages, like implementation and evaluation, highlights a significant gap in understanding how these policies are effectively operationalised and sustained over time [115, 116].

The predominance of qualitative studies underscores a focus on contextual and in-depth exploration. While this approach provides rich insights into social, political, and institutional dynamics, a greater balance between qualitative and quantitative studies could enhance the robustness and reflect missed opportunities for integrated analysis and evidence synthesis. The fluctuating publication trends also reveal inconsistent research attention, with peaks potentially linked to global health priorities, such as the Sustainable Development Goals or the COVID-19 pandemic. The recent trend in publications suggests renewed interest. Yet, the gaps in consistent research output indicate the need for a sustained effort to build the evidence base for universal health systems.

This review highlights several stakeholders whose roles in universal health system policy processes warrant deeper investigation. Industry interest groups, often influential in shaping health policy, remain underexplored, particularly regarding their impact on equity and accessibility. Similarly, the influence of transnational organisations such as the EU, BRICS, and ASEAN in shaping health system reforms across regions is insufficiently documented. The roles of NGOs and aid funders also require greater scrutiny to understand how their priorities align or conflict with national policy goals. Lastly, the role of consultancy companies in advising and influencing policy frameworks merits further research to uncover their broader implications.

Policies for universal health systems aim to uphold health as a human right by promoting equitable access to care [3]. However, this review highlights the profound influence of political, economic, and ideological forces in shaping the policy process, fundamentally affecting how universal health systems are conceptualised and implemented. Notably, these influences often fail to prioritise the principle of health as a human right at the core of their approach. While universal health coverage currently serves as the dominant unified framework, it remains unclear whether its focus on financialisation produces the broader outcomes demanded by universal health policies, such as equity, quality, and universal access [7, 9, 26, 115, 117]. Significant variation exists in the interpretation of ‘universal’ [15, 26], and the realisation of equity in policies for universal health systems depends on clear frameworks guiding how policies are to be defined, structured, sequenced and implemented [11, 118]. The lack of clarity in definitions and broader operational frameworks may contribute to fragmented policy design and implementation, undermining their effectiveness and equity.

While the literature emphasises the value of flexibility in allowing countries to develop context-specific solutions [1], this same flexibility can inadvertently foster the absence of a unified framework and standardised evaluation mechanisms. Such gaps leave policies vulnerable to being co-opted by vested interests [119]. This focus on coverage allows policymakers to selectively reframe objectives to align with local priorities or ideological agendas, leading to fragmented approaches that fail to deliver the quality, equity and inclusivity demanded by universal health system principles [15]. As Giedion et al. [115] emphasise, while universal health coverage schemes aim to improve access, a more holistic approach to addressing the multiple dimensions of access is crucial. Although affordability is critical, it alone cannot guarantee equitable and full access to health services. Without an explicit and intersectional commitment to reducing inequalities, merely expanding coverage risks perpetuating existing health disparities [120]. Although evaluating the success of these policies in specific country contexts falls outside the scope of this review, the findings highlight the urgent need for unified, holistic universal health systems frameworks to help mitigate these challenges.

Ideology and ideas serve as powerful unifying forces, enabling disparate actors from professional, economic, civic, academic, and labour groupings to rally around shared goals. Policy entrepreneurs utilise these shared ideas to build coalitions that leverage collective power to influence policy [121]. Despite differing institutional roles and perspectives, ideological alignment or political priorities frequently bridge divides, uniting actors in the policy process. As highlighted in the review findings, the ideological orientation of influential actors frequently shapes the scope, design, and sustainability of universal health system policies [41, 54, 63, 75]. Civic society, activists, professional associations, labour unions and academics are instrumental in elevating healthcare issues onto the public agenda and pressuring governments to address health inequities [122–125]. However, these groups do not uniformly support all policies labelled ‘universal health’ reforms or view solutions through the same lens [126–128], reflecting splintered perspectives on the state’s role in healthcare delivery.

Government institutions play a crucial role in shaping which actors gain political power, how this power is organised, and how actors mobilise within the policy process. Political regimes determine how power is exercised and how decisions are made regarding policies for universal health systems and the provision of public goods and services [129]. While these factors can facilitate reform, they can also undermine reform and the expansion of universal health systems. In some cases, actors strategically use their institutional positions to reinforce their policy goals, while political priorities or partisan objectives may align these efforts to drive or block reforms. However, this concentration of power, particularly in centralised systems, can limit broader participation and create opportunities for the selective advancement of reforms that align with specific ideological agendas, often undermining the expansion of equitable universal health coverage [30]. Shifts in political leadership frequently lead to re-evaluating or reforming existing policy frameworks, reflecting the dynamic interplay of power and ideology within universal health systems [75].

This review builds on McKee et al.’s [10] analysis, which found that left-wing parties and labour unions are integral to advancing universal health system reforms. Although true, this research reveals that politically ‘conservative’ governments, such as those in Turkey and Mexico, have pursued universal health system policies, often co-opted to align with market-oriented priorities. These tailored approaches reflect broader centre and right-leaning political and economic objectives, illustrating the challenges of achieving equitable, consistent health systems in politically dynamic environments.

Economic downturns, public health emergencies, and social unrest often expose vulnerabilities in healthcare systems, acting as catalysts for reform [82]. Despite their potential to catalyse immediate reforms, these forces alone do not guarantee sustained policy change [130]. Without structural reforms and strong institutional support, the influence of civic society, media, and crisis-driven reforms may be limited to short-term adjustments aimed at quick political gains rather than the foundational development of a universal health system [75].

Strengths and limitations

This scoping review contributes a timely and relevant synthesis, providing a foundation for policymakers and researchers seeking to understand and advance universal health system reforms. By providing a comprehensive overview of the literature on universal health system policy processes, this review identifies themes across political, economic, and social dimensions, offering nuanced insights into the complexities of health policy reform. Using an established framework enhances the analytical rigour and offers a structured approach to examining the roles of various actors in shaping health policy. Additionally, by mapping the existing research, this review identifies critical gaps in the literature, particularly in understanding actor dynamics, power structures, and policy contexts, guiding future research directions in universal health systems.

Limitations to this review process include the possibility that some relevant articles were missed. The focus on high-income and upper-middle-income countries may limit the applicability of findings to lower-income contexts, which often face distinct challenges. Only English-language studies were included, potentially excluding relevant findings from non-English publications. As this is a scoping review, a formal quality appraisal of the studies included was not deemed appropriate. However, there is a risk that lower-quality studies may have influenced the findings, potentially leading to skewed portrayals of countries’ health policy achievements or challenges. By drawing themes from the literature without assessing study rigour, the review may inadvertently reflect biases or oversights in individual studies, affecting how accurately countries’ health policy contexts and reforms are represented. Although themes were initially identified inductively from the data, they were subsequently organised through a deductive mapping process using Topp et al.’s [21] framework. This approach may have influenced the final structure of the findings and could have constrained the visibility of themes that did not align fully with the framework’s categories. Nonetheless, the themes that emerged from the analysis demonstrated strong alignment with the Topp et al. framework. Any substantive themes that did not align with this framework would have been explicitly reported.

Conclusions

Policies for universal health systems present a transformative opportunity to advance equitable access to healthcare globally, yet realising this vision demands far more than technocratic fixes. This review reveals the political, economic, social and ideological forces that shape and often constrain the policy process, underscoring that universal health coverage, while the dominant framework, remains insufficient. Its emphasis on financial and insurance mechanisms fails to fully address the systemic challenges of quality, accessibility, and equity principles fundamental to health as a human right. A more expansive, empirically grounded framework is needed to integrate these dimensions while navigating the complexities of resource allocation, institutional capacity, and power asymmetries inherent in the political economy of health systems.

The findings highlight that achieving universal health systems requires adaptive, resilient institutions, visionary leadership, and an engaged civil society to counterbalance institutional weaknesses and mitigate fragmentation. Civic society, activists, and labour unions are critical in holding governments accountable and pressing for reforms, yet their impact is mediated by the political motivations of those in power. Achieving genuine universality demands a shift away from reactive, crisis-driven policy responses toward proactive, strategic planning that builds accountability, strengthens institutional foundations, and ensures policies withstand the pressures of political co-optation and vested interests.
