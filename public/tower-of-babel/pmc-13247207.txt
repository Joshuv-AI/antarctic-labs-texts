Differential effect of antiseizure medications eslicarbazepine and carbamazepine on hippocampal synaptic transmission and plasticity
Authors: Zubin Singh Rana, Abhinav Modgil, Pradeep Punnakkal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAnti-seizure medication (ASM) is the most widely used treatment for patients with epilepsy (PWE). Despite the availability of multiple ASMs, about 30% of the patients become drug-resistant. Moreover, there are several cases of seizure aggravation and cognitive side effects associated with ASMs. Therefore, understanding how ASMs work is essential for improving epilepsy treatment. In this study, we investigated the effect of first-generation drug carbamazepine (CBZ) and third-generation drug eslicarbazepine acetate (ESL) on neuronal excitability, synaptic transmission and synaptic plasticity in the hippocampal neurons using patch clamp and field recording experiments. We found that both ESL and CBZ reduced the neuronal excitability differently. ESL reduced the action potential amplitude but did not affect the firing frequency, whereas CBZ application not only reduced the amplitude but also led to a significant reduction in the firing frequency. ESL reduced the amplitude of both spontaneous EPSCs and IPSCs, but CBZ did not alter spontaneous synaptic activity. Further, ESL reduced the magnitude of LTP in Schaffer collateral CA1 synapses but CBZ did not alter LTP. LTD was also studied to understand the effect of the drugs on bidirectional plasticity. We found that both ESL and CBZ reduced LTD, but the impairment caused by ESL was stronger than that caused by CBZ. This study underlines the complexity of the mechanism of action of ASM and also highlights the need for further research to develop better targeted drugs for epilepsy treatment.Keywords: Antiseizure medication, Carbamazepine, Eslicarbazepine, Action potential, Spontaneous currents, Synaptic plasticitySubject terms: Neurology, Neuroscience

Introduction

There have been multiple anti-seizure medications (ASMs) developed and approved for the treatment of epilepsy. The first drug used for the treatment of epilepsy was bromide, introduced in the 1850s1. Since then, there have been over 28 ASMs available2. There are different targets for ASMs, but all work by ultimately reducing the overall excitability of neurons in the brain, hence reducing the seizure initiation and propagation. Some class of drugs work better in partial seizures, and others work better in generalized seizures3. Hence, the origin of seizure is an important factor for determining the type of medication to be used in the treatment. It has been reported that sometimes ASMs aggravate the seizures and cause many side effects. The reason behind such adverse events is not known. This can be attributed to the incomplete understanding of the mechanism of action of the drugs, which are primarily evaluated for their efficacy on seizure suppression and limited efforts are directed towards elucidating their complete mechanism of action. Despite the availability of a wide range of drugs, patient satisfaction with treatment remains suboptimal. In addition, ASMs are linked to a wide range of unwanted side effects, among which neuropsychological side effects are of major concern4–7. Patients undergoing ASMs treatment, when observed under behavioral assessment showed worse scoring in memory and cognitive tasks than when they were not on medications8. While ASMs effectively suppress seizures, they often contribute to memory and cognitive deficits, raising concerns about their overall benefits, especially in the case of young patients.

In order to better understand the mechanism of the drug action, we have studied the effect of eslicarbazepine acetate (ESL) and carbamazepine (CBZ) on excitatory and inhibitory currents and synaptic plasticity in rat brain hippocampal synapses. CBZ is considered as a gold standard drug for the treatment of partial epilepsy9 which acts via inhibiting voltage gated sodium channels (VGSC) and L-type calcium channels10,11. As an older medication, side effects of CBZ are thoroughly documented in the literature12,13. Eslicarbazepine is a third-generation anti-seizure medication belonging to the same dibenzazepine family and is considered to be a better drug. As a new drug, there are only a few reports regarding the molecular mechanism of action at cellular level. We have conducted a comparative study between ESL and CBZ to understand how these drugs affect the synaptic transmission as well as synaptic plasticity in the hippocampus as an attempt to understand the side effects like learning and memory deficits associated with them.

Results

Effect of ESL and CBZ on action potential (AP) properties of CA1 pyramidal neurons

The effect of varying concentrations of ESL and CBZ on action potential properties were assessed using current clamp recordings from visually identified CA1 pyramidal neurons. Action potentials were evoked with 500 ms long series of hyperpolarizing to depolarizing currents (–80 pA to + 250 pA). Action potentials were first evoked in normal ACSF, after which ESL and CBZ were applied at varying concentrations (50 µM, 100 µM and 200 µM for ESL and 10 µM, 50 µM and 100 µM for CBZ) via bath application to evaluate their effect (Tables 1 and 2). Application of CBZ and ESL (in separate sets of experiments) produced a significant, concentration dependent decrease in action potential amplitude, with more significant reduction in amplitude with increasing concentrations (Fig. 1). There was no significant change in firing frequency in the case of ESL (Table 1) whereas, application of CBZ led to a significant reduction in firing frequency (Table 2). These results explained the differential effect of ESL and CBZ on different inactivation states of voltage gated sodium channel (VGSC) and how it ultimately alters the overall neuronal excitability14.

ESL but not CBZ reduced the spontaneous excitatory and inhibitory synaptic currents in CA1 pyramidal neurons.

To study how these drugs affect spontaneous neuronal activity, we studied two major receptors (AMPA and GABA A receptors) critically responsible for maintaining the excitatory to inhibitory balance for the proper functioning of the brain. We used clinically relevant concentrations (100 µM ESL and 10 µM CBZ) to better correlate the results with observed side effects. CA1 pyramidal neurons were voltage clamped at –80 mV for AMPA receptor currents (sEPSCs) and GABA A receptor currents (sIPSCs) were studied at 0 mV. After recording a stable baseline of sEPSCs, 100 µM ESL was perfused for 5 min. It was observed that, perfusion of 100 µM ESL reduced the sEPSCs amplitude (13.9 ± 0.92 pA vs 18.6 ± 1.75 pA, n = 17, N = 12, t = 3.259, P = 0.0049, paired T-test) (Fig. 2A and B) and increased the inter-event interval significantly (1105.97 ± 96.72 ms vs 825.56 ± 82.03 ms, n = 17, N = 12, t = 2.680, P = 0.0164, paired T-test) (Fig. 2C and D). Interestingly, 100 µM ESL also reduced the amplitude of sIPSCs (15.14 ± 0.86 pA vs 17.76 ± 1.12 pA, n = 23, N = 15, t = 2.975, P = 0.007, paired T-test) (Fig. 2E and F) and increased the inter event interval significantly (707.15 ± 78.87 ms vs 525.82 ± 66.64 ms, n = 23, N = 15, t = 2.990, P = 0.0068, paired T-test) (Fig. 2G and H). On the other hand, when 10 µM CBZ was perfused to study the effect on sEPSCs and sIPSCs, it was found that 10 µM CBZ had no effect on sEPSCs amplitude (16.09 ± 1.21 pA vs 16.45 ± 1.11 pA, n = 10, N = 5, t = 0.4467, P = 0.6657, paired T-test) (Fig. 3A and B) and inter event interval (1023.91 ± 211.46 ms vs 895.52 ± 207.59 ms, n = 11, N = 5, t = 0.742, P = 0.4770, paired T-test) (Fig. 3C and D). Similarly, CBZ didn’t have any effect on sIPSCs amplitude (15.83 ± 1.25 pA vs 16.86 ± 1.22 pA, n = 13, N = 10, t = 1.250, P = 0.2352, paired T-test) (Fig. 3E and F) as well as the inter-event interval (476.08 ± 70.88 ms vs 583.09 ± 96.04 ms, n = 13, N = 10, t = 1.725, P = 0.1102, paired T-test) (Fig. 3G and H) of GABA A currents. These results highlight the differential effect of ESL and CBZ on synaptic transmission with ESL having a broad modulatory effect on excitatory and inhibitory synaptic transmission.

ESL impairs long term potentiation in the Schaffer collateral hippocampal synapses

Long term potentiation (LTP) and long term depression (LTD) are the crucial processes in synaptic plasticity, the ability of the synapse to change depending upon the kind of stimulus it receives. LTP and LTD are recognised as a mechanism behind learning and memory. ASMs are known to modulate synaptic plasticity15,16. Since both the drugs modulate excitatory transmission differently, investigating the effects of ESL and CBZ on synaptic plasticity provides insight into the potential cognitive side effects. We investigated the LTP at Schaffer collateral synapses. Stimulation of the Schaffer collateral pathway was performed at 0.03 Hz using an ACSF-filled glass pipette, while field responses were recorded from the stratum radiatum with a second pipette containing ACSF. For LTP experiments, stimulation intensity was adjusted in a way that the response generated was 50% of the maximum response to allow potentiation. A stable baseline was recorded for 5 min before applying an LTP induction high frequency stimulation (HFS) protocol (100 pulses at 100 Hz, delivered in three trains with 10-s intervals). In control slices, LTP induction protocol resulted in a stable and significant potentiation of synaptic transmission (1.51 ± 0.04, n = 6, N = 5, t = 18.19, P < 0.0001, paired T-test). To study the effect of the drug on LTP, the drug was, perfused and any change in synaptic transmission was allowed to stabilize and then baseline was recorded for 5 min before LTP protocol was given. We found that, perfusion of 100 µM ESL significantly impaired LTP compared to control slices (1.08 ± 0.06, n = 6, N = 6 vs 1.51 ± 0.04, n = 6, N = 5, t = 6.639, P < 0.0001, unpaired T-test) (Fig. 4A and B). In contrast, when 10 µM CBZ was perfused, LTP remained comparable to control conditions, with no significant difference in synaptic potentiation (1.60 ± 0.10, n = 5, N = 5 vs 1.51 ± 0.04, n = 6, N = 5, t = 1.006, P = 0.3407, unpaired T-test, Control data same as Fig. 4A) (Fig. 4C and 4D). These findings indicate differential modulation of LTP by ESL and CBZ.

ESL causes a stronger and more significant impairment of LTD compared to CBZ

LTD is another form of synaptic plasticity observed in many brain areas. Both LTP and LTD are observed in a functional synapse. Under normal physiological condition, synapses can undergo both strengthening and weakening depending on the kind of stimulus they receive. It has been observed earlier, that epileptiform activity impairs LTD in hippocampal synapses17. Impairment of this bidirectional plasticity is observed under disease conditions17,18. This impairment is associated with the inability of synapses to undergo bidirectional plasticity under pathological conditions and is described as epilepsy induced metaplasticity which renders the synapse unable to induce LTD which may be associated with memory impairment in epilepsy17. For studying LTD in Schaffer collateral synapses, a glass pipette filled with ACSF was used to stimulate the Schaffer collateral pathway at a frequency of 0.03 Hz and the response was recorded by placing the glass pipette filled with ACSF in the stratum radiatum. After recording a control baseline at the maximum response for 5 min, low frequency stimulation (LFS) (900 pulses of 1 Hz) was given to induce LTD in Schaffer collateral synapses. After LFS protocol, we observed a significant reduction in the amplitude of fEPSPs under control conditions (0.81 ± 0.03, n = 6, N = 6, t = 5.715, P = 0.0023, paired T-test). To study the effect of drug on LTD, baseline was recorded in presence of the drug before LTD protocol was given. We found that perfusion of ESL resulted in complete impairment of LTD (0.99 ± 0.05, n = 5, N = 4 vs 0.81 ± 0.03, n = 6, N = 6, t = 2.969, P = 0.0157, unpaired T-test) (Fig. 5A and B). Whereas, perfusion of CBZ led to a significant reduction in LTD and was only able to induce 8% LTD in comparison to 19% LTD observed in control slices (0.92 ± 0.01, n = 5, N = 4 vs 0.81 ± 0.03, n = 6, N = 6, t = 2.895, P = 0.0177, unpaired T-test, Control data same as Fig. 5A) (Fig. 5C and D). According to these results, ESL has a very significant impact on both forms of synaptic plasticity, whereas CBZ did not alter LTP but reduced the magnitude of LTD.

Discussion

In this study, we tried to elucidate the mechanism of action of the drugs by investigating its effect on neuronal excitability, basal synaptic transmission and synaptic plasticity. This study provides novel insights into the differential effects of ESL and CBZ on various electrophysiological parameters of neurons, highlighting the complexity of mechanism of action of ASM, even though they belong to the same chemical family. First, we studied the effects of the drugs on neuronal excitability at different concentrations (50 µM, 100 µM, 200 µM for ESL and 10 µM, 50 µM, 100 µM for CBZ). We found that both ESL and CBZ altered the excitability of neurons, but in slightly different manner. ESL and CBZ both reduced action potential amplitude in a concentration dependent manner. However, CBZ reduced the frequency of action potential firing, but ESL had no significant effect on firing frequency. These results align with the previous studies showing that CBZ stabilizes the fast inactivated state of voltage-gated sodium (Na +) channels19. In patch clamp studies, CBZ shifted the voltage dependence of fast inactivation to more negative potentials (~ –7 mV), hence requiring longer duration for channel to recover. On the other hand, ESL does not alter the kinetics or voltage dependence of fast inactivation at comparable concentrations14. Instead, ESL preferentially enhances the slow inactivation of sodium channels20. ESL produced a pronounced leftward shift in the voltage dependence of slow inactivation (~ -30 mV), indicating that a much larger fraction of channels enter a long-duration inactivated state at relatively hyperpolarizing membrane potentials14. It is known that ESL has sixfold higher affinity towards the slow inactivation state than in the resting state, whereas CBZ only has a 1.7-fold higher affinity than the resting state14. This mechanism of action of ESL is comparable with lacosamide (LCM), which is known for selectively enhancing slow inactivation21. Whereas CBZ was able to significantly reduce the firing frequency due to its interaction with the fast inactivation state. Next, we wanted to study how the presence of drug in the CNS affects the basal synaptic transmission, since medication is generally given to the patient on a daily basis to prevent severe refractory seizure episodes, strict adherence to the medication regimen is crucial. Previous studies have demonstrated that noncompliance can significantly increase the risk of sudden unexpected death in epilepsy (SUDEP)22. However, that also implies that there are constant levels of ASM present in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) even when there is no hypersynchronous activity. So, we hypothesized that exposure of ASM to the brain during physiological neuronal activity may affect the basal synaptic transmission in the brain. We studied sEPSCs and sIPSCs and found that CBZ does not alter the basal synaptic transmission of CA1 pyramidal neurons. Whereas, ESL had strong effect on both sEPSCs and sIPSCs, reducing the amplitude and increasing the inter-event interval of both sEPSCs and sIPSCs. This suppression of synaptic activity by ESL may result from its interaction with voltage-gated sodium and calcium channels20. The ability to dampen synaptic activity may contribute to seizure suppression, as it has been shown that drugs that block glutamatergic synaptic transmission show anticonvulsant properties with neuroprotective effect in animal models23–25. Similarly blocking AMPA receptors by NBQX (15–30 mg/kg) before stimulation significantly supressed escalation of seizure and slowed acquisition of amygdala kindling26. However, any change in excitatory to inhibitory balance can also disrupt normal network function and ASMs are not designed to modulate the basal synaptic transmission. These results explain the distinct mechanism of action of two ASMs which belong to the same dibenzazepine family and have a relatively similar structure. Next, we questioned how these drugs would affect synaptic plasticity in Schaffer collateral CA1 pyramidal neuron synapses, a fundamental mechanism underlying learning and memory. We studied LTP and LTD, which are well-established models of synaptic plasticity and, in most cases, mirrors learning and memory in animal behaviour models27. In control hippocampal slices, the HFS protocol induced significant potentiation of synapses. Perfusion of ESL led to a significant impairment of LTP where CBZ had no effect on synaptic plasticity. In a previous study, perfusion of CBZ (50 µM) reduced the magnitude of LTP in Schaffer collateral synapses in mice28. D’Antuono et al. 2010, found that suprathreshold concentration (5–30 fold higher) of CBZ (50–200 µM) reduced the ictal discharges in 4-AP induced in vitro epilepsy model 29. In our study we have used the clinically relevant concentration (10 µM) matching the CSF levels of patients to better understand the mechanism of drug at physiological level30. Moreover, it has been documented in literature that CBZ at 10 µM concentration produced a significant reduction in ictal like after discharges induced by high frequency stimulation in 4-AP induced epilepsy model31 suggesting clinically relevant concentration produces a significant reduction in epileptiform activity in in vitro models. We also found that CBZ (10 µM) reduced the frequency and amplitude of epileptiform events in high potassium (7.5 mM) and zero magnesium epilepsy model in CA1 region of hippocampus (data not shown). The difference observed in these studies may be due to the animal models, area of interest and the concentrations used in the studies. It has been observed in an another study that CBZ rescues the memory impairment in rodent epilepsy models32,33 which aligns with our results. Impairment of LTP could also be associated with antiepileptogenic effect. Experimental studies have shown that blocking hippocampal LTP slows or supress the development of seizures34. We also studied LTD to understand the effect of ASM on both forms of synaptic plasticity. We found that ESL caused a stronger and more significant impairment of LTD, when compared with CBZ. In our previous study, we have found that LTD is impaired in in vitro epilepsy model, which is due to the epilepsy induced metaplasticity in the synapses17. LTD contributes equally to memory formation as LTP. Studies have shown that LTD has a specific role in refining memory traces and enhancing pattern separation35. LTD helps in storing the details to discriminate between similar experiences. It has also been documented that other neurological disorders like Alzheimer’s showed impaired synaptic plasticity36. Other studies have also shown the importance of bidirectional plasticity and how does the hyperexcitable state of epilepsy enhances synaptic efficacy preventing the establishment of long term depression in the synapses17,37. Since ESL altered sEPSCs and sIPSCs, which are responsible for basal synaptic transmission as well as synaptic plasticity, this impairment in synaptic plasticity could be the cumulative effect of the drug on these receptors. Our study brings new findings about the mechanism of action of ESL and CBZ in hippocampal synapses regarding synaptic transmission and plasticity.

Methods

Drugs

Eslicarbazepine acetate was purchased from ChemScene (USA) and TCI chemicals (India), and all other chemicals were purchased from Merck (Germany).

Animals

Wistar rats, aged (P14-P28) (Male/Female), were acquired from the PGIMER animal house facility. All procedures followed the guidelines approved by PGIMER, Chandigarh, India (121/IAEC/857) dated 30/07/22. The ARRIVE guidelines were followed throughout the experimental procedures. All experiments were conducted under standardized environmental conditions, with unrestricted access to food and water. All efforts were made to use the minimum number of animals and reduce their discomfort.

Hippocampal slice preparation

Slices were prepared as previously described38. Briefly, Wistar rat (P14-P28) was anesthetized using isoflurane followed by decapitation. The whole brain was dissected and transferred to chilled artificial cerebrospinal fluid (ACSF) composed of (in mM): 125 NaCl, 25 NaHCO3, 2.5 KCl, 1.21 NaH2PO4.2H2O, 10 D-glucose, 1 MgSO4, and 2 CaCl2 (pH 7.4, adjusted with HCl, 300–310 mOsmol l-1) continuously aerated with carbogen gas (95% O2 and 5% CO2). Transverse brain slices, 250–500 µm thick, were prepared using a vibratome (Tedpella, USA). Patch clamp experiments were performed using 250 µm thick slices, whereas 500 µm thick slices were used for field recordings. Subsequently, the slices were incubated for 30 min at 32°C in ACSF bubbled with carbogen and then stored at room temperature for further experimentation.

Action potential properties of CA1 pyramidal neurons

The action potential properties of CA1 pyramidal neurons were investigated using whole cell current clamp mode. Brain slices were transferred to a submerged recording chamber with a continuous flow of carbogen-saturated artificial cerebrospinal fluid (ACSF). Patch pipettes (glass capillaries, AM Systems, USA) were fabricated using a PC-10 vertical puller (Narishige, Japan). Patch pipettes, with resistances ranging between 3 and 5 MΩ, were back-filled with a potassium-based internal solution containing (in mM): 130 K-gluconate, 5 NaCl, 1 EGTA, 5 Mg-ATP, 0.5 Na-GTP, and 10 HEPES (pH adjusted to 7.4 with KOH, 280–300 mOsmol l-1). Pyramidal neurons in the CA1 region of the hippocampus were visually identified based on their characteristic pyramidal-shaped soma using differential interference contrast (DIC) microscopy (Olympus, Japan). Once the cell was in whole cell mode, Current-clamp experiments were conducted using a HEKA EPC10 amplifier with Patchmaster software (HEKA, Germany). Action potentials (APs) were evoked by applying a series of 500 ms long current step from -80 pA to + 250 pA with a 10 mV increment in each step. To investigate the firing frequency, the current injection with the maximum number of AP in the control condition (normal ACSF) was compared with the equivalent current injection in the presence of the drug. To determine the single action potential properties, the step current, which generated 1–2 AP was taken. The AP threshold was defined as the membrane voltage at which the action potential overshoots. The amplitude of AP was measured as the voltage from the threshold level to its maximum peak. AP duration was defined as the duration of AP at the level of the threshold. Rheobase was calculated as the minimum current injected to generate AP. Rise time was the time taken by the AP to reach its maximum peak from the threshold.

Spontaneous inhibitory and excitatory currents in CA1 neurons

Spontaneous excitatory and inhibitory postsynaptic currents (sEPSCs and sIPSCs) were recorded from CA1 pyramidal neurons using the voltage clamp technique. Spontaneous events were recorded using patch pipette filled with Caesium-based internal solution containing (in mM): (130 CsMeSO4, 5 CsCl, 5 EGTA, 10 HEPES, 1 Mg-ATP, 0.1 Na-GTP, 5 QX-314 (pH 7.4, adjusted with CsOH, 280–300 mosmol l-1). AMPA receptor currents were studied at -80 mV, and GABA A receptor currents were studied at a holding potential of 0 mV. The receptor currents were confirmed using the respective antagonists. AMPA receptor currents were confirmed using 20 µM DNQX, which led to blocking the currents at -80 mV. Similarly, at 0 mV, GABA A receptor currents were confirmed using 50 µM picrotoxin, which removed all currents at 0 mV. Series resistance was monitored continuously, and if there was a change in series resistance above 20%, the cells were not used for analysis. Recordings were done for at least 5 min, and minimum 2-min recordings were taken for analysis39.

Synaptic plasticity experiments

After incubating the slices at 32°C for 30 min, they were placed in recording chamber with a constant flow of carbogenated ACSF. The flow rate was maintained throughout the experiment (1.2–1.5 ml/min). Schaffer collateral pathway was stimulated by using a glass electrode filled with ACSF, while recordings were obtained from the stratum radiatum of CA1 region with ACSF filled electrode to study long term potentiation (LTP) and long term depression (LTD). To induce LTP, a high frequency (HFS) protocol was applied, comprising of 3 trains of 100 stimuli at 100 Hz, with 10 s interval. Similarly, to induce LTD in the hippocampal CA1 synapses a low frequency stimulation (LFS) protocol (900 pulses of 1Hz) was used. LTP and LTD magnitude was assessed as the average normalized peak amplitude of the last 5-min recording after applying the respective protocol.

Data analysis

Patch clamp recordings were conducted using a HEKA EPC 10 double patch amplifier and Patchmaster software (HEKA, Germany). Signals were low-pass filtered at 2.9 kHz and digitized at a sampling rate of 10 kHz. The sEPSCs and sIPSCs peaks were analysed using MiniAnalysis (Synaptosoft, USA). Action potential properties were studied using Fitmaster (HEKA, Germany). Field potential recordings were performed using a Model 1800 microelectrode amplifier (AM Systems), and stimulation was delivered via a Model 2100 isolated pulse stimulator (AM Systems). Signals were band-pass filtered between 1 Hz and 5 kHz and acquired using an N1 PCI 6221 data acquisition card. Data recording and analysis were performed using WinWCP (Strathclyde Electrophysiology Software). Data from male and female animals were combined, as no sex-specific differences were detected and are presented as mean ± SEM. Normality of the data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Based on the distribution, appropriate tests were applied. Statistical comparisons between two groups were performed using paired and unpaired Student’s t-test. For multiple group comparisons, repeated measures one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s post-hoc test was used for parametric data while the Friedman test with Dunn’s post-hoc correction was applied for non-parametric data. Kolmogorov–Smirnov (KS) test was used to analyse the statistical difference between the cumulative frequency of sEPSCs and sIPSCs amplitude and inter event interval. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001. n denotes the number of slices or cells, while N indicates the number of animals used in the experiments.
