Matroids from gain graphs over quotient groups
Zach Walsh

Abstract
We present a new construction for matroids from gain graphs that simultaneously generalizes several existing constructions. The construction takes as input a gain graph over a Frobenius group Γ \Gamma with Frobenius kernel Γ 1 \Gamma_{1} and outputs an elementary lift of the frame matroid of the underlying gain graph over the quotient group Γ / Γ 1 \Gamma/\Gamma_{1} . While the hypothesis that Γ \Gamma is a Frobenius group may seem unusual, we prove that it is in some sense necessary: if Γ \Gamma is any finite group with a nontrivial proper normal subgroup Γ 1 \Gamma_{1} and there is a construction that takes in a complete Γ \Gamma -gain graph and outputs an elementary lift M M of the frame matroid of the underlying ( Γ / Γ 1 ) (\Gamma/\Gamma_{1}) -gain graph so that a cycle of the graph is a circuit of M M if and only if it is Γ \Gamma -balanced, then Γ \Gamma is a Frobenius group with Frobenius kernel Γ 1 \Gamma_{1} .

Frame matroids and lift matroids have proven to be important in the study of hyperplane arrangements

[

[

]

Our main result is a construction that has both of Zaslavsky’s constructions, and also recent constructions of Anderson, Su, and Zaslavsky
[

]

[

We will work with gain graphs over a group

As we shall see in Section

However, our main result, which we will now state, also applies to infinite groups such as the special Euclidean group of direct isometries of

Theorem 1.1

If

•

•

If

Theorem

•

For any Frobenius group

The special case

Given a

.

2
Preliminaries

In this section we will review some background on Frobenius groups, and gain graphs and their associated matroids.

2.1
Group theory

We will first review the relevant group theory background.

2.1.1
Semidirect products

Many of the groups to which we will apply Theorem
1.1
will be defined using an operation called semidirect product that generalizes group direct products.
Let
\Gamma
be a group with identity
1
.
If
\Gamma
has subgroups
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
so that
\Gamma_{1}\cap\Gamma_{2}=\{1\}
,
\Gamma_{1}
is normal, and
\Gamma=\Gamma_{1}\Gamma_{2}
, then
\Gamma
is an
inner semidirect product
of
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
, and we write
\Gamma=\Gamma_{1}\rtimes\Gamma_{2}
.

Example 2.1
.

Let
D_{2n}
be the dihedral group of order
2n
with
n
odd, let
R
be the subgroup of rotations, and let
S
be a subgroup consisting of the identity and one reflection
s
.
Since
R
is a normal subgroup,
R
and
S
intersect only in the identity, and every reflection in
D_{2n}
is of the form
rs
for some
r\in R
, we have
D_{2n}=R\rtimes S
.

Example 2.2
.

Let
E(n)
be the
Euclidean group
of isometries (distance-preserving transformations) of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Let
T(n)
be the subgroup of translations and let
O(n)
be the
orthogonal group
, which is the subgroup of isometries that fix the origin.
Clearly these two subgroups share only the identity transformation, and
it is straightforward to show that
T(n)
is a normal subgroup of
E(n)
.
If
f\in E(n)
maps the origin to a vector
b
, then the map
x\mapsto f(x)-b
is in
O(n)
.
Therefore every map in
E(n)
can be uniquely written as the composition of a translation and an element of
O(n)
, and so
E(n)=T(n)\rtimes O(n)
.

Similarly, the
special Euclidean group

SE(n)
, which is the subgroup of
E(n)
consisting of isometries that also preserve orientation (called
direct isometries
), is a semidirect product
T(n)\rtimes SO(n)
where
SO(n)
is the
special orthogonal group
of direct isometries that fix the origin.

With a slightly different perspective, one can construct groups that decompose as an inner semidirect product.
If
\Gamma=\Gamma_{1}\rtimes\Gamma_{2}
, then there is a homomorphism
\phi
from
\Gamma_{2}
to
\Aut(\Gamma_{1})
(the group of automorphisms of
\Gamma_{1}
) defined by conjugation: for each
b\in\Gamma_{2}
the function
\phi_{b}
that maps
a\in\Gamma_{1}
to
b^{-1}ab
is an automorphism of
\Gamma_{1}
.
One can use this observation to combine two groups to form a new group that decomposes as an inner semidirect product.
Let
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
be groups (with
\Gamma_{1}
additive and
\Gamma_{2}
multiplicative) and let
\phi
be a homomorphism from
\Gamma_{2}
to
\Aut(\Gamma_{1})
, writing
\phi_{b}
for
\phi(b)
.
Then the group
\Gamma
on the cartesian product of the sets underlying
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
with group operation
\circ
defined by
(a,b)\circ(c,d)=(a+\phi_{b}(c),bd)
is an
outer semidirect product
of
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
, and we write
\Gamma=\Gamma_{1}\rtimes_{\phi}\Gamma_{2}
.
We may omit the subscript
\phi
when
\phi
is implicit.
It is straightforward to show that
\{(a,1)\colon a\in\Gamma_{1}\}
is a normal subgroup of
\Gamma
isomorphic to
\Gamma_{1}
and
\{(0,b)\colon b\in\Gamma_{2}\}
is a subgroup of
\Gamma
isomorphic to
\Gamma_{2}
, and that
\Gamma
is an inner semidirect product of these subgroups.
The identity element of
\Gamma
is
(0,1)
, and the inverse of
(a,b)
is
(\phi_{b^{-1}}(-a),b^{-1})
.

Example 2.3
.

Let
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
be any groups.
For all
b\in\Gamma_{2}
let
\phi_{b}
be the identity automorphism of
\Gamma_{1}
.
Then
\Gamma_{1}\rtimes_{\phi}\Gamma_{2}
is the direct product of
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
.

Example 2.4
.

Let
\Gamma_{1}
be any (additively-written) group and let
\{1,-1\}^{\times}
be the multiplicatively-written group of order
2
.
Let
\phi_{1}
be the identity automorphism of
\Gamma_{1}
and let
\phi_{-1}
be the automorphism that inverts each element of
\Gamma_{1}
.
Then
\Gamma_{1}\rtimes_{\phi}\{1,-1\}^{\times}
is an outer semidirect product.
Note that for all
a\in\Gamma_{1}
, the element
(a,-1)
of
\Gamma
is its own inverse.

Example 2.5
.

Let
\mathbb{F}
be a field, let
m
be a positive integer, and let
\mathbb{F}^{m}
be the additive group of
m
-dimensional vectors over
\mathbb{F}
.
For each
b\in\mathbb{F}^{\times}
define
\phi_{b}\colon\mathbb{F}^{m}\to\mathbb{F}^{m}
by scaling, so
\phi_{b}(x)=bx
.
Then
\mathbb{F}^{m}\rtimes_{\phi}\mathbb{F}^{\times}
is an outer semidirect product with
(a,b)\circ(c,d)=(a+bc,bd)
.

2.1.2
Frobenius groups

In Theorem
1.1
, if
\Gamma_{1}
is trivial or equal to
\Gamma
, then
\Gamma
can be any group.
However, when
\Gamma_{1}
is nontrivial and proper, the existence of the collection
\mathcal{A}
of nontrivial malnormal subgroups of
\Gamma
places significant structure on
\Gamma
.
In particular, when
\Gamma
is finite, it implies that
\Gamma
is a special finite permutation group called a Frobenius group.

Following
[
21
]
, a
Frobenius group
is a finite group
\Gamma
with a proper nontrivial malnormal subgroup
A
, called a
Frobenius complement
.
A remarkable result of Frobenius shows that the set
\Gamma_{1}=(\Gamma-(\cup_{g\in\Gamma}\,g^{-1}Ag))\cup\{1\}
is always a nontrivial normal subgroup of
\Gamma
, referred to as a
Frobenius kernel
of
\Gamma
.
Therefore every Frobenius group has the structure of the groups of Theorem
1.1
with
\mathcal{A}
as the set of conjugates of a Frobenius complement, and conversely, if
\mathcal{A}
contains a proper subgroup, then
\Gamma
is a Frobenius group (when
\Gamma
is finite).
Moreover, it is well-known (see
[
21
, Theorem 4.2]
) that the Frobenius kernel is unique and that any two Frobenius complements are conjugate, so a group is Frobenius in at most one way.

Frobenius groups can also be defined as special permutation groups.
Let
\Gamma
be a transitive group of permutations of a set
X
(for all
x,y\in X
there is a permutation sending
x
to
y
) so that each non-identity permutation has at most one fixed point, and some non-identity permutation has a fixed point.
It is straightforward to show that for each
x\in X
, the subgroup of permutations that fix
x
(the
stabilizer
of
x
) is malnormal, and that any two stabilizers are conjugate.
Therefore if
\Gamma
is finite then it is a Frobenius group.
Here the Frobenius kernel is the subgroup of fixed-point-free permutations (called
derangements
).
It turns out that every Frobenius group arises in this way by taking
X
to be the set of right cosets of a Frobenius complement (see
[
21
, Theorem 1.7]
).
The permutation perspective is often natural for describing examples.

Example 2.6
.

Let
X
be the set of vertices of a regular
n
-gon with
n
odd, and let
D_{2n}
be the dihedral group of order
2n
, viewed as a group of permutations of
X
.
Each nontrivial rotation has no fixed points, and each nontrivial reflection has one fixed point since
n
is odd.
Therefore
D_{2n}
is a Frobenius group where the kernel is the group of rotations and each reflection with the identity is a complement.

Example 2.7
.

For each finite field
\mathbb{F}
with at least three elements and each positive integer
m
, the group of affine linear transformations
x\mapsto a+bx
of
\mathbb{F}^{m}
with
b\neq 0
is a Frobenius group with
X=\mathbb{F}^{m}
.
The kernel is the subgroup of translations and the complements are the stabilizers.

In the following example it is more natural to simply identify a proper nontrivial malnormal subgroup.

Example 2.8
.

Let
\Gamma_{1}
be an (additively-written) nontrivial finite abelian group with odd order, and let
\Gamma=\Gamma_{1}\rtimes\{1,-1\}^{\times}
where
-1
acts by inversion, as in Example
2.4
.
It is straightforward to show that for each
a\in\Gamma_{1}-\{0\}
the subgroup
\{(0,1),(a,-1)\}
in
\mathcal{A}
is malnormal (we show this in a more general setting in Example
2.13
), so
\Gamma
is a Frobenius group.
The kernel is the subgroup isomorphic to
\Gamma_{1}
and the complements are the subgroups
\{(0,1),(a,-1)\}
with
a\in\Gamma_{1}-\{0\}
.
When
\Gamma_{1}
is cyclic we recover Example
2.6
and when
\Gamma_{1}=\GF(3)^{m}
we recover Example
2.7
for
\mathbb{F}=\GF(3)
.

More exotic examples of Frobenius groups can be found in
[
29
]
, and more can be constructed from the following fact: if
\Gamma_{1}\rtimes\Gamma_{2}
and
\Gamma_{1}^{\prime}\rtimes\Gamma_{2}
are Frobenius groups, then so is
(\Gamma_{1}\times\Gamma_{1}^{\prime})\rtimes\Gamma_{2}
, where
\Gamma_{1}\times\Gamma_{1}^{\prime}
is the direct product of
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{1}^{\prime}
.

We can use properties of Frobenius groups to state some useful properties of the groups of Theorem
1.1
when they are finite.
A group partition is
nontrivial
if it contains at least two nontrivial subgroups.

Proposition 2.9
.

Let
\Gamma
be a group with a normal subgroup
\Gamma_{1}
and a nontrivial partition
\{\Gamma_{1}\}\cup\mathcal{A}
so that if
A\in\mathcal{A}
then
A
is malnormal and every conjugate of
A
is in
\mathcal{A}
.
If
\Gamma
is finite then the following hold:

(i)

The pair
(\Gamma_{1},\mathcal{A})
is unique with
\Gamma_{1}
as the Frobenius kernel and
\mathcal{A}
as the set of Frobenius complements.

(ii)

Any two groups in
\mathcal{A}
are isomorphic.

(iii)

Any two groups in
\mathcal{A}
are conjugate.

(iv)

There is a group
A\in\mathcal{A}
so that
\Gamma=\Gamma_{1}\rtimes A
.

(v)

\Gamma_{1}
is nontrivial.

Proof.

Every proper nontrivial malnormal subgroup of
\Gamma
is a Frobenius complement, so every group in
\mathcal{A}
is a Frobenius complement.
It is well-known that any two Frobenius complements are conjugate (see
[
21
, Theorem 4.2]
), so
(ii)
and
(iii)
hold.
Since
\mathcal{A}
is closed under group conjugation, it follows that
\mathcal{A}
is the set of Frobenius complements and
\Gamma_{1}
is the Frobenius kernel of
\Gamma
, which is also known to be unique (see
[
21
, Theorem 4.2]
), so
(i)
holds.
It is also well-known that
(iv)
holds for any
A\in\mathcal{A}
(see
[
21
, Theorem 1.2]
).
Finally, each
A\in\mathcal{A}
is a proper subgroup because the partition is nontrivial, so
(iv)
implies that
\Gamma_{1}
is nontrivial.
∎

2.1.3
Group partitions

The notion of a group partition will help us describe the groups for which Theorem
1.1
applies.
A
partition
of a group
\Gamma
is a collection
\Pi
of subgroups of
\Gamma
so that each non-identity element of
\Gamma
is in exactly one subgroup in
\Pi

[
24
]
.
The partition is
nontrivial
if
\Pi
contains at least two nontrivial groups.
For example, every Frobenius group has a nontrivial partition into its Frobenius kernel and Frobenius complements.
Non-Frobenius groups can also have a nontrivial partition.
For example, the direct sum of at least two copies of a cyclic group of prime order is not Frobenius but has a nontrivial partition into its cyclic subgroups.
Amazingly, work of Baer
[
2
]
, Kegel
[
23
]
, and Suzuki
[
30
]
combines to completely classify the finite groups with a nontrivial partition into six families, one of which is the family of Frobenius groups.
We make the following definition to describe the partition from Theorem
1.1
.

Definition 2.10
.

Let
\Gamma
be a (possibly infinite) group.
A partition
\{\Gamma_{1}\}\cup\mathcal{A}
of
\Gamma
is a
Frobenius partition
if
\Gamma_{1}
is a normal subgroup of
\Gamma
, and if
A\in\mathcal{A}
then
A
is malnormal and every conjugate of
A
is in
\mathcal{A}
.
A Frobenius partition is
nontrivial
if it contains at least two nontrivial groups, and is
trivial
otherwise.

Note that every group
\Gamma
has trivial Frobenius partition
\{\Gamma\}
.
And if
\Gamma
is a Frobenius group with kernel
\Gamma_{1}
and set
\mathcal{A}
of complements, then
\{\Gamma_{1}\}\cup\mathcal{A}
is a nontrivial Frobenius partition of
\Gamma
.
Conversely, by Proposition
2.9
, every finite group with a nontrivial Frobenius partition is a Frobenius group.
We will next discuss properties of infinite groups with a nontrivial Frobenius partition, and see that there are many natural groups of this form.
This gives a seemingly new generalization of Frobenius groups to infinite groups, and leads to some problems (Problem
2.14
) in group theory that seem nontrivial and interesting.

2.1.4
Infinite groups

When
\Gamma
is infinite with a proper nontrivial malnormal subgroup
A
, it is not guaranteed that
(\Gamma-(\cup_{g\in\Gamma}\,g^{-1}Ag))\cup\{1\}
is a subgroup (see
[
22
, pg. 51]
or Example
2.13
), unless
\Gamma
has special properties
[
9
,
22
]
.

Example 2.11
.

Example

Let

Example 2.12
.

Example 2.13

Problem 2.14

Let

Biased graphs and their matroids

Every gain graph has an associated

(G,\mathcal{B})
where
G
is a graph and
\mathcal{B}
is a set of cycles of
G
so that no theta subgraph (see Figure

\mathcal{B}
.
This property of

is known as the

.
The cycles in
\mathcal{B}
are
balanced
, and the cycles not in
\mathcal{B}
are

.
In his original paper introducing biased graphs, Zaslavsky
[

]
showed that if
(G,\psi)

\Gamma
-gain graph and

\psi

[

(G,\mathcal{B})

A

using walks on

.
Inspired by

, we say that a

of closed walks on

, starting at the same vertex, so that each walk contains exactly one cycle of

, the walks do not contain the same cycle, and each edge of

is traversed once or twice by the concatenation

.
Note that there are edges

and

of

so that

is a tree, and for

the edge

is in

but not
W_{3-i}
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\Gamma
be a group, let
\Gamma_{1}
be a normal subgroup of
\Gamma
, and let

be a partition of
\Gamma
so that each

is malnormal and has all conjugates in
\mathcal{A}
.
Let

be a
\Gamma
-gain graph and let

be a theta graph or handcuff circuit of

The following are equivalent:

(i)

.

(ii)

For every switching function

-normalized spanning tree, there is a group

so that
\psi^{\eta}(C)\subseteq A

(iii)

of walks for

and a group

so that
\psi(W_{1}),\psi(W_{2})\in A
.

(iv)

For every cyclic covering pair
(W_{1},W_{2})
of walks for

there is a group

so that
\psi(W_{1}),\psi(W_{2})\in A
.

Proof.

Let
\circ
be the group operation of
\Gamma
.
We will show the implications
(i)\Rightarrow(iv)\Rightarrow(iii)\Rightarrow(ii)\Rightarrow(i)
.
Clearly
(iv)
implies
(iii)
and
(ii)
implies
(i)
.
We will first show that
(i)
implies
(iv)
.
Suppose that
(i)
holds for switching function
\eta
and group

.
Let
(W_{1},W_{2})
be any cyclic covering pair of walks for
C
starting at a vertex
v
.
Since

\psi^{\eta}(W_{i})\in A

.

holds.

We will complete the proof by showing that

implies
(ii)
.
Suppose that

holds for

and

, and let

be the starting vertex of

and

.
Let

be a switching function so that

has a
\psi^{\eta}
-normalized spanning tree

, and let

.
Let

, so

.
Note that

for

, so
\psi^{\eta}(W_{i})\in A^{\prime}
.
By the definition of cyclic covering pair we see that

and

each traverse one of

, and either

or

traverses exactly one of

.
We may assume that

traverses

but not

, and

traverses

.
Since

is
\psi^{\eta}
-normalized this implies that
\psi^{\eta}(W_{1})\in\psi^{\eta}(e_{1})
, and since
\psi^{\eta}(W_{1})\in A^{\prime}
we see that
\psi^{\eta}(e_{1})\subseteq A^{\prime}
.
Note that
\psi^{\eta}(W_{2})
is the product of an element in
\psi^{\eta}(e_{2})
and possibly an element in

(if

traverses

).
Since
\psi^{\eta}(W_{2})
and
\psi^{\eta}(e_{1})
are both in

\psi^{\eta}(e_{2})
is in
A^{\prime}
.
Since

is
\psi^{\eta}
-normalized and
\psi^{\eta}(e_{i})\in A^{\prime}
for

it follows that
\psi^{\eta}(C)\subseteq A^{\prime}
, as desired.
∎

Our proofs in this paper will only make use of the equivalence between
(i)
and
(ii)
, but the equivalences between

,
(iii)
, and
(iv)
will be useful in Section

when we compare Theorem

with the constructions of Anderson, Su, and Zaslavsky

]
and Bernstein

]
.

We will now prove that
\mathcal{C}(\Gamma,\Gamma_{1},\mathcal{A},G,\psi)
is a linear class of circuits of the frame matroid

.
Combined with Theorem

, this defines an elementary lift of

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
\Gamma
be a group, let
\Gamma_{1}
be a normal subgroup of
\Gamma
, let
\{\Gamma_{1}\}\cup\mathcal{A}
be a partition of
\Gamma
so that each
A\in\mathcal{A}
is malnormal and has all conjugates in
\mathcal{A}
, and let
(G,\psi)
be a
\Gamma
-gain graph.
Then
\mathcal{C}(\Gamma,\Gamma_{1},\mathcal{A},G,\psi)
is a linear class of circuits of the underlying
(\Gamma/\Gamma_{1})
-frame matroid
F(G,\psi/\Gamma_{1})
.

Proof.

We will write
N
for the frame matroid
F(G,\psi/\Gamma_{1})
,
\mathcal{C}
for
\mathcal{C}(\Gamma,\Gamma_{1},\mathcal{A},G,\psi)
, and
\varepsilon
for the identity element of
\Gamma
.
Let
C_{1},C_{2}\in\mathcal{C}
with
|C_{1}\cup C_{2}|=r_{N}(C_{1}\cup C_{2})+2
, and let
C
be a circuit of
N
contained in
C_{1}\cup C_{2}
with
C\notin\{C_{1},C_{2}\}
.
We will show that
C\in\mathcal{C}
.
We first prove two important properties of the graph
G[C_{1}\cup C_{2}]
.
The
cyclomatic number
of a graph is the minimum number of edges whose deletion leaves a forest.

Claim 3.3.1
.

The graph
G[C_{1}\cup C_{2}]
is connected and has cyclomatic number at most
3
.

Proof.

Note that
G[C_{1}]
and
G[C_{2}]
are both connected graphs.
Then
G[C_{1}\cup C_{2}]
is connected or else
C\in\{C_{1},C_{2}\}
.
Since
|C_{1}\cup C_{2}|=r_{N}(C_{1}\cup C_{2})+2
, we can delete two edges from
C_{1}\cup C_{2}
to obtain an independent set
I
of the frame matroid
N
.
This implies that each component of
G[I]
has at most one cycle.
If
G[I]
is connected, then the claim follows.
If
G[I]
is disconnected, then it has at most three connected components because it is obtained by deleting two edges from a connected graph.
If it has three components, then neither deletion decreased the cyclomatic number and the claim follows.
If it has two components, then one of the two deletions did not decrease the cyclomatic number and the claim follows.
∎

If
G[C_{1}\cup C_{2}]
has cyclomatic number
2
then
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are both
\psi
-balanced cycles.
If they share no edges, then
C\in\{C_{1},C_{2}\}
.
If they share an edge then
G[C_{1}\cup C_{2}]
is a theta graph, and it follows from the theta property for
\psi
-balanced cycles that
C
is a
\psi
-balanced cycle and is therefore in
\mathcal{C}
.
So for the remainder of the proof we may assume that
G[C_{1}\cup C_{2}]
has cyclomatic number
3
.
The next claim deals with
\psi
-balanced cycles.

Claim 3.3.2
.

If
G[C_{1}\cup C_{2}]
contains a
\psi
-balanced cycle, then

.

Proof.

Suppose

contains a
\psi
-balanced cycle

.
Let
T
be a spanning tree of
G[C_{1}\cup C_{2}]
that contains all but one edge of

.
By switching, we may assume that
T
is
\psi
-normalized.
Since
D
is
\psi
-balanced, it follows that
D
is
\psi
-normalized.
Since
G[C_{1}\cup C_{2}]
has cyclomatic number
3
, there are two edges
e
and
f
in
(C_{1}\cup C_{2})-(T\cup D)
.
We will first show that there is some
A\in\mathcal{A}
that contains
\psi(e)
and
\psi(f)
.
If
C_{1}
is a handcuff or a theta graph then
e,f\in C_{1}
, and since

there is some
A\in\mathcal{A}
that contains
\psi(e)
and
\psi(f)
.
The same reasoning applies if
C_{2}
is a handcuff or a theta graph, so we may assume that
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are both
\psi
-balanced cycles.
By choosing

we may assume that
C_{1}
is
\psi
-normalized.
Then
e
and
f
are both in
C_{2}
, and since

is
\psi
-balanced and every edge other than

and

is
\psi
-normalized it follows that
\psi(e)=\psi(f)=\{\alpha,\alpha^{-1}\}
for some

.
If

then

is
(\psi/\Gamma_{1})
-normalized and so

because
G[C_{1}\cup C_{2}]
has cyclomatic number
3
.
But then
|C_{1}\cup C_{2}|-r_{N}(C_{1}\cup C_{2})=3
, a contradiction.
So
\alpha\notin\Gamma_{1}
, so there is some
A\in\mathcal{A}
with
\alpha\in A
, and therefore
A
contains
\psi(e)
and
\psi(f)
.

If
C
is a handcuff or a theta graph, then

and it follows that
C\in\mathcal{C}
.
So we may assume that
C
is a
(\psi/\Gamma_{1})
-balanced cycle.
We must show that
C
is
\psi
-balanced.
If
C
contains neither

nor
f
, then
C
is
\psi
-normalized.
If
C
contains exactly one of
e
and
f
, then
C
is not
(\psi/\Gamma_{1})
-balanced because

, a contradiction.
So
C
contains
e
and
f
.
Let
W
be a simple closed walk around
C
.
Then
\psi(W)
is the product of an element in
\psi(e)
and an element in
\psi(f)
, so
\psi(W)\in A
.
And
\psi(W)\in\Gamma_{1}
because
C
is
(\psi/\Gamma_{1})
-balanced.
So
\psi(W)=\varepsilon
because

, and therefore

is
\psi
-balanced and in
\mathcal{C}
, as desired.
∎

By Claim
3.3.2
we may assume that
G[C_{1}\cup C_{2}]
contains no
\psi
-balanced cycles.
This implies that
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are not
(\psi/\Gamma_{1})
-balanced; if

were
(\psi/\Gamma_{1})
-balanced, it would be
\psi
-balanced because it is in
\mathcal{C}
.
Note that
C
may be
(\psi/\Gamma_{1})
-balanced.
We consider one more special case.

Claim 3.3.3
.

If
G[C_{1}\cap C_{2}]
has at most one cycle, then

.

Suppose that

Then there is an edge

that is in a cycle of

and an edge

.

Since

has cyclomatic number

Let

be an edge of this cycle, and note that

.
By switching we may assume that it is
\psi
-normalized.
Note that for

,
\psi(e_{i})\neq\{\varepsilon\}
because

contains no
\psi
-balanced cycles.
Since
C_{1}
contains
e_{1}
and

but not

and

that contains
\psi(e_{1})
and
\psi(e_{3})

.

that contains
\psi(e_{2})
and
\psi(e_{3})
.
Then

because both contain
\psi(e_{3})
.
Let
T
be a spanning tree of
G[C]
.
By switching within

we can normalize

so that the edge(s) in

have gain values in
A_{1}
.
If
C
is a theta graph or a handcuff this implies that

, so we may assume that
C
is a
(\psi/\Gamma_{1})
-balanced cycle.
Let

be a simple closed walk around

.
Clearly

.
Also,
\psi(W)\in\Gamma_{1}
since

is
(\psi/\Gamma_{1})

.
∎

By Claim
3.3.3
we may assume that
G[C_{1}\cap C_{2}]
contains at least two cycles.
And by Claim
3.3.2
we may assume that
C_{1}
and

are not cycles.
Since

Therefore for

,

is a non-minimal graph with cyclomatic number two, so

and

are loose handcuffs that contain the same pair of cycles, say

and

.
For

, let

be the path in

between

and

.
Let

be the end of

in

.
Let

and

be edges of

and

, respectively, that are incident with

.
Let

be any edge of

.
By switching we may assume that the spanning tree

of

is
\psi
-normalized.
Since
C_{1}
contains
e
and

but not

and

, there is some

.
Let

be the sets of ends of

and

, respectively.
Let

be the

-gain function obtained from

.
Then

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
\Gamma
be a group with identity
\varepsilon
, let
\Gamma_{1}
be a normal subgroup of
\Gamma
, and let
\{\Gamma_{1}\}\cup\mathcal{A}

\Gamma

\mathcal{A}
.

.

Since biased graphs with no vertex-disjoint unbalanced cycles are well-studied and complex (see
[

]
), this may be an interesting direction for future work.

Finally, we can describe how Anderson et al. use neutral circuits to define a matroid.

\{\varepsilon_{12},\gamma_{12}\}
is a circuit of
N
(a
(\psi/\Gamma_{1})
-balanced cycle) that is not in
\mathcal{C}
(because it is not
\psi
-balanced), a contradiction.
Therefore
\Gamma_{1}
is trivial and the theorem holds with
\mathcal{A}=\{\Gamma\}
.
So we may assume that
r(M)=n+1
.

For
\alpha\in\Gamma
we will write
E_{\alpha}
for
\{\alpha_{ij}\colon 1\leq i<j\leq n\}
, and for a set
X\subseteq\Gamma
we will write
E_{X}
for
\cup_{\alpha\in X}E_{\alpha}
.
Note that
r_{M}(E_{\varepsilon})=r_{N}(E_{\varepsilon})=n-1
because all cycles in
E_{\varepsilon}
are
\psi
-balanced.
Using
\psi
-balanced cycles we can show that for all
\alpha\in\Gamma-\Gamma_{1}
, every circuit of
N
contained in
E_{\{\varepsilon,\alpha\}}
is in
\mathcal{C}
.

Claim 6.2.1
.

For all
\alpha\in\Gamma-\Gamma_{1}
and
1\leq i<j\leq n
we have
r_{M}(E_{\varepsilon}\cup\alpha_{ij})=r_{M}(E_{\{\varepsilon,\alpha\}})=n
.

Proof.

We may assume that
(i,j)=(1,2)
.
Let
B=\{\varepsilon_{1j}\colon 2\leq j\leq n\}\cup\alpha_{12}
.
This is a basis of
N
and is therefore independent in
M
, so
r_{M}(E_{\varepsilon}\cup\alpha_{12})=n
since
r_{M}(E_{\varepsilon})=n-1
.
For each
j
with
3\leq j\leq n
, the
\psi
-balanced cycle
\{\alpha_{12},\varepsilon_{2j},\alpha_{1j}\}
shows that
\alpha_{1j}\in\cl_{M}(E_{\varepsilon}\cup\alpha_{12})
.
Then for all
2\leq i<j\leq n
, the
\psi
-balanced cycle
\{\varepsilon_{1i},\alpha_{ij},\alpha_{1j}\}
shows that
\alpha_{ij}\in\cl_{M}(E_{\varepsilon}\cup\alpha_{12})
.
Therefore
E_{\alpha}\subseteq\cl_{M}(E_{\varepsilon}\cup\alpha_{12})
, so the claim holds.
∎

We will now define an equivalence relation
\sim
on
\Gamma-\Gamma_{1}
; this equivalence relation will lead to
\mathcal{A}
.
For
\alpha,\beta\in\Gamma-\Gamma_{1}
we write
\alpha\sim\beta
if

r_{M}(E_{\{\varepsilon,\alpha,\beta\}})=n
.
Equivalently,
\alpha\sim\beta
if every circuit of
N
contained in
E_{\{\varepsilon,\alpha,\beta\}}
is in
\mathcal{C}
.
Clearly
\sim
is symmetric, and
\sim
is reflexive by Claim
6.2.1
.
To show that
\sim
is transitive, suppose
\alpha\sim\beta
and
\beta\sim\Gamma
for
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in\Gamma-\Gamma_{1}
.
Then
E_{\{\varepsilon,\beta\}}
spans
E_{\alpha}
in
M
because
\alpha\sim\beta
, and
E_{\{\varepsilon,\beta\}}
spans
E_{\gamma}
in
M
because
\beta\sim\gamma
.
Therefore
r_{M}(E_{\{\varepsilon,\alpha,\beta,\gamma\}})=r_{M}(E_{\{\varepsilon,\beta\}})=n
, so
\alpha\sim\gamma
.
So
\sim
is an equivalence relation on
\Gamma-\Gamma_{1}
.
Let
\mathcal{A}_{1}
be the set of equivalence classes of
\sim
, and let
\mathcal{A}=\{A\cup\varepsilon\colon A\in\mathcal{A}_{1}\}
.

Claim 6.2.2
.

Each
A\in\mathcal{A}
is a subgroup of
\Gamma
.

Proof.

Let
A\in\mathcal{A}
and let
\alpha,\beta\in A-\{\varepsilon\}
.
We will first show that
\alpha^{-1}\in A
.
The
\psi
-balanced cycle
\{\alpha^{-1}_{12},\alpha_{23},\varepsilon_{13}\}
shows that
\alpha^{-1}_{12}\in\cl_{M}(E_{\{\varepsilon,\alpha\}})
.
It then follows from Claim
6.2.1
that
E_{\{\varepsilon,\alpha\}}
spans
E_{\alpha^{-1}}
in
M
.
So
r_{M}(\{\varepsilon,\alpha,\alpha^{-1}\})=n
, so
\alpha\sim\alpha^{-1}
and therefore
\alpha^{-1}\in A
.

We will next show that
\alpha\circ\beta\in A
.
The
\psi
-balanced cycle
\{(\alpha\circ\beta)_{12},\beta^{-1}_{23},\alpha^{-1}_{13}\}
shows that
(\alpha\circ\beta)_{12}\in\cl_{M}(E_{A})
, and it follows from Claim
6.2.1
that
E_{\alpha\circ\beta}\subseteq\cl_{M}(E_{A})
.
So
r_{M}(E_{A\cup\{\alpha\circ\beta\}})=n
and therefore
\alpha\circ\beta\in A
by the definition of
\sim
.
∎

We have now shown that
\mathcal{A}
is a collection of subgroups of
\Gamma
so that each pair intersects in only the identity and each shares only the identity with
\Gamma_{1}
.
Note that since each
A\in\mathcal{A}
satisfies
r_{M}(E_{A})=r_{N}(E_{A})=n
, all circuits of
N
contained in
E_{A}
are in
\mathcal{C}
.
We will next show that each
A\in\mathcal{A}
is malnormal.

Claim 6.2.3
.

Each
A\in\mathcal{A}
is a malnormal subgroup of
\Gamma
.

Proof.

Suppose towards a contradiction that there is some
A\in\mathcal{A}
, some
\alpha\in A-\{\varepsilon\}
, and some
\gamma\in\Gamma-A
so that
\gamma^{-1}\circ\alpha\circ\gamma\in A
.
Let
\beta=\gamma^{-1}\circ\alpha\circ\gamma
, so
\alpha\sim\beta
.
Let
C=\{\varepsilon_{12},\alpha_{12},\varepsilon_{23},\varepsilon_{34},\beta_{34}\}
, so
C
is a circuit of
N
and
C\in\mathcal{C}
.
Consider the set
C\cup\gamma_{23}
.
Since
\gamma_{23}\in\cl_{N}(C)
we see that
|C\cup\gamma_{23}|-r_{N}(C\cup\gamma_{23})=2
.
Let
C^{\prime}=(C-\varepsilon_{23})\cup\gamma_{23}
and note that
C^{\prime}
is a circuit of
N
.
We claim that
C^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}
.
Let
\eta\colon[n]\to\Gamma
be the switching function so that
\eta(v_{1})=\eta(v_{2})=\gamma
and
\eta(v_{i})=\varepsilon
if
i\notin\{1,2\}
.
By Lemma
6.1
we see that
\bar{\eta}(C^{\prime})
is a circuit of
N
and that
\bar{\eta}(C^{\prime})\in\mathcal{C}
if and only if
C^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}
.
Note that
\psi(\bar{\eta}(C^{\prime}))
is contained in
A
.
Since every cycle contained in
E_{A}
is in
\mathcal{C}
, this implies that
\bar{\eta}(C^{\prime})\in\mathcal{C}
, and therefore
C^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}
.
Since
|C\cup\gamma_{23}|-r_{N}(C\cup\gamma_{23})=2
and
C\cup\gamma_{23}
contains the circuits
C,C^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}
, every circuit of
N
contained in
C\cup\gamma_{23}
is in
\mathcal{C}
because
\mathcal{C}
is a linear class.
If
\gamma\in\Gamma_{1}
then
\{\varepsilon_{23},\gamma_{23}\}
is a circuit of
N
that is in
\mathcal{C}
even though it is not
\psi
-balanced, a contradiction.
So
\gamma\notin\Gamma_{1}
.
Then
\{\varepsilon_{12},\alpha_{12},\varepsilon_{23},\gamma_{23}\}
is a tight handcuff circuit of
N
that is in
\mathcal{C}
, and is therefore a circuit of
M
.
Then
\gamma_{23}\in\cl_{M}(E_{A})
, and so Claim
6.2.1
implies that
r_{M}(E_{A\cup\gamma})=n
.
By the definition of
\sim
we see that
\gamma\in A
, a contradiction.
Therefore
A
is malnormal.
∎

Let
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}=\mathcal{C}(\Gamma,\Gamma_{1},\mathcal{A},K_{n}^{\Gamma},\psi_{n}^{\Gamma})
and let
M^{\prime}=M(\Gamma,\Gamma_{1},\mathcal{A},K_{n}^{\Gamma},\psi_{n}^{\Gamma})
from Definitions
3.1
and
3.4
.
We will now show that
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
, implying that
M=M^{\prime}
.
Since a cycle of
K_{n}^{\Gamma}
is in
\mathcal{C}
if and only if it is
\psi
-balanced,
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
contain the same cycles.
Let
C
be a handcuff or theta graph circuit of
N
.
Let
\eta\colon[n]\to\Gamma
be a switching function so that
\bar{\eta}(C)
has a
\psi
-normalized spanning tree
T
.
Let
e_{1},e_{2}\in C-T
.
Note that
\bar{\eta}(e_{1})
and
\bar{\eta}(e_{2})
are not in
\Gamma_{1}
since
C
is a circuit of
N
.
Consider the following statements:

(i)

C\in\mathcal{C}
.

(ii)

\bar{\eta}(C)\in\mathcal{C}
.

(iii)

There is some
A\in\mathcal{A}
so that
E_{A}
contains
\bar{\eta}(e_{1})
and
\bar{\eta}(e_{2})
.

(iv)

\bar{\eta}(C)\in\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
.

(v)

C\in\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
.

We will show that statements
(i)
and
(v)
are equivalent by showing that each consecutive pair of statements are equivalent.
Statements
(i)
and
(ii)
are equivalent by Lemma
6.1
applied to
M
, and
(iv)
and
(v)
are equivalent by Lemma
6.1
applied to
M^{\prime}
.
Statements
(iii)
and
(iv)
are equivalent by Lemma
3.2
.
So it suffices to show that
(ii)
and
(iii)
are equivalent.
If
(iii)
holds, then
\bar{\eta}(C)\subseteq E_{A}
, and since all circuits of
N
contained in
E_{A}
are in
\mathcal{C}
as discussed previously,
(ii)
holds.
Conversely, suppose that
\bar{\eta}(C)\in\mathcal{C}
.
Let
A\in\mathcal{A}
so that
\bar{\eta}(e_{1})\in E_{A}
, and let
\alpha\in\Gamma
so that
\bar{\eta}(e_{2})\in E_{\alpha}
.
Since
\bar{\eta}(C)
is a circuit of
M
we see that
E_{A}
spans
\bar{\eta}(e_{2})
, and it then follows from Claim
6.2.1
that
r_{M}(E_{A\cup\alpha})=n
.
By the definition of
\sim
, this implies that
\alpha\in A
, so
\bar{\eta}(e_{2})\in E_{A}
and
(iii)
holds.
We have now shown that
(i)
and
(v)
are equivalent.
Therefore
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
, so
M=M^{\prime}
, as desired.
∎

We comment that the statement of Theorem
6.2
is false when
n=3
because of the remark at the end of Section
3
and the fact that
K_{3}^{\Gamma}
has no vertex-disjoint unbalanced cycles.

7
Future work

While we have already presented several conjectures (Conjectures
4.12
,
4.13
,
5.8
, and
5.9
) and one problem (Problem
2.14
), we close by discussing several more interesting directions for future work.

7.1
Universal models

Following Kahn and Kung
[
20
]
, a
sequence of universal models
for a class
\mathcal{M}
of matroids is a sequence
M_{2},M_{3},\dots
of simple matroids in
\mathcal{M}
so that for each
r\geq 2
, the matroid
M_{r}
has rank
r
and every simple rank-
r
matroid in
\mathcal{M}
is a restriction of
M_{r}
.
For example, projective geometries form a sequence of universal models for matroids representable over a given finite field.
It is well-known that for every finite group
\Gamma
, the classes of
\Gamma
-frame matroids and
\Gamma
-lifted-graphic matroids each have a sequence of universal models (see
[
35
, page 47]
and
[
17
, Chapter 4]
).
This should be true more generally for
\mathcal{M}(\Gamma,\Gamma_{1},\mathcal{A})
as well when
\Gamma
is a Frobenius group.

Conjecture 7.1
.

If
\Gamma
is a Frobenius group with Frobenius kernel
\Gamma_{1}
and set
\mathcal{A}
of Frobenius complements, then
\mathcal{M}(\Gamma,\Gamma_{1},\mathcal{A})
has a sequence of universal models.

We plan to prove this conjecture in a sequel paper, and to show that the universal models are unavoidable minors of dense elementary lifts of frame matroids known as
(\alpha,1)
-frame matroids, which are conjectured by Geelen, Gerards, and Whittle
[
14
, Conjecure 7.5]
to be of fundamental importance in extremal matroid theory.

7.2
Rank-
t
lifts

A matroid
M
is a
rank-
t
lift
of a matroid
N
if there is a matroid
K
with a set
X
so that
K\!\setminus\!X=M
,
K/X=N
, and
r(M)-r(N)=t
.
Note that an elementary lift is a rank-
t
lift with
t\in\{0,1\}
.
For a group
\Gamma
, Theorem
1.1
takes in a
\Gamma
-graph
(G,\psi)
and outputs an elementary lift
M
of the frame matroid
F(G,\psi)
so that a cycle of
G
is a circuit of
M
if and only if it is
\psi
-balanced.
What if we wish to construct a rank-
t
lift with
t\geq 2
?

Problem 7.2
.

Let
t
be an integer with
t\geq 2
.
For which finite groups
\Gamma
with nontrivial normal subgroup
\Gamma_{1}
is there a construction that takes in a
\Gamma
-gain graph
(G,\psi)
and outputs a rank-
t
lift of the frame matroid
F(G,\psi/\Gamma_{1})
so that a cycle of
G
is a circuit of
M
if and only if it is
\psi
-balanced?

Our motivating example comes from the class of matroids with a representation over a field
\mathbb{F}
of the following form:

\begin{tabular}[]{|ccc|}\hline\cr&$t$ rows&\\
\hline\cr&&\\
&$\mathbb{F}^{\times}$-frame matrix&\\[14.22636pt]
\hline\cr\end{tabular}.

When
\mathbb{F}
is finite, this class of matroids is important in matroid structure theory for the same reasons as outlined in Section
5.3
in the case that
t=1
.
Each column of the matrix is naturally associated with a pair
(a,b)
where
a\in\mathbb{F}^{t}
and
b\in\mathbb{F}^{\times}
, so it should be possible to construct the vector matroid from a
(\mathbb{F}^{t}\rtimes\mathbb{F}^{\times})
-gain graph where
\mathbb{F}^{\times}
acts on
\mathbb{F}^{t}
by scaling.
So we expect that
(\mathbb{F}^{t}\rtimes\mathbb{F}^{\times})
is a valid choice for
\Gamma
in Problem
7.2
, but it is unclear whether or not such a construction is also possible for other groups.
Walsh
[
32
, Theorem 2]
and Bernstein and Walsh
[
3
, Theorem 2]

gave a construction for rank-
t
lifts of graphic matroids with
t\geq 2
, so one approach for Problem
7.2
would be to somehow combine the work of Bernstein and Walsh with Theorem
1.1
of the present paper.

7.3
Unified matroids in applied discrete geometry

As a final direction for future work, we discuss the possibility of combining Theorem
1.1
with work of Tanigawa
[
31
]
.
For any group
\Gamma
, Tanigawa generalizes the classes of
\Gamma
-frame matroids
[
31
, Section 4]
and
\Gamma
-lifted-graphic matroids
[
31
, Section 8]
by perturbing the rank function via a submodular function
\mu\colon 2^{\Gamma}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
called a
symmetric polymatroidal function
over
\Gamma
.
In Section 9 he discusses the possibility of unifying his two constructions, and this may now be possible via Theorem
1.1
.
In particular, if
M\in\mathcal{M}(\Gamma,\Gamma_{1},\mathcal{A})
, it may be possible to perturb the rank function of
M
(given by Proposition
4.2
) via two symmetric polymatroidal functions simultaneously, one over
\Gamma_{1}
and one over
\Gamma/\Gamma_{1}
.
Since Tanigawa presents many different applications of his constructions in rigidity theory
[
31
, Section 1.1]
, this potential generalization may have further applications in rigidity theory.
