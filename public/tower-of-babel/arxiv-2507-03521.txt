PINN-DG: Residual neural network methods trained with Finite Elements
Georgios Grekas, Charalambos G. Makridakis, Tristan Pryer

Abstract
. Over the past few years, neural network methods have evolved in various directions for approximating partial differential equations (PDEs). A promising new development is the integration of neural networks with classical numerical techniques such as finite elements and finite differences. In this paper, we introduce a new class of Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) trained using discontinuous Galerkin finite element methods. Unlike standard collocation-based PINNs that rely on pointwise gradient evaluations and Monte Carlo quadrature, our approach computes the loss functional using finite element interpolation and integration. This avoids costly pointwise derivative computations, particularly advantageous for elliptic PDEs requiring second-order derivatives, and inherits key stability and accuracy benefits from the finite element framework. We present a convergence analysis based on variational arguments and support our theoretical findings with numerical experiments that demonstrate improved efficiency and robustness.

1.
Introduction and method formulation

The model problem

In recent years, neural network methods have been developed in a variety of directions for approximating partial differential equations (PDEs). A particularly promising line of work involves integrating neural networks with established numerical methods such as finite element and finite difference schemes. The goal is to develop hybrid techniques that combine the strengths of both paradigms.

In this paper, we propose a novel formulation of Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) that incorporates finite element spaces into the training process. Specifically, rather than using standard quadrature rules that lead to collocation-based loss evaluation, we adopt a finite element-based approach that enables efficient computation of otherwise intractable loss functionals. This strategy eliminates the need for pointwise gradient evaluations, particularly advantageous in elliptic problems, where second-order derivatives are required, and inherits several desirable features of the finite element method, stability, flexibility, and scalability in complex geometries.

To illustrate the method, we begin with the classical Poisson problem:

(1.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &-\varDelta u=f\quad&&\text{in}\,\,&&\Omega\\[2.0pt]
&\quad u=0\quad&&\text{on}\,\,&&\partial\Omega,\\[2.0pt]
\end{aligned}\right.

where
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, for
d=1,2,3
.

We define the associated energy functional

(1.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}[u]=\int_{\Omega}\left|\varDelta u+f\right|^{2}dx,

and consider weak solutions of (
1.1
) as minimisers of
\mathcal{E}
over the space

V(\Omega)=\{w\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega):\varDelta w\in L^{2}(\Omega)\}
. This variational characterisation motivates a PINN approach, where one seeks minimisers of (
1.2
) over a neural network ansatz space.

Here we consider functions
u_{\theta}
defined through neural networks and we are interested in approximating minimisers of
(
1.2
) training the network parameters which are represented collectively by the index
\theta
.
Choosing regular enough activation functions, a discretized version of the energy functional in (
1.2
) is minimised. In fact,
the standard approach
training through Monte-Carlo quadrature
yielding a collocation type loss functional:
To be more precise we consider a collection
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots
of i.i.d.
\varOmega
-valued random variables, defined on an appropriate probability space representing a random choice of points in
\varOmega\,.

If we approximate the energy functional by Monte Carlo integration we shall need to evaluate the integrant at random points
X_{i}(\omega)\in\varOmega,

\omega
being a fixed instance:

(1.3)

\mathcal{E}_{N,\omega}(u)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\,\left|(\varDelta u+f)\,(X_{i}(\omega)\right|^{2}\,.

For the sake of simplicity in the exposition, we disregard the boundary terms that are typically included in a weak manner in the loss functional.
Standard PINN methods rely on the minimization of functionals of the form (
1.3
) over parametrized neural network spaces of a given architecture.
Point evaluations of the operator at the random points are particularly straightforward and yield a collocation-type loss. The primary advantage of such training methods is that they scale reasonably in high dimensions. However, collocation-type methods admit less natural discrete representations, and their implementation requires computationally demanding algorithms such as back-propagation for each operator evaluation. Our objective in this work is to propose alternative training methods that utilise finite elements to approximate the continuous loss.

Finite Elements and Neural Network Functions

We adopt standard notation for finite element functions defined on a triangulation
T_{h}
of a domain
\varOmega
, with local mesh size denoted by
h=h(x)
. For each
K\in T_{h}
, let
\mathbb{P}_{q}(K)
denote the set of polynomials of degree at most
q
. We define the
C^{1}
-conforming finite element space

(1.4)

{\mathcal{S}}_{h,1}(\varOmega):=\left\{v\in C^{1}(\bar{\varOmega})\,:\,{\left.\kern-1.2ptv\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{K}}\in\mathbb{P}_{q}(K),\,K\in T_{h}\right\}.

These spaces can be realised, for example, using Argyris elements and satisfy
{\mathcal{S}}_{h,1}(\varOmega)\subset H^{2}(\varOmega)
; see
[
10
, Chapter 3]
. We also consider the simpler
C^{0}
-conforming spaces of continuous piecewise polynomials,

(1.5)

{\mathcal{S}}_{h,0}(\varOmega):=\left\{v\in C^{0}(\bar{\varOmega})\,:\,{\left.\kern-1.2ptv\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{K}}\in\mathbb{P}_{q}(K),\,K\in T_{h}\right\}.

For a given space
{\mathcal{S}}_{h,1}(\varOmega)
, the interpolation operator
I_{{\mathcal{S}}_{h,1}}:C^{1}(\bar{\varOmega})\to{\mathcal{S}}_{h,1}(\varOmega)
is defined using the standard basis associated with the finite element space. When the target function
v
is sufficiently smooth, we may use this interpolant to define a discrete version of the energy functional
{\mathcal{E}}(v)
by

(1.6)

\mathcal{E}_{{\mathcal{S}}_{h,1}}(v)=\int_{\varOmega}\left|\varDelta I_{{\mathcal{S}}_{h,1}}v+I_{{\mathcal{S}}_{h,1}}f\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.

Minimisers of
\mathcal{E}_{{\mathcal{S}}_{h,1}}(v)
will be sought over neural network functions
v=v_{\theta}
, where
\theta
denotes the network parameters.

This approach, training neural networks via finite element interpolation, has been recently proposed in
[
29
]
. The idea is to evaluate the loss functional by interpolating the network output into a conforming finite element space, typically a subspace of
H^{2}(\Omega)
. However, implementing
C^{1}
-conforming spaces such as
{\mathcal{S}}_{h,1}(\varOmega)
can be challenging in practice, especially in three dimensions.

The methods proposed in this work employ the Discontinuous Galerkin framework to approximate the continuous energy functional
\mathcal{E}
. For ease of implementation, we adopt finite element spaces with lower regularity, specifically the standard
C^{0}
finite element space
{\mathcal{S}}_{h,0}(\varOmega)
. An interpolation operator
I_{{\mathcal{S}}_{h,0}}:C^{0}(\bar{\varOmega})\to{\mathcal{S}}_{h,0}(\varOmega)
is used to define the discrete energy functional:

(1.7)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{{\mathcal{S}}_{h,0}}(v)

\displaystyle=\sum_{K\in T_{h}}\int_{K}\left|\varDelta I_{{\mathcal{S}}_{h,0}}v+I_{{\mathcal{S}}_{h,0}}f\right|^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle+\operatorname{cons}(I_{{\mathcal{S}}_{h,0}}v,f)+\alpha\,\operatorname{pen}(I_{{\mathcal{S}}_{h,0}}v,f).

The additional terms
\operatorname{cons}(I_{{\mathcal{S}}_{h,0}}v,f)
and
\operatorname{pen}(I_{{\mathcal{S}}_{h,0}}v,f)
denote consistency and penalty contributions, respectively. These typically involve jumps and averages across element interfaces, as is standard in Discontinuous Galerkin methods. Precise definitions are given in (
3.1
). For related formulations in the finite element literature, see e.g.
[
9
,
21
,
37
,
46
,
27
]
.

Contribution, results and bibliography

DG-PINNs.

We propose a novel approach that connects Discontinuous Galerkin finite elements with Physics-Informed Neural Networks through
training
, by employing finite element spaces to evaluate the integrals appearing in the loss functional.

Residual-based loss formulations involve the differential operator directly, rather than its variational form, and thus require higher regularity. However, standard
C^{0}
finite element functions typically have discontinuous gradients. To ensure consistency and stability, we introduce appropriate gradient jump terms across element interfaces, following standard techniques in the Discontinuous Galerkin framework
[
9
]
.

While previous approaches have typically relied on quadrature- or Monte Carlo-based collocation methods, our method preserves the simplicity of standard neural network formulations for PDEs while incorporating well-established finite element tools (and software) for loss computation. This hybrid approach leads to improved accuracy and computational efficiency.

The resulting method is broadly applicable and can be extended to a wide class of PDEs, since least-squares loss formulations and Physics-Informed Neural Networks are in principle suited for both linear and nonlinear problems. However, the choice of finite element spaces and corresponding discrete loss formulations is non-trivial and must reflect the stability properties of the continuous problem. These methods are especially effective when appropriate finite element spaces can be constructed efficiently.

Stability and Convergence.

The theoretical foundations of our method are detailed in Section 3. To establish stability and convergence—as advocated in
[
24
]
—we derive first discrete coercivity estimates for the loss functional. Then, assuming abstract approximability properties of the discrete spaces, we prove convergence by employing the
\liminf
–
\limsup
framework of DeGiorgi (see Section2.3.4 of
[
16
]
), which is closely related to
\Gamma
-convergence, a standard tool in variational analysis.

To this end, we combine finite element techniques with arguments from the calculus of variations, and we establish both the stability of the method (see Proposition
3.1
) and its convergence (see Theorem
3.1
).

Numerical Experiments.

In Section
4
, we present numerical experiments that demonstrate the accuracy, robustness and efficiency of the proposed method. The integration of finite element discretisations into the training process enables consistent approximations across different network architectures and domain geometries, including cases with singularities. Compared to collocation-based PINNs, the proposed approach achieves comparable or improved accuracy at a significantly reduced computational cost and memory usage. The experiments also highlight the stabilising role of gradient jump terms, especially for deeper architectures and under-resolved training meshes.

Preliminaries

2.1.
Finite Element Spaces

We shall use standard notation for Sobolev spaces

W^{s,p}(\Omega)
, consisting of functions with weak derivatives up to order
s
in
L^{p}(\Omega)
, defined on a domain
\Omega
.
The corresponding norm is denoted by
\|\cdot\|_{W^{s,p}(\Omega)}
, the seminorm by
|\cdot|_{W^{s,p}(\Omega)}
, and the
L^{p}
-norm by

\|\cdot\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}
.

We assume that
\Omega
is a domain with sufficiently smooth boundary
\partial\Omega
, and let
T_{h}
be a triangulation of
\Omega
with mesh size
h
. To avoid additional complications in the subsequent analysis, we assume that any element
K
with an edge
e
on the boundary satisfies
e\subset\partial\Omega
. Furthermore, the partition is assumed to be shape-regular; see
[
10
]
.

Next, we define the standard space of continuous piecewise polynomial functions as

(2.1)

{\mathcal{S}}^{q}_{h}(\Omega):=\left\{v\in C^{0}(\bar{\Omega})\,:\,{\left.\kern-1.2ptv\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{K}}\in\mathbb{P}_{q}(K),\;K\in T_{h}\right\},

where
\mathbb{P}_{q}(K)
denotes the set of polynomials of total degree at most
q\in\mathbb{N}
on element
K
.

We also consider the discontinuous finite-dimensional space

(2.2)

\tilde{{\mathcal{S}}}^{q}_{h}(\Omega):=\left\{v\in L^{2}(\Omega)\,:\,{\left.\kern-1.2ptv\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{K}}\in\mathbb{P}_{q}(K),\;K\in T_{h}\right\}.

Let
E_{h}
denote the set of mesh edges. We partition
E_{h}
into boundary edges
E^{b}_{h}=E_{h}\cap\partial\Omega
and internal edges
E^{i}_{h}=E_{h}\setminus E^{b}_{h}
.

The trace of functions in
\tilde{{\mathcal{S}}}^{q}_{h}(\Omega)
lies in the space
T(E_{h}):=\prod_{e\in E_{h}}\mathbb{P}_{q}(e)
. We define the average and jump operators by:

(2.3)

\displaystyle\{\kern-3.125pt\{\cdot\}\kern-3.125pt\}:

\displaystyle T(E_{h})\to L^{p}(E_{h}),

\displaystyle\{\kern-3.125pt\{w\}\kern-3.125pt\}:=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left({\left.\kern-1.2ptw\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{K_{e^{+}}}}+{\left.\kern-1.2ptw\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{K_{e^{-}}}}\right),\quad\text{for }e\in E^{i}_{h}.

(2.4)

\displaystyle\llbracket\cdot\rrbracket:

\displaystyle T(E_{h})\to L^{p}(E_{h}),

\displaystyle\llbracket v\rrbracket:=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{\left.\kern-1.2ptv\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{K_{e^{+}}}}-{\left.\kern-1.2ptv\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{K_{e^{-}}}},&\text{for }e\in E^{i}_{h},\\
v,&\text{for }e\in E^{b}_{h}.\end{array}\right.

Note that the jump of the normal derivative satisfies

\llbracket\nabla v\cdot n_{e}\rrbracket={\left.\kern-1.2pt\nabla v\cdot n_{e^{+}}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{K_{e^{+}}}}+{\left.\kern-1.2pt\nabla v\cdot n_{e^{-}}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{K_{e^{-}}}},

where
K_{e^{+}}
and
K_{e^{-}}
are the elements sharing the internal edge
e
, and
n_{e^{+}},n_{e^{-}}
are the corresponding outward unit normals.

Approximation properties

For convenience, we briefly recall some standard approximation properties of finite element and neural network spaces.

We begin by defining the standard interpolation operator
I_{{\mathcal{S}}^{q}_{h}}
and summarising its approximation behaviour. Let
\Phi_{\mathcal{Z}}
denote the Lagrangian basis of
{\mathcal{S}}^{q}_{h}(\Omega)
, where
\mathcal{Z}
is the set of degrees of freedom. The interpolant
I_{{\mathcal{S}}^{q}_{h}}:C^{0}(\bar{\Omega})\rightarrow{\mathcal{S}}^{q}_{h}(\Omega)
is defined as

(2.5)

\displaystyle I_{{\mathcal{S}}^{q}_{h}}w(x)=\sum_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}w(z)\Phi_{z}(x).

If
u\in H^{s}(\Omega)
and
q\geq\lceil s\rceil-1
, then standard interpolation error estimates yield

(2.6)

\displaystyle|u-I_{{\mathcal{S}}^{q}_{h}}u|_{H^{m}(K)}\leq c\,h_{K}^{s-m}|u|_{H^{s}(K)},\quad 0\leq m\leq s,

where
h_{K}
denotes the diameter of an element
K\in T_{h}
.

For edges
e\subset\partial K
, the following trace estimate holds:

(2.7)

\displaystyle|u-I_{{\mathcal{S}}^{q}_{h}}u|_{H^{m}(e)}\leq c\,h_{K}^{s-m-1/2}|u|_{H^{s}(K)}.

From these estimates, we obtain the following proposition.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
v\in H^{s}(\Omega)
with
s>2
, and define
v_{h}=I_{{\mathcal{S}}^{q}_{h}}v
and
f_{h}=I_{{\mathcal{S}}^{q}_{h}}f
for some
q\geq\lceil s\rceil-1
. Then the following estimates hold:

(2.8)

\displaystyle\sum_{e\in E_{h}^{i}}\frac{1}{h_{e}}\int_{e}|\llbracket\nabla v_{h}\rrbracket|^{2}\lesssim h^{2s-4}|v|^{2}_{H^{s}(\Omega)},

and

(2.9)

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{e\in E_{h}^{i}}\int_{e}\{\kern-3.125pt\{\varDelta v_{h}+f_{h}\}\kern-3.125pt\}\llbracket\nabla v_{h}\cdot n\rrbracket\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim h^{s-2}|v|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}|v|_{H^{s}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle+(h+1)\|f\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}h^{s-2}|v|_{H^{s}(\Omega)}.

Proof.

Estimate (
2.8
), and the first term in (
2.9
) involving
\varDelta v_{h}
, follow by adapting the proof of
[
27
, Proposition 5.2]
, replacing the Hessian
D^{2}v_{h}
with the Laplacian
\varDelta v_{h}
.

To bound the term involving
f_{h}
, we apply the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and an inverse inequality of the form

\|u_{h}\|_{L^{2}(e)}\lesssim\frac{1}{h_{e}^{1/2}}\|u_{h}\|_{L^{2}(K_{e})},

where
e
is an edge,
K_{e}
are the elements containing
e
, and
h_{e}=|e|
. Combined with the interpolation estimate (
2.6
) and the assumption
f\in C^{0}(\Omega)
, we obtain

(2.10)

\displaystyle\sum_{e\in E_{h}^{i}}\int_{e}\left|f_{h}\llbracket\nabla v_{h}\cdot n\rrbracket\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{e\in E_{h}^{i}}\int_{e}|f_{h}|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\left(\sum_{e\in E_{h}^{i}}\int_{e}|\llbracket\nabla v_{h}\cdot n\rrbracket|^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(\sum_{e\in E_{h}^{i}}\frac{1}{h_{e}}\int_{K_{e}}|f_{h}|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\left(\sum_{e\in E_{h}^{i}}\frac{1}{h_{e}}\int_{e}|\llbracket\nabla v_{h}\rrbracket|^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|f_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\left(\sum_{e\in E_{h}^{i}}\frac{1}{h_{e}}\int_{e}|\llbracket\nabla v_{h}\rrbracket|^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\lesssim(h+1)\|f\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}h^{s-2}|v|_{H^{s}(\Omega)}.

∎

2.2.
Discrete spaces generated by Residual Neural Networks

We partially follow the exposition of
[
24
,
3
]
, considering functions

u_{\theta}
defined via neural networks. Specifically, we employ residual neural networks.

A residual neural network maps each point
x\in\Omega
to a scalar
u_{\theta}(x)\in{\mathbb{R}}
via

(2.11)

u_{\theta}(x)=C_{o}\circ\text{bl}_{L}\circ\text{bl}_{L-1}\cdots\circ\text{bl}_{1}\circ\sigma\circ C_{i}(x),\quad\forall x\in\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

The composition

(2.12)

\mathcal{C}_{L}:=C_{o}\circ\text{bl}_{L}\circ\text{bl}_{L-1}\cdots\circ\text{bl}_{1}\circ\sigma\circ C_{i}

defines a map
\mathcal{C}_{L}:{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{m^{\prime}}
. In our setting,
m=d
and
m^{\prime}=1
, so that
u_{\theta}(\cdot)=\mathcal{C}_{L}(\cdot)
.

Each map
\mathcal{C}_{L}
is a residual neural network with
L
blocks and activation function
\sigma
. It is characterised by the input and output layers
C_{i}
,
C_{o}
, which are affine maps:

(2.13)

\displaystyle C_{i}(x)

\displaystyle=W_{i}x+b_{i},

\displaystyle W_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N\times d},\;b_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N},

\displaystyle C_{o}(z)

\displaystyle=w_{o}\cdot z+b_{o},

\displaystyle w_{o}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N},\;b_{o}\in{\mathbb{R}},

and intermediate blocks defined by

(2.14)

\text{bl}_{k}(z)=\sigma\left(\left(W_{2k}\sigma(W_{1k}z+b_{1k})+b_{2k}\right)+z\right),

where
z,b_{jk}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
,
W_{jk}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N\times N}
for
j=1,2
and
k=1,\dots,L
. Here
\sigma(y)
denotes the vector obtained by applying
\sigma
componentwise:
\sigma(y)_{i}=\sigma(y_{i})
.

The index
\theta
collectively denotes all network parameters in
\mathcal{C}_{L}
. The set of all such networks with fixed structure (fixed
L,N,d
) is denoted by
\mathcal{N}
. We then define the corresponding space of neural network functions:

(2.15)

V_{\mathcal{N}}=\left\{u_{\theta}:\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}\;\middle|\;u_{\theta}(x)=\mathcal{C}_{L}(x)\text{ for some }\mathcal{C}_{L}\in\mathcal{N}\right\}.

It is important to note that
V_{\mathcal{N}}
is
not a linear space
. In general, if
u_{\theta},u_{\tilde{\theta}}\in V_{\mathcal{N}}
, there does not exist a network
\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{L}\in\mathcal{N}
such that
u_{\theta}+u_{\tilde{\theta}}=\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{L}
.

However, there is a one-to-one correspondence between parameters
\theta
and functions
u_{\theta}\in V_{\mathcal{N}}
, so we may identify

(2.16)

\theta\mapsto u_{\theta}\in V_{\mathcal{N}}.

The corresponding parameter space is

(2.17)

\Theta=\left\{\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\dim(\mathcal{N})}\;\middle|\;u_{\theta}\in V_{\mathcal{N}}\right\},

which is a linear subspace of
{\mathbb{R}}^{\dim(\mathcal{N})}
.

Since the main focus of this work is the design of stable and consistent loss functionals it will be useful for the forthcoming analysis to assume that the neural network spaces satisfy abstract approximation properties as follows: Given a function
w\in W^{s,p}(\Omega)
with
1\leq p\leq\infty
, we now describe the approximation properties of neural network spaces. For each
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
, we associate a neural network space
V_{\ell}\subset V_{\mathcal{N}}
such that for
m\geq s+1
, there exists
w_{\ell}\in V_{\ell}
satisfying

(2.18)

\|w_{\ell}-w\|_{W^{s,p}(\Omega)}\leq\tilde{\beta}_{\ell}^{[m,s,p]}\,|w|_{W^{m,p}(\Omega)},\quad\text{with}\quad\tilde{\beta}_{\ell}^{[m,s,p]}\to 0\quad\text{as }\ell\to\infty.

These bounds may hold only for
w=u,

u
being the exact solution of the PDE. These assumptions are compatible with approximation results for neural networks which can be found in
[
55
]
,
[
15
]
,
[
31
]
,
[

]
,
[

]
,
[

]
, and references therein.

2.3.
Motivation of the method

We seek functions
u_{\theta}\in V_{\mathcal{N}}
that approximate minimisers of the energy defined in (
1.2
). When
u_{\theta}\in H^{2}(\Omega)
, we may employ a conforming finite element space such that
I_{{\mathcal{S}}^{q}_{h}}u_{\theta}\in C^{1}(\Omega)
. This allows us to directly apply the training approach introduced in
[
29
]
, where
u_{\theta}
is substituted by
I_{{\mathcal{S}}^{q}_{h}}u_{\theta}
in the energy functional (
1.2
). However, it is well known that constructing such regular finite element spaces is complicated, especially in three dimensions.

For implementation simplicity, we instead choose
C^{0}(\Omega)
elements, so that
I_{{\mathcal{S}}^{q}_{h}}u_{\theta}\in H^{1}(\Omega)
, and continuity across element interfaces is imposed weakly through the discontinuous Galerkin framework.

To motivate the design of the method, consider the simplified setting where the energy functional has the form

(2.19)

\mathcal{E}(u)=\int_{\Omega}g(\varDelta u),

for some function
g:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, and where
\mathcal{E}
attains a minimum over a Banach space
X
. Let
w
denote a minimiser. Then, for all
z\in X
,

(2.20)

\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\mathcal{E}(w+\varepsilon z)=\int_{\Omega}g^{\prime}(\varDelta w)\varDelta z=0.

For further simplification, let us assume that

X=\left\{\hat{p}+v:\hat{p}\in\mathbb{P}_{2}(\Omega),\;v\in H^{2}(\Omega)\text{ periodic}\right\},

where
\mathbb{P}_{2}(\Omega)
denotes the space of degree-two polynomials on
\Omega
. Note that any
\hat{p}\in\mathbb{P}_{2}(\Omega)
is a critical point satisfying (
2.20
). Indeed, for any periodic
v\in H^{2}(\Omega)
,

(2.21)

\int_{\Omega}g^{\prime}(\varDelta\hat{p})\varDelta v=g^{\prime}(\varDelta\hat{p})\int_{\partial\Omega}v_{,i}n_{i}=0,

using integration by parts and the summation convention.

We aim for our numerical method to retain this key property, even when test functions have lower regularity. To illustrate the issue, let
\Omega=[0,1]\times[0,1]
and divide it into two triangles
K^{+}
and
K^{-}
sharing a common edge
e=K^{+}\cap K^{-}
. If a test function
v
is allowed to be discontinuous at the interface
e
, then

(2.22)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\mathcal{E}(\hat{p}+\varepsilon v)

\displaystyle=\int_{K^{+}}g^{\prime}(\varDelta\hat{p})\varDelta v+\int_{K^{-}}g^{\prime}(\varDelta\hat{p})\varDelta v

\displaystyle=g^{\prime}(\varDelta\hat{p})\int_{\partial K^{+}}v_{,i}n^{+}_{i}+g^{\prime}(\varDelta\hat{p})\int_{\partial K^{-}}v_{,i}n^{-}_{i}

\displaystyle=g^{\prime}(\varDelta\hat{p})\left(\int_{\partial\Omega}v_{,i}n_{i}+\int_{e}v^{+}_{,i}n^{+}_{i}+v^{-}_{,i}n^{-}_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=g^{\prime}(\varDelta\hat{p})\int_{e}\llbracket\nabla v\cdot n\rrbracket,

where
v^{+}_{,i},v^{-}_{,i}
denote the restrictions of
v
to
K^{+}
and
K^{-}
, and
n^{+},n^{-}
are the outward unit normals on
\partial K^{+}
and
\partial K^{-}
. The final expression shows that the fundamental property (
2.21
) is violated due to the discontinuity across
e
.

Motivated by the analysis in
[
37
]
, we modify the energy functional to

(2.23)

\mathcal{E}_{\text{dg}}(u)=\int_{K^{+}}g(\varDelta u)\,dx+\int_{K^{-}}g(\varDelta u)\,dx-\int_{e}\{\kern-3.125pt\{g^{\prime}(\varDelta u)\}\kern-3.125pt\}\llbracket\nabla u\cdot n\rrbracket\,ds.

This discontinuous Galerkin energy functional is now consistent in the sense that

\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\mathcal{E}_{\text{dg}}(\hat{p}+\varepsilon v)=0

holds for any periodic test function
v
that may be discontinuous across the internal edge
e
.

3.
Method formulation and convergence analysis

3.1.
The Discrete Energy Functional

Following the discussion in Section
2.3
, we define the discrete energy functional for
u_{\ell}\in V_{\ell}
as

(3.1)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{h}(u_{\ell})

\displaystyle=\sum_{K\in T_{h}}\int_{K}\left|\varDelta I_{{\mathcal{S}}^{q}_{h}}u_{\ell}+I_{{\mathcal{S}}^{q}_{h}}f\right|^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle-2\sum_{e\in E_{h}^{i}}\int_{e}\{\kern-3.125pt\{\varDelta I_{{\mathcal{S}}^{q}_{h}}u_{\ell}+I_{{\mathcal{S}}^{q}_{h}}f\}\kern-3.125pt\}\llbracket\nabla I_{{\mathcal{S}}^{q}_{h}}u_{\ell}\cdot n\rrbracket

\displaystyle+\alpha\,\mathrm{pen}(u_{\ell}).

As is standard in the Discontinuous Galerkin setting, the term
\alpha\,\mathrm{pen}(u_{\ell})
ensures coercivity. It penalises discontinuities across element interfaces and imposes boundary conditions in the spirit of Nitsche’s method
[
44
]
. Recent DG schemes for nonconvex variational problems
[
26
,
28
]
have proposed general penalty terms; however, due to the convexity of the integrand here, we adopt a simpler form:

(3.2)

\mathrm{pen}(u_{\ell})=\sum_{e\in E_{h}^{i}}\frac{1}{h_{e}}\int_{e}\left|\llbracket\nabla I_{{\mathcal{S}}^{q}_{h}}u_{\ell}\rrbracket\right|^{2}+\sum_{e\in E_{h}^{b}}\frac{1}{h_{e}}\int_{e}\left|I_{{\mathcal{S}}^{q}_{h}}(u_{\ell}-g)\right|^{2},

where
g
denotes the Dirichlet boundary condition in the original problem (
1
). In what follows, we assume
g=0
to simplify the treatment of boundary terms and focus on the core methodology.

An equivalent formulation of (
3.1
) may be obtained using a lifting operator, leading to a natural definition of a discrete Laplacian. The
L^{p}
-boundedness of the lifting operator in terms of the penalty ensures coercivity, and convergence can be proved via
\liminf
and
\limsup
arguments, as in
[
27
]
. For simplicity, we adopt this lifting-based formulation.

Let
R_{h}(\nabla u_{h}):T(E_{h})\to\tilde{{\mathcal{S}}}^{q}_{h}(\Omega)
be the lifting operator, defined as in
[
54
]
by

(3.3)

\int_{\Omega}R_{h}(\nabla u_{h})\,w_{h}=\sum_{e\in E_{h}^{i}}\int_{e}\{\kern-3.125pt\{w_{h}\}\kern-3.125pt\}\llbracket\nabla u_{h}\cdot n\rrbracket,\quad\forall w_{h}\in\tilde{{\mathcal{S}}}^{q}_{h}(\Omega),\;u_{h}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{q}_{h}(\Omega).

Observe that for
u_{h,\ell}=I_{{\mathcal{S}}^{q}_{h}}u_{\ell}
and
f_{h}=I_{{\mathcal{S}}^{q}_{h}}f
, since
\varDelta u_{h,\ell}+f_{h}\in\tilde{{\mathcal{S}}}^{q}_{h}(\Omega)
, we have

(3.4)

\int_{\Omega}R_{h}(\nabla u_{h,\ell})\,(\varDelta u_{h,\ell}+f_{h})=\sum_{e\in E_{h}^{i}}\int_{e}\{\kern-3.125pt\{\varDelta u_{h,\ell}+f_{h}\}\kern-3.125pt\}\llbracket\nabla u_{h,\ell}\cdot n\rrbracket.

This motivates the definition of the discrete Laplacian
L_{h}:{\mathcal{S}}^{q}_{h}(\Omega)\to L^{2}(\Omega)
via

(3.5)

L_{h}(u_{h})=\varDelta_{h}u_{h}-R_{h}(\nabla u_{h}),

where the piecewise Laplacian is defined elementwise by

{\left.\kern-1.2pt\varDelta_{h}u_{h}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{K}}=\varDelta\left({\left.\kern-1.2ptu_{h}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{K}}\right),\quad\forall K\in T_{h}.

This construction is analogous to the discrete gradient framework in
[
13
,
18
,
46
]
. Note that while
\varDelta u_{h}
is not globally defined in
L^{2}(\Omega)
, it belongs to
L^{2}(K)
locally, and
L_{h}(u_{h})\in L^{2}(\Omega)
via the lifting.

We may now rewrite the discrete energy (
3.1
) as

(3.6)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{h}(u_{\ell})

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left|\varDelta u_{h,\ell}+f_{h}\right|^{2}-2\int_{\Omega}R_{h}(\nabla u_{h,\ell})\,(\varDelta u_{h,\ell}+f_{h})+\alpha\,\mathrm{pen}(u_{\ell})

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left|L_{h}(u_{h,\ell})+f_{h}\right|^{2}-\left|R_{h}(\nabla u_{h,\ell})\right|^{2}+\alpha\,\mathrm{pen}(u_{\ell}).

To simplify notation, for each
\ell
we associate a finite element space
{\mathcal{S}}^{q}_{h(\ell)}
with mesh diameter
h(\ell)
such that
h(\ell)\to 0
as
\ell\to\infty
. We then write

(3.7)

\mathcal{E}_{\ell}(u_{\ell}):=\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{h}(u_{\ell}).

In the remainder of the analysis, we assume that the finite element spaces consist of piecewise polynomials of degree at least two.

3.2.
Properties of the lifting operator and discrete Laplacian

We recall well-known results on the boundedness of the lifting operator (see
[
12
,
13
,
18
]
) and its consequences for the discrete Laplacian.

Specifically, we use the estimate given in
[
13
, Lemma 7]
and
[
19
, Lemma 4.34]
:

(3.8)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left|R_{h}(\nabla v_{h})\right|^{2}\leq C_{R}\sum_{e\in E_{h}^{i}}\frac{1}{h_{e}}\int_{e}\left|\llbracket\nabla v_{h}\rrbracket\right|^{2},\quad\forall v_{h}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{q}_{h}(\Omega),

where
C_{R}
is a constant independent of
h
.

This bound implies the following estimate for the discrete Laplacian:

(3.9)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left|L_{h}(u_{h})\right|^{2}\lesssim\sum_{K\in T_{h}}\int_{K}\left|\varDelta u_{h}\right|^{2}+\sum_{e\in E_{h}^{i}}\frac{1}{h_{e}}\int_{e}\left|\llbracket\nabla u_{h}\rrbracket\right|^{2},\quad\forall u_{h}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{q}_{h}(\Omega).

We now state a compactness result, adapted from the proofs of
[
18
, Theorem 2.2]
and
[
27
, Lemma 5.2]
:

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
u_{h}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{q}_{h}(\Omega)
with
u_{h}\rightharpoonup u
in
H^{1}(\Omega)
, where
u\in V(\Omega)
. If

(3.10)

\displaystyle\sum_{K\in T_{h}}\int_{K}\left|\varDelta u_{h}\right|^{2}+\sum_{e\in E_{h}^{i}}\frac{1}{h_{e}}\int_{e}\left|\llbracket\nabla u_{h}\rrbracket\right|^{2}<C,

uniformly with respect to
h
, then

(3.11)

\displaystyle L_{h}(u_{h})\rightharpoonup\varDelta u\quad\text{in }L^{2}(\Omega).

Proof.

Let
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
. By the definition of the discrete Laplacian in (
3.5
), we have

(3.12)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}L_{h}(u_{h})\,\phi

\displaystyle=\sum_{K\in T_{h}}\int_{K}\varDelta u_{h}\,\phi-\int_{\Omega}R_{h}(\nabla u_{h})\,\phi

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}\nabla u_{h}\cdot\nabla\phi+\sum_{e\in E_{h}^{i}}\int_{e}\phi\,\llbracket\nabla u_{h}\cdot n\rrbracket-\int_{\Omega}R_{h}(\nabla u_{h})\,\phi.

Our goal is to show that the last two terms vanish as
h\to 0
. Let
\phi_{h}=I_{\tilde{{\mathcal{S}}}^{q}_{h}(\Omega)}\phi
be the Lagrange interpolant of
\phi
. Using the error estimates (
2.6
) and (
2.7
), the lifting bound (
3.8
), and the uniform bound (
3.10
), we estimate:

(3.13)

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{e\in E_{h}^{i}}\int_{e}\phi\,\llbracket\nabla u_{h}\cdot n\rrbracket-\int_{\Omega}R_{h}(\nabla u_{h})\,\phi\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{e\in E_{h}^{i}}\int_{e}\phi_{h}\,\llbracket\nabla u_{h}\cdot n\rrbracket+\int_{e}(\phi-\phi_{h})\llbracket\nabla u_{h}\cdot n\rrbracket-\int_{\Omega}R_{h}(\nabla u_{h})\,\phi_{h}-\int_{\Omega}R_{h}(\nabla u_{h})\,(\phi-\phi_{h})\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{e\in E_{h}^{i}}\int_{e}\left|\phi-\phi_{h}\right|\left|\llbracket\nabla u_{h}\cdot n\rrbracket\right|+\int_{\Omega}\left|R_{h}(\nabla u_{h})\right|\left|\phi-\phi_{h}\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim h\,|\phi|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\left(\sum_{e\in E_{h}^{i}}\frac{1}{h_{e}}\int_{e}\left|\llbracket\nabla u_{h}\rrbracket\right|^{2}\right)^{1/2}+h\,|\phi|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\lesssim h\,|\phi|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\to 0,

as
h\to 0
.

Therefore,

(3.14)

\displaystyle\lim_{h\to 0}\int_{\Omega}L_{h}(u_{h})\,\phi=-\lim_{h\to 0}\int_{\Omega}\nabla u_{h}\cdot\nabla\phi=\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\cdot\nabla\phi,

since
u_{h}\rightharpoonup u
in
H^{1}(\Omega)
. Hence
L_{h}(u_{h})\rightharpoonup\varDelta u
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
.
∎

3.3.
Stability and convergence

We begin with the stability of the method, formulated precisely below.

Proposition 3.1

(Equicoercivity)
.

Let
\mathcal{E}_{\ell}
be defined as in (
3.7
). Suppose
(v_{\ell})
is a sequence in
V_{\ell}
satisfying

(3.15)

\mathcal{E}_{\ell}(v_{\ell})\leq C,

for some constant
C>0
independent of
\ell
. Then there exists a constant
C_{1}>0
such that for sufficiently large penalty parameter
\alpha
, we have

(3.16)

\sum_{K\in T_{h(\ell)}}\int_{K}|\varDelta\widehat{v}_{\ell}|^{2}+\|\widehat{v}_{\ell}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}+\operatorname{pen}(v_{\ell})\leq C_{1},

where
\widehat{v}_{\ell}:=I_{{\mathcal{S}}^{q}_{h(\ell)}}v_{\ell}
and
f_{\ell}:=I_{{\mathcal{S}}^{q}_{h(\ell)}}f
.

Proof.

We first bound the consistency term from below using Cauchy–Schwarz and an inverse inequality:

(3.17)

\displaystyle-2\sum_{e\in E^{i}_{h(\ell)}}\int_{e}\{\kern-3.125pt\{\varDelta\widehat{v}_{\ell}+f_{\ell}\}\kern-3.125pt\}\llbracket\nabla\widehat{v}_{\ell}\cdot n\rrbracket\geq

\displaystyle-c_{1}\left(\sum_{K\in T_{h(\ell)}}\int_{K}|\varDelta\widehat{v}_{\ell}+f_{\ell}|^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\times\left(\sum_{e\in E^{i}_{h(\ell)}}\frac{1}{h_{e}}\int_{e}|\llbracket\nabla\widehat{v}_{\ell}\rrbracket|^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle-\frac{c_{1}\delta}{2}\sum_{K\in T_{h(\ell)}}\int_{K}|\varDelta\widehat{v}_{\ell}+f_{\ell}|^{2}-\frac{c_{1}}{2\delta}\sum_{e\in E^{i}_{h(\ell)}}\frac{1}{h_{e}}\int_{e}|\llbracket\nabla\widehat{v}_{\ell}\rrbracket|^{2},

for any
\delta\in(0,1)
and a constant
c_{1}>0
. Choosing
\delta
and
\alpha
such that
1-\frac{c_{1}\delta}{2}>0
and
\alpha-\frac{c_{1}}{2\delta}>0
, we obtain

(3.18)

\mathcal{E}_{\ell}(v_{\ell})\geq\left(1-\frac{c_{1}\delta}{2}\right)\sum_{K\in T_{h(\ell)}}\int_{K}|\varDelta\widehat{v}_{\ell}+f_{\ell}|^{2}+\left(\alpha-\frac{c_{1}}{2\delta}\right)\operatorname{pen}(v_{\ell})\geq 0.

Hence, the bound (
3.15
) implies uniform control over
\int_{K}|\varDelta\widehat{v}_{\ell}|^{2}
and
\operatorname{pen}(v_{\ell})
.

To estimate
\|\widehat{v}_{\ell}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}
, we integrate by parts elementwise:

(3.19)

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\widehat{v}_{\ell}|^{2}=\sum_{e\in E_{h(\ell)}}\int_{e}\widehat{v}_{\ell}\llbracket\nabla\widehat{v}_{\ell}\cdot n\rrbracket+\sum_{K\in T_{h(\ell)}}\int_{K}\widehat{v}_{\ell}\varDelta\widehat{v}_{\ell}.

For the boundary terms, observe that since the boundary conditions are weakly imposed and
g=0
, we have
\|\widehat{v}_{\ell}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}\lesssim h
. Using Cauchy–Schwarz and inverse estimates:

(3.20)

\sum_{e\in E^{b}_{h(\ell)}}\int_{e}|\widehat{v}_{\ell}\nabla\widehat{v}_{\ell}\cdot n|\lesssim\sum_{e\in E^{b}_{h(\ell)}}\|\widehat{v}_{\ell}\|_{L^{2}(e)}\|\nabla\widehat{v}_{\ell}\|_{L^{2}(e)}\lesssim\sum_{e\in E^{b}_{h(\ell)}}\frac{1}{h_{e}}\|\widehat{v}_{\ell}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(e)}\leq\operatorname{pen}(v_{\ell}).

For the remaining terms in (
3.19
), we use the Cauchy–Schwarz and Young inequalities with a trace–Poincaré inequality (see
[
9
, Lemma 10.2.20]
):

(3.21)

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{e\in E^{i}_{h(\ell)}}\int_{e}\widehat{v}_{\ell}\llbracket\nabla\widehat{v}_{\ell}\cdot n\rrbracket+\sum_{K\in T_{h(\ell)}}\int_{K}\widehat{v}_{\ell}\varDelta\widehat{v}_{\ell}\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\widehat{v}_{\ell}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\left[\left(\sum_{e\in E^{i}_{h(\ell)}}\frac{1}{h_{e}}\int_{e}|\llbracket\nabla\widehat{v}_{\ell}\rrbracket|^{2}\right)^{1/2}+\left(\sum_{K\in T_{h(\ell)}}\int_{K}|\varDelta\widehat{v}_{\ell}|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\widehat{v}_{\ell}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\lesssim\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\|\nabla\widehat{v}_{\ell}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\|\widehat{v}_{\ell}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}+\frac{C}{2\varepsilon},

for arbitrary
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. Since
\|\widehat{v}_{\ell}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}
is uniformly bounded (via the penalty), we conclude by choosing
\varepsilon
small enough to absorb the
H^{1}
term into the left-hand side.
∎

3.4.
Convergence of the Discrete Minimisers

In this section, we prove that minimisers of the discrete functional
\mathcal{E}_{\ell}
converge (up to interpolation) to the unique minimiser of the continuous functional
\mathcal{E}
, as the discretisation parameter
\ell\to\infty
.

The key analytical tool is
\Gamma
-convergence, specifically the standard
\liminf
–
\limsup
framework. We show that the interpolated discrete minimisers
I_{{\mathcal{S}}^{q}_{h(\ell)}}u_{\ell}
converge weakly in
H^{1}(\Omega)
to the exact solution
u
of the continuous variational problem, provided the approximation parameters are scaled appropriately.

Theorem 3.1

(Convergence of discrete minimisers)
.

Let
\mathcal{E}
and
\mathcal{E}_{\ell}
be the energy functionals defined in (
1.2
) and (
3.7
), respectively, and let
f\in C^{0}(\bar{\Omega})
. Let
u_{\ell}\in V_{\ell}
be a minimiser of
\mathcal{E}_{\ell}
, and define the interpolated function
\widehat{u}_{\ell}:=I_{{\mathcal{S}}^{q}_{h(\ell)}}u_{\ell}
.

Let

\beta_{\ell}:=\max\left\{\tilde{\beta}^{[1,0,2]}_{\ell},\,\tilde{\beta}^{[2,1,2]}_{\ell},\,\tilde{\beta}^{[3,2,2]}_{\ell},\,\tilde{\beta}^{[4,2,2]}_{\ell}\right\},

and suppose that the finite element parameters satisfy
h(\ell)=c\beta_{\ell}
for some
c>0
, and further that

\underline{h}_{E,\ell}^{-1/2}\left(\tilde{\beta}^{[2,0,\infty]}_{\ell}\right)^{1-2\epsilon}\leq C

for all
\ell
, where
\underline{h}_{E,\ell}:=\min_{e\in E^{b}_{h(\ell)}}h_{e}
.

Then, as
\ell\to\infty
,

(3.22)

\widehat{u}_{\ell}\rightharpoonup u\quad\text{in }H^{1}(\Omega),\quad\text{and}\quad\widehat{u}_{\ell}\to u\quad\text{in }L^{2}(\Omega),

where
u
is the unique minimiser of the continuous energy
\mathcal{E}
, and in particular satisfies
u\in V(\Omega)=\{v\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\,:\,\Delta v\in L^{2}(\Omega)\}
.

The proof of Theorem
3.1
follows the standard strategy of
\Gamma
-convergence, and is structured around the following Lemma
3.2
, showing lower semicontinuity and a
\liminf
inequality, and Lemma
3.3
giving a proof of the
\limsup
inequality.

Lemma 3.2

(Lower semicontinuity and
\liminf
inequality)
.

Let
v\in V(\Omega)
and suppose that a sequence
(v_{\ell})
with
v_{\ell}\in V_{\ell}
satisfies

\widehat{v}_{\ell}:=I_{{\mathcal{S}}^{q}_{h(\ell)}}v_{\ell}\rightharpoonup v\quad\text{in }H^{1}(\Omega).

Then

(3.23)

\mathcal{E}(v)\leq\liminf_{\ell\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{E}_{\ell}(v_{\ell}).

Proof.

Assume there exists a subsequence, still denoted by
v_{\ell}
, such that
\mathcal{E}_{\ell}(v_{\ell})\leq C
uniformly in
\ell
; otherwise the inequality holds trivially. Then the stability result of Proposition
3.1
implies the uniform bounds of (
3.16
).

These bounds, together with
\widehat{v}_{\ell}\rightharpoonup v
in
H^{1}(\Omega)
, imply that

L_{h(\ell)}(\widehat{v}_{\ell})\rightharpoonup\Delta v,\quad\text{in }L^{2}(\Omega),

by Lemma
3.1
. Since
f_{\ell}\to f
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
, we also have

L_{h(\ell)}(\widehat{v}_{\ell})+f_{\ell}\rightharpoonup\Delta v+f.

By the weak lower semicontinuity of the
L^{2}
norm,

\liminf_{\ell\to\infty}\|L_{h(\ell)}(\widehat{v}_{\ell})+f_{\ell}\|^{2}\geq\int_{\Omega}|\Delta v+f|^{2}=\mathcal{E}(v).

To complete the argument, we estimate the full energy:

(3.24)

\displaystyle\liminf_{\ell\to\infty}\mathcal{E}_{\ell}(v_{\ell})

\displaystyle=\liminf_{\ell\to\infty}\left[\|L_{h(\ell)}(\widehat{v}_{\ell})+f_{\ell}\|^{2}+|R_{h}(\nabla\widehat{v}_{\ell})|^{2}+\alpha\,\text{pen}(\widehat{v}_{\ell})\right]

\displaystyle\geq\liminf_{\ell\to\infty}\left[\|L_{h(\ell)}(\widehat{v}_{\ell})+f_{\ell}\|^{2}+(\alpha-C_{R})\,\text{pen}(\widehat{v}_{\ell})\right]

\displaystyle\geq\mathcal{E}(v),

provided that
\alpha>C_{R}
.
∎

Lemma 3.3

(
\limsup
inequality via recovery sequence)
.

For every
w\in V(\Omega)
, there exists a sequence
(w_{\ell})
with
w_{\ell}\in V_{\ell}
such that

\mathcal{E}(w)=\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\mathcal{E}_{\ell}(w_{\ell}).

Proof.

Let
w\in V(\Omega)
be arbitrary. For each
\delta>0
, choose a smooth function
w_{\delta}\in C^{m}(\overline{\Omega})
,
m\geq 4
, with
{\left.\kern-1.2ptw_{\delta}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\partial\Omega}}=0
, see Remark
3.1
, such that

(3.25)

\|w-w_{\delta}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}+\|\Delta w-\Delta w_{\delta}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\lesssim\delta,\quad|w_{\delta}|_{H^{s}(\Omega)}\lesssim\delta^{1-s}.

Then for each
\ell
, there exists
w_{\ell,\delta}\in V_{\ell}\cap H^{3}(\Omega)
such that

(3.26)

\|w_{\ell,\delta}-w_{\delta}\|_{H^{s-1}(\Omega)}\leq\tilde{\beta}_{\ell}^{[s,s-1,2]}\,|w_{\delta}|_{H^{s}(\Omega)}\leq\tilde{\beta}_{\ell}^{[s,s-1,2]}\delta^{1-s},

and
\tilde{\beta}_{\ell}^{[s,s-1,2]}\to 0
as
\ell\to\infty
for all
s\leq 4
.

Let
\widehat{w}_{\ell,\delta}:=I_{{\mathcal{S}}^{q}_{h(\ell)}}w_{\ell,\delta}
. We aim to show

(3.27)

\big|\|\Delta w_{\ell,\delta}+f_{\ell}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}-\mathcal{E}_{\ell}(w_{\ell,\delta})\big|\to 0,\qquad\big|\mathcal{E}(w)-\|\Delta w_{\ell,\delta}+f_{\ell}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\big|\to 0,

as
\ell\to\infty
. This proves the
\limsup
inequality with
w_{\ell}:=w_{\ell,\delta}
.

Using (
3.26
) and the approximation bounds from (
2.6
), we obtain:

(3.28)

\|\Delta w_{\ell,\delta}+f_{\ell}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\lesssim\frac{\tilde{\beta}^{[3,2,2]}}{\delta^{2}}+\frac{1}{\delta}+1.

We then estimate the difference using
||a|-|b||\leq|a-b|

(3.29)

\displaystyle\big|\|\Delta w_{\ell,\delta}+f_{\ell}\|_{L^{2}(K)}^{2}-\|\Delta\widehat{w}_{\ell,\delta}+f_{\ell}\|_{L^{2}(K)}^{2}\big|

\displaystyle=\big|\|\Delta w_{\ell,\delta}+f_{\ell}\|_{L^{2}(K)}-\|\Delta\widehat{w}_{\ell,\delta}+f_{\ell}\|_{L^{2}(K)}\big|

\displaystyle\times\big(\|\Delta w_{\ell,\delta}+f_{\ell}\|_{L^{2}(K)}+\|\Delta\widehat{w}_{\ell,\delta}+f_{\ell}\|_{L^{2}(K)}\big)

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\Delta w_{\ell,\delta}-\Delta\widehat{w}_{\ell,\delta}\|_{L^{2}(K)}\big(\|\Delta w_{\ell,\delta}+f_{\ell}\|_{L^{2}(K)}+\|\Delta w_{\ell,\delta}-\Delta\widehat{w}_{\ell,\delta}\|_{L^{2}(K)}\big)

\displaystyle\lesssim h(\ell)\,|w_{\ell,\delta}|_{H^{3}(K)}\cdot\|\Delta w_{\ell,\delta}+f_{\ell}\|_{L^{2}(K)}+h(\ell)^{2}\,|w_{\ell,\delta}|_{H^{3}(K)}^{2}.

Summing over
K\in T_{h}(\ell)
, and using the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, (
3.26
), and (
3.28
), we obtain the upper bound

(3.30)

\displaystyle\bigg|\sum_{K\in T_{h(\ell)}}\Big(

\displaystyle\|\Delta w_{\ell,\delta}+f_{\ell}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(K)}-\|\Delta\widehat{w}_{\ell,\delta}+f_{\ell}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(K)}\Big)\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{K\in T_{h(\ell)}}\big|\|\Delta w_{\ell,\delta}+f_{\ell}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(K)}-\|\Delta\widehat{w}_{\ell,\delta}+f_{\ell}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(K)}\big|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{K\in T_{h(\ell)}}h(\ell)|w_{\ell,\delta}|_{H^{3}(K)}\|\Delta w_{\ell,\delta}+f_{\ell}\|_{L^{2}(K)}+h^{2}(\ell)|w_{\ell,\delta}|_{H^{3}(\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq h(\ell)|w_{\ell,\delta}|_{H^{3}(\Omega)}\|\Delta w_{\ell,\delta}+f_{\ell}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+h^{2}(\ell)|w_{\ell,\delta}|_{H^{3}(\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{h(\ell)}{\delta^{3}}\left[\left(\tilde{\beta}_{\ell}^{[4,3,2]}+\delta\right)\left(\frac{\tilde{\beta}_{\ell}^{[3,2,2]}}{\delta^{2}}+\frac{1}{\delta}+1\right)+\frac{h(\ell)}{\delta^{3}}\left(\left(\tilde{\beta}_{\ell}^{[4,3,2]}\right)^{2}+\delta^{2}\right)\right].

We now fix
h(\ell)=\beta_{\ell}
and choose
\delta=h(\ell)^{1/4}
. With this choice, the estimate (
3.30
) tends to zero as
\ell\to\infty
.

It remains to show that the remaining terms of
\mathcal{E}_{\ell}(w_{\ell,\delta})
, namely the consistency and penalty terms in (
3.1
), also vanish as
\ell\to\infty
.

Consistency term.
Using (
2.9
) and (
3.26
), we estimate:

(3.31)

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{e\in E_{h}^{i}}\int_{e}\{\kern-3.125pt\{\Delta\widehat{w}_{\ell,\delta}+f_{\ell}\}\kern-3.125pt\}\,\llbracket\nabla\widehat{w}_{\ell,\delta}\cdot n\rrbracket\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim h(\ell)\left(|w_{\ell,\delta}|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}+1\right)|w_{\ell,\delta}|_{H^{3}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\lesssim h(\ell)\left(|w_{\ell,\delta}-w_{\delta}|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}+|w_{\delta}|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}+1\right)

\displaystyle\times\left(|w_{\ell,\delta}-w_{\delta}|_{H^{3}(\Omega)}+|w_{\delta}|_{H^{3}(\Omega)}\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim h(\ell)\left(\frac{\tilde{\beta}_{\ell}^{[3,2,2]}}{\delta^{2}}+\frac{1}{\delta}+1\right)\left(\frac{\tilde{\beta}_{\ell}^{[4,3,2]}}{\delta^{3}}+\frac{1}{\delta^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\to 0\quad\text{as }\ell\to\infty.

Penalty term.
Using (
2.8
) and (
3.26
), we estimate:

(3.32)

\displaystyle\sum_{e\in E^{i}_{h}}\frac{1}{h_{e}}\int_{e}\left|\llbracket\nabla\widehat{w}_{\ell,\delta}\rrbracket\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim h(\ell)^{2}\,|w_{\ell,\delta}|_{H^{3}(\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{h(\ell)^{2}}{\delta^{4}}\left(\frac{\left(\tilde{\beta}_{\ell}^{[4,3,2]}\right)^{2}}{\delta^{2}}+1\right)

\displaystyle=h(\ell)+h(\ell)^{3/4}\to 0\quad\text{as }\ell\to\infty.

Boundary penalty terms.
Since
w_{\delta}=0
on
\partial\Omega
, we use (
2.7
) and (
3.26
) to estimate:

(3.33)

\displaystyle\sum_{e\in E^{b}_{h(\ell)}}\frac{1}{h_{e}}\|\widehat{w}_{\ell,\delta}\|_{L^{2}(e)}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{e\in E^{b}_{h(\ell)}}\frac{1}{h_{e}}\left(\|\widehat{w}_{\ell,\delta}-w_{\ell,\delta}\|_{L^{2}(e)}^{2}+\|w_{\ell,\delta}-w_{\delta}\|_{L^{2}(e)}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim h(\ell)^{2}|w_{\ell,\delta}|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\frac{1}{h(\ell)}\|w_{\ell,\delta}-w_{\delta}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\cdot|w_{\ell,\delta}-w_{\delta}|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}.

The first term vanishes as
\ell\to\infty
:

(3.34)

\displaystyle h(\ell)^{2}|w_{\ell,\delta}|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim h(\ell)^{2}\left(|w_{\ell,\delta}-w_{\delta}|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+|w_{\delta}|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim h(\ell)^{2}\left(\left(\tilde{\beta}_{\ell}^{[3,2,2]}\right)^{2}|w_{\delta}|_{H^{3}(\Omega)}^{2}+\frac{1}{\delta^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim h(\ell)^{2}\left(\frac{\left(\tilde{\beta}_{\ell}^{[3,2,2]}\right)^{2}}{\delta^{4}}+\frac{1}{\delta^{2}}\right).

For the second term in (
3.33
), we estimate:

(3.35)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{h(\ell)}\|w_{\ell,\delta}-w_{\delta}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\cdot|w_{\ell,\delta}-w_{\delta}|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\tilde{\beta}_{\ell}^{[1,0,2]}\tilde{\beta}_{\ell}^{[2,1,2]}}{h(\ell)}\|w_{\delta}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\|w_{\delta}\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\beta_{\ell}^{2}}{\delta h(\ell)}(\delta+1)=h(\ell)^{3/4}(h(\ell)^{1/4}+1)\to 0,

completing the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Since
u_{\ell}
is a minimiser of
\mathcal{E}_{\ell}
, we have for any
v_{\ell}\in V_{\ell}
,

\mathcal{E}_{\ell}(u_{\ell})\leq\mathcal{E}_{\ell}(v_{\ell}).

In particular, taking
v_{\ell}=\tilde{u}_{\ell}
, the recovery sequence associated with
w=u
, we obtain

\mathcal{E}_{\ell}(u_{\ell})\leq\mathcal{E}_{\ell}(\tilde{u}_{\ell}).

By construction,
\mathcal{E}_{\ell}(\tilde{u}_{\ell})\to\mathcal{E}(u)
, and hence the sequence
(\mathcal{E}_{\ell}(u_{\ell}))
is bounded. The stability result of Proposition
3.1
then implies the uniform bound

(3.36)

\sum_{K\in T_{h(\ell)}}\|\Delta\widehat{u}_{\ell}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(K)}+\|\widehat{u}_{\ell}\|^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega)}+\mathrm{j}_{\ell}(\tilde{u}_{\ell})+\mathrm{b}_{\ell}(\tilde{u}_{\ell})\leq C.

By reflexivity and the Banach–Alaoglu theorem, there exists
\tilde{u}\in H^{1}(\Omega)
such that (up to a subsequence)

\widehat{u}_{\ell}\rightharpoonup\tilde{u}\quad\text{in }H^{1}(\Omega).

Moreover, Lemma
3.1
together with (
3.9
) implies that

L_{h(\ell)}(\widehat{u}_{\ell})\rightharpoonup\Delta\tilde{u}\quad\text{in }L^{2}(\Omega),

so
\Delta\tilde{u}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
. It remains to show that
\tilde{u}=u
.

From the bound on the penalty term
\operatorname{pen}(u_{\ell})
, we have

\displaystyle\|\tilde{u}\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq\|\tilde{u}-\widehat{u}_{\ell}\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}+\|\widehat{u}_{\ell}\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\tilde{u}-\widehat{u}_{\ell}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|\tilde{u}-\widehat{u}_{\ell}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}+h(\ell)\to 0,

as
\ell\to\infty
, using that weak convergence in
H^{1}(\Omega)
implies strong convergence in
L^{2}(\Omega)
, cf.
[
11
, Thm. 9.16]
. Thus,
\tilde{u}\in V(\Omega)
.

Now let
w\in V(\Omega)
be arbitrary, and
w_{\ell}\in V_{\ell}
its recovery sequence. Then

\mathcal{E}(\tilde{u})\leq\liminf_{\ell\to\infty}\mathcal{E}_{\ell}(u_{\ell})\leq\limsup_{\ell\to\infty}\mathcal{E}_{\ell}(u_{\ell})\leq\limsup_{\ell\to\infty}\mathcal{E}_{\ell}(w_{\ell})=\mathcal{E}(w).

Since
w
was arbitrary, we conclude that
\tilde{u}
is a minimiser of
\mathcal{E}
, and hence
\tilde{u}=u
by uniqueness. As all subsequences converge to
u
, the full sequence satisfies

\widehat{u}_{\ell}\rightharpoonup u\quad\text{in }H^{1}(\Omega),\quad\text{and}\quad\widehat{u}_{\ell}\to u\quad\text{in }L^{2}(\Omega).

∎

Remark 3.1

(Approximation by smooth functions)
.

Let
w\in V(\Omega)
be arbitrary. By the elliptic regularity estimates we know that
w\in H^{2}(\Omega).
For each
\delta>0
, standard density results imply the existence of a smooth function
w_{\delta}\in C^{m}(\overline{\Omega})
,
m\geq 4
, such that

(3.37)

\|w-w_{\delta}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}+\|\Delta w-\Delta w_{\delta}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\lesssim\delta.

To see why this function can be chosen so that
{\left.\kern-1.2ptw_{\delta}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\partial\Omega}}=0
, one has to follow the proof of
Theorem 3 in
[
22
, Section 5.3]
and use as mollifiers on the boundary convolutions with odd extension
\bar{w}(x)=w(x)
if
x\in B^{+}
and
\bar{w}(x)=-w(x_{1},\dots,-x_{d})
if
x\in B^{-}
where we follow the notation of
[
22
, Theorem 1, Section 5.4]
. Then using the fact that
w=0
on the boundary the arguments of
[
22
, Theorem 1, Section 5.4]
show that

\bar{w}
is smooth enough, odd with respect to
x_{d}
and zero on the local boundary
x_{d}=0.
It then follows that the corresponding mollifier is zero on the boundary. The property
|w_{\delta}|_{H^{s}(\Omega)}\lesssim\delta^{1-s}
follows by the fact that
the approximation constructed is a finite sum of convolutions.

4.
Computational Experiments

We now evaluate the numerical performance and robustness of the proposed method. In particular, we demonstrate its ability to approximate minimisers of the continuous energy functional (
1.2
) with high accuracy, and highlight its robustness under mesh refinement and varying neural network architectures. Additionally, we show how the proposed finite element training approach significantly reduces both the computational cost and memory usage associated with gradient evaluations of neural network functions.

The finite element discretisations used in training are based on uniform triangulations. For instance, the unit square is divided into 9 equal squares, each of which is further subdivided into two triangles, yielding a total of 18 triangles as illustrated in Figure

.

Throughout this section, we use continuous piecewise polynomial finite element spaces of degree 2. The penalty parameter in the discrete energy functional (

\alpha=60

Recalling the residual neural network (ResNet) architecture defined in Equations (

\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
. The width of each hidden layer is fixed at
N=64
, and we compare the approximation performance of networks with 2 and 4 residual blocks. The activation function used is

All neural network components are implemented in the PyTorch framework

4.1.
Approximating Solutions in Convex Domains

We begin by considering approximations on convex domains. Specifically, we choose the right-hand side
f
in (
1

(4.1)

\displaystyle u(x_{1},x_{2})=\sin(4\pi x_{1})\sin(4\pi x_{2}),\qquad(x_{1},x_{2})\in\Omega=[0,1]^{2}.

To approximate minimisers of the discrete energy functional (
3.1
), we employ a residual neural network with two blocks. We increase the resolution by refining the mesh (i.e., increasing the number of triangles) and simultaneously vary the precision of the integration rule used during training.

After
10^{5}

5\times 10^{-3}
, the
L^{2}(\Omega)
errors (solid curves) are reported in Figure

9.3\cdot 10^{-4}

10^{-3}

Since
u_{\theta}\in C^{2}(\Omega)

\llbracket\nabla I_{{\mathcal{S}}_{h}}u_{\theta}\rrbracket
on each edge

To explore the role of these terms, we repeat the preceding experiment while omitting the consistency term in (

Figure

This raises a natural question: what benefit, if any, is gained by including the gradient jump terms?

To better understand their role, we consider a test function designed to exhibit jump-like behaviour. Define

(4.2)

u(x)=\begin{cases}\sin(2\pi x),&\text{for }x\in\Omega_{1}=[0,0.5-\epsilon],\\
\sin(2\pi x)-1,&\text{for }x\in\Omega_{2}=[0.5+\epsilon,1],\end{cases}

with a smooth transition in the intermediate region
\Omega_{3}=[0.5-\epsilon,0.5+\epsilon]
. Choosing
f=4\pi^{2}\sin(2\pi x)
ensures that

\|\varDelta u+f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1})}=\|\varDelta u+f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{2})}=0
.
From standard approximation theory for neural networks, we know that there exists
\hat{u}_{\theta}\in V_{\mathcal{N}}
such that

\|\varDelta\hat{u}_{\theta}+f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1})}+\|\varDelta\hat{u}_{\theta}+f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{2})}<\delta\quad\text{for any }\delta>0.

This is also evident in practice, neural networks can approximate such step-like functions with high fidelity.

However, if the numerical integration rule does not place points in
\Omega_{3}
, the transition (or ”jump”) between
\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
may go undetected.
This allows discontinuous or piecewise solutions resembling (
4.2
) to persist undetected by the residual term alone.

Motivated by this, we examine the performance of deeper networks capable of representing such non-smooth features. Repeating the earlier experiment using a residual network
with four blocks, we observe a marked degradation in
L^{2}
error when the jump terms are omitted: the error increases by one to two orders of magnitude, compare the
dashed curves in Figures
3
and
2
. In particular, when using a second-order integration rule, the error grows from
\sim 10^{-3}
to

\sim 10^{-2}
as the number of training points increases (orange curve in Figure
3

4.1

By contrast, when minimising the full energy
\mathcal{E}_{h}
(including jump terms), the
L^{2}
errors are comparable to those obtained with the smaller architecture, again compare
Figures
3
and
2
. This illustrates that the proposed method offers improved robustness in that accurate solutions are obtained independently of the
network depth or quadrature precision.

5.
Conclusion

We have introduced and analysed a finite element-based training approach for physics-informed neural networks, with a focus on energy minimisation problems. The proposed method achieves high accuracy, improved computational efficiency, and robustness across different network architectures and problem geometries. In particular, the inclusion of consistency terms involving gradient jumps was shown to enhance stability in the presence of non-smooth solutions or under-resolved features. Numerical experiments confirm the method’s competitive performance compared to standard collocation-based PINNs, especially in terms of memory usage and training time. These results support the viability of finite element surrogates as a reliable and scalable framework for solving PDEs with neural networks.
