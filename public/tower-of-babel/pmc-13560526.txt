Clinical utility of in-ear EEG electrodes in hospitalized patients: comparison with standard scalp EEG
Authors: Monika Dzalto, Adela Desowska, Nina Moutonnet, Sajitha Cannadathu, Olivia Lucas, Ben Moore, Yeliz Demir, Clementine Tshuma, Robert Leech, Steven White, Danilo Mandic, Gregory Scott
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveClinical electroencephalography (EEG) is resource-intensive, requiring expensive equipment and trained specialists to attach scalp electrodes. Consequently, there is interest in alternative EEG technologies that could provide low-cost, user-friendly, and unobtrusive recording in clinical settings. Here, we aimed to evaluate the clinical utility of in-ear EEG electrodes in hospitalized patients through direct comparison with routine scalp EEG.MethodsFor thirty routine adult in-patient clinical EEG recordings at Charing Cross Hospital (London, United Kingdom), participants were recruited and had in-ear EEG electrodes placed alongside standard scalp electrodes. Recordings from ear and scalp electrodes were independently and blindly interpreted by a consultant clinical neurophysiologist using a bespoke assessment protocol. Scalp EEG served as the gold-standard reference.ResultsCompared with scalp EEG, interpretation of ear EEG achieved a sensitivity of 83.3%, specificity of 80.0%, and accuracy of 82.8% for the detection of any neurophysiological abnormality. There was substantial agreement between ear and scalp EEG for encephalopathy grading (quadratic weighted kappa = 0.72). One left posterior quadrant seizure, present in scalp EEG, was not detected by ear EEG interpretation alone.ConclusionIn-ear EEG demonstrated good agreement with conventional scalp EEG for the detection of clinically relevant abnormalities, with substantial agreement for encephalopathy severity grading. While reduced spatial coverage may limit sensitivity to focal phenomena, in-ear EEG appears well-suited to detecting diffuse cerebral dysfunction.SignificanceThis study paves the way for broader clinical development and validation of ear EEG-based devices as a pragmatic and scalable technology for use in acute hospital settings.Keywords: electroencephalography, ear, electrodes

Introduction

Since the pioneering recordings of Hans Berger over a century ago, electroencephalography (EEG) has become a cornerstone technique for non-invasive measurement of brain activity in clinical practice. Standard clinical EEG involves placement of electrodes across the scalp using conductive gel or paste. Although painless and safe, the setup process is labour-intensive and time-consuming, requiring the involvement of trained personnel and often needing careful electrode placement through hair to ensure reliable scalp contact. Access to clinical EEG may therefore be limited by, amongst other factors, the availability of trained operators, equipment, and preparation time (Burkholder et al., 2016; Petrossian et al., 2023a, Petrossian et al., 2023b). Consequently, there is growing interest in the development of alternative EEG technologies designed to simplify set up, lower costs, and improve accessibility (Casson, 2019).

Ear EEG represents one alternative approach (Kaongoen et al., 2023), and several systems have been developed to record EEG signals from electrodes placed within or around the ear. We have developed an in-ear EEG system (Goverdovsky et al., 2016; Mikkelsen et al., 2015), where electrodes are embedded into a compact viscoelastic earplug inserted into the ear canal. This design dramatically simplifies EEG electrode placement, with the device fitted by non-specialists or the wearer themselves. The configuration also avoids many of the challenges associated with scalp electrodes, including obstruction from hair. In addition, the compact and unobtrusive design may improve user comfort and discretion, making the system well suited to prolonged or ambulatory recording.

Whilst not yet in routine clinical use, ear EEG devices have shown potential in a range of non-clinical contexts (Kaongoen et al., 2023). For example, in our own validation studies in healthy people, ear EEG was able to detect visual and auditory evoked responses with a signal to noise ratio on par with scalp EEG (Kidmose et al., 2013; Kidmose et al., 2012a, Kidmose et al., 2012b). We also found ear EEG to be as effective for sleep staging as standard polysomnography (Alqurashi et al., 2018).

Whilst ear EEG has potential clinical benefits, there are some potential limitations as a clinical tool. The advantages of ease of setup and wearability come at the cost of limited spatial coverage, due to electrodes being confined to the ear. This potentially limits the sensitivity of ear EEG to important clinical phenomena, such as small, remote, ictal foci. Potential undersampling of this kind is a well-recognised problem for standard clinical EEG, which subtends the entire scalp with ∼20 electrodes (Casale et al., 2022). Nevertheless, many of the electrographic abnormalities commonly identified and graded in modern clinical EEG – particularly acute encephalopathy – are usually spatially widespread. Therefore, whilst ear EEG is unlikely to replace conventional scalp EEG for the precise localisation of focal pathology, it may still provide clinically meaningful information for the detection, grading, and in-patient monitoring of brain dysfunction.

A body of research literature has investigated the technical characteristics of ear EEG electrodes (Kaongoen et al., 2023). However, the clinical utility of ear EEG technology remains relatively underexplored, particularly for scenarios where recordings are interpreted by a clinical neurophysiologist, i.e., without the use of quantitative methods, as is the case for interpretation of routine clinical EEG in most healthcare settings.

Here, we aimed to assess the clinical utility of our in-ear EEG electrodes in an acute hospital setting, as interpreted by a clinical neurophysiologist by comparison with the gold standard of scalp EEG, using a blinded reporting process. We also sought to explore the expert interpreter's perspective on the in-ear EEG recording modality.

Methods

Participant recruitment

Hospitalized patients at Charing Cross Hospital (London, UK) were recruited. The inclusion criteria required that participants were aged 18 years and over and already scheduled to undergo a clinical EEG as part of their in-patient care. The exclusion criteria were any contraindication to placing electrodes within the ear canal (e.g., suspected or proven skull base fracture or ear infection) and lacking capacity to consent to study participation without an available consultee (see below).

Ethical approval and consent procedures

NHS Health Research Authority and Research Ethics Committee approvals were obtained prior to study commencement (reference 23/NW/0313). Where possible, participants themselves gave written consent to take part. If a participant did not have capacity to consent, an opinion was sought from a consultee (someone who knew the participant well, e.g., next of kin), keeping in mind the participant’s wishes and beliefs. Recordings took place either on the hospital wards, the intensive care unit, or in the neurophysiology department.

In-ear EEG electrode and placement

The in-ear EEG electrode (Fig. 1) was developed by DM (Department of Electrical and Electronic Engineering, Imperial College London), and is described in full in (Goverdovsky et al., 2016).

The In-ear EEG electrode used in this study. (A) The ear EEG electrode and (B) Its placement in the ear canal.

The sensor consists of a viscoelastic (memory foam) earplug onto which conductive silver-coated cloth electrodes are attached (Fig. 1A). The cloth electrodes are flexible and compressible, allowing the earpiece to be rolled, inserted, and allowed to expand in a manner analogous to a standard noise-reducing earplug. The memory foam earplug morphs to the shape of the ear canal upon insertion, redistributing outward pressure uniformly along the canal wall and providing a stable, low-impedance electrode-skin interface. After using abrasive gel to clean the ear, one in-ear EEG electrode was placed into each ear canal (Fig. 1B) during attachment of the routine clinical EEG scalp electrodes. Standard conductive gel was used. The cables from the in-ear electrodes were plugged into the same EEG amplifier used for the clinical acquisition, as auxiliary channels.

Concurrent clinical EEG

Alongside the in-ear EEG recording, participants underwent concurrent standard clinical EEG acquisition, with scalp electrodes positioned according to the international 10-20 system. All signals were acquired using a Natus Xltek EEG32 amplifier. The ear and scalp EEG channels were recorded within the same acquisition system and therefore used the same ground and acquisition reference electrodes, with the passive ground typically positioned at FPz and reference placed at FCz along the midline.

The recording was stored on the clinical EEG database (Natus Neuroworks). The ear EEG channels were ignored for the purposes of clinical care. For further analysis in this study, the recordings, containing all channels, were exported from the database as pseudonymised European Data Format (EDF) files.

Allied clinical data

Allied clinical data were extracted from the hospital’s electronic health records system (Cerner Millenium). The following data were extracted, in order to be shown to the clinical neurophysiologist (SW) as a brief clinical summary alongside the EEG recordings, for interpretation, in keeping with routine clinical practice: patient demographics (age, gender), referral question, relevant medication, relevant clinical imaging, blood data, and any other results, level of awareness, and clinical events during the recording. We also extracted additional clinical variables for use exclusively in this report.

EEG interpretation

We worked with SW, a consultant clinical neurophysiologist, to devise a structured protocol for interpreting the ear EEG and clinical EEG separately from one another whilst allowing cross-comparisons of interpretations between the two modalities. SW was not involved in the participants’ clinical care.

To facilitate protocol development, three randomly-selected datasets (EDF files and allied clinical data) were provided to SW to help him familiarise himself with the ear EEG signals alongside the scalp channels. The EDFbrowser viewing software (version 1.4) was used for all EEG interpretation. Two separate EEG montages were created in EDFbrowser (Fig. 2): (a) a montage showing only the two ear EEG channels, and (b) a standard bipolar longitudinal montage showing only the scalp EEG channels (Beniczky and Schomer, 2020). To both montages, the following filters were applied: high-pass 0.5Hz, low-pass 70Hz, and notch 50Hz (Beniczky and Schomer, 2020). Additionally, a moving average filter was added, to increase the similarity of the signals to those the rater normally encounters in a clinical setting.

The final structured EEG interpretation protocol proforma is shown in Supplementary Material. This was applied to both ear EEG and scalp EEG recordings. Briefly, the first step was to rate the recording as “normal” or “abnormal”. If it was “abnormal”, specific features present were scored as follows: EEG continuous or discontinuous; encephalopathic – no, mild, moderate or severe (below); interictal epileptiform discharges – unilateral or bilateral; sub-clinical electrographic seizure activity – unilateral or bilateral and whether discrete electrographic seizures or electrographic status epilepticus; any EEG asymmetries – no, asymmetric slowing, asymmetric interictal epileptiform discharges, asymmetric electrographic seizure activity. The encephalopathy grading scheme used the following definitions: Mild – predominance or excess of theta activity (but may include some intermixed alpha or mixed frequency activities); Moderate – mixed theta and delta activity; Severe – exclusively or predominantly delta activity (might include some theta activity mixed in as well, but mainly delta).

Space for additional free text comments was provided for the expert reviewer to indicate issues or uncertainties which had arisen during the process of rating the EEG recordings.

SW rated the two sets of EEG recordings (ear, scalp) separately, beginning with all the ear EEG recordings, over several sessions. He then rated the complete set of scalp EEG recordings independently, again over several sessions. Accordingly, he rated all the ear EEG recordings before he had seen any of the scalp EEGs. In keeping with routine clinical EEG reporting practice, he read the brief clinical summary for the patient before rating either the ear EEG or scalp EEG recordings. The recordings were identified only using a participant number 1-30. In practice, this procedure meant that he was unlikely to recall the ear EEG findings of individual patients when he was rating their scalp EEGs at a later stage. Given the nature of this sample, there was a good deal of overlap between the clinical details for different patients, so that seeing the clinical summary before the ear EEG rating and before the scalp EEG rating was unlikely to represent a significant contaminating factor. The cases reviewed included the three datasets that had been selected for protocol development (i.e., these cases were “seen” twice).

Statistical analysis

Participants’ demographic and clinical data, collected from patient health records, were summarised using appropriate descriptive statistics. The outputs of the structured EEG interpretations were analysed as follows: Abnormality detection, encephalopathy detection, and encephalopathy grading outputs were formatted as confusion matrices, using scalp EEG results as the expected values and the ear EEG results as observed values. Sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy values were then calculated. For encephalopathy grading, unweighted Cohen’s kappa and quadratic weighted kappa were calculated to provide measures of intra-rater cross-modality agreement.

Abnormality detection from ear EEG versus scalp EEG

For each participant, a consultant neurophysiologist (SW) interpreted the ear EEG recording (showing the left and right ear signals alone) first, alongside allied clinical data (e.g., patient demographics, the clinical referral) but blinded to the contemporaneous scalp EEG; subsequently, the scalp EEG was interpreted (in isolation from the ear EEG), providing a gold standard comparator. In one participant out of 30 (3.3%), both ear EEG channels were found to be so severely artefactual (thought likely to reflect faulty electrode placement) that data from this participant were excluded from comparative analysis.

We assessed whether any EEG abnormality (defined in 2.6) was detected via ear EEG recordings alone, compared to the gold standard provided by conventional scalp EEG (Fig. 3A). In-ear EEG demonstrated good agreement with scalp EEG for the detection of abnormality, with 83.3% sensitivity, 80.0% specificity, and 82.8% overall accuracy (i.e., abnormality or its absence was correctly detected in ear EEG). The positive predictive value (PPV) was high (95.2%), indicating that abnormalities identified on ear EEG were highly likely to be confirmed on scalp EEG, although negative predictive value (NPV) was lower (50.0%), reflecting some missed abnormalities.

Encephalopathy detection and grading from ear EEG versus scalp EEG

We then focused on whether ear EEG could detect encephalopathy (Fig. 3B). Performance was similar or slightly improved for the detection of any encephalopathy, with 85.7% sensitivity (18 out of 21 cases), 87.5% specificity, and 86.2% accuracy. The PPV remained high (94.7%), supporting the utility of ear EEG as a “rule-in” tool for encephalopathy in this context.

We next assessed ordinal encephalopathy severity grading (into None, Mild, Moderate, Severe) between ear and scalp modalities (Fig. 3C). Exact agreement was 51.7%; however, most discrepancies were confined to adjacent severity categories, with marked misclassification across multiple severity levels being rare. Unweighted Cohen’s kappa (κ) indicated fair agreement (κ=0.30), reflecting the equal penalisation of all disagreements. In contrast, quadratic weighted κ – which accounts for the magnitude of disagreement (Mandrekar, 2011) – indicated substantial agreement (κ=0.72), consistent with observations that most discrepancies were minor (adjacent-category). This suggests that ear EEG captures the broad severity of encephalopathy despite reduced spatial sampling.

Seizure detection

Only one seizure was observed among the 30 recordings, a left posterior quadrant electrographic seizure, which was detected on scalp EEG but not identified on ear EEG interpretation (Fig. 2C). The limited number of events precluded formal quantitative analysis of seizure detection performance.

Neurophysiologist’s comments

The expert reviewer made occasional free text comments to indicate issues or uncertainties which had arisen during the process of rating the ear EEG recordings. In a single case, this was the absence of any recognizable signal from either channel, presumably reflecting a problem with electrode placement in the ear canals. The reviewer found occasional difficulties in being completely certain whether waveforms were artefactual or might represent abnormal graphoelements, or about whether slow components were related to drowsiness or encephalopathy. This highlighted the extent to which the electroencephalographer normally depends on seeing the entire scalp EEG as a gestalt in routine clinical EEG reporting. Not having this information available may inevitably be an occasional problem in using ear EEG in clinical settings. However, this was not a frequent issue and uncertainties in identifying artefacts or distinguishing between drowsiness and mild encephalopathy will, of course, sometimes arise during standard scalp EEG reporting.

Discussion

In this study, interpretation of recordings obtained from an in-ear EEG system demonstrated good agreement with conventional scalp EEG for the detection of clinically relevant abnormalities and encephalopathy in a representative hospitalised adult population. Sensitivity and specificity were ≥80%, supporting the potential clinical utility of this unobtrusive and rapidly deployable modality.

Ear EEG has previously been validated primarily in controlled experimental settings, most often in healthy participants, where feasibility for unobtrusive and long-term monitoring has been established (Kaongoen et al., 2023). However, evaluation in acutely hospitalised adult populations remains limited. To our knowledge, this is the first study to assess the clinical interpretability of in-ear EEG in a heterogeneous in-patient cohort using standard neurophysiological reporting frameworks. As such, the present work extends prior literature by focusing on real-world diagnostic interpretation rather than signal-level validation.

A key strength of this study is its emphasis on ecological validity. Recordings were acquired alongside routine clinical EEG with minimal modification to standard protocols, and interpretation was undertaken within established clinical workflows, including access to relevant clinical information and conventional reporting practices. This design reflects real-world use of EEG in acute hospital settings, where timely assessment of brain function is often required but access to conventional scalp EEG may be limited.

Previous clinical studies of ear EEG have largely focused on specific use-cases, particularly epilepsy, and have suggested that ear-based systems may detect epileptiform discharges, especially of temporal origin (Joyner et al., 2024; Zibrandtsen et al., 2017). However, these studies typically employed multi-electrode ear arrays or hybrid configurations, which differ substantially from the simplified bilateral in-ear electrodes used in the present study. There is considerable heterogeneity across ear EEG technologies, including systems with electrodes positioned within the ear canal, around the ear, or combinations thereof, with varying numbers of electrodes (Kaongoen et al., 2023). These technical differences are likely to influence both signal characteristics and sensitivity to clinically important phenomena, particularly focal abnormalities, and may partly explain differences between studies.

The potential clinical role of ear EEG in acute care remains to be defined. Conventional scalp EEG resource-intensive, requiring specialised equipment and trained personnel, often resulting in delays to recording. In response, simplified rapid-deployment EEG systems have been developed, including reduced-montage and forehead-based devices (Davey et al., 2022). Ear EEG represents a complementary approach, offering rapid application, minimal setup, and improved patient tolerability. However, the reduced spatial sampling inherent to ear EEG is likely to limit sensitivity to focal pathology and thus constrain its role as a standalone diagnostic tool. In this context, ear EEG may be best positioned as an adjunct or triage tool, for example, to support early identification and grading of encephalopathy, to prioritise access to full scalp EEG, or to provide in-patient monitoring of diffuse cerebral dysfunction in settings where conventional EEG is unavailable. (See Zibrandtsen et al., 2017 for further discussion of potential limitations of the technology itself.)

Outside the acute setting, ear EEG systems have already demonstrated utility in ambulatory and home-based applications, including sleep staging with performance comparable to polysomnography (Alqurashi et al., 2018), and reliable patient self-application (Mikkelsen et al., 2022). These characteristics highlight the potential of ear EEG for long-term monitoring. However, translation into routine practice requires clearer definition of the clinical scenarios in which ear EEG can safely substitute for, or complement, conventional scalp EEG.

Several limitations of our study should be considered. First, it should be noted that the reference electrode used with our in-ear EEG electrodes was chosen to be the same as the simultaneous standard scalp EEG (FCz). Whilst this choice simplified the study and maintained compatibility with the scalp recording, a central reference/ground remote from the ear will clearly produce different signals versus if the reference/ground were placed near the ear (e.g., on the mastoid). Since our findings rely on a scalp (reference) electrode, this challenges their translatability to a complete “ear EEG” device, since such a device would need to incorporate an FCz electrode, which would pose technical challenges versus, e.g., an ipsilateral mastoid reference (which would yield different signals with potentially significantly different performance). Second, the modest sample size limited assessment of less frequent but clinically important phenomena, particularly seizures, with only a single focal seizure observed. Third, EEG interpretation was performed by a single expert reviewer, and the three studies used for familiarisation were seen ‘twice’. Although reflective of routine clinical practice, inter-rater variability was not assessed here. In further studies, with larger numbers of patients (across a range of clinical settings, e.g. critical care versus acute medical wards) as well as raters, we would wish to assess inter-rater reliability to further define the clinical validity of the method. It should be noted that interpretation of encephalopathy severity is inherently subjective and may have influenced our agreement metrics. Finally, we did not correlate the clinical referral question to either the level of consciousness during the recording or the EEG findings (either the standard scalp EEG or the in-ear EEG). Our evaluation protocol did not incorporate these elements, and the clinical EEG documentation of level of consciousness was not standardised. This is nonetheless important as part of fully assessing the clinical utility of novel technologies, and should be a focus of future work, along with factors such as whether in-ear EEG changes patient management, and cost-effectiveness.

Conclusions

In-ear EEG electrodes demonstrated promising performance for the detection of clinically relevant abnormalities in hospitalised patients, with good agreement with conventional scalp EEG for several findings. However, limitations in spatial resolution and sensitivity to focal pathology were evident. Our findings support continued technological development and clinical evaluation of in-ear EEG, potentially as a pragmatic, rapid-deployment adjunct within neurophysiology practice.
