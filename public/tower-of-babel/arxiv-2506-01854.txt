Black-Box Crypto is Useless for Pseudorandom Codes
Sanjam Garg, Sam Gunn, Mingyuan Wang

Abstract
A pseudorandom code is a keyed error-correction scheme with the property that any polynomial number of encodings appear random to any computationally bounded adversary. We show that the pseudorandomness of any code tolerating a constant rate of random errors cannot be based on black-box reductions to almost any generic cryptographic primitive: for instance, anything that can be built from random oracles, generic multilinear groups, and virtual black-box obfuscation. Our result is optimal , as Ghentiyala and Guruswami (2024) observed that pseudorandom codes tolerating any sub-constant rate of random errors exist using a black-box reduction from one-way functions. The key technical ingredient in our proof is the hypercontractivity theorem for Boolean functions, which we use to prove our impossibility in the random oracle model. It turns out that this easily extends to an impossibility in the presence of “crypto oracles,” a notion recently introduced—and shown to be capable of implementing all the primitives mentioned above—by Lin, Mook, and Wichs (EUROCRYPT 2025).

Introduction

A
pseudorandom code
(PRC) is a keyed error-correction scheme whose codewords appear pseudorandom to any computationally bounded adversary who doesn’t know the key.
In other words, it is a secret-key pseudorandom encryption scheme with the additional requirement that the decoding algorithm functions even if the transmission is corrupted.
Unless otherwise specified, a PRC should be robust to a
constant rate
of random errors.

PRCs were defined by
[
CG24
]
, where they were shown to be equivalent to robust and undetectable watermarking for large language models.
These ideas have since been used for practical watermarking of AI-generated images and videos
[
GZS25
,
HLLL25
]
.

Unfortunately, despite several constructions having been proposed
[
CG24
,
GG24
,
GM24
]
, every known PRC either suffers from quasipolynomial-time distinguishing attacks or relies on ad-hoc hardness assumptions.
It is therefore natural to ask,

is there a fundamental barrier to constructing a pseudorandom code from generic cryptographic primitives (such as one-way functions, public-key encryption, etc.)?

1.1
Our contribution

We answer this question in the affirmative by showing that the black-box use of virtually all cryptographic primitives cannot yield a PRC resilient to a constant fraction of errors.

Informal Theorem 1
.

Relative to a random oracle, there do not exist statistically-secure pseudorandom codes tolerating any constant rate of random bit-flip errors.

Random errors are the weakest error model considered in the literature. Since we are proving a lower bound, this only makes our result stronger.

There is a simple construction of pseudorandom codes tolerating any sub-constant error rate in the information-theoretic random oracle model
[
GG24
]
.
Therefore, our result is essentially optimal in terms of the error rate.

In fact, this theorem actually implies much stronger separation results.
In the secret-key setting, the encoder and decoder effectively share a

which is not accessible to the pseudorandomness adversary.

It turns out that a secret random oracle is far more powerful than idealized hash functions, as demonstrated by

Informal Corollary 2

Relative to any crypto oracle, there do not exist statistically-secure pseudorandom codes tolerating any constant rate of random bit-flip errors.

It is therefore not possible to build a PRC using black-box reductions to almost any generic cryptographic primitive.

By standard techniques in black-box separation literature

2.1
Definitions

In this work, we are interested in pseudorandom codes with robustness to the binary symmetric channel, which introduces random bit-flips.
Following the convention in Boolean analysis, we denote this channel by
\mathcal{N}_{\rho}
.
For
\rho\in[0,1)
and
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
,
\mathcal{N}_{\rho}(x)
replaces each bit of
x
with a random bit with probability
1-\rho
.
Note that
\mathcal{N}_{\rho}
is the binary symmetric channel
\mathsf{BSC}_{(1-\rho)/2}
.
This is the weakest model of noise considered in the literature for pseudorandom codes.

For the purposes of our overview, a
pseudorandom code
(or PRC) for
\mathcal{N}_{\rho}
will be a pair of polynomial-time randomized algorithms
\mathsf{Enc},\mathsf{Dec}
that satisfy the following properties:

•

Completeness / robustness
: If
\mathsf{\vphantom{p}sk}\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{\lambda}
is random,
c\leftarrow\mathsf{Enc}(\mathsf{\vphantom{p}sk})
, and
\tilde{c}\leftarrow\mathcal{N}_{\rho}(c)
, then
\mathsf{Dec}(\mathsf{\vphantom{p}sk},c)=1
with probability
1-\mathsf{negl}\left(\lambda\right)
.

•

Soundness
: For any fixed
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, we have
\Pr_{\mathsf{\vphantom{p}sk}\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{\lambda}}[\mathsf{Dec}(\mathsf{\vphantom{p}sk},x)=\bot]\geqslant 1-\mathsf{negl}\left(\lambda\right)
.

•

Pseudorandomness
: For any polynomial-time adversary
\mathcal{A}
and any
m=\mathsf{poly}\left(\lambda\right)
,

\absolutevalue{\Pr_{\substack{c_1, \dots, c_m \from\Enc(\sk) \\ \sk\from\{0,1\}^\secpar}}[\cA(c_1, \dots, c_m) = 1] - \Pr_{x_1, \dots, x_m \from\{0,1\}^n}[\cA(x_1, \dots, x_m) = 1]}\leqslant\mathsf{negl}\left(\lambda\right).

This definition is only the
secret-key, zero-bit
case of the more general definition of
[
CG24
]
.
Since we are proving an impossibility, it only strengthens our result to consider this special case.

2.2
Low-noise pseudorandom codes from pseudorandom functions

Before we get to our impossibility, let us recall how pseudorandom codes for
\mathcal{N}_{\rho}
can be built from pseudorandom functions (PRFs) if
\rho=1-\text{o}\left(1\right)
.
This argument is from
[
GG24
, Section 3]
.

Let
\ell=(\log\lambda)/(1-\rho)
and
\mathsf{PRF}_{\mathsf{\vphantom{p}sk}}:\{0,1\}^{\ell}\to\{0,1\}^{\ell}
be a PRF.
Our encoder
\mathsf{Enc}(\mathsf{\vphantom{p}sk})
first samples random strings
r_{1},\dots,r_{\lambda^{2}}\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{\ell}
, then outputs

c=\left(r_{1}\|\mathsf{PRF}_{\mathsf{\vphantom{p}sk}}(r_{1})\|\cdots\|r_{\lambda^{2}}\|\mathsf{PRF}_{\mathsf{\vphantom{p}sk}}(r_{\lambda^{2}})\right).

The decoder, upon receiving
x=\left(\widetilde{r}_{1}\|\widetilde{y}_{1}\|\cdots\|\widetilde{r}_{\lambda^{2}}\|\widetilde{y}_{\lambda^{2}}\right)
, simply checks if
\widetilde{y}_{i}=\mathsf{PRF}_{\mathsf{\vphantom{p}sk}}(\widetilde{r}_{i})
for any
i
.

Pseudorandomness follows from the security of the PRF and the fact that
\ell=\omega(\log\lambda)
if
\rho=1-\text{o}\left(1\right)
.
For soundness, suppose that
x
is independent of
\mathsf{\vphantom{p}sk}
:
Then for any
i
, the probability that
\widetilde{y}_{i}=\mathsf{PRF}_{\mathsf{\vphantom{p}sk}}(\widetilde{r}_{i})
is at most
2^{-\ell}+\mathsf{negl}\left(\lambda\right)
by security of the PRF.
If
\rho=1-o(1)
then this is
\mathsf{negl}\left(\lambda\right)
, and by a union bound so is the probability that the decoder outputs
1
.

For robustness to
\mathcal{N}_{\rho}
, suppose that
c\leftarrow\mathsf{Enc}(\mathsf{\vphantom{p}sk})
and
\tilde{c}\leftarrow\mathcal{N}(c)
.
Then
\mathsf{Dec}(\mathsf{\vphantom{p}sk},\tilde{c})=\bot
only if every block
r_{i}\|y_{i}
contains an error.
Each bit is correct with probability
\frac{1+\rho}{2}
, so each block contains an error with probability
1-\left(\frac{1+\rho}{2}\right)^{2\ell}
.
The probability that every block contains an error is therefore

\left(1-\left(\frac{1+\rho}{2}\right)^{2\ell}\right)^{\lambda^{2}}\leqslant\left(1-\frac{\lambda^{\rho^{2}/2}}{\lambda^{2}}\right)^{\lambda^{2}}=\mathsf{negl}\left(\lambda\right)

after simplifying.

This construction works when
\rho=1-\text{o}\left(1\right)
, but it fundamentally breaks down if
\rho=1-\Omega\left(1\right)
.
The issue is that pseudorandomness requires our seeds
r_{i}
to each have length at least
\ell=\omega\left(\log n\right)
in order to avoid collisions; whereas any completeness-soundness gap requires
\ell=\mathcal{O}\left(\log n\right)
, because a constant noise rate will produce an error on a string of length
\ell
with probability
1-\exp(-\ell)
.

We show that this threshold is not a result of an insufficiently clever scheme, but a fundamental barrier.
That is, while we have just seen that it is easy to build a PRC for any
\text{o}\left(1\right)
error rate in the random oracle model, it is impossible to build a PRC for any
\Omega\left(1\right)
error rate with black-box reductions to standard generic assumptions.

2.3
The proof strategy

The remainder of our overview is devoted to proving the impossibility.
The bulk of the proof is showing that queries to the random oracle are not useful for constructing a PRC.

In order to show that a random oracle is not useful in a two-party (e.g., encoder and decoder) protocol against a third-party eavesdropper (e.g., pseudorandomness distinguisher), a standard methodology in the black-box separation literature is to argue that the eavesdropper can learn all the
intersection queries

[
IR89
,
BM09
]
.
The intersection queries are those made by both parties engaging in the protocol; if the eavesdropper knows all the intersection queries, then the participating parties cannot establish a shared secret.

However, this is not possible in our setting.
Unlike every prior work we are aware of, we are in the
secret-key
setting, where the encoder and decoder share
\mathsf{\vphantom{p}sk}
.
The encoder and decoder can simply both query, say,
\mathsf{\vphantom{p}sk}
, making
\mathsf{\vphantom{p}sk}
an intersection query that the eavesdropper has no way of learning.
In fact, the encoder and decoder can prevent the eavesdropper from learning any query at all by prepending
\mathsf{\vphantom{p}sk}
to all of their queries.
One can therefore think of our model as a
secret random oracle model
, where only the secret key holders have access to the oracle.
Our approach will have to demonstrate that the noise channel makes it impossible for the encoder and decoder to put this secret random oracle to good use.

Consider an arbitrary PRC for
\mathcal{N}_{\rho}
, say
(\mathsf{Enc}^{\mathcal{R}},\mathsf{Dec}^{\mathcal{R}})
, that uses a random oracle
\mathcal{R}:\{0,1\}^{\lambda}\to\{0,1\}
.
Instead of defining an adversary, we will compile this PRC into a new one, say
(\widehat{\mathsf{Enc}}^{\mathcal{R}},\widehat{\mathsf{Dec}}^{\mathcal{R}})
, where the decoder uses
one fewer query
to
\mathcal{R}
, at the cost of a somewhat longer secret key and a small loss in completeness.
4
4

4

In
Section

4
we will remove all queries at once. We take the one-query-at-a-time approach in this overview because we find it conceptually somewhat simpler at this level of technicality.

By iterating this transformation, we can eliminate all queries from the decoder to the oracle—resulting in a PRC that is easily seen to be impossible: If the decoder makes no queries, then the encoder can simulate the oracle locally, and the oracle can be removed altogether.
But any PRC is in particular a pseudorandom encryption scheme, which cannot have statistical security in the standard model.

So, how does our compiled scheme
(\widehat{\mathsf{Enc}}^{\mathcal{R}},\widehat{\mathsf{Dec}}^{\mathcal{R}})
work?
Let
f_{\mathsf{\vphantom{p}sk}}
be the function mapping the received string
x
to the first query
\mathsf{Dec}^{\mathcal{R}}(\mathsf{\vphantom{p}sk},x)
makes to
\mathcal{R}
.
Suppose for simplicity that
f_{\mathsf{\vphantom{p}sk}}
is deterministic.
The basic idea of our compiled scheme is to transfer the oracle values on all queries
q
such that
\Pr_{x\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{n}}[f_{\mathsf{\vphantom{p}sk}}(x)=q]\geqslant\tau
to the secret key, for some threshold
\tau=1/\mathsf{poly}\left(\lambda\right)
.
We call such queries “low entropy” and otherwise “high entropy.”
Since there can be at most

\widehat{\mathsf{\vphantom{p}sk}}

The compiled scheme will simulate

•

For low entropy queries, both
\widehat{\mathsf{Enc}}
and
\widehat{\mathsf{Dec}}

•

If the first query made by

For all other queries, both

Observe that the first oracle query made by

Pseudorandomness and soundness for the compiled scheme are immediate, so we must only consider completeness.
Recall the completeness experiment:

1.

Sample

2.

Generate a codeword

Add noise to the codeword,
\tilde{c}\leftarrow\mathcal{N}_{\rho}(c)

4.

Apply the decoder,
\widehat{\mathsf{Dec}}^{\mathcal{R}}(\widehat{\mathsf{\vphantom{p}sk}},\tilde{c})

The only case where

might fail to perfectly simulate

is when the first query made by
\mathsf{Dec}

\widehat{\mathsf{Enc}}

\widehat{\mathsf{Enc}}

\widehat{\mathsf{Dec}}

We argue in

prevents

and
\widehat{\mathsf{Dec}}
from making the same high entropy query.

2.5
Upgrading to a crypto oracle

It turns out that our random oracle impossibility easily extends to our full result by using the ideas of
[
LMW25
]
.
That work introduces
crypto oracles
and shows that they are capable of implementing most generic cryptography.
A crypto oracle is an efficient, stateless algorithm with access to a secret random oracle.

Because a PRC is a “secret-key” primitive—that is, the pseudorandomness adversary cannot access some secret randomness—our encoder and decoder can simply use the (public) random oracle to simulate the crypto oracle.
That is, by prepending all of their queries to the random oracle with the secret key, the encoder and decoder can effectively share a secret random oracle.

Now any construction of a PRC relative to a crypto oracle
\mathcal{B}^{\mathcal{R}}
immediately implies a PRC in the secret random oracle model, where one simply lets the encoder and decoder simulate
\mathcal{B}^{\mathcal{R}}
using access to the secret oracle
\mathcal{R}
.
6
6

6

In a crypto oracle model, a PRC guarantees that an adversary cannot break pseudorandomness given access to
\mathcal{B}^{\mathcal{R}}
. In the compiled scheme, the adversary does not even have access to
\mathcal{B}^{\mathcal{R}}
and, hence, can only become weaker and achieve a lower advantage.

Consequently, our theorem immediately implies our separation result in the crypto oracle model.

We have

\displaystyle H(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})

\displaystyle=H(\mathsf{\vphantom{p}sk})+H(x_{1},\dots,x_{m}|\mathsf{\vphantom{p}sk})

\displaystyle=\ell+m\cdot H(x|\mathsf{\vphantom{p}sk}),

which means that
H(x|\mathsf{\vphantom{p}sk})=(H(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})-\ell)/m
.

Let
R
be a uniformly random sample from
\{0,1\}^{\ell+n}
.
Fact
1
implies the following two inequalities:

\bullet

\mathsf{SD}\left({(\mathsf{\vphantom{p}sk},x),R}\right)\leqslant\sqrt{n+\ell-H(\mathsf{\vphantom{p}sk},x)}=\sqrt{n-H(x|\mathsf{\vphantom{p}sk})}
, and

\bullet

H(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})\geqslant nm-2\varepsilon nm-2\sqrt{\varepsilon}
.

Therefore

\displaystyle\mathsf{SD}\left({(\mathsf{\vphantom{p}sk},x),R}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sqrt{n-H(x|\mathsf{\vphantom{p}sk})}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{n-(H(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})-\ell)/m}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sqrt{n-(nm-2\varepsilon nm-2\sqrt{\varepsilon}-\ell)/m}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2\varepsilon n+2\sqrt{\varepsilon}/m+\ell/m},

completing the proof.
∎

3.3
Pseudorandom codes

Following
[
CG24
]
, we define private-key pseudorandom codes as follows.
Our impossibility result will apply to the private-key setting, which immediately implies the corresponding impossibility for the public-key setting.
Therefore we omit definitions of public-key pseudorandom codes.

Definition 1

(Private-key PRC)
.

A private-key
(\delta,\varepsilon,\mu)
pseudorandom error-correcting code over an alphabet
\Sigma
and a channel
\mathcal{E}\colon\Sigma^{*}\rightarrow\Sigma^{*}
consists of a tuple of PPT algorithms
(\mathsf{KeyGen},\mathsf{Enc},\mathsf{Dec})
:

•

A key generation algorithm
\mathsf{KeyGen}
that samples a secret key
\mathsf{\vphantom{p}sk}\in\{0,1\}^{\ell(\lambda)}
.

•

An encoding algorithm
\mathsf{Enc}
that takes the secret key
\mathsf{\vphantom{p}sk}\in\{0,1\}^{\ell(\lambda)}
and the message
m\in\Sigma^{k(\lambda)}
as input and outputs a codeword
c\in\Sigma^{n(\lambda)}
.

•

A decoding algorithm that takes the secret key
\mathsf{\vphantom{p}sk}\in\{0,1\}^{\ell(\lambda)}
and a (potentially erroneous) codeword
c\in\Sigma^{n(\lambda)}
as input and outputs a message
m\in\Sigma^{k(\lambda)}\cup\{\bot\}
.

These algorithms must satisfy the following properties:

•

\delta
-Completeness (robustness).
For any message
m\in\Sigma^{k}
, it holds that

\operatorname{Pr}\left[\left.\mathsf{Dec}(\mathsf{\vphantom{p}sk},\widetilde{c})=m\ \middle|\ \begin{gathered}\mathsf{\vphantom{p}sk}\leftarrow\mathsf{KeyGen}(\cdot)\\
c\leftarrow\mathsf{Enc}(\mathsf{\vphantom{p}sk},m)\\
\widetilde{c}\leftarrow\mathcal{E}(c)\end{gathered}\right.\right]\geqslant 1-\delta.

•

\varepsilon
-Pseudorandomness.
For any PPT adversary
\mathcal{A}
, it holds that

\absolutevalue{\prob{\left.\cA^{\Enc(\sk,\cdot)}(\cdot) = 1 \ \middle|\ \sk\from\KeyGen(\cdot)\right.}
- \prob{\cA^{\cU(\cdot)}(\cdot) = 1}}\leqslant\varepsilon,

where
\mathsf{Enc}(\mathsf{\vphantom{p}sk},\cdot)
generates fresh encodings even if the same message is queried twice and
\mathcal{U}(\cdot)
is an oracle that simply outputs fresh random strings.

•

\mu
-Soundness.
For a fixed codeword
c^{*}\in\Sigma^{n}
, it holds that

\operatorname{Pr}\left[\mathsf{Dec}(\mathsf{\vphantom{p}sk},c^{*})=\bot\ |\ \mathsf{\vphantom{p}sk}\leftarrow\mathsf{KeyGen}(\cdot)\right]\geqslant 1-\mu.

We will drop the specification of
\delta,\varepsilon,\mu
in the case that they are all bounded by
\mathsf{negl}\left(\lambda\right)
.

Zero-bit PRC.

We consider PRCs with only one possible message,
m=1
, which are known as
zero-bit
PRCs and are useful for watermarking.
For random errors, one can convert a zero-bit PRC to a many-bit PRC simply by concatenating codewords and random strings
[
CG24
]
.
Conversely, a zero-bit PRC is immediately implied by any PRC with
k>0
, so our impossibility result is not weakened at all by restricting to this case.
We therefore drop the message in the remainder of this paper, writing
\mathsf{Enc}(\mathsf{\vphantom{p}sk})
instead of
\mathsf{Enc}(\mathsf{\vphantom{p}sk},1)
.
The decoder
\mathsf{Dec}(\mathsf{\vphantom{p}sk},x)
outputs
1
or
\bot
, indicating success or failure, respectively.

Binary Alphabet.

In this work we will restrict ourselves to a binary alphabet, i.e.
\Sigma=\{0,1\}
.
Note that a pseudorandom code over large alphabet implies a pseudorandom code for binary alphabet.
Since we are proving an impossibility result, this restriction only makes our lower bound stronger.

Noise channel.

In this work we will only consider the binary symmetric channel, which introduces i.i.d bit-flip errors on the codeword bits.
Adversarial noise channels such as those considered in
[
AAC+25
]
can easily simulate the binary symmetric channel, so our impossibility result applies to pseudorandom codes for those channels as well.
We will denote the binary symmetric channel by
\mathcal{N}_{\rho}
, defined as

\mathcal{N}_{\rho}(x)=x\oplus e

where
e\leftarrow\mathsf{Bin}(n,(1-\rho)/2)
.
We use
\mathsf{Bin}(n,p)
to denote the binomial distribution with
n
trials and probability
p
, and
\oplus
to denote the bitwise XOR.
In other words, each symbol of
x
is replaced by a random element with probability
1-\rho
in
\mathcal{N}_{\rho}(x)
.

3.4
Boolean analysis and hypercontractivity

A core part of our argument makes use of Boolean analysis.
We introduce minimal notation here to facilitate our proof; we refer the reader to
[
O’D14
]
for more details.
For any Boolean function
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, its
\ell
-norm
\|f\|_{\ell}
is defined as

\|f\|_{\ell}=\left(\mathop{\mathrm{E}}\limits_{x}\left[{f(x)^{\ell}}\right]\right)^{1/\ell}.

For any two Boolean functions
f
and
g
, their
inner product
is defined as

\langle f,g\rangle=\mathop{\mathrm{E}}\limits_{x}\left[{f(x)\cdot g(x)}\right].

By Cauchy-Schwartz, we have

\langle f,g\rangle\leqslant\|f\|_{2}\cdot\|g\|_{2}.

We now define the
noise operator
T_{\rho}
.
For any Boolean function
f
, the noise operator
T_{\rho}
on the function
f
defines a new function
T_{\rho}f
as

(T_{\rho}f)(x)=\mathop{\mathrm{E}}\limits_{y\leftarrow\mathcal{N}_{\rho}(x)}\left[{f(y)}\right].

The following is a special case of the
hypercontractivity theorem

[
KKL88
]
.

Theorem 1

(Hypercontractivity,
[
KKL88
]
)
.

For any function
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
and
\rho\in[0,1]
,

\norm{T_{\rho} f}_{2}\leqslant\norm{f}_{1+\rho^{2}}.

Hypercontractivity is the core ingredient in our main technical lemma,
Lemma

2
.

Lemma 2
.

Let
\alpha>0
. Suppose
f,g:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\mathcal{Y}
are (randomized) functions such that, for all
x^{*}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
,

\operatorname{Pr}\left[f(x)=g(x^{*})\ |\ x\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{n}\right]\leqslant\alpha.

Then, for any
\rho\in[0,1]
, it holds that

\operatorname{Pr}\left[\left.f(\widetilde{x})=g(x)\ \middle|\ \begin{gathered}x\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{n}\\
\widetilde{x}\leftarrow\mathcal{N}_{\rho}(x)\end{gathered}\right.\right]\leqslant\alpha^{\frac{1}{2}\cdot\left(\frac{1-\rho^{2}}{1+\rho^{2}}\right)}.

Proof.

The proof works by a careful application of Cauchy-Schwarz and hypercontractivity.
For
y\in\mathcal{Y}
and
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, let
p_{y}(x)=\Pr[f(x)=y]
and
q_{y}(x)=\Pr[g(x)=y]
.
Let
\mathcal{Y}^{*}=\{y\in\mathcal{Y}:\norm{q_y}_{1}>0\}
.

\displaystyle\operatorname{Pr}\left[\left.f(\widetilde{x})=g(x)\ \middle|\ \begin{gathered}x\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{n}\\
\widetilde{x}\leftarrow\mathcal{N}_{\rho}(x)\end{gathered}\right.\right]

\displaystyle=\E_{x\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{n}}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}^{*}}q_{y}(x)\cdot(T_{\rho}p_{y})(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}^{*}}\langle q_{y},T_{\rho}p_{y}\rangle

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}^{*}}\norm{q_y}_{2}\cdot\norm{T_{\rho}p_y}_{2}

(Cauchy-Schwarz)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}^{*}}\norm{q_y}_{2}\cdot\norm{p_y}_{1+\rho^{2}}

(Hypercontractivity)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sqrt{\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}^{*}}\norm{q_y}_{2}^{2}}\cdot\sqrt{\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}^{*}}\norm{p_y}_{1+\rho^{2}}^{2}}

(Cauchy-Schwarz)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}^{*}}\E_{x}q_{y}(x)^{2}}\cdot\sqrt{\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}^{*}}\left(\E_{x}p_{y}(x)^{1+\rho^{2}}\right)^{2/(1+\rho^{2})}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sqrt{\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}^{*}}\E_{x}q_{y}(x)}\cdot\sqrt{\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}^{*}}\left(\E_{x}p_{y}(x)\right)^{2/(1+\rho^{2})}}

Letting
P(y)=\Pr_{x\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{n}}[f(x)=y]
and
Q(y)=\Pr_{x\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{n}}[g(x)=y]
,

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}^{*}}Q(y)}\cdot\sqrt{\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}^{*}}P(y)^{2/(1+\rho^{2})}}

\displaystyle=1\cdot\sqrt{\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}^{*}}P(y)\cdot P(y)^{2/(1+\rho^{2})-1}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sqrt{\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}^{*}}P(y)\cdot\alpha^{2/(1+\rho^{2})-1}}

(Assumption on
f
)

\displaystyle=\alpha^{\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1-\rho^{2}}{1+\rho^{2}}\right)}.

4.1
Compiling out the random oracle

In this section, we present a compiler that compiles any PRC in the random oracle model into a PRC in the information-theoretic setting.
The new scheme has the same soundness and pseudorandomness, but it requires a larger secret key and incurs a loss in the completeness error.

We assume
\mathsf{KeyGen}^{\mathcal{R}}
does not make any oracle queries.
This is without loss of generality because we can simply include any
\mathcal{R}
queries/responses used by

in the secret key.

In the following lemma and proof, we leave the dependence of functions of the security parameter on the security parameter implicit, writing e.g.
\tau

Let

Secret key size.

Pseudorandomness & soundness.

, Lemma
2
implies that

\displaystyle\operatorname{Pr}\left[\left.\neg\mathsf{Bad}_{1}\wedge f_{\mathsf{\vphantom{p}sk}^{\prime}}(\tilde{x})=g_{\mathsf{\vphantom{p}sk}^{\prime}}(x)\ \middle|\ \begin{gathered}x\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{n}\\
\tilde{x}\leftarrow\mathcal{N}_{\rho}(x)\end{gathered}\right.\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\operatorname{Pr}\left[\left.f_{\mathsf{\vphantom{p}sk}^{\prime}}(\tilde{x})=g_{\mathsf{\vphantom{p}sk}^{\prime}}(x)\ \middle|\ \begin{gathered}x\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{n}\\
\tilde{x}\leftarrow\mathcal{N}_{\rho}(x)\\
\neg\mathsf{Bad}_{1}\end{gathered}\right.\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\tau^{\frac{1}{2}\cdot\left(\frac{1-\rho^{2}}{1+\rho^{2}}\right)},

completing the proof.
∎

4.2
The main theorem

Let
\mathcal{R}:\{0,1\}^{\lambda}\to\{0,1\}
be a random oracle which is only given to the algorithms of the pseudorandom code.
That is, we do not assume that pseudorandomness holds against adversaries who are allowed to access
\mathcal{R}
.
We are now prepared to prove the following theorem.

Theorem 3
.

Let
(\mathsf{KeyGen}^{\mathcal{R}},\mathsf{Enc}^{\mathcal{R}},\mathsf{Dec}^{\mathcal{R}})
be a zero-bit
(\delta,\varepsilon,\mu)
pseudorandom error-correcting code making queries to a secret random oracle
\mathcal{R}
.
Suppose that the code is robust to the
\rho
-noise channel, and that the number of queries made by one execution of
\mathsf{KeyGen}^{\mathcal{R}}
,
\mathsf{Enc}^{\mathcal{R}}
, and
\mathsf{Dec}^{\mathcal{R}}
is at most
Q
.
Then

\delta+\mu\geqslant 1-Q^{2}\cdot\lambda^{-\omega(1-\rho)}-\mathcal{O}\left(Q^{2}\sqrt{\varepsilon n}\right).

Proof.

We first remove any oracle queries made by
\mathsf{KeyGen}^{\mathcal{R}}
by sampling the responses at random and including them in the secret key.
If
\mathsf{Enc}^{\mathcal{R}}
or
\mathsf{Dec}^{\mathcal{R}}
tries to make any query whose response is included in the secret key, it uses the stored response instead of consulting
\mathcal{R}
.
This transformation does not change the parameters of our PRC at all, except to increase the length of the secret key by a polynomial in
\lambda
.

Next, we apply
Lemma

3
using
\tau=\lambda^{-c}
, where
c>0
is any constant.
This yields a statistically-pseudorandom
(\delta^{\prime},\varepsilon,\mu)
-PRC construction
(\mathsf{KeyGen}^{\prime},\mathsf{Enc}^{\prime},\mathsf{Dec}^{\prime})
in the standard model with

\delta^{\prime}=\delta+\mathsf{negl}\left(\lambda\right)+Q^{2}\cdot\lambda^{-\frac{c}{2}\cdot\left(\frac{1-\rho^{2}}{1+\rho^{2}}\right)}+\mathcal{O}\left(Q^{2}\cdot\sqrt{\varepsilon n}\right)

and
\mathsf{poly}\left(\lambda\right)
-length secret keys.
Now by
Lemma

1
, PRC codewords are
\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\varepsilon n}\right)
-indistinguishable from random strings
even to a distinguisher provided with the secret key
.
Therefore the completeness-soundness gap is
\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\varepsilon n}\right)
, i.e.,
\delta^{\prime}+\mu\geqslant 1-\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\varepsilon n}\right)
.
Substituting the above formula for
\delta^{\prime}
and simplifying yields the result.
∎

Corollary 4
.

There do not exist statistically-secure pseudorandom codes for any constant-rate noise channel, relative to any crypto oracle.

Proof.

Suppose that
(\mathsf{KeyGen}^{\mathcal{O}},\mathsf{Enc}^{\mathcal{O}},\mathsf{Dec}^{\mathcal{O}})
is a pseudorandom code for some
(1-\Omega(1))
-noise channel, relative to some crypto oracle
\mathcal{O}
.
Assume it is a zero-bit PRC, by always using the all-zero message and disregarding the decoder’s output.

Define
(\overline{\mathsf{KeyGen}}^{\mathcal{R}},\overline{\mathsf{Enc}}^{\mathcal{R}},\overline{\mathsf{Dec}}^{\mathcal{R}})
to behave identically to
(\mathsf{KeyGen}^{\mathcal{O}},\mathsf{Enc}^{\mathcal{O}},\mathsf{Dec}^{\mathcal{O}})
, except that they simulate
\mathcal{O}
using oracle access to a random function
\mathcal{R}
.
If
\mathcal{R}
is secret—that is, the pseudorandomness adversary is not allowed to query
\mathcal{R}
—then
(\overline{\mathsf{KeyGen}}^{\mathcal{R}},\overline{\mathsf{Enc}}^{\mathcal{R}},\overline{\mathsf{Dec}}^{\mathcal{R}})
is a pseudorandom code.
We can therefore invoke
Theorem

3
with
(\overline{\mathsf{KeyGen}}^{\mathcal{R}},\overline{\mathsf{Enc}}^{\mathcal{R}},\overline{\mathsf{Dec}}^{\mathcal{R}})
to conclude that

\delta+\mu\geqslant 1-\mathsf{negl}\left(\lambda\right),

which means that there is a negligible completeness-soundness gap.
∎
