Sodium Glucose Cotransporter 2 Inhibitor Use in Diabetic Patients With Cirrhosis Improves Liver-Related Outcomes and Survival
Authors: Sajid Jalil, Khurram Bari, Khalid Mumtaz, Ahmed Ibrahim, Talal K. Bhatti, Suvisesh Pothuraju, Mohammad Adam, Honor S. Magon, Nikki Duong, Paul Y. Kwo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
INTRODUCTION:The liver outcomes of sodium glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitor (SGLT-2i) in type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) patients with cirrhosis have not been well studied.METHODS:We conducted a retrospective cohort study (January 1, 2010–December 31, 2025) using 2 databases. Adults aged 18 years or older with T2DM and cirrhosis newly treated with SGLT-2i were identified. Patients were grouped into those receiving SGLT-2i and those not receiving SGLT-2i. Baseline characteristics were compared before and after propensity score matching. The primary outcome was the incidence of hepatic decompensation among SGLT-2i users versus nonusers.RESULTS:The intervention group consisted of 14,231 SGLT-2i users, and the control group consisted of 249,746 non-SGLT-2i users. After 1:1 propensity score matching, use of SGLT-2i was associated with reduced composite outcome of hepatic decompensation compared with control group at 1 year (9.9% vs 12.5%, P = 0.001), at 3 years (16.4% vs 18.1%, P < 0.001), and 5 years (19.9% vs 21.9%, P < 0.001). Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) rates were significantly lower in the SGLT-2i users at 1 year (2.3% vs 3.5%, P = 0.001), at 3 years (2.9% vs 4.2%, P = 0.001), and at 5 years (3.4% vs 4.8%, P = 0.001). There was a 31% reduction in mortality in the SGL-T2i arm (hazard ratio: 0.69; confidence interval: 0.56–0.83, P < 0.001), as compared with the non-SGLT-2i arm. These findings were validated in the EVERSANA database.CONCLUSIONS:Use of SGLT-2i was associated with a reduced composite outcome of hepatic decompensation and mortality among patients with T2DM and cirrhosis compared with non-SGLT-2i users across 2 independent data sets. Prospective randomized controlled trials are needed to confirm these findings.

INTRODUCTION

The prevalence of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is increasing globally and is estimated to be around 9.7% in the United States (1,2). Patients with T2DM are at least twice as likely to develop cirrhosis and tend to have worse outcomes (3). Sodium glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT-2is), including empagliflozin, dapagliflozin, and canagliflozin, are a new class of medications and recommended as first-line agents for the treatment of T2DM, especially in patients with established or high risk for kidney disease or cardiovascular disease (CVD) (4,5).

SGLT-2is promote glycosuria by inhibiting renal glucose reabsorption (5). This simple mechanism of action increases urinary glucose excretion, resulting in an osmotic diuresis, which is associated with multiple complex secondary effects, including improved cardiorenal outcomes (6–9). Although SGLT-2is have moderate benefits in reducing major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs) by 11% in patients with established atherosclerotic CVD, they have robust benefits on reducing hospitalization for heart failure (23%) and progression of renal disease (45%), regardless of existing atherosclerotic CVD or a history of heart failure (9).

The metabolic effects of sodium glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitor (SGLT-2i) are not well studied, although early studies suggest canagliflozin is effective and well tolerated in patients with T2DM and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) (10). SGLT-2i may reduce fat content in the liver and have a potential role in reducing oxidative stress (11). Empagliflozin improved liver steatosis and, more importantly, liver fibrosis in patients with metabolic dysfunction-associated liver disease (MASLD) without T2DM (12).

The number of patients with cirrhosis receiving SGLT-2i is likely to increase, given the rising prevalence of MASLD-related cirrhosis and the expanding use of SGLT-2i in T2DM, a major risk factor for MASLD. Consequently, it is crucial to evaluate the safety of SGLT-2i for patients with liver cirrhosis and their impact on various hepatic decompensations, including hepatorenal syndrome (HRS), spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP), hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), and ascites. A preliminary study has suggested that SGLT2i may be beneficial in those with refractory ascites (13). However, the liver-related outcomes of SGLT-2i in patients with cirrhosis and DM remain unknown and represent a significant knowledge gap.

We evaluated the impact of SGLT-2i on liver-related outcomes, need for liver transplant (LT), and all-cause mortality in T2DM patients with cirrhosis using 2 national healthcare databases.

METHODS

Study setting and data source

We performed a retrospective cohort analysis of adults using 2 databases—the TriNetX and EVERSANA databases spanning from January 2015 to July 2024. We studied the impact of using SGLT-2i in T2DM patients with cirrhosis on various liver-related outcomes. The TriNetX database is a global health research network that provides access to de-identified, retrospective electronic health record (EHR). The database comprises more than 90 million patients from 56 healthcare organizations across the United States. The TriNetX platform allows for comparison between cohorts while accounting for potential confounders, using a propensity score matching (PSM) model. Patients' records were identified using International Classification of Diseases, Ninth or Tenth Edition, Clinical Modification (ICD-9-CM or ICD-10-CM) codes for patients' diagnoses, International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Edition, Procedure Coding System (ICD-10-PCS), and Current Procedural Terminology© codes for procedures; Logical Observation Identifiers Names and Codes for laboratory tests; RxNorm and Veteran Affairs codes for medication use; and TriNetX codes for physical measurements and laboratory data.

EVERSANA Research Network database was used to further validate our findings from the TriNetX database. EVERSANA database comprised de-identified EHRs that have been aggregated and standardized from various sources, containing data from more than 120 million patients in the United States. The data are collected from diverse EHR systems, from more than 2000 outpatient/ambulatory health centers, more than 500 hospitals, and 30 healthcare systems, including academic medical centers. This data set provides a representative overview of healthcare activities across the US population, and the overall distributions of patient demographics align reasonably well with major geographic trends. EHR data were mapped for the observational medical outcomes partnership common data model version 5.3 such that standardized vocabularies, including the anatomical therapeutic chemical classification system, the RxNORM, and the International Classification for Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) were used to define patients and their outcomes. The Stanford Health Care Institutional Review Board granted an informed consent waiver for the study of EHR data. We used STrengthening the Reporting of OBservational studies in Epidemiology Guidelines for this database (14). Owing to the de-identified nature of the data in both databases, informed consent was waived.

Study population

Adult individuals aged 18 years or older with a history of both T2DM (International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, Tenth Revision [ICD-10]: E11) and cirrhosis ICD-10 code: codes K74.6 and K74.69, who were recently started on SGLT-2i (intervention group) (anatomical therapeutic chemical code: A10BK) were compared with those on T2DM treatment without SGLT-2i (control group). Patients using glucagon-like peptide 1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs) (ATC: A10BJ), a history of LT (ICD-10: Z94.4), or HCC were excluded from analysis (Figure 1).

GLP-1RAs are associated with improved liver-related outcomes (15,16); therefore, patients on GLP-1RAs were excluded to reduce confounding effect and selection bias. ICD 10 codes are listed in Supplementary Tables 2 and 3 (see the Supplementary Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/CTG/B582).

Study design

The data on demographics such as age, sex, ethnicity, index year, number of encounters, year of entry into the cohort, and Charlson comorbidity score were extracted. The laboratory variables, including serum sodium, creatinine, total bilirubin, albumin, and international normalized ratio (INR), were used. The model for end-stage liver disease (MELD) 3.0 score was calculated using individual laboratory variables.

We also analyzed patient-level data on EVERSANA database to validate our findings based on the TriNetX database. A cohort of patients with T2DM and cirrhosis on SGLT-2i (intervention) were compared with those not on SGLT-2i therapy (control). There were 14,231 patients and 249,746 patients identified in intervention group and control group, respectively. A 1:1 PSM was performed using all listed variables, including demographics, comorbidities, HMG CoA reductase inhibitors, commonly known as statins, and important laboratory measurements (albumin, bilirubin, platelet count, and INR).

Index event and time window definitions

The index date of inclusion for each patient within a cohort is the day on which the patient first met the selected criteria for the cohort. The time window can start on the same day as the index event or at any specified time interval after the index event.

To mitigate reverse causality and detection bias, all patients were required to have a minimum follow-up period of 1 month after cohort entry. Follow-up continued until the occurrence of outcomes, death or the end of the study period (July 31, 2024), whichever came first.

Study outcomes

The primary outcomes were to assess the impact of SGLT-2i on composite adverse liver-related outcomes and rates of HRS (ICD-10 code K76.7), SBP (ICD-10 code K65.2), and HCC (ICD-10 code C22.0).

Secondary outcomes included MACEs defined as 1 or more of the following: ischemic heart disease, stroke, and heart failure (MACE; ICD-10 code I21, I50, I63), need for LT (Current Procedural Terminology© code: 47135), and mortality among T2DM patients with cirrhosis.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to analyze the data for both the databases. The continuous variables were summarized as mean ± SD using the Welch t test. The categorical variables were grouped into percentages and compared using the χ2 test. To estimate the predictors for SGLT-2i, a logistic-regression PSM was used. The subjects were matched 1:1 for age, sex, race, MELD score, and Charlson comorbidity scores. The balance was confirmed with the standard mean differences. Primary and secondary outcomes for EVERSANA data were reported as incident rate ratios (IRRs), and statistically compared using a Poisson regression model for binary variables. Survival probability was analyzed using Kaplan-Meier survival curves, and hazard ratios (HRs) were calculated using Cox proportional hazards regression. Statistical significance was determined a priori using P value of < 0.05.

We used E-values to estimate the risk of unmeasured confounding. The E-value is an extra measure of statistical validity and is a modern statistical tool that allows for the estimation of the effect size that an unmeasured confounder would need to nullify the measured association (17). The higher the value, the bigger the effect size of the unmeasured confounder would have to be. Therefore, the more unlikely that an unmeasured confounder would have been unaccounted for. All statistically significant results should be interpreted in the context of their reported E-value, which ranged from quite fragile (1.1) to strong (2.7), where lower E-values suggest that a nonvisible confounder contributes to the result.

For TriNetX data, statistical analysis was performed in situ within the TriNetX platform. Normally distributed baseline characteristics are presented as mean and SD. Risk ratio (risk for cohort 1/risk for cohort 2) and 95% CIs are presented. The t test and χ2 statistical testing were conducted for differences in outcomes between cohorts. P < 0.05 was statistically significant.

PSM was performed to mitigate confounding variables and to enhance validity. Patients were matched for age at index, sex and race, body mass index (BMI), comorbid conditions such as MACEs, severity of liver disease, and use of diabetic medications and statins.

Missing data.

We did not perform imputation or backfilling unless explicitly stated in the methods. Owing to the heterogeneity of EHR data, imputation was considered more likely to introduce error than to provide benefit. For survival analyses and selected binary outcomes, right-censoring was applied, resulting in a decreasing denominator over time because patients were lost to follow-up. This censoring was applied after accounting for the required minimum follow-up period; in the absence of such a requirement, only right-censoring was used.

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics

There were a total of 263,977 patients with T2DM, who met the inclusion criteria of cirrhosis. The intervention group consisted of 14,231 SGLT-2i users, and the control group consisted of 249,746 non-SGLT-2i users. At baseline, patients in the SGLT-2i group were older (64.4 ± 10.8 vs 63.3 ± 11.8 years), predominantly male (61.6% vs 57.3%), White (70.6% vs 70.2%), and had similar BMI (31.8 ± 7.4 vs 31.2 ± 7.7). The SGLT-2i group had a higher prevalence of comorbid conditions, including congestive heart failure (38.8% vs 18.3%) and ischemic heart disease (46.1% vs 24.5%) (Table 1). After 1:1 PSM, 14,199 patients were distributed equally between each group. Matching variables include age, sex, race, BMI, comorbidities, medications (such as statins, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, and diuretics), and severity of liver disease based on laboratory values (Table 1). Primary outcomes

Hepatic decompensation.

The composite outcome of hepatic decompensation was significantly lower in the SGLT-2i group compared with the control group at 1 year (9.9% vs 12.5%, adjusted hazard ratio [aHR] 0.73, confidence interval [CI] 0.69–0.79, P = 0.001), at 3 years (16.4% vs 18.1%, aHR 0.79, CI 0.75–0.85, P < 0.001), and 5 years (19.9% vs 21.9%, aHR 0.86, CI 0.81–0.91, P < 0.001).

Hepatocellular carcinoma.

HCC rates were significantly lower in the SGLT-2i users at 1 year (2.3% vs 3.5%, aHR 0.62, CI 0.54–0.72, P = 0.001), at 3 years (2.9% vs 4.2%, aHR 0.65, CI 0.57–0.74, P = 0.001), and at 5 years (3.4% vs 4.8%, aHR 0.68, CI 0.61–0.77, P = 0.001) (Table 2).

Secondary outcomes

Need for LT.

LT rates were significantly lower in the SGLT-2i users at 1 year (0.4% vs 1.5%, aHR 0.24, CI 0.18–0.32, P = 0.001), at 3 years (0.9% vs 1.7%, aHR 0.44, CI 0.36–0.55, P < 0.001), and at 5 years (1.4% vs 2.0%, aHR 0.69, CI 0.57–0.82, P < 0.001).

Major adverse cardiovascular events.

The incidence of MACE was significantly lower in the SGLT-2i group at 1 year (6.9% vs 8.7%, aHR 0.72, CI 0.64–0.81, P = 0.001), at 3 years (13.0% vs 14.7%, aHR 0.75, CI 0.69–0.82, P < 0.001), and at 5 years (17.5% vs 20.0%, aHR 0.80, CI 0.74–0.87, P < 0.001).

Renal dysfunction.

Renal dysfunction was significantly lower in the SGLT-2i group at 1 year (8.1% vs 12.6%, aHR 0.61, CI 0.56–0.66, P < 0.001), and at 3 years and 5 years (both 15.8% vs 20.7%, aHR 0.69, CI 0.64–0.74, P < 0.001).

Death.

The death rates were significantly lower in the SGLT-2i group at 1 year (12.7% vs 18.7%, aHR 0.62, CI 0.59–0.66, P = 0.001), at 3 years (20.9% vs 26.7%, aHR 0.68, CI 0.65–0.71, P < 0.001), and at 5 years (25.1% vs 33.5%, aHR 0.69, CI 0.66–0.72, P < 0.001) (Table 2).

Overall mortality.

The Kaplan-Meier survival curves show statistical significance (log-rank test P < 0.001). The Cox regression analysis models revealed lower risk of mortality in SGLT-2i users compared with the controls (HR: 0.69; CI: 0.56–0.83, P < 0.001). Moreover, the 5-year mortality was lower in the SGLT-2i user (11.74%) as compared with SGLT-2i nonuser (15.09%, P < 0.001). The restricted mean survival time (9 years) for long-term use was 2,624.6 days for SGLT-2i users as compared with 2,375.5 days for the control group, resulting in a survival gain of 249 days (Figure 2).

Hepatorenal syndrome.

The incidence rate ratio of HRS was significantly lower in SGLT-2i user compared with the control group at 1 year (0.57% vs 1.85%, P = 0.001), at 3 years (1.28% vs 2.42%, P = 0.01), and at 5 years (1.64% vs 2.5%, P = 0.049). The E-value ranged from 1.6 to 2.8, suggesting there is a low chance of a confounding factor affecting the reliability of the results. The IRR was significantly lower in the SGLT-2i group (P < 0.01). The IRR for HRS was 0.31 (CI: 0.16–0.59) at 1 year, 0.47 (CI: 0.29–0.77) at 3 years, and 0.54 (CI 0.35–0.87) at 5 years in propensity score matched cohorts (Table 3).

Mortality.

There was a 31% reduction in mortality in the SGL-T2i arm (HR: 0.69; CI: 0.56–0.83, P < 0.001), as compared with the non-SGLT-2i arm (Figure 3).

Outcomes based on cirrhosis etiology.

In this etiology-based subgroup, SGLT-2i use among MASLD-related cirrhosis was associated with lower risks of mortality (aHR 0.55, 95% CI 0.45–0.67, P < 0.001), hepatic decompensation (aHR 0.63, 95% CI 0.57–0.70, P < 0.001), HCC (aHR 0.67, 95% CI 0.54–0.83, P < 0.001), and renal dysfunction (aHR 0.52, 95% CI 0.46–0.59, P < 0.001) (see Table 4). Similar but attenuated benefits were observed among patients with alcohol-related cirrhosis, with significant reductions in mortality, hepatic decompensation, and renal dysfunction.

DISCUSSION

The use of SGLT-2i has increased 2-fold from 2013 to 2020 since their US FDA approval, attributed to their preventive advantages against cardiovascular and renal diseases, independent of type 2 diabetes mellitus (18–20). Apart from their glucose-lowering properties, SGLT-2is are gaining increased interest in the management of patients with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) and cirrhosis, particularly given their favorable cardiometabolic profile and impact on hepatic steatosis (8,21). Using 2 large national databases, we showed that SGLT-2i use is associated with a significantly reduced risk of adverse liver outcomes (HRS, SBP, and HCC), need for liver transplantation, and all-cause mortality. These findings are congruent with previous studies reporting reductions in hepatic steatosis, liver stiffness, and aminotransferase levels in patients with MASLD treated with SGLT-2i (10,12,22). Although most earlier studies focused on noncirrhotic populations, our results uniquely suggest that the benefits of SGLT-2i may extend to patients with more advanced liver disease who are at higher risk of adverse liver outcomes. The association between SGLT-2i use and improved clinical outcomes was most pronounced among patients with MASLD-related cirrhosis. By contrast, among patients with viral cirrhosis, outcomes generally favored SGLT-2 inhibitor use; however, most associations did not reach statistical significance. Overall, these findings suggest that the therapeutic benefits of SGLT-2i may be greatest in metabolically driven liver disease, particularly MASLD-related cirrhosis.

SGLT-2i activates tubule-glomerular feedback and reduces hyperfiltration-driven kidney damage, which is considered a key mechanism (23). SGLT-2i blocks the reabsorption of glucose and sodium in the renal proximal tubule, leading to natriuresis and osmotic diuresis (24). SGLT-2 inhibitors improve the kidney's energy substrate use and delivery, reduce sympathetic activity, and enhance microvascular function. This results in a significant delay in the development of microalbuminuria and proteinuria (7). These effects are also observed in nondiabetic individuals.

Evolving data suggest that SGLT-2i may significantly ameliorate liver fibrosis, liver fat content levels, as well as inflammatory markers in MASLD and insulin resistance (25,26). These beneficial effects may reduce the risk of liver fibrosis progression and have a positive impact on MASLD patients with T2DM, and in part, may explain the benefits we observed in our cohorts. The use of SGLT-2is has been found to be safe about common adverse effects and may contribute to improved hepatic functioning (27). A recent meta-analysis suggests SGLT2i could reduce liver stiffness measurement and controlled attenuated parameter in contrast to other antihyperglycemic drugs (28).

Since decompensated liver cirrhosis and congestive heart failure share common pathophysiological features, namely renin-angiotensin-aldosterone axis and sympathetic nervous system activation as well as vasopressin secretion, SGLT-2i could also be beneficial in patients with decompensated cirrhosis, even in the absence of T2DM (29,30). Limited data suggest these medications promote natriuresis and improve ascites in patients with cirrhosis (13,31,32). Indeed, SGLT-2is were shown to improve survival in patients with cirrhosis who require additional pharmacotherapy for diabetes mellitus beyond metformin (33). In a propensity score-matched intention-to-treat analysis among veterans, who received either SGLT-2i or dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, SGLT-2i users had significantly reduced risk for death (33). Emerging evidence from recent retrospective studies suggests that SGLT-2 inhibitor use in diabetic patients with cirrhosis is associated with improved liver-related outcomes and survival (34–36). In patients with MASLD and T2DM, SGLT-2i use was associated with lower risks of HCC and all-cause mortality compared with active comparators (37).

The mechanisms underlying these benefits are likely multifactorial. In cirrhosis, SGLT-2i-induced natriuresis mimics the effect of diuretics by promoting salt excretion, reducing extracellular fluid volume, and potentially decreasing ascites formation (38). Indeed, a recent study suggests that adding an SGLT-2i to dual diuretic therapies may reduce serious liver events by approximately one-third and hospital stay (39). Unlike conventional diuretics, SGLT-2is exert minimal risk of electrolyte disturbances such as hypokalemia or significant hypotension. Their antiproteinuric effect may also preserve serum albumin, which is relevant given that hypoalbuminemia is a known risk factor for SBP (40). Regarding renal outcomes, SGLT-2i reduces intraglomerular pressure and proteinuria, improves volume status, and thereby may avoid the risk of HRS in cirrhotic patients (41–43). The observed reduction in HCC risk is novel and warrants further investigation, with plausible mechanisms involving improved insulin sensitivity, reduced lipotoxicity, and modulation of chronic inflammation, factors known to contribute to hepatic carcinogenesis (18,19).

Despite these promising findings, caution is warranted. Patients with decompensated cirrhosis are often excluded from SGLT-2i trials because of concerns about volume depletion, ketoacidosis, and infection risk. However, several real-world studies, including recent retrospective analyses and case series, have demonstrated tolerability in this population, especially when initiated at lower doses with close monitoring (31,33). From a practical standpoint, SGLT-2i may represent a favorable alternative to agents such as metformin, which are frequently withheld in cirrhosis because of risks of lactic acidosis and poor gastrointestinal tolerance (44). Although the study provides valuable insights, certain limitations must be acknowledged. The follow-up period for patients receiving SGLT-2i is relatively short, largely due to their recent market introduction. Clinical indices for cirrhosis severity such as MELD score or Child Pugh class are not available in TrinetX database. We used individual MELD laboratory test results such as bilirubin, INR, creatinine, and platelets as surrogates for cirrhosis severity. In addition, we used propensity score matching to ensure the 2 cohorts are comparable in cirrhosis severity. EVERSANA database included the MELD score where the 2 cohorts were matched based on the MELD score (see Supplementary Table 1, Supplementary Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/CTG/B582).

Despite PSM analysis, residual confounding may persist in our data. Other confounding variables such as socioeconomic status, medication adherence, and frequency of alcohol use were not assessed. The use of 2 independent data sets inherently introduces the possibility of confounding. Differences in study populations (e.g., demographics, disease severity, or baseline comorbidities), methods of data collection (including coding systems, laboratory assays, and timing of measurements), and outcome ascertainment may limit direct comparability. Variability in healthcare settings, clinical practices, and follow-up duration can further bias associations. In addition, differences in data completeness, handling of missing values, and quality of documentation across data sets may introduce systematic error. Such limitations are inherent to this type of study design. In addition, distinguishing between liver-specific mortality and all-cause mortality is important to determine whether the observed survival benefit reflects true hepatic protection; however, we could not get this information because of database limitations.

Our study adds to the growing literature suggesting that SGLT2i may have a pleiotropic effect in patients with diabetes and liver cirrhosis, addressing underlying metabolic dysfunction while potentially reducing hepatic complications and improving survival. Our study extends previous findings and suggests multiorgan benefits, including effects on MACE, adverse hepatic outcomes, HCC, reduced need for LT and overall survival, across 2 separate databases. These findings underscore the need for prospective randomized controlled multicenter trials to evaluate long-term hepatic safety and outcomes in those at risk for development of hepatic decompensation. Until then, a personalized approach targeting compensated patients with close clinical follow-up may be a reasonable and safe strategy.

In summary, use of SGLT-2i in this cohort of T2DM patients with cirrhosis was associated with reduced risk of adverse liver outcomes, lesser need for LT and improved survival benefit, with findings validated in 2 large data sets. Prospective randomized controlled trials are warranted to confirm these findings. Future studies may potentially focus on magnitude of effect, assess safety in cirrhosis, and its impact on extrahepatic outcomes such as cardiovascular events and kidney function.
