Development of a portable online nondestructive detection device for apple watercore based on visible/near-infrared spectroscopy
Authors: Yong Lin, Haijian Wu, Chunlin Zhao, Wenbin Zhang, Zhipeng Yin, Zikang Cao, Yaxing Ma, Yue Lu, Liju Liu, Din Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractVisible and near-infrared (Vis/NIR) spectroscopy has been widely applied in fruit quality detection due to its advantages of rapid efficiency, non-invasiveness, and suitability for detecting opaque samples. To address the issue of whether apple watercore occurs during the growth and maturation of apples, a portable on-line nondestructive detection device based on Vis/NIR spectroscopy was designed to achieve accurate detection of apple watercore. The device employs the AIOX2000-13 spectrometer as the detection unit, with an STM32F103VET6 ARM-based processor as the main control chip, and integrates a 4G wireless communication module to establish a stable data transmission channel between the processor and the computer. This structure ensures the efficient and stable transmission of apple spectral data and detection results, thereby meeting the need for in-field nondestructive detection of apple watercore on apple trees. The system is based on a self-designed spectral data acquisition mechanism and uses a transmission detection method to collect spectral data from 500 ‘Fuji’ apple samples in two directions. The spectral data were preprocessed using Standard Normal Variate (SNV), and the dataset was divided using the Spectral Projection based on X–Y distances (SPXY) algorithm. Important feature wavelengths related to apple watercore were extracted by combining the Uninformative Variable Elimination method with the Successive Projections Algorithm (UVE-SPA). Subsequently, a detection model, SNV-UVE-SPA-SVM, was constructed using a Support Vector Machine (SVM) optimized by the Honey Badger Algorithm (HBA), achieving a test set accuracy of 96%. After research and analysis, Direction 1 was identified as the optimal acquisition direction, and field verification was conducted on 50 apple samples, with a detection accuracy of 94%. The results show that the detection device has the advantages of portability, high efficiency, and suitability for in-field detection, making it suitable for the rapid in-field detection of apple watercore.Keywords: Portable, Online nondestructive detection, Apple watercore, Visible/near-infrared spectroscopy, Embedded systemSubject terms: Engineering, Optics and photonics

Introduction

The apple industry makes a significant contribution to agricultural economics1. It is a major fruit in many countries around the world, with stable production and consumption in Europe, while apple production in Asia and Africa is rapidly increasing2,3. Apples are rich in water, sugars, vitamins, minerals, and antioxidants, providing substantial nutritional benefits that help maintain health and enhance immunity4. The quality of apples is influenced by numerous factors5,6, with apple watercore being one of the key factors. Apple watercore is a physiological disorder, also known as “sugar core disease” or “honeyed fruit disease”7. Watercore apples, as a type of high-quality apple, have a unique taste, low yield, and are highly favored by consumers8. The main cause of watercore formation is the large day-night temperature difference in high-altitude regions, which prevents the timely conversion of sorbitol transported by leaves to apples, causing it to accumulate in large amounts around the core and vascular bundles, forming watercore9. The primary manifestation of watercore is the presence of translucent watery substances between the fruit cells around the core and vascular bundles during the maturation stage of the apple10. Apple watercore is completely unobservable during the apple’s growth period, but the product value of watercore apples is higher than that of normal apples. Therefore, accurately detecting whether apples have watercore during their growth and maturation is crucial for guiding apple harvesting, increasing the value of individual apples, and enhancing the income of fruit growers.

Currently, the detection of apple watercore mainly relies on manual cutting and inspection or empirical judgment based on the apple’s surface11. These methods inevitably cause damage to the apple, leading to certain economic losses. To achieve nondestructive detection, some scholars both domestically and internationally have attempted to use techniques such as thermal imaging, CT scanning, and nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR)12,13. Although these methods avoid fruit destruction to some extent and enable nondestructive detection of watercore, they still face issues such as high time consumption, high costs, and low identification accuracy, making them difficult to widely apply14. With the development of visible/near-infrared (Vis/NIR) spectroscopy technology15, Visible/Near-infrared spectroscopy, due to its high efficiency, non-invasiveness, and ease of operation16, has been widely applied in recent years for the internal quality assessment of fruits and vegetables17–19. Guo Zhimin et al.20 reviewed the application of near-infrared spectroscopy and imaging technology in nondestructive detection of fruits, covering quality parameters such as sugar content and moisture, and improving detection accuracy by combining various algorithms, emphasizing the advantages of hyperspectral imaging in integrating spatial and spectral information. Khodabakhshian R et al.21 used Vis/NIR spectroscopy combined with multivariate regression methods to accurately predict the maturity and internal quality of pomegranate fruits. Mancini M et al.22 applied Vis/NIR spectroscopy and Partial Least Squares Regression (PLSR) models for rapid detection of sugar content and hardness in strawberries. Egei M et al.23 used Vis-NIR spectroscopy to predict soluble solids and lycopene content in tomatoes, verifying its feasibility in the quality control of processed tomatoes. Furthermore, Ghooshkhaneh NG et al.24 combined NIR spectroscopy with Support Vector Machine (SVM) technology to effectively identify heart rot disease in citrus caused by Alternaria alternata, demonstrating the potential of near-infrared technology for early diagnosis of fruit diseases. These studies indicate that near-infrared spectroscopy has become an important tool for rapid, nondestructive evaluation of fruit quality. Of course, this technology has also yielded abundant research results in apple detection. Tian X et al.25 studied spectral data in the wavelength range of 500–1000 nm by analyzing the spectral data of moldy and healthy apples. Using Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and Partial Least Squares Regression (PLSR) models, they achieved an accuracy of over 92% in classifying moldy and healthy apples. Huang Hua et al.26 studied a method for predicting soluble solids content in mature apples based on Vis/NIR spectroscopy and functional linear regression models. By analyzing spectral data from 400–1000 nm and using functional linear regression models, they achieved a prediction accuracy of over 97.5% in the validation set. Zhao C et al.27 improved the accuracy of apple watercore detection using Vis/NIR spectroscopy combined with Gramian Angular Field (GAF) encoding technology and ConvNeXt deep learning networks. Chang H et al.28 proposed a method for online evaluation of apple watercore using Vis/NIR spectroscopy. The study used spectral data from 400–1000 nm and applied PLSR models to effectively differentiate apples with and without watercore, achieving an accuracy of 92.5%. Yin Z et al.29 proposed a nondestructive detection method for apple watercore based on a three-dimensional apple model. Using a complete 3D watercore model as a reference, they further integrated near-infrared spectroscopy with the GAF-ConvNeXt algorithm to achieve five-level nondestructive detection of watercore.Guo Junxian et al.30 studied a method for identifying Xinjiang ice sugar core ‘Fuji’ apples with watercore based on manifold learning and near-infrared transmission spectroscopy. The method combined manifold learning algorithms to extract feature information related to apple watercore and effectively distinguish between healthy apples and apples with watercore. The above studies primarily used Vis/NIR spectroscopy combined with advanced mathematical modeling methods to achieve high-precision, high-stability, and nondestructive detection of apple quality. However, most of these studies were limited to laboratory settings, with large and complex equipment, making it difficult to meet the demand for rapid on-site detection. Research on portable detection equipment remains relatively weak.

To address the above issues, recent studies have focused on the development of portable detection devices based on visible/near-infrared (Vis/NIR) spectroscopy technology. Guo Ya et al.31 developed a portable nondestructive apple sugar content detector using near-infrared spectroscopy technology. The device operates in the 800–1100 nm near-infrared spectral range and uses a Partial Least Squares Regression (PLSR) model to predict sugar content, enabling rapid and nondestructive detection of apple sugar content with an accuracy exceeding 93%. This portable device is compact, easy to operate, and suitable for on-site rapid detection. Fan S et al.32 developed a portable Vis/NIR device for nondestructively evaluating the soluble solids content of apples. The device uses near-infrared spectroscopy combined with PLSR models to predict the soluble solids content of apples. The portable device offers good accuracy and stability, is lightweight, easy to operate, and is suitable for on-site rapid detection. Guo Z et al.33 independently developed a portable Vis/NIR transmission system and combined it with chemometric methods to perform nondestructive detection of quality indicators such as soluble solids content, watercore degree, firmness, and pH value of apples. This study improved prediction accuracy by preprocessing spectral data and using the CARS-CNN model, providing a convenient and efficient nondestructive detection method for apple quality evaluation with potential for on-site application. These studies have used Vis/NIR spectroscopy’s characteristics of being nondestructive, efficient, and environmentally friendly to achieve nondestructive detection of internal indicators such as apple sugar content, soluble solids, and watercore content. However, several issues still remain: First, although portability has been achieved to some extent, the device is still too bulky, making it inconvenient to operate; Second, the apples need to be harvested before detection, and real-time, on-site detection and analysis on the tree are not yet possible; Third, the detection devices still require connection to a computer via data cables, preventing long-distance and rapid data transmission and analysis. Therefore, the development of a portable, efficient, and on-site nondestructive apple watercore detection device is of significant importance. This device would not only enhance apple management, improve harvesting and sorting efficiency, but also enable the grading and price increase of apples, promoting the apple industry’s development toward refinement and higher quality.

Based on the above analysis, this study designs a portable, online nondestructive apple watercore detection device based on visible/near-infrared (Vis/NIR) spectroscopy technology and microcontroller technology: (1) Portability. The instrument’s housing dimensions are only 147 × 91 × 63 mm, and it is simple to operate; (2) On-site and Real-time. The instrument enables real-time, on-site detection and result analysis of apple watercore directly on the tree; (3) Efficient and Accurate. The instrument establishes a stable and efficient connection between the ARM core processor and the computer using a 4G wireless module, and uses the SNV-UVE-SPA-SVM model for accurate detection of apple watercore. Design of ARM processing module

This module mainly consists of the STM32F103VET6 minimum system, a 0.96-inch OLED display, and the Silverloda air724 wireless transmission module. The STM32F103VET6 features a high-performance 32-bit ARM Cortex-M3 core with a clock frequency of up to 72 MHz, allowing for high-speed processing of various instructions and data. Design of the spectral collection module

The light source of this instrument must meet the following conditions: first, the spectral range should be no less than 350–1050 nm; second, the illuminance of the light source must be sufficient to transmit through the apple; third, the manufacturing cost should be low; and fourth, the light source must have stable output. Currently, LED lights with a wavelength range of 350–1050 nm are rare and expensive, and the illuminance of the suitable wavelength LED light bulbs cannot meet the measurement requirements. Considering the practical needs, the OSRAM MR16 halogen lamp cup, with dimensions of 50 × 50 × 37 mm, a power of 20 W, and a working voltage of 12 V, was chosen between the halogen lamp and LED light sources. Combined with the portable visible/near-infrared testing instrument for red grapes designed by Gao Sheng et al.35, the vacuum suction ball paired with the PEG-25 N vacuum suction cup was selected to ensure that the spectral collection probe adheres to the apple surface and reduce interference from external light on spectral data collection. To ensure the vacuum suction cup’s adhesion time, a 0.5 mm diameter flat stainless steel needle is built in to slowly balance the air pressure inside and outside the vacuum suction ball.

Based on the positional relationship between the light source and the collection probe, near-infrared nondestructive testing can be divided into two collection positions: diffuse reflection and diffuse transmission36. In diffuse reflection collection, the light source and collection probe are on the same side. This method has been applied earlier and more frequently in fruit quality detection and is suitable for static detection. However, due to the smooth surface of apples, diffuse reflection collection generates specular reflection signals, which are also collected by the probe and sent to the spectrometer, thereby reducing detection accuracy. In addition, due to the effects of light intensity and vascular bundle distribution, the sugar content on the sunny side of the apple is higher than that on the shaded side, and the areas near the fruit skin are higher than those inside, with the area near the calyx being higher than the area near the stem. Therefore, apples are biological entities with uneven internal component distribution. Diffuse reflection collection can only obtain spectral information from the shallow layers of the apple’s flesh and cannot provide information on the entire apple. In contrast, in diffuse transmission collection, the spectrometer and light source are positioned on opposite sides of the apple, allowing the spectrometer to collect spectral signals that pass through the sample. Liu Yande et al.37 compared the effects of various preprocessing methods on the detection accuracy of the two collection methods. Experimental results indicated that diffuse transmission detection significantly outperforms diffuse reflection detection. Qin Kai et al.38 used an LDA discriminant model to predict and determine the apple orientation, finding the highest accuracy when the apple was placed along the equatorial surface with the stem facing upward. Based on the above considerations, the collection method for this instrument is diffuse transmission. During field testing, the probe and light source are positioned along the equatorial surface of the apple. The physical image of this module is shown in Fig. 2-(g).

Materials and methods

Instrument collection method

During apple watercore collection, the instrument housing is worn using a waist-mounted method. An elastic strap slightly narrower than the height of the housing is used to vertically secure the instrument to the abdomen. After powering on the device, the display module indicates that all components are ready. The vacuum ball is pressed to attach the vacuum suction cup to the equatorial surface of the apple to be tested. Once attached, the vacuum ball is gently supported. With the other hand, the handle is held, and the light source is placed according to Direction 1 (Fig. 5). Then, the collection command control button (Fig. 2-d) is pressed. When the display module indicates that the apple watercore data collection is completed (approximately 1 s), the spectral data transmission button (Fig. 2-d) is pressed to send the spectral data to the computer and import it into the prediction model (approximately 2 s). After the model completes the prediction on the collected sample data, the result is saved to the computer and transmitted back to the display screen via the 4G module for real-time display. This completes the data collection for one sample. To collect the next sample, simply repeat the procedure.

Measurement direction.

Experimental samples and data collection

The test samples were Fuji apples from Ninglang, Lijiang, Yunnan. According to Jiang Daowei et al.39, the optimal harvest time for Fuji apples varies among different cultivars, generally falling between September and October. Based on the local farmers’ harvesting schedule, the experimental period was set to 25 days (from October 12, 2024, to November 5, 2024). A portable instrument was used to collect visible/near-infrared spectral data from 20 apple samples per day. Following the farmers’ harvesting progress, apples from the corresponding planting areas were collected daily under natural conditions. To reduce experimental error, each sample was measured three times, and the average value was taken as the final spectral data for that apple. To further investigate the effect of light transmission direction on measurement results, each apple sample was measured at two positions: with the light source and probe aligned 180 degrees (Direction 1) and 90 degrees (Direction 2) along the equatorial surface, as shown in Fig. 5.

Based on the actual situation, a total of 500 apple samples were collected during the entire data collection period. After data sorting, it was found that a large proportion of the samples contained watercore. To further ensure the accuracy of the apple watercore discrimination model, 200 sample data points from Direction 1 and 200 sample data points from Direction 2 were randomly selected from the dataset to establish the apple watercore discrimination model. As shown in Fig. 6, the dataset used in this study contains an equal number of normal apples and apples with watercore, with a ratio of 1:1.

Spectral processing and model optimization methods

Preprocessing and dataset splitting method

In order to eliminate noise, reduce irrelevant information, and improve the accuracy of the model, data preprocessing is necessary. The main preprocessing methods used in this paper include: Min-Max scaling (MMS), Savitzky-Golay convolution smoothing (S_G smoothing), standardization, and Standard Normal Variate (SNV) transformation. MMS is a commonly used data processing method, also known as data scaling or normalization. Its goal is to scale the data to a specified range, usually [0, 1], ensuring that the value ranges of different features are consistent. This helps improve the model’s performance and prevents certain features from having an excessive impact on the model. S_G smoothing is a method commonly used in signal processing and data smoothing. Standardization is a widely used data preprocessing technique aimed at transforming the data into a standard normal distribution with a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1. This is achieved by calculating the difference between each data point and the mean of its corresponding feature, and then dividing by the standard deviation. This method is suitable for scenarios where it is necessary to eliminate scale differences between features, improve model stability, and enhance interpretability. It is commonly used in machine learning and statistical analysis. The goal of SNV is to eliminate or reduce systematic shifts in spectral data, enhance spectral features, and improve the performance of the data in model training and analysis. The calculation formula for SNV is the same as that for standardization, with the difference being that the standardization algorithm processes a set of spectra, i.e., it works on the columns of the spectral data matrix, whereas the SNV algorithm operates on the rows of the spectral data matrix.

The KS algorithm is a classic sample selection method used to choose representative samples from a given dataset. The algorithm selects sample pairs that are the farthest apart in terms of Euclidean distance, ensuring that the selected samples are as spread out as possible across the entire dataset space. The specific steps are as follows: First, two samples are randomly chosen from the dataset as starting points. Then, the Euclidean distance between the remaining samples and the already selected samples is calculated, and the sample with the greatest Euclidean distance is chosen as a new representative sample. This process is repeated until the desired number of samples is selected. The SPXY algorithm is developed based on the KS algorithm. In the SPXY algorithm, both the x and y variables are taken into account when calculating the distance between samples. This paper primarily uses the SPXY algorithm. Prior to dataset partitioning, samples collected on different dates were mixed to avoid chronological bias. Moreover, apples with and without watercore were distributed throughout the entire 25-day sampling period, ensuring that temporal variation did not systematically correspond to specific class labels.

Feature extraction method

Although the spectral data have undergone preliminary preprocessing to remove part of the irrelevant noise, they still cannot be directly used due to the large data volume and the presence of redundant information and noise. In order to construct an efficient and accurate apple watercore classification model, it is necessary to perform effective data compression on the spectral data to reduce the data size while retaining the most informative features. The feature extraction methods adopted in this study mainly include Uninformative Variables Elimination (UVE), Successive Projections Algorithm (SPA), and Competitive Adaptive Reweighted Sampling (CARS). UVE is a feature selection method that identifies informative spectral variables by statistically analyzing the regression coefficients of spectral variables combined with noise variables. After adding noise, a matrix consisting of spectral variables and noise variables is used to perform regression on the target matrix. The statistical distribution of the regression coefficients is evaluated using the ratio of mean to standard deviation, and variables within a determined threshold range are selected as feature variables. SPA is a classical feature selection algorithm that projects data onto one dimension step-by-step, selects the feature that maximizes the variance after projection, removes the corresponding dimension, and repeats the process until the required number of features is selected. CARS is a widely used method in spectral analysis, chemometrics, and machine learning. It is based on the Adaptive Re-weighting Sampling (ARS) strategy. The core objective of CARS is to identify the optimal combination of variables that maximize the predictive and explanatory power of the model while minimizing the risk of overfitting.

Pattern recognition method

Support Vector Machine (SVM) is a modeling method first proposed by Corinna Cortes, Vapnik, and others in 199540, and it has been widely applied ever since. Due to the advantages of SVM classification algorithms, such as fast training speed, high accuracy, adaptability to small samples, and strong generalization ability, the algorithm has been extensively used in various fields since its introduction. SVM has two important parameters: the penalty coefficient c and the kernel function parameter g. c is used to control the balance between model complexity and training error, while g determines the “range” of the projection of the SVM in feature space. The selection of these two parameters has a significant impact on the performance of the SVM. This paper uses the Honey Badger Algorithm (HBA)41 to optimize SVM and find the optimal values for c and g. The following will refer to SVM directly, and the details of the HBA algorithm will not be elaborated.

Results and discussion

In this study, to improve the accuracy of apple watercore detection models, we compared several spectral preprocessing methods, including Min-Max Scaling (MMS), Savitzky–Golay smoothing (S-G smoothing), normalization, and Standard Normal Variate (SNV). A total of 200 apple samples were used, consisting of 100 watercore and 100 normal apples, and visible/near-infrared (Vis/NIR) spectra were collected from two directions. The dataset was divided using the SPXY algorithm with a 3:1 training-to-test ratio. Table 2 presents the classification accuracy of different methods on the training and test sets. Unprocessed spectra achieved 100% accuracy on the training set but dropped sharply on the test set to 30% for Direction 1 and 46% for Direction 2, indicating severe overfitting rather than underfitting. This phenomenon is primarily attributed to noise and inter-sample variability in the raw spectra, which limited the generalization capability of the model.

Among the preprocessing methods, MMS and normalization partially reduced the gap between training and test accuracies; for Direction 1, the test set accuracy increased to 86% and 88%, respectively. Although S-G smoothing effectively suppressed high-frequency noise, the test set accuracy improved only slightly (32% and 46%), suggesting that smoothing alone cannot sufficiently enhance watercore-related spectral features. In contrast, SNV provided the best balance, effectively mitigating spectral scattering and baseline shifts while enhancing watercore-related features. As a result, SNV achieved high stability and improved generalization performance, with training and test accuracies of 99.33% and 92% for Direction 1, and 100% and 82% for Direction 2, demonstrating high stability and accuracy across both datasets.

Figure 8 illustrates the preprocessed spectra for Direction 1. The overall trend of the spectra remained consistent before and after preprocessing. In the raw spectra (Fig. 8a), clear absorption peaks are evident, demonstrating that the portable device can reliably acquire spectra even under outdoor conditions. However, peak intensity alone is insufficient for direct watercore identification, necessitating the development of a classification model. The SVM classification results in Table 2 further confirm that preprocessing significantly improves accuracy, with SNV performing best for both directions. Therefore, SNV was selected as the preprocessing method for the model.

Apple spectral data and preprocessing results.

Direction 1 corresponds to a 180° angle between the acquisition probe and the light source, allowing the collection of spectra that penetrate most of the apple, whereas Direction 2 corresponds to a 90° angle, which cannot capture spectra covering the entire apple. Since watercore is unevenly distributed, primarily extending outward from the apple core along the vascular bundles, spectra collected from Direction 1 carry more comprehensive information regarding watercore, improving classification accuracy. SNV also performs better for Direction 1 than Direction 2; thus, Direction 1 was chosen as the data acquisition orientation for the portable device.

In the apple watercore detection model, feature wavelength selection was performed on the preprocessed spectral data to improve model accuracy and reduce redundant information. Each spectrum acquired by the portable device contains 701 data points, and the measured spectra still include some irrelevant noise, which may adversely affect watercore classification accuracy and increase the computational burden of the model. Therefore, effective feature extraction methods are required to select the most discriminative wavelength bands for model construction, thereby improving detection speed and the operational efficiency of the portable device.

In this study, several feature wavelength selection methods were employed, including Uninformative Variables Elimination (UVE), Successive Projections Algorithm (SPA), Competitive Adaptive Reweighted Sampling (CARS), and two hybrid methods (UVE-SPA and CARS-SPA), to extract key information related to apple watercore from the preprocessed spectral data. The UVE method selected 198 feature wavelengths covering the entire spectral range of 350–1050 nm, providing a comprehensive representation of the chemical and physical characteristics associated with watercore formation42. These wavelengths are mainly distributed in the visible and near-infrared regions and are closely related to the optical absorption properties of water, sugars, and tissue structure in apple flesh43. Specifically, the 600–750 nm region primarily reflects sugar content and light scattering characteristics, while the 750–830 nm region is closely associated with water content and changes in cellular tissue structure. By integrating previous research findings, the physiological significance of these feature wavelengths in watercore formation and its spectral response mechanism is further clarified, thereby enhancing the interpretability of the model.

The SPA method extracted eight feature wavelengths mainly concentrated in the 600–900 nm range, which is sensitive to variations in sugar and water distribution in apple flesh44. The CARS method selected 22 feature wavelengths, most of which are located near spectral absorption peaks and are closely related to water and sugar absorption characteristics in the visible and near-infrared regions45. To further reduce data dimensionality while retaining key information, the hybrid methods UVE-SPA and CARS-SPA were introduced, yielding 14 and 6 feature wavelengths, respectively. As shown in Fig. 9(a)–(e), the feature wavelengths selected by UVE are distributed across the entire 350–1050 nm range, whereas those selected by the other four methods are mainly concentrated in the 600–900 nm region and are predominantly located near spectral peaks. This indicates that the selected wavelengths have clear physiological significance, corresponding to absorption features related to water content, sugar content, and light scattering in apple flesh, thereby improving the accuracy and efficiency of the SVM-based watercore detection model.

Classification results.

To evaluate the impact of SVM parameter optimization on model performance, this study focused on a comparative analysis of the test set results, while keeping the training set accuracy unchanged to reflect model fitting during the training phase. The optimized model showed improved classification accuracy and F1-score on the test set, indicating that parameter optimization effectively enhanced the model’s generalization ability. For example, using the UVE-SPA feature extraction method, the test set accuracy increased from 94% before optimization to 96% after optimization, and the F1-score improved from 0.92 to 0.95, demonstrating that the optimization process effectively improved the prediction performance on unseen samples. Similar trends were observed for other feature extraction methods, confirming the effectiveness of the optimization strategy in enhancing model robustness and accuracy. The optimal SVM parameters obtained through the HBA algorithm were C = 98.2583 and γ = 0.0716785.

In summary, the apple watercore detection model is SNV-UVE-SPA-SVM, with the UVE-SPA method extracting feature wavelengths at 613 nm, 625 nm, 634 nm, 661 nm, 692 nm, 708 nm, 726 nm, 731 nm, 740 nm, 749 nm, 774 nm, 781 nm, 792 nm, and 827 nm.

It should be noted that the samples were collected over a 25-day period (October 12 to November 5), during which natural fruit maturity may gradually progress. To minimize potential bias caused by temporal variation, the SPXY algorithm ensured that both spectral characteristics and class labels were evenly distributed between the training and test sets. Since apples with and without watercore were present across the entire sampling period rather than being concentrated on specific dates, the model is unlikely to have learned purely maturity-related spectral patterns. The observed spectral differences are primarily associated with internal tissue structure changes caused by watercore formation, such as variations in light transmission and absorption properties. Therefore, although minor performance variation in field validation may be influenced by environmental or temporal factors, the overall modeling results predominantly reflect watercore-specific spectral characteristics.

Conclusions and limitations

In this study, a portable apple watercore detection instrument based on visible/near-infrared spectroscopy was successfully developed and experimentally validated. The instrument integrates an AIOX2000-13 spectrometer, an STM32F103VET6 ARM processor, and a 4G wireless communication module, enabling real-time, on-site spectral data acquisition, transmission, and analysis. Using the SNV–UVE–SPA–HBA–SVM modeling pipeline, the system achieved high detection accuracy. Repeated field tests on 50 Red Fuji apples demonstrated the instrument’s stability and repeatability, with results closely matching manual observations. These findings indicate that the instrument can provide rapid, non-destructive, and reliable watercore detection during apple growth and maturation, offering practical potential for orchard management, harvesting, and marketing.

However, this study still has certain limitations. The sample size was relatively limited and only included apples from a single orchard and variety, which may affect the model’s generalization ability to other varieties or growing regions. Field measurement conditions, such as ambient lighting, temperature, and operational disturbances, could introduce minor detection errors. In addition, the current system only performs binary classification of watercore presence and does not provide quantitative assessment of watercore severity. Future work will focus on expanding the dataset to include different varieties and environmental conditions, enhancing the robustness of the device under variable field conditions, and developing methods for quantitative evaluation of watercore, thereby further improving the practical value of the system.
