Developmental CA2 perineuronal net reduction restores social memory in Shank3 mutant mice
Authors: Emma J. Diethorn, Aarushi B. Rathaur, Brayan R. Ruiz Lopez, Chino K. Eke, Elizabeth Gould
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Individuals with Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) and related neurodevelopmental conditions, like Phelan-McDermid syndrome (PMDS), exhibit social recognition deficits. Shank3B knockout (KO) mice, a genetic model with relevance to ASD and PMDS, have deficits in social recognition in adulthood, but the developmental mechanisms underlying this dysfunction remain unknown. We found that Shank3B KOs have deficits in short-term social recognition during development that persist into adulthood along with CA2 network aberrations. In CA2, perineuronal nets (PNNs), extracellular matrix structures, support social recognition in adult mice. In developing CA2, we found excessive PNNs in KOs, greater sequestration of the guidance cue semaphorin-3A, and overgrowth of afferents. Reduction of PNN levels restored semaphorin-3A, afferent input to CA2, and social recognition, which persisted into adulthood and partially normalized CA2 network activity. These findings suggest that excess CA2 PNN formation impairs social recognition by disrupting afferent input, effects that are reversible by early life intervention.

Introduction

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) has a multifactorial etiology with both environmental and genetic factors implicated. In genome-wide association studies, mutations of the SHANK3 gene have been identified as one of several genetic risk factors for ASD1,2. In children, deletion or mutation of the SHANK3 gene contributes to social and cognitive developmental delays that are classified as Phelan-McDermid syndrome (PMDS)3. Most children with PMDS meet the criteria for ASD diagnosis and have diminished social interest and social novelty preference compared to typically developing children4,5. In adult mice with mutations in the Shank3 gene, including Shank3B knockouts (KOs), decreased interest in social stimuli and impaired social recognition have been reported6,7.

Healthy developing mice display evidence of social recognition in the first postnatal week, which manifests as a preference for the caregiving mother over a novel dam8. As mice develop, their social repertoire expands and preference for novel peers over familiar peers and littermates emerges toward the end of the second postnatal week9,10. Social recognition and social novelty preference have been linked to the CA2 region of the hippocampus in adulthood11,12, and specific patterns of network activity, including phase-amplitude coupling (PAC) and sharp-wave ripples (SWRs), play important roles in these functions13–15. The involvement of area CA2 in social recognition begins during the early postnatal period8–10 and expands as the region undergoes substantial remodeling characterized by afferent ingrowth and the appearance of perineuronal nets (PNNs)9,10,16,17.

PNNs are extracellular matrix structures that have been associated with developmental critical period closure, and in adulthood are thought to modulate plasticity18–20. Emergence of PNNs in area CA2 during the second postnatal week coincides with the onset of social novelty preference in typically developing mice9,10. As mice mature, healthy CA2 PNN levels continue to support social recognition21–24. In the developing hippocampus and cortex, semaphorin-3A (sema3A) is sequestered by PNNs and influences axon ingrowth and synapse elimination25–31. The CA2 region integrates afferent input from many regions that are important for social novelty detection, including the supramammillary nucleus of the hypothalamus (SuM)32–34, a pathway known to undergo considerable postnatal remodeling9,10. These findings raise the possibility that social dysfunction in Shank3B KO mice arises from atypical development of CA2 PNNs, which disrupts sema3A signaling and SuM-CA2 circuit formation.

To better understand mechanisms of developmental social dysfunction, we characterized social behavior in developing Shank3B KOs and observed deficits in the ability to form a short-term memory of a recently encountered peer in the second postnatal week, which persists into adulthood and is accompanied by disrupted CA2 network responses to social stimuli. We also observed that KO pups exhibit elevated CA2 PNNs, along with excessive sema3A and putative SuM ingrowth, when social dysfunction is first apparent. Lastly, we found that lowering CA2 PNNs in KOs during the second postnatal week reduces developmental sema3A and SuM ingrowth, while restoring social function. Restoration of social recognition and some aspects of CA2 network activity were evident in adult KOs after developmental reduction of PNNs. Together, these findings suggest that atypical CA2 PNNs contribute to impaired social recognition and network activity in Shank3B KO mice by disrupting postnatal development of CA2 circuitry, and that reduction of PNNs during development allows for healthy CA2 function to emerge and persist into adulthood.

Results

Shank3B KO pups show impaired social recognition of peers but typical behavior on other social and nonsocial tasks

Nonsocial memory is unaffected in Shank3B KO pups and adults

We next investigated whether memory for object location would be typical in developing KO mice, since KO adults have been reported to have intact object location memory7, but younger ages have not been probed. Although P14 WT pups did not investigate objects enough to conduct this task and were excluded from this experiment, both P21 and P60 WT and KO mice showed comparable object location memory (P21: t15 = 0.7940, p = 0.4396; P60: t27 = 0.0356, p = 0.9719) (Fig. 1c).

Shank3B KO pups exhibit impaired recognition after a single short-term, but not continual long-term, presentation of a social stimulus

To determine whether KO pups form memories for their littermates with which they were housed since birth, we used a direct social interaction test (DSIT) and first exposed pups to a littermate and, after a 1 h inter-trial interval (ITI), introduced a novel sex- and age-matched mouse (a “peer”), which typically developing pups spend more time investigating9,10. Both P14 WTs and KOs showed preference for the novel peer over the familiar littermate (t29 = 1.226, p = 0.2301) (Fig. 1d), suggesting that KO pups can recognize a social stimulus after continual long-term presentation in the nest. Next, we investigated whether KO mice have typical recognition memory after a short-term presentation of a novel social stimulus across three developmental time points: P14, when preference for novel peers first emerges9,10; P21, when pups are weaned37; and P60, at young adulthood37. Mice were first exposed to a novel peer and, after an ITI, were re-exposed to the same now-familiar peer. At P14, Shank3B KOs did not decrease investigation time between exposure to the novel peer and the same now-familiar peer, while WT controls did (interaction: F(1, 45) = 11.21, p = 0.0016; WT: p < 0.0001; KO: p = 0.8516) (Fig. 1e). KO pups also spent significantly less time investigating the novel peer in the initial exposure compared to WTs and had a lower social discrimination index (novel: p = 0.0004; index: t45 = 2.633, p = 0.0115) (Fig. 1e, f).

At P21, KOs remained unable to discriminate between novel and familiar mice and spent less time with the novel mouse (interaction: F(1, 22) = 6.899, p = 0.0154; WT: p = 0.0010; KO: p > 0.9999; novel: p = 0.0015) (Fig. 1e). The social discrimination index, although lower in KOs, was not statistically different between genotypes (Mann–Whitney U = 54, p = 0.3712). This could be due to high variability in the KO group (Fig. 1f). A 1 h ITI was used to constrain training and testing to single days for pups, but P60 mice underwent testing with 24 h between trials. As adults, KOs remained impaired relative to WTs, in line with previous findings (interaction: F(1, 30) = 9.212, p = 0.0049; WT: p < 0.0001; KO: p = 0.6845) (Fig. 1e)7. At this age, the discrimination index was significantly lower in KOs (t30 = 2.650, p = 0.0127) (Fig. 1f).

Shank3B KO adults exhibit aberrant CA2 neuronal activation and network activity during social behavior

Since area CA2 is required for social recognition and novelty preference11,12, and CA2 neuronal activity is modulated by social experience38, we investigated whether baseline and social novelty-induced neuronal activity differ between KO and WT mice using the immediate early gene (IEG) c-fos. We perfused KO and WT mice directly from the home cage to gather baseline c-fos data, or 1 h after exposure to a novel peer (Fig. 2a). No differences were observed in c-fos+ cell density between genotypes in mice perfused from the home cage (p = 0.9852), and both genotypes exhibited an increase in c-fos+ cell density after novel exposure (interaction: F(1, 32) = 6.627, p = 0.0149; WT: p < 0.0001; KO: p < 0.0001). However, novel-exposed KOs had significantly lower c-fos+ cell density in CA2 compared to novel-exposed WT mice (p = 0.0022) (Fig. 2b, c). Given that CA2 pyramidal cells do not robustly express IEGs 39, it is likely these c-fos+ cells are inhibitory interneurons. In CA2, synchronized network events like PAC and SWRs during awake behavior contribute to social recognition function13–15,40. To assess whether KO mice exhibit atypical oscillations, we recorded from CA2 under different social and nonsocial conditions (Fig. 2d, e). First, we found no differences in aperiodic (1/fx) exponent or offset of the power spectral density (PSD) between genotypes and only moderate fluctuations across recording sessions (Fig. 2f, S2a–d). Next, we examined power and found it was generally comparable between genotypes during periods of immobility (theta: F(1, 11) = 2.675, p = 0.1302; slow gamma: F(1, 11) = 0.3833, p = 0.5484; fast gamma: F(1, 11) = 0.0982, p = 0.7599), and gamma power seemed to rise in both genotypes during a post-social recording session (slow gamma: F(1.928, 21.21) = 6.250, p = 0.0078, E = 0.9640; fast gamma: F(1.565, 17.21) = 23.11, p < 0.0001, E = 0.7823) (Fig. S2e–g). We found no differences in power between genotypes during active investigation of the novel peer (F(1, 11) = 0.0227, p = 0.8830), but with the familiar peer, power in the slow gamma range (20–50 Hz) was lower in KOs (F(1, 11) = 6.085, p = 0.0313; slow gamma: p = 0.0154) (Fig. S2h). No statistically significant genotype effects were observed in theta (4–12 Hz) (p = 0.0683) nor fast gamma (50–140 Hz) (p = 0.6907) power with the familiar peer (Fig. S2h). Given that CA2 PAC between theta and gamma has been implicated in social recognition14,15, and slow gamma power was diminished in KOs during investigation of a familiar peer, we next assessed theta-slow gamma PAC within recording sessions. During periods of immobility, KOs exhibited PAC consistent with that of WTs regardless of recording context (Fig. 2g). However, during periods of active social investigation of either a novel or familiar peer, PAC was transiently increased in WTs (F(1, 7) = 25.06, p = 0.0016; novel: p = 0.0295; familiar: p = 0.0188), but not in KOs (F(1, 4) = 5.545, p = 0.0781), suggesting that during active social investigation, CA2 theta-gamma synchrony is modulated less in KOs than in WTs (Fig. 2h).

We next investigated high-frequency SWRs in CA213,40 and found that when mice were immobile during novel or familiar peer exposure, KOs exhibited lower frequency SWRs, a difference not seen when mice were alone in the pre- and post-social recordings nor during active social investigation (pre-social: t11 = 0.7228, p = 0.4849; novel: t11 = 3.230, p = 0.0080; post-social: t11 = 1.200, p = 0.2555; familiar: Mann–Whitney U = 5, p = 0.0295) (Fig. 2i, j) (Fig. S3a). Other SWR properties (rate, duration, amplitude, novel/familiar difference) were similar between genotypes within recording sessions (Fig. S3b–f; Fig. 2k). Lastly, despite no changes in SWR rate between genotypes, KOs exhibited a lower rate index score than WTs, an effect observed during active bouts of social investigation, not during immobility (interaction: F(1, 11) = 9.390, p = 0.0108; immobility: p = 0.1738; investigation: p = 0.0478) (Fig. 2l). These results collectively indicate that Shank3B KO mice exhibit specific differences in CA2 network events that suggest diminished oscillatory differentiation between novel and familiar social experiences.

Excess perineuronal nets in CA2 contribute to impaired social development and network dysfunction in Shank3B KOs

Given that CA2 PNN development coincides with emerging social recognition in healthy mice9,10, and that CA2 PNNs contribute to social discrimination abilities in adulthood21–24, we next characterized CA2 PNNs in Shank3B KO mice across development.

CA2 PNNs are more intense in Shank3B KO pups

We labeled P14 tissue with the plant lectin Wisteria floribunda agglutinin (WFA), which binds to chondroitin sulfate (CS)-glycosaminoglycan (GAG) chains, as well as with aggrecan (ACAN), the main neuronal CS proteoglycan of PNNs. Optical intensity of both WFA+ and ACAN+ PNNs in the CA2 region was significantly higher in P14 KOs compared to WTs, as was the percent area of CA2 occupied by PNNs (WFA intensity: t21 = 2.717, p = 0.0129; percent area: t21 = 2.373, p = 0.0273) (Fig. 3a, b) (ACAN intensity: t21 = 2.764, p = 0.0116; percent area: t21 = 2.184, p = 0.0405) (Fig. S4a, b). In CA2, PNNs surround both pyramidal cells (Fig. S5) and inhibitory interneurons, the majority of which are PV+ (Fig. S6). The intensity increase identified in P14 KOs was likely caused by PNNs surrounding pyramidal cells, since PNN intensity around PV+ interneurons was similar between genotypes (t19 = 0.7416, p = 0.4674) as were other PV+ cell characteristics (Fig. S6). At P21, the difference between genotypes in CA2 PNN intensity was no longer detectable with either WFA or ACAN (WFA intensity: Mann–Whitney U = 59, p = 0.9487; percent area: Mann–Whitney U = 56, p = 0.7969) (Fig. 3c, d) (ACAN intensity: Mann–Whitney U = 58, p = 0.8977; percent area: t20 = 1.312, p = 0.2043) (Fig. S4c, d). Likewise, no genotype differences were observed in CA2 PNN intensity at P60 (WFA intensity: t12 = 0.8142, p = 0.4314; percent area: Mann–Whitney U = 18, p = 0.4557) (Fig. 3e, f) (ACAN intensity: t12 = 0.5419, p = 0.5978; percent area: t12 = 0.1500, p = 0.8833) (Fig. S4e, f). Reducing PNNs in the developing CA2 of Shank3B KO pups restores healthy social recognition into young adulthood

The degradative enzyme chondroitinase ABC (chABC) has been used to transiently degrade PNNs in the CA2 of adult mice21,24,41,42. After determining a dose of chABC that, when injected into the KO CA2 at P10, reduced PNNs compared to KOs injected with the control enzyme, penicillinase (pnase), which does not affect major PNN components43 (t13 = 2.269, p = 0.0410) (Fig. 4a), P10 KOs and WTs received bilateral stereotaxic CA2 injections of either chABC or pnase and were tested on the DSIT on P14 (Fig. 4b). Like unoperated WT pups, WTs injected with pnase decreased interaction time between novel and familiar trials (p = 0.0014). Surprisingly, WT pups injected with chABC also demonstrated intact social recognition memory (p = 0.0006), possibly because our chABC treatment was designed to only partially reduce PNNs. Like unoperated KOs, those given pnase showed no decrease in interaction time between novel and familiar stimulus mice (p = 0.9743), and we noted a slightly negative discrimination index in this group. In line with our hypothesis that excess PNNs contribute to impaired social recognition in KOs, those treated with chABC decreased interaction time between novel and familiar exposures (p = 0.0031). SuM afferents to the CA2 appear to be overgrown in developing Shank3B KO mice, and are reduced by chABC treatment

Next, we labeled putative axons from the SuM with vesicular glutamate transporter 2 (vGlut2) and mossy fibers from DG with anti-zinc transporter 3 (ZnT3), since both projections to CA2 are present at P14 and participate in social recognition in adulthood9,10,15,32. The intensity of vGlut2 was significantly higher in P14 KOs compared to WTs (t20 = 3.062, p = 0.0062) (Fig. 6e), but no differences between genotypes were seen in ZnT3 (t21 = 0.2139, p = 0.8327) (Fig. 6f). Since a subset of SuM axons release the neuropeptide substance P (SP) which binds to neurokinin 1 receptors (NK1Rs) on CA2 neurons44, we also examined the intensity of SP and NK1R labeling and found no differences between P14 Shank3B KOs and WTs (SP: Mann–Whitney U = 44, p = 0.9682; NK1R: t20 = 0.3732, p = 0.7129) (Fig. 6g, h). Because sema3A inhibits synapse elimination29, we hypothesized that high levels of PNNs with their excess sequestered sema3A may be preventing the elimination of vGlut2+ axons. To determine whether excessive PNNs may be responsible for high vGlut2 intensity, we examined this measure in Shank3B KOs treated with chABC on P10 and found a reduction in vGlut2 intensity by P14 compared to KOs treated with pnase (t21 = 2.094, p = 0.0485) (Fig. 6e).

Measures of SuM-CA2 circuitry normalize in Shank3B KOs by adulthood, but new aberrations arise

Although untreated adult Shank3B KOs exhibit persistent impairments in social recognition, high CA2 vGlut2 intensity seen at P14 was no longer present in KO mice at P60 (t18 = 0.5693, p = 0.5762) (Fig. S9a). In contrast to results found on P14, however, innervation from SP-expressing SuM axons appeared to be aberrant in adult Shank3B KOs. In the CA2, SP+ boutons were significantly larger in KOs compared to WTs, although overall labeling intensity was not affected (size: t18 = 2.817, p = 0.0114; WT: 0.72 μm2 ± 0.0352; KO: 0.88 μm2 ± 0.0445) (intensity: t18 = 1.249, p = 0.2277) (Fig. S9b, c). In addition, the intensity of the NK1R, which SP binds to, was significantly diminished in the CA2 of adult KOs (t18 = 2.682, p = 0.0152) (Fig. S9d). These findings suggest that despite normalization of some afferent measures between P14 and adulthood, the Shank3B KO CA2 exhibits aberrations that vary across development and may contribute to persistent social recognition impairments.

Lastly, we assessed the responsiveness of SuM neurons to novel social stimuli using c-fos, since the SuM-CA2 circuit responds preferentially to social novelty32. We first confirmed that c-fos+ cell density in SuM was not different between adult KOs and WTs perfused directly from the home cage (p = 0.9592) (Fig. S9e–g). In a separate cohort of adult WTs and KOs perfused 1 h after exposure to a novel peer, SuM c-fos+ cell density was significantly elevated (WT: p < 0.0001; KO: p < 0.0001). Novel-exposed KOs, however, had significantly lower c-fos+ cell density in SuM compared to novel-exposed WTs, paralleling our findings from CA2 (interaction: F(1, 29) = 9.619, p = 0.0043; novel-exposed: p = 0.0007) (Fig. S9f). Because the SuM has reciprocal connections with the CA245, the SuM c-fos effect may be secondary, resulting from atypical projections from the CA2. Taken together, these findings suggest that PNNs and associated sema3A abnormalities in the developing CA2 disorganize incoming SuM axons, and continued sculpting of this circuit contributes to social impairments in adulthood.

Discussion

The results of these experiments suggest that social recognition deficits observed in developing and adult Shank3B KOs arise from excessive PNNs in the developing CA2 region, which sequester higher quantities of sema3A, potentially leading to delayed pruning of SuM inputs to the CA2. Reduction of CA2 PNNs during the postnatal period restores social recognition and some aberrant network activity in Shank3B KO adults, potentially by reducing sema3A and restoring SuM inputs to WT levels during a developmental critical period.

We found that many behaviors were typical in Shank3B KO mice at several developmental timepoints, including isolation-induced USVs on P5, long-term social recognition on P14, and object location memory on P21 and P60, but KO mice exhibited specific deficits in social novelty preference and short-term social recognition during development and in adulthood, when most healthy mice demonstrate this ability. These results strongly suggest that mutation or loss of Shank3, a synaptic scaffolding protein, does not disrupt development of circuits associated with social or hippocampal function in general, but rather produces a specific deficit that impairs the CA2 region. In healthy mice, hippocampal area CA2 is necessary for the ability to recognize and prefer novel peers both during development and in adulthood9–12. This ability is in part supported by the presence of PNNs, which begin to emerge in CA2 during the second postnatal week9,10,17. Atypical PNNs in CA2 have been characterized in adult mouse models for neurodevelopmental conditions and Alzheimer’s disease that exhibit social memory dysfunction21,22,46,47. We found that CA2 PNNs were atypical in developing Shank3B KOs, which showed excess PNN labeling compared to healthy WT controls on P14. This effect was not observed at weaning nor in young adulthood, but nevertheless social dysfunction persisted in adult Shank3B KOs and was accompanied by impairments in CA2 neuronal activation, as well as in PAC and SWRs. In adult mice that underwent developmental PNN reduction, social dysfunction was prevented, and CA2 neuronal activation, as well as PAC and SWRs during social experience, were partially improved.

These findings suggest that area CA2 may undergo a sensitive period of plasticity mediated by PNN emergence during postnatal development and that uncorrected PNN abnormalities during this time can persistently disorganize the region. Our results indicate that Shank3B KO pups have high CA2 PNNs because they undergo accelerated development in this region. Disruption of developmental timing by the premature presence of high CA2 PNNs might lead to a cascade of events that permanently disorganize CA2 circuitry46. This possibility also raises questions about whether conditions associated with delayed circuit maturation, such as Fragile X48, might have converse or even similar effects if there is an optimal level of CA2 PNNs required for typical development. Studies have shown that environmental conditions during development, such as enrichment and stress17,49, can alter CA2 PNNs, suggesting that within a putative range of optimal PNNs, there may be additional fine-tuning of CA2 circuity driven by experience.

Healthy social recognition abilities also require the ventral CA1, a major target of CA2 neurons50. Although developing KO mice exhibited typical PNNs in ventral CA1, studies have shown that adult Shank3B KO mice exhibit diminished activation of vCA1 neuronal ensembles in response to social experience6, and that chemogenetic activation of the CA2-vCA1 pathway can temporarily correct social recognition abilities7. In Shank3 mutants, diminished neuronal activity in vCA1 may be secondary to more direct effects on the CA2, although conditional knockout of Shank3 in vCA1 neurons alone can also impair social memory51.

Taken together, these findings suggest that transgenic Shank3 mutation directly impacts both CA2 and vCA1, either of which may be sufficient to produce social dysfunction. Despite these abnormalities, social recognition function can be restored temporarily by activating the CA2-vCA1 circuit in adulthood or lastingly by lowering CA2 PNNs during development. The extent to which developmental CA2 PNN correction affects vCA1 function remains unexplored.

We considered the relationship between PNNs and the axon guidance cue molecule sema3A, since it is sequestered within PNNs and influences local microcircuitry by mediating synapse growth and elimination28,29,52, and found that excess PNNs in Shank3B KO pups were accompanied by increased sequestration of sema3A, which may impede developing circuitry. Indeed, vGlut2+ innervation from the SuM was also increased in P14 Shank3B KO pups. This abnormality may contribute to the observed behavioral impairments due to the role of this pathway in social recognition in adulthood32. Infusing chABC into the CA2 on P10 lowered PNN levels in KO pups by P14, which was accompanied by lowered sema3A and vGlut2 labeling. It appears, however, that excess sema3A labeling in P14 KOs did not disrupt ZnT3+ mossy fiber input from the DG to CA2. It may be the case, then, that the role of PNN-associated sema3A in guiding afferent input does not broadly affect all afferent populations and only specifically impacts some developing innervation, namely from the SuM. Although measures of vGlut2 innervation normalized by adulthood in unoperated Shank3B KOs, other measures that reflect a subset of this projection known to release SP appear abnormal, changes that seem to occur later, perhaps due to compensatory mechanisms. These differences, along with diminished activation of SuM neurons in response to social novelty in Shank3B KOs, suggest persistent abnormalities in the SuM-CA2 circuit in adulthood. Since SuM inputs to the hippocampus have been linked to neuronal oscillations, including theta rhythm53 and SWRs54, persistently diminished activity in this afferent population may play a substantial role in electrophysiological and behavioral dysfunction in Shank3B KO adults.

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that excess PNNs and associated molecular changes in CA2 disrupt the maturation of social recognition circuits in Shank3B KO mice. By identifying a postnatal window during which PNN emergence shapes CA2 connectivity and behavior, potentially through sema3A signaling, our work highlights a developmental mechanism that may underlie both early and persistent social impairments. Future studies aimed at dissecting the developmental timing of SuM and other afferent pathways to the CA2 will be essential for understanding how early-life circuit miswiring leads to long-term social dysfunction and may be informative about interventions for neurodevelopmental disorders.

Methods

Animals

Mice were group-housed and kept on a 12:12 h light/dark cycle in Optimice cages with food and water provided ad libitum. Mixed groups of male and female mice (P5–P150) were used for all experiments. Male and female Shank3B heterozygous (HET) mice were purchased from Jackson Laboratories (strain #017688) and paired together for breeding, yielding WT, KO, and HET offspring. Pups were genotyped (Transnetyx) via ear punch prior to P14 and received individual toe tattoos for identification. Pups were weaned on P21 and group-housed by sex. Experimenters were blinded to genotype and/or treatment, and all studies received ethical approval from the Princeton University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use.

Behavioral testing

USV recording

USVs were recorded from P5 KO and WT pups in isolation to determine whether basic vocalizations would be typical in pups of this model. P5 pups were placed individually in a 30.5 cm × 30.5 cm matte plexiglass testing arena with a white base and black walls, maintained at 32 °C to simulate nest temperature, and USVs were recorded for 2 min. An Avisoft-UltraSoundGate 116Hb kit with a single microphone and Avisoft RECORDER software (v4.2.29) was used with the following parameters adapted from a previous report8: 300 kHz sampling rate, 16-bit format, 0.032 s buffer, 50% overlap and 256 FFT size. Sound files were processed using Avisoft SASLab Pro (v5.2.13) and put through a high-pass filter and an average image filter to exclude frequencies under 30 kHz and background noise, respectively. Spectrograms were then created with the following parameters: 512 FFT size, 100% frame rate, Hamming window, 75% temporal overlap. USVs were detected with automatic whistle tracking using a 2 ms minimum duration and 15 ms hold time. Calls with a peak amplitude less than −62 dB were rejected. From these data, call number, duration (sec), amplitude (dB), and frequency (kHz) were analyzed.

Direct social interaction test

Separate cohorts of mice were subjected to DSIT on P14, P21, or in adulthood (P60–P85) as previously described9 in the same arena used for USV testing. Mice were habituated to the room for 15 min prior to behavior, and then habituated to the arena for 5 min before testing. On the day of testing, mice were paired with a novel sex- and age-matched (±2 postnatal days) HET stimulus mouse (or a familiar HET littermate for the P14 littermate DSIT) and allowed to interact freely for 5 min. After an inter-trial interval (ITI) during which the mice were returned to the home cage, the test mouse and now-familiar stimulus mouse (or a new novel peer for the P14 littermate DSIT) were paired and allowed to interact for an additional 5 min. A 1 h ITI was used for P14 and P21 mice to constrain testing to a single postnatal day, while a 24 hr ITI was used for adult mice. Both trials were video-recorded, and the amount of time in seconds the test mouse spent interacting with the stimulus mouse was manually scored. The discrimination index was calculated from raw investigation time as novel investigation time minus familiar investigation time divided by total investigation time. Mice were not compared across timepoints, though we note that the average investigation time appears to increase with age, effects likely facilitated by maturation of the motor and visual systems during postnatal development. The apparatus was thoroughly cleaned with 70% ethanol between each mouse and trial.

Object location test

To assess hippocampus-dependent place memory, mice were subjected to the object location (OL) task on P21 and P60 as previously described7. P14 pups were not tested on OL memory because at this age, they failed to investigate the object. The apparatus was the same as above, with the addition of a 5 cm white vertical stripe along the center of one wall to provide an environmental cue. Mice were habituated to the empty apparatus 2× a day for 5 min each for 3 days, beginning on P18 for P21 testing. On Day 4, mice underwent the 10 min familiarization phase. Two identical objects were placed in the same orientation in adjacent quadrants of the arena, leaving room for the mouse to walk around the entire object, which was taped down. For P21 mice, testing occurred the same day with a 1 h ITI, while adults were tested after a 24 h ITI following the familiarization phase. For the test, the two identical objects remained the same, but one was moved to an adjacent quadrant and rotated 180°. The moved object was counterbalanced across trials. Both the familiarization and testing trials were recorded, and the investigation time of the moved vs. unmoved object in the test phase was manually quantified in seconds. The apparatus and objects were thoroughly cleaned with 70% ethanol between each mouse and trial. Locomotion in the P60 dataset was tracked automatically with ANY-maze (Stoelting) to confirm previously reports that KO mice are hypoactive7,55. As expected, we found that KOs exhibited decreased locomotion speed and distance (speed: 0.025 m/s ± 0.0027; distance: 7.5 m ± 0.7973) compared to WT mice (speed: 0.034 m/s ± 0.0039; distance: 10.3 m ± 1.188).

Pup surgery

P10 KO and WT pups were anesthetized with 1–3% isoflurane and transferred to a stereotaxic apparatus with a nose cone adaptor for pups (Kopf 940, Kopf 934-B), padded ear bars, and an infrared heating pad (Kent Scientific). Pups received subcutaneous injections of saline for hydration, and of Carprofen for pain relief (5 mg/kg). The scalp was incised, and the head was leveled using the bregma and lambda landmarks. Dorsal CA2 was targeted bilaterally at the following stereotaxic coordinates: AP = −1.80 mm, ML = ±1.60 mm, DV = −1.60 mm. chABC from Proteus vulgaris (15nL, 18 U/mL) (Sigma-Aldrich, C2905-10UN) or the control enzyme, pnase (15nL, 18 U/mL) (Sigma-Aldrich, C0389) were prepared in 0.1% BSA in saline, loaded into a 10 μl Nanofil syringe (WPI) fitted with a 33 g beveled needle (WPI), and infused into CA2 at a rate of 5nL/min. This volume (15 nL) was chosen based on cresyl violet injections indicating little to no spread beyond CA2. The scalp was then closed with nylon sutures (Ethilon).

Electrophysiology

Electrode implantation

To assess CA2 network activity in KOs with social recognition dysfunction, young adult mice were implanted with an electrode in the CA2 region. Although there was a robust, statistically significant social recognition deficit in adult KOs compared to WTs, we observed considerable variability in both genotypes in social preference (Fig. 1e, f). Because our goal was to examine neuronal oscillations of mice with intact versus impaired social discrimination, a pre-implant DSIT was used to select mice for implantation. WTs (or chABC-treated KOs) with a discrimination index > 0.24, indicating a strong novelty preference, and KOs (or pnase-treated KOs) with an index <0.08, indicating no social preference, were selected for an implant. This approach does not allow assessment of the entire distribution of KOs and WTs, but rather targets our analyses to mice that are within range of the average social preference score for each genotype.

Adult mice were implanted with a custom-made unilateral electrode implant with 4 platinum/iridium probes spaced 100 μm apart (MicroProbes) into dorsal CA2 of the right hemisphere. Mice were mounted into the stereotaxic apparatus and prepared as described above. Self-tapping bone screws (FST, #19010-10) were implanted on either side of the midline and anterior to the bregma for stabilization. A ground screw was inserted contralateral to the electrode, wrapped with silver ground wire, and covered with conductive paint (MG Chemicals, #842AR). The electrode was then implanted at the following coordinates: AP = −1.82 mm, ML = +2.15 mm, DV = −1.67 mm. C&B-Metabond dental cement (Parkell, S280) was used to stabilize the headstage to the skull and ground screw, and a second cement (Keystone, #0921090) was applied to further encase the implant. A threaded base of a 3D-printed protective cap was embedded into the cement surrounding the headstage, and the top of the cap was screwed onto the base to protect the input port.

Electrophysiological recording

Local field potential (LFP) was recorded in tethered, freely moving mice from up to 4 electrodes placed in the CA2 using an Omniplex recording system (Plexon, Inc.) at a sampling rate of 40 kHz. All recordings were conducted in a 30.5 cm × 30.5 plexiglass arena in dim lighting, and mice were habituated to the arena prior to the experiment. First, recordings were made over a 3 min pre-social baseline period while alone in a familiar arena. Immediately after, recordings were made during a 5 min exposure to a novel peer. Post-social recordings were conducted 1 h later for 5 min while the animal was alone in its home cage. Twenty-four hours after exposure to the novel peer, a final recording was conducted during exposure to the same now-familiar peer for 5 min. These recording sessions were chosen to capture times when acquisition, consolidation, and retrieval of social memories were likely occurring. For analysis of LFP data during immobility bouts and social investigation bouts, trials were recorded using a GoPro Hero8, and behavioral bouts were manually detected using BORIS (v7.13.9). Immobile bouts were characterized by a lack of walking and included behaviors such as grooming and rearing when not in contact with the stimulus mouse. In the post-social home cage, periods of stationary eating, digging, and nesting were also counted as immobile bouts. Social investigation was defined as periods of face-to-face sniffing, anogenital sniffing, close following, and cooperative rearing in the novel and familiar recording sessions.

Electrophysiological analyses

Power in different ranges, PAC between theta and gamma, and SWRs were assessed during all periods of immobility and social investigation during each of the recording sessions (pre-social, novel, post-social, familiar). Custom Python scripts were used to analyze electrophysiological data. For power analyses, LFPs were downsampled to 1 kHz, notch-filtered at 60 Hz, and band-pass filtered from 1 to 140 Hz. PSD was estimated using Welch’s method. Power in theta (4–12 Hz)56, slow gamma (20–50 Hz)57, and fast gamma (50–140 Hz)58 ranges were quantified by integrating the area under the PSD curve. Aperiodic (1/fx) component analyses of the exponent and offset (i.e., slope and intercept) were conducted according to previously published reports59,60. PSDs were fit within 2–40 Hz using the SpecParam toolbox with a knee-mode model, and the aperiodic components were extracted from these fits. To detect SWRs, a third-order Butterworth band-pass filter was applied to isolate 140–220 Hz. The filtered signal was Hilbert-transformed to compute the amplitude envelope, followed by Gaussian smoothing. The smoothed envelope was z-scored, and ripple events were identified as periods exceeding 3.5 standard deviations above the mean, ending when the signal dropped below 1.5 standard deviations. SWRs < 20 ms, >125 ms, or within 200 ms of another were excluded. Additionally, SWRs with mean amplitudes >0.4 mV were considered outliers and removed. SWR rate during behavioral bouts was determined by dividing the number of ripples by total bout duration. Throughout the hippocampus and in the CA2 specifically, SWRs, which have been linked to social recognition, occur not only during periods of immobility, but during awake behaving states40,61–63. PAC between theta and slow gamma, which has also been linked to social recognition14,15 was quantified using the modulation index (MI) method described in a previous report64.

Histology

Mice were perfused on P14, P21, or in adulthood for histological analysis of the CA2 region. Because previous studies have shown that neuronal activity in the CA2 increases with exposure to social stimuli38, additional groups of adult KOs and WTs were perfused 1 h after exposure to a novel mouse in the same arena used for the DSIT, to assess for differences in labeling with the IEG c-fos, an indirect measure of neuronal activation.

Mice were anesthetized with pentobarbital and perfused with cold 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). Brains were extracted and post-fixed in 4% PFA for 48 h, cryoprotected in 30% sucrose, and stored at −80 °C until sectioning. Brains were cut on a Leica 3050S cryostat at 40 μm and sections were stored in well plates filled with 0.1 M PBS with 0.1% sodium azide. Free-floating immunohistochemistry was performed on P14, P21, and adult tissue. Between 2 and 4 sections per brain were analyzed for each marker, and any brain or section with insufficient antibody labeling was excluded from the analysis.

Sections were first washed in 0.1 M PBS for 5 min and then transferred to a blocking solution of 3% normal donkey serum (NDS) in 0.1 M PBS with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 1.5 hr on a shaker. Sections were then incubated in blocking solution plus primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. For PNN labeling, sections were incubated in WFA and/or rabbit anti-ACAN or goat anti-ACAN. To examine parvalbumin (PV)+ interneurons, tissue was labeled with mouse anti-PV. For presumed DG-CA2 afferents, sections were incubated in rabbit anti-ZnT3, which has been shown to localize to DG mossy fiber terminals in ultrastructural studies65. For presumed SuM-CA2 afferents, sections were incubated in rabbit anti-vGlut2, which has been shown to decline in intensity in the CA2 when SuM axons are severed66, or rat anti-SP, as well as rabbit anti-NK1R, which are the active receptors of SP. All reactions contained either mouse anti-RGS14 or rabbit anti-PCP4 to label the CA2 region. Additionally, rabbit anti-c-fos was used for SuM and CA2 IEG labeling. After 24 h, sections were washed in 0.1 M PBS for 5 min and then transferred to the secondary antibody solution in 0.1 M PBS with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 1.5 h. Following secondary antibody incubation, sections were incubated in the counterstain Hoechst 33342 (1:5000) (Molecular Probes, H3570) for 10 min and then transferred to 0.1 M PBS. Sections were then mounted on Superfrost Plus slides (Fisher Scientific) and left to dry overnight, followed by coverslipping with Vectashield antifade mounting media (Vector Labs). To confirm the placement of each electrode in CA2 (stratum radiatum, pyramidal, or oriens), tissue was labeled with RGS14. Data from any electrode not within a CA2 layer were removed from all analyses. All histochemistry and immunolabeling information can be found in Supplementary Table 1.

Because sema3A is deeply embedded in PNNs, pretreatment is required to reveal antigenic sites during immunolabeling. We used previously published methods28 to label sema3A as follows. Sections were first incubated in 0.1 M Tris-HCl containing sodium acetate (0.03 M) and chABC (0.1 U/mL) for 2 h at 37 °C to facilitate antibody binding. After, they were washed with 0.2% TBS-Triton and then incubated in a solution containing TBS, methanol (10%), and hydrogen peroxide (0.3%) for 30 min. Then, tissue was blocked in 0.2% TBS-Triton containing 5% NDS for 1 h and incubated overnight at room temperature in blocking solution and rabbit anti-sema3A, mouse anti-RGS14, and the lectin WFA. The following day, sections were incubated in 0.2% TBS-Triton with secondary antibodies.

Confocal Imaging and Analyses

Slides were imaged with a 1–2 μm z-step and a 40x objective on a Leica SP8 confocal microscope, and analyses were conducted using custom macros in Fiji (Image J) (NIH) after being filtered with a rolling ball radius of 50 pixels. In area CA2, PNNs surround both pyramidal cells and inhibitory interneurons, the majority of which are PV+. PNNs around individual pyramidal cells in CA2 are not analyzable because they are densely packed. Therefore, in line with previous methods21,46, PNN intensity was measured from the entire RGS14+ pyramidal cell layer by collecting the maximum MGV of the WFA or ACAN z-stack, which was averaged across sections. PNNs around PV+ cells in the CA2 can be analyzed individually because PV+ cells are sparser than pyramidal cells. In these analyses, all individual PV+ cells with and without PNNs within the pyramidal cell layer were traced, and the MGV of each PNN was collected and averaged as described above. PV+ cell intensity, area, and number were also collected. In other hippocampal subregions, PNNs surround only inhibitory interneurons17,67. To assess whether any PNN differences between genotypes in the CA2 were specific to this hippocampal subregion, the dorsal DG, an afferent population to the CA215, as well as the dorsal CA1 (dCA1) and ventral CA1 (vCA1), both targets of the CA250,68 were analyzed by tracing all individual WFA+ PNNs within the pyramidal cell layer or granule cell layer, and the MGV of each PNN was collected and averaged as described above. Sema3A and vGlut2 were analyzed in the same manner as overall PNNs in the CA2. SP and NK1R analyses were confined to the oriens due to the pattern of afferent input69, while ZnT3 was confined to the stratum lucidum9,10. Background intensity was taken from the corpus callosum and subtracted from the MGV for punctate labels like vGlut2 and SP, yielding net intensity. NK1R exists in the developmental corpus callosum70, so background subtraction was not applied. Particle analyses were conducted on SP images to assess the size and presence of boutons (0.15–7.00 μm2) from thresholded (RenyiEntropy) z-stacks. c-fos+ cells in the CA2 were manually counted in the RGS14 + CA2 pyramidal cell layer, and in lateral SuM atlas traces were overlaid onto confocal images to identify the region.

Statistics and reproducibility

Two- or three-way ANOVAs with Šidák’s multiple comparisons, or unpaired t-tests were used to assess effects across or within genotype, sex, trial, treatment, and/or age, with repeated measures when applicable. Discrimination indices were calculated from raw investigation time as (novel − familiar) ÷ (novel + familiar). When datasets did not meet the assumptions of parametric statistics, the nonparametric Mann–Whitney U test was used. In the baseline electrophysiology experiment, one KO exhibited zero SWRs during investigation of the novel partner, preventing calculation of some spectral/temporal measures, so mixed-effects analyses were used. Correlation between sema3A and WFA intensity was conducted using simple linear regression. All statistical analyses were conducted using Graphpad Prism 10.
