Subrack Lattices of Conjugation Racks
Selçuk Kayacan

Abstract
A rack is a set with a binary operation such that left multiplications are automorphisms of the set and a quandle is a rack satisfying a certain condition. Let S S be a subset of a finite group G G which is closed under the conjugation operation a ⊳ b := a ​ b ​ a − 1 a\triangleright b:=aba^{-1} . The set S S with the conjugation operation ⊳ \triangleright is a quandle. We call those objects conjugation racks . The prime examples are • the group rack ( G , ⊳ ) (G,\triangleright) , • the conjugacy class rack ( C , ⊳ ) (C,\triangleright) , where C C is a conjugacy class in G G , and • the p p -power rack ( G p , ⊳ ) (G_{p},\triangleright) , where p p is a prime and G p G_{p} is the set of all elements in G G whose order is a power of p p . The set of all subracks of a finite rack form a lattice under inclusion. In this paper we study the subrack lattices of the conjugation racks. In particular, we show that the subrack lattice can be associated with a subposet of a partition lattice as well as with a subposet of an integer partition lattice in a canonical way if the rack is connected. And, if the rack is not connected, the study of the homotopy properties of the subrack lattice can be reduced into the study of the homotopy properties of the subposet of parabolic subracks. We also prove that for a certain class of p p -power racks the order of a Sylow p p -subgroup divides the reduced Euler characteristic of the subrack lattice of the p p -power rack. This statement can be considered as the rack analogue of a result by Brown in the field of subgroup complexes regarding the Euler characteristic of the poset of nontrivial p p -subgroups of a group. 2020 Mathematics Subject Classification. Introduction

There are many works in literature studying the combinatorial objects that are associated with the algebraic objects in a natural way. For example, if the algebraic object is a group
G
, one naturally defines the
subgroup lattice

\mathcal{L}(G)
as the lattice of all subgroups of
G
. In this context we may ask the following question: Given the subgroup lattice, how much information can be recovered about the group itself? An early result in this direction by Baer is that finite abelian groups can almost be distinguished by their subgroup lattices (see
[
1
]
or
[
2
, Corollary 1.2.8]
).

A relevant combinatorial object is the
intersection graph

\Gamma(G)
of a group
G
: The vertex set of
\Gamma(G)
is the set of proper nontrivial subgroups of
G
and there is an edge between two vertices if and only if they are distinct and their intersection is nontrivial. Observe that given
\Gamma(G)
one can recover
\mathcal{L}(G)
, but not vice versa. Intuitively, intersection graphs should be highly connected graphs and if there are some examples of such graphs with ‘low’ connectivity, they must be exceptional. The main results in
[
3
]
made this intuition more precise. It was proven in
[
4
, Theorem 5.2]
that finite abelian groups can be distinguished by their intersection graphs in the same way as their subgroup lattices. Therefore, we may conclude that subgroup lattices and intersection graphs hold the same amount of information on the subgroup structure when the groups in question are abelian.

Given a partially ordered set (abbreviated poset)
\mathcal{P}
, the

is defined as the simplicial complex whose faces are the totally ordered sets (chains) in
\mathcal{P}
. Introducing the order complexes is a standard way to further incorporate topological arguments into our combinatorial setting (see, for example,
[
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
]
). Let
G
be a group and
p
be a prime dividing the order of

as the poset of nontrivial
p
-subgroups of

[
5
]
or
[

\chi(\Delta(\mathcal{S}_{p}(G)))\equiv 1
modulo
p^{k}
, where
p^{k}
is the
p
-part of
|G|
. Similar work using subgroup lattices, frames, coset posets, and quandles has also appeared in literature (see,
[
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
]
).

In
[
13
]
the
intersection complex

K(G)
of a group

is defined as the simplicial complex whose simplices are the sets of proper subgroups of
G
which intersect nontrivially. The intersection graph
\Gamma(G)
is the
1

K(G)
. As is shown in
[

is contractible if the domination number of

is

A
rack

X
is a set (possibly empty) together with a binary operation
\triangleright
satisfying the following two axioms:

(A1)

for all
a,b,c\in X
we have
a\triangleright(b\triangleright c)=(a\triangleright b)\triangleright(a\triangleright c)

(A2)

for all
a,b\in X
there is a
unique

such that
a\triangleright x=b
.

By (A1) the map
\phi_{a}\colon X\to X;\,x\mapsto a\triangleright x
is a rack morphism for every
a\in X
and by (A2) those morphisms are bijections. Therefore, we may define a rack as a set with a binary operation such that left multiplications are automorphisms of the set. We say
X
is a
quandle
if, in addition, the following third axiom holds:

(A3)

for every
a\in X
we have
a\triangleright a=a
.

The defining axioms of racks and quandles are intimately related with the Reidemeister moves and those algebraic objects proved to be useful in the study of knot invariants (see, for example,
[
14
,
15
,
16
]
).

Preliminaries

In this paper we will be concerned with finite racks. As was remarked earlier, for a rack
X
, the map
\phi_{a}\colon X\to X;\,x\mapsto a\triangleright x
is an automorphism of
X
for all
a\in X
. Moreover, the map
\Phi\colon a\mapsto\phi_{a}
is a rack morphism between
X
and
\Phi(X)
, where
\phi_{a}\triangleright\phi_{b}:=\phi_{a}\phi_{b}\phi_{a}^{-1}
. We say
X
is
faithful
if the map
\Phi
is injective. Notice that
X
is isomorphic to a conjugation rack if it is faithful.

The
inner automorphism group
of
X
, denoted
\mathsf{Inn}(X)
, is defined as the (normal) subgroup of the full automorphism group of
X
generated by the elements of
\Phi(X)
, i.e.,
\mathsf{Inn}(X):=\langle\Phi(X)\rangle
. The group
\mathsf{Inn}(X)
acts on
X
by automorphisms and
X
can be written as the disjoint union of
\mathsf{Inn}(X)
-orbits. We say
X
is
connected
if the action of
\mathsf{Inn}(X)
on
X
is transitive, i.e., if
X
consists of a single
\mathsf{Inn}(X)
-orbit. The group
\mathsf{Inn}(X)
also acts on
\Phi(X)
by conjugation and the map
\Phi\colon a\mapsto\phi_{a}
is a
\mathsf{Inn}(X)
-equivariant map between
X
and
\Phi(X)
. Moreover,
X
is faithful and connected if and only if it is isomorphic, as a rack, to a conjugacy class
C
of a group
G
so that
G=\langle C\rangle
and
Z(G)=1
. For further details see
[
20
, Proposition 3.2]
or
[
22
, Proposition 2]
.

For a partially ordered set (poset)
\mathcal{P}
and an element
x\in\mathcal{P}
, we define
\mathcal{P}_{\geq x}
as the subposet of
\mathcal{P}
whose elements are the elements of
\mathcal{P}
that are greater than or equal
x
, i.e.,

\mathcal{P}_{\geq x}:=\{y\in\mathcal{P}\colon y\geq x\}.

In an analogous way, we define the subposets
\mathcal{P}_{>x}
,
\mathcal{P}_{<x}
,
\mathcal{P}_{\leq x}
. If
x\leq y
, we define the
closed interval

[x,y]
as the set of all elements
z\in\mathcal{P}
satisfying
x\leq z\leq y
. Similarly, for
x,y\in\mathcal{P}
, we define the
open interval

(x,y)
as the set
\{z\mid x<z<y\}
. Notice that
[x,y]=\mathcal{P}_{\geq x}\cap\mathcal{P}_{\leq y}
if
x\leq y
and
(x,y)=\mathcal{P}_{>x}\cap\mathcal{P}_{<y}
. We say
y

covers

x
if
x<y
and
(x,y)=\emptyset
. Suppose
\mathcal{P}
is finite. The
length

\ell(\mathcal{C})
of a chain (or a linearly ordered set)
\mathcal{C}
in
\mathcal{P}
is the number of elements in
\mathcal{C}
minus
1
. In such a case we say
\mathcal{C}
is a
\ell(\mathcal{C})
-chain. And the length
\ell(\mathcal{P})
of
\mathcal{P}
is the maximum of the lengths of chains in
\mathcal{P}
. The dual poset of
\mathcal{P}
, i.e., the poset
\mathcal{P}
with the order relation reversed, is denoted by
\mathcal{P}^{*}
. Notice that the set of chains of
\mathcal{P}
coincides with the set of chains of
\mathcal{P}^{*}
, so their order complexes are same.

A poset
\mathcal{P}
is called
bounded
if it has a unique maximal element, say
\hat{1}
, and a unique minimal element, say
\hat{0}
. If
\mathcal{P}
is a bounded poset, we define the proper part of
\mathcal{P}
as
\overline{\mathcal{P}}:=\mathcal{P}\setminus\{\hat{1},\hat{0}\}
. In particular, the proper part of the subrack lattice of a rack
X
is
\overline{\mathcal{R}(X)}:=\mathcal{R}(X)\setminus\{X,\emptyset\}
. When we say the order complex of a bounded poset
\mathcal{P}
, we mean the order complex
\Delta(\overline{\mathcal{P}})
. Notice that if
\mathcal{P}
is bounded, then
\Delta(\mathcal{P})
would be a cone which is a contractible space. Given a poset
\mathcal{P}
(which may be bounded or not) we define the bounded poset
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}:=\mathcal{P}\sqcup\{\hat{1},\hat{0}\}
by adding a unique maximum element
\hat{1}
and a unique minimum element
\hat{0}
. Notice that
\mathcal{P}\sqcup\{\hat{1},\hat{0}\}
is a disjoint union.

Let

Since the chains of a poset and its dual are same, by Lemma

Remark 2

If

In
[
6

Quillen introduced several poset fiber theorems. The most basic of those theorems states that if
f\colon\mathcal{P}\to\mathcal{Q}
is a poset map such that for all

\mathcal{P}
with
\mathcal{Q}
. A powerful generalization of this result is the following. For two topological spaces

and

if

and

are homotopy equivalent.

Theorem 2

(see
[
24
, Theorem 1.1]
)
.

Let
f\colon\mathcal{P}\to\mathcal{Q}
be a poset map such that for all

the fiber

is
\ell(f^{-1}(\mathcal{Q}_{<y}))
-connected. Then

\Delta(\mathcal{P})\simeq\Delta(\mathcal{Q})\vee\{\Delta(f^{-1}(\mathcal{Q}_{\leq y}))*\Delta(\mathcal{Q}_{>y})\mid y\in\mathcal{Q}\},

where

denotes the wedge (of
\Delta(\mathcal{Q})
and all
\Delta(f^{-1}(\mathcal{Q}_{\leq y}))*\Delta(\mathcal{Q}_{>y})

\Delta(\mathcal{Q})

, for each

. Consequently, if

The

\mathcal{P}(\Omega)

\subseteq
. The

A subset
\mathcal{C}
of a finite poset

is called a

if it has the following properties:

(i)

\mathcal{C}
is an antichain, i.e., no two elements of

are comparable.

(ii)

For every chain

\mathcal{P}

\mathcal{C}
which is comparable to each element in

.

(iii)

If
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{C}

, then the join

or the meet

.

For example, the set of maximal subracks of a finite rack

. Let

be a crosscut in
\mathcal{P}
. The

Proposition 4
.

For a finite lattice
\mathcal{L}
, its order complex
\Delta(\overline{\mathcal{L}})
and the complex
\Delta(\mathsf{Inf}(\mathcal{L}))
share the same homotopy type.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be the set of coatoms of
\mathcal{L}
. Consider the map
\epsilon\colon\overline{\mathcal{L}}\to\overline{\mathcal{L}}
defined in the following way: If
x\in\overline{\mathcal{L}}
and
\mathcal{C}_{x}:=\{c\in\mathcal{C}\mid x\leq c\}
, then
\epsilon(x)
is the meet of all elements in

. It is easy to observe that

is a closure operator. Since
\epsilon(\overline{\mathcal{L}})=\mathsf{Inf}(\mathcal{L})
, by Lemma

the result follows.

\mathcal{C}=\{c_{1},\dots,c_{m}\}

An

of a finite lattice
\mathcal{L}
is an element covering the unique minimum element and the lattice
\mathcal{L}
is said to be
atomic
if each element different from the unique minimum element is the join of some set of atoms of
\mathcal{L}
. Analogously, a
coatom
is an element that is covered by the unique maximum of the lattice and the lattice is called
coatomic
if any non-maximal element can be expressed as the meet of some set of coatoms.

Lemma 6
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a finite lattice having at least two coatoms. Then
\widehat{\mathsf{Inf}(\mathcal{L})}
is a coatomic lattice. If, further,
\mathcal{L}
is atomic, then
\widehat{\mathsf{Inf}(\mathcal{L})}
is atomic. Consequently, the lattice
\widehat{\mathsf{Inf}(\mathcal{L})}
is both atomic and coatomic when
\mathcal{L}
is the subrack lattice of a finite rack.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{C}=\{c_{1},\dots,c_{m}\}
be the set of coatoms of
\mathcal{L}
and let
x=c_{i_{1}}\wedge\dots\wedge c_{i_{k}}
and
y=c_{j_{1}}\wedge\dots\wedge c_{j_{l}}
be elements in
\mathsf{Inf}(\mathcal{L})
. Let
z
be the unique minimum
\hat{0}
of
\widehat{\mathsf{Inf}(\mathcal{L})}
if
c_{i_{1}}\wedge\dots\wedge c_{i_{k}}\wedge c_{j_{1}}\wedge\dots\wedge c_{j_{l}}
is the unique minimum of
\mathcal{L}
. Otherwise, let
z
be the element
c_{i_{1}}\wedge\dots\wedge c_{i_{k}}\wedge c_{j_{1}}\wedge\dots\wedge c_{j_{l}}
. Clearly,
z\in\mathsf{Inf}(\mathcal{L})
is the meet of
x
and
y
. More generally, the meet of any pair of elements is well-defined in
\mathsf{Inf}(\mathcal{L})
. Since
\mathcal{L}
is a finite lattice, by
[
32
, Proposition 3.3.1]
,
\mathsf{Inf}(\mathcal{L})
is a lattice. By its definition the lattice
\mathsf{Inf}(\mathcal{L})
is coatomic. This proves the first statement.

Next, suppose
\mathcal{L}
is atomic. Let
\mathcal{A}=\{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\}
be the set of atoms of
\mathcal{L}
. For each
i
,
1\leq i\leq n
, let
b_{i}
be the meet of the elements in
\{c\in\mathcal{C}\mid a_{i}\leq c\}
. Clearly, the elements
b_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq n
, form the set of minimal elements of
\mathsf{Inf}(\mathcal{L})
. Notice that
\hat{1}=b_{1}\vee\dots\vee b_{n}
. Let
x
be an arbitrary element of
\mathsf{Inf}(\mathcal{L})
. Since
\mathcal{L}
is atomic,

is the join of some atoms of
\mathcal{L}
, say
x=a_{i_{1}}\vee\dots\vee a_{i_{k}}
. Since
x=b_{i_{1}}\vee\dots\vee b_{i_{k}}
, we deduce that
\widehat{\mathsf{Inf}(\mathcal{L})}
is atomic.

By
[
17
, Corollary 2.6]
, the subrack lattice of a finite rack is atomic. Hence, by the previous observations the last statement follows.
∎

A finite simplicial complex

if its maximal faces can be arranged in linear order

in such a way that the subcomplex

is pure and (

)-dimensional for all

. Shellability is defined originally for pure simplicial complexes. The general version we give here is usually called ‘nonpure’ shellability in literature (see
[

,
27

). A shellable simplicial complex may be homeomorphic to a nonshellable simplicial complex; therefore, being shellable is not topologically invariant. However, shellability entails strong topological properties.

Theorem 7

(see

, Theorem 4.1]
or
[

)

A shellable simplicial complex has the homotopy type of a wedge of spheres (in varying dimensions), where for each

Remark 5

Consider the following question: For which racks

Theorem 8

(see
[
21
, Theorem 1]
)
.

Let

holds for every maximal subrack

is homotopy equivalent to a

-sphere, where

is the number of
\mathsf{Inn}(X)
-orbits in

.

Proof.

Suppose

is a finite rack having the stated property. Observe that a subrack

of

is maximal if and only if it is the union of all but one
\mathsf{Inn}(X)
-orbits in

\mathsf{Inf}(\mathcal{R}(X))
is isomorphic to the proper part of the Boolean algebra

is a triangulation of

∎

Proposition 9

Let

\mathcal{R}(C)
which is isomorphic to the disjoint union of
p^{t-k}
many
\overline{B_{p}}

Proof.

Let
P
be a Sylow

-subgroup of
G
. Since
G
is nilpotent,

is the unique Sylow
p
-subgroup of
G

is a conjugacy class of

(see, for example,
[

for the basic properties of nilpotent groups). By
[

, Lemma 9]
,

is a proper subgroup of

and the action of

on

by conjugation is not transitive. Moreover, the maximal subracks of

are unions of all but one

-orbits.

Observe that the subgroup

is normal in

as it is generated by a conjugacy class of

. Therefore, the

-orbits form a system of blocks for

(see
[

). In particular, the size of any
H
-orbit must be the same. Notice that the order of

must be

as it is generated by

and

contains

elements by assumption.

Take an element

. The centralizer

is of order

and since

is not in the center of

, the orders of

. Since

as well. However, that means the size of an

-orbit must be

.

Let

be an

However, the last equality implies that

. Let

be an

Let

Proposition 15
.

Let

be a finite group and let

be a conjugation rack. Suppose both

We shall introduce the following two subposets of the subrack lattice of a conjugation rack
X\subseteq G
.

•

\mathcal{S}(X):=\{S\in\mathsf{Inf}(\mathcal{R}(X))\mid S\text{ is a union of some }\mathsf{Inn}(X)\text{-orbits}\}

•

\mathcal{P}(X):=\{P\in\mathsf{Inf}(\mathcal{R}(X))\mid P\cap S\neq\emptyset\text{ for all }S\in\mathcal{S}(X)\}

Let
\mathcal{S}
be the set of all coatoms of
\mathcal{R}(X)
which are union of all but one
\mathsf{Inn}(X)
-orbits. It is easy to observe that
\mathcal{S}(X)
and
\mathsf{Inf}(\mathcal{R}(X),\mathcal{S})
are the same posets. The subracks in
\mathcal{S}(X)
are called

. Notice that
\mathcal{S}(X)
is isomorphic to the proper part of a Boolean algebra and, hence,
\Delta(\mathcal{S}(X))
is homeomorphic to a sphere. The subracks in
\mathcal{P}(X)
are said to be
parabolic
. Two particular cases worth mentioning:

(i)

If
X=G
is a group rack, then there are no parabolic subracks, i.e.,
\mathcal{P}(X)
is the empty poset, as any subset of a group that contains a representative element from each conjugacy class necessarily generates the whole group.

(ii)

If
X=C
is a connected conjugacy class rack, then any element of
\mathsf{Inf}(\mathcal{R}(X))
would be vacuously parabolic as
\mathcal{S}(X)
would be the empty poset in that case.

Remark 9
.

The underlying sets of
\mathcal{S}(X)
and
\mathcal{P}(X)
are disjoint for any conjugation rack
X
. Moreover, parabolic subracks cannot contain a conjugacy class
C
if
\mathsf{Inn}(X)
is a non-abelian simple group and

is different from

. To see this observe that an
\mathsf{Inn}(X)
-orbit of an element of

is a conjugacy class of
\mathsf{Inn}(X)
; hence, generates a normal subgroup.

Given two disjoint posets
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
, their
ordinal sum

\mathcal{P}\oplus\mathcal{Q}
is the poset whose underlying set is
\mathcal{P}\sqcup\mathcal{Q}
and

in

if and only if one of the following holds:

(i)

in
\mathcal{P}

(ii)

x\leq y
in
\mathcal{Q}

(iii)

x\in\mathcal{P}
and

Observe that simplices of

are exactly the simplices of
\Delta(\mathcal{P})*\Delta(\mathcal{Q})
. As a consequence, we have the following Lemma.

Lemma 16
.

For any two disjoint posets
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
, we have

\Delta(\mathcal{P}\oplus\mathcal{Q})=\Delta(\mathcal{P})*\Delta(\mathcal{Q}).

The next Proposition justifies the usefulness of the notions of spherical and parabolic subracks.

Proposition 17
.

Let
G
be a finite group and let
X\subseteq G
be a conjugation rack. Then

\Delta(\overline{\mathcal{R}(X)})\simeq\Delta(\mathcal{S}(X)\oplus\mathcal{P}(X)).

Proof.

By Proposition
4
we know that
\Delta(\overline{\mathcal{R}(X)})
is homotopy equivalent to
\Delta(\mathsf{Inf}(\mathcal{R}(X)))
. So it is enough to show that
\Delta(\mathsf{Inf}(\mathcal{R}(X)))
and
\Delta(\mathcal{S}(X)\oplus\mathcal{P}(X))
share the same homotopy type. Let
f\colon\mathsf{Inf}(\mathcal{R}(X))\to\mathcal{S}(X)\oplus\mathcal{P}(X)
be the map defined in the following way: If

does not intersect at least one
\mathsf{Inn}(X)
-orbit in

, then

is the union of the

-orbits of the elements in

. If

is a parabolic subrack, then

. Clearly,

is a poset map.
Let

. Next, we consider the fiber

. If

is spherical, then the fiber

is not pure. However,

. The boldface nodes in the figure correspond to the elements of

.

In the last example we observe that there are maximal elements of
\mathcal{P}(G_{2})
which are not union of some Sylow
2
-subgroups when
G=A_{5}
. However, any parabolic element in
\mathsf{Inf}(\mathcal{R}(G_{p}))
must necessarily be a union of Sylow
p
-subgroups if the minimal elements of
\mathcal{P}(G_{p})
are exactly the Sylow
p
-subgroups of
G
. Hence, we may ask the following question.

Question 2
.

For which finite groups
G
and prime numbers
p
, the minimal elements of
\mathcal{P}(G_{p})
are exactly the Sylow
p
-subgroups of
G
?

Let
G
be a group and
p
be a prime number dividing
|G|
. Recall that we define
\mathcal{S}_{p}(G)
as the poset of nontrivial
p
-subgroups of
G
. An important result in the field of subgroup complexes, nicknamed homological Sylow theorem, states that the Euler characteristic
\chi(\Delta(\mathcal{S}_{p}(G)))\equiv 1
modulo
|G|_{p}
, where
|G|_{p}
is the order of a Sylow
p
-subgroup of
G
(see
[
5
]
). Equivalently,
|G|_{p}
divides the reduced Euler characteristic
\tilde{\chi}(\Delta(\mathcal{S}_{p}(G)))
. Since
\mathcal{R}(G_{p})
is just the rack analogue of the poset
\mathcal{S}_{p}(G)
, it is natural to ask whether a similar result is valid in the context of subrack lattices. The next result tells us that this is indeed the case when
\mathcal{P}(G_{p})
has a certain property. The proof is also similar to the proof of the homological Sylow theorem (see
[
6
, Section 4]
and
[
8
, Proof 1 of Theorem 5.3.1]
). Recall that the
p
-core

O_{p}(G)
of
G
is defined as the largest normal
p
-subgroup of
G
. Obviously
O_{p}(G)
coincides with the
p
-power rack
G_{p}
exactly when
G
has a normal Sylow
p
-subgroup.

Theorem 21
.

Let
G
be a finite group and
p
be a prime number dividing
|G|
. Suppose for any
p
-subgroup
J
of
G
the set
\{S\in\mathcal{P}(G_{p})\mid J\subseteq S\}
has a unique minimum element. Then

\tilde{\chi}(\Delta(\overline{\mathcal{R}(G_{p})}))\equiv 0\mod{|G|_{p}}\;\text{ if and only if }\;O_{p}(G)\neq G_{p}.

Proof.

Suppose
P
is the normal Sylow
p
-subgroup of
G
. In such a case
\tilde{\chi}(\Delta(\overline{\mathcal{R}(G_{p})}))
would be either
+1
or
-1
as the subrack complex of a group rack is homotopy equivalent to a sphere by
[
12
, Proposition 1.3]
.

Next, suppose
G
has a non-normal Sylow
p
-subgroup
P
. In such a case
\Delta(\mathcal{P}(G_{p}))
would be non-empty. Let
p^{k}
be the order of
P
. Our aim is to show that
p^{k}
divides
\tilde{\chi}(\Delta(\overline{\mathcal{R}(G_{p})}))
.

For a simplicial complex
\Delta
we denote by
\#\Delta^{d}
the number of simplices of
\Delta
of dimension
d
. Let
m
be the dimension of
\Delta(\mathcal{S}(G_{p}))
and
n
be the dimension of
\Delta(\mathcal{P}(G_{p}))
. Consider the product

\displaystyle(-1)\tilde{\chi}(\Delta(\mathcal{S}(G_{p})))\cdot\tilde{\chi}(\Delta(\mathcal{P}(G_{p})))

\displaystyle=(-1)\sum\limits_{i=-1}^{m}(-1)^{i}\#\Delta(\mathcal{S}(G_{p}))^{i}\sum\limits_{j=-1}^{n}(-1)^{j}\#\Delta(\mathcal{P}(G_{p}))^{j}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{d=-2}^{m+n}(-1)^{d+1}\sum\limits_{i+j=d}\#\Delta(\mathcal{S}(G_{p}))^{i}\#\Delta(\mathcal{P}(G_{p}))^{j}

Observe that

\sum\limits_{i+j=d}\#\Delta(\mathcal{S}(G_{p}))^{i}\#\Delta(\mathcal{P}(G_{p}))^{j}=\#\Delta(\mathcal{S}(G_{p})\oplus\mathcal{P}(G_{p}))^{d+1}.

Since
\tilde{\chi}(\Delta(\mathcal{S}(G_{p})))
is either
+1
or
-1
, by Proposition
17
it is enough to show that
p^{k}
divides
\tilde{\chi}(\Delta(\mathcal{P}(G_{p})))
.

Let
P
be a Sylow
p
-subgroup of
G
. We shall show that the
P
-singular subcomplex
\Delta(\mathcal{P}(G_{p}))^{P-\mathrm{sing}}
of the complex of parabolic subracks is contractible. Let
J
be a nontrivial subgroup of
P
and
f\colon\mathcal{P}(G_{p})^{J}\to\mathcal{P}(G_{p})^{J}
be the map taking
S\in\mathcal{P}(G_{p})^{J}
to the meet of all coatoms of
\mathcal{R}(G_{p})
containing
J\vee S
in
\mathcal{R}(G_{p})
. Notice that the normalizer
N_{G}(S)
of
S
contains both
J
and
S
and its intersection with
G_{p}
must be properly contained in
G_{p}
as
S
is a parabolic subrack. This shows that the map
f
is well-defined and, further, it is a closure operator on
\mathcal{P}(G_{p})^{J}
. Obviously
f(\mathcal{P}(G_{p})^{J})
is a cone; hence,
\Delta(\mathcal{P}(G_{p})^{J})
is contractible. Then, by Lemma
5
,
\Delta(\mathcal{P}(G_{p}))^{P-\mathrm{sing}}
is contractible. In particular
\tilde{\chi}(\Delta(\mathcal{P}(G_{p}))^{P-\mathrm{sing}})=0
.

Take a parabolic subrack
T
which is not a vertex in
\Delta(\mathcal{P}(G_{p}))^{P-\mathrm{sing}}
. Since the stabilizer of
T
under the action of
P
is trivial,
P
acts on the set of simplices containing
T
freely. Therefore, we have the congruence

\#(\Delta(\mathcal{P}(G_{p}))^{P-\mathrm{sing}})^{i}\equiv\#\Delta(\mathcal{P}(G_{p}))^{i}\mod{p^{k}}

which implies the desired result.
∎

Remark 14
.

Unfortunately, we couldn’t prove Theorem
21
in full generality. Such a general Theorem would imply that
the complex
\Delta(\overline{\mathcal{R}(G_{p})})
is homotopy equivalent to a sphere if and only if
O_{p}(G)=G_{p}
. This last statement was pointed out by Volkmar Welker in a private communication and it can be considered as the rack analogue of a conjecture by Quillen in
[
6
]
stating
\Delta(\mathcal{S}_{p}(G))
is contractible if and only if
O_{p}(G)\neq 1
.
