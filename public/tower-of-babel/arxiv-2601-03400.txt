Eye-Q : A Multilingual Benchmark for Visual Word Puzzle Solving and Image-to-Phrase Reasoning
Ali Najar, Alireza Mirrokni, Arshia Izadyari, Sadegh Mohammadian, Amir Homayoon Sharifizade, Asal Meskin, Mobin Bagherian, Ehsaneddin Asgari

Abstract
Vision-Language Models (VLMs) have achieved strong performance on standard vision–language benchmarks, yet often rely on surface-level recognition rather than deeper reasoning. We propose visual word puzzles as a challenging alternative, as they require discovering implicit visual cues, generating and revising hypotheses, and mapping perceptual evidence to non-literal concepts in ways that are difficult to solve via literal grounding, OCR-heavy shortcuts, or simple retrieval-style matching. We introduce Eye-Q , a multilingual benchmark designed to assess this form of complex visual understanding. Eye-Q contains 1,343 puzzles in which a model observes a conceptually dense scene with a brief description and must infer a specific target word or phrase. The puzzles are intentionally unstructured and cue-implicit, with distractors and contextual relationships that demand selective attention, abstraction, and associative inference. The benchmark spans English, Persian, Arabic, and cross-lingual puzzles. We evaluate state-of-the-art VLMs using an open-ended, human-aligned protocol that probes hypothesis formation and revision under lightweight assistance. Results reveal substantial performance gaps, especially on abstract and cross-lingual puzzles, highlighting limitations in current models’ ability to construct and search over appropriate conceptual representations for flexible image-to-phrase inference; maximum accuracy reaches only 60.27%. Eye-Q is publicly available at https://huggingface.co/datasets/llm-lab/Eye-Q .

Introduction

Vision-language models (VLMs) have rapidly evolved into general-purpose assistants that can describe images, answer questions, and follow complex visual instructions
(
50
;
4
;
16
;
40
;
47
)
. As these systems become more capable and widely deployed, benchmarking them becomes increasingly important. Yet many existing benchmarks rely on questions with explicit, directly queryable evidence, and a large fraction of their content (or close variants) is available on the web or in widely circulated sources. This can let models succeed via surface recognition, shortcut heuristics, or training-data exposure rather than the reasoning we aim to measure
(
1
;
46
;
14
;
32
;
31
)
. A natural next step is to push toward evaluations that are harder to solve by appearance matching or retrieval. In particular, a challenging benchmark should be
(i) multimodal
in how it encodes meaning,
(ii) multi-step
in a way that supports hypothesis testing rather than one-shot guessing, and
(iii) multi/cross-lingual
, measuring performance beyond English scripts, while presenting these criteria in a creative and previously unseen manner.

Multimodality.

A broad suite of multimodal benchmarks probes higher-level cognition beyond perception, including multi-domain understanding
(
35
)
, compositional reasoning
(
55
;
29
)
, abstract reasoning
(
10
;
49
)
, and scientific understanding
(
36
;
52
)
. These benchmarks have been valuable, but many tasks still emphasize literal grounding: recognizing entities, reading text, and answering questions with evidence that is localized and directly queryable. Strong performance therefore does not always imply that a model can discover subtle cues in a complex scene or map perceptual evidence to non-literal concepts. This leaves a gap for evaluations where the signal is implicit.

Multi-step Reasoning.

Many evaluations reward systematic and rule-following reasoning that narrows toward a single correct solution
(
12
)
. This gap appears in puzzles such as rebus-like riddles and picture-word games
(
19
;
26
;
24
;
10
)
. Solving these tasks is abductive and iterative: solvers propose hypotheses about which aspects of the scene are cues, test them, and revise when they fail. In this setting, multi-step reasoning alone is insufficient, because without improving the underlying hypothesis, additional steps can amplify unproductive search. Recent puzzle-oriented evaluations underscore that even strong VLMs struggle when success depends on abstraction, symbolic substitution, and non-literal image-to-language associations
(
24
;
19
;
26
;
28
;
43
;
20
)
. However, many of these works rely on visually clean compositions or explicit text, making success largely a matter of OCR and shallow heuristics. Moreover, protocols can under-measure models when they do not allow hypothesis revision. This suggests a need for puzzle-style evaluations that are visually dense, cue-implicit, and paired with a human-aligned setup that supports controlled refinement without changing the task.

Multilinguality.

Multilinguality further compounds the challenge. While multilingual vision–language resources and evaluations have grown
(
34
;
30
;
15
)
, puzzle solving remains under-studied beyond English, particularly for non-Latin scripts and culturally grounded expressions. Puzzles often hinge on phonetic, orthographic, and idiomatic mechanisms that do not translate cleanly, thereby offering a rigorous test of reasoning across linguistic contexts. Uniquely, our work introduces the first systematic evaluation of visual word puzzles with Persian answers and cross-lingual puzzles that bridge English visual cues with Persian solutions.

Our Approach.

We introduce
Eye-Q
(Figure
1
), a multilingual benchmark for visual word puzzle solving and open-ended image-to-phrase inference. The task is designed to require implicit cue discovery in dense scenes and supports hypothesis revision via lightweight, human-aligned assistance. Even with these supports, performance across proprietary VLMs remains low, especially in cross-lingual settings, highlighting persistent gaps in non-literal vision–language reasoning.

Contributions.

Our work makes the following contributions:

•

Task Formulation.
We introduce visual word puzzle solving as a vision–language reasoning task requiring multi-modal cue integration, multi-step reasoning and search, and multilingual/cross-lingual generalization.

•

Benchmark Dataset.
We introduce a multilingual benchmark of 1,343 puzzles spanning English, Persian, Arabic, and cross-lingual settings.

•

Cue-Implicit Design.
The puzzles are cue-implicit, unstructured, and conceptually dense, avoiding token-explicit cues (OCR or templated layouts) and requiring abstraction and associative reasoning.

•

Evaluation Protocol.
We propose a human-aligned, open-ended evaluation protocol that probes hypothesis formation and revision through lightweight assistance (answer-length hints, partial character reveals, few-shot examples, iterative refinement).

•

Empirical Findings.
We benchmark state-of-the-art VLMs and reveal substantial performance gaps, especially on abstract and cross-lingual puzzles, highlighting limits in current models’ ability to construct and search over conceptual representations.

Position of
Eye-Q
.

Eye-Q
connects these threads by evaluating open-ended image-to-phrase inference for non-literal puzzles in cue-implicit, distractor-rich scenes, and by extending the setting to English, Persian, Arabic, and cross-lingual puzzles.

3
Eye-Q

This section introduces
Eye-Q
and the design principles behind its puzzles. We first summarize the benchmark composition and instance format, then describe puzzle design and the data sources used for the monolingual and cross-lingual subsets.

3.1
Benchmark Overview

Eye-Q
is a multimodal benchmark designed to evaluate open-ended image-to-phrase reasoning under cue-implicit visual settings and multilingual wordplay. The benchmark contains 1,343 picture puzzles organized into four subsets: English, Persian, Arabic, and cross-lingual (Table
1
).

Each puzzle consists of an image and a short textual prompt that specifies the game rule (i.e., the image encodes a hidden target word or short phrase) and requests a single final answer. The task is open-ended: models must generate the target word/phrase rather than select from candidates.

The images are intentionally cue-implicit and conceptually dense: scenes contain multiple objects, attributes, and relationships that may function as semantic cues, often alongside distractors. Cues can involve object orientation, item counts, color, relative size, spatial relations (e.g., containment, overlap, inversion), material properties, posture, and affect. Text may appear in some images, but
Eye-Q
is designed to be anti-OCR: solving rarely reduces to simply reading visible text.

3.2
Puzzle Design

The core of
Eye-Q
lies in requiring a semantic leap from literal recognition to a non-literal linguistic target. Unlike standard VQA tasks that ask “What is in the image?”, our puzzles ask “What does this scene imply or represent?”. Solving typically requires: (i)
cue discovery
(identifying which elements are informative versus distractors), (ii)
relational abstraction
(reasoning over relations and transformations rather than isolated objects), and (iii)
linguistic association
(mapping the inferred concept to an idiom, pun, phonetic resemblance, or conventional phrase in the target language).

Puzzles range from monolingual wordplay to cross-lingual reasoning. In cross-lingual instances, cues naturally suggest words or concepts in one language, while the correct answer is expressed in the other, requiring phonetic, orthographic, or semantic bridging between English and Persian. Figure
1
provides representative examples.

3.3
Data Collection and Ground Truth

Eye-Q
is built from two sources: (i) the mobile game
Aftabe
(Appendix
A
), and (ii) English and Arabic puzzles designed by the authors and rendered into images using text-to-image generation models. We have obtained permission from
Aftabe
to use and redistribute the curated puzzles as part of
Eye-Q
.

Persian and Cross-Lingual Puzzles.

We curated Persian and cross-lingual puzzles from
Aftabe
, which contains visual puzzles with Persian answers. For each selected instance, we preserve the original image and the game-provided intended solution as ground truth. Because puzzles can admit multiple interpretations, we manually filter out items that are ambiguous or under-specified, retaining puzzles with a clear intended reading and a unique target answer. We then categorize each retained puzzle as Persian or cross-lingual based on whether solving requires an English–Persian bridge.

English and Arabic Puzzles.

We build the English and Arabic subsets by first selecting a target word or short phrase and writing a concise scene description intended to lead a human solver to that target. We then render the scene using text-to-image models (GPT
(
40
)
and Nano Banana
(
42
)
). All generated images are manually reviewed, and we retain only those that clearly support the intended solution and do not contain rendering artifacts or alternative plausible interpretations. Because the target answer is fixed during design and each image is filtered for clarity, these instances have controlled ground-truth labels that are verified to be consistent with the visual content.

Evaluation Hints.

Lightweight assistance signals such as answer-length hints and partial character reveal are not separate annotation fields in
Eye-Q
. However, they are directly derivable from the ground-truth answer (e.g., character count or revealing a fixed fraction of characters), so any user of the dataset can apply the same hinting schemes. In our experiments (Section
4
), we use these derived hints as controlled evaluation variants to provide human-aligned assistance without changing the underlying task.

Experiments

This section evaluates vision–language models on
Eye-Q
. We compare multiple models under four prompt variants and report accuracy across the benchmark’s language subsets. We include both proprietary and open-source LVLMs. Our goal is to measure open-ended puzzle solving under a human-aligned setup that supports hypothesis formation and revision, while keeping the underlying task unchanged.

4.2
Prompt Design and Input Format

For each puzzle, we query the model with a single image and a text prompt instantiated from a fixed base template, shown in Figure
2
. The base template includes: (i) a brief explanation of the game objective, and (ii) subset-specific instructions for English, Persian, Arabic, or cross-lingual puzzles. Across all experiments, we keep the base instructions and output format consistent and vary only the experiment-specific modules described below. We provide the exact prompts used for the experiments in Appendix
D
.

Decoding and scoring.

All main results reported in the paper use each model’s default decoding configuration rather than enforcing a shared decoding strategy such as greedy or fixed-temperature sampling. We report exact-match accuracy against the ground-truth answer, using the model’s final answer string with only minimal cleanup, specifically trimming whitespace and removing Persian and Arabic diacritics (A’rab).

Experimental Variations.

Using the shared base template, we evaluate four prompting variants:

1.

Basic.

We append an orthographic hint indicating the target answer length in characters to narrow the search space, and ask the model to output a single final answer.

2.

Few-Shot Chain-of-Thought.

We prepend three solved examples to the prompt. Each demonstration includes an image, the correct answer, and a concise description of the intended image–answer relation. Demonstrations are sampled from the same subset pool as the test puzzle, English, Persian, Arabic, or cross-lingual, and exclude the test instance. For fair comparison, the selected demonstration set is fixed across models for each evaluated sample.

3.

Iterative Refinement.

We first query the model using the basic prompt. If the answer is incorrect, we append the model’s previous guess and a short instruction to revise, then re-query. We allow up to two such revisions, for a total of three attempts. A puzzle is counted as correct if any attempt matches the ground truth.

4.

Partial Character Reveal.

We reveal a randomly selected 25% subset of the ground-truth answer’s non-space characters and mask the remainder, for example with underscores, then ask the model to output the full answer. The revealed positions are sampled with a fixed random seed to ensure reproducibility.

Across all variants, the input image is unchanged and the core task remains open-ended image-to-phrase inference; only the prompting strategy and lightweight hinting described above are varied.

5.1
Overall Performance

Across all models and prompt variants, accuracy remains far from high, indicating that
Eye-Q
poses a challenging open-ended image-to-phrase inference problem. Even the best-performing models struggle substantially on the harder subsets. Considering the best result achieved on each subset across all evaluated models and prompt variants, the peak accuracy is 60.27% on English (Grok 4.1 Fast, Partial Character Reveal), 43.03% on Persian (Gemini 2.5 Pro, Partial Character Reveal), 19.15% on Arabic (Gemini 2.5 Pro, Few-Shot CoT), and 29.15% on cross-lingual puzzles (Gemini 2.5 Pro, Partial Character Reveal). Thus, while English can reach moderate performance under supportive prompting, Arabic and cross-lingual puzzle solving remain particularly difficult even for the strongest models.

Performance differences across model families are also clear. Proprietary models (GPT-5.2, Gemini 2.5, Grok 4.1) dominate overall, while open-source models (Llama 4 Scout, Qwen 3 VL) remain low, including near-zero performance on Arabic and cross-lingual subsets in multiple settings. This highlights persistent weaknesses in multilingual cue discovery and non-literal phrase inference.

5.2
Assistance Effects

Prompt variants provide lightweight, human-aligned assistance, but they do not close the gap. Averaged across models and language subsets, iterative refinement improves accuracy over the basic setup from 11.53% to 15.63%, suggesting that allowing hypothesis revision can recover from some initial misreadings. Partial character reveal improves further to 18.11% on average.

These assistance variants also directly reduce a common concern in open-ended evaluation: that low exact-match accuracy might be dominated by output-space brittleness (large lexical search space or superficial near-misses). Partial character reveal constrains the output space without supplying answer candidates, and refinement gives models multiple chances to correct small mistakes without turning the task into candidate verification. Despite this, accuracy remains low even under supportive settings: for example, Arabic stays below 10% on average even with partial character reveal, and cross-lingual performance remains around 12% on average. This persistence suggests that the dominant failure mode is upstream of surface form: models often fail to identify which elements in a complex, distractor-rich scene constitute the right cues and to construct the correct abstract interpretation linking the scene to the intended hidden phrase. In this sense,
Eye-Q
stresses a different bottleneck than OCR-heavy or visually clean rebus-style settings, where reading explicit tokens and combining them compositionally can account for much of the solution.

5.3
Model Size Effect

To probe the effect of scale in a controlled, same-family setting, Figure

Larger models consistently perform better, and the gains tend to be most visible under assistance variants that encourage revision or constrain the output space. However, even at the largest size, accuracy remains far from saturated, reinforcing that simply scaling parameters is insufficient to overcome the core challenge posed by
Eye-Q

5.4
Language Coupling

To test whether model strengths transfer across languages, we analyze language coupling by correlating model accuracies between language subsets in the basic setup. Figure
5
reports Pearson and Spearman correlations computed across models using subset-level accuracies. Correlations are high overall, indicating that relative model ranking is largely consistent across languages.

The tightest coupling is between Persian and cross-lingual performance (Pearson 0.98, Spearman 1.00), suggesting that models strong on Persian puzzles tend also to be strong on cross-lingual puzzles that require bridging Persian and English. In contrast, Arabic shows weaker coupling with other subsets (with correlations around 0.88 in multiple pairings), consistent with Arabic being the most challenging setting for current models and potentially requiring additional robustness beyond what drives performance in English and Persian.

5.5
Semantic Near-Miss Analysis

Exact-match evaluation can in principle underestimate performance if models frequently produce answers that are semantically close to the ground truth but differ in surface form. To test this, we measure semantic proximity between model outputs and ground-truth answers using cosine similarity in an embedding space. Specifically, we compute cosine similarity between embeddings of the model’s final answer under iterative refinement and the ground-truth answer. We compute this similarity over failure cases only and visualize density estimates across language subsets in Figure
6
. Additional plots for other prompting variants show a similar trend and are provided in Appendix
B.5
.

The density estimates are concentrated at low similarity values across all four language settings, indicating that incorrect outputs are typically not semantically close to the target phrase. This suggests that low accuracy on
Eye-Q
is not primarily driven by minor linguistic differences or near-miss paraphrases penalized by strict string matching. Instead, the dominant failure mode appears to arise earlier: models often fail to identify the right cues amid distractors and to form the correct abstract interpretation linking the complex scene to the intended hidden phrase.

6
Conclusion

We introduced
Eye-Q
, a multilingual benchmark for visual word puzzle solving and open-ended image-to-phrase inference.
Eye-Q
contains 1,343 cue-implicit puzzles spanning English, Persian, Arabic, and cross-lingual Persian–English settings. Unlike prior puzzle-style evaluations that often center on visually clean rebus compositions or explicit text cues,
Eye-Q
emphasizes visually dense scenes with distractors, where success requires discovering which elements function as clues and composing a non-literal conceptual interpretation.

Across six state-of-the-art LVLMs, performance remains low. Even with human-aligned assistance such as few-shot CoT, iterative refinement, and partial character reveal, models continue to struggle, particularly on Arabic and cross-lingual subsets. A semantic similarity analysis of failures further suggests that errors are typically not near-miss paraphrases, reinforcing that the bottleneck is not only output-space brittleness but also cue selection and abstraction. We hope
Eye-Q
will provide a useful testbed for developing and measuring multimodal systems that better support hypothesis generation, revision, and multilingual reasoning beyond literal grounding.

Limitations

First, on rare occasions where alternative interpretations exist,
Eye-Q
prioritizes the intended ground truth to maintain rigorous evaluation metrics.

Second, dataset generation relies on contributors who are fluent in the target languages. This can make it harder to scale the benchmark to additional languages, dialects, or low-resource varieties while keeping difficulty and style consistent.

Third, reliable human evaluation is also non-trivial. To assess correctness fairly across languages, each language subset should ideally be evaluated by native speakers or annotators with comparable proficiency. This increases coordination effort and cost, and it can introduce variability between annotator groups, especially when borderline cases depend on subtle linguistic judgments.

If these challenges are addressed, the benchmark’s coverage is likely to improve, and its evaluations to become more reliable and reproducible.

Temperature Ablation

Table
3
shows that performance is strongly and consistently sensitive to decoding temperature, with clear differences across models. For the most capable model, Gemini 2.5 Pro, accuracy is relatively stable at low temperature but declines steadily as
T
increases. This monotonic degradation matches the intuition that, in a constrained exact-match evaluation, additional sampling noise mainly perturbs reasoning traces that would otherwise remain correct. Grok 4.1 Fast follows the same overall trend from

to

, but drops sharply at
T{=}2.0
, suggesting a distinct failure regime once stochasticity becomes large.

, and GPT-5.2 displays a milder version of the same effect. Llama 4 Scout gains slightly at low temperature but breaks down completely at very high
T{=}5.0
. Overall, these findings suggest that modest stochasticity can help less reliable models recover from early incorrect decisions, whereas stronger reasoning-focused models are best evaluated under near-deterministic greedy decoding.

reports two complementary statistics as a function of model size: (i) the average number of attempts conditioned on solved puzzles only, and (ii) the average number of attempts over all puzzles, where unsolved instances are assigned the maximum of five attempts.

When conditioning on solved puzzles, the average number of attempts exhibits a non-monotonic trend, it first decreases from 8B to 32B models, then increases for the largest 235B model. The initial decrease suggests that moderately larger models can identify the correct interpretation more quickly, requiring fewer revision steps to reach a valid solution. The subsequent increase at the largest scale reflects a different regime where larger models appear able to sustain longer chains of productive reasoning, using additional refinement steps to recover from partial hypotheses and eventually converge on the correct answer. In contrast, when averaging over all puzzles, the mean number of attempts decreases monotonically with model size. This trend is driven by the sharp reduction in unsolved puzzles at larger scales, which otherwise dominate the mean by exhausting all five attempts.

Accuracy Scaling with Model Size.

Figure
8
extends the controlled scaling analysis of the Qwen3-VL family to all language subsets. We plot accuracy versus model size for Qwen3-VL-8B, 32B, and 235B under the same four prompt variants.

Across subsets, scaling generally improves performance, but the magnitude and stability of gains depend on both language and prompting. The English subset shows the most consistent and monotonic improvement with size across variants, with particularly clear gains under iterative refinement and partial character reveal, suggesting that larger models benefit more from settings that enable hypothesis revision or restrict the output space. Persian and cross-lingual subsets also trend upward with size, though improvements are smaller and more variant-dependent, indicating that cross-lingual bridging and non-Latin orthography remain challenging even for larger models. Arabic exhibits the weakest and least consistent scaling behavior. The accuracy remains low overall and improvements with size are modest, indicating that the dominant bottleneck is not simply capacity.

Overall, these results suggest that increasing parameters helps within a same-family comparison, yet performance remains far from saturated across all subsets, highlighting that scale alone does not resolve the core reasoning challenges posed by
Eye-Q
.

Appendix D
Prompt Options

This appendix describes the prompt building blocks used in our experiments. Each query includes one image and a text prompt formed by concatenating a fixed set of reusable modules, as outlined below.

D.1
Language and subset rules

We use four rule modules, one for each subset: English, Persian, Arabic, and cross-lingual. Each module specifies the required answer language and any subset-specific constraints. For instance, in the cross-lingual subset, the target answer language is Persian (Farsi), but solving the puzzle may require English knowledge because the image can contain English words, letters, numbers, or concepts. The intended answer may therefore include English elements directly (e.g., via transliteration) or combine English and Persian components.

D.2
Shared task and output instructions

All experimental settings include the same core instruction module. This block explains the game setup, states what the model should infer from the image and the accompanying text, and clarifies the expected form of the response. Crucially, it also enforces a strict output constraint to ensure consistent evaluation across models and prompt variants.

D.3
Hint modules

To provide additional guidance without changing the underlying task, we use three lightweight hint modules that can be optionally appended to the base prompt. These hints are designed to constrain the search space or support multi-round solving:

•

Answer-length hint:
provides the answer length in characters, excluding spaces.

•

Partial-reveal hint:
provides a masked pattern where a subset of characters is revealed and all remaining positions are replaced by underscores.

•

Iterative-refinement hint:
provides feedback that includes the model’s previous answer, enabling a revision step in subsequent rounds.

D.4
Few-shot CoT demonstrations

For the few-shot CoT condition, we prepend three solved demonstrations from the same subset as the target puzzle. We prepare one demonstration block per subset (English, Persian, Arabic, and cross-lingual). Each demonstration includes an image and a concise, structured rationale with two fields: primary clues and candidate answers. The full set of demonstrations used in our experiments is provided below.

You are an expert multi-modal puzzle solver. You solve picture word puzzles.

GAME DESCRIPTION:

- You will see exactly ONE image per puzzle.

- The image may depict objects, people, scenes, text, icons, or abstract compositions.

- The goal is to infer a SINGLE intended answer: one word or a short phrase.

- The image is a deliberately constructed clue for a linguistic target, NOT a request to describe the scene.

- The intended answer may be:

- a literal word,

- an idiom or proverb,

- a pun or wordplay,

- a common expression,

- a culturally meaningful phrase,

- or a proper noun / named entity (person, place, title, brand, named item).

1.

Identify candidate clue units in the image:

- the most salient objects/entities

- any text, letters, numbers, symbols, or icons

- any repeated motif/pattern

2.

Select ONLY 2–4 PRIMARY clue units:

- prefer central/emphasized/repeated units

- compress repeated motifs into one unit

- ignore minor background details unless they clearly change a primary unit

3.

Hypothesize a simple composition:

- the answer is usually formed by combining or transforming the primary units

- prefer the simplest coherent interpretation with the fewest assumptions

4.

Choose the best final answer:

- it should be natural/common in the target language

- it should explain the primary units as a single intended construction

- prioritize global coherence over matching every local detail

- Provide exactly ONE final answer (single word or short phrase).

- If uncertain, choose the most plausible candidate under the simplest coherent interpretation.
