Evolution of recursive trees with limited memory
Omer Angel, Shankar Bhamidi, Serte Donderwinkel, Neeladri Maitra, Akshay Sakanaveeti

Abstract
. Motivated by questions in social networks, distributed computing and probabilistic combinatorics, the last few years have seen increasing interest in network evolution models where new vertices entering the system need to make decisions based on a partial snapshot of the current state of the network. This paper considers a specific variant of the classical random recursive tree dynamics, where a vertex at time n + 1 n+1 has information only on those vertices that have arrived in the interval [ j ⁡ ( n ) , n ] [j(n),n] for a sequence j ⁡ ( n ) ↑ ∞ j(n)\uparrow\infty , and connects to vertices uniformly at random amongst this set. We consider two different regimes on the density information, termed macroscopic and mesoscopic regimes, which respectively correspond to j ⁡ ( n ) = θ ​ n j(n)=\theta n for some θ ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) \theta\in(0,1) , and j ⁡ ( n ) = n − n β j(n)=n-n^{\beta} for some β ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) \beta\in(0,1) . Our main interest is in studying asymptotics of various functionals of the network, including local weak limits and the height of the network. We show that in the macroscopic regime, the local limit is expressed in terms of an associated continuous time branching process that depends on the parameter θ \theta , while it is a Poisson ⁡ ( 1 ) \mathrm{Poisson}(1) -branching process in the mesoscopic regime for any β ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) \beta\in(0,1) . Furthermore, the height of the macroscopic tree is logarithmic, which we prove exploiting a connection with scaled-attachment random recursive trees (SARRTs) as studied in [ 20 ] , while it is polynomial in the mesoscopic regime; our argument in this latter case relies on a differential equation approach to track the ancestor indices of late-coming vertices, together with a multiscale analysis. Further, we develop an exploration algorithm to simultaneously reveal the ancestral path of youngest vertices. Using this algorithm, we show that in the mesoscopic regime, the global structure experiences a phase transition at β = 1 / 2 \beta=1/2 .

1.
Introduction

Driven by questions in probabilistic combinatorics, as well as the vast quantities of network valued data in the last few years, the last few years have seen the development of a number of new mathematical network models to understand the emergence of macroscopic properties in such systems
[
4
,
42
,
43
,
18
,
26
,
1
]
. One sub-area in this rapidly burgeoning field is dynamic or temporal networks,
[
32
,
39
]
.

The preferential attachment class of network evolution models assumes new vertices enter the system and then make probabilistic choices for connecting to existing vertices based on the
entire
state of the current network. There has been increasing interest in understanding the impact of limited information availability for new vertices on the system’s state and its role in the evolution of real world systems. This has motivated (at least) two distinct lines of recent research:

a)

Network evolution schemes where individuals try to form connections via limited scope explorations of the underlying network, such as random walks, initialized at uniform locations in the network run for a few number of steps
[
36
,
11
,
14
,
27
]
.

b)

More relevant for this work, incorporating limited temporal information, where new individuals have information only on a subset of individuals in the network and need to make their (probabilistic) decisions based on this information
[
8
,
34
,
24
,
10
,
9
]
.

The goal of this paper is to propose and understand a specific class of such models, where new individuals only have information on more recent individuals in the network.
We are motivated by real-world network scenarios where older individuals, even though had been influential during some time period, lose relevance over time, and thus are less preferred by younger incoming individuals for developing connections. Consider for example, networks of research papers where a pair of research papers are linked if one refers the another. A new incoming research paper is more likely to refer to a recent, well-explained survey, than a paper that is quite old by now, but greatly influential in its own time, perhaps simply because it was always a little harder to read. Let us proceed with the formal model definition next.

1.1.
Network model

We consider a model of recursively growing random trees
(\mathcal{T}_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
as follows.
\mathcal{T}_{1}
is the singleton tree with only vertex
1
and no edges. For
n\geqslant 1
, given
\mathcal{T}_{n}
, we construct
\mathcal{T}_{n+1}
by –

i)

letting the vertex set of
\mathcal{T}_{n+1}
to be the union of the vertex set of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
and a new vertex
n+1
;

ii)

drawing an edge between the vertex
n+1
and a random vertex of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
sampled with probability proportional to
f(D_{n}(v))\mathbf{1}_{\{j(n)\leqslant v\leqslant n\}}
,

where
f:\mathbb{N}\to(0,\infty)
is an attachment function measuring the attractiveness of individuals in the current network based on their degree,
D_{n}(v)
is the degree of the vertex
v
in the tree
\mathcal{T}_{n}
, and
j:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
is a monotone non-decreasing function satisfying
j(n)\leqslant n
for all
n\geqslant 1
.

In words, the new vertex
n+1
arrives in the already constructed network
\mathcal{T}_{n}
and has information only on the more recent individuals in the sense that it can connect only to a
young
vertex which arrived at time
j(n)
or later; in principle it can use the degree of this vertex to make its decision.
One should think of
j(n)
as a ‘age-preference threshold’ for the vertex
n+1
, beyond which it chooses not to develop connections.

In order to develop the salient features of such models, for this paper, we
focus on the case

f(x)=1
(uniform attachment) and consider the following two regimes for
j(n)
:

a)

Macroscopic regime:

j(n)=\lfloor\theta n\rfloor
The term “macroscopic” is meant to evoke the mental picture that in the large network limit, new incoming vertices have a positive density of information, in terms of the network size, to make their choices. In this regime, the model is a specific example of the so-called scaled attachment recursive trees (SARRT) introduced in
[
20
]
and whose depth and height asymptotics were studied in the above paper.

b)

Mesoscopic regime:

j(n)=n-\lfloor n^{\beta}\rfloor
for some
\beta\in(0,1)
.

The main goal of this paper is proposing the general model above and describing asymptotics in the specific “uniform attachment with bounded information” setting.

Remark 1.1
.

One can also consider the case
j(n)=\lfloor n^{\beta}\rfloor
, in which case we expect the model to mostly display similarities with the
j(n)=1
case, although we believe there are some potential differences. As the latter case has been extensively pursued in the literature, we don’t put any focus on this case in this paper, and leave this exploration for future work. Similarly, in the model definition, one can consider the case when the incoming vertex comes in with more than one edge, in which case the obtained networks are not trees anymore, and are more complex. We leave this for future work as well.

Results

2.1.
Results on
local functionals

We first start by describing the local limits in the two regimes.

2.1.1.
Macroscopic regime

To state our main result, we need some final notation. For
n\geqslant 1

\mathbb{T}_{n}
be the space of all rooted trees on

\mathbb{T}=\cup_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{T}_{n}

\mathbb{T}_{0}=\emptyset
will be used to represent the empty tree (tree on zero vertices). For any
\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{T}
, let
\rho_{\mathbf{t}}
denote the root of this tree.

Recall
j(n)=\theta n
for the macroscopic regime where
the parameter
\theta\in(0,1)
. Define
c_{\theta}=\log{\theta^{-1}}\in(0,\infty)
. Let
\mathcal{N}_{\theta}
denote a rate
(1-\theta)^{-1}
Poisson process on the interval
[0,c_{\theta}]
. Let
\BP_{\theta}
denote a continuous time branching process with offspring point process
\mathcal{N}_{\theta}
, started with one individual at time
t=0
.
Observe that since
c_{\theta}(1-\theta)^{-1}>1

Let
T_{1}

be an exponentially distributed random variable with mean
1
, which we write as

T_{1}\sim\exp(1)
, independent of
\BP_{\theta}
. Let
\boldsymbol{\varpi}_{\theta}
denote the distribution of
\BP_{\theta}(T_{1})
, viewed as a random finite rooted tree on
\mathbb{T}
, where we retain only genealogical information between individuals in
\BP_{\theta}(T_{1})
.

Theorem 2.1

(Local weak convergence in the macroscopic regime)
.

Fix
\theta\in(0,1)
. Then
\left\{\mathcal{T}(n):n\geqslant 1\right\}
converges in probability in the extended fringe sense (Def.
4.1
(
c
)) to the unique infinite
sin
-tree with fringe distribution
\boldsymbol{\varpi}_{\theta}
.

This result has the following implication. Here, with some abuse of notation, for any
t\geqslant 0
, we use
\mathcal{N}_{\theta}(t)
for the number of points in the point process
\mathcal{N}_{\theta}
and assume
\mathcal{N}_{\theta}
independent of
T_{1}\sim\exp(1)
as before.
Denote by
N_{k}(n)
the number of vertices with degree equal to
k
in the tree
\mathcal{T}_{n}
.

Corollary 2.2
.

In the setting of Theorem
2.1
, the degree counts
\left\{N_{k}(n):k\geqslant 1\right\}
of the random tree
\mathcal{T}_{n}
as in (
2
) satisfy for

,

\frac{N_{k}(n)}{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{N}_{\theta}(T_{1})=k-1)\text{ as }n\to\infty.

2.1.2.
Mesoscopic regime

Let
\boldsymbol{\varpi}_{{\sf Poisson},1}
denote the distribution of a critical Galton-Watson branching process with Poisson mean one offspring on
\mathbb{T}
. Recall from
[

,
6

that if
\mathcal{T}_{n}
is random rooted tree sampled uniformly amongst all

-tree with fringe distribution
\boldsymbol{\varpi}_{{\sf Poisson},1}
.

Theorem 2.3
.

Consider the sequence of random trees
\left\{\mathcal{T}_{n}:n\geqslant 2\right\}
in the mesoscopic regime with parameter
\beta\in(0,1)
. Then
\left\{\mathcal{T}_{n}:n\geqslant 2\right\}
converges in probability in the extended fringe sense (Def.

(

)) to the unique infinite

-tree with fringe distribution

. This implies in particular, the degree distribution satisfies the asymptotics for

\frac{N_{k}(n)}{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\longrightarrow}}\frac{e^{-1}}{(k-1)!}\text{ as }n\to\infty,

.

The above result thus implies that in the mesoscopic regime, the local weak limit of the sequence of trees, for any choice of

is the same as the local weak limit of a the uniform random tree (alternatively a uniformly sampled random spanning tree from the complete graph) as shown in

2.2.
Results on global functionals

For a rooted tree
T
with root
\rho
, we denote the height of
T
by
h(T)
, where by definition

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(T)=\sup\{d_{T}(v,\rho):v\in T\},

where
d_{T}
denotes the (graph) distance of
v
from
\rho
. From here on, throughout the paper, we denote by
H_{n}
the height
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
of the tree
\mathcal{T}_{n}
. As new incoming vertices prefer to connect to late arrived individuals (as quantified by
j(n)
), it is natural to expect that the height of the obtained trees should be stochastically larger than the case
j(n)=1
, in which case it turns out that the tree has height about
\log n
, see e.g., Pittel
[
44
]
. But how
tall
does it get? In this section, we answer this question for the two regimes we consider. We also establish a phase transition on the global geometry of our trees in the mesoscopic regime.

Before diving into the results, let us describe the non-obvious findings in each regime starting with the macroscopic regime. Recall the branching process
\BP_{\theta}
that arose to describe the local weak limit in this regime. Note that this branching process can go extinct with positive probability, namely
\pr(|\BP_{\theta}(\infty)|<\infty)>0
. Here we write
|\BP_{\theta}(\infty)|
for the final size of the process.

For each
n\geqslant 1
, let
T_{n}=\inf\left\{t:|\BP_{\theta}|=n\right\}
for the time for this process to get to size
n
with the convention
T_{n}=\infty
if
|\BP_{\theta}(\infty)|<n
, i.e., if the branching process dies out before it grows to size
n
. Write
\mathcal{T}_{n,\theta}^{\BP}=\BP_{\theta}(T_{n})
to denote the random tree representing the genealogical information of the individuals present in the population at this time and consider the sequence of trees
\left\{\mathcal{T}_{n,\theta}^{\BP}:n\geqslant 1\right\}
. This sequence of trees is
growing
only on the set
\BP_{\theta}(\infty)=\infty
and thus, this sequence of trees is different in distribution from
\left\{\mathcal{T}(n):n\geqslant 1\right\}
. The next proposition follows easily from classical results and proof techniques in Kingman and Pittel
[
35
,
44
]
and can be found in Section
5
. We need some additional notation. Define
for any
\lambda>1
,

(1)

\phi(\lambda)=\frac{1-\theta^{\lambda}}{\lambda(1-\theta)},\qquad\mu(a)=\inf_{\lambda>1}{\phi(\lambda)e^{\lambda a}},\qquad\kappa(\theta)=\sup\left\{a:\mu(a)<1\right\}.

Proposition 2.5
.

On the set
\left\{|\BP_{\theta}(\infty)|=\infty\right\}
, the height of the branching process genealogies
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{T}_{n,\theta}^{\BP})
satisfy
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{T}_{n,\theta}^{\BP})/\log{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{a.s.}}}{{\longrightarrow}}[\kappa(\theta)]^{-1}
where
\kappa(\theta)
is as in (
1
).

The next result shows that, not just local weak limits, but even the height of the original tree process has the same asymptotics as the branching process driven system.

Theorem 2.6
.

The height
sequence
(H_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
of the original growing trees
\left\{\mathcal{T}(n):n\geqslant 1\right\}
in the macroscopic regime satisfy
H_{n}/\log{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{a.s.}}}{{\longrightarrow}}[\kappa(\theta)]^{-1}
where
\kappa(\theta)
is as in Prop.
2.5
.

Thus, in the macroscopic regime, the effect of
j(n)
is not strong enough to increase the height (in order) from the
j(n)=1
case.

Remark 2.7
.

The convergence of the properly scaled height was already proven in
[
20
]
. The main contribution of the above result is showing the relationship between the limit constants derived in
[
20
]
and the so-called “first birth” problem in continuous time branching processes. The paper
[
20
]
focuses on the height and depth of a general class of random tree growth models; Section
3
shows how the local weak limits of these models can be derived and described using associated continuous time branching processes, using the proof techniques in this paper.

Now recall that Theorem
2.3
implies that in the mesoscopic regime, the local weak limit is insensitive to the choice of the parameter
\beta
and is in fact the same as the limit of a uniform random tree. The next results shows that the story is quite different for the height and in fact for the global scaling of distances.

Theorem 2.8
.

Consider the mesoscopic regime with
j(n)=n-n^{\beta}
for a fixed paramter
\beta\in(0,1)
. The height
H_{n}
of the tree
\mathcal{T}_{n}
satisfies
H_{n}/n^{1-\beta}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\longrightarrow}}\frac{2}{1-\beta}
as
n\to\infty
.

Thus, we note that in the mesoscopic regime, the effect of
j(n)
is strong enough to completely change the height behavior from the
j(n)=1
case.

We delve deeper into the global structure in the mesoscopic regime next. The following three theorems establish a phase transition in the global structure of the graph in the mesoscopic regime at
\beta=1/2
. Informally, for
\beta<1/2
, the global structure approximates a line. For
\beta>1/2
the tree contains many disjoint long paths, close in length to the height of the tree, consisting of the ancestral lines of the youngest vertices. For
\beta=1/2
, the ancestral lines of the youngest vertices all have length that approximate the height of the tree, but now they meet or
coalesce
at a height that is random on the leading order. Observe that this phase transition is not observed in the local limit, nor in the height asymptotics in Theorems
2.3
and
2.8
. For the relevant relatively standard definitions of Gromov-Hausdorff topology that are used in the next theorems, we refer the reader to
[
19
]
,
[
29
, Section 3]
.

Theorem 2.9
.

In the mesoscopic regime when
\beta\in(0,1/2)
we have

\frac{1-\beta}{2}n^{-(1-\beta)}\mathcal{T}_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\longrightarrow}}L

in the Gromov–Hausdorff topology, with
L
a line segment of length
1
.

Theorem 2.10
.

In the mesoscopic regime when
\beta\in(1/2,1)
we have, for any
k\geqslant 1
, for
\mathcal{T}^{(k)}_{n}
the subtree of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
spanned by the vertices
n,n-1,\dots,n-k+1
,

\frac{1-\beta}{2}n^{-(1-\beta)}\mathcal{T}^{(k)}_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\longrightarrow}}S_{k}

in the Gromov–Hausdorff topology, for
S_{k}
a star graph with
k
legs of length
1
.

Theorem 2.11
.

In the mesoscopic regime when
\beta=1/2
, for any
k\geqslant 2
and for
\mathcal{T}^{(k)}_{n}
the subtree of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
spanned by vertices
n,n-1,\dots,n-k+1
,

\frac{1}{4}n^{-1/2}\mathcal{T}^{(k)}_{n}\to T_{k}

in the Gromov–Hausdorff topology, for
T_{k}
a rooted
\mathbb{R}
-tree with
k
leaves, all at height
1
, and its branchpoints at heights
X_{1}<\dots<X_{k-1}
, where, for
U_{1},\dots,U_{k-1}
independent
\operatorname{uniform}[0,1]
random variables, we may define
X_{k-1}=U_{k-1}^{1/(4k(k-1))}
, and, recursively, for
2\leqslant\ell\leqslant k-1
,
X_{\ell-1}=X_{\ell}U_{\ell-1}^{1/(4\ell(\ell-1))}
.

In particular, observe that, although distances in
\mathcal{T}_{n}
are of the order
n^{1/2}
and the global structure is random on the leading order, a potential limit of
n^{-1/2}\mathcal{T}_{n}
can almost surely be distinguished from the Brownian continuum random tree, as the latter does not contain a pair of leaves that realize the height of the tree and have their most recent common ancestor at a non-trivial height. A full scaling limit result is better postponed for a future work, see also the open problems in Section
3.3
. But let us remark in passing that the limiting random fractals that should arise as such scaling limits, has the potential to give rise to novel universality classes in the world of random trees.

3.
Discussion and extensions

3.1.
Related work

The study of uniform random recursive trees (corresponding to
j(n)=1
of our model) goes back to papers by Na and Rapoport
[
41
]
, Moon
[
40
]
and Gastwirth
[
30
]
. We also refer the reader to the book of Drmota
[
25
]
and the survey by Smythe and Mahmoud
[
45
]
. The study of depth properties of random recursive trees began with the papers of Devroye
[
22
]
and Mahmoud
[
37
]
, who showed that the height of the vertex
n
is about
\log n
, and Pittel
[
44
]
later showed that the height of the tree is about
e\log n
by using a continuous time embedding.

As in our work, various generalizations of this model has been considered in the literature. The earliest and most notable being by Szymański
[
46
]
who considered the case when a new incoming vertex sample a parent
preferentially
with a probability proportional to its degree. This gives rise to
plane-oriented recursive trees
, also known as
preferential attachment trees
. Works of Mahmoud
[
38
]
and Pittel
[
44
]
analyze the height of these trees, which again turns out to be
\log n
in order. When an incoming new vertex is allowed to connect to more than one parent, the preferential choice gives rise to the well known
preferential attachment model
of Barabasi and Albert
[
12
]
. While when a new vertex samples more than one parent, but now uniformly at random, one obtains the so called
random recursive directed acyclic graphs
or
DAGs
, introduced by Devroye and Lu
[
21
]
, and found application later in modeling circuits, see Tsukiji and Xhafa
[
47
]
; Arya, Golin and Mehlhorn
[
7
]
.

In a different direction, Devroye, Fawzi and Fraiman
[
20
]
introduce the model of
scaled attachment random recursive trees
, or
SARRTs
(see Section
3.2
for the precise definition), where the sampling of a random parent by the incoming new vertex is guided by a given probability distribution on
[0,1]
, and prove that the height of these trees are of order
\log n
. Various other generalizations have been considered in the literature, see e.g.
[
11
]
for a model where the new incoming vertex uniformly samples a vertex in the current tree, and then goes up its ancestral line for a random amount and finally connects to where it ends up,
[
3
]
for a model where the new incoming vertex connects to a uniformly sampled
neighbor
, of a randomly sampled node in the existing network, and
[
15
]
for a model where the set of vertices of the tree is divided into two communities, and the new incoming vertex has to make probabilistic decisions on what type it assumes and what type it (uniformly) connects to. Also relevant are the two works
[
9
]
and
[
10
]
where the authors consider a model where incoming vertices actually prefer earlier arrived vertices to connect to, as a consequence of not having enough information on the current state of the network. We also refer the reader to the nice survey
[
33
]
on fringe trees by Holmgren and Janson, and to Devroye’s notes
[
23
]
on branching processes.

3.2.
Local weak limits of general SAARTs

One goal of this paper is to develop new proof techniques for proving local weak convergence of random tree models to limiting infinite objects. While Theorem
2.1
describes local weak convergence for the specific model occupying the majority of the paper, as mentioned in
Section
1.1
,
in the macroscopic regime
our model
is a specific example of scaled attachment random recursive trees (SARRT) as formulated in
[
20
]
.
The model is described as follows:

a)

Fix a distribution
\mu
on
[0,1]
. We will restrict to the setting where
\mu
has a density
f_{V}
with respect to the Lebesgue measure.

b)

Let
V_{1},V_{2},\ldots,
be
i.i.d.
with distribution
\mu
.

c)

Generate a sequence of random trees where recursively after constructing
\mathcal{T}_{n}
, to construct
\mathcal{T}_{n+1}
generate a new vertex labelled
n+1
and connect it to vertex labelled
\lfloor nV_{n}\rfloor
.

Consider the function

\lambda_{V}(x)=f_{V}(e^{-x}),\qquad x\geqslant 0.

Let
\mathcal{P}_{V}
denote a Poisson point process with intensity function
\lambda_{V}
. Let
\BP
be a continuous time branching process started with one individual at time
t=0
and offspring distribution
\mathcal{P}_{V}
. Let
T_{1}\sim\exp(1)
independent of
\BP_{V}
. Let
\boldsymbol{\varpi}_{V}
denote the distribution of

3.3.
Open questions

The goal of this paper is to understand the impact of lack of information on the state of the network in the evolution of the network. There are a host of unexplored directions.
Some of these include:

a)

Considering settings where instead of uniformly attaching to a vertex amongst those observed, one uses the degree distribution of the vertices available to make probabilistic decisions
in a preferential fashion.

b)

As outlined in Remark
1.1
, it is interesting to consider more general choices for the sequence

, and the case where the network growth is governed by vertices coming in with more than one edge, i.e., the non-tree setting.

c)

Deriving
more detailed second order asymptotics for the fluctuation of the height about its mean. The phase transition implied by Theorems

and
2.10
suggests that, in the mesoscopic regime, the second-order behaviour is different for

and
\beta>1/2

the maximum is realized by the length of a single long branch, whereas for

it results from a competition of many long branches, restricting the fluctuations in the latter case.

d)

Studying the limit in the Gromov–Hausdorff topology of

for
\beta=1/2
in the mesoscopic regime. We conjecture that this model converges to a compact random fractal that is approximated by the limit

of the subtree of

spanned by

by making

4.1.1.
Convergence on the space of trees

For

5.1.
Local weak convergence

We approximate the discrete tree using a continuous time branching process (CTBP). In this process, each vertex gives birth to children, and the time instants marking the child births follow a point process on the real line that is a Poisson process

Lemma 5.1

(Malthusian rate of growth)

Consider the branching process

The Malthusian rate of growth of the branching process
\BP_{\theta}
namely
\lambda(\theta)=1

b)

The offspring distribution satisfies the

Proof.

Recall the offspring point process

\mathscr{T}(\log n-\log\ell,\mathbf{w}^{(\ell)})
, where
w^{(\ell)}_{j,k}=\tau_{j,k}^{\ell}=\log\left(\frac{T^{\ell}_{j,k}}{T^{\ell}_{j,k-1}}\right)
,
j\geqslant 0,k\geqslant 1
. In particular, the fringe of a uniformly chosen vertex is given by

\mathscr{T}(\log n-\log\lceil nU\rceil,\mathbf{w}^{(\lceil nU\rceil)})
. By Corollary
5.3
,
(\log n-\log\lceil nU\rceil,\mathbf{w}^{(\lceil nU\rceil)})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}(T_{1},\mathbf{w}^{*})
where
w^{*}_{j,k}=\mathscr{E}_{j,k}
,
j\geqslant 0,k\geqslant 1
. Moreover, we have
\mathbb{P}\left((T_{1},\mathbf{w}^{*})\in\mathscr{W}^{c}\right)=0
.
Hence, by the continuous mapping theorem,
\mathscr{T}(\log n-\log\lceil nU\rceil,\mathbf{w}^{(\lceil nU\rceil)})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathscr{T}(T_{1},\mathbf{w}^{*})
, which has the law
\boldsymbol{\varpi}_{\theta}
. This implies the local weak convergence in the expected fringe sense.

The convergence of the (expected) empirical degree distribution is an immediate consequence of this local weak convergence.

\blacksquare

5.3.
Proof of local weak convergence in the mesoscopic regime: Theorem
2.3
:

The main idea of the proof is to do a
branching process approximation, to the breadth-first exploration of the fringe of a vertex in the process
\mathcal{T}_{n}
. Before we prove local weak convergence, we argue that the degree of a vertex can roughly approximated by a Poisson(1) random variable.
Let us denote by
d_{TV}
the total variation distance between two discrete distributions.

Lemma 5.5

(Poisson Approximation)
.

Let
D_{j,n}
be the degree of vertex
j
at time
n
.
For any
\epsilon>1
, there exists
j_{0}
such that for all
j_{0}\leqslant j\leqslant n
,

\displaystyle d_{TV}\left(D_{j,n},\text{Poisson}(1)\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\epsilon\left[1-(1+\epsilon j^{\beta-1})^{-\beta}\right]+\epsilon j^{-\beta}+\max(\epsilon-1,j^{-\beta})+\mathds{1}\left\{N_{j}\geqslant n-j\right\}\left|\frac{n-j}{j^{\beta}}-1\right|

where
N_{j}=\left|\left\{i\geqslant j:i-i^{\beta}\leqslant j\right\}\right|.

To explain the bound above, we call a vertex labeled
j
a
good vertex
at time
n
if
N_{j}\leqslant n
. Note that
N_{j}
is the maximal index of a vertex that can potentially attach to vertex
j
. In other words, a
good vertex
at time
n
had seen all the vertices that it could potentially connect to by time
n
. Thus, for good vertices its degree distribution can be approximated well by Poisson distribution. The error made by Poisson approximation for the
good vertices
is accounted by the first three terms, while the last term accounts for the error for
bad vertices
at time
n
.

Proof.

For any
j\leqslant i
, define
Y_{i,j}=\mathds{1}\left\{\text{vertex }i\text{ connects to vertex }j\right\}
, we then have
Y_{i,j}\sim\text{Ber}\left(\frac{\mathds{1}\left\{i-i^{\beta}\leqslant j\right\}}{i^{\beta}}\right).
Furthermore, for any
j\geqslant 1
, the collection of random variables
\left\{Y_{i,j}:i\geqslant j\right\}
are independent. Let
N_{j}=|\left\{i:i-i^{\beta}\leqslant j<i\right\}|
be the total number of vertices that have access to vertex
j
. Also, define
N_{j,n}=\min\left\{N_{j},(n-j)\right\}
to be the number of vertices upto time
n
that have access to vertex
j
. The following lemma gives a bound on
N_{j}
,
and its proof is provided at the end of the section.

Lemma 5.6
.

We have

i)

N_{j}\geqslant j^{\beta}-1
for all
j\geqslant 1
.

ii)

for any
\delta>1,
there exists
j_{0}\geqslant 1
such that
N_{j}\leqslant\delta j^{\beta}
for all
j\geqslant j_{0}
.

Using the above lemma and triangle inequality, for sufficiently large
j
, we have

\displaystyle d_{TV}\left(D_{j,n},Bin\left(N_{j,n},\frac{1}{j^{\beta}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{i=j}^{n}\mathds{1}\left\{i-i^{\beta}\leqslant j\right\}\left[\frac{1}{j^{\beta}}-\frac{1}{i^{\beta}}\right]

(24)

\displaystyle\leqslant N_{j,n}\left[\frac{1}{j^{\beta}}-\frac{1}{(j+\epsilon j^{\beta})^{\beta}}\right]\leqslant\epsilon\left[1-(1+\epsilon j^{\beta-1})^{-\beta}\right].

The last two lines follow from (ii) of Lemma
5.6
. Next, we approximate the binomial distribution in (
5.3
) with a Poisson(1) distribution. By triangle inequality, we have

\displaystyle d_{TV}\left(\text{Bin}\left(N_{j,n},\frac{1}{j^{\beta}}\right),\text{Poisson}(1)\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant d_{TV}\left(\text{Bin}\left(N_{j,n},\frac{1}{j^{\beta}}\right),\text{Poisson}\left(\frac{N_{j,n}}{j^{\beta}})\right)\right)+d_{TV}\left(\text{Poisson}\left(\frac{N_{j,n}}{j^{\beta}}\right),\text{Poisson}(1)\right)

(25)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{N_{j,n}}{j^{2\beta}}+\left|\frac{N_{j,n}}{j^{\beta}}-1\right|.

In the last line, the first term is bounded is using Poisson approximation of Binomial. The second term is bounded using total variation bound between Poisson distributions. Finally, note that
N_{j,n}\leqslant\epsilon j^{\beta}
and

\left|\frac{N_{j,n}}{j^{\beta}}-1\right|\leqslant\left|\frac{N_{j}}{j^{\beta}}-1\right|+\mathds{1}\left\{N_{j}\geqslant n-j\right\}\left|\frac{n-j}{j^{\beta}}-1\right|\leqslant\max(\epsilon-1,j^{-\beta})+\mathds{1}\left\{N_{j}\geqslant n-j\right\}\left|\frac{n-j}{j^{\beta}}-1\right|

Combining the above observations with (
5.3
) and (
5.3
), we have

\displaystyle d_{TV}\left(D_{j,n},\text{Poisson}(1)\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\epsilon\left[1-(1+\epsilon j^{\beta-1})^{-\beta}\right]+\epsilon j^{-\beta}+\max(\epsilon-1,j^{-\beta})+\mathds{1}\left\{N_{j}\geqslant n-j\right\}\left|\frac{n-j}{j^{\beta}}-1\right|.

\blacksquare

Proof of Theorem
2.3
.

Fix a tree
\mathcal{T}\in\mathbb{T}
with
\left|\mathcal{T}\right|=k
. Let
U\sim Unif(0,1)
be independent of the process
\left\{\mathcal{T}_{n}:n\geqslant 1\right\}
. We first show that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\pr\left(F(\lceil nU\rceil,\mathcal{T}_{n})=\mathcal{T}\right)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{0}^{1}\pr\left(F(\lfloor nu\rfloor,\mathcal{T}_{n})=\mathcal{T}\right)du\to\pr\left(\boldsymbol{\varpi}_{{\sf Poisson},1}=\mathcal{T}\right).

The proof of local weak convergence follows by appealing to Lemma
4.3
.

To this extent, fix
u\in[0,1]
. Consider the breadth-first exploration of the fringe of vertex
\lfloor nu\rfloor
, where we stop the exploration if the fringe/subtree rooted at
\lfloor nu\rfloor
differs from
\mathcal{T}
or if the subtree
=\mathcal{T}
.
Note that we only encounter at most
k
vertices during the exploration, where recall
|\mathcal{T}|=k
.
We first argue that the
k
vertices encountered in the exploration are all
good vertices
at time
n
. This helps in leveraging the Poisson approximation for these
k
vertices.
Note by letting
\delta=2
in (ii) of Lemma
5.6
, there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that index of any vertex that attaches to a vertex

is bounded at most by
j+2j^{\beta}
. Therefore, for
n\geqslant N/u
, we have that index of the vertex that attaches to vertex
\lfloor nu\rfloor
is bounded by
nu+2(nu)^{\beta}
.
Repeating this argument, we see that indices of the first
k
vertices in the fringe is bounded by
nu+2(nu)^{\beta}+2(nu+2(nu)^{\beta})^{\beta}+\dots(k-\text{terms})=nu+O(n^{\beta})
. Therefore, by Lemma
5.6
, we have all
k
vertices during the exploration are
good vertices
at time
n
.
Thus, by Lemma
5.5
, for any
\epsilon\geqslant 1
, there exists
\tilde{N}\geqslant N/u
such that for all
1\leqslant j\leqslant k

\displaystyle d_{TV}(D_{l_{j},n},\text{Poisson}(1))

\displaystyle\leqslant\epsilon\left[1-(1+\epsilon l_{j}^{\beta-1})^{-\beta}\right]+\epsilon l_{j}^{-\beta}+\max(\epsilon-1,l_{j}^{-\beta})=\epsilon-1+O(1)

for all

. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\left|\pr\left(F(\lfloor nu\rfloor,\mathcal{T}_{n})=\mathcal{T}\right)-\pr(\boldsymbol{\varpi}_{{\sf Poisson},1}=\mathcal{T})\right|\leqslant k(\epsilon-1)\text{ for all }\epsilon>1.

We refer the reader to proof of Lemma 9.6 in
[
17
]
for more details about the branching process approximation.
This completes the proof.

\blacksquare

We end this section with the proof of Lemma

.

Proof of Lemma
5.6
.

(i) For any
j\geqslant 1
, we have
\lfloor j^{\beta}\rfloor\leqslant(j+\lfloor j^{\beta}\rfloor)^{\beta}.
Thus,
(j+\lfloor j^{\beta}\rfloor)-(j+\lfloor j^{\beta}\rfloor)^{\beta}\leqslant j.
Therefore,
N_{j,n}\geqslant\lfloor j^{\beta}\rfloor\geqslant j^{\beta}-1.

(ii) If

6.
Proofs of global results

6.1.
Analysis of the height in the mesoscopic regime

In this section, we derive asymptotics for the height of the trees
\left\{\mathcal{T}_{n}:n\geqslant 1\right\}
in the mesoscopic regime.
Recall in this regime we have
j(n)=n-n^{\beta}
for a fixed parameter
\beta\in(0,1)
.

The proof involves the following three step recipe:

(i)

Deriving asymptotics of the Markov process
\left\{L_{n}(k):k\geqslant 1\right\}
which tracks the index of
k^{\text{th}}
ancestor of vertex
n
.

(ii)

Using the result in step-(i) to prove asymptotics of the distance
d(1,n)
between the root
1
and the vertex
n
.

(iii)

Arguing that the height of the tree is achieved by a vertex with index in
[(1-\delta)n,n]
for arbitrary
\delta>0
, with high probability, so that
d(1,n)
itself is also the height of the tree up to negligible errors.

Step
(i): For
1\leqslant k\leqslant d(1,n)
, let
L_{n}(k)
be the index of the
k^{\text{th}}
ancestor of vertex
n
. Define
L_{n}(0)=n
and
L_{n}(k)=0
if
k>d(1,n)
. Note that
\left\{L_{n}(k):k\geqslant 0\right\}
is a Markov process on
\left\{0,1,2,\dots,n\right\}
with
0
being an absorbing state. When
L_{n}(k)=l>0
,
L_{n}(k+1)
takes values in
\left\{l-1,l-2,\dots,l-\lfloor l^{\beta}\rfloor\right\}
with equal probability.

The following proposition proves that the natural LLN limit for the process
L_{n}(\cdot)
when scaled appropriately is given by the function
f_{\beta}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
defined as

\displaystyle f_{\beta}(t)=\begin{cases}&\left(1-\frac{1-\beta}{2}t\right)^{1/(1-\beta)},\;\;\text{if}\;\;t\in[0,\frac{2}{1-\beta}];\\
&0,\hskip 83.0pt\;\;\text{if}\;\;t>\frac{2}{1-\beta}.\end{cases}

Proposition 6.1
.

Fix
\beta\in(0,1)
, and let
T_{\beta}=2/(1-\beta)
. For any
\epsilon>0
and
T<T_{\beta}
, there exists constants
C>0
and
\xi=\xi(\epsilon)>0
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{t\leqslant T}\left|\frac{1}{n}L_{n}(\lfloor tn^{1-\beta}\rfloor)-f_{\beta}(t)\right|>n^{\frac{-1+\beta}{2}+\epsilon}\right)\leqslant\exp(-Cn^{\xi}).

Embedding the discrete chain
L_{n}(k)
into a continuous-time process
X_{n}(t)
allows us to represent the jumps using independent Poisson processes. This makes it easier to prove the concentration inequality using the associated ODE for
f_{\beta}(t)
, the function
f_{\beta}(\cdot)
, and properties of Poisson processes. To this extent, consider a continuous time Markov chain denoted by
\left\{X_{n}(t):t\geqslant 0\right\}
on state space
\left\{0,1,2,\dots,n\right\}
. The waiting time at any state has exponential distribution with rate
(n^{1-\beta})
. From state
l
, the chain moves to any of the states
\left\{l-1,l-2,\dots,l-\lfloor l^{\beta}\rfloor\right\}
with equal probability. Therefore, the embedded discrete time chain of
\left\{X_{n}(t):t\geqslant 0\right\}
has the same law as the process
\left\{L_{n}(k):k\geqslant 1\right\}.
Also, the state
0
is absorbing as noted before.

We now express the process
X_{n}(t)
in terms of time changed Poisson processes. Let
Y_{1},Y_{2},\dots
be i.i.d. rate
1
Poisson processes.
By Chapter 6 of
[
28
]
, we have

(26)

\displaystyle X_{n}(t)=n-\sum_{i=1}^{n}iY_{i}\left(n^{1-\beta}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\mathds{1}\left\{i\leqslant\lfloor X_{n}(s)^{\beta}\rfloor\right\}}{\lfloor X_{n}(s)^{\beta}\rfloor}ds\right).

Let
0=\tau_{0}\leqslant\tau_{1}\leqslant\tau_{2}\leqslant\dots
be the event times of the process

(27)

\displaystyle Z(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}\left(n^{1-\beta}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\mathds{1}\left\{i\leqslant\lfloor X_{n}(s)^{\beta}\rfloor\right\}}{\lfloor X_{n}(s)^{\beta}\rfloor}\right).

Let
n_{c}=\sup_{t}Z(t)
be the number of transitions in continuous time process
X_{n}(\cdot)
before it hits
0
. Similarly, for the discrete time process, define
n_{d}=\inf\left\{k:L_{n}(k)=0\right\}
.
From the definition of the process
X_{n}(\cdot)
, we have
n_{d}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}n_{c}<\infty
.
Furthermore the following equality
in distribution
holds,

(28)

\displaystyle\left\{X_{n}(\tau_{i}):0\leqslant i\leqslant n_{c}\right\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\left\{L_{n}(i):0\leqslant i\leqslant n_{d}\right\}.

We now have all the ingredients to provide the proof of Proposition
6.1
.

Proof.

We first prove the concentration for the continuous-time version process
\left\{X_{n}(t):t\geqslant 0\right\}
, and then leveraging (
28
) we conclude the result for discrete time process
\left\{L_{n}(k):k\geqslant 0\right\}.

We subtract the compensators of the Poisson processes in (
26
). This gives us a semi-martingale decomposition of the process
X_{n}(t)=A_{n}(t)+M_{n}(t)
, where
A_{n}(t)
is a predictable process and
M_{n}(t)
is a martingale, both with respect to the natural filtration
\mathcal{F}_{n}(t)=\sigma(\left\{X_{n}(s),Y_{1}(s),\dots,Y_{n}(s):s\leqslant t\right\})
, defined as

\displaystyle A_{n}(t)

\displaystyle=n-n^{1-\beta}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}i\mathds{1}\left\{i\leqslant\lfloor X_{n}^{\beta}(s)\rfloor\right\}}{\lfloor X_{n}^{\beta}(s)\rfloor}ds=n-n^{1-\beta}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\lfloor X_{n}^{\beta}(s)\rfloor+1}{2}ds

\displaystyle M_{n}(t)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}iN_{i}\left(n^{1-\beta}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\mathds{1}\left\{i\leqslant\lfloor X_{n}^{\beta}(s)\rfloor\right\}}{\lfloor X_{n}^{\beta}(s)\rfloor}ds\right)

where
N_{i}(s)=Y_{i}(s)-s
for
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
. Let
\bar{X}_{n}(t)=X_{n}(t)/n
. Note that for all
t\geqslant 0
, we have
f_{\beta}(t)=1-\int_{0}^{t}(f_{\beta}(s))^{\beta}ds
. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\bar{X}_{n}(t)-f_{\beta}(t)=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\left[\bar{X}_{n}^{\beta}(s)-f_{\beta}^{\beta}(s)\right]ds+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1-\{X_{n}^{\beta}(s)\}}{2n^{\beta}}ds+\bar{M}_{n}(t),

where
\bar{M}_{n}(t)=M_{n}(t)/n
captures the fluctuations, and
\left\{a\right\}=a-\lfloor a\rfloor
is the fractional part of
a
. Therefore, we have

(29)

\displaystyle\left|\bar{X}_{n}(t)-f_{\beta}(t)\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{0}^{t}\left|\bar{X}_{n}^{\beta}(s)-f_{\beta}^{\beta}(s)\right|ds+\frac{t}{n^{\beta}}+|\bar{M}_{n}(t)|.

Furthermore, for all
t\leqslant T<T_{\beta}
, we have
f_{\beta}(t)\geqslant f_{\beta}(T)>0
. Therefore, there exists a constant
M
such that
\left|X_{n}^{\beta}(s)-f_{\beta}^{\beta}(s)\right|\leqslant M\left|X_{n}(s)-f_{\beta}(s)\right|
. Thus, by (
29
), we have

\displaystyle\left|\bar{X}_{n}(t)-f_{\beta}(t)\right|\leqslant M\int_{0}^{t}\left|\bar{X}_{n}(s)-f_{\beta}(s)\right|ds+\frac{t}{n^{\beta}}+|\bar{M}_{n}(t)|.

Therefore, for any
T\geqslant 0
, by Gronwall’s inequality
(see Section 5.2 in
[
49
]
)
we have

(30)

\displaystyle\sup_{t\leqslant T}\left|\bar{X}_{n}(t)-f_{\beta}(t)\right|\leqslant\left[\frac{T}{n^{\beta}}+\sup_{t\leqslant T}|\bar{M}_{n}(t)|\right]e^{MT}.

Note that
\bar{M}_{n}(\cdot)
is a continuous time martingale with jumps at most of size
n^{1-\beta}.
Furthermore, the quadratic variation of the process
M_{n}(t)
is given by

\displaystyle\langle\bar{M}_{n}(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{n^{1-\beta}}{n^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}i^{2}\mathds{1}\left\{i\leqslant\lfloor X_{n}^{\beta}(s)\rfloor\right\}}{\lfloor X_{n}^{\beta}(s)\rfloor}ds\leqslant n^{-(1-\beta)}\int_{0}^{t}\bar{X}_{n}(s)^{2\beta}ds\leqslant n^{-(1-\beta)}T

for all
t\leqslant T
. By Lemma 2.1 in
[
48
]
, we have

\displaystyle\pr\left(\sup_{t\leqslant T}|\bar{M}_{n}(t)|\geqslant n^{-\frac{(1-\beta)}{2}+\epsilon}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\exp\left(-\frac{n^{-(1-\beta)+2\epsilon}}{n^{-(1-\beta)/2+\epsilon}n^{-(1-\beta)}+Tn^{-(1-\beta)}}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\exp\left(-n^{2\epsilon}/T\right).

Combining the above bound with (
30
), we have

(31)

\displaystyle\pr\left(\sup_{t\leqslant T}\left|\bar{X}_{n}(t)-f_{\beta}(t)\right|\geqslant n^{-(1-\beta)/2+\epsilon}\right)\leqslant\exp\left(-Cn^{2\epsilon}\right)

for some constant
C
depending on
T
and
M
.
This also proves that we have
\pr(\tau_{n,c}\leqslant T_{\beta}-T+\delta)\leqslant\exp(-Cn^{2\epsilon}).
Let
\tilde{Z}_{n}(T)
is a Poisson
(n^{1-\beta}T)
random variable. The last line follows from the observation that
Z_{n}(\cdot)
is has jumps according to Poisson
n^{1-\beta}
process before time
\tau_{n,c}
. Therefore,
Z_{n}(T)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\tilde{Z}_{n}(T)
when
\tau_{n,c}>T
, and hence

\pr\left(\left|Z_{n}(T)-n^{1-\beta}T\right|\geqslant n^{\frac{(1-\beta)}{2}+\epsilon}\right)\leqslant\pr(\tau_{n,c}\leqslant T_{\beta}-T)+\pr\left(\left|\tilde{Z}_{n}(T)-n^{1-\beta}T\right|\geqslant n^{\frac{(1-\beta)}{2}+\epsilon}\right)

Finally, using large deviation bounds of Poisson random variables, we have

\pr\left(\left|Z_{n}(T)-n^{1-\beta}T\right|\geqslant n^{\frac{(1-\beta)}{2}+\epsilon}\right)\leqslant\exp(-Cn^{2\epsilon})+\exp\left(-C^{\prime}n\right)

for some constant
C^{\prime}.
Using (
28
) and the above proves the result.

\blacksquare

The following corollary is a
straightforward
consequence of
Proposition

6.1
. This will be the main tool used in Step(ii).

Corollary 6.2
.

Fix
\beta\in(0,1)
. For any
\epsilon,\delta>0
and
T<T_{\beta}=2(1-\beta)^{-1}
there exist constants
C=C(\delta,T)>0
and
\xi=\xi(\epsilon)>0
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\exists\;\;v\in[\delta n,n]:\sup_{t\leqslant T}\left|\frac{1}{v}L_{v}(\lfloor tv^{1-\beta}\rfloor)-f_{\beta}(t)\right|>v^{\frac{-1+\beta}{2}+\epsilon}\right)\leqslant\exp(-Cn^{\xi}).

Proof.

By a union bound, the LHS above is at most

\displaystyle\sum_{v=\delta n}^{n}\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{t\leqslant T}\left|\frac{1}{v}L_{v}(\lfloor tv^{1-\beta}\rfloor)-f_{\beta}(t)\right|>v^{\frac{-1+\beta}{2}+\epsilon}\right)

which using Proposition
6.1
is at most
\sum_{v=\delta n}^{n}\exp\left(-Cn^{\xi}\right)\leqslant\exp\left(-Cn^{\xi/2}\right)
.

\blacksquare

Step
(ii): We now use the Corollary
6.2
to
complete Step (ii), i.e.,
prove convergence after scaling of the distance
d(1,n)
from
1
to
n
:

Proposition 6.3
.

For
\beta\in(0,1)
, it holds that

n^{-(1-\beta)}d(1,n)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\longrightarrow}}\frac{2}{1-\beta}.

Intuitively, the idea behind the proof is as follows. We start exploring the ancestral path to
1
starting from
n
. For small
\varepsilon>0
, we explore up to the ancestor with index
\left(\frac{2}{1-\beta}-\varepsilon\right)n^{1-\beta}
(with the convention that
n
itself is its ancestor with index
0
, the parent of
n
is the ancestor with index
1
, …). The concentration from Proposition
6.1
essentially guarantees that this ancestor itself has label about
\alpha n
, so that now we can consider the distance
d(1,\alpha n)
in the tree
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha n}
, i.e., we can work
inside
the tree
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha n}
. Let us call getting inside the tree
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha n}
, the
first hop
. Similarly, starting from here, the
second hop
gets us into the tree
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha^{2}n}
, and so on. Repeating these hops
K\log n
times for a suitably chosen
K
, we can essentially
get inside
the tree
\mathcal{T}_{\delta n^{1-\beta}}
for a sufficiently small
\delta>0
, and once we are in this final tree, we can simply bound the distance to
1
from any vertex in it by the size of this tree, which is
\delta n^{1-\beta}
. Combining this term with the total contribution to
d(1,n)
from all the hops, we show that the scaled distance
d(1,n)n^{-(1-\beta)}
is about
\frac{2}{1-\beta}
, perhaps off by a small constant, which suffices to establish Proposition
6.3
. Let us now provide the argument.

Proof.

Fix
\alpha>0
. We will later let
\alpha\to 0
, so think of
\alpha
being small. Define

\displaystyle t_{\alpha}:=f_{\beta}^{-1}(\alpha),

and note that
t_{\alpha}<\frac{2}{1-\beta}
whenever
\alpha>0
, and further,
t_{\alpha}\to\frac{2}{1-\beta}
as
\alpha\to 0
. Let
v_{0}=n
, and for
i\geqslant 0
, inductively define

\displaystyle v_{i+1}:=L_{\lfloor t_{\alpha}v_{i}^{1-\beta}\rfloor}(v_{i}).

For any constant
K>0
, consider the event

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{n}(K):=\bigcap_{i=0}^{K\log n}\mathcal{E}_{i},\;\;\text{where}\;\;\mathcal{E}_{i}:=\left\{v_{i+1}\in\left[\alpha v_{i}-v_{i}^{\frac{1+\beta}{2}+\varepsilon},\alpha v_{i}+v_{i}^{\frac{1+\beta}{2}+\varepsilon}\right]\right\},

where
we choose

\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small such that
\frac{1+\beta}{2}+\varepsilon<1
. Let us first check the behavior of
d(1,n)
on the event
\mathcal{S}(K)
. Consider the maps
g_{L}(x)=\alpha x-x^{(1+\beta)/2+\varepsilon}
and
g_{R}(x)=\alpha x+x^{(1+\beta)/2+\varepsilon}
. Observe that both
g_{L}
and
g_{R}
are monotone increasing on any interval of the form
(c,\infty)
,
for any
c
satisfying

(32)

c>\left[\left(\frac{1+\beta}{2}+\varepsilon\right)\alpha^{-1}\right]^{\left(\frac{1-\beta}{2}-\varepsilon\right)^{-1}}.

On the event
\mathcal{S}_{n}(K)
, for any
0\leqslant i\leqslant K\log n
, we have
g_{L}(v_{i})\leqslant v_{i+1}\leqslant g_{R}(v_{i})
. Let us denote
M(K)=K\log n
. If we can use that
g_{L}
and
g_{R}
are monotone increasing, recalling
v_{0}=n
, inductively, we can obtain

(33)

g_{L}^{(i)}(n)\leqslant v_{i}\leqslant g_{R}^{(i)}(n)\ \ \text{ for all }0\leqslant i\leqslant M(K)

where for any function
f(\cdot)
,
f^{(k)}(\cdot)
denotes its
k
-fold composition. It is straightforward to see that
g_{R}(x)
is monotone increasing on
(0,\infty)
. To argue the monotonicity for
g_{L}(\cdot)
, it is sufficient to claim that on the event
\mathcal{S}(K)
,
g_{L}(v_{i})

is always at least the RHS of (
32
), which will follow, if we in particular show that
g_{L}(v_{i})
diverges as
n\to\infty
for any
1\leqslant i\leqslant M(K)
. For this we need to choose
K
cleverly, which we make precise next.

Note that for
i=0
,
g_{L}(v_{0})=g_{L}(n)\gg 1
. It is enough to check that
g_{L}^{(M(K))}(n)
is divergent, where recall
M(K)=K\log n
for some constant
K>0
. From the definition of
g_{L}(\cdot)
, inductively arguing we note that for all large
n
,
g_{L}^{(M(K))}(n)\geqslant(\alpha-\varepsilon_{1})^{M(K)}n=n^{1-\log(1/(\alpha-\varepsilon_{1}))K}\gg 1
whenever
K<\log(1/(\alpha-\varepsilon_{1}))^{-1}
, for any
0<\varepsilon_{1}<\alpha
. Thus, for the monotonicity argument, we require the condition

(34)

M(K)<\log(1/(\alpha-\varepsilon_{1}))^{-1}\log n\;\;\text{for some}\;\;0<\varepsilon_{1}<\alpha.

Decomposing the distance

(35)

d(1,n)=\sum_{i=1}^{M(K)+1}d(v_{i-1},v_{i})+d(v_{M(K)+1},1),

we thus observe that on the event
\mathcal{S}(K)
, we have the upper bound

\displaystyle d(1,n)\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{M(K)+1}(t_{\alpha}v_{i-1}^{1-\beta}+1)+d(v_{M(K)+1},1).

Next, we note that by (
33
), the vertex
v_{M(K)+1}
is contained in the tree
T_{g_{R}^{(M(K))}(n)}
, which means we can simply bound the distance
d(v_{M(K)+1},1)\leqslant g_{R}^{(M(K))}(n)
. Furthermore, using the upper bounds
v_{i-1}\leqslant g_{R}(v_{i})
and the monotonicity of the function
g_{R}
, we thus obtain

\displaystyle d(1,n)\leqslant t_{\alpha}\sum_{i=0}^{M(K)}(g_{R}^{(i)}(n))^{1-\beta}+M(K)+g_{R}^{(M(K))}(n),

where we use the convention
g_{R}^{(0)}(n)=n
. By some easy analysis, it is easy to check that
g_{R}^{(i)}(n)\leqslant(\alpha+\varepsilon^{\prime})^{i}n
for any
\varepsilon^{\prime}>0
and
i=0,1,\dots
, so that we obtain

\displaystyle d(1,n)\leqslant\frac{t_{\alpha}n^{1-\beta}}{1-(\alpha+\varepsilon^{\prime})^{1-\beta}}+M(K)+(\alpha+\varepsilon^{\prime})^{M(K)}n.

Let

\displaystyle M(K)=\left\lfloor\frac{\beta\log n}{\log(1/(\alpha+\varepsilon^{\prime}))}+\frac{\log(1/\varepsilon^{\prime\prime})}{\log(1/(\alpha+\varepsilon))}\right\rfloor,

for any
\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}>0
, and let us immediately observe that by choosing
\beta,\varepsilon^{\prime}>0
sufficiently small (as a function of
\varepsilon_{1}
) the condition (
34
) is satisfied. We thus obtain

\displaystyle d(1,n)\leqslant\left(\frac{t_{\alpha}}{1-(\alpha+\varepsilon^{\prime})^{1-\beta}}+\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}\right)n^{1-\beta}+o(n^{1-\beta}).

Thus on the event
\mathcal{S}_{n}(K)
,
by choosing all of

\alpha,\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime},\varepsilon_{1}

sufficiently small,
and recalling
t_{\alpha}\to\frac{2}{1-\beta}
as
\alpha\to 0
, we obtain that for all large
n
,

\displaystyle d(1,n)\leqslant\left(\frac{2}{1-\beta}+\delta\right)n^{1-\beta}

for arbitrary
\delta>0
. A similar argument, starting again from (
35
) and now working with
g_{L}
instead of
g_{R}
, establishes a lower bound on
d(1,n)
on the event
\mathcal{S}_{n}(K)
as

\displaystyle d(1,n)\geqslant\left(\frac{2}{1-\beta}-\delta\right)n^{1-\beta},

so that we need only check
\mathcal{S}_{n}(K)
holds with high probability. To this end, we write

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{S}_{n}(K)^{c}\right)\leqslant\sum_{i=0}^{M(K)}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{i}^{c}).

Note that,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{i}^{c})\leqslant\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{i}^{c}\cap\mathcal{E}_{i-1})+\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{i-1}^{c}).

Furthermore, conditionally on
v_{i}
, applying Corollary
6.2
on the tree
T_{v_{i}}
, we obtain for any
0<\delta_{1}<\alpha
, and
i\geqslant 1
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{i}^{c})\leqslant\E\left[\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{i-1}}\exp(-Cv_{i}^{\xi(\varepsilon)})\right]+\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{i-1}^{c})

\displaystyle\leqslant\exp(-Cg_{L}^{(i-1)}(n)^{\xi(\varepsilon)})+\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{i-1}^{c})

\displaystyle\leqslant\exp(-C((\alpha-\delta_{2})^{i}n)^{\xi(\varepsilon)})+\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{i-1}^{c}),

for
C=C(\alpha-\delta_{1},t_{\alpha})
, a constant
\xi(\varepsilon)>0
, where recall the constants
C=C(\delta,T)
and
\xi(\varepsilon)
appearing in the statement of Corollary
6.2
, and for any
\delta_{2}>0
, where we again use the fact that
g_{L}^{(i-1)}(n)\geqslant(\alpha-\delta_{2})^{i}n
for any
i\geqslant 1
. Applying recursively, we thus obtain

Proposition 6.5
.

Fix

and let

Proof.

Fix

\displaystyle\approx\exp\left(\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{x}}}\log\left(1-\sum_{j=0}^{K}p_{j}^{n}(i,\boldsymbol{x})\right)\right)

\displaystyle\approx\exp\left(-\sum_{j=0}^{K}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{x}}}\mathds{1}\left\{i\leqslant T_{j,K}^{n}(\boldsymbol{x})\right\}\E(\mathds{1}\left\{\rho_{j}^{n}(\boldsymbol{x})/V\leqslant i<(\rho_{j}^{n}(\boldsymbol{x})+1)/V\right\})\right)

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{j=0}^{K}\E\left(\frac{1}{V}\mathds{1}\left\{-\log V\leqslant\log(T_{j,K}^{n}(\boldsymbol{x})/\rho_{j}^{n}(\boldsymbol{x}))\right\}\right)

The remainder of the proof follows as in Theorem
2.1
.

\blacksquare
