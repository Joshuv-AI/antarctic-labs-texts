Abstract
Numerical solutions of time-fractional differential equations encounter significant challenges arising from solution singularities at the initial time. To address this issue, the construction of nonuniform temporal meshes satisfying τ k / τ k − 1 ≥ 1 \tau_{k}/\tau_{k-1}\geq 1 has emerged as an effective strategy, where τ k \tau_{k} represents the k k -th time-step size. For the time-fractional Cahn-Hilliard equation, Liao et al. [ IMA J. Numer. Anal. , 45 (2025), 1425–1454] developed an analytical framework using a variable-step L2 formula with the constraint 0.3960 ≤ τ k / τ k − 1 ≤ r ∗ ​ ( α ) 0.3960\leq\tau_{k}/\tau_{k-1}\leq r^{*}(\alpha) , where r ∗ ​ ( α ) ≥ 4.660 r^{*}(\alpha)\geq 4.660 for α ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) \alpha\in(0,1) . The present work makes substantial theoretical progress by introducing innovative splitting techniques that relax the step-size ratio restriction to τ k / τ k − 1 ≤ ρ ∗ ​ ( α ) \tau_{k}/\tau_{k-1}\leq\rho^{*}(\alpha) , with ρ ∗ ​ ( α ) > ρ ¯ ≈ 4.7476114 \rho^{*}(\alpha)>\overline{\rho}\approx 4.7476114 . This advancement provides significantly greater flexibility in time-step selection. Building on this theoretical foundation, we propose a refined L2-type temporal approximation coupled with a fourth-order compact difference spatial discretization, yielding an efficient numerical scheme for the time-fractional Cahn-Hilliard problem. Our rigorous analysis establishes the scheme’s fundamental properties, including unique solvability, exact discrete volume conservation, proper energy dissipation laws, and optimal convergence rates. For practical implementation, we construct a specialized nonuniform mesh that automatically satisfies the relaxed constraint ρ ∗ ​ ( α ) > 4.7476114 \rho^{*}(\alpha)>4.7476114 . Comprehensive numerical experiments validate the method’s robustness and effectiveness, confirming theoretical predictions regarding accuracy, stability, and energy dissipation across various test cases. These results collectively demonstrate the method’s superior performance in challenging scenarios, establishing its practical utility for solving time-fractional partial differential equations.

Introduction

The Cahn-Hilliard equation was originally introduced by Cahn and Hilliard to model the intricate processes of phase separation and coarsening in binary systems within thermodynamic frameworks
[
1
]
. Over time, this equation has not only established itself as a fundamental tool in engineering and materials science
[
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
]
, but has also found widespread applications across diverse scientific disciplines
[
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
]
. In recent developments, researchers have recognized that the time-fractional Cahn-Hilliard equation serves as a natural generalization of the classical version. This extended formulation proves particularly valuable for systems whose dynamics depend on historical states
[
10
,
11
,
12
]
. The incorporation of fractional time derivatives enables the description of memory effects and anomalous diffusion during phase separation, features that remain beyond the reach of conventional Cahn-Hilliard models.

Here, we study the following time-fractional Cahn-Hilliard (TFCH) equation

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial^{\alpha}_{t}u(x,t)=\kappa\Delta f(u)+\kappa\varepsilon^{2}\Delta v(x,t),\quad x\in\Omega,\;t\in(0,T],\\
&v(x,t)=-\Delta u(x,t),\quad x\in\Omega,\;t\in(0,T],\\
&u(x,0)=u_{0}(x),\quad x\in\overline{\Omega},\end{aligned}\right.

(1.1)

where the spatial domain is given by
\Omega=(a,b)
, and
T>0
represents the final time. The nonlinear term is defined as
f(u)=u^{3}-u
, while
\varepsilon>0
and
\kappa>0
denote the interface width parameter and mobility coefficient, respectively. The Caputo fractional derivative
\partial^{\alpha}_{t}
with
0<\alpha<1
is expressed as

\partial^{\alpha}_{t}y(t)=I_{t}^{1-\alpha}y^{\prime}(t)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{-\alpha}y^{\prime}(s)\mathrm{d}s,

(1.2)

where
I_{t}^{\mu}
stands for the Riemann-Liouville fractional integral of order
\mu>0
, given by

\displaystyle I_{t}^{\mu}y(t)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\mu)}\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{\mu-1}y(s)\mathrm{d}s.

It is well established that the classical Cahn-Hilliard (CH) model
[
13
]
preserves mass conservation, which can be written as

\int_{\Omega}u(x,t)\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}u(x,0)\mathrm{d}x,\quad t\in(0,T].

(1.3)

Moreover, the model satisfies an energy dissipation property

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}E}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\kappa\|\nabla f(u)+\varepsilon^{2}\nabla v\|\leq 0\;\quad t\in(0,T],

where
E[u](t)
represents the Ginzburg–Landau energy functional defined by

E[u](t)=\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}|\nabla u|^{2}+F(u)\right)\mathrm{d}x,

(1.4)

with the double-well potential
F(u)=\frac{1}{4}(1-u^{2})^{2}
. When the fractional order
\alpha
approaches
1^{-}
, the TFCH model reduces to the standard integer-order CH model. A key question is whether the TFCH model retains these two fundamental properties.
Previous studies have addressed this question from different perspectives. Tang et al.
[
14
]
established a global energy dissipation law, proving that
E[u](t)\leq E[u](0)
for all
t>0
. Quan et al.
[
15
]
introduced nonlocal and weighted energy dissipation laws for time-fractional phase-field models. In
[
16
]
, Liao et al. derived an equivalent formulation involving the Riemann–Liouville derivative and demonstrated that the energy dissipation laws of certain numerical schemes, including the L1, L
1^{+}
, and L
1_{R}
methods, are asymptotically compatible with their integer-order counterparts. Further analysis of these schemes can be found in
[
17
,
18
,
19
]
. More recently, Quan et al.
[
20
]
constructed a modified energy functional
\widetilde{E}_{\alpha}[u]
, which serves as a decreasing upper bound for the original energy
E[u]
. As shown in Proposition 5.1 of
[
20
]
, this modified energy is asymptotically compatible with the classical Ginzburg–Landau energy functional.

Due to the nonlocal nature of fractional derivatives and the presence of nonlinear terms in equation (
1.1
), deriving analytical solutions for such problems is highly challenging. As a result, numerical methods have become an essential tool for investigating the dynamic behavior of these equations. A crucial aspect of such numerical approaches lies in the accurate approximation of the Caputo derivative, which requires the development of efficient numerical differentiation formulas.
Over the past few decades, significant progress has been made in constructing numerical approximations for the Caputo derivative of order
\alpha\in(0,1)
. A straightforward and widely used approach, proposed in
[
21
]
, employs piecewise linear interpolation to derive a weighted numerical integral, leading to the well-known L1 formula. Wu and Sun
[
22
]
later demonstrated that the L1 formula achieves an accuracy of order
2-\alpha
. To further improve convergence orders, Gao et al.
[
23
]
introduced the L
1-2
formula on a uniform mesh, which attains an order of
3-\alpha
at interior points
t_{k}
(
k\geq 2
), though the accuracy reduces to
2-\alpha
at the first time point
t_{1}
.
Building upon these developments, Alikhanov
[
24
]
proposed a more refined scheme known as the L
2-1_{\sigma}
formula, which achieves a uniform convergence order of
3-\alpha
at the shifted grid points
t_{k+\sigma}
, where
\sigma=1-{\alpha}/{2}
. Independently, Lv and Xu
[
25
]
constructed a different approximation, called the L2 formula, based on piecewise quadratic interpolation. While this method shares the same convergence order as the L
2-1_{\sigma}
formula, it employs a distinct interpolation strategy.
Building upon the aforementioned numerical differential formulas, significant progress has been made in developing numerical solutions for time-fractional differential equations. A common framework in these studies relies on the key assumption that the analytical solution and its higher-order derivatives satisfy prescribed smoothness conditions. Such regularity requirements are essential for ensuring the stability and convergence of numerical schemes.

Recent studies have revealed that exact solutions of time-fractional differential equations exhibit weak singularities at
t=0
. This weak singularity in the solution
u
poses a significant challenge, as it prevents numerical schemes from achieving their expected convergence orders. To address this issue, researchers have proposed constructing numerical differentiation formulas on non-uniform meshes
[
26
,
27
,
28
]
. The underlying principle of this approach involves concentrating grid points more densely near the initial point
t=0
while maintaining sparser distributions away from the origin. This temporal adaptation helps better capture the singular behavior near the initial time.
Following this concept, substantial progress has been made in developing numerical differentiation formulas for Caputo derivatives on non-uniform meshes. Quan et al.
[
29
]
pioneered the investigation of
H^{1}
-stability for L2-type formulas on general nonuniform meshes when applied to subdiffusion equations. Their work established the positive semidefiniteness with the difference multiplier
\triangledown_{\tau}w^{n}
under specific time step ratio constraints, requiring
0.4573328\leq\tau_{k}/\tau_{k-1}\leq 3.5615528
for all
k\geq 2
. Subsequently, Liao et al.
[
30
]
made significant advances in analyzing the variable-step fractional order BDF2 implicit scheme for the time-fractional Cahn-Hilliard model. Their work derived a local discrete energy dissipation law and substantially relaxed the time step ratio condition to
0.3960\leq\tau_{k}/\tau_{k-1}\leq r^{*}(\alpha)
for
k\geq 2
, where
r^{*}(\alpha)\geq 4.660
when
\alpha\in(0,1)
.

Building upon these theoretical advances, this work develops a numerical discretization of the Caputo derivative using the L2 formula on general nonuniform meshes. Our approach focuses on relaxing the temporal step ratio constraints to achieve greater computational flexibility. Furthermore, we construct an optimized nonuniform mesh that satisfies the proposed step ratio condition, leading to an enhanced numerical scheme for solving equation (
1.1
).
The main theoretical and computational contributions of this study include the following key aspects:

1.

Improved time step ratio condition for the discrete Caputo derivative:
Through careful modification of the splitting components, the derived time step ratio condition becomes
1\leq\tau_{k}/\tau_{k-1}\leq\rho^{*}(\alpha)
for
k\geq 2
, where the upper bound satisfies
\rho^{*}(\alpha)>\overline{\rho}\approx 4.7476114
. This new condition offers superior adaptability to non-uniform meshes compared to existing alternatives. A comprehensive analysis is presented in Section
2
.

2.

Development of an effective numerical scheme:
By combining the L2 formula with a fourth-order compact difference approximation for spatial derivatives, an efficient numerical scheme for equation (
1.1
) is naturally derived.

3.

Rigorous theoretical analysis:
The study provides complete theoretical justification for the proposed difference scheme, including proofs of unique solvability, discrete volume conservation properties, energy decay law, and convergence behavior.

4.

Construction of an optimized non-uniform mesh:
A novel non-uniform mesh is developed that better satisfies the improved time step ratio condition compared to existing mesh constructions.

The novel decomposition formulation of the L2 scheme for discretizing the Caputo derivative

For a positive integer
N
and given time
T
, the interval
[0,T]
is divided into
0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{k}<t_{k+1}<\cdots<t_{N}=T
, with variable stepsizes
\tau_{k}=t_{k}-t_{k-1}
for
1\leq k\leq N
. The maximum step size is denoted by
\tau_{\max}:=\max_{1\leq k\leq N}\tau_{k}
.
The adjacent time-step ratios are defined as
\rho_{1}:=0
and
\rho_{k}:=\tau_{k}/\tau_{k-1}\geq 1
for
2\leq k\leq N
. For any time-discrete function
w(t_{k})
, the backward difference operator is given by
\triangledown_{\tau}w(t_{k}):=w(t_{k})-w(t_{k-1})
for
1\leq k\leq N
.

For
n\geq 2
and
1\leq k\leq n-1
, the standard quadratic Lagrangian interpolation polynomial is employed:

\displaystyle L_{2,k}(t)=\sum_{j=-1}^{1}w(t_{k+j})\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}l=-1\ l\neq j\end{subarray}}^{1}\frac{t-t_{k+l}}{t_{k+j}-t_{k+l}},\quad k=1,2,\ldots,n-1,

which interpolates at the nodes
(t_{k-1},w(t_{k-1}))
,
(t_{k},w(t_{k}))
, and
(t_{k+1},w(t_{k+1}))
. For
k=n
, the polynomial
L_{2,n-1}(t)
is used instead. The derivative of the interpolation polynomial takes the form

\displaystyle L^{\prime}_{2,k}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\nabla_{\tau}w(t_{k})}{\tau_{k}}+\frac{2t-t_{k-1}-t_{k}}{\tau_{k}+\tau_{k+1}}\left[\frac{\nabla_{\tau}w(t_{k+1})}{\tau_{k+1}}-\frac{\nabla_{\tau}w(t_{k})}{\tau_{k}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\nabla_{\tau}w(t_{k+1})}{\tau_{k+1}}+\frac{2t-t_{k+1}-t_{k}}{\tau_{k}+\tau_{k+1}}\left[\frac{\nabla_{\tau}w(t_{k+1})}{\tau_{k+1}}-\frac{\nabla_{\tau}w(t_{k})}{\tau_{k}}\right].

Hence, the Caputo derivative
\partial^{\alpha}_{t}w(t)
evaluated at
t_{n}
for
2\leq n\leq N
is approximated through the definition (
1.2
) as follows

\displaystyle\partial^{\alpha}_{t}w(t_{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\int_{t_{k-1}}^{t_{k}}(t_{n}-s)^{-\alpha}L^{\prime}_{2,k}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{t_{n-1}}^{t_{n}}(t_{n}-s)^{-\alpha}L^{\prime}_{2,n-1}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\right]+R_{t}^{n}

(2.1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{n-k}^{(n)}\nabla_{\tau}w(t_{k})+\frac{\rho_{n}}{1+\rho_{n}}d_{0}^{(n)}\bigg[\nabla_{\tau}w(t_{n})-\rho_{n}\nabla_{\tau}w(t_{n-1})\bigg]

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}d_{n-k}^{(n)}\frac{\nabla_{\tau}w(t_{k+1})-\rho_{k+1}\nabla_{\tau}w(t_{k})}{\rho_{k+1}(1+\rho_{k+1})}+R_{t}^{n},

where the coefficients are given by

\displaystyle c_{n-k}^{(n)}

\displaystyle=\frac{(t_{n}-t_{k-1})^{1-\alpha}-(t_{n}-t_{k})^{1-\alpha}}{\tau_{k}\Gamma(2-\alpha)},

(2.2)

\displaystyle d_{n-k}^{(n)}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\left[(t_{n}-t_{k-1})^{2-\alpha}-(t_{n}-t_{k})^{2-\alpha}\right]}{\tau_{k}^{2}\Gamma(3-\alpha)}-\frac{(t_{n}-t_{k-1})^{1-\alpha}+(t_{n}-t_{k})^{1-\alpha}}{\tau_{k}\Gamma(2-\alpha)}.

For the special case when
n=1
, the linear Lagrangian interpolation polynomial is used:

\displaystyle L_{1,1}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{t-t_{1}}{t_{0}-t_{1}}w(t_{0})+\frac{t-t_{0}}{t_{1}-t_{0}}w(t_{1}),

yielding the approximation

\displaystyle\partial^{\alpha}_{t}w(t_{1})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}(t_{1}-s)^{-\alpha}L^{\prime}_{1,1}(s)\mathrm{d}s+R_{t}^{1}=c_{0}^{(1)}\triangledown_{\tau}w(t_{1})+R_{t}^{1},

(2.3)

where

\displaystyle c_{0}^{(1)}=\frac{1}{\tau_{1}^{\alpha}\Gamma(2-\alpha)}.

By reorganizing the terms in (
2.1
) and (
2.3
), we obtain the unified form

\partial^{\alpha}_{t}w(t_{n})=\sum_{k=1}^{n}B_{n-k}^{(n)}\triangledown_{\tau}w(t_{k})+R_{t}^{n}=:\sum_{k=1}^{n}B_{n-k}^{(n)}\triangledown_{\tau}w^{k},\\

(2.4)

where
w^{k}
denotes the numerical approximation of

w(t_{k})
, and the discrete kernels
B_{n-k}^{(n)}

are defined as follows:

B_{n-k}^{(n)}=\begin{cases}c_{0}^{(1)},&k=1,\,n=1,\\
c_{1}^{(2)}-\dfrac{d_{1}^{(2)}}{1+\rho_{2}}-\dfrac{\rho_{2}^{2}}{1+\rho_{2}}d_{0}^{(2)},&k=1,\,n=2,\\
c_{0}^{(n)}+\dfrac{d_{1}^{(n)}}{\rho_{n}(1+\rho_{n})}+\dfrac{\rho_{n}}{1+\rho_{n}}d_{0}^{(n)},&k=n,\,n\geq 2,\\
c_{n-1}^{(n)}-\dfrac{d_{n-1}^{(n)}}{1+\rho_{2}},&k=1,\,n\geq 3,\\
c_{1}^{(n)}+\dfrac{d_{2}^{(n)}}{\rho_{n-1}(1+\rho_{n-1})}-\dfrac{d_{1}^{(n)}}{1+\rho_{n}}-\dfrac{\rho_{n}^{2}}{1+\rho_{n}}d_{0}^{(n)},&k=n-1,\,n\geq 3,\\
c_{n-k}^{(n)}+\dfrac{d_{n-k+1}^{(n)}}{\rho_{k}(1+\rho_{k})}-\dfrac{d_{n-k}^{(n)}}{1+\rho_{k+1}},&2\leq k\leq n-2,\,n\geq 4.\end{cases}

(2.5)

Next, we carefully examine the truncation error of the above approximation formula, with the precise result given in the following theorem:

Theorem 1
.

For any function
w(t)\in C^{3}[0,T]
, the truncation error of the Caputo derivative approximation satisfies

\displaystyle\left|R_{t}^{n}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\partial^{\alpha}_{t}w(t_{n})-\sum_{k=1}^{n}B_{n-k}^{(n)}\triangledown_{\tau}w(t_{k})\right|\leq\begin{cases}\dfrac{\alpha}{2\Gamma(3-\alpha)}\max\limits_{t_{0}\leq t\leq t_{1}}|w^{\prime\prime}(t)|\tau_{1}^{2-\alpha},&n=1,\\[10.0pt]
\dfrac{3\alpha+1}{12\Gamma(2-\alpha)}\max\limits_{t_{0}\leq t\leq t_{n}}|w^{\prime\prime\prime}(t)|\tau_{\text{max}}^{3-\alpha},&n\geq 2.\end{cases}

Proof.

The proof proceeds by considering two cases based on the value of
n
. For the case when
n=1
, starting from equation (
2.3
), we obtain

\displaystyle\left|R_{t}^{1}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}(t_{1}-s)^{-\alpha}w^{\prime}(s)\mathrm{d}s-\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}(t_{1}-s)^{-\alpha}L^{\prime}_{1,1}(s)\mathrm{d}s\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\left|\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}(t_{1}-s)^{-\alpha}\left[w(s)-L_{1,1}(s)\right]^{\prime}\mathrm{d}s\right|.

Following the result from
[
23
]
, the interpolation error can be expressed as

\displaystyle w(t)-L_{1,1}(t)=\frac{1}{2}w^{\prime\prime}(\eta_{0})(t-t_{0})(t-t_{1}),\;\eta_{0}\in(t_{0},t_{1}),\;t\in[t_{0},t_{1}].

Applying integration by parts yields

\displaystyle\left|R_{t}^{1}\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\left|-\alpha\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}(t_{1}-s)^{-\alpha-1}\left[w(s)-L_{1,1}(s)\right]\mathrm{d}s\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{2\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\left|\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}w^{\prime\prime}(\eta_{0})(s-t_{0})(t_{1}-s)^{-\alpha}\mathrm{d}s\right|.

Since the integrand maintains constant sign over the interval, we deduce

\displaystyle\left|R_{t}^{1}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\alpha}{2\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\max_{t_{0}\leq t\leq t_{1}}|w^{\prime\prime}(t)|\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}(s-t_{0})(t_{1}-s)^{-\alpha}\mathrm{d}s.

The evaluation of the integral

\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}(s-t_{0})(t_{1}-s)^{-\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}s=\frac{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}{\Gamma(3-\alpha)}\tau_{1}^{2-\alpha},

directly implies that the truncation error at the first time step satisfies the bound

\left|R_{t}^{1}\right|\leq\frac{\alpha}{2\Gamma(3-\alpha)}\max_{t_{0}\leq t\leq t_{1}}|w^{\prime\prime}(t)|\,\tau_{1}^{2-\alpha},

For the case when
n\geq 2
, we begin with equation (
2.1
) to derive

\displaystyle\left|R_{t}^{n}\right|=

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{n}}\left(t_{n}-s\right)^{-\alpha}w^{\prime}(s)\mathrm{d}s\right.

\displaystyle\left.-\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\left[\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n-1}\int_{t_{k-1}}^{t_{k}}(t_{n}-s)^{-\alpha}L^{\prime}_{2,k}(s)\mathrm{d}s+\int_{t_{n-1}}^{t_{n}}(t_{n}-s)^{-\alpha}L^{\prime}_{2,n-1}(s)\mathrm{d}s\right]\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\Bigg|\int_{t_{n-1}}^{t_{n}}\left(t_{n}-s\right)^{-\alpha}\left[w(s)-L_{2,n-1}(s)\right]^{\prime}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle+\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n-1}\int_{t_{k-1}}^{t_{k}}\left(t_{n}-s\right)^{-\alpha}\left[w(s)-L_{2,k}(s)\right]^{\prime}\mathrm{d}s\Bigg|.

Using the interpolation error estimate

\displaystyle w(t)-L_{2,k}(t)=\frac{1}{6}w^{\prime\prime\prime}(\eta_{k})(t-t_{k-1})(t-t_{k})(t-t_{k+1}),\;\eta_{k}\in(t_{k-1},t_{k+1}),\;t\in[t_{k-1},t_{k+1}],

we obtain the following estimate:

\displaystyle\left|R_{t}^{n}\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\Bigg|-\alpha\int_{t_{n-1}}^{t_{n}}(t_{n}-s)^{-\alpha-1}\left[w(s)-L_{2,n-1}(s)\right]\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad-\alpha\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\int_{t_{k-1}}^{t_{k}}(t_{n}-s)^{-\alpha-1}\left[w(s)-L_{2,k}(s)\right]\mathrm{d}s\Bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\alpha}{6\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\max_{t_{0}\leq t\leq t_{n}}|w^{\prime\prime\prime}(t)|\Bigg\{\int_{t_{n-1}}^{t_{n}}\left|(s-t_{n-2})(s-t_{n-1})\right|(t_{n}-s)^{-\alpha}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\int_{t_{k-1}}^{t_{k}}\left|(s-t_{k-1})(t_{k}-s)(t_{k+1}-s)\right|(t_{n}-s)^{-\alpha-1}\mathrm{d}s\Bigg\}.

Bounding the polynomial factors in the integrands, we note that

|(s-t_{n-2})(s-t_{n-1})|\leq(\tau_{n-1}+\tau_{n})\tau_{n},

for
s\in[t_{n-1},t_{n}]
and

|(s-t_{k-1})(t_{k}-s)(t_{k+1}-s)|\leq\frac{\tau_{k}^{2}}{4}(\tau_{k}+\tau_{k+1})
for
s\in[t_{k-1},t_{k}]
. Substituting these estimates yields

\displaystyle\left|R_{t}^{n}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\alpha}{6\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\max_{t_{0}\leq t\leq t_{n}}\left|w^{\prime\prime\prime}(t)\right|\Bigg\{(\tau_{n-1}+\tau_{n})\tau_{n}\int_{t_{n-1}}^{t_{n}}(t_{n}-s)^{-\alpha}\mathrm{d}s\vskip 8.5359pt

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{\tau_{k}^{2}}{4}(\tau_{k}+\tau_{k+1})\int_{t_{k-1}}^{t_{k}}(t_{n}-s)^{-\alpha-1}\mathrm{d}s\Bigg\}\vskip 8.5359pt

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{6\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\max_{t_{0}\leq t\leq t_{n}}\left|w^{\prime\prime\prime}(t)\right|\Bigg\{\frac{(\tau_{n-1}+\tau_{n})\tau_{n}^{2-\alpha}}{1-\alpha}\vskip 8.5359pt

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3\alpha+1}{12\Gamma(2-\alpha)}\max_{t_{0}\leq t\leq t_{n}}\left|w^{\prime\prime\prime}(t)\right|\tau_{\max}^{3-\alpha}.

This completes the proof of the theorem.

\displaystyle\displaystyle\partial^{\alpha}_{t}w(t_{n})=\left(\theta c_{0}^{(n)}+\frac{\rho_{n}}{1+\rho_{n}}d_{0}^{(n)}\right)\triangledown_{\tau}w^{n}-\frac{\rho_{n}^{2}}{1+\rho_{n}}d_{0}^{(n)}\triangledown_{\tau}w^{n-1}+\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}\widetilde{c}_{n-k}^{(n)}\triangledown_{\tau}w^{k}.

which differs significantly from the simpler coefficient

. The discrete kernels
\widetilde{c}_{n-k}^{(n)}
are carefully constructed through
the following piecewise definition:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\widetilde{c}_{n-k}^{(n)}=\begin{cases}(1-\theta)c_{0}^{(1)},&\mathrm{if}\quad k=1,\;n=1,\\
c_{n-1}^{(n)}-\frac{1}{1+\rho_{2}}d_{n-1}^{(n)},&\mathrm{if}\quad k=1,\;n\geq 2,\\
(1-\theta)c_{0}^{(n)}+\frac{1}{\rho_{n}\left(1+\rho_{n}\right)}d_{1}^{(n)},&\mathrm{if}\quad k=n,\;n\geq 2,\\
c_{n-k}^{(n)}+\frac{1}{\rho_{k}\left(1+\rho_{k}\right)}d_{n-k+1}^{(n)}-\frac{1}{1+\rho_{k+1}}d_{n-k}^{(n)},&\mathrm{if}\quad 2\leq k\leq n-1,\;n\geq 3.\end{cases}

(2.7)

This modified decomposition offers several analytical advantages over previous approaches. The parameter
\theta
incorporates higher-order correction terms that account for both the fractional order
\alpha
and the characteristic value
\overline{\rho}
. The piecewise definition of the discrete kernels ensures proper handling of temporal discretization across different time steps, with special attention given to boundary cases and intermediate temporal nodes. The formulation maintains consistency while improving numerical stability through its balanced treatment of current and previous time step contributions.

Lemma 2
.

For any
\alpha\in(0,1)
, the following inequality holds

\displaystyle q(z,y,\alpha):=2\theta(2-\alpha)+\frac{2\alpha z-\alpha z^{2-\frac{\alpha}{2}}}{1+z}-\frac{\alpha y^{2-\frac{\alpha}{2}}}{1+y}>0\quad\text{for}\;1\leq z,y<\rho^{*},

where
\rho^{*}=\rho^{*}(\alpha)
represents the unique root of the equation

\frac{1+\rho^{*}}{\alpha}+\rho^{*}-(\rho^{*})^{2-\frac{\alpha}{2}}+2^{-\alpha}\alpha+\frac{1}{2}-\alpha=0.

Proof.

The proof proceeds by analyzing the behavior of the function
q(z,y,\alpha)
. First, differentiating with respect to
z
yields

\displaystyle\partial_{z}q(z,y,\alpha)=\frac{\alpha}{2(1+z)^{2}}\left[4-(4-\alpha)z^{1-\frac{\alpha}{2}}-(2-\alpha)z^{2-\frac{\alpha}{2}}\right].

Consider the auxiliary function
q_{1}(z,\alpha)=4-(4-\alpha)z^{1-\frac{\alpha}{2}}-(2-\alpha)z^{2-\frac{\alpha}{2}}
. Analysis shows that
q_{1}(z,\alpha)
decreases monotonically for
z>0
, with
q_{1}(1,\alpha)=2\alpha-2<0
. Consequently,
q(z,y,\alpha)
decreases in
z
for
z\in(1,+\infty)
. Furthermore, the derivative with respect to
y

\displaystyle\partial_{y}q(z,y,\alpha)=\frac{\alpha y^{1-\frac{\alpha}{2}}}{2(1+y)^{2}}\left[\alpha-4-(2-\alpha)y\right]<0\quad\text{for}\;y>0

demonstrates that
q(z,y,\alpha)
also decreases in
y
for
y\in(1,+\infty)
. Therefore, we obtain

\displaystyle q(z,y,\alpha)>q(\rho^{*},\rho^{*},\alpha)=2\theta(2-\alpha)+\frac{2\alpha\rho^{*}-2\alpha(\rho^{*})^{2-\frac{\alpha}{2}}}{1+\rho^{*}}.

To complete the proof, we examine the auxiliary function

\displaystyle q_{2}(\rho,\alpha)=\frac{1+\rho}{\alpha}+\rho-\rho^{2-\frac{\alpha}{2}}+2^{-\alpha}\alpha+\frac{1}{2}-\alpha\quad\text{for}\;\;\rho\geq 1,

whose derivative is

\displaystyle\partial_{\rho}q_{2}(\rho,\alpha)=1+\frac{1}{\alpha}-\left(2-\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)\rho^{1-\frac{\alpha}{2}}.

The equation
\partial_{\rho}q_{2}(\rho,\alpha)=0
possesses a unique root

. Thus, for any
\alpha\in(0,1)

q_{2}(\rho,\alpha)
increases on

\displaystyle q_{2}(g,\alpha)=\frac{1}{\alpha}+g\left(1+\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)\left(\frac{2-\alpha}{4-\alpha}\right)+2^{-\alpha}\alpha+\frac{1}{2}-\alpha>0.

while
q_{2}(+\infty,\alpha)<0
. This guarantees the existence of a unique
\rho^{*}\in(g,+\infty)

q_{2}(\rho^{*},\alpha)=0
for any
\alpha\in(0,1)

Combining these results yields
q(z,y,\alpha)>q(\rho^{*},\rho^{*},\alpha)=\frac{2\alpha}{1+\rho^{*}}q_{2}(\rho^{*},\alpha)=0

4
Theoretical analysis of the numerical scheme

4.1
Unique solvability

The unique solvability of the numerical scheme (
3.5
) can be established through the following framework. For arbitrary mesh functions
u,v\in\mathring{\mathcal{U}}_{h}
, we first introduce the essential inner products and associated norms:

\displaystyle(u,v)=h\sum\limits_{i=1}^{M-1}u_{i}v_{i},\quad\left\|u\right\|=\sqrt{(u,u)},\quad\left(\delta_{x}u,\delta_{x}v\right)=\frac{1}{h}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{M}(u_{i}-u_{i-1})(v_{i}-v_{i-1}),

\displaystyle\left\|\delta_{x}u\right\|=\sqrt{(\delta_{x}u,\delta_{x}u)},\quad\left(\delta_{x}^{2}u,\delta_{x}^{2}v\right)=h\sum\limits_{i=1}^{M-1}\left(\delta_{x}^{2}u_{i}\right)\left(\delta_{x}^{2}v_{i}\right),\quad\left\|\delta_{x}^{2}u\right\|=\sqrt{\left(\delta_{x}^{2}u,\delta_{x}^{2}u\right)},

\displaystyle\left(u,v\right)_{1,\mathcal{A}}=\left(\mathscr{A}u,-\delta_{x}^{2}v\right),\quad\left\|\delta_{x}u\right\|_{\mathcal{A}}=\sqrt{\left(u,u\right)_{1,\mathcal{A}}},\quad\left\|u\right\|_{\infty}=\max\limits_{1\leq i\leq M-1}\left|u_{i}\right|.

The following lemma provides crucial inequalities for the analysis:

Lemma 7
.

(
[
32
]
)
For any mesh function

\displaystyle\frac{2}{3}\left\|\delta_{x}u\right\|^{2}\leq\left\|\delta_{x}u\right\|_{\mathcal{A}}^{2}\leq\left\|\delta_{x}u\right\|^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{1}{3}\left\|u\right\|^{2}\leq\left\|\mathscr{A}u\right\|^{2}\leq\left\|u\right\|^{2}.

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\mathscr{A}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}B_{n-k}^{(n)}\triangledown_{\tau}u^{k}=\kappa\delta_{x}^{2}\left(u^{n}\right)^{\circ 3}-\kappa\delta_{x}^{2}u^{n}+\kappa\varepsilon^{2}\delta_{x}^{2}v^{n},\\

where the Hadamard power operation

Theorem 8

n
, it suffices to demonstrate that the corresponding homogeneous system

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\mathscr{A}B_{0}^{(n)}u^{n}=\kappa\delta_{x}^{2}\left(u^{n}\right)^{\circ 3}-\kappa\delta_{x}^{2}u^{n}+\kappa\varepsilon^{2}\delta_{x}^{2}v^{n},\\
\mathscr{A}v^{n}=-\delta_{x}^{2}u^{n},\end{cases}

possesses only the trivial solution.

Taking the inner product of the first equation with

\mathscr{A}u^{n}
yields

\displaystyle B_{0}^{(n)}\left\|\mathscr{A}u^{n}\right\|^{2}=-\kappa\left\|\delta_{x}(u^{n})^{\circ 2}\right\|_{\mathcal{A}}^{2}+\kappa\left\|\delta_{x}u^{n}\right\|_{\mathcal{A}}^{2}-\kappa\varepsilon^{2}\left(v^{n},u^{n}\right)_{1,\mathcal{A}}.

Similarly, the inner product of the second equation
with
\mathscr{A}v^{n}
gives

\displaystyle\left\|\mathscr{A}v^{n}\right\|^{2}=\left(v^{n},u^{n}\right)_{1,\mathcal{A}}.

Combining these results leads to the inequality

\displaystyle B_{0}^{(n)}\left\|\mathscr{A}u^{n}\right\|^{2}-\kappa\left\|\delta_{x}u^{n}\right\|_{\mathcal{A}}^{2}=-\kappa\left\|\delta_{x}\left(u^{n}\right)^{\circ 2}\right\|_{\mathcal{A}}^{2}-\kappa\varepsilon^{2}\left\|\mathscr{A}v^{n}\right\|^{2}\leq 0.

The non-positivity of the right-hand side implies
|u^{n}|^{2}=0
when the condition

\displaystyle B_{0}^{(n)}\left\|\mathscr{A}u^{n}\right\|^{2}-\kappa\left\|\delta_{x}u^{n}\right\|_{\mathcal{A}}^{2}\geq\left(\frac{B_{0}^{(n)}}{3}-\frac{4\kappa}{h^{2}}\right)\left\|u^{n}\right\|^{2}\geq 0

is satisfied by follows from definition (
2.5
) and Lemma
7
. This condition holds because

\displaystyle B_{0}^{(n)}\geq c_{0}^{(n)}+\frac{\rho_{n}}{1+\rho_{n}}d_{0}^{(n)}=\frac{2-\alpha+2\rho_{n}}{(1+\rho_{n})\Gamma(3-\alpha)\tau_{n}^{\alpha}}\geq\frac{12\kappa}{h^{2}}.

The mathematical induction principle therefore guarantees the unique solvability of the scheme at all time levels, which completes the proof.

4.2
Discrete volume conservation

This subsection establishes the discrete volume conservation property (
1.3
) for the proposed numerical scheme (
3.5
). The conservation law plays a crucial role in maintaining the physical fidelity of the numerical solution.

Theorem 9
.

The numerical scheme (
3.5
) maintains volume
conservation in the discrete sense, satisfying

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u(x,t_{n})\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}u(x,t_{n-1})\mathrm{d}x\quad\text{for all}\;1\leq n\leq N.

Proof.

The proof proceeds through several steps combining continuum analysis with discrete induction. First, consider the continuous equation (
1.1
). Integration over the domain
\Omega
followed by application of Green’s formula yields

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}^{\alpha}u(x,t)\;\mathrm{d}x=\kappa\int_{\Omega}\Delta f(u)+\varepsilon^{2}\Delta v(x,t)\;\mathrm{d}x=0,

where the vanishing right-hand side results from boundary conditions.

Discretizing this conservation property using the scheme (
2.4
) gives

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}B_{n-k}^{(n)}\left(u(x,t_{k})-u(x,t_{k-1})\right)\mathrm{d}x=0

for each
1\leq n\leq N
. The conservation property can be rigorously established through mathematical induction.

For the base case
n=1
, the temporal discretization scheme (
2.3
) combined with (
2.4
) produces

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}c_{0}^{(1)}\left(u(x,t_{1})-u(x,t_{0})\right)\mathrm{d}x=0,

immediately implying
\int_{\Omega}u(x,t_{1})\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}u(x,t_{0})\mathrm{d}x
.

Assuming the conservation property holds for all time steps up to
N-1
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u(x,t_{n})\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}u(x,t_{n-1})\mathrm{d}x\quad\text{for}\;1\leq n\leq N-1,

we examine the
N
-th time step. The discrete equation becomes

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\sum_{k=1}^{N}B_{N-k}^{(N)}\left(u(x,t_{k})-u(x,t_{k-1})\right)\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}B_{0}^{(N)}\left(u(x,t_{N})-u(x,t_{N-1})\right)\mathrm{d}x=0,

where the simplification follows from the induction hypothesis. This directly yields

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u(x,t_{N})\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}u(x,t_{N-1})\mathrm{d}x,

completing the inductive step and the proof.

4.3
Energy dissipation law

The energy dissipation law of the numerical scheme are established through careful analysis of the operator
\mathcal{H}=\mathscr{A}^{-1}\delta_{x}^{2}
. This operator plays a fundamental role in the discrete energy analysis and possesses the following essential properties:

Lemma 10
.

For the operator
\mathcal{H}=\mathscr{A}^{-1}\delta_{x}^{2}
acting on the space
\mathring{\mathscr{U}}_{h}
, the following properties hold:

(1)

The operator
\mathcal{H}
is symmetric, satisfying
(\mathcal{H}u,v)=(u,\mathcal{H}v)
for all
u,v\in\mathring{\mathscr{U}}_{h}
.

(2)

The operator
\mathcal{H}
is negative semidefinite, with
(\mathcal{H}u,u)\leq 0
for any
u\in\mathring{\mathcal{U}}_{h}
.

Proof.

According to the operator definition and inner product property, we derive:

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{H}u,v\right)=\left(\mathcal{A}^{-1}\delta_{x}^{2}u,v\right)=\left(\delta_{x}^{2}u,\mathcal{A}^{-1}v\right)=\left(u,\delta_{x}^{2}\left(\mathcal{A}^{-1}v\right)\right)=\left(u,\mathcal{H}v\right).

For the negative semidefinite property (2), Lemma
7
provides the necessary framework for the calculation:

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{H}u,u\right)=\left(\mathcal{A}^{-1}\delta_{x}^{2}u,u\right)=\left(\delta_{x}^{2}\mathcal{A}^{-1}u,u\right)=-\left(\delta_{x}^{2}\left(\mathcal{A}^{-1}u\right),-\mathcal{A}\left(\mathcal{A}^{-1}u\right)\right)=-\left\|\delta_{x}\left(\mathcal{A}^{-1}u\right)\right\|_{\mathcal{A}}^{2}\leq 0.

This completes the proof of both properties.

Building upon equations (
2.6
), (
3.2
), and Lemma
10
, we derive the following discrete evolution equation:

\displaystyle\left(\theta c_{0}^{(n)}+\frac{\rho_{n}}{1+\rho_{n}}d_{0}^{(n)}\right)\triangledown_{\tau}u^{n}-\frac{\rho_{n}^{2}}{1+\rho_{n}}d_{0}^{(n)}\triangledown_{\tau}u^{n-1}+\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}\widetilde{c}_{n-k}^{(n)}\triangledown_{\tau}u^{k}

(4.1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\kappa\mathcal{H}\left(u^{n}\right)^{\circ 3}-\kappa\mathcal{H}u^{n}-\kappa\varepsilon^{2}\mathcal{H}\left(\mathcal{H}u^{n}\right).

To properly analyze the energy dissipation, we introduce an
\mathcal{H}^{-1}
-like inner product structure. For arbitrary mesh functions
u,v\in\mathring{\mathcal{U}}_{h}
, define

\displaystyle(u,v)_{-\mathcal{H}}:=\left(u,\left(-\mathcal{H}\right)^{-1}v\right)

with the induced norm
\left\|u\right\|_{-\mathcal{H}}:=\sqrt{\left(u,u\right)_{-\mathcal{H}}}
. This structure leads to the definition of a modified discrete energy functional:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{n}=E^{n}+\frac{1}{\kappa}\left(\mathcal{G}\left[\triangledown_{\tau}u^{n}\right],1\right)_{-\mathcal{H}}\quad\text{for}\;n\geq 1,

(4.2)

where
\mathcal{G}
represents the non-negative functional from Theorem
6
. The discrete free energy
E^{n}
corresponds to the discretization of the continuous energy functional (
1.4
) and is given by

\displaystyle E^{n}:=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\left\|\nabla_{h}u^{n}\right\|^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\left\|\left(u^{n}\right)^{\circ 2}-1\right\|^{2},

with
\nabla_{h}
denoting a discrete gradient operator satisfying
(\mathcal{H}u,v)=-(\nabla_{h}u,\nabla_{h}v)
.

The following theorem establishes the energy dissipation law of the numerical scheme under appropriate time step constraints:

Theorem 11
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
8
with the time step restriction

\displaystyle\tau_{n}\leq\sqrt[\alpha]{\frac{4\epsilon^{2}q(\rho_{n},\rho_{n+1},\alpha)}{\kappa\Gamma(3-\alpha)}}\quad\text{for}\quad 2\leq n\leq N,

the modified energy satisfies the dissipation property

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{n}\leq\mathcal{E}^{n-1},\quad n=2,3,\cdots,N.

Proof.

First, taking the inner product of equation (
4.1
) with
(-\mathcal{H})^{-1}\triangledown_{\tau}u^{n}/\kappa
yields

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\kappa}\Bigg(\left(\theta c_{0}^{(n)}+\frac{\rho_{n}}{1+\rho_{n}}d_{0}^{(n)}\right)\triangledown_{\tau}u^{n}-\frac{\rho_{n}^{2}}{1+\rho_{n}}d_{0}^{(n)}\triangledown_{\tau}u^{n-1}+\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}\widetilde{c}_{n-k}^{(n)}\triangledown_{\tau}u^{k},\triangledown_{\tau}u^{n}\Bigg)_{-\mathcal{H}}

(4.3)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(u^{n}-\left(u^{n}\right)^{\circ 3},\triangledown_{\tau}u^{n}\right)+\varepsilon^{2}\left(\mathcal{H}u^{n},\triangledown_{\tau}u^{n}\right).

Next, let’s analyze the two sides of equation (
4.3
). Firstly, for its left side, Theorem
6
yields

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\kappa}\Bigg(\left(\theta c_{0}^{(n)}+\frac{\rho_{n}}{1+\rho_{n}}d_{0}^{(n)}\right)\triangledown_{\tau}u^{n}-\frac{\rho_{n}^{2}}{1+\rho_{n}}d_{0}^{(n)}\triangledown_{\tau}u^{n-1}+\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}\widetilde{c}_{n-k}^{(n)}\triangledown_{\tau}u^{k},\triangledown_{\tau}u^{n}\Bigg)_{-\mathcal{H}}

(4.4)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\kappa}\left(\mathcal{G}\left[\triangledown_{\tau}u^{n}\right],1\right)_{-\mathcal{H}}-\frac{1}{\kappa}\left(\mathcal{G}\left[\triangledown_{\tau}u^{n-1}\right],1\right)_{-H}+\frac{q\left(\rho_{n},\rho_{n+1},\alpha\right)}{2\kappa\tau_{n}^{\alpha}\Gamma(3-\alpha)}\left\|\triangledown_{\tau}u^{n}\right\|_{-\mathcal{H}}^{2}.

For the right-hand side terms, we employ the polynomial inequality

\displaystyle\left(\widetilde{a}-\widetilde{a}^{3}\right)\left(\widetilde{a}-\widetilde{b}\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}(\widetilde{a}-\widetilde{b})^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\left[\left(\widetilde{a}^{2}-1\right)^{2}-\left(\widetilde{b}^{2}-1\right)^{2}\right],

which leads to

\displaystyle\left(u^{n}-\left(u^{n}\right)^{\circ 3},\triangledown_{\tau}u^{n}\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}\|\triangledown_{\tau}u^{n}\|^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\left\|\left(u^{n}\right)^{\circ 2}-1\right\|^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\left\|\left(u^{n-1}\right)^{\circ 2}-1\right\|^{2}.

The second term on the right-hand side of equation (
4.3
)
can be expressed using the identity

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{H}u^{n},\triangledown_{\tau}u^{n}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\left(\nabla_{h}(\triangledown_{\tau}u^{n}+u^{n}+u^{n-1}),\nabla_{h}\triangledown_{\tau}u^{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left\|\nabla_{h}u^{n-1}\right\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\left\|\nabla_{h}u^{n}\right\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\left\|\nabla_{h}\triangledown_{\tau}u^{n}\right\|^{2}.

Combining these estimates yields the bound for the right-hand side of equation (
4.3
) in the form

\displaystyle\left(u^{n}-\left(u^{n}\right)^{\circ 3},\triangledown_{\tau}u^{n}\right)+\varepsilon^{2}\left(\mathcal{H}u^{n},\triangledown_{\tau}u^{n}\right)

(4.5)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|\triangledown_{\tau}u^{n}\|^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\left\|\left(u^{n}\right)^{\circ 2}-1\right\|^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\left\|\left(u^{n-1}\right)^{\circ 2}-1\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\left\|\nabla_{h}u^{n}\right\|^{2}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\left\|\nabla_{h}u^{n-1}\right\|^{2}-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\left\|\nabla_{h}\triangledown_{\tau}u^{n}\right\|^{2}.

Applying the generalized Hölder inequality

\displaystyle\|\triangledown_{\tau}u^{n}\|^{2}\leq\varepsilon^{2}\left\|\nabla_{h}\triangledown_{\tau}u^{n}\right\|^{2}+\frac{1}{4\varepsilon^{2}}\|\triangledown_{\tau}u^{n}\|_{-\mathcal{H}}^{2},

and combining with (
4.2
)-(
4.5
), we obtain the key estimate:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{n}-\mathcal{E}^{n-1}\leq\left(\frac{1}{8\varepsilon^{2}}-\frac{q(\rho_{n},\rho_{n+1},\alpha)}{2\kappa\tau_{n}^{\alpha}\Gamma(3-\alpha)}\right)\|\triangledown_{\tau}u^{n}\|_{-\mathcal{H}}^{2}.

The desired result
\mathcal{E}^{n}\leq\mathcal{E}^{n-1}
follows when the coefficient satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{1}{8\varepsilon^{2}}-\frac{q(\rho_{n},\rho_{n+1},\alpha)}{2\kappa\tau_{n}^{\alpha}\Gamma(3-\alpha)}\leq 0,

which completes the proof.

Remark 2
.

For the initial time step
n=1
, a similar analysis shows that under the condition
\tau_{1}\leq\sqrt[\alpha]{\frac{8\epsilon^{2}}{\kappa\Gamma(2-\alpha)}}
, the energy satisfies
E^{1}\leq E^{0}
.
As the fractional index
\alpha\to 1^{-}
, the discrete kernels
J_{n-k}^{(n)}\to 0
for
1\leq k\leq n
according to Theorem
5
and equation (
2.7
). This asymptotic behavior demonstrates that the modified energy
\mathcal{E}^{n}
converges to the original Ginzburg-Landau energy:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{n}\to E^{n}\quad\text{as}\quad\alpha\to 1^{-},

showing the consistency of our discrete formulation with the classical case.

4.4
Convergence analysis

This section establishes the convergence properties of the proposed numerical scheme through careful error analysis. We begin by introducing a modified Grönwall inequality that will be instrumental in our analysis.

Lemma 12
.

Let
\widetilde{C}>0
, and let
\left\{y_{n}\right\}
,
\left\{g_{n}\right\}
,
and
\left\{\phi_{n}\right\}
be non-negative sequences satisfying

\displaystyle\phi_{0}\leq\widetilde{C},\quad\phi_{n}\leq\widetilde{C}+\sum\limits_{j=0}^{n-1}y_{k}+\sum\limits_{j=0}^{n-1}g_{k}\phi_{k},\quad n\geq 1.

Then the following inequality holds:

\displaystyle\phi_{n}\leq\left(\widetilde{C}+\sum\limits_{j=0}^{n-1}y_{k}\right)\exp\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}g_{k}\right),\quad n\geq 1.

Let
l^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
denote the space of square-summable bilateral sequences, i.e., sequences satisfying
\sum_{i=-\infty}^{\infty}|u_{i}|^{2}<\infty
. We establish the following property of the operator
\mathcal{A}^{-1}
:

Lemma 13
.

The inverse operator
\mathcal{A}^{-1}
acting on
l^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
has operator norm
\frac{3}{2}
, that is,
\left\|\mathcal{A}^{-1}\right\|=\frac{3}{2}
.

Proof.

The operator
\mathcal{A}:l^{2}(\mathbb{Z})\to l^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
can be represented as a convolution operator with kernel
\left\{a_{k}\right\}_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}
given by

\displaystyle a_{k}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{12}&\text{if }k=-1,\\
\frac{5}{6}&\text{if }k=0,\\
\frac{1}{12}&\text{if }k=1,\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Thus, the action of
\mathcal{A}
can be expressed
as
\left(\mathcal{A}u\right)_{i}=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}a_{k}u_{i-k}
.
Through discrete-time Fourier transform analysis,
the symbol function of
\mathcal{A}
is given by

m(\theta)=\sum\limits_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}a_{k}e^{-\mathrm{i}k\theta},\;\theta\in[0,2\pi).

Substituting the kernel coefficients and using Euler’s formula yields

\displaystyle m(\theta)=\frac{1}{12}e^{\mathrm{i}\theta}+\frac{5}{6}+\frac{1}{12}e^{-\mathrm{i}\theta}=\frac{5+\cos\theta}{6}.

Since
\cos\theta\in[-1,1]
, we have
m(\theta)\in[{2}/{3},1]
. The positivity of
m(\theta)
guarantees the invertibility of
\mathcal{A}
.

The symbol function of the inverse operator
\mathcal{A}^{-1}
is given by

\frac{1}{m(\theta)}=\frac{6}{5+\cos\theta},

from which we obtain the operator norm

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{A}^{-1}\right\|=\sup_{\theta\in[0,2\pi)}\left|\frac{1}{m(\theta)}\right|=\sup_{\theta\in[0,2\pi)}\left|\frac{6}{5+\cos\theta}\right|=\frac{3}{2}.

Based on these preliminary results, we now
establish the main convergence theorem.

Theorem 14
.

Let
u(x,t)
be the exact solution of equation
(
1.1
) satisfying the regularity assumptions,
and let
f(u)
satisfy the Lipschitz condition,
i.e.,

\left|f(u^{1})-f(u^{2})\right|\leq L|u^{1}-u^{2}|

for some constant
L>0
. Define the error term

\displaystyle e_{i}^{n}:=u(x_{i},t_{n})-u_{i}^{n},\quad 0\leq n\leq N.

Then, under the condition that
\tau_{\max}\leq\sqrt[\alpha]{\frac{2\epsilon^{2}}{\kappa L^{2}\Gamma(2-\alpha)}}
for
1\leq n\leq N
, the following error estimate holds:

\displaystyle\left\|e^{n}\right\|\leq C_{1}C\sqrt{b-a}\begin{cases}\left(\tau^{2-\alpha}+h^{4}\right),&n=1,\\
\left(\tau^{3-\alpha}+h^{4}\right),&2\leq n\leq N.\end{cases}

Proof.

First, by subtracting equation (
3.5
) from equation
(
3.3
), we obtain the following error equation:

\begin{cases}\mathscr{A}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}B_{n-k}^{(n)}\triangledown_{\tau}e_{i}^{k}=\kappa\delta_{x}^{2}\left(f(u(x_{i},t_{n}))-f(u_{i}^{n})\right)+\kappa\varepsilon^{2}\delta_{x}^{2}\left(v(x_{i},t_{n})-v_{i}^{n}\right)+r_{i}^{n},\\
\mathscr{A}\left(v(x_{i},t_{n})-v_{i}^{n}\right)=-\delta_{x}^{2}e_{i}^{n},\\
e_{i}^{0}=0,\end{cases}

(4.6)

for
1\leq i\leq M-1,\;1\leq n\leq N
.
For subsequent analysis, we reformulate equation (
4.6
) as

\displaystyle\left(\theta c_{0}^{(n)}+\frac{\rho_{n}}{1+\rho_{n}}d_{0}^{(n)}\right)\triangledown_{\tau}e^{n}-\frac{\rho_{n}^{2}}{1+\rho_{n}}d_{0}^{(n)}\triangledown_{\tau}e^{n-1}+\widetilde{c}_{0}^{(n)}e^{n}-\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n-1}\left(\widetilde{c}_{n-k-1}^{(n)}-\widetilde{c}_{n-k}^{(n)}\right)e^{k}

(4.7)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\kappa\mathcal{H}\left(f\left(u\left(x_{i},t_{n}\right)\right)-f\left(u_{i}^{n}\right)\right)-\kappa\varepsilon^{2}\mathcal{H}\left(\mathcal{H}e^{n}\right)+\mathscr{A}^{-1}r^{n},

valid for
n\geq 2
.
Taking the inner product of (
4.7
) with
e^{n}
and applying the Lipschitz condition leads to

\displaystyle\Bigg(\left(\theta c_{0}^{(n)}+\frac{\rho_{n}}{1+\rho_{n}}d_{0}^{(n)}\right)\triangledown_{\tau}e^{n}-\frac{\rho_{n}^{2}}{1+\rho_{n}}d_{0}^{(n)}\triangledown_{\tau}e^{n-1}+\widetilde{c}_{0}^{(n)}e^{n}-\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\left(\widetilde{c}_{n-k-1}^{(n)}-\widetilde{c}_{n-k}^{(n)}\right)e^{k},e^{n}\Bigg)

(4.8)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\kappa L|\left(\mathcal{H}e^{n},e^{n}\right)|-\kappa\varepsilon^{2}\left\|\mathcal{H}e^{n}\right\|^{2}+\left(\mathscr{A}^{-1}r^{n},e^{n}\right).

The right-hand side of (
4.8
) is estimated through careful application of the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and Young’s inequality:

\displaystyle\kappa L|\left(\mathcal{H}e^{n},e^{n}\right)|-\kappa\varepsilon^{2}\|\mathcal{H}e^{n}\|^{2}+\left(\mathscr{A}^{-1}r^{n},e^{n}\right)\leq\kappa L\left(\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{L}\|\mathcal{H}e^{n}\|^{2}+\frac{L}{4\varepsilon^{2}}\|e^{n}\|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle-\kappa\varepsilon^{2}\|\mathcal{H}e^{n}\|^{2}+\left(\frac{\widetilde{c}_{n-1}^{(n)}}{2}+\frac{d_{1}^{(n)}}{2\rho_{n}\left(1+\rho_{n}\right)}\right)\|e^{n}\|^{2}+\frac{\|\mathscr{A}^{-1}\|^{2}}{2\widetilde{c}_{n-1}^{(n)}+\frac{2d_{1}^{(n)}}{\rho_{n}\left(1+\rho_{n}\right)}}\|r^{n}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\kappa L^{2}}{4\varepsilon^{2}}+\frac{\widetilde{c}_{n-1}^{(n)}}{2}+\frac{d_{1}^{(n)}}{2\rho_{n}\left(1+\rho_{n}\right)}\right)\|e^{n}\|^{2}+\frac{9}{8\widetilde{c}_{n-1}^{(n)}+\frac{8d_{1}^{(n)}}{\rho_{n}\left(1+\rho_{n}\right)}}\|r^{n}\|^{2}.

Similarly, the left-hand side of (
4.8
) can be estimated as:

\displaystyle\Bigg(\left(\theta c_{0}^{(n)}+\frac{\rho_{n}}{1+\rho_{n}}d_{0}^{(n)}\right)\triangledown_{\tau}e^{n}-\frac{\rho_{n}^{2}}{1+\rho_{n}}d_{0}^{(n)}\triangledown_{\tau}e^{n-1}+\widetilde{c}_{0}^{(n)}e^{n}-\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n-1}\left(\widetilde{c}_{n-k-1}^{(n)}-\widetilde{c}_{n-k}^{(n)}\right)e^{k},e^{n}\Bigg)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{\theta c_{0}^{(n)}+\frac{\rho_{n}}{1+\rho_{n}}d_{0}^{(n)}}{2}\left(\|e^{n}\|^{2}-\|e^{n-1}\|^{2}\right)-\frac{\rho_{n}^{2}d_{0}^{(n)}}{1+\rho_{n}}\left(\frac{1}{2\rho_{n}}\|e^{n}\|^{2}+\rho_{n}\|e^{n-1}\|^{2}+\rho_{n}\|e^{n-2}\|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle+\widetilde{c}_{0}^{(n)}\|e^{n}\|^{2}-\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{\widetilde{c}_{n-k-1}^{(n)}-\widetilde{c}_{n-k}^{(n)}}{2}\left(\|e^{n}\|^{2}+\|e^{k}\|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\theta}{2}c_{0}^{(n)}+\frac{1}{2}\widetilde{c}_{0}^{(n)}+\frac{1}{2}\widetilde{c}_{n-1}^{(n)}\right)\|e^{n}\|^{2}-\left(\frac{\theta c_{0}^{(n)}+\frac{\rho_{n}}{1+\rho_{n}}d_{0}^{(n)}}{2}+\frac{\rho_{n}^{3}d_{0}^{(n)}}{1+\rho_{n}}\right)\|e^{n-1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle-\frac{\rho_{n}^{3}d_{0}^{(n)}}{1+\rho_{n}}\|e^{n-2}\|^{2}-\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{\widetilde{c}_{n-k-1}^{(n)}-\widetilde{c}_{n-k}^{(n)}}{2}\|e^{k}\|^{2}.

Combining these estimates leads to the following key recursive inequality

\displaystyle\left(c_{0}^{(n)}-\frac{\kappa L^{2}}{2\varepsilon^{2}}\right)\left\|e^{n}\right\|^{2}\leq

\displaystyle\frac{9}{4\widetilde{c}_{n-1}^{(n)}+\frac{4d_{1}^{(n)}}{\rho_{n}(1+\rho_{n})}}\left\|r^{n}\right\|^{2}+\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n-1}G^{k}\left\|e^{k}\right\|^{2},

(4.9)

where the coefficients
G^{k}
are defined as

\displaystyle G^{k}=\begin{cases}\widetilde{c}_{n-k-1}^{(n)}-\widetilde{c}_{n-k}^{(n)},&k=1,2,\cdots,n-3,\;n\geq 4,\\
\widetilde{c}_{1}^{(n)}-\widetilde{c}_{2}^{(n)}+\frac{2\rho_{n}^{3}d_{0}^{(n)}}{1+\rho_{n}},&k=n-2,\;n\geq 3,\\
\widetilde{c}_{0}^{(n)}-\widetilde{c}_{1}^{(n)}+\theta c_{0}^{(n)}+\frac{\rho_{n}}{1+\rho_{n}}d_{0}^{(n)}+\frac{2\rho_{n}^{3}d_{0}^{(n)}}{1+\rho_{n}},&k=n-1,\;n\geq 2.\end{cases}

The condition
\tau_{\max}\leq\sqrt[\alpha]{\frac{2\epsilon^{2}}{\kappa L^{2}\Gamma(2-\alpha)}}
ensures that

c_{0}^{(n)}=\frac{1}{\tau_{n}^{\alpha}\Gamma(2-\alpha)}\geq\frac{\kappa L^{2}}{2\varepsilon^{2}}.

From (
3.4
), the truncation error satisfies

\left\|r^{n}\right\|^{2}\leq(b-a)C^{2}\begin{cases}\left(\tau^{2-\alpha}+h^{4}\right)^{2},&n=1,\\
\left(\tau^{3-\alpha}+h^{4}\right)^{2},&2\leq n\leq N.\end{cases}

(4.10)

Applying Lemma
12
to (
4.9
) and incorporating (
4.10
) establishes the desired error estimate:

\displaystyle\left\|e^{n}\right\|\leq C_{1}C\sqrt{b-a}\begin{cases}\left(\tau^{2-\alpha}+h^{4}\right),&n=1,\\
\left(\tau^{3-\alpha}+h^{4}\right),&2\leq n\leq N,\end{cases}

where
C_{1}=\sqrt{\frac{9}{\left(c_{0}^{(n)}-\frac{\kappa L^{2}}{2\varepsilon^{2}}\right)\left(4\widetilde{c}_{n-1}^{(n)}+\frac{4d_{1}^{(n)}}{\rho_{n}(1+\rho_{n})}\right)}}\exp\left(\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{G^{k}}{c_{0}^{(n)}-\frac{\kappa L^{2}}{2\varepsilon^{2}}}\right)
. This ends all the proofs.

Numerical examples

This section presents detailed numerical implementations and validation of the proposed methods. The examples demonstrate the effectiveness of the non-uniform temporal grid and verify the theoretical results established in previous sections.

5.1
Implementation details

Following Theorem
6
, the time-step ratios
\rho_{k}
must satisfy
1\leq\rho_{k}\leq\rho^{*}(\alpha)
for
k\geq 2
. To meet this requirement, we construct a special non-uniform grid defined by

\displaystyle\tau_{k}=t_{k}-t_{k-1}=\frac{(2k+1)^{3}}{N(N+2)(2N^{2}+4N+3)}T,\quad k=1,2,\cdots,N,

which guarantees the condition:

\displaystyle 1<\rho_{k}=\left(\frac{2k+1}{2k-1}\right)^{3}\leq\left(\frac{5}{3}\right)^{3}\approx 4.6296296<4.7476114\approx\overline{\rho}<\rho^{*}(\alpha).

These carefully designed meshes will be used throughout our numerical experiments for solving the TFCH equation (
1.1
).

The nonlinear fully discrete scheme (
3.5
) is solved using a simple iteration method with termination error tolerance
10^{-10}
.

The above numerical experiments provide rigorous verification of the theoretical analysis, establishing the effectiveness of the proposed numerical method for solving the time-fractional Cahn–Hilliard equation. As demonstrated in the computational results, the specially designed nonuniform temporal grid with step ratios satisfying
1\leq\tau_{k}/\tau_{k-1}\leq\rho^{*}(\alpha)
, where
\rho^{*}(\alpha)>\overline{\rho}\approx 4.7476114
, successfully resolves the initial singularity while achieving the theoretically predicted convergence rates. Specifically, the numerical solutions exhibit the expected temporal convergence order of
3-\alpha
, confirming the theoretical estimates derived in Section
4.4
.
Moreover, the proposed scheme maintains two fundamental physical properties of the continuous model: (1) exact volume conservation, and (2) energy dissipation throughout the simulation. These numerical observations, obtained under various parameter configurations, demonstrate the robustness of the method while preserving the essential features of the original physical system. The agreement between theoretical predictions and computational results across multiple test cases provides strong evidence for the reliability of the proposed approach.

6
Concluding remarks

This work has presented a high-order numerical scheme for the time-fractional Cahn-Hilliard equation, combining a
3-\alpha
-order temporal discretization on nonuniform grids with fourth-order spatial accuracy. The proposed method features several key theoretical advancements: an improved time-step ratio condition for the L2-type Caputo derivative approximation, rigorous proofs of unique solvability and discrete mass conservation, and establishment of energy decay properties. Numerical experiments comprehensively validate these theoretical results, demonstrating optimal convergence rates across different fractional orders while maintaining essential physical properties. The specially designed nonuniform temporal grids effectively handle the initial singularity, with the numerical results confirming both the high-order accuracy and stability of the proposed scheme.

The developed framework provides a solid foundation for future research on fractional phase-field models. Current efforts focus on extending this approach to more complex scenarios, including higher-dimensional problems, adaptive time-stepping strategies, and coupled systems involving multiple fractional operators. The combination of rigorous analysis and careful numerical implementation presented here offers a reliable template for solving challenging nonlinear fractional partial differential equations while preserving their fundamental mathematical and physical properties.
