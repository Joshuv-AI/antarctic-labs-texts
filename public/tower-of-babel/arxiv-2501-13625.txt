Information-theoretic limits and vector approximate message-passing for high-dimensional time series
Daria Tieplova, Samriddha Lahiry, Jean Barbier

Abstract
High-dimensional time series appear in many scientific setups, demanding a nuanced approach to model and analyze the underlying dependence structure. Theoretical advancements so far often rely on stringent assumptions regarding the sparsity of the underlying signal. In non-sparse regimes, analyses have primarily focused on linear regression models with the design matrix having independent rows. In this paper, we expand the scope by investigating a high-dimensional time series model wherein the number of features grows proportionally to the number of sampling points, without assuming sparsity in the signal. Specifically, we consider the stochastic regression model and derive a single-letter formula for the normalized mutual information between observations and the signal, as well as for minimum mean-square errors. We also empirically study the vector approximate message passing VAMP algorithm and show that, despite the lack of theoretical guarantees, its performance for inference in our time series model is robust and often statistically optimal.



I
Introduction

Analysis of high-dimensional time series are increasingly important with the advent of new technologies, with examples arising from macroeconomic forecasting
[
34
]
, genomics
[
2
]
, measurement of systemic risk in financial markets
[
9
]
, and fMRI studies
[
50
]
. Typically, these applications involve large datasets which exhibit both cross-sectional and temporal dependence. In many cases, questions related to the analysis of these structures can be formulated as estimation of a signal in a linear regression problem, as shown in
[
11
]
.

In the past decade, techniques from the high-dimensional statistics literature have been extended to deal with such dependent setups. However, the results so far have been entirely based on assumptions of low-dimensional structures, which often translate into the estimation of a very sparse signal in a linear regression task. On the other hand, a new paradigm has emerged in the high-dimensional asymptotics literature that are mainly motivated by techniques in statistical physics and information theory, where such stringent assumptions of sparsity are removed and one can analyze the same model when the number of features
p
and the number of samples
N
grow instead proportionally. In that regime, a particular problem of interest is computing the minimum mean-square error (MMSE) on the signal in a Bayesian setup, which is closely related to the computation of the mutual information between the signal and the observations. Specifically, one is interested in establishing a single-letter formula for the mutual information per observation in the large
N
limit, derive the limiting MMSE, and construct a computationally efficient estimator able to asymptotically match the MMSE performance, at least in some regimes. We note that while single-letter formulas for the aforementioned quantities have been rigorously established for linear regression with a design consisting of independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) Gaussian entries
[
3
,
8
,
49
,
4
]
, and later extended to deal with more structured designs drawn from certain dependent random matrix ensembles
[
8
,
22
,
23
,
24
,
32
,
42
]
, such results do not cover dependence structures that arise in time series models. In this article, we make an important step in that direction by rigorously establishing single-letter formulas for information-theoretic quantities in a stochastic regression model, where the rows of the measurement matrix come from an AR(1) process
[
11
]
.

I-A

Setting

To introduce our setup formally, let
(Y_{\mu},\mathbf{x}_{\mu})_{\mu=1}^{N}
be
N
observations from a time series, with the dependence structure outlined below. Let
\mathbf{x}_{\mu}
be a
p
dimensional, centered, stationary Gaussian process given by

\mathbf{x}_{\mu+1}=\mathbf{A}_{p}\mathbf{x}_{\mu}+\bm{\xi}_{\mu},\quad\mu\in\mathbb{Z},

(I.1)

where
\mathbf{A}_{p}
is a
p\times p
deterministic matrix and
\bm{\xi}_{\mu}
is a
p
-dimensional Gaussian random vector distributed as
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{1}^{2}\mathbf{I}_{p})
independently of everything else for each
\mu
.
The observed time series
Y_{\mu}
has the form

Y_{\mu}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{p}}\mathbf{x}_{\mu}^{\intercal}\bm{\beta}_{0}+Z_{\mu},\quad\mu=1,\ldots,N

(I.2)

where
\bm{\beta}_{0}
is a signal vector with i.i.d. entries drawn from the prior distribution
P_{0}
with bounded support and
\mathbb{E}\beta_{0,i}^{2}=\rho
, while
Z_{\mu}
is i.i.d. Gaussian noise with law
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{2}^{2})
. We can see that
Y_{\mu}
does not depend separately on
\sigma_{1}
or
\sigma_{2}
, but only on their ratio. Without loss of generality we can set one of the noise variances (
\sigma_{1}
or
\sigma_{2}
) to
1
. In what follows we consider
\sigma_{1}=1
and denote
\sigma_{2}=\sigma
.

We are interested in the high-dimensional regime where the number of observations
N
and the dimension
p
both tend to infinity at the same rate. That is, we assume that
p=p(N)
and for
c_{N}:=N/p
we have

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}c_{N}=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{N}{p}=c\in(0,\infty).

Let
\bm{\Phi}_{N}=(\mathbf{x}_{1}^{\intercal},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{N}^{\intercal})^{\intercal}
be a
N\times p
matrix whose rows are the vectors
(\mathbf{x}_{\mu})_{\mu=1}^{N}
. Further, let
\mathbf{Y}_{N}=(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{N})^{\intercal}
and
\mathbf{Z}_{N}=(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{N})^{\intercal}
be two vectors of length
N
containing the individual observations and noises, respectively. Then our model can be written in a vector form:

\displaystyle\mathbf{Y}_{N}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{p}}\bm{\Phi}_{N}\bm{\beta}_{0}+\mathbf{Z}_{N}.

(I.3)

The form of
\mathbf{A}_{p}
plays a major role in the type of resulting linear regression. An important example, addressed in, e.g.,
[
56
,
61
,
8
,
48
]
(with the final work proposing a near-optimal algorithm for the estimation of signal
\bm{\beta}_{0}
), is when
\mathbf{A}_{p}
is a symmetric random matrix with uniformly distributed eigenvectors. Then, one has the eigendecomposition
\mathbf{A}_{p}=\bm{U}\bm{D}\bm{U}^{-1}
with
\bm{U}
a Haar distributed random matrix and we may rewrite (
I.1
) as
\bm{U}^{-1}\mathbf{x}_{\mu+1}=\bm{D}\bm{U}^{-1}\mathbf{x}_{\mu}+\bm{\xi}_{\mu}
, noting that
\bm{U}^{-1}\bm{\xi}_{\mu}=\bm{\xi}_{\mu}
in law due to rotational invariance of the Gaussian noise. Thus the time series
\mathbf{z}_{\mu}=\bm{U}^{-1}\mathbf{x}_{\mu}
satisfies
\mathbf{z}_{\mu+1}=\bm{D}\mathbf{z}_{\mu}+\bm{\xi}_{\mu}
, which upon rewriting
\bm{\Phi}_{N}=\tilde{\bm{\Phi}}_{N}\bm{U}
with
\tilde{\bm{\Phi}}_{N}=(\mathbf{z}_{1}^{\intercal},\ldots,\mathbf{z}_{N}^{\intercal})^{\intercal}
brings us to the linear regression model with a right-rotationally invariant design matrix.

Our present interest lies in the extreme opposite setting where the previously Haar matrix
\bm{U}
is now proportional to the identity so that
\mathbf{A}_{p}
becomes diagonal, with a rather general choice of eigenvalues. Such a case
cannot
be treated with the aforementioned line of work and requires new techniques, both from the analytical and algorithmic perspective. We thus denote

\mathbf{A}_{p}={\rm diag}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{p})
with
|\lambda_{i}|<1
for all
i
. Under this assumption, equation (
I.1
) breaks down into
p
independent equations

x_{\mu+1,i}=\lambda_{i}x_{\mu,i}+\xi_{\mu,i},\quad i=1,\ldots,p\,,

which allows us to easily calculate the variance of
x_{\mu,i}
and the correlations between
x_{\mu,i}
and
x_{\mu+s,i}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(x_{\mu+1,i})^{2}=\lambda_{i}^{2}\mathbb{E}(x_{\mu,i})^{2}+1.

(I.4)

Since
\mathbf{x}_{\mu}
is a stationary process, its variance does not depend on
\mu
and the last equality gives us:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(x_{\mu,i})^{2}=(1-\lambda_{i}^{2})^{-1}.

(I.5)

From this we immediately obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(x_{\mu,i}x_{\mu+s,i})=\mathbb{E}(x_{\mu,i}(\lambda_{i}^{s}x_{\mu,i}+\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}\lambda_{i}^{j}\xi_{\mu-j+s-1,i}))=(1-\lambda^{2}_{i})^{-1}\lambda_{i}^{s}.

(I.6)

Independence of coordinates also implies that the columns of
\bm{\Phi}_{N}
are independent Gaussian vectors and, due to (
I.5
) and (
I.6
),
we can easily calculate the covariance matrix
\bm{\Lambda}_{i,N}
of the
i
-th column
(x_{1,i},x_{2,i},\ldots,x_{N,i})^{\intercal}
:

\displaystyle\bm{\Lambda}_{i,N}:=\dfrac{1}{1-\lambda_{i}^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1&\lambda_{i}&\ldots&\lambda_{i}^{N-1}\\
\lambda_{i}&1&\ldots&\lambda_{i}^{N-2}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\lambda_{i}^{N-1}&\lambda_{i}^{N-2}&\ldots&1\end{pmatrix}.

(I.7)

In what follows, we will often drop indices
N,p
when it is not crucial.
With this, we can present the design matrix as
\bm{\Phi}_{N}=(\bm{\Lambda}_{1,N}^{1/2}\mathbf{g}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\Lambda}_{p,N}^{1/2}\mathbf{g}_{p})
, where the
\mathbf{g}_{i}
are i.i.d. standard Gaussian vectors. In this form it is easy to see the connection to the previously well-studied models.

Case 1:
\lambda_{i}=0
for
i=1,\ldots,p.

In this case all
\bm{\Lambda}_{i,N}
are the identity and our model collapses to that of standard linear regression, where the design matrix has i.i.d. standard Gaussian elements. This model was studied in the CDMA
[
57
]

and compressed sensing contexts
[
21
,
31
,
3
,
7
,
49
]
.

Case 2:
\lambda_{i}=\lambda\neq 0
for
i=1,\ldots,p.

Again, all columns of
\bm{\Phi}_{N}
will share the same covariance matrix, denoted by
\bm{\Lambda}
. This allows us to rewrite
\bm{\Phi}_{N}=\bm{\Lambda}^{1/2}\mathbf{G}_{N}
, where
\mathbf{G}_{N}
is an
N\times p
matrix with i.i.d. standard Gaussian elements. Since
\mathbf{G}_{N}
is rotationally invariant, this results in the design matrix
\bm{\Phi}_{N}
belonging to the class of right-rotationally invariant matrices. Single-letter formulas for the mutual information of such models was obtained in
[
56
,
61
,
8
]
.

In this work, we aim for an arbitrary diagonal matrix
\mathbf{A}_{p}
with some natural assumptions. However, in order to address the general case, we first need to consider matrices

\mathbf{A}_{p}
for which the number of eigenvalues remains fixed to
k
as
p
goes to infinity. This assumption introduces a "block" structure that can be later "smoothed out" by taking afterwards
k\rightarrow\infty
.

Case 3:
We assume that diagonal matrix
\mathbf{A}_{p}
has
k
different eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k}
(
k
fixed) associated to the respective index sets
I_{1},\ldots,I_{k}
. More precisely, there is a partition of
\{1,\ldots,N\}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}I_{i}
such that
A_{jj}=\lambda_{i}
for
j\in I_{i}
. Importantly, we require that the cardinality of each such set grows proportionally with
p
, i.e., we have
|I_{i}|/p\rightarrow l_{i}>0
for each
i=1,\ldots,k
.

Without loss of generality, we further assume that
I_{1}
contains the first
|I_{1}|
integers,
I_{2}
contains the next
|I_{2}|
consecutive integers and so on, since it affects only the permutation of the columns of
\bm{\Phi}_{N}
. Then,

\bm{\Phi}_{N}
can be represented as a block matrix with
k
blocks of dimensions
N\times|I_{i}|
, i.e.,
(\bm{\Lambda}_{1,N}^{1/2}\mathbf{G}_{N}^{(1)},\ldots,\bm{\Lambda}_{k,N}^{1/2}\mathbf{G}_{N}^{(k)})
, where for every
i=1,\ldots,k
,
\mathbf{G}_{N}^{(i)}
is an
N\times|I_{i}|
independent matrix with i.i.d. standard Gaussian elements. With this, (
I.3
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\mathbf{Y}_{N}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}(\bm{\Lambda}_{1,N}^{1/2}\mathbf{G}_{N}^{(1)},\ldots,\bm{\Lambda}_{k,N}^{1/2}\mathbf{G}_{N}^{(k)})\bm{\beta}_{0}+\mathbf{Z}_{N}.

(I.8)

The block structure of the design matrix naturally propagates to the signal. In what follows we define
\bm{\beta}_{0}^{(i)}:=(\beta_{0,j})_{j\in I_{i}}\in\mathbb{R}^{|I_{i}|}
as the
i
-th block of
\bm{\beta}_{0}
, and similarly for
\bm{\beta}^{(i)}
.
We emphasize that in this setting the design matrix is not right-rotationally invariant, contrary to the Cases 1 and 2 described above. Instead, it exhibits a richer "block-wise" right-rotational invariance. Consequently, for the Cases 1 and 2 the MMSE can be obtained by the vector approximate message passing (VAMP) algorithm
[
48
]
(when no statistical-to-computational gap is present) which properly exploits the right rotationally invariant structure; see also the related concurrent orthogonal AMP algorithm (OAMP)
[
36
]
.

In the "block" model, however, the optimality of VAMP (or OAMP) is not guaranteed because of the absence of right rotational invariance. We will show empirically that in spite of the lack of theoretical guarantees for VAMP in this case, it nevertheless remains very robust and can consistently yield Bayes-optimal performance.

I-B

Related literature

We now discuss known results on high-dimensional time series. In particular, we compare ours to those from the closely related information theory literature, with a special focus on asymptotic formulas for mutual informations and MMSEs within the realm of (random) regression tasks. As we are going to also explore the algorithmic implications of our work using VAMP, we recall its original applications in scenarios involving right rotationally invariant ensembles of covariates matrices.

Analysis of high-dimensional time series is pervasive in the literature and a

II
Main results

In this section we state our main theorem on the asymptotic expression for the normalized mutual information. Consider the stochastic regression model given by equations (
I.2
) and (
I.1
). We assume that we are in the Bayesian optimal setting, meaning that the prior
P_{0}
is known together with the noise distribution as well as all hyperparameters of the problem. This means that we can write the true posterior distribution associated to (
I.3
):

\displaystyle dP(\bm{\beta}|\mathbf{Y}_{N}):=\mathbb{P}(\bm{\beta}_{0}\in(\bm{\beta},\bm{\beta}+d\bm{\beta})|\mathbf{Y}_{N})=\frac{P_{0}(\bm{\beta})\,d\bm{\beta}}{\mathcal{Z}(\mathbf{Y}_{N})}\text{exp}\Big\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|\mathbf{Y}_{N}-p^{-1/2}\bm{\Phi}_{N}\bm{\beta}\|^{2}\Big\},

(II.1)

where
\mathcal{Z}(\mathbf{Y}_{N})
is the partition function (i.e., normalisation constant) which reads, when expressing the observations in terms of the independent variables, as

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}(\mathbf{Y}_{N}):=\int P_{0}(\bm{\beta})\,\text{exp}\Big\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|p^{-1/2}\bm{\Phi}_{N}(\bm{\beta}_{0}-\bm{\beta})+\mathbf{Z}_{N}\|^{2}\Big\}\,d\bm{\beta},

(II.2)

and recall that
\bm{\beta}_{0}
is the ground truth. We interpret the posterior as a Boltzmann-Gibbs measure as in statistical mechanics and thus use the standard Gibbs bracket notation
\langle\cdot\rangle
to denote the expectation w.r.t. the posterior
dP(\bm{\beta}|\mathbf{Y})
(which thus remains a function of the data
\mathbf{Y}_{N}
even if not apparent from notation):

\displaystyle\langle g(\bm{\beta})\rangle:=\mathbb{E}[g(\bm{\beta})|\mathbf{Y}_{N}]=\int dP(\bm{\beta}|\mathbf{Y}_{N})g(\bm{\beta}).

(II.3)

II-A

Mutual information

One of the key objects of interest is the so-called
free energy

\bar{f}_{p}
, which is simply the standard Shannon entropy of the data distribution
H(\mathbf{Y}_{N})
given the design divided by the number of samples:

\bar{f}_{p}=-\frac{1}{p}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{Y}_{N}}\ln\mathcal{Z}(\mathbf{Y}_{N})=\frac{1}{p}H(\mathbf{Y}_{N}|\bm{\Phi}_{N})-\frac{N}{2}\log 2\pi\sigma.

(II.4)

It is connected to the mutual information through a simple relation

\displaystyle i_{p}:=\frac{1}{p}I(\bm{\beta}_{0};\mathbf{Y}_{N}|\bm{\Phi}_{N})=\frac{1}{p}H(\mathbf{Y}_{N}|\bm{\Phi}_{N})-\frac{1}{p}H(\mathbf{Y}_{N}|\bm{\beta}_{0},\bm{\Phi}_{N})=\bar{f}_{p}-\frac{c}{2}+o(1).

(II.5)

The aim of this article is to obtain a tractable low-dimensional variational formula for the normalized mutual information
i_{p}
between the observations
\mathbf{Y}_{N}
and the signal
\bm{\beta}
that is asymptotically correct as
N,p\rightarrow\infty
. This will be expressed thanks to a
replica symmetric potential
(this name comes from the derivation of such results based on the replica method from statistical physics
[
39
]
, see the Appendix) which in the present context reads

\displaystyle i_{\rm RS}(\mathbf{r}_{1},\mathbf{r}_{2}):=\frac{c}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\ln\Big(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{k}l_{i}\delta_{i}(\theta)r_{2,i}+1\Big)d\theta-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{k}l_{i}r_{1,i}r_{2,i}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}l_{i}I(\beta;\sqrt{r_{1,i}}\beta+Z),

(II.6)

where
\mathbf{r}_{1}=(r_{1,1},\ldots,r_{1,k})
and
\mathbf{r}_{2}=(r_{2,1},\ldots,r_{2,k})
are two
k
-dimensional vectors of parameters.
Here
\delta_{i}(\theta)
is the generating function (see Appendix
B
) of matrix
\bm{\Lambda}_{i,N}
(
I.7
) and given by

\displaystyle\delta_{i}(\theta):=\dfrac{1}{1-2\lambda_{i}\cos\theta+\lambda_{i}^{2}},

(II.7)

and
I(\beta;\sqrt{r_{1,i}}\beta+Z)
is the mutual information of the scalar channel
Y=\sqrt{r_{1,i}}\beta+Z
with
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
,
\beta\sim P_{0}
. We can now state our main theorem:

Theorem II.1

(Replica formula for the stochastic regression model)
.

Assume that the signal
\bm{\beta}
has i.i.d. entries with prior
P_{0}
with compact support and matrix
\mathbf{A}_{p}
is diagonal with a number of different eigenvalues
(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k})
independent of
N,p
(see
Case 3
or (
I.8
)). Then we have

\lim_{p\rightarrow\infty}i_{p}=\inf_{\mathbf{r}_{1}\in[0,+\infty)^{k}}\sup_{\mathbf{r}_{2}\in[0,\rho]^{k}}i_{\rm RS}(\mathbf{r}_{1},\mathbf{r}_{2}).

Remark II.1
.

The final replica formula for the limiting normalized mutual information can also be written as

\displaystyle\lim_{p\rightarrow\infty}i_{p}=\inf_{\Gamma}i_{\rm RS}(\mathbf{r}_{1},\mathbf{r}_{2}),

(II.8)

whenever the infimum of
\Gamma
, the set of critical points of the potential, is unique:

\displaystyle\Gamma=\Big\{

\displaystyle(\mathbf{r}_{1},\mathbf{r}_{2})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{k}\times[0,\rho]^{k}\ |\ \forall\,i=1,\ldots,k:

\displaystyle\qquad r_{2,i}=\text{mmse}(\beta|\sqrt{r_{1,i}}\beta+Z),\quad r_{1,i}=\frac{c}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{\delta_{i}(\theta)d\theta}{\sum_{j\leq k}l_{j}r_{2,j}\delta_{j}(\theta)+\sigma^{2}}\Big\}.

(II.9)

Above,
\text{mmse}(\beta|f(\beta))
is the MMSE on
\beta
given data
f(\beta)
. The equivalence of the two formulations has been shown in
[
4
]
and the equivalence in our current setup follows along similar lines.

General
\mathbf{A}_{p}

Let us also define the sets of Lipschitz functions

II-B

Minimum mean-square errors

An important criterion to measure the performance of an estimator is comparing it with the Bayes risk or the MMSE. In particular the estimator which minimizes the mean square error is the mean of the posterior distribution (
II.1
),
\langle\bm{\beta}\rangle
, and the MMSE is given by

\displaystyle{\rm mmse}(\bm{\beta}|\mathbf{Y}_{N},\bm{\Phi}_{N}):=\frac{1}{p}\mathbb{E}\|\bm{\beta}_{0}-\langle\bm{\beta}\rangle\|^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}l_{i}\,{\rm mmse}^{(i)}:=\sum_{i=1}^{k}l_{i}\frac{1}{|I_{i}|}\mathbb{E}\|\bm{\beta}_{0}^{(i)}-\langle\bm{\beta}^{(i)}\rangle\|^{2},

(II.17)

where
\bm{\beta}_{0}^{(i)}=(\beta_{0j})_{j\in I_{i}}\in\mathbb{R}^{|I_{i}|}
is the
i
th block of
\bm{\beta}_{0}
in its natural decomposition induced by the structure (
I.8
), and similarly for
\bm{\beta}^{(i)}
.
As it is not easy to access
\text{mmse}(\bm{\beta}|\mathbf{Y}_{N},\bm{\Phi}_{N})
directly from
i_{p}
, we introduce the
measurement MMSE
, which is the MMSE on the responses:

\displaystyle\text{ymmse}:=\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{E}\|\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}\bm{\Phi}_{N}\bm{\beta}_{0}-\langle\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}\bm{\Phi}_{N}\bm{\beta}\rangle\|^{2}.\

(II.18)

It is easy to find an expression for
ymmse
through the I-MMSE formula
[
28
]

\displaystyle\frac{di_{p}}{d\sigma^{-2}}=\frac{c_{N}}{2}\text{ymmse}.

(II.19)

From Theorem

we obtain the expression for the limit of

\displaystyle\lim_{p\rightarrow\infty}\text{ymmse}=\frac{2}{c}\lim_{p\rightarrow\infty}\frac{di_{p}}{d\sigma^{-2}}=\frac{2}{c}\frac{di_{\rm RS}(\tilde{\mathbf{r}})}{d\sigma^{-2}},

where

\Gamma

Proof.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}p}\sum_{\mu=1}^{N}Z_{\mu}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{r_{2,i}(t)\sqrt{l_{i}}}{\sqrt{R_{2,i}(t)}}(\bm{\Lambda}^{(i)}\mathbf{V}^{(i)})_{\mu}\Big)\ln\mathcal{Z}\Big]

\displaystyle i^{\prime}_{p,\epsilon}(t)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\langle\|\mathbf{u}_{t}\|^{4}\rangle_{t,\epsilon}]

Lemma IV.4

(Upper bound)

\displaystyle\limsup_{p\rightarrow\infty}i_{p}\leq\inf_{\mathbf{r}_{1}\in[0,+\infty)^{k}}\sup_{\mathbf{r}_{2}\in[0,\rho]^{k}}i_{\rm RS}(\mathbf{r}_{1},\mathbf{r}_{2})

(Lower bound)

\displaystyle\liminf_{p\rightarrow\infty}i_{p}\geq\inf_{\mathbf{r}_{1}\in[0,+\infty)^{k}}\sup_{\mathbf{r}_{2}\in[0,\rho]^{k}}i_{\rm RS}(\mathbf{r}_{1},\mathbf{r}_{2})

By similar arguments as in Lemma

i_{p}=\frac{1}{s_{p}^{2k}}\int_{[s_{p},2s_{p}]^{2k}}d\bm{\epsilon}\Big\{\sum_{i=1}^{k}l_{i}I_{1}(\int_{0}^{1}r_{1,i}(t,\bm{\epsilon})dt)+I_{2}(\int_{0}^{1}r_{2,1}(t,\bm{\epsilon})dt,\ldots,\int_{0}^{1}r_{2,k}(t,\bm{\epsilon})dt)\\

4

\displaystyle-\mathbf{y}^{\intercal}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{y}=\sum_{i,j}\frac{c}{2\pi\sigma^{4}}\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{y_{i}l_{i}l_{j}\delta_{i}(\theta)\delta_{j}(\theta)y_{j}}{(\sum_{m}l_{m}\delta_{m}(\theta)x_{m}/\sigma^{2}+1)^{2}}d\theta=\frac{c}{2\pi\sigma^{4}}\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{(\sum_{i}y_{i}l_{i}\delta_{i}(\theta))^{2}}{(\sum_{m}l_{m}\delta_{m}(\theta)x_{m}/\sigma^{2}+1)^{2}}d\theta\geq 0.

Then Jensen’s inequality yields

We want to show that

Indeed, let us define function

, since
I_{2}(\mathbf{r}_{2})
is concave,
g(\mathbf{r}_{2})
is also concave. From the definition of the solution
\mathbf{R}(t,\bm{\epsilon})
for each

reaches it’s maximum at
(r_{2,1}(t_{1},\bm{\epsilon}),\ldots,r_{2,k}(t_{k},\bm{\epsilon}))
. From what immediately follows

i_{p}-\bar{i}_{p}(k)\geq\frac{1}{p}\mathbb{E}\langle H-\bar{H}\rangle_{H}
, which gives

\displaystyle\bar{i}_{p}(k)-i_{p}\leq\frac{1}{p}\mathbb{E}\langle\bar{H}-H\rangle_{H}.

(IV.48)

Now what is left is to bound is
p^{-1}|\bar{H}-H|
:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}|\bar{H}-H|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}p}|p^{-1}(\bm{\beta}_{0}-\bm{\beta})^{\intercal}(\bar{\bm{\Phi}}-\bm{\Phi})^{\intercal}\bar{\bm{\Phi}}(\bm{\beta}_{0}-\bm{\beta})+p^{-1}(\bm{\beta}_{0}-\bm{\beta})^{\intercal}\bm{\Phi}^{\intercal}(\bar{\bm{\Phi}}-\bm{\Phi})(\bm{\beta}_{0}-\bm{\beta})

\displaystyle+2p^{-1/2}(\bm{\beta}_{0}-\bm{\beta})^{\intercal}(\bar{\bm{\Phi}}-\bm{\Phi})^{\intercal}\mathbf{Z}|\leq\|\bar{\bm{\Phi}}-\bm{\Phi}\|\Big(\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}p^{2}}\|\bm{\beta}_{0}-\bm{\beta}\|^{2}\|\bar{\bm{\Phi}}+\bm{\Phi}\|+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}p^{3/2}}\|\bm{\beta}_{0}-\bm{\beta}\|\|\mathbf{Z}\|\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\max_{i\leq k}\|\bm{\Lambda}_{i,N}-\bar{\bm{\Lambda}}_{i}(k)\|\times\|\mathbf{G}\|\Big(\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}p^{2}}\|\bm{\beta}_{0}-\bm{\beta}\|^{2}\|\bar{\bm{\Phi}}+\bm{\Phi}\|+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}p^{3/2}}\|\bm{\beta}_{0}-\bm{\beta}\|\|\mathbf{Z}\|\Big),

where
\mathbf{G}=\mathbf{G}_{N}
is the
N\times p
matrix composed of the blocks
\mathbf{G}_{N}^{(i)}
with i.i.d. Gaussian entries in
\bm{\Phi}
, see (
I.8
). The last inequality can be proved by verifying that for any vectors
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
or respective sizes, we have
\mathbf{x}^{\intercal}(\bar{\bm{\Phi}}-\bm{\Phi})\mathbf{y}\leq\mathbf{x}^{\intercal}\max_{i\leq k}\|\bm{\Lambda}_{i,N}-\bar{\bm{\Lambda}}_{i}(k)\|\mathbf{G}\mathbf{y}
. Applying twice the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality we obtain that the expectation w.r.t.
\mathbb{E}\langle\cdot\rangle_{H\,{\rm or}\,\bar{H}}
of the random part is of order one. To bound
\lim_{p\rightarrow\infty}\max_{i\leq k}\|\bm{\Lambda}_{i,N}-\bar{\bm{\Lambda}}_{i}(k)\|
we recall that asymptotically the eigenspaces of
\bm{\Lambda}_{i}
and
\bar{\bm{\Lambda}}_{i}(k)
are the same (see Appendix
B
) so

\displaystyle\lim_{p\rightarrow\infty}\|\bm{\Lambda}_{i,N}-\bar{\bm{\Lambda}}_{i}(k)\|\leq\Big|\frac{1}{(1-\lambda_{i})^{2}}-\frac{1}{(1-\bar{\lambda}_{i}(k))^{2}}\Big|\leq\frac{|\lambda_{i}-\bar{\lambda}_{i}(k)|(2-\lambda_{i}-\bar{\lambda}_{i}(k))}{(1-\bar{\lambda}_{i}(k))^{2}(1-\lambda_{i})^{2}}\leq\frac{C}{k}.

(IV.49)

This gives us
\lim_{p\rightarrow\infty}|i_{p}-\bar{i}_{p}(k)|=O(1/k)
.

Now, to deal with the RHS of (
IV.40
) we notice that for each pair of vectors
(\bar{\mathbf{r}}_{1},\bar{\mathbf{r}}_{2})\in\bar{\Gamma}(k)
we can write corresponding
Lipschitz bounded functions defined on
[a,b]
such that
r_{1}(\alpha_{i-1})=\bar{r}_{1,i}(k)
and
r_{2}(\alpha_{i-1})=\bar{r}_{2,i}(k)
. To see this, we return to the definition of
\bar{\Gamma}(k)
and define the corresponding functions
r_{1}(\lambda)
and
r_{2}(\lambda)
as

\displaystyle r_{2}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\text{mmse}(\beta|\sqrt{r_{1}(\lambda)}\beta+Z),

(IV.50)

\displaystyle r_{1}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{c}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{\delta(\theta,\lambda)d\theta}{\int r_{2}(s)\delta(\theta,s)d\bar{\eta}_{k}(s)+\sigma^{2}}.

(IV.51)

These automatically verify the aforementioned conditions
(r_{1}(\alpha_{i-1}),r_{2}(\alpha_{i-1}))=(\bar{r}_{1,i}(k),\bar{r}_{2,i}(k))
. Then we can rewrite the replica potential equivalently as

\displaystyle i_{\rm RS}(\bar{\mathbf{r}}_{1}(k),\bar{\mathbf{r}}_{2}(k))

\displaystyle=\frac{c}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\ln\Big(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\int_{a}^{b}\delta(\theta,\lambda)r_{2}(\lambda)d\bar{\eta}_{k}(\lambda)\Big)d\theta-\frac{1}{2}\int_{a}^{b}r_{1}(\lambda)r_{2}(\lambda)d\bar{\eta}_{k}(\lambda)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}I(\beta;\sqrt{r_{1}(\lambda)}\beta+Z)d\bar{\eta}_{k}(\lambda).

(IV.52)

Note that the optimal value of the potential written in this form w.r.t. functions
(r_{1},r_{2})
instead of simply vectors
(\bar{\mathbf{r}}_{1}(k),\bar{\mathbf{r}}_{2}(k))
enforces the solution(s) to verify (
IV.50
), (
IV.51
) at
\lambda\in\{\alpha_{0},\ldots,\alpha_{k-1}\}
, the only points on which the potential depends on. It implies that we can write

\displaystyle\inf_{[0,C]^{k}}\sup_{[0,\rho]^{k}}i_{\rm RS}(\bar{\mathbf{r}}_{1}(k),\bar{\mathbf{r}}_{2}(k))=

\displaystyle\inf_{\mathcal{C}_{1}}\sup_{\mathcal{C}_{2}}\Big\{\frac{c}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\ln\Big(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\int_{a}^{b}\delta(\theta,\lambda)r_{2}(\lambda)d\bar{\eta}_{k}(\lambda)\Big)d\theta

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\int_{a}^{b}r_{1}(\lambda)r_{2}(\lambda)d\bar{\eta}_{k}(\lambda)+\int_{a}^{b}I(\beta;\sqrt{r_{1}(\lambda)}\beta+Z)d\bar{\eta}_{k}(\lambda)\Big\},

(IV.53)

where
\mathcal{C}_{1}
is the set of Lipschitz functions
f:[a,b]\rightarrow[0,C]
and
\mathcal{C}_{2}
is the set of Lipschitz functions
f:[a,b]\rightarrow[0,\rho]
. We need to find the limit of the RHS as
k\rightarrow\infty
. In order to exchange the limit with the
\inf\sup
, it is sufficient to show that the potential on the RHS of (
IV.53
) converges uniformly on the set
\mathcal{C}_{1}\times\mathcal{C}_{2}
. But showing uniformity is straightforward: since
\bar{\eta}_{k}\rightarrow\eta
in Wasserstein-2 distance, it implies that
\int f(\lambda)d\bar{\eta}_{k}\rightarrow\int f(\lambda)d\eta
uniformly on the set of Lipschitz functions bounded by
\max(C,\rho)
.

Appendix A
Replica calculation

In this appendix we derive
the replica symmetric potential (
A.46
) of the free energy using the so called replica method
[
40
]
which was first introduced in the context of the spin glasses and became a powerful method of statistical physics.
It is based on the simple identities that is also known as replica trick

\lim_{N\rightarrow+\infty}\mathbb{E}f_{N}=-\lim_{N\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{1}{p}\mathbb{E}\ln\mathcal{Z}(\mathbf{Y})=-\lim_{N\rightarrow+\infty}\lim_{u\rightarrow 0}\dfrac{1}{pu}\ln\mathbb{E}\mathcal{Z}(\mathbf{Y})^{u},

(A.1)

where the replicated partition function is

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}(\mathbf{Y})^{u}=\int\prod_{a=1}^{u}dP_{0}(\bm{\beta}_{a})e^{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\sum_{a=1}^{u}\|p^{-1/2}\bm{\Phi}(\bm{\beta}_{0}-\bm{\beta}_{a})+\mathbf{Z}\|^{2}}.

(A.2)

Here and in what follows for convenience we omit dimensional indexes
N
and
p
in
\mathbf{Y}_{N}
,
\mathbf{Z}_{N}
,
\bm{\Phi}_{N}
, etc.
We compute

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathcal{Z}(\mathbf{Y})^{u}=\int\prod_{a=0}^{u}dP_{0}(\bm{\beta}_{a})dP_{X}dP_{Z}e^{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\sum_{a=1}^{u}\sum_{\mu=1}^{N}(p^{-1/2}\sum_{j=1}^{p}x_{\mu,j}(\bm{\beta}_{0}-\bm{\beta}_{a})_{j}+Z_{\mu})^{2}}.

(A.3)

Our goal is to calculate above expression as a Gaussian integral, and for this we introduce a new variable
v_{\mu.a}=p^{-1/2}\sum_{j=1}^{p}x_{\mu,j}(\bm{\beta}_{0}-\bm{\beta}_{a})_{j}+Z_{\mu}
. One can easily see, that conditionally on
\bm{\beta}
, vector
\mathbf{v}=(v_{\mu,a})_{\mu=1,a=1}^{N,u}
is Gaussian, with zero mean and covariance matrix

=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{j=1}^{p}(\bm{\beta}_{0}-\bm{\beta}_{a})_{j}\lambda_{j}^{|\mu-\nu|}(1-\lambda_{j}^{2})^{-1}(\bm{\beta}_{0}-\bm{\beta}_{b})_{j}+\delta_{\mu,\nu}\sigma^{2},

last equality being true due to (
I.6
).
We consider two different cases: when
\mathbf{A}
is multiple of identity and when

has finite number of different eigenvalues. While, obviously, first case is a part of the second, for the sake of clarity, we prefer to study the first case separately in more details and then transfer some results to

\displaystyle Q_{12}

Similarly to Case 1, we can write

for off diagonal and

(A.35)

the last equality being true because all terms are block-diagonal matrices. Now we can easily find the limit

Appendix B
Kac-Murdock-Szegö matrices

The Kac-Murdock-Szegö (KMS) matrix is the special class of symmetric Toeplitz matrices of the general form

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}_{N,\rho}=\begin{bmatrix}1&\rho&\rho^{2}&\dots&\rho^{N-1}\\
\rho&1&\rho&\dots&\rho^{N-2}\\
\rho^{2}&\rho&1&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\rho\\
\rho^{N-1}&\rho^{N-2}&\dots&\rho&1\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}\qquad(1)

(B.1)

the term originating from the 1953
paper
[
29
]
by Kac, Murdock, and Szegö. In
[
29
]
(see also the book
[
27
]
by Grenander and Szegö), a detailed study of
such matrices was an important first step for the development of general theorems on the
asymptotic (large-
n
) behaviour of eigenvalues and determinants of Toeplitz matrices. The entries of such matrix come from a Fourier coefficients of the function

\displaystyle F_{\rho}(\theta)=\frac{1-\rho^{2}}{1-2\rho\cos\theta+\rho^{2}}=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{+\infty}\rho^{|n|}e^{in\theta},

(B.2)

and because of this such function also sometimes called generating function of
\mathbf{A}_{N,\rho}
. From
[
27
]
, the eigenvalues of
\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A}_{N,\rho})<\lambda_{2}(\mathbf{A}_{N,\rho})<\ldots<\lambda_{N}(\mathbf{A}_{N,\rho})
are

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{A}_{N,\rho})=F_{\rho}(\theta_{i,N}),

(B.3)

where
\frac{(i-1)\pi}{N+1}<\theta_{i,N}<\frac{i\pi}{N+1}
, for
i=1,\ldots,N
. As
N\rightarrow\infty
, for any continuous function
f
we have

\displaystyle\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}f(\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{A}_{N,\rho}))=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}f(F_{\rho}(\theta))d\theta.

(B.4)

And as consequence, the sequence of eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{k}(\mathbf{A}_{N,\rho}\}
is asymptotically equally distributed to the sequence
\{F_{\rho}(2\pi k/N)\}
.
The same is true for the functions of several KMS matrices. An important property, that we mentioning throughout the proofs is that even thought for fixed
N
eigenvectors of
\mathbf{A}_{N}(\rho)
are different for different values of
\rho
, in the limit it does not depend on
\rho
. More precisely, if we take the set of KMS matrix
\{\mathbf{A}_{N}(\rho_{1}),\mathbf{A}_{N}(\rho_{1}),\ldots,\mathbf{A}_{N}(\rho_{m})
, then for a continuous function
f
, the distribution of eigenvalues of
f(\mathbf{A}_{N,\rho_{1}},\ldots,\mathbf{A}_{N,\rho_{m}})
is asymptotically equivalent to distribution of
\{f(F_{\rho_{1}}(2\pi k/N),\ldots,F_{\rho_{m}}(2\pi k/N))\}_{k=1}^{N}
.

Appendix C
Nishimori identity

In this paper we place ourselves in Bayesian-optimal setting which allows us to use so called Nishimori identities to simplify significantly some parts of calculations. They were initially discovered in the context of the gauge theory of spin glasses
[
44
]
.
To introduce them, we consider a generic inference problem where a Bayes-optimal statistician observes
\mathbf{Y}
that is a random function of some ground truth signal
\bm{\beta}^{*}
:
\mathbf{Y}\sim P_{y|\beta}(\bm{\beta}^{*})
. Then the following holds:

Proposition C.1

(Nishimori identity)
.

For any bounded function
f
of the signal
\bm{\beta}^{*}
, the data
\mathbf{Y}
and of conditionally i.i.d. samples from the posterior
\bm{\beta}^{j}\sim P_{\beta|y}(\,\cdot|\mathbf{Y})
,
j=1,2,\ldots,n
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\langle f(\mathbf{Y},\bm{\beta}^{*},\bm{\beta}^{2},\ldots,\bm{\beta}^{n})\rangle=\mathbb{E}\langle f(\mathbf{Y},\bm{\beta}^{1},\bm{\beta}^{2},\ldots,\bm{\beta}^{n})\rangle

(C.1)

where the bracket notation
\langle\,\cdot\,\rangle
is used for the joint expectation over the posterior samples
(\bm{\beta}^{j})_{j\leq n}
,
\mathbb{E}
is over the signal
\bm{\beta}^{*}
and data
\mathbf{Y}
.

Proof.

The proof follows directly from Bayes’ rule. An elementary proof can be found in
[
33
]
.
∎

Appendix D
Concentration of the free energy

Proposition D.1

(Concentration of the free energy)
.

\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big|\frac{1}{p}\log\mathcal{Z}-\mathbb{E}\Big[\frac{1}{p}\log\mathcal{Z}\Big]\Big|^{2}\Big]\leq Cp^{-1}

(D.1)

The proof follows by hierarchical concentration results. In particular
in the first step we prove concentration inequality when expectation is taken conditional on the variables
V^{(i)}_{j}
s,
G^{(i)}_{jk}
s, and
\beta^{(i)}_{0,j}
s. In the next step we prove the concentration inequality when the expectation is taken
conditional on
\beta^{(i)}_{0.j}
, while in the final step we prove concentration around the unconditional expectation. Thus Lemma
D.1
follows from the Lemmas
D.3
,
D.4
, and
D.5
.
Before diving into the calculations, we recall two classical concentration results, which will be the core of the proofs. Their proofs can be found in
[
15
]
, Chapter 3.

Lemma D.1

(Poincaré-Nash inequality)
.

Let
g:\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a continuously differentiable function and
\mathbf{X}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{p})
be a random vector with i.i.d.
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
entries. Then the following holds:

\mathrm{Var}(g(\mathbf{X}))\leq\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla g\|^{2}].

Lemma D.2

(Bounded difference)
.

Let
g:\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a function that satisfies the bounded difference property:

\sup_{u_{1},\ldots,u_{p},u^{\prime}_{i}}|g(u_{1},\ldots,u_{i},\ldots,u_{p})-g(u_{1},\ldots,u^{\prime}_{i},\ldots,u_{p})|\leq c_{i},\quad 1\leq i\leq p.

If
\mathbf{X}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{p})
be a random vector with i.i.d.
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
entries. Then the following holds:

\mathrm{Var}(g(\mathbf{X}))\leq\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i=1}^{p}c^{2}_{i}.

Let
\mathbf{W}_{1}=(\mathbf{Z},\tilde{\mathbf{Z}})
. As a first step, we prove the following lemma

Lemma D.3
.

\mathbb{E}\Big[\left|\frac{1}{p}\log\mathcal{Z}-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{W}_{1}}\Big[\frac{1}{p}\log\mathcal{Z}\right]\Big|^{2}\Big]\leq Cp^{-1}.

Proof.

Let
g(\mathbf{W}_{1})=\log\mathcal{Z}
. The partial derivatives with respect to
Z_{\mu}
and
\tilde{Z}^{(i)}_{j}
s are given by:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial g}{\partial Z_{\mu}}

\displaystyle=-\Big\langle Z_{\mu}+\sqrt{\frac{1-t}{p}}\Big(\bm{\Phi}\Big(\bm{\beta}_{0}-\bm{\beta}\Big)\Big)_{\mu}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sqrt{R_{2,i}(t)l_{i}}\Big(\bm{\Lambda}^{(i)}\Big(\mathbf{V}^{(i)}-\mathbf{v}^{(i)}\Big)\Big)_{\mu}\Big\rangle,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial g}{\partial\tilde{Z}^{(i)}_{j}}

\displaystyle=-\Big\langle\tilde{Z}^{(i)}_{j}+\sqrt{R_{1,i}(t)}(\bm{\beta}_{0}^{(i)}-\bm{\beta}^{(i)})_{j}\Big\rangle.

Using Jensen inequality we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big(\frac{\partial g}{\partial Z_{\mu}}\Big)^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq C\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big\langle Z^{2}_{\mu}+\frac{1-t}{p}\Big(\bm{\Phi}\Big(\bm{\beta}_{0}-\bm{\beta}\Big)\Big)^{2}_{\mu}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}R_{2,i}(t)l_{i}\Big(\bm{\Lambda}^{(i)}\Big(\mathbf{V}^{(i)}-\mathbf{v}^{(i)}\Big)\Big)^{2}_{\mu}\Big\rangle\Big],

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big(\frac{\partial g}{\partial\tilde{Z}^{(i)}_{j}}\Big)^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq C\mathbb{E}\Big[\langle(\tilde{Z}^{(i)}_{j})^{2}+R_{1,i}(t)(\beta_{0,j}^{(i)}-\beta_{j}^{(i)})^{2}\rangle\Big].

Combining all above and applying Nishimori identity, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\nabla g\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{\mu=1}^{N}\Big(\frac{\partial g}{\partial Z_{\mu}}\Big)^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{|I_{i}|}\Big(\frac{\partial g}{\partial\tilde{Z}^{(i)}_{j}}\Big)^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq C\mathbb{E}\Big[\|\mathbf{Z}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{p}\|\bm{\Phi}\|^{2}\|\bm{\beta}_{0}\|^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}R_{2,i}(t)l_{i}\|\bm{\Lambda}^{(i)}\|^{2}\|\mathbf{V}^{(i)}\|^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}(\|\tilde{\mathbf{Z}}^{(i)}\|^{2}+R^{2}_{1,i}(t)\|\bm{\beta}_{0}^{(i)}\|^{2})\Big]

\displaystyle\leq Cp.

The last inequality holds since all the quantities
\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{Z}\|^{2}],\mathbb{E}[\|\tilde{\mathbf{Z}}^{(i)}\|^{2}],\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{V}^{(i)}\|^{2}]
, and
\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{\beta}^{(i)}_{0}\|^{2}]
are
O(p)
. The operator norm of
\bm{\Phi}
also can be bounded us

\mathbb{E}\|\bm{\Phi}\|^{2}=\mathbb{E}\|\bm{\Phi}\bm{\Phi}^{\intercal}\|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\|\bm{\Lambda}_{i,N}\|\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{G}_{i}\mathbf{G}_{i}^{\intercal}\|]=O(p).

From Gaussian Poincaré inequality (Lemma
D.1
) it follows that

\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big|\frac{1}{p}\log\mathcal{Z}-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{W_{1}}}\Big[\frac{1}{p}\log\mathcal{Z}\Big]\Big|^{2}\Big]=p^{-2}\mathrm{Var}(g)\leq p^{-2}\mathbb{E}\|\nabla g\|^{2}=Cp^{-1}.

∎

Let
\mathbf{W_{2}}=(\mathbf{Z},\tilde{\mathbf{Z}},\mathbf{G},\mathbf{V})
where
\mathbf{G}
is the ensemble of Gaussian random variables in
\bm{\Phi}
. In the next step we prove that

Lemma D.4
.

\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big|\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{W_{1}}}\Big[\frac{1}{p}\log\mathcal{Z}\Big]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{W_{2}}}\Big[\frac{1}{p}\log\mathcal{Z}\Big]\Big|^{2}\Big]\leq Cp^{-1}.

Proof.

Let
h(\mathbf{G},\mathbf{V})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{W_{1}}}\Big[\log\mathcal{Z}\Big]
. We have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial h}{\partial V^{(j)}_{m}}=

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{W_{1}}}\Big[\Big\langle\sum_{\mu=1}^{N}\Big(Z_{\mu}+\sqrt{\frac{1-t}{p}}\Big(\bm{\Phi}(\bm{\beta}_{0}-\bm{\beta})\Big)_{\mu}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sqrt{R_{2,i}(t)l_{i}}\Big(\bm{\Lambda}^{(i)}(\mathbf{V}^{(i)}-\mathbf{v}^{(i)})\Big)_{\mu}\Big)\sqrt{R_{2,j}(t)l_{j}}\Lambda^{(j)}_{\mu m}\Big\rangle\Big]

\displaystyle\frac{\partial h}{\partial G^{(j)}_{sm}}=

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{W_{1}}}\Big[\Big\langle

\displaystyle\sum_{\mu=1}^{N}\Big(Z_{\mu}+\sqrt{\frac{1-t}{p}}\Big(\bm{\Phi}(\bm{\beta}_{0}-\bm{\beta})\Big)_{\mu}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sqrt{R_{2,i}(t)l_{i}}\Big(\bm{\Lambda}^{(i)}(\mathbf{V}^{(i)}-\mathbf{v}^{(i)})\Big)_{\mu}\Big)\sqrt{\frac{1-t}{p}}\Lambda^{(j)}_{\mu s}(\bm{\beta}_{0}^{(j)}-\bm{\beta}^{(j)})_{m}\Big\rangle\Big]

\displaystyle:=\sum_{s,m}\mathbb{E}[(I^{1}_{j,sm})^{2}]

\sum_{m}\mathbb{E}\Big[\frac{1}{p}\frac{\partial h}{\partial V^{(j)}_{m}}\Big]^{2}\leq Cp^{-1}.
