Implanting a gold-coated cardioverter-defibrillator as a solution to cardiac implantable device hypersensitivity: a case report
Authors: Grzegorz Ehrenhalt, Paweł Syska, Leszek Blicharz, Michał Lewandowski, Mariusz Pytkowski
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHypersensitivity to cardiac implantable electronic device (CIED) materials is a rare and underrecognized complication. It often mimics infection, leading to misdiagnosis and inappropriate management. This case report contributes to the literature by demonstrating a structured diagnostic approach and the successful use of a custom-made, gold-coated ICD.Case summaryA 40-year-old male with dilated cardiomyopathy underwent subcutaneous ICD implantation. Seven months later, he presented with symptoms suggesting pocket infection, resulting in device removal. Subsequently, a transvenous ICD was implanted, but within weeks, signs of local inflammation reappeared, progressing to a necrotizing fistula. Blood tests and wound cultures revealed no infection. Given the patient’s history of cutaneous allergic reactions to metals, hypersensitivity was suspected. Skin patch testing confirmed allergic responses to multiple device components. The patient underwent complete system extraction. A custom-made, gold-coated single-chamber ICD was provided by the manufacturer and successfully implanted. One-year follow-up revealed no signs of recurrent inflammation or allergic symptoms, confirming the diagnosis and effectiveness of the intervention.DiscussionHypersensitivity to CIED materials is an important differential diagnosis in cases of recurrent pocket inflammation with negative microbiological findings. Due to overlapping local symptoms, it is frequently mistaken for infection, leading to delayed or inappropriate management. A detailed allergy history and use of skin patch testing are essential in guiding diagnosis. Once confirmed, conservative measures such as corticosteroids have limited value, and definitive treatment requires full system explantation. Reimplantation with a hypoallergenic device—such as a gold-coated generator—represents an effective therapeutic strategy.

Introduction

Contact allergy to compounds of cardiac implantable electronic devices (CIEDs) is a rare, often misdiagnosed complication.1 The mechanism responsible for this phenomenon is thought to be a delayed type IV hypersensitivity reaction. Due to its symptomatology, device allergy is often initially misinterpreted as an infection and therefore inadequately treated, leading to recurrence.1 Effective management requires complete device explantation, identification of allergenic material, and subsequent implantation of a hypoallergenic device.2 This case report contributes to the limited clinical descriptions of device hypersensitivity, providing evidence for effective diagnosis and treatment and proposing a novel structured management approach.

Case report

In June 2022, at a regional hospital, a 40-year-old male patient with non-ischaemic dilated cardiomyopathy underwent implantation of a subcutaneous cardioverter-defibrillator (S-ICD, Emblem A219, Boston Scientific) for primary prevention of sudden cardiac death due to symptomatic heart failure with a reduced left ventricular ejection fraction of 25%. No early complications from the procedure were observed. Seven months post-discharge, signs of infection developed in the device pocket, resulting in complete device removal and administration of antibiotic therapy. Documentation for this period is incomplete—wound cultures were not performed, and blood workups remain unavailable. After another 5 months, a conventional single-chamber transvenous implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD-VR Gallant, Abbott) was implanted in the same centre. Within 1.5 months of implantation, early signs of local peri-device inflammation were observed, including erythema, swelling, and soreness. Three months following this procedure, the patient presented to our clinic with a necrotizing fistula extended from the device pocket to the patient’s skin (Figure 1).

Necrotizing fistula extended from the device pocket to the patient’s skin as a result of metal hypersensitivity.

Standard blood testing and microbiological cultures were performed—blood count was within normal limits, as were inflammatory markers; repeated swabs and cultures from the device pocket showed no bacterial growth. At this point, hypersensitivity to the compounds of the device was suspected. A thorough medical history was obtained. The patient reported a history of mild cutaneous allergic reactions to various metal objects, e.g. shirt buttons. Subsequently, the patient underwent a successful total extraction of the lead and device generator. After the procedure, samples from each part of the device were prepared in a form suitable for skin patch testing, which was then carried out (Figure 2). Multiple device components elicited a strong allergic reaction within 24 h. According to the device specifications, metals in contact with tissue included titanium, iridium, and platinum. Additional standard patch testing using the Polish Standard Test (PST-1000) with 2% gold sodium thiosulfate demonstrated a strong palladium allergy. Notably, no reaction to the gold solution was observed.

Positive hypersensitivity reaction to elements of the cardiac implantable device in skin patch tests.

Several CIED manufacturers were contacted to assess the feasibility of producing a customized, gold-coated, hypoallergenic, single-chamber ICD for the patient. Feasibility was confirmed by Medtronic. The patient was temporarily secured with a wearable defibrillation vest (ZOLL LifeVest®) until the customized device (Medtronic Cobalt™ XT ICD) was implanted in the right subclavian region. No short-term complications were noted. Follow-up over 1 year revealed no inflammation at the device pocket, ultimately confirming hypersensitivity to the CIED (Table 1).

Discussion

Implantable device allergy can mimic infectious complications, creating diagnostic challenges.3 Cardiac implantable electronic device infection is far more common and potentially life-threatening; therefore, it should be considered first.1 Both conditions initially present with peri-device inflammatory symptoms, typically erythema, swelling, and pain.1 Severe manifestations, such as ulceration or device exteriorization, may develop over time, as illustrated by the present case. Unlike infection, device hypersensitivity rarely induces systemic inflammatory responses, such as fever, leucocytosis, neutrophilia, or elevated C-reactive protein. It may, however, be associated with systemic eosinophilia, cutaneous eruptions, negative bacterial cultures from blood, wound, and explanted device parts, and occasionally generalized allergic manifestations, including erythroderma or anogenital dermatitis.1,4 Prior cutaneous reactions to metals provide an important diagnostic clue. In this context, Schalock and Thyssen have proposed a pre-implantation questionnaire to identify patients who may benefit from skin patch testing, along with diagnostic criteria for device allergy.5 Had these tools been applied prior to the first or second implantation in the present case, appropriate management could likely have been initiated earlier.

As regards this case report, skin patch testing was conclusive, and no further tests were required to confirm hypersensitivity. However, convincing identification of the specific allergen may sometimes be challenging. Currently, most CIED generators are made of pure titanium or titanium alloys, with other metals such as magnesium, nickel, or tin present only in trace amounts.6 Titanium patch testing has limited sensitivity and cannot reliably exclude titanium allergy.3,4 In cases of suspected hypersensitivity despite negative results, additional tests, such as in vitro lymphocyte transformation assays, may be warranted.1,4

Evidence regarding treatment of CIED allergy is limited, primarily consisting of case reports and retrospective case series. Guidelines are currently insufficient. There is no convincing evidence supporting conservative treatment of CIED hypersensitivity. Corticosteroids may temporarily reduce symptoms, but recurrence is common upon discontinuation, and long-term treatment is generally discouraged. Given the risk of misinterpreting infection as hypersensitivity, the recommended approach is complete device and lead removal.1,7

For reimplantation, the allergenic material should be replaced or isolated from the patient’s tissues. Two similarly effective strategies have been described: wrapping the generator with a biocompatible polymer sheet such as polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) or coating a titanium generator with a thin layer of gold.1,3 The first strategy, although more accessible, carries a higher infection risk.8 In contrast, gold has a well-established biocompatibility and high resistance to bacterial colonization.3 Gold coating is limited by longer manufacturing time, during which alternative arrhythmia protection is necessary. Neither strategy compromises defibrillation function, although PTFE may transiently increase high-voltage circuit impedance, typically resolving within weeks.3,7

Although uncommon, CIED hypersensitivity should be considered in patients presenting with recurrent peri-device inflammation in the absence of systemic infection. Timely recognition and tailored management are essential. Gold-coated devices constitute a safe and effective therapeutic option.
