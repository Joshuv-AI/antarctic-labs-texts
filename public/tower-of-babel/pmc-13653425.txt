Resistance to disinfection emerges in Salmonella Typhimurium following cyclic exposure to 3-(diethylamino)propylamine
Authors: Alberto Fau, Jorge Andaluz-Arbe, Francisco de Borja Sánchez-Melendo, Diego Gómez-Lozano, Rafael Pagán, Diego García-Gonzalo, Daniel Berdejo
Published: 2026

Introduction

The European Union One Health 2024 Zoonoses report recorded > 1000 foodborne outbreaks related to Salmonella spp., with salmonellosis being the second most frequently reported food-borne gastrointestinal disease (EFSA and ECDC, 2025). Therefore, the control of Salmonella spp. is a major priority, especially in eggs and egg products, mixed foods, and bakery products.

To control pathogenic bacteria such as Salmonella in the food industry, cleaning and disinfection (C&D) procedures are applied to reduce or eliminate these microorganisms in food processing environments (Maillard et al., 2013). Cationic disinfectants, such as quaternary ammonium compounds, are widely used due to their lower reactivity, surface compatibility, and reduced toxicity (Maillard and Pascoe, 2024). However, quaternary ammonium compounds have raised concerns regarding their significant negative impact on the environment due to bioaccumulation, persistence in soil or water, and ecotoxicity (Ng et al., 2025). As a result, alternative cationic disinfectants, such as amine-based compounds, are becoming increasingly relevant. 3-(diethylamino)propylamine (DEAPA) is an aliphatic diamine containing a tertiary amino group and is incorporated into some industrial disinfectant formulations, although compound-specific information on its antimicrobial use remains limited in the scientific literature. Amine-based disinfectants are characterised by broad-spectrum activity, efficacy in the presence of organic matter, and surface-active properties (Karsa, 2007). DEAPA has also been shown to act as a precursor of carcinogenic N-nitrosodiethylamine during chlorination and chloramination in water-treatment systems, indicating that its compatibility with oxidising disinfectants should be considered (Liu et al., 2023).

Although biocides are used to manage foodborne pathogens, the selective pressure exerted by these compounds on bacteria may enhance the emergence of antimicrobial resistance (Fraise, 2002). While reports of resistance to in-use concentrations are uncommon, factors such as dilution, the presence of organic matter, or surface interactions may reduce biocide efficacy under certain conditions. Although their occurrence and significance are highly context-dependent, these factors could, in specific scenarios, decrease antimicrobial pressure sufficiently to favour the survival and selection of variants with reduced susceptibility (Maillard and Pascoe, 2024).

To study the emergence and underlying mechanisms of antimicrobial resistance in vitro, adaptive laboratory evolution (ALE) assays can be used. These assays consist of exposing a microorganism to a stress agent, such as a biocide, under controlled conditions to select stable mutations that confer resistance. This exposure can be carried out following different protocols, most commonly involving increasing concentrations, constant subinhibitory concentrations, or repeated short-term lethal treatments.

The development of biocide resistance is often non-specific and may involve different mechanisms acting either individually or in combination, such as efflux pump alterations, variations in porin permeability, metabolic changes, or cell surface modifications (Maillard and Pascoe, 2024). ALE protocols allow the emergence of resistant variants (RVs) through distinct routes, thereby providing a better understanding of bacterial survival mechanisms in response to antimicrobials, while also helping identify resistance phenotypes that could reduce the effectiveness of treatments applied in the industry.

In this regard, mechanisms conferring direct tolerance/resistance to a specific stress can also result in broader cross-tolerance or cross-resistance, allowing bacteria to withstand other stresses unrelated to the initial exposure. In line with this, Paul et al. (2019) reported that some biocides and antibiotics exert similar effects on bacterial cells, potentially promoting resistance to both types of compounds. Cross-resistance to antibiotics has also been reported for other antimicrobials, such as essential oils (Berdejo et al., 2020) or food preservatives (Potenski et al., 2003).

Nonetheless, throughout the food chain, bacteria are not only exposed to chemical stresses but also to physical stresses (Giacometti et al., 2021). Heat treatments are the most widely used food preservation strategy, and several studies have demonstrated that pathogenic bacteria with acquired resistance to antimicrobials can also show thermotolerance (Berdejo et al., 2019; Campillo et al., 2025). This suggests that the mechanisms selected under chemical stresses may, in some cases, confer cross-tolerance to physical stresses.

To the best of our knowledge, although some mutations related to biocide resistance have been linked to increased resistance to other antimicrobials, such as antibiotics, the relationship between these mutations and the efficacy of C&D and food preservation methods remains unclear.

Thus, the aims of our study were: a) to evaluate the emergence of RVs of Salmonella enterica Typhimurium LT2 following exposure to DEAPA using three different ALE protocols tested within a single study; b) to assess the presence and magnitude of cross-tolerance to heat treatments and cross-resistance to antibiotics; and c) to identify the genetic determinants associated with the observed resistance and tolerance phenotypes in the obtained RVs.

Materials and methods

Microorganisms, growth conditions, and antimicrobial compounds

Salmonella enterica subsp. enterica serovar Typhimurium LT2 (SeT) was provided by the Spanish Type Culture Collection (CECT 722). SeT and the evolved strains obtained in this study were maintained at −80 °C in cryovials containing 20% (v/v) glycerol, and plates of tryptone soya agar (Oxoid, Basingstoke, England) with 0.6% yeast extract (Oxoid; TSAYE) were prepared weekly. For the preparation of fresh working cultures, overnight precultures were prepared by inoculating tubes containing 5 mL of fresh tryptone soya broth (Oxoid) supplemented with 0.6% yeast extract (TSBYE) with a single colony, followed by aerobic incubation under agitation at 160 rpm in an orbital shaker (Orb-Pro, LBX Instruments, Spain) at 37 °C for 12 h (Incubig, Selecta, Barcelona, Spain). Subsequently, working bacterial cultures were prepared in flasks containing 10 mL of TSBYE, inoculated to an initial concentration of approximately 106 colony-forming units per mL (CFU/mL) from the preculture and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h at 160 rpm until the stationary growth phase (approximately 3 × 109 CFU/mL) was reached. The bacterial concentration was verified in each experiment by diluting the culture in phosphate-buffered saline (Sigma-Aldrich, Steinheim, Germany; PBS) and spreading on TSAYE plates.

Fresh working cultures of the RVs obtained in this study were prepared following the same procedure described above for the parental strain. It should be noted that each RV was recovered as an individual colony on DEAPA-free TSAYE and subsequently cultured in the absence of DEAPA, with the aim of avoiding transient physiological responses associated with previous cultivation.

3-(diethylamino)propylamine (≥ 99%, CAS: 104–78–9; Sigma-Aldrich; DEAPA) was diluted daily in sterile distilled water to the working concentration. Antibiotics used for the determination of cross-resistance were ampicillin anhydrous (AMP), ciprofloxacin (≥ 98%; CIP), and gentamicin sulfate (GEN) (Sigma-Aldrich). For the preparation of stocks, 0.1 M citrate-phosphate buffer adjusted to pH 8.0, prepared from citric acid monohydrate and disodium hydrogen phosphate (PanReac-AppliChem, Darmstadt, Germany), was used for the AMP stock at 1280 mg/L, 0.1 N hydrochloric acid (Labkem, Barcelona, Spain) for the CIP stock at 40 mg/L, and distilled water for the GEN stock at 64 mg/L. All stocks were stored at −20 °C.

Determination of minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC)

In order to determine the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC), the broth microdilution method was used in TSBYE, following the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute guidelines (CLSI, 2015), but using a narrower concentration range. Starting from an initial concentration of 3200 mg/L of DEAPA, each subsequent well contained a DEAPA concentration 10% lower than the previous one. SeT was inoculated at approximately 3 × 105 CFU/mL and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h at 160 rpm. The MIC was determined using an absorbance microplate reader (Multiskan EX, Thermo Labsystems, China). It was defined as the concentration of DEAPA that reduced the optical density at 620 nm (OD620) by at least 90% compared to the positive control grown in the absence of DEAPA.

To determine increased resistance of evolved strains to clinically relevant antibiotics, MIC assays were performed following CLSI standards, using serial two-fold dilutions.

Lethal biocide treatments

For lethal biocide treatments, 1 mL of stationary phase culture (approximately 3 × 109 CFU/mL) was harvested by centrifugation (6000 × g, 5 min) in a microcentrifuge (Eppendorf MiniSpin Plus, Hamburg, Germany) and resuspended in the same volume of sterile distilled water to wash the cells. After a second centrifugation, the treatment was started by resuspending the pellet in 1 mL of DEAPA at 13,000 mg/L previously brought to 25 °C. The treatment conditions were selected on the basis of preliminary experiments, which showed that exposure to 13,000 mg/L DEAPA for 15 min at 25 °C produced approximately 5 log₁₀ cycles of inactivation while still allowing the recovery of surviving cells. DEAPA was prepared in sterile distilled water to avoid potential interactions with components of the culture medium that could modify its antimicrobial activity.

The treatment was carried out at 25 °C (Memmert, Schwabach, Germany) for 15 min. After treatment, a 1:10 dilution was prepared in 2% buffered peptone water (Oxoid) to neutralize the biocide. Then, dilutions were made in PBS and plated on TSAYE. After 24 h of incubation at 37 °C, microbial counts were performed using an automatic plate counter (Protos, Analytical Measuring Systems, Cambridge, United Kingdom) via image analysis. Inactivation was calculated as log10 (N0/Nt), where N0 represents the viable count immediately before treatment and Nt the viable count after treatment with DEAPA. Control experiments showed that exposure to distilled water alone for 15 min at 25 °C did not significantly affect bacterial viability.

Adaptive laboratory evolution (ALE)

ALE assays were conducted using three protocols: increasing concentrations (ALE-Inc), constant subinhibitory concentrations (ALE-Con), and repeated short-term lethal treatments (ALE-Let).

Each ALE protocol was performed in five parallel lineages to study different evolution patterns, as the pathways leading to the emergence of RVs may be highly heterogeneous. Additionally, after each exposure in the ALE-Inc and ALE-Let protocols, and after every five exposures in the ALE-Con protocol, samples of all lineages were preserved in cryovials containing 20% (v/v) glycerol at −80 °C.

Furthermore, five parallel lineages were maintained in the absence of the biocide for 30 days as a control for adaptation to the growth medium.

Increasing concentrations (ALE-Inc)

The SeT population was exposed during its growth phase to increasing concentrations of DEAPA, starting at 191 mg/L (0.085 × MIC). The increasing concentrations methodology was based on Campillo et al. (2025) and the classical approach described by Lopatkin et al. (2021). Briefly, an overnight subculture, prepared as previously described, was diluted 1:1000 in a flask containing 50 mL of fresh TSBYE and then incubated at 37 °C for 3.5 h at 160 rpm to obtain an exponential growth phase culture. From that culture, a test tube with 5 mL of TSBYE with 0.085 × DEAPA MIC (191 mg/L) was inoculated at an initial bacterial concentration of approximately 3 × 106 CFU/mL. After incubation at 37 °C for 24 h at 160 rpm, once the stationary phase was reached, the same procedure was repeated. The resulting suspension was diluted 1:1000 in 5 mL of TSBYE with a DEAPA concentration increased at each exposure (1.85 × daily) and incubated under the same conditions. Exposures were carried out until no visible growth was detected.

Constant subinhibitory concentration (ALE-Con)

The SeT population was exposed to a 30-day evolution assay at a subinhibitory constant concentration (0.7 × MIC) during the growth phase. The ALE-Con protocol is based on the methodology described by Berdejo et al. (2021). The method begins in the same way as the increasing concentration protocol. From the exponential growth phase culture, a test tube containing 5 mL of TSBYE with a subinhibitory concentration of DEAPA (1600 mg/L) was inoculated at an initial bacterial concentration of approximately 3 × 106 CFU/mL. After incubation at 37 °C for 24 h at 160 rpm, once the stationary phase was reached, the bacterial suspension was diluted to approximately 3 × 106 CFU/mL in 5 mL of TSBYE containing the same biocide concentration and incubated under the same conditions.

In order to isolate RVs and evaluate the heterogeneity of the population, five colonies per lineage were selected by plating on TSAYE the highest-concentration MIC dilution showing visible growth.

Lethal treatments (ALE-Let)

The SeT population in stationary phase was exposed to repeated lethal treatments (13,000 mg/L, 25 °C, 15 min) during a 10-exposure evolution assay. The lethal treatment methodology was adapted from Berdejo et al. (2021). Briefly, the population preparation and lethal biocide treatment conditions were carried out as previously described.

Subsequently, the surviving fraction was recovered by transferring a 1:100 dilution from the buffered peptone water in a test tube containing 5 mL of TSBYE, which was then incubated at 37 °C for 24 h at 160 rpm. The same process was repeated using the resulting culture. Daily, from each treatment, appropriate dilutions were made in PBS and plated on TSAYE plates. After 24 h of incubation at 37 °C, microbial counts were performed.

To isolate potential RVs, each evolved lineage was subjected to the same lethal treatment (13,000 mg/L, 25 °C, 15 min). Subsequently, two colonies were selected from the surviving fraction.

Phenotypic characterisation of RVs obtained from ALE

To identify RVs obtained using the different ALE protocols, phenotypic characterisation was performed by assessing direct resistance to DEAPA by MIC determination (see Section 2.2.), tolerance to lethal DEAPA treatments (see Section 2.3.), cross-resistance to clinically relevant antibiotics (see Section 2.2.), and cross-tolerance to heat treatments.

To assess thermotolerance, a heat treatment was performed following the methodology described by Campillo et al. (2025). After preliminary experiments (data not shown), a lethal heat treatment of 30 min at 54 °C resulting in an inactivation of 6.0 ± 0.5 log10 cycles was selected. Briefly, 1 mL of stationary phase culture (approximately 3 × 109 CFU/mL) was harvested by centrifugation (6000 × g, 5 min) and resuspended in the same volume of the treatment medium, citrate-phosphate buffer adjusted to pH 7.0 (PanReac-AppliChem). Subsequently, 60 μL of the resuspended cell suspension were transferred to sterile PCR tubes and subjected to heat treatment in a thermocycler (T100 Thermal Cycler, Bio-Rad, Spain). After 30 min, the samples were rapidly cooled to 4 °C to stop the treatment. Treated samples were diluted in PBS, spread-plated on TSAYE, and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Finally, microbial counts were performed.

Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) and identification of mutations

Total genomic DNA from SeT and the selected RVs was extracted using a DNeasy Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). Whole-genome sequencing was performed by GENEWIZ (Germany) on an Illumina NovaSeq platform using paired-end 2 × 150-bp reads. The sequencing project generated approximately 60 million reads, with mean Phred quality scores ranging from 34.01 to 39.04 and 83.70–95.36% of bases reaching Q30. Following the quality-control and read-cleaning procedures applied by the sequencing provider, clean reads were aligned to the Salmonella enterica subsp. enterica serovar Typhimurium LT2 reference genome (NCBI accession NC_003197.2) using BWA. The resulting sequencing depth ranged from approximately 150- to 350-fold. Single nucleotide variants (SNVs) and small insertions and deletions were identified using VarScan and BCFtools and annotated using SnpEff. Variants in the RVs were determined relative to the sequenced parental strain, thereby excluding differences already present between SeT and the LT2 reference genome.

Because the sequenced samples corresponded to individual isolates of a haploid bacterium, variants supported by at least 80% of the reads covering a genomic position were considered high-confidence consensus mutations and were retained for the primary comparative analysis. Calls below this variant allele frequency threshold were not used to establish genotype–phenotype associations, since lower-frequency calls may represent within-isolate heterogeneity or technical artefacts related to base calling, strand bias, repetitive or homologous regions, and local read misalignment (Bush, 2021). Nevertheless, all candidate variants below the 80% threshold are reported in Supplementary Table S3. Coverage and read-alignment patterns were also evaluated to identify large deletions and other structural rearrangements. No large-scale structural changes were detected, and only SNVs and small insertions or deletions were observed. Mutated genes were further analysed by assigning their functional orthologs according to the BRITE hierarchy, and the results were visualised in a Voronoi diagram.

All variants detected in the RVs, including low-impact and < 80% allele-frequency calls, are reported in Table S3. Parental-reference differences and domestication-control variants are provided in Tables S4 and S5, respectively.

The resulting genome sequences were deposited in the Sequence Read Archive of NCBI (BioProject ID: PRJNA1365184). The accession numbers of the samples are listed below: SeT (SAMN53274101), SeT_DC4.4 (SAMN53274102), SeT_DC5.4 (SAMN53274103); SeT_DL3.1 (SAMN53274104); SeT_DL3.2 (SAMN53274105); SeT_DL4.2 (SAMN53274107); SeT_DL5.1 (SAMN53274108); SeT_DL5.2 (SAMN53274109).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis and graph construction were performed using GraphPad Prism® software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). MIC values correspond to the mode obtained from at least five independent assays. All other results, except those derived from the ALE protocols, were obtained from at least three independent biological experiments performed with different bacterial cultures on separate working days. The ALE protocols were conducted using five independent evolutionary lineages.

Lethal treatment data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation of the replicates and expressed as log10 (N0/Nt). Significant differences (p ≤ 0.05) were assessed using one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s multiple-comparisons test.

Results

The study was designed to compare the emergence of DEAPA-RVs under three ALE protocols: increasing concentrations (ALE-Inc; lineages identified as SeT_DI), constant subinhibitory concentrations (ALE-Con; lineages identified as SeT_DC), and lethal treatments (ALE-Let; lineages identified as SeT_DL). The evolved populations and selected isolates were subsequently characterised phenotypically and genetically to determine changes in resistance and tolerance and to identify the genetic determinants potentially associated with the observed phenotypes.

The ALE-Inc protocol did not enable the isolation of RVs to DEAPA

Five parallel lineages (SeT_DI1 to SeT_DI5) were exposed to increasing concentrations of DEAPA. Starting from 191 mg/L, the concentration was increased until reaching the MIC value of 2240 mg/L at the fifth exposure. All evolved lineages grew during the first four exposures. However, no growth was observed once the SeT MIC was reached (Fig. S1).

The ALE-Con protocol enabled the isolation of RVs with slightly increased resistance to DEAPA

To perform the constant subinhibitory concentration protocol, five parallel SeT lineages (SeT_DC1 to SeT_DC5) were exposed to 1600 mg/L (0.7 × MIC) for 30 consecutive days (≈ 300 generations).

Four of the five ALE-Con lineages exhibited a 15% increase in MIC compared with SeT, reaching 2560 mg/L of DEAPA, whereas lineage SeT_DC3 did not display any increase (Table 1). To examine the heterogeneity within the evolved populations, five colonies were isolated from each lineage that had shown an increased MIC (Table 1). Each isolate was labelled according to its lineage of origin (e.g., SeT_DC1.1 and SeT_DC1.2 denote isolates from lineage SeT_DC1).

Despite the initial MIC increase observed at the population level, this phenotype was not consistently maintained in the individual isolates. Only a subset of isolates from lineages SeT_DC4 and SeT_DC5 showed increased resistance. SeT_DC4.2, SeT_DC4.4, SeT_DC5.2, and SeT_DC5.5 exhibited a 15% MIC increase, while SeT_DC5.4 showed a 30% increase, even exceeding the MIC of the lineage population.

SeT_DC4.4 and SeT_DC5.4 were selected for further analysis. However, when exposed to lethal DEAPA treatment, no increase in tolerance (p > 0.05) was observed (Fig. S2).

The ALE-Let protocol enabled the isolation of highly DEAPA-tolerant RVs

Consecutive lethal treatments (13,000 mg/L, 15 min, 25 °C) were applied to five parallel lineages (SeT_DL1 to SeT_DL5). All lineages showed a similar pattern throughout the ALE-Let protocol: after the first exposure, the inactivation obtained in the second treatment was lower, and this trend continued progressively over the following days. From day 6 onwards, all lineages displayed inactivation levels below 1 log10 cycle, and these values remained stable until the end of the evolution assay (Fig. 1).

Regarding direct resistance to DEAPA, MIC determination indicated that only isolates SeT_DL5.1 and SeT_DL5.2 showed an increase from 2240 to 2880 mg/L (Table S1).

Two RVs obtained from ALE-Let exhibited cross-resistance to gentamicin

MICs for three clinically relevant antibiotics were determined according to CLSI standards for seven RVs previously selected: two from ALE-Con (SeT_DC4.4 and SeT_DC5.4) and five from ALE-Let (SeT_DL3.1, SeT_DL3.2, SeT_DL4.2, SeT_DL5.1, and SeT_DL5.2).

For SeT, the MICs were 1 mg/L for ampicillin, 0.016 mg/L for ciprofloxacin, and 2 mg/L for gentamicin. None of the RVs showed increased MIC values for ampicillin or ciprofloxacin. However, for gentamicin, two RVs, SeT_DL3.1 and SeT_DL3.2, showed MIC values two-fold higher than that of SeT, while the remaining isolates displayed MICs similar to those of SeT (Table S2).

RVs obtained by ALE-Let exhibited enhanced cross-tolerance to heat treatments

Heat treatments were performed on selected RVs to assess cross-thermotolerance (Fig. 4). The isolates from ALE-Con showed inactivation levels similar to those of SeT (p > 0.05). Four isolates from ALE-Let displayed reduced inactivation compared with SeT (p ≤ 0.05), while SeT_DL4.2 showed inactivation levels comparable to SeT. The isolate with the lowest reduction was SeT_DL3.2, showing an inactivation of around 3 log10 cycles, whereas SeT was reduced by around 6 log10 cycles.

Genetic determinants in DEAPA-RVs identified by whole-genome sequencing

WGS was performed on selected RVs to identify genetic determinants potentially associated with increased resistance (Table 2). For this purpose, low-impact mutations (Table S3), genetic variations between SeT and the reference genome of Salmonella enterica subsp. enterica serovar Typhimurium str. LT2 (NCBI accession: NC_003197.2) (Table S4) and genetic variations identified in the domestication control (Table S5) were excluded from further analysis. After this filtering, most of the remaining mutations were located in open reading frames (ORFs), whereas only three were found in intergenic regions.

The most frequently affected genes were those involved in metabolism, specifically genes involved in the metabolism of cofactors and vitamins (folA), carbohydrates (adhE), nucleotides and nucleotide sugars (cyaA and rfbJ, respectively), and energy (atpF). Genetic variations were also observed in genes that form part of two-component cell signalling systems, such as cpxR, which is part of the CpxRA stress response system, or crp, which is part of the cAMP transcriptional regulation system. Concerning transport-related genes, mutations were identified in corA, involved in magnesium transport, and STM0884, responsible for carbohydrate transport. Regarding cell motility, two mutations were detected: cheZ, which is related to chemotaxis, and fliF, related to the biosynthesis of the flagellar M ring.

Discussion

The three different adaptive laboratory evolution (ALE) protocols tested in this study to evaluate the emergence of S. enterica Typhimurium RVs after exposure to 3-(diethylamino)propylamine (DEAPA) showed notable differences: the increasing-concentrations protocol (ALE-Inc) did not allow the isolation of RVs; the constant subinhibitory concentration protocol (ALE-Con) enabled the isolation of RVs with a slight increase in MIC; and isolates obtained from the lethal-treatment protocol (ALE-Let) showed increased tolerance to biocides and heat, and even gentamicin resistance. While the ALE-Inc and ALE-Con protocols represent exposure to residual or sublethal biocide concentrations, the ALE-Let protocol more closely reflects, among the three approaches tested, the short-term exposure to lethal biocide concentrations occurring during C&D procedures. To the best of our knowledge, no previous studies have investigated bacterial adaptation to DEAPA. Therefore, these findings provide initial evidence that repeated exposure can select variants with altered susceptibility in S. Typhimurium under laboratory conditions.

Regarding the ALE-Inc protocol, other authors have succeeded in isolating RVs of S. Typhimurium (Wu-Chen et al., 2023b), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (Mc Cay et al., 2010), or Escherichia coli (Jia et al., 2022) through exposure to increasing concentrations of the cationic biocide benzalkonium chloride. However, in this study, the ALE-Inc protocol did not allow the isolation of RVs; this may be attributable to species- and substance-dependent mutation occurrence (Schmidt et al., 2022) or even to exposure conditions such as incubation time or the concentration increase ratio.

In relation to the ALE-Con protocol, small increases in resistance were observed. Baquero (2001) indicates that low-level resistance is expected at low concentration exposures. This effect has been reported after exposure to other cationic biocides, such as benzalkonium chloride, in E. coli (Li et al., 2025; Pereira et al., 2021; Schmidt et al., 2024). In the present study, after 30 days of exposure to DEAPA, only a slight increase in the MIC of the evolved lineages, approximately 15%, was observed. However, when individual colonies were isolated, increased resistance was only observed in clones from two lineages (SeT_DC4 and SeT_DC5) (Table 1). Lee et al. (2010) point out that population dynamics affect antimicrobial resistance, which means that isolated individuals may be less resistant than the overall population. Although the increase in MIC is modest compared to the high concentrations (1000–10,000 mg/L) widely applied in the food industry (Maillard and Pascoe, 2024), the emergence of resistance could provide an evolutionary advantage by increasing bacterial survival during routine sanitation.

Despite exhibiting similar behaviour across all lineages during the ALE-Let protocol, the subsequent characterisation of these evolved lineages showed that not all of them retained significantly increased resistance compared to SeT (Fig. 2). This phenomenon is known as bacterial persistence, which refers to the transient and non-hereditary capacity of a subpopulation of susceptible bacteria to survive exposure to lethal doses (Blázquez et al., 2012). After two isolates were obtained from each lineage, it was observed that isolates from the same lineage showed similar tolerance, except for isolates SeT_DL4.1 and SeT_DL4.2 (Fig. 3). The observed difference in resistance between isolates from the same lineage could be attributed to the genetic heterogeneity of the evolved population (Berdejo et al., 2021). Nevertheless, several RVs showed a significant increase (p ≤ 0.05) in tolerance to lethal biocide treatments, which is consistent with the findings reported by Nordholt et al. (2021), who observed an increase in the surviving fraction of around 3 log10 cycles in RVs compared to SeT after 20 min of exposure to benzalkonium chloride.

Furthermore, RVs from the ALE-Let protocol not only showed increased tolerance to lethal biocide treatments, but some of them also exhibited cross-tolerance to heat treatments and cross-resistance to gentamicin. Four RVs (SeT_DL3.1, SeT_DL3.2, SeT_DL5.1, and SeT_DL5.2) exhibited strong thermotolerance, approximately 1000-fold higher survival than SeT after heat treatment (Fig. 4). However, SeT_DL4.2, despite showing greater tolerance to lethal biocide treatments (Fig. 3), did not differ significantly from SeT when exposed to heat. These results suggest that cross-tolerance to heat may arise through a wide variety of mechanisms (Maillard and Pascoe, 2024), but this phenomenon ultimately depends on the selection of specific underlying mutations. These results are consistent with previous studies that have reported significant increases in heat tolerance in antimicrobial RVs, such as thermotolerant ciprofloxacin-resistant S. Typhimurium LT2 (Campillo et al., 2025) and essential oil-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (Berdejo et al., 2019).

Regarding antibiotic cross-resistance, while several studies have reported significant increases in antibiotic resistance in RVs exposed to cationic biocides (Liu et al., 2025; Nordholt et al., 2021), our findings were more limited. Only the SeT_DL3 isolates developed a two-fold increase in gentamicin MIC. These results are consistent with those of Wu-Chen et al. (2023a), who mainly reported a 2- or 4-fold gentamicin MIC increase in clinical S. Typhimurium isolates. Although the MIC increase was modest, according to the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (2025) clinical breakpoints it can be classified as resistant.

Through phenotypic characterisation, SeT_DL3 isolates showed the most pronounced overall phenotype, with approximately 10,000-fold greater survival following lethal DEAPA treatment and 1000-fold greater survival following heat treatment, together with a two-fold increase in gentamicin MIC, from 2 to 4 mg/L, which resulted in their classification as resistant according to the EUCAST clinical breakpoint. Although these results are limited to laboratory systems, the markedly increased survival of these RVs following DEAPA and heat treatments suggests enhanced tolerance that could make their inactivation and subsequent elimination more difficult during food-processing operations.

To study the genetic variations involved in increased tolerance and resistance, WGS was performed. WGS showed that strains isolated from the different ALE protocols displayed very different patterns, with multiple genetic variations in the ALE-Con RVs and individual mutations in the ALE-Let RVs (Table 2). However, several functional categories and pathways were affected across the different evolved lineages.

In RVs isolated after the ALE-Con protocol (SeT_DC4.4 and SeT_DC5.4), a variety of mutations were identified in each isolate. Although most of these mutations have been linked to antimicrobial resistance, usually antibiotic resistance, the diversity of the functions affected (metabolism, cell motility, transporters, and signal transduction) does not allow the identification of a single mutation as responsible for the increase in resistance. Nevertheless, corA was the only mutation shared by both RVs. This gene encodes a protein involved in magnesium uptake and homeostasis, which is essential for the metabolism and virulence of the bacterium (Papp-Wallace and Maguire, 2008).

No studies have yet linked corA to increased antimicrobial resistance. However, since it is the only mutation present in both ALE-Con RVs (Table 2), its impact may not have been recognized, considering the importance of magnesium in cellular functions (Pilchova et al., 2017).

In RVs isolated after exposure to the ALE-Let protocol (SeT_DL3.1, SeT_DL3.2, SeT_DL4.2, SeT_DL5.1, and SeT_DL5.2), a single mutation was identified in each RV, suggesting that these mutations may contribute to the observed phenotypes. SeT_DL3 isolates showed a single mutation in the HTH crp-type domain of the crp gene. Loss of the global regulatory protein CRP (cAMP receptor protein) or its allosteric effector, cAMP (cyclic adenosine monophosphate), is involved in the expression of a large number of genes (Zheng et al., 2004) and has been previously linked with novobiocin and erythromycin resistance (Cho and Misra, 2021). Additionally, in our study, high thermotolerance was observed, which differs from the findings reported by Bayles and Uhlich (2006) in Listeria monocytogenes F2365 with a non-functional crp.

Related to CRP mutations, SeT_DL5.1 revealed a premature stop codon in cyaA, specifically in the catalytic region responsible for cAMP synthesis, which is expected to result in loss of protein functionality. The inactivation of cyaA has been linked with increased resistance to kanamycin (Mogre et al., 2017) or low-level resistance to fosfomycin (Herrera-Espejo et al., 2025).

SeT_DL4.2 and SeT_DL5.2 harboured mutations in metabolic genes adhE and atpF, respectively. In relation to the alcohol dehydrogenase gene adhE, in Streptococcus mutans, the adh operon plays a role in managing the stress response, and mutations in related genes could modify susceptibility to different stresses, such as sensitivity to alkaline pH or increased oxygen dependence (Zuber et al., 2022). Notably, SeT_DL4.2 showed increased tolerance to lethal biocide treatments but did not exhibit thermotolerance. These results are consistent with previous findings, where E. coli mutants lacking adh showed increased sensitivity to heat (Lorowitz and Clark, 1982). Regarding atpF, mutations in the ATP synthase operon are closely related to changes in antimicrobial susceptibility. On the one hand, mutations in the ATP synthase operon, such as those in atpE, have been linked to increased resistance to antibiotics such as bedaquiline or diarylquinoline in Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Ismail et al., 2019; Petrella et al., 2006). On the other hand, inactivation of genes involved in ATP subunit synthesis has been associated with altered gentamicin susceptibility in S. aureus (Vestergaard et al., 2016) and with increased susceptibility of Mycobacterium smegmatis to cell wall-targeting antimicrobials (Saw et al., 2019).

In strains SeT_DC4.4 and SeT_DC5.4, it is suggested, based on the mutations in each gene, that there could be synergistic or compensatory effects between mutated genes, and it is not possible to establish the main determinant responsible for the increase in MIC. In addition, the increase in resistance shown was not particularly significant, so the mutations in these genes do not appear to lead to the development of high resistance. On the other hand, fewer mutations were identified in the isolates obtained through the ALE-Let protocol, affecting crp (SeT_DL3.1 and SeT_DL3.2), adhE (SeT_DL4.2), cyaA (SeT_DL5.1), and atpF (SeT_DL5.2). These mutations may be associated with increased DEAPA tolerance, frequently accompanied by cross-tolerance to heat and, in SeT_DL3 isolates, a two-fold increase in gentamicin MIC.

Overall, the limited co-occurrence of mutations among variants, together with the diversity of affected cellular functions, suggests that similar resistance or tolerance phenotypes may be associated with different pathways or mechanisms. Taken together, these observations highlight the complexity underlying the development of resistance and cross-tolerance phenotypes and emphasise the need to investigate these responses using complementary methodologies that incorporate more representative environmental conditions, as well as to clarify the contribution of specific mutations to the phenotypes observed in the RVs.

The present study was designed as a controlled mechanistic approach using pure DEAPA, planktonic cultures, and a well-characterised laboratory strain, which facilitated the evaluation of DEAPA-specific selection and the associated genetic changes. Nevertheless, the behaviour of the evolved variants may differ under industrial conditions, where product formulation and environmental factors can influence antimicrobial efficacy. Although no detectable growth impairment was observed and the phenotypes were maintained during laboratory passage, their long-term stability and competitive performance under more representative conditions remain to be determined. Likewise, the limited antibiotic panel does not allow broad conclusions regarding cross-resistance. Further studies using commercial formulations, diverse strains, and more applied experimental models would build on these findings and clarify how the observed phenotypes behave under a broader range of food-processing conditions.

Conclusions

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first report showing that S. Typhimurium LT2 can develop resistance and tolerance to the alkylamine DEAPA. The three different adaptive laboratory evolution (ALE) protocols applied in this study allowed us to assess how resistance emergence and its underlying mechanisms varied depending on exposure conditions. Notably, the lethal treatment ALE (ALE-Let) protocol, in which the DEAPA concentration and contact time were selected to emulate conditions used during disinfection treatment, favoured the emergence of RVs with significantly increased tolerance to DEAPA, thermotolerance, and even gentamicin resistance, which could ultimately affect the effectiveness of cleaning and disinfection protocols in the food industry. Whole-genome sequencing revealed that the adaptive genetic changes were highly diverse and nonspecific. Mutations were associated with diverse functions, including global regulation systems, metabolic pathways, transport mechanisms, and cell motility, suggesting that similar resistance phenotypes may be achieved through multiple molecular mechanisms. These findings show that repeated DEAPA exposure can select S. Typhimurium variants with increased tolerance to DEAPA and heat under laboratory conditions, which could make their inactivation more difficult under comparable treatment conditions. Further studies are needed to determine whether variants with similar phenotypes can also emerge in food-processing environments and to evaluate their subsequent growth and competitive performance across diverse industrial settings, which will be essential to inform food safety risk assessment.
