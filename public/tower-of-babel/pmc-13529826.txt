Female vulnerability to microvascular transport dysfunction emerges early across the Alzheimer's disease continuum
Authors: Manxi Xu, Yian Gao, Peiling Ou, Haoyu Yang, Jia Wang, Wulong Liu, Ni Shu, Yu Sun, Liping Fu, Lidia Glodzik, Tracy A Butler, Gloria C Chaing, Yi Li, Yi Wang, Liangdong Zhou, Guolin Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONWe tested whether quantitative transport mapping (QTM)‐derived perfusion velocity would detect earlier and more sex‐specific microvascular alterations than cerebral blood flow (CBF) across the Alzheimer's disease (AD) continuum.METHODSIn 182 participants (cognitively normal = 53, subjective cognitive decline [SCD] = 48, mild cognitive impairment [MCI] = 31, AD = 50), seven‐delay arterial spin labeling was used to derive CBF and QTM velocity. Age/sex‐adjusted models assessed diagnostic and sex effects, associations with plasma biomarkers and Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE), and mediation of the hippocampal volume–cognition relationship.RESULTSQTM velocity was reduced already in SCD, including the hippocampus, whereas CBF declined mainly in MCI/AD. Sex × Diagnosis interactions were significant for QTM in gray and white matter, but not for CBF. QTM, especially in females, was inversely associated with plasma NfL/GFAP, related to MMSE, and parietal QTM partially mediated the hippocampal volume–MMSE association.DISCUSSIONQTM is a non‐invasive biomarker more sensitive than CBF to early, female‐biased microvascular transport dysfunction in AD.

BACKGROUND

Alzheimer's disease (AD), the leading cause of dementia, is biologically defined by abnormal amyloid beta (A, Aβ) and tau (T) pathology together with downstream neurodegeneration (N). 1 , 2 Beyond the ATN framework, converging evidence implicates cerebrovascular dysfunction, 3 neuroinflammation, 4 and impaired brain clearance pathways 5 , 6 as integral contributors to disease initiation and progression. A persistent and clinically important observation is the marked sex disparity in AD: Women account for roughly two‐thirds of prevalent AD cases 7 and, in many studies, show faster cognitive decline and a higher risk of progression after symptom onset. 8 While sex differences in amyloid and tau burden have been reported, 9 the mechanisms underlying women's heightened vulnerability remain incompletely understood. 10 One plausible pathway is sex‐specific susceptibility of the cerebral microvasculature, because efficient removal of Aβ depends on intact blood–brain barrier (BBB) function 11 , 12 and perivascular/glymphatic transport. 13 , 14 , 15 Early microvascular injury could therefore amplify amyloid accumulation and downstream neurodegenerative cascades, yet may be difficult to detect using standard perfusion metrics that are partially stabilized by cerebral autoregulation. 16

Sex differences in cerebrovascular imaging markers have been reported across multiple modalities in AD. White matter hyperintensities (WMHs), a common magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) signature of cerebral small‐vessel disease (CSVD), are often greater in women than men, particularly in older age, and WMH burden has been linked to faster cognitive decline in women. 17 Arterial spin labeling (ASL) MRI‐based cerebral blood flow (CBF), derived from traditional kinetic modeling, 18 is often higher in women than men during cognitively normal aging. 19 However, this apparent perfusion “advantage” may diminish with advancing vascular risk on clinical progression, complicating interpretation because CBF is strongly influenced by sex‐dependent baseline physiology and autoregulatory compensation. Cerebrovascular reactivity (CVR), 20 which reflects the vasodilatory reserve of resistance vessels, is increasingly recognized as a sensitive marker of microvascular health and endothelial function. 21 Several studies suggest sex‐specific CVR alterations across aging and AD‐spectrum populations, even when resting CBF differences are modest. 22 Together, these findings support a model in which women may experience distinct trajectories of microvascular injury and compensation. However, a non‐invasive imaging marker sensitive to the earliest microvascular dysfunction and transport‐related vascular impairment remains lacking.

Our group recently developed Quantitative Transport Mapping (QTM), 23 a biophysics‐based model for estimating voxel‐wise perfusion velocity from four‐dimensional (4D) time‐resolved multi‐delay ASL data. 24 QTM models the spatiotemporal evolution of the ASL tracer by fitting tissue concentration dynamics to the mass transport equation, enabling reconstruction of a three‐dimensional (3D) perfusion velocity field that complements conventional perfusion magnitude measures such as CBF. The approach has been validated in microvasculature‐level numerical simulations 23 and in experimental flow phantoms using porcine liver. 25 By leveraging the full spatiotemporal information in multi‐delay ASL and eliminating the need for an explicit arterial input function (AIF), QTM velocity provides a transport‐centric marker that is less dependent on upstream bolus specification and potentially more sensitive to microvascular impairment. In prior applications, QTM velocity showed sensitivity to perfusion velocity alterations across multiple diseases, including discrimination of breast cancer malignancy, 26 staging of progressive liver disease, 27 and identification of early AD‐spectrum impairment by separating mild cognitive impairment (MCI) individuals from cognitively normal (NC) ones. 28

Building upon these methodological validations and initial AD applications including higher sensitivity of QTM velocity in detecting perfusion alterations at early AD stages 28 and early change in perfusion velocity in middle cerebral artery‐supplied temporal lobe being the driven region of changes in other brain regions, 24 in this study, we analyzed a clinically defined AD‐spectrum cohort spanning four stages (n = 182; NC, subjective cognitive decline [SCD], MCI, and AD) using multi‐delay ASL with seven post‐labeling delays to derive voxel‐wise perfusion velocity by QTM. We further asked whether QTM‐derived perfusion velocity could reveal an early neurovascular signature of female vulnerability across the clinically defined AD continuum and whether such a signal is biologically anchored to injury and inflammatory markers and clinically linked to cognitive decline. We evaluated stage‐wise and sex‐by‐diagnosis effects in QTM velocity, examined associations with plasma biomarkers and cognition, and tested whether QTM velocity might be involved in the pathway linking hippocampal atrophy to cognitive performance. Together, these analyses were designed to determine whether microvascular transport dysfunction represented an early, sex‐dependent dimension of AD vulnerability beyond conventional CBF.

METHODS

Study design and participants

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of China‐Japan Friendship Hospital and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legally authorized representatives. Participants were recruited from the Department of Neurology at China‐Japan Friendship Hospital. The finalized analytic sample comprised 182 eligible participants after removing those subjects who failed to complete the neuropsychological tests or had severe MRI artifacts/motion issues, classified into four clinical groups: NC (n = 53), SCD (n = 48), MCI (n = 31), and AD (n = 50). The NC group was strictly defined by an unremarkable clinical interview, a Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE) score ≥ 27, and a Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) global score of 0. When available, diagnostic classifications were corroborated by Aβ positron emission tomography (PET) imaging (18F‐florbetaben) or plasma biomarker analyses to confirm the amyloid positivity for SCD, MCI, and AD. Participants with a primary clinical diagnosis of vascular dementia or cognitive impairment considered by the diagnosing clinicians to be primarily attributable to cerebrovascular disease were excluded during clinical screening since we were investigating cerebrovascular risk‐related changes in AD continuum.

Neuropsychological assessments

Global cognition and clinical severity were evaluated using a standardized neuropsychological battery. 29 , 30 The MMSE, a comprehensive 30‐item screening tool encompassing orientation, memory, attention, and language, served as the primary cognitive outcome in the association and mediation analyses. 31 Clinical severity across the diagnostic spectrum was characterized using the CDR global score. 32 Additionally, episodic memory and short‐term learning were assessed utilizing the immediate recall score of the Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test (RAVLT). 33

Plasma biomarker quantification

Fasting blood samples were collected with informed consent and quantified using the ultra‐sensitive Single Molecule Array (Simoa) platform 34 for a subset of 69 participants (NC, n = 8; SCD, n = 24; MCI, n = 13; AD, n = 24). The evaluated biomarker panel included Aβ40, Aβ42, phosphorylated tau 217 (p‐tau217), glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), and neurofilament light chain (NfL). Data for Aβ42 from one participant were excluded due to out‐of‐range signal intensity, yielding a final sample of n = 68 for Aβ42 and its related ratio analyses.

MRI data acquisition

All MRI examinations were performed on a 3.0T scanner (Discovery MR 750, GE Healthcare, USA) equipped with an eight‐channel head coil at China‐Japan Friendship Hospital using a standardized protocol. The core sequences included (1) 3D T1‐weighted (T1w) imaging for region of interest (ROI) segmentations (repetition time [TR]/echo time [TE] = 6.7/2.9 ms, flip angle = 12°, slice thickness = 1.0 mm, no gap, matrix = 256 × 256, field of view [FOV] = 256 × 256 mm2); (2) a multiple post‐labeling delay ASL (mPLD‐ASL) from enhanced ASL 35 sequence for CBF and QTM velocity quantification. This protocol encodes seven different PLDs (800, 1098, 1427, 1797, 2228, 2761, and 3492 ms) into a single acquisition, with effective label durations of 298, 328, 370, 432, 533, 731, and 1308 ms, TR/TE = 6273/11.4 ms, flip angle = 111°, voxel size = 1.72 × 1.72 × 4.5 mm3, matrix = 128 × 128, FOV = 220 × 220 mm2, NEX = 2); and (3) a T2‐fluid attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) sequence primarily utilized for quantifying WMHs representing small‐vessel injury (TR/TE = 8668/166 ms, flip angle = 111°, slice thickness = 0.5 × 0.5 × 5 mm3, inter‐slice gap = 1.0 mm, matrix = 256 × 256, FOV = 240 × 240 mm2).

Brain ROIs parcellation

T1‐weighted images underwent an automated cortical and subcortical segmentation pipeline to generate subject‐specific ROIs using FreeSurfer version 7.1. 36 These ROIs were combined bilaterally and included the global cerebral cortical gray matter (GM), cerebral white matter (WM), hippocampus (Hippo), and four primary cortical lobes (frontal, parietal, temporal, and occipital). In addition to the predefined primary ROIs, exploratory ROI analyses were performed across FreeSurfer‐defined regions to characterize regional patterns of CBF and QTM velocity alterations. These exploratory analyses included canonical AD‐vulnerable regions within the default mode network (DMN), such as the posterior cingulate cortex (PCC) and medial frontal cortex. To extract ROI values, mPLD‐ASL‐derived CBF maps and QTM velocity maps were co‐registered to each participant's T1w in FreeSurfer space. To mitigate potential partial‐volume effects at tissue boundaries, all ROIs were morphologically eroded by 1 mm prior to the extraction of mean regional values. WMHs segmented from T2‐FLAIR images in the FreeSurfer T1w space using a validated deep learning‐based neural network, 37 as a component of vascular risk, will be used to evaluate CBF and QTM velocity and their associations with cognition. Periventricular WMHs (pWMHs) dominate the overall WMH burden (∼90%), our analysis will be performed in pWMH only. All ROI volumetric values were normalized by intracranial volume (ICV) to adjust for the head size.

CBF mapping from mPLD‐ASL

CBF maps were reconstructed on the scanner (GE Discovery MR750) from the mPLD‐ASL acquisitions using arterial transit time (ATT)‐corrected kinetic modeling. 35 ATT correction was applied to reduce bias in CBF estimates arising from prolonged label arrival times, which has been reported to be more accurate than CBF derived from single‐delay ASL. 38 , 39 The CBF maps were then registered to each subject's FreeSurfer T1w space using FLIRT (FSL). 40 To improve alignment, registration was performed within a cortical mask. CBF values were extracted in FreeSurfer T1w space using the corresponding FreeSurfer parcellations.

QTM velocity from mPLD‐ASL

QTM estimates perfusion velocity by modeling the labeled tracer dynamics with a mass conservation (transport) equation: ∂tcr,t=−∇·cr,tur+∇·Dr∇cr,t−λcr,t, where c(r,t) is the tracer concentration at location r and time t, u(r) is the time‐invariant tracer velocity field, D(r) is the apparent diffusion coefficient, and λ is the signal decay rate. For ASL‐labeled endogenous water, we set λ=1/T1b, with T1b=1.65 s the T1 time of blood. For perfusion estimation, the diffusion term is assumed to be negligible because diffusion occurs much more slowly than perfusion‐related transport.

The velocity field u was reconstructed by minimizing u=argminu∑t=1Nt−1∥∂tc+∇·cu+λc∥22+α∥∇u∥1, where α is the regularization parameter that promotes the spatial smoothness of the estimated velocity field (α=0.05, selected using L‐curve analysis). Reconstructions were performed using in‐house MATLAB code with motion‐corrected (realigned) mPLD‐ASL data. Following prior QTM work, 23 u represents tracer velocity in capillaries across voxel surfaces with a typical unit mm/s in image space. The magnitude of u was denoted by ∥u∥ in L2 norm to represent the mean perfusion velocity in the QTM model at the voxel level.

QTM velocity maps were registered to each subject's FreeSurfer T1w space using the same transformation matrix applied to the CBF maps. QTM velocity ROI values were then extracted in the same manner as for CBF.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed in R version 4.5.2 (in RStudio 2025.09.2, Posit Software, PBC). Continuous variables are summarized as median [interquartile range], and categorical variables as count (%). All tests were two‐tailed with a significance threshold of p < 0.05. When multiple regional comparisons were performed, p values were adjusted using the Benjamini‐Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure.

Diagnostic group comparisons

Demographic, clinical, and biomarker characteristics were compared across the four diagnostic groups using Kruskal‐Wallis tests for continuous/ordinal variables and χ2 tests (or Fisher's exact tests) for categorical variables. Significant Kruskal‐Wallis findings were followed by Dunn's post hoc tests with FDR correction.

Diagnostic and sex effects on CBF and QTM velocity

Group differences in ROI‐averaged CBF and QTM velocity were assessed using Kruskal‐Wallis and FDR‐corrected Dunn's tests. Sex differences within the full sample and specific diagnostic stages were evaluated via Wilcoxon rank‐sum tests. To determine whether sex disparities varied with disease progression, age‐adjusted linear interaction models were fitted in the symptomatic cohort (SCD, MCI, and AD). The models included age, sex, ordinal diagnostic stage, and a Sex × Diagnosis interaction term to evaluate the attenuation or exacerbation of sex differences across clinical stages.

Associations of CBF and QTM velocity with plasma biomarkers

Correlations between plasma biomarkers and regional CBF and QTM velocity were evaluated using Spearman correlation analyses, both in the overall cohort and stratified by sex. FDR correction was applied independently for each specific biomarker and imaging modality to prevent cross‐interference. As sensitivity analyses, covariate‐adjusted linear regression models (adjusting for age and sex) were employed to confirm the robustness of significant associations.

Associations of CBF and QTM velocity with global cognition

The relationship between perfusion‐related measurements and global cognition (MMSE) was primarily assessed using Spearman partial correlations controlling for age and sex. These analyses were performed in both the full cohort and the symptomatic cohort, followed by FDR correction. Subsequently, multivariable linear regression models incorporating age, sex, diagnosis, and a Sex × (CBF or QTM velocity) interaction term were fitted to identify sex‐dependent cognitive associations. Sensitivity analyses in the symptomatic cohort further adjusted for ICV normalized hippocampal volume to isolate the specific contributions of CBF/QTM velocity to cognition independent of structural atrophy.

Mediation analysis

To investigate whether regional CBF or QTM velocity mediated the established atrophy–cognition relationship, a mediation analysis was conducted. Variables were z‐score normalized prior to modeling. Two covariate adjusted (age and sex) linear models were constructed: a mediator model (QTM ∼ Age + Sex + Hippo volume) regressing parietal QTM velocity on hippocampal volume and an outcome model (MMSE ∼ Age + Sex + Hippo volume + QTM) regressing MMSE on both parietal QTM velocity and hippocampal volume. The average causal mediation effect (ACME), average direct effect (ADE), total effect, and proportion mediated were estimated utilizing a non‐parametric bootstrap approach with 5000 simulations.

CBF reductions were primarily observed at later disease stages, as shown in Figure 3A–C. Compared with the NC group, significant decreases in CBF were detected in both MCI and AD across GM, Hippo, and WM, whereas no significant differences were observed between NC and SCD or between MCI and AD (all p > 0.05). Specifically, relative to NC, CBF was notably reduced in MCI and AD (all p < 0.01). In contrast, changes in the SCD group were minimal and not statistically significant. Notably, WM CBF showed significant reductions in both MCI and AD compared with NC, while no significant difference was detected between MCI and AD.

QTM revealed sensitive reductions in perfusion velocity at the early stages of the AD spectrum (Figure 3D–F). Overall, QTM velocity declined progressively across the ordered diagnostic groups, with a plateau from MCI to AD. Pairwise comparisons showed significant differences between all group pairs except MCI versus AD. Significant reductions in QTM velocity relative to NC were observed in the Hippo across the SCD, MCI, and AD groups, with marginal differences in GM and WM. Relative to NC (GM 5.87; Hippo 5.86; WM 4.47 mm/s), QTM velocity in SCD was lower in GM (5.48, −6.63%, p = 0.057), Hippo (5.26, −10.20%, p = 0.034), and WM (4.16, −7.01%, p = 0.056). In MCI, reductions were larger across all regions (all p < 0.001). In AD, QTM velocity remained reduced (all p < 0.001). QTM showed more pronounced stage‐wise reductions than CBF across the GM, WM, and Hippo, especially from NC to SCD, potentially due to microvasculature alteration during the AD progression. Diagnostic effects on CBF and QTM velocity extend broadly across multiple cortical and subcortical regions, including DMN brain regions, detailed in Tables S1 and S2.

Sex differences in CBF and QTM velocity

Across the full cohort, females showed higher CBF and higher QTM velocity than males in GM, WM, and Hippo (all p < 0.001; Figure S1). These findings indicate a higher overall perfusion baseline in females for both conventional and transport‐sensitive QTM velocity.

Sex by diagnosis effects on QTM velocity and CBF

We examined sex differences in CBF and QTM velocity stratified by diagnostic stage and observed stage‐dependent patterns for both metrics (Figure 4). For CBF, significant sex differences were primarily evident in the later clinical stages (SCD‐AD in GM, SCD and MCI in WM, and MCI in Hippo). In contrast, significant sex differences in QTM velocity were more prominent in the earlier stages, particularly NC and SCD (e.g., NC and SCD in GM, SCD in WM, and SCD and MCI in Hippo).

Associations of QTM and CBF with plasma biomarkers

To understand the underlying mechanism of QTM velocity and CBF along the AD spectrum, we investigated their associations with common plasma biomarkers including Aβ40, Aβ42, Aβ42/40, p‐tau217, NfL, and GFAP.

We found that, across brain regions, QTM velocity was robustly negatively associated with plasma NfL (GM: r = −0.536, p < 0.001; WM: r = −0.522, p < 0.001; Hippo: r = −0.510, p < 0.001; Figure 5A–C). When stratified by sex, these inverse associations remained significant in females with even higher correlation coefficients than the whole group (GM: r = −0.600; WM: r = −0.622; Hippo: r = −0.605; all p < 0.001). In contrast, the relationships were non‐significant in males (all p > 0.220; Figure 5A–C), indicating different sex‐specific neurovascular interactions.

Mediation analysis

Hippocampal atrophy is a well‐established structural hallmark strongly associated with cognitive decline in the AD continuum. Building on our findings that QTM velocity interacts with disease stage and cognitive performance, we hypothesized that regional QTM velocity might mediate this atrophy–cognition relationship. Initial exploratory models utilizing global cortical QTM velocity as a mediator only reach marginal significance (p = 0.074). Subsequent lobar‐level analysis identified parietal QTM velocity as the specific functional mediator.

In covariate‐adjusted models (age and sex), hippocampal volume was positively associated with parietal QTM velocity (path a: β = 0.283, p < 0.001), and parietal QTM velocity was positively associated with MMSE after accounting for hippocampal volume (path b: β = 0.197, p = 0.007; Figure 7). The direct effect of hippocampal volume on MMSE remained significant after including the mediator (path c′: β = 0.461, p < 0.001), indicating partial rather than full mediation. Bootstrapped mediation analysis (5000 simulations) yielded a significant indirect effect (ACME = 0.056, p = 0.034), along with significant direct (ADE = 0.462, p < 0.001) and total effects (total effect = 0.518, p < 0.001). Approximately 10.2% of the total association between hippocampal volume and MMSE was mediated by parietal QTM velocity (p = 0.034).

DISCUSSION

This study identifies microvascular transport dysfunction as an early and female‐biased neurovascular alteration across the clinically defined AD continuum. The signal was detectable at the SCD stage, before corresponding NC‐SCD differences emerged in CBF, and showed a sex‐dependent trajectory that was evident for QTM but not for bulk perfusion. The coupling of lower QTM velocity with higher NfL and GFAP, together with its association with cognition, supports the interpretation that QTM captures a transport‐related neurovascular process linked to inflammatory and axonal injury pathways. Rather than serving only as a more sensitive perfusion metric, QTM may provide a non‐invasive window into sex‐dependent vulnerability at the interface of cerebrovascular dysfunction, brain aging, and early AD transition.

QTM velocity and CBF in early AD stages

Our results demonstrated significant differences of QTM velocity between SCD and NC, in addition to the difference between MCI and NC consistent with previous work, 24 , 28 using different MRI platforms. Notably, QTM velocity revealed subtle declines in SCD relative to NC, whereas CBF did not. Widespread CBF reductions in AD and MCI compared to NC have been documented, especially in the Hippo. 41 Crucially, at the SCD stage, reported CBF changes relative to NC become highly polarized and inconsistent, even within vulnerable regions like the Hippo and PCC. 42 , 43 , 44 The inconsistent results of CBF change from NC to SCD or NC to MCI might stem, for example, from autoregulation of CBF at early stages, diagnostic group composition, 45 single‐delay ASL data impacted by prolonged ATT, and MRI scanners.

QTM leverages 4D mPLD‐ASL 35 , 38 to estimate perfusion velocity, offering a new perspective of perfusion efficiency quantification beyond CBF, 39 which enables us to evaluate spatiotemporal changes in the dynamic data without AIF. 23 Mechanistically, CBF and QTM velocity reflect a similar but different physiology of perfusion, 23 , 28 , 46 with CBF a quantification of blood reservation that might be impacted by autoregulation and compensatory mechanisms at early disease stages, 47 while QTM velocity for the blood transport velocity, which is sensitive to the change in cerebrovascular structure as well as the efficiency of tracer transport.

These mechanistic differences may help explain the improved sensitivity of QTM velocity in distinguishing SCD from NC. Specifically, early AD may involve autoregulatory/compensatory responses, including vasodilatory changes. From a hemodynamic perspective, an increase in vascular cross‐sectional area can reduce flow velocity even when bulk flow is relatively preserved. Another potential mechanism for the discrepant performance between CBF and QTM velocity is microvascular remodeling 48 , 49 (e.g., increased vessel tortuosity, luminal narrowing, partial occlusion, and altered vascular permeability) at an early stage of AD, whose changes can disrupt the coherent organization of the microvascular network, increase flow heterogeneity and dispersion, and thereby reduce the voxel‐level averaged perfusion velocity even when bulk CBF changes are subtle or inconsistent.

Sex difference of QTM velocity and CBF

We observed higher QTM velocity and CBF in females than in males across brain regions in the full cohort, consistent with prior reports. 28 , 50 These global sex differences likely reflect a combination of systemic, hormonal, 51 , 52 and hematologic factors, 53 including heart rate/cardiac output, blood pressure regulation, and hematocrit, that can influence cerebral perfusion.

When stratifying by diagnostic group, we found that the decline of QTM velocity accelerated (∼four‐fold) in females from SCD to MCI relative to that from NC to SCD, whereas in males the decline was comparable across both transitions. AD‐related symptoms typically emerge in later life, when many women are postmenopausal and circulating estrogen levels have declined substantially. As a result, the magnitude and trajectory of sex differences in cerebrovascular function may shift across aging and disease stages. The menopausal transition is accompanied by loss of estrogen's vasoprotective and anti‐inflammatory actions, which may increase vascular vulnerability, manifesting as greater BBB dysfunction, impaired microvascular regulation, increased vascular stiffness and tortuosity, 54 and heightened neuroinflammatory signaling. These female‐specific series of changes might be partly captured by QTM velocity, manifesting as an accelerated decline in early stages of AD.

Additionally, we applied age‐adjusted regression models with a Sex × Diagnosis interaction to test whether perfusion trajectories differed by sex across diagnostic stages. The interaction was significant for QTM velocity in both GM and WM, but not for CBF, indicating a sex‐specific progression pattern uniquely captured by QTM. Collectively, these findings suggest that females may exhibit greater vulnerability of microvascular transport to neuroinflammation, providing potential insight into their elevated risk for AD.

QTM and CBF with plasma biomarkers

QTM velocity was negatively associated with plasma GFAP, predominantly in females, whereas no comparable associations were observed for CBF. This pattern suggests that QTM more closely reflects processes related to astrocytic activation, neuroaxonal injury, and sex‐specific neuroinflammation. One mechanistic interpretation is that neuroinflammatory or astrocytic changes impair vascular‐tissue exchange 55 and perivascular transport, 56 reducing the efficiency of fluid movement from the intravascular compartment into the interstitial space. Because QTM integrates multi‐delay ASL dynamics across seven PLDs (0.8 to 3.25 s), it captures not only flow velocity but also delayed and dispersed tracer delivery 24 , 39 ; thus, impaired exchange may manifest as reduced voxel‐averaged QTM velocity even when mean CBF shows limited association with plasma biomarkers.

The female‐specific negative association between QTM and NfL further suggests that slowed tracer transport is more tightly linked to neuroaxonal injury in women. As NfL reflects axonal damage and WM microstructural disruption, such injury may alter extracellular space geometry and axon‐glia organization and impair microvascular regulation and fluid exchange, 57 thereby slowing and dispersing tracer propagation. Together with the GFAP findings, this supports a female‐biased vulnerability to glial‐vascular dysfunction, 58 consistent with heightened impaired glymphatic clearance‐associated amyloid burden in women. 9 Overall, the female‐specific coupling between reduced QTM velocity and elevated GFAP/NfL may reflect both heightened microvascular vulnerability and loss of estrogen‐mediated anti‐inflammatory protection.

QTM velocity and CBF with cognition

Both QTM velocity and CBF were positively associated with global cognition after adjusting for age and sex. Notably, hippocampal QTM velocity showed a significant Sex × QTM interaction, indicating that the relationship between microvascular transport and cognition differs by sex. One interpretation is that cognitive performance in females depends more strongly on microvascular transport efficiency, such that reductions in QTM velocity are more tightly coupled to cognitive decline. This aligns with epidemiological evidence of faster progression from MCI to dementia and greater cognitive vulnerability in women. 59 Importantly, combining hippocampal volume with GM or pWMH QTM velocity improved association of cognition, particularly in females, suggesting that neurodegeneration and microvascular dysfunction jointly contribute to cognitive decline, with stronger interaction effects in women.

Mediation analysis further showed that parietal QTM velocity partially mediated the association between hippocampal atrophy and global cognition. Both the Hippo and parietal cortex are affected early in AD, with established metabolic and connectivity disruptions, 60 particularly in the precuneus and PCC. 61 As key nodes of the DMN, 62 , 63 these regions support integrated cognitive functions, including memory, attention, and visuospatial processing. Multimodal evidence highlights the functional relevance of hippocampal‐parietal interactions, including causal modulation of hippocampal activity via parietal stimulation. 64 Hippocampal degeneration may therefore disrupt network connectivity, 65 leading to downstream physiological alterations in parietal regions detectable by QTM. Such changes may reduce network efficiency and contribute to global cognitive decline. Collectively, these findings suggest that microvascular transport dysfunction impacts cognition not only locally but also through disruption of large‐scale brain networks, with parietal QTM velocity representing a potential network‐level mechanism linking hippocampal neurodegeneration to cognitive impairment.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations that do not alter the central interpretation. First, its cross‐sectional design precludes causal inference regarding stage‐wise trajectories. Second, biological staging was incomplete, as amyloid/tau PET and plasma biomarkers were available only in a subset; thus, results reflect neurovascular vulnerability across a clinically defined AD continuum rather than A/T/N‐specific effects. Third, cognition was assessed primarily by MMSE, which may miss subtle domain‐specific deficits. In addition, modest sex imbalance within stages may have limited precision. Finally, although tissue segmentation and ROI definition were based on high‐resolution T1w images and ROI masks were eroded before regional extraction to reduce partial volume effects (PVEs), the PVEs related to the lower spatial resolution of ASL cannot be entirely removed, particularly for small regions. Conventionally, CBF in WM is considered to be unreliable due to its low value and potential PVE. Our analysis of CBF in WM should be treated as exploratory and needs to be confirmed by multi‐cohort studies in the future. Future longitudinal and multi‐cohort, biologically staged studies with richer cognitive measures are warranted.

CONCLUSION

In summary, this study identifies microvascular transport dysfunction as an early and female‐biased dimension of vulnerability across the clinically defined AD continuum. Compared with conventional CBF, QTM captured earlier stage transitions and sex‐dependent progression patterns, with detectable alterations already present at the SCD stage. Lower QTM velocity in females was linked to higher NfL and GFAP, supporting a transport‐related neurovascular process associated with inflammatory and axonal injury pathways. QTM was also associated with cognition and showed evidence of involvement in the pathway linking hippocampal atrophy to global cognitive decline. Together, these findings support QTM as a non‐invasive, transport‐sensitive framework for investigating sex‐dependent brain aging and early neurovascular dysfunction across the AD continuum.
