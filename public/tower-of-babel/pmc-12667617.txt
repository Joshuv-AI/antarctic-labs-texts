Modulation of JA–GA signaling and drought response by a truncated OsJAZ9 protein in rice
Authors: Jun Sung Seo, Taeyoung Um
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPlant growth and stress regulation are tightly regulated by phytohormone signals which are jasmonic acid (JA) and gibberellic acid (GA). Antagonistic crosstalk between JA and GA signaling pathways is mediated by repressor proteins, including JASMONATE ZIM-DOMAIN PROTEINs (JAZ) in the JA and DELLA proteins in the GA. In rice (Oryza sativa), the interaction between OsJAZ9 and rice DELLA protein SLENDER RICE 1 (SLR1) has been identified as a key role in the antagonistic interplay between JA and GA.In this study, we generated transgenic rice lines constitutively overexpressing a truncated form of OsJAZ9 that retains the N-terminal ZIM domain but lacks the C-terminal Jas domain (OsJAZ9NZ-OE). We hypothesized that this construct could alter JAZ–DELLA interactions and thereby affect both JA- and GA-associated processes. OsJAZ9NZ-OE plants displayed increased plant height and improved recovery under drought stress. At the transcriptional level, OsbHLH148 and OsMYC2 (JA-associated) and OsWRKY71 (ABA/stress-associated), and the GA-related transcription factors OsPIL14 and OsPIL15 were all upregulated, indicating that OsJAZ9NZ influences a broad set of hormone-responsive pathways that coordinate growth and stress responses.These findings indicate that structural modification of OsJAZ9 alters JA–GA signaling balance and is associated with transcriptional reprogramming that affects both growth and drought tolerance. While further work is required to clarify the underlying mechanisms, OsJAZ9NZ provides a useful genetic tool to probe hormone crosstalk and may represent a promising genetic resource for crop improvement under environmental stress.KEYWORDS: jasmonic acid (JA), gibberellic acid (GA), Oryza sativa, OsJAZ9, OsSLR1

Introduction

Plants continuously balance growth and defence to adapt to changing environmental conditions.1 The relationship between plant growth and defence is more complex than a simple resource–based antagonism. Recent studies report that growth–defence balance is regulated by complex hormonal and environmental signalling networks rather than by a unidirectional trade–off 2‐4 For example, activation of photoreceptors under specific light conditions can interact with jasmonate signalling to coordinate growth and defence outcomes, as illustrated by the shade–avoidance syndrome (SAS), which promotes elongation growth under low red:far–red light, and the defence syndrome (DS), which enhances jasmonate–dependent resistance responses under high light or stress conditions.2

The hormonal signalling networks for growth–defence balance, among which jasmonic acid (JA) and gibberellic acid (GA) play central, but often antagonistic, roles.5,6 JA primarily mediates defence responses against biotic and abiotic stresses by activating genes involved in resistance, wound healing, and drought tolerance. In contrast, GA promotes key developmental processes such as seed germination, stem elongation, and flowering. The antagonism between JA–GA pathways is thought to reflect a resource allocation strategy–favoring either defence or growth depending on environmental and developmental cues.5,7

A critical point of integration between these pathways is the interaction between their respective repressors: JASMONATE ZIM–DOMAIN (JAZ) proteins in the JA signalling pathway and DELLA proteins in the GA pathway.8,9 Under non–stress conditions, JAZ proteins inhibit the activity of JA–responsive transcription factors, such as MYC2, thereby repressing defence gene expression. Upon JA accumulation, JAZ proteins are rapidly degraded through the SCFCOI1 E3 ubiquitin ligase complex, enabling activation of JA signalling. Similarly, DELLA proteins repress GA responses by interacting with growth–promoting transcription factors, and GA induces their degradation via the SCFGID2 complex, thereby triggering GA–mediated developmental programmes.5,7‐9

JA–GA pathways intersect through direct physical interactions between JAZ and DELLA proteins, forming a molecular hub for integrating signals from both pathways. In Arabidopsis, DELLAs can bind to JAZs and relieve MYC2 from repression, thereby enhancing JA signalling even in the absence of elevated JA levels.5,7,8 Conversely, JAZ–DELLA interactions can also interfere with GA signalling by stabilising DELLA proteins. These interactions are mediated through conserved domains: the ZIM (TIFY) domain of JAZ proteins enables dimerisation and protein–protein interactions, while the GRAS domain of DELLA proteins facilitates interaction with transcriptional regulators. This cross–regulatory mechanism allows plants to fine–tune their growth–defence trade–offs in a dynamic environment.5,9‐11

In rice (Oryza sativa), components of the JA–GA crosstalk have been increasingly characterised. OsJAZ9, a member of the JAZ family, interacts with SLENDER RICE 1 (SLR1), a rice DELLA protein, to mediate antagonistic interactions between JA–GA signalling. Functional studies suggest that modulation of this interaction affects both stress responses and developmental outcomes.12 However, the potential to manipulate this crosstalk to improve both growth and stress tolerance in crops remains underexplored. Given the central role of JAZ–DELLA interactions in coordinating hormone signalling, targeted modulation of these protein domains represents a promising strategy for uncoupling the growth–defence trade–off.1,5,7

In OsJAZ9 overexpression transgenic rice, the expression of OsbHLH148 (a JA–responsive transcription factor associated with drought tolerance) was suppressed, whereas the expression of PHYTOCHROME–INTERACTING FACTOR–LIKE14 (OsPIL14) a GA–associated transcription factor linked to growth regulation) was increased. In contrast, in SLR1 overexpression lines, OsbHLH148 expression was increased, while OsPIL14 expression was decreased. The OsJAZ9 knockout mutant showed an increased JA response and reduced plant height.12

Additional regulators highlight the complexity of this signalling network. OsMYC2, a central transcription factor in JA signalling,13,14 has been implicated in balancing growth and defence. OsWRKY71 enhances cold stress tolerance by promoting ABA accumulation.15,16 Notably, its expression is induced by the DELLA protein RGL2 when GA levels are low, establishing a direct mechanistic link between GA signalling and ABA–mediated stress adaptation.17 These examples illustrate that JA, GA, and ABA are interconnected through shared transcriptional regulators and feedback loops, rather than acting as isolated pathways.

In particular, manipulating the ZIM domain of JAZ proteins which governs interactions with both DELLAs and transcriptional regulators–offers a novel approach to tuning JA–GA responses. Understanding and harnessing these regulatory interactions may contribute to the development of crops that are both resilient to stress and capable of sustaining high yields.

In this study, we aimed to explore whether manipulating this interaction could influence plant height and drought stress tolerance in rice by fine–tuning the balance between JA–GA responses. Based on previous evidence of the physical interaction between OsJAZ9 and the DELLA protein SLR1, we hypothesised that overexpression of a truncated OsJAZ9 variant retaining the ZIM domain but lacking the Jas degron (OsJAZ9NZ) would modulate JA–GA crosstalk and consequently alter both growth and stress responses. To test this hypothesis, we generated a transgenic rice line overexpressing a truncated form of OsJAZ9 that contains only the N-terminal region, including the ZIM domain (OsJAZ9NZ–OE), but lacks the Jas domain required for interaction with COI1 and subsequent degradation. By preserving its interaction capacity with DELLA proteins while preventing SCF^COI1-mediated degradation, OsJAZ9NZ could function as a modulator of the JA–GA interaction hub.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

Oryza sativa L. cv. Ilmi was used as the non–transgenic control. Two independent T4-generation OsJAZ9NZ-overexpressing lines and wild–type plants were grown in a paddy field at Kyungpook National University, Korea (128°34′E, 36°15′N).

Drought stress treatment and plant height

After germination on MS medium (dark, 28 °C, 3 days), seedlings were transferred to soil–filled pots (4 × 4 × 6 cm; 3 plants per pot). Plants were grown in a controlled greenhouse with the following conditions: 28 °C ± 2 °C (day)/25 °C ± 2 °C (night), relative humidity 60–70%, 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod.

Drought stress was applied to five–week–old plants by withholding water for 4 days, during which visible wilting symptoms to appear. When the majority of plants exhibited pronounced wilting, watering was resumed, and plants were allowed to recover for 10 days. These conditions were established based on preliminary trials, which identified this regime as sufficient to induce drought stress without causing irreversible damage. Initial soil moisture was adjusted to 80% of the fully saturated water content, which was determined gravimetrically by weighing soil at saturation and at the air–dried state. Soil moisture was continuously monitored throughout the treatment using a Soil Moisture Sensor SM150 (Delta–T Devices).

Plant responses, including drought tolerance and growth parameters, were subsequently evaluated. Plant height measurements were taken at 20 weeks from the soil surface to the tip of the longest culm (excluding panicles) with a measuring tape; Plants were grown in a paddy field; reported values are mean ± SD (n > 30).

Cloning of OsJAZ9NZ Constructs

For the generation of the PGD1:OsJAZ9NZ construct, OsJAZ9NZ cDNA was amplified from total RNA of Oryza sativa cv. Ilmi using SuperScript III Reverse Transcriptase (Invitrogen). The amplified OsJAZ9NZ fragment was cloned into the BamHI/SmaI sites of the binary vector pPZP200, which carries the constitutive PHOSPHOGLUCONATE DEHYDROGENASE1 (PGD1) promoter.10 The resulting recombinant plasmid was confirmed by nucleotide sequencing and subsequently introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain EHA101 for rice transformation. Primer sequences used in this study are provided in Table S1.

Quantitative RT–PCR (qRT–PCR) analysis

Total RNA was extracted from rice tissue using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) with slight modifications to the manufacturer’s protocol.11 cDNA was synthesised from 2 µg of total RNA in a 20 µl reaction using SuperScript III Reverse Transcriptase (Invitrogen). qRT–PCR was performed using a LightCycler 480 Instrument II (Roche) with SYBR Green I Master Mix according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The thermocycling programme consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 45 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s, annealing at 60 °C for 10 s, and extension at 72 °C for 10 s. Melting curve analyses were performed after each run to confirm the specificity of amplification products. OsTubA2 (Os11g0247300) was used as the internal control. Primer sequences are listed in Table S1.

Accession numbers

Sequence data for this article can be found in the Michigan State University Rice Genome Annotation Project database and the National Centre for Biotechnology Information GenBank under the following accession numbers: OsJAZ9 (Os08g0428400), OsPIL14 (Os07g0143200), OsPIL15 (Os01g0286100), OsbHLH148 (Os03g0741100), OsMYC2 (Os10g0575000), OsWRKY71 (Os02g0181300), OsDREB1A (Os09g0522200), OsDREB1B (Os09g0522000), and OsDREB1G (Os02g0677300).

Results

Constitutive overexpression of truncated OsJAZ9(ΔJas) in Rice

Because OsJAZ9 has been reported to interact with the DELLA protein SLR1,12we generated a truncated version of OsJAZ9 that retains the ZIM domain but lacks the Jas domain (OsJAZ9NZ). This construct was designed to test whether such a modification could affect JA–GA crosstalk in rice. We note that ΔJas or non–degradable JAZ variants have been reported to produce context–dependent outcomes,including dominant–negative repression of JA signalling in many studies.18–20 Therefore, in framing our hypothesis we anticipated an alteration in JA/GA signalling rather than an unequivocal enhancement of both pathways.

To test this, we generated transgenic rice lines expressing a truncated form of OsJAZ9 (OsJAZ9NZ) (Figure 1a). Expression analysis revealed that OsJAZ9NZ transcript levels in these transgenic lines were approximately 10–14-fold higher than those in non–transgenic control (NT) plants (Figure 1b). In two independent OsJAZ9NZ-overexpressing rice (OsJAZ9NZ-OE), the JA–responsive genes OsbHLH148 21, OsMYC2 13,14 and OsWRKY71 15,16 were significantly upregulated compared NT plants (Figure 1c-f). OsPIL14 and OsPIL15, are members of the PIF–like transcription factor family involved in growth regulation,12,22 were significantly upregulated compared NT plants (Figure 1f, g).

Phenotypically, OsJAZ9NZ-OE plants displayed increased plant height and length of internode relative to NT plants (Figure 2a, b). Detailed morphological analysis revealed that the length of the first internode did not differ significantly between OsJAZ9NZ-OE and NT plants, whereas the second node and internode were significantly longer in OsJAZ9NZ-OE plants (Figure 2 c–f), resulting in an overall increase in plant height. Despite this elongation, the number of tillers per plant was not significantly different from NT controls plants (Figure 2g). Together, these results demonstrate that OsJAZ9NZ overexpression modulates JA–GA signalling and promotes internode elongation in rice.

Enhanced drought resistance against drought stress by OsJAZ9NZ overexpression

To evaluate the drought resistance of OsJAZ9NZ-overexpressing plants (OsJAZ9NZ-OE), the survival rates of 5-week–old OsJAZ9NZ-OE and wild–type plants grown in a greenhouse were assessed under drought conditions (Figure 3a–c). During the drought treatment, both OsJAZ9NZ-OE and wild–type plants showed typical stress symptoms, such as wilting and leaf rolling (Figure 3a). However, OsJAZ9NZ-OE plants recovered more effectively during the 10-day rewatering period compared to wild–type plants. Notably, the survival rates of OsJAZ9NZ-OE plants ranged from 35% to 42%, while wild–type plants showed no signs of recovery (Figure 3c).

At the molecular level, the expression of OsbHLH148 and OsMYC2 a JA–responsive transcription factor previously shown to enhance drought tolerance,21 was significantly elevated in OsJAZ9NZ-OE plants (Figure 2b). Furthermore, we analysed the expression levels of drought–inducible genes OsDREB1A, OsDREB1B, and OsDREB1G in OsJAZ9NZ-OE plants (Figure 2d), suggesting activation of JA/ABA–linked stress pathways.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the role of a truncated form of OsJAZ9 (OsJAZ9NZ) that retains the ZIM domain but lacks the Jas degron, thereby avoiding COI1-mediated degradation. OsJAZ9NZ-overexpressing rice lines displayed altered expression of both JA–and GA–associated marker genes, increased plant height, and enhanced recovery following drought stress. These findings suggest that OsJAZ9NZ modulates JA–GA crosstalk in a way that influences both growth and stress responses.

Previous studies have shown that ΔJas or non–degradable JAZ variants often act as dominant repressors of JA signalling.18‐20 Our results indicate a more complex regulatory role in rice, suggesting that JAZ proteins participate in multiple hormones signalling networks (Figure 1). The induction of OsbHLH148 and OsMYC2, two key JA–responsive transcription factors,13,14,21 points to partial activation of JA–linked stress–responsive pathways. In addition, the upregulation of OsWRKY71, associated with ABA–dependent cold stress,15,16 suggests that OsJAZ9NZ affects additional stress–response networks. Together, these findings imply that OsJAZ9NZ influences cross–communication among JA, GA, and ABA signalling pathways, contributing to the coordination of growth and stress adaptation.

Interestingly, the GA–associated transcription factors OsPIL14 and OsPIL15 were significantly upregulated under normal conditions, providing a molecular explanation for the increased plant height phenotype observed in OsJAZ9NZ-OE plants. Because PIF–like transcription factors act downstream of GA to promote elongation growth,12,22 their induction suggests that OsJAZ9NZ may shift GA signalling toward a growth–promoting state. Together, these reciprocal changes upregulation of JA/ABA stress regulators alongside GA–associated growth regulators support the idea that OsJAZ9NZ fine–tunes the growth–defence tradeoff rather than simply enhancing or repressing a single pathway.

The drought resistance test provides a relevant case study for examining growth–defence regulation under abiotic stress. In OsJAZ9NZ-OE plants, the increased expression of OsbHLH148 together with drought–related OsDREB1A, OsDREB1B, and OsDREB1G supports the involvement of JA–linked transcriptional pathways in enhancing stress tolerance (Figure 3). These findings indicate that growth and defence are not governed by a simple resource–based antagonism but are dynamically modulated by hormone signalling networks.2,3,23

While our observations provide initial evidence for the involvement of OsJAZ9NZ in modulating JA–GA interactions, we acknowledge the limitations of this study. Only a limited number of JA–GA responsive genes and phenotypic traits were analysed.

In conclusion, our findings suggest that manipulating the structure of JAZ proteins, specifically the Jas domain, could alter JA–GA crosstalk in rice. Although additional data are needed to confirm the precise mechanism, OsJAZ9NZ represents a useful tool for probing growth–defence balance and may provide a potential avenue for developing crops with improved resilience to environmental stress while maintaining productive growth.
