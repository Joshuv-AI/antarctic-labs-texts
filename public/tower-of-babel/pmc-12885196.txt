Comparison of the Effects of Foot and Hand Reflexology on Pain and Anxiety in Patients Admitted to the Cardiac Intensive Care Unit
Authors: Pouya Zeidy, Ali Safdari, Azam Kerami, Mehdi Safarabadi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Anxiety and pain are common challenges among patients admitted to the cardiac care unit (CCU). This study aimed to compare the effects of hand and foot reflexology on anxiety and pain in CCU patients.Materials and Methods:A randomized clinical trial was conducted between June and September 2024. Of the 131 patients assessed for eligibility, 108 were enrolled and randomly assigned to three groups: hand reflexology, foot reflexology, and control. Reflexology sessions were performed twice daily over three consecutive days. Anxiety was evaluated using the Spielberger State-Trait Anxiety Inventory, and pain was measured with the visual analog scale. Data were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and paired t-tests, with a significance level of P < 0.05.Results:No significant differences were found among the groups regarding demographic or baseline clinical characteristics (P > 0.05), confirming initial group homogeneity. Following the intervention, both the hand and foot reflexology groups showed significant reductions in state and trait anxiety scores (P < 0.001), while the control group exhibited no significant changes. However, post-intervention pain scores did not significantly differ among the three groups (P = 0.563). Within-group analysis revealed a significant reduction in pain only in the hand reflexology group.Conclusion:Hand and foot reflexology were both effective in reducing anxiety in CCU patients. Hand reflexology also demonstrated potential for reducing pain, suggesting it may serve as a useful complementary therapy in critical care settings.

INTRODUCTION

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) are among the most common conditions leading to disability and death.[1] The prevalence of coronary artery disease and associated mortality is on the rise in developing countries.[2] It is estimated that half of all deaths in developing countries are due to CVDs.[3] According to the World Health Organization (WHO), approximately 17.9 million people die annually from CVDs.[4] In Iran, CVDs are also recognized as the leading cause of death among individuals over the age of 35.[1] Estimates suggest that around 46% of all deaths in Iran are attributable to circulatory diseases.[5] Many patients are admitted to cardiac care units (CCUs).[2]

Hospitalization in CCUs exposes patients to numerous distressing stressors. Factors such as a lack of awareness about care methods and uncertainty about their future condition exacerbate their stress.[6] Anxiety is one of the most common psychological reactions among cardiac patients, and it not only influences the course of the disease but also affects recovery.[5] The incidence of anxiety in cardiac patients is generally higher compared to patients with other conditions.[7] Anxiety increases sympathetic activity, leading to elevated myocardial oxygen demand, cardiac workload, heart rate, blood pressure, and respiratory rate, which may also cause cardiac arrhythmias.[8] High levels of anxiety increase cardiac stress, result in hemodynamic instability, and delay cardiac rehabilitation.[9] Furthermore, anxiety affects patients’ experiences with intensive care, their recovery, and overall well-being, underscoring the clinical importance of reducing anxiety in cardiac patients as part of nursing care.[10]

Pain is another common symptom in cardiac patients, and evidence suggests that pain relief in these patients may not always be adequately achieved.[8] Pain activates the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis and stimulates the sympathetic nervous system, leading to physiological changes such as increased heart rate, elevated blood pressure, and a rise in adrenaline levels. This unfavorable circulatory condition is associated with various arrhythmias, including atrial fibrillation.[3] Pain also reduces pulmonary ventilation and increases respiratory rate, exacerbating the risk of myocardial ischemia.[11]

Although analgesics are the most effective tools available to nurses for pain relief, they are not the only effective method. Integrating non-pharmacological interventions alongside pharmacological treatments is crucial for reducing anxiety and pain in cardiac patients.[8] Furthermore, pharmacological treatments, aside from causing side effects, may interact with cardiovascular medications.[9] Combining complementary medicine interventions with conventional treatments can reduce the need for analgesics and yield more favorable outcomes compared to using analgesics alone.[12] In recent years, there has been increasing interest in non-pharmacological methods, such as complementary therapies.[1314] A wide range of complementary therapies is available, which are not only simple and non-invasive but also cost-effective and either free from side effects or associated with fewer side effects compared to pharmacological treatments.[10]

Reflexology is a branch of complementary medicine that stimulates the flow of energy and reduces the activation of the sympathetic nervous system, thereby decreasing anxiety and pain.[15] Reflexology is a well-known, non-invasive, and safe method involving the application of pressure to specific points on the hands or feet, which correspond to specific areas of the body.[216] Massaging these points influences the neuromuscular system, increasing tissue oxygenation and blood flow, and improving the removal of waste products such as lactic acid.[17] Reflexology impacts the transmission of pain impulses and pain perception by releasing endorphins.[716] Nurses can easily perform this technique, thereby enhancing patient satisfaction.[18]

The effectiveness of reflexology has been explored in various medical conditions; however, there are few studies investigating the effects of reflexology during cardiovascular procedures. As such, further research in this area is recommended.[7] Moreover, while reflex points exist on both hands and feet, anatomical differences, such as longer fingers occupying a larger portion of the hand compared to toes on the foot, result in more concentrated reflex points in the hand,[19] which may lead to different reflexology effects between the two areas.[20]

To our knowledge, no study has yet compared the effects of hand and foot reflexology on pain and anxiety levels in patients admitted to CCUs. Considering that anxiety and pain are among the most significant issues for cardiac patients in intensive care units, appropriate and effective nursing interventions are essential. Therefore, this study aimed to examine the effects of reflexology on anxiety and pain in patients admitted to CCUs.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and sample

This study was conducted as a double-blind randomized clinical trial. This research aimed to compare the effects of hand and foot reflexology on anxiety and pain in patients admitted to the cardiac intensive care unit at Imam Khomeini Hospital in Khomein (Central Iran) from June to September 2024. The design and execution of this study adhered to the CONSORT statement.[21] and the guidelines for the Standards for Reporting Interventions in Controlled Trials of Acupuncture (STRICTA).[22] Figure 1 presents the flow diagram of the clinical trial.

CONSORT flowchart of the research

The sample size for this study was determined based on a previous study,[2] with a statistical power of 90% and a type I error rate of 0.05. This resulted in a requirement of 32 patients per group. Considering a potential 10% dropout rate, a total of 36 participants were included in each group (108 participants in total).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria for the study were as follows: age over 18 years; admission to the cardiac intensive care unit due to acute heart diseases; no prior history of hand or foot reflexology; oriented to time, place, and person; non-use of anti-anxiety medications; a state anxiety score of 43 or higher on the Spielberger State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI);[23] the presence of acute cardiac pain; and the signing of a written informed consent form. The exclusion criteria included hemodynamic instability (severe fluctuations in blood pressure or heart rate), the presence of skin disorders such as wounds or infections on the hands or feet that would prevent reflexology, emergency changes in the patient’s condition requiring immediate medical intervention, and inability to complete all stages of the intervention due to personal or medical reasons.

Randomization and blinding

Patients were randomly assigned to three intervention and control groups using six-block random allocation. To prevent any bias, patient assignments were placed in sealed envelopes, prepared by a third party with no involvement from the research team. The data-collecting researcher was blinded to the patient’s group allocation in this study. Data analysis was conducted by an independent statistician who was also unaware of the group assignments. Due to the application of reflexology on different parts of the body, blinding of patients was not possible.

Outcome measures and instruments

In this study, data were collected using a demographic information form (including gender, age, education, and marital status), the Spielberger STAI, and the visual analog scale (VAS) for pain.

Visual Analogue scale (VAS)

This scale measures patients’ pain on a 10-point scale, where 0 represents “no pain” and 10 represents “the worst imaginable pain.” All participants were asked to indicate their pain intensity by selecting a number that reflects its severity. Pain intensity was measured using the VAS before and after the intervention.

Spielberger State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI)

Anxiety levels were measured using the STAI, Form Y, a widely recognized and validated instrument for assessing both temporary (state) and long-term (trait) anxiety. The tool comprises 40 items divided into two subscales: STAI-Y1 for state anxiety and STAI-Y2 for trait anxiety, each containing 20 items. Respondents rate each item on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“not at all”) to 4 (“very much so”), with higher scores indicating greater anxiety levels. The Persian version of the STAI Form Y has demonstrated strong psychometric properties in various Iranian populations. In a study by Abdoli et al.,[24] the internal consistency was reported with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients of 0.846 for state anxiety and 0.886 for trait anxiety.

Interventions

Before the main study began, the researchers conducted a pilot study to assess the feasibility of the research, ensure the accurate identification of reflex points, and verify the quality of the massage technique. The reflexology method used in this study was the Rwo Shur technique,[25] which combines thumb-sliding and pressure techniques, applying gentle pressure on reflex points. Reflexology sessions were conducted by the second author of the article, who had received the necessary training before the study. The interventions took place in a room with standard lighting and humidity, with minimal environmental stimuli.

Patients in the hand reflexology group underwent reflexology interventions twice a day (each session lasting 30 min) for three consecutive days. The reflex points targeted were related to the cardiac and central nervous systems, with pressure applied at a slow pace, using a regular rhythm and a depth tolerable for the patients. To ensure consistency, all sessions followed a standardized protocol in terms of pressure intensity, stimulation duration at each point, and sequence of techniques, as established during the pilot phase. Reflexology was first applied to the patient’s right hand. Sunflower oil was used as a massage aid to minimize friction during the procedure. The reflexology intervention began with a 5-min preliminary massage of the right hand, followed by targeted reflexology movements on specific areas. The same techniques were then applied to the patient’s left hand. Patients in the foot reflexology group received reflexology on their feet, following the same protocol used for hand reflexology.

In the control group, patients received only the standard care provided in the cardiac intensive care unit, and no reflexology interventions were performed. Throughout the study, all patients remained at complete rest, and their usual medications were administered without any changes. Outcome assessments for the study were measured and recorded independently by a research assistant in all three groups.

Statistical analyses

Data analysis was performed using Stata software, version 14 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA). Descriptive and analytical statistical tests were applied for data analysis. The comparison of mean pain and anxiety variables between the interventions and control groups was conducted using one-way ANOVA, while paired t-tests were used for within-group comparisons. A significance level of 0.05 was considered for all tests.

RESULTS

A total of 131 patients were screened for eligibility, of which 23 participants were excluded. Subsequently, 108 participants were randomized into three groups: foot reflexology, hand reflexology, and control group. During the follow-up, two participants in the hand reflexology group and one participant in the control group were lost to follow-up. All participants in the foot reflexology group completed the intervention [Figure 1].

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were comparable across the three groups, with no significant differences in gender distribution (P = 0.556), marital status (P = 0.564), education level (P = 0.228), or age (P = 0.913) [Table 1].

Comparing the variations in pain and anxiety between groups

Similarly, there were no significant baseline differences in trait anxiety scores between the groups (P = 0.631). However, post-intervention analysis showed a significant reduction in trait anxiety in both the hand reflexology (P < 0.001) and foot reflexology groups (P < 0.001), while the control group did not show a significant difference (P = 0.256). The post-intervention between-group comparison indicated a statistically significant difference in trait anxiety levels (P = 0.021) [Table 2].

There was no significant difference in pain scores between the three groups at baseline (P = 0.080). After the intervention, significant reductions in pain were noted in the hand reflexology group (P < 0.001), while the reduction in pain in the control group (P = 0.246) and foot reflexology group (P = 0.076) was not significant. In addition, the post-intervention differences in pain scores between the three groups were not statistically significant (P = 0.563) [Table 2].

DISCUSSION

This study aimed to compare the effects of hand and foot reflexology on the levels of anxiety and pain in patients hospitalized in the CCU. The findings demonstrated that reflexology intervention effectively reduced patients’ anxiety levels.

According to the findings of this study, the effect of reflexology on pain intensity reduction among patients was not statistically significant when comparing the groups. This result contrasts with the findings of Ali et al.[7] The study by Sayar et al.[8] also demonstrated that foot reflexology significantly reduced chest pain intensity in patients with acute myocardial infarction compared to the control group, particularly 20 min after the intervention.

The variation in results between different studies may be due to differences in the location and duration of interventions and the studied populations. Some studies also support our findings. Reafaat Abdelkader Atia, in his study, demonstrated that foot reflexology effectively reduces pain intensity in patients undergoing open-heart surgery.[3] Another study also found foot reflexology to be effective in reducing pain scores in patients undergoing open-heart surgery.[18] It is suggested that reflexology reduces pain by releasing endogenous endorphins, the gate control theory, and blocking pain signal transmission.[7] Stimulating reflexology points may increase blood flow, stimulate endorphin release, aid in the removal of toxins from muscles and organs, and help regulate physiological function. Reflexology can improve the condition of internal organs.[17]

In our study, pain intensity decreased in all study groups after the intervention. However, this reduction was statistically significant only in the hand reflexology group. The lack of more studies comparing the effects of hand and foot reflexology in patients hospitalized in the CCU limits further discussion on this matter. Nonetheless, one potential reason for the difference in the effects of these interventions could be attributed to the anatomical differences between the hands and feet.[19] Providing conclusive findings in this area requires further research.

In our study, both hand and foot reflexology interventions effectively reduced state and trait anxiety in patients. However, this effect was not observed in the control group. Similarly, Şahin and Çilingir found that the increase in anxiety levels in patients undergoing open-heart surgery was lower in the foot reflexology group compared to the control group.[18] Other studies have also reported the positive effect of reflexology in reducing anxiety in patients with various heart-related conditions, such as coronary artery bypass graft surgery,[1726] coronary angiography,[7] and open-heart surgery.[318] Furthermore, the impact of reflexology on the sleep quality of patients with systolic heart failure[27] and the improvement of physiological indicators in patients after open-heart surgery[3] have been reported.

In their study, Ramezanibadr et al.[28] demonstrated that the anxiety levels of male patients undergoing coronary angiography were significantly lower in the foot reflexology group compared to the control and placebo groups. This suggests that reflexology is more effective than simple touch therapy. Rahmani Vasokolaei et al.[2] also found that hand reflexology was effective in reducing anxiety and stabilizing the vital signs of female patients in the CCU. Similarly, Adib-Hajbaghery et al.[6] reported that massage intervention was effective in reducing anxiety and stabilizing the vital signs of CCU patients.

Another clinical trial also indicated a reduction in state anxiety among patients scheduled for open-heart surgery.[1] However, Bang and Park, in their study, did not find auricular acupuncture to be effective in reducing anxiety in heart surgery patients; they attributed this finding to the short intervention duration and the presence of a variety of postoperative symptoms and discomforts affecting the recovery process.[9] In some other studies, such as the one by Sayar et al.[8] that examined the effect of reflexology intervention on anxiety in patients with acute myocardial infarction, no significant effect was observed. This discrepancy may be due to differences in patient demographics, clinical factors, intervention duration, and the method of reflexology application. Other possible reasons for these differences include varying disease conditions or individual differences, such as baseline anxiety levels or specific physiological conditions.

The findings of our study indicate that the high levels of anxiety in CCU patients necessitate further medical interventions. Anxiety in cardiac patients can be associated with dangerous cardiovascular outcomes, both fatal and non-fatal, as well as readmission.[2] For instance, one study found that cardiac patients in intensive care units experience high levels of death anxiety.[4] A 2019 meta-analysis also showed that reflexology was effective in reducing anxiety in patients undergoing cardiovascular interventions compared to the control group.[10] Thus, reflexology may play a beneficial role in this context.

In this study, no significant difference was observed between the hand and foot reflexology groups in terms of anxiety reduction in patients. However, it seems that hand reflexology may be more effective in reducing patients’ pain. Nonetheless, definitive conclusions in this regard require further evidence. Given the positive impact of reflexology on reducing patient anxiety, it can be recommended as a simple, non-pharmacological method for patients admitted to cardiac intensive care units. Additionally, it is suggested that educational programs be implemented to enhance the knowledge and skills of nurses and nursing students. Further studies should also be conducted to examine the long-term effects of reflexology on the clinical outcomes of cardiac patients.

CONCLUSION

This randomized clinical trial demonstrated that both foot and hand reflexology effectively reduced anxiety in CCU patients. While both forms of reflexology provided meaningful reductions in anxiety, the differences in post-intervention pain levels between the groups were not statistically significant. These findings support the use of reflexology as a complementary, non-pharmacological approach for anxiety management in critical cardiac care settings.

Limitations

The study’s limitations include its relatively small sample size and single-center design, which may restrict the generalizability of the results. Additionally, the short follow-up period limits understanding of reflexology’s long-term effects. Future research should focus on multicenter trials with larger sample sizes and assess additional physiological and psychological outcomes to further validate reflexology’s role in patient care.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

The study received ethical approval (IR.KHOMEIN.REC.1403.009). The protocol was also registered with the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials (IRCT20240609062052N1) on 22/06/2024. The researchers committed to adhering to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki throughout all stages of the study. Before the commencement of the study, participants were fully informed of the study’s objectives and the nature of the interventions. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before inclusion in the study. Participants were assured that the study results would be used solely for research purposes without disclosing any personal identifying information. Furthermore, they were informed of their right to withdraw from the study at any stage without affecting their access to standard healthcare services.
