Sexual health and REhabilitation online (SHAREonline): study protocol of an optimisation trial
Authors: Eva Michaela Miklos, Christopher J Recklitis, Jennifer Barsky Reese, Cheryl Medeiros-Nancarrow, Sharon L Bober
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionYounger female cancer survivors disproportionately suffer from treatment-related sexual dysfunction, yet evidence-based intervention for these prevalent and distressing side effects remains lacking. Previously, we developed and pilot-tested SHARE (Sexual Health and Rehabilitation), a single-session, in-person group educational intervention for younger female survivors with distressing sexual dysfunction. We recently adapted the intervention to be delivered via synchronous videoconference, now called SHAREonline. The aims of this optimisation trial are to (1) evaluate ‘proof-of-concept’, that is, clinically significant change in sexual function and psychological distress in younger female survivors, and (2) conduct pilot testing to assess feasibility of the intervention, including assessing the credibility of a control condition (ie, individual self-management (ISM)). Participant characteristics associated with clinically significant response to the intervention will also be explored to prepare for a larger efficacy trial.Methods and analysisThis trial will enrol 84 female survivors, ages 19–49 with clinically significant treatment-related sexual dysfunction. Participants will be randomised to either the SHAREonline intervention or ISM. Sexual function and psychological distress are assessed at baseline, 8 and 16 weeks postintervention. Outcomes will be assessed by evaluating change in sexual function and psychological distress from baseline to 8 weeks (primary endpoint), and feasibility, including credibility and acceptability of the control condition, will be evaluated. Postintervention qualitative interviews will be conducted to further explore the experience of trial participants. If findings are positive, we will next evaluate the efficacy of SHAREonline in a fully powered efficacy trial.Ethics and disseminationThis trial has been registered on ClinicalTrials.gov. Ethical approval has been granted by Dana-Farber Cancer Institute’s Institutional Review Board (4 January 2022) prior to data collection. Results will be published in peer-reviewed journals.Trial registration numberNCT06458049.

Introduction

Sexual dysfunction is one of the most distressing treatment-related consequences for younger female cancer survivors.1 2 Over half of younger survivors (age <50) report significant treatment-related sexual problems, including vulvo-vaginal atrophy, diminished arousal, decreased desire and loss of sexual satisfaction.3 4 The prevalence of sexual dysfunction reported by younger cancer survivors is four times greater than their same-age peers.5 Moreover, the psychosocial impact of sexual health sequelae can be devastating.6 7 Well-known negative consequences include anxiety and depression, as well as impaired sexual identity1 7 and loss of self-esteem.5 8 Associated difficulties also include starting and/or maintaining intimate relationships,9 which are common and highly stressful problems for younger adults. In fact, sexual dysfunction is often described as the most compromised quality-of-life issue5 9 for younger survivors. Unfortunately, unlike other late effects which may subside over time, sexual dysfunction is long-lasting and tends to worsen if unaddressed.5 These persistent effects can be particularly devastating for younger survivors who will be potentially navigating intimate relationships for many years. Despite the prevalence of distressing treatment-related consequences, up to 74% of younger survivors with sexual concerns do not receive any intervention for treatment-related sexual dysfunction, making it a critical gap in cancer survivorship care.10 11

Although there have been frequent calls to improve sexual health support for younger survivors, sexual health intervention is difficult to access,12 and prior interventions have been inadequate for several reasons.8 13 14 Despite consensus recommendations for sexual health intervention to deliver biopsychosocial support,8 14 that is, support addressing physical, psychological and interpersonal challenges, interventions to date rarely follow those guidelines. Instead, intervention content is typically narrow in scope and frequently insufficient.13 15 16 For example, women might receive biomedical recommendations (eg, use of a vaginal moisturiser) without attention to relationship issues or delays in dating and sexual debut. Similarly, information about rehabilitation strategies (eg, use of vaginal dilators) may be of limited use without the necessary skills training.17 Furthermore, the small number of existing sexual health interventions developed for online delivery have largely been hampered by limited uptake and high rates of drop-out,18 19 often over 50%. Barriers to uptake and adherence may be tied to reliance on asynchronous, unguided formats shown to neither adequately engage participants20 nor provide information that is adequately targeted for the patient population.21 To overcome these limitations and address the needs of younger female survivors with treatment-related sexual problems, we developed SHAREonline (Sexual Health And Rehabilitation Online), a group-delivered, single-session intervention guided by the biopsychosocial model of sexual health using synchronous videoconference.

Following the Obesity-Related Behavioral Intervention Trials (ORBIT) model for the systematic development evaluation and optimisation of behavioural health interventions,22 SHAREonline builds on our previous work developing a theory-driven sexual health intervention. Initially, we developed SHARE as an in-person intervention combining state-of-the-art sexual rehabilitation with body awareness skills training, elements of mindfulness-based cognitive therapy and individual action planning. Guided by Self-Determination Theory, SHARE emphasised the need for self-efficacy, competence and relatedness, three key elements of psychological well-being that have also been shown to promote maintained effects on health behaviour outcomes.23 This theoretical underpinning of self-determination theory is also echoed in the framework of patient self-management,24 a model of patient empowerment that supports sustainable behaviour change in the context of quality cancer care.25 26

The SHARE intervention was first developed for younger women with sexual dysfunction following risk-reducing oophorectomy,27 before being adapted for women treated for ovarian cancer.28 Subsequently, SHARE was adapted to address sexual health late effects in younger breast cancer survivors.29 Single-arm (ORBIT phase IIa) trials of SHARE in each of these populations demonstrated that SHARE improved women’s sexual function and decreased psychological distress.27–29 In published studies to date, SHARE was delivered exclusively in person and tested only in single-arm trials. To decrease logistical and geographic barriers to accessing SHARE at our cancer centre such as the need for transportation and time off work for distance travel, a briefer version was initially adapted for online presentation and delivered in the context of several clinical workshops. Feedback from these clinical workshops was very positive, at which point SHAREonline was further refined, adapted and manualised for delivery using a synchronous, videoconference format.

The present study protocol describes the next step in intervention development, specifically conducting ORBIT model phase IIa/IIb preliminary testing of SHAREonline. The primary objective of ORBIT phase IIa is to measure proof-of-concept, in this case, the detection of a clinically meaningful signal for SHAREonline to improve sexual function and decrease psychological distress in younger female cancer survivors. To date, few oncosexual health interventions have been tested in fully powered randomised controlled trials, and most interventions typically do not include an active control group but rather use a waitlist control design. Therefore, additional aims of this study are to assess the credibility and acceptability of the enhanced usual care condition (individual self-management (ISM)) and assess usability and satisfaction with the synchronous videoconference platform in preparation for a definitive (phase III) efficacy trial. These additional aims align with the ORBIT model phase IIb, which refers to preliminarily evaluating various aspects of study feasibility. In addition, exploratory analysis will examine participant factors associated with clinically significant responses to the intervention, including partner status, age, race/ethnicity, time since diagnosis and treatment type (see figure 1). This exploratory analysis is intended to be hypothesis-generating and will include identifying potential stratification variables prior to conducting a fully powered efficacy trial.

Methods

Ethical considerations

Prior to study activation and participant enrolment, the study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Dana-Farber/Harvard Cancer Center Institutional Review Board (IRB), (DF/HCC IRB 21–697). This study received a Waiver of Documentation of Informed Consent, as it was deemed a minimal risk study involving procedures (eg, questionnaires and educational session) for which signed consent is not required. Participants receive a US$10 Amazon gift card after completing the session evaluation, a US$25 Amazon gift card per completion of the 8-week assessment and a US$25 Amazon gift card per completion of the 16-week assessment.

Multiple steps have been taken to protect participants’ privacy during all aspects of intervention delivery and data collection. The Zoom30–32 videoconferencing platform used for the study is licensed to our Cancer Institute and is fully encrypted and Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) compliant. For additional security, passwords are required for all study Zoom meetings, and all Zoom meetings are locked. The Qualtrics33 web-based survey platform provides secure collection of survey data using encrypted data transfer. For this study, the participants do not enter any personal identifiers into the Qualtrics system, and participants are identified only by a study ID. Participants access the Qualtrics platform through a unique link connected only to their study ID, and no protected health information is stored on the platform.

Data privacy and security procedures include (a) storing and processing sensitive hard copies in locked files when not in use; (b) removing names, addresses and other direct identifiers from hard copy and computer-readable data after they are no longer necessary for patient tracking and then using encrypted codes for subsequent identification of subjects and (c) using restricted logon identification and password protection computer protocols for all computerised entry, retrieval and analysis. Only researchers and study staff have access to the data, which will be used for research purposes only. On completion of the trial, summary results will be submitted to ClinicalTrials.gov in accordance with policy from the National Institutes of Health (NIH).34 The datasets generated and analysed during this trial will also be made available from the corresponding author on reasonable request following completion of the trial and publication of the main outcomes.

Study design and overview

The design is an ORBIT phase IIa/IIb optimisation trial with repeated measures. During the study enrolment session (SES), participants are randomised (2:1) to receive either SHAREonline or ISM, a brief individual videoconference session introducing them to online sexual health resources. For individuals randomised to ISM, this session takes place directly at the end of the SES. Participants randomised to the intervention arm are (1) assigned to a SHAREonline session which takes place within 1 week of the SES, and (2) they receive an individual coaching telephone call 4 weeks after the group session. All participants complete follow-up measures at 8 and 16 weeks postintervention. After completing the final study assessment, participants in the control group (ISM) are offered a SHAREonline session. Following the 16- week follow-up, a subset of up to 20 participants from both arms will be invited to participate in qualitative interviews to provide a more comprehensive understanding about their experience. These interviews will also be conducted via videoconference, recorded and transcribed, with any identifiable information removed. This study protocol adheres to the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) 2013 Checklist for reporting clinical trial protocols.35

Patient and public involvement statement

Patients were involved in the design and conduct of this research. This research builds on previous pilot work with substantial patient involvement, starting with our initial SHARE pilot with younger women who underwent risk-reducing oophorectomy.27 During that trial, satisfaction with the content, length and format of the SHARE intervention was elicited from participants. Intervention materials were then revised for use with ovarian cancer survivors,28 and patient feedback was elicited both before and at the end of the pilot. Finally, feedback from younger female breast cancer survivors was also elicited in an iterative process consisting of individual and group interviews. The current study design includes qualitative participant interviews at the end of the study to garner additional feedback to prepare for a definitive efficacy trial.

Participants

Participants for SHAREonline will be 84 off-treatment female survivors aged 19–49 years. To be eligible for enrolment, participants must be diagnosed with cancer (excluding non-melanoma skin cancer) at age ≥18 and at least 1 year prior to enrolment, have received no cancer therapy (excluding chemoprevention) in the past 4 months and have no additional cancer therapy planned. Participants must be able to read and write in English, have regular access to the internet and experience significant sexual dysfunction as evidenced by a Female Sexual Functioning Index (FSFI) score ≤26.36 For detailed participant inclusion and exclusion criteria, see table 1.

Recruitment

Recruitment procedures are designed to allow potential participants to self-refer to the study. This includes informing patients via oncology clinics at our cancer centre and associated regional affiliated clinics (eg, informative letters/emails sent directly to potentially eligible patients), advertisements on social media (Instagram, Facebook, X) and patient advocacy group newsletters. Study flyers/information sheets are distributed in clinic waiting areas, and information is also displayed on hospital screens that promote patient-oriented information placed throughout the cancer centre and regional affiliate clinics. Study information is also included in newsletters, social media posts and other electronic patient bulletins distributed by various supportive care programmes at the cancer centre serving the needs of younger patients. In addition, study information materials are posted on research portals and social media sites of both regional and national organisations providing support and advocacy to cancer survivors.

Procedures

Screening and scheduling

Individuals interested in the study are directed to contact the research group via email or telephone. Patients who express interest in participation are invited to schedule an individual screening interview with a research team member via videoconference to assess eligibility. In this videoconference meeting, patients can ask any additional questions and complete the eligibility screening. Eligible patients who wish to enrol are then scheduled for an SES within 1 week prior to their intervention session. During the SES, verbal consent is obtained, participants are instructed on the web-based survey platform, baseline measures are completed, and randomisation is conducted. At initial submission of this protocol paper, recruitment was underway and continued through December 2025.

Randomisation and allocation

After completing baseline questionnaires, randomisation with a block size of 6 is performed using Sealed Envelope,37 a commercially available online software application for randomising patients into clinical trials. Treatment allocation is electronically generated by Sealed Envelope, and study staff have no foreknowledge of allocation sequence during the recruitment or consent process, maintaining allocation concealment. Patients are randomised (2:1) to either (1) SHAREonline or (2) ISM in order to maximise the amount of information obtained about the SHAREonline intervention. In addition, participants are asked to confirm preferences for future study contact and correspondence. Due to the nature of the intervention, researchers involved in the delivery and monitoring of the intervention are not blinded to group allocation of participants.

SHAREonline intervention delivery

SHAREonline is a single-session, group intervention conducted in a synchronous videoconference format. The session is led by a qualified interventionist (Master’s level or higher), trained by the PI, who is a clinical psychologist and expert in sexual rehabilitation for cancer survivors. Each group includes up to 10 participants, and the session lasts approximately 3 hours, including breaks. The synchronous group session is designed to provide a supportive group experience that allows participants to see and hear the presenter and other participants, view the presentation slides, ask questions and interact with the presenter and other participants. SHAREonline is delivered in three modules (see figure 2) comprising both didactic and experiential learning components. To help consolidate learning, copies of the presentation slides and other materials (eg, teaching sheets, cognitive-reframing thought record) are provided to participants by email. Women finish the group session by creating a personal action plan where they identify their individual goals as well as actionable steps to be implemented in the next month.

SHAREonline modules. SHAREonline, Sexual Health and Rehabilitation Online.

Module 1: psychoeducation and group discussion: education is presented on treatment-related sexual problems, common misconceptions and information about strategies for managing sexual dysfunction.38 Communication challenges are discussed, including how to begin a conversation about changes in sexual health with a current or prospective partner. Educational resources are also distributed. The goal of this module is to increase knowledge, awareness of resources, choices about self-help strategies and communication competence.

Module 2: body awareness and relaxation training: body awareness and relaxation training includes learning basic exercises for pelvic floor relaxation.39 Experiential learning includes exercises such as practising diaphragmatic breathing, a body scan meditation and specific physical poses such as the ‘yogi squat’. The goal of this module is to learn techniques for body awareness and pelvic floor relaxation.

Module 3: cognitive restructuring and mindfulness-based cognitive therapy: participants learn about cognitive strategies such as identifying and challenging negative thoughts that interfere with sexual enjoyment. Additional strategies from mindfulness-based sex therapy are also reviewed.

Action planning

After finishing each module, participants pause to review the material they learnt about and choose specific related problems that they want to focus on. They are asked to identify and commit to ‘next step’ strategies, which they can check off on a personal action plan. This plan then serves as their personal action plan, which they will use in the weeks following the intervention.

Coaching phone call

Women randomised to SHAREonline receive a single coaching phone call (estimated <30 min) 1 month postintervention at a prearranged time. The phone calls conducted by a trained bachelor’s-level research assistant following a structured outline are intended to help women review their progress and offer encouragement and coaching to support the goals identified in the personalised action plan. The research assistant can also refer women back to relevant resources to address outstanding questions.

ISM (control condition)

In ISM, women receive a brief (<20 min) individual videoconference session introducing them to the sexual health resources available online from the American Cancer Society (ACS), as well as the ACS booklet, ‘Sex and the Adult Female with Cancer’.40 The booklet, available publicly at no cost, explains common sexual problems and basic treatment strategies. This session is conducted by a trained bachelor’s-level research assistant, who uses a shared slide deck and scripted explanation of the ACS resource. Participants also receive instructions about how to access information on the ACS website41 and receive a hard copy of the paper booklet. The ISM condition was chosen as both a rigorous and pragmatic comparator. It is rigorous since it has been demonstrated that education alone can make a meaningful difference in the management of cancer treatment-related side effects.42 It is also pragmatic because cancer survivors often seek information and access patient advocacy resources such as the ACS website. In addition, the brief videoconference session with a trained RA serves to enhance the rigour of the comparator condition by controlling for human warmth and attention. Use of the ISM/enhanced usual care condition also addresses additional ethical and practical problems such as credibility/expectancy associated with waitlist or no-treatment controls.

Outcomes

The primary outcome measure is change in sexual function on the FSFI Full Scale Score from baseline to 8 weeks.36 43 The FSFI has frequently been used in studies with younger female cancer survivors.2 44 The secondary outcome measure is change in the Brief Symptom Inventory-18 (BSI-18) Global Severity Index (GSI), which reflects general psychological distress, from baseline to 8 weeks.45 Additional information assessing sexual distress, sexual satisfaction and self-efficacy is collected using the Sexual Distress Scale Short Form,46 the Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) Sexual Function and Satisfaction (PROMIS SexSF)47 and the Sexual Self-Efficacy Scale.48 Measures and time points are included in table 2.

Planned analysis

Primary aim: the primary endpoint to assess proof-of-concept is change in sexual dysfunction in the intervention group from baseline to 8-week follow-up, measured on the FSFI full-scale score, and the secondary endpoint is change in emotional distress captured on the BSI-18 (GSI) at 8-week follow-up. Change scores will be treated as continuous variables. Analysis will be within-subjects, comparing FSFI at baseline and FSFI at 8 weeks using a one-sample, repeated measures t-test. As an exploratory analysis, change in the FSFI score from baseline to 16 weeks postintervention will also be compared using a t-test. Exploratory analyses will examine effect sizes as well as logistic regression to identify participant factors (eg, age, race and cancer diagnosis) associated with a clinically meaningful difference on the FSFI full-scale at 8 weeks postintervention, defined as improvement of ≥4.5 points (scale range: 2–36, SD ≈10). Analysis of the BSI-18 will use the same t-test procedures.

Secondary aims: feasibility will be measured through study enrolment, engagement (with individual action plan) and retention to study completion (completion of all surveys) using the following benchmarks: Enrolment >40% of eligible candidates, study engagement >70% and intervention completion >60%. Acceptability measured on the Intervention Satisfaction Questionnaire will be assessed using a benchmark mean score of 4. Credibility of the intervention and control conditions will be assessed by the Credibility/Expectancy Questionnaire using a mean score of 4 as a benchmark. Descriptive statistics will be used to report distributions and describe how this benchmark is met or exceeded. Descriptive statistics will additionally be used to report patient session satisfaction questionnaires (including telehealth usability questions) as well as coaching evaluation questionnaires. The Sexual Distress and Self-Efficacy measures collected at baseline, 8 and 16 weeks will be analysed using repeated measures ANOVA or regression analysis as appropriate. Further, the PROMIS SexSF V.2 given at the baseline and 8-week time points will be analysed with similar methods.

Sample size

This study is powered to detect an effect size of d=0.4. This magnitude of effect is considered clinically meaningful and is a conservative estimate given observed effects of d=0.5 in previous SHARE interventions.27 29 With a sample of 75, 50 participants randomised (2:1) to the SHAREonline intervention group and 25 to the Enhanced Usual Care (EUC), the study will have 80% power to detect a pre-post change in the FSFI of d=0.4, using a two-sided, repeated measures t-test with alpha=0.05. As a hedge against attrition, we will enrol 84 women to provide complete data on 75 participants.

Qualitative interviews

Semistructured individual interviews will be conducted to gain meaningful insight into how participants experienced the SHAREonline intervention as well as the role that SHAREonline may have played in helping participants address their goals. On completing the 16-week assessment, a group of up to 20 participants from both arms will be invited to individually participate in semistructured interviews conducted by a research team member trained in qualitative methodology. The research team will use a purposive sampling strategy to gain a diverse sample based on age, diagnosis and other demographic variables (eg, marital status) to obtain maximum variation. All interviews will be conducted by videoconference, recorded and transcribed verbatim. The semistructured interview guide for participants includes questions about study engagement, for example, experience related to the online aspects of participation as well as other aspects of participation such as perceived usability and their experience with the individual coaching sessions. Following recommended guidelines for analysis of qualitative data,49 inductive and deductive methods will be used in interpretation. A thematic analysis50 will be employed to analyse the data using a predefined coding framework to organise the data by key themes, concepts and categories. Two reviewers will code the data for themes, and they will meet regularly with a third coder to discuss and resolve any discrepancies. The interviews are intended to broaden contextual understanding of the participant experience, such as helping determine the meaningfulness of change and inform refinement of a future efficacy trial, including how to improve recruitment strategies.51

Monitoring

Data management

All data are securely stored using HIPAA compliant servers and software approved for the use of protected health information. Additionally, data are deidentified on collection and stored under a study identification number. The Dana-Farber Cancer Institute’s IRB approved this study protocol.

Data monitoring

The principal investigator (SLB) will be responsible for the day-to-day oversight of data management activities and data sharing. DMS Plan compliance oversight and reporting will be handled by the principal investigator and institutional regulatory staff as part of general institutional stewardship, reporting and compliance processes for a minimal risk study.

Harms

Risk of discomfort or emotional distress

Although the IRB has deemed this a minimal risk study, it is possible that some participants may find it somewhat distressing to discuss or hear about problems related to sexual health and cancer. All participant sessions are conducted by a trained interventionist (Master’s level or above) with maximum sensitivity to such concerns. Participants who may want additional psychological support will be referred to the appropriate treatment and support services (eg, local mental health professional, social services, support group, etc).

Potential to be disappointed with being randomised to ISM

Recognising that some participants may be disappointed to be randomised to ISM, after completing the final assessment, participants in ISM will be offered access to the SHAREonline session.

Dissemination policy

In accordance with the NIH Data Management and Sharing policy, all scientific data and metadata generated from this project will be made available at the end of the study; data will be archived in a publicly available open access database. The duration of preservation and sharing of the data will be a minimum of 10 years after the funding period.

Privacy and confidentiality of human research subjects will be protected through the use of a deidentified dataset which will contain no Protected Health Information. All dates, such as the date of cancer diagnosis, will be provided as the year only. The study team will carefully review any demographic information in the dataset, and if it is determined that any information could be identifiable, will implement mitigation procedures (eg, reporting age as a range).

Discussion

Despite the substantial prevalence of significant treatment-related sexual sequelae, the majority of female cancer survivors do not receive appropriate care that addresses this fundamental aspect of physical, emotional and interpersonal well-being. Unfortunately, there remains a paucity of evidence-based interventions to address these problems. This study protocol describes the first theory-guided, sexual health intervention for younger female cancer survivors that is single session, group-delivered and presented via synchronous videoconference. Findings from this ORBIT phase IIa/IIb optimisation trial will be used to assess the preliminary effect of the intervention to address cancer-related sexual dysfunction, evaluate the operational viability of the trial design and set the stage for a fully powered efficacy trial. Potential moderator variables identified with exploratory analyses may also be tested prospectively in future efficacy trials. There are important strengths as well as limitations to underscore. Both the ultra-brief design and the biopsychosocial content of the intervention stand in contrast to previous female sexual health interventions, which are typically multisession, time-intensive and often either biomedical or psychosocial in nature. If successful, results from this developmental trial will support the hypothesis that a brief, group-delivered online intervention can provide a useful and effective format for sexual rehabilitation for young female cancer survivors. Furthermore, SHAREonline has the potential to be the first widely available, manualised sexual health intervention delivered by non-sexual health specialists (eg, nurses, health educators) in a variety of community-based settings, thus providing a much-needed resource to the majority of survivors who may not have access to sexual health rehabilitation. Additional strengths of the study include evaluating the credibility of a control condition (ie, ISM) where participants receive written resources overviewed by a trained research assistant. This active control condition represents a robust and pragmatic comparator which accounts for non-specific effects, such as attention and participant expectation. Finally, the trial includes patients from a range of cancers and is inclusive of women regardless of partner status, potentially leading to more generalisable findings. There are other potential limitations that should also be mentioned. SHAREonline is intended to reach a broad group of female survivors regardless of partner status or sexual orientation, yet outcome measures such as the gold-standard FSFI, though often used with cancer survivors, have items focusing on vaginal penetration, which may render the measure less relevant or potentially less accurate for both sexual minority women as well as women who are not sexually active when filling out the measure. It may be that other measures, such as assessment of sexual distress better capture meaningful change, especially for women who are not currently sexually active. It is also important to acknowledge that although the ISM arm represents a robust control condition, there is an imbalance regarding other factors such as time and peer interaction, which may also play a role. Finally, it should be noted that because participation is limited to individuals who can speak and read English, this may limit the cultural variability of participants in the study as well as the potential generalisability of findings.
