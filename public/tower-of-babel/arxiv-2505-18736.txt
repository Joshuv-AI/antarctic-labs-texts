Rethinking Direct Preference Optimization in Diffusion Models
Junyong Kang, Seohyun Lim, Kyungjune Baek, Hyunjung Shim†

Abstract
Aligning text-to-image (T2I) diffusion models with human preferences has emerged as a critical research challenge. While recent advances in this area have extended preference optimization techniques from large language models (LLMs) to the diffusion setting, they often struggle with limited exploration. In this work, we propose a novel and orthogonal approach to enhancing diffusion-based preference optimization. First, we introduce a stable reference model update strategy that relaxes the frozen reference model, encouraging exploration while maintaining a stable optimization anchor through reference model regularization. Second, we present a timestep-aware training strategy that mitigates the reward scale imbalance problem across timesteps. Our method can be integrated into various preference optimization algorithms. Experimental results show that our approach improves the performance of state-of-the-art methods on human preference evaluation benchmarks.

Introduction

Overview.
We propose an orthogonal method that can be applied to preference optimization algorithms. While the baseline method fails to align with human preferences, our solution enhances the alignment result through an exploration promotion and a timestep-aware training strategy.

Diffusion models
[
12
,
29
,
30
]
have emerged as a powerful generative framework, achieving remarkable success in Text-to-Image (T2I) generation
[
22
,
27
]
.
These models can synthesize high-fidelity, text-conditioned images by leveraging large-scale datasets of text-image pairs during training.
However, due to the uncurated nature of web-sourced training data, the output of such pre-trained models often misaligns with human preferences.

Motivated by recent advancements in aligning language models with human preferences
[
21
,
25
]
, recent works
[
3
,
9
,
35
,
7
,
23
,
31
,
18
,
37
,
41
]
have adapted similar post-training process to align diffusion models.
These methods generally fall into two categories: reward model-based fine-tuning
[
3
,
9
,
35
,
7
,
23
]
and preference optimization
[
31
,
18
,
37
,
41
]
.
The former approaches rely on large vision-language models trained to predict human preferences, such as PickScore
[
17
]
and ImageReward
[
35
]
.
However, these reward models often lead to a reward overoptimization problem
[
15
,
16
]
.
Preference optimization methods, on the other hand, align the model directly from preference data without reward models.
These methods construct loss functions using implicit rewards, which are defined by comparing likelihoods from the training model and a fixed reference model
[
25
,
1
,
8
]
.
For example, Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)
[
25
]
plugs the implicit rewards into the Bradley-Terry (BT) model to learn preference.
While most preference optimization methods are studied in the language domain, recent works
[
31
,
18
,
41
]
investigated various losses for preference optimization in diffusion models.
For example, Diffusion-DPO
[
31
]
adapts the DPO loss to the diffusion setting.

While existing preference algorithms only investigate preference loss formulations, we find that the model suffers from limited exploration, leading to suboptimal alignment results (Figure
1
).
In this work, we investigate an orthogonal direction of improving preference optimization for diffusion models.
Our method builds upon two strategies: (1) reference model update with regularization (Section
3.1
), (2) timestep-aware preference optimization (Section
3.2
).
The first strategy promotes exploration of the model by updating the reference model, motivated by recent works on language models
[
10
,
5
]
.
Our analysis, however, shows that naívely updating the reference model easily breaks the generative prior and leads to model degeneration.
To address this issue, we introduce a stability-enhancing algorithm, which regularizes the reference model’s divergence from the pretrained model.
This preserves the role of the reference model as a stable optimization anchor while enabling exploration.

The second strategy considers timestep dependent behaviors of diffusion during preference optimization.
Several prior works
[
2
,
4
,
32
]
have demonstrated that diffusion models synthesize semantic structures during early denoising steps while concentrating on texture details in later steps.
Motivated by this, we hypothesize that identifying and leveraging this temporal generation pattern can enhance preference alignment during training.

In standard diffusion training, the loss scale is significantly imbalanced across timesteps, so previous works prioritize earlier timesteps during the training stage for improved generation quality.
Similarly, we observe that the implicit reward scale is imbalanced across timesteps, making it harder to learn preferences at early stages.
To compensate for this imbalance, we propose a timestep-aware training strategy for preference optimization.
We first adopt a non-uniform distribution for sampling, oversampling the timestep variable from early steps when computing the loss function.
In addition, we propose a timestep-aware signal scale scheduling, which directly compensates for the imbalance in reward magnitudes across timesteps.
Utilizing these timestep-aware training strategies, we facilitate the preference optimization to learn preference signals in earlier timesteps.

Combining the reference model updating and the timestep-aware training strategies, our method yields significant improvement for Diffusion-DPO in terms of preference alignment performance (Section
4
).
Furthermore, our method is orthogonal and broadly applicable to various preference optimization algorithms, enhancing the performance of existing methods.
In summary, our contributions to this paper are as follows:

1.

We propose a novel direction for T2I preference alignment by introducing a stable reference model update and a timestep-aware training strategy that is orthogonal to existing algorithms.

2.

Our analysis provides new insights into reference model relaxation and timestep-dependent behavior of preference optimization in diffusion models, distinguishing from existing methods in language models.

3.

Our method achieves state-of-the-art performance when applied to Diffusion-DPO and also improves other preference optimization baselines.

2
Preliminaries

Diffusion Models.
Diffusion models are a class of generative models that learn to reverse a gradual noising process applied to data.
Following the DDPM
[
12
]
formulation, the forward process is defined as a Markov chain with a noise schedule
{\alpha_{t}}
, resulting in a sequence of latent variables
\boldsymbol{x}_{1:T}
:

q(\boldsymbol{x}_{1:T}|\boldsymbol{x}_{0})=\prod_{t=1}^{T}q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}|\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}),\quad\text{where}\quad q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}|\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1})=\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t};\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1},(1-\alpha_{t})\boldsymbol{I}).

(1)

The goal of the diffusion model is to learn a reverse process parameterized by a neural network
p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0:T})=p(\boldsymbol{x}_{T})\prod_{t=1}^{T}p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}_{t})
to obtain generated samples
p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})
.
Given
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\sim q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}|\boldsymbol{x}_{0})=\mathcal{N}(\bar{\alpha}_{t}\boldsymbol{x}_{0},(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t})\boldsymbol{I})
, where
\bar{\alpha}_{t}=\prod_{s=1}^{t}\alpha_{s}
, the model estimates the noise
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{I})
via
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t},t)
. The training objective is derived from the evidence lower bound (ELBO) on the data likelihood:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{DDPM}}=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\boldsymbol{\epsilon},t}\left[\lambda(t)\left||\boldsymbol{\epsilon}-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t},t)\right||_{2}^{2}\right],

(2)

where
t\sim\mathcal{U}(0,T)
and
\lambda(t)
denotes timestep-wise weighting function.
Recent works
[
6
,
11
,
38
]
suggest advanced weighting schedules for
\lambda(t)
to improve sample quality and convergence.

Preference Optimization in Diffusion.

To align the conditional distribution
p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}|\boldsymbol{c})
with human preferences, where
c\sim\mathcal{D}_{c}
denotes the prompt condition, RLHF methods
[
21
,
35
,
3
]
utilize a reward model
r(\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{x}_{0})
.
These methods aim to maximize the reward of the generated sample
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}
while keeping the distribution close to a reference distribution
p_{\text{ref}}
in terms of KL-divergence regularization:

\max_{p_{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{c}\sim\mathcal{D}_{c},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\sim p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}|\boldsymbol{c})}\left[r(\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{x}_{0})\right]-\beta\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}\left[p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}|\boldsymbol{c})\|p_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}|\boldsymbol{c})\right].

(3)

The reward model is typically learned from preference-annotated datasets under the Bradley-Terry model, where each data entry consists of a triplet
(c,\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{0},\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{0})
, representing a prompt, a preferred image, and a dispreferred image, respectively.
Rather than training a reward model, Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)
[
25
]
parametrizes the
implicit reward
using the current and the reference model:

r(\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{x}_{0})=\beta\log\frac{p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}|\boldsymbol{c})}{p_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}|\boldsymbol{c})},

(4)

where we omit the partition function
Z(\boldsymbol{c})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}p_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}|\boldsymbol{c})\exp\left(r(\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{x}_{0})/\beta\right)
as it does not contribute to the loss formulation.
Diffusion-DPO
[
31
]
expands the RLHF objective (Eq.
3
) into the diffusion trajectory
p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0:T})
, and then plugs the implicit reward into the Bradley-Terry model to obtain the following loss:

\mathcal{L}(\theta)=-\mathbb{E}_{(\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{0},\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{0})\sim\mathcal{D}}\log\!\sigma\!\biggl(\beta{\mathbb{E}}_{{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{1:T}\sim p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{1:T}|\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{0}),\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{1:T}\sim p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{1:T}|\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{0})\end{subarray}}}\left[\log\!\frac{p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{0:T})}{p_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{0:T})}\!-\!\log\!\frac{p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{0:T})}{p_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{0:T})}\right]\biggr)\ ,

(5)

which is intractable as the loss requires sampling from
p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0:T})
.
Note that we omit the prompt
c
for simplicity.
Utilizing Jensen’s inequality and approximating the reverse process
p_{\theta}
with the forward process
q
, Diffusion-DPO derives the final tractable loss:

\mathcal{L}(\theta)=-\mathbb{E}_{(\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{0},\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{0})\sim\mathcal{D},t\sim\mathcal{U}(0,T),\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{t}\sim q(\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{t}|\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{0}),\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{t}\sim q(\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{t}|\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{0})}\log\sigma\left(\beta T\lambda(t)\left(r_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{w})-r_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{l})\right)\right),

(6)

where we denote
r_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})=-(||\boldsymbol{\epsilon}-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t},t)\|^{2}_{2}-\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t},t)\|^{2}_{2})
.
From this approximation, we interpret
r_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})
as a timestep-wise implicit reward.
Thus, the above loss can be regarded as forcing the model to maximize the margin between
r_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{w})
and
r_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{l})
.

3
Method

Our approach aims to improve preference optimization in diffusion models.
To promote the model’s exploration behavior, we start by replacing the fixed reference model with the training model (Section
3.1
).
We observe that naively updating the reference model for exploration can lead to model degeneration.
To mitigate this issue, we propose regularizing the reference model by monitoring its divergence.
Unlike regularizing the training model, our approach enables it to explore solutions while maintaining generative capability.
Next, we delve into the timestep-dependent behavior of diffusion in preference optimization (Section
3.2
).
We propose a timestep-aware training strategy that deals with a reward scale imbalance problem, combining an earlier timestep oversampling method and a reward scale scheduling strategy.
Finally, we integrate the timestep-aware training strategy into the reference model update strategy to achieve a synergistic effect.

.

In DPO-based preference optimization methods
[
31
,
37
,
41
]
, the reference model
p_{\text{ref}}
acts as an optimization anchor, as the implicit reward
r_{t}(x_{0})
is defined from the log likelihood ratio of the training model and the reference model.
Typically, the reference model is set to the initial pre-trained model
p_{\text{init}}
.
In the language model alignment literature, TR-DPO
[
10
]
claims that a fixed reference model leads to a flatter loss landscape due to vanishing curvature.
While the model should absorb preference signals from new samples to avoid overfitting, loss flattening can hinder the exploration.

Following TR-DPO, our initial attempt is to periodically update the reference model every
\tau
training iterations to enable exploration.
In Figure
2
(a) and (b), we present example outputs from the fine-tuned model with the fixed reference and the updating reference with
\tau=32
.
We observe that naíve adaptation of the reference model update can lead to model degeneration, losing its ability to generate plausible images.
We hypothesize that this is because the frequent update of the reference model accelerates the deviation from the pre-trained model.
When the reference model starts to lose its generative capability due to the deviation, it fails to provide valid regularization during training, and thus the training model begins to degenerate.
Then, how can we safely handle the trade-off between exploration and training stability?

One possible solution is to add a new regularization to the training model to preserve its generative capability.
For example, this could be achieved by using the fixed initial pre-trained weight as a second reference model and applying an additional KL constraint to the training model.
However, adding a constraint to the training model can hinder its exploration behavior.
Instead, we consider
regularizing the reference model
.
Compared to the non-regularized reference model update, we restrict the divergence of the reference model from the initial pre-trained model (Figure
2
(c)).
This ensures that the reference model maintains its generative power, allowing the training model to explore further solutions while the reference model provides valid regularization.

To define the safe region for exploring the reference model, we calculate the divergence of the reference model
p_{\text{ref}}
from the initial pre-trained model
p_{\text{init}}
, denoted as
\mathbb{D}(p_{\text{ref}},p_{\text{init}})
.
Estimating the divergence requires computing the expectation with the joint distribution of
p_{\text{ref}}
or
p_{\text{init}}
across timesteps, which is intractable.
As discussed in Section
2
, we utilize the forward process
q
to approximate the divergence.
For example, the KL divergence can be approximated as follows:

\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}(p_{\text{ref}},p_{\text{init}})\approx\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\sim\mathcal{D},\,\boldsymbol{x}_{1:T}\sim q(\boldsymbol{x}_{1:T}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{0})}\bigl[\log\tfrac{p_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0:T})}{p_{\text{init}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0:T})}\bigr].

(7)

Using a similar derivation to Eq. (18) in
[
31
]
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}(p_{\text{ref}},p_{\text{init}})\approx T\ \mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\sim\mathcal{D},t,\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\sim q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}|\boldsymbol{x}_{0})}

\displaystyle\Bigl[\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}\!\bigl(q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}\!\mid\!\boldsymbol{x}_{0,t})\,\big\|p_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}\!\mid\!\boldsymbol{x}_{t})\bigr)

(8)

\displaystyle-\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}\!\bigl(q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}\!\mid\!\boldsymbol{x}_{0,t})\,\big\|p_{\text{init}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}\!\mid\!\boldsymbol{x}_{t})\bigr)\Bigr].

In other words, the divergence can be approximated by the difference in the diffusion training loss of each model.
We find that the KL approximation is sufficient to monitor the divergence, but the approximation error can produce negative values.
Therefore, we implement
\mathbb{D}(p_{\text{ref}},p_{\text{init}})
as the absolute value of the estimated divergence.
For the monitoring samples, we sample a small subset of preferred images
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{w}
from the training batch to reduce computational overhead.

After establishing the divergence metric, the next step is to choose a reference model that ensures training stability.
When the divergence of the current reference model
p_{\text{ref}}
exceeds a threshold
\delta
, we simply freeze the reference model near the
\delta
-boundary to prevent further updates.
We also explore a re-initialization option in Section
4.3
, which resets the reference model to the pre-trained model.

In standard diffusion model training, recent studies
[
6
,
38
]
have found that the model’s training dynamics differ across timesteps.
For example, Yu et al.
[
38
]
showed that as the model loss in Eq.
2
is significantly imbalanced across timesteps (Figure
3
(a)), using a constant
\lambda(t)
can cause the model to fail in learning to denoise early-timestep samples.

This imbalance problem is also present in our preference optimization setting.
To observe this, we conducted a simple analysis.
We utilize the model trained with our reference update policy and plot the average scale of implicit reward
r_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})
.
We divide the timestep range
[0,T]
into 10 intervals and randomly sample 500 image pairs from the Pick-a-Pic v2
[
17
]
validation set for each interval.
As demonstrated in Figure
3
(b), the scale of
r_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})
is also imbalanced.
To facilitate preference optimization in the earlier timesteps, we must address this reward scale imbalance problem.

Oversampling.
First, we adopt a sampling approach proposed by Yang et al.
[
37
]
.
Instead of sampling
t
from a uniform distribution as in Eq.
6
, this approach samples
t
from a categorical distribution
\text{Cat}({\gamma^{t}})
with the probability vector
\{\gamma^{t}/\sum_{t^{\prime}}\gamma^{t^{\prime}}\}
, where
\gamma\in[0,1]
.
This approach tackles the imbalance problem by oversampling timesteps where the reward scale is small.

Reward Scale Scheduling.
However, we empirically find that the sampling approach alone is not sufficient to adequately mitigate the scale imbalance problem (Section
4.3
).
We introduce a scale schedule to weight the implicit reward, directly compensating for the imbalance.
While Eq.
6
already presents the weighting schedule
\lambda(t)
, it has been ignored in previous works and treated as a constant
[
31
,
41
]
.
We design
\lambda(t)
to decrease over timesteps to balance the small scale in the earlier timesteps.
However,
\lambda(t)
also controls the implicit KL regularization
\beta
, assigning too small values for
\lambda(t)
in later timesteps can lead to model degeneration.
To address this, we must upper-bound
\lambda(t)
with a sufficiently large value.
Specifically, we define
\lambda(t)=1+\alpha\cdot\text{norm}(1/\sqrt{SNR(t)})
, where
SNR(t)
denotes the signal-to-noise ratio,
\text{norm}(\cdot)
indicates the normalization operator over time, and
\alpha>0
is a hyperparameter for scale range.
We plot this schedule in Figure
3
(c), comparing it with the constant schedule.

In summary, our method consists of the stabilized reference model update strategy, skewed timestep sampling, and timestep-aware reward scale control.
We also note that our method can be applied to other variants of preference optimization methods that utilize reference models and corresponding regularization coefficients.
In the next section, we investigate how our method can benefit various preference optimization algorithms, including Diffusion-DPO
[
31
]
, Diffusion-KTO
[
18
]
, and DSPO
[
41
]
.

4.1
Experimental Setup

Dataset.

Following prior works
[
31
,
18
]
, we use Pick-a-Pic v2 train dataset
[
17
]
for training.
The Pick-a-Pic v2 training set comprises about 900K training image pairs with approximately 58k distinct prompts.
Images were ranked by human evaluators, consisting of a preferred and a nonpreferred image for each given input prompt.
For evaluation, we employ test set prompts from the Pick-a-Pic v2 dataset
[
17
]
(MIT license), HPDv2 benchmark
[
34
]
(Apache-2.0 license), and the PartiPrompts dataset
[
39
]
(Apache-2.0 license).

Evaluation Protocol.

To quantitatively evaluate the performance of the proposed method, we adopt five reward models as evaluation metrics: PickScore
[
17
]
, HPSv2
[
34
]
, ImageReward
[
35
]
(Apache-2.0 license), CLIP
[
24
]
(MIT license), and Aesthetics Score
[
28
]
(MIT license).
For each reward model, we compare the win rates of our method and baseline approaches, each against the pre-trained model.
The win rate is the proportion of images with higher reward scores than those generated by the baseline model, under the same random seed.

Baseline Methods.

We utilize three baseline preference optimization algorithms, Diffusion-DPO
[
31
]
, Diffusion-KTO
[
18
]
, and DSPO
[
41
]
.
We reproduce each of the methods, and then orthogonally apply our method.
For comparison, we also employ public checkpoints for Diffusion-DPO (Apache-2.0 license) and Diffusion-KTO.
When reproducing these methods, we maintain consistency by employing the same hyperparameters from the original paper.
We also employ supervised fine-tuning (SFT) as a baseline, but we exclusively use the winning images, while previous studies have utilized both winning and losing images.

Implementation Details.

In this paper, we conduct experiments on Stable Diffusion v1.5 (SD1.5)
[
26
]
(CreativeML Open
RAIL-M license) and SDXL
[
22
]
(MIT license).
For the training, we employ AdamW optimizer with a batch size of 2048 and the learning rate of
2.048\times 10^{-8}
.
The total number of iterations to train models is set to 1000 for SD1.5 and 600 for SDXL.
In our reference model update methods, the period of the reference model update is set to
\tau\in\{32,64,128\}
steps, with the optimal hyperparameter selected for each method.
We make an exception for Diffusion-KTO as they employ a batch size of 8 and 10k training iterations.
In this case, we extend the training iterations to 50k to achieve better performance and set
\tau=4096
.
The monitoring divergence threshold
\delta
is empirically determined and is set to
0.005
for SD1.5 and
0.003
for SDXL.
For the timestep-aware training strategy, we set the discount factor
\gamma
for the timestep sampling to
0.9
and the reward scale hyperparameter
\alpha\in\{1,2\}
.
Other details and hyperparameters are provided in Appendix
A
.

5
Related Work

5.1
RLHF in Diffusion Models

Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) has proven highly effective in aligning human preference in the large language model domain
[
21
,
20
]
. Recently, similar approaches have been explored in the T2I diffusion domain, leveraging human feedback and various quality metrics as reward signals.
Previous works in RLHF to diffusion models have re-formulated the diffusion process as a Markov Decision Process (MDP).
DDPO
[
3
]
and DPOK
[
9
]
compute rewards at the final timestep and apply policy gradient method to fine-tune the model.
Alternatively, methods such as ReFL
[
35
]
, DRaFT
[
7
]
, and AlignProp
[
23
]
propose differentiable reward frameworks, enabling direct policy updates through backpropagation.

5.2
Direct Preference Optimization

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)
[
25
]

has emerged as a promising alternative to RLHF, because it obviates the need to train a separate reward model.
Building on the success of DPO, numerous variants have recently been explored in the language domain
[
1
,
10
,
19
,
33
,
13
,
40
]
.

DPO has also been extended to diffusion models to enhance alignment between generated images and human preferences. Notably, Diffusion-DPO
[
31
]
and D3PO
[
36
]
adapt the DPO loss to diffusion models.
Diffusion-KTO
[
18
]
substitutes the standard DPO loss with Kahneman-Tversky Optimization (KTO), training with single-instance data without requiring pairwise comparisons.
Meanwhile, some recent works consider the innate structure of diffusion models instead of naively applying the language model losses. Yang et al.,
[
37
]
modifies the uniform timestep sampling in Diffusion-DPO, deriving the loss from the densely defined rewards across timesteps. DSPO
[
41
]
fine-tunes diffusion models by aligning the output distribution with human preferences using score matching principles.

6
Conclusion and Limitations

We present a novel training framework for enhancing preference alignment in diffusion models.
Our method consists of two components: (1) reference model updates regularized by divergence constraints to enable stable exploration, and (2) timestep-aware training that addresses reward scale imbalance across denoising steps. The proposed strategies operate orthogonally to existing loss functions and can be integrated into a range of preference optimization methods. Experiments show consistent gains across baselines and benchmarks, establishing new state-of-the-art performance.
Although experimental results demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed method, a deeper theoretical analysis of the exploration behavior and the temporal dynamics of our approach remains unexplored.
Nevertheless, our approach opens new research directions for future research in diffusion preference alignment beyond loss function design.

Appendix C
Further ablation study

C.1
Analysis on Timestep-aware Training Strategy

We further analyze our timestep-aware training strategy.
Specifically, we compare the timestep-wise KL divergence approximation from the initial pre-trained model, with and without our timestep-aware training method.
In each timestep interval, we visualize the relative values, setting the divergence of the reference model with the update strategy only to 100%.
As seen in Figure
S1
, applying oversampling to the reference update strategy results in a sharp divergence at earlier timesteps.
While higher divergence allows for more exploration, excessive deviation from the initial model can harm the generative capability.
Compared to the constant baseline, our reward scale scheduling approach shows lower divergence, even though it achieves a higher score than the constant baseline.
This is because explicitly amplifying the rewards in earlier timesteps helps the model learn preference signals without changing its original scale range.
Since the
\beta
term controls implicit regularization, we can also interpret our reward scale scheduling as further regularizing our model in earlier timesteps.

Figure S1
:
Comparison of divergence before and after applying reward scale scheduling.

C.2
Analysis on Monitoring Threshold

We conduct experiments across varying the monitoring threshold
\delta
, which defines the safe region for the reference model update strategy.
Table
S4
presents results for three different
\delta
, based on Diffusion-DPO and SD1.5, evaluated on the Pick-a-Pic v2 test prompts.
The results show that
\delta=0.005
achieves the best performance, while both excessively small or large
\delta
lead to performance degradation.
With a small
\delta
, it does not allow the model to explore enough, while a large
\delta
fails to provide valid regularization for the reference model.
Although we experimentally choose
\delta
based on this trade-off, the optimal value of
