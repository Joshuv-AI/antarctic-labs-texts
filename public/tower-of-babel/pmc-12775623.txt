Resolution of hypertension symptoms following cerebrospinal fluid leak with acetazolamide: a case report
Authors: Elizabeth Raulston, Maya Bhutwala, Kyle Kesler
Published: 2025

Highlight box

Key findings

• This case report suggests that paradoxical intracranial hypertension (ICH) may be an underrecognized complication after cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leak repair in spine surgery. Clinicians should be vigilant for atypical headache patterns and consider high intracranial pressure as a potential cause, as prompt treatment with acetazolamide can lead to a quick and lasting recovery.

What is known and what is new?

• Incidental durotomy and subsequent CSF leaks are a recognized complication of spine surgery, with an incidence ranging from 1% to 17%.

• This report highlights a key diagnostic clue: the shift in headache characteristics. The patients initially had positional, low-pressure headaches but later developed headaches that were paradoxically worse when lying down, suggesting a transition from intracranial hypotension to hypertension.

What is the implication, and who should change now?

• Clinicians should recognize that a patient’s symptoms, following CSF leak, can evolve from classic low-pressure CSF headaches to high-pressure ICH, presenting as supine-worsening or nocturnal headaches, tinnitus, and visual changes, even after a CSF leak has been successfully treated. In such cases, treatment with acetazolamide—a carbonic anhydrase inhibitor that reduces CSF production—has led to rapid and sustained resolution of otherwise refractory symptoms. A short, structured taper proved effective and well tolerated, suggesting a reproducible management strategy for this clinical scenario.

Introduction

Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leaks are a known complication of spine surgery, occurring in approximately 1% to 17% of procedures, depending on the complexity and type of surgery performed (1). While many leaks resolve with primary repair, persistent or recurrent leaks can lead to significant morbidity, including orthostatic headaches and, in rare cases, paradoxical intracranial hypertension (ICH) (2,3). Although intracranial hypotension is the more commonly expected consequence of a CSF leak, ICH has been observed following leak resolution and dural healing, possibly due to CSF overproduction or altered CSF dynamics (4). Symptoms of ICH including headache, visual disturbances, tinnitus, and dizziness may be underrecognized in postoperative spine patients, especially when presenting after a resolved CSF leak (5). Acetazolamide, a carbonic anhydrase inhibitor, is used in the management of ICH as it reduces CSF production by inhibiting carbonic anhydrase in the choroid plexus and has been shown to alleviate symptoms and reduce intracranial pressure in conditions such as idiopathic ICH (6). Literature supporting the paradoxical ICH following prolonged CSF leaks and its treatment with Acetazolamide is very sparse (7,8). Recognizing this treatment pathway may provide an important option for managing persistent headaches after CSF leak repair in the spine surgery population. We present this case in accordance with the CARE reporting checklist (available at https://jss.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jss-25-103/rc) (9).

Case presentation

Case summary 1

Case 1 was a healthy 46-year-old male, actively employed at the time of a motor vehicle collision that resulted in a lumbar disc herniation (Figure 1). He underwent an outpatient single-level microdiscectomy and initially reported substantial symptomatic improvement at his 30 days postoperative visit. Soon after he developed new-onset, positional headaches that improved when supine and worsened upon sitting or standing. He also noted clear fluid drainage from the surgical site. Clinical evaluation confirmed a postoperative CSF leak, presumed to be incidental to the index surgery, requiring hospital admission for surgical dural repair (Figure 2). Following reoperation, he again reported symptomatic improvement but later experienced a recurrent disc herniation that necessitated a conversion to instrumented spinal fusion. During his postoperative recovery, the patient developed persistent headaches that paradoxically worsened when lying down, particularly during the early hours of the morning. He also reported intermittent tinnitus, nausea, dizziness, and fluctuating visual disturbances atypical of low-pressure CSF-related headaches. Despite extended conservative management, his symptoms remained refractory. A computed tomography (CT) myelogram was obtained and revealed elevated intradural opening pressure, and therapeutic CSF drainage during the procedure led to immediate, albeit temporary, relief of his symptoms. He was treated with a 1-month course of acetazolamide 250 mg twice daily followed by tapering in milligrams and frequency with rapid and sustained resolution of symptoms even after completion of acetazolamide. The medication taper was performed after 1 month of treatment by decreasing the daily dose by 125 mg each week until cessation.

Case summary 2

Case 2 was a 55-year-old female who initially underwent a revision lumbar spinal fusion surgery (Figure 3) for distal segment breakdown (Figure 4), foraminal stenosis, and a medial breached screw from a prior outside facility surgery. While the procedure was technically successful, an incidental durotomy occurred intraoperatively and was repaired with a single watertight suture. On postoperative day 1, the patient developed severe positional headaches that worsened when upright and resolved when supine both clinical features consistent with a CSF leak. She was returned to the operating room, where persistent leakage from the original dural repair was confirmed. A second watertight repair was performed; however, the patient experienced recurrence of her positional headaches, necessitating a third surgical intervention with dural patch application and diverting lumbar drain placement. She remained flat in the intensive care unit for dural drain management for 1 week, during which her activity gradually increased and drain output was monitored. With resolution of her positional headaches, she was discharged in stable condition. In the months following discharge, she began to experience nocturnally predominant headaches, most severe during the early morning hours. These headaches partially improved with upright positioning but remained persistent and low-grade, responding partially to acetaminophen. Symptom recurrence upon withdrawal of acetaminophen, along with associated tinnitus and intermittent visual disturbances resembling those seen in case 1, prompted consideration of ICH and initiation of a similar treatment protocol with acetazolamide. Her ICH symptoms quickly resolved with durable relief following cessation of acetazolamide.

Discussion

Incidental durotomy tear subsequent to CSF leaks is a well-documented complication that occurs during spine surgical procedures (10). While most postoperative CSF leaks resolve with primary repair, a subset of patients experience persistent or recurrent symptoms, including debilitating headaches. The more familiar clinical sequelae of CSF leaks involve intracranial hypotension, manifesting as orthostatic headaches that improve in the supine position. However, these two cases illustrate a lesser-known and paradoxical progression toward ICH following resolution of the CSF leak. Although rare, the development of ICH after treatment of a CSF leak has been previously documented in case series and imaging-based studies (3,4). The proposed mechanisms include compensatory CSF overproduction, changes in compliance within the craniospinal axis, and altered CSF absorption dynamics. The resulting elevation in intracranial pressure may mimic idiopathic ICH with clinical features such as tinnitus, visual disturbances, and nocturnal predominant headaches (5). In both patients described, symptom onset occurred after the apparent resolution of a CSF leak, with CT myelography in one case confirming elevated intradural pressure and transient relief following diagnostic CSF drainage. Acetazolamide, a carbonic anhydrase inhibitor, is the mainstay of treatment for idiopathic intracranial hypertension, ICH and other conditions involving elevated intracranial pressure. It reduces CSF production by targeting the enzyme activity within the choroid plexus (6). Both patients in this series demonstrated rapid and durable resolution of symptoms following treatment with a standard course of acetazolamide followed by a structured taper. While the literature on acetazolamide used in post-durotomy ICH is sparse, our findings support its therapeutic potential in this emerging clinical context. Differentiating between low- and high-pressure CSF-related headaches can be challenging, particularly in patients with prior surgical repair of a CSF leak. Standard imaging techniques, such as CT or magnetic resonance myelography, may aid in evaluating persistent leaks, but in cases where imaging is inconclusive, elevated opening pressure or symptom response to CSF drainage may provide key diagnostic insights (11). Importantly, the resolution of symptoms following CSF removal in case 1 helped pivot the clinical reasoning toward paradoxical hypertension, reinforcing the need to consider altered CSF dynamics even after presumed leak resolution. This case series emphasizes the importance of clinical vigilance in patients with atypical headache patterns following spinal surgery and dural repair. While further study is needed, these cases suggest that paradoxical ICH may be an underrecognized phenomenon in the postoperative spine population and that timely intervention with acetazolamide may offer significant clinical benefit.

Conclusions

ICH appears to be a delayed and inconsistent complication following successful repair of postoperative CSF leaks. In both patients, rapid and sustained symptom resolution was achieved with acetazolamide, underscoring its therapeutic potential in this context. Clinicians should maintain a high alert when evaluating persistent or evolving headaches after dural repair, as early recognition of the transition from low- to high-pressure CSF states can enable targeted treatment. Paradoxical ICH should be considered in patients with new or persistent headaches after CSF leak repair, as timely diagnosis and acetazolamide treatment can lead to rapid and lasting recovery.
