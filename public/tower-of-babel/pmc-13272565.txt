A culturomics biobank decodes extremophile evolution and metabolism in acid mine drainage
Authors: Xiu-Tong Li, Xuan Zhang, Zong-Lin Liang, Zhen Jiang, Ye Huang, Yu-Qian Han, Ze-Bao Tan, Ying-Liu, Zheng-Hua Liu, Hua-qun Yin, Shuang-Jiang Liu, Cheng-Ying Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Extreme environments such as acid mine drainage (AMD) host highly specialized microbial communities that drive profound biogeochemical cycles. Within these ecosystems, iron- and sulfur-metabolizing taxa catalyze mineral weathering, generating intense acidity and mobilizing heavy metals. However, more than 97% of these microorganisms remain uncultured "microbial dark matter," heavily restricting our understanding of extremophile metabolism and adaptation. Here we present the Microbial Biobank of AMD (mbAMD), a culturomics-derived collection of 652 isolates spanning 42 species—including 21 novel taxa—that achieves 86.7% coverage of the global AMD core microbiome. Functional validation demonstrates that 36 of these taxa possess active iron or sulfur metabolic capacities, including the discovery of the first pure cultures of acid-tolerant sulfate reducers. Comparative genomic analyses across these isolates reveal that extreme environmental adaptation is predominantly driven by pervasive horizontal gene transfer. Specifically, extremophiles preferentially acquire adaptive genes governing acid tolerance and metal resistance from phylogenetically proximal relatives rather than distant donors. These findings elucidate the modular evolutionary strategies of extremophiles and provide critical functional resources for advancing biohydrometallurgy and environmental bioremediation. This mbAMD resource will accelerate biohydrometallurgical process optimization and environmental bioremediation strategies while advancing evolutionary microbial ecology research.

Introduction

Acid mine drainage (AMD) ecosystems develop through the biogeochemical weathering of sulfide minerals exposed to air, water, and microbial communities [1]. Due to the reduced complexity of these communities, caused by low pH (pH < 3), elevated metal concentrations, and restricted electron donors and acceptors [2], AMD has been positioned as a model biotope for investigating microbial adaptation to extreme conditions [3], particularly in resolving energy transduction pathways [4] and element cycling mechanisms [5].

Acidophilic microbes, particularly those capable of oxidizing iron and sulfur, play a significant role in the formation of AMD. Ferrous ions remain stable under abiotic acidic conditions; however, acidophilic iron-oxidizing microorganisms—either autotrophic or facultatively autotrophic—can oxidize ferrous ions into ferric ions. This oxidation process initiates the attack by ferric ions and/or hydrogen protons on metal sulfides, generating energy-rich reduced inorganic sulfur compounds (RISCs), which are ultimately oxidized to sulfate by sulfur-oxidizing autotrophic microorganisms. These microbes significantly accelerate mineral oxidation and AMD formation compared with abiotic controls [6]. The prevalence of heterotrophic iron- or sulfate-reducing species suggests their involvement in iron/sulfur cycling and the assimilation of organic substances toxic to strictly autotrophic bacteria [4]. Microbes in AMD are functionally categorized into four metabolic guilds: sulfur oxidizers, iron oxidizers, iron reducers, and sulfate reducers. These functional groups play critical roles in the biogeochemical cycling of carbon, nitrogen, iron, and sulfur within AMD environments while driving mineral bioleaching processes and contributing to mine pollution remediation.

Nevertheless, current taxonomic cultivation efforts have identified only approximately 40 functional species and cover a restricted phylogenetic range. These species include members of the genera Acidianus, Acidiferrobacter, Acidimicrobium, Acidiphilium, Acidiplasma, Acidithiobacillus, Alicyclobacillus, Ferroplasma, Ferrimicrobium, Ferrithrix, Leptospirillum, Metallosphaera, Sulfobacillus, Sulfolobus, Sulfurococcus, Thiomonas, among others [7]. These cultured representatives constitute less than 2.7% of the metagenomic diversity in AMD ecosystems (58/2116 MAGs, n = 226 samples) [8], leaving more than 97.2% of microbial dark matter (MDM) uncharacterized. The primary means of deciphering MDM's functional roles, energy acquisition strategies, metabolic mechanisms, and complex interactions with other organisms and environments relies on isolating and culturing additional uncultured strains [9].

Cultivation challenges for AMD microorganisms are caused by stringent physiological requirements (e.g., pH homeostasis, metal resistance mechanisms, and electron donors/acceptors) and syntrophic dependencies [3]. Culturomics is a transformative approach to MDM exploration that involves designing appropriate media and optimal culture conditions, implementing in situ cultivation techniques to construct natural microenvironments, developing advanced culture technologies (e.g., high-throughput single-droplet sorting), and integrating mass spectrometry and single-cell genomic sequencing for species identification [10].

In this study, we designed 12 distinct culture conditions to isolate functional species from AMD samples. All microbial resources obtained were carefully preserved, and their genomes were sequenced, thereby establishing a robust foundation for subsequent in-depth functional and evolutionary investigations. The iron and sulfur metabolic capabilities of each strain were evaluated using serial culture tests, enabling assessment of their biogeochemical potential. In addition, comparative genomics was used to elucidate the evolutionary mechanisms of the isolated AMD strains. Notably, this analysis revealed a novel pattern of horizontal gene transfer (HGT) exclusive to extreme environments, shedding light on the genomic plasticity of microbes in acidic, metal-rich niches. Following a comprehensive analysis of phylogenetic relationships, morphological characteristics, physiological and chemotaxonomic profiles, and genomic datasets, taxonomic and nomenclatural proposals are put forward for the newly isolated species. Moreover, a holistic overview of AMD species is presented, providing a system-level understanding of the microbial composition and function within the AMD ecosystem. In summary, this study leverages culturomics to significantly expand the cultured biobank of microorganisms from extreme environments. It not only decodes the functional repertoire of MDM in AMD ecosystems but also provides novel insights into the evolutionary trajectories of extremophiles.

Materials and methods

Functional isolation

To investigate and isolate microorganisms from AMD and associated sediments, we collected samples from multiple mining sites in China. On September 22, 2017, six AMD samples and 11 sediment samples were collected from two naturally formed acid mine water pools in the Bainiuchang polymetallic mining area of the Mengzi Copper Mine (103.5° N, 23.3° E) in Yunnan Province. All samples were collected at a depth of 0–10 cm. In October 2018, we collected an additional six AMD samples and six sediment samples from naturally formed acid mine water pools in the Dabaoshan Multi-Metallic Mine (113.7° N, 24.5° E) in Guangdong Province, also at a depth of 0–10 cm. In the same month, nine water samples were collected from AMD collection ponds located in the bioleaching heap area of the Zijin Copper Mine (116.4° N, 25.2° E) in Fujian Province (Supplementary Fig. S1a). Detailed environmental factors for these samples have been described in a previous study [11]. Each 5 mL of AMD water or 5 g of sediment were incubated in 100 mL of medium from six systems (Supplementary Fig. S1b and Table S1). Cultures were incubated for 1 month at either 30 or 45 °C, with aerobic cultures shaken at 150 rpm in Erlenmeyer flasks and anaerobic cultures maintained in sealed bottles.

The components of the basal salt solution of the enrichment medium (BSS1) and the isolation medium (BSS2) were described in our previous study [12]. The pH and nutrient profiles of the media from the six enrichment systems are provided in Supplementary Table S1. Filter-sterilized yeast extract, glucose, FeSO4, K2S4O6, and tyndallized sulfur powder were added after autoclaving, and the final concentrations are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

The enriched cultures were subjected to low-speed centrifugation (200 rpm) to remove impurities, then serially diluted to 10−4, 10−5, and 10−6 based on biomass counts determined with a hemocytometer under a microscope. Subsequently, clones were obtained using two approaches: microfluidic technology and the traditional spread plate method, both on Isolation Plate medium (Supplementary Table S1), solidified with 1.2% gellan gum (Gelrite, Sigma-Aldrich), and incubated at the same temperature as the enrichment process.

The observed colonies were purified by repeated streaking on plates and preliminarily identified by 16S rRNA gene sequencing (Supplementary Fig. S1c). All isolated strains were collected and preserved using two preservation strategies: cryopreservation in glycerol at either −80 °C or in liquid nitrogen, and lyophilization followed by storage at 4 °C at the China General Microbiological Culture Collection Center, with type strains of potential novel species stored at the Korean Collection for Type Cultures or the Japan Collection of Microorganisms (Supplementary Fig. S1d).

Morphological, physiological, and chemotaxonomic analyses

Cell morphology was observed using a transmission electron microscope (JEM-1400, JEOL, Akishima, Japan) on cells grown in the exponential phase. The redox capacity of iron(II/III), sulfur oxyanions (S2O32−/S4O62−/SO32−/SO42−), and elemental sulfur of the strains was examined with reference to a previous study [12]. Gram staining was performed using an HYCX Gram staining kit (cat. no. G1060-100; HYCX, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

The growth temperature range was tested from 15 to 60 °C in 5 °C increments. The growth pH range was examined by adjusting the pH to between 0.5 and 6.0 at 0.5 intervals using 3 mol L−1 H2SO4 or 1 mol L−1 NaOH. Salinity tolerance was determined at a 0–5.0% (w/v) NaCl concentration range at 0.5 intervals. Antibiotic resistance was tested by treating filter paper disks with the same antibiotics used in our previous study [12]. Enzyme activities, carbon assimilation, acid production via fermentation, and other physiological properties were characterized using the API ZYM, API 50CH, and API 20NE kits with freshly collected cells, following the manufacturers’ instructions and our previous work [13]. Catalase and oxidase activities were detected by adding 5% H2O2 solution or the OX agent in the API 20NE kit to the cells.

Cells were cultured in 200 mL of liquid medium until they reached the exponential growth phase, then harvested by centrifugation and transferred to a 50 mL centrifuge tube. Fatty acids were subjected to saponification and methylation using methanol–NaOH and methanol–HCl solutions, respectively. Subsequently, fatty acid methyl esters were extracted with a hexane/methyl tert-butyl ether solution, along with NaOH and saturated NaCl solutions. The organic phase was analyzed by gas chromatography using an HP 6890 Series system (Hewlett-Packard, Palo Alto, CA, USA) [14,15]. Total lipids and respiratory quinones were extracted from lyophilized cells and analyzed using two-dimensional TLC plates (20 cm × 20 cm silica gel; Merck, Rahway, NJ, USA) [16,17] and HPLC equipped with a ZOBAX ODS C18 column (4.6 × 150 mm; Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA) [18], respectively (Supplementary Fig. S1f).

Genomic sequencing and data processing

Genomic DNA from the strains was extracted using the Wizard Genomic DNA Purification Kit (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) and sequenced on Illumina and PacBio Sequel platforms at Guangdong Magigene Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China) (Supplementary Fig. S1e). The sequencing depth was 400× for Illumina and 1200× for PacBio Sequel. Raw data were trimmed using fastp [19] to remove adapters, duplicates, and low-quality bases, and quality was assessed using FastQC with Q20 > 95% [20]. The resulting clean data were assembled using the spades.py script in --isolate mode [21]. Prodigal [22] was used to predict coding sequences. Genome annotation was performed using Prokka with the --kingdom Bacteria parameter [23]. We used QUAST to calculate the genome size and GC content using the --gene-finding parameter [24]. tRNAscan-SE with the parameter -B was used to identify tRNA [25]; rRNA was annotated with RNAmmer using the parameters -S bac and -multi [26]; and the cmscan program of Infernal 1.1 [27] was used to scan for sRNA in the genomes based on the Rfam database with the parameters --cut_ga, --rfam, and --nohmmonly [28]. Prophages were detected using the PhiSpy algorithm [29], and CRISPR repeats were identified using PILER-CR [30]. Genomic islands were predicted using the IslandPath-DIMOB Perl script Dimob.pl [31].

Phylogeny analysis

The 16S rRNA gene was amplified by polymerase chain reaction using primers (27F: 5′-AGAGTTTGATCMTGGCTCAG-3′ and 1492R: 5′-ACGGHTACCTTGTTACGACTT-3′). The amplification products were purified and sequenced. The full-length 16S rRNA gene sequence (approximately 1540 bp) was compared with sequences available from the EZBio Cloud and the National Center for Biotechnology Information GenBank database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genbank/). A maximum-likelihood tree based on 16S rRNA gene sequences of the type strains of each species was constructed in MEGA 7 [32], with partial gap deletion (95% cutoff). Multiple sequence alignments were performed using MUSCLE [33]. Model testing was based on the lowest corrected Akaike information criterion [34]; the General Time Reversible model was used with a gamma distribution (five discrete gamma categories) and invariant sites (G + I). The culture conditions and sources of the isolated strains were plotted on heat maps using STAMP software [35].

Comparative genomic analysis

Phylogenomic analysis was performed using GTDB-Tk v2.3.2 [36,37]. The classify_wf pipeline first identified 120 conserved single-copy core genes (bac120 marker set) from the 49 sequenced genomes (Supplementary Table S2) through homology searches against the Pfam v33.1 and TIGRFAMs v15.0 databases. These marker genes were extracted, aligned, and concatenated into a multisequence alignment. Genome assembly quality was assessed based on continuity (contig count <200, N50 >50 kb, largest contig >100 kb, and total length and GC content comparable to the reference strain) and completeness (all genomes >95% as determined by CheckM). Plasmids were identified using the IPGA v1.09 web server (https://nmdc.cn/ipga/). Genomic information, including GC content, genome size, number of genes, prophages, CRISPR arrays, genomic islands, tRNAs, sRNAs, 5S, 16S, and 23S rRNAs, was ranked according to the corresponding strains using iTOL [38]. Comparative genomic analysis was performed using the 49 sequenced genomes and all published reference genomes for each genus or family representative in GenBank with IPGA v1.09 (https://nmdc.cn/ipga/). The reference genomes used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S3. The FeGenie database was used to identify iron genes and iron gene neighborhoods in the genomes [39]. SCycDB was used to identify genes involved in sulfur cycling pathways [40]. Gene screening from the FeGenie and SCycDB databases was performed using a combination of profile hidden Markov models (HMMs) and Diamond-based sequence homology searches. First, protein sequences predicted from the bacterial genomes were compared with the curated HMM profiles and sequence alignments in the respective databases using HMMER. Subsequently, the initial hits and genes with high sequence similarity to curated references for iron (FeGenie) and sulfur (SCycDB) cycling genes were validated by Diamond. The functional and localization information of the proteins was checked on the KEGG (https://www.genome.jp/kegg/) and UniProt (https://www.uniprot.org) websites (Supplementary Fig. S1g). Putative HGT events were identified in the 49 genomes using HGTector2, with genomes of the same family assigned as “self-tax” and order-level assignments as “close-tax” [41]. The candidate proteins and their corresponding HGT donors were matched to BLAST results using Perl scripts. Directed networks were used to show the interactive relationships between donors and acceptors in Gephi 0.10.1, using the Force Atlas layout (Supplementary Fig. S1h) [42].

Results

Microbial biobank of acid mine drainage constructed by culturomics

Through culturomics, this study established an AMD microbial biobank (mbAMD) comprising 652 phylogenetically distinct strains isolated from AMD samples from three mining sites: the Mengzi Copper Mine (147 strains), the Dabaoshan Multi-Metallic Mine (338 strains), and the Zijin Copper Mine (167 strains) (Fig. 1). 16S rRNA gene sequence identity, average nucleotide identity, and genomic digital DNA–DNA hybridization were employed as key indicators for taxonomic classification, with thresholds of <98.7%, 95%, and 70%, respectively, to define novel species. Taxonomic classification showed that these strains belonged to 42 species, 22 genera, and 11 families, including 16 novel species, four new genera, and a previously undescribed family. Phylogenetic analysis positioned these taxa within Pseudomonadota, Acidobacteria, Nitrospirota, Actinomycetota, and Bacillota. Notably, Pseudomonadota dominated both diversity and abundance in the Mengzi Copper Mine and Dabaoshan Multi-Metallic Mine samples, whereas the Zijin samples exhibited Nitrospirota as the most abundant phylum alongside Pseudomonadota's persistent diversity dominance, which is consistent with metadata results [11].Fig. 1Phylogenetic tree constructed using the maximum-likelihood method based on the 16S rRNA gene sequences of the representative strains of the microbial biobank of acid mine drainage (mbAMD). Identification of novel taxa

Among candidate novel taxa identified through a 16S rRNA gene sequence divergence identity threshold of less than 98.7% [11,43] species were validated as new through polyphasic characterization integrating phylogenetic analysis, biochemical profiling, and comparative genomics (Supplementary Fig. S1). These taxa were formally described in accordance with the International Code of Nomenclature of Prokaryotes, with complete protological data cataloged in Table 1. Morphological characterization revealed rod-shaped morphotypes bearing different numbers of flagella or pili under different substrates (Supplementary Fig. S2). The results confirmed that ferrous ion (Fe2+) and elemental sulfur (S0) were the predominant substrates metabolized by the novel taxa, highlighting their important roles in AMD bioenergetics. For instance, members of the genera Acidithiobacillus and Sulfobacillus function as key sulfur/iron oxidizers; certain species additionally utilize Fe3+ and thiosulfate as energy sources. Within the family Acetobacteraceae, representatives of Acidiphilium and Rhodopila were identified as heterotrophic iron reducers. In contrast to their relatives commonly associated with spoiled fruit-based beverages, AMD-derived Alicyclobacillus species exhibited mixotrophic growth, utilizing yeast extract in combination with inorganic sulfur and/or ferrous ions. These findings highlight the diverse metabolic strategies employed by microbial communities to thrive in AMD environments. Substrate-dependent variations in appendage frequency suggested adaptive strategies for nutrient scavenging and energy transduction in oligotrophic environments [12]. Physiological assessments revealed enhanced antibiotic resistance in AMD isolates compared with reference strains from non-extreme environments, suggesting potential cross-protective mechanisms against heavy-metal co-stress via a shared detoxification pathway [44]. Metabolic profiling using 50 carbon substrates demonstrated limited metabolic versatility, with reduced carbon source utilization and diminished fermentative acidogenesis among the novel strains (Supplementary Table S4), indicative of their specialized metabolic adaptations to AMD's extreme biogeochemical environment. Functional novel bacterial taxa in AMD obtained through culture testing

Through systematic cultivation strategies, 16 sulfur-utilizing species were identified, among which seven were novel species affiliated with the genera Acidithiobacillus, Conexibacter, Acidiferrimicrobium, Sulfobacillus, Sulfoacidibacillus, and Alicyclobacillus. Notably, these isolates lacked detectable sulfite oxidation or sulfur-reduction capabilities. Simultaneously, iron redox activity was observed in 30 strains (13 novel) distributed across 16 genera (Fig. 1), including Acidiphilium, Rhodopila, Rhodovastum, Metallibacterium, Acidithiobacillus, Granulicella, Acidicapsa, Leptospirillum, Conexibacter, Trebonia, Aciditerrimonas, Acidiferrimicrobium, Sulfobacillus, Sulfoacidibacillus, Fodinisporobacter, and Alicyclobacillus (Fig. 1). Some isolates possessed both iron and sulfur metabolic functions.

Nine Acidithiobacillus strains (including two novel species) exhibited mesophilic growth and robust capacities for sulfur and tetrathionate oxidation, consistent with their ecological role in acidic, sulfur-rich environments [45]. Except for sulfur oxidation, At. ferridurans S30A9T and At. ferrooxidans S30A6T demonstrated dual iron oxidation/reduction capabilities, while At. Thiosulfatoxidans S30A8T emerged as the sole thiosulfate oxidizer in this genus. Two novel acidophilic Conexibacter species belonging to Actinomycetota expanded the known ecological range of this typically neutrophilic soil-dwelling genus [46]. Specifically, C. tetrathionatitrophus S30A1T showed enhanced growth when tetrathionate was added to the medium. Moreover, it could reduce iron(III) anaerobically. C. acidiphilus DBS9H8T had a strong ability to redox iron(II)/iron(III) under both oxic and anoxic conditions. In this study, five novel strains of Acidiferrimicrobium were isolated, each with different functional characteristics. Most of the strains, similar to the type species Am. australe USS-CCA1T, exhibited iron oxidation/reduction abilities, except for the strains Am. ferridurans DBS9H16T and S30H11, which lost the ability to oxidize iron. Unlike the other strains, Am. ferridurans MYW30-Hm14 exhibited sulfur oxidation and strong sulfate reduction abilities (Supplementary Fig. S3). Members of the genus Sulfobacillus were considered to have the potential to play roles in biomining operations due to their iron- and sulfur-oxidizing activities. In addition, most strains could grow anaerobically using ferric ions as electron acceptors and were moderately thermophilic or thermotolerant, with an optimum growth temperature ranging from 38.5 to 55 °C [47]. A novel mesophilic strain, Sulfobacillus mesophilus S30H30T, with an optimal growth temperature of 30 °C, was isolated in this study. It could oxidize sulfur and reduce ferric ions, but it lacked the ability to oxidize ferrous ions. Strain S30A10T, preliminarily identified as having the closest 16S rRNA gene sequence identity (99.0%) to Sulfoacidibacillus ferrooxidans S0ABT [48], was predicted to oxidize both iron and sulfur. However, the culture test results indicated that this strain could oxidize only sulfur and not iron. Based on genomic homology and biochemical characteristics, S30A10T was proposed as a novel species named Sa. sulfuriphilus.

Alicyclobacillus is a large genus comprising 29 species that have evolved to adapt to a wide range of environments. Strains inhabiting solfataric environments can grow mixotrophically using yeast extract and inorganic sulfur and/or ferrous iron substances [13]. Several strains, including three novel species within the genus Alicyclobacillus, were obtained. Among these, Al. ferrireducens S30H51T could utilize iron aerobically and anaerobically, while Al. curvatus ALEF1T, Al. Tolerans S30H17, and Al. aeris MYW30-Sm3 were able to oxidize both iron and sulfur. Notably, Al. curvatus ALEF1T, Al. mengziensis S30H14T, and the aforementioned Am. ferridurans MYW30-Hm14 were confirmed to be capable of reducing sulfate. They are the first sulfate-reducing pure cultures discovered in acidic environments and hold great potential for application in treating AMD pollution (Fig. 1; Supplementary Fig. S3).

Besides the strains with sulfur and iron metabolism abilities mentioned above, Fodinisporobacter—a novel genus of the family Alicyclobacillaceae, order Caryophanales, class Bacilli—was confirmed to have iron-oxidizing capacity, and was published in 2024 [49].

All strains of Acidiphilium could grow facultatively anaerobically, coupled with iron(III) reduction [50]. The novel species Ap. dabaoshanense DBS1H1T was no exception. Rhodopila and Rhodovastum are representatives of phototrophic purple nonsulfur bacteria in the family Acetobacteraceae of Rhodospirillales, which are usually involved in the geochemical cycling of sulfur [51]. However, the novel species Rp. acididurans MYW30-Hm1 and MYW30-Hm5T were found to lack the ability to redox sulfur-containing compounds, and neither could assimilate thiosulfate or sulfate, unlike the type species Rp. globiformis. Another novel species, S30H79T, showed only weak iron-reduction ability. The novel species Trebonia lentum S30H69T was identified as an iron-reducing bacterium through culture testing. It belongs to the order Streptosporangiales, family Treboniaceae, and is a deep-branching member of Actinomycetes [52]. The isolate strain Metallibacterium scheffleri S30H22 showed 100% 16S rRNA gene sequence identity to the type strain, Metallibacterium scheffleri DKE6T, which has been reported to reduce iron. Acidibrevibacterium is a genus of Acetobacteraceae, and to date, only one species, Acidibrevibacterium fodinaquatile, has been isolated from AMD. It is an obligately aerobic chemoorganotroph, and the type strain is G45-3T [53]. In this study, three isolated strains (S30H18, W30H34, and DBS1H2) with 99.22–100% 16S rRNA gene sequence identity to the strain G45-3T did not exhibit any redox capacity for iron or sulfur compounds (Fig. 1; Supplementary Fig. S3).

Sulfur metabolic network architecture

Although the transporters of RISCs remain relatively understudied, it has been suggested that a thiol-rich outer membrane protein could facilitate S0 uptake [54]. This would subsequently initiate a sulfur-oxidizing bifurcating process. Once inside the cell, elemental sulfur can undergo disproportionation by sulfur oxygenase/reductase (SOR) to form sulfide, thiosulfate, and sulfite. The sor gene was found in At. acidisediminis S30A2T, At. Thiooxidans S30A3, At. albertensis W30A5, Sa. sulfuriphilus S30A10T, and Al. Tolerans S30H17. Alternatively, it can be converted to glutathionate persulfide (GSSH) via sulfurtransferase (DsrEFH-Tus-Rdh) or heterodisulfide reductase (Hdr) complex for subsequent oxidation. HdrABC was found in all strains of Acidithiobacillus. Notably, no recognizable S0 oxygenase was detected in Sb. mesophilus S30H30T, Al. curvatus, or Al. aeris, suggesting that uncharacterized sulfur oxygenase or novel sulfur-oxidizing mechanisms require in-depth investigation (Supplementary Table S5; Fig. 2).

The generated sulfide can be oxidized by sulfide:quinone oxidoreductase (SQR) or sulfide dehydrogenase (FccAB) to elemental sulfur or by sulfite reductase (FSR, CysIJ, or Sir) to sulfite. Thiosulfate produced by elemental sulfur oxidation can be transformed in multiple ways. It can be converted to tetrathionate by thiosulfate dehydrogenase (TsdAB), to sulfite by thiosulfate sulfurtransferase (glpE or SseA), or to sulfate by the Sox system. The incomplete Sox system (including L-cysteine S-thiosulfotransferase SoxAX, S-sulfosulfanyl-L-cysteine sulfohydrolase SoxB, sulfite dehydrogenase SoxCD, and sulfur-oxidizing protein SoxYZ), which catalyzes thiosulfate to sulfate, was detected in the genomes of members of Acidithiobacillus and Acidiphilium. Notably, in At. Thiosulfatoxidans S30A8T, the only thiosulfate oxidizer, the incomplete Sox system may play an important role in the oxidation of cellular thiosulfate (Supplementary Table S5; Fig. 2).

Tetrathionate can finally be oxidized by tetrathionate hydrolase (TetH) for disproportionation into elemental S, thiosulfate, and sulfate. The tetH gene was detected in all reported genomes of tetrathionate oxidizers, such as members of Sulfobacillus, Acidithiobacillus, Al. aeris, and Al. Tolerans. Nevertheless, tetH was not detected in the novel species Sb. sulfuriphilus S30A10T, At. acidisediminis S30A2T, or At. Thiosulfatoxidans S30A8T, leaving their tetrathionate oxidation mechanisms enigmatic. Sulfite can ultimately be oxidized to sulfate by sulfite cytochrome c oxidoreductase (SorAB). The sorAB gene was detected in Rv. acidophilus S30H79T and all Acidiphilium strains except Ap. iwatense DBS1H4, indicating their capacity for sulfite oxidation. The electrons generated by RISC oxidation are presumed to be transferred to the electron respiratory chain to generate energy (Supplementary Table S5; Fig. 2).

Sulfate is transported into the cells of the sulfate reducers Al. curvatus ALEF1T, Al. mengziensis S30H14T, and Am. ferridurans MYW30-Hm14 via sulfate permease (SulP). Subsequently, in ALEF1T and MYW30-Hm14T, it is reduced to sulfide through the assimilatory sulfate reduction pathway. Sulfate is first converted to adenosine-5′-phosphosulfate (APS) by sulfate adenylyltransferase (Sat in ALEF1T and S30H14T and CysND in MYW30-Hm14). Then, sulfite is generated by adenylylsulfate kinase (CysC) or bifunctional enzyme (CysNC) and phosphoadenosine phosphosulfate reductase (CysH) in ALEF1T and MYW30-Hm14T. Finally, sulfide is produced by sulfite reductase (CysJI in MYW30-Hm14 or Sir in ALEF1T and MYW30-Hm14T). The genes cysH, cysJI, and sir have been lost in S30H14. Neither adenylylsulfate reductase (AprAB) nor dissimilatory sulfite reductase (DsrAB) of the traditional dissimilatory sulfate reduction pathway was found in the isolated genomes. The only sulfur-reduction-related protein, sulfide dehydrogenase (SudAB), was found in T. lentum S30H69T, Sb. mesophilus S30H30T, and At. albertensis S30A5, suggesting their potential sulfur-reduction capacity (Supplementary Table S5; Fig. 2).

Iron metabolic network architecture

In contrast to the enzymatic and species diversity of sulfur cycling, iron redox transformations in AMD systems exhibit phylum-level specialization. Various iron acquisition systems have been identified in AMD genomes. These include the FeoAB family, Nramp family, and Zip family ferrous transporters for ferrous transport, as well as the FbpABC family, FbpB–FutB family iron permease, FutC family ATPase, and siderophore and heme systems for ferric transport.

Cytochrome c complexes (e.g., c1 and c2 isoforms) and rusticyanin homologs constitute the primary iron oxidation systems, with rusticyanin-containing electron transport chains being phylogenetically restricted to Acidithiobacillia. Notably, sulfocyanin-mediated oxidation potentially involves novel copper–sulfur clusters. The coding gene for sulfocyanin was annotated from the genomes of At. ferridurans, At. ferrooxidans, Am. ferridurans MYW30-Hm14, Am. australe S30H5, Fodinisporobacter ferrooxydans MYW30-H2T, Al. ferrireducens S30H51T, Al. Tolerans, and Al. aeris. Cytochrome c1 and c2 are phylogenetically widespread, including in the identified oxidizers At. ferridurans and At. ferrooxidans, which explains their iron-oxidizing capacity.

Strikingly, validated iron oxidizers C. acidiphilus DBS9H8T, Am. australe S30H47, Al. ferrireducens S30H31 and S30H101 lack canonical iron-oxidation systems in FeGenie. Ferric reductase in acidophiles remains unclear. Phylogenomic analysis via FeGenie implicates cytochrome c2 (Cluster 3: cytochrome c-type protein family) as a putative iron-reductase component, with conserved orthologs identified in the validated iron-reducing acidophile Ac. acidiphila DBS1H16 (BCFAMBIJ_02197, BCFAMBIJ_01954), At. ferrooxidans (AFE_RS02980), and M. scheffleri S30H22 (HBDMNPLP_00926) (Supplementary Table S6; Fig. 3). This genomic signature correlates with experimentally demonstrated iron(III)-reduction capacities across these taxa.

Comprehensive whole-genome sequencing of 49 representative isolates revealed systematic genomic divergence across trophic strategies and phylogenetic lineages. Comparative genome analysis of 14 genera and two novel families revealed distinct evolutionary strategies driven by HGT. Novel isolates harbored significantly more unique genes than their cultured relatives within the same genus. Furthermore, rare species such as the novel species of Rhodopila, Rhodovastum, Conexibacter, and Trebonia exhibited 0.8–16.8% core genome conservation compared to their neutrophilic relatives (Supplementary Fig. S4). Further analysis showed that these unique genes were predominantly associated with mobile genetic elements (MGEs), including numerous transposons (IS3/IS5/IS6 families) and a few plasmids and phage-associated elements (prophage and CRISPR) (Supplementary Table S7), suggesting recurrent HGT events in the rare species.

A comprehensive investigation of HGT events revealed that HGTs contributed 3.5–39.6% of the genome content across AMD taxa (Supplementary Fig. S5). The largest number of HGTs was found in Rhodopila (2321 events), followed by Acidiferrimicrobium (average 1563 events) and Acidibrevibacterium (average 1341 events). The results demonstrate that taxa in closer evolutionary relationships are more likely to form a module and to share similar histories of HGT, and that strains are more likely to acquire genes from taxa in closer evolutionary relationships.

Discussion

The extreme acidophilic conditions prevalent in AMD ecosystems have fostered the emergence of unique microbial life forms and metabolic versatility. Despite significant advances, AMD's MDM, comprising >99% uncultured taxa, remains recalcitrant to isolation in pure culture [43], which emphasizes the critical need for culturomics-driven microbiome discovery to characterize extremophilic communities. The lack of systematic cultivation strategies impedes both the ecophysiological elucidation of microbial functions and the genetic dissection of mechanisms of extremophile adaptation. Previous studies have demonstrated that enrichment cultures using appropriate media and optimal culture conditions can isolate “recalcitrant” strains not only by enhancing their abundance but also by resuscitating viable but nonculturable cells [55]. Here, we employed culturomics to establish 12 enrichment culture systems targeting mesophilic/moderate thermophilic, autotrophic/heterotrophic, and iron/sulfur oxidizers. Our culturomics-constructed mbAMD encompasses 42 species across five phyla, including 21 novel taxa within Acidithiobacillus, Leptospirillum, Acidiphilium, Acidibacter, Acidimicrobiia, and Acetobacteraceae, representing 86.7% (13/15) of the global AMD core microbiome identified through a meta-analysis of 226 metagenomic datasets (Fig. 1) [8]. Notably, 70% of mbAMD species (36/42) demonstrated validated iron/sulfur metabolic capabilities, with 11 novel species named based on their redox phenotypes and polyextremophilic adaptations (Table 1). These strains exhibit characteristic AMD survival strategies: 83% (10/12 species) possess flagellar or type IV pilus systems (Supplementary Fig. S2), which correlate with genomic signatures of chemotaxis and mineral-surface attachment, both of which are critical for nutrient acquisition in oligotrophic environments. The successful cultivation of novel bacterial species can be attributed to three key strategic improvements over previous studies. First, we established diverse enrichment systems with varying temperatures, energy levels, and nutrient types, thereby facilitating the isolation of microbial strains with distinct functions and ecological niches. Second, we modified the culture media composition, based on our observation that a high potassium ion concentration favored the isolation of common genera, such as Bacillus and Pseudomonas. To avoid this bias, we reduced the concentrations of KH2PO4 and KCl to 0.05 g L−1 each, lower than the 0.1 g L−1 used in previous protocols [56,57]. Third, we extended the enrichment and subsequent isolation periods to at least one month to enhance the recovery of slow-growing taxa. Furthermore, we used a novel microfluidic technique to deposit droplets of the diluted enriched cultures onto solid plates, thereby enhancing our capacity to isolate rare species. This study systematically expands the catalog of culturable microorganisms from AMD systems, addressing a critical gap in the accessibility of microbial diversity. By resolving a substantial portion of MDM, this resource provides a foundation for functional genomics, physiological studies, and evolutionary inference, enabling a critical transition from metagenomic prediction to empirical validation [10]. Our findings suggest that uncultivated AMD organisms likely possess specialized metabolisms, including ultra-oligotrophy, strict autotrophy, or dependency on trace organics and microbial byproducts; some may even be strict symbionts requiring specific interspecific signals. Therefore, future methodologies should advance beyond standard media by employing in situ tools, such as diffusion chambers or microbial traps, alongside high-throughput microfluidic co-culture systems that simulate native chemical gradients and microniches.

Metabolic analyses revealed pronounced niche partitioning. Complete tricarboxylic acid cycles occurred exclusively in Acidibrevibacterium and phototrophic Rhodopila/Rhodovastum, while Calvin–Benson–Bassham cycles predominated in chemolithoautotrophs. The presence of these two parallel carbon-source utilization patterns indicates that AMD isolates exhibited 4.4–10.3 times fewer catabolic capabilities than their neutrophilic relatives (Supplementary Table S2). Evolutionary trade-offs were embodied in antibiotic resistance profiles, characterized by horizontally acquired multidrug efflux systems (mdt, emrK) and reduced metabolic versatility.

Our functional profiling significantly expands the known redox members of AMD. We identified 16 sulfur-oxidizing species (seven of which are novel). Of these, Acidithiobacillus thiosulfatoxidans S30A8T represents the first Acidithiobacillaceae member shown to utilize an incomplete Sox system for thiosulfate oxidation. The absence of traditional sulfur activation genes (sor, dsrEFH, and hdr) in Sb. mesophilus S30H30T, Al. curvatus, and Al. aeris suggests alternative strategies for elemental sulfur metabolism. Conexibacter tetrathionatitrophus S30A1T was experimentally confirmed to possess tetrathionate oxidation activity (Supplementary Fig. S3); however, genomic analysis revealed no homologs of confirmed tetrathionate-processing enzymes (tetH, ttrABC). This phenotypic–genomic discordance suggests the presence of novel tetrathionate oxidase(s) with low sequence similarity to known systems. Three novel acidophilic sulfate reducers (Al. curvatus ALEF1T, Al. mengziensis S30H14T, and Am. ferridurans MYW30-Hm14)—exclusively isolated and characterized pure cultures—harbor intact sulfate assimilation pathways critical for AMD bioremediation [58]. These isolates are the first pure cultures of acid-tolerant sulfate reducers, demonstrating the power of targeted culturomics to reveal metabolic novelty with direct implications for biotechnology in extreme environments. They provide genetic and metabolic templates for engineering bioremediation agents that operate under low pH and high metal stress, thereby facilitating the development of next-generation AMD treatment systems [58]. While this study focuses on acid-tolerant strains, our future aim is to cultivate acidophilic sulfate-reducing bacteria that are predicted to have superior remediation potential. This is supported by our previous research, which confirmed the presence of acidophilic sulfate-reducing bacteria in the AMD environment [8].

Traditional iron oxidases in acidophiles, such as sulfocyanin and cytochrome c complex, were not detected in the novel iron oxidizers C. acidiphilus DBS9H8T, Am. australe S30H47, Al. ferrireducens S30H31, or S30H101, implying that 37 iron redox species (15 novel) employ novel electron transport mechanisms. Given the significantly higher Fe2+/Fe3+ redox potential under acidic conditions (+0.8 V at pH 2) than under neutral conditions (+0.2 V at pH 7), ferric ions provide a thermodynamic advantage for microbial metabolism in AMD, likely contributing to the dominance of iron(III)-reducing microbes in such environments. Although cytochrome c2, a putative iron reductase component, was identified in iron-reducing acidophiles (Ac. acidiphila DBS1H16, At. ferridurans, At. ferrooxidans, and M. scheffleri S30H22), 77% of the characterized Fe3+ reducers lacked recognized redox complexes, highlighting significant gaps in our current mechanistic understanding of iron(III) reduction processes. The iron- and sulfur-metabolizing strains in the mbAMD collection constitute a valuable repository of novel enzymes and pathways for bioleaching and bioremediation [4,7].

Our comparative genomic data reveal that the extreme conditions of AMD, characterized by a persistently low pH, heavy metal stress, and oligotrophy, have imposed distinct evolutionary pressures on resident microorganisms. These pressures are directly reflected in the microbes’ genome stability, size, and adaptive strategies. The genomic features we observed are not random but can be interpreted as adaptive responses to this harsh habitat. We observed a clear dichotomy in genome architecture associated with trophic strategy. Obligate autotrophs, such as Acidithiobacillus, exhibited streamlined genomes (2.8–4.5 Mb), indicating adaptation to energy limitation. In constant energy-poor conditions, purifying selection favors the loss of superfluous genes to minimize replication costs and optimize core functions, such as iron and sulfur oxidation [59]. Conversely, mixotrophic lineages, such as Alicyclobacillus, possess larger, more complex genomes (4.9–6.6 Mb). This genomic expansion likely reflects a “generalist” strategy by retaining metabolic versatility to exploit fluctuating or diverse nutrient sources. This provides a competitive advantage in niches within the AMD ecosystem.

The low diversity and high stress levels in the AMD environment create a storm for HGT. This suggests that the AMD genome is highly dynamic. Stress conditions effectively act as a trigger for genomic rearrangements and the dissemination of adaptive cassettes in this harsh environment. Novel genomic features, including unique genes and diverse MGEs, were identified in mbAMD genomes, particularly among novel species. These observations implicated HGT as a pivotal driver of microbial adaptation in AMD systems. Directed network analyses revealed preferential gene acquisition from closely related phylogenetic neighbors, with transferred genes predominantly associated with acid tolerance, heavy metal resistance, and energy acquisition pathways—traits critical for survival in extreme acidophilic conditions. Notably, the most abundant HGT events were conserved across nearly all AMD strains, supporting the hypothesis that HGT donors exhibit phylogenetic proximity bias while transferred genes display functional specificity aligned with environmental selection pressures. For example, acidophilic bacteria have evolved specialized membrane lipid remodeling mechanisms, such as the incorporation of tetraether lipids and unsaturated fatty acids, to maintain membrane integrity and proton gradients under low-pH stress [60]. Bacteria employ diverse and adaptable defense strategies against specific metals, utilizing a combination of extracellular polysaccharide barriers, active efflux pumps, and intracellular/extracellular sequestration mechanisms [61]. The heavy metal resistance genes identified in this study—including merA, mntH, znuB, czcABC, asrB, and acr3—demonstrate that the AMD microbial community collectively responds to environmental stress by sharing resistance mechanisms through HGT. Rather than existing in isolation, these genes form a shared genetic pool within the microbial community, enabling them to function as cohesive units that maintain stability under heavy metal stress. These insights not only elucidate the ecological resilience of the AMD community but also provide a theoretical and practical foundation for developing microbial-based bioremediation technologies and sustainable mining practices in heavy metal–contaminated environments. In addition, novel iron/sulfur metabolic pathways, including dissimilatory iron reduction and elemental sulfur transport systems, have been implicated in energy acquisition from AMD substrates. However, the functional roles of flagella and pili in these processes remain enigmatic, representing key targets for future research.

Conclusion

This study establishes the mbAMD, the first comprehensive culturomics resource for AMD ecosystems, comprising 652 strains representing 42 species (including 21 novel taxa) across 22 genera and 11 families. The mbAMD achieved 86.7% coverage of core AMD bacterial lineages. This breakthrough significantly resolves MDM within AMD systems and facilitates the functional characterization of 36 taxa with validated iron/sulfur metabolic capabilities. Notably, this includes the first pure cultures of acid-tolerant sulfate reducers (Al. curvatus ALEF1T, Al. mengziensis S30H14T, and Am. ferridurans MYW30-Hm14). Comparative genomic analyses revealed HGT as the dominant evolutionary driver. Adaptive genes associated with acid tolerance, metal resistance, and energy metabolism were preferentially acquired from phylogenetically close relatives, highlighting a modular acquisition pattern essential for niche specialization. The integrated culturomics framework developed here, combining targeted enrichment with genomic and functional validation, provides a replicable model for recovering ecologically relevant microbiota from extreme environments. Collectively, the mbAMD advances the fundamental understanding of extremophile adaptation, unlocks novel metabolic mechanisms that underpin AMD biogeochemistry, and provides an essential resource for optimizing biohydrometallurgical processes and developing environmental bioremediation strategies.
