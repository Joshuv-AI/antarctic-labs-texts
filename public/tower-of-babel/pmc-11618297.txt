Task-driven framework using large models for digital pathology
Authors: Jiahui Yu, Tianyu Ma, Feng Chen, Jing Zhang, Yingke Xu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Microscopy is an indispensable tool for collecting biomedical information in pathological diagnosis, but manual annotation, measurement and interpretation are labor-intensive and costly. Here, we propose a task-driven framework powered by large models that excel in visual analysis and real-time control, paving the way for the next generation of microscopes. We achieve proof-of-concept success on clinical tasks, specifically in adaptive analysis of H&E-stained liver tissue slides. This work demonstrates the advanced capabilities for future digital pathology, setting a new standard for intelligent, efficient, and real-time analysis in clinical applications.

Introduction

Using a microscope to analyze Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E)-stained tissue slides is a routine procedure in pathological diagnosis1. Typically, pathologists manually control the microscope to navigate across slides, switch magnification levels, and move the objective lens to examine relationships between tissues2,3. This process is not only labor-intensive, but also limited by the inability to directly obtain computational results for clinical diagnosis, including cell counting, specific tissue or key region of interests (ROIs) segmentation and quantification. For instance, in the clinical context, Vessel Encapsulating Tumor Clusters (VETC) present a unique vascular architecture in hepatocellular carcinoma. VETC is a key prognostic marker associated with poor patient outcomes. Accurate analysis of VETC at low magnification is challenging due to the loss of detailed features and its morphological resemblance to tumor, requiring higher magnification for a reliable diagnosis4–6.

With advancements in scanning technology, whole-slide image (WSI) analysis has significantly advanced digital pathology. In addition, deep learning has been applied and introduced numerous efficient methods, including classification, segmentation, survival prediction, and ROIs detection7–10. While WSI-based digital technologies provide valuable tools for cancer diagnosis, they are often suboptimal due to their post-processing nature, high memory requirements and device costs. Recent work has explored smart microscopes for digital pathology, utilizing deep learning-based image processing, to provide quantitative visual feedback during manual observation11–13. However, the development of more intelligent and automatic capabilities in microscopes, particularly for supporting the hand-eye coordination tasks of pathologists, remains an underexplored area.

Here, we propose a task-driven framework (TDF) powered by large models to accelerate the development of digital pathology. TDF facilitates a smart microscope that achieves adaptive decision-making and analysis by responding to the features of pathological tissues. Specifically, we fine-tuned the visual model using a small liver dataset, achieving an average Dice score of 0.907 on WSIs. It analyzed tumor with a margin of error of 0.012 on a slide-based basis reliably. Then the image features drove microscopic component movements and image acquisition, to further detect VETC, achieving a Dice score of 0.852. Additionally, we introduce key image processing components to facilitate TDF perform automated procedures and adapt to other digital pathology tasks. This is a framework to synergistically combine image analysis, visual control, and microscopy integration, allowing for comprehensive, real-time, function-diverse, and less laborious H&E slide analysis for digital pathology. Notably, current technical reserves, including big medical data and deep learning methods, can support the rapid development of this framework. For proof-of-principle evaluation, we integrated TDF into a traditional light microscope and applied it to routine diagnostic tasks, specifically the real-time segmentation of liver tissues on H&E slides, a procedure typically achieved through WSI-based post-processing.

Results

We utilized internal collected data from local hospitals that comprise liver cancer biopsy cases. The dataset containing 452 samples, including 87 H&E-stained slides and 365 whole-slide images (WSIs). Each WSI was annotated by professional physicians to manually annotate tumor and VETC regions within the tumor that are highly associated with prognosis. The WSIs were scanned at 0.5 µm/pixel resolution, with dimensions ranging between 10,000 and 50,000 pixels in length or width. The WSIs dataset was split at 7:3 for fine-tuning the large models. The microscope and scanner settings were kept consistent. We used the Dice coefficient and Mean Pixel Accuracy (MPA) as the evaluation metrics. All evaluation metrics are reported as mean values and standard deviations under the 5-fold Monte Carlo cross-validation. Additionally, under microscope segmentation for liver cancer has also been deployed on the platform.

The TDF initially utilizes a 10× objective lens for slide screening and tumor analysis. Upon identifying all the tumor location, TDF autonomously adjusts critical components, such as switching to a 20× objective lens and analyzing the fields of view (FOVs) with a finer step size, thereby facilitating the analysis of VETC (Fig. 1a–c, Supplementary Movie 1, 2, 3)). Principal verification was conducted on 87 slides (Fig. 1d), during which the TDF automatically generated segmentation labels. The average Dice coefficients for the tumor and the VETC sets were 0.895 and 0.852, respectively. By simulating expert analysis, the smart microscope accurately marks and measures pathological sections. Discussion

In the present study, we introduce the adaptive visual-to-control framework designed to drive smart microscopes in digital pathology tasks. The TDF-driven microscope enables pathologists to seamlessly track the entire diagnosis process based on H&E slides, capture final statuses, and obtain the computational results without the need for manual operation and quantification. For instance, manually recording VETC on liver H&E slides is impractical, and post-analysis based on WSIs incurs additional costs. Importantly, TDF generates more precise spatial morphology and a greater number of pathological images, thereby specifically recording events of interest.

Benefiting from the extensive knowledge memory of large models, the TDF framework is generalizable to mainstream microscopes for real-time pathological analysis. It offers multi-threaded processing, adaptive feedback, and responsive image features. Although the evaluation in this work involved adaptive stage movement and focal length adjustment, TDF can achieve more complex operations. These include multi-level or continuous frame rate conversion and adjustment of key acquisition parameters such as excitation power and exposure time. The integration of control and visual processing technologies creates a human hand-eye coordination system applicable to most pathological diagnosis tasks. To meet the different needs in digital pathology: (1) By employing the provided fine-tuning strategies, the foundation model can be adapted to wider downstream tasks with minimal data input. This approach eliminates the tedious process of developing analysis strategies and training models from scratch. The limited data required for fine-tuning can be efficiently annotated using open-source frameworks such as Cell-Pose17. (2) TDF enhances batch analysis in digital pathology by adaptively controlling the microscope (stage, objective lens) based on the learned diagnostic processes and visual features. Additionally, we are developing methods to align the image features captured under the microscope with pathological diagnostic guidelines, thereby integrating diagnostic reports into the system to further expand the biomedical scope of future microscopes.

While the TDF represents an advancement in future microscopy and digital pathology, future works should consider the following studies. For example, efforts should be made to reduce the workload of pathologists, lower the costs of sample preparation, and produce readable pathological results efficiently. By combining image analysis, control, and optical technologies, we can further enhance cell tissue identification, precise tissue structure positioning, and spatial correlation analysis. Advances in large models will enable the systems to identify complex features and events, especially to provide explanations for cell phenotype and structure. Advancements in large models will simplify the diagnostic process for more diversified and complex pathological cases by offering interpretations of cellular phenotypes and structures. Leveraging these capabilities, the task-driven framework will support acquisition and computation in various pathological tasks, fostering future developments in pathology.

Methods

Source data and Processing

All experiments and protocols in this study were approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Department of Biomedical Engineering, Zhejiang University (registration number: [2024] 82). All ethical regulations relevant to human research participants were followed in this study. All internal digital data were de-identified before computational analysis and model development. Patients were not directly involved or recruited for the study. Informed consent was waived for archival pathology slides. The source dataset comprises both digital and H&E-stained slides, utilized for evaluating and deploying the proposed framework, respectively. Two pathologists independently annotated liver tumor and VETC. The Intersection over Union (IoU) was calculated for each sample to assess the agreement between their annotations. Annotations with an IoU exceeding 0.85 were deemed valid, while cases falling below this threshold were guided from a third pathologist18. To preserve tumor integrity and ensure objective evaluation, the union of the annotations was used as the gold standard. Clinical slides were captured at a 20× magnification using a scanner to create the training set, and randomly split in a 7:3 ratio to fine-tune visual model. Following the typical methodology, the background was separated from the images. By sliding the slices and discarding low-quality samples, the training set generated approximately 256,015 patches composed of tissue, tumor, and VETC. For tumor analysis, WSIs were segmented into 512 × 512 blocks at 20× magnification and unsampled to 256 × 256 pixels. The VETC analysis employed 256 × 256 pixel blocks at the same magnification. The images are finally resized to 224 × 224 via data augmentation. In the few-shot evaluation, data for fine-tuning was randomly selected from the training set, and using the same validation set for consistency. An additional 87 H&E-stained slides were used as the test set for evaluation and deployment, all of which contained liver tumor and VETC to ensure maximum fairness in the evaluation. The resolution of microscope imaging sensor is 1536 × 1024, including 6 × 4 tumors and 6 × 4 VETC fields.

Developing the TDF

The ideal smart microscope for pathology employs visual task-driven methods to enable adaptive analysis of liver tumor and VETC. The TDF comprises three critical components (Fig. 1a, b). (1) Microscope Control Circuit: This circuit integrates a camera, stage, and optical imaging system into a feedback loop, allowing for continuous image acquisition and adaptive movement based on specific task features. (2) Analysis Model: This component processes the microscopic FOV in real-time, generating task-relevant features to guide microscope operations. (3) Task Feedback: This process translates analytical features into actionable feedback, improving the accuracy and efficiency of the microscope’s operations.

This framework utilizes task-specific features to guide microscope analysis and quantification by mimicking the practices of pathologists. Initially, the microscope performs a linear scan of the slide by controlling and moving the stage. During this phase, it analyzes the distribution of tumor across a broader FOV (Supplementary Movie 1). The model provides visual drive and feedback for analysis by calculating real-time features. After scanning, the microscope generates a spliced tumor map. A threshold method is applied to segment the continuously distributed boundaries and extract image-level positional features of tumor. To map these to real stage, a mapping matrix and an image point vector are defined and multiplied to determine the real-world distribution. Subsequently, the tumor location prompts the microscopic to conduct a detailed analysis of VETC, including moving the stage to the tumor position, switching to a medium objective lens, and adjusting exposure time to ensure imaging quality (Supplementary Movie 2). During this phase, the microscope completed an adaptive analysis with a narrower FOV and finer steps (Supplementary Movie 3). A wide FOV is effective for detecting the morphological structure and distribution of tumor, while high magnification is essential for capturing the fine features of VETC. The TDF ultimately outputs analysis and quantification results. For rapid imaging, exposure times were set to 0.2 ms and 1.0 ms for analyzing tumor and VETC, respectively. Compared to the WSI-based models that rely on mechanical scanning and output, the TDF triggers analysis based on specific task features, thereby enhancing efficiency and specificity.

Fine-tuning of Large Models

PLIP was trained a powerful feature encoder on a dataset comprising over 200,000 image-text pairs in pathology. These datasets exhibit significant variability and incorporate expert prior knowledge, such as morphological features. In comparison to specialized models that focus on a single disease, PLIP demonstrates transferability and robustness. Foundation models have been selected to lead mainstream directions in pathological analysis1,19,20. Notably, with few-shot fine-tuning, the performance of these models can even surpass that of domain-specific supervised models19. This approach eliminates the need for training from scratch, paving the way for broader clinical applications in the future. This trend also caters to the wishes of non-computer pathology professionals.

We employ PLIP, based on Transformer, as the backbone for driving the smart microscope. We fine-tuned two versions for the microscope: one for normal tissues and liver tumor in the training set on 10× magnification, and the other was for VETC sampled by 20× magnification. The PLIP comprises a text encoder and an image encoder. The image encoder processes microscopic images and extracts feature embedding. Following the configuration, we replace the traditional single labels with textual semantics. In our dataset, we manually generate semantic labels in the format of “{Annotation} image of the liver.” To accommodate clinical relevance and deployment performance needs, we designate a “Background” class for non-target areas. For the segmentation task, text labels are established to guide the text encoder. The text encoder transforms these semantics into embedding, aligning them with the image embedding within the same feature space. Subsequently, we employ a similarity measurement function to optimize the deterministic relationship between microscopic images and their classes. By optimizing the metric function, the model parameters are fine-tuned.

During fine-tuning, data augmentation was implemented to improve the framework’s adaptability to varying optical conditions. It includes random rotations of up to 15°, a 50% probability of flipping, and color jitter adjustments (brightness, contrast, and hue). The review indicated that folds, deformations, under-staining or fading, connective tissue, and dislodged tissue accounted for majority of the mis-detected areas. These issues were influenced by the quality of the specimens, despite satisfactory segmentation in most cases. For failure cases with a very low cell count in some FOVs, implementing more refined filtering criteria instead of threshold-based strategies may improve it. Finally, we plotted the Dice coefficients from the TDF-powered analysis as a KDE curve. To demonstrate the robustness of the proposed method, the supervised method was applied to online analysis and compared with TDF under the same conditions (Fig. 1e).

Deploying a Smart Microscope

Visual-Task driven analytical frameworks, such as capturing cell mitosis13,21 and repairing fluorescence images12,22, represent innovative directions in microscopy imaging modes and intelligent analysis. These technologies improve expert efficiency by transmitting visual information to microscopes. Developing TDF is aimed at assisting pathologists in diagnosis and quantification by automatic imaging and analysis while reducing scanner costs. TDF adapts to various needs in digital pathology easily, such as cancer qualitative detection and grading, cell counting, and tissue area quantification. The TDF outputs visual segmentation and quantitative results, which can be easily deployed on commonly used microscopes and computers. Deployment involves configuring essential components of the microscope, specifically the stage coordinates and the objective lenses, followed by adjustments to the camera to ensure proper observation and imaging conditions. These procedures can be managed using “MM”. Communication between computers and microscopes is established via USB 3.0. The microscope is equipped with a high-speed imaging sensor of 0.5 μm/pixel and a three-dimensional controllable stage. Once launching, the deployed TDF captures image streams in real-time to automatically focus, image, and analyze. Autofocus is executed by calculating the distribution of preset focal points on the slide. To evaluate accuracy and robustness, the outputs were fairly compared with three commonly used segmentation methods based on Slide and WSI, respectively.

Statistics and Reproducibility

In our evaluation, IoU represents the “Intersection over Union,” Dice represents the “Dice coefficient,” and MPA stands for “Mean Pixel Accuracy.” We used IoU to assess the fairness of the annotations. To calculate Dice and MPA, we firstly run inference on the microscope platform to predict the distribution of different categories on each liver tissue slide. We then compute the Dice score and MPA based on these predictions. For assessing the robustness of TDF, we employ KDE curves.
