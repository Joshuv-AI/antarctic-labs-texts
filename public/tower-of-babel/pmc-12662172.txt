SYNE1 Deficiency Manifesting Primarily With Motor Neuron Disease
Authors: Henriette V.F. Senghor, Raúl Domínguez Rubio, Carla Marco, Ainara Salazar-Villacorta, Ariadna Padró-Miquel, Sergi Beltran, Leslie Matalonga, Fabián Márquez, Hamath Abdoul Sy, Mamadou Sy, Monica Povedano, Amadou Gallo Diop, Moustapha Ndiaye, Pedro M. Rodríguez Cruz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectivesSYNE1 deficiency is an autosomal recessive disorder with a broad phenotypic spectrum, most commonly presenting as adult-onset cerebellar ataxia with or without motor neuron dysfunction. We aimed to expand this spectrum by describing the clinical and genetic findings in 2 unrelated families with early-onset motor neuron disease and virtually no cerebellar signs over time.MethodsWe performed detailed clinical, neurophysiologic, and genetic studies of 2 unrelated families with juvenile amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) and biallelic variants in SYNE1.ResultsThe phenotypes of both families showed onset of symptoms in childhood or adolescence, with signs of upper and lower motor neuron dysfunction in multiple territories suggestive of juvenile ALS. Patients developed progressive muscle weakness, eventually leading to respiratory distress and bulbar signs. Whole-exome sequencing identified SYNE1 biallelic truncating variants in both families: a homozygous nonsense variant, c.23131C>T (p.Gln7711*), in Family 1, and a novel homozygous splice-site variant, c.17851-1G>A, in Family 2. Notably, mild or no cerebellar manifestations were observed during the follow-up.DiscussionThis report highlights a novel phenotype of SYNE1 deficiency characterized by early-onset motor neuron disease and virtually no cerebellar manifestations, broadening the phenotypic spectrum of this complex neurodegenerative disease. These findings support investigating SYNE1 variants in juvenile ALS and including SYNE1 in motor neuron disease gene panels.

Introduction

SYNE1 (MIM *608441) encodes nesprin 1, a nuclear envelope protein linking the plasma membrane to the actin cytoskeleton (Figure 1A).1 Pathogenic variants in SYNE1 have been associated with autosomal recessive arthrogryposis multiplex congenita 3, myogenic type (AMC3; MIM# 618484); autosomal dominant Emery-Dreifuss muscular dystrophy type 4 (EDMD4; MIM# 612998); and autosomal recessive cerebellar ataxia (SCAR8; MIM #610743). SCAR8 was initially described in French-Canadian populations as a slow-progressing cerebellar ataxia with minimal extracerebellar manifestations.2 Later studies revealed that SCAR8 also affects other populations and most often is accompanied by major noncerebellar features, predominantly upper and lower motor neuron signs, but also cognitive dysfunction, respiratory dysfunction, CK increase, or urinary incontinence.3-6 In this study, we expand the phenotype of SYNE1 deficiency by reporting 2 unrelated families presenting with juvenile motor neuron disease and no or mild cerebellar manifestations during the follow-up.

Ethics Statement

Consent for publication was obtained from all patients. Ethics approval was granted in Senegal from Comité National d'Éthique pour Ia Recherche en Santé (CNERS: SEN2021/26) and in Spain from Comité de Ética de la Investigación Hospital Universitari de Bellvitge (CEI: PR159/24).

Genetic Studies

Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood leukocytes. For Family 1, whole-exome sequencing (WES, >90X) was performed on the proband (III:8) using the KAPA HyperExome capture kit (Roche, Basel) on Illumina NovaSeq6000 (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA) at CNAG (Barcelona, Spain). Genomic analysis was performed using the RD-Connect Genome-Phenome Analysis Platform.7 Family 2 underwent WES of the proband (III:1), affected sibling (III:5), and healthy mother (II:1) using the xGen Exome Hybridization Panel v2.0 (IDT Inc., Coralville, IA) on NovaSeq6000. Alignment of reads was performed against hg19 (GRCh37) and bioinformatic analysis with Data Genomics Exome Pipeline v19.1 (HealthinCode Inc., Valencia). Sanger sequencing confirmed identified variants.

Neurophysiologic Studies

EMG studies included nerve conduction studies (NCSs) and needle EMG. SNAPs and CMAPs were recorded and compared with normal values from the Senegalese and Spanish populations. In needle EMG studies, impairment was evaluated in 4 anatomical territories: brainstem or bulbar, cervical, thoracic, and lumbar. A muscle was considered impaired if it showed signs of both active denervation and chronic reinnervation. For the cervical and lumbar territories, impairment required involvement of at least 2 muscles innervated by different roots and nerves. For the bulbar and thoracic regions, a single muscle was sufficient, in accordance with the Awaji criteria.8,9

Results

Clinical Description

Family 1 (Senegal) is an extended consanguineous family of Wolof ethnicity with 4 affected siblings (Figure 1B; Table). The proband (III:8) presented at age 10 with progressive distal muscle weakness and occasional falls. Examinations revealed tongue fasciculations, distal muscle atrophy (Figure 2A), and foot drop with increased reflexes and a positive Hoffmann sign, but no cerebellar dysfunction. NCSs showed normal SNAPs and low-amplitude CMAPs in all limbs. On needle EMG, cervical and lumbar territories were impaired. Individual III:9 was first evaluated at age 7 with similar symptoms and examination findings. Neurophysiologic examination showed normal SNAPs and low-amplitude CMAPs and impairment of cervical and lumbar territories on needle EMG. Bulbar and thoracic regions were not studied. At the last follow-up, at 16 and 12 years of age, respectively, both individuals showed mild progression but retained ambulation and cerebellar integrity (Video 1). By contrast, individual III:7 lost ambulation at around age 16. The examination at age 20 showed mild facial weakness, generalized muscle atrophy of distal predominance, marked hand contractures, and scoliosis, but no cerebellar dysfunction. An older sibling (III:2) with similar symptoms lost ambulation at age 18 and died of respiratory distress at 19 years of age. Individual III:1 died of an unrelated cause.

Family 2 (Spain) is a large consanguineous Roma family with 3 affected individuals (Figure 1B; Table). The proband (III:1) showed clumsiness and running difficulties by age 10. Symptoms progressed slowly with recurrent chest infections, weak voice, dysphagia, and decreased walking tolerance. By his 20s, he was started on nocturnal noninvasive ventilation (NIV) and later used a walker indoors and wheelchair outdoors. At 41 years, examination showed facial weakness, tongue atrophy, hypophonia, increased limb reflexes, spasticity, and mild weakness. NCSs showed normal SNAPs and low-amplitude CMAPs while needle EMG demonstrated impairment in bulbar, cervical, thoracic, and lumbar territories. Brain MRI at age 34 showed mild widening of the cerebellar folia (Video 2). He died at 42 years of age from a respiratory infection. Individual III:4 had complicated type 2 diabetes. Disease onset occurred at 27 years, with distal weakness and walking difficulties. NIV was initiated at 29 years, and he died of respiratory failure at 30. Her last examination showed flaccid dysarthria, tongue fasciculations, global hyperreflexia, and moderate muscle weakness with distal sensory impairment in lower limbs. EMG showed low-amplitude SNAPs and CMAPs. Needle EMG indicated bulbar, cervical, thoracic, and lumbar involvement. Individual III:5 developed dysarthria, generalized muscle weakness, and subsequently distal muscle atrophy by 15 years; started NIV at 21 years; and was largely wheelchair-bound by 30. Examinations revealed facial weakness, flaccid dysarthria, tongue atrophy, and proximal and distal weakness with bilateral foot drop and pyramidal signs. Brain MRI was reported as normal (Video 2). Neurophysiologic findings were similar to her siblings. At 31 years of age, the most recent follow-up examination of individual III:5 showed mild dysmetria and intention tremor (Video 1). There was no nystagmus, dysdiadochokinesia, or ataxic gait.

Genetic Results

In Family 1, WES (III:8) identified a homozygous nonsense variant in SYNE1(NM_182961.4): c.23131C>T (p.Gln7711*), causing a premature stop codon at position 7711. The variant was classified as pathogenic per ACMG guidelines (PVS1 very strong, PM2 supporting, PM3 supporting, PP1 moderate) and has been reported in ClinVar as pathogenic (3 submissions; no conflicts). Sanger sequencing showed that the variant segregates within the family (Figure 1C). In Family 2, after a negative ALS and dHMN targeted exome (eAppendix 1), trio WES identified a novel homozygous splice-site variant in SYNE1(NM_182961.4):c.17851-1G>A. This variant is absent in population databases (gnomAD v4.0) and is predicted to disrupt a natural splicing acceptor site (Splice AI10 Δ score = 1). It is classified as pathogenic using ACMG guidelines (PVS1 very strong, PM2 supporting, PM3 supporting, PP1 moderate). Sanger sequencing was used for confirmation in all available family members.

Discussion

This study expands the phenotypic spectrum of SYNE1 deficiency (Figure 2C) by describing 2 families with juvenile ALS and a near absence of cerebellar manifestations. Previously, SYNE1 pathogenic variants have been primarily associated with cerebellar ataxia or cerebellar ataxia plus, with up to 50% of cases showing additional manifestations, notably upper and lower motor neuron dysfunction. However, no pure forms of motor neuron disease secondary to SYNE1 deficiency have been described to date.

SYNE1 undergoes extensive alternative splicing, producing multiple isoforms of nesprin-1 with distinct functions and tissue-specific expression, which influences the phenotypic spectrum associated with SYNE1 variants. Variants identified in patients with AMC3 impair the major muscle-specific nesprin-1-alpha-2 isoform.11 By contrast, most SCAR8 variants primarily affect the giant isoform, which is particularly abundant in the CNS (including the cerebellum), while leaving nesprin-1-alpha-2 intact. EDMD4 is thought to result from a dominant-negative effect,12 whereas AMC3 and SCAR8 are associated with loss-of-function mechanisms. In patients with ataxia, biallelic truncating variants in SYNE1 have been reported along the length of the protein. While motor neuron phenotypes are more linked to homozygous truncating variants at the 3′ end of the gene (Figure 1A),6 several cases have shown variants more centrally located.3 This is consistent with the splice-site variant c.17851-1G>A observed in the second pedigree. We note that a very small number of individuals with SYNE1 deficiency have been reported to present without cerebellar manifestations.5 However, all subsequently developed major cerebellar features resulting in a cerebellar ataxia plus phenotype. There are also a very small number of individuals reported with a predominantly juvenile ALS phenotype and cerebellar symptoms at disease presentation or shortly during the follow-up.13-15 By contrast, the individuals reported here develop virtually no cerebellar manifestations after an extended follow-up: Family 1 (mean age 16 years, follow-up range 5–11 years); Family 2 (mean age 34 years, follow-up range 3–31 years). Although initially described as relatively mild and slowly progressive with variable age at onset (ranging from 6 to 40 years), SYNE1 deficiency can, in some cases, lead to premature death in mid-adulthood (30–40 years), particularly in individuals with multisystemic disease.4 The cases reported here align more closely with the severe multisystemic phenotype. Notably, individual III:4 from Family 2 died prematurely at 30 years of age, only 3 years after symptom onset, representing a significantly more rapid disease course than previously documented. While comorbidities such as obesity and complicated diabetes may have contributed to disease progression, we cannot exclude the influence of genetic modifiers on disease severity.

The mechanism by which SYNE1 deficiency results in motor neuron disease is unknown. Nesprin-1 anchors specialized myonuclei beneath neuromuscular junctions (NMJs).16 A muscle biopsy from an individual with SYNE1 deficiency revealed a 50% reduction in the number of muscle endplates with subsynaptic myonuclei compared with controls.2 Because Nesprin-1 also interacts with MuSK,16 an essential compo- nent of AChR clustering and NMJ maintenance, we could hypothesize that SYNE1-related motoneuron degeneration could result from insults to the muscle endplate.

In conclusion, we expand the phenotypic spectrum associated with SYNE1 deficiency by describing a pure form of juvenile-onset ALS without ataxia. The lack of a recognized association between SYNE1 deficiency and juvenile ALS may delay diagnosis, as SYNE1 is not routinely included in motor neuron disease gene panels. Given our findings, we propose that SYNE1 be considered for inclusion in ALS genetic testing panels as its contribution to ALS may currently be underrecognized.
