A shared-control ultrasound-guided robotic system for liver puncture under respiratory motion
Authors: Wenlong Zhang, Weihao Lu, Qijie Lu, Wentuo Yang, Le Xie, Yuchen Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ultrasound (US)-guided liver puncture is widely used in diagnosis and minimally invasive intervention. However, the procedure is highly dependent on the surgeon’s experience and is substantially affected by respiratory motion (RM), which can reduce imaging stability and puncture accuracy. To address these challenges, this paper presents a shared-control robotic system for US-guided liver puncture under RM. The proposed system integrates a puncture execution mechanism (PEM) that enables coordinated US probe manipulation and needle operation while preserving the surgeon’s control over needle orientation and insertion. To improve procedural stability, a dual-stage pose-force assistance strategy is developed. In the first stage, pose-guided assistance is used to position the US probe and obtain a suitable imaging view. In the second stage, force-regulated interaction control is applied to maintain stable probe-skin contact during RM, while the surgeon remains responsible for the puncture action. In addition, a bionic respiratory motion device (BRMD) was developed to simulate thoracoabdominal anatomy and RM using medical-imaging-compatible anthropomorphic phantoms. In phantom experiments, the proposed shared-control system reduced the average operation time, decreased the puncture RMS error, and increased the success rate compared with manual operation. An in vivo study in a Labrador dog was further conducted as a feasibility demonstration.

Introduction

Liver cancer is one of the most lethal malignancies worldwide, and early diagnosis combined with precise treatment is critical for improving patient survival rates1. In clinical practice, percutaneous puncture plays a key role in liver puncture and interventional therapy, typically relying on medical image guidance for accurate targeting and execution2–5. Among various imaging modalities, US, owing to its real-time capability, absence of ionizing radiation, and low cost, has become the most commonly used guidance method in liver tumor diagnosis and treatment6,7. However, US-guided liver puncture is highly dependent on the surgeon’s experience and is particularly is susceptible to RM. RM induces continuous displacement and deformation of the liver and surrounding tissues8,9, leading to target position variation, degraded imaging stability, and uncertainty in probe–tissue interaction, which in turn reduces puncture accuracy and efficiency and increases the risk of complications such as mispuncture and bleeding.

To mitigate RM-induced disturbances, robotic techniques have been introduced to improve the stability and efficiency of liver puncture, leading to various fully autonomous or strongly model-driven robotic systems10–12. These approaches typically rely on explicit models of RM, tissue mechanics, and needle–tissue interaction to predict and plan the puncture process. Although promising results have been reported under controlled experimental conditions, their clinical applicability remains limited due to stringent requirements on system calibration accuracy, imaging stability, and respiratory predictability. Moreover, liver puncture is inherently a tightly coupled perception–decision–execution process, in which surgeons must continuously interpret real-time US images and dynamically adjust their strategies13. Consequently, fully autonomous or strongly model-driven robotic robotic solutions still face practical challenges in safety, clinical acceptance, and responsibility attribution under current clinical and technical conditions14.

From a clinical task perspective, it is therefore necessary to decompose the US-guided liver puncture procedure and to allocate control authority rationally between the robot and the surgeon. Specifically, probe positioning, pose maintenance, and probe–tissue interaction stabilization demand high precision and repeatability and are highly sensitive to RM, whereas puncture timing determination, fine trajectory adjustment, and risk avoidance rely heavily on the surgeon’s real-time image perception and clinical experience. Under this rationale, shared control has emerged as an important paradigm in medical robotics, whereby the robot assists with precision- and stability-critical subtasks, while the surgeon retains control over perception-driven and critical decision-making operations15–17.

Some studies have explored this direction. Human-robot collaborative US systems based on teleoperation have demonstrated the feasibility and safety of surgeon-dominant, robot-assisted workflows in puncture scenarios18. Semi-automatic puncture systems have also been reported, in which subtasks such as target localization or needle insertion are delegated to robots under strong modeling and registration constraints19. In the broader field of interventional robotics, shared-control concepts have been systematically discussed to enable complementary cooperation between robotic systems and clinicians20. Nevertheless, systematic investigations that jointly address US probe manipulation stability under RM and its integration with puncture workflows remain limited.

In addition to control strategies, experimental validation under RM presents further challenges. Most existing liver phantoms are static and fail to reproduce respiratory-induced dynamics21. Although animal experiments provide higher physiological fidelity, they are associated with high cost, complex ethical approval processes, and limited repeatability22,23. Therefore, developing a reproducible, controllable, and physiologically relevant RM experimental platform is essential for systematic evaluation of robot-assisted puncture strategies24.

Motivated by these considerations, this paper proposes a shared-control US-guided liver puncture robotic system under RM. A PEM is designed to enable coordinated manipulation of the probe and needle within a compact structure, while preserving the surgeon’s authority over needle orientation and insertion. A dual-stage pose-force assistance scheme is further developed to establish a favorable imaging configuration through pose guidance and to stabilize the probe-tissue interaction via force regulation, thereby mitigating RM-induced disturbances. In addition, a bionic BRMD is developed for system validation, and comprehensive evaluations including positioning accuracy tests, force-control assessments, phantom experiments, and in vivo Labrador dog experiments are conducted. System design and mechanical analysis

System structure and workflow

As shown in Fig. 1, the proposed US-guided puncture system is designed under a shared-control paradigm and consists of an US imaging device (ACUSON S2000, Siemens Healthineers, Germany), an optical tracking system (OTS; Guangzhou Aimooe Technology Co., Ltd.), a robotic manipulator (UR5e, Universal Robots, Denmark), a PEM, a software control center (SCC), and a lesion target area. The US device provides real-time visualization of internal tissues, which serves as the primary information source for the surgeon during puncture. The SCC integrates CT/US imaging, optical tracking, and robotic state information to support surgeon-in-the-loop guidance and coordination. The OTS performs spatial tracking and registration among the imaging, robot, and patient coordinate frames, enabling consistent spatial alignment throughout the procedure. The PEM provides robotic assistance for US probe manipulation and image stabilization, while needle insertion remains under direct surgeon control.Fig. 1The shared-control US-guided puncture robotic system structure.

The shared-control US-guided puncture robotic system structure.

The overall workflow is organized into two sequential assistance phases. In the first phase, an OTS-guided pose assistance strategy is employed to guide the robotic manipulator in aligning the US probe with the target region and approaching the planned skin entry site. This phase establishes a stable imaging configuration for the surgeon, rather than executing puncture actions. In the second phase, once probe-skin contact is established, the system transitions to an force-feedback assistance mode to regulate probe-tissue interaction forces and suppress RM-induced disturbances, thereby maintaining imaging stability. Throughout the entire procedure, control authority is explicitly shared between the robotic system and the surgeon: the robot autonomously stabilizes the imaging workspace, while the surgeon performs needle orientation adjustment and insertion based on real-time feedback from both the SCC and US imaging.

Design of the PEM and mechanical motion analysis

The PEM in this study is developed to provide stable support, RM compensation, and force-feedback regulation during the procedure, while leaving the final needle insertion to the surgeon. This design not only meets current clinical requirements for safety and responsibility allocation, but also leverages the surgeon’s experience and real-time judgment, balancing the intelligence of robotic assistance with the flexibility of surgeon dominance. Such a strategy lays a practical foundation for the clinical translation of the system. In clinical puncture procedures, it is essential to ensure that the needle can be freely adjusted within 2D plane of the US probe. Therefore, in Fig. 2a, the PEM is equipped with US probe, puncture needle, drive motors, and various motion units. This PEM possesses multi-DOFs, with three of them dedicated to adjusting the spatial pose of the needle within the plane of the US probe. Another DoF is used to adjust the needle so that it is in the plane of the US probe. The final DoF facilitates the guidance and insertion of the needle. Methods

This study proposes a shared-control US-guided liver puncture paradigm in which control authority is distributed between the robot and the surgeon. The robot focuses on establishing and maintaining a favaroble and satable US imaging under RM, while the surgeon remains in the loop to perform perception-driven decision making and needle insertion based on real-time US feedback. Within this paradigm, a dual-stage control scheme is implemented. In the first stage, pose-guided probe positioning is provided to align the probe with the target region and establish a favorable imaging configuration under RM. In the second stage, force-regulated interaction stabilization is employed to maintain stable probe-skin contact and suppress RM-induced disturbances at the imaging interface. Together, these stages assist the surgeon by enhancing imaging quality and interaction robustness, without altering conventional clinical workflows or compromising the surgeon’s leading role during puncture.

Pose-guided US probe positioning under RM

This stage provides pose guidance for US probe positioning under RM and serves as a preparatory step for subsequent shared-control puncture execution. The primary objective is to guide the US probe to a planned body-surface pose that enables stable and clinically usable lesion visualization, while leaving needle orientation and insertion fully under surgeon control.

To ensure geometric consistency and numerical robustness when handling coupled rotation and translation, probe pose estimation and registration are formulated within a unified rigid-body representation. Multiple coordinate frames are introduced to describe the spatial relationships among system components. Specifically, the OTS frame is denoted as O and serves as the global reference; the robot end-effector frame is denoted as E; the optical tracking tool frame mounted on the PEM is denoted as T; the US probe frame is denoted as P; and the SCC image frame is denoted as I. Through system calibration, a transformation chain from the image space to the OTS frame is established, enabling global localization of the planned imaging target with respect to the physical workspace. Force-regulated probe-skin interaction stabilization under RM

This stage aims to stabilize the US imaging condition under RM-induced disturbances by regulating probe–skin interaction. We formulate the problem as safety-constrained normal-force regulation with tangential compliance, where the robot autonomously maintains the contact force within a prescribed band to suppress motion-induced image jitter while avoiding excessive compression and shear.

Force baseline compensation and gravity correction

To ensure accurate probe–tissue interaction regulation, the raw force measurements must be compensated for gravitational effects and sensor bias. Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_w$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_b$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_e$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_s$$\end{document} denote the world, robot base, end-effector, and force sensor coordinate frames, respectively. The gravitational component \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{G}_s$$\end{document} depends on the robot configuration and can be expressed as:13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{G}_s = - Stacking the measurements yields the linear system, i.e.,14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{F}_s^{(n)} = \begin{bmatrix} \boldsymbol{R}_b^s \boldsymbol{R}_w^b & \boldsymbol{I} \\ \vdots & \vdots \\ \boldsymbol{R}_b^s \boldsymbol{R}_w^b & \boldsymbol{I} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \hat{\boldsymbol{G}}_w \\ \boldsymbol{F}_o \end{bmatrix}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}which can be solved using a least-squares approach30. Force-regulated interaction stabilization under RM

To suppress RM-induced disturbances during US-guided puncture, a force-regulated interaction stabilization strategy is employed, as illustrated in Fig. 4. The objective of this strategy is to maintain stable probe-skin contact and preserve a favorable US imaging window during RM. After force baseline compensation and gravity correction, the external contact force acting on the US probe can be computed from Eq. (12), which is then transformed into the probe coordinate frame and expressed as:15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} {}^{p}\boldsymbol{F}_{ex} = \begin{bmatrix} {}^{p}f_{x}^{ex}&{}^{p}f_{y}^{ex}&{}^{p}f_{z}^{ex} \end{bmatrix}^{T}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${}^{p}f_{z}^{ex}$$\end{document} denotes the contact force along the probe normal direction, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${}^{p}f_{x}^{ex}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${}^{p}f_{y}^{ex}$$\end{document} denote the tangential interaction forces.

In this study, a direction-dependent force regulation scheme is adopted. Along the probe normal direction, the robot actively regulates the contact force to track a prescribed value \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_d$$\end{document}, thereby maintaining stable coupling between the probe and the skin. Along the tangential directions, the controller minimizes the contact force as much as possible. Therefore, the desired interaction force is defined as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${}^{p}\boldsymbol{F}_{d} = \begin{bmatrix} 0,&0,&f_d \end{bmatrix}^{T}$$\end{document}, and the corresponding force error is given by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${}^{p}\boldsymbol{e}_{f} = {}^{p}\boldsymbol{F}_{d} - {}^{p}\boldsymbol{F}_{ex}$$\end{document}. Here, the zero tangential force reference indicates that no active tangential contact force is intentionally applied. It does not imply that the tangential forces are strictly maintained at zero, since residual tangential forces may still exist due to RM-induced deformation, surface friction, and coordinate coupling. Then, the force error is mapped to a smooth Cartesian motion compensation of the probe through an admittance interaction control model:16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_B$$\end{document}. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{M}_{d}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{B}_{d}$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{K}_{d}$$\end{document} are the virtual inertia, damping, and stiffness matrices, respectively. For each direction \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n\in \{x,y,z\}$$\end{document}, Eq. In addition, to discuss the interaction stability with tissues of different stiffness, the local probe-tissue contact can be approximated as a passive linear viscoelastic model:18\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} {}^{b}\boldsymbol{F}_{ex} = \boldsymbol{F}_{0} + \boldsymbol{K}_{e}\Delta \boldsymbol{x} + \boldsymbol{B}_{e}\Delta \dot{\boldsymbol{x}}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{K}_{e}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{B}_{e}$$\end{document} denote the equivalent tissue stiffness and damping, respectively. Shared-control puncture strategy and puncture execution logic

A shared-control puncture strategy is proposed, in which the robot and the surgeon collaboratively perform complementary subtasks during US-guided liver puncture under RM. Rather than pursuing fully autonomous puncture execution, the proposed strategy aims to establish and maintain a stable puncture environment through robotic assistance, while allowing the surgeon to retain full authority over needle orientation adjustment and insertion timing. Meanwhile, the robotic arm supporting the US probe significantly reduces the burden on surgeons during prolonged probe-holding operations. The overall puncture execution workflow consists of the following three stages.

Preoperative planning and respiratory-gated probe positioning

In the preoperative stage, CT scans of the lesion region are acquired and imported into the SCC for 3D reconstruction, after which the surgeon performs path planning based on clinical experience. During intraoperative execution, the robotic manipulator drives the PEM toward the planned body-surface location using pose-guided control under RM. To mitigate the mismatch between the static CT reference and continuously varying respiratory states, a respiratory gating strategy is employed10. Specifically, probe positioning motions are permitted only when the registration error between the image space and the physical space falls below a predefined threshold; when the error exceeds the threshold, probe motion is temporarily suspended until the gating condition is satisfied. Through this mechanism, the robot assists in guiding the US probe to the vicinity of the planned probe pose, thereby establishing an initial imaging configuration for the surgeon. At this stage, the robot does not execute any needle insertion or needle orientation control.

Probe-skin interaction stabilization under RM

Once the US probe reaches the planned body-surface region and establishes contact with the skin, the system transitions to a force-regulated assistance mode. In this mode, the robot stabilizes the probe-skin interaction by regulating the 3D contact force between the probe and the skin. This force regulation strategy does not aim to compensate for internal organ motion or predict respiratory patterns; instead, it focuses on ensuring stable probe contact conditions to maintain adequate US imaging quality. As a result, despite respiration-induced surface motion, the US imaging can remain relatively stable.

Surgeon-in-the-loop puncture execution

Under the stabilized imaging condition, the surgeon performs needle orientation adjustment and insertion using the needle manipulation module integrated into the PEM. The surgeon relies on real-time US feedback to confirm lesion visibility and flexibly determine the needle insertion direction and timing. During this stage, a human-robot shared-control paradigm is explicitly adopted: the robot continuously maintains a stable imaging workspace through force regulation, while the surgeon is responsible for perception, decision making, and puncture execution. During puncture process, residual relative sliding and cyclic image drift may occur and may cause deviation of the actual puncture entry point from the planned location. However, the surgeon can continuously evaluate target visibility, needle-path visibility, and image stability in real time, and perform puncture during a favorable respiratory phase when the target is clearly visualized and the relative motion is relatively small. This collaborative control strategy preserves the surgeon’s clinical authority while reducing the influence of RM-induced imaging fluctuation and residual sliding on puncture execution.

Experimental setup and validation

To comprehensively evaluate the proposed shared-control US-guided liver puncture system, a series of experiments are conducted to verify both individual functional modules and overall system performance under RM. Specifically, the experimental study consists of five parts. Firstly, the design and motion fidelity of the BRMD are validated to ensure repeatable and physiologically relevant RM patterns. Secondly, the positioning accuracy of the pose-guided probe positioning stage is quantitatively evaluated. Thirdly, the performance of the force-regulated interaction stabilization strategy is assessed under respiratory disturbances. Fourthly, phantom-based puncture experiments are conducted on the BRMD platform to verify the effectiveness of the proposed shared-control framework under controlled dynamic conditions. Finally, a labrador dog in vivo experiment is performed to evaluate system robustness and procedural safety under physiological RM, supporting clinical relevance.

Based on the reconstructed anatomical information, the surgeon planned the target body-surface pose of the probe according to clinical experience, aiming to obtain a clear US imaging view covering the tumor region. Meanwhile, the robotic manipulator carrying the PEM with the probe was guided to move toward the planned body-surface region along the predefined path, as illustrated in Fig. 8d. Once the probe established contact with the skin surface, the system switched to a force-regulated assistance mode to maintain a stable probe-tissue contact force, thereby suppressing imaging disturbances induced by RM. On this basis, the surgeon fine-tuned the probe pose according to real-time imaging feedback, such that the target tumor was stably visualized within the imaging plane, and subsequently performed the puncture along the planned needle trajectory, as shown in Fig. 8e. Under the stabilized imaging condition provided by the force-regulated assistance, the surgeon selected an appropriate timing to complete the puncture based on real-time US feedback. The puncture outcome is presented in Fig. 8f. After the procedure, the tissue specimen obtained through the puncture needle was extracted and placed on sampling paper for inspection, as shown in Fig. 8g. This single-animal experiment served as an in vivo feasibility demonstration, showing that the proposed shared-control puncture system could support surgeon-performed puncture procedures. However, further repeated in vivo experiments with quantitative targeting metrics are required to comprehensively evaluate its robustness and translational potential.

Conclusion and discussion

The shared-control US-guided liver puncture robotic system is proposed to address the challenges introduced by RM during clinical puncture surgeries. The PEM is specifically designed to enable concurrent US probe manipulation and needle handling, while preserving the surgeon’s authority during needle orientation and insertion. In addition, a dual-stage pose–force assistance scheme is implemented. The pose-guided probe positioning stage establishes a favorable and spatially consistent imaging configuration, whereas the force-regulated interaction stabilization stage maintains stable probe–skin contact during RM. Together, these components form a surgeon-in-the-loop shared-control paradigm that enhances puncture procedural robustness without disrupting conventional clinical workflows.

Comprehensive experimental evaluations are conducted to validate both individual modules and overall system performance. The BRMD is developed as a reproducible experimental instrument and platform, capable of realistically mimicking thoracoabdominal anatomy and RM using heterogeneous, medical-imaging-compatible materials. Systematic experiments are performed to verify probe positioning accuracy and force-regulated interaction stability under RM. Phantom-based puncture experiments on the BRMD further demonstrate that the proposed shared-control strategy improves procedural efficiency, accuracy, and success rate compared with manual operation. In addition, an in vivo Labrador dog experiment is conducted under physiological RM to evaluate robustness and effectiveness in the presence of realistic tissue properties and respiratory variability, which can support its potential for clinical translation.

Despite these results, the current validation still has several limitations that should be addressed in future work. The phantom and in vivo experiments were mainly designed to evaluate the feasibility and preliminary effectiveness of the proposed shared-control system, and the sample size remains limited.In addition, because operator experience and operating habits may influence system performance, further validation involving more operators is helpful. The in vivo Labrador dog experiment served as a qualitative feasibility demonstration under physiological RM, while quantitative in vivo targeting metrics, such as needle-to-target error, entry-point deviation, and targeting success rate, were not systematically measured. Future work will therefore include larger-sample validation, multi-operator studies, quantitative in vivo targeting assessment, and the incorporation of US image processing and clinical operation data to further enhance the perceptual and assistive capabilities of the shared-control system.
