Does abnormal head posture aid in amblyopia prevention?
Authors: Masoud Khorrami-Nejad, Mohamad Reza Akbari, Babak Masoomian, Hassan Qahtan, Kimia Daneshvar, Arash Mirmohmmadsadeghi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo assess the prevalence and severity of unilateral amblyopia in patients with ocular abnormal head posture (AHP) and to investigate whether the presence of compensatory head posturing provides protection against the development of amblyopia.MethodIn this retrospective cross-sectional study, the medical records of 1400 patients with various etiologies of AHP were reviewed. This cohort included individuals with congenital superior oblique palsy (SOP) (n = 758), Duane retraction syndrome (DRS) (n = 582), and Brown syndrome (n = 60), all of whom were candidates for surgical treatment at Farabi Eye Hospital, Iran from 2015 to 2022. Unilateral amblyopia was defined based on the presence of ≥ 2 Snellen line difference in CDVA and CDVA being worse than 0.1 logMAR in the amblyopic eye.ResultsThe mean ages of patients with congenital SOP, DRS, and Brown syndrome were 12.8 ± 12.9 years, 19.3 ± 11.9 years, and 9.0 ± 7.0 years, respectively. The prevalence of amblyopia among patients with congenital SOP, DRS, and Brown syndrome was 65 (8.6%), 117 (20.1%), and 11 (18.3%), respectively. In the DRS group, patients without AHP had a significantly higher prevalence of amblyopia (26.4%) compared to those with AHP (18.1%) (P = 0.039). However, in the congenital SOP and Brown syndrome groups, there was no significant difference in the prevalence of amblyopia between patients with and without AHP (P > 0.05). There were no significant differences in the horizontal and vertical angles of deviation in primary position between groups of patients with and without AHP or amblyopia across all three ocular pathologies (all P > 0.05).ConclusionThe high prevalence of unilateral amblyopia among patients with ocular AHP, despite the presence of compensatory head posturing, indicates that while AHP can aid in maintaining binocular vision, it does not guarantee complete protection against the development of amblyopia.

Introduction

Abnormal head posture (AHP) is a common clinical finding in children and may result from ocular, musculoskeletal, or neurologic conditions [1]. While orthopedic conditions are the most common causes of AHP, ocular pathologies account for 25% to 40% of cases [2, 3]. Incomitance, mainly due to superior oblique muscle palsy, is the most common cause of ocular AHP, accounting for approximately 70% of cases, which compels individuals to adopt specific head positions to achieve alignment and fusion, thereby preventing diplopia [4–6]. In ocular cases, head positioning generally aids in achieving bifoveal fixation, thereby enhancing both binocular vision and visual acuity [7]. In less frequent situations, it may also function as a compensatory mechanism to address refractive errors [8] or visual field deficits [9].

The incidence of amblyopia is significantly lower among patients with AHP compared to individuals with no compensatory posturing [10]. While compensatory head posturing can aid in maintaining binocular vision, it does not guarantee protection against amblyopia. Research indicates that patients with unilateral congenital ptosis who adopt compensatory head positions may still develop amblyopia, even in the absence of significant refractive errors or strabismus [11]. Furthermore, paradoxical head tilts associated with conditions such as superior oblique palsy (SOP) can also result in amblyopia despite the use of compensatory strategies [12]. Thus, while AHP may partially serve as a compensatory mechanism to alleviate diplopia, the underlying ocular condition can still render the visual pathway susceptible to amblyopia.

Timely diagnosis and treatment of amblyopia are essential to prevent long-term visual consequences linked to prolonged misalignment or visual impairment. Although compensatory AHP has been proposed to reduce amblyopia risk, evidence remains inconsistent and is often based on small series or mixed etiologies. Moreover, diagnosis-specific data on amblyopia prevalence, severity, and mechanism in major ocular causes of AHP are limited. Therefore, we evaluated amblyopia prevalence and phenotype in a large cohort of patients with congenital SOP, DRS, and Brown syndrome, and compared unilateral amblyopia rates between patients with and without AHP within each diagnosis.

Method

Study design and participants

In this retrospective cross-sectional study, we reviewed the medical records of patients with congenital SOP, DRS, or Brown syndrome who were candidates for surgical treatment at Farabi Eye Hospital from 2015 to 2022 and classified them according to the presence or absence of AHP. The research protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Tehran University of Medical Sciences (IR.TUMS.FNM.REC.1402.055). Furthermore, all examination phases were conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

The study’s inclusion criteria focused on patients with ocular AHP resulting from congenital SOP, DRS, or Brown syndrome. Exclusion criteria included patients with orthopedic causes of AHP, such as congenital muscular torticollis due to sternocleidomastoid muscle tightness, Klippel-Feil anomaly, and brachial plexus injury. Neurologic causes of AHP, such as brain tumors, psychomotor delay, and focal dystonia, were also excluded. Additionally, patients with a history of Botox injections or surgeries like strabismus and refractive surgery were not included. Those with visual field deficits, uncorrected or under-corrected refractive errors, ptosis, or nystagmus, which could independently affect the prevalence of amblyopia, were also excluded. A total of 1400 patients, including 758 cases with congenital SOP and 582 patients with Duane retraction syndrome (DRS) and 60 cases with Brown syndrome, met the inclusion criteria for the study.

Clinical examination and measurements

The data collected from the medical records included information on the patient sex, age, best-corrected distance visual acuity (BDVA), refractive error, angle of deviation, presence of anisometropia, and occurrence of amblyopia. The routine ophthalmic examination protocol at our academic center encompassed several critical assessments, including the evaluation of CDVA using the Snellen E-chart, measurement of refraction with an auto-refractometer (Topcon KR-800, Japan), and confirmation of refraction through retinoscopy (Heine Beta 200, Germany). The angle of deviation was assessed using the alternate prism cover test while the patient fixated on 20/30 optotypes at a distance of 33 cm. Additionally, extraocular motility was evaluated, including the presence of over- or undershooting. Refraction corrections were utilized to ensure accurate results.

A thorough physical examination was performed to confirm the ocular source of AHP, encompassing an assessment of the bilateral sternocleidomastoid muscles and evaluation of the cervical range of motion in two planes. The manifestation of AHP was determined through direct observation from three angles: the front view, which determined head tilt; the above view, where the examiner gaze was perpendicular to the head to detect face turn; and the lateral view, which ascertained chin position. Duration/onset of AHP was not analyzed because onset age and duration were not consistently recorded in the medical charts.

Diagnostic criteria

Congenital SOP was diagnosed clinically in patients with vertical strabismus and/or abnormal head posture present since early childhood, supported by patient/parental history and, when available, old family photographs. Additional supportive features included absence of subjective diplopia, large vertical fusional vergence amplitudes, and a long-standing incomitant hypertropia pattern consistent with SOP. Patients with findings suggestive of acquired SOP such as acute onset of diplopia, history of head trauma, neurologic symptoms, or known intracranial disease, were excluded from the congenital SOP category. Neuroimaging was not routinely required for the diagnosis of congenital SOP in this cohort and was obtained only when clinically indicated by atypical presentations or neurologic concern [13, 14]. DRS was diagnosed clinically by strabismus specialists based on the presence of abduction limitation with co-contraction signs, including globe retraction and narrowing of the palpebral fissure on attempted adduction, and/or widening of the palpebral fissure on attempted abduction, with or without upshoot/downshoot. Cases with isolated abduction deficit without retraction/co-contraction features, or with clinical suspicion of alternative diagnoses (e.g., abducens nerve palsy) were not categorized as DRS. Neuroimaging was not routinely performed and was obtained only when clinically indicated (atypical presentation or neurologic concern) [15, 16]. Brown Syndrome was defined as an ocular motility disorder characterized by restricted elevation of the eye in adduction, primarily resulting from abnormalities in the superior oblique muscle and its tendon sheath. This condition was identified by clinical features, including AHP, V-pattern strabismus, and hypotropia in the primary position, all of which indicated underlying mechanical limitations [17].

Unilateral amblyopia was defined as an interocular difference of at least two lines in corrected distance visual acuity, with CDVA in the amblyopic eye worse than 0.10 logMAR, in the absence of an identifiable structural ocular abnormality that could explain the reduction in vision. Amblyopia severity was categorized according to CDVA in the amblyopic eye using mutually exclusive logMAR ranges. Mild amblyopia was defined as CDVA worse than 0.10 and up to 0.30 logMAR, moderate amblyopia as CDVA worse than 0.30 and up to 0.70 logMAR, and severe amblyopia as CDVA worse than 0.70 logMAR. Severity-specific analyses were performed only for the congenital SOP and DRS groups because the corresponding data were unavailable for patients with Brown syndrome [18]. Anisometropia is defined as a condition where there is a significant difference in the refractive power between the two eyes. This includes differences of -3.00 diopters or more in myopia, + 1.00 diopter or more in hyperopia, and − 1.50 diopters or more in astigmatism.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 26 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD, and categorical variables as frequency and percentage. Among patients with unilateral amblyopia, visual parameters were compared between the amblyopic eye and the fellow eye using paired t tests. In addition, univariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses were conducted separately within each diagnostic group to evaluate the association between AHP and amblyopia and to assess the potential confounding effect of refractive error (astigmatism and spherical equivalent of the affected eye). Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) are reported. Exploratory analysis compared amblyopia prevalence between hypertropic-pattern congenital SOP and hypotropic-pattern Brown syndrome using Fisher’s exact test and relative risk. A sensitivity analysis in DRS excluded patients with anisometropia, after which the association between AHP and amblyopia was reassessed using Fisher’s exact test and odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals. A two‑sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant characteristics

In our analysis, we included a total of 1400 patients, comprising 758 (54.14%) individuals with congenital SOP [383 (50.5%) males], 582 (41.57%) cases of DRS [247 (42.4%) males], and 60 (4.3%) patients diagnosed with Brown syndrome [35 (58.3%) males]. The mean ages of the patients were 12.8 ± 12.9 years (range: 3–58 years) for congenital SOP, 19.3 ± 11.9 years (range: 3–73 years) for DRS, and 9.0 ± 7.0 years (range: 3.5–45 years) for Brown syndrome. Among the 582 patients with DRS, 347 (59.6%) were type I, 148 (25.4%) type II, 82 (14.1%) type III, and 5 (0.9%) type IV. In the congenital SOP group (n = 758), 709 patients (93.5%) had unilateral involvement and 49 (6.5%) had bilateral involvement. In the DRS group (n = 582), 530 patients (91.1%) were unilateral and 52 (8.9%) were bilateral. For Brown syndrome (n = 60), laterality was recorded as right eye involvement in 34 patients (56.7%) and left eye involvement in 26 patients (43.3%).

Prevalence and severity of amblyopia

Amblyopia was identified in 65 patients (8.6%) within the congenital SOP group, 117 patients (20.1%) in the DRS group, and 11 patients (18.3%) in the Brown syndrome group. The distribution of amblyopia severity across these categories is detailed in Table 1. In the congenital SOP group, the prevalence of amblyopia severity included 49 patients (6.5%) with mild amblyopia, 10 patients (1.3%) with moderate amblyopia, and 6 patients (0.8%) with severe amblyopia. For the DRS group, the figures were 85 patients (14.6%) with mild amblyopia, 17 patients (2.9%) with moderate amblyopia, and 15 patients (2.6%) with severe amblyopia. Overall, mild amblyopia was the most commonly observed severity in the congenital SOP and DRS groups. Among amblyopic patients, amblyopia severity (mild/moderate/severe) was not associated with AHP in the DRS group (χ² = 0.060, df = 2, p = 0.971), while severity comparisons were not feasible in congenital SOP (all amblyopic patients had AHP) and were underpowered in Brown syndrome due to small subgroup sizes.

Discussion

Ocular-associated AHP is mainly observed due to incomitant strabismus, predominantly in congenital SOP and DRS. Despite the compensatory mechanism of associated AHP aiding in optimizing visual acuity and achieving binocular vision, a significant prevalence of amblyopia remains among patients, with rates of 8.6%, 20.1%, and 18.3% observed in congenital SOP, DRS, and Brown syndrome, respectively. Most cases of amblyopia are classified as mild. While patients with ocular AHP can still develop amblyopia, amblyopia was not significantly associated with the magnitude of horizontal or vertical deviation measured in primary position in this cohort. Nevertheless, it should be acknowledged that in restrictive strabismus conditions such as DRS or Brown syndrome, the angle of deviation may vary considerably across different gaze positions. Since in our study deviation was measured only in the primary position, this approach may not fully capture the dynamic nature of ocular misalignment in these patients. Future investigations assessing deviation across multiple gaze positions could provide a more comprehensive understanding of the relationship between abnormal head posture, angle variability, and amblyopia risk.

The previously reported prevalence of amblyopia among children with Brown syndrome varies significantly, with some studies reporting no amblyopic patients in a series of 126 individuals. In contrast, others indicated a frequency of up to 25%. This heterogeneity in reported prevalence can be attributed to small sample sizes and variations in the measurement of visual acuity across different studies [19–24]. Recent literature indicated that the prevalence of amblyopia in SOP ranges from 9% to 19% [25–27]. Amblyopia can exacerbate the clinical presentation by increasing vertical deviation and is a known risk factor for reoperation. Therefore, it is crucial to treat amblyopia in pediatric patients before considering early surgical interventions for congenital SOP [28]. The prevalence of amblyopia in individuals with Duane syndrome varies considerably based on the type of syndrome and whether it is unilateral or bilateral, with reported rates ranging from 3% to 27% [29–35].

To date, it has been believed that AHP serves as a protective mechanism against the development of amblyopia and the loss of binocularity. In a case-control study by Gladysheva et al., the prevalence of amblyopia was examined in a group of patients with Brown syndrome, DRS, nystagmus, or paralysis of the abducens or trochlear nerves, specifically comparing those with AHP versus those without. Individuals with AHP demonstrated a significantly higher rate of 83% for having both stereopsis and fusion compared to those without compensatory AHP. The rates of fusion and stereopsis did not differ among the various patterns of AHP. Ultimately, the overall incidence of amblyopia was significantly lower in patients with AHP than in those without it [10]. AHP may be adopted for different sensory reasons. While it can improve alignment in a gaze position that permits fusion in some patients, it may also be used to reduce the functional impact of diplopia by shifting the second image to a less bothersome peripheral position or facilitating suppression. Therefore, AHP should not be assumed to indicate binocular single vision, particularly in patients with amblyopia.

Although McCulloch et al. reported amblyopia in five of 11 preverbal children with unilateral congenital ptosis despite compensatory head posturing, the amblyogenic mechanism in congenital ptosis differs from that in the disorders evaluated in the present study. In congenital ptosis, obstruction of the visual axis may contribute to deprivation amblyopia, whereas amblyopia in congenital SOP, DRS, and Brown syndrome is more commonly related to strabismus, anisometropia, suppression, or combined mechanisms. Therefore, the findings of McCulloch et al. should be interpreted only as indirect clinical evidence that compensatory head posturing does not invariably eliminate amblyopia risk, rather than as direct mechanistic evidence applicable to our study population [11]. Similarly, our study demonstrated a statistically significant higher prevalence of amblyopia among DRS patients without AHP compared to those with AHP; however, there remained a considerable proportion of amblyopic patients in both groups, with 18.1% in the AHP group and 26.4% in the non-AHP group. In a related study, it was found that 71% (5 out of 7) of patients with unilateral or asymmetric congenital ptosis exhibiting compensatory AHP had amblyopia. This suggests that AHP did not effectively reduce the risk of developing amblyopia. Notably, none of the amblyopic patients had strabismus, and four of the five had anisometropia of less than 2 diopters (D), indicating that amblyopia can occur even in the absence of significant strabismus or anisometropia. Also, our regression analyses suggest that refractive error does not meaningfully explain the observed AHP–amblyopia association in DRS, because adjustment for astigmatism and spherical equivalent produced a negligible change in the AHP effect estimate. Although statistical significance was attenuated after adjustment, the stability of the odds ratio supports the interpretation that reduced precision (and/or collinearity with refractive variables), rather than true confounding, likely accounts for the wider confidence interval. Importantly, refractive error parameters were independently associated with amblyopia in DRS, reinforcing the need to evaluate and manage refractive status carefully in these patients, even when compensatory AHP is present. Therefore, clinicians should remain vigilant in monitoring patients with unilateral or asymmetric congenital ptosis and AHP, as the presence of AHP does not mitigate the risk of amblyopia [36]. In a previous study of 142 patients with ocular AHP, 28.2% were found to have amblyopia, indicating a significant prevalence among this population. The incidence varied significantly by diagnosis, with nystagmus blockage syndrome showing a 100% rate of amblyopia, while DRS had the lowest rate at 16%. The development of amblyopia was not significantly associated with fusion, stereopsis, or the types of AHP. This suggests that amblyopia can occur even in the presence of binocularity, and the visual system may remain susceptible to amblyopia due to other factors, such as the severity of the underlying ocular condition or the timing of its onset, where the visual system is not fully developed or is compromised. Thus, comprehensive evaluations are essential for AHP patients, and clinicians should be vigilant in addressing potential visual deficits, regardless of fusion and stereopsis capabilities [5]. Similarly, we demonstrated that the visual pathway is not at an equal risk of developing amblyopia across the three categories of ocular pathology.

Children with SOP often adopt compensatory head tilts to alleviate hypertropia. In 3.4% of children with SOP, a paradoxical head tilt toward the affected eye is observed [37]. This abnormal posture may lead to amblyopia in the unaffected eye, as it promotes a preference for image separation or suppression over maintaining stable binocular vision. The suppression mechanism in the unaffected eye is a typical response in unilateral SOP with paradoxical head tilt, as the brain attempts to avoid double vision by ignoring the weaker eye. Therefore, the interplay of suppression, fixation preference, and the dynamic nature of head posture in children with SOP underscores the complexity of amblyopia development despite the presence of AHP [12, 38]. In a study involving 37 patients with bilateral DRS, it was found that 21 patients (57%) exhibited AHP, and six patients (16%) had amblyopia. Notably, five out of the six patients with amblyopia also presented with AHP. Given that individuals with DRS often experience difficulties in achieving single binocular vision due to ocular misalignment, they may adopt specific head positions to align their gaze with the fixating eye. It is proposed that the observed head turn in patients with bilateral DRS serves primarily as a compensatory strategy to enhance visual clarity for the fixating eye rather than an effort to achieve binocular vision [32]. Thus, based on the available records and the significant prevalence of amblyopia among patients with ocular AHP in our study, AHP does not fully ensure protection against the development of amblyopia.

In patients with incomitant strabismus, AHP would mitigate the strabismus angle to the degree that peripheral fusion is achievable. However, we did not find any statistically significant difference between deviation angles in amblyopic and non-amblyopic eyes across all three groups of ocular pathologies. It can be proposed that the visual pathway may be amblyogenic in the presence of underlying ocular pathology, irrespective of the angle of deviation. Similarly, Ali et al. noted that 50% of amblyopia cases were observed in patients with a deviation angle of up to 15 degrees, indicating that even minimal angles of deviation can significantly contribute to the development of amblyopia. Furthermore, once amblyopia develops, correction becomes increasingly challenging, even with relatively small angles of deviation under 15 degrees [39]. Therefore, early detection and intervention for amblyopia in patients with ocular AHP, regardless of the angle of deviation, is critically important, as prompt treatment can effectively prevent the onset of amblyopia and enhance visual outcomes.

Several amblyogenic risk factors have been identified, which can be categorized into anisometropic (greater than 1.5 D) [40], strabismic [41], and deprivation amblyopia (e.g., ptosis or congenital cataract) [42]. In our study, we identified combined risk factors, strabismic and anisometropic, in patients with Brown syndrome and DRS, as well as strabismus in SOP, as significant amblyogenic risk factors. In our study, no cases of Brown syndrome with amblyopia were associated with pure anisometropia; instead, strabismus emerged as the primary contributor to amblyopia in Brown syndrome patients. Sekeroglu et al. reported an approximately 16% rate of amblyopia in Brown syndrome, identifying loss of sensory fusion as the primary risk factor associated with its development. Notably, no significant associations were found regarding ocular deviation in the primary position, anisometropia, or stereoacuity [21]. Similarly, Ozsoy et al. found that two out of 45 patients with Brown syndrome exhibited amblyopia, both of whom had strabismus [23]. Since a significant proportion of patients exhibit strabismus, particularly pure hypotropia (50%) and a V-pattern (75%), amblyopia in Brown syndrome is primarily linked to strabismus rather than anisometropia [19].

Prior research indicates that amblyopia resulting from anisometropia in patients with DRS is more prevalent than that associated with strabismus [33–35, 43]. However, some studies identify strabismus as the primary amblyogenic factor among DRS patients [31]. These discrepancies may stem from variability in study populations, as strabismus tends to be more pronounced in unilateral DRS, while anisometropia plays a more significant role in bilateral cases [44], along with differences in definitions and measurement methodologies.

Our findings confirm and extend prior reports that abnormal head posture (AHP) does not guarantee protection against amblyopia. The novelty of this study lies in its large sample size, diagnosis-specific prevalence data, and comparative analysis across three major congenital ocular causes of AHP (SOP, DRS, and Brown syndrome). Thus, while the principle is established, our work strengthens its generalizability and provides a broader clinical context.

This study is the first to comprehensively investigate amblyopia prevalence in Iranian children with diverse AHP sources requiring strabismus surgery, utilizing a large sample to enhance statistical power and generalizability across a wide age range. However, our study has several limitations. We investigated the prevalence of amblyopia and its types in patients with ocular AHP using a retrospective design. The small population size of SOP patients without AHP was another limitation of our study. A portion of the DRS dataset has been reported previously with a different emphasis (refractive characteristics and amblyopia profile). The present study expands on that work by focusing on the clinical question of AHP as a potential modifier of amblyopia risk and by providing a comparative analysis across congenital SOP, DRS, and Brown syndrome [34]. Furthermore, the failure to evaluate fusion and stereopsis may hinder our understanding of overall binocular function. Our study focused exclusively on the most common causes of ocular AHP and including additional causes, such as Dissociated Vertical Deviation (DVD), alphabetic syndrome, inferior oblique muscle palsy, and third nerve palsy, would offer valuable insights and enhance our understanding of the condition. Moreover, due to the retrospective design and wide age range, misclassification between congenital and acquired SOP cannot be completely excluded. Another limitation is that, due to the retrospective and surgical-case-based design, our study cannot robustly determine whether AHP truly protects against amblyopia. We only report prevalence in a selected surgical cohort, and therefore cannot quantify the degree of “protection” afforded by AHP. Future prospective studies comparing incidence of amblyopia in patients with and without AHP within the same pathological group would be required to clarify this question. However, we attempted to minimize this risk by relying on evidence of early-onset symptoms (history/photographs) and typical congenital clinical features and excluding cases with acute onset diplopia, trauma, or neurologic red flags. Another limitation of our study is that AHP was recorded qualitatively as present or absent, without standardized quantitative measurement of its magnitude (e.g., degrees of face turn, tilt, or chin position). As a result, we could not analyze whether different types or magnitudes of AHP relate differently to binocularity or amblyopia risk. Future studies incorporating quantitative assessment tools, such as goniometric or photographic measurements, would be valuable to clarify the impact of AHP magnitude and direction on visual outcomes. Furthermore, ocular motility limitation was documented qualitatively (e.g., presence of restriction, overshoots/undershoots) without standardized grading of severity (such as -1 to -4 scales, duction/version quantification, or forced duction testing). As a result, we could not analyze whether the degree of motility limitation was proportional to amblyopia prevalence or severity. Future studies incorporating quantitative grading of ocular motility limitation would be valuable to clarify its relationship with amblyopia risk. Another limitation is that sensory status (e.g., fusion, stereopsis, suppression) was not objectively measured in this retrospective study. As a result, the most plausible biological explanation for why amblyopia develops despite AHP—namely, the quality of binocular vision—remains speculative. Future prospective studies incorporating standardized sensory testing would be essential to clarify the role of binocular function in amblyopia risk among patients with AHP. It should also be noted that some patients demonstrated abnormal head posture despite apparently normal ocular alignment in primary position. This likely reflects compensatory strategies to expand the field of binocular single vision, reduce diplopia in eccentric gaze, or facilitate suppression. Without detailed sensory testing or binocular field mapping, the exact mechanism in these cases remains speculative.

In conclusion, this study underscores the significant prevalence of unilateral amblyopia among patients with ocular AHP, particularly those diagnosed with DRS and Brown syndrome. While AHP may serve as a compensatory mechanism that enhances visual alignment and mitigates diplopia, a substantial proportion of these patients still develop amblyopia. Thus, the visual pathway remains amblyogenic even with compensatory abnormal head posturing, potentially due to the severity of the underlying ocular and nerve palsy pathologies and their onset. This finding highlights the critical need for vigilant screening and timely intervention within this demographic, as the presence of AHP does not provide inherent protection against visual deficits. Ultimately, the results emphasize the importance of comprehensive evaluations to facilitate effective management and early detection of amblyopia in individuals exhibiting abnormal head postures.
