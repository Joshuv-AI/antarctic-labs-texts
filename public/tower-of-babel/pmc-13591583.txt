Ulnar Nerve Entrapment From Ganglion Cyst in Guyon’s Canal Causing Claw Hand Deformity: A Case Report
Authors: Nahel Y. Haji, Laurence Lentz, Anna Y. Babushkina
Published: 2026

Objective: Guyon’s canal is an anatomical location in the hand containing the ulnar nerve that is bordered by the transverse carpal ligament as the floor, volar carpal ligament as the roof, hook of the hamate as the radial border, and pisiform, pisohamate ligament and abductor digiti minimi muscle as the ulnar border. There are three zones of Guyon’s canal. Zone 1 is proximal to the bifurcation of the nerve, zone 2 surrounds the deep motor branch, and zone 3 surrounds the superficial sensory branch.

Ulnar nerve compression at Guyon’s canal is rare and can present itself in many ways depending on the zone of compression. Causes of compression can include but are not limited to lipoma, ulnar artery thrombosis or aneurysm, ganglion cyst, hook of hamate fracture, and muscle or bony anomaly. Patients may present with clawing of the 4th and 5th digits, weakened grasp and pinch, as well as numbness and tingling. Often times, the symptoms can progress quickly as compared to chronic neuropathy from repetitive movements/trauma. History of trauma may or may not be present but is usually absent.

Methods: 47-year-old female who presented with three weeks of pain, swelling, numbness, tingling, and loss of grip strength. She bruised her right hand while painting and then exacerbated the issue playing tennis a few days later. An ultrasound was interpreted as a pseudoaneurysm surrounding ulnar artery and compressing the ulnar nerve in Guyon’s canal. However, intraoperatively, a large ganglion cyst originating from the pisotriquetral joint in zones 1,2,3 of Guyon’s Canal that was enveloping the ulnar artery was found and excised.

Results: Pathology did confirm a benign ganglion cyst. At 3 weeks postoperatively, she began having improved contour of the hypothenar eminence but still had weakness with positive Froment’s sign and some clawing present. At 2 months, pinch strength was still mildly decreased, but strength improved significantly with good adduction, no further clawing, and no numbness.

Conclusions: It is important to have high clinical suspicion of a compressive neuropathy when a patient presents with rapidly progressive or sudden onset symptoms of pain, numbness, tingling, swelling, and/or weakness. Treating urgently is crucial to restoring hand function.
