Lnc-MEG8 regulates yak myoblast differentiation via the miR-22-3p/RTL1 axis
Authors: Xiaoming Ma, Yongfu La, Tong Wang, Chun Huang, Fen Feng, Xian Guo, Pengjia Bao, Xiaoyun Wu, Min Chu, Chunnian Liang, Ping Yan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe yak (Bos grunniens) is essential to the livelihoods of Tibetan people on the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau; however, its growth and productivity are constrained by the region's harsh climate and high altitude. Yak skeletal muscle myoblasts, which have evolved to thrive under these challenging conditions, offer a valuable model for investigating muscle development. In this study, we performed transcriptome profiling of yak longissimus dorsi muscle at different growth stages, identifying a key long non-coding RNA, LncRNA-XR_314844 (Lnc-MEG8), with a potential role in muscle development.ResultsWe developed a novel technique to isolate high-quality yak myoblasts, enabling detailed analysis of Lnc-MEG8. Our results indicated that Lnc-MEG8's subcellular localization varies during muscle cell growth: it is found in both the nucleus and cytoplasm during proliferation but shifts mainly to the cytoplasm during differentiation. Functional experiments showed that Lnc-MEG8 promotes cell proliferation and inhibits differentiation, while its silencing had the opposite effect. Further analysis revealed that both Lnc-MEG8 and the gene RTL1 share miR-22-3p as a common target. Dual-luciferase assays confirmed miR-22-3p directly targets both Lnc-MEG8 and RTL1 mRNA. Co-transfection of Lnc-MEG8 and a miR-22-3p mimic restored RTL1 expression, highlighting Lnc-MEG8's regulatory role. Lnc-MEG8 also counteracts miR-22-3p's suppression of key muscle genes such as MyF5 and MyoG, facilitating myotube formation.ConclusionThese findings demonstrate that the Lnc-MEG8-miR-22-3p-RTL1 axis plays a crucial role in yak muscle development, providing insights that could advance muscle tissue engineering and enhance yak meat quality.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-024-11038-y.Keywords: Yak, Skeletal myoblasts, Lnc-MEG8, RTL1, MiR-22-3p

Background

The yak, an essential livestock species distributed across the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau and neighboring mountainous regions of China, has sustained the livelihoods and animal husbandry practices of Tibetan communities for millennia [1]. However, natural environmental constraints and management limitations hinder yak growth, resulting in extended rearing periods and lower economic returns [2, 3]. Skeletal muscle development significantly influences livestock growth traits, yet the molecular mechanisms underlying yak muscle growth and development remain largely unexplored. Exploring these mechanisms through comprehensive analysis offers significant potential to accelerate yak growth rates and improve meat production, ultimately facilitating sustainable animal husbandry in the Tibetan region. This pursuit aligns with emerging research trends and holds significant potential for advancement. While yaks have evolved to thrive in the harsh plateau environment, their skeletal muscle development is still impacted by high altitude, low oxygen [4], and limited nutrition [5]. Adaptations, such as increased hemoglobin levels and modified metabolism, help mitigate these challenges, highlighting the yak’s unique role as a model for studying genetic and environmental interactions in muscle development. Continued research is needed to fully elucidate these adaptations.

Yak productive traits are largely determined by skeletal muscle growth and development, particularly in terms of growth rate and muscle mass, as these directly impact meat yield. Skeletal muscle development is a complex physiological process involving two critical stages: an increase in fiber number during the prenatal phase and fiber hypertrophy during the postnatal phase [6]. After birth, animals achieve muscle growth primarily through fiber hypertrophy, driven by myogenic cell proliferation and protein turnover [7]. Myogenic cells, specialized precursor cells, play a dual role in muscle development. First, they facilitate muscle regeneration by activating, proliferating, migrating, differentiating, and fusing into muscle fibers in response to injury. Second, they help maintain the nucleus-to-cytoplasm ratio within existing muscle fibers [8].

Recent research highlights the diverse regulatory roles of long non-coding RNAs (LncRNAs) in skeletal muscle development through their interaction with microRNAs (miRNAs) [9]. LncRNAs act as competitive binding partners, sequestering miRNAs and thereby reducing their inhibitory effects on target mRNAs [10]. This interaction influences several stages of muscle growth, from stem cell proliferation to myotube formation. For instance, the lncRNA MDNCR competes with miR-133a, promoting differentiation while suppressing proliferation in cattle myocytes [11]. Similarly, Lnc-MD and Lnc-MGenhance muscle differentiation in cattle and mice by acting as sponges for miR-125b, allowing increased translation of the pro-differentiation factor IGF2 [12, 13]. Lnc-MG further participates in muscle development by sponging miR-351-5p, promoting myotube formation and gene expression associated with myogenesis while inhibiting myocyte proliferation [14]. Another lncRNA, LncMGPF, acts as a sponge for miR-135a-5p, stabilizing mRNA and increasing the expression of MEF2C, which in turn regulates key myogenic genes such as MyoD and MyoG [15]. Overall, lncRNAs are vital in skeletal muscle development, modulating key regulatory factors at multiple stages to influence stem cell fate, myocyte proliferation and differentiation, myotube fusion, and ultimately, muscle fiber size and composition.

The RTL1 gene, located within the essential DLK1-Dio3 imprinted gene cluster, plays a critical role in development and muscle biology [16, 17]. Its effects extends beyond fetal growth and nutrient transport, impacting both neonatal development and muscle hypertrophy. Aberrant methylation in this region, including within RTL1, can impair its function, resulting in fetal complications and early-onset puberty [18]. Adding to the complexity, imprinted lncRNAs like MEG3 and MEG8 reside within DLK1-Dio3, contributing to a competitive endogenous RNA (ceRNA) regulatory network. MEG8 acts as a sponge for miR-181a-5p, bolstering PPARa protein expression and influencing vascular smooth muscle cells [19, 20]. Similarly, MEG3 sequesters miR-135, facilitating MEF2C expression and promoting bovine myoblast differentiation [21]. Understanding the intricate interactions between RTL1 and its associated lncRNAs within DLK1-Dio3, in both normal and disease contexts, offers valuable insights into skeletal muscle development across species. This knowledge could pave the way for novel therapeutic strategies targeting RTL1 and its associated regulatory network to address developmental and muscle-related disorders. Moreover, miR-22-3p is a pivotal microRNA that regulates skeletal muscle development and myoblast differentiation. It promotes satellite cell activation and proliferation [22], supports myogenic differentiation by inhibiting anti-differentiation factors, and plays key roles in muscle atrophy and hypertrophy [23]. Studies in animal models, including pigs [22], and sheep [24], have demonstrated its involvement in muscle regeneration, atrophy, and growth, positioning it as a potential therapeutic target for muscle disorders like muscular dystrophy, cachexia, and sarcopenia, as well as for improving muscle function in aging and athletic performance. In livestock research, regulating miR-22-3p expression could help counteract muscle atrophy, improve meat production and quality, and boost resilience to stress.

Building upon our previous identification of the cis-regulatory LncRNA-XR_314844 (Lnc-MEG8) within the RTL1 gene [25], we observed its upregulation alongside RTL1 in fetal bovine skeletal muscle compared to both adult and six-month-old cattle (Avs.E and Mvs.E groups). To further investigate its function, we constructed inhibition and overexpression plasmids for Lnc-MEG8, RTL1, and miR-22 to assess their effects on yak skeletal myoblast proliferation and differentiation. Additionally, we employed dual-luciferase reporter assays, and other techniques to explore the interaction between Lnc-MEG8, miR-22, and RTL1. By elucidating their targeting relationships and systematically analyzing the regulatory mechanism of the Lnc-MEG8/miR-22/RTL1 axis on myoblast behavior, we aim to provide novel insights and theoretical foundations for understanding yak muscle growth and development.

Result

The isolation and purification of yak myoblasts

In this study, we employed a five-step Percoll gradient (Fig. 1A) to isolate specific cell populations. Cells were separated into three fractions based on their respective Percoll densities: F1 (27.5–35%), F2 (35–40%), and F3 (40–55%). To assess the expression of myogenic and fibroblast marker genes, including MyoD, c-met, FGF7, and COL1A1, we performed qRT-PCR analysis. The results showed that F3 exhibited higher levels of myogenic markers and lower levels of fibroblast markers compared to F1 and F2 (Fig. 1B-E), indicating successful purification of myogenic cells in F3. Furthermore, we investigated the differentiation status of these cell fractions, finding that F3 contained the highest abundance of myotubes compared to other fractions (Fig. 1F-H), which supports the presence of myogenic cells in F3. Overall, our findings demonstrate the effectiveness of Percoll density gradient centrifugation in purifying myogenic cells from the longissimus dorsi muscle of yak without the need for expensive equipment or specialized techniques.

Upon induction with differentiation medium, the myoblasts began differentiating into myotubes by day 6. By day 8, most myoblasts had fully differentiated and fused into mature myotubes (Fig. 2A-E). The qRT-PCR results revealed that the expression of Lnc-MEG8 gradually decreased with the increasing days of skeletal muscle differentiation (Fig. 2F). These findings indicate that the isolated skeletal muscle myoblasts in this study possess myogenic differentiation potential.

To examine the nuclear-cytoplasmic distribution of Lnc-MEG8, we collected myoblasts in the proliferation stage and myotube cells in the differentiation stage. After isolating nuclear-cytoplasmic RNA from the cells, we detected the mRNA expression levels of Lnc-MEG8 in myoblasts and myotubes, using NEAT1 and GAPDH as control genes specific to the nucleus and cytoplasm, respectively. To further understand the expression patterns of Lnc-MEG8 and RTL1 across yak tissues, we collected samples from the heart, spleen, lung, kidney, longissimus dorsi muscle, and subcutaneous fat of three-year-old adult female yaks. Tissue profiling revealed that RTL1 showed higher expression in the kidney, fat, and muscle, while Lnc-MEG8 was similarly elevated in the kidney, fat, and muscle (Fig. 3C-D). These results indicate that RTL1 and MEG8 play important roles in maintaining skeletal muscle growth.

Cells were harvested 24 h post-infection to assess total RNA and protein, allowing for an evaluation of mRNA expression efficiency mediated by the lentivirus. Results indicated a ten-fold increase in Lnc-MEG8 mRNA expression in the overexpression group compared to the control (Fig. 4A), and a five-fold decrease in the interference experiment (Fig. 4A). These results were corroborated by fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) and qRT-PCR analyses (Fig. 4E). Lnc-MEG8 promotes yak myoblasts proliferation

The cell cycle is a key parameter for evaluating cell proliferation. Propidium iodide (PI), a fluorescent dye, allows for differentiation of DNA content at various stages of cell development (G0, G1, S, and G2 phases), enabling categorization by flow cytometry. In our study, flow cytometry was employed to assess the effects of Lnc-MEG8 overexpression and silencing on the cell cycle. Results indicated that Lnc-MEG8 overexpression significantly increased the proportion of cells in the S phase, while silencing significantly decreased it (Fig. 5A-C) Additionally, Edu cell proliferation assays showed a significant surge in cell proliferation with Lnc-MEG8 overexpression and a notable reduction upon silencing compared to controls, implying that Lnc-MEG8 fosters cell proliferation (Fig. 5D-F). Consistently, the CCK-8 assay demonstrated that Lnc-MEG8 overexpression enhanced myoblast proliferation, whereas silencing impaired myoblast proliferative capacity (Fig. 5G).

Competing endogenous RNA interaction between RTL1 and miR-22-3p in MEG8

In our previous study [25], we identified that RTL1 was both cis-regulated and targeted by Lnc-MEG8 (XR_314844.1) in both comparison groups through the analysis of differential LncRNA and differential mRNA-lncRNA cis/trans networks. To further explore the interaction between RTL1 and Lnc-MEG8, bioinformatics analysis identified bta-miR-22-3p as a potential regulator of both Lnc-MEG8 and RTL1. Using Miranda software, we found complementary base pairing sequences between Lnc-MEG8, miR-22-3p, and the target gene RTL1 (Fig. 6A).

To experimentally validate the regulatory relationship between miR-22-3p, Lnc-MEG8, and RTL1, we synthesized miR-22-3p binding sites for both genes and generated dual-luciferase reporter constructs, including wild-type (unmodified binding sites) and mutant (point-mutated binding sites) versions. A mutated miR-22-3p mimic was synthesized, and we employed dual-luciferase assays to investigate their interaction. The results demonstrated a significant decrease in the activity of the wild-type RTL1 reporter (MEG8-pmirGLO) (Fig. 6D) upon miR-22-3p mimic treatment, while the mutant MEG8 vector (MEG8-mut-pmirGLO) (Fig. 6D) remained unaffected. When the miR-22-3p mimic was mutated, it had no effect on either the wild-type or mutant MEG8 vectors (Fig. 6B). Similarly, the miR-22-3p mimic decreased the activity of the wild-type RTL1 reporter (RTL1-pmirGLO) (Fig. 6E), but did not affect the mutant RTL1 reporter (RTL1-mut-pmirGLO) (Fig. 6E). The mutated miR-22-3p mimic had no influence on either the wild-type or mutant RTL1 constructs (Fig. 6C). These results confirm that miR-22-3p directly targets both MEG8 and RTL1.

miR-22-3p promotes yak myoblasts proliferation

To further investigate the role of miR-22-3p in myoblast differentiation, myoblasts were transfected with either a miR-22-3p mimic or inhibitor. Following transfection, cells were induced to differentiate, and samples were collected 24 h post-induction for mRNA and protein analysis. qRT-PCR and Western blot analyses revealed that the miR-22-3p mimic significantly increased miR-22-3p expression and elevated levels of the differentiation markers MYF5 and MYOG at both mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 7A-D). Conversely, the miR-22-3p inhibitor significantly decreased the expression of miR-22-3p and significantly increased the expression of MYF5 and MYOG at both mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 7A-D). Immunofluorescence staining further revealed that the miR-22-3p mimic significantly promoted myotube formation (Fig. 7E-F), while the miR-22-3p inhibitor markedly inhibited it. These results indicate that miR-22-3p plays a crucial role in promoting myoblast differentiation.

RTL1 inhibits yak myoblasts proliferation

To explore the role of RTL1 in myoblast differentiation, we manipulated its expression using Sh-RTL1 and OE-RTL1 vectors. Myoblasts were collected for analysis 24 h post-transfection. Overexpression of RTL1 through OE-RTL1 significantly increased RTL1 levels, while notably suppressing MYOG and MYF5 at both mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 8A-D), thereby inhibiting differentiation markers. In contrast, Sh-RTL1 markedly reduced RTL1 expression, leading to a significant increase in MYOG and MYF5 expression (Fig. 8A-D). Immunofluorescence assays showed that OE-RTL1 significantly impaired myotube formation, whereas Sh-RTL1 strongly enhanced it (Fig. 8E-F). These findings suggest that RTL1 acts as an inhibitor of myoblast differentiation.

Lnc-MEG8 affects myoblast differentiation by competitively binding to RTL1 and miR-22-3p

This study elucidates the regulatory interactions among Lnc-MEG8, miR-22-3p, and RTL1. Experimental results revealed that Lnc-MEG8 overexpression significantly increased miR-22-3p levels while concurrently suppressing RTL1 mRNA and protein expression. Conversely, Lnc-MEG8 knockdown substantially reduced miR-22-3p levels, accompanied by a significant decrease in RTL1 mRNA and protein expression, although RTL1 levels subsequently increased (Fig. 9B, C). Additionally, the miR-22-3p mimic markedly reduced RTL1 expression (Fig. 9C, D), whereas the miR-22-3p inhibitor enhanced RTL1 expression (Fig. 9C, E). These findings suggest a positive regulatory relationship between MEG8 and RTL1, while miR-22-3p negatively regulates RTL1.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

Guided by the Animal Ethics Procedures and Guidelines of the People's Republic of China, the welfare of all yaks was prioritized throughout the study. This commitment was reflected in the approval granted by the Animal Administration and Ethics Committee of Lanzhou Institute of Husbandry and Pharmaceutical Sciences (Permit No. 2019–002).

Culturing and differentiation of isolated yak muscle myoblasts

The samples used to isolate primary skeletal muscle myoblasts were collected from the longissimus dorsi muscle of one healthy 3-day-old female calves and three-year-old 3 adult female yaks (for RTL1 and Lnc-MEG8 tissue qRT-PCR expression profiling detection) from Datong Cattle Farm in Qinghai Province. To extract myoblasts from a mixed cell corpus, start with an anesthetized yak, euthanized via arterial exsanguination. The exposed skeletal muscle tissue is sterilized with 1 × PBS and 10% doublet antibody then digested and mechanically minced with type II collagenase/neutral protease/CaCl2. Stop enzyme activity with cell culture medium (DMEM + 10% FBS). Post-digestion, centrifuge and filter the tissue, then suspend the cells in F-12 primary myoblast growth medium. Cells were then cultured in collagen-coated dishes to increase cell count over two days, with regular medium changes. Upon reaching 80% confluence, the cells were harvested and preserved.

The purification process involves the utilization of Percoll separation solutions, with densities added to a bovine serum pre-moistened tube. Load the cell concentration onto the Percoll gradient, then centrifuge. Cells collected from the gradient were washed twice. For activity assays, cells were cultured in primary myoblast medium (DMEM/F-12, 20% FBS, and 1% penicillin–streptomycin). Promote cell differentiation by replacing the growth medium with a differentiation medium (DMEM/F-12 + 2% horse serum + 1% penicillin/streptomycin) at 70% cell confluence, which should be changed every 2 to 3 days. Observing cell differentiation and fusion under an inverted microscope will mark the final stage, which yields myotube formations. Load an optimal volume of cells with uniform concentration onto the prepared Percoll gradient and centrifuge at 400 g for 20 to 25 min. Cells obtained from the 27.5–35%, 35–40%, and 40–55% Percoll fractions were categorized as F1, F2, and F3, respectively. The cells collected at the interfaces of the Percoll layers require two washes prior to culturing or conducting tests. For the activity assay, resuspend the cells in a culture dish and expand them in primary myoblast culture medium (DMEM/F-12 supplemented with 20% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin).

Vector construction and cell transfection

RTL1-AdEasy adenovirus system was used. Bovine RTL1 CDS (NM_001195020.1) was ligated into pShuttle-CMV, then recombined with pAdEasy-1 to generate RTL1-OE adenovirus. shRNA sequence was ligated to pShuttle-CMV for sh-RTL1 silencing adenovirus. Control adenoviruses (OE-NC and sh-NC) were also used (Table S1). Over-Expression and knockdown were confirmed by qRT-PCR and Western blot.

Lnc-MEG8: Lentiviral vectors were constructed for both overexpression (MEG8-OE) and silencing (Sh-MEG8) of Lnc-MEG8. HEK293T cells were transfected with a customized plasmid cocktail and incubated. The viral supernatant was collected, filtered, and stored. For silencing, an interference sequence was ligated into the pLVX-shRNA1 vector (Table S2). The constructs were then introduced into host cells, and successful vector construction was confirmed by qRT-PCR.

Bta-miR22-3p: General Biotech synthesized the desired interference/overexpression sequence and ligated it to the pLVX-shRNA1 lentiviral vector. This recombinant vector was transformed into E. coli, amplified, extracted, and sequenced to confirm successful integration. Verified vectors were then transfected into HEK293T cells along with helper vectors to facilitate lentivirus packaging and amplification. The collected lentiviral fluid was subsequently utilized for miRNA manipulation experiments. qRT-PCR analysis confirmed successful vector construction (Table S3).

Cell proliferation assay

The assessment of cell proliferation employed two distinct assays: the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, Wanleibio, Shenyang, China) assay and the 5-ethynyl-2'-deoxyuridine (EdU) proliferation assay. In the CCK-8 assay, yak skeletal myoblast cells were seeded in 96-well plates. Following transfection with OE-MEG8, OE-NC, Si-NC, and Si-MEG8, cell proliferation was gauged using CCK-8, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm. The timepoints for assessment were 0, 24, 48, 72, and 96-h post-transfection.

In the EdU proliferation assay, 10 μM of ethynyl deoxyuridine (EdU) staining solution (Keygentec, Jiangsu, China) was added to each group and incubated for 2 h at 37 °C with 5% CO2 in a cell culture incubator. Subsequently, the cells were collected, centrifuged, and washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). They were then resuspended in 1 mL of 4% paraformaldehyde and incubated in the dark at room temperature for 15 min. After two washes, the cells were resuspended in 500 μL of 1 × saponin permeability-enhancing solution and incubated for 20 min. Following that, the Click-iT reaction mixture was added to each tube or well and incubated at room temperature, shielded from light, for 30 min. In the cell apoptosis detection phase, the cells of interest underwent a single wash, were resuspended in 1 mL of buffer, and were subjected to flow cytometry analysis using the appropriate channel.

Immunofluorescence analysis

Following the protocols of Du et al. [26], well-differentiated yak skeletal myoblasts were washed three times with PBS and then fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 2 h. The cells were subsequently washed and permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 5 min. To block non-specific binding, the cells were incubated with 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) (Sangon, Shanghai, China) for 1 h. Next, the cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with a cocktail of primary antibodies, including anti-Desmin, anti-MYOD, and anti-MYF5 (all from Abcam, Massachusetts, USA). Fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software, and images were captured with an OLYMPUS DP73 microscope (OLYMPUS, Tokyo, Japan).

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

We isolated nuclear and cytoplasmic RNA from cells using the PARIS™ Kit (Invitrogen, Shanghai, China), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, 1 × 10⁷ FaDu cells were collected, washed with PBS, and centrifuged. The cell pellet was resuspended in 300 µL ice-cold Cell Fractionation Buffer and incubated on ice for 10 min. The sample was then centrifuged at 500 g for 5 min at 4 °C, after which the cytoplasmic fraction (supernatant) was carefully collected and stored on ice. The nuclear pellet was washed again with 300 µL ice-cold Cell Fractionation Buffer, centrifuged, and the supernatant discarded. The nuclear pellet was resuspended in 300 µL Cell Disruption Buffer, vortexed thoroughly, and kept on ice for further analysis.

Total RNA from tissue and cell samples was isolated using TRIpure (Bio-Teke, Jiangsu, China). cDNA synthesis was performed with a PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (TaKaRa, Dalian, China) and a Mir-X miRNA First-Strand Synthe-sis Kit (TaKaRa, Dalian, China). Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) reactions were conducted using SYBR Green (Solarbio, Beijing, China) and an Exicycler 96 real-time PCR detection system (BIONEER, Daejeon, USA). Each qPCR analysis was performed in a 20 µL volume, including 2 µL of 1:9 diluted DNA template (< 100 ng), 0.4 µL each of upstream and downstream primers, 10 µL SYBR Green, and 7.2 µL RNase-free ddH2O. The messenger RNA (mRNA) expression levels of target genes were normal-ized to Glyceraldehyde-3-Phosphate Dehydrogenase (GAPDH). The mean cycle threshold (Ct) value of each sample was used to calculate relative gene expression us-ing the 2^-ΔΔCt method. The primer sequences used for qRT-PCR are listed in Table Supplementary Table S4.

Western blot analysis

The protein extraction process commences by extracting proteins from a pre-warmed cell sample lysate containing 1% PMSF. After determining the mass and volume, the lysate is chilled on ice for five minutes and then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm in a refrigerated centrifuge at 4 °C to separate the protein-containing supernatant. Protein quantification is performed by constructing a standard curve using various concentrations of a BSA protein standard solution. Subsequently, a protein test solution is prepared by mixing 1 μl of the sample protein extract with BCA solution in a 50:1 volume ratio. This mixture is incubated at 37 °C for 20 min, leading to a color change from green to purple. The absorbance at 570 nm is measured to determine the protein concentration based on the standard curve.

The protein sample is then applied to an SDS-PAGE gel at the appropriate concentration for electrophoresis. Following separation, the proteins are transferred from the gel to a PVDF membrane, which is then sealed. Specific primary antibodies, such as MyF5 (Abcam, ab125078, 1:10000), MyoG (Abcam, ab77232, 1:1000), GAPDH (Wanleibio, WL01114, 1:1000), and RTL1 (Bio-Rad, orb185997, 1:500), are incubated with the membrane under controlled conditions. Following washing, a secondary antibody (IgG-HRP, Wanleibio, WLA023, 1:5000) is applied to the membrane. Protein bands are visualized via chemiluminescence using ECL reagents and subsequently imaged. Band intensities are quantified with Gel-Pro Analyzer software (Table S5).

Dual-luciferase reporter assay and activity detection

Luciferase reporter plasmids, pmirGLO (Fenghui Biotechnology, Hunan, China), were engineered to contain either the wild-type or mutant 3' untranslated region (UTR) of RTL1 and Lnc-MEG8 genes by Wanleibio (Shenyang, Liaoning, China). These plasmids were then amplified and purified for subsequent experiments. An empty plasmid control was also prepared and purified for comparison.

HEK 293 T cells were cultured and seeded into 96-well plates at a density sufficient to reach 80% confluence within 10–24 h. The cells were co-transfected with the reporter plasmids and either miR-370-3p mimics or a negative control. Each well received 10 ng of plasmid DNA, 0.2 μL of miR-370-3p mimic transfection reagent (20 nM final concentration), and Opti-MEM medium. The transfection reagent was pre-incubated with Opti-MEM for 5 min before complexing with plasmid DNA using Lipofectamine 3000, followed by a 15-min incubation. The transfection mixture was then added to the cells in equal volumes per well, with the medium replaced by fresh medium after 6 h.

After 36 h of incubation, the cells were harvested, and luciferase activity was measured using the Dual-Glo Luciferase Assay System (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). Complete DNA sequences of the vectors are available in Supplementary Table S6.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis of quantitative data (analyzed by the 2^-ΔΔCt method and presented as mean ± SEM) was performed using a two-tailed Student's t-test (GraphPad Prism 8) for normally distributed data. P-values less than 0.05 were considered significant.

Discussion

Cultivated primary skeletal muscle myoblasts serve as a robust model for elucidating the underlying mechanisms of muscle cell growth and maturation [27]. These cells not only mirror in vivo muscle cell development in vitro, but their high fidelity also underscores their utility as an essential resource in research studies [28]. Notably, skeletal myoblasts serve as a reservoir for stem cells, crucial for muscle tissue maintenance and regeneration [29]. However, isolating these cells through traditional collagenase digestion poses challenges. Common approaches rely on flow cytometry markers such as CD34, Sca-1, and α7 integrin [30]. In contrast, our study employed a novel isolation method, deviating from the standard single-step Type II collagenase digestion. Our two-step protocol uses Type II collagenase, neutral protease, and CaCl₂, providing distinct advantages. Type II collagenase, with its potent collagenase and clostridial protease activity, efficiently dissociates cells from various tissues, while calcium ions enhance collagenase activity. Neutral protease, a mild, bacterially derived enzyme, stabilizes under different temperatures and pH, improving basement membrane digestion. This combined approach significantly improves myoblast isolation. Furthermore, we incorporated Percoll purification, which, along with density gradient centrifugation and qRT-PCR, effectively isolates and verifies myogenic cells from yak muscle without the need for expensive equipment.

Our findings reveal a dynamic subcellular localization of Lnc-MEG8 during myogenic differentiation. While Lnc-MEG8 is present in both the nucleus and cytoplasm of proliferating myoblasts, its cytoplasmic enrichment increases as cells differentiate into myotubes. This suggests a potential shift in Lnc-MEG8's function during myogenesis, possibly transitioning from a nuclear role in proliferation to a cytoplasmic role in myotube function. This observed cytoplasmic enrichment aligns with studies demonstrating the presence of functional lncRNAs in the cytoplasm, where they can regulate protein translation or interact with other cytoplasmic factors [31]. Further investigation is required to clarify how Lnc-MEG8 contributes to myogenesis in both the nucleus and cytoplasm. Additionally, the co-expression of Lnc-MEG8 and RTL1 in tissues essential for skeletal muscle growth, such as kidney, fat, and muscle, implies a possible co-regulatory role. Future studies should explore the interaction between Lnc-MEG8 and RTL1 to better understand their roles in skeletal muscle development.

Lnc-MEG8 overexpression inhibits myogenesis, while its silencing promotes it suggesting that Lnc-MEG8 regulates muscle differentiation. MYOG and MYF5 are two well-characterized muscle regulatory factors (MRFs) belonging to the MYOD family of transcription factors. They play essential roles in myogenesis, acting together in a coordinated fashion to initiate and promote the differentiation of myoblasts into mature myotubes [32]. These MRFs bind to specific DNA sequences known as E-boxes within the regulatory regions of muscle genes, thereby promoting their transcription and expression [33]. The downregulation of MYOG and MYF5 following Lnc-MEG8 overexpression suggests that Lnc-MEG8 may act as a negative regulator of myogenesis, possibly by affecting the expression or activity of these key transcription factors. Further studies are required to clarify the molecular mechanisms through which Lnc-MEG8 modulates MYOG and MYF5 expression, and to investigate its specific role in the myogenic differentiation pathway.

The observed upsurge in the S phase cell population upon Lnc-MEG8 overexpression, as revealed by flow cytometry, indicates its potential to promote DNA replication, an essential step for cell division [34]. Conversely, Lnc-MEG8 silencing significantly reduces S-phase cells, likely hindering DNA replication and thereby impeding cell cycle progression. These findings are supported by Edu and CCK-8 assays, which show enhanced proliferation with Lnc-MEG8 overexpression and diminished proliferation with silencing. These results are in line with prior research highlighting the role of lncRNAs in regulating cell cycle progression within muscle cells [35]. Mechanistically, Lnc-MEG8 may influence myoblast proliferation by regulating key cell cycle genes or interacting with proteins involved in DNA replication and mitosis. Further investigation is needed to elucidate the precise molecular mechanisms through which Lnc-MEG8 affects myoblast proliferation.

In yak myogenesis, Lnc-MEG8's regulatory role suggests a potential mechanism via competing endogenous RNAs (ceRNAs). LncRNAs can exert their influence through diverse pathways [31]. Notably, cytoplasmic lncRNAs can function as ceRNAs by sequestering miRNAs and thereby disrupting miRNA-mediated repression of target genes, ultimately regulating myogenesis [36]. This concept is well-supported, as exemplified by Linc-MD1, which acts as a ceRNA for miR-133/135, promoting myoblast differentiation. [37]. Likewise, overexpression of lncRNA MAR1 enhances differentiation by weakening miR-487b's inhibition of Wnt5a [38]. The increased cytoplasmic presence of Lnc-MEG8 during differentiation suggests a similar ceRNA role. This is consistent with prior findings, such as the cell cycle-dependent translocation of MALAT1 from the nucleus to the cytoplasm in C2C12 cells, where it functions as a ceRNA for miR-133, alleviating SRF inhibition and promoting differentiation [39, 40]. These observations support the hypothesis that Lnc-MEG8 functions as a ceRNA in myogenesis.

Through computational prediction, we found that Lnc-MEG8, miR-22-3p, and RTL1 share complementary base-pair sequences. Additionally, during myogenic cell differentiation, miR-22-3p and Lnc-MEG8 exhibit inverse expression patterns. miR-22-3p is recognized as a critical regulator in various developmental and physiological processes, particularly in muscle development. Its involvement in muscle development is well-documented, as evidenced by its differential expression during myogenesis in Hu sheep [41]. Furthermore, miR-22-3p regulates the proliferation and differentiation of porcine skeletal muscle cells [22]. In C2C12 cells, miR-22-3p functions as a negative regulator of proliferation while promoting differentiation and the shift from fast-twitch to slow-twitch muscle fibers [42, 43]. Overexpression of miR-22-3p in Hu sheep inhibits proliferation and promotes differentiation of skeletal muscle cells by targeting IGFBP3 [24]. In mice, RTL1 deficiency and overexpression similarly impact skeletal muscle cell proliferation and myoblast structure [44]. Deficient skeletal muscle cells proliferated 1.5-fold more than wild-type, while overexpressed skeletal muscle cells showed an 0.8-fold decrease. This suggests RTL1 suppresses skeletal muscle cells proliferation and is required for normal rates. Myoblasts derived from these cells showed structural weakness, with some cells detaching and rounding [45]. Based on these observations, we hypothesized that Lnc-MEG8, miR-22-3p, and RTL1 interact in a regulatory network critical for yak myoblast differentiation. Dual-luciferase assays confirmed that miR-22-3p directly targets both Lnc-MEG8 and RTL1. Co-transfection with Lnc-MEG8 and miR-22-3p mimic rescued RTL1 expression, supporting this regulatory interplay. Furthermore, during myoblast differentiation, Lnc-MEG8 effectively inhibits the miR-22-3p-promoted activation of RTL1, as well as muscle differentiation marker genes, MYF5, and MYOG. It also suppresses myotube formation, further validating the significant contribution of Lnc-MEG8-miR-22-3p-RTL1 regulatory axis in regulating yak myoblast differentiation.

Conclusions

Our study reveals a novel regulatory network in yak myogenesis, with Lnc-MEG8 serving as a central hub. This Lnc-MEG8-miR-22-3p-RTL1 axis plays a crucial role in regulating muscle development in yaks. We identified Lnc-MEG8 as a key regulator with dynamic localization and established its function as a competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) for miR-22-3p, which influences RTL1 and differentiation markers. These findings enhance our understanding of the regulatory mechanisms governing yak myogenesis and p provide a basis for future research aimed at enhancing yak growth and muscle quality.
