Commentary on “impact of transversus abdominis plane block on length of stay and postoperative opioid use in anterior lumbar interbody fusions”
Authors: Annie L McDonald, Jonathan N Grauer
Published: 2025

Drexelius et al. [1] recently published a retrospective, single-center, cohort study in the North American Spine Society Journal. In this study, they sought to identify the correlation of transversus abdominis plane (TAP) blocks on postoperative opioid use and hospital length of stay for patients undergoing anterior lumbar interbody fusion (ALIF) with or without associated posterior percutaneous instrumentation.

This is a timely topic as ALIF use continues to be on the rise, and there is increasing focus on minimizing postoperative narcotic use and optimizing patient experience and outcomes following surgeries in general. To this end, erector spinae blocks are being considered for posterior procedrues [2]. Also, TAP blocks are widely used in general surgery, but their efficacy with ALIF has been mixed [[3], [4], [5]]. In their study, Drexelius et al. [1] thus sought to expand upon the sparse literature evaluating the effectiveness of TAP blocks for this patient population.

The authors reviewed the electronic medical record of all patients aged 18 years or older who underwent 1- or 2-level ALIF procedures with or without posterior percutaneous instrumentation at their academic institution. Patients were excluded if they underwent concomitant lateral interbody fusions, open posterior spinal decompression, revision surgery, 3 or more level surgery, and those experiencing significant complications not directly related to the TAP block. Notably, TAP blocks were performed with ultrasound guidance at the discretion of the anesthesiologist and in discussion with the surgeon.

In total, 295 patients met eligibility criteria for inclusion, for which TAP block was performed for 102 (34.6%). Demographics, including preoperative narcotic use, were not different between the 2 groups. No complications associated with the TAP blocks were identified.

In bivariable analysis, there were no statistically significant differences in the average length of stay (LOS, measured in hours) between patients who received a TAP block (41.2±33.4 hours) and those who did not (40.8±33.4 hours). Multivariable analysis showed that, regardless of TAP block, LOS corelated with patient age, obesity, and number of levels fused, but not preoperative opioid use or patient sex.

Regarding postoperative opioid usage, patients who had a TAP block actually received significantly more morphine milliequivalents (MME) on postoperative day zero and on combined postoperative days 1 and 2 than those without a TAP block. The authors note in their discussion that this may have been contributed to by bias in who got the blocks, but nonetheless TAP blocks were not found to shorten LOS or decrease postoperative opioid usage in the study population.

Overall, the reviewed paper represents a moderately large sample size that is reported to be the largest patient cohort for assessing TAP blocks for ALIF to date. The researchers took a methodical approach to review their patient data and took into consideration what they determined to be potentially confounding variables. A key limitation of the study is its retrospective design, which also precludes control over the selection criteria for patients who received a TAP block. Furthermore, the study did not assess pain management beyond their hospitalization or clinical satisfaction/outcomes between patients who received a TAP blocks relative to those who did not.

The findings of this reviewed study are provocative in that they question the utility of using a TAP block for patients undergoing ALIF. In the context of prior studies having mixed results on the utility of TAP blocks in this patient population, the current study casts doubt on this practice. These findings should prompt clinicians to critically consider the routine use of TAP blocks during ALIF. For those who support their efficacy, potentially prospective, randomized, controlled trials could be considered.
