Refined Shockley–Queisser Framework–Guided Acceptor Design Enables Loss-Aware Bandgap Targeting in Organic Solar Cells
Authors: Zhe Sun, Wonjun Kim, Sangjin Yang, Thi Le Huyen Mai, Jiyu Park, Hee-Seung Lee, Yongjoon Cho, Changduk Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Precise bandgap tuning is critical for maximizing the power conversion efficiencies (PCEs) of organic solar cells (OSCs). Here, we refined the Shockley–Queisser model by incorporating sub-bandgap absorption and nonradiative losses, predicting an optimal optical bandgap (E g) of ∼1.41 eV for Y-series acceptor-based OSCs, higher than the ideal SQ value of 1.34 eV. Guided by this loss-aware target, we designed and synthesized a new acceptor (YCF3-BO) with CF3-terminated core- and branched outer-side chains to achieve the target E g in PM6:YCF3-BO devices. The resulting binary and ternary devices achieved PCEs of 19.8% and 20.2%, respectively. This performance arises from two synergistic effects: tuning E g into the refined optimum window enhances photoluminescence quantum yield and suppresses nonradiative recombination, while branched side chains promote a 3D charge-transport network. Furthermore, PM6:YCF3-BO-based photocathodes operated efficiently in underwater solar hydrogen production. This study establishes a unified framework integrating loss-aware bandgap theory with acceptor design and solid-state packing control for advancing organic photovoltaics.

Introduction

Organic solar cells (OSCs) combine mechanical flexibility, light weight, semitransparency, and large-area processability, offering a promising route toward next-generation photovoltaic technologies. − Since the emergence of Y-series acceptors (particularly Y6), the power conversion efficiencies (PCEs) of OSCs have advanced significantly from ∼16% to over 20%, driven by the systematic evolution of their molecular framework through core functionalization, side-chain engineering, and end-group modification. − Notably, the prototypical PM6:Y6-based binary OSC has achieved certified PCE exceeding 19% under optimized processing conditions. , This advancement raises two critical questions: (i) what is the remaining performance ceiling of the Y6 scaffold, and (ii) how can rational design strategies be implemented to transcend incremental modifications and enable the discovery of next-generation Y-series acceptors with substantial performance gains?

According to Shockley–Queisser (SQ) theory, the detailed-balance limit for a single-junction solar cell under AM 1.5G illumination is ∼33.7% PCE, corresponding to an ideal optical bandgap (E g) of ∼1.34 eV. , Historically, significant leaps in OSC performance have aligned with successive narrowing of E g: from the early P3HT:PCBM (∼1.88 eV), to PTB7:PCBM (∼1.65 eV), to PM6:ITIC (∼1.55 eV), , and to PM6:Y6 (∼1.33 eV), as illustrated in Figure A and summarized in Table S1. This narrowing of E g in OSC devices has expanded light absorption into the near-infrared (NIR), boosting the short-circuit current density (J SC) and thereby improving PCEs. However, the red-shifted absorption associated with narrower E g values entails inherent trade-offs, such as diminished charge-transfer (CT) state separation efficiency and elevated nonradiative recombination losses (Figure B), which collectively limit the achievable PCE. − Therefore, establishing a theoretically sound and experimentally viable optimal E g window for Y-series acceptor-based OSC systems remains an unresolved challenge in materials design.

In this work, we refined the ideal SQ model by incorporating sub-bandgap absorption tailing and nonradiative voltage lossesphenomena intrinsic to OSCs. The resulting model predicts a revised optimal E g of ∼1.41 eV, notably higher than the 1.34 eV given by the ideal SQ limit. Guided by this insight, we designed and synthesized a benzotriazole-based Y-series acceptor, YCF3-BO, featuring a CF3-terminated core side chain and branched outer side chains. This molecular design enables precise E g tuning to coincide with the predicted optimum window. Note that our previously reported analogue YCF3 with linear n-undecyl outer-side chains has a narrower E g of 1.370 eV. , Charge-transport and recombination studies confirmed that the widened-E g PM6:YCF3-BO system not only retains efficient charge transport but also suppresses nonradiative recombination. As a result, PM6:YCF3-BO-based OSCs delivered notable PCEs of 19.8% and 20.2% in the binary and ternary blends, respectively. Furthermore, our investigation identified improved charge extraction as the primary factor responsible for the superior performance of the self-assembled monolayer (SAM) hole-transport layer (HTL) over PEDOT:PSS, while the effect of optical transmittance was relatively small. In the course of this work, the photocathode based on the best-performing PM6:YCF3-BO system demonstrated remarkable operational efficiency in underwater solar hydrogen production under spectrally filtered illumination.

Results and Discussion

Modification of the SQ Model

In the SQ model, the absorber is assumed to have an ideal step-function absorptance a(E), wherein all photons with E ≥ E g are absorbed with 100% quantum efficiency, while those with E < E g are not absorbed: a(E)={1,E≥Eg0,E<Eg

Unlike the idealized case in the SQ model, organic semiconductors exhibit a broadened absorption edge due to intrinsic energetic disorder (Figure C). , Kaiser et al. showed that the subgap absorption of Y-series acceptor systems follows an exponential kT tail. We therefore describe the sub-bandgap absorptance as a(E)={1,E≥Egexp(E−EgkT),E<Eg

Fits to the subgap FTPS-EQE spectra of nine donor–acceptor blends containing various Y-series acceptors (Figure D and Figures S1–S2) give apparent Urbach energies of ∼24 meV, close to kT = 25.7 meV at 298 K, supporting the use of this form.

Nonradiative recombination represents another key loss mechanism that limits the performance of OSCs below the ideal SQ limit. In the SQ framework, the internal radiative recombination fraction f c (defined as the ratio of radiative recombination to total recombination) is assumed to be unity, implying exclusively radiative recombination. In reality, OSCs suffer substantial nonradiative recombination. − Based on electroluminescence external quantum efficiency (EQEEL) measurements of nine donor–acceptor blend films incorporating various Y-series acceptorsand assuming a typical light-outcoupling efficiency of 20%we estimated f c to be on the order of 10–3 (Figure E). , This value is several orders of magnitude lower than that assumed in the ideal SQ model, underscoring the necessity of including an f c correction in realistic OSC efficiency analyses.

When subgap absorption and nonradiative recombination are included, the refined SQ optimum shifts upward from the classical 1.34 eV value to ∼1.41eV (Figure F, calculation details in Supporting Information). , This upward shift reflects the trade-off whereby, beyond a threshold, further narrowing of the bandgap yields only marginal improvement in J SC, while inducing disproportionately large losses in open-circuit voltage (V OC) due to increased sub-bandgap absorption and nonradiative recombination. A robustness analysis further indicates that this conclusion is insensitive to reasonable variations in the nonradiative voltage loss (ΔV nr). As ΔV nr varies from 100 to 300 mV, corresponding to f c = 10–2 to 10–5, the predicted optimum remains confined to a narrow range around 1.41 eV (Figure G). This trend is consistent with recent reports of state-of-the-art OSCs, which increasingly favor higher V OC values, as a pathway to improve PCE (Figure H).

Molecular Design and Photovoltaic Performance

Guided by the above theoretical insights, we designed and synthesized a new Y-series acceptor (YCF3-BO) to shift the effective device bandgap toward the refined optimal window predicted by our model. While sharing the same CF3-terminated core-side chain as our previously reported benzotriazole-based analogue (YCF3), YCF3-BO features branched 2-butyloctyl outer-side chains in place of linear n-undecyl groups (Figure A). The synthetic route of YCF3-BO is outlined in Figure S3, and detailed optimization procedures are provided in Supporting Information. All new compounds were unambiguously characterized by 1H-/13C-/19F NMR and mass spectrometry (Figures S4–S10). Density functional theory (DFT, B3LYP/6–31G) confirms that YCF3-BO and YCF3 exhibit nearly identical electrostatic potential maps and highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO)/lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) distributions, indicating minimal perturbation to the electronic structure (Figures S11 and S12). Consistently, cyclic voltammetry (CV) yields similar HOMO/LUMO energies of −5.63/–4.00 eV for YCF3 and −5.65/–3.98 eV for YCF3-BO, and both acceptors provide suitable energetic offsets with PM6 (Figure B and Figure S13).

UV–vis absorption spectra in dilute solution show that YCF3-BO and YCF3 have very similar optical absorption profiles, confirming that the electronic transition energies of the π-conjugated core remain essentially unchanged (Figure C). In contrast, in films, YCF3-BO exhibits a noticeable blue shift in both the absorption maximum and onset relative to YCF3, indicating altered intermolecular interactions and packing behavior in the solid state. The incorporation of the branched side chains appears to mitigate overly tight π–π stacking and reduce aggregation-induced red shift of the film spectrum.

OSC devices with the architecture ITO/2PACz/PM6:acceptor/H75/Ag were fabricated to evaluate photovoltaic performance. , The current density–voltage (J–V) characteristics were measured using an aperture mask (Figure S14). The J–V characteristics, statistical distributions of PCEs, and optimized photovoltaic parameters are presented in Figure D, E and Table . The OSC based on PM6:YCF3 delivered a PCE of 19.2% (V OC = 0.848 V, J SC = 29.1 mA cm–2, fill factor (FF) = 78.0%), while substitution with YCF3-BO increased the PCE to 19.8% (V OC = 0.905 V, J SC = 27.2 mA cm–2, FF = 80.4%). These results show that, despite a reduction in J SC (see EQE spectra and integrated current in Figure F), the higher V OC and FF together result in a net PCE improvement. The bandgap was determined using the derivative-based method reported by Rau et al., which is compatible with SQ-type analysis. , By this definition, the bandgap shifts from 1.370 eV for PM6:YCF3 to 1.420 eV for PM6:YCF3-BO (Figure G and H). Further comparison between idealized and measured FF shows that bandgap optimization corresponds to absolute FF gains of 0.34% and 0.53% in the classical SQ and refined-SQ frameworks, respectively; the latter accounts for ∼22% of the observed FF enhancement (Figure S15). This indicates that our branched-side-chain design not only raises the intrinsic FF limit through bandgap widening, but that the larger FF gain also points to improved charge transport and recombination behavior, as discussed in the next section. Furthermore, incorporating 10 wt % D18 donor polymer into the PM6:YCF3-BO blend raised the PCE to 20.2% (Figure S16), demonstrating a viable route for surpassing the 20% efficiency threshold in single-junction OSCs.

Charge Dynamics and Energy Loss

To clarify how the side-chain modification affects charge transport and energy loss, we conducted comprehensive investigations of charge-transport and recombination dynamics. Space-charge-limited current (SCLC) measurements reveal that the PM6:YCF3-BO blend possesses a higher electron mobility and a more balanced electron-to-hole mobility ratio compared to the PM6:YCF3 counterpart (Figure A and Figure S17). Photogenerated charge extraction by linearly increasing voltage (photo-CELIV) experiments further corroborate these findings: the PM6:YCF3-BO device displays an average mobility of 2.85 × 10–4 cm2 V–1 s–1, markedly higher than the 1.43 × 10–4 cm2 V–1 s–1 observed for PM6:YCF3 (Figure S18). These findings confirm that outer side-chain engineering widens the bandgap while preserving, and even enhancing, carrier transport properties. Complementary measurements of effective voltage and light-intensity dependence further show that the PM6:YCF3-BO device exhibits more efficient charge generation and extraction, together with suppressed carrier recombination (Figures S19 and S20). , The slightly higher relative dielectric constant of YCF3-BO (εr = 3.1 versus 2.8 for YCF3; Figure S21) is also consistent with the more favorable charge dissociation and transport characteristics observed in the blend. ,

Transient photovoltage (TPV) and transient photocurrent (TPC) analyses were performed to probe the recombination kinetics. The carrier lifetime (τc) was plotted as a function of carrier density n, following the relation τc=τ0(nn0)−λ where τ0 is a fitting constant and the exponent (λ) is proportional to the order of nongeminate recombination (Figure S22). As shown in Figure B, λ = 1.46 for the PM6:YCF3-BO device is smaller than 1.74 obtained for PM6:YCF3, indicating more suppressed nongeminate recombination. The recombination rate coefficient, k(n)=1τc(n)×n further confirms a markedly reduced recombination rate in PM6:YCF3-BO device (Figure C). These results show that the branched side-chain design in YCF3-BO improves the balance between charge transport and recombination losses.

Energy-loss analysis in Figure D and Table S2 shows that PM6:YCF3-BO exhibits lower overall energy loss than PM6:YCF3, with the loss partitioned into a radiative loss above the bandgap (ΔE 1), a radiative loss below the bandgap (ΔE 2), and a nonradiative loss (ΔE 3). While ΔE 1 and ΔE 2 remain largely unchanged, the higher EQEEL of PM6:YCF3-BO corresponds to a 17 meV reduction in ΔE 3 (Figure E), indicating that the energy-loss difference is dominated by nonradiative recombination. This behavior is consistent with the intrinsic photophysical properties of the acceptor. For neat Y-series acceptors, steady-state PL reveals a quantum yield (PLQY) of ∼1.5% for YCF3-BO, nearly twice that of YCF3 (∼0.8%), indicating fewer nonradiative decay pathways in YCF3-BO (Figure S23). , Time-resolved PL (TRPL) further supports this observation, with YCF3-BO exhibiting a substantially longer PL lifetime of 976.2 ps, compared to 662.9 ps for YCF3 (Figure F), reflecting reduced nonradiative dissipation. , In addition, YCF3-BO displays a smaller Stokes shift of 0.086 eV versus 0.118 eV for YCF3, signifying less structural relaxation upon excitation (Figure S24). , Together, these results identify YCF3-BO as the more emissive acceptor with higher intrinsic radiative efficiency, consistent with its smaller ΔE 3 and lower overall energy loss.

Crystal Structures and Film Morphology

To establish the crystallographic origin of the transport differences, single crystals of YCF3-BO were grown and compared with those of YCF3 (Figure A and B, Figures S25–S27 and Table S3). The two acceptors adopt divergent packing topologies: In YCF3, the molecules align serially along a single crystallographic direction to form polymer-like linear chains. Driven by “side-chain–end-group” contacts between the CF3-terminated side chains and the terminal end groups, these chains further register laterally to assemble into a 2D lamellar framework, with characteristic spacings of 15.6 and 28.5 Å. By contrast, the steric bulk and conformational versatility of the branched outer side chains in YCF3-BO engender multidirectional “side-chain–side-chain” contacts among the CF3-terminated chains, yielding a densely interwoven 3D mesh-like lattice with shorter repeat distances of 8.7 and 14.9 Å. Notably, whereas only two dimer configurations (Y′ and S′) are observed within the YCF3 unit cell, YCF3-BO additionally accommodates the S configuration. These crystallographic distinctions anticipate enhanced 3D connectivity for charge transport in YCF3-BO.

Grazing-incidence wide-angle X-ray scattering (GIWAXS) was employed to investigate the molecular packing of YCF3 and YCF3-BO in both neat and blended films (Figure C). All films adopt predominantly face-on orientation, as evidenced by the pronounced out-of-plane π–π (010) stacking peaks. In contrast to YCF3, the YCF3-BO neat film and its blend exhibit an additional (021) peak in the low-q region, signifying a tilted unit-cell packing motif with end groups oriented toward the substrate, which enables the formation of a 3D charge-transport network. , In line with this structural feature, the PM6:YCF3-BO blend exhibits a slightly larger π–π stacking distance and a shorter crystal coherence length (Table S4)parameters that would normally suggest lower mobility. Nevertheless, the 3D connectivity afforded by YCF3-BO compensates for these drawbacks, enabling excellent charge transport and device performance. These results validate that judicious outer side-chain engineering can harmonize π–π interactions with an optimized 3D transport network.

Atomic force microscopy (AFM) height images reveal comparable root-mean-square roughness (RMS) for both blends; however, the PM6:YCF3-BO film exhibits a more continuous and finely interwoven fibrillar network (Figure D). Statistical analysis verifies that relative to PM6:YCF3, the fibrils in the PM6:YCF3-BO blend are not only thicker but also more uniformly distributed, providing efficient percolation pathways for exciton diffusion and charge transport (Figure E).

To further clarify how the branched outer side chains shape the solid-state structure, we investigated the film-formation kinetics of YCF3-BO and YCF3. In situ UV–vis absorption monitoring during spin-coating of neat Y-series acceptor solutions reveals three successive stages: an initial solvent-evaporation stage, in which overall absorbance rapidly decreases while the peak wavelength remains constant; a nucleation and crystal-growth stage, marked by a steady red shift of the absorption peak position; and a dry-film stage, where both peak position and intensity stabilize. During the nucleation and crystal-growth stage, YCF3 completes aggregation within approximately 170 ms, whereas YCF3-BO requires about 240 ms (Figure F and Figure S28). The absorption-peak red-shift slope, which quantifies aggregation rate, is 0.447 nm ms–1 for YCF3 and 0.271 nm ms–1 for YCF3-BO, further confirming that the branched outer side chains substantially retard molecular aggregation. These distinct crystallization kinetics lead to different molecular packing arrangements and, consequently, result in a widened E g in the solid state. In situ absorption measurements on PM6:YCF3 and PM6:YCF3-BO blends show that both donor and acceptor aggregate more slowly in the PM6:YCF3-BO blend, yielding a gentler and more controllable phase evolution that favors optimal nanoscale morphology (Figure G,H and Figure S29).

Influence of Interlayers and Spectral Filtering

Beyond the intrinsic material properties discussed above, extrinsic factors such as device architecture and operating environment also play a critical role in determining OSC performance. Notably, most recently reported OSCs with PCE > 20% have employed a SAM as the HTL instead of the conventional PEDOT:PSS. , A key advantage of SAM-based HTLs lies in their exceptionally low parasitic absorption, which can improve the optical transmittance and contribute to improved photocurrent generation.

To quantify the potential optical gain, we calculated the transmitted fraction of the AM 1.5G solar spectrum through ITO/PEDOT:PSS and ITO/2PACz stacks. Indeed, the ITO/2PACz configuration exhibits a slightly higher transmission in the near-infrared region compared to the ITO/PEDOT:PSS (Figure A and Figure S30). However, upon integrating the active-layer absorption under these transmitted spectra, the projected enhancement in J SC due to the improved transmittance is estimated to be only ∼0.5% for both PM6:YCF3 and PM6:YCF3-BO devices. This modest optical gain falls short of the experimentally observed increase in J SC (∼3.5%) when replacing PEDOT:PSS with 2PACz (Figure B, Figure S31 and Table S5). Therefore, the primary benefit of using a SAM interlayer lies not in the marginal change in optical benefits, but rather in its ability to improve electrical characteristics such as enhancing charge extraction and suppressing interface recombination losses. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) and TPC measurements further support this interpretation: the SAM-based devices show reduced charge accumulation and faster transient extraction, indicative of more efficient charge transport/extraction at the anode interface (Figure C). This interpretation is consistent with the report by Kim et al., who showed that PEDOT:PSS can create a PSS–-rich, p-doped intermixed region at the HTL/active-layer interface, leading to photocurrent loss and reduced charge collection at the anode. In conclusion, while the use of a SAM layer marginally increases optical transmittance, the predominant contribution to performance improvement stems from the mitigation of electrical losses, particularly at the HTL/active-layer interface.

We further assessed device performance in underwater solar hydrogen production (Figure D), where long-wavelength NIR photons are strongly attenuated by water, effectively blue-shifting the incident spectrum toward the visible. − OSC-based photocathodes were illuminated with 1 sun at the water surface and evaluated at depths of 1, 3, 5, and 8 cm (Figure E). For PM6:YCF3, the photocurrent densities (J ph) at 0 V versus RHE dropped from 26.5 to 15.2 mA cm–2, whereas PM6:YCF3-BO showed a more moderate decline, from 26.3 to 18.5 mA cm–2. Correspondingly, the onset potential (V onset) shifted from 0.65 to 0.47 V for YCF3, but only from 0.66 to 0.54 V for YCF3-BO. These results indicate that the slightly wider bandgap and reduced nonradiative loss of YCF3-BO are more favorable under NIR-filtered illumination, enabling more effective harvesting of visible photons and better retention of photovoltage. Consequently, under spectrally constrained underwater operating conditions, the V OC and FF advantages imparted by precise bandgap tuning are amplified, endowing markedly enhanced operational robustness.

Conclusion

In summary, this work presented a loss-aware bandgap optimization strategy for Y-series acceptor-based OSCs by integrating refined theoretical modeling with molecular and solid-state design. By refining the SQ model to include sub-bandgap absorption and nonradiative recombination losses in OSCs, we identified an optimal E g of ∼1.41 eV for single-junction OSCs. Based on the preceding findings, we developed a new acceptor, YCF3-BO, with a CF3-terminated core side chain and branched outer side chains, which achieved the E g of 1.420 eV and enabled high efficiency: 19.8% PCE in a binary device (PM6:YCF3-BO) and up to 20.2% in a ternary blend. The superior performance of YCF3-BO-based OSCs originated from two synergistic effects: (i) widened E g enhanced PLQY and suppressed nonradiative recombination losses, and (ii) the branched outer side chains induced a tilted molecular packing motif, establishing an interconnected 3D charge transport network. We further show that the advantage of SAM-based interlayers over PEDOT:PSS is dominated by improved charge extraction rather than optical transmittance. Finally, PM6:YCF3-BO-based photocathodes exhibit efficient operation in underwater solar hydrogen production under spectrally filtered illumination, highlighting the relevance of bandgap-targeted acceptor design under constrained spectra. Overall, these results provide a unified framework linking bandgap theory, acceptor molecular design, and solid-state packing control for efficient and robust organic photovoltaics.
