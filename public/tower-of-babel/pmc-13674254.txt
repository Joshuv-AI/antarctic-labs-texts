Lactate-Informed Exercise Prescription in Mild Cognitive Impairment and Alzheimer’s Disease: A Translational Framework for Preserving Cognition, Mobility, and Independence
Authors: Xiaolin Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis narrative review proposes a conceptual, lactate-informed framework for individualized exercise prescription in mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and Alzheimer’s disease (AD). Exercise is recommended in these populations, but fixed external doses do not account for heterogeneity in disease stage, frailty, sarcopenia, cardiovascular risk, or recovery.Main TextWe integrate clinical exercise evidence, geriatric assessment, lactate physiology, muscle-brain signaling, implementation science, and trial methodology. The framework uses peripheral blood lactate as one component of a monitoring matrix with workload, heart rate, blood pressure, perceived exertion, fatigue recovery, gait safety, adherence, and caregiver support. Repeated, standardized lactate responses are classified as low, adaptive and recoverable, excessive, or non-interpretable/unsafe, prompting stepwise adjustment of aerobic, resistance, balance, and cognitive-motor training. Peripheral blood lactate is an internal-load marker; it does not directly quantify blood-brain barrier transport, CNS lactate kinetics, or target engagement. Mechanistic pathways involving monocarboxylate transport, HCAR1, BDNF/VEGF, myokines, and lactylation therefore remain hypotheses to be tested in humans with MCI or AD.ConclusionsThe novelty of the framework is its integration of disease stage, geriatric phenotype, peripheral metabolic response, and recovery into clinically actionable dose decisions rather than a universal lactate threshold. Prospective multicenter trials should standardize sampling, validate response thresholds, and determine whether this approach improves cognition, mobility, independence, safety, and caregiver-relevant outcomes beyond usual advice or fixed-dose exercise.

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is one of the major causes of cognitive and functional decline in older adults, but its clinical burden cannot be adequately captured by memory impairment alone. In geriatric care, AD increasingly presents as a multidimensional syndrome involving cognition, mobility, balance, activities of daily living, caregiver dependence, fall risk, and progressive loss of independence. Motor decline may precede or parallel cognitive decline in older adults, suggesting that impaired mobility should not be treated only as a late-stage complication of dementia.1 Consistent with this view, gait and balance impairments have been observed even in very mild AD, while falls are increasingly recognized as a major safety and independence issue in this population.2,3 Therefore, interventions for AD should be evaluated not only by cognitive scales, but also by their capacity to preserve mobility, daily function, safety, and caregiver-manageable independence.

Exercise is widely recommended for older adults and for individuals with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) or dementia, yet its translation into AD care remains underdeveloped. Current recommendations often emphasize increasing physical activity, but they frequently fail to specify exercise modality, intensity, progression, monitoring, safety thresholds, and phenotype-specific adaptation. This is a central problem because exercise is not a single intervention. Aerobic training, resistance training, balance and gait training, and dual-task training act on partly different physiological and functional systems. International recommendations for older adults emphasize the need to prescribe exercise with attention to intensity, type, frequency, progression, and safety rather than offering vague advice.4 Similarly, collaborative international guidance supports physical activity and exercise for prevention and management of MCI and dementia, while also acknowledging variation in certainty of evidence and the need for more clinically actionable implementation strategies.5

The heterogeneous results of exercise trials in dementia further illustrate why generic advice is insufficient. For example, the DAPA randomized controlled trial showed that moderate-to-high intensity aerobic and strength training improved physical fitness but did not slow cognitive impairment in people with mild-to-moderate dementia.6 This finding should not be read simply as evidence against exercise. Rather, it highlights that the same fixed exercise package may not match the biological and functional heterogeneity of older adults with cognitive impairment. By contrast, evidence from MCI and early AD suggests that earlier disease stages may retain greater plasticity and may represent a more appropriate window for interventions targeting cognition, vascular function, metabolic health, and functional reserve. In cognitively unimpaired older adults with elevated amyloid burden, higher daily step counts have been associated with slower amyloid-related tau accumulation and slower cognitive and functional decline.7 In symptomatic but early disease, randomized and controlled trials have also suggested potential cognitive, vascular, and functional benefits of physical activity or aerobic training, although effects remain variable.8,9

A major reason for this variability is that the external exercise prescription does not necessarily reflect the internal physiological stimulus. Two older adults may receive the same walking, cycling, or resistance-training program, but their metabolic response may differ because of frailty, sarcopenia, cardiorespiratory reserve, medication use, vascular disease, adherence, supervision, and recovery capacity. Sarcopenia and frailty are especially relevant because they influence gait speed, grip strength, chair-rise ability, fall risk, tolerance of training, and the ability to generate and recover from a meaningful exercise stimulus.10,11 Thus, an exercise prescription for older adults with MCI or AD should be individualized not only by cognitive diagnosis, but also by physical phenotype and physiological response.

Contracting skeletal muscle communicates with the brain through a coordinated set of myometabolites, myokines, neural signals, and hemodynamic changes. Lactate is relevant because it is both a readily sampled peripheral marker of muscular metabolic demand and, in healthy humans, a potential cerebral fuel during marked hyperlactatemia and exercise.12,13 It should nevertheless be treated as one signal within this broader muscle-brain cross-talk, not as proof that a circulating lactate value represents a central neurobiological dose.

Lactate may help address this prescription gap. Blood lactate reflects the balance between production, transport, oxidation, clearance, and recovery during and after exercise, but a single value is highly sensitive to workload, sampling site, timing, peripheral perfusion, recent diet and glycogen status, medication, analyzer or strip performance, and operator technique.14,15 These sources of variability are especially relevant in older adults with vascular disease, diabetes, cold or poorly perfused extremities, and impaired symptom reporting. Point-of-care testing also requires equipment, consumables, quality control, staff training, and additional visit time, so routine measurement may be infeasible or unjustified in some clinics. A pragmatic framework should therefore standardize the device and sampling schedule, interpret within-person trends rather than isolated values, and use lactate only when the result can alter a dose or safety decision.

The biological rationale for lactate-informed exercise prescription is supported by evidence that lactate can participate in muscle-brain communication. Experimental studies have shown that lactate may mediate exercise-related effects on learning and memory through hippocampal BDNF signaling, influence cerebral VEGF and angiogenesis via the lactate receptor HCAR1, and support synaptic protection in AD-like models.16–18 At the same time, lactate biology in AD is context-dependent. Acute, transient exercise-induced lactate pulses may support adaptive signaling, whereas chronic pathological lactate-lactylation burden may participate in microglial metabolic dysfunction and neuroinflammatory amplification.19 This duality makes it essential to avoid a simplistic “more lactate is better” interpretation. However, the extent to which an exercise-induced peripheral pulse changes CNS lactate exposure in aging or AD is unknown; these mechanistic observations do not validate peripheral lactate as a surrogate for central target engagement.

This review therefore proposes a lactate-informed exercise prescription framework for older adults with MCI and AD. The framework positions lactate as one component of a broader monitoring strategy that also includes disease stage, frailty, sarcopenia, gait safety, cardiovascular risk, heart rate, blood pressure, perceived exertion, fatigue recovery, adherence, caregiver support, and clinically meaningful outcomes. The central aim is not to maximize lactate, but to identify a safe and repeatable therapeutic window in which exercise provides sufficient metabolic stimulation without worsening fatigue, falls, cardiovascular instability, or functional vulnerability. The framework is intended as a translational tool to be tested prospectively, not as a validated clinical guideline.

Existing older-adult and dementia guidance uses frequency, intensity, time, type, progression, heart rate, perceived exertion, physical function, and safety, while threshold-based models individualize intensity but rarely integrate AD stage, frailty, caregiver capacity, and recovery. The proposed framework is distinct in combining those geriatric variables with standardized peripheral lactate trends and explicit low, adaptive, excessive, and unsafe response rules. It does not prescribe a universal lactate target or replace guideline-based exercise; it is an add-on decision structure for testing whether internal-load verification improves delivery of multimodal exercise.4,5,20

Accordingly, this clinical narrative review integrates evidence from AD staging, geriatric functional assessment, exercise interventions, lactate biology, muscle-brain communication, implementation science, and trial-design methodology. It argues that exercise care in AD should move beyond generic lifestyle advice toward individualized, measurable, and phenotype-specific prescription. The ultimate clinical objective is to preserve cognition, mobility, safety, daily independence, and caregiver-manageable function in older adults across the AD continuum.

Search Strategy and Evidence Selection

This review was developed through targeted searches of PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and Google Scholar, supplemented by backward and forward citation tracking of key trials, consensus statements, systematic reviews, and mechanistic studies. Search concepts included Alzheimer’s disease, mild cognitive impairment, exercise prescription, aerobic training, resistance training, dual-task training, lactate threshold, blood lactate, muscle-brain axis, sarcopenia, frailty, myokines, lactylation, activities of daily living, mobility, falls, caregiver burden, and implementation.

Priority was given to human studies in MCI, prodromal AD, and mild-to-moderate AD; major geriatric exercise and dementia guidance; randomized or controlled exercise trials; systematic reviews and meta-analyses; and mechanistic studies that clarify lactate transport, signaling, neurovascular effects, myokines, or lactylation. Evidence from animal and cellular models was included only when it informed plausible translational pathways, and such evidence is interpreted separately from clinical efficacy evidence. Because the included literature spans heterogeneous populations, interventions, mechanisms, and outcomes, effect-size pooling was not attempted.

Why Generic Exercise Advice is Insufficient in Alzheimer’s Disease

Alzheimer’s Disease as a Disorder of Cognition, Mobility, and Independence

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is still often introduced as a memory disorder, but this framing is too narrow for clinical intervention in older adults. In geriatric practice, AD is better understood as a progressive syndrome that disrupts cognition, gait, balance, physical function, daily independence, and caregiver sustainability. Motor decline may even precede measurable cognitive decline in some older adults, indicating that mobility impairment should not be treated as a secondary or late-stage complication only.1

This broader view is clinically important because balance and gait deficits can be detected even in very mild AD, while falls are substantially more common in AD than in cognitively normal aging. These impairments directly threaten mobility, safety, and community living, and they are not adequately captured by memory scores alone.2,3

Functional decline is equally central to the lived burden of AD. Loss of independence in basic ADL, particularly feeding and bathing, has been associated with increased caregiver burden, reinforcing that the clinical endpoint of AD care is not merely cognitive preservation but the maintenance of independence, dignity, and care feasibility.21 Therefore, exercise interventions for older adults with MCI or AD should be judged not only by changes in cognitive scores, but also by their ability to preserve gait, balance, ADL/IADL, fall safety, and caregiver sustainability.

The Clinical Gap in Current Exercise Recommendations

Exercise is widely recommended for older adults and for people with MCI or dementia, but current recommendations often remain too generic for clinical implementation. Statements such as “exercise more” or “increase physical activity” do not specify the therapeutic dose, target phenotype, progression strategy, supervision level, physiological monitoring, or safety thresholds needed for older adults with cognitive impairment. This is a serious limitation because exercise, like pharmacological therapy, has modality, dose, intensity, frequency, progression, adherence, and adverse-event dimensions.4

The heterogeneity of exercise trials in dementia illustrates this gap. The DAPA randomized controlled trial found that a moderate- to high-intensity aerobic and strength exercise program improved physical fitness but did not slow cognitive impairment in people with mild-to-moderate dementia. This result should not be interpreted as proof that exercise is clinically useless; rather, it highlights that a fixed exercise package may not match the biological and functional heterogeneity of dementia patients.6

By contrast, supervised multimodal exercise has shown benefits for strength, balance, mobility, and endurance in people with cognitive impairment and dementia, suggesting that physical-function outcomes may be more responsive and clinically meaningful than cognition alone in some stages of disease.22 The problem, therefore, is not that exercise lacks rationale, but that current clinical advice often fails to individualize exercise according to disease stage, frailty, sarcopenia, cardiometabolic status, fall risk, adherence capacity, and physiological response.

Rationale for Lactate-Informed Exercise Prescription

Lactate offers one possible way to make exercise prescription more measurable and individualized. It should not be framed as a single therapeutic target for AD. Rather, lactate can be used as a physiological signal linking exercise intensity, skeletal muscle metabolic engagement, cardiorespiratory tolerance, recovery capacity, and potential brain-relevant adaptation. This distinction is important: the aim is not to maximize lactate, but to identify whether a patient has received a sufficient, tolerable, and repeatable metabolic stimulus.

Mechanistic evidence supports lactate as a candidate mediator of exercise-brain communication. Experimental work has shown that lactate can mediate exercise-induced learning and memory effects through SIRT1-dependent activation of hippocampal BDNF signaling.16 Lactate signaling may also influence cerebral angiogenesis and neurovascular adaptation through HCAR1-dependent pathways.17 These findings justify translational interest, but they do not establish blood lactate as a direct surrogate for cognitive benefit in patients with MCI or AD.

More directly relevant to AD, recent preclinical work reported that treadmill exercise increased lactate, improved cognitive performance, and enhanced synapse-associated protein expression in AD-like models; exogenous lactate reproduced part of the benefit, while blockade of lactate transport attenuated exercise-induced protection.18 Although these findings do not justify using lactate as a stand-alone treatment in older adults with AD, they provide biological plausibility for using lactate response as part of a broader exercise-monitoring framework.

Clinically, lactate-informed prescription may help distinguish at least three scenarios: insufficient metabolic stimulation, adaptive stimulation, and excessive physiological stress. A low lactate response may suggest inadequate exercise intensity, poor muscle metabolic reserve, or poor adherence. Excessive lactate accumulation or delayed recovery may indicate that the session is too stressful for the patient’s current phenotype. An adaptive response is best defined not by a single lactate number, but by a recoverable rise accompanied by stable cardiovascular response, acceptable perceived exertion, preserved gait safety, and gradual functional adaptation.

Scope of This Review

This article is designed as a clinical narrative review, not a meta-analysis, perspective article, or basic mechanistic review. A narrative review is appropriate here because the central task is not to pool effect sizes, but to integrate heterogeneous evidence from geriatric medicine, dementia care, exercise physiology, neurobiology, rehabilitation, implementation science, and clinical trial design. The manuscript therefore emphasizes translational logic, mechanistic plausibility, clinical feasibility, and testable research questions.

The review focuses on older adults with MCI, prodromal AD, and mild-to-moderate AD. Its core question is not whether exercise is generally beneficial, but how exercise can be prescribed, monitored, progressed, and individualized to preserve cognition, mobility, and independence. Existing international guidance supports physical activity and exercise for the prevention and management of MCI and dementia, but also acknowledges that the certainty of evidence varies and that practical implementation requires clearer clinical specification.5

Accordingly, this review proposes a lactate-informed exercise prescription framework in which disease stage, frailty, sarcopenia, cardiometabolic status, fall risk, adherence capacity, and physiological response guide the selection and adjustment of aerobic, resistance, balance, and cognitive-motor training. Lactate is positioned as one candidate monitoring signal within this framework, not as a diagnostic biomarker or disease-modifying therapy. The intended contribution is to move AD exercise care beyond generic lifestyle advice toward a measurable, staged, and clinically actionable intervention model for older adults. Clinical Evidence and Sources of Heterogeneity in Exercise Interventions

Evidence Across Disease Stages

The clinical relevance of exercise in Alzheimer’s disease (AD) differs substantially across disease stages. In the preclinical phase, when amyloid and tau pathology may already be detectable but cognition remains largely preserved, physical activity is best conceptualized as a risk-modifying and trajectory-modifying exposure rather than as symptomatic treatment. Recent longitudinal evidence in cognitively unimpaired older adults indicates that higher daily step counts are associated with slower amyloid-related tau accumulation and slower cognitive and functional decline, particularly among individuals with elevated baseline amyloid burden.7 Earlier biomarker work also suggested that physical activity may attenuate age-related AD biomarker alterations in cognitively normal adults at increased risk, supporting the view that the earliest disease stages may provide the widest window for modifying downstream decline.28

In symptomatic but early disease, including mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and prodromal AD, exercise may still act on modifiable physiological systems with sufficient reserve: vascular function, insulin sensitivity, cardiorespiratory fitness, skeletal muscle performance, sleep, mood, and executive control. The randomized trial by Lautenschlager et al showed that a home-based physical activity intervention improved cognition in older adults with subjective memory impairment or MCI-level risk for AD, with benefits persisting beyond the active intervention period.8 In older adults with MCI, aerobic exercise has also been associated with cognitive improvement and sex-specific metabolic effects, although the sample size was modest and the intervention was not designed as a disease-modifying AD trial.9

More recent MCI trials further suggest that exercise may be particularly valuable before functional dependence is established. A one-year aerobic exercise trial in amnestic MCI demonstrated feasibility and physiological effects, including improved cardiorespiratory fitness, although cognitive effects were not uniformly robust.23 Related imaging work reported that 12 months of aerobic training altered regional brain perfusion in patients with MCI, reinforcing the idea that early clinical stages may retain vascular and metabolic plasticity relevant to exercise prescription.24

In mild-to-moderate AD, the expected clinical target should shift. Exercise should not be presented primarily as a tool to reverse cognitive decline. Rather, its more realistic role is to maintain mobility, reduce falls, slow functional dependence, support neuropsychiatric stability, and preserve caregiver-manageable daily routines. The Finnish Alzheimer Disease Exercise Trial found that long-term, intensive exercise rehabilitation in home-dwelling people with AD could benefit physical functioning and reduce fall risk, even when cognition was not the dominant endpoint.25 Similarly, aerobic exercise in early AD has shown signals for preserving functional ability and linking fitness gains with memory performance, but the evidence remains insufficient to support a uniform cognitive treatment claim.26

Taken together, the stage-specific evidence supports a pragmatic interpretation: MCI and early AD are likely the most appropriate windows for cognition-oriented exercise trials, whereas later AD requires a more geriatric goal structure focused on mobility, fall prevention, ADL preservation, behavioral stability, and delayed dependence.

Evidence Across Exercise Modalities

The exercise literature in MCI and AD should not be reduced to a binary question of whether exercise “works”. Different modalities target different physiological systems, and these systems are variably impaired across older adults with cognitive decline. Aerobic exercise primarily challenges cardiorespiratory fitness, vascular function, insulin sensitivity, and whole-body metabolic regulation. This makes it biologically plausible for MCI and early AD, where vascular and metabolic reserve may still be modifiable. In MCI, controlled aerobic exercise has been associated with cognitive benefits, while longer aerobic training has been linked to changes in cerebral perfusion, suggesting that aerobic training may act partly through vascular-metabolic mechanisms.9,24

Resistance training addresses a different but equally important clinical problem: the overlap between cognitive impairment, sarcopenia, frailty, slow gait, and loss of independence. In seniors with probable MCI, resistance training has been reported to promote cognitive and functional brain plasticity, and the SMART trial showed that progressive resistance training, alone or combined with cognitive training, improved global cognition in MCI.27,29 This evidence supports the view that resistance training should not be treated as an accessory intervention; for older adults with AD risk, it may be central to preserving the muscular substrate required for mobility, adherence, and metabolic responsiveness.

Multicomponent exercise may be especially appropriate for older adults with cognitive impairment because it combines aerobic, resistance, balance, flexibility, and often cognitive-motor elements. A randomized trial in older adults with MCI showed that multicomponent exercise improved cognitive outcomes and was associated with changes in brain atrophy markers, while a related trial in amnestic MCI found improvement in maximal walking speed even when dual-task reaction-time outcomes were not clearly improved.30,31 These findings are clinically useful because the goal in geriatric AD care is rarely improvement in a single biological domain. Patients need interventions that simultaneously address endurance, strength, balance, gait, and cognitive-motor control.

Dual-task training is particularly relevant because walking in real-world environments is rarely a purely motor task. Older adults with MCI and dementia often need to walk while navigating distraction, conversation, obstacles, medication routines, or environmental hazards. Meta-analytic evidence suggests that dual-task training can improve cognition, gait, physical function, and depressive symptoms in older adults with cognitive impairment, although effects vary by task design and study quality.32 Clinically, this supports the inclusion of cognitive-motor tasks not as an experimental add-on, but as a bridge between cognitive rehabilitation and fall-risk reduction.

A modality-specific interpretation is therefore needed. Aerobic exercise may be best suited to cardiovascular and metabolic stimulation; resistance training to sarcopenia, strength, and functional reserve; multicomponent exercise to broad geriatric needs; and dual-task training to the interface between cognition and mobility. No single modality is likely to be optimal for all older adults with MCI or AD.

Why Trial Results Remain Inconsistent

The inconsistent results of exercise trials in MCI and AD are not surprising. The clinical populations are heterogeneous, the interventions are complex, and the outcomes often differ from the physiological systems being targeted. Disease stage is a major source of variability: a cognitively unimpaired amyloid-positive person, a patient with amnestic MCI, a physically robust patient with mild AD, and a frail patient with moderate AD are unlikely to respond to the same exercise stimulus in the same way. International guidance now recommends physical activity and exercise for prevention and management of MCI and dementia, but it also acknowledges that certainty of evidence varies and that recommendations must be translated into more specific clinical practice models.5

Baseline physical capacity is another major determinant. Frailty, sarcopenia, slow gait, poor balance, low cardiorespiratory fitness, pain, multimorbidity, and polypharmacy can limit training intensity and progression. Sarcopenia is now defined as a muscle disease rooted in adverse muscle changes across the life course, and it directly affects strength, gait, balance, and independence.10 Frailty guidelines similarly emphasize that physical vulnerability is modifiable but requires structured identification and tailored management, not generic advice.11

Adherence and supervision further complicate interpretation. Exercise trials frequently report prescribed dose, but the biologically relevant dose is the dose actually completed at sufficient intensity and with adequate progression. In older adults with MCI or dementia, adherence is influenced by cognitive symptoms, caregiver support, supervision, transportation, motivation, neuropsychiatric symptoms, safety concerns, and fatigue. A systematic review of adherence to exercise interventions in older adults with MCI and dementia showed that adherence is highly variable and should be considered an active determinant of trial efficacy rather than a secondary operational issue.33

Outcome selection is another reason exercise trials appear inconsistent. Cognitive scales alone may miss clinically meaningful effects on gait, balance, ADL, falls, sleep, mood, caregiver burden, or institutionalization risk. Conversely, a trial powered for mobility or ADL may not be able to detect small cognitive changes. Consensus work on core outcomes for dementia interventions has emphasized the need for outcomes that reflect meaningful change for people living with dementia and their caregivers, rather than relying narrowly on one domain.34

Finally, most exercise studies in MCI and AD do not adequately monitor physiological response. Heart rate, rating of perceived exertion, achieved workload, recovery, fatigue, and metabolic markers are inconsistently reported, and lactate response is rarely used to verify whether the exercise stimulus was sufficient, excessive, or adaptive. This is a major limitation because two patients may receive the same written prescription but experience very different internal loads. Methodological guidance for nonpharmacological trials emphasizes that complex interventions require transparent reporting of intervention components, delivery, adherence, and implementation context.35

Thus, inconsistent trial results should not be read as evidence that exercise is ineffective in AD. The stronger interpretation is that exercise has often been prescribed, delivered, and evaluated too crudely for a heterogeneous geriatric neurodegenerative population. Exercise is not the problem; imprecise prescription is.

Lactate and the Aging Muscle-Brain Axis

Lactate as a Measurable Signal of Exercise Dose

For a lactate-informed exercise prescription to be clinically useful in older adults with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) or Alzheimer’s disease (AD), lactate should first be understood as a marker of internal exercise load rather than as a direct therapeutic readout. Blood lactate concentration reflects the balance between lactate production, transport, oxidation, clearance, and redistribution across tissues during and after exercise. It is therefore influenced by exercise intensity, active muscle mass, cardiorespiratory fitness, metabolic flexibility, sampling site, timing of measurement, and training status. This makes lactate clinically informative, but also context-dependent.14,36

The appeal of lactate is that it can provide information not captured by a written prescription. Two older adults may both be asked to walk for 30 minutes at a “moderate” intensity, but their internal metabolic responses may differ markedly. One may remain below a meaningful metabolic stimulus, whereas another may exceed tolerance and experience prolonged fatigue. Lactate threshold concepts have therefore been used for decades to individualize exercise intensity, although the exact threshold definition remains methodologically debated.15,37

For older adults, this issue is especially important because aging changes the relationship between external workload and internal physiological stress. Training above or below lactate threshold produces different adaptations in older adults, and lactate kinetics differ between trained and untrained individuals.38,39 Heart rate and rating of perceived exertion remain useful, but both can be distorted by beta-blockers, autonomic dysfunction, anxiety, cognitive impairment, pain, or poor symptom reporting. Lactate may therefore complement, rather than replace, heart rate, perceived exertion, and functional observation.40

The clinical message is cautious. Lactate can help determine whether an exercise prescription produces insufficient, adequate, or excessive metabolic stimulation, but it should not be used as a stand-alone predictor of AD improvement. In metabolic syndrome, lactate threshold-based and maximum heart rate-based high-intensity interval prescriptions can produce comparable health benefits, reinforcing that lactate-guided approaches are one route to individualization, not the only route.41 In MCI and AD, lactate should therefore be interpreted as part of a multimodal monitoring panel: workload, heart rate, blood pressure, perceived exertion, fatigue recovery, gait safety, adherence, and functional change.

Peripheral Lactate, the Blood-Brain Barrier, and CNS Signaling

Peripheral lactate can reach the healthy human brain, but transport is gradient- and context-dependent rather than a direct readout of a CNS dose. Lactate crosses the cerebrovascular endothelium through proton-linked monocarboxylate transport, with MCT1 prominent at the blood-brain barrier; subsequent cellular handling involves MCT isoforms expressed by astrocytes and neurons. In six healthy adults studied with arterial-jugular sampling and1–13 lactate, systemic lactate contributed 8% of cerebral energy expenditure at baseline, 19% during lactate infusion, and 27% during infusion plus exercise; net cerebral lactate uptake during exercise was 0.28 ± 0.16 mmol/min.13 A later healthy-human study found that cerebral uptake during incremental exercise tracked the rise in arterial lactate concentration, supporting a concentration-gradient mechanism rather than a fixed fraction transferred to the brain.42 These studies demonstrate uptake and oxidation in healthy adults, not in older people with MCI or AD.

Translation to AD is uncertain. Blood-brain barrier breakdown occurs with aging in the hippocampus,43 yet greater nonspecific permeability does not imply preserved MCT-mediated flux or neuronal use. Human AD cerebrovascular tissue shows reduced GLUT1 with unchanged MCT1 abundance,44 but postmortem abundance cannot establish transporter kinetics during exercise. CSF lactate also varies with age and blood-brain barrier integrity and did not consistently track AD severity or core CSF biomarkers in a 312-participant study.45 To our knowledge, no human study has directly quantified arterial-to-brain lactate flux during acute exercise in MCI or AD. Peripheral blood lactate must therefore be treated as an index of internal whole-body load and recovery, not a proxy for brain lactate, histone lactylation, or neuroprotection.

Within the brain, lactate may serve as both an energy substrate and a signaling molecule. The classical astrocyte-neuron lactate shuttle hypothesis proposed that glutamate uptake by astrocytes stimulates glycolysis and lactate release, thereby supporting neuronal energy metabolism during activity.46 Although this model has been debated, subsequent work showed that astrocyte-neuron lactate transport is required for long-term memory formation, providing a mechanistic link between lactate transport and cognition-relevant plasticity.47

At the cellular level, lactate can support synaptic plasticity through mechanisms beyond simple energy provision. Lactate has been shown to potentiate NMDA receptor-dependent signaling and induce expression of plasticity-related genes, while astrocyte-derived lactate can fuel learning-induced mRNA translation in excitatory and inhibitory neurons.48,49 These findings are relevant to AD because synaptic dysfunction is a major correlate of cognitive decline, but they should be interpreted as biological plausibility rather than direct proof of clinical benefit.

In rodents, acute prolonged exercise has been reported to increase brain region-specific expression of monocarboxylate transporters, including MCT1, MCT2, and MCT4, as well as metabolic proteins involved in glucose transport and oxidative metabolism.50 This finding suggests adaptive regulation of transport machinery but cannot establish human blood-brain barrier kinetics. Repeated, tolerable metabolic stimulation may matter more than a single acute lactate peak, but that proposition requires direct testing in MCI and AD.

Preclinical evidence also suggests that lactate may influence the neurovascular unit. HCAR1 provides one mechanism by which lactate can act as a signaling molecule rather than merely a fuel. Activation of HCAR1 can modulate neuronal network activity, while exercise-related HCAR1 signaling has been linked to cerebral angiogenesis pathways.51,52 These findings do not show that a peripheral lactate measurement quantifies receptor activation in the human AD brain.

This mechanistic evidence justifies continued study of lactate within an exercise framework, but it does not validate peripheral blood lactate as a CNS biomarker or make lactate the center of AD treatment. In older adults with MCI or AD, muscle-to-brain lactate signaling remains plausible rather than demonstrated. It should remain subordinate to the clinical question: whether the patient can safely achieve and repeat a physiologically meaningful exercise dose.

Sarcopenia, Frailty, and Metabolic Reserve

A lactate-informed exercise model cannot be separated from the aging muscle. In older adults with MCI or AD, the same written exercise prescription may generate very different lactate responses because skeletal muscle mass, muscle strength, mitochondrial capacity, gait efficiency, frailty status, and cardiometabolic reserve differ substantially between individuals. Sarcopenia is now defined as a progressive skeletal muscle disorder involving loss of muscle strength, muscle quantity or quality, and physical performance; this definition places muscle function, not simply muscle mass, at the center of clinical risk assessment.10,53

The overlap between sarcopenia and cognitive impairment is clinically meaningful. A recent meta-analysis showed a significant interrelationship between sarcopenia, MCI, AD, and other dementias, while longitudinal data from CHARLS suggested that sarcopenia and MCI are linked over time in older adults.54,55 This matters because exercise responsiveness in AD may depend partly on whether the patient has enough muscular reserve to generate, tolerate, and recover from the intended metabolic stimulus.

Simple physical measures may also carry prognostic information. Gait speed and grip strength have been associated with cognitive decline and dementia risk, and their combination may improve risk identification in older adults.56,57 These measures are cheap, repeatable, and feasible in memory clinics and geriatric outpatient settings, making them practical companions to lactate monitoring.

Frailty adds another layer. Physical frailty reflects vulnerability to stressors, reduced physiological reserve, and higher risk of adverse outcomes; cognitive frailty attempts to capture the intersection between physical vulnerability and cognitive impairment.58,59

The implication is direct: lactate response must be interpreted through the patient’s muscle and frailty phenotype. A low lactate response in a robust older adult may indicate insufficient exercise intensity, but in a sarcopenic or frail patient it may reflect low muscle mass, poor recruitment, or limited metabolic reserve. Conversely, a high lactate response in a frail patient may reflect excessive physiological stress rather than a desirable adaptive signal. Therefore, lactate-informed exercise prescription should begin with muscle and frailty assessment, not with lactate alone.

Myokines: Causal Mediators or Parallel Responses?

Irisin/FNDC5, cathepsin B, IGF-1, and apelin should be treated as parallel contraction-responsive components of a broader exerkine network, not automatically as downstream products of lactate. Exercise intensity, active muscle mass, energetic stress, calcium signaling, sympathetic drive, and training status can influence both lactate and myokine release; temporal co-occurrence therefore does not establish mediation.12

For irisin, contraction-related PGC-1α/FNDC5 signaling has been linked to hippocampal BDNF, and preclinical AD studies support synaptic and memory effects.60–62 Lactate can also converge on SIRT1/BDNF-related pathways,16 but convergence at BDNF does not show that a peripheral lactate pulse is necessary or sufficient for irisin secretion. Moreover, assay heterogeneity and limited AD-specific human data preclude using irisin as an exercise-dose marker.

Cathepsin B secretion has been associated with running and memory across mice, monkeys, and humans,63 whereas IGF-1 and apelin effects on neurogenesis, aging muscle, cognition, or neuroinflammation are supported mainly by animal studies.64–66 Other exercise-responsive metabolites, including β-hydroxybutyrate, can also influence BDNF-related signaling.67 The cited experiments do not establish a quantitative lactate-to-cathepsin B or lactate-to-apelin pathway.

The most defensible interpretation is that these molecules are partly parallel responses to exercise, with possible pathway convergence and context-specific cross-talk rather than a validated linear cascade. Serial concentration changes alone would still be insufficient to infer causality because exercise dose and active muscle mass are common causes of both lactate and exerkine responses.

Future mechanistic studies should collect serial lactate and myokines on matched time scales, test whether lactate or MCT/HCAR1 perturbation changes myokine release, and use mediation analyses linked to imaging or fluid biomarkers. Until then, myokines strengthen the biological plausibility of muscle-brain communication but should not guide clinical dose adjustment; peripheral lactate, heart rate, perceived exertion, gait, recovery, and function remain the practical monitoring variables.

Beneficial Lactate Pulses vs Maladaptive Lactate-Lactylation Burden

The most important conceptual advance is to distinguish acute exercise-induced lactate pulses from chronic pathological lactate-lactylation burden. In the exercise context, lactate rises transiently, returns toward baseline with recovery, and may participate in adaptive signaling related to synaptic plasticity, neurovascular function, and immune modulation. In the AD brain, however, chronic glycolytic reprogramming, inflammatory activation, impaired metabolic flexibility, and altered epigenetic regulation may create a very different lactate environment. These two states should not be conflated.

The protective side of the model is supported by evidence that exercise-induced lactate can contribute to neuroplastic and anti-inflammatory adaptations. Exercise-induced lactate has been reported to improve cognitive dysfunction and neuroinflammation through histone H3 lactylation in microglia in mouse models, suggesting that lactate-derived epigenetic signaling may sometimes support beneficial microglial phenotypic transition.68 This fits the broader view that histone lactylation can function as a metabolite-sensitive epigenetic modification rather than merely a marker of metabolic stress.69

The pathological side is equally important. In AD, microglial glucose metabolism can become locked into a harmful positive-feedback loop. A key study reported that histone H4 lysine 12 lactylation promotes a glycolysis/H4K12la/PKM2 feedback cycle in microglia, worsening AD-related pathology; inhibiting this cycle reduced amyloid burden and improved cognitive defects in models.19 This finding is central because it prevents a simplistic “lactate is good” narrative.

Other work supports a broader lactate-lactylation-neuroinflammation axis. In senescent microglia and AD-like models, elevated lactate and H3K18 lactylation have been linked to NF-κB signaling and senescence-associated inflammatory programs.70 Similarly, microglial lactate metabolism has been proposed as a therapeutic target in neuroinflammatory disease, with lactate-derived histone lactylation acting as a bridge between metabolic state and inflammatory transcription.71

This double-edged model also aligns with the broader literature on immunometabolism. Activated microglia undergo metabolic reprogramming toward glycolysis during neuroinflammation, and metabolic state can shape inflammatory phenotype, phagocytosis, and tissue repair.72,73 Inflammatory microglia may also disrupt neuronal lactate handling, suggesting that lactate availability alone does not guarantee effective neuronal utilization.74

The clinical interpretation is straightforward. The target of exercise prescription is not to drive lactate as high as possible. The target is to produce safe, transient, recoverable, and repeatable lactate pulses that occur within the patient’s functional and cardiometabolic tolerance. In older adults with MCI or AD, excessive fatigue, poor recovery, falls, cardiovascular instability, or persistent inflammatory stress should override any theoretical enthusiasm for higher lactate. A clinically responsible lactate-informed model therefore seeks a therapeutic window: enough metabolic stimulus to engage adaptive muscle-brain signaling, but not enough physiological stress to worsen frailty, neuroinflammation, or functional decline. A Clinical Framework for Lactate-Informed Exercise Prescription

Baseline Assessment Before Prescribing Exercise

A lactate-informed exercise prescription should begin with clinical staging, not with lactate measurement. Older adults across the Alzheimer’s disease (AD) continuum differ markedly in cognitive reserve, functional dependence, frailty, cardiometabolic risk, fall risk, and caregiver support. Therefore, the first step is to determine whether the patient is in a preclinical AD stage, mild cognitive impairment (MCI) or prodromal AD, mild AD, or moderate AD. The NIA-AA biological framework and clinical diagnostic recommendations provide the conceptual basis for distinguishing biomarker-defined AD from clinical syndromes, while MCI and dementia criteria remain essential for real-world care planning.75–77

Cognitive assessment should include a brief global screen and, when feasible, a domain-specific measure sensitive to memory, executive function, and processing speed. The Mini-Mental State Examination remains widely used, but it has ceiling effects and limited sensitivity in early disease; the Montreal Cognitive Assessment is more sensitive to MCI and executive dysfunction; and ADAS-Cog remains useful in clinical trials and pharmacological AD research.78–80

Functional assessment is equally important because the goal of exercise in older adults with MCI or AD is not merely to change test scores, but to preserve independence. Basic ADL and IADL should be recorded at baseline because they identify the practical consequences of cognitive impairment and define clinically meaningful targets for intervention.81,82

Mobility and muscle function should be measured before prescribing intensity. Gait speed, Timed Up and Go, Short Physical Performance Battery, 6-minute walk distance, grip strength, chair-stand performance, and muscle mass help identify whether the patient can safely perform aerobic training, needs resistance training first, or requires supervised balance and fall-prevention work before metabolic progression.83–85

Frailty, polypharmacy, cardiovascular disease, orthostatic symptoms, diabetes, pain, visual impairment, depression, and caregiver availability should also be considered before any lactate-guided progression. A frailty index can quantify accumulated deficits, while medication review is essential because psychoactive drugs, antihypertensives, hypoglycemic therapies, and anticholinergic burden can alter heart rate, fall risk, fatigue, and adherence.86,87 Thus, baseline assessment should define both the exercise target and the safety boundary.

Lactate and Physiological Monitoring

Lactate monitoring should be embedded in a broader physiological monitoring strategy. Resting lactate may provide a baseline metabolic reference; post-exercise lactate can indicate whether the session produced a meaningful metabolic stimulus; and recovery-phase lactate can help determine whether the stimulus was tolerable and adequately cleared. However, lactate alone is insufficient. It must be interpreted with heart rate, blood pressure, rating of perceived exertion, fatigue recovery, gait safety, and functional change.

For clinical and trial use, lactate sampling should be standardized rather than opportunistic. A pragmatic minimum set is resting lactate before exercise, post-exercise lactate at a fixed time point after the target bout, and recovery lactate at fixed recovery windows such as 3–5 and 10 minutes when feasible. The exact protocol should remain consistent within a patient and within a study. Interpretation should emphasize trends across repeated sessions rather than isolated values, because hydration, recent meals, glycogen availability, medication effects, anxiety, sampling technique, and peripheral circulation can shift single measurements.

Perceived exertion is particularly useful in older adults because it captures subjective effort when heart-rate targets are distorted by medications, autonomic dysfunction, anxiety, or poor fitness. The Borg scale has long been linked to physiological effort, and perceived exertion has been shown to correlate with both heart rate and blood lactate during exercise.88–90

Heart rate monitoring remains practical, but it should not be treated as perfectly reliable. Clinical exercise screening recommendations emphasize the need to match exercise testing and prescription to individual cardiovascular risk, while wearable heart-rate devices vary in accuracy depending on device type, exercise intensity, motion artifact, and user characteristics.91–93

Blood pressure and fatigue recovery should be monitored because older adults with cognitive impairment frequently have hypertension, diabetes, vascular disease, autonomic symptoms, or medication-related hemodynamic instability. Exercise can be beneficial for hypertension and cardiometabolic health, but the prescription must be adapted to risk profile, medication effects, and recovery capacity.94,95

Wearables may help track step count, heart-rate trends, sleep, sedentary time, and adherence, but they should support—not replace—clinical judgment. Training-load monitoring literature emphasizes that single metrics are weaker than integrated interpretation of external load, internal load, recovery, and performance trends.96,97 For MCI and AD, lactate should therefore be used as one element of a practical monitoring matrix: metabolic response, cardiovascular response, subjective effort, fatigue recovery, mobility safety, and function.

Core Exercise Prescription Components

The core prescription should include aerobic training, resistance training, balance and gait training, and cognitive-motor dual-task training. This multimodal structure is not cosmetic; it reflects the fact that older adults with MCI or AD have overlapping deficits in cardiorespiratory fitness, muscle strength, balance, gait automaticity, executive control, and functional independence. Standard exercise prescription principles require specification of frequency, intensity, time, type, progression, and recovery rather than vague lifestyle advice.98

Aerobic training should be prescribed to improve cardiorespiratory capacity, vascular function, metabolic flexibility, and fatigue tolerance. In older adults, public-health recommendations support moderate-to-vigorous aerobic activity, but AD-specific prescriptions must start from the patient’s stage, fall risk, and supervision capacity rather than from population targets alone.99,100

Resistance training should be considered essential, not optional. It addresses sarcopenia, chair-rise ability, gait speed, postural control, and resilience to daily physical stressors. Contemporary position statements emphasize that resistance training in older adults can improve strength, muscle mass, functional performance, and cardiometabolic health when appropriately progressed.101,102

Balance and gait training are necessary because falls and mobility impairment are major clinical consequences of cognitive impairment. Exercise programs that target balance, gait, and functional movement reduce falls in community-dwelling older adults, and these components are especially relevant when cognitive impairment reduces attentional control during walking.103,104

Cognitive-motor dual-task training should be included when safety allows because real-world mobility requires simultaneous cognitive and motor control. Dual-task exercise may improve balance, gait control, and executive-motor integration, although intervention design and progression must be individualized.105 The clinical prescription should therefore specify not only what patients do, but how the program progresses and how recovery is handled.

Lactate-Informed Dose Adjustment

Lactate-informed dose adjustment should classify the patient’s response rather than impose a uniform lactate target. The goal is to determine whether the prescribed exercise is too weak, too stressful, or appropriately adaptive. This principle aligns with broader exercise-science evidence showing that relative intensity prescriptions based on fixed percentages of maximal heart rate or VO2max may expose different individuals to very different internal loads, while threshold-based methods may better align exercise intensity with individual physiology.106,107

Low lactate responders may not be receiving enough metabolic stimulus, but this interpretation should be cautious. Low response may reflect insufficient intensity, poor adherence, limited muscle recruitment, sarcopenia, low glycogen availability, or impaired peripheral metabolic reserve. Evidence on training response variability shows that apparent “non-response” may be reduced by adjusting training dose, although true biological variability and measurement error must be carefully distinguished.106,108

Excessive lactate or fatigue responders require the opposite adjustment. A high lactate response accompanied by prolonged fatigue, unstable blood pressure, poor sleep, worsened gait safety, or reduced next-day function should not be interpreted as a superior training stimulus. Exercise can occasionally produce adverse cardiometabolic responses, and older adults with cognitive impairment may be less able to report symptoms reliably.107,109

Adaptive responders are those who show a modest and recoverable lactate rise, stable cardiovascular response, acceptable perceived exertion, preserved gait safety, and gradual improvement in mobility, strength, endurance, or daily function. This group may continue progressive overload, but progression should remain stepwise and anchored to both physiological response and functional outcomes. Studies of inter-individual adaptation show that dichotomous responder/non-responder labels can be misleading unless measurement error, baseline variability, and confidence intervals are considered.110,111

A practical lactate-informed adjustment framework should therefore avoid rigid targets. Instead, it should use lactate trends to guide clinical decisions: increase intensity or resistance work in low responders when safe; reduce intensity, extend recovery, and emphasize low-intensity activity in excessive responders; and maintain gradual progression in adaptive responders. Phenotype-Specific Prescriptions

A lactate-informed prescription should be adapted to clinical phenotype. For preclinical AD and subjective cognitive decline, the goal is prevention and maintenance of cognitive reserve. Here, exercise should be framed as part of multidomain risk reduction, alongside vascular risk monitoring, sleep, diet, cognitive engagement, and social participation. Preclinical AD and subjective cognitive decline frameworks support early identification of at-risk individuals before overt functional dependence develops.112,113

For MCI and prodromal AD, the goal is to delay cognitive and complex functional decline while the patient can still follow instructions, build habits, and participate in supervised or semi-supervised training. MCI is a heterogeneous syndrome, but its intermediate position between normal aging and dementia makes it a rational target for exercise and multidomain interventions.114–116

For mild AD, the prescription should prioritize maintenance of ADL/IADL, gait speed, mood, sleep, and caregiver-manageable routines rather than expecting robust cognitive reversal. Home-based exercise combined with caregiver behavioral management has been shown to improve physical health and reduce institutionalization-related outcomes in AD care models, supporting the need to involve caregivers as part of the intervention rather than as passive observers.117

For moderate AD or frailty, the prescription should shift further toward safety, basic mobility, transfers, fall prevention, and preservation of self-care capacity. Nursing-home and institutional AD studies suggest that exercise may help maintain physical function, but this population requires high supervision, low-to-moderate intensity progression, environmental safety, and simplified routines.118

For sarcopenia-dominant cognitive impairment, resistance training should be prioritized before aggressive aerobic progression. Strength and muscle mass are not cosmetic endpoints; they determine chair-rise ability, gait stability, fall resilience, metabolic capacity, and the ability to tolerate aerobic training. Resistance exercise meta-analysis supports its role in improving strength in older adults, and this is directly relevant to patients whose low lactate response may reflect inadequate muscle recruitment rather than insufficient willingness.102

For vascular-metabolic comorbidity, including hypertension, diabetes, obesity, and subcortical vascular cognitive impairment, prescriptions should emphasize blood pressure, glucose safety, heart-rate response, fatigue, and recovery. Aerobic exercise has shown cognitive effects in adults with mild subcortical ischemic vascular cognitive impairment, while diabetes guidelines emphasize careful integration of aerobic and resistance exercise with medication and hypoglycemia risk management.119,120

Finally, all phenotypes should be interpreted within a prevention framework. Population-level analyses suggest that modifiable risk factors account for a substantial proportion of AD cases, while physical activity guidelines support structured aerobic, strengthening, and balance work for older adults when adapted to health status.121,122 Thus, phenotype-specific prescription is not a deviation from the lactate-informed model; it is the condition that makes lactate interpretation clinically meaningful.

Outcomes, Implementation, and Future Research

Outcomes That Matter Clinically

The evaluation of lactate-informed exercise prescription in older adults with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) or Alzheimer’s disease (AD) should move beyond a narrow cognition-only model. Cognitive outcomes remain necessary, but they are insufficient as the sole endpoint because AD affects global function, mobility, safety, daily independence, neuropsychiatric stability, caregiver burden, and long-term care needs. Cognitive assessment should include global measures such as MMSE and MoCA, trial-oriented scales such as ADAS-Cog, and domain-specific measures of executive function, processing speed, and memory. Global severity measures such as the Clinical Dementia Rating may also help interpret whether changes in cognition translate into clinically meaningful disease-stage progression.123

However, clinically meaningful benefit in older adults with AD should be defined at least as much by preservation of mobility and independence as by cognitive change. Gait speed, Timed Up and Go, Short Physical Performance Battery, 6-minute walk distance, balance, chair-rise performance, and falls should be treated as core outcomes, not secondary conveniences. These measures are particularly relevant because mobility impairment directly increases fall risk, caregiver dependence, hospitalization risk, and loss of community living. A trial that fails to improve ADAS-Cog but slows deterioration in gait speed, fall frequency, ADL, or caregiver burden may still be clinically valuable.

Independence outcomes should include ADL, IADL, quality of life, and caregiver burden. Quality of life in dementia is multidimensional and cannot be inferred from cognition alone; patient and proxy perspectives may diverge, and both are clinically relevant.124 Caregiver burden should also be measured because exercise interventions in AD often rely on caregiver supervision, transportation, cueing, and safety monitoring.125 Neuropsychiatric symptoms should not be ignored, as agitation, apathy, depression, sleep disturbance, and irritability can determine adherence and caregiver feasibility as much as physical capacity.126

Safety outcomes should be explicitly reported. These include adverse events, exercise-related falls, cardiovascular events, musculoskeletal pain, excessive fatigue, post-exercise confusion, dropout, hospitalization, and caregiver-reported feasibility. In frail or moderate AD populations, a “successful” intervention is not the one that maximizes physiological stress, but the one that produces repeatable benefit without destabilizing function.

Exploratory biomarkers can strengthen mechanistic interpretation but should not replace clinical endpoints. Plasma p-tau217 is increasingly supported as a blood biomarker closely related to AD pathology and diagnostic discrimination.127 Plasma GFAP may provide information about astroglial activation and amyloid-related processes,128 while plasma neurofilament light may capture nonspecific neuroaxonal injury and neurodegeneration.129 Amyloid PET, tau PET, FDG-PET, ASL-MRI, and structural MRI may be useful in mechanistic or high-resource trials, but they should be framed as exploratory or stratification tools rather than mandatory clinical endpoints. The primary test of lactate-informed exercise prescription should remain practical: does it preserve cognition, mobility, independence, and safety in older adults?

Real-World Implementation in Geriatric Care

A lactate-informed exercise framework will have limited value if it cannot be implemented in real-world geriatric care. Older adults with MCI or AD are not usually managed in controlled laboratory environments; they are seen in memory clinics, primary care practices, geriatric outpatient services, rehabilitation programs, community aging centers, and home settings. Implementation therefore requires safety screening, caregiver engagement, practical monitoring, and integration with existing dementia care pathways.

Memory clinics and primary care systems are natural entry points for identifying candidates, staging disease, reviewing comorbidities, and initiating referral to exercise or rehabilitation services. Collaborative dementia care models have shown that structured, team-based management can improve quality of care and neuropsychiatric outcomes in AD, supporting the idea that exercise prescription should be embedded in a coordinated care model rather than left as isolated advice.130 Dementia disease-management programs have also improved quality of care, reinforcing that complex dementia interventions require care coordination, not just patient education.131

Caregiver-supported implementation is essential. Exercise sessions often require prompting, transportation, supervision, behavior management, and monitoring of fatigue or adverse events. Nonpharmacologic dementia care emphasizes tailoring interventions to the patient-caregiver dyad, home environment, behavioral symptoms, and daily routines.132 Translating evidence-based dementia interventions into practice also requires attention to training, fidelity, local resources, and caregiver burden.133

Community and home-based models are particularly relevant because many older adults with AD cannot attend frequent hospital-based sessions. Home-based fall-prevention programs provide a useful precedent: the Otago exercise program reduced falls and injuries in older adults when delivered through structured, progressive, home-based training.134 For cognitively impaired patients, similar models require additional cueing, caregiver involvement, environmental modification, and simplified progression rules.

Wearable monitoring may improve implementation by capturing steps, sedentary time, heart rate, sleep, and adherence between visits. Mobile and wearable devices are increasingly discussed as digital biomarker tools for AD because they can measure real-world behavior continuously rather than relying only on clinic-based snapshots.135 Nevertheless, wearables should not be treated as a substitute for clinical judgment, especially in patients with dementia who may forget to wear devices, misinterpret feedback, or require caregiver assistance.

Finally, implementation must address accessibility. Dementia care and exercise programs are affected by socioeconomic status, race, rurality, transportation, health literacy, insurance coverage, and caregiver availability. AD risk, diagnosis, and care access are not equally distributed across populations.136 A clinically responsible framework should therefore include low-cost options, community partnerships, caregiver training, home-based alternatives, and simplified monitoring strategies for resource-limited settings.

Future Trial Design

Future multicenter trials should test whether lactate-informed exercise prescription improves outcomes beyond usual exercise advice or fixed-intensity exercise. A suitable design would compare three arms: usual care or generic exercise advice, fixed-dose structured exercise, and lactate-informed individualized exercise prescription. This design would allow investigators to determine whether physiological personalization adds value beyond simple exercise participation.

Because lactate-informed exercise is a complex intervention, future trials should follow contemporary complex-intervention methodology. The updated Medical Research Council framework emphasizes intervention theory, context, implementation, stakeholder engagement, refinement, and long-term sustainability.137 Trials should also be designed with a clear decision about whether they are explanatory, pragmatic, or hybrid. The PRECIS-2 tool can help align eligibility, recruitment, setting, flexibility, follow-up, and analysis with the intended real-world purpose of the trial.138

Participants should be stratified by disease stage, because preclinical AD, MCI, mild AD, and moderate AD have different expected outcomes and safety profiles. Biomarker status should be included when feasible, using amyloid/tau status or the A/T/N framework to reduce biological heterogeneity.139 APOE ε4 status should also be considered because it is a major genetic risk factor and may influence AD risk, biomarker trajectory, vascular vulnerability, and possibly exercise responsiveness.140

Trial design should also include geriatric stratification: sarcopenia, frailty, gait speed, cardiometabolic disease, baseline cardiorespiratory fitness, fall risk, and baseline lactate response. These factors are not nuisance variables; they may define who can tolerate, respond to, or benefit from a lactate-informed prescription. Vascular risk should be measured carefully because blood-pressure control and vascular health influence cognitive outcomes in older adults, as shown in SPRINT MIND.141

Follow-up should usually be at least 6–12 months. Short trials may detect fitness or lactate-response changes but are unlikely to capture meaningful trajectories in cognition, ADL, falls, or caregiver burden. For early-stage or preclinical trials, sensitive cognitive composites may be useful because standard cognitive scales may be too crude to detect subtle decline.142 Reporting and analysis should follow CONSORT principles, but exercise trials also require additional attention to adherence, achieved dose, progression, and contamination between arms.143

Reporting Standards for Future Studies

Future studies of lactate-informed exercise in MCI and AD must report the intervention with enough detail to permit replication, clinical translation, and meta-research. Many exercise trials fail not because the idea is weak, but because the prescription, delivery, adherence, and achieved physiological dose are insufficiently described. The TIDieR checklist was developed to improve reporting of interventions by specifying what was delivered, by whom, how, where, how often, and with what tailoring or modification.144 Exercise studies should go further by using exercise-specific reporting standards such as the Consensus on Exercise Reporting Template, which requires detail on equipment, supervision, progression rules, tailoring, adherence, and home components.145

Trial protocols should predefine exercise dose, lactate measurement timing, safety thresholds, dose-adjustment rules, progression criteria, and stopping criteria. The SPIRIT statement provides standards for protocol completeness and should be used before enrollment begins, not retroactively after results are known.146

Safety reporting must be explicit. Trials should report musculoskeletal pain, falls, cardiovascular events, excessive fatigue, confusion, hypoglycemia, hospitalization, and withdrawals due to adverse events. The CONSORT harms extension provides a useful model for more complete reporting of adverse events, which is particularly important in frail older adults with cognitive impairment.147

Patient-reported and caregiver-reported outcomes should also be specified. Fatigue, quality of life, perceived mobility confidence, caregiver burden, and feasibility may determine whether an intervention can be sustained outside a trial. The CONSORT extension for patient-reported outcomes provides a framework for reporting these outcomes transparently.148

Because exercise adherence is partly behavioral, investigators should describe cueing, caregiver involvement, motivation strategies, environmental adaptation, and behavior-change techniques. The behavior-change taxonomy provides a standardized vocabulary for describing these components.149 If remote monitoring, apps, or wearables are used, digital-health reporting standards should be followed so that device type, data completeness, user engagement, privacy, and technical failures are transparent.150

Finally, early-phase studies should not pretend to be definitive efficacy trials. If the purpose is feasibility, acceptability, safety, or protocol refinement, investigators should use the CONSORT extension for pilot and feasibility trials and clearly separate feasibility outcomes from clinical efficacy claims.151 For lactate-informed exercise prescription, future studies should report not only the prescribed exercise, but the achieved dose, lactate and heart-rate response, perceived exertion, recovery, adherence, adverse events, frailty and sarcopenia status, caregiver participation, and cognitive, mobility, and ADL outcomes. Without these data, the field will remain unable to distinguish a failed intervention from a poorly specified prescription.

Limitations and Translational Boundaries

Several limitations must be explicit. First, lactate-informed exercise prescription is a proposed translational framework, not a validated disease-modifying treatment for AD. Evidence linking exercise-induced lactate to synaptic protection, BDNF/VEGF signaling, HCAR1 pathways, myokine release, or lactylation is biologically plausible but remains uneven across animal models, healthy adults, MCI populations, and patients with established AD. Second, peripheral blood lactate is not equivalent to brain lactate concentration, neuronal lactate uptake, or histone lactylation in microglia. It is a practical marker of internal load and recovery, not a direct readout of central mechanism.

Third, lactate response is strongly modified by sarcopenia, frailty, nutritional state, glycogen availability, diabetes, vascular disease, medications, temperature, anxiety, sampling technique, and adherence. These factors can make both low and high lactate responses clinically ambiguous unless interpreted with functional and safety measures. Fourth, cognitively impaired patients may have difficulty reporting symptoms, remembering instructions, wearing devices, or maintaining unsupervised routines. Caregiver participation and environmental safety are therefore not implementation details; they are part of the intervention.

Finally, this framework prioritizes precision and measurability, but excessive complexity could reduce feasibility in resource-limited settings. Future studies must determine the minimum effective monitoring set and whether lactate-guided personalization improves patient-centered outcomes beyond simpler, well-supervised multimodal exercise programs. Conclusions

Older adults with MCI and AD need more than generic encouragement to exercise. They need exercise prescriptions that are matched to disease stage, physical phenotype, muscle metabolic reserve, cardiovascular risk, fall risk, caregiver support, and observed physiological response. Lactate can contribute to this process by helping clinicians and researchers estimate internal exercise load, recovery capacity, and whether a prescribed session is likely to be under-dosed, adaptive, or excessive.

The translational value of lactate lies in its integration with clinical judgment, not in its use as an isolated target. A recoverable lactate pulse may reflect adequate metabolic engagement and may participate in muscle-brain communication, whereas excessive or poorly recovered lactate responses may signal physiological stress in frail patients. The proposed framework therefore seeks a therapeutic window: enough metabolic stimulation to support adaptation, but not enough stress to worsen fatigue, falls, cardiovascular instability, confusion, or functional dependence.

From a translational neurodegeneration perspective, the central question is now empirical. Future trials should test whether lactate-informed, phenotype-specific exercise prescription preserves cognition, mobility, ADL/IADL, safety, and caregiver-manageable independence better than usual advice or fixed-dose exercise. If validated, this approach could move exercise care in AD from general lifestyle recommendation toward precision non-pharmacological intervention.

Three priorities are immediate. First, studies should standardize device calibration, sampling site and timing, pre-exercise conditions, recovery windows, and reporting. Second, they should establish the reproducibility and clinically meaningful bounds of lactate-response phenotypes across disease stage and frailty, without treating peripheral values as CNS thresholds. Third, multicenter randomized trials should compare usual advice, fixed-dose structured exercise, and lactate-informed prescription, with nested mechanistic cohorts pairing serial peripheral lactate with plasma p-tau217, GFAP, and NfL and, when feasible, ASL/fMRI, magnetic resonance spectroscopy, or PET.13,42–45,127–129 These designs should retain cognition, mobility, ADL/IADL, falls, adverse events, and caregiver burden as core outcomes.
