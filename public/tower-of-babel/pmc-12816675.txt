Study on elasto-plastic zoning radius and failure characteristics of layered rock with a single hole under impact loading
Authors: Yunsi Liu, Yayu Miao, Yueyuan Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe zonal rupture phenomenon of surrounding rock in deep mining roadways is a complex nonlinear damage phenomenon, which poses a serious threat to the safety of roadway support. The zonal rupture mechanism of laminated rock under dynamic loading is crucial for optimizing the support design. In order to simulate the zonal rupture phenomenon of surrounding rock, this paper adopts the separated Hopkinson press bar system (SHPB) to conduct triaxial dynamic impact tests on the layered slate with a center hole, simulating the zonal rupture phenomenon of layered slate under different laminar inclination angles, impact loads, and surrounding pressure conditions. At the same time, based on the theory of elastic—plastic mechanics, the calculation formula of the elastic—plastic zoning radius of the slate specimen was deduced. Its correctness was verified through the test, and the damage mechanism of zonal rupture was analyzed in depth. The results showed that: (1) Radial tensile damage dominated the boundary of the zones, but the stress concentration in the laminated surface caused the crack to turn to the shear surface to form a penetrating fissure. (2) The dynamic compressive strength and zonal radius increased with the increase of impact load and surrounding pressure. Specimens with lower strengths have larger zonal radii and take shorter time to reach the peak.Keywords: Split Hopkinson pressure bar, Zonal rupture phenomenon, Bedded slate, Bedding plane inclinationSubject terms: Engineering, Natural hazards, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

The ongoing advancement of underground engineering has drawn increasing attention to the deformation and damage behaviors of deep rock masses. The high geo-stress characteristics of deep rock masses cause their deformation and damage mechanisms to differ significantly from those of shallow rock masses, exhibiting obvious nonlinear characteristics, this includes the emergence of zonal rupture, rock bursts, large deformations, and other nonlinear mechanical behaviors1–4. Zonal rupture is defined by the alternation of fractured and intact zones in the surrounding rock. This phenomenon occurs around and ahead of the working face during deep underground excavation. The zonal extent of zonal rupture in deep engineering projects is often irregular, and in studies of zonal rupture, the zonal distribution is typically modeled as concentric zones centered around the tunnel’s axis5.

The nonlinear nature of the zonal rupture phenomenon results in a distinct formation mechanism compared to the traditional perimeter loosening ring, which is typically described qualitatively using classical elasto-plasticity theory6,7. Some previous studies8–12 suggested that the surrounding rock is prone to zonal rupture when the direction of the maximum principal stress is parallel to the axis of the tunnel through theoretical analyses, numerical simulations and indoor tests. Based on the above conclusions, Wang et al.13 completed zonal rupture tests of prefabricated center-hole sandstone by indoor tests using Split Hopkinson Pressure Bar, and concluded that the maximum radial strain was generated at the boundary of the plastic zone, which led to the generation of ring cracks and the formation of zonal rupture. Furthermore, the initiation and propagation of complex three-dimensional cracks under dynamic loading, as investigated by Han et al.14, provide a fundamental understanding of fracture mechanics that is relevant to the zonal rupture phenomenon. Kurlenya et al.15 derived the fracture zones in zonal rupture phenomena as concentric circles of excavated tunnels and provided a relationship between the radii of the fracture zones. Feng et al.16 developed an element failure criterion based on the maximum tensile stress criterion and strain energy density theory, and predicted the number and distance of fracture zones in the actual project by means of numerical simulation, which was the same as the field monitoring results. Zhu et al.17 analyzed the variation of mechanical parameters (cohesion, angle of internal friction) of different enclosing rocks and the variation of zonal rupture of enclosing rocks under horizontal tectonic stresses, and obtained that rocks with high angle of internal friction and high cohesion can limit the damage range of enclosing rocks.

Meanwhile, laminated rock mass, as a common surrounding rock in deep engineering, has obvious anisotropy due to the presence of bedding planes18,19, and there have been many researches on zonal rupture phenomena of laminated rock mass. Applying the Mohr–Coulomb criterion within a particle flow (PFC2D) framework, Zhou et al.20 analyzed zonal fracture around deep circular tunnels in anisotropic rock. Their analysis revealed that the number of fracture zones grows as the elasticity parameter decreases and the ground stress increases. Wu et al.21 developed a plane-strain model that effectively captures the brittleness of rock and incorporates the influence of microscopic fissures. Their study concluded that zonal rupture in brittle rock manifests more regularly. Furthermore, they identified the presence of joints and an initial stress exceeding the rock’s compressive strength as necessary conditions for this phenomenon. Through a study of laminated rock, Chen et al.22 demonstrated that parallel and random joints cause new fissures near the tunnel to develop along the strike direction. Their research examined the influence of lamination geometry on mechanical behavior and acoustic emission energy during zonal disintegration.

Combined with the above studies, there are two major limitations in the current research on the zonal rupture mechanism of laminated rock masses. Firstly, theoretical models, such as elastic—plasticity theory, can qualitatively explain the rupture zoning phenomenon. However, it is challenging to quantify the controlling effect of laminated anisotropy. Secondly, existing tests predominantly focus on homogeneous rock masses and lack dynamic impact verification for laminated slate. This deficiency results in a lack of data support for support design. Therefore, this paper conducts the first triaxial dynamic impact test using the separated Hopkinson compression bar system (SHPB) on laminated slate with a pre—drilled center hole. The test simulates the coupling of blasting dynamic load and high ground stress in deep tunnels. By setting up the range of the inclination angle of the laminae from 0 to 90°, the paper quantifies and analyzes the morphology of zonal rupture. Additionally, based on elasto-plastic theory, this study derives a theoretical formula for the radius of the elastic–plastic zone to characterize the zoning evolution of the slate. The formation mechanism of zonal rupture was subsequently analyzed by correlating the specimen’s strain field with its damage characterization. To replicate the triaxial impact tests, seven finite element (FE) models with different dip angles of laminations were developed using the ABAQUS software. The research findings provide a theoretical basis for the stability evaluation and safe construction of deep engineering zonal rupture.

Dynamic impact test

Test preparation

The rock specimens used in the test are slate with significant laminations, interspersed with bright and dark bands. In this study, the angle between the lamination surface and the axial impact direction of the specimen is defined as the inclination angle . Three kinds of rock samples with inclination angles of 0°, 45°, and 90° were selected for core drilling, as shown in the schematic diagram of the rock specimen in Fig. 1. The standard size of the specimen is 50 mm in outer diameter, the thickness of the specimen is 25 mm, and a 10 mm diameter hole is prefabricated in the center of the specimen. The error in specimen diameter was controlled to ± 1 mm, and the specimen dimensions conformed to the standardized form recommended by ISRM for uniformity in impact fracture testing23.

Split Hopkinson pressure bar (SHPB).

Test methods

This study employed a Split Hopkinson Pressure Bar (SHPB) system, illustrated in Fig. 1, to conduct dynamic impact compression tests. The SHPB technique has been effectively applied to study wave propagation and dynamic mechanical properties of composite geomaterials24, supporting its suitability for investigating laminated rock masses. The test simulates the coupling of blasting dynamic load and high ground stress in deep tunnels. The system comprises a dynamic impact system, a confining pressure loading device, an axial pressure loading device, and a data acquisition system. The incident and transmission bars, fabricated from high-strength steel with a diameter of 50 mm, were instrumented with strain gauges positioned 1 m from the specimen and connected to the data acquisition system. To ensure data integrity and address potential concerns regarding gauge failure, several precautions were implemented. These include the protective positioning of the gauges at a safe distance from the specimen, secure installation procedures, and the inherent reliability of the bars themselves. Crucially, the experimental design included multiple repeated tests under each condition, which ensures that valid datasets are obtained even in the rare instance of an individual gauge malfunction. The data acquisition and processing system consists of a pulse waveform measuring instrument and a data analysis computer. The size of the impact load in the test was indirectly controlled by the compressed air pressure in the launching cavity. In order to distinguish different impact damage patterns of the slate, three experiments were carried out under each of the selected impact air pressures of 0.4, 0.6, and 0.8 MPa. Meanwhile, with the confining pressure loading device, a confining pressure of 0.5, 1.0, and 1.5 MPa was applied outside the rock specimen, respectively, in each set of experiments.

Laminated rock masses are commonly considered transversely isotropic materials, exhibiting distinct mechanical properties parallel versus perpendicular to the bedding planes. When the mechanical properties in one of these directions reach their ultimate strength, the rock unit yields and breaks along that direction. Define the most unfavorable shear slip surface, considering the potential for slip in both directions relative to the rock fabric or bedding plane. Figure 4b shows the schematic diagram of this most unfavorable shear surface. If this slip surface exists, the rock unit is damaged by shear along this surface. If the Mohr–Coulomb criterion is used to characterize the damage, the cohesion and the angle of internal friction should not be constants, but should vary as functions of the angle between the damage surface and the bedding plane of the material. Specifically, the Mohr–Coulomb criterion should account for these variations in cohesion and internal friction angle as a function of the angle between the damage plane and the bedding plane.

Layered rock bodies are usually characterized by their internal microstructure, where the bonding along the weakest bedding plane is the weakest, and the surface perpendicular to this bedding plane is the strongest. Consequently, the mechanical properties of the rock body vary, with the weakest properties found along the bedding plane and the strongest properties perpendicular to it. For any inclined surface relative to the bedding plane, the mechanical properties gradually change from weak to strong as the angle of inclination increases. In order to further clarify the evolution law of bedding plane inclination and layered fracture in slate, this study adopts the finite element analysis software ABAQUS to reproduce the dynamic impact test of slate. A numerical model of SHPB is established according to the size of the SHPB test setup, with the slate specimen model matching the test size. Discussion

Deep rock tunnel blasting excavation will induce high-intensity vibrations. During this operation, due to the high burial depth and the superimposed impact of confining pressure and blasting disturbance, alternating plastic and elastic strain zones will form within the surrounding rock. This is known as the zonal rupture phenomenon.

Combined with the previous section, it is found that the zonal rupture phenomenon of laminated slate has a close relationship with the bedding plane inclination angle. Under the same stress conditions, as the bedding plane inclination angle gradually increases from 0 to 90°, the strain and degree of destruction of the slate specimen show an initial increase followed by a decrease. Specimens with lower strength show a greater degree of destruction, which is due to the expanded plastic zone within a wider zonal radius . In addition, when the dip of the bedding planes changes, the slate with lower strength develops its plastic zone faster because it is more likely to undergo plastic deformation.

Figure 13b shows the damage morphology of the slate specimen in the dynamic impact test. Two primary failure modes are observed: slip damage along the bedding planes, attributed to the weakened interfaces, and rock spalling around the borehole, resulting from stress concentration. By integrating the theoretical formula for the partition radius (Section "Calculation of the radius of the partition boundary") and the calculation method for the most unfavorable shear surface (Section "Method for determining the least favorable shear surface") with the observed damage morphology, the cracking location around the specimen’s central hole can be predicted. This crack will develop rapidly, extending along the most unfavorable shear damage surface to form a penetrating crack under the combined influence of inherent structural weaknesses and axial impacts. The coupling damage between the plastic zone and the weak surface of the structure during deep tunnel blasting and excavation leads to cracking damage in the tunnel as shown in Fig. 13c, and all these damage phenomena are reflected in the modeling tests in this study.

Conclusions

Based on elasto-plastic analysis and numerical simulation, the following conclusions are drawn regarding the zonal rupture of slate specimens with a central borehole under triaxial dynamic impact:

Prefabricated hole-layered slate specimens showed zonal rupture phenomenon under triaxial dynamic impact. Based on the theory of elastic–plastic mechanics, the formula for calculating the zonal radius was derived, and the correctness of the formula was verified by tests.

Prefabricated hole-layered slate specimens showed zonal rupture phenomenon under triaxial dynamic impact. Based on the theory of elastic–plastic mechanics, the formula for calculating the zonal radius was derived, and the correctness of the formula was verified by tests.

Due to the impact, a plastic zone forms internally within the specimen. At its boundary, shear cracks develop. These shear cracks, induced by the combined influence of the weak surface structure and axial impact, rapidly propagate along a shear failure surface, eventually coalescing into through-going cracks. These shear cracks, induced by the combined influence of the weak surface structure and axial impact, rapidly propagate along a shear failure surface, eventually coalescing into through-going cracks. This, in turn, generates the zonal rupture phenomenon.

The dynamic compressive strength of the slate increases gradually with the increase of impact load and circumferential pressure, respectively, and the rupture zone radius increases under the same bedding plane inclination.

The inclination angle of the bedding planes significantly influences the zonal rupture in laminated slate.
