Measured resection, gap balancing, and force-sensor-guided total knee arthroplasty result in different femoral and tibial rotational alignment but similar combined knee rotation and clinical outcomes: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: José A Hernández-Hermoso, Lexa Nescolarde, Federico Yañez-Siller, Juan Calle-García, Ricard Pérez-Andrés, Damian García-Perdomo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDifferent alignment philosophies and balancing methods may alter femoral and tibial component rotation in distinct ways within the same knee. This study aimed to (1) determine which of three surgical techniques—measured resection (MR), gap balancing with computer-assisted surgery (GB-CAS), or a force-sensor soft-tissue balancing device (FS-STB)—most closely reproduces native femoral, tibial, and combined rotational alignment in mechanically aligned total knee arthroplasty (TKA), and (2) assess whether differences in rotational alignment affect outcomes at 5 years postoperatively.Material and methodsA total of 60 patients undergoing primary mechanical alignment TKA were randomly assigned to one of three surgical approaches (n = 20 per group): MR, GB-CAS, or FS-STB. Blinded observers assessed the Knee Society Score (KSS), Western Ontario MacMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC) score, and hip–knee–ankle angle preoperatively and at the 5-year follow-up visit. Pre- and postoperative two-dimensional (2D)-computed tomography scans were used to measure femoral rotation (BFA), tibial rotation, and combined femur-tibia rotation (TE_PTCA and BC_PTCA).Statistical analyses included paired t-tests, one-way analysis of variance, and effect size calculations.ResultsFemoral rotation remained unchanged in the MR and GB-CAS groups, but decreased slightly (1° external rotation) in the FS-STB group (P = 0.010). Tibial rotation increased significantly in internal rotation in the GB-CAS and FS-STB groups (P < 0.001), but not in the MR group (P = 0.061). The combined TE-PTCA rotation decreased slightly across all groups (P < 0.05), with no significant intergroup differences. Combined BCPTCA rotation increased only with GB-CAS (P = 0.006), but again without significant differences between the techniques. At 5 years, functional KSS and WOMAC scores improved in the FS-STB group compared with that in the MR group, although this difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.058 and P = 0.056, respectively).ConclusionsMeasured resection best preserved native knee rotation in mechanically aligned TKA. Although the individual component rotations varied by technique, the overall combined rotational alignment and functional outcomes did not differ significantly. Prosthesis design may govern the kinematics, and soft tissue adaptation may mitigate the impact of minor rotational differences in TKA procedures. More technologically assisted balancing methods may not provide meaningful functional advantages in terms of rotational alignment.Level of evidenceLevel I, therapeutic study.Trial registration Retrospectively registered on the UK’s Clinical Study Registry platform. Registration number: ISRCTN66642689). Introduction

The optimal alignment for total knee arthroplasty (TKA) remains unclear, as does the best method or technique to achieve this once the goal is established. Different alignment objectives [1] or surgical techniques [2] can influence the rotation of the femoral and tibial components within the same knee in different ways.

Considerable variability exists in the distal femoral rotational anatomy among patients undergoing TKA, with rotations ranging from 3.3° to 11° [3]. In TKA, the measured resection technique typically aims for 3° of external femoral rotation relative to the posterior condylar axis, whereas the gap balance technique can result in variable femoral rotation influenced by factors, such as original joint line alignment, varus or valgus morphotype, and ligament release [4, 5]. Some studies support the use of computer-assisted surgery (CAS) in TKA to more accurately reproduce true anatomical femoral rotation than traditional mechanical guides [6, 7], whereas others question this approach [8, 9], highlighting the variability in femoral rotation achieved by CAS in cadaveric specimens [10].

The measured resection surgical technique aims to replicate native tibial rotation by aligning the tibial plateau with the medial third of the tibial tuberosity. However, the anatomical position of the tibial tuberosity showed greater variability than any other point in the mediolateral plane [11, 12]. Therefore, variable tibial rotation based on self-seeking of the tibial plateau during knee extension has been recommended [13]. Whether it is preferable to reproduce the native femoral and tibial knee rotations or establish a new rotation that adapts to the alignment and ligament release established postoperatively remains unclear.

Computed tomography (CT), whether two-dimensional (2D) or three-dimensional (3D), is the gold standard for assessing TKA rotation. The Berger method [14] is widely used to measure femoral rotation [15–20], although no universally accepted method exists for measuring tibial and combined TKA component rotations [11, 21]. Significant variability in TKA rotation has been described on the basis of CT findings, with ranges up to 25° for the tibial component, 9° for the femoral component, and 12° for combined rotation [11, 22, 23].

The degree of malrotation or mismatch between the TKA components that can be tolerated without causing clinical symptoms remains debatable [1, 16]. Evidence suggests that combined [1, 14] internal rotation of the femoral and tibial components, as well as isolated internal rotation of either component, is less tolerated than external rotation. Internal rotation may be associated with increased pain [14, 15, 24–26] and higher failure rates [27].

Our study aimed to compare three different surgical techniques for achieving both mechanical and rotational alignment in TKA: (1) the measured resection (MR) technique, (2) the gap-balanced technique with computer-assisted surgery (CAS-GB), and (3) the gap-balanced technique using a force-sensor soft-tissue balancing device (FS-STB). We have two main objectives: (1) to determine which of these three surgical techniques most accurately reproduces native knee rotation in mechanically alignment TKA and (2) to assess how rotational alignment varies among these techniques and how these variations impact clinical outcome scores. We hypothesized that while all three surgical techniques will result in some variation in native knee rotational alignment, we anticipate that the rotational differences will be insufficient to significantly affect the 5-year clinical outcome scores.

Material and methods

Study design and patients

This prospective, randomized study included 60 patients with painful primary osteoarthritis who were unresponsive to nonoperative treatments and scheduled for TKA. Patients with a history of femoral or tibial fractures or previous tibial or femoral osteotomy were excluded to avoid potential alterations in rotational alignment. Eligible patients who agreed to participate were recruited between 2015 and 2017.

Patients were recruited consecutively and randomly assigned to 1 of 3 groups (20 patients per group): (1) a measured resection group (MR); (2) a computer-assisted gap-balancing group (CAS-GB); and (3) a force-sensor-guided soft-tissue balancing group (FS-STB); in a 1:1:1 ratio using a computer-generated randomization sequence created by an independent researcher (L.N.) not involved in patient recruitment or outcome assessment. Allocation was concealed using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes prepared in advance according to the randomization list. Envelopes were opened sequentially only after participant enrollment and completion of base line assessments.

All three groups underwent mechanically aligned TKA. All procedures were performed or directly supervised by the same experienced surgeon (JHH). No intraoperative deviations from the assigned alignment technique occurred. Five patients were excluded after randomization (one in the MR group due to progressive Parkinson’s disease; two in the CAS-GB group, one for acute infection and one for foot ischemia; and two in the FS-STB group, one for acute infection and one for stroke), leaving 55 patients for analysis (Fig. 1). Data were analyzed according to a modified intention-to-treat approach, including all randomized patients with available postoperative outcome data. No crossover between groups occurred. The study was reported in accordance with established recommendations for clinical research quality and transparency [28].

All patients received a cemented posterior stabilized (PS) Nex-Gen® TKA (Zimmer-Biomet, Warsaw, IN, USA). Antibiotics and thromboembolic prophylaxis were administered to all patients. Surgeries were performed under tourniquet control using a parapatellar approach. The tourniquet was inflated with the knee fully flexed to prevent the strangling of the quadriceps tendon when extended.

In the MR group, bone cuts were made first, followed by ligament balancing during extension. The proximal tibia was cut perpendicular to the mechanical axis in both the anteroposterior (AP) and sagittal planes, using an extramedullary guide. A distal femoral cut was made perpendicular to the mechanical axis in the AP and sagittal planes using an intramedullary guide. A consistent 3° external femoral component rotation was applied in all patients using a posterior reference cutting guide.

Staged ligamentous releases were performed as necessary to balance the flexion–extension gap. Tibial rotation was aligned with the medial third of the anterior tibial tuberosity (ATT). In varus knees, the deep medial collateral ligament is released following medial arthrotomy by removing approximately 1 cm of the periosteum from the tibia. All medial osteophytes were excised, and a minor release of the posterior medial capsule was performed, if necessary, to achieve adequate gap balance. In any case, major release of the superficial medial collateral ligament or semimembranosus is required.

For valgus knees, the procedure involved removing all lateral osteophytes and a minor release of the lateral posterior capsule if needed. No major releases of the popliteus tendon, fibular ligament, or lateral intermuscular septum on the femur were required. No patellar lateral releases or additional femoral recuts were performed.

In the CAS-GB group, a wireless, image-free CAS system was used, along with Orthosoft Knee 2.4 Universal knee navigation software (Zimmer® CAS Orthosoft®, Montréal, Québec, Canada). Two active wireless trackers were placed with two pins, one in the femur and one in the tibia. Using an optically tracked stylus, the medial and lateral epicondyles were palpated to establish the transepicondylar axis and digitally map the distal surface of the epicondyles to simulate the patient’s anatomy and bone cuts.

The proximal tibia was cut perpendicular to the mechanical axis in both the AP and sagittal planes. The distal femoral cut was made perpendicular to the mechanical axis in the AP plane, with 0° flexion applied in the sagittal plane. Staged ligament releases were performed to balance the extension gap, as described for the MR group, using a manual tensor device until a rectangular gap was achieved with tensed medial and lateral ligaments and a neutral mechanical axis. The flexion gap was balanced at 90° knee flexion using a manual tensor ligament device. The size and rotation of the femoral component were simulated using a computer to ensure the proper balance of the flexion gap without ligament release. Tibial rotation was determined by the self-seeking alignment of the tibial component during knee flexion and extension.

In the FS-STB group, the same surgical technique as in the MR group was used to perform the distal femoral and proximal tibial cuts, as well as to balance the extension gap. Additionally, an e-LIBRA Dynamic Knee Balancing System force sensor device (Synvasive Technology, Zimmer-Biomet®, Reno, Nevada, NV, USA) was employed to balance the extension and flexion gaps. The e-LIBRA femoral component was applied to the femur, and the tibial component was placed on the tibia after distal femoral and proximal tibial cuts were made with the knee at 90° flexion, with the tibial insert and patella in place.

The e-LIBRA is an electronic device with pressure sensors located on the medial and lateral sides of its tibial component. These sensors measure the pressure independently in the medial and lateral knee compartments and transmit this information to a display integrated into the tibial component in units of 1–20, with each unit represents approximately 15 N. Initially, a 9-mm tibial insert was used. If the e-LIBRA sensors detected insufficient pressure, a thicker insert was selected, and this process continued until a pressure difference of 0–1 was detected in one of the compartments. The flexion gap was balanced by adjusting the e-LIBRA femoral component rotation until the pressure difference between the medial and lateral compartments was minimized to a range of 0–1, with no ligament releases performed to achieve this balance. Tibial rotation was determined by the self-alignment of the tibial component during knee flexion and extension.

Patellar resurfacing was performed in all the procedures by subluxating the patella. All patients followed the same postoperative protocol.

Clinical and radiographic assessment

A blinded observer (F.Y.S.) performed all preoperative and 5-year postoperative evaluations, including the Knee Society Score (KSS) [29], Western Ontario MacMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC) score [30], and hip–knee–ankle radiographic angle [31]. Radiographic measurements were obtained on a long-leg weight-bearing radiograph with a semiautomatic software system (RAIM Viewer® version 2.5.0.511).

2D-CT Radiological measurements

Three blinded observers (two orthopedic surgeons, F.Y.S. and J.C.G., and one musculoskeletal radiologist, D.G.P.) independently reviewed all the CT images. All measurements were performed twice, with a minimum interval of 6 weeks. Non-contrast-enhanced helical 2D-CT scans were obtained 1–2 months preoperatively and at 6 months postoperatively using a LightSpeed VCT 64 scanner (General Electric Healthcare, Hino, Japan). The protocol for CT scan uses 140 kV, 400 mA, and 0.6-mm thick axial images, which were acquired at four locations of the knee: the femoral epicondylar axis, proximal tibial plateau, and tibial tubercle, as well as the ankle joint. Only axial images of the knee and ankle were considered for the analysis in this study. The patients were placed in the supine position with their knees fully extended and legs fixed in neutral rotation. The knee was scanned from the superior patellar margin to the inferior aspect of the anterior tibial tuberosity and 2–3 cm on either side of the tibiotalar joint. For the two regions, the X-, Y-, and Z-axes were fixed throughout the scan to ensure that the relative positions of the knee and ankle regions remained consistent.

Image processing was performed using dedicated software for DICOM images, specifically the Alma Workstation 4.2.3.0 (Alma Medical Imaging, Barcelona, Spain) program, which includes a measure tool for determining distances and angles. External rotation (ER) of the component was considered a positive value, whereas internal rotation (IR) was considered negative.

Femoral component rotation was quantified using Berger’s femoral component rotation angle (BFA), as outlined in Berger’s protocol [14]. BFA was determined by measuring the angle between the surgical transepicondylar axis (sTEAx) and the posterior femoral condylar axis (PFCAx) (Fig. 2). The sTEAx was defined as a line drawn between the lateral epicondylar prominence and the medial sulcus of the medial epicondyle, measured on a slice where both landmarks were clearly visible. PFCAx was defined as a line drawn tangentially to the posterior surface of the femoral condyles, either from the native knee or the femoral component post-TKA. To simplify the measurement, and in line with previous studies [15, 17], we did not apply the sex-specific correction for native rotation on the basis of the posterior condylar angle (which is reported as 0.3° ± 1.2° IR in women and 3.5° ± 1.2° IR in men). Consequently, we defined 0° femoral rotation as 3.5° ER for men and 0.3° ER for women as per Berger’s method.

Berger’s femoral component rotational angle (BFA) pre- and post-TKA: axial CT image illustrating the measurement of femoral component rotation using the surgical transepicondylar axis (sTEAx) and the posterior femoral condylar axis (PFCAx)

Tibial rotation was assessed using the anatomic tibial angle [11] (ATA) (Fig. 3). ATA was defined as the angle between a perpendicular line at the midpoint of the anatomic tibial axis (ATAx) and that perpendicular to either the posterior tibial axis (PTAx) in the native knee or the posterior tibial component axis (PTCAx) in the TKA knee. A line connecting the geometric centers of the lateral and medial tibial plateaus defines the ATAx. It was measured 9 mm below the joint line in the native knee or on the first tibial CT slice immediately under the TKA tibial component. The PTAx is the line tangential to the posterior cortex of the tibia, measured in a slice 9 mm below the joint surface of the native knee. The PTCAx is the line tangential to the posterior surface of the TKA tibial plateau measured in a single axial scan through the tibial component.

Anatomic tibial axis (ATA) rotation measurement pre- and post-TKA: ATA rotation is defined as the angle between the perpendicular at the midpoint of the anatomic tibial axis (ATAx)—measured 9 mm under the tibia joint surface preoperatively and at the first tibial CT slice just below the tibial component postoperatively—and the perpendicular to the posterior tibial axis (PTAx) preoperatively and to the posterior tibial component axis (PTCAx) postoperatively

The combined rotations of the femur and tibia in the native knee and TKA were analyzed using two key angles. First, the transepicondylar posterior tibial angle (TE_PTA) and transepicondylar posterior tibial component angle (TE_PTCA) were measured for native and TKA knees, respectively (Fig. 4). These angles are formed by the intersection of the sTEAx and PTAx in the native knee or PTCAx in TKA. Second, the bicondylar posterior tibial angle (BC_PTA) and bicondylar posterior tibial component angle (BC_PTCA) were measured for native and TKA knees, respectively (Fig. 5). These angles are formed by the intersection of PFCAx and PTAx in the native knee, or PTCAx in TKA. The BC-PTCA angle provides a measure of the rotational alignment between the femoral and tibial components, independent of anatomic landmarks. This angle represents the rotational alignment of the device or the implant.

Transepicondylar posterior tibial angle (TE_PTA) and transepicondylar posterior tibial component angle (TE_PTCA): these angles represent the orientation between the surgical transepicondylar axis (sTEAx) and the posterior tibial axis (PTAx) preoperatively, or the posterior tibial component axis (PTCAx) postoperatively

Bicondylar posterior tibial angle (BC_PTA) and the bicondylar posterior tibial component angle (BC_PTCA): these angles are defined by the intersection of the posterior femoral condylar axis (PFCAx) with the posterior tibial axis (PTAx) preoperatively or with the posterior tibial component axis (PTCAx) postoperatively

The inter- and intraobserver reliabilities of all 2D-CT rotational measurements have been reported [4] using the Bland–Altman method, with inter- and intraobserver averages of differences within −0.9° and 1.4° for BFA, −0.1° and 1.6° for ATA, −2.1° and 2.7° for TE_PTCA, and −0.5° and 1.8° for BC_PTCA.

Ethical approval

The local ethics committee approved the study (AC-14-033), and all participants provided written informed consent. The study was also retrospectively registered on the UK’s Clinical Study registry platform (ISRCTN66642689).

Sample size calculations

The primary endpoint of the study was the postoperative rotational alignment of femoral and tibial components assessed using 2D-CT. Secondary endpoints included clinical outcomes (KSS and WOMAC sores). This study was a part of a larger project. A previous publication [4] established the sample size and reliability of the 2D-CT angle measurements (BFA, ATA, TE-PTCA, and BC-PTCA). A desired sample size between 12 and 23 patients was calculated with an α-level of 0.05 and a β-level of 0.20 (80% power), using the MedCalc statistical software version 19.0.3 (MedCalc Software bvba, Ostend, Belgium) to assess agreement between the observers. The expected mean of differences, expected SD of differences, and maximum allowed difference between methods were set at −0.9°(1.6°), 6° for BFA; 0.1° (4.0°) and 16.0° for ATA; −0.5°(2.8°),11.0° for TE_PTCA; and −0.5° (2.0°) and 7.0° for BC_PTCA.

A desired sample size between 5 and 14 patients was calculated with an α-level of 0.05 and a β-level of 0.10 (90% power) to assess KSS, WOMAC, and 2D-CT angular measurements differences. The expected mean of differences and expected SD of differences at MR, GB_CAS, and FS_STB groups was set at 75.1 (35.3), 83.6 (32.2), and 81.7 (32.0) for KSS, respectively; 31.6 (17.6), 34.2 (15.2), and 34.8 (18.8) for KSS-Knee, respectively; 33.9 (22.2), 36.4 (18.3), and 41.1 (20) for KSS-function, respectively; 28.8 (17.7), 32.6 (16.1), and 45.3 (19.8) for WOMAC, respectively; 2.3° (2.0°), 2.2° (1.4°), and 1.4° (1.2°) for BFA, respectively; 4.0° (3.8°), 12.2° (5.9°), and 8.3° (7.4°) for ATA, respectively; 3.2° (2.6°), 2.8° (2.7°), and 3.1° (2.8°) for BC_PTCA, respectively; and 2.6° (1.2°), 2.4° (1.7°), and 2.3° (1.4) for TE_PTCA, respectively.

Statistical analyses

Normal distribution of the data was confirmed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test (n = 55), and the homogeneity of variances was assessed using Levene’s test and found to be satisfied (P > 0.05). Clinical and radiographic variables that were normally distributed are presented descriptively as the arithmetic means ± standard deviations (SDs), along with the lower and upper bound of 95% confidence intervals (CI) of the means. Nonnormally distributed data are presented as medians, interquartile ranges (IQR), and minimum and maximum values.

To analyze the demographic variables between the MR, CAS-GB, and FS-STB groups, the Kruskal–Wallis test was performed for non-parametric variables, and the chi-squared test was used for categorical variables. Additionally, η2 effect sizes for the Kruskal–Wallis test and Cohen’s ω effect size for the chi-squared test were also calculated.

To analyze intragroup differences pre- and post-TKA for the hip–knee–ankle angle, two-dimensional computed tomography (2D-CT) femoral, tibial, and combined total knee arthroplasty rotation angles, as well as KSS and WOMAC outcome scores between the MR, CAS-GB, and FS-STB groups and paired t-tests were conducted, and Cohen’s d effect size for the paired t-test was calculated. Additionally, to analyze intergroup differences pre- and post-TKA surgery, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) test was used, with Ω2 effect size for ANOVA small size determined. Pairwise group comparisons were performed using Tamhane’s T2 post hoc test (T2 test). Effect size calculations followed the methodology described by Serdar et al. [32].

The analyses were performed using statistical software IBM® SPSS® version 28.0 (IBM Corp.; Armonk, NY, USA). Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Discussion

Rotational malalignment has been associated with inferior functional outcomes [15, 33, 34], attributed to increased stress on the soft tissue envelope [35], bearing surfaces [36], and bone–prosthesis interfaces [35]. Once coronal and sagittal plane alignment and balance goals are defined, the best surgical technique for achieving these goals and its impact on rotational alignment remain debatable. Which technique best replicates native knee rotational alignment or whether replicating native rotational alignment is preferable than establishing a new, technique-specific alignment remains unclear.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to compare the impact of three surgical techniques—measured resection (MR), gap balancing with computer-assisted surgery (CAS-GB), and gap balancing with a force-sensor soft-tissue balancing device (FS-STB)—on component rotational alignment in mechanically aligned TKA. The principal finding was that the MR technique most accurately preserved the native femoral (BFA-pre), tibial (ATA-pre), and combined device rotational alignment (BC-PTA). By contrast, both gap-balanced techniques resulted in greater internal tibial rotation than the native alignment. The FS-STB technique produced less external femoral rotation than native. CAS-GB technique increased combined device external rotation (BC-PTCA). All three techniques reduced the native external combined knee rotation (TE-PTCA). Although each technique influenced isolated component rotation differently, combined femoral tibial rotation (TE-PTCA and BC-PTCA) did not differ among groups.

Despite these rotational alignment differences, all three techniques resulted in significant postoperative improvements in KSS and WOMAC scores. No significant between-group differences in the overall postoperative KSS scores were identified at the 5-year follow-up. FS-STB yielded better postoperative KSS-function and WOMAC scores compared with the MR group, although these differences were small.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. First, although the study had adequate power, the sample size was relatively small. Effect size helps to interpret how large the observed difference is, regardless of the statistical significance. Larger multicenter studies are required to confirm these findings and improve their generalizability. Second, the study results may not be applicable to patient-specific TKA techniques, where bony resections influence the laxity of the functional knee phenotype in both extension and flexion [37]. Third, this study exclusively included posteriorly stabilized fixed-bearing TKA implants with symmetrical tibial trays. Therefore, these findings may not be generalizable to patients with posterior cruciate-retaining designs, mobile-bearing implants, or asymmetric tibial components. Lastly, the follow-up period of 5 years limits the assessment of long-term outcomes related to rotational alignment. However, most functional improvements following TKA occur within the first 2 years [38].

Pre- and postoperative radiographic HKA and rotational alignment

Mechanical alignment remains the most widely used systematic approach in TKA. While kinematic and functional alignment, strategies are gaining interest but lack long-term data. Consistent with prior studies [12, 39, 40], CAS in this study produced a narrower distribution of postoperative HKA values compared with MR or FS-STB, although these differences were not statistically significant. Mechanical alignment has been shown to correlate strongly with the laxity functional knee phenotype (FKP) in extension, but not in flexion, independent of the bony FKP, and most knees (98% of patients) demonstrate grater lateral than the medial laxity in flexion [37].

The MR technique determines femoral and tibial component rotation on the basis of bone anatomy and population-based reference values, using selective ligament releases to achieve balance. Gap-balanced techniques adjust femoral rotation on the basis of intraoperative soft tissue tension and orientation of the proximal tibial cut. Whether such techniques reliably reproduce native rotational alignment is unsolved, particularly given the substantial anatomic variability seen among patients.

A central unanswered question is whether reproducing native rotational alignment is necessary or whether rotational alignment should instead be optimized relative to the surgical technique, implant design, and soft tissue envelope. Moreover, the influences of various alignment philosophies and balancing strategies on rotational alignment in TKA remain unclear. Current evidence does not define ideal thresholds for acceptable rotational alignment, which complicate clinical decision-making.

Multiple methods have been described for assessing femoral [14–20, 41, 42] and tibial [11, 21, 43] component rotation, but few demonstrate high precision and reproducibility [10, 20, 44, 45]. The effectiveness of computer navigation in improving rotational alignment remains debatable, with studies reporting positive [6, 7], inconclusive, and negative [8–10, 44] findings. Femoral component rotation also varies depending on whether MR or GB-technique is used within the same surgical procedure [2, 46–48].

Postoperative femoral rotation values in this study were comparable to those reported previously [5, 17, 22, 42, 49], closely approximating the native femoral rotation, except with FS-STB, where femoral external rotation was slightly reduced compared with native rotation and the GB-CAS was modestly increased. This difference may relate to technique-specific variations in flexion gap balancing. Because the lateral compartment is generally more lax than the medial compartment [37, 50], distraction of the flexion gap tends to induce internal femoral rotation, prompting surgeons to externally rotate the femoral component. Accordingly, and consistent with previous reports, the femoral component was more externally rotated with CAS-GB than with MR when ligament releases and distraction were performed to compensate for the extension and flexion gap [49], and more internally rotated when they were not [5]. The FS-STB device may apply less lateral force, reducing the need for compensatory external rotation. This may also apply to kinematic and functional alignment techniques, where no or minor ligament releases and no distraction are used to compensate for extension and flexion gaps [51].

In both gap-balanced techniques, the self-positioning of the tibial component produced internal rotation, consistent with prior reports of self-seeking tibia alignment during flexion–extension [13]. This phenomenon likely reflects the influence of the femoral component and soft tissue envelope on tibial positioning. This study provides the first clinical evidence that MR most reliable reproduces native tibial rotation, whereas self-seeking tibial alignment in gab-balanced inherently promotes internal rotation.

Combined femoral–tibial TKA rotation is often calculated as the arithmetic sum [14, 24, 26] or the subtraction [24, 26] of individual component rotations, depending on whether the femoral and tibial components rotate in the same or opposite directions. Such calculations do not account for soft-tissue envelope viscoelasticity [4]. The method used in this study incorporates soft tissue adaptation, potentially providing a more physiologic representation of combined rotation. Our values are consistent with cadaveric studies demonstrating 2–8° of femorotibial external rotation [52]. The native combined rotation measured relative to the transepicondylar axis (TE-PTA) differed by 2–3° from that measured relative to the posterior condylar axis (BC-PTA), consistent with known differences between these axis (TEAx and PFCAx) [53].

In the MR technique, the posterior femoral cut is typically aligned parallel to the sTEAx, a goal that may be or not achieved using the gap-balancing. The postoperative reduction in the native TE-PTA with no differences between the groups, as well as the comparable device rotation (BC-PTCA) between groups, despite the isolated tibial rotational differences, may be explained by the soft tissue adaptation. The postoperative increased BC-PTCA seen in CAS-GB likely reflects the greater external femoral rotation used to compensate for lateral laxity during gap balancing.

A strength of this study is the novel demonstration that, under mechanical alignment, the MR more closely reproduces native femoral and tibial rotation than gap-balancing techniques. Although techniques differed in isolated component rotation, combined rotation did not differ significantly between groups. We speculate that implant design may be the dominant determinant of postoperative rotational kinematics, with soft tissues adapting to accommodate component mismatch (Fig. 6). Different TKA designs, such as ultracongruent, medial-pivot, or cruciate-retaining implants, exhibit distinct kinematic behaviors and may be associated with different threshold for combined rotational alignment. However, across all TKA designs, excessive combined mismatch—as that reported in posterior-stabilized designs—may predict worse outcomes [4] and may still contribute to discomfort or complications.

Patient-reported outcomes scores and rotational alignment

The association between rotational malalignment and unexplained pain after TKA [54] remains debated [17, 55, 56]. Thresholds for problematic malrotation have been proposed. Femoral external rotation < 2° [1, 27] has been associated with a failure rate of 6.75%, whereas ER > 5° is associated with a lower failure rate of 1.9%. Additionally, a femoral component ER > 1.4° was identified as the threshold associated with good KSS outcomes [4]. Femoral component IR > 3.9° has been associated with unexplained knee pain [4, 24].

A tibial component IR of > 9° relative has been associated with postoperative pain and reduced range of motion [15, 26]. A tibial rotation of < 3.9° IR was associated with higher postoperative KSS knee scores [4]. Combined internal rotation of > 8.7° and component mismatch of > 5.6° have been implicated as risk factors for postoperative pain [24]. Minimal rotation mismatch between TKA components (device rotation), defined as an ER of < 3.8°, has been associated with improved KSS clinical outcomes [4].

Our results fall within the femoral and combined rotational ranges associated with favorable clinical outcomes, except for tibial rotation in the GB-CAS and FS-STB groups. Despite greater internal tibial rotation, clinical outcomes in the FS-STB group were, if anything, slightly improved. We speculate that reproducing native knee rotation after TKA may not be necessary to achieve satisfactory clinical outcomes; rather, improved control of soft tissue envelope stress, along with fewer releases, may contribute to better outcomes.

The lack of a standardized and reproducible method for assessing rotational alignment in TKA [21, 22, 42, 57–59], combined with the absence of a consensus on acceptable rotational thresholds [1], complicates both the diagnosis of malrotation in painful TKA [1, 23] and formulation of evidence-based surgical guidelines [15]. This uncertainty is reflected in the present study, where despite significant differences in rotational alignment, particularly in tibial component rotation, all three surgical techniques resulted in improved postoperative KSS and WOMAC scores.

Conclusions

In mechanical aligned TKA, the MR technique more closely preserved native femoral, tibial, and combined knee rotation. By contrast, both gap-balancing techniques tended to increase internal tibial component rotation, while FS-STB slightly reduced external femoral rotation. Despite these differences in isolated component rotations, combined femorotibial rotation was similar across techniques, and all groups demonstrated substantial improvements in clinical outcomes at 5 years. We speculate that soft-tissue adaptation may mitigate the functional effects of small rotational differences and recommend caution against attributing postoperative pain solely to component malrotation.
