Insidious pericardial journey of an orthodontic metallic retainer wire
Authors: Daniel Grandmougin, Pan Dan, Francesco Ferraro, Juan-Pablo Maureira
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIngestion of foreign bodies may be a frequent cause of painful dysphagia. We report the case of a metallic wire impacted in the oesophagus with potentially devastating consequences.Keywords: Oesophageal foreign bodies, Pericarditis, Fibroscopy, Pneumopericardium, Life-threatening complications

INTRODUCTION

Oesophageal foreign body (FB) with mural impaction is a challenging situation that requires a multidisciplinary awareness. Necessity of a prompt diagnosis and identification of risk factors is crucial to avoid potentially life-threatening complications [1] and ensure an optimal therapeutic strategy.

CASE PRESENTATION

A 64-year-old female patient was transferred to our department with an intrapericardial metallic FB. On admission, physical examination showed fever and asthenia.

Based on the patient’s history, 2 weeks after dental care, she started to report a painful dysphagia, with increasing swallowing difficulties, yielding loss of appetite and weight. A computed tomography scan had then revealed a metallic FB into the oesophageal lumen (Fig. 1A). Unfortunately, a fibroscopy, performed to remove the FB, had failed to retrieve it, while a mucosal laceration with pus leaking from a punctiform orifice had been located 1 cm above the oesogastric junction.

(A) CT scan 2 weeks after dental cares showing a metallic wire into the oesophageal lumen (white arrow). The pericardium (P) is not thickened. (B) CT scan 5 weeks after dental cares showing the metallic wire into the pericardial cavity (black arrow). Air (A) and fibrinous deposits are consistent with a pericarditis (P). (C) Preoperative cardiac echography showing air into the pericardial cavity (A) with heterogenous deposits surrounding the heart. CT: computed tomography; LA: left atrium; LV: left ventricle; NGT: nasogastric tube; RA: right atrium; RV: right ventricle.

Since accidentally ingested FBs, for the most part, pass through the gastrointestinal tract without any consequence, the patient had been discharged home and medicated with 2 weeks of oral antibiotherapy.

Nevertheless, dysphagia persisted with chest pains within the next 3 weeks with fever, asthenia and dyspnoea. She was then admitted in our department and a new computed tomography scan displayed a pneumopericardium with pericardial effusion related to the presence of a 2 cm-metallic wire into the pericardial cavity (Fig. 1B), consistent with clinical symptoms, increased white blood cell count (22 000/mm3) as well as C-reactive protein (85 mg/l). Echocardiography confirmed presence of air into the pericardium with heterogenous fibrinous deposits surrounding the heart (Fig. 1C).

Unlike the initial statement that ingested bodies are usually at low risk of consequences, this metallic object had obviously migrated from the oesophagus into the pericardial cavity.

Surgery was decided to remove the foreign metallic corpus and drain the infected pericardial cavity. Surgical access was gained through a median sternotomy. The heart was found densely adherent to the pericardial sack and cautiously liberated from pericardial inflammatory adherences. The pericardial sack was filled with fibrinous and infected membranes that were removed. Exposure of the diaphragmatic wall of the heart showed a 2-cm metallic wire embedded into a fibrinous granuloma, close to the right coronary artery (Fig. 2A) that was released from the epicardial adipose tissue, while the posterior aspect of the pericardium showed no reliquat of an oesophageal–pericardial fistula. However, as precaution, this latter area was covered with a bovine pericardial patch.

(A) Operative illustration showing the metallic wire (white arrow) close to the right coronary artery. (B) The 2-cm metallic wire at inspection. (C) Postoperative fibroscopy showing the healed laceration (HL) with a blind orifice (BO).

At inspection, the wire was identified as an orthodontic metallic wire (Fig. 2B). Postoperative outcome was uneventful and fibroscopy confirmed healing of the punctiform orifice (Fig. 2C), allowing to restore normal oral alimentation. The patient was discharged after 7 days postoperatively with an adapted antibiotherapy of 6 weeks.

DISCUSSION

Ingested FBs can have an insidious course since most of them spontaneously pass without incident. However, special attention should be paid to the morphologic and physical characteristics of FBs as well as their location in the oesophagus since they may influence the occurrence and severity of complications [1, 2].

Fishbones remain the most commonly reported FBs in all age groups and particularly in the Asian population because of distinct eating habits [2]. However, special attention must be paid to specific objects in the oesophagus like button batteries, magnets, metallic and sharp objects. In children, ingestion of button batteries dramatically increased and represents an emergency situation, since they generate hydroxide radicals in the oesophageal tissue, leading to caustic injuries like mural necrosis that may extend to adjacent tissue such as trachea or great vessels and aorta with devastating consequences.

On the other hand, in adults, ingested non-food FBs, like dental materials, are more frequently encountered and necessitate an adequate management considering the characteristics of these objects, which may influence the clinical course [1–4].

The behaviour of impacted FBs in the oesophagus is unpredictable. Nevertheless, the likelihood of major complications like obstruction, mural perforation, with either aorto-oesophageal or subclavian fistula, retropharyngeal abscess, migration into the thyroid, the bronchus or the pericardial cavity and fatal mediastinitis should be borne in mind [3]. Therefore, necessity to retrieve and remove impacted FBs is a crucial issue.

The initial management with the decision to discharge the patient with oral antibiotherapy remains controversial. Considering current guidelines [1, 4], this decision was not the best option since failure of fibroscopy to retrieve a sharp object, the pus-discharging oesophageal orifice consistent with a mural perforation as well as clinical symptoms should have been identified as risk factors for major complications. Indeed, they wrongly considered that the object might pass through the gastrointestinal tract without any consequences. However, if a 2nd computed tomography scan had been performed after fibroscopy, the metallic wire would have been probably located into the oesophageal wall. While the patient was discharged home, the metallic wire progressively migrated into the pericardial cavity with deleterious consequences, leading to a new hospital admission. This case highlights the necessity of a prompt and accurate diagnosis to avoid potentially devastating consequences. It also draws attention to investigate the origin and the morphologic characteristics of the FB, the organ impacted and the clinical symptoms in order to implement an adequate therapeutic strategy.

The endoscopic approach to remove impacted oesophageal FBs is the upfront treatment [1, 3, 4], while surgery is required in patients with oesophageal perforation or endoscopically irretrievable FB. Usually, complications like organ fistulas, retropharyngeal abscess, migration into the thyroid, the pericardial cavity, the cervical para-oesophageal space and mediastinum are managed by surgery. Vascular fistulas may benefit from an endovascular placement of a stent graft [5].
