Introduction

Anomalous origin of the left coronary artery from the pulmonary artery (ALCAPA), also known as Bland-White-Garland syndrome, is a rare but potentially fatal congenital anomaly [1, 2]. Although its incidence is low, it represents a major cause of myocardial ischemia and heart failure in infants. Typically, it manifests between 8 and 16 weeks of life, but clinical presentation varies according to the degree of collateral circulation from the right coronary artery [3].

In the neonatal period, patients may remain asymptomatic due to elevated pulmonary arterial pressures, which transiently allow anterograde flow through the anomalous coronary artery. However, with the physiological decline in pulmonary vascular resistance, a “coronary steal” phenomenon develops toward the pulmonary artery, leading to myocardial ischemia, left ventricular dysfunction, mitral regurgitation, and cardiomegaly. These manifestations can mimic dilated cardiomyopathy, and ALCAPA should always be considered in the differential diagnosis [3].

Diagnosis is often challenging, as the anomalous coronary origin is detected by echocardiography in only 50–75% of cases [2, 3]. A high index of suspicion is required, aided by advanced imaging such as strain echocardiography to detect regional wall motion abnormalities. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), coronary CT angiography, and cardiac catheterization provide essential visualization of coronary anatomy. However, coronary evaluation requires a dedicated protocol, and standard cardiac MRI performed for cardiomyopathy assessment may not adequately visualize coronary origins. The treatment of choice is surgical reimplantation of the left coronary artery into the aortic root, which significantly improves survival when performed in specialized centers with advanced circulatory support [3, 4].

This report describes an unusual case of ALCAPA in an older child, initially presenting as an asthma exacerbation complicated by severe pulmonary hypertension and heart failure. Notably, typical echocardiographic features such as intercoronary collateral circulation and retrograde filling of the left coronary artery were absent, likely influenced by markedly elevated pulmonary arterial pressures.

Case presentation

A 9-year-old male with a history of dilated cardiomyopathy and uncontrolled asthma was admitted with a moderate asthma exacerbation. Transthoracic echocardiography showed mildly depressed left ventricular ejection fraction (50–55%), severe mitral regurgitation, marked left atrial dilation, and severe pulmonary hypertension (PH) (estimated RVSP: 89 mmHg) (Fig. 1). There was no increased echogenicity of the endocardium or papillary muscles, nor imaging features suggestive of chronic ischemia. Discussion

Anomalous origin of the left coronary artery from the pulmonary artery (ALCAPA) represents one of the few truly reversible causes of severe dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) in childhood when diagnosed and treated early [1, 5]. Unlike most pediatric cardiomyopathies, myocardial dysfunction in ALCAPA is ischemic in origin and therefore potentially recoverable following surgical correction. Contemporary series from specialized centers report survival rates exceeding 80–90%, underscoring the critical importance of timely recognition and intervention [1].

The present case illustrates an unusual and diagnostically challenging presentation of ALCAPA in an older child initially diagnosed with idiopathic DCM and recurrent asthma exacerbations. The coexistence of chronic respiratory symptoms, heart failure, and pulmonary hypertension led to diagnostic anchoring on asthma-related cardiopulmonary disease and non-ischemic cardiomyopathy. Similar diagnostic delays have been well documented in the literature, particularly in late-presenting ALCAPA, where patients may remain compensated for years until a precipitating event—such as infection, increased metabolic demand, or intercurrent pulmonary disease—triggers decompensation [3]. Clinical presentations in older children and adolescents range from exertional dyspnea and chest pain to arrhythmias and sudden cardiac death [4].

Physiologically, neonates and young infants with ALCAPA may initially remain asymptomatic due to elevated pulmonary arterial pressures that allow antegrade flow into the anomalous left coronary artery. However, as pulmonary vascular resistance falls physiologically after birth, a coronary steal phenomenon develops, redirecting oxygenated blood from the left coronary artery into the pulmonary artery and resulting in ischemia of the anterolateral left ventricular myocardium [4]. Late-presenting patients, in contrast, often demonstrate more complex hemodynamic adaptations. In our patient, severe pulmonary hypertension likely maintained persistent antegrade flow into the left coronary artery, attenuating the classic retrograde coronary steal pattern and contributing to the atypical imaging findings. This pathophysiological variation represents an important deviation from the typical ALCAPA presentation and highlights the diagnostic complexity in patients with coexisting pulmonary hypertension.

Electrocardiography in ALCAPA classically demonstrates anterolateral ischemia, manifesting as deep Q waves in leads I, aVL, and V4–V6 [4]. However, older children may lack these features, particularly when ischemia is chronic and compensated through collateral development. Similarly, while cardiac troponin elevation may be present, this finding is nonspecific and frequently leads to misdiagnosis as myocarditis—a well-described diagnostic pitfall in the literature [2]. Our patient's ECG demonstrated no overt ischemic changes, further contributing to diagnostic delay and illustrating the limitations of electrocardiography in atypical presentations.

Echocardiography remains the first-line diagnostic modality but is highly operator-dependent and contingent upon adequate acoustic windows and appropriate clinical suspicion [3]. Typical echocardiographic features of ALCAPA include dilation of the right coronary artery, increased echogenicity of papillary muscles secondary to chronic ischemia, regional wall motion abnormalities, left ventricular dysfunction, and mitral regurgitation [3, 4]. Importantly, in idiopathic DCM, segmental wall motion abnormalities are uncommon; thus, their presence should prompt systematic evaluation of coronary artery origins [3]. In our case, none of the classic echocardiographic features—including papillary muscle echogenicity, coronary dilation, or visible collateral circulation—were present on initial examination, significantly delaying recognition of an ischemic etiology. This absence of typical findings emphasizes the importance of maintaining clinical suspicion even when conventional echocardiographic markers are absent.

The absence of collateral circulation in this patient is particularly noteworthy and deserves careful consideration. In classic ALCAPA, progressive myocardial ischemia stimulates angiogenesis between the right and left coronary arteries, leading to intercoronary collateral development and retrograde flow into the pulmonary artery [4]. This represents the body's compensatory attempt to maintain myocardial perfusion. However, in patients with persistent pulmonary hypertension and partial antegrade perfusion of the left coronary artery, the ischemic stimulus may be attenuated, thereby limiting collateral formation. This mechanism has been described in isolated reports of late-presenting ALCAPA with preserved or bidirectional flow, analogous to our case [6]. Consequently, while myocardial perfusion remains inadequate, the typical angiographic hallmarks—such as retrograde flow and extensive collateral networks—are absent, creating a diagnostic dilemma.

Chronic left ventricular ischemia leads to elevated left ventricular end-diastolic pressure, left atrial hypertension, pulmonary venous congestion, and ultimately postcapillary pulmonary hypertension. In our patient, severe pulmonary hypertension likely perpetuated antegrade flow into the left coronary artery, creating a self-reinforcing cycle of myocardial ischemia without the classic coronary steal physiology. This pathophysiological mechanism explains the paradoxical combination of severe left ventricular dysfunction, severe mitral regurgitation, and absent collateral circulation observed in this case. Mitral regurgitation itself is a frequent and clinically significant feature of ALCAPA, resulting from ischemic dysfunction or frank necrosis of the papillary muscles, coupled with left ventricular remodeling that impairs leaflet coaptation [4]. While the absence of mitral regurgitation in DCM may favor a non-ischemic etiology, the presence of severe mitral regurgitation—particularly in the pediatric population—should heighten suspicion for ischemic cardiomyopathy. In our patient, severe mitral regurgitation represented a key manifestation of papillary muscle ischemia, despite the absence of characteristic echogenic scarring on imaging studies.

Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is increasingly utilized to assess myocardial viability and fibrosis in pediatric cardiomyopathy. However, accurate visualization of coronary artery origins requires dedicated coronary imaging protocols [4]. In this case, MRI was performed as part of a pre-transplant evaluation and did not include coronary-specific sequences, illustrating a common limitation in the diagnostic pathway of presumed non-ischemic DCM. Ultimately, cardiac catheterization proved decisive, revealing the anomalous origin of the left coronary artery from the pulmonary artery with persistent antegrade flow and absence of collateral circulation (Fig. 4)—an atypical but pathophysiologically coherent pattern in the context of severe pulmonary hypertension. Surgical reimplantation of the left coronary artery into the aorta remains the treatment of choice and offers superior long-term outcomes compared with older techniques such as the Takeuchi procedure [1, 4]. Advances in perioperative care, myocardial protection strategies, and mechanical circulatory support (including extracorporeal membrane oxygenation [ECMO] and ventricular assist devices [VAD]) have dramatically improved survival outcomes [1]. However, ECMO must be employed judiciously, as excessive reduction in pulmonary artery pressure may exacerbate coronary steal phenomenon in unrepaired ALCAPA, potentially worsening myocardial ischemia. Despite delayed diagnosis in this case, timely surgical correction resulted in recovery of ventricular function and significant improvement of mitral regurgitation, highlighting that even in advanced and atypical presentations, ALCAPA remains a surgically curable disease when appropriately recognized [5].

This case expands the phenotypic spectrum of ALCAPA and emphasizes a critical principle: the absence of classic echocardiographic and angiographic findings does not exclude the diagnosis. ALCAPA should be systematically considered in the differential diagnosis of any child presenting with unexplained left ventricular dysfunction, severe mitral regurgitation, or pulmonary hypertension—particularly when clinical features are discordant with typical idiopathic DCM [3, 5]. High clinical suspicion, targeted coronary imaging with dedicated protocols, and multidisciplinary evaluation are essential to prevent missed or delayed diagnosis of this reversible cause of pediatric heart failure.

Conclusion

ALCAPA is a rare but potentially lethal congenital anomaly that requires early recognition and surgical correction. Although classically diagnosed in infancy, this case highlights that it can present later in childhood and mimic asthma or idiopathic dilated cardiomyopathy. A high index of suspicion, careful echocardiographic assessment, and use of advanced imaging are essential, especially in atypical presentations with pulmonary hypertension and absent collateral flow. Timely coronary reimplantation within a multidisciplinary framework can lead to a favorable outcome even when the initial diagnosis is unclear.
