q q -deformation of the Marchenko-Pastur law
Sung-Soo Byun, Yeong-Gwang Jung, Guido Mazzuca

Abstract
. We study a q q -deformed random unitary ensemble associated with the little- q q Laguerre weight, which provides a discrete analogue of the classical Laguerre unitary ensemble. In the double scaling regime q = e − λ / N q=e^{-\lambda/N} , where N N is the system size and λ ≥ 0 \lambda\geq 0 , we derive the limiting spectral distribution as N → ∞ N\to\infty , which yields a q q -deformation of the Marchenko-Pastur law. The limiting density undergoes a phase transition at an explicitly determined critical value λ c \lambda_{c} : for λ < λ c \lambda<\lambda_{c} , the support consists of a single band region, whereas for λ > λ c \lambda>\lambda_{c} an additional saturated region emerges adjacent to the band region. Our derivation of the limiting distribution is based on three complementary approaches: the method of moments, the analysis of a constrained equilibrium problem, and the asymptotic zero distribution of orthogonal polynomials. As a consequence, we establish the convergence of the empirical measure as well as a large deviation principle. In addition, we derive closed-form expressions for the spectral moments using the combinatorial structure of orthogonal polynomials, and obtain large- N N expansions for these moments.



1.
Introduction and main results

The Marchenko-Pastur law is one of the fundamental distributions of random matrix theory, governing the universal limiting behaviour of eigenvalues of Wishart (or sample covariance) matrices
[
54
]
. It forms a one-parameter family indexed by
c\geq 0
, which encodes the aspect ratio of the matrix, with density given by

(1.1)

\rho_{\mathrm{MP}}^{(c)}(x):=\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{\sqrt{(x_{+}-x)(x-x_{-})}}{x}\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{(x_{-},x_{+})}(x),\qquad x_{\pm}=(\sqrt{c+1}\pm 1)^{2}.

Beyond its classical formulation, a growing body of work across integrable probability, representation theory, and mathematical physics has highlighted the remarkable role of
q
-deformations
as a unifying mechanism interpolating between discrete and continuous structures; see e.g.
[
50
,
10
,
59
]
. Here, the parameter
q\in(0,1)
is referred to as the quantisation parameter, with the classical regime recovered in the limit
q\to 1
. Such
q
-deformations often reveal hidden algebraic symmetries, connect probabilistic models with exactly solvable frameworks, and generate families of limiting objects that retain rich integrability while deviating substantially from their classical counterparts. From this perspective, understanding how fundamental spectral laws in random matrix theory behave under
q
-deformation is a natural and compelling problem. Nevertheless, despite significant recent developments on
q
-analogues of classical ensembles

[
13
,
32
,
30
,
58
,
15
,
16
,
43
,
8
,
52
,
18
,
53
,
31
]
, a
q
-deformation of the Marchenko-Pastur law has remained largely unexplored.

In this work, we aim to contribute this direction by providing a unified and comprehensive understanding of the
q
-deformed Marchenko-Pastur law. In our main result Theorem
1.2
below, we analyse a natural
q
-deformed Laguerre unitary ensemble (LUE) and identify its limiting spectral distribution through three complementary approaches:

(A)

asymptotic behaviour of the spectral moments
;

(B)

equilibrium measure of logarithmic energy with upper constraint
;

(C)

limiting empirical zeros of orthogonal polynomials
.

Each perspective captures a different structural aspect of the ensemble: moment methods emphasise combinatorial interpretations of spectral moments, potential theoretic techniques reveal the underlying variational principles together with the framework of the large deviation theory, and the asymptotics of orthogonal polynomial zeros offer an analytic route to the limiting spectral distribution.

Before introducing our model and methods in detail, we first present the explicit form of the
q
-deformed Marchenko-Pastur law, which serves as the central object of this work. In addition to the parameter
c\geq 0
, we introduce a parameter
\lambda\geq 0
that encodes the quantisation via
q=e^{-\lambda/N}
, where
N
denotes the system size.

Definition:
q
-deformed Marchenko-Pastur law (
q=e^{-\lambda/N}
)

Let
c
and
\lambda
be given non-negative real numbers. Let
\lambda_{c}
be a unique positive number satisfying

(1.2)

e^{-\lambda}(1+e^{-c\lambda})=1.

Write
\mathsf{s}=e^{-\lambda}
and define

(1.3)

a=\Big(\sqrt{\mathsf{s}(1-\mathsf{s}^{c+1})}-\sqrt{\mathsf{s}^{c+1}(1-\mathsf{s})}\Big)^{2},\qquad b=\Big(\sqrt{\mathsf{s}(1-\mathsf{s}^{c+1})}+\sqrt{\mathsf{s}^{c+1}(1-\mathsf{s})}\Big)^{2}.

Then the
q
-deformed Marchenko-Pastur law
is defined by

(1.4)

\rho^{(c)}(x):=\frac{2}{\pi\lambda x}\arctan\sqrt{\frac{x-\mathsf{s}-\mathsf{s}^{c+1}+2\mathsf{s}\sqrt{\mathsf{s}^{c}(1-x)}}{\mathsf{s}+\mathsf{s}^{c+1}+2\mathsf{s}\sqrt{\mathsf{s}^{c}(1-x)}-x}}\mathbbm{1}_{(a,b)}(x)+\begin{cases}0&\textup{if }\lambda\leq\lambda_{c},\\
\displaystyle\frac{1}{\lambda x}\mathbbm{1}_{(b,1)}(x)&\textup{if }\lambda>\lambda_{c}.\end{cases}

Equivalently,

(1.5)

\rho^{(c)}(x)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{2}{\pi\lambda x}\arctan\bigg(\frac{\sqrt{(x-a)(b-x)}}{(\sqrt{1-x}+\sqrt{1-a})(\sqrt{1-x}+\sqrt{1-b})}\bigg)\mathbbm{1}_{(a,b)}(x)&\textup{if }\lambda\leq\lambda_{c},\\
\displaystyle\frac{2}{\pi\lambda x}\arctan\bigg(\sqrt{\frac{x-a}{b-x}}\frac{\sqrt{1-x}+\sqrt{1-b}}{\sqrt{1-x}+\sqrt{1-a}}\bigg)\mathbbm{1}_{(a,b)}(x)+\frac{1}{\lambda x}\mathbbm{1}_{(b,1)}(x)&\textup{if }\lambda>\lambda_{c}.\end{cases}

It is straightforward to check that, after the rescaling
\widehat{\rho}^{(c)}(x):=\lambda\rho^{(c)}(\lambda x)
, the classical Marchenko-Pastur law (
1.1
) is recovered in the
limit
q\to 1
, equivalently
\lambda\to 0
:

(1.6)

\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 0}\widehat{\rho}^{(c)}(x)=\rho_{\mathrm{MP}}^{(c)}(x).

In addition, one observes a non-trivial phase transition at a critical value
\lambda_{c}
; see Figure
2
for the graphs of
\rho^{(c)}
.

1.1.
LUE and Marchenko-Pastur law revisited

We begin by revisiting the classical developments that lead to the
Marchenko-Pastur law. By definition, the LUE or the Wishart matrix is given by
W=X^{*}X

[
29
, Chapter 3]
, where
X
is an
M\times N
(
M\geq N
) random matrix with independent standard complex Gaussian entries
[
12
]
.
The joint probability distribution function of eigenvalues
\mathbf{x}=\{x_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
of
W
is given by

(1.7)

d\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{Z_{N}}\prod_{1\leq j<k\leq N}(x_{j}-x_{k})^{2}\prod_{j=1}^{N}w(x_{j})\,dx_{j}.

Here,
Z_{N}
is a normalisation constant and the weight function
w\equiv w^{\rm(L)}
is given by

(1.8)

w^{(\mathrm{L})}(x):=x^{\alpha}e^{-x}\mathbbm{1}_{[0,\infty)}(x),\qquad\alpha=M-N.

When analysing the large-
N
asymptotic behaviour of the LUE, it is necessary to specify the scaling of the parameter
\alpha
.
A standard and sufficiently general choice is to assume a linear scaling of the form

(1.9)

\alpha=cN+d,\qquad c\geq 0,\quad d\in\mathbb{N}.

Then as
N\to\infty
, the empirical measure of
\{x_{j}/N\}_{j=1}^{N}
converges to the Marchenko-Pastur law (
1.1
).
We outline how the three approaches mentioned above can be adapted to derive the Marchenko-Pastur law (
1.1
).

In the first approach, we consider the spectral moments, a method that goes back to Wigner’s seminal work
[
66
]
deriving the semicircle law. (See also
[
24
,
56
,
39
,
67
,
23
,
3
,
20
,
34
]
for more recent development.) With
p
a non-negative integer, the
p
-th spectral moment of a random matrix is defined by

(1.10)

m_{N,p}:=\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{j=1}^{N}x_{j}^{p}\Big],

where the expectation is taken with respect to the distribution (
1.7
). This definition applies to general random matrix ensembles; for the LUE we add the superscript
(\mathrm{L})
to indicate the corresponding moments.
In this case, an exact evaluation of
m_{N,p}^{\rm(L)}
is available in the literature; see e.g.
[
41
, Theorem 2.5]
and
[
40
, Proposition 9.1]
. (See also (
2.10
) and (
2.11
) below.) Furthermore, when
\alpha
is scaled according to (
1.9
), it is well known that

(1.11)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N^{p+1}}m_{N,p}^{\rm(L)}=\sum_{j=0}^{p}\mathrm{N}(p,j)(1+c)^{j},\qquad\mathrm{N}(p,j):=\frac{1}{p}\binom{p}{j}\binom{p}{j-1}.

Here,
\mathrm{N}(p,j)
is the Narayana number.
The Marchenko-Pastur distribution is uniquely determined by its moments,
and we have

(1.12)

\int_{x_{-}}^{x_{+}}x^{p}\,\rho_{\mathrm{MP}}^{(c)}(x)\,dx=\sum_{j=0}^{p}\mathrm{N}(p,j)(1+c)^{j}

from which the Marchenko-Pastur law (
1.1
) follows.

The second approach originates from the viewpoint of statistical physics.
For the normalised eigenvalues
\{x_{j}/N\}_{j=1}^{N}
, the Gibbs measure (
1.7
) is proportional to
\exp(-\frac{\beta}{2}H_{N})
, where
\beta=2
and

(1.13)

H_{N}:=\sum_{1\leq j\not=k\leq N}\log\frac{1}{|x_{j}-x_{k}|}+N\sum_{j=1}^{N}V(x_{j}),\qquad V(x):=x-c\log x.

In the large-
N
limit, the continuum version of this Hamiltonian corresponds to the energy functional

(1.14)

I_{V}[\mu]:=\iint_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}\log\frac{1}{|x-y|}\,\mu(dx)\,\mu(dy)+\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}V(x)\,\mu(dx).

From the perspective of statistical physics, one naturally expects that the particles arrange themselves so as to minimise their Hamiltonian. In fact, by the standard theory of log-gases and large deviation principles (see e.g.
[
48
,
29
,
62
,
6
,
14
]
), the limiting global distribution is characterised as a unique minimiser of
I_{V}
. Then by applying the classical methods in logarithmic potential theory
[
61
]
, one finds that this minimiser is precisely the Marchenko-Pastur distribution.

The third approach can be regarded as the
\beta=\infty
extremal counterpart of the second method, but formulated at finite
N
. In the log-gas formulation (
1.7
), it is well known that the global limiting distribution does not depend on the (fixed) inverse temperature
\beta
. The idea is to consider the opposite extreme, namely the freezing low-temperature regime, where the configuration of the minimiser of the discrete Hamiltonian coincides with the zeros of the associated orthogonal polynomial.
For the LUE, the associated orthogonal polynomials are the generalised Laguerre polynomials
L_{n}^{(\alpha)}
, defined through the three-term recurrence relation

(1.15)

(n+1)L_{n+1}^{(\alpha)}(x)-(2n+\alpha+1-x)L_{n}^{(\alpha)}(x)+(n+\alpha)L_{n-1}^{(\alpha)}(x)=0,

with initial conditions
L_{0}^{(\alpha)}(x)=1
and
L_{1}^{(\alpha)}(x)=1+\alpha-x
.
From the theory of orthogonal polynomials, the Marchenko-Pastur law (
1.1
) can then be obtained as the limiting empirical distribution of the zeros of the rescaled Laguerre polynomial
L_{N}^{(\alpha)}(x/N)
.

1.2.
Main results

We first introduce the
q
-deformation of the LUE. In the
q
-deformed framework, the basic idea is that the integer
n
is replaced by the
q
-integer

(1.16)

[n]_{q}=\frac{1-q^{n}}{1-q},\qquad q\in(0,1).

Building on this, the other notions such as
q
-binomials or
q
-Pochhammer symbols naturally arise. For instance, the standard notations include

(1.17)

{\begin{bmatrix}n\\
m\end{bmatrix}}_{q}=\frac{[n]_{q}!}{[m]_{q}![n-m]_{q}!},\qquad(x;q)_{n}=\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}(1-xq^{j}),\qquad(x_{1},\cdots,x_{k};q)_{\infty}=\prod_{j=0}^{\infty}\prod_{l=1}^{k}(1-x_{l}q^{j}).

Using this, we define the little
q
-Laguerre weight (see e.g.
[
32
, Eq. (2.52)]
) by

(1.18)

w^{(\mathrm{qL})}(x;q)=x^{\alpha}(qx;q)_{\infty},\qquad\alpha>-1.

Notice here that by
[
13
, Eq. (2.16)]
, we have

(1.19)

\lim_{q\to 1}(1-q)^{-\alpha}w^{(\mathrm{qL})}((1-q)x;q)=w^{(\mathrm{L})}(x).

Next, we consider the system
\bm{x}=\{x_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
supported on the one-sided
q
-lattice
\{1,q,q^{2},\dots\}
having the joint distribution of the form (see e.g.
[
59
,
15
]
)

(1.20)

d\mathcal{P}(\bm{x})=\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}_{N}}\prod_{1\leq j<k\leq N}(x_{j}-x_{k})^{2}\prod_{j=1}^{N}\omega^{\rm(qL)}(x_{j}),

where
\mathcal{Z}_{N}
is the normalisation constant. We call the ensemble (
1.20
) as the
(little)
q
-LUE
. By (
1.19
), the renormalised system
x_{j}\mapsto(1-q)x_{j}
recovers the classical LUE as
q\to 1
.

Following the general notion (
1.10
), we define

(1.21)

m_{N,p}^{\rm(qL)}:=\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{j=1}^{N}x_{j}^{p}\Big]

for the spectral moments of the
q
-LUE. Here, the expectation is taken with respect to (
1.20
).
Unlike the continuum case
q=1
, where the spectral moments can be derived using well-known properties of Laguerre polynomials together with complex-analytic techniques, the
q
-deformed setting lacks comparable analytic tools for obtaining closed-form expressions (although the discrete loop equation approach of
[
10
]
may be adaptable to this setting; cf.
[
67
]
for the continuum case).
In recent years, significant progress has been made for other
q
-deformed
ensembles, with algebraic combinatorial methods – such as symmetric function theory
[
13
,
32
,
30
,
58
]
and the combinatorics of orthogonal polynomials
[
15
,
16
]
– yielding explicit summation formulas. In particular, the Flajolet–Viennot theory based on techniques in
[
15
,
16
]
produces positive formulas well suited for large-
N
asymptotics.
In Theorem
1.1
below, we establish analogous results for the
q
-LUE, where the underlying combinatorial structure turns out to be richer than in
[
15
,
16
]
.

Our first results establish a closed formula for the spectral moments and their large-
N
asymptotic behaviour. For the latter, we recall that for
a,b>0
, the regularised incomplete beta function is defined by

(1.22)

I_{x}(a,b)=\frac{\Gamma(a+b)}{\Gamma(a)\Gamma(b)}\int_{0}^{x}t^{a-1}(1-t)^{b-1}\,dt,\qquad(0<x<1);

see
[
60
, Section 8.17]
.

q

(Closed formula)
For any nonnegative integers
p,\alpha

N\in\mathbb{N}

(1.23)

(Large-

Here,

Note that when

(1.28)

I_{x}(m,n-m+1)=\sum_{j=m}^{n}\binom{n}{j}x^{j}(1-x)^{n-j}.

By (

\mathcal{M}_{p}^{\rm(qL)}
is given as follows:

(1.29)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{\rm(qL)}_{0}=1,\qquad\mathcal{M}^{\rm(qL)}_{1}=\frac{1}{\lambda}(1-\mathsf{s})(1-\mathsf{s}^{c+1}),\qquad\mathcal{M}^{\rm(qL)}_{2}=\frac{1}{2\lambda}(1-\mathsf{s})(1-\mathsf{s}^{c+1})(1+\mathsf{s}+\mathsf{s}^{c+1}-3\mathsf{s}^{c+2}).

In particular, the first moment
\mathcal{M}^{\rm(qL)}_{1}
plays an important role in the analysis of the equilibrium measure problem discussed later.

Next, we discuss the macroscopic limit of the density. For this, we recall that for a parameter
\mathsf{a}\in(0,1/q)
, the
little
q
-Laguerre
(also known as
Wall
) polynomial
p_{n}(x;\mathsf{a}|q)\equiv p_{n}(x)

[
44
, Section 14.20]
is defined by the three-term recurrence relation

(1.30)

q^{n}(1-\mathsf{a}q^{n+1})p_{n+1}(x)+(x-q^{n}(1-\mathsf{a}q^{n+1})-\mathsf{a}q^{n}(1-q^{n}))p_{n}(x)+\mathsf{a}q^{n}(1-q^{n})p_{n-1}(x)=0

with initial conditions
p_{0}(x)=1
and
p_{1}(x)=-x/(1-\mathsf{a}q)+1
.
Throughout this paper, we put

(1.31)

\mathsf{a}=q^{\alpha},\qquad\alpha>-1,\qquad\alpha=cN+d.

By taking the continuum limit, one can recover the classical Laguerre polynomial. More precisely, we have

(1.32)

\lim_{q\rightarrow 1}p_{n}((1-q)x;q^{\alpha}|q)=\frac{n!\,\Gamma(\alpha+1)}{\Gamma(n+\alpha+1)}\,L_{n}^{(\alpha)}(x).

The three-term recurrence relation (
1.15
) is also recovered from (
1.30
) via this scaling limit.

To present our main results, we introduce the associated finite-
N
observables.
The first is the averaged density (or one-point function) of the
q
-LUE, which, by the general theory of determinantal point processes, is expressed in terms of the
q
-Laguerre polynomials as

(1.33)

\rho_{N}(x):=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\frac{p_{j}(x;q^{\alpha}\,|\,q)^{2}}{h_{j}}w^{(\mathrm{qL})}(x;q),\qquad h_{j}:=(1-q)q^{(\alpha+1)n}\frac{(q;q)_{n}}{(q^{\alpha+1};q)_{n}}\frac{(q;q)_{\infty}}{(q^{\alpha+1};q)_{\infty}}.

The second observable is the empirical measure of the
q
-LUE (
1.20
):

(1.34)

\mu_{N}(dx):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\delta_{x_{j}}.

The third is the empirical zero distribution of the
q
-Laguerre polynomials, defined by

(1.35)

\nu_{n,N}(dx):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\delta_{x_{n,N}^{(j)}},

where
\delta_{x_{n,N}^{(1)}},\cdots,\delta_{x_{n,N}^{(n)}}
are the zeros of
p_{n}(x;q^{\alpha}|q)
.

In addition, in order to formulate the large deviation principle, we introduce the set

(1.36)

\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(0,1):=\bigg\{\mu(dx)\;|\;\operatorname{supp}(\mu(dx))\subseteq[0,1],\;\frac{\delta\mu(dx)}{\delta x}\leq\frac{1}{\lambda x}\bigg\}

of probability measures, where
\operatorname{supp}(\mu(dx))
is the support of the measure and
\frac{\delta\mu(dx)}{\delta x}
is the Radon-Nikodym derivative of the measure. Here, the upper constraint condition is the essential structural element inherited from the underlying discrete model; cf. Remark
6
.
We also introduce the energy functional

(1.37)

I[\mu]:=-\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\log(|x-y|)\,\mu(dx)\,\mu(dy)+\int_{0}^{1}\Big(\frac{1}{\lambda}\operatorname{Li}_{2}(x)-c\log x\Big)\,\mu(dx)\,,

where
\operatorname{Li}_{2}(x)
is the dilogarithm
[
60
, Chapter 25.12]
, defined for

x<1
by

(1.38)

\operatorname{Li}_{2}(x)=-\int_{0}^{x}\frac{\log(1-t)}{t}\,dt.

Here, the appearance of the dilogarithm arises from the asymptotic behaviour of the weight function
w^{(\mathrm{qL})}
; see Lemma
3.1
. We are now ready to state the main result of the paper.

Theorem 1.2

(
q
-deformed Marchenko-Pastur law via three approaches
)
.

Let
c\geq 0
and
d\in\mathbb{N}
be fixed, and let
q
be scaled according to (
1.24
). Let
\rho^{(c)}
be the
q
-deformed Marchenko-Pastur law (
1.5
).

(A)

(Probability measure determined by moments)

As
N\to\infty
, in the sense of integration against continuous test functions
f\in C([0,1])
, we have

(1.39)

\frac{1}{N}\rho_{N}(x)\to\rho^{(c)}(x).

(B)

(Equilibrium measure of the
q
-logarithmic energy)

The sequence of measures
\{\mu_{N}(dx)\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies a large deviation principle in the space of probability measures
\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(0,1)
with scale
N^{2}
and a good convex rate function
J[\mu]:=I[\mu]-\inf_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(0,1)}I[\mu]
, where
I[\mu]
is given by (
1.37
).
Furthermore,

(1.40)

I[\rho^{(c)}(x)\,dx]=\inf_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(0,1)}I[\mu]

and consequently, as
N\to\infty
, we have

(1.41)

\mu_{N}(dx)\to\rho^{(c)}(x)\,dx\qquad\text{almost surely}\,.

(C)

(Limiting zero distribution of little
q
-Laguerre polynomial)
As
N\to\infty
with
n/N\rightarrow 1
, in the sense of integration against continuous test functions
f\in C([0,1])
, we have

(1.42)

\nu_{n,N}(dx)\to\rho^{(c)}(x)\,dx.

1.3.
Discussions and related works

We end this section by providing several remarks on our main results.

Remark 1

(Saturated and band region)
.

In the definition of the
q
-deformed Marchenko-Pastur law (
1.5
), one can easily check that the critical value
\lambda_{c}
defined in (
1.2
) is decreasing function of
c
. For instance, we have

(1.43)

\lambda_{c}|_{c=0}=\log 2,\qquad\lambda_{c}|_{c=1}=\log\frac{\sqrt{5}+1}{2}.

In addition, we note that the critical value
\lambda_{c}
is characterised by the condition
b<1
.

When
\lambda>\lambda_{c}
, the limiting density (
1.5
) develops a portion of its support where it coincides with the upper constraint in (
1.36
). This part of the support is referred to as the
saturated region
. The other part, where the density is strictly below the upper constraint is called the
band region
; see e.g.
[
4
]
.

Such a phase separation is closely aligned with the well-known decomposition into
frozen (solid)
and
liquid (rough)
regions in random growth models; see e.g.
[
9
,
27
,
19
]
and the references therein.
In that setting, frozen regions correspond to portions of the limit shape where the local slope is forced to saturate boundary constraints, whereas liquid regions arise where the height function varies freely and exhibits nontrivial fluctuations.
The appearance of saturated and band regions in our model follows an analogous mechanism: the equilibrium measure becomes pinned to its upper constraint in the saturated phase, while in the band phase it remains strictly within the admissible range, leading to genuinely fluctuating behaviour. This structural parallel highlights a connection between discrete log-gas ensembles and integrable probabilistic models; see Remark
10
for more on this direction.

Remark 2

(Jackson
q
-integral)
.

In the
q
-deformed setting, the natural analogue of the integral, denoted by
d_{q}
, is the Jackson
q
-integral defined by

(1.44)

\int_{0}^{A}f(x)\,d_{q}x=(1-q)\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}Aq^{j}f(Aq^{j}).

Due to the discrete nature of the Jackson integral, the usual tools of complex analysis are no longer applicable, which is one of the main sources of difficulty in the
q
-deformed setting. Nonetheless, as an intermediate step in the proof of Theorem
1.1
(A), we obtain the following identity for the
q
-deformed Laguerre polynomials:

(1.45)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\quad\int_{0}^{1}x^{p}p_{j}(x;q^{\alpha}|q)^{2}w^{(\mathrm{qL})}(x;q)\,d_{q}x\\
&=(1-q)^{p+1}q^{(p+\alpha+1)j}\frac{(q;q)_{j}}{(q^{\alpha+1};q)_{j}}\frac{(q;q)_{\infty}}{(q^{\alpha+1};q)_{\infty}}[p]_{q}!\sum_{i=0}^{p}q^{(p-i)(\alpha-i)}{\begin{bmatrix}p\\
i\end{bmatrix}}_{q}{\begin{bmatrix}\alpha+j\\
i\end{bmatrix}}_{q}{\begin{bmatrix}p-i+j\\
j\end{bmatrix}}_{q}.\end{split}

This expression extends (
2.20
), which is recovered by taking
p=0
. Such explicit evaluations of Jackson
q
-integrals are of independent interest. In addition, such exact evaluations are naturally connected to the superintegrable structure of the model and to symmetric function techniques; see
[
53
, Section 3.3]
.

Remark 3

(The special case
c=0
: hard edge at the origin and
q
-deformed semi-circle law)
.

When
c=0
, the RHS of (
1.11
) reduces to the Catalan number
C_{p}:=\frac{1}{p+1}\binom{2p}{p}
.
This is consistent with the well–known fact that the moments of the semicircle law are given by the Catalan numbers, and that the Marchenko-Pastur law (
1.1
) with
c=0
reduces to the semicircle law under the quadratic change of variables
x\mapsto x^{2}
.

This phenomenon can also be observed in the
q
-deformed model.
Namely, if
c=0
, we have

(1.46)

\mathcal{M}^{\rm(qL)}_{p}\Big|_{c=0}=\frac{1}{\lambda p}I_{1-\mathsf{s}}(p+1,p).

This expression coincides with the leading term of the
2p
-th spectral moment of the
q
-deformed GUE recently obtained in
[
15
, Theorem 2.3]
.
Moreover, for the special case
c=0
, the density (
1.5
) simplifies to

(1.47)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\rho^{(0)}(x)&=\frac{2}{\pi\lambda x}\arctan\sqrt{\frac{1-\sqrt{1-x}}{1+\sqrt{1-x}}\frac{\sqrt{1-x}+1-2e^{-\lambda}}{\sqrt{1-x}-1+2e^{-\lambda}}}\mathbbm{1}_{(0,4e^{-\lambda}(1-e^{-\lambda}))}(x)\\
&\quad+\begin{cases}0&\textup{if }\lambda\leq\log 2,\\
\displaystyle\frac{1}{\lambda x}\mathbbm{1}_{(4e^{-\lambda}(1-e^{-\lambda}),1)}(x)&\textup{if }\lambda>\log 2.\end{cases}\end{split}

In this case the left edge coincides with the origin, producing a singularity that forms a hard edge. Moreover, its quadratic transform
|x|\rho^{(0)}(x^{2})
corresponds to the
q
-deformed semicircle law described in
[
15
, Theorem 2.6]
(see also
[
16
, Remark 4]
).

Remark 4

(Continuum limit
q\to 1
)
.

By construction, the leading-order moment
\mathcal{M}_{p}^{\rm(qL)}
can be written as

(1.48)

\mathcal{M}_{p}^{\rm(qL)}=\int_{a}^{b}x^{p}\rho^{(c)}(x)\,dx.

As noted earlier in (
1.6
), the appropriately rescaled density

\widehat{\rho}^{(c)}
converges to
\rho_{\rm MP}^{(c)}
as
\lambda\to 0
.
The same limiting phenomenon can also be observed directly in the corresponding moments.
For this, notice that

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{\lambda^{p}}\mathcal{M}_{p}^{\rm(qL)}=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{j=0}^{p}\binom{p}{j}\binom{2p-j}{p-1}c^{j}=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{j=0}^{p}\bigg(\sum_{k=j}^{p}\binom{p}{k}\binom{p}{k-1}\binom{k}{j}\bigg)c^{j}=\sum_{j=0}^{p}N(p,j)\sum_{k=0}^{j}\binom{j}{k}c^{k}.

Therefore, as
\lambda\to 0
, we obtain

(1.49)

\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{\lambda^{p}}\mathcal{M}_{p}^{\rm(qL)}=\sum_{j=0}^{p}\mathrm{N}(p,j)(1+c)^{j},

which recovers (
2.10
).

In addition, by
[
35
, Eq. (3.46)]
, for
c=0
, the spectral moment of the LUE satisfies the expansion

(1.50)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N^{p}}m_{N,p}^{\rm(L)}|_{c=0}=NC_{p}+\frac{d}{2}\bigg(\binom{2p}{p}-\delta_{p,0}\bigg)+O\Big(\frac{1}{N}\Big).

Note that by (
1.27
), we also have

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\frac{1}{\lambda^{p}}\mathcal{M}_{p,1/2}^{\rm(qL)}(d)=\frac{d}{2}\bigg(\binom{2p}{p}-\delta_{p,0}\bigg).

Therefore, the expansion (
1.50
) follows by taking
\lambda\to 0
limit of (
1.26
).

Such a limiting behaviour is also reflected at the level of the rate function in the large deviation principle. Notice first that as
\lambda\to 0
, the upper constraint in (
1.36
) disappears. Moreover, by the asymptotic behaviour

(1.51)

\frac{1}{\lambda}\operatorname{Li}_{2}(\lambda x)=x+O(\lambda),\qquad\textup{as }\lambda\to 0,

the energy functional (
1.37
) converges to the one (
1.14
) associated with the LUE.

Remark 5

(Large-
N
expansion of spectral moments)
.

While Theorem
1.1
(B) concerns primarily the leading-order asymptotic behaviour of
{m}_{N,p}^{(\mathrm{qL})}
, with some additional effort one can obtain the large-

(1.52)

q^{p}{m}_{N,p}^{(\mathrm{qL})}=\mathcal{M}_{p}^{\rm(qL)}N+\mathcal{M}_{p,1/2}^{\rm(qL)}+\mathcal{M}_{p,1}^{\rm(qL)}\frac{1}{N}+\cdots.

Although the coefficients in this expansion can in principle be computed explicitly, the resulting formulas are rather involved, and we refrain from presenting them here. For analogous large-

expansions in the classical setting, we refer the reader to
[
35
,
67
]
and the references therein.

Remark 6

(Upper constraint condition)
.

Compared with the classical case, the most notable feature of the large deviation principle for the
q
-deformed model is the presence of the upper constraint in (
1.36
). We briefly explain how this constraint naturally emerges from the discrete nature of the model; see also the discussion at the end of
[
25
, Subsection 1.2.2]
.

Since the model (
1.20
) lies on the exponential lattice
q^{j}
, there exists a sequence of
\ell_{j}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
x_{j}=q^{\ell_{j}}
and
|\ell_{j}-\ell_{k}|>1
for all
j,k
. Suppose that the upper constraint is violated, so that there exists an interval
I=(a,b)
and some
\varepsilon>0
for which

\mu(I)>\int_{a}^{b}\frac{1}{\lambda x}\,dx+\varepsilon=\frac{\log(b)-\log(a)}{\lambda}+\varepsilon\,.

Note that one can compute the measure of the set
I
according to the empirical measure
\mu_{N}(dx)
:

\displaystyle\mu_{N}(I)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\#\{j\in\mathbb{N}\,|\,a<x_{j}<b\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\#\Big\{j\in\mathbb{N}\,|\,-N\frac{\log(b)}{\lambda}<\ell_{j}<-N\frac{\log(a)}{\lambda}\Big\}\leq\frac{1}{N}\Big(N\frac{\log(b)-\log(a)}{\lambda}+1\Big).

Therefore it follows that
\mu(I)-\mu_{N}(I)>\varepsilon-1/N.
On the other hand, if
N>2/\varepsilon
the previous inequality reads
\mu(I)-\mu_{N}(I)>\varepsilon/2
, which in turn implies that
d(\mu,\mu_{N})>\varepsilon/2
for any admissible distance
d
. This yields a contradiction.

Remark 7

(Zero distributions of orthogonal polynomials)
.

The zero distribution of orthogonal polynomials plays a central role in many aspects of the theory, most notably in describing their asymptotic behaviour. A well-established framework for determining the limiting zero distribution is available, and in our setting we follow the general approach developed by Kuijlaars and Van Assche
[
45
]
; see also
[
42
]
and references therein.
Although Theorem
1.2
(C) follows relatively directly from this classical theory, it is still useful to state it explicitly, as it confirms the limiting object obtained in parts (A) and (B) and clarifies how these results fit together with the additional viewpoints discussed above.
In addition, we mention that recent developments have introduced a complementary perspective
based on finite free probability, offering further structural insights into such zero distributions; see
[
46
,
47
]
.

Remark 8

(
\beta
-ensemble generalisation)
.

In analogy with the classical
\beta
-ensemble, one may consider
finite-temperature extensions of the joint probability density function
(
1.20
) of the
q
-Laguerre ensemble, in which the
interaction is deformed by an additional temperature parameter.
A natural example is the
(q,t)
-extension (see e.g.
[
59
,
13
]
), which arises naturally from Macdonald polynomial theory and its underlying integrable structure. We also refer to
[
43
]
for a different type of deformation of the
q
-Laguerre ensemble.
In the regime
q^{-\lambda/N}
, the natural associated functional is given by

(1.53)

I_{\beta}[\mu]:=-\frac{\beta}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\log(|x-y|)\,\mu(dx)\,\mu(dy)+\int_{0}^{1}\Big(\frac{1}{\lambda}\operatorname{Li}_{2}(x)-c\log x\Big)\,\mu(dx)\,,

where
\mu(dx)\in\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(0,1)
.
Although the upper constraint on the support precludes obtaining the
minimiser by a trivial rescaling of our main result, the arguments developed in Subsection
3.2
extend in a straightforward manner to this setting.

In addition, in the high-temperature regime where
\beta=O(1/N)
, the resulting Hamiltonian involves not only an energy functional of the form (
1.53
) but also an entropy term; see e.g.
[
1
,
2
]
. A recent study
[
22
]
investigates such a high-temperature regime in discrete ensembles, and extending this analysis to the present setting appears to be an interesting direction for future research.

Remark 9

(Local statistics and scaling limits)
.

One of the most natural directions for further investigation is the study of local statistics in
q
-deformed ensembles. Owing to the underlying determinantal structure, these local statistics can be analysed through the asymptotic behaviour of the associated orthogonal polynomials. In particular, in the regime
q^{-\lambda/N}
, the exponential
q
-lattice
\{1,q,q^{2},\dots\}
effectively behaves like a linear lattice. Consequently, one expects that the universal local statistics identified in
[
4
]
emerge at the bulk and soft edges of the band regions, as well as at the transition points where the band meets the saturated regions. (We also refer to
[
8
,
38
]
for related developments in integrable probability, which—guided by the universality philosophy—are expected to be relevant to the present model, both in the hard-edge scaling limit and in the regime where the soft edge meets the hard edge.)
This constitutes an interesting direction that requires an asymptotic analysis of orthogonal polynomials in a double-scaling regime, for instance via the Riemann–Hilbert method, which we plan to pursue in future work.

Remark 10

(Connection to the last passage percolation)
.

It is by now well understood that random matrix theory (or log gases) provides an effective framework for deriving fundamental properties of integrable probability models. One prominent example is the seminal work of Johansson
[
49
]
, who used a bijection between last passage percolation (LPP) with i.i.d. geometric weights and the Meixner ensemble to obtain the law of large numbers, fluctuations, and large deviations of the last passage time. These ideas have since been developed in several variants
[
5
,
36
]
; see also the recent works
[
7
,
11
,
38
,
17
]
and references therein. More recently, connections to classical random matrix ensembles have also emerged. In particular, the above LPP model is linked to the Jacobi unitary ensemble
[
11
]
. As a special case, replacing geometric weights by exponential ones yields a model related to the classical LUE. From this perspective, it is perhaps surprising that the law of large numbers for the last passage time can be derived directly from the Marchenko-Pastur law.

Beyond the classical ensembles,
q
-deformations play a significant role in integrable probability. In
[
8
]
, LPP with
non-identically distributed
weights was related to a
q
-deformed model known as the discrete Muttalib–Borodin ensemble. Our model arises as a special case of this general framework, and, following the viewpoint of
[
11
]
, we expect that the law of large numbers for the last passage time in this broader setting should be governed by the
q
-deformed Marchenko-Pastur law.

2.1.

For a given weight function
w:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}

(2.1)

\rho_{N}(x)=\rho_{N,w}(x)=N\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N-1}}\mathbf{P}(x,x_{2},\cdots,x_{N})\,dx_{2}\cdots\,dx_{N}.

The
1

\{P_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
satisfying the orthogonality condition

(2.2)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}P_{n}(x)P_{m}(x)w(x)\,dx=h_{n}\delta_{nm}.

Then it is well known that

(2.3)

\rho_{N}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\frac{1}{h_{j}}P_{j}(x)^{2}w(x).

Furthermore, the spectral moment

in (
1.10

(2.4)

m_{N,p}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{p}\rho_{N}(x)\,dx.

Then, using the notation

(2.5)

\mathfrak{m}_{p,j}=\frac{1}{h_{j}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{p}P_{j}(x)^{2}w(x)\,dx,

we have the orthogonal polynomial representation of the spectral moments

(2.6)

m_{N,p}=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\mathfrak{m}_{p,j}.

These representations remain valid in the framework of the

-unitary ensemble and the

-orthogonal polynomial, where the usual integral is replaced with the Jackson

The theory of Flajolet and Viennot
[

establishes a correspondence between the moments of orthogonal polynomials and certain weighted lattice paths; see also

for a review. We briefly recall the aspects of this framework that are relevant to our discussion.
Within this theory, the fundamental structural feature of orthogonal polynomials is their three-term recurrence relation

(2.7)

P_{n+1}(x)=(x-b_{n})P_{n}(x)-\lambda_{n}P_{n-1}(x),

where
\{b_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
and
\{\lambda_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
are associated coefficient sequences. These sequences determine the weights assigned to the corresponding lattice paths.

A lattice path is a finite sequence
w=(s_{0},s_{1},\cdots,s_{n})

(s_{j},s_{j+1})
a

. In particular, we refer to each step
(s_{j},s_{j+1})
as follows:

•

East step
if
x_{j+1}=x_{j}+1
and
y_{j+1}=y_{j}
;

•

North-East step
if
x_{j+1}=x_{j}+1
and
y_{j+1}=y_{j}+1
;

•

South-East step
if
x_{j+1}=x_{j}+1
and
y_{j+1}=y_{j}-1
.

Furthermore, we say a step
(s_{j},s_{j+1})
is of height
k
if
y_{j}=k
.
A

is a lattice path in the first quadrant that consists

2.2.
Spectral moments of the LUE revisited

In preparation for enumerating the spectral moments of the

In the next section, we will see that the combinatorial interpretation for the

Proposition 2.1

(
Spectral moments of the LUE
)
.

Let
m_{N,p}^{(\mathrm{L})}
be the spectral moment of the LUE. Then for any nonnegative integers
p,\alpha
and
N\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

(2.10)

m_{N,p}^{(\mathrm{L})}=p!\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\sum_{i=0}^{p}\binom{p}{i}\binom{\alpha+j}{i}\binom{p-i+j}{j}.

We note that in the literature, some equivalent formulae for the spectral moment of LUE are investigated. For instance, we have

(2.11)

\displaystyle\begin{split}m_{N,p}^{(\mathrm{L})}&=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{p}(-1)^{i-1}\frac{(N+\alpha+p-i)_{p}(N+p-i)_{p}}{(p-i)!(i-1)!}\\
&=N(N+\alpha)(p-1)!\sum_{j=0}^{\left\lfloor\frac{p-1}{2}\right\rfloor}\frac{1}{j+1}\binom{N-1}{j}\binom{N+\alpha-1}{j}\binom{2N+\alpha+p-2j-2}{p-2j-1};\end{split}

see
[
41
, Theorem 2.5]
,
[

, Proposition 9.1]
,
[
57
, Appendix A]
and
[
35
, Proposition 3.11]
. We also refer to a recent work
[

]
for the corresponding results in the fixed-trace version of the LUE and for their applications to quantum information theory.

As noted earlier, the orthogonal polynomials corresponding to the Laguerre

are the generalised Laguerre polynomials.
They satisfy the orthogonality relation

(2.12)

\int_{0}^{\infty}L_{n}^{(\alpha)}(x)L_{m}^{(\alpha)}(x)w^{(\mathrm{L})}(x)\,dx=h^{(\mathrm{L})}_{n}\delta_{nm},\qquad h_{n}^{(\mathrm{L})}=\frac{\Gamma(n+\alpha+1)}{n!}.

Then it follows from (

\mathfrak{m}_{p,j}^{(\mathrm{L})}:=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{p}L^{(\alpha)}_{j}(x)^{2}}{h_{j}^{(\mathrm{L})}}w^{(\mathrm{L})}(x)\,dx=\sum_{\omega\in\mathrm{Mot}_{p,j,j}}\mathrm{wt}_{b,\lambda}(\omega),

where, by (

(2.14)

b_{n}=2n+\alpha+1,\qquad\lambda_{n}=n(n+\alpha).

To construct a bijection between the frameworks of a Motzkin path and
a bipartite matching, we introduce the notion of a
Laguerre history
.
This is obtained by assigning labels to the East and South–East steps
of a Motzkin path as follows.
For integers
0\leq m\leq k+\alpha
and
1\leq l\leq k
,

•

an East step at height
k
is labeled by either
m
or
\widetilde{l}
;

•

a South–East step at height
k
is labeled by a pair

[m,\widetilde{l}]
.

Thus, there are
(2k+\alpha+1)
possible labels for an East step and

k(k+\alpha)
possible labels for a South–East step.

With this labeling rule, we obtain the following enumeration of
Motzkin-path weights in terms of bipartite matchings.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
M_{n,j}^{(\alpha)}(l)
be the subset of matchings in
M_{n,j}^{(\alpha)}

that contain exactly
l
edges. Let

and
\lambda_{n}
be given by (

). Then we have

(2.17)

\sum_{\omega\in\mathrm{Mot}_{p,j,j}}\mathrm{wt}_{b,\lambda}(\omega)=\Big|M_{p,j}^{(\alpha)}(p)\Big|.

Proof.

The lemma follows from a one-to-one correspondence between Laguerre
histories on Motzkin paths in
\mathrm{Mot}_{p,j,j}
and bipartite
matchings in
M_{p,j}^{(\alpha)}(p)
.
We construct this correspondence as a dynamical bijection in the
following manner: as we traverse the steps of a Motzkin path, we
sequentially add vertices and edges to the bipartite matching according
to the step type and its assigned label. Each rule is illustrated in Figure

(S1)

Initially, place
\alpha

(S2)

Add

(0,0)
to
(0,j)

(S3)

For an East step of height

labeled

m

the left in the top row.

(S4)

For a North–East step, simply add the two new vertices to the top and bottom rows, but do not introduce any edge.

(S5)

For a South–East step of height

added bottom vertex to the
m
-th isolated top vertex, and another
connecting the newly added top vertex to the
l

(S6)

For an East step labeled
0

(S7)

For an East step of height
k

\widetilde{l}

Lemma 2.3
.

For any nonnegative integers
\alpha,p
and

, we have

(2.18)

\Big|M_{p,j}^{(\alpha)}(p)\Big|=p!\sum_{i=0}^{p}\binom{p}{i}\binom{\alpha+j}{i}\binom{p-i+j}{j}.

Proof.

Let
l
be the number of edges incident to vertices in
T_{-}
or
B_{-}
, and
i
be the number of edges incident to vertices in
T_{-}
. We then choose
i
vertices in
B_{+}
and form
i

\binom{\alpha+j}{i}\binom{p}{i}i!.

Next, there are
l-i
edges incident to the vertices in
B_{-}
. Choosing
l-i

B_{-}
and
l-i
vertices in
T_{+}
, and pairing them, contributes
\binom{j}{l-i}\binom{p}{l-i}(l-i)!.

The remaining

T_{+}
with vertices in
B_{+}
. Since there are
p-(l-i)
isolated vertices in
T_{+}
and
p-i
isolated vertices in

, this contributes
\binom{p-l+i}{p-l}\binom{p-i}{p-l}(p-l)!.

Combining the above contributions and summing over all admissible

gives that

(2.19)

\displaystyle\Big|M_{p,j}^{(\alpha)}(p)\Big|=p!\sum_{l=0}^{p}\sum_{i=0}^{l}\binom{p}{i}\binom{p-i}{l-i}\binom{\alpha+j}{i}\binom{j}{l-i}.

Notice here that the RHS of (

\displaystyle p!\sum_{i=0}^{p}\sum_{l=i}^{p}\binom{p}{i}\binom{p-i}{l-i}\binom{\alpha+j}{i}\binom{j}{l-i}=p!\sum_{i=0}^{p}\binom{p}{i}\binom{\alpha+j}{i}\sum_{k=0}^{p-i}\binom{p-i}{k}\binom{j}{k}.

Then by the classical Vandermonde identity (see e.g.
[

, p. 190]
), we obtain (

As previously noted, a fundamental way to interpret a

In the same spirit, unlike in the previous subsection—where the bijection to bipartite matchings allowed us to count all matchings uniformly—in the

To be more precise, we introduce a statistic, which we call the

(C2)

(C3)

a pair consisting of an edge
(a,\widetilde{b})
and an isolated vertex

with

The conditions (C2) and (C3) may appear less intuitive at first, but they also admit a direct pictorial interpretation.
By introducing an imaginary

and connected to all isolated vertices—we see that these conditions count precisely the effective crossings created by adjoining this additional vertex; see Figure

Proof of Proposition
2.4
.

By the bijection between Motzkin paths and bipartite matchings established in the proof of Lemma
2.2
, it suffices to show that
\mathrm{wt}_{b,\lambda}(\omega)=\sum^{*}q^{\mathrm{cr}(M)}
where the RHS runs over all matchings induced by every possible Laguerre history on the Motzkin path
\omega
. More precisely, the various admissible labellings arising in the Laguerre histories are all counted in the sum on the RHS.

Recall that in a Laguerre history consisting of
p
steps, each East step contributes one edge, each North–East step contributes no edge, and each South–East step contributes two edges. Since the Motzkin path starts and ends at the same height, the total number of edges in the corresponding bipartite matching is exactly
p
. Moreover, in the construction of a bipartite matching from a Laguerre history, the height of a step encodes the number of isolated vertices. More precisely, when a step begins at height
k
, there are
\alpha+k
isolated vertices on the top row and
k
isolated vertices on the bottom row; see Figure
5
.

To establish (
2.26
), it remains to compute the total contribution of the crossings in the associated bipartite matching.
We perform this counting in a dynamical manner.
Recall that the weight of a Motzkin path is given by the product of the weights of its individual steps: an East step and a South–East step at height
k
carry weights
b_{k}
and
\lambda_{k}
, respectively, while a North–East step has weight
1
.
Here
b_{k}
and
\lambda_{k}
are defined in (
2.25
).
Thus, in this dynamical viewpoint, it suffices to verify that the contribution of the crossing statistic is exactly
b_{k}
for each East step at height
k
, equals
\lambda_{k}
for each South–East step, and is
1
for each North–East step.

We begin by analysing the contribution of an East step.

•

(East step at height
k
with label
0
) In this case, the newly added vertical edge creates

–

\alpha+k
crossings with isolated vertices in the top row, and

–

k
crossings with isolated vertices in the bottom row.

Note that in this case the new edge cannot create any crossings with previously existing edges.

•

(East step at height
k
with label
m
and
1\leq m\leq k+\alpha
) In this case, note that any potential crossing between the newly added edge and an already existing edge has, in fact, been accounted for at the moment the corresponding vertex ceased to be isolated.
More precisely, when a vertex was isolated at some earlier step but later became matched, the crossing between that vertex and the infinity vertex was already counted; this corresponds exactly to the crossing that would otherwise appear here.
Thus, only the crossings involving isolated vertices need to be considered.
Consequently, the newly added edge creates

–

m-1
crossings with isolated vertices in the top row, and

–

k
crossings with isolated vertices in the bottom row.

•

(East step at height
k
with label
\widetilde{l}
and

–

\alpha+k

–

l-1
crossings with isolated vertices in the bottom row.

Combining the above cases, one sees that an East step of height

to our statistic.

Next, we consider the contribution of a South–East step. If we advance with a South–East step of height

and

\sum_{m=1}^{\alpha+k}\sum_{l=1}^{k}q^{\alpha+2k+m+l-3}=q^{\alpha+2k-1}[\alpha+k]_{q}[k]_{q}=\lambda_{k}.

Since North–East steps and the addition of isolated vertices do not contribute to the statistic, this completes the proof.

\sum_{M\in M_{t-l,0}^{(0)}(t-l)}q^{\mathrm{cr}(M)}=[t-l]_{q}!.

(Step 1)
We add one vertex at the leftmost position of the top row and one vertex in the bottom row, and then introduce an edge connecting these two vertices.
Since there are
t-l
existing vertices in the bottom row, there are
t-l+1
possible positions for the newly added bottom vertex.
If the added vertex in
B_{+}
is the
n
-th vertex from the left in the bottom row, then the added edge contributes
n-1
to the crossing number.
Consequently, summing over all admissible choices, the contribution of the first added edge to our statistic is
[t-l+1]_{q}
.

To add the second edge, we again insert a new leftmost vertex in the top row and a new vertex in the bottom row.
Now there are
t-l+2
possible positions for the newly added bottom vertex, and by the same reasoning, the admissible choices of the second edge contribute
[t-l+2]_{q}
.
Repeating this process
i
times, this step produces the factor

(2.34)

\frac{[t-l+i]_{q}!}{[t-l]_{q}!}.

(Step 2)
Using the same argument as in the previous step, we proceed as follows.
Since
i
edges were added, each newly added edge in the present step creates
i
additional crossings, contributing a factor of
q^{i}
.
As this operation is performed
l-i
times, the total contribution from crossings is
q^{i(l-i)}
.

Moreover, as in
(Step 1)
, the admissible positions of the added vertices in the bottom row contribute the factor

(2.35)

q^{i(l-i)}\frac{[t-i]_{q}!}{[t-l]_{q}!}.

(Step 3)

Since there are
t-i
existing vertices in
T_{+}
, we add
p-(t-i)
isolated vertices inductively.
By the definition of the crossing number, the contribution of a newly added vertex to the crossing count equals the number of vertices incident to edges that are positioned to the right of the newly added one.
Hence, by the standard combinatorial interpretation of
q
-binomial coefficients, this procedure contributes the factor

{\begin{bmatrix}(t-i)+(p-t+i)\\
t-i\end{bmatrix}}_{q}={\begin{bmatrix}p\\
t-i\end{bmatrix}}_{q}.

Similarly, we add
p-(t-l+i)
isolated vertices in
B_{+}
. By the same reasoning, this step contributes the factor

{\begin{bmatrix}p\\
t-l+i\end{bmatrix}}_{q}.

Therefore, the total contribution of this step is

(2.36)

{\begin{bmatrix}p\\
t-i\end{bmatrix}}_{q}{\begin{bmatrix}p\\
t-l+i\end{bmatrix}}_{q}.

(Step 4)

Since there are
i
existing vertices in
T_{-}
, we add
\alpha+j-i
isolated vertices in
T_{-}
. Each of these newly added vertices creates crossings with the
t-i
edges incident to vertices in
T_{+}
.
Moreover, there are already
i
connected vertices in
T_{-}
.
By the same reasoning as in
(Step 3)
, this yields the factor

q^{(t-i)(\alpha+j-i)}{\begin{bmatrix}i+(\alpha+j-i)\\
i\end{bmatrix}}_{q}=q^{(t-i)(\alpha+j-i)}{\begin{bmatrix}\alpha+j\\
i\end{bmatrix}}_{q}.

Similarly, since there are
l-i
existing vertices in
B_{-}
, we add
j-(l-i)
isolated vertices in
B_{-}
. These vertices create crossings with the
t-l+i
edges incident to vertices in
B_{+}
.
Therefore, this contributes the factor

q^{(t-l+i)(j-l+i)}{\begin{bmatrix}(l-i)+(j-l+i)\\
j-l+i\end{bmatrix}}_{q}=q^{(t-l+i)(j-l+i)}{\begin{bmatrix}j\\
l-i\end{bmatrix}}_{q}.

Combining these, the total contribution of this step is

(2.37)

q^{(t-i)(\alpha+j-i)+(t-l+i)(j-l+i)}{\begin{bmatrix}\alpha+j\\
i\end{bmatrix}}_{q}{\begin{bmatrix}j\\
l-i\end{bmatrix}}_{q}.

Now by combining all of (
2.33
), (
2.34
), (
2.35
), (
2.36
) and (
2.37
), we obtain the desired result (
2.32
).
∎

In this subsection, we show Theorem

Recall that

{m}_{N,p}^{(\mathrm{qL})}\Big|_{\alpha=cN+d}=(1-q)^{p}[p]_{q}!\sum_{i=0}^{p}q^{-(p-i)i}{\begin{bmatrix}p\\

We now analyse the large-

Lemma 2.7
.

Let

q^{-(p-i)i}(1-q)^{p}[p]_{q}!{\begin{bmatrix}p\\

Also by the definition of

We now examine the asymptotic behaviour of the term

Lemma 2.8

\begin{split}\prod_{m=1}^{i}(1-q^{(c+t)N+d-i+m})&=(1-\mathsf{s}^{c+t})^{i}\prod_{m=1}^{i}\Big(1+\frac{\mathsf{s}^{c+t}}{1-\mathsf{s}^{c+t}}(d-i+m)\frac{\lambda}{N}+O(N^{-2})\Big)\\

{\begin{bmatrix}(c+t)N+d\\

Now, by integrating both sides with respect to

from

3.1.
Proof of Theorem
1.2
(A)

By Theorem
1.1
(B), this part follows from a standard argument in random matrix theory, which derives the limiting spectral distribution from the large-
N
behaviour of the moments.
We denote by

(3.1)

g^{(c)}(y):=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\rho^{(c)}(x)}{y-x}\,dx,\qquad y\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}

the Cauchy transform of the limiting density
\rho^{(c)}
. Then by Theorem
1.1
(B), we have

(3.2)

\displaystyle\begin{split}g^{(c)}(y)&=\frac{1}{y}\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{y^{p}}\int_{0}^{1}x^{p}\rho^{(c)}(x)\,dx=\frac{1}{y}+\frac{1}{y}\sum_{p=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{y^{p}}\int_{0}^{1}\mathsf{s}^{pu}\sum_{i=0}^{p}(1-\mathsf{s}^{c+u})^{i}(\mathsf{s}^{c}(1-\mathsf{s}^{u}))^{p-i}\binom{p}{i}^{2}\,du\\
&=\frac{1}{\lambda y}\int_{\mathsf{s}}^{1}\frac{1}{t}\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}\sum_{i=0}^{p}\binom{p}{i}^{2}\Big(\frac{1-\mathsf{s}^{c}t}{\mathsf{s}^{c}(1-t)}\Big)^{i}\Big(\frac{t(1-t)\mathsf{s}^{c}}{y}\Big)^{p}\,dt\end{split}

as
y\to\infty.
Here, we used the change of variable
\mathsf{s}^{u}=t
in the last equality.
To simplify the double sum in the integrand, we first note that

(3.3)

\sum_{i=0}^{p}\binom{p}{i}z^{i}=(1-z)^{p}P_{p}\Big(\frac{1+z}{1-z}\Big),

where
P_{n}
is the Legendre polynomial. Then by the well-known generating function of the Legendre polynomial (see e.g.
[
44
, Eq. (9.8.68)]
), it follows that

(3.4)

\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}\sum_{i=0}^{p}\binom{p}{i}^{2}z^{i}w^{p}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-2w(1+z)+w^{2}(1-z)^{2}}},\qquad\textup{for }\lvert w\rvert<\min{\Big|{\frac{1+z\pm 2\sqrt{z}}{(1-z)^{2}}}}\Big|.

Using this identity and substituting
z
and
w
accordingly, we obtain

(3.5)

g^{(c)}(y)=\frac{1}{\lambda}\int_{\mathsf{s}}^{1}\frac{1}{t}\frac{dt}{\sqrt{y^{2}-2(t+\mathsf{s}^{c}t-2\mathsf{s}^{c}t^{2})y+t^{2}(1-\mathsf{s}^{c})^{2}}}.

Notice here that the condition for
w
in (
3.4
) is satisfied for

(3.6)

y\geq\begin{cases}b&\textup{if }\lambda\leq\lambda_{c},\\
1&\textup{if }\lambda>\lambda_{c},\end{cases}

where
b
is given by (
1.3
).
Moreover, note that for a fixed
0<x<1
, the inequality

(3.7)

x^{2}-2(t+\mathsf{s}^{c}t-2\mathsf{s}^{c}t^{2})x+t^{2}(1-\mathsf{s}^{c})^{2}<0

is satisfied for

(3.8)

t\in(\max\{x_{0}-x_{1},\mathsf{s}\},x_{0}+x_{1}),

where

(3.9)

x_{0}=\frac{x(1+\mathsf{s}^{c})}{(1-\mathsf{s}^{c})^{2}+4\mathsf{s}^{c}x},\qquad x_{1}=\frac{2x\sqrt{\mathsf{s}^{c}(1-x)}}{(1-\mathsf{s}^{c})^{2}+4\mathsf{s}^{c}x}.

Note that
x_{0}\pm x_{1}
are defined as the roots of the LHS of (
3.7
).
Then by the Sokhotski-Plemelj formula, we have

(3.10)

\begin{split}\rho^{(c)}(x)&=\frac{1}{\pi}\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\mathrm{Im}\,g^{(c)}(x-i\varepsilon)=\frac{1}{\pi\lambda}\int_{\max\{x_{0}-x_{1},\mathsf{s}\}}^{x_{0}+x_{1}}\frac{1}{t}\frac{dt}{\sqrt{-x^{2}+2(t+\mathsf{s}^{c}t-2\mathsf{s}^{c}t^{2})x-t^{2}(1-\mathsf{s}^{c})^{2}}}\\
&=\frac{1}{\pi\lambda\sqrt{(1-\mathsf{s}^{c})^{2}+4\mathsf{s}^{c}x}}\int_{\max\{x_{0}-x_{1},\mathsf{s}\}}^{x_{0}+x_{1}}\frac{1}{t}\frac{dt}{\sqrt{(x_{0}+x_{1}-t)(t-(x_{0}-x_{1}))}}.\end{split}

Then it follows that

(3.11)

\begin{split}\rho^{(c)}(x)&=\frac{1}{\pi\lambda\sqrt{(1-\mathsf{s}^{c})^{2}+4\mathsf{s}^{c}x}}\int_{\mathsf{s}}^{x_{0}+x_{1}}\frac{1}{t}\frac{dt}{\sqrt{(x_{0}+x_{1}-t)(t-(x_{0}-x_{1}))}}\mathbbm{1}_{(a,b)}(x)\\
&\quad+\frac{1}{\pi\lambda\sqrt{(1-\mathsf{s}^{c})^{2}+4\mathsf{s}^{c}x}}\begin{cases}0&\textup{if }\lambda\leq\lambda_{c},\\
\displaystyle\int_{{x_{0}-x_{1}}}^{x_{0}+x_{1}}\frac{1}{t}\frac{dt}{\sqrt{(x_{0}+x_{1}-t)(t-(x_{0}-x_{1}))}}\mathbbm{1}_{(b,1)}(x)&\textup{if }\lambda>\lambda_{c}.\end{cases}\end{split}

We now simplify the integrals in this expression.

First, note that if
e^{-\lambda}<x_{0}-x_{1}
, the integral evaluation

\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}\frac{1}{t}\frac{dt}{\sqrt{(t-\alpha)(\beta-t)}}=\pi\lvert\alpha\beta\rvert^{-1/2}

gives rise to

(3.12)

\frac{1}{\pi\lambda\sqrt{(1-\mathsf{s}^{c})^{2}+4\mathsf{s}^{c}x}}\int_{x_{0}-x_{1}}^{x_{0}+x_{1}}\frac{1}{t}\frac{dt}{\sqrt{(x_{0}+x_{1}-t)(t-(x_{0}-x_{1}))}}=\frac{1}{\lambda x}.

On the other hand, for
x
satisfying
x_{0}-x_{1}<\mathsf{s}
, we make use of the integral evaluation

\int_{\alpha}^{\gamma}\frac{1}{t}\frac{dt}{\sqrt{(t-\alpha)(\beta-t)}}=\int_{0}^{\theta_{\gamma}}\frac{2d\theta}{({\alpha+\beta})+({\beta-\alpha})\cos\theta},\quad\alpha<\gamma<\beta

where
\cos\theta_{\gamma}=(2\gamma-\alpha-\beta)/(\beta-\alpha)
, and obtain

(3.13)

\frac{1}{\pi\lambda\sqrt{(1-\mathsf{s}^{c})^{2}+4\mathsf{s}^{c}x}}\int_{\mathsf{s}}^{x_{0}+x_{1}}\frac{1}{t}\frac{dt}{\sqrt{(x_{0}+x_{1}-t)(t-(x_{0}-x_{1}))}}=\frac{2}{\pi\lambda x}\arctan\sqrt{\frac{x_{0}-x_{1}}{x_{0}+x_{1}}\frac{{x_{0}+x_{1}-\mathsf{s}}}{{\mathsf{s}-x_{0}+x_{1}}}}.

Combining the above, it follows that

(3.14)

\rho^{(c)}(x)=\frac{2}{\pi\lambda x}\arctan\sqrt{\frac{x_{0}-x_{1}}{x_{0}+x_{1}}\frac{{x_{0}+x_{1}-\mathsf{s}}}{{\mathsf{s}-x_{0}+x_{1}}}}\mathbbm{1}_{(a,b)}(x)+\begin{cases}0&\textup{if }\lambda\leq\lambda_{c},\\
\displaystyle\frac{1}{\lambda x}\mathbbm{1}_{(b,1)}(x)&\textup{if }\lambda>\lambda_{c},\end{cases}

Furthermore, by (
3.9
), we have the expression (
1.4
).
On the other hand, by (
1.3
), we have

\displaystyle\sqrt{ab}=\mathsf{s}-\mathsf{s}^{c},\qquad\sqrt{(1-a)(1-b)}=|1-\mathsf{s}-\mathsf{s}^{1+c}|.

Then by straightforward computations, one can show that (
3.14
) coincides with (
1.5
).
Since
\rho^{(c)}
is non-negative and integrates to one, it defines a probability density and can therefore be integrated against any continuous test function. This shows the desired convergence.
∎

3.2.
Proof of Theorem
1.2
(B)

In this subsection, we prove Theorem
1.2
(B).
By the general theory of large deviation principles
[
25
,
43
]
, the main task is to show that the minimiser of the functional
I[\mu]
in
(
1.37
) is given by (
1.5
).
For this, we solve the associated Euler–Lagrange variational conditions explicitly.

To identify the rate function
I[\mu]
, we begin with the Hamiltonian in (
1.20
) and analyse its large-
N
behaviour, which naturally suggests a candidate for the rate function.
For this purpose, we require the asymptotic behaviour of the weight function.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
q=e^{-\lambda/N}
with
\lambda\geq 0
. Let
\alpha
be scaled according to (
1.9
). Then for
0<x<1
, the weight function (
1.18
) satisfies

(3.15)

\log w^{(\mathrm{qL})}(x;q)=-N\Big(\frac{1}{\lambda}\operatorname{Li}_{2}(x)-c\log x\Big)+O(1),

as
N\to\infty.
Here,
\operatorname{Li}_{2}(x)
is given by (
1.38
).

Proof.

This follows immediately from the asymptotic behaviour of the
q
-Pochhammer symbol established in
[
55
, Theorem 4]
; see also
[
37
, Lemma 4.1]
.
∎

By Lemma
3.1
, we formally obtain the asymptotic of the joint probability distribution function (
1.20
):

(3.16)

\prod_{1\leq j<k\leq N}(x_{j}-x_{k})^{2}\prod_{j=1}^{N}\omega^{\rm(qL)}(x_{j})\approx\exp\Big(-N^{2}I[\mu]\Big),

where
I[\mu]
is given by (
1.37
). Here, the
N^{2}
terms is coherent with the speed of convergence claimed in Theorem
1.2
(B).

A standard approach to identifying the unique minimiser of an energy
functional of the form
I[\mu]
is via the Euler–Lagrange variational
conditions. In the present setting, the corresponding Euler–Lagrange conditions are formulated as follows;
see
[
4
, Section 2.1.4]
.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
\mu(dx)=\rho^{(c)}(x)\,dx
, where
\rho^{(c)}
is given by (
1.5
). Define

(3.17)

d=\begin{cases}b&\textup{if }\lambda<\lambda_{c},\\
1&\textup{if }\lambda>\lambda_{c}.\end{cases}

Then there exists an
\Omega\in\mathbb{R}
such that the following Euler-Lagrange variational conditions hold:

(3.18)

-2\int_{a}^{d}\log(|x-y|)\,\mu(dy)+\frac{1}{\lambda}\operatorname{Li}_{2}(x)-c\log(x)\begin{cases}=-\Omega&\textup{if }x\in(a,b),\\
\geq-\Omega&\textup{if }x\in(0,a)\cup(d,1),\\
\leq-\Omega&\textup{if }x\in(b,d).\end{cases}

Although it is sufficient to verify this proposition directly, it is more
instructive to illustrate how one can construct a probability measure
satisfying (
3.18
) in a setting where the solution is not known
a priori
.

For this purpose, we first recall some standard definitions from
[
4
]
.
Let
\mu(dx)\in\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(0,1)
. For any interval

I\subseteq(0,1)
, we use the following terminology:

•

I
is called a
void
if
\mu(I)\equiv 0
;

•

I
is called a
saturated region
if the upper constraint
\frac{1}{\lambda x}
is active for all
x\in I
, i.e.
\mu(I)=\int_{I}\frac{1}{\lambda x}dx
;

•

I
is called a
band
if it is neither a void nor a saturated region.

To construct a probability measure
\mu(dx)
satisfying
(
3.18
), one needs to assume a basic structural form of the measure. To be more precise, we suppose that there exist parameters

0\leq a<b\leq d\leq 1
such that
(0,a)
and
(d,1)
are voids;
(a,b)
is a band; and
(b,d)
is a saturated region.
Furthermore, we assume that
\mu(dx)
is absolutely continuous with respect
to the Lebesgue measure, so that there exists a density
\mu(x)
satisfying

\mu(dx)=\mu(x)\,dx
.

We note that such assumptions are far from obvious in general. However, in the present setting we already know the solution—albeit temporarily setting
this knowledge aside—from complementary approaches. This allows us to
verify
a posteriori
that these assumptions are natural, in view of the
explicit form (
1.5
).

Proof of Proposition
3.2
; Euler-Lagrange equality.

We first prove the equality part in (
3.18
). Let
x\in(a,b).

By differentiating (
3.18
) with respect to
x
, it follows from (
1.38
) that

(3.19)

2\operatorname{p.v.}\int_{a}^{d}\frac{\mu(y)}{x-y}\,dy+\frac{\log(1-x)}{\lambda x}+\frac{c}{x}=0,\qquad x\in(a,b),

where
\operatorname{p.v.}
is the Cauchy principal value.

We consider the Cauchy transform

(3.20)

g(z):=\int_{a}^{d}\frac{\mu(y)}{z-y}\,dy

of the equilibrium measure
\mu
. Under the assumed structure of the support of
\mu(dx)
, the Sokhotski–Plemelj theorem implies that
g(z)
satisfies the following Riemann–Hilbert problem (RHP).

RHP 3.1
.

The function
g(z)
satisfies the following conditions:

(1)

g(z)
is analytic for
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus(a,d)
;

(2)

\displaystyle g_{+}(x)-g_{-}(x)=-2\pi i\mu(x)
for all
x\in(a,b)
;

(3)

\displaystyle g_{+}(x)-g_{-}(x)=-\frac{2\pi i}{\lambda x}
for all
x\in(b,d)
;

(4)

\displaystyle g_{+}(x)+g_{-}(x)=-\frac{\log(1-x)}{\lambda x}-\frac{c}{x}
for all
x\in(a,b)
;

(5)

\displaystyle g(z)\sim\frac{1}{z}+\frac{\mathsf{M}_{1}}{z^{2}}+O(z^{-3})
as
z\to\infty
, where
\mathsf{M}_{1}:=\int x\mu(x)\,dx
.

Here,
g_{\pm}(x)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}g(x\pm i\varepsilon)
. Here, the last condition follows from

\displaystyle g(z)=\frac{1}{z}\int_{a}^{d}\frac{\mu(y)}{1-y/z}\,dy=\frac{1}{z}\int_{a}^{d}\Big(1+\frac{y}{z}+O(z^{-2})\Big)\mu(y)\,dy,

as
z\to\infty.
At this stage, we do not specify
\mathsf{M}_{1}
; however, we will later make essential use of the fact that
\mathsf{M}_{1}=\mathcal{M}_{1}^{(\mathrm{qL})}
, where
\mathcal{M}_{1}^{(\mathrm{qL})}
is given in
(
1.29
). This is precisely where the alternative approach based on the moment method plays a complementary role within the large deviation framework.

To solve
RHP
3.1
, we introduce the transformation

(3.21)

\varphi(z):=\frac{g(z)}{R(z)},\qquad R(z):=(z-a)^{\frac{1}{2}}(z-b)^{\frac{1}{2}},

where the square roots are taken with canonical branch cuts.
Note that

(3.22)

R_{+}(x)=\begin{cases}R_{-}(x)&\textup{if }x<a\textup{ or }x>b,\\
-R_{-}(x)&\textup{if }a<x<b.\end{cases}

The function

\varphi(z)
then satisfies the following Riemann–Hilbert problem.

RHP 3.2
.

The function
\varphi(z)
satisfies the following conditions:

(1)

\varphi(z)
is analytic for
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus(a,d)
;

(2)

\displaystyle\varphi_{+}(x)-\varphi_{-}(x)=-\frac{2\pi i}{\lambda xR(x)}
for all
x\in(b,d)
;

(3)

\displaystyle\varphi_{+}(x)-\varphi_{-}(x)=-\frac{\log(1-x)}{\lambda xR_{+}(x)}-\frac{c}{xR_{+}(x)}
for all
x\in(a,b)
;

(4)

\displaystyle\varphi(z)\sim\frac{1}{z^{2}}+\frac{\mathsf{M}_{1}+\frac{1}{2}(a+b)}{z^{3}}+O(z^{-4})
as
z\to\infty
.

Now, by using the second and the third condition in
RHP
3.2
and the Sokhotski–Plemelj formula, we have

(3.23)

\varphi(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{a}^{b}\Big(\frac{\log(1-s)}{\lambda sR_{+}(s)}+\frac{c}{sR_{+}(s)}\Big)\,\frac{ds}{z-s}+\int_{b}^{d}\frac{1}{\lambda R(s)s}\,\frac{ds}{z-s}.

By expanding (
3.23
) as
z\to\infty
we obtain the asymptotic behaviour

(3.24)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\varphi(z)&=\bigg(\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{a}^{b}\Big(\frac{\log(1-s)}{\lambda sR_{+}(s)}+\frac{c}{sR_{+}(s)}\Big)\,ds+\int_{b}^{d}\frac{1}{\lambda sR(s)}\,ds\bigg)\frac{1}{z}\\
&\quad+\bigg(\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{a}^{b}\Big(\frac{\log(1-s)}{\lambda R_{+}(s)}+\frac{c}{R_{+}(s)}\Big)\,ds+\int_{b}^{d}\frac{1}{\lambda R(s)}\,ds\bigg)\frac{1}{z^{2}}\\
&\quad+\bigg(\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{a}^{b}\Big(\frac{s\log(1-s)}{\lambda R_{+}(s)}+\frac{cs}{R_{+}(s)}\Big)\,ds+\int_{b}^{d}\frac{s}{\lambda R(s)}\,ds\bigg)\frac{1}{z^{3}}+O(z^{-4})\,.\end{split}

We now match the coefficients in (
3.24
) with the final condition in
RHP
3.2
. For this purpose, the integrals appearing in (
3.24
) are evaluated separately in Propositions
A.1
,
A.2
and
A.3
below. As a result, we deduce that the parameters

a,b,d
must satisfy the following nonlinear system of equations.

(3.25)

\displaystyle-\frac{c}{2}-\frac{1}{\lambda}\log\Big(\frac{\sqrt{(1-a)b}+\sqrt{(1-b)a}}{\sqrt{b}+\sqrt{a}}\Big)+\frac{1}{\lambda}\log\Big(\frac{\sqrt{b(d-a)}+\sqrt{a(d-b)}}{\sqrt{b(d-a)}-\sqrt{a(d-b)}}\Big)=0,

(3.26)

\displaystyle-\frac{c}{2}-\frac{1}{\lambda}\log\Big(\frac{\sqrt{1-a}+\sqrt{1-b}}{2}\Big)+\frac{2}{\lambda}\log\Big(\frac{\sqrt{d-a}+\sqrt{d-b}}{\sqrt{b-a}}\Big)=1,

(3.27)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&-\Big(c+\frac{1}{\lambda}+2\Big)\frac{a+b}{4}+\frac{1}{2\lambda}\bigg(1-\sqrt{(1-b)(1-a)}-(a+b)\log\Big(\frac{\sqrt{1-a}+\sqrt{1-b}}{2}\Big)\bigg)\\
&\quad+\frac{1}{\lambda}\bigg(\sqrt{(d-a)(d-b)}+(a+b)\log\Big(\frac{\sqrt{d-a}+\sqrt{d-b}}{\sqrt{b-a}}\Big)\bigg)=\mathsf{M}_{1}.\end{split}

Up to this point, we have not used any a priori information about
\mu(x)
except its structure of the support. To solve the resulting system of equations, however, we must specify
\mathsf{M}_{1}=\mathcal{M}_{1}^{(\mathrm{qL})}
as given in (
1.29
). It then follows from lengthy but straightforward computations that the parameters
a,b
, and
d
, defined in (
1.3
) and (
3.17
), indeed satisfy (
3.25
), (
3.26
), and (
3.27
).

Next, we compute
\mu(x)\,dx
using the second condition in
RHP
3.1
. By definition, we have

(3.28)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mu(x)&=-\frac{1}{2\pi i}(R_{+}(x)\varphi_{+}(x)-R_{-}(x)\varphi_{-}(x))\\
&=-\frac{R_{+}(x)}{2\pi i}(\varphi_{+}(x)+\varphi_{-}(x))\mathbbm{1}_{(a,b)}(x)-\frac{R(x)}{2\pi i}(\varphi_{+}(x)-\varphi_{-}(x))\mathbbm{1}_{(b,d)}(x)\,.\end{split}

We first consider the case
\lambda\leq\lambda_{c}
. Recall from (
3.17
) that, in the present case, we have
d=b
.
By evaluating the integrals in (
3.23
) using Propositions
A.1
and
A.3
, we obtain that, for
z\notin(a,b)
,

\displaystyle\varphi(z)

\displaystyle=-\frac{c}{2z\sqrt{ab}}-\frac{c}{2zR(z)}-\frac{\log(1-z)}{2\lambda zR(z)}+\frac{1}{2z\lambda R(z)}\log\bigg(\frac{\sqrt{z-b}+\sqrt{z-a}}{\sqrt{z-b}-\sqrt{z-a}}\bigg)

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{1}{2z\lambda R(z)}\log\bigg(\frac{\sqrt{(1-a)(z-b)}+\sqrt{(1-b)(z-a)}}{\sqrt{(1-a)(z-b)}-\sqrt{(1-b)(z-a)}}\bigg)-\frac{1}{z\lambda\sqrt{ab}}\log\bigg(\frac{\sqrt{b(1-a)}+\sqrt{a(1-b)}}{\sqrt{b}+\sqrt{a}}\bigg)\,.

Then, by taking the plus and minus boundary values of this expression,
and using both (
3.25
) and the identity
[
60
, Eq. (4.23.26)]

(3.29)

\arctan(z)=\frac{i}{2}\log\Big(\frac{i+z}{i-z}\Big),\qquad iz\not\in(-\infty,-1)\cup(1,+\infty)\,,

we deduce that for
x\in(a,b)
,

(3.30)

\begin{split}\varphi_{+}(x)+\varphi_{-}(x)=-\frac{2i}{x\lambda R_{+}(x)}\bigg(\arctan\bigg(\sqrt{\frac{1-a}{1-b}}\sqrt{\frac{b-x}{x-a}}\bigg)-\arctan\bigg(\sqrt{\frac{b-x}{x-a}}\bigg)\bigg)\,.\end{split}

Therefore, we obtain that for
x\in(a,b)
,

\displaystyle\mu(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{x\lambda\pi}\bigg(\arctan\bigg(\sqrt{\frac{1-a}{1-b}}\sqrt{\frac{b-x}{x-a}}\bigg)-\arctan\bigg(\sqrt{\frac{b-x}{x-a}}\bigg)\bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{x\lambda\pi}\arctan\bigg(\frac{(\sqrt{1-a}-\sqrt{1-b})\sqrt{(b-x)(x-a)}}{\sqrt{1-b}(x-a)+\sqrt{1-a}(b-x)}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{x\lambda\pi}\arctan\bigg(\frac{(\sqrt{1-a}-\sqrt{1-b})\sqrt{(b-x)(x-a)}}{\sqrt{1-b}(x-a)+\sqrt{1-a}(b-x)+(b-a)\sqrt{1-x}}\bigg),

where we have used the addition formula and the double-angle formula
for the arctangent function; see e.g.
[
60
, Eq. (4.24.15)]
. This shows that for
\lambda\leq\lambda_{c}
, the density of
\mu(x)
is given by (
1.5
).

We now consider the case
\lambda>\lambda_{c}
.
It is straightforward to see that
\mu(x)=1/(\lambda x)
for
x\in(b,1)
.
On the other hand, for
x\in(a,b)
, by calculations analogous to those above, one can show that

\displaystyle\mu(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{x\lambda\pi}\bigg(\arctan\bigg(\sqrt{\frac{1-a}{1-b}}\sqrt{\frac{b-x}{x-a}}\bigg)-\arctan\bigg(\sqrt{\frac{b-x}{x-a}}\bigg)+2\arctan\bigg(\sqrt{\frac{(x-a)(1-b)}{(b-x)(1-a)}}\bigg)\bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{x\lambda\pi}\bigg(\arctan\bigg(\frac{(\sqrt{1-a}-\sqrt{1-b})\sqrt{(b-x)(x-a)}}{\sqrt{1-b}(x-a)+\sqrt{1-a}(b-x)+(b-a)\sqrt{1-x}}\bigg)+\arctan\bigg(\sqrt{\frac{(1-b)(x-a)}{(1-a)(b-x)}}\bigg)\bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{x\lambda\pi}\arctan\bigg(\sqrt{\frac{x-a}{b-x}}\frac{\sqrt{1-x}+\sqrt{1-b}}{\sqrt{1-x}+\sqrt{1-a}}\bigg).

This again shows that for
\lambda>\lambda_{c}
, the density of
\mu(x)
is given by (
1.5
).

We have shown that, for
x\in(a,b)
, the derivative of the LHS of (
3.18
) with respect to
x
vanishes. Therefore, the equality part of (
3.18
) follows.
∎

Next we show the inequalities in (
3.18
).

Proof of Proposition
3.2
; Euler-Lagrange inequalities.

Let

(3.31)

h(x)=g_{+}(x)+g_{-}(x)+\frac{c}{x}+\frac{\log(1-x)}{\lambda x},

where
g(x)
is given by (
3.20
). Note that, by
RHP
3.1
, we have
h(x)=0
for
x\in(a,b)
.

We first consider the case
\lambda>\lambda_{c}
; thus
d=1
.
We claim that

(3.32)

h(x)\begin{cases}>0&\textup{if }x\in(0,a),\\
<0&\textup{if }x\in(b,1).\end{cases}

Then the desired conclusion follows by integration.

By residue calculations analogous to those above and (
3.26
), it follows that, for
x\in(0,a)\cup(b,1)
,

(3.33)

h(x)=-R(x)\bigg(\frac{c}{x\sqrt{ab}}+\frac{1}{\lambda}\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{sR(s)(s-x)}\,ds\bigg)=-\frac{R(x)}{x}\Big(\frac{c}{\sqrt{ab}}-\frac{J(0)}{\lambda}+\frac{J(x)}{\lambda}\Big),

where

(3.34)

J(x):=\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(s-x)R(s)}\,ds\,.

By standard integration techniques, such as Euler substitutions, one can evaluate
J(x)
as

(3.35)

J(x)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{2}{\sqrt{(a-x)(b-x)}}\log\bigg(\frac{(\sqrt{b-x}+\sqrt{a-x})\sqrt{1-x}}{\sqrt{b-x}\sqrt{1-a}+\sqrt{a-x}\sqrt{1-b}}\bigg)&\textup{if }0\leq x<a,\\
\displaystyle-\frac{2}{\sqrt{(x-a)(x-b)}}\log\bigg(\frac{(\sqrt{x-b}+\sqrt{x-a})\sqrt{1-x}}{\sqrt{x-b}\sqrt{1-a}+\sqrt{x-a}\sqrt{1-b}}\bigg)&\textup{if }b<x<1.\end{cases}

Therefore,
J(x)>0
for
x\in(0,a)
, whereas
J(x)<0
for
x\in(b,1)
. Furthermore, by (
3.25
) we have
\frac{c}{\sqrt{ab}}-\frac{J(0)}{\lambda}=0
. Therefore we conclude that (
3.32
) holds.

Next, we consider the case
\lambda\leq\lambda_{c}
, thus
d=b
. In this case, it suffices to show that

(3.36)

h(x)>0\quad\textup{if }x\in(0,a)\cup(b,1).

We notice that in this case, one can compute
h(x)
as

(3.37)

h(x)=-\frac{R(x)}{x}\left(\frac{c}{\sqrt{ab}}-\frac{J(0)}{\lambda}-\frac{2I(0)}{\lambda}+\frac{J(x)}{\lambda}+\frac{2I(x)}{\lambda}\right)\,,

where
J(x)
is given by (
3.34
) and

(3.38)

I(x):=\int_{b}^{1}\frac{1}{(s-x)R(s)}\,ds.

Similar to the above,
I(x)
is evaluated as

(3.39)

I(x)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{(b-x)(a-x)}}\log\bigg(\frac{\sqrt{b-x}\sqrt{1-a}+\sqrt{a-x}\sqrt{1-b}}{\sqrt{b-x}\sqrt{1-a}-\sqrt{a-x}\sqrt{1-b}}\bigg)&\textup{if }0<x<a,\\
\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\sqrt{(x-b)(x-a)}}\log\bigg(\frac{\sqrt{x-b}\sqrt{1-a}+\sqrt{x-a}\sqrt{1-b}}{\sqrt{x-a}\sqrt{1-b}-\sqrt{x-b}\sqrt{1-a}}\bigg)&\textup{if }b<x<1.\end{cases}

Notice that by (
3.25
), we have
c-\frac{J(0)}{\lambda}-\frac{2I(0)}{\lambda}=0
. Moreover, since
I(x)>0
for
x\in(0,a)\cup(b,1)
, we obtain (
3.36
). This completes the proof.
∎

1.2
(B).

The first assertion follows from the general theory developed in
[
43
, Theorem 1.1]
and
[
25
, Theorem 1.7]
, together with Lemma
3.1
. In particular, by considering the special case
\eta=\theta=1
and
\beta=2
in
[
43
, Theorem 1.1]
and then applying Varadhan’s lemma
[

]

The second assertion follows directly from Proposition

3.3.
Proof of Theorem
1.2
(C)

In this subsection, we study the limiting zero distribution of the little
q
-Laguerre polynomials as a third approach to the
q
-deformed Marchenko-Pastur law (
1.5
), and thereby establish Theorem
1.2
(C).
As mentioned earlier, our analysis follows the general method introduced by Kuijlaars and Van Assche
[
45
]
; see also the earlier work
[
64
]
.
To apply this method, we consider the double-indexed
normalised
little
q
-Laguerre polynomials
P_{n,N}(x)
defined by

(3.40)

P_{n,N}(x)=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{h_{n}}}p_{n}(x|e^{-\frac{\lambda(cN+d)}{N}};e^{-\frac{\lambda}{N}}).

Here,
h_{n}
is given by (
2.20
), and we use the scalings
q=e^{-\lambda/N}
and
\alpha=cN+d
.
Then, by the three-term recurrence relation (
1.30
) for
p_{n}
, we have

(3.41)

xP_{n,N}(x)=a_{n+1,N}P_{n+1,N}(x)+b_{n,N}P_{n,N}(x)+a_{n,N}P_{n-1,N}(x),

where the recurrence coefficients are given by

(3.42)

\begin{split}a_{n,N}&=e^{-\frac{\lambda n}{N}}\sqrt{e^{-\frac{\lambda(cN+(d-1))}{N}}(1-e^{-\frac{\lambda n}{N}})(1-e^{-\frac{\lambda(n+cN+d)}{N}})},\\
b_{n,N}&=e^{-\frac{\lambda n}{N}}(1-e^{-\frac{\lambda(n+cN+d+1)}{N}})+e^{-\frac{\lambda(n+cN+d)}{N}}(1-e^{-\frac{\lambda n}{N}}).\end{split}

For
t>0
, we write
\lim_{n/N\to t}X_{n,N}=X
if
\lim_{j\to\infty}X_{n_{j},N_{j}}=X
for any sequences
\{n_{j}\}
and
\{N_{j}\}
such that
N_{j}\to\infty
and
n_{j}/N_{j}\to t
as
j\to\infty
.
Note that by definition, we have

(3.43)

\begin{split}A(t):=\lim_{n/N\rightarrow t}a_{n,N}&=e^{-\lambda t}\sqrt{e^{-c\lambda}(1-e^{-\lambda t})(1-e^{-(c+t)\lambda})},\\
B(t):=\lim_{n/N\rightarrow t}b_{n,N}&=e^{-\lambda t}(1-e^{-(c+t)\lambda})+e^{-(c+t)\lambda}(1-e^{-\lambda t}).\end{split}

Recall that for
\mathfrak{a}<\mathfrak{b}
, the arcsine distribution
\omega_{[\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{b}]}
is a measure defined by

(3.44)

\frac{d\omega_{[\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{b}]}(x)}{dx}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi\sqrt{(\mathfrak{b}-x)(x-\mathfrak{a}})},&\text{if }x\in(\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{b}),\\
0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Applying
[
45
, Theorem 1.4]
, we deduce that the empirical zero distribution
\nu_{n,N}(dx)
of the little
q
-Laguerre polynomials converges to

(3.45)

\nu:=\lim_{n/N\rightarrow 1}\nu_{n,N}=\int_{0}^{1}\omega_{[B(s)-2A(s),B(s)+2A(s)]}\,ds.

We also note that by
[
45
, Eq. (1.10)]
, the support of the limiting zero distribution
\nu
is given by

(3.46)

I_{\lambda}:=\Big[\inf_{0<s<1}(B(s)-2A(s)),\sup_{0<s<1}(B(s)+2A(s))\Big].

Therefore, it follows that the density function of
\nu
is given by

(3.47)

\frac{d\nu(x)}{dx}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{t_{-}(x)}^{\min\{1,t_{+}(x)\}}\frac{ds}{\sqrt{(B(s)+2A(s)-x)(x-B(s)+2A(s))}},&\text{if }x\in I_{\lambda},\\
0,&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

where
t_{\pm}(x)
denote the endpoints of the interval

(3.48)

\big\{s>0:B(s)-2A(s)\leq x\leq B(s)+2A(s)\big\}.

Since this expression coincides with the integral representation of the limiting spectral density (
3.10
) under the change of variables
t=e^{-\lambda s}
, this completes the proof of Theorem
1.2
(C). ∎

Appendix A
Integral evaluation

In this appendix, we provide explicit computations of the integrals that appear in the coefficients of the asymptotic expansion (
3.24
) for
\varphi(z)
. Recall that for
0<a<b\leq 1
,

R(z)=(z-a)^{\frac{1}{2}}(z-b)^{\frac{1}{2}},\qquad R_{+}(z)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}R(z+i\varepsilon).

Proposition A.1
.

Let
0<a<b\leq 1
. Then we have

(A.1)

Proposition A.2
.

Let
0<a<b\leq d\leq 1
. Then we have

(A.4)

\int_{b}^{d}\frac{s^{j}}{R(s)}\,ds=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\sqrt{ab}}\log\Big(\frac{\sqrt{b(d-a)}+\sqrt{a(d-b)}}{\sqrt{b(d-a)}-\sqrt{a(d-b)}}\Big)&\textup{if }j=-1,\\
2\log\Big(\frac{\sqrt{d-a}+\sqrt{d-b}}{\sqrt{b-a}}\Big)&\textup{if }j=0,\\
\sqrt{(d-a)(d-b)}+(a+b)\log\Big(\frac{\sqrt{d-a}+\sqrt{d-b}}{\sqrt{b-a}}\Big)&\textup{if }j=1.\end{cases}

Proof.

We first observe that the argument of the square root is positive. We then proceed by introducing the change of variables
u=\sqrt{(s-b)/(s-a)}
. Then by straightforward computations, it follows that

(A.5)

\displaystyle\int_{b}^{d}\frac{1}{sR(s)}\,ds

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{\sqrt{\frac{d-b}{d-a}}}\frac{1}{b-u^{2}a}\,du=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{ab}}\log\Big(\tfrac{\sqrt{b(d-a)}+\sqrt{a(d-b)}}{\sqrt{b(d-a)}-\sqrt{a(d-b)}}\Big),

(A.6)

\displaystyle\int_{b}^{d}\frac{1}{R(s)}ds

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{\sqrt{\frac{d-b}{d-a}}}\frac{1}{1-u^{2}}\,du=2\log\Big(\tfrac{\sqrt{d-a}+\sqrt{d-b}}{\sqrt{b-a}}\Big),

(A.7)

\displaystyle\int_{b}^{d}\frac{s}{R(s)}\,ds

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{\sqrt{\frac{d-b}{d-a}}}\frac{b-au^{2}}{(1-u^{2})^{2}}\,du=\sqrt{(d-a)(d-b)}+(a+b)\log\Big(\tfrac{\sqrt{d-a}+\sqrt{d-b}}{\sqrt{b-a}}\Big).

This completes the proof.
∎

Proposition A.3
.

Let
0<a<b\leq d\leq 1
. Then for
z\not\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

(A.8)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{a}^{b}\frac{\log(1-s)}{R_{+}(s)s(z-s)}\,ds&=-\frac{\log(1-z)}{2zR(z)}+\frac{1}{2zR(z)}\log\bigg(\frac{\sqrt{z-b}+\sqrt{z-a}}{\sqrt{z-b}-\sqrt{z-a}}\bigg)\\
&\quad-\frac{1}{2zR(z)}\log\bigg(\frac{\sqrt{(1-a)(z-b)}+\sqrt{(1-b)(z-a)}}{\sqrt{(1-a)(z-b)}-\sqrt{(1-b)(z-a)}}\bigg)\\
&\quad-\frac{1}{z\sqrt{ab}}\log\bigg(\frac{\sqrt{b(1-a)}+\sqrt{a(1-b)}}{\sqrt{b}+\sqrt{a}}\bigg).\end{split}

Proof.

As in the proof of Proposition
A.1
, we have

(A.9)

\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{a}^{b}\frac{\log(1-s)}{R_{+}(s)s(z-s)}\,ds=\frac{1}{4\pi i}\int_{\gamma}\frac{\log(1-s)}{R(s)s(z-s)}\,ds\,,

where
\gamma
is the contour in Figure
8
(B). By taking the radius
R\to\infty
, we deduce that

(A.10)

\begin{split}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{a}^{b}\frac{\log(1-s)}{R_{+}(s)s(z-s)}\,ds&=\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\Res_{s=0}\frac{\log(1-s)}{R(s)s(z-s)}+\Res_{s=z}\frac{\log(1-s)}{R(s)s(z-s)}-\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{s(z-s)R(s)}\,ds\bigg)\\
&=-\frac{\log(1-z)}{2zR(z)}+\frac{1}{2z}\bigg(\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{R(s)(z-s)}\,ds-\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{R(s)s}\,ds\bigg).\end{split}

The remaining integral can now be evaluated using a standard Euler substitution, yielding the desired identity.
∎
