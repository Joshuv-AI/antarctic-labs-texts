Comparing a Mass Shell Model for Compact Binaries with Observed Gravitational Waves Preprint: PRD
Noah M. MacKay

Abstract
A recent study modeled coalescing compact binaries as a spinning and contracting mass shell geometry, reinterpreting the effective one-body model. Besides obtaining the respective waveform profiles that include rates of change in binary separation and orbital frequency, an expression for the mass-shell surface energy was derived to compute the anticipated energy radiated as gravitational waves. In this study, we revisit the surface energy derivation using a variational method that was recently used, and we recover the energy’s dependence on the compact binary’s reduced mass, symmetric mass ratio, and the normalized rotational speed at merger. For 31 out of 93 cataloged GW events within the O1, O2 and O3 observation runs, we compute the anticipated energy radiated as gravitational waves and compare these values with the observed energy, either directly measured or extracted from the total-minus-remnant mass difference. It is shown that the model presented in this work agrees well with observed energy measurements, within the error range and via 1:1 ratios spanning from 0.828 0.828 to 0.997 0.997 across 29 example events (the mean value of these 29 ratios being 0.941 0.941 ), with GW190403_051519 serving as an outlier with the 1:1 ratio of 0.462 0.462 , demonstrating the precision and universality of the energy expression to be applied to current and future detections.



I
Introduction

Since their discovery on 14 September 2015, gravitational waves (GWs) have been observed by the LIGO-Virgo-KAGRA (LVK) collaboration
LIGOScientific:2018mvr
;
LIGOScientific:2021usb
;
KAGRA:2021vkt
. GW sources are well understood to be coalescing compact binaries (CCBs), which are treated as non-zero quadrupolar sources in the linearized Einstein field equations (EFEs) when obtaining the respective waveforms. Analytical efforts to describe CCB dynamics include the effective one-body (EOB) approach
Buonanno:1998gg
;
Buonanno:2005xu
, which views a CCB as the reduced mass
\mu=m_{1}m_{2}/M
(
M=m_{1}+m_{2}
is the total mass) spiraling inward, towards the total mass innermost stable circular orbital (ISCO) radius, until it plunges into the total mass horizon with radius
r_{S}=2GM
, with
c=1
. The largeness of the symmetric mass ratio
\alpha:=\mu/M\in(0,1/4]
directly influences the duration of nearly-circular orbital paths and how soon they become inspiraling plunge paths
Buonanno:1998gg
.

A more recent study
MacKay:2024qxj
reinterpreted the EOB model, by describing a CCB as a spinning and shrinking hollow mass shell. In this framework, the CB masses
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
are distributed along a mass ring on the equatorial plane; inclination angles
\iota\in[0,\pi]
are “integrated” over by distributing the mass ring into a mass shell. The measure of the mass shell remains the reduced mass
\mu
– a logical choice for binary systems and staying in alignment with EOB –, and the rate of change in CCB separation
-\dot{L}
illustrates the rate of change in the shell diameter
-\dot{S}
. At the merger time
t=t_{C}
, coalescence ends where the masses touch surfaces:
L(t_{C})=r_{1}+r_{2}
, relating the shell diameter to the total mass horizon:
S(t_{C})=4GM
. Via
-\dot{L}=-\dot{S}
, the dynamic shell diameter is expressed as
S(t)=L(t)-r_{1}-r_{2}+4GM
(c.f. Ref.
MacKay:2024qxj
).

When the shrinking shell geometry (assumed to be a point mass under a luminosity distance
D\gg S
) is put in the quadrupole-sourced GW equation, the resulting waveform is readily transverse-traceless (TT) gauged, with rates of change in shell diameter and orbital frequency
\Omega
influencing the wave amplitude and envelope
MacKay:2024qxj
:

\displaystyle h_{+}^{\mathrm{TT}}=-\frac{G\mu S^{2}}{D}\left[\sin(2\Omega t)\left(\frac{2\dot{S}}{S}\left(\Omega+t\dot{\Omega}\right)+\dot{\Omega}+\frac{t}{2}\ddot{\Omega}\right)-\cos(2\Omega t)\left(\frac{\dot{S}^{2}}{2S^{2}}+\frac{\ddot{S}}{2S}-\left(\Omega+t\dot{\Omega}\right)^{2}\right)+\frac{\dot{S}^{2}}{2S^{2}}+\frac{\ddot{S}}{2S}\right],

(1a)

\displaystyle h_{\times}^{\mathrm{TT}}=-\frac{G\mu S^{2}}{D}\left[\sin(2\Omega t)\left(\frac{\dot{S}^{2}}{2S^{2}}+\frac{\ddot{S}}{2S}-\left(\Omega+t\dot{\Omega}\right)^{2}\right)+\cos(2\Omega t)\left(\frac{2\dot{S}}{S}\left(\Omega+t\dot{\Omega}\right)+\dot{\Omega}+\frac{t}{2}\ddot{\Omega}\right)\right].

(1b)

Under a mass shell model for CCBs, one can employ Newtonian postulates for a gravitating mass shell, which state: (i) the internal gravitational field lines cancel out, and (ii) the external gravitational field resembles that of a point mass. In the logic of the second postulate, the emission of GWs via their waveforms can be idealized as the quadrupolar radiation transporting energy from the mass shell surface. Ref.
MacKay:2024qxj
also obtained an expression for this surface energy, which serves to calculate the anticipated energy radiated as quadrupole-sourced GWs. The derivation employed a variational methodology on the EFEs, in the similar philosophy as quantum mechanical variational method: using the Laplace-Beltrami formulation for the Ricci tensor
Chow:2004
and a Kerr metric Ansatz for a spinning, compact system, an expression for the energy density
T_{00}\propto G_{00}
was obtained by only evaluating the geometric contributions of the
g_{00}
Kerr element. Under simple assumptions, the expression for the surface energy at coalescence
E(t_{C})\equiv T_{00}V(t_{C})
(
V(t)\propto\rho(t)^{3}
, with
\rho=S/2
being the shell radius) readily depends on the symmetric mass ratio
\alpha
, the reduced mass
\mu
, and a quadratic spin term encoding the normalized rotational velocity at merger:

E(t_{C})\simeq\frac{\pi}{6}\alpha\mu(1-5\beta_{C}^{2}).

(2)

In Eq. (
2
),
\beta_{C}=v_{C}/c
in SI units is the speed ratio entailing the normalized rotational velocity, which has the anticipated coalescence value of
v_{C}=GMf_{\text{GW,peak}}
(i.e. the peak orbital frequency times the total mass horizon radius).

Two representative examples: GW150914
LIGOScientific:2016aoc
and GW170817
LIGOScientific:2017vwq
; were chosen mainly for their CB type – respectively binary black holes (BBHs) and binary neutron stars (BNSs) – and to test for invariance in CB type. The respective energy values anticipated via Eq. (
2
), using center parameter values (low-spin prior for GW170817) in the GWTC papers
LIGOScientific:2018mvr
;
LIGOScientific:2021usb
, are
2.075M_{\odot}c^{2}
and
0.0885M_{\odot}c^{2}
. This is compared to the respective cataloged values of
E_{\mathrm{rad}}=3.1^{+0.4}_{-0.4}M_{\odot}c^{2}
and
E_{\text{rad}}\geq 0.04M_{\odot}c^{2}

LIGOScientific:2018mvr
. Constraining the GW energy for GW170817 can also be set by the ad hoc “rule-of-thumb” of roughly one-tenth of the chirp mass
\mathcal{M}\equiv\alpha^{3/5}M
(see the comparison between
M-M_{f}
and
\sim\mathcal{M}/10
listed in Refs.
LIGOScientific:2018mvr
;
LIGOScientific:2021usb
;
KAGRA:2021vkt
), which is
\sim 0.1186M_{\odot}c^{2}
via the spin-prior-mutual chirp mass
\mathcal{M}=1.186^{+0.001}_{-0.001}M_{\odot}

LIGOScientific:2018mvr
.

Addressing the scaling discrepancy between the model and the cataloged values rest in the assumptions taken to derive Eq. (
2
). A rigorous method in comparison, discussed in-text in Ref.
MacKay:2024qxj
but further analyzed in Section
II
of this work, utilized the non-vanishing Kerr-metric Kretschmann scalar
K=R_{\alpha\beta\mu\nu}R^{\alpha\beta\mu\nu}
as a surrogate for the Ricci scalar. More specifically,
R_{\text{eff}}=-\sqrt{K}/6
, which contributes to the new anticipated values for GW150814 and GW170817, respectively, to be
E(t_{C})\simeq 3.26M_{\odot}c^{2}
and
E(t_{C})\simeq 0.14M_{\odot}c^{2}
. Again, these values used center values for the parameters, and they are closer to the catalog (“rule-of-thumb” for GW170817) energy values.

In this work, we revisit the energy density expression that was first derived under simple assumptions in Ref.
MacKay:2024qxj
. However, we will use the Kretschmann scalar surrogate for the Ricci scalar, as the representative examples showed a closer approximation than the simplified values. To test its validity and universality, we compare energy calculations (anticipated values) with the catalog values over 31 examples of the 93 cataloged GW events as of 10 August, 2025. This is presented in Section
III.2
; after a discussion of our findings and methods in Section
IV
, we conclude in Section
IV.3
.

II
Methods

The Einstein field equations (EFEs) describe gravity as the response to geometric warpings caused by a source:

G_{\mu\nu}:=R_{\mu\nu}-\frac{1}{2}Rg_{\mu\nu}=8\pi G\,T_{\mu\nu}.

(3)

The Einstein tensor
G_{\mu\nu}
on the left-hand side encodes the geometric effects caused by the source
T_{\mu\nu}
via the Ricci
R_{\mu\nu}
and metric
g_{\mu\nu}
tensors and the Ricci scalar
R=g^{\mu\nu}R_{\mu\nu}
. The additive cosmological contribution
\Lambda g_{\mu\nu}
is neglected.

With GW sources being quadrupolar, the source term
T_{\mu\nu}
is treated as non-zero, regardless if the CB is BBH, BNS, or BH-NS. Under a weak-field approximation and a very far and very compact system (
D\gg L
,
D
is the luminosity distance and
L
is the CB separation), Eq. (
3
) is reduced to a traceless-transverse (TT)-gauged spatial wave equation with a non-zero quadrupolar source
Q_{ij}
:

h^{\text{TT}}_{ij}=\frac{2G}{D}\ddot{Q}^{\text{TT}}_{ij}.

(4)

If one considers the rotating and shrinking mass shell geometry for CBs, the quadrupole moment will depend on the system’s moment of inertia
I=\mu\rho^{2}
, if assumed to be a point mass under
D\gg S
. This yields the wave profiles given in Eq. (
1a
) and (
1b
), where
\rho=S/2
.

II.1
A Variational Method on the EFEs

In the same breath, we can define the energy-momentum tensor of a CB mass shell straightforwardly by re-arranging Eq. (
3
):

T_{\mu\nu}=\frac{1}{8\pi G}G_{\mu\nu}.

(5)

One needs to, therefore, determine the geometric contributions nested in
G_{\mu\nu}
. For the energy density,
T_{00}\propto G_{00}
. Known solutions to the EFEs, e.g. Schwarzschild
Schwarzschild:1916uq
;
Schwarzschild:1916ae
, Tolman-Oppenheimer-Volkoff
Tolman:1939jz
;
Oppenheimer:1939ne
, and Kerr
Kerr:1963ud
;
Boyer:1967
;
Chandrasekhar:1985kt
, were obtained from well-defined energy-momentum sources, either vacuum
Schwarzschild:1916uq
;
Schwarzschild:1916ae
;
Kerr:1963ud
;
Boyer:1967
or perfect fluid
Tolman:1939jz
;
Oppenheimer:1939ne
. However, it is the objective to find the energy desnity
T_{00}
for a CB modeled as a rotating and shrinking mass shell. This must be non-zero, to follow the same logic that CCBs are non-zero sources from which GWs propagate with energy. This means, instead of beginning with a well-defined source, we begin with a well-defined (or assumed) geometric signature. This task seems intractable if not counter-intuitive, unless we employ a variational methodology.

Variational method (also called approximational method) is typically used in perturbative quantum mechanics. The energy eigenvalue of an impossible Hamiltonian can be approximated by assuming an Ansatz wavefunction, finding the expectation value of the Hamiltonian to yield an energy functional, and minimizing the functional to obtain the anticipated ground state energy. We follow the same logic, as initially done in Ref.
MacKay:2024qxj
: we choose an Ansatz metric, on which we apply differential operators that are inherent in the Christofel symbols
\Gamma^{\alpha}_{\mu\nu}
and the Ricci tensor, and define
G_{\mu\nu}\propto T_{\mu\nu}
component-wise, with our focus purely being
G_{00}
.

Schematically, the Ricci tensor follows
R\sim\partial\Gamma+\Gamma\Gamma
(dropping the indices), and the Christofel symbols satisfy
\Gamma\sim g^{-1}\partial g
. Therefore, the Ricci tensor can be defined as a Laplace-like operator:
R\sim\nabla(g^{-1}\partial g)\sim\nabla^{2}g
, where
\nabla\sim(\partial+\Gamma)
is the covariant derivative. This interpretation is consistent with Lemma 3.32 from Chow and Knopf
Chow:2004
, which states:

R_{\mu\nu}\simeq-\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{\alpha}\nabla^{\alpha}g_{\mu\nu}\,\left(+~\mathrm{lower~order~terms}\right).

(6)

The operator
\nabla_{\alpha}\nabla^{\alpha}
is the Laplace-Beltrami operator, and when the metric is well-defined and has a non-zero determinant, it can be written in the Christoffel symbol-free form as:

\nabla_{\alpha}\nabla^{\alpha}:=\Delta^{\mathrm{LB}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{-g}}\,\partial_{\alpha}\left(\sqrt{-g}g^{\alpha\beta}\partial_{\beta}\right),

(7)

where
\sqrt{-g}=\sqrt{-\mathrm{det}(g_{\mu\nu})}
and
g^{\mu\nu}
is the inverse metric. Therefore, following Eq. (
3
),
G_{00}
depends on
R_{00}
,
R
, and
g_{00}
, and via Eq. (
6
)
R_{00}\propto g_{00}
. In this Laplace-Beltrami formulation, the energy density is defined as

T_{00}\simeq-\frac{1}{8\pi G}\left(\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{\alpha}\nabla^{\alpha}+\frac{1}{2}R\right)g_{00}.

(8)

One can see that, as
g_{00}
is negative under the
(-,+,+,+)
metric signature, the energy density is positive.

Nevertheless, both
\Delta^{\text{LB}}
in Eq. (
7
) and the Ricci scalar
R
depend on our choice of Ansatz metric. For a CB viewed from a luminosity distance
D\gg S
, we assume it to be a spinning, compact object. Therefore, our choice of Ansatz metric is the Kerr metric in Boyer-Linquist coordinates, modified to reflect the CB mass shell model:

ds^{2}=-\left(1-\frac{2G\mu r}{\Sigma}\right)dt^{2}+\frac{\Sigma}{\Delta}dr^{2}+\Sigma d\theta^{2}+\left(r^{2}+a^{2}+\frac{2G\mu ra^{2}}{\Sigma}\sin^{2}\theta\right)\sin^{2}\theta d\varphi^{2}-\frac{4G\mu ra\sin^{2}\theta}{\Sigma}d\varphi dt,

(9)

where
a=J/\mu
is a length-scale spin parameter, and

\displaystyle\Sigma=r^{2}+a^{2}\cos^{2}\theta,

(10a)

\displaystyle\Delta=r^{2}-2G\mu r+a^{2}.

(10b)

The metric determinant under the Kerr metric is
\mathrm{det}(g_{\mu\nu})=-\Sigma^{2}\sin^{2}\theta
, and since the metric elements depend only on the coordinates
r
and
\theta
, the resulting Laplace-Beltrami operator is compact:

\Delta^{\mathrm{LB}}_{\mathrm{Kerr}}=\frac{1}{\Sigma\sin\theta}\left[\sin\theta\partial_{r}\left(\Delta\partial_{r}\right)+\partial_{\theta}\left(\sin\theta\partial_{\theta}\right)\right].

(11)

Using Eq. (
6
), one can straightforwardly compute
R_{00}
to be a non-zero contribution:

R_{00}=\frac{G^{2}\mu^{2}}{2\Sigma^{4}}\left(a^{4}\left(\frac{3}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\cos(4\theta)\right)+4r^{2}\left(r^{2}-3a^{2}\right)+4a^{2}\cos(2\theta)\left(\frac{a^{2}}{2}-3r^{2}\right)\right).

(12)

The Ricci scalar under the Kerr metric is
R=0
, as the Kerr solution is conventionally for a rotating BH. This was the simple assumption that obtained Eq. (
2
), which utilized the non-zero
R_{00}
via Eq. (
12
), c.f. Ref.
MacKay:2024qxj
for the derivation details. For an alternative route, we will use the same derivation details, however with an effective Ricci scalar under a Kerr metric Ansatz.

II.2
The Surrogate for the Ricci Scalar

The discrepancy between Eq. (
2
) and the catalog energy values for e.g. GW150914 and GW170817, as introduced in Section
I
, lies in modeling choices. Assuming the variational methodology and the Kerr metric Ansatz collectively remain a valid strategy, an underestimated model calculation suggests that we should include an overall enhancing, non-zero Ricci scalar contribution (see Eq.
8
). Ref.
MacKay:2024qxj
explored the option of a Ricci scalar calculated via the Laplace-Beltrami formulated metric contraction
R\propto g^{\mu\nu}\nabla_{\alpha}\nabla^{\alpha}g_{\mu\nu}
, which was ultimately found to be divergent when one integrates
R
over the polar angle
\theta\in[0,\pi]
(a mathematical consequence to the mass shell geometry, where the inclination angle
\iota\in[0,\pi]
is the effective polar angle). A heuristic alternative is to leverage the non-zero Kerr-metric Kretschmann scalar
Henry:1999rm
;
Visser:2007fj
:

\displaystyle K_{\text{Kerr}}=\frac{48G^{2}\mu^{2}}{\Sigma^{6}}\Big(

\displaystyle 15\left(a^{4}r^{2}\cos^{4}\theta-a^{2}r^{4}\cos^{2}\theta\right)

(13)

\displaystyle+r^{6}-a^{6}\cos^{6}\theta\Big);

as a surrogate for the Ricci scalar. However, an effective Ricci scalar out of the Kretschmann scalar is not straight forward. First, we restore dimensionality by claiming
R_{\text{eff}}\propto\sqrt{K}
. Second, we recall via Eq. (
6
) a
-1/2
scaling that would have persisted in calculations:

\displaystyle R\simeq-\frac{1}{2}\left(g^{\mu\nu}\nabla_{\alpha}\nabla^{\alpha}g_{\mu\nu}\right)

\displaystyle\implies R_{\text{eff}}\simeq-\frac{1}{2}\left(\tilde{\lambda}\sqrt{K}\right),

(14)

where
\tilde{\lambda}
is a proportionality coefficient. Third, recalling the identity for the Kretschmann scalar:

K=C_{\alpha\beta\mu\nu}C^{\alpha\beta\mu\nu}+2R_{\alpha\beta}R^{\alpha\beta}-\frac{1}{3}R^{2},

(15)

(the first two terms are respectively the Weyl and Ricci curvature contributions) a
1/3
factor appears in the third, readily-negative Ricci-scalar-squared term. For reasons that will be articulated in the Section
IV.2
, we take the proportionality coefficient to be
\tilde{\lambda}=1/3
. This is the source of the surrogate
R_{\text{eff}}=-\sqrt{K}/6
mentioned in Section
I
and introduced in Ref.
MacKay:2024qxj
, which rewrites Eq. (
8
) as

T_{00}\simeq\frac{1}{8\pi G}\left(R_{00}+\frac{1}{12}g_{00}\sqrt{K_{\text{Kerr}}}\right)=:\frac{1}{8\pi G}G^{\text{eff}}_{00},

(16)

where
R_{00}
is given by Eq. (
12
), and
g_{00}
is given in the Kerr metric.

III
Results

III.1
The Anticipated Energy

The effective (00)-th Einstein tensor element
G^{\text{eff}}_{00}
on the right-hand side of Eq. (
16
), after implementing
K_{\text{Kerr}}
via Eq. (
13
) and
g_{00}
in Eq. (
9
), must be integrated over the polar angle
\theta\in[0,\pi]
to extract a full-sphere value. However, angular integration over
\sqrt{K}
via Eq. (
13
) proves to be difficult. While substitution techniques such as the Weierstraß transformation may be useful to tame the evaluation, we recall that the final spin parameter of CCBs is typically
a<1

LIGOScientific:2018mvr
;
LIGOScientific:2021usb
;
KAGRA:2021vkt
. That is, across all stages of CB coalescence, the spin parameter remains well below unity. This physical constraint motivates a Taylor expansion of Eq. (
16
) in small
a
, up to quadratic order to include a spin correction term. After that, then we integrate over
\theta
. Carrying out this procedure, we obtain a polynomial containing orders of
G
, i.e., a post-Minkowskian (PM) expansion
Damour:2016gwp
:

\implies G_{00}^{\mathrm{eff}}=\frac{2\pi G^{2}\mu^{2}}{r^{4}}-\frac{10\pi G^{2}\mu^{2}}{r^{4}}\frac{a^{2}}{r^{2}}-\frac{G\mu\sqrt{3}}{3r^{3}}+\frac{7\pi G\mu\sqrt{3}}{2r^{3}}\frac{a^{2}}{r^{2}}+\frac{2\pi G^{2}\mu^{2}\sqrt{3}}{3r^{4}}-\frac{23\pi G^{2}\mu^{2}\sqrt{3}}{6r^{4}}\frac{a^{2}}{r^{2}}.

(17)

The first two terms are the “small
a
” polar-integrated
R_{00}
contributions, whose PM order is 2. Thus, we discard the 1PM terms by choice, which leaves behind the zeroth-order, quasi-Newtonian term and a quadratic spin term:

G_{00}^{\mathrm{eff}}\simeq\frac{2\pi G^{2}\mu^{2}}{r^{4}}\left(1.577-8.320\frac{a^{2}}{r^{2}}\right).

(18)

After Eq. (
18
) is substituted in Eq. (
16
), we can find the surface energy such that
E=T_{00}V
at
r=\rho
. This further simplifies the spin term such that
a^{2}/\rho^{2}=\beta^{2}
, where
\beta=v/c
in SI units encodes the normalized rotational velocity of the CCB. We can further define the surface energy at the time of merger
t=t_{C}
, where
\rho=2GM
and
\beta=\beta_{C}\propto GMf_{\text{GW,peak}}
:

\implies E(t_{C})\simeq 0.826\frac{\mu^{2}}{M}\left(1-5.276\beta_{C}^{2}\right).

(19)

One can see that Eq. (
2
) is derived by considering only the
R_{00}
terms in Eq. (
17
) for Eq. (
16
). Comparing Eqs. (
2
) and (
19
), the latter presents a systematic enhancement to the energy computation, which yielded the anticipated GW150914 and GW170817 values to be, respectively,
E(t_{C})\simeq 3.26M_{\odot}c^{2}
and
E(t_{C})\simeq 0.14M_{\odot}c^{2}
. Also, one can determine the maximum coalescence rotational velocity the model allows, to avoid negative energy calculations. The energy via Eq. (
19
) is zero at
\beta_{C}^{2}\simeq 0.190
, which sets the ceiling value for the normalized velocity:
v_{C}\leq 0.435c
. This is comparable with the rotational velocity at the total mass ISCO radius:
v_{\text{ISCO}}=0.408c
.

Comparison with Known GW Signals

(provided in GWTC-1

IV
Discussion

The analytical effort in this work reinterprets the effective one-body approach, which originally views CB coalescence as the inspiraling plunge path of the reduced mass, by considering instead a rotating and shrinking mass shell of reduced-mass measure. The focus centers on the energy density of the CB mass shell, whose derivation utilized methods akin to quantum mechanical variational method. Using a Kerr metric Ansatz and an effective Ricci scalar that depends on a scaled square-root of the Kerr-metric Kretschmann scalar, the expression for energy at coalescence (Eq. [
19
]) was applied to known GW signals to test its precision and universality. It is provided in Tables
1
–
3
that the agreement between the analytical model and measurement rests in the 1:1 ratio of the center energy values, as all anticipated energy values were within the error range for each respective observed energy. The 1:1 ratios are consistently within
0.950\sim 0.998
, spanning the range
\in[0.828,\,0.997]
. That is, with the exception of GW190403_051519 with the 1:1 ratio of 0.462 (see Table
2
). Because the strain plots on the open access catalog had no legible chirp rise detail in either detector, and the mass measurements carry quite sizable error ranges, one could be charitable in discarding the 1:1 ratio for GW190403_051519 as an outlier.

IV.1
One-Tenth of the Chirp Mass?

One of the questions the CB mass shell model attempted to answer was whether the anticipated GW energy systematically supports the ad hoc scaling of
\sim\mathcal{M}/10
, where
\mathcal{M}\equiv\alpha^{3/5}M
is the chirp mass. This question was motivated by observing an emergent correlation between measured radiated values
E_{\text{rad}}
(alternatively the mass difference
M-M_{\text{f}}
) and roughly one-tenth of the corresponding chirp mass (see Refs.
LIGOScientific:2018mvr
;
LIGOScientific:2021usb
;
KAGRA:2021vkt
). While Eqs. (
2
) and (
19
) show that, systematically, it is not the case, it is nonetheless a convenient rule-of-thumb to use as an extra constraint whenever in doubt – especially if the measured energy is not well-defined (see GW170817 in Table
1
).

However, one must not heavily rely on this rule-of-thumb as the true (even a close approximate) energy value. Tables
4
and
5
contain almost all of the contents already listed in Tables
2
and
3
, however the chirp mass replaces the mass difference. The motivation is to test how well the ad hoc scaling
\sim\mathcal{M}/10
agrees with the model (assuming its confidence level is high, given the 1:1 ratios with the measured/extracted value), which is gauged by new 1:1 ratios. These new 1:1 ratios, offered in Tables
4
and
5
, are widely scattered across the range
\in[0.286,\,0.980]
in Table
4
and consistently within
0.87\sim 0.88
in Table
5
. This deduces that
E_{\text{rad}}\approx\mathcal{M}/10
, while a curious emergence across all detections, is not a reliable way to express the radiated GW energy.

IV.2
Why re-scale the Kretschmann scalar?

As GWs are geometric perturbations that carry energy, it was natural to seek an energy expression in which a geometric Ansatz for the source can be applied. Choosing the Kerr metric seemed suitable, as the mass-shell geometry gave the effective view of compact quadrupole systems as compact and spinning objects. The heuristic approach of using the purely geometrical, non-zero Kretschmann scalar – specifically its negated square-root – as an effective Ricci scalar further emphasizes the role curved geometry plays in generating GWs and the energy they transport, as in Eq. (
19
).

When introducing the square-root of the Kretschmann scalar as an effective Ricci scalar, the choice was made that
\tilde{\lambda}=1/3
serves as a re-scaling on top of the persistent
-1/2
factor from Eq. (
6
). A critical question arises: why
\tilde{\lambda}=1/3
as opposed to, for instance,
\tilde{\lambda}=\sqrt{3}
, as one might follow from Eq. (
15
)? Consider Eq. (
15
); if one naively neglects the Weyl tensor contribution and readily takes the Ricci tensor contributions to be zero, then one effectively yields:

\implies R_{\text{eff}}=-\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{-3K_{\text{Kerr}}}=-\frac{i}{2}\sqrt{3K_{\text{Kerr}}}.

(21)

This turns Eq. (
16
) into a complex value, where the physically acceptable real part is readily the simple case scenario:
\text{Re}(G^{\text{eff}}_{00})=R_{00}
via Eq. (
12
). One quickly recovers Eq. (
2
) as a result, and we find ourselves with the same underestimation problem. Conversely, choosing
\tilde{\lambda}=\sqrt{3}
leads to the effective Ricci scalar
R_{\text{eff}}=-\sqrt{3K_{\text{Kerr}}}/2
, where
\sqrt{3}/2\simeq 0.866
. This, quite the opposite of the simple case, overestimates the energy calculations, as explicitly shown in Ref.
MacKay:2024qxj
when the unscaled toy scenario
R_{\text{eff}}=-\sqrt{K_{\text{Kerr}}}
was explored. E.g., for GW150914 using center parameter values,
E(t_{C})\simeq 9.227M_{\odot}c^{2}
(c.f. Ref.
MacKay:2024qxj
), which is well above what is measured. Thus, the re-scaling of
\tilde{\lambda}\simeq 0.866
would not make much of a difference.

The source of this overshoot is purely geometric: for vacuum solutions like Kerr, the Kretschmann scalar is completely driven by the Weyl tensor contribution given vanishing Ricci tensor and scalar contributions (see Eq. [
15
]). Without moderation,
\sqrt{K}
injects an unrealistically large curvature scale into the energy estimate, above the quadrupolar radiation scale. The
1/3
factor, applied in addition to the inherent
-1/2
scaling from the Laplace-Beltrami formulation, tempers this excess and brings the scale in alignment with the qudrupole-radiation regime. This moderation proves to be essential, which is evident in Tables
1
–
3
, where the corrected model values show a close approximation with the observed GW energies.

While
\tilde{\lambda}=1/3
is technically ad hoc, its role is not an arbitrary numerical convenience. It is a physically motivated correction that not only balances the geometric dominance of the Weyl contribution, but it also corrects the anticipated output energy to be at the same regime as quadrupolar radiation, and it maintains the consistency between the analytical model and observational benchmarks.

IV.3
Concluding Statements

The methods and results presented in this work honors the EOB approach
Buonanno:1998gg
;
Buonanno:2005xu
with a reinterpretation of the CCB as a mass shell. We also present a novel application of the variational method to solve the EFEs, and therewith we provide an expression for radiated GW energy that can calculate anticipated values close to what’s been observed. The future extension of this work is the application of Eq. (
19
) to current and future GW detections, further reinforcing its universality.

Another route to pursue is to address the CCB mass-shell waveform profiles, which are analytically expressed as Eqs. (
1a
) and (
1b
) and were briefly discussed in Ref.
MacKay:2024qxj
. It is desired to explore further how the dynamic CCB separation and the orbital frequency, and their rates of change under a conserved angular momentum, influence the waveform’s profile in amplitude and in the wave envelope. It is of interest, in addition, to explore how these waveforms compare to the current and widely used numerical models, such as
SEOBNRv4

Bohe:2016gbl
,
IMRPhenom

Ajith:2007qp
, and the
SEOBNRv4T
tidal deformation extension
Hinderer:2016eia
;
Steinhoff:2016rfi
;
Steinhoff:2021dsn
