High-Birefringence Photoalignable Polymeric Films via In Situ Mesogenic Modification
Authors: Nobuhiro Kawatsuki, Keigo Uemura, Madoka Marui, Hirofumi Takatsuka, Moritsugu Sakamoto, Hiroshi Ono
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We report a strategy for tuning birefringence in photoalignable liquid-crystalline copolymer (PLCP) films through in situ modification of mesogenic side groups. A PLCP containing 4-formylphenyl benzoate (PA) and cinnamic acid (CA) units was reacted with five phenylamine derivatives after axis-selective photoreactions induced by linearly polarized light. The condensation sequence and phenylamine structure strongly affected molecular reorientation and birefringence development. Microwave-assisted processing enabled rapid condensation and simultaneous molecular reorganization, yielding a maximum birefringence of Δn = 0.47 at 527 nm for 2-aminofluorene (AF)-modified films. Thick (∼2000 nm) AF-modified PLCP films exhibited high transparency and retardation values up to 625 nm and were further applied to highly efficient near-infrared polarization gratings operating at 1550 nm with diffraction efficiencies of 86–87%. These results demonstrate that in situ mesogenic modification provides an effective route to highly birefringent PLCP films for photonic applications.

Introduction

Photoalignment has been widely used in liquid crystal (LC) displays and in birefringent optical components such as retardation films and polarization-sensitive diffraction elements. − In conventional photoalignment systems, linearly polarized (LP) light induces axis-selective photoreactions within a thin photosensitive polymer layer, generating small optical anisotropies that guide the alignment of functional LC materials. − When combined with curable LC layers, these systems enable patterned birefringence and polarization-dependent diffraction. − ,− Photoalignable liquid-crystalline polymers (PLCPs) provide an alternative approach in which LP light directly generates an oriented structure inside the polymer. − ,− Many PLCPs incorporate photoisomerizable (e.g., azobenzene) or photo-cross-linkable moiety (e.g., cinnamate) groups, and subsequent thermal treatment amplifies the orientation throughout the film through anisotropic reorganization.

High-birefringence (Δn) films are essential for thin-film optical elements because they allow thinner retarders and polarization gratings (PGs) with improved angular acceptance of the diffraction efficiency under Bragg conditions. ,,, Sakhno et al. fabricated various types of PGs by controlling the holographic optical conditions using PLCPs with cinnamate side moieties, where the generated Δn of the film was 0.25. , Achieving large Δn requires both efficient molecular orientation and intrinsically high-birefringence mesogenic units. PLCPs incorporating rigid mesogenic units such as biphenyl or tolane groups can exhibit Δn values approaching 0.4. − Copolymerization with highly anisotropic comonomers can further enhance birefringence when cooperative photoalignment occurs. However, such polymers often present synthetic challenges and poor solubility arising from their rigid structures.

To overcome these limitations, we previously developed an in situ postmodification strategy for enhancing birefringence in bulk-aligned PLCP films. Copolymers containing 4-formylphenyl benzoate (PA) and cinnamic acid (CA) side groups (P50, P80, Figure a) were synthesized, and the exposed films were treated with 2-aminofluorene (AF). Condensation between PA and AF generated oriented Schiff base structures with high intrinsic birefringence (Figure b), yielding Δn values up to 0.45 in ∼180 nm thick films. These findings suggested that increasing the PA content and employing additional phenylamine derivatives could further control birefringence. Additionally, establishing a high-Δn process for thick PLCP films is critical since practical optical films such as retarders and diffractive elements typically require high retardation (Δnd; d is film thickness) exceeding a quarter wavelength. −

(a) Chemical structures of PLCPs and phenylamine derivatives. (b) In situ condensation of PA side groups with phenylamine derivatives to form high-birefringence NBA derivatives.

In this work, we designed a PA-rich copolymer (P90, Figure a) to enhance birefringence through axis-selective photoreactions followed by in situ condensation with various phenylamine derivatives. Five derivativesAF, 4-(4-methoxyphenyl)­aniline (MPA), 4-amino-4′-cyanobiphenyl (ACB), 4-amino-4′-nitrobiphenyl (ANB), and 2,7-diaminofluorene (DAF)were investigated to clarify how mesogenic structure influences molecular reorientation and birefringence development.

Condensation was carried out either before or after thermally stimulated photoinduced reorientation using hot-stage treatment or microwave irradiation. By comparing these processes, we elucidate how reaction sequence and mesogenic structure govern orientational amplification, birefringence development, and applicability to birefringent films and near-infrared PGs.

Experimental Section

Materials

Methacrylate monomers bearing CA and PA side groups were supplied from Hayashi Telempu Corporation. Copolymer P90 was synthesized via free radical polymerization of the corresponding methacrylate monomers in tetrahydrofuran (THF) using AIBN as an initiator. The number- and weight-average molecular weights were 26,100 and 56,300, respectively. P90 exhibited a nematic phase between 41 and 115 °C. Full synthetic details are provided in the Supporting Information.

Photoirradiation

P90 films with 150 nm (for reorientation studies) or 1500–1600 nm (for birefringent film and PG fabrication) were prepared by spin-coating THF solutions onto quartz or CaF2 substrates. Films were irradiated with a high-pressure Hg lamp equipped with a 313 nm bandpass filter (Asahi Spectra REX-250), delivering an intensity of 2.7 mW/cm2 at 313 nm through a wire-grid polarizer.

In Situ New-NBA Formation

Oriented new-NBA side groups were introduced via three distinct processes as shown in Scheme . For all cases, the film thickness increased from 150 nm to approximately 180 nm.

Introduction of Oriented New-NBA Side Groups via Three Distinct Processes

Process A: After exposure to LP 313 nm light, thermal annealing was subsequently performed to induce molecular reorientation at 90 °C for 10 min. Then the film was placed on a hot stage (AsOne HP-1SA) at 70 °C and coated with a 4 wt % phenylamine solution in ethylene glycol/acetic acid (pH = 1.9). After heating at 70 °C for 30 min, the film was rinsed with methanol and diethyl ether to remove unreacted phenylamine derivatives.

Process B: An exposed (nonannealed) P90 film was placed on a hot stage at 70 °C and coated with 4 wt % phenylamine derivative solution. Following rinsing with methanol and diethyl ether, the film was annealed at 200 °C for 10 min to promote orientational amplification.

Process C: An exposed (nonannealed) P90 film was immersed in 1 wt % phenylamine solution in ethylene glycol/acetic acid (pH = 1.9) and subjected to microwave irradiation (Shikoku Instrumentation Co., Ltd., μReactor EX) at 700 W for 1 min. The reaction temperature reached 140 °C within 10 s and increased to ∼170 °C at the end of irradiation. After microwave treatment, the sample was rinsed with methanol and diethyl ether.

Bulk Photoalignment of Thick Films and Fabrication of PGs

High-retardation films were prepared from P90 films with a thickness of 1500–1600 nm. After exposure to a dose of 70 mJ/cm2, the films were immersed in a 1 wt % AF solution in ethylene glycol/acetic acid (pH = 1.9) and processed via Process C to achieve in situ condensation. After the processing, film thickness increased ∼1800–2000 nm.

For PG fabrication, films were exposed to two counter-rotating circularly polarized 355 nm laser beams with a light intensity of 7 mW/cm2 each for 15 s to inscribe a grating period of 9.5 μm.

Characterization

Copolymer structures were confirmed by 1H NMR spectroscopy (JEOL JNM-ECZ400S) and FT-IR spectroscopy (JASCO FTIR-6600). Molecular weights were determined by GPC (JASCO PU-2080), equipped with a Shodex column using THF as the eluent, and calibrated against polystyrene standards. Thermal properties were analyzed by polarized optical microscopy (POM, Olympus BX51) using a Linkam TH600PM stage and by differential scanning calorimetry (DSC; Hitachi High Technologies DSC-7200). Film thicknesses were measured by an interferometric system of a VertScan R3300H (Ryoka System, Japan).

Photoinduced anisotropy was evaluated via in-plane dichroism (D) and out-of-plane order parameter (Sh) derived from polarized UV–vis spectra (Hitachi U-3010) and polarized FT-IR spectra equipped with Glan-Taylor polarization prisms and wire-grid polarizers. Values of D and Sh were calculated according to 1 D=(A||−A⊥)/(A⊥+A||) 2 Sh=[Ainitial−(A⊥+A||)/2] where A || and A ⊥ denote absorbances parallel and perpendicular to the electric vector (E) of the LP 313 nm light, and A initial is the absorbance prior to irradiation.

Birefringence (Δn) was measured using an OPTIPRO compact polarimeter (ShinTech, Japan) at 527 nm wavelength and by the Sénarmont method at 532 and 1550 nm wavelength for thick films.

Results and Discussion

Photoinduced Reorientation of P90 Films

The synthesized P90 exhibited a liquid-crystalline phase between 41 and 115 °C (Figure S1). The isotropization temperature (Ti ) of P90 was lower than that of the previously reported P50(80), consistent with the larger fraction of methacrylate units bearing PA side groups.

Polarized UV–vis measurements on 150 nm P90 films revealed that axis-selective exposure to linearly polarized (LP) 313 nm light produced only a very small initial optical anisotropy for the cinnamic acid (CA) absorption band near 320 nm (A || – A ⊥ = ΔA < 0.001) (Figure a). Subsequent thermal annealing within the LC temperature window amplified this anisotropy with reversed sign in a manner analogous to that observed for P50(80). After exposure to 70 mJ/cm2 followed by annealing at 90 °C for 10 min, the film reoriented parallel to E of the LP light and reached a D value at 266 nm (D 266) of 0.51 (Figure b), indicating efficient in-plane molecular reordering after thermal stimulation.

(a) Change in the polarized UV–vis absorption spectra of a P90 film. Insets show enlarged views of the absorption band of CA side groups. (b) Change in the polarized UV–vis absorption spectra of a P90 film before and after exposure to LP 313 nm light for 70 mJ/cm2 and subsequent annealing at 90 °C for 10 min. (c, d) Dichroism (D) and out-of-plane order parameter (Sh) of annealed P90 films measured at 266 nm (c) as a function of exposure energy when the annealing temperature is 90 °C, and (d) as a function of annealing temperature when the exposure energy is 70 mJ/cm2.

The direction and extent of reorientation strongly depended on exposure energy (Figure c). At fluences below ∼10 mJ/cm2, the films instead showed reorientation perpendicular to E combined with a measurable out-of-plane component (Sh = 0.4, Figure S2a,b). In contrast, increasing the fluence above 20 mJ/cm2 switched the orientation to parallel to E, accompanied by a reduction in Sh (Figures c, S2c). Robust parallel alignment with D 266 > 0.3 was consistently obtained for the fluence in the range of ∼20 and 100 mJ/cm2, suggesting that a sufficient density of photogenerated species biases molecular mobility toward in-plane reorientation parallel to E. At fluences exceeding ∼200 mJ/cm2, molecular mobility was largely suppressed owing to extensive photo-cross-linking and the resulting network formation, which prevented thermal amplification of orientation (Figure S2d). The switching mechanism has been discussed previously , and is briefly summarized here. At low exposure doses, the photoproduct concentration is insufficient to induce anchoring, and thermal reorientation is governed primarily by the liquid-crystalline characteristics of the mesogenic side chains, resulting in alignment perpendicular to the polarization direction. At higher exposure doses, the increased amount of photoproducts provides photo-cross-linked anchor to dominate the thermal reorientation, leading to alignment parallel to the polarization direction. Additionally, effective in-plane reorientation was observed when the annealing temperature was within the LC temperature range of P90 (Figure d). These behaviors are consistent with those previously reported for P50-based systems.

In Situ Condensation of Oriented PA Side Groups with Phenylamine Derivatives

Process A: We previously demonstrated that in situ condensation of PA side groups in preoriented P50 films with an AF monomer yielded oriented AF-NBA structures. Building on this concept, we applied the same strategy to P90 films by using five different phenylamine derivatives.

Figure a–e shows the polarized absorption spectra of P90 films before and after LP 313 nm exposure (50 mJ/cm2), subsequent annealing, and in situ condensation with AF, MPA, ACB, ANB, and DAF. For AF, MPA, ACB, and ANB, the spectra exhibited bathochromic shifts accompanied by strong, highly anisotropic absorption bands in the 350 nm region. The resulting D 274 (D 350) values reached 0.64 (0.75), 0.66 (0.77), 0.67 (0.75), and 0.63 (0.76), respectively, indicating efficient formation of oriented new-NBA side groups while preserving the preorganized structure. In contrast, the films treated with DAF showed markedly smaller spectral changes, with D 274 (D 350) values limited to 0.54 (0.57). One possible explanation is that the bifunctional nature of DAF may promote local network formation during the condensation reaction, thereby partial disordering the preorganized structure. However, further investigation is required to verify this mechanism.

Change in the polarized absorption spectra of P90 films before and after exposure to LP 313 nm light for 50 mJ/cm2 followed by annealing at 90 °C for 10 min and subsequent in situ condensation with (a) AF, (b) MPA, (c) ACB, (d) ANB, and (e) DAF. In situ condensation was performed by coating an oriented film with a 4 wt % phenylamine derivative solution, heating at 70 °C for 30 min, and rinsing with methanol and diethyl ether (Process A).

Polarized FT-IR spectroscopy further confirmed successful photoinduced reorientation and subsequent condensation (Figure a–f). Prior to condensation, a reoriented P90 film showed anisotropic stretching bands at 1699 cm–1 (CHO), 1600 cm–1 (Ph rings), and 1258, 1210, and 1166 cm–1 (C–O–C), each with D = 0.3 (Figure a). After condensation with AF, MPA, ACB, and ANB, the CHO stretching band disappeared and an anisotropic CN stretching band emerged at 1620 cm–1 (Figure b–e). The conversion estimated from the integrated FT-IR peak showed conversions exceeding approximately 95% for all four derivatives. Phenyl and C–O–C stretching bands displayed enhanced anisotropy (D > 0.6), reflecting robust alignment of the newly formed NBA units. Additionally, CN stretching at 2215 cm–1 for ACB and NO2 stretching at 1341 cm–1 for ANB exhibited strong anisotropy (D = 0.7). In contrast, the DAF-condensed film retained the CHO band and showed weaker anisotropy in the C–O–C region (D = 0.3, Figure f), indicating incomplete conversion (∼40%) and disruption of orientational order.

(b–f) Change in the polarized FT-IR spectra of P90 films before exposure and after exposure to LP 313 nm light for 50 mJ/cm2 followed by heating at 70 °C for 30 min and subsequent in situ condensation with (b) AF, (c) MPA, (d) ACB, (e) ANB, and (f) DAF (Process A).

In Situ Condensation before Thermal Stimulation

Process B. In situ condensation performed immediately after photoexposure allows partial transfer of photoinduced molecular orientation into the newly formed NBA units. Figure a–e show polarized absorption spectra of P90 films before and after exposure to LP 313 nm light (50 mJ/cm2), followed by condensation and then thermal annealing according to Process B.

Change in the polarized absorption spectra of P90 films before and after exposure to LP 313 nm light for 50 mJ/cm2 followed by coating with a 4 wt % phenylamine derivative solution of (a) AF, (b) MPA, (c) ACB, (d) ANB, and (e) DAF for in situ condensation at 70 °C for 30 min, and subsequent annealing at 200 °C for 10 min (Process B).

For AF, MPA, ACB, and ANB, condensation proceeded quantitatively (>95%), as evidenced by the disappearance of the CHO stretching band in polarized FT-IR spectra (Figure S3a–d). The condensation, conducted within the LC temperature range of P90, enabled partial transfer of the thermally generated orientation of PA side groups to the newly formed NBA units. Correspondingly, D 274 (D 350) values reached 0.20 (0.23), 0.19 (0.27), 0.15 (0.16), and 0.23 (0.27), respectively. FT-IR spectra also showed a small anisotropy after the condensation. Although DAF showed a lower conversion (∼15%, Figure S3e), the D 274 (D 350) values [0.16 (0.19)] were comparable to those of the other derivatives, suggesting that the condensation reaction may hinder diffusion of DAF into the inner region of the PA matrix.

Subsequent annealing at 200 °C significantly amplified the orientational structure for AF, MPA, ACB, and ANB owing to the extended LC temperature range of the condensed films. The films exhibited pronounced D 274 (D 350) values of 0.67 (0.77) for AF, 0.74 (0.83) for MPA, 0.45 (0.42) for ACB, and 0.43 (0.46) for ANB. Notably, AF- and MPA-modified films exhibited significantly larger amplification than those modified with ACB or ANB, reflecting differences in thermally induced self-organization capabilities of the respective NBA structures. These trends are consistent with reported structure–property relationships governing thermally stimulated molecular reorientation. In contrast, DAF-containing films exhibited no additional amplification due to the restricted molecular mobility.

Furthermore, when condensation was carried out prior to photoexposure, sufficient thermal self-organization was not achieved even though condensation proceeded effectively (Figure S4a–h). For AF and MPA, partial reorientation perpendicular to E occurred due to axis-selective photoreactions of the newly formed NBA groups; however, for ACB and ANB, no reorientation was observed, again reflecting the influence of terminal substituents on mesogenic mobility.

In Situ Condensation by Microwave Irradiation

Process C. Microwave-assisted in situ condensation enabled rapid and efficient formation of oriented NBA side groups without requiring a separate annealing step. Figures a–e and a–e show the polarized UV–vis absorption and FT-IR spectra of P90 films before and after LP 313 nm light exposure (50 mJ/cm2), immersion in phenylamine-containing ethylene glycol solution, and microwave irradiation for 1 min. The reaction temperature reached 140 °C within 10 s, exceeding Ti of P90, and gradually increased to 170 °C (Figure S5).

This process achieved nearly quantitative (>95%) condensation for all derivatives, including DAF, as confirmed by the disappearance of the CHO stretching band (Figure ). Moreover, the condensation was accompanied by pronounced molecular reorientation for all derivatives except DAF, where high conversion was achieved but molecular reorientation did not develop, possibly because the bifunctional nature of DAF restricted molecular mobility during the condensation process. Strong anisotropy was observed for AF, MPA, ACB, and ANB, where the D 274 (D 350) values of 0.71 (0.82), 0.75 (0.85), 0.70 (0.74), and 0.69 (0.78), respectively. These values were slightly higher than those obtained using Processes A or B. Polarized FT-IR confirmed high anisotropy in CN and C–O–C stretching bands (D = 0.65–0.85), and the CN (ACB) and NO2 (ANB) bands also displayed strong anisotropy [D 2215 (D 1341) = 0.84 (∼0.8)], indicating that terminal substituents in ACB and ANB have minimal impact on reorientation efficiency.

Microwave irradiation enhances phenylamine diffusion into the exposed P90 film due to rapid heating of the ethylene glycol medium. Because the temperature exceeds Ti of P90 within 10 s, in situ condensation proceeds rapidly before substantial disordering of the mesogenic PA side groups. At elevated temperatures (up to 170 °C), the newly formed NBA units provide sufficient mobility to reorganize into well-aligned structures. Rapid heating above Ti allows condensation and molecular reorientation to proceed before relaxation of the initial anisotropy, thereby maximizing macroscopic alignment. In contrast, for DAF, the bifunctional nature of the molecule may have restricted molecular mobility during condensation, thereby suppressing molecular reorientation despite the high condensation efficiency. This result highlights that preserving molecular mobility is essential for effective thermally stimulated reorientation.

For films prepared by Process A, Δn exceeded 0.4 for all derivatives except DAF. The highest value, Δn = 0.46, was obtained for AF, which is slightly higher than that previously obtained for P50(80), consistent with the higher PA content in P90. Although the D values for MPA-, ACB-, and ANB-modified films were comparable to that of the AF-modified film, their Δn values were somewhat lower (0.40–0.42). This difference is attributed to the higher intrinsic birefringence of the AF-derived NBA unit. The superior birefringence of AF-derived films is likely associated with the rigid and planar fluorene framework, which enhances π-conjugation along the molecular long axis and increases anisotropic polarizability. , For DAF, Δn was limited to 0.28 because of both the lower degree of molecular orientation and the lower extent of condensation. In Process B, similarly high Δn values were obtained for AF (0.46) and MPA (0.43), consistent with their relatively large D values after thermal amplification. In contrast, the Δn values for ACB-, ANB-, and DAF-modified films were much smaller (<0.3), reflecting their less efficient molecular reorientation. Films prepared by Process C gave Δn values comparable to or slightly higher than those obtained by Process A for AF, MPA, ACB, and ANB, with a maximum value of 0.47 for AF. In contrast, the DAF-modified film showed essentially no measurable birefringence. At 1550 nm, smaller Δn values were obtained for AF (0.36) and MPA (0.31), consistent with the normal dispersion of birefringence. , Overall, high Δn was achieved only when efficient in-plane molecular orientation was combined with mesogenic units having intrinsically large birefringence.

Fabrication of Thick Birefringent Films and Polarization Gratings

High-retardation birefringent films (Δnd > 600 nm, where d is the film thickness) were fabricated from 2000 nm thick P90 films using Process C. As the film thickness increases, penetration of the phenylamine derivatives into the P90 matrix becomes more difficult. Figure a shows a photograph of an oriented film placed between crossed polarizers. The retardation at 532 (1550) nm reached 625 (600) nm, corresponding to an estimated Δn of 0.32 (0.30). The lower Δn compared with that of the 180 nm thick films is likely due to limited diffusion of phenylamine derivatives into the thicker film. Although diffusion of the phenylamine derivative is considered to be one of the major factors limiting birefringence development in thick films, other factors, including concentration gradients through the film thickness and reduced molecular mobility during the modification process, may also contribute. Further optimization of the processing conditions may improve the modification uniformity of thick films.

The thick arrow indicates the orientation direction, and the crossed arrows indicate the polarization directions of the polarizers. (c, d) Diffraction of circularly polarized 1550 nm light beams from a PG when the incident beam was (c) left-handed circularly polarized (LCP) and (d) right-handed circularly polarized (RCP) 1550 nm light, respectively.

Finally, polarization gratings with a 9.5 μm pitch were successfully fabricated by polarization holography, in which the molecular orientation direction was spatially rotated (Figure b). As shown in Figure c and d, the gratings exhibited high diffraction efficiencies of 86% and 87% for left- and right-handed circularly polarized 1550 nm light, respectively. The incident circular polarization was simultaneously converted into the opposite handedness upon first-order diffraction, demonstrating the potential of this system for highly efficient near-infrared polarization optics. Based on the theoretical diffraction expression, − the birefringence-induced retardation of the PG film was estimated to be 600 nm, in good agreement with that of the corresponding birefringent film.

Reactive mesogen polarization gratings have been extensively studied in the visible wavelength region, particularly for broadband and achromatic diffraction. In contrast, experimental evaluations of polymer PGs at a 1550 nm wavelength remain very limited, especially for bulk photoaligned polymer systems. The present results therefore demonstrate the feasibility of high-efficiency PGs based on bulk photoalignment in the near-infrared region and provide a basis for extending this approach to shorter operating wavelengths, where thinner devices are expected to provide the required retardation.

Conclusion

We demonstrated that birefringence in photoalignable liquid-crystalline copolymer (PLCP) films can be effectively enhanced through in situ condensation of PA side groups with phenylamine derivatives following axis-selective photoreactions. The molecular structure of the phenylamine and the processing sequence strongly governed molecular reorientation and the resulting optical anisotropy. Microwave-assisted processing enabled rapid condensation and simultaneous molecular reorientation, yielding a maximum birefringence of Δn = 0.47 for AF, whereas DAF exhibited suppressed molecular alignment, probably because of its bifunctional nature. The method was further extended to thick films, producing high-retardation transparent films and highly efficient polarization gratings exhibiting diffraction efficiencies up to 87% at 1550 nm. These findings provide a versatile strategy for fabricating highly birefringent photoalignable polymer films for advanced polarization optics and photonic applications.
