Trends in Dementia Associated Mortality among Patients with Underlying Stroke: A Nationwide Retrospective Analysis (1999–2023)
Authors: Syed Tawassul Hassan, Syeda Lyba Onaiz, Shehdev Meghwar, Fatima Kaleem Ahmed, Mirza Shaheer Hyder, Syeda Kashaf Batool, Abdirizak Mohamud Yusuf
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionDementia contributes significantly to morbidity and mortality associated with stroke, which has been a leading cause of death and disability in the U.S. We aim to determine the stroke mortality trends in the older population with dementia in the U.S from 1999 to 2023.MethodsCenters for Disease Control and Prevention’s Wide-ranging Online Data for Epidemiologic Research (CDC WONDER) database was utilized to analyze the death records of older patients (aged ≥ 65 years) listing stroke as the underlying cause and dementia as the contributing cause of death. Age-adjusted mortality rates were computed per 100,000 population, and their variations over time were estimated as annual percent changes (APC).ResultsA total of 259,607 deaths occurred from stroke in older patients with dementia, with a 59% decrease in AAMR from 1999 (28.09) to 2023 (16.51). A stark rise in rates occurred from 1999 to 2001 (APC: 21.25; P < 0.05), followed by continuous declines until 2023. AAMRs were higher in females (26.3) than in males (21.9) and peaked in NH African Americans (29.5) and older adults aged 85 + years (114.73). Further rates peaked among non-metropolitan residents, the west (26.9), and the south regions (26.8).ConclusionDespite the decline in the dementia related mortality among older people with underlying stroke, the burden is still considerable and disproportionately affects females, NH African Americans, and non-metropolitan areas, therefore necessitating stroke-informed dementia care, better risk stratification, and even distribution of resources.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s44197-025-00500-z.

Introduction

The stroke has been a major cause of mortality and disability in the U.S., affecting 133,000 people in the country, which is approximately 1 in 20 deaths nationwide [1]. However, older patients with underlying stroke often develop dementia, which increases their mortality and morbidity risk [2, 3]. It has been estimated that pre-stroke cognitive deficit or dementia affects as many as 30–40% of all older adults (≥ 65 years) with stroke, making dementia one of the most prevalent comorbidities in this age group [2, 3]. Further dementia-stroke co-occurrence yields unprecedented levels of poor outcomes compared to stroke patients with no dementia; older adults with both conditions have a higher likelihood of developing recurrent stroke, worse disability, and higher mortality rates [3].

Dementia worsens stroke outcomes through mechanisms including reduced neurological reserve, the delayed diagnosis of symptoms such as cerebral injury, increased frailty burden, and lower likelihood of receiving aggressive interventions, including reperfusion therapy, promptly [3, 4]. The combined effects of these factors then lead to poor functional recovery and mortality outcomes among stroke survivors who have pre-existing dementia [3, 4].

Although there is extensive research on mortality due to stroke in general, very little research has explored national surveillance studies to interrogate the mortality rates in people with both stroke and dementia. So far, no investigation at the national level has addressed all of these variables simultaneously: a stroke mortality pattern stratified by dementia status, by demographic or geographic subgroups. This gap is significant, as individuals with dementia follow distinct clinical trajectories and may be disproportionately affected by changes in healthcare delivery, population aging, and regional disparities. To address this gap, we aimed to investigate the national mortality trends of stroke among the elderly with dementia in the U.S., based on CDC mortality data. By examining mortality burden and trend changes, we explicitly hypothesized that stroke-related mortality among adults with dementia differs significantly across sex, race/ethnicity, age groups, and U.S. geographic region. Our work will inform equitable health policies, direct dementia-sensitive stroke care, and predict the increasing influence of dementia on stroke mortality in an aging population [5]

Methods

Study Setting and Population

We reviewed U.S mortality data on older adults (≥ 65 years) from 1999 to 2023 using the CDC WONDER multiple causes of death database. Records were included where stroke was the underlying cause of death and dementia appeared anywhere among contributing causes of death. The WONDER database is a publicly accessible system that provides mortality and population data covering all 50 states and the District of Columbia in the U.S [6]. The age threshold of ≥ 65 years (older adults) was set for the analysis as it follows the prior CDC/NCHS dementia mortality surveillance and population-based studies, given that the case counts below this threshold were very low, which might dilute the overall trends [7, 8]. The included records were identified by the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes (I: 60–64) for stroke and (F: 01, 03, G: 30, 31) for dementia. The stroke codes were intentionally restricted to I60-I64, which includes (subarachnoid hemorrhage, intracerebral hemorrhage, other non-traumatic intracranial hemorrhage, cerebral infarction, and stroke not specified), relevant to stroke mortality, while the codes I67 (other cerebrovascular diseases) and I69 (sequelae of cerebrovascular disease) were excluded as they might introduce etiologic heterogeneity and do not represent stroke mortality. However, the ICD-10 codes I60-I64 maximize positive predictive value (PPV) for acute stroke events and minimize misclassification from chronic cerebrovascular diseases or sequelae as recommended by the prior validity studies [9–11]. The study was exempted from the institutional review board (IRB) approval following the open-access nature of the database. Moreover, the study strictly followed Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [12].

Data Extraction

Datasets were stratified on the basis of population size, demographics (sex, race/ethnicity, age groups, and place of death), and geographics (urbanization, census regions, and states), with an age interval (65–85 + years) applied across all the stratifications following the inclusion of older adults. Race and ethnicities were defined as NH White, NH African American, NH Asian or Pacific Islander, and NH American Indian. Hispanic ethnicity was treated as a mutually exclusive category consistent with the database, where ethnicity is coded independently of race populations. Places of death were recorded as medical facilities, hospice care, nursing/long-term care, and the decedent’s home, and the NCHS Urban-Rural Classification Scheme for Counties was followed in terms of urbanization level stratification [13]. Unknown category for both places of deaths and urbanization, representing < 1% of total deaths, were excluded because this field was inconsistent and intermittently missing across years, precluding stable trend analysis. Further, the Northeast, South, Midwest, and West regions were evaluated according to the U.S Census Bureau definitions.

Statistical Analysis

The outcome measure AAMR was standardized to the 2000 U.S. population year and was computed per 100,000 older population for each stratification with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) [14]. These AAMRs were then analyzed using joinpoint regression software (Version 5.0, National Cancer Institute) to identify temporal trends in stroke mortality among older patients with dementia [15]. The computer program uses log-linear regression equations to identify drastic changes in the trends of mortality. It adds joinpoints step by step to depict the points where trend shifts occur. Annual percent changes (APCs) with 95% CIs were also calculated from log-transformed AAMRs. Two-sided t-tests were used to assess statistical significance at 0.05. Data-driven weighted Bayesian information criterion (BIC) was used to select models, allowing a maximum of 4 joinpoints. These model selection parameters were uniformly applied across all strata. A heteroscedastic error structure and weighted least squares estimation were used in the analysis. The minimum number of observations allowed between joinpoints was set to 2 years.

Results

Proportional Mortality Rates Distribution (1999–2023)

A total of 259,607 deaths occurred from stroke in older patients (aged ≥ 65 years) with dementia. Among demographics, females (67.5%), NH Whites (83.75%), individuals aged 85+ (61.06%), and nursing homes (51.1%) accounted for the majority of deaths. However, metropolitan areas (78.68%) and the south region (37.93%) represented the highest mortalities among geographics. These proportional mortality figures represent the distribution of deaths rather than AAMRs, which are presented in the subsequent trend analyses (Supplementary Fig. 1) (Supplemental Table 1).

Overall and Sex Specific Trends in Stroke Mortality among Older Patients with Dementia in the United States, 1999 to 2023

The overall AAMR standardized to the 2000 U.S. population decreased by 59% from 1999 (28.09) to 2023 (16.51). However, a significant increase in mortality rates was observed from 1999 to 2001 (APC: 21.25*; 95% CI: 6.18 to 38.45) followed by significant decline through 2006 (APC: −10.62*; 95% CI: −14.18 to −6.91) which continued until 2023 (APC: −2.41*; 95% CI: −2.91 to −1.91). Age Analysis

Adults aged 85+ (114.73) had ninefold the AAMR recorded in 75–84 (23.49) and 65–74-year (3.06) age groups. Mortality rates increased sharply from 1999 to 2001 across 85 + and 75–84-year age groups, followed by significant declines through 2006 and sustained decreases thereafter, with associated APCs of 22.52*, −10.49*, and − 2.45* in 85 + and 19.12*, −10.24*, and − 2.64* in 75–84-year age groups, respectively. However, individuals aged 65–74 years showed a significant decline in AAMR from 2001 to 2009 (APC: −9.49*; 95% CI: −12.29 to −6.61) after which the rates gained stability (Fig. 2) (Supplemental Tables 3, 4).

Race/Ethnicity Analysis

NH African Americans (29.5) recorded highest AAMR among other racial/ethnic groups (NH White: 25.2; Hispanic: 17.1; NH Asian: 16.8). NH Whites showed a sharp rise in mortality rates from 1999 to 2001 (APC: 20.31*; 95% CI: 6.85 to 35.46) followed by a significant decline through 2006 (APC: −10.41*; 95% CI: −13.65 to −7.06) and continued gradual reductions until 2023 (APC: −2.44*; 95% CI: −2.91 to −1.98). The AAMR declined significantly from 2001 to 2007 in NH Asians (APC: −12.87*; 95% CI: −19.22 to −6.02), followed by modest reductions till 2023 (APC: −1.69*; 95% CI: −2.84 to −0.54). Further, both NH African Americans and Hispanics experienced significant declines after 2001 onwards (APCs: −6.51* and − 5.31* respectively) with rates gaining stability in the subsequent years (Fig. 3) (Supplemental Tables 3, 5).

Regional Disparities

Nationwide mortality differences were observed, with superior 90th percentile states (Washington, Oregon, South Carolina, Tennessee, and Oklahoma; AAMR range: 35.3 to 39.4) having twice the average AAMR of lower 10th percentile states (Nevada, Arizona, New York, New Jersey, and Louisiana; AAMR range: 12.6 to 16.5). West (26.9) and South (26.8) regions had the highest rates, with lesser occurring in the Midwest (26.0) and Northeast (18.6). Discussion

Our study analyzed 25 years (1999–2023) of trends in dementia associated mortality among patients with underlying stroke in the U.S. using the CDC WONDER database. Firstly, this study demonstrates a substantial overall decline in mortality reflecting improvements in stroke care and secondary prevention. Secondly, significant differences were observed across various demographic and geographic populations, with females, NH African Americans, the oldest age groups, and non-metropolitan regions having increased mortality burden compared to other groups.

There is a substantial total reduction in dementia mortality among older adults with underlying stroke, where AAMRs have reduced by 59%. However, it is essential to address that mortality trends do not indicate underlying disease risk, since they may be influenced by variations in diagnostic techniques and survival rather than genuine epidemiological disparities. The same trends were represented by the national statistics, reporting a decrease in stroke mortality and increased post-stroke survival. Further, a 21.6% decrease in age-adjusted stroke mortality was observed by Wu et al., marking a 21.6% reduction between 2007 and 2016 and a growing number of deaths related to dementia [16]. Shoaib et al. also found a steady increase in dementia-related mortality, particularly among females, NH Whites, and those aged 85 and above, with the highest rates in the southern U.S [17]. The observed decrease in dementia-associated mortality among patients with underlying stroke may possibly be influenced by a combination of advances in acute stroke care, improved secondary prevention, and more effective management of vascular risk factors. The American Heart Association and American Stroke Association emphasize that better identification and treatment of vascular risk factors, especially hypertension, are central to reducing both stroke and dementia risk [18]. Population data link declines in stroke incidence and mortality to improved risk factor control, greater use of reperfusion therapies, and advances in post-acute care [19]. Intensive blood pressure control, as demonstrated in the SPRINT-MIND sub-study, lowers the incidence of mild cognitive impairment and dementia, and is currently the most evidence-based intervention for dementia prevention in this population [20]. Additionally, there is evidence that the incidence of vascular dementia has declined more rapidly than that of Alzheimer’s disease, likely due to these vascular interventions [21]. Our results also demonstrated a deviation from the overall declining trend, which was observed as a spike in AAMR values from 1999 to 2001, followed by a subsequent decline. The observed deviation cannot be explained by the present data. One hypothesis, supported by prior literature, is that advances in acute stroke management during this time period may have improved post-stroke survival, increasing the number of people at risk of further dementia-related mortality; however, this explanation is purely hypothetical and cannot be validated using mortality-only data [22].

The proportionate mortality and AAMRs were greater in females than in males. The observed variations in AAMRs should be viewed as descriptive epidemiologic patterns reflecting population-level variation rather than as evidence of direct causal relationships. The results are consistent with the previous reports that indicate that prevalence and mortality rates of dementia are higher in women, probably because of the longer lifespan, increased dementia risk, and frailty [21]. According to Sherzai et al., there is a positive correlation between female gender and the probability of having dementia among stroke patients [23],. Further, the greater mortality burden in women may reflect underlying differences in population characteristics, social support, or care environments, as suggested by prior literature, rather than directly measured factors [24].

Adults 85 and older had the highest mortality rates due to the exponential increase in stroke and dementia risk. In dementia-related stroke, age is the strongest predictor of mortality, with risk rising sharply in the elderly. This is driven by more severe strokes, higher comorbidity burden, cognitive decline, and frailty, all of which worsen outcomes [25]. Su et al. found that stroke patients with dementia had a 3.74 times higher risk of death compared to those without dementia, with one-year mortality of 17.3% for patients with dementia compared to 5.4% for those without [26]. The risk of post-stroke dementia and death is also determined by stroke severity, recurrence, and lesion site, with older adults having more severe strokes and being less likely to recover to full functional independence [27].

AAMRs for dementia-associated stroke have consistently been highest among NH African Americans, followed by NH Whites, Hispanics, and NH Asians. Although absolute AAMRs declined across all racial groups, the relative differences persisted, indicating that disparities have remained largely stable over the period analyzed [28]. This pattern is consistent with findings from Sherzai et al., who reported NH African American individuals had significantly higher odds of both Alzheimer’s dementia (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] 1.25, 95% CI 1.18–1.32) and vascular dementia (aOR 1.51, 95% CI 1.40–1.64) compared to NH White individuals [23]. The AHA reported increased prevalence of dementia among NH African American and Hispanic populations, attributing 40% of the cases to modifiable vascular risk factors that are higher than those in NH White and Asian groups [21]. Educational improvements and risk factor control might have contributed to the overall declines in dementia mortality, but an increase in hypertension, diabetes, and socioeconomical inequities, including poverty, housing instability, and limited health care access, may be associated with observed disparities among racial groups. These associations are presented as plausible explanations, not directly measured in our dataset [29, 30].

In terms of urbanization, non-metropolitan regions had the highest AAMR values, which can be linked to a lack of sophisticated medical care services in such areas, contributing to higher death rates [31]. Furthermore, residents in rural regions suffer obstacles in education, economic status, and transportation when compared to their metropolitan counterparts [32]. Vestergaard et al. found that socioeconomic disparities in poststroke dementia rates were most pronounced among immigrants who had less access to proper healthcare [33]. Telemedicine services have greatly contributed to the availability of medical services in rural areas; however, these gains have been compromised by the shutdown of rural hospitals [34]. To decrease the health disparities between metropolitan and non-metropolitan communities, better treatment, community awareness, and research on specific regions are required.

Nationwide reporting indicates that there are substantial disparities in AAMRs of stroke related to dementia. The state-level variation rates were the highest in Washington, Oregon, South Carolina, Tennessee, and Oklahoma. A possible hypothesis for this gap is the unequal distribution of insurance facilities, which has been documented in all states in the United States. As a result, policies supporting Medicaid expansion and more equal coverage for all patients could help close the disparity gap. Regional variation was also pronounced with South and West reporting higher AAMRs than Northeast and Midwest. Prior literature suggests that population-level differences in comorbidity burden, socioeconomic status, and healthcare access may contribute to these regional patterns [13, 35]. The burden is more obvious among older persons and minority populations, who have seen less deceleration in mortality rates than their counterparts in coastal and northeastern regions [35, 36].

Dementia-related mortality among stroke patients in the U. S. is a serious and rising public health concern. Despite overall improvements in stroke mortality, the absolute burden of dementia-related deaths is increasing, driven by population ageing and the strong causal link between stroke severity, recurrence, and subsequent cognitive decline. Dementia is an independent predictor of decreased survival after stroke, with afflicted individuals having up to a fourfold higher risk of mortality and significantly shorter survival times than those without dementia [16].

Study Limitations

The CDC WONDER data on the death certificates used in this study do provide the national coverage, yet it may have limitations related to underreporting and miscoding of dementia, as the documentation across the hospitals, nursing homes, states, and even patient attributes like race, age, or comorbidity burden might differ, and thus cause misclassification or underestimation in some subgroups. The comparability might have been compromised by changes in the practice of coding, including ICD-9 to ICD-10 transitioning and differences among hospital or state procedures. The ICD-10 codes cannot be reliably used to differentiate types of dementia and 25-year shifts in dementia diagnosis, and coding may artificially change reported trends in mortality. The database does not contain clinical data, including dementia severity, comorbidities, or life expectancy, so it is not possible to adjust for variables such as hypertension, diabetes, or cardiovascular disease, which have both an effect on stroke and dementia mortality. Further low population numbers within specific geographic units or subsets (e.g., there are some race/ethnicity or non-metropolitan strata) are likely to result in unstable estimates. Joinpoint did not use Bonferroni correction; 95% confidence intervals are reported to indicate precision of the APCs. Joinpoint does not explicitly test heteroscedasticity, which is a known software limitation. The ecological retrospective design rules out causality, and the correlations observed between demographic or geographic subgroups cannot be inferred to risk at an individual level; thus, there is a risk of ecological fallacy. Thus, any mortality differences must be viewed as descriptive epidemiologic trends, and subgroup differences as hypothesis-generating, and not necessarily final measures of risk. Precision is put into perspective by the counts given in the supplemental tables.

Conclusions

We observed a significant reduction in dementia related mortality among stroke patients during the course of our study. NH African Americans, females, Southerners, and non-metropolitan residents experienced the highest mortality rates. The existence of persistent race/ethnic, sex, age, and geographic gaps emphasize the importance of developing community-based programs to enhance prevention, early-stage diagnosis, and equitable access to dementia and stroke treatment, particularly among high-risk populations. More studies, such as meta-analyses and studies done on an individual level, are necessary to establish the causes of such disparities and the formulation of specific therapies that can reduce the burden of dementia-related mortality in stroke patients.
