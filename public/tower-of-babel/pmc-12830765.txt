Computational analysis and modeling of climate impact on Pteridium aquilinum (L.) populations
Authors: Masoud Sheidai, Maedeh Alaeifar, Fahimeh Koohdar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPteridium aquilinum is a medicinally important fern with a limited range in northern Iran, increasingly threatened by climate change. Using morphological, genetic, and environmental data, we assessed differentiation, adaptive capacity, and vulnerability across 11 populations. Factor analysis of mixed data (FAMD) identified stipe indument, pinnule shape, and pinnae number as key traits distinguishing populations. Redundancy and association analyses (RDA/CCA) revealed strong links between both morphological and genetic variation and climatic gradients, particularly temperature and humidity, indicating local adaptation. Several SCoT loci were detected as adaptive outliers. Spatial PCA showed that variation is shaped by both global and local spatial factors, forming clines and local variants. Populations varied in sensitivity and adaptive capacity; populations 2, 3, 7, and 8 exhibited the lowest adaptive indices and highest vulnerability. Connectivity modeling suggested that while some populations (e.g., 2, 4, and 6) may maintain or slightly improve connectivity, others risk isolation under future climates. Structural equation modeling (SEM) indicated a positive genetic contribution to adaptation, while differential equation modeling (DEM) predicted logistic growth with temporary instability and genetic decline in vulnerable groups. Overall, findings highlight spatially uneven adaptive responses and recommend targeted conservation through connectivity enhancement, assisted gene flow, and ex-situ preservation of adaptive genotypes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-33035-1.Keywords: Multivariate statistics, Statistical modelling, SCoT markers, Morphometric, Genetic markersSubject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Genetics, Plant sciences

Introduction

Adverse effects of climate change, encompassing phenomena such as increased mean temperatures, altered precipitation regimes, and more frequent extreme weather events, represent one of the primary global threats to the extinction of plant species1. These climatic shifts directly challenge species by disrupting the crucial relationship between genotypes and their environment. Depending on the species’ adaptive capacity, populations may respond by migrating to suitable habitats, undergoing phenotypic adjustments through plasticity or evolutionary changes, or facing localized extinction if adaptation is insufficient. Studies suggest that evolving phenotypic plasticity plays a prominent role in adaptive responses to climatic gradients, with divergence in environmentally responsive loci helping populations persist under changing conditions2,3. Moreover, genome-level plasticity, such as epigenetic modifications and structural variation, can generate additional genetic variation and enhance adaptive potential under rapid climate change4. Empirical evidence further supports these mechanisms: for example, in Populus trichocarpa, significant phenotypic plasticity related to growth and phenology has been observed under temporal environmental fluctuations. However, adaptive capacity is not unlimited: constraints such as habitat fragmentation, limited dispersal, and low gene flow may prevent migration or in situ adaptation5.

The urgency is particularly high for taxa with restricted geographical distributions, which often lack connectivity and adaptive capacity to track shifting suitable conditions6. Therefore, conservation programs aimed at preserving these vulnerable taxa are of paramount importance. To formulate truly effective and predictive strategies, a thorough knowledge of a species’ intrinsic adaptive capacity, genetic structure, ecological sensitivity, and landscape connectivity is necessary7,8. This need is particularly acute given that global assessments often overlook subpopulation vulnerabilities within widespread species9. Furthermore, advanced ecological and genetic modeling techniques, such as Differential Equation Modeling (DEM), are essential for quantifying the potential time-dependent impacts of climate change on population dynamics and genetic viability, thereby significantly informing proactive conservation planning for a particular species10.

Ferns are the second-largest group of vascular plants after angiosperms, contributing widely to global biodiversity with approximately 12,000 species11. These vascular plants, which reproduce via wind-borne spores, are widely distributed globally but show high sensitivity to environmental factors11,12. Abiotic factors are key drivers of fern species distribution at all scales13. Their dispersal is often more influenced by environmental conditions, such as water availability, light exposure, and soil properties, than by geographic distance12,14. This close relationship between ferns and their environment makes them valuable bioindicators. Given the strong influence of environmental factors on fern distribution, it is important to consider how these conditions may shape genetic differentiation across landscapes, a concept central to Isolation by Environment (IBE)15.

The Bracken genus (Pteridium) is a globally widespread group of coarse, deciduous ferns (family Dennstaedtiaceae) found across all continents except Antarctica, noted for having the widest distribution of any fern in the world11,12. Within this cosmopolitan group, P. aquilinum is the most recognized species, typically occupying temperate and subtropical regions.

Consequently, in global conservation assessments, this species is typically classified as Least Concern (LC) by global entities, such as the IUCN Red List, due to its ubiquitous presence and high ecological resilience16. In fact, due to its robust rhizomatous growth and allelopathic properties, P. aquilinum is often recognized internationally, and in regional zones like the Hyrcanian forests of Iran, as an aggressive weed that competes intensely with native flora and poses a management challenge in agricultural and pastoral landscapes17. However, this broad-scale resilience often masks critical local vulnerabilities. Despite the species’ overall success, studies focusing on peripheral or fragmented populations, such as those found across Iran’s geographical range, emphasize that local populations frequently exhibit significant genetic structure, differential adaptive capacity, and sensitivity to environmental shifts15. Therefore, while the species is not globally threatened, evaluating the long-term persistence and genetic viability of isolated populations in response to climate change is a critical conservation priority to prevent local extirpations and the loss of regional genetic diversity.

Such locally confined populations may face increased sensitivity to climatic variations, potentially experiencing demographic decline or even local extirpation, despite the species’ overall global security18. Therefore, predicting the persistence and resilience of these populations is crucial for effective conservation planning. This requires an assessment of their vulnerability, which represents the combined effect of a population’s sensitivity (its biological response to environmental conditions) and its exposure (the degree of climatic variation it experiences)18. Central to resilience is adaptive capacity, defined as the potential of species or populations to tolerate or adjust to change through mechanisms such as phenotypic plasticity19,20. This capacity is shaped by the interplay of intrinsic traits and extrinsic factors such as habitat fragmentation, which can constrain gene flow and reduce evolutionary potential19–21.

Landscape genetic studies are essential for understanding how spatial environmental variables influence the genetic structure of fern species, including P. aquilinum22,23. These studies can reveal patterns of Isolation by Distance (IBD) and Isolation by Environment (IBE), gene flow, and local adaptation. The Landscape Adaptive Index (LAI) is a key tool for assessing a species’ adaptability and resilience to environmental changes such as temperature and precipitation variability24,25. Adaptive capacity, influenced by phenotypic and genetic diversity, is a crucial component of a population’s response to environmental change18. Phenotypic plasticity, characterized by reaction norms, describes how a phenotype responds to environmental variation26,27. Investigating these responses is important for predicting population distribution and conservation status under future environmental scenarios28.

This research addresses a critical gap by providing the first integrative assessment of landscape genetics, phenotypic plasticity, and adaptive capacity in Iranian populations of P. aquilinum. The species was selected due to its medically significant status and high vulnerability as a fragmented taxon at the edge of its distribution range in Iran, making it an ideal model for studying climate change resilience in marginal populations. Crucially, our study moves beyond standard genetic approaches by employing a novel, multi-layered computational framework combining multivariate statistical methods (FAMD, sPCA, RDA, CCA) with advanced modeling (PLS-SEM and DEM). This robust approach allows us to not only quantify the effects of geographical and climatic factors on gene flow and adaptive traits but also to unravel the complex interplay that determines population survival. By establishing the Landscape Adaptive Index (LAI) and quantifying risk scores, we deliver specific, actionable conservation recommendations—a direct and non-generic outcome—that are vital for preserving this vulnerable species and serve as a crucial template for managing other rare, climate-sensitive ferns in the region.

The main objectives of this study are to provide a comprehensive assessment of eleven geographically isolated populations of P. aquilinum in Iran, focusing on their genetic connectivity, adaptive and landscape adaptive index, and phenotypic and genetic plasticity. Additionally, we aim to identify key morphological and genetic traits contributing to population differentiation, evaluate the potential impacts of environmental and climatic variables, and propose targeted conservation strategies to enhance the resilience and long-term survival of these rare and vulnerable populations.

Materials and methods

The fern P. aquilinum was chosen as the focal species for this landscape genetics and adaptive capacity study for three critical and interconnected reasons. Firstly, its vulnerable and highly fragmented distribution in Iran, primarily restricted to isolated populations in the northern regions, makes it an excellent sentinel species for assessing the impacts of climate change and habitat loss on marginal populations15. Secondly, the species holds significant ethnomedicinal and ecological value in the region, underscoring the urgent need for robust conservation planning to prevent local extinction and the loss of genetic resources29. Finally, as a widespread species in the world with a high capacity for phenotypic plasticity, P. aquilinum serves as an ideal model organism for employing multivariate computational modeling (such as FAMD and PLS-SEM) to unravel the complex interplay between geographical barriers, climatic variables, and local adaptation mechanisms23.

Plant material

We examined 133 plants belonging to 11 geographical populations of P. aquilinum from the northern provinces of Iran (Fig. 1). Within each population, individuals were randomly sampled, maintaining a minimum distance of approximately 10–15 m between sampled plants to ensure genetic independence. The geographical and ecological characteristics of the eleven P. aquilinum populations are provided in Supplementary Table S1. Samples were collected from the Hyrcanian forests in the northern provinces of Gilan and Mazandaran, Iran. This region is characterized by a humid temperate climate with high annual precipitation (ranging from 1,200 to 2,000 mm) and mild temperatures, supporting dense broadleaf deciduous forests30,31.

Distribution map of Pteridium aquilinum.

The study was conducted in Iran, covering populations across diverse ecological zones. Eleven populations were selected to represent the full geographical and environmental range of P. aquilinum in northern Iran. Selection criteria included variation in altitude, climatic conditions, habitat type, and accessibility for sampling. To ensure representativeness, we sampled individuals randomly within each population and recorded ecological variables such as altitude.

Genetic analysis

We used SCoT molecular markers for our genetic study, details of this have been given before32. Genomic DNA was extracted using the CTAB method, and DNA quality was assessed using gel electrophoresis. PCR amplification conditions and primers (SCoT1, SCoT2, SCoT41) were carefully optimized to ensure reproducible results, and clear, reproducible bands were scored as binary data. Genetic diversity and structure data, including results from AMOVA, UPGMA, DAPC, and LEA, were obtained from our previous study32 and subsequently used in the current analyses to explore population differentiation and adaptation. Full methodological details are provided in Alaeifar et al.32. Raw data were checked for missing values and scored consistently, and any ambiguous bands were excluded from further analyses.

Data analyses

Multicollinearity test

The multicollinearity of the studied variables was checked by using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) analysis in the ‘car’ package of R 4.3. A VIF value > 10 indicates multicollinearity issues33. Prior to multicollinearity testing, all variables were standardized to ensure comparability, and any highly correlated variables were carefully examined. Variables with VIF > 10 were either removed or combined to avoid redundancy in subsequent analyses. This step ensures the reliability of regression-based models such as RDA and RRMM.

This procedure was performed to ensure that the predictor variables included in the RDA and RRMM models were statistically independent, thereby allowing these models to accurately evaluate the relationships addressed in the research questions.

Population differences in morphological characteristics

Factor Analysis of Mixed Data (FAMD) was performed on normalized morphological traits among P. aquilinum populations to assess the contribution of individual characteristics to geographic population differentiation. FAMD is specifically designed for datasets that contain both quantitative and qualitative variables and enables the extraction of shared structure while preserving the integrity of mixed data types34,35. Prior to analysis, all continuous variables were standardized to zero mean and unit variance, and the dataset was screened for outliers to ensure analytical robustness following recommended procedures for multivariate analyses36. The R packages FactoMineR and factoextra were used to implement the analysis.

The number of retained dimensions was determined based on eigenvalues greater than 1 and examination of the cumulative proportion of explained variance, consistent with best practices in multivariate ordination37. Interpretation of FAMD dimensions was informed by variable loadings to identify traits with the strongest contribution to population differentiation. This approach provides a comprehensive assessment of morphological divergence across populations while accommodating the mixed nature of the dataset.

This analysis was conducted to address the research question regarding the extent to which morphological traits contribute to geographic population differentiation.

Landscape genetics

Association studies

We used Redundancy Analysis (RDA) and Canonical Correspondence Analysis (CCA) to assess the association between morphological traits and genetic loci with environmental and climatic variables. These constrained ordination methods are widely applied in ecological genomics because they enable direct quantification of how environmental gradients influence phenotypic and genetic variation38,39.

All analyses were performed in PAST 4.3 using 999 permutations to assess model significance. Prior to analysis, environmental predictors were standardized, screened for multicollinearity using Variance Inflation Factors (VIF < 10), and missing values were imputed following standard ecological statistics procedures40. RDA and CCA were selected because they are robust tools for partitioning variance attributable to environmental drivers, allowing identification of key climatic and ecological variables shaping morphological and genetic differentiation.

These analyses were conducted to address the research question concerning how environmental and climatic gradients contribute to patterns of morphological and genetic differentiation among populations.

Spatial principal components analysis (sPCA)

We performed sPCA to reveal the spatial structuring of genetic and morphological characteristics of P. aquilinum populations using the Adegenet package in R 4.341. This multivariate statistical approach produces estimates of genetic or morphological variation and the spatial structures of the studied populations41.

sPCA was selected due to its ability to integrate spatial information into the analysis of genetic and morphological variation. Prior to sPCA, data were centered and scaled to ensure comparability. The significance of global and local structures was evaluated using Monte Carlo tests provided in Adegenet. Mantel tests and Moran’s I were applied to quantify spatial autocorrelation, with careful interpretation to avoid overestimating the effect of spatial proximity. This workflow ensures that both global trends and local spatial patterns are robustly detected and biologically meaningful.

This analysis was conducted to address the research question related to identifying spatial patterns underlying morphological and genetic variation across populations.

Identification of potentially adaptive outlier genetic loci

To identify genetic loci potentially associated with environmental, spatial, and climatic gradients, we applied a multi-method analytical framework incorporating complementary statistical approaches. Using multiple algorithms improves the reliability of detecting adaptive signals because each method operates under different assumptions regarding population structure and selection processes42,43. In addition to RDA, CCA, and sPCA, we employed pcadapt and DAPC (Discriminant Analysis of Principal Components), enabling the detection of candidate loci based on both genotype–environment associations and population-level differentiation.

Prior to all analyses, genetic data underwent standard quality control, including filtering for missing data, retaining loci with minor allele frequency > 0.05, and removing loci exhibiting strong linkage disequilibrium to reduce false positives44. Analyses were conducted using the Adegenet and LEA packages in R 4.3. Outlier loci identified by different methods were subsequently compared and integrated using a Venn diagram (VennDiagram package in R), and only loci supported by at least two independent approaches were retained as robust candidate adaptive markers. This integrative strategy allowed for increased confidence in distinguishing true adaptive loci from statistical artifacts.

These analyses were conducted to address the research question concerning the identification of genetic loci potentially under selection and associated with environmental and spatial gradients.

Phenotypic and genetic plasticity analysis

The phenotypic plasticity of morphological and genetic data (genetic diversity and Fst values) across geographical populations was determined using the R packages ggplot2 and ggfortify. Prior to analysis, all traits were standardized and checked for outliers. Genetic diversity metrics were calculated according to standard protocols, and Fst values were computed to quantify population differentiation.

Additionally, we used the Random Regression Mixed Model (RRMM) approach to describe population-level responses and variation among individuals26 using the lme4 package in R 4.3. This model was chosen because it allows incorporation of covariates as fixed or random effects and estimates genotype-specific intercepts and slopes, enabling assessment of reaction norms across environments45. Model assumptions, including normality of residuals and independence of random effects, were checked, and model fit was evaluated using AIC test.

The MCMCglmm and lme4 packages of R 4.3 were used for these analyses. MCMCglmm fits multivariate generalized linear mixed models using Markov Chain Monte Carlo techniques, providing robust estimates of variance-covariance structures among traits46. Priors were carefully selected, and convergence diagnostics were performed to ensure reliable parameter estimation.

These analyses were conducted to address the research question regarding the extent and patterns of phenotypic and genetic plasticity among populations, and how individual and population-level variation respond to environmental gradients.

Populations’ ecological tolerance and adaptive capacity

Ecological tolerance

Ecological tolerance for each population was quantified as the mean of two components: Environmental Tolerance and Climate Tolerance, following standard approaches used in ecological niche theory and climate–trait variation studies47,48.

Environmental Tolerance was calculated based on the range or standard deviation of key geographical variables (longitude, latitude, and altitude) across the sampled distribution of each population. These variables describe spatial heterogeneity and are commonly used as proxies for environmental gradients experienced by natural populations.

Climate Tolerance was estimated using the range or standard deviation of essential climatic variables, including temperature, precipitation, and humidity. These climatic descriptors represent major abiotic drivers influencing ecological and physiological limits of plant species49.

All variables were standardized prior to analysis to ensure comparability across different units of measurement, and the dataset was screened for outliers to reduce potential bias. This combined approach provides an integrated estimate of each population’s tolerance breadth by capturing the variability of both geographic and climatic conditions within its distribution.

These calculations were conducted to address the research question regarding the ecological tolerance of populations and their capacity to persist under varying environmental and climatic conditions.

Predicting plant traits in response to climate change

We performed a random regression mixed model (RRMM) analysis to illustrate the phenotypic changes of populations in response to climate change. This method was chosen because it allows estimation of population-level variation in trait responses across environmental and climatic gradients, while quantifying both phenotypic plasticity and genetic differentiation in response to climate variables26,45.

RRMM incorporates spatial and temporal environmental data, enabling predictions of population resilience under future climate scenarios54. Prior to modeling, trait data were standardized, checked for outliers, and assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variances were verified. Model fit was evaluated using AIC test to ensure robustness of predictions.

This analysis was conducted to address the research question regarding the expected phenotypic responses of populations to projected climate change, allowing assessment of population resilience and trait variation under future environmental conditions.

Modeling the effects of climate change on population connectivity

We used two complementary models to study the impact of climate change on P. aquilinum populations’ connectivity:

1-Climate resistance model.

2-Genetic connectivity model55,56.

The change in genetic connectivity of P. aquilinum populations due to climate change was assessed as a quantitative numerical index. This index was derived using Least Cost Paths and genetic resistance surfaces among populations, which identify the least-cost routes for gene flow and the likelihood of allele transfer between neighboring populations55,56. Input data included the population resistance matrix and climatic resistance layers, and all calculations were performed in R using the raster and terra packages. Results are reported as numerical values of connectivity change for each population, and maps and plots (including Least Cost Path maps, Genetic Corridors, and population connectivity change plots) illustrate the magnitude of increase or decrease in connectivity.

Climate resistance model

Bioclimatic variables were obtained from WorldClim v2.157 at a spatial resolution of 30 arc-seconds (~ 1 km), including key descriptors such as mean annual temperature, temperature seasonality, and annual precipitation. Future climate projections for the year 2050 were derived from downscaled outputs of multiple General Circulation Models (GCMs) under the RCP 4.5 scenario, consistent with widely used climate-impact modeling approaches1,57.

For each population, relevant climatic variables (temperature-, precipitation-, and humidity-related proxies where available) were extracted using precise geographic coordinates. All raster layers were resampled to a common projection, extent, and resolution and were normalized using z-score standardization to ensure equal weighting of variables. Variable selection followed ecological criteria and statistical screening, including Pearson correlation analysis (|r|< 0.7) to avoid multicollinearity, a standard practice in ecological niche and climate-resilience modeling58.

Weights for climatic variables were assigned based on their ecological relevance and their reported influence on plant physiological thresholds and climate sensitivity in the literature49. All data extraction, processing, and raster computations were performed in R (version 4.3) using the raster and terra packages.

Genetic connectivity model

The genetic connectivity model integrates climate resistance with genetic distance among populations. Genetic distances were calculated using molecular markers and the Jaccard similarity coefficient, and connectivity was modeled using the Cost-Distance approach in the gdistance package of R 4.359. Combining climate resistance and genetic distance enables a comprehensive assessment of connectivity under future climate scenarios60.

All modeling and related analyses were performed using multiple R packages, including raster, rgdal, gdistance, ggplot2, reshape2, sf, osmdata, rnaturalearth, and rnaturalearthdata61,62. Assumptions of the models were checked, including spatial independence of residuals and appropriate scaling of input layers, to ensure reproducibility and biological relevance.

The genetic corridor Estimation and map

We constructed a map of the genetic corridor under the influence of the landscape context to compare it with climate resistance and genetic distance patterns. This analysis aimed to identify potential dispersal routes facilitating gene flow among P. aquilinum populations under current and future climate conditions.

First, the geographic coordinates of populations were converted into a Spatial Points object using the WGS84 geographic coordinate system. Then, a resistance surface was generated based on proximity to population points and adjusted using climatic and topographic layers to better reflect landscape heterogeneity.

A transition layer was constructed from the resistance raster using the mean of neighboring cell values, followed by geographic correction (geoCorrection) to ensure spatial accuracy and minimize edge effects. From this, a resistance distance matrix was computed to quantify potential movement costs between populations. Finally, accumulated cost maps were generated to identify the most feasible pathways and corridors for gene flow, representing regions of low resistance and high potential connectivity.

These analyses were performed in R 4.3 using the raster, gdistance, and sf packages. Model assumptions—such as isotropy of resistance values and spatial resolution consistency—were verified, and sensitivity analyses were conducted by varying cell size and resistance weighting to test model robustness.

These analyses were conducted to address the research question regarding how climate change is expected to alter population connectivity, identifying potential barriers and corridors for gene flow, and evaluating the capacity of populations to maintain genetic exchange under future environmental scenarios.

Differential equation modeling (DEM)

To explore the dynamic responses of P. aquilinum populations over time and under changing climatic conditions, we applied Differential Equation Modeling (DEM). This approach was used to simulate temporal changes in key biological parameters (morphological, genetic, environmental, and climatic) and to understand how they jointly influence population trajectories under projected scenarios.

DEM was selected because it can represent non-linear feedbacks and time-dependent interactions among ecological and genetic variables, which are not easily captured by static statistical models. These analyses were conducted using the deSolve package in R 4.367.

Model formulation

We assumed that the system dynamics could be described by a set of coupled ordinary differential equations (ODEs) representing the temporal evolution of biological variables. The model incorporated the following key considerations:

Spatial influence

Geographic coordinates (longitude and latitude) were included as spatial covariates influencing the rate of change of biological parameters, capturing potential gradients in growth and adaptation.

Biological interactions

Morphological and genetic parameters were modeled as interacting nonlinear terms, representing direct and indirect influences among biological traits. For example, genetic diversity may enhance morphological stability under environmental stress, while morphological adaptation can influence survival and dispersal potential.

Population dynamics

Each population was modeled as an independent unit with population-specific parameters (α, β, γ) to account for variability in environmental response. This allowed each differential equation system to reflect the ecological and genetic uniqueness of the population while maintaining a shared overall structure.

Numerical simulation and parameter Estimation

The system of ODEs was solved using the Runge–Kutta integration method, which provides numerical stability and accuracy in simulating dynamic systems68. Parameter estimation was performed using the Levenberg–Marquardt algorithm (LMA)—also known as the damped least squares (DLS) method—to minimize the residual sum of squares between observed and predicted data69,70.

All parameters (α, β, γ) were constrained within biologically plausible ranges based on prior empirical observations to prevent overfitting and to maintain ecological realism.

Mathematical representation

Model validation and sensitivity analysis

Model performance was evaluated using mean squared error (MSE) and R² values. Good model fit was indicated by lower MSE and higher R² values (> 0.8). A global sensitivity analysis was also performed to assess the relative contribution of each parameter to system dynamics, ensuring that the modeled interactions were not dominated by any single variable.

Graphical outputs of the ODE models were generated to visualize the predicted trajectories of biological parameters across time and space, allowing for comparison among populations and between current and future climate scenarios.

All DEM simulations were cross-validated using subsets of data (80% training, 20% testing) to ensure reproducibility and robustness.

This analysis was conducted to address the research question regarding the temporal dynamics of population responses under changing climatic conditions, integrating genetic, morphological, environmental, and climatic factors to predict population trajectories and assess resilience over time.

Landscape genetics results

Association study

Morphological data analysis confirmed a significant association with environmental and climatic variables. Redundancy analysis (RDA) performed on the morphological data against environmental and climatic variables (with 999 permutations) revealed a highly significant association (p = 0.001). This finding underscores that the morphological differentiation in the studied populations is under a strong influence of environmental factors.

Patterns of Morphological Correlation: The RDA (Fig. 3A) demonstrated a significant and contrasting relationship between the morphological characters and two key environmental factors. Temperature showed a significant positive correlation (p < 0.05) with several key morphological traits, including M1 through M3 (Stipe indument, Pinnule shape, and Pinnae number), as well as M7 (pinnule size). This suggests a common morphological response that potentially adapts to elevated temperatures in these traits. In contrast, humidity exhibited a notable negative correlation with M1-M3, M6 (Pinnae size), and M7. This differential pattern of correlation highlights the relative and contrasting importance of these two factors (temperature and humidity) in shaping the morphology of the studied populations.

The genetic and environmental latent factors were consistently and positively associated with all other latent factors. This result highlights their paramount importance as the fundamental drivers shaping morphological diversification, physiological tolerance, and overall adaptation of P. aquilinum in the studied areas. Despite the general positive trend, the SEM revealed critical, contrasting relationships that suggest biological trade-offs under environmental stress: Tolerance vs. Bioclimate: The bioclimatic factor (Bioclim) showed a significant negative association with plant tolerance (beta= -0.08, p = 0.001), even though Bioclim positively influenced other factors. As anticipated and confirmed by the model, internal variables like higher temperatures correlated with lower humidity levels within the Bioclim factor (which was constructed exclusively using temperature and humidity variables). Longitude and latitude also exhibited differing influences, reflecting their complex roles in structuring genetic and environmental variation across the landscape.

Discussion

Contrary to the paradigm of high gene flow traditionally assumed for spore-dispersed ferns, our study reveals that P. aquilinum at its Iranian range margin exhibits pronounced spatial genetic and morphological structure, indicating that local adaptation—not dispersal—dictates its response to climate change.

Our analyses demonstrate strong spatial structuring across populations of P. aquilinum in Iran, with both genetic and morphological variation being tightly linked to local environmental conditions71,72. This pattern suggests that the species, which has a restricted distribution in Iran, follows a “persist-in-place” strategy24, relying on localized adaptive mechanisms rather than range shifts. The Landscape Adaptive Index (LAI) (representing combined climatic and environmental tolerances) and the Adaptive Capacity (integrating genetic and morphological contributions) both exhibited substantial variation among populations, with low-LAI populations showing higher conservation risk scores73,74, while high-LAI populations represent critical genetic refugia for future conservation planning73,75.

Multiple lines of evidence from RDA, CCA, sPCA, and Isolation-by-Environment jointly establish that environmental heterogeneity is the primary driver of genetic and morphological divergence in P. aquilinum.

RDA and CCA results showed significant associations between specific genetic loci or key morphological traits and climatic/geographical variables, reinforcing the central role of environmental filtering in shaping divergence23. Strong genetic structure (AMOVA, Fst) and the IBE patterns detected by sPCA75 provide a mechanistic explanation for this differentiation, with drivers such as soil moisture and temperature gradients aligning precisely with adaptive genomic signals. These findings mirror global patterns in fragmented fern populations, where micro-environmental selection outweighs the homogenizing influence of spore dispersal62,63, and highlight that edge populations of Pteridium (despite the genus’s global ecological breadth) may be highly sensitive to marginal climatic variation76,77. Similar environmentally driven divergence is reported for other regionally restricted Iranian taxa, especially in the heterogeneous landscapes of the Zagros78–80, reinforcing the role of environmental filtering as a dominant evolutionary force.

Our results indicate that adaptation in P. aquilinum arises from the combined influences of genetically based local adaptation and high phenotypic plasticity, each contributing at different spatial and functional scales.

Candidate adaptive loci detected through RDA, DAPC, sPCA, and pcadapt provide a robust genetic foundation for local adaptation (Fig. 4), while the very high phenotypic plasticity index (PPI ≈ 0.99; Fig. 5) demonstrates the species’ strong short-term buffering capacity against environmental variation. Spatial structuring in both genetic and morphological traits (sPCA; Figs. 6 and 7) reflects the interplay of global and local selective pressures. SEM results further show that the Environmental latent factor is a major positive driver of overall adaptability, while the negative association between morphological and adaptive latent factors reflects expected evolutionary trade-offs rather than performance constraints19,20,49. Together, these findings highlight that populations may follow distinct adaptive trajectories depending on their inherent genetic variation and trait-level plasticity81,82.

Climate-response modeling (RRMM and PLS-SEM) indicates that future climatic shifts will generate population-specific functional trait transitions, producing an adaptive syndrome focused on enhanced water-use efficiency.

RRMM projections show heterogeneous shifts in traits such as trichome density and pinnule margin shape, forming a coordinated drought-tolerance strategy aligned with rising temperatures and declining humidity83. For example, trichome density is predicted to increase markedly in population 1 (consistent with known drought-mitigation mechanisms)84–86 while population 3 lacks this response, indicating vulnerability. Similarly, transitions toward entire pinnule margins, a known adaptation to warmer and drier conditions87–90, vary across populations. Reductions in pinnae number in certain populations91–94 further indicate structural adjustments aimed at reducing evaporative surface area. PLS-SEM clarifies that many of these responses are mediated by environmental factors such as soil moisture, highlighting the causal pathways underlying observed climate-trait interactions19,20. These heterogeneous, trait-specific trajectories illustrate that only certain populations possess the appropriate plastic or genetic potential to cope with future climate regimes.

Because populations are locally adapted and climate change is accelerating, the predicted decline in future gene flow represents an imminent threat to the persistence of P. aquilinum in Iran.

Our connectivity modeling shows that several populations will experience reduced genetic connectivity and increased bottleneck risk under future climate scenarios. Importantly, under the projected RCP 4.5 conditions, increasing aridity and ongoing habitat fragmentation elevate environmental resistance, directly leading to the predicted decline in future gene flow. This pattern aligns with connectivity declines reported for other geographically restricted plant species facing climate-induced habitat desiccation95–97. In Iran’s arid and semi-arid regions, where P. aquilinum already has low abundance, specialized microhabitats, and limited dispersal capacity98, the combination of climate-driven fragmentation and reduced long-distance spore dispersal poses a substantial conservation concern.

Taken together, our findings emphasize that conserving P. aquilinum requires strategies that simultaneously maintain genetic diversity, preserve locally adapted lineages, and mitigate climate-driven isolation.

Prioritizing vulnerable populations (especially populations 7 and 8) for immediate protection due to their low genetic variability and high-risk scores.

Conclusion

This study provides the first integrative assessment of landscape genetics and climate change vulnerability for the peripheral populations of P. aquilinum in Iran. Our findings conclusively demonstrate that the global resilience often attributed to this cosmopolitan species does not translate to resilience at its marginal, locally-adapted Iranian range limit.

The key findings are: (1) A strong Isolation by Environment (IBE) pattern exists, driven primarily by temperature and humidity, which has led to pronounced genetic and morphological divergence among populations. (2) Adaptation in P. aquilinum relies on a synergistic interplay between phenotypic plasticity (e.g., predicted shifts in trichome density and pinnule shape for water-use efficiency) and adaptive genetic loci. (3) The Landscape Adaptive Index (LAI) and risk modeling reveal a critical conservation paradox: despite global persistence predictions (IUCN Least Concern), the vulnerability at the local population level is extremely high, particularly for those with low genetic variability (Populations 7 and 8). Furthermore, our models predict a concerning decrease in genetic connectivity in several populations dueising environmental resistance, severely limiting long-term evolutionary potential. These results necessitate an action-oriented conservation strategy.

Ultimately, our research underscores the importance of fine-scale, genetic-based vulnerability assessments for widespread species, offering a crucial model for adaptive management under rapidly changing climatic conditions.

Limitations and future directions

Despite its comprehensive and integrative methodological framework, this study has some limitations that should be acknowledged to contextualize the findings and guide future research. First, although the sampling design covers the full known geographical and ecological range of P. aquilinum in northern Iran, the study is inherently limited to a regional scale. Consequently, the observed patterns of genetic structure, environmental associations, and adaptive potential reflect landscape-level processes within the Hyrcanian range and cannot be directly generalized to the species’ global distribution. Broader sampling across other continents and bioclimatic zones would help clarify whether the adaptive signatures detected in Iranian marginal populations reflect local specialization or part of global evolutionary trajectories.

Second, the genetic analyses rely on SCoT markers, which, despite their usefulness in detecting population structure and adaptive associations, provide limited genomic resolution compared with high-density approaches such as RADseq, GBS, or whole-genome resequencing. Genome-wide methods would allow more precise detection of selection signatures, linkage patterns, and the functional relevance of adaptive loci, and would also improve identification of fine-scale demographic processes such as inbreeding, bottlenecks, and historical migration.
