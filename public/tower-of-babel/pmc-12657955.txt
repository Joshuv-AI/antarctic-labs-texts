Disrupted theta synchronization and synaptic connectivity in the visual cortex of Fmr1 KO mice
Authors: Xi Cheng, Sanghamitra Nareddula, Hao-Cheng Gao, Yueyi Chen, Tiange Xiao, Yididiya Y Nadew, Fan Xu, Paige Alyssa Edens, Violeta Saldarriaga, Xinwan Hu, Christopher J Quinn, Adam Kimbrough, Fang Huang, Alexander A Chubykin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractFragile X syndrome (FX) is a leading inherited cause of autism spectrum disorder, characterized by sensory hypersensitivity and impaired visual learning. Visual experience induces synchronized theta oscillations in the primary visual cortex (V1) and lateromedial area (LM), supporting inter-areal sensory binding. Using the Fmr1 knockout (KO) mouse model of FX, we quantify experience-dependent c-Fos expression in V1 and LM via iDISCO whole-brain clearing. Simultaneous in vivo recordings and channelrhodopsin-2-assisted circuit mapping (CRACM) reveal synchronized V1–LM theta oscillations and strengthened feedforward V1 → LM connectivity in wild-type (WT) mice, but attenuated LM oscillations and impaired connectivity in FX mice. Using 4Pi single-molecule localization microscopy, we identify experience-driven dendritic spine remodeling in layer 5 pyramidal cells of V1 and LM in WT mice, which is absent in FX mice. FX mice also show elevated baseline spine density and length. Our findings demonstrate that visual experience drives inter-areal synchronization and synaptic plasticity, which are disrupted in FX.Subject terms: Autism spectrum disorders, Neural circuits

Introduction

Fragile X syndrome (FX) is the most common inherited form of autism spectrum disorders (ASDs) and intellectual disability1. Multiple studies have reported deficits in visual perception and learning in FX patients2–5. Sensory dysfunction is frequently observed in the Fmr1 knockout (KO) mouse model of FX, including hypersensitivity, impaired visual discrimination, and delayed perceptual learning6,7. Previously, we found that visual familiarity is encoded by persistent theta (4–8 Hz) frequency oscillations in both single units and local field potentials (LFPs) in the primary visual cortex (V1) of mice8. In Fmr1 KO mice, these visual familiarity-evoked theta frequency oscillations were found to be attenuated, exhibiting lower power and shorter duration, suggesting deficits in encoding familiar stimuli in V19. In addition to these impairments in visual perception and learning, multiple circuit and synaptic defects have been described in Fragile X patients and Fmr1 KO mice, including immature synaptic features10, increased density of unstable dendritic filopodia, impaired experience-dependent plasticity, and decreased long-term potentiation11–13. Short-term plasticity (STP) is critical for information processing14, and working memory15, yet the STP alterations in the cortical regions of Fmr1 KO mice have not been characterized. An imbalance of neocortical excitation and inhibition is reported in Fmr1 KO mice, reflecting network hyperexcitability16, and is mediated by decreased activity of Parvalbumin-positive interneurons in V1 of adult FX mice6,17. Consistent with these findings, and through the use of Channelrhodopsin-2-assisted circuit mapping (CRACM), we have previously observed impaired visual experience-dependent changes across inter-laminar synaptic connections in V1, specifically in projections from layer 5 (L5) pyramidal cells (PCs) to layer 4 (L4) interneurons9.

Recent decades of research have shown that visual processing in mice, like in primates, extends beyond V1 to higher-order visual areas (HVAs) that significantly influence V1 activity. The mouse visual cortex is comprised of multiple distinct areas, organized retinotopically and hierarchically, including anterolateral (AL), lateromedial (LM), and posteromedial (PM) areas18–21. V1 is interconnected with HVAs through feedforward (FF) and feedback (FB) pathways following the laminar projection patterns22 where FF connections transmit information from lower visual areas to HVAs, and FB connections operate in the opposite direction23. The functional and anatomical division of mouse HVAs resembles the parallel processing in the ventral and dorsal streams of the primate visual system24. Similarly, research has described AL and LM as the gateways of the ventral and dorsal streams, respectively, in the mouse visual cortex25,26. LM in mice corresponds to the ventrotemporal areas in primates involved in object recognition, forming part of the ventral pathway21,27,28. Our recent findings demonstrate that familiarity-evoked theta oscillations arise in V1 and synchronize with HVAs in a context-dependent fashion, suggesting that these oscillations may underlie the functional coupling between primary and secondary visual areas29. Although previous studies have documented impaired inter-areal connectivity in FX patients30, the network mechanisms responsible for these deficits are not well understood, and few animal studies have addressed this issue.

To bridge this gap, we evaluated neuronal activity in V1 and LM after visual experience using c-Fos immunostaining. Our analysis revealed baseline hyperactivity and impaired neural plasticity in FX mice. Simultaneous extracellular recordings showed that familiarity-evoked theta oscillations in LM had reduced power and duration in FX mice, and the increased phase synchronization between V1 and LM seen in WT mice was absent in FX mice. Using subcellular CRACM (sCRACM)31 to map functional synaptic projections, we found that visual experience normally strengthens feedforward (V1 → LM, layers 2/3 and 5) and FB (LM → V1, layer 5) pathways, which were impaired in Fmr1 KO mice, with significant deficits in STP. Finally, 4Pi single-molecule localization microscopy (SMLM)32 revealed that while visual experience increased dendritic spine density and size in WT mice, Fmr1 KO mice already had elevated basal spine density and length and failed to exhibit experience-dependent changes. These structural changes correlated with the functional synaptic and oscillatory deficits observed in FX mice.

Results

Whole-brain clearing revealed impaired c-Fos activity following visual experience in V1 and LM of FX mice

Our previous work has demonstrated the emergence and synchronization of oscillations in higher visual areas with those in V129. The immediate early gene c-Fos is a well-established marker of neuronal activity and plasticity33, making it an ideal target for studying experience-dependent changes in the brain. We sought to elucidate how visual stimuli influence neuronal activity and plasticity, particularly in conditions associated with neural hyperactivity, such as FX, by analyzing c-Fos expression in mouse visual areas before and after visual experience. We subjected mice to a passive visual perception paradigm to investigate these differences in V1 and LM of WT and FX mice. To establish visual familiarity, mice were trained to a filtered pink noise stimulus (0.12 cycles/degree of visual angle (cpd), 0.75 Hz) corresponding to the spatial frequency (SF) and time-frequency (TF) shown to elicit the strongest response in LM29. The training continued for 4–6 days, during which the filtered pink noise movie was presented for 200 ms, 200 times a day, with a grey screen between trials (inter-trial interval = 6 s, Fig. 1A). The pre-training mice were habituated to the same apparatus as the trained mice and were only presented with a grey screen for the same amount of time as our visual training protocol. We investigated how perceptual experience alters c-Fos expression in the visual cortical areas, specifically in V1 and LM, using a combination of the iDISCO brain clearing technique and light sheet microscopy for fast volume imaging of whole-brain samples (Fig. 1B, C).

We measured c-Fos expression in four groups of animals: WT pre-, FX pre-, WT post-experience, and FX post-experience. C-Fos expression was generally much higher in V1 than in other visual areas, including LM (Fig. 1D, E). Visual perceptual experience increased Fos expression in multiple layers of the LM, including L4, L5, and LGN in WT mice, but not in FX (Fig. 1G and Supplementary Fig. 1). Interestingly, the number of Fos-positive neurons was higher in FX mice prior to visual experience in multiple vision-related regions, including V1 (L2/3), LM (L4, L5), AL (L2/3, L4), and PL (L5) (Fig. 1F and G, and Supplementary Fig. 1). These results indicate hyperactivity in the visual areas of FX mice, consistent with the typical symptom of FX, hypersensitivity3. Interestingly, the c-fos level of multiple visual-related regions decreased after visual experience in FX mice, including V1 L2/3, V1 L4, LM L4 (Fig. 1F, G), and some other visual areas (Supplementary Fig. 1). Overall, the visual experience induced a prominent upregulation in c-Fos-positive (c-Fos+) neuron density in multiple visual areas of WT mice but downregulation in FX mice, suggesting the distinct induction of experience-dependent neuronal and/or synaptic plasticity between FX and WT.

The attenuated visual experience evoked oscillations and synchrony in the LFPs of FX mice in V1 and LM

To determine how visual experience influences dynamic interaction and sensory binding between visual areas, we performed simultaneous extracellular recordings from V1 and LM before and after visual experience. We applied the same visual experience paradigm as in the c-Fos experiments, activating both V1 and LM in WT and FX mice. 64-channel silicon probes were inserted into V1 and LM to record simultaneously from both regions after mice were habituated to the experimental setup, prior to and post-visual experience (Fig. 2A, B). Visually evoked potentials (VEPs) were evaluated by selecting the LFP channel with the strongest activity post visual stimulus presentation (0.5–1.0 s), averaged across 20 trials, and normalized to baseline activity (0–0.5 s). In both V1 and LM, visual experience induced strong stimulus-locked theta oscillations (4–8 Hz), which persisted beyond the visual stimulus offset (Fig. 2C, E). The second peak of the visual response—marking the onset of persistent theta oscillations—was amplified in both V1 and LM of WT mice (Fig. 2D, F). However, FX mice showed no significant increase in VEP amplitudes post-visual experience compared to naïve mice (Fig. 2D, F). This attenuation of oscillations in FX mice is consistent with our previous reports in V19,34. To characterize the magnitude of oscillatory activity, we performed a TF analysis of VEPs across several frequency bands (4–8 Hz, 8–12 Hz, 12–30 Hz, 30–40 Hz, 50–70 Hz, and 30–70 Hz) (Fig. 2G, J). Power spectrum analysis quantifying the area under the curve within each frequency band showed a significant increase in power within the 4–8 Hz range in WT compared to FX post visual experience, in both V1 and LM (Fig. 2I, J). To quantify the synchrony between the two brain regions, we computed the phase difference between 4 and 8 Hz oscillations in V1 and LM and discovered a shift towards zero post-visual experience in WT mice, as well as a narrowing of the distribution (Fig. 2K, L). The peak phase lag indicates the level of synchronization of the LFP activity of the two regions, while the width of the distribution indicates the overall trial-by-trial variability of the synchronized response. WT mice showed an overall strengthening of synchronization, as well as a decrease in V1-LM variability across trials between the two regions (Fig. 2K, L and Supplementary Fig. 2). However, FX mice showed no significant change in phase difference distribution pre- and post-visual experience, further indicating a decrease in synchrony and dynamic binding between V1 and LM (Fig. 2K, L).

To investigate the role of V1 in the emergence of theta oscillations in LM, and the plasticity changes that lead to synchronization, we performed an experiment where we optogenetically inhibited V1 during visual familiarity training (after visual stimulus offset to prevent disruption of visual input) for 4–6 days. This inhibition directly affected the emergence of oscillations in LM, indicating that experience-dependent V1 plasticity is critical for relaying familiarity information and driving synchronization between the regions (Supplementary Fig. 3). These results suggest that the attenuated oscillatory activity seen in V1 of FX mice may be the cause of the subsequent loss of synchronization shown here.

The disrupted synchrony between the two regions shown here in FX indicates an impairment in encoding familiarity. To investigate this in the context of behavior, we performed a pairwise visual discrimination task and evaluated performance in WT and FX mice. Once the mice learned the task, we introduced a novel stimulus and found that FX mice have a significant difficulty in distinguishing between novel and familiar stimuli (Supplementary Fig. 4). These behavioral observations might be indicative of the attenuation seen in FX mice.

Unit population firing rates showed decreased oscillatory activity in FX

To investigate the difference in unit population response between FX and WT mice, we analyzed baseline (0–0.5 s) normalized Z-score firing rates of all unit population firing rates, which show persistent oscillatory activity in the 4–8 Hz theta range post-visual experience, as seen previously9,34 (Fig. 3A–F). Prior to visual experience, unit population activity exhibited a single peak temporally locked to the presentation of the visual stimulus in both V1 and LM. Visual experience evoked multiple oscillation peaks persisting after stimulus offset in both regions (Fig. 3A–F). On average, WT mice typically showed four peaks, or oscillation cycles, while FX mice showed fewer oscillation cycles (Fig. 3C, E and Supplementary Fig. 2). Duration analysis of the detected peaks showed that this persistent oscillatory unit activity was shorter in V1 and LM in FX (Fig. 3D, F). Mean persistent activity durations were also significantly shorter in FX (Fig. 3D, F). The maximum firing rate Z-scores were calculated for each unit within each oscillation cycle time window. Average firing rates across the unit population were significantly higher in WT mice compared to FX for several oscillation cycles following the initial peak in V1 (cycle 2, cycle 3, and cycle 4), indicating an overall decrease in synchronous population firing of units (Fig. 3D, F). The attenuated power of the theta frequency unit activity in FX mice is consistent with the reduced c-Fos level in V1 and LM areas following visual experience.

Impaired plasticity of functional FFV1→LM connectivity in FX mice

We conducted a directed information analysis (DIA)9,35,36 to assess changes in putative functional connectivity between units in V1 and LM pre- and post-training, and to assess how those changes differed between WT and FX mice. The analysis entailed fitting parametric models to each unit’s spike train data conditioned on the spike train data of other units, evaluating directed information on those unit-level models, and then aggregating unit-level values to estimate area-level connection strengths. This was repeated for each experimental setting (WT/FX, pre/post-training). Differences in area-level connection strengths between different experimental settings were then calculated. We discovered a significant increase in the FF connectivity from V1 to LM post-visual experience in WT mice. However, in FX mice, no significant change was detected (Fig. 3H). Additionally, the FB input from LM to V1 demonstrated an increase in the strength of connectivity post-visual experience in WT mice, though it did not reach significance (Fig. 3H, I). Further analysis between the strains showed a significant increase in WT in both FF (V1-LM) and FB (LM-V1) compared to FX post-visual experience (Fig. 3I). These changes in the functional connectivity following visual experience suggest that there may be changes in the strength of synaptic connections between V1 and LM after visual experience, which are altered in FX.

Experience-dependent changes in the synaptic strength of the FFV1→LM pathway

To validate our in vivo directed information results, we directly measured the visual experience-dependent synaptic plasticity of the FFV1→LM or FBLM→V1 projections in FX and WT mice using CRACM in acute brain slices9,37. We first injected AAV1-ChR2-Venus into V1. After a three-week period of virus replication, we subjected the mice to a passive visual perceptual experience paradigm for 4-6 days, using the same method employed in c-Fos labeling and in vivo experiments (Fig. 4A). CRACM recordings from experienced mice were conducted following the final day of training. WT and FX mice were pseudo-randomly assigned to either pre-training or experienced groups to avoid observation bias. The genotypes of mice were blinded to operators during the experiment and throughout the analysis. To ensure that the expression levels of the light-gated channel channelrhodopsin-2 (ChR2) did not affect synaptic strength measurements, the fluorescent intensity of the recording regions from each mouse and the two brain regions was quantified, and no significant differences were found among groups (Supplementary Fig. 5). A fluorescent dye was added to the tip of the recording electrode, along with the internal solution, to trace cell morphology in L2/3, L4, and L5 of both regions. The sCRACM map was then overlaid with confocal imaging of the corresponding cells (Supplementary Fig. 6). The neurons patched in V1 were predominantly PCs, as determined by their morphological characteristics (Supplementary Fig. 6).

To measure interareal connectivity, we optically stimulated ChR2-positive axon terminals from anterograde neurons in a 10 × 10 grid (0.67 mm × 0.67 mm) covering a square area centered at the recipient neurons in LM L2/3 to L5 using a light-patterned illuminator (Fig. 4A). For each 5 ms light pulse at each pixel, excitatory post-synaptic currents (EPSCs) were recorded under a voltage clamp at −70 mV. All sCRACM recordings were conducted in the presence of 1 μM tetrodotoxin (TTX) and 100 μM 4-aminopyridine (4-AP)31, sodium, and potassium channel inhibitors to block action potentials and polysynaptic responses. Whole-cell patch clamp recordings of LM L2/3, L4, and L5 neurons were conducted in acute brain slices (Fig. 4B, top and Supplementary Fig. 7A) from four groups of animals: WT naive, FX naive, WT post-experience, and FX post-experience. The CRACM heatmap was plotted for EPSCsCRACM values for each grid of light stimulation. The average EPSCsCRACM values and their cumulative distributions (in logarithmic scale) were also plotted for each neuron. sCRACM results of L2/3 changed significantly pre- and post-visual experience as reported by the linear mixed model (LMM) analysis in both WT and FX animals, while there is no prominent difference between WT and FX (Supplementary Data 2). Accordingly, sCRACM heatmaps of L2/3 EPSC amplitudes in experienced WT and FX mice were hotter than those of naive groups (Fig. 4B, middle, F top). Kernel density plots of pixel-averaged EPSC values, along with mean EPSC amplitudes, were analyzed and demonstrated a significant increase in post-training compared to pre-training for both WT and FX mice (Fig. 4B, middle, F top). However, the increase in the average EPSCsCRACM in FX mice was smaller than in WT mice (Fig. 4B, middle, F top). The profile of the averaged EPSCsCRACM amplitude by grid position was significantly elevated after the visual experience in WT mice (Supplementary Fig. 7B). This elevation was less pronounced in FX mice, but it remained significant compared to naïve FX mice (Supplementary Fig. 7C). We detected no difference in EPSCsCRACM pre- and post-visual experience, or between WT and FX in V1 L4 (Supplementary Fig. 8A–E, Supplementary Fig. 9A, B, D, G, and Supplementary Data 2). The post-training CRACM heatmaps of the L5 WT and FX mice looked hotter than their corresponding naive groups (Fig. 4B, bottom, F bottom). Nevertheless, only the WT groups reached statistical significance (Fig. 4B, bottom, F bottom and Supplementary Data 2). Similarly, the average EPSCCRACM amplitude by grid position for L5 PCs was much higher in experienced WT mice compared to the naïve group, but there was no such difference within the FX groups (Supplementary Fig. 7E, F).

We found no significant difference between naïve WT and naïve FX mice (Supplementary Fig. 9A, C–E and Supplementary Data 2). However, the EPSCsCRACM amplitude, both global and averaged by grid position, was higher in WT mice compared to FX after visual experience in L5 PCs, but not in L2/3 or L4 PCs (Supplementary Fig. 9B, F–H). The sCRACM results demonstrated that visual experience strengthened the synaptic connectivity in the FFV1→LM pathway of WT mice, and this plasticity was substantially impaired in FX mice. These results validate our directed information functional connectivity analysis and suggest that impaired interareal synaptic connectivity may underlie the attenuated familiarity-induced theta oscillations in LM of experienced FX mice.

Experience-dependent changes in the synaptic strength of the FBLM→V1 pathway

To investigate the synaptic connectivity of the FBLM→V1 pathway, we employed the same visual experience and sCRACM setup (Fig. 4A), but instead of V1, we injected AAV1-ChR2-Venus into the LM. Three weeks later, we performed visual stimulation and sCRACM experiments (Fig. 5A, top). The same statistical analysis was conducted for the data from the FFV1→LM pathway as for the data from the FBLM→V1 pathway. The average EPSCsCRACM from LM to V1 L2/3 PCs showed a decreasing trend after the visual experience but did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 5A, middle and Supplementary Data). No difference was detected in the cumulative distributions of average EPSCsCRACM from LM to V1 L2/3 PCs (Fig. 5A, middle). After visual experience, the averaged EPSCsCRACM amplitude by grid position in both WT and FX mice showed a trend of decrease; however, LMM analysis did not reach significance (Supplementary Fig. 7H, I). Overall, the difference between the heatmaps of pre- and post-visual experience FX animals was not as significant as in the WT groups (Fig. 5A middle, E top). sCRACM analysis for LM to V1 L4 PCs showed no difference between pre- and post-experience groups for both WT and FX mice (Supplementary Fig. 8F–J). Consistent with this, heatmaps also appeared similar among the four groups (Supplementary Fig. 8G, I). For V1 L5 PCs, a trend of rise in averaged EPSCsCRACM amplitude was observed in WT animals, though LMM analysis did not reach significance (Fig. 5A, bottom and Supplementary Fig. 7K) and no difference between genotypes and visual experience (Supplementary Data 2). The cumulative distributions of average EPSCsCRACM and averaged EPSCsCRACM amplitude showed no significant difference in FX groups (Fig. 5E, bottom). Additionally, LMM revealed no changes in EPSCsCRACM amplitude by grid position between naïve or experienced groups (Supplementary Data 2), and no notable shift in the profile was observed in the FX groups (Supplementary Fig. 7L). No statistical difference was found when comparing genotypes in either naïve or experienced groups (Supplementary Fig. 9I, J and Supplementary Data 2). Overall, the connectivity strength was lower, and the visual experience-evoked plasticity was less prominent in the FBLM→V1 pathway, suggesting that the FFV1→LM pathway plays the pivotal role in visual learning, and the attenuation of plasticity in the FFV1→LM pathway of FX mice might be the primary mechanism of their visual learning deficiency. Interestingly, our results suggest that visual experience may reduce the receptive field size in L2/3 PCs of the FBLM→V1 pathway in FX mice (Fig. 5E, top), indicating that visual experience may rectify some atypical circuit connectivity in FX mice. The visual experience-induced synaptic plasticity of the interareal projections formed onto L5 PCs was most severely impaired in FX mice in both FFV1→LM and FBLM→V1 pathways compared to the projections to the superficial and middle laminar layers.

Altered E/I balance of FFV1→LM and FBLM→V1 inputs to PCs in FX mice

We examined the circuit in a subcellular or monosynaptic manner by applying TTX and 4-AP, which abolished mixed synaptic inputs and the disynaptic involvement of inhibitory interneurons31. In vivo, cortical network activity and modulation of neuronal responsiveness are achieved through a dynamic balance of excitation and inhibition in a behaviorally relevant manner38. Many previous studies indicate that E/I balance is impaired in multiple models of ASDs39–43, including Fmr1 KO mice17. To measure this balance in PCs of FFV1→LM and FBLM→V1 pathways, we utilized CRACM as previously described in ref. 44. The glutamatergic synaptic inputs to PCs were recorded in the same manner as the sCRACM experiment, but without the application of TTX and 4-AP. In contrast, the GABAergic synaptic inputs to PCs were recorded while holding the cells at the excitatory postsynaptic reversal potential. The compartmentalized E/I ratio recorded by CRACM, reflecting balanced synaptic strength in subcellular compartments, could shape subcellular circuitry and computation logic (e.g., soma vs dendrites)44. To examine the E/I ratio at distinct subcellular locations in LM pyramidal cells (PCs) within layers 2/3 and 5 of the FFV1→LM pathway (Fig. 4C, D, top), we defined four grids surrounding the recorded soma (Supplementary Fig. 11A, white triangle) as the ‘soma’ region (orange outline). The remaining outer grids were categorized as the ‘dendritic’ region (blue outline). The EPSC and IPSC traces of the whole neuron, soma, or dendrite were extracted to calculate the E/I ratio (Supplementary Fig. 11B, E/I ratio = peak of EPSC/peak of IPSC). The E/I ratio recorded in the LM L2/3 neurons of the FFV1→LM pathway remained unchanged from pre- to post-experience in both WT and FX mice, showing no difference between the WT and FX groups in either neurons or in the divided compartments (soma or dendrite area; Supplementary Fig. 11C–E). The two compartments also displayed similar E/I dynamics in the FFV1→LM pathway (Supplementary Fig. 11C–E). The average EPSCCRACM and IPSCCRACM, as well as the cumulative distributions of averaged EPSCCRACM and IPSCCRACM, showed no statistical difference after visual experience in either WT or FX mice (Fig. 4C, D, middle, G, H top), which explained the consistency of the E/I ratio in the V1 to LM L2/3 PCs pathway. The visual experience shifted the E/I balance toward excitation in FX mice but did not alter it in WT mice (Supplementary Fig. 11C–E). The average EPSCCRACM and IPSCCRACM, as well as the cumulative distributions of averaged EPSCCRACM and IPSCCRACM in L5, were both increased in WT mice (Fig. 4C, D, bottom). However, only EPSCCRACM was increased in FX mice (Fig. 4G, H, bottom).

The soma and dendrite areas showed distinct E/I dynamics in the FBLM→V1 pathway, but L2/3 and L5 neurons displayed similar E/I dynamics across the training stages and between the genotypes. In the FBLM→V1 pathway, the E/I balance showed an opposite trend compared to the FFV1→LM pathway, which moved toward excitation in WT mice after visual experience but not in FX (Supplementary Fig. 11F–H). The LMM analysis revealed no difference in average EPSCCRACM and IPSCCRACM of the FBLM→V1 pathway. Overall, the FFV1→LM and FBLM→V1 pathways exhibited distinct E/I dynamics in the superficial and deep layers, and between distinct cellular compartments. Interestingly, the visual experience altered the E/I balance, making it comparable between WT and FX mice in the FFV1→LM and FBLM→V1 pathways.

Presynaptic STP in FFV1→LM and FBLM→V1 pathways

To measure presynaptic STP, we used optogenetic paired-pulse stimulation of the synapses between V1 and LM with TTX and 4-AP in bath45–47 with full-field light stimulation delivered through the 10x objective lens (Fig. 4E, top). We calculated the paired-pulse ratios (PPR = EPSC2/EPSC1) for a range of inter-pulse intervals (IPI) from 25 ms to 2000 ms. Short IPI stimulations induced depression in all animals, but this depression decreased after visual experience in both WT and FX mice (Fig. 4E, I). The paired-pulse depression was dominant in both FF and FB corticocortical synapses between V1 and LM, consistent with other reports in the frontal cortex48. The PPRs show a trend of increase after visual experience in L2/3 neurons from the FFV1→LM pathway of WT mice, especially for IPIs: 50, 100, 150, 200, and 2000 ms (Fig. 4E Middle), but the LMM analysis showed no difference between genotypes or pre vs post visual experience (Supplementary Data 2). There was no significant difference between naïve and experienced FX mice (Fig. 4I, middle). The PPR appeared larger in visually experienced FX mice only for IPI = 50 ms; the PPR in the rest of the IPIs was either lower or similar compared to naïve mice (Fig. 4I, middle). When comparing the genotypes for the L2/3 neurons of the FFV1→LM pathway, naïve FX mice showed larger PPR than the corresponding WT mice, which was reversed after the visual experience (Supplementary Fig. 12A, B). In L5 neurons of the FFV1→LM pathway, LMM analysis revealed no difference among the four groups of animals, as in FFV1→LM L2/3 (Fig. 4E, I, bottom, Supplementary Fig. 12C, D, and Supplementary Data 2).

Consistent with the sCRACM results for the FBLM→V1 pathway, PPRs were not affected by visual experience or genotypes (Fig. 5D, H, Supplementary Fig. 12E–H, and Supplementary Data 2). Overall, the presynaptic STP was not significantly affected by visual experience or loss of FMRP.

Experience-dependent dendritic spine plasticity correlates with synaptic strengthening in V1 and LM and is impaired in FX mice

Previous studies reported abnormal dendritic spine phenotypes in the superficial, cortical L2/3 neurons, including increased spine density and relative immaturity of spines in adult FX mice11,49–52. However, other research reported no changes in the dendritic spine morphology11,53–55. This discrepancy in the reports may result from the limitations of confocal and two-photon imaging techniques, which have relatively low resolution of dendritic spines. SMLM56,57 has overcome the diffraction barrier and provided unprecedented opportunities to observe cellular functions, interactions, and dynamics at the nanoscale level58. Specifically, SMLM (also known as PALM/STORM), as well as its three-dimensional (3D) counterpart59,60, utilizes photo-switchable or convertible dyes to allow the detection and localization of isolated molecules with a precision as low as 5 nm in 3D. We have recently developed a new SMLM method for thick tissues, providing an opportunity to perform super-resolution imaging of synaptic ultrastructures in brain slices with preserved intact neural circuits61.

To image the dendritic spines of the V1 L5 PCs in naïve and experienced WT and FX mice, we performed an in vivo visual stimulation paradigm as described earlier using a double transgenic strain of mice, which is a cross of the Fmr1 KO and Thy1-ChR2-EYFP lines62. After sacrificing and processing the mouse brains for SMLM microscopy, we performed group-blinded imaging of the dendritic spines of V1 L5 PCs in all four different conditions: naïve Thy1-ChR2-EYFP, naïve Fmr1 KO x Thy1-ChR2-EYFP, experienced Thy1-ChR2-EYFP, and experienced Fmr1 KO x Thy1-ChR2-EYFP. To measure the size of dendritic spines of apical dendrites from L5 PCs, we developed an approach to clearly resolve and quantify the contour of a cross-section of the spine, neck, and head using SMLM (Fig. 6A–C), allowing for precise fitting and estimation of the size of the dendritic spines (Supplementary Fig. 13). As previously reported, we also found that the spine density was higher in FX mice than in WT mice in naïve groups (Fig. 6D, left). Interestingly, we found that the visual experience greatly affected the plasticity of the protrusion density in WT mice but did not affect the density in FX mice (Fig. 6D, left). The dendritic spines were longer in naïve FX mice than in naïve WT mice, but no difference was detected between genotypes in experienced groups (Fig. 6E, left). The visual experience did not alter the length of the protrusions for either WT or FX mice in the visual cortex (Fig. 6E, left). The spine volumes remained stable in WT and FX mice following the visual experience (Fig. 6F, left). The size of the spine neck (minimal spine area) was larger in naïve WT mice compared to FX mice (Fig. 6G, left). When comparing the head size (maximal spine area), we found that visual familiarity upregulated the spine head size (Supplementary Data 3). The post hoc test indicated statistical significance only in WT mice (Fig. 6H, left).

To assess the impact of perceptual experience on dendritic spine remodeling in the LM visual area, we also quantified spine density and morphology in L5 PCs of WT and FX mice before and after visual training, similarly to the V1 spines. Consistent with V1, spine density was higher in FX mice at baseline in LM, and also didn’t change significantly following visual experience. On the contrary, WT mice demonstrated a significant increase in spine density in LM following experience (Fig. 6D, right). Interestingly, spine length was significantly shorter in FX mice but was not altered by experience in either genotype (Fig. 6E, right). Experience significantly increased spine volume in WT mice but not in FX (Fig. 6F, right). The minimal spine area was significantly larger in FX mice compared to WT mice before training (Fig. 6G, right). The visual experience significantly increased the minimal and maximal spine areas in WT mice, while FX mice showed no significant change (Fig. 6G, H, right). Our results suggest that visual experience significantly increased spine density, volume, and head size of dendritic spines in V1 and LM L5 PCs in WT mice. However, FX mice exhibited deficits in experience-dependent spine plasticity in V1 and LM L5 PCs, particularly in spine enlargement, which may contribute to the altered circuit connectivity, theta oscillations, and sensory processing in FX.

Discussion

Impaired visual familiarity-dependent theta oscillations and synchrony in V1 and LM of FX mice

Theta frequency oscillations in cortical regions have been associated with visual familiarity8,29,34,63. Previously, we demonstrated that these oscillations within V1 were impaired in FX mice, characterized by a shifted frequency, reduced power, and shorter duration9. These oscillations may represent a communication mechanism between V1 and HVAs, including top-down modulation of V1, as previously demonstrated29,64,65, and could be utilized to synchronize activity across different brain areas, facilitating the processing of familiar content29. Furthermore, we recently discovered that oscillatory activity in V1 and LM becomes more phase-synchronized post-visual experience in a stimulus-selective, feature-dependent manner in WT mice29. Building on this, we demonstrate that in FX mice, visual experience-induced 4–8 Hz theta oscillations are attenuated not only in V1 but also in LM (Table 1). LFP analysis showed diminished theta band power for both V1 and LM of FX mice compared to WT mice (Table 1). Despite choosing a visual stimulus that was shown to elicit the strongest response in LM, phase synchronization was only seen in WT mice, indicating a loss of inter-areal interaction between these brain regions in FX29. Impairments in the oscillatory activity of FX mice were also represented in the unit population firing rates of V1 and LM, with decreased firing rates at the oscillation cycles following stimulus presentation. DIA revealed significant differences in the functional connectivity of V1 and LM post-visual experience between WT and FX mice. Most notably, visual experience resulted in an overall increase in FFV1→LM input in WT mice, which was not seen in FX mice. Furthermore, comparisons between the genotypes showed that both FFV1→LM and FBLM→V1 inputs were significantly stronger in WT compared to FX post-visual experience. These differences in functional connectivity suggest deficits in communication between V1 and HVAs of FX mice. Inhibition of V1 persistent oscillations during visual training, without suppressing the initial visual response, significantly impairs the emergence of oscillatory activity in LM (Supplementary Fig. 3). This finding suggests that familiarity-evoked theta oscillations in V1 are necessary to entrain familiarity in LM. The disruption of this synchronized theta activity may contribute to deficits in visual perceptual learning in FX, particularly in distinguishing familiar from novel stimuli during the visual discrimination task (Supplementary Fig. 4). While both WT and FX mice were able to learn to effectively discriminate between the Go and No-Go stimuli, adding a Novel stimulus to the task significantly impaired performance in FX mice (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Impaired V1-LM interareal synaptic connectivity after visual experience in FX mice

Our circuit mapping studies demonstrated uneven deficits in synaptic connectivity across FFV1→LM and FBLM→V1 pathways induced by visual experience in FX mice and further validated our functional connectivity results (Table 1). Specifically, we observed a significant strengthening of connections from V1 to the superficial, middle, and deep layer neurons in LM after visual experience in WT mice. In contrast, in FX mice, this enhancement was confined to the superficial layers and was not as pronounced. The alterations in connectivity strength triggered by visual experience in the FB pathway were not significant in WT mice, whereas in FX mice, the deficits remained limited in magnitude without substantial disruption. Additionally, we found that visual experience elicited synaptic plasticity in a layer-specific manner for both FFV1→LM and FBLM→V1 pathways, with varying degrees of impairment across cortical layers in FX mice. These distinctions may be attributed to the laminar preferences for cortico-cortical connectivity due to the hierarchical organization of areas in the mouse visual cortex23,66. In rodents, projections from V1 terminate from inner L1 out to L6, while FBLM→V1 projections from the extrastriate cortex to V1 typically avoid L4, preferentially targeting layers L1, L2/3, L5, and L623. In previous work, we reported synaptic strengthening from L5 projections onto L2/3 and L4 after visual experience in V1, as evidenced by in vivo patch-clamp recordings combined with optogenetics, aligning with our FFV1→LM circuit mapping results34. The uneven deficits observed were consistent with our findings that interareal reciprocal connectivity between V1 and LM exhibits distinct strengthening according to laminar specificity, providing direct evidence supporting previous studies that demonstrated distinct connections and response properties between V1 and HVAs21,67,68. Interestingly, our results are also consistent with our previous work describing layer-specific alterations in visual responses to the oddball paradigm in FX mice, potentially providing insights into the circuit mechanisms underlying these impairments69.

The visual training paradigm specific for the ventral path elicited an elevation in the number of c-Fos+ neurons in LM of WT mice as part of the ventral pathway but not in the areas of the dorsal pathway, including AL, AM, or PM29. The number of c-Fos+ neurons in V1 was almost ten times that in LM (Fig. 1). Additionally, the excitatory postsynaptic currents measured by CRACM for the FFV1→LM pathway were significantly higher than those in the FBLM→V1 pathway (Figs. 4 and 5 and Table 1). The weaker FBLM→V1 projections we observed are consistent with findings by Burkhalter, A. et al., who reported significantly stronger FFV1→LM connections compared to FBLM→V1 as shown by sCRACM results67.

Other studies have shown that FX mice displayed altered hippocampal synaptic plasticity, including enhanced augmentation, but not short-term facilitation, in hippocampal synapses, indicating abnormal STP during high-frequency stimulations70,71. Here, we found that the STP in synapses from distinct layers of FFV1→LM and FBLM→V1 pathways was not consistently altered in naïve FX mice (Supplementary Fig. 12). However, the experience-dependent elevation of PPRs was impaired and was generally lower in FX compared to WT mice after the visual experience, especially in the synapses of the FFV1→LM pathway (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. 12). These findings suggest that FX mice exhibit impaired experience-dependent modulation of synaptic dynamics.

In WT mice, visual experience led to an increase in PPRs across the entire temporal range, whereas in FX mice, by contrast, changes were observed only for certain temporal intervals, suggesting altered temporal filtering properties of the inter-areal synapses in FX. In the sCRACM experiment, we found increased synaptic strength in the reciprocal connectivity between V1 and LM. The STP did not show a significant difference between WT and FX, as revealed by LMM analysis. Although there was a trend that didn’t reach significance, based on the LMM test, lower PPRs and a higher probability of release (Pr) were observed in both pre- and post-visual experience FX mice. Importantly, pre-synaptic Pr does not linearly predict synaptic strength in cortical networks. A decrease in Pr does not necessarily indicate reduced synaptic efficacy, as synaptic strength is a function of both presynaptic (Pr, size of the readily releasable pool, presynaptic Ca 2+ dynamics, etc.) and postsynaptic (spine structure, AMPAR, NMDAR activity, etc.) modifications72. Multiple studies have shown that in mature cortical circuits, decreased Pr is often associated with homeostatic adaptation to an overall increase in synaptic strength, preventing overexcitation73,74. This could explain why, despite a reduced Pr in WT, the overall excitatory synaptic strength, as indicated by sCRACM, increased.

Reports of changes in Pr range from decreased, unaltered, to increased in Fmr1 KO mice16,70,75–78. Consistent with our findings in the visual cortex, studies of CA3-CA1 hippocampal synapses in a mouse model of FX demonstrated that while the loss of FMRP did not alter basal synaptic transmission or basal release probability, it led to an abnormally increased release probability, specifically during repetitive activity79. The trend of increased Pr in FX could reflect an attempt to compensate for weaker postsynaptic responses due to low AMPAR surface expression76,80 and dendritic spine instability and insensitivity to modulation by sensory experience81. Our CRACM and SMLM spine morphology data are also consistent with these reports. There is a scarcity of studies focused on the inter-areal interaction and sensory binding in ASDs82. Our results provide valuable insights into the mechanisms of interareal interaction in the visual cortex that underlie visual learning deficits in individuals with autism, addressing a significant gap in the existing research.

E/I imbalance disruption may contribute to the attenuated oscillations in FX mice

Our findings of the elevated baseline c-Fos levels in FX mice align well with the prior reports of reduced inhibition, general cortical hyperactivity, and increased E/I ratio6. However, our inter-areal synaptic mapping data suggest that FFV1→LM and FBLM→V1 excitation and inhibition may be distinct from the conventional intracortical E/I ratio. Further supporting this complexity, our previous directed information data indicates that interlaminar inhibitory input from L2/3 fast-spiking (FS) interneurons to L5 RS PCs was increased in naïve FX mice9. These findings suggest that while overall interneurons may be hypoactive in V1, some subsets of synaptic connections between PCs and FS neurons may demonstrate synaptic connectivity distinct from the overall E/I balance. Collectively, our previous and current results indicate that relying solely on the E/I ratio may oversimplify neural circuit analysis in FX. Consistent with our data showing decreased inter-areal connectivity in FX mice, EEG synchrony analysis in children with ASD revealed diminished interhemispheric connectivity between visual areas during visual stimulation83. Similarly, resting-state fMRI studies in individuals with high-functioning autism reported decreased long-range functional connectivity84. More recently, CRACM studies demonstrated experience-dependent weakening of interhemispheric synaptic connections in the absence of postsynaptic FMRP85. In the context of theta oscillations, computational models suggest that to synchronize two oscillators together, one needs to strengthen the connection between them86. Thus, our data, which describe visual experience-dependent persistent theta oscillations in LM synchronized with V1 and the corresponding increase in inter-areal connectivity, are consistent with the computational predictions. The disrupted connectivity and reduced theta oscillations observed in the LM of FX mice support this model, suggesting that deficits in synaptic connectivity may contribute to altered sensory processing.

FX and dendrite morphology in visual familiarity

Dendritic spines exhibit a strong potential for morphological plasticity, enabling neurons to modify synaptic connectivity, adaptively remodel neural circuits, and support learning and memory processes87. Sensory experiences, even in adulthood, can drive the formation and elimination of synapses88. Typically, during development, the dendritic spine becomes shorter, the heads of the dendritic spine become smaller, and the necks widen89,90. Here, we find that the visual familiarity paradigm can also induce morphological plasticity, resembling the developmental changes described. Cell-autonomous removal of Fmr1 postnatally in postsynaptic L2/3 or L5 neurons leads to a specific reduction in the strength of callosal synaptic function mediated by AMPA receptors (AMPARs), while functions mediated by NMDA receptors remain unaffected, suggesting the presence of immature synapses in Fmr1-removed groups85. However, comparisons between P14 and P37 mice using STED microscopy revealed only subtle differences between WT and FX mice90. SMLM achieved a much higher resolution than STED91, allowing us to assess dendritic spine density and morphology in V1 and LM L5 PCs in adult WT and FX mice. We observed significant differences in dendritic spine plasticity: FX mice exhibited higher spine density, longer spines, and narrower spine necks compared to WT (Fig. 6). Visual experience increased spine density and enlarged spine heads in WT mice, but no changes were observed in FX mice (Fig. 6 and Table 1). sCRACM analysis demonstrated that visual experience-induced strengthening of synaptic connectivity was more pronounced in the FFV1→LM pathway compared to the FBLM→V1 pathway (Table 1). This differential enhancement may be attributed to stronger experience-dependent plasticity in the dendritic spines of LM neurons relative to those in V1. Consistently, super-resolution SMLM revealed a more substantial increase in dendritic spine size in LM L5 PCs than in V1, further supporting enhanced synaptic plasticity of FFV1→LM synapses (Fig. 6 and Table 1).

Our findings reveal that impaired inter-areal synchrony and plasticity in FX mice may arise from disrupted synaptic inter-areal connectivity and weakened theta oscillations, which in turn impact visual processing. These improve our understanding of FX-related circuit dysfunction and suggest potential circuit-specific therapeutic targets for restoring cognitive function in ASDs and FX.

Methods

Data collection statement

All measurements were taken from distinct samples.

Experimental model and subject details

All experimental procedures were approved by the Purdue Animal Care and Use Committee (PACUC, protocol number 140800111). Three- to four-month-old homozygous male and female Fmr1 KO (B6.129P2-Fmr1tm1Cgr/J, JAX Stock No. 003025), B6 (C57BL/6, JAX), and Thy1-ChR2-YFP (B6.Cg-Tg(Thy1-COP4/EYFP)18Gfng/J, JAX Stock No. 007612) were used for the experiment.

Head plate installation and virus injection

For surgeries of virus injection, head cap, or head plate implantation, mice were anesthetized by inhaling 1.5–2.0% isoflurane anesthesia and fixed in a stereotaxic apparatus. The location of V1 (3.0 mm lateral of midline, 0.8 mm anterior of the transverse sinus (TS) or higher visual area LM (4.0 mm lateral of midline, 1.4 mm anterior of TS) was stereotaxically identified and marked on the skull by a marker pen. For injections, the skull on the marked sites was thinned by a dental drill to the extent that it could be penetrated by an injection glass pipette, tip diameter 20 μm. Intracerebral injections were conducted by Nanoject III Injector. The glass pipette was inserted stereotaxically 0.4 mm and 0.7 mm underneath the pial surface. Fifty nanoliters of pACAGW-ChR2-Venus-AAV1 (Addgene cat#20071, titer ≥ 1 × 1013 vg/mL) was released to express ChR2 throughout the cortical layers and their outgoing axons for a long-range CRACM experiment measuring reciprocal connectivity in the visual cortex (FBLM→V1, FFV1→LM). For super-resolution imaging of LM L5 neurons, 50–100 nL diluted pACAGW-ChR2-Venus-AAV1 (titer ≥ 2 × 1012 vg/mL) was injected at 0.7 mm underneath the coordinate of LM. A customized head plate was placed on top of the skull. The skull and the head plate were covered with Metabond (Parkell S380). To control the ChR2 expression level, we injected an adequate amount of the virus, and we waited for the virus expression to plateau two to three weeks after infection before habituation. For in vivo recordings, an additional reference pin was attached to the skull and the head plate. For in vivo recordings involving V1 inhibition, 500 nL of pAAV5-CAG-ArchT-GFP (Addgene #29777) was injected into V1 at a depth of 400 µm below the pial surface, followed by a head plate and reference pin installation. After a two-week period for viral expression, the mice underwent habituation.

Visual experience paradigm and optogenetic inhibition

Mice were habituated to the training apparatus before passive visual experience training, which followed two weeks post-surgery. During the 2–3 days of habituation, a monitor displaying a grey screen was placed 17 cm in front of head-fixed mice in a dark environment for an hour/day. For passive visual training, a filtered pink noise picture of 0.12 cycles/degree was displayed, with the illuminance intensity of each pixel altered in a sinusoidal function of time at 0.75 Hz to generate a movie. Filtered pink noise movie stimulus was displayed using PsychoPy. Mice were shown 200 trials of a 200-ms pink noise movie stimulus, with a 6-s grey screen between trials, for 4–6 days. For in vivo recordings, mice were presented with 20 stimulus trials on the day of recording. For V1 inhibition in vivo experiments, two groups of mice were used; one group was injected with AAV5-CAG-ArchT-GFP virus, while the other group was not injected with any virus and served as a control. Following a two-week period to allow for viral expression, the skull over V1 was thinned down to make the area transparent for both the injected and control groups. Mice were then trained for 4–6 days using the paradigm described for the passive visual training. Across training days, V1 was inhibited post-visual stimulus (0.7–2.7 s) by placing an optic fiber on the surface of the thinned skull and sending a 2-s 532 nm laser pulse at 7.5 mW, for both the injected and control groups. On the recording day, the thinned skull was removed over V1, and a craniotomy was performed over LM to record simultaneously from both regions, while presenting 20 trials of the visual stimulus.

iDISCO whole-brain immunostaining

The brains were first perfused and collected 90 min after the visual experience paradigm was conducted. They were then stored in a 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) solution for 24 h, followed by three washes with 1× PBS for 30 min each. The samples are processed following the protocol from the previous literature92–96. The fixed samples underwent a series of washes in methanol of increasing concentrations: 20%, 40%, 60%, 80%, and 100%, each for 1 h, with two repetitions at 100% methanol. Subsequently, the samples were incubated overnight in a 66% dichloromethane (DCM) solution and 33% methanol. The next day, the samples were washed twice with 100% methanol for 1 h each. To bleach the brains, they were exposed overnight to a solution of 5% hydrogen peroxide in methanol at 4 °C overnight. The next day, the samples underwent a rehydration process using a reverse series of methanol concentrations ranging from 100% to 20% in double-distilled water (ddH2O), with additional 1-h washes in PBS. Next, the brains underwent two 1-h washes in PBS/0.2% Triton-X-100, followed by two days of permeabilization at 37 °C in a solution composed of 0.3 M glycine/20% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO)/1× PBS with 0.2% Triton-X-100. Subsequently, the samples were blocked for two days at 37 °C in a solution of PBS containing 0.2% Triton-X-100, 10% DMSO, and 6% normal donkey serum. The brains were then incubated for seven days in a primary antibody solution (rabbit anti-c-Fos, 1:1000; Synaptic Systems #226-003) diluted in PBS containing 0.2% Tween-20, 5% DMSO, 3% normal donkey serum, and 10 μg/mL heparin. The samples were then washed in PBS containing 0.2% Tween-20 and 10 μg/mL heparin (PtWH) over 24 h, with 5 changes of solution. The brains were incubated in a secondary antibody solution (donkey anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 647, 1:500; Thermo Fisher Scientific #A-31573) diluted in PBS containing 0.2% Tween-20, 5% DMSO, 3% donkey serum, and 10 μg/mL heparin for seven days. Subsequently, the brains were washed with PtWH for 24 h as before, followed by dehydration using a decreasing series of methanol concentrations in ddH2O (20% to 100%), as previously described in ref. 93. Specifically, fixed samples were washed in 20% methanol (in double-distilled H₂O) for 1 h, 40% methanol for 1 h, 60% methanol for 1 h, 80% methanol for 1 h, and 100% methanol for 1 h twice. The following day, the brains were incubated in a solution of 66% DCM/ 33% methanol for 3 h and then twice in 100% DCM for 15 min each. After the DCM incubation, the brains were transferred to a clean tube containing dibenzyl ether until they were ready for imaging.

Light sheet microscope image acquisition

The right hemispheres of the iDISCO+ processed samples were positioned for sagittal imaging (with the right lateral side facing up) using an UltraMicroscope II light-sheet microscope (Miltenyi Biotec, Bielefeld, Germany) equipped with a scientific complementary metal–oxide–semiconductor camera (Andor Neo), a 2×/0.5 objective lens (MVPLAPO 2×), and a dipping cap with a working distance of 6 mm. The image capture was facilitated by InspectorPro software version 7.1.4. The microscope was equipped with an NKT Photonics SuperK EXTREME EXW-12 white light laser with three fixed light-sheet-generating lenses on each side. Scans were made at 0.8× magnification (1.6× effective magnification) with a light-sheet numerical aperture of 0.148. Excitation filters of 480/30 and 560/40 were used. Emission filters of 525/50 and 595/40 were used. The samples were scanned with a step size of 3 μm for the 480 and 560 channels (1 acquisition per plane with 560 ms exposure. Laser power was set at 55% for 480 channels and 10% for 560 channels).

Identification of activated brain regions

Images that were acquired from the light-sheet microscope were analyzed from the end of the olfactory bulbs (the olfactory bulbs were not included in the analysis) to the beginning of the hindbrain and cerebellum. Counts of c-Fos+ nuclei from each sample were identified for each brain region using ClearMap295,97. ClearMap uses autofluorescence acquired in the 488 channel to align the brain to the Allen Mouse Brain Atlas and then registers c-Fos counts to regions annotated by the atlas. C-Fos counts of the midbrain reticular nucleus (MRN), primary visual area (VISP), and lateral visual area (VISl) were quantified.

Data acquisition and analysis of extracellular recordings

In vivo, simultaneous extracellular recordings were taken using two 64-channel silicon probes. Craniotomies were performed on the mouse over the V1 and LM regions on the stereotaxic surgery apparatus under isoflurane gas. The mouse was then head-fixed, and the two probes were placed over the craniotomies98 and inserted at V1 and LM using micromanipulators (NewScale). Before insertion, artificial cerebrospinal fluid (ACSF) was added to the brain’s surface, and the probes were inserted at a speed of 50 µm/min to a depth of 950 µm. Fifteen minutes after insertion, data acquisition was initiated using OpenEphys hardware and software. Band-pass filtered data (300–6000 Hz) was acquired at 20KHz. Units were detected using the default KiloSort parameters and manually sorted using the Phy template GUI8,99.

All data analysis was performed using Python code written in our lab. Raw data was acquired at 20 kHz, passed through a low band-pass filter at 300 Hz, and then down-sampled to 1 kHz. The noise was removed from raw traces using a 60 Hz notch filter. LFP traces were selected for each mouse from the channel (from the three shanks) showing the strongest response post visual stimulus (0.5–1 s) and averaged across 20 trials for comparison between groups. These trial-averaged LFP traces were used to perform TF analysis filtered at various frequencies between 4 Hz and 80 Hz using the Morlet wavelet method. Power spectrum analyses were done by averaging the extracted powers normalized to baseline period dB within a time window of 0.7–1.25 s post visual stimulus, and further quantified by calculating the area under the curve for these time window averaged power values for 4–8 Hz, 8–12 Hz, 12–30 Hz, 30–40 Hz, 50–70 Hz, and 30–70 Hz ranges of frequencies.

Single units were obtained from raw data bandpass filtered at 300–6000 Hz using Kilosort for spike clustering. Noisy units were removed by manually sorting through Phy. Peristimulus time histograms (PSTHs) were calculated with 10 ms bins for each unit. The histogram was smoothed using a 30 ms standard deviation Gaussian kernel. The firing rates of each unit were normalized by calculating the Z-scores ((FRt-mean(FRt0–t1))/std(FRt0–t1)). Population Z-score line plots were normalized to the baseline period (0–0.5 s). Duration of oscillations was quantified using a peak detection function with a minimum peak height of at least 1.0 SD from baseline and a minimum separation of 100 ms between peaks.

Statistical analysis was done using Python packages SciPy, and Pingouin. Unit population distributions for the duration of oscillations were compared between groups by performing two-sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to check for the normality of the data. For normally distributed data, two-way ANOVA was used, followed by the corresponding Tukey’s HSD test. For non-normal distributions, the Mann–Whitney U-test was used.

DIA

We conducted a DIA as described previously in ref. 9,35,36.

Spike train preprocessing

For each unit, we used data that was between 500 ms and 1500 ms after each stimulus onset. This window was chosen to capture activity within the stimulus-induced persistent oscillatory period. Time was discretized to 1 ms bins, leading to binary-valued spike trains. Periods of time for which no units had activity for 100 ms were removed to avoid a large data imbalance.

Regression—overview

With each unit’s spiking represented as a binary-valued vector, we modeled the propensity of spiking through a logistic regression model (equivalently, a generalized linear model using the logit link function). The spiking propensity could depend on each unit’s recent spiking activity, as well as that of other units. For each unit Y, the likelihood of a spike at time t, denoted as Y(t)=1, conditioned on a column vector x of exogenous variables we will describe in more detail shortly (including a constant offset) and with a row vector θ of model parameters, was modeled as

Regression—exogenous variables

In modeling the activity of each unit Y, Y’s own recent spiking activity was represented with two exogenous variables. The first variable was Y(t−1)+Y(t−2), designed to represent the refractory period. The second variable was Y(t−3)+…+Y(t−10) designed to capture self-dependence. For each unit X, a single variable X(t−1)+…+X(t−10) was used to model Y’s activity. Like in refs. 9,29, a 10 ms window was chosen to balance capturing effects from putative pre-synaptic units with the effects of latent common inputs.

Regression—training/testing sets

For each unit Y, after exogenous variables were computed, the data from trials with the same stimulus were pooled and then randomly split 50/50 into a training set and a test set. The random split was stratified by the binary Y(t) values.

Regression—regularization

We used an L1 regularization term in our logistic regression (similar to the LASSO method for linear regression) to mitigate overfitting. For a parameter vector θ, its L1 norm, denoted as ∣θ∣1, is the sum of the absolute values of each element except for the constant offset. Thus, the time-averaged negative log-likelihood of unit Y’s activity, using model vector θ, denoting the vector of exogenous variables computed for Y at time t as x(t), and with a hyper-parameter λ, was

Conceptually, minimizing the first term alone, the negative log-likelihood, will lead to a model (parameterized by θ) that fits the data, as well as possible, likely using all exogenous variables (equivalently, all elements of θ will be non-zero). Minimizing the second term alone, which can be viewed as a measure of model complexity, will drive elements of θ to 0, leading to a model that ignores all exogenous variables except the constant offset. We used a cross-validation procedure, described below, to choose the hyperparameter λ.

Regression—cross-validation

We used 5-fold cross-validation to select a hyper-parameter λ used for each unit Y. We considered forty candidate values of λ uniformly spaced on a log-scale between 1e − 4 and 1e + 4. Those values of λ were chosen to ensure the empirically selected λ was always within the largest and smallest candidate values and to balance computational complexity (considering more candidate λ values require more computation) with accuracy (so the selected λ would be close to what would have been chosen with a finer grid).

For that procedure, the training set was randomly partitioned into five parts. For each value of λ, each part was held out once, a model was fit on data from the remaining four parts using the current λ, and the log-likelihood of the resulting model was evaluated on the held-out data. The five log-likelihoods (one for each part that was held out) for the current λ were averaged. The hyperparameter λ with the highest log-likelihood was selected. A new model was fit on all training data using that λ. Putative pre-synaptic units of Y were those with non-zero entries in the final fitted θ vector. We will later discuss calculating directed information from test data using the final model.

Regression—implementation

Regularized regression was performed in Python (v3.8.8) using the LogisticRegressionCV function in the scikit-learn (v1.1.1) package with an ‘l1’ penalty, five-folds, and with fixed hyper-parameter λ values for cross-validation noted above.

We next describe how we aggregated unit-level normalized directed information values into area-wise values. For each unit Y, we fit models and then evaluated directed information with candidate pre-synaptic units for each combination of recording areas: using candidate units from both V1 and LM, only using units from V1, only using units from LM, and a model only using Y’s own past. Let θAll, θV1, θAL, and θNone denote the corresponding model parameter vectors, and let IAll, IV1, IV1, and INone=0 denote the respective directed information values.

Significance testing

We computed the significance of differences post and pre-training using Monte–Carlo approximations for two-sided permutation tests. The 1e + 5 Monte–Carlo iterations were used for each difference reported. For each iteration, each Shapley value (of normalized directed information values) used to calculate the median values from a pre-synaptic area to a unit in a post-synaptic area was relabeled as pre-training or a post-training connection using corresponding proportions of connections in the pre- and post-stimulus training experiments. The difference of the medians (after relabeling) was then computed.

The Monte–Carlo approximations were computed in Python (v3.8.8). We corrected the p-values separately for each figure with the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure (Benjamini and Hochberg, 1995), implemented in the Python statsmodels (v0.13.2) package.

Acute V1 slices preparation

The naïve or visually experienced mice with virus injections were anesthetized with a mixture of saline-diluted ketamine (59399-114-10, Akron, 100 mg/kg body weight) and xylazine (343750, HVS, 16 mg/kg body weight), and deep anesthesia was confirmed by toe pinching. The perfusion and acute slice recovery strategies were the same as those described in our previous paper9, except that the sucrose holding cutting buffer was substituted with the choline holding buffer. The mice were first perfused with ice-cold choline artificial cerebrospinal fluid (choline ACSF, composition in mM: 110 choline chloride, 26 NaHCO3, 11.6 ascorbic acid, 10 Dextrose, 7 MgCl2, 3.1 Na-pyruvate, 2.5 KCl, 1.25 NaH2PO4, 0.5 CaCl2; pH = 7.3–7.4) transcardially till the livers turned pale. The mice were then decapitated, and the brains were extracted and trimmed to be glued to the cutting chamber of the vibratome (VT 1000 Leica). 300-micrometer coronal brain sections containing the V1 were cut in ice-cold carbogenated (carbogen: 95% O2, and 5% CO2) choline ACSF using a vibratome. The slices were then transferred to an incubation chamber filled with ACSF (composition in mM: 124 NaCl, 26 NaHCO3, 10 Dextrose, 2.5 KCl, 2 CaCl2, 1.25 NaH2PO4, 0.8 MgCl2; pH = 7.3–7.4) and warmed up in a 32 °C water bath for 30 min. The chamber was removed from the water bath, and slices were incubated at room temperature for 1 h. After the equilibrium, the slices were ready for clamp-patch recording and remained healthy for 10 h in the incubation chambers.

Electrophysiology and data acquisition for whole-cell patch-clamp recording

Patch-clamp recordings were conducted on an electrophysiology rig (SliceScope Pro 1000, Scientifica). The brain slices were settled down in a submersion-type recording chamber with a slice hold-down and perfused with carbogenated ACSF continuously during the whole recording. Neurons were imaged with infrared differential interference contrast optics from the rig equipped with a 10× objective lens and a 40× water immersion objective lens (Olympus) and ROLERA™ Bolt Scientific CMOS Camera (QImaging). The resistance of the recording pipette was 4–7 MO, and the pipette was filled with the intracellular pipette solution (composition in mM: 103 D-gluconic acid, 103 CsOH, 20 HEPES, 10 Na2− phosphocreatine, 5 QX314-Cl, 5 TEA-Cl, 4 Mg-ATP, 2.8 NaCl, 0.3 Na2-GTP, 0.2 EGTA; pH = 7.2–7.3; 290 mOsm). In some experiments, a small amount of 4% w/v Alexa Fluor 568 (A-10437, ThermoFisher Scientific) dissolved in internal solution was back-loaded into the glass electrode to label the patched cell. All neurons were whole-cell patched in voltage-clamp mode. All EPSCs were recorded in voltage-clamp mode with a holding potential of −80 mV to −70 mV, while IPSCs were recorded at 0 mV to 10 mV. Electrophysiological data were acquired with an amplifier (Multiclamp 700B, Molecular Devices) and a digitizer (Digitata 1550, Molecular Devices). The acquired data were recorded with Clampex 10.4 (Molecular Devices) and filtered with a 20 kHz low-pass filter. Series resistance was continuously monitored during recordings, and cells showing changes greater than ±10% were excluded from analysis.

Channelrhodopsin-assisted circuit mapping (CRACM)

To measure the FFV1→LM and FBLM→V1, we patched the excitatory PCs in LM or V1 layer 2/3, 4, 5, respectively, identified by the morphology and relative location in the cortical layers of coronal slices. 470 nm LED light to activate ChR2 was generated with an LED light source (high-power LED Collimator Source, 470 nm, 50 W, Mightex), and the light stimulation patterns were produced by a patterned illuminator (Polygon 400, Mightex)103. The square light, 67 μm × 67 μm, was delivered in a 10 by 10 grid covering 670 μm × 670 μm areas of the cortical regions centered at patched neurons in a pseudo-random sequence through the 10× objective lens. The LED and patterned illuminator were controlled by the manufacturer’s software, and stimulation and recording were synchronized by the Digidata 1550. CRACM heatmaps were plotted from the amplitudes of light-induced post-synaptic current at each pixel.

Histology

The acute brain slices with Alexa Fluor 568 dye-filled were fixed with 4% PFA and imaged by a confocal microscope (Zeiss LSM 710). For Super-resolution imaging sample preparation, we used the male litters of male Thy1-ChR2-EYFP crossed with female B6 for control, and male Thy1-ChR2-EYFP crossed with female Fmr1 KO and virus-injected B6 and Fmr1 KO mice.

The 3–4-month-old mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injections of the mix of saline-diluted ketamine (59399-114-10, Akron, 100 mg/kg body weight) and xylazine (343750, HVS, 16 mg/kg body weight) and transcardially perfused with phosphate-buffered saline (1× PBS, 1:10 diluted from DSP32060, Dot Scientific), followed by 4% PFA (1:4 diluted with 1× PBS from PFA 16% Aqueous Solution, Electron Microscopy Sciences) to pre-fix the brain. The brain was excavated from the skull and immersed in 4% PFA at 4 °C overnight. The fixed brain sample was then cut into 30–50 μm coronal sections on a vibratome (1000 Plus, Vibratome). For immunohistochemistry staining, the brain sections were washed three times in the wash buffer (0.1% Triton X-100 in 1× PBS) with gentle shaking (120 rpm, Orbi-Shaker, Benchmark) for 15 min each time. The brain sections were then incubated in a blocking buffer (5% BSA in 1× PBS; A9647, Sigma-Aldrich) for 1.5 h with gentle shaking. After blocking, the brain sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with chicken anti-GFP antibody (ab13970, Abcam, diluted 1:1000 in blocking buffer). To remove extra primary antibodies, the sections were washed three times as in the first step. The slices were incubated with goat anti-chicken Alexa Fluor 647-conjugated antibody (A21449, Invitrogen) diluted 1:600 in the wash buffer for 2 h with gentle shaking. After being washed three times in the wash buffer, the sections were kept in 1× PBS before being mounted for super-resolution imaging.

4Pi super-resolution microscope setup

A 4Pi single-molecule localization microscope was built using dual opposing 100×/1.4-NA oil-immersion objectives (UPLSAPO 100XO, Olympus) and an sCMOS camera (Orca-Flash4.0v2, Hamamatsu). This setup allowed for a magnification of 50×, a field of view of 20 × 20 μm², and an effective pixel size of 129 nm32,58,104,105. The coherent fluorescence from both objectives coincided and was aligned using nonpolarized and polarized beam splitters (NT47-009 and NT49-002, Edmund Optics) to generate four beams. These four beams were directed to different regions on the sCMOS camera and acquired simultaneously. Customized Babinet-Soleil BK7/Quartz wedges were used to control the P- and S-polarized phase delays, and two deformable mirrors (Multi-5.5, Boston Micromachines) were placed in conjugate pupil planes of respective objectives to control systematic aberrations. A 642-nm continuous-wave laser (2RU-VFL-P-2000-642-B1R, MPB Communications) was utilized for HILO excitation through the bottom objective. The tilt angle of the excitation was optimized to 54°, enabling the highest signal-to-background ratio during imaging. Quad-band dichroic and bandpass filters (FF01-446/523/600/677-25, Di01-R405/488/561/635-17.5 × 24, Semrock) were used to filter fluorescence from the excitation and unwanted background. The microscope setup was synchronized through a custom LabVIEW program.

Optical clearing and sample mounting

Right before data acquisition, the labeled brain sections were placed on cleaned coverslips, and the visual cortex regions were positioned at the center of the coverslips. The optical clearing was performed by adding 200 μL ultrafast optical clearing (FOCM) reagent of 30% w/v urea (U15-500, Fisher Scientific), 20% w/v D-sorbitol (S1876-500G, Sigma-Aldrich), and 5% w/v glycerol (G5516, Sigma-Aldrich) in DMSO (276855, Sigma-Aldrich) on the samples for 1–2 min at room temperature106. After optical clearing, the samples were washed once with 1× PBS, and 1 μL 200-nm crimson beads (1:106 diluted from custom-designed beads in deionized water, Invitrogen) were added near the outer edge region of the visual cortex. The samples were mounted with 70 μL freshly prepared cyclooctatetraene-based buffer (10% w/v glucose in 50 mM Tris, 50 mM NaCl, 10 mM MEA (M9768, Sigma-Aldrich), 50 mM BME (63689, Sigma-Aldrich), 2 mM COT (138924, Sigma-Aldrich), 2.5 mM PCA (37580, Sigma-Aldrich) and 50 nM PCD (P8279-25UN, Sigma-Aldrich), pH 8.0) and another coverslip on top107. The sandwiched samples were sealed using silicone dental glue (Twinsil speed 22, Picodent) and left to dry for 30 min at room temperature.

Spine data acquisition and analysis

The excitation power was set to 30 W/cm2 for finding regions of interest (ROI) and bead measurement. Before imaging, excitation was elevated to 12 kW/cm2 and scanned around the ROI to transfer most of the fluorophores into dark states and bleach autofluorescence for 30–60 s. For imaging, the excitation was set to 7 kW/cm2, and 100,000 to 140,000 frames of raw blinking images were recorded with an acquisition time of 20 ms. Each raw image was first cropped into four quadrants based on arranged regions on the sCMOS camera and underwent 4Pi super-resolution reconstruction using the phase-retrieval-based 4Pi single molecule localization algorithm (PR-4Pi)105. The 4Pi point spread function (4Pi-PSF) was modeled using the two pupil functions of the objectives and the interfered bead stack from a single bead near the ROI. To retrieve pupil functions, the bead was scanned across z positions from –1 to 1 µm, with a step size of 100 nm, a frame rate of 10 Hz, and 5 frames per z position, and acquired fluorescence from the top and bottom objectives, respectively. The resulting bead measurements were subjected to a phase retrieval algorithm to obtain the pupil functions. The interferometric bead data were acquired by scanning across z positions from –1 to 1 µm, with a step size of 20 nm, a frame rate of 10 Hz, and 5 frames per z position, using interfered coherent fluorescence.

3D spine structure quantification

The targeted spine was manually identified from the reconstruction results by marking along the spine neck to the spine head. The software then traced these marks as a curve (‘fit’, model type ‘smoothingspline’, MATLAB), straightened the spine along the X direction, and segmented the localizations into cross sections. Each cross-section possessed dimensions of 600–1000 nm in width, 600–1000 nm in height, and 150 nm in length, with a 50 nm overlap in the X direction between neighbors. For visualization, the segmented localizations were rendered as XY and YZ cross-sectional images with a pixel size of 6 nm. These images were pseudo-colored based on their relative Z positions and were smoothed using a 2D Gaussian blur with an RMS width of 6 nm. To quantify the structure, the software captured the boundaries of the largest structures from discrete localizations (‘alphaShape’, alpha shape radius of 0.4, MATLAB). The structural centers and the semi-minor axis of the structures were estimated by fitting an elliptical function to the localizations within the alpha-shape area, which were used in the spine area and volume calculation.

Visual discrimination behavior paradigm

Mice were placed on water restriction in order to train them for the visual discrimination task. Their weights were recorded every day, and once they reached an ideal weight (>80% of their original weight), they were started on behavior training using open-field visual touchscreen chambers (Lafayette Instrument)108. Mice were trained on two pre-training stages before moving to the visual discrimination task. In the initial stage (stage 1), mice received water for every trial, signaled by a white light near the water port. Once mice had learned to drink water from the port, they were moved to stage 2. During this stage, mice were required to touch a grey screen to receive a water reward. Once they completed 70 successful trials, they were moved to the final stage—the pairwise visual discrimination task. Mice were shown two different stimuli simultaneously, a Go stimulus (filtered pink noise, 0.12cpd) and a No-Go stimulus (filtered pink noise, 0.03cpd) for 3 s. Trials were randomly presented with the Go stimulus shown on the right to the No-Go, or to the left. Trials were separated by a grey screen shown for 5 s. If the mice touched the Go stimulus, they would receive a water reward; if they touched the No-Go stimulus, they would be given a timeout of 10 s. Once mice were able to complete 70 trials with the correct response for three consecutive days, they were considered to have learned the discrimination task. To test the ability of mice to distinguish between the familiar Go stimulus and a Novel stimulus (30° sinusoidal grating, 0.2 cpd), we performed a test once they had completed the final stage. On the test day, we randomly presented trials with the Go and No-Go stimuli (presented either on the left or right) along with trials with the Go stimulus and a completely Novel stimulus (presented either on the left or right).

Trials where mice actively touched either the Go stimulus or the No-Go stimulus were considered to be “active trials”, while trials where mice did not touch either stimulus were considered “inactive trials”. To account for motivation, analysis was done by taking the percentage of all correct responses to the total number of trials presented, as well as to only the “active trials”.
