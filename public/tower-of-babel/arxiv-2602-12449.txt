Computationally sufficient statistics for Ising models
Abhijith Jayakumar, Shreya Shukla, Marc Vuffray, Andrey Y. Lokhov, Sidhant Misra

Abstract
Learning Gibbs distributions using only sufficient statistics has long been recognized as a computationally hard problem. On the other hand, computationally efficient algorithms for learning Gibbs distributions rely on access to full sample configurations generated from the model. For many systems of interest that arise in physical contexts, expecting a full sample to be observed is not practical, and hence it is important to look for computationally efficient methods that solve the learning problem with access to only a limited set of statistics. We examine the trade-offs between the power of computation and observation within this scenario, employing the Ising model as a paradigmatic example. We demonstrate that it is feasible to reconstruct the model parameters for a model with ℓ 1 \ell_{1} width γ \gamma by observing statistics up to an order of O ⁡ ( γ ) O(\gamma) . This approach allows us to infer the model’s structure and also learn its couplings and magnetic fields. We also discuss a setting where prior information about structure of the model is available and show that the learning problem can be solved efficiently with even more limited observational power.



1
Introduction

Trade-offs between sample and computational complexities of learning tasks has garnered much interest in learning theory (
[
25
,
1
]
). However, in certain cases, the learner’s access to the data is itself constrained by limited observational power, i.e., instead of observing full samples, they may only observe some aggregated statistics or low-order moments. This raises a fundamental question: how does this observational power interact with computational tractability?

We study this question in the context of learning Gibbs distributions over discrete variables, where such a setting naturally arises as an interpolation between two sets of prior works. The first are hardness results that show that learning the parameters of such a distribution given only sufficient statistics is a computationally intractable problem
[
14
,
3
]
. On the other hand, starting from the breakthrough by Bresler (
[
4
]
), provably efficient algorithms that use full independent samples from the model are known for this problem. Several other works have also looked at this learning task from other types of data models like samples drawn from a dynamic (
[
8
]
) or from a metastable distribution (
[
10
]
). All the efficient algorithms here assume that full sample configurations of the model can be observed. This motivates us to look for an intermediate regime that has been left unexplored by the aforementioned literature. We ask the question:

Is it possible to learn a discrete graphical model from a limited set of statistics, such that the computational effort scales as a polynomial in the number of variables and in the data size?

We initiate the study of this question in the Ising class of models.
Here the distribution is over
p
binary spins,
\underline{\sigma}\in\{-1,1\}^{p}
. The distribution that we are attempting to learn is a Gibbs distribution associated with a general quadratic function over these variables,

\displaystyle\mu(\underline{\sigma})

\displaystyle\propto\exp(E(\underline{\sigma};\underline{\theta}^{*})),

(1)

\displaystyle E(\underline{\sigma};\underline{\theta}^{*})

\displaystyle=\sum_{u\neq v}\theta^{*}_{u,v}\sigma_{u}\sigma_{v}+\sum_{u}\theta^{*}_{u}\sigma_{u}.

(2)

Following previous works, our aim here will be to learn the parameters
\underline{\theta}^{*}
. We will assume that we know an upper bound on the
\ell_{1}
-width
of the model. That is, we know a
\gamma
such that,
\sum_{v}|\theta^{*}_{uv}|+|\theta^{*}_{u}|\leq\gamma,~~\forall u\in[p].

We refer to the expectation values of monomials (
\mathbb{E}\sigma_{i}
,
\mathbb{E}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j},~\mathbb{E}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}\sigma_{k},\ldots
) as to the
statistics
of this model. It is well known that the parameters of the model are entirely fixed by the first (
\mathbb{E}\sigma_{i}
) and second order (
\mathbb{E}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}
) statistics. However, performing the actual inversion from these statistics to the parameter has been generally proven to be a hard problem (
[
14
,
3
]
). Efficient estimators which are based on observing full sample configurations, on the other hand, can be implicitly viewed as having complete access to all the statistics of the model (up to order
p
). In this paper we will consider a setting where the learner is given access to only monomials up to some degree
d
.

For solving this learning problem, we will use a modification of the
Interaction Screening Estimator
(ISE). The ISE, first introduced in
[
23
]
, is an M-estimator that works by minimizing a convex loss function for every variable
u\in[p]
subject to constraints on the
\ell_{1}
-width of the model. Given
n
samples from the true distribution,

\mathcal{L}_{u,n}(\underline{\theta})\equiv\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}e^{-\sigma^{(t)}_{u}(\theta_{u}+\sum_{v\neq u}\theta_{u,v}\sigma^{(t)}_{v})},

(3)

\underline{\hat{\theta}}_{u}=\min_{\underline{\theta}:|\underline{\theta}|\leq\gamma}\mathcal{L}_{u,n}(\theta).

(4)

This constrained optimization problem is solved for each of the
p
variables in the model to estimate the parameters in the energy function connected to it. It has been shown to efficiently recover the parameters and structure of Ising models and other discrete graphical models
[
23
,
24
]
. As shown in these works, this estimator requires exact configurations to be evaluated. However, the exponential function, combined with the
\ell_{1}
constraint, suggests the possibility of approximating the exponential function with a low-degree polynomial.

This idea is explored briefly in
[
13
]
by expanding the interaction screening loss to second-order. At sufficiently low
\gamma
this second-order approximation can be expected to approximate the full IS estimator well. But this strategy is bound to fail at higher values of
\gamma
. Moreover, expanding the exponential as a polynomial can potentially lead to a loss of convexity, and in turn to the loss of learning guarantees the output of ISE. If we could find a provably effective way to approximate the exponential in the interaction screening method using, say, a degree
d
polynomial, then that implies that the parameters of the model can be estimated using statistics up to order
O(d)
.

In this work, we propose to approximate the gradient of the IS loss using a polynomial approximation. This allows us to analyze this problem as a convex optimization problem with a corrupted gradient oracle. From the known robustness properties of gradient descent, we show that we can get close the true optimum using this approximate gradient. Our results show that computing statistics up to order
O(\gamma)
from
O(e^{8\gamma}\gamma^{5}\log(p))
observations is sufficient to match learning guarantees from the setting where full configurations can be observed.

Our results also give rise to a natural question about the corresponding lower bounds, i.e., what is the lowest order of statistics that will allow us to tractably learn the model. Our results point to the existence of a type of information-computation tradeoff in this learning problem. In particular, the power of statistics between information-theoretically sufficient (order
2
) and that is proven in this work to be computationally sufficient (order
O(\gamma)
) is currently not quantified in literature.

1.1
Further Related Work

Learning from statistical queries.

The Statistical Query (SQ) model, introduced by Kearns (
[
11
]
), formalizes learning scenarios in which the algorithm accesses data only through expectations of bounded functions rather than individual sample. Follow-up works have shown that many learning problems that are information-theoretically solvable become computationally intractable when restricted to SQ access alone (
[
6
,
7
]
). These results show that the form in which data is observed can play a crucial role in determining computational feasibility.
Our setting differs from the classical SQ model in two aspects. First, we consider access to a fixed collection of low-degree moments rather than arbitrary adaptively chosen queries. Second, we allow these moments to be reused across multiple optimization steps. Nonetheless, both settings emphasize how restrictions on observable statistics affect the computational complexity of learning.

Mean-field heuristics.

An approach to the inverse Ising problem is to replace the true Gibbs measure with a tractable mean-field approximation, yielding explicit estimators for the parameters of the model purely from empirical statistics (
[
16
]
). In the simplest mean field reconstruction, inverting the correlation matrix gives a closed form estimate for the parameters of the models.
More accurate variants incorporate linear-response corrections and, in particular, the Onsager reaction term (
[
19
,
22
]
). These methods are attractive for their simplicity, though they are ultimately uncontrolled approximations which fail at larger
\gamma
.

Continuous variables and quantum generalizations.

It is natural to consider the same problem in other types of alphabets. For continuous variable models, learning Gibbs distributions can be solved computationally efficiently using score matching, which can be viewed as morally equivalent to Interaction Screening or Pseudo-likelihood methods as it breaks down the global learning problem into single variable problems
[
18
,
9
]
. However, score matching does not require access to full samples to learn. For a distribution specified by a degree-
d
polynomial energy function, score matching loss only requires access to statistics up to order
2d
. This shows an important distinction between continuous and discrete alphabet learning scenarios.

The quantum generalization of the Gibbs learning task has been shown to be tractable in recent works
[
2
,
15
]
. These algorithms work by using a sum-of-squares hierarchy to match certain statistics of the true quantum gibbs state with those of a parametric hypothesis. This implicitly suggests, even in the classical setting, that the learning problem is solvable using a set of statistics strictly smaller than the complete set. However, in the quantum setting the structure of the model is assumed to be known and sparse. In the classical setting, this makes the learning problem manifestly tractable using some lower order moments that depend only on the sparsity. This setting is discussed in Section
2.3
. These considerations show that the minimal set of statistics required to solve such a problem will also depend strongly on the prior information we have about the true model.

Suppose we want to achieve error

for every

The

is the Lambert-

The total number of steps the algorithm takes is

Proof of Theorem
1
.

The main idea of the proof is as follows. Consider the ideal interaction screening loss function given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{u}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-E_{u}(\underline{\sigma},\underline{\theta}_{u})}\right].

(5)

For each
u
, the ideal IS loss function in (
5
) is convex and is known to have its minimum at the true model parameters
\underline{\theta}^{*}
, see
[
23
]
. Gradient descent for minimizing convex functions has the crucial property of
robustness
, i.e. bounded errors incurred in the gradient computation translate favorably in a controlled way to errors in the final loss function
[
5
]
(also see Lemma
3
). Using this fact and by bounding the error incurred by computing the approximate gradients as in Algorithm
1
we can prove Theorem
1
.

The error in the approximate gradient computation is from two sources. The first is a
polynomial approximation error
(
\varepsilon_{poly}
) incurred by approximating the exponential by a finite degree polynomial. The second is a
statistical approximation error
(
\varepsilon_{stat}
) representing the finite sample error from replacing the expectation values by their
n
sample average.

We first describe how to obtain the approximate gradients using only moments up to order
d
from the data. The exact gradients of the loss function
\mathcal{L}_{u}
are,

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{u}(\underline{\theta}_{u})}{\partial\theta_{u}}

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma_{u}e^{-E_{u}(\underline{\sigma},\underline{\theta}_{u})}\right];\ \ \dfrac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{u}(\underline{\theta}_{u})}{\partial\theta_{uv}}=-\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma_{u}\sigma_{v}e^{-E_{u}(\underline{\sigma},\underline{\theta}_{u})}\right],~~u\neq v,\ \

(6)

Going forward, we will focus on
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{u}(\underline{\theta}_{u})}{\partial\theta_{uv}}
to demonstrate how the polynomial approximation works. The approximation to
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{u}(\underline{\theta}_{u})}{\partial\theta_{u}}
is mechanically the same.

We begin by approximating the exponential function using a polynomial approximation

\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma_{u}\sigma_{v}e^{-E_{u}(\underline{\sigma},\underline{\theta}_{u})}\right]=\sum_{k\leq d}\dfrac{(-1)^{k}\mathbb{E}[\sigma^{k+1}_{u}\sigma_{v}(\sum_{j\neq u}\theta_{uj}\sigma_{j}+\theta_{u})^{k}]}{k!}+\varepsilon_{poly}

(7)

Here we have used
\varepsilon_{poly}
to denote the truncation error. Later in the proof we will set
d
by tuning this quantity via Lemma
2
.

Now we will expand the term in the expectation value using the multinomial expansion. For this purpose define,

\mathcal{M}_{k}\equiv\{(m_{1},\ldots,m_{p})\in\mathbb{N}^{p}|\sum_{i}m_{i}=k\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad C_{\underline{\theta},\underline{m}}\equiv\frac{k!\ \ \theta_{u}^{m_{u}}\prod_{j\neq u}\theta_{uj}^{\,m_{j}}}{\prod_{j\in[p]}m_{j}!}.

Using these we can show that,

\mathbb{E}[\sigma^{k+1}_{u}\sigma_{v}(\sum_{j\neq u}\theta_{uj}\sigma_{j}+\theta_{u})^{k}]=\sum_{\underline{m}\in\mathcal{M}_{k}}C_{\underline{\theta},\underline{m}}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sigma_{v}\sigma_{u}^{\,k+1}\prod_{j\neq u}\sigma_{j}^{\,m_{j}}\right]

(8)

This expansion allows us to estimate the expectation value of a random variable that depends on the optimization parameters by using parameter independent statistics computed from data. The largest value that
k
can take is
d
. From (
8
), we see that an estimate for
\mathbb{E}[\sigma^{k+1}_{u}\sigma_{v}(\sum_{j\neq u}\theta_{uj}\sigma_{j}+\theta_{u})^{k}]
for every

can be constructed entirely using statistics from

which are precisely the estimates used to compute the approximate gradient in Algorithm

\displaystyle\widetilde{\nabla}_{n}\mathcal{L}(\underline{\theta})=\sum_{k\leq d}\dfrac{(-1)^{k}\mathbb{E}_{n}[\sigma^{k+1}_{u}\sigma_{v}(\sum_{j\neq u}\theta_{uj}\sigma_{j}+\theta_{u})^{k}]}{k!}

(9)

where
\mathbb{E}_{n}
denotes the expectation using finite
n
-sample approximation. As alluded to earlier, the error in this approximate gradient is given by the sum of the polynomial and the statistical approximation errors.

\displaystyle\|\nabla\mathcal{L}(\underline{\theta})-\widetilde{\nabla}_{n}\mathcal{L}(\underline{\theta})\|_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon_{poly}+\varepsilon_{stat},

(10)

where
\varepsilon_{poly}
is defined by (
7
) and

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{stat}=\sum_{k\leq d}\dfrac{(-1)^{k}\mathbb{E}_{n}[\sigma^{k+1}_{u}\sigma_{v}(\sum_{j\neq u}\theta_{uj}\sigma_{j}+\theta_{u})^{k}]}{k!}-\sum_{k\leq d}\dfrac{(-1)^{k}\mathbb{E}[\sigma^{k+1}_{u}\sigma_{v}(\sum_{j\neq u}\theta_{uj}\sigma_{j}+\theta_{u})^{k}]}{k!}.

The statistical error is bounded using appropriate concentration equalities and is given in the following lemma. The proof can be found in Appendix

2.1
Structure learning

In the Ising learning task, recovering the
structure
(i.e. the underlying interaction graph) is important because it determines which pairwise couplings are nonzero and hence which statistical dependencies are present. In many standard formulations, structure recovery is itself a primary goal: once the neighborhood of each node is identified, one can restrict attention to a much smaller set of parameters and thereby reduce both statistical and computational complexity. This perspective is central to several provably efficient approaches that learn Ising models from full samples, including interaction screening and related neighborhood-selection methods
[
4
,
23
,
24
]
.

In our setting, there is a more direct motivation, moment-based gradient approximations require combining a large number of low-degree monomial statistics, and the number of relevant monomials can be reduced significantly if we only keep those involving variables in the estimated neighborhood of
u
. Thus, structure learning provides a way to focus the subsequent estimation steps (including magnetic field learning) on the true local interactions, avoiding unnecessary dependence on
p
in intermediate quantities.

To this end define an edge set,
E=\{(u,v)\in[p]\times[p]\ |\ \theta^{*}_{u,v}\neq 0\}.
Additionally we denote by
\alpha
a known lower bound on smallest non-zero pair-wise parameter in the model, i.e.
0<\alpha\leq\min_{(u,v)\in E}|\theta^{*}_{u,v}|
.

Given an estimate
\hat{\theta}
for the pair-wise parameters, define the thresholded edge set

\hat{E}\equiv\{(u,v)\in[p]\times[p]~:~|\hat{\theta}_{u,v}|\geq\alpha/2\}.

(16)

Theorem 2

(Structure recovery by thresholding)
.

Assume the true model satisfies a separation condition

This is the same standard reduction from parameter learning to structure learning that appears in prior work on interaction screening and related methods (e.g.

2.2
Learning magnetic fields

We call the coefficients of the linear terms in (

)
magnetic fields
. Even if they are present in the true model, Theorem

For this we assume that the underlying graphical model has a maximum degree of

2.3
Learning under stronger structural priors

So far we have looked at cases where the prior information on the model is only a bound on its
\ell_{1}
width. It is also interesting to consider cases where even stronger information about the model is known. The main motivation for this comes from studying Gibbs distributions arising in statistical physics and quantum field theory (
[
2
,
21
]
). There it is often known that the energy function is defined on a
D-
degree graph representing nearest-neighbor spatial points.

Suppose for instance that the structure of the graph (in terms of the edge set
E
) is known exactly and it is
D
-regular. Then the neighborhood,
|N_{u}|=D
for every
u
, and the interaction screening loss at node
u
depends only on the spins in
\{u\}\cup N_{u}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{u,n}(\underline{\theta}_{u})

\displaystyle\equiv\mathbb{E}_{n}\Big[e^{-\sigma_{u}\left(\theta_{u}+\sum_{v\in N_{u}}\theta_{u,v}\sigma_{v}\right)}\Big].

(22)

Define the following function on the
(D+1)
spins

f_{u}(\underline{\sigma}_{\{u\}\cup N_{u}})\;\coloneqq\;e^{-\sigma_{u}\left(\theta_{u}+\sum_{v\in N_{u}}\theta_{u,v}\sigma_{v}\right)},\qquad\underline{\sigma}_{\{u\}\cup N_{u}}\in\{-1,1\}^{D+1}.

(23)

Functions
\{-1,1\}^{D+1}
admits a multilinear Fourier expansion,

f_{u}(\underline{\sigma}_{\{u\}\cup N_{u}})=\sum_{K\subseteq\{u\}\cup N_{u}}\widehat{f}(\underline{\theta})_{u,K}\prod_{i\in K}\sigma_{i}.

Plugging this into the definition of
\mathcal{L}_{u,n}
yields,
\mathcal{L}_{u,n}(\underline{\theta}_{u})=\sum_{K\subseteq\{u\}\cup N_{u}}\widehat{f}(\underline{\theta})_{u,K}\,\mathbb{E}_{n}\Big[\prod_{i\in K}\sigma_{i}\Big].

Hence, evaluating (and differentiating)
\mathcal{L}_{u,n}
only requires access to empirical moments
\mathbb{E}_{n}[\prod_{i\in K}\sigma_{i}]
of order
|K|\leq D+1
.
In particular, these are exactly the moments contained in the reduced-statistics set
S_{D+1,n}
defined earlier (restricted to subsets
K\subseteq\{u\}\cup N_{u}
). It can be shown that this learning problem requires
n=O(2^{2D}e^{8\gamma}\gamma^{4})
observations to solve using gradient descent method as in Algorithm
1
. Proof of this is given in Appendix
G

3
Conclusion

We have shown that having access to
O(\gamma)
statistics is both computationally and statistically efficient for learning Ising models. As it stands, we see that there is gap in our understanding on the power of statistics below this
O(\gamma)
threshold but higher than sufficient statistics (order
2
). As we have seen Section
2.3
, if we assume stronger priors on the model, the learning problem can be potentially solved with even lower order statistics (when
D=o(\gamma)
). This suggests that restricting the model class in other ways (Ferromagnetic models, Sherrington-Kirkpatrick models,
etc.
) can potentially lower the order of statistics required to solve the learning problem. We leave these extensions for future work.

M(0)=0
. We show
M(x)\geq 0
for all
x
, which is equivalent to the desired inequality.

Case 1:
x\geq 0
.

Here
|x|=x
, so

M(x)=(2+x)(e^{-x}-1+x)-x^{2}.

Differentiating,

M^{\prime}(x)=1-(1+x)e^{-x},\qquad M^{\prime\prime}(x)=x\,e^{-x}>0\quad(x>0).

Hence
M^{\prime}
is increasing on
[0,\infty)
with
M^{\prime}(0)=0
, so
M^{\prime}(x)\geq 0
for
x\geq 0
and
M
is nondecreasing on
[0,\infty)
. Since
M(0)=0
, we get
M(x)\geq 0
for all
x\geq 0
.

Case 2:
x\leq 0
.

Here
|x|=-x
, so

M(x)=(2-x)(e^{-x}-1+x)-x^{2}.

Differentiating,

M^{\prime}(x)=3(1-e^{-x})-4x+x\,e^{-x},\qquad M^{\prime\prime}(x)=4(e^{-x}-1)-x\,e^{-x}.

For
x<0
we have
e^{-x}-1>0
and
-x>0
, hence
M^{\prime\prime}(x)>0
. Thus
M^{\prime}
is increasing on
(-\infty,0]
and
\lim_{x\to 0^{-}}M^{\prime}(x)=0
, which implies
M^{\prime}(x)\leq 0
for
x\leq 0
. Therefore
M
is nonincreasing on
(-\infty,0]
, so for every
x\leq 0
we have
M(x)\geq M(0)=0
.

Combining the two cases gives
M(x)\geq 0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
, i.e.

(2+|x|)\bigl(e^{-x}-1+x\bigr)\;\geq\;x^{2},

which is equivalent to
g(x)\geq x^{2}/(2+|x|)
. Equality holds at
x=0
.
∎

Appendix G
Sample complexity of learning with structural assumptions

Theorem 4

(Interaction screening with known
D
-regular structure)
.

Assume the interaction graph (edge set
E
) is known and
D
-regular, so
|N_{u}|=D
for every
u\in[p]
. Fix a target error
\epsilon\leq 4\gamma
and failure probability
\delta\in(0,1)
. Then, for each
u\in[p]
, projected gradient descent on the interaction screening loss over the feasible set
\{\underline{\theta}_{u}:\|\underline{\theta}_{u}\|_{1}\leq\gamma\}
, using only statistics in
S_{D+1,n}
, returns an estimate
\hat{}\underline{\theta}_{u}
satisfying

\|\hat{}\underline{\theta}_{u}-\underline{\theta}_{u}^{*}\|_{\infty}\leq\epsilon

with probability at least
1-\delta
.

In particular, it suffices to take

T=\left\lceil 2^{8}(D+1)\,\gamma^{2}(1+\gamma)^{2}e^{8\gamma}\,\epsilon^{-4}\right\rceil,\qquad\eta=\frac{2\gamma}{L\sqrt{T}},\qquad L=2\sqrt{D+1}\,e^{\gamma},

and

n=\left\lceil 2^{2D+7}\,\gamma^{2}(1+\gamma)^{2}e^{8\gamma}\,\frac{(D+2)\log 2+\log\!\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)}{\epsilon^{4}}\right\rceil.

Proof.

There is no polynomial approximation error in this case unlike Theorem
1
, so we only need to account for statistical error.

\|\nabla\mathcal{L}_{u}(\underline{\theta})-\nabla\mathcal{L}_{u,n}(\underline{\theta})\|_{\infty}\leq\sum_{K}(|\nabla\widehat{f}_{u,K}(\underline{\theta})|)\max_{K\subseteq\{u\}\bigcup N_{u}}|\mathbb{E}[\underline{\sigma}_{K}]-\mathbb{E}_{n}[\underline{\sigma}_{K}]|\coloneqq\varepsilon_{stat}

(61)

Now each term of the gradient of
\mathcal{L}_{u}
can be seen as a function on
\{-1,1\}^{D+1}
. Using the standard Fourier (Walsh–Hadamard) expansion on the boolean hypercube (see, e.g.,
[
17
, Ch. 1]
), we may write

\nabla\mathcal{L}_{u}(\underline{\theta})=\sum_{K\subseteq\{u\}\cup N_{u}}\nabla\widehat{f}_{u,K}(\underline{\theta})\,\underline{\sigma}_{K},\qquad\nabla\widehat{f}_{u,K}(\underline{\theta})=2^{-(D+1)}\sum_{\tau\in\{-1,1\}^{D+1}}\nabla f_{u}(\tau;\underline{\theta})\,\tau_{K}.

In particular, since
\tau_{K}\in\{-1,1\}
, we have the uniform bound

\bigl\|\nabla\widehat{f}_{u,K}(\underline{\theta})\bigr\|\leq\max_{\tau\in\{-1,1\}^{D+1}}\bigl\|\nabla f_{u}(\tau;\underline{\theta})\bigr\|=\|\nabla\mathcal{L}_{u}(\underline{\theta})\|_{\infty},

and therefore

\sum_{K\subseteq\{u\}\cup N_{u}}\bigl\|\nabla\widehat{f}_{u,K}(\underline{\theta})\bigr\|\leq 2^{D+1}\,\|\nabla\mathcal{L}_{u}(\underline{\theta})\|_{\infty}\leq 2^{D+1}e^{\gamma}.

Next we bound the empirical moment errors. Fix any
K\subseteq\{u\}\cup N_{u}
. The random variable
\prod_{i\in K}\sigma_{i}
takes values in
\{-1,1\}
, so by Hoeffding’s inequality,

\Pr\Bigl[\bigl|\mathbb{E}[\underline{\sigma}_{K}]-\mathbb{E}_{n}[\underline{\sigma}_{K}]\bigr|\geq t\Bigr]\leq 2e^{-2nt^{2}}.

There are at most
2^{D+1}
such subsets
K
, hence a union bound gives

\Pr\Bigl[\max_{K\subseteq\{u\}\cup N_{u}}\bigl|\mathbb{E}[\underline{\sigma}_{K}]-\mathbb{E}_{n}[\underline{\sigma}_{K}]\bigr|\geq t\Bigr]\leq 2^{D+2}e^{-2nt^{2}}.

Therefore, for

n\geq\left\lceil\frac{(D+2)\log 2+\log\!\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)}{2t^{2}}\right\rceil

we have with probability at least
1-\delta
that

\max_{K\subseteq\{u\}\cup N_{u}}|\mathbb{E}[\underline{\sigma}_{K}]-\mathbb{E}_{n}[\underline{\sigma}_{K}]|\leq t
, and consequently

\varepsilon_{stat}\leq 2^{D+1}e^{\gamma}\,t.

Setting
t=\varepsilon_{stat}/(2^{D+1}e^{\gamma})
yields the sufficient condition

n\geq\left\lceil\frac{2^{2D+1}e^{2\gamma}}{\varepsilon_{stat}^{2}}\Bigl((D+2)\log 2+\log\!\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\Bigr)\right\rceil.

Robust gradient descent.

Having established a uniform bound

\sup_{\|\underline{\theta}\|_{1}\leq\gamma}\|\nabla\mathcal{L}_{u}(\underline{\theta})-\widetilde{\nabla}\mathcal{L}_{u}(\underline{\theta})\|_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon_{stat}
, we can invoke Lemma
3
(robustness of projected gradient descent) exactly as in the proof of Theorem
1
.
In the notation of Lemma
3
, we may take

\varepsilon\coloneqq 4\gamma\,\varepsilon_{stat},\qquad L\coloneqq 2\sqrt{D+1}\,e^{\gamma},

where the choice of
L
follows from the crude bound
\|\nabla\mathcal{L}_{u}(\underline{\theta})\|_{\infty}\leq e^{\gamma}
and the fact that the local parameter vector has dimension
D+1
.
Lemma
3
then yields the iteration complexity and step size

T=\left\lceil\frac{16\gamma^{2}L^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right\rceil=\left\lceil\frac{L^{2}}{\varepsilon_{stat}^{2}}\right\rceil,\qquad\eta=\frac{2\gamma}{L\sqrt{T}},

which ensure that the averaged iterate satisfies
\mathcal{L}_{u}(\bar{}\underline{\theta}^{T})-\mathcal{L}_{u}(\underline{\theta}_{u}^{*})\leq\varepsilon
.

From loss error to parameter error (curvature).

Finally, we convert the loss optimality gap into a coordinate-wise parameter error bound using the same curvature argument as in the proof of Theorem
1
.
Let
\underline{\Delta}_{u}\coloneqq\bar{}\underline{\theta}^{T}-\underline{\theta}_{u}^{*}
. Define the curvature functional

\delta\mathcal{L}_{u}^{*}(\bar{}\underline{\theta}^{T})\coloneqq\mathcal{L}_{u}(\bar{}\underline{\theta}^{T})-\mathcal{L}_{u}(\underline{\theta}_{u}^{*})-\langle\nabla\mathcal{L}_{u}(\underline{\theta}_{u}^{*}),\underline{\Delta}_{u}\rangle.

Since
\underline{\theta}_{u}^{*}
is a minimizer of the convex loss over the feasible set, we have
\delta\mathcal{L}_{u}^{*}(\bar{}\underline{\theta}^{T})\leq\mathcal{L}_{u}(\bar{}\underline{\theta}^{T})-\mathcal{L}_{u}(\underline{\theta}_{u}^{*})\leq\varepsilon
.
Moreover, applying Lemma
8
exactly as in Appendix
C
yields the lower bound

\delta\mathcal{L}_{u}^{*}(\bar{}\underline{\theta}^{T})\geq\frac{e^{-3\gamma}}{2+2\gamma}\,\|\underline{\Delta}_{u}\|_{\infty}^{2}.

Combining the two displays gives

\|\bar{}\underline{\theta}^{T}-\underline{\theta}_{u}^{*}\|_{\infty}\leq e^{3\gamma/2}\sqrt{2(1+\gamma)\,\varepsilon}=e^{3\gamma/2}\sqrt{8\gamma(1+\gamma)\,\varepsilon_{stat}}.

To achieve a target parameter accuracy
\|\bar{}\underline{\theta}^{T}-\underline{\theta}_{u}^{*}\|_{\infty}\leq\epsilon
with overall success probability at least
1-\delta
, it suffices to set

\varepsilon_{stat}\leq\frac{\epsilon^{2}e^{-3\gamma}}{8\gamma(1+\gamma)}

(and use the same
\delta
in the Hoeffding + union bound above).
Since the statistics in
S_{D+1,n}
are computed once (before running the optimization) and then reused across all
T
iterations, this single high-probability event implies the gradient approximation guarantee holds uniformly for every iterate, and no additional union bound over the
T
steps is needed.
Substituting this choice into the sufficient condition for
n
yields

n\geq\left\lceil 2^{2D+7}\,\gamma^{2}(1+\gamma)^{2}e^{8\gamma}\,\frac{(D+2)\log 2+\log\!\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)}{\epsilon^{4}}\right\rceil.

∎
