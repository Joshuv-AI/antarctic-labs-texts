Ultrasound-assisted engineering of dietary fiber from cauliflower waste: a cavitation-driven process–structure–function approach
Authors: Bhagyashree Pasarad, Mushtaq Ahmad Beigh, Tashooq Ahmad Bhat, Sajad Ali, Muyassar H. Abualreesh, Tariq Ahmad Raja, Shahnaz Wani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

Introduction

Dietary fiber has attracted considerable attention as a sustainable functional ingredient owing to its well-established health benefits and its ability to improve the techno-functional characteristics of food systems. Among its two major fractions, soluble dietary fiber (SDF) exhibits superior hydration, viscosity, emulsification, and gel-forming properties, making it particularly valuable for the development of functional foods, whereas insoluble dietary fiber (IDF) primarily contributes to gastrointestinal health through bulking effects [1]. Consequently, considerable research efforts have focused on developing efficient strategies to convert naturally abundant IDF into value-added SDF with improved functional performance.

The rapid expansion of fruit and vegetable processing industries has resulted in the generation of substantial quantities of agro-industrial by-products, accounting for approximately 25–60% of the total processed biomass (Singh et al., 2026). Among these, cauliflower (Brassica oleracea var. botrytis) processing residues, including leaves, stems, and trimmings, represent an abundant yet underutilized lignocellulosic resource rich in dietary fiber, predominantly in the insoluble fraction [2]. Valorization of these residues into functional food ingredients is consistent with circular bioeconomy principles by simultaneously reducing environmental burdens and creating value-added products. However, the compact lignocellulosic architecture of cauliflower waste restricts solvent accessibility and limits the extractability and functionality of dietary fiber, necessitating innovative processing technologies capable of modifying its structural organization [3].

Ultrasound-assisted extraction has emerged as an environmentally friendly, non-thermal technology capable of intensifying extraction processes through acoustic cavitation. The formation, growth, and implosive collapse of cavitation bubbles generate localized shear forces, microjets, and turbulence that enhance solvent penetration, disrupt plant cell walls, and improve mass transfer. When combined with enzymatic hydrolysis, ultrasound-assisted enzymatic extraction (UAEE) offers additional advantages because cavitation enhances enzyme accessibility by disrupting the lignocellulosic matrix, while enzymes selectively hydrolyze cellulose, hemicellulose, and pectic polymers [4]. The combined application of ultrasonication amplitude and treatment time enabled efficient extraction under relatively mild processing conditions while minimizing excessive thermal exposure and preserving the functional properties of the extracted dietary fibre.

Recent studies have demonstrated the potential of ultrasound-assisted processing for improving dietary fiber recovery from citrus peel, oat bran, mulberry fruit, broccoli by-products, and other agro-industrial residues [5], [6], [7]. Nevertheless, these investigations have largely focused on extraction yield or soluble dietary fiber production, whereas comparatively few have systematically linked ultrasound processing parameters with cavitation-induced structural modification, physicochemical functionality, and subsequent performance in finished food products [8]. Moreover, validation of ultrasound-modified dietary fiber in practical food systems remains limited, and the mechanistic relationships connecting processing conditions, structural transformation, and functional behavior are still insufficiently understood. Consequently, establishing a comprehensive process–structure–function relationship remains an important research challenge in ultrasound-assisted food processing.

Beyond improving extraction efficiency, ultrasonication can induce significant structural modification of dietary fiber through partial depolymerization of polysaccharides, cleavage of glycosidic linkages, disruption of hydrogen-bonding networks, and increased exposure of hydrophilic functional groups [9]. These molecular and microstructural changes facilitate the conversion of IDF into SDF while enhancing hydration properties such as water holding capacity, swelling capacity, and oil holding capacity, all of which are critical determinants of ingredient functionality in food systems. Despite these recognized advantages, the mechanisms linking cavitation-induced structural changes with functional performance remain inadequately established, particularly for vegetable processing residues.

Cauliflower processing waste was selected in the present study because it is generated in large quantities during commercial trimming operations and contains substantial amounts of cellulose, hemicellulose, and pectic polysaccharides that are predominantly present as insoluble dietary fiber. The complex lignocellulosic structure of this biomass provides an excellent model for investigating ultrasound-assisted structural modification and insoluble-to-soluble dietary fiber transformation. Efficient utilization of this underexploited biomass therefore offers significant opportunities for sustainable waste management, circular bioeconomy implementation, and production of value-added functional ingredients.

Furthermore, successful incorporation of modified dietary fiber into commercial food products requires evaluation of its influence on product quality. Bakery products, particularly biscuits, provide an ideal model system because their physicochemical properties, texture, and sensory quality are highly sensitive to changes in fiber composition and hydration behavior. Dietary fiber incorporation may alter dough rheology, gluten network development, water distribution, and gas retention, thereby influencing the technological and sensory characteristics of the final product [10]. Therefore, optimization of fiber functionality should be validated not only through laboratory characterization but also through practical food applications.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study integrating ultrasound-assisted enzymatic extraction of dietary fiber from cauliflower processing waste with comprehensive structural characterization (SEM and FTIR), evaluation of hydration-related functional properties, correlation analysis linking particle size with functional performance, and validation of the modified dietary fiber in a biscuit model together with storage stability assessment. Accordingly, the objectives of this study were to (i) optimize ultrasound-assisted enzymatic extraction of dietary fiber from cauliflower processing waste, (ii) investigate ultrasound-induced structural modifications and their influence on functional properties, (iii) establish mechanistic relationships between processing conditions, structural transformation, and functionality, and (iv) evaluate the technological performance of the extracted dietary fiber in functional biscuits. The findings provide new insights into ultrasound-enabled engineering of dietary fiber and demonstrate a sustainable strategy for converting vegetable processing residues into high-value functional food ingredients.

Material & methods

Raw materials and chemicals

Fresh cauliflower (Brassica oleracea var. botrytis) processing residues, comprising outer leaves, stems, and trimmings, were procured from the Food Processing and Training Centre (FPTC), SKUAST-Kashmir, India. The collected biomass was immediately transported under hygienic conditions to minimize biochemical degradation.

Analytical-grade enzymes, including heat-stable α-amylase, protease, and amyloglucosidase, were used for enzymatic pre-treatment. All chemicals and reagents used were of analytical grade and obtained from certified suppliers.

Sample preparation and preconditioning

The collected cauliflower waste was thoroughly washed to remove adhering soil and impurities, followed by size reduction into uniform pieces. The material was dried in a hot air oven at 50–60 °C until moisture content reached approximately 5%, ensuring minimal thermal degradation of bioactive components.

The dried samples were milled using a laboratory grinder and sieved through a 1 mm mesh to obtain uniform particle size. The resulting powder was stored in airtight containers under controlled conditions (ambient temperature, low humidity) until further use.

A full-factorial Completely Randomized Design (3 × 3) was adopted to evaluate the effects of two major ultrasound processing variables, namely ultrasonication amplitude and treatment time, on dietary fiber extraction and functional modification. Unlike optimization-oriented studies employing Response Surface Methodology (RSM), the present investigation was designed to systematically examine the individual and interactive effects of these parameters and to establish mechanistic relationships between ultrasound processing, structural modification, and functional performance. The selected amplitudes (40, 60, and 80%) were chosen based on preliminary experiments and previous reports indicating that amplitudes below 40% generally produce insufficient cavitation intensity, whereas amplitudes above 80% may result in excessive structural degradation and higher energy consumption without proportional improvement in extraction efficiency. Ultrasound-assisted enzymatic extraction (UAEE)

Enzymatic pre-treatment

Cauliflower powder was dispersed in 0.05 M sodium acetate buffer (pH 5.0) at a solid-to-liquid ratio of 1:20 (w/v) and subjected to sequential enzymatic hydrolysis. Heat-stable α-amylase (120 U g−1 substrate) was first added at 0.5% (w/w) and incubated at 95 °C for 30 min to hydrolyse starch fractions. After cooling to 55 °C, protease (60 U g−1 substrate) was added at 1.0% (w/w), and the suspension was incubated for 60 min to remove proteinaceous components. Subsequently, amyloglucosidase (300 U g−1 substrate) was incorporated at 0.5% (w/w), and hydrolysis was continued at 60 °C for 60 min. The pH was maintained at 5.0 ± 0.1 throughout enzymatic treatment. Following hydrolysis, enzymes were inactivated by heating the suspension at 95 °C for 10 min, after which the samples were immediately cooled prior to ultrasonication.

Ultrasonication treatment

The enzymatically pretreated slurry was subjected to high-intensity probe ultrasonication using an ultrasonic processor operating at 20 kHz (Model: VCX-750, Manufacturer: USA). The processor had a maximum nominal output power of 750 W and was equipped with a 13 mm titanium probe. A full factorial experimental design consisting of nine treatment combinations was employed. Three ultrasonic amplitudes (40%, 60%, and 80%) were independently combined with three treatment durations (20, 30, and 40 min), resulting in nine ultrasound-assisted enzymatic extraction treatments.

The actual ultrasonic power delivered to the sample was determined calorimetrically and ranged from approximately 180 to 420 W, corresponding to an energy density of 216–1008 J mL−1, depending on treatment conditions. Sonication was carried out in pulse mode (5 s ON/5 s OFF) to minimize excessive heating while maintaining cavitation intensity. Samples (200 mL) were processed in a double-walled glass vessel under continuous magnetic stirring (300 rpm), with the probe immersed approximately 2 cm below the liquid surface. Sample temperature was maintained below 40 ± 2 °C throughout sonication using an external ice-water bath.

Ultrasonication enhances extraction through acoustic cavitation, where repeated formation and collapse of microbubbles generate localized shear forces, microjets, turbulence, and shockwaves that disrupt plant cell walls, increase solvent penetration, and accelerate mass transfer. Following sonication, the slurry was filtered through muslin cloth, and the recovered fiber residue was washed with distilled water, dried in a hot-air oven at 50 °C to constant weight, milled, and stored in airtight containers for subsequent analyses.

Total dietary fiber (TDF), soluble dietary fiber (SDF), and insoluble dietary fiber (IDF) were determined using the enzymatic–gravimetric method (AOAC 991.43). The method involves sequential enzymatic digestion followed by filtration and gravimetric quantification [6].

Particle size distribution was determined using a laser diffraction particle size analyzer (Mastersizer 3000, Malvern Panalytical, UK). Functional properties

Water holding capacity (WHC)

Water-holding capacity (WHC) was determined following the method of Robertson et al. (2000) with minor modifications. Briefly, 1.0 g of dietary fibre was mixed with 20 mL distilled water in a pre-weighed centrifuge tube and allowed to hydrate for 30 min at room temperature (25 ± 2 °C) with intermittent mixing. The suspension was centrifuged at 3000g for 20 min, after which the supernatant was carefully decanted. The hydrated residue was weighed immediately, and WHC was calculated as:

WHC (g g−1) = (Weight of hydrated residue − Dry sample weight) / Dry sample weight.

Oil holding capacity (OHC)

Oil-holding capacity (OHC) was determined using the same procedure, replacing water with refined sunflower oil (density approximately 0.91 g mL−1). One gram of dietary fibre was mixed with 20 mL oil, equilibrated for 30 min at room temperature, and centrifuged at 3000 × g for 20 min. Excess oil was carefully decanted, and the residue was weighed. OHC was calculated as:

OHC (g g−1) = (Weight after oil absorption − Dry sample weight) / Dry sample weight.

Swelling capacity (SC)

Swelling capacity (SC) was determined according to Robertson et al. (2000). One gram of dietary fibre was transferred to a graduated cylinder containing 10 mL distilled water and allowed to hydrate for 18 h at room temperature without agitation. The final volume occupied by the hydrated sample was recorded, and swelling capacity was expressed as:

SC (mL g−1) = Final swollen volume / Dry sample weight.

These properties reflect the hydration behavior and functional performance of dietary fiber in food systems.

Structural and physicochemical analysis

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM–EDX)

Scanning electron microscopy with energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM–EDX)

The surface morphology and elemental composition of the extracted cauliflower dietary fibre were examined using a field-emission scanning electron microscope (Gemini SEM 500, Carl Zeiss AG, Oberkochen, Germany) equipped with an Oxford Instruments Energy Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDX) detector. The dried dietary fibre samples were mounted on aluminium stubs using double-sided conductive carbon tape and sputter-coated with a thin layer of gold under vacuum to improve electrical conductivity. Micrographs were acquired at an accelerating voltage of 5–10 kV, a working distance of approximately 8–10 mm, and at appropriate magnifications to evaluate changes in surface morphology. Elemental composition and elemental distribution were determined by EDX analysis using point spectra and elemental mapping under the same operating conditions. The acquired spectra and elemental maps were processed using the instrument's integrated analysis software.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

Fourier transform infrared spectra were recorded using an FTIR spectrometer (Perkin Elmer Spectrum Two) equipped with an attenuated total reflectance (ATR) accessory. Spectra were collected over the range of 4000–400 cm−1 with a spectral resolution of 4 cm−1 and 32 accumulated scans per sample. Background spectra were collected before each measurement, and the resulting spectra were baseline-corrected using the instrument software.

Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC)

Thermal properties of the untreated and ultrasound-assisted extracted dietary fiber samples were evaluated using a Differential Scanning Calorimeter (DSC) (DSC 8000, PerkinElmer). Approximately 5–8 mg of oven-dried sample was accurately weighed into a hermetically sealed aluminum pan, while an empty sealed aluminum pan was used as the reference. The samples were heated from 25 to 300 °C at a constant heating rate of 10 °C min−1 under a continuous nitrogen atmosphere (50 mL min−1) to prevent oxidative degradation. The onset temperature (Tonset), peak temperature (Tp), endset temperature (Tendset), and enthalpy change (ΔH) associated with thermal transitions were recorded using the manufacturer's analysis software (Proteus®, NETZSCH). All measurements were performed in triplicate, and the results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation.

Statistical analysis and modeling

All experiments were conducted in triplicate, and the results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Data were analyzed using analysis of variance (ANOVA) under a completely randomized design (CRD) at a significance level of p < 0.05.

For the ultrasound-assisted enzymatic extraction experiment, a two-way ANOVA was performed to evaluate the individual and interactive effects of ultrasonication amplitude (Factor A) and treatment time (Factor B) on the response variables, including extraction yield, soluble dietary fibre (SDF), particle size, water-holding capacity (WHC), oil-holding capacity (OHC), and swelling capacity (SC).

For the biscuit formulation study, one-way ANOVA was used to compare the effects of different dietary fibre substitution levels on the physicochemical, textural, nutritional, and sensory properties of the biscuits. When significant differences were detected, mean values were separated using Tukey's Honest Significant Difference (HSD) post-hoc test.

For the storage stability study, two-way ANOVA was employed to evaluate the effects of formulation (B0 and B2) and storage duration (0, 30, 60, and 90 days), together with their interaction, on physicochemical, microbiological, textural, and sensory quality attributes. Where significant differences were observed, mean comparisons were performed using Tukey's Honest Significant Difference (HSD) test at p < 0.05.

To establish a process–structure–function framework, Pearson's correlation coefficients were calculated between particle size, SDF content, and hydration properties (WHC, OHC, and SC). Furthermore, multiple linear regression (MLR) models were developed to predict key responses (extraction yield, SDF, and WHC) as functions of ultrasonication amplitude and treatment time. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (Version 26.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R Studio (Version 4.2.1, RStudio Team, Boston, MA, USA).

The observed enhancement is likely associated with the combined effects occurring during the ultrasound-assisted enzymatic extraction process. Soluble dietary fiber (SDF) transformation

SDF exhibited the most pronounced response among the measured dietary fibre fractions. Its content increased from 12.05% for U1T1 to 21.01% for U3T3, corresponding to an approximately 74.4% increase (Table 4). These findings indicate that the combined ultrasound-assisted enzymatic extraction process was associated with a substantial increase in the soluble dietary fibre fraction.

The observed increase may be related to structural modifications of the fibre matrix during processing. Cavitation-associated mechanical effects and enzymatic hydrolysis may together facilitate fragmentation of the lignocellulosic structure and increase the accessibility or release of soluble polysaccharide fractions [12]. The structural changes observed by SEM (Fig. 3a, Fig. 3b) are consistent with this interpretation. However, the present experimental design does not permit the independent contribution of ultrasonication to be separated from that of enzymatic treatment.

The regression models adequately describe the experimental trends within the investigated processing range. The positive coefficients for both amplitude and treatment time indicate an overall increase in extraction yield and SDF with increasing processing intensity. For both responses, the coefficient associated with treatment time was larger than that of amplitude, consistent with the ANOVA results indicating a greater contribution of treatment duration to the observed variation. These models should be interpreted as empirical descriptions of the responses within the investigated experimental range and should not be extrapolated beyond the studied processing conditions. Effect of ultrasonication on particle size and functional properties

Particle size reduction

Particle size was significantly affected by the investigated processing conditions (Table 7). The mean particle diameter decreased from 159.70 ± 1.25 µm under the mildest treatment condition (U1T1) to 92.88 ± 0.95 µm under the most intensive condition (U3T3), corresponding to an overall reduction of approximately 41.8%.

At a constant treatment time of 20 min, increasing the ultrasonication amplitude from 40% to 80% was associated with a decrease in particle size from 159.70 to 114.17 µm. Similarly, increasing treatment duration generally resulted in a progressive reduction in particle size at each amplitude level. These results indicate that increasing the severity of the investigated ultrasound-assisted enzymatic extraction conditions was associated with greater fragmentation of the dietary fibre matrix.

The observed reduction in particle size is consistent with structural disruption occurring during ultrasound-assisted enzymatic processing. Acoustic cavitation may contribute to localized shear forces, microstreaming, and pressure fluctuations that facilitate physical disintegration of the plant matrix, while enzymatic treatment may increase the accessibility and susceptibility of cell-wall components to subsequent mechanical disruption [13]. However, because separate ultrasound-only and enzyme-only control treatments were not included, the individual contribution of ultrasonication and enzymatic treatment cannot be independently quantified.

The particle-size reduction is also consistent with the SEM observations, which showed a transition from a relatively compact fibre structure towards a more heterogeneous and porous morphology with increasing processing severity. These observations collectively support an association between the applied processing conditions and modification of the physical architecture of the extracted dietary fibre.

Functional properties (WHC, OHC, and SC)

The functional properties of the extracted dietary fibre were also significantly influenced by the investigated processing conditions (Table 6). Statistical analysis and regression modeling of the effects of UAEE processing conditions on particle size and functional properties

To statistically evaluate the effects of ultrasonication amplitude and treatment time within the ultrasound-assisted enzymatic extraction (UAEE) system, a two-way ANOVA was performed for particle size and the functional properties, including water-holding capacity (WHC), oil-holding capacity (OHC), and swelling capacity (SC) (Table 7). Both processing variables and their interaction significantly affected all evaluated responses (p < 0.001). The significant amplitude × time interactions indicate that the response to one processing variable depended on the level of the other within the investigated experimental range.

As shown in Table 7, ultrasonication amplitude and treatment time accounted for significant variation in particle size, WHC, OHC, and SC. The treatment means presented in Table 6 showed that particle size generally decreased with increasing amplitude and treatment time, whereas WHC, OHC, and SC increased across the investigated UAEE treatment combinations. Structural, morphological and thermal characterization of cauliflower dietary fibre

Scanning electron microscopy and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM–EDX)

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was used to examine morphological differences between the untreated cauliflower dietary fibre and fibre obtained under the investigated ultrasound-assisted enzymatic extraction (UAEE) conditions (Fig. 3a). The untreated fibre exhibited a relatively compact and smooth surface with tightly packed fibrillar structures and limited visible porosity. In contrast, the treated samples displayed progressively rougher and more heterogeneous surfaces, characterized by irregular fragmentation, surface fissures, microchannels, and increased visible porosity.

The extent of these morphological differences increased across the investigated amplitude–time combinations, with the most pronounced disruption observed for the sample processed at 80% amplitude for 40 min. These observations are consistent with increased structural disintegration under more intensive UAEE processing conditions. Acoustic cavitation may have contributed to the observed physical disruption through localized shear forces, microstreaming, and pressure fluctuations (Siddique et al., 2025), while enzymatic treatment may also have contributed by modifying accessible components of the plant cell-wall matrix [15]. Therefore, the observed morphological changes should be interpreted as resulting from the combined processing environment rather than being attributed exclusively to ultrasonication.

The morphological trends were consistent with the reduction in particle size and the increases in WHC, OHC, and SC observed under the investigated processing conditions. A less compact and more porous fibre structure may provide greater accessibility to water and oil, thereby contributing to the observed changes in hydration- and adsorption-related properties. However, the SEM observations demonstrate morphological differences only and cannot independently establish the molecular mechanisms responsible for these changes, such as polysaccharide depolymerization or cleavage of specific chemical bonds.

To complement the morphological analysis, energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) was used to examine the elemental composition and distribution within the treated dietary fibre (Fig. 3b). Carbon (C) and oxygen (O) were the predominant detected elements, consistent with the polysaccharide-rich nature of the dietary fibre. Minor amounts of potassium (K), calcium (Ca), sodium (Na), phosphorus (P), and chlorine (Cl) were also detected, reflecting naturally occurring mineral constituents of the cauliflower tissue.

The elemental distribution observed by EDX did not indicate the introduction of additional elemental constituents associated with the UAEE treatment. Accordingly, the combined SEM–EDX observations suggest that the investigated processing conditions were primarily associated with changes in fibre morphology while retaining the characteristic elemental composition of the material.

Overall, the SEM–EDX results provide morphological and elemental evidence supporting structural differences between untreated and UAEE-treated dietary fibre. These structural differences are consistent with the changes in particle size and functional properties reported in Section 3.2, although the present analyses do not permit the individual contributions of ultrasonication and enzymatic treatment to be independently quantified.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

FTIR spectroscopy was used to compare the functional-group environments of untreated and UAEE-treated cauliflower dietary fibre (Fig. 4). Both samples exhibited characteristic absorption bands associated with the polysaccharide-rich composition of the material, including bands attributable to cellulose, hemicellulosic components, and pectic polysaccharides.Fig. 4FTIR spectra of untreated and ultrasound-assisted enzymatically extracted cauliflower dietary fibre, showing differences in the intensity and/or profile of characteristic absorption bands associated with the functional-group environment of the polysaccharide matrix.

The broad absorption region around 3300–3400 cm−1, associated primarily with O–H stretching vibrations, showed differences in band intensity and broadening between untreated and treated samples. These spectral differences are consistent with changes in hydrogen-bonding environments and/or the accessibility of hydroxyl-containing groups within the fibre matrix. Similarly, moderate differences were observed in the 1600–1650 cm−1 region, which may be associated with changes in the local environment of carbonyl-containing groups and absorbed water.

Minor variations were also observed in the 1000–1100 cm−1 region corresponding to C–O–C and C–O stretching vibrations of polysaccharide structures. Similar spectral differences have been reported in processed dietary fibres and have been associated with alterations in the structural organization and functional-group environment of lignocellulosic materials.

Importantly, the observed changes in FTIR spectra should not be interpreted as direct evidence of specific molecular events such as glycosidic-bond cleavage, depolymerization, or conversion of one polysaccharide fraction into another. FTIR primarily provides information regarding the chemical environment of functional groups and cannot independently establish molecular-scale structural mechanisms.

The spectral differences were consistent with the observed changes in hydration-related functional properties. Increased accessibility of polar groups within the modified fibre matrix may contribute to stronger interactions with water, thereby contributing to the higher WHC and SC observed under more intensive UAEE conditions [16]. However, this relationship should be interpreted as an association rather than direct proof of a specific mechanism.

Overall, the FTIR results indicate differences in the functional-group environment between untreated and UAEE-treated dietary fibre while retaining the principal spectral features characteristic of the polysaccharide matrix. Further techniques, such as molecular-weight distribution analysis, X-ray diffraction, Raman spectroscopy, or nuclear magnetic resonance, would be required to directly establish molecular-scale changes induced during processing.

Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) of cauliflower dietary fibre

Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) was performed to characterize the thermal behaviour of the cauliflower dietary fibre obtained following UAEE (Fig. 5). The thermogram exhibited a broad endothermic transition between approximately 150 and 220 °C, with a pronounced minimum near 200 °C. This thermal event is consistent with the removal of strongly associated moisture and relaxation of amorphous regions within the polysaccharide-rich fibre matrix.Fig. 5Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) thermogram of cauliflower dietary fibre obtained following ultrasound-assisted enzymatic extraction.

At higher temperatures, further thermal transitions were observed between approximately 250 and 350 °C, consistent with progressive thermal degradation of structural polysaccharide components. The continued thermal response above 350 °C may be associated with degradation of more thermally stable components and residual carbonaceous material.

The absence of a major thermal transition below approximately 170–180 °C indicates that no substantial thermal degradation was evident within the temperature range relevant to the biscuit-baking conditions used in the present study. Therefore, the DSC profile suggests that the extracted dietary fibre remained thermally stable within the investigated baking temperature range.

However, thermal stability alone does not demonstrate that the functional properties of the fibre are completely retained during biscuit processing. The DSC results should therefore be interpreted as evidence of thermal behaviour and stability of the material rather than direct confirmation of its functional performance during baking.

Overall, the DSC analysis provides supporting evidence that the UAEE-derived cauliflower dietary fibre possesses thermal characteristics compatible with its subsequent evaluation as a bakery ingredient.

Volume and specific volume

A significant (p < 0.05) decrease in biscuit volume and specific volume was observed as the level of dietary fiber increased (Table 8). Volume decreased from 26.50 ± 0.15 cm3 in the control to 18.37 ± 0.22 cm3 at the highest substitution level, corresponding to a reduction of approximately 31%. Similarly, specific volume declined from 1.44 to 1.04 cm3 g−1.

The reduction in product volume is consistent with the dilution of gluten-forming proteins caused by partial replacement of wheat flour with dietary fiber. In addition, the modified fiber possesses a high affinity for water, thereby reducing the amount of free water available for gluten hydration and network development. Increased fiber surface area may also physically interfere with starch–gluten interactions, resulting in reduced dough extensibility and lower gas retention during baking. Similar reductions in loaf or biscuit volume following incorporation of dietary fiber have been reported by Silva et al. [18], although the decrease observed in the present study was slightly greater, likely because the ultrasonically modified fiber exhibited superior hydration capacity compared with untreated dietary fibers.

Although higher substitution levels negatively affected expansion during baking, moderate incorporation levels (B2) maintained acceptable product volume while providing substantial nutritional enrichment, indicating a favourable balance between technological performance and functional enhancement.

Process–structure–product relationship

The physicochemical changes observed in the biscuits demonstrate a clear process–structure–product relationship. Ultrasound-assisted enzymatic extraction modified the physical characteristics of cauliflower dietary fiber by reducing particle size and increasing porosity, which in turn enhanced its water holding capacity and hydration behaviour (Sections 3.2 and 3.3). These improved functional properties influenced dough water distribution during biscuit manufacture, leading to increased moisture retention but reduced product expansion during baking.

The results therefore demonstrate that ultrasonically modified dietary fiber functions as a structure-modifying ingredient, altering hydration dynamics and the physical characteristics of the baked product. Importantly, the beneficial effects of enhanced moisture retention were achieved without compromising microbiological stability, while moderate dietary fiber incorporation (10%, B2) provided the most favourable balance between nutritional improvement and physicochemical quality. These findings support the potential application of ultrasound-assisted dietary fiber as a functional ingredient for bakery products and further illustrate the practical relevance of the process–structure–function framework established throughout this study.

Appearance scores decreased significantly (p < 0.05) from 8.6 ± 0.18 for the control biscuits to 6.8 ± 0.27 at the highest fiber incorporation level (Table 9). Taste

Taste scores gradually declined from 8.5 ± 0.20 to 6.7 ± 0.28 with increasing dietary fiber incorporation. This reduction may be attributed to the presence of naturally occurring phytochemicals within the dietary fiber, which can impart mild vegetal or slightly bitter notes while partially masking sweetness. Comparable sensory responses have been reported in fiber-enriched bakery products, where increasing substitution levels resulted in progressive reductions in flavour acceptability because of changes in flavour balance and mouthfeel [19]; Plakantonaki et al., 2023).

Texture acceptability decreased significantly (p < 0.05) from 8.7 ± 0.17 in the control to 6.4 ± 0.27 in biscuits containing the highest fiber concentration. These sensory observations are consistent with the instrumental texture measurements presented in Section 3.6, which showed increased hardness with increasing dietary fiber incorporation. The reduced texture scores are likely associated with partial dilution of the gluten network, modified dough hydration, and increased fiber content, which collectively produced a denser biscuit structure. Similar relationships between instrumental hardness and sensory texture have been reported for fiber-fortified bakery products [18].

Overall sensory acceptability declined progressively from 8.6 ± 0.19 in the control to 6.5 ± 0.26 at the highest substitution level. Although increasing dietary fibre incorporation reduced sensory scores, all formulations remained within the acceptable range of the 9-point hedonic scale, indicating good sensory acceptability.

Among the fibre-enriched formulations, B2 (10% dietary fibre) achieved the most favourable balance between nutritional enrichment, functional improvement, and sensory quality, with an overall sensory acceptability score of 7.7 ± 0.22. Beyond this substitution level, the combined effects of darker colour, increased hardness, and flavour modifications resulted in a more pronounced decline in sensory acceptability.

The sensory findings are consistent with the physicochemical and structural changes observed in the present study. The enhanced hydration capacity of the ultrasound-modified dietary fibre increased moisture retention within the biscuit matrix, while partial replacement of wheat flour altered dough structure and baking characteristics. Together, these changes influenced texture and overall eating quality. Similar relationships between dietary fibre functionality, dough structure, and sensory performance have been reported in previous studies on fibre-enriched bakery products [19].

Overall, the results demonstrate that ultrasound-assisted modification enabled the successful incorporation of cauliflower waste-derived dietary fibre into biscuits, with 10% substitution (B2) providing the optimal balance between functional enhancement and overall sensory acceptability.

Lightness (L)*

Lightness (L*) decreased significantly (p < 0.05) from 69.14 ± 0.71 in the control biscuits (B0) to 63.39 ± 0.31 in the highest dietary fiber formulation (B4), corresponding to an overall reduction of approximately 8.3% (Table 10). The darker appearance of fiber-enriched biscuits is likely associated with the intrinsic colour of cauliflower dietary fiber together with enhanced non-enzymatic browning during baking. The greater water retention capacity of the modified dietary fiber may also have influenced heat and moisture distribution during baking, thereby contributing to colour development. Similar reductions in lightness have been reported in biscuits fortified with vegetable and cereal dietary fibers [18].

Redness (a)*

The redness parameter (a*) increased significantly from 6.84 ± 0.02 to 9.55 ± 0.02, representing an increase of approximately 40% with increasing dietary fiber incorporation. This shift toward reddish-brown colour is consistent with the formation of Maillard reaction products and caramelization pigments during baking. Comparable increases in a* values have been observed in bakery products enriched with plant-derived dietary fibers, where the presence of additional reducing compounds and prolonged thermal exposure promoted browning reactions [19].

Yellowness (b)*

A significant reduction (p < 0.05) in yellowness (b*) was observed, with values decreasing from 28.37 ± 0.01 in the control to 24.64 ± 0.02 at the highest substitution level. The reduction in b* is likely associated with progressive browning during baking, which masked the original yellow colour of the wheat-based biscuit matrix. Similar trends have been reported following incorporation of dietary fiber from vegetable by-products, where increasing browning intensity reduced yellowness while increasing redness [20].

Total colour difference (ΔE)

The total colour difference (ΔE) increased significantly with dietary fiber incorporation, from 0.00 in the control formulation to 10.27 ± 0.12 in B4 (Table 10). According to established colour perception criteria, ΔE values below 2 are generally considered imperceptible, values between 2 and 5 represent slight but noticeable differences, whereas values exceeding 5 indicate clearly visible colour changes. Accordingly, the colour difference observed for B2 (ΔE = 5.15) was noticeable but remained acceptable, whereas B4 (ΔE = 10.27) exhibited substantial visual darkening.

The progressive increase in ΔE is consistent with the reduction in L* and increase in a*, indicating that dietary fiber incorporation modified the overall appearance of the biscuits. Although the present study did not directly quantify Maillard reaction products, the observed colour changes are consistent with previously reported effects of dietary fiber incorporation on thermal browning during baking [19]. Furthermore, the darker colour observed at higher substitution levels corresponded well with the lower appearance scores obtained during sensory evaluation (Section 3.5), demonstrating good agreement between instrumental and sensory measurements.

Overall, the colour analysis indicates that ultrasound-modified cauliflower dietary fiber significantly influenced biscuit appearance, primarily through changes associated with dietary fiber incorporation during baking. Importantly, moderate substitution levels (≤10%, B2) produced colour characteristics that remained acceptable to the sensory panel while providing enhanced nutritional and functional properties, highlighting their suitability for functional bakery applications.

Hardness

Hardness increased significantly (p < 0.05) from 28.65 ± 0.02 g in the control biscuits (B0) to 45.33 ± 0.02 g in biscuits containing the highest dietary fiber level (B4), representing an increase of approximately 58% (Table 11). The increase in hardness is consistent with partial replacement of wheat flour by dietary fiber, which reduces the proportion of gluten-forming proteins and alters dough hydration. In addition, the modified dietary fiber exhibited high water holding capacity (Section 3.2), thereby reducing the amount of free water available for gluten development during dough formation. Similar increases in biscuit hardness following dietary fiber enrichment have been reported by Ranasinghe et al. [21]. The increase observed in the present study was slightly greater than those reported for conventional fiber-fortified biscuits, which may be attributed to the higher hydration capacity of the ultrasound-modified dietary fiber.

Fracturability

Fracturability decreased significantly from 9.22 ± 0.02 g in the control to 7.15 ± 0.02 g at the highest substitution level. The reduction in fracturability indicates that fiber incorporation produced biscuits with lower crispness and greater structural rigidity. This behaviour is consistent with previous reports demonstrating that incorporation of plant-derived dietary fiber modifies the continuity of the biscuit matrix and reduces the formation of a uniform porous structure during baking [22]. Consequently, greater force was required to initiate fracture in fiber-enriched biscuits.

Cohesiveness

Cohesiveness showed a gradual decline from 0.55 to 0.44 with increasing dietary fiber incorporation, although the reduction remained relatively small at moderate substitution levels. Lower cohesiveness suggests a reduction in the internal structural integrity of the biscuit matrix, which is likely associated with partial disruption of the continuous gluten–starch network following flour replacement. Comparable reductions in cohesiveness have been reported for bakery products enriched with vegetable dietary fibers [23].

Springiness

Springiness decreased significantly from 1.44 in the control to 1.04 in the highest fiber formulation. This reduction indicates lower elastic recovery following compression and reflects the reduced ability of the biscuit matrix to deform and recover after application of force. The decrease is consistent with the dilution of gluten proteins and reduced development of a continuous viscoelastic network during dough preparation. Similar reductions in springiness have been reported in fiber-enriched bakery products [24].

Chewiness

Chewiness increased from 13.27 ± 0.12 to 15.44 ± 0.03 g, corresponding to an increase of approximately 16%. Because chewiness is influenced by hardness, cohesiveness, and springiness, the observed increase primarily reflects the substantial rise in hardness despite reductions in the other textural parameters. Similar increases in chewiness have been reported in fiber-fortified bakery products, where increased structural density required greater energy during mastication[25].

Overall, the instrumental texture results are consistent with the physicochemical and structural changes observed throughout this study. The enhanced water holding capacity of the ultrasound-modified dietary fiber (Section 3.2) altered dough hydration, while the reduction in particle size and increased surface area influenced interactions within the dough matrix. These combined effects produced biscuits with higher hardness and chewiness but lower elasticity and cohesiveness. Importantly, these instrumental measurements closely agreed with the sensory evaluation (Section 3.5), where texture scores progressively decreased as dietary fiber incorporation increased.

Among the tested formulations, B2 (10% dietary fiber) maintained favourable instrumental texture while achieving satisfactory sensory acceptance, indicating that this substitution level provides the most appropriate balance between technological performance, nutritional enhancement, and consumer acceptability.

Moisture content

Moisture content increased gradually during storage in both formulations (Table 12). The control biscuits exhibited an increase from 3.50 ± 0.05% to 4.53 ± 0.07%, whereas the dietary fiber-enriched biscuits increased from 5.17 ± 0.06% to 6.07 ± 0.09% after 90 days. The consistently higher moisture content in B2 reflects the enhanced water holding capacity of the ultrasound-modified dietary fiber (Section 3.2), which improved water retention throughout storage.

Similar increases in moisture content during storage have been reported for bakery products enriched with dietary fiber, where hydrophilic polysaccharides slowed moisture migration and maintained product hydration [26]. Although the fiber-enriched biscuits retained more moisture than the control, the increase remained moderate and did not adversely affect product quality.

Microbiological stability

Total plate count increased progressively with storage time in both formulations (Table 12). The microbial population increased from 108.67 ± 3.2 to 237.00 ± 7.2 CFU g−1 in the control biscuits and from 120.67 ± 3.8 to 517.00 ± 8.1 CFU g−1 in the dietary fiber-enriched biscuits after 90 days.

The slightly higher microbial counts observed in the fiber-enriched formulation are consistent with its greater moisture content. However, all microbial counts remained well below generally accepted microbiological limits for bakery products, indicating satisfactory microbial quality throughout storage. Similar trends have been reported for fiber-fortified biscuits, where modest increases in microbial counts occurred during storage without compromising product safety because of the low water activity of the product matrix [27].

Water activity

Water activity increased gradually during storage, rising from 0.32 to 0.39 in the control biscuits and from 0.40 to 0.49 in the dietary fiber-enriched formulation. Despite this increase, all measured values remained below the critical threshold of 0.60, which is widely recognized as limiting the growth of most bacteria, yeasts, and moulds.

The low water activity observed throughout storage indicates that a substantial proportion of water remained physically bound within the dietary fiber matrix rather than existing as free water available for microbial growth. Consequently, incorporation of ultrasound-modified dietary fiber improved moisture retention while maintaining microbiological stability.

Visual mould growth

No visible mould growth was detected in either formulation throughout the 90-day storage period. This observation agrees with the low water activity values and relatively low microbial counts obtained during storage, confirming that both formulations remained microbiologically stable under ambient storage conditions.

Overall storage performance

The storage results demonstrate that incorporation of ultrasound-modified dietary fiber influenced moisture dynamics without compromising product stability. The enhanced water holding capacity of the modified fiber increased moisture retention throughout storage, while water activity remained below the threshold required for significant microbial proliferation. Consequently, although fiber-enriched biscuits exhibited slightly higher moisture content and microbial counts than the control, both formulations maintained satisfactory microbiological quality and showed no visible mould development during the 90-day storage period.

These findings are consistent with previous reports demonstrating that dietary fiber can improve moisture retention in bakery products while maintaining shelf stability when water activity remains below critical values [26]. The present results therefore indicate that ultrasound-assisted modification of cauliflower dietary fiber enhances the functional properties of biscuits without adversely affecting storage stability, supporting its application as a sustainable functional ingredient for bakery products.

Changes in sensory attributes during storage

Appearance scores gradually decreased during storage for both formulations, with the control biscuits declining from 8.8 ± 0.20 to 7.5 ± 0.27, while the dietary fiber-enriched biscuits decreased from 8.4 ± 0.22 to 7.1 ± 0.30 after 90 days (Table 13). The reduction in appearance scores is likely associated with gradual moisture uptake and minor surface changes that occur during storage. Similar observations have been reported for baked products stored under ambient conditions, where moisture migration slightly altered the visual appearance without causing unacceptable quality deterioration [28].

Taste scores also decreased progressively throughout storage. The control biscuits showed a reduction from 8.8 ± 0.21 to 7.4 ± 0.28, whereas the fiber-enriched biscuits declined from 8.3 ± 0.23 to 6.9 ± 0.31. This gradual reduction is consistent with the loss of volatile flavour compounds and normal flavour changes associated with prolonged storage of bakery products. Comparable trends have been reported by Applied Sciences Editorial Team (2024), who attributed the decline in flavour primarily to oxidative and volatilization processes occurring during storage.

Texture acceptability followed a similar trend, decreasing from 8.6 ± 0.19 to 7.3 ± 0.26 in the control biscuits and from 8.2 ± 0.21 to 6.8 ± 0.29 in the dietary fiber-enriched formulation. These sensory observations are consistent with the gradual increase in moisture content observed during storage (Table 12), which likely contributed to slight softening of the biscuit matrix. Although minor changes in texture occurred, the products retained acceptable structural integrity throughout the storage period.

Overall acceptability gradually declined during storage but remained above the generally accepted threshold of 6.0 on the 9-point hedonic scale for both formulations. The control biscuits decreased from 8.7 ± 0.20 to 7.4 ± 0.27, while the dietary fiber-enriched biscuits showed a similar decline, indicating that consumer acceptance remained satisfactory throughout the 90-day storage period.

The gradual reduction in sensory quality closely corresponded with the physicochemical changes observed during storage. The increase in moisture content (Table 12) and the accompanying changes in texture were consistent with the slight decline in sensory texture scores, whereas the low water activity values (<0.60) and absence of visible mould growth confirmed that sensory deterioration was associated with normal physicochemical ageing rather than microbial spoilage. Similar relationships between moisture migration, texture modification, and sensory quality have been reported for fiber-enriched bakery products during storage [29].

Overall, the results demonstrate that incorporation of ultrasound-modified cauliflower dietary fiber did not adversely affect the sensory stability of biscuits during storage. Although a gradual decline in sensory quality was observed, both formulations maintained acceptable appearance, flavour, texture, and overall acceptability throughout the 90-day storage period, confirming the suitability of the modified dietary fiber for the development of shelf-stable functional bakery products.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that ultrasound-assisted enzymatic extraction is an effective and sustainable approach for valorizing cauliflower (Brassica oleracea var. botrytis) processing waste into functional dietary fibre with enhanced physicochemical and techno-functional properties. Among the treatment combinations investigated, 80% ultrasonication amplitude for 40 min produced the highest extraction yield (19.60%) and soluble dietary fibre content (21.01%), representing a substantial improvement over the lowest treatment. Ultrasound-assisted processing also reduced particle size, generated a more porous fibre microstructure, and significantly improved water-holding capacity, oil-holding capacity, and swelling capacity, thereby enhancing the functional performance of the extracted dietary fibre.

Incorporation of the modified dietary fibre into biscuits successfully improved nutritional quality while maintaining desirable technological and sensory attributes. A 10% dietary fibre substitution (B2) provided the best balance between nutritional enhancement, functional performance, and sensory acceptability, achieving an overall sensory score of 7.7/9. Furthermore, biscuits containing the selected formulation remained microbiologically safe and sensorially acceptable throughout 90 days of ambient storage, with water activity remaining below 0.60.

Overall, this study demonstrates that ultrasound-assisted enzymatic extraction provides a practical strategy for converting cauliflower processing waste into a value-added functional ingredient suitable for bakery applications, supporting sustainable by-product utilization and circular bioeconomy initiatives.
