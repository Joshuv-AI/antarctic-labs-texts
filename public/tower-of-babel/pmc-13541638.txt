Computational biology and bioinformatics for infectious disease research: From molecular mechanisms to population-level surveillance
Authors: Tao Huang, Junyu Luo, Yixue Li
Published: 2026

Infectious diseases remain among the most formidable challenges to global public health, as vividly demonstrated by the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic. The rapid accumulation of genomic, proteomic, epidemiological, and clinical data has created unprecedented opportunities for understanding and combating infectious diseases, yet it has also underscored the critical need for advanced computational and bioinformatics methodologies capable of transforming massive, heterogeneous datasets into actionable insights. From decoding the molecular mechanisms of host-pathogen interactions to forecasting epidemic trajectories at the population level, computational biology has become an indispensable pillar of modern infectious disease research. Therefore, we present this special issue on Computational Biology and Bioinformatics in Biosafety and Health, which brings together ten original articles spanning the full spectrum from molecular-level analysis to population-level surveillance. These contributions collectively illustrate how cutting-edge computational approaches, including large language models (LLMs), deep learning, knowledge graphs, explainable artificial intelligence (XAI), and semi-mechanistic modeling, are reshaping our capacity to predict, interpret, and respond to infectious disease threats:

Language models for molecular-level sequence analysis

The advent of large language models, originally developed for natural language processing, has catalyzed a paradigm shift in biological sequence analysis. By treating nucleotide and amino acid sequences as linguistic constructs, these models capture evolutionary constraints, structural motifs, and functional signals embedded in biological sequences with remarkable fidelity. Luo et al. [1] provide a comprehensive review of LLM applications in biological sequence analysis for infectious disease research, systematically surveying how models such as protein language models and nucleotide language models are being adapted for tasks ranging from antigenicity prediction to functional annotation of viral genomes. The authors establish a foundational framework for understanding the transformative potential of LLMs in this domain, while also identifying key challenges such as data scarcity for emerging pathogens and the need for domain-specific pre-training strategies.

Building upon this conceptual foundation, two studies in this issue demonstrate the practical power of language models at the molecular level. Shen et al. [2] developed a ribonucleic acid (RNA) language model-based approach for predicting SARS-CoV-2 genome interactions, leveraging the learned representations of RNA sequences to infer RNA-RNA interactions between the viral genome and host transcripts. Their method provides critical insights into how SARS-CoV-2 may hijack host regulatory networks through RNA-mediated interactions, offering a novel computational lens for understanding viral pathogenesis. Complementarily, Wang et al. [3] introduced DeepHVI, a multimodal deep learning framework that integrates protein language model embeddings with sequence-derived and network-based features to predict human-virus protein–protein interactions. By fusing heterogeneous modalities, DeepHVI achieved superior performance in identifying potential viral-host interaction interfaces, thereby facilitating the prioritization of therapeutic targets and the elucidation of viral entry and immune evasion mechanisms.

Taken together, these three studies underscore a shared paradigm: by leveraging the rich contextual representations learned from massive sequence databases, language models can uncover latent biological signals that traditional feature engineering approaches often miss, thereby providing a unified computational framework for diverse molecular-level prediction tasks in infectious disease research.

Knowledge graphs and explainable AI for knowledge integration

While language models are good at extracting patterns from sequence data, the integration of diverse molecular and clinical knowledge into coherent frameworks remains challenging. Knowledge graphs, which encode entities and their relationships in structured networks, have emerged as powerful tools for bridging this gap. Peng et al. [4] presented MTB-ImmunogenKG, an LLM-assisted knowledge graph specifically designed for antigen selection in tuberculosis vaccine research. By leveraging LLMs to extract and curate antigen-related knowledge from the literature, MTB-ImmunogenKG systematically integrates information on antigen immunogenicity, epitope distribution, and experimental validation status, enabling researchers to rationally prioritize vaccine candidates against Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB). This work exemplifies how the synergy between LLMs and knowledge graphs can accelerate the translational pipeline from data to vaccine design.

In a related effort focused on COVID-19, Yang et al. [5] developed a knowledge graph that integrates molecular variant information with clinical insights, leveraging both curated databases and LLM-assisted extraction from the literature. Their framework, CoVAR-KG, connects SARS-CoV-2 genomic variants with clinical phenotypes, therapeutic responses, and epidemiological metadata, providing a unified resource for understanding the clinical significance of emerging variants. The integration of LLMs for automated knowledge extraction addressed a critical bottleneck in knowledge graph construction, enabling the framework to keep pace with the rapid evolution of the pandemic.

Beyond knowledge integration, the interpretability of computational predictions is essential for building trust and guiding experimental validation. Cen et al. [6] addressed this need by developing an explainable artificial intelligence framework that reveals mutations associated with drug resistance in Mycobacterium tuberculosis. Their approach combines ensemble machine learning classifiers with SHAP-based feature attribution to identify specific genomic mutations contributing to resistance against first-line and second-line anti-TB drugs. By providing transparent, interpretable predictions, this framework not only advances our understanding of resistance mechanisms but also offers a practical tool for guiding personalized treatment regimens and surveillance of drug-resistant TB.

Collectively, these three studies illustrate a complementary relationship: while knowledge graphs provide the structured scaffolding for integrating heterogeneous data sources, explainable AI methods ensure that the predictions derived from these integrated data are transparent and interpretable, thereby forming a closed loop from data integration to trustworthy knowledge delivery.

Epidemiological modeling and population-level surveillance

The translation of molecular insights into effective public health strategies requires robust epidemiological models and surveillance frameworks. This special issue features four studies that address complementary aspects of population-level infectious disease analysis. Liu et al. [7] introduced TempSnap-Trace, a temporal snapshot-based framework for haplotype network tracing that enables the dynamic reconstruction of viral evolutionary trajectories over time. By partitioning genomic surveillance data into temporal snapshots and constructing haplotype networks for each time window, TempSnap-Trace captures the emergence, expansion, and extinction of viral lineages with high resolution, providing a powerful tool for real-time genomic epidemiology and early warning of emerging variants.

At the level of disease forecasting, Hao et al. [8] proposed a semi-mechanistic modeling strategy for infectious disease prediction that integrates mechanistic compartmental models with data-driven error correction and probabilistic prediction. Their approach addresses a fundamental limitation of purely mechanistic models—their inability to capture complex, time-varying dynamics driven by behavioral changes and policy interventions—by incorporating a learned error correction module. The resulting framework produces calibrated probabilistic forecasts that quantify prediction uncertainty, which is essential for informed decision-making during epidemic outbreaks.

Understanding the heterogeneous transmission dynamics of infectious diseases is equally critical. Wang et al. [9] investigated the attribution of super-spreaders to the COVID-19 outbreak, quantifying the disproportionate contribution of a small fraction of infected individuals to overall transmission. Their analysis employed the dispersion parameter to characterize the degree of overdispersion in transmission chains, demonstrating that super-spreading events play a dominant role in sustaining and amplifying COVID-19 outbreaks. These findings have direct implications for intervention strategies, suggesting that targeted measures aimed at preventing super-spreading events may be more effective than uniform population-wide interventions.

Finally, Yan et al. [10] developed the Urban Infection Susceptibility Index (UISI) and applied it to cities across China, providing a spatially explicit framework for assessing urban vulnerability to infectious disease outbreaks. The UISI integrates multiple dimensions of urban characteristics—including population density, healthcare infrastructure, climate conditions, and socioeconomic factors—into a composite index that identifies cities at elevated risk. This work bridges the gap between epidemiological modeling and urban planning, offering a practical tool for resource allocation and preparedness planning in the face of emerging infectious threats.

In concert, these four studies reveal a methodological continuum from genomic surveillance to urban-scale vulnerability assessment: TempSnap-Trace tracks evolutionary dynamics at the sequence level, the semi-mechanistic model translates these dynamics into forecasts of disease spread, the super-spreader analysis identifies the individual-level drivers of transmission heterogeneity, and the UISI contextualizes these dynamics within the broader urban environment, together forming a multi-layered surveillance framework that connects molecular evolution to public health preparedness.

Concluding remarks and future perspectives

The ten articles in this special issue collectively demonstrate the breadth and depth of computational biology and bioinformatics approaches applied to infectious disease research. A unifying theme across these contributions is the increasing convergence of methodologies: language models provide contextualized representations of biological sequences, knowledge graphs integrate heterogeneous data into structured frameworks, explainable AI ensures that computational predictions are transparent and actionable, and semi-mechanistic models bridge the gap between data-driven and theory-driven approaches to epidemic forecasting. Together, these tools form a multi-scale computational ecosystem that spans from molecular interactions to population-level dynamics, enabling a more holistic understanding of infectious diseases. Notably, the methodological convergence observed across these studies is not merely coincidental but reflects a deeper scientific imperative: the complexity of infectious disease systems demands computational frameworks that can seamlessly bridge scales, from molecular interactions to population dynamics, and integrate diverse data modalities into coherent analytical pipelines.

Several important directions emerge from this special issue. First, the integration of LLMs with domain-specific knowledge graphs represents a particularly promising avenue for accelerating translational research, as demonstrated by the MTB-ImmunogenKG [4] and CoVAR-KG [5] studies. Second, the development of explainable AI frameworks, as illustrated by the MTB drug resistance study [6], will be critical for ensuring that computational predictions can be trusted and acted upon in clinical and public health settings. Third, the incorporation of uncertainty quantification into epidemiological models, as shown by the semi-mechanistic forecasting approach [8], is essential for robust decision-making under the inherent unpredictability of epidemic dynamics. Finally, the spatial and temporal dimensions of infectious disease surveillance, captured by the UISI [10] and TempSnap-Trace [7] frameworks, respectively, highlight the importance of developing computational tools that can operate in real time and at scale.

Despite the remarkable promise of LLMs and other computational approaches highlighted in this special issue, it is important to acknowledge their current limitations. Large language models trained on biological sequences may inherit biases from the uneven taxonomic representation in training data, potentially leading to reduced performance for underrepresented pathogens or host species. Furthermore, the lack of explicit structural and mechanistic reasoning in current LLM architectures means that their predictions may not always be biologically interpretable or grounded in known molecular mechanisms. The computational cost of training and deploying large-scale models also poses practical challenges for resource-limited settings where infectious disease burdens are often highest. Addressing these limitations will require continued methodological innovation, including the development of structure-aware sequence models, hybrid approaches that combine data-driven learning with mechanistic constraints, and efficient model architectures that can be deployed in diverse computational environments.

As the field continues to evolve, we anticipate that the integration of these complementary approaches will become increasingly powerful and reliable for infectious disease research. We hope that this special issue will inspire further interdisciplinary collaboration and methodological innovation at the intersection of computational biology, bioinformatics, and public health.
