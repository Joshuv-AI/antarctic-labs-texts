Can Sound Replace Vision in LLaVA With Token Substitution?
Ali Vosoughi Jing Bi Pinxin Liu Yunlong Tang Chenliang Xu Computer Science Department, University of Rochester, NY, USA {ali.vosoughi@, jing.bi@, pliu23@ur., yunlong.tang@, chenliang.xu@}rochester.edu

Abstract
While multimodal systems have achieved impressive advances, they typically rely on text-aligned representations rather than directly integrating audio and visual inputs. This reliance can limit the use of acoustic information in tasks requiring nuanced audio understanding. In response, SoundCLIP explores direct audio-visual integration within multimodal large language models (MLLMs) by substituting CLIP’s visual tokens with audio representations and selecting sound-relevant patch tokens in models such as Large Language and Vision Assistant (LLaVA). We investigate two configurations: (1) projecting audio features into CLIP’s visual manifold via a multilayer perceptron trained with InfoNCE on paired audio-video segments, and (2) preserving raw audio embeddings with minimal dimensional adjustments. Experiments with five state-of-the-art audio encoders reveal a fundamental trade-off. While audio-to-video retrieval performance increases dramatically (up to 44 percentage points in Top-1 accuracy) when audio is projected into CLIP’s space, text generation quality declines: BLEU and ROUGE-L scores drop by 0.024, BERTScore by 0.057, and GPT-4 o evaluations by 0.13–0.37 points. Encoders pre-trained with text supervision (CLAP, Whisper, ImageBind) maintain stronger generative capabilities than those focused primarily on audiovisual alignment (Wav2CLIP, AudioCLIP), highlighting the value of language exposure for generation tasks. We introduce WhisperCLIP , an architecture that fuses intermediate representations from Whisper, as well as AudioVisual Event Evaluation (AVE-2) , a dataset of 580,147 three-second audiovisual clips with fine-grained alignment annotations. Our findings challenge the assumption that stronger cross-modal alignment necessarily benefits all multimodal tasks; instead, a Pareto frontier emerges wherein optimal performance depends on balancing retrieval accuracy with text generation quality. The codes and datasets can be accessed via https://github.com/ali-vosoughi/SoundCLIP .

1
Introduction

Despite remarkable advances in multimodal AI, a critical limitation is that most existing systems rely on text as an intermediary for cross-modal tasks. This text-centric paradigm—exemplified by CLIP
(
Radford et al. 2021
)
for image-text and CLAP
(
Elizalde et al. 2023
)
for audio-text—creates an information bottleneck, especially for modalities like audio where rich acoustic cues cannot be captured by textual descriptions. To address this limitation, we introduce and rigorously evaluate a direct audio-visual training approach that bypasses text-mediated alignment, leveraging off-the-shelf pre-trained audio encoders to facilitate robust audio-visual binding without discarding critical acoustic information. This strategy has significant implications for the development of multimodal language models capable of handling diverse data sources.

A key scientific contribution of this work is the discovery and quantification of a previously unrecognized trade-off in cross-modal representation learning between retrieval performance and generation quality. Through controlled experiments using multiple audio encoders and benchmarks, we present the first empirical evidence challenging the widespread assumption that stronger cross-modal alignment unilaterally benefits multimodal systems. These findings advance our conceptual understanding of representation learning and carry broad ramifications for multimodal AI research.

Our proposed framework, SoundCLIP, enables a training-free integration of audio features into CLIP-based language models by employing a principled token substitution scheme. Unlike prior approaches that either discard modality-specific information or mandate extensive architectural overhauls, SoundCLIP retains the rich acoustic attributes required for nuanced comprehension while preserving architectural compatibility with existing vision-language models. This method aligns with recent theoretical insights on embedding space geometry
(
Li et al. 2021
)
and also delivers practical innovations for multimodal integration.

Comprehensive evaluation across five state-of-the-art audio encoders—ImageBind
(
Girdhar et al. 2023
)
, Whisper
(
Radford et al. 2023
)
, CLAP
(
Elizalde et al. 2023
)
, AudioCLIP
(
Guzhov et al. 2022
)
, and Wav2CLIP
(
Wu et al. 2022
)
—on four diverse benchmarks reveals that audio encoders exhibit dramatically different CLIP-compatibility, with projection improving Top-1 audio-to-video retrieval by up to 44.4 percentage points. We further observe a systematic inverse relationship between retrieval performance and generation quality, as evidenced by GPT-4
o
evaluation scores decreasing by 0.13–0.37 points for more tightly aligned representations. Moreover, encoders with prior text exposure demonstrate superior generation capabilities even when alignment occurs without text, suggesting essential inductive biases for cross-modal reasoning.

To encourage further progress, we introduce two key resources. The first is WhisperCLIP, a method that fuses intermediate representations from Whisper to better reconcile the retrieval-generation trade-off. The second is AVE-2, a carefully curated dataset of 580,145 audio-visual clips with fine-grained alignment annotations spanning five quality dimensions. Both resources will be publicly released to spur more extensive investigation into this newly identified trade-off.

Our findings establish a Pareto frontier in cross-modal representation learning, underscoring that task-specific optimization is crucial. While tighter alignment with CLIP’s visual manifold benefits retrieval tasks, generation tasks require preserving richer modality-specific detail. This work thus advances both the theoretical understanding of cross-modal representations and offers practical guidance for designing multimodal AI systems that can flexibly and effectively handle diverse modalities while maintaining their unique informational strengths.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. We first introduce the
AVE-2
dataset, describing its design principles and quality control mechanisms. We then present the
SoundCLIP
framework, including the mathematical formulation of cross-modal alignment and the WhisperCLIP architecture. Next, we detail the experimental results that quantify the retrieval-generation trade-off. We position our findings in the context of related research before concluding with broader implications and directions for future work.

2.1
Dataset Design

AVE-2 comprises 580,147 video segments (3 seconds each), evaluated through our five-dimensional alignment scoring:
Temporal Alignment
(mean: 7.54),
Spatial Coherence
(8.35),
Contextual Relevance
(6.32),
Physical Causality
(9.02), and
Sound Source Visibility
(8.04), following the prompts described in
(
Vosoughi et al. 2025
)
. These dimensions were selected to capture fundamental aspects of audio-visual perception, from temporal synchronization to semantic congruency. Score distributions vary meaningfully across dimensions, with physical causality showing high consistency (99.71% of visible source segments score >8.0) while temporal alignment exhibits greater variability.

2.2
Dataset Composition

The dataset balances visible sources (490,546 segments), invisible sources (337,069), and silent objects (446,567) across diverse categories. This three-way categorization creates a more comprehensive evaluation benchmark than prior datasets, which predominantly feature only visible sound sources. The content spans 157,800 unique visible source categories, 111,644 invisible source categories, and 300,265 silent object categories, with distinctive frequency patterns (human subjects dominate visible sources, ambient sounds prevail in invisible sources, environmental elements in silent objects).

2.3
Alignment Evaluation Framework

AVE-2’s five-dimensional framework enables: (1) analysis of which alignment aspects most affect cross-modal integration; (2) targeted filtering for specialized experiments; (3) granular quality control; and (4) creation of task-optimized subsets. Our annotation process achieved high inter-annotator agreement (Cohen’s κ > 0.82) across all dimensions.

AVE-2 supports various applications including cross-modal alignment research, sound source localization, and multimodal scene understanding. The complete dataset, with alignment scores and annotation tools, will be publicly released.

3
SoundCLIP Framework

We formulate the problem of audio integration into vision-language models as a representation alignment challenge: can audio embeddings effectively substitute for visual tokens without architectural modifications to the underlying LLM? Our solution,
SoundCLIP
, enables direct token-level substitution through a principled manifold alignment approach, treating audio tokens as if they were visual tokens within CLIP’s embedding space.

Figure 3
:
SoundCLIP Architecture.
Our framework performs training-free audio-to-vision integration through: (1) encoding audio using pretrained models, (2) aligning representations with CLIP’s visual space via a lightweight MLP (1.9M params) or preserving raw embeddings, and (3) substituting the CLIP [CLS] token while selecting top-k similar visual patches based on cosine similarity.

3.1
Cross-Modal Manifold Alignment

Building on geometric approaches to cross-modal learning
(
Li et al. 2021
)
, we formulate audio-visual token substitution as an embedding space alignment problem. Given that vision-language models like LLaVA expect inputs from CLIP’s visual manifold, we must either transform audio representations to match this distribution or adapt the model to handle audio’s native geometric properties.

Problem Formulation.

Let an audiovisual segment
\mathcal{C}=(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{v})
consist of audio waveform
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{T_{a}}
and visual frames
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{T_{v}\times H\times W\times 3}
. A frozen audio encoder
\phi_{a}
produces embedding
\mathbf{h}_{a}=\phi_{a}(\mathbf{a})\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}}
, while a frozen CLIP ViT-L/14 encoder
\phi_{v}
yields
\mathbf{V}=\phi_{v}(\mathbf{v})\in\mathbb{R}^{(N+1)\times 1024}
, whose first row
\mathbf{c}
is the global
[CLS]
token and the remaining
N\!=\!576
rows are patch tokens
\{\mathbf{v}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
. Our objective is to learn a mapping
\mathcal{M}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{1024}
such that
\mathbf{f}_{a}=\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{h}_{a})
can effectively substitute for visual tokens in the downstream language model while preserving relevant acoustic information.

Alignment Strategies.

We investigate two complementary approaches for audio-visual integration that represent opposite priorities: (i)
CLIP-Aligned (Proj-MLP)
: A manifold projection module transforms audio into CLIP’s visual space using a three-block MLP structure with 1.9M parameters; (ii)
Non-Aligned (Raw-Pad)
: A dimensionality-matching approach that preserves the native audio embedding distribution through simple padding or truncation. This parameter-free approach serves both as a baseline and as an alternative strategy that prioritizes preserving modality-specific information.

To maximize the effectiveness of audio token substitution, we introduce WhisperCLIP, a novel audio encoder that leverages Whisper’s rich acoustic representations while addressing its speech-centric bias. Through systematic analysis of Whisper’s internal representations, we discovered that higher layers progressively filter out non-speech audio information—a beneficial property for ASR but detrimental for general acoustic understanding.

Multi-Layer Fusion.

Given Whisper’s layer-wise hidden states
\mathbf{H}=\{\mathbf{H}^{(l)}\}_{l=1}^{L}
where
\mathbf{H}^{(l)}\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times S\times d_{w}}
, we explore multiple strategies to extract audio representations that balance speech understanding with general acoustic information. Our comprehensive ablations revealed that the
average-all
variant offers the best performance:
\mathbf{z}_{a}=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\text{MeanTime}(\mathbf{H}^{(i)})
, where
\text{MeanTime}(\cdot)
performs temporal averaging across sequence positions. This approach achieves 8.2pp higher Top-1 retrieval (lower rank) accuracy compared to using only the final layer.

3.3
Token-Level Integration and Training-Free LLM Adaptation

SoundCLIP enables training-free audio integration with vision-language models through selective token substitution. Our approach (Algorithm
1
in Appendix) performs four operations: (1) audio representation extraction, (2) manifold alignment, (3) CLS token replacement, and (4) similarity-guided patch selection retaining the
k
most relevant visual patches.

The transformed representation
\mathbf{V}^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{(N+1)\times 1024}
feeds directly into LLaVA’s vision pathway with no additional training, offering practical advantages: minimal overhead (1.9M parameters, 0.8ms latency, <2MB VRAM) and compatibility with any CLIP-based model.

Our WhisperCLIP layer fusion approach (Figure
4
) preserves acoustic information from intermediate layers that would otherwise be filtered out, balancing speech content with environmental sounds and musical elements.

This investigation reveals a fundamental trade-off: CLIP-aligned embeddings improve retrieval performance (+44pp) but reduce generation quality. This confirms our theoretical model where output quality is proportional to
I_{shared}+\alpha I_{unique}
, with different tasks weighting these components differently. This discovery has significant implications for multimodal system design, suggesting that optimal architectures depend on whether the primary objective is retrieval or generation.

Our cross-modal retrieval experiments reveal several key insights about audio-visual compatibility. As shown in Figure
5

Dramatic Improvement Through Alignment.

Our results demonstrate that audio-visual alignment via projection is extremely effective, with Top-1 accuracy improving by up to +44.4 percentage points (ImageBind on AudioCaps). This dramatic enhancement suggests that the fundamental barrier to audio-visual integration in CLIP-based language models is primarily representational misalignment rather than semantic incompatibility.

Encoder-Specific Alignment Capacity.

While all encoders benefit from CLIP alignment, their improvement magnitudes differ substantially. ImageBind exhibits exceptional compatibility with CLIP’s visual space (mean rank improves from 50.23 to 4.78), while language-aligned encoders like CLAP show more modest improvements (53.94 to 13.61). This differential alignment capacity reveals that audio encoders pretrained with visual supervision inherit latent representational structures more compatible with CLIP’s visual manifold.

Finding 2.

Our WhisperCLIP multi-layer fusion approach provides significant retrieval improvements over standard Whisper (+3.4pp to +4.9pp across datasets), confirming that intermediate Whisper layers preserve valuable non-speech acoustic information that enhances cross-modal alignment capabilities.

WhisperCLIP’s Enhanced Integration.

Our proposed WhisperCLIP architecture improves retrieval performance over standard Whisper by 3.4-4.9 percentage points across datasets through its multi-layer fusion approach. This confirms our hypothesis that standard Whisper progressively discards non-speech acoustic information in higher layers, and that reintroducing intermediate-layer information enhances cross-modal alignment capacity.

Symmetrical Bidirectional Performance.

The relative performance patterns remain consistent across both Audio→Video and Video→Audio retrieval directions (see Table

4.4
Mechanistic Analysis of the Retrieval-Generation Trade-off

Finding 6.

We identify a fundamental trade-off in cross-modal alignment: stronger retrieval improvement correlates strongly with greater generation quality loss (r=0.89), with encoders showing variable efficiency in managing this trade-off (from 0.05% to 0.65% quality loss per percentage point of retrieval gain).

To understand the mechanism behind the retrieval-generation trade-off, we conducted a systematic analysis of embedding distributions, information content, and performance correlations.

Figure

visualizes the relationship between retrieval improvement and generation quality reduction across all encoders. The strong positive correlation (r=0.89) confirms that this trade-off is a systematic property of cross-modal alignment rather than an implementation artifact. Notably, encoders with larger retrieval gains (ImageBind, WhisperCLIP) exhibit more severe generation degradation, suggesting that stronger alignment comes at a higher cost to generative capacity.

Information-Theoretic Perspective.

We hypothesize that this trade-off arises from an information bottleneck effect during projection: aligning audio to CLIP’s visual manifold requires compressing acoustic information that doesn’t correspond to visual patterns, thereby discarding details crucial for nuanced generation. This is supported by the observation that semantic similarity (BERTScore) decreases more severely (-23.9%) than surface-level metrics like ROUGE-L (-8.1%) when using aligned embeddings.

Encoder-Specific Trade-off Efficiency.

Different encoders exhibit varying trade-off efficiencies, quantified as generation quality loss per percentage point of retrieval gain. Wav2CLIP shows the most favorable ratio (0.05% per point), while CLAP exhibits the highest cost (0.65% per point). This variation suggests that certain architectural properties can mitigate—but not eliminate—the fundamental tension between alignment and preservation.

Linear Interpolation Analysis.

Additional experiments with linear interpolation between raw and aligned embeddings failed to identify a middle ground that preserves both retrieval and generation performance, confirming that the trade-off represents a fundamental limitation rather than a simple optimization challenge.

)
has demonstrated the value of natural correspondences in preserving acoustic details, though challenges remain in handling temporal misalignment and off-screen sounds, which our AVE-2 dataset specifically addresses. Recent multimodal LLMs
(
Liu et al. 2023
;
Li et al. 2023
)
predominantly process visual inputs through frozen CLIP encoders projected into language space, while audio integration lags behind, typically reduced to text via ASR
(
Radford et al. 2023
)
, discarding rich acoustic information. Unlike approaches that rely on textual pivoting
(
Nguyen et al. 2024
;
Vosoughi et al. 2024
;
Bi et al.
)
or image-centric intermediaries
(
Su et al. 2024
)
, SoundCLIP offers direct alignment of audio with CLIP’s visual token space through a lightweight projection module (1.9M parameters) that requires no architectural modifications to the underlying language model. Our systematic analysis of the retrieval-generation trade-off provides the first quantitative evidence challenging the assumption that stronger cross-modal alignment universally benefits multimodal systems.

6
Conclusion

SoundCLIP reveals a fundamental trade-off in cross-modal representation learning: MLP projection onto CLIP’s visual manifold dramatically improves retrieval performance (+44 percentage points) while degrading text generation quality. This challenges the widely held assumption that stronger alignment universally benefits multimodal systems. The trade-off stems from information compression—alignment enhances cross-modal correspondences while sacrificing modality-specific acoustic details that lack direct visual counterparts. Our research contributions include both theoretical insights (Information Geometry Theory of Modal Manifolds) and practical tools (WhisperCLIP’s multi-layer fusion architecture and AVE-2’s five-dimensional alignment metrics), establishing a Pareto frontier where projected embeddings excel for retrieval tasks while raw embeddings deliver superior generation quality. This work advances multimodal systems toward seamless audio-visual integration while preserving each modality’s distinctive characteristics.

Appendix B
Additional Analysis

B.1
Linear Interpolation Analysis

We conducted experiments with linear interpolation between raw and aligned embeddings. We computed interpolated representations as
\mathbf{z}_{\text{interp}}=\alpha\cdot\mathbf{z}_{\text{raw}}+(1-\alpha)\cdot\mathbf{z}_{\text{aligned}}
for
\alpha\in\{0.0,0.25,0.5,0.75,1.0\}
. These experiments failed to identify a middle ground that preserves both retrieval and generation performance, confirming that the trade-off represents a fundamental limitation rather than a simple optimization challenge.

B.2
Practical Guidelines

Based on our comprehensive evaluation, we recommend:

•

For
retrieval tasks
: Use
projected embeddings
(ImageBind-FT performs best on AudioCaps with 45.2% Top-1 A→V accuracy)

•

For
generation tasks
: Use
raw embeddings
with cosine-guided patch replacement (CLAP-150tk-raw achieves highest GPT-4o overall score of 1.92)

•

For
WhisperCLIP configuration
: Use InfoNCE loss, average-all pooling, and backbone size of at least Whisper-small

•

For
token budget
: Allocate at least 150 visual tokens for best generation quality

This dichotomy highlights that the "best embedding" depends critically on the downstream task, challenging the conventional wisdom that projection into a shared embedding space is universally beneficial.

D.3
Data Filtering

Despite its scale, AudioSet contains many clips with weak or ambiguous audio-visual links. To mitigate noise, we adopt a five-score alignment scheme. This approach estimates temporal/spatial coherence, scene consistency, and other factors, assigning an overall alignment score to each audio-video pair. We retain only those segments with a score above 8.0 (chosen empirically to strike a balance between data quantity and quality). While filtering removes some usable clips, it substantially reduces label noise and stabilizes training. This preprocessing step is a practical measure for reproducibility.

Appendix E
Implementation Details

The SoundCLIP projection networks were structured as three-layer MLPs, incorporating LayerNorm, GELU activations, and a dropout rate of 0.1, which represents approximately 1.9 million parameters. These models were trained over 10 epochs using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of
5\times 10^{-5}
and a ReduceLROnPlateau scheduler with a factor of 0.1, which is adjusted based on patience with validation loss. For token substitution, we investigate two budget options:
k=15
driven by audio and
k=150
driven by vision, to examine the trade-off impacting audio-visual balance. The reproducibility of all reported results is ensured by seed randomization using PyTorch Lightning’s seed_everything.

Retrieval Evaluation Methodology.

We evaluate cross-modal retrieval using mean retrieval rank, where lower values indicate better performance. For each experiment, we sample 100 random items from a pool of up to 500 test samples, compute normalized cosine similarity between audio and video embeddings, and measure the position (rank) where the correct match appears in the sorted list. This process is repeated 5 times with different random subsets, and the mean rank is averaged across all runs. A perfect retrieval system would achieve rank=1, indicating that the correct match consistently appears first in the sorted similarity list. Top-k accuracy (T1, T3, T10) measures the percentage of queries where the correct match appears within the top k results.

Appendix F
SoundCLIP Framework Details

F.1
Theoretical Analysis of Cross-Modal Manifold Alignment

To formalize the mechanisms underlying cross-modal alignment, we develop the Information Geometry Theory of Modal Manifolds (IGTMM). This framework provides mathematical foundations for understanding a fundamental trade-off in cross-modal representation learning that we empirically observe in our experiments. We begin by decomposing any audio representation into orthogonal information components:
I(\mathbf{h}_{a})=I_{shared}(\mathbf{h}_{a};\mathbf{v})+I_{unique}(\mathbf{h}_{a})
, where
I_{shared}(\mathbf{h}_{a};\mathbf{v})
represents the mutual information between audio and visual modalities, while
I_{unique}(\mathbf{h}_{a})
captures modality-specific information with no visual correspondence.

A key theoretical insight is that audio and visual embedding manifolds exhibit fundamental incompatibility properties:

Theorem F.1

(Manifold Incompatibility)
.

Let
\mathcal{M}_{a}
and
\mathcal{M}_{v}
be the natural manifolds of audio and visual representations. Then any diffeomorphism
\phi:\mathcal{M}_{a}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}_{v}
must distort at least one of: (1) local geometry, (2) global topology, or (3) probability measure.

This incompatibility results from the intrinsic dimensional mismatch between modalities: audio representations require temporal dimensions to encode frequencies, harmonics, and sequence information that have no direct visual analogues in CLIP’s patch-based spatial representation. Our theoretical analysis reveals that different downstream tasks have fundamentally different information requirements:
\text{Retrieval performance}\propto I_{shared}(\mathbf{h}_{a};\mathbf{v})
and
\text{Generation quality}\propto I_{shared}(\mathbf{h}_{a};\mathbf{v})+\alpha\cdot I_{unique}(\mathbf{h}_{a})
, where
\alpha>0
is a task-dependent weighting factor. This formalization precisely explains why optimizing cross-modal alignment for retrieval may not necessarily improve generative capacity—indeed, it can degrade it by sacrificing modality-specific information.

We can now formally prove that our projection process creates a Pareto frontier between retrieval and generation performance:

Proposition F.2
.

Under information-theoretic constraints, an increase in
I_{shared}(\mathbf{h}_{a};\mathbf{v})
through projection necessarily decreases
I_{unique}(\mathbf{h}_{a})
by at least
\beta\cdot\Delta I_{shared}
where
\beta>0
is a constant determined by the manifold structures.

This theoretical result, which we validate empirically in Section
4.4
, explains why stronger alignment with CLIP’s visual space improves retrieval but simultaneously degrades generation quality—a previously unrecognized trade-off in multimodal representation learning.

F.2
Detailed Alignment Strategies

Our CLIP-Aligned approach uses a three-block MLP structure:

\mathbf{f}_{a}^{\text{mlp}}=W_{3}\sigma\!\Bigl(\mathrm{LN}\!\bigl(W_{2}\sigma(\mathrm{LN}(W_{1}\mathbf{z}_{a}+b_{1}))+b_{2}\bigr)\Bigr)+b_{3}

where
W_{1}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{1024\times d_{a}}
,
W_{2},W_{3}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{1024\times 1024}
,
\sigma=\text{GELU}
, and LN denotes layer normalization. This structure was selected after extensive ablation studies comparing different architectures, with this configuration offering optimal parameter efficiency while maximizing alignment quality.

The Non-Aligned approach implements dimensionality matching through:

\mathbf{f}_{a}^{\text{pad}}=\begin{cases}[\mathbf{z}_{a};\mathbf{0}_{1024-d_{a}}]&\text{if }d_{a}<1024\\
\mathbf{z}_{a}[0:1024]&\text{if }d_{a}>1024\\
\mathbf{z}_{a}&\text{if }d_{a}=1024\end{cases}

F.3
WhisperCLIP Theoretical Motivation

The multi-layer fusion strategy is motivated by insights from representation bottleneck theory
[
Tishby and Zaslavsky 2015
,
Alemi et al. 2016
]
: as information flows through Whisper’s layers, non-speech acoustic information is progressively filtered out due to the model’s ASR-focused training objective. By integrating intermediate representations, WhisperCLIP preserves acoustic features crucial for cross-modal alignment while maintaining speech understanding capabilities.

F.4
Contrastive Learning Details

The CLIP-Aligned variant of SoundCLIP employs a specialized training objective to optimize cross-modal alignment while preserving acoustic semantics. Our objective combines bidirectional InfoNCE with distribution matching regularization:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{NCE}}=-\frac{1}{2B}\sum_{i=1}^{B}\Bigl[\log\frac{e^{\mathbf{f}_{a}^{i}\cdot\mathbf{c}^{i}/\tau}}{\sum_{j}e^{\mathbf{f}_{a}^{i}\cdot\mathbf{c}^{j}/\tau}}+\log\frac{e^{\mathbf{c}^{i}\cdot\mathbf{f}_{a}^{i}/\tau}}{\sum_{j}e^{\mathbf{c}^{i}\cdot\mathbf{f}_{a}^{j}/\tau}}\Bigr]

\mathcal{L}_{\text{dist}}=\lambda\|\mu_{a}-\mu_{v}\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\|\sigma_{a}-\sigma_{v}\|_{2}^{2}

\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{NCE}}+\mathcal{L}_{\text{dist}}

where
\tau=0.07
is the temperature parameter and
\lambda=0.02
controls the strength of distribution matching. This combined objective serves two complementary purposes: InfoNCE loss aligns semantic content across modalities, ensuring that audio and visual features from the same segment are mapped close together, while distribution matching regularization encourages the projected audio features to match the first- and second-order statistics of CLIP’s visual distribution, facilitating seamless integration.
