Graph Powers of Groups
Gabe Cunningham, Igor Minevich

Abstract
The Lights Out Puzzle, played on a graph Γ \Gamma , has been studied using linear algebra over 𝔽 2 \mathbb{F}_{2} and more generally over ℤ / k ​ ℤ \mathbb{Z}/k\mathbb{Z} . We generalize the setting by allowing the states of vertices to be the elements of a group G G , where a click in vertex v v multiplies the state of v v and its neighbors by an element g ∈ G g\in G on the right. Starting with the identity element e ∈ G e\in G for all vertices, the totality of all achievable state configurations forms a group G Γ G^{\Gamma} . This group generalizes parallel products of group actions and provides a rich structure for analysis. For many graphs, which we term “RA” (reducible to abelian), the problem reduces — regardless of G G — to a linear algebra question over ℤ \mathbb{Z} . We discuss a chain of five different subgroups consisting of commutators and introduce techniques for showing that families of graphs are RA using each. In particular, using Heisenberg groups, we establish that a graph is RA precisely when a certain lattice spans ℤ | Γ | \mathbb{Z}^{|\Gamma|} . While most graphs appear to be RA, we show the odd-dimensional cube graphs Q 2 ​ n + 1 Q_{2n+1} and folded cube graphs □ d \square_{d} , for d d odd or 2, are not.

Introduction

The classic Lights Out Puzzle is played on a grid of squares that can be either on or off, and clicking a square makes it and the adjacent squares switch states. The goal is to start with all squares being on and get to a state where all are off. Equivalently, we could start with all the squares being in state 0 and thinking about clicking like adding 1 to the state of a vertex and its neighbors
\pmod{2}
, with the goal being to get to all 1’s.

We explore a natural generalization of the Lights Out Puzzle, or Confused Electrician Games
[
3
]
, by changing the geography of the puzzle as well as the number and quality of states that a given puzzle piece can experience. Instead of a 5x5 board, we have a finite graph
\Gamma
whose vertices are the placeholders substituting what used to be squares. And now, the vertices need not simply have two states (modeled by the group of two elements,
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
). Instead, they are colored according to the set of all the elements of
any
given group
G
, and their states change accordingly. A “click” in a vertex must specify an element
g
of the group
G
that is performed on the vertex and its neighbors, and then the states of the vertex and its neighbors are multiplied on the right by
g
.

As it turns out, the set of all possible states that could be achieved simultaneously in all the vertices, starting from the identity element
1\in G
in all of them, is a group.
If we start with any other state, the states we can reach from there are in direct 1-1 correspondence with this group, which we call
G^{\Gamma}
.
Many authors (far too many to list) have analyzed this kind of situation in the case of abelian groups
G
(see
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
,
9
,
10
,
14
,
15
]
), so we are most interested in the non-abelian case.

The short exact sequence

1\to[G,G]^{|\Gamma|}\cap G^{\Gamma}\to G^{\Gamma}\to(G^{\textrm{Ab}})^{\Gamma}\to 1

(1)

turns the exploration of
G^{\Gamma}
into (1) an “abelian question” (what is
(G^{\textrm{Ab}})^{\Gamma}
), for which we provide some answers in
Section

4
but for the most part defer to other papers, and (2) the amazing exploration of
[G,G]^{|\Gamma|}\cap G^{\Gamma}
. If the latter is all of
[G,G]^{|\Gamma|}
, then we can change the state of any vertex by any commutator independent of the rest, which reduces the question completely to the abelian case, making the description of
G^{\Gamma}
straightforward. Whenever
[G,G]^{|\Gamma|}\subseteq G^{\Gamma}
for all
G
(and a fixed
\Gamma
), we say that
\Gamma
is
RA (reducible to abelian)
.

Indeed, we show many graphs are RA (including grids, trees, cycles, complete bipartite graphs, and more) – see Section
5.2
. We also describe a method for directly computing whether a graph is RA; see Theorem
6.11
. The problem of constructing families of non-RA graphs seems difficult, though we exhibit two families of non-RA graphs; see Corollary
6.14
.

For some families of graphs, it is easy to show that they are RA using a subgroup of
[G,G]^{|\Gamma|}\cap G^{\Gamma}
that is easy to work with, namely the subgroup generated by commutators of clicks. There are RA graphs that cannot be proven to be RA using only the commutator tricks of Section
5.2
, and we will explore these in
[
1
]
.
Here we begin to explore the chain of inclusions of subgroups of
[G,G]^{|\Gamma|}\cap G^{\Gamma}
(see (
4
)). The structure of Heisenberg groups of order
p^{3}
is so nice that these subgroups turn out to coincide for every
p
, and we are able to use a local-to-global principle to make the characterization of RA graphs in Theorem
6.11
.

Our work sheds light on subgroups of direct products of groups and provides heretofore unexplored generalizations of topics related to the Lights Out Puzzle. It also leads naturally to a generalization of the
parallel product
(see
[
16
]
) of group actions. In the examples in Section
3.1
and Example
5.3
, we briefly explore one situation where this sort of action may arise, namely when we have Rubik’s Cubes strung together in a way that when you move one of them, the adjacent ones also move in the same way.

Preliminaries

2.1
Graph theory

All graphs in this paper will be finite undirected simple graphs (no loops or multiple edges). If
v
is a vertex of a graph
\Gamma
, then
B(v)
will be the closed neighborhood of
v
; that is, the set that contains
v
and all of its neighbors in
\Gamma
. The number of vertices of
\Gamma
will be
|\Gamma|
.

Unless explicitly stated otherwise, we will assume the vertices of the graph are labeled
v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{n}
(with
n=|\Gamma|
) and the notation
\mathbf{e}_{i}
refers to the
|\Gamma|
-dimensional vector
\langle 0,\dots,0,1,0,\dots,0\rangle
with the
1
in the
i
-th place, corresponding to
v_{i}
. More generally, if
v
is a vertex of
\Gamma
, then
\mathbf{e}_{v}
is the vector with a 1 in the coordinate corresponding to
v
and a 0 everywhere else.

By the
indicator vector
for a subset
U\subseteq V(\Gamma)
, we mean
\sum_{v\in U}\mathbf{e}_{v}
, i.e. the vector with a 1 in each coordinate that corresponds to a member of
U
.

We will refer to the following standard graphs:

1.

The path graph
P_{n}
with
n
vertices,

2.

The cycle graph
C_{n}
with
n
vertices,

3.

The complete graph
K_{n}
with
n
vertices,

4.

The complete bipartite graph
K_{m,n}
with
m+n
vertices,

5.

The star graph
\St_{n}
with
n+1
vertices, and

6.

The
d
-dimensional cube graph
Q_{d}
with
2^{d}
vertices, represented by bit strings of length
d
, with two vertices adjacent if and only if they differ in a single bit.

The adjacency matrix of
\Gamma
will be denoted
\Adj(\Gamma)
.

2.2
Group theory

We will denote the identity of a group by
1
and abbreviate
\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
to
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
. The commutator of
x,y\in G
is
[x,y]=xyx^{-1}y^{-1}
.
We will denote the abelianization of
G
(that is,
G/[G,G]
) by
G^{\textrm{Ab}}
.

We will denote by
D_{2n}
the dihedral group (of size
2n
) on
n
vertices and use two presentations for it:

\displaystyle D_{2n}

\displaystyle=\langle x,y\mid x^{2}=y^{2}=(xy)^{n}=1\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle r,s\mid s^{2}=(rs)^{2}=r^{n}=1\rangle.

Recall (see e.g.
[
2
]
) for any prime
p
, the Heisenberg group is the group of
3\times 3
upper triangular matrices in
\GL_{3}(\mathbb{F}_{p})
with 1’s on the main diagonal, which we will refer to as
H(\mathbb{F}_{p})=H_{1}(\mathbb{F}_{p})
; more generally, Heisenberg groups
H_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{p})
of order
p^{2n+1}
exist, whose elements are
(n+2)\times(n+2)
matrices of the form

\begin{pmatrix}1&\bf a&c\\
0&I_{n}&\bf b\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix}.

By explicit computation, it is easy to deduce several key properties:

1.

All such groups have exponent
p
if
p>2
, and if
p=2
, the exponent is 4.

2.

For the special case where
p=2
and
n=1
, we have
H(\mathbb{F}_{2})\cong D_{8}
.

3.

The commutator subgroup of any Heisenberg group is the center, namely the subgroup (isomorphic to)
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
where
\bf a=\bf b=\bf 0
.

4.

The abelianization of
H_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{p})
is
(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^{2n}
, and we can find the
2n
generators for the direct summands by taking
\bf a=\bf e_{i}
and
\bf b=\bf 0
and vice-versa, with
c=1
; such generators are involutions when
p=2
.

2.3
Linear algebra

The Smith Normal Form
[
2
]
of an
m\times n
matrix
M
with integer coefficients is the diagonal matrix
N
with integer entries such that there exist matrices
U\in GL_{m}(\mathbb{Z}),V\in GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
with
UMV=N
and the entries along the diagonal are the
elementary divisors
(or invariant factors)
a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{\min(m,n)}
of
M
, with
a_{1}\mid a_{2}\mid a_{3}\mid\cdots
. It is well-known that the
i
-th elementary divisor equals
d_{i}(M)/d_{i-1}(M)

[
12
]
, where
d_{i}(M)
is the greatest common divisor of the
i\times i
minors of
M
and
d_{0}(M)=1
.

We briefly mention one useful fact about elementary divisors.

Proposition 2.1
.

Suppose
M
is
m\times n
with
m\geq n
. If there is a prime
p
such that every row sum of
M
is divisible by
p
, then
M
has an elementary divisor that is divisible by
p
(possibly equal to 0).

Proof.

The submatrix corresponding to any
m\times m
minor multiplied by
\vec{1}=(1,1,\dots,1)^{T}
results in
\vec{0}\pmod{p}
, which means it is a singular matrix
\pmod{p}
, hence its determinant is divisible by
p
. Thus,
p\mid d_{m}(M)
, and since
d_{0}(M)=1
and each
d_{i}(M)
divides
d_{i+1}(M)
, there is an index
i
somewhere along the chain such that
p\nmid d_{i}(M)
but
p\mid d_{i+1}(M)
, and for that
i
we have the
i
-th elementary divisor
a_{i}
divisible by
p
.
∎

3
Graph powers of groups

3.1
Definition and examples

The guiding metaphor for what we will call the
graph power of a group
is this: start with a graph
\Gamma
and a group
G
. Put the identity element of
G
at every vertex of
\Gamma
– this corresponds with the identity element of
G^{n}
. If we ‘click’ vertex
v
with group element
g
, then we want to multiply the states of all vertices in the closed neighborhood
B(v)
by
g
on the right. The set of achievable states is what we call
G^{\Gamma}
.

We will start with a somewhat more general way to construct subgroups of
G^{n}
. First, we make the following definition.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
G
be a group, and let
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
.
We define

g^{\mathbf{x}}=(g^{x_{1}},\ldots,g^{x_{n}})\in G^{n}.

The following properties are clear:

Proposition 3.2
.

1.

g^{\mathbf{x}}g^{\mathbf{y}}=g^{\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y}}

2.

If
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, then
(g^{k})^{\mathbf{x}}=g^{k\mathbf{x}}

3.

If every component of
\mathbf{x}
is 0 or 1, then
g^{\mathbf{x}}h^{\mathbf{x}}=(gh)^{\mathbf{x}}

Definition 3.3
.

Suppose
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. We define

G^{S}=\langle g^{\mathbf{x}}\mid g\in G,\mathbf{x}\in S\rangle.

If
M
is an integer matrix, then we define
G^{M}
to be
G^{S}
where
S
is the set of rows of
M
.

Note that by Proposition
3.2
,
G^{S}=G^{\langle S\rangle}
, so we will usually think of
S
as a set of generating vectors, rather than an entire integer lattice.

Now suppose that
\Gamma
is a graph with vertex set
\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\}
.
We define the
activation matrix of
\Gamma
to be

A_{\Gamma}=\Adj(\Gamma)+I_{n},

where
I_{n}
is the
n\times n
identity matrix.
If
v
is a vertex of
\Gamma
and
\mathbf{x}
is the corresponding row of
A_{\Gamma}
, then ‘clicking
v
with
g
’ is accomplished by multiplying the state by
g^{\mathbf{x}}
. In other words, our metaphor is realized with the following definition:

Definition 3.4
.

If
\Gamma
is a finite graph with
n
vertices, then the
graph power of
G
with respect to
\Gamma
(or simply the
\Gamma
power of
G
),
denoted
G^{\Gamma}
, is the subgroup of
G^{n}
defined by
G^{\Gamma}=G^{A_{\Gamma}}
, where
A_{\Gamma}=\Adj(\Gamma)+I_{n}
.

For convenience, if
v
is a vertex of
\Gamma
, then we will write
g^{v}
to mean
g^{\mathbf{x}}
, where
\mathbf{x}
is the row of
A_{\Gamma}
that corresponds to
v
. Similarly, if
k
is an integer, we understand
g^{kv}
to mean
(g^{k})^{v}
. Recall that
\mathbf{e}_{v}
is the vector with a 1 in the coordinate corresponding to
v
and 0 everywhere else, which implies that
g^{\mathbf{e}_{v}}
is the element of
G^{n}
that has a
g
in the coordinate corresponding to
v
and a
1
(i.e. the identity of
G
) everywhere else.

We will continue to use the Lights Out metaphor, so that multiplying (on the right) by
g^{v}
will also be referred to as
clicking
v
with
g
. The utility of this metaphor will become apparent.

For GAP code that can be used to compute
G^{\Gamma}
, see Figure
1
in
Appendix B.

Let us explore several illustrative examples.

Example 3.5
.

Suppose that
\Gamma
is a complete graph
K_{n}
. Then since every vertex is adjacent to every other vertex, clicking any vertex with
g
has the effect of multiplying every coordinate by
g
. Thus
G^{\Gamma}
is the
diagonal subgroup
of
G^{n}
, where every coordinate is equal. This shows that the group
G^{\Gamma}
is a generalization of the diagonal subgroup of the product of groups.

Example 3.6
.

Imagine a graph
\Gamma
with a Rubik’s Cube attached to each vertex, and any time we perform a move on one cube, that same move is performed on its neighbors. Then we can ask questions like: is it possible to simultaneously solve every Rubik’s cube this way, no matter what the original configuration is? This is equivalent to the question of whether
G^{\Gamma}=G^{|\Gamma|}
.

Example 3.7
.

More generally, suppose that we attach a set
S_{i}
to each vertex
v_{i}
of
\Gamma
and that
G
acts on each
S_{i}
. We model the state of this “game” with an element of
S=S_{1}\times\cdots\times S_{|\Gamma|}
, and clicking a vertex with
g
acts on the state of that vertex and its neighbors by
g
. This gives us an action of
G^{\Gamma}
on
S
which generalizes the
parallel product
of group actions (see
[
16
]
); indeed, if
\Gamma=K_{n}
, then the action of
G^{\Gamma}
is the parallel product of the actions on the
n
sets.

Example 3.8
.

Suppose that
\Gamma
is a path graph of length 3, with vertices numbered in the natural way. Then clicking vertex 2 with
g
and clicking vertex 1 with
g^{-1}
yields
(1,1,g)
, so
(1,1,g)\in G^{\Gamma}
for every
g
. Similarly,
(g,1,1)\in G^{\Gamma}
. Finally, since clicking vertex 2 with
g
yields
(g,g,g)
, it follows that
(1,g,1)\in G^{\Gamma}
, and thus
G^{\Gamma}=G^{3}
. This means it is indeed always possible to solve three Rubik’s cubes that are connected in a row as described in the introduction, provided each one is in a solvable state itself (let
G
= the Rubik’s Cube group; we have shown that we can act on each Rubik’s Cube individually by using combinations of moves).

Example 3.9
.

Now suppose that
\Gamma
is a cycle with
4
vertices, and let
G=(\mathbb{Z},+)
, the additive group of the integers. Then
G^{\Gamma}
is the subgroup of the additive group of
\mathbb{Z}^{4}
that is generated by the four permutations of
(1,1,1,0)
. Clearly, every element of
G^{\Gamma}
has a coordinate sum that is divisible by 3, so it is a proper subgroup of
\mathbb{Z}^{4}
.

If instead, we set
G=\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, then clicking any three vertices with
1
yields a permutation of
(1,0,0,0)
. All four permutations are obtainable this way, and thus,
G^{\Gamma}=G^{4}
.

What we see here is that for some graphs,
G^{\Gamma}
is the whole direct product
G^{n}
regardless of
G
. Similarly, sometimes
G^{\Gamma}
is a proper subgroup of
G^{n}
regardless of
G
. On the other hand, for some graphs, the choice of
G
influences the index of
G^{\Gamma}
in
G^{n}
.

Note that if
G=\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, then
G^{\Gamma}
is essentially the state space of playing “Lights Out” on
\Gamma
. In the literature
[
4
]
, a graph
\Gamma
is Always Winnable (AW) if, essentially,
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\Gamma}=\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
.

More generally, we will see that
\mathbb{Z}
plays an important role. The following result follows from the definition of
G^{S}
, but we will find it useful to spell things out.

Proposition 3.10
.

If
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{n}
and
\langle S\rangle=\mathbb{Z}^{n}
(so that
\mathbb{Z}^{S}=\mathbb{Z}^{n}
), then
G^{S}=G^{n}
for every group
G
. In particular, if
\mathbb{Z}^{\Gamma}=\mathbb{Z}^{|\Gamma|}
, then
G^{\Gamma}=G^{|\Gamma|}
for every group
G
.

Proof.

If
\langle S\rangle=\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, then in particular,
\mathbf{e}_{i}\in\langle S\rangle
for each
i=1,\ldots,n
. In other words, if
S=\{\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{m}\}
, there is a linear combination that yields
\mathbf{e}_{i}
, say

a_{1}\mathbf{x}_{1}+\cdots+a_{m}\mathbf{x}_{m}=\mathbf{e}_{i},

for some integers
a_{j}
. Now, for every
g\in G
, it follows that
g^{a_{1}\mathbf{x}_{1}}\cdots g^{a_{m}\mathbf{x}_{m}}=g^{\mathbf{e}_{i}}
. In other words,
G^{S}
contains the element of
G^{n}
with
g
in coordinate
i
and the identity everywhere else. Then clearly, we can set each coordinate of an element of
G^{S}
independently from the rest, and so
G^{S}=G^{n}
.
∎

The argument of Proposition
3.10
can be generalized as follows; the argument is essentially the same.

Proposition 3.11
.

Let
k>1
be an integer. Suppose
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{n}
and that
(k\mathbb{Z})^{n}\leq\langle S\rangle
. Fix a group
G
and let
H
be the subgroup of
G
generated by
k
-th powers. Then
H^{n}\leq G^{S}
. In particular, if the
k
th powers of elements of
G
generate
G
, then
G^{S}=G^{n}
.

We see that the structure of
\mathbb{Z}^{\Gamma}
is instrumental in understanding
G^{\Gamma}
. We will focus on determining
\mathbb{Z}^{\Gamma}
in
Section

4
.

3.2
Subgroups of
G
and
G^{S}

We will now work out some basic structural results of
G^{\Gamma}
and, more generally,
G^{S}
. Recall that the commutator subgroup
[G,G]
generated by all the commutators
[x,y]\in G
is a normal subgroup; we will consider arbitrary normal subgroups
K
here for generality, but the reader may find it convenient to keep in mind that
K
will be
[G,G]
in the most important applications in the future. We will need to think about the abelianization
G/[G,G]
of
G
, and then the decomposition of that abelianization as the direct product of cyclic groups, so we start with two results that will be instrumental in the rest of the paper:

Proposition 3.12
.

Let
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{n}
.

1.

If
K\lhd G
, then there is a natural epimorphism from
G^{S}
to
(G/K)^{S}
with kernel
K^{n}\cap G^{S}
.

2.

(G\times H)^{S}
is naturally isomorphic to
G^{S}\times H^{S}
.

Proof.

The first result is straightforward. For the second one, let
K\lhd G
and let
\varphi:G^{n}\to(G/K)^{n}
be the natural epimorphism. This induces a natural epimorphism from
G^{S}
to
(G/K)^{S}
, and the kernel consists of all elements of
G^{S}
that have an element of
K
in each component.
∎

Proposition 3.13
.

Let
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{n}
.
If
K\lhd G
and
G^{S}=G^{n}
, then
(G/K)^{S}=(G/K)^{n}
.

Proof.

For any element
h
of
(G/K)^{n}
, pick a preimage
g
in
G^{n}
. Then we can write
g
as a product of generators of
G^{S}
, and the product of the images of these generators in
(G/K)^{S}
yields
h
.
∎

Proposition 3.14
.

Let
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{n}
and let
K\lhd G
. Then
G^{S}=G^{n}
if and only if
K^{n}\leq G^{S}
and
(G/K)^{S}=(G/K)^{n}
.

Proof.

If
G^{S}=G^{n}
, then clearly
K^{n}\leq G^{S}
. Proposition
3.13
proves the rest of that direction.

Now suppose that
K^{n}\leq G^{S}
and that
(G/K)^{S}=(G/K)^{n}
. Consider
(g_{1},\ldots,g_{n})\in G^{n}
. Projecting to
(G/K)^{n}
we get
(Kg_{1},\ldots,Kg_{n})
in
(G/K)^{n}
. Since
(G/K)^{S}=(G/K)^{n}
, we can write this element as
h_{1}^{\mathbf{x}_{1}}\cdots h_{m}^{\mathbf{x}_{m}}
in
(G/K)^{S}
. That is, each
h_{i}\in G/K
. For each
h_{i}
, let
\tilde{h_{i}}\in G
such that
\tilde{h_{i}}
projects to
h_{i}
. Then
\tilde{h_{1}}^{\mathbf{x}_{1}}\cdots\tilde{h_{m}}^{\mathbf{x}_{m}}
is an element of
G^{S}
that projects to
h_{1}^{\mathbf{x}_{1}}\cdots h_{m}^{\mathbf{x}_{m}}=(Kg_{1},\ldots,Kg_{n})
. Thus, the element of
G^{S}
differs from
(g_{1},\ldots,g_{n})
only by an element of
K^{n}
, and since
K^{n}\leq G^{S}
, it follows that
(g_{1},\ldots,g_{n})\in G^{S}
as well.
∎

Corollary 3.15
.

Let
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{n}
and let
K\lhd G
. Suppose that
K^{S}=K^{n}
and
(G/K)^{S}=(G/K)^{n}
. Then
G^{S}=G^{n}
.

Remark 3.16
.

Note that the converse of Corollary
3.15
is not true, even in the restricted case where we work with
G^{\Gamma}
in place of
G^{S}
. For example, consider
\Gamma=C_{5}
,
G=S_{4}
, and
K=[G,G]=A_{4}
. In GAP we verified that
(S_{4})^{C_{5}}=(S_{4})^{5}
, but
(A_{4})^{C_{5}}\neq(A_{4})^{5}
. Thus in this case, we have
K^{5}\leq G^{\Gamma}
, but
K^{5}\not\leq K^{\Gamma}
. In other words, we need to click with elements outside of
K
in order to get some elements of
K^{5}
.

3.3
How the structure of
\Gamma
affects
G^{\Gamma}

Our main interest is in the group
G^{\Gamma}
for graphs
\Gamma
. Let us study how some basic graph properties of
\Gamma
affect
G^{\Gamma}
. The first result is simple.

Proposition 3.17
.

If
\Gamma
has connected components
\Gamma_{1},\ldots,\Gamma_{k}
, then
G^{\Gamma}=G^{\Gamma_{1}}\times\cdots\times G^{\Gamma_{k}}
.

Next we extend Example
3.5

Definition 3.18
.

Proposition 3.19
.

Suppose that
\Gamma
is a graph with vertices

Proof.

First, note that if

There is a natural epimorphism from

4
Graph powers of an abelian group

4.1
Determining
G^{S}
from the lattice
\mathcal{L}=\langle S\rangle

We saw in Propositions
3.10
and
3.11
some ways that the structure of
\langle S\rangle
can affect the structure of
G^{S}
. In fact, we will see that if
G
is abelian, then in some sense
G^{S}
is completely determined by
\langle S\rangle
.

We will find it easier to work with matrices here rather than subsets of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. If
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{n}
and
|S|=m
, then we can find a basis for the integer lattice
\mathcal{L}=\langle S\rangle
and then construct an
m\times n
matrix
M
whose rows are the vectors in the chosen basis. Then
G^{M}=G^{\langle S\rangle}=G^{S}
.

If
G
is abelian, one way of understanding the structure of
G^{M}
is by working over the principal ideal domain
\mathbb{Z}
and finding the Smith Normal Form
N
of
M
(cf.
[
2
]
) and the elementary divisors. If we have
U\in GL_{m}(\mathbb{Z}),V\in GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
so that
UMV=N
, then
G^{S}=G^{n}M=G^{n}U^{-1}NV^{-1}=G^{n}NV^{-1}
is isomorphic to
G^{n}N
. If the main diagonal of
N
contains
s
1’s, followed by
a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{t}
(with
a_{1}\mid a_{2}\mid\cdots\mid a_{t}
) and then
u
0’s (where
u=\min(m,n)-s-t
), then we have

\displaystyle G^{M}\cong G^{s}\oplus\bigoplus_{i=1}^{t}a_{i}G\qquad\text{ and }

(2)

\displaystyle G^{n}/G^{M}\cong\bigoplus_{i=1}^{t}G/a_{i}G\oplus G^{u}

(3)

Thus, below we will content ourselves with finding the
s+t
nonzero elementary divisors of the matrix
M
and allow the reader to use Equations (
2
) and (
3
) to describe
G^{S}
. See
[
3
]
, Theorem 5 for one explicit description of the tuples in
G^{S}
when
G
is finite.

4.2
Description of
\mathbb{Z}^{\Gamma}
for some basic graphs

For the remainder of this section, we will work with the special case
M=A_{\Gamma}
for some graph
\Gamma
and determine the elementary divisors for the activation matrices corresponding to some well-known families of graphs. From there, Equations (
2
) and (
3
) can be used to describe
G^{\Gamma}
for every abelian group
G
.

We will prove the following results by finding a convenient basis for
G^{\Gamma}
that essentially starts with rows with a leading 1, meaning we can set the value of the corresponding vertex to anything we like, regardless of the values of the vertices that have come before. We will then show the rest of the vertices are completely fixed (meaning their corresponding row can be removed from
A_{\Gamma}
without any loss since that row can be expressed as a linear combination of previous rows) or can be changed by a multiple of some number
x
(meaning
x
is the leading entry in the row).

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
\Gamma=P_{n}
, the path graph with
n
vertices. If
n\equiv 2
(mod
3
), then the elementary divisors of
\Gamma
are
(1^{n-1},0)
, and otherwise they are
(1^{n})
so that
\mathbb{Z}^{\Gamma}=\mathbb{Z}^{n}
.

Proof.

It is always possible to set the first
n-1
coordinates to be anything we wish (say
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n-1},*)
); first click
v_{2}
with
a_{1}
, and then proceed to the right, always clicking
v_{i}
with whatever we need to set the
(i-1)
-st coordinate to
a_{i-1}
. The only question that remains is how much freedom we have to set the last coordinate. Let
\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n}
be the rows of
A_{\Gamma}
. If
n\equiv 0
(mod
3
), then

\mathbf{e}_{n}=(\mathbf{x}_{2}+\mathbf{x}_{5}+\cdots+\mathbf{x}_{n-1})-(\mathbf{x}_{1}+\mathbf{x}_{4}+\cdots+\mathbf{x}_{n-2}),

and so we can also set the last coordinate arbitrarily. Similarly, if
n\equiv 1
(mod
3
), then

\mathbf{e}_{n}=(\mathbf{x}_{1}+\mathbf{x}_{4}+\cdots+\mathbf{x}_{n})-(\mathbf{x}_{2}+\mathbf{x}_{5}+\cdots+\mathbf{x}_{n-2}),

and so we can also set the last coordinate arbitrarily in that case. Thus, if
n\not\equiv 2
(mod
3
), then
\mathbb{Z}^{\Gamma}=\mathbb{Z}^{n}
.

Now suppose that
n\equiv 2
(mod
3
). Then

\mathbf{x}_{3n+2}=(\mathbf{x}_{1}+\mathbf{x}_{4}+\cdots+\mathbf{x}_{3n+1})-(\mathbf{x}_{2}+\mathbf{x}_{5}+\cdots+\mathbf{x}_{3n-1}),

which means the last row of an echelon form of
M
must be zero, hence so must the last elementary divisor.
∎

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
\Gamma=C_{n}
, the cycle graph with
n
vertices. If
n
is divisible by
3
, then the elementary divisors are
(1^{n-2},0^{2})
, and otherwise they are
(1^{n-1},3)
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{x}_{i}=\mathbf{e}_{i-1}+\mathbf{e}_{i}+\mathbf{e}_{i+1}
with indices mod
n
. We can set the first
n-2
coordinates to be anything, say
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n-2}
: click
v_{2}
with
a_{1}
, and work our way to the right, clicking
v_{i+1}
with whatever we need to make the
i
th coordinate equal to
a_{i}
. This shows that the first
n-2
elementary divisors are 1. The question that remains is which vectors of the form
(0,\ldots,0,*,*)
we can obtain.

First, suppose that
n=3k
. Then

\mathbf{x}_{3n}=(\mathbf{x}_{1}+\mathbf{x}_{4}+\cdots+\mathbf{x}_{3k-2})-(\mathbf{x}_{3}+\mathbf{x}_{6}+\cdots+\mathbf{x}_{3k-3})

and

\mathbf{x}_{3n-1}=(\mathbf{x}_{1}+\mathbf{x}_{4}+\cdots+\mathbf{x}_{3k-2})-(\mathbf{x}_{2}+\mathbf{x}_{5}+\cdots+\mathbf{x}_{3k-4}).

This implies the last two rows of
H_{\Gamma}
are zero.

Now suppose that
n
is not divisible by 3. If
n=3k+2
, then

\displaystyle(\mathbf{x}_{1}+\mathbf{x}_{4}+\cdots+\mathbf{x}_{3k+1})

\displaystyle-(\mathbf{x}_{2}+\mathbf{x}_{5}+\cdots+\mathbf{x}_{3k-1})

\displaystyle=(0,0,\ldots,0,1,*).

Similarly, if
n=3k+1
, then

\displaystyle-(\mathbf{x}_{1}+\mathbf{x}_{4}+\cdots+\mathbf{x}_{3k-2})

\displaystyle+(\mathbf{x}_{2}+\mathbf{x}_{5}+\cdots+\mathbf{x}_{3k-1})

\displaystyle=(0,0,\ldots,0,1,*).

This gives us an additional elementary divisor of 1.

It remains to find the last elementary divisor. Note that

\sum\mathbf{x}_{i}=(3,3,\ldots,3),

and since
\mathbf{e}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{e}_{n-2}
are in
\mathbb{Z}^{\Gamma}
, it follows that
3\mathbf{e}_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{\Gamma}
. On the other hand, since every row has sum 3, Proposition
2.1
implies that the last elementary divisor is divisible by 3. It follows that the last elementary divisor is precisely 3.
∎

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
\Gamma=K_{m,n}
, the complete bipartite graph. Then the elementary divisors of
\Gamma
are
(1^{m+n-1},mn-1)
.

Proof.

Let us label the vertices in one partition
u_{1},\ldots,u_{m}
and in the other
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}
. First, let us show that if we drop
v_{n}
, we can get any combination of integers in the remaining coordinates. Suppose we want to end up with
x_{i}
in each
u_{i}
and
y_{j}
in each
v_{j}
except for
v_{n}
. Let
x=\sum x_{i}
. If we click each
u_{i}
with
x_{i}
, click
v_{j}
with
-x+y_{j}
for
j\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}
, and then click
v_{n}
with
(n-1)x-(y_{1}+\cdots+y_{n-1})
, then we accomplish our goal.

Before proceeding, let us prove a lemma. Suppose
v_{i}
and
v_{j}
in
\Gamma
have the same neighbors. If
a_{1}\mathbf{x}_{1}+\cdots+a_{n}\mathbf{x}_{n}
has coordinates
i
and
j
equal, then
a_{i}=a_{j}
. To see this, let
\mathbf{v}=a_{1}\mathbf{x}_{1}+\cdots+a_{n}\mathbf{x}_{n}
and let
s=\sum a_{k}
, over those
k
such that
v_{k}
is a neighbor of
v_{i}
. Then the value of coordinate
i
in
\mathbf{v}
is
s+a_{i}
, and it is
s+a_{j}
in coordinate
j
, and the lemma follows.

Now consider the elements of
\mathbb{Z}^{\Gamma}
that have a 0 in every coordinate other than
v_{n}
. By the lemma above, the vertices
u_{i}
are all clicked with the same element, say
a
, and the vertices
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n-1}
are all clicked with the same element, say
b
. Let
c
be the element that we click
v_{n}
with. Now, considering that we want the state of
v_{1}
to be
0
means that
b+ma=0
, and so
b=-ma
. Then, considering that we want the state of
u_{1}
to be
0
means that
a+(n-1)b+c=0
. Thus,

a+(n-1)(-ma)+c=0,

and so
c=a((n-1)m-1)
. Then the state of vertex

is

am+c=am+a((n-1)m-1)=(mn-1)a.

Since
a
was arbitrary to begin with, we have described a basis for
G^{\Gamma}
consisting of rows of the form
(0,\dots,0,1,*,\dots,*)
and
(0,\dots,0,mn-1)
. Thus we see that the last elementary divisor is
(mn-1)
.
∎

Let

are
(1^{n},n-1)

5
Reducing nonabelian graph powers to the abelian case

5.1
Definitions

Having seen how to determine the structure of
G^{\Gamma}
when
G
is abelian, we now turn to the case of nonabelian groups. We will exploit Proposition
3.12
(a), which implies that there is an epimorphism from
G^{\Gamma}
to
(G^{\textrm{Ab}})^{\Gamma}
with kernel
[G,G]^{|\Gamma|}\cap G^{\Gamma}
. If we can determine
[G,G]^{|\Gamma|}\cap G^{\Gamma}
, then combined with the results from the previous section, this will give us a description of
G^{\Gamma}
. We will define things in the general context of
G^{S}
, though the fact that the rows of
A_{\Gamma}
consist only of
0
s and
1
s is an essential component of much of our analysis.

Definition 5.1
.

If
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, then the
coordinate-wise commutator subgroup

\text{Comm}(G,S)
is
[G,G]^{n}\cap G^{S}
. In other words, it is those elements of
G^{S}
with an element of
[G,G]
in each coordinate. If
\Gamma
is a graph,
\text{Comm}(G,\Gamma)
is
[G,G]^{|\Gamma|}\cap G^{\Gamma}
.

We will see that for a wide-ranging class of graphs,
[G,G]^{|\Gamma|}\leq G^{\Gamma}
(so that
\text{Comm}(G,\Gamma)=[G,G]^{|\Gamma|}
), which makes the description of
G^{\Gamma}
depend in a straightforward way on
(G^{\textrm{Ab}})^{\Gamma}
. We thus make the following definition.

Definition 5.2
.

The graph
\Gamma
is
G
-RA
(short for
reducible to abelian
) if
[G,G]^{|\Gamma|}\leq G^{\Gamma}
.
If
\Gamma
is
G
-RA for every group
G
, then we simply say that
\Gamma
is RA.

Example 5.3
.

It is well-known that the commutator subgroup of the Rubik’s Cube group
G
has index 2 (
[G,G]
is just the subgroup of even permutations of the faces of the cubies), which implies the following.

Consider again the “Rubik’s Cube game” on
\Gamma
, where each vertex has a Rubik’s Cube attached and a move at one vertex affects the cube at that vertex and those at the adjacent vertices. Let
G
be the Rubik’s Cube group, and suppose that
\Gamma
is
G
-RA.

For each Rubik’s cube at every vertex, we can determine whether the starting position is in
[G,G]
or not by determining if it is an odd or even permutation from the solved state. Then we can write down the state as a 1 if odd or 0 if even. The overall starting position of the Rubik’s Cubes will be solvable if and only if the corresponding Lights Out Game on the graph has a solution (that is, if you can get back to the all-0 state). This is because a solution exists if and only if we can perform moves on the Rubik’s cubes to make each state reduce to
\bar{0}
in the abelianization
G/[G,G]
in each coordinate. We know we can act on each coordinate by any element of
[G,G]
independently, so we can get to the solved state independently for each cube.

As a corollary, every initial state is solvable if and only if every initial state of the Lights Out game on
\Gamma
is solvable.

In general, determining
\text{Comm}(G,S)
directly is difficult. We will make use of several subgroups of
[G,G]^{n}
. First, note that since
(G^{\textrm{Ab}})^{S}
is abelian, that implies that
[G^{S},G^{S}]
is contained in
\text{Comm}(G,S)
. Further, let us define two subgroups of
[G^{S},G^{S}]
.

Definition 5.4
.

The
basic commutator subgroup

\text{Comm}_{b}(G,S)
is the subgroup of
[G^{S},G^{S}]
generated by
[g^{\mathbf{x}},h^{\mathbf{y}}]
for any
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in S
and elements
g,h\in G
. Similarly, if
\Gamma
is a graph, then
\text{Comm}_{b}(G,\Gamma)
is the subgroup of
[G^{\Gamma},G^{\Gamma}]
generated by
[g^{u},h^{v}]
for any vertices
u,v\in\Gamma
and elements
g,h\in G
.

The
distinct basic commutator subgroup

\text{Comm}_{d}(G,S)
is the subgroup of
[G^{S},G^{S}]
generated by
[g^{\mathbf{x}},h^{\mathbf{y}}]
for any
g,h\in G
and
distinct
vectors
\mathbf{x}\neq\mathbf{y}\in S
. Similarly,
\text{Comm}_{d}(G,\Gamma)
is the subgroup of
[G^{\Gamma},G^{\Gamma}]
generated by
[g^{u},h^{v}]
for any
g,h\in G
and
distinct
vertices
u\neq v\in\Gamma
.

Thus, in general we have

\displaystyle\text{Comm}_{d}(G,S)

\displaystyle\leq\text{Comm}_{b}(G,S)\leq[G^{S},G^{S}]

(4)

\displaystyle\leq\text{Comm}(G,S)\leq[G,G]^{n}

Note that
[G^{S},G^{S}]
is the normal closure of
\text{Comm}_{b}(G,S)
in
G^{S}
, and since
[G,G]^{S}
is the subgroup of
G^{S}
generated by
[g^{\mathbf{x}},h^{\mathbf{y}}]
, we have
\text{Comm}_{b}(G,S)=\langle\text{Comm}_{d}(G,S),[G,G]^{S}\rangle
.

For each of the containments in (
4
), there are examples that show that the containment may be proper. Proposition
5.19
below shows that for even-dimensional cubes
\Gamma=Q_{d}
,
\text{Comm}_{d}(D_{8},\Gamma)
does not equal
\text{Comm}_{b}(D_{8},\Gamma)
. The examples in
Section

6.2
show
\text{Comm}_{b}(G,S)
may not equal
[G^{S},G^{S}]
and
[G^{S},G^{S}]
may not equal
[G,G]^{|S|}\cap G^{S}
. Furthermore, in a future paper we will show that the crown graph on
2p+4
vertices (i.e. the tensor product
K_{2}\times K_{p+2}
) is an example of a graph where
[G^{\Gamma},G^{\Gamma}]\neq\text{Comm}(G,\Gamma)
for
G=H(\mathbb{F}_{p})
(see
[
1
]
). Finally, as we will show in Corollary
6.14
, the cube graphs
Q_{2n+1}
of odd dimension are not RA.

For the rest of this section, we will shift to working with
G^{\Gamma}
only. We will show that many graphs have the property that for every
G
, the group
\text{Comm}_{d}(G,\Gamma)
or
\text{Comm}_{b}(G,\Gamma)
is equal to all of
[G,G]^{|\Gamma|}
.
In particular, when
\text{Comm}_{d}(G,\Gamma)=[G,G]^{|\Gamma|}
, the groups in (
4
) all coincide and
\Gamma
is RA.
The group
\text{Comm}_{d}(G,\Gamma)
is also instrumental in analyzing
G^{\Gamma}
when
\Gamma
is a product of graphs (see
[
1
]
), so we make the following definition.

Definition 5.5
.

The graph
\Gamma
is
strongly RA
if, for every group
G
,

\text{Comm}_{d}(G,\Gamma)=[G,G]^{|\Gamma|}
. In particular, if
\Gamma
is strongly RA, then it is RA.

In order to understand the groups
\text{Comm}_{d}(G,\Gamma)
and
\text{Comm}_{b}(G,\Gamma)
, let us analyze the commutators
[g^{u},h^{v}]
.

Proposition 5.6
.

Let
u,v\in\Gamma
, and let
\mathbf{x}
be the indicator vector for
B(u)\cap B(v)
. Then
[g^{u},h^{v}]=[g,h]^{\mathbf{x}}
.

Proof.

It suffices to consider the effect that
[g^{u},h^{v}]
has on three different kinds of vertices:

1.

If
w
is not adjacent to
u
or
v
, then both
g^{u}
and
h^{v}
leave it unchanged.

2.

If
w
is adjacent to
u
but not
v
, then
w
is affected only by
g^{u}
and
(g^{-1})^{u}
, and the net effect is that it is unchanged. Similarly, if
w
is adjacent to
v
but not
u
, it ends up unchanged.

3.

If
w
is adjacent to
u
and
v
, then the state of
w
is multiplied by
[g,h]
.

Thus
[g^{u},h^{v}]
precisely gives
[g,h]
at the vertices in
B(u)\cap B(v)
.
∎

Let us consider these subgroups in a simple graph and show how it can help establish that a graph is RA.

Example 5.7
.

Let
\Gamma
be the cycle graph
C_{4}
. Label the vertices cyclically
v_{1},\ldots,v_{4}
. Then

[g^{v_{1}},h^{v_{2}}]=([g,h],[g,h],1,1),

[g^{v_{1}},h^{v_{3}}]=(1,[g,h],1,[g,h]).

The group
\text{Comm}_{d}(G,C_{4})
is generated by elements like these; namely, elements of
G^{C_{4}}
that have the same commutator on any pair of vertices. If
G=D_{8}
, then there is only a single nontrivial commutator
c
, and it has order
2
. It follows that
\text{Comm}_{d}(D_{8},C_{4})
is the subgroup of
\langle c\rangle^{4}
that has
c
on an even number of coordinates, a group of order 8.

In the group
\text{Comm}_{b}(G,C_{4})
, we are additionally allowed to use

[g^{v_{1}},h^{v_{1}}]=([g,h],[g,h],1,[g,h]).

Then it follows that
\text{Comm}_{b}(G,C_{4})
contains

[g^{v_{1}},h^{v_{1}}][h^{v_{1}},g^{v_{3}}]=([g,h],1,1,1).

Similar arguments show that
[g,h]^{\mathbf{e}_{i}}\in G^{C_{4}}
for all
i
. It follows that
\text{Comm}_{b}(G,C_{4})=[G,G]^{4}
and so
C_{4}
is RA (but not strongly RA).

In some graphs, the relationship between
[G,G]^{|\Gamma|}
and
G^{\Gamma}
depends on
G
:

Example 5.8
.

Let
\Gamma
be the graph of a cube. Using GAP, we find that if
G
is dihedral of order
10
, then indeed
[G,G]^{8}\leq G^{\Gamma}
. On the other hand, if
G
is dihedral of order
8
, then
\text{Comm}(G,\Gamma)
has index
2
in

. See Figure
2
in
Appendix B.

More generally, suppose that the vertex
v_{1}
in the cube has neighbors
v_{2}
,
v_{3}
, and
v_{4}
. Then:

[h^{v_{1}},g^{v_{1}}]\cdot[g^{v_{1}},h^{v_{2}}]\cdot[g^{v_{1}},h^{v_{3}}]\cdot[g^{v_{1}},h^{v_{4}}]=([g,h]^{2})^{e_{1}}.

Similar computations show that for any commutator
c
and any

, we have
c^{2\mathbf{e}_{i}}\in[G^{\Gamma},G^{\Gamma}]
. Thus, if
[G,G]
is generated by the squares of commutators, then

is contained in

.

5.2
Graph families that are RA

For a fixed group
G
and graph
\Gamma
, there are computational methods to help determine whether
\Gamma
is
G
-RA. We defer these methods to

6
. For now, our goal is to find ways to show that a graph

(i.e. with
\text{Comm}_{b}(G,\Gamma)

\text{Comm}_{d}(G,\Gamma)

The first result follows directly from Proposition
5.6
.

Proposition 5.9
.

If
u
and
v
are adjacent vertices with no neighbors in common, then
[g,h]^{\mathbf{e}_{u}+\mathbf{e}_{v}}\in\text{Comm}_{d}(G,\Gamma)
.

Lemma 5.10
.

If
u
is a vertex of degree two, with its neighbors
v
and
w
not connected by an edge, then
[g,h]^{\mathbf{x}}\in\text{Comm}_{b}(G,\Gamma)
for
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{e}_{u},\mathbf{e}_{v},\mathbf{e}_{w}
and any
g,h\in G
.

Proof.

By Proposition
5.9
,
[g,h]^{\mathbf{e}_{u}+\mathbf{e}_{v}}\in\text{Comm}_{d}(G,\Gamma)
. Multiplying by
[h,g]^{u}
gives us
[h,g]^{\mathbf{e}_{w}}\in\text{Comm}_{b}(G,\Gamma)
. By a similar argument,
[h,g]^{\mathbf{e}_{v}}\in\text{Comm}_{b}(G,\Gamma)
, and multiplying
[h,g]^{\mathbf{e}_{v}}[h,g]^{\mathbf{e}_{w}}
by
[g,h]^{u}
implies that
[g,h]^{\mathbf{e}_{u}}\in\text{Comm}_{b}(G,\Gamma)
, proving the result.
∎

Lemma 5.11
.

If there exists a vertex
u\in\Gamma
with neighbors
v
and
w
that have no common neighbors other than
u
, then
[g,h]^{\mathbf{e}_{u}}\in\text{Comm}_{d}(G,\Gamma)
for every
g,h\in G
.

Proof.

Under these assumptions,
[g^{v},h^{w}]=[g,h]^{\mathbf{e}_{u}}
.
∎

Having established several ‘local’ results, we now have the tools we need to prove some ‘global’ results.

Lemma 5.12
.

If
\Gamma
is a connected triangle-free graph and there exists a vertex
v
such that
[g,h]^{\mathbf{e}_{v}}
is in
\text{Comm}_{d}(G,\Gamma)
(resp.
\text{Comm}_{b}(G,\Gamma),[G^{\Gamma},G^{\Gamma}]
) then
[G,G]^{|\Gamma|}
equals
\text{Comm}_{d}(G,\Gamma)
(resp.
\text{Comm}_{b}(G,\Gamma),[G^{\Gamma},G^{\Gamma}]
) . In any case,
\Gamma
is RA. That is, if we can put any commutator in a single vertex, then we can put any commutator by itself anywhere.

Proof.

Suppose
[g,h]^{\mathbf{e}_{v}}\in G^{\Gamma}
. The fact that the graph is triangle-free implies than any neighbor
w
of
v
shares no neighbors with
v
, so by Proposition
5.9
, we can multiply by
[h,g]^{\mathbf{e}_{v}+\mathbf{e}_{w}}
, and so
[h,g]^{\mathbf{e}_{w}}\in G^{\Gamma}
and thus
[g,h]^{\mathbf{e}_{w}}\in G^{\Gamma}
. Similarly, we can extend the reasoning to neighbors of neighbors and so on, and since
\Gamma
is connected, it follows by induction that
[g,h]^{\mathbf{e}_{u}}\in G^{\Gamma}
for every vertex
u
. Furthermore, since we obtained
[g,h]^{\mathbf{e}_{u}}
by multiplying
[g,h]^{\mathbf{e}_{v}}
by elements of
\text{Comm}_{d}(G,\Gamma)
, it follows that
[g,h]^{\mathbf{e}_{u}}
is in
\text{Comm}_{d}(G,\Gamma)
(resp.
\text{Comm}_{b}(G,\Gamma)
) if
[g,h]^{\mathbf{e}_{v}}
is in
\text{Comm}_{d}(G,\Gamma)
(resp.
\text{Comm}_{b}(G,\Gamma)
).
∎

Recall that the
girth
of a graph is the length of its smallest cycle.

Corollary 5.13
.

If
\Gamma
is a connected graph with girth
5
or more and at least
3
vertices, then
\Gamma
is strongly RA.

Proof.

Let
\Gamma
be a graph satisfying the hypotheses. Then there is some vertex
u
with degree 2 or more. Consider neighbors
v
and
w
of
u
. Since
\Gamma
has girth at least 5,
v
and
w
are not adjacent, and they have no neighbors in common other than

. Thus
[g,h]^{\mathbf{e}_{u}}
is in
\text{Comm}_{d}(G,\Gamma)
by Lemma

, and Lemma
5.12
completes the proof.
∎

Recall for a moment that we restrict ourselves to connected graphs that are neighborhood-distinguishable; that is, no vertices
u
and
v
have the same closed neighborhood. Note that if
|\Gamma|\geq 3
and

and

have the same closed neighborhood, then they have a common neighbor

Let us now use Corollary
5.13
and highlight a few graphs of interest.

Corollary 5.14
.

The following graphs are all strongly RA:

1.

The Petersen graph

2.

C_{n}
with
n\geq 5

3.

Trees with at least
3
vertices.

Having fully determined
\text{Comm}(G,\Gamma)
for graphs
\Gamma
of girth at least 5, we next consider graphs of girth 4. In light of Lemma

, it seems likely that many graphs of girth 4 will be RA, but Example

proves that not every graph of girth 4 is RA. We will later describe a family of graphs of girth 4 that are not RA. For now, let us use Lemma

to help describe what a non-RA graph of girth 4 would have to look like.

Definition 5.15
.

We say that the graph
\Gamma
satisfies the

if every path with three vertices can be completed to a 4-cycle.

Proposition 5.16
.

Suppose that
\Gamma
is a connected graph with girth
4
. If
\Gamma
has at least one vertex of degree
2
, then
\Gamma
is RA. If
\Gamma
does not satisfy the square completion property, or if
\Gamma
has at least one vertex of degree

, then
\Gamma
is strongly RA.

Proof.

If
\Gamma
has a vertex of degree
2
, then Lemma
5.10
and Lemma
5.12
imply that
\text{Comm}_{b}(G,\Gamma)=[G,G]^{|\Gamma|}
, and so
\Gamma
is RA.

If
\Gamma
does not satisfy the square completion property, then there is a path with three vertices
u,v,w
that cannot be completed to a
4
-cycle, which implies that

is the only common neighbor of
u
and

. Then
\text{Comm}_{d}(G,\Gamma)=[G,G]^{|\Gamma|}
by Lemma
5.11
and Lemma

, so
\Gamma
is strongly RA. In particular, if
\Gamma
has a vertex of degree 1, then it cannot satisfy the square completion property and thus is strongly RA.
∎

Example 5.17
.

Cube graphs
Q_{d}
with

have the square completion property, as do complete bipartite graphs

. An

Let us now examine some consequences of Proposition

for some families of graphs of girth 4.

Theorem 5.18
.

1.

C_{4}
is RA but not strongly RA.

2.

An

3.

The complete bipartite graph

is RA, and is strongly RA if and only if

and

are relatively prime.

Proof.

(a) This follows from Example
5.7
.

(b) This follows from Proposition
5.16
and Example
5.17
.

(c) If
u
and
v
are vertices in the same set of the bipartition of
K_{m,n}
, then

[h^{u},g^{v}][g^{u},h^{u}]=[g,h]^{\mathbf{e}_{u}},

and then Lemma
5.12
shows that

is RA. Note that since
K_{m,n}
satisfies the square completion property, Proposition
5.16
does not apply. Now, to show that
K_{m,n}
is strongly RA, we have to only consider the effect of commutators
[g^{u},h^{v}]
with
u\neq v
. Let us assume without loss of generality that
m\leq n
. Let the bipartition of
K_{m,n}
consist of sets
A
and
B
with
|A|=m
and
|B|=n
. Then if
u
and
v
are distinct vertices of
A
(resp.
B
), the element
[g^{u},h^{v}]
consists of
[g,h]
on every element of
A
(resp.
B
). If

and

, then
[g^{u},h^{v}]
consists of
[g,h]
on

and

only.

Suppose that

and

have a nontrivial common factor

, and let
G=D_{8}=\langle x,y\mid x^{2}=y^{2}=(xy)^{4}=1\rangle
.
Then the only nontrivial commutator is

. Since
[x,y]
has order 2, we find that any of the generating moves preserve the relation that the number of vertices of

that has state
[x,y]
is congruent modulo

to the number of vertices of
B
with state
[x,y]
. In particular, we cannot have
[x,y]
on just a single vertex. This shows that

is not strongly RA in this case.

Now suppose that
m
and
n
are relatively prime, so that
m<n
. Fix elements

and let

. Then we can put

on

vertices of

by first putting it on every vertex, (using

with a

and

in

, and the repeating for a

and

in

), and then picking

to multiply by

. If

on every vertex of

on

. Now we repeat the process with the roles of

.
∎

We have seen that the cube

Proposition 5.19
.

The cube graph
\Gamma=Q_{d}

Proof.

To show that

is not strongly RA, consider

, and let

be the single nontrivial commutator. If

, then

(either corresponding to

and

Now let

v_{0}=00\cdots 0
, and for

, let

be the neighbor of

with a

in the

th coordinate. Then for each

,

and

v_{0}
and another vertex

. Then

only at

and

. Thus,

[g^{v_{0}},h^{u_{1}}]\cdots[g^{v_{0}},h^{u_{d/2}}]\cdot[h^{v_{0}},g^{v_{0}}]

.

Theorem 5.20
.

The following graphs are RA:

The wheel graph

,

2.

The graph with vertices labeled

(a) Label the central vertex of

For any RA graph – including all of the graphs in Theorem

, Proposition

, and Theorem

– we can understand
G^{\Gamma}
just by understanding
(G^{\textrm{Ab}})^{\Gamma}
. So far, we have only encountered one graph that is not RA (namely,

6
Computing whether a graph is RA

6.1
The RA Matrix

How can we determine whether a graph
\Gamma
is RA?
By Proposition
3.19
, we can first make sure we’re working with a graph that is connected and neighborhood-distinguishable. Then, we might compute the girth and try to apply Corollary
5.13
or Proposition
5.16
. This leaves out graphs with 3-cycles and certain graphs of girth 4. What techniques remain to determine whether such a graph is RA?

If we can show that elements of the form
[g^{u},h^{v}]\in[G^{\Gamma},G^{\Gamma}]
actually generate all of
[G,G]^{|\Gamma|}
, i.e. if
\text{Comm}_{b}(G,\Gamma)=[G,G]^{|\Gamma|}
, for every
G
, then we know
\Gamma
is RA. To do so, we need to consider the set of indicator vectors for
B(u)\cap B(v)
. Just as in the proof of Proposition
3.10
, if such vectors span
\mathbb{Z}^{|\Gamma|}
then the elements
[g^{u},h^{v}]
would generate all of
[G,G]^{|\Gamma|}
. This gives us a new practical tool to help test whether a graph is RA.

Definition 6.1
.

The
RA Matrix

C_{\Gamma}
of
\Gamma
is the matrix whose rows are the indicator vectors for
B(v_{i})\cap B(v_{j})
where
v_{i}
and
v_{j}
range over all the vertices of
\Gamma
.

The above discussion gives us

Theorem 6.2
.

If
\mathbb{Z}^{C_{\Gamma}}=\mathbb{Z}^{|\Gamma|}
, i.e. if the
\mathbb{Z}
-span of
C_{\Gamma}
is all of
\mathbb{Z}^{|\Gamma|}
, then
\Gamma
is RA.

Note that, if
\mathbb{Z}^{C_{\Gamma}}\neq\mathbb{Z}^{|\Gamma|}
, then that only establishes that for some groups
G
we may have
\text{Comm}_{b}(G,\Gamma)\neq[G,G]^{|\Gamma|}
. It could still be the case that
\Gamma
is RA. For now though, we will only use Theorem
6.2
to verify certain graphs as RA and to find promising candidates of non-RA graphs. Let us start with some computational data.

Proposition 6.3
.

Every (connected, neighborhood-distinguishable) graph on up to
7
vertices is RA.

Proof.

Using the graph database in SageMath, we found that
\mathbb{Z}^{C_{\Gamma}}=\mathbb{Z}^{|\Gamma|}
for all eligible graphs with at most 7 vertices. (See Figure
3
in
Appendix B.
)
∎

The bound on Proposition
6.3
is as sharp as possible, since we already determined that the cube graph
Q_{3}
(with 8 vertices) is not RA (see Example
5.8
).

If we are searching for graphs that are not RA, then we can restrict our attention to graphs whose RA Matrix has nontrivial elementary divisors. Sometimes, the structure of the graph makes it clear that this will occur.

Proposition 6.4
.

Let
p
be a prime number. Suppose that every vertex of
\Gamma
has degree congruent to
-1\pmod{p}
, that every pair of adjacent vertices of
\Gamma
has a number of common neighbors that is
-2\pmod{p}
, and that every pair of vertices of
\Gamma
that are distance 2 apart have a number of common neighbors that is divisible by
p
. Then
C_{\Gamma}
has an elementary divisor that is a multiple of
p
.

Proof.

There are three types of rows of
C_{\Gamma}
. The rows that correspond to
neighborhoods of single vertices have row sum
d+1
for vertices of degree
d
. The rows corresponding to
B(u)\cap B(v)
where
u
and
v
are adjacent have row sum
q+2
if they have
q
common neighbors. The rows corresponding to
B(u)\cap B(v)
where
u
and
v
are distance two apart have row sum
r
equal to how many common neighbors they have. The given conditions ensure that each of these sums is divisible by
p
, which ensures that the matrix has an elementary divisor that is divisible by
p
(see Proposition
2.1
).
∎

6.2
The sufficiency of the RA Matrix

After Theorem
6.2
, we remarked that we had not established the converse. Certainly, there are some groups
G
where the RA matrix does not tell us the entire story about
\text{Comm}(G,\Gamma)
when
\Gamma
is not
G
-RA. However, we will see that the RA matrix does suffice to tell us whether a group is
G
-RA for every Heisenberg group, and this will be enough to determine whether a graph is RA over every group or not.

Let us start by considering a more general and somewhat more difficult question. Recall the chain of subgroups described in (
4
). Let us focus on the containment
[G^{\Gamma},G^{\Gamma}]\leq\text{Comm}(G,\Gamma)
. Our question is: could it be that these groups are always equal?

First, consider some examples where we replace
\Gamma
with a matrix
M
and ask the analogous question.

Example 6.5
.

Let
M=[2]
and
G=D_{8}=\langle r,s\mid r^{4}=s^{2}=1,srs=r^{-1}\rangle
. Then
r^{2}
is the only nontrivial commutator in
D_{8}
and clicking with
r
gives us
(r^{2})\in G^{M}
; indeed,
G^{M}=\{(r^{2}),(1)\}
since clicking with any element gives the square of that element and the only squares are
r^{2}
and
1
, so
[G^{M},G^{M}]=1\subsetneqq[G,G]^{1}\cap G^{M}
.

Example 6.6
.

Let

M=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
1&-1\\
\end{pmatrix}.

Then we can explicitly compute that
[D_{8}^{M},D_{8}^{M}]=\{(r^{2},r^{2}),(1,1)\}
but
r^{v_{1}}r^{v_{2}}=(r^{2},1)\in D_{8}^{M}
so again
[G^{M},G^{M}]\subsetneqq[G,G]^{2}\cap G^{M}
.

Thus, using matrices with anything other than 0’s and 1’s is liable to produce examples like the above with
[G^{M},G^{M}]\subsetneqq[G,G]^{n}\cap G^{M}
. We have already seen a small hint that matrices with only 0’s and 1’s may act in a special way; consider Proposition
3.2
(c). In our current context, the point is that
[g^{v},h^{v}]=[g,h]^{v}
, whereas this equation need not hold if we replace
v
with a general vector
\mathbf{x}
.

We will broaden our scope slightly though, to allow any set of
\{0,1\}
-vectors.
In order to tease out the difference between
[G^{S},G^{S}]
and
\text{Comm}(G,S)
, note that working modulo
[G^{S},G^{S}]
essentially lets you rearrange your clicks. For example,

g_{1}^{\mathbf{x}_{1}}g_{2}^{\mathbf{x}_{2}}[G^{S},G^{S}]=g_{2}^{\mathbf{x}_{2}}g_{1}^{\mathbf{x}_{1}}[G^{S},G^{S}].

Let us make a slightly technical definition.

Definition 6.7
.

We say
G
has
faithful abelian generators
if
G^{\textrm{Ab}}\cong\bigoplus_{\alpha=1}^{k}\mathbb{Z}/r_{\alpha}\mathbb{Z}
and for each
\alpha
there is a representative
y_{\alpha}
for
\overline{\bf e_{\alpha}}\in G^{\textrm{Ab}}
so that the order of
y_{\alpha}
in
G
equals
r_{\alpha}
.

Note that by definition, for each
\alpha
there always exists a representative
y
for
\overline{\bf e_{\alpha}}
such that
y^{\alpha}
is in
[G,G]
; what we need is for
y^{\alpha}
to actually be the identity element of
G
. Not every group
G
has faithful abelian generators, and in fact the requirements of the next theorem can be lessened somewhat
[
1
]
, but many of the common groups do have faithful abelian generators; symmetric, dihedral, and Heisenberg groups are only a few examples.

Theorem 6.8
.

Let
S\subset\{0,1\}^{n}
. Let
G
be any group with faithful abelian generators. Then
[G^{S},G^{S}]=\text{Comm}(G,S)
.

Proof.

By definition
[G^{S},G^{S}]\leq\text{Comm}(G,S)
, so we just need to show the opposite containment. Since
[G^{S},G^{S}]\lhd G^{S}
, we will take a coset of
[G^{S},G^{S}]
represented by an element of
\text{Comm}(G,S)=[G,G]^{n}\cap G^{S}
. If
S=\{\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{s}\}
, such an element is a product of clicks
g_{i}^{\mathbf{x}_{i}}
, and modulo
[G^{S},G^{S}]
, we can rearrange the order of such clicks, so a new equivalent representative can be written as

\displaystyle g_{11}^{\mathbf{x}_{1}}g_{12}^{\mathbf{x}_{1}}\cdots g_{1,t_{1}}^{\mathbf{x}_{1}}\cdots g_{s,1}^{\mathbf{x}_{s}}g_{s,2}^{\mathbf{x}_{s}}\cdots g_{s,t_{s}}^{\mathbf{x}_{s}}

\displaystyle=(g_{11}g_{12}\cdots g_{1,t_{1}})^{\mathbf{x}_{1}}\cdots(g_{s,1}g_{s,2}\cdots g_{s,t_{s}})^{\mathbf{x}_{s}}

(using the fact that each vector
\mathbf{x}_{i}
has only 0’s and 1’s in it; see Proposition
3.2
).
Let
g_{i}=g_{i1}g_{i2}\cdots g_{i,t_{i}}
and write the representative of the coset of
[G^{S},G^{S}]
as
g_{1}^{\mathbf{x}_{1}}\cdots g_{s}^{\mathbf{x}_{s}}[G^{S},G^{S}]
. Write
G^{\textrm{Ab}}\cong\bigoplus_{\alpha=1}^{k}\mathbb{Z}/r_{\alpha}\mathbb{Z}
and for each
\alpha
, pick a representative
y_{\alpha}
for
\overline{\bf e_{\alpha}}\in G^{\textrm{Ab}}
so that the order of
y_{\alpha}
in
G
equals
r_{\alpha}
(this is the hypothesis about
G
).
Now, for each
i
, if the image
\overline{g_{i}}
of
g_{i}
in
G^{\textrm{Ab}}
is
(q_{i,1},q_{i,2},\dots,q_{i,k})
then we can write
g_{i}=\left(\prod_{\alpha=1}^{k}y_{\alpha}^{q_{i,\alpha}}\right)c_{i}
for some
c_{i}\in[G,G]
. Since
\prod_{i=1}^{s}g_{i}^{\mathbf{x}_{i}}[G^{S},G^{S}]\in[G,G]^{n}
, for each coordinate
j=1,2,\dots,n
we have
\overline{\prod_{i=1}^{s}g_{i}^{(\mathbf{x}_{i})_{j}}}=\bar{1}\in G/[G,G]
so for any fixed coordinate
j=1,\dots,n
and any fixed summand
\alpha=1,\dots,k
of
G^{\textrm{Ab}}
we have
\prod_{i=1}^{s}y_{\alpha}^{q_{i,\alpha}(\mathbf{x}_{i})_{j}}\in[G,G]
. In other words,
\sum_{i=1}^{s}q_{i,\alpha}(\mathbf{x}_{i})_{j}\equiv 0\pmod{r_{\alpha}}
so
\prod_{i=1}^{s}y_{\alpha}^{q_{i,\alpha}(\mathbf{x}_{i})_{j}}=1\in G
and, putting these together, for each
\alpha
we have
\prod_{i=1}^{s}(y_{\alpha}^{q_{i,\alpha}})^{\mathbf{x}_{i}}=(1)_{j=1}^{n}\in G^{n}
.

But that means, modulo
[G^{S},G^{S}]
, we can rewrite the representative

\prod_{i=1}^{s}\left(\prod_{\alpha=1}^{k}y_{\alpha}^{q_{i,\alpha}}c_{i}\right)^{\mathbf{x}_{i}}

of the coset of
[G^{S},G^{S}]
as

(\prod_{i=1}^{s}(y_{1}^{q_{i,1}})^{\mathbf{x}_{i}})(\prod_{i=1}^{s}(y_{2}^{q_{i,2}})^{\mathbf{x}_{i}})\cdots(\prod_{i=1}^{s}(y_{k}^{q_{i,k}})^{\mathbf{x}_{i}})(\prod_{i=1}^{s}c_{i}^{\mathbf{x}_{i}})

and each of the first
k
products is simply
(1)_{j=1}^{n}
, while the last product is in
[G^{S},G^{S}]
, so the original coset of
[G^{S},G^{S}]
is the identity coset.
∎

Corollary 6.9
.

Let
S\subset\{0,1\}^{n}
. For any symmetric, dihedral, or Heisenberg group
G
, we have
\text{Comm}(G,S)=[G^{S},G^{S}]
.

Proof.

The abelianization of a symmetric group
S_{m}^{\textrm{Ab}}\cong\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
is generated by
\overline{(12)}
. Any dihedral group
G=D_{2k}
has
G^{\textrm{Ab}}\cong\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
, or
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
and in either case we can take the representative of each coordinate to be a flip. The abelianization of any Heisenberg group of order
p^{2n+1}
is a direct sum of
2n
copies of
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
’s, and the group has exponent
p
for
p>2
so any representative of any coset will have order
p
. For
p=2
, we can take the representatives to be elements of order 2 (just let
\vec{a}=\vec{0}
or
\vec{b}=\vec{0}
and set the upper-right element to 1). If
G\cong[G,G]\oplus G^{\textrm{Ab}}
, there is a natural monomorphism from
G^{\textrm{Ab}}
to
[G,G]
under which the generators of
G^{\textrm{Ab}}\cong\mathbb{Z}/r_{1}\mathbb{Z}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathbb{Z}/r_{k}\mathbb{Z}
go to elements of
G
of the same order.
∎

Theorem 6.10
.

1.

If
G
is any Heisenberg group
H(\mathbb{F}_{p})
and
\Gamma
is any graph, then
\text{Comm}(G,\Gamma)=[G^{\Gamma},G^{\Gamma}]=\text{Comm}_{b}(G,\Gamma)=\langle[g^{u},h^{v}]\mid g,h\in G,u,v\in\Gamma\rangle
.

2.

For every prime
p
,
\Gamma
is RA over
H(\mathbb{F}_{p})
if and only if
C_{\Gamma}
has full rank modulo
p
.

3.

If the graph
\Gamma
is RA over every Heisenberg group, then
C_{\Gamma}
has full rank over
\mathbb{Z}
and
\text{Comm}_{b}(G,\Gamma)=[G,G]^{n}
for every group
G
; in particular,
\Gamma
is RA over every group.

Proof.

(1) We have shown the first equality in Corollary
6.9
, and the last is just the definition of
\text{Comm}_{b}(G,\Gamma)
. The middle equality follows from
[G^{\Gamma},G^{\Gamma}]
being the normal closure of
\text{Comm}_{b}(G,\Gamma)
and the fact that the commutator subgroup of every Heisenberg group is central.

(2) Let
G
be a Heisenberg group of order
p^{3}
. Note that
[G,G]^{n}\cong(\mathbb{Z}_{p})^{n}=\langle c\rangle^{n}
for a fixed commutator
c
. Since
[g^{u},h^{v}]=[g,h]^{B(u)\cap B(v)}
and
[g,h]
is a power of
c
,
\text{Comm}_{b}(G,\Gamma)=(\mathbb{Z}_{p})^{C_{\Gamma}}
.

By (1),
\text{Comm}(G,\Gamma)=\text{Comm}_{b}(G,\Gamma)
.
\Gamma
is RA over
G
if and only if
[G,G]^{n}\subseteq\text{Comm}(G,\Gamma)
. That is, if and only if
(\mathbb{Z}_{p})^{n}\subseteq(\mathbb{Z}_{p})^{C_{\Gamma}}
; i.e.
C_{\Gamma}
has full rank modulo
p
.

(3) By part (2), if
\Gamma
is RA over every Heisenberg group, then
C_{\Gamma}
is full rank modulo every prime
p
, so
\mathbb{Z}^{C_{\Gamma}}=\mathbb{Z}^{|\Gamma|}
(no elementary divisors are divisible by any prime, hence are just 1), which implies the result by Theorem
6.2
.
∎

We now can prove our main theorem of this section.

Theorem 6.11
.

\Gamma
is RA if and only if
\mathbb{Z}^{C_{\Gamma}}=\mathbb{Z}^{|\Gamma|}
.

Proof.

Theorem
6.2
proves one direction. For the other, suppose that
\Gamma
is RA. Then it is RA over every Heisenberg group, and Theorem
6.10
proves the claim.
∎

Theorem
6.11
gives a practical way of determining if a graph is RA. The RA matrix
C_{\Gamma}
may have some elementary divisors, but by the formula
\frac{d_{i}(C_{\Gamma})}{d_{i-1}(C_{\Gamma})}
for the
i
-th elementary divisor, since
C_{\Gamma}
contains the rows of the activation matrix
A_{\Gamma}
for
\Gamma
, each
d_{i}(C_{\Gamma})
divides
d_{i}(A_{\Gamma})
, and therefore any primes dividing the elementary divisors of
C_{\Gamma}
must also divide the largest elementary divisor
d
of
A_{\Gamma}
. That means one can look at just the primes
p
dividing
d
, compute the rank of
C_{\Gamma}\pmod{p}
for each such prime
p
, and if the rank turns out to be full for all such primes, we know the graph is RA. Theorem
6.11
also tells us that there is no hidden information between the layers
\text{Comm}_{b}(G,\Gamma),[G^{\Gamma},G^{\Gamma}]
, and
\text{Comm}(G,\Gamma)
that may make it difficult to determine if the graph is RA. It is enough to determine whether
\mathbb{Z}^{C_{\Gamma}}=\mathbb{Z}^{|\Gamma|}
.

Considering Proposition
6.4
, we get the following corollary of Theorem
6.11
.

Corollary 6.12
.

Let
p
be a prime number. Suppose that every vertex of
\Gamma
has degree congruent to
-1\pmod{p}
, that every pair of adjacent vertices of
\Gamma
has a number of common neighbors that is
-2\pmod{p}
, and that every pair of vertices of
\Gamma
that are distance 2 apart have a number of common neighbors that is divisible by
p
. Then
\Gamma
is not RA over
H(\mathbb{F}_{p})
.

In particular, letting
p=2
, we have the following result.

Corollary 6.13
.

If
\Gamma
has girth 4, every vertex has odd degree, and every pair of vertices at a distance 2 apart has an even number of common neighbors, then
\Gamma
is not
D_{8}
-RA.

We can now present our first families of graphs that are not RA.

Corollary 6.14
.

The following graphs are not
D_{8}
-RA:

1.

The cube
Q_{d}
with
d
odd.

2.

The folded cube
\square_{d}
with
2^{d-1}
vertices, where
d
is odd or 2.

Proof.

Part (a) follows directly from Corollary
6.13
, since vertices that are distance 2 apart have exactly two common neighbors.

For part (b), we will think of the folded cube
\square_{d}
as represented by the hypercube
Q_{d-1}
where opposite vertices are connected (those that are distance
d-1
apart). If
d=2
or 3, the graph is complete (
K_{2}
or
K_{4}
), respectively, and those graphs have neighborhood-indistinguishable vertices, so let’s assume
d>3
. This graph has girth 4 and each vertex has odd degree
d
. If two vertices are a distance 2 apart then they have 2 neighbors in common. Indeed, either they are connected by changing two bits in their binary representation (in which case they have the same neighbors as
Q_{d-1}
) or they are connected by an edge joining opposite vertices and one more edge representing a single bit flip, which can be done in either order, thus giving exactly 2 neighbors in common, and Corollary
6.13
finishes the proof.
∎

7
Conclusion

Our work has built on previous work generalizing the classic 2-color
5\times 5
Lights Out Puzzle by extending the possibility of the color space to elements of a group
G
. As a result, we have a new notion of the “graph product” of a group, which not only generalizes the diagonal subgroup of a product of groups but also allows for the invention of a slew of new puzzles. Any puzzle that involves some permuting (like the cubies of a Rubik’s Cube) could be put into the vertices of a graph and the puzzles could be connected in a way that makes it so when you perform an action on one puzzle, the puzzles in adjacent vertices have the same action done on them. The theory developed here can tell you which starting states would be solvable in such a setting, and the authors would be thrilled to hear of interesting scenarios that the reader might invent.

We have largely focused on applications where the graph in question is simple and undirected, but it is possible to consider directed graphs as well as multigraphs and graphs with multiple self-loops. There is likely more to explore with particular families of simple undirected and more general graphs.

One particularly interesting scenario to explore is when a vertex does not affect itself; for instance, in the seminal work
[
13
,
14
]
Sutner deduces that it is rather simple to figure out the size of
G^{\Gamma}
when
G=\mathbb{Z}_{2}
and
\Gamma
is an
m\times n
grid if clicking a vertex
does not
affect itself, but if a vertex
does
affect itself, it is complicated and involves finding the degree of the
\gcd
of two polynomials.

We have shown that many graphs are RA (those with girth
\geq 5
and many others, see the various results in Section
5.2
), and we have a characterization of RA graphs in Theorem
6.11
, but one clear possibility for future research remains in exploring other families of graphs and finding families that either are or are not RA. There is also more to be said about the structure of
G^{\Gamma}
when
\Gamma
is not RA. We will explore some of these ideas in
[
1
]
.

Appendix Appendix A.
Explicit Description of
G^{\Gamma}

We have only described
G^{\Gamma}
via the short exact sequence

1\to[G,G]^{n}\cap G^{\Gamma}\to G^{\Gamma}\to(G^{\textrm{Ab}})^{n}

for now, which allows us to have a rough description of
G^{\Gamma}
and, for instance, to know its size. If we want to actually write down elements in
G^{\Gamma}
, we can reduce
A_{\Gamma}
to an echelon form by using only two row operations: adding a multiple of one row to another and swapping rows. We will find that
A_{\Gamma}
is row-equivalent to a matrix
H
in
Hermite normal form
, whose entries are integers, such that

1.

H
is upper triangular,

2.

Every pivot (leading coefficient) is positive and is to the right of the pivots of the rows above it, and

3.

The elements below pivots are zero, and the elements above the pivots are nonnegative numbers that are smaller than the pivots.

In fact,
H=UA_{\Gamma}
where
U
is a unimodular matrix, i.e.
\det U\in\{\pm 1\}
.

In our context, we can also allow renumbering the coordinates, which rearranges the columns, so
H
can end up in the nice form

\begin{pNiceArray},

(5)

where
I_{r}
is an
r\times r
identity matrix and
T
is upper triangular of size
k\times k
with pivots
a_{1}\leq\cdots\leq a_{k}
along the diagonal of
T
(with
a_{1}>1
).

The first
r+k
resulting rows, say
\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{r+k}
of
H
, are a nicer basis of
\mathbb{Z}^{\Gamma}
to work with than the original rows of
A_{\Gamma}
because now we can see that for every
g_{1},\ldots,g_{r}\in G
,

g_{1}^{\mathbf{x}_{1}}\cdots g_{r}^{\mathbf{x}_{r}}=(g_{1},\ldots,g_{r},*,\ldots,*).

Thus, just as in
\mathbb{Z}^{\Gamma}
, we have complete freedom in
G^{\Gamma}
to set the first
r
coordinates. For coordinates
r+i
with
1\leq i\leq k
, the fact that we can change them by any multiple of
a_{i}
in
\mathbb{Z}^{H}
tells us that in
G^{\Gamma}
, we can multiply them (sequentially) by any element
g^{a_{i}}
. Note that, depending on
G
, the effect of this can be drastically different.
For example, if
a_{i}=3
and
G
has exponent 3, then we cannot change coordinate
r+i
by clicking coordinate
r+i
itself, and the only way to change it may be to click coordinates
r+j
with
1\leq j<i
.
On the other hand, if the cubes of
G
generate all of
G
, then we can change coordinate
r+i
any way we wish by clicking that coordinate itself.

In particular, for a divisible group
G
, or a group that is
p
-divisible for every prime
p
dividing the nonzero elementary divisors of
A_{\Gamma}
, we can set the first
r+k
coordinates however we wish.
If
G
is abelian, then just as with
\mathbb{Z}^{H}
, it is not possible to change the remaining
n-r-k
coordinates (corresponding to the zeroes on the main diagonal of
H
) without affecting the previous coordinates. For nonabelian groups, this is not necessarily the case: if
\Gamma
is
G
-RA, then we can nudge these coordinates by any commutator in
G
.

Example.

Let
G
be abelian, and let
\Gamma=C_{4}
. Then we can reduce
A_{\Gamma}
to

H=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0&2\\
0&1&0&2\\
0&0&1&2\\
0&0&0&3\end{pmatrix}.

Then, writing
\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{4}
for the rows of

g_{1}^{\mathbf{x}_{1}}\cdots g_{4}^{\mathbf{x}_{4}}=(g_{1},g_{2},g_{3},g_{1}^{2}g_{2}^{2}g_{3}^{2}g_{4}^{3}).

In fact, every element of

Let us point out why we need

(g_{1},g_{2},g_{3},*)

g_{2}^{\mathbf{x}_{2}}g_{1}^{\mathbf{x}_{1}}g_{3}^{\mathbf{x}_{3}}g_{4}^{\mathbf{x}_{4}}=(g_{1},g_{2},g_{3},g_{2}^{2}g_{1}^{2}g_{3}^{2}g_{4}^{3}).

Note that the last coordinate may be different from before if
G
is nonabelian, but that it does differ by a commutator in this case.

def allNeighborhoodsDistinct(g):
n = g.order()
M = g.adjacency_matrix() + matrix.identity(n)
return len(Set(M.rows())) == n

# Find the Smith normal form of A+I
def snf(g):
n = g.order()
M = g.adjacency_matrix() + matrix.identity(n)
Ms = M.smith_form()[0]

# Build a matrix of all intersections of closed neighborhoods
def RAMat(g):
n = g.order()
M = g.adjacency_matrix() + matrix.identity(n)
cols = M.columns()
newcols = []
for c1 in cols:
for c2 in cols:
newcol = [c1[i]*c2[i] for i in range(len(c1))]
newcols.append(newcol)
M2 = matrix(newcols).transpose()
return M2

M2 = RAMat(g)
M2s = M2.smith_form()[0]
return all([M2s[i][i] == 1 for i in range(n)])
