Changes in physico-chemical and functional properties of liquid egg white by ohmic heating process
Authors: M. Balakrishnan, A. Lilly Prasanna, A. Ramalakshmi, V. Vishnu Priya, P. Preetha, K. Thangavel
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
In the present study, ohmic heating system was developed for the pasteurization of liquid egg white. A batch reactor system was designed with a capacity of 100 ml and operated at varied gradients of voltage (20, 15, 10 V/cm), frequencies (10, 55, 100 Hz), holding times (1, 2.5, 4 min) at two different waveforms (sine and square). The treated liquid egg white was evaluated for validation parameters viz., heating rate, turbidity, soluble protein content, foaming capacity, and foaming stability. The viscosity of ohmically treated egg white was observed by subjecting the egg white to the shear rate ranges from 0.167 to 68 (s−1) where the viscosity decreased as the shear rate increased. The ohmic heating process variables were optimized using the Box-Behnken design and had a significant effect (P < 0.005) on the responses. The optimized parameters 17.93 V/cm voltage gradient, 10 Hz frequency, and 1.6 min holding time for sine waveform resulted in 19.6 °C/min heating rate, 0.01 turbidity, 98.35% soluble protein, 405.68% foaming capacity, and 31.84% foaming stability with the highest desirability of 78% of liquid egg white. The model developed from the dataset of this design can be used for predicting the responses within the limits of process variables.

Introduction

Eggs are being consumed by customers in large quantities because of their high nutrition [1–3]. The food industries are in high demand for liquid egg-products because it is used as a component in the preparation of bakery, pasta, meringues and meat products for its foaming, gelling and emulsification properties [4]. Raw or ready-to-use liquid egg products are highly preferred by consumers. However, it gets spoiled rapidly even when stored under refrigerated conditions. As many recipes use raw eggs as an ingredient, special treatments become mandatory for the processing of eggs to establish their microbial safety [5, 6]. To ensure the safety of egg products, pathogenic microbes such as yeast, mold, and bacteria (Salmonella enteritidis) must be eliminated [7, 8].

The traditional method of preservation is heating the food materials, including liquid egg white (LEW), but it is sensitive to a higher temperature and the protein is coagulated [9]. Even conventional pasteurization of eggs at moderate temperature results in protein coagulation [10]. The changes during various treatments make it unsuitable for use as an ingredient in cakes [11]. It is a big challenge to pasteurize the core region quickly and slower along the wall [12]. The United States Food and Drug Administration suggests that egg white has to be heated up to 55.6 °C for 372 s. To meet the requirements, liquid eggs are heated up to 68 °C for 300 s inorder to attain 5 log reduction in Salmonella enteritidis. This temperature causes the destruction of microbes but changes the quality of the LEW [13] giving rise to the demand for alternative heating methods.

The novel method of preserving the products includes the thermal and non-thermal preservation methods where the exposure of the food products to heat were minimized [14, 15]. The consumer and food industry are demanding high-quality food with less heat treatment [16, 17]. Innovative technologies viz., High voltage pulsed electric field, High-pressure processing, and Ohmic Heating (OH) are widely applied as other methods for heating [18, 19]. OH is used as a novel method in preserving the LEW wherein an alternating current is made to pass across the food material while heat is generated within it [20]. OH products are of high quality for their heating takes place uniformly [21, 22]. OH of liquid whole egg results in minimal structural changes [10, 23, 24]. Food industries are showing more interest in using OH for its safety. This is in line with consumer demands for safe and natural or preservative-free, minimally processed foods. [25] conducted a dynamic analysis of thermal protein denaturation and color changes in whole-egg yolk during ohmic heating. [4] Reported ohmic heating as an alternative to traditional pasteurization for whole eggs. [24] found that the use of OH in conjunction with simultaneous external heating shortened the processing time and kept the liquid eggs from overheating.

Even though numerous studies have been conducted on OH of liquid eggs, only limited studies were reported on ohmic heating of liquid egg white [23, 26, 27]. Hence this study aims to develop an OH system for liquid egg white, optimize the process parameters for pasteurization of LEW and analyze the effect of these process parameters viz., voltage gradient, frequency, holding time, and wave shape on physico-chemical and functional properties of LEW.

Materials and methods

Ohmic heating unit

Ohmic heating chamber was fabricated using acrylic sheet and stainless steel (SS) electrode with of size of about 15 cm length × 4 cm width and 8 cm height. A Variac (M/s. Delta Control, Mumbai, India) capable of varying the voltage in the range of 0 to 270 V (25 A capacity) was used to supply power to the OH chamber. The electric power to the Variac was taken from the domestic AC (Alternating Current) supply of 240 V and 50 Hz. A digital type V-A-W (500 V, 20A, and 2000W M/s. Delta control, Mumbai, India) meter was used to monitor voltage, current and power consumption simultaneously. The temperature of the samples was recorded by using stainless steel (316) resistance temperature detector (RTD) that can withstand up to 300 °C. A temperature indicator unit was attached to the RTD probe (M/sHeatran Services, Coimbatore, India).

A specially designed process control system (M/s. Vi Microsystems, Chennai, India) was used to vary the frequency and wave shape. The main components of the circuit were a bridge rectifier with a capacitor filter, microcontroller, single-phase inverter, LC filter, and a 15 V power supply. The input i.e.AC voltage from the varying source was supplied to the bridge rectifier which converted the AC signal into DC (Direct Current) and was smoothened using a capacitor filter. Then the rectified and smoothened DC current was supplied to the single inverter through the MOSFET circuit which works according to the signals of the microcontroller. The microcontroller was a “dsPIC 30F4011 based PWM Controller (Micro-4011)” set up with a LCD display provided with switches to vary the frequency and duty cycle of the output. The microcontroller was programmed to control the MOSFET and other minor components (opto coupler, drivers, etc.) through gate signals. So the microcontroller coupled with a single-phase inverter circuit gives the output current in the square wave shape in its AC form. There may be some reduction in the magnitude level of the voltage due to circuit heating. An inductor-capacitor circuit is connected parallel to the output terminals to get AC current in the form of sine wave. A 15 V power supply was given to the microcontroller and other minor circuits through a step-down transformer and an adapter. Preparation of samples

Freshly laid white leghorn eggs were collected from poultry farm of the Animal Husbandry Department, TNAU, Coimbatore, India. Whole eggs were used for finding the physical properties of egg. Preparation of egg white liquid for the ohmic heating experiments included the processing steps viz., cleaning, breaking, separating, blending. The collected eggs were cleaned with tissue paper to remove dirt and stains. The cleaned eggs were manually broken, and the albumen and yolk were removed with an egg separator. The thick and thin fractions of liquid egg white (LEW) were thoroughly mixed using a laboratory-type stirrer (M/s. Kadavil electro mechanical industries, Ernakulam, India) without foam formation. A digital pH meter was used to measure the pH of fresh liquid egg white. (M/s. Eutech Instruments, Singapore).

Processing of liquid egg white (LEW)

The LEW sample (100 mL) was poured into the rectangular ohmic heating chamber in such a way that the sample touched two electrodes. The LEW was heated from 30 to 56 °C by varying the gradients of voltage (20, 15, and 10 V/cm), frequency (10, 55 and 100 Hz), and wave shape (Sinusoidal and Square) by adjusting the voltage supply using the variac. As soon as the sample reached the pasteurization temperature (56 °C), it was maintained at the same temperature (56 °C ± 1 °C) for varying holding time (1, 2.5 and 4 min) in the OH chamber for each voltage gradient. The system was manually operated by adjusting the power on and off in order to maintain the sample at 56 °C at the prescribed holding time. The treated samples were taken for further analysis to check their quality and functional properties.

Analytical methods

The heating rate was observed by recording the temperature (°C) rise of the LEW during OH for every 30 s intervals using a thermometer. LEW was diluted as 10% v/v at pH 7.6 in 0.2 M Tris–HCl. The protein solubility was measured as per the Bradford method [28].

Measurement of turbidity

A tenfold dilution of the egg white (10% v/v or 9.64 g protein/ L) was prepared in 0.2 M Tris–HCl at pH 7.6 [28]. The absorbance of the diluted egg white solution was measured by reading the absorbance at 650 nm using a spectrophotometer (Make: HACH, Germany [5]). Demineralized water was used for calibration. The absorbance value of 0% corresponds to zero turbid solution [28] was noted.

Foaming capacity and stability

The foaming capacity and foaming stability of the LEW were determined by following Tan et al. [11]. The egg white was diluted to 1:1 (v/v) with distilled water. The 50 ml of the diluted solution was beaten in a centrifuge at 12000 rpm for 2 min at 30 °C. Estimation of alpha amylase activity

The alpha amylase activity in the fresh and the ohmically heated egg white was estimated for effective pasteurization [29] using Dinitro Salicylic Acid (DNS) method. The activity of amylase enzyme activity was estimated in terms of µg of maltose per unit volume and time (µg of maltose ml−1 min−1).

Rheological properties of fresh and ohmically heated liquid egg white

Rheological properties of liquid egg white were measured by using a rotational type of rheometer (Model MCR-52, M/s. Anton Paar, Austria) equipped with computer software, Rheoplus /32 V3.61. Parallel plate geometry (50 mm diameter, 1.0 mm gap) was used to measure the flow of LEW. Rheometer with parallel plate geometry, shown in.

Viscosity measurement as a function of shear rate

Viscosity measurement as a function of shear rate was carried out following the method described by [30]. Apparent viscosity, which is the ratio of shear stress and shear rate of liquid egg fractions was measured using a rheometer equipped with a parallel-plate measuring system. Measurements were carried out with an increasing shear rate (0.17 to 68 s-1). The duration of the experiment was set to 10 min. Data was adjusted by linear regression to the Hershel Bukley model [31].

Time-dependent viscosity

Rheological measurements were made as described by [32], in a rheometer equipped with a parallel-plate measuring system. Samples were sheared for 5 min at a fixed rotational speed of 55 rpm or 300 s-1. The shear stress (Pa) for the corresponding shear rate was observed. The fresh and the ohmically heated egg white were subjected to a test condition where a constant shear rate was applied and the sample response in terms of stress decay was observed with respect to time.

Experimental design

Box Behnken Design (BBD) of response surface methodology (RSM) was adopted to optimize the process variables of ohmic heating process using the statistical software Design Expert 6.0.8. The independent variables viz., voltage gradient, frequency, holding time and wave shape were optimized based on the physicochemical and foaming properties. The heating rate, turbidity, soluble protein, foaming stability and foaming capacity were taken as the response values. Turbidity of OH-treated LEW

The highest turbidity value of 0.068 was observed for the treatment of 20 V/cm, 55 Hz, and 4 min holding time (T12) for square wave. At higher voltage, LEW becomes more turbid due to higher heating rate. During OH treatment, the proteins in the liquid egg will denature increasing the turbidity leading to higher turbidity values [5]. As protein gets coagulated during the treatment, it becomes more turbid and not able to transmit [35]. The higher turbidity of the OH treated LWE is due to the high voltage gradient, frequency and holding time which indirectly confirmed the coagulation in the sample. Similar findings were observed by [34] who stated that increase in turbidity of OH treated tender coconut water might be due to the increase of total soluble solids (TSS). This is due to the fact that suspended solids have the capacity to impede light transmission in a sample; hence, light scattering becomes more intense as TSS concentration rises.

The lowest value of 0.01 was observed at 10 V/cm, 55 Hz, and 1 min holding time (T2) with sine wave represented the low rate of heating with the minimum holding time and frequency which might be the cause for its low turbid nature. Similar results of increase in turbidity value for eggs with increasing heating rates was observed by [5] and [28]. Figure 3 shows the effect of OH process parameter on turbidity of the LEW which implies that at higher voltage gradient, holding time and frequency, the turbidity gets increased. It was observed that the absorbance value of the samples treated in square wave form was found to be more than sine wave form for all the treatment combinations. The high heating rate observed in the square wave form may be the reason for more turbid nature of the samples. The reason for the difference in the effect could be correlated and characterized with the shape of the wave itself. In sinusoidal wave, the rise and fall of the magnitude is gradual whereas in the case of square wave the rise and fall of the magnitude is sharp/sudden. Yildiz et al. [36] also reported that turbidity of the pomegranate juice increases after OH was due to the higher voltage. Soluble protein content of OH-treated LEW

During the OH treatment at 10 V/cm, 55 Hz and 1 min holding time (T2) with sine wave, LEW had the maximum soluble protein concentration of 99.58%. With square wave treatment, the lowest soluble protein level of 82.33% was recorded at 15 V/cm, 100 Hz, and 4 min of holding time (T9). It was found that the decrease in soluble protein content was observed as the frequency increased from 10 to 100 Hz. Due to the higher heating rate in the square wave, the treated egg white exhibited a lower amount of soluble protein compared to the sine wave. Kannan et al. [5] found negative correlation detected between turbidity and the soluble protein content during the treatment suggested that the increase in heating rate causes turbidity to rise, which in turn leads to greater protein denaturation for eggs.

The decrease in soluble protein content from 99.58 to 89.58% was observed as the holding time increased from 1 to 4 min for treatments 10 V/cm, 55 Hz, 1 min holding time (T2) with sine wave and 10 V/cm, 55 Hz, 4 min holding time (T3) with sine wave, respectively. As the holding time increased, the soluble protein decreased from 96.42 to 82.33% for treatments 10 V/cm, 55 Hz, 1 min holding time (T2) and 15 V/cm, 100 Hz, 4 min holding time (T9) with square wave. The solubility gets decreased at high temperature, which may be due to the denaturation of protein in the egg [23]. Similar declining trend in solubility with increase in heat treatment time were studied by [24]. The primary causes of the variations were (i) the heating treatment, which partially denatures the protein and causes its molecules to swell, exposing its hydrophobic groups and reducing its hydration. (ii) When protein molecules unfold, they will interact with hydrophobic surfaces to form insoluble polymers, which will reduce the solubility of the liquid whole egg.

In addition, Van der Planken et al. [28] showed that LEW had lesser solubility at the temperature of 56–71 °C. Foaming capacity of OH-treated LEW

The maximum foaming capacity of 450% was achieved at 20 V/cm voltage, 10 Hz frequency and holding time of 2.5 min (T10), whereas, the minimum foaming capacity of 266.66% was observed for 10 V/cm voltage gradient, 100 Hz frequency and 2.5 min holding time (T4). Foaming capacity increases as the voltage gradient increases, frequency decreases and lesser holding time. This was in agreement with [4] who found similar foaming capacity results for OH whole egg white liquid. This may be due to the shear stress applied during the treatment which disrupts the protein molecules causing coagulation.

The foaming capacity of egg white subjected to square wave form had a larger foaming capacity than the sine wave form. When egg white was exposed to square wave and maximum holding time (4 min) treatments, there was a noticeable reduction in foaming capacity regardless of the voltage gradient and frequency. It was interesting to observe that egg whites treated with square waves with 4 min holding time showed higher turbidity than those treated with sine waves, indicating that the heat damage to the egg proteins. ANOVA (Table 3) suggested that ohmic heating significantly (p < 0.05) affected the foaming ability of LEW. Significant differences were also observed between the voltage gradient, frequency, and wave type characteristics (p < 0.05).

The viscosity of most of the ohmically treated egg white which were close to the untreated egg white shows that there was minimum protein coagulation. Low viscosity values of 18.9 and 21 Pa s were noted for ohmically treated egg whites for treatments T3SQ and T1SQ at shear rates of 0.167 s−1. Notably, the egg white samples with the lowest viscosity values were square wave treated subjected to low voltage gradient and high holding times, resulting in a prolonged heating period. At the shear rate of 0.167 s−1, high viscosity values such as 34.6 and 56.9 Pa s, were observed in ohmically heated egg white treatment, such as T11SQ and T12SQ. Similar findings by [5] reported that minimum protein coagulation was observed for the egg white treated at 0.6 ºC/min, whereas decrease in viscosity was significantly higher at maximum heating rate of 2.03 ºC/min (one-third of the untreated eggs). The coagulated nature of the T11SQ and T12SQ treatments mentioned above was confirmed by its corresponding high turbid and low soluble protein contents of 0.042, 91.08% and 0.168, 83.33%, respectively due to high voltage gradient and frequency treatment condition. Similar protein coagulation was seen in heat pasteurizations of egg white by [6].

The Herschel-Bulkley model was used to rheologically analyse the behavioural change in the ohmically treated egg white. The rheological properties of fresh egg white, such as fluid type, flow index, and yield stress, were found to be shear thinning, 0.01 and no yield stress, respectively. According to Table 4, the majority of the ohmically heated egg white samples revealed similar properties to the fresh egg white. Some treatments (T3SN, T6SQ, T9SQ, T11SN, T13SN and T13SQ) expressed shear thinning behaviour with increase in flow index (0.01 < n > 1) and yield stress values with the corresponding turbidity (0.012 to 0.05) and soluble protein content (99.57 to 88.81%) observed from the results assured the minor protein change in the egg white. Whereas treatments (T1SN, T11SQ and T12SQ) exhibited shear thickening behaviour with increase in flow index (n > 1) and yield stress withthe corresponding turbidity (0.023, 0.042, 0.168) and soluble protein content values (92.16%, 91.5% and 83.33%) were observed. Ohmic heating causes substantial protein destruction in the samples, which is explained by the high turbidity and decreased soluble protein concentration. The highest flow index and yield stress value was recorded for T11SQ its corresponding values are 8.11 Pa and 9.1 Pa.

From the Table 4, it was observed that the R2 value of most of the ohmically heated egg white value ranged between 0.83 and 0.98. The consistency index of all the treated egg white was found to be less than the fresh egg (152.16 Pa) and the values varied from 149.05 to 14.36 Pa s depending on the severity of the treatments. A positive linear relation was found between R2 and K values i.e. the both R2 and K value increase and decrease together based on the wave shape. Optimization of process parameters

The OH process parameters of LEW were optimized for maximum heating rate, turbidity, foaming capacity, foaming stability and minimum soluble protein. Second order polynomial models were fitted with each response in order to obtain the intended optimum condition. Derringers desirability method was used for optimization. Conclusion

The present study was aimed at applying the OH to the LEW at pasteurization temperature (56˚C) and to find the effect of applied gradient of voltage, frequency, time of holding and wave shape on the physico-chemical properties of LEW. Box Behnken design was used to optimize the process variables for the OH process of LEW. Responses other than foaming stability had (p < 0.05) statistical significance for the second-order polynomial models. The optimum conditions were standardized based on the responses. Experimental values of the responses at optimized condition are close to the predicted value. The optimized parameters of 17.93 V/cm voltage gradient, 10 Hz frequency, 1.6-min holding time and sine wave can achieve 19.6˚C/min heating rate, 0.01 turbidity, 98.35% soluble protein, 405.68% foaming capacity and 31.84% foaming stability with the highest desirability of 78%. Hence, the required pasteurization of liquid egg white can be achieved using OH process. The optimized parameters of OH can be applied effectively as the energy is dissipated directly to LEW.
