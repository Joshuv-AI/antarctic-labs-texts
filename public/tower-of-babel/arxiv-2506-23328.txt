Littlewood–Paley estimates for pure-jump Dirichlet forms Thanks: The research was supported by National Science Centre, Poland, 2023/49/B/ST1/04303.
Michał Gutowski

Abstract
. We employ the recent generalization of the Hardy–Stein identity to extend the previous Littlewood–Paley estimates to general pure-jump Dirichlet forms. The results generalize those for symmetric pure-jump Lévy processes in Euclidean spaces. We also relax the assumptions for the Dirichlet form necessary for the estimates used in previous works. To overcome the difficulty that Itô’s formula is not applicable, we employ the theory of Revuz correspondence and additive functionals. Meanwhile, we present a few counterexamples demonstrating that some inequalities do not hold in the generality considered in this paper. In particular, we correct errors that appear in previous works.



1.
Introduction

Consider a regular pure-jump Dirichlet form
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E}))
and let
X
be the associated symmetric pure-jump Hunt process. By
J
we denote the jumping kernel of
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E}))
(or
X
). Let
(P_{t})
be the associated semigroup. In
[
5
]
, Bañuelos, Bogdan, and Luks investigated the following
square functions
(or
Littlewood–Paley functions
):

(1.1)

\displaystyle G(x)

\displaystyle:=\left(\frac{1}{2}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\int\limits_{\hskip 8.19447pt\mathclap{E\setminus\{x\}}}(P_{t}f(y)-P_{t}f(x))^{2}\,J(x,dy)dt\right)^{1/2},

(1.2)

\displaystyle\widetilde{G}(x)

\displaystyle:=\left(\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\int\limits_{\hskip 8.19447pt\mathclap{E\setminus\{x\}}}(P_{t}f(y)-P_{t}f(x))^{2}\,\chi(P_{t}f(x),P_{t}f(y))J(x,dy)dt\right)^{1/2}.

Here,
f
is a fixed real-valued function in the
L^{p}
-space for
1<p<\infty
, and
\chi(s,t)=\ind_{\{|s|>|t|\}}+\tfrac{1}{2}\ind_{\{|s|=|t|\}}
. The authors of
[
5
]
studied the
Littlewood–Paley estimates
of those square functions. That is, they investigated the existence of constants
c_{p},C_{p}>0
such that

(1.3)

\displaystyle c_{p}\mathopen{\lVert}f\mathclose{\rVert}_{p}\leq\mathopen{\lVert}G\mathclose{\rVert}_{p}\leq C_{p}\mathopen{\lVert}f\mathclose{\rVert}_{p},

(1.4)

\displaystyle c_{p}\mathopen{\lVert}f\mathclose{\rVert}_{p}\leq\mathopen{\lVert}\widetilde{G}\mathclose{\rVert}_{p}\leq C_{p}\mathopen{\lVert}f\mathclose{\rVert}_{p}.

While the function
G
seems more natural, it lacks an upper bound; see Example 2 of
[
5
]
.
Therefore, the authors also considered its modification
\widetilde{G}
.
It turns out that if
X
is a symmetric pure-jump Lévy process satisfying the Hartman–Wintner condition, then
\widetilde{G}
satisfies (
1.4
) for some
c_{p},C_{p}>0
and for the entire range of
1<p<\infty
.

In
[
5
]
, two distinct techniques were employed to establish this result: the Hardy–Stein identity and the Burkholder–Davies–Gundy inequality.
The latter involves the parabolic martingale
M_{t}:=P_{T-t}f(X_{t})-P_{t}(X_{0})
.
The authors applied Itô’s formula to relate its quadratic variation
\langle M\rangle
to the third square function:

(1.5)

\displaystyle H(x)

\displaystyle:=\left(\frac{1}{2}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\int\limits_{E}\int\limits_{\hskip 8.19447pt\mathclap{E\setminus\{z\}}}(P_{t}f(y)-P_{t}f(z))^{2}\,J(z,dy)P_{t}(x,dz)dt\right)^{1/2}.

In the next step, the authors established the upper bound

\mathopen{\lVert}H\mathclose{\rVert}_{p}\leq C_{p}\mathopen{\lVert}f\mathclose{\rVert}_{p}

for
2\leq p<\infty
. Next, they demonstrated the inequality
\widetilde{G}\leq 2H
utilizing the “time doubling”; see the proof of Lemma 4.2 in
[
5
]
.
Finally, by combining these observations, they derived a similar upper bound for
\widetilde{G}
.
The lower bound for
1<p<2
was derived employing the ultracontractivity of the semigroup associated with a Lévy process.

In Li and Wang
[
45
]
, an attempt was made to apply a similar approach under weaker assumptions; however, the reasoning contains an error.
It turns out that in the greater generality, the inequality
\widetilde{G}\leq 2H
does not hold. In
[
45
]
, the proof of this inequality relies on an unjustified assumption that the “time doubling” still can be employed and that
P_{t}[P_{t}f(k(\cdot,y))](x)
is equal to
P_{2t}f(k(x,y))
for some function
k
. For more details, we refer to Remark
4.4
below.
In the present work, we provide a counterexample demonstrating that the above error is irreparable; see Example
4.5
below.

The Hardy–Stein identity in a non-local case was proved in
[
5
]
, where symmetric pure-jump Lévy processes were considered. See also the non-symmetric extension in Bañuelos and Kim
[
6
]
as well as the polarized version presented in Bogdan, Gutowski, and Pietruska-Pałuba
[
15
]
.
See also Bogdan, Kutek, and Pietruska-Pałuba
[
17
]
.
The Hardy–Stein identity was originally established by Stein in his book
[
74
, pp. 86–88]
for the Laplace operator. His approach was based essentially on the chain rule:

\Delta u^{p}=p(p-1)u^{p-2}|\nabla u|^{2}+pu^{p-1}\Delta u
.
Stein applied this identity to establish Littlewood–Paley estimates for square functions associated with the Laplace operator.

Recent papers derive a generalization of the Hardy–Stein identity.
In this context, we refer to Gutowski
[
38
]
and Gutowski and Kwaśnicki
[
39
]
.
The main goal of the present work is to employ the aforementioned generalization to extend previous Littlewood–Paley estimates for a broader class of pure-jump processes (and Dirichlet forms).
While
[
45
]
suggests that:

It seems that such an identity depends heavily on the characterisation of Lévy processes, and may not hold for general jump processes
(see p. 424 therein),
our results demonstrate that the Hardy–Stein identity remains valid in a more general setting.
The aforementioned generalization allows us to extend previous results not just to an extended class of Markov processes on the Euclidean space
{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
, but also to a wider variety of topological spaces
E
.

We want to focus on a general pure-jump Dirichlet form possessing a jumping kernel.
Consequently, Itô’s formula is not applicable, and we do not assume the ultracontractivity of the semigroup. Instead, we employ the Revuz correspondence to establish a link between the quadratic variation
\langle M\rangle
and the square function
H
.
Nevertheless, the reasoning presented in this article draws extensively on the approach outlined in
[
5
]
.

The Revuz correspondence describes the connection between additive functionals and a certain class of Radon measures.
In particular, in the case of positive continuous additive functionals (abbreviated as PCAF) this correspondence is a bijection.
The Revuz correspondence has been the subject of extensive study. Notable contributions include the works of Getoor
[
35
]
,
[
36
]
, as well as Fitzsimmons and Getoor
[
31
,
32
]
. Additionally, we refer to Section 75 of Sharpe’s book
[
69
]
.
Moreover, a relatively recent paper Li and Ying
[
46
]
extends previous results to the case of bivariate measures.

Since the parabolic martingale
M
is an additive functional with respect to the space-time process
(t,X_{t})
,
its quadratic variation
\langle M\rangle
is a PCAF.
In the present paper, we characterize the Revuz correspondence in this specific case; see Lemma
2.9
in the next section.

Summarizing the results presented in this paper, we obtain the following Littlewood–Paley estimates for
\widetilde{G}
in the generality we consider. In particular, the previously mentioned Example
4.5
demonstrates that, in general, these estimates cannot be established for the entire range of
1<p<\infty
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
1<p<\infty
and
f\in L^{p}(m)
. Under the strong stability assumption (i.e.
\lim_{T\to{\infty}}\mathopen{\lVert}P_{T}f\mathclose{\rVert}_{p}=0
)
there are constants
c_{p},C_{p}>0
such that

\displaystyle c_{p}\mathopen{\lVert}f\mathclose{\rVert}_{p}\leq\mathopen{\lVert}\widetilde{G}\mathclose{\rVert}_{p},\quad 2\leq p<\infty,

and

\displaystyle\mathopen{\lVert}\widetilde{G}\mathclose{\rVert}_{p}\leq C_{p}\mathopen{\lVert}f\mathclose{\rVert}_{p},\quad 1<p\leq 2.

However, there does not exist a universal constant
\tilde{C}_{p}>0
such that

(1.6)

\displaystyle\mathopen{\lVert}\widetilde{G}\mathclose{\rVert}_{p}\leq\tilde{C}_{p}\mathopen{\lVert}f\mathclose{\rVert}_{p},\quad 2<p<\infty.

In light of the impossibility of obtaining the estimate for the square function
\widetilde{G}
, we then proceed to investigate the function
H
and obtain the following results.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
1<p<\infty
and
f\in L^{p}(m)
. Under the strong stability assumption
and the conservativeness assumption (i.e.
P_{t}1=1
a.e.)
there are constants
c_{p},C_{p}>0
such that

\displaystyle c_{p}\mathopen{\lVert}f\mathclose{\rVert}_{p}\leq\mathopen{\lVert}H\mathclose{\rVert}_{p},\quad 3\leq p<\infty,

and

\displaystyle\mathopen{\lVert}H\mathclose{\rVert}_{p}\leq C_{p}\mathopen{\lVert}f\mathclose{\rVert}_{p},\quad 2\leq p<\infty.

Additionally, if
f\in L^{p}(m)\cap L^{2}(m)
, then the constant
c_{p}
can be chosen so that

\displaystyle c_{p}\mathopen{\lVert}f\mathclose{\rVert}_{p}\leq\mathopen{\lVert}H\mathclose{\rVert}_{p},\quad 1<p\leq 2.

However, there does not exist a universal constant
\tilde{C}_{p}>0
such that

(1.7)

\displaystyle\mathopen{\lVert}H\mathclose{\rVert}_{p}\leq\tilde{C}_{p}\mathopen{\lVert}f\mathclose{\rVert}_{p},\quad 1<p<2.

For the same reason that no upper bound exists for the function
G
, there is also no upper estimate for the function
H
; see Example
4.9
below.
Therefore, at the later stage we study the fourth square function given by

(1.8)

\displaystyle\widetilde{H}(x)

\displaystyle:=\left(\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\int\limits_{E}\int\limits_{\hskip 8.19447pt\mathclap{E\setminus\{z\}}}(P_{t}f(y)-P_{t}f(z))^{2}\,\chi(P_{t}f(z),P_{t}f(y))J(z,dy)P_{t}(x,dz)dt\right)^{1/2}.

In the present work, we derive the following Littlewood–Paley estimates for the function
\widetilde{H}
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
1<p<\infty
and
f\in L^{p}(m)
. Under the strong stability assumption
there is a constant
c_{p}>0
such that

\displaystyle c_{p}\mathopen{\lVert}f\mathclose{\rVert}_{p}\leq\mathopen{\lVert}\widetilde{H}\mathclose{\rVert}_{p},\quad 3\leq p<\infty.

Additionally, if we assume the conservativeness, then there is a constant
C_{p}>0
such that

\displaystyle\mathopen{\lVert}\widetilde{H}\mathclose{\rVert}_{p}\leq C_{p}\mathopen{\lVert}f\mathclose{\rVert}_{p},\quad 2\leq p<\infty.

At this point, the question of whether it is possible to obtain estimates for the remaining ranges of
1<p<\infty
remains open.
It is unclear which methods should be employed in this context.
Approaches relying on the Hardy–Stein identity seem particularly suitable for the functions
G
and
\widetilde{G}
, whereas martingale techniques appear to be more effective for the function
H
.

2.
Preliminaries

In this paper, we work under the standard topological configuration for Dirichlet forms; see (1.1.7) in Fukushima, Oshima, and Takeda
[
33
]
.
More precisely, we consider a locally compact separable metric space
E
and denote the
\sigma
-algebra of all Borel sets in
E
by
\mathcal{B}(E)
. Let
m
be a Radon measure on
E
of full support, that is,
\supp{m}=E
.

Let
1\leq p\leq\infty
. The real-valued Banach space
L^{p}(E,\mathcal{B}(E),m)
, equipped with the norm
\mathopen{\lVert}\cdot\mathclose{\rVert}_{p}
, will be referred to as
L^{p}(m)
. Let
C(E)
be the class of continuous functions on
E
. By
C_{c}(E)
we denote the class of functions from
C(E)
with compact support.

We frequently use the notation

f(x)\lesssim g(x)
(resp.
f(x)\gtrsim g(x)
)
to indicate that there exists a positive constant
C_{p}
depending only on
p

such that
f(x)\leq C_{p}g(x)
(resp.
f(x)\geq C_{p}g(x)
)
for all relevant arguments
x
.
If both

f(x)\lesssim g(x)
and
f(x)\gtrsim g(x)

hold,
we denote this by
f(x)\asymp g(x)
.
Additionally, when two sequences
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(b_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of non-negative real numbers are asymptotically equal, that is,
a_{n}/b_{n}\to 1
as
n\to{\infty}
, we write
a_{n}\sim b_{n}
. Similarly, for non-negative functions
f
and
g
, we will write
f(x)\sim g(x)
as
x\to x_{0}
whenever
\lim_{x\to x_{0}}f(x)/g(x)=1
and say that those functions are asymptotically equal as
x\to x_{0}
.

Consider a regular Dirichlet form
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E}))
with
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E})\subseteq L^{2}(m)
. We assume that
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E}))
is pure-jump, that is, it consists only of the jumping part in the Beurling–Deny decomposition; see Lemma 4.5.4 in
[
33
]
. In other words, the form
\mathcal{E}
is given by the following formula:

(2.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(u,v)=\frac{1}{2}\iint\limits_{\mathclap{E\times E\setminus d}}(u(y)-u(x))(v(y)-v(x))\,J(dx,dy)

for all quasi-continuous
u,v\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E})
.
Here,
J
is the
jumping measure
of
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E}))

and

d:=\{(x,y)\in E\times E:x=y\}
is the diagonal of
E\times E
.
Further, we always assume that we choose the quasi-continuous version of
u\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E})
without mentioning; see Theorem 2.1.7 in
[
33
]
.
We will also write
\mathcal{E}[u]:=\mathcal{E}(u,u)
.

It should be noted that, due to our convention, the measure
J
considered here is a factor of two larger than the one in
[
33
]
. Compare (
2.1
) with
[
33
, (4.5.13)]
.
This choice is inspired by the probabilistic interpretation of the jumping measure
J
. We refer here to
[
33
, (5.3.6)]
. Moreover, it is consistent with the usage found in other works.

Throughout the paper
X=(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t}),(\theta_{t}),(X_{t}),(\mathbb{P}_{x}))
will denote a symmetric pure-jump Hunt process associated with the Dirichlet form
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E}))
.
Then the semigroup
(P_{t})
of
X
extends to a semigroup on
L^{p}(m)
(
1\leq p\leq\infty
) which is a semigroup of sub-Markovian contractions on
L^{p}(m)
(
1\leq p\leq\infty
), strongly continuous on
L^{p}(m)
(

), symmetric on
L^{2}(m)
, and satisfies

(2.2)

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E})&=\left\{u\in L^{2}(m):\text{finite }\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{t}\int_{E}(u-P_{t}u)u\,dm\text{ exists}\right\},\\
\mathcal{E}(u,v)&=\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{t}\int_{E}(u-P_{t}u)v\,dm,\quad u,v\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E}).\\
\end{aligned}\right.

For the correspondence between symmetric Hunt processes and regular Dirichlet forms, we refer to Sections 4.2 and 7.2 in
[
33
]
. For the correspondence between regular Dirichlet forms and the semigroups on
L^{2}(m)
we refer to Section 1.3 in
[
33
]
. For the
L^{p}(m)
-extension of
(P_{t})
we refer to
[

and Section 1 of Farkas, Jacob, and Schilling
[

]
. See also Section 2.6 of the monograph by Jacob
[

. The last two references assume the setting

, but the same argument applies for general

.

For such a class of semigroups, the following maximal inequality of Stein is valid. We refer to
[
75
, p. 73]
.

Lemma 2.1

(Stein’s maximal inequality)
.

Let
1<p\leq\infty
and
f\in L^{p}(m)
. Denote

f^{*}(x):=\sup_{t\geq 0}|P_{t}f(x)|
.
Then, there exists a constant
D_{p}>0
such that,

(2.3)

\displaystyle\mathopen{\lVert}f^{*}\mathclose{\rVert}_{p}\leq D_{p}\mathopen{\lVert}f\mathclose{\rVert}_{p}.

Remark 2.2
.

The above maximal inequality may also be derived from Doob’s maximal inequality for martingales.
For this, see the discussion in
[
75
, pp. 106–107]
, where Rota’s theorem was used.
For this reason, in the literature, the above constant
D_{p}
is commonly taken to be equal to
\tfrac{p}{p-1}
if
1<p<\infty
and
1
if
p=\infty
.

Let
A_{p}
be the
infinitesimal generator
of the semigroup
(P_{t})
on
L^{p}(m)
:

(2.4)

\displaystyle A_{p}u

\displaystyle:=\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{t}(P_{t}u-u)\quad\text{in }L^{p}(m),

with the natural domain

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(A_{p}):=\{u\in L^{p}(m):\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\tfrac{1}{t}(P_{t}u-u)\text{ exists in }L^{p}(m)\}.

Denote the lifetime of the process
X
by
\zeta
.
Let
\Delta
be the cemetery state of
X
. By
E_{\Delta}
we denote the one-point compactification of
E
.
When
E
is already compact, then
\Delta
is an isolated point of
E_{\Delta}
.
Since
X
is symmetric, it possesses the
self-dual
property with respect to the reference measure
m
. This implies that, for any
T>0
and a non-negative measurable function
\Phi
defined on the space of trajectories,

(2.5)

\displaystyle\int\limits_{E}\mathds{E}_{x}\Phi((X_{(T-t)-}:t\in[0,T]))\ind_{\{T<\zeta\}}\,m(dx)

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{E}\mathds{E}_{x}\Phi((X_{t}:t\in[0,T]))\ind_{\{T<\zeta\}}\,m(dx).

In particular, for a non-negative Borel function
\varphi\colon[0,\infty)\times E\to\mathbb{R}
, we may write

\displaystyle\int\limits_{E}\mathds{E}_{x}\left(\int\limits_{0}^{T}\varphi(T-t,X_{t})\,dt\right)\ind_{\{T<\zeta\}}\,m(dx)

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{E}\mathds{E}_{x}\left(\int\limits_{0}^{T}\varphi(t,X_{T-t})\,dt\right)\ind_{\{T<\zeta\}}\,m(dx)

(2.6)

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{E}\mathds{E}_{x}\left(\int\limits_{0}^{T}\varphi(t,X_{t})\,dt\right)\ind_{\{T<\zeta\}}\,m(dx).

For some of the results we will need the following assumption.

Assumption 2.3

(Conservativeness)
.

P_{t}1=1
a.e.
for every
t>0
.

If we impose the above assumption, then the identity (
2
) takes the following form

(2.7)

\displaystyle\int\limits_{E}\mathds{E}_{x}\left(\int\limits_{0}^{T}\varphi(T-t,X_{t})\,dt\right)\,m(dx)=\int\limits_{E}\mathds{E}_{x}\left(\int\limits_{0}^{T}\varphi(t,X_{t})\,dt\right)\,m(dx).

We assume additionally that the jumping measure
J
is given by the kernel
J(x,dx)
, i.e.,

(2.8)

\displaystyle J(dx,dy)=J(x,dy)m(dx).

This assumption is not too restrictive. Indeed, for a general jumping measure
J
we may always write

J(dx,dy)=N(x,dy)\nu(dx)
,
where
(N,H)
is the Lévy system of
X
and
\nu
is the Revuz measure of
H
; see Theorem 5.3.1 in
[
33
]
. The notion of the Revuz measure is described in Subsection
2.4
.

We want to stress that, due to our convention,
J
is equal to
N(x,dy)\nu(dx)
, hence it is two times greater than the one appearing in
[
33
]
.
The motivation comes from the probabilistic meaning of the jumping measure (see (5.3.6) in
[
33
]
); this convention also aligns with those adopted by several other authors.

Under assumption (
2.8
), the regular Dirichlet form
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E}))
possesses a
carré du champ operator
given by:

(2.9)

\displaystyle\Gamma[u](x):=\frac{1}{2}\int\limits_{\hskip 8.19447pt\mathclap{E\setminus\{x\}}}(u(y)-u(x))^{2}\,J(x,dy).

Since the integrand is non-negative, the above operator is well-defined for every measurable function
u
, possibly infinite.
In particular,
\Gamma[u]
is finite a.e. for
u\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E})
.

It is easy to see that for any
u\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E})\cap L^{\infty}(m)
, the function
\Gamma[u]
is the unique element of
L^{1}(m)
such that

\displaystyle\int\limits_{E}f\Gamma[u]\,dm=\mathcal{E}(uf,u)-\tfrac{1}{2}\mathcal{E}(f,u^{2}),\quad f\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E})\cap C_{c}(E).

Therefore, in light of our convention, the function
\Gamma[u]
is two times smaller than the one in
[
33
]
and
[
18
]
. To see this, compare the above formula with
[
33
, (3.2.14)]
and
[
18
, Proposition 4.1.3]
.

It is known that the above operator is continuous as a mapping
\Gamma\colon\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E})\to L^{1}(m)
, where the space
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E})
is equipped with the seminorm
\sqrt{\mathcal{E}[\cdot]}
, that is,

(2.10)

\displaystyle\mathopen{\lVert}\Gamma[u]\mathclose{\rVert}_{1}\leq\mathcal{E}[u],\quad u\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E});

see Proposition 4.1.3 in Bouleau and Hirsch
[
18
]
.
Additionally, when
u\in\mathcal{D}(A_{2})
, then
u^{2}\in\mathcal{D}(A_{1})
and

(2.11)

\displaystyle\Gamma[u]=\tfrac{1}{2}A_{1}(u^{2})-uA_{2}u;

see Theorem 4.2.2 in
[
18
]
.

We consider also a modified version of the operator
\Gamma
:

(2.12)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Gamma}[u](x):=\int\limits_{\hskip 8.19447pt\mathclap{E\setminus\{x\}}}(u(y)-u(x))^{2}\,\chi(u(x),u(y))\,J(x,dy),

where

(2.13)

\displaystyle\chi(s,t)

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }|s|>|t|,\\
\tfrac{1}{2}&\text{if }|s|=|t|,\\
0&\text{if }|s|<|t|.\end{cases}

Note that
\chi
is the characteristic function of the set
\{(s,t):|s|>|t|\}
, with a minor modification when
|s|=|t|
.
Since
\chi(s,t)+\chi(t,s)=1
, from the symmetry of the jumping measure
J
we have

(2.14)

\displaystyle\int\limits_{E}\Gamma[u]\,dm=\int\limits_{E}\widetilde{\Gamma}[u]\,dm=\mathcal{E}[u],\quad u\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E}).

2.1.
Derivatives on
L^{p}

Let
1\leq p<\infty
and
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be some interval. Given a mapping

I\ni t\mapsto u(t)\in L^{p}(m)
we denote

\displaystyle\Delta_{h}u(t):=u(t+h)-u(t)\quad\text{if }t,t+h\in I.

We call
u

continuous
on
I
with values in
L^{p}(m)
if
\Delta_{h}u(t)\to 0
in
L^{p}(m)
as
h\to 0
for every
t\in I
.
A function
u
is called
differentiable
on
I
with values in
L^{p}(m)
if the limit
u^{\prime}(t):=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{1}{h}\Delta_{h}u(t)
exists in
L^{p}(m)
for every
t\in I
.
We say that
u
is
continuously differentiable
(or shortly
C^{1}
) on
I
with values in
L^{p}(m)
if
u
is differentiable and the mapping
I\ni t\mapsto u^{\prime}(t)\in L^{p}(m)
is continuous.

The idea of employing
L^{p}
-derivatives was motivated by the works
[
16
,
15
]
.
In
[
16
]
,
L^{p}
-derivatives were applied to the study of contractivity of perturbed semigroups; see the proof of Theorem 3 therein.
In
[
15
]
, the authors used
L^{p}
-derivatives to prove the polarized Hardy–Stein identity. For multidimensional
L^{p}
-calculus, we refer to
[
15
, Appendix B]
.

In the following part of this subsection, we establish some auxiliary facts concerning
L^{p}
-derivatives. They will play a role in the proof of the main result of Section
3
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
1<p<\infty
. Let
u
be differentiable on
I\subseteq[0,{\infty})
with values in
L^{p}(m)
. Then
P_{t}u(t)
is differentiable on
I\setminus\{0\}
and

(2.15)

\displaystyle(P_{t}u(t))^{\prime}=A_{p}P_{t}u(t)+P_{t}u^{\prime}(t),\quad t\in I\setminus\{0\}.

Additionally, under the assumption that for each
t\in I
,
P_{t}u(t)\in\mathcal{D}(A_{1})
, then
P_{t}u(t)
is differentiable on
I
with values in
L^{1}(m)
and

(2.16)

\displaystyle(P_{t}u(t))^{\prime}=A_{1}P_{t}u(t)+P_{t}u^{\prime}(t),\quad t\in I.

Proof.

Let
1\leq p<\infty
. Observe that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{h}\Delta_{h}(P_{t}u(t))=P_{t+h}u^{\prime}(t)+P_{t+h}\left(\frac{1}{h}\Delta_{h}u(t)-u^{\prime}(t)\right)+\frac{1}{h}(P_{t+h}-P_{t})u(t).

Due to the strong continuity of
(P_{t})
, the first term converges to
P_{t}u^{\prime}(t)
as
h\to 0
.
The second term converges to zero, because

\displaystyle\left\lVert P_{t+h}\left(\frac{1}{h}\Delta_{h}u(t)-u^{\prime}(t)\right)\right\rVert_{p}\leq\left\lVert\frac{1}{h}\Delta_{h}u(t)-u^{\prime}(t)\right\rVert_{p}\to 0,\quad\text{as }h\to 0.

The above inequality follows from the contraction property of
(P_{t})
.

The last part converges to
A_{p}P_{t}u(t)
by the definition of the generator
A_{p}
.
Indeed, when
p>1
, the semigroup
(P_{t})
is analytic on
L^{p}(m)
; see
[
75
, Theorem 1 in Chapter II]
. Hence, for any
f\in L^{p}(m)
and
t>0
,
P_{t}f\in\mathcal{D}(A_{p})
. In particular,
P_{t}u(t)\in\mathcal{D}(A_{p})
, when
t\neq 0
.
In the case of
p=1
, the convergence follows from the assumption
P_{t}u(t)\in\mathcal{D}(A_{1})
.
In summary of the above observations,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{h}\Delta_{h}(P_{t}u(t))\to P_{t}u^{\prime}(t)+0+A_{p}P_{t}u(t)

in
L^{p}(m)
as
h\to 0
.
∎

Remark 2.5
.

For
1<p<\infty
, if we assume that
u(0)\in\mathcal{D}(A_{p})
, then
P_{t}u(t)
is differentiable on
I
and (
2.15
) holds for all
t\in I
.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
f\in L^{2}(m)
and let
T>0
. The mapping
[0,T)\ni t\mapsto P_{t}[(P_{T-t}f)^{2}]
is
C^{1}
with values in
L^{1}(m)
and

\displaystyle(P_{t}[(P_{T-t}f)^{2}])^{\prime}=2P_{t}(\Gamma[P_{T-t}f]).

Proof.

Denote
u(t):=(P_{T-t}f)^{2}
. Due to (
2.16
) from Lemma
2.4

\displaystyle(P_{t}[(P_{T-t}f)^{2}])^{\prime}=A_{1}P_{t}[(P_{T-t}f)^{2}]+P_{t}u^{\prime}(t).

Let us justify why the use of this theorem is valid.
For every
t\in[0,T)
,
P_{T-t}f\in\mathcal{D}(A_{2})
due to the fact that the semigroup
(P_{t})
is analytic on
L^{2}(m)
. Therefore,
(P_{T-t}f)^{2}\in\mathcal{D}(A_{1})
and also
P_{t}[(P_{T-t}f)^{2}]\in\mathcal{D}(A_{1})
; see
[
18
, Theorem 4.2.2]
.

According to
[
15
, Lemma B.3(i)]
,

u^{\prime}(t)=2P_{T-t}f(P_{T-t}f)^{\prime}=-2P_{T-t}fA_{2}P_{T-t}f
.

Bringing everything together,

\displaystyle(P_{t}[(P_{T-t}f)^{2}])^{\prime}

\displaystyle=A_{1}P_{t}[(P_{T-t}f)^{2}]-2P_{t}[P_{T-t}fA_{2}P_{T-t}f]

\displaystyle=P_{t}[A_{1}[(P_{T-t}f)^{2}]-2P_{T-t}fA_{2}P_{T-t}f]

\displaystyle=2P_{t}(\Gamma[P_{T-t}f]).

The last line follows from (
2.11
).

In the end, we show that the above derivative is continuous with values in
L^{1}(m)
. Fix
t_{0}\in[0,T)
. We may write

\displaystyle\mathopen{\lVert}P_{t}(\Gamma[P_{T-t}f])-P_{t_{0}}(\Gamma[P_{T-t_{0}}f])\mathclose{\rVert}_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\mathopen{\lVert}P_{t}(\Gamma[P_{T-t}f])-P_{t}(\Gamma[P_{T-t_{0}}f])\mathclose{\rVert}_{1}

\displaystyle\quad+\mathopen{\lVert}P_{t}(\Gamma[P_{T-t_{0}}f])-P_{t_{0}}(\Gamma[P_{T-t_{0}}f])\mathclose{\rVert}_{1}

The second term converges to zero as
t\to t_{0}
by the strong continuity of
(P_{t})
.

Since the semigroup
(P_{t})
is a contraction on
L^{1}(m)
, the first term can be estimated as follows,

\displaystyle\mathopen{\lVert}P_{t}(\Gamma[P_{T-t}f])-P_{t}(\Gamma[P_{T-t_{0}}f])\mathclose{\rVert}_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\mathopen{\lVert}\Gamma[P_{T-t}f]-\Gamma[P_{T-t_{0}}f]\mathclose{\rVert}_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sqrt{\mathcal{E}[P_{T-t}f]}+\sqrt{\mathcal{E}[P_{T-t_{0}}f]}\right)\sqrt{\mathcal{E}[P_{T-t}f-P_{T-t_{0}}f]}

The last inequality follows from the continuity of
\Gamma
; see (
2.10
).
As the semigroup
(P_{t})
is strongly continuous on
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E})
equipped with the seminorm
\sqrt{\mathcal{E}[\cdot]}
(see Lemma 1.3.3(iii) in
[
33
]
), the right-hand side tends to zero as
t\to t_{0}
.
∎

2.2.
Hardy–Stein identity

Following the approach from Bañuelos, Bogdan, and Luks
[
5
]
, we use the Hardy–Stein identity to obtain some of the Littlewood–Paley estimates. In the setting presented above, this identity is given by the following formula:

(2.17)

\displaystyle\int\limits_{E}|f(x)|^{p}\,m(dx)-\lim_{T\to{\infty}}\mathopen{\lVert}P_{T}f\mathclose{\rVert}_{p}^{p}

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\iint\limits_{\hskip 5.7361pt\mathclap{E\times E\setminus d}}F_{p}(P_{t}f(x),P_{t}f(y))\,J(dx,dy)dt.

Here,
f\in L^{p}(m)
, where
1<p<\infty
,

(2.18)

\displaystyle F_{p}(a,b):=|b|^{p}-|a|^{p}-pa^{\langle p-1\rangle}(b-a)

is the
Bregman divergence
, and
a^{\langle\gamma\rangle}:=\left\lvert a\right\rvert^{\gamma}\sgn a
is the
French power
. Note that
F_{p}
is the second-order Taylor remainder of the convex function
\mathbb{R}\ni a\mapsto|a|^{p}\in\mathbb{R}
. In particular,
F_{p}
is non-negative.
The Hardy–Stein identity holds for every symmetric Hunt process (not only pure-jump). For this, we refer to Corollary 1.2 in Gutowski and Kwaśnicki
[
39
]
and to Gutowski
[
38
]
.

For the Bregman divergence
F_{p}
the following estimate holds

(2.19)

\displaystyle F_{p}(a,b)\asymp\left\lvert b-a\right\rvert^{2}(\left\lvert a\right\rvert\vee\left\lvert b\right\rvert)^{p-2}\asymp\left\lvert b-a\right\rvert^{2}(\left\lvert a\right\rvert+\left\lvert b\right\rvert)^{p-2},\quad a,b\in\mathbb{R}.

Hence, if
p\geq 2
, then also

(2.20)

\displaystyle F_{p}(a,b)\asymp\left\lvert b-a\right\rvert^{2}(\left\lvert a\right\rvert^{p-2}+\left\lvert b\right\rvert^{p-2}),\quad a,b\in\mathbb{R}.

For
1<p<2
we only have

(2.21)

\displaystyle F_{p}(a,b)\lesssim\left\lvert b-a\right\rvert^{2}(\left\lvert a\right\rvert^{p-2}+\left\lvert b\right\rvert^{p-2}),\quad a,b\in\mathbb{R}.

The first estimate in (
2.19
) (in its vectorized form) was derived by Pinchover, Tertikas, and Tintarev in
[
66
]
; see (2.19).
However, earlier one-sided bounds may be found in Shafrir
[
68
, Lemma 7.4]
and, for
2\leq p<\infty
, in Barbatis, Filippas, and Tertikas
[
8
, Lemma 3.1]
.
The one-dimensional case was presented also by Bogdan, Dyda, and Luks in
[
13
]
; see Lemma 6. We also refer to Bogdan, Grzywny, Pietruska-Pałuba, and Rutkowski
[
14
, Lemma 2.3]
.
For optimal constants in specific ranges of
p
, we refer to
[
68
, Lemma 7.4]
and Bogdan, Jakubowski, Lenczewska, and Pietruska-Pałuba
[
16
]
.

To associate the right-hand side of the Hardy–Stein identity (
2.17
) with the carré du champ operator
\Gamma
, we use estimate (
2.20
). Precisely, if
p\geq 2
, then we can write

\displaystyle\iint\limits_{\hskip 5.7361pt\mathclap{E\times E\setminus d}}

\displaystyle F_{p}(P_{t}f(x),P_{t}f(y))\,J(dx,dy)

\displaystyle\asymp\iint\limits_{\hskip 5.7361pt\mathclap{E\times E\setminus d}}(P_{t}f(y)-P_{t}f(x))^{2}(|P_{t}f(x)|^{p-2}+|P_{t}f(y)|^{p-2})\,J(dx,dy)

\displaystyle=2\iint\limits_{\hskip 5.7361pt\mathclap{E\times E\setminus d}}(P_{t}f(y)-P_{t}f(x))^{2}|P_{t}f(x)|^{p-2}\,J(dx,dy)

(2.22)

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{E}\Gamma[P_{t}f](x)|P_{t}f(x)|^{p-2}\,m(dx).

For
1<p<2
, we only have
\lesssim
-bound in the above estimate, as given in (
2.21
).

Similarly, we treat the operator
\widetilde{\Gamma}
. For any
1<p<\infty
, by estimate (
2.19
),

\displaystyle\iint\limits_{\hskip 5.7361pt\mathclap{E\times E\setminus d}}

\displaystyle F_{p}(P_{t}f(x),P_{t}f(y))\,J(dx,dy)

\displaystyle\asymp 2\iint\limits_{\hskip 5.7361pt\mathclap{E\times E\setminus d}}(P_{t}f(y)-P_{t}f(x))^{2}(|P_{t}f(x)|\vee|P_{t}f(y)|)^{p-2}\,J(dx,dy)

\displaystyle=\iint\limits_{\hskip 5.7361pt\mathclap{E\times E\setminus d}}(P_{t}f(y)-P_{t}f(x))^{2}|P_{t}f(x)|^{p-2}\chi(P_{t}f(x),P_{t}f(y))\,J(dx,dy)

(2.23)

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{E}\widetilde{\Gamma}[P_{t}f](x)|P_{t}f(x)|^{p-2}\,m(dx).

Combining the above observations with the Hardy–Stein identity, we obtain the following estimate between the
p
-norm of the function
f
and the carré du champ operator
\Gamma
and its modification
\widetilde{\Gamma}
.
When
1<p\leq 2
, then we get

(2.24)

\displaystyle\int\limits_{E}|f(x)|^{p}\,m(dx)

\displaystyle\gtrsim\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\int\limits_{E}\widetilde{\Gamma}[P_{t}f](x)|P_{t}f(x)|^{p-2}\,m(dx)dt,

while, if
2\leq p<\infty
, then

\displaystyle\int\limits_{E}|f(x)|^{p}\,m(dx)

\displaystyle\gtrsim\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\int\limits_{E}\widetilde{\Gamma}[P_{t}f](x)|P_{t}f(x)|^{p-2}\,m(dx)dt

\displaystyle\asymp\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\int\limits_{E}\Gamma[P_{t}f](x)|P_{t}f(x)|^{p-2}\,m(dx)dt.

For some of Littlewood–Paley estimates, we need the following not overly restrictive assumption.

Assumption 2.7

(Strong stability)
.

\lim_{T\to{\infty}}\mathopen{\lVert}P_{T}f\mathclose{\rVert}_{p}=0

for every
f\in L^{p}(m)
.

When the semigroup
(P_{t})
is conservative (see Assumption
2.3
) and the jumping measure satisfies some irreducibility assumption, then the limit
\lim_{T\to{\infty}}\mathopen{\lVert}P_{T}f\mathclose{\rVert}_{p}
is equal to
\mathopen{\lVert}\bar{f}\mathclose{\rVert}_{p}
, where
\bar{f}
is the mean value of
f
when
m(E)<{\infty}
and
\bar{f}=0
when
m(E)={\infty}
. For more details, see the discussion in Appendix A of
[
38
]
.

Without Assumption
2.7
, most of the lower Littlewood–Paley estimates are not valid. Indeed, if there exists some non-zero
g\in L^{p}(m)
such that
\lim_{T\to{\infty}}\mathopen{\lVert}P_{T}g\mathclose{\rVert}_{p}>0
, then, for
f:=\lim_{T\to{\infty}}P_{T}g
, we have
P_{t}f=f
and
\mathopen{\lVert}f\mathclose{\rVert}_{p}>0
. It is known that the inner integral of the right-hand side of the Hardy–Stein identity (
2.17
) equals

\displaystyle\iint\limits_{\hskip 5.7361pt\mathclap{E\times E\setminus d}}F_{p}(P_{t}f(x),P_{t}f(y))\,J(dx,dy)=\lim_{h\to 0^{+}}\frac{p}{h}\int\limits_{E}(P_{t}f-P_{h}P_{t}f)(P_{t}f)^{\langle p-1\rangle}\,dm.

See the notion of the Sobolev–Bregman form in
[
39
]
.
Since
P_{t}f=P_{h}P_{t}f=f
, the right-hand side is equal to zero. Hence, by (
2.23
),

\displaystyle\int\limits_{E}\widetilde{\Gamma}[P_{t}f](x)|P_{t}f(x)|^{p-2}\,m(dx)=0.

It is easy to see that this implies
\widetilde{\Gamma}[P_{t}f]=0
a.e.

Imposing Assumption
2.7
, we can strengthen estimates between
\mathopen{\lVert}f\mathclose{\rVert}_{p}
and
\Gamma
and
\widetilde{\Gamma}
as follows.
Then, if
1<p\leq 2
, then

\displaystyle\int\limits_{E}|f(x)|^{p}\,m(dx)

\displaystyle\asymp\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\int\limits_{E}\widetilde{\Gamma}[P_{t}f](x)|P_{t}f(x)|^{p-2}\,m(dx)dt

\displaystyle\lesssim\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\int\limits_{E}\Gamma[P_{t}f](x)|P_{t}f(x)|^{p-2}\,m(dx)dt,

while, if
2\leq p<\infty
, then

(2.25)

\displaystyle\int\limits_{E}|f(x)|^{p}\,m(dx)

\displaystyle\asymp\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\int\limits_{E}\widetilde{\Gamma}[P_{t}f](x)|P_{t}f(x)|^{p-2}\,m(dx)dt

\displaystyle\asymp\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\int\limits_{E}\Gamma[P_{t}f](x)|P_{t}f(x)|^{p-2}\,m(dx)dt.

2.3.
Square functions

Fix
f\in L^{p}(m)
, where
1<p<\infty
. The main scope of this work is to study the
square functions
(or
Littlewood–Paley functions
) introduced in Section
1
; see equations (
1.1
), (
1.2
), (
1.5
), and (
1.8
). Utilizing notions of operators
\Gamma
and
\widetilde{\Gamma}
defined by (
2.9
) and (
2.12
) we can rewrite earlier formulas of square functions in the following way:

\displaystyle G(x)

\displaystyle:=\left(\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\Gamma[P_{t}f](x)\,dt\right)^{1/2},

\displaystyle\widetilde{G}(x)

\displaystyle:=\left(\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\widetilde{\Gamma}[P_{t}f](x)\,dt\right)^{1/2},

\displaystyle H(x)

\displaystyle:=\left(\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}P_{t}\Gamma[P_{t}f](x)\,dt\right)^{1/2},

\displaystyle\widetilde{H}(x)

\displaystyle:=\left(\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}P_{t}\widetilde{\Gamma}[P_{t}f](x)\,dt\right)^{1/2}.

Due to the fact that
\widetilde{\Gamma}[u]\leq 2\Gamma[u]
a.e., we immediately obtain
\widetilde{G}\leq\sqrt{2}G
and
\widetilde{H}\leq\sqrt{2}H
.
In view of (
2.14
), the
L^{2}
-norms of
G
,
\widetilde{G}
,
H
, and
\widetilde{H}
are all equal to

\displaystyle\left(\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\int\limits_{E}\Gamma[P_{t}f]\,dmdt\right)^{1/2}.

Impose for a moment Assumption
2.7
.
Observe that for
p=2
the Bregman divergence is equal to
F_{2}(a,b)=(b-a)^{2}
. Therefore, as a straightforward consequence of the Hardy–Stein identity, we obtain the following fact.

Proposition 2.8
.

Impose Assumption
2.7
. Let
f\in L^{2}(m)
. Then, the following equivalence between
L^{2}
-norms holds:

\displaystyle\mathopen{\lVert}G\mathclose{\rVert}_{2}=\mathopen{\lVert}\widetilde{G}\mathclose{\rVert}_{2}=\mathopen{\lVert}H\mathclose{\rVert}_{2}=\mathopen{\lVert}\widetilde{H}\mathclose{\rVert}_{2}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\mathopen{\lVert}f\mathclose{\rVert}_{2}.

2.4.
Additive functionals and Revuz correspondence

In this subsection, let us assume that the process

X=(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t}),(\theta_{t}),(X_{t}),(\mathbb{P}_{x}))

is an arbitrary Hunt process, not necessarily pure-jump or symmetric. Assume that
(\mathcal{F}_{t})
is the minimum completed admissible filtration.

Recall that
(P_{t})
is the semigroup associated with the process
X
. For each
t\geq 0
, an operator
P_{t}
has its adjoint operator
\hat{P}_{t}
on
L^{2}(m)
, i.e.,

\langle P_{t}f,g\rangle=\langle f,\hat{P}_{t}g\rangle
,
for all
f,g\in L^{2}(m)
. Clearly,
P_{t}=\hat{P}_{t}
for symmetric
X
.
By

\displaystyle R_{\alpha}f:=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\alpha t}P_{t}f\,dt,\qquad\hat{R}_{\alpha}f:=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\alpha t}\hat{P}_{t}f\,dt,\quad\alpha>0,

we denote the
resolvent
(resp.
coresolvent
) of
X
. Here, the above integrals are understood in the Bochner sense in
L^{2}(m)
.
The resolvent and coresolvent are strongly continuous, that is for
f\in L^{2}(m)
, we have
\alpha R_{\alpha}f\to f
and
\alpha\hat{R}_{\alpha}f\to f
in
L^{2}(m)
as
\alpha\to{\infty}
.

Let
\alpha\geq 0
. A non-negative Borel function
f
is
\alpha
-excessive
(resp.
\alpha
-coexcessive
) with respect to the semigroup
(P_{t})
, if
e^{-\alpha t}P_{t}f\nearrow f
(resp.

e^{-\alpha t}\hat{P}_{t}f\nearrow f
) as
t\searrow 0
.
Let
f
be a non-negative Borel function. A simple example of an
\alpha
-excessive (resp.
\alpha
-coexcessive) function is
\alpha R_{\alpha}f
(resp.
\alpha\hat{R}_{\alpha}f
).
We say that a Borel measure
\tilde{m}
on the space
(E,\mathcal{B}(E))
is
excessive
if it is
\sigma
-finite and
\int_{E}P_{t}\ind_{B}d\tilde{m}\leq\tilde{m}(B)
,
B\in\mathcal{B}(E)
.
In particular, if the semigroup
(P_{t})
is symmetric, then the measure
m
introduced in Section
2
is excessive.

Let
\tilde{m}
be a Borel measure on
(E,\mathcal{B}(E))
.
For a measure
\mu
on
(E,\mathcal{B}(E))
we denote
\mathbb{P}_{\mu}(\Lambda):=\int_{E}\mathbb{P}_{x}(\Lambda)\,\mu(dx)
.
Recall that we say that a set
B\subseteq E
is
nearly Borel
(relative to
X
) if, for each probability measure
\mu
, there exist Borel sets
B_{1},B_{2}\subseteq E
such that
B_{1}\subseteq B\subseteq B_{2}
and

\mathbb{P}_{\mu}(X_{t}\in B_{2}\setminus B_{1}\text{ for some }t)=0
; see Definition (10.21) in Blumenthal and Getoor
[
12
]
or
[
33
, p. 392]
.
Before we introduce the notion of additive functionals, we need to provide some definitions of sets which are “small” or “big” with respect to the process
X
and measure
\tilde{m}
.
Denote by
\sigma_{B}:=\inf\{t>0:X_{t}\in B\}
the
hitting time
of a set
B
for
X
.
We say that a nearly Borel set
D\subseteq E
is
\tilde{m}
-polar
if

\mathbb{P}_{\tilde{m}}(\sigma_{D}<{\infty})=0
;
see Definition (6.3) in Getoor and Sharpe
[
36
]
.
A nearly Borel set
\widetilde{E}\subseteq E
is
absorbing
if
\mathbb{P}_{x}(\sigma_{E\setminus\widetilde{E}}<{\infty})=0
for all
x\in\widetilde{E}
; see
[
36
, p. 17]
.
Finally, we say that a nearly Borel set
N\subseteq E
is
\tilde{m}
-inessential
if
N
is
\tilde{m}
-polar and
N^{c}
is absorbing; see Definition (6.8) in
[
36
]
.

We call a family of functions
A
on
\Omega
with values in
[-\infty,+\infty]
an
additive functional
(AF in abbreviation), if there exist a
defining set

\Lambda\in\mathcal{F}_{\infty}
and an
\tilde{m}
-inessential set
N
(called an
exceptional set
for
A
) such that the following conditions hold:

(A.1)

A_{t}
is measurable with respect to
\mathcal{F}_{t}
.

(A.2)

\mathbb{P}_{x}(\Lambda)=1
for all
x\in E\setminus N
.

(A.3)

\theta_{t}[\Lambda]\subseteq\Lambda
for all
t>0
.

(A.4)

A_{0}(\omega)=0
for all
\omega\in\Lambda
.

(A.5)

\left\lvert A_{t}(\omega)\right\rvert<{\infty}
for all
\omega\in\Lambda
,
t<\zeta(\omega)
.

(A.6)

A_{t}(\omega)=A_{\zeta(\omega)}(\omega)
for all
\omega\in\Lambda
,
t\geq\zeta(\omega)
.

(A.7)

A_{s+t}(\omega)=A_{s}(\omega)+A_{t}(\theta_{s}\omega)
for all
\omega\in\Lambda
,
t,s\geq 0
.

(A.8)

For each
\omega\in\Lambda
, the function
[0,\zeta(\omega))\ni t\mapsto A_{t}(\omega)
is right continuous and has left limits.

We say that
A
is a
positive additive functional
(PAF in abbreviation) if,
in addition, for all
\omega\in\Lambda
,
t\geq 0
, we have
A_{t}(\omega)\geq 0
.
We say that
A
is a
positive continuous additive functional
(PCAF in abbreviation), when we replace condition
(A.8)
in the above definition with an even stronger condition:

(A.8’)

For each
\omega\in\Lambda
, the function
[0,{\infty})\ni t\mapsto A_{t}(\omega)
is continuous and non-negative.

Later, we will see that each PCAF corresponds to a unique Borel measure within the class of so-called smooth measures.

Denote by
\mathcal{B}^{*}(E)
the
universal completion
of
\mathcal{B}(E)
, that is,
\mathcal{B}^{*}(E):=\bigcap_{\mu}\mathcal{B}^{\mu}(E)
, where
\mathcal{B}^{\mu}(E)
is the completion of
\mathcal{B}(E)
with respect to the measure
\mu
and the above intersection is taken over all probability measures on
(E,\mathcal{B}(E))
.
By
\mathcal{B}^{e}(E)
we denote the
\sigma
-field generated by
\mathcal{B}^{*}(E)
-measurable functions which are
\alpha
-excessive for some
\alpha\geq 0
.
A set
B\in\mathcal{B}^{e}(E)
is called
\tilde{m}
-semi-polar
, if

\mathbb{P}_{\tilde{m}}(X_{t}\in B\text{ for uncountably many }t)=0
.
Denote by
\tau_{B}:=\inf\{t>0:X_{t}\notin B\}
the
exit time
of
X
from a set
B
.
Note that
\tau_{B}=\sigma_{E_{\Delta}\setminus B}
.
We say that an increasing sequence of sets
(B_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathcal{B}^{e}(E)
is a
\tilde{m}
-generalized nest
provided

\mathbb{P}_{\tilde{m}}(\lim_{n\to{\infty}}\tau_{B_{n}}<\zeta)=0
.
We call a set
B

finely open
if for each
x\in B
there exists a nearly Borel set
B_{x}\supseteq B^{c}
such that

\mathbb{P}_{x}(\sigma_{B_{x}}>0)=1
; see Definition (4.1) in
[
12
]
.
Finally, we call a Borel measure
\nu

smooth
if it charges no
\tilde{m}
-semi-polar set and admits an associated
\tilde{m}
-generalized nest
(G_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of finely open sets with
\nu(G_{n})<{\infty}
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

When we restrict our considerations to symmetric processes
X
, then there is a regular Dirichlet form
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E}))
associated with
X
and the class of smooth measures can be defined more easily by employing the notion of capacity corresponding to
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E}))
.

Let
\mathcal{O}
be the family of all open subsets of
E
. For
A\subseteq E
denote by
\mathcal{O}_{A}
the family of all open supersets of
A
. For
U\in\mathcal{O}
, we define

\mathcal{L}_{U}:=\{u\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E}):u\geq 1\ m\text{-a.e. on }U\}
. The
capacity
corresponding the Dirichlet form
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E}))
is defined in the following way: for
A\in\mathcal{O}
we define

(2.26)

\displaystyle\Capa(A):=\begin{cases}\inf_{u\in\mathcal{L}_{U}}(\mathcal{E}[u]+\mathopen{\lVert}u\mathclose{\rVert}_{2}^{2})&\text{if }\mathcal{L}_{U}\neq\emptyset,\\
{\infty}&\text{if }\mathcal{L}_{U}=\emptyset,\end{cases}

and for any
A\subseteq E
,

(2.27)

\displaystyle\Capa(A):=\inf_{U\in\mathcal{O}_{A}}\Capa(U).

An increasing sequence of sets
(B_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a
m
-generalized nest if and only if

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to{\infty}}\Capa(K\setminus B_{n})=0\quad\text{for every compact set }K.

Finally, a Borel measure
\nu
is smooth if and only if it charges no set of zero capacity and admits an associated
m
-generalized nest
(F_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of closed sets such that
\nu(F_{n})<{\infty}
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
. For a proof of the equivalence of the two definitions of a smooth measure given above; see
[
32
]
. See also
[
33
, p. 83]
or Appendix B of Li and Ying
[
46
]
.

We are ready to establish the correspondence between the class of PCAFs and the class of smooth measures. Let
A
be an additive functional and fix some excessive measure
\tilde{m}
(with respect to
X
).
The
Revuz measure

\nu
of
A
related to a reference measure
\tilde{m}
is defined by

(2.28)

\displaystyle\int\limits_{E}f\,d\nu:=\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{t}\int\limits_{E}\mathds{E}_{x}\left(\ \int\limits_{(0,t]}f(X_{s})\,dA_{s}\right)\,\tilde{m}(dx),\quad f\in\mathcal{B}^{e}(E)_{+},

or, equivalently,

\displaystyle\int\limits_{E}f\,d\nu=\lim_{\beta\to{\infty}}\beta\int\limits_{E}\mathds{E}_{x}\left(\ \int\limits_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\beta t}f(X_{t})\,dA_{t}\right)\,\tilde{m}(dx),\quad f\in\mathcal{B}^{e}(E)_{+}.

Here,
\mathcal{B}^{e}(E)_{+}
is the class of non-negative
\mathcal{B}^{e}(E)
-measurable functions.
We say that such
A
and
\nu
are in the
Revuz correspondence
. The notions of Revuz measure and Revuz correspondence are named in honor of Revuz; see
[
67
]
.

We provide the following simple example of
\nu
and
A
in the Revuz correspondence characterized by a Borel function
g
.
Let

(2.29)

\displaystyle A_{t}(\omega):=\int\limits_{0}^{t}g(X_{s}(\omega))\,ds,\quad t\geq 0.

Then
A
is an AF, PAF, and PCAF for
g
bounded, non-negative, and bounded non-negative, respectively. Moreover

A
is
in the Revuz correspondence (related to the reference measure
m
) with measure
g(x)m(dx)
. For this we refer to
[
26
, p. 41]
.
We may refer also to Section 3 of
[
81
]
, where a similar result in the context of generalized Dirichlet forms was derived.

We say that processes
A
and
B
are
\tilde{m}
-equivalent
, if

(2.30)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\tilde{m}}(A_{t}\neq B_{t})=0,\quad\text{for all }t>0.

In such a situation, we write
A_{t}\overset{\tilde{m}}{\simeq}B_{t}
.

The Revuz correspondence is a one-to-one correspondence between the class of smooth measures and the class of PCAFs (up to
\overset{\tilde{m}}{\simeq}
-equivalence).
For this statement, we refer to Theorem (3.11) in
[
32
]
.

If we limit our discussion to the symmetric processes
X
, the Revuz correspondence can be characterized by the following formula:

(2.31)

\displaystyle\int\limits_{E}fh\,d\nu

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{t}\int\limits_{E}h(x)\mathds{E}_{x}\left(\int\limits_{0}^{t}f(X_{s})\,dA_{s}\right)\,\tilde{m}(dx)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\beta\to{\infty}}\beta\int\limits_{E}h(x)\mathds{E}_{x}\left(\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\beta t}f(X_{t})\,dA_{t}\right)\,\tilde{m}(dx),

where
h
is any
\alpha
-excessive function (
\alpha\geq 0
) and
f
is any non-negative Borel function; see Section 5.1 of
[
33
]
. See also
Chapter 2 of Fukushima
[
26
]

and
Appendix B of
[
46
]
.

Several generalizations of identity (
2.31
) to the non-symmetric case have been proposed in the literature. In these studies, authors rely on the non-symmetric generalization of Dirichlet form: semi-Dirichlet form. Under some technical assumptions, like the sector condition, the Revuz correspondence can be characterized by:

\displaystyle\int\limits_{E}f\hat{h}\,d\nu

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{t}\int\limits_{E}\hat{h}(x)\mathds{E}_{x}\left(\int\limits_{0}^{t}f(X_{s})\,dA_{s}\right)\,\tilde{m}(dx)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\beta\to{\infty}}\beta\int\limits_{E}\hat{h}(x)\mathds{E}_{x}\left(\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\beta t}f(X_{t})\,dA_{t}\right)\,\tilde{m}(dx),

where
\hat{h}
is any quasi-continuous
\alpha
-coexcessive function (
\alpha\geq 0
). We refer here to
[
46
]
and
[
30
]
.
To prove the main result of this paper, the above formula is needed for a special case of the process
X
. To be precise, we require this formula for the space-time process of a symmetric Hunt process. To bypass the mentioned assumptions, we prove the above formula for this special case in Subsection
2.5
below.

2.5.
Space-time process

Here, we revert to our global assumption that
X=(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t}),(\theta_{t}),(X_{t}),(\mathbb{P}_{x}))
is a symmetric Hunt process.
By
\check{X}=(\check{\Omega},\check{\mathcal{F}},(\check{X}_{t}),(\check{\mathbb{P}}_{(t_{0},x)}))
we denote its
space-time process
. Here,

\displaystyle\check{\Omega}

\displaystyle:=(0,\infty)\times\Omega,

\displaystyle\check{\mathcal{F}}

\displaystyle:=\sigma\{\mathcal{B}((0,\infty))\times\mathcal{F}\},

\displaystyle\check{\mathbb{P}}_{(t_{0},x)}

\displaystyle:=\delta_{t_{0}}\otimes\mathbb{P}_{x},\quad(t_{0},x)\in\check{E}_{\Delta},

\displaystyle\check{X}_{t}(\tau,\omega)

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}(\tau+t,X_{t}(\omega))&\text{when }t<\zeta(\omega),\\
(\infty,\Delta)&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

where
\check{E}_{\Delta}:=(0,\infty)\times E\cup\{(\infty,\Delta)\}
. Here,
\delta_{t_{0}}
denotes the Dirac delta measure at a point
t_{0}\in(0,\infty)
and the point
\{(\infty,\Delta)\}
is the cemetery state of
\check{X}
. The space-time process
\check{X}
is a non-symmetric Hunt process with respect to the following admissible filtration

\displaystyle\check{\mathcal{F}}_{t}:=\sigma\{\mathcal{B}((0,\infty))\times\mathcal{F}_{t}\}.

Denote the expected value on the space
(\check{\Omega},\check{\mathcal{F}},\check{\mathbb{P}}_{(t_{0},x)})
by
\mathds{E}_{(t_{0},x)}
.
A conditional expectation of any
\check{\mathcal{F}}_{\infty}
-measurable
\Phi\colon\check{\Omega}\to\mathbb{R}
given
\check{\mathcal{F}}_{t}
can be written as

(2.32)

\displaystyle\mathds{E}_{(t_{0},x)}[\Phi|\check{\mathcal{F}}_{t}]=\mathds{E}_{x}[\Phi(t_{0},\cdot)|\mathcal{F}_{t}],\quad\check{\mathbb{P}}_{(t_{0},x)}\text{-a.s.}

Indeed, for any
A\in\mathcal{B}((0,\infty))
and
B\in\mathcal{F}_{t}

\displaystyle\mathds{E}_{(t_{0},x)}(\Phi\ind_{A\times B})

\displaystyle=\ind_{A}(t_{0})\mathds{E}_{x}(\mathds{E}_{x}[\Phi(t_{0},\cdot)|\mathcal{F}_{t}]\ind_{B})=\mathds{E}_{(t_{0},x)}(\mathds{E}_{x}[\Phi(t_{0},\cdot)|\mathcal{F}_{t}]\ind_{A\times B}).

We provide a brief overview of the essential properties of the space-time process, primarily following
[
69
, Section 16]
.
We denote the Lebesgue measure on
(0,\infty)
by
dr
and write the space
L^{2}((0,\infty)\times E,\mathcal{B}((0,\infty)\times E),dr\otimes m)
shortly as
L^{2}(dr\otimes m)
.
Let
(\check{P}_{t})
be the semigroup of the space-time process
\check{X}
. Then
\check{P}_{t}
can be written as

\displaystyle\check{P}_{t}f(r,x)=P_{t}[f(r+t,\cdot)](x),\quad f\in L^{2}(dr\otimes m).

Its adjoint operator
\hat{P}_{t}
on
L^{2}(dr\otimes m)
is given by

\displaystyle\hat{P}_{t}g(r,x)=\ind_{[0,r)}(t)P_{t}[g(r-t,\cdot)](x),\quad g\in L^{2}(dr\otimes m).

At the same time, the semigroup
(\hat{P}_{t})
is associated with the
backward space-time process

\hat{X}
defined on the same family of probability spaces

(\check{\Omega},\check{\mathcal{F}},(\check{\mathbb{P}}_{(t_{0},x)}))
by

\displaystyle\hat{X}_{t}(\tau,\omega):=\begin{cases}(\tau-t,X_{t}(\omega))&\text{for }t<\tau\wedge\zeta(\omega),\\
(\infty,\Delta)&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

To rephrase,
\hat{X}
represents the Cartesian product of
X
and the uniform motion to the left on
(0,\infty)
with killing at
0
.

Note that the measure
dr\otimes m
is excessive with respect to the semigroup
(\check{P}_{t})
. Indeed, for
\check{B}\in\mathcal{B}((0,{\infty})\times E)
, by the symmetry of
(P_{t})
, we can write

\displaystyle\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\int\limits_{E}\check{P}_{t}\ind_{\check{B}}\,dmdr

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\int\limits_{E}P_{t}[\ind_{\check{B}}(r+t,\cdot)](x)\,m(dx)dr=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\int\limits_{E}\ind_{\check{B}}(r+t,x)P_{t}1(x)\,m(dx)dr

\displaystyle\leq\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\int\limits_{E}\ind_{\check{B}}(r+t,x)\,m(dx)dr=\int\limits_{t}^{\infty}\int\limits_{E}\ind_{\check{B}}(r,x)\,m(dx)dr\leq dr\otimes m(\check{B}).

At the end of this subsection,
we prove a non-symmetric variant of equation (
2.31
) for the space-time process. This result will be useful in
Section
3
.

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
A
be a PCAF related to the space-time process
\check{X}
described above. Let
\nu
be the Revuz measure of
A
(related to the reference measure
dr\otimes m
). Then
\nu
satisfies

(2.33)

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathclap{(0,{\infty})\times E}}f\hat{h}\,d\nu

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{t}\int\limits_{\mathclap{(0,{\infty})\times E}}\hat{h}(r,x)\mathds{E}_{(r,x)}\left(\int\limits_{0}^{t}f(\check{X}_{s})\,dA_{s}\right)\,drm(dx)

for any
f\in\mathcal{B}^{e}((0,\infty)\times E)_{+}
and
\alpha
-coexcessive function
\hat{h}
(
\alpha\geq 0
).

Before we derive the proof of this lemma, we need to introduce the notion of the
\alpha
-subprocess following
[
69
, Exercise (12.34)]
. Fix
\alpha>0
. We want to define the process
{\check{X}}^{\alpha}
, which is obtained by killing
\check{X}
at a random and independent time
T^{\alpha}
following the exponential distribution with parameter
\alpha
. To be precise, let

\displaystyle{\check{\Omega}}^{\alpha}

\displaystyle:=(0,{\infty})\times\check{\Omega},

\displaystyle{\check{\mathcal{F}}}^{\alpha}

\displaystyle:=\sigma\{\mathcal{B}((0,\infty))\times\check{\mathcal{F}}\},

\displaystyle{\lambda}^{\alpha}(d\xi)

\displaystyle:=\alpha e^{-\alpha\xi}d\xi,

\displaystyle{\check{\mathbb{P}}}^{\alpha}_{(t_{0},x)}

\displaystyle:={\lambda}^{\alpha}\otimes\check{\mathbb{P}}_{(t_{0},x)},\quad(t_{0},x)\in\check{E}_{\Delta},

\displaystyle\check{X}_{t}(\xi,\tau,\omega)

\displaystyle:=\check{X}_{t}(\tau,\omega),

\displaystyle{\check{X}}^{\alpha}_{t}(\xi,\tau,\omega)

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}\check{X}_{t}(\xi,\tau,\omega)&\text{when }t<\xi,\\
(\infty,\Delta)&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Let

T^{\alpha}(\xi,\tau,\omega):=\xi
and

{\zeta}^{\alpha}:=\zeta\wedge T^{\alpha}

be the lifetime of
{\check{X}}^{\alpha}
.
We call
{\check{X}}^{\alpha}
the
\alpha
-subprocess
of
\check{X}
.
The semigroup of the process
{\check{X}}^{\alpha}
is
(e^{-\alpha t}\check{P}_{t})
. We use the notation

\mathds{E}_{(t_{0},x)}

either for the expected value on the spaces

(\check{\Omega},\check{\mathcal{F}},\check{\mathbb{P}}_{(t_{0},x)})

or

({\check{\Omega}}^{\alpha},{\check{\mathcal{F}}}^{\alpha},{\check{\mathbb{P}}}^{\alpha}_{(t_{0},x)})
.
Similarly, we define the
\alpha
-subprocess of
\hat{X}
denoted by
\hat{X}^{\alpha}
.

Proof of Lemma
2.9
.

Recall that the semigroup of
\check{X}^{\alpha}
is
(e^{-\alpha t}\check{P}_{t})
.
Consequently, as
\hat{h}
is
\alpha
-coexcessive for
\check{X}
, the measure
\hat{h}m
is excessive for
\check{X}^{\alpha}
.
Additionally, the mapping

[0,{\infty})\ni t\mapsto\hat{h}(\hat{X}^{\alpha}_{t})
is
{\mathbb{P}}^{\alpha}_{m}
-almost surely right continuous; see Proposition (4.2) and Theorem (4.8) in
[
12
]
. Due to self-duality of
X
(see (
2.5
)), the mapping

(0,\zeta^{\alpha})\ni t\mapsto\hat{h}(\check{X}^{\alpha}_{t-})
is
{\mathbb{P}}^{\alpha}_{m}
-almost surely left continuous.

Let
A^{\alpha}_{t}
denote the PCAF
A
stopped at time
T^{\alpha}
.
As a consequence of the above observations, we can invoke Corollary (8.11) of
[
36
]
and write

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{t}\int\limits_{\mathclap{(0,{\infty})\times E}}

\displaystyle\mathds{E}_{(r,x)}\left(\int\limits_{0}^{t}f(\check{X}^{\alpha}_{s-})\hat{h}(\check{X}^{\alpha}_{s-})\,dA^{\alpha}_{s}\right)\,drm(dx)

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{t}\int\limits_{\mathclap{(0,{\infty})\times E}}\hat{h}(r,x)\mathds{E}_{(r,x)}\left(\int\limits_{0}^{t}f(\check{X}^{\alpha}_{s-})\,dA^{\alpha}_{s}\right)\,drm(dx).

Given that
A
is continuous and the discontinuity points of the sample paths
t\mapsto\check{X}^{\alpha}_{t}
are at most countable, we may reformulate the above equation as follows:

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{t}\int\limits_{\mathclap{(0,{\infty})\times E}}

\displaystyle\mathds{E}_{(r,x)}\left(\int\limits_{0}^{t}f(\check{X}^{\alpha}_{s})\hat{h}(\check{X}^{\alpha}_{s})\,dA^{\alpha}_{s}\right)\,drm(dx)

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{t}\int\limits_{\mathclap{(0,{\infty})\times E}}\hat{h}(r,x)\mathds{E}_{(r,x)}\left(\int\limits_{0}^{t}f(\check{X}^{\alpha}_{s})\,dA^{\alpha}_{s}\right)\,drm(dx).

We clam that we can replace
\check{X}^{\alpha}_{s}
and
A^{\alpha}_{s}
by
\check{X}_{s}
and
A_{s}
in the above formula.
Denote

B_{t}:=\int_{0}^{t}f(\check{X}_{s})\,dA_{s}

and

B_{t}^{\alpha}:=\int_{0}^{t}f(\check{X}^{\alpha}_{s})\,dA^{\alpha}_{s}=B_{t\wedge T^{\alpha}}

.
We can write

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{t}\int\limits_{\mathclap{(0,{\infty})\times E}}\hat{h}(r,x)\mathds{E}_{(r,x)}\left(\int\limits_{0}^{t}f(\check{X}^{\alpha}_{s})\,dA^{\alpha}_{s}\right)\,drm(dx)

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\left[\,\frac{1}{t}\int\limits_{\mathclap{(0,{\infty})\times E}}\hat{h}(r,x)\mathds{E}_{(r,x)}B_{t}^{\alpha}\ind_{\{T^{\alpha}>t\}}\,drm(dx)+\frac{1}{t}\int\limits_{\mathclap{(0,{\infty})\times E}}\hat{h}(r,x)\mathds{E}_{(r,x)}B_{t}^{\alpha}\ind_{\{T^{\alpha}\leq t\}}\,drm(dx)\right].

When
\check{\omega}\in{\check{\Omega}}^{\alpha}
is such that
T^{\alpha}(\check{\omega})>t
, then
B_{t}^{\alpha}(\check{\omega})=B_{t}(\check{\omega})
. Moreover, as
B_{t}
and
T^{\alpha}
are independent, we can write

\mathds{E}_{(r,x)}B_{t}^{\alpha}\ind_{\{T^{\alpha}>t\}}=\mathds{E}_{(r,x)}B_{t}\cdot\mathds{E}_{(r,x)}\ind_{\{T^{\alpha}>t\}}=e^{-\alpha t}\mathds{E}_{(r,x)}B_{t}
. Therefore

(2.34)

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{t}\int\limits_{\mathclap{(0,{\infty})\times E}}\hat{h}(r,x)\mathds{E}_{(r,x)}B_{t}^{\alpha}\ind_{\{T^{\alpha}>t\}}\,drm(dx)=\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{t}\int\limits_{\mathclap{(0,{\infty})\times E}}\hat{h}(r,x)\mathds{E}_{(r,x)}B_{t}\,drm(dx).

When the right-hand side of (
2.34
) is infinite, then the claim is obviously true. Assume that it is finite. Then, since
B_{t}^{\alpha}\leq B_{t}
, we get

\mathds{E}_{(r,x)}B_{t}^{\alpha}\ind_{\{T^{\alpha}\leq t\}}\leq\mathds{E}_{(r,x)}B_{t}\ind_{\{T^{\alpha}\leq t\}}=\mathds{E}_{(r,x)}B_{t}\cdot\mathds{E}_{(r,x)}\ind_{\{T^{\alpha}\leq t\}}=(1-e^{-\alpha t})\mathds{E}_{(r,x)}B_{t}
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{t}\int\limits_{\mathclap{(0,{\infty})\times E}}\hat{h}(r,x)

\displaystyle\mathds{E}_{(r,x)}B_{t}^{\alpha}\ind_{\{T^{\alpha}\leq t\}}\,drm(dx)

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}(1-e^{-\alpha t})\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{t}\int\limits_{\mathclap{(0,{\infty})\times E}}\hat{h}(r,x)\mathds{E}_{(r,x)}B_{t}\,drm(dx)=0.

Summarizing, we show that

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{t}\int\limits_{\mathclap{(0,{\infty})\times E}}\hat{h}(r,x)

\displaystyle\mathds{E}_{(r,x)}\left(\int\limits_{0}^{t}f(\check{X}^{\alpha}_{s})\,dA^{\alpha}_{s}\right)\,drm(dx)

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{t}\int\limits_{\mathclap{(0,{\infty})\times E}}\hat{h}(r,x)\mathds{E}_{(r,x)}\left(\int\limits_{0}^{t}f(\check{X}_{s})\,dA_{s}\right)\,drm(dx).

Analogously, one can show that

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{t}\int\limits_{\mathclap{(0,{\infty})\times E}}

\displaystyle\mathds{E}_{(r,x)}\left(\int\limits_{0}^{t}f(\check{X}^{\alpha}_{s})\hat{h}(\check{X}^{\alpha}_{s})\,dA^{\alpha}_{s}\right)\,drm(dx)

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{t}\int\limits_{\mathclap{(0,{\infty})\times E}}\mathds{E}_{(r,x)}\left(\int\limits_{0}^{t}f(\check{X}_{s})\hat{h}(\check{X}_{s})\,dA_{s}\right)\,drm(dx).

To summarize,

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{t}\int\limits_{\mathclap{(0,{\infty})\times E}}

\displaystyle\mathds{E}_{(r,x)}\left(\int\limits_{0}^{t}f(\check{X}_{s})\hat{h}(\check{X}_{s})\,dA_{s}\right)\,drm(dx)

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{t}\int\limits_{\mathclap{(0,{\infty})\times E}}\hat{h}(r,x)\mathds{E}_{(r,x)}\left(\int\limits_{0}^{t}f(\check{X}_{s})\,dA_{s}\right)\,drm(dx).

Finally, since
\nu
is the Revuz measure of
A
, it follows from (
2.28
) that the left-hand side is equal to
\int_{(0,{\infty})\times E}f\hat{h}\,d\nu
.
∎

2.6.
Martingale

For any
x\in E_{\Delta}
a real valued process
((M_{t}),(\mathcal{F}_{t}),\mathbb{P}_{x})
is a
martingale
provided
\mathds{E}_{x}[M_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{s}]=M_{s}

\mathbb{P}_{x}
-a.s. for all
s\leq t
. We say that
M=((M_{t}),(\mathcal{F}_{t}),(\mathbb{P}_{x}))
is a
martingale
when
((M_{t}),(\mathcal{F}_{t}),\mathbb{P}_{x})
is a martingale for all
x\in E_{\Delta}
.

Let
M=((M_{t}),(\mathcal{F}_{t}),(\mathbb{P}_{x}))
be a martingale such that

M_{0}=0

and

\mathds{E}_{x}M_{t}^{2}<\infty
for
t>0,x\in E_{\Delta}
.
There exists
a unique integrable and predictable increasing process

\langle M\rangle
starting at zero such that
((M_{t}^{2}-\langle M\rangle_{t}),(\mathcal{F}_{t}),(\mathbb{P}_{x}))
is a martingale.
In particular,

(2.35)

\displaystyle\mathds{E}_{x}\langle M\rangle_{t}=\mathds{E}_{x}M_{t}^{2},\quad\text{for all }t\geq 0,x\in E_{\Delta}.

In addition, whenever
M
is an AF,
\langle M\rangle
is
a unique PCAF (up to
\overset{m}{\simeq}
-equivalence) such that
((M_{t}^{2}-\langle M\rangle_{t}),(\mathcal{F}_{t}),(\mathbb{P}_{x}))
is a martingale.
We call the process
\langle M\rangle
the
sharp bracket
(or
predictable quadratic variation
) of
M
.
For more details, we refer to Section A.3 of
[
33
]
. See also Section 4 of
[
81
]
and Théorème 3 in Meyer
[
53
, III]
.

The
square bracket
of the process
M
, denoted by
[M]
, may be defined by

\displaystyle[M]_{t}:=\langle M^{c}\rangle_{t}+\sum_{s\leq t}\Delta M^{2}_{s},

where
\Delta X_{t}:=X_{t}-X_{t-}
and
M^{c}_{t}
is the continuous part of
M
; see (A.3.6) in
[
33
]
.
Observe that
[M]_{t}=\langle M\rangle_{t}
for continuous
M
.

Let
1\leq p<\infty
. The Burkholder–Davies–Gundy inequality states that:

(2.36)

\displaystyle\mathds{E}_{x}|M_{T}|^{p}\asymp\mathds{E}_{x}([M]_{T})^{p/2},\quad T>0,x\in E_{\Delta}.

It should be noted that, in general, the square bracket
[M]
cannot be replaced by the sharp bracket
\langle M\rangle
. However, as demonstrated in Lenglart, Lépingle, and Pratelli
[
44
, Remarque 4.2]
and Barlow, Jacka, and Yor
[
9
, (4.b’), Table 4.1, p. 162]
, the following one-sided estimates hold: if
2\leq p\leq\infty
, then

(2.37)

\displaystyle\mathds{E}_{x}(\langle M\rangle_{t})^{p/2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathds{E}_{x}([M]_{t})^{p/2},\quad t>0,x\in E_{\Delta},

and, when
1\leq p\leq 2
,

(2.38)

\displaystyle\mathds{E}_{x}([M]_{t})^{p/2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathds{E}_{x}(\langle M\rangle_{t})^{p/2}\quad t>0,x\in E_{\Delta}.

3.
Sharp bracket of parabolic martingale

Fix
T>0
and
f\in L^{p}(m)
.
In the present article, we consider the so-called
parabolic martingale
:

(3.1)

\displaystyle M_{t}:=P_{(T-t)\vee 0}f(X_{t\wedge T})-P_{T}f(X_{0}).

The process
M
is a martingale
P_{T-t}f(X_{t})-P_{T}f(X_{0})
starting at zero and stopped at
T
with

M_{t}=f(X_{T})-P_{T}f(X_{0})
for
t\geq T
.

The parabolic martingale was first used in Bañuelos and Méndez-Hernández
[
7
]
for Brownian motion to obtain
L^{p}
-estimates for the Beurling–Ahlfors operator.
Further applications may be found in Applebaum and Bañuelos
[
1
]
and Bañuelos and Bogdan
[
4
]
where more general Lévy processes are considered.
See also
[
17
]
, where the parabolic martingale was used to derive the Hardy–Stein identity in Orlicz spaces.

The main result of this section is the following relation between the sharp bracket of the martingale
M
and the carré du champ operator
\Gamma
:

(3.2)

\displaystyle\langle M\rangle_{t}\overset{m}{\simeq}\int\limits_{0}^{t\wedge T}2\Gamma[P_{T-s}f](X_{s})\,ds=\int\limits_{0}^{t\wedge T}\int\limits_{\hskip 8.19447pt\mathclap{E\setminus\{X_{s}\}}}(P_{T-s}f(y)-P_{T-s}f(X_{s}))^{2}\,J(X_{s},dy)ds,

when the function
f
belongs to
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E})
; see Corollary
3.4
below. Here,
\overset{\tilde{m}}{\simeq}
is the equivalence relation defined by (
2.30
) and
\Gamma
is given by (
2.9
).
This result generalizes Lemma 3.1 of
[
45
]
, which requires some continuity assumption on the semigroup
(P_{t})
.
While the main idea of the proof remains the same, omitting the mentioned assumption requires employing the Revuz correspondence for certain functionals.

Remark 3.1
.

We would like to clarify certain inaccuracies that have appeared in the literature in the context of identity (
3.2
).
Since the process is pure-jump, the martingale
M
is not continuous in general and
[M]\neq\langle M\rangle
, hence the same statement does not hold for the square bracket
[M]
.
While the sharp bracket
\langle M\rangle
admits a continuous version, the same assertion cannot be assumed for
M
, as was done in
[
45
, Proof of Lemma 3.1]
. Due to this error, the authors conclude (
3.2
) with
\langle M\rangle
replaced by
[M]
; see
[
45
, (3.2)]
.

The same incorrect equation appears in
[
5
]
for
X
, assuming it is a pure-jump Lévy process; see the first displayed equation on page 471 therein. The authors refer to
[
1
]
, where the following identity is displayed; see
[
1
, (3.4)]
. Rewriting it to our context,

\displaystyle[M]_{t}=\int\limits_{0}^{t\wedge T}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(P_{T-s}f(X_{s-}+y)-P_{T-s}f(X_{s-}))^{2}\,\tilde{N}(ds,dy),

where
\tilde{N}
is a compensated Poisson random measure of
X
. This identity is an application of Itô’s formula. The authors of
[
5
]
incorrectly rewrite the above equation in the form of (
3.2
) with
\langle M\rangle
replaced by
[M]
.

Nevertheless, we want to stress that the mentioned inaccuracies do not affect the correctness of the main results of
[
5
]
and
[
45
]
. Due to estimates (
2.37
) and (
2.38
), the reasoning in
[
5
]
can be easily corrected. The main result of
[
45
]
, however, remains irreparable due to a different issue; see Remark
4.4
below.

Let us return to the discussion of the proof of the relation (
3.2
).
Since the martingale
M
is not an additive functional, we cannot apply the Revuz correspondence directly. Nevertheless, its time-space counterpart is an additive functional:

(3.3)

\displaystyle\check{M}_{t}(\tau,\omega):=\begin{cases}P_{T-\tau-t}f(X_{t}(\omega))-P_{T-\tau}f(X_{0}(\omega))&\text{if }t+\tau\leq T,\\
f(X_{T-\tau}(\omega))-P_{T-\tau}f(X_{0}(\omega))&\text{if }\tau\leq T<t+\tau,\\
0&\text{if }T<\tau.\end{cases}

Writing compactly,

\displaystyle\check{M}_{t}(\tau,\omega)=P_{(T-\tau-t)\vee 0}f(X_{[t\wedge(T-\tau)]\vee 0}(\omega))-P_{(T-\tau)\vee 0}f(X_{0}(\omega)).

Note that
\langle\check{M}\rangle
is a PCAF.
Observe that
\check{M}_{t}(0,\cdot)=M_{t}
and
\check{M}_{0}=0
.

We begin by showing that
\check{M}
is indeed a martingale.

Proposition 3.2
.

The process
\check{M}=((\check{M}_{t}),(\check{\mathcal{F}}_{t}),(\check{\mathbb{P}}_{(t_{0},x)})
given by (
3.3
) is a martingale.

Proof.

According to the fact that
\check{M}
is the process
P_{T-\tau-t}f(X_{t})-P_{T-\tau}f(X_{0})
stopped at
t=T-\tau
, it is enough to consider
t+\tau\leq T
.
Employing the Markov property of the Hunt process
X
, we have

(3.4)

\displaystyle P_{T-t_{0}-t}f(X_{t})=\mathds{E}_{X_{t}}f(X_{T-t_{0}-t})=\mathds{E}_{x}[f(X_{T-t_{0}})|\mathcal{F}_{t}],\quad\mathbb{P}_{x}\text{-a.s.}

for all
x\in E_{\Delta}
. Let
0\leq s\leq t\leq T-t_{0}
. Due to (
2.32
) and (
3.4
),

\displaystyle\mathds{E}_{(t_{0},x)}[\check{M}_{t}|\check{\mathcal{F}}_{s}]

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}_{x}[P_{T-t_{0}-t}f(X_{t})|\mathcal{F}_{s}]-P_{T-t_{0}}f(x)

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}_{x}[\mathds{E}_{x}[f(X_{T-t_{0}})|\mathcal{F}_{t}]|\mathcal{F}_{s}]-P_{T-t_{0}}f(x)

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}_{x}[f(X_{T-t_{0}})|\mathcal{F}_{s}]-P_{T-t_{0}}f(x),\quad\mathbb{P}_{(t_{0},x)}\text{-a.s.}

Again, by (
3.4
),

\displaystyle\mathds{E}_{x}[f(X_{T-t_{0}})|\mathcal{F}_{s}]-P_{T-t_{0}}f(x)=P_{T-t_{0}-s}f(X_{s})-P_{T-t_{0}}f(x)=\check{M}_{s},\quad\mathbb{P}_{(t_{0},x)}\text{-a.s.}

Since
x\in E_{\Delta}
and
t_{0}>0
were chosen arbitrarily, the proof is complete.
∎

We are ready to prove the main theorem of this section.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E})
. Let
\check{M}
be the martingale given by (
3.3
). Then

(3.5)

\displaystyle\langle\check{M}\rangle_{t}\overset{dr\otimes m}{\simeq}\int\limits_{0}^{t}2\Gamma[P_{T-\tau-s}f](X_{s}(\omega))\ind_{(0,T]}(\tau+s)\,ds,

where
\Gamma
is the carré du champ operator given by (
2.9
) and
\overset{\tilde{m}}{\simeq}
is the equivalence relation defined by (
2.30
).

Proof.

Observe that the additive functional on the right-hand side of (
3.5
) takes the form (
2.29
) with
\check{X}_{s}
and

g(t,x):=2\Gamma[P_{T-t}f](x)\ind_{(0,T]}(t)
. Therefore, its Revuz measure is

\displaystyle 2\Gamma[P_{T-t}f](x)\ind_{(0,T]}(t)m(dx)dt.

We denote by
\nu
the Revuz measure of
\langle\check{M}\rangle
and take an arbitrary bounded
\alpha
-coexcessive function
\hat{f}
with respect to the space-time process
\check{X}
.
By applying (
2.33
) and (
2.35
), we derive

(3.6)

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathclap{(0,{\infty})\times E}}\hat{f}\,d\nu

\displaystyle=\lim_{h\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{h}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\int\limits_{E}\hat{f}(t,x)\mathds{E}_{(t,x)}\langle\check{M}\rangle_{h}\,m(dx)dt

\displaystyle=\lim_{h\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{h}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\int\limits_{E}\hat{f}(t,x)\mathds{E}_{(t,x)}\check{M}_{h}^{2}\,m(dx)dt.

If
h+t\leq T
, then

\mathds{E}_{x}P_{T-t-h}f(X_{h})=P_{h}P_{T-t-h}f(x)=P_{T-t}f(x)
. Hence, by (
2.32
),

\displaystyle\mathds{E}_{(t,x)}\check{M}_{h}^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}_{x}\check{M}_{h}^{2}(t,\cdot)=\mathds{E}_{x}(P_{T-t-h}f(X_{h}))^{2}-2P_{T-t}f(x)\mathds{E}_{x}P_{T-t-h}f(X_{h})+(P_{T-t}f(x))^{2}

(3.7)

\displaystyle=P_{h}[(P_{T-t-h}f)^{2}](x)-(P_{T-t}f(x))^{2}.

Moreover, by Lemma
2.6
,
we get

(3.8)

\displaystyle P_{h}[(P_{T-t-h}f)^{2}]-(P_{T-t}f)^{2}

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{0}^{h}2P_{s}(\Gamma[P_{T-t-s}f])\,ds

\displaystyle=h\int\limits_{0}^{1}2P_{hs}(\Gamma[P_{T-t-hs}f])\,ds.

Here, the integral on the right-hand side is understood in the Bochner sense in
L^{1}(m)
.

Similarly, when
t\leq T<h+t
,

(3.9)

\displaystyle\mathds{E}_{(t,x)}\check{M}_{h}^{2}

\displaystyle=P_{T-t}[f^{2}](x)-(P_{T-t}f(x))^{2}

and

(3.10)

\displaystyle P_{T-t}[f^{2}]-(P_{T-t}f)^{2}

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{0}^{T-t}2P_{s}(\Gamma[P_{T-t-s}f])\,ds

\displaystyle=h\int\limits_{0}^{1}2P_{hs}(\Gamma[P_{T-t-hs}f])\ind_{[0,T-t]}(hs)\,ds.

Obviously,
\mathds{E}_{(t,x)}\check{M}_{h}^{2}=0
for
T<t
.

Combining (
3.6
), (
3
), (
3.8
), (
3.9
), and (
3.10
), we can write

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathclap{(0,{\infty})\times E}}\hat{f}\,d\nu

\displaystyle=\lim_{h\to 0^{+}}\int\limits_{0}^{T}\int\limits_{E}\hat{f}(t,\cdot)\int\limits_{0}^{1}2P_{hs}(\Gamma[P_{T-t-hs}f])\times

\displaystyle\quad\times\left[\ind_{(0,T-h]}(t)+\ind_{[0,T-t]}(hs)\ind_{(T-h,T]}(t)\right]\,dsdmdt

\displaystyle=\lim_{h\to 0^{+}}\int\limits_{0}^{T}\int\limits_{0}^{1}\int\limits_{E}2\hat{f}(t,\cdot)P_{hs}(\Gamma[P_{T-t-hs}f])\times

\displaystyle\quad\times\left[\ind_{(0,T-h]}(t)+\ind_{[0,T-t]}(hs)\ind_{(T-h,T]}(t)\right]\,dmdsdt.

The above follows from an application of Tonelli’s theorem.
Note that if
t\in(0,T-h]
, then
t\leq T-h\leq T-hs
, hence
\ind_{[0,T-t]}(hs)=1
.
Therefore, for all
t\in(0,T]
we have

\ind_{(0,T-h]}(t)+\ind_{[0,T-t]}(hs)\ind_{(T-h,T]}(t)=\ind_{[0,T-t]}(hs)
.
Now, we claim that

\displaystyle\lim_{h\to 0^{+}}\int\limits_{0}^{T}\int\limits_{0}^{1}

\displaystyle\int\limits_{E}2\hat{f}(t,\cdot)P_{hs}(\Gamma[P_{T-t-hs}f])\ind_{[0,T-t]}(hs)\,dmdsdt

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{0}^{T}\int\limits_{0}^{1}\lim_{h\to 0^{+}}\int\limits_{E}2\hat{f}(t,\cdot)P_{hs}(\Gamma[P_{T-t-hs}f])\,dm\ind_{[0,T-t]}(hs)\,dsdt.

For all sufficiently small
h\geq 0
, due to the boundedness of
\hat{f}
,

\displaystyle\int\limits_{E}2\hat{f}(t,\cdot)P_{hs}(\Gamma[P_{T-t-hs}f])\,dm

\displaystyle\leq 2\int\limits_{E}\Gamma[P_{T-t-hs}f]\,dm\sup_{(r,x)\in(0,T]\times E}\left\lvert\hat{f}(r,x)\right\rvert

\displaystyle=2\mathcal{E}[P_{T-t-hs}f]\sup_{(r,x)\in(0,T]\times E}\left\lvert\hat{f}(r,x)\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathcal{E}[f]\sup_{(r,x)\in(0,T]\times E}\left\lvert\hat{f}(r,x)\right\rvert<{\infty}.

At this point, we made use of the assumption that
f\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E})
. The dominated convergence theorem implies our claim.

Lemma
2.6
states that the mapping
[0,T-t)\ni r\mapsto P_{r}(\Gamma[P_{T-t-r}f])\in L^{1}(m)
is continuous. Thus,
P_{hs}(\Gamma[P_{T-t-hs}f])\to\Gamma[P_{T-t}f]
in
L^{1}(m)
as
h\to 0^{+}
. Hence,

\displaystyle\lim_{h\to 0^{+}}\int\limits_{E}2\hat{f}(t,\cdot)P_{hs}(\Gamma[P_{T-t-hs}f])\,dm=\int\limits_{E}2\hat{f}(t,\cdot)\Gamma[P_{T-t}f]\,dm.

Clearly,

\ind_{[0,T-t]}(hs)\to 1

for every
t\in(0,T]
as
h\to 0^{+}
.

Following the above observations, we obtain

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathclap{(0,{\infty})\times E}}\hat{f}\,d\nu

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{0}^{T}\int\limits_{0}^{1}\int\limits_{E}2\hat{f}(t,\cdot)\Gamma[P_{T-t}f]\,dmdsdt

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\int\limits_{E}\hat{f}(t,x)\cdot 2\Gamma[P_{T-t}f](x)\ind_{(0,T]}(t)\,m(dx)dt.

The above identity can be extended to any non-negative
\hat{f}\in C_{c}((0,{\infty})\times E)
. Indeed,
\hat{f}
can be approximated by the bounded
n
-coexcessive functions

\hat{f}_{n}:=n\hat{R}_{n}\hat{f}
, where

\hat{R}_{n}
is the coresolvent of the space-time process
(\check{X}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
.
Since
\hat{f}_{n}\to\hat{f}
in
L^{2}(m)
as
n\to{\infty}
,
there exists a subsequence
(\hat{f}_{n_{k}})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
\hat{f}_{n_{k}}\to\hat{f}
almost everywhere as
k\to{\infty}
.

Finally, since
\hat{f}
is arbitrary non-negative function in
C_{c}((0,{\infty})\times E)
, we obtain the following equality of the Revuz measures:

\displaystyle\nu(dt,dx)=2\Gamma[P_{T-t}f](x)\ind_{(0,T]}(t)m(dx)dt.

The statement of the theorem follows from the uniqueness of the PCAF with a given Revuz measure.
∎

With the above theorem at hand, due to the fact that
\langle\check{M}\rangle_{t}(0,\cdot)=\langle M\rangle_{t}
, we obtain the desired result.

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E})
. Let
M
be the martingale given by (
3.1
). Then the equivalence (
3.2
) holds.

4.
Square functions

4.1.
Square function
G

Our starting point is the following square function:

\displaystyle G(x)=\left(\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\Gamma[P_{t}f](x)\,dt\right)^{1/2}.

It does not possess the desired boundedness of
p
-norm for all
1<p<\infty
. In
[
5
]
, it was shown that for the Dirichlet form corresponding to the Cauchy process, the square function
G
may be too large for certain
f\in L^{p}(m)
with
1<p<2
; see
[
5
, Example 2]
.
As we will see later in Subsection
4.3
, the square function
H
is affected by the same problem; see Example
4.9
below. This is the reason for working with the operator
\widetilde{\Gamma}
instead of
\Gamma
.

However, the authors of
[
5
]
demonstrated that
\mathopen{\lVert}G\mathclose{\rVert}_{p}\asymp\mathopen{\lVert}f\mathclose{\rVert}_{p}
for
2<p<\infty
in the context of symmetric Lévy processes in the Euclidean space. These results were later extended to the non-symmetric case by Bañuelos and Kim in
[
6
]
.

For the broader class of processes considered here, the lower bound for
\mathopen{\lVert}G\mathclose{\rVert}_{p}
remains valid when
2<p<\infty
. As shown in the next subsection, the Hardy–Stein identity leads to the following result.

Proposition 4.1
.

Impose Assumption
2.7
.
Let
2<p<\infty
and
f\in L^{p}(m)
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathopen{\lVert}f\mathclose{\rVert}_{p}\lesssim\mathopen{\lVert}G\mathclose{\rVert}_{p}.

The above results follow directly from
\widetilde{G}\leq\sqrt{2}G
and the estimate for
\widetilde{G}
, which is provided in Theorem
4.3
below.

4.2.
Square function
\widetilde{G}

Let us now consider the following square function:

\displaystyle\widetilde{G}(x)

\displaystyle:=\left(\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\widetilde{\Gamma}[P_{t}f](x)\,dt\right)^{1/2}.

The square function
\widetilde{G}
overcomes the unboundedness issue for
1<p<2
raised in Example 2 of
[
5
]
. By employing the Hardy–Stein identity, we deduce the following two estimates.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
1<p\leq 2
and
f\in L^{p}(m)
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathopen{\lVert}\widetilde{G}\mathclose{\rVert}_{p}\lesssim\mathopen{\lVert}f\mathclose{\rVert}_{p}.

Theorem 4.3
.

Impose Assumption
2.7
.
Let
2\leq p<\infty
and
f\in L^{p}(m)
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathopen{\lVert}f\mathclose{\rVert}_{p}\lesssim\mathopen{\lVert}\widetilde{G}\mathclose{\rVert}_{p}.

In the next two proofs, we repeat mainly the argument used in
[
5
, Proofs of Lemma 4.3 and Lemma 4.5]
.

Proof of Theorem
4.2
.

Denote
f^{*}(x):=\sup_{t\geq 0}|P_{t}f(x)|
. Since
p\leq 2
,

\displaystyle(\widetilde{G}(x))^{2}

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\widetilde{\Gamma}[P_{t}f](x)dt

\displaystyle=(f^{*}(x))^{2-p}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\widetilde{\Gamma}[P_{t}f](x)|f^{*}(x)|^{p-2}\,dt

\displaystyle\leq(f^{*}(x))^{2-p}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\widetilde{\Gamma}[P_{t}f](x)|P_{t}f(x)|^{p-2}\,dt.

By Hölder’s inequality, inequality (
2.24
), and Stein’s maximal theorem (
2.3
),

\displaystyle\int\limits_{E}(\widetilde{G}(x))^{p}\,m(dx)

\displaystyle\leq\int\limits_{E}(f^{*}(x))^{(2-p)p/2}\left(\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\widetilde{\Gamma}[P_{t}f](x)|P_{t}f(x)|^{p-2}\,dt\right)^{p/2}\,m(dx)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\int\limits_{E}(f^{*}(x))^{p}\,m(dx)\right)^{1-p/2}\left(\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\int\limits_{E}\widetilde{\Gamma}[P_{t}f](x)|P_{t}f(x)|^{p-2}\,m(dx)dt\right)^{p/2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(\int\limits_{E}(f^{*}(x))^{p}\,m(dx)\right)^{1-p/2}\left(\int\limits_{E}|f(x)|^{p}\,m(dx)\right)^{p/2}

\displaystyle\asymp\int\limits_{E}|f(x)|^{p}\,m(dx).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\int\limits_{E}(\widetilde{G}(x))^{p}\,m(dx)

\displaystyle\lesssim\int\limits_{E}|f(x)|^{p}\,m(dx).

∎

Proof of Theorem
4.2
.

Denote
f^{*}(x):=\sup_{t\geq 0}|P_{t}f(x)|
.
Due to the strong stability of the semigroup, estimate (
2.25
) is valid.
By this, Hölder’s inequality, and Stein’s maximal theorem (
2.3
), we obtain

\displaystyle\int\limits_{E}|f(x)|^{p}\,m(dx)

\displaystyle\asymp\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\int\limits_{E}\widetilde{\Gamma}[P_{t}f](x)|P_{t}f(x)|^{p-2}\,m(dx)dt

\displaystyle\leq\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\int\limits_{E}\widetilde{\Gamma}[P_{t}f](x)|f^{*}(x)|^{p-2}\,m(dx)dt

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{E}(\widetilde{G}(x))^{2}|f^{*}(x)|^{p-2}\,m(dx)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\int\limits_{E}(\widetilde{G}(x))^{p}\,m(dx)\right)^{2/p}\left(\int\limits_{E}|f^{*}(x)|^{p}\,m(dx)\right)^{1-2/p}

\displaystyle\asymp\left(\int\limits_{E}(\widetilde{G}(x))^{p}\,m(dx)\right)^{2/p}\left(\int\limits_{E}|f(x)|^{p}\,m(dx)\right)^{1-2/p}.

This proves the desired inequality:

\displaystyle\int\limits_{E}|f(x)|^{p}\,m(dx)

\displaystyle\lesssim\int\limits_{E}(\widetilde{G}(x))^{p}\,m(dx).

∎

When the Dirichlet form corresponds to the pure-jump Lévy process, then the square function
\widetilde{G}
possesses the both-side
L^{p}
-estimates for the entire range of
1<p<\infty
, as shown in Section 4 of
[

]
.
However, this does not hold in general, as demonstrated in Example

below.
Before we move on to this example, we would like to make some comments on the conclusions derived from it.

Remark 4.4
.

The result below shows that the statement of Theorem 3.4 in
[
45

is not valid. The problem lies in the proof of Lemma 3.3, where the inequality
G(x)\leq\sqrt{2}H(x)
is stated.
It was incorrectly assumed that

is equal to
P_{2t}f(k(x,y))
for some function

This statement may be true, in the case of

Example 4.5

(Brownian motion on interval with removed segment)
.

The present example was proposed by Mateusz Kwaśnicki. The goal is to demonstrate that, for
2<p<\infty
, there is generally no uniform constant
C_{p}
satisfying

\mathopen{\lVert}\widetilde{G}\mathclose{\rVert}_{p}\leq C_{p}\mathopen{\lVert}f\mathclose{\rVert}_{p}
for all
f\in L^{p}(m)
. Consequently, the same conclusion applies to the function
G
.

Idea.

We construct a discrete approximation of the reflected Brownian motion on the interval
[0,\tfrac{\pi}{4}]\cup[\tfrac{3\pi}{4},\pi]
with additional jumps between
\tfrac{\pi}{4}
and
\tfrac{3\pi}{4}
. The intensity of the jumps are chosen so that the function
\cos x
becomes an eigenfunction of the generator.

In other words, this process is an approximation of the reflected Brownian motion in
[0,\pi]
, but with segments of paths lying on the part
(\tfrac{\pi}{4},\tfrac{3\pi}{4})
deleted.

By the approximation, we mean the following process:
a Markov chain with continuous time
which can be viewed as a reflected symmetric nearest-neighbor random walk
X
on
\{1,2,\ldots,4n\}
, with parts of paths corresponding to
\{(t,X_{t}):X_{t}\in\{n+1,n+2,\ldots,3n\}\}
removed.

To work with the standard notation for the generator matrix of a Markov chain, we reindex the state space:
we shift the states
3n+1,3n+2,\ldots,4n
to the left and call them
n+1,n+2,\ldots,2n
.
Therefore,
E=\{1,2,\ldots,2n\}
. The space
E
is equipped with the counting measure
m
.

Generator.

The generator of the process
X
is expressed as the following
2n\times 2n
matrix
A=[a_{i,j}]_{i,j=1}^{2n}
:

\displaystyle A=\left(\begin{array}[]{*{14}{c}}-1&1&0&0&\cdots&0&0&0&0&\cdots&0&0&0&0\\
1&-2&1&0&\cdots&0&0&0&0&\cdots&0&0&0&0\\
0&1&-2&1&\cdots&0&0&0&0&\cdots&0&0&0&0\\
0&0&1&-2&\cdots&0&0&0&0&\cdots&0&0&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&0&\cdots&-2&1&0&0&\cdots&0&0&0&0\\
0&0&0&0&\cdots&1&\alpha^{*}_{n}&\alpha_{n}&0&\cdots&0&0&0&0\\
0&0&0&0&\cdots&0&\alpha_{n}&\alpha^{*}_{n}&1&\cdots&0&0&0&0\\
0&0&0&0&\cdots&0&0&1&-2&\cdots&0&0&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&0&\cdots&0&0&0&0&\cdots&-2&1&0&0\\
0&0&0&0&\cdots&0&0&0&0&\cdots&1&-2&1&0\\
0&0&0&0&\cdots&0&0&0&0&\cdots&0&1&-2&1\\
0&0&0&0&\cdots&0&0&0&0&\cdots&0&0&1&-1\end{array}\right),

where

\displaystyle\alpha_{n}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{1+\cot\tfrac{\pi}{8n}}

and

\alpha^{*}_{n}:=-1-\alpha_{n}
.
In other words, the jumping kernel is given by

J(x,dy)=J(x,y)m(dy)
and

J(i,j):=a_{i,j}
for
i\neq j
, where

\displaystyle a_{i,j}=\begin{cases}1&\text{when }|i-j|=1\text{ and }\{i,j\}\neq\{n,n+1\},\\
\alpha_{n}&\text{when }\{i,j\}=\{n,n+1\},\\
0&\text{when }|i-j|>1.\end{cases}

J(x,y)
is illustrated in Figure

Eigenfunction.

We consider the following function on
E
:

\displaystyle f(k)

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}\cos\tfrac{(2k-1)\pi}{8n}&\text{if }k\leq n,\\
\cos\tfrac{(2k-1+4n)\pi}{8n}&\text{if }k>n.\end{cases}

Now, we will show that
f
is an eigenfunction of
A
, namely

\displaystyle Af(k)=-\lambda_{n}f(k),

where

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}:=2(1-\cos\tfrac{\pi}{4n})=4\sin^{2}\tfrac{\pi}{8n}.

Observe that
f(2n+1-k)=-f(k)
and
Af(2n+1-k)=-Af(k)
, hence it is enough to consider
k\leq n
.
By the sum-to-product formula, for
k=2,3,\ldots,n-1
, we get

\displaystyle Af(k)

\displaystyle=f(k+1)+f(k-1)-2f(k)

\displaystyle=\cos\tfrac{(2k+1)\pi}{8n}+\cos\tfrac{(2k-3)\pi}{8n}-2\cos\tfrac{(2k-1)\pi}{8n}

\displaystyle=2\cos\tfrac{(2k-1)\pi}{8n}\cos\tfrac{\pi}{4n}-2\cos\tfrac{(2k-1)\pi}{8n}=-\lambda_{n}f(k).

Similarly, for
k=1

\displaystyle Af(1)

\displaystyle=f(2)-f(1)

\displaystyle=\cos\tfrac{3\pi}{8n}-\cos\tfrac{\pi}{8n}

\displaystyle=\cos\tfrac{3\pi}{8n}+\cos\tfrac{-\pi}{8n}-2\cos\tfrac{\pi}{8n}

\displaystyle=2\cos\tfrac{\pi}{8n}\cos\tfrac{\pi}{4n}-2\cos\tfrac{\pi}{8n}.

Thus, again
Af(1)=-\lambda_{n}f(1)
. Finally, when
k=n
, we have

\displaystyle Af(n)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{n}(f(n+1)-f(n))+(f(n-1)-f(n))

\displaystyle=\alpha_{n}(\cos\tfrac{(6n+1)\pi}{8n}-\cos\tfrac{(2n-1)\pi}{8n})+\cos\tfrac{(2n-3)\pi}{8n}-\cos\tfrac{(2n-1)\pi}{8n}

\displaystyle=-2\alpha_{n}\cos\tfrac{(2n-1)\pi}{8n}+2\sin\tfrac{(n-1)\pi}{4n}\sin\tfrac{\pi}{8n}

\displaystyle=2\left(-\alpha_{n}+\frac{\sin\tfrac{(n-1)\pi}{4n}\sin\tfrac{\pi}{8n}}{\cos\tfrac{(2n-1)\pi}{8n}}\right)\cos\tfrac{(2n-1)\pi}{8n}.

Note that the parameter
\alpha_{n}
is chosen so that the right-hand side is equal to
-\lambda_{n}f(n)
. Indeed,

\displaystyle\left(-\alpha_{n}+\frac{\sin\tfrac{(n-1)\pi}{4n}\sin\tfrac{\pi}{8n}}{\cos\tfrac{(2n-1)\pi}{8n}}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{-\sin\tfrac{\pi}{8n}}{\sin\tfrac{\pi}{8n}+\cos\tfrac{\pi}{8n}}+\frac{\tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}(\cos\tfrac{\pi}{4n}-\sin\tfrac{\pi}{4n})\sin\tfrac{\pi}{8n}}{\tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}(\cos\tfrac{\pi}{8n}+\sin\tfrac{\pi}{8n})}

\displaystyle\qquad=-\frac{(1-\cos\tfrac{\pi}{4n}+\sin\tfrac{\pi}{4n})\sin\tfrac{\pi}{8n}}{\cos\tfrac{\pi}{8n}+\sin\tfrac{\pi}{8n}}

\displaystyle\qquad=-\frac{(2\sin^{2}\tfrac{\pi}{8n}+2\sin\tfrac{\pi}{8n}\cos\tfrac{\pi}{8n})\sin\tfrac{\pi}{8n}}{\cos\tfrac{\pi}{8n}+\sin\tfrac{\pi}{8n}}

\displaystyle\qquad=-2\sin^{2}\tfrac{\pi}{8n}=-\frac{\lambda_{n}}{2}\,.

Square functions.

According to the earlier part, since
f
is an eigenfunction of the generator
A
,
the semigroup operator
P_{t}
acting on
f
is given by

P_{t}f(k)=e^{-\lambda_{n}t}f(k)
.
Recall that the square function
\widetilde{G}
for the function
f
is given by

\displaystyle(\widetilde{G}(x))^{2}=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\int\limits_{\hskip 8.19447pt\mathclap{E\setminus\{x\}}}(P_{t}f(y)-P_{t}f(x))^{2}\chi(P_{t}f(x),P_{t}f(y))\,J(x,y)m(dy)dt.

Since
\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda_{n}t}\,dt=1/\lambda_{n}
and
m
is the counting measure, we may write

\displaystyle(\widetilde{G}(k))^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}}\sum_{l\neq k}(f(l)-f(k))^{2}\chi(f(k),f(l))J(k,l).

Moreover,
\widetilde{G}(2n+1-k)=\widetilde{G}(k)
.
When
k=1,2,\ldots,n-1
, we have
0<f(k+1)<f(k)
and

\displaystyle(\widetilde{G}(k))^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}}(f(k+1)-f(k))^{2}=\frac{4(\sin\tfrac{k\pi}{4n}\sin\tfrac{\pi}{8n})^{2}}{4\sin^{2}\tfrac{\pi}{8n}}=\sin^{2}\tfrac{k\pi}{4n}.

Also, if
k=n
, then
|f(n)|=|f(n+1)|=-f(n)
, hence

\displaystyle(\widetilde{G}(n))^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha_{n}}{2\lambda_{n}}(f(n+1)-f(n))^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{(2\cos\tfrac{(2n-1)\pi}{8n})^{2}}{8\sin^{2}\tfrac{\pi}{8n}(1+\cot\tfrac{\pi}{8n})}

\displaystyle=\frac{(\sqrt{2}(\cos\tfrac{\pi}{8n}+\sin\tfrac{\pi}{8n}))^{2}}{8\sin\tfrac{\pi}{8n}(\sin\tfrac{\pi}{8n}+\cos\tfrac{\pi}{8n})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sin\tfrac{\pi}{8n}+\cos\tfrac{\pi}{8n}}{4\sin\tfrac{\pi}{8n}}=\frac{1+\cot\tfrac{\pi}{8n}}{4}\,.

Now, the
p
-norm of
\widetilde{G}
is given by

(4.1)

\displaystyle\mathopen{\lVert}\widetilde{G}\mathclose{\rVert}_{p}^{p}

\displaystyle=2\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\sin^{p}\tfrac{k\pi}{4n}+2(\widetilde{G}(n))^{p}.

The first term may be written as

\displaystyle 2\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\sin^{p}\tfrac{k\pi}{4n}=2n\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{n}\sin^{p}\tfrac{k\pi}{4n}-2\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\right)^{p}.

Note, that the sum on the right-hand side is the Riemann sum of the function
2\sin^{p}\tfrac{\pi x}{4}
over
[0,1]
.
Therefore, the first term on the right-hand side of (
4.1
) is asymptotically equal to
c_{1}n
, where
c_{1}:=2\int_{0}^{1}\sin^{p}\tfrac{\pi x}{4}\,dx>0
.

The second term in (
4.1
) is asymptotically equal to
2(\tfrac{1}{4}\cdot\tfrac{8n}{\pi})^{p/2}=2(\tfrac{2n}{\pi})^{p/2}
.
Hence, for
p>2
,

\displaystyle\mathopen{\lVert}\widetilde{G}\mathclose{\rVert}_{p}^{p}

\displaystyle\sim\frac{2^{1+p/2}}{\pi^{p/2}}\,n^{p/2}.

Particularly, we see that
\widetilde{G}(n)
is the dominating term in the
p
-norm of
\widetilde{G}
.
On the other hand,

\displaystyle\mathopen{\lVert}f\mathclose{\rVert}_{p}^{p}

\displaystyle=2\sum_{k=1}^{n}\cos^{p}\tfrac{k\pi}{4n}\sim c_{2}n,

where
c_{2}:=2\int_{0}^{1}(\cos\tfrac{\pi x}{4})^{p}\,dx>0
. Finally,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathopen{\lVert}\widetilde{G}\mathclose{\rVert}_{p}}{\mathopen{\lVert}f\mathclose{\rVert}_{p}}

\displaystyle\sim\frac{2^{1/p+1/2}}{\pi^{1/2}c_{2}^{1/p}}\,n^{1/2-1/p}.

This proves that for
2<p<\infty
there is no universal constant
C_{p}>0
such that

\displaystyle\mathopen{\lVert}\widetilde{G}\mathclose{\rVert}_{p}

\displaystyle\leq C_{p}\mathopen{\lVert}f\mathclose{\rVert}_{p}.

What is more, since
\widetilde{G}\leq\sqrt{2}G
, the above statement holds also for the square function
G
.

4.3.
Square function
H

The next square function we will analyze is the following:

\displaystyle H(x)

\displaystyle:=\left(\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}P_{t}\Gamma[P_{t}f](x)\,dt\right)^{1/2}.

The function
H
overcomes the problems with
\widetilde{G}
demonstrated for
2<p<{\infty}
in Example
4.5
.
Indeed, the following
L^{p}
-estimates hold.

Theorem 4.6
.

Impose Assumption
2.3
. Let
2\leq p<\infty
and
f\in L^{p}(m)
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathopen{\lVert}H\mathclose{\rVert}_{p}\lesssim\mathopen{\lVert}f\mathclose{\rVert}_{p}.

Theorem 4.7
.

Impose Assumptions
2.3
and
2.7
.
Let
1<p\leq 2
and
f\in L^{p}(m)\cap L^{2}(m)
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathopen{\lVert}f\mathclose{\rVert}_{p}\lesssim\mathopen{\lVert}H\mathclose{\rVert}_{p}.

Proposition 4.8
.

Impose Assumption
2.7
.
Let
3\leq p<\infty
and
f\in L^{p}(m)
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathopen{\lVert}f\mathclose{\rVert}_{p}\lesssim\mathopen{\lVert}H\mathclose{\rVert}_{p}.

The last statement is a straightforward consequence of Proposition
4.11
below, due to
\widetilde{H}\leq\sqrt{2}H
.
The rest of the estimates can be shown employing the martingale
M
introduced in Subsection
2.6
and the Burkholder–Davies–Gundy inequality; see the proofs at the end of the current subsection.
However, the square function
H
shares the same issue as
G
, namely, since it is defined by the operator
\Gamma
instead of
\widetilde{\Gamma}
, it is too large when
1<p<2
. To demonstrate this, we adopt Example 2 of
[
5
]
, see Example
4.9
below.
Moreover, the lower bound of
\mathopen{\lVert}H\mathclose{\rVert}_{p}
is still an open question for the range
2<p<3
.

Example 4.9
.

Consider the Dirichlet form on the Euclidean space
E={\mathbb{R}^{n}}
(
n\geq 2
) associated with the Cauchy process (
\alpha
-stable process with
\alpha=1
), that is,
J(x,dy)=J(x,y)dy
, where

\displaystyle J(x,y)=\frac{\mathcal{A}_{n,1}}{|y-x|^{n+1}}.

Here

\mathcal{A}_{n,\alpha}:=2^{\alpha}\Gamma((n+\alpha)/2)\pi^{-n/2}/\left\lvert\Gamma(-\alpha/2)\right\rvert
,
where
\alpha\in(0,2)
;
see
[
5
, (2.11)]
.
In this context,
\Gamma(\cdot)
stands for the Gamma function.
The semigroup is given by the following transition density:

\displaystyle p_{t}(x,y)=\frac{c_{n}t}{(t^{2}+|y-x|^{2})^{(n+1)/2}}

and
c_{n}=\Gamma((n+1)/2)\pi^{-(n+1)/2}
.

Denote the ball of radius
r
centered at a point
x
by
B(x,r)
.
Let
h(x):=\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{-(n+1)/2}
and
f(x):=h(x)\ind_{B(0,1)}(x)
.
Note that
f
belongs to
L^{p}(dx)
for
1<p<2n/(n+1)<2
.
We claim that the square function
H
of the function
f
is identically equal to infinity.
The problem fundamentally arises from the rapid growth of
\Gamma[P_{t}f]
as
t
tends to zero.

To illustrate this, we introduce the following function:

\displaystyle v(x,t):=\begin{cases}P_{t}h(x),&\text{for }t>0,\\
h(x),&\text{for }t=0.\end{cases}

Note that
h
is locally integrable on
{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
and vanishes at infinity, hence the function
v
is well-defined and continuous except at the point
(x,t)=(0,0)
.

We first calculate
P_{t}f(x)
. Observe that
p_{t}(x,y)
satisfies the following scaling property

\displaystyle p_{t}(x,y)=\frac{1}{t^{n}}p_{1}\!\left(\tfrac{x}{t},\tfrac{y}{t}\right).

Obviously
h(x)=t^{-(n+1)/2}h(x/t)
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle P_{t}f(x)

\displaystyle=c_{n}\int\limits_{\mathclap{B(0,1)}}\frac{1}{t^{(n+1)/2}}\,h\!\left(\tfrac{y}{t}\right)\cdot\frac{1}{t^{n}}\,p_{1}\!\left(\tfrac{x}{t},\tfrac{y}{t}\right)\,dy

\displaystyle=c_{n}\int\limits_{\mathclap{B(0,1/t)}}\frac{1}{t^{(n+1)/2}}\,h\!\left(y\right)\cdot\frac{1}{t^{n}}\,p_{1}\!\left(\tfrac{x}{t},y\right)t^{n}\,dy

\displaystyle=t^{-(n+1)/2}v_{t}\!\left(\tfrac{x}{t},1\right),

where

\displaystyle v_{s}(x,t):=\int\limits_{\mathclap{B(0,1/s)}}h(y)p_{t}(x,y)\,dy.

Analogously,

\displaystyle v(x,t)=t^{-(n+1)/2}v\!\left(\tfrac{x}{t},1\right),\quad t>0,\,x\in{\mathbb{R}^{n}}.

Now we can rewrite the square function of
f
as follows:

\displaystyle(H(x))^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}_{n,1}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\int\limits_{{\mathbb{R}^{n}}}\int\limits_{{\mathbb{R}^{n}}}\frac{(P_{t}f(y)-P_{t}f(z))^{2}}{|z-y|^{n+1}}\,p_{t}(x,z)\,dydzdt

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}_{n,1}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\int\limits_{{\mathbb{R}^{n}}}\int\limits_{{\mathbb{R}^{n}}}\frac{1}{t^{n+1}}\cdot\frac{(v_{t}(\tfrac{y}{t},1)-v_{t}(\tfrac{z}{t},1))^{2}}{|z-y|^{n+1}}\,p_{t}(x,z)\,dydzdt

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}_{n,1}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\int\limits_{{\mathbb{R}^{n}}}\int\limits_{{\mathbb{R}^{n}}}\frac{1}{t}\cdot\frac{(v_{t}(y,1)-v_{t}(\tfrac{z}{t},1))^{2}}{|z-ty|^{n+1}}\,p_{t}(x,z)\,dydzdt

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}_{n,1}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\int\limits_{{\mathbb{R}^{n}}}\int\limits_{{\mathbb{R}^{n}}}t^{n-1}\cdot\frac{(v_{t}(y,1)-v_{t}(z,1))^{2}}{|tz-ty|^{n+1}}\,p_{t}(x,tz)\,dydzdt

(4.2)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}_{n,1}c_{n}\int\limits_{{\mathbb{R}^{n}}}\int\limits_{{\mathbb{R}^{n}}}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{t}\cdot\frac{(v_{t}(y,1)-v_{t}(z,1))^{2}}{|z-y|^{n+1}(t^{2}+\left\lvert tz-x\right\rvert^{2})^{(n+1)/2}}\,dtdydz.

In the last line, we used Tonelli’s theorem.
Observe that
v_{t}(y,1)\to v(y,1)>0
as
t\to 0^{+}
. Therefore, we have the following asymptotic equivalence of the integrand. For
x\neq 0
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{t}\cdot\frac{(v_{t}(y,1)-v_{t}(z,1))^{2}}{|z-y|^{n+1}(t^{2}+\left\lvert tz-x\right\rvert^{2})^{(n+1)/2}}\sim\frac{(v(y,1)-v(z,1))^{2}}{|z-y|^{n+1}\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{n+1}}\cdot\frac{1}{t},\quad\text{as }t\to 0^{+},

and when
x=0
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{t}\cdot\frac{(v_{t}(y,1)-v_{t}(z,1))^{2}}{|z-y|^{n+1}(t^{2}+\left\lvert tz-x\right\rvert^{2})^{(n+1)/2}}\sim\frac{(v(y,1)-v(z,1))^{2}}{|z-y|^{n+1}(1+\left\lvert z\right\rvert^{2})^{(n+1)/2}}\cdot\frac{1}{t^{n+2}},\quad\text{as }t\to 0^{+}.

In both cases, the inner integral of (
4.9
) diverges to infinity. Therefore,
H\equiv{\infty}
.

Proof of Theorem
4.6
.

Initially, we assume that
f\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E})
. For
p\geq 2
, Jensen’s inequality yields

\displaystyle\int\limits_{E}(H(x))^{p}\,m(dx)

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{E}\left(\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}P_{t}\Gamma[P_{t}f](x)\,dt\right)^{p/2}\,m(dx)

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{E}\left(\mathds{E}_{x}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\Gamma[P_{t}f](X_{t})\,dt\right)^{p/2}\,m(dx)

\displaystyle\leq\int\limits_{E}\mathds{E}_{x}\left(\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\Gamma[P_{t}f](X_{t})\,dt\right)^{p/2}\,m(dx)

\displaystyle=\lim_{T\to{\infty}}\int\limits_{E}\mathds{E}_{x}\left(\int\limits_{0}^{T}\Gamma[P_{t}f](X_{t})\,dt\right)^{p/2}\,m(dx).

In the last line, we used the monotone convergence theorem.
Since the semigroup is conservative, we can utilize self-duality of the process
X
(see (
2.7
)) to write

\displaystyle\int\limits_{E}(H(x))^{p}\,m(dx)

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{T\to{\infty}}\int\limits_{E}\mathds{E}_{x}\left(\int\limits_{0}^{T}\Gamma[P_{T-t}f](X_{t})dt\right)^{p/2}\,m(dx).

Let
M
be the martingale given by (
3.1
).
By the assumption
f\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E})
and Corollary
3.4
,

\displaystyle\int\limits_{E}\mathds{E}_{x}\left(\int\limits_{0}^{T}\Gamma[P_{T-t}f](X_{t})dt\right)^{p/2}\,m(dx)=2^{-p/2}\int\limits_{E}\mathds{E}_{x}(\langle M\rangle_{T})^{p/2}\,m(dx).

Employing the Burkholder–Davies–Gundy inequality (
2.36
), together with inequality (
2.37
), we obtain

\displaystyle\int\limits_{E}(H(x))^{p}\,m(dx)

\displaystyle\lesssim\lim_{T\to{\infty}}\int\limits_{E}\mathds{E}_{x}(\langle M\rangle_{T})^{p/2}\,m(dx)

\displaystyle\lesssim\lim_{T\to{\infty}}\int\limits_{E}\mathds{E}_{x}([M]_{T})^{p/2}\,m(dx)

\displaystyle\lesssim\lim_{T\to{\infty}}\int\limits_{E}\mathds{E}_{x}|M_{T}|^{p}\,m(dx)

\displaystyle=\lim_{T\to{\infty}}\int\limits_{E}\mathds{E}_{x}|f(X_{T})-P_{T}f(x)|^{p}\,m(dx).

Since
|a+b|^{p}\leq 2^{p-1}(|a|^{p}+|b|^{p})
, we get

\displaystyle\int\limits_{E}(H(x))^{p}\,m(dx)

\displaystyle\lesssim\lim_{T\to{\infty}}\int\limits_{E}(\mathds{E}_{x}|f(X_{T})|^{p}+|P_{T}f(x)|^{p})\,m(dx)

\displaystyle=\lim_{T\to{\infty}}\int\limits_{E}(P_{T}[|f|^{p}](x)+|P_{T}f(x)|^{p})\,m(dx).

Due to the contraction property of
(P_{t})
, it follows that
\mathopen{\lVert}P_{T}f\mathclose{\rVert}_{p}^{p}\leq\mathopen{\lVert}f\mathclose{\rVert}_{p}^{p}
.
By the conservativeness of
(P_{t})
, we have

\int_{E}P_{T}[|f|^{p}](x)\,m(dx)=\int_{E}|f(x)|^{p}\,m(dx)
.
Summarizing,

\displaystyle\int\limits_{E}(H(x))^{p}\,m(dx)

\displaystyle\lesssim\int\limits_{E}|f(x)|^{p}\,m(dx).

At this point, we relax the assumption
f\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E})
.
Let
f
be an arbitrary function in
L^{p}(m)
.
Since the Dirichlet form is regular, we may take the sequence
(f_{n})
of compactly supported functions from
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E})
such that it converges almost everywhere to
f
.

By
H[f_{n}]
we denote the square function
H
applied to
f_{n}
.
Then, by Fatou’s lemma,

\displaystyle\int\limits_{E}(H(x))^{p}\,m(dx)

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{n\to{\infty}}\int\limits_{E}(H[f_{n}](x))^{p}\,m(dx)

\displaystyle\lesssim\lim_{n\to{\infty}}\int\limits_{E}|f_{n}(x)|^{p}\,m(dx)=\int\limits_{E}|f(x)|^{p}\,m(dx).

∎

Proof of Theorem
4.7
.

As in the previous proof, we start with
f\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E})
. We now consider the case
p<2
; hence by Jensen’s inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle\int\limits_{E}(H(x))^{p}\,m(dx)

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{E}\left(\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}P_{t}\Gamma[P_{t}f](x)\,dt\right)^{p/2}\,m(dx)

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{E}\left(\mathds{E}_{x}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\Gamma[P_{t}f](X_{t})\,dt\right)^{p/2}\,m(dx)

\displaystyle\geq\int\limits_{E}\mathds{E}_{x}\left(\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\Gamma[P_{t}f](X_{t})\,dt\right)^{p/2}\,m(dx)

\displaystyle=\lim_{T\to{\infty}}\int\limits_{E}\mathds{E}_{x}\left(\int\limits_{0}^{T}\Gamma[P_{t}f](X_{t})\,dt\right)^{p/2}\,m(dx).

The last line follows from the monotone convergence theorem.
By self-duality of the process
X
(formula (
2.7
)),

\displaystyle\int\limits_{E}(H(x))^{p}\,m(dx)

\displaystyle\geq\lim_{T\to{\infty}}\int\limits_{E}\mathds{E}_{x}\left(\int\limits_{0}^{T}\Gamma[P_{T-t}f](X_{t})\,dt\right)^{p/2}\,m(dx).

We again employ the martingale
M
given by (
3.1
).
Given that
f\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E})
, we deduce from Corollary
3.4
that

\displaystyle\int\limits_{E}\mathds{E}_{x}\left(\int\limits_{0}^{T}\Gamma[P_{T-t}f](X_{t})dt\right)^{p/2}\,m(dx)=2^{-p/2}\int\limits_{E}\mathds{E}_{x}(\langle M\rangle_{T})^{p/2}\,m(dx),

By the Burkholder–Davies–Gundy inequality (
2.36
), together with inequality (
2.38
), we get

\displaystyle\int\limits_{E}(H(x))^{p}\,m(dx)

\displaystyle\gtrsim\lim_{T\to{\infty}}\int\limits_{E}\mathds{E}_{x}(\langle M\rangle_{T})^{p/2}\,m(dx)

\displaystyle\gtrsim\lim_{T\to{\infty}}\int\limits_{E}\mathds{E}_{x}([M]_{T})^{p/2}\,m(dx)

\displaystyle\gtrsim\lim_{T\to{\infty}}\int\limits_{E}\mathds{E}_{x}|M_{T}|^{p}\,m(dx)

\displaystyle=\lim_{T\to{\infty}}\int\limits_{E}\mathds{E}_{x}|f(X_{T})-P_{T}f(x)|^{p}\,m(dx).

By the elementary estimate
|a+b|^{p}\geq 2^{1-p}|a|^{p}-|b|^{p}
, we get

\displaystyle\int\limits_{E}(H(x))^{p}\,m(dx)

\displaystyle\gtrsim\lim_{T\to{\infty}}\int\limits_{E}(\mathds{E}_{x}|f(X_{T})|^{p}-2^{p-1}|P_{T}f(x)|^{p})\,m(dx)

\displaystyle=\lim_{T\to{\infty}}\int\limits_{E}(P_{T}[|f|^{p}](x)-2^{p-1}|P_{T}f(x)|^{p})\,m(dx).

By the conservativeness of
(P_{t})
, we have

\int_{E}P_{T}[|f|^{p}](x)\,m(dx)=\int_{E}|f(x)|^{p}\,m(dx)
.
Moreover, under Assumption
2.7
,

\lim_{T\to{\infty}}\int_{E}|P_{T}f(x)|^{p}\,m(dx)=0
. Hence, finally,

\displaystyle\int\limits_{E}(H(x))^{p}\,m(dx)

\displaystyle\gtrsim\int\limits_{E}|f(x)|^{p}\,m(dx).

We relax the assumption
f\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E})
, consider an arbitrary
f\in L^{p}(m)\cap L^{2}(m)
, and approximate
f
by
P_{s}f
for some
s>0
.
Observe that since
f\in L^{2}(m)
, we have
P_{s}f\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E})
.

Denote by
H[P_{s}f]
the square function
H
acting on
P_{s}f
, namely,
H[P_{s}f]
is given by

\displaystyle H[P_{s}f](x)=\left(\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}P_{t}\Gamma[P_{t}P_{s}f](x)\,dt\right)^{1/2}=\left(\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}P_{t}\Gamma[P_{s+t}f](x)\,dt\right)^{1/2}.

By the monotone convergence theorem,

\displaystyle(H(x))^{2}

\displaystyle=\lim_{s\to 0^{+}}\int\limits_{s}^{\infty}P_{t}\Gamma[P_{t}f](x)\,dt=\lim_{s\to 0^{+}}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}P_{s}P_{t}\Gamma[P_{t}P_{s}f](x)\,dt

(4.3)

\displaystyle=\lim_{s\to 0^{+}}P_{s}(H[P_{s}f]^{2})(x).

Since the result holds for
P_{s}f
,

\displaystyle\int\limits_{E}(H[P_{s}f](x))^{p}\,m(dx)

\displaystyle\gtrsim\int\limits_{E}|P_{s}f(x)|^{p}\,m(dx).

Combining this with the monotone convergence theorem and Jensen’s inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle\int\limits_{E}(H(x))^{p}\,m(dx)

\displaystyle=\lim_{s\to 0^{+}}\int\limits_{E}\left(P_{s}(H[P_{s}f]^{2})(x)\right)^{p/2}\,m(dx)\geq\lim_{s\to 0^{+}}\int\limits_{E}(H[P_{s}f](x))^{p}\,m(dx)

\displaystyle\gtrsim\lim_{s\to 0^{+}}\int\limits_{E}|P_{s}f(x)|^{p}\,m(dx)=\int\limits_{E}|f(x)|^{p}\,m(dx).

The last line follows from
the strong continuity of the semigroup
(P_{t})
.
∎

4.4.
Square function
\widetilde{H}

The last subsection is dedicated to the last square function:

\displaystyle\widetilde{H}(x)

\displaystyle=\left(\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}P_{t}\widetilde{\Gamma}[P_{t}f](x)\,dt\right)^{1/2}.

We conjecture that the above function resolves the issue of
L^{p}
-unboundedness, which affects
\widetilde{G}
for
p>2
(see Example
4.5
) and
H
for
1<p<2
(see Example
4.9
).
However, the method for establishing Littlewood–Paley estimates for this function remains unclear at this point.
Methods based on the Hardy–Stein identity seem to be well-suited for the functions
G
and
\widetilde{G}
,
whereas martingale methods appear to be more applicable to the function
H
.

On the one hand, we are able to obtain the upper bound directly from Theorem
4.6
and the fact that
\widetilde{H}\leq\sqrt{2}H
.

Proposition 4.10
.

Impose Assumption
2.3
. Let
2\leq p<\infty
and
f\in L^{p}(m)
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathopen{\lVert}\widetilde{H}\mathclose{\rVert}_{p}\lesssim\mathopen{\lVert}f\mathclose{\rVert}_{p}.

On the other hand, when
p
is sufficiently large for the estimate
|P_{t}f|^{p-2}\leq P_{t}(|f|^{p-2})
a.e. to hold, one may adapt the procedure from the proof of Theorem
4.2
, which is based on the Hardy–Stein identity, to obtain the lower bound of
\widetilde{H}
.
Nevertheless, it remains an open question whether this statement holds for
p\in(2,3)
.

Proposition 4.11
.

Impose Assumption
2.7
.
Let
3\leq p<\infty
and
f\in L^{p}(m)
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathopen{\lVert}f\mathclose{\rVert}_{p}\lesssim\mathopen{\lVert}\widetilde{H}\mathclose{\rVert}_{p}.

Proof.

Since
p\geq 3
, the function
a\mapsto|a|^{p-2}
is convex. Thus, by Jensen’s inequality,

|P_{t}f|^{p-2}\leq P_{t}(|f|^{p-2})
a.e.
Therefore, by (
2.25
), the symmetry of the operator
P_{t}
, and Hölder’s inequality

\displaystyle\int\limits_{E}|f(x)|^{p}\,m(dx)

\displaystyle\asymp\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\int\limits_{E}\widetilde{\Gamma}[P_{t}f](x)|P_{t}f(x)|^{p-2}\,m(dx)dt

\displaystyle\leq\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\int\limits_{E}\widetilde{\Gamma}[P_{t}f](x)P_{t}(|f|^{p-2})(x)\,m(dx)dt

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\int\limits_{E}P_{t}\widetilde{\Gamma}[P_{t}f](x)|f(x)|^{p-2}\,m(dx)dt

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{E}(\widetilde{H}(x))^{2}|f(x)|^{p-2}\,m(dx)

\displaystyle\leq\biggl(\int\limits_{E}(\widetilde{H}(x))^{p}\,m(dx)\biggr)^{2/p}\biggl(\int\limits_{E}|f(x)|^{p}\,m(dx)\biggr)^{1-2/p}.

This implies

\displaystyle\int\limits_{E}|f(x)|^{p}\,m(dx)

\displaystyle\lesssim\int\limits_{E}(\widetilde{H}(x))^{p}\,m(dx).

∎
