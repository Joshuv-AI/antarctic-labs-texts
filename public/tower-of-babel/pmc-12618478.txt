Composite dietary antioxidant index is inversely associated with all-cause, cardiovascular and cancer mortality in adults with hyperlipidemia
Authors: Yingcong Liang, Changhong Shi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
To explore the association between the Composite Dietary Antioxidant Index (CDAI) and all-cause, cardiovascular disease (CVD), and cancer mortality in U.S. adults with hyperlipidemia, and to assess the potential mediating role of inflammation-related biomarkers in this association. 25,383 patients with hyperlipidemia from the 2001–2018 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) were included. The association between CDAI and mortality was assessed using weighted Cox regression models, Kaplan-Meier survival curves, subgroup analyses, and sensitivity analyses. Mediation analysis evaluated the roles of leucocytes, neutrophils, monocytes, lymphocytes, and the systemic inflammatory response index (SIRI). A total of 3,810 all-cause deaths, 1,218 CVD deaths, and 883 cancer deaths were recorded during the follow-up period. Fully adjusted models showed that highest CDAI (Q4 group) compared to the lowest quartile (Q1) was associated with a lower risk of all-cause (HR = 0.79, 95% CI: 0.68–0.91, P = 0.001), CVD (HR = 0.73, 95% CI: 0.55–0.96, P = 0.026) and cancer (HR = 0.72, 95% CI: 0.55–0.93, P = 0.011) mortality. The protective effect of vitamin E on the risk of death was most significant. The results of subgroup and sensitivity analyses were robust. Inflammatory indicators mediated the association between CDAI and mortality, with a range of changes from 1.11% to 4.12%. Higher CDAI, especially vitamin E intake, was independently associated with a significant reduction in all-cause, CVD, and cancer mortality in hyperlipidemic patients. Introduction

Hyperlipidemia, characterized by abnormal lipid levels in the bloodstream, is a major risk factor for cardiovascular disease (CVD), type 2 diabetes, and stroke, contributing significantly to global morbidity and mortality1–3. It has become an increasing public health concern not only in Western countries such as the United States and Europe but also across many developing countries. According to a recent epidemiologic survey, nearly 25 million adults in the United States were affected by hyperlipidemia between 2017 and 20204. This underscores the urgent need for effective strategies to address this widespread issue. Although pharmacological treatments are widely used, non-pharmacological strategies—particularly nutritional interventions—have emerged as essential components in comprehensive lipid management5.

Oxidative stress is a physiological condition characterized by an imbalance in the oxidative-reduction status within the cells or the entire body, leading to overproduction of reactive oxygen species (ROS). It plays a critical role in various conditions such as obesity, insulin resistance, hyperglycemia, chronic inflammation, and hyperlipidemia6–12. Emerging evidence suggests that increasing antioxidant intake in the diet may be a promising strategy to manage hyperlipidemia by reducing oxidative stress levels in the body13–16. For example, in a study in children and adolescents, Chakrabarti et al. demonstrated that supplements with vitamin A and vitamin E significantly reduce blood lipids through their antioxidant properties17. Similarly, a cross-sectional study from Korea conducted by Kim et al. found a significant association between dietary total antioxidant capacity and prevalence of hyperlipidemia18.

The Composite Dietary Antioxidant Index (CDAI) was developed to assess overall dietary antioxidant intake by evaluating six key nutrients: zinc, selenium, total carotenoids, and vitamins A, C, and E. It has been associated with a reduced risk of hypertension, heart failure, and depression19–23. More recently, higher CDAI scores have been associated with improved survival outcomes and a reduced risk of CVD and cancer mortality, particularly among individuals with hypertension or metabolic disorders24. However, little is known about the role of CDAI in predicting mortality outcomes specifically among individuals with hyperlipidemia. Furthermore, whether the association between CDAI and mortality is mediated through systemic inflammation has not been fully investigated. Given that inflammation-related biomarkers such as leukocytes, neutrophils, monocytes, and the systemic inflammation response index (SIRI) are associated with increased mortality risk25–29, investigating their potential mediating role is of both scientific and clinical interest.

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the association between CDAI and all-cause, CVD, and cancer mortality among U.S. adults with hyperlipidemia, using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 2001–2018. We further explored whether systemic inflammation serves as a potential mediator in this association, providing novel insights for targeted dietary strategies in hyperlipidemia management.

Materials and methods

Study population

Data for this study were obtained from NHANES, a program conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) employing a complex, multistage, probability sampling design to collect nationally representative health and nutritional data from the U.S. civilian, noninstitutionalized population. The NHANES combines structured household interviews, comprehensive physical examinations, and laboratory tests. All procedures were approved by the NCHS Ethics Review Board, and informed consent was obtained from all participants.

We included data from 9 NHANES survey cycles spanning 2001 to 2018. Participants aged 18 years or older, excluding pregnant and lactating women, who had a clinical diagnosis of hyperlipidemia and complete information on dietary antioxidant intake, demographic variables, laboratory data, and mortality outcomes were eligible for inclusion. Individuals with missing key variables or identifiers were excluded (Fig. 1). A total of 25,383 participants met the inclusion criteria. All analyses were weighted using appropriate sample weights to ensure national representativeness.

Evaluation of CDAI

Dietary intake was assessed through two 24-hour dietary recall interviews: the first was conducted in person at a mobile examination center (MEC), and the second via telephone 3–10 days later. For this analysis, the average of the two dietary recalls was used. The CDAI was calculated using the method developed by Wright et al.19, incorporating the intake of six dietary antioxidants: total carotenoids, zinc, selenium, and vitamins A, C, and E, based solely on food consumption (supplemental intake was excluded). Definition of hyperlipidemia

Hyperlipidemia was defined in accordance with the National Cholesterol Education Program Adult Treatment Panel III (NCEP ATP III) guidelines as meeting one or more of the following criteria30: LDL-C ≥ 130 mg/dL, TC ≥ 200 mg/dL, TG ≥ 150 mg/dL, or HDL-C < 40 mg/dL in men and < 50 mg/dL in women. Participants were also classified as hyperlipidemic if they self-reported a diagnosis or were receiving lipid-lowering medications. The definition of hyperlipidemia was primarily based on laboratory criteria, with self-reported diagnosis or medication use serving as supplementary information.

Ascertainment of mortality

The NCHS oversaw the NHANES program, which connected the survival odds of participants to the National Death Index (NDI). The NCHS Ethics Review Board approved the connection method, and all personal data were protected to ensure privacy. All-cause, CVD, and cancer mortality were the primary outcomes. Death from heart disease or cerebrovascular disease was referred to as CVD mortality.

Covariates

Based on previous prospective studies, covariates were selected based previous literature and include gender, age, race, marital status, education level, poverty-to-income ratio (PIR), smoking and drinking status, liver disease, chronic kidney disease (CKD), diabetes, hypertension, CVD, healthy eating index-2020 (HEI-2020), energy intake, body mass index (BMI), leucocyte count (total leucocytes, neutrophils, monocytes, lymphocytes), and SIRI, which is computed by dividing the multiplication of neutrophil and monocyte counts by the number of lymphocytes. BMI was categorized as ≥ 30, 25–29.9, and < 25 kg/m². Drinking status was defined as consumption of ≥ 12 drinks in the previous year, and smoking status was classified as never, former, or current smoker31,32. Liver disease was identified based on self-reported physician diagnoses and laboratory indicators33,34. CKD was defined by abnormal levels of urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio and estimated glomerular filtration rate35. Self-reported medical diagnosis, insulin or hypoglycemic medication usage, and abnormal glycated hemoglobin or glucose levels were used to characterize diabetes. To diagnose hypertension, a self-reported diagnosis or antihypertensive prescription was combined with an elevated blood pressure result. CVD, which includes coronary artery disease, heart failure, myocardial infarction, stroke, or angina, was established by self-reported medical diagnoses. HEI-2020 was developed based on the Dietary Guidelines for Americans, and its calculation involves 13 components36,37.

Statistical analysis

The research used the appropriate sample weights to ensure estimates that adequately represented the country and considered the complex survey structure. Categorical variables were presented as percentages (%), while continuous variables were presented as means ± standard errors. A one-way ANOVA was used to compare groups for continuous variables, and a chi-square test for categorical variables.

Survival differences were visualized using Kaplan-Meier survival curves, and hazard ratios (HRs) for mortality were estimated using weighted Cox proportional hazards regression models. Three models were constructed: Model 1 (unadjusted), Model 2 (adjusted for gender, age, race, marital status, education level, and PIR), and Model 3 (further adjusted for liver disease, CKD, diabetes, hypertension, CVD, smoking and drinking status, BMI, HEI-2020, and energy intake). In addition, the proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals, and no violations were detected (P-values > 0.05), indicating the validity of the Cox models.

Subgroup analyses and sensitivity analyses were performed to test the robustness of the results. Additionally, the relationship between inflammatory markers (leukocytes, neutrophils, monocytes, lymphocytes, and SIRI) and CDAI and mortality was investigated using generalized linear models and Cox regression analysis. The mediation proportion was calculated as the ratio of the indirect to total effect. Evidence of a mediating impact was defined as a positive percentage of mediation, a significant indirect effect, and total effect29. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.4.3), with a P-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Study population’s characteristics

Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of 25,383 adults with hyperlipidemia, stratified by CDAI quartiles. The CDAI ranged from − 7.89 to 45.37, with quartiles defined as Q1 (−7.89 to −2.59), Q2 (−2.59 to −0.60), Q3 (−0.60 to 1.87), and Q4 (1.87 to 45.37). Compared with the lowest quartile (Q1), individuals in the highest quartile (Q4) were more likely to be younger (P < 0.001), male (P < 0.001), non-Hispanic White (P < 0.001), married (P < 0.001), highly educated (P < 0.001), and have a higher income (P < 0.001). Additionally, Q4 participants had lower smoking rates (P < 0.001), lower BMI (P < 0.001), higher HEI-2020 (P < 0.001), and fewer comorbid conditions, including liver disease, CKD, diabetes, hypertension, and CVD (P < 0.05). In terms of dietary intake, individuals in the survival group consumed higher levels of antioxidants (e.g., total carotenoids, zinc, selenium, and vitamin E) than those in the mortality group (Table S1).

Kaplan-Meier survival curve

During a median follow-up of 111 months, there were 3,810 all-cause deaths, 1,218 deaths from CVD, and 883 deaths from cancer. Figure 2 displays the Kaplan-Meier survival curves for all-cause, CVD, and cancer mortality, stratified by CDAI quartiles. Participants in Q4 of CDAI had significantly lower mortality rates compared with those in Q1 for all-cause mortality (log-rank test, P < 0.001), CVD mortality (P < 0.001), and cancer mortality (P < 0.001).

Association between CDAI and mortality

Multivariable-adjusted Cox regression analysis (Table 2) revealed that a higher CDAI was associated with a significantly reduced risk of all-cause, CVD, and cancer mortality. Specifically, compared with Q1, participants in Q4 had a 21% lower risk of all-cause mortality (HR = 0.79, 95% CI: 0.68–0.91, P = 0.001), a 27% lower risk of CVD mortality (HR = 0.73, 95% CI: 0.55–0.96, P = 0.026), and a 28% lower risk of cancer mortality (HR = 0.72, 95% CI: 0.55–0.93, P = 0.011). The association between CDAI and mortality was consistent across all subgroups analyzed, including by age, sex, and BMI.

Subgroup analyses and sensitivity analyses

Subgroup analyses were conducted to evaluate whether the association between CDAI and mortality differed across key demographic and clinical subgroups (Fig. 3). These analyses revealed no significant interactions between CDAI and mortality risk based on sex or race (P for interaction > 0.05). However, a stronger inverse association between CDAI and both all-cause and CVD mortality was observed among individuals without diabetes compared to those with diabetes, and this difference was statistically significant (P for interaction < 0.05).

Three sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the findings. First, to minimize potential reporting bias associated with self-reported hyperlipidemia diagnoses, participants who self-reported hyperlipidemia were excluded, and the results remained consistent with the primary analysis (Table S2). Second, when participants who died within the first two years of follow-up were excluded, the association between CDAI and mortality did not substantially change, suggesting that the observed relationship was unlikely to be influenced by early mortality (Table S3). In addition, given the long follow-up period, changes in diet and lifestyle may have introduced regression dilution bias38. To account for this, participants with follow-up durations of > 18 and > 15 years were excluded in sensitivity analyses (Table S4). The associations remained consistent, though became stronger, suggesting reduced predictive power of baseline CDAI for long-term mortality.

Association of inflammation-related markers with CDAI and mortality

We further investigated the relationship between inflammation-related biomarkers and CDAI, as well as their association with mortality outcomes. Table 4 presents the results of generalized linear models assessing the associations between CDAI and inflammation-related markers, including total leukocytes, neutrophils, monocytes, lymphocytes, and SIRI. CDAI was inversely associated with several inflammation-related markers. Specifically, higher CDAI scores were significantly associated with lower levels of total leukocytes (β = −0.02, 95% CI: −0.03 to − 0.01, P = 0.001), neutrophils (β = −0.02, 95% CI: −0.03 to − 0.01, P = 0.001), monocytes (β = −0.001, 95% CI: −0.003 to − 0.0003, P = 0.011), and SIRI (β = −0.007, 95% CI: −0.01 to − 0.002, P = 0.007). However, no significant associations were observed with lymphocyte levels.

In terms of mortality, inflammation-related markers such as leukocytes, neutrophils, and SIRI were significantly associated with increased risk of all-cause and CVD mortality (Table 5). For example, higher neutrophil levels were associated with a 7% increased risk of all-cause mortality (HR = 1.07, 95% CI: 1.05–1.08, P < 0.001) and a 14% increased risk of CVD mortality (HR = 1.14, 95% CI: 1.09–1.20, P < 0.001). Similarly, higher SIRI was associated with an 18% increased risk of all-cause mortality (HR = 1.18, 95% CI: 1.13–1.22, P < 0.001) and a 36% increased risk of CVD mortality (HR = 1.36, 95% CI: 1.28–1.45, P < 0.001).

Mediating role of inflammation-related indicators

Mediation analysis was performed to investigate whether inflammatory biomarkers mediated the association between CDAI and mortality. Inflammatory markers, including leukocytes, neutrophils, monocytes, lymphocytes, and SIRI, were considered as potential mediators of the relationship between CDAI and all-cause, CVD, and cancer mortality. The results revealed that inflammatory markers partially mediated the association between CDAI and both all-cause (Fig. 4) and CVD mortality (Fig. 5). Specifically, neutrophils mediated 4.12% of the association between CDAI and all-cause mortality, while SIRI accounted for 3.60%. Leukocytes and monocytes also contributed to the mediation effect, with the proportion of mediation ranging from 1.21% to 1.61%. In addition, inflammatory biomarkers significantly mediated the association between CDAI and CVD mortality, ranging from 1.11% to 3.35%. However, no significant mediation effect was observed for cancer mortality (Table S5). Results of the sensitivity analysis support the robustness of the mediation results. Table S6 and Table S7 show the results of mediation analysis after excluding the individuals who died within 1 and 2 years of follow-up.

Discussion

This study provides compelling evidence that higher CDAI, reflecting increased dietary antioxidant intake, is associated with reduced all-cause, CVD, and cancer mortality in U.S. adults with hyperlipidemia. Our findings align with previous studies indicating the protective effects of antioxidant-rich diets in individuals at high risk for chronic diseases, particularly CVD and cancer19–23. Additionally, we found that systemic inflammation may partially mediate the relationship between CDAI and mortality, suggesting that antioxidants exert their protective effects through the reduction of inflammation. Similar results were found in other cohort studies, where higher antioxidant intake was linked to a lower risk of CVD and cancer mortality39–42.

The association between CDAI and mortality risk in individuals with hyperlipidemia is likely due to the combined effects of multiple antioxidant nutrients. Among the components of CDAI, vitamin E emerged as the most consistently protective nutrient, showing significant associations with reduced all-cause, CVD, and cancer mortality. These findings are consistent with previous studies that have demonstrated higher vitamin E intake or supplementation is associated with improved survival outcomes, particularly for CVD and cancer-related mortality24,43. For example, a meta-analysis by Bjelakovic et al. reported that vitamin E supplementation is associated with a significant reduction in the risk of mortality from CVD events44. The relatively stronger association observed for vitamin E may be explained by its biological properties. As a fat-soluble antioxidant, vitamin E is incorporated into lipid membranes, where it plays a key role in interrupting lipid peroxidation and protecting membrane integrity45. This localization enables it to effectively scavenge lipid-soluble reactive oxygen species. In addition, vitamin E has been reported to influence lipid metabolism and modulate inflammatory processes, which are central to the development of atherosclerosis and other chronic diseases46. These mechanistic features may underlie the comparatively stronger association observed between vitamin E intake and mortality outcomes in this study.

One of the key contributions of this study is the exploration of systemic inflammation as a mediator of the relationship between CDAI and mortality. Inflammation is a well-established driver of many chronic diseases, including CVD and cancer47,48. Our findings suggest that dietary antioxidants, represented by CDAI, may reduce systemic inflammation, which in turn contributes to lower mortality risk. Specifically, we found that inflammatory biomarkers, such as neutrophils and SIRI, mediated a small proportion of the association between CDAI and both all-cause and CVD mortality. These observations are consistent with previous studies reporting that higher levels of systemic inflammation, such as the systemic immunity-inflammation index, are associated with increased mortality risk in individuals with hyperlipidemia9–11. These results provide preliminary support for the hypothesis that antioxidant-rich diets may influence mortality through anti-inflammatory mechanisms. Although the mediation pathways through inflammatory markers were statistically significant, the effect sizes were modest (1.11%–4.12%). This indicates that systemic inflammation plays only a minor mediating role and is unlikely to be the primary mechanism through which dietary antioxidants influence mortality risk. The overall protective association observed with higher CDAI likely reflects a combination of biological pathways, including oxidative stress reduction, metabolic regulation, and immune modulation. Further studies are warranted to disentangle these mechanisms and better characterize their relative contributions.

Subgroup analyses further revealed heterogeneity in the association between CDAI and mortality across different populations. Notably, the inverse associations between CDAI and both all-cause and CVD mortality were more pronounced among individuals without diabetes, whereas the associations were attenuated in those with diabetes. These findings suggest that the protective effects of dietary antioxidants may be stronger in non-diabetic individuals, potentially because dietary restrictions, insulin use, or hypoglycemic medications in diabetic patients may impair the efficacy of dietary antioxidants49. Furthermore, the co-occurrence of hyperglycemia and hyperlipidemia in individuals with diabetes may intensify oxidative stress and systemic inflammation, thereby weaken the body’s antioxidant defense system and reduce the bioavailability and protective efficacy of dietary antioxidants50. These findings highlight the importance of considering comorbid metabolic conditions when evaluating the effect of dietary exposures.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, due to the observational design of this study, causal relationships cannot be inferred. Although adjustments were made for multiple covariates, unmeasured or residual confounding may still be present. Future randomized controlled trials are necessary to confirm the observed associations. Second, the study population consisted exclusively of U.S. adults, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to populations with different dietary habits, cultural backgrounds, or genetic predispositions. Third, dietary intake data were based on self-reported 24-hour recalls, which are subject to recall bias and may not accurately reflect habitual intake. While we used the average of two recall days where available, misclassification remains possible. Furthermore, CDAI was calculated based on food-derived antioxidants only, excluding intake from dietary supplements. This may have led to an underestimation of total antioxidant exposure, particularly in older adults or individuals with chronic conditions who are more likely to use supplements. In addition, hyperlipidemia was primarily defined using laboratory criteria, with supplemental identification based on self-reported diagnosis and medication use. This composite definition may have introduced misclassification bias, although sensitivity analyses excluding self-report–based cases yielded consistent results. Moreover, given the long follow-up period (approximately 20 years), participants’ dietary patterns and health behaviors may have changed over time, potentially leading to regression dilution bias and attenuation of the observed associations. Finally, although we adjusted for key confounders, certain influential factors—such as physical activity, medication adherence, and socio-environmental variables—were not fully captured in the dataset. As such, residual confounding and potential reverse causation cannot be completely ruled out.

Despite these limitations, this study provides valuable insights into the potential role of dietary antioxidants in reducing mortality risk among individuals with hyperlipidemia. The mediating role of inflammation further highlights the importance of inflammation-targeted dietary interventions for managing hyperlipidemia and preventing related chronic diseases. Future studies, particularly randomized controlled trials, will be crucial to confirm the mechanisms through which antioxidants influence mortality and to develop evidence-based dietary guidelines for high-risk populations.

Conclusions

In conclusion, higher CDAI, particularly through increased intake of antioxidants such as vitamin E, is associated with reduced mortality from all-cause, CVD and cancer in hyperlipidemic adults. Inflammation may partially mediate this association, with a modest mediation effect. These findings suggest that dietary interventions focused on increasing antioxidant intake may play a key role in managing hyperlipidemia and reducing mortality in high-risk populations.
