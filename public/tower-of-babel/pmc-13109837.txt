Evaluating ChatGPT's Adherence to Medical Ethics: A Prerequisite for Artificial Intelligence in Medicine
Authors: Ying Zhang, Yuyang Liu, Tingyu Lv, Junhui Wang, Hui Liu, Xiaoying Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAs artificial intelligence continues to play an expanding role in healthcare, ensuring its compliance with medical ethics is essential. However, the ethical performance of artificial intelligence in medical contexts remains insufficiently studied. This study aimed to evaluate the ability of ChatGPT to address questions related to medical ethics and to compare its performance with that of human experts.MethodsA Medical Ethics Evaluation dataset was developed, consisting of 465 single‐choice questions derived from a range of medical ethics standards. These questions were used to assess two artificial intelligence models, GPT‐3.5 and GPT‐4. Model responses were compared with those provided by two medical ethics experts. Each test was conducted independently twice to ensure consistency. Accuracy was calculated for each model and expert, and chi‐square tests were used to compare differences in performance.ResultsGPT‐3.5 achieved an overall accuracy of 38.92%, while GPT‐4 achieved 27.10%. In comparison, two medical ethics experts achieved substantially higher accuracies of 86.23% and 78.32%, respectively. Both experts performed significantly better than GPT‐3.5 and GPT‐4. These findings indicate a substantial gap between artificial intelligence models and human experts in understanding and applying medical ethics principles. The relatively low performance of the models, compared with their reported strengths in diagnostic tasks, may reflect the complexity and nuance of ethical reasoning in medicine. Nevertheless, the large language models showed some ability to align with core medical ethics principles, particularly in ethical dilemma scenarios, and were also able to generate responses that addressed psychological needs.ConclusionsArtificial intelligence models currently show limited accuracy in medical ethics decision‐making compared with human experts. Although these models demonstrate some alignment with fundamental ethical principles, the performance is not yet sufficient for reliable use in ethically sensitive medical contexts. Further optimization is needed to improve their ability to meet the ethical demands of medical practice.Keywords: artificial intelligence, ChatGPT, ethical dilemmas, medical ethics

Background

AI has enormous potential for enhancing diagnostic clinical care, health research, and the implementation of various public health interventions [1]. LLMs represent cutting‐edge AI technology capable of autonomously learning from diverse knowledge sources, including books, literature, and the Internet, with promising applications in the medical field [2]. One notable example is GPT‐4, a dialogue‐based AI developed by OpenAI and released in March 2023, recognized for its proficiency in addressing multidisciplinary questions. Previous studies have demonstrated GPT‐4′s strong performance in medical examinations, including passing performance across multiple years of the Japanese National Medical Licensing Examination, as well as promising results in specialty‐specific domains such as cardiology and radiology [3, 4, 5, 6]. Moreover, LLMs exhibit unexpected learning capabilities and scalability in the medical field through fine‐tuning methods, suggesting their potential to catalyze breakthroughs in medical research and clinical support. Anticipated applications encompass medical knowledge acquisition, scientific research article composition, patient communication, and more [7].

The safe deployment of new technologies, including AI, can help the world achieve the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals [8]. While contributing to advancements in medicine, LLMs also pose a series of ethical challenges in areas such as privacy, data security, the enduring digital divide, the collection of data that incorporates clinical biases, and a lack of treatment options after diagnosis. The rapid progress of LLMs technology has outpaced ethical research, resulting in numerous ethical dilemmas that hinder LLMs development and risk harm to individuals. Medical ethics is one of the fastest‐evolving and most contentious fields within applied ethics, extending well beyond traditional boundaries to encompass ethical considerations in biology, medicine, environmental sciences, education, scientific research, economics, and anthropology [9]. Adherence to fundamental ethical principles is essential not only for qualified physicians but also for the responsible development of medical AI technologies [10, 11].

Validating the potential application of AI techniques in medical practice through performance analysis on human question sets is an effective approach [12]. The United States Medical Licensing Examination (USMLE) is a pivotal assessment that evaluates a wide range of skills essential for measuring candidates’ medical knowledge and their ability to navigate complex interpersonal scenarios and ethical judgments [13]. Recent studies have examined the performance of LLMs on the USMLE. Kung et al. evaluated GPT's performance on the USMLE and found that it approached or met the passing threshold for all three sub‐exams without specialized training or reinforcement [14]. Gilson assessed GPT's performance specifically on questions within the scope of the USMLE Step 1 and Step 2 exams, highlighting GPT as its improvement in natural language processing models for medical question‐answering tasks [15]. In addition to using comprehensive test questions to assess LLMs, accuracy analyses in subdivisions such as ophthalmology and soft skills have also been conducted. Haddad et al. studied the ability of GPT‐3.5 and GPT‐4 to answer ophthalmology‐related questions across different levels of ophthalmology training [16]. Brin et al. explored GPT's performance on soft skills using the USMLE, indicating the potential of LLMs to meet the complex interpersonal, ethical, and professional demands inherent in medical practice [17]. While there is preliminary consensus on LLMs’ performance in the USMLE, further exploration is needed to assess their effectiveness in addressing issues related to medical ethics. The detection and monitoring of AIs in terms of medical ethics are crucial, as several risk factors may arise if AI's adherence to medical ethics is overlooked. Previous research demonstrates that governments and companies could violate human rights and harm health by using AI without a focus on medical ethics [17]. If the scope of medical ethics is not clearly defined, responsibility may become blurred when algorithms or data used to train AI technology make mistakes [18]. In unequal societies, AI may exhibit biases toward majorities; societal biases and discrimination are often replicated by AI technologies, potentially becoming normative [19]. Establishing AI's medical ethics principles could have significant implications for safety, as issues such as algorithm hacking may jeopardize patient safety if AI usage is not carefully managed [20].

To address these identified issues, we collected medical ethics questions from diverse sources and curated a comprehensive MEE dataset of ethical scenarios based on expert insights. Utilizing this dataset, we conducted a detailed comparison of the overall and subclassification accuracy of GPT‐3.5 and GPT‐4, facilitating a thorough performance evaluation of LLMs in the domain of medical ethics. Additionally, we performed an interpretability analysis to examine how LLMs make decisions when confronted with specific ethical dilemmas.

It is imperative to acknowledge that contrasting perceptions of medical ethical issues may emerge across different cultural contexts. As an initial exploration of these ethical concerns, the present study seeks to conducting relatively objective researches by delineating three specific region: (1) the standardized responses within the test set are derived from a predominantly western cultural framework, specifically European and American perspectives; (2) the test set is comprised exclusively of choice questions, thereby enabling a quantitative descriptive analysis; (3) the data in the test set, including the questions posed, standardized answers, and test responses, are restricted to the English language to mitigate discrepancies in comprehension that may arise from translation between languages.

Materials and Methods

Medical Ethics Question Dataset

The USMLE was developed by the National Board of Medical Examiners (NBME) and consists of three steps: (1) assesses basic medical subjects, (2) evaluates clinical knowledge (CK), and (3) focuses on clinical skills (CS). Step 1 is specifically designed to gauge fundamental science knowledge and is widely utilized by medical education programs for candidate screening during the application process. The exam questions within Step 1 are categorized into 10 sections based on subject matter, with behavioral science encompassing the empirical study of human behavior and its underlying laws. Behavioral science within the USMLE context spans various fields such as medical ethics, sociology, anthropology, and psychiatry, reflecting its interdisciplinary and comprehensive nature.

For this study, we compiled the MEE dataset from four primary sources: USMLE sample materials provided on the official USMLE website, Kaplan Medical USMLE question bank resources, First Aid for the USMLE Step 1, and the AMBOSS question bank (Table S1). A total of 542 single‐choice questions related to medical ethics were collected from these sources and subsequently reviewed by experts in the field. After eliminating duplicate and low‐quality entries, we curated a final test set consisting of 465 high‐quality medical ethics questions. Specifically, the contributions to the dataset included 30 questions from USMLE, 172 from Kaplan, 135 from First Aid, and 128 from AMBOSS.

To ensure the quality and reliability of the MEE dataset, we applied a systematic three‐step filtering process for identifying and resolving low‐quality or inconsistent entries: (1) all collected questions were screened for semantic duplication using cosine similarity and manual inspection. Questions with ≥ 95% similarity in content, context, and options were deemed redundant. In such cases, only the most complete and unambiguous version was retained. (2) Each question was evaluated based on the clarity of language; validity of ethical reasoning, ambiguous phrasing, or logically inconsistent answer options were excluded. (3) The remaining questions were independently reviewed by two experts holding either a doctoral degree in medical ethics or an associate senior professional title (equivalent to associate professor or above). Each expert assessed the validity and standard answer for every item. In cases of disagreement—whether in answer key selection or quality rating—a third expert with equivalent qualifications was consulted as an independent arbitrator. This arbitration process involved: (a) reaching consensus on the gold standard answer when disputed; (b) refining the question or answer wording to improve clarity; (c) flagging unresolved items for exclusion if consensus was not achieved.

Only items that passed this three‐tiered review process were retained in the final dataset. This rigorous methodology ensured that all included questions were ethically sound, unambiguous, and suitable for comparative evaluation of AI and human performance.

Answer Generated by GPT

Prompt Construction

Each question and its corresponding answer choices were organized into a prompt that serves as the input for GPT‐3.5 and GPT‐4 to generate relevant responses. To enhance the accuracy of the LLMs in answering medical ethics questions, we developed specific prompt structures that included pertinent informative factors. Furthermore, questions derived from different datasets were standardized into a uniform input format. This standardization ensured that the accuracy of the LLM‐generated answers depended solely on its ability to apply medical knowledge to ethical dilemmas, rather than on its text parsing capabilities. The prompt structure consisted of three key components: the role setting of the LLMs, the medical questions, and the answer options (Figure 1).

Model Selection

We selected GPT‐3.5 and GPT‐4, developed by OpenAI, as representative examples of contemporary language models to evaluate their performance in medical ethics. These models are capable of processing and generating text that mimics human‐like conversations by predicting the next word (token) based on a given input. GPT‐3.5 is an earlier version with approximately 175 billion parameters, while GPT‐4 is a more recent, subscription‐based version that shares a similar architecture but features a larger parameter size.

Performance Testing

To evaluate the stability and consistency of the models, each prompt was independently processed by GPT‐3.5 and GPT‐4 twice. Additionally, two medical ethics experts were tasked with answering all questions in the MEE dataset under controlled conditions, without access to external references, within a designated 8‐h time frame. The study engaged two certified medical ethics experts with over 5 years of clinical ethics experience. Although the number of experts is limited, their divergent accuracy profiles reflect the inherent variability in ethical interpretation. Future studies will expand the expert cohort to enhance representativeness and reduce potential bias. Our analysis will include intra‐class comparisons among the LLMs and inter‐class comparisons between the LLMs and the experts. The MEE dataset is organized based on question type (conceptual and situational questions) and subject content (key aspects of medical ethics, interactions between physicians and patients, physicians and society, physicians and colleagues, and medical research). Subsequently, we evaluated the performance of GPT‐3.5, GPT‐4, and the experts across various metrics, including overall performance, response patterns, question categories, and specialized topics. Finally, we assessed the consistency of responses generated by GPT‐3.5 and GPT‐4.

Model Settings and Parameter Sensitivity Analysis

To ensure transparency and reproducibility, we provide a detailed description of the model configurations and inference parameters used in this study. Both GPT‐3.5 and GPT‐4 were accessed via the OpenAI API in October 2024, using the GPT‐3.5(gpt‐3.5‐turbo‐0613) and the GPT‐4(GPT‐4‐0613). The models were prompted through a standardized pipeline, without any system message customization, and processed in text‐only mode with a context window of 4096 tokens. We set the temperature parameter to 0.7 by default, consistent with prior studies aiming to balance coherence and output diversity. However, given GPT‐4′s tendency to generate multi‐option or ambiguous responses, we conducted a parameter sensitivity analysis on a randomly selected subset (n = 93, 20% of the dataset). The same questions were tested at three temperature values: 0.3, 0.7, and 1.0. Table 1 summarizes the accuracy and uncertainty rates observed across temperatures. For the main test, the temperature was fixed at 0.7, which is the default and widely used value for balanced creativity and coherence.

Results

Overall Performance Under Rigid Standards

All questions in the constructed MEE dataset are single‐choice items, with one option designated as the gold standard. In certain cases, LLMs generated multiple responses based on complex reasoning processes, rather than providing a single answer as prompted. To evaluate overall performance, this study established a rigorous classification criterion consisting of correct answers, uncertain answers, and incorrect answers. A correct answer is defined as a generated response that precisely matches the gold standard. An uncertain answer indicates that one of the multiple generated responses includes the gold standard. An incorrect answer refers to a response that either does not contain the gold standard or fails to provide any answer whatsoever.

The overall accuracy rates based on stringent criteria are presented in Table 2. Specifically, GPT‐3.5 achieved an overall precision of 38.92% in addressing medical ethics questions, with an uncertain answer occurrence rate of 1.62%. In contrast, GPT‐4 attained an overall precision of 27.10%, accompanied by a higher occurrence rate of uncertain answers at 15.27%. The experts demonstrated statistically higher precision rates of 86.23% and 78.32% on the MEE dataset. Detailed results are provided in Table 2.

Performance of Different Question Type

The MEE dataset is categorized into conceptual and situational questions based on distinct evaluation methods (see Appendix 1). Conceptual questions concentrate on fundamental principles of medical ethics, with answer choices crafted to probe underlying principles and essential knowledge. These questions primarily assess the understanding and application of basic concepts in medical ethics. In contrast, situational questions present additional background information, requiring examinees to select the most appropriate option based on the given context. These questions evaluate the ability to apply knowledge through situational analysis.

Experts demonstrated superior performance compared to both GPT‐3.5 and GPT‐4 across conceptual and situational questions. Notably, both GPT‐3.5 and GPT‐4 showed greater proficiency in addressing conceptual questions than situational ones. Additionally, the AI models exhibited a lower rate of uncertain responses for conceptual questions compared to situational questions. Specifically, in the context of situational questions, GPT‐4 displayed a markedly higher percentage of uncertain responses (33.17%) compared to GPT‐3.5 (3.90%), along with a lower accuracy rate for GPT‐4 (26.34%) in contrast to GPT‐3.5 (38.05%) (Table 2 and Figure 2).

Performance of Different Categories

We calculated and compared the rates of correct answers based on the Handbook of Medical Ethics published by the World Medical Association (WMA), dividing the MEE into five sections according to semantic content: Principal Features of Medical Ethics, Physicians and Patients, Physicians and Society, Physicians and Colleagues, and Medical Research [21].

Using the WMA Medical Ethics Manual as a framework, we categorized the questions in the MEE dataset into five distinct classes and compared the accuracy of GPT‐3.5 and GPT‐4 across these categories. In the four major categories—Principal Features of Medical Ethics (47.00% for GPT‐3.5, 39.00% for GPT‐4), Physicians and Patients (44.34% for GPT‐3.5, 24.53% for GPT‐4), Physicians and Society (30.00% for GPT‐3.5, 18.00% for GPT‐4), and Medical Research (47.47% for GPT‐3.5, 32.32% for GPT‐4)—GPT‐3.5 consistently demonstrated higher accuracy rates than GPT‐4. Additionally, expert assessments yielded an overall higher accuracy compared to both GPT‐3.5 and GPT‐4. However, the performance within each category displayed considerable individual variation and did not achieve statistical significance (see Table 2 and Figure 3).

Statistical Comparison and Significance Testing

To assess whether the observed differences in model and expert performance were statistically significant, we conducted formal hypothesis testing using both McNemar's test for paired data and chi‐square tests for unpaired categorical comparisons. Table 3 provides an overview of statistical comparisons across different evaluation groups. The results show that GPT‐3.5 outperformed GPT‐4 in overall accuracy on the same set of 465 questions (McNemar's x 2 = 20.11, p < 0.00001). Both models performed worse than Expert 1 (x 2 = 220.23 and 328.75, respectively, both p < 0.0001). In contrast, GPT‐3.5 demonstrated relatively stable performance across two independent test runs (McNemar's x 2 = 3.12, p = 0.077), suggesting robustness under repeated sampling conditions.

Discussion

In this study, we compared the performance of representative AI models (GPT‐3.5 and GPT‐4) on the proposed MEE dataset, conducting intra‐class comparisons among the language models and inter‐class comparisons between the models and expert human performance. The overall performance of the language models in the MEE dataset was statistically inferior to that of human experts, as confirmed by chi‐square tests (GPT‐3.5 vs. Expert 1: x 2 = 220.23, p < 0.0001; GPT‐4 vs. Expert 1: x 2 = 328.75, p < 0.0001). To our knowledge, this is the first report to compare the performance of language models and experts in the domain of medical ethics, despite previous studies demonstrating the strong performance of these models on USMLE question sets. These findings offer valuable insights into the potential and limitations of AI in medical ethics, which are discussed not to discourage the application of AI in healthcare but to ensure that it realizes its substantial potential and promise.

Unexpectedly, GPT‐4 did not perform as well as GPT‐3.5 on single‐choice medical ethics questions. Prior research indicated that GPT‐4 tends to excel in medical problem‐solving due to its capacity to handle image‐based tasks and engage in higher‐level reasoning. However, GPT‐4 exhibited a propensity to assign excessive uncertainty to its answers, leading to a decrease in absolute accuracy. Specifically, in response to single‐choice questions, GPT‐4 frequently provided ambiguous or incomplete answers, particularly evident after stabilization experiments, where it sometimes favored multiple answers or produced insufficiently detailed responses. To better understand the underlying causes of AI errors, we performed an attribution analysis across all incorrect responses. We identified three dominant error types: (1) misinterpretation of ethical principles (e.g., autonomy vs. beneficence), (2) inability to prioritize conflicting values, and (3) overgeneralization in ambiguous cases. Notably, errors involving research ethics often stemmed from model confusion about informed consent procedures and data handling norms.

Consider the question illustrated in Figure S1, for which the correct answer is option C. However, GPT‐4 provided answers for both options A and C (Figure S1a). Drawing on a comprehensive knowledge base, GPT‐4 inferred the conclusion that “an integrated approach is likely to be the most beneficial.” While GPT‐4 demonstrates proficiency in generating extensive text, it faces challenges when addressing intuitive single‐choice questions. Another factor contributing to GPT‐4′s lower performance on the MEE dataset may be its reluctance to resolve ethical dilemmas. As depicted in Figure S1b, GPT's response strictly adhered to the principles of medical ethics, suggesting that when faced with a decision between two alternatives, it deemed both options unacceptable [22]. Consequently, GPT‐4 employed strategies such as refuting the paradox, valuing theoretical approaches, and seeking alternative solutions, rather than selecting a singularly correct answer [23].

The consistency of decisions made by AI is a critical criterion for assessing its performance in medical ethical decision support. Therefore, this study also examined the stability of the two LLMs. GPT‐4 demonstrated greater consistency, likely due to its more robust training mechanism. In contrast, GPT‐3.5 exhibited more variability across the two tests, suggesting it may be better suited for scenarios where decision‐making stability is less critical and diversity is more favorable.

Unlike other experiments that apply the USMLE to evaluate LLMs, our focus emphasized the ethical and moral dimensions of medical practice. Utilizing the Handbook of Medical Ethics published by the WMA, our experiments revealed that LLMs achieved commendable results across various specialized branches of medical ethics. Both GPT‐3.5 and GPT‐4 proved to be particularly effective in medical ethical scenarios that require precise answers and explicit learning capabilities.

When comparing conceptual questions to situational questions, the latter demand higher‐level reasoning abilities from LLMs, necessitating a profound semantic understanding of contextual information and comprehensive knowledge application for accurate judgments. Experimental results indicate that LLMs perform worse on situational problems than on conceptual ones. This discrepancy arises because medical ethics, situated within behavioral medicine, involves human concepts and value systems—elements often overlooked in artificial intelligence modeling tasks. In addressing situational questions, GPT‐4 leverages its superior reasoning ability to generate analytical responses with unspecified answers. While this method adheres to the principles of medical ethics, the reluctance to confront ethical dilemmas, especially when a unique answer is required, diminishes GPT‐4′s accuracy rate on the MEE.

The ethical implications of artificial intelligence represent an expanding area of interest. A recent notable study utilized qualitative interviews to elucidate the ethical issues observed or anticipated by AI researchers involved in the development and application of AI in medicine [24]. This investigation found that artificial intelligence tends to present its responses in more socially acceptable manners. Our study also analyzed the nature of the AI's responses, and we identified three representative types: Response A offers a nuanced analysis, while Response B, although imprecise, provides relevant options along with constructive feedback. Response C advocates for recognizing individual variability within gender groups, suggesting the optimal answer and recommending a refined question (Figure S2).

Although the conclusion states that GPTs “adhere to ethical principles,” this must be contextualized. The low accuracy rate suggests that the models often misunderstood scenario‐specific applications of principles. GPT‐4′s tendency to refuse morally ambiguous options, even when a standard answer exists, indicates a rigid or overcautious application of ethics rather than ignorance. Thus, “principle compliance” may reflect theoretical alignment rather than pragmatic adequacy.

Four significant limitations of this study are apparent. First, the homogeneity in the type and format of input questions constrained the full utilization of the reasoning and judgment capabilities of LLMs. Second, the study engaged only two experts; expanding the expert team will enhance the study's authority and mitigate potential biases. Third, the MEE dataset is unevenly distributed. The “Physicians and Colleagues” category comprised only 60 questions, and the low sample size limits statistical robustness. The 16.67% accuracy rate in this category should be interpreted cautiously, and future expansions of the MEE dataset will aim to increase the representation of this domain, it is important to note that the MEE dataset is grounded in a predominantly Western ethical framework. As such, interpretations by both AI and human experts may reflect Western norms (e.g., emphasis on autonomy). Cultural differences—such as Confucian values in East Asian ethics—may lead to different prioritizations (e.g., collective good over individual autonomy), which were not addressed in this iteration [25, 26]. Future work will incorporate cross‐cultural ethical scenarios to assess model adaptability.

Conclusions

This study introduced the MEE dataset and benchmarked ChatGPT's performance in the domain of medical ethics. While GPT‐3.5 and GPT‐4 demonstrated theoretical alignment with core ethical principles, their practical decision‐making remains inconsistent and error‐prone, particularly in complex situational contexts. The findings underscore the need for targeted optimization of LLMs and highlight the importance of interdisciplinary collaboration to ensure their safe and ethical integration into healthcare environments. Future studies should incorporate cross‐cultural frameworks, expanded expert panels, and deeper interpretability analyses to better evaluate and guide the development of ethical AI.
