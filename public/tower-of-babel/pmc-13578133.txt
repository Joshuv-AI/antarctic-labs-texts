Experience with Tenecteplase in Treatment of Acute Ischaemic Stroke in Window: Analysis and Outcomes at a Tertiary Care Institute in Central India
Authors: Priyanka V Kashyap, Vaibhav OP Pandey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIntravenous thrombolysis with alteplase has been the standard for acute ischaemic stroke (AIS) within 4.5 h, but tenecteplase (TNK) is gaining promising corner in intravenous thrombolysis with some unique features and superior efficacy in large vessel occlusion (LVO).PurposeThis study evaluates real-world TNK outcomes at a Central Indian tertiary centre.MethodsThis ambispective single-centre study included 36 AIS patients presenting within <4.5 h of onset, meeting inclusion criteria (age >18 years, National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS)-assessed, blood pressure <185/110 mmHg, no contraindications). Weight-based TNK (0.25 mg/kg bolus) was administered. NIHSS was noted at admission, 48 h, 7 days and discharge; modified Rankin Scale at discharge and at 90 days was also recorded. Demographics, comorbidities, vessel angiography status and complications were analysed using descriptive statistics and t-tests (p < .05 significant).ResultsThe mean age was 57.67 ± 14.32 years, and 75% of patients were males. Hypertension (61.1%) and smoking (30.6%) were the predominant comorbidities. Mild stroke (NIHSS 1–5) occurred in 58.33% of cases, and LVOs were present in 27.78% (bilateral internal carotid artery (ICA) in 13.89%, middle cerebral artery M1/M2 in 13.89%). Patients with bilateral ICA occlusions experienced a mean NIHSS reduction of 6 points, compared to 3.5 points in others (p = .0188). Successful thrombolysis with TNK was achieved in 77.78% of cases. Complications included haemorrhagic transformation in 8.33% and mortality in 8.33% (unrelated to TNK). Age and comorbidities did not predict outcomes (p > .05).ConclusionTNK use had robust success in lowering NIHSS scores, particularly in LVOs, with excellent safety, being a single bolus, more cost-effective, no time wastage for infusion preparation supporting its preference over standard alteplase. TNK may be a better choice in resource-limited set-up. Multicentre trials are needed for its widespread adoption and also for its conjunctive use with mechanical thrombectomy.Keywords: Stroke, tenecteplase, thrombolysis, window period

Introduction

Stroke remains a major societal burden with disability. Approximately 12.2 million new strokes occur each year, with one in four individuals above the second decade experiencing at least one stroke event in their lifetime, 1 of which 62% are ischaemic strokes. 2 Reperfusion with intravenous thrombolysis of acute ischaemic stroke (AIS) within the treatment window period has revolutionised stroke management by salvaging the ischaemic penumbra and reducing neurological deficits. It began in 1995 with the landmark National Institute of Neurological Disorders and Stroke trial of intravenous thrombolysis using alteplase (rtPA) as an acute reperfusion therapy for ischaemic stroke. 3 This proved that ‘Time is Brain’ and the initial golden hour must be used promptly and wisely for brain tissue salvageability without leaving any stone unturned. Over two decades of use of alteplase, another genetically modified variant of alteplase, named tenecteplase (TNK), with higher fibrin specificity and longer half-life, came into use that is showing promising results, particularly in cases of large vessel occlusion (LVO).4, 5 Several studies have reported its non-inferiority and potential superiority to alteplase in terms of ease of administration, with a time-saving feature as single-bolus dosing, better recanalisation rates, functional outcomes and fewer haemorrhagic transformations. 6 Initially used for ST-elevation myocardial infarction, TNK is increasingly being recommended as a robust, viable alternative to alteplase. 7 A single-bolus thrombolytic has several potential advantages over alteplase, in addition to saving time in preparing the solution. The common time gaps between the alteplase bolus administration and its 1-h infusion start time, which is the culprit for its underdosing, are not present with TNK. 8 Moreover, alteplase infusion requires a second, dedicated intravenous catheter insertion that may further delay treatment initiation if access is difficult to obtain. We conducted this study to assess the real-world use, safety and outcomes of TNK in patients with AIS in the window.

Methods

Patients of AIS presenting at the All India Institute of Medical Sciences Emergency Department (ED) within 4.5 h of onset of sudden focal neurological deficits, who met the criteria for thrombolysis, were enrolled and thrombolysed with TNK (available agent in ED) and were admitted to Neurology. National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) was assessed at admission, then at 48 h, 7 days and at the time of discharge, along with the modified Rankin Scale (mRS) at discharge and at 90 days and compared. Their clinico-aetiological profile was noted. Neuroimaging included stroke protocol images (including vessel status), and relevant biochemical parameters for stroke, which included complete blood count, blood sugars, lipid profile and renal parameters, were obtained. All patients underwent cardiac assessment, and post-thrombolysis complications were recorded and analysed.

Data Collection

In this ambispective single-centre study, patient-related data were collected for a duration of 2 years (December 2022 to September 2024). Patients with AIS of less than 4.5 h duration with no haemorrhage on non-contrast computed tomogram (NCCT) head and who consented to undergo thrombolysis were included. NIHSS scores were calculated, and all patients were thrombolysed with an appropriate weight-based dose of TNK (0.25 mg/kg) and monitored as per protocol (Figure 1). NIHSS and mRS scores were calculated again at discharge, at 1 month and at 90 days. CT angiography of the carotids was performed to look for vessel-related abnormalities, including LVOs.

Results

In this 2-year study at our centre on AIS patients eligible for thrombolysis, a total of 36 patients were included of whom 55.56% belonged to the 50–70 years range, followed by 30–50 years (27.78%) and patients above 85 years of age accounted for 16.67%. The mean age was 57.67 years (standard deviation 14.32 years) (Table 1A), and the median age was 57 years, with an interquartile range (IQR) of 47.00–67.25, showing that half of the participants fell within this range. Twenty-seven were males (75%), and nine were females (25%). All of the patients were residents of Bhopal or were in Bhopal at the time of the event, which facilitated their timely arrival in the hospital.

The most common comorbidity associated with stroke was hypertension (61.1%), with habit dependence on cigarette smoking (30.6%), tobacco and alcohol intake (25%) (Table 1B).

Non-hypertensive patients had more severe stroke (60.0%), while hypertensives had more moderately severe stroke (70.6%) than severe stroke (40.0%). Non-diabetic patients had a higher proportion in the severe category (90.0%) compared to those with diabetes (10.0%). Similar trends are observed with coronary artery disease (CAD), where patients without CAD show a higher percentage in the severe category of stroke (90.0%) and non-smokers had a higher proportion of severe strokes (90.0%) compared to smokers (10.0%). All non-obese patients suffered from mild stroke, while obese patients showed a proportion in the severe category (10.0%). Non-tobacco chewers had a higher proportion of mild stroke (88.9%) than tobacco users (11.1%) (Table 1B). These differences were not statistically significant.

Bilateral vertebral and basilar artery dissections were seen in one patient and hypoplastic right vertebral artery with V4 segment thrombosis in one subject (2.78%) (Table 3A). All these patients had successful thrombolysis and achieved a 50% reduction in mean NIHSS compared to baseline, exhibiting robust outcomes with TNK.

There was no statistically significant difference in NIHSS changes among different age groups. The 30–50-year age group had more mild strokes (45.5%), while the 50–70-year age group had mild to moderately severe strokes (64.7%), suggesting a more serious impact of stroke in this age range. The oldest age group (up to 85 years) suffered the severe stroke subtype (25.0%). Successful and uneventful thrombolysis was achieved in 28 patients (77.78%), whereas eight (22.22%) patients developed complications; of them three suffered haemorrhagic transformations, one restroke, and one had a hypersensitivity reaction and there were three deaths of unrelated causes (Table 3B). Of the three patients who suffered haemorrhagic transformation, two (66.66%) were symptomatic, while one (33.33%) was asymptomatic. The discharge mRS was 4 in patients with symptomatic haemorrhage transformation. The causes of death were arterial dissection of the basilar artery; an LVO (M1); and unknown in the third case.

All patients with ICA occlusions (5, 13.89%) achieved more than a 3-point drop in NIHSS. In the other five (13.89%) cases with LVOs, one death (M1) was recorded, while the other four experienced a moderate reduction in NIHSS at discharge (Table 4A). Endovascular thrombectomy was considered in all 10 patients, but was ultimately deferred in four due to very low ASPECTS scores, reflecting limited salvageable tissue and in the remaining due to caregiver refusal.

NIHSS scores improved significantly from admission to discharge in the cohort, with the median NIHSS decreasing from 11.0 (IQR 5.75–15.0) to 5.5 (IQR 2.0–11.0); the paired comparison was performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test and was statistically significant (V = 42.0, two-sided p = .000189). At discharge, the median mRS was 2.0 (IQR 0.5–3.0), and at 90 days it improved to 1.0 (IQR 0.0–1.0), indicating better functional outcome over time. These data are to be interpreted with caution, as only 15 out of 36 patients could be followed up in our OPD; the rest were lost to follow-up. However, reduction in NIHSS was not significantly associated with 90-day functional outcome in the paired subset with available data, as the correlation between NIHSS fall and 90-day mRS was weak and non-significant (Spearman r = 0.156, p = .579; Kendall τ = 0.133, p = .549).

The mean reduction in NIHSS among patients with bilateral ICA occlusions (n = 5) was 6 points, which was higher than a mean reduction of 3.5 points in the remaining patients (n = 31). The NIHSS improved from admission to discharge in all five patients. This reduction showed statistical significance on paired Wilcoxon signed-rank testing (V = 0.0, p = .0525). This indicated that patients with bilateral ICA occlusions benefited most and demonstrated a greater clinical response to thrombolysis with TNK (Table 4B).

Discussion

Our study assessed the uniqueness of TNK in AIS in the window period and found some interesting features to share. Though the demographic profile was similar to previous studies, with a trend of about 16.67% of patients aged up to 85 years receiving thrombolysis, reflecting increased awareness of thrombolysis and fading fear of thrombolysis in the elderly population despite associated risks. 9 This also shows narrowing of the knowledge-practice gap for thrombolysis. Our study also confirmed that ageing adds to stroke incidence, as the majority (55.56%) were aged 50–70 years. 1 The aetiological profile revealed that hypertension (61.1%) was the most common comorbidity, and cigarette smoking (30.6%) and oral tobacco use (25%) were the most prevalent habit-dependent behaviours, consistent with previously established stroke aetiologies.10, 11 Most presented with mild stroke (58.33%) (NIHSS 1–5), which reflects the increasing awareness to reach the hospital when symptoms have just begun and endorses the selection bias for thrombolysis. 12 NIHSS improvement has no relation with age range (p = .539), indicating that age does not predict thrombolysis outcomes, 13 emphasising that age cannot be a deciding criterion for thrombolysis. A serial glance at NIHSS scores from admission to discharge showed a mean NIHSS reduction of seven points in LVO, particularly ICA occlusions, including M1/M2 occlusions (60% achieving favourable outcomes). This unique observation with TNK thrombolysis aligns with other studies highlighting TNK’s superior efficacy over alteplase in LVOs.5, 6 Our study also highlights that complications related to TNK use were mild, including haemorrhagic transformation (8.33%), and had a self-limiting course, proving TNK is a safer agent. The deaths in our series (3, 8.33%) were unrelated to the agent, as 1 had arterial dissection, which per se has a guarded prognosis, and the other two had large vessel stroke, which has an independent high mortality. 14 There is now robust evidence in favour of the preferred use of TNK, as it has an easy route of administration and is less time-consuming, with lower cost and proves to be more efficacious in LVOs. Menon et al., in the AcT trial, compared TNK and alteplase in patients with carotid tandem lesions and concluded that TNK had excellent functional outcome (mRS 0–1 at 90 days), supporting its efficacy in complex LVO stroke management. 15 The safety profile of this study matched with our series. A meta-analysis and pooled trial assessment analysed TNK versus alteplase in tandem carotid occlusions and demonstrated TNK was non-inferior to alteplase for recanalisation and functional outcomes, without increased haemorrhagic risk, indicating TNK’s suitability as an alternative thrombolytic agent for tandem lesions. 16 This was further confirmed in other reports and pooled analyses of ETIS and TETRIS studies on LVO and tandem carotid occlusions that confirmed that TNK has superior benefits in complicated cases of stent-assisted carotid occlusions.17, 18 The benefits included superior early recanalisation and better functional outcome rates compared to alteplase, with a comparable safety profile, supporting its role in LVO management. Other studies showed favourable outcomes with TNK compared to alteplase, especially in LVO and tandem ICA occlusions, with no added haemorrhagic risk. Our study is consistent with the study findings of various centres (Sharma et al. 19 ; comprehensive review by Gong et al. 20 ) who emphasised its practical, easy bolus formulation with enhanced reperfusion rates and comparable functional outcomes to alteplase, in major vessel and tandem occlusions. Recently, a subgroup analysis of a multicentre randomised trial comparing TNK and alteplase in AIS with ICA involvement by Tang et al. revealed TNK is non-inferior for clinical and radiological outcomes with similar safety, supporting its use across varied aetiologies of AIS. 21 Patel et al., summarised its efficacy in their case series on use of intravenous TNK and endovascular therapy for acute stroke, including ICA occlusions. 22 Patients experienced favourable recanalisation and good functional outcomes, suggesting TNK’s game-changing role in AIS reperfusion protocols. Hence, this study adds to the evidence that TNK may be a better choice for LVOs and may show higher reperfusion in patients selected for mechanical thrombectomy (MT). Further strengthening the role of TNK in acute stroke was the official inclusion of TNK over alteplase in the AHA/ASA Stroke guidelines published in 2026, which now recommends TNK 0.25 mg/kg in place of alteplase for patients presenting within 4.5 h of symptom onset. 23

The study favours the use of TNK in LVOs and is safe in the geriatric population. This study raised many questions to be worked upon, including the role of TNK reperfusion in patients selected for MT and a time-saving strategy with TNK would be more beneficial in patients selected for MT. Our study has a small sample size and an ambispective design. We could not compare the thrombolytic agents, as the only available agent was TNK and another agent needed to be brought from outside. This opens the gateway to larger prospective studies needed to validate these findings and further investigate the aetiological profile with prognostic outcomes analysis. 25 We could not compare the time matrix changes between TNK and alteplase use as it was a single-arm study with TNK, and it was the only thrombolytic agent available with us.

Conclusion

This study highlights the effectiveness of IV TNK in patients presenting with AIS, particularly in those with moderate stroke severity and LVOs. Despite not achieving ideal DTNT (ours is more than 60 min), patients experienced substantial clinical improvement, with a significant reduction in NIHSS scores from admission to discharge. Age and comorbidities, such as hypertension and smoking, were important factors influencing stroke severity but did not appear to significantly affect thrombolysis outcomes. Overall, TNK was associated with favourable clinical outcomes in the majority of patients, although complications such as haemorrhagic transformation and death still occurred, particularly in those with severe strokes or complex vascular pathology. Moving forward, efforts to improve thrombolysis delivery times and further explore the nuances of thrombolytic therapy in different patient populations are warranted. TNK can be preferred as a thrombolytic in LVO stroke. TNK has a higher recanalisation rate, as shown in a French real-world data study. 24
