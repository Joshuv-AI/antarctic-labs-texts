Regional Disparities in Case Volumes and Surgeon Distribution in Gastroenterological Surgery
Authors: Hiroshi Hasegawa, Hiraku Kumamaru, Momoko Ara, Yoshihiro Kakeji, Hideko Yamauchi, Masafumi Inomata, Sadako Akashi‐Tanaka, Koji Ito, Shinji Itoh, Hidetoshi Eguchi, Kazumi Kawase, Yoshiaki Kinoshita, Yuki Kiyozumi, Shogo Kobayashi, Ikuko Shibasaki, Chiyoe Shirota, Hiroyuki Suzuki, Chie Takasu, Aoi Fujikawa, Saki Bessho, Kiyomi Horiuchi, Kosei Maemura, Ayako Maruo, Takuya Miura, Yuji Miyamoto, Toshiki Yajima, Ayuko Yasuda, Toshiharu Yamaguchi, Tadashi Iwanaka, Hideo Baba, Akinobu Taketomi

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundRegional disparities in surgical resources may influence the delivery and sustainability of gastroenterological surgery, particularly in countries experiencing rapid population aging and depopulation.MethodsThis nationwide cross‐sectional study used data from the Japanese National Clinical Database to examine temporal trends (2013–2023) in surgical volume, surgeon supply, and annual operative volume per surgeon for colectomy, gastrectomy, and pancreaticoduodenectomy. Analyses were conducted at the prefectural level and stratified by population density and surgeon experience.ResultsA total of 955 168 colectomies, 514 155 gastrectomies, and 126 008 pancreaticoduodenectomies were analyzed. Colectomy volume increased despite declining surgeon numbers in medium‐ and high‐density areas, resulting in increased operative volume per surgeon. Gastrectomy volume decreased nationwide, whereas pancreaticoduodenectomy volume increased across all regions, accompanied by a reduction in surgeon supply and a rise in procedures per surgeon. By 2023, operative volume per surgeon was largely comparable across population density categories. Notably, surgeons with ≥ 20 years of experience were disproportionately represented in low‐density areas.ConclusionsAlthough regional differences in population density persist, surgeon workload for major gastroenterological procedures has converged across regions in recent years. However, surgical care in low‐density areas depends heavily on senior surgeons, raising concerns regarding generational transition and long‐term sustainability of gastroenterological surgical services in Japan.Keywords: gastroenterological surgery, national clinical database (NCD), population density, regional disparities

Introduction

Equitable access to surgical care is a foundational principle of health systems with universal coverage. Nevertheless, substantial regional disparities in health care delivery persist worldwide, reflecting differences in population structure, geographic accessibility, transportation infrastructure, and distribution of health care professionals and facilities. Rural–urban gaps in access to specialized surgical services remain a persistent challenge even in high‐income countries [1, 2, 3, 4]. Addressing such inequities requires sustained policy efforts and data‐driven workforce planning.

Population aging and increasing life expectancy are global demographic trends, progressing most rapidly in several high‐income Asia–Pacific countries [5, 6]. Japan is one of the most rapidly aging societies and has drawn international attention regarding the long‐term sustainability of its health care workforce. Anticipating rising demand associated with demographic change, the Japanese government expanded medical school enrollment beginning in 2006 as part of a national physician workforce strategy. Despite this expansion, specialty‐specific imbalances persist. According to the Ministry of Health, Labor and Welfare, although the total number of physicians increased by 13.4% between 2012 and 2022, the number of surgeons declined by 2.1% during the same period [7].

Similar workforce patterns have been reported internationally. In the United States, the density of general and subspecialty surgeons declined between 2004 and 2017 in both rural and urban areas [8]. Regional maldistribution of surgeons has also been documented in several European countries despite stable overall physician numbers [9]. The Lancet Commission on Global Surgery likewise identified surgical workforce shortages and maldistribution as major barriers to equitable access to surgical care worldwide [10]. However, nationwide longitudinal evidence simultaneously evaluating surgical case volume, active surgeon supply, and surgeon‐level workload across regions remains limited.

The Japanese National Clinical Database (NCD) provides comprehensive nationwide procedural and surgeon‐level data [11, 12, 13]. Using this resource, the present study examined temporal trends in surgical demand and surgeon supply across regions with differing population density. We focused on three representative gastroenterological procedures—colectomy, gastrectomy, and pancreaticoduodenectomy—to evaluate regional patterns in surgical volume, operating surgeon distribution, and annual operative volume per surgeon. These findings may inform surgical workforce planning in aging and depopulating health systems.

Methods

Study Design and Data Sources

This nationwide registry‐based cross‐sectional study was approved by the ethics review committee of Hokkaido University Hospital. An information disclosure statement was publicly posted on the website of the Japanese Surgical Society (JSS) beginning in January 2025, and notification was distributed to JSS members.

We analyzed data from the Japanese National Clinical Database (NCD), which captures more than 95% of all surgical procedures performed nationwide [13]. Records were extracted for patients who underwent colectomy, gastrectomy, or pancreaticoduodenectomy between January 1, 2013, and December 31, 2023. The three procedures were selected through a consensus process among all authors, including specialists in general surgery, upper and lower gastrointestinal surgery, hepatobiliary‐pancreatic surgery, and medical statistics. These procedures were chosen because they represent major gastroenterological surgeries across different subspecialties and correspond to the most common gastrointestinal malignancies in Japan, namely colorectal, gastric, and pancreatic cancers.

Surgeons were included if they served as the primary operator for at least one eligible procedure in a given year. Accordingly, surgeons who operated in multiple prefectures within the same year were counted separately in each prefecture. This approach may have led to a slight overestimation of the number of surgeons at the regional level.

Procedure definitions followed standardized NCD coding. Colectomy (NQ0541, NQ0542, NQ0548, NQ0550, OQ310, OQ311, OQ0312, OQ0323, OQ0326) was defined as colon resection with reconstruction and excluded rectal and rectosigmoid resections. Gastrectomy (NQ0740, NQ0741, OQ134, OQ0135, OQ136, OQ0137, OQ0138, OQ0140, OQ0141) included distal, proximal, and total gastrectomy with reconstruction and excluded partial resections. Pancreaticoduodenectomy (NQ0521, NQ0580, NQ0729, NQ0752, OQ0190, OQ0191, OQ0269, OQ0271, NQ0272) included standard and pylorus‐preserving procedures. Prefecture‐level population data were obtained from official government statistics (e‐Stat) [14]. Japan consists of 47 prefectures defined by administrative and geographic boundaries. Detailed characteristics of each prefecture, including population, area, population density, proportion of elderly residents, and number of medical schools, are summarized in Supporting Information Table S1. Temporal population trends by prefecture from 2013 to 2023 are shown in Supporting Information Table S2.

Population Density Classification

Prefectures were categorized by population density using prespecified thresholds consistent with prior literature: High density (≥ 1 000 persons/km2), medium density (200–999 persons/km2), and low density (< 200 persons/km2) [15, 16, 17, 18]. The population density classification map of Japan shown in Figure 1A was created using an online map‐generation tool (Start Point) [19].

Outcomes

Primary measures included:

(a) annual procedures per 100 000 population.

(b) surgeons per 100 000 population.

(c) mean annual procedures per surgeon.

Mean procedures per surgeon were calculated by dividing procedures per 100 000 population by surgeons per 100 000 population. Surgeons were stratified by years since joining the JSS (0–9, 10–19, ≥ 20 years) as a proxy for career stage.

Statistical Analysis

Temporal trends from 2013 through 2023 were evaluated descriptively and graphically by population density category and surgeon experience group. Analyses were conducted at the prefectural level. Because the study used complete national registry data rather than sampling, emphasis was placed on effect size and trend patterns rather than on hypothesis testing alone. To estimate the surplus or deficiency of surgeons in each prefecture relative to the national average operative workload, we used a model assuming equal operative volume per surgeon nationwide. The calculation method is illustrated in Supporting Information Figure S3, and the estimated surplus/deficiency values are presented in Supporting Information Tables S4–S6.

Results

Population Trends and Study Cohort

Between 2013 and 2023, the population increased by 2.0% in high‐density prefectures but declined by 4.3% in medium‐density and 7.9% in low‐density prefectures (Supporting Information Table S3). The proportion of residents aged ≥ 65, ≥ 75, and ≥ 85 years increased over time across all population density categories (Supporting Information Figure S1). The proportion of residents aged 65 years or older was inversely correlated with population density (Figure 1B). The study included 955 168 colectomies performed by 101 288 surgeons, 514 155 gastrectomies performed by 81 902 surgeons, and 126 008 pancreaticoduodenectomies performed by 32 469 surgeons.

Trends in Case Volume and Surgeon Supply by Population Density

Trends in surgical volume and number of surgeons, and average annual cases per surgeon were analyzed by population density for colectomy, gastrectomy, and pancreaticoduodenectomy between 2013 and 2023 (Figure 2). Supporting Information Figure S2 illustrates the relationship between procedure volume per 100 000 population and surgeon supply per 100 000 population in 2023 for each prefecture.

Annual Trends in Procedure Volume and Surgeon Supply by Population Density. (A) Colectomy, (B) Gastrectomy, and (C) Pancreaticoduodenectomy.

For colectomy, case volume increased across all density categories and was consistently highest in low‐density regions. The number of operating surgeons increased slightly in low‐density areas but declined in medium‐ and high‐density areas. As a result, procedures per surgeon increased across all regions, and regional differences narrowed over time.

Gastrectomy volume declined steadily across density categories, paralleling national incidence trends. Surgeon numbers also decreased, but to a lesser extent than procedural volume. Consequently, procedures per surgeon decreased across all regions and converged by the end of the study period.

Pancreaticoduodenectomy volume increased over time, with relatively small differences across density categories. Surgeon numbers declined overall, resulting in rising procedures per surgeon across regions. Regional variation in per‐surgeon case volume was limited.

Estimated prefecture‐level surplus or deficiency of surgeons based on operative volume is shown in Supporting Information Tables S4–S6 for colectomy, gastrectomy, and pancreaticoduodenectomy, respectively.

Trends by Surgeon Experience

We analyzed temporal changes from 2013 to 2023 in surgical case volume per 100 000 population, number of surgeons, and cases per surgeon, stratified by population density (low, medium, high) and surgeon experience (0–9, 10–19, ≥ 20 years). Trends were visualized separately for colectomy (Figure 3), gastrectomy (Figure 4), and pancreaticoduodenectomy (Figure 5).

Trends in Gastrectomy Volume and Surgeon Workforce by Population Density and Surgeon Experience. (A) Gastrectomy 0 to 9 years, (B) Gastrectomy 10 to 19 years, and (C) Gastrectomy 20 years or more.

Trends in Pancreaticoduodenectomy Volume and Surgeon Workforce by Population Density and Surgeon Experience. (A) Pancreaticoduodenectomy 0 to 9 years, (B) Pancreaticoduodenectomy 10 to 19 years, and (C) Pancreaticoduodenectomy 20 years or more.

Early‐career surgeons (0–9 years) performed the largest share of colectomies. Their numbers declined in high‐density regions but increased in low‐density regions over time. Surgeons with 20 or more years of experience were consistently more prevalent in low‐density regions across all procedures.

Procedures per surgeon increased over time among early‐ and mid‐career surgeons for colectomy and pancreaticoduodenectomy, while remaining relatively stable among late‐career surgeons. For gastrectomy, procedures per surgeon declined across experience groups.

Additional analyses using a more detailed five‐category population density classification (≥ 1 000, 400–999, 200–399, 100–199, and < 100 persons/km2) demonstrated trends consistent with the primary findings (Supporting Information Figures S4–S7).

Detailed distributions of surgeons according to annual operative volume, population density category, and years of surgical experience in 2023 are provided in Supporting Information Tables S7–S12.

Discussion

In this nationwide registry study, we evaluated 10‐year trends in surgical case volume, operating surgeon supply, and surgeon workload across regions stratified by population density. Three principal findings emerged. First, procedure‐specific trends differed substantially, with increasing colectomy and pancreaticoduodenectomy volumes but declining gastrectomy volumes. Second, the number of operating surgeons declined for several procedures despite stable or increasing case volume. Third, regional differences in procedures per surgeon narrowed over time, resulting in broadly similar surgeon workload across density categories by 2023.

Procedure‐specific trends were consistent with national cancer incidence patterns [20]. Rising colon and pancreatic cancer incidence likely contributed to increasing colectomy and pancreaticoduodenectomy volumes, whereas declining gastric cancer incidence paralleled decreasing gastrectomy volume. However, surgeon supply did not increase proportionally for higher‐demand procedures, leading to increased per‐surgeon case volume.

Despite convergence in procedures per surgeon across regions, this does not necessarily indicate an equalization of the overall burden of surgical practice. Surgeons in low‐density regions often serve larger geographic catchment areas and may face greater on‐call and travel burdens that are not captured by procedural counts alone. Moreover, low‐density regions had a higher proportion of late‐career surgeons, suggesting greater dependence on an aging workforce. This workforce structure may represent a vulnerability for long‐term regional surgical capacity.

This study did not assess patient movement across population density categories when calculating procedure rates per population. For commonly performed gastrointestinal surgeries, such as colectomy and gastrectomy, procedure volumes were higher in areas with lower population density. This finding is consistent with the higher proportion of elderly individuals in less densely populated areas, which is associated with an increased disease burden, and suggests that patient movement across population density categories may be relatively limited for these procedures. In contrast, no consistent association between population density and procedure volume was observed for the highly specialized procedure of pancreaticoduodenectomy. This may reflect greater patient movement across population density categories or differences in surgical indications according to population density. Furthermore, given the declining incidence of gastric cancer, gastrectomy may become an increasingly specialized procedure in the future.

Several national and regional initiatives have been implemented in Japan to address the decline and maldistribution of the surgical workforce. One potential strategy is the regional quota system in medical schools, which allocates a fixed number of students (approximately 20–30 per prefecture) with the aim of improving physician distribution across regions [7]. In addition, the Ministry of Health, Labor and Welfare has introduced policy measures to mitigate the decline in surgeons, including increased reimbursement for emergency surgeries during nights and holidays, as well as financial incentives linked to surgical procedure fees for gastroenterological surgeons. While the long‐term impact of these initiatives remains to be fully evaluated, our findings provide important context for assessing their effectiveness and highlight the need for continued efforts to ensure a sustainable and well‐distributed surgical workforce.

From an international perspective, many countries face simultaneous population aging and regional depopulation [21]. Our findings provide quantitative evidence that surgeon workload distribution may appear balanced even when the underlying workforce age structure differs substantially. Workforce planning strategies should therefore consider not only surgeon numbers and case volumes but also career stage distribution and regional sustainability.

This study has several limitations. First, although the NCD captures most surgical procedures nationwide, some operations are not included. Second, only three representative procedures were examined and may not reflect all gastroenterological surgeries. Third, population density categories were broad and may obscure within‐category variation. Fourth, patient outcomes, referral patterns, hospital volume, and surgeon full‐time equivalency were not available and could not be analyzed. Furthermore, this system may impose additional burdens, including travel time and limited availability for emergency coverage and perioperative management, which are not captured by procedure‐based metrics. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted with caution, as the operative volume per surgeon may overestimate the stability and accessibility of surgical care in rural regions. Fifth, we defined an active surgeon as any individual who performs at least one procedure annually, without distinguishing between full‐time staff and temporarily dispatched surgeons. In Japan, it is common for surgeons affiliated with urban or academic institutions to provide surgical services in rural or low‐population‐density areas on a part‐time or rotational basis. This “dispatch” system may contribute to the observed equalization of operative volume per surgeon across regions; however, such apparent balance does not necessarily indicate an adequate or sustainable local surgical workforce. Instead, surgical capacity in low‐density areas may be partially supported by transient external resources. Sixth, the procedures analyzed in this study primarily represent major oncological and elective surgeries. Therefore, the generalizability of our findings to benign or emergency general surgical conditions may be limited. In contrast to elective oncologic procedures, emergency surgeries—such as appendectomy or operations for generalized peritonitis—require immediate and continuous availability of surgical care. As a result, disparities in workforce distribution may be more pronounced in these settings, particularly in low‐population‐density areas where access to on‐site surgeons may be limited. Future studies focusing on emergency and benign surgical conditions are warranted to better understand regional disparities in urgent surgical care. Finally, defining active surgeons as those performing at least one procedure annually may overestimate the clinically active workforce. Although our analysis suggested that the number of procedures per surgeon was broadly comparable across population density categories in 2023, this metric may mask heterogeneity in actual workload. In particular, in regions where a notable proportion of surgeons perform only a small number of procedures per year or participate primarily in assisting or occasional roles, the routine surgical workload may be concentrated among a smaller subset of consistently active surgeons.

In conclusion, surgeon workload for major gastroenterological procedures became broadly similar across regions despite large differences in population density. However, low‐density regions rely disproportionately on late‐career surgeons, indicating potential vulnerability in long‐term workforce sustainability. These findings provide data‐driven insight for surgical workforce planning in aging and depopulating societies.
