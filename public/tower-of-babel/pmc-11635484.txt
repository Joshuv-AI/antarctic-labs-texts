Structural Characteristics of the Fault Zone and Fault-Controlled Hydrocarbon Accumulation in the Chenghai Area of Dagang Offshore
Authors: Yan Chen, Haitao Liu, Zhenglong Jiang, Jinghui Sun, Zhenyu Bai, Xiaoying Guo, Xiongying Dong, Hongjun Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Chenghai area is a secondary structural unit within the Qikou sag of the Bohai Bay Basin, located in the southern part of the Dagang offshore area, known for its abundant oil and gas resources. Influenced by multiple episodes of tectonic activity, the Chenghai area exhibits a highly developed fault system, which significantly impacts oil and gas exploration in the region. To investigate the structural characteristics of fault zones in the Chenghai area and their petroleum geological significance, this study builds upon previous research by utilizing oilfield drilling data and relevant seismic information. Through methods such as fault growth index analysis, structural evolution history, and examination of typical oil and gas reservoir profiles, a detailed structural analysis of fault-controlled hydrocarbon accumulation in this petroleum region was conducted. The results reveal three types of fault classifications in the study area: basal-controlled continuously active faults, buried reactivated segmented growth-connected faults, and late-stage continuously active isolated growth faults. The structural evolution is divided into four stages: the pre-Paleogene basement formation stage, the Rifting I stage of intense extensional deformation, the Rifting II stage of extensional stress reversal and intense deformation, and the post-Eocene weak extensional deformation-stable burial stage. The fault-controlled accumulation models include three types: fault-guided longitudinal accumulation, lateral sealing fault masking accumulation, and far-source step-like fault-guided accumulation. The formation of fault zones and fault-controlled oil and gas reservoirs in the Chenghai area is primarily influenced by inherited development faults, which determine the overall macroscopic distribution of oil and gas. The formation of oil and gas reservoirs occurred relatively late, with a general trend of decreased fault activity during this period, facilitating the effective accumulation and sealing of hydrocarbons.

Introduction

The term “offshore” refers to the maritime area where the intertidal zone meets the ocean, serving as a transitional zone between land and sea. In the field of petroleum geology, it commonly denotes the region delineated from the coastline to the 5-m isobath, along with the adjacent marine and terrestrial areas.1 The offshore morphology in the Bohai Sea is controlled by the coastline, presenting an overall “C″ shape. Based on geographical location and mining rights, it is divided into three major offshore regions: the northern Nanpu offshore, the southern Shengli offshore, and the western Dagang offshore (Figure 1).

Geographic location of the Chenghai area.

The Dagang offshore, located in the western part of the Bohai Sea, is surrounded by land on three sides and belongs to the backfill area of the Yellow River entering the sea, exhibiting highly complex natural conditions. The entire Dagang offshore is influenced by factors such as river input, wind and waves, and ocean currents. The surface sediment is loose and uneven, with the development of thick layers of silt characterized by low bearing capacity, leading to significant siltation. There are large tidal variations, frequent storm surges, long winter cycles, and severe ice conditions, making it historically considered a challenging area for exploration and development. In recent years, with continuous innovations in the ideology of marine engineering construction and the practical application of characteristic supporting technologies such as the entry route of physical structures into the sea, the entry route of permeable structures into the sea, and the construction of offshore drilling platforms, especially the construction of artificial islands in the offshore area (Figure 1), significant technological support has been provided for the high-quality and efficient development of the Dagang offshore oilfield. This has addressed the technical challenges of “land-based equipment unable to go down, and sea-based equipment unable to come up,″ as described by previous research.1 Consequently, the Dagang offshore block has gradually become an exploration hotspot, achieving breakthroughs in exploration. Recent exploration and development activities indicate that the Chenghai area in the southern part of the Dagang offshore is a key exploration area for increased reserves and production in the Dagang oilfield.2 The most significant geological feature in the Chenghai area is the presence of numerous fault zones, which play a significant control role in hydrocarbon accumulation.3−5

Horizontal extensional movement refers to the relative discrete displacement between blocks in the extensional direction, causing horizontal elongation in the crustal profile. Horizontal extensional movement can trigger the formation of rift basins, vertical differential uplift and subsidence between basin blocks, and tilting movements of block bodies. When the fault trend is not perpendicular to the horizontal extensional movement direction between blocks, horizontal extensional movement also induces relative strike-slip motion between block bodies.6,7 Therefore, during the extensional process, the main types of block movements include horizontal extensional movement, vertical differential uplift and subsidence, block tilting (or tilting) movement, and different levels of detachment or sliding within the upper, middle, and lower crust or lithosphere (i.e., different levels of detachment or sliding at shallow, intermediate, and deep levels). In addition, the accompanying strike-slip action during extensional movements manifests as horizontal displacement of block bodies. For the continental rift basins in eastern China, the result of extensional movement is the development of numerous normal faults within the basin, which is widespread in the Bohai Bay Basin. In terms of classification, normal faults with a steep upper and gentle lower dip are generally referred to as shovel-type normal faults, while those formed by connecting steep and gentle sections are called slope-platform normal faults. Considering both geometric morphology and kinematic characteristics, normal faults can be further categorized into nonrotational planar normal faults, rotational planar normal faults, shovel-type normal faults, and slope-platform normal faults as four basic types. Different types of normal faults exhibit various combinations both in cross sections and on planes. Cross sections often show combinations of main faults and branch faults forming features like horse-tail, feather-like, step-like, and Y-shaped patterns.6−9

Faults act as a crucial link in the entire process of oil and gas generation, migration, accumulation, and preservation within oil and gas-bearing basins. The “four controls” determine the spatial distribution of oil and gas in ancient and modern times,2,4,10 The segmented growth of main boundary faults controls the migration of depressions, thereby influencing the distribution of effective hydrocarbon source kitchens at different periods. Transform faults act as channels for the inflow of water systems into basins, featuring valley-controlled source areas and sand-controlled characteristics of transform faults. Fault deformation and connectivity control the formation of various types of traps, and secondary tectonic modification of reservoirs is associated with faults. Faults associated with hydrocarbon sources serve as vertical migration channels, and accumulation faults control the enrichment of oil and gas. After the reservoir-forming period, reactivated faults adjust deep-seated oil and gas to shallow depths, forming secondary oil and gas reservoirs. The coupled relationship between faults and cap rocks determines oil and gas preservation.

The Bohai Bay Basin has experienced multiple phases of superimposed tectonic activities, resulting in faults exhibiting multidirectional superimposed deformations, making it a complex fault structural belt. Fault-related oil and gas reservoirs are the most abundant type in terms of reserves and production in rift basins, and they still play a significant role in key exploration areas in mature exploration zones.11−13 Oil and gas exploration in areas with complex fault zones still carries considerable risks, as the differential enrichment of fault-related oil and gas reservoirs is apparent. Factors such as vertical closure, lateral closure, and the activity history of faults can lead to failures in drilling fault-bound traps. Therefore, this paper takes the Chenghai area in the Dagang offshore region as an example. On the basis of a comprehensive analysis of the structural characteristics of fault zones, it clarifies the role of faults in oil and gas accumulation, laying the foundation for further exploration and development in the Chenghai area.

Geological Setting

The Dagang offshore area exhibits complex structural fragmentation and extensive fault development due to multiple phases of tectonic movements, particularly making the Chenghai area’s fault system highly intricate (Figure 2). The basement in the Chenghai area, located in the southwestern part of the Dagang offshore region (Figure 1), formed during the Mesozoic era. This area, situated in the western Bohai Sea, is part of the southern region of the Huanghua sag, a typical oil and gas-rich basin comprising the Huanghua Depression and the Qikou Depression. The research scope extends from the Qidong tectonic zone in the north to the northern margin of the Chengning Uplift in the south, reaching the Yang’erzhuang-Youyi area in the west and the boundary of the Dagang mining area in the east (Figure 2). The exploration area covers approximately 1700 km2, with the offshore area constituting around 1400 km2, accounting for approximately four-fifths of the total exploration area (Figure 1).

Structural geological map of the Chenghai area (This plan shows the distribution of faults during the Ng period, and the f6 fault does not cut through the Ng formation, so the f6 fault is not marked on the map).

The Chenghai area features a large-scale multistage faulted slope with abundant oil and gas resources14 and a stacked-shale thrust fault system (Figure 2), facilitating the formation of fault-related anticline, fault nose, and structural-lithological type oil and gas reservoirs.15−17 Currently, multiple oil and gas-bearing formations, including the Upper Paleozoic, Mesozoic, and Cenozoic formations such as the Shahejie Formation (Es), Dongying Formation (Ed), and the Neogene Guantao Formation (Ng) and Minghuazhen Formation (Nm), have been identified in the Chenghai area. Based on the three-dimensional seismic interpretation results, drilling data, and log interpretation data in the Dagang offshore area, a detailed study on the structural geology and oil and gas accumulation in the Chenghai area has been conducted. This research plays a guiding role in revealing the structural origin, evolutionary processes, and fault-controlled reservoirs in the study area.

Sedimentary Features

Drilling data reveals that the Neogene formations in the Chenghai area, from bottom to top, include the Paleogene Shahejie Formation (Es) and Dongying Formation (Ed), and the Neogene Minghuazhen Formation (Nm) and Guantao Formation (Ng), as well as the Quaternary Pingyuan Formation (Qp). Specifically, the Shahejie Formation (Es) can be further subdivided from bottom to top into Sha-3 (Es3), Sha-2 (Es2), and Sha-1 (Es1), while the Dongying Formation is divided into Dong-3 (Ed3), Dong-2 (Ed2), and Dong-1 (Ed1) (Figure 3).

Comprehensive column chart and reservoir-seal association features of the Chenghai area.

Influenced by the tectonic movements during the Yanshanian period, the residual Mesozoic strata beneath the Cenozoic formations in the Chenghai area are divided by the Yang’erzhuang -Zhaobei Fault. The downthrown block retains relatively thick and well-preserved Middle to Lower Triassic, Middle to Lower Jurassic, and Upper Jurassic to Lower Cretaceous formations. Conversely, the uplifted block in most areas preserves only partial Triassic formations, with localized regions such as the Zhuanghai 2 × 1 well area retaining a small extent of the Middle to Lower Jurassic strata.15,16

During the Eocene (Es) epoch of the Paleogene, the study area underwent significant changes as a result of a large-scale intrusion of lake water, marking the transition to a new phase of Cenozoic basin evolution. The Es deposits extensively overlay formations from different ages on the Chengning Uplift, and the sedimentary characteristics are controlled by both the boundaries and internally synchronous faults. The Es formation is a primary exploration target in the region, especially the dark shales of Es3 and Es1 developed during two significant transgressive events, which represent the most crucial source rocks in the area (Figure 3).

During the Es3 sedimentation period, it marked the first significant transgressive event of the Cenozoic era, depositing a set of multicycle dark lacustrine sand-mudstones with a thickness ranging from 500 to 1200 m. The lower part of the formation is dominated by brown mudstones interbedded with sand and gravel layers, with sand-gravel layer thickness ranging from approximately 7 to 15 m. The upper part consists of deep gray mudstones interbedded with sandstones, with sandstone thickness ranging from 3 to 20 m, showing an overall coarsening-upward sequence. This formation overlies the Paleogene formations from north to south, exhibiting a characteristic of being thicker in the north and thinner in the south. Es3 serves as the primary source and reservoir rock in the region, widely distributed north of the Yang’erzhuang Fault, and it is unconformably in contact with the underlying Paleogene formations.

Es2 is the result of deposition during a subsequent transgressive period following the uplift and erosion at the end of Es3, with a thickness ranging from 150 to 250 m. It comprises gray to dark gray mudstones interbedded with sandstones, exhibiting fine lithology and showing a characteristic of being thicker in the north and thinner in the south. This formation unconformably contacts Es3 and is primarily distributed in the downthrown block of the Yang’erzhuang -Zhaobei Fault.

Drilling data reveals that Es1 is widely distributed in the study area with a thickness ranging from 200 to 400 m. It conformably contacts Es2 and unconformably overlies Es3, extending southward to the upthrown block of the Zhaobei Fault. Formed during the midterm of the second Cenozoic transgressive event, Es1 represents a stable water environment with extensive sedimentation. It is characterized by thick layers of dark lacustrine mudstones and is one of the main source rocks for oil in the region. The lower and middle parts of Es1 exhibit a distinct lithological combination of dark gray muddy siltstones, oil shales, and dark gray mudstones interbedded with thin layers of grayish-white muddy dolomite. The upper part consists of interbedded dark gray mudstones and thin layers of light gray silty sandstone.

Drilling data reveals that the Ed formation is distributed north of the Yang’erzhuang Fault, with a thickness ranging from 300 to 800 m. The strata are more complete near the center of the basin and thin out toward the southern edge of the Chengning Uplift. During the Ed period, the basin experienced contraction, resulting in deltaic sedimentation. The study area is far from the northern provenance of the Yanshanian movement, mainly characterized by delta front and pro-delta facies, displaying an overall retrogradation pattern. In the early and middle stages, due to limited sediment supply, a large set of gray mudstones interbedded with thin sandstones was deposited. In the later stages, as the lake water receded and sediment supply increased, coarser lithologies such as gray mudstones interbedded with fine sandstones and siltstones predominated. At the end of the Ed period, the basin gradually disappeared due to tectonic uplift during the Himalayan orogenic movement, leading to regional unconformities between the Paleogene and Neogene formations (Figure 3).

After the uplift and erosion during the late Ed period, the Neogene basin entered a subsidence stage in the Cenozoic era. The sedimentary range expanded, the source area shifted backward, and the deposition was mainly characterized by braided and meandering river systems, forming extensive sand-mud alluvial plains. The Ng formation has a thickness of 300 to 400 m, characterized by coarser upper beds and finer lower beds. The upper part consists of gray sandstone and gravel interbedded with gray-green mudstone, while the lower part consists of interbedded gray-green mudstone and gray sandstone. It shows an angular unconformity with the underlying Paleogene and is progressively onlapped toward the Chengning Uplift slope, even reaching an onlap termination.

The Nm formation has a thickness of 800 to 1000 m and can be divided into upper and lower segments based on lithological characteristics. The lower segment is characterized by finer lithology, dominated by reddish-brown and brownish-yellow mudstones interbedded with gray-green sandstone. The upper segment has relatively coarser lithology, consisting of reddish-brown and yellowish-green mudstones interbedded with sandstone.

The Qp formation represents coastal-interaction deposits, consisting of gray and yellow sand layers interbedded with brownish-yellow clay, with a thickness of approximately 200 to 300 m.

Source–Reservoir–Cap Combination Features

Hydrocarbon Generation Conditions

Spatial and Temporal Distribution of Hydrocarbon Source Rocks

The Qikou Sag is the largest hydrocarbon-generating sag in the Huanghua Depression, adjacent to the Chenghai area in the south (Figure 2). It is characterized by a large and open rift-type basin with continuous subsidence, providing favorable conditions for organic-rich sedimentation due to its open water, abundant organic matter, and high sedimentation rates. The sag has developed multiple sets of excellent hydrocarbon source rocks within various lacustrine system domains, primarily including Es3, Es2, Es1, and Ed four sets of oil-bearing stratigraphic sequences (Figure 3). The maximum cumulative thickness of oil-bearing rocks exceeds 3500 m, with Es3 and Es1 dark mudstones being the main source rocks, accumulating to thicknesses of 1500 to 2500 m.18

The Es3 source rock is a product of the first large-scale transgression in the early Eocene, representing the first oil-bearing stratigraphic sequence in the Qikou Sag. It has two thickness centers, one located approximately 20 km north of the Chenghai area with a thickness exceeding 1.5 km, and the other about 10 km west of the Chenghai area with a thickness greater than 350 m. During the Es2 deposition period, the water depth was shallow, the range was small, and the sediment and mudstone thickness significantly reduced. Sandstones were well developed, and the source rock belonged to a marginal shallow lake facies mudstone with relatively poor development. The thickness in the main part of the sag ranged from 300 to 400 m. In the Es1 deposition period, there was strong fault activity, significant extension in the study area, deepening of the lake, expanded sedimentation range, and the formation of another major oil-bearing stratigraphic sequence in the sag. The thickness center of the source rock is located approximately 20 km south of the Chenghai area, with an overall thickness of about 1.2 km. During the Ed deposition period, a large-scale transgression occurred, leading to subsequent regression after overcompensated sedimentation, causing a contraction in the distribution range of source rocks. Although Ed mudstone thickness is significant, exceeding 700 m in both the Zhangbei area and the western Chenghai area, only the mudstone between Ed3 and Ed2 is considered a good source rock, with various oil-bearing conditions inferior to Es.

Organic Matter Abundance, Types, and Maturity of Hydrocarbon Source Rocks

A sufficient amount of organic matter in rocks is the material basis for the formation of oil and gas and is the main factor determining the hydrocarbon generation capacity of rocks. Organic matter abundance is usually used to represent the relative content of organic matter contained in rocks and to measure and evaluate the hydrocarbon generation potential of rocks. Currently, commonly used organic matter abundance indicators mainly include organic carbon content (TOC), rock pyrolysis parameters (hydrocarbon generation potential, S1 + S2), chloroform bitumen “A″ and total hydrocarbon (HC) content, etc. Hydrocarbon source rock pyrolysis is a conventional analytical method in modern oil and gas exploration. The relationship between organic carbon content and hydrocarbon generation potential of hydrocarbon source rock pyrolysis (S1 + S2) is commonly used to discriminate the grade of hydrocarbon source rocks. The relationship between hydrocarbon generation potential S1 + S2 and TOC of hydrocarbon source rocks in the Chenghai area is shown in Figure 4a. It can be known from the figure that there is an approximately linear relationship between hydrocarbon generation potential and organic carbon content of hydrocarbon source rocks, and most samples belong to the medium–good grade of hydrocarbon source rocks.

Characteristics of hydrocarbon source rocks in the Chenghai area. (a) Organic matter abundance. (b) Organic matter type. (c) Degree of thermal evolution.

The hydrocarbon generation capacity of different types of organic matter varies greatly. The Paleogene in the Chenghai area was lacustrine sedimentation. During the development period of the lake basin, due to different sedimentary environments and differences in organic matter input, the types of organic matter changed. The kerogen organic element method can be used to make a detailed division of the organic matter types in different horizons and slope zones in the Chenghai area. After calculating the atomic ratios of H/C and O/C, and plotting all the samples in the Van Krevelen diagram (Figure 4b), it can be seen that the organic matter types in the Chenghai area are mainly type II2, and a small number of samples are type II1, which have good potential for generating oil and gas.

Vitrinite reflectance (Ro) is a widely used parameter that effectively characterizes the maturity of source rocks. The relationship between the measured vitrinite reflectance of hydrocarbon source rocks and depth in the Chenghai area is shown in Figure 4c. It can be seen from the figure that when the depth of hydrocarbon source rocks is about 3000m, Ro reaches 0.5%, and the organic matter begins to enter the hydrocarbon generation threshold; when the depth reaches about 3500m, Ro reaches 0.7%, and the organic matter begins to generate a large amount of oil; when the depth exceeds 4300m, Ro is 1%, and the organic matter enters the high maturity stage; when the depth exceeds 4800m, Ro is 1.3%, and the organic matter enters the overmaturity stage. Overall, the maturity of organic matter in the Chenghai area is at the low-maturity to maturity stage. During the late stages of Nmx deposition, Es1 source rocks entered the maturity threshold, initiating substantial hydrocarbon expulsion. At present, Es3 source rocks in the northern Zhaojiapu region have entered the hydrocarbon generation threshold, experiencing a peak in hydrocarbon generation. In the central Qikou depression area, Ro values for Es3 can reach 3.5%, signifying entry into the postmaturity stage. Hydrocarbon generation in Es3 source rocks begins during the Es1 period, with a significant increase in hydrocarbon expulsion during the late Ed period, reaching a peak during the late stages of Nmx deposition.16,17

Reservoir Conditions

In the Chenghai area, four major sets of reservoirs are developed from bottom to top: the Es and Ed of the Paleogene, and the Ng and Nm of the Neogene (Figure 3). Influenced by tectonic evolution and sedimentary sources, different types of sand bodies develop during different periods in various regions. During the Es deposition period, various types of sand bodies such as nearshore fan bodies, limestone gravel cone fan bodies, and coastal shoals and barriers are predominantly developed. In the Ed deposition period, lake delta sand bodies are the main feature. During the deposition periods of Ng and Nm, fan-shaped rivers and meandering river sand bodies are widely developed. These vertically and horizontally intersecting sand bodies, with weak diagenetic processes, exhibit favorable petrophysical conditions. Particularly, the Neogene sandstones are characterized by high porosity and permeability, creating excellent reservoir space for oil and gas accumulation in the Chenghai area.

Cap Rock Conditions

The Chenghai area exhibits favorable cap rock conditions with three regional cap rock sets developed from bottom to top: Es1, Ed2, and Nm (Figure 3). Among them, the central mudstone layer of Es1 has the widest distribution range, substantial thickness, mature expulsion of hydrocarbons, and remains in a subpressured state. It combines physical closure, overpressure closure, and hydrocarbon concentration closure mechanisms, demonstrating excellent sealing performance. Additionally, Ed2 consists of a continuous and thick mudstone layer with good sealing capacity. These two sets of regional cap rocks play a crucial role in preserving hydrocarbons in the Paleogene reservoirs. The lower part of Nm, characterized by meandering river deposition, features mud-clast sand with a mud-to-sand ratio of 1:3. The mudstone is 5–20m thick, locally reaching 130–170m, exhibiting significant thickness, stable distribution, and effective sealing performance. The presence of this regional cap rock set is crucial in preventing the escape of hydrocarbons from the lower formations. Almost every upper part of the reservoir is covered by thick mudstone layers, serving as local cap rocks. Together with the three sets of regional cap rocks widely developed in the area, they collectively form an impermeable layer preventing hydrocarbon migration.

Paleoenvironmental Characteristics of the Main Hydrocarbon Source Rocks in the Chenghai Area

The vertical variation diagrams of elements such as CaO/Al2O3, Sr/Al2O3, Sr/Ba and V/(V+Ni) can be used to invert the paleoenvironment of the main hydrocarbon source rocks (diagram). Establishing a comprehensive elemental columnar profile can more intuitively analyze the vertical variations of various parameters in the Es3 hydrocarbon source rocks in the Chenghai area. The two parameters of CaO/Al2O3 and Sr/Al2O3 respectively represent that the aquatic source is dominant and the terrigenous clastic input is dominant, and the difference can be used to analyze the enrichment degree of the aquatic source elements relative to the terrigenous clastic input and the changes in the water body properties. The Sr/Ba value is relatively sensitive to the changes of paleosalinity and is often used to discriminate the sedimentary environment type and restore the paleosalinity of the water body. Generally, when the Sr/Ba value is greater than 1, it indicates marine saline water deposition, when the Sr/Ba value is less than 0.6, it indicates continental fresh water deposition, and when the Sr/Ba value is 0.6–1, it indicates transitional facies brackish water deposition.19 V/(V+Ni) is a commonly used parameter indicating the oxidation and reduction conditions of the sedimentary environment. When the V/(V+Ni) value is greater than 0.60, it indicates an anaerobic reducing environment; when the V/(V+Ni) value is 0.46–0.60, it indicates an oxygen-deficient sedimentary environment; when the V/(V+Ni) value is less than 0.46, it indicates an oxygen-rich sedimentary environment.19

It can be seen from Figure 5 that the values of CaO/Al2O3, Sr/Al2O3 and Sr/Ba in the Es3x formation are generally large and have a large fluctuation range as a whole, and show a gradually decreasing trend from bottom to top, indicating that the aquatic source elements are relatively enriched compared to the terrigenous clastic input, suggesting that the salinity of the lake water is relatively high and there is a salinization phenomenon in the lake basin. The large fluctuation range of the parameter values indicates that the sedimentary environment is not stable. The hydrocarbon source rocks of Es3z and Es3x in the Chenghai area are interactive high-quality hydrocarbon source rocks, and the sediments are sensitive to climate changes, and the lithological combination shows a good rhythm. The gradually decreasing trend of its parameter values from bottom to top indicates a gradual decrease in the salinity of the lake water. Judging from the V/(V + Ni) ratio, a numerical mutation occurs at a depth of approximately 5050m, rapidly changing from an ordered state to a disordered state. The fluctuating change of this parameter may be a manifestation of unstable material source input and lake water chemical characteristics caused by climate change. The values of CaO/Al2O3, Sr/Al2O3 and Sr/Ba in the Es 3s formation are low and the orderliness increases, indicating that the enrichment degree of aquatic source elements further decreases compared to the terrigenous clastic input.

Oil Source Comparison in Chenghai Area

After analyzing various biomarker parameters pertaining to organic input and sedimentary conditions, which remain relatively unaffected by secondary alterations such as biodegradation and migration, we identified 13 geochemical indicators that effectively differentiate between different types of crude oil in the Chenghai area. These indicators include Pr/Ph, tricyclic terpane/pentacyclic terpane, C24TeT/C26TT, hopane C29/C30, Ga/C30H, Ts/Tm, C29 sterane 20S/(20S + 20R), C29 sterane ββ/(ββ+αα), C27 rearranged sterane/regular sterane, Ga/C31H, homohopane/hopane, sterane C27/C29, and regular sterane/hopane. They are instrumental in determining the genetic relationship of crude oil samples in the Chenghai area. Based on these distinct characteristics, the crude oil in the Chenghai area is categorized into two types: Chenghai Type A crude oil and Chenghai Type B crude oil (refer to Figure 6a,b for visual representation).

Crude oil classification and oil-source correlation in Chenghai area (a ∼ b, GC-MS spectra of crude oil in Chenghai area; c ∼ d, the parameter characteristic diagram of biomarker compounds of crude oil in Chenghai area; e ∼ f, characteristics of biomarker compounds of source rocks in Chenghai area).

Based on the oil and gas geochemical composition information provided by mass spectrometry, the regular steranes of ααα20RC27, C28, C29 in the two types of crude oil exhibit L-type distribution characteristics, with dominance by C27 steranes. Additionally, the distribution of n-alkanes appears approximately bimodal, indicating a continental origin for both crude oil types. Chenghai Type A crude oil primarily originates from the Es3 reservoir, situated within the Zhangjuhe and Wangxuzhuang oilfields in the Chenghai area. Samples of this crude oil display relatively high Pr/Ph values (ranging from 0.90 to 1.69), C24TeT/C26TT ratios (ranging from 0.91 to 1.29), and C27 rearranged sterane/regular sterane ratios (ranging from 0.11 to 0.43). Conversely, they exhibit relatively low Ga/C30H ratios (ranging from 0.08 to 0.15), Ga/C31H ratios (ranging from 0.11 to 0.39), and regular sterane/hopane ratios (ranging from 0.11 to 0.33). Regarding geochemical parameters related to the organic matter source, the C24TeT/C26TT ratio is higher, the regular sterane/hopane value is lower (with an average of 0.20), and the sterane C27/C29 is widely distributed (ranging from 0.82 to 1.33), indicating mixed organic matter input characteristics for Chenghai Type A crude oil. Furthermore, higher values of C27 rearrangement/regular sterane (>0.39) and Pr/Ph (>1.57), coupled with lower Ga/C30H (<0.15) in Chenghai Type A crude oil, suggest weak reduction-reduction and freshwater deposition conditions. The average value of αααC29 20S/(20S + 20R) is approximately 0.39, and the distribution range of C29ββ/(ββ+αα) falls between 0.32 and 0.53, indicating that Chenghai Type A crude oil has reached maturity (refer to Figure 6a for illustration).

In Chenghai Type B crude oil, as observed from the mass spectrum of terpenoids (m/z 191), the relative content of C24TeT is notably low, while the relative content of gammacerane is comparatively high. Both the C24TeT/C26TT (ranging from 0.33 to 0.80) and regular sterane/hopane (ranging from 0.11 to 0.30) ratios are lower compared to those of Type A. Additionally, sterane C27/C29 exceeds 1.0. These geochemical parameters, along with the sterane distribution characteristics, suggest that organic input in this crude oil type is predominantly from aquatic organisms. Pr/Ph (ranging from 0.30 to 0.89) and Ga/C30H (ranging from 0.51 to 1.33) exhibit the lowest and highest values, respectively, among the two types, indicating deposition conditions involving saltwater and varying reducibility. The ratios of C2920S/(20S + 20R) and C29 ββ/ (ββ+αα) fall within the range of 0.21 to 0.47 and 0.22 to 0.49, respectively, suggesting that the majority of Chenghai Type B crude oil is low-mature oil, with a minor proportion being mature oil (Figure 6b).

Comparing the geochemical composition characteristics serves as an effective and widely utilized method for elucidating the relationship between oil and its source. In the Chenghai area, two distinct types of crude oil are identified based on their geochemical traits. Chenghai Type A crude oil is predominantly situated within the Es3 reservoir of the Zhangjuhe and Wangxuzhuang oilfields within the Chenghai sag. Analysis of biomarker characteristics reveals pronounced features in Chenghai Type A crude oil, characterized by high Pr/Ph, C24TeT/C26TT, and C27 rearranged sterane/regular sterane ratios, along with low gammacerane values and regular sterane/hopane ratios. These findings suggest a sedimentary environment characterized by weak reduction to reduction conditions, with a mixed source of parent material. The geochemical traits of Chenghai Type A crude oil closely align with those of the Es3 source rock, indicating a significant contribution from this source (refer to Figure 6c,e). On the other hand, Chenghai Type B crude oil is widely distributed across the Es1x and Ng strata in the Chenghai area, spanning the Wangxuzhuang and Koucun oilfields in the slope area and the Liuguanzhuang oilfield in the uplift area. This type of crude oil exhibits strong correlation with Es1x source rocks, characterized by high gammacerane levels and regular sterane/hopane ratios, coupled with low Pr/Ph, C24TeT/C26TT, and C27 rearranged sterane/regular sterane ratios. The sedimentary conditions, indicative of saltwater and reducing environments, along with the predominance of organic matter from aquatic organisms, further suggest Es1x source rocks as the primary origin for this crude oil type (refer to Figure 6d,f).

Oil and Gas Exploration Progress in the Chenghai Area

The Chenghai area, located in the Dagang offshore area, is a complex structural zone with alternating depressions and uplifts that have evolved since the Paleogene. The area hosts four sets of hydrocarbon-bearing formations: Es3, Es2, Es1, and Ed2. High-quality reservoirs are well-developed, including alluvial fans, subaqueous fans, deltas, fan deltas, braided river deltas, gravity flows, and lacustrine carbonate rocks, widely distributed in the offshore area. These geological conditions provide the prerequisites for the formation of large and medium-sized oil and gas reservoirs.20

Recent exploration indicates that despite the complex engineering geological conditions, high technical requirements for exploration and development, high costs, and associated risks, the Chenghai area in the offshore environment holds significant oil and gas resources with favorable reservoir conditions.1,20 Since the 1960s, exploration efforts have led to the discovery of eight hydrocarbon-bearing formations, including Nm, Ng, Ed, Es1, Es2, Es3, Mesozoic, and Permian. By the end of 2022, the confirmed geological reserves amounted to 67.17 million tons, with recoverable reserves reaching 37.69 million tons and remaining geological resources totaling 123 million tons. Additionally, several oil and gas structures such as Zhangdong, Zhaodong, Yang’erzhuang, Youyi, Liuguanzhuang, Hai 4 Well Oilfields, as well as Zhangbei, Zhangdongdong, Guanjiapu, and Chenghai, have been discovered. Commercially developed oilfields include Chenghai Oilfield, Zhaodong Oilfield, Zhangdong Oilfield, Zhangdongdong Oilfield, Guanjiapu Oilfield, Yang’erzhuang Oilfield, and Liuguanzhuang Oilfield, showcasing the region’s abundant oil and gas resources and promising exploration prospects. The Chenghai area is recognized as a key exploration area for increasing reserves and production in the Dagang Oilfield and one of the key exploration areas in the offshore and nearshore region of the Bohai Bay Basin.1

Structural Geological Features of Chenghai Area

The Chenghai area, located in the Dagang offshore region within the Qikou sag, is characterized by a highly developed fault system and abundant petroleum resources. By utilizing interpreted data from three-dimensional seismic surveys in the study area, attributes such as fault throw, depth, and hanging wall and footwall of individual faults were extracted. Through quantifying and categorizing different types of faults and their activity characteristics, an in-depth study of the structural geological features of the Chenghai area can be conducted. This can unveil the complex fault structures, activity history, and evolutionary processes of the Chenghai fault zone, aiding in the understanding of the formation process and distribution patterns of oil and gas reservoirs.

Fault Growth Stages and Activity

In the assessment of the causative mechanisms of the complex fault zone in the Qikou Depression, previous studies suggested that the Qikou Depression, since the Eocene Es3, has not undergone significant erosion. Therefore, the fault growth index can be applied to evaluate the fault activity in the Chenghai area.2 When the growth index is greater than 1, it indicates that fault activity is synchronous with sedimentation; when the growth index is less than or equal to 1, it suggests that there was no apparent seismic activity controlling sedimentation at that time, and the fault was in a dormant stage. The buried depth–throw curve reflects the vertical displacement variation pattern at different stratigraphic levels. The minimum value in the curve represents the connection point of the fault, while the maximum value represents the nucleation point of the fault segment. The combination of the fault growth index with the buried depth–throw curve provides a comprehensive characterization of the fault activity phases in the Chenghai area (Figures 7 and 8).

In the Es2 period, between line 2744 and line 2944, and line 3044 to line 3244, the growth index graph indicates a cessation of fault activity, marking a burial period. During the Es1 period, the faults reactivated, and in the Ed period, the fault displayed inheritable development. The Ng period saw a substantial reduction in fault activity. The buried depth–throw curve of the Qidong fault exhibits two maximum values and one minimum value. The nucleation point of the fault is located at the Es3 maximum, and the minimum indicates the vertical growth connection point in the Es2 stratum. The local fault activity interruption during the Es2 period and subsequent reactivation in the Es1 period are evident (Figures 7a, 8a). Therefore, the Qidong fault development pattern can be summarized as intense activity–local activity cessation–reactivation–steady subsidence.

Similarly, the Zhangbei fault has been continuously active since the Es3 period, without a burial period. During the Es1 period, the fault’s control over sedimentation was most prominent. The buried depth–throw curve for the Zhangbei fault is approximately linear overall, with throw values gradually decreasing with decreasing fault burial depth. The maximum throw occurs at the basal level, and the nucleation point is in the Es3 period or earlier, indicating that the Zhangbei fault is a basal resurrection-type fault controlled by preexisting faults in the basement (Figures 7b and 8b).

The Zhangdong-Hai4 fault, despite the stratigraphic loss at line 3044–3144, can be considered an ongoing fault as most growth indices are greater than 1 (Figure 7c). The Zhaobei fault exhibits Es2 stratum loss at line 2094–2394 and displays a clear two-peak-one-valley pattern in growth indices during the Es3 period, a characteristic of segmented fault growth connections (Figure 7d).

The Nandagang fault shows significant control over sedimentation in the Es3 period, gradually diminishing toward both sides. Line 1544–1944 exhibits stratigraphic loss during the Es2 period, and fault activity ceases at line 1944 during the Es1 period. The buried depth–throw curve at line 1944 displays a minimum value, suggesting segmented fault growth for the Nandagang fault, with this point being the segmented growth point (Figures 7e and 8c).

The F11 fault exhibits inactivity during the Es2 and Es3 periods, becomes active from the Es1 period onward, and the growth index gradually decreases. The buried depth–throw curve for F11 shows only one maximum value, located at the Es1 period. The nucleation point has the maximum throw value and gradually decreases on both sides, indicating that F11 is a cosedimentary fault (Figures 7f and 8d).

Fault Activity Intensity and Growth Propagation Mode

The throw-distance curve is a graph with the horizontal axis representing the planar position of a fault and the vertical axis representing the throw value. The throw-distance curve for an isolated fault typically exhibits a semielliptical shape, while the curve for a fault with segmented growth and connection shows a wave-like pattern characterized by multiple sets of maximum and minimum throw values. The minimum values on the curve indicate the segmented growth and connection points of the fault, while the maximum values represent the nucleation positions of fault segments.21 Fault activity rate refers to the ratio of the displacement formed by fault activity to the corresponding sedimentary time, providing a quantitative measure of the intensity of fault activity. The combined use of throw-distance curves and fault activity rate curves allows for a comprehensive characterization of fault activity intensity and its growth and propagation patterns (Figure 9).

Fault throw-distance curves and fault activity rate curves in the Chenghai area.

The f11 fault represents a typical isolated growth fault, and its throw-distance curve exhibits an overall semielliptical shape. The maximum throw is at the central point, gradually decreasing toward the periphery, reaching zero at the end points. The central part of the fault has larger throw and activity rate, while the sides have smaller values. The prominent wave peaks are concentrated in the central part of the fault, and on the fault plane, it shows an almost straight-line configuration. The f11 fault started its activity in the Es1 period and has gradually weakened since then (Figure 9a,b).

During the Es3 period, the Zhangbei fault was an isolated growth fault. In the Es2 period, an abrupt drop in activity rate occurred in the middle-northern part of the Zhangbei fault, indicating a discontinuity in fault activity (Figure 9c,d, indicated by arrows). This point represents a segmented growth point where the activity rate significantly decreases, signifying a segmented growth phase. The fault activity continued the trend observed in the Es2 period during the Es1 period, and it became more pronounced. By the Ed period, the segmented growth of the fault gradually diminished, and by the Ng and Nm periods, there was no apparent segmentation. The overall structural evolution of the Zhangbei fault includes isolated growth, segmented growth, connected growth, and gradual burial (Figure 9c,d).

Similarly, the Qidong fault has at least two segmented growth points (Figure 9e,f, indicated by arrows). The Qidong fault began segmented growth in the Es3 period, with weakened segmented growth during the Ed period. In the Ng period, it began to gradually connect and evolve into an independently developing fault. The overall structural evolution of the Qidong fault involves initial segmented growth, growth connection, and independent fault development.

Joint analysis using fault growth indices, burial-depth throw curves, throw-distance curves, and fault activity rate curves enables the classification of the main faults in the Chenghai area into three types: basement-controlled continuously active faults, burial-reactivated segmented growth faults, and late-stage continuously active isolated growth faults.

Basement-Controlled Continuously Active Faults (e.g., Zhangbei Fault): These faults formed before the basin’s development, exhibiting linear burial-depth throw curves and growth indices consistently greater than 1. Inherited development is evident, and the noncontinuous stress applied by the linear basement significantly influences the local stress field, playing a crucial role in controlling the basin’s structural pattern, rift configuration, and strain distribution.

Burial-Reactivated Segmented Growth Faults (e.g., Qidong Fault): Initially, these faults had noncontinuous sliding rates on the hanging wall, maintaining a continuous sliding state without segmented activity. After a period of burial and inactivity spanning millions of years (Ma), reactivation leads to segmented growth due to significant differences in sliding rates. Each segment may activate at different times, causing sliding and connection at different positions along the fault.

Late-Stage Continuously Active Isolated Growth Faults (e.g., f11 Fault): These faults are relatively small in overall scale, mostly formed during the rift II phase (Es1 and Ed periods). They experience extensional stress in a single direction, without significant torsional structural features. Growth indices show a higher value in the central part during the same period, resembling a normal distribution. The burial-depth throw curve exhibits a single maximum value, linearly converging below the maximum, while the throw-distance curve and fault activity rate curve often present a semicircular pattern, with higher values in the central part than on the sides.

Tectonic Evolution History of the Chenghai Area

Based on the scale and roles of faults in a rift basin, previous researchers classified faults in rift basins into three categories: first-order faults, with the largest scale, reaching lengths of up to hundreds of kilometers, typically appearing as shovel-shaped in cross-section, controlling the formation of oil-producing sag basins; second-order faults, with a relatively smaller scale, around tens of kilometers in length, typically serving as the basement faults of basin margins; third-order faults, with the smallest scale, representing small faults developed within basins or secondary faults derived from main faults. These faults usually have lengths less than ten kilometers and appear as sheet-like in cross-section. When combined, they form a dense fault zone, often controlling the formation of fault blocks.22 In the Chenghai area, faults are densely distributed, with a prevailing northward dip, constituting about three-fourths of the faults. Based on the cross-cutting relationships and development scale,22 the study area lacks obvious first-order faults. The main faults in the Chenghai area are all second-order faults. From north to south, these include the Qizhong Fault, f11 Fault, Qidong Fault, Nandagang Fault, Zhangbei Fault, Zhangdong-Hai4 Fault, Zhaobei Fault, and Yang’erzhuang Fault (Figure 2). Statistical analysis of fault trends in the Chenghai area is presented in a rose diagram (Figure 10). The results indicate that during the Es3–Es2 period, the predominant trend of faults is NEE, suggesting that the extensional stress direction during the rifting I phase is primarily NW-SE. In the Es1 and Ed periods, the main trend of faults shifts from NEE to nearly EW, reflecting a slight change in the extensional stress direction during the rifting II phase. At this time, due to the inconsistent direction between the extension and the normal direction of the basement faults, oblique extension occurs, leading to a right-lateral slip component within the fault zone. During the Ng and Nm periods, the main trend of faults is nearly EW, indicating that extensional deformation still predominates within the fault zone, but a right-lateral slip component persists.

Rose diagram of fault trends in the Chenghai area.

In the early 20th century, the renowned scholar Chamberlain first introduced the concept of balanced cross-section techniques, assuming that the deformed upper layer’s area remains constant during the process of tectonic deformation. Chamberlain applied this concept to calculate the depth of the basement detachment surface in orogenic belts using balanced cross-section techniques.23 Currently, balanced cross-section techniques play a crucial role in the field of oil and gas exploration and are widely used for correcting geological interpretation schemes in basins, structural analysis in extensional basins, and basin modeling.2,24−26

The overall structural pattern of the fault zone in the Chenghai area is controlled by the tectonic evolution process of the Qikou Depression. Combining previous studies on the Qikou Depression,25 the tectonic evolution history of the Chenghai area was reconstructed using balanced cross-section techniques (Figure 11). The Neogene tectonic evolution in the Chenghai area is divided into four stages:①Pre-Paleogene Basement Formation Stage; ②Strong Extensional Stage of Rifting I; ③Slight Oblique Deflection of Extensional Stress and Intense Deformation of Rifting II; ④Post-Miocene Weak Extensional Deformation–Stable Burial Stage. The periods of Rifting I and Rifting II are collectively referred to as the Strong Rifting Period.

Tectonic evolution history of the Chenghai area (AA’ profile location shown in Figure 2).

In the Early Proterozoic, the North China Craton formed as a unified and intact landmass. From the Midto-Late Proterozoic to the Paleozoic, the craton underwent continental reorganization, entering a stable developmental stage. Since the Middle Paleozoic, a series of rift zones and basins developed in the North China Craton, often associated with magmatic activity. These tectonic activities played a role in the reformation of the ancient landmass, although block assembly had not yet occurred during this period. In the Paleozoic era, the North China Craton underwent overall uplift and subsidence movements, marking an important period for the development of basin cover layers in the North China region.27

During the Ordovician, the North China Craton formed a stable Cambrian-Ordovician marine carbonate platform. In the Silurian, the craton experienced compression and uplift, followed by weathering and erosion. In the Early Carboniferous, the craton underwent slow overall subsidence and entered a shallow marine environment, filling the Carboniferous-Permian strata. In the Mesozoic, the North China Craton entered a stage of destruction, experiencing multiple transformations during the Indosinian, Yanshanian, and Himalayan tectonic movements.27−30 These events led to significant changes in the physical and chemical properties of the lithospheric mantle, resulting in the loss of about 100 km of lithospheric mantle.

In the Triassic, the strata underwent compression and uplift, and the Triassic strata in the southern part of the Chenghai area were completely eroded. In the Jurassic, the North China Craton experienced left-lateral strike-slip motion, generating strike-slip shear zones.31 This left-lateral strike-slip motion was primarily localized in the southern part of the Tanlu Fault Zone, where the preexisting and steep Tanlu Fault Zone was only locally active. In the early Cretaceous, metamorphic core complexes in the central and eastern parts of the North China Craton indicated a WNW-ESE regional extension direction. The Bohai Bay Basin developed mainly with NE-NNE-trending rift basins, normal faults, and NW-trending transfer faults. This extensional tectonics may have been related to the high-angle subduction or increased subduction rate of the ancient Pacific Plate.31 This high-angle subduction caused intense extension and destruction in the eastern part of the North China Craton.

Therefore, in the Chenghai area of the Qikou Depression, the basement of the Cenozoic basins mainly developed along preexisting NNE, NE, and NW-trending faults. During the later stages of tectonic deformation, these preexisting structures were reactivated, influencing the structural geological patterns in the Chenghai area.

Strong Extensional Stage of Rifting I: During this period, intense tectonic activity and significant crustal extension occurred, resulting in a substantial increase in extensional strain compared to the Es3 period, with a forward extension rate increase of 7.6%. The Qizhong Fault exhibited the most vigorous activity during this phase, with vertical throw exceeding 2 km and a pronounced control on sedimentation. Main faults such as the Yang’erzhuang Fault, Zhaobei Fault, Zhangdong Fault, Qidong Fault, and f6 Fault were highly active, and fault throws were concentrated along these main faults. Influenced by the inherited development of faults, the throws of the Qidong Fault and Zhangdong Fault gradually expanded, cutting through the entire stratigraphy. The fault control effects strengthened progressively, leading to the rotation of strata and the gradual development of wedge-shaped structures in the hanging wall. The Qizhong Fault and Zhangdong Fault had opposite fault dip directions to the slope direction. The blocks between these two faults exhibited rotational tilting, making it easier for sand bodies to accumulate on the downthrown side of the fault. This phenomenon hindered the transport of sand bodies toward the northern depression area. The f6 Fault, which was active during the Early Paleogene, experienced a significant reduction in activity during the first rifting episode until it became inactive. It had no apparent control on sedimentary deposition and could not provide sufficient strain space. As a result, the block between the Zhangdong and Zhaobei Faults experienced reverse secondary adjustment fractures due to continuous extension.

Slight Oblique Deflection of Extensional Stress and Intense Deformation of Rifting II: During this stage, tectonic activity remained intense, with a 7.0% increase in the pre-extension rate compared to Es1. The rose diagram of fault trends (Figure 10) indicates a slight change in the extensional stress direction during the second rifting episode, leading to a right-lateral slip component due to oblique extension. In Es1, the hanging wall of the Qizhong Fault became progressively complex, forming numerous coexisting extensional and contractional adjustment fractures. Influenced by the Zhangdong Fault and Qizhong Fault, strata formed a distinct wedge-shaped body, with strata thinning gradually from south to north. This is attributed to the inherited development of the Zhangdong Fault and Qizhong Fault, resulting in a continuous reduction in the angle between the fault dip direction and the slope direction. The rotation and tilting of fault blocks became more pronounced during this period. At this time, sand bodies tended to accumulate preferentially in the low-lying areas of the hanging wall, leading to less sediment transport to the higher positions of the hanging wall. Between the Zhangdong and Zhaobei Faults, a slope body formed with a few reverse adjustment fractures that were inactive and in a buried state, resulting in stable sedimentation within the slope body. In the Ed period, adjustment fractures in the hanging wall of the Qizhong Fault gradually entered a burial period, with only the main faults and anticlinal faults remaining active. Secondary faults ceased activity, and there was no penetration through the Ed strata. The synchronous development of the Qidong Fault and Qizhong Fault led to the balanced sedimentation of the Ed strata in the hanging wall of the Qidong Fault, and the wedge-shaped strata ceased to develop. During the second rifting episode, a horsetail-shaped fault developed at the top of the Qidong Fault, displaying numerous Y-shaped fault combinations on the cross-section. The horsetail-shaped fault formed during Es1 may be related to the change in the extensional stress direction from NW-SE to nearly SN during that period.

From Es3 to Ed, examining the fault-controlled variations in stratal thickness reveals that the fault controlling the Es3 strata with the most significant thickness variation is the Qizhong Fault. For the Es1 strata, the Qidong-Zhangdong Fault exhibits the greatest control on thickness variation, while for the Ed strata, the Zhaobei Fault exerts the most substantial influence. Consequently, the fault-controlled ability in the Chenghai area follows a growth sequence and developmental trend of early control in the north and late control in the south, indicating a pattern of northward subsidence and southward uplift.

Weak Extensional Deformation–Stable Burial Stage: During this stage, the extension rate is approximately 3.2%, with the majority of extensional deformation concentrated in the Ng and Nm periods. In the Neogene, the Chenghai area enters a postrifting stage characterized by stable subsidence in the basin. During this period, besides the continued activity of main fault systems, extensional tectonic movements lead to the development of numerous secondary adjusting faults. However, the fault-controlled impact is not significant, and there are few apparent sedimentary differentiation phenomena. In the Quaternary, there is no substantial extensional deformation, and only localized secondary faults penetrate the surface, with no obvious fault-controlled sedimentary effects.

Discussion on Shallow Deformation and Deep Structural Dynamic Processes in the Chenghai Area

The main body of the Huanghua Sag is situated in the central part of the Bohai Bay Basin on the eastern block of the North China Craton, representing one of the manifestations of the destruction of the North China Craton during the Paleogene.32 The sediment thickness in the Paleogene is substantial. The formation and evolution of the fault system in the Chenghai area are closely related to the formation and evolution of the Huanghua Sag. This fault system developed on the hanging wall of a large detachment fault in the crust, and its evolution process was influenced by various factors such as lithospheric extension rate, crustal extension mechanism, and the global geodynamic background. The tectonic dynamic mechanism of the fault system in the Chenghai area can be analyzed with reference to the dynamic mechanisms of the Huanghua Sag and the Bohai Bay Basin.

The Bohai Bay Basin is situated in the eastern part of the Eurasian Plate, bordered by the Pacific Plate to the west, the Philippine Plate to the southwest, and connected to the Indian-Australian Plate in the south.33−37 Its formation and evolution are controlled by the compression, collision, and subduction of surrounding plates, especially the subduction of the Pacific Plate from the southeast toward the Eurasian Plate, which has the most significant impact on the Cenozoic tectonic evolution of the Bohai Bay Basin.37,38 According to the inversion results of the Moho surface and the top surface of the LAB (Lithosphere-Asthenosphere Boundary), seismic tomography characteristics, OBS detection results, etc., both the Moho surface and the top surface of the LAB exhibit an upward doming spatial pattern, which mirrors the Cenozoic rifting of the Bohai Bay Basin.39 This indicates that the primary deep dynamic source of the Cenozoic in the Bohai Bay Basin is the upwelling of the LAB induced by plate subduction. The shallow response induced by deep processes is manifested in the Chenghai area as a positive correlation between extension, sedimentation, and subsidence rates. Furthermore, sedimentation and subsidence rates are negatively correlated with the rate of Pacific Plate subduction toward the Eurasian Plate.

Between approximately 65–42Ma, the subduction of the Pacific Plate beneath the Eurasian Plate induced upwelling of thermal material in the asthenosphere.40 This upwelling generated a NW-SE oriented extensional stress field, leading to crustal thinning.39 This process had a positive impact on tectonic movements along plate boundaries,41−44 representing a cyclic and mutually reinforcing development.

During the Paleogene to Early Eocene (65–40Ma), the Pacific Plate subducted in a northwest-westward (NWW) direction beneath the Eurasian Plate. Eastern China experienced continuous uplift along the intracontinental plate margin. Rigid blocks, such as the Chengning Uplift within the Huanghua Depression, rose simultaneously with troughs. During this period, basement normal faults like the Qizhong Fault and Yang’erzhuang Fault in the Chenghai area had developed, exerting a certain control on sedimentation. The structural manifestation of the basin was characterized by fault-controlled step-like patterns formed by existing faults.

During the Es3 to E2 period (42–33.6Ma), influenced by the thermal mantle upwelling, the Huanghua Depression and the entire Bohai Bay Basin underwent a phase of intense extensional rifting. The basin exhibited a combination of rigid blocks and brittle troughs as structural features. Due to the coexistence of these different tectonic elements, there were significant variations in crustal extensional stress across different regions during this period. The extensional stress also varied slightly in direction, giving rise to a series of NE- and NEE-trending overlapping normal faults.

During the Es1 to Ed period (33.6–24.6Ma), the influence of mantle thermal upwelling remained strong. Additionally, the expansion of the Sea of Japan had an impact on the study area, introducing a compressional tectonic stress field in the NEE direction. This, in turn, affected the main extensional stress direction, transitioning from NW-NNW to NNW and approximately SN directions. The newly formed faults continued to trend predominantly in the NE and NEE directions, creating a series of en-echelon and broom-shaped fault systems. This suggests that during this period, the basement faults in the Chenghai area experienced a right-lateral strike-slip displacement. The sedimentary center was controlled by the topography of the basement, with a predominant disc-shaped fault-controlled sedimentation observed in cross sections. There was no significant migration of large-scale troughs in the sedimentary basin, and the basin remained centered around the main depression in the northeastern part, exhibiting a radial transition toward the periphery. The Chengning Uplift area did not show significant sedimentary deposition exceeding 100 m in thickness during this period.

In the Neogene and Quaternary periods (approximately 24.3Ma to the present), the diminishing dynamics of the mantle’s upwelling on the asthenosphere led to a gradual reduction in regional extensional forces in the Chenghai area. The predominant tectonic influence shifted toward overall accelerated subsidence, as indicated by Xin.39 The main orientation of the remaining extensional stresses became approximately SN, deviating from the NE orientation of the existing basement faults. This resulted in oblique extensional conditions, with prominent characteristics of strike-slip deformation on the newly formed faults. Various feather-like fault combinations, such as the Zhangbei, Nandagang, and Zhangdong-Hai4 fault systems, developed during this period.

In conclusion, a structural dynamics model can be established for the Chenghai area, as depicted in Figure 12. The subduction of the Pacific Plate beneath the Eurasian Plate triggers deep-seated mantle upwelling, inducing NW-SE and approximately SN extensional deformations in the Chenghai area, accompanied by a right-lateral strike-slip component. The former contributes to the fault system oriented in the NE and NEE directions during the rifting I, while the latter forms oblique NEE and approximate EW-oriented strike-slip fault combinations. The unique extensional-strike-slip deformation features in the shallow crust of the Chenghai area represent expressions of deep-seated dynamic processes.

Tectonic evolution dynamics in the Chenghai area.

The overall structural evolution of the Chenghai area fault zone is controlled by the tectonic evolution of the Qikou Depression. The Neogene tectonic activity in the Qikou Depression is primarily divided into the Paleogene strong rifting stage and the Neogene stable subsidence and tilting stage. Specifically, the Paleogene strong rifting stage can be further divided into Rifting Phase I (Es3 to Es2 sedimentary period). During this period, the Qikou Depression was influenced by the subduction of the Pacific Plate beneath the North China Plate, resulting in crustal thinning and mantle upwelling, creating NW-SE extensional stress. This led to the formation of a dense fracture zone primarily oriented in the NE and NEE directions within the Qikou Depression. Rifting Phase II (Es1 to Ed sedimentary period) occurred under the combined influence of the Indian Plate and the Pacific Plate. The extensional stress direction underwent a slight right-rotation, transitioning to a near-SN orientation, resulting in the development of numerous fractures primarily oriented in the NE and NEE directions. During the Neogene Ng and Nm periods, tectonic activity weakened, the controlling effect of the boundary faults diminished, and the basin entered a phase of rapid subsidence.25

Analysis of Fault-Controlled Hydrocarbon Accumulation Modeling in Chenghai Area

Faults with the same trend within a fault zone are referred to as a “fault domain,″ and the region where two sets of fault domains interact is known as the “fault domain boundary.″ Whether in a fault-dense zone caused by extensional factors, a fault-dense zone resulting from rotational factors, or a zone with a combination of extension and rotation, they all exhibit only one fault domain boundary on cross sections. On either side of the fault domain boundary are two fault domains with opposite trends. This phenomenon arises because stresses are released during the formation of faults, leading to stress reduction zones around the faults. When stress reduction zones of two adjacent dipping faults interact, the larger fault tends to inhibit the propagation of the smaller fault. If the two faults have the same trend, their growth is not inhibited. Consequently, faults with the same trend are more likely to concentrate and form fault combinations with “step-like,″ “flower-like,″ and “cabbage-like” structural patterns.45,46

Fault-controlled hydrocarbon accumulation refers to the influence of the formation and evolution of faults on the processes of hydrocarbon generation, migration, accumulation, and preservation. Faults play a crucial role in controlling the sedimentary filling of basins, and their segmented growth process can adjust the migration patterns of troughs, indirectly controlling the spatial distribution of source rocks and affecting the burial history and thermal evolution of source rocks. Faults that communicate with source rocks and are active during the accumulation period can serve as conduits for the vertical migration of hydrocarbons. Faults with strong self-sealing characteristics can provide barriers for the lateral accumulation of oil and gas, forming fault-related closures. Reactivated faults after the accumulation period can disrupt the originally sealed environment of oil and gas reservoirs, causing oil and gas to adjust and migrate along the faults toward shallower depths.

The fault zones in the Chenghai area have undergone multiple stages of stress-induced deformation with different extensional directions, playing a crucial role in the process of hydrocarbon accumulation. Therefore, establishing fault-controlled accumulation models can help deepen our understanding of the hydrocarbon accumulation mechanisms in the Chenghai area, enhance geological insights, and provide effective references for optimizing exploration strategies. Through in-depth analysis of reservoir profiles in the study area, three types of fault-controlled accumulation models have been identified in the Chenghai area: fault-controlled longitudinal migration accumulation model, transverse sealing fault masking accumulation model, and far-source fault-step conduit-controlled accumulation model (Figure 13).

Vertical Accumulation Mode of Fault-Controlled Hydrocarbon Accumulation

In extensional rift basins, the abundance of faults and relatively rapid shifts in sedimentary facies can lead to decreased lateral continuity of conduits for sand bodies. Therefore, in the exploration and development processes of extensional rift basins, the hydrocarbon migration system is characterized by a dominant fault-controlled system, with reservoir bodies playing a secondary role. Among the various modes of fault-controlled systems, vertical migration along faults is the most common pattern.47 In the Chenghai area, this mode of hydrocarbon accumulation is primarily controlled by major faults such as Zhangdong-Hai4, Zhaobei, and Yang’erzhuang, with hydrocarbons distributed in the vicinity of these main controlling faults. Faults, as conduits for hydrocarbon migration, exhibit three main vertical migration modes: hydrocarbons migrating directly upward through fault zones in source rocks, hydrocarbons initially migrating laterally to the fault zones through connected sand bodies and unconformities and then migrating vertically along the faults, and early stage hydrocarbons migrating along faults to shallow traps in the vicinity. Many of the main controlling faults in the area have experienced long-term inheritance activity. In the hanging wall of the fault, various types of traps have formed not only in the form of Quaternary alluvial fans and subaqueous fans, allowing hydrocarbons to migrate to the fault zone, but also on both sides of the fault, resulting in diverse trap types. This provides ample storage space for hydrocarbon accumulation.

During periods of fault activity, early hydrocarbon reservoirs formed by mature source rocks migrate upward along the faults due to the pressure differential. They accumulate on both sides of the faults in suitable traps, forming vertically stacked, composite oil and gas reservoirs. After hydrocarbons migrate along the main fault to shallower depths, they are often influenced by secondary faults, leading to the redistribution of hydrocarbons and their entry into traps at different locations. The “Y″-shaped combination developed in the hanging wall of the main fault is favorable for the enrichment of midshallow hydrocarbons. Oil and gas reservoirs in the Zhaodong oilfield, associated with the “Y″-shaped combination, illustrate this accumulation model (Figure 13A). This reservoir formation pattern is a crucial mechanism controlling the accumulation of midshallow hydrocarbons in the region.

Lateral Sealing Faults Obstructive Control Mode of Hydrocarbon Accumulation

The intrinsic factor causing lateral sealing in faults is the presence of significant permeability contrasts.48,49 The permeability of fault zones directly determines their lateral sealing capacity. In the Chenghai area, lateral sealing faults obstructing oil and gas accumulation can be broadly categorized into two types: juxtaposed sealing and shale smearing sealing. Juxtaposed sealing refers to the juxtaposition of sandstone and shale on both sides of the fault. Due to the lower permeability of shale, it acts as a lateral seal for hydrocarbon migration. The conditions for the formation of juxtaposed sealing faults in the Chenghai area include the presence of abundant interbedded sand-mud formations during the sedimentary process, small fault scales, relatively weak fault deformation, low overall fault fragmentation, and direct contact between the hanging wall and footwall formations.

Shale smearing sealing, on the other hand, mainly occurs in plastic-rich mud formations (containing over 40% clay content). The degree of shear-type shale smearing in the Chenghai area is controlled by the relative strength difference between shale layers and surrounding rocks. Previous studies have quantitatively evaluated the sealing characteristics of the Zhangbei Fault in the Chenghai area.2 The results indicate that the Zhangbei Fault acts as a lateral sealing fault in the Es2, Es1, and Ed formations. The lateral sealing capacity directly controls the degree of hydrocarbon charging within the fault blocks. The sealing type is shale smearing sealing, and oil and gas from the western side of the Chenghai area are uplifted along the Zhangbei Fault and blocked in reverse, leading to hydrocarbon accumulation (Figure 13B).

Far-Source Stepped Fault-Conduction Control Mode

The far-source stepped fault conduit-controlled accumulation model refers to a hydrocarbon transport network composed of interconnected sand bodies, unconformities, and faults, presenting a profile characterized by connected sand bodies (unconformities)–faults–connected sand bodies (unconformities)–fault migration (forming a stepped pattern). The hydrocarbon source rocks are located far from the oil and gas enrichment zone, resulting in long-distance transport, extending laterally for more than 30 km. Oil and gas exhibit a stepped upward migration from deep to shallow layers, accumulating in suitable traps to form oil and gas reservoirs. In the Chenghai area, these reservoirs can migrate to the southern slope of Chenghai. This complex migration and accumulation pattern is widespread in the region, with faults predominantly playing a role in vertical transport, thus dominating the formation of oil and gas reservoirs.

In the Qikou main depression and the western Chenghai area, the oil and gas generated first migrate laterally along sand bodies or unconformities in the faulted depression. Some of the hydrocarbons accumulate in effective traps during lateral migration, while others migrate vertically along fault planes, resulting in vertical oil and gas distribution. Part of the hydrocarbons accumulates in effective traps, while those that do not continue to migrate laterally along interconnected sand bodies, unconformities, or faults until encountering suitable traps, forming a stepped or tiered migration and accumulation pattern (Figure 13C). This stepped fault-controlled oil and gas accumulation model is observed in areas such as Zhangdong, Zhaodong, Yang’erzhuang, and Guanjiapu.

Discussion

To explore the genesis mechanism of fault-block hydrocarbon reservoirs in the Chenghai area, this study extensively dissected the hydrocarbon accumulation patterns based on actual reservoir profiles from oil fields like Zhaobei and Zhangdong. It conducted a detailed analysis to delve into the controlling factors behind the formation of fault-controlled hydrocarbon reservoirs in the study area.

The Structural Background of Basement Uplift Shapes Preexisting Main Controlling Faults

The Chenghai area is characterized by a stepped structural belt developed on the background of the Paleozoic-Mesozoic ancient slope. It began to take shape since the early Paleogene and, during the Mesozoic, accompanied the development of the Chengning uplift, forming a preliminary structural pattern characterized by a north–south high-low alternation and an east–west ridge-depression alternation. In the Paleogene, major controlling faults developed, and the stepped structures, progressively uplifting from the Qikou depression to the Chengning uplift, gradually took shape. During this period, numerous structural traps, lithological traps, structural-lithological traps, and stratigraphic traps were formed. The Neogene represents the completion phase of stepped structures, with weakened tectonic activity. While some faults exhibit inherited activity, they no longer control sedimentation. Under the influence of neotectonic movements, a series of middle to shallow traps were formed. Using the Zhangdong-Hai4 fault, Zhaobei fault, and the southern fault of Yang’erzhuang as boundaries, it can be inferred that the Chenghai area has a southern slope area and multiple stepped areas, forming a “three-step, one-slope” structural pattern. This long-term development of stepped structures provides a favorable geological background for lateral, vertical, and mixed migration of oil and gas2. This unique structural geological background not only determines the region as an inherited low-relief area, constantly in the direction of oil and gas migration but also provides a complex three-dimensional network system as the migration pathway. After the initial migration from the source rock, oil and gas typically follow a transport system composed of connected sand bodies (unconformities)–faults–connected sand bodies (unconformities)–faults, forming a complex stepped migration path and accumulating in various traps developed in the slope background of this region.

Long-Term Inherited Main Controlling Faults Control the Macro Distribution of Oil and Gas

The Chenghai area is characterized by well-developed faults of varying sizes, mainly trending northeast to east–west. Notable faults include Qidong, Zhangdong, Hai4, Zhaobei, Yang’erzhuang, and the southern fault of Yang’erzhuang. These major controlling faults, originating from the Paleogene and exhibiting long-term inherited growth, play a crucial role in controlling the regional tectonics, stratigraphic deposition, and trap formation. They serve as the primary oil-source faults in the area, connecting oil and gas sources with reservoirs, and consequently governing the pattern of hydrocarbon accumulation. Examining the distribution characteristics of discovered oil and gas reservoirs, it is evident that these major controlling faults influence the macroscopic distribution of hydrocarbons. Oil and gas reservoirs are primarily distributed along major faults such as Zhangdong, Hai4, Yang’erzhuang, which have undergone long-term development. This results in the formation of oilfields or hydrocarbon-bearing structures, including Qidong, Zhangdong, Zhangdongdong, Zhaodong, and Guanjiapu, arranged from north to south. The hydrocarbons are mainly distributed within traps on both sides of the major northeast-trending controlling faults, while faults trending more northeast to east-northeast have a relatively weaker control over hydrocarbon distribution.

The geometric configuration of cross sections influences the structural positioning and vertical distribution of oil and gas. Vertical migration along ramp-flat faults encounters greater resistance, whereas migration along shovel-shaped faults faces relatively lower resistance. In the north–northeast direction, a seat-shaped main controlling fault, along with its oppositely trending secondary faults, constitutes a vertical migration pathway for oil and gas. The hydrocarbons accumulate in the hanging wall of the main fault, as exemplified by the Zhaobei Oilfield (Figure 14a). In the east–west direction, oil and gas predominantly accumulate in the middle to deep layers along ramp-flat faults, with a single prominent accumulation layer primarily distributed in the Paleogene Shahejie Formation, as observed in the Zhangdong Oilfield (Figure 14b). Shovel-shaped main controlling faults, along with their oppositely trending secondary faults, form vertical migration pathways. In the Neogene, these derived faults together create an effective supply channel. Under similar displacement pressures, the overcoming of lower resistance facilitates large-scale migration of oil and gas from source rock layers through faults to reservoir layers, resulting in the accumulation of hydrocarbons in multiple stratigraphic levels, such as in the Yang’erzhuang Oilfield (Figure 14c). Building upon the previous analysis of the main controlling fault growth index and structural development history (Figures 5 and 13), the activity characteristics of faults determine the distribution of oil and gas reservoirs. For instance, the main fault in the Yang’erzhuang Oilfield and Zhaodong Oilfield exhibited strong activity during the late Miocene, resulting in the formation of secondary oil and gas reservoirs in the Miocene and Guantao formations. In contrast, the northern segment of the Zhaobei Fault experienced weaker activity during the late Miocene, leading to the absence of Neogene secondary oil and gas reservoirs in the Youyi Oilfield; oil and gas accumulation occurred primarily in the Paleogene (Figure 14d). On a planar scale, the activity characteristics of the main fault also impact the degree of oil and gas enrichment on either side of the fault. For example, the Zhaobei Fault exhibits a characteristic of decreasing horizontal extension from west to east. In the western segment, the horizontal extension is significant in various periods, approximately 2 km in the Upper Shahe Formation, 1.5 km in the Lower Shahe Formation, and 0.5 km in the Neogene. Consequently, the Yang’erzhuang Oilfield formed in the western segment. In contrast, the Yang’erzhuang Fault exhibits the opposite trend, with an increasing horizontal extension from west to east. In the eastern segment, the horizontal extension is significant in various periods, leading to the formation of the Zhaodong Oilfield. This is attributed to intense deformation in the fault zone, characterized by severe section fragmentation, bending, and fissure development, resulting in increased permeability. This dynamic helps overcome the poor lateral connectivity of sand bodies, thus facilitating the migration and accumulation of oil and gas.

Additionally, the sealing characteristics of both the main controlling faults and secondary faults play a crucial role in controlling the accumulation of oil and gas in this region. The periods of intense fault activity, such as the Es period, the late Ed period, and the Nm period, serve as key phases when faults act as lateral and vertical channels for oil and gas migration. Coupled with sand bodies and unconformity conduits, these faults facilitate the transport of hydrocarbons generated in the hydrocarbon generation sag into the study area, with the Nm period contributing the most. During other geological periods, especially in the oil and gas accumulation period, whether faults act as conduits or barriers is constrained by various factors, thereby controlling the pattern of oil and gas enrichment in this region. For example, the Zhangdong Fault, during tectonic activity periods, functioned as an open conduit, enabling the migration of oil and gas into the fault uplift, where hydrocarbons generated in the Qikou Sag were transported and accumulated in suitable traps. Currently, this fault exhibits good sealing properties, acting as a barrier for oil and gas. Consequently, hydrocarbons are sealed in the hanging wall due to the barrier effect of the fault, as illustrated in Figure 14b.

Weakening of Fault Activity during the Accumulation Period

Hydrocarbon expulsion history simulations confirm that the peak hydrocarbon expulsion periods for the Es3 and Es1 source rocks in the study area occurred relatively late. The peak expulsion period for the Es3 source rock occurred in the late Nm deposition period, continuing into the Nm-Q deposition period. The Es1 source rock exhibited a delayed onset of hydrocarbon expulsion, commencing in the Ng period and reaching its peak expulsion period at the end of the Nm deposition, with significant hydrocarbon expulsion continuing into the Nm-Q deposition period.2 Inclusion experiments have revealed that the charging period for oil and gas in this area is during the Nm-Q period.18

As indicated by the previous study on the structural evolution history (Figure 13), the Chenghai area experienced uplift during the late Yanshanian Movement, inheriting characteristics from the ancient uplift during the sedimentation processes of the Paleogene. Controlled by the main faults in the region, various types of structures and traps were formed, with the structural traps mainly taking shape during the Es period. In the Neogene, influenced by the new tectonic movement in the Bohai Sea, a series of midshallow traps were also formed. The timing of trap formation predates the large-scale hydrocarbon accumulation period. The continuous migration of hydrocarbons expelled from Paleogene source rocks into these traps facilitates the enrichment of oil and gas. Additionally, the relatively weak tectonic activity after the Nm period, coupled with favorable preservation conditions, contributed to the significant accumulation of oil and gas in the study area without causing extensive destruction of hydrocarbon reservoirs.

Conclusions

To investigate the structural evolution characteristics of fault zones in the Chenghai area and their role in hydrocarbon accumulation, this study employed various methods including rose diagrams, buried depth–throw curves, activity rate analysis, balanced cross sections, and dissecting typical oil and gas reservoirs. These approaches facilitated an in-depth analysis of the structural features and evolution processes of fault zones in the Chenghai area, along with exploring their significance in hydrocarbon accumulation. Subsequently, a fault-controlled hydrocarbon accumulation model specific to the region was constructed. The main findings of this study are summarized below:

1. In the Chenghai area, the main faults are classified into three types: continuous active faults controlled by the basement (e.g., Zhangbei Fault), which formed before the Cenozoic and have been continuously active without intermittent periods; buried reactivated segmented growth faults (e.g., Qidong Fault), which experienced at least one period of fault activity cessation, displaying a pattern of intense activity–cessation–reactivation–steady subsidence; and late-stage continuously active isolated growth faults (e.g., f11 Fault), characterized by relatively smaller overall sizes, mostly formed during the rift II stage, and subjected to extensional stress in the near SN direction without evident right-lateral strike-slip.

2. The primary deep structural dynamics in the Chenghai area during the Cenozoic originated from mantle upwelling, with the asthenosphere upwelling caused by the subduction of the Pacific Plate beneath the Eurasian Plate. Regional rifting induced by asthenospheric upwelling led to NW-SE extensional deformation in the crust. During the rift II stage, the expansion of the Japan Sea induced a right-lateral strike-slip component in the Chenghai area, resulting in the present extensional-strike-slip structural pattern.

3. Based on the role of faults in hydrocarbon accumulation, the hydrocarbon accumulation models in the study area are categorized into fault-controlled vertical migration models, lateral sealing and blocking fault-controlled models, and far-source stepped fault-controlled migration models.

4. The preexisting main controlling faults were formed against the backdrop of basement uplift. The long-term inherited development of main controlling faults during the Neogene controlled the spatial configuration of suitable fault-type traps. The peak period of hydrocarbon expulsion in the Chenghai area occurred relatively late, and during the hydrocarbon accumulation period, fault activity diminished, ensuring the degree of hydrocarbon enrichment.
