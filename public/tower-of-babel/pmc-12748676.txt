Mixed-mode control of multiphase interleaved parallel circuit for pulsed laser diode driving
Authors: Bin Wu, Junliang Liu, Haoyu Zhao, Jindong Guo, Zhaojun Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWith advancements in industrial technology, the switching power supply has gradually emerged as a preferred choice for laser power supplies due to its advantages of lower power consumption and compact size. However, the simultaneous achievement of short rise time, current flatness, and high accuracy pose significant difficulties for the switching power supply. In this paper, a mixed-mode control of multiple interleaving parallel circuits is proposed. The proposed mixed-mode control incorporates burst-mode voltage control for the light-load state of the laser diode to both fasten the response and eliminate extra current. And a dual-loop current control is employed, where the inner controller averages the branch current and the outer controller regulates the total output current, and hence a balanced current distribution in each branch is achieved to increase the stability of the system. The control mode changing depends on whether the pulse is required to be output in correspondence to the control signal. A simulation model and a multiphase interleaved parallel circuit were constructed to validate the control scheme. Experimental results demonstrate that this algorithm effectively reduces rise time and ensures a stable output current.Subject terms: Electrical and electronic engineering; Lasers, LEDs and light sources

Introduction

Semiconductor lasers play an important role in industry, medicine, aviation technology1–3. The laser power supply has a significant influence on the output performance of the laser4–7. Semiconductor laser requires the drive power supply in the form of a current source with a rectangular pulse waveform8–10, of which the rising edge and falling edge should be as steep as possible. The output current of laser pulse power supply (PPS) should be as flat-top as possible without overshooting. Therefore, good current stability and fast rise time are the basic requirement of a PPS11–13.

At present, most pulse drive power supplies adopt linear drive topology11,14–16, of which the power transistor works in the linear region. The driver controls the output current by adjusting the transistor voltage. However, transistors operating in the linear region results in a high on-resistance and hence an increased system loss, of which the most is converted into heat. This leads to not only the reduction of the drive efficiency of the power supply, but also a possible damage of the power transistor.

With the development of the power electronics industry, switching power supplies are also used as the driving power of lasers17,18. But it is difficult for the traditional switching power supply to achieve fast rise time and low current ripple. Many researchers have made efforts to reach the goals above. In 2015, Penovi et al. proposed a novel multi-structure power converter capable of generation high current pulses with short rise and fall time. But this structure can only be used for high-power currents17. In 2016, Zhu et al. proposed a bipolar pulsed current source used for a highly inductive load. The current source exploits a noninductive capacitor to store or release the energy back to and from inductive load during switching on or off. However, it can drive inductive load, and its rise and fall time is down to 200 μs6. In 2021, Miao et al. proposed a combination of a buck converter and a linear regulation module to realize constant pulse current output to the load by adjusting metal-oxide-silicon field-effect transistors (MOSFETs) operating in the linear region5,19. But its essential idea is to regulate the current within the linear region of the MOSFETs, which leads to a large power loss. It is difficult for these switching-based pulsed current sources to simultaneously achieve low ripple, fast rise time, and high current.

In recent years, multiphase buck circuits have been widely used because of their advantages of high current and low ripple20–23. However, most of the researches used multiphase buck circuits to construct voltage sources, and yet few multiphase buck circuits have been used as pulsed current sources. In 2021, a digital control strategy based on predictive current control under a multiphase PPS converter circuit is proposed with a faster dynamic response and a higher accuracy24. A 360-W dual-interleaved PPS prototype is built for the verification of effectiveness of the proposed control strategy. In all, there is a great need for the design of pulsed current source circuits with short rise time, low ripple, and high current.

This design combines multiple interleaved pulsed current sources based on mixed-mode control of voltage and current for idle and pulse output state, respectively. Mixed-mode control was proposed to reduce the rise time of the pulse current while achieving high power efficiency. The proposed mixed-mode control incorporates voltage control for the light-load state of the laser diode to both fasten the response and eliminate extra current. Additionally, dual-loop control and high-resolution PWM was adopted in current-mode control, where the output pulse current is regulated more precisely with the multiphase interleaved scheme, and hence a balanced current distribution in each branch is achieved to increase the stability of the system. This strategy of digital control makes the circuit highly flexible and effectively reduces the rise time of the pulsed current source while ensuring high efficiency. A simulation model is built to verify the system, and a multiple interleaved circuit is designed for the implementation of pulse source topology to lower the ripple during the pulse current output. Finally, a prototype is built to verify the effectiveness of the system.

Control strategy

The system controller consists of a voltage controller and a pulse current controller. The laser diode is a current device with a threshold voltage for laser output. The driving current of the laser is proportional to the driving voltage after reaching the threshold voltage. When the driving voltage is below the threshold voltage, the current of laser diode is almost zero. Therefore, if the output current is used as a feedback control parameter, the rise time of the pulse current is excessively long. After the end of the pulse current output, the output voltage drops below the threshold voltage Vth, and the laser diode becomes a light load, causing a prolonged tail. The output waveform of the entire PPS is shown in the Fig. 1.

Pulsed current source waveform diagram.

Two different control modes are designed for pulsed operation, where voltage-mode control is for idle state and current-mode control is used during pulse signal generation. When the pulse start signal is not received, the system controls the output voltage to be lower than the threshold voltage. When the pulse start signal is received, the system adopts current control mode. The current control strategy employs output current and inductor currents in a double-loop control scheme to achieve current sharing in a multiphase buck converter.

Light load volatge control

When the output voltage does not reach the threshold voltage of laser, the switching power supply operates in a light-load state, with its output load consisting of the output capacitor and its equivalent series resistance (ESR). The control principal diagram for voltage control is shown in the Fig. 2. Burst-mode control is adopted for voltage control. The top transistor is activated when the output voltage falls below the set minimum voltage Vl. Its on-time is a constant value of Ton. After one switching cycle, the output voltage is measured. If the voltage does not reach the desired value Vh, the power transistor turns on for one cycle, and repeat it until the output voltage reaches the desired value. Then, the output voltage is maintained with both top and bottom transistors off, and the inductive current flows through the body diode of the bottom transistor.

Voltage control strategy diagram.

Current control

Upon receiving the pulse start signal, the system enters current control mode, where a double-loop control strategy is proposed, as shown in Fig. 3. Due to the different parasitic parameters of each inductor circuits, the inductor currents may be different. In order to realize the current sharing, a double-loop control mode is adopted. The outer-loop controller is responsible for controlling the total output current Iout, while the inner-loop controller controls the average inductance current of each branch. The input to the inner loop is the current of each inductor InL. In order to maintain the independence of the inner and outer loops, the inner loop controls current sharing between the phases of the inductor currents, while the outer loop regulates the total output current.

Current control strategy diagram.

The outputs of controller are the duty cycles to control the switching transistors in each phase branch. In order to effectively reduce the output ripple of the system, the switching transistor delay between each phase is set to 360° ÷ N, where N is the number of circuit phases.

The inner-loop controller is shown in Fig. 3. The input of inner-loop controller is each phase inductance InL and duty. The current value of each phase inductance is input into the current sharing module. The average value of inductance current collected for a period is compared with the reference value of inductance current of each phase, and the current difference is converted to the duty cycle compensation value un. Then the duty cycle signal of each phase can be obtained by making the difference between the phase compensation value and the duty cycle.

The diagram of the outer-loop controller is shown in Fig. 3. The total output current Iout serves as the input to the outer-loop controller. The output current is compared with the reference current value Iref and fed into the PID controller. The result of PID controller is processed through the inner-loop controller, which the output of inner-loop controller is the duty cycle of the power transistor. The obtained duty cycle is converted and fed into output serialization/deserialization (OSERDES) module, which generates driver signal with different delays to drive the power transistors of different branches.

The parasitic parameter of the circuit causes the unbalance of the inductance current of each phase. If the resolution of PWM generator is coarse, even a change of one least-significant bit in duty cycle leads to a large variation in the inductance current, which is not conducive to current sharing. Therefore, a high resolution PWM generator is needed to realize the inductance current sharing of each branch.

In order to have a higher resolution of PWM, the OSERDES function of the field programmable gate array (FPGA) is used to generate the duty signal. The signal input to the OSERDES module is generated by a digital PID controller. The FPGA clock frequency is set to 125 MHz, with a 500 MHz clock used for the I/O clock. Eight bits of data which are grouped together, input in parallel into the OSERDES module. Each bit of data represents the output pulse signal with a 1 ns time width; when the data is 1, the output is high. If the data is 0, the output is low. Using the OSERDES module as the PWM generator, the pulse width resolution is down to 1 ns, which enables faster output adjustments.

System circuit design

This design is implemented for laser diode (LD) driving. The pulse driver of LD is required to have fast rise time, flat-top pulse current, and low ripple. Additionally, the output pulse current of the high-power pulse drive supply is large, making it difficult to balance high current with low ripple using a single buck converter. Therefore, multiphase interleaved parallel buck converters are adopted as the circuit topology.

The system utilizes an FPGA as the main digital control device. The FPGA operates at high speed, enabling multi-channel synchronous operation and the generation of PWM waveforms with a resolution of 1 ns. Therefore, it is well-suited to serve as the control device for the system. Additionally, GaN transistors are selected as the power transistors.

Results and discussion

In order to verify the performance of the system, an experimental prototype was built for verification. The multiphase interleaved parallel circuit of the system is shown in Fig. 8. The circuit consists of control circuit and power circuit. The control circuit adopts Xilinx’s FPGA(XC7A35T-2CSG325I) as the main control device. The LTC2325 was selected as an inductance current sampling analog-to-digital converter (ADC), which enables quad-channel synchronous sampling up to 5 MHz. Two ADCs (ADS8681) are used for output voltage and current sampling, respectively. The GaN transistors (EPC2034) are selected as the power transistor. High switching frequency reduces the size of inductors in the circuit, but excessively high frequency pose challenges to both the circuit’s sampling accuracy and switching losses. Therefore, a 1 MHz switching frequency was chosen.

Photograph of multiphase interleaved parallel circuit.

In the test, a 1 Ω resistor was used for functional verification of the circuit. Figure 9a shows the voltage signal across the resistor. It can be observed from this signal that the rise time is 50 µs at an output current of 20 A. The output current ripple of the system is 0.15 A, which is only 0.75% of the output. There is a damped oscillation at the falling edge, which was caused by fast-dropping current and stray inductance and capacitance. We then tested the resistive load waveform with only 2 channels in function, using the same circuit. Under the same testing conditions, the rise time was 73 μs, which was longer than that of 4-channels. And its current ripple is around 2%, which is much higher than the current ripple of a 4-channel system of 0.75%.

Subsequently, the current sharing module of each phase inductors are tested. Figure 10a shows the inductance current with an inner loop controller, and Fig. 10b shows the inductance current without an inner controller. It can be seen from the results that the inner loop controller can effectively reduce the current imbalance caused by various phase parasitic parameters.

Finally, a laser diode (HAD-N107-3-D12-0003, HUAGUANG Optoelectronics) was used as the load to test the system. To measure the output current of the laser, a 0.1 Ω resistor was connected in series with the negative terminal of the laser.

In this test, both current control and mixed-mode control algorithms were used to evaluate the electrical performance of the power supply.

The test results are shown in Fig. 11, the red line represents the voltage of the laser, and the black line represents the current signal. When the voltage rises rapidly to the threshold voltage, the voltage signal and current signal oscillates due to sudden load change, and then the voltage and current signal rise slowly with a total rise time of 102 μs. When the pulse signal is turned off, the voltage and current begin to decrease. Once the voltage drops to the threshold value, the current becomes zero, and the rate of voltage decrease slows down.

Voltage and current waveform of laser without voltage control.

Finally, the mixed-mode control algorithm was employed to evaluate the performance of the power supply, as shown in Fig. 12. The voltage reference value was set to 10 V. When no pulse current is generated, the voltage across the laser fluctuates around 10 V. Since the voltage does not reach the laser’s threshold at this stage, the output current remains zero. When the pulse signal is generated, the voltage across the laser begins to increase. The current rise time is 85 μs.

Voltage and current waveform of laser with mixed-mode control.

Comparing the results of the two algorithms reveals that the mixed-mode control algorithm reduces the rise time of the system. Furthermore, due to the shorter rise time, the mixed-mode control algorithm mitigates oscillations caused by sudden load changes.

Conclusion

This paper proposes a mixed-mode control of multiple interleaved parallel circuits for use as a laser driver. This control exploits the electrical properties of the laser. When no pulse current is generated, the output voltage is set near the reference voltage using voltage control. Once the pulse current is generated, the laser output current is controlled by current control. Additionally, the power load variations in both control modes are analyzed. The design adopts a digital power control mode, enhancing the power supply’s flexibility.

Finally, the power supply was validated through simulation and prototype testing. The current ripple was only 0.75% of the output, and the rise time can be reduced from 102 to 85 μs by using mixed mode control instead of current control. The experimental results can show that the PPS not only effectively reduces the rise time but also exhibits excellent current flatness, making it suitable for use as a laser power supply.
