Exact dynamics and qubit inversion of non-Hermitian driven two-level systems
Ivan A. Bocanegra-Garay, Luis M. Nieto

Abstract
The supersymmetric structure of a generalized non-Hermitian driven two-level system is demonstrated. A unitary rotation turns the Hamiltonian into a more convenient form. After decoupling a set of differential equations, the supersymmetric structure of the problem can be unequivocally appreciated. Performing a spectral analysis of an auxiliary stationary Schrödinger-like equation, complex time-dependent driving functions are obtained for which the corresponding (time-dependent) Schrödinger equation can be straightforwardly solved. Such complex drivings are seen to produce transitions in the qubit state in different, however interesting, manners. We believe that the results reported here will be of interest for designing and carrying out various experiments in laboratories specializing in nuclear magnetic resonance or in optics with gain and loss materials.



I
Introduction

The semi-classical Rabi model for a qubit
Rabi 1936
;
Rabi 1937
, i.e. the Schrödinger equation for the Hamiltonian

\mathcal{\hat{H}}=\Delta\hat{\sigma}_{z}+w(t)\hat{\sigma}_{x},

(1)

has been the subject of active interest in research in the last decades
Braak et al. 2016
. The real constant
\Delta
in (
1

w(t)
represents a time-dependent driving function stimulating transitions between the two qubit energy levels. The case of a classical harmonic electric field
w(t)=\cos(\omega t)
driving the two-level system was analyzed
Lefebvre et al. 2009
;
Moiseyev 2011
;
Lee and Joglekar 2015
;
Castaños 2019
. However, exact solutions associated with time-dependent Hamiltonians of the type (
1
) remain rare (for some exceptions, see
Gangopadhyay et al. 2010
;
de Clercq et al. 2016
;
Giscard and Bonhomme 2020
).

Non-Hermitian versions of the system (
1
) have also been considered in the literature
Bender et al. 2007
;
Moiseyev 2011
;
Ibáñez et al. 2011
;
Torosov et al. 2013
;
Zheng et al. 2013
;
Lian et al. 2014
;
D’Ambroise et al. 2014
;
Gong and Wang 2015
;
Lee and Joglekar 2015
;
Wu et al. 2017
;
Hassan et al. 2017
;
Xie et al. 2018
(see also
Pires and Macrì 2021
). Non-Hermitian (driven) qubits have attracted attention in recent years
Dogra et al. 2021
;
Xu and Guo 2022
;
Leng and Chen 2023
;
Kivelä et al. 2024
;
Pan and Wu 2024
;
Jebraeilli and Geller 2025
;
Liu et al. 2025
;
Fan et al. 2025
. The non-Hermitian
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Rabi model obtained if

in (
1
) has been studied in detail
Moiseyev 2011
;
Liu et al. 2025
, and is said to be
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric because this Hamiltonian
commutes with the product of the parity operator
\mathcal{P}=\hat{\sigma}_{z}
and the time-reversal operation
\mathcal{T}
(complex conjugation):
[\mathcal{PT},\mathcal{\hat{H}}]=0

(see also
Rüter et al. 2010
;

\mathcal{PT}

Supersymmetric quantum mechanics (SUSY QM) is a valuable technique for finding new exactly solvable quantum potentials, for instance, in the context of one-dimensional stationary Schrödinger equations, starting from given exactly solvable quantum potentials
Cooper et al. 1995
;
Fernández and Hussin 1999
;
Mielnik et al. 2000
;
Samsonov and Negro 2004
;
Correa and Plyushchay 2007
;
Miri et al. 2013
;
Correa et al. 2015
;
Cruz y Cruz et al. 2020
;
García-Muñoz et al. 2023
(free-particle, harmonic oscillator or Lamé). See also
Zuñiga-Segundo et al. 2014
;
Schulze-Halberg and Roy 2017
;
Contreras-Astorga and Jakubskỳ 2019
;
Maldonado-Villamizar et al. 2021
;
Kafuri et al. 2024
;
Bocanegra-Garay et al. 2024a
;
Bocanegra-Garay et al. 2024b
for SUSY transformations in miscellaneous contexts. In particular, exactly solvable one-dimensional complex potentials can be constructed from exactly solvable real ones
Rosas-Ortiz et al. 2015
;
Rosas-Ortiz and Zelaya 2018
;
Zelaya et al. 2020
;
Bocanegra and Cruz y
Cruz 2022
.

In this work, we use a SUSY QM approach
Bender and Milton 1998
to construct complex time-dependent coupling functions
w(t)
for a non-Hermitian time-dependent semi-classical Rabi model of the type (
1
). We do this by bringing the Hamiltonian into a suitable form via a unitary transformation, and identify the resulting system of two differential equations (after decoupling) as a SUSY pair of stationary Schrödinger-like equations
Cooper et al. 1995
;
Fernández and Hussin 1999
;
Mielnik et al. 2000
;
Samsonov and Negro 2004
;
Correa and Plyushchay 2007
;
Correa et al. 2015
;
Cruz y Cruz et al. 2020
. By fixing the potential of one of the stationary Schrödinger equations, the coupling function
w(t)
is fixed as well, and the solutions of the time-dependent Schrödinger equation for the Hamiltonian (
1
) are straightforwardly obtained.
In this paper we elucidate exciting features of the system under consideration. For instance, we show that when the coupling function
w(t)
is purely imaginary, the transitions between the two energy levels are suppressed. In contrast, when
w(t)=w_{R}(t)+iw_{I}(t)
, with
w_{R}\neq 0
and
w_{I}
real functions of time, the qubit might perform transitions. Both periodic and nonperiodic transitions appear when
w_{R,I}(t)\neq 0
, for some interval of
t
. Thus, transitions are possible even in the presence of a nontrivial imaginary part of the coupling function, i.e.
w_{I}(t)\neq 0
.

Non-Hermitian physics has gained exceptional importance, since real physical processes are, in fact, nonconservative. Nevertheless, as mentioned, there are only a few time-dependent non-Hermitian exactly-solvable systems in the literature. In this framework, the present paper aims to fill that gap in an elegant way by using the simplest approach: we take advantage of the form of the differential equations that govern the evolution of the non-Hermitian time-dependent system and the available knowledge on one-dimensional SUSY QM in stationary systems.

This work is organized as follows.
In Section
II
, the model under examination is presented: the SUSY structure of the decoupled system of differential equations defining a unitary rotation of the model under investigation is reviewed; in particular, the construction of complex
superpotentials
(coupling functions) is considered.
In Section
III
, the simplest example of the general SUSY approach is examined.
In Section
IV
, some numerical experiments are presented to show the applicability of the results obtained in the previous sections.
In Section
V
, the experimental feasibility of the proposed theoretical model is discussed.
Finally, in Section
VI
, the main conclusions of the manuscript are presented, and some future work is proposed.

II
Exactly-solvable model

Let us consider a two-level system (Figure
1
A) with energy split
\Delta=\omega_{e}-\omega_{g}
, where
\omega_{e,g}
are the energies of the excited
|e\rangle
and ground
|g\rangle
states, respectively, subjected to a mechanism that stimulates transitions in the qubit, and to another one that acts as an external gain/loss (Figure
1
B). The Schrödinger equation (
\hbar=1
) reads

\mathcal{\hat{H}}|\phi\rangle=i\partial_{\tau}|\phi\rangle,\qquad\mathcal{\hat{H}}=\Delta\hat{\sigma}_{z}+ig(\tau)\hat{\sigma}_{x},

(2)

where
\hat{\sigma}_{z},\hat{\sigma}_{x}
are Pauli matrices. Note that
\mathcal{\hat{H}}
is a time-dependent (
\tau
) non-Hermitian Hamiltonian. The complex term
ig(\tau)
represents a time-dependent coupling between the qubit levels and also accounts for the gain/loss of the system. When
g(\tau)
is restricted to be a real even function of time, the Hamiltonian (
2
) belongs to the family of
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonians
Bender and Boettcher 1998
;
Rüter et al. 2010
, with
\mathcal{P}=\hat{\sigma}_{z}
and
\mathcal{T}
the complex-conjugation. Up to now,
g(\tau)
is a complex function to be determined, and no further assumptions on it have been made. By defining a dimensionless time
t=\Delta\tau
, the following Schrödinger equation is obtained

\hat{H}_{0}|\phi\rangle=i\partial_{t}|\phi\rangle,\ \ \hat{H}_{0}=\hat{\sigma}_{z}+if(t)\hat{\sigma}_{x},\ \ f(t)={g(\tau)}/{\Delta}.

(3)

Letting
|\phi\rangle=\exp(-i\frac{\pi}{4}\hat{\sigma}_{y})|\psi\rangle
, (
3
) transforms into

\hat{H}|\psi\rangle=i\partial_{t}|\psi\rangle,\qquad\hat{H}=if(t)\hat{\sigma}_{z}-\hat{\sigma}_{x}.

(4)

In the following, a simple method for solving the Schrödinger equation (
4
), developed on SUSY foundations, will be presented.

II.1
SUSY structure of the problem

By writting
|\psi\rangle=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}a_{1}(t)\\
a_{2}(t)\end{array}\right)
, with
a_{1,2}(t)
complex functions of
t
, (
4
) becomes the set of coupled differential equations

\displaystyle i\dot{a}_{1}(t)=if(t)a_{1}(t)-a_{2}(t),

(5)

\displaystyle i\dot{a}_{2}(t)=-if(t)a_{2}(t)-a_{1}(t),

(6)

where the dots represent derivation with respect to
t
. By differentiating, the following pair of uncoupled second-order stationary Schrödinger-like (in the variable
t
, not
x
) differential equations is obtained

\hat{H}_{1}a_{1}(t)=Ea_{1}(t),\quad\hat{H}_{2}a_{2}(t)=Ea_{2}(t),

(7)

with
\hat{H}_{1,2}=-\partial_{tt}+V_{1,2}(t)
,
E=0
and where we have defined
Rosas-Ortiz et al. 2015
;
Rosas-Ortiz and Zelaya 2018

V_{1}(t)=f^{2}(t)+\dot{f}(t)-1,\quad V_{2}(t)=f^{2}(t)-\dot{f}(t)-1.

(8)

The SUSY structure of (
7
) can be seen by identifying (
8
) as two SUSY quantum potentials
V_{1}(t)
and
V_{2}(t)
(see
Rosas-Ortiz et al. 2015
;
Rosas-Ortiz and Zelaya 2018
and references therein), where

V_{2}(t)=V_{1}(t)-2\dot{f}(t),

(9)

connected by the coupling (called
superpotential
in the SUSY QM jargon) or driving function defined by
f(t)
.
When defining the operators

\hat{A}=-\partial_{t}-f(t),\qquad\hat{B}=\partial_{t}-f(t),

(10)

equations (
7
) can be cast, respectively, in the form

(\hat{A}\hat{B}+\epsilon)a_{1}(t)=0,\qquad(\hat{B}\hat{A}+\epsilon)a_{2}(t)=0,

(11)

where
\epsilon=-1
. Thus,
a_{2}(t)\propto\hat{B}a_{1}(t)
and
a_{1}(t)\propto\hat{A}a_{2}(t)
. Indeed, it is found that

a_{2}(t)=-i\hat{B}a_{1}(t),\qquad a_{1}(t)=i\hat{A}a_{2}(t),

(12)

which is consistent with (
5
) and (
6
).

It is possible to take advantage of the SUSY pair in (
7
) by choosing
V_{1}(t)
and
V_{2}(t)
as any known pair of SUSY partner potentials, for which the solutions
a_{1}(t)
and
a_{2}(t)
are known
Cooper et al. 1995
;
Fernández and Hussin 1999
;
Mielnik et al. 2000
;
Samsonov and Negro 2004
;
Correa and Plyushchay 2007
;
Miri et al. 2013
;
Correa et al. 2015
;
Cruz y Cruz et al. 2020
;
García-Muñoz et al. 2023
. This fixes the superpotential (coupling or driving function)
f(t)
that connects the partner Hamiltonians via (
9
).

The constant
\epsilon
in both expressions of (
11
) is known as the
factorization energy
. In usual SUSY approaches, the factorization energy
\epsilon
is a free parameter
Mielnik et al. 2000
;
Rosas-Ortiz et al. 2015
;
Rosas-Ortiz and Zelaya 2018
. However, in this case, it is already fixed, by the form of the coupled equations (
5
) and (
6
), as
\epsilon=-1
.

It is also interesting that a SUSY pair of equations similar to (
7
) is given in equations (16) and (17) of Reference
Gong and Wang 2015
. However, there are some notable differences worth mentioning.
First, the method used in Ref.
Gong and Wang 2015
to obtain such a pair of SUSY equations is somewhat cumbersome; in our approach it is a natural consequence of the change of variable indicated above (
4
).
Second, in
Gong and Wang 2015
the authors specifically consider a periodic modulation; in our approach an explicit form of
f(t)
has not been assumed. In fact, we will consider both periodic and nonperiodic modulation functions
f(t)
.

II.2
Complex driving functions (superpotentials)

The superpotential
f(t)
defining the coupling between the two qubit levels can be taken as a real function. If such a function is even, the Hamiltonian (
2
) is
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric, with properly defined
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{T}
operators, as mentioned earlier. However,
f(t)
can be taken as a complex function satisfying (
7
) and (
8
).
Taking
f(t)=f_{R}(t)+if_{I}(t)
we arrive to the form of
f(t)
in terms of a solution to an Ermakov equation (see References
Rosas-Ortiz et al. 2015
;
Rosas-Ortiz and Zelaya 2018
for details). However, it can be shown that such an approach is equivalent to choosing

f(t)={\dot{u}(t)}/{u(t)},

(13)

with
u(t)
having the form
Zelaya et al. 2020

u(t)=c_{1}u_{1}(t)+\left(\frac{c_{2}}{2}-i\frac{\lambda}{w_{0}}\right)u_{2}(t).

(14)

Here,
u_{j}(t)
are lineraly independent solutions of

\hat{H}_{1}u_{j}(t)=\epsilon u_{j}(t),\quad j=1,2,

(15)

with
\hat{H}_{1}
given in (
7
) and (
8
), where
\epsilon=-1
.
Besides,
w_{0}
is the nonzero Wronskian of
u_{1}(t)
and
u_{2}(t)
,
\lambda
is an arbitrary real constant and
c_{\ell}
,
\ell=1,2,3
are positive numbers that must satisfy
Zelaya et al. 2020

c_{2}^{2}-4c_{1}c_{3}={-4\lambda^{2}}/{w_{0}^{2}}.

(16)

If
V_{1}(t)
is a real function of
t
, the functions
u_{1,2}(t)
can also be chosen with real values. Then
\lambda

regulates
the imaginary part of the function
f(t)
, and the real part of the driving or coupling function in Hamiltonian (
2
). If
\lambda=0
, the function
f(t)
is real, and if
f(t)
is an even function of time, the
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetry
of the Hamiltonian (
2
) is preserved.

In the following, a particular example of a quantum potential
V_{1}(t)
satisfying (
8
) is considered. Complex, periodic and nonperiodic, coupling functions of the form in (
13
) are obtained, for which the functions
a_{1,2}(t)
that solve (
7
) will be also evaluated.

III.2
Population inversion and purely imaginary drivings (real superpotentials)

A dynamical variable of interest in a two-level system is the population inversion

W(t)=\langle\phi|\hat{\sigma}_{z}|\phi\rangle=|c_{1}(t)|^{2}-|c_{2}(t)|^{2},

(23)

which gives the difference between the probabilities of finding the qubit in the excited and the ground states, i.e.
W(t)=p_{ee}(t)-p_{gg}(t)
, where

|\phi\rangle=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}c_{1}(t)\\
c_{2}(t)\end{array}\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&-1\\
1&1\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}a_{1}(t)\\
a_{2}(t)\end{array}\right).

(24)

It can be shown that

W(t)=-2[a_{1}^{R}(t)a_{2}^{R}(t)+a_{1}^{I}(t)a_{2}^{I}(t)],

(25)

where the superscripts denote the real (
R
) or imaginary (
I
) parts of the functions
a_{1,2}(t)
.
Taking into account the first expression in (
7
) and (
12
), it can be proved that

\partial_{t}W(t)\propto f_{I}(t),\quad\textrm{for all $t$}.

(26)

This means that a purely imaginary coupling in the Hamiltonian in (
2
) does not produce population transitions, in accordance with what is stated at the end of Section II in
Gong and Wang 2015
. However, this does not occur when
f(t)
has a nontrivial imaginary part, giving rise to a real part of the coupling function in the Hamiltonian (
2
).

In the following, we present numerical examples of complex time-dependent coupling functions, for which solutions of (
2
) are known, and which give rise to controlled transitions in the qubit state.

V
Proposed experimental observations

There are several experimental platforms on which the non-Hermitian Hamiltonian (
2
) can be easily implemented. Here, we briefly review the actual experimental possibilities.
First, recall that driven two-level systems usually appear in the context of nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR)
Bloch 1946
;
Silver et al. 1984a
;
Silver et al. 1984b
;
Silver et al. 1985
;
Giscard and Bonhomme 2020
). Numerous driven Hermitian systems have been considered in the existing literature (see, e.g.,
Silver et al. 1985
;
Martínez-Tibaduiza et al. 2023
;
Enríquez and Cruz y
Cruz 2018
).
In the non-Hermitian regime, several
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric systems have been studied
Bender et al. 2007
;
Moiseyev 2011
, and in particular the non-Hermitian system defined by (
2
) can also be interpreted in the context of NMR. To see this, compare the Hamiltonian (
2
) with the one given in equation (27) of Ref.
Martínez-Tibaduiza et al. 2023

\hat{H}_{\rm{Ref}}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\Omega(t)\hat{T}_{+}+\delta\omega\hat{T}_{c}+\frac{1}{2}\Omega^{*}(t)\hat{T}_{-},

(37)

where the operators
\hat{T}_{\pm}
,
\hat{T}_{c}
are
\hat{\sigma}_{\pm}
and
\hat{\sigma}_{z}/2
, and the star denotes complex conjugation. In our case, (
2
) has the form

\hat{H}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{O}(t)\hat{T}_{+}+\delta\omega\hat{T}_{c}+\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{O}(t)\hat{T}_{-},

(38)

with
\mathcal{O}(t)=-2ig(t)
and
\delta\omega=\Delta
. This leads to a slight modification of the Ricatti equation (26) in
Martínez-Tibaduiza et al. 2023
:

\dot{f}-\frac{i}{2}\Omega^{*}(t)f^{2}+i\Delta\omega f+\frac{i}{2}\Omega(t)=0.

(39)

Instead, we get

\dot{F}-\frac{i}{2}\mathcal{O}(t)F^{2}+i\Delta\omega F+\frac{i}{2}\mathcal{O}(t)=0.

(40)

This modifies the structure of the Bloch equations (24) in
Martínez-Tibaduiza et al. 2023
, leading to a non-Hermitian version of the usual NMR scheme. Indeed, it is this slight modification that is responsible for the non-Hermitian nature of (
2
) and hence the gain/loss of the current system. Moreover, according to
Moiseyev 2011
, non-Hermitian Hamiltonians like (
2
) model the interactions between metastable (resonant) states of the two-level system.

On the other hand, the non-Hermitian Hamiltonian (
2
) can be directly implemented in the context of coupled waveguides, i.e., as non-Hermitian (integrated gain/loss
Rüter et al. 2010
) directional couplers (i.e., within the framework of the strong-binding model), where both the refractive index and the gain/loss profile depend on the longitudinal direction of propagation. The reader is referred, for example, to (1) in
Liu et al. 2020
, which can be compared with (
2
) in the present manuscript (see also
Luo et al. 2013
). Besides, note the similarities between Hamiltonian (1) in
Hassan et al. 2017
and the one in (
4
). This implementation is feasible with the technology currently available
Rüter et al. 2010
;
Hassan et al. 2017
.

VI
Conclusions

The SUSY technique allows for the straightforward construction of complex drivings for the semiclassical Rabi system defined by (
2
), as well as for solutions of the time-dependent Schrödinger equation.
Therefore, the known SUSY systems available in the literature
Cooper et al. 1995
;
Fernández and Hussin 1999
;
Mielnik et al. 2000
;
Samsonov and Negro 2004
;
Correa and Plyushchay 2007
;
Miri et al. 2013
;
Correa et al. 2015
;
Cruz y Cruz et al. 2020
;
García-Muñoz et al. 2023
are suitable to be used to produce complex driving functions
w=ig(t)
in (
2
).
This, in turn, will increase the number of time-dependent, exactly solvable (analytically solvable) non-Hermitian problems, specifically those of the type (
2
).
It is important to emphasize that the solution of the Schrödinger equation (
2
) for the coupling function(s) defined by (
27
) is not trivial at all, since the Hamiltonian given in (
2
) for such choices of
g(t)
is non-Hermitian and time-dependent. However, as shown, the SUSY approach presented in this manuscript turns such a task into a completely tractable analytical problem.

In particular, the simplest case of a constant potential
V_{1}
allows the construction of complex driving functions (
27
), which produce transitions in the qubit state.
Two types of controlled transitions were obtained: periodic and nonperiodic, depending on the nature of the coupling or driving function.
In the frame of Ref.
Gong and Wang 2015
, it is essential to mention that the present results prove that not only a periodic impulse stabilizes the dynamics of a non-Hermitian system, but also a nonperiodic modulation function
g(t)
, as shown in section
IV.2.1
.
Moreover, this is just the beginning, as there are still many SUSY pairs to be analyzed
Cooper et al. 1995
;
Fernández and Hussin 1999
;
Mielnik et al. 2000
;
Samsonov and Negro 2004
;
Correa and Plyushchay 2007
;
Miri et al. 2013
;
Correa et al. 2015
;
Cruz y Cruz et al. 2020
;
García-Muñoz et al. 2023
.
Specifically, a quantum potential
V_{1}
with a point-like spectrum (with bound states) could expand the richness of the dynamics of the time-dependent non-Hermitian problem. For example, the Scarf II-type potential seems a good candidate for testing the current formalism
Bocanegra and Cruz y
Cruz 2022
, which is proposed as future work. Furthermore, the present analysis elucidates the importance of the theoretical study of the SUSY partners of quantum potentials, particularly the free particle potential.

Certainly, non-Hermitian Hamiltonians like (
2
) are of essential importance, because they describe physical interactions in the context of NMR (
Bloch 1946
;
Silver et al. 1984a
;
Silver et al. 1984b
;
Silver et al. 1985
;
Martínez-Tibaduiza et al. 2023
;
Enríquez and Cruz y
Cruz 2018
).
In particular, they model interactions between metastable states (resonances), e.g., an atom interacting with combined AC and DC fields
Moiseyev 2011
.
Furthermore, the non-Hermitian Hamiltonian (
2
) can be simulated by a pair of electromagnetic waveguides coupled via evanescent fields (as a non-Hermitian directional coupler), once the refractive indices and the gain/loss profile are allowed to depend on the propagation coordinate
Liu et al. 2020
;
Luo et al. 2013
.

Finally, it is worth mentioning that the presented results are in agreement with previously published conclusions
Gong and Wang 2015
;
Martínez-Tibaduiza et al. 2023
;
Enríquez and Cruz y
Cruz 2018
and give a deep, complementary insight into the dynamics and overall behavior of non-Hermitian systems that in turn are important in current major scenarios as shortcuts to adiabaticity
Bender et al. 2007
;
Ibáñez et al. 2011
;
Zheng et al. 2013
;
Torosov et al. 2013
, phase transitions
Pires and Macrì 2021
, quantum chaos
Cornelius et al. 2022
, to mention a few.
