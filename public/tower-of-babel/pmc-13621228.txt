Inflammatory Pseudotumour in Common Seal (Phoca vitulina): A Case Report
Authors: Javier M. De Pablo‐Moreno, Rocío Canales, Mireia Mainez, Nicolás Aradilla, Blanca Chinchilla, Néstor Porras, Ignacio Vargas‐Castro, Antonio Rodríguez‐Bertos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPinnipeds, including seals, sea lions and walruses, are marine mammals susceptible to a variety of pathological conditions. Although neoplastic lesions in these species are receiving increased attention, relatively little information is available compared to neoplastic studies in domestic animals. In this report, a 19‐year‐old intact male common seal (Phoca vitulina) presented with a chronic ulcer on the fifth phalanx. Radiological examination revealed cortical reaction with significant osseous alterations in this animal and in another companion common seal, although the flipper appeared macroscopically normal. Histopathological analysis identified nests of fusiform to oval cells accompanied by mixed lymphohistiocytic inflammation. Immunohistochemical evaluation demonstrated that neoplastic cells were positive for vimentin, α‐smooth muscle actin and calponin. Negative results were for pancytokeratin, desmin, CD31 and IRF4, supporting a myofibroblastic phenotype. The Ki‐67 proliferation index was low (5%). All histopathological and immunohistochemical features were consistent with an inflammatory pseudotumour. Microbiological culture isolated Vibrio alginolyticus from the lesion, a marine and fish pathogen previously linked to chronic cutaneous infections in humans. No herpesviral DNA was detected by polymerase chain reaction (PCR). Chronic bacterial infection and repeated microtrauma may have contributed to lesion development, consistent with reports of inflammatory pseudotumours arising in the context of persistent inflammatory stimuli.

Introduction

Pinnipeds belong to the broader group of marine mammals and are found in both natural habitats and captivity. The suborder Pinnipedia comprises the families Otariidae (fur seals and sea lions), Odobenidae (walruses) and Phocidae, which includes the true seals, such as the common seal (Phoca vitulina) (Berta 2009).

A comprehensive understanding of diseases affecting pinnipeds is critical for their health management and conservation, particularly given their genetic significance with neoplasms representing one of the most important concerns among these conditions (Browning et al. 2015; Newman and Smith 2006). The aetiology of neoplastic conditions in pinnipeds is multifactorial, involving environmental chemical carcinogens (Rothenberg et al. 2023), infectious agents such as oncogenic viruses (Deming et al. 2021) and genetic predispositions (Browning et al. 2014). Environmental contaminants may induce transient immunocompromise and increase susceptibility to viral infections, such as gammaherpesvirus in Otariidae, which has been associated with urothelial carcinomas in California sea lions (Zalophus californianus) (Rothenberg et al. 2023).

Carcinomas, particularly squamous cell carcinomas in various anatomical locations and urothelial carcinomas, are the most common neoplasms seen in pinnipeds (Browning et al. 2015; Newman and Smith 2006). Mesenchymal neoplasms, although less common, have also been reported and include undifferentiated sarcomas (Martínez‐Fernández et al. 2011) and giant cell sarcomas (Martony et al. 2019). Additional reports describe malignant lymphoid and histiocytic neoplasms such as histiocytic sarcomas, T‐cell lymphomas and various leukaemias (Reisfeld et al. 2022). Despite these findings, the literature on neoplasms in pinnipeds remains limited compared to domestic animals. This scarcity is partly due to challenges in obtaining well‐preserved tissue samples from free‐ranging individuals, which complicates diagnosis (Newman and Smith 2006). While sample preservation is more feasible in captive animals, the low incidence of reported cases restricts the development of comprehensive case series, prognostic evaluations, histological grading systems and therapeutic studies, in contrast to the more thoroughly investigated neoplastic diseases in small animal medicine (Avallone et al. 2021).

This study aims to present novel findings from a previously undocumented case of inflammatory myofibroblastic tumour (IMT) in a common seal (P. vitulina). The report provides a comprehensive description of the clinical course, gross, histopathological characteristics and immunohistochemical findings, thereby contributing to the growing body of knowledge on neoplastic diseases in pinnipeds.

Case Presentation

A 19‐year‐old intact male common seal (P. vitulina) presented with an ulcerated lesion at the ungual base of the lateral digit of the left hind flipper. The lesion (Figure 1A) was chronic, and multiple microbiological cultures were obtained over time, yielding variable results likely influenced by the aquatic environment and the presence of secondary infections.

Based on antimicrobial susceptibility testing, the animal received several courses of antibiotic and antifungal therapy; however, the lesion failed to resolve and continued to enlarge despite treatment. The therapeutic regimen included enrofloxacin (5 mg/kg PO BID) and doxycycline (5 mg/kg PO BID) for 45 days, as well as fluconazole (200 mg PO once weekly) for 1 year. Radiographic evaluation to assess potential bone involvement revealed cortical bone reaction in the affected distal phalanx (Figure 1B). During this assessment, the contralateral flipper of the same individual, as well as flippers from other companion seals without external lesions, were evaluated for comparison. Cortical bone reaction was also observed in the distal phalanges on these control radiographs (Figure 1C). A biopsy was obtained from the lesion for microbiological and histopathological evaluation using a 6‐mm dermatological biopsy punch (Aesthetic group, ZAC de la Gobette, 60540 Puiseux‐le‐Hauberger, France).

Microbiological cultures on Blood Agar Base and Marine Agar at 37°C identified Vibrio alginolyticus using MALDI‐TOF Biotyper technology (version 3.1.66, 11,897 entries) with an identification score of 2.407. The isolate demonstrated resistance to enrofloxacin, tetracycline, florfenicol and flumequine.

The 0.5 cm punch biopsy was fixed in 10% buffered formaldehyde for 48 hours. Histological examination revealed a mass occupying nearly the entire tissue section, characterized by moderate cellularity and nests of cells separated by desmoplastic connective fibres (Figure 1D). At higher magnification, the nests comprised fusiform to oval cells (10–15 µm) with smooth borders, clear cytoplasm and centrally located nuclei (Figure 1E). Mitoses were infrequent, and vascular invasion was absent. Moderate infiltration of lymphocytes and histiocytes was observed among the neoplastic cells. The superficial biopsy was ulcerated with a lack of epithelium and coagulated necrosis, containing numerous neutrophils, basophilic bacillary structures consistent with bacterial colonies and cellular debris. These findings indicated an unclassified neoplasm with secondary chronic bacterial infection. The affected flipper, including the ulcerated area, was surgically resected with amputation of the distal phalanx at the joint level to achieve wide surgical margins of at least 2 cm. Histological examination of the surgical borders confirmed infiltration of neoplastic cells into the deep tissue layers (Figure 1F).

Subsequently, immunohistochemical analysis was conducted in the punch‐biopsy to characterize the neoplastic cell population, as most of the inflammatory infiltrate masked the neoplastic cells. The immunohistochemical panel included: Pancytokeratin AE1/AE3 (Monoclonal Anti‐Mouse, Vitro Master Diagnostica, Granada, Spain; Ready to use‐RTU, reference MAD‐00001000QD), Vimentin (Monoclonal Anti‐Rabbit, Vitro Master Diagnostica, Granada, Spain; RTU, reference MAD000326QD), α‐Smooth Muscle Actin (α‐SMA, Monoclonal Anti‐Mouse, ThermoFisher, Waltham, MA, USA; 1:100, reference MA1‐06110), Desmin (Monoclonal Anti‐Mouse, ThermoFisher, Waltham, MA, USA; 1:100, reference MA5‐13259), CD31 (Monoclonal Anti‐Mouse, Dako, Glostrup, Denmark; 1:100, reference M0823), Calponin (Monoclonal Anti‐Mouse, ThermoFisher, Waltham, MA, USA, 1:100, reference MA5‐11620), CD3 (Polyclonal Anti‐Rabbit, Dako, Glostrup, Denmark; RTU, reference IR503), B‐cell‐specific activator protein (BSAP‐PAX5; Monoclonal Anti‐Mouse, Dako, Glostrup, Denmark; RTU, reference GA650), Interferon Regulatory Factor 4 (IRF4; Monoclonal Anti‐Rabbit, Dako, Glostrup, Denmark; RTU, reference ab124691), Ionized Calcium Binding Adapter Molecule 1 (Iba1; Polyclonal Anti‐Rabbit, ThermoFisher, Waltham, MA, USA; 1:100, reference PA5‐27436) and ki‐67 (Monoclonal Anti‐Rabbit, Vitro Master Diagnostica, Granada, Spain; RTU, reference MAD‐000310QD‐7). Positive control samples for the different antibodies were as follows: spleen from a healthy mouse for PAX5 +, CD3 + and Iba1 + cells; spleen from a mouse inoculated with myeloma cells for IRF‐4 + cells and small intestine from a healthy mouse for Ki‐67. For the remaining antibodies, mouse skin was used as a positive control. For negative controls, the primary antibody was omitted and substituted with tris‐buffered saline. Neoplastic cells were negative for pancytokeratin, desmin, CD31, and IRF4. All neoplastic cells exhibited strong and diffuse cytoplasmic immunoreactivity for vimentin (Figure 2A). Approximately 40% of cells showed mild, diffuse cytoplasmic positivity for α‐SMA (Figure 2B). Calponin was positive in all neoplastic cells, demonstrating a strong, granular cytoplasmic immunoreactivity (Figure 2C). Among round cell markers, CD3 showed strong immunoreactivity in approximately 40% of nest cells (Figure 2D). Iba1 was strongly expressed in approximately 60% of cells, particularly in the central regions of the nests (Figure 2E). Few PAX5‐positive cells were observed surrounding the nests. The Ki‐67 proliferation index, assessed by immunohistochemistry, ranged from 0% to 10% depending on the field with average value of 5% (Figure 2F). These findings support a diagnosis of myofibroblastic phenotype. The immunohistochemical results are summarized in Table 1.

To investigate the possible involvement of herpesvirus, 5‐µm‐thick scrolls of formalin‐fixed, paraffin‐embedded tissue blocks from the ulcerated flipper, representing different tissue‐section planes, were processed for dewaxing and DNA extraction using a commercial kit according to the manufacturer's instructions (DNeasy, Blood & Tissue Kit, Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). All samples were subjected to a panherpesvirus nested PCR assay targeting the DNA polymerase gene (Van Devanter et al. 1996), yielding negative results.

Discussion

IMT is a neoplasm with low metastatic potential, characterized by myofibroblast proliferation and an accompanying inflammatory infiltrate characterized by rearrangements in anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) (Choi 2025). These tumours are well documented in human medicine, affecting all age groups and occurring in diverse anatomical locations, including the head, limbs, abdominal cavity and lungs (Choi 2025). In human medicine, the term IMT encompasses several lesion types with many studies including inflammatory pseudotumour (IPT) within this category (Patnana et al. 2012). IPT is also defined by the proliferation of myofibroblasts and fibroblasts with a heterogeneous inflammatory cell population. Currently, IPT and IMT are distinguished based on aetiology, with IPT considered acquired and not genetically based (Choi 2025). IPTs have been attributed to chronic infections, autoimmune diseases and traumatic injuries (Patnana et al. 2012). In this case report, minor repetitive microtraumas against the enclosure walls may have caused subungual hematomas and subsequent injury to the nail base, as described in human medicine (Lee et al. 2013), thereby contributing to the development of the inflammatory lesion observed. Granulomatous dermatitis has also been associated with the presence of anisakid nematodes; these dermal parasites are prevalent in marine mammals, although very few pathologists appear to examine the skin of marine mammals (van Beurden et al. 2015).

In veterinary medicine, these tumours are rarely reported, though cases have been described in dogs (Knight et al. 2009) and cats (Ferro et al. 2021; Machida et al. 2022). To the authors' knowledge, this is the first report of IPT in common seal (P. vitulina). IMT/IPT in animals typically demonstrates immunopositivity for vimentin, calponin (Ferro et al. 2021; Knight et al. 2009; Machida et al. 2022) and α‐smooth muscle actin (Ferro et al. 2021; Knight et al. 2009; Machida et al. 2022; Paździor‐Czapula et al. 2023). Desmin expression is variable with reports of positivity (Ferro et al. 2021), partial positivity in fusiform cells (Knight et al. 2009), or complete negativity (Machida et al. 2022; Paździor‐Czapula et al. 2023). The current case aligns with this immunoprofile, showing positivity for vimentin, calponin and α‐smooth muscle actin; and negativity for desmin. Regarding the differential diagnosis between IMT and IPT from an immunohistochemical perspective, the detection of ALK is not always conclusive, as negative results do not exclude the possibility of IMT; therefore, additional molecular or genetic techniques are necessary (Choi 2025). The inflammatory infiltrate in IMT/IPT generally consists of lymphocytes, plasma cells and occasional eosinophils with some cases demonstrating histiocytic populations (Ferro et al. 2021; Tang et al. 1990). In the present case, a mixed inflammatory infiltrate was observed, predominantly T lymphocytes and macrophages with fewer B lymphocytes.

In this case, no herpesvirus was detected, although certain viral agents, such as Epstein–Barr virus and herpesvirus, have been implicated in IPT pathogenesis (Bai et al. 2020; Choi 2025). Although PCR on paraffin‐embedded tissues is not the preferred method, other studies have still detected positivity when herpesvirus is identified (Hydeskov et al. 2018). In contrast, V. alginolyticus was identified in the lesion. This bacterium is a common pathogen in fish (Mohamad et al. 2019), particularly marine species and has been associated with chronic skin infections following wounds in humans (Escudero et al. 2015; Slifka et al. 2017). Its presence in this case may suggest a potential association with tumour development, consistent with previous reports linking IPT to chronic infections (Choi 2025; Patnana et al. 2012).

In conclusion, this case represents an additional instance of IPT in veterinary medicine and the first reported occurrence in the common seal (P. vitulina), thereby expanding the documented spectrum of neoplasms in marine mammals and pinnipeds.
