Risk factors for colonisation and infection with multidrug-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa in intensive care unit: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Serge Eyebe, Guy Sadeu Wafeu, Emilia Lyonga Mbamyah, Chritine Danielle Evina, Pierre Ongolo-Zogo, Alain Bertrand Dongmo, Arthur Mbida
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Background

Pseudomonas aeruginosa (PA) is a ubiquitous Gram-negative bacterium with minimal requirements for survival in the environment. It has a remarkable ability to colonise surfaces even in the most restricted environments.

P. aeruginosa can also cause infection in immunocompromised individuals [1]. It is one of the most common pathogens involved in healthcare-associated infections in intensive care units (ICU) [2]. The prevalence of hospital-acquired P. aeruginosa carriage was 15.3% in an adult French population hospitalised in ICUs [3]. P. aeruginosa resistance to various antibiotics is increasing, along with the global burden of antibiotics resistance [4]. Pseudomonas spp. was found as the most common pathogen (28%) in ICU infections. In the same study, Multidrug-resistance was observed in 80.1% of PA isolates [5]. Infection by MDR-PA is associated with increased morbidity and mortality rates [5–8]. An average length of ICU stay of 25 days and an associated mortality rate of 20% was found as associated with MDR-PA infection [5]. It is also associated with a high surgery incidence and an increased number of invasive procedures [5–7].

Infection with P. aeruginosa in the ICU is known to be either endogenous or exogenous or both, but the role of each of these contamination route remains to be clarified [9]. Endogenous transmission has been well described [1, 10]. It is the main source of contamination associated to the gut microbiota under antibiotic pressure [11]. Exogenous transmission comes from aqueous environments such as faucets, invasive medical devices, other patients and health workers through their hands [12, 13].

Most studies tend to favour the endogenous source, with the exogenous source more often implicated in outbreaks [2, 9, 12].

Furthermore, the source of P. aeruginosa in ICUs is not well understood, nor are the factors associated with colonisation and infection in ICUs [9]. This may be due to a lack of data on the prevalence, risk factors and environmental determinants of MDR-PA colonisation or infection. For example, in a systematic review conducted in 2018, Raman and colleagues found significant gaps and inconsistencies in the knowledge of risk factors associated with the occurrence of MDR P. aeruginosa in nosocomial infections. Raman's systematic review did not specifically address the issue of ICUs and was limited to endogenous risk factors. Raman's systematic review did not specifically address the issue of ICUs and was limited to endogenous risk factors [14].

Therefore, there is a growing interest in understanding both individual and environmental factors associated with multidrug-resistant P. aeruginosa in the ICU, where high morbidity and mortality rates have been reported [5, 6, 15].

The primary objective of this systematic review was to investigate individual (endogenous) and environmental (exogenous) factors associated with the colonisation and infection with MDR Pseudomonas aeruginosa in intensive care units. The secondary objectives of this study were to (i) estimate the prevalence of Multidrug resistant P. aeruginosa colonisation in ICU, and (ii) describe the main environmental sources of MDR-PA acquisition and infection in ICU.

Methods

This systematic review is reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) [16]. The review was performed as indicated in the protocol published [17].

Data collection

Type of studies included

This review included cross-sectional studies, case–control studies (nested or not) and cohort studies (either prospective, retrospective or ambi-directional) carried out in intensive care units (ICUs). For meta-analysis only articles in which risk factors of MDR Pseudomonas aeruginosa acquisition/infection could be assessed were selected. Studies without data on MDR-PA were excluded. For multi-disease studies, when MDR-PA data was not available separately in isolation, these studies were excluded. This was also the case for studies conducted in several hospital wards where it was not possible to obtain data from patients in the ICU.

Type of participants from selected studies

Studies including all patients, admitted in an ICU, whatever the reason for admission and the origin (community, another hospital, or another unit of the same hospital) of patients, were taken into consideration. No specific definition of ICU was applied, and all studies which authors reported as having been conducted in an ICU were considered. We also included studies where it was possible to identified patients with specimens collected and tested for P. aeruginosa (positivity and antimicrobial susceptibility) during hospitalization and/or samples collected from the ICU patient’s environment (including staff hands and gloves) for P. aeruginosa testing. No specific restrictions on type of patients were applied.

Outcomes

Our study focused on people admitted to the intensive care unit in whom MDR-PA was found.

The primary outcome

(i) Factors associated with acquisition of MDR-PA. Since 2000, the most commonly used definition of MDR for Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria is 'resistance to three or more antimicrobial classes' [18, 19].

Secondary outcomes

(ii) Risk factors of carriage. Carriage was defined as positive screened specimens. These specimens could be collected from rectal swabs, nasal swabs or tracheal aspiration or others [20].

(iii) Risk factors of colonisation. Colonization was defined as positive clinical specimens, and infection as positive clinical specimens with clinical signs confirming infection.

The prevalence of MDR-PA was calculated as the number of people admitted to the medical intensive care unit who were found to have MDR-PA out of the total number of patients admitted to the intensive care unit during the same study.

Search strategy

Six databases were searched from inception onward, namely MEDLINE (Pubmed), EMBASE (OVID), the Cochrane Library (Wiley), Web of Science, CINAHL (EBSCOHost) and LILACS (BIREME). We also searched in grey literature using Google Scholar and Open Grey.

A search strategy using Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and text words related to Pseudomonas aeruginosa, intensive care units, carriage, colonization or infection were used as described in Table 1 for MEDLINE (Pubmed); these were adjusted for the search in other databases cited above. For studies that were not found via internet search, the authors were directly contacted in order to obtain them. Furthermore, when a full text was not available online, the author was asked by email to provide it.

Search strategy

Study selection

The search was conducted by an experienced information specialist and there were no language restrictions. For trials published in a language other than English, Deepl translate was used. The search was conducted until 31 December 2023. The results of the electronic searches were uploaded into Rayyan software [21] to identify and remove duplicates. After this stage, two reviewers independently screened the titles and abstracts of all the studies identified by the search strategy. First, they screened the studies according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria. The full text of these selected studies was then retrieved for a second analysis to decide whether the study should be included in the final analysis or not. Disagreements between the two reviewers were resolved by discussion and consensus. The PRISMA flowchart template was used to describe the number of articles retrieved and screened at each step [18, 21].

Data synthesis and management

Firstly, a descriptive analysis of the characteristics of the studies, the people included and the risk factors identified was performed.

Second, prevalence, odds ratio, relative risk (RR) with 95% confidence intervals were calculated from the collected data. For each study, we extracted the following information from the tables provided, the total number of individuals with multidrug-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa (MDR-PA), the total number of people without MDR-PA. In addition, within each of these two groups (MDR-PA positive and negative), we determined the number of exposed and unexposed individuals for the risk factor(s) of interest.

Thirdly, a meta-analysis of case–control and cohort studies with similar populations, same parameters and P. aeruginosa testing was performed. Factors influencing the estimates were evaluated.

Data were analysed using the meta package in R (R Studio). A fixed effect model was used to pool the different statistics. Specifically, the Mantel–Haenszel method was used for proportions and the inverse variance method for continuous data. We chose to use a fixed effect model because it was assumed that the included studies estimated a common true effect, reflecting a homogeneous population and consistent study designs. This approach was considered appropriate given the focus on specific, well-defined risk factors and outcomes. Heterogeneity between pooled studies was tested using the standard chi-squared test with the Q statistic (p < 0.10 statistically significant).

The degree of heterogeneity was quantified using the I2 statistic, with I2 > 50% considered to represent substantial inconsistency or significant statistical heterogeneity. Where statistical pooling is not possible, results are presented in a synthetic narrative form.

Quality of studies and evidence assessment

The methodological quality of the studies was assessed using the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute Critical Appraisal Tools for each appropriate study design (cross-sectional, case–control, cohort). Potential bias in the design, conduct and analysis of each study was assessed by two independent reviewers. Confidence in the evidence was discussed among the authors.

Publication bias was assessed graphically using a funnel plot. Futhermore,the statistical significance of publication bias was assessed using Egger's and Beggar's tests. A p-value of less than 0.05 was set as the threshold for determining the presence or absence of publication bias.

The studies were published between 1984 and 2023. Of the 13 articles extracted, risk factors for MDR Pseudomonas aeruginosa acquisition/infection were assessed in 8 studies selected for meta-analysis, see Fig. 1 [19, 22, 23, 27, 29–31, 34].

Environmental data found in only 4 studies [20, 24, 26, 31]. There were no standard definitions of MDR-PA. Many definitions of MDR-PA were used from 1983 to 2021. For example, in their studies, Aloush et al. defined MDR-PA as resistance to all the agents studied (ceftazidime, cefepime, aztreonam, ciprofloxacin, piperacillin and gentamicin) [6], while Kumar et al. defined it as bacteria "resistant to three or more antimicrobial classes" [18]. In addition, Jung et al. had defined it as a bacterium that is resistant to at least three of the following four drugs: ceftazidime, imipenem, ciprofloxacin and tobramycin [35]. Of the 13 extracted articles, risk factors for acquisition/colonisation of MDR Pseudomonas aeruginosa were assessed in 8 studies selected for meta-analysis (Table 2).

Descriptive analysis of main risk factors in MDR-PA acquisition and infection in the ICU

These risk factors could be divided into four groups. The first group were factors related to clinical status; the second group were factors related to medications received; the third group were factors related to invasive procedures or the use of some medical devices; and finally, environmental factors.

Factors related to clinical status

Previous ICU stay is a factor associated with acquisition and carriage of MDR-PA. In the selected articles, the clinical status of patients was assessed using many types of scores. These were either the APACHE II (Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II) score, the SAPS II (Simplified Acute Physiology Score II), the TISS-28 (Therapeutic Intervention Scoring System), the QSOFA (Quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment) or the Omega score. It appears that patients with a score reflecting a severe condition, such as bedridden patients [6] or those with a QSOFA > 2 [32], are at higher risk of MDR-PA infection. In addition, some specific conditions are associated with a high risk of MDR-PA infection. These included severe haematological disease [22], a history of infection or colonisation with Pseudomonas aeruginosa [24], and underlying cerebral disease [32].

Factors related to drugs received

The most cited and apparently most important factor is "antibiotic pressure" [5, 19, 20, 22–26]. The authors define it in three ways. First, it is related to the number of antibiotics received by a patient, as defined by Aloush et al. [6], or the number of days on antibiotics [24], or the percentage of days spent on antibiotics, or the number of days on antibiotics [29]. Second, the type of antibiotic is of primary importance for MDR-PA acquisition. The most commonly used antibiotics are carbapenems (imipenem), quinolones and fluoroquinolones, beta-lactams (cefepime, piperacillin-tazobactam, penicillin), aminoglycosides and metronidazole [5, 19, 20, 22–26]. Thirdly, some therapies such as sedation [29], vasopressor treatment and immunosuppressive therapy [6] have been associated with the risk of MDR-PA infection.

Factors related to invasive procedures or the use of some medical devices

Mechanical ventilation has been identified as a risk factor of MDR-PA infection [6, 24, 29, 32, 33]. Some authors suggest that the duration of mechanical ventilation is an important determinant of MDR-PA infection [29, 32]. For example Saherman et al. found a duration of more than 5 days for the acquisition of MDR-PA [32]. Haemodialysis [31], central venous catheters [23, 26], urinary catheters [23, 26], Foley catheters [6], arterial catheters [29], parenteral nutrition [33], and tracheostomy [29] are risk factors for MDR-PA infection.

Main environmental sources with MDR-PA acquisition and colonization in ICU

Main environmental sources with MDR-PA infection and colonization in ICU

Some studies point to a contaminated environment as a potential factor in the acquisition of MDR-PA. This factor would be related to the pressure of colonisation of objects or surfaces, especially previously occupied bed or room [25]. Thus, the type of environment may be a factor of an acquisition in close relation to the length of stay in the ICU. Objects such as sinks, faucets, water taps and nursing equipment are those most at risk of producing carriage, colonisation and infection [25, 28, 29, 32].

Prevalence infection of MDR aeruginosa in ICU

A total of 13 studies reported the prevalence of MDR-PA in ICU patients. There was a high degree of heterogeneity between the results of these studies (I2 = 98%, p < 0.001). However, the pooled prevalence was 4% (95% CI: 0%—11%). Figure 2 shows the prevalence with 95% CI of all the studies reporting the prevalence of MDR-PA.

Discussion

The pooled prevalence was 4% (95% CI: 0%-11%). This result seems low compared to many authors who found more than 15% [19, 24, 36].

In our study, we found that the use of some antibiotics during the ICU stay, the use of invasive devices and procedures were risk factors for infection or acquisition of MDR-PAs. Some authors such as Raman et al., Hirsch and Tam found the same results [14, 15]. In addition, the length of stay in the ICU (more than 8 days) and inanimate objects and surfaces susceptible to high bacterial colonisation pressure (sinks, taps…) were also identified as risk factors. All this strongly suggests the role of endogenous and exogenous transmission in the infection or acquisition of MDR-PA in ICUs.

One explanation could be that ICU patients, who are often critically ill, require intensive contact with caregivers, treatment and invasive devices. All this tends to compromise protective barriers and increase the risk of exogenous contamination [6]. Furthermore, repeated sterilisation in the ICU favours Pseudomonas aeruginosa strains, which are more likely to thrive in this environment, to the detriment of other pathogens [1]. In addition, the selection pressure exerted by broad-spectrum antibiotics, which are widely used in intensive care units, and the long-term or repeated use of antibiotics could lead to the development of MDR-PAs. It could be a possible consequence of the eradication of competing flora, which is replaced by MDR-PA strains [6, 37].

Regarding MDR-PA, there are two opposing hypotheses. The first hypothesis is endogenous transmission of MDR-PA infection [38] and the second is exogenous transmission [39]. This review was intended to be as broad as possible in order to obtain as much information as possible to better contribute to this debate. The debate is particularly unbalanced because many authors have focused on endogenous acquisition factors. The two previous reviews cited [15, 16] did not address the exogenous aspect of MDR-PA acquisition/infection. In this review, the evidence clearly suggests that these two routes of transmission (endogenous and exogenous) are likely to operate together. Unfortunately, insufficient data and methodological weaknesses in selected studies did not allow us to perform a meta-analysis to estimate the association between MDR-PA acquisition/infection and objects and surfaces.

Limitations

Despite many articles retrieved via open keywords, only 13 articles were selected. This means that information on antimicrobial resistance in ICUs is scarce, especially for environmental data. It is therefore necessary to increase the number of studies that address environmental issues.

Geographically, the data gap is still large, especially in certain continents such as Latin America, the Middle East, Asia and Africa. It's necessary to carry out larger cohort or case–control studies and surveillance around the world [40].

The studies eligible for inclusion were heterogeneous. Although many authors chose case–control and cohort studies, the definition of cases or controls, exposure and risk factors varied between authors. In our meta-analysis, the I2 different statistic calculated shows that there was a significant heterogeneity between studies. The lack of standard definitions in the primary studies could be considered a limitation of the systematic review and meta-analysis. We tried to address this issue by using similar comparison groups in the meta-analysis. Our definitions of cases and controls were intended to be as inclusive as possible, considering patients with MDR-PA, regardless of the author's definition, and patients without MDR-PA, regardless of whether they had comorbidities or not.

The funnel plot shows an asymmetry. This may be explained by the small number of studies included, but more importantly by the large differences in the sample sizes of these studies. [37], ranging from 36 to 7071. In addition, funnel plots are most effective in detecting publication bias when at least 20 studies are analysed [40].

Conclusion

The increasing prevalence of multidrug-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa among hospitalised patients in intensive care units remains a challenge for practitioners. Both individual (endogenous) and environmental (exogenous) factors are associated with colonisation and infection with MDR Pseudomonas aeruginosa in ICUs. The pooled prevalence of MDR-PA infection in ICUs was 4% (95% CI: 0%—11%). Mechanical ventilation, parenteral nutrition, catheters, previous use of antibiotics, especially carbapenems, and length of stay in the ICU (> 8 days), inanimate objects and surfaces exposed to high bacterial contamination pressure were significant risk factors for acquisition or infection with MDR-PA in the ICU. Our review did not allow us to correctly estimate the contribution of all exogenous factors. This is an insufficient data worldwide with a weak methodological approach. It is therefore necessary to carry out larger studies (cohort, case–control and surveillance) worldwide with standard definitions of case/exposure and that estimate the association of exogenous factors in the acquisition/infection of MDR-PA.
