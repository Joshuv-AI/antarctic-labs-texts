Resuscitation of newborn infants and postnatal support of transition – An overview of the European Resuscitation Council guideline 2025
Authors: Lukas Peter Mileder, Michael Wagner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In October 2025, the European Resuscitation Council published its latest practice guideline about “Newborn Resuscitation and Support of Transition of Infants at Birth”. In this focused review, we summarize the most relevant elements of this guideline in a structured manner. For this purpose, we first outline the main aspects that remained unchanged from the 2021 guideline before focusing on the implemented modifications and changes.

Introduction

The International Liaison Committee on Resuscitation (ILCOR) was established in 1992, based on a collaboration between national and international resuscitation councils from Europe, North and South America, Africa, Australia, and New Zealand 1 . Its declared aim was to strengthen and structure global collaboration on the topic of resuscitation.

In 1999, the ILCOR Pediatric Working Group published an advisory statement, addressing “the unique and changing physiology of the newly born infant within the first few hours after birth” 1 . Therein, central concepts of postnatal resuscitation were defined based on expert consensus, including the primary focus on effective ventilation in the non-vigorous newborn infant, the need for chest compressions if the heart rate remains below 60 beats per minute despite effective ventilation for 30 seconds, the compression-to-ventilation ratio of 3:1, the goal of 90 compressions and 30 inflation breaths per minute during cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR), and the administration of adrenaline in case of persistent bradycardia after 30 seconds of CPR 2 . Almost as important, the ILCOR Pediatric Working Group also addressed areas of debate as well as the need for additional research on key aspects of postnatal resuscitation.

Since then, ILCOR regularly reviews current peer-reviewed resuscitation science, updating and publishing its “Consensus on Science With Treatment Recommendations” every five years 3 . These publications form the foundation of national and international resuscitation council guidelines.

In October 2025, the European Resuscitation Council (ERC) published its latest practice guideline about “Newborn Resuscitation and Support of Transition of Infants at Birth”, constituting an update of the previous guideline from 2021. With the goal of presenting “a logical approach to resuscitation and support of transition to extra-uterine life, for both preterm and term newborn infants”, the ERC guideline addresses antepartum factors including the birth setting and required training and education for professionals attending deliveries, key aspects of postnatal stabilization and resuscitation, communication with parents, and ethical considerations 4 .

Aim

In this focused review, we will summarize the most relevant elements of the latest ERC guideline in a structured manner rather than perform a critical appraisal of the document. For this purpose, we will outline the main aspects that remained unchanged from the 2021 guideline 5 before focusing more on the implemented modifications and changes.

Instead of aiming for completeness, which is difficult to achieve especially within the confines of a “focused review”, we will highlight key topics that we deem most relevant for neonatal care providers. Therefore, reading this review should only be done in conjunction with the original ERC guideline. For consistency purposes, we will adhere to the nomenclature used by the ERC Newborn Life Support Writing Group, using the term “newborn infant” when referring to a “baby at birth” 4 .

Key aspects that remained mostly unchanged

Before birth

Known risk factors for postnatal compromise should be used to decide how to prepare for every delivery. Based on the individual risk assessment, “an interprofessional team with appropriate experience and training” should be quickly available or present at birth 4 .

Timely preparation of the delivery room environment and equipment necessary to stabilize newborn infants is recommended. In addition, a briefing of the responsible team using a checklist and/or a cognitive aid is considered important 4 .

Umbilical cord management

In stable newborn infants without the need for respiratory support, “at least 60 seconds” of delayed cord clamping should be facilitated. In contrast, if life-saving interventions are required, the cord should be clamped and cut within 30 seconds “to minimize any delay to necessary interventions” 4 .

The latest ERC guideline emphasizes that delaying cord clamping for longer than 60 seconds is generally preferred, especially in preterm neonates below 34 weeks of gestation, while making it clear not to milk the umbilical cord in preterm neonates below 28 weeks of gestation 4 .

Thermal control

Newborn infants’ body temperature should be monitored “regularly or continuously after birth”, targeting a range from 36.5°C to 37.5°C. Among the interventions recognized to contribute to maintaining normothermia are a draught-free birth environment with elevated ambient temperature, drying and removing wet linen, covering the newborn infant’s head and body, early skin-to-skin-placement, and stabilization under a pre-heated radiant warmer. In addition, preterm neonates below 32 weeks of gestation should have their bodies placed in a plastic bag or have it covered with a wrap, while heated humidified respiratory gases should be considered 4 .

Postnatal assessment

Initial assessment should be performed “as soon as possible after birth”, focusing on spontaneous breathing efforts, heart rate, and muscle tone. Methods for continuous assessment of heart rate, such as electrocardiography or pulse oximetry, are encouraged if interventions are required or when stabilizing premature newborn infants. Inadequate spontaneous breathing in newborn infants with reduced muscle tone and a heart rate below 100 beats per minute should quickly prompt interventions to support transition, including tactile stimulation and, if ineffective, non-invasive ventilation. It is emphasized that skin color is not reliable in the assessment of oxygenation 4 .

Airway patency

An open airway is central for successful lung aeration after birth. Placing the newborn infant in supine position with its head in a neutral position is critical to achieve airway patency. The jaw thrust maneuver enlarges the pharyngeal space and should ideally be performed by two providers right from the start. Routine oropharyngeal suctioning of amniotic fluid or secretions is not recommended and should only be performed in case of unsuccessful lung inflation 4 .

Lung inflation and support of breathing

If newborn infants remain “apneic, gasping or not breathing effectively” despite tactile stimulation and having an open airway, positive pressure ventilation – using either a face mask or nasal interface – should be commenced “ideally within 60 seconds” after birth. Delivering five inflation breaths with an inflation time of up to three seconds is recommended to achieve lung aeration, with a starting peak inspiratory pressure of 30 cmH 2 O in newborn infants equal to or above 32 weeks of gestation, and of 25 cmH 2 O in those below this threshold. Visible thoracic excursions and an increase or stabilization in heart rate indicate effective ventilation. In these cases, positive pressure ventilation should be continued with 30 inflation breaths (inspiratory time about one second) per minute, until sufficient spontaneous breathing efforts are present. A respiratory function monitor can help deliver tidal volumes within the target range of four to eight milliliters per kilogram 4 .

If initial positive pressure ventilation does not result in visible chest rise and an increase in heart rate, equipment and head position should be checked. Alternative airway opening techniques should be considered, and if needed, peak inspiratory pressures may be increased. If non-invasive ventilation remains ineffective, insertion of a supraglottic airway device, i. e., a laryngeal mask, or ultimately, endotracheal intubation should be considered, depending on level of expertise and competency 4 .

Spontaneously breathing newborn infants with clinical signs of respiratory distress should be supported by continuous positive airway pressure. The recommended level of positive end-expiratory pressure was slightly changed to a starting level of 6 cmH 2 O. Especially in preterm neonates, a T-piece resuscitator should be used both for positive pressure ventilation with a positive end-expiratory pressure and for delivery of continuous positive airway pressure, depending on availability 4 .

Cardiopulmonary resuscitation

Chest compressions are known to be ineffective before successful lung aeration. Therefore, chest compressions are indicated in case of a heart rate below 60 beats per minute and not before “at least 30 seconds of effective ventilation”. There were no changes to the recommended compression-to-ventilation ratio of 3:1, the target compression depth of one third of the anterior-posterior chest diameter, and the increase in oxygen concentration to 100% during CPR. The use of a two-handed technique for chest compressions, as recommended in 2021 5 , has been clarified as being “the two-thumb-hands-encircling-technique with overlapping or adjacent thumbs” 4 .

Vascular access and adrenaline

Umbilical venous access should be used during postnatal resuscitation, while intraosseous vascular access is considered an alternative 4 .

If the heart rate of a newborn infant remains below 60 beats per minute and “is not increasing” after at least 30 seconds of CPR, adrenaline should be administered at a dose of 10–30 µg per kilogram, preferably via an umbilical venous catheter or intraosseous vascular access. In case of persistent bradycardia, subsequent doses of adrenaline are now recommended every four minutes 4 , which is more specific than the recommended interval of every three to five minutes in the 2021 guideline 5 .

Key aspects that were modified or changed

Scope of the guideline

The ERC guideline from 2021 did not specifically outline in which situations its algorithm should be applied to stabilize and/or resuscitate newborn infants and neonates 5 . In 2025 it was made clear that the guideline on “Newborn Resuscitation and Support of Transition of Infants at Birth” should be used for newborn infants, regardless whether born in- or out-of-hospital. In addition, this guideline “can also be used during Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU) stay, especially in preterm infants or term infants with primary respiratory problems” 4 .

In contrast, using the Pediatric Life Support guideline 6 is recommended in collapsing neonates after their first discharge from hospital, and its application is considered reasonable in newborn infants and neonates “(i) after cardiac surgery, (ii) in known cardiac arrhythmia, and (iii) in other non-respiratory cardiac arrests” 5 .

While this guidance is helpful, especially considering the lack of robust evidence to guide the transition from the neonatal to the pediatric resuscitation algorithm, institutions are still required to find individual solutions, either based on hospital location, patient age, cause of cardiac arrest, or provider training 7 .

Telemedicine

The ERC guideline acknowledges that maintaining neonatal resuscitation skills is challenging especially for healthcare professionals in delivery units with low birth numbers. Therefore, considering telemedical support by a neonatologist “where the technology is available” is recommended 4 .

This is based on findings from retrospective or observational studies linking telemedical support with improved quality of postnatal stabilization and increased adherence to practice recommendations 8 .

Video laryngoscopy for intubation

If endotracheal intubation is required with a competent provider being present, video laryngoscopy should be used, if available 4 .

This recommendation has been clearly upgraded and reinforced in comparison to the guideline from 2021 5 , acknowledging findings from a recent meta-analysis, showing an improved first attempt intubation success rate with video laryngoscopy 9 .

Initial oxygen concentration

The current ERC guideline has simplified the recommendation regarding the initial concentration of oxygen during early phases of respiratory support. While in newborn infants equal to or above 32 weeks of gestation positive pressure ventilation or continuous positive airway pressure should be started in room air, a concentration of at least 30% is recommended in more immature newborn infants 4 .

In this regard it is worthwhile noting that not reaching an arterial oxygen saturation of 80% at five minutes after birth is associated with intraventricular hemorrhage above grade 3 in newborn infants below 32 weeks of gestation 10 . Furthermore, an individual participant data network meta-analysis involving 1,055 newborn infants identified low certainty evidence that positive pressure ventilation with an initial oxygen concentration of 90% or higher was associated with reduced mortality at hospital discharge 11 .

Arterial oxygen saturation targets

Instead of the previous target values at two, five, and ten minutes after birth 5 , the ERC guideline now recommends titrating oxygen based on pre-ductal pulse oximetry, targeting the following ranges: 70–75% at three minutes, 80–85% at five minutes, and 85–95% at ten minutes after birth, respectively 4 .

Previous oxygen saturation targets were based on the seminal research of Jennifer Dawson and colleagues 12 , who used data from newborn infants, 66% of whom were born at term, to derive oxygen saturation reference values. Percentile charts for arterial oxygen saturation in extreme and moderate preterm infants with favorable outcome, defined as survival without cerebral injury at term-equivalent age, have been published as a secondary analysis of data from the COSGOD III trial 13 . Combining these two studies led to the newly introduced arterial oxygen saturation targets, which now include a more balanced share of (premature) transitional physiology.

Improving ventilation during cardio-pulmonary resuscitation

It is now recommended to consider either placement of a supraglottic airway or endotracheal intubation whenever chest compressions are performed. The current ERC guideline considers the use of a supraglottic airway device reasonable, especially if providers lack intubation competency or if intubation has failed 4 .

This clearly recognizes supraglottic airways as effective airway adjuncts, especially in newborn infants and neonates over 34 weeks of gestation and with a birth weight above 1500 g 14 . The current recommendation also encourages the use of a mnemonic during situations with ventilation difficulties such as “MR SOPA”, where the A stands for any alternative airway 15 .

Glucose

“If possible”, blood glucose levels should be measured during resuscitation. The ERC guideline does not further specify at which time point or how often to measure blood glucose but recommends administering 2 ml per kilogram of 10% glucose intravenously or via an intraosseous access “if blood glucose is low” 4 .

With this recommendation, the ERC identifies both hypo- and/or hyperglycemia as commonly encountered problems during neonatal stabilization and resuscitation 16 , while choosing objective measurement of blood glucose levels instead of empirical treatment. Nonetheless, to date there is no universally agreed level of normoglycemia, not even in apparently healthy newborn infants above 35 weeks of gestation 17 .

Sodium bicarbonate and naloxone

Both of these drugs are no longer recommended by the current ERC guideline 4 .

The ILCOR task force withdrew its previous recommendation, concluding that “there is neither direct nor indirect evidence to inform a treatment recommendation” with sodium bicarbonate 3 . A Cochrane review did not find sufficient evidence to prove that naloxone administration actually benefits newborn infants exposed to maternal opioid analgesia, while raising concern about its safety in this specific context and suggesting limiting the use of naloxone to randomized controlled trials 18 .

Out-of-hospital birth

Delivery of a newborn infant outside of a hospital should be considered a remote or lower resource setting, especially in case of unexpected or premature birth. The ERC guideline highlights the prevention or treatment of hypothermia and hypoxia as the primary focus for out-of-hospital healthcare providers 4 .

An unplanned out-of-hospital birth is associated with increased neonatal morbidity and mortality, while hypothermia is a common finding that further aggravates the prognosis of newborn infants 19 20 . Thus, emergency medical services should be sufficiently prepared and trained to manage such infrequent events, especially in regard to preventing hypothermia and managing respiratory problems 4 . Regular participation in dedicated trainings programs may help prepare emergency medical personnel better for these tasks 21 22 .

Continuing education and training

Healthcare professionals should be provided with the opportunity to participate in training sessions frequently, with the aim of “maintaining up-to-date knowledge as well as technical and non-technical skills” in stabilization and resuscitation of newborn infants. Furthermore, the ERC guideline specifically recommends training at a frequency of “at least once per year to prevent skill decay” and “preferably supplemented with more frequent short-duration booster sessions” 4 .

While these are important recommendations, it is in our opinion necessary to distinguish between technical or algorithm-based skills training, addressing mask ventilation, delivery of chest compressions, placement of emergency vascular access, and drug administration within the environment of a simulated resuscitation, and dedicated simulation-based team training with its primary focus on non-technical skills and team performance. Technical skills necessary for providing postnatal resuscitation significantly decay within six to twelve months 23 , leading to the current recommendation of frequent short booster training sessions every three to six months 4 . For team-oriented simulation training, reasonable training intervals will depend on pre-defined learning goals, the target population, and educational resources. In this regard, in situ simulation in the real healthcare environment is being recognized not only as effective for training in human factors and crisis resource management 24 , but also for the assessment of team structures, emergency preparedness, the delivery room environment, and emergency equipment 25 26 .

Conclusion

The 2025 ERC guideline constitutes a logical development and enhancement of the previous guideline from 2021. Its general structure and focus remained unchanged, while important topics, such as the transition to the pediatric resuscitation algorithm, telemedicine, and video laryngoscopy as the new standard for endotracheal intubation, were adopted. The main principles of delivery room resuscitation and the postnatal support of transition remained intact, and only slight modifications were implemented here and there. This is not surprising, because changing already existing recommendations requires convincing new evidence, while more recent randomized controlled studies on important issues, such as the VentFirst 27 and SURV1VE trial 28 , failed to present evidence showing the need to change clinical practice.

While the ERC guideline again provides a helpful framework to inform delivery room practices, there remain several unanswered questions and unresolved topics. These include, and are not limited to, modes of postnatal stabilization of extremely premature newborn infants, delayed cord clamping procedures, choice of the interface for initial respiratory support, initial oxygen concentration in newborn infants below 32 weeks of gestation, and primary vascular access route. Thus, individual institutions should use the current ERC guideline to develop internal standards and identify best local practices, based on the target population, resources, and team competence.
