AD-EE: Early Exiting for Fast and Reliable Vision-Language Models in Autonomous Driving
Lianming Huang, Haibo Hu, Yufei Cui, Jiacheng Zuo, Shangyu Wu, Nan Guan, Chun Jason Xue

Abstract
With the rapid advancement of autonomous driving, deploying Vision-Language Models (VLMs) to enhance perception and decision-making has become increasingly common. However, the real-time application of VLMs is hindered by high latency and computational overhead, limiting their effectiveness in time-critical driving scenarios. This challenge is particularly evident when VLMs exhibit over-inference—continuing to process unnecessary layers even after confident predictions have been reached. To address this inefficiency, we propose AD-EE, an Early Exit framework incorporates domain characteristics of autonomous driving and leverages causal inference to identify optimal exit layers. We evaluate our method on large-scale real-world autonomous driving datasets, including Waymo and the corner-case-focused CODA, as well as on a real vehicle running the Autoware Universe platform. Extensive experiments across multiple VLMs show that our method significantly reduces latency, with maximum improvements reaching up to 57.58%, and enhances object detection accuracy, with maximum gains of up to 44%.

•

We conduct extensive experiments on real autonomous vehicles, benchmarking against traditional full-layer inference. Our results demonstrate significant gains in both efficiency and accuracy, substantiating the practical value of AD-EE enhanced VLMs in real driving conditions.

Related Work

2.1
Vision-Language Models in Autonomous Driving

The integration of Vision-Language Models (VLMs) in autonomous driving has garnered increasing attention for their ability to bridge visual perception and linguistic understanding. Recent advancements, such as DriveVLM-Dual
Tian et al. 2024
, have demonstrated how VLMs can assist smaller models in decision-making by enhancing contextual awareness and semantic interpretation. Furthermore, Talk2BEV
Choudhary et al. 2024
extends VLM capabilities to Bird’s Eye View (BEV) perception, enabling richer environmental understanding through multi-modal learning. Text-to-Drive
Nguyen et al. 2024
further explores VLM-driven planning strategies, showcasing how natural language instructions can dynamically adjust vehicle navigation and obstacle avoidance. Despite these advancements, deploying VLMs in real-time driving remains challenging due to the substantial computational cost and inference latency inherent in large-scale models. This latency bottleneck severely constrains their application in safety-critical tasks such as dynamic object detection and path planning, where timely responses are imperative.

2.2
Causal Inference

Causal inference is a key technique for understanding cause-and-effect relationships in observational and experimental data, widely applied in fields like healthcare, finance, and public policy
Glass et al. 2013
;
Atanasov and Black 2016
;
Imbens and Rubin 2015
. It primarily relies on two frameworks: the Structural Causal Model (SCM), which uses directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) to map causal relationships, and the Potential Outcome Framework, which focuses on treatment effects for counterfactual analysis
Pearl 2009
;
Winship and Morgan 1999
. Recent advancements in Large Language Models (LLMs) have enabled new applications of causal inference, especially in handling unstructured, high-dimensional data
Feder et al. 2022
;
Kiciman et al. 2023
. Models like GPT-4, LLaMA, and Claude 2 have demonstrated capabilities in causal reasoning, making causal inference a powerful tool for identifying layer-wise impacts in VLM predictions
OpenAI 2023
;
Touvron et al. 2023
;
Anthropic 2023
.

2.3
Early Exit Mechanisms

Early exiting techniques optimize model inference by dynamically reducing processed layers, allowing models to halt inference once confident predictions are made
Liu et al. 2020
;
Zhu 2021
;
Fan et al. 2024
. These methods fall into three categories: training-based, which jointly train classifiers and backbones at high computational costs
Zhu 2021
;
Zhou et al. 2020
;
Zhu et al. 2023
; semi-training-based, which update only classifiers, sacrificing accuracy for reduced costs
Zhu et al. 2023
; and training-free, which use heuristics for fast inference but lack generalization
Sun et al. 2022
;
Schuster et al. 2022
;
Bae et al. 2023
. Their application to accelerate VLMs in autonomous driving is detailed in the next section.

3
Methodology

3.1
Motivation

Figure 3:
VLMs and AD Task Models collaborate or operate independently to process autonomous driving tasks, including perception, planning, and decision-making.

Large and small models either collaborate or independently participate in perception, planning, and decision-making within autonomous driving modules (as illustrated in Figure
3
). However, the deep multi-layer processing of VLMs introduces significant latency issues. Existing acceleration techniques, such as Mixture of Experts (MoE)
Shazeer et al. 2017
and parameter reduction methods
Dehghani et al. 2018
, optimize model size and component activation but overlook a critical problem in Transformer architectures of over-inference. This phenomenon refers to unnecessary deep-layer processing even when reliable predictions are already achievable at earlier stages
Chen et al. 2024
, leading to latency and inefficiency. Given that tasks like 2D perception in autonomous driving often involve identifying simple objects, the most effective solution is Early Exit, which halts inference at the optimal layer once sufficient confidence is achieved. Furthermore, Early Exit can seamlessly integrate with existing VLM acceleration methods, making it a crucial step towards real-time, efficient VLM-driven autonomous driving.

To optimize the integration of Early Exit in VLMs for autonomous driving, it is crucial not only to identify the optimal exit layer but also to validate its effectiveness in improving latency and accuracy. Traditional approaches often rely on heuristics or confidence-based measures, which lack theoretical guarantees of optimality. To address this, we introduce a Causal Inference-based framework to discover the most effective early exit points. Causal Inference enables us to understand the contributions of different layers in VLMs from a causal perspective, rather than merely relying on correlation analysis. This allows for more reliable identification of the optimal exit layers, maximizing inference efficiency and performance.

3.2
Problem Formulation

Our primary objective is to maximize accuracy and minimize latency during the inference process of Vision-Language Models (VLMs). This optimization can be represented as follows:

\max_{L}\operatorname{Acc}(L),{Lat}(L_{min})=F_{\text{VLM}}(\Omega(L^{*}))

(1)

In this formulation,
\operatorname{Acc}(L)
represents the accuracy at layer
L
,
\operatorname{Lat}(L)
is the latency at layer
L
,
F_{\text{VLM}}(\Omega(L^{*}))
denotes the VLM’s output performance at the optimal exit layer
L^{*}
.
We introduce
\Omega(\cdot)
to represent the Early Exit mechanism, indicating a dynamic exit strategy at a specified layer.
The optimal layer
L^{*}
is which explores the difference between the full model
L_{\text{full}}
and each candidate layer
L_{x}
, ensuring that the selected layer is not only optimal in terms of performance but also causally valid.

3.3
Early Exit in Autonomous Driving

Figure 4:
Early Exit in VLMs

The Early Exit Mechanism, represented by
\Omega(\cdot)
, is designed to dynamically determine the optimal layer
L^{*}
for termination during the inference process. Unlike conventional full-layer inference,
\Omega(\cdot)
enables the model to exit early based on a predefined matching mechanism, significantly reducing latency while preserving accuracy.

Given a Vision-Language Model (VLM) with
N
layers, we denote the representation at layer
L
as
h_{L}
. The early exit decision is formulated as a matching condition that evaluates the alignment of the current layer’s representation with a target threshold:

\Omega(L)=\begin{cases}\text{Exit at }L&\text{if }M(h_{L},T)\\
\text{Continue to }L+1&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(2)

where
M(h_{L},T)
is a matching function that evaluates whether the current layer is the exit layer. As illustrated in Figure 4,
M
denotes the matching process
M(h_{L},T)
. If the matching succeeds, an early exit is triggered; otherwise, the prediction continues to the next layer until the final layer is reached.

Figure 5:
VLM Prompt for autonomous driving

In this study, we focus on the 2D detection task in autonomous driving as the primary evaluation scenario. To effectively implement early exit mechanisms in VLMs, prompt engineering plays a crucial role. As illustrated in Figure
5
, we design prompts based on the three major categories from the Waymo dataset and the seven categories from the CODA dataset, enabling VLMs to predictively identify objects and facilitate early exits. Furthermore, the distinction between static and dynamic objects, which is unique to autonomous driving, is critical for downstream modules such as planning and decision-making. This classification lays the foundation for subsequent tasks, ensuring that object detection results are accurately interpreted and acted upon by the autonomous system.

3.4
Causal Inference-Based Method for Optimal Exit Layer Selection

Figure 6:
Casual Inference

To identify the optimal early exit layer in Vision-Language Models (VLMs) integrated with autonomous driving tasks, we draw inspiration from ROME
Meng et al. 2022
and Causal Inference principles. We use a three-stage framework—Clean Run, Corrupted Run, and Restoration and Explore Run—to progressively search for the optimal exit layer, as show in Fig
6
. Unlike traditional step-by-step layer-wise testing, our Causal Inference-based method enables deeper exploration into which specific layer or combination of layers can achieve the best performance. Moreover, the integration of causal inference metrics, and the Average Treatment Effect (ATE) provides robust quantitative evidence to justify the choice of the optimal exit layer, enhancing both latency reduction and classification accuracy.

3.4.1
Clean Run

In the Clean Run, the VLM performs a complete forward pass through all layers
L_{\text{full}}
to establish the baseline accuracy:

Acc_{\text{clean}}=f(x,L_{\text{full}})

(3)

where
x
is the input and
L_{\text{full}}
represents the full set of layers in the VLM.

3.4.2
Corrupted Run

In the Corrupted Run, we progressively remove layers from the final layer towards the beginning:

Acc_{\text{corrupted}}=f(x,L_{\text{full}}-L_{x})

(4)

where
L_{x}
represents the subset of layers removed at step
x
. If the
Acc_{\text{corrupted}}
score drops, it indicates that the current layer is not suitable for early exit. Conversely, if the score exceeds the
Acc_{\text{clean}}
value, it suggests that the current layer is a viable exit point. Since we progressively remove layers from the full model, any valid exit found at step x inherently implies a latency reduction.

3.4.3
Restoration and Explore Run

Once the optimal corrupted layer
L^{*}
is identified, the Restoration Run begins. This stage attempts:

•

Layer Restoration: Adding layers back from
L^{*}+1,L^{*}+2,\dots
to restore the VLM to a clean state, eliminating other potential factors affecting VLM inference accuracy, thereby confirming that the accuracy improvement originates from the exit layer.

•

Layer Explore: Further removing layers from
L^{*}-1,L^{*}-2,\dots
to explore if a more optimal exit layer.
regained.

The optimal configuration is expressed as:

L^{*}=\operatorname*{arg\,max}\operatorname{Acc}(f(x,L_{x}-L_{\text{explore}}),\\
where\operatorname{Acc}(f(x,L_{x}+L_{\text{res}}))=Acc_{\text{clean}}

(5)

where The formula identifies the optimal exit layer
L^{*}
by maximizing accuracy under layer exploration, while ensuring that restoring removed layers recovers the original clean accuracy.

Experiment

We evaluated the proposed Early Exit VLM method on real-world autonomous driving datasets CODA
Li et al. 2022
and WAYMO
Sun et al. 2020
, assessing its improvements in accuracy and latency reduction. Additionally, the method was further tested in real-vehicle environments.

4.1
Implementation Details

Datasets:
Our experiments are conducted on two large-scale autonomous driving datasets: WAYMO and CODA. The WAYMO dataset serves as a standard benchmark for autonomous driving perception, covering typical driving scenarios. We extract 14,000 front-camera images from the training set, including 11,031 vehicles, 5,845 person, and 1,325 bike, ensuring representative evaluation. In contrast, the CODA dataset focuses on critical edge cases, allowing rigorous assessment of Vision-Language Models (VLMs) under challenging conditions.

Task:
The experiment consists of three main components: (1) recognition of the three common classes (bike, person, and vehicle) from the WAYMO dataset, representing typical scenarios in autonomous driving; (2) classification of the seven major categories in the CODA dataset, showcasing the VLM’s capability to handle corner cases in autonomous driving; and (3) recognition of both static (Traffic Facility, Obstruction, Misc) and dynamic (Pedestrian, Cyclist, Vehicle, Animal) classes from CODA. The distinction between static and dynamic objects is crucial in autonomous driving, as it directly influences subsequent planning and decision-making processes.

Experiment Setup:
All datasets adopt an 8:1 split for classification tasks. For example, in the WAYMO dataset, among the 5,845 person images, 5,195 are used for identifying the optimal exit layer, while the remaining 650 are reserved for evaluating accuracy and latency. All experiments were conducted on a machine equipped with an NVIDIA A100-SXM4-80GB GPU, an AMD EPYC 7763 64-core processor (32 vCPUs), and 240GB of RAM.

VLM Setting:
We employ three Vision-Language Models (VLMs) for our experiments: DeepSeek-VL2-Tiny, DeepSeek-VL2-Small, and LLaVA-v1.6-Vicuna-7B-HF. DeepSeek-VL2-Tiny and DeepSeek-VL2-Small are both VLMs based on the LLaMA-2 architecture, designed to efficiently process visual and textual inputs with varying levels of model capacity. LLaVA-v1.6-Vicuna-7B-HF, on the other hand, is built upon the LLaMA-1 framework, integrating vision-language understanding through advanced multi-modal fusion techniques. We select DeepSeek and LLaVA because they represent the most advanced and effective open-source VLMs currently available.

4.3
Early Exit Result

We evaluate the performance of our method (denoted with AD-EE) against the baselines (DS-Tiny, DS-Small, and LLaVA-7B) on the CODA dataset and Waymo dataset, focusing on Accuracy and estimated Latency Improvement.

4.3.1
CODA dataset

Static and Dynamic Recognition:
The experiment results in Table
2
show that applying AD-EE consistently enhances the average accuracy (Avg) and reduces latency across all evaluated models. Specifically, DS-Tiny achieves a minor improvement from 43.50 to 44.77, while DS-Small improves significantly from 61.88 to 84.14, and LLaVA-7B jumps from 50.60 to 94.17. This indicates that larger models with deeper layers benefit more from AD-EE, as it allows them to exit at optimal points without processing all layers unnecessarily. A particularly notable improvement is observed in LLaVA-7B, where full inference fails to recognize Traffic Facility, Obstruction, and Misc (0% accuracy for all). However, with Early Exit, these categories reach 100% accuracy, suggesting that AD-EE has a correction capability, enabling the model to produce valid predictions even when full-layer inference entirely misses these static objects. Additionally, DS-Small
AD-EE
demonstrates substantial gains for Pedestrian, Cyclist, Vehicle, and Animal, significantly outperforming its full-inference counterpart. This improvement underscores the effectiveness of Early Exit for dynamic object recognition, enabling the model to capture motion-related features more accurately with fewer processing steps. Due to the early exit of layers, our AD-EE significantly reduces latency, especially in LLaVA-7B, where the largest reduction in layer processing leads to the most substantial latency improvement.

Conclusion

We proposed Causal Inference-driven Early Exiting to optimize Vision-Language Models (VLMs) for real-time autonomous driving. Our method efficiently identifies optimal exit layers, significantly reducing latency and enhancing accuracy. Experiments on Waymo, CODA, and real-vehicle tests validate its effectiveness in complex scenarios. Our findings demonstrate that our method is a crucial step toward efficient and scalable VLM deployment in autonomous driving.

6
Limitations

This work primarily evaluates the application of VLMs for object recognition and anomaly detection in autonomous driving scenarios using a real-vehicle setup. However, due to hardware limitations during real-world testing, we were unable to comprehensively assess multiple VLM models under identical conditions. In reality, many modern autonomous vehicles are equipped with powerful computing capabilities that can support more extensive model evaluations. Furthermore, although we have tested several prompt designs and selected three specific prompts based on early exit strategies and the unique characteristics of autonomous driving, the exploration of prompt diversity remains incomplete. With the advent of techniques like Chain-of-Thought prompting, the impact of different prompt designs on model performance could be significantly more substantial than currently observed. Future work will explore broader prompt variations to fully unlock VLM’s potential in early exit for complex driving scenarios.
