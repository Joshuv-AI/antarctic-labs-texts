Bleeding complications following intramuscular injections among hospitalized anticoagulated patients: a retrospective observational comparative study
Authors: Marina Davidov, Ronen Loebstein, Hagith Yonath, Itai Gueta
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBleeding complications following intramuscular (IM) injections are generally considered rare with reported incidence of 0.06%. However, evidence on safety of IM injections among anticoagulated hospitalized patients is lacking. The objective of the current study was to examine the incidence of injection site bleeding complications following IM injection among anticoagulated hospitalized patients.MethodsA retrospective comparative study comprised of all hospitalized patients ≥ 18 years old that were treated with ≥ 1 IM injection between 2009 and 2019 in a large tertiary medical center. Bleeding complications were defined as focal hematoma, local bleeding, intramuscular bleeding or compartment syndrome. Each case with IM injection was searched for ICD9 codes (e.g., hematoma, hemorrhage or compartment syndrome) and for indirect evidence suggestive of potential bleeding: hemoglobin drop ≥ 2 g/dl, AST or CPK increase, packed red blood cell transfusion, or abrupt cessation of the anticoagulation. These case were then verified for true injection-site bleeding by natural language processing model and manual review of the electronic medical record.ResultsA total of 71,710 patients were treated with 236,406 IM injections. Mean age 53 (± 22) and 63% were females. Concomitant anticoagulation (Heparins: 90.3%, warfarin: 6.8% and DOACs: 4.7%) occurred in 40,819 IM injections (8189 patients). Suspected bleeding complications at the IM injection site were identified among 7,111 patients following 23,089 IM injections, the majority were unrelated to the IM injection-site (e.g., gastrointestinal bleeding, retroperitoneal, etc.). Two cases were verified as true injection site bleeding complication, both in the anticoagulated group (2/8189, 0.02%).ConclusionBleeding complications at site of IM injections among anticoagulated hospitalized patients are rare, and their risk is probably not higher compared to patients without anticoagulation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12959-024-00662-z.

Background

Intramuscular (IM) injections are frequently administered to hospitalized patients for various indications, and are considered convenient and relatively safe. However, rare complications with potential significant clinical consequences seldom occur including abscess formation, intramuscular hematoma, compartment syndrome, nerve injury and embolisms [1–3]. Greenblatt et al. reported injection-site complications among 48/12,134 (0.4%) hospitalized subjects, of them only 6 and 2 patients suffered from hematoma and bleeding, respectively [3].

The risk for injection-site hematoma or intramuscular bleeding following IM administration in the presence of anticoagulation may be theoretically higher, but the evidence is limited to small cohorts of subjects treated with warfarin in outpatient settings [4]. Given the rarity of such events, larger population-based studies are necessary to determine their incidence [4–7].

In light of the limited evidence assessing these complications, the objective of the current study was to examine the incidence of bleeding complications following intramuscular injections in a large cohort of hospitalized patients.

Methods

Study design and cohort definition

We conducted a retrospective comparative study comprised of all hospitalized patient at the Chaim Sheba Medical Center, a large tertiary medical center in Israel, that were treated with an intramuscular injection between 2009 and 2019. Inclusion criteria were age ≥ 18 years old, IM injection of any preparation, and hospital admission for at least 24 h. When a patient received more than one IM injection, we included the first 15 administrations. The study was approved by the institution ethical board (SMC), which waived the requirement of inform consent.

Outcomes definition, covariates and data extraction

Outcomes were defined as injection-site bleeding complications that could be attributed to an IM injection, including focal hematoma, local bleeding, intramuscular bleeding or compartment syndrome. Cases were screened for suspected injection-site complications by identifying (1) ICD-9 codes for either hematoma, hemorrhage or compartment syndrome, and (2) by indirect evidence (i.e., "triggers") suggestive of potential bleeding, muscle injury or an adverse event related to anticoagulation: hemoglobin drop ≥ 2 g/dl during the 3 days following each IM injection, transfusion of packed red blood cells, AST or CPK increase to above × 3 ULN, or by abrupt cessation of the anticoagulation (≤ 24 h following the IM injection) [8, 9]. Since these triggers do not necessarily mean that a real bleeding complication did occur, the electronic medical record (EMR) of each case with a trigger was searched for free-text phrases associated with frank bleeding: This was conducted by employing a natural language process (NLP) model followed by manual review of the daily follow-up for up to 5 days following each IM injection (Fig. 1). Data collected for each IM injection included the administered drug and dose, patient’s demographics, comorbidities associated with bleeding (hypertension, diabetes mellitus, liver cirrhosis, platelet disorders, hemophilia, etc.), relevant medications (inhibitors of platelet aggregation, anticoagulant agents, phosphodiesterase inhibitors, SSRI/SNRIs, corticosteroids), laboratory values (hemoglobin, platelets count, urea concentration, PT, PTT, INR, AST, CPK), as well as date of admission and discharge. Patients were considered to be anticoagulated if they were exposed to LMWH, DOACs or warfarin during ≤ 12 h, ≤ 24 h and ≤ 2 days prior to the IM injection, respectively. Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR) as appropriate for continuous variables and proportions for categorical variables. Missing variables were excluded from the analysis. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 25 IBM ®, SPSS ® Inc).

Results

Overall, 236,406 IM injections were administered to 71,170 hospitalized patients between 2009 and 2019. Mean age 53 (± 22) and 44,740 (63%) were females. Hypertension, diabetes mellitus and atrial fibrillation were present among 18,151 (25.5%), 9693 (13.6%) and 7138 (10%) patients, respectively. Additional baseline characteristics are presented in Table 1. Patients received a median of 3 (IQR: 1–6) IM injections. Most prevalent IM preparations included metoclopramide (13.1%), diclofenac (10.8%) and pethidine (10.7%). Of 236,406 injections, 41,514 (17.6%) were while on antiaggregant agents. A total of 40,819 (17.2%) injections occurred among 8189 (11.5%) anticoagulated patients: Low molecular weight heparin (90.3%), warfarin (6.8%) and DOACs (4.7%). The anticoagulated group was older (65 vs. 51 years) and showed higher prevalence of patients with hypertension (43% vs. 23%) and diabetes mellitus (24% vs. 12%). Additional information on the type of IM medication, dosage and anticoagulation agents is available in Table S1-S3, supporting material. Platelet count did not differ between groups and median INR among warfarin treated patients was 2 (IQR: 1.5–2.6). Baselines characteristics

Suspected bleeding complications at the IM injection site were identified among 7,111 patients following 23,089 IM injections: the majority were triggered by packed RBC transfusion (10,817 injections), followed by AST/CPK rise > 3 ULN (7283 injection), abrupt cessation of the anticoagulant agent (2596 injections), and hemoglobin drop ≥ 2 g/l (744 cases). Employing the NLP model on the daily follow-up of those cases yielded 3,921 bleeding events among 1,895 patients, of which the majority were unrelated to the IM injection-site (e.g., gastrointestinal bleeding, retroperitoneal bleeding, etc.) but 2 (0.024%); both among anticoagulated patients (Fig. 1 and Table 2). Characteristics of patients with bleeding complication at IM injection site

The first bleeding event occurred in a 32 years old female following daily IM diclofenac indicated for pain relief 6 days after urgent cesarean section and was treated with prophylactic LMWH injections. Following the 5th IM diclofenac injection, she complained on right buttock pain at the site of injection. The treating physician noticed a small hematoma and focal stiffness. The LMWH was temporarily stopped and no additional intervention was required.

The second case occurred in a 91 years old female hospitalized at the rehabilitation center due to right hemiparesis following a stroke. She was treated with twice daily LMWH (indicated for atrial fibrillation), aspirin, venlafaxine and IM metoclopramide injections. Bleeding occurred following a second IM injection and was speculated to involve an arteriole. Topical tranexamic acid and pressure bandage were introduced and the bleeding ceased.

Discussion

In this large retrospective comparative study, the incidence of bleeding complications at IM injection site among anticoagulated patients was 0.02% (2/8189), while no cases were identified among patients not treated with anticoagulation.

Previous observational studies have attempted to evaluate the incidence of bleeding complications following IM vaccination in primary care setting, mostly with relatively small sample size: Casajuna et al. prospectively compared safety outcomes among 229 VKA treated patients, of whom 129 received IM vaccination and 100 SC vaccination, and observed no significant difference between groups [10]. Similar findings were also reported in additional small-scale studies which were recently reviewed and published as a meta-analysis [4, 10–14]. Although no events of major bleeding were reported, the rarity of these events requires larger cohorts [7]. Contrary, Gendron N. et al. have found bleeding complications in 0.6% of 358 anticoagulated subjects who received 561 IM covid19 vaccine [15]. Our study, comprised of hospitalized patients, found 2 bleeding events following 40,819 IM injections among 8,189 anticoagulated patients: One while on prophylactic enoxaparin and the other on therapeutic regimen; both cases were identified by the abrupt cessation of the anticoagulation treatment. Difference between findings may probably be explained by the differences in studies population and settings including injection-site (deltoid vs. gluteus), injected volume, vaccine adjuvants, patient mobility and staff experience.

Notably, we did not identify any clinically relevant bleeding among patients without anticoagulation: in many subjects the IM injections were accompanied by elevated AST/CPK serum concentration or by hemoglobin drop ≥ 2 g/dl, factors suggestive of muscle damage and bleeding; these however were actually associated with bleeding not related to the IM injection itself. Hence, while it seems that no clinically relevant bleeding complications (as defined by the ISTH) occurred, we cannot exclude the possibility that minor hematomas that did not draw the physician or nurse’s attention, nor been associated with muscle damage, did occur. Greenblatt et al. reported 8 (0.06%) bleeding events following IM injections among 12,134 hospitalized patients not exposed to anticoagulants [3]. Given the increased bleeding risk inherently present among anticoagulated patients, these findings suggest that the observed incidence in the current study is probably not higher than that among subjects not on anticoagulation therapy.

Several limitations must be mentioned: given the study retrospective nature, we cannot exclude the presence of a potential treatment bias, in which patients at very high bleeding risk were not treated with IM injections. Additionally, missing values following each IM injection (e.g., CPK, AST, etc.) were not available for all patients which may result in missing cases with potential bleeding complication. However, to overcome this we searched for several indirect evidence (i.e., triggers) for each case. We were not able to affirm whether LMWH was indicated for prophylactic or therapeutic use. Yet, among 37% of LMWH cases, last enoxaparin dose was 60 mg or higher, a range commonly associated with therapeutic indications. Also, differences in staff and patients characteristics, generalization to the outpatient setting should be treated with caution.

Conclusion

Among hospitalized patients treated with anticoagulation, the incidence of bleeding complications at the IM injection site is very rare, and their risk is probably not higher compared to patients without anticoagulation.
