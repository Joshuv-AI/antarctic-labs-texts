Multidisciplinary Practice Patterns and Barriers to Preoperative Radiotherapy for Soft Tissue Sarcoma: A National Multicenter Survey
Authors: Yuan Qu, Jishi Li, Defu Liu, Keng Chen, Weijie Wen, Jijie Huang, Jun Dong, Ping an Wu, Anne W M Lee, Brian O’Sullivan, Zusheng He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPreoperative radiotherapy (pre-opRT) is an established component of multimodal treatment for soft tissue sarcoma (STS), offering advantages in margin control and long-term functional outcomes. Its integration into clinical practice depends on effective multidisciplinary decision-making between surgical and radiation oncology teams. We conducted a national multicenter survey to evaluate practice patterns, multidisciplinary coordination, and barriers to pre-opRT implementation in China.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted across 25 provinces, including 123 clinicians (67 radiation oncologists and 56 surgeons). The questionnaire assessed institutional multidisciplinary team (MDT) structure, pre-opRT utilization, and perceived barriers. Associations between MDT structure and pre-opRT use were explored.ResultsPre-opRT utilization was low: 30% of clinicians reported no use, and over 60% applied pre-opRT in fewer than 10% of eligible patients. Surgeons identified postoperative wound complications as the primary concern, while non-core surgeons frequently reported limited access to sarcoma-specialized radiation oncologists. Radiation oncologists highlighted difficulty achieving cross-disciplinary agreement. Clinicians practicing within structured MDT settings were significantly more likely to report higher pre-opRT utilization (P = 0.019). Despite limited adoption, more than 70% of respondents recognized the clinical value of pre-opRT.ConclusionThe use of pre-opRT for STS is shaped not only by clinical considerations but also by the effectiveness of multidisciplinary coordination. Strengthening MDT structures, addressing surgical concerns regarding wound complications, and improving cross-specialty communication may facilitate more consistent integration of pre-opRT into sarcoma care.

Introduction

Soft tissue sarcomas (STS) are rare malignant tumors comprising approximately 1% of all adult cancers,1 most commonly arising in the extremities. For high-grade or large tumors, multimodal treatment incorporating surgery and radiotherapy is standard practice. Preoperative radiotherapy (pre-opRT) has gained increasing attention because of several advantages over postoperative radiotherapy (PORT). Evidence across retrospective analyses, prospective studies, and randomized trials has shown that pre-opRT improves the likelihood of achieving R0 resection,2,3 enhances margin quality,4 provides prognostic information through pathological complete response assessment,5,6 and achieves excellent local control comparable to PORT.7 Importantly, pre-opRT significantly reduces long-term complications such as fibrosis, joint stiffness, and edema.8–11 These concepts have been embraced by international groups including the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) which has expressed a preference for pre-operative over post-operative radiotherapy.12

Additional clinical and practical benefits of pre-opRT include improved local control after unplanned excisions,13,14 higher patient adherence,15,16 and greater reproducibility in target delineation.17 Together with emerging radiobiological and clinical evidence, these advantages have supported the development and adoption of hypofractionated pre-opRT regimens,18–21 such as 42.75 Gy in 15 fractions, which achieve a 4-year local recurrence-free survival of 93%.19 Myxoid liposarcoma, a highly radiosensitive histologic subtype, demonstrates excellent outcomes with even lower doses (eg, 36 Gy in 18 fractions).22–27

While pre-opRT is associated with a higher risk of postoperative wound complications,7,28 and therefore requires careful patient selection, international practice patterns indicate that it remains the preferred sequencing strategy in many high-volume sarcoma centers. In a French cohort, 78% of extremity STS cases received pre-opRT,16 a German series reported a rate of 72%,29 and analysis of the US National Cancer Data Base showed an increase in pre-opRT utilization from 16.8% to 29.7% between 2004 and 2012.30 An international survey likewise found that clinicians treating more than 20 sarcoma cases per year used pre-opRT twice as often as PORT.31 Utilization is notably high in histologic subtypes known for their radiosensitivity: in a 40-year cohort from the University of Florida, 69% of patients with synovial sarcoma treated with combined-modality therapy received pre-opRT,32 and in a 2008 series of myxoid liposarcoma, pre-opRT accounted for 46% of all radiotherapy cases.33 These findings illustrate a consistent global trend favoring pre-opRT when tumor biology, clinical expertise, and institutional resources align.

Although pre-opRT has been recognized in Chinese expert consensus and guideline documents as an evidence-based treatment option for selected patients with STS,34 upfront surgery remains the dominant treatment paradigm in real-world clinical practice in mainland China. Newly diagnosed patients are typically first evaluated by surgical teams, which therefore play a central role in treatment sequencing decisions. Within this workflow, PORT may be more readily integrated into existing clinical pathways, whereas pre-opRT requires earlier engagement of radiation oncologists and coordinated multidisciplinary planning prior to surgery including attention to specific requirements such as specialty-based pathology evaluations and imaging prior to surgery and early discussions concerning long term functional outcome of the patient. Variations in institutional resources, radiotherapy expertise, and MDT infrastructure may further contribute to differences between Chinese real-world practice and international recommendations regarding treatment sequencing.

To better understand these practice patterns and explore factors associated with pre-opRT implementation, we conducted a nationwide multidisciplinary survey to characterize clinician perceptions, report practice patterns, and understand perceived barriers related to pre-opRT use in China.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Sampling

A national, cross-sectional survey was conducted using purposive sampling to target clinicians involved in STS management. Radiation oncologists with experience treating sarcoma were identified through professional networks and invited to participate. Each radiation oncologist was asked to recruit a collaborating surgeon who manages STS cases (orthopedic or general surgery). All respondents completed the survey independently, and surgeon and radiation oncologist responses were not paired or intended to represent joint opinions. The study aimed to obtain at least 50 surgeons and 50 radiation oncologists.

Questionnaire Development

The questionnaire was developed through literature review and multidisciplinary discussion among clinicians involved in sarcoma care, including radiation oncologists and surgeons. Prior to national distribution, internal pilot testing was conducted to assess item clarity, feasibility, and interpretability, and minor revisions were made accordingly. The questionnaire used in this study is available in the original Chinese (Supplementary Appendix 1) or English translation (Supplementary Appendix 2).

Survey Instrument

This survey focused on clinicians involved in the management of trunk and limb soft tissue sarcoma, as these subsites represent the primary population for which preoperative radiotherapy is generally considered among international groups. Responses were collected anonymously, and the questionnaire was approved by the Ethics Committee of The University of Hong Kong-Shenzhen Hospital.

Data Collection

The survey was conducted in two phases. In Phase I, radiation oncologists specializing in STS were invited through professional networks. Invitations were distributed to radiation oncologists at 60 tertiary general hospitals or cancer centers across 25 provinces (including autonomous regions and municipalities) in mainland China. Among the initial 91 respondents, 57 were radiation oncologists and 34 were surgeons, corresponding to a hospital-level response rate of 96.4% and an estimated surgeon response rate of approximately 50%.

To improve regional and disciplinary representation, Phase II expanded the sampling frame to 10 additional hospitals. During this phase, targeted invitations were directed specifically to STS surgeons to increase surgical participation. To maintain balanced specialty representation, surgeons in Phase II were not asked to recruit additional radiation oncologists; this approach prevented disproportionate over-representation of a single specialty and preserved the intended multidisciplinary composition of the sample.

Across both phases, a total of 123 valid responses were collected, comprising 67 radiation oncologists and 56 surgeons.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize survey results, including distributions of proportions and response frequencies. Subgroup comparisons among core STS surgeons, non-core surgeons, and radiation oncologists were primarily descriptive. As an exploratory, hypothesis-generating survey, the analyses were intended primarily to characterize practice patterns and identify potential associations for future investigation rather than to establish definitive effect estimates or causal relationships.

To explore the association between MDT structure and pre-opRT utilization, MDT structure was dichotomized as regular MDT meetings versus case-by-case discussion or no formal MDT. Pre-opRT utilization was categorized as <10% versus ≥10% of eligible patients. The association between MDT structure and pre-opRT utilization was assessed using the chi-square test.

Statistical analyses were conducted for exploratory purposes and were not intended to support definitive causal inference. A two-sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant for this predefined analysis.

Characteristics, Preoperative Radiotherapy Utilization, MDT Practices, and Reported Barriers Among Core and Non-Core STS Surgeons and Radiation Oncologists (N = 123)

Clinician Characteristics and Case Volume

A total of 123 clinicians participated (67 radiation oncologists and 56 surgeons), all of whom treated trunk or limb soft tissue sarcoma within their respective practices. Core STS surgeons had the highest case volume, with 67.7% managing more than 20 cases annually, compared with 8.0% of non-core surgeons and 20.9% of radiation oncologists. Non-core surgeons more often reported very low case volume, with 36.0% treating fewer than five cases. Most respondents practiced in tertiary general hospitals; core STS surgeons were more frequently affiliated with dedicated cancer centers, reflecting Phase I recruitment of institutions with established sarcoma expertise.

Utilization of Preoperative Radiotherapy

Pre-opRT utilization was low across all clinician groups. Approximately one-third of respondents (32.8%) reported no cases managed by pre-opRT in the past year, including similar proportions of core surgeons (32.3%), non-core surgeons (40.0%), and radiation oncologists (32.8%). Among those who had used pre-opRT, most applied it to fewer than 10% of eligible patients, with this pattern most frequently reported among non-core surgeons (73.3%). In contrast, core surgeons demonstrated greater cumulative exposure to pre-opRT, with a higher proportion reporting 6–10 or >10 pre-opRT cases managed in the past year.

MDT Operation and Multidisciplinary Practices

Regular MDT meetings were most frequently reported by core STS surgeons (54.8%), followed by non-core surgeons (28.0%) and radiation oncologists (23.9%). Non-core surgeons had the highest proportion reporting no formal MDT active at their institution (20.0%). Radiation oncologists most frequently described case-by-case (on-demand) MDT discussions (67.2%).

Association Between MDT Structure and Pre-opRT Utilization

Clinicians practicing in settings with regular MDT meetings were more likely to report higher utilization of preoperative radiotherapy (≥10% of eligible cases) than those working within limited or absent MDT frameworks (Table 2). Barriers to Implementing Preoperative Radiotherapy

Barriers Among Clinicians with Zero or <10% Pre-opRT Use

Non-core surgeons predominantly cited limited access to STS-specialized radiation oncologists (60.0%), while core surgeons emphasized concerns about postoperative wound complications (48.4%). Radiation oncologists reported coordination and communication challenges, reflecting difficulties integrating pre-opRT into existing MDT workflows. Approximately one-third of respondents reported uncertainty about the incremental advantage of pre-opRT versus PORT, indicating a substantial knowledge gap that may influence sequencing decisions.

Additional Clinical Challenges Among Higher Pre-opRT Users

Among clinicians reporting ≥10% pre-opRT utilization, the leading challenge for core surgeons concerned potential management of postoperative wound complications (58.1%). Non-core surgeons reported similar concerns but also highlighted the burden of achieving cross-disciplinary agreement within the MDT (40.0%), a barrier also reported most frequently by radiation oncologists (58.2%). Challenges with patient adherence and concerns regarding radiologic response or functional outcomes were reported at comparable frequencies across clinician groups, suggesting that even clinicians with relatively higher utilization of pre-opRT continue to face practical and multidisciplinary obstacles during implementation.

Perceptions About Pre-opRT Value

Despite low utilization, 83.6% of clinicians believed pre-opRT provides meaningful advantages and should be considered first-line therapy in selected patients. More than 70% agreed that pre-opRT holds promising prospects for wider adoption in China.

Discussion

This national survey provides the first comprehensive assessment of clinician perceptions and multidisciplinary barriers to pre-opRT for STS in China. Although international evidence strongly supports pre-opRT as a key component of multimodal STS management,35–37 including its preferred choice for adjuvant RT by the NCCN,12 its adoption in China remains limited. Nearly 30% of clinicians had never used pre-opRT, and almost 60% used it in fewer than 10% of eligible patients. Even among core STS surgeons with substantial annual case volumes, utilization remained low, underscoring a substantial evidence–practice gap.

The barriers identified in this study reveal distinct specialty-specific concerns. Surgeons predominantly emphasized postoperative wound complications as the major deterrent—an issue consistently reported in the literature,7,28 yet often weighted more heavily than justified by current evidence demonstrating significantly lower long-term toxicity with pre-opRT.8–11 The concern surrounding wound complications may reflect not only clinical considerations but also the asymmetry between immediate and visible postoperative morbidity and the longer-term functional advantages associated with pre-opRT, which are no less important but may be less apparent during routine treatment planning discussions. Additional postoperative interventions, prolonged recovery, increased communication requirements, and the potential impact on patient satisfaction may collectively increase the perceived implementation burden associated with pre-opRT pathways.

Importantly, our data provide quantitative support for the role of MDT structure in shaping pre-opRT utilization. Regular, structured MDT meetings were reported predominantly by core STS surgeons, whereas non-core surgeons more frequently described the absence of a formal MDT structure, and radiation oncologists most often relied on case-by-case (on-demand) discussions. Clinicians practicing in settings with regular MDT meetings were significantly more likely to report higher use of pre-opRT than those relying on case-by-case discussions or lacking a formal MDT structure.

Although MDT structure alone is unlikely to fully explain the implementation gap, this finding reinforces the concept that structured MDT processes may facilitate the routine consideration of pre-opRT in clinical practice. Beyond technical expertise, regular MDT engagement appears to create an environment in which treatment sequencing decisions, such as the use of pre-opRT, are more consistently discussed and aligned.

This interpretation is further supported by our finding that radiation oncologists frequently reported difficulty achieving cross-disciplinary agreement within MDT workflows as a major barrier to pre-opRT implementation. Notably, our findings further suggest that pre-opRT implementation is not simply a function of institutional size, case volume, or access to radiotherapy equipment. Even among clinicians practicing in high-volume referral centers and institutions with established MDT structures, utilization remained low. This observation suggests that organizational culture, workflow integration, and the efficiency of multidisciplinary decision-making may be as important as technical resources in determining whether pre-opRT can be successfully incorporated into routine clinical pathways.

Non-core surgeons additionally reported limited access to sarcoma-specialized radiation oncologists, indicating structural disparities in subspecialty distribution across institutions. Approximately one-third of clinicians also expressed uncertainty about the incremental advantage of pre-opRT versus PORT, highlighting a meaningful knowledge gap that may influence sequencing decisions. In contrast, radiation oncologists most frequently described difficulty achieving cross-disciplinary agreement within the MDT, reflecting the coordination and communication burden associated with introducing pre-opRT into existing workflows. The observed differences in perceptions and reported practice patterns between clinician groups should also be interpreted in the context of substantial heterogeneity in subspecialty training, institutional experience, and available resources. Such heterogeneity reflects the realities of sarcoma care delivery in China and may itself represent an important determinant of variation in pre-opRT implementation. These findings illustrate the “transaction costs” inherent in multidisciplinary oncology care: delivering pre-opRT is not simply a clinical preference but an organizational effort requiring alignment across surgical and radiation oncology teams.

The Chinese guideline landscape may help explain why these gaps persist despite the availability of international evidence. In 2015, the Chinese Anti-Cancer Association Sarcoma Committee and Chinese Society of Clinical Oncology published a national expert consensus on STS,34 in which pre-opRT received a class 2A recommendation for selected complex cases—such as large, deep tumors with close proximity to major vessels and nerves, where achieving safe margins or limb-sparing surgery is challenging. However, this recommendation was presented only briefly and primarily framed within the context of highly selected, difficult cases, which may inadvertently have reinforced the perception that pre-opRT is a niche option rather than a routinely considered sequencing strategy. More recently, the 2024 radiotherapy contouring and planning guideline issued by the National Cancer Center/National Quality Control Center focuses mainly on target delineation and planning principles for both pre-opRT and PORT in limb and superficial trunk STS,38 but does not address indications, patient selection, or comparative advantages of pre-opRT. Together, these documents support the technical feasibility of pre-opRT yet provide limited practical guidance on when and for whom it should be prioritized, which may contribute to the observed uncertainty and heterogeneity in clinical use. Consequently, upfront surgery remains the default clinical pathway in many institutions, and implementation of pre-opRT often requires modification of established workflows and earlier multidisciplinary engagement than is traditionally required for postoperative radiotherapy.

Importantly, additional insights emerged from clinicians with higher pre-opRT utilization (>10%). Even these clinicians with relatively higher utilization continued to report postoperative wound complication management, challenges in obtaining cross-disciplinary agreement, patient adherence difficulties, and concerns regarding radiologic response or functional outcomes. These data suggest that the barriers to pre-opRT are not solely related to lack of exposure; rather, they persist across the adoption spectrum and reflect systemic limitations in MDT coordination, patient counseling, and clinical workflow integration. More fundamentally, the complexity of identifying appropriate candidates for pre-opRT may itself become a barrier to implementation. Because treatment sequencing decisions depend simultaneously on tumor characteristics, surgical considerations, anticipated reconstruction requirements, institutional expertise, and patient preferences, many clinicians may preferentially adopt more familiar and operationally efficient pathways, such as upfront surgery followed by selective postoperative radiotherapy when required.

International practice patterns further contextualize these findings. High-volume sarcoma centers in Europe and the United States use pre-opRT as a preferred sequencing strategy, with utilization rates often exceeding 70% in extremity STS.16,29 Adoption is particularly high in radiosensitive subtypes such as synovial sarcoma (69%)32 and myxoid liposarcoma (46%).33 These patterns reflect the alignment of specialized expertise, established MDT processes, and institutional familiarity with pre-opRT—conditions not yet consistently present across Chinese institutions. At the same time, surgical and radiation oncology sarcoma specialists are also mindful that the decision-making for these patients is complicated and challenging. In particular it should be recalled that not all patients require pre-opRT. Some patients may be managed using surgery alone as the preferred approach. However specialists are aware that despite the desire to avoid using radiotherapy where possible in such cases, some will subsequently require PORT based on the pathology findings at the time of surgery (eg in the event of inadequate resection margins). This possibility needs to be considered at the point of decision-making since the ensuing PORT may be anticipated to be undesirable based on the volume and tissues requiring treatment. In other patients presenting with very advanced lesions, pre-opRT may be less desirable or even contra-indicated in the presence of intractable pain, bleeding, infection, or bone invasion with impending bone fracture that would require urgent surgery that should not be delayed, as would be the case with pre-opRT. In other rare patients, post-operative radiotherapy may be preferred depending on the type of reconstruction required which might include an advantage by inducing fibrosis to reduce the size of bulky tissue reconstruction in some anatomic locations.

Despite these challenges, clinician attitudes toward pre-opRT were generally positive. More than 70% believed that pre-opRT holds meaningful value and deserves broader clinical promotion, indicating strong conceptual receptiveness. Addressing this evidence–practice gap will therefore require coordinated strategies to disseminate updated clinical data, strengthen MDT infrastructure, expand access to sarcoma-specialized radiation oncologists, and streamline cross-disciplinary communication.

Weaknesses of the Study

While this survey provides valuable insights into the implementation of pre-opRT in China, several limitations should be considered. First, the snowball sampling strategy used in this survey may have introduced selection bias and may limit the representativeness of the findings. Radiation oncologists were asked to invite collaborating surgeons to participate, which may have preferentially captured clinicians practicing within more established multidisciplinary relationships or institutions with greater experience with or familiarity with pre-opRT. Accordingly, the findings should not be interpreted as nationally representative estimates of practice patterns in China. However, even within this relatively favorable implementation context, reported utilization of pre-opRT remained remarkably low, suggesting that implementation barriers in broader real-world settings may be even more substantial.

Second, the study relied on self-reported data from clinicians and may therefore be subject to recall bias, social desirability bias, and personal perceptions, potentially influencing the reporting and interpretation of barriers to pre-opRT adoption.

Third, because surgeon and radiation oncologist responses were collected independently and were not institutionally paired, the present study evaluated perceptions and experiences related to multidisciplinary collaboration rather than directly assessing the effectiveness or maturity of MDT processes within individual institutions. In addition, the survey instrument cannot fully capture the highly individualized and context-dependent nature of MDT-based decision-making in STS, which is influenced by multiple patient- and institution-level factors, including tumor location and size, proximity to neurovascular structures, clinician preferences, and radiotherapy resource availability and technical capacity.

Fourth, although the survey aimed to capture perspectives from clinicians involved in sarcoma care across China, the sample size remained relatively small in certain subgroups and geographic regions, and the majority of respondents were from tertiary referral centers or academic institutions. Consequently, caution should be exercised when generalizing these findings to smaller hospitals or regions with fewer sarcoma-specific resources.

Finally, although the questionnaire underwent internal pilot testing to improve clarity and usability, formal psychometric validation procedures were not performed. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as exploratory assessments of clinician perceptions and practice patterns rather than precise measurements of underlying attitudinal constructs. Moreover, the cross-sectional design does not allow longitudinal assessment of how perceptions and barriers may evolve over time with increasing experience with pre-opRT, expanding MDT infrastructure, or changes in institutional support.

Future Directions

Future efforts to expand the appropriate use of pre-opRT in China should focus on three major areas. First, improving the evidence-to-practice pipeline through national consensus statements, structured training modules, and multicenter case-based workshops. These efforts can reduce uncertainty regarding pre-opRT efficacy and risks. Second, strengthening MDT infrastructure through policy-level support, accreditation of sarcoma MDTs, and development of regional sarcoma networks. Standardizing communication pathways and implementing digital MDT tools may reduce the coordination burden currently reported by radiation oncologists. Third, advancing clinical research to identify optimal candidates for pre-opRT based on wound-risk profiles, functional outcomes, and histologic radiosensitivity. Prospective registries and implementation-science studies in China will be essential to evaluate the impact of MDT reforms on clinical outcomes and treatment adoption.

In addition, future national guidelines will need to move beyond primarily technical recommendations and provide clearer guidance on pre-opRT indications, patient selection, and sequencing strategies. Updated consensus statements that explicitly integrate clinical, biological, and workflow considerations could help harmonize practice patterns and support more consistent adoption across diverse institutions.

Collectively, these strategies highlight the need for coordinated clinical, educational, and policy actions to modernize sarcoma care in China.

Conclusion

Enhancing the adoption of pre-opRT in China will require not only greater clinician awareness but also system-level reforms that strengthen multidisciplinary cancer care. National initiatives supporting the regionalization of rare tumor management, accreditation of sarcoma MDTs, and formalized referral pathways will help ensure that patients receive evaluation in centers with adequate cross-disciplinary expertise. Standardizing MDT workflows and expanding sarcoma-focused training may reduce coordination barriers and cognitive differences between specialties. Efforts to improve pre-opRT implementation in China may therefore require not only stronger MDT infrastructure and closer collaboration between surgeons and radiation oncologists, but also greater patient engagement and education regarding the role of neoadjuvant treatment strategies.
