Synergistic inhibition effect of Chlorella sp. and benzotriazole on the corrosion of Q235 carbon steel in alkaline artificial seawater
Authors: Shan Chen, Shen Zhang, Mingzhe Yuan, Ping Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe interaction of microalgae on the reinforced concrete with corrosion inhibitor is not well understood. Moreover, the inhibition role of microalgae on corrosion has been reported in recent years. In this study, the corrosion inhibition behavior of Q235 carbon steel (CS) due to the presence of Chlorella sp. and benzotriazole (BTA) in alkaline artificial seawater was investigated by means of weight loss, electrochemical measurements including open circuit potential, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy, and potentiodynamic polarization curves, and surface analysis including scanning electron microscopy, energy dispersion spectrometer, and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy. The results of reduced corrosion rates in the algae-BTA system demonstrated that Chlorella sp. could facilitate the corrosion inhibition efficiency of BTA on the CS specimens. Moreover, polarization measurement showed the algae-BTA system had a mixed-type corrosion inhibition effect. The mechanisms of inhibition were proposed to be the precipitation of iron complexes such as Fe-BTA-EPS and Fe-BTA and iron compounds on the steel surface in the presence of the microalgae and BTA–. This study highlights the application of CS corrosion control by combining biological and chemical approaches that can be used for future research and practice, rather than purely chemical approaches.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-74557-4.Keywords: Corrosion inhibition, Synergistic effect, Biological and chemical inhibitors, Alkaline artificial seawater, Q235 carbon steelSubject terms: Corrosion, Biomaterials - cells

Introduction

Reinforced concrete (RC) structures are widely used in various environments, such as ocean, atmosphere, soil, sewage, and nuclear power industries1. Seawater is a considerably complex medium containing not only chloride ions but also various microorganisms, which can enhance the localized corrosion process of metallic materials and the localized deterioration process of concrete2,3. Chloride ion penetration leading to steel corrosion in RC structures has been discussed extensively4,5. On the other hand, microorganisms such as bacteria and algae can cause corrosion, which is known as microbiologically influenced corrosion. For instance, sulfate reducing bacteria (SRB) produce hydrogen sulfide with a very low pH value under anaerobic conditions to destroy the RC structures in sewer pipes6. Another type of corrosion-enhancing bacteria, sulfur-oxidizing bacteria, is aerobic and can co-exist with SRB7. Chlorella, Stichococcus, and Gloeocapsa are the most commonly encountered microalgal genus found in the construction materials1,8. The metabolic activities of the microorganisms often lead to a decrease in the pH values in the RC environments4, which is approximately 12 for undeteriorated RC structures9. Clearly, the microalgae interact with the interior of the RC structures. However, the corrosion behavior of microalgae on the RC structures under alkaline conditions are yet to be reported in detail.

Carbon steel (CS) is a commonly used material in the RC structures, but it may undergo a critical integrity degradation process in marine environments. This process can cause many harmful results, such as economic and environmental impacts, serious damage of concrete support structures, and even fatal safety disasters10. Adding corrosion inhibitors into the RC structures is a widely adopted approach to protect the functionality and durability of the CS11. The corrosion inhibitors are divided into organic, inorganic, and hybrid types, which have different action mechanisms, such as formation a passive film or precipitate, consumption of aggressive species, enhancement of hydrophobicity, etc12. Benzotriazole (BTA), an organic corrosion inhibitor, is widely used in engineering applications because of its ability to inhibit both anodic and cathodic reactions13. BTA has been proved by many studies to have an efficient inhibition effect against steel and iron corrosion14,15. The benefits of applying this corrosion inhibitor include low toxicity and cost, good thermal stability, and reasonable solubility in water16. In addition to the chemical corrosion inhibitors, green corrosion inhibitors, especially biological corrosion inhibitors, are emerging as a promising and environmentally friendly option due to their cost-effective and biodegradable properties17. The dual role of microorganisms has been reported in recent years18–20. Some microalgae, such as Spirulina platensis and Dunaliella salina, have been shown to inhibit metallic corrosion21,22. To date, no studies have been conducted on the combined effect of corrosion inhibitors and microalgae on the corrosion process of the CS in alkaline conditions.

In this study, a laboratory investigation was carried out on the combined effect of BTA with Chlorella sp. on the corrosion inhibition behavior of Q235 CS specimens under alkaline artificial seawater conditions using a range of chemical and electrochemical measurements. Chlorella sp., a common species of marine microalgae, was selected for this study because of its reported inhibition effect against CS corrosion in f/2 medium23. Therefore, we hypothesized that the combined inhibition effect of the microalgae and BTA on CS corrosion was superior to that of BTA alone. This study provides a new insight into CS corrosion control by combining biological and chemical approaches for future research and applications.

Experimental

Material preparation

The corrosion specimens in this study were cut from a Q235 CS sheet. The elemental composition (wt%) of the specimens was 0.17 C, 0.11 Si, 0.02 Mn, 0.017 P, 0.006 S, and balance Fe. The measurements were carried out using square shape specimens with a side length of 1 cm, and the rest was sealed with epoxy resin and soldered with a copper wire. Before immersion experiment, all the specimens were abraded through 200-, 400-, 600-, 800-, and 1200-grit emery paper, degreased in absolute ethanol, washed with ultrapure water, dried by air dry oven, and desiccated until use. In addition, the specimens were sterilized by an ultraviolet lamp for 30 min to ensure the absence of other bacteria.

The Chlorella sp. in this study was provided from Yellow Sea Fisheries Research Institute, Chinese Academy of Fishery Sciences, and cultivated in the f/2 medium23. The inoculums were incubated in an incubator at 25 ± 1 °C under a light: dark (12:12) cycle with an irradiance of 2400 lx. All experimental devices and media used were sterilized by autoclaving at 121 °C for 30 min prior to use. The cell counts were determined using a hemocytometer under an optical microscope (Leitz Laborlux, Germany) at 400× magnification. The algal biomass was calculated by algal volume as per the previous study24. BTA was purchased from San Yao Hong Co., Ltd. (Macau, China). Ultrapure water (18.2 MΩ × cm) was obtained using a Millipore Direct-Q 3UV system (Merck, Billerica, MA, USA).

Test solution

The immersion experiment lasted for 384 h in a 250-mL beaker filled with 100 mL corrosive solution. The corrosive solution in this study was alkaline artificial seawater, containing sterilized filtered river water and 3.5% NaCl. Its solution pH was adjusted to 10 with 0.1 mol·L−1 NaOH. The stock solution of BTA was prepared with neutral artificial seawater. To verify the hypothesis that the microalgae and BTA inhibit CS corrosion better than BTA alone, two treatment groups (algae-BTA-CS and BTA-CS), one control group (CS alone), and one blank group (algae alone) were studied. Before the immersion experiment, the microalgal cell and BTA stock solution in the algae-BTA group were magnetically stirred for 1 h under a neutral condition to ensure that the adsorption/desorption equilibrium was reached. Then, the rest of the corrosive solution was added into this system to simulate the onset of corrosion, followed by the prepared specimen into the corrosive solution. The initial concentration of BTA was set as 10 mg·L−1, and the initial biomass of the microalgae was set as 160 mg·L−1. The microalgal biomass and/or BTA concentration (3 mL) from each beaker were detected at 0, 24, 48, 72, 96, 192, 288, and 384 h. Three mL of fresh alkaline artificial seawater with and without 10 mg·L−1 BTA was replenished to the beakers in the two treatment groups and the blank group, respectively. All the experiments were performed within the incubator under the same culture condition. To simulate flow disturbances and prevent microalgal deposition, the beakers were gently shaken, and their positions were randomly changed every 24 h for uniform distribution of light. The dissolved oxygen (DO) concentration of the corrosive solution was measured with an oxygen electrode (DO-957, Leici, China). All the measurements were carried out at room temperature and repeated three times for reproducibility.

Weight loss measurement

All the specimens were weighed by an analytical balance (ATX224R, Shimadzu, Japan) with an accuracy of 0.1 mg before and after the immersion experiment. The specimens were taken out from the corrosive solution, and the passive films were sequentially stripped with a solution of 2.5% glutaraldehyde in phosphate buffer saline (PBS) and a Clarke’s solution (20 g antimony trioxide + 50 g stannous chloride + 1000 mL 37% concentrated hydrochloric acid). The exposed specimen surfaces were cleaned in absolute ethanol, rinsed with ultrapure water, and dried by the air dry oven. The exposed specimens and passive films were used for further surface analysis.

Electrochemical measurements

The open circuit potential (OCP), electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS), and potentiodynamic polarization curves were performed in the corrosive solution using an electrochemical workstation (Gamry Interface 1000E, USA). A saturated calomel electrode (SCE) and a platinum plate served as the reference and counter electrodes, respectively. Q235 CS specimens were used as the working electrodes one at a time. The three-electrode cell system was open without aeration. The EIS measurement was obtained after immersion for 1, 24, 48, 72, 96, 192, 288, and 384 h by applying a sinusoidal voltage signal of 10 mV in a frequency range of 105 Hz to 10−2 Hz. The EIS data were analyzed by Gamry Echem Analyst software, and fitted with a suitable circuit model. After 384 h, potentiodynamic polarization curves were measured by scanning the potential from − 400 mV/SCE to + 600 mV/SCE vs. OCP at a sweep rate of 0.167 mV·s−1.

Surface analysis

After the immersion experiment, the exposed specimens and passive films were examined using scanning electron microscope (SEM, Zeiss sigma 500, Carl Zeiss, Germany). Energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) was used for elemental analysis of the passive films, and 3-D optical profiler (S neox, Sensofar, Spain) was used to characterize the surface roughness of the exposed specimens. Before SEM observation, the exposed specimens were dried and placed in desiccators; the passive films were collected in PBS solution containing 2.5% glutaraldehyde, and then dehydrated using serial dilution of ethanol (50%, 60%, 70%, 80%, 90%, 95%, and 100%) for 10 min each. An X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo Scientific K-Alpha, Thermo Fisher Scientific, UK) analysis was also adopted to evaluate chemical compounds in the passive films, and the data were fitted by Avantage 5.948 software (Thermo Fisher Scientific, UK).

Results

Open circuit potential measurement

Figure S1 shows the OCP variation of Q235 CS specimen in the control, BTA, and algae-BTA groups within 384 h. The changes of OCP in the three groups were similar, where the highest values reached at 1 h, followed by a rapid decrease within the first 72 h and a slow increase to 192 h, and remained stable until 384 h. The experimental results can be explained by the formation of passive films. Initially, the electrode surface had no protective film to hinder the corrosive solution. However, in the early stage of the CS corrosion, the microalgae began to reproduce and colonize rapidly, and a biofilm appeared on the surface of the electrode25,26. In addition, BTA could also form a polymeric film to prevent corrosion of the immersed CS specimens27.

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy measurement

Figures S2 and S3 illustrate the Nyquist and Bode diagrams of the impedance measurements for Q235 CS specimens exposed to the control, BTA, and algae-BTA groups within 384 h. Fig. S2 suggests that the corrosion rate in the algae-BTA group was lower than that in the control and BTA groups due to the larger the diameters shown in Nyquist plots indicating the lower the corrosion rates28. However, the diameters of these three groups changed within the immersion, indicating a change in the metal/solution interface29. Figure S3 shows that the phase angle at middle frequencies of the algae-BTA group was higher than that of the control and BTA groups. It means that the corrosion resistance was the highest in the presence of the microalgae and BTA30. Moreover, as a decisive parameter, impedance modulus at low frequency (|Z|0.01 Hz) was taken as it reflects the corrosion process at the metal/solution interface31. Figure 1 presents the plot of the logarithmic value of |Z|0.01 Hz as a function of immersion time for the three groups. It shows that the value of log |Z|0.01 Hz in the algae-BTA group was always significantly higher than that in the control and/or BTA groups (p < 0.05), except for 48 and 288 h, indicating that the algae-BTA group had the highest corrosion resistance performance32. The microalgae and BTA may form a more compact passive film than BTA, thereby slowing down the corrosion rate and increasing the low-frequency impedance modulus.

Surface morphology of exposed specimens and corrosion products

The surface morphology of Q235 CS specimens without passive films was examined using SEM, which is shown in Fig. 3. At 192 h, clear grinding scratches on the specimens of the BTA (Fig. 3b) and algae-BTA (Fig. 3c) groups could be easily recognized. However, some corrosion pits could also be found in the control (Fig. 3a) and BTA (Fig. 3b) groups. At 384 h, the scratches disappeared, and the uniform and pitting corrosion could be observed in all groups (Fig. 3a’, b’, c’). Figure S5 and Table S1 show the 3-D surface morphology and surface roughness of the CS specimens after removing the passive films. As can be seen from Fig. S5, the maximum pitting pit depths in the algae-BTA group were 5 µm (Fig. S5c) and 16 µm (Fig. S5c’) at 192 and 384 h, respectively, which were less than those in the control (Fig. S5a and S5a’) and BTA (Fig. S5b and S5b’) groups. It indicates that the addition of the microalgae could reduce the pitting pit depth. However, the phenomenon of uniform corrosion still existed in the algae-BTA group (Fig. S5c’). Moreover, by comparing the SEM and 3-D surface morphologies between 192 and 384 h, it was found that the CS corrosion of all groups became more severe with time. In Table S1, the average surface roughness (Ra) was the highest in the BTA group at 192 and 384 h, indicating that the inhibitor had a very short protection period for CS uniform corrosion in this study. The Ra in the algae-BTA group at 192 h was the lowest, which means that the microalgae could slow down the corrosion rate of the CS specimens in a period of time after the onset of corrosion.

SEM morphologies of Q235 carbon steel specimens after removing corrosion products in the control (a,a’), BTA (b,b’), and algae-BTA (c,c’) groups after 192 and 384 h of immersion, respectively.

SEM morphologies and the corresponding EDS results of the passive films are shown in Fig. S6. The EDS results show that the key elements were C, O, and Fe, the secondary elements were Na, Cl, and P, and the trace elements were K and Si in the elemental constituent of the corrosion products. Therefore, it can be concluded that the corrosion products of all groups were similar. XPS results further revealed the composition of the chemical compounds of the corrosion products. Figure 4 presents the high-resolution XPS spectra of survey, Fe 2p, O 1s, and C 1s for corrosion products from different groups at 192 and 384 h. In Fig. 4a and a’, the survey spectrum of the algae-BTA group was different from that of the control and BTA groups. For example, in the algae-BTA group, the Fe content was higher than that in the other two groups, while the Na, Cl, and P contents were lower than those in the other two groups. The Fe 2p peak (Fig. 4b and b’) contains contributions from Fe2O3 at 711.71 eV, Fe(II) at 715.58 and 729.18 eV, Fe(III) at 719.58 eV, and FeCl2 at 724.88 eV36,37. The variations of the valence state and composition of Fe reflect the changes in the composition and structure of the passive films. The high content of Fe2O3 in the algae-BTA group is beneficial to the stability of the passive films and the improvement of corrosion inhibition efficiency38. With regard to the O 1s peak (Fig. 4c and c’), it includes three basic components, that is, Fe2O3 and/or FeCO3 at 531.17 eV, Fe(OH)3 at 533.08 eV, and organic O and/or adsorbed H2O at 536.08 eV39. The presence of FeCO3 in each group is due to the conversion of dissolved CO2 into CO32– in the corrosive solution and/or microalgal growing environment40. Compared with the BTA and algae-BTA groups, the relative amount of Fe(OH)3 in the control group was the highest, indicating that Fe(III) hydroxide mainly exists existed in the alkaline artificial seawater without inhibitors, which is consistent with the result of Wang et al.41. The content of the adsorbed H2O in the control and BTA groups was much higher than that in the algae-BTA group, which means that the adsorbed H2O was responsible for accelerating the surface corrosion of the CS specimens. In addition, the peak of C 1s (Fig. 4d and d’) at 284.8, 286.51, and 288.37 eV correspond to C-C and/or C = C, C-O and/or C = O, and CO32– and/or C-N42. It indicates that a biofilm of organic compounds produced by the microalgae and/or a BTA polymeric film existed in the algae-BTA and BTA groups.

Microalgal biomass and BTA and DO concentrations

Figure S7 shows the microalgal biomass and BTA concentration in different groups of corrosive solutions within 384 h. The comparison between the blank and algae-BTA groups shows that the presence of BTA and Q235 CS specimens had no significant effect on the growth of microalgal cells (Fig. S7a). The concentration of BTA firstly decreased within 96 h due to the consumption of the inhibitor for corrosion control, and then increased to the end of the immersion due to the addition of BTA at each sampling time point, indicating that the added BTA mass was greater than the consumed BTA mass (Fig. S7b).

In Table S3, the DO concentration of the blank group was higher than that of the control, BTA, and algae-BTA groups. It suggests that the life activity of the microalgae changed the concentration of DO in the alkaline artificial seawater. However, the DO concentration of the algae-BTA group was the lowest, and the corrosion rate of this group was also the lowest. A possible explanation for this observation is that the more oxygen vacancies on the surface of the passive films had a corrosion inhibition effect, which was reported on 316 L stainless steel by Liu et al.44.

Discussion

BTA is a heterocyclic organic compound with three nitrogen atoms in its structure. The nitrogen atoms and the π electrons in the aromatic ring are the active sites of BTA molecules. BTA molecules can form a polymeric film by a donor-acceptor reaction with the metallic surface, resulting in corrosion resistance of immersed metals27. The structure of BTA molecules varies with pH, and is a deprotonated anionic species (BTA−) at pH > 8.645. There are several studies on corrosion prevention of steel in acidic electrolytes containing BTA46,47. However, there are only few studies on the anticorrosion performance of BTA in alkaline saline solutions41,48. In these studies, the corrosion inhibition mechanism of BTA has been reported as physical and chemical adsorption. The physical adsorption is due to the presence of van der Waals forces between the corrosion inhibitors and the metals49. The chemical adsorption rises from a polymeric film formed on the steel surface due to the complexation between the Fe2+ cations with empty 3d orbitals and the central N atoms with lone-pair electrons in the BTA structure16. In this study, the reduced corrosion rate in the potentiodynamic polarization curves and weight loss measurements show that the addition of BTA inhibited the corrosion of Q235 CS specimens in comparison with the control specimens in the alkaline artificial seawater (Table 2 and S2). XPS results demonstrate that it is mainly the presence of corrosion product film of iron oxides, hydroxides, and carbonates (Fig. 4). In addition, the alkaline environment is favorable for the transformation from 1 H-benzotriazole to 2 H-benzotriazole50, which mainly exists in the form of BTA−. Moreover, the composition of the polymeric complex film was considered as [Fen(BTA)m] and [Fen(Cl)p(BTA)m] in the study of Mennucci et al.48. These polymeric complexes can effectively hinder the dissolution and oxidation of iron, and are the main corrosion inhibition mechanism of BTA.

Many studies have reported the synergetic effect of two types of inhibitors on metallic corrosion inhibition40,51,52. Previous studies also found an increase in corrosion inhibition efficiency of BTA in combination with other inorganic or organic chemical inhibitors38,53,54. However, there are limited studies on the synergistic corrosion inhibition effect of chemical and biological corrosion inhibitors. In this study, the corrosion inhibition behavior of a mixture of a microalga and BTA for Q235 CS specimens in alkaline artificial seawater and its possible mechanisms were investigated for the first time. The potentiodynamic polarization curves and weight loss measurements show that Chlorella sp. and BTA could reduce the corrosion rates and improve the corrosion inhibition efficiency on the CS specimens for RC structure compared with BTA alone (Table 2 and S2). The potentiodynamic polarization curves also indicate that the BTA inhibitor and the microalgae and BTA combined inhibitors were both mixed-type inhibitors (Fig. 2). It is because the Ecorr displacement for both systems did not exceed 85 mV. However, based on the different directions of the Ecorr displacement, one can argue that the dominating corrosion inhibition mechanisms of these two systems were different: the BTA system was better at inhibiting the anodic reaction process, while the algae-BTA system was better at inhibiting the cathodic reaction process. The corrosion inhibition action of BTA has been reported to act as an anode-type inhibitor in low alkaline solutions41, which is consistent with the result of its positive displacement observed in this study. The Chlorella sp. was a mixed-type inhibitor in the f/2 medium, which has been reported in our previous study, preferring to inhibit the cathodic reaction process23. Combined with the results of βc and βa, it was found that the addition of the microalgae could enhanced the corrosion inhibition efficiency of BTA in both anodic and cathodic reaction processes.

The BTA– can be adsorbed in the surface region of the corrosion product film since the oxygen atoms positions of Fe2O3 can be occupied by BTA–41. However, it has been reported that BTA– adsorption conditions were strict due to the requirement of satisfying the inclination of the BTA molecular plane55. Therefore, one can speculate that the microalgae owning to their natural adhesion properties may be able to access CS surfaces more quickly than BTA. Moreover, the interaction between the microalgae and BTA may increase the corrosion resistance of BTA, because BTA is adsorbed into the biofilm in the form of [Fe[BTA][EPS]y(OH)z]1–y–z to stabilize the layer, which may make it easier for BTA to reach the metallic surface. The amount of BTA– was sufficient due to each sampling supplement of BTA, as shown in Fig. S7b. Two iron complexes containing EPS in the biofilm have been reported in our previous study23. In addition, the corrosion product film of iron oxides, hydroxides, carbonates, and BTA-containing polymeric complex film as described above were also present in the algae-BTA group. Conclusions

In this study, Chlorella sp. could further reduce the corrosion rates of Q235 CS specimens treated by BTA alone via the results of multiple testing approaches including EIS, potentiodynamic polarization curves, and weight loss measurements, and surface morphologies. Moreover, the 3-D surface morphologies of the CS specimens showed that the microalgae could inhibit the depth and longitudinal development of the corrosion pits. However, the occurrence of uniform and pitting corrosion indicated that the corrosion protection due to the synergistic corrosion inhibition effect of the microalgae and BTA was declining with time. The results of XPS analysis indicated that the main corrosion products and polymeric complexes of the CS specimens in the BTA system were mainly Fe2O3, Fe(OH)3, FeCO3, [Fen(BTA)m], and [Fen(Cl)p(BTA)m], while the iron complexes of the CS specimens in the algae-BTA system mainly included [Fe[BTA][EPS]y(OH)z]1–y–z additionally.
