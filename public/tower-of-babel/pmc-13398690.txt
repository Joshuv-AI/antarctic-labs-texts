Transnasal endoscopy for eosinophilic esophagitis surveillance in adolescents with mild autism spectrum disorder: A single‐center case series
Authors: Natalee Sarintra, Robbie Pesek, Travis Ayers, Christina Tootle, Sulaiman S. Bharwani, Alejandro Ramirez, Hamza Hassan Khan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChildren with autism spectrum disorder (ASD) often experience sensory sensitivities and procedural anxiety, which can complicate sedated esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD). Transnasal endoscopy (TNE) is a less invasive, unsedated alternative, but data on its use in this population are limited. We evaluated the safety, tolerability, and clinical utility of sedation‐free TNE in adolescents with Level 1 ASD (mild autism) undergoing surveillance for eosinophilic esophagitis (EoE). In this retrospective case series, five adolescents (mean age 13.2 years; 80% male) underwent TNE between April 2024 and April 2025. We assessed procedural success, adverse events, biopsy adequacy, and behavioral strategies. All procedures were completed successfully without sedation or serious adverse events. Child Life Specialists (CLSs) supported 80% of cases, using behavioral aids, such as virtual reality (VR) goggles and desensitization tools. Mean procedure time was 10.75 ± 1.96 min. We concluded that TNE is a safe and feasible alternative to sedated EGD in carefully selected adolescents with mild ASD and warrants further study in larger cohorts.

INTRODUCTION

Eosinophilic esophagitis (EoE) is a chronic, immune‐mediated condition characterized by inflammation of the esophagus. 1 The diagnostic gold standard is esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD), with esophageal biopsies demonstrating at least 15 eosinophils per high‐power field. 1 However, EGD typically requires sedation under general anesthesia, which can pose added challenges for certain pediatric populations, particularly in children with autism spectrum disorder (ASD), who often experience increased sensory sensitivities and procedural anxiety.

Transnasal endoscopy (TNE) has emerged as a less invasive, unsedated alternative to EGD, and several studies have shown it to be a safe, efficient, and well‐tolerated option for EoE surveillance in the general pediatric population. Sabe et al. reported a median total TNE encounter‐time of 33.5 min compared to 158 min for EGD in a cohort of 33 patients aged 4–20 years undergoing 65 TNE procedures, with no serious adverse events. 2 Similarly, Friedlander et al. found that among 21 patients aged 8–17 undergoing TNE, none experienced serious adverse events, and 76.2% expressed willingness to repeat the procedure. 3 Notably, both studies utilized distraction tools, such as video goggles and tablet‐based entertainment, to help patients tolerate the procedure.

Despite these promising results, data on the use of TNE specifically in pediatric patients with ASD remain limited. Given the heightened sensory sensitivities and procedural anxiety common in this population, it would be valuable to explore whether similar distraction techniques could facilitate successful and tolerable TNE experiences for children with ASD. To address this gap, we report a single‐center retrospective case series of pediatric patients with mild ASD who underwent TNE for EoE surveillance to assess efficacy and tolerability in this population.

METHODS

Ethics statement

For this retrospective case series, informed consent was obtained from the patients' legal guardians prior to data collection and review, and the study was exempted from full review by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) at our institution in accordance with IRB guidelines.

Data collection

We conducted a retrospective case series at Arkansas Children's Hospital, a tertiary pediatric medical center, identifying children with ASD who underwent unsedated TNE between April 2024 and April 2025. During this study period, initial TNE was offered clinically as an alternative to sedated EGD for EoE surveillance after shared decision‐making with families. All candidates had previously undergone sedated EGD to establish the diagnosis of EoE, and none of them had undergone TNE before.

Candidates were pre‐screened by the treating team for the likelihood of tolerating an unsedated transnasal approach (e.g., ability to tolerate simple instructions, tolerate topical nasal/oropharyngeal anesthesia, and cooperate without physical restraints). Patients deemed unlikely to tolerate the procedure—particularly those with higher support needs—were typically scheduled for sedated EGD. For this analysis, we reviewed all pediatric TNEs performed during the study period and identified those with a documented ASD diagnosis.

The diagnosis of ASD was based upon prior developmental‐behavioral specialists' evaluation. Although the specific level of ASD (as defined by the DSM‐5 criteria) was not consistently documented in the records, clinical notes suggested that these patients most likely met criteria consistent with Level 1 ASD (mild ASD), indicating a need for support without substantial impairments in functioning.

Informed consent was obtained from the respective guardians via telephone before data collection and documented in the chart. Data collection included patient's age, gender, vital signs (heart rate and blood pressure) prior to the procedure, the experience of the endoscopist performing the TNE, presence of child life, procedural success, procedure time, encounter time from check‐in to check‐out, endoscopic findings, number of biopsies obtained, biopsies' adequacy, immediate complications after the procedure, family's access to the managing team using the patient portal messaging system (MyChart) to report any complications after discharge, and delayed complications up to 1 month after the procedure.

Success was defined by the patient's ability to complete the TNE and obtain adequate biopsies for EoE surveillance. Endoscopist TNE experience was defined as the number of TNEs that the endoscopist had performed before the current TNE. Of note, MyChart is a patient portal messaging tool in the institutional electronic health record (e.g., Epic Electronic Medical Record system) through which both patients/guardians and the managing provider can reach each other regarding patient updates.

As part of the TNE encounter, all families and patients were expected to view an online TNE video before the encounter. On the day of the encounter, all patients were assessed by our nursing team after triage. A Child Life Specialist (CLS) met with the patient before entering the procedure room and was present throughout the procedure. The CLS reviewed the procedure and distraction tools/techniques (such as virtual reality [VR] goggles, fidget spinners, or stress balls) with the patient and family, followed by the procedure provider reviewing the procedure again before obtaining consent.

All procedures were performed using EvoEndo® Single‐Use Transnasal Gastroscopes (3.5 mm outer diameter, 2.0 mm working channel). Prior to scope insertion, the scope was tested for proper functioning. As part of the procedure, the provider would administer 0.5% phenylephrine in each nostril (total volume 0.25 mL each nostril) to aid in decongestion, followed by 4% (40 mg/mL) lidocaine spray into each nostril and the oropharynx (1 mL each) for local anesthesia of nasopharynx and oropharynx. To help with the distaste of the lidocaine, patients were provided with a Ring Pop®. The gastroscope was then inserted under direct visualization through one nare and advanced through the esophagus and stomach. Endoscopic visual assessment of the esophagus and stomach was performed, and biopsies were routinely obtained from the distal, mid, and proximal esophagus.

RESULTS

A total of 37 patients underwent TNE during the period for EoE surveillance, of which five patients (13.5%) had a known diagnosis of mild ASD. None of these patients underwent repeat TNE during this period.

Of these five patients, the mean age was 13.2 ± 1.7 years (range: 10–16 years), and 80% of the cohort were male. All procedures were successfully completed without the need for sedation, yielding a procedural success rate of 100%. The mean procedure time was 10.75 ± 1.96 min (range: 8–14 min). Total check‐in‐to‐check‐out time ranged from 66 to 228 min, reflecting clinic flow and scheduling variability rather than procedure duration alone.

No major complications were observed. One patient reported mild procedural discomfort immediately following the TNE, while another experienced a sore throat and minor epistaxis 2 days post‐procedure; neither event required medical intervention.

CLS were involved in 80% of cases, providing behavioral support. Distraction and desensitization techniques, including VR goggles for patient distraction and fidget spinners or stress balls, were employed to enhance patient cooperation and comfort during the procedure.

Endoscopic findings included furrows (n = 3), crepes (n = 1), exudates (n = 1), and normal mucosa (n = 1). In all cases, six esophageal biopsies were obtained (two each from the distal, mid, and proximal esophagus), and all samples were deemed histologically adequate for diagnostic evaluation. DISCUSSION

TNE is a safe and well‐tolerated method for EoE surveillance in the pediatric population. Table 2 summarizes the experiences of prior studies regarding using TNE. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 Our retrospective case series further demonstrates that TNE is a feasible surveillance modality in adolescent patients with ASD with minimal support needs. All five patients in our pre‐selected cohort successfully completed unsedated TNE with no major complications, and procedure times were within the range reported in prior pediatric TNE studies. Although the study was not designed to compare ASD and non‐ASD populations, these descriptive data support the feasibility of a sedation‐free approach in carefully selected patients.

Sedation‐free TNE may also reduce anesthesia‐related charges. In a study by Nguyen et al., charges for TNE with biopsy were 53.4% lower than for EGD with biopsy. 4 This cost differential is relevant for ongoing surveillance in EoE and for assessing histologic response to therapies, including food elimination diet and response to pharmacologic interventions. However, successful implementation of TNE in our cohort frequently required CLS, which represents additional resources and potential costs that could offset some of these savings and potentially limit scalability in non‐tertiary settings.

The involvement of CLS and the use of behavioral adjuncts appeared to facilitate procedural tolerance and cooperation, which is particularly relevant given the heightened sensory sensitivities and procedural anxiety common in children with ASD. The CLS was present throughout the procedure. Distraction modality was tailored to patient preference (VR vs. fidget/stress tools, or both). Additional supportive strategies included allowing extra time for desensitization to nasal spray and scope introduction while providing clear stepwise narration with predictable countdowns and minimizing environmental stimuli (such as noise) with limited personnel in the room. These distraction techniques, previously validated in neurotypical pediatric populations, may also be effective in this more procedurally vulnerable group.

Biopsy adequacy and diagnostic yield were maintained across all cases, indicating that TNE provides reliable tissue sampling comparable to traditional EGD. The mean procedure duration for patients with ASD averaged approximately 11 min (range: 8–14 min), demonstrating that TNE can be performed efficiently in this population. Although not powered for formal inference, procedure times appeared longer when performed by an endoscopist early in their TNE experiences, consistent with a learning‐curve effect. These findings suggest that more experienced providers may be preferable when initiating TNE in patients with mild ASD.

To our knowledge, this is the first report specifically evaluating the feasibility and outcomes of TNE in Level 1 ASD (mild ASD) children. Prior data from the TNE study utilizing a “TNEase” scoring system has demonstrated that unsedated TNE is feasible in pediatric populations and that children with ASD can successfully undergo the procedure without decreased tolerance; ASD may not independently predict poor tolerance in selected cohorts. 9 Our findings extend these observations by providing focused data in a small group of adolescents with Level 1 ASD.

Although the results are encouraging, this study is limited by its small sample size, retrospective study design, and single‐center experience, which may restrict generalizability. Because TNE candidates were clinically pre‐screened for cooperation and ability to tolerate an unsedated procedure, this case series is subject to selection bias, and feasibility findings likely apply primarily to adolescents with mild ASD requiring minimal support. More investigation into the tolerability of TNE in younger patients and in those with Level 2 or 3 ASD, who may experience greater challenges with sensory processing and procedural compliance, should be considered. Moreover, we did not include a non‐ASD comparator cohort; therefore, we cannot make inferences regarding differences in tolerance or procedure duration by neurodevelopmental status. Future prospective studies with larger cohorts are warranted to confirm these findings and further explore optimal behavioral support strategies tailored to children with ASD.

CONCLUSION

In this small, pre‐selected cohort of primarily adolescent patients with mild ASD, sedation‐free TNE was completed successfully without serious adverse events. Broader feasibility for younger children or those with Level 2–3 ASD remains uncertain. Incorporating distraction and desensitization tools may enhance the tolerability of the procedure, potentially reducing reliance on sedation and improving the overall patient experience.
