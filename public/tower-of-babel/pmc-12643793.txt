Current Understanding of Carotid Body Paraganglioma Management
Authors: Young-Wook Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCarotid body paraganglioma (CBP) is a rare neuroendocrine tumor that typically develop at the carotid bifurcation. Although most CBPs are non-functional and slow growing, some pose a risk of local invasion and distant metastasis. Advances in genetic testing have revealed that up to 50% of paragangliomas (PGLs) are linked to germline mutations, most commonly in the succinate dehydrogenase (SDHx) genes. The clinical presentation of CBPs is often subtle, with patients noticing a painless, slowly enlarging mass in the neck. CBP diagnosis often relies on imaging (both anatomical and functional) and biochemical testing. In managing CBPs, surgical resection remains the standard treatment for localized tumor, but the procedure can be technically demanding due to high vascularity and close proximity of the tumor to cervical neurovascular structures. Other treatment options, especially for patients with unresectable or metastatic CBPs, include radiotherapy, radionuclide therapy, and chemotherapy. This review discussed current CBP management, from diagnosis to treatment and surveillance, highlighting the importance of individualized care based on tumor behavior, genetic background, and surgical risk.Keywords: Paraganglioma, Carotid body, Head and neck neoplasms, Genetic predisposition to disease, Radionuclide imaging

INTRODUCTION

Paraganglioma (PGL) is a rare neuroendocrine neoplasm (NEN) that arises from the paraganglia, which are clusters of neuroendocrine cells dispersed throughout the body and are closely related to the autonomic nervous system, with either parasympathetic or sympathetic functions. The largest cluster of paraganglial cells is located within the adrenal medulla, while smaller collections are found in the paravertebral space and the head and neck region, usually along major blood vessels and nerves. Notably, head and neck PGLs arise from the carotid bodies or nerves in the autonomic nervous system, such as the cranial nerves (e.g., the vagus and glossopharyngeal nerves) [1].

Traditionally, PGLs are classified into sympathetic and parasympathetic PGLs, according to their derivation. Sympathetic PGL arises from chromaffin cells in the paraganglia, which are located along the sympathetic nerves. These include PGLs arising from the adrenal medulla (e.g., pheochromocytoma [PCC]) and extra-adrenal PGLs. Interestingly, extra-adrenal sympathetic paraganglia are distributed along the prevertebral and paravertebral sympathetic chains, and sympathetic nerve fibers innervate the retroperitoneum, thorax, and pelvis (Fig. 1) [2,3]. Moreover, approximately 85% of sympathetic PGLs arise below the diaphragm, with the largest paraganglia originating from the adrenal medulla and organ of Zuckerkandl. The organ of Zuckerkandl is comprised of a small mass of chromaffin cells derived from the neural crest located along the distal abdominal aorta. The organ of Zuckerkandl is thought to be a homeostatic regulator of blood pressure during the early gestation by secreting catecholamines into the fetal circulation. Subsequently, the organ of Zuckerkandl regresses at the end of gestation and following birth to form the para-aortic sympathetic paraganglia in adults [4].

Sympathetic PGLs secrete catecholamines. In contrast, most parasympathetic PGLs are non-secretory and located along the glossopharyngeal and vagal nerves in the neck and at the base of the skull. According to their anatomic sites of origin, parasympathetic include carotid body PGLs (CBPs), jugulotympanic PGLs (previously called glomus jugulare or glomus tympanicum), vagal PGLs, laryngeal PGLs, and cardiac or pulmonary PGLs.

Extra-adrenal PGLs and PCCs are indistinguishable at the cellular level. Therefore, the umbrella term pheochromocytomas-paragangliomas (PPGLs) is commonly used to encompass both PCCs and PGLs. Generally, PCCs have been classified as a separate disease entity from PGLs. However, the 2004 World Health Organization (WHO) classification of tumors of endocrine organs designated PCCs as sympathetic adrenal PGLs [5]. According to WHO, PCCs are classified as one of the four subtypes of PGLs: adrenal PGL (PCC), sympathetic abdominal PGL, sympathetic head and neck PGL, and parasympathetic carotid body PGL (Table 1) [6,7].

Importantly, PGLs are no longer classified as benign or malignant since the fourth edition of the WHO classification of PPGLs (Table 1), published in 2017, because all PGLs have metastatic potential and there are no clear-cut histological features that can predict which lesions have malignant tendency [8].

Most head and neck PGLs are parasympathetic, and they rarely arise from the cervical sympathetic chains, superior cervical ganglion, ciliary ganglion (orbital PGL), or paraganglia of mixed parasympathetic and sympathetic innervations [9,10]. CBP is the most common among the head and neck PGLs, representing approximately 50%-60% of all head and neck PGLs [11]. This review focuses primarily on CBPs.

EPIDEMIOLOGY

To date, the specific incidence of PGLs remains unknown. In the United States, medical professionals diagnose approximately 500 to 1,000 PGLs annually, and the combined incidence of PPGLs is approximately 0.7 to 1.0 per 100,000 person-years [12]. Head and neck PGLs comprise 3% of all PGLs and account for <0.5% of all head and neck cancers [13]. CBPs constitute up to 60% of all PGLs in the head and neck region. Most PGLs are diagnosed in individuals between 30 to 50 years of age, with a mean age of 47 years at diagnosis [12].

Three different types of CBPs have been described: familial, sporadic, and hyperplastic. The sporadic form is the most common (85%), while the familial type is more prevalent among younger individuals. The hyperplastic form is common in patients with chronic hypoxia, such as those who live at high altitudes, and patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) or cyanotic heart disease [14]. Patients with head and neck PGLs tended to be slightly older at presentation than those with abdominal PGLs (mean age: 43 vs. 36 years). This difference may be due to more frequent symptoms secondary to catecholamine hypersecretion in patients with PGLs arising in the abdomen or pelvis [15].

Approximately 40%-50% of PGLs are known to be caused by pathogenic germline variants [16]. Therefore, understanding the characteristics of PGLs associated with genetic mutations is important. Patients with hereditary PGLs develop the condition approximately a decade earlier than those with sporadic PGLs [17,18]. Moreover, multiple or bilateral PGLs are more common (17%-85%) in hereditary PGLs compared to sporadic PGLs (1.2%) [19,20]. Furthermore, although sporadic tumors are more common in females (71%), the male-to-female ratio is approximately equal among patients with hereditary PGLs [21]. Therefore, the possibility of hereditary PGLs is higher in males with PGLs than in females. Additionally, multiple and catecholamine-secreting sympathetic PGLs are more likely to be hereditary PGLs rather than sporadic PGLs [22,23].

Familial PGLs are found to be 5.8 times more common among patients with CBPs than among those with PGLs at other sites [18]. Therefore, a more thorough investigation of family history is required for patients with CBPs than for those with PGLs at other sites. Sporadic or hyperplastic CBPs are more frequent in inhabitants living at high altitudes and in patients with chronic hypoxia (e.g., those with cyanotic congenital heart disease or COPD) [24-26]. Although a causal relationship is unclear, a consistent link exists between hypoxia/hypoxia-inducible factors and PGL development. According to Rodríguez-Cuevas et al. [26], patients with CBPs living at high altitudes are predominantly female and have lower rates of bilateral tumors and family histories than patients at lower altitudes. These findings suggest that the pathogenesis of CBPs in high-altitude inhabitants may be different from that of familial PGLs.

GENETICS OF PGLS

As described above, approximately 40%-50% of PGLs are known to be due to pathogenic germline variants [16]. PGLs caused by germline mutations have been increasingly reported following advancements in next-generation sequencing technologies [27]. Most inherited head and neck PGLs are associated with the pseudohypoxia pathway (cluster 1), which is caused by a germline mutation in succinate dehydrogenase (SDHx) genes [28]. The five-SDH genes (SDHA, SDHB, SDHC, SDHD, and SDAF2) are collectively referred to as SDHx. SDH in the citric acid cycle converts succinate to fumarate. Mutations in any SDHx gene can result in mitochondrial dysfunction (SDH deficiency), leading to neoplasia [29]. Another 40%-50% of PPGLs exhibit somatic mutations in at least 20 known susceptibility genes [30-32]. Thus, by combining these incidences, approximately 75% of all patients with PPGLs could be assigned to one of the three molecular clusters (Table 2) [32,33]. In particular, most pediatric patients with PPGLs have detectable germline mutations. Therefore, it is strongly recommended that all pediatric patients with PGLs undergo genetic testing and whole-body imaging to detect extra-adrenal PGLs and metastatic disease [34].

Among head and neck PGLs with SDHx gene mutations (cluster 1), SDHB mutations are associated with a higher rate of metastasis than mutations in other SDHx subtypes. Therefore, whole-body functional imaging studies are recommended for patients with SDHB mutations to detect multiple primary tumors or distant metastases [21]. In addition, susceptibility to PPGLs is an established component of four genetic syndromes: multiple endocrine neoplasia types 2A and 2 B (MEN2), neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1), von Hippel–Lindau (VHL) disease, and the Carney–Stratakis dyad [35].

CLINICAL FEATURES OF CAROTID BODY PGLS

CBPs are typically painless tumors of the lateral neck, just below the mandibular angle. Most tumors are non-functional. Symptoms are rare but may result from the mass effect of the tumor. On physical examination, the lump is a rubbery, non-tender, and often pulsatile mass with lateral mobility. Furthermore, a carotid bruit may be audible over the tumor in 30%-40% of cases. In extremely rare cases, this tumor may cause headache, episodic hypertension, diaphoresis, palpitations, tachycardia, arrhythmia, and/or flushing secondary to catecholamine secretion. In patients with jugulotympanic PGLs, pulsatile tinnitus is usually present with or without conductive hearing loss, facial palsy, vertigo, or hoarseness. As described above, hereditary PGLs have clinical features and prognoses that are distinct from those of sporadic PGLs.

DIAGNOSIS OF CAROTID BODY PGLS

1) Biochemical tests for PGLs

Known biomarkers for PPGLs include chromogranin A, synaptophysin, and S100; however, these biomarkers do not allow differentiation from other neuroendocrine tumors. Accordingly, biomarkers are not commonly used for the initial diagnosis of PGLs.

Chromogranin A is a useful biomarker to distinguish patients with PPGLs from unaffected individuals [37,38]. Chromogranin A may assist in the identification of biochemically silent PGLs (associated with normal levels of metanephrine, normetanephrine, and 3-methoxytyramine levels). Additionally, serum or urine levels of catecholamine metabolites are important diagnostic tools for sympathetic PGLs.

The role of biochemical tests in diagnosing CBPs is relatively low because most head and neck PGLs are non-secretory tumors. Approximately 4% of these tumors show symptoms related to clinically significant catecholamine excess [12]. To date, there has only been one anecdotal report of a dopamine-secreting PGL in the head and neck region [39]. Although most CBPs are non-secretory tumors, plasma and urine catecholamines/metanephrines should be assessed to exclude tumor secretory activity, especially in genetically predisposed patients with CBPs.

The recommended biochemical screening tests for PPGLs include plasma-free metanephrine or urinary fractionated metanephrine levels [40]. Metanephrines are the metabolites of epinephrine and norepinephrine, whereas 3-methoxytyramine is a metabolite of dopamine. Plasma 3-methoxytyramine levels can be elevated in patients with PPGLs owing to excessive dopamine production. Importantly, this finding is associated with a high risk of metastatic PPGLs [41]. Plasma concentrations of normetanephrine, metanephrine, or 3-methoxytyramine more than two-fold above the upper cut-off levels for the reference intervals indicate a high probability of PPGLs, even at a low pre-test prevalence of the disease [41,42]. Of note, for an accurate biochemical test, the appropriate use of measurement methods is required along with the implementation of recommended precautions before blood sampling.

2) Imaging studies

Imaging studies are important for the diagnosis of PGLs to assess the localization, extension, and multiplicity of the tumor, as well as the presence of metastasis. In addition to anatomical assessments, functional imaging tools can be used to determine treatment strategies for tumors.

Various imaging techniques are employed for the anatomical assessment of the CBPs (e.g., contrast-enhanced computed tomography [CT], magnetic resonance imaging [MRI], ultrasound [US], and carotid angiography). Among these, contrast-enhanced CT is the first-line anatomical imaging modality. MRI is also a good imaging tool when patients are poor candidates for contrast-enhanced CT (e.g., children, pregnant women, patients with known germline mutations, and those with recent excessive radiation exposure).

Although US is not as sensitive as contrast-enhanced CT or MRI for the diagnosis of CBPs, this technique can easily detect a highly vascular mass lesion at the carotid bifurcation. Arteriography is not routinely performed to diagnose CBPs. Nonetheless, arteriography can be used for preoperative tumor embolization or to assess tumor-feeding arteries before surgery. A typical intense tumor blush and feeding vessels from the external carotid artery (ECA) are noted on an arteriogram. Anatomical imaging studies is also useful for assessing the surgical feasibility or potential difficulties of tumor resection based on tumor extension and invasion into the surrounding neurovascular structures. In the assessment of patients at high risk of metastasis, CT is the preferred modality over MRI for detecting lung metastases, while MRI provides superior sensitivity in detecting head and neck PGLs [40].

The Shamblin classification is used to predict and categorize the surgical complexity of the tumor before the surgical treatment of CBPs [43]. This classification is based on tumor involvement in the surrounding carotid arteries (Fig. 2) [43]. Lozano-Corona et al. [44] reported on the use of three-dimensional volumetric reconstruction (3DVR) in the preoperative CT angiographic studies of 57 CBPs and correlated the findings with estimated blood loss (EBL), duration of surgery, and length of hospital stay. They reported a significant positive correlation between 3DVR and EBL.

According to a recent analysis of preoperative images to predict surgical complications following CBP resection, distance to the base of skull (DTBOS) and tumor volume have been suggested as new predictors of surgical complications in addition to the Shamblin classification [45,46]. Kim et al. [47] reported that every 1 cm decrease in DTBOS was associated with a 1.8-fold increase in the risk of >250 mL of blood loss and a 1.5-fold increase in the risk of cranial nerve injury. They concluded that both the Shamblin grading and DTBOS were significantly correlated with EBL during surgery and cranial nerve injuries, whereas tumor volume was significantly associated with EBL.

Functional imaging should be performed as a method complementary to anatomical imaging to provide specific information regarding the functional characteristics of the tumor. Nuclear medicine techniques, such as single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) and positron emission tomography (PET), are common functional imaging modalities. These imaging modalities can detect radiotracers that are absorbed by tumor cells or bound to peptide receptors on their membranes. Combining anatomical imaging (e.g., CT) with functional imaging can increase the detection rates of primary and metastatic PGLs. Clinical indications for functional imaging include initial diagnosis, tumor staging at presentation, restaging and follow-up after surgery, and selection for targeted molecular RT in patients with inoperable/metastatic PGLs, particularly in those at a high risk of metastasis (e.g., large tumors, extra-adrenal, bilateral, or hereditary PGLs).

Various radiotracers are used for the functional imaging of PGLs to target specific sites. When whole-body screening is required to detect multiple primary tumors or distant metastases, 123I-MIBG scintigraphy/SPECT, 68Ga-DOTA-somatostatin analog (SSAs) PET/CT, or 18F-DOPA PET/CT are commonly used depending on the tumor genotype, location, and clinical situation. In all patients with PGLs, except those at low risk of metastatic disease, whole-body CT, MRI, or radionuclide imaging is recommended before surgery to exclude metastatic disease or multiplicity, which may influence surgical treatment decisions [52].

3) Genetic testing

PGLs are among the most heritable tumors. Consultation with a genetic specialist is essential for evaluating hereditary PGLs, and genetic testing should be considered for first-degree relatives of patients with hereditary PGLs [53].

Most hereditary PGLs cases are attributed to pathogenic variants of SDHD, SDHB, SDHC, VHL, and NF1 [17]. The Korean PPGL Task Force recommends 10 basic panel genes for routine germline screening: FH, MAX, NF1, RET, SDHA, SDHB, SDHC, SDHD, TMEM127, and VHL [40].

4) Needle biopsy

Biopsy is usually not recommended, and often contraindicated, for the diagnosis of head and neck PGLs, even with an image-guided procedure, owing to significant risks of adverse effects, such as neck hematoma and cranial nerve injury.

DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSIS OF CERVICAL PGLS

In the jugular and carotid regions, jugular schwannoma, sympathetic chain PGL, meningioma, skull base tumors, and lymph node metastasis should be considered as differential diagnoses.

Jugulotympanic PGL typically presents as a middle ear mass, resulting in tinnitus (80%) and hearing loss (60%). The cranial nerves in the jugular foramen (i.e., the glossopharyngeal, vagus, and spinal accessory nerves) may be compressed, resulting in swallowing difficulties or ipsilateral weakness of the upper trapezius and sternocleidomastoid (SCM) muscles.

When a tumor is located around the carotid bifurcation, vagal schwannoma, lymph node metastasis, or sympathetic chain PGLs should be differentiated from CBPs. Thus, it is important to determine the differentiation points between CBP and vagal schwannomas. Notably, schwannomas originate from Schwann cells in the nerve sheath and exhibit slow growth rates. Schwannomas may occur in the cervical region, affecting cranial nerves, such as the vagus, glossopharyngeal, spinal accessory, and hypoglossal nerves. Among these, vagal schwannomas are the most common tumors that occur in the parapharyngeal space. As CBPs and schwannomas differ in their natural courses, treatment strategies, and postoperative complications, an accurate preoperative diagnosis is critical. Schwannomas typically present as homogeneously enhancing masses with cystic changes on conventional MRI. In contrast, a CBPs typically demonstrate heterogeneous enhancement with a “salt-and-pepper” appearance and may exhibit necrotic or cystic changes [54,55].

Fig. 4 shows the characteristic imaging features of a CBP and a vagal schwannoma [56]. The vagus nerve runs through the carotid sheath between the carotid artery and internal jugular vein (IJV). Therefore, a vagal schwannoma typically displaces the internal carotid artery/common carotid artery (ICA/CCA) anteriorly and the IJV posteriorly, and exhibits a tail-like extension on its superior and inferior aspects, suggestive of an entering and exiting nerve [57]. In contrast, sympathetic chain schwannoma, another differential diagnosis in this region, displaced the ICA and IJV in the same direction due to anatomical location of the cervical sympathetic nerves [58].

CBPs arise at the carotid bifurcation and show the characteristic splaying of the internal and external carotid arteries. These tumors are highly vascular. They typically exhibit bright and rapid enhancement on a CT image faster than a schwannoma, and a characteristic “salt and pepper sign” on T2-weighted MRI, confirming the high vascularity in these tumors [59].

TREATMENT OF CAROTID BODY PGLS

The management of head and neck PGLs is increasingly individualized depending on tumor size, location, growth rate, patient age, genetic associations, and comorbidities. Treatment options vary, including active surveillance (“watchful waiting”), surgical resection, radiotherapy (RT), PRRT, ablation, and systemic chemotherapy [13]. Surgical resection is the standard treatment for localized head and neck PGLs. Although CBPs are slow-growing tumors, early surgical removal is recommended for several reasons: smaller tumors are easier to remove; the tumor may become locally invasive; the tumor may grow into the skull base, making removal difficult; and the tumor may metastasize [60].

Surgical removal of CBP is challenging because it is adherent to the carotid artery and cervical cranial nerves, and the tumor has high vascularity. Compared to carotid endarterectomy, CBP resection usually requires more time and carries a greater risk of bleeding or cranial nerve injury. Hence, preoperative assessment of tumor volume and extent is essential for predicting the surgical complexity of the tumor.

Preoperative tumor embolization can reduce intraoperative blood loss [61]. However, its benefits remain controversial. Some authors have reported beneficial effects, such as a lower incidence of vascular injury and blood loss, and shorter operation duration, while others highlighted that the benefits are not statistically significant, especially in relation to cranial nerve palsy, stroke, and length of hospital stay [62,63]. If preoperative embolization is planned, performing embolization as a “same-day procedure” with tumor resection is recommended [64]. The biggest reason against performing preoperative tumor embolization is the potential risk of stroke, although the frequency of this occurring is very low.

SURGICAL RESECTION OF CAROTID BODY PGLS

For CBP resection, an oblique skin incision is made along the anterior border of the SCM muscle or a transverse cervical incision centered on the carotid bifurcation is used. Tumor size, cephalad extension of the tumor, and the possibility of carotid reconstruction should be considered preoperatively when choosing the type of skin incision.

When the parotid gland encounters the upper end of a skin incision, it is retracted anteriorly. The great auricular nerve is identified close to the external jugular vein behind the angle of the mandible, which should be preserved during dissection and retraction of the wound. Compression or cauterization injury of the marginal mandibular branch of the facial nerve should be avoided during upward traction of the skin edge close to the mandible.

The carotid sheath, which contains the CCA, IJV, and vagus nerve, is encountered after dividing the common facial vein across the carotid bifurcation. The carotid sheath opens along the IJV, and the vein is retracted laterally to expose the carotid artery bifurcation. The proximal CCA is encircled with a vessel loop in preparation for proximal control of the vessel and possible carotid shunt insertion in case of unexpected carotid artery injury. During CBP resection, careful tumor dissection is the most important technical step for avoiding injury to the carotid artery or cranial nerves. Surgical resection is usually performed along the plane between the carotid artery and tumor using fine bipolar cauterization and individual ligation of multiple tumor-feeding vessels (Fig. 5). Gordon-Taylor [65] described a safe dissection plane (“white line”) along the sub-adventitial layer of the carotid artery. Other authors have recommended keeping the dissection plane along the outside of the adventitia of the carotid artery [66]. When the tumor invades the adventitia, dissection is performed in the subadventitial plane [66,67].

If further cephalad extension is required for tumor resection, the digastric muscle is divided along with the stylohyoid muscle. During CBP resection, the vagus, hypoglossal, and superior laryngeal nerves must be identified and preserved. The vagus nerve runs within the carotid sheath between the IJV and CCA, whereas the hypoglossal nerve runs transversely at the superior aspect of the tumor. The superior laryngeal nerve can be identified after elevating the tumor from its bed between the ICA and ECA (Fig. 6) [68].

If the tumor invades the ECA, it can be excised alongside the tumor without arterial reconstruction. However, if the tumor invaded the ICA or carotid bifurcation, the invaded arterial wall was resected along with the tumor and ICA is usually reconstructed using either a vein or a prosthetic graft. The long-term patency of polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) grafts is comparable with that of autogenous vein grafts in carotid artery construction [69]. The ascending pharyngeal artery is a branch of the ECA that runs deep into the ECA, just distal to the carotid bifurcation. This branch represents the most prominent feeding artery to the CBP and should be meticulously ligated and divided to avoid avulsion injury of the carotid wall during tumor retraction.

In current practice, key technical points for CBP resection can be summarized as follows: sufficient exposure of the carotid arteries, preparedness for unexpected carotid injury, keeping the avascular dissection plane between the carotid artery and tumor, the use of bipolar cautery or another energy device to avoid neurovascular injury, securing individual suture ligations of the feeding branches during tumor dissection, and the identification and preservation of the cervical cranial nerves (e.g., the vagus, hypoglossal, and superior laryngeal nerves).

Skull base and neck PGLs most frequently metastasize to the cervical lymph nodes. Therefore, some surgeons include ipsilateral regional neck lymph node (LN) dissection as part of the surgery in case of occult metastatic disease [70]. When a tumor encases the cranial nerves, every attempt should be made to preserve the nerves even when the tumor is split using bipolar forceps.

Most CBPs are unilateral. Bilateral tumors (Fig. 3) represent approximately 5% of all affected patients and occur frequently (32%) in patients with SDH variant mutations [71]. The recommended treatment for bilateral CBPs is staged surgical resections. In particular, resection of the smaller tumor is usually recommended first. This is because if surgical complications, such as vocal cord palsy, occur (more likely with larger tumors), accomplishing the second surgery would be difficult.

SURGICAL COMPLICATIONS

Bleeding and cranial nerve injury are the two most common complications of CBP surgical resection. A large amount of intraoperative bleeding is a well-known surgical complication of CBP resection, especially in patients with large Shamblin class 3 tumors that extend to the skull base. As described above, routine preparation for unexpected carotid tears by encircling the CCA, ICA, and ECA with vessel loops is recommended before tumor dissection from the carotid bifurcation. The author prefers to use 6-0 prolene suture ligation of the feeding branches close to the carotid wall, rather than simple ligation or cauterization. In the event of an unexpected carotid artery tear during tumor dissection, finger compression, intravenous heparin administration, and control of the carotid arteries using pre-encircled vessel loops are performed to repair the arterial injury site. If prolonged carotid clamping is required, carotid shunting is performed from the CCA to the ICA, and meticulous repair of the arterial tear site can be performed. Hurried and blind clamping of the carotid artery carries the risk of stroke and cranial nerve injury.

Intraoperative, direct or compressive injury to the cervical cranial nerves can cause temporary or permanent cranial nerve palsy, such as hoarseness, abnormality in tongue motion, or difficulty in swallowing. Notably, vagus nerve injury results in dysphagia and dysphonia. A high vagus nerve injury results in more significant symptoms than a recurrent laryngeal nerve deficit. Hypoglossal nerve injury can lead to weakness and atrophy of the tongue muscles, as well as tongue deviation toward the ipsilateral side. Superior laryngeal nerve is a branch of the vagus nerve. Traditionally, superior laryngeal nerve palsy has been thought to limit the production of high-pitched sounds. However, more recent research has revealed multiple roles of this nerve in swallowing, laryngeal cough reflex, and speech patterns [72]. Injury to the cervical sympathetic nerve can cause Horner syndrome (ipsilateral eyelid ptosis, myosis, and facial anhidrosis) or first-bite syndrome (severe sharp pain in the parotid region at the first bite of each meal). When the great auricular nerve or marginal mandibular branch of the facial nerve is damaged, numbness of the ipsilateral earlobe or lower lip weakness, can arise, leading to an asymmetrical smile. Notably, although stroke due to carotid artery injury or cerebral embolization is a rare complication of CBP surgery in current practices, it remains a devastating potential complication.

A baroreceptor embedded in the carotid bifurcation enables the circulatory system to maintain blood pressure, heart rate, and blood volume within a physiologically normal range. However, baroreflex dysfunction following bilateral carotid body resection has rarely been reported. The symptoms of baroreflex dysfunction include headaches, excessive sweating, hypertension, and tachycardia in response to stress, anxiety, and emotional lability [73]. A long-term mortality study of 384 consecutive bilateral carotid body removal surgeries performed by the Medicare Appeals Council did not reveal an increase in mortality. Physiological studies in patients with severe COPD indicate that bilateral CBP resection results in further hypoxemia and hypercapnia, with no further impairment of pulmonary function [74,75]. This can be attributed to the large number of redundant chemoreceptors present in the body.

TREATMENT OF LOCALLY ADVANCED OR METASTATIC PGLS

According to the PPGL management guidelines [76], surgery for non-functioning head and neck PGLs must be carefully considered, weighing the benefits from curative resection and the risks of surgery-related morbidities, especially cranial nerve injuries when resecting vagal and jugular PGLs. Surgical resection of tumors with regional LN dissection is generally the first-line treatment for recurrent PGLs without distant metastasis. In patients with advanced cervical PGLs, surgery is not recommended because it offers no clear advantage and carries a high risk of serious surgical complications.

Although PGL are not radiosensitive tumors, external beam RT or nonsurgical local ablation therapy can be attempted for patients with locally advanced PGLs. Considering the tumor response rates to RT, RT is insufficient as a radical treatment for CBPs [77,78]. Furthermore, preoperative RT may render the surgery more difficult because of radiation-induced fibrosis. Therefore, RT is usually reserved for patients deemed poor candidates for surgery, such as those with large, unresectable, or recurrent PGLs. Treatment results with newer radiation modalities such as proton beams, carbon-ion beams, and robotic stereotactic RT (CyberKnife) are currently being assessed [79].

Various targeted radionuclide therapies (molecular RT) using 131I-MIBG, high specific activity (HSA) 131I-MIBG [80], and 177Lu-DOTATATE PRRT [81,82] are other important treatment options for patients with progressive and symptomatic metastatic disease.

Chemotherapy (e.g., cyclophosphamide, vincristine, and dacarbazine) or antiangiogenic treatment (e.g., sunitinib) [83] can be attempted in patients with progressive metastatic diseases. The U.S. Food and Drug Administration has approved Azedra (iobenguane I-131; 131I-MIBG) to treat patients who are at least 12 years of age, and who have been diagnosed with iobenguane scan–positive, locally advanced unresectable, or metastatic PPGL. However, in August 2023, the pharmaceutical company (Lantheus) announced that the production of Azedra (iobenguane I-131) would be discontinued in 2024.

In patients with PGLs, distant metastases are defined as the presence of chromaffin cells in tissues that normally do not contain chromaffin cells (e.g., bone and lymph nodes) [84]. The majority of metastases occur in the local and distant lymph nodes, bones, liver, and lungs. PGL cells appear to spread via lymphatic and hematogenous routes [85].

Skull base and neck PGLs most frequently metastasize to cervical lymph nodes [86]. In contrast, PGLs located below the neck often metastasize to the bones, liver, and lungs. Thus, it may be challenging to differentiate between metastatic and multifocal primary PGL lesions in patients with suggestive PGL lesions in the lungs or liver, especially if the past medical history is negative because the lungs and liver are considered to contain ganglia [87,88]. The identification of metastases is not straightforward, particularly in patients with genetic predisposition syndromes, because these patients may present with multifocal primary tumors rather than metastatic PGLs. Therefore, the identification of PGL lesions in unusual locations (e.g., the lungs and liver) is generally not regarded as metastasis, as these are more likely to be multiple PGLs [6].

Familial PGLs with SDHB mutations have the highest risk of metastasis, whereas approximately 10% of PCCs exhibit metastasis [41,89,90]. It is essential to recognize that all patients with PGLs are at risk of recurrence, even after complete surgical resection. In other words, PGL has metastatic potential. The known risk factors for metastasis in patients with PGLs are large sized (≥5-6 cm) tumors, extra-adrenal locations, a noradrenergic or dopaminergic phenotype at the initial presentation, high chromogranin A levels, young (<20 years) age of onset, multiple tumors, and, most importantly, pseudohypoxia cluster 1-related germline mutations (especially SDHA/B) [41,49,89,91-93]. Therefore, in patients with these risk factors, more rigorous preoperative workup, more regular postoperative follow-up to detect metastatic lesions, and family screening, are required.

Additionally, several attempts have been made to devise a histological scoring system to predict the metastatic behavior of PGLs (Table 5) [94-97]. However, none of these scoring systems is currently endorsed in the practice guidelines for managing PGLs [98].

POSTOPERATIVE FOLLOW-UP

It should be emphasized that all patients with a history of PGL are at risk of recurrence or metastasis, even after complete surgical resection [52,84]. Therefore, postoperative follow-up is important, even after curative surgical resection of CBPs.

This is especially important for patients with a high metastatic risk. Moreover, lifelong surveillance is recommended for all high-risk patients, which should include at least one annual clinical investigation and assessment of urinary or plasma metanephrine and plasma 3-methoxytyramine levels. In patients with elevated metanephrine levels before PGL surgery, plasma or urine metanephrine and plasma 3-methoxytyramine levels should be reassessed during pain-free recovery at 3–6 weeks postoperatively. Similarly, in patients with elevated chromogranin A levels before PGL surgery, chromogranin A levels should be measured again 3-6 weeks after resection. In patients with non-functional PGLs (no tumoral synthesis of catecholamines) or persistently elevated postoperative metanephrines or 3-methoxytyramine, imaging should be performed 3-4 months after the presumed complete resection of the tumor (if possible, with the recommended radionuclide imaging, see above) [98].

Functional imaging can be helpful during follow-ups. Experts in the field [99] recommend the use of 123I-MIBG scintigraphy when the risk of metastasis or disease recurrence is high, whereas 18F-FDG PET/CT is indicated only for established metastatic disease. The use of more than one functional imaging modality may be considered in selected cases, such as the use of both 68Ga-DOTATATE and 18F-FDG PET/CT in patients with small lesions, when there is a high likelihood of metastatic disease, and in patients with SDHx mutations [99,100].

Nölting et al. [101] suggested individualized follow-up for patients with a history of PPGLs, depending on the underlying mutation status and disease characteristics. Furthermore, they suggested performing radionuclide imaging 3-4 months after complete resection of metastatic or multiple PPGLs or in high-risk (SDHA/B) mutation carriers in the case of postoperative abnormal biochemistry or a non-functional tumor (Table 6). As a follow-up imaging tool, MRI is the preferred imaging modality to minimize radiation exposure. However, tumors in unusual locations may be missed using MRI. Furthermore, patients with long bone metastases tend to have a more favorable prognosis for survival, whereas those with soft tissue metastases typically have a poorer prognosis. The median 5-year survival rate of patients with metastatic PGLs is approximately 60%-70%, but this varies according to the study population [102].

In summary, PGLs are rare neuroendocrine tumors that arise from the paraganglia and are dispersed throughout the body. CBPs are the most common PGLs of the head and neck, and the majority of these tumors are non-functional. Approximately 40%-50% of PGLs result from pathogenic germline mutations, and all PGLs have metastatic potential. For patients with localized CBPs, curative surgical resection is the standard treatment. Other treatment options, including RT, radionuclide therapy, and chemotherapy, are used for patients with locally advanced unresectable tumors or metastases. Management of head and neck PGLs is increasingly individualized, considering tumor size, location, growth rate, patient age, genetic status, and comorbidities. Additionally, investigation of the family history of PGLs, genetic screening, and postoperative surveillance should be emphasized for patients with CBPs which are at a high risk of recurrence or metastasis.
