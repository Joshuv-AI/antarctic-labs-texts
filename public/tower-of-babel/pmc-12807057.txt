Genomic analysis and characterization of new loci associated with seed protein and oil content in soybeans
Authors: Tri D. Vuong, Liliana Florez‐Palacios, Leandro Mozzoni, Michael Clubb, Chuck Quigley, Qijian Song, Shaila Kadam, Yuxuan Yuan, Ting Fung Chan, Mohamed Abdur Rouf Mian, Henry T. Nguyen
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBreeding for increased protein without a reduction in oil content in soybeans [Glycine max (L.) Merr.] is a challenge for soybean breeders but an expected goal. Many efforts have been made to develop new soybean varieties with high yield in combination with desirable protein and/or oil traits. An elite line, R05‐1415, was reported to be high yielding, high protein, and low oil. Several significant quantitative trait loci (QTL) for protein and oil were reported in this line, but many of them were unstable across environments or genetic backgrounds. Thus, a new study under multiple field environments using the Infinium BARCSoySNP6K BeadChips was conducted to detect and confirm stable genomic loci for these traits. Genetic analyses consistently detected a single major genomic locus conveying these two traits with remarkably high phenotypic variation explained (R 2), varying between 24.2% and 43.5%. This new genomic locus is located between 25.0 and 26.7 Mb, distant from the previously reported QTL and did not overlap with other commonly reported QTL and the recently cloned gene Glyma.20G085100. Homolog analysis indicated that this QTL did not result from the paracentric chromosome inversion with an adjacent genomic fragment that harbors the reported QTL. The pleiotropic effect of this QTL could be a challenge for improving protein and oil simultaneously; however, a further study of four candidate genes with significant expressions in the seed developmental stages coupled with haplotype analysis may be able to pinpoint causative genes. The functionality and roles of these genes can be determined and characterized, which lay a solid foundation for the improvement of protein and oil content in soybeans.

INTRODUCTION

Cultivated soybean [Glycine max (L.) Merr.] is a unique leguminous crop with 38%–42% of high‐quality vegetable protein and 18%–22% of edible oil content in seeds that provide invaluable nutrient sources for human consumption, animal feed, and also for many industrial applications. Among these, meal protein is a primary dietary requirement for the livestock industry that accounts for 68% of total global plant‐based protein consumption (Wilson, 2008; Zhang et al., 2015), while soybean oil, as a by‐product of meal production, accounts for more than half of global oilseed production (Seo et al., 2019).

Seed protein and oil content are quantitatively inherited traits and are considerably affected by various environmental conditions. For decades, several genetic and genomic studies have been conducted to determine the genetic control and identify quantitative trait loci (QTL)/genes that underly these complex traits using conventional linkage mapping or association analysis approach (Patil et al., 2017a). Among genetic mapping studies, Diers et al. (1992) first reported two significant QTL for protein and oil content on chromosomes (Chrs.) 15 and 20 (formerly linkage groups E and I, respectively). Between these, a major QTL on Chr. 20 showed a larger additive effect on seed protein content and a favorable allele inherited from a wild soybean (Glycine soja) accession, PI 468916. Since then, many efforts have been made to detect additional QTL for these traits. Along with several other significant QTL detected on different chromosomes, a major QTL associated with these traits was commonly mapped to overlapping genomic regions on Chr. 20 in various genetic backgrounds and environments (Chung et al., 2003; Nichols et al., 2006; Panthee et al., 2005; Pathan et al., 2013; Patil et al., 2018; Phansak et al., 2016; Warrington et al., 2015; T. Zhang et al., 2021). This QTL was first designated cqProt‐003 (Nichols et al., 2006) and later updated and listed as cqSeed Protein‐003 in SoyBase (https://soybase.org) (Brzostowski et al., 2017).

As an alternative genetic mapping approach, resulting from the accessibility of a large volume of single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) markers, genome‐wide association study has been widely adopted and consistently reported significant QTL on Chr. 20 for protein and oil content (Bandillo et al., 2015; Hwang et al., 2014; Lee et al., 2019; Sonah et al., 2015; Vaughn et al., 2014; T. Zhang et al., 2021). Using the whole‐genome re‐sequencing (WGRS) dataset derived from a diverse panel of 106 soybean accessions as previously reported (Patil et al., 2018), Valliyodan et al. (2016) performed a haplotype analysis and identified different haplotypes of the major protein and oil QTL on Chr. 20. The analysis indicated that a newly identified Chr. 20 QTL overlapped with the previously confirmed cqSeed Protein‐003 locus.

In an effort to further characterize this common QTL, Fliege et al. (2022) conducted fine mapping and were able to narrow the genomic region down from a 31.74‐ to 31.82‐Mbp interval (Nichols et al., 2006) to a 77.8kb interval. Based on the sequence polymorphism and structural variance analyses of the candidate genes harbored in this interval, the Glyma.20G85100 was identified as a causative gene that encodes a transposon insertion within the CCT domain protein, accounting for high/low protein alleles of the QTL cqSeed Protein‐003. In another study, Marsh et al. (2022) used the WGRS dataset of the soybean pan‐genome (Bayer et al., 2021) to conduct a comprehensive haplotype analysis of the cqSeed Protein‐003 locus and concluded that structural variance in the second exon of the gene Glyma.20G85100 was strongly correlated with the high‐protein phenotypes and likely represented causal variation. Recently, Goettel et al. (2022) reported that a domestication gene, POWR1, has a large effect on protein and oil QTL, which was also located on Chr. 20.

It has been reported that exotic germplasm is an invaluable source of high protein, which is greatly beneficial for protein improvement in soybeans. Among these, a Korean soybean cultivar Danbaekkong (PI 619083, MG IV) with 51% (dry weight basis [DW]) of seed protein has been reported and utilized in genetic analyses and breeding programs (Kim et al., 1996). Analyzing a mapping population derived from a cross between cv. Benning and cv. Danbaekkong, Warrington et al. (2015) reported four QTL for crude protein on Chrs. 14, 15, 17, and 20. Among these, Chr. 20 QTL explained up to 55% of phenotypic variation (PV). Since then, a positive allele derived from this exotic accession has been deployed in soybean breeding programs, leading to the development of several high‐protein elite lines (Mian et al., 2017).

Besides the Chr. 20 QTL reported in PI 468916 and Danbaekkong, a recessive wp allele, which confers pink flower color and mapped to Chr. 02 (Stephens et al., 1993), was also reported to be associated with increased protein and decreased oil content in some soybean lines (Hegstad et al., 2000). Recently, Brzostowski et al. (2017) evaluated the impacts of favorable alleles from those three sources on seed composition and agronomic traits in northern US soybean backgrounds. The analyses reported an increase in protein content but a decrease in seed yield in breeding lines with the presence of the HP allele at the cqSeed Protein‐003 locus, whereas the effects of the wp allele on protein and yield‐related traits were variable among genetic backgrounds.

Many efforts have been made to overcome the drawbacks of pleiotropic effects or yield drag of reported QTL by exploring diverse germplasm or available breeding elite lines. Among these, P. Chen et al. (2011) reported a germplasm line R05‐1415 with 47% (DW) of seed protein, which was 7% greater than that of the checks, as a new promising high‐protein source. This line was subsequently used to develop an advanced inbred line population for genetic analysis of seed protein and oil content (Wang et al., 2015a; Wang et al., 2015b). The analyses identified two QTL associated with these traits on Chrs. 5 and 20. Between these, the locus on Chr. 5 showed small and inconsistent effects among environments, while another major QTL with R 2 values of 27%–42% was mapped to Chr. 20 in a 13.2‐cM interval. Although this newly identified QTL overlapped with the previously reported Chr. 20 QTL regions (Chung et al., 2003; Nichols et al., 2006; Panthee et al., 2005; Pathan et al., 2013; Patil et al., 2018; Phansak et al., 2016; Warrington et al., 2015; T. Zhang et al., 2021), results showed that it was located in a fairly large genomic interval of 1219 kbp (soybase.org, Wm82.a2.v1 assembly) with low marker polymorphism and flanked by the simple sequence repeat (SSR) markers, Satt451 and Satt614. Apparently, it is a great challenge for the molecular breeding implementation to improve protein/oil in soybeans.

For decades, both bead chip‐ or sequencing‐based genotyping technologies that can generate a large volume of quality SNP datasets greatly facilitated genetic analyses and the identification of candidate gene(s) in various crop plants, including soybeans. However, each genotyping platform has advantages and disadvantages that require careful comparisons and evaluations to choose a cost‐effective and efficient method for QTL mapping and other genomic studies (Vuong et al., 2021). In this study, we report the results of a genetic analysis using the Infinium BARCSoySNP6K BeadChips and the detection of a new major QTL for protein and oil content in a recombinant inbred line (RIL) population derived from a high‐protein germplasm line R05‐1415. The analysis also led to the identification of candidate genes harbored in this QTL region and laid a solid foundation for haplotype analysis and functionality study.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Plant materials and field experiments

A mapping population was developed from a cross of R05‐1415 and R05‐638 as previously described (Wang et al., 2015a). These parental lines were selected based on their good contrasting protein and oil profiles. R05‐1415 was registered as PI 659697 with high‐protein (46.9% dry matter basis [DB]) and low oil (17.1% DB) content and was derived from an MFS 591 × V96‐4486 cross (P. Chen et al., 2011). Line R05‐638 with low protein (LP; 41% DB) and high oil (HO; 21% DB) content was derived from an R98‐2390F × R96‐1559 cross (Dr. Chen, personal communication, 2018).

For field evaluations, 214 F5:6 RILs of this population along with their two parents were grown as single rows in Fayetteville, AR, in 2014. In the winter of 2014/2015, these lines were grown as single rows in the soybean nursery in Costa Rica for population advancement. In 2015 and 2016, the RILs were grown as a randomized complete block design (RCBD) with two replications at two locations, Stuttgart and Fayetteville, AR. The plots consisted of 3‐m single rows spaced 0.76 m apart in Stuttgart and 0.91 m in Fayetteville. In the following years, 2017 and 2018, this F6:8 RIL population was also grown as an RCBD with two replications in 2‐m single rows spaced 0.76 m apart in the Fisher Delta Research, Extension and Education Center (FDREEC), the University of Missouri, Portageville, MO.

Marker genotyping and genetic linkage analysis

Marker genotyping was performed in the Soybean Genomics and Improvement Laboratory, USDA‐ARS, Beltsville, MD, using the Illumina Infinium BARCSoySNP6K BeadChips (Illumina, Inc.) as previously described (Song et al., 2020). Subsequently, SNP alleles were called using GenomeStudio Genotyping Module 2.0 (Illumina, Inc.). A genetic linkage map was constructed using JoinMap 4.0 (van Ooijen, 2006). A likelihood of odds (LOD) threshold score of 3.0 and a maximum genetic distance of 50 cM were used for the initial linkage grouping of markers. The soybean genetic linkage groups were then assigned to corresponding chromosome number as described in the public soybean database (https://www.soybase.org).

Protein and oil quantification and phenotypic data analysis

The total protein and oil content of RILs and their parental lines harvested from the multi‐environment field evaluations were quantified using a FOSS near‐infrared reflectance (NIR) spectroscopy system Model 6500 (FOSS, Nils Foss Alle 1, DK‐3400), following a well‐established assay protocol in the Soybean Breeding and Genetic Program in the Fisher Delta Research, Extension and Education Center, the University of Missouri, Portageville, MO. Briefly, ∼25–30 g of whole seed samples were scanned five times using an NIR equation. Averaged measurements were reported on 13% moisture basis and then converted to g kg−1 on moisture‐free basis. A total of six phenotypic datasets, referred to as CR_14 (grown in Costa Rica in the winter of 2014–2015), FAY_14, FAY_15, FAY_16 (grown in Fayetteville, AR, in 2014, 2015, and 2016), DC_17, DC_18 (grown in the Fisher Delta Research, Extension and Education Center in 2017 and 2018), were subsequently used for statistical and genetic analyses.

Seed protein and oil content of each dataset were subjected to a normality test using the PROC UNIVARIATE procedure of SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute) in which the Shapiro–Wilk (w) statistic was measured to determine the normal distribution of protein content (Elliott, 1999). An analysis of variance (ANOVA) was then conducted using the PROC MIXED procedure of SAS 9.4 in which a linear statistical model contained the effects of replication within environment (R), genotype (G), environment (E), and genotype × environment interaction (G × E). The variance components of G, E, and G × E were considered fixed effects. The broad‐sense heritability (h 2 b ) of protein content was estimated based on the expected means squares derived from the ANOVA procedure (Nyquist, 1991).

Quantitative trait loci analysis of protein and oil content

A comprehensive analysis approach to detect significant QTL for protein and oil content was performed as previously described (Vuong et al., 2010, 2021) for each individual environment and across six environments. Briefly, an initial interval mapping for preliminary QTL detection was carried out using the MapQTL5.0 software (van Ooijen, 2004). A composite interval mapping (CIM) was then conducted using the multi‐QTL method with the appropriate co‐factor selection. A permutation test (Churchill & Doerge, 1994) with 1000 runs was estimated to determine the Pr. = 0.05 genome‐wide significance level for declaring a QTL as significant. The proportion of the phenotypic variance explained by the QTL effects was estimated by CIM at the QTL peaks. Additive (A) effect and favorable allele of significant QTL were determined from an output of the program MapQTL 5.0.

Association analysis was also conducted to validate genomic loci newly identified by the traditional linkage mapping. The entire SNPs derived from the Illumina Infinium BARCSoySNP6K BeadChips were filtered and subsequently analyzed using the SNP Variation & Suites program (Golden Helix) as previously described (Do et al., 2019). Significant genomic loci were visualized in Manhattan plots using SoyBase Wm82 Genome Browser version 2 (https://soybase.org).

Identification of candidate genes and synteny analysis

Within the 1‐LOD confidence interval of the identified QTL region on Chr. 20, ∼1430‐kb upstream and downstream of the QTL peak were scanned to identify candidate genes. Their gene ontology annotations were obtained based on the descriptions in the SoyBase browser (https://soybase.org). Predicted gene models were also explored and compared using both Williams 82 reference genome assemblies, Wm82.a2.v1 and Wm82.a4.v1, by performing synteny analysis through SoyBase (https://soybase.org) and local blast (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi) by blasting and comparing gene start and end positions. Available gene expressions were also obtained from soybase.org and ePlant (Severin et al., 2010; Waese et al., 2017) (http://bar.utoronto.ca/eplant_soybean/).

Phylogenetic analysis

Phylogenetic analysis was conducted using a subset of 62 soybean accessions (Nguyen Lab, unpublished data), which consisted of the two parental lines of the current study, released breeding lines, high‐protein (HP) cultivars, plant introductions (PIs) reported as new HP sources, and landraces with normal protein content. The analysis adopted a developed genomic analysis pipeline. Briefly, the short sequence reads of the 62 selected soybean lines were aligned to the soybean cv. Williams 82 genome assembly version 4 (Valliyodan et al., 2019) using BWA mem (v0.7.17‐r1188) (Li, 2013). Picard (v2.20.4) was used to mark duplications. SNP calling followed the best practice pipeline of the Genome Analysis Toolkit (v3.8‐1‐0) (Van der Auwera et al., 2013). BCFtools (v1.8) (Li, 2011) was used to filter SNPs with parameter settings as “filter ‐e ‘DP < 10 || QD < 2.0 || MQ < 40.0 || FS > 60.0 || MQRankSum ← 12.5 || ReadPosRankSum ← 8.0.’” Plink (v1.90b618) (Chang et al., 2015) was used to prune SNPs with parameter settings as “–maf 0.05 –geno 0.1 –indep‐pairwise 50 10 0.2.” Eventually, 15,352 high‐quality SNPs remained to be used in IQ‐TREE (v1.6.12) (Nguyen et al., 2015) to build a phylogenetic tree. The parameter settings of IQ‐TREE were “‐m GTR ‐st DNA ‐bb 1000.” Interactive Tree of Life v6 (Letunic & Bork, 2021) was used to plot the phylogenetic tree.

Homolog analysis

Diamond (v0.9.26) (Buchfink et al., 2015) was used to build the soybean protein database based on the G. max and G. soja proteins published in SoyBase (Grant et al., 2010) (https://v1.legumefederation.org/data/v2/Glycine). The proteins extracted from the targeted Chr. 20 regions were aligned to the constructed soybean protein database using Diamond with a parameter setting as “blastp ‐f 6 ‐e 1e‐5” to check if the extracted proteins are unique or homologous to proteins in Chr. 20 or other chromosomes. From the result, the extracted proteins were uniquely present in the target region of Chr. 20.

RESULTS

Phenotypic variation of protein and oil content

Total protein and oil content quantifications of the two parental lines, R05‐1415 and R05‐638, and RIL mapping population evaluated in six environment conditions are summarized in Table 1. The highest protein content (50.1%) was observed in the FAY_14 environment, and the lowest one (46.1%) was observed in the FAY_16 environment. In contrast, the highest oil content (19.0%) was observed in the FAY_16 environment, and the lowest one (16.2%) was observed in the FAY_14 environment. The data also showed that great phenotypic variations were observed among the RILs in all environments studied, while the two parental lines retained their typical phenotypes across all six environments, for example, line R05‐1415 consistently showing HP, low oil (LO), and line R05‐638 consistently showing LP and HO (Figure 1). The frequency distribution of protein and oil among RILs showed large genetic variation and introgression (Figure 2). The normality tests using Shapiro–Wilk (w), skewness, and kurtosis statistics indicated that the protein contents of the CR_14, FAY_15, and FAY_16 environments were normally distributed, while protein phenotypes of the FAY_14, DC_17, and DC_18 environments were not. For oil content, it showed a slight difference from the protein trait. The phenotypic data of the FAY_14, FAY_15, FAY_16, and DC_17 showed normal distributions; however, the CR_14 and DC_18 environments were not normally distributed (Table S1). Estimated broad‐based heritability (h 2 b ) of both protein and oil content was rather high (Table S1).

Analysis of the phenotypic correlation between protein and oil content for each individual environment indicated that these two traits were negatively correlated as expected, including the average content as estimated across environments. Pearson correlation coefficients (r 2) were strong and highly significant (p < 0.0001), varying from −0.79 to −0.87 (Table S2A). Additionally, when analyzing correlations of protein or oil content of the RILs between environments studied, the results revealed positive and statistically significant (p < 0.0001) correlation coefficients (Table S2B). It suggested the performance of the RILs was highly consistent in various field environments.

Genetic linkage analysis

Among the marker loci of the BARCSoySNP6K BeadChips genotyped in the mapping population, 1592 SNPs (26.5%) were polymorphic and subsequently employed for constructing a genetic linkage map, which spanned over 1850 cM of the soybean genome with an average distance of 1.2 cM across 20 soybean chromosomes (Table 2). High‐density SNP marker linkages were successfully developed for most chromosomes (Figure S1). However, it was noted that due to the lack of genetic polymorphic marker loci between the parents in certain genomic regions, large genetic gaps, varying from 10 to 20 cM, were observed in many chromosomes, such as Chrs. 1, 8, 14, and 16. In addition, the lack of polymorphism also caused some unmerged linkage fragments, for instance, Chrs. 13_B and 20_B.

QTL associated with protein and oil content

Linkage analysis of protein content consistently detected a major QTL significantly associated with this trait and mapped to the same genomic region on Chr. 20 for each individual environment as well as across all six environments (Table 3 ). Logarithm of the odds (LOD) scores at the peaks varied among six environments, from 12.6 to 22.6, and were much greater than a threshold cutoff value of 4.2 at a probability of 0.01 (Pr. = 0.01) as per the genome‐wide permutation test. PV explained (R 2) with a range of 24.2%–43.5% showed large variations of protein content in the RIL population, which were greatly explained by the identified QTL. Additive effects, varying from 0.9% to 1.5%, also indicated that a favorable allele, which was inherited from the HP parent, R05‐1415, positively contributed to a reasonable increase in protein content of 2.0%–3.0% in soybean progenies. The results of the association analysis of each individual environment also confirmed the major QTL detected on Chr. 20 with significant SNPs and ‐log10 (p‐values) varying from 10.2 to 20.4. This genomic locus was located between the physical positions of Gm20:25063646 and Gm20:26785339 bp (1.77 Mb) based on Wm82.a2.v1 reference genome assembly (Figure S2).

Similar to the protein trait, linkage analysis of oil content identified only one major significant QTL that was consistently mapped to the same genomic locus on Chr. 20 for each individual and across six environments studied (Table 3). The LOD scores at the peaks ranged from 9.4 to 21.5 and were much greater with a threshold cutoff value of 4.0 (Pr. = 0.01) as per the genome‐wide permutation tests. The analyses also showed that large variations of oil QTL in the mapping population were greatly explained by PV with R 2 values of 19.1%–36.8%. Additive effects, varying from −0.5% to −0.7%, indicated that a favorable allele, which was inherited from the HO content parent, R05‐638, positively contributed to a reasonable increase in oil content of 1.0%–1.4% in soybean progenies. Association analysis of each individual environment also confirmed the same QTL detected on Chr. 20 with significant SNPs and ‐log10 (p‐values) varying from 10.5 to 18.5. The physical positions of this oil QTL were located between Gm20:25,063,646 and Gm20:26,500,747 (1.43 Mb), which was slightly different from the position of the newly identified HP QTL region. However, it overlapped the genomic region of the protein trait.

Identification of candidate genes and synteny analysis

Within the QTL confidence interval, 34 candidate genes were identified based on the Wm82.a2.v1 reference genome assembly (Table 4). Gene annotation analyses indicated that the candidate genes are involved in many biological processes, such as proteolysis, Golgi vesicle transport, RNA methylation, and a few others with unknown functions. Based on the gene expression database of soybeans (Severin et al., 2010; Waese et al., 2017) and e‐Plant Soybean (https://bar.utoronto.ca/eplant_soybean), four genes, Glyma.20G073000, Glyma.20G073900, Glyma.20G074000, and Glyma.20G074500, showed different expression patterns at either early or late seed developmental stages (Figure S3). Moreover, the availability of a new genome assembly of Williams 82 (Wm82.a4.v1) facilitated the synteny analysis of the identified candidate genes. The comparisons revealed remarkable changes in gene identification and expression. Among 34 identified candidate genes, 18 genes were found in the Wm82.a4.v1 assembly version. However, further analysis using gene positions indicated that besides these 18 genes, six additional genes were newly added to the new genome assembly (Figure 3). As a result, a total of 24 candidate genes were identified in the Wm82.a4.v1 genome assembly and functionally grouped either as phosphatase or phosphate transporters along with a few other genes with unknown functions (Table 5).

Phylogenetic analysis

A phylogenetic tree (Figure 4) developed from the analysis of similarity relationship showed at least six main clusters (groups) identified among 62 soybean lines studied. Within the main clusters (groups), several subgroups consisting of soybean lines with close genetic similarity were observed. The results indicated that the two parents, R05‐1415 and R05‐638, were grouped into two different main clusters. However, the HP parental line, R05‐1415, was close to a few breeding lines or other plant introductions, as shown in the same main cluster. Figure 4 reveals a phylogenetic relationship distant from Danbaekkong, an exotic soybean germplasm (https://www.ars‐grin.gov) that has been known as a high‐protein source and widely utilized in soybean breeding programs. It was also noted that most N17 breeding lines, which were derived from cv. Danbaekkong, were grouped close to their progenitor in the same main cluster. Other breeding lines of different breeding programs belonged to different main clusters.

Homolog analysis

We aligned the proteins extracted from the target Chr. 20 QTL region, in which 34 candidate genes were identified, to the published soybean protein database and examined if those extracted proteins are uniquely located on Chr. 20 or present in other soybean chromosomes. From the homolog searching using a protein alignment blast method in conjunction with other selection criteria, such as percentage of mismatching, positions of reference protein, and E‐value (Table S3), results indicated that there were no extracted proteins that were identified in other soybean chromosomes. It suggested that the target proteins are uniquely located in the Chr. 20‐HP QTL region. In other words, the proteins identified in this study are newly reported, which could be highly related to soybean protein content.

DISCUSSION

Phenotypic variation

For a genetic study of quantitative traits in crop plants, including soybean seed compositions, it has been largely expected that multi‐environmentally assessed phenotypic data should be accessed for reliable analysis and reproducible results. In our study, seed protein and oil content of the RIL population and their parental lines were evaluated in multiple field environments (years and locations) and employed for different subsequent analyses. A simple statistic summary (Table 1) indicated that the average protein and oil content of the two parents varied across environments, but the two parents, R05‐1415 and R05‐638, consistently remained as their typical phenotypes, meaning high protein/low oil versus LP/HO, respectively (Figure 1). Such consistent performance sustained good contrasting phenotypes that resulted from great parental allelic variation that facilitated the precise detection of genomic loci for protein and oil content as reported in the present study.

The normality of the quantitative traits in a mapping population is also expected for traditional QTL analysis. In our study, we observed both typical normal and non‐normal distributions, for instance, protein datasets of CR_14, FAY_15, and FAY_16, or oil datasets of FAY_14, FAY_15, and FAY_16, showed normal distributions, while the remaining were not normally distributed (Figure 2; Table S1). However, in many previous mapping studies, including seed composition traits in soybeans, a lack of normality has been commonly reported (Panthee et al., 2005; Patil et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2015a; Wang et al., 2015b; Warrington et al., 2015). For a reliable QTL detection on such occasions, the effect of non‐normality can be significantly reduced by performing genome‐wide permutation tests to determine empirically derived threshold values for LOD score significance (Churchill & Doerge, 1994), coupled with CIM and cofactor marker selection (Zeng, 1993, 1994). In this study, using original data without data transformation followed by appropriate permutation tests, cofactor analysis, and the CIM method, we successfully identified one single major QTL significantly associated with protein and oil content (Table 3; Figure S2). The results indicated that reliable QTL analysis can be conducted in spite of the non‐normality of the phenotypic data without transformation. Our results were in good agreement with previous genetic studies.

Broad‐sense heritability (h 2 b ) was estimated from the ANOVA of the protein and oil datasets evaluated in the Fisher Delta Research, Extension and Education Center, MO, in 2017 and 2018. The results indicated that h 2 b values (0.99) (Table S1) were very high and greater than those previously reported (Lee et al., 2019; Patil et al., 2017b; Wang et al., 2015a; Wang et al., 2015b; Zhang et al., 2018). This suggested that a genetic component of the HP/LO parental line, R05‐1415, played an essential role in the metabolic processes during seed developmental stages that were conducive to increased protein and oil content. These results were in good agreement with explained PV shown by great R 2 values (Table 3).

Similar to many reports in previous studies (Lee et al., 2019; Patil et al., 2017b; Zhang et al., 2018), phenotypic correlations between protein and oil content of the RILs within each individual environment were negatively correlated (Table S2A), indicating a strong pleiotropic effect of genetic factors controlling these two traits, which has been a great challenge for simultaneously improving these traits in soybeans (Bandillo et al., 2015; Lee et al., 2019; Patil et al., 2018). However, it was noted that the correlations of either protein or oil trait between environments were consistently positive (Table S2B). Therefore, despite a wide range of environments studied, the performance of the RIL population was highly consistent, which significantly contributed to the QTL detection and stability.

QTL for protein and oil content

Following the registration of this elite line as a new high‐protein source (P. Chen et al., 2011), Wang et al. (2015a) and Wang et al. (2015b) conducted genetic linkage analyses using an RIL population derived from this elite line. The analysis of seed protein content identified two QTL regions on Chrs. 5 and 20. Between these, the major QTL on Chr. 20 was consistently detected across environments; the second one on Chr. 5, however, was minor and inconsistent as evaluated among environments of year and location. Similarly, the analysis of oil content identified two QTL regions on Chrs. 5 and 20. Between these, the QTL region on Chr. 20 was consistent across environments, while the second one on Chr. 5 was minor and inconsistent.

Unlike earlier reports by Wang et al. (2015a) and Wang et al. (2015b), our study did not detect minor QTL for protein and oil in any environment we studied, but consistently detected and mapped a major genomic region for both traits on Chr. 20 (Table 3). It was speculated that Wang et al. (2015a) and Wang et al. (2015b) initially genotyped F2 segregating populations for genetic linkage analyses and subsequently phenotyped seed composition traits of advanced inbred lines. It was likely that genotype–phenotype association was not properly performed when phenotypic data and genotypic data were not concurrently generated from the same or genetically close seed generation. As a result, phenotypes assessed in later generations did not accurately represent genotypes in early generations because genetic recombination events, which resulted in allelic variations, took place during seed generation advancement from F2 to F2:4 or F2:5 for developing advanced inbred line materials. Moreover, strong environmental effects that were commonly observed in studies of complex traits in soybeans likely caused false positive detections of instable QTL (Anderson et al., 2019; Specht et al., 2014). Our results indicated that the elite line R05‐1415 had a highly inheritable allele favorable for increasing seed protein content as shown by additive effect (Table 3) and high heritability (Table S1).

Considering genetic and physical positions of the protein and oil QTL on Chr. 20, Wang et al. (2015a) and Wang et al. (2015b) reported this QTL region located in a large genetic distance of 13.2 cM, between the flanking SSR markers, Satt451 and Satt614. The physical positions of these markers were at 1,682,957 and 3,903,416 bp (GlymaWm82.a2.v1; www.soybase.org) in a 2.2‐Mb interval. It appeared that a linkage group with fewer mapped markers could not provide adequate resolution for a narrow marker interval. However, within this interval, the two other markers, Satt562 and Satt419, located at 1,882,497 and 1,885,503 bp (GlymaWm82.a2.v1), were shown to be closest to the QTL peak at a genetic location of 12.3 cM (Wanget al., 2015a; Wang et al., 2015b) that were defined as reference makers for the QTL. The analysis showed that the previously reported QTL located ∼24.8 Mb, far distant from the major Chr. 20‐QTL region, located between 25,063,646 and 26,785,339 bp, as reported in our study (Table 3, Figure S2). This indicated that newly identified QTL on Chr. 20 in our study was likely a new genomic locus associated with protein and oil content from the line R05‐1415, which has not been reported before. Moreover, when investigating a pedigree of this HP parental line (P. Chen et al., 2011), it indicated that this line was selected from an MFS‐591 × V96‐4486 cross in which line MFS‐591 was traced back to a Camp × Rocky cross. The data of the Soybean Pedigree Information (https://www.soybase.org) show that cv. Camp was among progenies selected from an original cross between cv. Essex and a G. soja accession. Thus, it was speculated that line R05‐1415 possibly has an HP allele originating from the ancestors, including a G. soja accession, of which the identification is not available.

Diers et al. (1992) first reported a major QTL for protein on Chr. 20 using an interspecific mapping population. Since then, many efforts have been made to characterize the significant genomic region from cultivated and wild soybeans and using different genomic analysis approaches. Among these, Patil et al. (2018) analyzed haplotypes of Chr. 20‐HP QTL from a wild soybean accession and found that this QTL was located in a 3.6‐Mbp interval. This interval overlapped with multiple genomic regions associated with high protein and was located between 28.7 and 33.1 Mbp (Wm82.a2.v1 assembly), as earlier reported in both linkage mapping and association analyses (Bandillo et al., 2015; Bolon et al., 2010; Hwang et al., 2014; Lee et al., 2019; Phansak et al., 2016; Vaughn et al., 2014; Warrington et al., 2015; J. Zhang et al., 2015). In continuation of further characterizing the early reported Chr. 20‐HP QTL (Diers et al., 1992), which was later designated cqPRO‐003 (Fliege et al., 2022), Nichole et al. (2006) fine mapped a 77.8‐kb interval and identified the gene Glyma.20G085100, located at 31,774–31,779 kb, encoding a transposon insertion within the CCT domain protein, which was associated with a causal allele for this HP QTL region. Interestingly, when conducting haplotype mapping of the major protein locus on Chr. 20 using the soybean pan‐genome dataset (Bayer et al., 2021), Marsh et al. (2022) also concluded that the gene Glyma.20G085100 with structural variation was strongly correlated with the HP phenotype in soybeans. In a separate study, Goettel et al. (2022) concluded the same gene Glyma.20G085100 underlying large effect on protein and oil variation and designated POWR1 for this gene. These results indicated that the Chr. 20‐HP genomic locus newly identified in our study did not overlap with the position of the commonly reported, well‐studied HP QTL that harbored the gene Glyma.20G085100.

Candidate genes and synteny analysis

Among 34 genes identified in the QTL region based on the Wm82.a2.v1 assembly, most did not have the gene expression information in the database of e‐Plant Soybean (https://bar.utoronto.ca/eplant_soybean). However, further analysis showed that a small set of four genes had expression patterns in different seed developmental stages (Figure S3). When comparing these genes between two versions of the reference genome assemblies, the synteny analysis identified 18 genes reported in the Wm82.a4.v1 assembly, including a small set of the four genes showing expression during seed development. In addition, six new genes with their biological functions, such as protein phosphatase 2C and sodium‐dependent phosphatase transporters/glycerol‐3‐phosphatase transporter, were also reported in this new genome assembly version (Table 5). However, to date, gene the expression information of these genes is not available in the e‐Plant Soybean database. It suggests that a further investigation using an integrative analysis approach, such as RNA‐Seq (H. Zhang et al., 2017) and QTL‐Seq (Singh et al., 2022), to analyze the gene expression pattern of these 24 candidate genes is warranted. Among ongoing efforts to identify candidate genes harbored in the QTL region in line R05‐1415, we have developed near‐isogenic lines for fine mapping using the residual heterozygous lines derived from the RIL population. A subset of eight SNP markers closely linked to the new QTL was utilized for genotyping to ensure the heterozygosity of the target region (the Nguyen Lab, unpublished data). We anticipate that the results of this analysis will allow us to pinpoint causal gene(s) of the HP phenotype and clarify if the new QTL region is located in a different interval from the gene Glyma.20G085100.

Homolog analyses

As indicated above, the physical position of the Chr. 20‐HP QTL detected in this study was at least 5.0 Mb apart from the gene Glyma.20G085100 (Fliege et al., 2022; Marsh et al., 2022) or other QTL reported in earlier studies (Bandillo et al., 2015; Bolon et al., 2010; Hwang et al., 2014; Vaughn et al., 2014; Warrington et al., 2015). However, there has been an inquiry about whether the newly detected QTL in this study resulted from paracentric chromosome inversion of the known genomic region in Chr. 20 during domestication or the recent breeding process in soybeans. For the investigation of the effects or roles of chromosome aberrations, different approaches have been developed in plants, including soybeans. Palmer et al. (2000) characterized pollen sterility and crossover events in F1 and F2 plants that were developed from cross‐combinations of cultivated and wild soybean accessions and heterozygous for the inversion segments. The results enabled the authors to determine that the paracentric inversions in the soybean accessions studied were identical. Recently, in a review of chromosome inversions in plants, Huang and Rieseberg (2020) mentioned various approaches employed for investigating the effects of chromosome inversions. Among these, comparative genomic studies explored high‐quality genome assemblies coupled with other genomic data and detected chromosomal rearrangement between closely related species in Arabidopsis (Zapata et al., 2016) and many crop plants (J. Chen et al., 2013; Garcia‐Mas et al., 2012; Huang et al., 2009; Qin et al., 2014; Yang et al., 2019; Zhu et al., 2019).

For soybeans, the reference genome assemblies of several Glycine accessions available in Legume Information System (https://www.legumeinfo.org/collections/glycine) provide a valuable resource for chromosome inversion studies. The analysis in our study showed that the reference proteins extracted from the HP QTL on Chr. 20 were unique and not identified on any other chromosome (Table S3), indicating there was no chromosome translocation. Moreover, the physical positions of the extracted proteins (Table S3) confirmed that the newly identified Chr. 20‐HP genomic locus did not result from a chromosome inversion occurring between this genomic locus and the adjacent chromosome segment that harbors the gene Glyma.20G085100.

CONCLUSIONS

This study reported the detection of a single major QTL on Chr. 20, conferring seed protein and oil content in an elite line R05‐1415. This new genomic region physically located ∼28‐5 Mb distant from the previously reported QTL (Wang et al., 2015a; Wang et al., 2015) and did not overlap with either other commonly reported QTL (Bandillo et al., 2015; Bolon et al., 2010; Hwang et al., 2014; Lee et al., 2019; Phansak et al., 2016; Vaughn et al., 2014; Warrington et al., 2015; J. Zhang et al., 2015) or the recently cloned gene Glyma.20G085100 (Fliege et al., 2022; Marsh et al., 2022). Further analyses also indicated that even though the new genomic region was upstream of the recently cloned gene, it did not result from the paracentric chromosome inversion with an adjacent genomic fragment that harbors the reported QTL.

With respect to protein improvement, wild soybean (G. soja) germplasm has been demonstrated to be valuable and a unique source; however, due to linkage drag on favorable agronomic traits, the deployment of these accessions for breeding has been limited. In such a circumstance, line R05‐1415, as a promising high‐yielding and favorable HP allele, which inherited its high protein from exotic germplasm, has many advantages over wild soybean for protein improvement. However, the pleiotropic effect of the QTL for protein and oil traits raises a challenge for soybean breeders to simultaneously improve these two traits. Consequently, a small set of four genes with significant expressions in the seed developmental stages in conjunction with ongoing haplotype analysis in the Nguyen Lab, MU (Dr. Nguyen, personal communication, 2022), may be able to pinpoint causative genes for protein enhancement. Moreover, with recent advances in genome editing technology, the functionality and role of the causative gene(s) can be determined and well characterized, which lay a solid foundation for the improvement of protein without a reduction in oil content using a breeding‐by‐design strategy.
