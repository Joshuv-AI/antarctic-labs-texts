Determination of the optimal resistance in RS01 for robust aerosol performance of inhaled controlled-release budesonide powder for chronic obstructive inflammatory respiratory diseases patients
Authors: Le Van Bui, Tamara Vukanovic, Tommaso Fiaschi, Romain Merlos, Thami Sebti, Yvan Vander Heyden, Karim Amighi, N. Wauthoz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chronic obstructive inflammatory respiratory diseases are among the most common non communicable respiratory diseases worldwide. Although inhaled corticosteroids are effective, inadequate inspiratory flow through an inhaler can still result in poor asthma and COPD control. The patient's inhalation pattern directly determines the Fine Particle Dose (FPD) delivered to the lungs. This study evaluates which internal resistance of the RS01 inhaler, (low, medium-low, medium, or medium-high resistance) provides the most consistent FPD, of controlled-release budesonide dry powder, across inspiratory profiles typical in asthmatic and COPD patients. Inspiratory profiles were defined by peak inspiratory flow rates (PIFR) from 30 to 100 L/min, inhaled volumes (Vi) from 1 to 5 L and times to inhale (Ti) from 1 to 5 s. They were generated using a Breath-Simulator (BRS300i) coupled to a Next Generation Impactor connected to Alberta Idealized Throat, for strong in vitro/in vivo correlations. The FPD was measured for each combination of PIFR, Vi, and Ti for the four different RS01 inhalers, using a central composite Design of Experiment setup. Results indicate that the medium-low and medium-high resistance RS01 inhalers provide the most stable FPD across the full range of inspiratory profiles. Among the both, the medium-low RS01 inhaler delivered the most stable drug amount. On the other hand, the FPD delivered with the low resistance and medium resistance RS01 inhalers was significantly influenced by PIFR. The applied approach may help to select more adequately a device for a developed dry powder formulation taking account of the patient's inspiratory profiles.

Introduction

Asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) worldwide are the most prevalent non-communicable respiratory diseases which affect hundreds of millions of people (World Health Organization, 2026). According to the World Health Organization (WHO), an estimated 363 million people in 2023 suffered from asthma and 442,000 died from it (World Health Organization, 2026). Globally, it affects up to 15% of the population across various countries, where 9.1% of the global prevalence of asthma is attributed to children, while 11% and 6.6% are to adolescents and adults, respectively [The Global Asthma report, 2022; Our World in Data, 2026]. On the other hand, COPD was the fourth leading cause of death, in 2021, affecting 400 millions people and leading to 3.5 million deaths worldwide (de Oca et al., 2025; WHO COPD, 2026). The burden of chronic obstructive inflammatory respiratory diseases is characterized by the mortality rate and disability-adjusted life years and is likely to increase over the next years. The burden of asthma is characterized by the mortality rate and disability-adjusted life years and is likely to increase over the next years (Yuan et al., 2025). Although the disability-adjusted life years decreased from 1990 to 2021 (4.80 to 2.48), asthma incidence increased by 9.4% in the same period (Feng et al., 2025; GBD 2021 Asthma and Allergic Diseases Collaborators, 2025). Similarly to asthma, the disability-adjusted life years for COPD decreased of 1.7% from 1990 to 2021 but remain the third leading cause of death worldwide (de Oca et al., 2025). For asthma, daily treatments based on inhaled corticosteroids (ICS), are prescribed for maintenance treatment, and, recently, also in reliever treatment with the formoterol long-acting β2-agonist (LABA) presenting a rapid onset of action (i.e. 5 min) (Global Initiative for Asthma, 2025). ICS are the cornerstone of asthma treatment and are generally combined with bronchodilators, such as LABA into the same inhaled medicine (Global Initiative for Asthma, 2025). In specific cases of COPD, although this disease is treated with combination of bronchodilators, whenever blood eosinophilia is above 300 cells/μl, ICS and bronchodilators combinations are prescribed (Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD), n.d.). To deliver ICS, with or without LABA, pressurized metered dose inhalers (pMDI) and dry powder inhalers (DPI) are the most used inhalation devices for reliever and maintenance treatments (Global Initiative for Asthma, 2025). As recommended by the GINA report 2025, the choice of medication, device and dose for maintenance and reliever treatment should be based on assessment of symptom control, risk factors which inhalers are available for the relevant medication class, which of these the patient can use correctly after training, their cost, their environmental impact and the patient's likely adherence (Global Initiative for Asthma, 2025). DPIs have the advantage over pMDIs to be more environmentally friendly and not to require coordination between the actuation and inhalation (Fidler et al., 2022; Kumar Subramani et al., 2024). However as DPIs are breath actuated, they require sufficient flow through the device to aerosolize and disperse the powder (Kumar Subramani et al., 2024).

However, despite the treatments available on the market are efficient, poor asthma control is observed, which increases the mortality and reduces the quality of life (Yuan et al., 2025; Melani et al., 2011; Levy et al., 2013; Westerik et al., 2016; Chan et al., 2023; Broeders et al., 2003; Usmani et al., 2018; Kocks et al., 2018; Bosnic-Anticevich et al., 2018; Sanchis et al., 2016). The main reasons for poor asthma disease control are the irregular use (poor adherence) to chronic maintenance treatment and/or crucial errors during the inhalation technique (Yuan et al., 2025; Melani et al., 2011; Levy et al., 2013; Westerik et al., 2016; Chan et al., 2023; Broeders et al., 2003; Usmani et al., 2018; Kocks et al., 2018; Bosnic-Anticevich et al., 2018; Sanchis et al., 2016; Chrystyn et al., 2017; Lavorini et al., 2008; Rootmensen et al., 2010; Giraud and Roche, 2002; Virchow et al., 2008; Dolovich et al., 2005; Luczak-Wozniak et al., 2018; Monteiro et al., 2022). One of the major crucial errors, i.e. leading to suboptimal therapeutic lung dose, is the inability to inhale with sufficient force inside the DPI or to not coordinate well the aerosol actuation and the inhalation for pMDI (Yuan et al., 2025; Levy et al., 2013; Usmani et al., 2018; Kocks et al., 2018; Dolovich et al., 2005; Luczak-Wozniak et al., 2018; Monteiro et al., 2022; Laube et al., 2011; Mahler et al., 2014; Loh et al., 2017). Up to now, it is reported for DPI that, depending on the inhaler, 32% to 90% of the patients do not use their inhalers correctly (Bosnic-Anticevich et al., 2018; Dolovich et al., 2005; Al-Showair et al., 2007). Moreover, studies have also highlighted that the adherence rate and continuation of the medication taking were higher among patients using combined medication in a single inhaler, rather than among patients who are on multi-inhaler device therapies (Monteiro et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2020).

Regarding DPIs, the patient's inspiratory flow is one of the key elements for powder dispersion within the inhaler system, leading to an optimal lung deposition (regarded as the fine particle dose, or FPD), and is strongly associated with the patient's inspiratory force (Pilcer et al., 2012). This feature often depends on disease severity, but also on patient's inhalation technique and expiration (Westerik et al., 2016; Broeders et al., 2003; Kocks et al., 2018; Lavorini et al., 2008; Loh et al., 2017). Furthermore, the severity of the disease has an impact on the inspiratory profile. Inflammation-mediated symptoms in asthma originate from the narrowing of the bronchial airways and resistance to airflow, resulting in lower lung performances, compared to healthy individuals (Global Initiative for Asthma, 2025). Moreover, in COPD, the decline in lung functions is largely irreversible (Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD), n.d.). Therefore, the ability of patients to take their medicine can vary depending on the severity of their disease (high intra- and inter-patient variabilities) and impacts the lung doses (Yuan et al., 2025; Al-Showair et al., 2007; van der Palen et al., 1995). In that regard, it is important to guarantee a constant FPD which is not influenced by the inspiratory profiles of the patient.

For that, the dry powder for inhalation should be correctly dispersed through the DPI dispersion system, regardless of the patient's inspiratory profile. The dispersion system implies impaction, inertia, and turbulent stresses to disperse and overcome cohesive/adhesive forces between the microparticles or between the microparticles and the carrier (Pilcer et al., 2012; Chrystyn, 2007; Levy et al., 2019; Haughney et al., 2010; Selvam et al., 2010; Almeida et al., 2022; Dorosz et al., 2021). The more complex the geometrics inside the inhaler, the higher its internal resistance and, often, the more efficient dispersion become (Selvam et al., 2010; Lee et al., 2018). In general, internal resistance is classified according to the airflow able to exert a pressure drop of 4 kPa through the device. An airflow of (i) 30 to 60 L/min defines a high internal resistance (0.04 kPa1/2/(L.min)) DPI, (ii) 30 to 60 L/min for a medium resistance (0.02 to 0.04 kPa1/2/(L.min)), and (iii) 90 L/min or higher for low resistance (0.008 to 0.02 kPa1/2/(L.min)) (Almeida et al., 2022; Gauani et al., 2022). However, FPD depends not only on the dispersion ability of the DPI but also on the velocity of the powder aerosol, its interaction with the DPI, the flow direction within the DPI mouthpiece, and other factors (Dorosz et al., 2021; Lee et al., 2018; Hoppentocht et al., 2014). Therefore, the interaction between the inhaled dry powder, the DPI, and the patient's inspiratory airflow that determines FPD is complex and requires experimental evaluations.

The DPI choice for our study was made on RS01 inhalers as it exists in four different internal resistances without modifying the external design for the patient (i.e. capsule reservoir, piercing mechanism and mouthpiece). With these RS01 inhalers, a pressure drop of 4 kPa is obtained with 100 L/min for low resistance, 80 L/min for medium-low resistance, 65 L/min for medium resistance and 40 L/min for medium-high resistance devices.

Usually, high-resistance DPIs disperse better than lower resistance DPIs due to higher turbulence induced by the dispersion mechanisms. This is why the Pulmicort Turbohaler®, composed mainly of microparticles, is a high-resistance DPI (Wauthoz et al., 2018). However, this DPI has shown inconsistent FPD with formoterol or fluticasone, across varying airflows generated by the asthmatic patients (Wauthoz et al., 2018; Buttini et al., 2016). Even though 30 L/min seems to be a low airflow to reach, the energy required from the patient to achieve it through a high-resistance DPI is higher than the energy required to reach 100 L/min in a low-resistance DPI (Capstick et al., 2024).

The inspiratory profile is defined using three parameters, the peak inspiratory flow rate (PIFR), the inhaled volume (Vi) and the time to inhale (Ti) (Broeders et al., 2003; Broeders et al., 2004; Dorosz et al., 2022; Prime et al., 2015). For this study, the limits of peak inspiratory flow rates (PIFR), inhaled volumes (Vi) and times to inhale (Ti), were defined according to values reported in the literature, regarding healthy/mildly to severely affected asthmatic and COPD patients (Prime et al., 2015; Horváth et al., 2017; Farkas et al., 2024). The PIFR ranges from 30 to 100 L/min considering the pressure drop (0.08 to 0.04 kPa1/2/(L.min)) in the DPIs upon inhalation in clinical studies and spirometry tests (Haughney et al., 2021; Hanon et al., 2022; Mahler, 2017; Ghosh et al., 2017; Sharma et al., 2017; Haidl et al., 2016). Ti was determined to range from 1 to 5 s based on several studies (Levy et al., 2019; Dorosz et al., 2022; Farkas et al., 2024; Haughney et al., 2021; Kondo et al., 2017; Azouz et al., 2015; Weers et al., 2015) and Vi from 1 to 5 L based on these studies (Dorosz et al., 2022; Kondo et al., 2017; Azouz et al., 2015; Chamaon et al., 2025). To generate the inhalation profile, the combination of a Next Generation Impactor (NGI), approved by the European Pharmacopeia (European Pharmacopoeia Commission, 2023), coupled to the Alberta Idealized Throat (AIT) and a breath simulator was chosen. This system can generate inhalation profiles with selected PIFR, Ti and Vi, and has already demonstrated strong in vitro-in vivo correlations (Weers et al., 2015).

Therefore, the aim of this study is to evaluate an approach to determine the most adequate DPI for a dry powder for inhalation that will deliver the most constant FPD, across the inspiratory profiles of asthmatic and COPD patients.

A BUD dry powder for inhalation developed to deliver this ICS once a day at a lower dose to decrease the frequency and the adverse side effects was used in this study (Bui et al., 2025; Bui et al., 2026). Reduced frequency and dosage could increase the patient's adherence to chronic maintenance treatment based on ICS (Chan et al., 2023; Bui et al., 2025; Bui et al., 2026; Drouin et al., 2022; Barnes, 2010).

With this approach, the most appropriate RS01 inhaler to deliver a robust FPD (in other words, a constant and stable amount of BUD), across of asthmatic and COPD inspiratory profiles is to be selected.

Materials and methods

Material

Micronized BUD, Dv90 of 2.8 μm (measured by laser diffraction), (Chemo Pharmaceuticals, Madrid, Spain) was provided in bulk. Analytical grade Isopropanol (IPA), phosphoric acid 85% and acetonitrile were purchased from Merck Millipore (Berlington, Massachusetts, USA). Hydrogenated castor oil (HCO) (Kolliwax) is purchased from BASF (Ludwigshaven, Germany), D-α-Tocopherol polyethylene glycol 1000 succinate (TPGS) was from Tokyo Chemical Industry (Zwijndrecht, Belgium), hypromellose (HPMC) Quali-V®-I capsule size 3 from Qualicaps Europe (Madrid, Spain), and Poloxamer 407 from BASF. MilliQ water is produced using a Purelab-Ultra system (Elga LabWater, Lane End, UK).

Methods

Budesonide determination

BUD quantification in samples was performed using high-pressure liquid chromatography with an ultraviolet detector (HPLC-UV) (Agilent, Santa Clara, California, USA) coupled with an Altima C18 column (5 μm), 4.6 × 150 mm (Hichrom, Theale, UK) and a pre-column (5 μm, 4.6 × 10 mm, C18 ALLGRDS, from Hichrom). The mobile phase was a mixture of sodium dihydrogen phosphate (NaH2PO4) in MilliQ water (3.9 mg/mL final concentration) supplemented with phosphoric acid 85% to reach pH 3.2, and acetonitrile (65:35 v/v), filtered after mixing under vacuum on a Stericup® Quick Release Filter Durapore® with a 0.22 μm pore size polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane (Merck Millipore). Isocratic chromatographic analysis was performed with a flow rate of 1.5 mL/min, at 40 °C and with an injection volume of 100 μL. The run time was 22 min and the wavelength for detection 245 nm. Chromatographic peaks of A and B epimers (BUD bulk is provided in racemic mixture) were integrated at 19 min and 17 min, respectively. A linear calibration curve was established between 0.2 and 20 μg/mL of BUD with an R2 above 0.99 and a quality control sample at a concentration of 3.5 μg/mL with a recovery 100 ± 5%.

Production of controlled and sustained release budesonide microparticles

HCO and TPGS were solubilized in IPA, heated to 65 °C, then BUD was added and solubilized at 3% of solid content (w:v) in the solution to reach a final ratio of BUD, HCO, TPGS 20:79.2:0.8, w/w/w. The resulting solution was then spray-dried using a mini Spray Dryer B-290 (Büchi Labortechnik, Flawil, Switzerland) under the following conditions: feed rate 2.8–3.0 g/min, inlet temperature 70 °C, leading to 43–45 °C for the outlet temperature, two-fluid nozzle of 0.7 mm with a 1.5 mm of nozzle cap, compressed air 800 L/min, and a drying air flow rate of 35 m3/h. The dried microparticles with 20% BUD were collected and stored in a closed glass container at room temperature in a desiccator. Regarding the diluent for a blend, the same production procedure was performed for HCO/TPGS (99:1 w/w).

Production of dry powder for inhalation

Dry powders for inhalation, used for the DoE, were obtained by blending controlled and sustained release BUD microparticles (at 20% BUD w/w) with the spray-dried diluent to obtain a BUD concentration of 6.66% in the final blend and a powder mass of 20 ± 0.5 mg in the HPMC capsule.

The blends were produced using the sandwich mixing method where one half of the diluent was added to the recipient (20 mL-glass vial) before the BUD-based microparticles, and the other half after. The blend was made with a ratio 2:1 of diluent:BUD based microparticles (w/w) after passing each type of powder through a 315 μm stainless steel sieve. The blend is then mixed using a Turbula mixer (Turbula®, Muttenz, Switzerland) at medium speed of 50 rpm for 15 min. Then, the mixture is passed through the 315 μm stainless steel sieve to remove aggregates. The sieved blend was mixed again using the same procedure. Finally, the powder is sieved one more time to remove the aggregates before filling it into size 3 HPMC capsules. To assess the BUD content uniformity, 10 samples of about 20.00 mg powder were weighed and then dissolved in a solution of IPA/Water (60:40, v/v) supplemented with Poloxamer 407 (0.5% m/v) in volumetric flasks (50.0 ml). The solution was sonicated at least for 30 min at 65 °C, until obtaining limpidity. Then, the samples were filtered using 0.45 μm size PVDF filters (Merck Millipore) and placed in vials with PTFE-type caps of 13.0 mm diameter (Avantor, Radnor Township, Pennsylvania, USA). The blend was considered homogeneous if all samples showed recoveries between 75% and 125% of the mean, with at least nine samples within 85–115%. These acceptance criteria were defined based on commonly applied uniformity of content of single-dose preparations described in the European Pharmacopeia (European Pharmacopoeia Commission, 2023). A coefficient of variation (CV) ≤ 5% could be also used as acceptance criterion (Pilcer et al., 2012)). Both acceptance criteria were fulfilled (Supplementary data Table S1).

Fine Particle Dose through Asthmatic Inhalation Profiles

An NGI with Alberta idealized throat (AIT) as induction port was connected to the breath simulator BRS300i (Copley Scientific, Nottingham, UK). Vi (L) and Ti (s) were set using the BRS software as a sinusoidal profile of inhalation. The flow rate was set using a DFM 2000 Flowmeter connected to a critical flow controller model TPK 2000 and to two HCP5 pumps, all from Copley Scientific. Before the evaluations, the P3/P2 ratio (absolute downstream and up-stream of the flow control valve) inferior to 0.5 was checked to guarantee applying a critical flow rate.

An impaction test is performed using three size 3 HPMC capsules filled with 20 ± 0.5 mg of the blend, inserted into the RS01 inhaler connected onto the AIT using an RS01 adapter (Copley Scientific). RS01 inhalers (Plasti-APE, Osnago, Italy) were either low resistance RS01 (R1) (≥100 L/min for a pressure drop of 4 kPa), medium-low resistance RS01 (R2) (80 L/min for a pressure drop of 4 kPa), medium resistance RS01 (R3) (65 L/min for a pressure drop of 4 kPa), or medium-high resistance RS01 (R4) (40 L/min for a pressure drop of 4 kPa). The amounts of BUD recovered from the capsule, device, AIT and each stage of the impactor were solubilized in a heated (65 °C) mixture of IPA/water (60:40 v/v) supplemented with 0.5% w/v poloxamer 407. The collected solutions were sonicated for 30 to 60 min, and the quantification of the BUD content was performed after filtration (on 0.45 μm PVDF filter) using the HPLC-UV method described in the BUD determination section. Then, the FPD was determined using Copley Inhaler Testing Data Analysis Software (CITDAS, Copley Scientific).

Experiment Design (DoE)

To assess the FPD distribution across the defined breathing pattern limits, a Central Composite Design for 3 factors was designed with the Design-Expert program version 13 (Stat-Ease, Minneapolis, MN). Delimitations of the domain covering the extreme factor levels were determined according to the literature (Chrystyn, 2007; Gauani et al., 2022; Dorosz et al., 2022; Prime et al., 2015; Horváth et al., 2017; Farkas et al., 2024; Haughney et al., 2021; Hanon et al., 2022; Mahler, 2017; Ghosh et al., 2017; Sharma et al., 2017; Haidl et al., 2016; Rigby, 2024; Kondo et al., 2017; Chamaon et al., 2025). The extreme levels used for Ti were between 1 and 5 s, and between 1 and 5 L for Vi. For PIFR, ranges of 30–100; 38–80; 28–65; and 25–40 mL/min were applied for the low resistance, medium-low resistance, medium resistance and medium-high resistance RS01 inhalers, respectively. The upper limits were defined based on the flow rates required to generate a pressure drop of up to 4 kPa across the different RS01 inhalers, in accordance with Gauani et al. (2022) (Gauani et al., 2022). Therefore, the upper limits were set according to the findings of Gauani et al., using a common pressure drop of 4 kPa for all inhalers, and did not consider airflows that would lead to a higher pressure drop. The lower limits were defined based on data from literature and clinical studies involving patients with asthma and COPD (Chrystyn, 2007; Gauani et al., 2022; Dorosz et al., 2022; Prime et al., 2015; Horváth et al., 2017; Farkas et al., 2024; Haughney et al., 2021; Hanon et al., 2022; Mahler, 2017; Ghosh et al., 2017; Sharma et al., 2017; Haidl et al., 2016; Rigby, 2024; Kondo et al., 2017; Chamaon et al., 2025). The lower limits of flow rate, measured by spirometry in clinical trials, were converted into equivalent airflow rates generated within the RS01 device as a function of its internal resistance, using the equation provided in Table 1. However, PIFR assessed using spirometry may overestimate the value achievable with an inhaler device. Unlike most inhalers, spirometers have minimal internal resistance; therefore, specific equations are required to estimate the PIFR that can be achieved with inhalers of varying internal resistances. These equations were derived by fitting regression models that relate standard spirometric measurements to PIFR values obtained when inhaling through resistances representative of different inhalers. Because the decrease in flow is non-linear and becomes less pronounced at higher resistance, a logarithmic model was required. According to the guidelines of asthma therapy management, the dosage of inhaled BUD medicine is given to be 200–400 μg daily for low dose, 400–800 μg daily for moderate dose and > 800 μg daily for high dose with a maximum of 800 μg twice a day. Assuming a fine particle fraction (FPF) of 50% is considered for these medicines, a resulting 100–200 μg for low dose, 200–400 for moderate dose and more than 400 μg daily for high doses with a maximum of 800 μg daily, is administered in lower airways. The robust FPD region was defined as 400–800 μg (i.e. 600 ± 200 μg), corresponding to the inhaled BUD doses typically prescribed for patients with severe asthma, who generally exhibit the highest variability in inspiratory profiles (Global Initiative for Asthma, 2025). However, the paramount feature to be observed remain the constant delivery regarding the FPD, rather than the amount of drug delivered.

The aim of this study is to evaluate each of the devices through breathing patterns displayed by patients suffering from mild to severe asthma and COPD and select the most appropriate one that would deliver a steady amount of dry powder, regardless of the breathing pattern. Briefly, the aim is to determine the device that demonstrate a steady FPD despite modifying the PIFR, Vi, and Ti. Therefore, the factors should not have any significant impact on the response.

Results

Different regression models were evaluated because of the specific nature of this study. In typical method optimization, the objective is to model and optimize the response as a function of the experimental factors which are known to be the most important.

In contrast, the present study aims to achieve a response (i.e. FPD) that remains essentially constant (robust) across the domain, i.e. without impact from the different factors (PIFR, Ti and Vi).

Different models were then tested to see whether or not some factors affect the response. In case of no factor affects the response, it can be considered as independent on the factors, therefore independent from the inhalation profiles.

The significance of the coefficients of determination (R2), adjusted R2, and predicted R2 was evaluated to assess the goodness of fit of the tested models (linear, 2-FI, and quadratic) for all devices (R1, R2, R3, and R4). These values were obtained through ANOVA analysis and are reported in Table 2. They provide an initial indication of how well each model explains the experimental variability in the data.

Furthermore, the influence of the factors on the response was also evaluated by ANOVA considering their p-values for all considered models. The p-value associated with the model determines whether the overall regression is statistically significant compared to the residuals, while the p-values of the individual factors (PIFR, Ti, and Vi) quantify the specific impact of each parameter on the response variable (FPD). These results are summarized in Table 3.

Discussion

In inhaled ICS medicine for asthma and COPD, the inhaler device is one of key parameters of the interaction between the formulation, the inhaler and the patient. For DPI, the patient's inspiratory airflows interact with the device's internal resistance, which is often determined by the complexity of the powder dispersion system. This interaction converts the energy generated by inhalation into forces that overcome the cohesive and adhesive interactions between the microparticles of the carrier and drug particles, while also imparting sufficient velocity to disperse the powder aerosol (Pilcer et al., 2012). This results in the FPD. However, varied inspiratory airflows, depending on the severity of asthma and COPD, can induce varied FPD. Therefore, with the aim of decreasing the impact of the patients' inspiratory airflows on the FPD, the ideal is to select a DPI for a given formulation where the inspiratory airflows currently recovered in asthma or COPD patients have no influence.

In this study, the desired range of responses was defined as 600 ± 200 μg to represent the daily dose required to treat severe asthma (Global Initiative for Asthma, 2025). The DoE approach was used to determine the potency of a DPI to steadily deliver FPD from dry powders for inhalation through inhalation patterns of asthmatic and COPD patients defined by their three major factors (PIFR, Ti and Vi). A DoE is generally used for planning experiments within a domain delimited by the factor levels in the aim of determining the most suitable factor combinations to obtain optimal (i.e. the highest or lowest) responses.

Recent findings showed that particle size and FPD do not govern by themselves the amount of powder that eventually reaches the lung upon inhalation. It is rather an association between these variables with the airflow exerted by the patients and inertial impaction in the upper airways. Moreover, DPIs are known to have high dependency (positive or negative) to airflow, which further suggest that relying on particle size is misleading. Therefore, it might seem challenging to rely on size-based parameters such as the fine particle fraction (FPF) and/or FPD to assess and verify good lung deposition (Weers et al., 2026). In the present study, we aimed to identify an inhaler system capable of delivering robust and reproducible aerosol performance across a wider range of inspiratory airflow conditions, representative of patients with asthma and COPD. Within this framework, the FPD response was used as a practical and comparative in vitro metric to evaluate the performance of the assessed RS01 inhalers. By systematically varying the inspiratory profile (flow rate, inhaled volume and inhalation duration), the influence of airflow dynamics on particle behavior within each device was characterized. Particular emphasis was placed on minimizing the variability in drug delivery, given the heterogeneity of patient inhalation profiles in real-world settings.

Accordingly, the device selection was guided by the consistency and robustness aerosol performance rather than the achievement of a specific FPD value. The target interval of 600 ± 200 was defined to match clinically relevant ICS dose ranges, given the GINA recommendations. Therefore, this interval serves solely as a benchmark within which the stability of aerosol delivery could be meaningfully assessed.

As previously mentioned, our approach is quite different as the aim was to determine the internal resistance DPIs where the FPD was not dependent on the varying factors (i.e. PIFR, Ti and Vi) in the range of asthmatic and COPD inspiratory breathing profiles. Different polynomial models (i.e. quadratic, 2IF, and linear) were built and evaluated for their possibility to explain the (in)dependence of the response.

In the present study, four different internal resistance RS01 DPIs (R1 for low, R2 for low-medium, R3 for medium and R4 for medium-high internal resistance RS01 DPIs) were examined.

PIFR showed a significant influence on FPD for only R3 in a linear model (p-value of regression 0.005) and in a trend for a linear model for R1 (p-value of regression 0.14), while Ti and Vi did not influence the response in any case study.

These data support the importance of the patient capacity to reach sufficient inspiratory airflow (i.e. PIFR), of which the failure can be considered as a critical error, i.e. influencing the FPD leading to suboptimal therapeutic doses (Broeders et al., 2003; van der Palen et al., 1995). The internal resistance of three of the RS01 DPIs (low-medium R2, medium-high R4 and low R1 DPI) is able to deliver a rather constant FPD, often close to the desired range (i.e. 600 ± 200 μg) without influence of PIFR, Ti and Vi. Each of these DPI was able to deliver a rather constant FPD but each in a somewhat different FPD range.

No adequate model was possible with the R2 and R4 inhalers, which means that the response is not significantly affected by any factor (unlike the R1 and R3 devices that somehow were impacted by PIFR, with p = 0.04 and p = 0.001, respectively, but from doubtful models). Since no factors seems to really influence the response, consequently the suggested “model” for R2 and R4 is the average of the responses.

With these first insights, the R1, R2 and R4 devices seem to be the most interesting inhalers to deliver a constant amount of formulation, regardless of the inhalation profiles. Indeed, even though the delivery is borderline impacted by the PIFR for the low resistance RS01 inhaler (R1), Fig. 2 shows that the FPD does not clearly rise proportionally to the PIFR. A rather constant dosage around 750 μg is obtained in the flow rate range between 50 and 85 L/min. This FPD range suggests that a higher inspiratory flow rate is able to deagglomerate the dry powder for inhalation and deliver up to 1000 μg of BUD (Fig. 2). Such powder behavior was already associated with FPDs and inhalers (i.e. for Diskus® and Aerolizer®) where, to have a higher amount of delivered drug, the patient is encouraged to inhale deeply and forcefully, but not too fast (Al-Showair et al., 2007; Chrystyn, 2007; Selvam et al., 2010).

The FPD from the R3 inhaler is significantly influenced by the PIFR but also to some extent by the Ti (borderline non-significant). The linear model was suggested as preferred to model the response. In that regard, the impact of two factors does not lead to constant delivery regardless of the breathing pattern. The FPD varied from 300 to 1300 μg, and the region of interest varied with the PIFR (Fig. 5A-C).

Related to the desired range, R2 was preferred as it showed 91% of the runs in the FPD range of 600 ± 200 μg, while the data observed with R4 showed 67% runs in the same region. FPDs generated from the medium-low and medium-high internal resistance RS01 inhalers (R2 and R4, respectively) are stable over the entire flow rate tested but provide a somewhat different dosage, but most in the desired range (Fig. 3 and Fig. 6). However, the R2 is mainly higher in the range, while the R4 is mainly lower.

The R2 DoE-domain was expanded further by adding several runs, while for R4, expanding the domain with lower PIFR did not allow easy recovery with the BRS300i-coupled NGI system. In general, high resistance inhalers are not prescribed to severe asthma or COPD patients because they need to make more effort upon inhalation. These inhalers would be more suitable for patients who are able to inhale at 40 L/min, a feature among less severe COPD patients (Buttini et al., 2016). However, the high internal resistance devices are not always the best. This is rather a misconception since DPIs do not always perform best at 4 kPa pressure drop, reached at 40 L/min in high resistance inhaler (Wauthoz et al., 2018; Buttini et al., 2016). Moreover, the use of DPIs is only recommended for patients who can generate a minimum effective flow of 30 L/min in the high resistance DPI, which therefore involves adults who can also demonstrate a Ti of 5 s (van der Palen et al., 1995).

Little difference is observed between the R2 and R4 inhalers. Given the rather constant FPD ranges in the datasets, [670–920] for R2 and [600–680] for R4 (without the extreme values). If the overall mean is the global response considered, the medium-low R2 inhaler seems to be the most adequate to use for the robust delivery of dry powder for inhalation based on controlled release BUD microparticles. The choice of the R2 inhaler is also supported by its ability to maintain a robust delivery in the region of interest (600 ± 200 μg), in the 35 to 80 L/min airflow range, where only two of the 23 runs were above 900 μg (Fig. 3).

Although patients experiencing severe exacerbations may not be able to demonstrate an inhalation flow powerful enough to generate turbulent energy, the DoE-based experiments consider the inhalation profiles of both asthmatic and COPD patients, who have restricted lung capabilities, and of healthy individuals. However, one limitation of the study is the lack of details regarding the inhalation profile generation (time to reach the PIFR, human-like decrease of the airflow, powder sedimentation time, etc.…) and the manipulation variability regarding the NGI system. To address this limitation, the integration of real patient-derived inhalation profiles into the breath simulator software could provide experimental conditions to better reflect reality.

Conclusions

This study aimed to define the optimal internal resistance of RS01 to deliver constant FPDs, preferably at 600 ± 200 μg, of a once-daily BUD microparticles-based dry powder for inhalation, across inspiratory profiles of asthmatic and COPD patients. Four RS01 DPI, presenting a similar external design for the patient, but with different internal resistances, are tested as study cases. The internal resistance ranged from low (R1), over medium-low (R2) and medium (R3), to medium-high (R4).

The best results were observed with the medium-low resistance RS01 (R2), for which 91% of the responses fell within the desired FPD range. The medium-high resistance RS01 (R4) followed, with 67% of the responses within the target FPD range The low resistance RS01 (R1) produced a robust FPD region (i.e. 750 ± 200 μg), although values were consistently higher than the desired FPD range. In contrast, the medium resistance RS01 (R3) did not provide stable delivery of the dry powder, with responses ranging from 300 to 1300 μg, well outside the targeted FPD range.

Therefore, choosing the medium-low R2 resistance RS01 DPI for the once daily BUD dry powder for inhalation could lower the impact of the patient's inspiratory airflows on the therapeutic doses and therefore on the disease control, which could increase patient's quality of life and therapeutic outcomes.
