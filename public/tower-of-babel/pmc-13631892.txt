Letter regarding "Clinical outcomes and feasibility of nonthermal, nontumescent ablation for isolated below-the-knee great saphenous vein incompetence"
Authors: Ahmet Daylan, Fatih İslamoğlu
Published: 2027

We read with great interest the multicenter experience reported by Min et al1 on nonthermal, nontumescent ablation for symptomatic isolated below-the-knee great saphenous vein incompetence. We congratulate the authors on advancing the evidence in this important anatomic zone. The favorable clinical and anatomic outcomes and absence of nerve injury provide encouraging evidence that below-the-knee reflux can be safely treated with nonthermal techniques. These findings also support a comprehensive, full-length treatment approach when reflux extends distally.

However, the reported 100% occlusion rate requires a clear definition. The authors reported immediate occlusion and no partial or complete recanalization but did not specify the criteria applied. Was complete occlusion defined as closure of the entire treated segment, or were short patent segments still considered successful? Clarifying the definitions of complete occlusion and partial recanalization would improve interpretation of the reported outcomes. In our comparative study of cyanoacrylate and radiofrequency ablation and our subsequent full-length hybrid study, technical success was defined as the absence of a discrete patent segment longer than 5 cm within the treated great saphenous vein.2,3 Such a threshold would improve reproducibility and facilitate meaningful comparisons, particularly because recanalization after cyanoacrylate closure may be irregular and discontinuous.4

Further technical details regarding the cyanoacrylate treatment would also be valuable. Although the VenaSeal (Medtronic) system was identified, the viscosity of the adhesive, which influences its polymerization characteristics, was not stated. These properties influence catheter positioning, delivery pattern, segmental spacing, adhesive distribution, and potentially recanalization. Clarifying these features would help determine whether the results are formulation-specific or generalizable across cyanoacrylate systems.

Our hybrid approach combining proximal cyanoacrylate ablation with catheter-directed 3% polidocanol foam below the knee achieved successful full-length treatment without deep vein thrombosis or nerve injury.2 Although the populations differ, both studies support the safe treatment of documented distal reflux rather than routinely terminating ablation at the knee.
