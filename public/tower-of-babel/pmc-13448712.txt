Loss of NCCRP1 overcomes immune evasion in lung adenocarcinoma
Authors: Lu Liu, Shihao Qi, Liye Shao, Junyang Cai, Qingqing Zeng, Xu Jiang, Fang Ma, Weiwei Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe low response rate to immunotherapy in patients with lung adenocarcinoma is primarily due to tumor immune evasion. The tumor immunosuppressive microenvironment orchestrates this evasion, yet the underlying mechanisms remain elusive. Here we identify non-specific cytotoxic cell receptor protein 1 (NCCRP1) as a critical and previously uncharacterized regulator of this process.MethodsWe first analyzed publicly accessible patient-derived single-cell RNA sequencing and RNA sequencing data to investigate the expression of NCCRP1 in lung adenocarcinoma and its impact on the tumor immune microenvironment. Subsequently, the Nccrp1 gene was knocked out in mouse lung adenocarcinoma cells using CRISPR-Cas9. The effect of NCCRP1 deletion on tumor growth was then evaluated using subcutaneous transplantation models in both NSG and C57BL/6 mice. Single-cell RNA sequencing, flow cytometry and multiple targeted in vivo interventions were employed to assess the influence of NCCRP1 on the tumor immune microenvironment. To explore the underlying mechanisms by which NCCRP1 regulates the tumor immune microenvironment, we conducted co-immunoprecipitation, RNA pull-down, ubiquitination assay, mass spectrometry, isobaric tags for relative and absolute quantitation proteomics, dual-luciferase reporter gene assay, and ELISA.ResultsLoss of NCCRP1 inhibits lung tumor growth and prolongs survival in immunocompetent C57BL/6 mouse models. NCCRP1 deficiency upregulates CX3CL1 to recruit CX3CR1+ antitumoral macrophages. These macrophages subsequently secrete CXCL9 and CXCL10, enhancing the infiltration of CD8+ T cells and NK cells into the tumor microenvironment. Mechanistically, NCCRP1 and STAU1 competitively bind to NEDD4. NCCRP1 deficiency enhances the STAU1-NEDD4 interaction, promoting ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation of STAU1, thereby increasing CX3CL1 messenger RNA stability. Remarkably, ablating NCCRP1 synergized with anti-programmed cell death protein 1 or interferon-γ therapy, leading to complete tumor eradication.ConclusionOur findings highlight NCCRP1 targeting as a promising therapeutic strategy to reprogram the immunosuppressive microenvironment and overcome the “cold tumor” phenotype in lung adenocarcinoma.Keywords: Lung Cancer

Introduction

Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer mortality worldwide, with annual deaths approaching 2 million.1 As the predominant histological subtype,2 lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) constitutes 40–50% of all cases and accounts for this high mortality.3 Despite advances in the application of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), the 5-year survival rate of patients with LUAD remains suboptimal.4 5 This therapeutic limitation stems from the LUAD immunosuppressive microenvironment, which drives the “cold tumor” phenotype that promotes progression and impairs immunotherapy response.6 7 Consequently, reprogramming this immunosuppressive niche to transform “cold tumor” into “hot tumor” represents a critical therapeutic imperative for improving patient survival.

CD8+ T cells and natural killer (NK) cells constitute pivotal cytotoxic lymphocytes that mediate direct tumor cell cytolysis, and their intratumoral infiltration density and functional status collectively dictate the potency of antitumor immune responses.8 9 In LUAD, the paucity of these lymphocytes establishes an immune-cold microenvironment, fundamentally driving resistance to ICIs.10 Within the tumor microenvironment (TME), macrophages function as central regulators, possessing phenotypic plasticity that allows differentiation into antitumor (CD86+) or protumor (CD206+) states,11 while critically driving TME remodeling and modulating immunotherapy responses.12 Specifically, antitumoral macrophages augment CD8+ T cell and NK cell infiltration into the TME via CXCL9/CXCL10 secretion.13 14 Thus, enhancing antitumoral macrophage infiltration to reverse LUAD’s “cold” phenotype represents a key strategy for boosting immunotherapy responses.

Aberrant gene expression within tumor cells drives the establishment and maintenance of the immunosuppressive TME.15 To identify key regulators of this process in LUAD, we performed integrated multi-database analyses that identified non-specific cytotoxic cell receptor protein 1 (NCCRP1) as a critical candidate gene modulating the TME. While NCCRP1 has been extensively characterized in teleost fish for its pivotal role in governing immune cell activity,16 its functional significance and mechanistic contributions within mammalian systems remain largely uncharted.

In this study, we demonstrated that NCCRP1 knockout profoundly reprograms the immunosuppressive TME in LUAD. Specifically, NCCRP1 deletion upregulates CX3CL1 to recruit antitumoral macrophages, which subsequently secrete CXCL9/CXCL10 to facilitate CD8+ T cell and NK cell infiltration into the TME. Notably, combining NCCRP1 targeting with either anti-programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) or interferon (IFN)-γ therapy significantly enhances antitumor efficacy. Collectively, our findings establish NCCRP1 as a master regulator of the immunosuppressive microenvironment in LUAD, and identify NCCRP1-directed therapy as a novel strategy to reprogram immunologically cold tumors for clinical benefit.

Results

NCCRP1 facilitates immune evasion in lung adenocarcinoma

To investigate the expression and clinical relevance of NCCRP1 in lung cancer, we first analyzed The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) dataset. This revealed that NCCRP1 messenger RNA (mRNA) was significantly upregulated in both LUAD and lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC) tissues compared with normal controls (figure 1A, online supplemental figure S1A). We next assessed the prognostic value of NCCRP1 expression using the Kaplan-Meier (KM) plotter database.

In patients with LUAD, high NCCRP1 expression was statistically significantly associated with shorter overall survival (figure 1B). In patients with LUSC, however, no significant association was found between NCCRP1 expression and overall survival (online supplemental figure S1B). Given the pronounced and significant prognostic value of NCCRP1 specifically in LUAD, we focused subsequent validation on this subtype. Consistent with the bioinformatic findings, immunohistochemical analysis of a tissue microarray containing 75 paired LUAD and adjacent normal tissues confirmed that NCCRP1 protein levels were significantly higher in tumor tissues (figure 1C–E). Given the significance of infiltrating immune cells to tumor prognosis,17 18 we further analyzed Cancer Single-cell Expression Map (CancerSCEM) with a focus on this aspect. In LUAD, the expression level of NCCRP1 mRNA in tumor cells was negatively correlated with the infiltration density of antitumor immune cells, including CD8+ T cells and CD4+ T cells (figure 1F). Collectively, the above data indicate that NCCRP1 may promote LUAD progression, potentially through modulating immune cell infiltration.

To clarify the role of NCCRP1 in LUAD progression, we knocked out the Nccrp1 gene in two mouse lung adenocarcinoma cell lines (LLC and CMT167) using CRISPR-Cas9 technology. Monoclonal cell clones with Nccrp1 knockout were generated in both cell lines through screening and validated by DNA sequencing (online supplemental figure S1C,D). Western blot also confirmed that the NCCRP1 protein was completely deleted in the two cell lines (online supplemental figure S1E,F). Thereafter, subcutaneous tumor models were established in both immunodeficient NSG mice (which lack T, B, and NK cells) and immunocompetent C57BL/6 mice. In NSG hosts, Nccrp1 knockout in either LLC or CMT167 cells did not significantly affect tumor growth or host survival compared with the control group (figure 1G–J). In stark contrast, within immunocompetent C57BL/6 mice, sgNccrp1 tumors from both cell lines exhibited markedly slower growth and led to prolonged survival (figure 1K–N). In conclusion, the results suggest that NCCRP1 promotes tumor progression in these LUAD models by regulating immune escape.

NCCRP1 deficiency remodels the immunosuppressive microenvironment in lung adenocarcinoma

Multiple distinct mechanisms mediate tumor immune escape, notably the creation of an immunosuppressive TME.19 20 To investigate whether NCCRP1 influences the tumor microenvironment, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) on sgCtrl and sgNccrp1 LLC tumors. Using published cell type-specific gene markers,21–23 we identified 11 distinct cell populations, including tumor cells (expressing Myc), CD8+ T cells (expressing Cd8a), CD4+ T cells (expressing Cd4), NK cells (expressing Ncr1), macrophages (expressing C1qa, Chil3), neutrophils (expressing S100a9), dendritic cells (expressing Cd209a), B cells (expressing Ms4a1), endothelial cells (expressing Cdh5), fibroblasts (expressing Col1a1) and pericytes (expressing Rgs5) (online supplemental figure S2A,B). Overall, the number of both CD8+ T cells and NK cells was significantly greater in sgNccrp1 tumors compared with sgCtrl tumors (figure 2A,B). Flow cytometry further confirmed a significant increase in the number of CD8+ T cells and NK cells in sgNccrp1 lung tumors (figure 2C,D, online supplemental figure S2C,D and S3A-D). Given the critical role of CD8+ T cells and NK cells as antitumor effector cells, we assessed the expression of cytotoxicity-associated molecules in CD8+ T cells and NK cells from sgCtrl and sgNccrp1 tumors using scRNA-seq. Analysis revealed comparable expression levels of these molecules between both groups (online supplemental figure S2E,F). These findings indicate that NCCRP1 deficiency increases the infiltration of CD8+ T cells and NK cells without affecting their cytotoxic function.

In the tumor immune microenvironment, tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) exert more profound influences than other innate immune cell populations.24 25 We therefore classified TAMs into two subclusters: antitumoral macrophages (expressing Il1b,26 27 Irf5,28 and Cd8027) and protumoral macrophages (expressing Apoe,26 C1qa,26 and C1qc26) (online supplemental figure S4A,B). The proportion of antitumoral macrophages was elevated in sgNccrp1 tumors, while the proportion of protumoral macrophages was reduced relative to sgCtrl tumors (figure 2G). We further evaluated CD86+F4/80+ and CD206+F4/80+ macrophage populations in both tumor groups using flow cytometry. We observed that the percentage of CD86+F4/80+ macrophages was significantly increased, whereas that of CD206+F4/80+ macrophages was reduced in sgNccrp1 tumors compared with sgCtrl tumors (figure 2H,I, online supplemental figure S4C and S5A-C). These data suggest that NCCRP1 deletion promotes the polarization and/or recruitment of antitumoral macrophages.

To identify the specific immune cell types involved in NCCRP1-mediated regulation of lung tumor growth, we depleted CD8+ T cells or NK cells using neutralizing antibodies in tumor models. The results showed that the depletion of either CD8+ T cells or NK cells significantly reduced the differences in lung tumor growth and survival between the sgNccrp1 and sgCtrl groups (figure 2E,F). Moreover, clodronate liposome-mediated macrophage depletion abolished the differences in tumor growth and survival between the two groups (figure 2J, online supplemental figure S4D). Collectively, our findings demonstrate that NCCRP1 knockout reprograms the immunosuppressive TME by increasing infiltration of CD8+ T cells, NK cells and antitumoral macrophages in LLC tumors.

NCCRP1 deficiency enhances CD8+ T cells and NK cells recruitment via CXCL9/10 derived from antitumoral macrophages

TME reprogramming is predominantly driven by cell–cell interactions.29 30 Therefore, we initially used CellPhoneDB to compare the interaction strengths among tumor cells, CD8+ T cells, NK cells, and antitumoral macrophages between sgCtrl and sgNccrp1 tumors based on scRNA-seq data. Analysis revealed that Nccrp1 knockout significantly strengthened the interactions of antitumoral macrophages with tumor cells, CD8+ T cells, and NK cells compared with sgCtrl tumors. However, no significant enhancement was observed in the interactions between tumor cells and CD8+ T cells or NK cells (figure 3A). This suggests that NCCRP1 in tumor cells likely regulates the infiltration of CD8+ T cells and NK cells through antitumoral macrophages. Subsequently, the differences in potential interactions between macrophage ligands and CD8+ T cell or NK cell receptors in sgCtrl and sgNccrp1 tumors were analyzed using NicheNet. Across analyses of antitumoral macrophage-CD8+ T cell and antitumoral macrophage-NK cell interactions, Cxcl9-Cxcr3 and Cxcl10-Cxcr3 consistently exhibited the most significant upregulation in interaction scores in sgNccrp1 tumors relative to sgCtrl tumors (figure 3B,C).

In line with this finding, our scRNA-seq results showed significantly higher expression of Cxcl9 and Cxcl10 in antitumoral macrophages from sgNccrp1 tumors, with corresponding upregulation of Cxcr3 in CD8+ T cells (online supplemental figure S6A–C). NK cell expression of Cxcr3 remained unchanged (online supplemental figure S6D). ELISA analysis of sgNccrp1 TAM supernatants and tumor tissue homogenates revealed elevated levels of CXCL9 and CXCL10 compared with the sgCtrl group (figure 3D, online supplemental figure S6E,F). CXCL9 and CXCL10 are chemokines mainly secreted by antitumoral macrophages. These chemokines bind to CXCR3 on CD8+ T cells and NK cells, recruiting them into the TME.13 14 Thus, our data suggest that loss of NCCRP1 recruits CD8+ T cells and NK cells through increased CXCL9/10 secretion. Last, CXCL9/10-CXCR3 signaling blockade using neutralizing antibodies abolished differences in tumor growth (figure 3E), survival (figure 3F), and CD8+ T/NK cell infiltration (figure 3G,H, online supplemental figure S6G,H) between sgCtrl and sgNccrp1 tumors. Notably, this blockade also eliminated the differences in the infiltration of CXCR3+CD8+ T cells and CXCR3+ NK cells (online supplemental figure S7A–F), directly demonstrating that NCCRP1 loss drives the selective recruitment of CXCR3+ lymphocytes. Meanwhile, depletion of macrophages eliminated the difference in CXCL9/10 secretion between sgCtrl and sgNccrp1 tumors (online supplemental figure S7G). Together, these results indicate that NCCRP1 knockout recruits increased CD8+ T cells and NK cells by promoting CXCL9/10 secretion from antitumoral macrophages.

NCCRP1 deficiency inhibits lung adenocarcinoma growth via CX3CL1-mediated recruitment of CX3CR1+ antitumoral macrophages

To determine whether NCCRP1 regulates macrophages by polarization or recruitment, we analyzed scRNA-seq data. Specifically, Nccrp1 knockout increased the abundance of antitumoral macrophages while protumoral macrophages remained unchanged (figure 4A). And Gene Ontology (GO) analysis revealed that antitumoral macrophages were predominantly enriched in migration pathways compared with protumoral macrophages, including leukocyte cell–cell adhesion, leukocyte migration and myeloid leukocyte migration (online supplemental figure S8A). This indicates that NCCRP1 knockout promotes the recruitment of antitumoral macrophages. Subsequently, we analyzed the expression of multiple chemokines31 in tumor cells from scRNA-seq data and found that Nccrp1 knockout only significantly upregulated Cx3cl1 mRNA expression (figure 4B). Besides, Cx3cr1 (CX3CL1 receptor)32 expression was elevated in antitumoral macrophages of sgNccrp1 tumors compared with sgCtrl tumors (online supplemental figure S8B). Therefore, this suggests the CX3CL1-CX3CR1 axis may be involved in the migration of antitumoral macrophages to the TME. Then ELISA confirmed that CX3CL1 levels were significantly increased in the LLC supernatants and tumor tissue homogenates from sgNccrp1 groups compared with sgCtrl groups (figure 4C, online supplemental figure S8C). Flow cytometry also revealed a significant increase in CX3CR1+CD86+ antitumoral macrophages from sgNccrp1 tumors (figure 4D, online supplemental figure S8D).

Finally, we used AZD8797 to block CX3CR1 signaling in vivo. The results showed that the difference in tumor growth and survival was eliminated between sgCtrl and sgNccrp1 tumors after injection of AZD8797 (figure 4E, online supplemental figure S8E). Furthermore, flow cytometry analysis of the treated sgNccrp1 tumors showed complete reversal of the numbers of CX3CR1+CD86+ antitumoral macrophages, CD8+ T cells and NK cells (figure 4F,H, and online supplemental figure S9A–C). Collectively, our results confirm that loss of NCCRP1 promotes CX3CL1 secretion to recruit CX3CR1+ antitumoral macrophages, thereby increasing the infiltration of CD8+ T cells and NK cells and suppressing LLC tumor growth.

To determine whether NCCRP1-dependent recruitment of CX3CR1+ antitumoral macrophages is conserved in human systems, we knocked down NCCRP1 in human LUAD cell lines A549 and H1299 (online supplemental figure S10A,B). We found that NCCRP1 knockdown also upregulated CX3CL1 mRNA expression and secretion (online supplemental figure S10C–F). Subsequently, we isolated human CX3CR1+CD86+ MDMs by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) (online supplemental figure S10G) and performed transwell recruitment assays. We observed that NCCRP1 knockdown in both A549 and H1299 cells markedly enhanced the recruitment of CX3CR1+CD86+ MDMs (online supplemental figure S10H,I). Furthermore, the recruited MDMs from NCCRP1-knockdown groups exhibited increased CXCL9 and CXCL10 secretion (online supplemental figure S10J,K). Collectively, these results demonstrate that NCCRP1 deficiency promotes CX3CL1-mediated recruitment of antitumoral macrophages, in line with our findings in the murine model.

NCCRP1 deficiency stabilizes Cx3cl1 mRNA by downregulating STAU1-mediated 3'UTR degradation

To elucidate the molecular mechanism by which NCCRP1 regulates Cx3cl1 mRNA expression, we first assessed Cx3cl1 transcriptional activity following Nccrp1 knockout. The results indicated that Nccrp1 knockout did not affect Cx3cl1 transcriptional activity (figure 5A). Subsequently, mRNA stability assays revealed that loss of Nccrp1 significantly slowed the degradation rate of Cx3cl1 mRNA compared with that in the sgCtrl group (figure 5B). As the regulatory activity of the 3'untranslated region (3'UTR) is a critical determinant of mRNA stability,33 we used a dual-luciferase reporter containing the Cx3cl1 3'UTR. This analysis revealed that Nccrp1 knockout significantly enhanced 3'UTR activity compared with the control (figure 5C). This indicated that knockout of Nccrp1 enhances Cx3cl1 mRNA stability by increasing its 3'UTR activity. Next, to explore the mechanism by which NCCRP1 modulates Cx3cl1 mRNA degradation, we performed isobaric tags for relative and absolute quantitation (iTRAQ) proteomics in sgCtrl and sgNccrp1 cells to identify differentially expressed proteins (figure 5D). Concurrently, RNA pull-down assays coupled with mass spectrometry (MS) were employed to identify RNA binding proteins associated with the Cx3cl1 3'UTR. We then identified seven potential regulatory targets by overlapping differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) from iTRAQ proteomics and proteins bound to the Cx3cl1 3'UTR (figure 5E). Among them, STAU1 was a potential candidate. STAU1 is a highly conserved dsRNA binding protein that specifically recognizes and binds to the 3'UTR regions of mRNAs, thereby mediating mRNA degradation.34 And iTRAQ proteomics revealed that Nccrp1 knockout significantly downregulated STAU1 protein expression (figure 5F). Western blot and RNA pull-down assays further confirmed that Nccrp1 knockout downregulates STAU1 expression in LLC cells and that STAU1 binds to the Cx3cl1 3'UTR (online supplemental figure S11A,D). Consistently, NCCRP1 knockdown in A549 and H1299 cells also reduced STAU1 protein expression (online supplemental figure S11B,C). These results suggest that STAU1 may be the key mediator of NCCRP1-dependent regulation of Cx3cl1 mRNA stability. To further confirm our hypothesis, we overexpressed Stau1 in sgNccrp1 cells (figure 5G). Notably, Stau1 overexpression markedly reduced Cx3cl1 mRNA expression and accelerated Cx3cl1 mRNA degradation (figure 5H,I). Furthermore, dual-luciferase reporter assays demonstrated that Stau1 overexpression substantially reduced the activity of the Cx3cl1 3'UTR (figure 5J). Consistently, in vivo overexpression of Stau1 led to significantly increased tumor volume and a concomitant decrease in CX3CL1 secretion within the tumor tissue (online supplemental figure S11E,F). These results collectively indicate that knockout of NCCRP1 enhances Cx3cl1 mRNA stability by downregulating STAU1 expression.

NCCRP1 deficiency promotes STAU1 ubiquitination and degradation by enhancing STAU1-NEDD4 binding

We next elucidated the molecular mechanism by which knockout of Nccrp1 downregulates STAU1 protein expression. First, scRNA-seq data and quantitative PCR (qPCR) analysis revealed no decrease in Stau1 mRNA levels following Nccrp1 knockout (figure 6A, online supplemental figure S12A), indicating that NCCRP1 regulates STAU1 not at the mRNA level. To determine whether NCCRP1 affects STAU1 expression through protein modification, sgCtrl and sgNccrp1 cells were pretreated with the proteasome inhibitor MG132 to inhibit degradation, followed by exposure to the protein synthesis inhibitor cycloheximide (CHX) for 4, 8, and 12 hours. Western blot revealed significantly accelerated STAU1 degradation in sgNccrp1 cells compared with sgCtrl cells (figure 6B). Subsequently, we further performed ubiquitination assays to assess whether NCCRP1 affects STAU1 ubiquitination. To assess whether STAU1 undergoes ubiquitination, we expressed Flag-Stau1 together with HA-tagged ubiquitin in LLC cells. Flag immunoprecipitates were then probed with anti-HA, revealing ubiquitinated STAU1 (figure 6C). We next compared endogenous STAU1 ubiquitination between sgCtrl and sgNccrp1 cells, and found that Nccrp1 knockout markedly increased STAU1 ubiquitination (figure 6D). These findings indicate that Nccrp1 knockout facilitates STAU1 protein degradation by enhancing its ubiquitination.

To identify the ubiquitin-related proteins mediating NCCRP1-dependent STAU1 ubiquitination, we performed Flag-Stau1 co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) coupled with MS and intersected the results with known ubiquitinases and deubiquitinases. This analysis identified NEDD4 and OTUD4 as high-confidence STAU1 interactors (figure 6E). NEDD4 is an HECT-domain E3 ubiquitin ligase that catalyzes the attachment of ubiquitin chains to substrate proteins, primarily targeting them for proteasomal degradation.35 Conversely, OTUD4 is a deubiquitinating enzyme belonging to the OTU subfamily, known to remove ubiquitin chains from specific substrate proteins, thereby counteracting ubiquitin-mediated degradation.36 ITRAQ analysis and western blot revealed decreased OTUD4 expression in sgNccrp1 cells compared with sgCtrl cells, whereas NEDD4 expression remained unchanged (figure 6F, online supplemental figure S12B). To determine which protein is critical for mediating NCCRP1-regulated STAU1 ubiquitination and degradation, we consequently overexpressed Otud4 and knocked down Nedd4, respectively, in sgNccrp1 cells (online supplemental figure S12C,D). Crucially, knockdown of Nedd4 in sgNccrp1 cells effectively rescued STAU1 protein stability, as shown by CHX chase assays (figure 6G). However, overexpression of Otud4 in sgNccrp1 cells failed to rescue STAU1 stability (online supplemental figure S12E), further supporting the specific role of NEDD4.

To further investigate how NCCRP1 regulates STAU1 ubiquitination and degradation via NEDD4, we performed Flag-Nccrp1 Co-IP coupled with MS. The MS results also identified NEDD4 protein (figure 6H). Then, we co-transfected either Flag-Stau1 or Flag-Nccrp1 with HA-Nedd4 plasmids into cells and performed Co-IP. The results confirmed that both STAU1 and NCCRP1 interact with NEDD4 (figure 6I,J). Based on these findings, we hypothesized that NCCRP1 might stabilize the STAU1 protein by competing with STAU1 for NEDD4 binding. Crucially, when we co-transfected Flag-Stau1 and HA-Nedd4 plasmids into sgCtrl and sgNccrp1 cells, we observed a significant increase in STAU1 levels in the HA-NEDD4 immunoprecipitates on Nccrp1 knockout (figure 6K). This suggests that NCCRP1 deletion enhances STAU1-NEDD4 interaction. Last, cells were co-transfected with fixed amounts of Flag-Stau1 and HA-Nedd4 plasmids alongside increasing concentrations of Flag-Nccrp1. On progressive transfection with increasing doses of Flag-Nccrp1 plasmid, we observed a dose-dependent decrease in Flag-Stau1 bound to HA-Nedd4 in Co-IP assays (figure 6L). This establishes that NCCRP1 can disrupt the STAU1-NEDD4 interaction in a concentration-dependent manner. Collectively, our results demonstrate that NCCRP1 protects STAU1 from degradation and enhances its expression by competitively binding NEDD4.

NCCRP1 deficiency synergizes with PD-1 blockade or IFN-γ to eradicate lung tumors

Although NCCRP1 knockout significantly inhibits tumor growth and prolongs survival, complete tumor eradication was not achieved. This partial response indicates that the antitumor immunity established by NCCRP1 knockout, while robust, may be limited during later tumor progression by the immunosuppressive TME. To overcome this limitation, we next evaluated whether combining Nccrp1 knockout with immune-activating strategies could enhance therapeutic outcomes. Specifically, NCCRP1 knockout combined with either PD-1 blockade or IFN-γ resulted in complete tumor regression and sustained survival through the 60-day endpoint; these outcomes were significantly superior to monotherapy and demonstrated clear synergistic efficacy (figure 7A–D). Together, these results demonstrate that NCCRP1 targeting has therapeutic potential and synergizes with IFN-γ or PD-1 blockade, establishing a novel paradigm for combination immunotherapy in these lung cancer models.

To dissect the immune mechanisms underlying the synergistic efficacy of combined NCCRP1 deficiency and anti-PD-1 therapy, we performed flow cytometry analysis of intratumoral immune cells from treated mice. Although anti-PD-1 treatment did not further increase the total numbers of CD8+ T cells or NK cells in sgNccrp1 tumors (online supplemental figure S13B,E), it significantly elevated the percentage of granzyme B+ cells among both CD8+ T cells and NK cells (online supplemental figure S13C,F), indicating enhanced cytotoxic function. By contrast, anti-PD-1 therapy did not alter the proportions of CD86+F4/80+ antitumoral TAMs or CD206+F4/80+ protumoral TAMs in sgNccrp1 tumors (online supplemental figure S14A,B). Collectively, these results demonstrate that the synergistic antitumor effect of combining NCCRP1 deficiency with PD-1 blockade is primarily driven by the functional enhancement of CD8+ T cell and NK cell cytotoxicity, rather than by further increasing lymphocyte infiltration or modulating macrophage polarization.

Discussion

The central barrier to LUAD immunotherapy efficacy originates from tumor-intrinsic evasion mechanisms that dynamically establish immunosuppressive niches and compromise antitumor immune responses.37 Here, we identified NCCRP1 as a master regulatory gene orchestrating this immune escape program. We observed that NCCRP1 knockout suppressed lung tumor progression in immunocompetent C57BL/6 mice, whereas this suppression was abrogated in lymphocyte-deficient NSG mice. This study definitively established that NCCRP1-mediated immune evasion subverts immune surveillance, consequently fostering an immunosuppressive niche. Targeting NCCRP1 represents a promising therapeutic strategy to overcome the “cold tumor” phenotype in LUAD through immune microenvironment reprogramming (online supplemental file 1).

TAMs serve as pivotal regulators within the TME.38 39 Our group previously demonstrated that TAM reprogramming effectively suppresses LUAD progression and enhances antitumor immunity.7 40 This study identifies TAMs as central hubs through which NCCRP1 orchestrates the LUAD immune landscape. Specifically, we found that knockout of NCCRP1 in LUAD cells promoted the secretion of CX3CL1, which led to the recruitment of CX3CR1+ antitumoral macrophages. Importantly, a recent study by Xiao et al has identified the CX3CR1+CD86+ macrophage subset as a crucial mediator of antitumor immunity.26 Through blockade of CX3CR1 with AZD8797, we further established the critical role of this specific macrophage population in enhancing antitumor immune responses. Our findings not only provide new evidence for elucidating the antitumor mechanisms of CX3CR1+CD86+ macrophages but also lay a theoretical foundation for the development of immunotherapeutic strategies targeting this TAM population.

CX3CL1 is a key chemokine that critically influences the immune microenvironment of LUAD and modulates immune cell infiltration.41 Its aberrant expression is closely associated with worse prognosis and therapy resistance.41 Our study reveals that NCCRP1 modulates CX3CL1 expression post-transcriptionally by suppressing its mRNA stability. Specifically, this regulation is mediated by the downstream factor STAU1, which directly binds the Cx3cl1 3'UTR. These findings establish the NCCRP1-STAU1-CX3CL1 axis as both a novel regulatory pathway and a potential therapeutic target. Furthermore, we uncovered that NCCRP1 regulates STAU1 protein stability by competing with STAU1 for binding to the E3 ligase NEDD4, thereby protecting STAU1 from NEDD4-mediated ubiquitination and degradation. This identifies NCCRP1 as a novel regulator of ubiquitination, and future structural studies of these interactions will inform therapeutic development.

PD-1 blockade is a widely used cancer immunotherapy that enhances the antitumor function of CD8+ T cells and NK cells.42 43 IFN-γ, a potent immunostimulatory cytokine, further augments the effector functions of multiple immune cells within the TME, including macrophages, CD8+ T cells, and NK cells.7 40 44 45 Our flow cytometry analyses revealed that anti-PD-1 treatment significantly increased granzyme B expression in intratumoral CD8+ T cells and NK cells from Nccrp1-deficient tumors (online supplemental figure S13). Furthermore, in the context of combination therapy with NCCRP1 knockout, IFN-γ treatment led to a further increase in CX3CL1 expression (online supplemental figure S15). This aligns with our established mechanism whereby CX3CL1 recruits CX3CR1+ antitumoral macrophages. Together, these effects explain why combining PD-1 blockade or IFN-γ with NCCRP1 knockout elicited a robust antitumor response. Encouragingly, the combination therapies led to complete tumor ablation in all mice, with all animals surviving beyond 60 days and showing no overt toxicity. Together, these findings provide a mechanistic basis for the observed synergy.

The most clinically relevant finding of our study is the potent synergistic efficacy of NCCRP1 ablation combined with PD-1 blockade or IFN-γ in achieving complete tumor eradication (figure 7). PD-1 blockade has been approved for treating multiple malignancies, including LUAD, and IFN-γ is a potent immunostimulatory cytokine with established clinical applications in certain immune disorders. The elevated expression of NCCRP1 in human LUAD, its association with poor prognosis, and the cross-species conservation of the NCCRP1-CX3CL1 axis collectively point to both NCCRP1 and CX3CL1 as plausible therapeutic targets. Furthermore, our finding that STAU1 stability depends on competition between NCCRP1 and STAU1 for NEDD4 binding suggests that disrupting this protein–protein interaction represents an additional therapeutic vulnerability. Several important challenges remain before clinical translation can be envisioned. First, pharmacological agents capable of directly targeting NCCRP1 or disrupting its protein interactions remain to be developed. Second, whether NCCRP1 inhibition affects normal physiological processes requires thorough investigation, despite the absence of overt toxicity in our preclinical models. Third, evaluating NCCRP1-targeting strategies in higher-order models that more closely approximate human physiology will be essential to bridge the gap between murine studies and clinical application. In summary, while our work represents a foundational exploration, it provides genetic validation, mechanistic definition, and initial translational evidence that collectively position NCCRP1 as a promising candidate for future immunotherapeutic development in LUAD.

This study employed both low-immunogenic (LLC) and more immunogenic (CMT167) transplantable tumor models to validate the function of NCCRP1 in LUAD, thereby covering a spectrum of immune contexts relevant to clinical settings. While these models provide valuable insights, employing spontaneous tumor models (such as KP mice) in future studies could help further elucidate the role of NCCRP1 in more physiological tumor-stroma interactions and metastatic processes. Additionally, exploring the function of NCCRP1 in other cancer types and its potential effects on other components of the TME will be important to fully understand its therapeutic potential. To address these points, future work will focus on generating conditional knockout mice to dissect the cell-type-specific functions of NCCRP1, establishing KP; Nccrp1fl/fl mice to investigate its role in spontaneous tumorigenesis and metastasis, and ultimately evaluating NCCRP1-targeting agents to validate their efficacy in models that more closely recapitulate human disease progression.

Materials and methods

Bioinformatics analysis

To investigate the expression profile and clinical relevance of NCCRP1 in LUAD and LUSC, we analyzed NCCRP1 expression levels in tumor tissues and matched normal lung tissues from the TCGA database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/).46 Subsequently, we analyzed the correlation between NCCRP1 expression and overall survival in patients with LUAD and LUSC using the KM plotter database (https://kmplot.com/analysis/).47 Finally, leveraging the CancerSCEM database (https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/cancerscem/)48 containing scRNA-seq data from seven patients (a total of 28 LUAD samples), we quantified NCCRP1 expression in tumor cells and assessed the abundance of infiltrating cells within each sample. After stratifying the seven patients into high-NCCRP1 and low-NCCRP1 expression groups based on the median expression level, we compared differences in proportions of various infiltrating cell populations between these groups.

Tissue microarray and immunohistochemistry

Tissue samples for immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis were obtained from an LUAD tissue microarray (HLugA150CS04; Shanghai Outdo Biotech). The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Outdo Biotech, and written informed consent was obtained from all patients. Subsequent IHC scoring and statistical analysis were restricted to intact tissue spots within the microarray. Following a 2 hours incubation at 65°C to melt the paraffin, the sections were subjected to sequential washes with xylene, 100% ethanol, 95% ethanol, 90% ethanol, 70% ethanol, 50% ethanol, and phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), each for 10 min. The sections were treated with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 10 min to block endogenous peroxidase and then washed three times with PBS for 5 min each. Antigen retrieval was carried out by heating the sections in 10 mM citrate buffer (pH 6.0) using a microwave oven. The sections were then blocked with goat serum for 1 hour at room temperature and subsequently incubated overnight at 4°C with the anti-NCCRP1 antibody (PA5-62693; Invitrogen, RRID: AB_2644511). After primary antibody incubation and three PBS washes, the sections were incubated with a biotin-labeled anti-rabbit IgG for 40 min at 37°C, followed by treatment with an avidin-biotin-peroxidase complex. The sections were subjected to DAB staining (ZLI-9018; Zsgb Bio) as per the manufacturer’s protocol. The staining intensity was assessed on a 0–3 scale (0, negative; 1, weak; 2, moderate; 3, strong), while the extent of staining was rated according to the percentage of positive cells (0: 0–5%; 1: 6–25%; 2: 26–50%; 3: 51–75%; 4: 76–100%). A composite score was derived from the multiplication of these two values.

Cell culture

LLC cells (CSTR: 19375.09.3101MOUTCM7, RRID: CVCL_4358), HEK-293 cells (CSTR: 19375.09.3101HUMGNHu43, RRID: CVCL_0045), A549 cells (CSTR: 19375.09.3101HUMSCSP503, RRID: CVCL_0023), and H1299 cells (CSTR: 19375.09.3101HUMTCHu160, RRID: CVCL_0060) were obtained from the Chinese Academy of Sciences Cell Bank (Shanghai, China). CMT167 cells (RRID: CVCL_2405) were obtained from the European Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures. All cell lines were verified by short tandem repeat analyses and tested negative for Mycoplasma. LLC cells, A549 cells, and H1299 cells were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 (G4535, Servicebio) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (164210, Procell) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin solution (P/S) (PB180120, Procell). HEK-293 cells and CMT167 cells were cultured in high glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (G4515, Servicebio) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% P/S. All cells were maintained under a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2 at 37°C.7

Cell transfection

Lentivirus was produced in HEK-293 cells (RRID: CVCL_0045) co-transfected with GV392-sgCtrl/sgNccrp1 or pLVX-scramble/shNCCRP1#1/shNCCRP1#2, psPAX2 (RRID: Addgene_12260), and pMD2.G (RRID: Addgene_12259) using liposomal reagent GTR (REGV007, GeneChem). Cells were transduced with the lentivirus and then selected using puromycin (5 µg/ mL) for 2 weeks. Then LLC cells (RRID: CVCL_4358) or CMT167 cells (RRID: CVCL_2405) were serially diluted into 96-well plates and monoclonal clones were subsequently isolated. Following expansion culture, gene knockout efficiency was verified by Sanger sequencing and western blot analysis. The plasmids GV392-sgNccrp1 and GV392-sgCtrl were provided by Shanghai GeneChem. The pLVX-scramble, shNCCRP1#1, shNCCRP1#2, psPAX2, and pMD2.G plasmids were available in our laboratory. The Nccrp1-targeting sgRNA sequence is 5′-TTC AAC CTC AGT CGG TGC AT-3′, and the negative control sgRNA sequence is 5′-CGC TTC CGC GGC CCG TTC AA-3′.

Western blot

Cells were lysed to extract total protein using Radioimmunoprecipitation Assay (RIPA) buffer (BL504A, Biosharp) containing protease inhibitor (BL612A, Biosharp) and phosphatase inhibitor (BL615A, Biosharp). Equal amounts of total protein, quantified prior to separation by Sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), were electrophoretically transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (IPVH00010, Merck Millipore). Following blocking with 5% skim milk (1172GR100, BioFroxx) for 1 hour at room temperature, the membrane was incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4℃, followed by incubation with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies. The chemiluminescent signals were detected using the e-BLOT Touch Imager contact imaging system. The antibodies used are as follows: anti-NCCRP1 (56–470, Prosci, RRID: AB_3717293), anti-NCCRP1 antibody (PA5-71715; Invitrogen, RRID: AB_2717569), anti-STAU1 (sc-390820, Santa Cruz, RRID: AB_3076745), anti-Ub (sc-8017, Santa Cruz, RRID: AB_628423), anti-OTUD4 (PA5-20707, Invitrogen, RRID: AB_11153736), anti-NEDD4 (2740, Cell Signaling Technology, RRID: AB_2149312), anti-Flag (M20008, Abmart, RRID: AB_2713960), anti-HA (M20003, Abmart, RRID: AB_2864345), anti-β-tubulin (P60039, Abmart), anti-β-actin (52901, Signalway Antibody, RRID: AB_3717289), Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG, Peroxidase Conjugated, H+L (BL003A, Biosharp, RRID: AB_2827666) and Goat Anti-Mouse IgG, Peroxidase Conjugated, H+L (BL001A, Biosharp, RRID: AB_2827665).

Tumor models and treatment

For subcutaneous tumor formation, SgCtrl or sgNccrp1 LLC cells, along with CMT167 cells (1×10⁶ cells/mouse suspended in 100 µL PBS), were separately implanted into the right flank of 8-week-old female C57BL/6 (RRID: IMSR_JAX:000664) and NSG (RRID: IMSR_NM-NSG-001) mice. After tumor detection, tumor length (a) and width (b) were measured every 3 days. Tumor volumes were computed using V=a×b2/2. Mice were sacrificed on reaching any endpoint: spontaneous death, tumor volume >1,500 mm³, tumor diameter >15 mm, or ≥20% body weight loss. Survival was monitored until day 40 or 60 post-inoculation. At the end of the experiments, mice were euthanized by anesthesia for tumor measurement and further analyses. To deplete CD8+ T cells or NK cells, mice received intraperitoneal injections of 200 µg anti-CD8α antibody (BE0004-1, Bio X Cell, RRID: AB_1107671) or anti-NK1.1 antibody (BE0036-1, Bio X Cell, RRID: AB_1107737) or InVivoMAb rat lgG2a isotype control (BE0089, Bio X Cell, RRID: AB_1107769) on days 0, 3, and 6 post-tumor inoculation. To deplete macrophages, mice were intraperitoneally injected with 200 µg of liposomal clodronate (C-005, LIPOSOMA) or PBS liposomes (P-005, LIPOSOMA) on days 0, 3, and 6 after tumor inoculation. To neutralize CXCL9 and CXCL10, mice received intraperitoneal injections of 100 µg anti-CXCL9 antibody (BE0309, Bio X Cell, RRID: AB_2736989) and 100 µg anti-CXCL10 antibody (BE0440, Bio X Cell, RRID: AB_3696163) or InVivoMAb polyclonal Armenian hamster IgG (BE0091, Bio X Cell, RRID: AB_1107773) on days 0, 3, 6, 9, 12, and 15 after tumor inoculation. To block CX3CR1, mice received daily intraperitoneal injections of AZD8797 (1 mg/kg, HY-13848, MedChemExpress) after tumor inoculation. For anti-PD-1 antibody and IFN-γ treatment, mice received intraperitoneal injections of 100 µg anti-PD-1 antibody (BE0146, Bio X Cell, RRID: AB_10949053) or 2.5 µg IFN-γ (C746, Novoprotein) on days 3, 6, 9, 12 and 15 after tumor inoculation. All the mice were purchased from Cyagen Biosciences and Shanghai Model Organisms. All animal experiments were performed in accordance with relevant regulatory standards and approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Bengbu Medical University.

ScRNA-seq library construction and sequencing

Subcutaneous xenograft tumors were dissociated into single-cell suspensions. Cell viability (>90%) was confirmed using a fluorescence cell analyzer with acridine orange/propidium iodide (AO/PI) reagents. 1×10⁶/mL cells were processed using the SeekOne Digital Droplet Single Cell 3’ Transcriptome Kit (Seekin Biotech). Single-cell capture via microfluidic emulsion (SeekOne Chip) with barcoded gel beads, complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis, amplification, and library construction were performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Libraries passing quality control (QC) (size: 350–750 bp, concentration ≥1 ng/µL) were sequenced on Illumina NovaSeq 6000 (≥50,000 reads/cell).

ScRNA-seq data analysis

Raw data were processed using fastp (RRID: SCR_016962) and SeekSoul tools to generate gene matrices. Subsequent analysis was performed in R (RRID: SCR_001905) with the Seurat package (V.4.2.0, RRID: SCR_016341). Cells underwent rigorous quality control: those with nFeature_RNA between 500 and 4,000, nCount_RNA between 500 and 20,000, and mitochondrial gene expression <5% were retained. DoubletFinder (V.2.0.4, RRID: SCR_018771) was employed to remove doublets. Batch effects between samples were corrected using Harmony (V.0.1.0, RRID: SCR_023543). Dimensionality reduction was performed using principal component analysis (PCA), retaining the top 50 principal components for graph-based clustering (resolution=3.0). Clusters were visualized using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (RRID: SCR_018217). Marker genes were identified using the FindAllMarkers, and cell type annotation was subsequently performed based on these markers with SingleR (V.1.81, RRID: SCR_023120) and the CellMarker (V.2.0, RRID: SCR_018503). Differential gene expression was analyzed and visualized via Seurat and ggplot2 (V.3.3.6, RRID: SCR_014601). Cell–cell interactions were interrogated through two approaches, including CellPhoneDB (V.4.0.0, RRID: SCR_017054) and NicheNet (RRID: SCR_023158). GO enrichment analyses were performed using clusterProfiler (RRID: SCR_016884).

Mouse tumor cell isolation

Mouse tumor tissues were rinsed in Hanks’ Balanced Salt Solution (BL561A, Biosharp) containing 5% FBS (HBSS-FBS) and minced into 1–2 mm fragments. Tissue fragments were enzymatically digested with 1 mg/mL collagenase I (GC19589, GLPBIO) and 1 mg/mL DNase I (HY-108882, MedChemExpress) at 37°C for ~1 hour until no obvious tissue fragments were detectable. After digestion, cells were filtered through a 70 µm filter. Red blood cells were then lysed using red blood cell lysis buffer (BL503B, Biosharp). Cells were washed two times with HBSS-FBS, resuspended in Stain Buffer (FBS) (554656, BD), and counted.

Reverse transcription was performed with HiScript II Q Select RT SuperMix (R233-01, Vazyme) to generate cDNA. Reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) was performed using ChamQ SYBR qPCR Kit (Q341-02, Vazyme) on a Roche LightCycler 480 real-time PCR system. The primers used were as follows: mouse Cx3cl1 (Forward, 5’-CAG TGG CTT TGC TCA TCC GCT A-3’; Reverse, 5’-AGC CTG GTG ATC CAG ATG CTT C-3’); mouse Stau1 (Forward, 5’-GCT CCT TAC AGA ACG AGG TCT TC-3’; Reverse, 5’-TCT CAG CAG CAT TAC GCT TGG C-3’); mouse Gapdh (Forward, 5’-CAT CAC TGC CAC CCA GAA GAC TG-3’; Reverse, 5’-ATG CCA GTG AGC TTC CCG TTC AG-3’); human CX3CL1 (Forward, 5’- ACA GCA CCA CGG TGT GAC GAA A-3’; Reverse, 5’-AAC AGC CTG TGC TGT CTC GTC T-3’); human GAPDH (Forward, 5’- GTC TCC TCT GAC TTC AAC AGC G −3’; Reverse, 5’- ACC ACC CTG TTG CTG TAG CCA A −3’). Mouse Gapdh or human GAPDH was used as the endogenous control.

Isolation and FACS of primary human CX3CR1+CD86+ MDMs

To generate primary human MDMs, peripheral blood mononuclear cells were first isolated from whole blood of healthy volunteers by density gradient centrifugation using Ficoll-Hypaque solution (LTS1077, TBDscience). Monocytes were differentiated into macrophages by culturing for 6 days in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 15% FBS, 1% P/S, and 20 ng/mL recombinant human M-CSF (C417, Novoprotein). The resulting MDMs were then treated with 100 ng/mL recombinant human IFN-γ (C014, Novoprotein) for 48 hours. Cells were harvested and stained with FITC anti-human CX3CR1 antibody (341606, BioLegend, AB_1626272) and APC anti-human CD86 antibody (374208, BioLegend, AB_2721449) at 4°C for 30 min in the dark. CX3CR1+CD86+ MDMs were sorted on a CytoFLEX SRT cell sorter (Beckman Coulter). All experiments involving human blood samples were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all healthy volunteers prior to blood collection.

Transwell recruitment assay

A549 or H1299 cells (scramble control, shNCCRP1#1, or shNCCRP1#2) were seeded into 24-well plates in 600 µL of culture medium. After 24 hours, FACS-sorted human CX3CR1+CD86+ MDMs (5×10⁴ cells in 150 µL) were loaded into the upper chamber of Transwell inserts (3422, Corning). After 36 hours of co-culture, the inserts were removed, and recruited cells on the lower surface of the membrane were fixed, stained with crystal violet, imaged, and quantified. In parallel, additional inserts were processed for ELISA: non-recruited cells on the upper surface were removed, and the inserts were transferred to a fresh 24-well plate containing culture medium. Recruited MDMs were cultured for an additional 24 hours, and the conditioned medium was collected to measure CXCL9 and CXCL10 levels by ELISA as previously described.

Dual-luciferase reporter gene assay

Reporter plasmids containing either the promoter region or the 3'UTR of Cx3cl1 cloned into the pGL3-Control vector (RRID: Addgene_212937) were purchased from BGI (China). SgCtrl or sgNccrp1 LLC cells were co-transfected with each reporter plasmid and the pRL-TK (E2241, Promega) plasmid using the liposomal transfection reagent GTR. After 48 hours of transfection, firefly and renilla luciferase activities were measured using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay Kit (DL101-01, Vazyme) on a Tecan Infinite 200 PRO microplate reader according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

RNA stability assay

LLC cells (RRID: CVCL_4358) were plated 24 hours prior to treatment. Cells were treated with 5 µM actinomycin D (Act D; A113142, Aladdin) and harvested at 0, 2, 4, 6, and 8 hours for total RNA extraction. Subsequently, cDNA was synthesized, and changes in the abundance of target genes were quantified by RT-qPCR to assess RNA stability.

Isobaric tags for relative and absolute quantitation

This study used Astral DIA proteomics to profile protein expression in sgNccrp1 and sgCtrl LLC cells, with services provided by BestMS Technologies (Qingdao, China). Protein identification and quantification were performed based on liquid chromatography-tandem MS data acquired using an Orbitrap Astral mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) coupled to a Vanquish Neo UHPLC (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Raw MS data were subsequently searched and analyzed using Spectronaut V.14.2. DEPs were identified with the following criteria: a fold change ≥1 for upregulation or ≤1 for downregulation, and a p value≤0.05 in pairwise comparisons.

RNA pull-down

Biotin-labeled sense and antisense sequences targeting the Cx3cl1 mRNA 3'UTR were obtained from Fitgene Biotechnology (Guangzhou, China). RNA pull-down assays were performed with Biotin RNA pull-down kit (FI8702, Fitgene Biotechnology) to capture and detect bound proteins, according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Proteins bound to the target RNA were identified by MS (Fitgene Biotechnology, Guangzhou, China), with specific interactions validated by western blot.

Protein degradation assay

LLC cells (RRID: CVCL_4358) were plated 24 hours prior to treatment. Cells were pretreated with 10 µM MG132 (HY-13259, MedChemExpress) for 8 hours. The medium was then replaced with fresh medium containing 50 µg/mL CHX (HY-12320, MedChemExpress). Cells were harvested at 0, 4, 8, and 12 hours post-CHX treatment, followed by total protein extraction. Protein expression was then analyzed by western blot. The shNedd4 plasmid (P23871, P23601) and the Flag-Otud4 plasmid (P60247) were purchased from Wuhan Miaoling Bioscience and Technology.

Ubiquitination assay

LLC cells (RRID: CVCL_4358) were co-transfected with plasmids encoding Flag-Stau1 and HA-Ub. 24 hours post-transfection, cells were treated with MG132 (10 µM, HY-13259, MedChemExpress) for 6 hours. Cells were then lysed on ice for 30 min using RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Cleared lysates were incubated overnight at 4°C with anti-Flag magnetic beads (HY-K0207, MedChemExpress, RRID: AB_3717275). Following immunoprecipitation and washing, the immunoprecipitated complexes were analyzed by western blot using an anti-HA antibody (M20003, Abmart, RRID: AB_2864345) to detect the ubiquitination of Flag-Stau1. To assess endogenous STAU1 ubiquitination levels, sgCtrl or sgNccrp1 LLC cells were treated with MG132 (10 µM) for 6 hours. Lysates were then incubated with STAU1 antibody (sc-390820, Santa Cruz, RRID: AB_3076745) coupled to protein A/G magnetic beads at 4°C overnight to immunoprecipitate the STAU1 proteins. The immunoprecipitated complexes were analyzed by Western blot using an anti-Ub antibody (sc-8017, Santa Cruz, RRID: AB_628423) to detect the ubiquitination of STAU1. The HA-Ub plasmid (P1838) was purchased from Wuhan Miaoling Bioscience and Technology. The Flag-Stau1 plasmid was purchased from BGI (China).

Co-immunoprecipitation

Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. After 30 min of lysis on ice, cell lysates were centrifuged at 13,000×g for 30 min at 4°C. The supernatant was harvested, and a 20% aliquot was reserved as the input sample. The remaining supernatant was incubated with Anti-Flag magnetic beads (HY-K0207, MedChemExpress) or Anti-HA magnetic beads (HY-K0201, MedChemExpress) overnight at 4°C under constant rotation. After magnetic separation and washing with phosphate-buffered saline with Tween-20 (PBST), the beads were resuspended in 1×SDS loading buffer and boiled at 100°C for 5 min. Protein complexes isolated by Co-IP were identified by MS (Applied Protein Technology, Shanghai, China), with candidate proteins validated by western blot.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis and graphing were performed using GraphPad Prism (V.8.2.1, RRID: SCR_002798). Data are presented as means±SD or SEM. The means of the two groups were compared using the unpaired Student’s t-test. The growth curves of the tumor volume were analyzed using two-way analysis of variance. Survival data were assessed by KM curves and log-rank tests. Differential gene expression analysis in scRNA-seq data were performed via the Wilcoxon test. P values were denoted as follows: *p<0.05, **p<0.01, ***p<0.001, ****p<0.0001 and ns, not significant.
