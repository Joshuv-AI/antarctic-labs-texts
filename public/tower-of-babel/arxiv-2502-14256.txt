QMCPy: A Python package for randomized low-discrepancy sequences, quasi-Monte Carlo, and fast kernel methods
Aleksei G. Sorokin

Abstract
Low-discrepancy (LD) sequences are widely used as efficient experimental designs for high-dimensional numerical integration and function approximation. This article presents QMCPy , an open-source Python library that provides a unified framework for randomized LD sequences, quasi-Monte Carlo (QMC) methods, and fast kernel-based computations. We systematically describe the supported rank- 1 1 lattices, digital nets (including higher-order constructions), and Halton point sets, together with randomization techniques such as random shifts, linear matrix scrambling (LMS), nested uniform scrambling (NUS), digital shifts, and digital permutations. We emphasize practical implementation issues such as extensible sequence generation, Gray code ordering, and efficient digital operations. Beyond integration, QMCPy supports fast kernel methods in reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces (RKHSs) by pairing LD point sets with shift-invariant (SI) and digitally shift-invariant (DSI) kernels, including higher-order variants, which yields structured Gram matrices. In particular, the resulting Gram matrices have circulant or recursive symmetric block Toeplitz (RSBT) structure, allowing the costs of matrix-vector products and linear solves to be reduced from 𝒪 ⁡ ( n 2 ) − 𝒪 ⁡ ( n 3 ) \mathcal{O}(n^{2})-\mathcal{O}(n^{3}) to 𝒪 ⁡ ( n ​ log ⁡ n ) \mathcal{O}(n\log n) by using fast Fourier transforms (FFTs) and fast Walsh–Hadamard transforms (FWHTs). We derive a new computable form of an order- 4 4 DSI kernel, develop efficient eigenvalue and transform-update algorithms, and present numerical experiments that demonstrate the accuracy, convergence rates, and computational efficiency of the implemented methods across a range of test integrands and dimensions. These capabilities in QMCPy provide a practical, reproducible platform for applying randomized QMC and kernel-based techniques in computational science and engineering.

Low-discrepancy (LD) sequences, also called quasi-random sequences, are designed to judiciously explore the unit cube in high dimensions. Despite the maturity of the underlying theory, software support for randomized LD sequences and their use in both QMC and fast kernel methods remains fragmented. In doing so, we aim to bridge the gap between the rich theoretical literature on QMC and kernel methods and a practical, reproducible Python implementation that can be readily used in computational science and engineering.

1.4
Contributions and scope

This article focuses on equipping
QMCPy
with expanded support for randomized low-discrepancy (LD) sequences and new tools for fast kernel methods, while providing a unified mathematical and implementation framework. Compared to previous work on
QMCPy
, the main contributions are to provide:

A broadened scope of randomized LD sequences.

We extend
QMCPy
to support a wider class of LD point sets and randomizations, including:

1.

higher-order digital nets constructed via digital interlacing,

2.

higher-order scrambling of digital nets using either linear matrix scrambling (LMS) or nested uniform scrambling (NUS), and

3.

Halton point sets with LMS- and NUS-based randomizations as well as digital permutations.

These features are integrated into a consistent Python interface, with careful attention to extensible sequence generation, Gray code ordering, and efficient digital operations.

Integration with the
LDData
repository.

QMCPy
now interfaces with the
LDData
repository, which provides collections of rank-
1
lattice generating vectors and digital net generating matrices in standardized formats. This integration enables users to access high-quality constructions (including higher-order variants) from the literature with minimal effort, and to combine them directly with
QMCPy
’s randomization and QMC routines.

SI and DSI kernels of varying smoothness.

QMCPy
implements families of shift-invariant (SI) and digitally shift-invariant (DSI) kernels of varying smoothness for use with rank-
1
lattices and digital nets, respectively, enabling fast kernel-based computations in RKHSs. In particular, we build on existing Walsh-based constructions to provide an alternative computable form of a higher-order
\alpha=4
DSI kernel (
Theorem

5.1
) originally studied in
[
3
]
; the full derivation and its relation to
Baldeaux et al. [2]
are given in
Section

5.3
.

Fast transforms and efficient Gram matrix eigenvalue updates.

By pairing lattices with SI kernels and digital nets with DSI kernels,
QMCPy
exploits circulant and recursive symmetric block Toeplitz (RSBT) structure in the resulting Gram matrices. We provide an implementation of the fast Walsh–Hadamard transform (FWHT) as well as wrappers of
SciPy
’s
[
107
]
FFT and IFFT algorithms called FFTBR and IFFTBR which additionally perform bit-reversals for compatibility with lattices in natural order. Moreover, we develop practical algorithms to update eigenvalues and transformed vectors as the number of points is doubled. These tools reduce the cost of matrix-vector products and linear solves from
{\mathcal{O}}(n^{2})-{\mathcal{O}}(n^{3})
to
{\mathcal{O}}(n\log n)
in
n
, while preserving extensibility of the underlying point sets.

Numerical validation and practical guidance.

We present numerical experiments that (1) compare the cost of generating various randomized LD sequences and applying fast transforms, and (2) study the root-mean-squared error (RMSE) convergence of randomized QMC estimators for a range of test integrands, including classical Genz functions and problems from uncertainty quantification. These results illustrate the strong empirical performance of higher-order digital nets with LMS and digital shifts, the benefits of pairing LD point sets with structured kernels, and the practical trade-offs among different randomizations. Together, they provide guidance on choosing point sets, randomizations, and kernels in applied settings.

Overall, this work positions
QMCPy
as a practical, reproducible platform that unifies randomized LD sequence generation, adaptive QMC methods, and fast kernel-based computations within a single, well-documented Python library.

3.1
QMC methods for approximating integrals and expectations

Monte Carlo (MC) and quasi-Monte Carlo (QMC) methods approximate a high-dimensional integral over the unit cube by the sample average of function evaluations at certain sampling locations:

\mu:=\int_{[0,1]^{d}}f({\boldsymbol{x}})\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{x}}\approx\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}f({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})=:{\widehat{\mu}}.

(1)

Here
f:[0,1]^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is a given integrand and
\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n-1}\subset[0,1]^{d}
is a point set. The integral on the left-hand side may be viewed as taking the expectation
{\mathbb{E}}[f({\boldsymbol{X}})]
where
{\boldsymbol{X}}
is a standard uniform
{\boldsymbol{X}}\sim{\mathcal{U}}[0,1]^{d}
. MC methods choose the sampling locations to be independent and identically distributed (IID)
d
-dimensional standard uniforms
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0},\dots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n-1}\overset{\mathrm{IID}}{\sim}{\mathcal{U}}[0,1]^{d}
. IID MC methods for (
1
) have a root-mean-squared-error (RMSE) of
{\mathcal{O}}(n^{-1/2})
.

QMC methods
[
74
,
19
,
55
,
16
,
63
,
99
,
17
]
replace IID point sets with LD point sets which more evenly cover the unit cube
[0,1]^{d}
. This greater uniformity leads to faster convergence of QMC methods compared to IID Monte Carlo methods for regular enough functions. Specifically, for integrands with bounded variation, plugging LD point sets into (
1
) yields a worst-case error rate of
{\mathcal{O}}(n^{-1+\delta})
with
\delta>0
arbitrarily small compared to the
{\mathcal{O}}(n^{-1/2})
worst-case error rate of IID Monte Carlo methods.

Randomized quasi-Monte Carlo (RQMC) uses randomized LD point sets to give improved convergence rates and enable practical error estimation. Specifically, if we again assume the integrand has bounded variation, then RQMC methods using digital nets with NUS achieve a RMSE of
{\mathcal{O}}(n^{-3/2+\delta})
. Moreover, let
\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1i}\}_{i=0}^{n-1},\dots,\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{Ri}\}_{i=0}^{n-1}
denote
R

is small, e.g.,

. Then, following

and
Owen [84, Chapter 17]

{\widehat{\mu}}=\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}{\widehat{\mu}}_{r}\qquad\text{where}\quad{\widehat{\mu}}_{r}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}f({\boldsymbol{x}}_{ri})

(2)

gives a practical
100(1-\tau)\%
confidence interval
{\widehat{\mu}}\pm t_{R-1,\tau/2}{\widehat{\sigma}}/\sqrt{R}
for
\mu
where
{\widehat{\sigma}}^{2}=(R-1)^{-1}\sum_{r=1}^{R}({\widehat{\mu}}_{r}-{\widehat{\mu}})^{2}
and

is the

distribution with

degrees of freedom.
will show how to code up such an estimate in
QMCPy
. For rank-
1
lattices, randomization is typically done using random shifts modulo

. For digital nets, randomization is typically done using NUS or the cheaper combination of LMS with digital shifts and/or digit permutations
[

,
82
,
83
,
80
]
.

in (

3.2
Kernelized worst-case error and kernel interpolation computations

Certain randomized LD point sets may be used to accelerate kernel-based computations. Let
\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n-1}
be a point set,
K:[0,1]^{d}\times[0,1]^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a symmetric positive definite (SPD) kernel, and
{\mathsf{K}}=\left(K({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i^{\prime}})\right)_{i,i^{\prime}=0}^{n-1}
be the
n\times n
SPD Gram matrix of kernel evaluations at all pairs of points. Two motivating kernel computations are described below:

Kernelized worst-case error computations.

The error of the approximation (
1
) can be bounded by the product of two terms: a measure of variation of the function
f
and a measure of the discrepancy of the point set
\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n-1}
. A classical example of this type of bound is the Koksma–Hlawka inequality
[
41
,
17
,
43
,
74
]
. The discrepancy can be used to design low-discrepancy point sets.
Rusch et al. [97]
present a newer idea in this area where good point sets are generated using neural networks trained with a discrepancy-based loss function.

Let us consider the more general setting of approximating the mean
\mu
in (
1
) by a weighted cubature scheme
\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\omega_{i}f({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})
. If
f
is assumed to lie in the RKHS
H
with kernel
K
, then
Hickernell [41]
showed that the squared worst-case error equals the generalized kernel discrepancy which takes the form

\int\int K({\boldsymbol{u}},{\boldsymbol{v}})\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{u}}\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{v}}-2\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\omega_{i}\int K({\boldsymbol{u}},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{u}}+\sum_{i,i^{\prime}=0}^{n-1}\omega_{i}\omega_{i^{\prime}}K({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i^{\prime}})

where integrals are understood to be over the unit cube domain
[0,1]^{d}
.
This worst-case error formula serves as the foundation for modern quasi-Monte Carlo (QMC) point construction, including fast component-by-component (CBC) construction algorithms in weighted and higher-order spaces
[
57
,
76
,
2
,
13
,
61
,
77
,
75
,
56
,
78
]
. Evaluating the squared worst-case error formula above requires computing
{\mathsf{K}}{\boldsymbol{\omega}}
where
{\boldsymbol{\omega}}=\{\omega_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n-1}
. Moreover, this squared worst-case error is minimized for a given point set by setting the weights to
{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{\star}={\mathsf{K}}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}
where
{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}=\{\kappa_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n-1}
collects
\kappa_{i}=\int_{[0,1]^{d}}K({\boldsymbol{u}},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{u}}
for
0\leq i<n
.

Kernel interpolation computations.

Suppose we would like to approximate
f:[0,1)^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
given observations
{\boldsymbol{y}}=\{y_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n-1}
of
f
at
\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n-1}
satisfying
y_{i}=f(x_{i})
for
0\leq i<n
. Then a kernel interpolant approximates
f
by
{\widehat{f}}\in H
where

{\widehat{f}}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\omega_{i}K({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})

and
{\boldsymbol{\omega}}={\mathsf{K}}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{y}}
. The above kernel interpolant may be reinterpreted as the posterior mean of a Gaussian process regression model. Fitting such a Gaussian process often includes optimizing a kernel’s hyperparameters, which may also be done by computing
{\mathsf{K}}{\boldsymbol{\omega}}
and
{\mathsf{K}}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{y}}

[
90
]
.

Underlying these problems, and many others, is the requirement to compute the matrix-vector product
{\mathsf{K}}{\boldsymbol{y}}
or solve the linear system
{\mathsf{K}}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{y}}
. The standard costs of these computations are
{\mathcal{O}}(n^{2})
and
{\mathcal{O}}(n^{3})
respectively. One method to reduce these high costs is to induce structure into
{\mathsf{K}}
.
Zeng et al. [111]
,
Zeng et al. [112]
proposed structure-inducing methods which use certain kernels
K
and points
\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n-1}
which enable both computations to be done at only
{\mathcal{O}}(n\log n)
cost. Two such pairings exist:

1.

When a rank-
1
lattice point set
\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n-1}
is paired with a SI kernel,
{\mathsf{K}}
has a circulant structure and thus matrix multiplication and linear system solutions in
{\mathsf{K}}
may be computed by performing FFTs and IFFTs. For rank-
1
lattices in natural order, the FFT and IFFT with bit-reversals, called FFTBR and IFFTBR respectively, are used.

2.

When a base-
2
digital net
\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n-1}
is paired with a DSI kernel,
{\mathsf{K}}
has a recursive symmetric block Toeplitz (RSBT) structure and thus matrix multiplication and linear system solutions in
{\mathsf{K}}
may be computed by only performing FWHTs.

Randomized low-discrepancy sequences

4.1
Common definitions

For a fixed base
b\in{\mathbb{N}}
, write
i\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
as
i=\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}{\mathsf{i}}_{t}b^{t}
so
{\mathsf{i}}_{t}
is the
t^{\mathrm{th}}
digit in the base-
b
expansion of
i
. We denote the vector of the first
m
base-
b
digits of
i
by

{\boldsymbol{D}}_{m}(i)=({\mathsf{i}}_{0},{\mathsf{i}}_{1},\dots,{\mathsf{i}}_{m-1})^{\intercal}.

For
{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{i}}}={\boldsymbol{D}}_{m}(i)
, we denote the digit-reversal of
i
by

F_{m}({\boldsymbol{\mathsf{i}}})=\sum_{t=1}^{m}{\mathsf{i}}_{t-1}b^{-t}\in[0,1)

Finally, let

v(i)=\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}{\mathsf{i}}_{t-1}b^{-t}

(3)

so that
v(i)=F_{m}({\boldsymbol{D}}_{m}(i))
when
0\leq i<b^{m}
, which is always the case in this article. The van der Corput sequence in base
b
is
\{v(i)\}_{i\geq 0}
.

4.2
Rank-
1
lattices

Consider a fixed
generating vector

{\boldsymbol{g}}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{d}
and fixed prime base
b
. Then we define the
lattice sequence

{\boldsymbol{z}}_{i}=v(i){\boldsymbol{g}}\bmod 1,\qquad i\geq 0.

(4)

If
n=b^{m}
for some
m\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, then the lattice point set
\{{\boldsymbol{z}}_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n-1}\subset[0,1)^{d}
is equivalent to
\{i{\boldsymbol{g}}/n\bmod 1\}_{i=0}^{n-1}
where we say the former is in
natural (radical inverse) order
while the latter is in
linear order
. When
n
is not of the form
b^{m}
, then linear and natural order will not generate the same lattice point set. Linear order assumes the number of points
n
is fixed, while natural order is extensible in
n
, i.e., increasing
n
through powers of
b
simply extends the sequence without discarding previous points. On the other hand, linear order assumes
n
is fixed, so increasing
n
through powers of
b
will not share points, e.g., in linear order with
b=2
the first
4
points when
n=4
are different from the first
4
points when
n=8
. Our implementation supports linear order for any base
b
, and natural order only for base
b=2
.

4.3
Digital nets

Consider a fixed prime base
b
and
generating matrices

{\mathsf{C}}_{1},\dots,{\mathsf{C}}_{d}\in\{0,...,b-1\}^{t_{\mathrm{max}}\times m}
where
t_{\mathrm{max}}\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
are fixed and
b
is a given prime base. Our implementation supports any prime base
b
, with extensions to theoretically-supported non-prime bases left as future work. The first
n=b^{m}
points of a digital sequence form a
digital net

\{{\boldsymbol{z}}_{i}\}_{i=0}^{b^{m}-1}\subset[0,1)^{d}
where, in
natural (digit-reversal) order
, for
1\leq j\leq d
and
0\leq i<b^{m}
with base-
b
digit vector
{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{i}}}={\boldsymbol{D}}_{m}(i)
,

z_{ij}=F_{t_{\mathrm{max}}}({\boldsymbol{\mathsf{z}}}_{ij})\qquad\text{with}\qquad{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{z}}}_{ij}={\mathsf{C}}_{j}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{i}}}\bmod b\in\{0,\dots,b-1\}^{t_{\mathrm{max}}}.

Digital shifts (DSs).

Similar to lattices, one may apply a shift
{\mathsf{\Delta}}\in[0,1)^{t_{\mathrm{max}}\times d}
to the digital net to get a digitally-shifted digital net
\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\}_{i=0}^{b^{m}-1}
where

x_{ij}=F_{t_{\mathrm{max}}}(({\boldsymbol{\mathsf{z}}}_{ij}+{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}_{j})\bmod b)

and
{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}_{j}
is the
j^{\mathrm{th}}
column of
{\mathsf{\Delta}}
. Digital nets with random digital shifts use
{\mathsf{\Delta}}\overset{\mathrm{IID}}{\sim}{\mathcal{U}}\{0,\dots,b-1\}^{t_{\mathrm{max}}\times d}
with IID uniform entries from
\{0,\dots,b-1\}
.

Digital permutation (DPs).

In what follows we will denote permutations of
\{0,\dots,b-1\}
by
\pi
. Suppose we are given a set of permutations

{\mathsf{\Pi}}=\{\pi_{j,t}:\quad 1\leq j\leq d,\quad 0\leq t<t_{\mathrm{max}}\}.

Then we may construct the digitally permuted digital net
\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\}_{i=0}^{b^{m}-1}
where

x_{ij}=F_{t_{\mathrm{max}}}((\pi_{j,0}({\mathsf{z}}_{ij0}),\pi_{j,1}({\mathsf{z}}_{ij1}),\dots,\pi_{j,t_{\mathrm{max}}-1}({\mathsf{z}}_{ij(t_{\mathrm{max}}-1)}))^{\intercal}).

Randomly permuted digital nets use independent permutations chosen uniformly over all permutations of
\{0,\dots,b-1\}
.

Nested Uniform Scrambling (NUS).

NUS is often called Owen scrambling for its conception by
Owen [81]
. As before,
\pi
denotes permutations of
\{0,\dots,b-1\}
. Now suppose we are given a set of permutations

{\mathcal{P}}=\{\pi_{j,{\mathsf{v}}_{1}\cdots{\mathsf{v}}_{t}}:\,1\leq j\leq d,\,0\leq t<t_{\mathrm{max}},\,{\mathsf{v}}_{k}\in\{0,\dots,b-1\},\,0\leq k\leq t\}.

Then a
nested uniform scrambling
of a digital net is
\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\}_{i=0}^{b^{m}-1}
where

x_{ij}=F_{t_{\mathrm{max}}}\left(\begin{bmatrix}\pi_{j,}({\mathsf{z}}_{ij0})\\
\pi_{j,{\mathsf{z}}_{ij0}}({\mathsf{z}}_{ij1})\\
\pi_{j,{\mathsf{z}}_{ij0}{\mathsf{z}}_{ij1}}({\mathsf{z}}_{ij2})\\
\vdots\\
\pi_{j,{\mathsf{z}}_{ij0}{\mathsf{z}}_{ij1}\cdots{\mathsf{z}}_{ij(t_{\mathrm{max}}-2)}}({\mathsf{z}}_{ij(t_{\mathrm{max}}-1)})\end{bmatrix}\right).

As the number of elements in
{\mathcal{P}}
is

\lvert{\mathcal{P}}\rvert=d(1+b+b^{2}+\dots+b^{t_{\mathrm{max}}-1})=d\frac{b^{t_{\mathrm{max}}}-1}{b-1},

NUS implementations cannot afford to generate all permutations a priori. In
QMCPy
, permutations are generated and stored only as needed. At the time of writing, this is accomplished with an adaptively built search tree where each node either stores the permutation for that digit if it has children or stores permutations for all remaining digits if it does not have children. As with digitally-permuted digital nets, NUS uses independent uniform random permutations.

Linear matrix scrambling (LMS).

LMS is a computationally cheaper version of NUS which has empirically shown the same improved convergence rate of NUS for many practical problems. LMS uses scrambling matrices
{\mathsf{S}}_{1},\dots,{\mathsf{S}}_{d}\in\{0,\dots,b-1\}^{t_{\mathrm{max}}\times t_{\mathrm{max}}}
and sets the LMS generating matrices
{\widetilde{\mathsf{C}}}_{1},\dots,{\widetilde{\mathsf{C}}}_{d}\in\{0,\dots,b-1\}^{t_{\mathrm{max}}\times m}
so that

{\widetilde{\mathsf{C}}}_{j}={\mathsf{S}}_{j}{\mathsf{C}}_{j}\bmod b

for
j=1,\dots,d
. Following
Owen [82]
, let us denote elements in
\{1,\dots,b-1\}
by
h
and elements in
\{0,\dots,b-1\}
by
g
. Then common structures for
{\mathsf{S}}_{j}
include lower-triangular digit matrices with positive diagonals

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}h_{11}&0&0&0&\dots\\
g_{21}&h_{22}&0&0&\dots\\
g_{31}&g_{32}&h_{33}&0&\dots\\
g_{41}&g_{42}&g_{43}&h_{44}&\dots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}h_{1}&0&0&0&\dots\\
g_{2}&h_{1}&0&0&\dots\\
g_{3}&g_{2}&h_{1}&0&\dots\\
g_{4}&g_{3}&g_{2}&h_{1}&\dots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\end{bmatrix},\quad\text{and}\quad

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}h_{1}&0&0&0&\dots\\
h_{1}&h_{2}&0&0&\dots\\
h_{1}&h_{2}&h_{3}&0&\dots\\
h_{1}&h_{2}&h_{3}&h_{4}&\dots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\end{bmatrix}

which corresponds respectively to Matoušek’s linear scrambling
[
72
]
, Tezuka’s
i
-binomial scrambling
[
105
]
, and Owen’s striped LMS
[
82
]
(not to be confused with NUS which is often called Owen scrambling). These three LMS methods choose
g
and
h
values all independently and uniformly from
\{1,\dots,b-1\}
and
\{0,\dots,b-1\}
respectively.
Owen [82]
analyzes these scramblings and their connection to NUS.

Digital interlacing for higher-order nets.

Digital interlacing enables the construction of higher-order digital nets. For integer order
\alpha\geq 1
, interlacing
{\mathsf{A}}_{1},{\mathsf{A}}_{2},\dots\in\{0,\dots,b-1\}^{t_{\mathrm{max}}\times m}
gives
\widehat{{\mathsf{A}}}_{1},\widehat{{\mathsf{A}}}_{2},\dots\in\{0,\dots,b-1\}^{\alpha t_{\mathrm{max}}\times m}
satisfying
\widehat{{\mathsf{A}}}_{jtk}=A_{\widehat{j},\widehat{t},k}
where
\widehat{j}=\alpha(j-1)+(t\bmod\alpha)+1
and
\widehat{t}=\lfloor t/\alpha\rfloor
for
j\geq 1
and
0\leq t<\alpha t_{\mathrm{max}}
and
1\leq k\leq m
. Here
\widehat{{\mathsf{A}}}_{jtk}
is the element in row
t
and column
k
of matrix
\widehat{{\mathsf{A}}}_{j}
and similarly for
{\mathsf{A}}_{\widehat{j},\widehat{t},k}
. For example, with
m=2
,
t_{\mathrm{max}}=2
, and
\alpha=2
we have

{\mathsf{A}}_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}a_{101}&a_{102}\\
a_{111}&a_{112}\end{bmatrix},\quad{\mathsf{A}}_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}a_{201}&a_{202}\\
a_{211}&a_{212}\end{bmatrix},\quad\longrightarrow\quad\widehat{{\mathsf{A}}}_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}a_{101}&a_{102}\\
a_{201}&a_{202}\\
a_{111}&a_{112}\\
a_{211}&a_{212}\end{bmatrix}

{\mathsf{A}}_{3}=\begin{bmatrix}a_{301}&a_{302}\\
a_{311}&a_{312}\end{bmatrix},\quad{\mathsf{A}}_{4}=\begin{bmatrix}a_{401}&a_{402}\\
a_{411}&a_{412}\end{bmatrix}\quad\longrightarrow\quad\widehat{{\mathsf{A}}}_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}a_{301}&a_{302}\\
a_{401}&a_{402}\\
a_{311}&a_{312}\\
a_{411}&a_{412}\end{bmatrix}

where
a_{jtk}\in\{0,\dots,b-1\}
. Without scrambling, higher-order digital nets may be directly generated from
\widehat{{\mathsf{C}}}_{1},\dots,\widehat{{\mathsf{C}}}_{d}
, the interlaced generating matrices resulting from interlacing
{\mathsf{C}}_{1},\dots,{\mathsf{C}}_{\alpha d}
. Higher-order NUS
[
23
]
requires generating a digital net from
{\mathsf{C}}_{1},\dots,{\mathsf{C}}_{\alpha d}
, applying NUS to the resulting
\alpha d
-dimensional digital net, and then interlacing the resulting digit matrices
\{{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{z}}}_{i,1}^{\intercal}\}_{i=0}^{b^{m}-1},\dots,\{{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{z}}}_{i,\alpha d}^{\intercal}\}_{i=0}^{b^{m}-1}\in\{0,\dots,b-1\}^{t_{\mathrm{max}}\times m}
. For higher-order LMS with interlacing, one applies LMS to the generating matrices
{\mathsf{C}}_{1},\dots,{\mathsf{C}}_{\alpha d}
to get
{\widetilde{\mathsf{C}}}_{1},\dots,{\widetilde{\mathsf{C}}}_{\alpha d}
, then interlaces
{\widetilde{\mathsf{C}}}_{1},\dots,{\widetilde{\mathsf{C}}}_{\alpha d}
to get
\widehat{{\widetilde{\mathsf{C}}}}_{1},\dots,\widehat{{\widetilde{\mathsf{C}}}}_{d}
, then generates the digital net from
\widehat{{\widetilde{\mathsf{C}}}}_{1},\dots,\widehat{{\widetilde{\mathsf{C}}}}_{d}
. As we show in the numerical experiments in
Section

6
, LMS is significantly faster than NUS (especially for higher-order nets) while still achieving higher-order rates of RMSE convergence.

A subtle difference between the above presentation and practical implementation is that
t_{\mathrm{max}}
may change with randomization in practice. For example, suppose we are given generating matrices
{\mathsf{C}}_{1},\dots,{\mathsf{C}}_{d}\in\{0,\dots,b-1\}^{32\times 32}
but would like the shifted digital net to have
64
digits of precision. Then we should generate
{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}\in\{0,\dots,b-1\}^{t_{\mathrm{max}}\times d}
with
t_{\mathrm{max}}=64
and treat
{\mathsf{C}}_{j}
as
t_{\mathrm{max}}\times t_{\mathrm{max}}
matrices with appropriate trailing rows and columns zeroed out.

This is backed by both our generation-time experiment and RMSE convergence experiment described in

SI kernels, rank-

A kernel is said to be
shift-invariant
(SI) when

K({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime})={\widetilde{K}}(({\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime})\bmod 1)

for some
{\widetilde{K}}
, i.e., the kernel is only a function of the component-wise difference between inputs modulo
1
. One class of SI kernels take the form

K({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime})=\zeta\sum_{{\mathfrak{u}}\subseteq\{1,\dots,d\}}\gamma_{\mathfrak{u}}\prod_{j\in{\mathfrak{u}}}K_{\alpha_{j}}(x_{j},x_{j}^{\prime})

(6)

where

K_{\alpha}(x,x^{\prime})=\frac{(2\pi)^{2\alpha}}{(-1)^{\alpha+1}(2\alpha)!}B_{2\alpha}((x-x^{\prime})\bmod 1)

with smoothness parameters
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{d})\in{\mathbb{N}}^{d}
, general scale
\zeta>0
, weights
\{\gamma_{\mathfrak{u}}\}_{{\mathfrak{u}}\subseteq\{1,\dots,d\}}
, and
B_{\ell}
denoting the Bernoulli polynomial of degree
\ell
. The corresponding RKHS
H
is a weighted periodic unanchored Sobolev space of smoothness
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{d}
with norm

\lVert f\rVert_{H}^{2}:=\sum_{{\mathfrak{u}}\subseteq\{1,\dots,d\}}\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2\lvert{\mathfrak{u}}\rvert}\gamma_{\mathfrak{u}}}\int_{[0,1]^{\lvert{\mathfrak{u}}\rvert}}\left\lvert\int_{[0,1]^{s-\lvert{\mathfrak{u}}\rvert}}\left(\prod_{j\in{\mathfrak{u}}}\frac{\partial^{\alpha_{j}}}{\partial y_{j}^{\alpha_{j}}}\right)f({\boldsymbol{y}})\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{y}}_{-{\mathfrak{u}}}\right\rvert^{2}\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{y}}_{\mathfrak{u}},

where
f:[0,1]^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
,
{\boldsymbol{y}}_{\mathfrak{u}}=({\boldsymbol{y}}_{j})_{j\in{\mathfrak{u}}}
,
{\boldsymbol{y}}_{-{\mathfrak{u}}}:=(y_{j})_{j\in\{1,\dots,d\}\setminus{\mathfrak{u}}}
, and
\lvert{\mathfrak{u}}\rvert
is the cardinality of
{\mathfrak{u}}
. The space
H
is a special case of the weighted Korobov space which has real smoothness parameter
\alpha
characterizing the rate of decay of Fourier coefficients
[
52
,
53
,
13
,
12
,
100
,
60
]
.

The kernel (
6

2^{d}
terms and thus becomes impractical to compute for large

. A simplified space assumes
\gamma_{\mathfrak{u}}
are
product weights
which take the form
\gamma_{\mathfrak{u}}=\prod_{j\in{\mathfrak{u}}}\gamma_{j}
for some
\{\gamma_{j}\}_{j=1}^{d}
. The kernel with product weights takes the simplified form

K({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime})=\zeta\prod_{j=1}^{d}\left(1+\gamma_{j}K_{\alpha_{j}}(x_{j},x_{j}^{\prime})\right).

(7)

Kaarnioja et al. [53]
gives a review of lattice-based kernel interpolation in such weighted spaces along with the development of serendipitous weights, which are a special form of high-performance product weights.

Suppose
n=2^{m}
for some
m\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
and let
\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\}_{i=0}^{b^{m}-1}
be a shifted lattice with shift
{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}\in[0,1)^{d}
generated in
linear order
. For
0\leq i,k<n

K({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k})=K\left(\frac{(i-k){\boldsymbol{g}}}{n},{\boldsymbol{0}}\right),

so the Gram matrix
{\mathsf{K}}
is circulant. Let
R_{m}(i)
flip the first
m
bits of

in base
b=2
so that if
i=\sum_{t=0}^{m-1}{\mathsf{i}}_{t}2^{t}
then
R(i)=\sum_{t=0}^{m-1}{\mathsf{i}}_{m-t-1}2^{t}
. The first step in the radix-2 decimation-in-time (DIT) FFT (a base-
2
Cooley–Tukey algorithm)
[
11
]
is to reorder the inputs
\{y_{i}\}_{i=0}^{2^{m}-1}
into bit-reversed order
\{y_{R(i)}\}_{i=0}^{2^{m}-1}
. The last step in computing the IFFT is to reorder the outputs
\{y_{R(i)}\}_{i=0}^{2^{m}-1}
into bit-reversed order
\{y_{i}\}_{i=0}^{2^{m}-1}
. Therefore, one should generate lattice points in
natural order
and skip the first step of the FFT and last step of the inverse FFT. We call such algorithms the FFTBR and IFFTBR respectively.

The Fourier matrix is
\overline{{\mathsf{F}}_{m}}=\{W_{m}^{ij}\}_{i,j=0}^{2^{m}-1}
where
W_{m}=\exp(-2\pi\sqrt{-1}/2^{m})
. Let
\overline{{\mathsf{T}}_{m}}=\{W_{m}^{iR_{m}(j)}\}_{i,j=0}^{2^{m}-1}
so that

\overline{{\mathsf{F}}_{m}\{y_{i}\}_{j=0}^{2^{m}-1}}=\overline{{\mathsf{T}}_{m}\{y_{R(j)}\}_{j=0}^{2^{m}-1}}.

For
\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\}_{i=0}^{2^{m}-1}
a lattice in natural order, we have the eigendecomposition

{\mathsf{K}}=\frac{1}{n}{\mathsf{T}}_{m}{\mathsf{\Lambda}}_{m}\overline{{\mathsf{T}}_{m}}

where
{\mathsf{\Lambda}}_{m}
is a diagonal matrix of eigenvalues.

Notice that
\overline{{\mathsf{T}}_{m+1}}=\left\{W_{m+1}^{iR_{m+1}(j)}\right\}_{i,j=0}^{2^{m+1}-1}
. For
0\leq j<2^{m}
we have

so

. For

we have
R_{m+1}(j)=2R_{m}(j-2^{m})+1
so
W^{iR_{m+1}(j)}_{m+1}=W_{m}^{iR(j-2^{m})}W_{m+1}^{i}
. Moreover, for
0\leq i<2^{m}
we have
W_{m+1}^{2^{m}+i}=-W_{m+1}^{i}
. Define
{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{w}}}_{m}=\{W_{m+1}^{i}\}_{i=0}^{2^{m}-1}
. Then

\overline{{\mathsf{T}}_{m+1}}=\begin{bmatrix}\overline{{\mathsf{T}}_{m}}&\mathrm{diag}({\widetilde{\boldsymbol{w}}}_{m})\overline{{\mathsf{T}}_{m}}\\
\overline{{\mathsf{T}}_{m}}&-\mathrm{diag}({\widetilde{\boldsymbol{w}}}_{m})\overline{{\mathsf{T}}_{m}}\end{bmatrix}

and
{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{w}}}_{m+1}
is the
\alpha=2
interlacing of
{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{w}}}_{m}
and
W_{m+1}{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{w}}}_{m}
.

5.3
DSI kernels, digital nets, and the FWHT

For
x,y\in[0,1)
with
x=\sum_{t=1}^{m}{\mathsf{x}}_{t-1}b^{-t}
and
y=\sum_{t=1}^{m}{\mathsf{y}}_{t-1}b^{-t}
let

x\oplus y=\sum_{t=1}^{m}(({\mathsf{x}}_{t-1}+{\mathsf{y}}_{t-1})\bmod b)b^{-t}\quad\text{and}\quad x\ominus y=\sum_{t=1}^{m}(({\mathsf{x}}_{t-1}-{\mathsf{y}}_{t-1})\bmod b)b^{-t}

denote digital addition and subtraction respectively. Note that in base
b=2
these operations are both equivalent to the exclusive or (XOR) between bits. For vectors
{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}\in[0,1)^{d}
, digital operations
{\boldsymbol{x}}\oplus{\boldsymbol{y}}
and
{\boldsymbol{x}}\ominus{\boldsymbol{y}}
act component-wise.
A kernel is said to be
digitally-shift-invariant
(DSI) when

K({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime})={\widetilde{K}}({\boldsymbol{x}}\ominus{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime})

for some
{\widetilde{K}}
.

Suppose
n=b^{m}
for some
m\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
and let
\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\}_{i=0}^{b^{m}-1}
be a digitally-shifted digital net in
natural order
(possibly of higher-order and/or with LMS).
Rathinavel and Hickernell [92, Theorem 5.3.1 and Theorem 5.3.2]
shows that, for
b=2
, the Gram matrix
{\mathsf{K}}
is
RSBT
(recursive symmetric block Toeplitz) which implies the eigendecomposition

{\mathsf{K}}=\frac{1}{n}{\mathsf{H}}_{m}{\mathsf{\Lambda}}_{m}{\mathsf{H}}_{m}

where
{\mathsf{H}}_{m}
is the
2^{m}\times 2^{m}
Hadamard matrix defined by
{\mathsf{H}}^{(0)}=[1]
and the relationship

{\mathsf{H}}_{m+1}=\begin{bmatrix}{\mathsf{H}}_{m}&{\mathsf{H}}_{m}\\
{\mathsf{H}}_{m}&-{\mathsf{H}}_{m}\end{bmatrix}.

Multiplying by
{\mathsf{H}}_{m}
can be done at
{\mathcal{O}}(n\log n)
cost using the FWHT.
Note that
{\mathsf{K}}
is not generally RSBT when
\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\}_{i=0}^{b^{m}-1}
has a NUS.

We now describe one-dimensional DSI kernels
K_{\alpha}(x,x^{\prime})
of higher-order smoothness
\alpha\geq 2
. Weighted sums over products of these one-dimensional kernels may be used to construct higher-dimensional kernels, e.g., those with product weights

K({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime})=\zeta\prod_{j=1}^{d}\left(1+\gamma_{j}K_{\alpha_{j}}(x_{j},x_{j}^{\prime})\right).

To begin, let us write
k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
as
k=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\#k}{\mathsf{k}}_{a_{\ell}}b^{a_{\ell}}
where
a_{1}>\cdots>a_{\#k}\geq 0
are the
\#k
indices of non-zero digits
{\mathsf{k}}_{a_{\ell}}\in\{1,\dots,b-1\}
in the base-
b
expansion of
k
. Then the
k^{\mathrm{th}}
Walsh coefficient is

\mathrm{wal}_{k}(x)=e^{2\pi\sqrt{-1}/b\sum_{\ell=1}^{\#k}{\mathsf{k}}_{a_{\ell}}{\mathsf{x}}_{a_{\ell}+1}}.

For
\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}
we also define the weight function from
Dick [22]
as

\mu_{\alpha}(k)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\min\{\alpha,\#k\}}(a_{\ell}+1).

For
\alpha\geq 2

K_{\alpha}(x,x^{\prime})=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\frac{\mathrm{wal}_{k}(x\ominus x^{\prime})}{b^{\mu_{\alpha}(k)}}

(8)

]

Numerical experiments

All numerical experiments were carried out on a 2025 M4 MacBook Air.
Figure

4
compares the wall-clock time required to generate point sets and perform fast transforms for high dimensions and/or number of randomizations. For IID and randomized LD generators, we vary the number of randomizations
R
, the number of dimensions
d
, and the sequence size
n
. Timings include initialization, and a new point set is generating for each plotted
(R,n,d)
. Fast transforms are applied to
R
sequences of size
n
in a vectorized fashion.

Following the theory, our implementation scales linearly in the number of dimensions and randomizations. For large numbers of randomizations, our vectorized
QMCPy
LD generators significantly outperform the looped implementations in
SciPy
and
PyTorch
. For fast transforms, our FFTBR and IFFTBR implementations are the same speed as
SciPy
’s FFT and IFFT algorithms. Our FWHT implementation is significantly faster than the
SymPy
implementation, especially when applying the FWHT to multiple sequences simultaneously. IID points, randomly shifted lattices, and digital nets (DNs) with LMS and digital shifts (DSs), including higher-order versions, are the fastest sequences to generate. Digital nets in base
b=2
exploit Gray code order, integer storage of bit-vectors, and exclusive or (XOR) operations to perform digital addition. Halton point sets are slower to generate as they cannot exploit these advantages. NUS, especially higher-order versions, are significantly slower to generate than LMS randomizations. Even so, an LMS interlacing scrambling of digital shifts empirically achieves the
{\mathcal{O}}(n^{-\alpha-1/2+\delta})
RMSE convergence rate which is theoretically guaranteed only for NUS scrambling, as we show in the next experiment.

Conclusion

This work has reviewed our
QMCPy
implementations of routines for generating randomized LD sequences and applying them to fast kernel methods. We presented lattices, digital nets, and Halton point sets with randomizations spanning shifts, digital permutations, digital shifts, LMS, and NUS. Higher-order scramblings for digital nets were also considered, including higher-order NUS and LMS with interlacing. We described classes of kernels which pair with LD sequences to enable fast kernel computations. These included SI and DSI kernels of higher-order smoothness. The fast kernel methods utilized the fast transform algorithms FFTBR, IFFTBR, and FWHT. We showed our implementations in
QMCPy
achieve state-of-the-art speed, accuracy, and flexibility.
