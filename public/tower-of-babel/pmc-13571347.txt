Oral Albuterol for the Treatment of Rate-Dependent QT Prolongation and Polymorphic Ventricular Tachycardia
Authors: Pooja P Shah, Marissa Heyer, Uri Hochfeld, Robert Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveWe describe a case of rate-related QTc prolongation and related polymorphic ventricular tachycardia (VT) managed using oral albuterol to increase the heart rate, decrease the QTc, and control the VT instead of the conventional management with an automated implantable cardioverter-defibrillator.Key Steps1) Establish that the patient had QTc prolongation. 2) Verify that polymorphic VT was related to prolonged QTc, which was in turn related to heart rate by titrating dopamine in the intensive care unit and monitoring with telemetry. 3) Titrate albuterol to increase heart rate and then discontinue dopamine. 4) Monitor the patient for stability on oral albuterol.Potential PitfallsThe patient became mildly hypotensive due to albuterol; this was managed with midodrine. The patient may experience other arrhythmias due to albuterol.Take-Home MessageAlbuterol is an oral B1 agonist that may be repurposed for increasing heart rate in rate-related polymorphic VT, as in this case, and perhaps in cases of symptomatic sinus bradycardia.Key words: oral albuterol, oral B agonist, oral medication, QT prolongation, rate related prolonged QTc, rate related VT (ventricular tachycardia)

Ventricular arrhythmias can be triggered by QT prolongation and cause ventricular tachycardia and cardiac arrest. QT interval is inversely related to heart rate (HR), and bradycardia can cause widening of the QT interval, resulting in life-threatening polymorphic ventricular tachycardia (VT). Polymorphic VT is more commonly noted with heart block rather than with sinus bradycardia.1 The QT prolongation is related to delay in the potassium current with excessive positive ions within the cell membrane, causing prolonged repolarization. If an ectopic beat falls on the T wave (R-on-T), then VT and torsades de pointes may be triggered.2 We describe a case of oral albuterol use to maintain an elevated HR, a decreased QT, and prevention of VT.

Take-Home Messages

•Albuterol tablets are an oral B1 agonist which can be repurposed as a novel therapy for increasing heart rate, as in this case, with rate-related polymorphic VT for which the usual treatment of an automated implantable cardioverter-defibrillator was not practical.

Case Summary

The patient was a 65-year-old with a history of mildly decompensated liver cirrhosis and ascites, who was admitted to the medical intensive care unit (MICU) with lethargy and abdominal fullness. The patient was not known to be taking any medications on admission. QTc on the initial electrocardiogram (ECG) on June 29 could not be measured, as T waves appeared flat. (Figure 1). The patient was found to have spontaneous bacterial peritonitis complicated by septic shock secondary to Escherichia coli, large-volume ascites, and acute kidney injury. Initially the patient was receiving vasopressors, which were tapered off; ultimately, the patient was transferred to the medical floors hemodynamically stable and managed with meropenem, albumin, and midodrine. Several days later on July 12, the patient had an episode of nonsustained VT, likely torsades de pointes. The patient was started on dopamine 5 μg/kg/min. Later the patient became acutely hypotensive, and pulseless sustained VT was noted on telemetry monitoring, requiring cardiopulmonary resuscitation. Electrolytes were normal at that time, with Na 143, K 4.0, Ca 8.8, and Mg 1.73. Sepsis had resolved already and was unlikely to be contributing to the VT. Echocardiogram showed low normal left ventricular systolic function with an ejection fraction of 50% to 55% and grade I left ventricular diastolic dysfunction. Right ventricular systolic pressure was estimated to be 49 mm Hg. Severe cardiomyopathy due to cirrhosis was not seen. Electrocardiogram before cardiac arrest was notable for nonsustained polymorphic VT, possibly torsades, with an HR of 93 beats/min and QTc of 0.59 seconds (Figure 2). Return of spontaneous circulation was achieved after 3 minutes of cardiopulmonary resuscitation, 1 dose of epinephrine, and 1 defibrillation at 300 J, after which the patient was awake and alert. At the time of the arrest, the only proarrhythmic drug was pantoprazole, which was discontinued.

Procedural Steps

The patient was transferred to the MICU on dopamine infusion. ECG performed the next day (July 13) showed an HR of 109 beats/min and QTc of 0.445 on dopamine 10 μg/kg/min (Figure 3). As dopamine was down-titrated, the patient's QTc became prolonged, with decreasing heart rate on telemetry. The patient was seen to have nonsustained VT on the monitors. Restarting dopamine corrected the problem. The patient had heart rate dependency for this arrhythmia. The patient was seen by electrophysiology and was not a candidate for pacemaker or automated implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (AICD) placement. Patient was felt to be a poor candidate, as the patient would not follow up after placement of a permanent pacemaker or AICD. The patient was a visitor from another country and planned to return there after discharge. Amiodarone and sotalol would have increased QT further. The patient remained dopamine dependent for 1 week. On July 19, HR was 100 beats/min and QTc was 0.470 still on dopamine. (Figure 4). Given a lack of established oral drugs for increasing the heart rate and patient's accompanying life-threatening arrhythmia, we decided to initiate and titrate oral albuterol tablets for a positive chronotropic effect on July 19. Initially the patient was started on albuterol tablets 4 mg orally every 6 hours. The patient had mild hypotension from the albuterol, but this was managed with midodrine. By July 22, the patient was off of dopamine and on albuterol 6 mg orally every 6 hours. ECG on July 22 showed HR of 102 beats/min and QTc of 0.474. Blood pressure was 108/55 mm Hg (Figure 5). The patient was ultimately transferred out of the MICU to the general medical floor on oral albuterol in stable condition.

Potential Pitfalls

The albuterol tablets could have caused hypertension, but this was not observed. Blood pressure was lower, perhaps due to B2 effects, and the patient was managed with midodrine. The patient did not have any serious side effects such as seizures, hypokalemia, or other arrhythmias on discharge from the intensive care unit. The patient did not report any chest pain, palpitations, anxiety, tremor, diaphoresis, headache, muscle cramps, or paradoxical bronchospasm. In general, the patient tolerated the medication well.

Conclusions

Patients requiring continuous intravenous infusions of dopamine for persistent symptomatic bradycardia are encountered routinely. Tachycardia is a well-established consequence of both oral and nebulized albuterol as described in the literature via beta adrenergic receptors. Minimal literature exists for albuterol use or other oral medications in persistent symptomatic bradycardia and polymorphic VT prevention in particular. We present a case of prolonged QTc that led to polymorphic VT and cardiac arrest requiring dopamine infusion for hemodynamic stability. This patient was not eligible for other oral agents such as amiodarone or sotalol, nor a candidate for implantable cardioverter-defibrillator placement. The addition of oral albuterol allowed for discontinuation of dopamine infusion and safe transfer out of the MICU without further QT prolongation or VT episodes.

Oral albuterol a B1, B2 agonist for asthma, may be repurposed as a novel alternative for rate-related QTc prolongation if other alternatives are not practical. Additionally, patients with symptomatic sinus bradycardia might also be candidates for oral albuterol. Further research is needed to establish the safety and generalizability of albuterol for these patient populations. Long-term clinical follow-up of this patient would be beneficial to see if the patient becomes refractory to albuterol and to see if complications develop. Currently there is no approved oral drug for increasing heart rate on a long-term basis in the U.S.
