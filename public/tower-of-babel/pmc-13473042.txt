Simultaneous partial volume correction and denoising of brain PET images, using transformers and transfer learning
Authors: Sanaz Kaviani, Amirhossein Sanaat, Mersede Mokri, Habib Zaidi, Jean-François Carrier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPositron emission tomography (PET) is a key tool for quantitative brain imaging, but its image quality and quantitative reliability are strongly dependent on injected radiotracer activity and acquisition time. Reducing injected dose or acquisition time lowers radiation exposure but increases image noise, while partial volume effects (PVE) further degrade signal accuracy, especially in cortical regions critical for neurodegenerative disease assessment. Conventional partial volume correction (PVC) methods such as Iterative Yang can improve quantification but are highly sensitive to noise and perform poorly in low count settings, while also relying on accurate anatomical information, typically derived from MRI, which may be unavailable, misregistered, or impractical in routine clinical workflows; in parallel, most deep learning (DL) approaches reported to date focus mainly on denoising and do not jointly address PVE. A method capable of simultaneously mitigating low count noise and PVE is needed. This study aimed to evaluate whether a DL approach can generate full count, PVC brain PET images from fast or low count non PVC scans, using both clinical and phantom datasets.ResultsUsing clinical and phantom brain PET datasets with 18F-FDG and 18F-Florbetapir, we compared low count PET images at 10% of full counts with images processed by Iterative Yang PVC and a Swin Unetr based DL-PVC model. The DL-PVC method consistently outperformed low count non-PVC PET across all evaluations. It achieved higher structural similarity index (SSIM) by about 2%, higher peak signal to noise ratio (PSNR) by about 11%, and lower relative root mean square error (rRMSE) by about 4%, indicating improved image quality and quantitative accuracy. In the clinical test sets, DL-PVC further showed clear gains for both tracers, with SSIM improvements of 2.1% for 18F-FDG and 2% for 18F-Florbetapir, PSNR increases of 21.6% and 22.3% respectively, and substantial rRMSE reductions of 50% for 18F-FDG and 44% for 18F-Florbetapir.ConclusionsOur study introduced a DL based PVC method based on Transformer and Unet architectures. By combining phantom and clinical data through a transfer learning strategy, the network benefits from the phantom ground truth activity maps while also learning the complexities present in real clinical PET. With this approach, it improves PET image quality and quantitative agreement with a high count reference, and at inference it requires only PET input, so it can be deployed without MR images for PVC.

Introduction

Positron emission tomography (PET) is a valuable modality for quantifying biological phenomena, such as the deposition of amyloid-beta plaques and neurofibrillary tangles in the cerebral cortex, particularly in the context of neurodegenerative disorders like Alzheimer’s disease (AD) [1–3]. However, the low spatial resolution of PET images, typically 5–8 mm full width at half maximum (FWHM), can lead to the partial volume effect (PVE) [4], where the measured signal in a given voxel is a mixture of signals from multiple tissues. This effect is particularly pronounced in the presence of morphological changes in the regions of interest (ROI), such as thinning of the cortical gyri due to brain atrophy, which can result in an underestimation of the cortical radioactivity concentration. As a result, there is a need to correct this spillover from gray matter (GM) for accurate quantitative and cross-sectional studies using amyloid PET. Additionally, low-count and fast PET imaging techniques, while beneficial for reducing radiation exposure and accelerating imaging speed, can further exacerbate the PVE due to the limited number of detected photons.

A number of Partial Volume Correction (PVC) methods are suggested to compensate PVE in PET Images [5, 6]. These algorithms can be broadly categorized into post-processing and reconstruction-based methods [7]. Reconstruction-based methods integrate the correction process into the image reconstruction framework, this can be achieved through fully data-driven mapping from measured signal to images [8, 9] or deep learning-based regularization [10, 11]. On the other hand, post-processing methods, such as Müller-Gärtner (MG) [12], geometric transfer matrix (GTM) [13], Labbé (LABBE) [14], regional voxel-based (RBV) [15], and iterative Yang (IY) [16] correct for PVE after the image has been reconstructed. Common PVC methods for brain PET imaging, like Müller-Gärtner, GTM, and IY often rely on additional anatomical images (mostly MR images), introducing challenges such as the need for two separated scans in some centers where PET/MR scanners are not available, and potential errors due to MRI’s related misregistration artifact or inaccurate segmentation. Some methods utilize the PET scanner’s Point Spread Function (PSF), for PVC which requires an accurate estimation of the scanner’s PSF across the field-of-view (FOV) that might be challenging to measure [5]. These drawbacks highlight the demand for an end-to-end PVC method capable of producing high-resolution PET or even SPECT images without requiring additional anatomical images or prior knowledge of scanner characteristics.

Furthermore, deep learning has demonstrated significant potential in medical image processing for instance in image quality enhancement and denoising [17–23], and quantification [24–26]. Several recent studies have demonstrated the potential of deep learning for PVC and denoising of PET images [27–29]. Azimi et al. [30] proposed a deep learning-based framework that can perform PVC and noise reduction on PET images without using anatomical information. They found that their method could effectively perform joint denoising and PVC for PET images using a 2D U-net architecture. Matsubara et al. [28] discuss the limitations of MRI-based PVC and the potential of deep learning for improved accuracy and efficiency. The authors propose a deep learning model, DeepPVC, that uses both MR and PET images to predict PVC maps. They find that DeepPVC performs well and is faster than traditional methods using 2D U-net for generating PVC maps. Sanaat et al. [29] introduced a Cycle Generative Adversarial Network-based method able to do PVC on a multi-tracer brain PET dataset without using extra anatomical information. They demonstrated that deep learning-based PVC images are immune from artifacts and abnormalities which can be propagated from MR images to PVC images. Recent unsupervised DL-PVC methods have shown promise by benchmarking against multiple classical PVC algorithms (RL, RVC, IY, DeepPVC). For instance, Cui et al. demonstrated competitive performance across simulated and clinical datasets using a self-supervised paradigm, though requiring paired high/low-count data during training [31]. Our supervised Swin-UNETR approach complements these by leveraging both digital phantom ground truth and clinical IY-PVC surrogates, achieving MR-free inference with demonstrated improvements in low-count settings.

One of the major limitations of the abovementioned studies is that the training and evaluation were performed on “not true ground truth” dataset. In all previous studies, the authors used conventional PVC methods and anatomical information for PVC, and they assumed the PVC images as a surrogate of the ground truth for the model’s training and evaluation. This assumption is not necessarily accurate and bears inherent limitations. A realistic dataset can be generated through a phantom or simulation study.

In the present study, we aim to leverage the power of the transformers for the task of PVC in low-count PET imaging. A diverse dataset, including the Brainweb phantom and clinical datasets using 18F-Fluorodeoxyglucose (18F-FDG) and 18F-Florbetapir radiotracers, were used for training and evaluation of the model. In this investigation we tried to address the limitation of the previous works by using phantom ground truth images for training, using 3D models for considering the activity distribution, and using transformers for enhancing the performance of our model by considering the local and global features.

Materials and methods

Study design

In this study, seven main steps were carried out which are summarized/visualized in Fig. 1. In the initial phase, the Brainweb phantom dataset - including two well-established and common radio tracers 18F-Florbetapir and 18F-FDG - was used. Subsequently, low-count sinograms were generated, by randomly under sample count levels between 90 and 120 million (10% of high-count data), and high-count sinograms were constructed with a fixed count level of one billion using the NiftyPET software [32]. The reconstruction process was then applied to both low-count and full-count images using the NiftyPET software. The next step was the registration of PET and MRI images using the Freesurfer software [33], employing a non-rigid algorithm for optimal alignment. To enrich the dataset and enhance model robustness, data augmentation techniques were introduced, including image rotation within a 35-degree limit, and horizontal/vertical flips with a 0.3 probability. Employing the IY method through the PETPVC toolbox [34] facilitated the PVC of PET images. Finally, the Swin-Unet deep learning model was chosen for training, culminating in a comprehensive methodology that combines advanced image processing and machine learning to refine the accuracy of PET images.

Data acquisition and processing

Phantom dataset

The Brainweb phantom [35] was prepared as a benchmark dataset to assess the efficacy of our PVC method. This phantom comprises 20 T1-weighted MRI volumes with detailed brain anatomy defined on volumetric fuzzy tissue probability maps. To generate a ground-truth activity distribution (activity map), we assigned tracer uptake values to the different tissue classes based on typical clinical 18 F-FDG brain PET images and a 3:1 GM: WM ratio as reported in [36]. Specifically, activity concentrations were randomly chosen between 4.8 and 6.0 in gray matter (GM), 1.5 to 2.0 in white matter (WM), 0.8 in skin, 0 in air, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), and bone, and 0.2 in all other tissues. Three spherical lesions with random radii between 2 and 8 mm were inserted in different brain regions; hot lesions were assigned uptake values randomly chosen between 4.5 and 5 SUV, and the surrounding cold lesion background was set to 50% lower activity. To ensure compatibility with the Siemens Biograph mMR scanner, the PET activity map, attenuation map, and T1-weighted MR images were resampled to match the scanner field-of-view and voxel grid (matrix 344 × 344 × 127; voxel size 2.08 × 2.08 × 2.03 mm³).

To increase the dataset’s size and enhance the generalizability and robustness of our model, we applied various data augmentation techniques. These techniques included image rotation of up to 35 degrees and both horizontal and vertical flips with a probability of 0.3. We generated full-count and low-count (10% of full-count) sinograms for each dataset using NiftyPET software. Noise-free data was obtained by forward-projecting ground-truth images by considering the system matrix and attenuation map, resulting in a total count level of 1 billion counts. Poisson noise was then introduced to create low-count sinograms with randomly selected count levels ranging from 90 to 120 million resulting in sinograms with counts of 10% of the high-count sinograms. The simulation did not account for scatters and randoms. Low-count data was reconstructed using the ordered subset expectation maximization (OSEM) algorithm with 10 iterations, 6 subsets, and a PSF of 4 mm, producing a reference image using NiftyPET software.

For PET data generation, we used the NiftyPET software package [27] to simulate the acquisition process. First, the ground-truth activity map was forward-projected using the system matrix and corresponding attenuation map to obtain noise-free full-count sinograms, with the total number of detected counts fixed at 1 × 109. Poisson noise was then added to these noise-free sinograms to generate noisy low-count realizations with randomly selected count levels between 90 and 120 million counts, corresponding to approximately 10% of the full-count statistics. The simulation modelled attenuation and detector response but did not explicitly simulate scatter or random events, which is acknowledged as a limitation. Full-count and low-count PET images were reconstructed from their respective sinograms using an ordered-subsets expectation maximization (OSEM) algorithm with 10 iterations, 6 subsets, and a 3D Gaussian point spread function (PSF) kernel with 4 mm FWHM implemented in NiftyPET. To increase the dataset size and enhance the generalizability and robustness of our model, we applied data augmentation to the reconstructed images. Augmentations included random in-plane rotations of up to 35° and horizontal and vertical flips, each applied with a probability of 0.3.

Clinical dataset

For the analysis of 18F-FDG patient images, we employed the open-access Monash DaCRA PET/MRI dataset [37, 38], comprising 25 brain PET scans acquired simultaneously on the Siemens Biograph mMR system at Monash University in Clayton, Australia. The PET scans utilized 18F-FDG as a radiotracer, administered to subjects at a dose of 260 MBq. The acquisition of PET data was obtained in list mode, synchronized with T1 images. Utilizing NiftyReg [39], the MRI images underwent rigid registration to align with the PET images. The clinical scans were conducted with approval from the Monash University Human Research Ethics Committee. List-mode PET data were histogrammed using the NiftyPET [32], producing a 20-min sinogram for high-count images and a low-count sinogram (10% of the high-count sinograms) for low-count images.

For the OASIS-3 dataset [40], a dataset including 30 cognitively normal subjects was selected for assessing the model belonging to 18F-Floebetapir. Stringent procedures have been implemented to ensure data integrity, including detailed screening for image quality and post-processing procedures to derive common anatomic measures. The imaging sessions were conducted using one of three Siemens scanners: ECAT-HRplus PET scanner, Biograph PET/CT scanner, and Biograph-mMR PET-MR. Dynamic imaging was employed, and late frames were utilized to generate both 20-min high-count and 2-min low-count images. Typically, intensity values from acquired frames were averaged to derive the mean radioactivity concentration during the scan. This robust methodology ensures the acquisition of high-quality imaging data for precise analysis in the context of 18F-Floebetapir research.

For both clinical cohorts, PET images were reconstructed using the vendor-recommended OSEM protocol including corrections for attenuation, scatter and random events. In contrast to the phantom simulations, where scatter and randoms were not explicitly modelled, all patient images therefore underwent standard clinical scatter correction before further processing and PVC.

Deep learning training

Network architecture

Swin UNETR [42] is a novel architecture that leverages the power of Swin Transformers for the semantic segmentation of brain tumors in multi-modal MRI images. The model is designed with a U-shaped network, which has been proven effective for 2D and 3D segmentation and regression tasks. The encoder of the model is a hierarchical Swin Transformer. It takes as input a 1D sequence of embeddings, which is a projection of the multi-modal input data. The encoder extracts features at five different resolutions by utilizing shifted windows for computing self-attention. This allows the model to capture long-range information, which is crucial for the image-to-image transformation. The decoder of the model is a Fully Convolutional Neural Network (FCNN). It is connected to the encoder at each resolution via skip connections. This design allows the decoder to leverage the multi-scale feature maps generated by the encoder, which can help improve the model performance. The use of Swin Transformers could enhance the model’s ability to capture global contextual information efficiently, which is crucial for understanding the overall structure of the brain.

The Swin Transformer (Fig. 2 ) serves as the encoder, processing low-count PET data in a hierarchical fashion. It operates on 2 × 2 × 2 patches at five different resolutions, resembling a pyramid structure. Each stage within the encoder employs two Swin Transformer blocks, which utilize “shifted windows” for efficient self-attention, enabling global information exchange within local neighborhoods. This self-attention mechanism allows the model to capture contextual relationships across different image regions. To ensure efficient processing as the resolution increases, patch merging is employed at each stage, reducing the number of patches while preserving key features.

The CNN-based decoder mirrors the encoder in structure, operating as an inverted pyramid. It receives extracted features from the encoder via skip connections, reintroducing high-resolution details lost during downsampling. Each level in the decoder upsamples the features using transposed convolutions and refines them using additional convolutional layers. This approach progressively increases the spatial resolution while incorporating high-resolution information from the corresponding encoder level, enabling precise pixel-level denoising.

The integration of skip connections is a defining characteristic of Swin UNETR. These connections directly inject high-resolution details from the encoder into the decoder at each upsampling step. This crucial information flow ensures both global contextual understanding and accurate localization, leading to superior segmentation performance.

Model training

Our model was developed using PyTorch 2.0 on an NVIDIA V100 GPU. We employed a two-stage transfer learning strategy to leverage the strengths of both phantom and clinical data. In Stage 1 (pretraining), we trained the Swin-UNETR model exclusively on the BrainWeb phantom dataset using 5-fold cross-validation, where the input was the reconstructed low-count PET image and the supervision target was the ground-truth activity map (true activity distribution). In Stage 2 (fine-tuning), we initialized the network weights from the best phantom-pretrained model and fine-tuned it separately on the clinical datasets (18F-FDG and 18F-Florbetapir) using the same 5-fold cross-validation scheme, with the high-count PET + IY-PVC images as supervision targets.

To assess the contribution of the network architecture and the transfer learning strategy, we performed an ablation study with three variants. In the first variant, a standard 3D U‑Net was trained only on the clinical 18F‑FDG data, using low‑count PET as input and high‑count PET (+ IY‑PVC) as target. In the second variant, the full Swin‑UNETR architecture was also trained only on clinical data with the same supervision. In the third (proposed) variant, Swin‑UNETR was pretrained on the BrainWeb phantom dataset using the true activity map as the target and then fine‑tuned on the 18F‑FDG clinical data, as described above. All three models were trained with identical data splits, loss function, optimizer, and hyperparameters, differing only in architecture (U‑Net vs. Swin‑UNETR) and the use of phantom pretraining. Performance was evaluated on the phantom test data and on the 18F‑FDG clinical test set using SSIM and PSNR computed within the brain mask.

Evaluation

To evaluate the efficacy of the proposed approach, we conducted a comparative analysis with the low-count without PVC images and full-count with IY-PVC images. Quantitative metrics, including peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR) and structural similarity index (SSIM), were adopted to assess the impact of various image enhancement algorithms on image quality. PSNR measures the ratio of the maximum signal value to the impact of distorting noise on image quality. SSIM is a metric that assesses the similarity between two images, quantifying the degradation in image quality resulting from compression and transmission losses. Patient quantitative evaluation is done by Freesurfer tools, a widely employed tool in neuroimaging research (http://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu/) as follows: The acquired T1-weighted images underwent meticulous preprocessing through the FreeSurfer image analysis suite involving a comprehensive pipeline documented in prior publications [43–45]. This process included motion correction, averaging of volumetric T1 weighted images, removal of non-brain tissue using a hybrid watershed/surface deformation procedure, automated Talairach transformation, segmentation of subcortical WM and deep GM volumetric structures (such as the hippocampus, amygdala, caudate, putamen, ventricles), intensity normalization, tessellation of the gray matter-white matter boundary, automated topology correction, and surface deformation following intensity gradients to optimally place the gray/white and gray/cerebrospinal fluid borders at the location where the greatest shift in intensity defines the transition to the other tissue class. Concurrently, PET to MR registration employed a vector-gradient algorithm (VGM) [46] symmetrically, using the average transformation for PET to MR. The regional PET processing, utilizing FreeSurfer segmentation examined each FreeSurfer region and generated comprehensive reports of regional measurements. Subsequently, the Standardized Uptake Value Ratios (SUVR) by employing the pons as a reference region [47] and residual variance across voxels in each brain region for all were computed.

Statistical analysis

Voxel-wise correlation, employing the Pearson correlation coefficient, was utilized to evaluate the effectiveness of various techniques. This metric evaluates the similarity between corrected images and ground truth images on a voxel-by-voxel basis, using the corresponding voxel in the high-count image as the reference image.

Discussion

This study aims to suggest and evaluate a deep learning-based model for simultaneously PVC, image quality enhancement, and noise reduction of low-count PET images. Many conventional PVC techniques rely heavily on additional anatomical data, such as CT or MRI scans. However, this dependence becomes a limitation when these modalities are either unavailable or affected by artifacts. Moreover, the inherent noise present in PET images often results in quantitative errors, highlighting the pressing need for image quality improvement. Introducing a model capable of reducing PVE and noise from PET images could prove immensely advantageous. Our approach integrates two neural network structures, namely the Transformer and UNET, enabling the incorporation of both global and local features while fostering the exchange of information between patches. We propose the MSE loss function to assess the accuracy of the processed images. The convergence investigation of this loss function indicates progressive improvement in the network’s ability to represent the features of the data.

The proposed method consistently outperformed low-count PET, demonstrating higher PSNR values indicative of superior image quality and noise reduction, significantly elevated SSIM scores highlighting better structural preservation, and lower rRMSE values reflecting enhanced accuracy in images. These findings collectively emphasize the effectiveness of the proposed method in improving both image quality and accuracy compared to low-count PET across the Brainweb dataset.

In our results, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of SUV images across different brain regions using the Brainweb phantom. The box and whisker plots facilitate a quantitative comparison of different methods, including low-count PET images, a reference activity map, and PET images corrected with our deep learning algorithm for PVC. The algorithm not only enhances the SUV values consistently across different anatomical segments but also brings them closer to the reference activity map. This is visually represented in the box and whisker plots, where the median (represented by the line inside the box), the interquartile range (represented by the box), and the range (represented by the whiskers) of the SUV values are shown for each method and each brain regions. The closer alignment of the SUV values from our algorithm with the reference activity map, as compared to the low-count PET image, underscores the effectiveness of our algorithm in improving the quality of PET images.

The emphasis is on the algorithm’s efficacy, visually represented in the plots, showcasing its consistent ability to enhance SUV values across GM, WM, and Lesion segments. These findings hold both statistical significance and qualitative relevance, suggesting the algorithm’s potential to contribute to more accurate and reliable PET image analysis. The demonstrated improvements underscore the algorithm’s impact on enhancing SUVR values, with implications for advancing PET imaging in clinical and research settings.

The model, trained with real patient data, shows potential for clinical application, capable of reconstructing smooth images while preserving edges. However, without ground truth, it is challenging to make quantitative claims or judge the authenticity of structures made apparent with DL-PVC.

The proposed DL-PVC method in this paper represents an advancement in the field of PET image processing, particularly in the simultaneous application of denoising and PVC for low-count images. While previous studies, such as the one conducted by Azimi et al. [27] utilized a 2D U-net architecture for joint denoising and PVC, our approach surpasses this limitation by utilizing a 3D swin UNET network. The choice of transformer architectures, known for their capability to capture long-range dependencies and contextual information, enhances the learning process and improves overall performance compared to traditional 2D architectures. Moreover, we address the concerns raised by Matsubara et al. [28], who highlighted the challenges in capturing spatial relationships between slices in 3D PET images. Our DL-PVC method effectively addresses this issue, demonstrating strong quantitative agreement with clinical reference images in the final PV-corrected maps. Moreover, the proposed model generates PVC images from PET images without needing extra imaging data, such as MRI or CT scan, eliminating the need for precise registration, segmentation, or point spread function calculation. Additionally, we acknowledge and overcome the limitations observed in the Sanaat et al. [29] study, as our method provides robust PVC results with comprehensive evaluation, including multi-institutional data and the Brainweb phantom, demonstrates the robustness and reliability of our DL-PVC method in real-world scenarios.

The DL-PVC framework offers two primary clinical benefits for brain PET imaging. First, by enabling the use of low-count (10% of standard counts) PET images while restoring image quality comparable to high-count + IY-PVC references, our method supports substantial reductions in either scan time or injected activity. For a typical 18 F-FDG brain PET protocol (260 MBq injected activity, 20-minute acquisition), DL-PVC could enable: (i) ~ 2-minute scans at standard dose (90% scan time reduction) or (ii) similar 20-minute scans with ~ 26 MBq (90% dose reduction), both yielding quantitative accuracy comparable to standard protocols. The latter would reduce the effective dose from ~ 5.2 mSv to ~ 0.52 mSv, a 10-fold reduction beneficial particularly for pediatric patients, longitudinal studies, and research cohorts requiring multiple scans.

Second, unlike conventional IY-PVC (which requires ~ 15–30 min of MRI segmentation and registration per case), DL-PVC inference requires only PET input and runs in under 2 min on modern GPU hardware with no preprocessing or anatomical data needed. This eliminates the need for PET/MR scanners, co-registered MRI, or time-intensive FreeSurfer segmentation, which may facilitate PVC in PET/CT-only workflows and centers without MRI access. Together, these advantages could substantially increase PVC adoption for quantitative brain PET applications such as amyloid imaging, FDG metabolism studies, and dementia assessment.

Our framework differs from recent unsupervised DL-PVC methods [31] by using supervised learning with phantom true activity maps for pretraining and clinical IY-PVC fine-tuning, enabling end-to-end low-count denoising + PVC without MR at inference. Key advantages include: (i) 3D Swin-UNETR architecture capturing long-range dependencies superior to 2D U-Nets; (ii) MR-free clinical deployment (unlike DeepPVC); (iii) demonstrated 10x count reduction with 48% PSNR gains over low-count PET; and (iv) transfer learning from digital phantom providing physics-grounded initialization. While unsupervised methods reduce data requirements, our approach shows promising quantitative agreement with clinical references at ultra-low counts and may serve as a research framework for future low-count PET applications.

The ablation analysis further clarifies the role of the proposed design choices. Replacing the U‑Net baseline with Swin‑UNETR yielded consistent gains in both SSIM and PSNR on phantom and 18F‑FDG data, underscoring the benefit of long‑range self‑attention and multi‑scale feature encoding for joint denoising and PVC. In addition, phantom pretraining followed by clinical fine‑tuning improved structural similarity on both domains, indicating that learning from true activity maps helps the network better preserve fine anatomical details in clinical images. The modest differences in PSNR across variants on the clinical data suggest a balance between stronger denoising and sharper edge preservation, which is consistent with the visual impression of less over‑smoothed DL‑PVC images.

Joint denoising plus PVC has precedent in Azimi et al. [30]. and our previous CycleGAN-PVC study [29], showing single-network approaches avoid error propagation from cascaded pipelines. Advantages include: (i) consistent error propagation; (ii) shared feature learning; (iii) single inference pipeline. Limitations include: difficulty isolating contributions, potential IY bias inheritance. The unified framework offers several advantages over separate denoising/PVC: (i) consistent error propagation (no intermediate noisy PVC artefacts); (ii) shared feature learning (denoising benefits PVC kernel estimation); (iii) single inference pipeline (processing time < 2 min). Limitations include difficulty isolating individual contributions and potential bias inheritance from IY‑PVC clinical targets. While global metrics (SSIM/PSNR) assess technical performance, brain PET quantification prioritizes regional SUVR accuracy, which our FreeSurfer analysis confirms.

However, the proposed method’s limitation lies in the inherent complexities associated with the denoising and PVC tasks. Despite the effectiveness of our method, there might be specific image features or scenarios where the model’s performance could be suboptimal. Furthermore, while we strive for versatility in handling low-count images, it is important to recognize that the performance of our DL-PVC method may vary based on the severity of noise and PVE. Moreover, the dataset used for training and evaluation, though comprehensive, was relatively small. While we aimed to cover a diverse range of scenarios, a larger dataset could offer more robustness and generalizability to our model. Additionally, while the IY method served as a reference for PVC, it’s essential to recognize that it may not fully represent the true activity distribution, and thus, cannot be unequivocally considered as ground truth. Moving forward, addressing these limitations could involve expanding the dataset size to incorporate a wider range of noise levels and PVE scenarios and developing more accurate ground truth data to improve the evaluation and validation of the proposed approach.

Clinical results are inherently limited by the absence of true physical ground truth, relying instead on high‑count IY‑PVC as a surrogate reference. While DL‑PVC demonstrates superior agreement with this reference compared to low‑count PET (SUVR bias reduction 35–42%, residual variance 28–36% lower), these improvements reflect IY‑like performance at lower counts rather than absolute accuracy. Demonstrating clinical utility will require prospective studies with test‑retest reproducibility, reader agreement analyses, and diagnostic performance metrics against established endpoints.

As noted in our prior works [29] and also based on a recent review [48], IY remains primarily a research tool rather than routine clinical method due to noise sensitivity, MR dependence, and lack of large‑scale validation. Simpler alternatives (MG, GTM, RBV) exist with established clinical workflows, though often ROI‑limited. Our DL‑PVC inherits IY biases during clinical fine‑tuning but offers PET‑only inference (< 2 min) vs. IY’s 15‑30 min MR processing. DL‑PVC is positioned as research framework demonstrating IY‑like performance at 10x lower counts, not validated for routine clinical replacement pending multi‑method comparisons and prospective studies.

The clinical datasets span multiple Siemens scanners (Biograph mMR, ECAT‑HR+, Biograph PET/CT from OASIS‑3) and acquisition protocols (20‑min static vs. late dynamic frames), providing initial evidence of cross‑scanner robustness within the Siemens ecosystem. However, reconstruction parameters differed slightly between sites (e.g., iteration/subset counts, post‑filters), and performance was not stratified by scanner type. External validation on non‑Siemens systems (GE, Philips) and harmonized reconstruction protocols (e.g., EANM standards) is needed to confirm domain generalization. Future work will test scanner‑agnostic training (multi‑vendor phantoms) and domain adaptation techniques to enhance applicability across diverse clinical PET environments.

Conclusion

We suggested a deep learning-based PVC method combining transformer and U-net architectures with transfer learning from phantom ground truth to clinical data, offering a promising research framework for reducing both PVE and noise in brain PET images. Furthermore, our model performs PVC directly from PET images without requiring MR or CT images as input at inference, which makes it practical for scenarios where anatomical imaging is unavailable or impractical because of artifacts, although MR was used in this study to generate IY‑PVC training labels and for regional quantitative evaluation.
