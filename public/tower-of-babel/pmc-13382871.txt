Lanthionine synthetase C-like protein 1 (LanCL1): a therapeutic target for neuropathic pain
Authors: David C. Spanswick, Haifeng Wei, Fei-Yue Zhao, Matilde P. Caldeira Borges da Cruz, Andrew D. Whyment, Adam Campbell-Galland, Kelly O'Sullivan, François Autelitano, Marie Guillemot, David Kenley, Andrew J. Gearing, Anthony E. Pickering, Wendy L. Imlach
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Supplemental Digital Content is Available in the Text.LanCL1 is a novel target for the treatment of neuropathic pain, and modulation of its activity may provide a new strategy to manage this condition.

1. Introduction

The mammalian LanCL family of proteins comprises 3 members: LanCL1, LanCL2, and LanCL3. LanCL1 is extensively expressed in the nervous system, as is LanCL2, the latter also widely expressed in peripheral tissues.5,31,45,46,58,59,75 LANCL3 has lower expression, and its function remains poorly explored. LANCL1 is primarily a cytosolic protein with weak association to the plasma membrane; however, in pathological states, it may function as a cell-surface protein.34 LanCL3 is also suggested to be cytosolic, whereas LanCL2 is associated with the plasma membrane, anchored through the myristoylated N-terminal domain.5,46

In contrast to their bacterial counterparts, mammalian LanCL proteins do not participate in lanthionine synthesis.31 Instead, they catalyze the addition of glutathione to dehydroalanine or dehydrobutyrine (Dha/Dhb) residues in damaged proteins, leading to irreversible C-glutathionylation. This process may facilitate protein repair or clearance by neutralising reactive electrophiles and preventing dysregulatory effects on the proteome.45 LanCL1 may also play a protective role in the nervous system by scavenging reactive oxygen species (ROS) through a glutathione S-transferase–like mechanism and activating Akt signalling.33,82 The highly conserved signalling molecule abscisic acid (ABA) is considered a potential endogenous ligand for LanCL2, with a lower affinity for LanCL1.12,70,74,78 Acting through LANCL1 and LANCL2, ABA signals through an AMPK/PGC-1α/Sirt1-dependent pathway promotes glucose uptake, cytoskeletal and contractile protein synthesis, and mitochondrial function by increasing mitochondrial proton gradient, DNA content, and respiration.74–76

Abscisic acid has shown therapeutic effects in immune-mediated, metabolism-related conditions associated with chronic inflammation.7,19,22–25,38,47,54,69 Recently, a role for LanCL2 in inflammation associated with neuropathic pain has been suggested.55 Maixner et al. showed that chronic dosing with ABA reduced thermal hyperalgesia and mechanical allodynia by suppressing spinal microglia and astrocyte reactivity, ERK activity, and TNFα levels. Treatment with ABA restored LanCL2 protein levels that were downregulated in neuropathic pain, and knockdown of LanCL2 in the spinal cord induced neuropathic pain,55 suggesting a role for endogenous ABA/LanCL2 in sensory information processing in the dorsal horn. This is further supported by reports of ABA reducing acute pain induced by tail-pinch, hot-plate, and formalin tests, in which centrally administered ABA raised pain threshold through a PPARβ/δ- and opioid signalling–dependent mechanism.63 Similarly, ABA reduced pain sensitivity in female rats in a mouse model of attention deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), an effect attributed to a reduction in neuroinflammation.62

Although evidence supports a role for LanCL2 in pain through suppression of inflammatory responses, little is known of LanCL1 roles in chronic pain. Genome-wide association studies have identified LanCL1 as being associated with chronic pain in humans94 and in the mouse dorsal horn.2 A recent study suggested that LanCL1 may be a protective factor during the development of neuropathic pain states through a miR-6325/LanCL1 axis and may be an immune marker for neuropathic pain.71 However, the functional role of LanCL1 in chronic pain remains poorly understood. By investigating the mechanism of action of the peptide LAT8881, a C-terminal fragment of human growth hormone, with biological activity independent of the GH receptor, we identify LanCL1 as a potential new target for the treatment of neuropathic pain.

2. Methods

2.1. Animals

Experiments were conducted on adult male and female Sprague-Dawley rats (8-10 weeks old or at weights described for each model), purchased from Charles River UK Ltd (UK) or the Monash Animal Research Platform (Australia). Animals were housed in groups of 4 in an air-conditioned room at a temperature of 20 ± 1°C and humidity of 50 ± 10% on a 12-hour:12-hour light/dark cycle. Food and water were available ad libitum. Rats were randomly assigned to experimental groups, and investigators were blinded to treatments.

2.2. Persistent hypersensitivity models

2.2.1. Nerve ligation models of neuropathic hypersensitivity

Models were prepared using methods described previously for the Chung spinal ligation model32 and the chronic constriction injury (CCI) models of neuropathic hypersensitivity.6 In brief, animals were anaesthetized with 5% isoflurane in oxygen followed by an intramuscular (i.m) injection of ketamine 90 mg/kg plus xylazine 10 mg/kg. For the Chung model, a paramedial incision was made on the skin covering the L4-6 level, and the L5 spinal nerve was carefully isolated and tightly ligated with a single tight ligation using 6/0 silk suture. For the CCI model, male and female rats were anaesthetized with isoflurane, and a small incision was made to the lateral side of the left hind limb to expose the sciatic nerve, where 4 ligations at an interval of ∼1 mm were placed around the nerve with 4/0 silk suture. For both preparations, the wound was closed in layers after a complete hemostasis. For both the Chung and CCI models, baseline touch sensitivity on the hind paws was examined with von Frey hairs (as described below) for 3 consecutive days before surgery and reassessed on the sixth to eighth day after surgery and on the 12th to 14th day after surgery. Drug testing was carried out on the 12th to 14th day after surgery. For behavioural experiments, the paw withdrawal threshold (PWT) was assessed before and at 1, 2, and 4 hours following drug or vehicle administration.

2.2.2. Chemotherapy-induced neuropathy

Animals were anaesthetized with isoflurane, and 4 mg/kg oxaliplatin was injected intravenously through the tail vein twice per week for 2 weeks.50 The development of neuropathic hypersensitivity, characterised by significant mechanical allodynia, was monitored using PWT in response to von Frey hair stimulation. Only those rats with significant mechanical allodynia (PWT ≤ 4.0 g) were selected for further study.

2.2.3. Diabetic neuropathy

Male rats weighing 220 to 250 g were injected (i.p) with 50 mg/kg streptozotocin (STZ).57 Blood glucose levels were measured 7 days later, and animals with levels below 14 mmol/L were given a second injection of STZ. Animals that did not develop the features of a model of type 1 diabetes after 2 injections were excluded from this study. Rats with glucose levels above 14 mmol/L, as well as PWT ≤ 4 g (average of both hind limbs), were used for compound testing.

2.2.4. Reserpine-induced fibromyalgia model

Male rats weighing 220 to 250 g were injected (s.c) with 1 mg/kg reserpine for 3 consecutive days.64 Animals were used for drug testing 5 days after the last dose of reserpine.

2.2.5. Brennan paw incision model

Male rats weighing 250 to 270 g were anaesthetised with isoflurane, and a 1.5-cm incision was made in the middle of the left hind paw. The underlying muscle and tendon were carefully separated, and an incision was made along the longitudinal axis. After hemostasis, the wound was closed in layers with 4/0 silk stitch thread.9 Animals were dosed with compound/vehicle just before the surgery, followed by behavioural tests and then again on the second day before tests.

2.2.6. Carrageenan-induced thermal hyperalgesia

Male rats weighing 220 to 250 g were injected intraplantarly with carrageenan (0.1 mL, 2% wt/vol in distilled water) into the left hind paw while under isoflurane anaesthesia.30 Thermal hyperalgesia was assessed by determination of paw withdrawal latency (PWL) to a noxious heat stimulus using a beam of radiant light (modified Hargreaves apparatus).

2.2.7. Monosodium iodoacetate–induced arthralgia

Male rats under isoflurane anaesthesia were injected with monosodium iodoacetate (MIA) (2 mg in 25 µL, in distilled water) into the left knee joint using a 26-g needle attached to a 1 mL syringe.39 Drug testing was carried out 2 weeks following MIA injection. Weight-bearing test was conducted to monitor the time course of analgesia in this model.

2.2.8. Complete Freund adjuvant model

Male and female rats were anaesthetized with isoflurane, and the left hind paw was injected (intraplantarly) with 0.05 mL complete Freund adjuvant (CFA) emulsion in saline (CFA: saline = 1:1, vol/vol).87 Paw withdrawal latency to a noxious heat stimulus using a beam of radiant light (modified Hargreaves apparatus) was used to monitor the time course of analgesia in this model. Paw withdrawal latency was assessed once daily for 3 days before CFA injection, reassessed 1 day after CFA injection, before drug dosing and at 1, 2, and 4 hours after drug dosing.

2.2.9. Formalin test

Formalin (50 μL, 5% solution) was injected subcutaneously into the dorsal surface of the right hind paw of each rat.15 Paw licking time was used to monitor the time course of analgesia in this model.

2.3. Behavioural hypersensitivity assays

2.3.1. Paw withdrawal threshold: von Frey test

To assess the degree of mechanical allodynia, animals were tested by applying von Frey filaments to the mid-lateral plantar area of the left hind paw to identify the threshold that produces a withdrawal. Animals were acclimated in individual Perspex boxes on a raised metal mesh for at least 40 minutes before applying the filament of lowest force until slightly bent for 6 seconds. If no response was observed, then a hair with force immediately higher was tested, and if the animal withdrew or lifted the paw upon stimulation, then a hair with force immediately lower than that tested was used. The lowest amount of force required to induce reliable responses (positive in 3 out of 5 trials) was recorded as the PWT value. Baseline PWT was measured over 3 consecutive days before generating hypersensitivity models. Any animal identified with a baseline PWT less than 8 g was excluded from this study (approximately 5%). Only animals with significant mechanical allodynia (PWT ≤ 4.0 g) were selected for further testing.

2.3.2. Measurement of paw withdrawal latency by thermal stimulation

Thermal hyperalgesia was assessed by determination of paw withdrawal latency to a noxious heat stimulus using a beam of radiant light (modified Hargreaves apparatus). Rats were acclimated in individual plastic boxes on top of a glass plate for 10 to 20 minutes before applying a noxious heat stimulus that was focused through the glass plate onto the plantar surface of a hind paw, with a cut-off latency of 20 seconds. The PWL was defined as the time in seconds from the start of heat stimulation to the time the animal withdrew its hind limb. The average PWL of 3 trials from each hind paw was calculated.

2.3.3. Weight-bearing test

Changes in hind paw weight bearing were assessed using an incapacitance meter (Linton Instruments, United Kingdom), which incorporates a dual-channel sensor used to separately assess the weight distributed to each hind paw. The rat was placed in a Perspex chamber designed to detect each hind paw using separate transducer pads for 2 to 3 minutes to acclimate. The average difference in weight load exerted by the hind paws over a period of 5 seconds was noted. Three measurements were taken at 60-second intervals, and the mean was calculated. Weight-bearing time was measured before and after CFA injection for assessing the development of sensitivity and remeasured at 1, 2, and 4 hours after drug dosing. Only rats that spent less than 41% on the ipsilateral side were selected for further study.

2.3.4. Measurement of paw licking time

Animals were placed individually in a transparent plastic box following formalin injection/compound dosing. A video monitoring system was used to record behaviour for 50 minutes following formalin injection. The time (in seconds) spent licking the affected paw over 5-minute bins was analysed offline.

2.3.5. Measurement of tail-flick latency

The tail-flick test was carried out using a modified version of the procedure first described by D'Amour and Smith.13 In brief, the animal was loosely restrained in a towel, and its tail was placed on the surface of the tail-flick analgesia meter (Pamotor, Burlingame, CA). Radiant heat from a halogen lamp was focused on the ventral surface of the tail (4-8 cm from the end of the tail). Tail-flick latency was defined as the time that elapsed in seconds between the start of heat stimulation and the tail-flick response. The intensity of the radiant heat stimulus was adjusted to yield a baseline tail-flick latency of 1.5 to 3 seconds, with a 10-second cut-off time. Tail-flick latency was measured from the same site on the tail before (baseline) and at 60, 120, and 240 minutes after vehicle or compound dosing.

2.4. Electrophysiology

Electrophysiological experiments in vivo were conducted in behaviourally validated CCI models (PWT in the affected limb ≤ 4.0 g) using methods similar to those described by us previously.92,93 After behavioural validation, rats were anaesthetized with urethane 1.2 g/kg, i.p., for induction and subsequently maintained with 0.1 to 0.5 g/kg (i.v.), if required. The right carotid artery and jugular vein were cannulated separately to monitor blood pressure and permit test compound administration, respectively. Body temperature was monitored and controlled within a physiological range using a thermoblanket system. The electrocardiogram was routinely monitored through a pair of stainless-steel needles inserted into the left and right forepaws.

2.4.1. Recording peripheral nerve ectopic firing of neuroma and dorsal root ganglion origin

For ectopic discharge of neuroma origin, an incision was made on the lateral side of the left hind limb. Under a dissection microscope, the sural nerve below the sciatic nerve ligation area was exposed and carefully isolated from the surrounding connective tissues. The skin was stitched to a metal O-ring to form a pool, which was later filled with warm mineral oil to protect the nerve. The sciatic nerve above the ligation area was sectioned. The nerve sheath was then carefully removed. A small bundle of nerve filaments was teased from the distant cut end of the sural nerve and looped onto a unipolar silver wire recording electrode with a reference electrode connected to the connective tissues nearby. For ectopic discharge of dorsal root ganglion (DRG) origin, recordings were obtained from the sciatic nerve above the ligation area, below the level of the DRG.

2.4.2. Electrophysiological recording from wide dynamic range neurons in the spinal cord’s dorsal horn

For recording wide dynamic range (WDR) neurons from the spinal cord’s dorsal horn, a laminectomy was carried out to expose the T11 to L2 segments. The incised back skin was clamped to a plastic film to form an oil pool to prevent the surface of the spinal cord from becoming dehydrated. The dura mater over the exposed spinal cord was opened. For dorsal horn neuron recording, carbon-fibre microelectrodes (impedance, 0.4-0.8 MΩ at 1 kHz) were lowered into the spinal cord dorsal horn using a manual hydraulic manipulator to record neuronal activity. The neurons recorded were from deep layers (lamina IV or V at approximately 500-900 µm from the surface of the spinal cord) of the L4 to L5 level. Proprioceptive neurons were excluded from analysis based upon their firing pattern and responses to joint movement. For peripheral and central recordings, neural activity was amplified and monitored using standard electrophysiological recording techniques and recorded onto a PC using CED Spike 5 software (Cambridge Electronics Design, CED). The electrical signal was amplified using a Digitimer AC amplifier (NL104) and filtered with a low-pass filter set at 50 to 500 Hz and a high cut at 500 to 5 kHz. The signals were then recorded through a CED micro-1401 interface to a PC and analysed offline.

2.5. Induction of wind-up and after-discharge

Wide dynamic range neurons were identified and recorded as described in detail by us previously.92 In brief, WDR neurons were identified based on their response to gentle brush, squeeze, and von Frey hair stimulation (1.15, 4, and 15.4 g). Once identified, a pair of fine needle electrodes was inserted into the receptive field to deliver electrical stimulation. Thresholds for evoking action potentials of C-fibre–mediated responses were determined by delivering single electrical pulses of 1-millisecond duration of increasing strength. Once the thresholds were detected, a train of electrical pulses (16 pulses in 5 seconds, 1-millisecond duration) at an intensity of 2 times the threshold was delivered once every 5 minutes. The neural activity (spontaneous firing and evoked responses) was recorded for at least 20 minutes before the vehicle or compound administration and then for a further 60 minutes after dosing.

2.5.1. Measurement of spontaneous activity

The average spontaneous firing frequency over consecutive 5-minute periods (expressed as the number of action potentials per minute), immediately before electrical stimulation, was measured before and at 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, and 60 minutes after compound injection.

2.5.2. Measurement of wind-up

Wind-up was measured using a protocol based on the methods described in a previous study.79,92 In brief, wind-up was calculated as the total number of evoked action potentials of a neurone in response to all 16 electrical pulses minus 16 times the action potentials induced by the first electrical pulse in that train. First, the number of action potentials evoked between 90 and 300 milliseconds after the first electrical stimulation pulse (A) was counted. Second, the total number of action potentials induced by the whole train of electrical pulses (16 pulses) in those 5 seconds (B) was counted. Then, the number of wind-up action potentials was calculated as follows: wind-up action potential number = B – (A × 16). The wind-up action potential number immediately before compound administration (0 minutes) and at 10-minute intervals after compound injection was counted. In some cases, wind-up was completely inhibited following compound administration; the number of wind-up action potentials (B) was even lower than control levels (A × 16), leading to a negative reading. In such cases, the wind-up was set to 0 (completely inhibited) for ease of statistical analysis.

2.5.3. Measurement of after-discharge

The total number of action potentials recorded within 10 seconds, starting from 300 milliseconds after the last electrical pulse of the train (ie, the 16th electrical stimulus), was used as a marker or indicator of the extent of after-discharge for that neuron.

2.5.4. Electrophysiological recording from dorsal horn neurons in the spinal cord in vitro

Electrophysiological experiments, in vitro, were conducted in behaviourally validated Chung models (PWT at the affected limb ≤ 4.0 g) using methods modified from those described by us previously.73,80 Once validation of a neuropathic state had been confirmed, animals were terminally anesthetized using isoflurane, cervically dislocated, and decapitated. A laminectomy was performed, and the spinal cord, with spinal roots and DRG attached, was removed and cut into 250 to 450 µm-thick slices using a Leica VT1000S vibrating microtome (Leica Microsystems UK, Milton Keynes, United Kingdom). Slices were maintained in artificial cerebrospinal fluid (aCSF) at room temperature for 1 hour after slicing before recording. For recording, individual slices were held between 2 grids in a custom-built chamber continuously perfused with aCSF at a rate of 4 to 10 mL.min−1, illuminated from below, and viewed under a dissection microscope. The aCSF was of the following composition (mM): NaCl, 127; KCl, 1.9; KH2PO4, 1.2; CaCl2, 2.4; MgCl2, 1.3; NaHCO3, 26; D-glucose, 10; equilibrated with 95% O2-5% CO2.

Whole-cell recordings were performed at 35 ± 1°C from neurons in the dorsal horn of spinal cord slices using a Multiclamp 700B amplifier or Axopatch 1D amplifier and the “blind” version of the patch-clamp technique.73 For electrophysiological recording using a grease-gap configuration, a spinal cord slice was transferred to a custom-built chamber. The essential features of the chamber were a central recording chamber where the spinal cord slice was positioned and an interconnected second chamber, arranged in parallel with the central slice recording chamber, where the connecting dorsal roots and DRG were placed. The arrangement permitted the creation of an assembly where the connected slices and roots with attached DRG were continuously perfused in parallel with independent sources of aCSF at 35 ± 1°C, separated by a grease gap formed at the junction of the dorsal roots and spinal cord entry zone. The flow rates (5-10 mL·min−1) to the slice and roots were maintained constant throughout recording. This arrangement facilitated application of test compounds selectively to the dorsal roots or to the spinal slice. To ensure no mixing of the bathing solutions, methylene blue was applied to either the slice or root securing chambers to ensure no transfer of solution between compartment reservoirs. Patch pipettes were pulled from thin-walled borosilicate glass with resistances of between 3 and 8 MΩ when filled with intracellular solution of the following composition (mM): potassium gluconate, 140; KCl, 10; EGTA-Na, 1; HEPES, 10; Na2ATP, 4; Na-GTP, 0.3. Voltage-current (VI) relations were determined in current-clamp mode by recording membrane potential responses to injection of a series of hyperpolarising and depolarising rectangular-wave current pulses (500 ms-1 s duration) of constant increment (5-60 pA) in the absence and presence of the test compound. Changes in membrane potential were determined by comparing the membrane potential in the absence of any current injection at baseline and subsequently in the presence of compound. Neuronal input resistance was determined from the membrane potential response (−5 to −10 mV) to injection of the smallest amplitude hyperpolarising current pulse, using Ohm’s law, in the absence and subsequently presence of the test compound. The reversal potential of test compound-induced responses was estimated as the point of intersection of VI curves generated in the absence and subsequently in the presence of the test compound. A concentric bipolar stimulating electrode was placed appropriately either on the dorsal root or the dorsal root entry zone to evoke postsynaptic potentials/currents in recorded cells. Low-frequency single shocks were delivered to evoke excitatory postsynaptic currents (EPSCs) and potentials (EPSPs) in voltage- and current-clamp, respectively (0.1-0.8 ms pulse width, 2-35 V, 0.16-0.33 Hz). EPSCs/EPSPs were confirmed as monosynaptic on the basis of being characterised by a constant latency and rise time with few failures. In addition, data were low-pass filtered at 2 to 5 kHz (1 kHz for voltage-clamp data), digitized at 2 to 10 kHz and stored on a PC running pCLAMP 10 data acquisition software (Axon Instruments). Analysis of electrophysiological data was carried out using Clampfit 10 software.

2.5.5. Spinal cord microsome photoaffinity labelling with PAL probe and LC-MS/MS analysis in situ competitive photoaffinity labelling

In brief, spinal cord microsomes were treated with 50 µM LAT8881, followed by PAL treatment for 30 minutes and subsequent UV irradiation (365 nm). Microsomes were then lysed and proteins were extracted for affinity capture of PAL photocrosslinked proteins with streptavidin-coated beads. Proteins eluted from the beads (50 µL per well) were resolved using SDS-PAGE. Proteins were identified using mass spectrometry and confirmed using Western blotting with an anti-LanCL1 antibody (Invitrogen, PA5-57107). A full description of these methods is included in the Supplementary methods section.

2.5.6. Formulation and injection of siRNA into L5 dorsal root ganglion

MISSION Predesigned siRNA (Merck) were reconstituted as described in the manufacturer's instructions. In brief, siRNA were reconstituted in RNAse-free water to a final concentration of 100 μM, and on the day of the experiment, 1 μL per animal was mixed with 0.18 μL 4.5 mg/mL polyethylenimine (PEI, Sigma cat no 765090) in 5% dextrose and incubated at room temperature for 10 minutes. Equal volumes of 2 siRNAs for LanCL1 (Merck siRNA ID: SASI_Rn01_00109957 and SASI_Rn01_00109956) in complex with PEI were mixed before injection. A universal negative control (MISSION siRNA control #1, Merck) was prepared in the same way. On the day before dosing with LAT8881, a combination of sharp and blunt dissection was used to expose the lateral aspect of the fifth lumbar (L5) vertebra and the intervertebral foramen. The foramen was slightly enlarged by the removal of a small portion of laminar bone, exposing the ipsilateral L5 DRG. Two microliters (2 µL) of siRNA for LanCL1 or scrambled siRNA solution was injected into the L5 DRG of Chung model rats, and the wound was closed.

2.5.7. Immunohistochemical profiling of LanCL expression

Lumbar DRG and spinal cord samples were postfixed in PFA for 4 hours, washed in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (PB), and transferred to 30% sucrose in PB overnight. Tissue was mounted in Tissue-Tek OCT, sectioned at 20 µm on a cryostat directly onto Superfrost Plus glass slides. Sections underwent heat-induced antigen retrieval using a citrate buffer and were immunolabelled with primary antibodies for LanCL1 (Invitrogen PA5-57107), NeuN (Thermofisher PA5143586), TRPV1 (Invitrogen PA1-29770), CGRP (abcam ab81887), NF200 (abcam ab4680), IB4 (Invitrogen I21412), glutamine synthetase (abcam ab64613), GFAP (Biolegend 644704), DAPI (Sigma MBD0015), and appropriate secondary Alexa fluorophore–conjugated antibodies. LanCL1 antibody specificity in immunohistochemical studies was confirmed with siRNA knockdown experiments and controls where the primary antibody was removed (see supplemental digital content, Fig. 4, http://links.lww.com/PAIN/C510). Sections were imaged on a Nikon C1 confocal microscope or a Nikon AX R NSPARC.

2.5.8. Compounds and formulation

Cyclic peptides: The peptides LAT8881 (YLRIVQCRSVEGSCGF), LAT9991 (CRSVEGSCG), LAT9991F (CRSVEGSCGF), LAT9993 (SCRSRPVESSC) and LAT7771 (CRIIHNNNC) were manufactured by Auspep Pty Ltd. PAL LAT9991F [a terminal propargyl-diazirine photocrosslinker and a reporter functional alkyne group = PAL] was covalently conjugated to the cyclised peptide CRSVEGSCGF) by Evotec.11 For in vivo studies, all compounds were dissolved in DMSO and subsequently diluted in 0.9% saline to yield a final concentration of 5% DMSO in saline. A vehicle control of 5% DMSO in saline was used. Morphine sulphate was purchased from Martindale Pharmaceuticals (UK) and was employed as a positive control in studies on models of inflammatory hypersensitivity. Gabapentin, purchased from Brown & Burk UK Ltd, was dissolved in 0.9% saline and was used as a positive control for studies on neuropathic models. For in vitro studies, LAT8881, LAT9991, LAT9993, LAT7771, and PAL LAT9991F were made up as concentrated aliquots in DMSO and diluted in aCSF immediately before electrophysiological experiments. The final concentration of DMSO in vitro studies was 0.1% or less.

2.5.9. Statistical analysis

One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for statistical analysis to compare different groups at the same time points. When appropriate, Fisher least significant difference or Tukey multiple comparisons post hoc tests were used to compare groups. Two-way ANOVA with repeated measures was used to compare the postdosing difference between 2 different test compound groups at the same dose. A paired Student t test was used to compare baseline to LAT8881 response in the same group. The significance level was set at P < 0.05.

4. Results

4.1. LAT8881 and related peptides reduce hypersensitivity in rodent models of neuropathy

To investigate potential antiallodynic effects of the peptide LAT8881 and its metabolite LAT9991 in neuropathic hypersensitivity, we first investigated their effects on mechanical allodynia in sciatic nerve-injury models. Both compounds significantly reduced ipsilateral PWT in a spinal nerve ligation (SNL, Chung) model when dosed orally, increasing at 1 to 2 hours and declining by 4 hours (Figs. 1A and B). All doses of LAT9991 and 2 and 5 mg/kg LAT8881 were not significantly different from the positive control, 100 mg/kg gabapentin (see supplemental digital content, tables 1-2, http://links.lww.com/PAIN/C510). Similarly, when dosed intramuscularly, both compounds increased PWT in a dose-dependent manner (see supplemental digital content, tables 3-4, http://links.lww.com/PAIN/C510). PWT on the contralateral side was not significantly affected throughout the observation period for either route of administration (P > 0.05 for all time points compared with predosing levels and vehicle). When intravenous LAT8881 was tested in the chronic constriction sciatic nerve injury model (CCI), a similar time course of antiallodynic effect was observed, with no significant differences between male and female rats (Fig. 1C).

(A and B) LAT8881 and LAT9991 administered orally induce a dose-dependent reversal of mechanical allodynia in SNL (Chung) models of neuropathic hypersensitivity. (C) LAT8881 administered intravenously was also effective at reducing PWT in a CCI model of neuropathic hypersensitivity, with no significant sex differences. (D-G) Effect of 5 mg/kg LAT8881 or vehicle (p.o.) on mechanical allodynia in (D) oxaliplatin-induced chemotherapeutic neuropathic models, (E) painful diabetic neuropathy models, (F) reserpine-induced fibromyalgia, and (G) postsurgical incision (Brennan model). (H) Summary of the maximal effect on mechanical allodynia of LAT8881 (5 mg/kg) compared with vehicle at the same time point in neuropathic, nociplastic, and surgical hypersensitivity models. Data are shown as mean ± SEM, n = 6 to 8 for each group.

To test whether LAT8881 was antiallodynic in other rodent models of neuropathic hypersensitivity that have different causes and pathophysiology, we investigated the effects on PWT in models of chemotherapy-induced neuropathy and hypersensitive type 1 diabetic neuropathy. Oxaliplatin, administered to rats to induce peripheral neuropathy, incrementally reduced PWT from 14.58 ± 0.42 g to 3.83 ± 0.11 g over 2 weeks. Following dosing with 5 mg/kg LAT8881 (PO), PWT significantly increased to 6.00 ± 0.26 g and 8.50 ± 0.22 g at 1 and 2 hours postdosing, respectively (P < 0.001) (Fig. 1D). The antiallodynic effect of LAT8881 in painful diabetic neuropathy was assessed in the streptozotocin (STZ)-induced model of type 1 diabetes. Before STZ injection, the average PWT of the rats in the LAT8881 group was 15.00 ± 0.00 g, which gradually decreased to 3.83 ± 0.11 g over 2 weeks. Following dosing with LAT8881, PWT significantly increased at 1 hour postdosing (7.33 ± 0.56 g, P < 0.01, compared with predose), peaking at 2 hours postdosing (10.25 ± 1.22 g, P < 0.01). The effect slightly declined at 4 hours postdosing but still remained significantly higher than the PWT at baseline (8.33 ± 0.80 g, P < 0.01) (Fig. 1E).

To investigate whether LAT8881 was effective at reducing nociplastic hypersensitivity, we tested its effect in the reserpine model of fibromyalgia. Interestingly, we found that 5 mg/kg LAT8881 (PO) completely reversed mechanical allodynia, peaking at the 4- and 6-hour time points (12.92 ± 2.08 g and 12.50 ± 1.71 g, respectively). The antiallodynic effect in this model was long lasting, with PWT remaining significantly elevated 24 hours (11.58 ± 1.96 g, P < 0.01) and 48 hours (8.67 ± 2.08 g, P < 0.05) postdosing, while PWT in the vehicle group did not significantly change over the whole observation period (Fig. 1F).

Finally, to determine whether LAT8881 was effective at reducing sensitivity in a postsurgical hypersensitivity model, rats were dosed with vehicle or LAT8881 before and 24 hours after performing a plantar surgical incision. PWT on the ipsilateral hind paw of the LAT8881-dosed groups decreased sharply from 15.00 ± 0.00 g to 1.71 ± 0.71 g 2 hours postsurgery. At 4 hours after surgery, PWT remained low in the vehicle-dosed group but increased to 3.00 ± 0.93 g in the LAT8881-dosed group. Allodynia in this group continued to wane, with PWT increasing to 6.29 ± 0.68 g by 24 hours. A second dose of LAT8881 significantly increased PWT at 2 and 4 hours (6.29 ± 0.68 g and 8.00 ± 0.44 g, respectively, P < 0.05 compared with vehicle) (Fig. 1G). We found LAT8881 significantly reduced mechanical allodynia in all models that involved peripheral or central sensitization (Fig. 1H), with fast onset and a gradual decline in activity when administered orally, intravenously, and intramuscularly.

We also compared the effects of LAT8881 with 2 other related ligands, LAT7771 and LAT9993, in the Chung model of neuropathic hypersensitivity. LAT7771 is a cyclized peptide, and LAT9993 is a homologue of LAT9991. When administered at 10 mg/kg (i.m), both compounds significantly increased the PWT of the ipsilateral hind paws compared with predose and vehicle levels (see supplemental digital content, Fig. 1, http://links.lww.com/PAIN/C510).

4.2. LAT8881 is not effective at reducing inflammatory hypersensitivity in rodents

To investigate the role of LAT8881 in inflammatory pain, we administered LAT8881 orally to rats with CFA-induced plantar inflammation and tested PWL with a heat stimulus. Surprisingly, we found no significant differences in PWL between vehicle and LAT8881 across all doses, up to 30 mg/kg, over the 4-hour testing period. Morphine, used as a positive control, rapidly increased PWL to pre-CFA levels (Fig. 2A, see supplemental digital content, table 5, http://links.lww.com/PAIN/C510). The PWL on the contralateral side was not significantly changed over the observation period (P > 0.05 for all time points, see supplemental digital content, table 6, http://links.lww.com/PAIN/C510). In a separate experiment, 10 mg/kg LAT8881 administered intraperitoneally showed no significant effect on thermal PWL or mechanical PWT in either male or female rats (Fig. 2A).

To further investigate the lack of effect seen in the CFA model, we next tested 5 mg/kg LAT8881 in the carrageenan-induced model of inflammatory hypersensitivity in rats. The thermal PWL in both the vehicle- and LAT8881-treated groups declined in the hours following the carrageenan injection, but the LAT8881-treated group decreased to a slightly lesser extent, and at the 4-hour time point, the PWL was significantly higher in the LAT8881 group than in the vehicle group (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2B).

We next tested LAT8881 in a formalin-induced hypersensitivity model, which induces a biphasic nociceptive response, with an early response that reflects acute hypersensitivity and a late phase that arises from sensitization of central nociceptive neurons. As the peak effect of LAT8881 occurred around 1 to 2 hours postdosing in neuropathic models, we tested the effects of preemptive dosing at 90 minutes before injection of formalin into a hind paw. During the first 5 minutes after injection of formalin, rats injected with LAT8881 spent significantly less time licking the affected paw than those in the vehicle-treated group (51.12 ± 9.86 seconds for the LAT8881 group compared with 85.81 ± 10.07 seconds for the vehicle-treated group, P < 0.05) (Fig. 2C). To investigate whether LAT8881 would be effective at reducing osteoarthritis hypersensitivity, we used an MIA-induced arthritis model in rats. The average static weight-bearing time on the ipsilateral side decreased sharply 2 weeks following MIA injection. After dosing, no significant differences between vehicle and LAT8881 groups were observed, with time spent on each side remaining stable throughout this period (Fig. 2D).

4.3. LAT8881 does not reduce physiological nociceptor function

To test the effect of LAT8881 on responses to a noxious thermal stimulus in normal animals, we tested the tail-flick test in response to a radiant heat stimulus. Neither LAT8881, dosed at 10 mg/kg (po), nor vehicle had any significant effect on tail-flick latency in male or female rats. Tail-flick latency increased in both groups throughout the observational period, but none of these effects were statistically significant compared with their baseline latency or to vehicle groups at the same time points (Fig. 2E). In summary, no consistent analgesic effects were seen following administration of LAT8881 in the inflammatory or nociceptive models tested (Fig. 2F).

4.4. LAT8881 suppresses ectopic firing originating at the level of the dorsal root ganglion in neuropathic models

Data from behavioral studies indicated that LAT8881 was most effective in models of neuropathic hypersensitivity. Thus, we used electrophysiological recordings in vivo in CCI models of neuropathic pain to determine the potential sites of action of LAT8881 (Figs. 3A and B). LAT8881 had no significant effect on spontaneous ectopic firing of neuroma origin. Spontaneous firing rate, recorded distal to the neuroma, before dosing amounted to 425.6 ± 77.9 spikes/min (7.1 Hz) and was unchanged after dosing with 1 mg/kg LAT881 (iv), amounting to 437.3 ± 88.0 (102.3 ± 4.9% of baseline, 7.3 Hz), 460.1 ± 78.1, 452.5 ± 73.5, and 444.5 ± 70.1 spikes/min at 10-, 20-, 30-, and 40-minute postdosing, respectively (Fig. 3C). In a parallel vehicle control group, firing rate at baseline amounted to 742.4 ± 199.6 spikes/min (12.3 Hz) and did not significantly change after dosing with vehicle, amounting to 736.2 ± 160.9, 737.1 ± 185.1, 789.8 ± 224.16, and 790.3 ± 238.8 spikes/min at the 10-, 20-, 30-, and 40-minute time points postdosing with vehicle. To validate the assay and recording configuration, lidocaine (5 mg/kg, i.v.) was administered at the end of recording and significantly reduced ectopic discharge of neuroma origin. Therefore, ectopic firing of neuroma origin in sural nerve fibres after dosing with LAT8881 was neither significantly different from the control level before dosing (P > 0.05, t test) nor from that in the vehicle group at any time point (P > 0.05, compared with the groups at the same time points, one-way ANOVA) (Fig. 3).

(B) Schematic showing recording configurations. Scissors indicate where nerves were sectioned to enable isolation of firing originating from the DRG with contribution from the neuroma removed. The effect of LAT8881 on ectopic discharge generated at the level of the DRG was also tested. The spontaneous firing rate before dosing was 555.3 ± 147.4 spikes/min (9.3 Hz) and was not significantly different from that in the vehicle group before dosing (P > 0.05, one-way ANOVA). Following dosing with LAT8881, ectopic firing rate progressively decreased to 540.1 ± 147.1, 528.5 ± 137.7, 508.4 ± 130.4, and 392.8 ± 119.7 spikes/min (69.4 ± 7.4% of baseline) at the 10, 20, 30-, and 40-minute time points, respectively (Fig. 3A). This effect on ectopic firing was significant at the 40-minute time point postdosing compared with control (P < 0.05, paired Student t test) and the vehicle group at the same time point (P < 0.05, one-way ANOVA). Subsequent dosing with the positive control lidocaine (5 mg/kg, i.v.) further significantly suppressed ectopic firing of DRG origin (Fig. 3C).

4.5. LAT8881 suppresses spontaneous firing and wind-up in spinal cord wide dynamic range neurons in neuropathic hypersensitivity models

To understand the effect of LAT8881 on nociceptive circuit excitability, we performed in vivo electrophysiological recordings from spinal cord wide dynamic range (WDR) neurons in CCI model rats, recording from the depth of 400 to 700 µM, roughly corresponding to lamina V. We found that LAT8881, dosed at 1 mg/kg (i.v.), decreased spontaneous activity from a baseline firing rate of 859.1 ± 280.4 spikes/min (14.3 Hz) to 587.6 ± 231.7 (9.8 Hz, 73.4 ± 14.7%) within 20 minutes postdosing, which was sustained over the 40-minute recording period (Figs. 4A and B). This decrease in activity was significant compared with the vehicle control group (P < 0.05), which increased slightly from baseline over the recording period, with significant elevation at the 30-minute time point (P < 0.05).

LAT8881 suppresses spontaneous firing and wind-up in deep dorsal horn WDR neurons. (A) In vivo recordings from WDR neurons before and after LAT8881 (1 mg/kg; i.v.) showing spontaneous activity and after-discharge in a CCI model of neuropathic hypersensitivity. (B–D) Summary data of the effects of LAT8881 on (B) spontaneous activity, (C) wind-up, and (D) after-discharge of WDR neurons. Data were normalised to baseline controls. Data are shown as mean ± SEM, n = 6 per group. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, compared with the vehicle group (Two-way ANOVA with Sidak multiple-comparison test).

As WDR neurons exhibit a transient sensitization phenomenon called wind-up when exposed to repetitive or high-intensity stimuli from peripheral nociceptors, we were interested in examining the effect of LAT8881 on this activity. Following electrical stimulation of the dorsal roots, the number of action potentials associated with wind-up amounted to 51.4 ± 24.3 spikes (0.85 Hz) in the vehicle group, which did not change significantly at any point postdosing. By contrast, LAT8881 significantly reduced wind-up, reducing spikes from 32.8 ± 6.1 to 5.6 ± 1.9 (0.54-0.09 Hz, P < 0.01) by 20 minutes, which was sustained over 40 minutes (Fig. 4A and C).

We also examined the effects of LAT8881 on after-discharge, a characteristic of wind-up where WDR neurons continue to fire following the cessation of repetitive afferent stimulation. We found that vehicle injection did not affect after-discharge (P > 0.05), while LAT8881 slightly, but not significantly, reduced after-discharge, with the number of action potentials reduced from a baseline of 203.3 ± 60.6 spikes to 105.9 ± 39.5 (68.3 ± 13.9%) at 40 minutes postdosing, respectively (Fig. 4D).

4.6. LAT8881 suppresses activity in dorsal horn neurons by activating resting membrane conductances

To further characterise the site and mechanism of action of LAT8881, we performed whole-cell patch-clamp recordings in the dorsal horn of spinal cord slices prepared from Chung (SNL) models of neuropathic hypersensitivity. In a subset of dorsal horn neurons (n = 7/23), LAT8881 induced membrane potential hyperpolarisation and suppression of spontaneous action potential firing, an effect that was concentration-dependent over the range 10 nM-10 μM (Fig. 5A). In these neurons, membrane potential significantly hyperpolarised from a baseline level of −49.3 ± 2.4 mV to a new steady-state level of −63.0 ± 2.4 mV in the presence of 10 μM LAT8881 (P < 0.05) amounting to a 13.7 ± 3.9 mV membrane hyperpolarisation. LAT8881-induced membrane potential hyperpolarisation was associated with a significant reduction in neuronal input resistance from a baseline level of 579.6 ± 178.9 MΩ to 300.9 ± 103.2 MΩ in the presence of LAT8881 (P < 0.05) amounting to 49.4 ± 4.4% of baseline control (P < 0.001) (Fig. 5B). Voltage-current (VI) relations were generated for each neuron in the absence and presence of LAT8881 to probe the potential ionic mechanisms underlying LAT8881-induced membrane potential hyperpolarisation. The reversal potential of LAT8881-induced membrane potential hyperpolarisation indicated by the point of intersection of VI relations in the presence and absence of LAT8881, ranged between −92 mV and −74 mV with a mean of −83.3 ± 2.7 mV (Fig. 5C). These data suggest that LAT8881 induced membrane potential hyperpolarisation by activating 1 or more potassium conductances, although a role for a chloride-dependent mechanism cannot be entirely ruled out.

(B) LAT8881-induced inhibition of dorsal horn neurons was characterised by significant membrane hyperpolarisation and reduction in neuronal input resistance. *P < 0.05, compared with control (nonnormalised data). 4.7. LAT8881 suppresses excitatory dorsal root afferent–mediated synaptic inputs and enhances inhibitory synaptic transmission

Initial experiments in spinal cord slice preparations from rat Chung (SNL) models of neuropathic hypersensitivity also suggested an effect of LAT8881 on dorsal root afferent–mediated excitatory synaptic inputs. To further explore this possibility, we investigated the effects of LAT8881 in spinal cord slice preparations prepared from CCI models of neuropathic hypersensitivity, to align with our previous in vivo recordings (Figs. 3 and 4). To localise the site of action of LAT8881, we used a customized grease-gap approach where a layer of grease was positioned between the spinal cord slice and the dorsal roots, permitting changes to bathing solutions on either the DRG/dorsal root afferent compartment or the spinal cord compartment.

Bath application of LAT8881 (5 μM) to the DRG/dorsal root afferent compartment had no significant effect on dorsal horn neuron activity (n = 5). The mean resting potential at baseline amounted to −54.5 ± 4.6 mV and −57.0 ± 3.8 mV in the presence of LAT8881 applied to the dorsal roots and DRG only (P = 0.19 vs baseline). Following washout, subsequent exposure of the spinal cord compartment to LAT8881 induced significant membrane potential hyperpolarisation in all neurons to −64.8 ± 2.0 mV (P < 0.05; paired Student t test), amounting to a 10.3 ± 3 mV membrane potential hyperpolarisation. Neuronal input resistance in baseline control was 911 ± 151 MΩ (n = 6) and following exposure of the dorsal roots and DRG alone, this decreased to 750 ± 139 MΩ (84.1 ± 11.5% of baseline) and further decreased to 473 ± 112 MΩ (50.2 ± 8.0% of control) following exposure of the spinal cord slice to LAT8881 (P < 0.05, one-way ANOVA, n = 5) (Fig. 6A).

(B) Superimposed EPSPs evoked by 1 Hz electrical stimulation of dorsal roots in a lamina I/II neurone were suppressed in 1 μM LAT8881. (C) Effects of 5 μM LAT8881 on dorsal root afferent-evoked EPSPs. Grouped data are shown as mean ± SEM, n = 5 per group, * P< 0.05, one-way ANOVA with Tukey multiple-comparison test.

Electrical stimulation of DRG/Dorsal roots at a frequency of 0.1 Hz evoked excitatory postsynaptic potentials (EPSPs), which were suppressed in the presence of 1 μM (Figs. 6B) and 5 μM LAT8881 applied to the DRG/dorsal root compartment (Fig. 6C). The amplitude of these EPSPs was significantly suppressed from a baseline level of 14.3 ± 1.5 mV to 6.4 ± 0.8 mV (P < 0.01 compared with baseline; one-way ANOVA). Subsequent application of LAT8881 to the spinal cord compartment reduced the EPSP peak amplitude to 5.5 + 0.7 mV applied to the spinal cord slice, although this effect was not statistically significant.

4.8. Identification of molecular target of LAT8881 and validation of LanCL1

To identify protein targets of LAT8881 and LAT9991 (the LAT8881 metabolite), a propargyl-diazirine photocrosslinker (PAL) was covalently conjugated to LAT9991F (LAT9991F-PAL). Activity of LAT9991F-PAL was confirmed using whole-cell patch-clamp recording in spinal cord slices. LAT9991F-PAL (1 μM) mimicked effects of LAT8881 and LAT9991, inducing membrane potential hyperpolarization and cessation of spontaneous activity in dorsal horn neurons (Fig. 7A). Spinal cord microsomes were prepared from freshly excised spinal cords from Chung models of neuropathic hypersensitivity and were treated with LAT9991F-PAL (5 µM) peptide in the absence or presence of LAT8881 (50 µM), followed by crosslinking between the PAL peptide and its interacting proteins by UV irradiation (365 nm for 20 minutes). After a click chemistry reaction between the alkyne reporter tag on the PAL probe and an azido-biotin probe, biotin-labelled proteins were enriched with streptavidin beads. Input, flow-through (FT), and eluted proteins were separated on SDS-PAGE, bands were excised, and mass spectrometry sequencing was employed to identify LanCL1 as a primary target that was enriched after binding to LAT9991F-PAL and decreased in the presence of excess LAT8881 (Fig. 7B). Proteins with an adjusted P value (or q value) <5% and an FC of >2 were selected to be differentially modulated. A protein was considered a hit of LAT8881 when identified with at least 2 peptides, FC > 2, and adjusted P < 0.05 in the 2 comparisons: PAL/DMSO and LAT991F-PAL/LAT991F-PAL + LAT8881. The presence of LanCL1 in the eluted protein band was confirmed by Western blotting with anti-LanCL1 antibodies, which consistently revealed 2 prominent bands at approximately 37 kDa and 50 kDa (Fig. 7C). The predicted molecular weight of LanCL1 is ∼37 kDa, and to date, no reports in the literature have described a 50 kDa isoform or posttranslationally modified variant of LanCL1. In HEK293 cells transiently transfected with a LanCL1 expression plasmid, the 50 kDa band remains unchanged compared with untransfected controls, suggesting it is not related to LanCL1 overexpression. By contrast, the 37 kDa band showed a marked increase in intensity, consistent with LanCL1 overexpression (supplemental digital content, 2B, http://links.lww.com/PAIN/C510). Conversely, in A549 cells subjected to LanCL1 knockdown using siRNA (siLanCL1), the 50 kDa band remains unaffected, while the 37 kDa band is significantly reduced (see supplemental digital content, Fig. 2C, http://links.lww.com/PAIN/C510). These observations support the conclusion that the 37 kDa band corresponds to LanCL1, whereas the 50 kDa band likely represents a nonspecific signal or cross-reactivity.

To validate LanCL1 as the target for LAT8881, we performed intra-DRG injections of siRNA for LanCL1 or control (scrambled) siRNA in Chung model rats, which were dosed the following day with either LAT8881 or vehicle. Before LAT8881 administration, we found that the ipsilateral PWT in the LanCL1 siRNA group decreased slightly from a predosing value of 3.33 ± 0.33 g to 2.82 ± 0.38 g, although this effect was not statistically significant. Following oral administration of LAT8881 (10 mg/kg), the PWT of the control siRNA group increased significantly to 13.20 ± 1.80 g (2 hours postdose, P < 0.001); however, LAT8881 administered to the LanCL1 siRNA group was without effect. PWT of the LanCL1 siRNA groups dosed with vehicle and LAT8881 was not significantly different at any time point postdosing (P > 0.05, one-way ANOVA), and the control siRNA group dosed with LAT8881 was significantly different from the vehicle group at all time points postdosing (Fig. 7D). No change in PWT was observed on the contralateral side in any groups.

As an additional control, we also explored the potential role of LanCL2. The day after injecting siRNA for LanCL2 in Chung rats, we administered LAT8881 (10 mg/kg), which significantly increased PWT to 9.89 ± 1.39 at 2 hours postdosing (P < 0.001, compared with the predosing level and vehicle control) (data not shown). Gabapentin (100 mg/kg; PO) was employed in a positive control group. Ipsilateral PWT increased significantly, reaching 10.13 ± 1.60 g at the 2-hour point (P < 0.01, compared with the predosing level).

Finally, to confirm that the LanCL1 protein had been reduced in siRNA-treated rats, we terminally anaesthetized the animals from the behavioural studies and collected their lumbar DRG, which were sectioned and immunolabelled with selective antibodies for LanCL1. We found that LanCL1 expression was reduced in all DRG sections (n = 57) screened from animals pre-injected with siRNA for LanCL1 compared with those treated with scrambled siRNA (Fig. 7D).

4.9. LanCL1 is expressed in dorsal root ganglion satellite glial cells

Having identified LanCL1 as a molecular target of LAT compounds in models of neuropathic hypersensitivity, we undertook immunohistochemical studies to identify where LanCL1 is expressed in sensory information processing pathways and if it is subject to changes in functional expression in neuropathic hypersensitivity. Initial experiments investigating cellular expression of LanCL1 in the DRG sections from neuropathic models showed immunolabelling that was located around neurons, in addition to labelling within the DRG soma (Fig. 8A). To investigate whether LanCL1 is expressed in the satellite glial cells (SGCs) that ensheath the neuronal membrane, sections of L4-5 DRG were immunolabelled for detection of LanCL1, with the SGC marker glutamine synthetase (GS) and the neuronal marker NeuN (Fig. 8AB). LanCL1 was found to colocalize with the GS marker in neuropathic animals, while DRG from inflammatory models and naive controls had less SGC expression but increased neuronal LanCL1. To investigate whether LanCL1 was localized to activated glial cells in neuropathic DRG, tissue sections were immunolabelled with GFAP, which labels activated SGCs, in addition to nonmyelinating Schwann cells, and NeuN (Fig. 8C). The results showed clear areas of colocalization with GFAP and LanCL1 in DRG from neuropathic animals, with less colocalization in inflammatory hypersensitivity models and little to no GFAP labelling in DRG from naive animals.

4.10. Cytosolic LanCL1 expression in small-medium diameter dorsal root ganglion neurons is reduced in neuropathic hypersensitivity states

To investigate the DRG subtypes that exhibit cytosolic LanCL1 expression, sections of L4-5 DRG were immunolabelled for specific neuronal markers and LanCL1 expression (Fig. 9A). LanCL1 was expressed in the cytosol of 16.9 ± 1.3% of DRG neurons (NeuN) from naive animals and 17.6 ± 2.0% DRG from inflammatory models. However, following the development of neuropathic hypersensitivity, there was a significant reduction in neuronal cytosolic expression of LanCL1, which decreased to 3.6 ± 0.9% (P < 0.001, one-way ANOVA with Dunnett multiple-comparison test). No significant difference in neuronal LanCL1 expression was detected when DRG from male and female animals were compared in each group. The reduction in neuronal LanCL1 expression was also seen in IB4-positive, nonpeptidergic small-diameter DRG neurons, with 30.3 ± 3.9% colocalization in naive DRG and 9.9 ± 4.6% in the neuropathic group (P = 0.02). However, some of these nonmyelinated C-fibres showed LanCL1 labelling closer to the edge of the soma, suggesting the protein may be associated with the membrane (Fig. 9B). LanCL1 was detected in 29.5 ± 5.9% of TRPV1-expressing DRG neurons in naive DRG, which was reduced to 10.1 ± 2.7% in neuropathic DRG (P = 0.01). Peptidergic CGRP-expressing DRG neurons from naive animals expressed higher levels of LanCL1 with 46.6 ± 5.7% colocalization, which was reduced in neuropathic DRG to 8.8 ± 1.9% (P < 0.001). The large-diameter NF200-expressing neurons showed the least expression of LanCL1, with colocalization detected in 2.6 ± 1.7% of neurons from the naive group and 0.5 ± 0.5% of neurons from the neuropathic group (P = 0.6, one-way ANOVA with Dunnett multiple-comparison test).

In the dorsal horn, LanCL1 was detected in the afferent fibres within the white matter, extending into the superficial layers of the dorsal horn, with similar expression in the ipsilateral and contralateral sides in CCI models (Fig. 10A); however, it is not known whether this expression is cytosolic or membrane associated. To localize the region of the superficial dorsal horn innervated by LanCL1-positive neurons, sections were co-labelled with IB4 to stain nonmyelinated C-fibres that innervate inner lamina II. LanCL1 labelling was observed in lamina 1 through lamina II, just below the IB4 boundary (Figure 10B, magnification shown in supplemental digital content, Fig. 3, http://links.lww.com/PAIN/C510).

5. Discussion

Data presented in this study using a novel class of peptide compounds demonstrate a clear role for LanCL1 in neuropathic hypersensitivity and suggest that this could be a viable molecular target for treating neuropathic pain. LAT8881 (and related peptides) suppressed mechanical allodynia in multiple rodent models of neuropathic hypersensitivity, including models of chemotherapy-induced neuropathic hypersensitivity and diabetic neuropathy. Conversely, LAT8881 was ineffective in acute and chronic models of inflammatory hypersensitivity and physiological nociceptive pain, suggesting a selective role of LanCL1 in neuropathic syndromes.

Through our studies using a photoaffinity-labelled conjugate of LAT9991, spinal cord microsomes from rodent Chung models, and mass spectrometry sequencing, we identified LanCL1 as the protein that mediates the effects of LAT8881 and LAT9991. We subsequently validated LanCL1 as the molecular target through behavioural studies in neuropathic models where siRNA knockdown of LanCL1 at the level of the DRG eliminated the effects of LAT8881. This, to our knowledge, is the first description of a role for LanCL1 in neuropathic hypersensitivity.

Electrophysiological studies in vivo demonstrated a significant reduction in spontaneous ectopic firing at the level of the DRG in response to LAT8881 but intriguingly had no effect on ectopic firing isolated at the level of the neuroma. Whole-cell patch-clamp recording from acutely dissociated DRG neurons, prepared from Chung models of neuropathic hypersensitivity, revealed no significant effect of LAT8881 or LAT9991 on electrophysiological properties in the vast majority of small, medium, and large-diameter neurons, although effects on membrane potential and input resistance were encountered in some neurons (data not shown). Immunohistochemical studies from DRG revealed a dynamic shift in expression of LanCL1, with expression being reduced in neuropathic hypersensitivity in the cytosol of small-diameter peptidergic and nonpeptidergic C-fibres and large-diameter fibres compared with naive models. However, LanCL1 expression was markedly increased in activated SGCs of neuropathic models, unlike naive and inflammatory models. Taken together, these data indicate that LanCL1 in SGC of peripheral nerves contributes to neuropathic hypersensitivity, and intact neuron-glia units may be required for the antiallodynic effects of LAT8881 and LAT9991.

Satellite glial cells are expressed throughout peripheral ganglia and are believed to be activated rapidly, within hours and days of nerve injury in affected ganglia.90 They have a distinct morphology, wrapping themselves closely around the soma of the cell bodies of sensory ganglion neurons, forming neuron-glia units, the close proximity facilitating interaction and communication between component units.27,29,36,37,60,66,68 SGCs have been shown to be activated in response to nerve-injury models of neuropathic hypersensitivity, indicated by upregulation of glial fibrillary protein (GFAP), including spinal nerve ligation models,40,51 streptozotocin-induced diabetic neuropathy,28 chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy,49,85 and axotomised rats.86 Activation of SGC is also associated with downregulation of the potassium channel Kir4.181,83–85,91; increased synthesis and release of cytokines; enhanced gap-junction–mediated coupling between SGC and SGC and DRG neurons and changes in expression of connexin and pannexin proteins; increased ATP-mediated signalling and sensitivity.29,60 Upregulation of LanCL1 can now also be added to the list of signalling mechanisms subject to functional reorganisation in SGC in neuropathic hypersensitivity.

Evidence is accumulating that activation of SGC and changes in SGC signalling contribute to chronic pain by enhancing neuronal activity in peripheral nerves. Similarly, activation of SGC is thought to be triggered by increased activity resulting from nerve injury, although currently the mechanisms by which this occurs are uncertain.29 In relation to this, increased expression of LanCL1 in SGC could be a consequence of increased neuronal activity in neuropathic sensitisation, spontaneous ectopic firing being a “hallmark” of this condition. Previous studies suggest that expression of LanCL1 is activity-dependent and contributes to antioxidant activity and neuroprotection in the CNS.33 Thus, it is tempting to speculate that increased LanCL1 expression in SGC in neuropathic pain is driven by nerve injury–induced and subsequent ectopic firing-dependent neuronal activity to confer antioxidant activity and neuroprotection in painful neuropathies in which oxidative stress is known to contribute, including chemotherapy-induced neuropathy.1,17,41,42,72 Currently, further studies are ongoing to determine the mechanism underlying the role of LanCL1 at the level of the SGC and in neuropathic pain signalling mechanisms.

In addition to a role for LanCL1 in neuropathic hypersensitivity at the level of the DRG, in vivo electrophysiological recordings indicated a significant effect at the level of the spinal peripheral afferent in the spinal cord. LAT8881 significantly suppressed spontaneous activity and wind-up in WDR neurons in the dorsal horn of CCI models of neuropathic hypersensitivity. Whole-cell recordings in isolated spinal cord slice preparations from Chung models revealed that LAT8881 induced a concentration-dependent suppression of spontaneous activity and suppressed dorsal root afferent–mediated excitatory synaptic transmission. These data suggest a potential site of action at the level of the primary afferent input in the dorsal horn, a notion further supported by immunohistochemical localization of LanCL1 to fibre tracts at the entry zone to the dorsal horn in sections from CCI models of neuropathic hypersensitivity. Taken together, the data indicate a key role of LanCL1 in dorsal root afferent fibre tracts and terminals at the level of the dorsal horn in neuropathic hypersensitivity, where LAT8881 acts to suppress dorsal root afferent–mediated excitatory synaptic transmission. This is the first report of a role for LancL1 in neuropathic hypersensitivity and primary afferent–mediated synaptic transmission and further adds to the growing interest on the roles of LanCLs in health and disease. Previous studies demonstrated a role for LanCL2 in neuropathic pain in the dorsal horn. However, unlike LanCL1, LanCL2 expression was restricted exclusively to microglia in the dorsal horn. siRNA-driven knockdown of LanCL2 in the dorsal horn mimics the peripheral nerve spinal ligation model behavioural and molecular phenotype. Using a proposed endogenous ligand of LanCL2, abscisic acid (ABA), a role for LanCL2 and ABA in regulating spinal neuroinflammation and pain information processing was proposed.55 Interestingly, as ABA is a potential ligand recognising both LanCL1 and LanCL2, albeit with higher affinity for LanCL2,12,70,74,78 a role for LanCL1 at the level of the peripheral afferent input in the effects of ABA in the study by Maixner et al. cannot be discounted. A role for both LanCL2 centrally and LanCL1 peripherally is highly likely, although a central role for LanCL1 requires further study. Recent analyses of databases from cortical samples suggest that LanCL1 is a key immune marker for neuropathic pain, that LanCL1 may be a protective factor in this condition, and that the miR-6325/LancL1 axis could contribute to neuropathic pain development.71 The effects of LAT8881 in vivo persisted after knockdown of LanCL2 at the level of the DRG, suggesting LAT8881 has no effect at this receptor or that LanCL2 has relatively little role in neuropathic pain peripherally compared with its role centrally. Interestingly, we also observed effects of LAT8881 on dorsal horn neurons that involved activation of a potassium conductance, suggesting a direct effect of LAT8881 on some central neurons. Whether these effects are mediated by LanCL1 or represent “off-target” effects of the compound is currently unclear. However, we have also noted a potential role for LanCL3 in mediating effects of LAT8881 and LanCL3 may also have a role in neuropathic hypersensitivity (unpublished observations).

Although we have observed that LanCL1 is expressed at the level of the primary afferent terminals in the dorsal horn and effects of LAT8881 act through LanCL1 to suppress primary afferent synaptic input, we do not currently know the signal transduction mechanisms following ligand-receptor coupling that gives rise to suppression of neuronal excitability and reversal of mechanical allodynia. LanCL1, through a mechanism resembling glutathione S-transferase, protects neurons from ROS, acting as a ROS scavenger. ROS are well documented to enhance excitatory primary afferent–mediated synaptic transmission, suppress inhibitory GABA-mediated synaptic transmission in the dorsal horn, and promote pain signalling, whereas ROS scavengers have the opposing effect.4,8,18,20,26,43,48,65,89 Whole-cell patch-clamp recordings in spinal cord slices from models of neuropathic hypersensitivity described in our study showed similar effects of LAT8881 on dorsal root afferent–mediated synaptic transmission, excitatory inputs being suppressed and dominant inhibitory transmission revealed or enhanced.

LanCL1 in addition to acting as a ROS scavenger is also suggested to enhance the serine/threonine kinase Akt activity forming a means of defence essential for neuronal function and protection.33,82 In relation to this, the PI3-kinase/Akt pathway is involved in spinal central sensitization associated with neuropathic pain,52 and multiple studies indicate a key role for this pathway in neuropathic pain.16,21,35,44,88,91 An alternative pathway that may couple with LanCL1-dependent signalling is an adenosine monophosphate–activated protein kinase (AMPK)74–77 which, like LanCL1, is functionally dependent on electrical excitability. Adenosine monophosphate–activated protein kinase itself has emerged as a potential target for treating chronic pain3,61 and has been demonstrated to contribute to neuropathic and postsurgical pain in a range of rodent models.10,14,53,56,67 Further work is being undertaken to elucidate the signal transduction pathways by which LAT8881 engages LanCL1-dependent signalling to alleviate neuropathic pain symptoms. In summary, data presented in this study provide evidence supporting a role for LanCL1 in neuropathic pain and as a potential target to treat this condition in its multiple forms.
