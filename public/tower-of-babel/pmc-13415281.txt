Indirect Treatment Comparison of the Efficacy of Long-Acting Injectable Cabotegravir Compared With Lenacapavir for HIV Pre-exposure Prophylaxis
Authors: Neil Hawkins, Aniruddha Hazra, Marisa Brizzi, Juliette Thompson, Richard Grove, Marius Sieverding, Piotr Budnik, Jenny Scherzer, Natalya Danchenko, Sarah-Jane Anderson, Laure Dupont-Benjamin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionLong-acting injectable cabotegravir (hereafter referred to as cabotegravir) is the first long-acting injectable approved for pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) based on the HPTN 083 and 084 trials. Long-acting injectable lenacapavir (hereafter referred to as lenacapavir) was approved for PrEP based on the PURPOSE 1 and 2 trials. In the absence of a head-to-head trial, we conducted an indirect treatment comparison (ITC) to assess efficacy of lenacapavir versus cabotegravir in reducing the risk of HIV acquisition.MethodsA systematic literature review identified all relevant trials. Efficacy inputs for this ITC were derived from re-adjudicated data from the injection phase of the HPTN trials or published data from the PURPOSE trials. The ITC was performed using 2 network approaches: (1) using no PrEP and (2) using daily oral tenofovir disoproxil fumarate/emtricitabine (TDF/FTC) as common comparators, with adjustments accounting for differing levels of adherence to TDF/FTC across trials. Sensitivity analyses were also performed using data from both the oral lead-in and injection phases of the HPTN trials.ResultsBoth network approaches estimated high and comparable efficacy of cabotegravir and lenacapavir. Using no PrEP (network approach 1), the base case estimated hazard ratio (95% CrI) of lenacapavir versus cabotegravir was 1.04 (0.19, 5.69) in men who have sex with men and transgender women and 0.00 (0.00, 3.21) in cisgender women. Predicted efficacy (95% CrI) rates of cabotegravir versus no PrEP were high in men who have sex with men and transgender women 96% (90%, 98%)] and cisgender women [98% (89%, 100%)] in the HPTN trials, which were comparable to the PURPOSE trials [men and gender-diverse people, 96% (82%, 99%); cisgender women, 100%(96%,100%)]. Sensitivity analyses confirmed these findings.ConclusionBased on this ITC, cabotegravir and lenacapavir offer similar and high efficacy in preventing HIV acquisitions compared with no PrEP or TDF/FTC.Graphical abstract and video abstract available for this article.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12325-026-03591-7.Keywords: Cabotegravir, Efficacy, HIV, Indirect treatment comparison, Lenacapavir, Long-acting injectable, Oral tenofovir disoproxil fumarate/emtricitabine, Pre-exposure prophylaxis, PrEP

Introduction

The first injectable pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) regimen, long-acting injectable cabotegravir (hereafter referred to as cabotegravir), was approved for administration every 2 months by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in December 2021 and by the European Medicines Agency in September 2023 [1, 2]. In the phase 3 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) HPTN 083 (men who have sex with men and transgender women) and HPTN 084 (cisgender women), cabotegravir was superior versus daily oral tenofovir disoproxil fumarate/emtricitabine (TDF/FTC) for PrEP, with few HIV acquisitions in people receiving cabotegravir in both trials [3, 4]. Approved for every 6-months dosing by the FDA in June 2025 [5], long-acting injectable lenacapavir for PrEP (hereafter referred to as lenacapavir) demonstrated superior efficacy in reducing HIV acquisitions versus background HIV incidence (primary analysis) or daily oral TDF/FTC (secondary analysis) in the phase 3 PURPOSE 1 (cisgender women) and PURPOSE 2 RCTs (cisgender gay, bisexual, and other men; transgender women and men; and gender non-binary individuals who have sex with men). [6, 7]

There are currently no head-to-head trials assessing the efficacy of cabotegravir versus lenacapavir for PrEP. Although RCTs are considered the gold standard of clinical trial design, they are not always practical for comparing efficacy and safety of certain medications [8]. For instance, comparing cabotegravir and lenacapavir via a head-to-head trial could require a very large sample size and extended period of observation time due to the low incidence of HIV acquisitions associated with both regimens [3, 4, 6, 7]. To inform decision-making more rapidly, alternative methods can be considered, including an indirect treatment comparison (ITC), which allows a comparison of treatments indirectly by using a common comparator (e.g., a placebo or standard treatment). An ITC leverages existing clinical trial data to provide insights when direct comparisons are not practical.

Comparing cabotegravir with lenacapavir for PrEP by combining information from clinical trials that investigated the same PrEP options (evidence synthesis) [9] is an important part of the standard health technology assessment (HTA) process. Additionally, a transparent analysis based on a systematic literature review (SLR) is important to ensure comprehensive identification of all available evidence. Clinicians, policymakers, healthcare institutions, researchers, and communities can make timely and informed healthcare decisions by using evidence synthesis effectively. [10]

The primary objective of this analysis was to perform an ITC to provide an indirect estimate of the efficacy of cabotegravir compared with lenacapavir for PrEP in men who have sex with men, cisgender women, and gender-diverse individuals via the common comparators of no PrEP and TDF/FTC for PrEP. The primary outcome was risk of HIV acquisition for lenacapavir versus cabotegravir.

Methods

ITC Design

This ITC utilized data from separate trials in which each PrEP option was compared with a common third option. An anchored ITC with two network approaches (each using a common comparator) was used to compare cabotegravir versus lenacapavir.

In network approach 1 (Fig. 1A), we used no PrEP as the common comparator to anchor the comparison between cabotegravir versus lenacapavir. This was feasible because the PURPOSE trials included a counterfactual estimate of lenacapavir versus background HIV incidence (i.e., no PrEP) [6, 7]. Although the HPTN trials included a comparison of cabotegravir versus TDF/FTC and did not have a no PrEP estimate [3, 4], we leveraged previous research conducted by Hawkins et al. to compare cabotegravir versus no PrEP. [11]

Hawkins et al. lcompared cabotegravir with no PrEP using 8 RCTs that compared TDF/FTC versus placebo (i.e., no PrEP), thereby creating a connected network with TDF/FTC as a common comparator to indirectly compare cabotegravir with no PrEP (Fig. 1B) [11]. These 8 RCTs were identified through an SLR and reported varying levels of effectiveness, attributed to varying levels of adherence to TDF/FTC, which acts as a treatment-effect modifier. To account for this, a meta-regression of the relationship between levels of adherence to TDF/FTC (measured as the presence of detectable tenofovir in plasma) and the effectiveness of TDF/FTC was performed using data from these 8 RCTs. These estimates, together with the observed effectiveness of cabotegravir versus TDF/FTC from the HPTN trials, allowed for the estimation of cabotegravir effectiveness versus no PrEP in each trial population. The relative risk (RR) of cabotegravir for PrEP versus no PrEP was estimated as:

RRcabotegravir vs. no PrEP = RRcabotegravir vs. TDF/FTC × RRTDF/FTC vs. no PrEP,

where RRTDF/FTC vs. no PrEP is estimated at the level of TDF/FTC adherence observed in the HPTN trials.

In network approach 2 (Fig. 1C), we used oral TDF/FTC as the common comparator to anchor the comparison between cabotegravir and lenacapavir. This was feasible because the HPTN and PURPOSE trials included oral TDF/FTC as a direct comparator to cabotegravir or lenacapavir [3, 4, 6, 7]. However, differences in oral TDF/FTC adherence levels reported across the HPTN and PURPOSE trials are a potential confounder that may affect the relative efficacy between cabotegravir and lenacapavir [3, 4, 6, 7], as previous research shows that the efficacy of oral TDF/FTC observed in clinical trials varies due to differing levels of adherence [12]. For that reason, we leveraged the same methodology by Hawkins et al. for the comparison of cabotegravir versus no PrEP to account for differences in levels of adherence to TDF/FTC observed between PURPOSE and HPTN trials (i.e., trial-level adjustment). [11]

Adherence data reported for the PURPOSE trials used dried blood spot (DBS) assays [6, 7] rather than plasma samples (which were obtained in the 8 RCTs and employed for the meta-regression in Hawkins et al [11)]. The PURPOSE trials categorized adherence as low (< 2 tablets per week), medium (2–3 tablets per week), or high (≥ 4 tablets per week) on the basis of DBS data obtained at all trial visits [6, 7]. Per evidence available from the HPTN trials, which reported adherence based on both plasma and DBS samples (threshold of ≥ 2 doses per week), there is potential deviation between adherence measured using plasma and DBS samples, as adherence was 86% using plasma and 82% using DBS (≥ 2 doses per week) in HPTN 083, and 56% using plasma and 36% using DBS (≥ 2 doses per week) in HPTN 084 [3, 4]. In the absence of alternative data, we assumed for this ITC that ≥ 2 doses per week as measured by DBS in the PURPOSE trials would be equivalent to the level of detectable tenofovir in plasma for application of the meta-regression.

This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

Data Sources

An SLR (searches conducted January 2025) was conducted to identify the clinical trials in this ITC. MEDLINE, EMBASE, and Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials databases were searched, utilizing population, intervention, comparison, outcomes, and study criteria (Table S1; search strategies are available upon request). A supplementary search of key conferences was also conducted. Trial identification was conducted by two systematic reviewers. A PRISMA diagram detailing the SLR search process is presented in Figure S1. [13] Eligible trials of cabotegravir and lenacapavir for PrEP were included. A risk of bias assessment was conducted on the included studies using the tool recommended by the Centre for Reviews and Dissemination [14]. The available trials form a connected network, allowing an indirect comparison between cabotegravir and lenacapavir via the common comparator of no PrEP (Fig. 1A) or oral TDF/FTC (Fig. 1C).

The primary efficacy endpoint from the HPTN trials was originally calculated using data from both the oral lead-in and injection phases of cabotegravir [3, 4]. Given that oral lead-in use is optional per the summary of product characteristics [15] and is limited in clinical practice [16, 17], this ITC primarily focused on the injection phase results (secondary endpoint in HPTN trials). Extended retrospective virologic testing was performed after the primary analysis of data from the HPTN trials to better characterize the timing of HIV acquisitions [3, 4]. As a result, some cases were retrospectively determined to be baseline (pre-initiation of PrEP) HIV acquisitions rather than incident HIV acquisitions, and thus were re-adjudicated [3, 4]. In this ITC, we compared the post hoc re-adjudicated efficacy data from the HPTN trials with efficacy data from PURPOSE 1 and 2 (defined as all randomized participants who took ≥ 1 dose of any study drug and were not diagnosed with HIV on or before the first dose date).

Data Analysis

The statistical analysis framework is presented in Fig. 2. Base case analysis included the injection phase with re-adjudicated efficacy data from the HPTN trials and primary efficacy data from the PURPOSE trials. Sensitivity analyses included the oral lead-in and injection phases from the HPTN trials and primary efficacy data from the PURPOSE trials. The indirect comparison between lenacapavir and cabotegravir for base case and sensitivity analyses was conducted using the two network approaches described earlier (Fig. 2).

Statistical analysis framework. FTC emtricitabine, OLI oral lead-in, PrEP pre-exposure prophylaxis, TDF tenofovir disoproxil fumarate.

Bayesian methods were used to allow the meta-regression and indirect comparison to be combined into a single analysis, with the uncertainties in the individual trial estimates and meta-regression coefficients jointly represented in the final estimates of comparative efficacy. The indirect comparisons were implemented as Bayesian hierarchical models using the methods described in Hawkins et al., [18] with parameters estimated using Markov Chain Monte Carlo techniques as implemented in Just Another Gibbs Sampler [19]. Three chains were run starting from different initial values. Models were run for 50,000 iterations as a burn-in period, and a further 50,000 iterations were used for estimation. Convergence was judged sufficient by application of the "blue finger" test on trace plots and an R-statistic of < 1.05 [20]. Gelman–Rubin–Brooks plots were also examined [20]. The accuracy of estimation was judged sufficient if the Monte Carlo standard error divided by posterior standard deviation for all parameters was < 5%. Vague prior distributions were used.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was risk of HIV acquisition via the indirect estimates of the comparative efficacy of lenacapavir versus cabotegravir [expressed as hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% credible intervals (CrIs)]. Additionally, efficacy of cabotegravir versus no PrEP or TDF/FTC and efficacy of lenacapavir versus no PrEP or TDF/FTC were estimated (expressed as the percentage in HIV acquisition risk reduction; HRs and 95% CrIs for risk of HIV acquisition are also expressed).

Results

Identified Clinical Trials and Regimen Effect Estimates

Overall, the SLR identified 4 clinical trials for cabotegravir (n = 2) and lenacapavir (n = 2), which were included in the ITC [3, 4, 6, 7]. No concerns were raised regarding risk of bias per assessment of the SLR-included studies (n = 21) reporting data from the 4 trials (Figure S2). Details of the trials included in the ITC, such as the populations studied and location of trial sites, are presented in Table 1. Estimated HRs (95% CI) for HIV acquisition versus TDF/FTC for HPTN 083 were 0.15 (0.07, 0.35) when including only the injection phase (base case) and 0.26 (0.14, 0.49) when including the oral lead-in and injection phases (sensitivity analyses; Table 2). For HPTN 084, estimated HRs (95% CI) for HIV acquisition versus TDF/FTC were 0.01 (0.00, 0.09) for the injection phase and 0.04 (0.01, 0.12) for the oral lead-in and injection phases. Incidence rate ratio (95% CI) for HIV acquisition versus TDF/FTC was 0.00 (0.00, 0.10) in PURPOSE 1 and 0.11 (0.02, 0.51) in PURPOSE 2. In HPTN 083 and 084, TDF/FTC adherence was 86% and 56%, respectively. Estimated oral TDF/FTC adherence rates were 24% and 80% for PURPOSE 1 and PURPOSE 2, respectively.

Base Case Analysis (HPTN Injection Phase)

HIV Acquisition Risk for Lenacapavir Versus Cabotegravir

Predicted risk of HIV acquisition [HR (95% CrI)] for lenacapavir versus cabotegravir was 1.04 (0.19, 5.69) for men who have sex with men and transgender women (PURPOSE 2 also included cisgender gay, bisexual, and other men; transgender women and men; and non-binary individuals who have sex with men) and 0.00 (0.00, 3.21) for cisgender women when using no PrEP as a common comparator (network approach 1; Fig. 3A and Table 3). Results were comparable when using oral TDF/FTC as a common comparator, with all credible intervals crossing 1 [network approach 2, men who have sex with men and transgender women, 0.65 (0.11, 4.01); cisgender women, 0.00 (0.00, 10.07)]. As few HIV acquisitions occurred in the included trials [3, 4, 6, 7], the comparison of lenacapavir versus cabotegravir demonstrated wide 95% CrIs for HRs (all crossing 1), reflecting no evidence of superiority for either regimen.

Predicted Efficacy of Cabotegravir and Lenacapavir Versus No PrEP or TDF/FTC

Based on the network approach 1, the predicted efficacy (95% CrI) of reduction in risk of HIV acquisition versus no PrEP in men who have sex with men and transgender women was 96% (90%, 98%) for cabotegravir in HPTN 083 and 96% (82%, 99%) for lenacapavir in PURPOSE 2 (Table 3) [6]; the predicted efficacy in cisgender women was 98% (89%, 100%) for cabotegravir in HPTN 084 and 100% (96%, 100%) for lenacapavir in PURPOSE 1.

Results were comparable when using oral TDF/FTC as a common comparator (network approach 2). The predicted efficacy of reduction in risk of HIV acquisition versus TDF/FTC in men who have sex with men and transgender women was 85% (65%, 93%) for cabotegravir in HPTN 083 and 90% (49%, 98%) for lenacapavir in PURPOSE 2 (Table 3); the predicted efficacy in cisgender women was 99% (91%, 100%) for cabotegravir in HPTN 084 and 100% (90%, 100%) for lenacapavir in PURPOSE 1.

In the base case analysis, HRs for risk of HIV acquisition versus no PrEP or TDF/FTC between similar populations in the HPTN and PURPOSE trials were low and comparable (Fig. 3B; Table 3). Sensitivity analyses (HPTN oral lead-in and injection phases) further confirmed these findings (Figure S3; Table S2).

Discussion

This ITC estimates that both cabotegravir for PrEP and lenacapavir for PrEP offer similar and high efficacy in reducing HIV acquisitions across diverse populations, with no evidence of differences between the long-acting PrEP options. When using no PrEP or TDF/FTC as common comparators the risk of HIV acquisition [HR (95% CrI)] for lenacapavir versus cabotegravir was, respectively, 1.04 (0.19, 5.69) and 0.65 (0.11, 4.01) in men who have sex with men and transgender women and 0.00 (0.00, 3.21) and 0.00 (0.00, 10.07) in cisgender women in the base case analysis, with comparable results in sensitivity analyses. We observed wide CrIs in this ITC of lenacapavir versus cabotegravir, reflecting the few HIV acquisitions reported in the included trials, as expected with these highly efficacious PrEP options. In the base case and sensitivity analyses, no evidence of superiority for either regimen was observed.

The base case analysis, which best reflects clinical practice, suggests that the predicted efficacy of cabotegravir versus no PrEP (network approach 1) is high across populations of men who have sex with men, transgender women, and cisgender women (HPTN 083, 96%; HPTN 084, 98%), achieving similar efficacy to that observed in the PURPOSE trials in similar populations (PURPOSE 2, 96%; PURPOSE 1, 100%).

Because oral lead-in with cabotegravir was as an option in the HPTN trials, is not required per the summary of product characteristics [15], and is limited in real-world settings [16, 17], the base case results are the most applicable to clinical practice. Still, the sensitivity analyses including both oral lead-in and injection phase HPTN efficacy data confirm the results from the base case analysis.

This ITC provides valuable insights, especially in the absence of a direct comparison of cabotegravir versus lenacapavir. The results of this ITC can be used in conjunction with clinical trial data and available real-world evidence to support or inform decision-makers on the relative clinical value of long-acting PrEP. Additionally, this ITC may assist providers and people who could benefit from PrEP when choosing a PrEP option. It is also possible that cabotegravir and lenacapavir comparisons will be required for future HTA appraisals and re-appraisals of PrEP options.

Notably, the demonstration of comparable efficacies between cabotegravir and lenacapavir in this study relies on an ITC using data from randomized controlled trials. In practice, outcomes are contingent on the conditions under which PrEP is delivered and used [21]. Considering other factors, including injection site reactions, drug–drug interactions, drug resistance profiles, real-world data, dosing frequency, preferences, drug cost and accessibility, and lenacapavir or cabotegravir open-label extension data, would contribute to a comprehensive clinical value assessment. Additionally, these factors may influence the experience with and adherence of individuals who use PrEP, as well as their persistence on a specific regimen, which can translate into impacts on the real-world efficacy of cabotegravir or lenacapavir for PrEP [22]. A discussion of these factors in addition to the results presented by this ITC is necessary for comprehensive decision-making by people who could benefit from PrEP and their providers when choosing between these 2 long-acting injectable PrEP regimens.

Real-world effectiveness of cabotegravir and lenacapavir depends on implementation [23, 24]. Beyond demonstrated efficacy in clinical trials, factors such as cost [25], dosing frequency, and accessibility can meaningfully shape outcomes, particularly for underserved populations [26]. For example, regular clinic visits for injections can be barriers for people, especially those with work constraints [25], unstable housing, or limited access to transport [27]. However, it can also serve as a relevant clinic-based option for people who prefer or require greater clinic engagement through regular visits [26]. Medication costs can also be a barrier to long-acting injectable PrEP implementation, and people may rely on insurance coverage and programs that vary across settings [25]. Implementation gaps can reduce real-world effectiveness even when clinical efficacy is high [21]. Due to the novelty of long-acting injectable PrEP regimens, there is not yet comparative effectiveness data from the real-world setting to support validation of this ITC.

This is the first ITC study to compare the efficacy of cabotegravir and lenacapavir; however, there are limitations. Few studies are available, limiting the ability to identify and adjust for potential confounding covariables by meta-regression. Heterogeneity regarding populations, geographical locations, and background HIV incidence across the studies prevents adjustment for these variables. There are other existing ITC approaches using individual participant-level data to apply population-adjustment approaches (e.g., matching adjusted indirect comparisons, simulated treatment comparisons). For this ITC, the lack of access to individual participant-level data prevented the use of population-adjustment approaches. Oral TDF/FTC adherence levels reported in the different trials were potential confounders of the efficacy estimate; as such, external data were employed to model the relationship between adherence and effectiveness for oral TDF/FTC (Figure S4) [11]. Oral TDF/FTC adherence data reported for the PURPOSE trials used DBS assays rather than plasma samples [6, 7]. For this ITC, we assumed that ≥ 2 doses per week as measured by DBS in the PURPOSE trials would be equivalent to the level of detectable tenofovir for application of the meta-regression. In the HPTN trials, which reported TDF/FTC adherence based on both plasma and DBS samples, adherence using plasma was similar to that using DBS (≥ 2 doses per week) in HPTN 083, but there was some deviation between these measures in HPTN 084. Though network approach 2 in this ITC requires the assumption that DBS in the PURPOSE trials is comparable with detectable plasma tenofovir in the HPTN trials for the PURPOSE trials to use the meta-regression, network approach 1 directly uses the reported no-PrEP counterfactuals from the PURPOSE trials and does not rely on this assumption. We found the overall conclusions to be comparable between these methods.

Although adherence is important for interpreting efficacy, adherence measures used in the HPTN (plasma tenofovir) and PURPOSE (DBS) trials differed, and adherence data were only available for a subset of participants from the HPTN and PURPOSE trials, limiting the applicability of population-adjustment approaches. Similarly, the rarity of resistance events against cabotegravir and lenacapavir prevented analysis in this ITC. In the future, long-term resistance monitoring in diverse cohorts can help to address this evidence gap. A critical consideration in the long-term effectiveness and public health impact of PrEP is the potential for HIV drug resistance, particularly in situations of suboptimal adherence. As new PrEP options become available, it is important to understand how “forgiving” the effectiveness of each regimen is in situations of low adherence, and how that relates to resistance. Therefore, strong adherence counseling and ongoing support remain key parts of all PrEP programs. Another limitation is that this ITC did not compare adverse drug reactions between cabotegravir and lenacapavir. Nevertheless, injection site reactions, which are among the most frequently reported adverse events with long-acting injectable PrEP regimens [3, 4, 6, 7], with cabotegravir versus lenacapavir are being investigated in the ongoing CLARITY randomized crossover study [28].

These ITC results may also be impacted by differing dosing frequencies and variation in trial duration. Cabotegravir was dosed as a single intramuscular gluteal injection every 8 weeks [3, 4], whereas lenacapavir was dosed subcutaneously as 2 injections in the abdomen every 26 weeks [6, 7]. The median follow-up at time of publication was shorter in the PURPOSE trials (PURPOSE 1, 44.0 weeks; PURPOSE 2, 39.4 weeks) [29] compared with the HPTN trials (HPTN 083, 72.8 weeks; HPTN 084, 64.5 weeks) [3, 4]. Thus, efficacy results may change as new data emerge from the open-label extension phases of the included trials [6, 7]. Notably, three additional incident HIV acquisitions occurred in participants receiving lenacapavir post the PURPOSE trials primary analyses [5], which were not included in the trial regimen effect estimates used for this ITC.

The feasibility of an ITC per subgroup was limited by partial availability of subgroup data in the RCTs. The HPTN 083 trial reported similar efficacy of cabotegravir across subgroups of age, race, geographic region, and transgender women versus men who have sex with men [4]. However, comparable subgroup analysis data were not available for lenacapavir. Indirect treatment comparisons provide additional evidence to complement RCTs and are preferred to naive comparisons because they utilize appropriate methods for adjustment where necessary and feasible [30]. This ITC serves as a clinical reference in the absence of a head-to-head comparison between cabotegravir and lenacapavir. However, the ITC should not be considered as substitutive of a head-to-head RCTs.

Conclusion

This ITC suggests that cabotegravir and lenacapavir both offer similar and high efficacy in HIV acquisition risk reduction compared with no PrEP and oral TDF/FTC. Notably, the comparison of lenacapavir versus cabotegravir reflected no evidence of superiority for either regimen, reinforcing their roles as effective agents for HIV prevention. Furthermore, long-acting PrEP offers a highly effective and convenient option for people who may have challenges with taking a daily pill. Additional factors associated with these regimens (e.g., injection site reactions, drug–drug interactions, resistance profiles, cost and accessibility, dosing regimens, longer term data from open-label extension trial phases, real-world data, preferences) are important to comprehensively assess clinical value, assist with clinical decision-making, and support tailored approaches for people who may benefit from PrEP. This ITC contributes to the growing body of evidence supporting the diversification of effective HIV prevention strategies and underscores the progress in addressing global End HIV Epidemic goals to improve outcomes for people who may benefit from PrEP.
