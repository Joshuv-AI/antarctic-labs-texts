PERRY: Policy Evaluation with Confidence Intervals using Auxiliary Data
Aishwarya Mandyam*, Jason Meng*, Ge Gao, Jiankai Sun, Mac Schwager, Barbara E. Engelhardt, Emma Brunskill

Abstract
Off-policy evaluation (OPE) methods aim to estimate the value of a new reinforcement learning (RL) policy prior to deployment. Recent advances have shown that leveraging auxiliary datasets, such as those synthesized by generative models, can improve the accuracy of these value estimates. Unfortunately, such auxiliary datasets may also be biased, and existing methods for using data augmentation for OPE in RL lack principled uncertainty quantification. In high stakes settings like healthcare, reliable uncertainty estimates are important for comparing policy value estimates. In this work, we propose two approaches to construct valid confidence intervals for OPE when using data augmentation. The first provides a confidence interval over the policy performance conditioned on a particular initial state V π ​ ( s 0 ) V^{\pi}(s_{0}) – such intervals are particularly important for human-centered applications. To do so we introduce a new conformal prediction method for high dimensional state MDPs. Second, we consider the more common task of estimating the average policy performance over many initial states; to do so we draw on ideas from doubly robust estimation and prediction powered inference. Across simulators spanning robotics, healthcare and inventory management, and a real healthcare dataset from MIMIC-IV, we find that our methods can use augmented data and still consistently produce intervals that cover the ground truth values, unlike previously proposed methods.



1
Introduction

Off-policy evaluation (OPE) is used to estimate the value of a new reinforcement learning (RL) policy prior to deployment using historical dataset from a different behavior policy. This strategy is especially important in high-stakes domains where directly deploying new policies can be costly or even harmful to participants. OPE has been used in a variety of domains including healthcare
(
Gottesman et al. 2020
)
, education
(
Mandel et al. 2014
)
, and robotics
(
Fu et al. 2020
)
. Several methods have been proposed to perform OPE include importance sampling (IS)
(
Precup et al. 2000
)
, direct method (DM)
(
Li et al. 2010
;
Beygelzimer and Langford 2009
;
van Seijen et al. 2009
;
Harutyunyan et al. 2016
;
Le et al. 2019
;
Voloshin et al. 2021
)
, and doubly robust (DR)
(
Farajtabar et al. 2018
;
Dudik et al. 2011
;
Jiang and Li 2016
)
approaches. However, standard OPE methods generally suffer from dataset coverage issues. For example, if the target policy takes actions that are underrepresented in the behavior dataset, OPE estimates can be erroneous. To address this, several recent works have proposed using augmented data to improve the coverage of the behavior dataset and subsequently the accuracy of OPE methods
(
Tang and Wiens 2023
;
Gao et al. 2024
;
Mandyam et al. 2024
)
. Despite these advances, OPE estimates may still vary across different estimators
(
Nie et al. 2024
)
, making it difficult for practitioners to obtain robust estimates of the policy value.

One strategy to enable more meaningful comparisons between OPE methods is to provide confidence intervals for individual OPE estimates. Confidence intervals communicate a degree of uncertainty, which makes it possible to assess whether differences in OPE estimates for a given target policy are statistically significant. Prior approaches have derived asymptotic
(
Thomas et al. 2015a
;
Thomas et al. 2015b
)
and sample-dependent confidence intervals
(
Taufiq et al. 2022
;
Foffano et al. 2023
)
. However, no prior works provide valid confidence intervals for a setting in which uses both offline trajectories and a set of augmented trajectories.

In this work, we introduce two strategies to generate valid confidence intervals for OPE while leveraging augmented datasets
. First, in several high-stakes domains it is useful to have confidence intervals for policy values when conditioned on an initial state. For example, in a clinical domain, it is important to have confidence intervals for the value of a new treatment policy for all patients that are at similar stage of a disease. We propose our first estimator, CP-Gen, to address this need by calculating conformal prediction intervals for the value of a policy conditioned on a starting state. However, approximating the full value of the target policy via a union bound over state-condition policy values can lead to impractically wide confidence intervals. We propose a second estimator, DR-PPI, for estimating unconditional policy values. DR-PPI leverages doubly robust estimation and prediction-powered inference
(
Angelopoulos et al. 2023
)
to generate valid confidence intervals for the value of the target policy by correcting for erroneous generated trajectories. Our results demonstrate that both proposed approaches are able to generate valid confidence intervals that are empirically superior to baseline approaches. Our work makes the following contributions:

1.

We introduce two methods to calculate confidence intervals for OPE
when using a dataset that contains augmented data (
Section

3
).

2.

We prove that both methods yield valid confidence intervals
that achieve the desired coverage probability for the true policy value either asymptotically or within a margin of error for finite sample sizes (
Section

4
).

3.

We empirically evaluate the estimators
in four domains including inventory control, sepsis, robotic control, and MIMIC-IV, showing that our methods leverage augmented data to often produce tighter confidence intervals with the correct coverage. In contrast, prior approaches either do not use augmented data and have valid but often wide intervals, or do use augmented data but fail to provide valid coverage (
Section

5
).

2
Background

2.1
Problem setting

We consider a decision-making setting defined by the Markov decision process (MDP)
\mathcal{M}=(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},P,R,d_{0},\gamma,H)
.
\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A}
denote the possibly infinite state and action spaces respectively.
P:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\Delta(\mathcal{S})
represents the transition dynamics,
R:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\Delta(\mathbb{R})
is the reward function, and
d_{0}\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})
is the initial state distribution.
\gamma
is the discount factor and
H
is the fixed horizon. A trajectory is defined as
\tau:\{s_{t},a_{t},r_{t}\}_{t=1}^{H}
where
s_{t},a_{t},r_{t}
are the corresponding state, action, and instantaneous reward observed at timestep
t
. The return of the trajectory
\tau
is
J(\tau)=\sum_{t=1}^{H}\gamma^{t-1}r_{t}
where
\pi
is the policy used to generate the trajectory. The value of the policy
V^{\pi}=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi}[J(\tau)]
is calculated as an expectation over the possible trajectories that could arise from the policy
\pi
.

2.2
Off-policy evaluation

The goal of off-policy evaluation (OPE) is to estimate the value of a target policy
\pi_{e}
given a dataset of behavior trajectories
D_{\pi_{b}}
that arise from a distinct behavior policy
\pi_{b}
. There are several standard approaches for OPE, including IS
(
Precup et al. 2000
)
, direct method (DM)
(
Li et al. 2010
;
Beygelzimer and Langford 2009
;
van Seijen et al. 2009
;
Harutyunyan et al. 2016
;
Le et al. 2019
;
Voloshin et al. 2021
)
, and doubly robust approaches (DR)
(
Farajtabar et al. 2018
;
Dudik et al. 2011
;
Jiang and Li 2016
)
. IS-based estimators re-weigh each trajectory in the behavior dataset using an inverse propensity score (IPS)
\rho(\tau)=\prod_{t=1}^{H}\frac{\pi_{e}(s_{t},a_{t})}{\pi_{b}(s_{t},a_{t})}
. DM estimators learn a reward model using the behavior trajectories and use either Monte Carlo sampling or fit a reward model to calculate the value of the target policy. DR methods combine the advantages of IS and DM estimators and provide favorable guarantees when either the IPS ratio or the reward model is inaccurate. Similar to most prior work, we assume that
\pi_{b}
and
\pi_{e}
are known
(
Thomas and Brunskill 2016
;
Farajtabar et al. 2018
)
.

2.3
Off-policy evaluation with data augmentation

As discussed in
Section

1
, standard OPE methods suffer when the behavior dataset has limited coverage. Because OPE typically focuses on the finite sample setting, and with a limited behavior dataset, OPE estimates can be either biased or have high variance
(
Precup et al. 2000
;
Jiang and Li 2016
;
Thomas and Brunskill 2016
)
. To address this concern, several works have proposed using auxiliary information to enhance OPE estimators, using data augmentation either from a secondary dataset
(
Tang and Wiens 2023
;
Mandyam et al. 2024
)
or generating synthetic trajectories based on historical data
(
Gao et al. 2024
;
Sun et al. 2023
;
Gao et al. 2023
)
. These works find that leveraging augmented data substantially improves OPE estimators in some domains such as robotic control. However, these approaches may introduce additional bias due to errors in the augmented data, and lack theoretical guarantees or analysis.

2.4
Conformal prediction for off-policy evaluation

Conformal prediction is a strategy to produce statistically valid prediction regions for any point prediction that arises from a machine learning model.

Tibshirani et al. 2020
relaxed a limiting exchangeability assumption, and
Taufiq et al. 2022
extended conformal prediction to the OPE setting for contextual bandits through reweighting.

Foffano et al. 2023
later extended this work to create conformal intervals for OPE in MDPs. Crucially, their approximation relies on an integral that is difficult to compute, and it is unclear how to use their method for continuous high-dimensional state spaces. Our CP-Gen, is inspired by the last approach, but uses a new way to compute the critical weights needed for the conformal intervals, allowing us to tackle settings with continuous and high-dimensional state and action spaces. In addition, this prior work did not consider data augmentation. Our new approach achieves tighter confidence intervals
than earlier work through a careful use of augmented datasets.

2.5
Prediction-powered inference

There are several challenges when applying ML methods to settings in which data is expensive to obtain. In these settings, it is useful to use ML methods to generate predictions for unlabeled samples. Prediction-powered inference (PPI) allows us to calculate confidence intervals on downstream task performance given both an original dataset and predictions from an ML model
(
Angelopoulos et al. 2023
)
. PPI has been shown to produce accurate confidence intervals across a variety of ML tasks and dataset domains. PPI also has strong overlap with doubly robust estimation techniques in our setting. However, the problem setup in PPI is distinct from ours. PPI assumes that we have access to a large dataset of observations that are unlabeled; the role of the ML model is to label the observations. In contrast, in our setting, we assume we have a finite dataset of observations (i.e., trajectories) and labels (i.e., returns), and that we must use a generative model to produce additional observations.

3
Methods

As discussed in
Section

2
, prior work has explored various strategies for sourcing auxiliary datasets for OPE—most notably through generative models—and has shown that augmenting datasets in this manner can substantially improve estimator performance. However, trajectories produced by generative models may be biased or drawn from a distribution different from that of the offline behavior policy. Consequently, any OPE method that leverages these augmented datasets must carefully account for this potential distributional shift. To the best of our knowledge, there remains an unmet need to calculate confidence intervals for a setting in which we have both offline and generated data. To address this open problem, we propose two estimators.

3.1
Confidence Intervals for OPE Value Estimation from a Particular State: CP-Gen

In settings like healthcare, it is common for stakeholders to want to select a decision policy to maximize the benefits of particular individuals: for example, a doctor may recommend a particular treatment policy for a patient, or a teacher may design a customer strategy for a student. In these cases the decisions may be made from an initial starting state, and stakeholders may wish to base their decision on both a point estimate of the expected return, and confidence intervals over that potential expectation. This setting has been slightly understudied in the OPE RL literature, which tends to focus on averaging performance over the full population of initial states. However, we believe that estimating confidence intervals for value performance from a particular initial state represents an important task for OPE RL. We expect in such settings that data augmentation may be particularly important, since data from individual starting states is sparse, and yet obtaining valid confidence intervals is of particular importance.

To address this, we propose CP-Gen, an conformal prediction method for OPE estimation for initial-state dependent policy value estimates.
Given an initial state
s_{0}
, we estimate
V^{\pi_{e}}(s_{0})
as follows:

\displaystyle V^{\pi_{e}}(s_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\tau\sim p^{\pi_{e}}|s_{0}=s}p^{\pi_{e}}(\tau)R(\tau)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\hat{\tau}\sim\tilde{p}^{\pi_{e}}|s_{0}=s}\tilde{p}^{\pi_{e}}(\hat{\tau})R(\hat{\tau})+\left[\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\tau\sim p^{\pi_{e}}|s_{0}=s\end{subarray}}p^{\pi_{e}}(\tau)R(\tau)-\sum_{\hat{\tau}\sim\tilde{p}^{\pi_{e}}|s_{0}=s}\tilde{p}^{\pi_{e}}(\hat{\tau})R(\hat{\tau})\right]

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\sum_{\hat{\tau}\sim\tilde{p}^{\pi_{e}}|s_{0}=s}\tilde{p}^{\pi_{e}}(\hat{\tau})R(\hat{\tau})+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}R(\tau_{i}|s_{0}=s)-\frac{1}{nM}\sum_{j=1}^{nM}R(\hat{\tau}_{j}|s_{0}=s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\hat{\tau}\sim\tilde{p}^{\pi_{e}}|s_{0}=s}\tilde{p}^{\pi_{e}}(\hat{\tau})R(\hat{\tau})+\frac{1}{nM}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{M}(R(\tau_{i}|s_{0}=s)-R(\hat{\tau}_{ij}|s_{0}=s)),

where
\tilde{p}
is the dynamics distribution induced by the generative model,
n/M
is the number of behavior/synthetic trajectories, and
R(\tau|s_{0}=s)
is the return of trajectory
\tau
given initial state
s
.

Inspired by conformal prediction for regression, our goal is to produce an interval
\hat{C}_{n}(s)

such that the return difference between any offline trajectory and its corresponding generated trajectory that starts from the same initial state
s_{0}
lies in this band with high probability i.e.,

P^{\pi_{e}}\left(R(\tau|s_{0}=s)-R(\hat{\tau}|s_{0}=s)\in\hat{C}_{n}(s)\right)\geq\alpha,

(1)

where
P^{\pi_{e}}
is the probability measure induced by the target policy
\pi_{e}
and
\alpha
is the confidence level.
Given this goal, the final conformal prediction interval for the value of the initial state
s
,
V^{\pi_{e}}(s)
, is

\sum_{\tilde{\tau}\sim\tilde{p}^{\pi_{e}},s_{0}=s}\tilde{p}^{\pi_{e}}(\hat{\tau})R(\hat{\tau})+\hat{C}_{n}(s).

(2)

In this way, if we set
\alpha
appropriately (i.e., depending on the reward distribution), the band will cover the expected value of
R(\tau_{i}|s_{0}=s)-R(\hat{\tau}_{ij}|s_{0}=s)
. This enables the final interval to cover
V^{\pi_{e}}(s)
.

However, in some settings, we still want an estimated value of the target policy aggregated across all states. This is not possible with the CP-Gen framework, as this would rely on a union bound that would make the confidence interval estimates impractically wide. To address this limitation, we introduce a second estimator, DR-PPI, which builds on prior literature in doubly robust estimation and prediction-powered inference. Here, our goal is to construct an estimator of
V^{\pi_{e}}=E_{s_{0}}[V^{\pi_{e}}(s_{0})]
with which we can calculate confidence intervals. First, we assume that the initial-state distribution
d_{0}
is known (though our results extend to settings in which
d_{0}
must be estimated). Now, we construct a cross-fitted, doubly-robust estimate of the policy value
V^{\pi_{e}}
as follows. First, we split the behavior dataset
D_{\pi_{b}}
into two equal parts, which we refer to as
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
. We first use
D_{1}
to fit a generative model
f_{1}

f_{1}
to generate

\pi_{e}

\widehat{V}_{\text{DR-PPI}:1}^{\pi_{e}}=\frac{1}{N_{f}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{f}}R(\hat{\tau}_{i})\;+\;\frac{1}{n/2}\sum_{j\in D_{2}}\Bigl(\tilde{R}(\tau_{j})\;-\;\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}R(\hat{\tau}_{m,j}\mid s_{0,j})\Bigr),

from the target policy. To ensure that the data is used efficiently, we use cross-fitting

\widehat{V}_{\text{DR-PPI}:1}

3.3
Practical considerations

There are several practical considerations to enable OPE in environments with large state and action spaces as well as settings in which
\pi_{b}
and
\pi_{e}
differ substantially. First, it is occasionally necessary to clip the largest IPS ratios to avoid extremely large intervals.
Ionides 2008
shows that using a clip constant set to
n^{1/2}
where
n
is the number of dataset samples, provides an optimal first order rate in the resulting mean-squared error of the OPE estimator, balancing the bias introduced by the clipping with the variance reduction. This clipping constant also ensures the resulting estimate is still consistent. Following this, we set the clipping constant at a rate of
n^{1/2}
.

Additionally, in
Algorithm

2
, we propose a procedure that involves splitting the behavior dataset into two portions and aggregating the OPE estimate calculated using each portion. In practice, it may not possible to split the behavior dataset due to its size. For these settings, we choose not to perform cross-fitting, and instead report results without sub-dividing the dataset. As discussed in
Section

5
, this can still result in valid, but higher variance confidence intervals.

4
Theoretical Results

Now, we discuss the theoretical guarantees of our proposed approaches.
As is standard in prior OPE literature, we assume that the target and behavior policies share common support, and that the instantaneous rewards and IPS ratios are bounded
(
Farajtabar et al. 2018
;
Thomas and Brunskill 2016
)

Assumption 1

(Common support)
.

\pi_{e}(a|s)>0\rightarrow\pi_{b}(a|s)>0,\forall s\in\mathcal{S},\forall a\in\mathcal{A}
.

Assumption 2

(Bounded reward)

0\leq R(\tau)\leq C_{r}
for all
\tau\sim p
.

Assumption 3

(Bounded IPS weights)
.

c_{ips}\leq\frac{\pi_{e}(a|s)}{\pi_{b}(a|s)}\leq C_{ips}
,
\forall s\in\mathcal{S},\forall a\in\mathcal{A}
.

4.1
CP-Gen produces valid conformal prediction intervals

We make a few additional assumptions to analyze CP-Gen.
4

In
5

5
Empirical Results

Our theoretical results demonstrated that CP-Gen and DR-PPI can calculate valid confidence intervals under mild assumptions. To complement this analysis, we seek to answer the following questions using empirical results:
1)
Does the
\epsilon
-approximation used in CP-Gen cause the estimated interval to be biased?
2)
Do DR-PPI and CP-Gen produce intervals that cover the ground truth policy value?
3)
When do the DR-PPI estimates outperform baselines?

5.1
Datasets

To answer our empirical questions, we use the following domains.

Inventory Control

(
Foffano et al. 2023
)
: We adapt this simulator to accommodate a continuous state and reward space. The state is the current inventory level, actions are the number of units purchased, and the reward is the end-of-day earnings. We approximate the dynamics using a feed-forward network.

Sepsis

(
Oberst and Sontag 2019
)
: In this popular sepsis simulator, patient state is represented by an 8-dimensional vector containing information about relevant vitals and any types of ongoing treatment. There are 8 possible actions, with each action corresponding to a different combination of 3 binary treatments. The reward is a function of whether the patient is on treatment and whether their vitals are stable. The maximum horizon is
H=20
. We approximate the dynamics using a feed-forward network.

D4RL HalfCheetah

(
Fu et al. 2020
)
: The HalfCheetah environment is a Mujoco task in the D4RL suite
(
Fu et al. 2020
)
. Each state is represented as a 17-dimensional vector that contains information about the position and velocity of each of the cheetah’s joints. Each action applies torque to a subset of the joints, and the goal of the environment is to get the cheetah to move forward as quickly as possible. The reward corresponds to how far the cheetah traveled. We approximate the dynamics in the HalfCheetah environment using a variational auto-encoder (VAE)
(
Gao et al. 2023
)
.

MIMIC-IV
(
Johnson et al. 2020
;
Goldberger et al. 2000
)
: We consider a subset of patients from the MIMIC-IV dataset that receive potassium repletion. The patient state is a 20-dimensional vector containing information about labs, administered medicines, and static covariates; each state represents a 4-hour interval in a patient’s hospital stay. There are five actions, each corresponding to a dosage of potassium. The reward function is a binary indicator of whether the patient’s potassium lab value is within the reference range post-repletion. To emulate a setting in which we have access to both a behavior and target cohort, we construct two sub-cohorts from our patient population. The first consists of patients who receive lower dosages (<20 mEq/L), and the second consists of patients who receive higher dosages (>= 20mEq/L). The former is the behavior dataset and the latter is the target dataset; our goal is to learn the value of the target policy (i.e., repletion strategy in the higher-dosage cohort).

5.2

Importance Sampling (IS)

(
Precup et al. 2000
)
: We use standard IS, deriving a bound using CLT or bootstrapping.

Augmented Importance Sampling (AugIS)
: We use both the original dataset and a set of synthetic trajectories to calculate an IS estimate, with bounds estimated using either CLT or boostrapping.

Direct Method (DM)
(
Li et al. 2010
;
Beygelzimer and Langford 2009
;
van Seijen et al. 2009
;
Harutyunyan et al. 2016
;
Le et al. 2019
;
Voloshin et al. 2021
)
: We use rollouts from the learned model and calculate the expectation of the trajectory returns.

Doubly Robust (DR)
(
Jiang and Li 2016
)
: Here, we compute a DR estimated using DQL to learn the reward model.

Augmented Doubly Robust (AugDR)
: Here, we use both offline trajectories and synthetic trajectories to learn the DQL reward model and then compute a DR estimate.

5.3
Results

CP-Gen produces valid CP intervals
. As discussed in
Section

3
, to scale prior conformal prediction approaches to MDPs with large state and action spaces, we use an
\epsilon
-approximation strategy. Despite this approximation, we find that CP-Gen still results in conformal prediction intervals that covers the true value
V^{\pi_{e}}(s)
with the specified confidence level, often with a small interval size (
Table

1
). We compare to a DM-style baseline where we average the return of synthetic trajectories that start in the given initial state. We find that the DM-style baseline can produce a biased result with a poor generative model (e.g., in D4RL, MIMIC-IV). We also evaluate the baseline reported in
Foffano et al. 2023
using a discretized version of the inventory control MDP (See Appendix for details). Their baseline covers the ground truth value but produces 25.69% wider intervals than our approach. Note that it is not possible to apply
Foffano et al. 2023
in settings where the state and reward are continuous.These results suggest our CP-Gen and its
\epsilon
-approximation newly enable conformal prediction for OPE in MDP settings with large state and action spaces.

DR-PPI identifies valid confidence intervals that cover
v(\pi_{e})
across all domains
. Across all domains, DR-PPI produces valid confidence intervals that cover
v(\pi_{e})
, as our theoretical results suggest (
Figure

1
, inventory control results in
Section

7
). In contrast, most baseline approaches either have wide confidence intervals or have intervals that do not cover
v(\pi_{e})
. In fact, any baseline approach that uses generated trajectories (e.g., AugIS, AugDR) produces a biased interval, which suggests that naively incorporating auxiliary datasets results in a biased estimator. Furthermore, we find that in the D4RL and MIMIC-IV settings, DQL is unable to identify an accurate Q-learning function; as a result, the confidence intervals become exponentially large.

DR-PPI performs best in stochastic domains with high quality generative models
.When the environment is deterministic (e.g., D4RL HalfCheetah), or settings where the model is of poor quality (e.g., D4RL HalfCheetah, MIMIC-IV) DR-PPI performs similarly to the IS baselines. In such settings there may be little variance reduction from computing differences between the return of observed trajectories and generated ones. In contrast, in settings where the environment is stochastic and our learned generative model is reasonably accurate, DR-PPI has tighter CIs.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we propose two estimators—CP-Gen and DR-PPI—for constructing valid confidence intervals for OPE in MDP settings using auxiliary data generated from a learned model. We first establish theoretical guarantees for both estimators in
Section

4
, and then empirically validate them across four domains, including a real-world electronic health records dataset (
Section

5
). Our results demonstrate that these estimators consistently yield valid confidence intervals across diverse settings.

Limitations and future work.

In this work, we use one specific choice of generative model. Future research could explore alternative approaches, such as diffusion models. Additionally, CP-Gen assumes that
\pi_{b}
and
\pi_{e}
are Lipschitz continuous, which may restrict its use in certain settings. Finally, it is valuable to investigate ways to mitigate the impact of poor-quality generate trajectories on the resulting intervals.

Empirical Settings

In the main text, we consider empirical results using four datasets. Here, we expand the description of each dataset.

Inventory Control
:

The inventory control simulator is adapted from a version featured in
Foffano et al. 2023
. As described in the main text, the state represents the current inventory level, an action represents the number of units purchased, and the reward is the earnings at the end of a given day. We make several adaptations to make this inventory control environment more suitable for our work. First, the distribution of the stochastic demand in the inventory is
o
is changed from Poisson to normal
\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma)
. Additionally, the cost of buying items is
k\times\mathbb{1}_{\{a>0\}}+c(\min(N,x_{t}+a)-x_{t})
, where
k>0
is the fixed cost for a single order,
c>0
is the cost of a single unit bought,
N
is the inventory upper-bound,
x_{t}
is the state at timestep
t
, and
a_{t}
is the action at timestep
t
. The next state
x_{t+1}
is calculated as
x_{t+1}=\max(0,\min(N,x_{t}+a_{t})-o_{t+1})
. The instantaneous reward observed at the end of the day is the sum of the costs and earnings listed above (e.g.,
r(x_{t},a_{t},x_{t+1})=10^{2}\times(-k1_{\{a_{t}>0\}}-zx_{t}-c(\min(N,x_{t}+a_{t})-x_{t})+p\max(0,\min(N,x_{t}+a_{t})-x_{t+1}))
).
When testing our algorithm, we chose the following parameters:
N=10,k=1,c=2,z=2,p=4,\mu=5,\sigma=1,H=20
.

Sepsis
:

The Sepsis simulator is taken directly from
Oberst and Sontag 2019
, which models a synthetic Sepsis treatment setting. The state is an 8-dimensional vector which contains information about relevant vitals and labs, indicators of ongoing treatment (e.g., antibiotics, vasopressors, ventilator), and an indication of whether the patient is diabetic. There are 8 possible actions, each corresponding to a different combination of 3 binary treatments (e.g., antibiotics, vasopressors, ventilator). The reward is
+1
if the synthetic patient is off of treatment and has stable vitals,
-1
if the patient has unstable vitals, and
+0
otherwise. We do not alter any environment details, and report results with a maximum horizon of
H=20
.

D4RL HalfCheetah
:

The HalfCheetah environment is a Mujoco task in the D4RL suite
(
Fu et al. 2020
)
. The cheetah is a two-dimensional robot that has 9 body parts and 8 joints connecting the body parts. Each state is represented as a 17-dimensional vector that contains information about the position and velocity of each of the joints. Each action is represented as a 5-dimensional vector, and applies torque to a subset of the joints, and the goal of the environment is to get the cheetah to move forward as quickly as possible. The reward corresponds to how far the cheetah traveled, with negative reward indicating that the cheetah moved backward. We report results using a maximum horizon of
H=1000
.

MIMIC-IV
:

MIMIC-IV is an electronic health records dataset collected from patients admitted to the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center in Boston, MA
(
Johnson et al. 2020
;
Goldberger et al. 2000
)
. We consider a subset of patients that receive potassium repletion through an intravenous (IV) line. We represent the patient state as a 20-dimensional vector containing information about important labs, administered medicines, and static covariates such as age and gender; each state represents a 4-hour interval in a patient’s hospital stay. There are five actions, each corresponding to a dosage of potassium delivered through an IV. The reward function is a binary indicator of whether the patient’s potassium lab value is within the potassium reference range (3.5-4.5 mmol/L), within 2 hours of receiving the administered potassium.

In the OPE task, we assume access to a behavior policy
\pi_{b}
and a target policy
\pi_{e}
, and evaluate using RMSE. It is not immediately obvious how to create this setup within MIMIC-IV. To emulate a setting in which we have access to both a behavior and target cohort, we construct two sub-cohorts from the patients that receive potassium repletion. The behavior sub-cohort consists of patients who receive lower dosages (<20 mEq/L) of potassium, and the target sub-cohort consists of the patients who receive higher dosages (>= 20mEq/L) of potassium. The behavior policy corresponds to the repletion policy in the behavior cohort, and the target policy corresponds to the repletion policy in the target cohort. Both policies are inferred using behavior cloning. Our goal is to learn the value of the target policy, and a ground truth calculation of this value is calculated by averaging the returns of the target trajectories. We observe that the maximum horizon length is
H=189
, though most patients have trajectories that are less than 20 timesteps.
