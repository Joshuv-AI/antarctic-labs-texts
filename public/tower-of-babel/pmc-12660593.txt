Case study of the separation-individuation process in an identical twin following the onset of schizophrenia in a sibling
Authors: Léa Plessis, Evelyne Bouteyre, Hélène Wilquin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFew studies have explored the experience of psychosis within twin pairs. Developmental processes such as separation-individuation might be particularly vulnerable when one twin develops a psychotic disorder. This study examines the separation-individuation challenges that may arise in twinships when one co-twin is affected by psychosis.MethodThis case study examines a 26-year-old male whose twin brother was diagnosed with schizophrenia. Two research interviews were conducted with him, and a thematic analysis was performed to identify recurrent issues related to his twin relationship.ResultsAnalysis revealed four primary themes: relationship intensity, separation, guilt, and reparation.DiscussionThe typical separation-individuation process within twinships appears to be further complicated by the presence of a psychotic disorder in one twin. These findings underscore the importance of evaluating potential identity issues in the unaffected twin and suggest avenues for further research on clinical support for twin dyads affected by psychosis.Keywords: Identical twin, Schizophrenia, Separation-individuation process, Identity, Case study

Introduction

Twins have always been an object of fascination, featuring heavily in myths, fairytales, false beliefs and scientific research. The fact that their physical and/or behavioral similarities remain with them all their lives, from cradle to grave, inevitably raises questions about the uniqueness of the two individuals concerned [33]. What makes them special is that even before they are born, they share the same space and same everyday existence. A distinction is made between identical (i.e., monozygotic, from a single zygote) and nonidentical (i.e., dizygotic, sharing approximately 50% of their genetic inheritance) twins [3].

Francis Galton was the first scientist to take an interest in twins. In 1876, he created the twin method to study differences between identical and nonidentical twins. This made it possible to distinguish between the contributions of genes (nature) and the environment (nurture) in identical and nonidentical twinships. The twin method enjoyed a considerable revival of interest at the end of the twentieth century when the results of the Minnesota Study of Twins Reared Apart were published [9, 23, 41]. This research focused on differences and similarities within and between twinships, in order to find out how genes contribute to intelligence, personality, social attitudes, and even leisure activities. However, these highly descriptive studies mainly sought to explain differences between co-twins, instead of exploring their mental and developmental processes. It was under the impetus of René Zazzo [44] that twin research first turned its attention to relationships between twins and, more specifically, to the identity issues they encounter. Entering young adulthood as a twin is an extremely singular experience in terms of identity—so much so that Zazzo [44] talked about couple identity. According to him, in order to understand a twin as an individual, we first need to understand the twin couple. The fact that co-twins share some, if not all, of their genetic inheritance raises questions about similarity and otherness (we are partly the same, but I am not you; identification, and differentiation (I resemble you in some respects, but differ from you in others) that can shape how adolescents and emerging adults construct their identity [10]. Perceived membership of a twin couple can potentially impede the separation-individuation process that allows the dyad members to see themselves as individuals and find their own separate identities [7]. Separation-individuation is a central developmental process in identity formation. In its most general sense, it refers to the progressive differentiation of the self from significant others, allowing an individual to achieve a coherent and autonomous identity [7, 8, 27]. According to Mahler et al. [27], the first separation-individuation process takes place in infancy, when the child gradually differentiates from the mother through phases of exploration and emotional autonomy. For twins, this is a dual separation, as they have to separate themselves both from their mother and from their co-twin [27]. A second separation-individuation process occurs in adolescence, and is key to the construction of identity [7], in terms of independence, differentiation, and emotional autonomy. Independence is acquired when adolescents start making decisions for themselves, differentiation when they seek to distinguish themselves from others through their beliefs or values, and emotional autonomy when they gradually relinquish the need for the approval of others or for guilt in conflicts [8]. In both infancy and adolescence, separation-individuation is often accompanied by ambivalent feelings toward attachment figures, including a desire for autonomy with concerns about harming valued relationships ([24, 43]). As cognitive and emotional capacities mature, individuals become increasingly able to recognise the potential impact of their actions on others, which can evoke feelings of guilt [21, 24]. When this guilt is acknowledged rather than denied, it can motivate reparative behaviours (i.e., acts intended to restore trust, reaffirm connection, or repair perceived damage to the relationship [17]). Such reparative responses are crucial for sustaining emotional bonds while pursuing autonomy, enabling individuation without alienation [31]. Conversely, when the capacity for repair is limited, guilt may lead either to withdrawal from separation efforts or to defensive forms of individuation that undermine both identity consolidation and relational stability. This process of separation-individuation can be complicated for twins because of their strong identification with their co-twin (i.e. twinning, [25]. Such complexity may hinder the development of distinct identities [1, 5]. While some authors claim that twins never truly manage to differentiate from each other[1, 5] others are more moderate, suggesting that twinship may simply postpone the achievement of individuation [2, 34, 42]).

A range of factors may contribute to difficulties in achieving individuation. [26] identified four main variables: (1) cultural representations of twins (e.g., twins are two identical persons), (2) parental attitudes that fail to recognise the specific needs and characteristics of each twin, (3) physical resemblance, and (4) socio-economic status, which can reinforce erroneous cultural representations. Supporting Leonard’s point, cross-cultural studies show that beliefs and expectations about twins vary widely across societies, shaping both parental practices and the twins’ identity development [33, 37]. Such cultural frameworks may facilitate or constrain the separation-individuation process within the dyad. In most cases, environmental changes such as starting a family, entering the workforce, or forming new social relationships facilitate individuation. However, an unexpected event during this period (such as the diagnosis of a mental disorder) may disrupt or derail this process. Difficulty in achieving individuation has been identified as a risk factor for psychopathological disorders in one or both twins [13]. For example, [16] observed in their study of twin sisters with schizophrenia that the onset of psychosis occurred following an abrupt separation. According to these authors, the twins’ psychotic break occurred when they were brutally separated. There have been few studies in clinical psychology and psychopathology of twinships where one member has been diagnosed with schizophrenia, whereas there is an abundant and longstanding body of epidemiological and genetic research. Findings point to moderate genetic involvement, but there is not yet any consensus on the genetic etiology of schizophrenia, leaving families and close friends and relatives in limbo [22, 39, 40]. Siblings have no idea what the future holds for them [36], which undermines their mental health [28]. The potential influence of their genes is a particular issue for twins, especially identical twins, given that they share the same genetic heritage.

Although a number of scientific articles have been published on the twins of individuals with schizophrenia, research has so far been confined to the fields of neuroscience and genetics (anatomical, functional and electrophysiological brain characteristics, search for endophenotypes, genetic factors, etc.). To our knowledge, no studies have yet been conducted in clinical psychology or psychopathology to illustrate the separation-individuation process in the twins of young men with schizophrenia. The aim of the present study was therefore to shed light on the separation-individuation issues that may arise when one member of a twinship develops a mental disability.

Method

Research objectives and framework

A young adult whose identical twin brother had schizophrenia was interviewed twice by researchers, in sessions that each lasted approximately one hour. These semi structured interviews were designed to explore the participant’s experience of family relationships, focusing on relationships with his co-twin with schizophrenia, his parents, and his two other (healthy) siblings. Plenty of time was set aside for narration and free association during each interview.

Interview modalities

This young adult was interviewed as part of the follow-up to research on adults’ experience of relationships with siblings (not necessarily twins) who have been diagnosed with schizophrenia [28, 29]. Participants in this previous study had been asked whether they would be willing to be contacted again in order to take part in individual interviews with researchers. As part of the inclusion criteria, participants were also asked in a preliminary questionnaire to indicate whether they suffered from a psychiatric disorder (Yes/No),Fernand (not his real name) reported no psychiatric difficulties. This is therefore how we came to contact Fernand. As Fernand was not in France at the time of the first interview, it was conducted in the form of a videoconference, using institutional software in settings that respected the confidentiality of both parties. We also used the videoconference modality for the second interview. The interval between the two interviews was approximately 12 months. Audio recordings were made, with the participant’s permission. After being informed of the study objectives and procedure, the participant gave his written consent. All the names, together with some of the personal details, have been changed to respect the participant’s anonymity.

The interviews were semi-structured and guided by open-ended questions. For example, in the first interview, the participant was asked: “Can you describe your relationship with your twin brother?” and “How did you react when you learned about the diagnosis of schizophrenia?” In the second interview, examples of questions included: “Looking back at our first discussion, are there aspects you would like to clarify or add?” and “How would you describe the specificity of the bond you share with your twin brother?”.

Fernand: identical twin of a young adult with schizophrenia

Fernand (participant) and Victor (his twin brother) were 26 years old at the time of the first interview. They had grown up in a large family comprising their parents, an older brother, and a younger sister. As well as having a striking physical resemblance, Fernand and Victor remained close throughout their schooling, from kindergarten to post-high school preparatory classes for engineering school entrance exams. Their first separation came when they left these preparatory classes.

Admission to engineering schools in France is via highly competitive entrance exams. Although they were initially both very motivated, Victor gradually lost interest. Despite encouragement from Fernand, the closer the exams came, the more withdrawn Victor became, refusing to speak and sleeping all day. The results of the exams put an end to their enduring closeness. Fernand and Victor won places in different engineering schools in different cities. This would be the first time they had been apart for any length of time.

Fernand and Victor therefore separately embarked on their engineering courses. Being away from his brother gave Fernand an opportunity to gain his independence, but Victor did not see things the same way. He attempted suicide a few months after the start of the academic year, and remained in hospital for several months. The family were informed that Victor had had his first psychotic break. It was when he was hospitalized a few months later that he was diagnosed with schizophrenia.

At the time of the first interview, some three years after Victor’s first psychotic episode, Fernand was living on his own in Japan. When he had graduated from engineering school, he had decided he wanted to travel, and had managed to find a temporary job in his specialist field. The purpose of this departure was to help Fernand find his place in the world. At the time of the second interview, Fernand was living in the parental home with his twin brother.

Thematic analysis of clinical discourse

We adopted an inductive approach in this case study [6]. Our objective was to explore Fernand’s experience of family relationships in order to understand the course of the separation-individuation process. In line with Bardin’s [4] recommendations, we carried out a thematic analysis. By producing verbatim transcripts of the recorded interviews, listening several times to the audio recordings, and reading through the transcripts several times, we were able to fully absorb their content. We descriptively annotated the transcripts line by line, in order to extract the main discursive themes. We then attempted to interpret these themes in order to give meaning to the experiences that Fernand had described.

Both interviews were conducted by the same researcher, who also led the thematic analysis to ensure continuity and coherence. Two additional researchers participated in the coding process and in discussions to reach consensus on the identification and interpretation of themes, enhancing the reliability and rigor of the analysis.

Results

Analysis of Fernand’s discourse revealed four interdependent themes that characterized his separation-individuation process: relationship intensity, separation, guilt, and reparation.

Relationship intensity

Whenever he broached the subject of his relationship with Victor, Fernand described it as unwavering. Nothing could supplant such a strong connection. The fact that they were identical twins was an added reason for this intensity: “I think we’re bound together in inverted commas […] The fact that we have a shared experience and that we’re identical twins means that we’re even more tightly bound together […] I’d say that it [relationship with his brother] is unique, as there are always two of us to face things together.”

When he spoke about their childhood, he told how they were so closely bonded that they could not tell where one ended and the other began. Together, they constructed a protective bubble to shield them from life’s difficulties, and this defensive structure made them stronger. Some comments were reminiscent of a couple’s shared identity (“well, I was going to say we were more than brothers”).

Separation

Although Fernand had painted an extremely positive and united picture of their relationship, he admitted wanting to put some distance between them so that he could separate himself from his brother and find his own identity. When the prodromal signs (withdrawal, hypersomnia, apragmatism, muteness) of Victor’s schizophrenia appeared, both he and Fernand were preparing for the entrance exams, and Fernand assumed that they reflected the same need for separation-individuation.

Fernand’s desire for separation came through in his discourse, where we observed changes in the types of personal pronoun he used. There was a passage where we was replaced with I, and another where us became him. These discursive modifications seemed to illustrate the processes of individuation and differentiation. More specifically, when describing his years in preparatory classes, Fernand tended to use I instead of we, as if acknowledging his need for individuation, while the shift from us to him seemed to underscore Fernand’s stated need to differentiate himself from his brother when the disease first started to manifest itself.

Fernand also mentioned his need for separation-individuation vis-à-vis the family unit. In the first interview, in reply to the question “So from now on, how do you intend to live out your relationship with your brother?”, he explained that he needed to distance himself from his family in order to “find my place […] and today, I can see that I need to live my own life, which isn’t easy to do, and that’s why I’m in Japan, to help me do that, but… yes, it wasn’t easy [to leave] […] Or rather, it was feasible, but I’d rather lost the taste for quite a lot of things, and that made it quite difficult, and today, what I’m trying to do is detach myself from that. Leave him to his life and live mine, that’s what I’m looking forward to and what I’m hoping for in the future, that we’ve each found our place.” The need to find his place resurfaced several times in Fernand’s discourse, and can be understood as a need to find a singular identity not only in his relationship with his brother and within his family, but also more broadly within society.

Guilt

Fernand spoke regretfully of the first few months he spent in a different engineering school from that of his brother. As far as he was concerned, both he and Victor were enjoying life and taking the opportunity to assert their individuality. Blissfully unaware of his brother’s distress, he assumed that Victor wanted the same thing as him. However, whereas Fernand blossomed in his quest for independence, Victor wasted away, experiencing the first symptoms of schizophrenia. During the interviews, Fernand expressed guilt about being happy at a time when his brother was not: “Again, as I was saying, I imagined at the start that he was also having a good time, but in actual fact that wasn’t the case, and I therefore had to go through a period of mourning for the person I thought he was in inverted commas. I thought he was experiencing the same thing as me, but in fact he was going through something very different.”

Riven by guilt, Fernand preferred to sacrifice his own happiness: “From my point of view, the separation was quite positive for me. What was harder for me was knowing what a difficult time he had had, as it raised questions and affected my life from that point onwards. So, I don’t know, it’s perhaps unconscious, but it’s held me back, it’s really held me back.”

The guilt expressed by Fernand stemmed above all from the fact that he had not been affected by the same disease as his brother. Prey to a form of survivor’s guilt, he asked by what lottery and what turn of fate he had been spared. Then again, had he really been spared? The uncertainty surrounding this disease prompted him to study the genetic risk for schizophrenia within a twinship: “… For a long time, I thought I had it. I believe that there’s around a 50% risk of developing the disease too when you’re a twin brother, so it’s pretty high, and partly unconsciously maybe we want to say hey I’m ill too, because there’s my brother who’s ill, so why him and not me?” When one member of a twinship develops schizophrenia, it suddenly makes that co-twin different, threatening the twin couple. Out of loyalty to his brother, Fernand countenanced the prospect of being diagnosed with schizophrenia too. It was a prospect that brought him some peace.

By distancing himself from his family, Fernand had hoped to find his place: not as a parent for his brother or a savior for his family, but a place that was truly his. Fernand believed he was best placed to understand and help his brother. However, the geographical distance he created by going to Japan made it impossible for him to intervene, and he found this distressing. This strong ambivalence was probably linked to a new fear that he might be confused with Victor if he was physically by his side, and might, if only for an instant, be perceived as a schizophrenic by the rest of the world.

Reparation

To assuage his guilty feelings, Fernand felt he needed to go to his brother’s assistance. He described how being a twin brother meant that he had a special role that he needed to fulfil for the sake of all his family: “Yeah, even when it’s a tricky situation, it’s easier, and it’s perhaps because of that that I felt obliged to help him, and also because it’s easier for me in inverted commas than for my parents. Even so, it’s not easy for me either.”

Realizing that the family situation was deteriorating, Fernand decided to symbolically dispossess his father of his role. When he recounted how he had taken his father’s place, we did not know what that actually represented for him, and he gave no indication beyond saying that his intention had been to restore structure to his family. According to him, his father was not properly fulfilling his functions within the family unit: “At that point, he [his father] could no longer play his role in inverted commas, and I don’t know why I assigned myself this mission, but I felt I needed to take over his role at that point. More or less unconsciously, it wasn’t deliberate, but they needed some structure to keep going.”

Fernand then set about finding out more about the disease. However, the lack of answers from professionals and the difficulty of finding immediate solutions made Fernand feel impotent: “We were confronted with a problem for which there was no solution. We learned to understand that there was no solution… But we weren’t told that, nobody told us that, and we were trying to find solutions, we were asking questions. There were doctors, psychiatrists, why didn’t they give his disease a name? Why didn’t they give him medication? We didn’t know. But later we understood, we worked it out for ourselves.”

Despite their physical proximity, Fernand was unable understand what his twin was going through. His helplessness in the face of his brother’s plight and the absence of a solution made him feel utterly powerless. The symbiotic relationship of their childhood no longer existed, and he realized that he would have to live with constant uncertainty about the disease. “I think the hardest thing is not knowing and not understanding, it’s the being in the dark that makes things so difficult.”

Fernand was greatly affected by what his brother was going through. Even so, he did not wish to talk about it with his friends or relatives, as he reasoned that his own feelings were paltry, compared with his brother’s suffering: “I told myself that what my brother was going through was far more important, so I even forbade myself from existing in a certain sense, it’s bizarre, but well… It was a case of we’ve got a problem [Victor’s schizophrenia] and we really need to resolve it.”

Analysis and discussion

To our knowledge, no published scientific article has so far dealt with the separation-individuation process in individuals whose twin sister or brother has schizophrenia.

Previous studies on siblings of individuals suffering from schizophrenia have highlighted general tendencies such as decreased closeness, increased conflict, and sibling rivalry [28, 29]. While these group-based analyses provide valuable insights into broad patterns, they do not capture the unique dynamics that may occur within specific sibling constellations. The present case study, focusing on a twin relationship, offers a distinctive perspective on a form of sibling bond that remains underexplored in psychopathology.

The intensity and closeness characteristic of twin relationships can amplify both protective and disruptive aspects of the sibling tie, providing a unique window into separation-individuation processes. By examining a single case in depth, this study illustrates how case-study approaches can complement group analyses and enrich our understanding of the specific experiences of siblings in the context of schizophrenia.

Building on this framework, four main themes relating to our participant’s experience of sibling and family relationships emerged from his discourse, in the following order: 1) relationship intensity, 2) separation, 3) guilt, and 4) reparation.

At the start of the first interview, Fernand dwelt on the intensity of his relationship with his twin brother. The terms he used to describe this relationship prior to Victor’s illness evoked symbiosis (“we’re bound together”, “we quickly set about constructing our bubble”, “there are always two of us to face things together”). This feeling of brotherly unity corroborated previous studies describing the couple identity of twins [19, 44]. The two brothers did not see themselves as individuals, but as a dyad displaying the characteristics of a single person. We also came across this couple identity in Fernand’s experience of family life. Fernand’s discourse made no allusions to sibling rivalry. For rivalry to emerge, the siblings need to be clearly differentiated, whereas Fernand and Victor had a relationship that knew almost no frontiers (“we were more than brothers”). Fernand scarcely ever mentioned his sister and his other brother. When the subject did come up, Fernand only gave them a cursory mention in his discourse. He claimed that his parents were not best placed to understand and therefore help his twin brother, suggesting that his relationship with his twin was all powerful. He was certainly convinced that the relationship of trust he had established with his brother could dispel his hallucinatory symptoms.

In the twin relationship, the co-twin can be regarded as an attachment figure providing safety and security [38]. However, whereas Fernand saw himself as an attachment figure for Victor, the opposite did not come through in his discourse. He was the person who could best comfort his brother and alleviate his symptoms, but he did not receive any reciprocal reassurance from his brother.

The power of this bond was reflected in the massive use of the personal pronoun we in Fernand’s discourse whenever he spoke about his relationship with his brother. By the same token, the gradual replacing of this we with I or him illustrated Fernand’s attempt to separate himself from his brother and construct a distinct identity for himself.

The separation-individuation process is generally fostered by the presence of friends or romantic partners [42]. There is increased exploration of the domains of best friends and romantic relationships, and an attendant decrease in commitment toward siblings [42]. Fernand had wanted to become more independent of his twin brother after preparatory classes. Separating oneself from this almost the same [32] can be complicated—so much so that Coslin [11] suggested that twins find the separation-individuation process harder than nontwin siblings do. Whereas Fernand seemed ready to embark on this experience and meet new people, it appears that the separation-individuation process did not operate within the same timeframe for Victor. As he was not yet ready to relinquish his original identity, he may well have experienced this asynchrony as an abrupt and violent distancing [32], and this additional source of stress may have precipitated his mental health problems.

When mental illness strikes one family member, it triggers a veritable earthquake, disturbing the personal equilibrium of all the other members, as well as their place within the family [18]. Fernand’s twin status exacerbated these effects, causing him to wonder about his own mental health. His trip to Japan had been intended to help him find his place. This flight in search of individuation proved fruitless, and after his return Fernand was still trying to define his place within the family and within the twinship. Completing the individuation process was made more complex for Fernand by feelings of guilt (failure to realize that his brother was struggling, survivor guilt, loyalty to the twin couple they formed).

Possibly trying to explain to himself why he had not immediately come to his brother’s assistance, Fernand admitted on several occasions that he had been unaware that his brother was in trouble while he was busy living his new life to the full. The subsequent shock of realisation was so great that Fernand fell apart too, abandoning the separation-individuation process in order to be at Victor’s side during his ordeal. The schizophrenia forcibly differentiated between the co-twins, with Fernand doing his utmost to resist this. Out of loyalty to his brother and to their couple, he looked for signs that might confirm he too had the disease.

With something akin to what Crespelle [12] called reactional guilt, Fernand wondered about the probability of his developing schizophrenia one day. He looked for an explanation as to why it should have affected Victor rather than him. The question Why him and not me? evoked by [14, 15]) in the case of psychosis in nontwin siblings is even more pertinent for identical twins. According to Scharmann (2007), the twin relationship can be conceived not only as two individuals, but also as a ‘twin couple,’ a psychic entity in its own right. The onset of schizophrenia in Victor disrupted this triangular configuration (Fernand, Victor and their couple), leaving the brothers without the feeling of being ‘three’ and reduced to ‘two’. Each had to mourn the twin couple they once embodied.

Fernand sought to repair the error he felt so guilty about through a variety of mechanisms. Reparation can accelerate the process of mourning and thus of resilience. Fernand began by describing his overriding need to go to the aid of his brother, as though there were some sort of brotherly pact. He discounted his parents, assuming that he was better placed than them to manage and understand his brother’s experience. However, trying to usurp his father’s position simply accentuated his guilt, and he rapidly reinstated the family members to their respective roles and places.

At the same time, in order to assuage his guilt, Fernand, like many siblings of individuals with schizophrenia, searched for information about the disease and advice on how to help his twin brother. He was initially disappointed by the dearth of information, as well as by the lack of support from professionals. Patients and their families who are given a diagnosis by a physician, especially if it concerns a chronic disease, are immediately confronted with what Giraudet [20] called the ordeal of reality. One of the many reactions they may display, and which illustrates the traumatising effect of this announcement, is dissociative amnesia, where important personal information disappears from consciousness [30]. In Fernand’s case, the harshness of Victor’s diagnosis may have triggered such a mechanism, as he stated that he could not remember any of the information that had been communicated to him. Although this hypothesis cannot be confirmed, it offers a plausible psychological interpretation of his reaction, consistent with the protective function of dissociative amnesia, namely to protect the individual from a reality that seems unbearable to them at that moment.

Making amends also required sacrifice on Fernand’s part [12]. He described how he stepped aside for a while to avoid attracting the family’s attention when it needed to be focused on his brother Victor. He even forbade himself to exist so that he would be there at all times for his brother, and had gone back to living with his parents and his brother Victor on his return from Japan. All this can be seen as a sacrifice for the sake of the twinship. Fernand’s older brother and younger sister were both living abroad, far away from the family home. Fernand was the only one to make a sacrificial gesture and return home to support the family. By sacrificing himself for the good of the family, Fernand could don the mantle of hero and savior, in the hope of gaining approval and recognition from the rest of the family. Fernand seemed to have lost much of his self-confidence since the onset of Victor’s schizophrenia. The gratitude of his relatives might alleviate this loss and give his life worth. If it was only for a short while, this sacrifice might enable Fernand to make peace with his feelings of guilt and resume the separation-individuation process.

Clinical implications: The present case highlights that the separation-individuation process of a healthy twin may be significantly disrupted by a co-twin’s schizophrenia. Clinicians should be aware that co-twins may experience identity confusion, heightened guilt, and challenges in autonomy. Supporting the separation-individuation process could therefore be considered a valuable clinical objective, either through psychoeducation, family interventions, or individualized support, to help co-twins maintain a coherent sense of self while preserving the sibling bond. Monitoring these processes could also serve as an early indicator of psychological strain, allowing timely intervention before more severe distress emerges.

Limitations and perspectives

This groundbreaking study illustrated the separation-individuation process in a young man whose identical twin had been diagnosed with schizophrenia. However, it had several limitations. First, it dealt with a single case, so the results cannot be generalized. Nevertheless, case studies are extremely useful, as they provide an opportunity to undertake in-depth analyses of particular problems [35], here, the separation-individuation process. Second, as this case study focused solely on the healthy twin, we could not draw any conclusions about the relational dynamics between the two brothers. Third, Fernand’s absence of psychiatric history was based on self-report, which means that we cannot fully guarantee the accuracy of this information. However, such a declarative approach is consistent with research in the social and human sciences, where the subjective account is not only considered valid data but also essential to understanding lived experience. Future studies of the separation-individuation process in twins could involve both members of the dyad. The twins of individuals with schizophrenia have rarely been the subject of research in clinical psychology and psychopathology, despite the growing interest due to increased numbers of multiple births. This exploratory study will encourage new studies of these sibships. The clinical objective is to inform healthcare professionals about the psychopathological risks for the twins of individuals with schizophrenia, so that they can be on the lookout for signs.

Conclusion

The present study shows just how complex our participant’s separation-individuation process became after the onset of his twin’s schizophrenia. There is agreement in the literature that twins may encounter greater difficulty when undertaking this process than nontwins, and a diagnosis of schizophrenia can create yet more obstacles. Our participant’s questioning of his identity and the difficulty he had finding his place within his family underscore the importance of paying attention to these co-twins, who are generally considered solely in terms of their genetic heritage.
