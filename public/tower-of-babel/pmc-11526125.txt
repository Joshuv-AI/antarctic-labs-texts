Reply to: Geomorphic markers tell a different story about fault slip rates in Tierra del Fuego, Patagonia
Authors: Francisca Sandoval, Gregory P. De Pascale
Published: 2024

Replying to: R. Vassallo et al.; Scientific Reports 10.1038/s41598-024-66469-0 (2024).

In this paper we address interest on our recent research1 brought up by Vassallo et al.2 regarding the plate boundary Magallanes Fault System (MFS) faults in Chile and Argentina. We have been working in Tierra Del Fuego for several years and enjoy keeping ourselves up to date with current research and they2 interestingly suggest we ignored their research, however our paper1 (based on the MSc Thesis work by F. Sandoval3—with Thesis fieldwork in 2018, Thesis completed in early 2019, and defended in August 2019) was published nearly simultaneously with theirs4, now almost five years ago in 2020. There is extensive contemporary work along the MFS which is the main strike slip crustal fault seismic source in Patagonia5, and was also studied by4 where they only focused on the master Magallanes Fault (MF) in Argentina. Our fieldwork1 in both Argentina and Chile was along the master MF and the Deseado and Hope Faults (DF and HF). Importantly, the MF slip rates from both studies1,4 (and from different but nearby sites, and using different methods, and using similar but different ages) in Argentina provide very similar slip rate results (6.4 ± 0.9 mm/yr2 versus 7.8 ± 1.3 mm/yr1) with overlapping values, in addition to having lengths of measured fault offsets being consistent (Table 1). The master Magallanes fault and Hope fault in Chile near Lake Fagnano (or Cami)

They question two “fault segments” (i.e. faults), in Chile (first mapped7 prior to 1994) suggesting that our markers and results are biased. Regarding the master MF, they2 show an annotated Google Earth image (their Fig. 12) with our MF trace (where both gully systems are actually consistently offset) and suggest no surface expression within Quaternary deposits over several kilometers and that displacements are instead meanders. Their Fig. 1 is flawed2, and thus their critique is indefensible for the following reasons, (a) the yellow lines they draw to show the outline of the terrace risers are based on vegetation alone, (b) the yellow lines are not based on topography as can only be seen in our SFM models and impossible to see from Google Earth alone (which is why we collected drone photography), and (c) they did not do field validation as we did which allowed us to validate the high-resolution models we generated and track the MF fault trace and see how it influenced the tectonic geomorphology1. Importantly, we used standard fault analysis methods for channels6, not only based on the thalweg, but also using gully/channel morphology as defined by the both the true right, middle, and left, of the post-glacial incised channel using our model contours1, all of which are displaced and measured on both sides of the MF with a fault trace that is consistent over several kilometers. Secondly they4 question the HF’s scarps, first mapped7 in 1994, parallel to Mt Hope between Lake Fagnano and the Almirantazgo Sound1. They present again our uninterpreted SFM models with contours (Fig. 22) that still clearly show left-lateral offsets in panels A and B that have left-lateral offsets < 25 m, offsets which does not look impressive at the scale of these maps, yet nevertheless exist1. These sites are also interesting because the youngest surfaces are to the west and oldest to the east. This is likely due to isostatic rebound. Site C, our westernmost site does not have the HF trace visible, however this is very close to sea level, and is impacted by construction of an airfield hence mapping the HF here as “inferred”. We believe that the HF here has not ruptured at Site C since it emerged from the sea, however it is observed immediately offshore of Site C in multibeam sonar mapping data (Hernandez, M. et al. in preparation). East of Site C we were impressed by the HF scarps in our models and photography (consistent with mapping by Klepeis7). Importantly, in stark contrast to the dryer conditions in Argentina8, this area in Chile is almost completely covered in thick (sometimes > 1 m thick) moss deposits. In addition to making it very difficult to travel, we speculate that these moss deposits “heal” fault traces at the surface shortly after MF and HF ruptures (i.e. this moss grows and quickly conceals the fault traces), which preserves only the larger-scale, multi-event scarps and displacements over several seismic cycles (e.g. river terraces and gullies) such as those we documented (but were not eroded much by the same rivers that are incising due to isostatic rebound). We have recently observed this in Iceland where the moss mats opens along faults during fault slip, but because it is alive, it rapidly reforms (i.e. heals) back into a coherent mat—obscuring the evidence at the surface of a surface rupture (Fig. 1). We suspect lidar-derived topography will have limited value here because of the thick moss. Nevertheless, Lago Fagnano drainage initiation timing coupled with better isostatic uplift rates will certainly aid in constraining HF slip rates.

The well-documented glacial retreat history from last glacial maximum limits and impact on slip rates in Chilean Tierra Del Fuego

Age dating was not part of our research, our goal was to map, identify, and document faults and displacements, because this area is already extremely well studied regarding Late-Quaternary glacial retreat, however they question2 our lack of absolute ages. A comprehensive paleoseismic study will leverage all available dating and direct dating is not always required in well-dated areas. This is especially true and a valuable tool when funding for oftentimes expensive geochronology is unavailable. A comprehensive literature review of ages combined with good mapping and local and regional context can be very powerful. Nevertheless, they question the literature ages we used (i.e. 12.5- 10.2 ka) for onshore locations in Chile adjacent to the Darwin Cordillera. Importantly, they remark that there was “New ages as old as 17 ka obtained near the Darwin Cordillera suggest an extremely rapid retreat of the Inutil Bay lobe from Last Glacial Maximum positions to within a few kilometers of the present ice extent”2. We agree, however the timing of the almost 200 km retreat along the fiords from Inutil Bay, to the sites west of Lake Fagnano are well documented as much younger than 17 ka. Marine research shows everything is younger than 16 ka in our study area9 with marine sediments accumulating in the western Almirantazgo Sound (AS) at ~ 13 to 14 ka. Additional cores from AS10 show that sedimentation started prior to 14.3 ka, with glacier-proximal deposits present until 13.5 ka, and after 12.3 ka most glaciers had retreated to land, i.e. where we did our fieldwork1, with marine conditions after 9.8 ka. Additional AS core analysis (at the Martinelli glacier) show ice presence from 15.5 ka until before 12.5 ka, when ice retreated from the AS into southern valleys11. Although it remains unknown until when glacial ice covered the MF and HF onshore in Chile, there was likely ice there until at least 12.5 ka. Because of the narrow onshore valleys (with topographic shading that can preserve valley glaciers), we expect ages younger than 12.5 ka. Lake Fagnano sediment analysis12 suggests that moraines were being deposited east of our site perhaps as late as 9.3 ka. Onshore in Chile, the MF and HF are located in glacial valleys at distances < 5 km from modern cirque glaciers (e.g. glacier ice is only 1.7 km away from the MF in 2021 imagery), thus our ages may actually be overestimates and the actual slip rates faster. Based on the literature dates and modern near HF glaciers in Chile, the ages we used are realistic. However we agree that more detailed dating will help to constrain glacial retreat timing.

Consistent reported offsets and slip rates along the Magallanes fault in Argentina

The only field-derived MF slip rates in Argentina are from two studies1,4 (Table 1), and these slip rate values overlap closely. But despite these overlaps, they4 suggest the Argentina ages we used1 are too young. However, their displacements4 were measured on digital elevation models (DEMs) created from “Pléiades images”, although how these DEMs were generated and their resolution is not mentioned in their paper, i.e. the methodology section of their paper leaves the reader to wonder what they did, which increases uncertainty in their results and conclusions and eliminates reproducibility. Importantly, drone surveys are undertaken globally along faults because these data are superior to satellite data. Nevertheless, in Argentina their displacements4 are slightly smaller (i.e. shorter) than ours1, whereas we used a number of measurements crossing the MF from fit-for-purpose field collected models (Table 1), that we field validated. Thus, we are left wondering based on their figures and text exactly what was measured, how it was measured, and where they measured MF offsets4. Their4 lower-quality16,17 oblique to the MF channels with extensive beaver dam impacts (i.e. ponding), as opposed to our higher-certainty/quality MF-perpendicular offset geomorphic features1,6,17,18 suggests their displacements are perhaps mischaracterised or interpreted. Synergies are still possible by data integration, and by applying our higher quality Argentina MF displacements (105–140 m1) with their ages4 (20–16 ka), we obtain new MF slip rate in Argentina of 6.9 ± 1.3 mm/yr (Table 1). Here lidar-derived topography would shine (i.e. lower density vegetation9,15), would permit filtering of vegetation, and facilitate higher precision displacement measurements and for identification of additional sites for dating because site histories are perhaps regionally variable.

Slip rates along the Deseado fault in Chile

They4 question our inference of an at least 1 mm/yr slip rate along the DF1, because there were no offsets nor geochronological data. We presented dendrochronology analysis in our paper1 to defend DF ruptures which showed the last surface rupture along the DF happened after 1680 CE. The strike slip faults we have worked on in the field in similar forested environments with clear multi-event tectonic fault scarps like the DF including in California, (e.g. the San Andreas, Rodgers Creek, Hayward faults), Chile (e.g. LOFZ13,14), or in New Zealand (e.g. Alpine, Hope, Porters Pass15,16), all have slip rates > 1 mm/yr. In light of this field experience along active faults, the prominent DF tectonic geomorphology provides compelling evidence for a strike slip slip rate of at least 1 mm/yr1,7. Multiple recent ruptures in a young post-glacial landscape are required to generate clear and prominent fault scarps like observed along the DF1. Please recall that our inferred DF slip rate is a minimum and ongoing work will help shed light on this issue.

Conclusions

In summary, our drone-photography derived models, field observations, and the dating studies we used to determine our slip rates1 (Table 1) that we published in 2020 are sound, and consistent with the only other geological slip rate along the MF4 in Argentina. Although the two studies were from different sites, and used different methods, they reach the same conclusion. That is really excellent as it provides additional support to the data included in each study. Two independent studies1,4 reaching roughtly the same conclusions is exciting science. A further testing of this will be by ongoing onshore, coastal, and offshore observations along the MFS (Hernandez, M., et al., in preparation). As mentioned earlier in this paper, where are the other MFS faults in Argentina (besides the MF) and how fast do these slip? Also does the Master MF “offshore” in Lake Fagnano (or Cami) partition into several sub-parallel faults? These outstanding questions are critical for seismic hazard and neotectonic implications.
