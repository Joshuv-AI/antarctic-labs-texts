Unbalanced Zarankiewicz problem for bipartite subdivisions with applications to incidence geometry
Lili Ködmön, Anqi Li, Ji Zeng

Abstract
For a bipartite graph H H , its linear threshold is the smallest real number σ \sigma such that every bipartite graph G = ( U ⊔ V , E ) G=(U\sqcup V,E) with unbalanced parts | V | ≳ | U | σ |V|\gtrsim|U|^{\sigma} and without a copy of H H must have a linear number of edges | E | ≲ | V | |E|\lesssim|V| . We prove that the linear threshold of the complete bipartite subdivision graph K s , t ′ K_{s,t}^{\prime} is at most σ s = 2 − 1 / s \sigma_{s}=2-1/s . Moreover, we show that any σ < σ s \sigma<\sigma_{s} is less than the linear threshold of K s , t ′ K_{s,t}^{\prime} for sufficiently large t t (depending on s s and σ \sigma ). Some geometric applications of this result are given: we show that any n n points and n n lines in the complex plane without an s s -by- s s grid determine O ⁡ ( n 4 / 3 − c ) O(n^{4/3-c}) incidences for some constant c > 0 c>0 depending on s s ; and for certain pairs ( p , q ) (p,q) , we establish nontrivial lower bounds on the number of distinct distances determined by n n points in the plane under the condition that every p p points determine at least q q distinct distances.

Introduction

The
Zarankiewicz problem
asks for the maximum number of edges, denoted by
z(m,n;s,t)
, an
m
-by-
n
bipartite graph
G
can have without containing a copy of
K_{s,t}
, i.e. an
s
-by-
t
complete bipartite graph. Here, “containing a copy” requires slightly more than “containing a subgraph”: the
s
-side of
K_{s,t}
must sit in the
m
-side of
G
. The most fundamental result towards the Zarankiewicz problem is the upper bound of Kővári, Sós, and Turán
[
21
]
:

z(m,n;s,t)\leq O(mn^{1-1/s}+n).

From this bound, we notice that
n\gtrsim m^{s}
implies
z(m,n;s,t)\lesssim n
. Motivated by this observation, we make the following definition: the
linear threshold
of a bipartite graph
H
is the smallest real number
\sigma
satisfying for any
\epsilon>0
there exists
\delta
such that every bipartite graph
G=(U\sqcup V,E)
with
|V|\geq\epsilon|U|^{\sigma}
and
|E|\geq\delta|V|
must contain a copy of
H
. For example, our observation from the Kővári–Sós–Turán bound says that the linear threshold of
K_{s,t}
is at most
s
.

It is natural to consider the linear thresholds of other forbidden subgraphs. Erdős and Füredi
[
13
]
showed that the linear threshold of a bipartite graph
G=(U\sqcup V,E)
is at most
\Delta(V)
, that is, the
maximum degree
of the right hand side vertex set
V
. (We remark that only the
\Delta(V)=2
case was stated in
[
13
]
.) The
complete bipartite subdivision

K_{s,t}^{\prime}
is the bipartite graph obtained by substituting each edge of
K_{s,t}
by a path of length two. Here, we put the subdivision vertices on the right hand side of
K_{s,t}^{\prime}
as a convention. (More formally, we define
K_{s,t}^{\prime}
by vertex set
\{u_{i},v_{j}\}\sqcup\{w_{ij}\}
and edge set
\{u_{i}w_{ij},v_{j}w_{ij}\}
for all
1\leq i\leq s
,
1\leq j\leq t
.) Hence their result
[
13
]
says the linear threshold of
K_{s,t}^{\prime}
is at most 2. In this paper, we give an improved bound that is not far from the true answer.

Theorem 1.1
.

For every integer
s\geq 1
, the linear threshold of
K_{s,t}^{\prime}
is at most
\sigma_{s}=2-1/s
. Moreover, any
\sigma<\sigma_{s}
is less than the linear threshold of
K_{s,t}^{\prime}
for sufficiently large
t
(depending on
s
and
\sigma
).

The
forbidden subgraph problem
asks for the maximum number of edges, denoted by
\text{ex}(n,G)
, a graph can have without containing a subgraph
G
. At an asymptotic level, we can relate
\text{ex}(n,K_{s,t})
with
z(n,n;s,t)
. Hence this problem can be considered a variation of the Zarankiewicz problem in a balanced setting. There is a series of studies on
\text{ex}(n,K_{s,t}^{\prime})
(see e.g.
[
17
,
29
,
5
,
4
,
19
]
), and they demonstrate the phenomenon that
\text{ex}(n,K_{s,t}^{\prime})
is smaller than
\Theta(n^{3/2})
, the general bound for bipartite graphs with right hand side maximum degree two (
[
13
,
1
]
). However, it is worth noting that
\text{ex}(n,C_{6})=O(n^{4/3})
where
C_{6}
is the
6
-cycle graph, but a construction by de Caen and Székely
[
6
]
shows that 2 is the linear threshold of
C_{6}
. Thus, a smaller
\text{ex}(n,G)
does not guarantee a smaller linear threshold of
G
. On the other hand, the linear threshold of
C_{2k}
for
k\geq 4
is always smaller than 2 by a result of Naor and Verstraëte
[
25
]
, despite
\text{ex}(n,C_{k})=(\frac{1}{4}+o(1))n^{2}
whenever
k\geq 3
is odd. Thus, the linear threshold of
G^{\prime}
(subdivision of
G
) is not determined by the asymptotic behavior of
\text{ex}(n,G)
.

Many results in incidence geometry follow divide-and-conquer strategies with space-partitioning techniques (see e.g.
[
22
,
20
]
). Moreover, it is a common practice to upper bound incidences by linear terms in unbalanced parts of the partitioning, and our notion of linear thresholds measures this level of unbalance. As a specific example, we point out the following point-variety incidence bound which motivated our study. For a point set
P
and a collection of algebraic varieties
\mathcal{V}
, both in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we define their incidence graph
I(P,\mathcal{V})
as the bipartite graph with vertex set
P\sqcup\mathcal{V}
and edge set
\{(p,V)\in P\times\mathcal{V}:p\in V\}
. We denote the size of its edge set by
|I(P,\mathcal{V})|
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
H
be a bipartite graph with its linear threshold at most
\sigma
. If
P
is a set of
m
points and
\mathcal{V}
is a set of
n
constant-degree algebraic varieties, both in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, such that
I(P,\mathcal{V})
does not contain a copy of
H
, then for every
\epsilon>0
and as
m,n\to\infty
, we have

|I(P,\mathcal{V})|\leq O\left(m^{\frac{(d-1)\sigma}{d\sigma-1}+\epsilon}n^{\frac{d(\sigma-1)}{d\sigma-1}}+m+n\right).

A point-line configuration
(P,L)
is called an
s
-by-
s
grid
if the line collection
L
is the union of
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
each containing
s
lines, and the point collection
P
equals
\{\ell_{1}\cap\ell_{2}:\ell_{1}\in L_{1},\ell_{2}\in L_{2}\}
which has size exactly
s^{2}
(square of
s
). Mirzaei and Suk
[
24
]
proved that point-line configurations in the real plane without a grid determine
O(n^{4/3-c})
incidences for some
c>0
. This is better than the celebrated Szemerédi–Trotter bound
O(n^{4/3})
, which has corresponding extremal constructions but with grid substructures (
[
32
,
7
]
). While the argument in
[
24
]
is based on space-partitioning, Balko and Frankl
[
3
]
recently gave a different proof using crossing-number-related techniques (
[
31
,
26
]
). It is unclear how to generalize these proofs to the complex plane where the analogous Szemerédi–Trotter-type theorems are much more involved (
[
33
,
34
]
). Nevertheless, we employ a technique of Solymosi and Tao
[
28
]
, together with our Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
, to prove the following bound.

Theorem 1.3
.

For fixed integer
s\geq 1
and real number
\epsilon>0
, any
n
points
P
and
n
lines
L
in the complex plane
\mathbb{C}^{2}
without an
s
-by-
s
grid sub-configuration satisfy

|I(P,L)|\leq O\left(n^{\frac{4}{3}-\frac{1}{9s-6}+\epsilon}\right).

A classical problem by Erdős is to study the extremal function
f(n,p,q)
, the number of colors required to edge-color
K_{n}
such that every
p
edges receive at least
q
distinct colors. Using the probabilistic method, Erdős and Gyárfás
[
8
]
showed that

f(n,p,q)\leq cn^{\frac{p-2}{\binom{p}{2}-q+1}}~\text{where $c$ depends only on $p,q$.}

A geometric variation of
f(n,p,q)
is
d(n,p,q)
, the smallest number of distinct distances determined by
n
points in the plane under the condition that every
p
points determine at least
q
distinct distances. Due to its geometric nature, one would expect
d(n,p,q)
to behave quite differently from
f(n,p,q)
. One such geometric result that we are aware of is due to Fox, Pach, and Suk
[
12
]
:

d\left(n,p,\binom{p}{2}-p+6\right)\geq n^{\frac{8}{7}-o(1)}.

Using our Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
, we derive lower bounds for
d(n,p,q)
that will contrast the Erdős–Gyárfás bound
[
8
]
for certain new pairs of
(p,q)
. Our bounds somewhat interpolate the result of Fox–Pach–Suk
[
12
]
and another lower bound by Pohoata and Sheffer
[
27
]
for
f(n,p,q)
.

Theorem 1.4
.

For fixed integer
s\geq 1
and real number
\epsilon>0
, we have

d\left(n,p,\binom{p}{2}-p+3\cdot\left\lfloor\frac{p}{2s}\right\rfloor+2s+2\right)\geq\Omega\left(n^{\frac{8}{7}+\frac{18}{7(7s-4)}-\epsilon}\right).

and

2
Linear thresholds of bipartite subdivisions

We shall prove the first half of Theorem
1.1
in this section. The following lemma originates from a result of Erdős and Simonovits (Theorem 1 in
[
9
]
, see also
[
18
,
5
]
). Here, our treatment is more complicated due to the host graph being sparse.

Lemma 2.1
.

For each integer
s\geq 2
, there is a sufficiently small positive constant
c_{s}
such that any bipartite graph
G=(U\sqcup V,E)
with
|V|\geq|U|^{2-1/s}
and
|E|\geq\delta|V|
(
1\leq\delta\in\mathbb{R}
) contains a subgraph
\tilde{G}=(\tilde{U}\sqcup\tilde{V},\tilde{E})
with
|\tilde{U}|\geq|U|^{c_{s}}
satisfying the following conditions:

\displaystyle\makebox[0.0pt][l]{$\text{(I)}~|\tilde{V}|\geq|\tilde{U}|^{2-1/s},$}\phantom{\text{(III)}~c_{s}\Delta_{\tilde{G}}(\tilde{V})\leq|\tilde{E}|/|\tilde{V}|,}\qquad\qquad\text{(II)}~|\tilde{E}|\geq c_{s}\delta|\tilde{V}|,

\displaystyle\text{(III)}~c_{s}\Delta_{\tilde{G}}(\tilde{V})\leq|\tilde{E}|/|\tilde{V}|,\qquad\qquad\text{(IV)}~c_{s}\delta^{c_{s}}|\tilde{U}|\Delta_{\tilde{G}}(\tilde{U})^{1-1/s}\leq|\tilde{E}|.

Proof.

We construct
\tilde{G}
by two iterative procedures. In the first procedure, we let
G_{0}=(U_{0}\sqcup V_{0},E_{0})=G
and construct
G_{i}=(U_{i}\sqcup V_{i},E_{i})
as follows. Given
G_{i}
already constructed, we set
V_{i+1}
to be the
|V_{i}|/16
many vertices in
V_{i}
with largest degrees. If less than half of
E_{i}
are adjacent to
V_{i+1}
, we terminate the first procedure. Otherwise, by a simple averaging argument, we can find
U_{i+1}\subset U_{i}
with
|U_{i+1}|=|U_{i}|/16^{\frac{s}{2s-1}}
such that

|E_{G_{i}}(U_{i+1},V_{i+1})|\geq\frac{1}{2}|E_{i}|/16^{\frac{s}{2s-1}}.

Here,
E_{G_{i}}(U_{i+1},V_{i+1})
denotes the set of edges in
G_{i}
between
U_{i+1}
and
V_{i+1}
. Then we take
G_{i+1}
as the subgraph of
G_{i}
induced on
U_{i+1}\sqcup V_{i+1}
. Let
\ell
be the index where the first procedure terminates at
G_{\ell}
. We have the following identities:

|U_{\ell}|=|U_{0}|/16^{\frac{\ell s}{2s-1}},\quad|V_{\ell}|=|V_{0}|/16^{\ell},\quad|E_{\ell}|\geq\frac{1}{2^{\ell}}|E_{0}|/16^{\frac{\ell s}{2s-1}}.

Combining them with
|U_{\ell}||V_{\ell}|\geq|E_{\ell}|
and
|E_{0}|\geq|V_{0}|
, we can solve for

\ell\leq\log(|U_{0}|)/3.

(2.1)

Next, we let
\tilde{V}=V_{\ell}\setminus V_{\ell+1}
. Notice that
|\tilde{V}|=(15/16)|V_{\ell}|
and
|E_{G_{\ell}}(U_{\ell},\tilde{V})|\geq|E_{\ell}|/2
. By an averaging argument, we can find
U^{\prime}\subset U_{\ell}
with
|U^{\prime}|=(15/16)^{\frac{s}{2s-1}}|U_{\ell}|
such that the subgraph
G^{\prime}\subset G_{\ell}
induced on
U^{\prime}\sqcup\tilde{V}
satisfies
|E^{\prime}|\geq(15/16)^{\frac{s}{2s-1}}|E_{\ell}|/2
, where
E^{\prime}
is the edge set of
G^{\prime}
. Since
V_{\ell+1}
is chosen among vertices with largest degrees,

\Delta_{G^{\prime}}(\tilde{V})\leq\frac{|E_{\ell}|}{|V_{\ell+1}|}\leq 30\cdot(16/15)^{\frac{s}{2s-1}}\cdot\frac{|E^{\prime}|}{|\tilde{V}|}.

(2.2)

In the second procedure, we let
G^{\prime}_{0}=(U^{\prime}_{0}\sqcup\tilde{V},E^{\prime}_{0})=G^{\prime}
and construct
G^{\prime}_{i}=(U^{\prime}_{i}\sqcup\tilde{V},E^{\prime}_{i})
as follows. Given
G^{\prime}_{i}
already constructed, we set
U^{\prime}_{i+1}
to be the
|U^{\prime}_{i}|^{1-1/s}
many vertices in
U^{\prime}_{i}
with largest degrees. If less than half of
E^{\prime}_{i}
are adjacent to
U^{\prime}_{i+1}
, we terminate the second procedure. In this case, we let
\tilde{U}=U^{\prime}_{i}\setminus U^{\prime}_{i+1}
and
\tilde{G}
be the subgraph of
G^{\prime}_{i}
induced on
\tilde{U}\sqcup\tilde{V}
and denote its edge set as
\tilde{E}
. Since
U^{\prime}_{i+1}
is chosen among vertices with largest degrees, we have

\Delta_{\tilde{G}}(\tilde{U})\leq\frac{|E^{\prime}_{i}|}{|U^{\prime}_{i+1}|}\leq\frac{2|\tilde{E}|}{|U^{\prime}_{i+1}|}=\frac{2|\tilde{E}|}{|U^{\prime}_{i}|^{1-1/s}}\leq\frac{2|\tilde{E}|}{|\tilde{U}|^{1-1/s}}.

(2.3)

If more than half of
E^{\prime}_{i}
are adjacent to
U^{\prime}_{i+1}
, we take
G^{\prime}_{i+1}
as the subgraph of
G^{\prime}_{i}
induced on
U^{\prime}_{i+1}\sqcup\tilde{V}
. Suppose the second procedure has not terminated after
s\log(s)
iterations, then we terminate it and define
\tilde{U}=U^{\prime}_{s\log(s)}
and
\tilde{G}=G^{\prime}_{s\log(s)}
. In this case, we have

|\tilde{U}|=|U^{\prime}_{0}|^{(1-1/s)^{s\log(s)}}\leq|U^{\prime}_{0}|^{1/s}\leq|\tilde{V}|^{1/s}.

(2.4)

Now we check that
\tilde{G}
satisfies the conditions we claimed. Using (
2.1
) from the first procedure, we can check that
|U_{\ell}|\geq|U_{0}|^{\frac{2s-3}{6s-3}}
. It is a consequence of the second procedure terminating in at most
s\log s
iterations that
|\tilde{U}|\geq|U^{\prime}_{0}|^{(1-1/s)^{s\log(s)}}
. Hence
|\tilde{U}|\geq|U|^{c_{s}}
is satisfied for small enough
c_{s}
.

It is easy to see that
|V_{i}|\geq|U_{i}|^{2-1/s}
are guaranteed throughout the first procedure, and
|\tilde{V}|\geq|U^{\prime}|^{2-1/s}\geq|\tilde{U}|^{2-1/s}
. So condition (I) is satisfied. We can inductively check that
|E_{i}|\geq\delta|V_{i}|
in the first procedure. It is clear from the second procedure that
|\tilde{E}|
and
|E_{\ell}|
only differ by a multiplicative constant depending on
s
. This verifies condition (II) for small enough
c_{s}
. Similarly, we can verify condition (III) using (
2.2
).

Finally, if the second procedure terminated with (
2.3
), we can compute

\displaystyle|\tilde{U}|\Delta_{\tilde{G}}(\tilde{U})^{1-1/s}

\displaystyle\leq|\tilde{U}|\left(\frac{2|\tilde{E}|}{|\tilde{U}|^{1-1/s}}\right)^{1-1/s}=\left(|\tilde{U}|^{2-1/s}\right)^{1/s}\left(2|\tilde{E}|\right)^{1-1/s}\leq|\tilde{V}|^{1/s}\left(2|\tilde{E}|\right)^{1-1/s}

\displaystyle\leq\left(c_{s}^{-1}\delta^{-1}|\tilde{E}|\right)^{1/s}\left(2|\tilde{E}|\right)^{1-1/s}=2\cdot\left(2c_{s}\delta\right)^{-1/s}|\tilde{E}|.

If the second procedure terminated with (
2.4
), we have

|\tilde{U}|\Delta_{\tilde{G}}(\tilde{U})^{1-1/s}\leq|\tilde{V}|^{1/s}|\tilde{V}|^{1-1/s}\leq|\tilde{V}|\leq c_{s}^{-1}\delta^{-1}|\tilde{E}|.

Hence we can verify condition (IV) for small enough
c_{s}
. Overall, the proof is finished.
∎

The following proposition is the main part of Theorem
1.1
. Its proof follows the argument of Theorem 4.1 in the paper
[
5
]
by Conlon, Janzer, and Lee.

Proposition 2.2
.

For integers
t\geq s>1
, there exists a sufficiently large constant
\delta
such that the following holds. If a bipartite graph
G=(U\sqcup V,E)
satisfies the conditions for
\tilde{G}=(\tilde{U}\sqcup\tilde{V},\tilde{E})
in Lemma
2.1
with parameters
s
,
c_{s}
, and
\delta
, then
G
contains a copy of
K_{s,t}^{\prime}
.

Proof.

For a contradiction, suppose that
G
does not contain a copy of
K_{s,t}^{\prime}
. Write
|U|=m
,
|V|=n
, and
|E|=e
. We consider a weighted graph
W
with vertex set being
U
and each pair
\{u,v\}\subset U
is assigned with weight

W(u,v)=|N_{G}(\{u,v\})|.

Here, the
neighborhood

N_{G}(\{u,v\})
is the set of vertices adjacent to all of
\{u,v\}
in
G
. We say that
\{u,v\}
is a
light edge
if
1\leq W(u,v)<\binom{s+t}{2}
, and is a
heavy edge
if
W(u,v)\geq\binom{s+t}{2}
.

Claim 2.3
.

If
W(U):=\sum_{\{u,v\}\in\binom{U}{2}}W(u,v)\geq 8(s+t+1)^{2}n
, then the number of light edges in
W
is at least
\frac{W(U)}{4(s+t+1)^{3}}
.

This claim follows from Lemma 10 in
[
17
]
(see also Lemma 4.3 in
[
5
]
) so we omit its proof. Roughly speaking, if we have an abundant number of heavy edges, then we can embed the complete subdivision graph
K_{s+t}^{\prime}
(which contains
K_{s,t}^{\prime}
) into
G
by a greedy process. Consequently, most weights should contribute to light edges, hence creating many of them.

Using double counting and Jensen’s inequality, we can compute
W(U)\geq n\binom{e/n}{2}\geq\frac{e^{2}}{4n}
. Using condition (II) from Lemma
2.1
, we can guarantee the hypothesis of Claim
2.3
by taking
\delta
to be sufficiently large relative to
s,t,c_{s}
.

For distinct
u_{1},\dots,u_{s}\in U
, we write
N^{\prime}_{W}(u_{1},\dots,u_{s})
for the set of
x\in U\setminus\{u_{1},\dots,u_{s}\}
for which there exist distinct
v_{1},\dots,v_{s}\in V
such that
v_{i}\in N_{G}(\{u_{i},x\})
. We claim the following estimate:

\sum_{\tau\in\binom{U}{s}}|N^{\prime}_{W}(\tau)|\geq\Omega\left(\frac{e^{2s}}{m^{s-1}n^{s}}\right).

(2.5)

We prove this by counting the number of
(s+1)
-tuples
(x,u_{1},\dots,u_{s})\in U^{s+1}
such that

(i)

Each
xu_{i}
is a light edge in
W
.

(ii)

For any
i\neq j
, we have
N_{G}(x)\cap N_{G}(u_{i})\cap N_{G}(u_{j})=\emptyset
.

For any
x\in U
, let
d_{1}(x)
be the number of light edges adjacent to
x
in
W
. Then by Claim
2.3
, we have
\sum_{x}d_{1}(x)\geq\Omega(e^{2}/n)
. The number of
(s+1)
-tuples satisfying (i) is

\sum_{x}d_{1}^{s}(x)\geq m\left(\frac{\sum_{x}d_{1}(x)}{m}\right)^{s}\geq\Omega\left(\frac{e^{2s}}{m^{s-1}n^{s}}\right).

On the other hand, we can enumerate all the
(s+1)
-tuples not satisfying (ii). First, we choose a pair
i,j
that violates (ii) which has
s^{2}
options. Second, we choose
x
from
U
which has
m
options. Then, we choose
v\in N_{G}(x)
that is adjacent to the potential
u_{i},u_{j}
, and we have at most
\Delta(U)
options. Next, we choose
u_{i},u_{j}
from
N_{G}(v)
which have at most
\Delta(V)^{2}
options. Finally, we choose the remaining
u_{i}
’s as neighbors of neighbors of
x
, and this gives at most
(\Delta(U)\Delta(V))^{s-2}
options. Hence the number of
(s+1)
-tuples satisfying (i) but not (ii) is at most

s^{2}\cdot m\cdot\Delta(U)^{s-1}\cdot\Delta(V)^{s}\leq s^{2}\cdot\left(c_{s}^{-s}\delta^{-sc_{s}}\frac{e^{s}}{m^{s-1}}\right)\cdot\left(c_{s}^{-1}\frac{e^{s}}{n^{s}}\right)\leq\sum_{x}d_{1}^{s}(x).

Here, the first inequality can be checked using conditions (III) and (IV) from Lemma
2.1
, and the second inequality follows from choosing a sufficiently large
\delta
. Notice that any
(s+1)
-tuple satisfying (i) and (ii) implies
x\in N^{\prime}_{W}(u_{1},\dots,u_{s})
, so we can conclude (
2.5
).

We also need the following fact about
K_{s,t}^{\prime}
-copy-free graphs. We omit the proof as it is almost identical to Corollary 4.6 in
[
5
]
.

Claim 2.4
.

Let
G
be a
K_{s,t}^{\prime}
-copy-free bipartite graph with vertex bipartition
U\sqcup V
. Suppose that for some distinct
u_{1},\dots,u_{s}\in U
,
|N_{W}^{\prime}(u_{1},\dots,u_{s})|\geq C
for some sufficiently large
C
depending on
s,t
. Then there exist
v\in V
,
1\leq k\leq s
, and
\Omega(|N_{W}^{\prime}(u_{1},\dots,u_{s})|^{s})
many
s
-sets
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{s}\}\subset N_{W}^{\prime}(u_{1},\dots,u_{s})
such that
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{s}\}\cup\{u_{k}\}\subset N_{G}(v)
and no
x_{i}x_{j}
is a heavy edge.

Using (
2.5
),
e\geq c_{s}\delta n
, and
n\geq m^{2-1/s}
(from Lemma
2.1
), we can guarantee that

\sum_{\tau\in\binom{U}{s}}|N^{\prime}_{W}(\tau)|\geq\Omega\left(\frac{e^{2s}}{m^{s-1}n^{s}}\right)\geq 2Cm^{s},

by taking
\delta
sufficiently large relative to
C
, where
C
is the constant required in Claim
2.4
. As a consequence of this inequality, writing
U_{s}^{\prime}
as the collection of
\tau\in\binom{U}{s}
with
|N^{\prime}_{W}(\tau)|\geq C
, we have

\sum_{\tau\in U_{s}^{\prime}}|N^{\prime}_{W}(\tau)|\geq\Omega\left(\frac{e^{2s}}{m^{s-1}n^{s}}\right).

By Claim
2.4
, for each
\tau=\{u_{1},\dots,u_{s}\}\in U_{s}^{\prime}
, there exist
v\in V
, some
1\leq k\leq s
, and
\Omega(|N^{\prime}_{W}(\tau)|^{s})
many
s
-sets
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{s}\}\subset N^{\prime}_{W}(\tau)
such that
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{s}\}\cup\{u_{k}\}\subset N_{G}(v)
and no
x_{i}x_{j}
is a heavy edge. We use this information to estimate the number of
2s
-tuples
(x_{1},\dots,x_{s},u_{1},\dots,u_{s})\in U^{2s}
with the following properties:

(i)

All
x_{i}
and
u_{j}
are distinct.

(ii)

There exist
v\in V
and
1\leq k\leq s
such that
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{s}\}\cup\{u_{k}\}\subset N_{G}(v)
.

(iii)

For each
i,j
,
N_{G}(x_{i})\cap N_{G}(u_{j})\neq\emptyset
.

(iv)

No
x_{i}x_{j}
determines a heavy edge in
W
.

By Jensen’s inequality and the lower bound above, the number of such
2s
-tuples is at least

|U^{\prime}_{s}|\cdot\Omega\left(\left(\frac{\sum_{\tau\in U_{s}^{\prime}}|N^{\prime}_{W}(\tau)|}{|U^{\prime}_{s}|}\right)^{s}\right)\geq\Omega\left(m^{s}\left(\frac{\frac{e^{2s}}{m^{s-1}n^{s}}}{m^{s}}\right)^{s}\right)\geq\omega\left(n\Delta(V)^{2s}\right).

Here, the last inequality is checked by conditions (I)–(III) from Lemma
2.1
, and the
\omega
-notation is understood as
m\to\infty
. On the other hand, there are at most
ns\Delta(V)^{s+1}
ways to choose
v,k,x_{1},\dots,x_{s},u_{k}
such that property (ii) holds. Thus for at least one such choice, there are at least
\omega(\Delta(V)^{s-1})
ways to extend it to a suitable
2s
-tuple. Therefore, we conclude that there exist distinct
x_{1},\dots,x_{s}\in U
such that no
x_{i}x_{j}
is heavy and the number of
u\in U
with
N_{G}(x_{i})\cap N_{G}(u)\neq\emptyset
for all
i
is at least
\omega(\Delta(V))
.

Finally, we argue the existence of
K_{s,t}^{\prime}
based on these
x_{1},\dots,x_{s}
. Since no
x_{i}x_{j}
is heavy, we have
|\bigcup_{i<j}(N_{G}(x_{i})\cap N_{G}(x_{j}))|=O(1)
, so the number of
u\in U
with
N_{G}(x_{i})\cap N_{G}(x_{j})\cap N_{G}(u)\neq\emptyset
for some
1\leq i\leq j\leq s
is
O(\Delta(V))
. Hence there is a set
A\subset U
of
\omega(\Delta(V))
many vertices distinct from
x_{1},\dots,x_{s}
, such that for each
u\in A
there are distinct
v_{1},\dots,v_{s}\in V
with
v_{i}\in N_{G}(x_{i})\cap N_{G}(u)
. Take a maximal subset
A^{\prime}=\{u_{1},\dots,u_{r}\}\subset A
such that there exist distinct
w_{ij}\in N_{G}(x_{i})\cap N_{G}(u_{j})
for
1\leq i\leq s
and
1\leq j\leq r
. If
r\geq t
, we have found a copy of
K_{s,t}^{\prime}
inside
G
. So we assume that
r<t
. For any
u\in A\setminus A^{\prime}
, there exists distinct
v_{i}\in N_{G}(x_{i})\cap N_{G}(v)
. By the maximality of
A^{\prime}
, we must have
v_{i}=w_{jk}
for some
i,j,k
. So, we have
u\in N_{G}(w_{jk})
for this
j,k
. Therefore,

|A|\leq|A^{\prime}|+|\bigcup_{1\leq i\leq s,~1\leq j\leq r}N_{G}(w_{ij})|\leq t+st\Delta(V),

which contradicts
|A|\geq\omega(\Delta(V))
. This concludes the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Without loss of generality, we can assume
s\leq t
. Our main goal is to show that for any
\epsilon>0
there exists
\delta
such that every bipartite graph
G=(U\sqcup V,E)
with
|V|\geq\epsilon|U|^{\sigma_{s}}
and
|E|\geq\delta|V|
must contain a copy of
K_{s,t}^{\prime}
.

The
s=1
case is simple: we can delete every vertex
v\in V
with degree
d(v)<t
while the resulting graph still contains half of its original edges (due to a large enough
\delta
); then we can find a vertex
u\in U
with
d(u)\geq t
and greedily embed a copy of
K_{1,t}^{\prime}
using
N_{G}(a)
. It is easy to argue that any number smaller than
\sigma_{1}=1
cannot be a linear threshold of
K_{1,2}^{\prime}
. For example, we can add isolated vertices to the right hand side of a star graph
K_{m,1}
.

To deal with the
s\geq 2
case, we observe that by an averaging argument, a bipartite graph
G=(U\sqcup V,E)
with
|V|\geq\epsilon|U|^{\sigma}
and
|E|\geq\delta|V|
contains a subgraph
G^{\prime}=(U^{\prime}\sqcup V^{\prime},E^{\prime})
with
|V^{\prime}|\geq|U^{\prime}|^{\sigma}
and
|E^{\prime}|\geq\epsilon^{\frac{1}{\sigma}}\delta|V^{\prime}|
. Hence, we can apply Lemma
2.1
and Proposition
2.2
to
G^{\prime}
.

The “Moreover” part follows from a simple probabilistic construction: include each edge in an
m
-by-
n
bipartite random graph with probability
p
independently, and delete a vertex on the left hand side from each
K_{s,t}^{\prime}
in the random graph. We can upper bound the expected number of
K_{s,t}^{\prime}
in this random graph by
O(m^{s+t}n^{st}p^{2st})
, and we notice the expected number of edges is
mnp
. Hence by setting

m^{s+t}n^{st}p^{2st}\cdot n\ll mnp\quad\text{and}\quad n\ll mnp,

we can guarantee the resulting graph contains
\omega(n)
many edges and is
K_{s,t}^{\prime}
-copy-free. These condition can be satisfied with
n\ll m^{2-1/s-1/t}
. Hence it suffices to take
1/t<\sigma_{s}-\sigma
to conclude
\sigma
is smaller than the linear threshold of
K_{s,t}^{\prime}
.
∎

3
Incidence bounds via linear thresholds

We outline a proof of Theorem
1.2
in this section for the sake of completeness. This theorem is essentially due to Fox, Pach, Sheffer, Suk, and Zahl
[
11
]
where the special case
H=K_{s,t}
and
\sigma=s
is proven as their Theorem 1.2. In particular, all our “variety”-related notions are identical to those in
[
11
]
, which are standard in the study of real algebraic geometry. The proof is an induction on the following more general statement.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
H
be a bipartite graph with its linear threshold at most
\sigma
. Suppose
P
is a set of
m
points and
\mathcal{V}
is a set of
n
constant-degree algebraic varieties, both in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, such that the incidence graph
I(P,\mathcal{V})
does not contain a copy of
H
. Suppose furthermore that
P
is fully contained in an irreducible variety
V
of dimension
e
and degree
D
, and no member
S\in\mathcal{V}
contains
V
. Then for every
\epsilon>0
and as
m,n\to\infty
, we have

|I(P,\mathcal{V})|\leq\alpha_{1,e}m^{\frac{(e-1)\sigma}{e\sigma-1}+\epsilon}n^{\frac{e(\sigma-1)}{e\sigma-1}}+\alpha_{2,e}(m+n),

(3.1)

where
\alpha_{1,e}
and
\alpha_{2,e}
are constants depending on
H,\sigma,d,e,D,\epsilon
and the degrees of varieties in
\mathcal{V}
.

Proof.

We will induct both on
e
and
m+n
. Clearly, one can take
V=\mathbb{R}^{d}
in the above statement to conclude Theorem
1.2
. First, we consider the base case for the induction. If
m+n
is small, then (
3.1
) is implied by choosing sufficiently large
\alpha_{1,e}
and
\alpha_{2,e}
. Similarly, when
e=0
, (
3.1
) is also guaranteed by choosing sufficiently large
\alpha_{1,0}
and
\alpha_{2,0}
.

By the definition of linear threshold, we can assume without loss of generality that
n<m^{\sigma}
. Otherwise, there exists a constant
\delta
depending on
H
such that
|I(P,\mathcal{V})|<\delta n
and we can take
\alpha_{2,e}>\delta
to ensure (
3.1
). In particular, we have

n=n^{\frac{e-1}{e\sigma-1}}n^{\frac{e(\sigma-1)}{e\sigma-1}}<m^{\frac{(e-1)\sigma}{e\sigma-1}}n^{\frac{e(\sigma-1)}{e\sigma-1}}.

(3.2)

Next, we consider the inductive step. Assume that
(
3.1
)
holds when
|P|+|\mathcal{V}|<m+n
or
\dim V<e
. By polynomial partitioning (Theorem 4.2 in
[
11
]
, see also
[
15
]
), we find a polynomial
f
of degree at most
C_{part}\cdot r^{1/e}
, where
C_{part}
depends only on
e
and
r
shall be determined later, such that
f
does not completely vanish on
V
and no connected components of
V\setminus Z(f)
contains more than
(1/r)
-fraction of
P
.

Let
\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2},\dots,\Omega_{z}
be the connected components of
V\setminus Z(f)
. By a Milnor–Thom-type result (e.g. Theorem A.2 in
[
28
]
), there exists a constant
C_{cell}
depending on
d,e,D
such that
z\leq C_{cell}\cdot r
. We divide the edge set of
I(P,\mathcal{V})
into three subsets:

•

I_{1}
consists of the incidences
(p,S)
such that
p\in Z(f)
and
S
properly intersects every irreducible component of
V\cap Z(f)
that contains
p
.

•

I_{2}
consists of the incidences
(p,S)
such that
p
is contained in an irreducible component of
V\cap Z(f)
that is fully contained by
S
.

•

I_{3}
consists of incidences not in
I_{1}
or
I_{2}
, that is, all of
(p,S)
such that
p
is not contained in
V\cap Z(f)
.

Bounding
|I_{1}|
: Let
V^{\prime}=V\cap Z(f)
and let
m_{0}=|P\cap V^{\prime}|
. Since
f
does not vanish on
V
,
V^{\prime}
is a variety of dimension
e^{\prime}\leq e-1
and can be written as a union of
\gamma_{1}
irreducible (over
\mathbb{R}
) components, each of dimension at most
e^{\prime}
and degree at most
\gamma_{2}
. Here
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
only depends
D
,
e
,
d
and the degree of
f
(see e.g.
[
14
]
). We can apply the inductive hypothesis to obtain

|I_{1}|\leq\gamma_{1}\cdot\alpha_{1,e-1}m_{0}^{\frac{(e-2)\sigma}{(e-1)\sigma-1}+\epsilon}n^{\frac{(e-1)(\sigma-1)}{(e-1)\sigma-1}}+\alpha_{2,e-1}(m_{0}+n).

Here, the constants
\alpha_{1,e-1}
and
\alpha_{2,e-1}
are independent of
m
and
n
. Using
n<m^{\sigma}
, we can check that

m^{\frac{(e-2)\sigma}{(e-1)\sigma-1}}n^{\frac{(e-1)(\sigma-1)}{(e-1)\sigma-1}}\leq m^{\frac{(e-1)\sigma}{e\sigma-1}}n^{\frac{e(\sigma-1)}{e\sigma-1}}.

(A detailed computation can be found as equation (12) in
[
11
]
.) Combining above inequalities, absorbing the
a_{2,e-1}\cdot n
term by (
3.2
), and choosing sufficiently large
\alpha_{1,e},\alpha_{2,e}
, we can conclude

|I_{1}|\leq\frac{\alpha_{1,e}}{3}m^{\frac{(e-1)\sigma}{e\sigma-1}+\epsilon}n^{\frac{e(\sigma-1)}{e\sigma-1}}+\frac{\alpha_{2,e}}{2}m_{0}.

(3.3)

Bounding
|I_{2}|
: Denote the vertex set of
H
as
A\sqcup B
. Note that
V^{\prime}
can be written as a union of
\gamma_{1}
irreducible (over
\mathbb{R}
) components, each of dimension at most
e-1
, where
\gamma_{1}
depends only on
D
,
e
,
d
and the degree of
f
. Since the incidence graph of
I(P,\mathcal{V})
does not contain a copy of
H
, each of the irreducible components of
V^{\prime}
either contains less than
|A|
points of
P
, or is contained in less than
|B|
members of
\mathcal{V}
. The contribution to
|I_{2}|
from the first scenario is at most
|A|\gamma_{1}n
, and from the second scenario is at most
|B|m_{0}
. Combining these bounds with (
3.2
) and choosing
\alpha_{1,e},\alpha_{2,e}
to be sufficiently large depending on
H
and
\gamma_{1}
, we have

|I_{2}|\leq\frac{\alpha_{1,e}}{3}m^{\frac{(e-1)s}{es-1}+\epsilon}n^{\frac{e(s-1)}{es-1}}+\frac{\alpha_{2,e}}{2}m_{0}.

(3.4)

Bounding
|I_{3}|
: Let
P_{i}:=P\cap\Omega_{i}
and
\mathcal{V}_{i}
be the set of elements in
\mathcal{V}
that properly intersect
\Omega_{i}
. Write
m_{i}=|P_{i}|
and
n_{i}=|\mathcal{V}_{i}|
. By the property of the polynomial
f
, we have an upper bound
m_{i}\leq m/r
. Note also that
m^{\prime}:=\sum_{i=1}^{z}m_{i}=m-m_{0}
. By a Bézout-type theorem, see e.g.
[
28
]
Theorem A.2, and proper intersection, there exists a constant
C_{inter}
such that

\sum_{i=1}^{z}n_{i}\leq C_{inter}\cdot\kappa nr^{(e-1)/e},

where
\kappa
is the maximum degree of the varieties in
\mathcal{V}
. By Hölder’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{z}n_{i}^{\frac{e(\sigma-1)}{e\sigma-1}}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{i=1}^{z}n_{i}\right)^{\frac{e(\sigma-1)}{e\sigma-1}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{z}1\right)^{\frac{e-1}{e\sigma-1}}

\displaystyle\leq\left(C_{inter}\cdot\kappa nr^{(e-1)/e}\right)^{\frac{e(\sigma-1)}{e\sigma-1}}\left(C_{cell}\cdot r\right)^{\frac{e-1}{e\sigma-1}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{Hold}\cdot n^{\frac{e(\sigma-1)}{e\sigma-1}}r^{\frac{(e-1)\sigma}{e\sigma-1}},

where
C_{Hold}
depends on
\kappa,C_{inter},C_{cell},e,\sigma
.

By the induction hypothesis, we have

\displaystyle|I_{3}|=\sum_{i=1}^{z}|I(P_{i},\mathcal{V}_{i})|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{z}\left(\alpha_{1,e}m_{i}^{\frac{(e-1)\sigma}{e\sigma-1}+\epsilon}n_{i}^{\frac{e(\sigma-1)}{e\sigma-1}}+\alpha_{2,e}(m_{i}+n_{i})\right)

\displaystyle\leq\alpha_{1,e}m^{\frac{(e-1)\sigma}{e\sigma-1}+\epsilon}\left(r^{\frac{(e-1)\sigma}{e\sigma-1}+\epsilon}\right)^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{z}n_{i}^{\frac{e(\sigma-1)}{e\sigma-1}}+\sum_{i=1}^{z}\alpha_{2,e}(m_{i}+n_{i})

\displaystyle=\alpha_{1,e}C_{Hold}\cdot r^{-\epsilon}m^{\frac{(e-1)\sigma}{e\sigma-1}+\epsilon}n^{\frac{e(\sigma-1)}{e\sigma-1}}+\alpha_{2,e}m^{\prime}+\alpha_{2,e}nC_{inter}\cdot\kappa r^{(e-1)/e}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\alpha_{1,e}}{3}m^{\frac{(e-1)\sigma}{e\sigma-1}+\epsilon}n^{\frac{e(\sigma-1)}{e\sigma-1}}+\alpha_{2,e}m^{\prime}.

(3.5)

Here in the last step, we first choose
r
to be sufficiently large depending on
\epsilon
and
C_{Hold}
such that
C_{Hold}\cdot r^{-\epsilon}\leq 1/6
. Then we use (
3.2
) and choose
\alpha_{1,e}
to be sufficiently large depending on
r,C_{inter},\kappa,\alpha_{2,e}
, absorbing the last term into the first term.

We conclude the inductive process by combining (
3.3
), (
3.4
), and (
3.5
).
∎

4
Grid-free complex point-line arrangements

In this section, we prove the theorem below from which Theorem
1.3
follows as a corollary. Our proof is inspired by a constant-degree-polynomial-partitioning technique of Solymosi and Tao
[
28
]
. Here, a
2-flat
refers to a 2-dimensional affine subspace.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
P
be a set of
m
points and
\mathcal{F}
be a set of
n
2-flats, both in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
, such that every pair of 2-flats in
\mathcal{F}
has at most one intersection, and the incidence graph
I(P,\mathcal{F})
does not contain a copy of
K_{s,t}^{\prime}
. Then for every
\epsilon>0
, we have

|I(P,\mathcal{F})|\leq C_{1}m^{\frac{2s-1}{3s-2}+\epsilon}n^{\frac{2s-2}{3s-2}}+C_{2}(m+n),

(4.1)

where
C_{1},C_{2}
are large constants depending on
s,t,\epsilon
.

Proof.

We shall prove (
4.1
) by induction on
m
, where
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are to be chosen later. The claim holds trivially for
m=0
. We assume that
m\geq 1
and (
4.1
) holds for smaller values in place of
m
.

Observe that we may restrict our attention to values of
n
such that

m^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq n\leq m^{2-\frac{1}{s}}.

After choosing
C_{2}
to be large enough (depending on
s,t
), the first inequality comes from the Kővári–Sós–Turán bound together with the hypothesis that two flats intersect at most once (see Section 3 in
[
28
]
), and the second inequality comes from our Theorem
1.1
.

We may assume that every point has at least half of the average incident 2-flats. If a point is incident to fewer 2-flats, we delete it. By polynomial partitioning (Corollary 5.5 in
[
28
]
, see also
[
15
]
), there exists a degree
D<(12M)^{1/4}
polynomial
Q
which partitions
\mathbb{R}^{4}\setminus\{Q=0\}
into
M
components

\mathbb{R}^{4}=\{Q=0\}\cup\Omega_{1}\cup\dots\cup\Omega_{M}

such that no component contains more than
m/M
points, where M is a constant depending on
\epsilon
and
s
to be determined later.

By the Harnack curve theorem
[
16
]
, each 2-flat not on the surface
\{Q=0\}
meets at most
D^{2}
cells in
\{\Omega_{i}\}
. Let
\mathcal{F}_{i}
denote the set of 2-flats in
\mathcal{F}
intersecting
\Omega_{i}
, then we have

\sum_{i=1}^{M}|\mathcal{F}_{i}|\leq D^{2}n.

Now using the induction hypothesis, we have

\displaystyle|I(P\cap\Omega_{i},\mathcal{F}_{i})|

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}|P\cap\Omega_{i}|^{\frac{2s-1}{3s-2}+\epsilon}|\mathcal{F}_{i}|^{\frac{2s-2}{3s-2}}+C_{2}\left(\frac{m}{M}+|\mathcal{F}_{i}|\right)

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\left(\frac{m}{M}\right)^{\frac{2s-1}{3s-2}+\epsilon}|\mathcal{F}_{i}|^{\frac{2s-2}{3s-2}}+C_{2}\left(\frac{m}{M}+|\mathcal{F}_{i}|\right).

Also, by Hölder’s inequality, we have

\sum_{i=1}^{M}|\mathcal{F}_{i}|^{\frac{2s-2}{3s-2}}\leq\left(\sum_{i}|\mathcal{F}_{i}|\right)^{\frac{2s-2}{3s-2}}M^{\frac{s}{3s-2}}\leq\left(D^{2}n\right)^{\frac{2s-2}{3s-2}}M^{\frac{s}{3s-2}}.

From these, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{M}|I(P\cap\Omega_{i},\mathcal{F}_{i})|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{M}\left[C_{1}\left(\frac{m}{M}\right)^{\frac{2s-1}{3s-2}+\epsilon}|\mathcal{F}_{i}|^{\frac{2s-2}{3s-2}}+C_{2}\left(\frac{m}{M}+|\mathcal{F}_{i}|\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\left(\frac{m}{M}\right)^{\frac{2s-1}{3s-2}+\epsilon}\sum_{i=1}^{M}|\mathcal{F}_{i}|^{\frac{2s-2}{3s-2}}+C_{2}m+C_{2}\sum_{i=1}^{M}|\mathcal{F}_{i}|

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}12^{\frac{s-1}{3s-2}}M^{-\epsilon}m^{\frac{2s-1}{3s-2}+\epsilon}n^{\frac{2s-2}{3s-2}}+C_{2}\left(m+D^{2}n\right),

where we used that
D\leq(12M)^{1/4}
.

If we choose
M
large enough, say
M\geq(4\times 12^{1/3})^{\frac{1}{\epsilon}}
, we get

\sum_{i=1}^{M}|I(P\cap\Omega_{i},\mathcal{F}_{i})|\leq\frac{1}{2}C_{1}m^{\frac{2s-1}{3s-2}+\epsilon}n^{\frac{2s-2}{3s-2}}+C_{2}\left(m+D^{2}n\right).

Notice that

m^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq n\leq m^{2-\frac{1}{s}}\implies m^{\frac{2s-1}{3s-2}}n^{\frac{2s-2}{3s-2}}\geq\max\{m,n\}.

Thus, we can guarantee

\frac{1}{3}C_{1}m^{\frac{2s-1}{3s-2}+\epsilon}n^{\frac{2s-2}{3s-2}}\geq C_{2}(m+D^{2}n),

by taking
C_{1}
to be a large constant depending on
M
and
C_{2}
. Thus, the second term can be absorbed into the first term. Therefore,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{M}|I(P\cap\Omega_{i},\mathcal{F}_{i})|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}C_{1}m^{\frac{2s-1}{3s-2}+\epsilon}n^{\frac{2s-2}{3s-2}}+\frac{1}{3}C_{1}m^{\frac{2s-1}{3s-2}+\epsilon}n^{\frac{2s-2}{3s-2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{5}{6}C_{1}m^{\frac{2s-1}{3s-2}+\epsilon}n^{\frac{2s-2}{3s-2}}.

(4.2)

Next, we estimate the incidences on the surface
\{Q=0\}
. In order to avoid singular points on
\{Q=0\}
, we perform a technical decomposition by introducing the following sequence of hypersurfaces
S_{0},S_{1},\dots,S_{D}
and setting
S_{0}:=\{Q=0\}
,
Q_{0}:=Q
, and

Q_{i+1}=\sum_{j=1}^{4}\alpha_{j}^{(i)}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}Q_{i}\quad\text{for $1\leq i\leq D$,}

which defines the surface
S_{i+1}=\{Q_{i+1}=0\}
, where the
\alpha_{j}^{(i)}
are generic reals. All
S_{i}
have degree at most
D
, and for each point
p\in P\cap\{Q=0\}
, there is an index
i=\text{ind}(p)
between
0
and
D
which is the smallest
i
such that
p\notin S_{i+1}
. We remark that this makes
p
a smooth point of
S_{i}
, so that the tangent space to
S_{i}
at
p
is three-dimensional. Thus, the tangent space to
S_{i}
at
p
may contain at most one 2-flat in
\mathcal{F}
, and at most one 2-flat in
\mathcal{F}
incident to
p
is entirely contained in
S_{i}
. All other 2-flats incident to
p
intersect
S_{i}
in a one-dimensional curve. We consider the set of points
P_{i}=\{p\in P:\ \text{ind}(p)=i\}
and bound the number of incidences between
P_{i}
and
\mathcal{F}
. The 2-flats on
S_{i}
give at most
|P_{i}|
incidences, so it is sufficient to consider the incidences between
P_{i}
and the set of 2-flats intersecting
S_{i}
in a curve. We call these intersection curves
\mathcal{V}_{i}
for every
0\leq i\leq D
. The curves in
\mathcal{V}_{i}
are one-dimensional algebraic curves of some constant degree (depending on
D
). Let us project the points
P_{i}
and the curves
\mathcal{V}_{i}
onto a generic plane, and apply Theorem
1.2
with
d=2
(because of projection) and
\sigma=2-1/s
(because of Theorem
1.1
). Thus, for each
0\leq i\leq D
we obtain

|I(P_{i},\mathcal{V}_{i})|\leq C_{3}\left(|P_{i}|^{\frac{2s-1}{3s-2}+\epsilon}|\mathcal{V}_{i}|^{\frac{2s-2}{3s-2}}+|P_{i}|+|\mathcal{V}_{i}|\right),

where
C_{3}
is a constant depending on
D
,
\epsilon
,
s
, and
t
. Thus, we have that the number of incidences on
\{Q=0\}
is at most

\sum_{i=0}^{D}|I(P_{i},\mathcal{V}_{i})|+\sum_{i=0}^{D}|P_{i}|\leq C_{3}(D+1)\left(m^{\frac{2s-1}{3s-2}+\epsilon^{\prime}}n^{\frac{2s-2}{3s-2}}+m+n\right)+m

(4.3)

Thus, if we choose constants
C_{1}
,
C_{2}
to be sufficiently large depending on
C_{3}
and
D
, we can upper bound the quantity in (
4.3
) by one-sixth of the first term in (
4.1
). Therefore, we can combine (
4.2
) and (
4.3
) to conclude the inductive proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Observe that, after a point-line duality (see Section 5.1 of
[
23
]
), an
s
-by-
s
grid corresponds to a copy of
K_{s,s}^{\prime}
in the incidence graph. Identify
\mathbb{C}
with
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and apply Theorem
4.1
to
P
,
L
, and
\mathbb{C}^{2}=\mathbb{R}^{4}
to obtain

\displaystyle|I(P,L)|

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}n^{\frac{2s-1}{3s-2}+\epsilon}n^{\frac{2s-2}{3s-2}}+C_{2}(n+n)

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}n^{\frac{4s-3}{3s-2}+\epsilon}+2C_{2}n

\displaystyle\leq C_{4}n^{\frac{4}{3}-\frac{1}{9s-6}+\epsilon},

where
C_{4}
is some sufficiently large constant depending on
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
.
∎

5
Distinct distances under local conditions

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.4
by transforming it into a point-variety incidence problem in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
, thereby allowing us to apply Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
. We remark that each incidence in our framework will correspond to a distance repetition, i.e. two pairs of points with the same distance, but the relationship between the number of distinct distances and the number of distance repetitions is subtle. For example, if we are given distance repetitions
|ab|=|cd|
,
|cd|=|xy|
, and
|ab|=|xy|
, then the third repetition gives no useful information. To overcome this subtlety, we leverage the fact that our
K_{s,t}^{\prime}
-free incidence bound does not depend on
t
asymptotically (together with some bookkeeping).

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

Let
P
be the set of
n
points such that every
p
points determine at least
q
distinct distances, where

q=\binom{p}{2}-p+3\cdot\left\lfloor\frac{p}{2s}\right\rfloor+2s+2.

We define

\mathcal{E}=\{(a,b,c,d)\in P^{4}:|ac|=|bd|>0\}

where
|ab|
denotes the distance between
a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Suppose the number of distinct distances determined by
P
is
r
and suppose these distances are
d_{1},\ldots,d_{r}
. We write
E_{i}=\{(a,b)\in P^{2}:|ab|=d_{i}\}
so that
\sum_{i=1}^{r}|E_{i}|=n(n-1)
. Using Jensen’s inequality, we have

|\mathcal{E}|=\sum_{i=1}^{r}|E_{i}|^{2}\geq\frac{1}{r}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{r}|E_{i}|\right)^{2}=\Omega\left(\frac{n^{4}}{r}\right).

Consequently, it suffices for us to prove that

|\mathcal{E}|\leq O\left(n^{\frac{20}{7}-\frac{18}{7(7s-4)}+\epsilon}\right).

(5.1)

To this end, for every pair
(a,b)\in P^{2}
, we define a corresponding point in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
by
v_{a,b}=(a_{x},a_{y},b_{x},b_{y})
. Here
a_{x}
(respectively
a_{y}
) is the
x
-coordinate (respectively
y
-coordinate) of the plane point
a
. We also define a quadric in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
as
Q_{a,b}:(x-a_{x})^{2}+(y-a_{y})^{2}=(z-b_{x})^{2}+(w-b_{y})^{2}
. We observe that if
v_{a,b}
lies on
Q_{c,d}
then it follows
|ac|=|bd|
. We consider a random equitable partition of the point pairs
P^{2}=P_{1}\sqcup P_{2}
, that is,
\left||P_{1}|-|P_{2}|\right|\leq 1
and we pick such a partition uniformly at random. Note that the probability for two pairs
(a,b),(c,d)\in P^{2}
to be in the same part in this partition is at least
1/2
. Define
\mathcal{P}=\{v_{a,b}:(a,b)\in P_{1}\}
and
\mathcal{Q}=\{Q_{a,b}:(a,b)\in P_{2}\}
. Then our observation, together with a basic probabilistic argument, tell us that

|\mathcal{E}|\leq 2\cdot|I(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{Q})|+O(n^{2}).

It suffices for us to show the incidence graph
I(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{Q})
does not contain a copy of
K_{s,t}^{\prime}
with
t=(2s+p)^{2}+1
. Indeed, if this is given, we apply our Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
with
d=4
and
\sigma=2-1/s
to conclude (
5.1
). For the rest of our proof, the word “point” refers to a point in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and we say a point
x
is in a vertex
v_{a,b}
(or
Q_{a,b}
) if
p\in\{a,b\}
.

For the sake of contradiction, we suppose there is a copy of
K_{s,t}^{\prime}
in
I(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{Q})
. We shall describe an iterative procedure of processing the edges of this
K_{s,t}^{\prime}
which will produce a set
\mathcal{A}
of
p
points determining less than
q
distinct distance, contradicting our hypothesis on
P
. We denote the vertex set of
K_{s,t}^{\prime}
as
S\sqcup T\sqcup K
where
|S|=s
,
|T|=t
, and,
|K|=st
under the natural identification. We let
\mathcal{S}
be the set of points in vertices of
S
. Note that we have
|\mathcal{S}|\leq 2s
. We also identify a subset
T^{\prime}\subset T
with the following property:
|T^{\prime}|=t^{\prime}>p
and there is an enumeration
v_{a_{1},b_{1}},\dots,v_{a_{t^{\prime}},b_{t^{\prime}}}
of
T^{\prime}
such that

\{a_{i},b_{i}\}\not\subset\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{i-1},b_{1},\ldots,b_{i-1}\}\cup\mathcal{S}.

The existence of such
T^{\prime}
is guaranteed by
t
being large enough.

Now we describe our edge-processing procedure. Let us write
S=\{u_{1},\dots,u_{s}\}
,
T^{\prime}=\{v_{1},\dots,v_{t^{\prime}}\}
, and
w_{i,j}
the vertex in
K
adjacent to both
v_{i}
and
u_{j}
. Initially, we set
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{S}
. During our procedure, we will add points into
\mathcal{A}
, and we will label some point pairs in
\mathcal{A}
by other pairs. When a pair
ac
is labeled by another pair
bd
, we will always have
|ac|=|bd|
, and our intuition is that
ac
will not “contribute” to the number of distinct distance determined by
\mathcal{A}
. Our procedure has at most
t^{\prime}
rounds but may terminate early. During the
i
-th round, we consider the vertices
w_{i,1},\dots,w_{i,s}
in order and process the two edges
u_{j}w_{i,j}
and
v_{i}w_{i,j}
in order. When an edge with end-vertices
v_{a,b}
and
Q_{c,d}
is processed, we add the points
a,b,c,d
into
\mathcal{A}
and try to produce a labeled pair in the following way: if both pairs
ac
and
bd
are unlabeled, we label
ac
with
bd
; otherwise, if
ac
is unlabeled and
bd
is labeled with a pair
xy
different from
ac
, we label
ac
with
xy
; otherwise, if
bd
is unlabeled and
ac
is labeled with a pair
xy
different from
bd
, we label
bd
with
xy
. Let us remark that because
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
are disjoint,
ac
and
bd
will be pairs, i.e.
a\neq c
and
b\neq d
. If at a certain step, adding the points into
\mathcal{A}
would result in
|\mathcal{A}|>p
, we terminate the whole procedure before adding the points. We can notice that
|\mathcal{A}|
increases by at most two from each edge, because there is always one end-vertex that appeared before. The properties of
T^{\prime}
guarantee that our procedure will end with
|\mathcal{A}|=p
or
|\mathcal{A}|=p-1
. In the latter case, we add an arbitrary point from
P
into
\mathcal{A}
so that we end up with a set of
p
points.

During the
i
-th round of our procedure, we use
p_{i}
to denote the number of points newly added into
\mathcal{A}
and
\ell_{i}
to denote the number of labeled pairs we produced. By analyzing our procedure through some careful bookkeeping, we can verify the following claim.

Claim 5.1
.

If
\ell_{i}\leq 2s-2
, then
p_{i}\leq\ell_{i}
.

Given this claim, we argue the
p
points in
\mathcal{A}
determine less than
q
distinct distances. We write

x=|\{i:p_{i}=2s\}|,\quad y=|\{i:p_{i}=2s+1\}|,\quad z=|\{i:p_{i}=2s+2\}|.

Note that
p_{i}\leq 2s+2
always holds. Since
2s\cdot x+(2s+1)\cdot y+(2s+2)\cdot z\leq p
, we have

x+y+z\leq\left\lfloor\frac{p}{2s}\right\rfloor.

From our Claim
5.1
, we can argue

\sum_{i}c_{i}+x+2y+3z\geq\sum_{i}p_{i}\geq p-1-|\mathcal{S}|\geq p-2s-1.

Combining these two identities, we conclude

\sum_{i}c_{i}\geq p-2s-1-3(x+y+z)\geq p-2s-1-3\cdot\left\lfloor\frac{p}{2s}\right\rfloor.

Observe that the distance of each labeled pair equals the distance of its label, which is another unlabeled point pair. Therefore, the number of distinct distances determined by the
p
points in
\mathcal{A}
is at most

\binom{p}{2}-\sum_{i}c_{i}\leq q-1,

which contradicts our hypothesis on
P
.

We devote the rest of our proof to Claim
5.1
. Suppose we have
\ell_{i}\leq 2s-2
for some index
i
, then there are
2s-\ell_{i}
many edges that did not produce any labeled pairs. We call them
bad edges
and their end-vertices in
K

bad vertices
. Note the following important property of our procedure: no new points are added into
\mathcal{A}
while processing a bad edge. Indeed, one possibility for an edge
v_{a,b}Q_{c,d}
to be bad is that
bd
is already labeled with
ac
, then these points must have appeared before and hence are already in
\mathcal{A}
. The other possibilities can be argued similarly. Therefore, the points added into
\mathcal{A}
during the
i
-th round must be in either
v_{i}
or a
good
(i.e. not bad)
w_{i,j}
(
1\leq j\leq s
). We also call
v_{i}
a good vertex as a convention.

We call a vertex
w_{i,j}

singular
if it is adjacent to exactly one bad edge. We call a point
a
in a good vertex
v_{a,b}

redundant
if
a=b
, or it is in another good vertex, or appeared in previous rounds, or in
\mathcal{S}
. The definition for redundant point is similar for
b
in
Q_{a,b}
. We proceed with our proof under the assumption that
2s-\ell_{i}
is odd: we argue that there are at most one singular vertex and no redundant points, otherwise our Claim
5.1
is true. Indeed, if there are two singular vertices, the total number of bad vertices is at least

\left\lceil\frac{2s-\ell_{i}-2}{2}\right\rceil+2=\frac{2s-\ell_{i}+3}{2}.

Hence we can compute
p_{i}<\ell_{i}
using
p_{i}
being at most twice the number of good vertices. If there is a redundant point, then
p_{i}
is at most twice the number of good vertices minus one (since we do not need to count the redundant point), which is at most

2\cdot\left(s+1-\left\lceil\frac{2s-\ell_{i}}{2}\right\rceil\right)-1=\ell_{i}.

If no bad edge is adjacent to
v_{i}=v_{a_{1},b_{1}}
, there are
2s-\ell_{i}\geq 2
singular vertices and we are done. Thus, suppose
v_{a_{1},b_{1}}Q_{a_{2},b_{2}}
is the first bad edge adjacent to
v_{i}
we encounter. Since we do not produce a labeled pair while processing this edge, we must know from previous edges that

|a_{1}a_{2}|=|c_{1}c_{2}|=|c_{3}c_{4}|=\dots=|d_{1}d_{2}|=|b_{1}b_{2}|.

(5.2)

We can assume that all equal signs here are produced by distinct good edges, otherwise this equation can be simplified. We also assume
a_{1}\neq b_{1}
otherwise there is a redundant point in
v_{i}
.

Now we consider the edge
e_{1}
that produced
|a_{1}a_{2}|=|c_{1}c_{2}|
. If
e_{1}
is processed in previous rounds, then
a_{1}
in
v_{i}
is redundant and we are done. Hence we assume
e_{1}
is processed in this round, and our proof diverges into the following cases:

•

If
e_{1}
is not adjacent to
v_{a_{1},b_{1}}
or
Q_{a_{2},b_{2}}
, we consider the end-vertex
w\in K
of
e_{1}
. Notice
w
is already adjacent to a good edge
e_{1}
, and
v_{i}w
cannot be bad otherwise
v_{a_{1},b_{1}}Q_{a_{2},b_{2}}
is not the first bad edge adjacent to
v_{i}
we encounter. Thus
w
is a good vertex, and the point
a_{1}
is either in
w
or in
\mathcal{S}
, making
a_{1}
in
v_{i}
redundant.

•

If
e_{1}
is adjacent to
v_{a_{1},b_{1}}
, upon renaming
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
, there are two possibilities. (To see they are all the cases, one may consider where
a_{1}
in
|a_{1}a_{2}|=|c_{1}c_{2}|
comes from.)

–

e_{1}=v_{a_{1},b_{1}}Q_{a_{2},c_{2}}
with
b_{1}=c_{1}
. In this case, the pairs
b_{1}b_{2}
and
c_{1}c_{2}
must be different, otherwise we must have
b_{2}=c_{2}
, making
v_{a_{1},b_{1}}Q_{a_{2},b_{2}}
simultaneously good and bad. In particular, the term
|c_{3}c_{4}|
exists in (
5.2
). Denote the edge that produced
|c_{1}c_{2}|=|c_{3}c_{4}|
as
e_{2}
and its end-vertex in
K
as
w^{\prime}
. If
e_{2}
is processed in previous rounds or
c_{1}
is not in
w^{\prime}
, then
b_{1}=c_{1}
in
v_{i}
will be redundant and we are done. Hence we assume
e_{2}
is a good edge during the
i
-th round and the point
c_{1}
is in
w^{\prime}
. Since
w^{\prime}
and
Q_{a_{2},c_{2}}
cannot both be singular, one of them must be a good vertex. If
w^{\prime}
is a good vertex,
b_{1}=c_{1}
in
v_{i}
will be redundant. If
Q_{a_{2},c_{2}}
is good,
c_{2}
in
Q_{a_{2},c_{2}}
is redundant, because the point
c_{2}
appears in the left end-vertex of
e_{2}
, hence is in
\mathcal{S}
.

–

e_{1}=v_{a_{1},b_{1}}Q_{c_{2},a_{1}}
with
a_{1}=c_{1}
and
b_{1}=a_{2}
. In this case, the pairs
b_{1}b_{2}
and
c_{1}c_{2}
must be different, otherwise we must have
c_{1}=b_{2}=a_{2}
and
c_{2}=b_{1}=a_{2}
, making the edge
v_{a_{1},b_{1}}Q_{a_{2},b_{2}}
simultaneously good and bad. Similarly,
|c_{3}c_{4}|
exists and we define
e_{2}
and
w^{\prime}
. If
e_{2}
is processed in previous rounds or
c_{1}
is not in
w^{\prime}
,
a_{1}=c_{1}
in
v_{i}
will be redundant. If
e_{2}
is a good edge during the
i
-th round and the point
c_{1}
is in
w^{\prime}
,
a_{1}=c_{1}
in
v_{i}
is still redundant because it appears in both
w^{\prime}
and
Q_{c_{2},a_{1}}
, among which at least one is good (otherwise there will be two singular vertices).

•

If
e_{1}
is adjacent to
Q_{a_{2},b_{2}}
, the point
a_{1}
must be in
Q_{a_{2},b_{2}}
, otherwise it will be in the end-vertex of
e_{1}
inside
S
, making
a_{1}
in
v_{i}
redundant. Also,
a_{1}\neq a_{2}
because
a_{1}a_{2}
is a pair. Thus, upon renaming
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
, we have
e_{1}=v_{c_{1},a_{2}}Q_{a_{2},b_{2}}
with
a_{1}=b_{2}
and
a_{2}=c_{2}
. Notice that the left end-vertex of
e_{1}
is also
v_{c_{1},c_{2}}
, hence
b_{1}\in\{c_{1},c_{2}\}
would imply
b_{1}
in
v_{i}
being redundant. Thus, we assume
b_{1}\not\in\{c_{1},c_{2}\}
and consequently the pairs
b_{1}b_{2}
and
c_{1}c_{2}
are different. In particular, the term
|d_{1}d_{2}|
exists in (
5.2
). In this case, we also know that

is the only singular vertex. Then the edge that produced
|d_{1}d_{2}|=|b_{1}b_{2}|
will make
b_{1}
in
v_{i}
redundant.

This finishes the proof under the assumption that
2s-\ell_{i}
is odd. The proof with
2s-\ell_{i}
being even follows from very similar arguments. In that case, we allow no singular vertex and wish to find two redundant points. We skip the proof for
2s-\ell_{i}
being even and conclude Claim

, hence also the proof of Theorem
1.4
.

6
Remarks

We mention three related open problems (eventually only two). The first problem asks whether we can say
\sigma_{s}=2-1/s
is indeed the linear threshold of
K_{s,t}^{\prime}
for
s\geq 1
and large
t
. Essentially, the problem concerns the dependence of
t
on
\sigma
in Theorem
1.1
.

Problem 6.1
.

Is the following true: for every integer
s\geq 1
, any
\sigma<\sigma_{s}
is less than the linear threshold of
K_{s,t}^{\prime}
for sufficiently large
t
depending only on
s
?

The second problem asks for an extension of Theorem
1.1
to hypergraph subdivisions. We use
K_{s}^{r}
to denote the complete
r
-partite
r
-uniform hypergraph with each part of
K_{s}^{r}
having
s
vertices, and we use
(K_{s}^{r})^{\prime}
to denote its subdivision:
(K_{s}^{r})^{\prime}
has left hand side vertex set
\{(i,j):1\leq i\leq r,~1\leq j\leq s\}
, right hand side vertex set
\{(j_{1},j_{2},\dots,j_{r}):1\leq j_{1},j_{2},\dots,j_{r}\leq s\}
, and two vertices
(i,j)
and
(j_{1},j_{2},\dots,j_{r})
are adjacent if and only if
j_{i}=j
.

Problem 6.2
.

Is the linear threshold of
(K_{s}^{r})^{\prime}
strictly less than
r
?

Remark 6.3
.

After an earlier version of this paper was posted on arXiv, Oliver Janzer commented that Problem
6.2
can be answered in the affirmative following the ideas in
[
17
]
: We fix an arbitrary bipartite graph
G=(U\sqcup V,E)
with
|V|\geq|U|^{r-\epsilon}
and
|E|\geq\delta|V|
, and our goal is to show
G
contains a copy of
(K_{s}^{r})^{\prime}
when
\epsilon
and
\delta
are properly chosen; Firstly, we can assume
G
is close to being biregular using an argument similar to our Lemma
2.1
; Secondly, we call a
r
-set
\{u_{1},\dots,u_{r}\}\subset U

heavy
if
u_{1},\dots,u_{r}
have at least
s^{r}
common neighbors, and we call it
light
if there are at least one but less than
s^{r}
common neighbors; We can assume there is no
K_{s}^{r}
formed by heavy
r
-sets in
U
(otherwise we can find a copy of
(K_{s}^{r})^{\prime}
in
G
greedily), then using an argument similar to Lemma 10 in
[
17
]
, we can argue that there are
\Omega(|U|^{r-\epsilon})
light
r
-sets in
U
; Now, the Erdős–Simonovits supersaturation theorem
[
10
]
guarantees the existence of
O(n^{rs-s^{r}\epsilon})
many copies of
K_{s}^{r}
formed by light
r
-sets in
U
; Finally, every
K_{s}^{r}
-copy in
U
should correspond to a
(K_{s}^{r})^{\prime}
-copy in
G
except for the degenerate case where there are less vertices on the right hand side, but the number of such exceptions can be upper bounded by
o(n^{rs-s^{r}\epsilon})
, completing the proof. Here, the assumption of
G
being close to regular is used in the last step, and
\epsilon,\delta
are chosen to make inequalities hold throughout the proof.

Remark 6.4
.

Suppose
P
is a set of
m
points and
\mathcal{H}
is a set of
n
hyperplanes, both in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, such that the incidence graph

does not contain a copy of

, Apfelbaum and Sharir
[
2
]
proved that

where the constant hidden in the

-notation depends on

. Combining the affirmative answer to Problem
6.2
and our Theorem
1.2
, we can obtain a polynomial improvement of (

) with the additional hypothesis that

does not contain a copy of

. We remark that a

-copy in

naturally corresponds to a

-dimensional grid structure. However, it is unknown whether (

) is asymptotically tight beyond the case

(see
[
30
]
), so (

) could possibly be improved without forbidding any substructures.

The third problem is from incidence geometry. Our Theorem
1.2
fails to answer this problem due to the linear threshold of

being 2 as shown by de Caen and Székely
[

]
. We call a point-line configuration

a

if

and

.

.
