Designing Strong, Tough, Fire‐Retardant and Self‐Healing Elastomers with Phosphorus/Nitrogen‐ and Biphenyl‐Containing Segments
Authors: Yijiao Xue, Shu Gong, Zhezhe Zhou, Zhewen Ma, Yanlong Luo, Mark Lynch, Jiabing Feng, Yan Zhang, Meng Zhang, Yonghong Zhou, Wenlong Cheng, Pingan Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHigh‐performance polyurethane (PU) elastomers have demonstrated many important industrial applications in areas such as soft robotics, flexible sensors and electronic devices. However, it has been challenging to design strong and tough elastomers that are capable of fire‐extinguishing and self‐healing due to different governing mechanisms associated with these properties. Here we present a molecular engineering strategy to achieve strong, tough, fire‐retardant, and healable PU elastomers by rationally designing a phosphorus/nitrogen (P/N)‐ and π–π interacting biphenyl‐containing diol as hard segments with side groups. The formation of relatively strong interchain π–π stacking enables the elastomer to achieve superior mechanical and self‐healing properties, while the combination of π–π stacking and P/N elements promotes exceptional fire retardancy. The resultant elastomer displays a record‐high large break strain of ∼2500%, a large toughness (ca. 379 MJ/m3) and a tensile strength of 46 MPa, and a healing efficiency as high as 95% (tensile strength) and 99% (break strain). Also, the elastomer can self‐extinguish with a high limiting oxygen index of 38.6%. We then demonstrate its application for high‐sensitivity multi‐mode tattoo sensors. This work opens new avenues for developing strong, flexible, tough elastomers with multiple integrated functionalities.Keywords: π–π stacking, fire‐retardant; polyurethane elastomers; self‐healing, tattoo sensor

Introduction

Polyurethanes have been extensively investigated over the past decades due to their tunable mechanical properties, excellent chemical stability, good weather resistance, and versatility [1, 2]. Thermoplastic polyurethane (TPU) elastomers are an important branch of polyurethanes, featuring ease of processing and considerable flexibility depending on the design of their molecular structures. By tailoring the compositions of the reactive soft and hard monomers/segments involved, TPU can be engineered to possess many desirable features and functions, garnering extensive interest for applications in many industrial sectors, such as aircraft tires, flexible electronics, stretchable devices, and batteries [3, 4].

Unfortunately, to date, there are very few successes in achieving the combination of exceptional mechanical, fire retardancy, and self‐healing properties in one TPU system. This is primarily because of multiple conflicts or trade‐offs among the three physical features (Figure 1a). Specifically, to achieve satisfactory fire retardancy, the inclusion of high loading of additive fire retardants or chemically introducing fire‐retardant segments can often lead to instable or destroy the microphase separation structure of the final TPU, thus compromising the overall mechanical properties [5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10]. Moreover, the trade‐off between mechanical and self‐healing properties has become a long‐standing, perplexing problem. While enhancing interchain interactions can improve mechanical strength, it in turn restricts the movement of polymer chains/segments, thus resulting in a poor self‐healing ability [11, 12]. Furthermore, fire retardancy is fundamentally different from self‐healing properties in terms of their governing mechanisms.

Many recent efforts have been dedicated to endowing TPU materials with self‐healing properties at ambient or elevated temperatures without sacrificing mechanical performance. It has proven an effective strategy to improve both the strength/toughness and self‐healing ability of TPU by the introduction of dynamic non‐covalent interactions or dynamic bonds, such as hydrogen bonding [13, 14], metal‐ligand coordination [15], disulfide [16, 17], and Diels‐Alder bonds [18]. For example, Eom et al. reported a carbonate‐type thermoplastic polyurethane elastomer that self‐heals at 35°C and exhibits a tensile strength of 43 MPa, resulting from a dual mechano‐responsive mode through a reversible disorder‐to‐order transition of its hydrogen‐bonding array [19]. In another example, by integrating supramolecular engineering and dynamic Diels‐Alder adaptive networks, Li et al. fabricated a self‐healing PU‐based solid‐state ion‐conductive elastomer with a high tensile strength (7.62 MPa) and elongation at break of 1200%, along with 96% self‐healing efficiency (after self‐healing at 120°C for 24 h) [20]. However, fire‐retardant properties have not been presented in these works (Figure 1b). Recently, a few functional fire‐retardant segments, e.g., DOPO [21], Schiff base [1, 22], and sulfur‐containing motifs [23] have been used to fabricate inherently fire‐retardant and mechanically robust TPU elastomers. The combination of excellent mechanical and fire‐retardant properties can be achieved by designing chain extenders with fire‐retardant elements or groups and constructing ideal microstructures through regulating ratios and structures of hard/soft segments. Nevertheless, none of them show a self‐repairing capability (Figure 1b). Therefore, there has been a long‐standing knowledge gap in the design of strong, tough TPU elastomers that are fire‐retardant and self‐healable due to a lack of rational molecular design strategies.

To fill this knowledge gap, we report a molecularly engineered DOPO derivative diol (DHDP) as a bifunctional hard segment for synthesizing TPU elastomers that are strong, tough, fire‐retardant, and self‐healing (Figure 1c). The designed DHDP contains phosphorus/nitrogen (P/N) elements that impart fire retardance to the resulting TPU (PU‐DHDP). Also, rigid π–π stacking in DHDP induces microscopic phase separation by forming hard phases within the polymer matrix to serve as physical cross‐linking points. Moreover, the biphenyl groups form dynamic π–π stacking, which thus can not only reinforce/toughen but also endow the final elastomers with a self‐healing ability. As a proof‐of‐concept application, we demonstrate one potential application of as‐designed PU‐DHDP elastomers as encapsulant to prepare customizable tattoo sensors that show multi‐mode high‐precision sensing of bending, stretching, and pressure. This study presents a facile yet effective molecular engineering approach to the creation of strong, tough, fire‐retardant, and self‐healing TPU elastomers, which are expected to find extensive applications in smart sensors, e‐skin, and related devices.

Results and Discussion

Molecular Design and Characterizations

To achieve the unique combination of robust mechanical properties, fire retardancy and self‐healing in one polyurethane elastomer system that has never been realized before (Figure 1g), we rationally designed a phosphorus/nitrogen (P/N)‐ and π–π interacting biphenyl‐containing diol motif to serve as the hard segment of target PU elastomers (PU‐DHDP), with polytetramethylene glycol (PTMEG) used as the soft segment (Figure 1c) due to its high strength and toughness [24, 25]. The presence of P/N elements contributes to fire retardancy, whereas the introduction of biphenyl groups promotes the formation of π–π stacking interactions, as evidenced by molecular dynamics (MD) simulation results (Figure 1c,e), which contribute to robust mechanical properties and the self‐healing ability of PU‐DHDP. To understand the role of the presence and position of biphenyl structures in the polymer chains in governing the overall properties of the target PU elastomers, we also designed PU elastomers as control systems, namely PU‐MDM (without P/N elements or π–π stacking), PU‐BHAPE (only with P/N elements), and PU‐DBP (only with π–π stacking) (Figure 1d).

The as‐designed PU‐DHDP motif shows strong π–π stacking interactions through biphenyl groups (Figure 1e), as confirmed by UV–Vis (Figure 1f) and XRD [26] (Figure S6) results. The 1H NMR and 31P NMR results confirm the chemical structure of as‐synthesized DHDP and BHAPE (Figures S1, S2, S7, and S8). The disappearance of ‐NCO groups at around 2264 cm−1 in IR spectra indicates the successful synthesis of PU‐DHDP with different hard/soft ratios (Figure S9) and three control PUs (Figure S10). The chemical structures of all PU samples with different hard segments are further confirmed by 1H NMR results (Figures S11–14). Also, the as‐synthesized four TPU elastomers have relative weight‐averaged molecular weights (M n) in the range of 12–56 kDa and relatively narrow polydispersity indexes (PDI) in the range of 1.8–2.8, as shown by gel permeation chromatography (GPC) (Table S1).

The microstructures of PU‐DHDP and three controls were observed using atomic force microscopy (AFM) (Figure S15). PU‐DHDP exhibits a worm‐like microphase‐separated structure, with phase domains (hard segments) of a size of ∼100 nm in width dispersed in the continuous matrix (soft segments). Likewise, PU‐DBP displays a microphase separation morphology with well‐defined borders, but with the size of hard phases (ca. 200 nm) larger than that of PU‐DHDP. For PU‐MDM and PU‐BHAPE, the phase domain size of hard segments significantly increases to 300 nm or more, and the morphology changes to an island structure, due to the lack of rigid groups in the hard segments, causing difficulties in forming a phase‐separated morphology [27].

To explore the cohesive energy of the hard segment in PU, an all‐atom MD simulation was used, with construction models shown in Figures S3 and S4, and results shown in Figure 1e and Table S2 [24, 28]. The energy contribution of the π–π stacking (E π–π) to the total cohesive energy (E total) in PU‐DHDP is higher than that in PU‐DBP, due to the contribution of hard‐segment interactions to the total cohesive energy (20.8% vs 16.7%), manifesting that biphenyl groups in the side chains tend to form more π–π stacking than in the main chains, and the strong π–π stacking between hard segments can act as cross‐linkers to induce the formation of physical cross‐linking networks in PU‐DHDP. In contrast, the cohesive energies of hard segments in PU‐MDM and PU‐BHAPE are relatively lower than those of PU‐DHDP and PU‐DBP due to a lack of strong π–π stacking interactions between polymer chains. To demonstrate the dominated influence of π–π stacking rather than H‐bonding interactions in four PUs, the cohesive energy of H‐bonding has been roughly calculated by subtracting the cohesive energy of π–π stacking and combination of soft segment (PTMEG) and hexatomic ring in hard segment (IPDI) from the total cohesive energy, with construction models shown in Figure S5, and results shown in Table S2. The contribution of H‐bonding follows the order as PU‐DHDP<PU‐DBP<PU‐BHAPE<PU‐MDM, while the contribution of π–π stacking follows the adverse order as PU‐DHDP>PU‐DBP>PU‐BHAPE>PU‐MDM. Since the mechanical properties, fire retardancy, and self‐healing ability of PU‐DHDP are superior to those of the other three PUs, the π–π stacking is dominant in influencing the comprehensive performance of PUs in this work.

To examine how significantly π–π stacking interactions affect chain movements in PU elastomers, the creep behaviors were examined (Figure S16a) [29]. PU‐DHDP first experiences an increase in strain, which quickly levels off, resulting in a long‐term plateau, indicative of the presence of physical cross‐linking networks within the polymer matrix due to strong interchain π–π stacking and intermolecular H‐bonding. PU‐DBP behaves in a similar manner, but the long‐term plateau occurs slightly later. In contrast, despite the presence of a long‐term plateau, it takes much longer for PU‐MDM and far longer for PU‐BHAPE to exhibit this effect, which is accompanied by larger strains, because there is only intermolecular H‐bonding and weak van der Waals forces present. To further measure the long‐term stability and durability of PU‐DHDP, the creep test of target PU‐DHDP was performed at 0.5 MPa, and the force was revoked for 40 h (Figure S16b). The result shows that the strain of PU‐DHDP recovers by over 85%, from 14.1% to 2.1%. Less irreversible deformation is caused by long‐term stress through dislocation slip, grain boundary sliding, or molecular chain rearrangement, which is a normal phenomenon for incompletely pure elastic material.

The in situ XRD of PU‐DHDP in the range of 25°C–100°C has been conducted to measure the reversible interaction behavior of π–π stacking under variable temperatures (Figure S17). The result shows that the intensity of characteristic peaks at 23.6° and 31.8° for π–π stacking [26] initially decreases when the temperature increases from 25°C to 100°C due to the increasing space of stacking, and then the intensity increases with decreasing temperature, manifesting reversible behavior of π–π stacking. In addition, the intensity of the characteristic peak at 15.6° for the hard segment follows the same trend as that of π–π stacking [29]. This is because the ordered arrangement of hard segment regions due to H‐bonding gradually becomes disordered by heating, and the hard segment can return to an ordered structure when the temperature decreases to 25°C, manifesting reversible behavior of H‐bonding in the hard segment. The higher physical crosslinking density at lower temperature resulting from π–π stacking and H‐bonding leads to higher modulus, and the modulus decreases with increasing temperature due to dissociation of π–π stacking and H‐bonding (Figure S18).

Exceptional Mechanical Properties

In general, the ratios of soft/hard segments have a significant impact on the mechanical properties of the resulting PU elastomers. For as‐synthesized PU‐DHDP, its tensile properties show a strong dependence on the DHDP to PTMEG ratios. For example, when the ratio of DHDP/PTMEG is at 0.5/0.5, the PU elastomer exhibits superior stretchability with a break strain of 2700% but a relatively small tensile strength of only 12 MPa (Figure 2a). When the content of DHDP increases, the break stress of PU‐DHDP(1:0.4:0.6) increases sharply to 46 MPa but with a marginally lower break strain of 2500%, showing balanced mechanical strength and extensibility. With a further increase in DHDP concentrations, the ultimate tensile strength and modulus show steady increases, but the break strain is reduced. For instance, PU‐DHDP(1:0.2:0.8) only retains a break strain of 1000% and a tensile strength of 33 MPa. In brief, the tensile properties of PU‐DHDP elastomers can be readily tuned by adjusting the DHDP to PTMEG ratios, with the as‐synthesized PU‐DHDP(1:0.4:0.6) showing better overall mechanical performances than other PU‐DHDP formulations.

To elucidate the role of π–π stacking in determining mechanical properties of PU elastomers, PU‐DHDP with three control elastomers with different kinds of hard segments and the same soft/hard ratio (0.4/0.6) were compared. As shown in Figure 2b,d–f, Figure S19 and Table S3, PU‐DHDP displays the best tensile properties with a high strength of ∼46 MPa, a high break strain of ∼2500, and a toughness of ∼379 MJ/m3. The true stress at break of PU‐DHDP is as high as 1.2 GPa (Figure 2c), as observed by a thin strip of PU‐DHDP (60 mg, 0.3 mm thick, 4.0 mm wide) capable of supporting a weight (6.0 kg) 100,000 times its own weight without breaking (inset of Figure 2c). Despite the existence of π–π stacking, PU‐DBP still shows slightly inferior tensile strength and extensibility (break strength: 38 MPa, break strain: 2360%) relative to PU‐DHDP. This is because the biphenyl structures in the main chains have a lower degree of freedom, leading to weaker π–π stacking interactions, as evidenced in Table S2. For PU‐MDM and PU‐BHAPE, both elastomers exhibit somewhat poorer tensile strength and toughness than PU‐DHDP and PU‐DBP due to the lack of strong noncovalent interactions, such as π–π stacking, amongst the polymer chains.

In addition to strong interchain π–π stacking, the better mechanical performances of PU‐DHDP benefit from its strain‐hardening or self‐reinforcement properties facilitated by π–π stacking, as demonstrated by its highest elastic modulus and “whitening” at larger strains (Figure S20 and Movie S1) [30]. The strain‐hardening effect is further confirmed by growing deformed scattering circles in SAXS patterns (Figure 2g) and the appearance of crystal diffraction arcs in WAXD patterns (Figure 2h) upon stretching, which is an indication of steady alignment of phase domains in the stretching direction [28, 31]. Indeed, stronger π–π stacking at high strains (Figure 2i) induces the orientation and crystallization of soft PTMEG segments that can lead to strain‐hardening (Figure 2j) [32], which can dissipate energy, adapt to deformation, or optimize intermolecular interactions, enabling the entire PU system to maintain stability under strain and exhibit superior mechanical properties. When the strain is decreased, the intensity of peaks for π–π stacking and PTMEG crystallization is gradually decreased, manifesting reversible behavior of π–π stacking and strain‐hardening in PU‐DHDP under different strains.

We also conducted loading‐unloading tension experiments at various strains and with 11 cycles of tension at the same strain to examine the anti‐fatigue performances of as‐designed PU elastomers. Among the four PU elastomers, PU‐DHDP shows the best elasticity or recovery ability, as reflected by more compliant cycles and smaller displacement of strains (Figure S21). PU‐DHDP shows a residual strain of less than 250% after ever‐increasing strains, and less than 100% after 11 cycles of tension at 400% strain. In comparison, the other three samples exhibit much higher residual strain in these two tension modes. The superior elasticity of PU‐DHDP is primarily because of the combination of dynamic π–π stacking and H‐bonding that facilitates exchange of interchain interactions, which leads to a large number of modes of elastic deformation [33, 34]. Owing to dynamic, rapid rupture and reformation of the π–π stacking in the high‐density hard domains, PU‐DHDP exhibits the best crack tolerance among the four PU elastomers, with a fracture energy of 113.1 kJ m−2 far exceeding that of PU‐MDM (9.7 kJ m−2), PU‐BHAPE (1.9 kJ m−2), and PU‐DBP (29.8 kJ m−2) (Figure S22). Additionally, a PU‐DHDP film with a thickness of only 0.2 mm can withstand the force exerted by an adult when pierced with a ball pen tip, illustrating its superior puncture resistance (Movie S2).

The excellent tensile properties of as‐designed PU‐DHDP are ascribed to its strong π–π stacking interactions amongst biphenyl groups in the side chain of the hard segments. The combination of strong π–π stacking and H‐bonding interactions acts as cross‐linkers of the soft segments and induces the formation of a physical crosslinking network in PU‐DHDP, which contributes to the formation of a microphase‐separated morphology (Figure 1c and Figure S15) and increases the entropic elasticity of polymer chains. When stretched, the stronger π–π stacking induces the orientation and crystallization of soft PTMEG segments that can lead to strain‐hardening, and the H‐bonding in the hard segment becomes ordered (Figure 2i,j), which can dissipate energy and adapt to deformation. When the stretch further increases, the interchain π–π stacking and H‐bonding interactions break before covalent bonds, thus consuming large amounts of any external force and further enhancing the tensile strength. When the force is released, the polymer chains are prone to rebound, ascribed to the microphase separation structure and the reversible nature of non‐covalent π–π stacking and H‐bonding interactions (Figure 2k).

Exceptional Intrinsic Fire Retardancy

Another feature of as‐designed P/N containing DHDP motifs capable of forming π–π interactions was to rationally incorporate fire retardancy to the resulting PU elastomers. P/N containing compounds are extensively reported to act in both the condensed and gas phases, to interrupt the thermal degradation and burning of the polymer matrix, promote the char formation, and dilute the combustible gases and available oxygen [35, 36]. The π–π interactions have also been reported to play an active role in restricting the thermal degradation of the polymer matrix, so improving the strength of the residual char, and thus are expected to improve the fire retardancy of PU‐DHDP [25].

Vertical burning testing is often used to assess fire retardancy levels of industrial materials. As shown in Figure 3a–d, PU‐DHDP shows a self‐extinguishing ability and passes the desired UL‐94 V‐0 rating (Figure 3e). The other three control PU elastomers fail, clearly indicating that both P/N‐containing moieties and π–π interactions contribute to the exceptional fire retardancy of PU‐DHDP. Moreover, PU‐DHDP gives a higher LOI value (38.6%) than the other three control elastomers. Despite a UL‐94 V‐2 rating for PU‐MDM, PU‐BHAPE, and PU‐DBP, they still exhibit higher LOI values than those of PU‐PTD (1,4‐butanediol as hard segment), which means that P/N‐containing units and/or π–π interactions in hard segments play a role in providing fire retardance. In addition, microscale cone calorimetry (MCC) was employed to further evaluate the fire performances of as‐prepared PU elastomers. PU‐DHDP presents the lowest peak of heat release rate (PHRR) among all PU elastomers, which is reduced by 27% as compared to PU‐PTD, suggesting its effectiveness as a fire retardant (Figure 3f and Table S4). Meantime, PU‐DHDP also shows the lowest total heat release (THR) value compared to other samples, which means that DHDP motifs play a key role in reducing the heat release of the elastomer.

Since the modes of action of P/N‐containing compounds have been well‐reported before, this work does not elaborate further. Though it is worth probing the effect of π–π interactions on the fire retardancy of PU‐DHDP. Using PU‐BHAPE as a control, its viscosity plummets with increasing temperature (Figure 3g), because the intermolecular interactions quickly diminish due to increased space between polymer chains when heated. In contrast, PU‐DHDP shows a more moderate trend of decrease in viscosity as temperature increases, especially for temperatures below 150°C, resulting from physical crosslinking by π–π stacking in the PU system. In addition, the self‐extinguish ability (Figure 3c,d), UL‐94 rating (Figure 3e), and LOI value (Figure 3f) of PU‐DHDP are superior to those of PU‐BHAPE, which is ascribed to the strong π–π stacking interactions in PU‐DHDP chains that impart more heat‐resistance [26, 37]. The existence of π–π stacking during heating can be detected by in situ XRD [38, 39], where a peak at 25° appears in the spectrum, and this peak gradually diminishes on heating (Figure 3h). The π–π stacking can serve to physically cross‐link sites and thus contributes to restricting thermal degradation of PU‐DHDP, as reflected by its higher T 5 (the temperature where 5 wt.% loss occurs) than PU‐BHAPE (Figure 3i). Additionally, PU‐DHDP yields more char residues with a higher degree of graphitization than PU‐BHAPE (Figure S23).

Self‐Healing Properties

In theory, the existence of as‐designed dynamic π–π stacking interactions is expected to enable the PU‐DHDP elastomer to be healable upon heating to a certain temperature. To ensure the cut elastomer to closely contacts after cutting, a PU‐DHDP strip was first cut in half width by a blade and then allowed to heal in the oven at varied temperatures for different periods of time. First, we healed the PU‐DHDP sample at 25°C, 40°C, 60°C, 80°C, respectively, but the cracks still exist in half‐cut samples even after 7 days (Figure S24a). When the healing temperature increases to 90°C, the crack can fully vanish by heating for 3 h (Figure S24b). Hence, we fix the healing time at 3 h, and the healing efficiency of the sample gradually enhances with increasing temperature, with PU‐DHDP showing the highest healing efficiency at 95% of tensile strength and 99% of break strain when healed at 110°C for 3 h (Figure 4a and Figure S25a). However, with a further increase in temperature, the elastomer tends to degrade due to aging, as observed by a darker color (Figure S26a), thus leading to reduced mechanical properties (Figure 4a and Figure S25a). Then the tensile tests of PU‐DHDP at 110°C for 0.5–4 h were tested, but the samples under shorter (0.5–2 h) or longer (4 h) times both failed to receive satisfying tensile properties due to insufficient molecular motion or aging (Figure 4b, Figures S25b and S26b). Therefore, we chose 110°C, 3 h as the most efficient and economical way to heal the PU‐DHDP sample.

The healing capability of PU‐DHDP was characterized against other damage modes, i.e., scratched and holed. The sample can be healed by heating at 110°C for 3 h (Figure 4c,d), as visually verified by microscopic observation with a slight drop in mechanical properties (strength and break strain) for the healed samples observed (Figure 4e). The self‐healing properties of PU‐DHDP are further illustrated by an all‐atom model MD simulation, where a crack is set in the cells [40, 41]. With increasing healing time, the two halves adjacent to the crack region approach gradually (see Movie S3) and the density distribution at the crack zone gradually recovers (Figure 4h), giving an indication of the self‐healing ability of PU‐DHDP.

Comparing the healing efficiency between the four as‐synthesized elastomers, they follow the order: PU‐DHDP > PU‐BHAPE > PU‐MDM > PU‐DBP (Figure 4f,g). PU‐DBP exhibits the worst healing effect primarily because rigid biphenyl groups in the main chains require a considerable amount of space (free volume) and energy to overcome the potential barriers of rotation within the backbone, leading to a higher activation energy (E a) required for chain segments to diffuse (Figure 4j and Figure S27c) [42, 43]. In contrast, rigid biphenyl groups at the side chains of PU‐DHDP are only connected to the N element at the main chain, while the other end is free. The movement of biphenyl is relatively independent and only requires a local, small space for swinging or rotating, with less restraint from the surrounding chains, thus leading to a lower E a value and higher healing efficiency of PU‐DHDP than PU‐DBP (Figure 4i,j) [44, 45]. This result can also be supported by a higher contribution of π–π stacking to the total cohesive energy of PU‐DHDP (with side‐chain biphenyl) than PU‐DBP (with main‐chain biphenyl) (20.8% vs 16.7)%), since the side‐chain biphenyl is more prone to form π–π stacking than main‐chain biphenyl. Although PU‐MDM and PU‐BHAPE with softer main chains show lower E a than PU‐DHDP (Figure 4j, Figure S27a,b), they show inferior healing efficiency due to the lack of an internal driving force for chain diffusion. Additionally, PU‐BHAPE shows a higher healing efficiency than PU‐MDM because more lateral methyl groups in its hard segments allow easier chain diffusion.

To further confirm the importance of π–π stacking to the healing effect, a DSC test has been conducted to measure the thermal transformation of PUs. As observed in Figure S28, the T gs of PUs are ranged within 41°C–52°C, which are below the fully healed temperature of 110°C, illustrating that the self‐healing effect of PUs can be motivated by heat‐induced segmental diffusion, and the healing efficiency is gradually enhanced with increasing temperature (Figure 4a). The T g of PU‐DHDP is higher than the other three PUs, but the self‐healing efficiency of PU‐DHDP is the highest, manifesting a crucial contribution of non‐covalent π–π exchange to the healing effect. Therefore, the healing behavior is dominated by non‐covalent π–π exchange, which acts as an internal driving force for chain diffusion.

PU‐DHDP as Encapsulants for Multi‐Mode Tattoo Sensors

To explore the potential of as‐developed PU‐DHDP elastomer as substrates of e‐skin, we fabricated a crack‐programmed bending sensor with standing gold nanowires as the sensing material [46] with cracks on the designated areas to detect the bending of the middle fingers (Figure 5a,b and Movie S4). With finger bending, only the designated crack areas show crack propagation, which leads to a significant increase in electrical resistivity. The PU‐DHDP e‐skin is divided into six regions or areas, where only areas 1, 2, and 5 (the three finger joints areas of the middle finger) are strain sensitive. The e‐skin shows selective sensitivity in the cracked area (Figure 5c), so demonstrating its potential in detecting specific finger bending states.

We also explored the possibility of PU‐DHDP as an ultrathin encapsulant for tattoo sensors. The tattoo‐like gold nanowire film with a sub‐10 µm thickness was fabricated as described in previous work [46]. This film was transferred and then encapsulated with PU‐DHDP by way of spin‐coating to produce a tattoo strain sensor (Figure 5d). The strain sensor shows nearly the same sensitivity as one prepared without PU‐DHDP encapsulant under consecutive stretching (Figure 5e), so showing the great potential of PU‐DHDP as a sensor encapsulant. In addition, the sensitivity of the strain sensor increases from k1 of 0.18%−1 in the strain range of 0–20% to k2 of 0.44%−1 in the strain range of 20–50%. The higher sensitivity in larger strain is mainly because cracks propagate with increased tensile force, resulting in an exponential (rather than proportional) decrease in the conductive path.

To further investigate the real‐time pressure‐sensing capability of PU‐DHDP‐based e‐skin (Figure 5f), we designed a tattoo‐like gold nanowire‐based spiral‐shaped pressure sensor for an eco‐flex mimetic finger. The sensor exhibits a rapid and stable response to varied pressures (Figure 5g), indicating that its maximum relative resistance to change is in direct proportion to the applied pressure. Additionally, the relative resistance change returns to its initial level upon pressure release because of the physical separation of the electrode and dielectric layer, so highlighting the excellent sensibility and stability of the PU‐DHDP‐based pressure sensor. The sensitivity of the pressure sensor can be divided into two linear regions: the sensitivity S1 of 0.21 kPa−1 in the pressure range from 0 to 7.5 kPa, and the sensitivity S2 of 1.13 kPa−1 in the pressure range from 10 to 20 kPa (Figure 5h). The lower sensitivity observed in low‐pressure regions compared to the high‐pressure regions is possibly because the PU‐DHDP dielectric layer is not in close contact with gold nanowire electrodes when the pressure is low. In this case, the resistance increase mainly originates from out‐of‐plane deformation‐induced piezoresistivity changes, which lead to low sensitivity and low signal intensity. The increased sensitivity from S1 to S2 is due to the large pressure‐induced friction induced between the electrode and PU‐DHDP dielectric layer.

To confirm the long‐term stability and durability of as‐prepared sensors, we performed 500 loading/unloading cyclic tests at 10 kPa. No significant decay in resistance amplitudes over 500 cycles is observed, showing excellent stability and durability (Figure 5i), which is crucial to its practical long‐term applications. There is only an insignificant reduction in output resistance during 300 loading/unloading cycles, presumably because of the pressure‐induced friction between PU‐DHDP and gold nanowire layers. However, it seems that the reduction in output data can almost be ignored after 300 loading/unloading cycles.

Given the excellent strain‐sensing performance, we further designed a proof‐of‐concept tattoo sensor on nitrile gloves coated or encapsulated with PU‐DHDP (Figure 5j,k). The signals of the sensor perfectly align with finger movements (see Movie S5). We further coated the above PU‐DHDP glove on the manipulator and characterized its bending behavior (Movie S6). An increasing trend in resistance of sensors appears in the response curve when bending angles increase from 0 to 150° (Figure 5l), and the resistance signals nearly overlap as the bending angles decrease from 150 to 0° (Figure S29), underscoring good repeatability and stability. The PU‐DHDP film demonstrates excellent biocompatibility, as confirmed by skin irritation testing [47], revealing no adverse dermal reactions upon film removal (Figure S30). The above results clearly demonstrate the great application possibilities of the as‐designed PU‐DHDP elastomer as an ideal e‐skin for a variety of tattoo sensors and wearable devices.

For testing the intrinsic flame retardancy in electronic devices, we designed another pressure sensor with PU‐DHDP as encapsulating material (Figure S31a–c). Upon pressing the sensor, the signals are as accurate as the unencapsulated sensor, manifesting that the sensitivity of the sensor is not affected by encapsulation of PU‐DHDP (Figure S31d and Movie S7). Once the sensor is ignited, the PU‐DHDP can self‐extinguish immediately without affecting the normal operation of the sensor (Figure S31e–h, Movies S8 and S9), which can hardly be achieved by the commercial PDMS encapsulating material [38]. Therefore, the as‐developed PU‐DHDP can be used as an ideal fire‐safe encapsulation material for electronic sensors and has the potential to reduce the fire hazard of wearable devices.

Conclusion

We have proposed a molecular engineering strategy to produce strong, tough, fire‐extinguishing, and self‐healing PU elastomers (PU‐DHDP) by rationally designing a multifunctional P/N‐ and π–π interacting biphenyl‐containing diol as hard segments and side groups. The π–π interacting biphenyl groups residing in the side groups facilitate the formation of dynamic interchain π–π stacking, which can induce microphase separation, thus reinforcing/toughening the final elastomers by providing physical cross‐linking sites, as well as imparting desirable healing capability. The combination of a P/N‐containing moiety and interchain π–π stacking works cooperatively to endow the elastomers with exceptional intrinsic fire retardancy. Our demonstrations show that as‐developed PU‐DHDP elastomers represent a promising e‐skin material for strain/bending/pressure sensors. In addition, the preparation of the functional DHDP motif only requires a one‐step, mild, and rapid synthesis process, making its mass production commercially viable. This work offers a facile molecular design approach to produce high‐performance elastomers that show many possible real‐world industrial applications in e‐skin, soft robots, and wearable sensors.

Experimental Section/Methods

Materials

Polytetramethylene ether glycol (PTMEG, Mn = 2000 g/mol) was purchased from Rhawn. The 9,10‐Dihydro‐9‐oxo‐10‐phosphaphenanthrene‐10‐oxide (DOPO), diethanolamine, and paraformaldehyde were provided by Shanghai Energy Chemical Reagent Co, Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Isophorone diisocyanate (IPDI, 99%), N, N‐dimethylacetamide (DMAc, 99.8%), diethyl phosphite, 4,4'‐Dihydroxybiphenyl, 1,4‐butanediol, Dibutyltin dilaurate (DBTDL, 95%), N‐Methyldiethanolamine (MDM, 98%), and 4,4'‐Dihydroxydiphenyl (DBP, 97%) were purchased from Macklin.

Synthesis of DHDP and BHAPE

The DHDP was synthesized based on previous work [48]. 21.6 g DOPO, 4.5 g paraformaldehyde, 9.6 g diethanolamine, and 100 mL chloroform were added to a 250 mL three‐necked flask equipped with a mechanical stirrer, a condenser, and a thermometer. The mixture was heated to 60°C and continuously stirred for 12 h, then it was cooled to room temperature. The white crude product was filtered and washed with ethanol for several times, followed by drying in reduced pressure at 150°C for 12 h. Finally, a transparent yellow sticky product (DHDP) was obtained, and its yield was about 97%. The synthetic route for DHDP is shown in Figure S1.

The preparation process for BHAPE was the same as DHDP, as long as to replace the DOPO with diethyl phosphite. The yield was about 95%. The synthetic route for BHAPE is shown in Figure S2.

Synthesis of PU Elastomers

The typical synthetic routes for PU‐DHDP (1:0.4:0.6) are described in detail as follows. First, a three‐neck round‐bottom flask equipped with a mechanical stirrer was charged with PTMEG (M n of 2000 g mol−1, 2 mmol), followed by heating at 110°C under vacuum and stirring for 30 min to remove the moisture. After cooling the reaction system to 80°C, the mixture of IPDI (5 mmol) and dibutyltin dilaurate (DBTDL, 0.02 g) dissolved 20 mL in DMAc was added into the flask. The reaction system was further heated at 80°C under N2 atmosphere and stirred for 3 h. Consequently, DHDP (3 mmol) as the chain extender, dissolved in 20 mL DMAc, was added into the reaction system that was further kept at 80°C for 3 h under stirring and N2 atmosphere. Viscous and orange solutions of the PU‐DHDP (1:0.6:0.4) were finally obtained. The as‐obtained polymer solutions were finally poured into PTFE plates and then dried at 80°C overnight in a drying oven, followed by drying at 80°C for 12 h under vacuum to obtain the elastomer samples. The films (thickness: ca. 300 µm) were cut into different shapes for further measurements.

Following the same procedure, PU‐MDM (1:0.6:0.4), PU‐BHAPE (1:0.6:0.4), and PU‐DBP (1:0.6:0.4) were synthesized by replacing DHDP with MDM, BHAPE, and DBP, respectively. PU‐DHDP (1:0.3:0.7), PU‐DHDP (1:0.5:0.5) and PU‐DHDP (1:0.6:0.4) were synthesized by changing the feed mass of PTMEG and DHDP as follows: PTMEG (1.5 mmol) and DHDP (3.5 mmol) for PU‐DHDP (1:0.3:0.7); PTMEG (2.5 mmol) and DHDP (2.5 mmol) for PU‐DHDP (1:0.5:0.5); PTMEG (3.0 mmol) and DHDP (4.0 mmol) for PU‐DHDP (1:0.6:0.4). PU‐PTD (1:0.6:0.4) with 1,4‐butanediol as hard segment was also prepared for fire‐retardancy comparison.
