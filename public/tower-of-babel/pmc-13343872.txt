Pneumosinus dilatans frontalis: a cause of headaches mimicking chronic sinusitis—a case report and review of the literature
Authors: Tesfaye Deresse Assefa, Abdulfatah Workicho Mustafa, Salhadin Mohammed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPneumosinus dilatans (PD) is a rare condition characterized by abnormal enlargement of one or more paranasal sinuses without changes to the sinus walls, with the frontal sinuses (pneumosinus dilatans frontalis) being the most commonly affected. To our knowledge, descriptions of pneumosinus dilatans frontalis (PDF) are limited to a cluster of cases, which restricts the development of appropriate diagnostic and management strategies.Case presentationWe present a case of a 23-year-old Ethiopian man who has been experiencing intermittent nasal obstruction and headache localized to the frontal region. The discomfort has persisted for over 2 years and responds well to over-the-counter analgesics. The patient has a prior diagnosis of chronic frontal sinusitis based on clinical evaluation. During the physical examination, all findings were normal. Routine laboratory tests showed normal results, indicating no underlying systemic issues. Given these findings, the treatment plan included the use of analgesics to manage the pain, along with regular follow-up appointments to monitor the patient’s condition. A CT scan was done to further assess the frontal sinuses and to rule out any other potential complications or underlying causes contributing to the patient's symptoms. The scan revealed that the frontal sinuses were abnormally enlarged, with air-filled spaces, while the sinus walls remained intact and normal in thickness.ConclusionsPneumosinus dilatans is a rare but significant differential diagnosis to consider in patients, particularly young men, when they present with frontal headaches.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13256-026-06103-x.

Introduction

Pneumosinus dilatans frontalis (PDF) is an uncommon condition characterized by the abnormal expansion of the frontal sinus beyond normal anatomical boundaries in the absence of bone destruction or mucosal disease. Meyes first described it in 1898, and the exact cause of pneumosinus dilatans remains unclear [1]. Although rare, the condition has been increasingly recognized in the literature. A 2017 systematic review identified 145 reported patients [2], while a subsequent comprehensive review documented 171 cases reported over the past century [3], confirming the rarity of this entity. Frontal sinus involvement represents the most frequently affected site [4].

Clinically, PDF may present with nonspecific symptoms, such as headache or facial discomfort, and, in some cases, with aesthetic deformities, such as frontal bossing. Due to this nonspecific presentation, it is frequently misdiagnosed as chronic rhinosinusitis or other more common causes of “sinus headache,” leading to inappropriate management.

Radiologic evaluation plays a critical role in diagnosis, as pneumosinus dilatans is characterized by hyperaeration of the sinus cavity with intact walls and absence of mucosal thickening, distinguishing it from inflammatory sinus disease. Awareness of this entity is, therefore, essential to ensure accurate diagnosis and avoid unnecessary interventions.

We present a case of PDF in a young adult male presenting with chronic headache, emphasizing the diagnostic challenges, radiologic features, and management considerations of this uncommon condition, along with a review of the literature.

Case presentation

Patient information

A 23-year-old Ethiopian man presented to our outpatient clinic with complaints of dull, aching headaches localized to the frontal region. The headaches are characterized as mild to moderate in intensity and occur intermittently. He has experienced this discomfort for the past 2 years, and it responds to over-the-counter analgesics. The patient has a previous diagnosis of chronic frontal sinusitis based on clinical evaluation.

He denies experiencing any swelling or facial deformity. He has no history of diabetes mellitus, hypertension, or other chronic medical conditions and denies any family history of such illnesses. The patient denied significant psychosocial stressors, including recent life stress, psychiatric illness, or substance use. He had no history of prior surgical or medical interventions related to sinus disease. Prior to presentation, he had self-administered over-the-counter analgesics intermittently, which provided temporary symptomatic relief without affecting the overall course or frequency of headaches.

The patient had not undergone prior formal diagnostic evaluation, including imaging studies or specialist consultation, for the previously presumed diagnosis of chronic sinusitis. No structured medical therapy, such as antibiotics, intranasal corticosteroids, or decongestants, had been initiated prior to presentation, and symptom management was limited to intermittent use of over-the-counter analgesics with only temporary relief.

Clinical findings

On physical examination, the patient appeared stable. His vital signs were as follows: blood pressure 120/70 mmHg, pulse rate 90 bpm, respiratory rate 18 breaths per minute, oxygen saturation 96%, and temperature 36.0°C. The general medical examination was otherwise unremarkable.

Estimated frontal sinus volumes were calculated using the ellipsoid formula (0.52 × height × width × depth) [5–7]. The calculated volume was approximately 19.38 cm3 on the right and 17.11 cm3 on the left, resulting in a combined volume of approximately 36.49 cm3. In adult males, normal frontal sinus volume typically ranges from 4 to 10 cm3 per side, with a combined bilateral volume of 8–20 cm3. Therefore, the measured volumes in our patient substantially exceed expected physiologic values, confirming marked sinus hyperaeration consistent with pneumosinus dilatans frontalis.

Primary headache disorders such as migraine and tension-type headache were considered. However, the absence of photophobia, phonophobia, nausea, aura, or typical migraine periodicity, together with radiologic evidence of sinus hyperaeration, supported a structural secondary cause.

Conservative management in this case was aimed at symptomatic control of headache through analgesic therapy, without direct modification of the underlying sinus expansion. This approach is appropriate in patients without neurological deficits, cosmetic concerns, or complications. In contrast, alternative management strategies include surgical interventions, such as craniofacial remodelling or endoscopic sinus procedures, which are typically reserved for cases with significant deformity, functional impairment, or compressive complications.

Discussion

Pneumosinus dilatans frontalis (PDF) presents a diagnostic challenge, as it can easily be misinterpreted as chronic sinus disease, leading to erroneous interventions [8]. The significance of PDF in medical literature lies both in its uncommon nature and its presentation, which can simulate more prevalent sinus pathologies [9]. Accurate identification is critical to avoid unwarranted surgical procedures that may exacerbate the patient’s condition rather than alleviate it [10, 11]. Consequently, awareness of this entity allows healthcare professionals to differentiate it from other sinus diseases, thereby enhancing diagnostic accuracy and improving clinical outcomes [11].

PDF is characterized by the abnormal expansion of the frontal sinus in response to an underlying inflammatory response [12]. The exact cause of this rare disorder is not well-understood [2]. Several mechanisms have been proposed. The ‘ball-valve’ theory suggests intermittent obstruction at the sinus ostium allows air trapping and progressive expansion [13]. More recent evidence using fluorine-18 sodium fluoride PET–CT has demonstrated increased osteoblastic activity in affected sinus walls, supporting the concept that pneumosinus dilatans is primarily an osteogenic bone-remodeling disorder rather than purely an air-pressure phenomenon.

Pneumosinus dilatans is usually a benign and asymptomatic condition that is often discovered incidentally through imaging studies [1]. While many individuals do not experience any symptoms, some may report issues, such as facial pain, pressure, headaches, visual disturbances (including proptosis and diplopia), and nasal obstruction, depending on the specific sinus involved and the extent of its expansion [1, 14]. In some cases, it can lead to noticeable facial deformities, most commonly a prominent forehead, a condition referred to as "frontal bossing," particularly when the frontal sinus is affected [14]. When frontal deformity is prominent and aesthetic concerns are paramount, surgical options such as osteotomy may be recommended to address both functional and cosmetic issues, emphasizing the need for a tailored approach in management [15].

In rare cases, pneumosinus dilatans may signal the presence of an intracranial meningioma, especially when it occurs near the skull base [16]. In one clinical series, the presence of pneumosinus dilatans was associated with a significantly increased likelihood of an underlying skull-base meningioma, with an odds ratio of 5.67 [17], suggesting a meaningful association rather than a coincidental finding. Detecting it on imaging studies can sometimes help indicate the likelihood of a nearby meningioma, particularly when examining the sphenoid or ethmoid sinuses [16]. Although CT imaging in our patient demonstrated intact sinus walls without erosion, hyperostosis, or focal bony thickening, features that can suggest underlying tumor, MRI was not performed due to resource constraints. Given the absence of neurological deficits, orbital symptoms, visual disturbance, or radiologic signs of intracranial mass effect, the pre-test probability of associated intracranial pathology was considered low. Nevertheless, small or early intracranial lesions cannot be completely excluded without MRI, and this remains a limitation of the present case.

In resource-limited settings such as ours, diagnostic evaluation may be influenced by financial and accessibility constraints, which can limit the use of advanced imaging modalities, such as MRI. These factors may pose challenges in definitively excluding associated intracranial pathology, although clinical and CT findings can still provide a reliable basis for diagnosis.

From a prognostic perspective, isolated PDF without evidence of associated pathology or complications is generally associated with a favorable clinical course. However, longitudinal follow-up remains essential to detect potential progression, development of symptoms, or rare associations with underlying intracranial lesions.

The clinical presentation of pneumosinus dilatans and chronic sinus disease can be quite similar, leading to potential overlap in symptoms. However, the key difference between the two conditions is found in imaging results [15]. CT scans of patients with pneumosinus dilatans typically show significant enlargement of the paranasal sinuses, without the mucosal thickening that is usually associated with chronic sinus disease. A well-defined air-filled cavity, particularly noted in the frontal sinus, is a characteristic feature of pneumosinus dilatans. This distinction is emphasized in recent literature, which highlights the abnormal dilation of the sinuses as opposed to the chronic inflammatory changes seen in sinus disease [18].

The management of PDF primarily involves monitoring and follow-up for asymptomatic cases [19]. However, patients who exhibit symptoms may require surgical intervention to reshape the affected frontal sinus [14, 19]. A useful framework for understanding this condition is the classification and management algorithm proposed by Ricci [20]. This model stratifies pneumosinus dilatans according to the sinus involved, the number of sinuses affected, and the presence or absence of associated pathology. Single-sinus involvement—most commonly the frontal sinus—accounts for the majority of cases and is least frequently associated with secondary disease. In contrast, sphenoid or ethmoid involvement carries a higher likelihood of underlying intracranial or orbital pathology. Ricci’s algorithm emphasizes initial exclusion of associated lesions through imaging, followed by treatment decisions based on symptom burden.

According to this framework, asymptomatic or mildly symptomatic isolated cases are best managed conservatively with observation and periodic imaging. Surgical intervention is reserved for patients with significant cosmetic deformity, sinus outflow obstruction, or complications, such as optic nerve compression. Our patient fits the profile of isolated frontal pneumosinus dilatans without associated pathology and with minimal symptomatology. Therefore, conservative monitoring was chosen, consistent with the algorithm’s recommendation [20]. This approach minimizes surgical risk while allowing early detection should progression or new symptoms occur.

Conclusion

In conclusion, pneumosinus dilatans frontalis represents a distinctive clinical entity that poses unique diagnostic challenges, often mimicking the symptoms of chronic sinus disease. Its rare presentation can lead to misdiagnosis and unnecessary treatments if not recognized adequately. Therefore, clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion, particularly in patients exhibiting atypical sinus pathology or those who do not respond to conventional therapies.
